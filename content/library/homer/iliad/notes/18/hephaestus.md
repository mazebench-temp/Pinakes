summary: Thetis promises her son armor from Hephaestus and finds him in his house of bronze, making tripods that go of themselves to the assembly of the gods. He remembers that she and Eurynome saved him when his mother wished to hide him away for his lameness, comes out to her supported by golden handmaids, hears her plea, and promises armor that men will marvel at. Then he sets his bellows to work (see [[forge]]) and makes the shield, the corselet, the helmet and the greaves.

**Promised.** Thetis promises Achilles that at dawn she will return “bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.137), and she tells the Nereids that she will go to Olympus, “to Hephaestus, famed for his craft, in the hope that he may be willing / to give my son glorious armor, gleaming all over” (18.143–144; see [[thetis]] and [[famed-for-his-craft]]). Achilles tells Iris that his mother “promised to bring fine armor from Hephaestus” (18.191; see [[iris]]). The armor that Hector stripped from Patroclus was the gods' gift to Peleus (18.84–85; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]), and the poem has not said who made it (see the note on Book 17). See [[the-making-of-the-arms]].

**His house.** Thetis finds him at home:

{{quote:b4cfbdd5-75f0-5b26-8685-e29db2097dd0}}

He built it for himself, as he built a house for each of the gods on Olympus (1.606–608) and Hera's chamber with its secret bolt (14.166–168; see [[house]]). ἀστερόεντα, “starry”, probably means adorned with starlike ornaments, and χάλκεον, “of bronze”, may allude to walls coated with polished metal, like the bronze walls of Alcinous' palace (*Odyssey* 7.86), or may be the custom of making divine things of metal, as the island of Aeolus has a wall of bronze (*Odyssey* 10.3–4; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.370; see [[starry]], [[imperishable]] and the translation's note on 18.370). κυλλοποδίων, “the crook-footed god”, names him by his lameness (18.371; see [[crook-footed]]).

**The tripods.** She finds him “sweating as he turned about among his bellows, / in haste”, making twenty tripods on golden wheels “so that of themselves they might enter the divine assembly / and go back again to his house, a wonder to see” (18.372–377; see [[tripod]]). θεῖον ἀγῶνα is the phrase with which Hector spoke of the Trojan women “who will enter the divine assembly, praying for me” (7.298), where it is usually taken for the sanctuary of the gods' images; here it is the assembly of the gods themselves (see the translation's notes on 7.298 and 18.376). The handles are not yet fixed on, and he is forging the rivets (18.378–379); Leaf notes that the oldest bronze tripods found at Olympia are fastened with rivets (on 18.378). He compares the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard the palace of Alcinous, immortal and ageless (*Odyssey* 7.91–94), and the golden handmaids below (18.417–420), and finds the very sparing use of such marvels even on Olympus noteworthy (on 18.376; see [[the-golden-handmaids]]). Aristotle, arguing that a master needs slaves because tools cannot do their own work, imagines tools that could, like “the tripods of Hephaestus, which the poet says enter of themselves the divine assembly” (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1; see [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]). He toils “with his cunning skill” (ἰδυίῃσι πραπίδεσσι, 18.380), a phrase that Homer uses only of his work: the houses of the gods (1.608), the shield (18.482), the colonnades in the house of Zeus (20.12), and the dogs of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.92; see [[with-cunning-skill]]).

**The fall.** His mother wished “to hide me away because I was lame” (18.397): χωλὸν ἐόντα, the words with which he complains in the Odyssey that Aphrodite dishonors him because he is lame (*Odyssey* 8.308). κυνώπιδος, “dog-faced” (18.396), is Achilles' insult to Agamemnon (κυνῶπα, 1.159) and Helen's word for herself (3.180); here the son uses it of his mother, and in the Odyssey he uses it of his wife Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.319; see [[dog-face]] and [[hera]]). In Book 1 it was Zeus who threw him from the threshold of heaven when he tried to defend his mother, and he fell all day and came down on Lemnos at sunset, with little life left in him, where the Sintians cared for him (1.590–594; see [[lemnos]] and [[sintians]]). Walter Leaf notes that the two stories have the same application with different details, and that Hephaestus and his mother are generally represented as close allies (on 18.395; see the translation's note on 18.395–405, [[fall-of-hephaestus]] and [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]). Thetis and Eurynome, a daughter of Oceanus, took him to their bosom (18.398–399; see [[eurynome]] and [[oceanus]]), and for nine years he made jewelry for them, “brooches and curved spirals, rosettes and necklaces, / in a hollow cave”, with the stream of Oceanus flowing boundless around it, and no one else, god or mortal, knew of it (18.400–405). The whole verse of the jewelry recurs in the *Hymn to Aphrodite*, when Anchises takes the goddess's ornaments from her body (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 163), and its last words describe them when she first appears to him (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 87–88; Leaf on 18.401; see the translation's note on 18.401).

**The price of his life.** “And now she has come to our house; so I must indeed / pay Thetis of the lovely tresses the full price for my life” (18.406–407; see [[of-the-lovely-tresses]] and [[zoagria]]). ζῳάγρια was first the price paid to a warrior who took a prisoner alive, and here it is a playful extension (Leaf on 18.407; see the translation's note on 18.407); in the Odyssey Nausicaa tells Odysseus that he owes it to her (*Odyssey* 8.461–462). He bids Charis “set fine guest-gifts before her” (18.408; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[xenia]]).

**The god comes out.** “He spoke, and rose from the anvil block, a huge, panting god, / limping; and his slender shins moved nimbly beneath him” (18.410–411). αἴητον is a word of unknown meaning, found only here, and πέλωρ an odd word for Hephaestus (Leaf on 18.410; see the translation's note on 18.410); the second verse returns when he goes down to the battle of the gods (20.37). He sets his bellows away from the fire, gathers his tools into a silver chest, and with a sponge wipes “his face and both his hands / and his massive neck and his shaggy chest” (18.412–415); then he puts on his tunic and takes “a thick staff” (σκῆπτρον, 18.416, the only place where the word is not a scepter), and goes out limping, held up by golden handmaids that have mind and voice and strength (18.417–421; see [[the-golden-handmaids]] and [[handmaids]]). ἔρρων, “hobbling” (18.421), is going painfully because of his lameness (see the translation's note on 18.421). In Book 1 the gods laughed without end to see him bustling through the hall as he poured the nectar (1.597–600; see [[unquenchable-laughter]]).

**The promise.** He takes her hand and greets her in Charis' words (see [[visit-scene]]), and adds: “Say what is in your mind; my heart bids me accomplish it, / if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished” (18.426–427), the promise that Aphrodite made to Hera in Book 14 (14.195–196) and Calypso to Hermes in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.89–90). When Thetis has told her sorrows and asked for armor (18.429–461; see [[thetis]]), he answers:

{{quote:a4519b03-5f64-50cb-8f9d-656910f65115}}

Leaf explains the form: the certainty of one event is affirmed by contrasting it with the impossibility of another, put first as a wish (on 18.464). The verb ἀποκρύψαι, “hide … away”, is the one he used of his mother's wish to hide him (κρύψαι, 18.397). In Book 19 Achilles will say that the arms are such as befit the works of immortals, and no mortal man could make (19.21–22).

**The forge.** “So saying he left her there, and went to his bellows; / and he turned them toward the fire and bade them work” (18.468–469). Leaf observes that the bellows, like the tripods, are intelligent automata, obeying the god's will (on 18.469). Twenty of them blow on the crucibles, χοάνοι (a word also in Hesiod, *Theogony* 863; Leaf on 18.470), sending out a blast now strong and now slack as the work requires (18.470–473; see the translation's note on 18.472). “And he cast into the fire unyielding bronze and tin / and precious gold and silver” (18.474–475; see [[bronze]], [[tin]] and [[gold-and-silver]]), set the great anvil on its block, and took the hammer in one hand and the tongs in the other (18.476–477; for the words of the forge see the translation's table of key words).

**The arms.** First he makes the shield, “great and heavy, / working it intricately all over”, with a threefold rim, a silver strap and five layers, “and on it / he made many intricate designs with his cunning skill” (18.478–482; see [[shield]] and the translation's note on 18.480). The description of the shield follows, scene by scene, from the earth, the sky and the sea to the river Oceanus around its rim (18.483–608; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]); the maker is named in it twice, “the renowned god, lame in both legs”, who makes the pasture and inlays the dancing floor like the one that Daedalus made for Ariadne in Cnossus (18.587, 18.590–592; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]], [[daedalus]], [[ariadne]] and [[cnossus]]). Then “he made him a corselet brighter than the light of fire, / and he made him a heavy helmet fitted to his temples, / fine and intricately worked, and set a golden crest on it, / and he made him greaves of soft tin” (18.610–613; see [[corselet]], [[helmet]] and [[greaves]]). “And when the famous god, lame in both legs, had made all the armor, / he lifted it and set it before the mother of Achilles” (18.614–615), and Thetis carries it down from Olympus, “the gleaming armor from Hephaestus” (18.617).
