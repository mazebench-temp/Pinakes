summary: Three lions in Book 18. Hector at the body of Patroclus is a tawny lion that shepherds cannot drive from a carcass (18.161–164); Achilles, lamenting him, is a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen (18.318–322); and on the Shield two lions seize a bellowing bull at the head of a herd, while the dogs dare not bite them (18.579–586).
# λέοντʼ (18.161) and λεόντων (18.585) occur only here in Homer.
grc: [λέοντʼ, λεόντων]
en: [lions]
---

**The hungry lion.** In the last fight for the body of Patroclus the lion is a Trojan. Hector will not let go of the body, and the two Ajaxes cannot frighten him away from it, “as shepherds of the fields cannot drive a tawny lion / away from a carcass at all, when he is very hungry” (18.161–162; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[hector]]). In Book 17 the defenders of the body were the lions (see the note on that book).

**The lion robbed of its cubs.** That night Achilles leads the lament over Patroclus, “groaning thick and fast, like a bearded lion / whose cubs a deer hunter has snatched away / out of the thick wood” (18.318–320). The lion grieves because it came back too late, and it follows the man's tracks through the valleys in bitter anger (18.320–322; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]] and [[achilles]]). λίς, the rarer word, and ἠϋγένειος, “bearded”, are the words of the lion that Menelaus was like when he gave ground in Book 17 (17.109; see [[bearded]]).

**The lions on the Shield.** Outside the similes, the lions of this book are a picture made by Hephaestus. A herd of straight-horned cattle goes out to pasture with four golden herdsmen and nine dogs, and “two terrible lions among the foremost cows / had seized a bellowing bull”; they tear open its hide and gulp down the entrails and the black blood, while the herdsmen set on their dogs in vain, and the dogs shrink back from biting the lions and stand close by, barking (18.573–586; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]). The lions do what the lions of the similes do: λαφύσσετον, “were gulping down”, is the verb of the lion that gulps down the blood and entrails of a cow in Books 11 and 17 (11.176, 17.64; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Here the herdsmen and their dogs cannot stop them, as Menelaus in Book 17 was a lion that dogs and herdsmen dared not come near (17.65–67).
