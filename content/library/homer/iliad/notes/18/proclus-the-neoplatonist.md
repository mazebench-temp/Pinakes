---
summary: Proclus defends Achilles from Book 18: Thetis' prophecy excuses the hair he gave Patroclus, and Iris' report of Hector's design on the body justifies the dragging. He reads Hephaestus' twenty tripods and the jewelry he forged for Thetis and Eurynome as the god's shaping of heaven and of the world below the moon, Hera's sending down of the sun as the mist of the air, and he quotes Orphic verses that set the earth, the sea and the crowned sky inside the aether in the words of the Shield.
---

- **Achilles' hair.** Against Plato's charge that Achilles gave Patroclus the hair he had vowed to the river Spercheius, Proclus answers that cutting it for the river on his return was his first choice; but once he had given up his return, persuaded by his mother's prophecy, “for straight after Hector your own fate is ready”, it was necessary to cut it in honor of his friend, and the hair had not yet become the river's, since it was promised only on his return (*On the Republic* I 149 Kroll, on 18.96; see [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]]).
- Hector's design on the body, known to Achilles from Iris, as the justification of the dragging of Hector (*On the Republic* I 150 Kroll, on 18.175–178): [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]].
- **Hephaestus the shaper.** In his essay on the bonds of Ares and Aphrodite, Proclus makes Hephaestus the maker of the whole visible order: he sets twenty tripods about the heaven, to adorn it with the most perfect of the many-sided figures, and fashions the varied forms below the moon, “brooches and curved spirals, rosettes and necklaces” (*On the Republic* I 141 Kroll, on 18.373 and 18.401). In the commentary on the *Timaeus* he asks again whether Hephaestus does not shape all things in the world, “making brooches and curved spirals, rosettes and necklaces” (*On the Timaeus* II 70 Diehl), and he finds the number nine Hephaestean, since the god says, “With them for nine years I forged many intricate designs” (*On the Timaeus* I 146 Diehl, on 18.400). See [[hephaestus]].
- **The sun sent down.** Hera is the air, and so Homer says that she spread a deep mist before the Trojans, and that ox-eyed queen Hera sent the tireless Sun to the streams of Oceanus: he calls the setting of the sun the thickest mist, made through Hera (*On the Cratylus* 170, on 18.239–240 and 21.6–7; see [[hera]] and [[helios]]).
- **The Orphic Zeus.** Proclus quotes the Orphic verses in which Night tells Zeus how all things may be one and each apart: “surround all things with ineffable aether, and in its midst set the sky, and in it the boundless earth, and in it the sea, and in it all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (*On the Timaeus* I 314 Diehl). The last words are the Shield's, ἐν δὲ τὰ τείρεα πάντα, τά τʼ οὐρανὸς ἐστεφάνωται, and the list follows its order, earth, sky and sea and then the constellations (18.483–485; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]).
