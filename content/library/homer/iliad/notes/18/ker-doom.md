**Accepting doom.** Achilles answers his mother's prophecy: “and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods. / For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom” (18.115–117; see [[achilles]], [[zeus]] and [[heracles]]). δέξομαι is “I will accept”, willingly (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.115), and he will speak the same verses, but for the first word, over the dying Hector (22.365–366). His mother had told him of two κῆρες, “two different fates”, carrying him toward the end of death (9.410–411; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]); now his fate is ready “straight after Hector” (18.96; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[thetis]]).

**Doom on the shield.** In the city at war on the shield of Achilles, “deadly Doom” is in the throng with Strife and Tumult (18.535; see [[strife]], [[tumult]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]), “holding one man alive and freshly wounded, another unwounded, / and another, dead, she was dragging through the fray by the feet; / and the cloak on her shoulders was blood-red with the blood of men” (18.536–538; see [[blood-red]]). ἄουτος, “unwounded”, is found only here (see the translation's table of key words). Leaf thinks that Doom at least was probably conceived as a winged demon, like the figure carrying off a soul in her arms on the Lycian sarcophagus in the British Museum, and that the Κῆρες, themselves ghosts of the departed, take souls to the underworld, as with “the fates of death” that Odysseus speaks of in Book 2 (2.302), and in his false tale in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 14.207–208; on 18.535). The four verses recur in the *Shield of Heracles* ascribed to Hesiod, which adds a fifth: she glares terribly and roars with a clashing din (*Shield* 156–160; see [[shield-of-heracles]]).
