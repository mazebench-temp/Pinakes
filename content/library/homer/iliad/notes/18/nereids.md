**The gathering.** Achilles groans aloud at the news of Patroclus' death, and his mother hears him “as she sat in the depths of the sea beside her aged father” (18.36), the verse with which she heard his prayer in Book 1 (1.358). She wails, and her sisters gather around her:

{{quote:cb4d6e3f-07ae-549c-b682-b853e3a1fc70}}

**The catalogue.** The poet names thirty-three of them in ten verses that join the names with τε and καί (18.39–48), and closes the list with a verse that repeats most of the one before it: “and the other Nereids that were in the depths of the sea” (18.49; compare 18.38). Only three names have an epithet: “ox-eyed Halia” (18.40), “famous Galateia” (18.45) and “Amatheia of the lovely tresses” (18.48; see [[halia]], [[galateia]], [[amatheia]], [[ox-eyed]] and [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Many of the names seem to speak of the sea: of the sea itself ([[halia]], [[limnoreia]]), its waves ([[cymodoce]], [[cymothoe]]), its islands, shores, caves and sands ([[nesaea]], [[actaea]], [[speio]], [[amatheia]]) and its color ([[glauce]]). Others speak of swiftness ([[thoe]], [[amphithoe]]), of giving and power ([[doto]], [[pherusa]], [[dynamene]], [[dexamene]]), of truthfulness ([[nemertes]], [[apseudes]]), and of fame, beauty and rank ([[agave-the-nereid|Agave]], [[clymene-the-nereid|Clymene]], [[callianeira]], [[callianassa]]).

Walter Leaf quotes Didymus' report that the catalogue had been athetized, already by Zenodotus, as Hesiodic in character, since Homer speaks of the Muses and the Eileithyiae as groups and gives them no names. He thinks the judgment right, the names being chosen, it seems, from the longer list in Hesiod (*Theogony* 243 ff.), and takes the repetition of 18.38 in 18.49 as a sign of interpolation (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.39; see [[zenodotus]], [[muses]] and [[eileithyia]]). Seventeen of the thirty-three names stand in Hesiod's list, and one verse is shared whole (18.43 = *Theogony* 248; see [[hesiod]]).

**The lament.**

{{quote:fbe5e8a4-034b-5b5b-a908-75b440382f69}}

The cave is Thetis' home in the sea, which she leaves at 18.65. The sisters beat their breasts in the words used of the captive women who ran out of doors around Achilles: χερσὶ δὲ πᾶσαι / στήθεα πεπλήγοντο (18.30–31), αἳ δʼ ἅμα πᾶσαι / στήθεα πεπλήγοντο (18.50–51). Thetis leads the lamentation, ἐξῆρχε γόοιο (18.51; see [[lament]]), and her speech is addressed to them: “Hear me, Nereids, my sisters” (18.52). She mourns a son who is still alive, whom she will not welcome home again (18.54–64; see [[thetis]]).

**To Troy and back.**

{{quote:b9bb07af-53f7-568b-bd52-42e26a834d80}}

The poem says nothing of them while Thetis speaks with her son (18.70–137). When she has promised to bring him armor from Hephaestus at dawn, she turns to “her sisters of the sea” (18.139) and sends them home:

{{quote:b519aadc-e63f-5ea0-840c-9d0aaad399b5}}

They are to “tell him everything” (18.142), and the poem leaves them there. Thetis goes on alone to Olympus (18.146–148).

**What Thetis left.** Earlier in the book Achilles wishes that his mother had stayed “among the immortal goddesses of the sea” and that Peleus had taken a mortal wife (18.86–87). Later Thetis tells Hephaestus that Zeus subdued her to a man “out of all the other goddesses of the sea” (18.432–434; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). The sisters who lament with her are the goddesses from whom her marriage set her apart.
