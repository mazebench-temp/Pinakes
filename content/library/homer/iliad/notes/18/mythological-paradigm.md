summary: Achilles' paradigm is a death: “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom, / who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus; / but fate brought him down, and the grievous anger of Hera” (18.117–119). The example is offered not to persuade another but to settle his own mind, and he draws the conclusion at once, “So I too” (18.120).
passages:
  - 18.115-121 | Not even Heracles escaped his doom
---

The usual paradigm in the Iliad is told by one man to move another, as Phoenix tells the story of Meleager to bring Achilles back to the fight (9.524–605). Here Achilles is speaker and listener at once, and he chooses the greatest of the sons of Zeus to show that no favor of the gods saves a man from death (see [[heracles]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf observes that the Iliad knows nothing of Heracles' apotheosis (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.117; see the translation's note on 18.117). Hephaestus' story of his fall and of the nine years with Thetis and Eurynome is told for a different end, to explain a debt (18.394–407; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]] and [[zoagria]]).
