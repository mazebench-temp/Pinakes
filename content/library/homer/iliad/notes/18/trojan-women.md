**Fame.**

{{quote:883d6c6f-54f8-5019-b7d3-00f9ff4633ff}}

Thetis has told Achilles that his own death will come straight after Hector's (18.95–96), and he answers that when he is dead he will lie still, but now he will win noble fame (18.120–121; see [[achilles]] and [[kleos]]). The fame is to be seen in the women of Troy, wiping the tears from their soft cheeks with both hands, who will learn from their grief how long he has been away from the war. They are Τρωϊάδες and Δαρδανίδες, “the Trojan and the deep-bosomed Dardanian women” (18.122; see [[dardanians]] and [[deep-bosomed]]).

**Our women.** Polydamas warns the Trojans that Achilles will not stay on the plain, “but he will fight for our city and our women” (18.265; see [[polydamas]]). The city and its women are the stake of the war, and Walter Leaf notes the same words in the Odyssey, where Odysseus asks the shade of Agamemnon whether he died fighting for a city and its women (περὶ πτόλιος … ἠδὲ γυναικῶν, *Odyssey* 11.403; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.265).

**The captives.** Over Patroclus' body Achilles promises him mourners:

{{quote:6cbe91dc-d38d-5274-9fb1-51783b81f2e7}}

They are women of the Troad whom Achilles and Patroclus won by the spear when they sacked its cities, like the slave women who cried aloud and beat their breasts when the news came (18.28–31; see [[captive-women]]). καμόμεσθα, “won by toil” (18.341): Walter Leaf notes that applied to slaves the word makes them mere chattels, and suggests that the captive women are set to do the work of professional mourners, as at Hector's bier (24.720), in a kind of triumphant mockery; but the author of Briseis' lament over Patroclus, a passage probably suggested by these lines, did not see it so (19.282–302; on 18.341). There the women answer Briseis “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302; see [[briseis]] and [[lament]]).
