summary: In Book 18 Hephaestus tells a second fall. He tells his wife Charis that when he had fallen far, by the will of his mother, who wished to hide him away because he was lame, Thetis and Eurynome took him in, and for nine years he forged jewelry for them in a cave by the stream of Oceanus; so now he must pay Thetis the full price for his life (18.394–407).

**Told to Charis.** Charis has called Hephaestus out to Thetis, and he answers her, not the guest: “Truly then a goddess I fear and respect is in our house” (18.394). The story of his fall is the reason for the welcome. It ends in the debt, “so I must indeed / pay Thetis of the lovely tresses the full price for my life” (18.406–407; see [[zoagria]]), and in an order to his wife to set guest-gifts before her (18.408; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]] and [[xenia]]). In Book 1 he told his fall to his mother, to warn her; here his mother is the cause of it, “my dog-faced mother” (18.396; see [[hera]] and [[dog-face]]).

**Hidden.** Hera wanted to hide him away because he was lame (κρύψαι χωλὸν ἐόντα, 18.397), and hidden he was, though not as she meant: Thetis and Eurynome kept him in a hollow cave around which the stream of Oceanus flowed, “and no one else / knew of it, neither of the gods nor of mortal men, / but Thetis and Eurynome knew, who had saved me” (18.403–405). For nine years he worked there, εἰνάετες, making them “brooches and curved spirals, rosettes and necklaces” (18.400–401). Walter Leaf takes the πόρπαι for brooches or buckles, the ἕλικες for spirals of some kind, perhaps bracelets, and the κάλυκες for bud-like ornaments, and notes that the line was copied in the *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* by a poet who may not have known its sense (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.401; see the translation's note on 18.401).

**Thetis' bosom.** “Had not Eurynome and Thetis taken me to their bosom” (18.398): Θέτις … ὑπεδέξατο κόλπῳ is said in Homer only of Hephaestus and of Dionysus, whom Thetis took to her bosom when Lycurgus drove him into the sea (6.135–137; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and [[thetis]]). Achilles, in Book 1, reminds his mother of another service of hers to the gods: she alone freed Zeus when the other Olympians wished to bind him (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]). Eurynome is “daughter of backward-flowing Oceanus” (18.399; see [[eurynome]] and [[backward-flowing]]). Leaf thinks that Payne Knight was right to expel the verse as a gloss, since it is very unusual to repeat the first of two names (on 18.399).

**Two falls.** In Book 1 Zeus hurled Hephaestus from heaven by the foot when he tried to defend his mother, and he fell all day and landed on Lemnos (1.590–594). Leaf observes that the two stories make the same use of Hephaestus with different details, and that elsewhere Hephaestus and his mother are generally close allies (on 18.395; see the translation's note on 18.395–405). The debt explains why Thetis comes to him now, why he bids her say what she wants before she has asked anything (18.426–427), and why he promises the armor at once (18.463–467; see [[hephaestus]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).
