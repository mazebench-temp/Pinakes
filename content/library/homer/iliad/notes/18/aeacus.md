summary: Aeacus is named through his son and his grandson. At the ditch the voice of Achilles is twice the voice of “the grandson of Aeacus”, and Thetis calls the mortal she was made to marry “Peleus, son of Aeacus” (18.221–222, 18.433).
# The dative Αἰακίδῃ (18.433) is Peleus, Aeacus' son, and is found only here in Homer; the genitive Αἰακίδαο
# (18.221, 18.222) is Achilles, as always.
grc: [Αἰακίδῃ]
---

**The grandson.** “so clear then rang out the voice of the grandson of Aeacus. / And when they heard the bronze voice of the grandson of Aeacus, / the hearts of all were shaken” (18.221–223; see [[grandson-of-aeacus]] and [[achilles]]). Αἰακίδαο ends both verses, the first closing the simile of the trumpet and the second opening its effect on the Trojans (see the translation's note on 18.222). It is the name the narrator gives Achilles when he shouts from the ditch, unarmed, with Athena's aegis on his shoulders (18.203–229; see [[athena]]).

**The son.** Αἰακίδῃ Πηλῆϊ, “Peleus, son of Aeacus” (18.433; see [[peleus]] and [[thetis]]), is Thetis' name for the man to whom Zeus subdued her against her will. The dative occurs only here; the nominative Αἰακίδης names Peleus at 16.15 and 21.189, where Achilles sets out his line over the body of Asteropaeus, a river's grandson: Zeus begot Aeacus, Aeacus Peleus, and Peleus him (21.187–189).

**Zeus' line.** When Achilles chooses his death he names Heracles, “who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (18.118), and adds, “So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me” (18.120; see [[heracles]]). Walter Leaf explains “a like fate” as “the same fate, as we are of the same divine origin”: Achilles, too, comes from Zeus through Aeacus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.120).
