**Another account.** Book 17 ended with Menelaus and Meriones carrying the body toward the ships while the two Ajaxes held off the Trojans (17.715–761). Book 18 takes the fight up again and tells it otherwise. The Achaeans flee to the ships and the Hellespont, and could not have drawn Patroclus out of range of the missiles, “for once more the army and the horses caught up with him, / and Hector, son of Priam, like a flame in his courage” (18.148–154; see [[like-a-flame]]). Walter Leaf took 18.153–165 for an account of the fight over the body that cannot be reconciled with the end of Book 17: nothing is said of the carrying, and the Achaeans drag the body, its feet within Hector's reach (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.151; see the translation's note on 18.151–165).

**Three times.**

{{quote:803a2b40-9352-5812-aaf8-8e12f3cfa749}}

The fight over the body ends as Patroclus' own last fight went: three times he climbed the angle of the wall, and three times Apollo drove him back (16.702–703); three times he charged and killed nine men, and at the fourth onset he met his end (16.784–787). The verb of the Ajaxes, ἀπεστυφέλιξαν, “drove him back” (18.158), is the verb of Apollo at the wall, and the Iliad has it only in these two places (16.703). Hector is like a hungry lion that shepherds cannot drive from a carcass (18.161–164; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]), and the next verse, “And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory” (18.165), is the one with which Menelaus would have dragged Paris away by his helmet in Book 3, had not Aphrodite broken the strap (3.373–375; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Iris.** Hera sends Iris to Achilles without the knowledge of Zeus (18.166–168, 18.184–186; see [[iris]] and [[hera]]). Leaf suspected the line on secrecy, since nothing more is said of it when Athena and Hera themselves intervene, and Zeus makes no complaint (on 18.168). Iris tells Achilles that the two sides are killing each other over the body, and that Hector is bent on dragging it away, “and his heart bids him fix the head / on the stakes, when he has cut it from the tender neck” (18.171–177). Her appeal is to shame: “let shame come into your heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (18.178–179), the second verse being Menelaus' in Book 17, when he called the Achaean leaders to the body (17.255; see [[sebas]] and [[dogs]]). Aristarchus objected that Hector meant to exchange the body for Sarpedon's, so that in Iris' mouth the threat to the head would be a pious fraud to rouse Achilles; Leaf answers that in Book 17 Hector had meant to cut off the head (17.125–127), and that the passage is of independent origin, so that the discrepancy need cause no surprise (on 18.154; see the translation's notes on 18.154–156 and 18.177).

**Without armor.** Achilles cannot go into the fight: the Trojans have his armor, his mother has forbidden him to arm until she returns, and he knows of no armor he could put on “unless it were the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon”, and Ajax is fighting over Patroclus himself (18.188–195; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). Iris tells him to go to the ditch as he is and show himself to the Trojans (18.197–201).

**The shout.** Athena throws the aegis around his shoulders, sets a golden cloud about his head as a crown, and kindles a flame from him (18.203–206; see [[aegis]], [[athena]] and the translation's note on 18.206). He stands at the ditch, apart from the Achaeans, as his mother commanded (18.215–216), and shouts, and Athena shouts with him; his voice rings out like a trumpet when enemies surround a city (18.217–221; see [[the-trumpet]]). Three times he shouts, three times the Trojans and their allies are thrown into confusion, and twelve of their best men die “among their own chariots and spears” (18.228–231). Leaf found the phrase hard, since there should be no chariots in the fighting line, and took it to mean that the men fell beside their own chariots and on their own spears (on 18.230).

**The body saved.** “Nor would the well-greaved Achaeans have drawn Patroclus / out of range of the missiles” (18.151–152): what they could not do before the shout, they do after it, and “gladly drew Patroclus out from under the missiles / and laid him on a bier” (18.231–233). Achilles follows, weeping, and the sun goes down (18.234–242; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).
