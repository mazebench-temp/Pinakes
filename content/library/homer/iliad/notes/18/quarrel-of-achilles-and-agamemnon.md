summary: In Book 18 Achilles renounces the passions of the quarrel. He wishes that strife and anger might perish from among gods and men, “so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now”, and lets the past be past (18.107–113). Thetis tells Hephaestus the quarrel's story, from the girl taken from Achilles to the death of Patroclus (18.444–456).

**The wish.** Achilles has chosen to kill Hector and die, and he turns on the passions that kept him from the war:

{{quote:4157716a-c44e-51cd-8547-47327ab6c258}}

ἔρις, “strife”, is the word for the quarrel from the proem on, which dates the poem from the time “when first the two of them stood apart in strife” (ἐρίσαντε, 1.6; see [[eris]]). χόλος, “anger”, is Achilles' own (see [[cholos]]). Walter Leaf reads the smoke as anger that fills men's hearts from a small beginning, as smoke from a very small fire fills a whole house (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.110). Then Achilles names the quarrel and puts it behind him: “so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now. / But we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief, / taming the dear heart in our breasts under compulsion” (18.111–113; see [[agamemnon]]). He began with the same words when he decided to send Patroclus out (16.60), and he will say the two verses again before the army in Book 19, when he renounces his anger in Agamemnon's presence (19.65–66; see the translation's table of formulas). Heraclitus blamed the poet for this wish, since without opposites there would be no harmony and no living things (see [[heraclitus-and-achilles-wish]]).

**From Troy.** Polydamas tells the Trojans what the quarrel meant to them: “As long as this man nursed his wrath against brilliant Agamemnon, / so long the Achaeans were easier to fight” (18.257–258; see [[polydamas]] and [[menis]]).

**Thetis' account.** To Hephaestus Thetis tells the quarrel and its consequences in thirteen verses: the girl whom the sons of the Achaeans picked out as Achilles' prize, taken back by Agamemnon; his grief for her; the Achaeans penned in at the sterns; the elders' entreaties and his refusal; Patroclus sent out in his armor and killed by Apollo (18.444–456; see [[taking-of-briseis]], [[geras]], [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). “Grieving for her, he was wasting his heart away” (18.446) is the withdrawal of Book 1, where he “wasted away his own heart” by his ships (φθινύθεσκε φίλον κῆρ, 1.491; see [[achilles-withdraws]]), and the Trojans penning the Achaeans in at the sterns (18.447) is the defeat he asked for through her in Book 1 (1.408–410), which she has reminded him that Zeus fulfilled (18.74–77). The quarrel itself ends in Book 19 (19.56–144).
