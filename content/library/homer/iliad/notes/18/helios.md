summary: The light of the sun is life itself in the mourning for Patroclus and for Achilles to come, and Thetis promises to return at dawn with the rising sun. In the evening Hera sends the tireless Sun, unwilling, down to the streams of Oceanus, and the third day of battle ends. On the shield of Achilles Hephaestus sets the tireless sun with the full moon.
# Ἠέλιον at 18.239 is printed with a capital: the god whom Hera sends down.
passages:

**The light of the sun.** To live is to look on the light of the sun. Achilles remembers what his mother once told him, that the best of the Myrmidons, while he still lived, “at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun” (18.10–11; see [[myrmidons]] and the translation's note on 18.10–11). She laments that “while he lives and looks on the light of the sun / he grieves, and I can do nothing to help him by going to him” (18.61–62 = 18.442–443; see [[thetis]]). The same words, ζώει καὶ ὁρᾷ φάος ἠελίοιο, are said of the absent Odysseus in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 4.833, 14.44, 20.207), and Pandarus boasted that Diomedes “shall not look much longer on the bright light of the sun” (5.118–120). Thetis promises to come back with the sun: “for at dawn I will return with the rising sun” (18.136; see [[eos]]).

**Sunset in a simile.** The blaze over Achilles' head is like the beacon fires of a besieged island: “and as the sun goes down / the beacon fires blaze in rows” (18.210–211). Walter Leaf finds the point of the simile in those words, when the smoke and the fire of the beacons are seen together, as the dark cloud and the fire over Achilles' head (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.207; see [[the-beacon-fires]] and the translation's note on 18.207–214).

**The unwilling Sun.** When the Achaeans have drawn the body of Patroclus out of the missiles, Hera ends the day:

{{quote:55847896-8abd-599d-82fc-4260cd5bd65d}}

The text prints Ἠέλιον with a capital: the Sun is the god who sees all things and hears all things, called on to witness the oaths of Book 3 (3.277). ἀκάμαντα, “tireless”, is said of the sun here and on the shield (18.239, 18.484; see [[tireless]]). He goes down ἀέκοντα, “unwilling”, because before his time; Leaf notes that the only other interference of a god with the course of nature in Homer is Athena's holding back of the dawn for Odysseus and Penelope (*Odyssey* 23.243–246; on 18.240; see the translation's note on 18.240). The day began with the dawn of Book 11 (11.1–2), and Leaf remarks that it is so overburdened with events through the growth of the poem that one can hardly think of this ending as premature without a smile (on 18.240; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[hera]]). On the first day of battle Agamemnon prayed that the sun might not go down before he had thrown down the hall of Priam (2.412–414); now a goddess sends it down early, and the Achaeans have their night. Zeus had promised Hector power to kill until the sun went down (11.192–194; see [[zeus]]).

**On the shield.** The first things that Hephaestus sets on the shield are the earth, the sky and the sea, “and the tireless sun, and the moon at the full, / and on it all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (18.484–485; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]], [[earth]], [[sky]] and [[sea]]).
