summary: As the Achaeans flee to the ships, Hector catches up with the body of Patroclus once more, “like a flame in his courage”, and three times seizes it by the feet (18.148–160). Then Achilles shows himself at the ditch with a flame on his head, and the body is drawn away.
passages:
  - 18.148-165 | Hector seizes the body again
---

αὖτις γὰρ δὴ τόν γε κίχον λαός τε καὶ ἵπποι / Ἕκτωρ τε Πριάμοιο πάϊς φλογὶ εἴκελος ἀλκήν: “for once more the army and the horses caught up with him, / and Hector, son of Priam, like a flame in his courage” (18.153–154). The half-verse is the one the Trojans used of Idomeneus, φλογὶ εἴκελον ἀλκήν (13.330), with ἀλκήν, “in his courage”, limiting the likeness; for Hector it is the second flame in the fight for Patroclus' body, after “like the flame of Hephaestus, / unquenchable” in Book 17 (17.88–89; see the note on that book). The flame is his courage, and the next verses show it: three times he seizes the body by the feet, three times the two Ajaxes drive him back, and “he did not draw back at all” (18.155–160; see [[hector]], [[alke]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). A simile of a hungry lion follows at once (18.161–164; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]).

Another fire answers his. Iris comes from Olympus to rouse Achilles (18.165–202), and Athena kindles a flame from his head, “gleaming all over”, whose blaze reaches the upper air and terrifies the Trojan charioteers (18.205–227; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). Hector's flame was a likeness; Achilles' is a fire that both armies see, and while the Trojans are thrown into confusion the Achaeans draw Patroclus out from under the missiles (18.228–233). The half-verse returns once more, when Hector, seeing his brother Polydorus killed, comes against Achilles “like a flame” (20.423).
