summary: The Shield's last picture of men is a dance on a dancing floor like the one Daedalus made for Ariadne at Cnossus: young men and maidens hold one another by the wrist, now running in a circle as smoothly as a potter's wheel, now in ranks toward one another, while a great throng watches and two tumblers lead the song and dance (18.590–606). Young men dance at the weddings of the city at peace (18.494), and at the vintage the grape-carriers follow the boy with the lyre, skipping with their feet (18.572).

**The dancers.** The girls wear fine linen and garlands, the young men tunics that shine softly with oil and golden knives on silver straps (18.595–598; see [[tunic]] and [[knife]]); the maidens are ἀλφεσίβοιαι, worth many oxen to a suitor (18.593; see [[bringing-in-oxen]]). Walter Leaf compares the dance called the “necklace” described by Lucian, in which young men and girls dance in a line, the young man leading with steps he will later use in war (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.593), and takes it that the dancers leave the circle from time to time to dance in lines facing one another, ἐπὶ στίχας, a phrase used elsewhere only of soldiers (on 18.601; see [[battle-order]]).

**Cnossus.** The dance is Cretan in character, and the scholia call the tumblers and the armed dance Cretan; some have concluded that the passage was added by a Cretan poet, which Leaf cannot refute but finds unproved (on 18.590; see the translation's note on 18.590–606). Whether Hephaestus copied a mortal's work troubled some ancient critics, who read οἶον, “the only thing ever made like it” (Leaf on 18.591). See [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]], [[daedalus]], [[ariadne]] and [[cnossus]].

**The leaders.** The printed text keeps the singer at 18.604–605 and makes the two tumblers lead the song and dance, μολπῆς ἐξάρχοντες (18.606; see [[lyre]], [[the-singer-on-the-shield]] and the translation's note on 18.604–606). κυβιστητήρ, “tumbler”, is the word of Patroclus' mockery when Cebriones dives from his chariot: there are tumblers among the Trojans too (16.745–750; see [[cebriones]]). See [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[the-linus-song]].
