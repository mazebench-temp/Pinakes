summary: Achilles wishes that anger might perish with strife from among gods and men: anger “which sets even a man of much wisdom to rage, / and which, far sweeter than dripping honey, / swells in the breasts of men like smoke” (18.108–110), the anger Agamemnon roused in him (18.111). Heracles fell by fate and Hera's anger (18.119); the lion that has lost its cubs is seized by bitter anger (18.322); and Achilles turns his own anger on the Trojans, vowing to kill twelve of their children “in my anger over your killing” (18.337).
grc: [ἐχόλωσεν]
---

The wish renounces the anger of Book 1, and the verses that follow let the past be past (18.112–113 = 19.65–66; see [[eris]] and [[menis]]). Aristotle quotes the honey verses twice in the *Rhetoric* for the pleasure that goes with anger (*Rhetoric* 1370b12, 1378b6; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[aristotle-anger]]), and Walter Leaf reports that Aristotle also tells how Heraclitus blamed Homer for the wish that strife might perish (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.107; see the translation's note on 18.107). The anger does not end; it changes its object (18.337; see [[human-sacrifice]]).
