summary: Athena throws the aegis around Achilles' shoulders, crowns his head with a golden cloud and kindles a flame from him, and cries out with him from the ditch, so that the Trojans are thrown into confusion. In the Trojan assembly she takes away their wits, and they applaud Hector's bad counsel. On the shield of Achilles she leads the sortie from the besieged city with Ares, both of gold and taller than men.

**The aegis and the golden cloud.** When Iris has gone, Achilles rises, and Athena makes him terrible to see:

{{quote:0de772a7-c95e-5511-b8db-2748ef82afa7}}

βάλʼ αἰγίδα θυσσανόεσσαν, “flung the tasseled aegis”, are the words with which she slung the aegis around her own shoulders when she armed against Ares (5.738; see [[aegis]] and [[tasseled]]); Walter Leaf refers to the hundred golden tassels of the aegis that she carried among the Achaeans in Book 2 (2.447–449; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.204). δῖα θεάων, “brilliant among goddesses”, ends the verse (18.205; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]). The cloud is golden, like the clouds under which Ares sits on Olympus, held back from the war (13.523–525), and the cloud that Zeus draws over himself and Hera on Gargarus (14.350–351; see [[the-golden-cloud]]). ἐκ δʼ αὐτοῦ, “from him”, is from the man's own body, as she kindled fire from the head and shoulders of Diomedes at the start of Book 5 (5.4–7; Leaf on 18.206; see the translation's note on 18.206 and [[diomedes]]). The blaze reaches the upper air like the smoke and fire of beacons from a besieged island at sunset (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]).

**Her cry.** At the ditch Achilles stands apart from the Achaeans, as his mother bade him: “There he stood and shouted, and apart from him Pallas Athena / cried out” (18.217–218; see [[pallas]]). Leaf is not sure whether Achilles or Athena roused the untold tumult among the Trojans, “probably the former” (on 18.218; see the translation's note on 18.218). The charioteers are struck with terror “when they saw the tireless fire / blazing terribly above the head of the great-hearted son of Peleus; / the gray-eyed goddess Athena kindled it” (18.225–227; see [[gray-eyed]] and [[tireless]]), and twelve of the Trojans' best men die among their own chariots and spears (18.230–231). Leaf notes that nothing is said of secrecy when Athena intervenes (on 18.168; see [[zeus]]). In Book 20, when the gods go down to battle, she will shout again, standing now by the ditch outside the wall and now on the shore, while Ares shouts on the other side (20.48–53; see [[ares]]).

**The Trojans' wits.** When Hector has rejected Polydamas' counsel, “the Trojans shouted in applause, / fools; for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits” (18.310–311; see [[nepios]], [[phrenes]] and [[polydamas]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad it is Zeus who takes away a man's wits: Glaucus', when he exchanged gold armor for bronze (6.234), Agamemnon's, as Achilles says and as Agamemnon will say himself (9.377, 19.137; Leaf on 18.311). “For they approved of Hector, who devised evil, / but no one approved of Polydamas, who had devised good counsel” (18.312–313; see [[hector]]).

**On the shield.** In the city at war on the shield of Achilles, the defenders go out to lie in ambush, “and Ares led them and Pallas Athena, / both of gold, and clad in golden clothing, / fine and tall in their armor, as gods should be, / plain for all to see from every side; and the people were smaller” (18.516–519; see [[ares]], [[gold-and-silver]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Leaf calls the two gods the only distinctively Hellenic touch in the shield (on 18.516), and reads ὑπʼ ὀλίζονες, “smaller at their feet” (on 18.519; see the translation's note on 18.519). When the armies first met, the two gods roused opposite sides: “Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those” (4.439). Here they lead the same men.
