summary: The book is the first lament for Patroclus. At the news Achilles fouls his head and face with dust and ash and tears his hair, and the slave women cry aloud and beat their breasts (18.22–34); in the sea Thetis wails and leads the Nereids' lament for her son, who is still alive (18.35–64); and at night Achilles leads the Myrmidons' lament over the washed body (18.314–355). Between them he imagines the Trojan women groaning for their dead, and promises Patroclus that captive women will weep for him night and day (18.122–124, 18.339–342).

**The news.** The gestures of mourning come before the body. Achilles pours the sooty dust over his head, the ash settles on his tunic, and he lies stretched in the dust tearing his hair (18.23–27; see [[great-over-a-great-space]] and [[hair]]). The slave women whom he and Patroclus had taken “cried aloud, and ran out of doors / around wise-hearted Achilles, and with their hands all of them / beat their breasts” (18.29–31; see [[captive-women]]), and Antilochus “mourned, letting tears fall, / holding the hands of Achilles” (18.32–33). His groan is heard in the depths of the sea (18.35).

**The Nereids.** Thetis wails, the Nereids gather round her in the silver-white cave and beat their breasts, “and Thetis led the lamentation” (18.37–51; see [[nereids]]). Her lament is for a son who is alive: “Ah me, wretched that I am, ah me, unhappy mother of the best of men” (18.54); she reared him like a plant on the slope of an orchard, “but I shall not welcome him again / returning home to the house of Peleus” (18.59–60; see [[nostos]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). She says the same verses again to Hephaestus (18.437–441). The Odyssey remembers the Nereids wailing again at Achilles' own funeral (*Odyssey* 24.47–59).

**The night.** When the sun has set, “the Achaeans / all night long groaned over Patroclus, lamenting. / And among them the son of Peleus led the unceasing lamentation, / laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion” (18.314–317). The verse that sets Achilles at the head of the lament returns at the funeral (18.316 = 23.17). He speaks to the dead man, as a mourner does: “But now, Patroclus, since I shall go under the earth after you” (18.333), and vows him a funeral with Hector's head and armor and twelve Trojan youths (18.334–337; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). Then the body is washed and laid on the bier, and the Myrmidons groan over him all night (18.343–355; see [[bathing]]).

**Laments to come.** Achilles wants fame, and he imagines it as the lament of the enemy's women: some Trojan or Dardanian woman wiping the tears from her cheeks with both hands, groaning “without ceasing” (18.121–124; see [[kleos]]). For Patroclus he promises the captive women, the Trojan and Dardanian women won by the spear, who “shall weep night and day, shedding tears” around his body (18.339–342). The women's lament for Patroclus is told in the next book, led by Briseis (19.282–302).
