---
summary: The Achaeans are driven in confusion to the ships and the Hellespont, and cannot draw the body of Patroclus clear until Achilles' shout from the ditch lets them; at sunset they cease from the war, and all night they mourn Patroclus. Polydamas tells the Trojans that they were easier to fight while Achilles kept his wrath, and Zeus taunts Hera that the Achaeans must be her own children (18.6–7, 18.148–152, 18.231–242, 18.257–258, 18.314–315, 18.356–359).
---

**Driven to the ships.** Achilles sees “the long-haired Achaeans / driven in confusion toward the ships, in terror across the plain” (18.6–7; see [[long-haired]] and [[achilles]]), and Thetis tells him that this is what he prayed for, “that all the sons of the Achaeans should be penned in among the sterns / for want of you, and suffer unseemly deeds” (18.76–77; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and the translation's note on 18.77). He calls himself such as “none of the bronze-armored Achaeans / in war” (18.105–106; see [[bronze-armored]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). The narrator returns to the plain: “the Achaeans, / with a wondrous shout, before man-slaying Hector, / in their flight reached the ships and the Hellespont” (18.148–150; see [[hellespont]]), and “Nor would the well-greaved Achaeans have drawn Patroclus / out of range of the missiles” (18.151–152; see [[well-greaved]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]), had not Iris come to Achilles. She sends him to the ditch so that “the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath, / worn out as they are” (18.200–201; see [[warlike]] and [[nestor]]). He stands there apart: “but with the Achaeans / he did not mingle; for he heeded his mother's shrewd command” (18.215–216; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The body saved.** After his shout “the Achaeans / gladly drew Patroclus out from under the missiles / and laid him on a bier” (18.231–233; see [[patroclus]]). At sunset “the brilliant Achaeans ceased / from the fierce din of battle and from war, common to all” (18.241–242; see [[brilliant]], [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and the translation's note on 4.315, 4.444, 9.440).

**Seen from Troy.** Polydamas tells the Trojans that while Achilles kept his wrath against Agamemnon “so long the Achaeans were easier to fight” (18.257–258; see [[polydamas]]). Hector boasts that Zeus has granted him “to win glory at the ships, and to pen the Achaeans against the sea” (18.294; see [[hector]]), and tells the Trojans to give their goods to the people rather than leave them to the Achaeans (18.300–302; see the translation's note on 18.300–302).

**The mourning.** “but the Achaeans / all night long groaned over Patroclus, lamenting” (18.314–315; see [[lament]]). The scene ends with the same words of the Myrmidons around Achilles (18.354–355; see [[myrmidons]]).

**Hera's children.** Zeus, seeing Achilles roused, taunts Hera: “truly then, from your own self / the long-haired Achaeans must have been born” (18.358–359; see [[hera]] and [[zeus]]). She answers that the best of goddesses, his own wife, might surely work evil for the Trojans she resents (18.360–367).

**In Thetis' story.** The girl was the Achaeans' gift to Achilles, “The girl whom the sons of the Achaeans picked out for him as his prize” (18.444), and when he withdrew “the Achaeans / the Trojans were penning in among the sterns, and would not let them / come out of doors” (18.446–448; see [[thetis]] and [[geras]]).
