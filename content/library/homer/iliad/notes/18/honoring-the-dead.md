summary: Patroclus' body is saved, washed and laid out, but his funeral is put off. Iris rouses Achilles with the threat that Hector will cut off the head and leave the body to the Trojan dogs (18.175–180); that night Achilles vows to give Patroclus no burial rites until he has brought back Hector's armor and head, and to kill twelve Trojans at his pyre (18.333–337), and has the body washed, anointed and covered on a bier (18.343–353).

**The threat.** Iris tells Achilles that Hector is bent on dragging the body away, “and his heart bids him fix the head / on the stakes, when he has cut it from the tender neck” (18.176–177): “let shame come into your heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs; / yours will be the disgrace, if the corpse comes back disfigured at all” (18.178–180; see [[sebas]], [[lobe]] and [[dogs]]). The “plaything for dogs” is the curse Poseidon laid on any man who held back from fighting, in his words to Idomeneus, and the shame Menelaus held up to the Achaean leaders over this same body (13.232–234, 17.255). Aristarchus objected that Hector meant to trade the body for Sarpedon's (17.160–163), not to mutilate it; Walter Leaf thinks the passage of independent origin (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.154; see the translation's notes on 18.154–156 and 18.177).

**The vow.** Achilles answers the threat with one of his own. He will not give Patroclus his rites, κτεριῶ (18.334), the verb Odysseus used of his own burial, “the brilliant Achaeans will give me burial rites” (11.455), until he has brought Hector's armor and head, and before the pyre he will kill twelve Trojans (18.334–337; see [[human-sacrifice]], [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[pyres]]). Until then the body is to lie by the ships, mourned by captive women (18.338–342; see [[lament]]). The book that began with the fear of a body given to the dogs ends with a body kept unburied.

**The washing.** The companions heat water in a tripod, wash the clotted blood away, anoint him with oil and fill his wounds with ointment, “and laying him on a bier they covered him with a soft linen cloth / from head to foot, and over it with a white cloak” (18.343–353; see [[bathing]], [[tripod]], [[bed]], [[heanos]] and [[cloak]]). Thetis will keep the body from corruption with ambrosia and nectar in the next book (19.23–39).
