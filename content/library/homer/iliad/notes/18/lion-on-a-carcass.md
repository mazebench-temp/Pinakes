{{quote:7d2d2e65-977d-590f-a41f-310364152bdf}}

ὡς δʼ ἀπὸ σώματος οὔ τι λέοντʼ αἴθωνα δύνανται / ποιμένες ἄγραυλοι μέγα πεινάοντα δίεσθαι: “And as shepherds of the fields cannot drive a tawny lion / away from a carcass at all, when he is very hungry” (18.161–162). The lion is αἴθων, “tawny”, the color of fire, like the lion driven from the cattle yard in Book 11 (11.548) and the lion skins that Agamemnon and Diomedes put on for the night raid (10.23–24, 10.177–178; see [[tawny]]). The men are ποιμένες ἄγραυλοι, “shepherds of the fields”, herdsmen who live out in the open with their flocks (see [[of-the-fields]]). δίεσθαι is “drive away”. As in Book 3, hunger is the lion's whole reason: there he was πεινάων, “when he is hungry” (3.25), and here μέγα πεινάοντα, “very hungry”. The σῶμα is a carcass. Walter Leaf, comparing the earlier simile, notes the same emphasis on hunger here, but sees no reason why σῶμα may not mean the body of an animal the lion has killed himself (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.161); in Book 3 he took it for a quarry just killed by a hunting party (on 3.23).

The tenor: ὥς ῥα τὸν οὐκ ἐδύναντο δύω Αἴαντε κορυστὰ / Ἕκτορα Πριαμίδην ἀπὸ νεκροῦ δειδίξασθαι (18.163–164). δύνανται in the simile answers ἐδύναντο in the tenor, “cannot”, “could not”, and the shepherds' δίεσθαι, “drive away”, becomes δειδίξασθαι, “frighten away”: what the Ajaxes fail to do is to make Hector afraid. The carcass is the body of Patroclus, νεκρός, and Hector, who has three times seized it by the feet and been three times driven off, will not leave it (18.155–160; see [[hector]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]).

The roles have turned since Book 17. There the defenders of the body were the lions, Menelaus standing over it as a lion over the cow it has killed, Ajax as a lion over its young (17.61–69, 17.132–137; see [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]). Here the Trojan is the lion and the Ajaxes are the herdsmen who cannot drive him off; in Book 13 the same two, δύω Αἴαντε κορυστά, were themselves a pair of lions snatching a goat from the dogs (13.197–202; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]]). The hungry lion would have won: Hector “would have dragged him off and won untold glory”, had not Iris come to rouse Achilles (18.165–168; see [[would-have-had-not]]).
