---
summary: Achilles vows that before Patroclus' pyre he will cut the throats of twelve splendid children of the Trojans, “in my anger over your killing” (18.336–337). The vow is repeated at the funeral, and kept: he takes twelve young men alive from the river and kills them at the pyre (23.22–23; 21.26–28, 23.175–176).
grc: [ἀποδειροτομήσω]
en: [cut the throats]
---

The killing is promised to the dead man and to Achilles' anger, not to a god: the twelve are taken “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (ποινὴν Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο θανόντος, 21.28; see [[poine]] and [[cholos]]). The two verses of the vow return word for word when Achilles speaks to the dead man at the start of the funeral (18.336–337 = 23.22–23), and Walter Leaf leaves open whether they belong to both places or were put here to prepare for Book 23 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.336; see the translation's note on 18.336–342). When the deed is done the narrator says of him: κακὰ δὲ φρεσὶ μήδετο ἔργα, “he devised evil deeds in his mind” (23.176), the half-verse said of him as he leaps into the river (21.19); Leaf denies that the words pass a moral judgment (on 23.176; see the note on Book 21). ἀποδειροτομέω, “cut the throat”, is the word of sacrifice: in the Odyssey Odysseus cuts the throats of the sheep over the pit for the dead (*Odyssey* 11.35; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[sacrifice]]).
