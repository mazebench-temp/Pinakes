summary: Zeus' will is what has been fulfilled and what is to come. Thetis tells Achilles that Zeus has done what he prayed for, Achilles will accept his death whenever Zeus is willing, and not even Heracles, dearest to Zeus, escaped his doom. Hera sends Iris to Achilles in secret from Zeus, and Hector boasts of the glory that the son of Cronus has granted him. At nightfall Zeus taunts Hera for rousing Achilles, and she answers him. Thetis tells Hephaestus that Zeus gave her, alone of the goddesses of the sea, to a mortal man.

**Fulfilled by Zeus.** Thetis' first words to her weeping son are that “These things have been fulfilled for you / by Zeus, as you prayed before, lifting up your hands” (18.74–75), and he answers: “My mother, these things the Olympian has indeed fulfilled for me; / but what pleasure have I in them, since my dear companion has perished” (18.79–80; see [[thetis]], [[olympian]] and [[achilles]]). What Zeus fulfilled is the request that Thetis carried to him in Book 1, that the Achaeans be penned in among their ships' sterns and along the sea as they were slaughtered (1.407–412, 1.493–530; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]), the plan of the proem: “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Whenever Zeus is willing.** Achilles accepts his own death: “and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (18.115–116), the verses, but for the first word, with which he will answer the dying Hector (22.365–366; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.115; see [[ker-doom]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). His example is Heracles, “who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus; / but fate brought him down, and the grievous anger of Hera” (18.118–119; see [[heracles]], [[moira]] and [[hera]]). Διὶ Κρονίωνι ἄνακτι, “to lord Zeus, son of Cronus”, ends the verses of the gift of Agamemnon's scepter and of the Achaeans' prayer before the duel of Ajax (2.102, 7.194, 7.200; see [[son-of-cronus]]). In Book 19 Agamemnon will tell how Hera deceived Zeus himself on the day Heracles was to be born (19.95–133). Over Patroclus, Achilles says it again, of his promise to Menoetius to bring his son home: “But Zeus does not fulfill for men all their plans” (18.328; see [[zeus-does-not-fulfill-all-plans]] and [[menoetius]]).

**In secret from Zeus.** Hera sends Iris to Achilles “in secret from Zeus and the other gods” (18.168), and Iris tells him that “the son of Cronus, throned on high, does not know” (18.185; see [[iris]] and [[high-throned]]). Walter Leaf suspects 18.168: it seems to assume that Zeus is still on Olympus with the other gods rather than on Ida, nothing is said of secrecy when Athena and Hera intervene in person (18.203, 18.239), and Zeus does not complain that he has been deceived, even if his taunt to Hera is original, for his purpose has been fully attained in the defeat of the Achaeans (on 18.168; see the translation's note on 18.168). Zeus woke on the peaks of Ida in Book 15 (15.4–6) and shook the aegis there in Book 17 (17.593–596; see [[ida]]). When Achilles rises to go to the ditch, he is “dear to Zeus” (18.203; see [[dear-to-zeus]]).

**Glory from Zeus.** In the Trojan assembly Hector answers Polydamas that Troy's treasures have gone to Phrygia and Maeonia, “possessions sold away, since great Zeus grew angry” (18.292; see the translation's note on 18.288–292), but that now “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has granted me / to win glory at the ships, and to pen the Achaeans against the sea” (18.293–294; see [[crooked-counseling]], [[kydos]] and [[hector]]). Zeus had promised him power “to kill, until he comes to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194; again of the Trojans, 17.454–455), and the sun has gone down (18.239–241; see [[helios]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). The Trojans applaud Hector, “fools; for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits” (18.311; see [[athena]]): elsewhere it is Zeus who takes away a man's wits (6.234, 9.377, 19.137).

**Zeus and Hera.** That night, while the Achaeans mourn Patroclus and Thetis goes to Hephaestus, Zeus speaks to his wife:

{{quote:c6fe33a5-4e6d-512a-870f-ce2e3fb0cfd8}}

The first verse is the verse of Book 16, where he asked her whether to save Sarpedon (18.356 ≈ 16.432; see the note on Book 16 and [[kasignetos]]). ἔπρηξας, “you have had your way”, is used only here without an object, and Leaf takes καί as “once more”, an allusion to Hera's earlier offenses (on 18.357; see the translation's note on 18.357). She answers with the verse with which she has answered him since Book 1, “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” (18.361; see [[most-dread]]), and with the claim that she made in Book 4, to be the best of goddesses “on both counts, by birth and because I am called / your wife, and you are lord among all the immortals” (18.364–366 = 4.60–61; see [[hera]]). Zeus does not reply. For their earlier quarrels, see [[quarrel-on-olympus]].

**Thetis' marriage.** Thetis tells Hephaestus that no goddess on Olympus has endured “as the sorrows Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me beyond all of them”: “Out of all the other goddesses of the sea he subdued me to a man” (18.431–432), and “he gave me a son to bear and to rear” (18.436; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]] and [[peleus]]). In Book 24 Hera will say that she herself reared Thetis and gave her to Peleus (24.59–61).
