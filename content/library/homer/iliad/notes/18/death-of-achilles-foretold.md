---
summary: Book 18 makes Achilles' death certain and near. When he resolves to kill Hector, Thetis tells him that his own fate is ready straight after Hector's, and he accepts it at once (18.95–98). He will take his doom whenever Zeus wills it, as Heracles took his, and win noble fame first (18.114–121); he tells the Myrmidons that he and Patroclus are fated to redden the same earth at Troy (18.329–332); and Hephaestus wishes he could hide him from death (18.464–465).

  - 18.94-126 | Thetis' prophecy and Achilles' choice

**The term.** Achilles tells his mother that he will not live unless Hector dies first under his spear (18.90–93), and she answers:

{{quote:7ad41646-13da-5a69-a669-c3b03071bd02}}

ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, is the word with which she lamented his short life in Book 1 (1.417; see [[swift-fated]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). There she gave no term; now she gives one, μεθʼ Ἕκτορα, “after Hector”. οἷʼ ἀγορεύεις, “from what you say”, makes his death follow from his own words: Walter Leaf calls the phrase “our vernacular ‘from what you say’”, found elsewhere only in the Odyssey (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.95).

**The choice.** Achilles takes up her αὐτίκα, “at once”, in his first words (Leaf on 18.98):

{{quote:391a8560-ab46-5296-a16d-b156eb0794f5}}

In Book 9 he had a choice between two fates, a long life without fame at home or imperishable fame at Troy without a homecoming, and he chose the long life (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Now he chooses the other. He will accept his doom “whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (18.115–116), and will say the same two verses over the dead Hector (22.365–366; Leaf on 18.115). His example is Heracles, whom fate and the anger of Hera brought down (18.117–119; see [[heracles]]). “But now may I win noble fame” (18.121): κλέος ἐσθλόν is the fame he would have lost by going home (9.415; see [[kleos]]). Thetis does not argue. She grants that it is no bad thing to ward off destruction from his companions, and goes for armor (18.128–137; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

**The same earth.** Over Patroclus' body Achilles tells the Myrmidons: “for it is fated that both of us redden the same earth / here in Troy, since I too shall not come home / to be welcomed in his halls by the old horse-driving Peleus, / nor by my mother Thetis, but the earth will hold me here” (18.329–332). The indicative πέπρωται, “it is fated”, does not recur in Homer (Leaf on 18.329). He speaks of his death as near: “since I shall go under the earth after you” (18.333; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**No homecoming.** Thetis laments that she will not welcome him “returning home to the house of Peleus” (18.59–60 = 18.440–441), and Achilles tells her the same of himself (18.89–90; see [[nostos]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). Asking Hephaestus for armor, she calls him again “my swift-fated son” (18.458), and Hephaestus can promise the armor but not his life: “If only I could hide him as surely from grim-sounding death, / away and apart, when dread fate comes upon him” (18.464–465; see [[hephaestus]]).

**Plato.** Leaf notes that Achilles' answer was much quoted in antiquity, notably by Plato (on 18.98). Socrates, before his judges, cites Thetis' warning and Achilles' “May I die at once” as the example of a man who feared disgrace more than death (*Apology* 28c–d; see [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]]).
