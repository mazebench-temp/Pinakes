ἣ δʼ ἴρηξ ὣς ἆλτο κατʼ Οὐλύμπου νιφόεντος / τεύχεα μαρμαίροντα παρʼ Ἡφαίστοιο φέρουσα: “And she like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus, / carrying the gleaming armor from Hephaestus” (18.616–617). The likeness is two words, ἴρηξ ὥς (see [[hawk]]), with ὥς after its noun, as in ἀστὴρ ὥς, “like a star”, of Achilles' helmet in the next book (19.381). Nothing is said of the hawk's quarry; the point is speed, the speed of the bird that the simile of Apollo's descent called “the swiftest of winged birds” (15.238), on the long way down from Olympus to the ships. ἆλτο, “leapt”, is the verb of warriors who leap from their chariots to the ground in their armor (σὺν τεύχεσιν, 3.29 and often), and it was said of Thetis once before: when Zeus had nodded his assent in Book 1, she “leapt down into the deep sea from gleaming Olympus” (1.532; see [[thetis]]). Now she leaps from snowy Olympus with the armor in her arms (see [[niphoeis]]).

The armor was made in one night. Thetis had told her son not to arm until she came back, “for at dawn I will return with the rising sun, / bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.134–137), and when Hephaestus has finished the shield, corselet, helmet and greaves he sets them before her (18.609–615; see [[hephaestus]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The hawk takes her the rest of the way, and the next book begins with Dawn rising from the streams of Oceanus and Thetis reaching the ships with the god's gifts (19.1–3).

Gods go down like birds of prey elsewhere in these books: Poseidon leaves the Ajaxes like a hawk rising from a rock (13.62–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]), Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, the dove-killer (15.237–238), and Athena will dart down from the sky like a long-winged bird of prey (19.350–351).
