summary: Fate in the book is Achilles' and Heracles'. Thetis calls her son “swift-fated” (ὠκύμορος, 18.95, 18.458), and he answers that not even Heracles escaped his doom: “fate brought him down”, and he will lie still if a like fate is prepared for him (18.117–121). He tells the dead Patroclus that “it is fated that both of us redden the same earth / here in Troy” (18.329–330), and Hephaestus speaks of the day “when dread fate comes upon him” (18.465). On the Shield the Bear alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus (ἄμμορος, 18.489).
grc: [ὠκυμόρῳ, μόρος, ἄμμορός, πέπρωται, πότμος]
en: [no share, it is fated]
---

πέπρωται, “it is fated” (18.329), is the perfect of a verb of giving, the share allotted; the participle names the end that is fated in the duel of Book 3, “for which of the two the end of death is fated” (3.309), and the mortal man “long since ordained to his portion” whom Hera will not let Zeus save (16.441). ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, is the word Thetis used of her son in Book 1 (1.417; see [[swift-fated]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).
