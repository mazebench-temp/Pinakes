# The plural Αἴαντες (18.157) and the dual Αἴαντε (18.163) are claimed by ajax; the pair takes the lines by refs.

  - 18.155-165 | The two Ajaxes and Hector over the body

{{quote:803a2b40-9352-5812-aaf8-8e12f3cfa749}}

θοῦριν ἐπιειμένοι ἀλκήν, “clothed in furious courage” (18.157; see [[clothed-in-courage]] and [[furious]]), is the half-verse that brought them to their feet for Hector's challenge and out behind Diomedes (7.164 = 8.262). δύω Αἴαντε κορυστά, “the two Ajaxes, helmeted men” (18.163; see [[helmeted]]), closes a lion simile here and in Book 13. There the Ajaxes were the lions, holding the body of Imbrius high “as two lions snatch a goat away from sharp-toothed dogs” (13.198–202; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]] and [[imbrius]]); here Hector is the hungry lion, and they are the shepherds who cannot drive him from the carcass (18.161–164; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[hector]]). Hector would have dragged the body off, had not Iris come to rouse Achilles (18.165–168; see [[would-have-had-not]]), and only Achilles' shout from the ditch lets the Achaeans draw it out of the missiles (18.228–233; see [[achilles]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]).

Walter Leaf found this account of the fight hard to reconcile with the end of Book 17, where Menelaus and Meriones carry the body while the two Ajaxes hold back the Trojans behind them; here the Achaeans are dragging it, and Hector can still seize it by the feet (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.151; 17.715–761; see the translation's note on 18.151–165).
