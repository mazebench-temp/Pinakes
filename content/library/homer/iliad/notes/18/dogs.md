---
summary: In Book 18 the Trojan dogs are still the threat to the body of Patroclus, and Polydamas warns that dogs will eat the Trojans; Hephaestus calls his mother “dog-faced”; and on the Shield nine herd dogs follow the cattle to pasture but will not face the lions (18.179, 18.271, 18.283, 18.396, 18.578–586).
---

**The threat.** Iris rouses Achilles with a verse that Menelaus spoke to the Achaean leaders in Book 17: “let shame come into your heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (18.178–179; the second verse is 17.255; see [[iris]] and [[sebas]]). That night, in the Trojan assembly, Polydamas turns it on the Trojans themselves. If Achilles finds them on the plain in the morning, “glad will he be to reach sacred Ilios, / whoever escapes, and dogs and vultures will eat many / of the Trojans; may that be far from my hearing!” (18.270–272; see [[polydamas]] and [[vultures]]). If they stay behind their walls, Achilles will never sack the city: “before that the swift dogs will eat him” (18.283; see [[swift-dogs]]). In one speech the dogs are promised first to the Trojans and then to their enemy.

**Dog-faced.** Hephaestus, welcoming Thetis, remembers that she saved him when he had fallen “by the will of my dog-faced mother”, Hera, who wished to hide him away because he was lame (18.395–397; see [[dog-face]], [[hera]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). It is the word of reproach for shamelessness that Achilles threw at Agamemnon (1.159) and that Helen uses of herself (3.180); in the Odyssey Hephaestus uses it again, of his faithless wife (*Odyssey* 8.319).

**The herd dogs.** On the Shield nine dogs follow the four golden herdsmen and the cattle to pasture, κύνες πόδας ἀργοί, “dogs, fleet of foot”, a phrase found only here (18.578). When two lions seize a bull at the head of the herd, the dogs and the young men go after it, and the herdsmen set their swift dogs on, “for nothing”: “for the dogs shrank back from biting the lions, / but standing very close they kept barking, and kept out of the way” (18.581–586; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]). ὑλάκτεον, “kept barking”, is the only barking in the Iliad. The dogs behave as the dogs of the lion similes do, which shout with the herdsmen from far off and dare not come near (17.65–67; see [[lion-among-cattle]]), or cower around a lion while others do the fighting (5.476; see [[dogs-around-a-lion]]).
