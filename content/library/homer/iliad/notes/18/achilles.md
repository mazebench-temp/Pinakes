summary: Antilochus brings Achilles the news that Patroclus is dead, and he lies in the dust while his cry brings Thetis and the Nereids up from the sea. To his mother he chooses to kill Hector, though his own death must follow, wishes strife and anger gone from gods and men, and lets his anger against Agamemnon go. Unarmed, at Iris' bidding, he stands at the ditch with the aegis on his shoulders and a flame about his head, and his three shouts rout the Trojans and save the body. All night he leads the lament over Patroclus, and vows not to bury him until he has brought back Hector's armor and head (18.1–35, 18.70–137, 18.165–238, 18.314–355).

**The foreboding.** Antilochus finds him “in front of the straight-horned ships, / pondering in his heart things that had now been fulfilled” (18.3–4; see the translation's note on 18.3). Watching the Achaeans driven back toward the ships, he speaks to his own heart (18.5–14; see [[deeply-troubled]]) and remembers a prophecy, “as my mother once made plain to me and told me, / that the best of the Myrmidons, while I was still alive, / at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun” (18.9–11; see [[thetis]] and [[myrmidons]]). The narrator of Book 17 said that she had not told him this (17.408–411; see the translation's notes on 17.408–411 and 18.10–11). He guesses the rest: “Surely now the valiant son of Menoetius is dead, / hard man! And yet I bade him, once he had thrust off the consuming fire, / to come back to the ships, and not to fight Hector in force” (18.12–14; see [[patroclus]]). The charge he gave in Book 16 warned Patroclus of Apollo and did not name Hector (16.87–96).

**The news.** Then “the son of noble Nestor came near him, / shedding hot tears, and told his grievous news” (18.16–17; see [[antilochus]] and [[messenger-scene]]), in the words with which Menelaus had sent him (18.18–21; 17.686, 17.693). The news strikes him down:

{{quote:f370bfcb-c8fb-5060-97cf-0a3291470b36}}

The first verse is the narrator's for Hector when he learns that Podes has fallen (17.591), and Laertes grieves in nearly the same three verses in the Odyssey (Od. 24.315–317; see [[achos]] and [[laertes]]). μέγας μεγαλωστί, “great over a great space” (18.26), was said of Cebriones lying dead in the dust (16.776; see [[cebriones]]), and Agamemnon's shade will say it of Achilles himself, lying dead in the dust at Troy while the best of the Trojans and the Achaeans were killed around him (Od. 24.36–40). The slave women whom he and Patroclus had taken cry aloud and beat their breasts (18.28–31; see [[captive-women]]). Antilochus mourns beside him, holding his hands, “for he feared that he would cut his throat with the iron” (18.34): the English keeps the Greek's uncertain subject, and the line, which brings in the thought of suicide, was rejected by Bentley and many others (see the translation's note on 18.34 and [[antilochus]]).

**His mother.** “And he groaned aloud terribly; and his lady mother heard him / as she sat in the depths of the sea beside her aged father” (18.35–36), as she heard him weeping by the shore in Book 1 (1.357–358; see [[thetis]]). She comes up with the Nereids and takes her son's head in her hands (18.71), the gesture of mourners over the dead: Achilles holds Patroclus' head at the funeral, and Andromache Hector's when she leads the lament (23.136, 24.724; see [[lament]]). Her question is the one she asked in Book 1, “My child, why are you weeping? What grief has come upon your heart?” (18.73 = 1.362; see [[speak-out]]), and she reminds him of his own wish: “These things have been fulfilled for you / by Zeus, as you prayed before, lifting up your hands” (18.74–75), the prayer that Zeus granted in Book 1 and that Achilles recalled when he prayed for Patroclus (1.407–412, 16.236–238; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]).

**What pleasure have I.** Zeus has done it, “but what pleasure have I in them, since my dear companion has perished, / Patroclus, whom I honored above all my companions, / as my own life?” (18.80–82; see [[hetairos]]). Hector has stripped off the armor, the gods' gift to Peleus on the day of Thetis' marriage (18.82–85; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]), and he wishes that she had stayed among the goddesses of the sea and Peleus had married a mortal (18.86–87; see [[peleus]]), since now she must grieve for a son “whom you will not welcome again / returning home” (18.89–90). He will not live “unless Hector / is first struck by my spear and loses his life” (18.91–92; see [[hector]]). Thetis answers in two verses: “Then you will be swift-fated, my child, from what you say; / for straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.95–96; see [[swift-fated]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, is her word for him in Book 1 (1.417).

**The choice.** He takes up her αὐτίκα, “at once”, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.98):

{{quote:391a8560-ab46-5296-a16d-b156eb0794f5}}

He blames himself. Patroclus “lacked me to be his defender against disaster” (18.100), “and I was no light at all to Patroclus” (18.102). Patroclus had gone out in the hope of becoming “a light to the Danaans”, in Nestor's words and his own, and Achilles had told him to turn back “once you have brought light to the ships” (11.797, 16.39, 16.95; see [[nestor]]). He has been sitting “a burden on the earth” (18.104), the words with which one of the suitors scorns the beggar Odysseus in the Odyssey (Od. 20.379; see the translation's note on 18.104). He is the best of the Achaeans in war, he says, though others are better in the assembly (18.105–106; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]] and the translation's note on 18.105–106); the book draws the same line between Polydamas and Hector, “but the one excelled far in words, the other with the spear” (18.252; see [[polydamas]]). Plato's Socrates, on trial for his life, sets the choice before his judges, quoting Thetis' warning and Achilles' answer (*Apology* 28c–d; see [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]]).

**Strife and anger.** Then the wish: “may strife perish from among gods and from among men, / and anger, which sets even a man of much wisdom to rage, / and which, far sweeter than dripping honey, / swells in the breasts of men like smoke; / so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now” (18.107–111; see [[eris]], [[cholos]], [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[agamemnon]]). On Heraclitus' objection to the wish, see the translation's note on 18.107. And he lets the anger go: “But we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief, / taming the dear heart in our breasts under compulsion” (18.112–113). In Book 16 he began the same verse and went on to keep his wrath until the fighting reached his own ships (16.60–63); he will say both verses again to the assembled Achaeans when he renounces it (19.65–66; see [[menis]]).

{{quote:314d4301-7c3b-5769-8382-323addb1e640}}

The doom he accepts he will accept again, in the same words, over the dying Hector (18.115–116 = 22.365–366; see [[ker-doom]]). Heracles, “the dearest of all to lord Zeus”, did not escape it (18.117–119; see [[heracles]]); Walter Leaf explains “a like fate” as “the same fate, as we are of the same divine origin”, for Achilles too descends from Zeus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.120; 21.187–189; see [[aeacus]]). κλέος ἐσθλόν, “noble fame”, is what he said in the embassy he would lose by going home: “my noble fame is lost, but my life will be long” (9.415; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]], [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and [[kleos]]). Here he chooses it, and with it the grief of the Trojan women, whom he will make weep, “and let them know that I have long ceased from the war” (18.122–125; see [[trojan-women]]). The narrator echoes him when the Trojans tremble in their assembly, “because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (18.247–248). His last words to his mother are Hector's to Helen: “Do not keep me from the battle, though you love me; you will not persuade me” (18.126; compare 6.360; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Thetis consents, “truly this is no bad thing, / to ward off sheer destruction from your hard-pressed companions” (18.128–129), but his armor is with Hector, and he is not to enter “the toil of Ares” until she comes back at dawn with armor from Hephaestus (18.130–137; see [[hephaestus]]).

**Iris.** Hera sends Iris to him “in secret from Zeus and the other gods” (18.165–168; see [[iris]], [[hera]] and the translation's note on 18.168). She greets him with Agamemnon's words in the quarrel, “Rise, son of Peleus, most terrible of all men” (18.170; compare 1.146; see [[most-terrible-of-men]]), tells him that Hector means to cut off Patroclus' head and fix it “on the stakes” (18.176–177), and ends: “let shame come into your heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs; / yours will be the disgrace, if the corpse comes back disfigured at all” (18.178–180; see [[sebas]] and [[lobe]]). His answer, “Goddess Iris, who of the gods sent you to me as a messenger?” (18.182), is, Walter Leaf notes, the first speech of a single verse in the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.182). He cannot go: “Those men have my armor; / and my dear mother does not allow me to arm” (18.188–189), and there is no other man's armor he could put on but the shield of Ajax (18.192–193; see [[ajax]]). Iris tells him to go “just as you are to the ditch and show yourself to the Trojans” (18.198; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The shout from the ditch.**

{{quote:0de772a7-c95e-5511-b8db-2748ef82afa7}}

Athena gave Diomedes such a fire on his great day, “a tireless fire from his helmet and his shield” (5.4–7; see [[diomedes]]), and the charioteers see “the tireless fire” above Achilles' head (18.225; see [[aegis]] and [[athena]]). The blaze reaches the sky like the beacon fires of a besieged island (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). He goes from the wall to the ditch but does not mix with the Achaeans, “for he heeded his mother's shrewd command” (18.215–216), and shouts, while Athena shouts apart from him (18.217–218): “so clear then rang out the voice of the grandson of Aeacus” (18.221; see [[the-trumpet]] and [[grandson-of-aeacus]]). “Three times across the ditch brilliant Achilles shouted aloud, / and three times the Trojans and their famous allies were thrown into confusion” (18.228–229), as three times Hector had seized the body and three times the Ajaxes had driven him off (18.155–158). Twelve of the Trojans' best men die in the rout, and the Achaeans draw Patroclus out of the missiles and lay him on a bier (18.230–233). Achilles follows “shedding hot tears”, as Antilochus came to him (18.235, 18.17; see [[hot-tears]]), “when he saw his trusted companion / lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze, / whom he had sent out with horses and chariot / into the war, and did not welcome him home again” (18.235–238; see [[trusted-companion]]).

**Not welcomed home.** The verb runs through the book. Thetis will not welcome her son “again / returning home to the house of Peleus” (18.59–60 = 18.440–441); Achilles tells her that she will not welcome him (18.89–90); he did not welcome Patroclus home (18.238); and over the body he says it of himself: “since I too shall not come home / to be welcomed in his halls by the old horse-driving Peleus, / nor by my mother Thetis” (18.330–332; see [[nostos]]).

**In the Trojan assembly.** The Trojans meet standing, afraid to sit, because he has appeared (18.246–248). Polydamas dates their success to his wrath: “As long as this man nursed his wrath against brilliant Agamemnon, / so long the Achaeans were easier to fight” (18.257–258; see [[menis]]); now, “such is his overweening spirit”, he will fight for the city and the women, and before he sacks it “the swift dogs will eat him” (18.262–265, 18.283; see [[polydamas]]). Hector answers that if “brilliant Achilles has risen up beside the ships”, he will stand against him (18.305–309; see [[hector]] and [[enyalius]]).

**The lament.** “And among them the son of Peleus led the unceasing lamentation, / laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion” (18.316–317; see [[lament]]). The two verses return at Patroclus' funeral (23.17–18), and Priam will kiss the same man-slaying hands (24.478–479; see [[man-slaying]]). He groans like a lion whose cubs a hunter has taken (18.318–322; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]), and speaks among the Myrmidons:

{{quote:47747473-e850-55bf-9eb6-9dd69ca90944}}

He promised Menoetius to bring his son home to Opus (see [[menoetius]] and [[opus]]); now both of them will lie in Trojan earth. He vows not to bury Patroclus until he has brought back Hector's armor and head, and to cut the throats of twelve Trojans before his pyre (18.333–337; see [[patroclus]]), and orders the body washed (18.343–353). The Myrmidons groan all night around him (18.354–355; see [[myrmidons]]), and Zeus tells Hera that she has had her way, “rousing Achilles, swift of foot” (18.357–358; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]).

**In Thetis' story.** To Hephaestus Thetis tells his life in brief (18.429–461): she repeats her lament from the sea (18.436–443 = 18.55–62), then the girl taken back by Agamemnon, the elders' entreaty and his refusal, and Patroclus sent out in his armor and killed by Apollo (18.444–456; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). She asks for armor “to give my swift-fated son” (18.458). Hephaestus answers: “If only I could hide him as surely from grim-sounding death, / away and apart, when dread fate comes upon him, / as he will have fine armor” (18.464–466). When it is made he sets it “before the mother of Achilles” (18.615), and she leaps down from Olympus “like a hawk” to bring it to him (18.616–617; see [[like-a-hawk]]).
