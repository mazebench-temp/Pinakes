summary: Patroclus is dead from the first verses: Achilles foresees it and Antilochus tells it. Three times Hector seizes the body by the feet and three times the two Ajaxes drive him off, until Achilles' shout from the ditch lets the Achaeans carry it out of the missiles and lay it on a bier. All night Achilles and the Myrmidons lament him; Achilles vows not to bury him until he has brought back Hector's armor and head, and has the body washed, anointed and covered (18.9–21, 18.80–102, 18.148–180, 18.231–238, 18.314–355).

**The best of the Myrmidons.** Achilles, seeing the Achaeans driven back, remembers that his mother once told him that “the best of the Myrmidons, while I was still alive, / at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun” (18.10–11; see [[achilles]] and [[myrmidons]]). Walter Leaf reports from the scholia that Rhianus and Aristophanes left the two verses out, perhaps because Patroclus was not a Myrmidon but a Locrian from Opus; Leaf keeps them, since for an oracle the prediction was near enough (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.10; see [[opus]] and the translation's note on 18.10–11). “Surely now the valiant son of Menoetius is dead, / hard man!” (18.12–13; see [[son-of-menoetius]]). Antilochus confirms it: “Patroclus lies dead, and they are fighting around his corpse, / naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (18.20–21; see [[antilochus]]); the second verse is, but for the case of one word, the one with which Menelaus twice described the body in Book 17 (17.122, 17.693).

**As my own life.** To Thetis Achilles names him “Patroclus, whom I honored above all my companions, / as my own life” (18.81–82). He means to make Hector pay “for making Patroclus, son of Menoetius, his prey” (18.93; see [[hector]]), and reproaches himself that he was not there to defend “my companion / when he was killed” (18.98–99): “and I was no light at all to Patroclus” (18.102). Patroclus had gone out in the hope of becoming “a light to the Danaans” (16.39; 11.797).

**The last fight for the body.** The narrator returns to the plain, where the Achaeans could not have drawn him “out of range of the missiles, the corpse of the attendant of Achilles” (18.151–152; see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). “Three times shining Hector seized him from behind by the feet, / eager to drag him off” (18.155–156), and three times the two Ajaxes drove Hector back without frightening him away, as shepherds cannot drive a hungry lion from a carcass (18.157–164; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). Walter Leaf found the account hard to reconcile with the end of Book 17, where Menelaus and Meriones carry the body while the Ajaxes guard the rear; here the Achaeans drag it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.151; see the translation's note on 18.151–165). Iris tells Achilles what Hector means to do with it: cut the head “from the tender neck” and fix it on the stakes (18.175–177), as the narrator said of him in Book 17 (17.125–127). Leaf reports that Aristarchus objected that Hector meant not to mutilate the body but to exchange it, as Glaucus had proposed (17.160–163), so that Iris' words are a pious fraud to rouse Achilles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.154; see the translation's note on 18.154–156, 18.175–177). Her appeal ends “that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (18.179), the verse with which Menelaus called the leaders to the body (17.255; see [[dogs]]).

**Brought back.** When Achilles has shouted from the ditch, “the Achaeans / gladly drew Patroclus out from under the missiles / and laid him on a bier” (18.231–233), and his companions stand around him weeping. Achilles sees him “lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze” (18.236); φέρτρον, “litter”, is found only here in Homer. He had sent him out “with horses and chariot / into the war, and did not welcome him home again” (18.237–238; see [[nostos]]).

**The lament.** All night the Achaeans groan over him (18.314–315), and Achilles leads the lament, “laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion” (18.317; see [[lament]]). The twelve Trojans are killed at the pyre in Book 23, where the vow is spoken again (23.22–23, 23.175–176; see [[pyres]]). The head of Hector that Achilles vows to bring back answers Hector's purpose for the head of Patroclus (18.176–177). The captive women who are to weep for him night and day are named in the words Achilles used to his mother of the women he would make weep: “the Trojan and the deep-bosomed Dardanian women” (18.339; 18.122; see [[captive-women]] and [[trojan-women]]).

**The washing.** “So saying, brilliant Achilles called to his companions / to set a great tripod over the fire, so that with all speed / they might wash the clotted blood from Patroclus” (18.343–345; see [[tripod]] and [[bathing]]). They wash him, anoint him with olive oil, fill his wounds “with ointment nine years old” (18.351; see the translation's note on 18.351), lay him on a bier and cover him “with a soft linen cloth / from head to foot, and over it with a white cloak” (18.352–353; see [[cloak]]). The Myrmidons then groan over him all night around Achilles (18.354–355; see [[myrmidons]]). Apollo, at Zeus' bidding, gave Sarpedon the same care, washing him in the river, anointing him with ambrosia and dressing him in immortal clothes (16.667–680; see [[sarpedon]]); Achilles will have Hector's body washed and anointed by the slave women, wrapped in a cloak and a tunic, and laid on a bier (24.582–590; see [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**In Thetis' story.** Thetis tells Hephaestus that Achilles “put his own armor on Patroclus, / and sent him into the war” (18.451–452), and that Apollo “after the valiant son of Menoetius had done much harm, / killed him among the champions, and given glory to Hector” (18.455–456; see [[apollo]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). She does not mention Euphorbus, who struck him first (16.806–815; see [[euphorbus]]). The armor is lost with him: “for what he had, his trusted companion lost / when he was brought down by the Trojans” (18.460–461; see [[trusted-companion]] and [[the-armor-of-peleus]]).
