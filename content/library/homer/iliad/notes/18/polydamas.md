summary: In the Trojan assembly on the night after Achilles shows himself, Polydamas, Hector's companion, born on the same night, who alone saw before and after, counsels the Trojans to go back into the city before dawn, since Achilles will not stay on the plain and the towers and gates will guard them. Hector calls him a fool and refuses, and the Trojans applaud Hector; “no one approved of Polydamas, who had devised good counsel”. It is his last appearance in the poem.

**The assembly.** When the sun has set, the Trojans unyoke their horses and gather in assembly before they think of supper. “The assembly was held with all standing, and no one dared / to sit down; for trembling held them all, because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (18.246–248; see [[assembly]] and [[trojans]]). Walter Leaf notes that in a regular assembly all sat but the speaker, so that standing is a mark of confusion and haste (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.246).

**The counselor.**

{{quote:0f6b25c8-9117-59d9-99fa-b3c29a3b12dd}}

The first verse introduced Antenor in the Trojan assembly of Book 7, with the same epithet: “and among them wise Antenor began to speak” (7.347; see [[antenor]] and [[wise]]). The next, “With good intent he addressed them and spoke” (18.253), introduces Nestor (7.326, 9.95), Priam in that same assembly (7.367) and Thoas (15.285; see [[nestor]], [[priam]] and [[thoas]]). That he “alone saw before and after” gives him the foresight that Achilles denies Agamemnon (1.343) and Menelaus grants to old men (3.109; see [[before-and-after]]). Leaf doubts that the words mean the past and the future, and takes them of a man who looks about him on every side, as Polydamas' first words bid the Trojans do: “Consider this well on every side” (18.254; on 18.250). The Odyssey brings Halitherses into the assembly of the Ithacans in the same words, “for he alone saw before and after” and the whole verse “With good intent he addressed them and spoke” (*Odyssey* 24.452–453), and there too the counsel is refused: more than half the assembly springs up with a great shout and follows Eupeithes (*Odyssey* 24.463–466).

Only now does the poem set the two men side by side: they were companions, born on one night, and one excelled in words and the other with the spear (18.251–252). In Book 13 Polydamas had told Hector that a god gives one man the works of war and another a good mind (13.726–734; see [[different-gifts]] and [[hector]]). Leaf compares Virgil's Mimas, the companion and age-mate of Paris, whom Theano bore on the same night that Hecuba bore Paris (*Aeneid* 10.702–706; see [[virgil]]), and notes from the scholia that two men of such different gifts born on one night troubled those who looked for astrology in Homer (on 18.251).

**The counsel.** They must go to the city now and not wait for dawn on the plain: “we are far from the wall” (18.255–256; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The argument is Achilles. While “this man nursed his wrath against brilliant Agamemnon” the Achaeans were easier to fight, and Polydamas himself used to rejoice, spending the night beside the ships in the hope of taking them (18.257–260; see [[menis]] and [[achilles]]). Walter Leaf refers this to the Trojans' night on the plain after their victory in Book 8 (on 18.259). Now he is afraid: such is Achilles' spirit that he will not stay on the plain, where the two armies share the fury of Ares, “but he will fight for our city and our women” (18.261–265; see [[trojan-women]]).

{{quote:aaa87272-7e3d-59b3-aee1-fb8deb979380}}

If they do as he says, they will keep their strength through the night in the place of assembly, the towers, the high gates and the bolted doors will guard the city, and at dawn they will stand on the towers. Achilles may drive his horses about beneath the city, but he will not get in, “nor will he ever sack it; before that the swift dogs will eat him” (18.273–283; see [[swift-dogs]]). Heyne, Düntzer, Nauck and Fick rejected 266–283 as a later addition; Leaf would at most remove 272–276, since Hector's reply takes up Polydamas' own words (on 18.266; see the translation's note on 18.266–283).

**The answer.** Hector looks at him darkly and answers in the words of his refusal at the ditch, “Polydamas, what you say is no longer pleasing to me” (18.284–285 = 12.230–231; see [[hector]] and [[looking-darkly]]). With Antenor's name in place of his, the second verse is the one with which Paris refused Antenor's counsel in the assembly of Book 7 (7.357). He calls him a fool (νήπιε, 18.295; see [[nepios]]), forbids the Trojans to obey him, and sets out the opposite plan in Polydamas' own verses. “Come then, let us all do as I say” (18.297) was Polydamas' verse at the ditch (12.75). Both men say “early, at daybreak, armed in our armor” (18.277, 18.303), Polydamas of a stand on the towers and Hector of an attack at the ships, and Hector's “the worse it will be for him, if he wants it so” (18.306) answers Polydamas' “the worse for him, if he wants” (18.278). Walter Leaf calls the repetition sarcastic, pointing the contrast of the two conclusions (on 18.303), and takes Hector's advice to the man who is anxious for his possessions, to give them to the people to consume in common rather than leave them to the Achaeans, as irony aimed at Polydamas (18.300–302; on 18.300).

**The verdict.**

{{quote:55449eb0-496a-538c-a1fe-5150546344b1}}

The narrator's last words for him, ὃς ἐσθλὴν φράζετο βουλήν, “who had devised good counsel” (18.313), take up his complaint at the ditch, that Hector always rebuked him in the assemblies “when I give good counsel” (ἐσθλὰ φραζομένῳ, 12.211–212; see [[boule]]). Hector took two of his four counsels, at the ditch and at the ships (12.80, 13.748), and refused the other two, the reading of the omen (12.230–250; see [[one-omen-is-best]]) and this last.

**Borne out.** The next day Achilles routs the Trojans, and those who escape come “gladly” into the city (ἀσπάσιοι, 21.607), as Polydamas said they would (ἀσπασίως, 18.270). Hector alone stays outside the walls, and there he remembers this night: Polydamas “will be the first to heap reproach on me”, for he bade him lead the Trojans to the city “during this deadly night”, when Achilles rose up, “but I did not obey” (22.99–103). After this book Polydamas is named only in that speech.
