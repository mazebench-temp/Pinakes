---
summary: Aristotle quotes Achilles' anger “far sweeter than dripping honey” twice in the Rhetoric, and Hector's “Enyalius is impartial” as a common maxim; the Poetics defends the Bear that “alone” never sets, the Politics cites the tripods of Hephaestus that go of themselves to the gods' assembly, and the Eudemian Ethics reports Heraclitus' complaint against Achilles' wish that strife might perish.
---

- The anger that “far sweeter than dripping honey, / swells in the breasts of men like smoke”, quoted for the pleasure of anger and of the hope of revenge (*Rhetoric* 1.11, 1370b10–14, and 2.2, 1378b4–7, on 18.109–110): [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[aristotle-anger]]; compare [[plato-the-pleasure-of-anger]].
- Hector's “Enyalius is impartial”, a hackneyed maxim worth using for those who are weaker (*Rhetoric* 2.21, 1395a15, on 18.309): [[aristotle-one-omen]] and [[victory-shifts]].
- The Bear that “alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus”: “alone” said by metaphor, for the best known is alone (*Poetics* 25, 1461a20–21, on 18.489): [[alone-without-a-share]].
- The tripods of Hephaestus, which the poet says go of themselves to the assembly of the gods, as the tools that would make slaves unnecessary (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1, on 18.376): [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]].
- Heraclitus' rebuke of the poet who wished that strife might perish from among gods and men (*Eudemian Ethics* 7.1, 1235a25–29, on 18.107): [[heraclitus-and-achilles-wish]].
