---
summary: The book in which Achilles takes his short life. Thetis tells him that his death is ready straight after Hector's, and he answers “May I die at once”, since he failed his companion (18.95–100); he will go to find Hector and accept his doom whenever Zeus wills, as not even Heracles escaped his (18.114–121). He tells the dead Patroclus that both of them will die at Troy (18.329–333), Thetis calls him swift-fated before Hephaestus (18.458), and Hephaestus wishes he could hide him from death as surely as he will give him armor (18.464–467).
refs: [18.95-96, 18.329-333, 18.458, 18.464-465]

{{quote:391a8560-ab46-5296-a16d-b156eb0794f5}}

**The choice.** In Book 1 the shortness of his life was a reason to claim honor (1.352–356, 1.415–418). Now it is the price he accepts. Thetis' words are the fact: “Then you will be swift-fated, my child, from what you say; / for straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.95–96). His answer turns on the failure: he was “no light at all to Patroclus”, and sits by the ships “a burden on the earth” (18.102–104). The road he chose against in Book 9 is now closed: “But now, since I am not going home to my dear native land” (18.101; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[nostos]]).

{{quote:314d4301-7c3b-5769-8382-323addb1e640}}

**Heracles.** The example is the greatest of the sons of Zeus, whom fate and Hera's anger brought down (18.117–119; see [[heracles]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Walter Leaf observes that the Iliad knows nothing of Heracles' apotheosis, which first appears in the Odyssey (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.117; *Odyssey* 11.602–604; see the translation's note on 18.117). Achilles takes the comfort that is left: he will lie still when he is dead, “but now may I win noble fame” (18.121; see [[kleos]]).

**At Troy.** Over the body he says what he knows: “it is fated that both of us redden the same earth / here in Troy, since I too shall not come home” (18.329–330), and he speaks to Patroclus as one who will follow him, “since I shall go under the earth after you” (18.333; see [[zeus-does-not-fulfill-all-plans]]). Thetis tells his story to Hephaestus as a story of a short life: she reared him and sent him to Troy and will not welcome him home, and he is “my swift-fated son” (18.436–441, 18.458). Hephaestus' answer is the most the gods can do: “If only I could hide him as surely from grim-sounding death, / away and apart, when dread fate comes upon him, / as he will have fine armor” (18.464–466; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
