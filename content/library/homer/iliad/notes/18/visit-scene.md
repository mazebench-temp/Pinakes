summary: Thetis' visit to Hephaestus is the fullest visit scene of the Iliad. She arrives at his house of bronze and finds him sweating at his bellows, making tripods (18.369–381); his wife Charis comes forward, clings to her hand and asks why she has come, seats her on a silver-studded throne with a footstool and calls Hephaestus (18.382–392); he puts away his tools, washes, dresses and comes out with his golden handmaids, sits beside her, clings to her hand and asks the same question (18.393–427); and Thetis tells her errand (18.428–461).

**The words of welcome.** Charis' greeting and Hephaestus' are the same lines: “Why, Thetis of the long robes, have you come to our house, / respected and dear? Before now you have not come often” (18.385–386 = 18.424–425). They are the lines with which Calypso welcomes Hermes in the Odyssey, and Charis' offer of hospitality, “But come farther in, so that I may set good cheer before you” (18.387), and Hephaestus' promise, “Say what is in your mind; my heart bids me accomplish it, / if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished” (18.426–427), are Calypso's too (*Odyssey* 5.87–91; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.386). The promise is also Aphrodite's to Hera (14.195–196). See [[called-him-by-name]], [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]].

**The host at work.** The finding is a picture of the craftsman: he turns about among his bellows, sweating, in haste, fitting handles to twenty tripods that will go of themselves to the assembly of the gods (18.372–379; see [[tripod]]). Before he greets a goddess he puts his bellows aside, gathers his tools into a silver chest, sponges his face, hands, neck and chest, and puts on his tunic (18.410–416; see [[forge]] and [[tunic]]). He comes out leaning on a staff and on his golden handmaids (18.416–421; see [[scepter]], [[handmaids]] and [[the-golden-handmaids]]).

**The seat.** Thetis is given a silver-studded throne, “fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet” (18.389–390), the seat of an honored guest (see [[throne]] and [[footstool]]). The errand follows only when Hephaestus has sat down beside her and asked; she does not need to beg, since he has promised before she speaks, and she comes to his knees all the same (18.457; see [[supplication]]). See [[hephaestus]] and [[thetis]].
