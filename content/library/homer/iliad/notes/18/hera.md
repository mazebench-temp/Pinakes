summary: Achilles names the anger of Hera among the powers that brought down Heracles. She works for the Achaeans: she sends Iris to Achilles without the knowledge of Zeus, and she sends the unwilling Sun down early to end the battle. At night she answers Zeus' taunt that she has had her way. Hephaestus remembers that his “dog-faced mother” wished to hide him away because he was lame.

**The anger of Hera.** Not even Heracles escaped his doom, says Achilles, though he was dearest to Zeus, “but fate brought him down, and the grievous anger of Hera” (18.119; see [[heracles]] and [[cholos]]). The poem has told of that anger before: Sleep remembered the storm she raised against Heracles as he sailed home from sacking Troy, carrying him off to Cos (14.249–256), and Zeus reminded her that he hung her from the sky with two anvils at her feet in punishment (15.18–30). In Book 19 Agamemnon will tell how she deceived Zeus himself on the day Heracles was to be born (19.95–133).

**Iris sent in secret.** Hector would have dragged off the body of Patroclus, “had not swift Iris, wind-footed, to the son of Peleus / come running from Olympus as a messenger, to arm, / in secret from Zeus and the other gods; for Hera had sent her” (18.166–168; see [[iris]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). When Achilles asks who sent her, Iris answers: “Hera sent me, the glorious wife of Zeus” (18.184; Διὸς κυδρὴ παράκοιτις, found only here). So in Book 1 Hera sent Athena down to restrain Achilles, “loving both men alike in her heart and caring for them both” (1.195–196), and in Book 2 she sent her to stop the Achaeans' flight to the ships (2.155–165; see [[athena]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad Zeus sends Iris (2.786–787, 8.398–399, 11.185–186, 15.157–158, 24.77, 24.143–144), or she comes with no sender named (3.121, 5.353, 23.198–199); in Book 15 Hera called her to Ida, but at Zeus' command (15.143–148; see the note on Book 15). Here alone Hera sends her on an errand of her own. Walter Leaf suspects the verse of secrecy (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.168; see [[zeus]] and the translation's note on 18.168).

**The Sun sent down.** When the Achaeans have drawn Patroclus out of the missiles, “Then ox-eyed queen Hera sent the tireless Sun, / unwilling, to go back to the streams of Oceanus; / and the sun set” (18.239–241; see [[helios]], [[oceanus]] and [[ox-eyed]]). The Sun goes down unwilling because before his time, and the only other interference of a god with the course of nature in Homer is Athena's holding back of the dawn in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 23.243–246; Leaf on 18.240; see the translation's note on 18.240). So ends the third day of battle, begun with the dawn of Book 11 (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**Her answer to Zeus.** Zeus taunts her that she has had her way in rousing Achilles, as if the Achaeans were born from her own self (18.357–359; see [[zeus]]). She answers:

{{quote:bb937068-2c7d-5713-ad1b-f10697917b9a}}

“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” is the verse of her answers to him in Books 1, 4, 8, 14 and 16 (1.552, 4.25, 8.462, 14.330, 16.440; see [[most-dread]]). Her claim to be the best of goddesses “on both counts, by birth and because I am called / your wife” is made in the words of Book 4 (18.365–366 = 4.60–61), where she began it with her birth: “crooked-counseling Cronus fathered me, the most senior” (4.59; see [[cronus]] and [[queenly]]). κακὰ ῥάψαι, “stitch together evil”, is a figure of plotting, and Leaf compares κακορραφίη (on 18.367), the word of Zeus' complaint against her “grievous mischief” when he woke on Ida (15.16). Zeus does not answer.

**The dog-faced mother.** Hephaestus tells Charis that Thetis “saved me when pain came on me after I had fallen far / by the will of my dog-faced mother, who wished / to hide me away because I was lame” (18.395–397; see [[hephaestus]], [[dog-face]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). In Book 1 he took her side, and it was Zeus who threw him out (1.586–594), and in Book 14 she promised Sleep a throne that “Hephaestus, my son, the god lame in both legs” would make (14.239). Walter Leaf notes that Hephaestus and his mother are generally represented as close allies (on 18.395). In Book 21 she will call on him, “crook-footed, my child”, to fight the river (21.331; see [[crook-footed]]).
