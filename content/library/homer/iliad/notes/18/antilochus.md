summary: Antilochus reaches Achilles in front of the ships, “shedding hot tears”, and tells him that Patroclus is dead, in the words in which Menelaus sent him; then he weeps beside him and holds his hands as he groans (18.2, 18.15–21, 18.32–34).
# Νέστορος υἱός (18.16) is Antilochus, taken by refs; the same words are Thrasymedes elsewhere (10.196, 10.229).
refs: [18.16-21, 18.33-34]
passages:
  - 18.15-34 | Antilochus brings the news
---

**The messenger.** The book's second verse brings him: “and Antilochus, swift of foot, came to Achilles as a messenger” (18.2; see [[messenger-scene]]). πόδας ταχύς, “swift of foot”, is said of him only here; at the end of the book it is said of Achilles (18.354, 18.358), and the translation keeps it apart from πόδας ὠκύς and ποδώκης, “swift-footed” (see [[swift-footed]]). While Achilles is still pondering his fear, “the son of noble Nestor came near him, / shedding hot tears, and told his grievous news” (18.16–17; see [[nestor]], [[noble]] and [[hot-tears]]). ἀγαυοῦ Νέστορος υἱός, “the son of noble Nestor”, is found only here; elsewhere he is the son of “great-hearted Nestor” (μεγαθύμου Νέστορος υἱός, 5.565, 13.400, 17.653, 23.541, 23.596; see [[great-hearted]]).

He says what Menelaus told him to say. The second verse is Menelaus' “grievous news, which I wish had never been” (17.686), and the last, but for the case of one word, is the verse with which Menelaus described the body when he sent him (17.693; 17.122). He adds his own cry, ὤ μοι, “Ah me”, and names Achilles by his father, “son of wise-hearted Peleus” (18.18; see [[son-of-peleus]] and [[wise-hearted]]); he leaves out Menelaus' hope that Achilles might bring the corpse safe to his ship (17.692).

**Beside Achilles.** While the slave women cry aloud, “Antilochus on the other side mourned, letting tears fall, / holding the hands of Achilles” (18.32–33; see [[lament]] and [[achilles]]). δάκρυα λείβων, “letting tears fall”, is said once before, of Harpalion's father following his son's body to Ilios (13.658; see [[harpalion]]). The next verse gives the reason, “for he feared that he would cut his throat with the iron” (18.34), and the subjects of the three verses change abruptly; the English keeps the Greek's uncertain subject (see the translation's note on 18.34). Walter Leaf reports that Bentley and many others rejected the line, and that some ancient critics took Antilochus to fear for his own throat; he would rather think that Antilochus holds Achilles' hands “out of affectionate sympathy” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.34). He is not named again in the book.
