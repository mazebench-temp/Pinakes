summary: Achilles fears that “the best of the Myrmidons” has died, as his mother foretold; the Nereids come up the shore where the Myrmidons' ships are drawn up thick around him; and at night Achilles speaks among the Myrmidons over Patroclus, who groan around him until dawn (18.10–11, 18.68–69, 18.323–355).
passages:
  - 18.314-355 | The Myrmidons mourn Patroclus
---

**The best of the Myrmidons.** Achilles remembers his mother's prophecy, “that the best of the Myrmidons, while I was still alive, / at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun” (18.10–11; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). Walter Leaf reports from the scholia that Rhianus and Aristophanes left the verses out, perhaps because Patroclus was not a Myrmidon but a Locrian from Opus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.10; see [[opus]] and the translation's note on 18.10–11); in Book 16 he led the Myrmidons into battle in Achilles' armor (16.257–277).

**The ships.** The Nereids come up out of the sea one after another, “where thick / the ships of the Myrmidons were drawn up around swift Achilles” (18.68–69; see [[thetis]]).

**The mourning.** At night the Achaeans groan over Patroclus (18.314–315; see [[achaeans]]), and Achilles, who leads the lament, speaks “among the Myrmidons” (18.323; see [[lament]]): his speech begins with the vain promise he made to Menoetius and ends with the captive women “whom we ourselves won by toil with our strength and our long spear” (18.324–342; see [[menoetius]] and [[captive-women]]). At his order his companions wash and anoint the body and lay it on a bier (18.343–353), and the scene closes with the words that opened it, the Achaeans now become the Myrmidons: “Then all night long, around Achilles, swift of foot, / the Myrmidons groaned over Patroclus, lamenting” (18.354–355; 18.314–315). In Book 23 Achilles will keep the Myrmidons from scattering, and they will drive their horses three times around the body, lamenting (23.4–14).
