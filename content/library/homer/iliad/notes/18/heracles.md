summary: Achilles takes Heracles as his example when he accepts his own death. Not even mighty Heracles, the dearest of all to Zeus, escaped his doom, for fate brought him down, and the grievous anger of Hera; so Achilles too will lie still when he is dead, but first he will win noble fame (18.117–121).

**The example.** Thetis has just told Achilles that his own fate is ready at once after Hector's (18.95–96), and he accepts it with an example from the past, as Phoenix argued from the story of Meleager (9.524–605; see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Heracles is named by his might, βίη Ἡρακλῆος (18.117; see [[might-of-heracles]]). Walter Leaf takes the first οὐδέ with the whole sentence and the second with “mighty Heracles”: “for neither did even the mighty Heracles” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.117). The example cuts both ways. Heracles was the son of Zeus and the dearest of all to him (18.118), and Zeus' grief for him was told in Books 14 and 15 (14.265–266, 15.24–30; see [[zeus]]); yet he died.

**Fate and Hera.** Two things brought him down: μοῖρα, “fate”, and “the grievous anger of Hera” (ἀργαλέος χόλος Ἥρης, 18.119; see [[moira]], [[hera]] and [[cholos]]). The poem has shown Hera's anger at work against him once already: she drove him to Cos with a storm when he sailed home from sacking Troy (14.249–256, 15.18–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). In Book 19 Agamemnon tells how she held back his birth so that Eurystheus would rule over him (19.95–133; see [[eurystheus]]).

**A like fate.** “So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me, / shall lie still when I am dead” (18.120–121). Leaf takes ὁμοίη as “the same fate”, because Achilles and Heracles are of the same divine origin, the sons of gods, and rejects the attempts to make it “the common fate” (on 18.120). Achilles will win his fame first: “but now may I win noble fame” (18.121; see [[kleos]]).

**Dead, not deified.** For the example to hold, Heracles must be dead, and in the Iliad he is. Leaf notes that legend as yet knows nothing of his apotheosis, which first appears in the Odyssey (on 18.117; see the translation's note on 18.117). There Odysseus sees in the underworld “the might of Heracles, an image”, while he himself feasts among the immortal gods with Hebe as his wife (*Odyssey* 11.601–604; see [[hebe]]).
