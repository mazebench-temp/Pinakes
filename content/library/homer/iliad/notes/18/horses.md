---
summary: At Achilles' shout from the ditch the Trojan horses turn the chariots back, “for they foresaw sorrows in their hearts” (18.223–224). At sunset the Trojans unyoke their horses for the assembly, where Polydamas pictures Achilles giving his own their fill of running beneath the city (18.244, 18.280–281); and on the Shield the besiegers of the city at war ride out behind their high-stepping horses to save their herds (18.530–532).
---

**The fight for the body.** When Hector catches up with the body of Patroclus once more, “the army and the horses” are with him (18.153). Then Achilles shouts at the ditch, and “the fine-maned horses / turned the chariots back; for they foresaw sorrows in their hearts” (18.223–224; see [[the-trumpet]] and [[fine-maned]]). ὄσσοντο is the verb of seeing with the mind's eye, of sensing what is coming, as a heaving sea senses the paths of the winds (14.17; see [[the-heaving-sea]]); the horses know before the men what Achilles' return will mean. Walter Leaf notes that τρόπεον, “turned”, is found only here (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.224). Their drivers are struck with terror at the fire on Achilles' head, and twelve of the best Trojans perish “among their own chariots and spears” (18.225–231). Achilles watches the body brought in: he had sent Patroclus out “with horses and chariot / into the war”, and did not welcome him home again (18.237–238; see [[chariot]] and [[nostos]]).

**The Trojan assembly.** At sunset the Trojans “unyoked their swift horses from under the chariots” and met standing, before they thought of supper (18.243–246; see [[assembly]]). Polydamas, urging them back into the city, says that if they keep to the walls, Achilles will go back to the ships “when he has given his horses with high-arched necks / their fill of running every way as he roams beneath the city” (18.280–281; see [[high-necked]] and [[polydamas]]). Achilles' horses are the immortal pair who wept for Patroclus in the last book (17.426–440; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

**On the Shield.** When the besiegers of the city at war hear the din among their cattle, they “mounted / behind their high-stepping horses and went after them, and quickly reached them” (18.530–532; see [[the-two-cities]]). ἀερσίπους, “high-stepping”, is said only of horses, here and twice more in the Iliad (3.327, 23.475; see [[high-stepping]]).
