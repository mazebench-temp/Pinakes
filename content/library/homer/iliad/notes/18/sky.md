---
summary: On the Shield Hephaestus makes the sky with the earth and the sea, and on it the sun, the moon and “all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (18.483–489).
---

{{quote:28b36646-3452-5c0b-bcd0-801e7ec51533}}

The sky is the second thing on the Shield, after the earth and before the sea, and its lights are named one by one: the tireless sun, the moon at the full, and the constellations (see [[earth]], [[sea]], [[helios]] and [[moon]]). ἐν δὲ τὰ τείρεα πάντα, τά τʼ οὐρανὸς ἐστεφάνωται, “and on it all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (18.485). τείρεα, “constellations”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 18.485). Walter Leaf explains ἐστεφάνωται as “has set around it as a crown”, the stars being the sky's garland, and notes that the phrase recurs in a like line of Hesiod (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.485): in the *Theogony* Dawn bears the Morning Star and ἄστρα τε λαμπετόωντα, τά τʼ οὐρανὸς ἐστεφάνωται, “the shining stars with which the sky is crowned” (*Theogony* 381–382). Four are named, the Pleiades, the Hyades, Orion and the Bear (18.486–489; see [[pleiades]], [[hyades]], [[orion]] and [[the-bear]]).

The sky of the Shield is a sky of lights, not of storm or of battle: in the last book it was the bronze vault that the iron din of the fight reached (17.424–425; see the note on that book). Earlier in this book the sky's own epithet, ἀστερόεις, “starry” (οὐρανὸς ἀστερόεις, 4.44, 5.769), was given to the house of Hephaestus, which Leaf takes as adorned with starlike ornaments (18.370; Leaf on 18.370; see [[starry]] and the translation's note on 18.370).
