---
summary: Polydamas “alone saw before and after” (18.250), and he foresees the rout of the Trojans if they wait on the plain for Achilles (18.254–283); Hector overrules him, and the narrator calls the Trojans fools for cheering (18.310–313). Achilles' mind runs ahead of the news, “pondering in his heart things that had now been fulfilled” (18.4–14), and the Trojans' horses “foresaw sorrows” when he shouted (18.224). On the Shield the herdsmen “did not foresee the trick at all” (18.526).

Walter Leaf doubts the common reading of πρόσσω καὶ ὀπίσσω as past and future and takes the words of a man who looks about him on every side (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.250; see the translation's note on 18.250 and [[before-and-after]]). Polydamas' foresight is proved in the next days: the Trojans who stay on the plain are routed, and Hector, before he dies, remembers that Polydamas told him to lead them into the city (22.99–103; see [[polydamas]] and [[retreat]]).
