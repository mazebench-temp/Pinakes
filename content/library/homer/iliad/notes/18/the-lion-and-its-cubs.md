τοῖσι δὲ Πηλεΐδης ἁδινοῦ ἐξῆρχε γόοιο / χεῖρας ἐπʼ ἀνδροφόνους θέμενος στήθεσσιν ἑταίρου: “And among them the son of Peleus led the unceasing lamentation, / laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion” (18.316–317). Walter Leaf notes an old variant that would make the hands Patroclus' (ἀνδροφόνου), but holds that the epithet, almost confined to Hector, is too moving on the hands of Achilles to be lost, and that the thought is taken further in Book 24, when Priam kisses the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.317; 24.478–479; see [[man-slaying]] and [[lament]]).

πυκνὰ μάλα στενάχων ὥς τε λὶς ἠϋγένειος, “groaning thick and fast, like a bearded lion” (18.318). λίς is the rarer word for the lion, and ὥς τε λὶς ἠϋγένειος is the half-verse of Menelaus giving ground in Book 17 (17.109; see [[lion]] and [[bearded]]). Leaf quotes the scholiast, who says that the poet speaks “very knowingly”, since the male lion does not lead cubs, and that λέαινα, “lioness”, is a later word; Leaf takes λέων as Homer's only word for lion and lioness alike, λέαινα being a later formation, so that the adjectives are masculine even when a mother is meant (on 18.318; compare his note on 17.134). σκύμνοι, “cubs”, are named only here in Homer. ἐλαφηβόλος is literally “deer-shooter”; Leaf reports Aristonicus' note that the poets use it loosely of any hunter (on 18.319; see the translation's note on 18.319).

ὃ δέ τʼ ἄχνυται ὕστερος ἐλθών, / πολλὰ δέ τʼ ἄγκεʼ ἐπῆλθε μετʼ ἀνέρος ἴχνιʼ ἐρευνῶν / εἴ ποθεν ἐξεύροι· μάλα γὰρ δριμὺς χόλος αἱρεῖ: “and he grieves when he comes back too late, / and goes through many valleys, searching out the man's tracks, / in the hope that he may find him somewhere; for very bitter anger seizes him” (18.320–322). ἄγκεα, “valleys”, occurs only here, and ἴχνια are the tracks a quarry leaves, as the hounds of the Odyssey's boar hunt go “searching out the tracks” on Parnassus (ἴχνιʼ ἐρευνῶντες, *Odyssey* 19.436). The lion has become the hunter. Leaf explains the optative ἐξεύροι as a hope, as though the beast said “would I could find him” (on 18.322). δριμύς, “bitter”, is said elsewhere in the Iliad of “the piercing dart” of a woman's labor pains and of “a bitter battle” at the ships (11.269–270, 15.696).

The tenor is a single verse: ὣς ὃ βαρὺ στενάχων μετεφώνεε Μυρμιδόνεσσιν, “so, groaning heavily, he spoke among the Myrmidons” (18.323). The likeness is in the grief, and the simile's words are Achilles' own. The lion “grieves”, ἄχνυται, the verb of his grief (18.62, 18.446; see [[achos]]); it comes back ὕστερος, “too late”, as he was not there to defend his companion when he was killed (18.98–100); it goes after the man, as he has said that he will go to find Hector, “the destroyer of that dear life” (18.114–115); and its anger is χόλος, the word of the anger he has just wished away from gods and men (18.107–111; see [[cholos]]). The speech that follows ends in that anger: he will not bury Patroclus until he brings Hector's armor and head, and before the pyre he will cut the throats of twelve “splendid children of the Trojans, in my anger over your killing” (18.334–337; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[achilles]]). The lion robbed of its young will take the young of its enemies.

In Book 17 Ajax stood over the body of Patroclus like a lion over its young as the huntsmen came on (17.132–137). Now the hunter has taken them, and the lion is Achilles.
