summary: The Trojans hold the armor of Achilles and fight for the body of Patroclus until Achilles shows himself at the ditch; three times he shouts, three times they are thrown into confusion, and twelve of their best men die. That night, in an assembly held standing, they refuse Polydamas' counsel to go back into the city and applaud Hector, fools, for Athena has taken away their wits. Achilles vows to cut the throats of twelve of their children at Patroclus' pyre.

**The armor and the body.** Achilles' armor “is held among the Trojans” (18.130), Hector wearing it (18.131–133; see [[hector]] and [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). At the body Hector calls loudly to the Trojans (18.156), and Iris tells Achilles that they strive “to drag it off to windy Ilios” (18.174–175; see [[windy]]) and that the corpse may become “a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (18.179), the words of Menelaus in Book 17 (17.255; see [[dogs]]). Thetis sent her son to Ilios “to fight with the Trojans” (18.59 = 18.440), and in the prophecy that Achilles remembers before the news comes, the best of the Myrmidons was to leave the light of the sun “at the hands of the Trojans” (18.10–11; see [[patroclus]]).

**The shout.** Iris bids Achilles go to the ditch and show himself to the Trojans, “in the hope that in fear of you they may hold off from the war” (18.198–200; see [[iris]]). He shouts, and “among the Trojans he roused untold tumult” (18.218; see the translation's note on 18.218), his voice ringing out like a trumpet when enemies surround a city (18.219–221; see [[the-trumpet]]).

The horses foresee their sorrows, and the charioteers see the fire that Athena has kindled over Achilles' head (18.223–227; see [[achilles]] and [[athena]]). Of the twelve who perish “among their own chariots and spears”, Walter Leaf thinks it probably meant that the front rank, turning in sudden flight, fell on the spears of the men behind them, though, he adds, there ought to be no chariots in the fighting line (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.230). Then Hera sends the sun down and the battle ends (18.239–242; see [[hera]]).

**The assembly.** “And the Trojans on the other side, away from the fierce battle, / withdrew and unyoked their swift horses from under the chariots, / and gathered in assembly before they thought of supper” (18.243–245). They hold it standing, for trembling has seized them all because Achilles has appeared (18.246–248; see [[assembly]]). Polydamas, Hector's companion, bids them go back into the city before dawn, for if Achilles finds them on the plain, “dogs and vultures will eat many / of the Trojans” (18.254–283; see [[polydamas]] and [[vultures]]). Hector refuses: “none of the Trojans will obey you; I will not allow it” (18.296). They are to take their supper throughout the army by companies, keep watch, and at dawn attack the ships, and the man who is anxious for his possessions may give them to the people to consume in common (18.297–304; see [[night-watch]]). “So Hector spoke, and the Trojans shouted in applause, / fools; for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits” (18.310–311; see [[nepios]]). Achilles used the same words of Agamemnon, with Zeus in place of Athena: “for Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (9.377; see [[agamemnon]]). They approve of Hector, “who devised evil”, and no one of Polydamas (18.312–313; see [[boule]]), and they take their supper throughout the army, as they did after Priam's orders in Book 7 (18.314 ≈ 7.380). Polydamas had warned that Achilles “will fight for our city and our women” (18.265; see [[trojan-women]]); the next day those who escape him come gladly into the city (21.606–607).

**Hera and Thetis.** Hera answers Zeus that she could not but “stitch together evil for the Trojans, in my resentment against them” (18.367; see [[hera]]). Thetis tells Hephaestus that the Trojans had penned the Achaeans in among the sterns of their ships and would not let them out, and that the elders of the Argives begged Achilles, naming many glorious gifts (18.446–449); and that Patroclus was “brought down by the Trojans” (18.461; see [[thetis]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**Twelve.** Twelve of their best men die at Achilles' shout (18.230), and that night he vows over Patroclus that he will cut the throats of “twelve / splendid children of the Trojans” before the pyre (18.336–337; see [[human-sacrifice]]). He takes twelve youths alive from the river (21.26–28) and kills them at the pyre (23.175–176).
