---
summary: Pausanias knew a dance of Ariadne carved in white stone at Cnossus among the works of Daedalus, a sanctuary of Eurynome near Phigalia whose image was half woman and half fish, Homer's Charis among the Graces, and the Linus song of the vintage, which he took for a song about Linus.
---

- The dance of Ariadne at Cnossus, “which Homer too mentioned in the Iliad”, worked on white stone, among the works of Daedalus (*Description of Greece* 9.40.3–4, on 18.590–592): [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]].
- **Eurynome.** Where the Lymax runs into the Neda near Phigalia stands a sanctuary of Eurynome, opened one day a year. The people of Phigalia take Eurynome for a title of Artemis, but those who know the old records say that she is the daughter of Oceanus whom Homer mentions in the Iliad as having received Hephaestus with Thetis (18.398–405). Pausanias did not see the image, but was told that golden chains bind it, and that it is a woman to the hips and a fish below, a fitting token for a daughter of Oceanus who lives in the depths of the sea with Thetis, and none for Artemis (*Description of Greece* 8.41.4–6; see [[eurynome]]).
- **Charis.** Writing of the Graces of Orchomenus, Pausanias notes that Homer too mentions them, making one of them the wife of Hephaestus and giving her the name Charis (18.382–383), and making Sleep the lover of Pasithea; Hesiod makes the Graces the daughters of Zeus and Eurynome, named Euphrosyne, Aglaea and Thalia (*Description of Greece* 9.35.4–5; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]] and [[graces]]).
- **The Linus song.** On Helicon there is an image of Linus in a small rock worked like a cave, and offerings are made to him every year before the sacrifice to the Muses. Linus, Pausanias says, was the son of Urania and Amphimarus, and Apollo killed him for rivaling him in song; the mourning for him spread even among the barbarians, so that the Egyptians have a song Linus, which they call Maneros. Homer, knowing that the sufferings of Linus were a song among the Greeks, says that Hephaestus made on the shield of Achilles, among other things, a boy singing to the lyre “the things about Linus” (τὰ ἐς Λίνον), and he quotes the two verses (*Description of Greece* 9.29.6–7, on 18.569–570; see [[the-linus-song]]). He takes λίνον for the man the song laments, where the Iliad's word is the song itself. Pamphos called him Oetolinus, and Sappho sang of Adonis and Oetolinus together (*Description of Greece* 9.29.8).
