summary: Achilles calls himself “a burden on the earth” and foresees that he and Patroclus will redden the same earth at Troy, which will hold him there (18.104, 18.329–333); on the Shield the earth is the first thing Hephaestus makes, and its plowland, though made of gold, grows dark behind the plowmen (18.483, 18.541–549).
# ἄρουραν (18.541) is the Shield's plowland; it also catches 8.486, 11.68 and 11.558, all the earth or a field, as ἄρουρα and ἀρούρης do.
grc: [ἄρουραν]
refs: [18.548]

**A burden on the earth.** “But I sit idly beside the ships, a burden on the earth” (18.104): ἄχθος ἀρούρης, a dead weight on the land. Walter Leaf, comparing the suitor's sneer at the beggar in the Odyssey, αὔτως ἄχθος ἀρούρης, “a mere burden on the earth” (*Odyssey* 20.379), takes ἐτώσιον as an adverb, “idly” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.104; see the translation's note on 18.104). Achilles says it of himself a few verses after he has spoken of his “dear native land”, to which he will not return (18.101; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[nostos]]).

**The same earth.** Over the body of Patroclus he says that it is fated that both of them “redden the same earth / here in Troy”, since his father and mother will not welcome him home, “but the earth will hold me here”; and he speaks to the dead man as one who will follow him: “since I shall go under the earth after you” (18.329–333; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The earth that holds the dead, as it already held Helen's brothers in Book 3 (3.243), will be the same for both; in Book 23 the ghost of Patroclus asks that their bones lie in one urn (23.83–92). Thetis tells Hephaestus that her son “lies on the ground, grieving in his heart” (18.461), as he lay stretched out in the dust when the news came (18.26–27).

**The earth on the Shield.** “On it he made the earth, on it the sky, on it the sea” (18.483; see [[sky]], [[sea]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The earth comes first, and the scenes of the Shield are set on it. In the plowing scene it is “a soft fallow field, rich plowland, / broad, thrice plowed” (18.541–542), and when the plowmen reach “the end of the field” they are given wine (18.544–546). Then the wonder: “And the earth grew dark behind them, and looked like plowed land, / though it was made of gold; that was a wonder beyond all” (18.548–549; see [[the-plowing]]).
