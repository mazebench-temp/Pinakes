summary: Hector gives the soldier's form of Paris' maxim when he refuses Polydamas' advice: he will face Achilles, “and either he will carry off the great mastery, or I might. / Enyalius is impartial, and he kills the man who would kill” (18.308–309).
grc: ["ξυνὸς Ἐνυάλιος"]
en: [is impartial, he kills the man who would kill]

{{quote:0291c8d7-8bc6-5ae5-b7ed-4c99cb4d4724}}

ξυνός is “common”, shared by both sides: the war god favors neither, and kills the man who would kill. Walter Leaf compares Paris' “victory shifts from man to man” (6.339), and takes κτανέοντα as a desiderative present, “the man who would kill”, against Cobet's future κτενέοντα (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.309; see the translation's note on 18.309). The optative of 18.308, “or I might”, puts the second outcome hesitantly in a confident speech (Leaf on 18.308). Aristotle quotes the first words as a common maxim for those who are weaker (*Rhetoric* 1395a15; see [[enyalius]], [[aristotle-one-omen]] and [[kratos]]). The maxim is spoken by the man who will die, and it comes true when Achilles kills him (see [[hector]] and [[retreat]]).
