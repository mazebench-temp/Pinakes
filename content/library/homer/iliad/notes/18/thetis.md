summary: Thetis hears her son's groan in the depths of the sea and leads the Nereids' lament for a son she will not welcome home. She comes to him, learns that Patroclus is dead, tells him that his own fate is ready straight after Hector's, and promises new armor from Hephaestus at dawn. In the house of Hephaestus, whom she once saved, she tells her sorrows, from her marriage to a mortal against her will to the armor her son has lost, and she carries the god's work down from Olympus like a hawk.

**The cry from the sea.** Achilles lies in the dust when Antilochus has told him the news (18.18–34; see [[achilles]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]), and then “he groaned aloud terribly; and his lady mother heard him / as she sat in the depths of the sea beside her aged father, / and then she wailed aloud” (18.35–37; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). The middle verse is the verse of Book 1 (18.36 = 1.358), where he wept by the shore and prayed to her, and she rose from the gray sea like a mist (1.357–359; see [[like-a-mist]]). There he called her; here she hears him without a call. The goddesses of the sea gather around her, “all the Nereids that were in the depths of the sea” (18.38; see [[nereids]]), and the poet names thirty-three of them (18.39–49), a catalogue athetized in antiquity as Hesiodic (see the translation's note on 18.39–49).

**The lament.** “The silver-white cave was filled with them; and all of them together / they beat their breasts, and Thetis led the lamentation” (18.50–51; see [[lament]]). ἐξῆρχε γόοιο, “led the lamentation”, is said of the chief mourner: Achilles will lead the lament over Patroclus that night and again in Book 23 (18.316, 23.17), Hecuba and Helen over Hector (22.430, 24.747, 24.761). Thetis leads a lament while her son is alive. She calls the Nereids her sisters (18.52) and grieves for him as already lost:

{{quote:319c60ba-51dc-5d2c-abb6-8b532b7e7dde}}

δυσαριστοτόκεια, “unhappy mother of the best of men”, is found only here; the scholiast explains it as “having borne the best to an evil end” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.54; see the translation's note on 18.54). Leaf compares her first lament in Book 1: “Ah me, my child, why did I raise you, when I bore you to such sorrow?” (1.414). There she said that his portion of life was brief (1.416–418); here she knows that he will not come home (see [[nostos]]). The simile of the sapling and the plant in the orchard (18.56–57; see [[like-a-sapling]]) returns, with the rest of the lament, in her plea to Hephaestus (18.437–443). “And while he lives and looks on the light of the sun / he grieves, and I can do nothing to help him by going to him” (18.61–62; see [[helios]]); and yet she goes, “so that I may see my dear child, and hear / what grief has come upon him while he stays away from the war” (18.63–64). Plato's Socrates would ask the poets not to make the gods lament, and gives her verse of 18.54 as his example (*Republic* 3.388b–c; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]).

**At the ships.** The Nereids go with her out of the cave in tears, and they come up out of the sea onto the shore, one after another, where the ships of the Myrmidons are drawn up around Achilles (18.65–69; see [[myrmidons]]). “And as he groaned heavily his lady mother stood beside him, / and wailing shrilly she took her son's head in her hands” (18.70–71). To hold the head is the gesture of the chief mourner over the dead: Achilles holds the head of Patroclus as they carry him to the pyre (23.136), and Andromache holds Hector's as she begins the lament (24.723–724). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles how his mother came out of the sea with the immortal sea goddesses when she heard the news of his death, with a cry over the sea that set the Achaeans trembling, and how the daughters of the old man of the sea stood around him lamenting, and clothed him in immortal garments (*Odyssey* 24.47–59).

Her first words are her words of Book 1: “My child, why are you weeping? What grief has come upon your heart? / Speak out; do not hide it” (18.73–74; 18.73 = 1.362, and 18.74 begins as 1.363; Leaf on 18.73). But now she knows the answer to her own question in part, for “These things have been fulfilled for you / by Zeus, as you prayed before, lifting up your hands, / that all the sons of the Achaeans should be penned in among the sterns / for want of you, and suffer unseemly deeds” (18.74–77; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The prayer was his request to her in Book 1, made with hands stretched out to her, that Zeus should “pen the Achaeans in among their ships' sterns and along the sea / as they are slaughtered” (1.351, 1.409–410; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]), and he reminded Zeus of it himself when he sent Patroclus out: “once before you listened to my word when I prayed” (16.236).

**Swift-fated.** Achilles answers that the Olympian has fulfilled it, but that Patroclus is dead and Hector has stripped the armor, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus / on the day when they put you into the bed of a mortal man” (18.84–85; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). He wishes that she had stayed among the goddesses of the sea and that Peleus had taken a mortal wife (18.86–87; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]] and [[peleus]]), for it was so that she might have countless grief for a son “whom you will not welcome again / returning home” (18.88–90; see the translation's note on 18.88): the words of her lament, which he did not hear (18.59–60). He will not live, he says, unless Hector dies under his spear (18.90–93). She answers in two verses:

{{quote:7ad41646-13da-5a69-a669-c3b03071bd02}}

ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, is her word of Book 1 (1.417), and she will use it again to Hephaestus (18.458; see [[swift-fated]]). The prophecy is now exact: his own fate is ready “straight after Hector” (see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). In Book 9 he spoke of the two fates she had told him of (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]); here he chooses. “May I die at once” (18.98) takes up her word αὐτίκα, “straight”, as Leaf notes (on 18.98), and he will accept his doom whenever Zeus is willing (18.115–116; see [[ker-doom]]). For Plato's use of the choice, see [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]].

**Her answer.** She does not argue: “Yes, my child, truly this is no bad thing, / to ward off sheer destruction from your hard-pressed companions” (18.128–129). Hector wears the armor and glories in it, “but I say / he will not exult in it long, since his death is near him” (18.132–133; see [[hector]]). “But do not yet enter the toil of Ares / until you see me come back here with your own eyes; / for at dawn I will return with the rising sun, / bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.134–137; see [[ares]] and [[hephaestus]]). He keeps her command. He tells Iris that his dear mother does not allow him to arm until he sees her come back (18.189–191; see [[iris]]), and at the ditch “he heeded his mother's shrewd command” and did not mingle with the Achaeans (18.216). She comes back as she promised, as Dawn rises from the streams of Oceanus, bringing the gifts of the god (19.1–3; see [[eos]]).

**Her sisters.** She sends the Nereids home: “Now go down into the broad bosom of the sea, / to see the Old Man of the Sea and our father's house, / and tell him everything” (18.140–142). She goes herself “to high Olympus, / to Hephaestus, famed for his craft” (18.142–143; see [[high-olympus]] and [[famed-for-his-craft]]), and “her feet carried her to Olympus” (18.148; see [[olympus]]). Οὔλυμπον δέ at 18.146 and 18.148 is “to Olympus”, with the suffix of direction written apart, as at 1.221 and 15.133 (see the translation's note on 15.133).

**In the house of Hephaestus.** “And silver-footed Thetis came to the house of Hephaestus” (18.369; see [[silver-footed]], [[the-making-of-the-arms]] and [[visit-scene]]). Charis sees her, clings to her hand, calls her by name and welcomes her: “Why, Thetis of the long robes, have you come to our house, / respected and dear? Before now you have not come often” (18.385–386; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]], [[called-him-by-name]] and [[long-robed]]). Calypso greets Hermes with nearly the same words in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.87–88), and Hephaestus greets Thetis with them again (18.424–425), adding the promise that Aphrodite made to Hera, “Say what is in your mind” (18.426–427 = 14.195–196 = *Odyssey* 5.89–90). He tells Charis who she is: “Truly then a goddess I fear and respect is in our house, / who saved me when pain came on me after I had fallen far” (18.394–395). His mother had wished to hide him away because he was lame, “had not Eurynome and Thetis taken me to their bosom” (18.396–398; see [[eurynome]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). ὑπεδέξατο κόλπῳ, “took to her bosom”, are the words of Diomedes' story, in which Thetis received Dionysus when Lycurgus drove him into the sea (6.136; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and [[dionysus]]). For nine years Hephaestus worked for the two goddesses in a hollow cave by the stream of Oceanus, and no one else knew of it, “but Thetis and Eurynome knew, who had saved me” (18.400–405). Achilles reminded her in Book 1 that she alone had saved Zeus from bondage, when she brought the hundred-handed Briareus to Olympus (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]] and [[briareus]]): in the Iliad she is the gods' rescuer. Now Hephaestus must pay her “the full price for my life” (ζῳάγρια, 18.407; see the translation's note on 18.407).

**Her sorrows.** Hephaestus sits down beside her and asks what she wants, and she answers with her whole grief:

{{quote:351f7a81-c51d-5ab0-97e0-8171b051426d}}

ἀνδρὶ δάμασσεν, “subdued me to a man”: δαμάζω is the verb of taming, and of bringing a man down in battle, as Patroclus was “brought down by the Trojans” (18.461), and the oath of Book 3 used it of wives given to other men, “and may their wives be subdued by others” (3.301; see [[marriage]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Achilles said that the gods “put” her into the bed of a mortal man (18.85); in Book 24 Hera says that she herself reared Thetis and gave her to Peleus for his wife, and that all the gods came to the wedding (24.59–63; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). Peleus “now, by grievous old age / lies worn out in his halls” (18.434–435). Her son she bore and reared and sent to Troy, and she will not welcome him home (18.436–443; 18.437–443 = 18.56–62). Then she tells the story of the wrath as she sees it: Agamemnon took back the girl, Achilles wasted his heart away, the elders begged him with gifts and he refused, he sent Patroclus out in his own armor, and the Achaeans would have sacked the city that day, had not Apollo killed him and given glory to Hector (18.444–456; see [[briseis]], [[the-embassy-to-achilles]], [[apollo]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus athetized 18.444–456 because the recapitulation is out of place and the sending of Patroclus is made to follow the embassy (18.451), and judges that the whole speech may well have been composed for this place from the first; he quotes the scholiast's dry remark that she imitated the character of women, answering not what she was asked but what grieved her (on 18.429). “That is why I come now to your knees” (18.457; see [[supplication]]): she asks for a shield and a helmet, greaves and a corselet for her “swift-fated son” (18.458–461; see [[shield]], [[helmet]], [[greaves]] and [[corselet]]).

**The armor.** Hephaestus answers that he wishes he could hide her son from death as surely as he will have armor that men will marvel at (18.463–467; see [[hephaestus]]), and leaves her where she is to go to his bellows (18.468). When all is made, “he lifted it and set it before the mother of Achilles. / And she like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus, / carrying the gleaming armor from Hephaestus” (18.615–617; see [[like-a-hawk]], [[hawk]] and [[niphoeis]]). So Apollo went down from the mountains of Ida “like a hawk, / a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (15.237–238). Leaf observes that, to our notions at least, it seems strange of Thetis to depart without a word of thanks (on 18.614).
