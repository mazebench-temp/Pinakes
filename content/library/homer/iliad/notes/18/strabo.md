---
summary: Strabo reads the Shield as geography. The Bear that “alone” never bathes in Oceanus stands for the whole arctic circle, the Oceanus around the rim that Oceanus surrounds the earth, and “backward-flowing” Oceanus that Homer knew the tides; he names the passage “the Hoplopoiia”. Opus, where Achilles promised to bring Patroclus home, and Polydamas' “we are far from the wall” serve him too.
---

- **The Bear.** Homer hints that the far north also borders on Oceanus when he says of the Bear that it “alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus” (18.489): by the Bear and the Wagon he means the arctic circle, and by Oceanus the horizon. Those who charge him with knowing one Bear instead of two are wrong, since the second was not yet a constellation; Crates wrongly wrote οἶος for οἴη, and Heraclitus did better, naming the Bear for the arctic circle as Homer does (*Geography* 1.1.6): [[alone-without-a-share]].
- **The rim of the shield.** That Oceanus lies in a circle around the earth Homer shows in Hera's words on the limits of the earth (14.200–201), “and in the Hoplopoiia of the shield of Achilles he sets Oceanus in a circle around it, on the rim” (*Geography* 1.1.7, on 18.607–608; see [[oceanus]] and [[title-of-book-18]]).
- **Backward-flowing.** In the same chapter Strabo counts it part of Homer's diligence that he knew of the ebb and flow of the ocean, calling it ἀψόρροος, “backward-flowing” (*Geography* 1.1.7). Homer gives Oceanus the epithet in this book, of Eurynome, “daughter of backward-flowing Oceanus” (18.399), and once in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 20.65; see [[backward-flowing]]).
- **Opus.** Opus is the mother city of the Locrians, fifteen stades from the sea. “That Patroclus was from Opus Homer says, and that he fled to Peleus after an involuntary killing, while his father Menoetius stayed in his native land; for it was there, Achilles says, that he had promised Menoetius to bring Patroclus home on his return from the expedition” (*Geography* 9.4.2, on 18.324–327 and 23.85–88). Menoetius was not king of the Opuntians, he adds, but Ajax the Locrian was; and the boy whom Patroclus killed, whom Homer calls only the son of Amphidamas (23.87), is named Aeanes, and a precinct called the Aeaneium and a spring called Aeanis are shown after him. See [[opus]], [[menoetius]] and [[patroclus]].
- **Far from the wall.** Following Demetrius of Scepsis, Strabo argues that old Ilium stood at the Village of the Ilians, thirty stades from the Ilium of his day (*Geography* 13.1.35). The naval station still so called lies so near the present city, he says, that one would marvel at the Greeks for leaving it unwalled so long and at the Trojans for never attacking it; Homer puts a great distance between the ships and the city. Among the proofs are Odysseus' tale to Eumaeus, “for we went very far from the ships” (*Odyssey* 14.496), the spy sent to learn whether the Trojans will stay by the ships, far from their own wall, or go back to the city (10.208–210), and Polydamas' words in this book: “Consider this well on every side, friends; for I bid you / go now to the city, … we are far from the wall” (18.254–256; *Geography* 13.1.36; see [[polydamas]] and [[troy]]).
