summary: Book 18 is the news and the first night of mourning. Achilles fears what has happened before Antilochus tells him that Patroclus lies dead (18.6–21); the death is told again to Thetis, by Iris, at the bier and in the lament, and Thetis, telling it to Hephaestus, names Apollo as the killer (18.454–456). Achilles resolves to kill Hector, though his own death must follow, and vows not to bury Patroclus until he has brought back Hector's armor and head (18.88–126, 18.333–337).

**The foreboding.** Before the news comes, Achilles guesses it. He sees the Achaeans driven to the ships and fears the evil that his mother once foretold, that “the best of the Myrmidons, while I was still alive, / at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun” (18.6–11; see [[prophecy]]). In Book 17 the narrator said that his mother had not told him that his dearest companion had perished (17.408–411); Walter Leaf finds a clear contradiction between the two places, and keeps 18.10–11, which Rhianus and Aristophanes left out, since for an oracle the prediction was near enough to the fact (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.10; see the translation's note on 18.10–11). Achilles remembers his own warning too: “I bade him, once he had thrust off the consuming fire, / to come back to the ships, and not to fight Hector in force” (18.13–14). In Book 16 he told Patroclus to come back once he had driven the Trojans from the ships, and warned him not against Hector but against Apollo, who loves the Trojans (16.87–96).

**The news.** Antilochus brings it in four verses:

{{quote:b180cde1-38d5-514d-881e-c5981a3943d3}}

λυγρῆς … ἀγγελίης, “grievous news”, is the phrase of Ajax and Menelaus in Book 17, when a messenger was sought and sent (17.642, 17.686; see [[antilochus]]). Achilles lies in the dust and fouls his face and hair, the women cry aloud, and Antilochus weeps and holds his hands (18.22–34; on the fear of 18.34 see the translation's note, and see [[achilles]] and [[achos]]); his groan brings his mother up from the sea (18.35–38; see [[thetis]] and [[nereids]]).

**Told again.** The book tells the death over and over, each time from another side. To Thetis, Achilles tells it as his own loss and his own failure: “my dear companion has perished, / Patroclus, whom I honored above all my companions, / as my own life”, and he was not there to defend him “when he was killed” (18.80–82, 18.98–100). Iris tells it as a fight still going on, with Hector bent on fixing the head on the stakes (18.171–177; see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). At the bier Achilles sees the body “lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze, / whom he had sent out with horses and chariot / into the war, and did not welcome him home again” (18.236–238). And Thetis, telling her sorrows to Hephaestus, tells it as the end of her son's story since Book 1: Achilles put his own armor on Patroclus and sent him into the war, and they would have sacked the city that day, “had not Apollo, / after the valiant son of Menoetius had done much harm, / killed him among the champions, and given glory to Hector” (18.450–456; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). She names the god, as the dying Patroclus did (16.849–850); Hector, in her account, received the glory. The horse Xanthus will repeat her last verse word for word to Achilles (19.414 = 18.456; see [[apollo]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

**Not welcomed home.** The death is told in the words of a homecoming denied. Achilles “did not welcome him home again” (οὐδʼ αὖτις ἐδέξατο νοστήσαντα, 18.238); Thetis will not welcome her son home (18.59–60 = 18.440–441, 18.89–90); and Achilles tells the Myrmidons that Peleus and Thetis will not welcome him either, for he and Patroclus are fated to redden the same earth at Troy (18.329–332; see [[nostos]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). His promise to bring the son of Menoetius home to Opus was “a vain word” (18.324–327; see [[menoetius]] and [[opus]]).

**The vow.** The death turns Achilles' anger from Agamemnon to Hector (18.107–116; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). He will not live unless Hector pays for making Patroclus his prey (18.90–93), and over the body he vows not to give his friend burial rites until he brings Hector's armor and head, and to cut the throats of twelve Trojan children before the pyre (18.333–337; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). The verses on the twelve (18.336–337) recur in Book 23, where Achilles repeats the promise (23.22–23) before he keeps it at the pyre (23.175–176); Leaf allows either that the verses belong in both places or that 18.336–342 were put here to prepare for that book (on 18.336). Until then the body lies by the ships, washed, anointed and covered, and the Myrmidons lament it through the night (18.338–355; see [[lament]] and [[bathing]]).
