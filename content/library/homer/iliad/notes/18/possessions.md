summary: Hector's picture of Troy's poverty: once all men spoke of Priam's city as rich in gold and bronze, “but now the fine treasures of our houses are gone, / and many have gone to Phrygia and lovely Maeonia, / possessions sold away, since great Zeus grew angry” (18.288–292). He tells any Trojan troubled about his possessions to give them to the people to consume in common (18.300–302). On the Shield the besiegers would divide in two the wealth of the city (18.511–512).
grc: [δόμων]

Walter Leaf finds the lines on the city's wealth confusing and without bearing on the situation, since its losses would argue for defense rather than attack; he takes περνάμενα, “sold away”, as perhaps to pay the allies, as Hector wears down his people with gifts and food for them (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.288, 18.292; 17.225–226). He calls 18.300–302 “very obscure in thought and expression” and reads them as irony against Polydamas (on 18.300; see the translation's notes on 18.288–292 and 18.300–302 and [[trade]]).
