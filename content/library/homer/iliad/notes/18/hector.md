summary: Hector wears the armor he stripped from Patroclus, and Thetis says that his death is near; Achilles will not live unless Hector dies by his spear, though his own death must follow. Like a flame, Hector three times seizes the body by the feet, meaning to fix the head on the stakes, and would have dragged it off had Iris not roused Achilles. That night he rejects the counsel of Polydamas, his companion born on the same night, to go back into the city, and resolves to face Achilles, for “Enyalius is impartial”; the Trojans applaud him, fools.

**The armor.** Antilochus' news ends with Hector: “Patroclus lies dead, and they are fighting around his corpse, / naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (18.20–21), the verse with which Menelaus told Ajax and then Antilochus of the loss (17.122, 17.693; see [[antilochus]] and [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]). Achilles tells his mother that Hector “stripped off the armor, huge, a wonder to see, / and fine, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (18.83–84; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). Thetis answers that “Hector of the gleaming helmet / wears it on his own shoulders and glories in it; but I say / he will not exult in it long, since his death is near him” (18.131–133; see [[thetis]]). Zeus said as much when Hector put it on: death “is now close to you” (17.202; see [[zeus]]).

**Achilles' resolve.** Before the news comes, Achilles thinks of Hector: he had told Patroclus to come back to the ships “and not to fight Hector in force” (18.14), though his orders in Book 16 were to come back once he had driven the Trojans from the ships, and not to lead the way on toward Ilios (16.87–96). Now he will not live “unless Hector / is first struck by my spear and loses his life, / and pays for making Patroclus, son of Menoetius, his prey” (18.91–93; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). Thetis answers, “for straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.96), and Achilles accepts it. He failed his companions, “the many who were brought down by brilliant Hector” (18.103; see [[brilliant]]), and he will go to find “the destroyer of that dear life, / Hector”, and take his own doom whenever Zeus wills it (18.114–116; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). From here on, Hector's death and Achilles' are bound together.

**At the body.** The Achaeans flee “before man-slaying Hector” until they reach the ships and the Hellespont (18.149–150; see [[man-slaying]] and [[hellespont]]), and “Hector, son of Priam, like a flame in his courage” catches up with the body again (18.153–154; see [[like-a-flame]] and [[son-of-priam]]):

{{quote:803a2b40-9352-5812-aaf8-8e12f3cfa749}}

He is the hungry lion, and the two Ajaxes the shepherds who cannot drive him from the carcass (see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). “And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory” (18.165), had not Hera sent Iris to Achilles (18.166–168; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[kydos]] and [[iris]]). Walter Leaf takes 153–165 for an interpolated account of the fight, hardly to be reconciled with the end of Book 17, where Menelaus and Meriones carry the body and the Ajaxes guard the rear: here the Achaeans drag it, with the feet in Hector's reach (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.151; see the translation's note on 18.151–165 and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Three times Hector seizes the body, and three times the Ajaxes drive him off (18.155–157); three times Achilles will shout from the ditch, and three times the Trojans and their allies will be thrown into confusion (18.228–229).

**The head.** Iris tells Achilles that “above all shining Hector / is bent on dragging it; and his heart bids him fix the head / on the stakes, when he has cut it from the tender neck” (18.175–177; see [[phaidimos]]). The narrator gave him the same purpose in Book 17: he was dragging Patroclus “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127; see [[dogs]]). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus, who held that Hector meant to exchange the body for Sarpedon's, as Glaucus had proposed (17.160–163), took Iris' words for a pious fraud to rouse Achilles; Leaf himself takes the passage to be of independent origin, so that the difference need cause no surprise (on 18.154; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]). The stakes may be a palisade along the top of the Trojan wall, as Leaf prefers (on 18.177; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). Achilles answers in kind over the body that night: he will not bury Patroclus “until I bring here Hector's / armor and head, your great-hearted slayer's” (18.334–335), and he will cut the throats of twelve Trojan youths at the pyre (18.336–337; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

**Polydamas.** When Polydamas rises in the Trojan assembly, the poem says what it has not said before: “he was Hector's companion, and they were born in the same night, / but the one excelled far in words, the other with the spear” (18.251–252; see [[polydamas]]). His counsel is to go back into the city before dawn, since Achilles will not stay on the plain (18.254–283).

**The answer.** “Looking at him darkly, Hector of the gleaming helmet said: / ‘Polydamas, what you say is no longer pleasing to me’” (18.284–285): the two verses with which he began his answer to Polydamas at the ditch (12.230–231), the second also Paris' answer to Antenor (7.357; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[antenor]]). He scorns the walls, “Have you not had your fill yet of being penned within the towers?” (18.287; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The city of Priam was once called rich in gold and bronze; now its treasures have gone to Phrygia and Maeonia, sold away, “since great Zeus grew angry” (18.288–292; see [[priam]]). But now, he says, “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has granted me / to win glory at the ships, and to pen the Achaeans against the sea” (18.293–294; see [[crooked-counseling]]). The words echo Achilles' own request, which Thetis carried to Zeus, that he “pen the Achaeans in among their ships' sterns and along the sea” (ἀμφʼ ἅλα ἔλσαι Ἀχαιούς, 1.409), and Thetis has just told her son that Zeus has fulfilled it for him (18.74–77). The glory Hector takes for his own is the answer to Achilles' prayer. And Zeus' promise to Hector had a term: power to kill until he came to the ships, “and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.192–194, 11.207–209; 17.453–455). Hera has just sent the sun down (18.239–241; see [[hera]]).

He calls Polydamas “fool” (νήπιε, 18.295; see [[nepios]]) and forbids the Trojans to obey him: “I will not allow it” (18.296). “Come then, let us all do as I say” (18.297) is the verse with which Polydamas put forward his first plan at the ditch, which Hector took (12.75). The orders that follow are Priam's in the Trojan assembly of Book 7, with the army in place of the city: “Now take your supper throughout the army, by companies, / and remember to keep watch, and let each man stay awake” (18.298–299 ≈ 7.370–371; see [[night-watch]]). At dawn they will rouse “sharp Ares beside the hollow ships” (18.304), as he said in his assembly on the plain in Book 8 (18.303–304 = 8.530–531; see [[ares]]). “And early, at daybreak, armed in our armor” is also the verse with which Polydamas began the last part of his plan (18.277), and “the worse it will be for him, if he wants it so” (18.306) answers Polydamas' “the worse for him, if he wants” (18.278): Walter Leaf calls the repetition sarcastic, pointing the contrast of the two conclusions (on 18.303).

**Enyalius.**

{{quote:0291c8d7-8bc6-5ae5-b7ed-4c99cb4d4724}}

Hector is not sure that Achilles has risen (“if it is true”). ξυνός, “common”, makes the god of war a thing both sides share. Leaf renders the verse “the god of battle is impartial and slays him that would slay”, and compares Paris' saying that “victory shifts from man to man” (6.339; on 18.309; see [[enyalius]] and [[victory-shifts]]). Aristotle quotes the first words as a hackneyed maxim for men who are the weaker side (*Rhetoric* 2.21, 1395a15; see [[aristotle-one-omen]]). Hector will not flee from Achilles, he says (18.307); when Achilles comes on in Book 22, trembling seizes him and he flees (22.136–137).

**Fools.** “So Hector spoke, and the Trojans shouted in applause” (18.310), the verse that closed his assembly on the plain in Book 8 (8.542). This time the narrator goes on: “fools; for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits. / For they approved of Hector, who devised evil, / but no one approved of Polydamas, who had devised good counsel” (18.311–313; see [[athena]] and [[boule]]). κακὰ μητιόων, “devising evil”, is said elsewhere of Hera's plot against Heracles (15.27) and of the gods (*Odyssey* 1.234). Hector will remember the night. Waiting for Achilles before the walls in Book 22, he fears that “Polydamas will be the first to heap reproach on me”, who bade him lead the Trojans to the city on the night when Achilles rose, and he did not obey (22.99–103).

**Glory.** Thetis ends her account to Hephaestus with the death of Patroclus: Apollo “killed him among the champions, and given glory to Hector” (18.456; see [[apollo]] and [[hephaestus]]).

His epithets in the book are κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (18.21, 18.131, 18.284), φαίδιμος, “shining” (18.155, 18.175), ἀνδροφόνος, “man-slaying” (18.149), and δῖος, “brilliant” (18.103); he is Πριάμοιο πάϊς and Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (18.154, 18.164), and φλογὶ εἴκελος ἀλκήν, “like a flame in his courage” (18.154; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]], [[phaidimos]], [[man-slaying]], [[brilliant]], [[son-of-priam]] and [[like-a-flame]]).
