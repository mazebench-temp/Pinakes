**The welcome.** While Hephaestus works at his tripods, Thetis comes near:

{{quote:14bc87a2-45d7-5170-8fae-2f4a73b9a7d0}}

The welcome is the hostess's part, and her words are those of Calypso when Hermes comes to her cave in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.87–88, 5.91), with the name and title of the guest changed (see [[visit-scene]] and [[xenia]]). αἰδοίη τε φίλη τε, “respected and dear”, joins two words that Homer likes to pair (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.386; see [[aidos]]), and Leaf compares προτέρω, “farther in”, with the Italian *Avanti!*, “come in” (on 18.386). Hephaestus will greet Thetis with her first two verses (18.424–425). Then she “led her in, brilliant among goddesses” (18.388; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]), and sat her “on a silver-studded throne, / fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet” (18.389–390; see [[throne]], [[footstool]] and [[silver-studded]]), nearly the verses with which Circe seats Odysseus in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 10.314–315, 10.366–367). The guest is seated before her business is asked, as the custom of hospitality requires (see [[guest-gifts]]).

**The call.** “And she called to Hephaestus, famed for his craft, and spoke a word: / ‘Hephaestus, come out here; Thetis has need of you’” (18.391–392; see [[hephaestus]] and [[famed-for-his-craft]]). ὧδε is “hither”, as Leaf takes it against Aristarchus' rule that the word means only “thus” in Homer (on 18.392). Hephaestus answers her: a goddess he fears and respects is in the house, who saved him when he fell (18.394–407; see [[thetis]], [[eurynome]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]), “But you, for now, set fine guest-gifts before her, / while I put away my bellows and all my tools” (18.408–409). The poem does not mention her again.
