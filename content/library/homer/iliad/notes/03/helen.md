**The web.** Iris, in the likeness of Helen's sister-in-law Laodice, finds her at work:

{{quote:5cdf6064-d775-5899-ae3c-f49a4ef09c27}}

Helen makes a picture of the war, and it is her own story: the “trials” (ἀέθλους) that both armies suffer “for her sake”. An ancient commentator saw in the web a model of Homer's own poetry (bT scholia on 3.126–127; see [[helens-web]]). In Book 22 Andromache weaves the same double purple cloth, with flowers (22.440–441). See [[weaving]].

**Longing.** Iris tells her that the war has stopped and that she will be “the dear wife of the one who wins” (3.138).

{{quote:19d518bc-1b73-5700-b47d-d1d286fe3793}}

The longing (ἵμερος) comes from a goddess, and it is for everything she left. She veils herself and goes to the Scaean gates in tears, with her handmaids [[Aethra]] and [[Clymene]]. See [[himeros]].

**The elders.** The old men of Troy see her coming to the tower:

{{quote:476eb6c1-ae21-5073-8975-ce9354c33f2e}}

In the next breath they want her gone: “let her go home in the ships” (3.159). Homer never describes her face. He shows her beauty by its effect on the old men of Troy, and Lessing made the passage his model of how poetry shows beauty (*Laocoön*, 1766, ch. 21; see [[lessing-helen]]). See [[nemesis]], [[no-blame]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]].

**Before Priam.** Priam calls her “dear child” and clears her: “it is the gods who are to blame” (3.164). She does not clear herself:

{{quote:327b71cb-428c-5699-9f2d-72d3c3c29fa7}}

She names Agamemnon, and ends with herself:

{{quote:82cd427d-2ad7-57f8-9b02-06fb851c7091}}

κυνῶπις (“dog-faced”) calls herself shameless, for in Homer the dog is the image of shamelessness (see [[dog-face]]); to Hector she will call herself a “dog” (6.344, 6.356). εἴ ποτʼ ἔην γε (“if that was ever so”) speaks of a past that no longer seems real. Priam uses it of his dead son (24.426). She calls Priam ἑκυρός (“father-in-law”, 3.172) and Agamemnon δαήρ (“brother-in-law”): she has in-laws on both sides of the war (see [[in-laws]]).

**The leaders and the brothers.** She names Odysseus and Ajax, and Idomeneus unasked, whom Menelaus used to entertain “in our house” (3.233). Why Priam needs her help in the war's tenth year is an old question. Most readers take the scene as one of several in Books 2–4 that belong to the war's beginning and are told near its end (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]] and [[the-war-in-small]]). Then she looks for two men she cannot find:

{{quote:70e03702-ee22-558f-a91f-bbf93bc38989}}

She supposes that shame at her disgrace keeps them away. The narrator knows what she does not. See [[castor]] and [[polydeuces]].

**Aphrodite.** After the duel Aphrodite comes to her in the likeness of an old wool-comber from Lacedaemon: Alexander calls her home (3.383–394). Helen knows the goddess by her neck, her breasts and her flashing eyes, and turns on her:

{{quote:a597c362-d6e6-5694-adf8-d958a691e854}}

She tells a goddess to give up Olympus and become Paris' wife or his slave. Aphrodite answers with a threat, to hate her as much as she now loves her and to set Trojans and Danaans against her (3.414–417). Helen, “child of Zeus”, is afraid, and goes in silence (3.418–420). See [[helen-and-aphrodite]].

**Paris.** The goddess sets a chair for her facing Paris, and Helen turns her eyes away:

{{quote:37a8b10e-3acb-5079-b6bd-04bc4ed917db}}

Her wish before Priam was for her own death; this one is for his. Paris answers with “sweet longing”, the words that described her longing for home (3.139, 3.446), and she follows him to bed (3.447). The book that began with her longing for her first husband ends with her following her second.

Book 3 gives every view of her guilt. Priam blames the gods, the elders blame no one and want her gone, Helen blames herself, and Aphrodite's threat shows her compelled. The poem does not choose among them. See [[who-is-to-blame]], [[aitios]] and [[paris]].
