**Before the duel.** Paris steps out as a champion “brandishing two spears tipped with bronze” (δοῦρε δύω κεκορυθμένα χαλκῷ, 3.18), literally “helmeted” with it. Hector holds back the Trojan ranks “gripping his spear by the middle” (μέσσου δουρὸς ἑλών, 3.78), the spear held level across the front of the line, and the armies sit down. At rest the men lean on their shields, “and their long spears are fixed beside them” in the ground (3.135). Iris and Idaeus both say that the two will fight “with their long spears” (μακρῇς ἐγχείῃσι, 3.137, 3.254), and the lot decides “which of the two should first let fly his bronze spear” (3.317; see [[lots]]). When Paris arms he takes up one spear, “a strong spear that fitted his grip” (3.338).

**The throws.** Paris throws first. His “long-shadowed spear” (δολιχόσκιον ἔγχος; see [[long-shadowed]]) strikes the shield of Menelaus, “but the bronze did not break through, and its point (αἰχμή) was bent back in the strong shield” (3.346–349). Menelaus prays and throws, and his spear goes through the shield and the corselet and cuts the tunic beside the flank, but Paris swerves “and avoided black doom” (3.355–360). The sword breaks next, and Menelaus complains to Zeus that “my spear flew from my grip in vain, and I did not hit him” (3.367–368). After the helmet comes away he springs back “eager to kill him with his bronze spear” (3.379–380), and Aphrodite takes Paris away. See [[shield]], [[corselet]] and [[single-combat]].

**Afterwards.** Helen tells Paris that he used to boast he was the better man “in your strength and your hands and your spear”, and warns him that he may “soon be brought down by his spear” (3.431, 3.436).

In Paris' simile of the axe that goes “through a beam” (διὰ δουρός, 3.61), δόρυ keeps its old sense of “timber”, as in the rotting timbers of the ships in Book 2 (2.135).
