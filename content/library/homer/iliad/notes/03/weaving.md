**Helen's web.**

{{quote:5cdf6064-d775-5899-ae3c-f49a4ef09c27}}

Helen is weaving a double-folded purple cloth and “working into it” (ἐνέπασσεν) the many ἄεθλοι, the “trials, struggles”, of the Trojans and the Achaeans, which they suffer for her sake at the hands of Ares. ἐμπάσσω, “sprinkle in”, is used of figured weaving, the pattern worked into the cloth as it grows. The verse that names the figures in her web, “of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans” (3.127), comes back four lines later in Iris' invitation to go and see the men themselves (3.131). She leaves the woven war for the real one. An ancient commentator saw in the web an image of the poem, and modern readers have followed him (see [[helens-web]] and [[loom]]).

**The wool-worker.** Aphrodite comes to Helen on the wall “in the likeness of an aged woman, a wool-comber (εἰροκόμῳ), who, when she lived in Lacedaemon, used to work fine wool (εἴρια) for her, and loved her most of all” (3.386–388). The goddess of love takes the shape of a servant who prepared the wool for her mistress's weaving, a figure from the house in Sparta that Helen left (see [[divine-epiphany]]).

**The handmaids.** When Helen comes into Paris' house, “the handmaids turned quickly to their tasks” (ἐπὶ ἔργα, 3.422), ἔργα being the usual word for the women's spinning and weaving.

**Weaving words.** Antenor remembers how Menelaus and Odysseus spoke in the Trojan assembly, “when they began to weave their words and counsels before all” (μύθους καὶ μήδεα πᾶσιν ὕφαινον, 3.212). The verb of Helen's web is used of speech. See [[assembly]] and [[scepter]].
