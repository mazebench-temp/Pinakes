Menelaus asks for “two lambs, a white ram and a black ewe, for Earth and the Sun”, and the Achaeans will bring “another for Zeus” (3.103–104). The Greek says ἄρνʼ, “two lambs”, and gives them colors and sexes by the adjectives, ἕτερον λευκόν, ἑτέρην δὲ μέλαιναν, “one white (male), the other black (female)”. The poem does not match them to the powers. The white male suits the Sun and the black female the Earth, the kind of match between victim and god that later Greek practice observed. The Trojans provide the victims for Earth and the Sun, and the Achaeans the victim for Zeus; the poem does not say why the gods are divided so.

Hector's heralds go to the city for the lambs, and Talthybius to the ships for “a lamb” (3.116–120). The heralds carry the two through the city with the wine, as “the offerings for the trusty oaths of the gods” (3.245–246). At the oath Agamemnon cuts hair from their heads (3.273), and then their throats (3.292). They are laid on the ground, “gasping, their life failing, for the bronze had taken away their strength” (3.293–294). Afterwards Priam puts his lambs in the chariot and takes them back to Troy (3.310). The poem says nothing of the Achaeans' lamb. See [[oath]], [[knife]] and [[sacrifice-type-scene]].

In Book 1 lambs were offerings to be accepted, “the savor of lambs and unblemished goats” that might win Apollo over (1.66). The lambs of Book 3 are given to no god. They stand for the man who will break the oath. The ram to which Priam likens Odysseus is another word, ἀρνειός (3.197; see [[ram]]).
