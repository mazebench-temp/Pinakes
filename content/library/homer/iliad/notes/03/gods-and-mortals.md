summary: In Book 3 a mortal woman is compared with the immortal goddesses and then quarrels with one. Helen tells Aphrodite to give up the paths of the gods and become a mortal man's wife or slave; the goddess answers that she can make her hated and destroy her.
refs: [3.158, 3.308, 3.381, 3.406-409, 3.414-417]
passages:
  - 3.154-160 | The old men on the tower
  - 3.396-420 | Helen and Aphrodite
---

**Like the goddesses.** The old men on the tower say that Helen is “terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on” (3.158). She is also a daughter of Zeus (Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, 3.199, 3.418; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]). The likeness does not make her free. Later in the book a real goddess shows her how far apart they are.

**The quarrel.** When Aphrodite comes to take her to Paris' bed, Helen knows her by her beauty (3.396–398) and answers her as no mortal in the Iliad has yet answered a god.

{{quote:a597c362-d6e6-5694-adf8-d958a691e854}}

If Aphrodite loves Paris so much, let her go and sit beside him, “and give up the paths of the gods, and never again turn your feet back to Olympus”, and serve him until he makes her his wife or his slave. Helen imagines the goddess living a mortal woman's life, which is her own. The goddess' answer states the difference.

{{quote:79d9e453-2269-5f6e-a419-69b8f47a58ee}}

Aphrodite can turn her love into hate, raise hatreds between the two armies, and let Helen perish. Helen “was afraid”, and goes (3.418–420). A goddess can make a mortal's life or end it; a mortal can only refuse, and then obey.

**Ease and effort.** Aphrodite saves Paris “very easily, as a god can” (ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός, 3.381). Priam, who cannot bear to watch his son fight, says that Zeus and the other immortals know which of the two is fated to die (3.308–309). What men fear and cannot know, the gods know and do without effort. See [[divine-intervention]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]].
