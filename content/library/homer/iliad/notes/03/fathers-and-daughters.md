**Daughters of Zeus.** Helen is “child of Zeus” (Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, 3.199, 3.418) and “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (κούρη Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, 3.426), and Aphrodite is “daughter of Zeus” (Διὸς θυγάτηρ, 3.374). The quarrel between them in Helen's chamber is a quarrel between two daughters of the same father, and the goddess wins it by threat (3.413–420). See [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]].

**A father-in-law's child.** Priam calls Helen “dear child” (φίλον τέκος, 3.162, 3.192), and she calls him “dear father-in-law” (3.172; see [[in-laws]]). In her lament over Hector in the last book she remembers that he was always gentle to her, “like a father” (24.770).

**A daughter left behind.** In her first answer to Priam, Helen wishes she had died before she followed his son, “leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen, and my darling daughter” (παῖδά τε τηλυγέτην, 3.174–175). The daughter is not named in the Iliad; the *Odyssey* calls her Hermione (*Odyssey* 4.14). The meaning of τηλυγέτη is disputed: “darling” or “late-born, only”. See [[darling]].

**Named for their fathers.** Laodice is “most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (3.124), and Helen's handmaid Aethra is named as “daughter of Pittheus” (3.144; see [[aethra]] and [[pittheus]]). See [[fathers-and-daughters]].
