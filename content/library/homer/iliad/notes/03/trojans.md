**Clamor and silence.** The book opens by setting the two armies side by side:

{{quote:5676e478-163f-51ba-8f88-54e6329cc2fe}}

The Trojans come on "with clamor and with cries, like birds", like the cranes that fly to Oceanus to make war on the Pygmies; the Achaeans come on in silence, "breathing fury", eager to stand by one another (see [[cranes-and-pygmies]] and [[silence]]). The contrast returns in Book 4, where the Trojans' cry is like the bleating of ewes, because their army is made of many peoples with many tongues, while the Achaeans advance in silence (4.422–438). Their epithets in this book are "proud" (ἀγέρωχοι, 3.36; see [[proud]]) and, four times, "horse-taming" (ἱππόδαμοι, 3.127, 3.131, 3.251, 3.343; see [[tamer-of-horses]]).

**Cowards?** Hector ends his rebuke of Paris with a charge against his own people: "the Trojans are great cowards" (δειδήμονες), or they would have stoned him long ago (3.56–57; see [[tunic-of-stone]]). They have not punished Paris, and they do not give Helen back. But by the end of the book they hate him.

**The hope of peace.** When Menelaus accepts the duel, "the Achaeans and the Trojans rejoiced, hoping for an end to miserable war" (3.111–112). Both armies get down from their chariots and lay their armor on the ground, "and there was little ground between them" (3.113–115). At the sacrifice the heralds give the lambs' hair to the best of the Trojans and the Achaeans (3.274), and "Achaeans and Trojans alike" pray the same curse on whoever first breaks the oaths (3.297–301) and the same prayer before the lots (3.318–323). For a few hundred lines the two peoples pray as one. See [[oath]] and [[the-oaths-sworn]].

**The terms.** If Paris dies, the Trojans must give back Helen and the possessions and pay the Argives "satisfaction" (τιμή), and if they refuse, Agamemnon will fight on for the penalty (ποινή, 3.284–291; see [[poine]]). At the end he declares the victory and demands the terms from "Trojans and Dardanians and allies" (3.456–460). The book ends before they answer. In Book 4 the answer comes from their side: Pandarus, leader of the Trojans of Zeleia, breaks the truce with an arrow (4.104–126).

**Hateful to them all.** While Menelaus ranges through the throng looking for Paris, no one will show him:

{{quote:f33d8b09-db86-5ab5-9ca6-26eb273ec9dc}}

The Trojans fight for Paris' cause, not out of love for him (φιλότητι, 3.453; see [[like-black-doom]]). The Trojan women have their own part in the book ([[trojan-women]]).
