{{quote:f054d837-abd8-59e4-ab3a-b67b9633b0e2}}

Menelaus accepts Paris' challenge with a condition: Priam must come and swear the oaths himself, for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted (3.105–107). The reason is a maxim. The minds (φρένες) of younger men are always ἠερέθονται, “flighty”, literally “fluttering in the air”; but in whatever an old man takes part, he looks “both before and after” (ἅμα πρόσσω καὶ ὀπίσσω), so that things turn out far the best for both sides.

In Book 1 the same words named what Agamemnon lacked: he “does not know how to look both before and after” (1.343). Menelaus, his brother, now asks for it in the enemy's king, and he trusts the old man's judgment to serve both armies at once (μετʼ ἀμφοτέροισι). The foresight is needed because the oath binds the future: whoever breaks it will pay (3.298–301; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

In Book 3 ὀπίσσω by itself means “hereafter” twice: the old men do not want Helen left as a grief to them and to their children “after us” (3.160), and Helen fears that the Trojan women will blame her “hereafter” (3.411). The phrase joins what lies ahead and what lies behind; which Homer imagined as ahead and which as behind is debated. Bernard Knox argued that for early Greek the future lies behind a man, unseen, and the past before him (*Backing into the Future*, 1994). See [[foresight]], [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[phrenes]].
