---
summary: The epithet names Odysseus three times in Book 3: when Helen identifies him, when Antenor remembers him rising to speak, and when he rises for the oath.
---

Helen names Odysseus with his patronymic and his epithet, and then explains the epithet: he “knows all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels” (3.200–202; [[son-of-laertes]], [[craggy]]). Antenor's memory of the embassy to Troy puts the epithet to the test:

{{quote:1400c24c-8075-5e4a-b7ee-66655be59e11}}

The man of many wiles looks like a fool until he speaks, and then his words come like the snowflakes of winter (3.221–224; [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). At the oath, “Odysseus of many wiles” rises beside Agamemnon (3.268), and with Hector he measures the ground and shakes the lots (3.314–316), where he is “brilliant Odysseus” ([[brilliant]]).
