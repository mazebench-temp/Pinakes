summary: Two goddesses come to Helen in borrowed shapes, and she sees through the second. Paris is called a seducer, Odysseus is a master of tricks who looked like a fool, Antenor vouches that Helen's word is exact, and the oath calls down punishment on false swearers.

**Disguises.** Two goddesses come to Helen in mortal shapes. Iris comes “in the likeness of her sister-in-law” Laodice (εἰδομένη, 3.122), and Helen goes to the wall without question. Aphrodite comes in the likeness of an old wool-worker from Lacedaemon (ἐεισαμένη, 3.386–389), and Helen knows her by her neck, her breasts and her eyes (3.396–398). In Book 2 Odysseus knew Athena by her voice and Hector knew Iris by her word (2.182, 2.807); Helen knows a goddess by her beauty, and says what she sees: “Strange one, why do you long to deceive me (ἠπεροπεύειν) like this?” (3.399). She calls the goddess δολοφρονέουσα, “with treachery in your heart” (3.405). See [[eidos]] and [[eperopeuo]].

**Seducer and trickster.** Hector calls Paris ἠπεροπευτά, “seducer” (3.39), the deceiver who led another man's wife away. Helen names Odysseus as the man who “knows all kinds of tricks (δόλους) and shrewd counsels” (3.202). Antenor's memory shows the trickster's art at work: Odysseus looked like “a man who knows nothing”, surly and a fool, until he spoke (3.216–224; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]). Appearance deceived the Trojans, and the words did not.

**True words.** Antenor confirms Helen's description from his own knowledge: “Lady, truly this word you have spoken is exact” (νημερτές, 3.204). Truth here rests on a witness who was present, as the Catalogue's rests on the Muses (2.484–486; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]]).

**False oaths.** The oath calls on the powers below the earth who punish “whoever has sworn a false oath” (ἐπίορκον, 3.279). A lie sworn before the gods is paid for after death. In Book 4 the Trojans break the oath, and Agamemnon declares that Zeus will not help liars (4.235). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].
