The book's plan is a human one. Paris proposes the duel, Hector announces it, Menelaus accepts, and both kings swear to its terms. The gods are called only as witnesses (3.276–280). Then they act, and undo it.

**A longing.** Iris comes to Helen “in the likeness of her sister-in-law” Laodice (3.121–122) and tells her of the duel: “So saying, the goddess put sweet longing in her heart for her former husband, and her city, and her parents” (3.139–140). The longing is sent, and it is Helen's own: she goes at once, weeping. The narrator names no god who sent Iris, and does not say why she went. See [[himeros]].

**A refusal.** The armies pray at the oath that whoever first breaks it may die with his children, “but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). The prayer at the lots and Menelaus' prayer before his spear-cast go the same way. When his sword shatters, Menelaus blames Zeus aloud: “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365). See [[prayer]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].

**A rescue.** Menelaus has Paris by the helmet and is dragging him to the Achaeans.

{{quote:88c3bd5a-ac1a-5264-a834-1d5b62f3b533}}

The rescue is told in the construction that marks a story turned by a god: “and now he would have dragged him off … had not Aphrodite … been quick to see it” (καί νύ κεν … εἰ μὴ ἄρʼ, 3.373–374), as the army's homecoming against fate was stopped by Hera in Book 2 (2.155–156). She breaks the strap, and then snatches him away “very easily, as a god can”, hides him in thick mist, and sets him down in his fragrant chamber (3.380–382). See [[divine-rescue]] and [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]].

**A command.** Aphrodite then comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-worker from Lacedaemon (3.386–389). Helen knows her by her beauty and refuses (3.396–412). The goddess threatens her (3.414–417), “and Helen, child of Zeus, was afraid”, and went, “and the divinity led the way” (3.418–420). Athena let Achilles choose to obey (1.216–218); Aphrodite gives Helen no choice. See [[helen-and-aphrodite]] and [[daimon]].

**A god on each side.** Paris explains his defeat by the same means: Menelaus won “with Athena's help”, and “we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440). The narrator has not shown Athena in the duel. Paris' claim may be only his excuse, but in Book 4 Athena does stand by Menelaus, turning aside the arrow of Pandarus (4.127–133). See [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].
