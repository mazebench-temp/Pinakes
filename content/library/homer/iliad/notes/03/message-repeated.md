summary: Paris' proposal is passed on from speaker to speaker, by Hector to the armies, by Iris to Helen and by Idaeus to Priam, and each changes it for his hearer; Agamemnon fixes it in the oath, and at the end claims what the oath added.

**Paris to Hector.** Paris proposes the duel in his own voice: “make the other Trojans and all the Achaeans sit down, and set me in the middle with Menelaus, dear to Ares” (3.68–69).

**Hector to the armies.** Hector passes it on as “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose” (3.87), with the changes that delivery requires. “Make the other Trojans and all the Achaeans sit down” becomes “He bids the other Trojans and all the Achaeans lay aside their fine armor on the bountiful earth” (3.88–89), and “set me in the middle” becomes “himself, in the middle … to fight alone” (οἴους, 3.90–91). The next two verses are Paris' own (3.92–93 = 3.71–72). Hector stops at the friendship and the oaths (3.94), and leaves out where each side will live (3.74–75).

**Iris to Helen.** The goddess tells the listener what concerns her. The two men “will fight with their long spears for you (περὶ σεῖο), and you will be called the dear wife of the one who wins” (3.136–138). The possessions drop out, and Helen hears herself named as the prize.

**Idaeus to Priam.** The herald uses Iris' verses, but for Priam the prize is “the woman” (ἀμφὶ γυναικί), and the possessions come back: “to the one who wins, the woman and the possessions would go” (3.253–255). Then he repeats the end of Paris' proposal with its persons changed. Paris said “let the rest of you … live in deep-soiled Troy, and let them go back” (ναίοιτε … νεέσθων, 3.73–74); the Trojan herald says “the rest of us … would live in deep-soiled Troy, and they would go back” (ναίοιμεν … νέονται, 3.256–257).

**The oath.** Agamemnon puts the terms into the oath, but changes them: “If Alexander kills Menelaus … but if fair-haired Menelaus kills Alexander”, and he adds a satisfaction to be paid (3.281–287; see [[oath]]).

**The claim.** At the end Agamemnon claims the victory and demands what the oath added. His last verse and a half repeat the words of the oath, “and pay such satisfaction as is fitting, one that will be remembered among men who are yet to come” (3.459–460; 3.286–287). The proposal has passed through five mouths, and it ends as a demand that no Trojan answers (see [[single-combat]]).
