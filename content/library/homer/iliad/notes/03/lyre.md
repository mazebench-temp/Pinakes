summary: Hector names Paris' lyre, κίθαρις, with the gifts of Aphrodite, his hair and his looks, as things that will not help him against Menelaus (3.54–55).
grc: [κίθαρις]
---

{{quote:d10cf66b-61cf-5306-8071-257cfecad967}}

κίθαρις is Homer's other word for the lyre, beside φόρμιγξ, and can also mean the playing of it; its verb κιθαρίζω is used of playing the φόρμιγξ (18.569–570). In Hector's taunt the lyre stands with Paris' hair and looks among “the gifts of Aphrodite”, the accomplishments of a lover, of no use to a man lying in the dust. Paris answers that the gifts of the gods are not to be thrown aside (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[no-help-to-you]]). After the duel Aphrodite describes him in his chamber as if he were on his way to a dance (3.393–394; see [[dance]]).

The one hero who plays the lyre in the Iliad is Achilles, who sings to it of the famous deeds of men while he keeps out of the war (9.186–189). Paris' lyre is not said to sing of anything.
