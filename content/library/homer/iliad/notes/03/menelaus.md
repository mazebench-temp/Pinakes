**The lion.** When Paris steps out in front of the Trojans, Menelaus rejoices “as a lion rejoices when he comes upon a great carcass” (3.23), “for he thought he would take vengeance on the wrongdoer” (3.28). ἀλείτης (“wrongdoer, offender”) comes from ἀλιταίνω (“offend, sin”). He leaps from his chariot in his armor, and Paris shrinks back into the crowd “in fear of the son of Atreus” (3.29–37). See [[lion-on-a-carcass]].

**The terms.** When Hector brings Paris' offer, Menelaus answers for the Achaeans:

{{quote:9969c943-0010-57de-a9b5-2730ebdba8c8}}

He calls the war ἐμῆς ἔριδος, “my quarrel”, and offers to die if that will end it. Then he sets the ritual: two lambs from the Trojans for Earth and the Sun and one from the Achaeans for Zeus, and Priam himself to swear, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted”, since the minds of younger men are flighty and an old man “looks both before and after” (3.103–110; see [[before-and-after]]). His distrust is borne out in Book 4, when the truce is broken from the Trojan side by [[Pandarus]]' arrow. See [[oath]].

**Remembered by Antenor.** Menelaus and Odysseus came to Troy once as envoys. Standing, Menelaus rose above Odysseus by his broad shoulders (3.210), and he spoke like this:

{{quote:b6a818ca-956d-5ba8-b88b-4c02db0ff479}}

ἐπιτροχάδην (“fluently, running on”), παῦρα … λιγέως (“few words, but clear”): the style of a man who says what is needed and stops. Ancient critics made it Homer's model of the plain style (see [[three-styles-of-speech]]). See also [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[odysseus]].

**The duel.** Paris' spear bends back on his shield (3.346–349). Menelaus throws second, with a prayer:

{{quote:d5f22761-0101-592a-98d1-97e34d0d6970}}

He asks vengeance on “the man who first wronged me”, and gives a reason that concerns all men to come: the punishment should make anyone shudder to wrong a host who has received him in friendship. The prayer states the Achaeans' case in one sentence. In Book 13 he will charge the Trojans again with defying Zeus, the god of guests (13.624–625). See [[xenia]] and [[philotes]].

His spear goes through Paris' shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, but Paris swerves (3.355–360). His sword shatters on the ridge of Paris' helmet (3.361–363), and he cries out:

{{quote:29b73f9e-837c-549b-9a51-284d2c2b9adc}}

The man who prayed to Zeus now blames him: ὀλοώτερος, “more baneful”. He seizes Paris by the helmet and drags him toward the Achaeans, and he “would have … won untold glory” if Aphrodite had not broken the chin strap (3.369–376). He springs at Paris again with his spear, but the goddess has carried him off (3.377–382). See [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]].

**The wild beast.** The book that began with Menelaus rejoicing like a lion ends with him hunting in vain: “the son of Atreus ranged through the throng like a wild beast, to see if he could catch sight of godlike Alexander” (3.449–450; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]). No Trojan would have hidden Paris from him (3.451–454). Agamemnon claims the victory for him (3.455–460); Paris explains it as Athena's doing (3.439).

ἀρηΐφιλος (“dear to Ares”) is his epithet thirteen times in the book (see [[dear-to-ares]]). He is also “good at the war cry” (3.96), “fair-haired” (3.284, 3.434) and “warlike” (ἀρήϊος, 3.339). To Iris, Priam and Helen herself he is Helen's “former husband” (3.140, 3.163, 3.429).
