---
summary: In Book 3 the θυμός is where the Achaeans are eager to stand by one another, where Menelaus' sorrow reaches, where Iris puts longing and Aphrodite stirs Helen, and where Helen keeps her griefs; the lambs of the oath gasp it out.
passages:
  - 3.8-9 | The Achaeans come on in silence
  - 3.139-140 | Sweet longing
  - 3.292-294 | The lambs die
  - 3.395-398 | Aphrodite stirs Helen's heart
---

The word comes seven times in Book 3, and most of its senses appear.

**Eagerness.** The Achaeans advance in silence, “eager in their hearts (ἐν θυμῷ μεμαῶτες) to stand by one another” (3.9), against the clamor of the Trojans (see [[silence]]).

**Feeling.** Sorrow comes to Menelaus' θυμός (3.97–98; see [[algos]]). Iris puts “sweet longing” in Helen's θυμός (3.139; see [[himeros]]). Helen tells Aphrodite she has griefs past measure in her θυμός (3.412; see [[achos]]), and Paris asks Helen not to rebuke his θυμός with harsh reproaches (3.438; see [[oneidos]]).

**Stirred by a god.** Aphrodite's summons to Paris' chamber “stirred the heart in her breast” (θυμὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ὄρινε, 3.395). The same words were used of Agamemnon's speech stirring the army to flight in Book 2 (2.142). Here the stirring meets resistance: Helen knows the goddess and refuses her (3.396–412).

**Life.** At the oath Agamemnon cuts the lambs' throats, and they lie on the ground gasping, “their life failing” (θυμοῦ δευομένους), “for the bronze had taken away their strength” (3.293–294; see [[menos]]). The victims show what the oath threatens: whoever breaks it will lose his life as they lose theirs (3.298–301; see [[oath]]).
