**Helen's names.** The word gathers the relations of the book around Helen. Iris, in the likeness of her sister-in-law, calls her “dear bride” (νύμφα φίλη, 3.130) and tells her she will be called “the dear wife” of the man who wins (φίλη … ἄκοιτις, 3.138). Priam calls her “dear child” (φίλον τέκος, 3.162, 3.192) and invites her to see her kin and her friends (φίλους, 3.163). She answers “dear father-in-law” (φίλε ἑκυρέ, 3.172; see [[in-laws]]). Her brothers, whom she looks for on the plain, already lie buried “in their own dear native land” (φίλῃ ἐν πατρίδι γαίῃ, 3.244).

**Love given and threatened.** Antenor “welcomed” the envoys in his halls (φίλησα, 3.207), the verb of hospitality (see [[xenia]]). The old wool-worker whose shape Aphrodite takes “loved her most of all” (μάλιστα δέ μιν φιλέεσκε, 3.388). Helen asks whether the goddess has some other mortal “who is dear to you” in Phrygia or Maeonia (3.402), and Aphrodite answers with a threat:

{{quote:79d9e453-2269-5f6e-a419-69b8f47a58ee}}

“I may … hate you as much as now I love you beyond measure” (ἀπεχθήρω ὡς νῦν ἔκπαγλʼ ἐφίλησα). A goddess' love can turn to hatred, and Helen obeys (3.418–420).

**His own.** Paris' heart is struck with fear when he sees Menelaus (φίλον ἦτορ, 3.31), and Priam cannot bear to watch “my dear son” fight (φίλον υἱόν, 3.307).

The noun φιλότης, “friendship, love” (3.73, 3.94, 3.256, 3.323, 3.354, 3.441, 3.445, 3.453), has an entry of its own: see [[philotes]].
