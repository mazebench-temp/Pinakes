**An exact word.** Helen names Odysseus for Priam as the man who “knows all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels” (3.202), and Antenor answers: “Lady, truly this word (ἔπος) you have spoken is exact” (νημερτές, 3.204). νημερτής is “unerring”, from the negative νη- and ἁμαρτάνω, “miss the mark”. He knows because he received Odysseus as a guest (3.205–208; see [[xenia]]).

**Two ways of speaking.** Antenor then describes the two envoys in the Trojan assembly. Menelaus spoke fluently, “few words but very clear, since he was not a man of many words nor one who missed the point” (οὐδʼ ἀφαμαρτοεπής, 3.214–215). The adjective, “missing the mark in words”, is built from ἔπος and ἁμαρτάνω, the verb that is negated in νημερτής: Helen's word hits the mark, and so did Menelaus'. Odysseus stood with his eyes on the ground and the scepter stiff, until he let his great voice go from his chest “and words (ἔπεα) like the snowflakes of winter” (3.221–222).

Few clear words against words falling thick as snow: the one speaker says just enough, the other overwhelms. See [[snowflakes-of-winter]], [[mythos]] and [[odysseus]].

**Other words.** The book's first speech is introduced with “shaming words” (αἰσχροῖς ἐπέεσσιν, 3.38; see [[aischros]]), and the old men on the tower speak “winged words” to one another, softly (3.155; see [[winged-words]]).

## Outside Homer

Ancient critics took the two envoys, with Nestor from Book 1, as models of the three styles of oratory: Menelaus the plain, Nestor the middle, Odysseus the grand (Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 12.10.64; Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 6.14.7; see [[three-styles-of-speech]]).
