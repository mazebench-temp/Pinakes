summary: In Book 3 persuasion is quick among men and forced by a goddess. Hector's rebuke moves Paris to fight, heralds and companions obey at once, Iris sends Helen to the wall with a longing, and Aphrodite, refused, gets her way by threat.

**Among men.** Persuasion among the men of Book 3 is quick. Hector's rebuke shames Paris into offering the duel (3.38–75), and Hector “rejoiced greatly when he heard his words” and carries them to the armies (3.76). The armies stop fighting at Agamemnon's shout (3.84–85). Talthybius “did not disobey” Agamemnon (3.120), and Priam's companions “quickly obeyed” (3.260; see [[peitho]]). Nobody argues.

**A goddess's longing.** Iris does not argue with Helen either. She tells her of the duel, puts a sweet longing in her heart, and Helen goes at once (3.139–145; see [[himeros]]).

**A goddess's threat.** Aphrodite's summons is the one that is refused. Helen sees through her disguise, rebukes her, and says she will not go to Paris' bed (3.399–412). Aphrodite does not persuade; she threatens.

{{quote:79d9e453-2269-5f6e-a419-69b8f47a58ee}}

“So she spoke, and Helen, child of Zeus, was afraid, and she went” (3.418–419). The line has the form of two in Book 1, where Chryses is afraid of Agamemnon and obeys (1.33) and Hera is afraid of Zeus and sits in silence (1.568; see [[deos]]). Here the narrator does not say that she obeyed. She goes in silence, “and the divinity led the way” (3.420). In Book 1 Achilles obeyed Athena because it is better to obey the gods (1.216–218); Helen goes because she is afraid. See [[divine-intervention]] and [[silence]].
