In Books 1 and 2 δῶρα were things that pass between men and gods and between men: offerings, prizes, compensation. In Book 3 they are what a god has given a man, and they cannot be given back.

{{quote:d10cf66b-61cf-5306-8071-257cfecad967}}

Hector names them in his rebuke: Paris' lyre, his hair and his looks are “the gifts of Aphrodite” (τά τε δῶρʼ Ἀφροδίτης), and they will not help him when he lies in the dust. Paris does not deny them.

{{quote:84c6e108-9fd6-59f3-a330-14bcae59dfc8}}

“Do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite. The glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside, whatever they themselves may give; no man could take them by his own will.” A gift between men creates an obligation and can be refused, as Achilles will refuse Agamemnon's in Book 9. A god's gift of beauty or strength becomes part of the man, and it cannot be sent back. Paris' argument makes his beauty a fact, not a fault.
