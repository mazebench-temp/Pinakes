---
summary: Desire comes into Book 3 at its end. After the duel Paris tells Helen that never before, not even on the island of Cranae, has desire so wrapped his mind.
passages:
  - 3.437-447 | Paris answers Helen
---

The noun occurs once in Book 3 and the verb once, both in Paris' last speech. Helen has told him to go and challenge Menelaus again, and then told him not to, or he will die (3.428–436). He answers her scorn in two lines. Menelaus won this time “with Athena's help”; another time he will win, “for we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440). Then he turns to bed.

{{quote:e4a4c8f4-648d-5cde-9fe8-3b71699aad7e}}

Desire has “wrapped his mind” (ἔρως φρένας ἀμφεκάλυψεν, 3.442), more than on the first night, when he took her from Lacedaemon and lay with her on the island of Cranae (3.443–445; see [[cranae]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The last line joins the verb of desire and the word of longing: “as I love you (ἔραμαι) now, and sweet longing (ἵμερος) takes hold of me” (see [[himeros]]). Earlier in the book Paris called the gifts of Aphrodite that Hector threw in his face ἐρατά, “lovely”, that is, desirable (3.64).

The scene has a double among the gods. In Book 14 Hera comes to Zeus on Ida wearing Aphrodite's band. “Desire wrapped his shrewd mind” (14.294), and he invites her to bed in words close to Paris': he recites his past loves and ends with Paris' last line, “as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.328 = 3.446). The man who lost the duel and the king of the gods speak the same words of desire, and both meetings are arranged by a goddess, Aphrodite in Book 3 and Hera, with Aphrodite's band, in Book 14 (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).

While the two lie down on the corded bed, Menelaus ranges through the throng like a wild beast, looking for him (3.448–450). See [[philotes]] and [[husbands-and-wives]].
