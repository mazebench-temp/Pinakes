**Iris.** Iris comes “in the likeness (εἰδομένη) of her sister-in-law”, Laodice, “most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (3.122–124). She finds Helen at her loom, stands near and speaks (3.125–138). Nothing shows that Helen knows her. The goddess “put sweet longing in her heart” (3.139), and Helen goes to the wall weeping (3.139–145). The god works through the mortal's heart, unrecognized.

**Aphrodite.** Aphrodite comes to the wall in the likeness of an aged wool-comber who had served Helen in Lacedaemon and “loved her most of all” (3.386–389), a figure chosen to be trusted, as the Dream chose Nestor's shape in Book 2 (2.20–22). She plucks Helen's robe and gives Paris' summons. Helen knows her:

{{quote:df8d2ca8-fead-5edf-bcf7-78e1a032b790}}

She knows the goddess of love by her beauty, and she is amazed (θάμβησεν), as Achilles was when he turned and saw Athena (1.199). Her words are no reverent answer. She calls the goddess δαιμονίη, “strange one” ([[strange-one]]), accuses her of deceit, and tells her to give up the paths of the gods and sit by Paris herself (3.399–412). The goddess answers “in anger” with a threat, “Do not provoke me, wretched woman” (3.413–417), and Helen obeys. The epiphany does not end with a departure: “the divinity led the way” (ἦρχε δὲ δαίμων, 3.420), and in Paris' chamber the goddess carries a chair for her and sets it down facing Alexander (3.424–425; see [[stool]]).
