summary: The duel is fought to decide whose wife Helen is. The book names her former and her present husband and the in-laws of both houses, and Helen throws at Aphrodite the two fates of a woman in a man's house, “his wife, or his slave”.

**Whose wife.** Iris tells Helen that she “will be called the dear wife (φίλη … ἄκοιτις) of the one who wins” (3.138). Hector reproaches Paris with having the “flourishing wife” (θαλερὴν παράκοιτιν) of a man he dares not face (3.53), a woman he carried off as the bride of a people of spearmen (3.49), and he wishes that Paris had “died unmarried” (ἄγαμος, 3.40; see [[privative-adjectives]]). Menelaus is her “former husband” (πρότερόν τε πόσιν, 3.163; πρότερος πόσις, 3.429), and Paris is “the husband of Helen of the lovely hair” (3.329). At the end “his wife followed with him” (ἄκοιτις, 3.447) to his bed (see [[bed]]).

**In-laws.** Book 3 speaks the language of the family. Iris comes in the likeness of Helen's sister-in-law (γαλόῳ, 3.122), the wife of Antenor's son. Helen calls Priam “dear father-in-law” (φίλε ἑκυρέ, 3.172) and names Agamemnon as her “brother-in-law” (δαήρ, 3.180), and Priam calls her to see “your former husband, and your kin by marriage and your friends” (3.163). She has left in Sparta her bridal chamber, her kinsmen, her daughter and the companions of her youth (3.174–175).

**Wife or slave.** Helen tells Aphrodite to go and sit beside Paris herself, “until he makes you his wife, or his slave” (ἢ ἄλοχον ποιήσεται ἢ ὅ γε δούλην, 3.409). The two conditions set apart in Book 1, the wedded wife and the woman held by a master, are the two fates she wishes on the goddess (see [[captive-women]]).

**The curse.** The curse of the oath ends with the wives: “and may their wives be subdued by others” (ἄλοχοι δʼ ἄλλοισι δαμεῖεν, 3.301). The penalty for breaking the oath is the wrong with which the war began, a man's wife taken by another. See [[oath]], [[philotes]] and [[helen]].
