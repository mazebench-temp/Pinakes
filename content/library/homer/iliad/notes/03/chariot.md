When Menelaus sees Paris, “at once he leapt from his chariot (ἐξ ὀχέων) to the ground in his armor” (3.29). ὄχεα, used only in the plural, is one of the words for the chariot. At the truce the men hold their horses in the ranks and get down (3.113).

Priam goes to the oath by chariot. He has his companions yoke the horses, mounts, “and pulled back the reins” (κατὰ δʼ ἡνία τεῖνεν ὀπίσσω), and Antenor steps up beside him “into the beautiful chariot” (περικαλλέα … δίφρον); they drive through the Scaean gates to the plain (3.259–263), and step down “from the chariot” (ἐξ ἵππων, 3.265, where “the horses” stand for the whole team). The old king drives himself, with his counselor beside him. When the oath is sworn he puts the lambs in the δίφρος, and nearly the same two verses take the pair back to Ilios (3.311–312; 3.261–262). See [[priam]], [[antenor]] and [[horses]].

δίφρος, the car of the chariot, is also a seat. At 3.424 it is the one Aphrodite carries for Helen (see [[stool]]).
