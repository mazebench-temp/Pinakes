The scepter here is the speaker's staff, as when Achilles held it in the assembly of Book 1 (1.234–245), and Odysseus in the assembly of Book 2 (2.279). An ordinary speaker moved it as he spoke. Odysseus “would not move the scepter backward or forward” (σκῆπτρον δʼ οὔτʼ ὀπίσω οὔτε προπρηνὲς ἐνώμα) but held it ἀστεμφές, “unmoving, stiff”, with his eyes fixed on the ground, and seemed ἀΐδρις and ἄφρων, “a man who knows nothing” and “a fool” (3.217–220). The iterative verbs (στάσκεν, ἴδεσκε, ἔχεσκεν) say that this was his way each time he rose. Then the voice came, and “words like the snowflakes of winter” (see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]).

In Book 2 Odysseus took Agamemnon's scepter and used it as a king's weapon, on the backs of the commoners and of Thersites (2.198–199, 2.265–269). In Antenor's memory his scepter does nothing at all, and the stillness of the staff sets off the force of the words.
