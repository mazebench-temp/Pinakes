summary: Zeus does not appear in Book 3, but its oaths and prayers are addressed to him, and none is answered in the book. Agamemnon swears by “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida”, the armies curse whoever breaks the truce, and Menelaus prays to him for vengeance and then calls him the most baneful of the gods.

**The oaths of Zeus.** Menelaus asks for three lambs, two from the Trojans for Earth and the Sun and a third from the Achaeans for Zeus (3.103–104). Priam must swear in person, “so that no one may violate the oaths of Zeus by transgression” (Διὸς ὅρκια, 3.107). The oaths belong to Zeus, and so does their punishment. See [[oath]].

**The witness.** Agamemnon's prayer begins with him:

{{quote:21e4284e-c1b5-5925-be78-6308bf3433a8}}

Ἴδηθεν μεδέων (“who rule from Ida”) names Zeus by his seat on the mountain above Troy (see [[ida]] and [[who-rules-from-ida]]). Both armies use the same invocation when the lots are shaken (3.320). The Achaeans use it again before Ajax fights Hector (7.202), and Priam when he sets out for Achilles' hut (24.308).

**Not yet.** The armies pour out the wine and curse whichever side first breaks the oaths:

{{quote:77023816-983f-5be9-aff0-dea0c88f39ef}}

The narrator's last half-line is the one that refused Agamemnon's prayer in Book 2 (2.419). “Not yet” looks beyond the book. When the Trojans break the truce in Book 4, Agamemnon answers it: if the Olympian does not fulfill an oath at once, he fulfills it late, and the breakers pay with their heads, their wives and their children; a day will come when Ilios perishes (4.158–165). See [[the-oaths-sworn]].

**Menelaus.** In the duel Menelaus prays to Zeus for vengeance on the guest who wronged his host (3.351–354; see [[xenia]]). His spear fails to kill and his sword breaks, and he cries out, “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365). The narrator does not say whether Zeus heard either prayer; it is Aphrodite who decides the duel (3.374–382). Priam, leaving the field, has already said that Zeus knows “for which of the two the end of death is fated” (3.308–309).

**Father.** Helen is “child of Zeus” (Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, 3.199, 3.418) and “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (3.426). So is the goddess who drives her to Paris, Ἀφροδίτη Διὸς θυγάτηρ (“Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus”, 3.374). See [[daughter-of-zeus]].
