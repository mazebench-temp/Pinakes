**The rebuke.** In Book 2 Hector obeyed Iris without a word (2.807–808). His first speech in the poem is addressed to his brother, who has just slipped back into the crowd at the sight of Menelaus:

{{quote:de4e7f0d-8444-505b-aad1-ec33c6f00d32}}

The words are αἰσχρά ("shaming", 3.38; see [[aischros]]), and shame is their subject. Hector wishes Paris had never been born (ἄγονος) or had died unmarried (ἄγαμος): the marriage is the offense (see [[never-born]]). He imagines the Achaeans laughing at a champion chosen for his looks, with "no strength in his heart, and no courage" (3.43–45). Then he tells the story of the abduction as a Trojan sees it: the voyage, the companions, the "lovely woman" brought from a distant land, "a great grief to your father and your city and all the people, a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself" (3.46–51; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The lyre, the hair and the looks, "the gifts of Aphrodite", would not help him in the dust (3.54–55). His last charge is against his own people: the Trojans are cowards, or Paris would already wear "a tunic of stone" (3.56–57), usually understood as death by stoning ([[tunic-of-stone]]). Hector judges his brother by what others see and say. In Book 6 he will give the same reason for his own courage: he would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women if he held back from the fighting (6.441–443).

**The truce.** Paris answers that his brother's heart is like an axe (3.60–63; see [[heart-like-an-axe]]) and offers to fight Menelaus alone, and Hector "rejoiced greatly" (3.76). He walks into the middle and holds back the Trojan battalions, "gripping his spear by the middle" (μέσσου δουρὸς ἑλών, 3.78), and they sit down. The Achaeans shoot at him until Agamemnon stops them: "Hector of the gleaming helmet means to say something" (3.79–83; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]). Then he proclaims Paris' terms to both armies, almost in Paris' own words, and names his brother as the man "for whose sake the quarrel arose" (3.86–94; see [[message-repeated]]). Nearly the same three lines bring him out in Book 7 to announce his own challenge (7.54–56). He sends two heralds to the city for the lambs and for Priam (3.116–117).

**The lots.** Hector, "son of Priam", and Odysseus measure out the ground and shake the lots in a helmet (3.314–317). While the armies pray that "whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides" may die, "great Hector of the gleaming helmet" shakes them, "looking away" (ἂψ ὁρόων), and the lot of Paris leaps out (3.320–325; see [[lots]]). The brother who wished him unborn draws the lot that gives him the first throw. Hector has no part in the rest of the book.
