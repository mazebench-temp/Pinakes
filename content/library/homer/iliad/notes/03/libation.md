{{quote:77023816-983f-5be9-aff0-dea0c88f39ef}}

In Book 1 wine was poured over the burning thigh-pieces, and the first drops of the feast's wine went to the gods before men drank (1.462–463, 1.470–471). The libation of the oath is neither. The heralds bring wine from Troy “in a goatskin”, with a mixing bowl and golden cups (3.246–248), and mix it in the bowl at the oath (3.269–270). After the lambs are killed, “drawing wine from the mixing bowl in cups they poured it out (ἔκχεον), and prayed to the everlasting gods” (3.295–296). No one drinks it. All of it goes to the ground, and the prayer that goes with it is a curse: “may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine” (3.300). The libation acts out the penalty. See [[oath]], [[wine]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[cup]].

The poem does not say what the wine was mixed with. Nestor spoke of the oaths of the expedition as sealed with “libations of unmixed wine” (σπονδαί τʼ ἄκρητοι, 2.341), and Agamemnon uses the same line of the broken truce in Book 4 (4.159). The men who pour it belong to both armies. The wine comes from one bowl, and the curse is what “a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297; see [[tis-speech]]).
