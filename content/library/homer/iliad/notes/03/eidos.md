summary: Book 3 is a book of looking. Hector calls Paris “best in looks” and nothing more, the old men and Priam judge Helen and the Achaeans by their looks, Antenor sets Odysseus' looks against his words, and two goddesses come to Helen in borrowed likenesses.

  - 3.386-398 | Aphrodite in the likeness of an old woman

**Best in looks.** Hector's first words to Paris sum him up.

{{quote:450c586e-307a-5d7b-8b64-fee70366ca71}}

εἶδος ἄριστε, “best in looks” (see [[best-in-looks]]): ἄριστος (“best”) is the word of heroic excellence, and in Paris it has shrunk to his appearance (see [[aristos]]). The Achaeans are laughing, says Hector, “who thought our champion a prince because he has handsome looks; but there is no strength in his heart, and no courage” (καλὸν εἶδος … ἀλλʼ οὐκ ἔστι βίη φρεσὶν οὐδέ τις ἀλκή, 3.44–45). His lyre, his hair and his looks are “the gifts of Aphrodite”, and they will be no help when he lies in the dust (3.54–55; see [[doron]]). Paris does not deny that he has them. He answers that the gifts of the gods are not to be thrown away (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). The woman he brought back is εὐειδής, “lovely” (3.48). Hector will throw the same line at him again in Book 13 (13.769).

**Most beautiful in form.** Iris comes to Helen “in the likeness of” (εἰδομένη) her sister-in-law Laodice, “most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (3.121–124). The goddess takes a mortal's form, and the mortal is named for her looks.

**Helen's looks.** Homer never describes Helen's face. The old men on the tower say only that she is “terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on” (εἰς ὦπα, 3.158), and her beauty is shown by what it does to them (see [[no-blame]]). Priam in turn judges the Achaeans by their looks. He has never seen a man so handsome (καλόν) or so stately as Agamemnon, “for he looks like a king” (3.169–170); Odysseus is shorter by a head but broader (3.193–194); Ajax towers above the Argives by his head and shoulders (3.227).

**Looks and words.** Antenor's memory of the embassy turns on the gap between looks and power. Standing, Menelaus was the taller; seated, Odysseus was the more stately (3.210–211). Then Odysseus rose to speak.

{{quote:1400c24c-8075-5e4a-b7ee-66655be59e11}}

He looked like a man who knew nothing, surly and a fool, until his voice came out of his chest with words like the snowflakes of winter: “then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks” (3.224). Paris' looks hide a lack; Odysseus' hide a power. See [[snowflakes-of-winter]].

**Borrowed likenesses.** Aphrodite comes to Helen on the tower in the likeness of an old wool-worker who had loved her in Lacedaemon (ἐεισαμένη, 3.386–389). Her beauty shows through. When Helen notices the goddess's beautiful neck, her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes, she is amazed and names her (3.396–398; see [[thambos]]). The goddess describes Paris by his looks as well: he sits in his chamber “shining with beauty and fine clothes”, as if he had come from a dance and not from a fight (3.391–394). See [[beauty-and-its-price]].
