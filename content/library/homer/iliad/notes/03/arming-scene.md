**The moment.** The lot has given Paris the first throw (3.325), and the armies sit down in their ranks, where the horses stand and the armor lies (3.326–327). Where the fight should begin, the poem stops to arm the man who proposed it. When he stepped out as a champion that morning he wore a leopard's skin and carried a bow, a sword and two spears (3.17–18; see [[leopard-skin]]). His armor has lain on the ground since the truce (3.114). Now he puts on the full armor of a spearman, and the bow is not mentioned again.

**The borrowed corselet.** Every verse of the scene is formula except one. The corselet is “the corselet of his brother Lycaon” (οἷο κασιγνήτοιο Λυκάονος), and ἥρμοσε δʼ αὐτῷ, “it fitted him”, or, as the translation takes it, “he fitted it to himself”. The poem does not say why he needs another man's. The usual explanation is that the archer who came out in a leopard's skin had no corselet of his own. The brother whose armor protects him is the son of Priam whom Achilles will kill without pity by the river in Book 21 (21.34–135; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The corselet does its work: Menelaus' spear passes through shield and corselet and only cuts the tunic (3.357–360; see [[corselet]]).

**The helmet.** The helmet has a horsehair crest, “and the plume nodded terribly above it” (δεινὸν δὲ λόφος καθύπερθεν ἔνευεν). It is the piece that nearly kills him. Menelaus' sword shatters on its ridge, and he drags Paris by it until the strap chokes him (3.361–372; see [[helmet]]).

**Menelaus.** His arming takes one verse: “And in the same way warlike Menelaus put on his armor” (ὣς δʼ αὔτως Μενέλαος ἀρήϊος ἔντεʼ ἔδυνεν, 3.339). The poem gives the full scene to the man who will lose.

**The first of four.** It is the shortest of the Iliad's four full arming scenes and the only one before a single combat (the others are Agamemnon's, Patroclus' and Achilles', 11.15–46, 16.130–144, 19.364–391). Its verses return in each of them. Patroclus too will go out in armor that is not his own (16.130–144).
