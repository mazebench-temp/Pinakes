**“Had not.”** The scene opens on the glory Menelaus is about to win: he “would have dragged him off and won untold glory” (ἄσπετον … κῦδος). The condition names the goddess by her father, “Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus”, and by her speed, ὀξὺ νόησε, “quick to see it”. The oath called on Zeus to watch a fight between two men (3.276–280), and a daughter of Zeus ends it.

**Two moves.** First she breaks the strap, and “the empty helmet came away in his strong hand” (3.376). Menelaus whirls it and throws it among the Achaeans, and it is the only prize he takes from the duel (3.377–378). Then, as he springs back to kill Paris with his spear, she snatches him away “very easily, as a god can” (ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός) and hides him in thick mist (3.379–381).

**From battlefield to bedroom.** She sets him down “in his fragrant, perfumed chamber” (ἐν θαλάμῳ εὐώδεϊ κηώεντι, 3.382) and goes to fetch Helen. In her report he is “shining with beauty and fine clothes”, not like a man back from a fight but like one going to the dance (3.391–394; see [[dance]]). Hector had told him that the gifts of Aphrodite would be no help to him in the dust (3.54–55); the goddess herself is his help (see [[no-help-to-you]]). The rescue leads to the bed at the book's end (3.441–448; see [[bed]]).

**The one left behind.** Menelaus “ranged through the throng like a wild beast, to see if he could catch sight of godlike Alexander”. No Trojan could show him, and none would have hidden him, “for he was hateful to them all, like black doom” (3.449–454; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]). The rescue leaves the attacker baffled, as it does again in Books 5 and 20, and it leaves the man it saves hated by his own side.

**What it costs.** The rescue saves Paris and leaves the oath with nothing to settle, for its terms spoke only of death (see [[single-combat]] and [[oath]]). Paris explains his defeat by a god: “This time Menelaus has won, with Athena's help; another time I will beat him, for we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440). Aphrodite rescues again in Book 5, when she carries Aeneas out of the fight with Diomedes and is wounded for it (5.311–351). See [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]].
