---
summary: Book 3 uses φιλότης in all three of its senses, for the friendship the armies hope to swear, the friendship Paris betrayed as a guest, and the love to which he takes Helen while the armies wait. At the end no Trojan would hide him out of love.
passages:
  - 3.67-75 | Paris proposes the duel
  - 3.318-323 | Friendship and trusty oaths
  - 3.351-354 | A host who has shown him friendship
  - 3.441-448 | To bed
  - 3.451-454 | Hateful to them all
---

**The truce.** Paris proposes that he and Menelaus fight alone for Helen and the possessions, and that the rest, “making friendship and swearing a trusty truce” (φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμόντες), live in peace (3.73). The phrase comes back in nearly the same words when Hector announces the proposal (3.94) and when the herald Idaeus summons Priam to the oath (3.256). Before the lots are shaken, both armies pray that whichever man brought these troubles on both sides may die, “and that friendship and trusty oaths may be ours” (ἡμῖν δʼ αὖ φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστὰ γενέσθαι, 3.323). φιλότης is what the whole plain wants. See [[oath]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**The host.** Before his spear-cast Menelaus names Paris' crime with the same word.

{{quote:d5f22761-0101-592a-98d1-97e34d0d6970}}

The host is the man who “has shown him friendship” (ὅ κεν φιλότητα παράσχῃ). The war began when a guest took his host's wife, and the φιλότης of hospitality was the first bond broken (see [[xenia]]).

**The bed.** After the duel the word takes its third sense. Paris answers Helen's scorn with an invitation.

{{quote:e4a4c8f4-648d-5cde-9fe8-3b71699aad7e}}

“Let us go to bed and take our pleasure in love” (φιλότητι τραπείομεν εὐνηθέντε), and never has he wanted her so much, not even on the island of Cranae, where they first lay together “in love and bed” (φιλότητι καὶ εὐνῇ, 3.445; see [[cranae]]). The duel was fought so that one man might have Helen and the armies might part in friendship. It ends with the loser in bed with her and the truce unresolved. In the *Odyssey* Penelope uses the words of 3.445 of Helen herself: Argive Helen would never have lain “in love and bed” with a foreign man had she known that the Achaeans would bring her home again (*Odyssey* 23.218–221).

**No love.** The last use is a denial. Menelaus ranges through the throng like a wild beast, and no one will show him Paris.

{{quote:f33d8b09-db86-5ab5-9ca6-26eb273ec9dc}}

“They would not have hidden him out of love” (φιλότητι), “for he was hateful (ἀπήχθετο) to them all, like black doom.” The pair of love and hate has just been spoken by a goddess. Aphrodite threatens Helen that she may come to hate her (ἀπεχθήρω) “as much as now I love you beyond measure” (ἐφίλησα), and devise hatreds (ἔχθεα) between Trojans and Danaans (3.415–416). The book that began with a treaty of friendship ends with a man whom even his own people hate. See [[philos]] and [[ker-doom]].
