**Vengeance.** Menelaus enters the book as a man who wants to make Paris pay. Seeing him step out before the Trojans, he rejoices like a hungry lion that comes upon a carcass, “for he thought he would take vengeance (τίσεσθαι) on the wrongdoer (ἀλείτην)” (3.28; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). His prayer before his spear-cast asks for the same thing: “Lord Zeus, grant me vengeance (τίσασθαι) on the man who first wronged me” (3.351). When his sword breaks on Paris' helmet, he complains in the same word: “truly I thought I would pay back (τίσασθαι) Alexander for his wickedness” (3.366). Three times the book states his hope, and three times it is denied. See [[menelaus]].

**Satisfaction and penalty.** Agamemnon's oath sets out the terms.

{{quote:59d5bc87-66e8-5173-920f-b3a4c7391d6a}}

If Paris kills Menelaus, the Achaeans sail home. If Menelaus kills Paris, the Trojans are to give back Helen and the possessions and also pay a satisfaction ([[time|τιμή]]) “that will be remembered among men who are yet to come”. If they refuse, Agamemnon will fight on “for the sake of the penalty” (εἵνεκα ποινῆς) until he reaches the end of the war. The terms that Paris proposed and Hector announced spoke only of Helen and the possessions (3.71–72, 3.92–93). The satisfaction is Agamemnon's addition, and when he claims the victory at the end of the book he repeats it in the same two lines (3.459–460). The phrase εἵνεκα ποινῆς is the one used of the lawsuit on the shield of Achilles (18.498). The war is being fought like a blood-feud over a wrong done to one house, and a whole city is to pay.

**Punishers below.** Among the witnesses of the oath Agamemnon calls on “you two who below the earth punish (τίνυσθον) dead men, whoever has sworn a false oath” (3.278–279). The dual is usually taken to mean Hades and Persephone. The penalty for perjury is paid after death. See [[oath]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].
