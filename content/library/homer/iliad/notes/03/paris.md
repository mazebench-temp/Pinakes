**The challenge and the retreat.** When the armies at last meet, the first man named is Alexander. He steps out in front of the Trojans with a leopard's skin, a bow and two spears, and challenges the best of the Argives (3.16–20). Menelaus sees him and rejoices like a hungry lion, "for he thought he would take vengeance on the wrongdoer" (ἀλείτην, 3.28; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). Paris sees Menelaus:

{{quote:c6707bf8-4db7-5da5-9c28-ecc33e6edbd2}}

He backs away like a man who has met a serpent in the mountains, with trembling limbs and pale cheeks (see [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]). The passage ends on the epithet of his looks, "godlike Alexander".

**The gifts of the gods.** Hector's rebuke (3.39–57) is his first speech in the poem. Paris is a champion chosen for his looks, who sailed off and brought back another man's wife and will not face the husband; the Trojans, Hector says, are cowards, or Paris would already wear "a tunic of stone", which is usually understood as death by stoning (see [[never-born]] and [[tunic-of-stone]]). Paris answers without resentment. The rebuke is fair (κατʼ αἶσαν, 3.59), his brother's heart is like an axe ([[heart-like-an-axe]]), and his beauty is not his fault:

{{quote:84c6e108-9fd6-59f3-a330-14bcae59dfc8}}

In the same speech he turns from apology to proposal, and offers what no one has asked of him: to fight Menelaus alone for Helen and her possessions, so that the rest may swear friendship and a trusty truce (3.67–75). See [[gifts-of-the-gods]], [[beauty-and-its-price]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]].

**The cause.** Everyone in the book names him as the cause of the war. Hector proclaims "the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose" (3.87). Menelaus speaks of the evils the armies have suffered "because Alexander began it" (3.100). Both armies pray that "whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides" may die (3.321), and Menelaus asks Zeus for vengeance "on the man who first wronged me", so that men to come may shudder to wrong a host (3.351–354; see [[xenia]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The duel.** His lot leaps out first (3.325), and he arms. It is the first full arming scene in the poem, and the corselet is his brother Lycaon's (3.328–338; see [[arming-scene]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). His spear bends on Menelaus' shield. Menelaus' spear passes through his shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, and he escapes by swerving; Menelaus' sword shatters on his helmet; then Menelaus drags him by the helmet toward the Achaeans, the strap choking his "soft throat" (3.346–372; see [[single-combat]] and [[helmet]]). Aphrodite breaks the strap, snatches him away in a mist and sets him down in his fragrant bedroom (3.373–382; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). To Helen the goddess describes him as a man coming from a dance, not from a fight (3.391–394; see [[dance]]).

**Paris and Helen.** Helen, seated facing him, wishes he had died and mocks his boasts (3.428–436). He answers her calmly. Menelaus has won "with Athena's help", he will win another time, "for we have gods on our side too" (3.438–440). No god helped Menelaus in the narrative; the one who intervened helped Paris. He explains his defeat, as he explained his beauty, by the gods. Then:

{{quote:e4a4c8f4-648d-5cde-9fe8-3b71699aad7e}}

His desire is greater now than when he first lay with her, on the island of Cranae after the abduction (see [[cranae]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The words return in Book 14, when Zeus desires Hera: his speech ends with the same line (14.328 = 3.446; see [[eros]], [[himeros]] and [[philotes]]).

**Hateful to all.** While the two lie on the corded bed, Menelaus ranges through the throng "like a wild beast", looking for him (3.449–450; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]). No one shows him:

{{quote:f33d8b09-db86-5ab5-9ca6-26eb273ec9dc}}

The book that opened with Paris stepping out as the Trojans' champion ends with the Trojans unwilling to hide him (see [[like-black-doom]]).
