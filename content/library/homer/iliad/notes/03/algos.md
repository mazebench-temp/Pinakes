The word comes twice in Book 3, and between them it measures the war's cost from both sides.

**Menelaus' sorrow.** Menelaus accepts Paris' challenge: “Hear me now as well; for sorrow comes most of all to my heart” (μάλιστα γὰρ ἄλγος ἱκάνει θυμὸν ἐμόν, 3.97–98). The wronged husband feels the pain of the war more than anyone, and he wants the armies parted, “since you have suffered many evils for the sake of my quarrel” (3.99–100). His ἄλγος is private; theirs is common.

**The armies' sorrows.** The old men on the tower see Helen and say that there is no blame if Trojans and Achaeans “suffer sorrows for so long” (πολὺν χρόνον ἄλγεα πάσχειν, 3.157) for such a woman (see [[no-blame]]). ἄλγεα πάσχειν, “to suffer sorrows”, is the phrase of both proems: the wrath laid countless ἄλγεα on the Achaeans (1.2), and Odysseus suffered many ἄλγεα on the sea (*Odyssey* 1.4). The verb of suffering runs through the book. Helen weaves into her cloth the trials that the Trojans and Achaeans “were suffering for her sake” (ἔπασχον, 3.128), and Menelaus says they have suffered many evils (πέπασθε, 3.99). See [[achos]] and [[tears]].
