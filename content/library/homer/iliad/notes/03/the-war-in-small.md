summary: Book 3 holds the whole war in one day. It recalls the abduction and the embassy that tried to undo it, reduces the war to the two husbands fighting for the woman and her goods, reviews the Achaean leaders from the walls, and in the curse of the oath foretells the city's end.

**The beginning.** Hector's rebuke tells the story of the war's origin in six lines: Paris gathered his companions, sailed over the sea in his ships, mingled with foreigners, and brought back a lovely woman from a distant land, the bride of a people of spearmen, “a great grief to your father and your city and all the people” (3.46–51). At the end of the book Paris tells the same voyage from his side, as the first night of his love: he snatched Helen from Lacedaemon, sailed off with her and lay with her on the island of Cranae (3.443–445; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). Helen tells it as a loss: she left her bridal chamber, her kinsmen, her daughter and the companions of her youth (3.174–175).

**The claim.** The terms of the duel are the terms of the whole war: Helen “and all her possessions” (κτήμασι πᾶσι, 3.70; see [[possessions]]). The possessions are named with her again and again, in Paris' proposal, in Hector's announcement, in Idaeus' summons, in Agamemnon's oath and in his claim of victory (3.72, 3.91, 3.93, 3.255, 3.282, 3.285, 3.458). Paris took a man's goods as well as his wife, and the war is fought to get both back. In Book 7 Paris offers to return the goods and keep the woman (7.357–364).

**The embassy.** Antenor remembers when Menelaus and Odysseus came to Troy to ask for her before the fighting (3.204–224; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). On the wall Helen looks for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces as if they were still alive, and the narrator tells us that the earth already held them in Lacedaemon (3.236–244). Time has passed at home that Helen knows nothing of.

**The review.** From the wall Priam asks Helen to name the enemy's leaders, as if he were seeing them for the first time (3.161–242; see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). He also remembers an older war, when he fought as an ally of the Phrygians against the Amazons and saw no army so large as this one (3.184–190).

**The end.** The duel is meant to end the war, and both armies rejoice “hoping for an end to miserable war” (3.111–112). The oath adds a curse on whoever breaks it first: may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine, theirs and their children's, “and may their wives be subdued by others” (3.300–301). That is the fate of a sacked city, and it will be Troy's. “But the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). The war nearly ends in an afternoon: Menelaus “would have dragged him off and won untold glory, had not Aphrodite … been quick to see it” (3.373–374). Instead the book ends where the war began, with Paris and Helen in bed (3.447–448). See [[the-war-in-small]], [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]].
