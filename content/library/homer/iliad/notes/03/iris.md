In Book 2 Zeus sent Iris to the Trojans (2.786–787). In Book 3 she comes to Helen, and the poem does not say who sent her.

{{quote:60101102-e667-5737-9806-117b96639b61}}

**The likeness.** She comes in disguise, as she did in Book 2. There she took the voice of Polites, a son of Priam (2.791); here she takes the likeness of a daughter of Priam, [[Laodice]], Helen's sister-in-law (γαλόως, "husband's sister"; see [[in-laws]]) and the wife of Antenor's son [[Helicaon]]. Laodice is "most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam" (θυγατρῶν εἶδος ἀρίστην, 3.124; see [[best-in-looks]]), and the goddess who goes to the most beautiful of women takes the form of the most beautiful of the princesses.

**The web.** She finds Helen at her loom:

{{quote:5cdf6064-d775-5899-ae3c-f49a4ef09c27}}

Helen is working into a great purple cloth "the many trials" (ἀέθλους) that Trojans and Achaeans suffer for her sake "at the hands of Ares" (see [[weaving]] and [[loom]]). Iris' message is that what Helen is weaving has stopped for the day:

{{quote:b64a80cf-e106-58e1-8e59-2739fa268876}}

The armies that brought "tearful Ares" on one another now sit in silence, leaning on their shields (3.132–135), and Helen is the prize: "you will be called the dear wife of the one who wins". Iris gives the news as Idaeus will give it to Priam, in nearly the same words (3.136–138 ~ 3.253–255).

**Sweet longing.** Her words do more than inform:

{{quote:19d518bc-1b73-5700-b47d-d1d286fe3793}}

The longing (ἵμερος) the goddess puts into Helen points home, to Menelaus, Sparta and her parents. At the end of the book Paris feels "sweet longing" (γλυκὺς ἵμερος) for Helen (3.446), and the two longings pull her in opposite directions (see [[himeros]]). Iris' visit sets the view from the wall in motion. Helen veils herself, and with two handmaids, [[Aethra]] and [[Clymene]], she goes weeping to the Scaean gates (3.141–145; see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]).

Her epithet is the one she had in Book 2, πόδας ὠκέα Ἶρις, "swift-footed Iris" (3.129).
