summary: In Book 3 the narrator knows what his characters cannot. Helen looks for brothers who are already dead, the armies pray for a peace that Zeus “was not yet going to” grant, and Priam leaves to the gods the knowledge of which man is fated to die; Menelaus asks for an old man because he looks before and after.

**Before and after.** Menelaus wants Priam to swear the oath because young men's minds are flighty, while an old man “looks both before and after” (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). In Book 1 Achilles denied Agamemnon that sight (1.343).

**What the narrator knows.** Helen looks for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces among the Achaeans and guesses why she cannot see them: they did not come, or they came and are ashamed of her.

{{quote:70e03702-ee22-558f-a91f-bbf93bc38989}}

The narrator answers in two lines what she cannot know: “but the life-giving earth already held them fast there in Lacedaemon, in their own dear native land” (3.243–244). The same narrator comments on the armies' prayer at the oath: “But the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). The word “not yet” (οὐδʼ … πω) looks past the book to the breaking of the truce in Book 4 and to the fall of the city (see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**What the gods know.** Priam, leaving the field, says it plainly: “Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods, for which of the two the end of death is fated” (3.308–309). The old king who looks before and after knows only that he does not know. See [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]] and [[moira]].
