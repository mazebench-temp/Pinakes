summary: Book 3 turns a battle into a duel by words. Hector's rebuke shames Paris into offering to fight, Agamemnon's shout stops the shooting so that Hector can speak, an oath binds the duel's terms, and after it Helen rebukes Paris with words he answers only with desire.

**Words that force a deed.** The book's first speech is a rebuke. Hector rebukes Paris “with shaming words” (3.38): he is a disgrace, a joy to the enemy, and should have been stoned (3.39–57; see [[aischros]] and [[tunic-of-stone]]). Paris accepts the rebuke as fair and turns it into a proposal: let him fight Menelaus alone, and let the rest make peace (3.59–75). Words have done what the sight of Menelaus could not; they have sent Paris back to fight.

**Words that stop battle.** Hector goes into the middle to announce the proposal, and the Achaeans keep shooting at him until Agamemnon shouts, “Hold, Argives! … Hector of the gleaming helmet means to say something” (3.82–83). The armies fall silent, the word is heard, and the war stops for the rest of the book (3.84–95; see [[silence]]). The duel is a battle fought under an agreement spoken aloud and sworn before the gods (3.276–301; see [[oath]]).

**Words after the duel.** When Paris is carried off, the fight between the husbands continues in the chamber in words. Helen rebukes him: go and challenge Menelaus again, and then, don't, or he will kill you (3.428–436). Paris answers, “Lady, do not rebuke my heart with harsh reproaches” (3.438; see [[oneidos]]), and takes her to bed. Violence has failed to settle the quarrel, and words cannot move the man who lost it. See [[neikos]] and [[the-war-in-small]].
