**Her gifts.** Aphrodite is named before she appears. Hector dismisses Paris' lyre, hair and looks as "the gifts of Aphrodite" (3.54–55), and Paris will not have "the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite" (χρυσέης Ἀφροδίτης; see [[golden]]) thrown in his face, for no one can have the gifts of the gods by choosing them (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[doron]]). In this book beauty is her gift, and it is also her power.

**The rescue.** Menelaus has Paris by the helmet and is dragging him toward the Achaeans:

{{quote:88c3bd5a-ac1a-5264-a834-1d5b62f3b533}}

The sentence turns on "had not" (εἰ μὴ … ὀξὺ νόησε): the victory and its "untold glory" were a moment away, until the "daughter of Zeus" (Διὸς θυγάτηρ; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]) saw it. She does not fight. She breaks the ox-hide strap, and when Menelaus comes at him again with his spear, she takes Paris away "very easily, as a god can" (ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός), hides him in mist, and sets him down in his bedroom. The rescue undoes the duel. Menelaus is left holding an empty helmet, and the oath that was to settle the war is left without a clear outcome: Agamemnon claims the victory, but Paris is alive (3.455–461). In Book 5 she will try the same for her son Aeneas and be wounded by Diomedes, and Zeus will tell her that the works of war have not been given to her: "you see to the lovely works of marriage" (5.311–351, 5.428–430). See [[divine-rescue]] and [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]].

**The old wool-comber.** She goes to call Helen, who is on the tower among the Trojan women, and speaks in the likeness of an old woman who used to comb wool for her in Lacedaemon and "loved her most of all" (3.386–388). Iris came to Helen as her sister-in-law (3.121–124); Aphrodite comes as a servant from her girlhood home. Her message is a picture of Paris:

{{quote:ca5838d5-b994-51ba-8b95-184b2e7e5340}}

The words stir Helen's heart (3.395). Then she sees through the disguise, knowing the goddess by her beautiful neck, her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes, and is amazed (θάμβησεν, 3.396–398; see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[thambos]]).

**Helen's refusal.** No other mortal in the Iliad rebukes a god face to face at such length. She calls the goddess δαιμονίη ("strange one", [[strange-one]]) and asks why she longs to "deceive" her (ἠπεροπεύειν), the verb behind Hector's insult for Paris, ἠπεροπευτά, "seducer" (3.39; see [[eperopeuo]]); Diomedes will use it of the goddess herself, "is it not enough that you beguile weak women?" (5.349). Will Aphrodite lead her on to some favorite in Phrygia or Maeonia (3.399–402)? Then:

{{quote:a597c362-d6e6-5694-adf8-d958a691e854}}

The taunt has a sting: Aphrodite did lie with a mortal, Anchises (2.820–821; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]). Helen will not go to his bed, for the Trojan women would blame her.

**Anger and obedience.** The goddess answers in anger (χολωσαμένη, 3.413):

{{quote:79d9e453-2269-5f6e-a419-69b8f47a58ee}}

She loves Helen "beyond measure" (ἔκπαγλα) and can hate her as much, and Helen, whom she could make hateful to Trojans and Danaans alike, lives by that love. Helen goes in silence, unseen by the Trojan women, and "the divinity" (δαίμων) leads the way (3.418–420). In Paris' house the goddess who has just threatened her takes a chair ([[stool]]) and sets it for her, facing Alexander: "laughter-loving Aphrodite" (φιλομμειδής) at a servant's task (3.424–425; see [[laughter-loving]] and [[helen-and-aphrodite]]).

**Desire.** Paris' last speech is in her language: ἔρως ("desire") has wrapped his mind, and "sweet longing" (γλυκὺς ἵμερος) holds him (3.442–446). When Hera borrows Aphrodite's embroidered strap to seduce Zeus, Zeus ends his speech with Paris' line (14.328 = 3.446; see [[eros]] and [[himeros]]).
