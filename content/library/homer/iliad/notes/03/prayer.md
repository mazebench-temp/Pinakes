Book 3 is the first in the poem in which the two armies pray together. At the oath Agamemnon prays for both sides, with lifted hands (3.275), and after the lambs are killed both armies pour out wine and pray (εὔχοντο) to the everlasting gods (3.296). At the lots, “the people prayed (ἠρήσαντο), and lifted up their hands to the gods” (3.318). ἠρήσαντο is from ἀράομαι, the other verb of praying, whose noun ἀρή is both a prayer and a curse, and the prayers of this book come close to curses. The first asks that the brains of the oath-breakers pour out on the ground like the wine (3.300–301). The second asks that the man who brought these troubles on both sides “die and go down into the house of Hades” (3.322). Menelaus, “praying to father Zeus” (ἐπευξάμενος, 3.350), asks for vengeance on the man who wronged him (3.351–354). See [[prayer-type-scene]], [[oath]] and [[tis-speech]].

None of them is answered as it is asked. Zeus “was not yet going to fulfill” the curse (3.302). The man who caused the war escapes the house of Hades, carried off by Aphrodite. Menelaus' spear misses and his sword breaks, and his next words to Zeus are a reproach: “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365).

εὔχομαι keeps its sense of claiming and boasting. Helen reminds Paris that “before now you boasted (εὔχεʼ) that you were better than Menelaus, dear to Ares, in your strength and your hands and your spear” (3.430–431).
