In Book 2 the serpent was a portent sent by Zeus, darting from under the altar at Aulis (2.308–320). In Book 3 it is a danger met by chance in the mountains. The simile tells nothing about the creature itself, its color, its size or its stare, only what the sight of it does to a man: he starts back, trembling seizes his limbs, and pallor takes his cheeks. The fear is given to Alexander, who has just stepped out as champion and now slips back into the ranks “in fear of the son of Atreus” (3.37).

The simile answers the one before it. Menelaus saw Alexander and rejoiced like a hungry lion that comes upon a carcass (3.23–28). The same half-line, “and when … caught sight of him” (τὸν δʼ ὡς οὖν ἐνόησεν, 3.21, 3.30), opens both, and both are set in the mountains. See [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]] and [[lion-on-a-carcass]].

The name δράκων is usually derived from δέρκομαι (“look, glare”). A few hundred lines later the two men walk out to fight “glaring terribly” (δεινὸν δερκόμενοι, 3.342).
