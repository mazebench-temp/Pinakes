---
summary: In Book 3 dogs are the hunters' dogs of the lion simile, which rush at the feeding lion in vain, and the word of Helen's self-reproach, “dog-faced as I am”.
grc: [κύνες, κυνώπιδος]
---

**The hunting dogs.** The lion to which Menelaus is compared devours the carcass he has found, “even though the swift dogs and the young men in their prime rush at him” (ταχέες τε κύνες θαλεροί τʼ αἰζηοί, 3.26). Dogs and young men are the hunters' team, and they do not stop the lion. On the shield of Achilles the same pair, dogs and young men, follow two lions that have seized a bull, and the dogs will not face them (18.579–586). See [[lion-on-a-carcass]].

**Dog-faced.** Helen names Agamemnon for Priam and adds, “and he was my brother-in-law, dog-faced as I am, if that was ever so” (δαὴρ αὖτʼ ἐμὸς ἔσκε κυνώπιδος, εἴ ποτʼ ἔην γε, 3.180). κυνῶπις, “dog-faced”, is the feminine of κυνώπης, the insult Achilles threw at Agamemnon (1.159). In Helen's mouth it is turned against herself: the dog is the emblem of shamelessness (see [[anaideia]]). She calls herself a dog again in Book 6 (6.344, 6.356), and “dog-faced” again in the Odyssey (Odyssey 4.145). See [[dog-face]] and [[helen]].
