---
summary: Book 3 has no laughter, only laughter imagined and named. Hector pictures the Achaeans cackling at Paris, and the goddess who brings Helen to Paris' bed against her will is “laughter-loving Aphrodite”.
refs: [3.43, 3.424]
passages:
  - 3.43-45 | The Achaeans cackling with laughter
  - 3.424-427 | Laughter-loving Aphrodite
---

No one laughs in Book 3. Hector imagines it: “Surely the long-haired Achaeans are cackling with laughter (καγχαλόωσι), who thought our champion a prince because he has handsome looks” (3.43–45). The verb καγχαλάω is loud, mocking laughter, the laughter that in the Iliad falls on someone brought low, as the army laughed at Thersites in Book 2 (2.270). Paris' flight has made the Trojans a joke to their enemies (see [[honor-and-dishonor]]).

The book's last divine act is done by the goddess of laughter. “Laughter-loving Aphrodite” (φιλομειδὴς Ἀφροδίτη) takes a chair and sets it for Helen facing Paris (3.424–425), and Helen, who has just been threatened into coming, sits and turns her eyes away (3.426–427). The epithet is standing and the scene is grim; the goddess's pleasure is not shared. See [[laughter-loving]] and [[helen-and-aphrodite]].
