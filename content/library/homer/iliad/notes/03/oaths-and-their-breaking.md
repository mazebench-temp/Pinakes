**Who may swear.** Menelaus accepts the duel on one condition. Priam must come and swear the oaths himself, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted, so that no one may violate the oaths of Zeus by transgression” (ὑπερβασίῃ, 3.105–107). An old man looks before and after (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). The man who proposed the truce is the one whose word is least worth having.

{{quote:f054d837-abd8-59e4-ab3a-b67b9633b0e2}}

**The oath.** Priam shudders at the summons, and comes (3.259–263). Agamemnon cuts hair from the lambs' heads and calls on Zeus, the Sun, the rivers, the earth and the two who punish perjurers below the earth to witness and keep watch over the trusty oaths (3.271–280). He then states the terms, cuts the lambs' throats, and both armies pour wine and pray (3.281–301; see [[oath]] and [[poine]]).

{{quote:77023816-983f-5be9-aff0-dea0c88f39ef}}

The prayer names no one. It curses “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths”, and it describes the punishment by the rite itself: as the wine is poured on the ground, so their brains. The narrator's next line looks ahead to Book 4, and further: “the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer”. Not yet: the curse will be fulfilled on Troy.

**What the oath said.** The terms of the oath were a killing: “if Alexander kills Menelaus … but if fair-haired Menelaus kills Alexander” (3.281–284). Menelaus had put it the same way: whichever of the two death and fate are prepared for, let him die (3.101–102). Paris and Hector had spoken of winning: “whichever of us wins and proves the stronger” (3.71, 3.92). Nobody is killed. When Paris vanishes, Agamemnon claims the victory in the words of the proposal, not of the oath: “victory clearly belongs to Menelaus” (νίκη, 3.457), and he demands Helen, the possessions and the satisfaction (3.456–460). The Achaeans applaud (3.461). The Trojans do not answer. See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[the-oaths-sworn]].
