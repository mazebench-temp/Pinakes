**Clamor and silence.** The book begins with a contrast. The Trojans come on “with clamor and with cries, like birds”, like cranes flying against the Pygmies (3.2–7), “but the Achaeans came on in silence (σιγῇ), breathing fury, eager in their hearts to stand by one another” (3.8–9). Silence is discipline and resolve; noise is the enemy's. See [[cranes-and-pygmies]] and [[plato-silent-achaeans]].

**Silence to hear.** When Hector comes out between the armies, Agamemnon stops the shooting, “and they held back from battle and fell silent (ἄνεῳ) at once” (3.84–85). After Hector's announcement “they all fell hushed in silence” (ἀκὴν ἐγένοντο σιωπῇ, 3.95), until Menelaus speaks. In Book 2 Calchas asked the stunned army, “Why are you silent (ἄνεῳ)?” (2.323).

**The war is silent.** Iris tells Helen: the men who were bringing tearful war on one another “now they are sitting in silence, and the war has stopped, leaning on their shields” (ἕαται σιγῇ, 3.134–135). On the tower the old men speak “softly” (ἦκα) to one another when they see Helen coming (3.155).

**Helen's silence.** At the end Helen gives in to Aphrodite's threat and goes to Paris' chamber, “wrapping herself in her shining white robe, in silence (σιγῇ), and escaped the notice of all the Trojan women” (3.419–420). She has spoken her refusal to the goddess; to the city she says nothing. Her silence is the last thing she can keep. See [[helen]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]].
