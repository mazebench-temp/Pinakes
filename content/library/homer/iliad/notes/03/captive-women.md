summary: Book 3 sets the fate of captive women in the curse of the oath, “may their wives be subdued by others”, and in Helen's taunt to Aphrodite that Paris may make her “his wife, or his slave”. Helen herself was taken from her husband's house, and is claimed as the prize of the duel.
refs: [3.301, 3.409]
passages:
  - 3.298-301 | The curse
  - 3.406-409 | Go and sit beside him yourself
---

**The curse.** The men of both armies, pouring wine at the oath, pray that whoever first breaks it may have their brains poured out like the wine, theirs and their children's, “and may their wives be subdued by others” (ἄλοχοι δʼ ἄλλοισι δαμεῖεν, 3.301). The verb δάμνημι, “tame, subdue”, is used of a woman taken by a man. The curse describes what happens to the women of a sacked city, and it is what Hector foresees for Andromache (6.454–463). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[husbands-and-wives]].

**Wife or slave.** Helen, refusing Aphrodite's summons, tells her to go and sit beside Paris herself and watch over him, “until he makes you his wife, or his slave” (ἢ ἄλοχον … ἢ ὅ γε δούλην, 3.409). The two conditions of a woman in a man's house stand side by side, as they did for Chryseis in Book 1, whom Agamemnon valued above his wedded wife (1.113–115).

**Helen and Aethra.** Helen was not a captive in the ordinary sense; the Iliad's characters speak of Paris snatching her away (3.444) and of her following him (3.174). She calls herself “hateful” (3.404), and she is claimed as the prize of the duel (3.138, 3.458). Among her handmaids on the wall is “Aethra, daughter of Pittheus” (3.144); on the later story that made Aethra a captive, see [[aethra]], [[aethra-line]] and [[helen-and-theseus]].
