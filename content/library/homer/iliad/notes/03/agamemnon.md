**Holding back the army.** When Hector steps between the armies holding his spear by the middle, the Achaeans shoot arrows and throw stones at him. Agamemnon stops them: “Hold, Argives! Do not shoot, young men of the Achaeans! Hector of the gleaming helmet means to say something” (3.82–83). In Book 2 his test sent the army running for the ships; here it stops at a word. Later he sends his herald [[Talthybius]] to the ships for a lamb (3.118–120).

**Seen from the wall.** He is the first man Priam asks about:

{{quote:f53d8b3e-57d3-539e-888d-5ff9caa4a7dc}}

Helen answers with his name and his office: “this is the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, both a good king and a strong spearman” (3.178–179). Then she adds that he was her brother-in-law (3.180). Xenophon's Socrates read her line as a lesson for generals: a strong spearman is one who makes his whole army strong, and a good king one who makes his people happy (*Memorabilia* 3.2.2). Priam calls him blessed, μοιρηγενής (“child of fortune”) and ὀλβιοδαίμων (“favored by heaven”; see [[child-of-fortune]]), and measures his fortune by the men subject to him: the Phrygians whom Priam once saw gathered against the Amazons “were not as many as the bright-eyed Achaeans” (3.182–190; see [[priam-and-the-amazons]]).

**The oath.** On the plain he rises with Odysseus, and the heralds bring the victims and the wine and pour water over the kings' hands (3.267–270). Then he acts as the priest of the oath. He draws the knife that always hung beside the sheath of his sword, cuts hair from the lambs' heads for the heralds to give to the best men of both armies, and prays aloud, lifting up his hands (3.271–275):

{{quote:21e4284e-c1b5-5925-be78-6308bf3433a8}}

The terms follow. If Alexander kills Menelaus, he keeps Helen and the possessions, and the Achaeans sail home. If Menelaus kills Alexander, the Trojans give back Helen and the possessions and pay the Argives “satisfaction” (τιμή), “one that will be remembered among men who are yet to come”. If Priam and his sons then refuse to pay, “I will fight on for the sake of the penalty (ποινή), staying here, until I reach the end of the war” (3.281–291). τιμή is the compensation that Achilles said the army came to win for Menelaus (1.159; see [[war-for-menelaus]], [[time]] and [[poine]]). He cuts the lambs' throats “with the pitiless bronze” and lays them on the ground, gasping (3.292–294). See [[oath]] and [[the-oaths-sworn]].

The oath provides for a death on either side. It does not provide for what happens: neither man dies. See [[single-combat]].

**The claim.** When Paris has vanished, Agamemnon speaks to the Trojans and their allies:

{{quote:3c697f55-1b5c-581a-9dc0-a79ce19074de}}

He claims what the oath gave only to a Menelaus who had killed his man, and he repeats the oath's words about a satisfaction to be remembered (3.459–460 = 3.286–287). The Achaeans applaud (3.461). The Trojans do not answer. At the start of Book 4 Zeus himself grants that the victory is Menelaus' (4.13), and then the gods decide how the truce is to be broken.
