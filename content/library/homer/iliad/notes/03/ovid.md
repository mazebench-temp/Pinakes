---
summary: Ovid gives Paris and Helen letters of their own in the Heroides, and in the Metamorphoses his Ulysses stands up to speak as Antenor describes Odysseus in Book 3.
---

- Paris writes to Helen, and Helen answers, naming her maids Clymene and Aethra as go-betweens (*Heroides* 16–17): [[ovid-heroides]].
- In the contest for the arms of Achilles, Ulysses rises to answer Ajax. He stands a moment with his eyes on the ground, then raises them to the chiefs and speaks (*Metamorphoses* 13.123–127). It is Antenor's Odysseus, who stood "fixing his eyes on the ground" before his words came down like snow (3.216–223; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]).
- "Ulysses was not handsome, but he was eloquent," Ovid tells the would-be lover, "and yet he tormented goddesses of the sea with love" (*Art of Love* 2.123–124). Antenor's memory ends on the same contrast: once Odysseus had spoken, "then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks" (3.224).
