In Book 1 the cause of the war was a debt of honor owed to Menelaus, and Helen was not named (1.159). In Book 2 the gods and the old men named her and the payment the Trojans owed (2.160–162, 2.354–356). In Book 3 the two men whose quarrel it is face each other, and the war is offered a way to end.

**The stakes.** Paris proposes to fight Menelaus “for Helen and all her possessions” (ἀμφʼ Ἑλένῃ καὶ κτήμασι πᾶσι, 3.70), and whoever wins is to “take all the possessions and the woman, and lead them home” (3.72). The formula is repeated by Hector, by Idaeus and in the oath (3.91–93, 3.255, 3.282, 3.285). Helen and the treasure are claimed together, as they were carried off together. Menelaus accepts in the language of a private dispute: the Argives and the Trojans have suffered many evils “for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (εἵνεκʼ ἐμῆς ἔριδος καὶ Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἀρχῆς, 3.100; see [[eris]] and [[arche-or-ate]]), and it is time they were parted (3.97–102). See [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]].

**Satisfaction and penalty.** In the oath Agamemnon adds something the proposal lacked.

{{quote:59d5bc87-66e8-5173-920f-b3a4c7391d6a}}

τιμή here is “satisfaction”, the compensation of 1.159, where Achilles said the Achaeans came to Troy “to win satisfaction for Menelaus” (τιμὴν ἀρνύμενοι). Now its size is set: one “that will be remembered among men who are yet to come”. If it is refused, Agamemnon will fight on “for the sake of the penalty” (εἵνεκα ποινῆς), the price owed for a wrong (see [[time]] and [[poine]]). The war is to be ended by payment, and continued if payment is refused.

**The wronged host.** Before he throws his spear, Menelaus prays for vengeance on “the man who first wronged me”, so that men to come may shudder “to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (3.351–354). The war for his honor is also a punishment for a crime against guest-friendship (see [[xenia]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]).

**The claim.** The duel ends without a death. Aphrodite carries Paris away, no Trojan will hide him, and Agamemnon claims what the oath promised:

{{quote:3c697f55-1b5c-581a-9dc0-a79ce19074de}}

The Achaeans applaud (3.461). In Book 4 Zeus himself grants that the victory is Menelaus', and proposes that Priam's city stand and Menelaus take Helen home (4.13–19); Hera will not have it, and the oaths are broken. The war for Menelaus goes on, now with a broken oath on the Trojan side. See [[the-oaths-sworn]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

The Trojans have their own account of the cause. The old men on the wall find no blame in fighting for such a woman, but want her sent home (3.156–160); Priam blames the gods (3.164–165); and no one will hide Paris, “for he was hateful to them all, like black doom” (3.454; see [[like-black-doom]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).
