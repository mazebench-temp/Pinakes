The first verse is the corselet's verse in every arming scene (see [[arming-scene]]). The second is Paris' own: the corselet belongs to his brother Lycaon, and ἥρμοσε δʼ αὐτῷ, “it fitted him”, or “he fitted it to himself”. When he stepped out that morning he had a leopard's skin where a corselet would be (3.17; see [[leopard-skin]]). The poem does not say why he had none of his own. See [[lycaon-son-of-priam]].

When both men have armed, “they had armed (θωρήχθησαν) on either side of the throng” (3.340), the verb that means “put on the corselet” used for arming as a whole.

In the duel the borrowed corselet is tested. Menelaus' spear goes through Paris' shield, “and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet” (καὶ διὰ θώρηκος πολυδαιδάλου ἠρήρειστο), and cuts the tunic beside the flank, but Paris swerves (3.357–360; see [[tunic]]). The same verse returns in Book 4, when Pandarus' arrow pierces Menelaus' own corselet in the broken truce (4.135–136).
