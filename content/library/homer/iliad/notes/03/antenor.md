**Among the elders.** Antenor is the last named of the old men who sit with Priam at the Scaean gates. He and Ucalegon are "both wise men" (πεπνυμένω ἄμφω, 3.148). πεπνυμένος ("wise, sensible"; see [[wise]]) is a word for sound judgment; in Book 7 the same pair of words is given to the heralds Talthybius and Idaeus (7.276). See [[trojan-elders]] and [[ucalegon]].

**The embassy.** Helen has just named Odysseus as a man of "all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels" (3.202). Antenor confirms it from his own knowledge:

{{quote:44f89616-789d-51d9-8c87-874d535a7106}}

Years before, Odysseus and Menelaus came to Troy "on an embassy about you" (σεῦ ἕνεκʼ ἀγγελίης; Aristarchus took ἀγγελίης as a masculine noun, "as a messenger"). Antenor received them as guests (ἐξείνισσα, from ξεῖνος, "guest, host") and made them welcome in his house. The Iliad tells no more of the embassy here. In the *Cypria*, as Proclus summarizes it, the envoys came after the Achaeans had landed at Troy (see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]), and a dithyramb of Bacchylides, *The Sons of Antenor*, gives Menelaus the speech the Iliad leaves out ([[bacchylides-embassy]]). In Book 11 the poem recalls another Trojan, Antimachus, who urged the assembly to kill Menelaus on the spot when he came as an envoy with Odysseus (11.138–142). Antenor was their host, in a war that began when a guest wronged his host (3.351–354; see [[xenia]]).

**Two speakers.** His memory becomes a double portrait. Standing, Menelaus was the taller by his broad shoulders; seated, Odysseus was the more stately (γεραρώτερος), the word Priam used of Agamemnon (γεραρόν, 3.170). When they spoke before the Trojans, Menelaus spoke "fluently, few words but very clear" (ἐπιτροχάδην, παῦρα μὲν ἀλλὰ μάλα λιγέως), no man of many words and none who missed the point (3.213–215). Odysseus seemed at first to have nothing to say:

{{quote:1400c24c-8075-5e4a-b7ee-66655be59e11}}

He stood with his eyes on the ground and held the scepter stiff, "like a man who knows nothing", so that "you would have said he was some surly fellow, and a fool as well" (ζάκοτος, ἄφρων; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]). Then came the great voice and the words "like the snowflakes of winter" (see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). The portrait ends on looks: "then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks" (εἶδος, 3.224). This is a book that measures men by their looks. Paris is "best in looks" and nothing more (3.39, 3.44–45), Agamemnon "looks like a king" (3.170), Odysseus is "like a ram" (3.197). The Trojan elder remembers a man whose worth could not be seen until he spoke. See [[eidos]] and [[odysseus]].

**At the oath.** Antenor rides beside Priam in the chariot to the oath sacrifice and back (3.262, 3.312). His son Helicaon, Ἀντηνορίδης, "the son of Antenor", is married to Priam's daughter Laodice (3.122–124; see [[helicaon]] and [[laodice]]). In Book 7 it is Antenor who proposes to give Helen back, because the Trojans are now fighting after breaking the oaths sworn here (7.347–353).

Ancient critics read the two portraits as types of eloquence. With Nestor, whose voice flowed "sweeter than honey" (1.249; see [[sweeter-than-honey]]), Homer was held to have drawn the three styles: the plain in Menelaus, the middle in Nestor, the grand in Odysseus (Quintilian 12.10.64; Aulus Gellius 6.14.7; see [[three-styles-of-speech]]).
