The verb νοέω, “notice, perceive”, marks four turns of the book.

**Two sightings.** The duel begins with two men catching sight of each other. “When Menelaus, dear to Ares, caught sight of him” (ἐνόησεν), he rejoiced like a hungry lion (3.21–28); “when godlike Alexander caught sight of him”, his heart was struck with fear and he drew back into the crowd (3.30–32). The same verb begins both reactions, and the similes that follow show the difference (see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]).

**A goddess notices.** Menelaus is dragging Paris by the helmet and would have won, “had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (ὀξὺ νόησε, 3.374). The goddess's quick perception undoes the duel (see [[divine-rescue]]).

**Helen notices.** When Aphrodite comes to her in disguise, Helen “noticed” (ἐνόησε) the goddess's beautiful neck, her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes, and was amazed (3.396–398; see [[thambos]]). She sees through a goddess's disguise.

**A fearless mind.** The noun comes once, in Paris' praise of Hector: “so fearless is the mind (νόος) in your breast” (3.63; see [[kradie]]).
