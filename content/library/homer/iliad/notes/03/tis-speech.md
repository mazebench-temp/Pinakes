---
summary: Twice at the truce the anonymous voice speaks for both armies at once, “of Achaeans and Trojans alike”: first the curse on whoever breaks the oaths, then the prayer that the man who caused the war may die (3.297–301, 3.319–323).
passages:
  - 3.295-302 | The curse
  - 3.318-325 | The prayer at the lots
---

The verse is the same both times: ὧδε δέ τις εἴπεσκεν Ἀχαιῶν τε Τρώων τε, “and this is what a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297, 3.319). The second half-line, which elsewhere has the speaker “looking at his neighbor”, names instead the two armies, so that the one nameless voice belongs to both sides. The iterative εἴπεσκεν says that many men said this, or something like it.

These two speeches are prayers to Zeus, not comments on something the crowd has just seen. The first, spoken over the poured wine, is a curse on “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths” (3.298–301; see [[oath]] and [[libation]]). The second, spoken while Hector shakes the lots, asks that “whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides” may die and go down into the house of Hades, “and that friendship and trusty oaths may be ours” (3.320–323; see [[lots]]). In effect the Trojans pray with the Achaeans for the death of their own prince. Each speech is closed with the plural, ὣς ἔφαν, “So they spoke” (3.302, 3.324), and the first is answered by the narrator: Zeus was not yet going to fulfill it (3.302; see [[so-he-spoke]]).

The book has one other speech by a group, the old men's words at the sight of Helen, spoken “softly … to one another” (3.155–160; see [[winged-words]] and [[no-blame]]).
