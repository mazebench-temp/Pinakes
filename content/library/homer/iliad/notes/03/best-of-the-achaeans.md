**A challenge to the best.** Paris steps out before the Trojans and “challenged all the best of the Argives to fight him face to face” (3.19). The challenge assumes that he is the best of his own side, and the Achaeans had believed it, “who thought our champion a prince” (ἀριστῆα, 3.44). He shrinks back as soon as Menelaus appears (3.30–37). See [[aristos]].

**The best from the wall.** Priam's questions produce a ranking of the Achaeans by sight. Agamemnon is “both a good king and a strong spearman” (3.179), the two excellences that Book 1 divided between him and Achilles (see [[a-good-king-and-a-strong-spearman]]). Ajax towers above the Argives by his head and broad shoulders (3.227), and Helen calls him “huge Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans” (ἕρκος Ἀχαιῶν, 3.229; see [[bulwark-of-the-achaeans]]). In Book 1 Nestor called Achilles the man “who for all the Achaeans is a mighty bulwark against evil war” (1.283–284), and the Catalogue made Ajax the best of the men “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769). With Achilles absent, the title passes to Ajax, and Helen gives it to him.

**Better and stronger.** The duel is fought to decide which of two men is the stronger: “whichever of us wins and proves the stronger” (κρείσσων, 3.71, 3.92). After it Helen measures her husbands in the words of Book 1's quarrel. Paris was beaten “by a stronger man” (ἀνδρὶ … κρατερῷ, 3.429), and before that he boasted that he was “better” (φέρτερος) than Menelaus in strength, hands and spear (3.430–431). Nestor used the same pair of Achilles and Agamemnon: Achilles is strong (καρτερός), Agamemnon the greater (φέρτερος), since he rules over more (1.280–281). See [[paris]] and [[menelaus]].
