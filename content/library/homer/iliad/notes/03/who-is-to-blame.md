summary: Book 3 puts the question of blame for the war to nearly everyone in it. Hector blames Paris, Paris the gifts of the gods, Menelaus the man who began it, the old men excuse the war and Priam excuses Helen, Helen blames herself and then Aphrodite, and the armies pray that the guilty man may die without naming him.

The duel is proposed to settle the war between the two men it concerns, and the question underneath it is who is to blame for the war. Nearly everyone in the book answers it.

**Hector: Paris.** Hector's rebuke is the first speech of the book. Paris is “a disgrace” (λώβη) and “scorned by others” (3.42), “a great grief to your father and your city and all the people, a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (3.50–51). If the Trojans were not such cowards, he would long ago have been stoned “for all the evil you have done” (3.56–57; see [[tunic-of-stone]]).

**Paris: the gifts of the gods.** Paris accepts the rebuke, “since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (3.59), but defends what he is. His beauty and his charm are gifts of the gods, and no one can take such gifts by his own will (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). He does not say that the gods made him take Helen.

**Menelaus: the man who began it.** Hector announces the duel as the proposal of Alexander, “for whose sake the quarrel arose” (3.87). Menelaus accepts, since the armies have suffered “for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἀρχῆς, 3.100). An ancient variant read ἄτης (“folly”) for ἀρχῆς, the ending that the same phrase has at 6.356 and 24.28 (see [[arche-or-ate]]). He wants Priam himself to swear, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted” (3.106).

**The old men and Priam: no one.** On the tower the old men say softly that there is no blame (νέμεσις) in suffering so long for such a woman, and in the next line that she should go home (3.156–160; see [[no-blame]]). Priam goes further: “you are not to blame for anything in my eyes; it is the gods who are to blame” (3.164; see [[aitios]]).

**Helen: herself.** Helen does not take the absolution. She wishes that evil death had pleased her before she followed his son, and she wastes away in tears (3.173–176). She calls herself “dog-faced” (3.180; see [[dog-face]]), and imagines her brothers staying away from the battle “for fear of the shame and the many reproaches that are mine” (3.242).

**The armies: whoever it was.** The prayers of both armies name no one. At the oath: “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths”, may their brains pour out like this wine (3.299–300). At the lots: “whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides, grant that he may die” (3.321–322). The guilty man is to be found by the gods through the duel. Zeus does not answer the prayer (3.302), and Menelaus, whose prayer for vengeance on “the man who first wronged me” (3.351) fails with his sword, blames Zeus: “no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365).

**Helen: Aphrodite.** When the goddess comes to take her to Paris' bed, Helen blames her. She calls her a deceiver (ἠπεροπεύειν, 3.399; see [[eperopeuo]]) with “treachery in your heart” (3.405), and refuses: “it would be a thing for blame”, and the Trojan women would all reproach her (3.410–412; see [[nemesis]]). Threatened, she goes, and the narrator gives both agents in one line: she went in silence, “and the divinity led the way” (3.420; see [[daimon]]).

**Paris: Athena.** Paris explains his defeat as he explained his gifts: “This time Menelaus has won, with Athena's help … for we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440).

**The Trojans: Paris.** The last verdict is the Trojans'. None of them would have hidden him out of love, “for he was hateful to them all, like black doom” (3.454). Agamemnon then claims the victory and the penalty (3.456–460; see [[poine]]). In Book 4 the Trojans make themselves guilty in turn, when Pandarus, persuaded by Athena, breaks the oaths with an arrow (see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).
