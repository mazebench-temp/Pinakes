summary: Priam calls Helen to sit beside him on the wall and tells her that she is not to blame in his eyes; it is the gods who are to blame, who roused the war against him.

  - 3.161-170 | Priam calls Helen

Priam's absolution comes as an aside inside an invitation. He calls Helen to sit in front of him, so that she may see her former husband and her kin by marriage, and so that she may name for him a huge Achaean on the plain.

{{quote:0bf4d405-d02f-599f-ab87-63f250320f9f}}

The old men around him have just said that there is no blame (νέμεσις) in fighting so long for such a woman, and in the same breath that she should go home (3.156–160; see [[no-blame]]). Priam goes further. He excuses her as well as the war, and names the gods instead. The form of his words is Achilles' in Book 1, οὔ τί μοι, “not … in my eyes”: the Trojans “have done me no wrong” (1.153), and the heralds are “not to blame in my eyes” (1.335). There the blame moves to Agamemnon. Here it moves to the gods, “who roused against me the tearful war of the Achaeans” (3.165).

Helen does not take the absolution. Her answer begins with respect and fear of her father-in-law (3.172; see [[in-laws]]) and turns at once to blaming herself: she wishes evil death had pleased her before she followed his son (3.173–175), and she calls herself “dog-faced” (3.180; see [[dog-face]]). In her lament over Hector she remembers the old man's kindness: “my father-in-law was always gentle, like a father” (24.770). The book goes on to ask the question Priam has answered, and it gives other answers. See [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[helen]].
