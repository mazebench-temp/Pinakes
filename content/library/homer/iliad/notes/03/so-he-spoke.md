summary: Nineteen verses of Book 3 close a speech with the formula, and the rest of the line tells what the words did. The armies rejoice or fall silent, Priam shudders, Helen is stirred and afraid, and twice the narrator adds what the speakers cannot know.

The closings of Book 3: 3.76, 84, 95, 111, 139, 161, 181, 243, 259, 292, 302, 310, 324, 355, 369, 395, 418, 447, 461. New forms are the plurals ὣς ἄρʼ ἔφαν and ὣς ἔφαν, “So they spoke”, for the voices of a crowd (3.161, 3.302, 3.324); ὣς εἰποῦσα, “So saying”, of Iris (3.139); and ἦ ῥα καί, “He spoke, and”, with the particle ῥα (3.310, 3.355, 3.447).

**The armies.** The proposal of the duel is met with joy and silence. “Hector rejoiced greatly when he heard his words” (3.76); the Achaeans “held back from battle and fell silent at once” (3.84–85); at Hector's announcement “they all fell hushed in silence” (3.95); and after Menelaus' answer “the Achaeans and the Trojans rejoiced, hoping for an end to miserable war” (3.111–112). The book ends as Agamemnon's claim is closed by the Achaeans' applause, and nothing from the Trojans: “So spoke the son of Atreus, and the other Achaeans applauded” (3.461).

**What the speakers cannot know.** Twice the second half of the line turns from the speakers to the narrator's knowledge. Helen has wondered why she cannot see her brothers among the Achaeans:

And when the two armies have prayed over the oath, the narrator gives Zeus' answer in the words of Book 2: “So they spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302; 2.419).

**Feeling.** Idaeus' summons makes the old king shudder (ῥίγησεν, 3.259). Aphrodite's summons “stirred the heart in her breast” (3.395), the half-line with which Agamemnon's test of the army stirred the men in Book 2 (2.142). After the goddess's threat, “Helen, child of Zeus, was afraid” (ἔδεισεν, 3.418), the verb of Chryses' fear and Hera's in Book 1 (1.33, 1.568).

**Word and deed.** ἦ καί and ἦ ῥα καί lead straight from speech to act: Agamemnon cuts the lambs' throats (3.292), Priam puts the lambs in his chariot (3.310), Menelaus throws his spear (3.355) and then leaps on Paris (3.369), and Paris leads Helen to bed (3.447).

In Antenor's speech ἦ καί is not the formula: ἦ καὶ γένει ὕστερος ἦεν, “and indeed he was the younger” (3.215).
