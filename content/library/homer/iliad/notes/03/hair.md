---
summary: Paris' hair is one of the gifts of Aphrodite that Hector scorns (3.55), and at the oath Agamemnon cuts hair from the lambs' heads for the heralds to share among the leaders (3.273–274).
grc: [κόμη, τρίχας, ἠϋκόμοιο]
---

**Paris' hair.** Hector tells his brother that his lyre would be no help against Menelaus, “nor the gifts of Aphrodite, your hair and your looks (ἥ τε κόμη τό τε εἶδος), when you lay mingled with the dust” (3.54–55). The hair belongs with the looks and the music of the lover, not of the fighter. See [[no-help-to-you]], [[eidos]] and [[gifts-of-the-gods]].

**The lambs' hair.** At the oath Agamemnon draws his knife and “cut hair (τρίχας) from the heads of the lambs; and then the heralds gave it out to the best of the Trojans and Achaeans” (3.273–274). In an ordinary sacrifice the first hairs from the victim's head are thrown into the fire as the opening offering (Odyssey 3.445–446, 14.422). Here there is no fire. The hair goes to the men who swear, and so, it seems, each of them takes a share in the victims over which the oath is sworn. The poem does not explain the gesture. The same cutting of hair begins Agamemnon's oath in Book 19 (19.254). See [[oath]] and [[knife]].

**Epithets.** The Achaeans are “long-haired” (κάρη κομόωντες, 3.43, 3.79; [[long-haired]]), Menelaus “fair-haired” (ξανθός, 3.284, 3.434; [[fair-haired]]), and Helen “of the lovely hair” (ἠϋκόμοιο, 3.329; [[lovely-haired]]). The horsehair crest of the helmet is another kind of hair (see [[helmet]]).
