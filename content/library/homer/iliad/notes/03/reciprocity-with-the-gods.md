**Offerings without a return.** The oath is sworn over victims brought for the gods: a white ram and a black ewe for Earth and the Sun, and a lamb for Zeus (3.103–104). The lambs die, the wine is poured, and the men of both armies pray (3.292–301). “But the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). At the lots they pray again that whoever brought these troubles may die, and that there may be friendship and trusty oaths (3.318–323). Neither prayer is granted in Book 3.

**Menelaus.** Before his spear-cast Menelaus prays to Zeus: “grant me vengeance on the man who first wronged me”, so that men to come may fear to wrong a host (3.351–354). His prayer claims no past gift; it asks for justice. The spear misses its kill and the sword breaks, and he complains:

{{quote:29b73f9e-837c-549b-9a51-284d2c2b9adc}}

“Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (οὔ τις σεῖο θεῶν ὀλοώτερος ἄλλος). The prayer's failure is taken as the god's hostility. In Book 2 Zeus accepted Agamemnon's offering and refused his prayer (2.419–420); in Book 3 he refuses his brother's. See [[prayer]] and [[prayer-type-scene]].

**Gifts that hold.** The one gift of the gods that cannot fail in Book 3 is Paris'. His beauty is a gift of Aphrodite, and the gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). The goddess who gave it saves him (3.373–382). See [[divine-intervention]].
