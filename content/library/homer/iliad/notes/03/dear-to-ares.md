---
summary: Menelaus' standing epithet in Book 3, in thirteen lines, spoken by Trojans and Achaeans alike, from his first sight of Paris to Agamemnon's claim of victory.
grc: [ἀρηΐφιλος, ἀρηϊφίλῳ, ἀρηϊφίλου]
---

ἀρηΐφιλος Μενέλαος fills the end of the verse after the caesura in the third foot, like ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν Ἀγαμέμνων ([[lord-of-men]]), and Book 3 uses it in thirteen lines. The narrator gives it to Menelaus twice: when he first sees Paris coming out to fight (3.21), and at the end, when no one can show Paris to him (3.452). Every other use is in a speech, and most of the speakers are Trojans. Hector taunts Paris with the man he will not face (3.52) and announces the duel (3.90). Paris proposes it (3.69), and Iris (3.136) and the herald Idaeus (3.253) repeat his terms. Antenor remembers Menelaus' embassy to Troy (3.206), Helen his hospitality to Idomeneus (3.232), and Priam cannot bear to watch his son fight him (3.307). The enemy names the wronged husband by his warrior's epithet.

Agamemnon uses it last, in the claim of victory: “victory clearly belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (3.457). Menelaus' other epithets in the book are [[good-at-the-war-cry]] (3.96), [[fair-haired]] (3.284, 3.434) and [[warlike]] (3.339).
