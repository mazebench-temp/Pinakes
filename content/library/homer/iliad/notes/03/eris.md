The word comes three times in Book 3, in three senses: the strife of battle, a quarrel between two men, and rivalry in excellence.

**Battle.** The book opens with the Trojans coming on with clamor, like cranes that fly toward the streams of Oceanus bringing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men, “and at dawn they bring on their evil strife” (κακὴν ἔριδα προφέρονται, 3.7; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]).

**A quarrel.** Menelaus accepts the duel because the armies have suffered many evils “for the sake of my quarrel (ἔριδος), and because Alexander began it” (3.100). He calls the war his own ἔρις: a quarrel between two men, which the whole of two armies has had to fight. The proem dated the Iliad's story from the day Achilles and Agamemnon “stood apart in strife” (1.6); Book 3 goes back to the older quarrel under the war.

**Rivalry.** When Odysseus spoke, “no other mortal could rival” him (οὐκ ἂν ἔπειτʼ Ὀδυσῆΐ γʼ ἐρίσσειε βροτὸς ἄλλος, 3.223). The verb ἐρίζω is used here, as of Nestor and Menestheus in Book 2 (2.555), for rivalry in a skill. See [[epos]].
