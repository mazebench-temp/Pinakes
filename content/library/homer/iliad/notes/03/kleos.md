κλέος does not occur in Book 3. Its adjective does, once, in a standing phrase: none of the Trojans or their “famous allies” (κλειτῶν τʼ ἐπικούρων, 3.451; see [[famous]]) could show Paris to Menelaus. But the book looks often toward the audience that κλέος is for, the men who will hear of these events.

**Helen's web.** Iris finds Helen at her loom.

{{quote:5cdf6064-d775-5899-ae3c-f49a4ef09c27}}

She is working into a great purple cloth “the many trials” (ἀέθλους) of Trojans and Achaeans, suffered for her sake. Helen makes a record of the war while it is fought, as the poet does. In Book 6 she tells Hector that Zeus set an evil fate on her and Paris, “so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (6.357–358). See [[weaving]] and [[helens-web]].

**Men yet to come.** Menelaus prays that his vengeance on Paris may make “any man of those born hereafter” (ὀψιγόνων ἀνθρώπων) shudder to wrong a host (3.353–354). Agamemnon's oath asks for a satisfaction “that will be remembered among men who are yet to come” (ἥ τε καὶ ἐσσομένοισι μετʼ ἀνθρώποισι πέληται, 3.287), and he repeats the line when he claims it (3.460). Both brothers want the war's outcome to be told. Menelaus nearly wins the other kind of glory, the κῦδος of a victory, when he drags Paris by the helmet (3.373; see [[kydos]]).
