The word frames Helen's part in the book. Iris' message ends with the prize of the duel: “you will be called the dear wife of the one who wins” (3.138). Then:

{{quote:19d518bc-1b73-5700-b47d-d1d286fe3793}}

The longing is the goddess's doing, and it is also Helen's own. No argument is needed; she veils herself and goes at once, “letting her tender tears fall” (3.141–142; see [[divine-intervention]]). It is a longing for the whole life she left, and the list comes back when she speaks to Priam: her bridal chamber, her kinsmen, her daughter and the companions of her youth (3.174–175), and then her brothers, whom she looks for in vain (3.236–242). Both times she ends in tears (3.142, 3.176).

The second ἵμερος is Paris', and it is sexual: “as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (3.446; see [[eros]]). The two uses face each other across the book. Helen's longing draws her toward Menelaus and home; Paris' draws her back to his bed. Between them Aphrodite, whose “lovely breasts” (στήθεά θʼ ἱμερόεντα, 3.397) are one of the signs by which Helen knows her, threatens her into going (3.413–420).
