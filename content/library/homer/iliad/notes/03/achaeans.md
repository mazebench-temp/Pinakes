**Silence.** The book opens with a contrast. The Trojans come on with clamor, like cranes, but the Achaeans come on “in silence, breathing fury, eager in their hearts to stand by one another” (3.8–9). Plato's Socrates quotes the line as an example of discipline (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]). See [[silence]] and [[breathing-fury]].

**The truce.** Hector imagines the “long-haired Achaeans” cackling at Paris' retreat (3.43). When he steps out holding his spear by the middle, they shoot arrows and throw stones at him until Agamemnon stops them (3.79–83). Menelaus' acceptance makes both armies rejoice, “hoping for an end to miserable war”; they get down from their chariots and lay their armor on the ground, close to the Trojans' (3.111–115). Twice, at the oath and at the lots, one nameless voice speaks “of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297–301, 3.319–323), and the two armies pray as one. See [[tis-speech]] and [[oath]].

**Seen from the wall.** Priam asks who “this Achaean man” is (Ἀχαιὸς ἀνήρ, 3.167, 3.226), and marvels at their numbers: the Phrygians he once saw gathered against the Amazons “were not as many as the bright-eyed Achaeans” (3.190). Helen can see “all the other bright-eyed Achaeans” and could name them (3.234). At the end the Achaeans applaud Agamemnon's claim of victory (3.461). See [[bright-eyed]] and [[well-greaved]].
