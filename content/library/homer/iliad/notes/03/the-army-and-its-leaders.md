**Two armies.** The book begins with the armies marshaled “each company with its leaders” (3.1), the Trojans with clamor, the Achaeans in silence (3.2–9). When Menelaus accepts the duel, “the Achaeans and the Trojans rejoiced, hoping for an end to miserable war”; they rein in their horses, get down, strip off their armor and lay it on the ground “close to one another, and there was little ground between them” (3.111–115). For the rest of the book the people of both sides sit together and watch (3.326–327, 3.341–343).

**One voice.** Twice the narrator gives the people's words in the form “and this is what a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297, 3.319; see [[tis-speech]]). At the oath they curse whoever breaks it first (3.298–301); at the lots they pray that whoever brought these troubles may die, and that there may be friendship and trusty oaths (3.320–323). The two armies speak as one crowd, and their wish is the same.

**The leaders named.** From the wall the enemy's leaders are seen as a king would see them, one by one. Priam picks out Agamemnon, “both a good king and a strong spearman”, whose army is larger than any he has seen (3.166–190); Odysseus ranging through the ranks like a ram through the flock (3.193–198); Ajax towering above the Argives, “the bulwark of the Achaeans” (3.226–229); and Idomeneus among the Cretans, with the leaders of the Cretans gathered round him (3.230–231). Helen can name all the rest (3.234–235). See [[the-view-from-the-wall]] and [[marshals-of-the-people]].

**The people's verdict.** At the end the Trojan people judge their own leader's son. None of them would have hidden Paris from Menelaus, “for he was hateful to them all, like black doom” (3.451–454). When Agamemnon claims the victory, “the other Achaeans applauded” (3.461). See [[laos]], [[trojans]] and [[achaeans]].
