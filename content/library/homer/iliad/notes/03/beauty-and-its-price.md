summary: Book 3 is built around looking at beauty and counting its cost. Paris' looks hide a coward, Helen's make old men forgive a war, Priam admires the enemy king, Odysseus' plain looks hide his power, and Aphrodite's beauty shows through her disguise.

**The handsome coward.** The book begins with a beautiful man who runs. Paris steps out in a leopard skin to challenge the best of the Argives, sees Menelaus, and shrinks back like a man who has met a serpent (3.15–37; see [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]). Hector's rebuke puts looks and courage on opposite sides of the scale: “best in looks”, and “no strength in his heart, and no courage” (3.39, 3.44–45). His lyre, his hair and his looks, the gifts of Aphrodite, will not help him in the dust (3.54–55). The woman he brought home is also “lovely” (εὐειδής, 3.48). See [[eidos]].

**A beauty that forgives a war.** On the tower the old men of Troy, too old to fight, see Helen coming.

{{quote:476eb6c1-ae21-5073-8975-ce9354c33f2e}}

Her beauty excuses the war: she is “terribly” (αἰνῶς, from αἰνός, “dread”) like the immortal goddesses to look on. The admiration has fear in it, and the next lines draw the conclusion: “But even so, beautiful as she is, let her go home in the ships” (3.159–160). See [[no-blame]].

**The enemy's beauty.** Priam's questions to Helen begin with admiration of the enemy king: “so handsome a man I have never yet seen with my eyes, nor one so stately; for he looks like a king” (3.169–170). His praise of Agamemnon's army follows (3.182–190). Seen from the wall, the Achaeans are measured by their looks: Agamemnon handsome and kingly, Odysseus shorter but broader, like a ram among the sheep (3.193–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]), Ajax towering by head and shoulders (3.227).

**Looks that deceive.** Antenor's Odysseus reverses the pattern of Paris. Seated, he was the more stately; rising to speak, he looked like a fool until his words came “like the snowflakes of winter” (3.211–224). Paris is more beautiful than he is brave; Odysseus is more powerful than he looks.

**Beauty in the chamber.** After the duel Aphrodite describes Paris to Helen: “shining with beauty and fine clothes; you would not say that he had come from fighting a man, but that he was going to the dance” (3.392–394). Helen sees through the goddess's own disguise by her beauty, her neck, her breasts, her flashing eyes (3.396–398; see [[thambos]]). When she sits facing Paris, she turns her eyes away from him (3.427).

**The price.** The book counts the cost in its own words. Helen is “a great grief” (πῆμα) to Paris' father and city (3.50), and the old men want her gone so that she will not be a grief (πῆμα) to them and their children (3.160). She calls herself “dog-faced” and “hateful” (3.180, 3.404). The last word on Paris is that he was hateful to all the Trojans, “like black doom” (3.454). See [[beauty-and-its-price]] and [[who-is-to-blame]].
