---
summary: Book 3 measures honor in public shame and public payment. Paris is a disgrace, laughed at by the enemy; Helen fears the reproaches that attach to her; Menelaus nearly wins untold glory; and Agamemnon's oath demands a satisfaction “that will be remembered among men who are yet to come”.

**Disgrace.** Hector's rebuke is a catalogue of dishonor. Paris is a λώβη, “a disgrace”, and “scorned by others” (3.42); the long-haired Achaeans are “cackling with laughter” at the Trojans for fielding a champion with nothing but looks (3.43–45); he is “a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (3.51). Dishonor in Homer is what the enemy laughs at. See [[aischros]] and [[oneidos]].

**Shame that spreads.** Helen imagines her brothers kept from the battle “for fear of the shame and the many reproaches that are mine” (3.242), and she refuses to go to Paris' bed because the Trojan women would blame her (3.410–412; see [[nemesis]]). Dishonor attaches to her family and to her name in the city.

**Glory denied.** Menelaus has Paris by the helmet, and he “would have dragged him off and won untold glory” (ἄσπετον ἤρατο κῦδος, 3.373), had not Aphrodite broken the strap. The κῦδος of victory is taken from him in the moment of winning (see [[kydos]]).

**Honor paid.** Agamemnon's oath treats the war as a matter of compensation. If Menelaus kills Paris, the Trojans must give back Helen and the possessions, “and pay the Argives such satisfaction (τιμήν) as is fitting, one that will be remembered among men who are yet to come” (3.286–287). If they refuse, he will fight on “for the sake of the penalty” (3.290). It is the τιμή that Achilles said the army came to win for Menelaus (1.159), and at the end of the book Agamemnon claims it (3.459–460). See [[time]] and [[poine]].
