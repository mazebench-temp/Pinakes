summary: The duel is proposed to decide “whichever of us wins”, and the word runs through the book to its last speech. Menelaus wins without killing Paris; Paris says Menelaus won with Athena's help; Agamemnon says that victory clearly belongs to Menelaus.
passages:
  - 3.67-75 | Paris proposes the duel
  - 3.436-440 | With Athena's help
  - 3.455-461 | Agamemnon claims the victory
---

**Whoever wins.** Paris proposes the terms, and Hector repeats them: “whichever of us wins and proves the stronger, let him take all the possessions and the woman, and lead them home” (νικήσῃ, 3.71–72, 3.92–93). Iris tells Helen that she will be called the dear wife “of the one who wins” (3.138), and the herald Idaeus tells Priam that to the one who wins the woman and the possessions will go (3.255). Four times the book states the prize in the language of victory.

**What counts as winning.** The oath says something else. Agamemnon swears to the terms “if Alexander kills Menelaus … but if fair-haired Menelaus kills Alexander” (3.281–284), and Menelaus had asked that the man for whom death is prepared should die (3.101–102). The duel ends with no one dead. Helen, refusing Aphrodite, says that Menelaus “has now beaten brilliant Alexander” (νικήσας, 3.403–404). Paris concedes it and credits a goddess: “This time Menelaus has won (ἐνίκησεν), with Athena's help; another time I will beat him” (3.439–440). Agamemnon claims it before both armies in the proposal's word: “victory (νίκη) clearly belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (3.457). The Achaeans applaud (3.461). The Trojans do not answer, and in Book 4 the gods see to it that the question is settled by war. See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]].
