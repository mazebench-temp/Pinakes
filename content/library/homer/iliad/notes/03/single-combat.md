**The proposal.** Shamed by Hector, Paris offers the terms himself: let the others sit down, set him and Menelaus “in the middle” to fight for Helen and all her possessions, and let “whichever of us wins and proves the stronger” take the woman and the possessions home; the rest will swear friendship, the Trojans to stay in Troy and the Achaeans to sail home (3.67–75). Hector holds back the Trojan ranks with his spear, Agamemnon stops the Achaean archers (3.76–85), and Hector repeats the proposal to both armies (3.86–94; see [[message-repeated]]).

**The acceptance.** Menelaus accepts in sterner words, “whichever of us two death and fate are prepared for, let him die” (3.101–102), and asks for an oath with victims, sworn by Priam himself (3.103–110; see [[oath]]). The men get down from their chariots and lay their armor on the ground, “and there was little ground between them” (3.113–115).

**The stakes.** Helen and her possessions (see [[possessions]]). Each speaker frames them for his listener. Iris tells Helen that she “will be called the dear wife of the one who wins” (3.138); Idaeus tells Priam that the two will fight “for the woman” (3.254). The oath changes “wins” to “kills” and adds a satisfaction to be paid if Paris falls (3.281–291).

**The ground and the lot.** Hector and Odysseus, one from each side, measure out the ground and shake the lots in a helmet to decide who will throw first (3.314–317). The armies pray that the man who caused the war may die, and Paris' lot leaps out (3.318–325; see [[lots]]). Paris arms in full, Menelaus in a single verse (3.328–339; see [[arming-scene]]), and they walk into the middle and stand close “on the measured ground” (διαμετρητῷ ἐνὶ χώρῳ, 3.344), brandishing their spears.

**The fight.** They throw in turn, as the lot decided. Paris' spear strikes Menelaus' shield, and its point bends back (3.346–349). Menelaus prays to Zeus and throws; his spear goes through Paris' shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, but Paris swerves (3.350–360). Menelaus' sword shatters on Paris' helmet, and he seizes the helmet and drags him toward the Achaeans, choked by the strap (3.361–372). See [[spear]], [[sword]] and [[helmet]].

**What goes wrong.** The rules have no place for a god. “And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory, had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it”: she breaks the strap and carries Paris to his bedroom (3.373–382; see [[divine-rescue]]). No one has been killed, and the oath spoke only of killing. Agamemnon falls back on Paris' word, victory, and claims it for his brother:

The demand for satisfaction repeats the words of the oath (3.459–460; 3.286–287). The other side grants the victory in its own words. Helen says that Menelaus “has now beaten brilliant Alexander” (3.403), and Paris that “this time Menelaus has won, with Athena's help” (3.439), though the narrative shows no help from Athena. But the Trojans give no answer. The book ends with the Achaeans' applause (3.461), and the narrator has already said that Zeus was not yet going to fulfill the oath (3.302). In Book 4 a Trojan arrow breaks the truce, and the war goes on (see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]).
