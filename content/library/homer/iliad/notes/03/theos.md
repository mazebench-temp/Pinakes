---
summary: In Book 3 the gods are blamed and credited by turns. Priam says the gods are to blame for the war, Paris that their gifts cannot be refused and that “we have gods on our side too”; Helen is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on, and tells a goddess to give up the paths of the gods.
grc: [θεῇς]
passages:
  - 3.161-165 | Priam calls Helen
  - 3.380-382 | As a god can
  - 3.396-412 | Helen and Aphrodite
---

**Blame and credit.** Priam absolves Helen: “it is the gods who are to blame (θεοί … αἴτιοι), who roused against me the tearful war of the Achaeans” (3.164–165; see [[aitios]]). Paris claims that “the glorious gifts of the gods” are not to be cast aside (3.65), and after his defeat that Menelaus won with Athena, “for we have gods on our side too” (3.440). Menelaus, whose sword breaks, cries that no god is more baneful than Zeus (3.365). Priam, leaving the field, says that Zeus “and the other immortal gods” know which of the two is fated to die (3.308–309).

**Like a god.** Helen is “terribly like the immortal goddesses (θεῇς) to look on” (3.158), and Idomeneus stands among the Cretans “like a god” (θεὸς ὣς, 3.230). Aphrodite snatches Paris away “very easily, as a god can” (ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός, 3.381): what is impossible for a man costs a god no effort.

**The paths of the gods.** Helen tells Aphrodite to go and sit beside Paris herself, “and give up the paths of the gods, and never again turn your feet back to Olympus” (3.406–407), until he makes her his wife or his slave. A mortal woman tells a goddess to become mortal. The goddess answers with a threat, and the mortal obeys (3.413–420). See [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[divine-intervention]].
