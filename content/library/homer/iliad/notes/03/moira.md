**Death and fate.** Menelaus accepts the duel: “whichever of us two death and fate (θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα) are prepared for (τέτυκται), let him die” (3.101–102). The pairing is common in Homer, and it makes μοῖρα the portion that ends in death. Priam, leaving the field, says the same thing another way: Zeus and the gods know for which of the two “the end of death is fated” (πεπρωμένον, 3.309), a word from a different root, πορεῖν, “give, allot”. See [[thanatos]].

**Child of fortune.** Told that the huge, handsome Achaean is Agamemnon, Priam calls him blessed: “O blessed son of Atreus, child of fortune, favored by heaven” (μοιρηγενὲς ὀλβιόδαιμον, 3.182). μοιρηγενής is “born with a good portion”, one whose share was given at birth (see [[child-of-fortune]]). The next line gives the proof: “truly many young men of the Achaeans have been made subject to you” (3.183). A king's μοῖρα is measured in the men he rules. See [[daimon]] and [[a-good-king-and-a-strong-spearman]].

A third word of portion comes into Paris' answer to Hector: the rebuke is κατʼ αἶσαν, “fair” (3.59; see [[aisa]]).
