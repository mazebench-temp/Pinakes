**Death by agreement.** Menelaus accepts the duel on the terms of a death: “whichever of us two death and fate (θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα) are prepared for, let him die (τεθναίη); and let the rest of you be parted” (3.101–102). The war is to be ended by one man's death instead of many. The oath repeats the condition as a killing (3.281–284), and the prayer before the lots asks the gods to send the guilty man “down into the house of Hades” (3.322). No one dies in Book 3.

**Death wished for.** Helen answers Priam with a wish: “I wish that evil death (θάνατος κακός) had pleased me when I came here with your son” (3.173). It is one of three wishes in the book that someone had died. Hector wishes that Paris had never been born or had died unmarried (3.40; see [[never-born]]), and after the duel Helen tells Paris she wishes he had died on the field (3.428).

**Death fated.** Priam leaves the field before the duel, unable to watch his son fight.

{{quote:01991671-c87d-547d-8896-d1949f23cece}}

“Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods, for which of the two the end of death (θανάτοιο τέλος) is fated.” The old king knows that one of them is meant to die, and that the gods know which. Neither is killed. See [[moira]] and [[ker-doom]].
