---
summary: Hector wishes that Paris had never been born and had died unmarried (ἄγονος, ἄγαμος, 3.40), the book's one privative pair. Its single privatives gather around Paris, Priam's sons and Odysseus.
grc: [ἄγονός, ἄγαμός]
en: [unmarried]
---

**The pair.** Hector's rebuke begins with a wish built on two privatives: αἴθʼ ὄφελες ἄγονός τʼ ἔμεναι ἄγαμός τʼ ἀπολέσθαι, “I wish you had never been born, or had died unmarried” (3.40; see [[never-born]]). The two words are spread across the verse, each with its verb and its τε. ἄγονος here means “unborn”; in later Greek it can also mean “childless”. The wish takes back Paris' birth and his marriage, the marriage from which the war came (see [[marriage]]).

**Singles.** Paris calls Hector's heart ἀτειρής, “unyielding”, and his mind ἀτάρβητος, “fearless” (3.60, 3.63), and says that the gifts of the gods are not ἀπόβλητα, “to be cast aside” (3.65; see [[litotes]]). Menelaus calls Priam's sons ἄπιστοι, “not to be trusted” (3.106). Before he spoke, Antenor says, Odysseus held the scepter ἀστεμφές, “unmoving”, and looked ἀΐδρις, “knowing nothing”, and ἄφρων, “a fool” (3.219–220). Menelaus would have won ἄσπετον … κῦδος, “untold glory” (3.373), and Helen has griefs ἄκριτα, “past measure” (3.412).
