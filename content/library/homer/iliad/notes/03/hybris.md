Menelaus accepts the duel and asks for Priam to swear the oaths himself, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted, so that no one may violate the oaths of Zeus by transgression” (3.106–107). Both words are compounds of ὑπέρ, “over, beyond”. ὑπερφίαλος describes a man who goes beyond measure, arrogant and violent; in the *Odyssey* it is the suitors' word, and Athena says of them that they “behave with outrage, overbearingly” (ὑβρίζοντες ὑπερφιάλως, *Odyssey* 1.227). ὑπερβασίη, “a stepping over”, is the transgression itself, the crossing of a line that an oath or a custom has drawn.

Menelaus ties the fault to youth: the minds of younger men are always flighty, while an old man looks before and after (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). Near the end of the poem the same Menelaus hears the other side. In the chariot race Antilochus has cut him off by a trick, and when Menelaus challenges him, the young man gives way and asks pardon: “you know what the transgressions (ὑπερβασίαι) of a young man are like; his mind is quicker, but his judgment thin” (23.589–590).
