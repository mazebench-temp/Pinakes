**Sent for.** Priam is asked for before he appears. Menelaus accepts the duel on one condition:

{{quote:f054d837-abd8-59e4-ab3a-b67b9633b0e2}}

"The mighty Priam" is Πριάμοιο βίην, literally "the might of Priam", an epic way of naming a hero ([[might-of-priam]]). The Achaean king trusts the old Trojan king's oath, not his sons', and the saying about old men who look "both before and after" is spoken with Priam in mind (see [[before-and-after]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**"Dear child."** When the poem finds him, he is sitting by the Scaean gates with the elders (3.146–153; see [[trojan-elders]]). The old men say that no one could blame the two armies for suffering so long for such a woman, and that she should go home all the same. Priam calls her to sit in front of him:

{{quote:0bf4d405-d02f-599f-ab87-63f250320f9f}}

He calls her φίλον τέκος ("dear child") and clears her before she speaks: she is not αἰτίη ("to blame"); the gods are αἴτιοι. See [[aitios]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]. It is his judgment, not Helen's: she answers, calling him "dear father-in-law" (ἑκυρέ; see [[in-laws]]), by wishing she had died before she followed his son (3.172–176). In her lament for Hector at the end of the poem she remembers that Priam was "always gentle, like a father" (24.767–772).

**The view from the wall.** He asks her to name "this huge man", handsomer and more stately than any he has seen, "for he looks like a king" (3.166–170). It is Agamemnon, and Priam answers with praise and a memory:

{{quote:5ded9b53-35be-55b4-9746-5622553418d1}}

μοιρηγενής ("born to good fortune") and ὀλβιοδαίμων ("blessed by a god") occur only here in Homer ([[child-of-fortune]]). In his youth he went to vine-rich [[Phrygia]] as an ally (ἐπίκουρος) and fought beside the people of [[Otreus]] and [[Mygdon]] on the [[Sangarius]] on the day the [[Amazons]] came (see [[priam-and-the-amazons]]). He measures the present by the past, as Nestor does ([[old-age-and-memory]]), and this time the past is smaller: the Phrygian host was not as large as the army below him. The king who once went to help others is now defended by allies himself ([[trojan-allies]]). Next he asks about Odysseus and finds his image in the flocks: a thick-fleeced ram going through a great flock of white sheep (3.191–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). Last he asks about Ajax (3.225–227).

Readers have long asked why Priam, in the tenth year of the war, needs to be told who the Achaean leaders are. Some answers look to the scene itself: the armies are at rest and without armor (3.114, 3.195), the king is old, he wants to draw Helen out. Most modern critics hold that the scene belongs to the beginning of the war and that the poet has set it in the tenth year, as the duel and the Catalogue of Ships also recall how the war began (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]).

**The oath.** The herald [[Idaeus]] calls him by his father's name, "Rise, son of Laomedon" (3.250; see [[laomedon]]). "The old man shuddered" (ῥίγησεν, 3.259), but he has the horses yoked, takes the reins himself and drives out through the Scaean gates with Antenor (3.259–263). At the sacrifice he says nothing. Agamemnon's terms bind "Priam and the sons of Priam" to pay if Alexander falls (3.288–290).

**He cannot watch.** Then "Priam, descendant of Dardanus" (Δαρδανίδης, 3.303; see [[dardanus]]) speaks to both armies:

{{quote:01991671-c87d-547d-8896-d1949f23cece}}

Zeus knows the end; the father does not want to see it. "The man equal to the gods" (ἰσόθεος φώς, [[equal-to-the-gods]]) puts the lambs into his chariot and drives back to the city (3.310–313). The poem does not say what becomes of them; the boar of Agamemnon's oath in Book 19 is thrown into the sea (19.266–268). The father who will not watch this duel will see Hector's death from the wall (22.25–78) and lament it (22.408–428).
