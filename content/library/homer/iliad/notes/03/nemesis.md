The word comes twice in Book 3, once to excuse and once to refuse.

**No blame.** When the old men on the tower see Helen, they say to one another: “There is no blame (οὐ νέμεσις) that Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans should suffer sorrows for so long for such a woman” (3.156–157). The negative marks what is excusable: no one could be indignant at men who fight for a beauty like a goddess'. But they go on, “even so, beautiful as she is, let her go home in the ships” (3.159). The war is excused; her staying is not. See [[no-blame]].

**A thing for blame.** After the duel Aphrodite comes to take Helen to Paris' bed, and Helen refuses.

{{quote:a597c362-d6e6-5694-adf8-d958a691e854}}

“I will not go there; it would be a thing for blame” (νεμεσσητὸν δέ κεν εἴη). The indignation she fears is that of the Trojan women, who “would all hereafter blame me” (μωμήσονται, from μῶμος, “blame”). Menelaus has won, and by the terms of the duel she is his (3.403–404); to share Paris' bed now would be improper in the eyes of the city. She is afraid of what people will say, and it is the goddess who overrules her fear (3.413–420).

In Book 6 Helen tells Hector that she wishes she were the wife of a better man, one who knew “the indignation and the many reproaches of men” (νέμεσίν τε καὶ αἴσχεα πόλλʼ ἀνθρώπων, 6.351). Paris does not; she does. See [[aidos]], [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[helen]].
