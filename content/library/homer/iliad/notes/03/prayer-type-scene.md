**The oath.**

1. **Setting and gesture.** At the oath sacrifice, “lifting up his hands” (χεῖρας ἀνασχών), in the verse of Chryses' second prayer with the king's name for the priest's (3.275; 1.450). See [[sacrifice-type-scene]].
2. **Invocation.** No “hear me”. Agamemnon names four powers with their titles: Zeus “who rules from Ida, most glorious, greatest”, with a place, as Chryses named Chryse and Cilla, but the place is the mountain above Troy; the Sun, who sees and hears all things; rivers and earth; and the two powers below who punish perjurers (3.276–279).
3. **Claim.** None. The gods are asked to watch, not to give: “be witnesses, and keep watch over the trusty oaths” (3.280).
4. **Request.** In place of a wish, the terms, in three conditions (3.281–291; see [[oath]]).
5. **Answer.** The prayer that follows the libation, spoken by men of both armies, is answered as Agamemnon's prayer was in Book 2: “So they spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302; 2.419).

**At the lots.** While Hector shakes the lots, “the people prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods” (3.318), and one voice speaks for both armies (see [[tis-speech]]):

{{quote:0115f9fd-4466-5ebf-b227-3529b71e8c61}}

The invocation repeats Agamemnon's first verse (3.320 = 3.276). The request names no one, but “whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides” means Paris, and the Trojans pray it too. No answer is told. The lot leaps out for Paris (3.325), and in the duel he does not die.

**Menelaus.** As he rushes forward with his spear he prays:

{{quote:d5f22761-0101-592a-98d1-97e34d0d6970}}

There is no gesture and no claim: “Lord Zeus” (Ζεῦ ἄνα), and at once the request, “grant me vengeance” (δὸς τίσασθαι). What stands in place of a claim is a law. Men to come must learn to fear wronging a host who has shown them friendship (see [[xenia]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Again no answer is told, and the event gives it: the spear goes through shield and corselet and misses the man, and the sword shatters. Menelaus turns the address of a prayer into a reproach: “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365–368).
