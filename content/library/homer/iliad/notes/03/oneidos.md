**The reproaches that are hers.** Helen looks along the Achaean ranks for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces and cannot find them. Either they did not come, or they will not enter the battle “for fear of the shame and the many reproaches that are mine” (αἴσχεα δειδιότες καὶ ὀνείδεα πόλλʼ ἅ μοί ἐστιν, 3.242). The reproaches belong to her, but they fall on her kin: brothers share a sister's disgrace. The narrator answers at once that the earth already held them in Lacedaemon (3.243–244). See [[aischros]], [[castor]] and [[polydeuces]].

**Paris reproached.** Paris is reproached twice in the book, by his brother and by his wife. Hector's rebuke is the first speech of the book: Paris is “a disgrace” (λώβη) and “scorned by others” (3.42), “a shame to yourself” (κατηφείη, 3.51). After the duel Helen tells him to go and challenge Menelaus again, and then not to, or he will die (3.428–436). He answers:

{{quote:547b685e-ecba-5024-aa0e-46931b724c5a}}

“Lady, do not rebuke my heart with harsh reproaches” (χαλεποῖσιν ὀνείδεσι). To Hector he conceded that the rebuke was fair, and defended himself with the gifts of the gods (3.59–66). To Helen he concedes the defeat, and credits it to a god: Menelaus won “with Athena's help”, and “we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440). Neither reproach changes him.
