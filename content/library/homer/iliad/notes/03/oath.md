**Friendship and oaths.** Paris offers to fight Menelaus for Helen and her possessions, and the rest to part “making friendship and swearing a trusty truce” (φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμόντες, 3.73). The phrase is literally “cutting trusty oath-offerings”: an oath of this kind is made by killing victims. Book 2 used it for a truce Agamemnon only imagined (2.124); Book 3 performs it. See [[philotes]] and [[oath-formulas]].

**Lambs, and Priam.** Menelaus accepts, and names what the oath needs:

{{quote:3a0bb7c5-c688-51b1-ac9f-4f7013b4fecc}}

The Trojans are to bring two lambs, a white male and a black female, for Earth and the Sun, and the Achaeans a third for Zeus. The poem does not say which lamb is whose; the colors and sexes suit the white male to the Sun and the black female to Earth. Priam must swear “himself”, since his sons are ὑπερφίαλοι καὶ ἄπιστοι, “overbearing and not to be trusted”: ἄπιστοι is the negative of the πιστά the oaths are to be. The oaths belong to Zeus (Διὸς ὅρκια), and to break them is ὑπερβασίη, “overstepping” (3.107). See [[lambs]] and [[priam]].

**The offerings.** Hector sends two heralds to the city for lambs and for Priam, and Agamemnon sends Talthybius to the ships for a lamb (3.116–120). Then the heralds carry through the city “the offerings for the trusty oaths of the gods, two lambs, and wine that cheers the heart, the fruit of the earth, in a goatskin”, and Idaeus brings a mixing bowl and golden cups (3.245–248). Here ὅρκια are things that can be carried: the victims and the wine are the oath before a word of it is spoken. See [[heralds]] and [[idaeus]].

**The rite.** Priam drives down to the plain with Antenor (3.259–266), and the rite is told in forty lines:

1. The heralds bring the offerings together, mix wine in the bowl, and pour water over the kings' hands (3.268–270; see [[hand-washing]] and [[mixing-bowl]]).
2. Agamemnon draws the knife that hangs beside the sheath of his sword, cuts hair from the lambs' heads, and the heralds give it out to the leaders of both sides (3.271–274; see [[knife]] and [[hair]]).
3. He prays aloud with lifted hands, calls the witnesses (3.275–280) and states the terms (3.281–291).
4. He cuts the lambs' throats and lays them on the ground (3.292–294).
5. Wine is drawn from the bowl in cups and poured out, and the men of both armies speak the curse (3.295–301; see [[libation]]).

**The witnesses.**

{{quote:21e4284e-c1b5-5925-be78-6308bf3433a8}}

Zeus is invoked as the god “who rules from Ida”, the mountain above Troy; the Sun because he sees and hears everything; then the rivers and the earth, and “you two who below the earth punish dead men, whoever has sworn a false oath”. The dual τίνυσθον implies two powers, and the text does not name them. They are usually taken to be Hades and Persephone ([[hades]]); some think of the Erinyes, who in the oath of Book 19 punish false swearers under the earth (19.258–260). Punishment after death is rare in the Iliad, and here it is kept for the perjurer. See [[who-rules-from-ida]], [[helios]] and [[earth]].

**The terms.** If Alexander kills Menelaus, he keeps Helen and all the possessions, and the Achaeans sail home. If Menelaus kills Alexander, the Trojans give back Helen and the possessions and pay a τιμή, a “satisfaction”, “one that will be remembered among men who are yet to come”. If Priam and his sons will not pay it, Agamemnon will fight on “for the sake of the penalty” (εἵνεκα ποινῆς) until the war is ended (3.281–291). Two things have changed since the proposal. Paris offered to fight until one of them “wins and proves the stronger” (3.71); the oath speaks only of killing. And the satisfaction is Agamemnon's own addition: Paris, Hector and Idaeus named only Helen and the possessions (3.72, 3.93, 3.255). Both changes matter when the duel ends with no one dead. See [[time]], [[poine]] and [[single-combat]].

**The killing and the curse.** Agamemnon “cut the lambs' throats with the pitiless bronze, and laid them on the ground, gasping, their life failing, for the bronze had taken away their strength” (3.292–294). The victims die where they stand. There is no altar, no barley and no fire; nothing is burned for the gods and nothing is eaten (see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]). The death is told in the words of battle, the “pitiless bronze” ([[pitiless-bronze]]), the strength (μένος) that the bronze takes away. Then the wine is poured:

{{quote:77023816-983f-5be9-aff0-dea0c88f39ef}}

The wine on the ground is the image of the curse: “may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine” (ὧδε … ὡς ὅδε οἶνος). It reaches the perjurer's children too, and his wife, who will be “subdued by others”. The narrator answers at once, in the words that closed Agamemnon's prayer in Book 2: the son of Cronus “was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302; 2.419).

**What the victims are for.** Priam says that he cannot bear to watch his son fight, puts the lambs in his chariot and drives back to Ilios (3.303–313). The poem does not say what becomes of them, or of the Achaeans' lamb for Zeus, but they are not eaten. When Agamemnon swears over a boar in Book 19, the herald throws the carcass into the sea for the fish (19.266–268). The victims of an oath are not a meal shared with the gods, as at Chryse (1.447–474). They show what will happen to the man who breaks it. Priam, whom Menelaus wanted because he could be trusted, says nothing in the rite. Agamemnon speaks the terms for both sides, and the curse is what “a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297; see [[tis-speech]]).

**Broken.** In Book 4 the gods decide that the Trojans shall be the first to do harm “against the oaths” (ὑπὲρ ὅρκια, 4.66–72), the words of the curse (3.299), and Pandarus' arrow wounds Menelaus in the truce. Over his wounded brother Agamemnon says that the oath and “the blood of lambs” are not in vain, and that Zeus, if not at once, will fulfill it late (4.155–168). See [[the-bow-of-pandarus]] and [[the-oaths-sworn]].

## Outside Homer

Other languages also make a treaty with a blow: Latin says *foedus ferire*, “to strike a treaty”, and Hebrew *kārat bərît*, “to cut a covenant”; Jeremiah tells of a calf cut in two, between whose halves the parties to a covenant passed (Jeremiah 34:18). The curse by analogy has close parallels. In Livy the Roman fetial priest, making a treaty, asks Jupiter to strike the Roman people if they are the first to break it, as he strikes the pig before him, and kills it with a flint (1.24.7–9). Christopher Faraone set the wine of Iliad 3 among such rites (“Molten Wax, Spilt Wine and Mutilated Animals: Sympathetic Magic in Near Eastern and Early Greek Oath Ceremonies”, *Journal of Hellenic Studies* 113, 1993). Pausanias still knew the rule that no one eats a victim over which an oath has been sworn, and cites the boar of Iliad 19 for it (5.24.10). He also saw near Sparta the “Tomb of the Horse”, where Tyndareus buried the horse on whose pieces Helen's suitors had sworn to defend the man she married (3.20.9).
