---
summary: In Book 3 the φρένες are where Paris lacks strength, where young men's thoughts flutter, and where desire covers Paris' mind. Odysseus, about to speak, looks like a man without them.
grc: [φρονέω, ἄφρονα]
en: [minds, I think, fool]
passages:
  - 3.43-45 | No strength in his heart
  - 3.105-110 | Bring the mighty Priam
  - 3.216-224 | Odysseus rises to speak
  - 3.441-446 | Let us go to bed
---

**Strength and sense.** The φρένες hold courage as well as thought. The Achaeans took Paris for a champion because of his looks, “but there is no strength in his heart (φρεσίν), and no courage” (3.45).

**Young and old.** Menelaus wants Priam to swear the oath, because “the minds (φρένες) of younger men are always flighty”, while an old man looks both before and after (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). He has already stated his own judgment with the verb of the φρένες: φρονέω, “I think”, that the armies should now be parted (3.98).

**Looking like a fool.** Antenor remembers Odysseus rising to speak with his eyes on the ground and the scepter held stiff, like a man who knows nothing (see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]): “you would have said he was some surly fellow, and a fool (ἄφρονα) as well” (3.220). ἄφρων is literally “without φρένες”. Then his voice came out, and no other mortal could rival him (3.221–224).

**Desire.** Paris tells Helen that never has desire so wrapped his φρένες (ἔρως φρένας ἀμφεκάλυψεν, 3.442; see [[eros]]). The mind that desire covers is the one Hector found empty of courage.
