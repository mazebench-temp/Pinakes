The word comes twice in Book 3, once for a spectacle and once for a god.

**The spectacle.** When the two men have armed, they walk into the middle between the Trojans and the Achaeans, “glaring terribly; and amazement held those who watched” (θάμβος δʼ ἔχεν εἰσορόωντας, 3.342). The armies have laid down their arms and sit as spectators (3.113–115, 3.326–327), and the war has become something to watch. Helen and the old men watch it too, from the tower. See [[single-combat]].

**The goddess.** Aphrodite comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-worker from Lacedaemon and tells her that Paris is waiting in his chamber (3.386–394).

{{quote:df8d2ca8-fead-5edf-bcf7-78e1a032b790}}

Helen notices the goddess's beautiful neck, her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes, “and she was amazed” (θάμβησεν), and she speaks to her. The scene answers Achilles' meeting with Athena in Book 1. There too a mortal was amazed and knew the goddess at once, and there too the recognition is told through eyes: “and her eyes blazed terribly”, in the translation's reading of an ambiguous line (1.199–200; see [[whose-eyes-blaze]]). Achilles asked Athena why she had come (1.202); Helen asks Aphrodite why she longs to deceive her (3.399). Achilles took the goddess's advice, since “whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen” (1.218). Helen refuses, and goes only under threat (3.414–420). See [[divine-epiphany]] and [[divine-intervention]].
