summary: Book 3 is a contest between two husbands for one wife. Hector taunts Paris with the wife of a better man, the duel is to decide whose wife Helen will be called, the oath curses the wives of whoever breaks it, and the book ends with the loser and his wife in bed while the winner hunts for him.

**The better man's wife.** Hector's rebuke names the marriage Paris broke. If he would stand and face Menelaus, “you would learn what kind of man he is whose flourishing wife you have” (θαλερὴν παράκοιτιν, 3.53; see [[flourishing]]). The man who has the wife and the man she belongs to are not the same.

**Whose wife.** Iris tells Helen what the duel will decide: “you will be called the dear wife (ἄκοιτις) of the one who wins” (3.138). Then she fills her with longing “for her former husband” (3.139–140), and Priam calls her to see him (3.163). Paris arms as “the husband of Helen of the lovely hair” (3.329). The book gives both men the title, and the duel is meant to take it from one of them. See [[posis]].

**Wives as a penalty.** The curse sworn by both armies at the oath ends with wives: whoever first breaks the oaths, may their brains pour out like this wine, theirs and their children's, “and may their wives be subdued by others” (ἄλοχοι δʼ ἄλλοισι δαμεῖεν, 3.301). A broken truce is to be paid for with other men's marriages, the fate of every sacked city (see [[captive-women]]).

**Wife or slave.** Helen, refusing to go to Paris, tells Aphrodite to go and sit beside him herself, until he makes her “his wife, or his slave” (ἢ ἄλοχον … ἢ ὅ γε δούλην, 3.409). A woman taken by a man may be either. She will not share his bed: “it would be a thing for blame” (3.410; see [[nemesis]]).

**Husband and wife.** When she is brought to his chamber, she sits facing him, turns her eyes away, “and she rebuked her husband” (πόσιν, 3.427): she wishes he had died, “beaten by a stronger man, who was my former husband” (3.428–429). Paris answers with desire (3.441–446; see [[eros]]), and “his wife followed with him” (ἄκοιτις, 3.447). Outside, the husband who won the duel ranges through the throng like a wild beast looking for him (3.449–450). The book ends with the marriage it was meant to undo. See [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[in-laws]].
