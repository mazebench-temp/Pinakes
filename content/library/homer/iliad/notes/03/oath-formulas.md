---
summary: No one in Book 3 swears “by” a god. The oath is sworn in the language of ritual, “cutting trusty oaths” with friendship, calling the gods as witnesses, stating terms, and cursing whoever first does harm “against the oaths”.
grc: [ὅρκια πιστὰ, Διὸς ὅρκια, ὑπὲρ ὅρκια, μάρτυροι ἔστε]
en: [trusty oaths, in defiance of the oaths, be witnesses]
passages:
  - 3.276-301 | The words of the oath
---

Book 1 swore by a god or a thing, with ναὶ μά and οὐ μά and the “great oath” sworn on the scepter (1.86, 1.233–244). Book 3 has none of these. Its oath is made by a rite, and its words describe the rite or belong to it (see [[oath]]).

**“Trusty oaths.”** ὅρκια πιστά occurs eight times in the book (3.73, 3.94, 3.245, 3.252, 3.256, 3.269, 3.280, 3.323). With the verb τάμνω, “cut”, ὅρκια names the whole act, “to cut trusty oaths”, that is, to kill the victims over which the oath is sworn (3.73, 3.94, 3.105, 3.252, 3.256). Four times the phrase is paired with friendship, φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστά (3.73, 3.94, 3.256, 3.323), the last time in the armies' prayer at the lots, that “friendship and trusty oaths may be ours”. The oath makes a φιλότης, a bond, between enemies (see [[philotes]]). The oaths are πιστά, “to be trusted”, and Priam must come because his sons are ἄπιστοι, “not to be trusted” (3.106). Book 2 used the phrase once, for an imagined truce (2.124).

**Whose oaths.** They are “the oaths of Zeus” (Διὸς ὅρκια, 3.107) and the gods' (θεῶν … ὅρκια πιστά, “the offerings for the trusty oaths of the gods”, 3.245, 3.269). In these two lines ὅρκια are the offerings themselves, the lambs and the wine that the heralds carry and bring together.

**Witnesses and terms.** Agamemnon names the powers that are to watch, and gives them their task: ὑμεῖς μάρτυροι ἔστε, φυλάσσετε δʼ ὅρκια πιστά, “be witnesses, and keep watch over the trusty oaths” (3.280). Achilles called the heralds μάρτυροι in Book 1 (1.338); here the witnesses are gods. The perjurer is ὅτις κʼ ἐπίορκον ὀμόσσῃ, “whoever has sworn a false oath” (3.279). Then come the terms, as conditions: εἰ μέν κεν …, εἰ δέ κʼ …, εἰ δʼ ἂν …, “if … but if … but if …” (3.281, 3.284, 3.288).

**Breaking.** Breaking an oath is going beyond it. Menelaus fears that someone may “violate the oaths of Zeus by transgression” (ὑπερβασίῃ … δηλήσηται, 3.107), and the curse falls on “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths” (ὁππότεροι πρότεροι ὑπὲρ ὅρκια πημήνειαν, 3.299). The words come back in Book 4, when the gods plan that the Trojans shall be the first to do harm “against the oaths” (ὑπὲρ ὅρκια δηλήσασθαι, 4.66–72).

**The curse.** The last formula of the oath is a curse on the perjurer, spoken over the poured wine: “may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine, theirs and their children's, and may their wives be subdued by others” (3.300–301). It is the same kind of speech as Odysseus' threat in Book 2, which staked his own head and name (2.259–264), but here each man calls it down on his own side if it breaks faith.
