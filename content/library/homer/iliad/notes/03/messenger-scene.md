**Iris.** The first messenger scene of the book lacks its first step. “Iris came as a messenger (ἄγγελος) to white-armed Helen” (3.121), but the poem does not say who sent her. In Book 2 she came “from aegis-bearing Zeus” (2.786–787). She takes the likeness of Laodice, Helen's sister-in-law, finds her “in the hall” at her loom (τὴν δʼ εὗρʼ ἐν μεγάρῳ, 3.125), as the heralds found Achilles in Book 1 (1.329), stands near and speaks (3.129). Her message is news and an invitation: the war has stopped, and the two men will fight for her (3.130–138). There is no reply. Iris puts longing in her heart, and Helen goes (3.139–145). See [[iris]], [[laodice]] and [[divine-epiphany]].

**Idaeus.** Hector sends two heralds to the city to bring the lambs and summon Priam, and Agamemnon sends Talthybius to the ships (3.116–120). In the city the herald Idaeus, “standing beside the old man”, urges him with the message (3.249–258). It repeats the terms of the duel in the words that Paris and Hector used, with the persons changed for a Trojan speaking to his king (see [[message-repeated]]). Priam shudders, but obeys at once (3.259–260). See [[idaeus]] and [[heralds]].

**Aphrodite.** After the duel Aphrodite goes to call Helen, “and she found her on the high tower” (τὴν δὲ κίχανε / πύργῳ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῷ, 3.383–384). She comes as the old wool-comber who loved Helen in Lacedaemon, plucks her robe, and delivers a summons:

{{quote:ca5838d5-b994-51ba-8b95-184b2e7e5340}}

The poem reports no word from Paris between the rescue and the summons. The sender is named, but the message is the goddess's own. It is the only message in the book that is refused. Helen knows the goddess and will not go (3.399–412), until Aphrodite threatens her (3.414–417).

Twice the summons begins δεῦρʼ ἴθι, “Come here” (3.130, 3.390), and Priam too calls Helen to him with δεῦρο (3.162). Helen is summoned three times in the book, and each time she goes.
