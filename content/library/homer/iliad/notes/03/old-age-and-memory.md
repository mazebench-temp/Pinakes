**An old man's judgment.** Menelaus asks that Priam himself swear the truce, because “the minds of younger men are always flighty; but in whatever an old man takes part, he looks both before and after” (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). Old age is trusted for the future as well as the past.

**The old men on the tower.** At the Scaean gates sit the elders of the people, Priam's counselors, “done with war now because of their age, but excellent speakers, like the cicadas that in the woods sit on a tree and send out their lily-soft voice” (3.149–152; see [[like-cicadas]] and [[trojan-elders]]). They can no longer fight, but they can still speak, and their judgment of Helen is the most famous speech in the book (3.156–160; see [[no-blame]]). On the wall and at the summons to the oath the narrator calls Priam simply “the old man” (ὁ γέρων, ὁ γεραιός; 3.181, 3.191, 3.225, 3.249, 3.259).

**Memory.** Two old Trojans remember the world before the war. Priam, seeing the Achaean army, remembers when he went to Phrygia as an ally and saw the Phrygian host gathered by the Sangarius against the Amazons, and says that even they were not as many as the Achaeans (3.184–190; see [[priam-and-the-amazons]]). Antenor remembers the embassy of Menelaus and Odysseus, whom he received as guests, and how each of them spoke in the Trojan assembly (3.205–224; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). Helen's memories run back too, to the home she left (3.173–175) and to Menelaus entertaining Idomeneus in their house (3.232–233).

**An old woman.** When Aphrodite comes to call Helen from the tower, she takes the likeness of “an aged woman, a wool-comber, who, when she lived in Lacedaemon, used to work fine wool for her, and loved her most of all” (3.386–388). The goddess borrows an old servant's shape and an old affection from Helen's past. It does not hide her beauty (3.396–398). See [[eidos]] and [[priam]].
