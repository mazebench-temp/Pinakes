Priam has called Helen to sit beside him, calling her “dear child” and telling her that she is not to blame (3.162–165). Her first words to him are the words of αἰδώς.

{{quote:327b71cb-428c-5699-9f2d-72d3c3c29fa7}}

αἰδοῖός τέ μοί ἐσσι φίλε ἑκυρὲ δεινός τε: “You are one I respect, dear father-in-law, and one I fear.” αἰδοῖος is the one to whom αἰδώς is due. The pairing of αἰδοῖος and δεινός is a formula of reverence: Hephaestus says of Thetis, “a dread and revered goddess is in my house” (δεινή τε καὶ αἰδοίη, 18.394), and in the *Odyssey* Athena makes Odysseus “dread and revered” to the Phaeacians (δεινός τʼ αἰδοῖός τε, *Odyssey* 8.22). Helen's respect for Priam is the right relation of a daughter-in-law to her husband's father (see [[in-laws]]), and she turns from it at once to blame herself.

The book's other fears of shame are hers as well. She imagines her brothers staying away from the battle “for fear of the shame (αἴσχεα) and the many reproaches that are mine” (3.242; see [[aischros]] and [[oneidos]]), and she refuses Paris' bed because the Trojan women would blame her (3.410–412; see [[nemesis]]). αἰδώς is felt before others, and in Book 3 it is Helen who feels it.
