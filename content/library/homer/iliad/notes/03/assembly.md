The Trojan elders who sit at the Scaean gates are “done with war now because of their age, but excellent speakers” (ἀλλʼ ἀγορηταὶ / ἐσθλοί, 3.150–151). For the old, the assembly has taken the place of the battle (see [[trojan-elders]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

Antenor remembers Menelaus and Odysseus among “the Trojans gathered in assembly” (Τρώεσσιν ἐν ἀγρομένοισιν, 3.209). Standing, Menelaus was the taller; seated, Odysseus looked the more stately. When “they began to weave their words and counsels before all”, Menelaus spoke (ἀγόρευε) fluently, in few words but very clear; Odysseus stood with his eyes on the ground and his scepter unmoving, until the great voice came (3.209–224). See [[scepter]], [[weaving]], [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[three-styles-of-speech]].

The truce makes one gathering of the two armies. Hector goes into the middle and the Trojans sit down (3.77–78), the Achaeans fall silent (3.84–85), and four speakers address both sides with the opening of an assembly speech, “Hear me” (κέκλυτε): Hector (3.86), Menelaus (3.97), Priam (3.304) and, at the end, Agamemnon (3.456). See [[hear-me]].

The elders' words about Helen are not an assembly speech. They speak “softly … to one another” (ἦκα πρὸς ἀλλήλους … ἀγόρευον, 3.155; see [[winged-words]]).
