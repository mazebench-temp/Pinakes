summary: Book 3 is the book in which the war stops. Both armies hope for an end to miserable war, sit in silence with their spears fixed beside them, and watch two men fight; the old men on the tower are done with war, and Agamemnon swears to fight on until the end of the war if the Trojans refuse the penalty.

The book begins as a battle and becomes a spectacle. The armies advance on each other (3.1–15), Paris steps out and challenges the best of the Argives “to fight him face to face in grim combat” (μαχέσασθαι, 3.19–20), and within a hundred lines the war has stopped.

**An end to war.** At Menelaus' acceptance of the duel, Achaeans and Trojans alike rejoice, “hoping for an end to miserable war” (ὀϊζυροῦ πολέμοιο, 3.112). They get down from their chariots, strip off their armor and lay it on the ground, with little ground between them (3.113–115). Iris tells Helen what has happened: the men who were bringing tearful Ares on one another, “longing for deadly war”, now sit in silence, “and the war has stopped” (πόλεμος δὲ πέπαυται, 3.132–135). The old men of Troy on the tower are also “done with war” (πολέμοιο πεπαυμένοι, 3.150), because of their age. The same verb, παύω, “stop”, is used of the whole plain and of the old men.

**Two men fight.** The fighting is now done by two men, while the armies sit and watch (3.340–345; see [[single-combat]]). Priam cannot bear to see his son fight (μαρνάμενον, 3.307). After the duel Aphrodite tells Helen that Paris looks as if he had come not “from fighting a man” but from a dance (μαχεσσάμενον, 3.393), and Helen tells Paris to stop, and not “make war face to face” with Menelaus, or he will die (3.433–436).

**The end of the war.** Agamemnon's oath looks further. If the Trojans refuse the penalty, he will stay and fight “until I reach the end of the war” (τέλος πολέμοιο, 3.291). See [[the-war-in-small]] and [[poine]].
