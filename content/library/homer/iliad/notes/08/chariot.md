summary: Book 8 is a battle of chariots. Nestor's trace horse is shot and Diomedes takes the old man into his own car to drive for him; Hector loses one charioteer after another; the gods drive to Ida and out to the gates of heaven; and at nightfall Poseidon sets the chariot of Zeus on its stand while the Trojans tie their horses beside their chariots on the plain.

**The trace horse.** When the Achaean leaders give way, Nestor alone stays, and not by choice: Paris' arrow has struck one of his horses in the brain, and its writhing throws the team into confusion (8.80–86; see [[horses]]). It is his trace horse, a third horse running in harness of its own beside the yoked pair, and the old man darts at it with his sword to cut the harness away (παρηορίαι, 8.87–88). The same accident stops Achilles' chariot in Book 16. Sarpedon's spear kills the trace horse Pedasus, the yoked pair spring apart, the yoke creaks and the reins tangle, until Automedon draws his sword and cuts the dead horse loose (16.467–476).

**One car, two men.** Diomedes tells Nestor that his attendant is a weakling and his horses slow, and takes him into his own car, drawn by the horses of Tros he took from Aeneas (8.102–108). The attendants take charge of Nestor's team, and the old horseman takes the reins:

{{quote:95ffd98d-3d19-5b06-b075-3388c6f2539f}}

The roles are those Aeneas offered Pandarus in Book 5: one man drives, the other fights (5.221–228). Nestor is the poem's master of the chariot, who marshaled the Pylian horsemen and told them how to fight from their cars (4.297–309), and who will teach his son how to drive round the turning post (23.306–348). Yet when Zeus' thunderbolt falls before the horses, “the glossy reins slipped from Nestor's hands” (8.137), and it is the driver, not the fighter, who reads the sign and turns the team to flight (8.139–157; see [[thunderbolt]]).

**Hector's charioteers.** Diomedes' spear misses Hector and kills his charioteer Eniopeus as he holds the reins (8.118–123; see [[eniopeus]]). Hector finds another at once, “and not for long were the two horses without a driver” (8.126–127; see [[archeptolemus]]). Elsewhere σημάντωρ is a commander (4.431) or a herdsman (15.325); here, rendered “driver”, it is the one who directs the team. Teucer's arrow, meant for Hector, kills Archeptolemus in the verses of Eniopeus' death (8.313–317 ≈ 8.121–125), and Hector gives the reins to his brother Cebriones and leaps “to the ground from the all-gleaming chariot” to fight on foot with a stone (8.318–321; see [[cebriones]] and [[stone-throwing]]). The verse returns once, when Diomedes leaps down from his car at the end of the chariot race (23.509). Cebriones too will die at his post, struck by Patroclus' stone while “holding the horses' reins” (16.737–743), the half-verse of Eniopeus' death (8.121).

**Falling from the chariot.** Eniopeus, Agelaus and Archeptolemus fall in the same half-verse, ἤριπε δʼ ἐξ ὀχέων, “he fell from the chariot” (8.122, 8.260, 8.314; as at 5.47, 5.294). Agelaus is speared in the back as he turns his team to flight (8.257–259; see [[agelaus]]).

**The gods' chariots.** Zeus drives to Ida and back, and Hera and Athena set out for the war and are turned back at the gates of heaven (8.41–50, 8.381–396, 8.432–441; see [[chariot-journey]]). Athena steps into the “flaming chariot” (ὄχεα φλόγεα, 8.389 = 5.745; see [[flaming]]), and Zeus threatens to hurl the goddesses from it and smash it (8.402–403). When he comes home from Ida, Poseidon puts his chariot away:

{{quote:a1d0ff60-902c-50af-a940-d517062ad98e}}

βωμός, an altar everywhere else in the book (8.48, 8.238, 8.249; see [[altar]]), is here a stand or platform, like the pedestals on which the golden youths stand in Alcinous' hall (Od. 7.100). λῖτα is a linen cloth. One is spread under the chair Telemachus sets for the disguised Athena (Od. 1.130), and fine linen covers Patroclus' body and, later, his bones (λιτί, 18.352, 23.254). A chariot not in use was kept covered, as the Myrmidons' chariots stand “well covered” in the huts while Achilles keeps out of the war (2.777), and Pandarus' eleven new chariots stand at home under cloths (5.194–195).

**On the plain.** At nightfall the Trojans step down from their chariots to hear Hector (ἐξ ἵππων, 8.492, where “the horses” are the team and car together). Then they unyoke their horses “and tied them with straps beside their chariots, each man by his own” (8.543–544), and the horses stand beside the cars all night (8.565; see [[horses]]). A chariot is also wealth: the gift of honor Agamemnon promises Teucer may be “two horses with their chariot” (8.290; see [[geras]]).
