The imagined speech is not a nameless man's but Hector's, and it is a boast (ἀπειλήσει, “he will boast”, 8.150). Its frame is that of the speeches of “someone” in time to come: a future day, a speaker, a one-line speech in direct words, and a closing verse, ὥς ποτʼ ἀπειλήσει, “So he will boast one day”, in place of ὥς ποτέ τις ἐρέει, “so someone will say one day” (see [[tis-speech]]). Agamemnon closed such a speech in Book 4 with the same wish: he imagined a Trojan leaping on Menelaus' tomb and taunting the Achaeans, “So someone will say one day: then may the wide earth gape for me” (4.176–182). Hector's own imagined speeches are of praise and fame (6.459–462, 7.87–91; see [[kleos]]); Diomedes imagines his shame in his enemy's mouth.

Nestor's answer turns the imagined audience against the speaker: “Even if Hector calls you a coward and a weakling, the Trojans and the Dardanians will not believe him, nor the wives of the great-hearted shield-bearing Trojans, whose husbands in their prime you have flung in the dust” (8.153–156). The widows are the proof, and they would be the judges.
