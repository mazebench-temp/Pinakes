summary: Book 8 is the first place where Homer says where the fallen Cronus is. At dawn Zeus threatens to hurl any god who disobeys him into Tartarus, as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth (8.13–16), and at nightfall he tells Hera that he does not care for her anger, not even if she goes to the lowest limits of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronus sit in Tartarus without sun or wind (8.477–483).

**Iapetus and Cronus.** Zeus ends the day's quarrel with Hera by naming the place of his father:

{{quote:0eec6ccb-bbef-5d71-886b-fff2591c49d5}}

τὰ νείατα πείραθʼ … γαίης καὶ πόντοιο, “the lowest limits of earth and sea”, are the bottom of the world. There Iapetus and Cronus are ἥμενοι, “sitting”, as prisoners, with no joy “in the rays of Hyperion the Sun or in the winds”, and Τάρταρος is “all around them” (see [[tartarus]], [[cronus]], [[iapetus]] and [[hyperion]]). This is the only place where Homer names Iapetus. In Hesiod he is a Titan, a brother of Cronus and the father of Atlas and Prometheus (*Theogony* 134, 507–511). The rest of the Iliad keeps the same picture without the names: Zeus “set Cronus beneath the earth and the barren sea” (14.203–204), and the Titans are “the gods below, who are around Cronus” (14.274, 15.225) and “beneath Tartarus” (14.278–279). Hesiod has the same phrase for them, “the Titans beneath Tartarus, who are around Cronus” (*Theogony* 851).

**The threat at dawn.** The same day began with the same place. Any god who goes off to help either side, Zeus told the assembly, will be beaten, or Zeus will take him and hurl him “into misty Tartarus, very far off, where the pit beneath the earth is deepest, where the gates are of iron and the threshold of bronze, as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth” (8.13–16). Hesiod uses the same measure, but from the earth: Tartarus lies as far beneath the earth as the sky is above it, a fall of nine days and nights for a bronze anvil (*Theogony* 720–725), and he too gives the place a threshold of bronze (811). Hesiod's Tartarus is the prison of the defeated Titans (*Theogony* 717–735). Zeus' threat puts a disobedient god where the old gods are, and at nightfall he names them. His rule rests on the victory that put his father there (see [[zeus]]).

**Son and father.** Book 8 calls Zeus the son of Cronus often. Athena opens her plea with “Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords” (8.31), Nestor says that “Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today” (8.141), Iris that “this is the threat of the son of Cronus, and he will make it good” (8.415), and Hera calls him “most dread son of Cronus” (8.462). Hera is herself “daughter of great Cronus” (8.383). In the prophecy Zeus speaks of himself in the third person, “you will see the son of Cronus, supreme in might” (8.470), and nine lines later he names Cronus himself, sitting in Tartarus (8.479).
