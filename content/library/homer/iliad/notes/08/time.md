summary: Hector taunts Diomedes with the honors of the feast that the Danaans will now take back; Athena complains that Zeus has granted Thetis' plea to honor Achilles; Agamemnon promises Teucer a gift of honor; and Hector ends the day wishing that he were honored as Athena and Apollo are honored.

**Honor withdrawn.** As Nestor turns Diomedes' horses to flight, Hector shouts after him: “Son of Tydeus, the Danaans of the swift horses honored you above others with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups; but now they will dishonor you: you are no better than a woman after all” (8.161–163). The honors are the ones a king gives his best men. Agamemnon told Idomeneus in Book 4, in a verse that Hector's echoes, “Idomeneus, I honor you above the Danaans of the swift horses”, with a cup that always stands full (4.257–263), and Sarpedon names seat, meat and full cups, in Hector's very words, as the reason why he and Glaucus must stand in the front line (12.310–321). Honor at the feast is paid for in battle; a man who runs forfeits it, and ἀτιμήσουσι, “they will dishonor”, is Hector's threat that the Achaeans themselves will take it back (see [[retreat]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]).

**Honor promised.** Agamemnon, watching Teucer shoot from under Ajax's shield, promises him the first gift after his own when Ilios is sacked, “a tripod, or two horses with their chariot, or a woman” (8.286–291). The word is πρεσβήϊον, “a gift of honor”, kept apart in the translation from γέρας, “prize” (see [[geras]] and [[teucer]]). The promise sets out the scale of honor in the army: the king first, then the man who has done most.

**Honor for Achilles.** Athena, arming against her father's ban, says that he has fulfilled the plans of Thetis, who kissed his knees, “begging him to honor Achilles, sacker of cities” (8.370–372). τιμῆσαι is the word of Thetis' request in Book 1, “honor my son” (1.505–510; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]), and Zeus will use the same verse when he tells Hera the whole of his plan (15.76–77). The day's defeat is the honor of Achilles, paid in Achaean lives.

**The honor of gods.** At the end of his night assembly Hector wishes that he were immortal and ageless all his days, “and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored” (8.539–540; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). τίω is the verb of the honor paid to gods and to the best of men. Hector's people already treat him almost as a god: Achilles, over his body, will boast that the Trojans prayed to him as to a god, and Hecuba that they greeted him like one (22.393–394, 22.433–435). Here Hector asks for the gods' honor itself (see [[gods-and-mortals]]).
