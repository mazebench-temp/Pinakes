summary: Athena, and later Hera in the same words, promises that the gods will keep out of the war but “put counsel in the Argives' minds”. Hera does so, putting it into Agamemnon's mind to rally the army; and Athena complains that Zeus has fulfilled the plans of Thetis.

en: [should will]

**Counsel instead of war.** Zeus has forbidden the gods to help either side. Athena answers for them: “We will keep out of the war, as you command; but we will put counsel in the Argives' minds that will help them, so that they do not all perish because of your anger” (8.35–37). Hera repeats the words to Zeus at the end of the day, after the two goddesses have tried to drive into the battle and been turned back (8.463–468; see [[athena]] and [[hera]]). βουλή here is advice put into men's minds (ὑποθησόμεθα, “we will suggest”), the help a god may give without fighting. The book shows it given once. Hector would have burned the ships, “had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind” to rouse the Achaeans (8.217–219; see [[phrenes]] and [[aidos]]), and the rally that follows lasts until Zeus rouses the Trojans again (8.335). The next counsel comes from a man: in the night Nestor proposes the embassy to Achilles (9.93–113).

**The plans of Thetis.** Athena, arming to go against her father's command, says that Zeus hates her now “and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis” (Θέτιδος δʼ ἐξήνυσε βουλάς, 8.370), who kissed his knees and begged him to honor Achilles (8.370–372; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). The proem calls what is being fulfilled “the plan of Zeus” (Διὸς δʼ ἐτελείετο βουλή, 1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Athena names the same plan from the other side, as the request of a goddess whom Zeus has preferred to his daughter (see [[fathers-and-daughters]]). Zeus, for his part, is “the highest counselor” (ὕπατον μήστωρα, 8.22) and “Zeus the counselor” (μητίετα, 8.170; see [[counselor]] and [[metis]]).

**Willing victory.** Hera reproaches Poseidon, who receives the Achaeans' gifts at Helice and Aegae: σὺ δέ σφισι βούλεο νίκην, “so you should will them victory” (8.204). βούλεο, from βούλομαι, occurs only here; the translation takes it as an imperative, though it can also be read as “you used to wish them victory” (see its note on 8.204 and [[nike]]).
