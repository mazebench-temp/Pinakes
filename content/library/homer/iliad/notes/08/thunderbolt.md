summary: The thunderbolt is first thrown in the poem in Book 8, in front of Diomedes' horses, and Nestor reads it as a sign that Zeus is not with them. Then Zeus threatens Hera and Athena with wounds from it that will not heal in ten years.
passages:

  - 8.402-405 | The threat to Hera and Athena
---

**Before Diomedes.** Diomedes and Nestor drive at Hector, and the Trojans would have been penned in Ilios like lambs, had not Zeus been quick to see it (8.130–132; see [[would-have-had-not]]):

{{quote:1a09a5c3-6b65-5143-b366-ea91657ff640}}

The bolt does not strike the men. It falls in front of the horses, a warning that stops the charge, and Nestor reads it at once: “Do you not see that no help from Zeus goes with you?” (8.140; see [[teras]]). Diomedes still wavers, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.170–171; see [[decision-scene]]). Before the thunderbolt there was only thunder and a blazing flash, the sign that followed the scales at midday (8.75–77). The thunderbolt is the god's own hand in the battle on the day he has forbidden every other god to fight (8.5–27).

**Against the gods.** When Hera and Athena drive out to help the Achaeans, Zeus sends Iris with his threat: he will lame their horses, hurl them from the chariot and smash it, “and not in ten circling years will they heal of the wounds the thunderbolt deals them” (8.402–405, repeated by Iris at 8.416–419; see [[message-repeated]] and [[circling-years]]). At dawn he threatened a beating, or Tartarus (8.10–16); the thunderbolt is the weapon that makes the threat good. When the goddesses have turned back, he tells them what would have happened: “struck by the thunderbolt, you would not have come back on your chariot to Olympus, where the seat of the immortals is” (8.455–456).
