summary: The fear of Book 8 comes from Zeus. Pale fear takes the Achaeans when he thunders and flashes from Ida after weighing the day, and his thunderbolt terrifies Diomedes' horses and old Nestor. At night he mocks Hera and Athena, whose limbs trembled before they ever saw the war.

**Fear sent by Zeus.** The night before, Zeus thundered over the feasting armies, “and pale fear took hold of them” (7.478–479; see the note on Book 7). At noon of the next day he weighs the armies' fates, thunders from Ida and sends a blazing flash among the Achaeans, “and when they saw it they were amazed, and pale fear took hold of them all” (8.75–77; see [[pale-fear]] and [[thambos]]). Then the leaders break: Idomeneus, Agamemnon and the two Ajaxes do not dare to stay, and only Nestor is left, held by a wounded horse (8.78–86; see [[retreat]]). The second sign falls in front of Diomedes' chariot (see [[thunderbolt]]). “Thundering terribly, he let fly a gleaming thunderbolt”, the flame of burning sulfur rises, the two horses cower beneath the chariot in terror, and the reins slip from Nestor's hands, “and he was afraid in his heart” (8.133–138). Fear here is the right response to Zeus, and Nestor's counsel to flee is the reasoning of a frightened man who is also right (8.139–144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Diomedes' fear is of another kind: of what Hector will say of him (8.147–150; see [[aidos]]).

**The goddesses' fear.** When Hera and Athena come back from their aborted drive, Zeus taunts them with it: “trembling seized your shining limbs before you ever saw the war and the grim deeds of war” (8.452–453). τρόμος, “trembling”, is the fear of men before a stronger enemy (7.215, Hector's Trojans at the sight of Ajax). Iris had already called Athena a “shameless dog” (κύον ἀδεές, 8.423) for daring to lift her spear against her father. ἀδεής is literally “without fear”, and to be fearless before Zeus is to be shameless. Hera calls Artemis by the same name when the gods fight in Book 21 (21.481).
