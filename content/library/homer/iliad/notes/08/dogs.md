summary: Book 8 names dogs more often than any book before it, and most of them are insults. Teucer calls Hector a mad dog, Iris calls Athena a shameless dog, Zeus tells Hera that nothing is more doglike than she, and Hector calls the Achaeans dogs borne along by the fates; there are also the hound of a simile, the carrion dogs of Athena's wish, and the hound of Hades.

**Insults.** Four speakers use the dog as an insult, and two of the four are gods.

Teucer, when eight of his arrows have found their men, says of Hector: “but this mad dog I cannot hit” (κύνα λυσσητῆρα, 8.299; see [[mad-dog]], [[teucer]] and [[hector]]). λυσσητήρ, “raging one”, occurs only here; it is formed from λύσσα, the battle-fury that Odysseus, in the embassy of Book 9, tells Achilles “has entered” Hector (9.239, 9.305). In later Greek λύσσα is also the madness of dogs, rabies. It is the first time an Achaean calls Hector a dog, and not the last: “Again you have escaped death, dog”, Diomedes and Achilles both shout after him (11.362, 20.449), and Achilles answers his last plea with “Do not beg me by my knees, dog” (22.345).

Iris, carrying Zeus' threat to Hera and Athena, adds words of her own to Athena's face: “But you are most dread, you shameless dog, if you really dare to lift your huge spear against Zeus” (8.423–424). κύον ἀδεές, “fearless” and so “shameless dog”, is Hera's word for Artemis in the battle of the gods (21.481; see [[shameless-dog]]). Zeus had given Iris no such words, and the verses 8.420–424 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.420–424 and [[iris]]).

Zeus himself ends his answer to Hera: he does not care about her sulking, not even if she goes to the lowest limits of earth and sea, “since there is nothing more shameless than you” (οὐ σέο κύντερον ἄλλο, 8.483; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]). κύντερος is a comparative made from κύων, “more doglike”. The Odyssey uses it of the belly, which makes a man remember it however grieved he is (*Odyssey* 7.216), and of a woman who murders her husband, as Clytemnestra did (11.427). The dog is the emblem of shamelessness (see [[anaideia]] and [[dog-face]]).

At nightfall Hector hopes “to drive away from here these dogs borne along by the fates, whom the fates bring here on the black ships” (κύνας κηρεσσιφορήτους, 8.527–528; see [[borne-by-the-fates]] and [[ker-doom]]). The compound occurs only here, and the next verse spells it out.

**The hound.** In the narrator's simile Hector harries the Achaeans as a hound on swift feet catches at a wild boar or a lion from behind and watches for it as it wheels (8.338–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). Teucer's insult has become a picture: the mad dog is now a hunting dog, and the beasts it chases are the Achaeans.

**Carrion.** Athena, about to arm and help the Achaeans, wants to see whether some Trojan “will glut the dogs and the birds with his fat and his flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans” (8.379–380). The pair is the proem's, “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5), and Agamemnon's threat to the shirker (2.393; see [[birds]]).

**The hound of Hades.** Athena recalls that Eurystheus sent Heracles “to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades”, and that she saved him (8.367–369). Homer does not name the dog; Hesiod calls him Cerberus (*Theogony* 311; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]] and [[hades]]).
