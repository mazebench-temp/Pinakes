summary: Horses carry the battle of Book 8 and end it. Nestor's trace horse is shot in the brain, Diomedes drives the horses of Tros, Hector calls on his four horses by name, the gods' horses are yoked, threatened and stabled, and the Trojans' horses stand all night beside their chariots, munching barley and spelt, waiting for dawn.

**Nestor's horse.** Paris' arrow strikes Nestor's trace horse “on the top of the crown, where the foremost hairs of horses grow on the skull, and a blow is deadliest” (8.83–84). The narrator knows the anatomy of a horse as he knows a man's. The half-verse μάλιστα δὲ καίριόν ἐστι, “and a blow is deadliest”, occurs only twice in Homer, here and at the collarbone where Hector's stone strikes Teucer (8.325–326). The horse rears, the arrow sinks into its brain, and its writhing throws the team into confusion until Nestor cuts it loose (8.85–88; see [[chariot]]).

**The horses of Tros.** Diomedes calls Nestor's horses slow (8.104) and invites him into his own car, “so that you may see what the horses of Tros are like, how they know the plain, how to pursue and to flee very swiftly this way and that” (8.105–107). The three verses are the ones Aeneas spoke when he invited Pandarus into his chariot behind the same horses (5.221–223), and μήστωρε φόβοιο, “masters of rout”, is the title they bore then (8.108 = 5.272; see [[master-of-rout]]). They are the breed Zeus gave Tros for his son Ganymede, and Diomedes took them from Aeneas in Book 5 (5.263–273, 5.319–327; see [[tros]] and [[aeneas]]). Their captor praises them in their first master's words. Old Nestor's horses are slow again in Book 23, where he warns his son Antilochus that his team is the slowest in the chariot race (23.309–310).

**In fear.** When Zeus' thunderbolt falls before them, “the two horses in terror cowered beneath the chariot” (8.136); καταπτήτην, “cowered”, is found only here (see [[thunderbolt]]). When a charioteer is killed, his horses “shied back” (ὑπερώησαν, 8.122, 8.314; again 15.452).

**Hector's four.** Hector calls on four horses by name, Xanthus, Podargus, Aethon and Lampus, and asks them to repay their keep (see [[the-horses-of-hector]]):

{{quote:4244ef5b-f11b-5bfb-b263-e573e36f4c95}}

A Homeric team is a pair, sometimes with a trace horse; a team of four appears only here, at 11.699, and in a simile of the Odyssey (Od. 13.81), and Hector's speech addresses its horses with duals (8.186, 8.191; see the translation's note on 8.185–191). Wine given to horses occurs nowhere else in Homer (see [[wine]]). The horses' wine is mixed for them “to drink whenever your heart desired” (πιεῖν, ὅτε θυμὸς ἀνώγοι, 8.189), nearly the Greek words Agamemnon used of the full cup he keeps for Idomeneus, the cup of honor (πιέειν ὅτε θυμὸς ἀνώγοι, 4.263; see [[feast]]).

**The ditch.** Hector boasts that his horses “will easily leap over the ditch they have dug” (8.179), and when the Achaeans are driven back, the space inside the ditch fills “with horses and shield-bearing men” (8.213–214). Diomedes is the first to drive his horses back across it (8.254–255). In Book 12 the Trojans' horses will balk at its edge and neigh, afraid of the leap (12.50–59; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The gods' horses.** Zeus yokes “his two bronze-footed horses, swift-flying, with flowing manes of gold” (8.41–42), in the verses of Poseidon's yoking in Book 13 (13.23–24; see [[bronze-footed]]). Hera's horses wear golden headbands (8.382; see [[with-golden-headbands]]). Zeus threatens to lame them under the chariot (8.402, 8.416), and on Olympus the Hours unyoke them and tie them “at their ambrosial mangers” (8.433–434; see [[chariot-journey]] and [[ambrosia]]).

**Night on the plain.** Hector orders the Trojans to unyoke their fine-maned horses and set fodder beside them (8.503–504; see [[fine-maned]]), and they do it in the verse of a stable at Sparta: “They unyoked their sweating horses from under the yoke” (8.543 = Od. 4.39). The book ends with the horses:

{{quote:571a0630-c99f-55ed-821c-0f311cd3e32c}}

“Munching white barley and spelt” is said of Pandarus' horses standing idle at home (5.196). The Trojans' horses stand ready, waiting with the army for the dawn that will carry them to the ships (see [[night-watch]]).
