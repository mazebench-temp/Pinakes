**Help from Zeus.** When the thunderbolt falls before their horses, Nestor asks Diomedes: “Do you not see that no help from Zeus goes with you?” (ὅ τοι ἐκ Διὸς οὐχ ἕπετʼ ἀλκή, 8.140). ἀλκή is rendered “courage” elsewhere in this translation, but here it is the protecting strength that Zeus lends a side and can withhold (see the translation's note on 8.140). Hector puts the thought in the same words when it is his turn to read the signs: “easily known among men is the help of Zeus”, both for those to whom he gives the higher glory and for those he diminishes and will not defend (15.490–492; see [[kydos]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The book's formula for the day's turn, ἑτεραλκέα νίκην, “the victory that turns the battle” (8.171), is made from the same noun: strength given to one of two sides (see [[nike]]).

**Courage remembered.** Hector rallies the Trojans with a verse he uses throughout the poem: “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (8.174; also 6.112, 11.287, 15.487, 15.734, 17.185, and Patroclus to the Myrmidons, 16.270). Courage is something a man can forget and must be reminded of. Its opposite is the insult Diomedes fears. Nestor tells him that even if Hector calls him “a coward and a weakling” (κακὸν καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, 8.153), the Trojans will not believe him, nor the wives of the men he has killed (8.152–156; see [[retreat]] and [[deos]]).

**Clothed in courage.** When the Achaeans rally, most of the heroes who follow Diomedes across the ditch are those who stood up to face Hector in Book 7, and four verses are the same (8.262–265 = 7.164–167): Agamemnon, now with Menelaus, “the two Ajaxes, clothed in furious courage”, Idomeneus and Meriones, and Eurypylus. In Book 7 nine stood up, Thoas and Odysseus among them (7.161–168). Here too there are nine, and the ninth is the archer: “and Teucer came ninth, stretching his back-bent bow” (8.266; see [[teucer]]).
