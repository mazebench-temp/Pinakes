**The two Ajaxes.** When Zeus thunders from Ida and sends his flash among the Achaeans, the leaders give way: “Then neither Idomeneus dared to stay, nor Agamemnon, nor did the two Ajaxes stay, attendants of Ares” (8.78–79; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). θεράποντες Ἄρηος, “attendants of Ares”, is a title of the whole army (2.110, 6.67, 7.382; see [[danaans]]); here, and when the two volunteer for the night raid (10.228), it belongs to the pair, given to them in the verse that tells their retreat. When the eagle of Zeus has rallied the army they come out after Diomedes, “clothed in furious courage”, in the verse that brought them to their feet for Hector's challenge (8.262 = 7.164; see [[furious]]).

**At the end of the line.** Agamemnon goes to the ship of Odysseus, in the middle of the camp, so that his shout can carry to both ends: “both to the huts of Ajax, son of Telamon, and to those of Achilles”, who had drawn up their ships at the farthest ends, “trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands” (8.222–226; see [[agamemnon]] and [[achilles]]). The two strongest fighters hold the flanks. The same verses return when Strife shouts from Odysseus' ship (11.5–9); here 8.224–226 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.222–226). The patronymic Τελαμωνιάδης, “son of Telamon”, appears for the first time in this book (8.224, 8.267), and in Homer it always names Ajax (see [[telamon]] and [[telamonian]]).

**The shield over Teucer.** Teucer comes ninth across the ditch and takes his stand under the shield of Ajax. Ajax moves it aside for him to shoot, and Teucer slips back to him, “like a child under its mother”, to be hidden again (8.266–272; see [[teucer]], [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). The great Ajax is the mother of the simile. When Hector's stone breaks Teucer's bowstring and fells him, the shelter becomes a rescue:

{{quote:a1f0d41f-bcfb-50af-8d02-c3dbcaebfb12}}

περιβαίνω, “stand over”, is the fighter's stance astride a fallen friend (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). In Book 5 Idaeus, son of Dares, “did not dare to stand over his slain brother” (5.21; see [[idaeus-son-of-dares]]); Ajax “did not neglect his brother” (κασιγνήτοιο, 8.330; see [[kasignetos]]). In Book 17 he stands over the body of Patroclus with his broad shield like a lion over its cubs (17.132–137).
