summary: Zeus forbids the other gods to fight and watches the battle alone from the peaks of Ida, “exulting in his glory”. Hera and Athena watch from Olympus and cannot bear it; Zeus sees them set out and turns them back, and promises Hera a sight for the next morning. At night the gods turn away from the savor of the Trojans' sacrifice.

**The single spectator.** Zeus begins the day by taking the battle from the gods: none of them may help either side (8.5–27). Then he drives to Gargarus, the peak of Ida where he has a precinct and an altar, hides his horses in mist, and sits on the peaks, “exulting in his glory, looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (8.47–52; see [[gargarus]], [[ida]] and [[kydos]]). The same two verses seat him apart from the other gods on the next day of battle, when they blame him for wanting to give the Trojans glory, and a third verse adds what he sees: the flash of bronze, the killers and the killed (11.80–83). From Ida he acts on what he sees: he weighs the day, thunders and flashes (8.68–77), and he is “quick to see” the moment when the Trojans would have been penned in their city (8.132; see [[noos]]). Hera, urging Poseidon to join her, pictures Zeus as a spectator left with nothing to watch: if the gods who favor the Danaans held him off, “he would sit there alone on Ida and fret” (8.205–207).

**The goddesses watch.** Hera, “seeing the men” driven back to their ships, pities them (8.350; see [[pity]]), and Athena arms so that she may see whether Hector will be glad when the two goddesses appear, or whether some Trojan too will glut the dogs and birds (8.375–380). Their watching turns into action, and Zeus, watching them, stops it: “when father Zeus saw them from Ida, he was terribly angry” (8.397; see [[cholos]] and [[iris]]). At the end of the day he offers Hera a sight instead of a part: “At dawn you will see the son of Cronus, supreme in might, more than ever, if you wish, ox-eyed queen Hera, destroying the great army of the Argive spearmen” (8.470–472; see [[prophecy]]).

**The gods turn away.** At night the Trojans sacrifice on the plain, “and the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven”. The bracketed verses that the Second Alcibiades preserves say that the blessed gods took no share of it and did not wish to, for Ilios and Priam and his people were hateful to them (8.548–552; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The book ends with a mortal watcher in a simile: the shepherd who sees the stars come out over the peaks on a windless night and is glad at heart (8.555–559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]). What he watches is the thousand Trojan fires on the plain (8.560–563), and the Achaeans, watching the same fires from their ships, are in despair (9.1–8, 9.76–77; see [[war-and-night]]).
