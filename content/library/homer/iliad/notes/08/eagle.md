---
summary: Zeus answers Agamemnon's prayer with the first eagle of the poem. It carries a fawn in its talons and drops it beside the altar of Zeus, and the Achaeans, seeing that the bird has come from Zeus, rally and cross the ditch (8.245–252).
passages:
  - 8.245-252 | The eagle and the fawn
---

{{quote:408eeff6-8fd8-5722-997f-a90eca3dab07}}

**The sign.** Agamemnon has shamed the army from the ship of Odysseus and prayed with tears: he never passed an altar of Zeus on his way to Troy without burning the fat and thighs of oxen on it, and he asks now only that the Achaeans may escape (8.228–244; see [[agamemnon]] and [[prayer]]). Zeus pities him and nods that the people will be saved and not perish (8.245–246), and the eagle is the nod made visible. It is τελειότατον πετεηνῶν, “surest in omen of winged birds” (8.247): τέλειος is “full-grown” and also “bringing to fulfillment” (τέλος), and the bird is taken as the one whose sign comes true (see [[surest-of-birds]] and the translation's note on 8.247). In its talons it holds νεβρόν, “a fawn, the young of a swift deer” (8.248; see [[deer]]).

**The altar.** The eagle drops the fawn πὰρ Διὸς βωμῷ περικαλλέϊ, “beside the beautiful altar of Zeus”, where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus πανομφαῖος, “lord of all omens” (8.249–250; see [[altar]]). The title is found only here in Homer: ὀμφή is a voice or sign from a god, and Zeus is the source of every one (see [[lord-of-all-omens]] and the translation's note on 8.250). The place answers Agamemnon's plea: the bird drops its prey where the Achaeans' offerings to Zeus were made. The poem does not interpret the sign further. No seer is needed: “when they saw that the bird had come from Zeus, they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (8.251–252). Diomedes is first across the ditch, and Teucer's archery follows (8.253–272; see [[teras]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]).

**What it promises.** The sign answers the prayer that was made. Agamemnon asked for escape, not for victory (8.242–244), and Zeus grants escape: the Achaeans rally, but by nightfall they are driven back to their ships (8.335–349). Zeus means to save the army and to honor Achilles at once, and the poem says so again in Book 13: he wanted victory for Hector and the Trojans to glorify Achilles, “but he did not at all / wish the Achaean army to perish before Ilios” (13.347–350; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**First and last.** The next eagle of omen also drops its prey, a serpent, among the Trojans at the ditch, and that one is read as a warning (12.200–229). The last comes in Book 24, when Priam asks Zeus for a sign before he goes to Achilles' hut, and it is sent in the verse that sent this one: αὐτίκα δʼ αἰετὸν ἧκε τελειότατον πετεηνῶν (8.247 = 24.315). The poem's first eagle of omen is sent to the Achaean king in his despair, and its last to the Trojan king.
