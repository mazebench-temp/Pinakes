---
summary: In the Theaetetus Socrates reads Zeus' golden rope as the sun, and the Second Alcibiades, handed down among Plato's works, is the only source of the four verses in which the gods refuse the Trojans' sacrifice. The Phaedo quotes the line on the depth of Tartarus, the Laches the praise of Aeneas' horses, and the Republic the honors that Hector says the Danaans gave Diomedes.
---

- The golden rope as the sun (*Theaetetus* 153c–d): [[plato-golden-rope]].
- The Trojans' hecatombs refused by the gods (*Alcibiades II* 149c–150a): [[the-refused-sacrifice]].
- **Tartarus.** Describing the greatest of the chasms that run through the whole earth, Socrates quotes Zeus' threat, “very far off, where the pit beneath the earth is deepest” (8.14), and adds that Homer elsewhere, and many other poets, call it Tartarus (*Phaedo* 112a; see [[tartarus]]).
- **The horses of Aeneas.** Arguing that courage is not only standing one's ground, Socrates recalls that Homer praises the horses of Aeneas for knowing “how to pursue and to flee very swiftly this way and that” (8.107, as at 5.223), and calls Aeneas himself a master of rout. In the Iliad the phrase, in the dual, belongs to the horses (8.108, 5.272; *Laches* 191a–b; see [[retreat]] and [[master-of-rout]]).
- **A hero's honors.** In the *Republic* the city will honor its brave men “with seats of honor and meats and fuller cups”. These are the honors that Hector says the Danaans gave Diomedes, “with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (8.162), and that Sarpedon says the Lycians give their kings (12.311; *Republic* 5.468d–e; see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]).
