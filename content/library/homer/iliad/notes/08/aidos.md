---
summary: Hera puts it into Agamemnon's mind to rouse the army, and he opens his rebuke with the verse she herself shouted in Book 5, “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” Elsewhere in the book shame works without the word, in Diomedes' dread of Hector's boast and in the gods' insults.
passages:
  - 8.217-235 | Agamemnon shames the Achaeans
---

**The shout.** Hector has penned the Achaeans against their ditch and would have burned the ships, “had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind to bestir himself and quickly urge on the Achaeans” (8.217–219). Agamemnon climbs Odysseus' ship in the middle of the line and shouts: “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (8.228). The verse is Hera's. In Book 5 she shouted it in the likeness of Stentor, whose voice was as loud as fifty men's, when the Achaeans were giving ground before Ares and Hector (5.784–791). Now she puts the thought into the king's mind, and he speaks her words. αἰδώς used alone as a cry means “Have shame!”: it calls on the men to feel what others will see. The rest of the verse names what they will be called if they do not, ἐλέγχεα, “disgraces” (see [[elenchos]]), and what they seem to be, “admirable in looks” (see [[eidos]]). Agamemnon goes on to their empty boasts on Lemnos (8.229–235; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). Later rallies begin with the same cry: Poseidon's to the young Achaeans at the ships (13.95), Ajax's (15.502), and Sarpedon's to his Lycians, “Shame, Lycians! Where are you fleeing?” (16.422).

**Shame without the word.** Diomedes accepts Nestor's counsel to flee and cannot bear it: Hector will say among the Trojans, “The son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships” (8.148–150; see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[tis-speech]]). Hector says it to his face as he goes: the Danaans honored him with seat, meat and full cups, and now will dishonor him, “no better than a woman” (8.161–166; see [[time]] and [[retreat]]). The same pressure that held the Achaeans silent before Hector's challenge in Book 7, shame on one side and fear on the other, now holds Diomedes three times on the point of turning (8.167–171; see the note on Book 7 and [[deos]]).

**Among the gods.** The gods trade the same reproaches. Iris, adding to Zeus' message, calls Athena a “shameless dog” (κύον ἀδεές, 8.423), and Zeus tells Hera that there is nothing more shameless than she (κύντερον, 8.483; see [[shameless-dog]], [[anaideia]] and [[dogs]]). ἀδεής is literally “without fear”: the shameless are those who fear nothing, not even what a god will do.
