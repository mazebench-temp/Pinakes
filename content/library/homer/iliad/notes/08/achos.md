---
summary: Grief has two causes in Book 8. Hector twice loses a charioteer, “dread grief” clouds his mind, and twice he leaves the dead man and finds another driver; Diomedes feels the same “dread grief” at the thought of Hector's boast, the grief of shame that Agamemnon felt in Book 4.
passages:
  - 8.118-129 | Hector loses Eniopeus
  - 8.145-150 | Diomedes answers Nestor
  - 8.309-319 | Hector loses Archeptolemus
---

**Grief for a comrade.** Diomedes' spear kills Eniopeus, Hector's charioteer, and Teucer's arrow later kills Archeptolemus, who took his place. Each time the same three verses follow: “Dread grief for his charioteer clouded Hector's mind; yet he left him, grieving though he was for his comrade, to lie there” (8.124–126, 8.316–317; see [[phrenes]] and [[hector]]). The grief is real and brief. The battle leaves no time to mourn, and Hector's next act both times is to find a driver: Archeptolemus “and not for long were the two horses without a driver”, then his brother Cebriones (8.126–129, 8.318–319; see [[archeptolemus]] and [[cebriones]]). Book 17 uses the first verse once more, when Apollo tells Hector that Menelaus has killed Euphorbus (17.79–83).

**Grief for a name.** Diomedes, told by Nestor to turn and flee, answers: “But this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit” (8.147), and the grief is for what Hector will say: that the son of Tydeus fled before him to the ships (8.148–150). αἰνὸν ἄχος is what Agamemnon said he would feel if Menelaus died and a Trojan mocked his tomb (4.169–182; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]). The Iliad's grief covers a dead friend and a damaged name with one word, and in Book 1 it was ἄχος that Achilles felt when Agamemnon took his prize (1.188; see the note on Book 1).
