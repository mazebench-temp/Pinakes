summary: Agamemnon prays to Zeus in tears that the Achaeans may at least escape, and is answered at once with a nod and an eagle. Penned at their ships, the Achaeans lift their hands to all the gods, the night they prayed for comes, and Hector hopes, praying to Zeus, to drive them from Troy; most of the book's εὔχομαι words, though, mean boasting.

**Agamemnon's prayer.** Agamemnon shames the army from Odysseus' ship (8.228–235; see [[exhortation]]), and then turns to Zeus. The prayer opens with a reproach, not a request: “Father Zeus, have you ever before struck any of the mighty kings with such blind folly, and robbed him of great glory?” (8.236–237; see [[ate]] and [[kydos]]). The claim follows, the offerings he burned on every altar of Zeus on his way to Troy (8.238–241; see [[sacrifice]]), and then the request, cut down to the least that can be asked:

{{quote:f1708082-e05c-55eb-b9a0-137f39ca4fcb}}

In Book 2 he prayed that the sun might not set before he had burned Priam's palace, and Zeus did not fulfill it (2.412–420). Now he asks only to escape, and Zeus grants it: “So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears, and nodded to him that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.245–246), and an eagle confirms the nod (8.247–252; see [[prayer-type-scene]], [[fulfill-this-wish]] and [[teras]]). The last verse of the prayer returns at the end of Nestor's prayer at the ships in Book 15 (8.244 = 15.376), and Zeus answers that one with thunder (15.377–378).

**At the ships.** Driven back across the ditch, the Achaeans halt by the ships, “calling to one another, and to all the gods they lifted up their hands, and each man prayed aloud” (8.346–347). The three verses return when they are driven back to the ships again in Book 15 (15.367–369). This time no answer is told. Hera sees them and pities them (8.350), and Zeus stops her (8.397–408).

**The night prayed for.** At sunset the night came to the Achaeans “welcome, thrice prayed for” (τρίλλιστος, 8.488; see [[thrice-prayed-for]] and [[sunset-lines]]). The word occurs only here, and is formed from the root of λίσσομαι, “entreat” (see [[supplication]]). The prayers at the ships went unanswered; the night is granted, and Hector says that the darkness has saved the Argives (8.500–501).

**Hector.** Hector closes his orders for the night with a hope: “I hope, as I pray to Zeus and the other gods, to drive away from here these dogs borne along by the fates” (8.526–527). The verse occurs nowhere else; the translation takes εὐχόμενος here as praying (see [[euchomai]]).

**Boasting.** εὔχομαι means “claim” and “boast” as well as “pray”, and in Book 8 it is mostly a boast: the “cries of triumph” of men killing (εὐχωλή, 8.64; see [[the-armies-clash]]), Hector's claim to be Andromache's husband (8.190), his speech to his horses, “So he spoke, boasting” (8.198), Agamemnon's “Where have our boasts gone?” (8.229), and none of the Danaans “could boast” that he crossed the ditch before Diomedes (8.254). Those lines are not counted here (see [[euchomai]]).
