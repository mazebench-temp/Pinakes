**Fewer.** The Trojans arm “throughout the city, fewer as they were” (8.55–56). In Book 2 Agamemnon reckoned that the Achaeans outnumber the Trojans who live in the city by more than ten to one, and that it is the allies who keep him from sacking it (2.123–133; see [[trojan-allies]]). They fight “under hard necessity, for their children and for their wives” (8.57; see [[women-and-war]]), and the gates open as they did when they first marched out (8.58 = 2.809; see [[troy]]).

**Zeus' day.** At noon Zeus weighs “two fates of death that lays men low, of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans”, and the Trojans' fates are lifted toward the sky (8.69–74; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[ker-doom]]). When Diomedes drives at them they would have been “penned up in Ilios like lambs”, had Zeus not thrown his thunderbolt (8.130–136; see [[penned-like-lambs]]), and he thunders three times from Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.170–171; see [[that-turns-the-battle]]). After the Achaean rally, “the Olympian roused might in the Trojans” again, and they drive the Achaeans back across the ditch to the ships (8.335–344; see [[menos]] and [[hector]]). On Olympus Athena hopes that “some Trojan too will glut the dogs and the birds” (8.379–380), and Zeus mocks her and Hera for “destroying the Trojans, against whom you have conceived a dread grudge” (8.449; see [[kotos]]).

**The night on the plain.** “For the Trojans the light went down against their will” (8.487; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[war-and-night]]). At the end of the first day of battle they went back into the city and met in assembly at Priam's doors (7.345–380); now they stay on the plain. They hear Hector's orders, shout their applause, and carry them out (8.489–547). Verses known only from the Second Alcibiades add that they offered hecatombs and that the gods would take no share of the savor, “for sacred Ilios was hateful to them” (8.548–552; see [[hecatomb]]). They sit all night “in high spirits, along the bridges of war”, a thousand fires burning between the ships and the river, with fifty men at each (8.553–563; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]).

Their epithets in the book are “horse-taming” (8.71, 8.110, 8.516, 8.525; see [[tamer-of-horses]]), “great-hearted” and, in Nestor's mouth, “shield-bearing” (8.155, 8.523; see [[great-hearted]] and [[shield-bearing]]).
