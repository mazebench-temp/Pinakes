---
summary: Odysseus is “much-enduring” for the first time in the Iliad in the verse in which he runs past Diomedes' call to stand: “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus did not hear.”
grc: [πολύτλας]
---

**The formula.** πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς is the Odyssey's name for its hero, used there thirty-seven times, and here it appears for the first time in the Iliad ([[brilliant]]). Hector is bearing down on Nestor, and Diomedes calls Odysseus by his full titles, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,” and asks where he is fleeing, “turning your back like a coward in the throng” (8.92–96; [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]], [[of-many-devices]]).

{{quote:5628a4fe-b36c-5fa0-a36c-096fde9509cb}}

**Did not hear.** The verb ἐσάκουσε occurs only here in Homer. It can mean “did not hear,” as the translation renders it, which leaves Odysseus' flight unexplained but makes it no refusal, or “did not heed,” which makes him ignore the call. Either way, the epithet of endurance stands in the verse of his flight, and Diomedes goes to Nestor alone (8.99–100; [[son-of-neleus]]). In Book 11 the same man stands alone when all the Argives have fled, and tells himself that cowards leave the fighting, but the man who is best in battle must stand his ground (11.401–410).

The formula's other uses in the Iliad come in the report of the embassy, the night raid and the funeral games (9.676, 10.248, 23.729, 23.778). See [[odysseus]] and [[diomedes]].
