summary: Hera is the Achaeans' champion in Book 8. Angered by Hector's boast, she shakes Olympus and asks Poseidon to join her against Zeus, and he refuses; she puts it into Agamemnon's mind to rally the army, sets out with Athena in her chariot, gives up when Iris brings Zeus' threat, and at nightfall answers his mockery and hears his prophecy in silence.

**Poseidon refuses.** At Hector's boast (8.184–197), "queen Hera was indignant; / she shook on her throne, and made high Olympus quake" (8.198–199; see [[nemesis]] and [[high-olympus]]). Zeus' nod shook Olympus in Book 1 (1.528–530); his wife shakes it with her anger. She turns to Poseidon: the Danaans bring him gifts at Helice and Aegae, and if all the gods who help them were willing to drive back the Trojans and hold off Zeus, "he would sit there alone on Ida and fret" (8.201–207; see [[helice]] and [[aegae]]). Poseidon answers "deeply troubled", calls her "reckless in speech", and will not fight Zeus, "since he is far the stronger" (8.208–211; see [[poseidon]] and [[reckless-in-speech]]). In Book 14 she finds another way to keep Zeus on Ida: she seduces him on Gargarus, and Poseidon helps the Achaeans while he sleeps (14.153–360; see [[gargarus]]).

**Agamemnon.** Hector would have burned the ships, "had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind / to bestir himself and quickly urge on the Achaeans" (8.217–219; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[agamemnon]]). So in Book 1 she put it in Achilles' mind to call the assembly (1.55). It is the only help any god but Zeus gives the Achaeans in this book, and it comes as a thought, not in person.

**The chariot.** When the Achaeans have been driven back behind the ditch, she pities them and turns to Athena: "shall we two no longer / care for the Danaans as they perish, even at the last" (8.350–356; see [[pity]] and [[athena]]). The scene is the one of Book 5, with the roles exchanged. There Hera proposed, and "the gray-eyed goddess Athena did not disobey" (5.711–719); here Athena answers with a long complaint against her father and tells Hera to harness the horses, "and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey" (8.357–381; see [[white-armed]]). Hera harnesses "the horses with golden headbands", "Hera, the august goddess, daughter of great Cronus" (8.382–383 = 5.720–721; see [[with-golden-headbands]], [[august-goddess]] and [[cronus]]), and whips them out through the gates of heaven that the Hours keep (8.392–396 = 5.748–752; see [[hours]] and [[chariot]]).

**Turned back.** Zeus' threat is aimed at Athena. Of Hera he says that he is not so indignant or so angry, "for she is always used to thwarting whatever I say" (8.407–408), and Iris repeats it to her face (8.421–422; see [[iris]]). It is Hera who gives up:

Hephaestus too warned his parents not to quarrel "for the sake of mortals" (1.574), and Apollo will refuse to fight Poseidon for wretched mortals, who are like leaves (21.462–467; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). She turns the horses back, and the two goddesses sit down on golden chairs among the other gods, "grieving at heart" (8.432–437).

**Nightfall.** On Olympus the two sit apart from Zeus and say nothing, until he mocks them. Then the scene of Book 4 is repeated: Athena keeps silent, sulking, "but Hera's breast could not contain her anger" (8.457–461 = 4.20–24), and she begins, as there, "Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!" (8.462 = 4.25; see [[most-dread]]). Her speech repeats Athena's of the morning, with one word changed: "We will keep out of the war, if you command" (8.466), where Athena said "as you command" (8.35). Zeus answers "ox-eyed queen Hera" with the prophecy of the battle over Patroclus (8.470–477; see [[ox-eyed]] and [[zeus]]), and with an insult. He will not care if she goes to the lowest limits of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronus sit, "since there is nothing more shameless than you" (8.477–483; see [[tartarus]]). κύντερον, "more shameless", is literally "more doglike" (see [[dogs]]). "So he spoke, and white-armed Hera said nothing to him" (8.484). Their quarrel in Book 1 ended the same way, with Hera sitting in silence (1.568–569; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]).
