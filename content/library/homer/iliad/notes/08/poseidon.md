**Hera's appeal.** Angered by Hector's boast, Hera turns to Poseidon, "the great god", with a reproach: "Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, not even your / heart grieves in your breast for the Danaans as they perish" (8.200–202; see [[hera]], [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[wide-in-strength]]). It is the address with which Zeus answered him in Book 7 (7.455). The Danaans bring him "many and pleasing" gifts at Helice and Aegae, two seats of his worship (8.203–204; see [[helice]] and [[aegae]]). σὺ δέ σφισι βούλεο νίκην is taken as a command, "so you should will them victory"; it can also be read as a reproach, "and you used to wish them victory" (see the translation's note on 8.204). If all the gods who help the Danaans were willing to hold off Zeus, he would sit alone on Ida and fret (8.205–207).

**His refusal.**

{{quote:e30792ba-ffae-5030-a911-8de4e47442d2}}

He answers "deeply troubled", as Zeus answered him in Book 7 (7.454; see [[deeply-troubled]] and [[earth-shaker]]), and calls her ἀπτοεπής, "reckless in speech", one who says what others would not dare to say (see [[reckless-in-speech]]). His reason is the one Nestor gave Diomedes after the thunderbolt, in the same words: "since he is far the stronger" (8.211 = 8.144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The god who will not fight Zeus here goes among the Achaeans against his will in Book 13, while Zeus is looking away (13.10–16, 13.351–360), and leads them openly once Hera has put Zeus to sleep (14.354–401). When Iris brings him Zeus' order to leave the battle, he protests that he is Zeus' equal in honor, since the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot, and then yields (15.174–217; see [[iris]]).

**On Olympus.** When Zeus drives back from Ida, "the famous shaker of the earth unyoked the horses, / and set the chariot on its stand and spread a cloth over it" (8.440–441; see [[famous]] and [[chariot]]). The task is a groom's: Iris unyokes Ares' horses for Aphrodite (5.368–369), and the Hours unyoke Hera's a few lines before (8.433–435; see [[hours]]). Here the god of the sea does it for his brother. βωμοί, altars elsewhere in this book, are here a stand for the chariot (see the translation's note on 8.441).
