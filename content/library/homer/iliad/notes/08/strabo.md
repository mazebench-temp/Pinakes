---
summary: Strabo reads Book 8 as a geographer and a grammarian. The sun that falls into Ocean shows him that Homer had heard of the far west, and he suggests that Tartarus took its name from Tartessus; the “thrice prayed for” night is a hyperbole; and Demetrius of Scepsis made Cebriones, Hector's new charioteer, the eponym of the Cebrenian country.
---

- **Tartarus and Tartessus.** Homer, Strabo argues, was a man of many voices and much learning, and his words, the less accurate as well as the more accurate, show that he had heard of Tartessus and the far west of Iberia. He had heard that it was the farthest land toward the sunset, where, as he says himself, the bright light of the sun falls into Ocean, “drawing black night over the grain-giving earth” (8.485–486). Since night is ill-omened and near to Hades, and Hades to Tartarus, one might guess that he heard of Tartessus and named Tartarus after it, the farthest of the places beneath the earth, adding a myth in keeping with poetry (*Geography* 3.2.12; see [[tartarus]] and [[oceanus]]).
- **Thrice prayed for.** Circe says that Charybdis sucks down the sea three times a day (*Odyssey* 12.105), when, Strabo holds, it is really twice. Strabo explains the “three” as a hyperbole common to all speakers, like “thrice-blessed” and “thrice-wretched”; so the poet says “thrice-blessed Danaans” (*Odyssey* 5.306), “welcome, thrice prayed for” of the night that ends Book 8 (8.488), and “in three and four pieces” (*Odyssey* 9.71; *Geography* 1.2.36; see [[thrice-prayed-for]]).
- **Cebriones.** Demetrius of Scepsis, as Strabo reports, held that the land around Ilios that Hector ruled reached from the naval station as far as Cebrenia, and that Homer's Cebriones, “bastard son of glorious Priam” (16.738), was probably the eponym of the country or, more plausibly, of its city, Cebrene (*Geography* 13.1.33; see [[cebriones]]).
