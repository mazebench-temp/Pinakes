summary: The verb and its noun cover every sense in Book 8: the cries of triumph of the first clash, Hector's boasts that anger Hera, Agamemnon's question “Where have our boasts gone?”, and the prayers of the Achaeans at their ships and of Hector at night. Both sides boast, and both pray.
passages:
  - 8.184-198 | Hector's speech to his horses
  - 8.228-235 | Agamemnon shames the Achaeans
  - 8.345-347 | The Achaeans pray at the ships
---

**Cries of triumph.** The first clash of the day is told in the verses of Book 4: “Then there were cries of pain and cries of triumph together from men killing and being killed” (8.64–65 = 4.450–451). εὐχωλή here is the shout of the man who kills, set against the groan of the man who dies.

**Hector's boasts.** Hector ends his speech to his horses by reminding them that Andromache fed them before him, “who boast that I am her husband in my prime” (8.190). The claim is one of status, like Glaucus' “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (6.211). Then he hopes to take Nestor's shield and Diomedes' corselet and drive the Achaeans to their ships that night, and the narrator calls the whole speech a boast: “So he spoke, boasting, and queen Hera was indignant” (8.198; see [[nemesis]] and [[kleos]]). A boast is a claim on the future, and the gods take note of it.

**The Achaeans' boasts.** Agamemnon throws the army's old boasts back at it: “Where have our boasts gone, when we said that we were the best?” (8.229–234; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). After the omen of the eagle the verb is used of the claim that no one can make: none of the Danaans “could boast that he had held his swift horses ahead of the son of Tydeus”, who is first across the ditch (8.253–255; see [[diomedes]]).

**Prayers.** When the Trojans drive them back across the ditch, the Achaeans halt by the ships, call to one another, and “to all the gods they lifted up their hands, and each man prayed aloud” (8.346–347; see [[prayer]]). At night Hector, encamped on the plain, tells the Trojans: “I hope, as I pray to Zeus and the other gods, to drive away from here these dogs borne along by the fates” (8.526–527; see [[ker-doom]]). The same verb serves both: the men who boasted on Lemnos now pray at their ships, and the man who boasted at noon prays at night. Hera, who heard his boast, has already heard Zeus tell her what the next day will bring (8.470–477).
