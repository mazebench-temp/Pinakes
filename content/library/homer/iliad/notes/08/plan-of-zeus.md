summary: Book 8 is the day on which the plan of Zeus begins to be fulfilled. He forbids the gods to take part in the war, weighs the fates of the two armies, turns the battle with his thunder and gives Hector the glory of the day, and at nightfall he tells Hera for the first time how it will end: Hector will not stop until Achilles is roused beside the ships, over the dead Patroclus (8.470–477).

**The plan resumes.** Since the lying Dream of Book 2 the plan has been out of sight. Through the first day of fighting the Achaeans held their own without Achilles, and gods fought on both sides (see [[the-first-battle]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). At the end of Book 7 “all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil for them”, for both armies, thundering terribly (7.478–479). Book 8 is the day on which the promise to Thetis is kept in battle, and Zeus keeps it in person: he bans the other gods from the war, weighs the fates at noon, puts the Achaeans to flight with his thunder, stops Diomedes with a thunderbolt, and turns back Hera and Athena (see [[zeus]], [[the-scales-of-zeus]], [[thunderbolt]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]).

**To accomplish these things.** The ban states its purpose. No god is to help either side, “so that I may accomplish these things as soon as I can” (8.9). τελευτήσω, “accomplish, bring to an end”, is a verb of fulfillment, of the family of the proem's Διὸς δʼ ἐτελείετο βουλή, “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5), and of the word with which Zeus sealed his nod to Thetis: what he nods to cannot be ἀτελεύτητον, “unfulfilled” (1.527; see [[neuo]]). Athena answers that the gods who favor the Achaeans will keep out of the war but will put βουλή, “counsel”, in the Argives' minds (8.36). Against the plan of Zeus she offers a plan of her own, and Hera offers it again at nightfall in the same words (8.467; see [[boule]]).

**Glory for a day.** The plan has its terms, and the speakers of Book 8 read them. Nestor reads the thunderbolt: “For now Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today; afterward, if he wishes, to us as well he will give it” (8.141–143; see [[kydos]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Hector knows that the son of Cronus “has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory” (8.175–176; see [[hector]]), and the narrator agrees, “now that Zeus had given him glory” (8.216). The glory is for a day, σήμερον, and it has a limit. When Agamemnon prays only that the army may escape, Zeus pities him and nods “that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.245–246; see [[eagle]]). The plan is to honor Achilles by the Achaeans' defeat, not to destroy them, as the narrator says in Book 13: Zeus did not wish the Achaean army to perish utterly before Ilios, but he was giving glory to Thetis and her stout-hearted son (13.347–350).

**Thetis' plans.** Athena names the cause of the day. Her father hates her now, “and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis, who kissed his knees and took his chin in her hand, begging him to honor Achilles, sacker of cities” (8.370–372; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[athena]]). In her mouth the βουλαί are Thetis', and Zeus has only carried them out; ἐξήνυσε, “has fulfilled”, speaks of them as done on the evening of the first day on which they have worked.

This is the first account in the poem of the plan's course, and the first word of Patroclus' death (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[thesphaton]]). It names an end point, Achilles roused beside the ships, and nothing beyond it. Zeus states the plan once more to Hera, in Book 15, and there it goes further: Hector will kill Patroclus, Achilles will kill Hector, and from then on the Achaeans will drive the Trojans back until they take Ilios “through the counsels of Athena”, but Zeus will not cease from his anger until the wish of Achilles is fulfilled, “as I promised and nodded with my head, on the day when the goddess Thetis took my knees” (15.64–77). The plan made for Achilles leads on to the fall of Troy, which lies beyond the poem, and both readings of the proem's phrase, the promise to Thetis and a design for the whole war, find support in it.

**Not for Troy.** Zeus gives the day to the Trojans for the sake of an Achaean. On the night of Hector's victory the winds carry the savor of the Trojans' offerings from the plain up to heaven (8.549). In the verses known only from the Second Alcibiades, which are not in the manuscripts, the offerings are hecatombs, and the gods take no share of them, for Ilios and Priam and his people were hateful to them (8.548, 8.550–552; see [[the-refused-sacrifice]] and the translation's note on 8.548, 8.550–552). The last of those verses is the line in which Zeus himself had named the city he honored most of all cities under the sky (8.552 = 4.47).
