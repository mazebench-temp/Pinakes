summary: Agamemnon, praising Teucer's archery, recalls that Telamon reared him in his own house, “bastard though you were”, and asks him to repay his father with glory. Hector, losing his charioteer to Teucer's arrow, gives the reins to his brother Cebriones, whom a later book names as Priam's bastard son.
passages:
  - 8.278-291 | Agamemnon praises Teucer
  - 8.316-319 | Cebriones takes the reins
---

Agamemnon's praise of Teucer turns on his birth: “shoot on like this, and you may become a light to the Danaans and to your father Telamon, who reared you when you were small and cared for you in his own house, bastard though you were; bring him to glory, far away though he is” (8.282–285; see [[teucer]] and [[telamon]]). The bastard owes his father the rearing that was not his by right, and he can repay it with fame (see [[threptra]] and [[kleos]]). The same verb of care, κομίζω, is in Hector's speech to his horses earlier in the book, which are to “repay me for your keep” (κομιδήν, 8.186): care received is a debt. The verse that names the bastardy, 8.284, is reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.284). As it stands, it makes the father's generosity part of the praise.

The Trojan side has its bastard in the same scene. Teucer's arrows, meant for Hector, kill Gorgythion, a son of Priam by another wife, Castianeira (8.302–305), and then Hector's charioteer Archeptolemus. Hector gives the reins to Cebriones, “his brother, who was close by” (8.318–319). Book 8 says only “brother”; the Iliad names him “the bastard son of glorious Priam” when Patroclus kills him (16.738; see [[cebriones]] and [[gorgythion]]). Telamon's bastard kills the charioteer, and Priam's bastard takes his place. Bastards in Homer often drive for their brothers, as Isus did for his legitimate brother Antiphus (11.101–104; see [[fathers-and-sons]]).
