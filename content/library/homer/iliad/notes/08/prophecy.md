summary: Zeus foretells to Hera the course of the war to come: Hector will not stop fighting until Achilles is roused beside the ships, in the fight over the dead Patroclus, “for so it is decreed”. It is the poem's first word of Patroclus' death. Against it the book sets Hector's own forecasts, each partly fulfilled and partly not.

**Zeus' prophecy.** Hera has repeated Athena's plea of the morning, and Zeus answers with what she will see:

{{quote:49a53fa3-4b0f-5958-b526-0294b982193c}}

The prophecy names the next stage of the plan that Zeus nodded to Thetis in Book 1: the Achaeans will be destroyed until Achilles returns (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). It also names what will bring him back, which Thetis never asked for: Patroclus dead. This is the first time the poem speaks of it. ὣς γὰρ θέσφατόν ἐστι, “for so it is decreed”, makes it a thing already declared, and the word is found only here in the Iliad (see [[thesphaton]]). In Book 15 Zeus tells Hera the whole sequence: Achilles will send Patroclus out, Patroclus will kill Sarpedon, Hector will kill Patroclus, and Achilles will kill Hector; then the Achaeans will take Ilios through Athena's counsels (15.59–71). The event departs from the words of Book 8 in its place: Patroclus is killed on the plain, the fight over his body is fought there, and Achilles shows himself at the ditch, after the Achaeans have fled to their ships (16.786–857, Book 17, 18.148–231; see the translation's note on 8.475–476 and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**Hector's forecasts.** Hector forecasts too, and the book lets the reader measure him. He tells his horses that with Nestor's shield and Diomedes' corselet he could make the Achaeans board their ships “this very night” (8.196–197); night falls first, and he admits that it has saved them (8.500–502; see [[war-and-night]]). He promises the Trojans that when he reaches the ships there will be “some remembrance of consuming fire” (8.180–183); the first fire falls on a ship eight books later, on the ship that brought Protesilaus (15.704–706, 16.112–124). And he says of Diomedes that tomorrow, among the foremost, “he will lie wounded, and many of his companions around him” (8.536–537). Diomedes is wounded the next day among the foremost, but by an arrow of Paris, after his own spear has stunned Hector (11.349–400; see [[diomedes]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**Words that will come to pass.** The book's other forecasts are threats and promises with the formula “I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass”. Agamemnon uses it to promise Teucer a gift of honor if Troy is sacked (8.286–291; see [[time]]); Zeus uses it for his threat to lame the goddesses' horses (8.401), and at night, when they have turned back, he says it in the past: “I will tell you this, and it would have come to pass” (8.454–456; see [[foresight]]).
