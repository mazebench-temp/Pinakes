---
summary: Book 8 is crowded with the pity of the gods. Athena, and later Hera, tell Zeus that they grieve for the Danaan spearmen; Hera reproaches Poseidon whose heart does not grieve; Zeus pities Agamemnon's tears and grants him safety for the day; and Hera, seeing the Achaeans driven to their ships, pities them and sets out to help.
passages:
  - 8.30-40 | Athena's plea
  - 8.236-252 | Agamemnon's prayer
  - 8.350-356 | Hera pities the Achaeans
---

**Grief spoken.** Athena answers Zeus' ban for the gods: “we too know well that your strength is unyielding; but still we grieve for the Danaan spearmen, who will fill up the measure of an evil fate and perish” (8.32–34). ὀλοφύρομαι is to lament for someone, and so to pity him. At night Hera repeats the words to Zeus almost exactly (8.463–465). In between, Hera taunts Poseidon that “not even your heart grieves in your breast for the Danaans as they perish”, though they bring him gifts (8.201–204; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Pity is what the goddesses claim, and what they want from the other gods.

**Zeus pities.** Agamemnon prays in tears that the Achaeans may at least escape, “and the father pitied him as he shed tears, and nodded to him that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.245–246). The same verse tells how Zeus pities Ajax, weeping in the mist over the body of Patroclus, and clears the air so that the Achaeans may at least see to fight (17.645–650). Each time the pity grants a small request and leaves the plan as it was: the eagle comes, the Achaeans rally, and before the day is out Zeus rouses the Trojans again (8.247–252, 8.335; see [[neuo]] and [[rather-the-people-be-safe]]).

**Hera pities.** When Hector drives the Achaeans back to their ships, “Seeing the men, the white-armed goddess Hera pitied them”, and she asks Athena: “shall we two no longer care for the Danaans as they perish, even at the last?” (8.350–353). The formula τοὺς δὲ ἰδοῦσʼ ἐλέησε, “seeing them, she pitied them”, is the one used of Zeus when he pities Hector, Sarpedon and Priam (15.12, 16.431, 24.332; see [[watching-the-war]]). Hera's pity leads to action, and the action is forbidden: Iris turns the two goddesses back at the gates of Olympus (8.397–425). Zeus answers the night's plea with no pity at all, only the prophecy of more Achaean dead (8.470–477; see [[prophecy]]).
