Heracles is not named in Book 8. Athena calls him only "his son", Zeus' son, in her complaint against her father (8.362; see [[athena]] and [[zeus]]). She saved him "very often", "when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him" (8.362–363; see [[eurystheus]]). ἄεθλοι are contests and ordeals, the word for athletes' contests and for Heracles' labors (see [[contests]]). "He would weep to the sky, and Zeus / would send me down from the sky to help him" (8.364–365). Above all she remembers the descent to the house of Hades to bring back its hound from Erebus, from which he would not have escaped the streams of Styx without her (8.366–369; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[hades]], [[erebus]] and [[styx]]).

The story is told against Zeus. Zeus once sent Athena to help his own son; now he hates her and helps Thetis' son by sending the Achaeans to defeat (8.370–372; see [[thetis]]). The weeping hero is unlike the Heracles of the other allusions in the Iliad, who sacks Troy with six ships, shoots Hera and Hades, and cares nothing for the harm he does (5.392–404, 5.638–642; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). Zeus also grieves for him in Agamemnon's story, whenever he sees his son "doing shameful labor under Eurystheus" (19.132–133). The ghost of Heracles confirms Athena's part in the Odyssey: Eurystheus sent him down for the hound, "and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena" (*Odyssey* 11.620–626). Hesiod too gives her a share in the labors: Heracles killed the Hydra "by the plans of Athena, driver of spoil" (*Theogony* 316–318; see [[driver-of-spoil]]).
