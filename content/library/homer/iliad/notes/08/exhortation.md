summary: Hector rallies the Trojans with Zeus' promise and the feebleness of the Achaean wall; Agamemnon shames the Achaeans with their boasts on Lemnos and then turns his speech into a prayer; and Hector's night speech ends with orders for the morning and a boast.

**Hector.** “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand, be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (8.173–174). The order is the verse of his rally in Book 6 (6.112), and the address, heard here for the first time, becomes his own: the two verses return together in his rallies of Books 11, 15 and 17 (11.286–287, 15.486–487, 17.184–185). His reasons are the will of Zeus, “I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory” (8.175–176; see [[nike]] and [[kydos]]), and the weakness of the enemy's defenses:

{{quote:4e53e53b-2f75-52f3-b3f6-52647836fda1}}

The goal is fire: “let there be some remembrance of consuming fire, so that I may set fire to the ships” (8.181–182; see [[ships]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Agamemnon.** Agamemnon shouts from Odysseus' ship, in the middle of the line (8.222–227; see [[cloak]]): “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (8.228). The verse is the one Hera shouted in Book 5 in the likeness of Stentor (5.787; see [[aidos]]). His reasons are the army's own boasts on Lemnos (8.229–234; see [[feast]]) and its shame now, not worth even one man, Hector (8.234–235). Then he turns from the army to Zeus, and the exhortation becomes a prayer (8.236–244; see [[prayer]]). The army is roused not by the speech but by the eagle Zeus sends in answer: “they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (8.252; see [[charme]] and [[teras]]).

**Hector at night.** Hector's speech at nightfall is an order of the camp (8.497–522; see [[night-watch]]) that ends as an exhortation for the morning, “early, at daybreak, armed in our armor, we will rouse sharp Ares beside the hollow ships” (8.530–531), and a boast: “If only I were immortal and ageless all my days, and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored, as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (8.538–541; see [[hector]]).
