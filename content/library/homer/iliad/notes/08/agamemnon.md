summary: Agamemnon runs with the other leaders when Zeus thunders, but when the Achaeans are penned inside the ditch, Hera rouses him to shame the army from Odysseus' ship, a purple cloak in his hand, and to pray to Zeus in tears, and Zeus answers with an eagle. He crosses the ditch behind Diomedes and promises Teucer a gift of honor from the spoils of Troy (8.78, 8.217–252, 8.261, 8.278–291).

**Flight.** When Zeus thunders from Ida and his flash falls among the Achaeans, “neither Idomeneus dared to stay, nor Agamemnon” (8.78). The commander is named among the leaders who run, and only Nestor, whose horse has been shot, is left (8.80–86; see [[nestor]]).

**Roused by Hera.** The Achaeans are hemmed in behind their ditch, and Hector “would have burned the balanced ships with blazing fire, had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind to bestir himself and quickly urge on the Achaeans” (8.217–219; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[hera]]). ἐπὶ φρεσὶ θῆκε … Ἥρη is how she put it into Achilles' mind to call the assembly in Book 1 (1.55). He goes along the huts and the ships “holding a great purple cloak in his strong hand” (8.221; see [[cloak]] and [[purple]]). The poem does not say why he carries the cloak instead of wearing it; it may be meant to make him seen. He stands by the ship of Odysseus, in the middle of the line, so that his shout can carry to both ends (8.222–227; see [[ajax]] and [[achilles]]).

**The rebuke.** His first words are Hera's when she took the likeness of Stentor: “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (8.228 = 5.787; see [[aidos]] and [[argives]]). Then he throws their boasts back at them, the boasts they made on Lemnos over meat and wine, “that each of you would stand against a hundred, two hundred Trojans in battle? But now we are not worth even one man, Hector” (8.229–235; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]] and [[lemnos]]).

**The prayer.**

{{quote:d6ce38c6-b705-5bf0-beed-3d0e53cec7a4}}

He lays his blind folly on Zeus, as he did in Book 2, “Zeus, son of Cronus, has bound me fast in heavy blind folly” (2.111), and as he will again in the assembly that night and in his apology to Achilles (9.18, 19.86–138; see [[ate]]). He reminds the god of the oxen he burned at every altar on the voyage (8.238–241; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[altar]]), and he asks for little: “let us at least escape and get away” (8.243). That night he will propose that the army sail home (9.26–28). “So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears, and nodded to him that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.245–246). The verse of Zeus' pity returns when Ajax prays to him in the mist over Patroclus' body (17.648). The answer is an eagle with a fawn in its talons, dropped beside the altar of Zeus, and the Achaeans spring on the Trojans (8.247–252; see [[eagle]] and [[teras]]).

**Behind Diomedes.** In the charge that follows, “after him came the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus” (8.261; see [[atreidae]] and [[menelaus]]). In Book 7 he rose first of the nine who volunteered to fight Hector (7.162); now he comes second, after Diomedes (see [[diomedes]]).

**The promise to Teucer.** Watching Teucer shoot from under Ajax's shield, he comes to praise him and promises him, if Zeus and Athena grant the sack of Ilios, a gift of honor “first after me”: “a tripod, or two horses with their chariot, or a woman, who would go up into your bed with you” (8.278–291; see [[teucer]], [[dear-heart]] and [[geras]]). The commander who took Achilles' prize in Book 1 promises prizes from a city not yet taken, and that night he will offer Achilles tripods, horses and women (9.122–134). Teucer answers that he needs no urging (8.293–294).
