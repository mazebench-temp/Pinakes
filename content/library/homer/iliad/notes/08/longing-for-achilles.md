---
summary: Book 8 is the first day on which Achilles' oath begins to come true. Many Achaeans fall at the hands of man-slaying Hector, Agamemnon prays in tears that the army may at least escape, and Zeus tells Hera that Hector will not stop until Achilles is roused beside the ships (8.335–347, 8.228–244, 8.473–476).
passages:
  - 8.228-244 | Agamemnon's despair
  - 8.473-476 | Until Achilles is roused
---

Achilles swore on the scepter that a longing for him would come upon all the sons of the Achaeans, “when many at the hands of man-slaying Hector fall dying”, and that Agamemnon would tear his heart in anger that he had paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans (1.240–244). Through the long first day of fighting the oath was not fulfilled: the Achaeans held their own without him (see [[the-first-battle]]). In Book 8 Zeus gives the battle to the Trojans, and the words of the oath begin to describe what happens. Hector drives the Achaeans back across the ditch, “always killing the hindmost”, and many are “brought down at the hands of the Trojans” (8.342–344; see [[hector]] and [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]).

Achilles himself is named only in passing until the end of the day. When Agamemnon goes to the ship of Odysseus to rally the army, the narrator says that the huts of Ajax and of Achilles lay at the two ends of the line, where they had hauled up their ships, “trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands” (8.222–226); the verses 8.224–226, which return whole in Book 11 (11.7–9), are reported to have been rejected here in antiquity: Agamemnon is shouting to the Danaans, and Achilles is out of the fighting (see the translation's note on 8.222–226). Agamemnon does not name him. He blames Zeus for striking him with blind folly and prays only that the Achaeans may escape (8.236–244; see [[agamemnon]] and [[ate]]). The longing is not yet spoken. That night Agamemnon admits his folly and sends the embassy to Achilles (Book 9).

Zeus names him at nightfall. Hector will not cease from war “until the swift-footed son of Peleus is roused beside the ships”, over Patroclus dead (8.473–476; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The oath said that the Achaeans would long for Achilles; the prophecy says what it will cost him to come back.
