Below, a mortal wishes he were immortal and ageless, and at night the gods turn away from the Trojans' sacrifice.

**Above gods and men.** Zeus ends his threat to the gods with a contest he cannot lose. Let them hang a golden rope from the sky and all pull on it together: they could not drag him down, but he could pull them up, earth and sea and all, and leave everything hanging from a peak of Olympus. “So far am I above the gods, and above men” (8.18–27; see [[the-golden-rope]]). The distance between gods and men, the usual measure of the Iliad, is here set beside a second one, between Zeus and the other gods. Nestor and Poseidon draw the same conclusion in the same words, the man and the god yielding to Zeus “since he is far the stronger” (8.144, 8.211; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Zeus weighs the mortals' lives in his scales (8.69–74; see [[ker-doom]]).

**For the sake of mortals.** When Iris has turned them back, Hera tells Athena: “I will no longer let the two of us fight against Zeus for the sake of mortals. Let one of them perish and another live, as chance may fall” (8.427–430). βροτῶν ἕνεκα, “for the sake of mortals”, is the gods' phrase for quarrels not worth having. Hephaestus used it to stop the quarrel of Zeus and Hera in Book 1 (ἕνεκα θνητῶν, 1.573–576; see [[worse-things-prevail]]), and in the battle of the gods Apollo refuses to fight Poseidon for the sake of mortals, who are like leaves (21.461–467). Hera's indifference lasts one sentence. She and Athena sit apart, grieving, and are “devising evils for the Trojans” (8.437, 8.458).

**What divides them.** The book draws the difference in small things. The goddesses' horses are unyoked by the Hours and tied at ambrosial mangers (8.433–435), as the gods' horses are fed ambrosia elsewhere (5.369, 5.777, 13.35); the Trojans' horses stand by their chariots eating white barley and spelt (8.564). Hector ends his night assembly with the wish to be immortal and ageless all his days and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). And in verses known only from the Second Alcibiades, the gods take no share of the savor of the Trojans' hecatombs, because Ilios, Priam and his people are hateful to them (8.548–552; see [[the-refused-sacrifice]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Zeus pities the weeping Agamemnon and Hera pities the Achaeans (8.245, 8.350; see [[pity]]), but the decisions of the day are made on Ida and Olympus, and the men below know only their signs (see [[divine-intervention]] and [[watching-the-war]]).
