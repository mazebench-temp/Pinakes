The sun “fell into Oceanus”, the river that runs round the earth (see [[oceanus]]); ἐν δʼ ἔπεσʼ Ὠκεανῷ occurs only here. The other two verses see the sunset from both sides of the plain. For the Trojans the light went down ἀέκουσιν, “against their will”; for the Achaeans the night came ἀσπασίη, “welcome”, and τρίλλιστος, “thrice prayed for”, a word found only here (see [[thrice-prayed-for]] and [[prayer]]). The night itself is ἐρεβεννή, “murky”, dark as Erebus (see [[murky]]). In the Odyssey the sunset is welcome in the same way, to the plowman who longs for his supper and to Odysseus, longing for home (Od. 13.31–35). In Book 18 it is the sun itself that goes down unwilling, when Hera sends it into Oceanus to end the fight over Patroclus' body (18.239–242).

The night saves the Achaeans, as Hector says: “darkness came first, and that, above all, has now saved the Argives and their ships on the shore of the sea” (8.500–501). He yields to it, “But now let us yield to black night” (8.502; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[war-and-night]]). In Book 2 Agamemnon prayed that the sun might not set before he had burned Priam's palace (2.412–415); now the sunset is his army's rescue.
