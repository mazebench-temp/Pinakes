---
summary: Agamemnon backs his promise to Teucer with the formula of a solemn assurance; Zeus uses it for his threat to Hera and Athena, and turns it into a counterfactual when they have obeyed: “and it would have come to pass”.
grc: ["ὡς καὶ τετελεσμένον ἔσται", "τὸ δέ κεν τετελεσμένον ἦεν"]
en: [it would have come to pass]
---

The formula is a speaker's guarantee: “I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass” (see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). Book 8 uses it three ways.

**A promise.** Agamemnon gives it to his promise of a gift of honor for Teucer: “And I will tell you this, just as it will surely come to pass” (8.286; see [[geras]]). The gift depends on the sack of Troy, which the poem will not tell.

**A threat.** Zeus gives it to his threat against the goddesses, “For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass” (8.401), and Iris passes the threat on with an assurance of her own, “and he will make it good” (ᾗ τελέει περ, 8.415; see [[message-repeated]]).

**A counterfactual.** When the goddesses have turned back, Zeus tells them what would have happened, and changes the tense of the formula: “For I will tell you this, and it would have come to pass” (ὧδε γὰρ ἐξερέω, τὸ δέ κεν τετελεσμένον ἦεν, 8.454). The past form is found only here in Homer (see [[would-have-had-not]]).
