**Agamemnon's claim.** In his prayer Agamemnon reminds Zeus of what he is owed: “Yet I say that I never passed by your beautiful altar in my many-benched ship on my ill-starred way here, but on every one I burned the fat and the thighs of oxen, eager to sack well-walled Troy” (8.238–241). It is the claim of a prayer, the service that gives the one who prays a right to be heard, as Chryses claimed the thigh-pieces he had burned for Apollo (μηρίʼ ἔκηα, 1.40; see [[prayer]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). The offerings were made for a purpose, the sack of Troy, and the purpose has not been served. Nestor will make the same claim at the ships in Book 15, for all who ever burned fat thighs to Zeus in Argos and prayed to come home (15.372–376).

**At the altar of the camp.** The eagle drops the fawn “beside the beautiful altar of Zeus … where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus, lord of all omens” (8.249–250). ῥέζεσκον is iterative: this is the altar of the army's regular sacrifices (see [[altar]] and [[teras]]).

**Gifts for Poseidon.** Hera reproaches Poseidon: the Danaans “bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae, many and pleasing; so you should will them victory” (8.203–204; see [[helice]] and [[aegae]]). The gifts are offerings at the god's own cult places, and the argument is the one of every prayer: the god is bound to those who give to him (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Poseidon will not fight Zeus for them (8.209–211).

**The Trojans' sacrifice.** At night on the plain the Trojans bring cattle and fat sheep from the city (8.505, 8.545), “and the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven” (8.549). The manuscripts have only that verse. Four more, printed in brackets, are known from the Second Alcibiades ascribed to Plato (149d–e), which reports in indirect speech that the Trojans offered hecatombs and that the gods refused them (see [[hecatomb]]):

The dialogue cites the verses for the point that the gods are not won over by sacrifices (see the translation's note on 8.548, 8.550–552, and [[the-refused-sacrifice]]). They turn the poem's formula of an accepted sacrifice inside out. After the plague the Achaeans offered Apollo perfect hecatombs, and the savor reached the sky (1.315–317); here the savor reaches heaven, and the gods take no share of it. In Book 4 Zeus said that of all cities he honored Ilios most, “and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear”, because his altar never lacked an equal share of the feast, the libation and the savor, “for that is the prize allotted to us” (4.44–49; see [[geras]]). 8.552 is that verse, now said of a city the gods hate; Agamemnon and Hector use it in their certainty that the day will come when sacred Ilios will perish (4.164–165, 6.448–449). The hatred is the one the last book gives to Hera, Poseidon and Athena, who kept their enmity to “sacred Ilios, and Priam and his people” (24.25–28).
