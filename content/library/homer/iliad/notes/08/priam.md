---
summary: Priam is not seen in Book 8, but his sons are: Hector drives the Achaeans to their ships, Teucer's arrow kills Gorgythion, Priam's son by Castianeira of Aesyme, and the bastard Cebriones becomes Hector's charioteer. In verses known only from the Second Alcibiades, the gods refuse the Trojans' sacrifice, for Ilios and Priam and his people are hateful to them (8.550–552).
---

**His sons.** Hector is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam”, when he hems the Achaeans in behind their ditch and when Hera complains of the harm he has done (8.216, 8.356), and Athena calls him “the son of Priam, Hector of the gleaming helmet” (8.377; see [[hector]] and [[son-of-priam]]). Teucer's arrow, meant for Hector, strikes “noble Gorgythion, the brave son of Priam”, whose mother was “lovely Castianeira”, a bride from Aesyme (8.302–305; see [[gorgythion]], [[castianeira]] and [[aesyme]]). Hector's new charioteer is his brother Cebriones (8.318), whom Book 16 calls a bastard son of Priam (16.738; see [[cebriones]], [[nothos]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). Gorgythion's mother is not Hecuba, and Cebriones' is not named: they are among the sons whom, as Priam says, other “women in my halls” bore him (24.495–497; see [[hecuba]]).

**The verses of the Second Alcibiades.** When Hector's assembly is over, the Trojans feast on the plain, “and the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven” (8.549). Four verses that are not in the manuscripts complete the scene, and the translation prints them in brackets: the Trojans offer hecatombs, “but the blessed gods took no share of it, nor did they wish to; for sacred Ilios was hateful to them, and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (8.548, 8.550–552). They are known from the Second Alcibiades ascribed to Plato (149d–e; see the translation's note on 8.548, 8.550–552). The last verse is Zeus' own from Book 4, where he tells Hera that sacred Ilios was the most honored city in his heart, “and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear”, because his altar never lacked the savor of the burnt fat (4.46–49). The added verses turn that love into hatred, and the savor rises in vain. The same verse ends the forecast, made by Agamemnon and by Hector, that “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.164–165, 6.448–449; see [[a-day-will-come]] and [[sacrifice]]).
