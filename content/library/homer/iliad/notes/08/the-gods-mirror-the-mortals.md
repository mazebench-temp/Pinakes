summary: Book 8 is framed by two assemblies, the gods' on Olympus at dawn and the Trojans' on the plain at night, the only two in the poem introduced with the words ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, and both opened with “Hear me”. Between them gods and men drive chariots, are “quick to see”, yield to Zeus in the same half-line, and insult each other as dogs.

**Two assemblies.** At dawn “Zeus who delights in thunder held an assembly of the gods” on the topmost peak of Olympus (Ζεὺς δὲ θεῶν ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, 8.2); at nightfall “shining Hector held an assembly of the Trojans” on a clear space of the plain (Τρώων αὖτʼ ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, 8.489). The phrase occurs nowhere else in the Iliad. Both speakers begin “Hear me” (κέκλυτέ μευ, 8.5, 8.497; see [[hear-me]] and [[assembly]]), and both give orders: Zeus forbids the gods to help either side, Hector tells the Trojans to camp on the plain and keep watch. Zeus' speech ends with his strength: “So far am I above the gods, and above men” (8.27). Hector's ends with the wish to be immortal and ageless and honored as Athena and Apollo are (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The gods answer in silence, “amazed at his word” (8.28–29); the Trojans shout in applause (8.542).

**Two kinds of chariot.** Zeus yokes his bronze-footed horses, puts on gold, and drives to Ida (8.41–50); Hera and Athena harness their horses and drive out through the gates of heaven, and are turned back (8.374–437); Zeus drives home, and Poseidon unyokes his horses for him and sets the chariot on its stand (8.438–443). Below them the battle is fought from chariots: Nestor's trace horse is shot, Diomedes takes him into his own chariot, and Hector loses two charioteers (8.80–129, 8.312–319; see [[chariot]] and [[horses]]). Both days of driving end the same way. The Hours unyoke the goddesses' horses and tie them at their ambrosial mangers (8.433–435); the Trojans unyoke theirs and tie them beside their chariots, where they eat white barley and spelt and wait for Dawn (8.543–544, 8.564–565).

**The same verses.** The formula of a sudden rescue is used of a man and then of a god forty lines apart: “had not Diomedes … been quick to see it” and “had not the father of men and gods been quick to see it” (8.91, 8.132; see [[noos]]). Nestor tells Diomedes to yield to Zeus, “since he is far the stronger”, and Poseidon tells Hera the same, in the same half-line (8.144, 8.211; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). When the goddesses come back and sit apart, “and said nothing to him, and asked him nothing; but he understood in his heart, and spoke” (8.445–446), the verses are, but for one word, those of the heralds who came to Achilles' hut for Briseis and stood before him in fear (1.332–333): the two goddesses face their king as the heralds faced theirs. And the insults of men and gods are the same: Teucer calls Hector a mad dog (8.299), Iris calls Athena a shameless dog (8.423), and Zeus tells Hera that nothing is more shameless than she (κύντερον, “more doglike”, 8.483), on the day Hector calls the Achaeans dogs borne by the fates (8.527; see [[dogs]] and [[words-and-violence]]).
