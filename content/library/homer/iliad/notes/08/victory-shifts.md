summary: Nestor gives Paris' maxim its religious form when the thunderbolt falls before Diomedes' horses: Zeus grants glory to Hector “today”, and “afterward, if he wishes”, to the Achaeans. The defeated man's comfort becomes an old man's reason to retreat.
refs: [8.141-143]
passages:
  - 8.139-144 | Nestor's counsel to Diomedes
---

Paris said it of himself after his defeat and again before his return to battle: another time he will win, for the gods are on his side too, and victory shifts from man to man (3.439–440, 6.339). Nestor says it to Diomedes under Zeus' thunderbolt: “For now Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today; afterward, if he wishes, to us as well he will give it” (8.141–143). The thought is the same, that success passes from one side to the other, but the giver is named and his will is the condition: αἴ κʼ ἐθέλῃσι, “if he wishes”. The words σήμερον· ὕστερον αὖτε, “today; afterward”, are those in which Apollo and Hector each proposed in Book 7 to stop the fighting for a day (7.30, 7.291). Paris used the maxim to excuse a defeat; Nestor uses it to justify a retreat, and he adds the reason that Paris did not need, that no man can hold back the mind of Zeus (8.143–144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[kydos]]). The day bears him out: Zeus gives Hector the battle, and at night he tells Hera that Hector will not stop until Achilles is roused (8.470–477).
