# ἠώς at 8.66 (= 11.84) is "morning", the part of the day before noon, not the goddess.
grc: ["^Ἠῶ", "ἠοῦς ἠριγενείης"]
except: [8.66]
passages:

The book's first verse and its last are Dawn's. It opens: "Now saffron-robed Dawn was spreading over all the earth" (8.1). κροκόπεπλος, "with a robe of saffron", is her epithet at three other dawns of the Iliad (19.1, 23.227, 24.695), and the verse returns, with δέ for μέν, at 24.695 (see [[saffron-robed]] and [[dawn-line]]). The day that begins is the second day of battle, which Zeus gives to the Trojans (see [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). It ends with the Trojans camped on the plain around their fires, and the last verse leaves their horses "munching white barley and spelt", standing beside the chariots, waiting "for Dawn of the fair throne" (ἐΰθρονον Ἠῶ, 8.564–565; see [[of-the-fair-throne]]). ἐΰθρονος is used of Dawn only here in the Iliad, and five times in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 6.48, 15.495, 17.497, 18.318, 19.342).

Between the two, the dawn to come is what Zeus and Hector speak of. Zeus tells Hera that "at dawn" she will see him destroying the Argives (ἠοῦς, 8.470), and Hector orders fires to burn all night "until early-born Dawn" (8.508; see [[early-born]]) and promises to speak again "at dawn" (8.525). The morning they wait for does not come until Book 11, when Dawn rises from the bed of Tithonus (11.1–2). The night between holds Books 9 and 10.

In the battle, ἠώς is also the time of day: "As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing", the missiles of both sides struck home (8.66–67 = 11.84–85). Here Dawn's word means the morning, and the index does not count the line as hers (see the translation's note on 8.66).
