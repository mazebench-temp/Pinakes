**Alone.** When the Achaean leaders flee from Zeus' lightning, “only Nestor stayed, the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans, not of his own will” (8.80–81; see [[gerenian-horseman]] and [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]]). Paris has shot one of his horses on the crown of the head, “where the foremost hairs of horses grow on the skull, and a blow is deadliest” (8.83–84; see [[paris]]). It rears, the arrow sinks into its brain, and its writhing throws the team into confusion. It is the trace horse, and the old man is cutting its harness away with his sword when Hector's chariot comes on (8.85–91; see [[chariot]] and [[horses]]). Automedon does the same for Achilles' team when Sarpedon kills the trace horse Pedasus (16.467–475). In Book 4 Nestor told his Pylians that no one should fight “alone, ahead of the others” (4.304); now he is left alone (οἶος, 8.80) against his will.

**The old man.** Diomedes, “alone as he was” (8.99), comes to him and names his plight: “Old man, the young fighters are truly pressing you hard; your strength is loosed, and grievous old age is upon you; your attendant is a weakling, and your horses are slow” (8.102–104; see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[geron]]). Nestor said the same of himself in Book 4, “now old age is upon me” (4.321), and in the chariot race he warns Antilochus that their Pylian horses are the slowest in the field (23.309–310; see [[antilochus]]). He mounts Diomedes' chariot and takes the reins (8.112–117; see [[eurymedon-attendant-of-nestor]]). The horseman who taught the Pylians the chariot tactics of the men of old (4.297–309) drives for the youngest of the kings (see [[diomedes]]).

**The sign.** When Zeus' thunderbolt falls before the horses, the reins slip from Nestor's hands, and he is the first to read it:

{{quote:d7508f7b-7fb0-5601-aac8-3ae84ebb84ef}}

His last half-line, “since he is far the stronger” (ἐπεὶ ἦ πολὺ φέρτερός ἐστι, 8.144), is the one with which Poseidon refuses Hera later in the book, when she asks him to help the Achaeans against Zeus (8.211; see [[poseidon]]). The old man and the god give the same reason for not fighting Zeus (see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Nestor's words also give the plan of the book: the glory is Hector's “today”, and Zeus will give it to the Achaeans afterward, “if he wishes” (8.141–142; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[kydos]]). When Diomedes fears that Hector will call him a coward, Nestor answers that neither the Trojans nor the wives of the men he has killed will believe it, and turns the horses back through the onslaught (8.151–158).

**The golden shield.** Hector wants Nestor's shield above all other spoils, the shield “whose fame now reaches the sky, that it is all of gold, its rods and the shield itself” (8.192–193; see [[shield]] and [[kleos]]). It is mentioned nowhere else.

**Outside Homer.** The rescue has a famous double. In Pindar, Paris' arrows wound a horse of Nestor's chariot, Memnon comes on, and Antilochus, called by his father, stands his ground and buys his father's life with his own (*Pythian* 6.28–42; see [[pindar]]). In the *Aethiopis*, as Proclus summarizes it, Memnon kills Antilochus, and the Odyssey knows that “the shining son of bright Dawn” killed him (Od. 4.187–188; see [[proclus]]). Whether the Iliad's scene was shaped by the story of Memnon, or the later poem by the Iliad, is disputed (see [[antilochus]]).
