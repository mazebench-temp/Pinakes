summary: The deer of Book 8 is the eagle's prey. Zeus answers Agamemnon's prayer with an eagle holding a fawn, “the young of a swift deer”, which it drops beside the altar of Zeus (8.247–250).
grc: [νεβρὸν]
en: [fawn]
---

νεβρὸν ἔχοντʼ ὀνύχεσσι τέκος ἐλάφοιο ταχείης, “holding in its talons a fawn, the young of a swift deer” (8.248). ταχείης is feminine, so the mother is a hind. The same words name the hind of a simile in Book 11, whose young a lion crushes in their lair while she, trembling, cannot help them (ἐλάφοιο ταχείης νήπια τέκνα, 11.113–119). The fawn is the weakest and most timid of creatures in the similes. Agamemnon rebuked the Argives who hung back as fawns that stand dazed when they have run themselves out (4.242–246; see [[like-fawns]]); in later books a dog springs on a wounded fawn (15.579–581), and Achilles hunts Hector as a dog hunts a fawn through the glens (22.188–193).

The eagle drops its prey beside the altar where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus, “lord of all omens” (8.249–250), and the Achaeans, seeing that the bird has come from Zeus, take heart and attack (see [[eagle]] and [[teras]]). The poem does not say what the fawn stands for. The sign answers a prayer for the army's escape (8.242–246), and it is read by its source and its place, not by its prey.
