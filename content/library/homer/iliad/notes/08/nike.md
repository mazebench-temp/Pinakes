---
summary: In Book 8 victory belongs to Zeus to give, and he gives it to the Trojans. He thunders three times from Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle”; Hector claims the promise aloud; and Hera, who cannot fight, urges Poseidon to will victory for the Danaans, and he will not.
passages:
  - 8.167-176 | The sign of victory
  - 8.198-211 | Hera's appeal to Poseidon
---

**The sign.** Hector shouts insults after Diomedes as Nestor turns his horses, and Diomedes is torn whether to turn back and fight: “Three times he pondered in his mind and in his heart, and three times Zeus the counselor thundered from the mountains of Ida, giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.169–171). The phrase ἑτεραλκέα νίκην is the one Apollo used when he asked Athena whether she had come to give the Danaans such a victory (7.26; see [[that-turns-the-battle]] and the note on Book 7). It is taken either as a decisive victory or as one that swings the fight to the side that was losing (see the translation's note on 7.26). Here the swing is plain: a moment before, the Trojans would have been penned in Ilios like lambs (8.130–132), and now they will drive the Achaeans to their ships. Hector hears in the thunder Zeus' promise “of victory and great glory, and of a bane to the Danaans” (8.175–176; see [[kydos]] and [[neuo]]).

**Willing victory.** Hector's boast over the shield of Nestor and the corselet of Diomedes angers Hera, who shakes Olympus on her throne and turns to Poseidon: the Danaans bring him many pleasing gifts at Helice and Aegae, “so you should will them victory” (σὺ δέ σφισι βούλεο νίκην, 8.203–204). βούλεο occurs only here. The translation takes it as an imperative; it can also be an unaugmented imperfect, “and you used to wish them victory”, a reproach for Poseidon's present indifference (see the translation's note on 8.204 and [[boule]]). Either way Hera speaks in the formula of divine partisanship: Apollo “wanted victory for the Trojans” (7.21), and Zeus wants it for the Trojans and Hector when he gives them the battle at the ships (13.347, 16.121). Poseidon refuses. He will not fight against Zeus, “since he is far the stronger” (8.210–211; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[poseidon]]).

**No prayer for victory.** In Book 7 the Achaeans prayed to Zeus to grant victory to Ajax (7.203). In Book 8 no Achaean asks for it. Agamemnon, in tears, asks only that they may escape and not be beaten down by the Trojans, and Zeus grants that much (8.242–246; see [[rather-the-people-be-safe]]). At nightfall Hector, who had hoped to burn the ships before dark, admits that the darkness has saved the Argives, and puts his victory off until dawn (8.497–541; see [[war-and-night]]).
