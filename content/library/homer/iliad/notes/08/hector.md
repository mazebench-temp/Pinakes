**Zeus' day.** Everyone knows whose gift Hector's day is. “Now Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today”, Nestor tells Diomedes as he turns their chariot (8.141; see [[kydos]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Hector reads the same sign in Zeus' thunder: “I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory” (8.175–176; see [[nike]]). Nestor's “Do you not see” and Hector's “I know” are one verb, γιγνώσκω (8.140, 8.175). The narrator agrees (8.216), and Zeus names the day's end: “mighty Hector will not cease from war until the swift-footed son of Peleus is roused beside the ships” (8.473–474; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Yet Hector kills no named man in the book, and he loses two charioteers.

**His charioteers.** Three times a weapon thrown at him kills another man. Diomedes' spear kills his driver Eniopeus, and Teucer's arrows kill Gorgythion, a son of Priam, and the next driver, Archeptolemus (8.118–123, 8.300–315; see [[eniopeus]], [[gorgythion]] and [[archeptolemus]]). Both drivers die in the same verses, and Hector grieves in the same words: “Dread grief for his charioteer clouded Hector's mind; yet he left him, grieving though he was for his comrade” (8.124–125 = 8.316–317). The second time he gives the reins to his brother Cebriones, leaps down, and breaks Teucer's bowstring with a jagged stone (8.318–329; see [[cebriones]] and [[teucer]]). Cebriones drives for him until Patroclus kills him, and then Hector does not leave the body (16.737–782; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

**The taunt.** As Nestor turns Diomedes' horses, Hector shouts after him:

{{quote:b237aad7-3e41-5baa-b04e-3451e50d490c}}

His first words turn Agamemnon's praise of Idomeneus, “I honor you above the Danaans of the swift horses” (4.257), into scorn, and the seat, the meat and the full cups are the honors Sarpedon says the Lycians pay their kings (12.310–311). Answering Ajax in Book 7, Hector would not be tested like a boy or a woman (7.235–236; see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]]); now he calls Diomedes “no better than a woman” and a “wretched doll” (8.163–164; see [[wretched-doll]]). Diomedes had foreseen the boast (8.148–150; see [[diomedes]]).

**The wall and the ships.** He rouses “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians” (8.173) with contempt for the Achaeans' new wall: “Fools, who contrived these walls, feeble things, worth nothing!” (8.177–178; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[nepios]]). His horses will easily leap the ditch, he says; in Book 12 they balk at its edge (12.50–59). He promises fire for the ships (8.180–183), Agamemnon fears it (8.235), and it comes in Book 16 (16.112–124). He calls his four horses by name (8.184–197; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]), and his boast makes Hera shake Olympus (8.198–199; see [[hera]]). When Zeus rouses the Trojans again, Hector drives the Achaeans across the ditch as a hound runs at the haunches of a boar or a lion, “always killing the hindmost” (8.338–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]), and wheels his horses before the ships “with the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.348–349; see [[gorgon]]). Hera says he “rages past all bearing”, and Athena wishes him dead in his own land (8.355–359; see [[athena]]).

**The night assembly.** The book began with Zeus' assembly of the gods (θεῶν ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, 8.2) and ends with Hector's assembly of the Trojans (Τρώων αὖτʼ ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, 8.489), the only two uses of the phrase. He speaks on the plain, in a space clear of corpses, leaning on the spear of eleven cubits he carried into Paris' house (8.491–496; 6.318–320). Night has saved the Achaeans, and he bids the Trojans “yield to black night” (8.500–502), as he told Ajax after their duel (7.293; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[war-and-night]]). His orders are a general's: fodder for the horses, food and wine from the city, fires all night so that the Achaeans cannot slip away unhurt, the boys and the old men on the towers, and a watch against ambush (8.503–522; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The army does it in his words (8.543–547 ≈ 8.503–507). Then he answers Diomedes, who had wanted Hector to “know” what his spear could do (εἴσεται, 8.111). “I will know” (εἴσομαι), Hector says, whether Diomedes will push him back or die on his spear (8.532–538), and he ends with a wish:

{{quote:55efc135-2a0d-5109-a2f8-0388855b9dea}}

The impossible wish is the measure of his certainty (see [[immortal-and-ageless]]), and he makes it again to Ajax, wishing to be the son of Zeus and Hera (13.825–828). The prediction comes true only in part: the next day Diomedes' spear stuns Hector, and Paris' arrow, not Hector's spear, drives Diomedes from the field (11.349–400). In Book 18 Hector holds a second assembly on the plain, gives the same order for the dawn (18.303–304 = 8.530–531), and the Trojans shout their applause in this book's verse (18.310 = 8.542), but there the narrator calls them “fools” (νήπιοι, 18.311; see [[assembly]]).
