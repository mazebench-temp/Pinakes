summary: Night ends the day that Zeus gave Hector, unwelcome to the Trojans and “thrice prayed for” by the Achaeans. Hector admits that the darkness has saved the Argives, yields to it, and turns it to his own use: fires all night so that the Achaeans cannot slip away, a watch on the city, and a camp on the plain whose thousand fires shine like the stars.

**The night that saves.** Hector had hoped to finish the war before dark. He told his horses that with Nestor's shield and Diomedes' corselet he would make the Achaeans board their ships “this very night” (αὐτονυχί, 8.197), and he would have burned the ships had not Hera roused Agamemnon (8.217–219). Then the sun sets:

{{quote:afbb3fe7-2380-54db-a972-5bc74db915bc}}

The two sides feel the same sunset in opposite ways, and the verse gives each its own word: ἀέκουσιν, “against their will”, for the Trojans, and for the Achaeans ἀσπασίη τρίλλιστος, “welcome, thrice prayed for”; τρίλλιστος occurs only here in Homer (see [[thrice-prayed-for]], [[sunset-lines]] and [[oceanus]]). Hector says it himself at his assembly on the plain: “darkness came first, and that, above all, has now saved the Argives and their ships on the shore of the sea” (8.500–501). He yields to it in the formula that Nestor will use in the Achaean camp, “But now let us yield to black night” (8.502; 9.65; see [[yield-to-night]]).

**Night as a field of war.** Hector's orders make the night part of the battle. Fires are to burn all night, “in case even by night the long-haired Achaeans set out to flee over the broad back of the sea”, and if they do, they are to be wounded as they leap aboard, so that others may shrink from making war on Troy (8.508–516; see [[retreat]]). In the city the heralds are to set boys and gray-templed old men on the towers, the women are to burn fires in their halls, “and let there be a steadfast watch, so that no ambush may enter the city while the army is away” (8.517–522; see [[night-watch]]). “But for the night we will guard ourselves”, he ends, and at daybreak they will rouse Ares at the ships (8.529–531). The Trojans then sit “all night long” on the bridges of war, a thousand fires with fifty men at each, like the stars around the moon on a windless night (8.553–563; see [[stars-around-the-moon]], [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[phrenes]]).

**The hinge of two days.** The night is the space between the day Zeus gave Hector and the day he has promised him. Zeus tells Hera what she will see “at dawn” (8.470); Hector will speak his other word “at dawn” (8.525); the horses stand by the chariots munching barley “and waited for Dawn of the fair throne” (8.564–565; see [[dawn-line]] and [[eos]]). The night lasts through two books. The Achaeans, looking at the fires, are seized by panic and send an embassy to Achilles, and Nestor tells them that “this night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.1–8, 9.76–78); before it is over Odysseus and Diomedes raid the Trojan camp (Book 10). Dawn rises only at the start of Book 11 (11.1–2).
