**The ninth.** When the eagle of Zeus has put heart into the Achaeans, Diomedes drives out across the ditch first and the leaders follow him; “and Teucer came ninth, stretching his back-bent bow” (8.253–266; see [[diomedes]] and [[back-bent]]). Nine leaders stood up to face Hector in Book 7, and the middle of the list is the same four verses (7.164–167 = 8.262–265); Thoas and Odysseus have dropped out, and Menelaus and Teucer come in. It is the archer's first real part in the poem, after half a verse in Book 6 (6.31).

{{quote:aabfc98f-6cd2-5550-aa96-3210a7c52486}}

The iterative verbs make the tactic a routine, repeated with every arrow, and the simile gives the huge Ajax the part of the mother (see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]], [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[ajax]]).

**Eight men.** “Whom then did noble Teucer kill first of the Trojans?” (8.273). The narrator asks it as he asks before the kills of Hector and of Patroclus, without “whom last” (see [[whom-first-whom-last]]), and answers with eight names in three verses, men named nowhere else (8.274–276; see [[orsilochus-killed-by-teucer]], [[ormenus]], [[ophelestes]], [[daetor]], [[chromius-killed-by-teucer]], [[lycophontes]], [[amopaon]] and [[melanippus]]). The closing verse, “all of them, one after another, he brought down to the bountiful earth” (8.277), returns for Leonteus' kills and for Patroclus' (12.194, 16.418; see [[androktasia]]).

**Agamemnon's praise.** Agamemnon sees him “destroying the battalions of the Trojans with his strong bow” and comes to his side (8.278–280):

{{quote:5e9a94e3-700c-567d-a7a9-a70bd7315697}}

φίλη κεφαλή, “dear heart”, literally “dear head”, is found nowhere else in Homer (see [[dear-heart]]). Τελαμώνιε κοίρανε λαῶν, “son of Telamon, lord of the people”, is the half-verse with which Hector addressed Ajax (7.234); here it goes to the bastard brother (see [[telamonian]]). “And you may become a light to the Danaans” are the words with which Nestor will urge Patroclus to go out in Achilles' armor, and Patroclus will repeat them to Achilles (11.797, 16.39; see [[patroclus]]). The praise turns on Teucer's birth: Telamon reared him in his own house, “bastard though you were” (8.284; see [[nothos]], [[telamon]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The verse is reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.284), yet it is no slight. The father gave the bastard a place in his house, and the son can repay him with glory that reaches him “far away though he is” (8.285).

Then a promise, if Ilios falls: “a gift of honor, a tripod, or two horses with their chariot, or a woman, who would go up into your bed with you” (8.289–291). πρεσβήϊον, “gift of honor”, occurs only here in Homer (see [[geras]]). The man who took Achilles' prize promises one from a city not yet taken, and that night he will offer Achilles tripods, horses and women (9.122–134; see [[agamemnon]]).

**The answer.** Teucer needs no urging: “Eight long-barbed arrows I have let fly, and all of them were fixed in the flesh of young men swift in war; but this mad dog I cannot hit” (8.297–299; see [[long-barbed]], [[swift-in-war]] and [[mad-dog]]). His count matches the narrator's list.

**The arrows meant for Hector.** The first misses Hector, in the half-verse that told how Diomedes' spear missed him (8.302 ≈ 8.119), and kills Gorgythion, a son of Priam, whose head droops like a poppy (8.300–308; see [[gorgythion]] and [[the-poppy]]). The second misses “for Apollo made it swerve” (8.311), and kills Archeptolemus, the charioteer Hector found when Diomedes killed Eniopeus; his death and Hector's grief are told in the verses used for Eniopeus (8.313–317 ≈ 8.121–125; see [[archeptolemus]] and [[eniopeus]]). Twice in one afternoon a weapon aimed at Hector kills the man who drives him. Cebriones takes the reins (8.318–319; see [[cebriones]]).

**The stone.**

{{quote:56f02d84-de83-58c2-9a39-06172ed95274}}

“A blow is deadliest” there, as it was where Paris' arrow struck Nestor's horse (μάλιστα δὲ καίριόν ἐστι, 8.84, 8.326; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[bow]]). Ajax stands over his brother with his shield, and Mecisteus and Alastor carry him “groaning heavily” to the ships (8.330–334; see [[mecisteus-son-of-echius]] and [[alastor-who-carries-teucer]]).

**The string breaks again.** On the next day of fighting he is back at the wall, “just come from his hut” (12.336). At the ships he aims at Hector once more, and Zeus, guarding Hector, snaps the string, and “the bow fell from his hand”, in the half-verse of 8.329 (15.458–465). Twice the best archer of the Achaeans draws on Hector, and twice his string breaks.
