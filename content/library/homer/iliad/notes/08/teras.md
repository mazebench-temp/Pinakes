summary: Zeus gives signs all day. He thunders and sends a flash after weighing the fates at noon, throws a thunderbolt before Diomedes' horses, thunders three times as “a sign” of victory for the Trojans, and, after Agamemnon's prayer, sends an eagle with a fawn, which the Achaeans understand at once.

**Thunder and flash.** At noon Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies in his golden scales, and the day of the Achaeans sinks (8.68–74; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Then “he himself thundered loudly from Ida, and sent a blazing flash among the army of the Achaeans; and when they saw it they were amazed, and pale fear took hold of them all” (8.75–77). The thunder that ended Book 7 brought the same pale fear (χλωρὸν δέος, 7.479; see [[pale-fear]]). Neither needs a seer: the army reads the sign as it falls.

**The thunderbolt and the threefold sign.** When Diomedes drives at Hector, Zeus throws a thunderbolt before his horses (8.133–136; see [[thunderbolt]]), and Nestor reads it for him: “Do you not see that no help from Zeus goes with you? For now Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today” (8.140–142). Diomedes ponders whether to turn and fight, and “three times Zeus the counselor thundered from the mountains of Ida, giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.170–171; see [[that-turns-the-battle]] and [[decision-scene]]). σῆμα is the word, a sign set before the Trojans as a promise. The god answers each turn of the man's mind with a sign, until he gives it up.

**The eagle.** Agamemnon prays in tears that the Achaeans may at least escape, and Zeus pities him and nods (8.236–246; see [[prayer-type-scene]]):

{{quote:408eeff6-8fd8-5722-997f-a90eca3dab07}}

τελειότατον πετεηνῶν, “surest in omen of winged birds”, is said again only of the eagle Zeus sends to Priam (24.315; see [[surest-of-birds]] and [[eagle]]). πανομφαῖος, “lord of all omens”, from ὀμφή, a divine voice or sign, occurs only here (8.250; see [[lord-of-all-omens]]). No seer reads this sign either. The army sees “that the bird had come from Zeus” and charges, and the narrator does not explain why the fawn is dropped beside the altar where they sacrifice (see [[altar]] and [[birds]]). It is the only sign of the day that favors the Achaeans, and the only one that answers a prayer. What Zeus nods to is limited: “that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.246). By evening they have been saved, by the night that Hector says came too soon (8.500–501).
