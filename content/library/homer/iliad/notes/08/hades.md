**Beneath Hades.** Tartarus lies "as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth" (τόσσον ἔνερθʼ Ἀΐδεω, 8.16; see [[tartarus]]). Ἀΐδεω is a genitive in -εω, like Πηληϊάδεω in the first line of the poem. Here the god's name stands for his realm, where the dead go; Tartarus is lower still. In Hesiod the same measure separates Tartarus from the earth (*Theogony* 720).

**The gatekeeper.** Athena recalls the descent of Heracles: "when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades the gatekeeper / to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades" (8.367–368; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[eurystheus]] and [[erebus]]). πυλάρτης, "the gatekeeper", from πύλαι, "gates", and ἀραρίσκω, "fit, fasten", is Hades' epithet also at 13.415 and *Odyssey* 11.277 (see [[gatekeeper]]). His realm has gates, which the dead pass on their way in (5.646, 23.71), and a keeper. Hesiod gives the keeping to the hound, which fawns on those who go in and eats anyone it catches going out (*Theogony* 769–773). στυγερός, "hateful", is the god's word: his dank houses are hateful even to the gods (20.64–65), and the next verse names the Styx (8.369; see [[styx]]). The hound has no name in Homer; Hesiod calls him Cerberus (*Theogony* 311). The wound that Heracles gave Hades ἐν Πύλῳ, "in Pylos" (5.397), has been read since antiquity as a wound given "at the gate", on this same descent (see [[pylos-or-the-gate]]).
