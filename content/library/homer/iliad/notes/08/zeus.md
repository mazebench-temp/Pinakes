summary: Book 8 is Zeus' book. He forbids the gods to take part in the war on pain of Tartarus, weighs the fates of the two armies in his golden scales, and from Ida turns the battle against the Achaeans with thunder and a thunderbolt; when Hera and Athena set out to help them he turns them back through Iris, and at nightfall he foretells the fight at the ships over the dead Patroclus.

**The ban.** At dawn Zeus calls the gods to assembly "on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus" (8.3; see [[many-ridged-olympus]], [[olympians]] and [[assembly]]). No god, male or female, is to help either side:

{{quote:3a0e2a1b-df4f-5bd7-ab27-d9d570a06f03}}

He once hung Hera from the sky with anvils on her feet and flung down any god who tried to free her (15.18–24); this time the punishment is the deepest pit of all (see [[tartarus]]). Then he dares them all to pull against him on a golden rope: they could not drag him down, and he could haul them up with earth and sea. "So far am I above the gods, and above men" (8.18–27; see [[the-golden-rope]]).

**Athena answered.** The gods fall silent (8.28–29 = 9.693–694; see [[silence]]). Athena alone answers: they will keep out of the war, but they will give the Danaans counsel (8.30–37; see [[athena]]). Zeus smiles: "Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak / in full earnest, and I want to be kind to you" (8.38–40; see [[smiling-at-him]]). After the threats the mild answer is strange, and 8.28–40 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.28–40). At nightfall Hera says Athena's words to him again, and he does not smile (8.462–468).

**Gargarus.** He yokes his bronze-footed horses and puts on gold in the verses that yoke Poseidon's horses at Aegae (8.41–44 = 13.23–26; see [[bronze-footed]] and [[chariot-journey]]), and drives to Gargarus on Ida, "where he has a precinct and a fragrant altar" (8.48; see [[gargarus]] and [[ida]]). There he sits on the peaks, "exulting in his glory, / looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans" (8.51–52; see [[watching-the-war]]).

**The scales.** The battle is even all morning. At midday he weighs the fates of the two armies:

{{quote:77eb0408-3f65-5f0c-b442-d4815e180737}}

The scene returns only once, when Hector's day sinks before Achilles (22.209–213). Whether the scales show Zeus a fate he must accept or are an image of his own will is debated; here the verdict is the one he promised Thetis (1.524–527; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]).

**Thunder.** He thunders from Ida and sends a blazing flash among the Achaeans, and "pale fear took hold of them all" (8.75–77; see [[pale-fear]]). When Diomedes is about to pen the Trojans in their city, Zeus drops a thunderbolt in front of his horses (8.130–136; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Nestor reads the sign: "No man can hold back the mind of Zeus" (8.139–144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[kydos]]). Three times Diomedes thinks of turning to fight, and three times Zeus thunders, "giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle" (8.167–171; see [[that-turns-the-battle]] and [[teras]]), and Hector reads it as the promise of the son of Cronus (8.175; see [[hector]]).

**The eagle.** Agamemnon reminds Zeus of the thighs he burned on every altar of his on the way to Troy, and begs that the army at least escape (8.236–244; see [[agamemnon]]). "The father pitied him as he shed tears", nodded that the people would be saved, and sent an eagle with a fawn in its talons to the altar "where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus, lord of all omens" (8.245–250; see [[pity]], [[eagle]] and [[lord-of-all-omens]]). The rally lasts until "again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans" (8.335).

**Iris sent.** When Hera and Athena drive out through the gates of heaven, he is "terribly angry" and sends Iris to threaten them: he will lame their horses and smash their chariot, "so that the gray-eyed one may know what it is to fight her father" (8.397–406; see [[iris]]). He is less angry with Hera, "for she is always used to thwarting whatever I say" (8.407–408; see [[hera]] and [[nemesis]]).

**The prophecy.** He drives back to Olympus, and great Olympus shakes beneath his feet as he takes his throne (8.438–443; see [[olympus]] and [[poseidon]]). He mocks the two silent goddesses (8.447–456), and to Hera's protest he answers with the poem's first word of the death of Patroclus:

{{quote:49a53fa3-4b0f-5958-b526-0294b982193c}}

θέσφατον, "decreed", is the language of oracles (see [[thesphaton]]). The event is looser than the words: Patroclus is killed out on the plain, and Achilles shows himself at the ditch (see the translation's note on 8.475–476, [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). He ends with contempt for Hera's anger, even if she goes to the lowest limits of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronus sit (8.477–483; see [[iapetus]] and [[cronus]]). σέθεν δʼ ἐγὼ οὐκ ἀλεγίζω, "I do not care about you", is what Agamemnon said to Achilles (8.477; 1.180).

**The ban afterward.** The ban is broken in Book 13, when Zeus turns his eyes from Troy, expecting no god to help either side (13.7–9, echoing 8.10–11), and Poseidon goes among the Achaeans; Hera's seduction of Zeus on Gargarus lets him fight openly (14.153–360). Zeus wakes and restores the ban (15.4–33, 15.157–219), and lifts it in Book 20, sending the gods to help whichever side they will (20.20–30). See [[the-second-day-of-battle]].
