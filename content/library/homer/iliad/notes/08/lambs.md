summary: Lambs come into Book 8 only in a comparison. When Diomedes drives at Hector with Nestor beside him, the Trojans “would have been penned up in Ilios like lambs”, had Zeus not thrown his thunderbolt (8.130–132).
grc: [ἄρνες]
---

καί νύ κε σήκασθεν κατὰ Ἴλιον ἠΰτε ἄρνες (8.131). Lambs are the young of the flock, and σηκάζω, found only here in Homer, is to shut them in a pen, σηκός, such as those in which the Cyclops keeps his lambs and kids (*Odyssey* 9.219–222). The likeness is of helplessness and confinement: driven back behind their walls, the Trojans would be like young animals shut in the fold. It does not happen. Zeus thunders and sends his bolt into the ground before Diomedes' horses, and by evening it is the Achaeans who are shut in behind their wall (8.133–136, 8.213–216; see [[penned-like-lambs]]).

In Book 3 lambs were the victims of the oath, killed on the plain between the armies (3.103–120, 3.292–294), and in Book 4 the ewes of a rich man's fold bleated for their lambs (4.433–436; see [[sheep-in-the-fold]]). In later similes lambs are the prey of wolves and eagles (16.352–355, 22.308–310; see [[wolves]] and [[eagle]]).
