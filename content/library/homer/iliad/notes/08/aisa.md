At noon Zeus holds out his golden scales with two fates of death in them, one for each army, and lifts them by the middle: “and the fated day of the Achaeans sank” (ῥέπε δʼ αἴσιμον ἦμαρ Ἀχαιῶν, 8.72; see [[ker-doom]] and [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). αἴσιμος is the adjective of αἶσα. Elsewhere it means what is fitting or due: Agamemnon's advice to his brother is αἴσιμα, “what was due” (6.62, 7.121; see the note on Book 6). With ἦμαρ, “day”, it is the day allotted to a man as his portion, the day of his death. The day that sinks in the scales here is the army's, and the same half-line weighs the fate of one man in Book 22: “and Hector's fated day sank, and went down to Hades” (22.212–213). Achilles uses the phrase when he tells Lycaon that he spared Trojans before Patroclus “met his fated day” (21.100), and in the Odyssey Odysseus says that the suitors' fated day is already at hand (*Odyssey* 16.280).

ἦμαρ, “day”, joined to an adjective of fate or condition, names a turning point of a life: the day of freedom and the day of slavery (6.455, 6.463; see [[eleutheros]] and [[doulios]]), the day of return (*Odyssey* 1.9), and here the day of death. In Book 8 the day is literal as well. The scales are lifted at midday, and the Achaeans' day of losses ends only at sunset (8.485–488; see [[war-and-night]]).
