summary: Three times the narrator stops the story at the edge of another ending: Nestor would have died had not Diomedes seen it, the Trojans would have been penned in Ilios had not Zeus seen it, and Hector would have burned the ships had not Hera prompted Agamemnon. Athena and Zeus use the same turn in their speeches.

Each of Book 8's counterfactuals turns the day.

**Nestor.** His trace horse is down and Hector is upon him, “and now the old man would have lost his life there, had not Diomedes, good at the war cry, been quick to see it” (8.90–91; see [[nestor]] and [[diomedes]]). The rescuer is a man, in the verse that elsewhere names a god's rescue (εἰ μὴ ἄρʼ ὀξὺ νόησε; see [[divine-rescue]]).

**Troy.** Diomedes and Nestor drive at Hector:

{{quote:97dcf488-4c4d-5e4d-b4ec-a19b95799549}}

The ending prevented is the fall of Troy, and Zeus prevents it with his thunderbolt (8.133–136; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[lambs]]). The opening, ἔνθά κε λοιγὸς ἔην, “Then there would have been ruin”, returns when Odysseus and Diomedes make a stand against Hector in Book 11 (11.310).

**The ships.** Hector has the Achaeans penned between the ditch and the wall, “And now he would have burned the balanced ships with blazing fire, had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind to bestir himself and quickly urge on the Achaeans” (8.217–219; see [[hera]] and [[agamemnon]]). The burning of the ships is put off until Book 16, when the Muses are asked to tell “how fire first fell on the ships of the Achaeans” (16.112–124; see [[ships]]).

A man saves an Achaean, Zeus saves the Trojans, and Hera saves the ships. Between them the three keep the battle in the balance Zeus intends: the Achaeans are beaten but not destroyed, and the Trojans are not driven back into their city (see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**In speeches.** Athena tells Hera that if she had known Zeus' present mind when Eurystheus sent Heracles for the hound of Hades, “he would never have escaped the sheer streams of the water of Styx” (8.366–369; see [[heracles]]). Zeus tells the two goddesses what would have happened had they gone on: “struck by the thunderbolt, you would not have come back on your chariot to Olympus” (8.455–456), opening with a formula of prophecy turned into a counterfactual, “and it would have come to pass” (8.454; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]).
