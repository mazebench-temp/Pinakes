summary: Hector speaks to the Trojans at nightfall leaning on his spear eleven cubits long, as Agamemnon leaned on his scepter in the assembly of Book 2. Athena takes up her massive spear to go to war against her father's command, and Diomedes means to show Hector that his spear too can rage.
passages:
  - 8.493-496 | Hector leans on his spear
---

**Hector's spear.** At the assembly on the plain Hector holds “a spear eleven cubits long; and before him shone the bronze point of the spear, and a golden ring ran around it” (8.494–495), in the verses that described it when he came to Paris' house (6.318–320). The next verse is new to the description: “Leaning on it, he spoke his words among the Trojans” (8.496). With the Trojans in place of the Argives, it is the verse in which Agamemnon addressed the army in Book 2, leaning on the scepter that had come down to him from Zeus: τῷ ὅ γʼ ἐρεισάμενος ἔπεʼ Ἀργείοισι μετηύδα (2.100–109). The king leaned on the scepter of his house; Hector, speaking to an army in the field, leans on his spear (see [[scepter]] and [[assembly]]).

**Athena's spear.** Athena arms on Olympus and takes up her spear, “heavy, huge, and massive, with which she overpowers the ranks of men, of heroes, against whom she, the daughter of the mighty father, bears resentment” (8.389–391 = 5.745–747; see [[arming-scene]]). This time the spear is lifted against Zeus' command, and Iris, delivering his threat, adds a taunt of her own: Athena is “most dread, you shameless dog, if you really dare to lift your huge spear against Zeus” (8.423–424; see [[message-repeated]]).

**Diomedes' spear.** Diomedes takes Nestor into his chariot “so that Hector too may know whether my spear also rages in my hands” (8.111). His spear misses Hector and kills the charioteer (8.118–123), and later he plants it in the back of Agelaus (8.258). The phrase comes back in Book 16, when Diomedes lies wounded and Achilles says that “the spear does not rage in the hands of Diomedes son of Tydeus, to keep ruin from the Danaans” (16.74–75). Hector ends his night speech with the hope that Diomedes will learn his own prowess “if he stands up to my spear as it comes at him” (8.535–536).
