summary: Diomedes, told by Nestor to flee Zeus' thunderbolt, imagines Hector boasting among the Trojans that the son of Tydeus fled before him, and ends with Agamemnon's wish from Book 4, “then may the wide earth gape for me”. Nestor answers that no one in Troy would believe Hector.

Diomedes grants that Nestor's counsel is right, in the verse with which Agamemnon once granted that Nestor was right (8.146 = 1.286; see [[moira]]). Then he names the “dread grief” (αἰνὸν ἄχος) that Agamemnon felt over his wounded brother (8.147, 4.169; see [[achos]]), and the speech of the enemy that he cannot bear to hear. Agamemnon imagined a Trojan leaping on Menelaus' tomb; Diomedes imagines Hector speaking in the Trojan assembly, in a single line: “The son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships” (8.149; see [[tis-speech]]). The boast is already half true. Diomedes is retreating, and Hector shouts after him a moment later that the Danaans will dishonor him and that he is no better than a woman (8.161–166; see [[time]]). The wish is the one that ends Agamemnon's speech, “then may the wide earth gape for me” (8.150 = 4.182), and in both mouths the shame imagined is worse than death.

Nestor answers the imagined boast with the real audience: “Even if Hector calls you a coward and a weakling, the Trojans and the Dardanians will not believe him, nor the wives of the great-hearted shield-bearing Trojans, whose husbands in their prime you have flung in the dust” (8.153–156). The widows are Diomedes' witnesses; his fame is written in their losses. Then Nestor turns the horses himself (8.157; see [[retreat]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]).
