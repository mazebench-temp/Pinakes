**Saffron-robed Dawn.** The second day of battle begins:

{{quote:0ec9175d-80ce-5795-9f70-e2c14c45e0f8}}

The verse is found once more, on the last morning of the poem, when the Trojans gather to burn Hector (24.695, with δέ for μέν). κροκόπεπλος, “saffron-robed”, is Dawn's epithet in two other dawns of the Iliad, the morning Thetis brings Achilles his new armor (19.1–3) and the morning star before Patroclus' pyre (23.226–227; see [[saffron-robed]] and [[eos]]). The day that begins here is Day 25 of the poem, which Zeus gives to the Trojans (see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The hours.** The day is marked in formulas. “As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing, so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell” (8.66–67 = 11.84–85; the first verse also Od. 9.56). ἠώς here is the morning, the part of the day before the sun stands at its height (see the translation's note on 8.66). “But when the sun stood astride the middle of the sky”, Zeus holds out his scales (8.68–69; compare 16.777, Od. 4.400; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). At sunset the fighting ends (8.485–488; see [[sunset-lines]]).

**Waiting for dawn.** The night is spent waiting for the next day. Zeus tells Hera that “at dawn” she will see him destroying the Argives (ἠοῦς, 8.470); Hector will speak again among the Trojans at dawn (8.525), burns his fires “until early-born Dawn” (8.508; see [[early-born]]), and means to fight “early, at daybreak” (8.530). The book ends with the horses that “stood beside the chariots and waited for Dawn of the fair throne” (8.565; see [[horses]]). That dawn does not come until Book 11 (11.1–2), after the night of the embassy to Achilles and of the night raid.
