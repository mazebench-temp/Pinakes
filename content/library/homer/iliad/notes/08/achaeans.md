summary: The battle is even until midday, when the scales of Zeus sink the Achaeans' fate; routed by his thunder and penned inside their ditch, they are rallied by Agamemnon and the eagle of Zeus, then driven back to the ships, where they pray. The night that saves them comes “welcome, thrice prayed for” (8.53–79, 8.213–252, 8.335–347, 8.487–488).

**The scales.** “And the long-haired Achaeans took their meal quickly among the huts, and straight after it they armed themselves” (8.53–54). The battle is even all morning. At midday Zeus lifts his golden scales and sets in them “two fates of death … of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans”, “and the fated day of the Achaeans sank” (8.66–74; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[ker-doom]]). He thunders from Ida and sends his flash among them, “and when they saw it they were amazed, and pale fear took hold of them all” (8.75–77). Their leaders run, and only Nestor is left (8.78–80).

**Penned and rallied.** By the afternoon they are hemmed in behind their ditch (8.213–216; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Agamemnon shames them with their boasts on Lemnos and prays to Zeus in tears, and Zeus answers with an eagle: “When they saw that the bird had come from Zeus, they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (8.251–252; see [[agamemnon]], [[eagle]] and [[charme]]).

**Back to the ships.** Zeus rouses the Trojans again, and Hector harries the Achaeans like a hound at the heels of a boar, “always killing the hindmost; and they fled” (8.341–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). Across the ditch they halt by the ships, “calling to one another, and to all the gods they lifted up their hands, and each man prayed aloud” (8.346–347; see [[prayer]]). Hera sees them and pities them (8.350).

**Night.** “For the Trojans the light went down against their will, but to the Achaeans welcome, thrice prayed for, came the murky night” (8.487–488; see [[war-and-night]]). Hector fears that they will try to flee across the sea in the dark, and orders fires to burn all night to watch them (8.508–511); a thousand fires burn on the plain (8.562). He calls them “these dogs borne along by the fates, whom the fates bring here on the black ships” (8.527–528; see [[dogs]]). The next book opens with the army in grief, and Agamemnon, weeping, proposes that they sail home (9.1–28).
