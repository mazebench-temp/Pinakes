**The ban.** At dawn Zeus tells the assembled gods that whoever goes to help either the Trojans or the Danaans will come back to Olympus “struck in no seemly way”, or be hurled into misty Tartarus, “as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth” (8.10–16; see [[tartarus]] and [[the-golden-rope]]). Athena accepts it and keeps one kind of help in reserve: the gods will not fight, but “we will put counsel in the Argives' minds that will help them” (8.35–37; see [[boule]]). The ban still holds when the next day of battle begins, and Strife alone of the gods is in the fighting while the others sit in their halls (11.73–77). Later that day Poseidon breaks it, in secret, because “Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.351–357); Zeus restores it when he wakes in Book 15, and he lifts it in Book 20, sending the gods to help whichever side they please (20.22–25).

**Zeus alone.** With the other gods held back, Zeus intervenes by signs and by force. He weighs the armies' fates at noon and thunders and flashes among the Achaeans (8.69–77; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]); he throws a thunderbolt in front of Diomedes' horses and thunders three times from Ida as a sign of victory for the Trojans (8.133–136, 8.170–171; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[teras]]); he sends an eagle with a fawn to Agamemnon's prayer (8.245–252); and when the Achaeans have rallied, “again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans” (8.335; see [[menos]]). Every turn of the day's battle is his.

**What the ban allows.** Two interventions by other gods pass without comment. Hera puts it into Agamemnon's mind to rally the army (8.218–219), the counsel Athena had said the gods might give (see [[phrenes]]). And when Teucer shoots at Hector a second time, “he missed again, for Apollo made it swerve” (8.311; see [[apollo]]). Apollo's help goes to the side Zeus is favoring, and nothing more is said of it.

**The sally that fails.** Hera and Athena are the only gods who try to break the ban in the open. Hera, seeing the Achaeans driven to their ships, pities them; Athena arms; the gates of heaven open of themselves (8.350–396). Zeus sees them from Ida and sends Iris with his threat, and they turn back at the outermost gates of Olympus (8.397–437; see [[iris]] and [[messenger-scene]]). In Book 5 the same two goddesses drove out with Zeus' leave and fought beside Diomedes (5.711–909). The difference is the will of Zeus, which the poem now shows at work from a mountaintop (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).
