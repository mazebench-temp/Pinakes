summary: Athena alone answers Zeus' ban, promising to keep out of the war but to give the Argives counsel. In the evening she rails against her father, who has forgotten how often she saved his son Heracles, arms herself and sets out with Hera, until Zeus' threat, carried by Iris, turns them back, and she sits on Olympus in silent anger.

**The morning.** When the gods fall silent at Zeus' threat, "At last the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke among them" (8.30; see [[gray-eyed]]). ὀψὲ δὲ δὴ μετέειπε is the half-verse for the one who breaks a long silence, as Diomedes does after the Trojan offer and after Achilles' refusal (7.399, 9.696; see [[silence]]). She addresses Zeus as she does in the Odyssey, "Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords" (8.31; *Odyssey* 1.45, 1.81, 24.473; see [[most-high]]). She grants that his strength is unyielding and asks only to give the Argives counsel, "so that they do not all perish because of your anger" (8.32–37; see [[boule]]). He calls her Tritogeneia and "dear child", and answers mildly (8.39–40; see [[tritogeneia]] and [[zeus]]).

**Her grievance.** In the evening, when Hera proposes that they go to the Achaeans' help (8.350–356; see [[hera]]), Athena first wishes Hector dead, "killed at the hands of the Argives in his native land" (8.358–359), and then turns on her father:

The labors are those that Eurystheus set Heracles, whom Hera made his master on the day of his birth (19.95–133; see [[eurystheus]] and [[heracles]]). The ghost of Heracles bears her out in the Odyssey: when he brought up the hound of Hades, "Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena" (*Odyssey* 11.626; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]). Now Zeus "hates" her and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis, who kissed his knees and begged him to honor Achilles (8.370–372; see [[thetis]]). Her complaint ends in confidence: "Yet the day will come when he calls me his dear gray-eyed one again" (8.373). It does. In Book 15 Zeus promises that the Achaeans will take Ilios "through the plans of Athena" (15.71), and in Book 22 he answers her again with "Take heart, Tritogeneia" and lets her go down to bring about Hector's death (22.183–185).

**Arming.** She lets her robe fall on her father's floor, puts on the tunic of Zeus, and arms "for tearful war"; she steps onto the flaming chariot and takes up the heavy spear "with which she overpowers the ranks of men" (8.384–391; see [[arming-scene]], [[flaming]] and [[daughter-of-a-mighty-father]]). The verses are those of her arming in Book 5 (5.733–737, 5.745–747), less the aegis and the golden helmet (5.738–744). Hera drives, and the gates of heaven open for them (8.392–396; see [[hours]]).

**Turned back.** Zeus' threat is aimed at her. The wounds his thunderbolt deals will not heal in ten years, "so that the gray-eyed one may know what it is to fight her father" (8.406). Iris repeats it, and adds an insult he did not give her: "But you are most dread, you shameless dog, if you really / dare to lift your huge spear against Zeus" (8.423–424; see [[iris]] and [[dogs]]). Hera gives up for both of them (8.427–431). On Olympus, mocked by Zeus, the two goddesses mutter and sit side by side, "devising evils for the Trojans", and "Athena kept silent and said nothing, / sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her" (8.457–460 = 4.20–23; see [[cholos]]). In Book 4 she sat silent while Zeus proposed to end the war in friendship; here she is silent because he has shut her out of it.

**Named by men.** Agamemnon promises Teucer a gift of honor "if aegis-bearing Zeus and Athena grant me / to sack the well-built citadel of Ilios" (8.287–288; see [[teucer]]). Hector, boasting at nightfall, wishes he were immortal and ageless "and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored" (8.538–540; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[apollo]]).
