summary: Aristotle quotes Zeus' boast that the gods could not drag him down from the sky as words well spoken for an unmoved mover, takes Diomedes' fear of Hector's boast as an example of the courage that comes from shame, and cites the verses on the wound of Nestor's horse for the thinness of a horse's skull.

- Zeus, whom nothing can move (*Movement of Animals* 4): [[aristotle-golden-rope]].
- The courage of citizens (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, 1116a17–29): [[aristotle-civic-courage]].
- **The horse's crown.** Horses, Aristotle says, show gray hair with age more than any other animal we know, because for their size they have the thinnest bone around the brain. The proof is that a blow to that place is fatal to them, and that is why Homer composed his verse as he did. The verses are those on Nestor's trace horse, struck by Paris' arrow “where the foremost hairs of horses / grow on the skull, and a blow is deadliest” (8.83–84). Moisture flows in easily through the thin bone, the heat of the body fails with age, and so those hairs turn gray (*Generation of Animals* 5.5; see [[nestor]] and [[horses]]).
