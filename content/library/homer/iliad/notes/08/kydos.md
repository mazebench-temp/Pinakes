summary: Book 8 is the day on which Zeus gives glory to Hector. Zeus sits on Ida “exulting in his glory”; Nestor, Hector and the narrator say in turn that he grants it to Hector, and Agamemnon asks whether Zeus has ever robbed a king of glory as he has robbed him.
passages:

  - 8.236-246 | Agamemnon's prayer
---

**Zeus' own glory.** Zeus drives to Gargarus, unyokes his horses, and sits on the peaks of Ida “exulting in his glory” (κύδεϊ γαίων, 8.51), looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans. The phrase belongs to a god who sits apart in his strength: Briareus beside Zeus, when Thetis brought him to Olympus (1.405), Ares beside Zeus after Diomedes wounded him (5.906), and Zeus again on the next day of battle, when the other gods blame him “because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (11.78–83; see [[watching-the-war]]). The god who has κῦδος in himself gives it to men.

**Glory to Hector.** The book says four times that Zeus gives Hector glory, each time in another voice (see [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). Nestor, under the thunderbolt, tells Diomedes that “Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man today”, and another day, if he wishes, will give it to them (8.141–143; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Hector, after Zeus has thundered three times from Ida, tells the Trojans: “I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory, and of a bane to the Danaans” (8.175–176; see [[nike]] and [[neuo]]). The narrator confirms it as Hector pens the Achaeans against their ditch, “now that Zeus had given him glory” (8.216), a half-line repeated of him in Books 11 and 19 (11.300, 19.204). And Agamemnon, standing on the ship of Odysseus, asks Zeus whether he has ever struck one of the mighty kings with such blind folly “and robbed him of great glory” (8.236–237; see [[ate]]). The verb, ἀπηύρας, is the one Achilles used of Agamemnon himself when he took the prize of honor from him (αὐτὸς ἀπούρας, 1.356; see [[geras]]): glory, like a prize, can be taken away.

Each speaker treats the glory as Zeus' to give and to take, and none can see how long it will last. Nestor's “today” is the true measure. Zeus pities Agamemnon and sends an eagle, and the Achaeans rally for a while (8.245–277); then he rouses the Trojans again (8.335), and at the end of the book he tells Hera that Hector will not stop until Achilles is roused beside the ships (8.470–477; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The phrase “grants glory” returns in later books whenever a fighter explains the strength of an enemy whom Zeus is favoring: Menelaus of Hector over the body of Patroclus (17.566), and Agenor of Achilles before he stands up to him (21.570).

**The family.** Teucer, answering Agamemnon's praise, calls him “most glorious son of Atreus” (Ἀτρεΐδη κύδιστε, 8.293), the formal address to the king (1.122, 2.434). And Zeus, mocking Athena and Hera when they come back to Olympus, asks whether they have tired themselves “in the battle where men win glory” (μάχῃ ἔνι κυδιανείρῃ, 8.448; see [[where-men-win-glory]]): the glory they set out to fight for was not theirs to win.
