**The fame of a shield.** Hector urges his horses to follow hard, “so that we may take Nestor's shield, whose fame now reaches the sky, that it is all of gold, its rods and the shield itself” (8.191–193). The κλέος of the shield is a report, and the clause that follows says what the report is: that it is all of gold. No such shield is described anywhere else in the poem (see the translation's note on 8.192–193, [[nestor]] and [[shield]]). Hector wants the corselet of Diomedes too, which Hephaestus made, and expects to drive the Achaeans to their ships with the two prizes (8.194–197). To take a famous piece of armor is to take its fame with it (see [[stripping-the-dead]]). οὐρανὸν ἵκει, “reaches the sky”, is what the Iliad says of things that rise: the shout of an army (2.153), the gleam of bronze (2.458), the savor of sacrifice (1.317). With κλέος the phrase occurs only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it is the fame of a song, of Penelope, and of Odysseus himself, who names himself to the Phaeacians with it (*Odyssey* 8.74, 19.108, 9.19–20). Two more things rise to the sky in this book. Hector orders fires burned all night, “and their glare may reach the sky” (8.509), and the savor of the Trojans' sacrifice is carried up to heaven, where the gods will not take their share of it (8.549–552; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).

**Fame for a father.** Agamemnon, watching Teucer bring down Trojans from under Ajax's shield, urges him to shoot on, so that he may become a light to the Danaans and to his father Telamon, who reared him in his own house though he was a bastard: “bring him to glory, far away though he is” (8.281–285; see [[teucer]], [[telamon]] and [[nothos]]). ἐϋκλείη is good fame, and ἐπίβησον, “set him upon it”, is the causative of ἐπιβαίνω, “mount, go aboard”: in this book Hector sets his new charioteer on the chariot with it (8.128–129) and hopes to make the Achaeans board their ships (8.196–197). The son's deeds at Troy will carry the old man's name in Salamis, as Hector fought to win “great fame for my father and for myself” (6.446; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). The adjective returns in later books. Odysseus prays to Athena that he and Diomedes may come back to the ships “with glory” from their night raid (10.281; ἐϋκλεῖας), and Hector, waiting for Achilles, decides it is better to die “gloriously” before the city than to go back inside (22.108–110).
