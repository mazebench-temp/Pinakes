summary: When the other Achaean leaders flee, Diomedes rescues Nestor, takes him into his chariot and drives at Hector, until Zeus' thunderbolt turns them back. Hector's taunt makes him think three times of turning, and three times Zeus thunders; later he is the first across the ditch, and at night Hector boasts that he will kill him.

**The rescue.** When Zeus' flash sends the leaders running, Diomedes, “good at the war cry”, is the one who sees Nestor caught with a dying horse and Hector bearing down (8.80–91; see [[nestor]] and [[good-at-the-war-cry]]). He calls on Odysseus to stand with him, and Odysseus “did not hear” (8.92–98; see [[odysseus]]). Then, “alone as he was”, he goes to the old man (8.99). He invites him into his chariot to see the horses of Tros, in the words with which Aeneas invited Pandarus into his own (8.105–107 = 5.221–223), and these are the horses he took from Aeneas himself in Book 5 (8.108; see [[tros]] and [[aeneas]]). Nestor takes the reins, as Athena did when she pushed Sthenelus from the chariot in Book 5 (8.116; 5.840; see [[sthenelus]]), and Diomedes aims at Hector, “so that Hector too may know whether my spear also rages in my hands” (8.110–111). The spear misses Hector and kills his charioteer (8.118–123; see [[eniopeus]]).

**The thunderbolt.** The Trojans would have been penned in Ilios like lambs, had Zeus not thrown a thunderbolt in front of Diomedes' horses (8.130–136; see [[penned-like-lambs]], [[thunderbolt]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Nestor tells him to turn: Zeus gives glory to Hector today (8.139–144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Diomedes agrees, but fears what he will hear:

When Agamemnon feared what “someone” would say if Menelaus died, he ended with the same curse, in a verse of the same shape (4.182; see [[tis-speech]] and [[may-the-earth-gape]]). Nestor answers that no one will believe Hector if he calls Diomedes “a coward and a weakling” (8.153; Aristotle quotes Diomedes' fear as the type of civic courage, see [[aristotle-civic-courage]]), not the Trojans, nor the wives of the men Diomedes has killed (8.152–156). Nestor opens with the words of Agamemnon's rebuke in Book 4, “Ah, son of wise-hearted Tydeus” (8.152; 4.370; see [[tydeus]]). In Book 9 Diomedes will remind Agamemnon that he called him a weakling before the Danaans, with the word Nestor uses here (ἀνάλκιδα, 9.34–35; 8.153).

**Torn two ways.** Hector's taunt follows at once (8.161–166; see [[hector]]). Diomedes is “torn two ways in thought”, whether to turn his horses and fight face to face: “Three times he pondered in his mind and in his heart, and three times Zeus the counselor thundered from the mountains of Ida” (8.167–170; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). In Book 5 he charged Apollo three times, and it took a fourth charge and the god's warning to make him give way (5.436–444). Now Zeus answers each thought before it becomes a charge.

**First across the ditch.** After Zeus sends his eagle, none of the Danaans can claim to have driven out over the ditch ahead of Diomedes: “far the first”, he kills Agelaus son of Phradmon with a spear in the back as he turns to flee (8.253–260; see [[agelaus]]). The other leaders follow him, Teucer ninth (8.261–266; see [[teucer]]).

**Hector's prediction.** At night Hector names Diomedes as the man he will meet at the ships: “Tomorrow he will learn his own prowess, if he stands up to my spear as it comes at him” (8.535–536; see [[arete]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The next day Diomedes' spear strikes Hector's helmet and brings him to his knees, and Diomedes is wounded, but in the foot, by Paris' arrow (11.349–400).
