summary: Zeus speaks to the gods “what the heart in my breast commands me”, and then tells Athena that he does not speak with an earnest heart. Teucer's heart is eager to hit Hector, and Hector's to hit Teucer; Andromache mixed wine for Hector's horses “whenever your heart bade you”, in the words with which Agamemnon honors his best men.

**The heart of Zeus.** Zeus opens the assembly of the gods with the verse of a man who calls an assembly: “Hear me, all you gods and all you goddesses, so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me” (8.5–6). The second line is the one Hector, Antenor and Priam used in Book 7 (7.68, 7.349, 7.369; see [[hear-me]]), and in the story Agamemnon tells in Book 19 Zeus opens with nearly the same two verses when he announces to the gods the birth of a son, on the day Ate deceived him (19.100–105). Then, when Athena has pleaded for the Danaans, he answers with a smile: “Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak in full earnest, and I want to be kind to you” (8.39–40). οὔ τι θυμῷ πρόφρονι, literally “not with a willing heart”, takes back the tone, though not the ban (see [[fathers-and-daughters]]). He says the same two verses to her when she objects to his wish to save Hector (22.183–184). At the end of the day Hera leaves the battle to him, “thinking his own thoughts in his heart”, to judge between Trojans and Danaans (8.430–431).

**Hearts in the fight.** Nestor is afraid “in his heart” when the thunderbolt falls (8.138), and Diomedes answers him that dread grief “comes upon my heart and spirit” at the thought of Hector's boast; three times he ponders “in his mind and in his heart” whether to turn and fight (8.147, 8.169; see [[phrenes]] and [[retreat]]). Later the two archers of the book meet in the same phrase. Teucer shoots twice straight at Hector, “and his heart was eager to hit him” (8.301, 8.310), and misses twice, the second time because Apollo turns the arrow aside; Hector leaps from his chariot with a stone and goes straight at Teucer, “and his heart bade him hit him” (8.322), and does (see [[teucer]] and [[hector]]).

**The horses' heart.** Hector reminds his horses of the care Andromache gave them before she gave it to him: honey-sweet wheat, and wine mixed “for you to drink whenever your heart bade you” (8.188–189). The half-line, πιέειν ὅτε θυμὸς ἀνώγοι, is the one Agamemnon used to Idomeneus in Book 4, when he said that Idomeneus' cup always stands full, like his own, “to drink whenever your heart bids you” (4.262–263). The horses are fed as a king honors his best man. That horses have a θυμός is no surprise in Homer, where Achilles' horses weep for Patroclus (17.426–440); wine given to horses is found nowhere else in the poem (see the translation's note on 8.188–190 and [[the-horses-of-hector]]).
