summary: The quarrel is fought again at the end of the day. Zeus comes back from Ida and mocks Hera and Athena for the chariot he has turned back; Athena sulks in silence, and Hera answers in the words of Athena's plea at dawn; Zeus replies with the prophecy of the battle at the ships and tells her he does not care for her anger, and Hera says nothing (8.438–484).

**Zeus comes home.** In Book 1 the quarrel began when Zeus came home from his meeting with Thetis and sat on his throne among the gods, and Hera knew at once what the two had planned (1.531–543). Book 8 ends its day in the same place. Zeus drives from Ida to the seats of the gods; Poseidon unyokes his horses and covers the chariot; he sits on his golden throne, “and great Olympus shook beneath his feet” (8.438–443; see [[zeus]] and [[olympus]]). Hera shook Olympus too that day, when she shook on her throne in anger at Hector's boast (8.198–199), and in Book 1 Zeus' nod made it quake (1.528–530). Athena and Hera sit apart, saying nothing and asking nothing, and he understands (8.444–446).

**The mockery.** He asks why they are so grieved: surely they have not tired themselves destroying the Trojans in battle. His might and his “irresistible hands” are such that all the gods on Olympus could not turn him back, and trembling seized their limbs before they ever saw the war; had they gone on, his thunderbolt would have struck them, and they would not have come back to Olympus in their chariot (8.447–456; see [[kertomia]]). The words are those of his threat to Hera in Book 1, when he warned her that “all the gods there are on Olympus” would be no help to her when he laid his irresistible hands on her (1.566–567; 8.450–451; see [[irresistible-hands]]). The goddesses' journey and its end are told in [[the-second-day-of-battle]].

**The lines of Book 4.** The two goddesses mutter, sit side by side and plan evil for the Trojans; Athena keeps silent, sulking at her father, but Hera's breast cannot contain her anger, and she begins, “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” (8.457–462). The six verses are those with which the two met Zeus' proposal of peace in Book 4 (4.20–25; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). There the quarrel ended in a bargain, and the war went on as Hera wished. Here nothing is bargained. Hera repeats the plea that Athena made at dawn (8.463–468 ≈ 8.32–37) with two changes: Zeus' strength, “unyielding” in Athena's words, is now “no weakling's”, and “as you command” becomes “if you command” (8.32, 8.35; 8.463, 8.466).

**Zeus' answer.** At dawn he smiled at Athena and said that he did not speak in earnest (8.38–40). At nightfall he answers Hera with the plan itself: tomorrow she will see him destroying the Argive army, and Hector will not cease from war until Achilles is roused beside the ships, over Patroclus dead (8.470–477; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). He does not care for her anger, not even if she goes to the lowest limits of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronus sit in Tartarus, “since there is nothing more shameless than you” (8.477–483; see [[fall-of-cronus]] and [[dogs]]). “So he spoke, and white-armed Hera said nothing to him” (8.484). In Book 1 she sat in silence too, afraid, after his threat (1.568–569).

**No reconciliation.** Book 1 ended the quarrel with Hephaestus' cup and story, the laughter of the gods, and a feast until sunset (1.571–611; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]). Book 8 has no peacemaker. The sun sets on Hera's silence (8.484–486), the scene turns to Hector's army on the plain, and no feast of the gods is told. Zeus had said it beforehand to Iris: “With Hera I am not so indignant or so angry, for she is always used to thwarting whatever I say” (8.407–408), as he had told Thetis that Hera “always” quarrels with him among the gods and says that he helps the Trojans (1.518–521). The quarrel is taken up again in Books 14 and 15, when Hera deceives him on Ida and he wakes to threaten her (14.153–360, 15.4–33; see [[hera]]).
