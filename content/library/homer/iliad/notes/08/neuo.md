summary: Zeus nods twice in Book 8, once by Hector's account and once in the narrator's. Hector reads the thunder from Ida as a nod promising him victory and glory; Zeus, pitying Agamemnon's tears, nods to him that his people will be saved and not perish.
grc: [κατένευσε]
passages:
  - 8.170-176 | Hector reads the thunder
  - 8.245-252 | Zeus pities Agamemnon
---

**Hector's nod.** Three times Diomedes thinks of turning his horses on Hector, and three times Zeus thunders from the mountains of Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.169–171; see [[nike]] and [[teras]]). Hector reads the sign aloud: “I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory, and of a bane to the Danaans” (8.175–176). No nod is described. The thunder is the sign, and Hector turns it into the gesture that binds Zeus, the one with which Zeus confirmed his promise to Thetis (1.524–530; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). He is right for the day, and for the next: Zeus' prophecy at the end of the book gives Hector the battle until Achilles is roused (8.470–477; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[kydos]]).

**Agamemnon's nod.** Agamemnon prays, in tears, that the Achaeans may at least escape and not be beaten down by the Trojans (8.242–244), and this time the narrator reports the nod itself: the father pitied him “and nodded to him that his people would be saved and not perish” (8.245–246; see [[pity]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]). An eagle with a fawn in its talons confirms it (8.247–252). The words of the nod are Agamemnon's own from Book 1, where he said, giving up Chryseis, that he would rather the people be safe than perish (λαὸν σῶν ἔμμεναι ἢ ἀπολέσθαι, 1.117; see [[rather-the-people-be-safe]]). The two verses are the only places in the poem where the phrase occurs. What Agamemnon claimed to prefer in Book 1 he now begs for, and Zeus grants it for the day.

The two nods do not contradict each other. Hector's glory and the Achaeans' rescue both serve the promise Zeus made to Thetis, honor for her son, and the fall of Troy, which he granted Hera in Book 4, lies beyond them both (4.37–49; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).
