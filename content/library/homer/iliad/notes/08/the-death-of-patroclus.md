**Where it comes.** Hera has just repeated Athena's plea to be allowed to give the Argives counsel (8.462–468). Zeus answers with the course of the war. Tomorrow he will destroy the Argive army “more than ever”, and Hector will not stop until Achilles himself is roused (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[quarrel-on-olympus]]). Patroclus, who has not been named since he led Briseis out of Achilles' hut in Book 1 (1.337–347), comes into the poem again as a dead man. The phrase is short, περὶ Πατρόκλοιο θανόντος, “over Patroclus dead”, and Zeus gives no reason and no killer. ὣς γὰρ θέσφατόν ἐστι, “for so it is decreed” (8.477): it is a thing spoken by a god, and here the god who speaks is the one who will bring it about (see [[thesphaton]]).

**The prophecy and the event.** Zeus names the day as the one “when they fight at the sterns of the ships”, ἐπὶ πρύμνῃσι, “in the direst strait” (στείνει ἐν αἰνοτάτῳ). That day comes in Books 15 and 16: Hector takes hold of the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls for fire (15.704–718), and when the stern is burning, Achilles sends Patroclus out (16.122–129). But Patroclus is killed out on the plain beneath the walls of Troy (16.777–867), the fight for his body is fought there through Book 17, and it ends at the ditch, where Achilles shows himself after the Achaeans have fled to the ships and the Hellespont (18.148–150, 18.203–231). The prophecy compresses the fight at the sterns and the fight over the body into one “direst strait” (see the translation's note on 8.475–476). In Book 15 Zeus tells Hera the plan again, this time naming the killer and the place: Hector will kill Patroclus “before Ilios” (15.64–68).

**Who knows it.** In Book 8 only Hera hears it. Achilles is not told. His mother often told him the mind of Zeus, but she did not tell him of this (17.401–411), and Antilochus brings it to him as “grievous news, which ought never to have happened” (18.18–21; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]).
