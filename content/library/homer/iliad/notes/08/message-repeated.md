**The message.** Zeus' instructions to Iris begin with an order for her, to turn the goddesses back (8.399–400), and then give the threat itself, under the formula of a solemn promise, “For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass” (8.401; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]): he will lame their horses, hurl them from the chariot and smash it, and the thunderbolt's wounds will not heal in ten years, “so that the gray-eyed one may know what it is to fight her father”; with Hera he is not so angry, “for she is always used to thwarting whatever I say” (8.402–408; see [[thunderbolt]]).

**The delivery.** Iris puts a frame of her own around it, “Where are you rushing? What madness is in your hearts? The son of Cronus will not let you help the Argives” (8.413–415), and then repeats the threat, changing the persons as the delivery requires. “I will lame” becomes “that he will lame” (8.402, 8.416), and “so that the gray-eyed one may know” becomes “so that you may know, gray-eyed one” (8.406, 8.420), spoken to Athena's face. The verses 8.420–424 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.420–424, and [[shameless-dog]]). As the text stands, the messenger, like Idaeus in Book 7, says more than she was told (7.385–397). The insult is Iris' own, but Zeus ends the day with one like it: there is nothing more shameless than Hera, κύντερον (8.483; see [[dogs]]).
