---
summary: Book 8 is the first day on which the Achaeans are routed, and it argues over retreat from beginning to end. The leaders flee before Zeus' lightning, Odysseus runs past Diomedes' call, Nestor persuades Diomedes that it is no shame to yield to Zeus, and Diomedes, who fears Hector's boast more than death, three times nearly turns back.

**The rout.** Zeus thunders from Ida and flashes among the Achaeans, and pale fear takes them all (8.75–77). “Then neither Idomeneus dared to stay, nor Agamemnon, nor did the two Ajaxes stay”; only Nestor is left, “not of his own will”, because Paris has shot his trace horse (8.78–86; see [[nestor]] and [[deos]]). Diomedes shouts to Odysseus: “where are you fleeing, turning your back like a coward in the throng? Do not let someone plant a spear in your back as you flee” (8.94–95; see [[like-a-coward]]). “So he spoke, but much-enduring brilliant Odysseus did not hear, and darted past toward the hollow ships” (8.97–98). The verb ἐσάκουσε occurs only here; it can mean “did not hear” or “did not heed”, and the translation's choice leaves his flight unexplained rather than a refusal (see its note on 8.97 and [[odysseus]]). The warning about the spear in the back comes true for a Trojan: when the Achaeans rally, Diomedes kills Agelaus, who “had turned his horses to flight”, by planting his spear in his back between the shoulders (8.257–259; see [[agelaus]]). Hector will use the same words to Achilles: “you will not plant your spear in my back as I flee” (22.283).

**Yielding to Zeus.** The thunderbolt falls before Diomedes' horses, and Nestor tells him to turn: Zeus is giving the glory to Hector today, and no man can hold back his mind (8.139–144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Diomedes grants that the advice is right and gives his reason for refusing it: Hector will say among the Trojans, “The son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships” (8.146–150; see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[aidos]]). Nestor answers that the Trojans and their widows will not believe it, and turns the horses himself (8.152–157). Hector shouts that the Danaans will dishonor him now (8.161–166), and three times Diomedes ponders whether to turn and fight, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida (8.167–171). In Book 5 Diomedes himself led the retreat before Ares, keeping the men's faces to the enemy (5.596–606); here he must be persuaded to give way before Zeus, and the poem lets both his shame and Nestor's good sense stand.

**The last retreat.** Later in the day the Trojans drive the Achaeans back across the ditch, Hector “always killing the hindmost”, like a hound at the haunches of a boar or a lion (8.338–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). At night Hector fears that the Achaeans will flee by sea in the dark, and orders fires so that they cannot board their ships unhurt (8.508–516; see [[war-and-night]]). The flight he fears is what Agamemnon will propose in earnest at the start of the next book (9.26–28).
