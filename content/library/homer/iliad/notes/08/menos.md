summary: Zeus “roused might in the Trojans”, and Hector boasts that the Achaean wall will not hold back his. Two of Hector's charioteers die in the same verse, “his soul and his strength were loosed”; Athena wishes the same end on Hector and calls her father “the thwarter of my purposes”; and Zeus tells the goddesses, “such is my might and such are my irresistible hands”.

**Might given.** The battle opens in the verses of Book 4, with the clash of shields and spears “and the fury of men in bronze corselets” (μένεʼ ἀνδρῶν, 8.60–65 = 4.446–451). Then Zeus weighs the day, and the might goes to one side. Hector tells the Trojans that the Achaeans' walls, “feeble things, worth nothing”, will not hold back “my might” (μένος ἁμόν, 8.177–178; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[nepios]]). After Teucer's arrows have turned the fight for a while, Zeus intervenes again: “And again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans” (8.335), and Hector goes among the foremost “exulting in his strength” (8.337; see [[exulting-in-strength]]). μένος is what a god puts into a side, as Athena put it into Diomedes in Book 5 (see the note on Book 5), and Book 8 shows Zeus doing it for an army.

**Strength loosed.** Diomedes' spear kills Hector's first charioteer, Eniopeus, and Teucer's arrow his second, Archeptolemus, and each death ends in the same verse: “and there his soul and his strength were loosed” (8.123, 8.315; first of Pandarus, 5.296). μένος here is the strength of the living body, which goes out of it with the ψυχή (see [[psyche]]). Athena wishes the same end on Hector himself: “how I wish that man would lose his strength and his life, killed at the hands of the Argives in his native land” (8.358–359).

**Purposes.** In the same speech Athena calls Zeus “hard as he is, forever perverse, the thwarter of my purposes” (ἐμῶν μενέων ἀπερωεύς, 8.361). The plural μένεα are intents, what one has in mind, the sense nearest to the root of the word, “have in mind, be intent on”; ἀπερωεύς, “one who holds back”, occurs only here (see [[fathers-and-daughters]] and [[athena]]).

**The might of Zeus.** When the two goddesses come back from their aborted drive, Zeus mocks them: “such is my might and such are my irresistible hands, that all the gods there are on Olympus could not turn me back” (8.450–451). The phrase μένος καὶ χεῖρες ἄαπτοι is the measure of the strongest warriors: the Trojans rejoiced that Hector had escaped “the might and the irresistible hands of Ajax” (7.309; see the note on Book 7), and later it is Hector's that men must face (17.638). Zeus describes his strength as the poem describes a hero's, and adds that no god can stand against it (see [[the-golden-rope]] and [[zeus]]).
