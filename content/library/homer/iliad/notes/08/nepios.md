summary: Hector calls the Achaeans who built the wall “Fools”, with the word the narrator uses for men who do not know what is coming. He boasts that his horses will easily leap the ditch; in Book 12 they balk at its edge, and in Book 18 the narrator calls the Trojans fools for cheering him.

  - 8.172-183 | Hector rouses the Trojans

Hector reads Zeus' thunder as a promise of victory and turns on the Achaean wall: “Fools, who contrived these walls, feeble things, worth nothing! They will not hold back my might, and the horses will easily leap over the ditch they have dug” (8.177–179; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[menos]]). νήπιοι stands first in its verse, the place where the narrator puts it when he passes judgment on a man who does not see his fate: Agamemnon believing the Dream (2.38), Nastes coming to war dressed in gold (2.873), Asius driving at the wall (12.113), Patroclus forgetting Achilles' warning (16.686). Here a character takes the narrator's word for his enemies, and the poem answers him in kind.

The ditch is the first test. In Book 12 Hector drives at it again, and his horses will not cross: they stand neighing on the lip, for it was not easy to leap over or to cross, with its overhanging banks and sharp stakes (12.50–59). The words are Hector's own, turned back on him: ῥέα … ὑπερθορέονται, “will easily leap over” (8.179), and οὔτʼ … ὑπερθορέειν … ῥηϊδίη, “not to be leapt across from close at hand, nor crossed with ease” (12.53–54). Polydamas has to persuade the Trojans to leave their chariots and cross on foot (12.60–79). And when Hector rejects Polydamas' advice to retreat after Achilles has shown himself at the ditch, the Trojans cheer him, and the narrator gives them the word Hector gave the Achaeans: “Fools, for Pallas Athena took away their wits” (νήπιοι, 18.310–313; see [[hector]] and [[foresight]]).
