In Book 7 the herald Idaeus parted Hector and Ajax with the words “it is good to yield to night too”, and Hector repeated them (7.282, 7.293). In Book 8 Hector yields to night again, not as a champion in a duel but as the commander of an army that has nearly won. He says so plainly: he thought he would destroy the ships and all the Achaeans and go home to Ilios, “but darkness came first, and that, above all, has now saved the Argives and their ships on the shore of the sea” (8.498–501). For the Trojans the light went down “against their will” (8.487; see [[war-and-night]]). The verse with which he yields, ἀλλʼ ἤτοι νῦν μὲν πειθώμεθα νυκτὶ μελαίνῃ, is followed in each of its uses by supper (δόρπα, 8.503). Nestor says it in the Achaean assembly the same night, when he proposes a watch at the ditch and a council of the elders (9.65–66), and in the Odyssey Eurylochus says it to persuade Odysseus to land on the island of the Sun, with ruinous results (*Odyssey* 12.291–292).

Hector yields to night only so far. He orders fires to burn till dawn, so that the Achaeans cannot slip away, a watch on the city, and an attack at daybreak (8.508–531). In Book 18, on the night after Achilles has shown himself at the ditch, he rejects Polydamas' advice to go back inside the walls and gives the same orders for the night and the dawn, in the same words (18.296–304, repeating 8.530–531), and the narrator calls the Trojans who cheer him fools (18.310–313; see [[nepios]]).
