summary: Book 8 is a book of threats. Zeus threatens the gods with a beating or with Tartarus and has to strike no one; his threat to the two goddesses is carried by Iris, who adds an insult of her own. Among men, taunts lead straight to the spear, the arrow and the stone.

**Threats that are enough.** Zeus opens the day with a threat to every god: whoever goes to help either side will come back to Olympus “struck in no seemly way”, or be hurled into Tartarus (8.10–17; see [[tartarus]] and [[the-golden-rope]]). No god is struck in Book 8. When Hera and Athena drive out, Zeus sends a second threat, to lame their horses, smash their chariot and wound them with his thunderbolt, and he vouches for it with the formula “I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass” (8.401–405). It is enough to turn them back, and at night he tells them what would have happened: “I will tell you this, and it would have come to pass” (8.454–456; see [[prophecy]]). He also boasts as he did to Hera in Book 1, when he warned her that “all the gods there are on Olympus” would be no help to her once he laid his irresistible hands on her (1.565–567). Now his might and irresistible hands are such “that all the gods there are on Olympus could not turn me back” (ὅσοι θεοί εἰσʼ ἐν Ὀλύμπῳ, 8.450–451; see [[menos]]).

**The messenger's addition.** Iris gives the goddesses Zeus' threat in his own words, with the persons changed (8.416–422; see [[message-repeated]] and [[messenger-scene]]). Then she adds two verses of her own, which Zeus did not give her: “But you are most dread, you shameless dog, if you really dare to lift your huge spear against Zeus” (8.423–424). The verses 8.420–424 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity (see the translation's note on them and [[shameless-dog]]). Insult passes freely between the gods in this book. Poseidon calls Hera “reckless in speech” for proposing that they hold Zeus off (8.209; see [[reckless-in-speech]]), and Zeus ends the day by telling her that nothing is more shameless than she (8.483; see [[aidos]]).

**Words before blows.** Among men the taunt comes before the blow, or instead of it. Hector shouts after the retreating Diomedes that he is no better than a woman and a “wretched doll”, and Diomedes, held back by Zeus' thunder, can answer only in thought (8.161–171; see [[wretched-doll]] and [[retreat]]). Teucer tells Agamemnon that he has hit eight men, “but this mad dog I cannot hit” (8.297–299); Hector answers him with a stone that breaks his bowstring and numbs his hand (8.320–329; see [[mad-dog]], [[teucer]] and [[dogs]]). At night Hector calls the Achaeans “dogs borne along by the fates” (8.527; see [[ker-doom]]).
