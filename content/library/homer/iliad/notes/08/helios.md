summary: The sun marks the turns of the second day of battle. At midday Zeus weighs the fates, at evening the sun's light falls into Oceanus against the Trojans' will, and Hector boasts of what the next sunrise will see; below, the Titans have no share in the rays of "Hyperion the Sun".
passages:
  - 8.66-74 | The scales of Zeus
  - 8.485-488 | Night falls on the second day of battle
---

**Midday.** The fighting is even "as long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing", "but when the sun stood astride the middle of the sky", Zeus holds out his golden scales (8.66–69; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The same verses mark the turn of battle on two later days. The fighting of the next morning is told in the same two verses, and the Achaeans break the Trojan lines at the hour when a woodcutter takes his meal (11.84–90; 11.84–85 = 8.66–67). On the day of Patroclus' death the missiles strike home on both sides as long as the sun stands at midheaven, but when it turns toward the time for unyoking oxen, the Achaeans prevail "beyond their portion" (16.777–780 ≈ 8.67–68).

**Hyperion the Sun.** Iapetus and Cronus sit in Tartarus with no joy "in the rays of Hyperion the Sun" (8.480; see [[hyperion]] and [[tartarus]]). The sun that sees everything on earth does not reach them (see [[who-sees-all-things]]).

**Sunset.**

{{quote:afbb3fe7-2380-54db-a972-5bc74db915bc}}

The day ends when the sun sets, and the two armies feel it differently. The Trojans, who were winning, lose the light "against their will"; the Achaeans welcome the night they have prayed for (see [[war-and-night]], [[sunset-lines]] and [[oceanus]]). Agamemnon prayed for the opposite on the first day of battle, that the sun might not set before he had burned Priam's palace (2.412–415). On the day of Patroclus' death Hera sends "the unwearied sun", unwilling, down into the streams of Oceanus, and the Achaeans can rest from the fighting (18.239–242).

**Sunrise.** Hector ends his night assembly with a boast about the morning. Diomedes will lie wounded among the foremost, with many companions around him, "when the sun rises tomorrow" (8.535–538; see [[hector]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).
