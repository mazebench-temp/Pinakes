**Before battle.** At dawn “the long-haired Achaeans took their meal quickly among the huts, and straight after it they armed themselves” (8.53–54; see [[arming-scene]]). δεῖπνον is the meal that comes before the day's work, here eaten in haste.

**The honors of the feast.** Hector shouts after Diomedes as he turns his chariot: “Son of Tydeus, the Danaans of the swift horses honored you above others with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups; but now they will dishonor you: you are no better than a woman after all” (8.161–163). Honor at the feast is rank made visible. Agamemnon tells Idomeneus that his cup always stands full, like the king's own (4.257–263), and he honored Ajax with the long chine after his duel (7.321–322). Sarpedon names the same three honors in the same verse when he tells Glaucus why the kings of Lycia must fight in the front rank: they are honored “with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (12.310–312). Hector turns the honors into a taunt: a man who runs will lose his place at the feast (see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]).

**Boasts over the wine.** Agamemnon reminds the army of the boasts they made on Lemnos, “eating much meat of straight-horned cattle and drinking mixing bowls brim-full of wine”, that each of them would stand against a hundred or two hundred Trojans (8.229–234; see [[lemnos]], [[wine]], [[empty-boasting]] and [[straight-horned]]). The feast is where boasts are made, and the battle where they are tested. Apollo puts the same question to Aeneas in Book 20: where are the threats he made to the Trojan kings over his wine (20.83–85)?

**Supper on the plain.** At sunset Hector camps the army on the plain: “But now let us yield to black night and make ready our supper” (8.502–503; see [[yield-to-night]]). They are to bring cattle and fat sheep from the city, “quickly, and provide yourselves with honey-sweet wine and bread from the halls, and gather much wood besides” (8.505–507), and the verses of the order are repeated as narrative when it is carried out (8.545–547). In the verse known only from the Second Alcibiades the Trojans also sacrifice (8.548; see [[sacrifice]]). They eat in the field, by a thousand fires (8.553–563; see [[night-watch]]). Later that night Nestor proposes the same supper and a watch for the Achaeans, in the first two verses of Hector's order (9.65–66 = 8.502–503).
