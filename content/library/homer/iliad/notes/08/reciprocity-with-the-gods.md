summary: Book 8 tests the exchange with the gods against the will of Zeus. Hera reminds Poseidon of the Achaeans' gifts at Helice and Aegae, and he will not act; Agamemnon reminds Zeus of his sacrifices at every altar, and Zeus grants him safety for the day; and at night, in verses known from the Second Alcibiades, the gods refuse the savor of the Trojans' hecatombs.

**Gifts to Poseidon.** Hera tries to rouse Poseidon with the argument of a prayer, made on behalf of others: the Danaans “bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae, many and pleasing; so you should will them victory” (8.203–204; see [[helice]], [[aegae]] and [[nike]]). Helice and Aegae are seats of his cult (which Aegae is meant was disputed already in antiquity), and the gifts are the debt she calls in. Poseidon does not deny the debt; he refuses because Zeus is far the stronger (8.209–211; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The exchange binds the gods only as far as Zeus allows.

**Agamemnon's altars.** Agamemnon's prayer has the form of Chryses' prayer in Book 1 (1.37–42). He names his gifts: “Yet I say that I never passed by your beautiful altar in my many-benched ship on my ill-starred way here, but on every one I burned the fat and the thighs of oxen, eager to sack well-walled Troy” (8.238–241). Then he asks, and asks little: let the Achaeans at least escape (8.242–244). Zeus grants that much, and the sign is placed where the gifts were given: the eagle drops the fawn “beside the beautiful altar of Zeus … where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus, lord of all omens” (8.249–250; see [[altar]], [[teras]] and [[lord-of-all-omens]]). The sacrifices are acknowledged, but what they were made for, the sack of Troy, is not granted. Zeus has his own altar on Gargarus, the peak of Ida from which he watches the battle (8.48; see [[gargarus]]), where Hector, he says in Book 22, used to burn him the thighs of oxen (22.170–172).

**The refused savor.** At night the Trojans bring cattle and sheep from the city and feast on the plain, “and the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven” (8.549). Four verses around this one are not in the manuscripts; they are known from the Second Alcibiades ascribed to Plato, which reports that the Trojans offered perfect hecatombs and that the blessed gods took no share of the sweet savor and did not wish to, “for sacred Ilios was hateful to them, and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (8.548, 8.550–552; *Alcibiades II* 149d–e; see the translation's note on these lines, [[the-refused-sacrifice]] and [[hecatomb]]). The dialogue uses them in an argument that the gods look to the soul and not to the size of a sacrifice. They reverse what Zeus told Hera in Book 4: of all cities he honored sacred Ilios most, “and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear”, for his altar never lacked its share of the feast (4.44–49). The last verse is the same, and the gods' hatred is told in the words of the last book, where Ilios, Priam and his people are hateful to Hera, Poseidon and Athena (24.25–28; see [[kotos]]). Whether the verses belong to the poem or not, they state the end of the exchange between Troy and its gods.
