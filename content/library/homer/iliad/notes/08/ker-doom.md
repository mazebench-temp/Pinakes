**In the scales.** The battle is even all morning. When the sun stands at the middle of the sky, Zeus weighs the two armies:

{{quote:77eb0408-3f65-5f0c-b442-d4815e180737}}

Each army has one κήρ, a fate “of death that lays men low” (see [[that-lays-men-low]]), set in the scales in the dual (δύο κῆρε), and the pan that sinks to the earth carries death. The verses then change number, and the Achaeans' fates are plural, κῆρες, with a dual verb, ἑζέσθην (see the translation's note on 8.69–74). What sinks is also called “the fated day of the Achaeans” (αἴσιμον ἦμαρ; see [[aisa]]). Zeus thunders from Ida at once and sends a flash among the Achaeans, and pale fear takes them all (8.75–77; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[pale-fear]]). The verse that sets the two fates in the scales is used once more, when Zeus weighs the deaths of Achilles and Hector, and Hector's day sinks and goes down to Hades (22.209–213). In between Hector himself “knows the sacred scales of Zeus” and turns his chariot to flight (16.658). Whether the scales show Zeus a fate fixed apart from him or are the form his own decision takes, the poem does not say (see [[moira]] and [[zeus]]).

**Borne by the fates.** At the end of the day Hector tells the Trojans what he hopes for the next: “to drive away from here these dogs borne along by the fates, whom the fates bring here on the black ships” (8.527–528). κηρεσσιφόρητος, “carried by the κῆρες”, occurs only here, and the next verse spells it out (see [[borne-by-the-fates]]). The κῆρες carry men off and lead them on elsewhere too: Odysseus calls “witnesses” all those “whom the fates of death have not carried away” since Aulis (2.301–302), and the sons of Merops went to Troy against their father's will, “for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Hector turns the image into an insult. The Achaeans came to Troy only to die there (see [[dogs]]). The scales had said as much at noon, for the day; Sarpedon says it of every man, that “the fates of death stand over us, countless”, which no mortal can flee or escape (12.326–327; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]).
