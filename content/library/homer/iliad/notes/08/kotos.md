---
summary: The grudge of Book 8 is Athena's and Hera's against Troy. Athena takes up the spear with which she overpowers the heroes “against whom she … bears resentment”, and Zeus mocks the goddesses for coming back without destroying “the Trojans, against whom you have conceived a dread grudge”.
passages:
  - 8.384-396 | Athena arms
  - 8.447-456 | Zeus mocks the goddesses
---

**The spear of resentment.** Athena arms in her father's house to go out against his ban, and takes up her spear, “heavy, huge, and massive, with which she overpowers the ranks of men, of heroes, against whom she, the daughter of the mighty father, bears resentment” (8.389–391). The three verses are those of her arming in Book 5 (5.745–747; see [[arming-scene]] and [[athena]]). There she went out with Hera, took Diomedes' chariot and wounded Ares through him (5.835–863). Here the gates of heaven open for the goddesses (8.393–396), and they get no farther than the outermost gates of Olympus, where Iris meets them with Zeus' threat (8.409–425). κοτέσσεται names a resentment that waits for its object, like the κότος that Calchas says a king keeps in his chest until he has satisfied it (1.81–83). Athena's waits throughout the book.

**A dread grudge.** When Zeus comes back to Olympus and finds the two goddesses sitting apart, he mocks them: “Surely you have not tired yourselves in the battle where men win glory, destroying the Trojans, against whom you have conceived a dread grudge” (8.448–449; see [[where-men-win-glory]]). κότον αἰνὸν ἔθεσθε, “you have set a dread grudge”, makes the grudge a thing they have laid up and keep. Zeus has known it since Book 4, when he asked Hera what evils Priam and his sons had done her, that she would eat them raw if she could (4.31–36; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). The poem gives its cause only in its last book: sacred Ilios and Priam and his people were hateful to Hera, Poseidon and Athena from the time Alexander insulted the goddesses who came to his courtyard (24.25–30; see [[paris]]). The lines that the Second Alcibiades adds to the end of Book 8 use the same words, that sacred Ilios, Priam and his people were hateful to the gods, who therefore took no share of the Trojans' sacrifice (8.550–552; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
