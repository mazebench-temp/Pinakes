**The omen.** Book 8 has the first bird that the poem shows sent as an omen. αἰετὸν ἧκε τελειότατον πετεηνῶν: Zeus “sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds” (8.247; see [[eagle]]). πετεηνός, “winged”, is the word that named the many tribes of birds on the Asian meadow (2.459; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). Here it ranks the eagle above all others as the bird whose sign comes true. The army needs no seer to read it: “when they saw that the bird had come from Zeus” (ὄρνις, the common word for a bird, 8.251), they sprang on the Trojans and remembered the joy of battle (8.251–252; see [[teras]] and [[divination]]). Calchas, “by far the best of the bird-seers” (1.69), is not called.

**Carrion.** Athena, about to arm and drive down to the battle against Zeus' command, wants to see “whether some Trojan too will glut the dogs and the birds with his fat and his flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans” (8.379–380). κύνας ἠδʼ οἰωνούς is the pair of Agamemnon's threat in Book 2 (2.393), and of the proem, where the dead were “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5; see [[dogs]]). The goddess wishes on Trojans the fate the poem began by giving to Achaeans.
