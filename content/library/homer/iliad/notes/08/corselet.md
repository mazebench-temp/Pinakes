summary: Hector wants to take from Diomedes' shoulders “the intricately worked corselet that Hephaestus toiled to make”, a corselet mentioned nowhere else. The verb of the corselet, θωρήσσω, arms both armies at dawn, Athena on Olympus, and the Trojans for the next day.
grc: [θωρήξομαι, θωρηχθέντες]
en: [arm myself]
passages:
  - 8.191-197 | Nestor's shield and Diomedes' corselet
---

**Diomedes' corselet.** Hector names the two prizes he means to take: Nestor's golden shield, “and from the shoulders of Diomedes, tamer of horses, the intricately worked corselet that Hephaestus toiled to make. If we could take these two, I would hope to make the Achaeans board their swift ships this very night” (8.194–197; see [[shield]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). The corselet is not mentioned anywhere else, and the poem does not say how Diomedes came by a god's work. The half-verse that names its maker, Ἥφαιστος κάμε τεύχων, is used only of Agamemnon's scepter, which Hephaestus “toiled to make” (2.101), and of the armor Achilles puts on in Book 19, “the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus toiled to make” (19.368; see [[hephaestus]]). δαιδάλεος, “intricately worked”, is a word for fine craftsmanship (see [[intricately-worked]]).

**Arming.** θωρήσσω, “put on the corselet”, is Homer's word for arming. The Achaeans eat and “armed themselves” (θωρήσσοντο, 8.54); Athena goes into her father's house to “arm myself in armor for war”, and does (8.376, 8.388; see [[arming-scene]]); and Hector tells the Trojans that “early, at daybreak, armed in our armor”, they will rouse Ares beside the ships (θωρηχθέντες, 8.530), a verse he will speak again in Book 18 (18.277, 18.303). The fighting men of the clash are χαλκεοθώρηκες, “in bronze corselets” (8.62 = 4.448; see [[armored-in-bronze]]).
