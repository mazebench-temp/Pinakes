**Asking after husbands.** When Hector reaches the Scaean gates, “the wives and daughters of the Trojans came running around him, asking after their sons and brothers and kinsmen and husbands (πόσιας)” (6.238–240). He tells them to pray, and the narrator adds: “sorrows were in store for many” (6.241). The first thing the city asks of the man from the battlefield is news of its men. See [[trojan-women]] and [[kasignetos]].

**Husband in his prime.** Andromache ends the story of her losses with the one who is left:

{{quote:a07873ef-41d8-55e7-8356-abd8df4d97d6}}

θαλερὸς παρακοίτης, “husband in his prime”, is literally “the flourishing one who lies beside me” (παρά, “beside”, and κοῖτος, “bed”; see [[flourishing]]). Hector used the feminine of the same phrase in Book 3, when he told Paris that Menelaus was the man “whose flourishing wife you have” (θαλερὴν παράκοιτιν, 3.53), and Nestor uses its plural for the Trojan women's husbands whom Diomedes has laid in the dust (θαλεροὺς παρακοίτας, 8.156). The husband gathers into one man all the kin she has lost, father, mother and brothers (6.413–428). Briseis, lamenting Patroclus, tells a story of the same kind: she saw the husband her father and mother gave her killed before his city, and her three brothers, who all met their day of destruction (19.291–294; see [[chere]] and [[captive-women]]).

**Her husband pitied her.** Hector gives the child back to Andromache, “and she took him to her fragrant bosom, laughing through her tears; and her husband (πόσις) saw it and pitied her” (6.483–484). In this scene the narrator names the two by what they are to each other and to the child: when the boy shrinks from the helmet, “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (6.471), and at the moment of pity Hector is her husband. Then Hector strokes her with his hand and calls her by name (6.485), the gesture of Thetis to Achilles and Dione to Aphrodite (1.361, 5.372). See [[husbands-and-wives]], [[hector]] and [[andromache]].

The book's other marriage runs the other way. Helen wishes that she had been the wife (ἄκοιτις) of a better man (6.350), and Paris tells Hector that “my wife” (ἄλοχος) persuaded him back to the war with gentle words (6.337–338; see [[nemesis]] and [[marriage]]).
