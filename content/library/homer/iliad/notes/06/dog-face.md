---
summary: Helen twice calls herself a dog in her speech to Hector, “dog that I am,” with the noun from which κυνώπης is made.
refs: [6.344, 6.356]
en: [dog that I am]
---

In Book 3 Helen called herself κυνῶπις, “dog-faced” (3.180). To Hector she uses the noun itself, twice in one speech. She begins, “Brother-in-law to me, dog that I am, evil-scheming and chilling” (κυνὸς κακομηχάνου ὀκρυοέσσης, 6.344), and she ends by saying that the toil of war has beset his mind “for my sake, dog that I am, and for Alexander's blind folly” (εἵνεκʼ ἐμεῖο κυνός, 6.356; [[ate]], [[evil-scheming]], [[chilling]]). As in Book 3, the genitive agrees with the “me” in ἐμεῖο. The dog is the image of shamelessness ([[dogs]], [[aidos]]). Her self-blame is set against Zeus' part in the same speech: he “has set an evil fate” on her and on Paris, so that they may be a theme of song for men to come (6.357–358; [[helen]], [[who-is-to-blame]]).
