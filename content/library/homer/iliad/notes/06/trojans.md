summary: Routed by the Achaean leaders, the Trojans are about to flee into Ilios when Helenus' advice sends Hector through the ranks. They rally so sharply that the Argives think a god has come down to help them, and Hector tells them to hold while he goes to the city (6.1–118).

**The rout.** With the gods gone from the field, the Achaean leaders kill fourteen Trojans and allies in thirty-two lines, and not one Achaean falls (6.5–36; see [[androktasia]]). Adrastus' horses bolt toward the city, “where the others too were fleeing in terror” (6.41), and the Trojans would have gone up into Ilios “beaten by their own cowardice” (ἀναλκείῃσι δαμέντες, 6.74) had Helenus not spoken (see [[helenus]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). In between, Agamemnon wishes every one of them dead, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb” spared (6.57–60; see [[agamemnon]]).

**The rally.** Hector goes through the army, and “they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans” (6.106), in the verses of the rally in Book 5 (6.103–106 = 5.494–497; see [[hector]]). The Argives give ground “and thought that one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky to bring help to the Trojans, so sharply did they rally” (6.108–109). No god has come. The gods have left the battle (6.1), and the Trojans are doing on their own what the Achaeans put down to a god.

{{quote:b77e2fa6-a3ed-54bf-affe-964f8837b1c4}}

**Hector's words.** “High-hearted Trojans and far-famed allies, be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (6.111–112). The address and the call to courage are the formulas of Hector's exhortations (8.173–174, 15.486–487; see [[exhortation]] and [[trojan-allies]]). He tells them what he is going to do: go up to the city and ask the elders and the wives to pray and promise hecatombs (6.113–115; see [[trojan-elders]] and [[hecatomb]]).

**In the city.** The rest of the book shows the people the fighting is for. The wives and daughters of the Trojans crowd around Hector at the Scaean gates, asking after their men (6.237–241; see [[trojan-women]]). The Trojans made Theano priestess of Athena (6.300; see [[theano]]). Hector tells Andromache that he would feel shame before “the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes” if he held back from the war (6.441–443; see [[aidos]]), and at the end he tells Paris that it grieves him to hear the reproaches of the Trojans, “who have much toil for your sake” (6.524–525; see [[paris]]).
