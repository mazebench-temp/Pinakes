summary: Book 6 holds the war's beginning, its middle years and its end. Its first battle names the Achaean leaders once more, each with his victims; in Troy the robes from Sidon recall Paris' voyage with Helen, Andromache tells of Achilles' raids on the towns of the Troad, and Hector foresees the fall of the city while his household mourns him alive.

**The leaders once more.** With the gods gone from the field (6.1), the Achaean leaders kill one after another: Ajax, Diomedes, Euryalus, Polypoetes, Odysseus, Teucer, Antilochus, Agamemnon, Leitus and Eurypylus, and then Menelaus takes Adrastus alive (6.5–38). The list names the army's leaders a last time before the story moves into Troy, as the Catalogue, Helen on the wall and the review of Book 4 named them (see [[the-review-of-the-army]] and [[androktasia]]). The victims come from all over Troy's world: a Thracian ally, a rich host from Arisbe, twin grandsons of Laomedon, a man of Percote, a man of Pedasus on the Satnioeis (see [[death-in-battle]]).

**The beginning.** Hecuba's robes were “the work of women of the Sidonians, whom godlike Alexander himself had brought from Sidon, sailing over the wide sea on that voyage on which he brought back Helen” (6.289–292; see [[sidonians]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The offering to Athena comes from the voyage that began the war. Herodotus quotes these lines to show that Homer knew of Paris' wandering to Sidon on his way home (2.116). Helen, in the next scene, curses the day she was born, before “these things came to pass” (6.345–348).

**The years between.** Andromache's story is the war before the Iliad. Achilles sacked Thebe, killed her father and her seven brothers among their herds, and ransomed her mother (6.414–428), in the years of raiding on the towns of the Troad from which Chryseis and Briseis also came (1.366–369, 2.689–693; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). Her advice about the fig tree recalls three assaults on the wall at that point, by the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus, the sons of Atreus and Diomedes, which the Iliad tells nowhere else (6.433–439; see [[the-fig-tree]] and [[andromaches-advice]]).

**The end.** Hector says to Andromache what Agamemnon said over his brother: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (6.447–449; see [[a-day-will-come]]), and sees her led away captive (6.454–465). The household he leaves behind mourns him “while he was still alive” (6.500–502), as the city will mourn him dead (24.719–776). The book ends with hope, the mixing bowl of freedom that the Trojans may set up when the Achaeans are gone (6.526–529; see [[eleutheros]]). See [[sack-of-troy-foretold]].
