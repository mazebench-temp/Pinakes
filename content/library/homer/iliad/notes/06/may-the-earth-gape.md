summary: Hector turns the wish on his brother, “There and then may the earth gape for him!”, and then on himself: he would rather be dead, and the heaped earth over him, than hear Andromache's cries as she is dragged away into slavery. Andromache and Helen too wish for the earth or for death in Book 6.

**For Paris.** Hector tells his mother that he will go and fetch Paris, “if he is willing to listen to what I say. There and then may the earth gape for him! For the Olympian has raised him as a great bane to the Trojans and to great-hearted Priam and his sons” (6.281–283). He goes further: “If I could see him going down into the house of Hades, I would think my heart had forgotten its joyless misery” (6.284–285). Agamemnon's wish in Book 4 was for himself, to escape the shame of an enemy's boast (4.182). Hector's is for his brother, and the shame is Paris' and the city's: the earth should take the man who brought the war (see [[paris]], [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**For himself.** At the end of his answer to Andromache the wish takes its first form again. Hector has imagined a man in Argos pointing at her as she weeps, “This is the wife of Hector”, and the fresh sorrow it will bring her (6.459–463). Then he says:

This is the saying's shape: a speech of “someone” imagined, and a wish to be under the earth before it can be heard (see [[tis-speech]]). χυτὴ γαῖα, “heaped earth”, is the earth of a grave mound; Diomedes uses it of his father, “whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes” (14.114). The words Hector cannot bear are not a boast over him but his wife's cries as she is dragged away (see [[doulios]] and [[captive-women]]).

**Others' wishes.** The book has two more. Andromache tells Hector, “for me it would be better, if I lose you, to go down beneath the earth” (χθόνα δύμεναι, 6.410–411), and Helen wishes that on the day she was born a storm had carried her off to a mountain or into the sea (6.345–348; see [[never-born]]). Hector's wish is granted: he dies before Troy falls. In the last book Andromache, lamenting over his body, foresees what he did not want to hear: her own slavery, and for the child slavery or death, hurled from the tower by some Achaean (24.725–738).
