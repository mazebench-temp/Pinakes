**A suppliant's knees.** Adrastus, thrown from his chariot at Menelaus' feet, “clasped his knees and begged him” (λαβὼν ἐλίσσετο γούνων, 6.45). The gesture binds the man who is held, and Menelaus is persuaded; but Agamemnon runs up, Menelaus pushes the suppliant away with his hand, and Agamemnon kills him (6.51–65). The pushing away undoes the clasp. The same gesture fails the sons of Antimachus, who beg Agamemnon from their chariot in Book 11 (γουναζέσθην, 11.130), and Lycaon, who holds Achilles' knees with one hand and his spear with the other (21.71–72). See [[supplication]], [[apoina]] and [[adrastus-the-trojan]].

**A goddess's knees.** Helenus' instructions, Hector's and the rite itself repeat one line: the robe is to be laid “on the knees of Athena of the lovely hair” (Ἀθηναίης ἐπὶ γούνασιν ἠϋκόμοιο, 6.92, 6.273, 6.303). The knees are those of the goddess's image in her temple on the height of the city, and they imply a seated figure. Strabo, discussing the claims of later Ilium, took the passage as evidence for a seated image and noted that many ancient images of Athena were seated (13.1.41). The offering is laid where a suppliant would lay his hands. Theano prays, promising twelve cows, “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). The knees received the robe, and the goddess refused the prayer. Elsewhere the outcome of a fight is said to “lie on the knees of the gods” (θεῶν ἐν γούνασι κεῖται, 17.514, 20.435); here the gift lies on a goddess's knees and the outcome is already decided. See [[the-robe-for-athena]], [[temple]], [[athena]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].

**A stallion's knees.** Paris comes down from Pergamus like a stalled horse that has broken its halter and gallops across the plain: “trusting in his splendor his knees carry him swiftly to the haunts and pasture of the horses” (ῥίμφά ἑ γοῦνα φέρει, 6.510–511). The knees are the seat of speed and strength, the part that fails first in age (4.313–314) and is “loosened” in death (5.176). The simile returns word for word for Hector in Book 15 (15.263–268). See [[the-stallion]] and [[paris]].
