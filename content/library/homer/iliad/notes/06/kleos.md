summary: Hector will not keep out of the war, because he has learned to fight among the foremost, “winning great fame for my father and for myself”. The book also imagines the fame that others will speak, of Hector as a man in Argos points out his widow, of his son, and of Helen and Paris in song.

**Fame for a father.** Andromache has begged Hector to stay on the tower (6.431). He answers that he would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women, and gives the reason:

{{quote:4042d2ed-5830-5c3d-9b18-dd32069bc8e0}}

ἀρνύμενος, “winning”, is the verb of earning a prize or a satisfaction (τιμὴν ἀρνύμενοι, 1.159). The fame is his father's before it is his own. κλέος passes along a family, and a son can add to it or shame it, as Glaucus was sent to Troy “not to shame the race of my fathers” (6.209; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). It is also learned: μάθον, “I have learned”, makes courage a lesson Hector was taught. See [[aidos]] and [[hector]].

**What someone will say.** κλέος is what other people say, and Hector twice imagines them saying it. A man who sees Andromache weeping as a slave in Argos will point her out: “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting of the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought around Ilios” (6.460–461). The fame lasts, and it is spoken over her captivity: “and it will be fresh sorrow for you” (6.462; see [[doulios]]). In his prayer for his son Hector imagines another speaker: “may someone say one day, ‘He is far better than his father’” (6.479; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]). Both are speeches of “someone” (τις), the form in which a hero hears his fame, or his shame, before it is spoken (see [[tis-speech]]). The same closing line, “so someone will say one day” (ὥς ποτέ τις ἐρέει), ends Agamemnon's imagined Trojan boast (4.182), Hector's picture of Andromache (6.462) and, in Book 7, his picture of the tomb of the champion he hopes to kill: “and my fame will never perish” (7.91).

**Song.** Helen speaks of the fame that suffering brings. Zeus has set an evil fate on her and Paris, “so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (ἀοίδιμοι, 6.357–358). She knows that she will be sung, and what the song will be (see [[song-and-fame]] and [[helen]]). Glaucus claims the other kind of report for his family: “many men know” its lineage (6.151; see [[genee]]).

**Famous allies.** The adjective κλειτός, “famous”, comes twice, in the standing phrase for Troy's allies. Hector calls on the “far-famed allies” (τηλεκλειτοί, 6.111), and Diomedes tells Glaucus that there are many Trojans and “famous allies” for him to kill (6.227). See [[famous]] and [[trojan-allies]].
