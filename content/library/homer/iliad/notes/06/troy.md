**The city from inside.** In Book 3 the poem looked out from the wall with Priam and Helen. In Book 6 it follows Hector through the city itself. He comes in by the Scaean gates and the oak, where the wives and daughters of the Trojans run to him for news (6.237–241; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). He goes on to Priam's palace with its polished colonnades and its fifty chambers for the king's sons (6.242–250; see [[priams-palace]]), and then to Paris' house, which stands with Priam's and Hector's own “on the height of the city” (6.317; see [[paris]]). Athena's temple is there too (ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ, 6.88, 6.297; see [[temple]]), and so is Pergamus, from which Paris runs down at the end (6.512; see [[pergamus]]). Hector goes to his own house and does not find Andromache; she has gone to “the great tower of Ilios” (6.386; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). He hurries back “the same way through the well-built streets” and crosses “the great city” to the gates (6.390–393).

**Its names.** The book calls the city “sacred Ilios” (Ἰλίου ἱρῆς, 6.96, 6.277; Ἴλιος ἱρή, 6.448; see [[hieros]]), “deep-soiled Troy” (Τροίῃ ἐριβώλακι, 6.315) and “the great city” (μέγα ἄστυ, 6.392). Its houses belong to its defenders: Hector named his son Scamandrius, but the Trojans called him Astyanax, “lord of the city”, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.402–403; see [[astyanax]]). Hector prays that the boy may one day “rule Ilios with might” (6.478), and tells Andromache that war is the concern “of all the men who were born in Ilios, and mine most of all” (Ἰλίῳ ἐγγεγάασιν, 6.492–493).

**Its end.** Andromache points to the one weak place, by the wild fig tree, “where most of all the city can be scaled and the wall is open to assault” (6.433–434; see [[the-fig-tree]]). Hector's answer foresees the fall: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish, and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (6.448–449). Agamemnon spoke the same two lines in Book 4, as a threat (4.164–165); Hector says them as a certainty that he must fight through (see [[a-day-will-come]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The book ends with the opposite hope, Hector's wish to set up the mixing bowl of freedom “when we have driven the well-greaved Achaeans out of Troy” (ἐκ Τροίης, 6.529; see [[mixing-bowl]]).
