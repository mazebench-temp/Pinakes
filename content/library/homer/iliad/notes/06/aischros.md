The family of αἶσχος, “disgrace”, marks the book's two kinds of men: those who fear what will be said of them, and the one who does not.

**Not to shame the fathers.** Hippolochus sent Glaucus to Troy with a charge, “always to be the best and to excel above the others, and not to shame the race of my fathers” (μηδὲ γένος πατέρων αἰσχυνέμεν, 6.208–209). αἰσχύνω is to disgrace, to make ugly; a son can disfigure a lineage (see [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[genee]]).

**Paris.** Hector finds Paris in his chamber: “Seeing him, Hector rebuked him with shaming words” (αἰσχροῖς ἐπέεσσι, 6.325). It is the line that introduced his first rebuke, in Book 3, when Paris shrank from Menelaus (3.38). Helen then wishes that she were the wife of a man “who felt the blame and the many reproaches (αἴσχεα) of men” (6.351), and at the end of the book Hector gives Paris the Trojans' verdict: “my heart grieves in my spirit when I hear shameful things (αἴσχεʼ) said of you by the Trojans, who have much toil for your sake” (6.524–525). He has begun with praise: no fair-minded man would dishonor Paris' work in battle, “since you are valiant” (6.521–522). The reproaches of Book 6 are what others say. Paris hears them from his brother and from his wife, and Helen, at least, thinks he does not feel them. See [[nemesis]], [[oneidos]], [[paris]] and [[praise-and-blame]].
