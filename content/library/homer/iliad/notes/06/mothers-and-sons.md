**Hecuba and Hector.** Helenus sends Hector to “your mother and mine” (6.87), and she comes to meet him with his sister Laodice. She “clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (6.253), in the verse with which Andromache will greet him at the gates (6.406):

{{quote:16309e16-6be9-56dc-94c2-577b41bb1cac}}

τέκνον, “my child”, is the word with which Thetis greets Achilles when he weeps by the sea (1.362). The mother guesses why her son has come, and offers what a mother can: wine to pour to Zeus and to drink, for strength (6.258–262). Hector refuses the wine and gives her an errand instead. She is to lead the old women to Athena with the finest of her robes (6.264–285), and she does, choosing the one that “lay beneath all the others” (6.288–296; see [[hecuba]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]). At their next meeting she will stand on the wall, bare her breast and beg him by it to come inside (22.79–89).

**Andromache and Astyanax.** The mother of the book's last scene is Andromache. The child shrinks from his father's helmet into the bosom of the nurse (6.467), and “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (6.471). Hector's prayer asks that the boy grow up to be a warrior, and ends with his mother: “may he bring back bloody spoils when he has killed his enemy, and may his mother's heart rejoice” (6.480–481). Then he puts the child in her arms, and “she took him to her fragrant bosom, laughing through her tears” (6.482–484). See [[andromache]], [[astyanax]] and [[meter]].

**A goddess's bosom.** Diomedes' story of Lycurgus has a divine mother too. Lycurgus drove the nurses of “raving Dionysus” down the Nysean mount, and the god, put to flight, “plunged beneath the wave of the sea, and Thetis took him to her bosom in his fear” (6.132–137). Hephaestus uses the same words of the day he was thrown from Olympus: Eurynome and Thetis “took me to their bosom” (18.395–398). In Homer Thetis is the goddess who receives the frightened young, as she comes up from the sea to her own son (1.357–361). See [[thetis]], [[dionysus]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]].

**Mothers named in births.** The book's genealogies name mothers too. A Naiad nymph bore twin sons to Bucolion, whose own mother had borne him in secret (6.21–26); Laodameia bore Sarpedon to Zeus, and Artemis killed her “in her anger” (6.198–205; see [[laodameia]] and [[sarpedon]]).
