---
summary: The epithet names Lycurgus as he strikes the nurses of Dionysus with an ox-goad, and returns to Hector at the end of his visit, in the verse that brings Andromache home to mourn him.
---

In Diomedes' story the warrior's epithet is given to a man attacking women: the nurses of Dionysus let their sacred wands fall to the ground “as man-slaying Lycurgus struck them with his ox-goad” (6.134–135; [[lycurgus]], [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). In Book 4 it named Ares (4.441); here it names a man who fights a god and is blinded for it (6.138–140).

Hector's own formula, Ἕκτορος ἀνδροφόνοιο, comes back as his wife reaches home: “And quickly she came to the well-appointed house of man-slaying Hector” (6.497–498; [[well-peopled]]). There she rouses lamentation among the handmaids, and they mourn Hector in his house while he is still alive (6.498–502; [[lament]], [[handmaids]], [[andromache]]). The epithet that first named him in Achilles' oath (1.242) names the man whose house is already mourning him ([[hector]]).
