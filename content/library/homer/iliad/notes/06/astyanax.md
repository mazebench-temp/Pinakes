Hector speaks of him before he is seen, among the household he goes home to see: “my infant son” (νήπιον υἱόν, 6.366). He comes to the gates at a handmaid's breast and is named twice, Scamandrius by his father and Astyanax by everyone else (6.399–403; see [[handmaids]]). Hector looks at him and smiles in silence (6.404; see [[smiling-at-him]]). To Andromache he is the reason Hector should stay: her husband has no pity “for your infant son” (6.408), and she begs him not to make “your child an orphan” (6.432; see [[andromache]]).

{{quote:700aad80-c2c0-5ca3-a91a-2109b9d4ce22}}

ταρβήσας, “afraid”, is a word of the battlefield, most often with a negative: a hero answers his enemy “unafraid” (οὐ ταρβήσας, 5.286, 11.384), as Hector will answer Achilles (20.430). The child is afraid of what makes his father a warrior, the bronze and the horsehair crest (λόφον ἱππιοχαίτην, found only here; see [[horsehair-crested]] and [[helmet]]). The helmet gives Hector his commonest epithet, κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]). He takes it off to hold his son and lays it on the ground, “all gleaming” (παμφανόωσαν, 6.473). The parents' laughter at 6.471 is the only time Hector laughs in the Iliad.

Then he prays that the boy may become “as I am, preeminent among the Trojans, as good in strength, and rule Ilios with might” (6.477–478), the rule that his name promises (ἄναξ, “lord”); and that someone may say of him, as he comes back from war with bloody spoils, “He is far better than his father”, “and may his mother's heart rejoice” (6.479–481; see [[bloody-spoils]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). εἴποι, “may someone say”, is a wish: the same formula with εἴπῃσι predicts what a stranger will say of Andromache as a slave (6.459). ἀνιόντα, “as he comes back”, agrees with no word in the sentence; the words are said of the boy.

He puts the child in his mother's arms (6.482–483). The poem does not say whether the gods heard. Andromache's laments in Books 22 and 24 foresee a different life for him, an orphan's, and a death thrown from the tower (22.484–507, 24.726–739).
