**The fate of every man.** When Hector has given the child back to his mother, he pities her, strokes her with his hand, and speaks to her for the last time in the poem:

{{quote:34de5dbc-2d29-51f0-ae6d-743235718a97}}

The first sentence is a comfort: no one will send him to Hades “beyond my portion” (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν; see [[aisa]]). The second is a rule, and the comfort in it is hard. μοῖρα here is the portion of life allotted to each man, and it is fixed ἐπὴν τὰ πρῶτα γένηται, “once he has been born”. Other speakers picture the allotment as a thread spun at birth. Hera says that Achilles will suffer whatever his portion spun for him with its thread when his mother bore him (20.127–128), and Hecuba says the same of Hector (24.209–210); in the Odyssey his portion and the Spinners (κλῶθες) spin it (*Odyssey* 7.196–198). Hector's οὐ κακὸν οὐδὲ μὲν ἐσθλόν, “no coward and no brave man”, sets courage aside: the brave man dies no sooner for his courage, and the coward no later for his fear. Achilles says the same in anger in Book 9, “the coward and the brave are held in a single honor; the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much” (9.319–320), and Sarpedon makes it the reason to fight, since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, “which no mortal can flee or escape” (12.326–327). See [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]], [[hector]] and [[andromache]].

**An evil fate.** Helen uses μόρος, “doom”, the other noun of the family. Zeus has set an “evil fate” (κακὸν μόρον) on her and on Alexander, “so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (6.357–358). The fate is given, and its purpose is the song (see [[song-and-fame]] and [[ate]]).

**Ill-fated.** Andromache names herself in the first lines of her plea: “you have no pity for your infant son or for me, ill-fated (ἄμμορον), who soon will be your widow” (6.407–409). ἄμμορος is “without a share”, the negative of μοῖρα: a woman without a portion. The shield of Achilles uses the word literally of the Bear, which alone “has no share” in the baths of Ocean (18.489). Andromache means her lot, which is to lose everyone she has (6.413–430; see [[chere]]). Helen calls herself ἄμμορος in her lament for Hector (24.773), and Andromache, lamenting him in Book 22, says that she and Hector were born to one portion, and that Eetion raised “an ill-fated daughter” (αἰνόμορον, 22.477–481). She also names Hector's death as his πότμος, “when you have met your fate” (6.412), the lot a man comes to.
