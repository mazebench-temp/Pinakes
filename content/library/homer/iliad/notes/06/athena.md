**Absent.** Athena went back to the house of Zeus with Hera at the end of Book 5 (5.907–909), and Book 6 opens on a battle “left to itself” (6.1). She does not come down again until Book 7, when she sees the Argives dying (7.17–20). In this book she does one thing: she refuses.

**The request.** With the Trojans driven back to their gates, Helenus sends Hector into the city. Hecuba is to gather the old women at Athena's temple on the citadel, lay the loveliest robe in her own hall on the goddess's knees, and promise her twelve cows “if she will pity the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children, so that she may hold the son of Tydeus back from sacred Ilios” (6.86–96; see [[helenus]], [[hecuba]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]). Hector gives the orders to his mother in almost the same words (6.269–278). The Trojans ask the goddess to restrain the man she has made “the strongest of the Achaeans” (6.98). In Book 5 she gave Diomedes might and daring, took the mist from his eyes, and rode beside him in his chariot against Ares (5.1–8, 5.121–132, 5.835–863; see [[diomedes]]).

**Her names.** The Trojans name her with every honor. She is “gray-eyed Athena” (6.88; see [[gray-eyed]]) and “Athena of the lovely hair” (6.92, 6.273, 6.303), an epithet that elsewhere belongs to Leto, Thetis, Hera, Helen and Briseis, and that Athena has only in this line (see [[lovely-haired]]). Theano calls her “Lady Athena, protector of the city, brilliant among goddesses” (6.305), and the narrator “the daughter of great Zeus” (6.304, 6.312; see [[protector-of-the-city]] and [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]). Hector, twice, calls her ἀγελείη, “driver of spoil” (6.269, 6.279), a title explained in antiquity either as the goddess who drives off plunder or as the leader of the host (see [[driver-of-spoil]]). A city in danger calls on the goddess of war by both her names, the one who keeps a city and the one who takes its spoils. To the women of Troy she is “the dread goddess” (6.380, 6.385; see [[propitiation]]).

The last line is built on the formula for a prayer that is heard, as after Diomedes' own prayer in Book 5: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121; so also 10.295, 23.771). Here the same beginning ends in refusal. ἀνένευε, a toss of the head upward and back, is the Greek gesture of “no”, the reverse of the nod with which Zeus grants Thetis' prayer (1.528; see [[theano]] and [[prayer]]). The narrator gives no reason, and the audience knows several. Athena hates Troy (4.20–24), and the last book names the cause, the Judgment of Paris (24.25–30); the man the women pray against is her favorite; and the robe they offer comes from the voyage on which Alexander brought Helen home (6.289–292; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The women do not know that they have been refused, and they go on praying (6.312).

## Outside Homer

In the *Little Iliad*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Odysseus and Diomedes carry the Palladium, the old image of Pallas, out of Troy; Apollodorus adds that the city could not be taken while it stood within the walls (*Epitome* 5.10–13). On Strabo's inference that Homer's image was seated, and on the comparison with the robe brought to Athena at the Athenian Panathenaea, see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]].
