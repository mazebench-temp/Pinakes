---
summary: Book 6 has no dawn of its own. Its one dawn is in Glaucus' story, where the king of Lycia entertains Bellerophon for nine days and asks to see the sign he brought when “the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared”.
passages:
  - 6.173-177 | The tenth dawn in Lycia
---

The whole of Book 6 belongs to the day that dawned in Book 2 (2.48–49), and that day does not end until Book 7. The book's only dawn is in a story of the heroic past:

{{quote:7e23c267-3ac1-527b-aaaf-ec8ef8ee199d}}

The king of Lycia does what a host should, and honors his guest before he asks his business. Nestor, too, waits until his guests have eaten before he asks who they are (Od. 3.69–70; see [[xenia]] and [[bellerophon]]). But nine days of feasting and nine oxen are a lavish welcome, and the count makes the tenth day the day of the reckoning, when the host reads the “evil sign” his guest has carried (6.178; see [[writing]]).

Nine days and then a tenth is one of Homer's usual measures of time. For nine days Apollo's arrows fell on the army, and on the tenth Achilles called the assembly (1.53–54; see [[numbers-and-counting]]). The line returns at the end of the poem with another epithet, “light-bringing” in place of “rosy-fingered”: for nine days the Trojans gathered wood, and when the tenth dawn appeared they laid Hector on the pyre (ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ δεκάτη ἐφάνη φαεσίμβροτος ἠώς, 24.784–787).
