Priam's married sons and daughters live with their husbands and wives in his palace (6.244–250), and Andromache, “richly dowered”, has become everything to Hector that her lost family was (6.394, 6.429–430).

**The king's daughter.** When the king of Lycia knew that his guest was “the noble offspring of a god”, “he kept him there, and gave him his daughter, and gave him half of all his royal honor”, and the Lycians marked out for him a domain of orchard and plowland (6.191–195). The same verse tells how Cisseus kept his grandson Iphidamas in Thrace and gave him his daughter (αὐτοῦ μιν κατέρυκε, δίδου δʼ ὅ γε θυγατέρα ἥν, 6.192 = 11.226; see [[cisseus]]), and Tydeus, an exile, settled in Argos, married a daughter of Adrastus and had a rich house with plowland enough (14.119–122). A stranger who proves himself is kept in the land by a marriage to the king's daughter. Glaucus' story begins with one son-in-law of the king, Proetus, and ends with another, Bellerophon (6.170, 6.177; see [[in-laws]], [[proetus]] and [[bellerophon]]).

**Priam's house.** Priam's sons sleep in fifty chambers “beside their wedded wives” (παρὰ μνηστῇς ἀλόχοισι, 6.246), and his sons-in-law across the courtyard in twelve, “beside their modest wives” (παρʼ αἰδοίῃς ἀλόχοισιν, 6.250). μνηστή, “wooed”, is from μνάομαι, “woo”: a wife sought and won with gifts. The married daughters live in their father's house with their husbands, as the married sons do with their wives (see [[priams-palace]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**Andromache.** She is ἄλοχος πολύδωρος, “richly dowered” (6.394). The word may mean the wife who brought many gifts to her husband's house, as the translation takes it, or the wife won with many gifts: Hector led her from Eetion's house “when he had given countless bride-gifts” (ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα, 22.470–472). Her father, her mother and her seven brothers are dead, and the marriage has taken their place: “Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother, and brother, and you are my husband in his prime” (6.429–430; see [[andromache]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Hector's picture of her as a slave in Argos, led away by an Achaean, is the reverse of a wedded wife's lot (6.454–458; see [[captive-women]]).
