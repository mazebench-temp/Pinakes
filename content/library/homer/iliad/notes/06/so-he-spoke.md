summary: The closing verses move Hector through the city: twice “Hector of the gleaming helmet went away”, once he says nothing to Paris, and at the gates each of his speeches ends in a gesture. A prayer closes with a refusal, and a dual closes the speeches of Glaucus and Diomedes together.
grc: ["ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντε", "ἦ ῥα γυνὴ ταμίη"]
en: [So the two spoke, The housekeeper spoke]
except: [6.441]
---

The closings of Book 6: 6.51, 61, 72, 102, 116, 166, 212, 232, 286, 311, 342, 369, 390, 466, 482, 494.

**Departures.** “So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet went away” (ὣς ἄρα φωνήσας ἀπέβη κορυθαίολος Ἕκτωρ) closes his exhortation at the gates and his answer to Helen (6.116, 6.369). The verse takes him out of the battle and out of Paris' house, two stages of his errand (see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). It returns once more in the poem, when Hector leaves the Trojans to put on Achilles' armor (17.188). The third ὣς ἄρα φωνήσας of the book sends him back to the war: “So he spoke, and shining Hector took up his helmet” (6.494; see [[helmet]]).

**Persuaded, turned, obeyed.** Adrastus' plea nearly succeeds, “So he spoke, and he began to win over the heart in his breast” (6.51). Agamemnon's rebuke succeeds: “So saying, the hero turned his brother's mind” (6.61; see [[supplication]]). The verse returns with παρέπεισεν, “persuaded”, in place of ἔτρεψεν, when Agamemnon keeps Menelaus from fighting Hector in Book 7 (7.120), and again when Paris persuades Hector (13.788). Helenus is obeyed: “So he spoke, and Hector did not disobey his brother” (6.102; see [[litotes]]).

**A refusal.** “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). The closing of a heard prayer, “and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121), has its second half reversed (see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**Silence.** Paris promises to arm and follow, and “Hector of the gleaming helmet said nothing to him” (6.342), the verse of his silence to the wounded Sarpedon in Book 5 (5.689). Helen speaks instead (6.343).

**Gestures.** At the gates each of Hector's speeches ends in an act: “So saying, shining Hector reached out for his son” (6.466), “So saying, he placed in the arms of his dear wife his son” (6.482–483), and the helmet taken up again (6.494). See [[hector-and-andromache]].

**Two speakers, and a story.** A dual closes two speeches at once: “So the two spoke, and leapt down from their chariots” (ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντε, 6.232; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). Inside Glaucus' story the formula closes Anteia's lie, “So she spoke, and anger seized the lord at what he heard” (6.166; see [[anteia]]).

**The housekeeper.** “The housekeeper spoke” (ἦ ῥα γυνὴ ταμίη, 6.390): ἦ is the old imperfect of ἠμί, “say”, as in ἦ καί, “he spoke, and” (see [[handmaids]]). At 6.441 ἦ καί is something else, the particle of assurance and “too”: “All these things are on my mind too, lady” (ἦ καὶ ἐμοὶ τάδε πάντα μέλει). That line is not counted here.
