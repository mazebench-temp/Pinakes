**Andromache's future.** Hector says that no sorrow troubles him so much as hers, “when one of the bronze-armored Achaeans leads you away in tears, taking away your day of freedom” (6.454–455). Then he describes the life that follows:

{{quote:11b4ae31-3183-5861-a2bf-ee7f8f6330a4}}

The loom and the water are the captive's work, as Agamemnon told Chryses his daughter would work at the loom in Argos and share his bed (1.29–31). πρὸς ἄλλης, “at another woman's bidding”, puts her under a mistress; ἀεκαζομένη, “much against your will”, is from the root of ἀέκων, “unwilling”, the word for Briseis as she was led away (ἀέκουσα, 1.348). Hector's picture ends with what others will say of her and what he cannot bear to hear, her cries as she is dragged away (6.459–465; see [[slavery-and-freedom]], [[doulios]] and [[may-the-earth-gape]]). The Iliad does not tell what happened to her; later poets did (see [[euripides-andromache]] and [[andromache]]).

**A captive mother.** Andromache already belongs to the story of captive women. When Achilles sacked Thebe, her mother, “who was queen beneath wooded Placus”, was brought to the Achaean camp “with the rest of the possessions” and released for a boundless ransom (6.425–427). It was the same raid in which Chryseis was taken and given to Agamemnon (1.366–369; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). The queen was ransomed and went home to die in her father's halls (6.428); Chryseis was kept as a prize, and her father's ransom was refused (1.12–32).

**Wives and children.** The city's prayer to Athena names the people who will be the spoils if Troy falls: “the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (6.95, 6.276, 6.310). The Trojan women who run to Hector at the gate are asking after the men who stand between them and captivity (6.238–241; see [[trojan-women]]). Agamemnon had promised his army exactly this: “we will lead away their dear wives and little children in our ships” (4.238–239).
