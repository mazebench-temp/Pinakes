summary: The only dawn in Book 6 rises in Glaucus' story: “But when the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared”, after nine days of feasting, the king of Lycia asks Bellerophon for the sign he has brought (6.175). The verse is the counting form of the dawn formula.
grc: ["δεκάτη ἐφάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠὼς"]
en: [the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared]
---

ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ δεκάτη ἐφάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, “But when the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared” (6.175), joins the counting opening of “the twelfth dawn” of Book 1, ἀλλʼ ὅτε δή … δυωδεκάτη γένετʼ ἠώς (1.493), to the verb and the epithet of the whole-verse formula, φάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς (1.477; see [[dawn-line]], [[rosy-fingered]] and [[eos]]). It closes a count of nine: “nine days he entertained him and sacrificed nine oxen” (6.174). The Iliad counts nine days and a tenth elsewhere. The plague lasts nine days, and on the tenth Achilles calls the assembly (1.53–54). For nine days the Trojans gather wood for Hector's pyre, and the last book uses the same verse, with another epithet, for the dawn of his burning: ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ δεκάτη ἐφάνη φαεσίμβροτος ἠώς, “but when the tenth dawn appeared, bringing light to mortals” (24.784–786). The narrative of Book 6 has no dawn of its own. The whole book belongs to the day that began in Book 2 (2.48–49; see [[bellerophon]] and [[writing]]).
