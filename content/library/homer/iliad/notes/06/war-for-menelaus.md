---
summary: In Book 6 Menelaus is ready to spare a Trojan for ransom, and Agamemnon turns him from it by recalling how the Trojans treated him in his house; the debt is to be paid by all of them, down to the child in the womb (6.37–65).
passages:
  - 6.37-65 | Adrastus begs for his life
---

Menelaus takes Adrastus alive when his horses wreck his chariot. Adrastus clasps his knees and offers a boundless ransom from his wealthy father, and Menelaus is persuaded and about to send him to the ships (6.37–53; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]], [[supplication]] and [[apoina]]). Agamemnon comes running:

{{quote:d8884ba6-7c1c-5274-bcc8-360256525a95}}

“Were you so very well treated in your house by the Trojans?” The question names the wrong for which the war is fought: the guest who came to Menelaus' house and left with his wife and possessions (3.351–354, 13.624–627; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]] and [[xenia]]). The injured husband would take a ransom, as a man might for any prisoner. The brother who leads the army will take no payment but the Trojans themselves. He turns his brother's mind, “urging what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών), and stabs the suppliant himself (6.61–65). Whether the narrator approves, the Trojans having broken the oaths, or means only that the advice agreed with what was fated, is debated (see the translation's note on 6.62). Agamemnon acts on what he said over his wounded brother in Book 4, that the oath-breakers would pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.161–162). In Book 11 he refuses two more suppliants who beg in Adrastus' words, the sons of Antimachus, because their father took Alexander's gold, would not let Helen be given back to Menelaus, and urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came to Troy as an envoy (11.122–147; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

In Troy the same debt is counted from the other side. Hector tells Paris that it is for his sake that the war blazes around the city (6.328–329), and Helen that Hector bears the toil “for my sake, dog that I am, and for Alexander's blind folly” (6.355–356; see [[ate]]).
