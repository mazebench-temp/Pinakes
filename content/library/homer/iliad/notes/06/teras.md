summary: Proetus sends Bellerophon to Lycia with “grim signs” scratched on a folded tablet, the only thing like writing in Homer, and the Lycian king reads “the evil sign of his son-in-law”. Bellerophon then kills the Chimaera “obeying the portents of the gods”, in the half-line used of Tydeus at Thebes.
passages:
  - 6.166-170 | Proetus sends Bellerophon to Lycia
  - 6.175-183 | The sign and the Chimaera
---

**Signs made by a man.** Proetus will not kill Bellerophon himself, “but he sent him to Lycia, and gave him grim signs, scratching on a folded tablet many things to destroy a man's life, and told him to show them to his own father-in-law, so that he would perish” (6.168–170). σήματα λυγρά, “grim signs”, are the only thing in Homer that looks like writing. γράφω, “scratch”, is elsewhere the verb of a spear that grazes a bone (17.599) or an arrow that scratches the skin (ἐπέγραψε, 4.139), and in Book 7 Ajax knows his lot by the mark he scratched on it, a sign that only he could read (ἐπιγράψας, σῆμα, 7.187–189). Whether Proetus' signs were letters, pictures or marks of some other kind cannot be decided from the text. See [[writing]] and [[bellerophons-letter]].

**The sign read.** The Lycian king entertains his guest for nine days, “but when the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared, then he questioned him and asked to see the sign (σῆμα ἰδέσθαι) that he was bringing from his son-in-law Proetus. And when he had received the evil sign (σῆμα κακόν) of his son-in-law” (6.175–178), he sets Bellerophon to his tasks. The word is the one used for signs from the gods, such as the serpent at Aulis, “a great sign” (2.308), and like them this one has a meaning that the one who receives it must see. Here a man has made the sign, and its bearer carries his own death to the one who reads it. See [[bellerophon-in-lycia]] and [[in-laws]].

**Portents obeyed.** The first task is the Chimaera, and Bellerophon kills her “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 6.183). The half-line is the one Agamemnon used of Tydeus sparing Maeon after the Theban ambush (4.398). In both stories the gods' signs guide a hero through dangers set for him by his hosts, and in neither does the Iliad say what the portents were (see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Later poets gave Bellerophon the winged horse Pegasus for the fight (Hesiod, *Theogony* 319–325), tamed with a golden bridle that Athena gave him in a dream (Pindar, *Olympian* 13.63–92; see [[chimaera]]). At the end of the tasks the king reads a sign of another kind in the man himself: “when the king knew that he was the noble offspring of a god” (6.191), he kept him and gave him his daughter.
