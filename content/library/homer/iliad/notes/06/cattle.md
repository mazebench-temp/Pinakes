**The vow.** Helenus tells Hector that the Trojan women are to promise Athena “twelve cows … yearlings, never touched by the goad”, if she will pity the city (6.93–94). The words are repeated by Hector (6.274–275) and in Theano's prayer (6.308–309). ἤνις is explained as “a year old” and ἠκέστας as “ungoaded”; ἠκέστας occurs only in these lines, and its sense is uncertain (see the translation's note on 6.94). An animal that has never worked is fit for a god. Diomedes, praying to Athena on the night raid, vows her a yearling heifer, “unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke”, with gold on her horns (10.292–294), and Nestor makes the same vow in the same lines in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 3.382–384); both times the goddess heard the prayer (10.295; *Odyssey* 3.385). The Trojans' cows are never sacrificed, for Athena refuses their prayer (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[sacrifice]]).

**The host's oxen.** The king of Lycia entertains Bellerophon for nine days and sacrifices nine oxen before he asks what message his guest has brought (6.173–177; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]] and [[xenia]]).

**The ox-goad.** Lycurgus strikes the nurses of Dionysus with a βουπλήξ, a tool named from the ox (βοῦς) and from striking (πλήσσω): an ox-goad, or an axe for felling oxen (6.135; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and the translation's note on 6.134–135).

**The price of armor.**

{{quote:db540de2-4ce8-55df-a865-408804c2074a}}

ἑκατόμβοια are things “worth a hundred oxen”, like the golden tassels of the aegis (ἑκατόμβοιος, 2.449) and the price Achilles got for Lycaon when he sold him on Lemnos (21.78–79), and ἐννεάβοια things “worth nine”; ἐννεάβοιος occurs only here. Cattle are the Homeric measure of value, whether or not any cattle change hands. At the funeral games of Patroclus a tripod is valued at twelve oxen and a woman skilled in handiwork at four (23.702–705), and Laertes paid twenty oxen for Eurycleia (*Odyssey* 1.430–431). See [[glaucus-and-diomedes]].

**Among the herds.** Andromache's seven brothers were killed on one day by Achilles “among their shambling cattle and white sheep” (βουσὶν ἐπʼ εἰλιπόδεσσι καὶ ἀργεννῇς ὀΐεσσι, 6.423–424; see [[sack-of-thebe]] and [[sheep]]). εἰλίπους, “shambling”, describes the rolling gait of cattle (see [[shambling]]). The sons of a king were keeping the herds when the raider came, as Aeneas was when Achilles came on his cattle on Ida and chased him down the mountain (20.89–93, 20.188–190).
