**The gods withdrawn.** Book 5 ended with Hera and Athena going home to the house of Zeus, having stopped Ares from his killing (5.907–909). Book 6 begins: “Now the dread din of battle between Trojans and Achaeans was left to itself” (6.1). οἰώθη, “was left alone”, answers Aphrodite's complaint in Book 5 that it was “no longer the dread din of battle between Trojans and Achaeans”, since the Danaans were fighting the immortals too (5.379–380). Now the war belongs to men again. When the Trojans rally, the Argives think that “one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky to bring help to the Trojans” (6.108–109): a god is imagined, and none has come. See [[divine-intervention]].

**Not against the gods.** Diomedes, who wounded Aphrodite and Ares earlier the same day, meets a stranger and will not fight him if he is a god (6.128–129). His question suggests that the sight Athena gave him in Book 5, to tell god from man (5.127–128), is his no longer. He gives his reason in a story: Lycurgus son of Dryas, who drove Dionysus into the sea, did not live long:

{{quote:2e321903-afc1-51fb-90b4-bc26882d6a88}}

The difference between the two kinds is put in their epithets. The gods are θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, “the gods who live at ease” (6.138); men are βροτοί, “who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142). See [[who-fights-the-gods]], [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]], [[who-live-at-ease]] and [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]].

**Favor and hatred.** Glaucus answers with the leaves, the image of mortal generations (6.146–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]), and with a story in which the gods do everything. They gave Bellerophon beauty and manhood (6.156), sent him to Lycia “under the blameless escort of the gods” (6.171), and let him kill the Chimaera “obeying the portents of the gods” (6.183), and the Lycian king knew him for “the noble offspring of a god” (6.191). Zeus lay with his daughter Laodameia, who bore Sarpedon (6.198–199). Then, with no reason given, the gods turned:

{{quote:296fd6e7-87eb-5117-9a97-cecc0cf42708}}

The story shows the whole range of divine dealing with one family: gifts, guidance, love, and then hatred, a son killed by Ares and a daughter by Artemis. The poem does not explain the change (see [[bellerophon]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). A few lines later the narrator says that Zeus took away Glaucus' wits (6.234; see [[phrenes]]).

**In the city.** Inside Troy the gods are prayed to and do not answer. Hector will not pour wine to Zeus with hands spattered with blood (6.266–268). The women lay their finest robe on Athena's knees and pray for the city, “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The Trojans explain their suffering by the gods: Paris is the bane the Olympian raised for Troy (6.282), “the gods ordained these evils” (6.349), Zeus has set an evil fate on Helen and Paris (6.357), and Hector does not know whether “the gods will now bring me down at the hands of the Achaeans” (6.368). In Andromache's story the gods appear at the edges of a human life: the mountain nymphs planted elms around her father's tomb, and Artemis struck down her mother (6.419–420, 6.428; see [[nymphs]] and [[artemis]]).
