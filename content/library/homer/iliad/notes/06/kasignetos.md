summary: Book 6 is full of brothers. Hector obeys his brother Helenus; Agamemnon turns his brother's mind against the suppliant; the Trojan women ask after their brothers; Andromache's seven brothers died on one day, and Hector is now her brother; Hector foresees his own brothers falling; and Paris greets him at the end with “Dear brother”.

**Brothers who advise.** Two pairs of brothers open the book, and in each one brother turns the other. Agamemnon runs up as Menelaus is about to spare Adrastus, and “the hero turned his brother's mind” (ἀδελφειοῦ φρένας, 6.61): the elder decides for the younger, as he will again in Book 7, in the same words, when he keeps Menelaus from facing Hector (7.120–121). Then Helenus, Priam's son and the best of the bird-seers, tells Hector what to do, and “Hector did not disobey his brother” (6.102). Helenus sends him to “your mother and mine” (6.87): they are full brothers, sons of Hecuba. See [[helenus]] and [[agamemnon]].

**Brothers asked after.** At the Scaean gates the Trojan women ask Hector after “their sons and brothers (κασιγνήτους) and kinsmen (ἔτας) and husbands” (6.239). ἔται are the men of one's own family and people in a wider sense; κασίγνητοί τε ἔται τε, “brothers and kinsmen”, are the ones who bury a dead man (16.456 = 16.674). Hecuba uses the word of all the city's men when she tells Hector that he is weary “from defending your kinsmen” (ἔτῃσι, 6.262).

**Seven brothers.** Andromache had seven brothers, and they died together:

{{quote:2ab282c3-c19f-5c9b-a3ba-c96b517a38f7}}

They were killed at their work, as herdsmen, like Aeneas' near escape from Achilles among his cattle on Ida (20.89–92) and like the two sons of Priam whom Achilles caught among the sheep (11.104–106). Her list of the dead ends with the living: “Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother, and brother (κασίγνητος), and you are my husband in his prime” (6.429–430; see [[andromache]], [[sack-of-thebe]] and [[posis]]).

**Hector's brothers.** When Hector foresees the fall of Troy, he tells Andromache that the sorrow of the Trojans, of Hecuba and of Priam troubles him less than hers, “nor of my brothers, who, many and brave, may fall in the dust at the hands of hostile men” (6.450–453). The Iliad will show many of them die. See [[sons-of-priam]] and [[a-day-will-come]].

**Dear brother.** The book ends with the brother Hector rebuked. Paris, running down from Pergamus, “came upon brilliant Hector, his brother” (ἀδελφεόν, 6.515), and greets him with an affectionate word, ἠθεῖε, “Dear brother” (6.518; see [[dear-brother]]). The same greeting, in almost the same words, is given to Hector once more: Athena, in the likeness of his favorite brother Deiphobus, stops him at the end of his flight from Achilles with “Dear brother, surely swift Achilles is hard on you” (ἠθεῖʼ ἦ μάλα δή σε, 22.229), and he stands and dies. See [[paris]] and [[hector]].
