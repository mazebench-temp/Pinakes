**Eetion's mound.** Achilles killed Eetion but did not strip him, “but he burned him with his intricate armor and heaped a mound over him” (ἠδʼ ἐπὶ σῆμʼ ἔχεεν, 6.418–419; see [[cremation]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). χέω, “pour”, is the verb for heaping up the earth of a grave, and the same words close the poem: the Trojans laid Hector's bones in a grave “and quickly heaped a mound” (σῆμʼ ἔχεαν, 24.799). Around Eetion's mound grew trees:

{{quote:ea9b6b13-cf8a-5ff4-a559-e4af9444b4be}}

The nymphs of the mountain honor the king of the city under Mount Placus. The enemy who killed him gave him his burial, and goddesses gave the grave its trees (see [[eetion]], [[nymphs]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]).

**The heaped earth.** Hector ends his answer to Andromache with a wish for his own death: “But may I be dead, and the heaped earth cover me” (χυτὴ κατὰ γαῖα καλύπτοι, 6.464). χυτὴ γαῖα, “heaped earth”, is the grave mound, as when Diomedes says that his father lies at Thebes, “whom the heaped earth covers” (14.114), and when Nestor says that Aegisthus, had Menelaus found him alive, would not even have had the heaped earth poured over him (Od. 3.256–258; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]).

**Signs, not tombs.** At 6.176 and 6.178 σῆμα is the sign that Bellerophon carried to Lycia (see [[writing]]), and those lines are not counted here. It is the same word. A mound is a σῆμα because it is a sign, marking where a man lies (see [[tomb]]).
