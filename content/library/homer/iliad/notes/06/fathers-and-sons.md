summary: Book 6 is built on fathers and sons. Glaucus answers Diomedes with his lineage and his father's charge, and Diomedes, who cannot remember his own father, recognizes his grandfather's guest-friend; Hector, who fights for his father's fame, takes off his helmet so that his son will know him, and prays that the boy may be better than he.

**Lineage.** Diomedes asks Glaucus who he is, and Glaucus names his fathers: Sisyphus son of Aeolus, Glaucus, Bellerophon, Hippolochus, and himself (6.152–206; see [[genee]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The list is not only of glory. Bellerophon, to whom the gods had given beauty and manhood, ended hated by them, wandering alone, and he lost two of his three children, Isander to Ares and Laodameia to Artemis (6.200–205). The son who survived sent his own son to Troy with a charge: “always to be the best and to excel above the others, and not to shame the race of my fathers” (6.208–209; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]).

**The grandfathers' bond.** Diomedes knows his own family the same way, by report and by what was handed down. He does not remember Tydeus, “since I was still little when he left me” (6.222–223), but he knows that his grandfather Oeneus entertained Bellerophon for twenty days, and he still has the gold cup Bellerophon gave (6.216–221). The bond the grandfathers made binds the grandsons, and Diomedes plants his spear in the earth (6.213; see [[xenia]] and [[oeneus]]).

**A father's fame.** Hector explains his courage to Andromache by his father: he has learned to fight among the foremost, “winning great fame for my father and for myself” (6.444–446; see [[kleos]]). Then he turns to his son.

The child does not know his father in the helmet. The helmet that makes Hector terrible in battle frightens his son, and he has to take it off and lay it on the ground before he can kiss the boy and dandle him in his arms (6.474; see [[helmet]] and [[horsehair-crested]]). Then he prays that the boy may become like him and more: “may someone say one day, ‘He is far better than his father’” (6.479; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]). The wish runs the other way from Hippolochus' charge. Glaucus' father asked that the son not fall below the fathers; Hector asks that his son rise above him.

**Other fathers.** The book counts fathers among the dead and the captured. Adrastus offers the ransom that his wealthy father would pay if he heard his son was alive (6.46–50; see [[apoina]]). Bucolion, the eldest son of Laomedon, had twin sons by a nymph, and Euryalus kills them both (6.21–28; see [[bucolion]]). Achilles killed Andromache's father Eetion, and her seven brothers “on a single day” (6.414–424; see [[eetion]]). And Hector names Paris as “a great bane to the Trojans and to great-hearted Priam and his sons” (6.282–283; see [[sons-of-priam]]).
