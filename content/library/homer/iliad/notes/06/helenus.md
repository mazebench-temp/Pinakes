summary: Helenus stops the Trojan flight. He tells Aeneas and Hector to hold the army before the gates, and sends Hector into the city: Hecuba is to lay a robe on Athena's knees and promise her twelve cows, if she will keep Diomedes from Ilios (6.73–101).

passages:
  - 6.73-101 | Helenus sends Hector to Troy
---

**The rescue.** The Trojans are about to go up into Ilios “beaten by their own cowardice”, and would have, the narrator says, had not Helenus stood beside Aeneas and Hector and spoken (6.73–76; see [[would-have-had-not]]). He is introduced by his art, as the best of the bird-seers, but he reads no birds here. His speech has two parts, a soldier's counsel and a ritual one.

{{quote:97c787a1-fa13-587e-af7f-814c1078a9ec}}

**The line.** He speaks to the two men on whom the war rests: “on you most of all rests the toil of the Trojans and the Lycians”, for they are “the best in every undertaking, both in fighting and in counsel” (6.77–79; see [[aeneas]] and [[hector]]). They are to go through the army and hold it before the gates, before the men “fall into the arms of their women in flight, and become a joy to their enemies” (6.81–82). The rest will stand and fight, “hard pressed though we are; for necessity compels us” (6.85).

**The robe.** Then Hector alone is sent into the city with a message for “your mother and mine” (6.86–87). Hecuba is to gather the old women at Athena's temple on the height of the city, open its doors with the key, lay on the goddess's knees the robe that is loveliest and largest in her hall and dearest to herself, and promise twelve yearling cows, if Athena will pity the city and the Trojans' wives and children and hold Diomedes back from Ilios (6.87–97; see [[hecuba]], [[temple]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]). Hector gives the instructions to his mother almost word for word (6.269–278; see [[message-repeated]]), and the women carry them out (6.286–311).

{{quote:2077b143-18a4-50f7-b542-bb34d47335cd}}

**Diomedes and Achilles.** Helenus measures Diomedes by the absent Achilles, “who they say was born of a goddess” (6.100): the Trojans never feared even him like this. μαίνεται, “rages” (6.101), is the verb of a warrior possessed by the fury of battle (see [[menos]] and [[diomedes]]). The line that describes him, ἄγριον αἰχμητὴν κρατερὸν μήστωρα φόβοιο, “that savage spearman, mighty master of rout” (6.97), returns in Hector's message (6.278; see [[master-of-rout]]).

**Obeyed.** “Hector did not disobey his brother” (6.102). He does both things. He rallies the army in the verses with which he rallied it in Book 5 (6.103–106 = 5.494–497), and he goes to the city (6.116–118). The seer's counsel is followed to the letter, and it fails: when Theano asks Athena to break Diomedes' spear, the goddess shakes her head (6.311; see [[athena]]). What the errand does is bring Hector home, to his mother, to Paris and Helen, and to his wife and son (see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). The poem does not say why the best fighter of the Trojans, and not a herald or Helenus himself, is sent into the city.
