summary: Zeus does not act in the battle of Book 6, but its stories and prayers turn to him. In the tales of Lycurgus and Bellerophon he blinds the man who attacked Dionysus, gives Proetus his scepter and fathers Sarpedon; in Troy, Hector will not pour wine to him with bloody hands, and prays to him that his son may be better than his father.

**In the stories.** The two tales told on the battlefield give Zeus three acts. When Lycurgus attacked the nurses of Dionysus, “the son of Cronus made him blind” (6.139; see [[lycurgus]] and [[dionysus]]). In Glaucus' story Proetus was the mightier man, “for Zeus had made them subject to his scepter” (6.159; see [[proetus]]). So the translation takes a much-debated sentence; others make Bellerophon the one whom Zeus had made subject to Proetus. Either way the king's power comes from Zeus, as Nestor told Achilles (1.279) and Odysseus told the army (2.205–206; see [[scepter]]). And Zeus lay with Bellerophon's daughter Laodameia, who bore “godlike Sarpedon, armed in bronze” (6.198–199; see [[laodameia]] and [[sarpedon]]). This is the only place in Homer where Sarpedon's mother is named. The son whom Zeus kept from death in Book 5 (5.662) is Glaucus' cousin.

The narrator gives Zeus a fourth act at the end of the meeting: “Zeus, son of Cronus, took away Glaucus' wits” (6.234), and he gave gold armor for bronze. Agamemnon uses the same words of his own delusion (φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, 19.137), and Achilles says nearly the same of Agamemnon (φρένας εἵλετο, 9.377). Whether the narrator is joking at Glaucus' expense, judging a bad bargain, or pointing to his generosity is debated (see [[glaucus]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]).

**In Troy.** Hecuba supposes that his heart has sent him “to come and lift up your hands to Zeus from the height of the city” (6.256–257), and offers him wine to pour to “father Zeus and the other immortals” before he drinks (6.259). He refuses both:

{{quote:8ecd2a60-af19-5ef9-b989-e85409430d03}}

Hector used to burn the thighs of oxen for Zeus on the peaks of Ida and on the height of the city (22.170–172). A man must be clean to pray. Achilles washes his hands and the cup before he pours wine to Zeus (16.228–232), and so does Priam before he sets out for Achilles' hut (24.302–307; see [[hand-washing]], [[libation]] and [[purification]]). In his anger at Paris, Hector names Zeus as the one who raised him: “the Olympian has raised him as a great bane to the Trojans” (6.282; see [[paris]]). Helen, too, makes Zeus the author of her fate and Paris', and gives it a purpose: “Zeus has set an evil fate, so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (6.357–358; see [[helen]]). The Odyssey says the same of the whole war: the gods spun destruction for men so that there might be song for those to come (Od. 8.579–580).

**The prayer for his son.** Hector takes off his helmet, kisses the child and dandles him, and prays “to Zeus and the other gods”:

{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

The narrator does not say whether Zeus heard. He nodded to Thetis (1.528), and he granted half of Achilles' prayer for Patroclus (16.249–252). Of Hector's prayer the poem says nothing. The audience knows what Andromache will fear in the last book, that some Achaean will hurl the boy from the tower (24.734–735; see [[astyanax]] and [[better-than-our-fathers]]). Earlier in this book Athena shook her head at the Trojan women (6.311).

**The last word.** The book ends with Zeus. Hector tells Paris that they will make things right later, “if ever Zeus grants us, for the everlasting gods of heaven, to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls, when we have driven the well-greaved Achaeans out of Troy” (6.526–529; see [[mixing-bowl]]).
