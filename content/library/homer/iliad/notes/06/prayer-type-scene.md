summary: The women's prayer to Athena is the book's full prayer scene, planned by Helenus, repeated by Hector and performed by Theano, with a gift and a vow in place of the claim and a refusal for an answer (6.86–98, 6.269–278, 6.297–311). Hector's prayer for his son has an invocation and a wish, and no answer at all (6.475–481).

**Planned, repeated, performed.** The prayer is told three times. Helenus lays it out, with the place, the gift, the vow and the request (6.86–98). Hector passes it on to his mother in the same words (6.269–278; see [[message-repeated]]). Then it is performed, with the priestess speaking for the women (6.297–311; see [[theano]]).

**The parts.**

1. **Setting and gesture.** The temple is opened, and “with a loud cry they all lifted up their hands to Athena” (6.301). The gift comes before the words: Theano lays the robe on the knees of the image (6.302–303; see [[robe]] and [[temple]]).
2. **Invocation.** “Lady Athena, protector of the city, brilliant among goddesses” (6.305). There is no “Hear me” (see [[hear-me]]).
3. **Claim.** There is none. Chryses reminded Apollo of the temple he had roofed (1.39–41), and Diomedes reminded Athena of her favor to his father (5.116–117). The Trojans have no past service to recall. They offer a gift now and a sacrifice to come: “so that we may now at once sacrifice twelve cows to you in your temple … if you will pity the city” (6.308–310). The vow is conditional (αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃς, “if you will pity”), a bargain that the goddess can decline (see [[sacrifice]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
4. **Request.** Helenus had asked that she hold Diomedes back from Ilios (6.96). Theano asks that she break his spear and let him fall headlong before the Scaean gates (6.306–307).
5. **Answer.** “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). The verse is the answer formula of 5.121 with its ending reversed (see [[athena]] and [[so-he-spoke]]).

**Hector's prayer.** A second prayer, at the gates, is a scene with most of its parts missing. The gesture is replaced by the child: Hector has taken off his helmet, kissed his son and dandled him in his arms (6.472–474; see [[helmet]]). “He spoke in prayer to Zeus and the other gods” (6.475), with an invocation, “Zeus and you other gods”, and at once the wish: δότε, “grant” (6.476). He makes no claim and promises nothing. The narrator gives no answer, and the next verse has Hector putting the boy in his mother's arms (6.482–483; see [[prayer]] and [[astyanax]]). The prayer that is refused has a reply; the prayer for the boy has none.
