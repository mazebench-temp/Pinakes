summary: Adrastus, thrown from his chariot at Menelaus' feet, clasps his knees and begs to be taken alive for ransom; Menelaus is persuaded, but Agamemnon kills the suppliant himself (6.37–65). It is the first plea for life on the battlefield of the Iliad, and like every other it fails.

**The suppliant.** A suppliant usually comes to the man he begs. Adrastus is brought by his horses. They bolt, break the chariot on a tamarisk branch, and throw him out “face down in the dust, on his mouth”, with Menelaus standing over him holding his long-shadowed spear (6.38–44; see [[chariot]]). “Then Adrastus clasped his knees and begged him” (λαβὼν ἐλίσσετο γούνων, 6.45; see [[gounata]]). What he offers is a ransom from his father's treasures, not a claim on the man he holds (6.46–50; see [[apoina]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]).

**Persuaded.** “So he spoke, and he began to win over the heart in his breast” (6.51). ἔπειθε, the imperfect, is a persuasion under way, and Menelaus is about to give Adrastus to his attendant to lead down to the ships (6.52–53). The one who breaks it off has not been touched. Agamemnon comes running and rebukes his brother:

{{quote:d8884ba6-7c1c-5274-bcc8-360256525a95}}

The suppliant asks for pity; Agamemnon answers with the wrong done in Menelaus' own house (6.55–57; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]] and [[xenia]]), and refuses pity in advance to every Trojan, even the child in the womb: all are to perish “unmourned and without a trace” (6.60; see [[privative-adjectives]]).

**Refused.** Menelaus “pushed away from him with his hand the hero Adrastus” (6.62–63). The push undoes the grip on which the suppliant's claim rested, and Agamemnon kills him where he lies:

{{quote:5bbbf86b-d2ca-5f60-8342-f366d5bc4e01}}

The narrator's word for the advice, αἴσιμα, “what was due”, is discussed under [[aisa]]. See [[adrastus-the-trojan]], [[menelaus]] and [[agamemnon]].

**Every plea refused.** No one who begs for his life in the fighting of the Iliad is spared. Dolon is struck down as he reaches for Diomedes' chin (10.454–457). The sons of Antimachus beg Agamemnon from their chariot in Adrastus' own verses, and he kills them both (11.130–147). Tros, son of Alastor, clasps Achilles' knees and is stabbed in the liver while he begs (20.463–472), and Lycaon, holding Achilles' knees with one hand and his spear with the other, is killed with the sword (21.64–119). Menelaus alone comes near to granting a plea. The rite that wins Priam his son's body in the last book (24.477–479) does not save men taken in battle.
