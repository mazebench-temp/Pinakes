**Among her women.** Helen is first named in the book in the story of Hecuba's robes: Paris brought the Sidonian women home “on that voyage on which he brought back Helen, daughter of a noble father” (6.292; see [[sidonians]] and [[daughter-of-a-noble-father]]). When Hector comes into Paris' chamber, “Argive Helen sat among her slave women and was directing her handmaids in their glorious work” (6.323–324; see [[argive-helen]], [[handmaids]] and [[weaving]]). In Book 3 Iris found her at the loom, weaving “the many trials” of the war (3.125–128). Paris says that she has just persuaded him, “with gentle words”, to go back to the fighting (6.337–338).

**Her speech.** Hector has said nothing to Paris (6.342). Helen speaks to Hector “with soothing words” (6.343; see [[soothing-words]]), and of herself with none:

{{quote:769f0202-86e0-5497-83b5-9f1544e6f129}}

δᾶερ, “brother-in-law”, is what she called Agamemnon in Book 3, “if that was ever so” (3.180); now it is Hector, twice (6.344, 6.355; see [[in-laws]]). The genitives go with ἐμεῖο, “of me”: κυνός, “dog”; κακομήχανος, “evil-scheming”, used elsewhere of strife (9.257); and ὀκρυοέσσης, usually explained as κρυοέσσης, “chilling”, wrongly divided from an older genitive κακομηχάνοο κρυοέσσης (see [[chilling]]). In Book 3 she was “dog-faced” (3.180; see [[dog-face]]) and wished she had died before she followed Paris (3.173–175). Now she wishes that a storm had carried her off on the day she was born. Penelope, in words close to these, wishes that a storm would snatch her away (θύελλα … οἴχοιτο προφέρουσα, Od. 20.63–64).

**A better man.** Since the gods ordained these evils, she wishes she had been the wife of a better man, “one who felt the blame and the many reproaches of men” (6.349–351). Paris does not: “this man's mind is not steadfast now, nor will it ever be hereafter; and so I think he will reap the fruit of it” (6.352–353; see [[nemesis]] and [[paris]]). ἐπαυρήσεσθαι, “reap the fruit”, is bitter, as when Achilles wishes the Achaeans penned at their ships “so that they may all have joy of their king” (1.410).

**A theme of song.** Then she asks Hector in to sit:

{{quote:68891035-1f22-566e-874e-02ece79cf6f4}}

She names herself first, then Alexander's blind folly (see [[ate]]), and Zeus as the giver of their evil fate. Her last words give its purpose: they are to be ἀοίδιμοι, “a theme of song” (literally “sung of”), for men who are yet to be. The word occurs only here in Homer. Helen, who in Book 3 wove the war into her cloth (see [[helens-web]]), sees herself and Paris as the matter of songs to come. In the Odyssey Alcinous says the same of the whole war: the gods spun ruin for men so that there might be song for those who come after (Od. 8.579–580; see [[kleos]]).

**Hector's refusal.** “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me; you will not persuade me” (6.360). He must go back to the Trojans, and first home to his wife and son; she is to rouse Paris, so that he catches up with him inside the city (6.361–368). Each of the three women of his family whom Hector meets in Troy tries to keep him: Hecuba with wine (6.258), Helen with a seat, Andromache with the plea to stay on the tower (6.431). In her lament over his body Helen calls him the dearest of her brothers-in-law, who never spoke a harsh word to her and checked the others who did (24.762–772).
