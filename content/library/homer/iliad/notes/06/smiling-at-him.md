---

“And he smiled as he looked at his son in silence” (ἤτοι ὃ μὲν μείδησεν ἰδὼν ἐς παῖδα σιωπῇ, 6.404). Everywhere else in the Iliad μείδησεν, “smiled”, answers words: “So he spoke, and … smiled”, of Hera, Zeus and Achilles (1.595, 5.426, 14.222, 15.47, 21.434, 23.555), or “smiling at him, … said”, which opens a reply (4.356; see [[smiling-at-him]]). Hector smiles at what he sees, and says nothing (see [[silence]] and [[astyanax]]). The next verse sets Andromache beside him, weeping: “but Andromache stood close beside him, shedding tears” (6.405). The two verses are built as a pair, ὃ μέν … Ἀνδρομάχη δέ, his smile and her tears, and she is the one who speaks (6.407–439; see [[shedding-tears]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]).

The smile turns to laughter later in the scene. When the child cries out in fear of the helmet, “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (ἐκ δʼ ἐγέλασσε, 6.471), and when Hector has prayed and put the boy in her arms, Andromache takes him “laughing through her tears” (δακρυόεν γελάσασα, 6.484; see [[helmet]]). The only other laughter in the book is Paris', as he runs down from Pergamus in his armor (καγχαλόων, 6.514; see [[the-stallion]]).
