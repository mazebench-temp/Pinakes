**The horses of Adrastus.** Menelaus takes Adrastus alive because his horses fail him. “Bolting in terror across the plain”, they catch the chariot in a tamarisk branch, break the curved chariot “at the end of the pole”, and go on by themselves toward the city, where the others are fleeing; Adrastus tumbles out beside the wheel, face down in the dust (6.37–44; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[chariot]]). The pole broke at its front end, where the yoke was bound on (see the translation's note on 6.40). The same thing happens to many Trojan teams in the ditch in Book 16, which break their poles and leave their masters' chariots behind (16.370–371). The horses escape, and the man is killed as he begs for his life (6.45–65; see [[supplication]]).

**Charioteers.** Axylus dies with Calesius, “who at that time drove his horses as his charioteer” (ὑφηνίοχος, “driver under him”, a word found only here; 6.18–19; see [[axylus]] and [[calesius]]). When Glaucus and Diomedes have agreed to be guest-friends, they “leapt down from their chariots” (καθʼ ἵππων ἀΐξαντε, 6.232), and clasp hands on foot. ἵπποι, “horses”, is often the chariot and team together, as the translation renders it here (see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]).

**The stallion.** The one horse simile of the book is given to Paris. As a stalled horse, “fed full of barley at the manger”, breaks his halter and gallops over the plain to his familiar river and the pasture of the horses, head high and mane streaming, so Paris runs down from Pergamus in his armor (6.506–514; see [[the-stallion]] and [[paris]]). Barley is the fodder of well-kept horses: Pandarus' teams at home stand beside their chariots munching white barley and spelt (5.195–196). The six lines are used again of Hector in Book 15 (15.263–268).

**Horses in the epithets.** Argos is “horse-pasturing” (6.152), Antenor a “tamer of horses” (6.299), and the Trojans “horse-taming” (6.461; see [[horse-pasturing]] and [[tamer-of-horses]]). The helmets have horsehair crests: Acamas' helmet is “horsehair-crested” (6.9; see [[horsehair-crested]]), and Hector's frightens his son with its “plume of horsehair” (λόφον ἱππιοχαίτην, 6.469) and its “horsehair crest” (ἵππουριν, 6.495; see [[helmet]]).
