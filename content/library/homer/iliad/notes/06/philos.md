summary: In Book 6 φίλος names the bond of host and guest, in Axylus, “a friend to all men”, and in Diomedes' “dear guest-friend”, and the bonds of the family, in the farewell of Hector and Andromache, where the narrator calls each of them dear.
grc: [φιλέεσκεν, φίλου]
en: [friend, love]
passages:
  - 6.12-19 | Axylus
  - 6.466-485 | Hector and his son
---

**Friend and host.** The first man Diomedes kills in the book “was a friend to all men” (φίλος δʼ ἦν ἀνθρώποισι), “for he lived in a house beside the road and gave welcome to all” (πάντας γὰρ φιλέεσκεν, 6.14–15). φιλέω here is the host's verb, as when Antenor “welcomed” the Achaean envoys (φίλησα, 3.207; see [[xenia]] and [[axylus]]). Diomedes, who kills him, spares Glaucus two hundred lines later, and names the bond in the same word: “now I am your dear guest-friend (ξεῖνος φίλος) in the heart of Argos” (6.224).

**Friends in the army.** Both armies are addressed as φίλοι before the fighting: Nestor calls on the Achaeans, “Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares” (6.67), and Hector on the Trojans, “be men, my friends” (6.112). See [[exhortation]].

**Dearest.** The robe the Trojan women give Athena is to be the one that seems to Hecuba “the loveliest and largest in her hall, and by far the dearest to her own heart” (πολὺ φίλτατος αὐτῇ, 6.90–91, 6.271–272). A gift to a god is worth what it costs the giver (see [[charis]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]).

**Dear, in the family.** Helen tries to keep Hector with her, and he refuses her: “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me” (φιλέουσά περ, 6.360). He goes home “to see my household and my dear wife (ἄλοχόν τε φίλην) and my infant son” (6.366). In the scene at the gates the narrator gives the word to each member of the family in turn. The boy is frightened “at the sight of his dear father” (6.468); “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (6.471); Hector kisses “his dear son” (φίλον υἱόν, 6.474) and places him “in the arms of his dear wife” (6.482); and when they part, “his dear wife went home” (ἄλοχος δὲ φίλη, 6.495). The last of these goes with her as she leaves him; they do not meet again in the poem. See [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[husbands-and-wives]].

Hector himself, entering Paris' house with his spear, is “dear to Zeus” (Διῒ φίλος, 6.318; see [[dear-to-zeus]]).
