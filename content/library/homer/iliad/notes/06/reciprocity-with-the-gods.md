summary: The Trojan women give Athena their finest robe and promise her twelve cows if she will pity the city and break Diomedes' spear, and she refuses. Their prayer has no “if ever” to call on, only a gift given and a gift promised. Hector will not pour wine to Zeus with bloody hands, and his prayer for his son promises nothing.

**A gift and a promise.** Helenus lays down the rite. Hecuba is to gather the old women to Athena's temple, lay on the goddess's knees the robe that is loveliest and largest and dearest to her, “and promise to sacrifice twelve cows to her in her temple, yearlings, never touched by the goad, if she will pity the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children, so that she may hold the son of Tydeus back from sacred Ilios” (6.86–97). Hector repeats the instructions to his mother almost word for word (6.269–278), and the prayer, when Theano speaks it, keeps the same terms:

{{quote:6d83ccd3-0ec9-5127-93d6-ddbc7cd049d1}}

The prayer is a bargain with a gift in hand and a gift to come: the robe is given before a word is spoken, and the cows are promised if the goddess answers (see [[charis]], [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[sacrifice]]). What it lacks is the claim that opened Chryses' and Diomedes' prayers, the “if ever” of past service or past favor (1.39–41, 5.115–117). The women have nothing of that kind to call on, and they ask for the death of the goddess's favorite. Athena shakes her head (6.311). The goddess who has a temple and a priestess on Troy's citadel is the city's enemy, and the poem gives no reason here. The audience knows that she and Hera have been planning evils for the Trojans since the start of Book 4 (4.20–21), and Book 24 traces their hatred to the Judgment of Paris (24.25–30). The robe lies on her knees, and the city's fate is not changed (see [[gounata]] and [[athena]]).

**Wine and clean hands.** Hecuba would have Hector pour a libation “to father Zeus and the other immortals first”, and then drink himself (6.258–260). The order is the order of every feast: the gods' share first. Hector refuses, and his reason is a condition of exchange: “with unwashed hands I shrink from pouring sparkling wine to Zeus; nor may a man in any way offer the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, a prayer when he is spattered with blood and gore” (6.266–268). A gift to a god must be given clean (see [[hand-washing]], [[libation]] and [[aidos]]). Hector's errand was to tell the elders and the wives of Troy “to pray to the gods and promise them hecatombs” (6.113–115), and he tells the women at the gate to pray (6.240); he does not pray himself until he holds his son.

**A prayer without a gift.** Hector's prayer for Astyanax asks the gods that the boy may be preeminent among the Trojans, rule Ilios with might, and come home with bloody spoils (6.476–481). It recalls no gift and promises none, and the poem does not say whether it is heard (see [[euchomai]] and [[better-than-our-fathers]]).
