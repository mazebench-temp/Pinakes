summary: Glaucus tells Bellerophon's story as the heart of his lineage. Falsely accused by the wife of Proetus and sent to Lycia with grim signs, Bellerophon survives every task the king sets him and marries the king's daughter, until he is hated by the gods and wanders alone; Diomedes knows him as his grandfather's guest.

**The accusation.** The gods gave him beauty and manhood, and Proetus “devised evil against him in his heart” and drove him from the land (6.156–159; on the disputed construction of 6.158–159, see [[proetus]]). The cause follows:

{{quote:d72dec0f-d342-572b-b991-44547f1b5b31}}

ἐπεμήνατο, “was mad with longing”, occurs only here in Homer. Anteia's lie gives Bellerophon her own desire: φιλότητι μιγήμεναι, “to lie with … in love”, names her longing and then her charge (6.161, 6.165; see [[philotes]] and [[anteia]]). Proetus is angry but “shrank from killing him, for his heart held back in awe from that” (6.167; see [[sebas]]), the words used of Achilles, who would not strip the dead Eetion (6.417).

**The signs.** So he sends him to his father-in-law in Lycia with σήματα λυγρά, “grim signs, scratching on a folded tablet many things to destroy a man's life” (6.168–170). It is the only mention in Homer of anything like writing, and whether the signs were letters is disputed (see [[writing]] and [[bellerophons-letter]]). Bellerophon carries his own death sentence, “under the blameless escort of the gods” (6.171). The Lycian king entertains him for nine days before he asks to see the sign (6.172–177; see [[xenia]] and [[lycia]]).

**The tasks.**

{{quote:56e5fba0-42b3-5371-81ba-b2f149fffcb1}}

Each task should have killed him (see [[chimaera]], [[solymi]] and [[amazons]]). Then the king sets an ambush of the best men of Lycia, and none of them comes home (6.187–190). Diomedes' father survived the same kind of trap at Thebes and killed every man in it but one, sparing Maeon “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.391–398); the half-line is used only of Tydeus and of Bellerophon (4.398, 6.183). Diomedes' father and Glaucus' grandfather are heroes of one kind (see [[tydeus]] and [[ambush]]).

**The reward.** When the king “knew that he was the noble offspring of a god”, he kept him and gave him his daughter and half his royal honor, and the Lycians cut him a τέμενος, a domain “of orchard and plowland” (6.191–195). Sarpedon and Glaucus hold such an estate by the Xanthus, and Sarpedon names it as the reason why they must fight in the front rank (12.313–321; see [[lycians]]).

**The end.**

{{quote:296fd6e7-87eb-5117-9a97-cecc0cf42708}}

The poem does not say why the gods turned against him, or why Artemis killed his daughter (see [[laodameia]], [[isander]] and [[aleian-plain]]). His lonely wandering ends Glaucus' account of him without a death. Lycurgus, in Diomedes' story a few lines before, was likewise “hated by all the immortal gods” (6.140; see [[lycurgus]]): both stories told at this meeting arrive at a man the gods hate. Glaucus turns from it at once to the son who survived: “But Hippolochus begot me” (6.206; see [[hippolochus]]).

**The guest.** For Diomedes the name is a family memory. His grandfather Oeneus kept Bellerophon twenty days in his halls, and they exchanged a belt bright with purple and a golden cup (6.216–221; see [[oeneus]] and [[guest-gifts]]). See [[glaucus]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]].
