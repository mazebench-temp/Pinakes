ἄνιπτος, “unwashed” (ἀ-, “not”, and νίζω, “wash”, the verb in χερνίπτομαι), occurs only here in Homer. Washing the hands comes before prayer, libation and sacrifice, as at Chryse, where the Achaeans wash their hands before Chryses prays over the hecatomb (1.449; see [[hand-washing]]), and Hesiod forbids pouring wine to Zeus with unwashed hands (*Works and Days* 724–726).

What makes Hector's hands unfit is the battle. He is “spattered with blood and gore” (αἵματι καὶ λύθρῳ πεπαλαγμένον, 6.268). λύθρον is the gore of the slain. At the height of their great days Agamemnon and Achilles “spattered their invincible hands with gore” (λύθρῳ δὲ παλάσσετο χεῖρας ἀάπτους, 11.169, 20.503). The blood of killing keeps a man from the gods until it is washed away. After their night raid Odysseus and Diomedes wash off their sweat in the sea and bathe before they sit down to eat and pour honey-sweet wine to Athena (10.572–579). Achilles washes his hands before he pours and prays for Patroclus (16.230–231), and Priam before he sets out for Achilles' hut (24.302–306). Hector, who has come from the fighting and will go back to it, does not stop to wash, and he sends his mother to the gods in his place (6.269–278; see [[prayer]], [[libation]] and [[purification]]).
