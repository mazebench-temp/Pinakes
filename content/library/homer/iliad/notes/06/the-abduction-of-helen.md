---
summary: Book 6 recalls the voyage home by way of Sidon, from which Paris brought the embroidered robes that Hecuba keeps in her storeroom (6.289–292), and Agamemnon asks Menelaus whether the Trojans treated him so well in his house (6.55–57). In Troy, Hector wishes Paris dead for the evil he has brought, and Helen curses herself and names “Alexander's blind folly” (6.280–285, 6.344–358).

**Sidon.** When Hecuba goes into her storeroom to choose a robe for Athena, the narrator tells where her finest robes came from:

{{quote:ee8d70a6-1700-5b5d-9f29-89bc6c3e804f}}

The women who made them were Sidonians whom Alexander brought home from Sidon, “on that voyage on which he brought back Helen” (see [[sidon]] and [[sidonians]]). It is the only word in Homer on the route of the voyage home. In the *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Hera sent a storm against the lovers, and Paris was carried to Sidon and took the city before he sailed to Troy. Herodotus quotes these four lines, and says that they stand “in the aristeia of Diomedes” (Herodotus 2.116). He uses them to show that Homer knew of Paris' wanderings on his way home, and so knew the story that Helen had been in Egypt, and he adds that the *Cypria*, in which Paris came from Sparta to Troy in three days with a fair wind, cannot be Homer's (2.117; see [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[title-of-book-6]]). The robe Hecuba chooses from these is the one she offers to Athena, and Athena refuses it (6.293–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]).

**The host.** On the plain, Menelaus is ready to spare Adrastus for a ransom, and Agamemnon asks him: “Were you so very well treated in your house by the Trojans?” (6.55–57). The Trojan who came to his house as a guest took his wife and his possessions (3.351–354, 13.624–627; see [[xenia]]), and Agamemnon makes the offense a reason to spare no Trojan at all (see [[war-for-menelaus]] and [[adrastus-the-trojan]]).

**Hector.** In Troy, Hector tells his mother that he is going to find Paris, “if he is willing to listen to what I say. There and then may the earth gape for him! For the Olympian has raised him as a great bane to the Trojans and to great-hearted Priam and his sons” (6.280–283; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]). To Paris' face he says that it is for his sake that the war cry and the war blaze around the city (6.328–329), and at the end of the book that the Trojans have much toil for his sake (6.525). He had said the same in Book 3: Paris brought home a woman who was “a great grief to your father and your city and all the people” (3.50).

**Helen.** Helen blames herself first:

{{quote:769f0202-86e0-5497-83b5-9f1544e6f129}}

She calls herself a dog (κυνός, 6.344, 6.356), as she called herself “dog-faced” before Priam (3.180; see [[dog-face]]). Since the gods have ordained these evils, she wishes she were the wife of a better man, one who felt the blame of men (6.349–351), and she names the cause of the war twice over:

{{quote:68891035-1f22-566e-874e-02ece79cf6f4}}

Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἄτης, “for Alexander's blind folly” (6.356), is the phrase the narrator uses in the last book for the Judgment of Paris, the cause of the gods' hatred of Troy (24.28). In Book 3 Menelaus says that the war was fought “because Alexander began it” (Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἀρχῆς), and an ancient variant there read ἄτης as well (3.100; see [[arche-or-ate]] and [[ate]]). Helen takes both answers to the question of blame at once, her own shame and Alexander's folly, and sets over both the evil fate that Zeus has laid on them, which will make them a song (see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[helen]]).
