**Prayers ordered.** Hector leaves the battle to set prayers going. The elders and the wives are to “pray to the gods and promise them hecatombs” (6.114–115; see [[hecatomb]]). At the Scaean gates the wives and daughters of the Trojans run to him asking after their men, “and he told them to pray to the gods, all of them in turn; and sorrows were in store for many” (6.240–241). The narrator answers their prayers before they are made.

**Not with blood on his hands.** Hecuba supposes that her son has come to “lift up your hands to Zeus from the height of the city” (6.257), and offers him wine to pour. He will not pour or pray: “nor may a man in any way offer the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, a prayer when he is spattered with blood and gore” (6.267–268; see [[hand-washing]] and [[libation]]). The man who has come from killing is unfit to pray, and he sends his mother in his place (6.269–278).

**The women's prayer.** Theano prays for the women with the robe on the goddess's knees and the vow of twelve cows, and Athena shakes her head (6.301–311; see [[theano]], [[athena]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]). “So they prayed to the daughter of great Zeus” (6.312): the women go on praying after a refusal they have not seen.

**Hector's prayer.** The man who would not pray with blood on his hands prays at the gates with his son in his arms:

{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

He claims nothing and promises nothing. He asks that the boy be what he is, preeminent among the Trojans and strong, that he rule Ilios with might, and that one day someone say he is “far better than his father” as he comes home with bloody spoils to gladden his mother (see [[astyanax]], [[fathers-and-sons]], [[tis-speech]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). The narrator does not say whether any god heard. Hector has just told Andromache that sacred Ilios will perish (6.447–449). In the last book Andromache, mourning him, foresees that their son will not reach manhood: he will follow her into slavery, or some Achaean whose kinsman Hector killed will take him by the hand and hurl him from the tower (24.727–739).

**Claims.** εὔχομαι also means “claim”. Glaucus claims his lineage (6.211) and Diomedes their bond as guest-friends from their fathers' time (6.231). Those lines are not counted here (see [[euchomai]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).
