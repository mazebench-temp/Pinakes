{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

Hector has taken off the helmet that frightened the child, kissed him and dandled him in his arms (6.466–474). The prayer asks that the boy become what his father is, “preeminent among the Trojans, as good in strength”, and rule Ilios with might; then that he surpass him. The words are a wish. The optative εἴποι, “may someone say”, sets them apart from Hector's prophecy a few lines before, where “someone will say” (εἴπῃσιν) what he sees as certain (6.459). ἀνιόντα, “as he comes back”, agrees with no word in its sentence: the speaker sees the young man coming home from battle with bloody spoils, and the prayer ends with his mother's joy.

The saying's two uses face each other. In Book 4 Sthenelus boasted that the sons are far better than their fathers, and he made the boast at the fathers' expense: they perished by their own recklessness (4.405–409). Hector wishes it for his son at his own expense. Only a father can wish to be surpassed (see [[kleos]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Sophocles' Ajax, holding his son before his death, asks for less: that the boy be luckier than his father, and in all else like him (*Ajax* 550–551; see [[sophocles-tecmessa]]).

The Iliad gives the prayer no fulfillment. Andromache, lamenting Hector in the last book, does not think the boy will reach his youth before the city falls: he will follow her into slavery, or some Achaean will hurl him from the tower (24.726–738; see [[astyanax]]). Diomedes, the son measured against his father in Book 4, says in Book 6 that he does not remember Tydeus at all (6.222–223; see [[tydeus]]).
