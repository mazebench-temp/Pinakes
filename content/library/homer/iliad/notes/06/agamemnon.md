**Elatus.** In the opening list “Agamemnon, lord of men” (6.33; see [[lord-of-men]]) kills Elatus, a man from steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis (6.33–35; see [[elatus]]).

**No quarter.** Menelaus has taken Adrastus alive and is about to send him to the ships for ransom when Agamemnon “came running to meet him”, and rebuked him (6.51–54; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]):

{{quote:d8884ba6-7c1c-5274-bcc8-360256525a95}}

He opens with ὦ πέπον, “My dear” (6.55). πέπων is “ripe, soft”, and as an address it can be tender or scornful: Thersites calls the whole army πέπονες, “weaklings” (2.235). Then comes a taunt: “Were you so very well treated in your house by the Trojans?” (6.56–57). The wrong done in Menelaus' own house, the theft of Helen by a guest, is Agamemnon's answer to a plea for pity (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]] and [[xenia]]). The wish that follows spares no one, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb”, and ends with the whole people of Ilios gone, “unmourned and without a trace” (6.58–60). ἀκήδεστοι, “unmourned”, is “without κήδεα”, the care of the dead, and ἄφαντοι, “without a trace”, is the word Poseidon uses in Book 20, when he saves Aeneas so that the race of Dardanus may not perish “without seed and without a trace” (ἄσπερμος … ἄφαντος, 20.303; see [[aeneas]]).

{{quote:5bbbf86b-d2ca-5f60-8342-f366d5bc4e01}}

**What was due.** “So saying, the hero turned his brother's mind, urging what was due” (6.61–62). Whether αἴσιμα παρειπών approves the killing or says only that it agreed with what was fated is debated, and the translation leans toward approval (see [[aisa]]). The same words close his advice to Menelaus in Book 7, where they plainly mean sound counsel: he keeps his brother from facing Hector (7.120–121). In Book 4, over his wounded brother, he said that the oath-breakers would pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.161–162; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). His speech here holds the Trojans to that curse.

**His own hand.** Agamemnon stabs Adrastus in the flank and sets his heel on his chest to pull out his spear (6.64–65), the gesture of Menelaus over Peisander and of Patroclus over Sarpedon (λὰξ ἐν στήθεσι, 13.618, 16.503). Three times in the poem Agamemnon refuses a ransom: Chryses' for his daughter (1.24–32), Adrastus' here, which his brother would have taken, and that of the sons of Antimachus, who beg him in Adrastus' words (11.130–147; see [[apoina]] and [[supplication]]).

At the end of the book Andromache names “the sons of Atreus” among the best of the Achaeans who have three times tried the wall by the fig tree (Ἀτρεΐδας, 6.437; see [[atreidae]] and [[the-fig-tree]]).
