---
summary: Paris goes down to the battle “trusting in his swift feet,” and his swift feet carry him, laughing, to Hector; the book adds κραιπνός, another word for speed.
grc: [ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι, ταχέες δὲ πόδες]
---

The speed of Paris frames the simile of the stallion. He “hurried through the city, trusting in his swift feet” (ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι πεποιθώς, 6.505), and after the simile, “gleaming in his armor like the shining sun, laughing aloud, and his swift feet carried him” (ταχέες δὲ πόδες φέρον, 6.513–514; [[the-stallion]], [[like-the-sun]]). κραιπνός (“swift, rushing”) is used of feet and of winds; ταχύς is the ordinary word. In Book 5 the same swift feet saved Ares: “But my swift feet carried me away” (5.885). In the simile the horse trusts “in his splendor,” and his knees carry him swiftly to the pastures (6.510–511). The man trusts in his feet, and they carry him to his brother and to the war ([[paris]]).
