Hector walks away with his shield slung at his back from its strap, and it is so tall that its rim strikes both his neck and his ankles. ἄντυξ is the rim, the word also for the rail around a chariot (see [[chariot]]); the nominative occurs only here in the Iliad. ἣ πυμάτη θέεν, “that ran around the outer edge”, places it at the shield's edge, and δέρμα κελαινόν, “black hide”, is the leather of which it was made or with which it was bound. The shield is ὀμφαλόεσσα, “bossed”, the epithet of round shields with a central boss (see [[bossed]]).

A shield that reaches from neck to ankle is a body shield, like the “tower” shields of early Mycenaean art or the shield of Periphetes of Mycenae in Book 15, which reached to his feet: he turned, tripped on its rim and fell (15.645–646). A central boss belongs rather to round shields. The two descriptions sit together in these lines, and the passage has been cited on both sides of the question whether Homer's arms belong to one age or several (see [[shield]]). The formula ἀσπίδος ὀμφαλοέσσης may have been used here without thought of the shape.

Paris, whom Hector finds in his chamber, is “busy with his beautiful armor, his shield and his corselet” (6.321–322; see [[corselet]] and [[bow]]).
