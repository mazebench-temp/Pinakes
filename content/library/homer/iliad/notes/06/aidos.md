summary: Hector's answer to Andromache is the Iliad's plainest statement of a warrior's shame, “most terribly I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes, if like a coward I shrink away from the war”. Before Zeus he feels a reverence of another kind, and will not pour wine to him with hands spattered with blood.

**Shame before his own people.** Andromache has asked Hector to stay on the tower and to post the army by the fig tree (6.431–439; see [[andromaches-advice]]). He does not argue with her plan. He says why he cannot take her advice:

{{quote:4042d2ed-5830-5c3d-9b18-dd32069bc8e0}}

αἰδέομαι, “I feel shame before”, takes its object in the accusative: the people before whom he would be ashamed. They are the Trojans and the Trojan women, the whole city he defends, and the women are named with the men. The fear is of a name, κακός, “a coward”, and the shame is felt in advance, for what he would become if he stayed. It is joined at once to what he has learned and to fame (see [[kleos]]). Here αἰδώς is not the check on violence of Book 1 but the force that sends a man into battle, as Agamemnon asked the army to “feel shame before one another” (5.529–532; see [[shame-saves-more]]).

The line returns at the end of Hector's life. Alone outside the Scaean gates, waiting for Achilles, he thinks of going inside and cannot: “I feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes, for fear some other man, a worse one than I, may say: ‘Hector, trusting in his own strength, destroyed the people’” (22.105–107). The shame that sends him back to the war in Book 6 keeps him outside the wall in Book 22. See [[hector]] and [[tis-speech]]. Cicero made the half-line his own shorthand for the judgment of his peers (see [[cicero-trojans]]).

**Reverence.** When Hecuba offers him wine to pour to Zeus and to drink, Hector refuses both. The wine would unnerve him, and “with unwashed hands I shrink from (ἅζομαι) pouring sparkling wine to Zeus; nor may a man in any way offer the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, a prayer when he is spattered with blood and gore” (6.266–268). ἅζομαι is the verb of awe before the gods, the reverence Chryses asked the Achaeans to show Apollo in Book 1 (ἁζόμενοι, 1.21). Hector's awe is felt for the god and shown in the body: a man fresh from killing is not clean enough to pray. See [[hand-washing]], [[libation]] and [[purification]].

**Modest wives.** In the description of Priam's palace the sons-in-law of the king sleep “beside their modest wives” (παρʼ αἰδοίῃς ἀλόχοισιν, 6.250). αἰδοῖος is the one to whom respect is due, and the one who shows it; Book 2 called the maiden Astyoche αἰδοίη (2.514). See [[in-laws]] and [[priams-palace]].

Two other words of holding back come in the book: σέβας, the awe that kept Proetus from killing Bellerophon and Achilles from stripping Eetion (6.167, 6.417; see [[sebas]]), and αἰσχύνω, “shame”, the charge Hippolochus laid on Glaucus (6.209; see [[aischros]]).
