summary: Diomedes asks the stranger who he is, and Glaucus answers first with the generations of leaves and then with six generations of his family, from Aeolus to himself (6.119–211). The genealogy makes the two men guest-friends, and they do not fight.

**The challenge.** The two step out between the armies and come close, and Diomedes speaks first: “Who are you, good sir, among mortal men? For I have never seen you in the battle where men win glory before now” (6.123–125; see [[when-they-came-close]]). The threat that goes with the question is a line Achilles will use to Asteropaeus, “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (6.127 = 21.151; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]). Diomedes' question has two answers ready. If the stranger is a god, Diomedes will not fight him, and he tells of Lycurgus to show why; if he is one “of the mortals who eat the fruit of the field”, let him come nearer and die (6.128–143; see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]).

**The answer.** Glaucus questions the question, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (6.145), and answers with the leaves: the generations of men come and go like the leaves of the forest (6.146–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). γενεή is both the “lineage” he is asked for and the “generation” of the leaves (see [[genee]]). Then he gives it all the same, with the couplet that Aeneas will use to Achilles: “But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well our lineage—and many men know it—” (6.150–151 = 20.213–214). The lineage runs through six generations, Aeolus, Sisyphus, Glaucus, Bellerophon, Hippolochus and Glaucus (6.152–155, 6.196–206), and most of it is the story of Bellerophon (6.156–205; see [[bellerophon]] and [[sisyphus]]). It closes with his father's charge and the closing verse of the pattern:

{{quote:355eb7a5-a8c1-5675-b8b5-f98c69a34fa6}}

“Always to be the best and to excel above the others” is the charge Peleus gave Achilles (11.784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]), and “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” closes Aeneas' genealogy too (20.241). The speech begins and ends with γενεή (6.145, 6.211; see [[ring-composition]]).

**The outcome.** In the other meetings of the kind the genealogy is followed by the fight. Here it names Bellerophon, whom Diomedes' grandfather Oeneus once entertained, and Diomedes is glad, fixes his spear in the earth, and claims Glaucus as a guest-friend from their fathers' time (6.212–231; see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]). A lineage recited to show why a man should be feared becomes the ground of a friendship. See [[glaucus]], [[diomedes]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]].
