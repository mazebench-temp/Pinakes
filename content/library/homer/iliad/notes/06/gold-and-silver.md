The book's other gold is kept in houses: the golden cup of Bellerophon in Diomedes' home, the treasures of Adrastus' father, and the golden ring on Hector's spear.

**Gold for bronze.** The exchange of armor seals the guest-friendship of Glaucus and Diomedes, and the narrator ends the scene with a judgment:

{{quote:db540de2-4ce8-55df-a865-408804c2074a}}

χρύσεα χαλκείων, “golden for bronze”, and ἑκατόμβοιʼ ἐννεαβοίων, “worth a hundred oxen for worth nine”: the price is reckoned in cattle, as the Homeric world reckons it (see [[cattle]]). A Lycian prince goes to war in golden armor, like the Carian leader who came to Troy “wearing gold, like a girl”, whom Achilles killed and stripped (2.872–875), and the Thracian king Rhesus, whose golden armor was a wonder “not fit at all for mortal men to wear, but for the immortal gods” (10.438–441). Diomedes' bronze is the common metal of war.

Whether Zeus' taking of Glaucus' wits is a joke, a sober verdict on a bad bargain, or a sign of Glaucus' generosity is debated (see [[phrenes]]). Aristotle already took Glaucus as the example of a man who gives away what is his own and so suffers no injustice (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13), and the half-line became a proverb for a lopsided trade (see [[gold-for-bronze]]). Walter Donlan read the unequal gift in the light of Homeric gift-exchange, in which the greater giver gains standing (“The Unequal Exchange between Glaucus and Diomedes in Light of the Homeric Gift-Economy”, *Phoenix* 43, 1989). See [[guest-gifts]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]].

**Gold kept at home.** Diomedes' proof of the old bond is gold. Oeneus gave Bellerophon a belt bright with purple, “and Bellerophon a two-handled cup of gold, which I left in my house when I came here” (6.219–221). The guest-gift is kept as treasure, and it carries the bond to the grandson (see [[xenia]] and [[cup]]). Adrastus' plea counts his father's stored treasures, “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” (6.47–48), the metals of a ransom (see [[apoina]]); the same line returns in the pleas of Dolon and of the sons of Antimachus (10.379, 11.133).

**Hector's spear.** When Hector goes into Paris' house he holds a spear eleven cubits long, and “before him shone the bronze point of the spear, and a golden ring ran around it” (6.319–320; see [[spear]]).
