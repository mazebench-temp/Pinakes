**Made priestess.** “For the Trojans had made her priestess of Athena” (6.300). ἱέρεια, the feminine of ἱερεύς, occurs only here in Homer, and Theano is the only priestess in the poems. Like Dolopion, who “had been made priest of the Scamander” (5.77), she holds her office from the people: the Trojans “made” her (ἔθηκαν). She comes from the first families of Troy. She is the daughter of Cisseus, who raised her son Iphidamas in Thrace (11.221–224), and the wife of Antenor, one of the elders who sit with Priam on the tower (3.146–148), and she reared Antenor's bastard son Pedaeus as her own (5.69–71; see [[theano]], [[cisseus]] and [[antenor]]). The men the Iliad calls priests serve gods, Apollo, Hephaestus, the Scamander and Zeus (1.11, 5.10, 5.77–78, 16.604–605). The one priestess serves a goddess, as goddesses were often served by women in later Greek cult.

**What she does.** In Helenus' instructions the queen was to open the temple with the key and lay the robe on the goddess's knees (6.88–92). When the women come, the priestess does both: she opens the doors, takes the robe from Hecuba, lays it on the knees of the image, and prays for them all while they lift up their hands (6.298–305). The priest acts for the worshipers, as Chryses prays for the Achaeans at the hecatomb in Book 1 (1.450–456; see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**Heard and refused.** A priest's prayer rests on his service to the god. Chryses reminded Apollo of the temple he had roofed and the thigh-pieces he had burned, and was heard at once (1.39–43). Theano names no service and makes a promise, twelve cows if the goddess will pity the city, and Athena shakes her head (6.308–311). Dares and Dolopion lost sons in Book 5 (5.9–24, 5.76–83); here the priestess of the city's goddess loses the city's prayer (see [[athena]] and [[temple]]).
