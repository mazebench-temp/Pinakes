summary: Hector imagines two speeches of “someone” in time to come: a man in Argos who will see Andromache weeping and say, “This is the wife of Hector” (6.459–462), and someone who will say of his son, “He is far better than his father” (6.479). The first is a foreboding, the second a prayer.
grc: ["ποτέ τις εἴπῃσιν", "ποτέ τις εἴποι"]
en: [may someone say one day, So someone will say]
passages:
  - 6.454-465 | Hector foresees Andromache's slavery
  - 6.476-481 | Hector's prayer for his son

**The wife of Hector.** Hector's picture of Andromache's slavery ends in the words of a stranger:

“and someday a man will say as he sees you shedding tears: ‘This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting of the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought around Ilios.’ So someone will say one day; and it will be fresh sorrow for you” (6.459–462).

The speaker is an Achaean in Argos, and his words are Hector's fame, spoken by an enemy over the slave who was his wife (see [[kleos]] and [[captive-women]]). The same opening, καί ποτέ τις εἴπῃσι, brings in Hector's other imagined monument, in his challenge in Book 7: a sailor passing the mound of the champion Hector has killed will say, “This is the mound of a man who died long ago” (7.87–91). Hector's shame “before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes”, which he names here (6.442), comes back in the same verse before his death, with another imagined speech: someone baser than he will say that Hector, trusting in his own strength, destroyed his people (22.105–107; see [[aidos]]).

**The better son.** Hector's prayer ends with a speech of the same kind: “and may someone say one day, ‘He is far better than his father,’ as he comes back from war” (6.479–480). The optative εἴποι, “may someone say”, makes the formula a wish, where the subjunctive εἴπῃσι in 6.459 and 7.87 foretells. ἀνιόντα, “as he comes back”, agrees with no word in the sentence: the praise is said of the boy (see [[prayer]], [[astyanax]] and [[better-than-our-fathers]]).

The two speeches answer each other. In the first a stranger speaks of the father's glory to the mother in her grief; in the second someone speaks of the son's glory, and the mother's heart rejoices (6.481). See [[hector-and-andromache]].
