Hecuba, whom Helenus calls “your mother and mine”, meets Hector with wine and leads the women to Athena; Andromache's mother was ransomed and then struck down by Artemis; a mother's joy ends Hector's prayer for his son; and Agamemnon would not spare even the child in its mother's womb.

**Your mother and mine.** Helenus sends Hector to the city with a message for “your mother and mine” (μητέρι σῇ καὶ ἐμῇ, 6.87): Hecuba is to gather the old women and lay a robe on Athena's knees. When Hector reaches the palace, “his generous mother (ἠπιόδωρος μήτηρ) came to meet him”, and she clings to his hand and asks why he has left the war (6.251–257). Hector's refusal of her wine begins with her title, πότνια μῆτερ, “lady mother” (6.264). His last message to her is an errand: go to the temple and pray (6.269–279). See [[hecuba]], [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]].

**Andromache's mother.** Andromache's plea counts her dead. After her father and her seven brothers comes her mother, “who was queen beneath wooded Placus”: Achilles brought her to the camp with the other possessions, released her for a boundless ransom, “and in her father's halls Artemis of the showering arrows struck her down” (6.425–428). The mother survived capture and ransom and died at home, by a goddess's arrow. In Homer a woman's sudden death is Artemis' work, as a man's is Apollo's (*Odyssey* 3.279–280, 15.409–411): Hera calls her “a lion to women” (21.483), and Odysseus asks his mother's ghost whether Artemis killed her “with her gentle arrows” (*Odyssey* 11.172–173). So Andromache can say, “I have no father and no lady mother” (6.413), and give both names to her husband: “Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother” (6.429). See [[artemis]] and [[apoina]].

**A mother's joy.** When the child is frightened by the helmet, “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (6.471). Hector's prayer for him ends not with the father's pride but with the mother's joy: “may he bring back bloody spoils when he has killed his enemy, and may his mother's heart rejoice” (χαρείη δὲ φρένα μήτηρ, 6.480–481). See [[astyanax]] and [[andromache]].

**Mothers and the unborn.** The book's harshest words concern a mother. Agamemnon tells Menelaus to spare no Trojan, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb, not even he escape” (6.58–59). Other mothers are named in births. Bucolion was the eldest son of Laomedon, “but his mother bore him in secret” (6.24), and he fathered twins on a Naiad nymph (6.21–26; see [[bucolion]] and [[abarbarea]]). Helen wishes that a storm had carried her away “on the day when my mother first bore me” (6.345; see [[never-born]]).
