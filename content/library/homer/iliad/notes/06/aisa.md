**What was due.** Adrastus, thrown from his chariot, clasps Menelaus' knees and offers ransom, and Menelaus is about to send him to the ships alive. Agamemnon runs up and demands that no Trojan escape, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb” (6.55–60). Then the narrator speaks:

{{quote:5bbbf86b-d2ca-5f60-8342-f366d5bc4e01}}

αἴσιμα παρειπών, “urging what was due”, is the narrator's own judgment, and readers have long disagreed about what it judges. αἴσιμος can mean “right, proper”, as when Poseidon says that it is good for a messenger to know “what is proper” (αἴσιμα, 15.207). On that reading the narrator approves: the Trojans broke the sworn truce, and Agamemnon swore then that they would pay with their wives and children (4.157–162). αἴσιμος can also mean “fated”, as in “the day of fate” (αἴσιμον ἦμαρ) of the Achaeans that sinks in Zeus' scales (8.72); on that reading the words say only that the advice agreed with what was destined for Troy. The same two words close Agamemnon's advice to Menelaus in Book 7, where they plainly mean sound counsel: he persuades his brother not to face Hector (7.120–121). The translation renders “urging what was due”, which leans toward approval. Agamemnon refuses ransom twice more in the poem: from Chryses in Book 1 (1.24–32), and in Book 11 from the two sons of Antimachus, who beg in Adrastus' very words and die because their father once urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came to Troy as an envoy (11.122–147). See [[apoina]], [[supplication]] and [[adrastus-the-trojan]].

**Fairly, and beyond.** When Hector rebukes Paris in his chamber, Paris answers with the line he used in Book 3: “Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (κατʼ αἶσαν … οὐδʼ ὑπὲρ αἶσαν, 6.333 = 3.59). In Book 3 the concession led to a defense of Aphrodite's gifts (3.64–66); here it leads to a promise to follow Hector to the war (6.337–341). At the end of the book the adjective ἐναίσιμος, “in due measure, fair”, comes twice. Paris asks whether he has come “in due time” (ἐναίσιμον, 6.519), and Hector answers that no man “who was fair-minded” (ἐναίσιμος) would dishonor his work in battle (6.521–522; see [[paris]] and [[praise-and-blame]]).

**Beyond my portion.** Hector's comfort to Andromache begins: “for no man will hurl me down to Hades beyond my portion” (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν, 6.487). Here αἶσα is the measure of a life. Hector denies for himself what the poem elsewhere allows, that something may happen, or nearly happen, beyond what is fated: the Achaeans' homecoming “beyond fate” (ὑπέρμορα, 2.155), the Achaeans prevailing “beyond their portion” in Patroclus' last fight (16.780), and the Argives winning glory “beyond the portion of Zeus” (17.321). See [[moira]] and [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]].
