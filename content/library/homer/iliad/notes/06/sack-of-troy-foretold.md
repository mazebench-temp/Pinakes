---
summary: Book 6 looks at the fall of Troy from both sides of the wall. Agamemnon wants no Trojan spared, not even the child in the womb (6.57–60), and Hector tells Andromache that a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish, and pictures her led away to slavery (6.447–465).
passages:

  - 6.447-465 | Hector foresees the fall of Troy
  - 6.526-529 | The mixing bowl of freedom
---

**None to escape.** On the plain, Menelaus is ready to spare Adrastus for a ransom. Agamemnon runs up and rebukes him:

{{quote:d8884ba6-7c1c-5274-bcc8-360256525a95}}

He wants more than victory: the whole people destroyed, “unmourned and without a trace” (ἀκήδεστοι καὶ ἄφαντοι), down to the boy in his mother's womb. The narrator says that he urged “what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών, 6.62), and he kills the suppliant himself (see [[adrastus-the-trojan]], [[supplication]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). Over his wounded brother in Book 4 he said that the oath-breakers would pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.161–162).

**The city's prayer.** Inside the walls the rite for Athena asks her to pity “the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (6.95, 6.276, 6.310), and she refuses (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]). The wives and daughters of the Trojans crowd around Hector at the gate asking for their men, “and sorrows were in store for many” (6.238–241). Hector tells Paris to go out “for fear the city will soon burn with consuming fire” (6.331), and Andromache shows where the wall can be scaled, by the wild fig tree, where the best of the Achaeans have three times tried it (6.433–439; see [[the-fig-tree]]).

**A day will come.** Hector's answer to Andromache gives the fall of Troy in the words Agamemnon used in Book 4 (4.163–165; see [[a-day-will-come]]):

{{quote:b9953f06-8c76-5966-8daa-48e14fff186c}}

He goes on to what the fall will mean for her. Some Achaean will lead her away in tears; in Argos she will weave at another woman's loom and carry water from a spring; a man will see her weeping and say, “This is the wife of Hector” (6.454–462). He prays to be dead and buried before he hears her cry as they drag her away (6.464–465; see [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[captive-women]]). The Iliad never shows the sack. It imagines it through its victims, here and in Priam's plea to Hector from the wall, with his sons killed, his daughters dragged away and the little children dashed to the ground (22.59–71).

**The child.** Hector prays that his son may rule Ilios with might (6.476–478). Andromache, mourning Hector in Book 24, knows better: the city will be sacked before the child grows up, and some Achaean will throw him from the tower (24.727–738). In the *Iliupersis*, as Proclus summarizes it, Odysseus killed Astyanax and Neoptolemus took Andromache (see [[astyanax]]).

**Mourned alive.** When Andromache comes home, her handmaids lament Hector in his own house while he is still alive, “for they thought he would never come back again from the war” (6.500–502; see [[lament]]).

**The mixing bowl of freedom.** The book that looks furthest ahead to Troy's fall ends with the other hope, in Hector's last words to Paris:

{{quote:cd53a2f4-1789-5cca-b968-e057609a3aa0}}

κρητὴρ ἐλεύθερος, “the mixing bowl of freedom”, is found only here: a bowl for the gods set up in thanks for deliverance. Its adjective is the one of ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, “your day of freedom”, which Hector fears Andromache will lose (6.455; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[eleutheros]] and the translation's note on 6.528). The two brothers go out to the war together (7.1–7).
