**Glaucus' wits.** Glaucus and Diomedes leap down from their chariots, take each other's hands and pledge their faith. Then the narrator comments:

{{quote:db540de2-4ce8-55df-a865-408804c2074a}}

φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, “Zeus took away his wits”, is a phrase for real delusion. Achilles says it of Agamemnon (9.377), Agamemnon of himself when he confesses his blind folly (ἀασάμην, 19.137; see [[ate]]), Alcimedon of Automedon, who is fighting the Trojans alone (17.469–471), and the narrator of the Trojans who cheer Hector's fatal plan to camp on the plain (18.311). Here it is said of a bargain: armor worth a hundred oxen given for armor worth nine. Whether the narrator means it gravely, as a joke at Glaucus' expense, or as a way of marking his generosity is disputed. It is in any case the narrator's own judgment of a character's choice, like his νήπιος, “fool”, for Patroclus as he begs to go into battle (16.46); at 18.311 he uses both words of the Trojans, “fools, for Pallas Athena took away their wits”. The phrase gives the comment on Glaucus its weight (see [[gold-and-silver]], [[glaucus]] and [[nepios]]). Aristotle cites the exchange when he asks whether a man can be wronged willingly (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13), and the half-line became a byword (see [[gold-for-bronze]]).

**A brother's mind.** Menelaus is about to spare Adrastus when Agamemnon rebukes him: “So saying, the hero turned his brother's mind” (ἔτρεψεν ἀδελφειοῦ φρένας, 6.61; see [[aisa]] and [[kasignetos]]).

**Paris' mind.** Helen tells Hector that she wishes she were the wife of a better man, “but this man's mind is not steadfast now, nor will it ever be hereafter; and so I think he will reap the fruit of it” (τούτῳ δʼ οὔτʼ ἂρ νῦν φρένες ἔμπεδοι, 6.352–353). ἔμπεδος, “firm, steadfast”, is the word for might that holds (5.254). Then she turns to Hector, “since the toil has beset your mind most of all” (6.355). See [[paris]] and [[helen]].

**Other minds.** Aeneas and Hector are the best of the Trojans “both in fighting and in counsel” (μάχεσθαί τε φρονέειν τε, 6.79). Bellerophon refused Anteia “for his thoughts were good” (ἀγαθὰ φρονέοντα, 6.162). Hector says that if he saw Paris dead, “I would think my heart had forgotten its joyless misery” (φρένʼ, 6.285), and knows the fall of Troy “in my mind and in my heart” (6.447). His prayer ends with the mother: “may his mother's heart rejoice” (6.481).
