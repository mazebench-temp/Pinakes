summary: Hector finds Paris in his chamber and rebukes him for “this anger” without saying what it is; Paris answers that it was not anger at the Trojans that kept him there. In Glaucus' story anger seizes Proetus at his wife's lie, and Artemis kills Laodameia “in her anger”, for no reason the poem gives.

**Paris' anger.** Hector's rebuke begins: “Strange one, you do wrong to keep this anger in your heart” (χόλον τόνδʼ ἔνθεο θυμῷ, 6.326). He does not say what Paris is angry at. Paris takes it as anger at the Trojans, who hated him “like black doom” (3.454), and denies it: “It was not so much because I was angry or indignant with the Trojans that I sat in my chamber; I wanted to give myself up to grief” (χόλῳ οὐδὲ νεμέσσι, 6.335–336; see [[nemesis]] and [[achos]]). The word may be Hector's tactful name for what he calls, at the end of the book, a willful holding back: “you hold back of your own will and do not want to fight” (ἑκὼν μεθιεῖς, 6.523). In Book 3 he pictured the Achaeans laughing at Paris' handsome looks, “but there is no strength in his heart, and no courage” (3.43–45). See [[paris]] and [[praise-and-blame]].

**Anger in Glaucus' story.** Anteia tells Proetus to kill Bellerophon, “and anger seized the lord at what he heard” (χόλος λάβεν, 6.166). The anger is real and its cause is false, and it sends an innocent man to the deadly tasks of Lycia (see [[proetus]] and [[sebas]]). At the end of the story, “Artemis of the golden reins killed his daughter in her anger” (χολωσαμένη, 6.205). The poem does not say why the goddess was angry with Laodameia, the mother of Sarpedon by Zeus (see [[laodameia]] and [[artemis]]). Divine anger without a named cause belongs with the gods' hatred of her father, which also has none (6.200; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).
