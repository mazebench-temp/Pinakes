**Alive.** The list of kills ends, and Menelaus, “good at the war cry” (6.37; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]]), takes his man alive (ζωὸν ἕλʼ, 6.37–38). The horses of Adrastus have wrecked his chariot, and Menelaus stands over him with his long-shadowed spear (6.43–44; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]). Adrastus clasps his knees and offers a ransom, and “he began to win over the heart in his breast” (6.51). The imperfect ἔπειθε is a persuasion under way: Menelaus is about to give him to his attendant to lead down to the ships (6.52–53; see [[supplication]]).

**The younger brother.** Agamemnon runs up and asks whether the Trojans treated him so well in his own house (6.55–57; see [[agamemnon]]). The taunt goes to the root of the war, the wrong that Paris did him as a guest (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). “So saying, the hero turned his brother's mind” (6.61), and Menelaus “pushed away from him with his hand the hero Adrastus” (6.62–63). He does not kill the man; his brother does. The pattern of the two brothers recurs. Agamemnon grieves over the wounded Menelaus as if he were already dead (4.148–182), and in Book 7 he keeps him from taking up Hector's challenge, in the words that close this scene (7.107–121). See [[atreidae]].

**Pity.** In Book 5 Menelaus pitied the twin sons of Diocles as they fell (5.561; see [[crethon]]), and here he nearly spares an enemy. In the fighting the Iliad narrates, no other Achaean comes so near to granting a Trojan's plea for his life; Achilles only recalls that before Patroclus died he took many Trojans alive and sold them (21.100–102). Later Apollo, taunting Hector, calls Menelaus a man “who before was a soft spearman” (μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής, 17.587–588).

At the end of the book Andromache names “the sons of Atreus” among the best of the Achaeans, who have three times tried the wall by the fig tree (Ἀτρεΐδας, 6.437; see [[the-fig-tree]]).
