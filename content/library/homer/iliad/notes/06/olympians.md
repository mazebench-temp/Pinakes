**God or man.** When Hector rallies the Trojans, the Argives give ground, thinking “that one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky to bring help to the Trojans” (6.108–109). A few lines later Diomedes asks the same of the stranger who faces him: is he “one of the immortals come down from heaven” (6.128)? If so, he will not fight him, and he tells the story of Lycurgus to show why (6.129–140; see [[dionysus]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). He ends:

{{quote:6a470c30-76c0-5b3f-aa9e-e20f9a020b64}}

In fourteen lines he names the gods as a body five ways: the immortals (6.128), the gods of heaven (θεοῖσιν ἐπουρανίοισι, 6.129, 6.131), the gods who live at ease (θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, 6.138), all the immortal gods (6.140), and the blessed gods (μακάρεσσι θεοῖς, 6.141). Against them stand the mortals “who eat the fruit of the field”. Men live on bread and die; the gods eat no bread and drink no wine, and so they are bloodless and immortal (5.339–342; see [[immortals]], [[blessed-gods]], [[who-live-at-ease]] and [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]). ῥεῖα ζώοντες, “who live at ease”, is found only here in the Iliad and twice in the Odyssey (Od. 4.805, 5.122). Achilles says the same thing bitterly in the last book: the gods have spun it for wretched mortals to live in grief, while they themselves are free of care (24.525–526; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**Angry together.** In the story the gods act as one. “The gods who live at ease were angry with him” (ὀδύσαντο, 6.138), and ὀδύσσομαι, “be angry”, is the verb from which the Odyssey derives the name of Odysseus (Od. 19.406–409). Lycurgus did not live long, for he was “hated by all the immortal gods” (6.140). Glaucus uses the same words when Bellerophon's fortune turns: he “was hated by all the gods” (6.200; see [[bellerophon]]).

**The gods of heaven.** ἐπουράνιος, “in the sky, of heaven”, is found in the Iliad only in Book 6: twice in Diomedes' refusal (6.129, 6.131), and once in Hector's last words, when he hopes that Zeus will let the Trojans set up a mixing bowl of freedom “for the everlasting gods of heaven” (6.527; see [[zeus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The Odyssey has it once (Od. 17.484).
