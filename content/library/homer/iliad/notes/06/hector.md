summary: Sent into the city by Helenus, Hector refuses his mother's wine, rebukes Paris, refuses Helen's chair, and meets Andromache and their son at the Scaean gates. He foresees the fall of Troy and her slavery, prays that his son may be better than his father, and goes back to the war mourned in his own house as if he were already dead.

**The errand.** Helenus sends him into the city with a message for their mother: she is to lay her finest robe on Athena's knees and promise twelve cows, if the goddess will keep Diomedes from Ilios (6.86–101; see [[helenus]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]). The errand takes Troy's best fighter home (see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). He rallies the army in the lines that rallied it in Book 5 (6.103–106 = 5.494–497) and tells the Trojans he is going to ask “the elders who sit in council, and our wives” to pray (6.113–114; see [[trojan-elders]]). As he goes, the black hide of his great shield knocks against his neck and ankles (6.117–118; see [[shield]]). At the Scaean gates the wives and daughters of the Trojans run to him asking for their men; “he told them to pray to the gods, all of them in turn; and sorrows were in store for many” (6.240–241; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[trojan-women]]).

**His mother.** In Priam's palace Hecuba clings to his hand and offers him honey-sweet wine, to pour to Zeus and to drink (6.251–262; see [[hecuba]] and [[visit-scene]]). He refuses both:

{{quote:8ecd2a60-af19-5ef9-b989-e85409430d03}}

Wine would take his strength, and a man spattered with blood may not pray (see [[libation]], [[hand-washing]] and [[aidos]]). He sends her to Athena with Helenus' instructions, almost word for word (6.269–278; 6.90–97), and ends with a curse on his brother: “may the earth gape for him!” (6.282; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]).

**Paris and Helen.** He finds Paris in his chamber handling his armor, and rebukes him: the people are dying around the wall for his sake (6.313–331; see [[paris]]). Paris answers with the line he used in Book 3, “Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (6.333; 3.59), and Hector says nothing to him (6.342). Helen, calling herself a dog, asks him to sit and rest (6.343–358; see [[helen]]). He refuses: “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me” (6.360). Then he says where he is going:

**Andromache.** She is not at home. The housekeeper tells him she has gone to the great tower “like a woman out of her mind” (6.386–389; see [[handmaids]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]), and she meets him at the gates “by which he meant to go out to the plain”, with the nurse and their son (6.393–403; see [[andromache]], [[astyanax]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]). She begs him to stay on the tower. He answers with the reasons of his life:

{{quote:4042d2ed-5830-5c3d-9b18-dd32069bc8e0}}

It is shame before his people, and a courage he has “learned” (μάθον), fighting for his father's fame and his own (see [[kleos]]). Then he tells her what he knows, in the three lines with which Agamemnon foretold the fall of Troy (4.163–165; see [[a-day-will-come]]):

{{quote:b9953f06-8c76-5966-8daa-48e14fff186c}}

What grieves him most is not the sorrow of the Trojans, “nor of Hecuba herself, nor of lord Priam”, nor of his brothers, but hers. Some Achaean will lead her away in tears; she will weave at another woman's bidding in Argos and carry water from Messeis or Hypereia; and a stranger will point her out: “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting” (6.450–463; see [[messeis]], [[doulios]] and [[tis-speech]]). He would rather lie dead under the heaped earth than hear her cries (6.464–465; see [[heaped-earth]]).

**His son.** The child shrinks crying from the bronze and the nodding crest; both parents laugh; Hector lays the helmet on the ground, kisses the boy and dandles him, and prays (6.466–475; see [[helmet]]):

{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

He asks that his son be what he is, and more: that someone may say, “He is far better than his father” (6.479; see [[better-than-our-fathers]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The poem does not say whether Zeus heard. Andromache will foresee the boy thrown from the tower (24.734–735).

**Mourned alive.** He strokes her with his hand and comforts her: no man will send him to Hades beyond his portion, and no man, coward or brave, has escaped his fate (6.486–489; see [[aisa]], [[moira]] and [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). She is to go home to her loom and distaff; “war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). She goes home, turning back again and again, and rouses lament among her handmaids:

{{quote:aa7b4810-7428-54b9-9759-dad270f3582a}}

It is the first lament for Hector in the poem (see [[lament]]). The last is sung over his body, and Andromache leads it (24.719–745).

**Paris again.** Paris catches up with him “just as he was about to turn from the place where he had talked intimately with his wife” (6.515–516; see [[oaristys]]). Hector's last words in the book are gentler than his first: Paris is brave, but holds back of his own will, and they will make it right later, “if ever Zeus grants us, for the everlasting gods of heaven, to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls” (6.521–528; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[eleutheros]]).
