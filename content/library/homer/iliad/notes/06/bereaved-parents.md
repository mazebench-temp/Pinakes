**Parents in the obituaries.** The book's first deaths include two brothers with a history. Euryalus kills Aesepus and Pedasus, “whom once the nymph Abarbarea, a Naiad, bore to noble Bucolion”, the eldest son of Laomedon, who lay with her while he tended his sheep (6.21–28; see [[bucolion]], [[abarbarea]] and [[death-in-battle]]). Adrastus begs for his life in the name of a father who would pay a boundless ransom “if he heard that I was alive at the ships of the Achaeans” (6.46–50). He is killed, and his father will not hear it (see [[apoina]]).

**A father bereaved by the gods.** In Glaucus' story Bellerophon, hated by all the gods, loses two of his three children: “Ares, insatiable of war, killed his son Isander as he fought the glorious Solymi; and Artemis of the golden reins killed his daughter in her anger” (6.203–205; see [[isander]] and [[laodameia]]). The father whom the gods once favored ends in grief, wandering alone and “eating his heart out” (6.201–202; see [[thymos]]).

**Andromache's parents.** Andromache is the child left behind. Achilles killed her father Eetion when he sacked Thebe, and her seven brothers “all went down on a single day into the house of Hades”; her mother was taken captive, ransomed, and struck down by Artemis in her father's halls (6.414–428; see [[eetion]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). “I have no father and no lady mother”, she says, and gives their names to her husband (6.413, 6.429–430).

**Parents waiting.** In Troy the parents' grief is still to come. The women at the Scaean gates ask Hector after “their sons and brothers and kinsmen and husbands” (6.239–240). Hector names Hecuba and Priam among those whose sorrow he foresees, though less than his wife's (6.450–451); the Iliad will show it, when they watch his death from the wall and lament him (22.405–436, 24.747–759). And Agamemnon's words at the start of the book go furthest: not even the boy in his mother's womb is to escape (6.58–60).
