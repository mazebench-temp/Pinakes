The book's other uses are prayers, among them the Trojan women's prayer to Athena, refused in the verse in which she granted Diomedes' prayer in Book 5.

**A claim of blood.** Glaucus' answer to Diomedes ends:

{{quote:355eb7a5-a8c1-5675-b8b5-f98c69a34fa6}}

ταύτης τοι γενεῆς τε καὶ αἵματος εὔχομαι εἶναι, “this is the lineage and the blood I claim”. εὔχομαι here is not a boast. It is the formal declaration with which a man states who he is, and the line is a formula: Aeneas ends his own genealogy with it when he meets Achilles (20.241; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Diomedes claims to be “of a good father” in the same way (γένος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 14.113), and Achilles “of the race of great Zeus” (21.187). See [[genee]] and [[fathers-and-sons]].

Diomedes answers with a claim of his own, and makes it public. “Let us exchange armor with each other, so that these men too may know that we claim to be (εὐχόμεθʼ εἶναι) guest-friends from our fathers' time” (6.230–231). A claim made before others binds the man who makes it (see the note on Book 4). The two armies are to see what the two men declare, and the armor is the proof (see [[xenia]]).

**Prayers.** Everywhere else in Book 6 the verb is prayer, and prayer runs through the whole visit to the city. Hector tells the women at the gates “to pray to the gods” (θεοῖς εὔχεσθαι, 6.240), and he himself will not pray to Zeus with bloody hands (εὐχετάασθαι, 6.268; see [[aidos]]). At the temple Theano lays the robe on Athena's knees “and made her prayer to the daughter of great Zeus” (εὐχομένη δʼ ἠρᾶτο, 6.304). The refusal follows at once: “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχομένη, ἀνένευε δὲ Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, 6.311). The verse is built like the one that ends Diomedes' prayer in Book 5: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος· τοῦ δʼ ἔκλυε Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, 5.121). The goddess who heard the Achaean refuses the Trojans in the same words, and the prayer they made was for his death (6.306–307). The women cannot see the refusal: “So they prayed to the daughter of great Zeus”, the narrator goes on (6.312). Last, Hector holds his son and “spoke in prayer (ἐπευξάμενος) to Zeus and the other gods” (6.475). The poem does not say whether this prayer is heard. See [[prayer]], [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].
