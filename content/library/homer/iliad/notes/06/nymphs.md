**A Naiad's sons.** The first nymph of the poem is a mother. Euryalus kills “Aesepus and Pedasus, whom once the nymph Abarbarea, a Naiad, bore to noble Bucolion” (6.21–22). The obituary tells how they came to be born. Bucolion, the eldest son of Laomedon, was born to his mother in secret, and he lay with the nymph “while he was tending his sheep” (6.23–26; see [[abarbarea]], [[bucolion]], [[aesepus-son-of-bucolion]] and [[pedasus-son-of-bucolion]]).

The poem tells such births more than once, and always among the herds of the Troad. Aphrodite bore Aeneas “to Anchises as he tended his cattle” (βουκολέοντι, 5.313), and a Naiad bore Satnius to Enops as he herded cattle by the Satnioeis, with the same word (βουκολέοντι, 14.444–445). Bucolion's own name is formed from βουκόλος, “cowherd”. Aphrodite at least tries to carry her son out of the fighting (5.311–318); the Naiads' sons fall. Of the twins the son of Mecisteus “loosened the strength and the shining limbs” and stripped their armor (6.27–28; see [[euryalus]] and [[the-war-in-small]]).

**Elms for Eetion.** The mountain nymphs come in Andromache's account of her father's death. Achilles killed Eetion but did not strip him, “for his heart held back in awe from that”; he burned him in his armor (6.416–418; see [[eetion]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). The goddesses finish what the killer began:

{{quote:ea9b6b13-cf8a-5ff4-a559-e4af9444b4be}}

ὀρεστιάδες, “of the mountains”, is found only here in Homer, and so is the planting of trees at a grave. The mound (σῆμα) marks the place of the dead man, and the trees make it a grove (see [[tomb]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad elms grow on the banks of the Scamander, where Achilles clutches one in his fight with the river and Hephaestus' fire burns them (21.242, 21.350). Andromache tells of the honor paid to her father in the speech in which she tells Hector that he is now father and mother and brother to her (6.429–430; see [[andromache]]).
