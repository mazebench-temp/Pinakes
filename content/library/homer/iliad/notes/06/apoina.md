**The offer.** Adrastus' horses bolt in terror, catch the chariot in a tamarisk branch and break the pole, and he tumbles out face down in the dust at Menelaus' feet (6.37–44). He clasps Menelaus' knees and begs:

{{quote:a09c4a8d-5d7c-5156-ad44-7bdf2a0389fd}}

The speech is a bargain and nothing more. It gives no reason for pity: only a rich father, an inventory of the treasures in his house (κειμήλια, “stored treasures”, of bronze, gold and worked iron), and the condition, “if he heard that I was alive at the ships”. ζωγρέω, “take alive”, is the verb of a capture made for ransom (ζωός, “alive”, and ἀγρέω, “catch”). The offer works. Menelaus is persuaded and is about to hand Adrastus to his attendant, until Agamemnon runs up and tells him to spare no one, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb” (6.51–60). Agamemnon kills Adrastus himself, and the narrator calls his words αἴσιμα, “what was due” (6.61–65; see [[aisa]], [[supplication]] and [[adrastus-the-trojan]]).

**The same words, refused again.** The lines are a formula. Dolon, caught by Diomedes and Odysseus in the night, offers the same ransom in the same words (10.378–381), and the two sons of Antimachus, thrown from their chariot, beg Agamemnon in Adrastus' verses (11.131–135). Diomedes kills Dolon (10.454–457), and Agamemnon kills the brothers. Agamemnon refused Chryses' ransom in Book 1 (1.24–32); here he refuses Adrastus' for his brother, and in Book 11 he refuses the sons of Antimachus. In the Iliad he is the steadiest voice for killing a captive rather than selling him back (see [[agamemnon]]).

**Before Patroclus.** Andromache tells of a time when ransom was taken. Achilles killed her father and her seven brothers when he sacked Thebe, but her mother, the queen, “he brought here with the rest of the possessions, and then he set her free, taking a boundless ransom” (ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, 6.426–427). The phrase is the one used of Chryses' ransom (1.13). Book 11 recalls that he once caught two sons of Priam on Ida and released them for ransom (11.104–106), and in Book 21 he tells Lycaon that before Patroclus died he liked to spare Trojans, and took many alive and sold them, but now no one will escape (21.99–105). Andromache's mother was freed in the years of ransom, and her freedom did not last: Artemis struck her down in her father's halls (6.428). See [[sack-of-thebe]], [[andromache]] and [[captive-women]].
