---
summary: The translation's “fair-flowing” renders two more river epithets in Book 6, ἐϋρρείτης of the Satnioeis, home of a man Agamemnon kills, and ἐϋρρεής of the river in which the stallion of the simile is used to bathe.
grc: [ἐϋρρείταο, ἐϋρρεῖος]
---

ἐϋρρείτης and ἐϋρρεής (genitive ἐϋρρεῖος) are compounds of εὖ (“well”) and the root of ῥέω (“flow”), like καλλίρροος, and the translation gives all three the same English. Elatus, whom Agamemnon kills, “lived beside the banks of the fair-flowing Satnioeis, in steep Pedasus” (6.34–35; [[satnioeis]], [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]], [[elatus]]). ἐϋρρείτης occurs once more in Homer, of the river of Egypt (Od. 14.257).

The other is the river of the simile that closes the book. Paris runs down from Pergamus like a stalled horse that has broken its halter and gallops across the plain, “used to bathing in the fair-flowing river” (εἰωθὼς λούεσθαι ἐϋρρεῖος ποταμοῖο, 6.506–508; [[the-stallion]]). The same verses describe Hector in Book 15 (Il. 15.263–265). The genitive comes back in a verse used three times of the river of Troy, “when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river, eddying Xanthus” (Il. 14.433–434, 21.1–2, 24.692–693; [[scamander]]). A river named in an obituary and a river in a simile of freedom get the same praise.
