**At the gates.**

{{quote:e1e6f770-9724-596c-b20a-af6b4affeada}}

They ask after “their sons and brothers and kinsmen and husbands” (6.239–240), every man a woman might lose (see [[kasignetos]]). Hector can only tell them to pray, “all of them in turn” (ἑξείης), and the narrator adds what he knows: “sorrows were in store for many” (6.241).

**The old women.** Helenus' plan makes the women the city's suppliants. Hecuba is to gather the old women (γεραιαί) and promise Athena twelve cows, “if she will pity the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (6.87–95; see [[hecuba]]). The line is spoken three times, by Helenus, by Hector and in Theano's prayer (6.95, 6.276, 6.310), and it names what the war puts at stake: Agamemnon has promised the Achaeans that they will “lead away their dear wives and little children” (4.238). At the temple the women lift their hands to Athena “with a loud cry” (ὀλολυγῇ, 6.301), the women's cry at a prayer or a sacrifice (Od. 3.450, 4.767), and the goddess refuses (6.311; see [[athena]], [[theano]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]).

**Shame.** Hector asks the slave women whether Andromache has gone “to the temple of Athena, where the other Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess” (6.379–380), and the housekeeper repeats his words (6.384–385; see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[handmaids]]). In his answer to Andromache they are the women before whom he would be ashamed to hold back: “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes” (6.442; see [[with-trailing-robes]] and [[aidos]]). He says the same line to himself outside the Scaean gates as Achilles comes on (22.105).
