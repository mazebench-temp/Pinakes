**Named with Hector.** In Book 5 Diomedes crushed Aeneas' hip with a stone, and the gods carried him from the field before he came back healed (5.297–318, 5.512–518). Now the seer Helenus sets him beside Hector as one of the two men on whom “most of all rests the toil of the Trojans and the Lycians” (6.77–78; see [[helenus]]), “the best in every undertaking, both in fighting and in counsel” (6.78–79). The pair of virtues is the Iliad's measure of a leader, and Pandarus called Aeneas “counselor of the bronze-armored Trojans” (5.180; see [[counselor]]). The speech treats the two as a team. They are to “stand fast here” and hold the people back before the gates (6.80), and the verb of the next task is dual: “when you two have urged on all the battalions” (ἐποτρύνητον, 6.83).

**Left with the army.** Only Hector is sent to the city (6.86). Aeneas stays on the plain, and the book does not name him again. The same crisis returns in Book 17 in the same words: the Trojans “would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice” (17.319–320 = 6.73–74), and this time it is Aeneas, roused by Apollo, who turns them, and “they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans” (17.343 = 6.106; see [[trojans]]).

Agamemnon's wish in this book is that the whole people of Ilios perish “unmourned and without a trace” (ἄφαντοι, 6.60). In Book 20 Poseidon saves Aeneas from Achilles so that the race of Dardanus may not perish “without seed and without a trace” (ἄφαντος, 20.303): Aeneas and his children's children will rule the Trojans (20.307–308; see [[agamemnon]]).
