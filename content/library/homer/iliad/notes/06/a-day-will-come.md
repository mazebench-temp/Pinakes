summary: Hector speaks Agamemnon's three lines to Andromache. In his mouth the prophecy of Troy's fall is not a threat but a certainty he carries into battle, and it opens a speech built on days, the day of freedom Andromache will lose and the day of slavery he cannot ward off.
passages:
  - 6.447-465 | Hector foresees the fall of Troy
---

{{quote:b9953f06-8c76-5966-8daa-48e14fff186c}}

The lines are Agamemnon's in Book 4, spoken over his wounded brother as a threat against the oath-breakers (4.163–165). Hector says them word for word to his wife, and the same words change sides. Agamemnon knew that Zeus would punish Troy; Hector knows that his city will fall, and says so to the woman who has just begged him to stay on the wall (6.431). He does not use the knowledge to stay. He has just told her that shame will not let him hold back (6.441–446), and the certainty of defeat comes after it, as one more thing he knows “in my mind and in my heart” (see [[aidos]] and [[thymos]]).

What follows is a list of griefs: the Trojans, Hecuba, Priam, his brothers, and above all Andromache (6.450–455). J. T. Kakridis called this order the “ascending scale of affection” and compared the story of Meleager in Book 9, who refuses the elders, the priests, his father, his sisters and mother and his companions, and yields only to his wife when she tells him what happens to the people of a captured city (9.574–596; J. T. Kakridis, *Homeric Researches*, 1949). The speech is also built on days. After ἔσσεται ἦμαρ, “a day will come”, it names two more: the day of freedom that an Achaean will take from her (ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, 6.455) and the day of slavery that Hector will not be there to ward off (δούλιον ἦμαρ, 6.463).
