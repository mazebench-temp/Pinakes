summary: Andromache's first appearance. Hector finds her gone to the tower; she meets him at the Scaean gates with their son, begs him to stay, since Achilles has left her no one but him, and hears him foretell her slavery. She goes home weeping and leads her handmaids in mourning him while he lives.

**Absent.** She enters the poem as an absence. Hector goes home to see “my household and my dear wife and my infant son” (6.366), and the house is without her. The housekeeper tells him that she heard the Trojans were hard pressed and has gone to the great tower, “like a woman out of her mind” (μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα, 6.389; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). In Book 22 she will run to the wall again, “like a maenad” (μαινάδι ἴση, 22.460), and see him dragged away dead.

**The plea.** She meets him at the Scaean gates, running, with the nurse and the child (6.394–403; see [[richly-dowered]] and [[astyanax]]). She takes his hand and reproaches him: his own might will destroy him, and she will soon be his widow (6.405–413; see [[menos]] and [[chere]]). Then she tells her history. Achilles killed her father and her seven brothers on one day, and took her mother captive and let her go for ransom (6.414–428; see [[eetion]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). So Hector must be all of them:

{{quote:a07873ef-41d8-55e7-8356-abd8df4d97d6}}

One formula runs through the scene. She has “no father and no lady mother” (6.413); Hector is “father to me, and lady mother” (6.429); and when their son cries at the helmet, “his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (6.471). θαλερὸς παρακοίτης, “my husband in his prime”, occurs only here; in Book 3 Hector told Paris that he had taken the “flourishing wife” (θαλερὴν παράκοιτιν) of Menelaus (3.53; see [[husbands-and-wives]]).

She ends with a soldier's advice: to post the army by the wild fig tree, where the wall can be scaled and the best of the Achaeans have tried it three times (6.433–439; see [[the-fig-tree]]). Aristarchus rejected these lines as unfit for her to speak, and Hector's answer does not take them up (see [[andromaches-advice]]).

**The answer.** Hector gives her the future she fears (6.441–465; see [[hector]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]). Some Achaean will lead her away in tears, “taking away your day of freedom” (6.455; see [[eleutheros]]); she will weave at another woman's loom in Argos and carry water from its springs (6.456–458; see [[messeis]] and [[weaving]]); and a stranger who sees her weep will say, “This is the wife of Hector” (6.460). Her husband's fame will be spoken over her slavery (see [[kleos]]).

**Laughter and tears.**

{{quote:fb59cb06-b196-5840-9c4e-121528ed5cd8}}

He sends her home to “the loom and the distaff” (6.491), the work he has just imagined her doing as a slave. She goes, “turning back again and again” (ἐντροπαλιζομένη, 6.496), the word used of Ajax and of Menelaus giving ground before the enemy, turning to face them as they go (11.547, 17.109). At home she rouses her handmaids to lament, and they mourn Hector while he is still alive (6.497–502; see [[handmaids]], [[lament]] and [[tears]]).
