---
summary: In Book 6 persuasion decides lives. Adrastus begins to win Menelaus over and Agamemnon talks him out of it; Anteia cannot persuade Bellerophon, and Bellerophon obeys the gods' portents; Helen persuades Paris back to the war, and cannot persuade Hector to sit; and Hector does not disobey his brother Helenus.
grc: [ἔπειθε, πεῖθʼ, παρειπών, παρειποῦσʼ]
en: [win over, persuading]
passages:
  - 6.45-65 | Adrastus begs for his life
  - 6.337-341 | Victory shifts from man to man
  - 6.360-368 | Hector answers Helen
---

**Persuaded and dissuaded.** Adrastus' plea works at first: “So he spoke, and he began to win over the heart in his breast” (τῷ δʼ ἄρα θυμὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἔπειθε, 6.51). The imperfect is exact; Menelaus is being persuaded, and is about to hand the captive to his attendant, when Agamemnon runs up and “turned his brother's mind” (6.61), “urging what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών, 6.62). παρειπεῖν is to talk a man round. The same verb is Paris' word for Helen's persuasion: “just now my wife, persuading me with gentle words (παρειποῦσʼ … μαλακοῖς ἐπέεσσιν), spurred me to the war” (6.337–338). The brother talks a man into a killing, the wife talks a man into a fight. See [[aisa]], [[apoina]] and [[paris]].

**Not persuaded.** Anteia “could not persuade wise-hearted Bellerophon, for his thoughts were good” (οὔ τι πεῖθʼ, 6.161–162), and her lie follows (see [[anteia]]). Hector refuses each woman who would keep him in the city, and says so to Helen in the verb itself: “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me; you will not persuade me” (οὐδέ με πείσεις, 6.360). Patroclus will refuse Nestor's offer of a seat with the same half-line, οὐδέ με πείσεις (11.648; see [[visit-scene]]).

**Obedience.** Verbs of the same family mean obedience. “Hector did not disobey his brother” (οὔ τι κασιγνήτῳ ἀπίθησεν, 6.102) when Helenus sent him to the city (see [[helenus]] and [[kasignetos]]), and Bellerophon killed the Chimaera “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 6.183; see [[teras]]).

**Trust.** The perfect πέποιθα is “trust”. Paris runs through the city “trusting in his swift feet” (ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι πεποιθώς, 6.505), and the stallion he is likened to gallops “trusting in his splendor” (ἀγλαΐηφι πεποιθώς, 6.510; see [[the-stallion]]).
