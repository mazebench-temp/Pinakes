---
summary: Hades is named three times in Book 6, each time as the end of a life. Hector wishes he could see Paris go down into his house, Andromache's seven brothers all went there on a single day, and Hector tells Andromache that no man will hurl him to Hades beyond his portion.
passages:
  - 6.281-285 | Hector's wish for Paris
  - 6.421-424 | Andromache's brothers
---

**A wish.** Hector, angry with Paris, wishes that the earth would gape for him: “If I could see him going down into the house of Hades, I would think my heart had forgotten its joyless misery” (6.284–285; see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[paris]]). Ἄϊδος εἴσω is short for the fuller δόμον Ἄϊδος εἴσω, “into the house of Hades”. The armies used the full phrase when they prayed at the casting of the lots that the man who began the war might go down there (3.320–323).

**A lament.** Andromache's seven brothers “all went down on a single day into the house of Hades”, killed by Achilles among their cattle and their sheep (6.421–424; see [[andromache]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). ἰῷ here is “one”, from ἴος, the old word for “one”, and not ἰός, “arrow”.

**A comfort.** Hector's last words to his wife begin with the god: “for no man will hurl me down to Hades beyond my portion” (6.487). προϊάπτω, “hurl forth”, is the verb of the proem, where the wrath of Achilles hurled many mighty souls to Hades (1.3), and of Zeus as he is about to send many mighty heads there (11.53–55). When Poseidon warns Aeneas to give way before Achilles, it is so that he may not go to the house of Hades “beyond his portion” (20.336). Hector denies that this can happen to him (see [[aisa]], [[moira]] and [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]).

The book also speaks of death without naming the god. Calesius and his master Axylus “went down beneath the earth” (6.18–19), Andromache would rather “go down beneath the earth” than lose Hector (6.410–411), and Hector would rather be dead, “and the heaped earth cover me”, than hear her cries as she is dragged away (6.464–465).
