summary: Dionysus first appears in Homer in the story Diomedes tells Glaucus. Lycurgus chased the nurses of “raving Dionysus” down the Nysean mount; the god fled in terror into the sea, where Thetis took him in; and Zeus blinded the man who had attacked him.

**The warning.** Diomedes, facing a warrior he has never seen, asks whether he is one of the immortals: “I would not fight with the gods of heaven” (6.128–129). Then he gives his reason:

{{quote:5969c907-d704-59a1-af96-11db2ca6c422}}

It is the only story of Dionysus in Homer, and it is told as a warning (see [[mythological-paradigm]], [[lycurgus]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). Dione said the same of Diomedes himself in Book 5: “the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). That day he wounded Aphrodite and Ares with Athena beside him. Alone before a stranger, he will not risk a god (see [[diomedes]]).

**The god.** The picture already has the marks of his later cult. His epithet is μαινόμενος, “raving” (6.132). His nurses (τιθῆναι) are on a holy mountain, the Nysean mount (ἠγάθεον Νυσήϊον, 6.133; see [[nysa]]). They let fall their θύσθλα, the sacred things of the rite, usually taken to be the thyrsi; the word occurs only here (6.134). Lycurgus strikes them with a βουπλήξ, an ox-goad or an axe for felling oxen (6.135). The god is a child in the care of women on a mountain, the scene that later poets give to his maenads.

**The flight.** Here a god runs from a man. Dionysus “plunged beneath the wave of the sea” in fear, for “a strong trembling had hold of him at the man's shouting” (6.136–137). No other god in the Iliad is so afraid of a mortal. Thetis takes him “to her bosom” (ὑπεδέξατο κόλπῳ), the words Hephaestus uses of Thetis and Eurynome, who caught him when Hera threw him from heaven (18.398; see [[thetis]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]).

**The punishment.** Dionysus does not avenge himself. “The gods who live at ease were angry with him, and the son of Cronus made him blind” (6.138–139), and Lycurgus did not live long, since he was “hated by all the immortal gods” (6.140; see [[olympians]] and [[zeus]]). In later versions the god punishes the king himself, with madness (Apollodorus 3.5.1) or with a prison of rock (Sophocles, *Antigone* 955–965). In Homer the gods act for him together, and Zeus does the work. The same words begin Bellerophon's fall in Glaucus' answer: he “was hated by all the gods” (6.200; see [[bellerophon]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**Raving and a nurse.** Book 6 has one more of each. Andromache hurries to the wall “like a woman out of her mind” (μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα), and a nurse carries her child (6.389). In Book 22, hearing the wailing for Hector, she runs from the house “like a maenad” (μαινάδι ἴση, 22.460), the only maenad in Homer (see [[andromache]]).
