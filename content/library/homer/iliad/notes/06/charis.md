**The loveliest robe.** Helenus tells Hector what the women must do: Hecuba is to take the robe “that seems to her the loveliest (χαριέστατος) and largest in her hall, and by far the dearest to her own heart”, and lay it on Athena's knees (6.90–92). Hector repeats the words to his mother (6.271–273). χαρίεις, “pleasing, lovely”, is the adjective of χάρις: the robe is chosen to please, and a gift that pleases lays on the one who receives it the obligation of a return. Hecuba goes down to her fragrant chamber and chooses among the robes that Sidonian women made, “the one that was most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest, and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others” (6.294–295). The return asked for is plain: pity for the city, the wives and the children, and the death of Diomedes (6.305–310). “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). The robe lies on the goddess's knees, and the favor it was meant to win is refused. Book 2 showed a god taking the gift and refusing the prayer: Zeus accepted Agamemnon's sacrifice and would not grant what he asked (2.419–420). See [[the-robe-for-athena]], [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[robe]].

**Gladly given.** Adrastus, clasping Menelaus' knees, promises that from the treasures in his father's house “my father would gladly give you a boundless ransom” (τῶν κέν τοι χαρίσαιτο πατὴρ ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, 6.49). χαρίζομαι is to give as a favor, freely and with goodwill, and the ransom is offered in that language. It is refused as well: Agamemnon kills him (6.53–65; see [[apoina]]). Both offers made in the book to win a favor fail, one to a goddess and one to a man.
