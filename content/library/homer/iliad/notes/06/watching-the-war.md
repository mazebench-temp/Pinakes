**On the tower.** When Hector comes home, Andromache is not there. The housekeeper tells him where she has gone: “she has gone to the great tower of Ilios, because she heard that the Trojans were hard pressed, and the Achaeans had great strength. She has gone hurrying to the wall like a woman out of her mind” (6.386–389; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]] and [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]). She has gone to see the battle, as Helen went to the wall in Book 3 and as she herself will run to it in Book 22, to see Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot (3.141–145, 22.460–465).

**What a watcher knows.** Her plea ends with advice about the wall. Hector should post the army by the wild fig tree, “where most of all the city can be scaled and the wall is open to assault. For three times the best of them have come there and made trial of it”, the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus, the sons of Atreus and Diomedes (6.433–437). She knows the weak place and the names of the men who tried it, and she guesses why: a seer told them, or their own hearts (6.438–439). Ancient editors thought the knowledge unfitting for her and rejected the lines (see [[andromaches-advice]] and [[the-fig-tree]]). Hector passes it over and sends her home (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]).

**Those who cannot see.** At the Scaean gates the Trojan women run to Hector “asking after their sons and brothers and kinsmen and husbands” (6.238–240). They are inside the walls, and the man from the field is their news (see [[trojan-women]]).

**Seen by the armies.** On the plain the fighting has its own onlookers. When the Trojans rally, the Argives give ground and think “that one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky to bring help to the Trojans, so sharply did they rally” (6.107–109). Diomedes proposes the exchange of armor “so that these men too may know that we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time” (6.230–231): the bond is made before both armies, as a thing to be seen (see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]).
