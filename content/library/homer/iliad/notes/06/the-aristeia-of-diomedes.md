summary: Diomedes' great day runs on into Book 6 and ends there. He kills Axylus, Helenus names him the strongest of the Achaeans, and the Trojan women pray to Athena to break his spear; then he meets Glaucus between the armies, will not fight a god, and exchanges armor with a guest-friend.

**Axylus.** In the battle that the gods have left to the armies, Diomedes kills Axylus of Arisbe, a rich man who lived in a house beside the road and welcomed all who passed, and his charioteer Calesius with him (6.12–19; see [[axylus]] and [[calesius]]). None of the men he had entertained was there to save him.

**Feared.** The Trojans are giving way, and Helenus tells Hector why they must pray to Athena:

{{quote:2077b143-18a4-50f7-b542-bb34d47335cd}}

In Book 5 Pandarus and Dione called Diomedes “the best of the Achaeans” (5.103, 5.414). Now a Trojan seer puts him above Achilles, and the Trojans take him to be the danger to their city (see [[best-of-the-achaeans]] and [[helenus]]). The whole rite of the robe is aimed at him. Helenus asks that Athena keep him from the city (6.96); Theano, laying the robe on the goddess's knees, asks her to break his spear and let him fall headlong before the Scaean gates (6.305–307). They pray against him to the goddess who has fought at his side, and she refuses (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]).

**The end.** The day ends in a meeting, not a killing. Diomedes challenges Glaucus between the armies and asks first whether the stranger is a god, though Athena gave him the sight to tell god from man (5.127–128). He tells the story of Lycurgus and says he would not fight the gods of heaven (6.119–143; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). The man who wounded Aphrodite and Ares the same day now keeps to Apollo's warning (5.440–442). When Glaucus' lineage shows them to be guest-friends, he fixes his spear in the ground, and the two men clasp hands and exchange armor, gold for bronze (6.212–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). The aristeia ends there (see [[the-aristeia]]).

**The name.** Herodotus quotes four lines of Book 6, on the robes from Sidon, as standing “in the aristeia of Diomedes” (Herodotus 2.116, on 6.289–292). For him the episode of that name ran on past Diomedes' meeting with Glaucus into the Trojan women's rite, which was performed to stop him (see [[title-of-book-5]] and [[title-of-book-6]]).
