---
summary: Glaucus' answer to Diomedes turns on the word. He asks why Diomedes wants his lineage, compares the generations of men to leaves, and then tells his lineage and claims it. The book also uses it of Bucolion, the eldest son of Laomedon by birth, and of the Chimaera, “of divine race, not of men”.
passages:
  - 6.145-151 | Glaucus answers Diomedes
  - 6.206-211 | Glaucus' lineage
---

**Why do you ask?** Diomedes has asked the stranger who he is, and whether he is a god (6.123–143). Glaucus answers with a question: “Great-hearted son of Tydeus, why do you ask about my lineage?” (τί ἢ γενεὴν ἐρεείνεις, 6.145). The next line takes the same word in its widest sense: “As is the generation of leaves, so is that of men” (6.146). A lineage is one generation after another, and each generation falls like the leaves of a year (6.146–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]).

**Our lineage.** Then he tells it anyway: “if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well our lineage (ἡμετέρην γενεήν), and many men know it” (6.150–151). The family is known; it has κλέος (see [[kleos]]). Sixty lines later, after Sisyphus, Glaucus son of Sisyphus, Bellerophon and Hippolochus, he closes the account with the same word: “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (ταύτης τοι γενεῆς τε καὶ αἵματος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 6.211; see [[euchomai]]). His father's charge, which he quotes just before, is “not to shame the race of my fathers” (γένος πατέρων, 6.209; see [[aischros]] and [[always-to-be-the-best]]). The word frames the whole speech, and it decides the meeting: the lineage shows Diomedes that their grandfathers were guest-friends (6.215–231; see [[xenia]]). Aeneas will use the same verses of his own lineage when he faces Achilles in Book 20 (20.213–214, 20.241; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**Birth and race.** Two other uses in the book are the plain ones. Bucolion was “the eldest by birth” (πρεσβύτατος γενεῇ) among the sons of Laomedon, though his mother bore him in secret (6.23–24; see [[bucolion]] and [[nothos]]), and the Chimaera “was of divine race, not of men” (θεῖον γένος οὐδʼ ἀνθρώπων, 6.180; see [[chimaera]]).
