**Nestor.** “Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares” (6.67) is the address with which Agamemnon opened his test of the army in Book 2, and Ajax and Agamemnon use it again (2.110, 15.733, 19.78). The command is one of discipline: let no one stay behind to load himself with spoils, but kill men, and strip the dead later at their ease (6.68–71; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[nestor]]). The closing verse, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (6.72), closed Ares' exhortation of the sons of Priam in Book 5 (5.470).

**Helenus' orders.** Helenus tells Aeneas and Hector to hold the army before the gates, “ranging everywhere, before they fall into the arms of their women in flight, and become a joy to their enemies” (6.80–82). The reason is shame, before their own women and before the enemy (see [[helenus]] and [[aidos]]).

**The rally.** Hector obeys at once. He leaps from his chariot and goes through the army brandishing his sharp spears, urging them to fight, “and he awoke the dread din of battle” (6.103–105). The three verses are those of his rally after Sarpedon's rebuke in Book 5, and they return in Book 11 (5.494–496, 11.211–213). So does the verse of the result, “And they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans” (6.106 = 5.497, 11.214, 17.343). The Argives give ground, thinking that one of the immortals has come down from the starry sky to help the Trojans, “so sharply did they rally” (6.107–109; see [[divine-intervention]]).

**Be men.** Then Hector calls out to them, “shouting aloud” (ἐκέκλετο μακρὸν ἀΰσας, 6.110), as Nestor did (6.66):

“High-hearted Trojans and far-famed allies, be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage, while I go up to Ilios and tell the elders who sit in council, and our wives, to pray to the gods and promise them hecatombs” (6.111–115).

The middle verse is a whole-line exhortation, ἀνέρες ἔστε φίλοι, μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς. It returns six times in the poem, four times from Hector and once each from Ajax and Patroclus (6.112; 8.174, 11.287, 15.487, 17.185; 15.734, 16.270; see [[alke]]). The reason he gives is his own absence. They must hold without him while he goes to set the city praying (see [[prayer]] and [[trojan-elders]]). The sequence returns in Book 17, where Hector tells the Trojans and their allies to be men and remember their furious courage “while I put on the armor of noble Achilles”, and the same verse takes him away, “So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet went away” (17.183–188; 6.116; see [[so-he-spoke]]).
