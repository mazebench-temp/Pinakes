summary: Hector goes to tell the Trojan elders and wives to pray and promise the gods hecatombs (6.113–115); the offering vowed to Athena is twelve yearling cows. The word's own measure, a hundred oxen, reckons the worth of Glaucus' golden armor (6.236).
passages:
  - 6.110-115 | Hector sends the Trojans' prayers ahead
---

**Promised.** Hector tells the Trojans to hold fast while he goes up to Ilios to tell the elders and the wives “to pray to the gods and promise them hecatombs” (6.113–115). A hecatomb is the offering of a crisis, vowed now and paid when the god has helped. The offering that the women actually vow to Athena is precise: twelve yearling cows, to be sacrificed in her temple if she will pity the city (6.93–94, 6.308–309; see [[sacrifice]] and [[prayer]]). Twelve cows make a hecatomb in Homer's sense, a great sacrifice, not a hundred beasts (see [[hecatomb]]).

**A hundred oxen.** The count survives in a compound. Glaucus gave Diomedes gold armor for bronze, ἑκατόμβοιʼ ἐννεαβοίων, “the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine” (6.236). ἑκατόμβοιος, “worth a hundred oxen”, is formed like ἑκατόμβη, from ἑκατόν, “hundred”, and βοῦς, “ox”, and Homer reckons value in oxen. Each of the hundred golden tassels of the aegis is worth a hundred oxen (2.448–449), Lycaon fetched a hundred oxen's worth when Achilles sold him on Lemnos (21.78–79), and at the funeral games a great tripod is valued at twelve oxen and a skilled slave woman at four (23.702–705). ἐννεάβοιος, “worth nine oxen”, occurs only here.
