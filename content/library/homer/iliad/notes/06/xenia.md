**A host.** The first life that Book 6 tells is a host's. Diomedes kills Axylus of Arisbe, “wealthy in substance, and he was a friend to all men; for he lived in a house beside the road and gave welcome to all” (φίλος δʼ ἦν ἀνθρώποισι· πάντας γὰρ φιλέεσκεν, 6.14–15). φιλέω is the verb of hospitality (see [[philos]]), and a house by the road is one that every traveler reaches. None of the men he had welcomed was there to stand in front of him (6.16–17). See [[axylus]] and [[death-in-battle]].

**A guest and his message.** In Glaucus' story Bellerophon comes to Lycia carrying the signs that are meant to kill him. The king receives him first as a guest: “the lord of wide Lycia honored him with a ready heart; nine days he entertained him and sacrificed nine oxen” (6.173–174). Only on the tenth day does he ask to see the sign (6.175–177). The order is the rule of Homeric hospitality, food before questions (compare Telemachus' welcome, *Odyssey* 1.123–124, and Nestor's, *Odyssey* 3.69–70). Then the king sets his guest tasks that should be the death of him (6.179–190), as Proetus, who “shrank from killing him”, had sent him away with the message (6.167–170; see [[sebas]]). Neither king kills the man with his own hands. See [[bellerophon]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]].

**Guest-friends from our fathers' time.** Diomedes has asked the stranger whether he is a god (6.123–143), and Glaucus has answered with six generations of his family (6.145–211; see [[genee]]). At the name of Bellerophon Diomedes is glad. He plants his spear in the earth and speaks “with soothing words”:

{{quote:dd780a31-cd41-5b0b-a53a-8faf38d3930d}}

A ξεῖνος πατρώϊος, a guest-friend “from our fathers' time”, is a bond that is inherited, as land is. The grandfathers made it with a stay of twenty days and with gifts (ξεινήϊα; see [[guest-gifts]]): a belt bright with purple and a two-handled cup of gold. The cup is still in Diomedes' house, and it is his proof. He cannot remember his own father, who died at Thebes when he was little (6.222–223), but he has his grandfather's guest-gift. The bond makes each man the other's host in his own country: “your dear guest-friend in the heart of Argos, and you are mine in Lycia, whenever I come to that land” (6.224–225).

{{quote:a5895b06-011b-5f8c-a672-2c7bc462c45c}}

The bond does not stop the war. Each man still has enemies enough to kill (6.227–229); guest-friendship only takes these two out of each other's reach. The exchange of armor makes it public, “so that these men too may know”, and the two leap from their chariots, take each other's hands and pledge their faith (6.230–233). The narrator then says that Zeus took away Glaucus' wits, for he gave gold for bronze (6.234–236; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[phrenes]]).

**Two hosts.** The same Diomedes who kills Axylus at the start of the book spares Glaucus in the middle of it. Axylus welcomed all men, and none of them was there for him; Glaucus is spared for a welcome given two generations before. The scene is the Iliad's classic example of the hereditary guest-friendship that bound families across the lines of peoples and cities (see Gabriel Herman, *Ritualised Friendship and the Greek City*, 1987). See [[glaucus]], [[diomedes]], [[oeneus]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]].
