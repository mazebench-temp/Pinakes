**The best and the dearest.** Helenus tells Hecuba to choose the robe “that seems to her the loveliest and largest in her hall, and by far the dearest to her own heart” (6.90–91), and Hector repeats the words (6.271–272). A gift to a god must cost the giver something. The robe is to be the finest in the house and the one its owner would least like to lose (see [[doron]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Book 6 calls it by the word Book 5 used for the goddesses' robes, πέπλος (5.315, 5.734; see [[robe]]).

**The fragrant chamber.** “But she herself went down into the fragrant chamber” (αὐτὴ δʼ ἐς θάλαμον κατεβήσετο κηώεντα, 6.288). The verse is a formula for going to the treasure room. Priam goes down to it to fetch the ransom for Hector, and takes twelve fine robes from his chests (24.191, 24.228–229); Menelaus goes down to his for a parting gift for Telemachus (Od. 15.99; see [[house]]).

**From Sidon.** The robes are “the work of women of the Sidonians, whom godlike Alexander himself had brought from Sidon”, “on that voyage on which he brought back Helen” (6.289–292; see [[sidonians]], [[sidon]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). τάς, “whom”, is feminine: Alexander brought the women themselves, and the poem does not say whether as captives or as purchased slaves. The gift the Trojans offer Athena comes from the voyage that began the war.

**The robe that shone like a star.**

{{quote:28c616d6-599e-5f33-90c4-6e5b3338e815}}

ποίκιλμα, “embroidery”, is work woven in colors or figures, like the battles Helen wove into her web (3.125–128; see [[weaving]]). The finest robe lies at the bottom of the chest, under all the others. The Odyssey uses the same two verses for the robe Helen gives Telemachus when he leaves Sparta, for his bride to wear on their wedding day. That robe is one she made herself (Od. 15.104–108, 15.123–128); Hecuba's is the work of foreign women (see [[like-a-star]] and [[the-autumn-star]]).

**On the knees.** Theano takes the robe and lays it on the knees of the goddess's image (6.302–303), and Athena refuses the prayer that goes with it (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]], [[temple]] and [[athena]]). The scene has often been compared with the robe brought to Athena at the Panathenaea in Athens (see [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]]). The Iliad's robes end at Hector's burial. His bones are laid in a golden chest and covered with soft purple robes (24.795–796).
