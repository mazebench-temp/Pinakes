summary: Book 6 sets the two marriages of Troy side by side. Hector finds Paris with his armor and Helen with her women, and Helen wishes she were the wife of a better man; then he meets Andromache at the Scaean gates, and their parting is the Iliad's fullest scene of husband and wife. Glaucus' story adds a wife's false charge and a king who rewards a hero with his daughter.

**The city's marriages.** Inside the walls every house is a marriage. The Trojan women run to Hector at the gate asking after their husbands among the rest (6.238–240). In Priam's palace his sons sleep “beside their wedded wives” (μνηστῇς ἀλόχοισι, wives wooed and won) and his sons-in-law “beside their modest wives” (6.246, 6.250; see [[priams-palace]] and [[in-laws]]). Theano, who opens Athena's temple, is named as “the wife of Antenor” (6.299; see [[theano]]).

**Paris and Helen.** Hector finds Paris in his chamber busy with his armor and his bow, and Helen sitting among her slave women, directing their work (6.321–324). It is Helen who has sent him back to the war: “just now my wife, persuading me with gentle words, spurred me to the war” (6.337–338). Her own words to Hector are about the marriage. She wishes she had died on the day she was born, and then that she were “the wife of a better man, one who felt the blame and the many reproaches of men” (6.345–351; see [[nemesis]]). In Book 3 she wished Paris dead; here she judges him and ties her fate to his, for Zeus has set an evil fate on the two of them, so that they may be a song for men to come (6.357–358; see [[song-and-fame]]). See [[helen]] and [[paris]].

**Hector and Andromache.** Hector goes home to see his wife and son and does not find them (6.365–389). He meets her at the Scaean gates as he is about to go out: “there his richly dowered wife came running to meet him” (6.394). She comes as his mother came, “to meet him” (ἐναντίη, 6.251, 6.394), and like his mother she clings to his hand and calls him by name (6.253 = 6.406). What follows is the longest conversation between a mortal husband and wife in the Iliad. She speaks first, with a charge, a history and a plea: his own might will destroy him; Achilles killed her father and her brothers; he is father, mother, brother and husband to her; let him stay (6.407–439; see [[andromache]] and [[chere]]). He answers as a partner, “All these things are on my mind too, lady” (6.441), and tells her what she means to him: the sorrow of the Trojans, of Hecuba and Priam and his brothers, troubles him less than hers, when she is led into slavery (6.450–465; see [[a-day-will-come]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Then the child is frightened by his helmet, and the two of them laugh together, the only laughter they share (6.466–471; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]). He prays for the boy, gives him back, pities her as she laughs through her tears, and sends her home with comfort and a division: fate for all men, war for men (6.476–493; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]).

ἐντροπαλιζομένη, “turning back again and again”, is her last look at him. They do not meet again in the poem. Paris finds Hector just as he turns from the place “where he had talked intimately with his wife” (6.516; see [[oaristys]]). The next time Andromache sees him he is being dragged behind Achilles' chariot (22.462–465). See [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[the-city-and-the-household]].

**A wife's lie.** Glaucus' story has a marriage of another kind. Anteia, the wife of Proetus, is mad with desire for Bellerophon, and when he refuses her she tells her husband, “Either die, Proetus, or kill Bellerophon, who wanted to lie with me in love against my will” (6.160–165). Proetus believes her. The story of a rejected wife who accuses the young man who refused her has often been compared with Joseph and Potiphar's wife (Genesis 39.7–20) and with Phaedra and Hippolytus (Euripides, *Hippolytus*). At the end the Lycian king gives Bellerophon his own daughter and half his royal honor (6.191–193). See [[anteia]], [[proetus]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]].
