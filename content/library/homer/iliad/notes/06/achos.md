Four uses of the word family mark four griefs in Troy.

**Paris' grief.** Rebuked for sitting apart, Paris answers that it was not anger at the Trojans: “I wanted to give myself up to grief” (ἔθελον δʼ ἄχεϊ προτραπέσθαι, 6.336). He does not name the grief, and the defeat of Book 3 is the likeliest cause. Grief in Homer is close to anger, and Paris sets the one against the other (see [[nemesis]], [[cholos]] and [[paris]]).

**Andromache's grief.** Andromache tells Hector what his death will leave her: “there will be no other comfort for me, when you have met your fate, only grief” (ἀλλʼ ἄχεʼ, 6.411–413). Her father, mother and brothers are dead; ἄχεα, “griefs”, are all that will remain (see [[chere]] and [[andromache]]).

**Hector's comfort.** At the parting Hector strokes her with his hand and begins, “Strange one, do not grieve too much in your heart for me” (μή μοί τι λίην ἀκαχίζεο θυμῷ, 6.486). ἀκαχίζω, “grieve, be distressed”, is from the same root. The comfort that follows is fate: no man will send him to Hades beyond his portion (6.487–489; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]).

**Hector's grief.** His last words to Paris end with his own distress: “my heart grieves in my spirit (ἄχνυται ἐν θυμῷ) when I hear shameful things said of you by the Trojans” (6.523–525). The grief of a brother is at what others say (see [[aischros]] and [[praise-and-blame]]).
