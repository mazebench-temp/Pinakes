---
summary: Book 6 adds the adjective ἐπουράνιος, “of heaven,” in Diomedes' refusal to fight the gods, in the story of Lycurgus, who fought them, and in Hector's hope of a thank-offering for freedom.
grc: [θεοῖσιν ἐπουρανίοισι, θεοῖσιν ἐπουρανίοισιν, ἐπουρανίοισι θεοῖς]
en: [gods of heaven]
---

ἐπουράνιος = ἐπί (“on, in”) + οὐρανός (“sky, heaven”): “in heaven, heavenly,” the same idea as Οὐρανίωνες with another word. Homer uses it four times, three of them in Book 6. Diomedes will not fight a god: “I would not fight with the gods of heaven” (6.129), for Lycurgus, “who strove with the gods of heaven,” did not live long (6.131; [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]], [[who-fights-the-gods]]). The fourth use belongs to the same question, whether a stranger may be a god: when Antinous strikes the disguised Odysseus, another suitor warns him that the beggar may be “some god of heaven,” since the gods go about the cities in the likeness of strangers (Od. 17.483–487; [[good-sir]]).

The last use in the book is Hector's. He tells Paris they will make things right later, “if ever Zeus grants us, for the everlasting gods of heaven, to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls” (6.526–528; [[gods-who-live-forever]], [[mixing-bowl]], [[eleutheros]]).
