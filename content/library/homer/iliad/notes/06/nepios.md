---
summary: In Book 6 νήπιος has only its first sense, the small child. The women pray for Troy's little children, and Hector's son is “an infant of tender mind, quite small”; the same words return in Andromache's laments, when the child has lost his father.
passages:
  - 6.399-403 | Astyanax
---

**Little children.** The prayer that Helenus prescribes, that Hector repeats and that Theano speaks asks Athena to pity “the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (νήπια τέκνα, 6.95, 6.276, 6.310). The phrase names those who cannot fight and will be taken if the city falls; Agamemnon promised his army “their dear wives and little children” (4.238; see [[captive-women]] and [[the-city-and-the-household]]).

**Hector's son.** Hector goes home to see “my household and my dear wife and my infant son” (νήπιον υἱόν, 6.366). At the Scaean gates the handmaid carries him, “an infant of tender mind, quite small” (ἀταλάφρονα νήπιον αὔτως, 6.400; see [[of-tender-mind]]), and Andromache's plea begins with him: “you have no pity for your infant son (παῖδά τε νηπίαχον) or for me” (6.407–408). See [[astyanax]].

**The same words in mourning.** νήπιος αὔτως, “an infant, still quite small”, returns twice in Andromache's laments, when Hector is dead and the child is left: “and the boy is still an infant, whom you and I bore, ill-fated both” (22.484–485, 24.726–727). Book 22 also gives the word its other sense, in the narrator's voice. While Hector lies dead on the plain, Andromache is at her loom ordering a bath for him, “the fool (νηπίη), who did not know” (22.445–446). See [[chere]] and [[andromache]].
