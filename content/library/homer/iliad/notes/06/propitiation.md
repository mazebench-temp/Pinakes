ἱλάσκονται, “are appeasing”, is the verb of making a god ἵλαος, “gracious”. Hector uses it when he asks the slave women where his wife has gone: to his sisters, to his brothers' wives, or to Athena's temple, “where the other Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess” (6.378–380). The housekeeper answers him with the same verses turned negative: she has gone to none of them (6.383–385; see [[message-repeated]] and [[handmaids]]). The two verses fix what the women of Troy are doing while Hector goes through the city.

The listener knows that it is in vain. Athena has already shaken her head at Theano's prayer (6.311), and the narrator left the women praying (6.312; see [[prayer]] and [[athena]]). In Book 1 the Achaeans appeased Apollo with a hecatomb and a day of song, and he was glad to hear it (1.472–474; see [[paean]]). Here the offering was made and refused.

δεινὴ θεός, “dread goddess”, occurs in the Iliad only in these two verses. The Odyssey uses the phrase for Circe and Calypso, and once for Athena (Od. 7.41), and joins it there to ἐϋπλόκαμος, “with lovely tresses”, of the goddess herself: Κίρκη ἐυπλόκαμος, δεινὴ θεός (Od. 10.136). Here the lovely tresses belong to the Trojan women, and the dread to the goddess they are trying to appease (see [[trojan-women]]).
