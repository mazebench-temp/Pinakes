---
summary: Hector calls on the “far-famed allies” with the Trojans as he leaves for the city (6.111), and Diomedes tells Glaucus that there are “many Trojans and famous allies” for him to kill (6.227). Several of the men killed in the list that opens the book came to Troy from elsewhere: the Thracian leader Acamas, Axylus of Arisbe, Pidytes of Percote and Elatus of Pedasus.
---

**In the list.** Of the fourteen men killed at the start of the book, several came to the war from outside the city: Acamas, the leader of the Thracians (6.5–11), Axylus of Arisbe and Pidytes of Percote, from the towns near the Hellespont that Asius led (6.12–19, 6.30; 2.835–839), and Elatus of Pedasus on the Satnioeis, the town of the Leleges (6.33–35; 21.86–87). See [[acamas-of-thrace]], [[axylus]], [[pidytes]] and [[elatus]].

**Far-famed.** Hector's address, Τρῶες ὑπέρθυμοι τηλεκλειτοί τʼ ἐπίκουροι, “High-hearted Trojans and far-famed allies” (6.111), is a formula that returns for the army camped on the plain (9.233) and at the Achaean wall (12.108). τηλεκλειτός is τῆλε (“far”) + κλειτός (“famed”): famous far away, a fit word for men who came from far away. Diomedes uses a shorter form, Τρῶες κλειτοί τʼ ἐπίκουροι, “Trojans and famous allies” (6.227; also 18.229; see [[exhortation]]).

**The Lycians.** Helenus says that “the toil of the Trojans and the Lycians” rests on Aeneas and Hector (6.77–78). The Lycians, who came farthest, stand for all the allies, as in Hector's other address, “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand” (8.173; see [[lycians]]). In the middle of the book one Lycian, Glaucus, meets Diomedes, and the two part as guest-friends (6.119–236; see [[glaucus]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]).
