summary: Book 6 measures everyone by the best. Hippolochus charges Glaucus “always to be the best”, a stranger in Argos will remember Hector as “always the best in the fighting”, and the word ranks allies, seers, builders, fathers and a princess. Helenus calls Diomedes “the strongest of the Achaeans”, stronger than Achilles ever was.

**To be the best.** The verb ἀριστεύω, “be the best”, frames the charge that fathers give their sons and the fame that follows it. Hippolochus sent Glaucus to Troy “always to be the best (αἰὲν ἀριστεύειν) and to excel above the others, and not to shame the race of my fathers, who were by far the best (μέγʼ ἄριστοι) in Ephyra and in wide Lycia” (6.208–210; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]). Hector's life answers to it. In his vision of Andromache's captivity a man will say, “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting (ὃς ἀριστεύεσκε μάχεσθαι) of the horse-taming Trojans” (6.460–461; see [[kleos]]). The iterative ἀριστεύεσκε, “used always to be best”, is the verb of a whole career, and Hector will be remembered by it.

**The strongest of the Achaeans.** Helenus names the man the Trojans fear: Diomedes, “who, I say, has become the strongest of the Achaeans (κάρτιστον Ἀχαιῶν). We never feared like this even Achilles, leader of men, who they say was born of a goddess” (6.98–100). The absent Achilles is still the measure (see [[best-of-the-achaeans]] and [[the-aristeia]]).

**Best at a thing.** The superlative ranks men and women in every kind of worth. Acamas was “the best among the Thracians” (6.7). Helenus is “by far the best of the bird-seers” (6.76; see [[best-of-bird-seers]]), and Aeneas and Hector “are the best in every undertaking, both in fighting and in counsel” (6.78–79). The king of Lycia chose “the best men” of his country to ambush Bellerophon, and not one came home (6.188–190). Paris built his house with “the men who were then the best builders in deep-soiled Troy” (6.314–315). Laodice is “the most beautiful in form of her daughters” (6.252; see [[best-in-looks]]). “The best of them” have tried the wall three times by the fig tree (6.435). Once the word is bitter. Agamemnon asks Menelaus, who is about to spare a Trojan, “Were you so very well treated in your house by the Trojans?” (ἦ σοὶ ἄριστα πεποίηται, 6.55–57; see [[aisa]]).
