“Then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares, would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice, had there not stood beside Aeneas and Hector, and spoken to them, Helenus, son of Priam, by far the best of the bird-seers” (6.73–76). ἀναλκείῃσι, “by their own cowardice”, is the lack of ἀλκή, “courage” (see [[alke]]). What would have happened is a flight into the city, the end of the fighting on the plain. The one who prevents it is a man, Priam's son the seer, and his advice starts the action of the book: rally the army at the gates, and send Hector to have their mother offer Athena a robe (6.77–101; see [[helenus]], [[exhortation]] and [[message-repeated]]).

The first two verses return in Book 17, when the Trojans are again about to be driven into Ilios and “the Argives would have won glory even beyond the portion of Zeus”. There the turn is a god's: Apollo, in the likeness of the herald Periphas, rouses Aeneas (17.319–323). Both times the rescue begins by speaking to Aeneas; in Book 6 it comes from a Trojan seer, in Book 17 from the god disguised as a Trojan herald (see [[aeneas]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

A narrower turn comes earlier in the book, without the unreal condition. Menelaus “was on the point of sending him to the swift ships of the Achaeans … but Agamemnon came running to meet him” (6.52–54): an outcome begun and cut off, as in the looser form of Book 5 (5.22–23; see [[supplication]]).
