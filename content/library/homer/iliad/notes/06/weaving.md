**Women's work.** The robes in Hecuba's fragrant chamber are “richly embroidered, the work of women of the Sidonians” (6.289–290; see [[robe]] and [[sidonians]]). In Paris' house “Argive Helen sat among her slave women and was directing her handmaids in their glorious work” (6.323–324). ἔργα, “work”, is the usual word for the women's spinning and weaving (3.422), and the mistress of the house sets it. In Book 3 Helen was weaving a web of her own (3.125–128; see [[handmaids]] and [[helen]]).

**Another woman's loom.** Hector foresees his wife's slavery: “and in Argos you may weave at the loom at another woman's bidding, and carry water from Messeis or Hypereia” (πρὸς ἄλλης ἱστὸν ὑφαίνοις, 6.456–457). It is the fate Agamemnon promised Chryseis in Book 1, walking to and fro at the loom in his house in Argos (1.29–31; see [[captive-women]] and [[loom]]). Weaving and fetching water are the work of a slave woman; the loom is hers, the web her mistress's.

**Her own work.** At the end Hector sends Andromache home: “But go into the house and attend to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids to go about their work” (6.490–492). ἠλακάτη is the distaff, which holds the wool for spinning, and spinning comes before weaving. ἔργον ἐποίχεσθαι, “go about their work”, has the verb of walking to and fro along the loom (ἱστὸν ἐποιχομένην, 1.31). “Your own work” (τὰ σʼ αὐτῆς ἔργα) answers the loom of “another woman” thirty-four lines before: for now she weaves in her own house. The Odyssey gives the same verses to Telemachus, sending his mother back to her room, with “speech” and then “the bow” in place of “war” (Od. 1.356–359, 21.350–353). When Hector dies, Andromache is at her loom in the inner room of the house, weaving a double purple cloth with a pattern of flowers, and has told her maids to heat water for his bath (22.440–444; see [[andromache]]).

**A woven trap.** In Glaucus' story, when Bellerophon comes back from his three tasks, the king of Lycia “wove another cunning trap for him” (πυκινὸν δόλον ἄλλον ὕφαινε, 6.187). The verb of the loom is used of a plot, as in Book 3 it was used of the envoys' words and counsels (3.212; see [[ambush]] and [[bellerophon]]).
