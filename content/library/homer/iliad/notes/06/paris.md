**A bane to Troy.** Hector's first words about his brother in the book are a curse. He will go and call Paris, he tells Hecuba, and wishes the earth would gape for him, “for the Olympian has raised him as a great bane to the Trojans and to great-hearted Priam and his sons” (6.281–283); to see him dead would make him forget his misery. In Book 3 he called him “a great grief to your father and your city” (πῆμα both times, 3.50). See [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[hector]].

**At home.** He has a house of his own:

{{quote:514d22c8-e34e-5605-9e89-27c45034e2b0}}

Hector finds him “in his chamber busy with his beautiful armor, his shield and his corselet, and handling his curved bow”, while Helen directs her women at their work (6.321–324). It is the chamber to which Aphrodite carried him from the duel (3.382), and he has been sitting in it since (ἥμην ἐν θαλάμῳ, 6.336). See [[priams-palace]], [[house]] and [[bow]].

**The rebuke.** “Strange one, you do wrong to keep this anger in your heart” (6.326; see [[strange-one]]). Hector does not say what the anger is. Paris takes it as anger at the Trojans (6.335), who in Book 3 hated him “like black doom” (3.454); the word may be Hector's tactful name for a holding back that he will call willful at the end of the book (6.523). The people are dying around the wall, and it is “for your sake” that the war blazes around the city (6.327–329).

{{quote:c5564e6d-b572-5602-875b-309d639d02e7}}

He begins with the line he used in Book 3 (6.333 = 3.59; see [[aisa]]). Grief kept him at home, he says, more than anger or indignation, and he does not say what grieved him (see [[nemesis]]). His wife has just urged him back to the war “with gentle words” (6.337); in Book 3 she mocked him and told him to keep away from Menelaus (3.428–436). He ends with a maxim, “victory shifts from man to man” (6.339; see [[victory-shifts]]), as in Book 3 he told Helen that he would beat Menelaus another time (3.440). Hector gives him no answer (6.342; see [[silence]]). Helen passes judgment instead: “this man's mind is not steadfast now, nor will it ever be hereafter” (6.352–353; see [[helen]]).

**The stallion.** “Nor did Paris linger long in his lofty house” (6.503). He puts on the armor he was handling and runs through the city “trusting in his swift feet” (6.505), like a stalled horse that breaks its halter and gallops over the plain, head high, “trusting in his splendor” (6.506–511; see [[the-stallion]]). “So Paris, son of Priam, came down from the height of Pergamus, gleaming in his armor like the shining sun, laughing aloud” (6.512–514; see [[pergamus]] and [[like-the-sun]]). The horse returns, all six lines, for Hector revived by Apollo (15.263–268), and τεύχεσι παμφαίνων ὥς τʼ ἠλέκτωρ, “gleaming in his armor like the shining sun”, for Achilles going out to battle (19.398). Here Paris has the words of the poem's greatest fighters. καγχαλόων, “laughing aloud”, is Hector's word in Book 3 for the Achaeans' laughter at the Trojans' champion (3.43).

**The last word.** Paris apologizes for “lingering” (δηθύνων, the narrator's word of 6.503), and calls Hector ἠθεῖε, “dear brother”, the word Menelaus uses to Agamemnon (10.37). Hector answers:

{{quote:810aaf86-0bf4-5bc4-a724-99a2f33672e1}}

He grants him the courage he said in Book 3 he lacked (ἄλκιμος, 6.522; οὐδέ τις ἀλκή, 3.45), and puts the fault in his will: ἑκὼν μεθιεῖς, “you hold back of your own will”. Then, “let us go” (6.526). Book 7 opens with the two brothers going out through the gates together, and Paris kills a man at once (7.1–10).
