summary: Diomedes tells the story of Lycurgus, who drove off the nurses of Dionysus and was blinded by Zeus, to explain why he will not fight a god (6.128–141). He applies to himself the lesson that Dione drew about him in Book 5, and Glaucus' story of Bellerophon ends with the same words.

**Told of himself.** A paradigm is usually told to someone else, to move him: Nestor tells Agamemnon and Achilles of the men of old (1.259–274), and Agamemnon tells Diomedes of his father (4.370–400; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Diomedes tells his to explain what he himself will do. “But if you are one of the immortals come down from heaven, I would not fight with the gods of heaven. For not even the son of Dryas, mighty Lycurgus, lived long, who strove with the gods of heaven” (6.128–131). The story follows: Lycurgus drove the nurses of raving Dionysus down the Nysean mount, the god fled into the sea to Thetis, and Zeus blinded the man, “and he did not live long after, since he was hated by all the immortal gods” (6.132–140; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]], [[lycurgus]] and [[dionysus]]). Then the first verse comes back: “Nor would I wish to fight with the blessed gods” (6.141). The story is framed by its lesson (see [[ring-composition]]).

**Dione's lesson.** In Book 5 Dione comforted Aphrodite with stories of gods wounded by men, and ended with a warning about the man who had wounded her: the son of Tydeus does not know “that the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.406–407). Diomedes now tells that lesson of Lycurgus, in the same terms, οὐ … δὴν ἦν, “did not live long” (6.130–131, 6.139–140), and lives by it (see [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[the-aristeia]]).

**Bellerophon.** Glaucus answers with a story of his own, but it is a genealogy, told because he was asked who he is (6.145–211; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). It turns at the same words. Bellerophon too came to be “hated by all the gods” (ἀπήχθετο πᾶσι θεοῖσιν, 6.200), the end of the verse that ended Lycurgus' life (6.140), and he wandered alone over the Aleian plain (6.200–202; see [[bellerophon]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). The two men's stories, told one after the other, both end with a hero the gods hate.
