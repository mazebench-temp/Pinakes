The laughter of the Iliad usually falls on someone: the gods laugh at Hephaestus, the army at Thersites, and Hector imagines the Achaeans cackling at Paris (1.599–600, 2.270, 3.43). Book 6 has laughter of another kind.

**A smile.** When Hector meets Andromache at the gates, with the nurse carrying their son, “he smiled as he looked at his son in silence” (ἤτοι ὃ μὲν μείδησεν ἰδὼν ἐς παῖδα σιωπῇ, 6.404). The smile comes before a word is spoken, and Andromache answers it with tears (6.405; see [[silence]] and [[smiling-at-him]]).

**Laughing together.** The child shrinks from his father's bronze and nodding horsehair crest, “and his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud” (ἐκ δʼ ἐγέλασσε, 6.471). It is the one moment in the poem when a mortal husband and wife laugh together, and they laugh at the fear of their child, just after Hector has foreseen the fall of their city (see [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Hector takes off the helmet, kisses the boy, prays for him, and gives him back:

{{quote:fb59cb06-b196-5840-9c4e-121528ed5cd8}}

δακρυόεν γελάσασα, “laughing through her tears”, holds the whole scene in two words (see [[tears]]). The narrator gives it no more than that; Hector's answer is pity (see [[posis]]).

**Paris.** The book's last laugh is his. Running down from the height of Pergamus in his armor like a stallion broken loose, “gleaming in his armor like the shining sun, laughing aloud” (καγχαλόων, 6.513–514), he catches up with his brother. καγχαλάω is loud, exultant laughter. It was the word for the Achaeans' imagined mockery of Paris in Book 3 (καγχαλόωσι, 3.43); now it is Paris' own delight in himself (see [[the-stallion]], [[like-the-sun]] and [[paris]]). In Book 10 Odysseus laughs so as he drives off the horses of Rhesus (10.565).
