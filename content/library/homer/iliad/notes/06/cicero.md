---
summary: Cicero translated Bellerophon's lonely wandering as an image of grief, borrowed Hector's shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women for his own fear of the optimates, and refused to repay Atticus “gold for bronze”.
---

- **Bellerophon's grief.** Some men in grief seek out solitude, Cicero writes, “as Homer says of Bellerophon”, and he renders 6.201–202 in two Latin hexameters: *qui miser in campis maerens errabat Aleis, / ipse suum cor edens, hominum vestigia vitans*, “who wandered wretched and grieving over the Aleian plains, eating his own heart, shunning the tracks of men” (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.63). See [[bellerophon]] and [[aleian-plain]].
- “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.5.1, 7.1.4 and later): [[cicero-trojans]].
- “Not gold for bronze, but like for like” (*Letters to Atticus* 6.1.1, 6.1.22): [[gold-for-bronze]].
