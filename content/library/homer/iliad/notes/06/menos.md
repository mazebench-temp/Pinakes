**Diomedes' might.** Book 5 was the book of Diomedes' μένος, the might Athena put in him (5.1–8, 5.125–126). In Book 6 the Trojans still measure the battle by it. Helenus sends Hector to the city because Diomedes “rages beyond measure, and no one can match his might” (οὐδέ τίς οἱ δύναται μένος ἰσοφαρίζειν, 6.101), and the Trojan women are to pray that Athena hold him back from Ilios (6.96–98). Diomedes claims it himself when he meets Glaucus: “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (ἐμῷ μένει, 6.127; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]). The book also ends his aristeia. At the end of the meeting he plants his spear in the earth and greets a guest-friend (6.213; see [[xenia]] and [[the-aristeia]]).

**Wine and might.** Hecuba offers Hector honey-sweet wine to pour to Zeus and then to drink, “for in a weary man wine greatly increases might” (ἀνδρὶ δὲ κεκμηῶτι μένος μέγα οἶνος ἀέξει, 6.261). He refuses: “Do not bring me wine sweet to the heart, lady mother, for fear you unnerve me and rob me of my might, and I forget my courage” (6.264–265). ἀπογυιόω, “unnerve”, is literally to take the limbs (γυῖα) from a man. Mother and son disagree about what wine does to μένος. In Book 19 Odysseus takes Hecuba's side: the army must eat and drink before battle, “for that is might and courage” (τὸ γὰρ μένος ἐστὶ καὶ ἀλκή, 19.161). Hector also has another reason, the blood on his hands (6.266–268; see [[aidos]] and [[wine]]).

**Might that destroys.** Andromache's first words to Hector at the Scaean gates are a charge: “Strange one, your own might will destroy you” (φθίσει σε τὸ σὸν μένος, 6.407). The quality that makes him the city's defender is the one that will kill him, and she says it before she asks him to stay. In Book 22, when she hears the wailing from the tower, she fears exactly this: that Achilles has cut Hector off and ended the grievous manhood that held him, for he never stayed in the throng of men, “but ran far out in front, yielding his might to no one” (τὸ ὃν μένος οὐδενὶ εἴκων, 22.454–459). See [[andromache]], [[hector]] and [[hector-and-andromache]].

**Life and fire.** The word keeps its older senses. Euryalus kills the twin sons of Bucolion, “and of these too he loosened the strength and the shining limbs” (ὑπέλυσε μένος, 6.27): μένος is the force that leaves a man at death (see [[death-in-battle]]). Nestor “urged on the might and spirit of every man” (6.72), the verse of Ares and Hera in Book 5 (5.470, 5.792; see [[exhortation]]). The Chimaera breathes out “the terrible might of blazing fire” (πυρὸς μένος, 6.182; see [[chimaera]]). And the handmaids mourn Hector alive, thinking he will never come back from the war, “escaping the might and the hands of the Achaeans” (6.502).
