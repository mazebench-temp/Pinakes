summary: Adrastus' plea has every part of the scene: a fall at the victor's feet, the grip on his knees, an appeal that offers ransom in verses other suppliants use, and a refusal, given not by the man he holds but by his brother (6.37–65).
refs: [6.45-53, 6.62-65]
passages:
  - 6.37-65 | Adrastus begs for his life
---

**Approach.** There is none. The suppliant is thrown at the victor's feet when his horses bolt and the chariot breaks, and he lies face down in the dust with Menelaus standing over him (6.38–44). The sons of Antimachus in Book 11 also come to beg from a chariot out of control. The reins slip from their hands, the horses panic, and they supplicate Agamemnon from the car (γουναζέσθην, 11.126–130).

**Gesture.** He clasps the knees (λαβὼν … γούνων, 6.45). The chin, which Thetis touched (1.500–501), is not mentioned. Dolon will be killed as he reaches for it (10.454–457).

**Appeal.**

{{quote:a09c4a8d-5d7c-5156-ad44-7bdf2a0389fd}}

The claim is not a past service, as in the prayers of Chryses and the plea of Thetis (1.39–41, 1.503–504), but a price. The verses belong to the scene. Dolon offers the same bronze, gold and iron and the same boundless ransom (10.378–381), and the sons of Antimachus repeat Adrastus' plea almost word for word (11.131–135; see [[apoina]]).

**Answer.** The one supplicated is persuaded and about to agree (6.51–53). The answer comes from another man: Agamemnon runs up, rebukes his brother, and turns his mind, and Menelaus pushes the suppliant away (6.53–63). Agamemnon then kills him with his own spear (6.63–65). In Book 11 the brothers supplicate Agamemnon himself, and he answers them with their father's crime: Antimachus once urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came to Troy as an envoy (11.138–142). Both refusals come from Agamemnon, and both answer an appeal to ransom with an old wrong. See [[supplication]] and [[agamemnon]].
