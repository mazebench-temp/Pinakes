Hector finds his house without its mistress, stands on the threshold and asks the slave women where she has gone: to his sisters, to his brothers' wives, or to Athena's temple with the other Trojan women (6.374–380). The housekeeper answers him. She is ὀτρηρή, “ready”, the word used of Agamemnon's “ready attendants” (1.321; see [[ready-attendants]]), and she repeats his three guesses before she corrects them (6.378–380, 6.383–385; see [[message-repeated]]). She knows why her mistress went, because she heard that the Trojans were hard pressed, and how, “like a woman out of her mind”, and that the nurse has the child (6.386–389). The line that brings her in is a formula: in the Odyssey “Eurynome the housekeeper spoke to her in turn” in the same words, with her name in place of the epithet (Od. 17.495, 18.169).

The other servants of the book work in the background. Hecuba's handmaids gather the old women for the procession to Athena (6.286–287; see [[hecuba]]). Helen sits “among her slave women”, directing her handmaids at their work (6.323–324; see [[helen]] and [[weaving]]). A handmaid carries Hector's son at her breast, and he shrinks from the helmet into the bosom of “his fair-belted nurse” (6.399–400, 6.467; see [[fair-belted]] and [[astyanax]]). Hector sends Andromache home to tell her handmaids “to go about their work” (6.491–492), and she finds many of them in the house and rouses them to lament (6.497–500; see [[lament]]). They are the first to mourn Hector.
