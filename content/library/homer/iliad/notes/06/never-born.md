In Book 3 Hector wished that his brother had never been born, or had died unmarried (3.40). In Book 6 Helen, speaking to the same Hector with Paris beside her, makes the wish for herself, in the same form. ὄφελε with the infinitive, “would that it had”, states a wish about the past that cannot now come true, as αἴθʼ ὄφελες did in Hector's curse. Her version is not “unborn” but snatched away at birth: on the very day her mother bore her, a storm wind should have carried her off to a mountain or into the sea, and a wave swept her away “before these things came to pass” (πάρος τάδε ἔργα γενέσθαι, 6.348). The deeds she means are the war.

The picture of a storm that snatches a woman away returns in the Odyssey. Penelope, grieving, wishes that Artemis would shoot her, or that a storm would seize her and carry her off (θύελλα οἴχοιτο προφέρουσα) along the misty ways to the streams of Ocean, as storms once carried off the daughters of Pandareus (*Odyssey* 20.61–66). The words of the two wishes are the same.

Helen has made the wish before and will make it again. On the wall in Book 3 she told Priam that she wished evil death had pleased her before she followed his son (3.173–175), and in her lament over Hector in the last book she says of Paris, “who brought me to Troy: how I wish I had died before that” (24.764). In Book 6 the wish has a second part. Since the gods have ordained these evils, she says, then she wishes she had been the wife of a better man (6.349–351; see [[nemesis]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Other wishes for death fill the book: Andromache would rather go beneath the earth than lose Hector (6.410–411), and Hector would rather be dead than hear her cries as a captive (6.464–465; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]).
