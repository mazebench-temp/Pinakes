**Stored treasures.** “Many treasures lie stored in the house of my wealthy father, bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” (πολλὰ δʼ ἐν ἀφνειοῦ πατρὸς κειμήλια κεῖται, 6.47–48). κειμήλια, from κεῖμαι, “lie”, are things laid up in a house, the wealth that is not used but kept, and given or paid out when it is needed: for a ransom, as here, or as a gift to a guest (Od. 1.312; see [[apoina]], [[iron]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The same two verses are the offer of the sons of Antimachus to Agamemnon (11.132–133), and Dolon offers the same metals (10.379; see [[supplication]]).

**The spoils of Thebe.** Achilles brought Andromache's mother to the camp “with the rest of the possessions” (ἅμʼ ἄλλοισι κτεάτεσσιν, 6.426), and then set her free for a boundless ransom. The Iliad lets us see some of those possessions. From Thebe came Chryseis, Agamemnon's prize (1.366–369); the lyre on which Achilles sings the glories of men in his hut (9.186–189); the horse Pedasus, the mortal trace horse that ran beside the immortal team (16.152–154); and the lump of iron Eetion used to throw, which Achilles brought away “with the other possessions” and sets as a prize at Patroclus' games (σὺν ἄλλοισι κτεάτεσσι, 23.826–829). See [[sack-of-thebe]], [[eetion]] and [[possessions]].
