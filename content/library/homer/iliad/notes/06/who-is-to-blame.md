**Helen.** Book 3 set every view of Helen's guilt side by side, and Book 6 puts them all in her own mouth. She begins with herself:

{{quote:769f0202-86e0-5497-83b5-9f1544e6f129}}

κύων, “dog”, is her own word for herself, and she says it twice (6.344, 6.356; see [[dog-face]]). The wish that she had never lived is a judgment on herself (see [[never-born]]). In the next breath she names the gods: “But since the gods ordained these evils so” (6.349). Then Paris, whose mind will never be steadfast (6.352–353), and his “blind folly” (ἄτη), which with her own guilt has brought Hector his toil (6.356; see [[ate]]). Last, Zeus, who has set an evil fate on Paris and on her, “so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (6.357–358; see [[song-and-fame]]). The speech does not choose among these causes. It holds them all, as the poem does. See [[helen]].

**Hector.** Hector's blame falls on Paris. To his mother he calls him “a great bane” that the Olympian has raised for the Trojans and Priam and his sons, and wishes the earth would swallow him (6.280–285; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]). To Paris he says it plainly: “it is for your sake that the war cry and the war blaze around this city” (6.328–329), and at the end, the Trojans “have much toil for your sake” (6.525). Paris is the cause, and the Olympian, Hector says, raised him to be one (6.282). See [[paris]] and [[the-beginning-of-evils]].

**All the Trojans.** Agamemnon makes the guilt collective. “Were you so very well treated in your house by the Trojans?” he asks Menelaus, and demands that none escape, not even the unborn (6.55–60). The wrong done in Menelaus' house by one Trojan guest is laid on the whole people, and the narrator calls his words αἴσιμα, “what was due” (6.62; see [[aisa]], [[xenia]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]).

**Blame in the stories.** The two stories of the meeting on the plain show blame of other kinds. Proetus believes his wife's false charge and blames the innocent Bellerophon (6.160–166). Lycurgus is punished for a fault that is named: he fought the gods (6.130–140; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Bellerophon is hated by all the gods, and no fault is named at all (6.200–205; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). And the narrator, commenting on Glaucus' exchange of gold for bronze, lays it on Zeus, who “took away Glaucus' wits” (6.234; see [[phrenes]]).
