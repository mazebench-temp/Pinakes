---
summary: Hector weighs sorrows. The sorrow of the Trojans in the time to come troubles him less than Andromache's, and her captivity will bring her fresh sorrow each time someone points her out as the wife of Hector.
passages:
  - 6.450-465 | Hector foresees Andromache's captivity
---

The word comes twice in Hector's answer to Andromache, at the beginning and at the end of his vision of her captivity. “But it is not so much the sorrow of the Trojans hereafter that troubles me (οὔ μοι Τρώων τόσσον μέλει ἄλγος ὀπίσσω), nor of Hecuba herself, nor of lord Priam, nor of my brothers … as yours” (6.450–454). ἄλγος is the grief that will follow the fall of the city, and Hector ranks it by nearness; hers comes last and weighs most (see [[a-day-will-come]]). Then, after the loom and the water-jar in Argos and the stranger who names her as Hector's wife, “it will be fresh sorrow for you (σοὶ δʼ αὖ νέον ἔσσεται ἄλγος) to lack such a man to ward off the day of slavery” (6.462–463; see [[doulios]] and [[captive-women]]). The fame of the husband is the widow's renewed grief (see [[kleos]]).

Hector's own word for what Paris costs him is another. If he saw his brother going down to Hades, “I would think my heart had forgotten its joyless misery” (ὀϊζύος, 6.285; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]).
