summary: Book 6 is full of kin by marriage. Priam's palace houses his sons with their wives and his daughters with their husbands; Helen addresses Hector as her brother-in-law; Hector asks whether Andromache has gone to his sisters or his brothers' wives; and in Glaucus' story a deadly message passes from a son-in-law to his father-in-law.

**Priam's house.** When Hector reaches the palace the narrator stops to describe it: fifty chambers of polished stone where Priam's sons slept beside their wedded wives, and on the other side of the courtyard twelve roofed chambers where “the sons-in-law (γαμβροί) of Priam slept beside their modest wives” (6.242–250). The sons bring their brides home, as sons did; the daughters stay too, and their husbands come to live with them. The house is a picture of a great king's family gathered in one place, and of the family that the war will destroy. The Odyssey has one such house, that of Aeolus, king of the winds, who gave his six daughters to his six sons (*Odyssey* 10.5–7). γαμβρός is the man who marries into the house: a son-in-law to the father, a brother-in-law to the sons (see the note on Book 5, and [[priams-palace]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**Brother-in-law.** Helen begins her speech to Hector with δᾶερ, “brother-in-law”, and says it again when she turns from Paris to invite him in (6.344, 6.355). She called Agamemnon δαήρ in Book 3, “if that was ever so” (3.180); Hector is the brother-in-law she has. In her lament in the last book she calls him “dearest to my heart by far of all my brothers-in-law” (δαέρων, 24.762), and remembers that he always checked the others who reproached her, “brothers-in-law or husband's sisters or brothers' well-robed wives” (24.768–769). See [[helen]] and [[husbands-and-wives]].

**Andromache's in-laws.** Not finding Andromache at home, Hector asks the slave women where she has gone: “Has she gone to my sisters, or to my brothers' well-robed wives” (ἐς γαλόων ἢ εἰνατέρων ἐϋπέπλων, 6.378), and the housekeeper answers in the same words (6.383). γάλως is a husband's sister and εἰνάτερες are the wives of a husband's brothers: Hector names his own family by the words his wife would use for them, for the old kin terms look out from the wife's side. Andromache has no family left but these. The same women will stand around her when she faints on the wall at the sight of Hector's body (γαλόῳ τε καὶ εἰνατέρες, 22.473). Andromache herself says it most plainly: “Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother, and brother, and you are my husband in his prime” (6.429–430; see [[andromache]] and [[kasignetos]]).

**Son-in-law and father-in-law.** In Glaucus' story Proetus sends Bellerophon to Lycia with signs on a folded tablet, and tells him “to show them to his own father-in-law (πενθερῷ), so that he would perish” (6.170). The Lycian king asks to see the sign “that he was bringing from his son-in-law Proetus”, and reads “the evil sign of his son-in-law” (γαμβροῖο, γαμβροῦ, 6.177–178). The father-in-law of Proetus, whom Homer does not name, is the father of Anteia, the wife who accused Bellerophon (6.160–165). The story ends with the king giving Bellerophon his daughter in marriage, so that the man the message was meant to destroy becomes his son-in-law too (6.191–195). See [[bellerophon]], [[proetus]] and [[anteia]].
