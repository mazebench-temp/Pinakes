---
summary: The epithet names Bellerophon twice: as he refuses Anteia, “for his thoughts were good,” and as the father of three children by the daughter of the Lycian king.
---

Anteia could not “persuade wise-hearted Bellerophon, for his thoughts were good” (ἀγαθὰ φρονέοντα δαΐφρονα Βελλεροφόντην, 6.161–162; [[anteia]], [[bellerophon]]), and she lies to her husband that he tried to seduce her (6.163–165). The translation's sense of δαΐφρων, “with a knowing mind,” suits the man whose thoughts were good; the other ancient sense, “warlike in mind,” suits the man who killed the Chimaera, the Solymi and the Amazons. In Book 4 the epithet was given to Pandarus just before Athena persuaded him to break the truce (4.93); here it is given to a man who cannot be persuaded. The second use ends his labors: the king gives him his daughter, “and she bore three children to wise-hearted Bellerophon” (6.196–197; [[isander]], [[hippolochus]], [[laodameia]]).
