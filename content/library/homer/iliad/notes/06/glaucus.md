**The meeting.** Hector leaves the battle for the city (6.116–118), and before he reaches the Scaean gates (6.237) the poem fills the interval with two men who come out “between the two armies, eager to fight” (6.119–120). Glaucus has been named once before, second to Sarpedon in the Catalogue (2.876). This is his first scene. Diomedes has never seen him “in the battle where men win glory”, and wonders at a daring that has brought him out in front of all to face his spear (6.124–126). He asks whether the stranger is a god, tells the story of Lycurgus, and ends with a threat: “come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.143; see [[diomedes]] and [[lycurgus]]). Challenge and answer follow a fixed pattern, which the poem uses again for Aeneas and for Asteropaeus against Achilles (see [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**The leaves.** Glaucus answers with a question, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (6.145), and a comparison:

{{quote:65f01ef6-4b08-5821-8757-5cfe98c55065}}

γενεή is both a “generation” and a “lineage” (see [[genee]]). The simile makes a man's lineage worth no more than a year's leaves, and a threat of death no surprise. Then Glaucus tells it anyway, “and many men know it” (6.150–151; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]).

**The lineage.** It runs from [[Sisyphus]] at Ephyra through Glaucus' great-grandfather and namesake to [[Bellerophon]], whose story fills most of the speech (6.152–205; see [[glaucus-son-of-sisyphus]] and [[ephyra-in-argos]]). It ends with the man the gods hate and the deaths of two of his children, and then turns to the son who survived:

{{quote:355eb7a5-a8c1-5675-b8b5-f98c69a34fa6}}

His father's charge, αἰὲν ἀριστεύειν καὶ ὑπείροχον ἔμμεναι ἄλλων, is the one Peleus gave Achilles in the same words (11.784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]], [[hippolochus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The last line is a formal claim (εὔχομαι; see [[euchomai]]). The story has also shown who his cousin is: [[Sarpedon]], the king he serves under, is the son of Bellerophon's daughter (6.198–199).

**Guest-friends.** Diomedes is glad. He plants his spear in the earth and speaks “with soothing words” to “the shepherd of the people” (6.212–214; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). Their grandfathers were host and guest, and the bond is theirs by inheritance (see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]). They will avoid each other's spears even in the throng, and exchange armor “so that these men too may know” (6.226–231).

**Gold for bronze.**

{{quote:db540de2-4ce8-55df-a865-408804c2074a}}

The handclasp seals the pact, and then the narrator comments. φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, “Zeus took away (his) wits”, is the phrase Agamemnon uses of his own blindness in Book 19 (19.137; compare 9.377, 18.311; see [[phrenes]] and [[ate]]). The armor is valued in cattle, Homer's measure of worth, as a tripod is worth twelve oxen and a skilled woman four (23.702–705). Whether the comment is a joke at Glaucus' expense, a sober verdict on a bad bargain, or a way of pointing to his magnificence, since in an exchange of gifts the one who gives more wins the greater honor, is debated. Aristotle cites the last line to show that a man who gives away his own goods willingly is not wronged (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13), and Plato's Socrates accuses Alcibiades of trying to get “gold for bronze” (*Symposium* 218e–219a; see [[gold-for-bronze]]).

**Afterward.** Glaucus returns in Book 7 as “son of Hippolochus, leader of the Lycian men”, killing Iphinous (7.13–16). When Sarpedon has fallen, he rebukes Hector for leaving the body to the Argives, though Sarpedon was his “guest-friend and companion” (ξεῖνον καὶ ἑταῖρον, 17.150): he holds Hector to the bond that saved him here. See [[glaucus-and-diomedes]].
