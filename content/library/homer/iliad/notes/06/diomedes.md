**The man Troy fears.** The book opens on the same day as Book 5, and Diomedes' first act in it is a killing: Axylus of Arisbe, “a friend to all men”, and his charioteer (6.12–19; see [[axylus]] and [[xenia]]). In the city he is the danger the Trojans pray against. Helenus calls him “that savage spearman, mighty master of rout”, the strongest of the Achaeans, feared more than Achilles ever was (6.96–101; see [[master-of-rout]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and Hector repeats the words to his mother (6.277–278). Theano asks Athena to break his spear and let him fall before the Scaean gates, and the goddess refuses (6.305–311; see [[athena]]). Andromache counts “the valiant son of Tydeus” among the leaders who have tried the wall by the fig tree (6.437).

**The challenge.**

{{quote:42eaca3f-4db9-5cd9-9908-200883c9efd9}}

The question looks back to Book 5. That day Athena took the mist from his eyes “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (5.127–128), and he wounded Aphrodite and then Ares. Now he will not fight any god, and gives his reason in the story of Lycurgus, who fought the gods and “did not live long” (6.130–141; see [[lycurgus]]). Dione had said the same of Diomedes himself (5.406–407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). The poem does not say whether Athena's gift has lapsed or whether the question is caution, courtesy or menace. It is also the form in which a champion challenges an unknown opponent (see [[genealogy-in-battle]]), and Achilles will use two of its lines, “it is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (6.127 = 21.151) and “come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.143 = 20.429).

**Guest-friends.** He hears sixty lines of Glaucus' lineage, and at the end of it he is glad:

{{quote:dd780a31-cd41-5b0b-a53a-8faf38d3930d}}

He knows the story as a family memory, and the golden cup in his house proves it (see [[oeneus]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Of his father he knows less: “Tydeus I do not remember, since I was still little when he left me, when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223; see [[tydeus]] and [[thebes]]). It is the only time he speaks of his own memory of Tydeus, whom Agamemnon and Athena have held up to him (4.370–400, 5.800–813). He proposes that they avoid each other in the fighting, since each has enemies enough to kill, and exchange armor so that the armies may see the bond (6.224–231; see [[xenia]]).

**The end of the day of glory.** The aristeia that began at the start of Book 5 ends here, with a pact instead of a kill, and with gold armor for bronze (6.232–236; see [[glaucus]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). Diomedes is not seen in battle again until Book 8, when he saves Nestor (8.90–171).
