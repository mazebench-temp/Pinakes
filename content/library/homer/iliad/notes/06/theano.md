**The priestess.** She is introduced by her father and her husband, “fair-cheeked Theano … daughter of Cisseus, the wife of Antenor, tamer of horses” (6.298–299; see [[cisseus]], [[antenor]] and [[fair-cheeked]]), and then with her office: “for the Trojans had made her priestess of Athena” (6.300). ἱέρεια, “priestess”, occurs only here in Homer, and the line says how she came to the office: the Trojans appointed her (ἔθηκαν). See [[priesthood]] and [[temple]].

**The rite.** Helenus had told Hecuba to open the temple doors with the key herself (6.89); when the women come, Theano opens them (6.298). They raise their hands to Athena with the ὀλολυγή, the high cry of women at prayer and sacrifice (6.301). In the Odyssey the women raise it at Nestor's sacrifice (ὀλόλυξαν, Od. 3.450), and Penelope raises it when she prays to Athena, and the goddess hears her (ὀλόλυξε, Od. 4.767). Theano takes the robe and lays it “on the knees of Athena of the lovely hair” (6.302–303), as Helenus and Hector directed (6.92, 6.273): the goddess's image is imagined as seated. See [[hecuba]], [[robe]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]].

**The prayer.**

{{quote:6d83ccd3-0ec9-5127-93d6-ddbc7cd049d1}}

The vow is the one Helenus proposed and Hector repeated, twelve yearling cows (6.93–94, 6.274–275), but the request goes further. They asked that Athena “hold the son of Tydeus back from sacred Ilios” (6.96, 6.277). Theano asks her to break his spear and let him “fall headlong before the Scaean gates” (6.306–307; see [[diomedes]] and [[scaean-gates]]). She calls her ἐρυσίπτολις, “protector of the city” (see [[protector-of-the-city]]), and the goddess she calls on has spent Book 5 at Diomedes' side.

**The refusal.** The line that closes the prayer begins like the one that closed Diomedes' prayer in Book 5: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121). Here its second half turns: ἀνένευε δὲ Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). ἀνανεύω, to toss the head back, is the Greek gesture of refusal, the reverse of the nod by which Zeus grants (1.528; see [[neuo]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]). No one in the scene responds to it. The next line leaves the women still praying (6.312), and the poem turns to Hector.
