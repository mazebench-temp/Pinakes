Dione said it of Diomedes as a threat: the man who fights the immortals is not long-lived (οὐ δηναιός, 5.407). Diomedes did not hear her, and later the same day he says the same thing himself, with a word of the same stem. Not even Lycurgus, he says, “lived long” (δὴν ἦν, 6.131), and after Zeus blinded him “he did not live long after” (οὐδʼ ἄρʼ ἔτι δὴν ἦν, 6.139–140). The story is his reason for asking whether the stranger is a god: “I would not fight with the gods of heaven” (6.129), and “nor would I wish to fight with the blessed gods” (6.141). The man who wounded two gods in Book 5, with Athena beside him, now keeps the rule for mortals (see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[diomedes]]).

The story itself is the saying in action. Lycurgus drove the nurses of Dionysus down the Nysean mount with an ox-goad, and the god fled into the sea to Thetis (6.132–137). The gods were angry, Zeus blinded him, and he died soon, “since he was hated by all the immortal gods” (ἐπεὶ ἀθανάτοισιν ἀπήχθετο πᾶσι θεοῖσιν, 6.140; see [[lycurgus]], [[dionysus]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). The punishment of blinding is like the maiming of Thamyris, who boasted against the Muses (2.594–600).

Glaucus' answer brings the same half-line back. His grandfather Bellerophon, after all his triumphs, “when he too was hated by all the gods” (ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ καὶ κεῖνος ἀπήχθετο πᾶσι θεοῖσιν, 6.200), wandered alone over the Aleian plain. καὶ κεῖνος, “he too” or “even he”, may set him beside Lycurgus, though no offense of his is named (see [[bellerophon]]). The two stories told on the plain, one by each man, both end with a man whom the gods hated, and the two tellers part as friends.
