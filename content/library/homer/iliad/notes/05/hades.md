**Aidoneus.** Pandarus thought he would “hurl him down to Aidoneus” (Ἀϊδωνῆϊ προϊάψειν, 5.190), with the verb of the proem, where Achilles' wrath “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls” (Ἄϊδι προΐαψεν, 1.3). Ἀϊδωνεύς is a longer form of the name, used again only at 20.61, where Aidoneus, lord of those below, leaps from his throne in fear that Poseidon will split the earth open.

**Wounded.** Dione's third example of a god who suffered at a mortal's hands:

{{quote:576bd4a6-0f6f-595d-94ef-c04b4b388804}}

πελώριος, “huge” (5.395; see [[huge]]). The place is an old crux. ἐν Πύλῳ ἐν νεκύεσσι is printed as “in Pylos among the dead”. So read, it recalls Heracles' attack on Nestor's Pylos, when he killed eleven of the twelve sons of Neleus (11.689–693; see [[pylos]]), and “the dead” are the fallen of that battle. Pindar recalls a fight at Pylos in which Poseidon, Apollo and Hades stood against Heracles, and then breaks off (*Olympian* 9.29–41). Others read ἐν πύλῳ, “at the gate”, the gate of the underworld, where Heracles went to fetch the hound of Hades (8.366–368); “the dead” are then the dead below. See [[pylos-or-the-gate]], [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]].

Wounded in the shoulder, he went up “to the house of Zeus and high Olympus”, and Paeëon healed him, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.398–402; see [[paeeon]] and [[olympus]]). The lord of the dead cannot die.

**Gates and horses.** Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon that he will “pass the gates of Hades” (5.646), and the ghost of Patroclus will ask Achilles for burial so that he may pass them (23.71). Sarpedon answers that Tlepolemus will give “your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (5.654), a line that returns at 11.445 and 16.625 (see [[psyche]]). Both warriors speak of death as a going down to the god's house.

**The helmet.** Athena puts on “the helmet of Hades, so that mighty Ares should not see her” (5.845; see [[helmet-of-hades]]), perhaps with a play on the name that the Greeks explained as “the unseen”.
