summary: The narrator says of the hunter Scamandrius, whom Artemis herself had taught, that “Artemis of the showering arrows was no help to him then, nor his far-shooting” (5.53–54). The threat of Books 1 and 3 becomes the epitaph of a man whose skill could not save him.
grc: ["οὔ οἱ τότε γε χραῖσμʼ"]
en: [was no help to him then]
---

In Books 1 and 3 the formula was a threat spoken to a living man. Hector told Paris that his lyre and the gifts of Aphrodite “would be no help to you then” in the dust (3.54–55). In Book 5 the narrator says it over a dead man. Menelaus kills Scamandrius, son of Strophius, a good hunter, “for Artemis herself had taught him to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains”:

{{quote:1240a022-2c88-5c54-8e4f-b64209c85597}}

The goddess who taught him and the skill she taught fail him together, and the spear goes into his back as he runs (5.55–58; see [[scamandrius]], [[artemis]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). The same idea runs through the other killings of the book without the formula: the shipbuilder whom Athena loved, the dream-reader who read no dreams for his sons, and the priest whose god could save only one of his two (5.59–64, 5.148–151, 5.9–24; see [[androktasia]] and [[divination]]). The Catalogue said it of the augur Ennomus: “but his birds did not save him from black doom” (2.859).
