summary: Book 5 shows every kind of divine intervention the Iliad knows. Athena gives Diomedes strength, clears his sight and guides his spear; Hephaestus, Aphrodite and Apollo snatch their favorites from death; Ares and Hera rally the armies in mortal shapes; and in the end gods fight in the battle in person.

**Strength and sight.** The book opens with a god's gift: Athena gives Diomedes might and daring and kindles fire from his helmet (5.1–8; see [[menos]] and [[tharsos]]). When he prays, wounded, she makes his limbs light and takes the mist from his eyes (5.121–128; see [[achlys]]). Apollo puts might into Aeneas when he sends him back healed (5.512–513).

**Guidance.** Athena guides Diomedes' spear into Pandarus' face (βέλος δʼ ἴθυνεν Ἀθήνη, 5.290), as she guided Pandarus' arrow to Menelaus' belt in Book 4 (4.132), and she drives his spear into Ares' flank (5.856–857). She turns Odysseus' heart against the Lycians when he ponders whether to pursue Sarpedon (5.674–676). A god's prompting can be meant for harm: Ares urges on the might of Menelaus, “intending that he should be brought down at the hands of Aeneas” (5.563–564).

**Rescue.** Three times a god snatches a man from death. Hephaestus hides Idaeus in night for his father's sake (5.22–24); Aphrodite wraps Aeneas in the fold of her robe (5.311–318), and when she drops him Apollo takes him up in a dark cloud, carries him to his temple and leaves a phantom in his place (5.343–346, 5.445–453; see [[divine-rescue]], [[would-have-had-not]] and [[eidolon]]). Zeus keeps ruin from Sarpedon (5.662).

**Disguise and presence.** Ares rouses the Trojans in the likeness of Acamas (5.461–470), Hera the Argives in the likeness of Stentor (5.784–792). Then the disguises fall away. Ares fights beside Hector, and Diomedes knows him (5.592–606); Athena throws Sthenelus out of the chariot and takes the reins herself, wearing the helmet of Hades so that Ares cannot see her (5.835–845).

**Double motivation.** The mortals explain success by the gods, and they are right. Pandarus decides that Diomedes “does not rage like this without a god” (5.185), and Diomedes says that “always at his side” Hector has one of the gods (5.603). Yet the deeds remain the men's. Athena guides the spear, but Diomedes throws it; the narrator can say that Athena turned Odysseus' heart and that he chose. See [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-aristeia]].
