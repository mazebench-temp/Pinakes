summary: Ares fights for the Trojans through Book 5. Athena leads him out of the battle, and he lends his horses to the wounded Aphrodite; at Apollo's call he rouses the Trojans and fights beside Hector, until Diomedes, with Athena in his chariot, wounds him in the belly. He bellows like ten thousand men, complains to Zeus, is rebuked as the most hateful of the gods, and is healed.

**His names.** Book 5 is full of his epithets. Athena and Apollo both address him with the same line: “Ares, Ares, ruin of mortals, blood-stained stormer of walls” (5.31 = 5.455; see [[ares-ares]]). In the book he is “furious” (θοῦρος) seven times, and also “baneful” (οὖλος, 5.461, 5.717), “insatiable of war” (ἆτος πολέμοιο, 5.388, 5.863), “bronze” (χάλκεος, 5.704, 5.859, 5.866) and “mighty” (ὄβριμος, 5.845). Diomedes calls him “the warrior with the oxhide shield” (5.289). His enemies add insults: to Hera he is “this fool, who knows no law” (5.761), to Athena “this madman, an evil through and through, a turncoat”, and Zeus repeats the last word (5.831, 5.889; see [[alloprosallos]]). See [[furious]] and [[baneful]].

**Led away, bound, and lent.** Athena takes him by the hand and persuades him to leave the battle to the mortals, and seats him by the Scamander (5.29–36). He is still sitting there, his spear and horses resting in a mist, when Aphrodite comes to beg for his chariot. She calls him “dear brother” (5.359): both are children of Zeus, and in the Odyssey they are lovers (Od. 8.266–366). Dione's first example of a god who suffered from men is Ares himself, bound in a bronze jar by the sons of Aloeus until Hermes stole him away (5.385–391; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]]).

**Beside Hector.** Apollo calls him back to drag Diomedes out of the fight (5.455–459). In the likeness of Acamas, leader of the Thracians, he rouses the sons of Priam (5.461–470; see [[acamas-of-thrace]]). He covers the battle in night, urges on the might of Menelaus, meaning him to fall at Aeneas' hands, and leads the Trojans with Enyo, a huge spear in his hands, now in front of Hector, now behind (5.506–508, 5.563–564, 5.592–595; see [[enyo]] and [[tumult]]). Diomedes recognizes him “in the likeness of a mortal man” and gives ground (5.604–606). Hera complains to Zeus that Ares has destroyed an army of the Achaeans “idly and against all order” (5.759), and Athena tells Diomedes that he had promised the two goddesses to fight for the Argives (5.832–834). The poem never shows that promise, but Book 21 remembers the desertion: his mother, Athena says, is angry with him “because you left the Achaeans” (21.412–414).

**The wound.** He is stripping huge Periphas, the best of the Aetolians, the one man the poem shows him killing with his own hand (5.842–848; see [[periphas]]). He lunges at Diomedes; Athena turns the spear aside and drives Diomedes' spear into his flank, “where he girded on his band” (5.851–857).

His cry is measured by the war that bears his name. He rises into the sky like a murky mist out of the clouds (5.864–867; see [[dark-mist-from-the-clouds]], [[diomedes-wounds-ares]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]).

**On Olympus.** He shows Zeus “the immortal blood” flowing from the wound (5.870; see [[ichor]]). His complaint begins with the words Hera used against him, Ζεῦ πάτερ οὐ νεμεσίζῃ … τάδε καρτερὰ ἔργα, “Father Zeus, do you not blame … these violent deeds” (5.757, 5.872), and repeats Apollo's two lines on Diomedes (5.883–884 = 5.458–459). The second verse is Agamemnon's to Achilles (5.891 = 1.177; see [[strife-is-always-dear]]). He has “your mother's fury … Hera's” (5.892–893), and only his birth saves him: the son of any other god would long ago have been sent “lower down than the sons of Uranus” (5.896–898; see [[zeus]]). Paeëon heals him as fast as fig juice curdles milk, Hebe bathes him, and he sits beside Zeus “exulting in his glory” (5.899–906; see [[paeeon]], [[hebe]] and [[exulting-in-glory]]), the words of Briareus in Book 1 (1.405).
