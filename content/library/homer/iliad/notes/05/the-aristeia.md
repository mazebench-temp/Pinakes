summary: Book 5 is the aristeia of Diomedes, the first and fullest in the poem. Athena kindles him, Pandarus wounds him, she answers his prayer, and he kills Pandarus, wounds Aeneas and Aphrodite, is stopped by Apollo, gives ground before Ares, and at last, with the goddess in his chariot, wounds Ares himself.

**The start.** There is no arming scene. Diomedes armed in Book 4, and the bronze rang on his chest as he leapt from his chariot (4.419–421). Book 5 opens instead with the goddess:

{{quote:55bc383e-6419-5b33-8227-16c2972361f7}}

The aim is stated at once, κλέος ἐσθλόν, “noble fame” (see [[kleos]]), and the sign follows: a tireless fire from his helmet and shield, like the star of late summer (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]). Athena then leads Ares out of the battle (5.29–36), and the field is clear for her man.

**The rout.** He kills Phegeus, son of Dares, while Hephaestus hides the brother (5.9–26), and the narrator leaves the other Achaean leaders to their single kills (5.37–84) before coming back to him with a figure of his own: “you could not have told which side he was on” (5.85–86; see [[apostrophe]]). He sweeps across the plain like a winter torrent (5.87–94; see [[river-in-flood]]).

**The wound and the prayer.** Pandarus' arrow strikes his right shoulder, and Pandarus boasts that the best of the Achaeans will not last long (5.95–105). Sthenelus draws the arrow out, and Diomedes prays to Athena, claiming the favor she showed his father (5.106–120; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[healing]]). She hears, makes his limbs light, puts his father's might in his breast, and takes the mist from his eyes so that he can tell god from man, with one limit: he is to fight no god but Aphrodite (5.121–132).

**Three times as fierce.** “Now three times as great a fury seized him” (5.136), like a lion grazed but not killed by a shepherd (5.136–143; see [[wounded-lion]]). He kills eight men in four pairs, and three of the pairs are brothers with a father behind them: the sons of the dream-reader Eurydamas, the only sons of old Phaenops, whose kinsmen divide his wealth, and two sons of Priam (5.144–165; see [[androktasia]]).

**The great opponents.** Aeneas and Pandarus come against him in one chariot (5.166–275). He kills Pandarus and crushes Aeneas' hip with a stone (5.276–310; see [[stone-throwing]]); when Aphrodite carries her son off, he wounds her hand (5.311–351). Then he attacks Aeneas under Apollo's own protection, and at the fourth charge the god stops him: gods and men are not alike (5.431–444; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]).

**The check.** Ares enters the battle, and the aristeia pauses. Diomedes, who can now see gods, recognizes him beside Hector, shudders like a traveler at a flooding river, and tells the Achaeans to give ground with their faces to the enemy (5.590–606). He rests by his chariot, cooling his wound (5.793–798).

**The last assault.** Athena rebukes him with his father, as Agamemnon did (5.800–813; see [[mythological-paradigm]]), releases him from her command, takes Sthenelus' place in the chariot, and drives him at Ares; she turns the god's spear away and drives Diomedes' spear into his belly (5.826–859). Ares bellows like ten thousand men and goes up to the sky (5.859–867).

**What is its own.** Other aristeiai end with the hero wounded or dead. Diomedes' ends with a god wounded and gone, and the hero still standing. It is framed by Athena, who begins it and drives the chariot at its end, and its bounds are set by gods: Athena's command, Apollo's warning (5.440–442) and Dione's threat that the man who fights the gods does not live long (5.406–409; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). The episode runs into Book 6, where Diomedes, meeting Glaucus, says he will not fight the heavenly gods and tells of Lycurgus, who fought them and “did not live long” (6.128–141). The ancient name of the whole is Διομήδους ἀριστεία (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]] and [[diomedes]]).
