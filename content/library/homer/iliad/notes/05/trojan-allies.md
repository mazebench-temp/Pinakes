**The ally's case.** The word ἐπίκουρος runs through Sarpedon's rebuke of Hector (see [[sarpedon]] and [[hector]]). Hector boasted that he could hold the city “without the people and the allies” (5.473); now “we are the ones who fight, we who are only allies among you” (5.477); “I too, as an ally, have come from very far away” (5.478); Hector should be “begging the leaders of your far-famed allies to hold on unceasingly” (5.491–492). The argument is about stakes. Sarpedon left a wife, a son and possessions at home, and at Troy has “nothing of mine here that the Achaeans could carry off or drive away” (5.483–484); the Trojans are fighting for their own wives (5.486).

The complaint returns. After Sarpedon's death Glaucus tells Hector that the allies, “far from friends and native land”, are wasting their lives for him (16.538–540), and in Book 17 he threatens to take the Lycians home (17.154–155). Hector gives his own account of what he asks of them: not their numbers, but the defense of the Trojans' wives and children, for which he wears out his own people with gifts and food for the allies (17.220–226).

**The allies in the fighting.** Book 5 is the first in which the allies fight and die in numbers. Pandarus came from Zeleia, as he says, “to do a favor to brilliant Hector” (5.211), and dies (5.290–296; see [[pandarus]]). Odius of the Halizones and the Maeonian Phaestus fall to Agamemnon and Idomeneus (5.38–48; see [[odius]] and [[phaestus-son-of-borus]]). Menelaus and Antilochus kill Pylaemenes of the Paphlagonians and his charioteer Mydon (5.576–589; see [[pylaemenes]] and [[mydon]]). Amphius of Paesus, whose “fate led him to be an ally to Priam and his sons”, is killed by Ajax (5.612–618; see [[amphius-son-of-selagus]]). Odysseus kills seven Lycians (5.677–678; see [[lycians]]). Ares borrows the likeness of the Thracian leader Acamas to rouse the Trojans (5.462; see [[acamas-of-thrace]]).
