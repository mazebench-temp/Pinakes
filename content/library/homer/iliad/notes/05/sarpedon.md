**The rebuke.** Sarpedon's first words in the poem are a reproach to Hector:

{{quote:67bcc58c-80d6-539e-a425-c1a491eb70f1}}

Hector boasted, he says, that he could hold the city alone with his brothers-in-law and his brothers, without the people and the allies. The boast is known only from this report (φῆς που, “you said, I think”). Now Hector's kin cower like dogs around a lion (see [[dogs-around-a-lion]]), and the allies do the fighting. Then Sarpedon sets out his own stake:

{{quote:a90508ef-4a25-5e2f-b155-dfae6433946a}}

The ally has left wife, son and wealth at home and has nothing at Troy to lose. The Trojans have everything to lose, and Hector does not even tell them to defend their wives (ὤρεσσι, 5.486, a rare word for “wife”). Sarpedon ends with the leader's duty: to beg the allies' leaders “to hold on unceasingly, and to put aside harsh rebuke” (5.492). The last words are ambiguous. Hector is to give the allies no cause for rebukes like this one, or to stop rebuking them himself. The speech stings Hector into rallying the Trojans (5.493–497; see [[hector]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Its complaint returns after Sarpedon's death, when Glaucus accuses Hector of abandoning the allies and of leaving Sarpedon to be “prey and spoil” for the Argives (16.538–540, 17.150–151): Sarpedon's own words here, ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα (5.488).

**Son and grandson of Zeus.** “Mighty fate” brings Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, against him, and the narrator names the pair by their descent: “the son and the grandson of Zeus the cloud-gatherer” (5.631). Sarpedon is thus a half-brother of Tlepolemus' father. Tlepolemus calls him a coward and denies that he is Zeus' son. Sarpedon grants that Heracles sacked Troy, but says it was for Laomedon's bad faith, and promises Tlepolemus' soul “to Hades of the famous horses” (5.633–654; see [[tlepolemus]], [[heracles]] and [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). They throw together. His spear goes through Tlepolemus' neck, and Tlepolemus' spear through his left thigh:

{{quote:48dbacbe-43e1-54b2-9bdc-a19c24e10f45}}

ἔτι, “still”, looks ahead: in Book 16 Zeus thinks of saving his son, and lets him die (16.431–461; see [[loigos]]). Here even Odysseus is turned away from him, for “it was not fated for great-hearted Odysseus to kill the mighty son of Zeus” (5.674–675; see [[odysseus]]).

**The plea.** In their haste his companions carry him off with the spear still dragging in the wound (5.663–667). When Hector comes, Sarpedon begs him:

{{quote:9ee140e1-ce4a-5f6c-b0c9-de1cf6dd92b5}}

Hector says nothing and runs past (5.689–691). The man who rebuked Hector for neglecting the allies is himself passed by. He named his wife and infant son in the rebuke (5.480) and names them again here, and Hector will use the same words of his own wife and son (6.366).

**Under the oak.**

{{quote:7b8205dc-9953-54e9-b121-1866d5ba6a91}}

Pelagon pushes the spear out, and Sarpedon faints: his ψυχή leaves him and a mist is poured over his eyes, until the North Wind brings his spirit back (see [[pelagon-of-lycia]], [[the-oak-of-zeus]], [[psyche]], [[achlys]] and [[boreas]]). The verb ζώγρει, “revived”, is the one a beaten man uses when he begs to be taken alive (6.46, 11.131). The scene rehearses his death. In Book 16 Hera speaks of the moment “when his ψυχή and his life (αἰών) have left him” (16.453), the words of Sarpedon's plea and of his faint (5.685, 5.696), and Patroclus draws the spear from his chest, and his ψυχή with it (16.503–505). The other son of a god on the Trojan side fares the same way in this book: Aeneas is struck at the hip, black night covers his eyes, and his mother carries him off (5.305–318; see [[aeneas]]). Both are saved, for now.
