**The rescue.** When Diomedes' stone brings Aeneas down, his mother sees it in the verse with which she saved Paris from Menelaus (5.312 = 3.374):

{{quote:404d182a-8d6a-524c-9d87-0a281dd00a1a}}

In Book 4 Zeus teased Hera and Athena with her constancy: she “is always at his side and wards off doom from him”, and has just saved Paris (4.11–12; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). She tries the same for her son, and this time it fails. See [[divine-rescue]].

**The wound.** Athena, taking the mist from Diomedes' eyes, has forbidden him to fight the gods, “but if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the war, her you may wound with the sharp bronze” (5.131–132; he repeats it at 5.819–821). He goes after her “knowing that she was a feeble god” (ἄναλκις θεός), not one of the goddesses who rule over the war of men, like Athena and Enyo (5.330–333; see [[enyo]]). Only this book of Homer calls her Κύπρις (5.330, 5.422, 5.458, 5.760, 5.883), “the Cyprian”, for the island of her precinct at Paphos (*Odyssey* 8.362–363). The spear passes through the robe the Graces made her and cuts the hand at the wrist (5.336–339; see [[graces]] and [[robe]]):

{{quote:22e0313e-ee00-5cfb-8120-bb9d50179d2e}}

It is Homer's one account of what flows in the gods (see [[ichor]]). She cries out and drops her son, and Apollo takes him up (5.343–346). Diomedes shouts after her:

{{quote:1b4e8496-e3fb-5283-82cb-29c70f2de4ad}}

ἀνάλκιδας, “feeble”, is the narrator's word for the goddess herself (5.331): she is feeble, and so are the women she deceives. ἠπεροπεύεις, “deceive”, is the charge Helen made to her face (3.399; see [[eperopeuo]]).

**Olympus.** Iris leads her out “weighed down with pain”, her beautiful skin darkening (5.353–354). She falls on her knees before her “dear brother” Ares and begs his horses, for a mortal has wounded her who “would fight even with father Zeus” (5.355–362; see [[iris]] and [[ares]]). In the Odyssey Ares is her lover (*Odyssey* 8.266–366); here he is her brother. On Olympus she falls on the knees of her mother, Dione, whom no other scene in Homer names (5.370–371; see [[dione]]). Her complaint puts her son first, “Aeneas, who is dearest to me by far of all”, and then the scandal: “now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.378–380). Dione consoles her with the gods' old sufferings at the hands of men, warns of Diomedes' fate, and wipes the ichor from her hand, which heals (5.382–417).

**Mockery.** Athena and Hera, “looking on” (εἰσορόωσαι), provoke Zeus “with cutting words” (κερτομίοις ἐπέεσσι, 5.418–419). In Book 4 it was Zeus who provoked Hera with cutting words, praising Aphrodite to the goddesses who “sit apart and look on” (4.5–12). Athena turns the wound into a scratch: Cypris must have been coaxing another Achaean woman to follow the Trojans, “whom she now loves beyond measure”, and scratched her slender hand on a golden brooch (5.421–425). It is the story of Helen told as farce, and ἔκπαγλα φιλεῖν, to love “beyond measure”, is Aphrodite's own phrase for her love of Helen (3.415).

**The works of marriage.** Zeus smiles (see [[smiling-at-him]]) and calls her to him:

{{quote:5138b2fe-7b43-5b65-8872-95dd551a8a6f}}

Each god has his province (see [[gods-and-mortals]]). Hers is marriage and desire; war belongs to Ares and Athena, who will meet in it before the day is out (5.846–863). Apollo and then Ares report her wound to others in the same verse, “First he came close to Cypris and wounded her hand at the wrist” (5.458 ~ 5.883), and Hera blames “Cypris and Apollo” for letting Ares loose on the Achaeans (5.759–761). In the battle of the gods Athena strikes her down as she leads the wounded Ares away (21.416–426). See [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]].
