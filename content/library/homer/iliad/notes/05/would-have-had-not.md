summary: The turn comes four times in Book 5. Hephaestus saves Idaeus, Aphrodite saves Aeneas, Eeriboea saves Ares in Dione's story, and Hector stops Odysseus among the Lycians; and Ares, complaining to Zeus, uses it of himself.

**Hephaestus.** The first is a looser form, with ἀλλά (“but”) in place of εἰ μή: Idaeus “himself would not have escaped black doom either, but Hephaestus protected him, and saved him, covering him in night” (5.22–23). The god saves the son of his own priest, “so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief” (5.24; see [[dares]], [[idaeus-son-of-dares]] and [[divine-rescue]]).

**Aphrodite.** “And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have perished there, had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (5.311–312). The second verse is the one that saved Paris in Book 3 (3.374), and the same goddess comes to the same kind of rescue. This time it fails: Diomedes wounds her, she drops her son, and Apollo has to finish the work (5.334–346). Aeneas is a man the tradition keeps alive. Poseidon will save him again from Achilles, because he is fated to survive and to rule the Trojans (20.302–308).

**Ares.** Dione uses the narrator's device inside her own story, in the narrator's own verse with a god's name in it: “and now Ares, insatiable of war, would have perished there, had not their stepmother, the beautiful Eeriboea, brought word to Hermes” (5.388–390; 5.388 ~ 5.311). What the gods cannot suffer, death, is brought as near to Ares as it was to Aeneas seventy-seven lines earlier (see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[eeriboea]]).

**Hector.** “And brilliant Odysseus would have killed still more of the Lycians, had not great Hector of the gleaming helmet been quick to see it” (5.679–680). The second verse is the rescue verse, but the one who sees is a man, and what he saves is a people, not a single friend. Odysseus has been brought to the edge twice in a few lines: it was not fated for him to kill Sarpedon, the son of Zeus, so Athena turned him against the Lycians (5.674–676), and then Hector stopped the slaughter. The first half-verse returns when Achilles would have killed still more of the Paeonians, had not the river spoken to him (21.211–212).

**Ares again.** Wounded, Ares describes to Zeus the other story that did not happen to him: “my swift feet carried me away; or else for a long time I would have suffered woes there among the grim heaps of the dead, or lived on strengthless from the blows of the bronze” (5.885–887). A god cannot die, and the outcome he imagines in its place is to lie among the dead, or to live without strength (see [[ares]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).
