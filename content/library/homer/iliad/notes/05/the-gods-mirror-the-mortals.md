---
summary: In Book 5 the gods do what the mortals do, in the mortals' words. A wounded goddess is comforted by her mother in the verse that Thetis used for Achilles, two goddesses ride to war as two heroes do, gods complain to their father as men to their king, and Zeus rebukes Ares with Agamemnon's words to Achilles.

**A mother comforts.** Wounded, Aphrodite falls on her mother's knees, and Dione “stroked her with her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name” (5.372). It is the verse in which Thetis comforted Achilles by the sea (1.361; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). The scene of a hurt son and his divine mother is played again between two goddesses.

**Two goddesses in a chariot.** Hera proposes to Athena that they go down to the battle in the words that open her appeal in Book 2, “Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone” (5.714 = 2.157), and closes it with a verse that Diomedes spoke to Sthenelus in Book 4, “But come, let us two also think of our furious courage” (5.718 = 4.418). The goddesses arm, harness the horses and drive out like a pair of heroes, one driving and one armed (5.719–777). Then Athena takes Sthenelus' place in Diomedes' own chariot, and the oaken axle groans under the weight of “a dread goddess and the best of men” (5.835–839). A goddess becomes a charioteer; the mortal pair and the divine pair change places.

**Complaints to the king.** On Olympus the gods bring their complaints to Zeus as subjects bring theirs to a king. Hera and Ares ask him the same question, whether he does not blame these violent deeds, each about the other's side (5.757, 5.872; see [[nemesis]]). They abuse one another as men do: Hera calls Ares a fool who knows no law, and Ares calls Athena senseless and ruinous (5.761, 5.875–876; see [[phrenes]] and [[themis]]).

**The king's rebuke.** Zeus' answer to Ares repeats the words Agamemnon spoke to Achilles in the quarrel: “Of all the gods who hold Olympus, you are the most hateful to me; strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles” (5.890–891). In Book 1 it was “Of all the kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (1.176–177; see [[strife-is-always-dear]]). The king of the gods has his own Achilles, a fighter he cannot abide; unlike Agamemnon, he heals him (5.895–904). See [[gods-and-mortals]].
