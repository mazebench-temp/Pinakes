**The archer's god.** Pandarus boasts that “the lord, the son of Zeus” roused him from Lycia (5.105): the god who gave him his bow (2.827) and to whom he prayed in Book 4 (4.119–121). Nothing in the book shows the god helping him. It is Athena who guides the spear of Diomedes into his face (5.290; see [[pandarus]]).

**The rescue.** When Aphrodite, wounded, drops her son, “Phoebus Apollo took him up in his arms and protected him in a dark cloud” (5.344–345). The purpose that follows, “for fear one of the Danaans of the swift horses might strike the bronze into his chest and take away his life”, is the one that explained Aphrodite's robe (5.316–317 ~ 5.345–346). The goddess who failed and the god who succeeds are given the same words (see [[divine-rescue]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**The fourth charge.** Diomedes springs at Aeneas “though he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him” (5.433). To hold one's hands over a man or a city is to protect it; so Zeus over the Achaeans and over Troy (4.249, 9.420). “He had no awe even of the great god”:

Diomedes draws back “a little way, avoiding the wrath of Apollo who shoots from afar” (5.443–444; see [[menis]]). The sequence returns, nearly verse for verse, when Apollo stops Patroclus at the wall of Troy (16.702–711), and Patroclus, who charges a fourth time again, dies (16.784–789; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]).

**Pergamus.** Apollo sets Aeneas down “in sacred Pergamus, where his temple had been built”, and there his mother Leto and his sister Artemis heal him in the great inner shrine (5.445–448; see [[pergamus]], [[leto]] and [[artemis]]). On the field he leaves a phantom “like Aeneas himself, and in armor such as his” for the armies to fight over (5.449–453; see [[eidolon]] and [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]]).

**Ares.** Athena led Ares out of the battle at the beginning of the book (5.29–36). Apollo brings him back. He hails him with Athena's verse (5.455 = 5.31), asks him to drag out the man “who now would fight even with father Zeus” (5.457 ~ 5.362), and reports: “First he came close to Cypris and wounded her hand at the wrist, and then he rushed at me myself, like a god” (5.458–459). Ares will say the same two verses to Zeus of his own wound (5.883–884). Then Apollo sits “on the height of Pergamus” (5.460), where he looked down on the battle in Book 4 (4.508). Ares rouses the Trojans in the likeness of Acamas (5.461–470; see [[ares]] and [[acamas-of-thrace]]), “fulfilling the commands of Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who had told him to wake the spirit of the Trojans, when he saw that Pallas Athena was gone” (5.508–511). The god then sends Aeneas back from the inner shrine, with might (μένος) put into his breast (5.512–513; see [[aeneas]] and [[menos]]).

**Blame.** The battle that follows is “the toil that the god of the silver bow had awakened, and Ares, ruin of mortals, and Strife” (5.517–518; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]), and Hera complains to Zeus that “Cypris and Apollo, god of the silver bow,” take their pleasure at their ease, having let Ares loose (5.759–761). In this book Apollo does not fight. He shields, warns, and sends others in.
