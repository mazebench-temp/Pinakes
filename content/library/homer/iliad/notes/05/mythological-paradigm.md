summary: Book 5 has three paradigms, all about gods and the sons of gods. Dione consoles Aphrodite with three gods who suffered at mortal hands (5.382–404); Tlepolemus shames Sarpedon with Heracles, who sacked Troy with six ships, and Sarpedon turns the story against him (5.633–654); and Athena rouses Diomedes with Tydeus, small but a fighter (5.800–813).

**Dione.** The fullest paradigm of the book is a goddess's. Dione tells her wounded daughter to endure, since many of the gods “have endured cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another” (5.382–384), and gives three examples. Ares was bound by Otus and Ephialtes and lay in a bronze jar for thirteen months, until Hermes stole him away (5.385–391); Heracles shot Hera in the breast with a three-barbed arrow (5.392–394); and he shot Hades “in Pylos among the dead”, so that Hades went up to Olympus with the arrow in his shoulder until Paeëon healed him (5.395–402). Each story begins with τλῆ, “endured” (5.385, 5.392, 5.395), and the whole is introduced by the same verb, τέτλαθι, “be patient”, and τλῆμεν, “have endured” (5.382–383; see [[anaphora]]). The lesson of the stories turns into a threat against the man who wounded her: “fool, the son of Tydeus does not know this in his heart, that the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.406–415; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]).

The three stories are told nowhere else in Homer, and some of their details were already puzzles in antiquity, such as whether Hades was wounded “in Pylos” or “at the gate” (see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]], [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]] and [[pylos-or-the-gate]]). The Odyssey knows Otus and Ephialtes as the giants who threatened to pile Ossa and Pelion on Olympus (Odyssey 11.305–320), but not their binding of Ares. Stories told once, and shaped so exactly to the case, are the kind that M. M. Willcock thought the poet could adapt, or make, for the occasion (see [[mythological-paradigm]]).

**Tlepolemus and Sarpedon.** A paradigm can be an insult. Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, tells Sarpedon, son of Zeus, that he falls far short of the men born of Zeus in former times, and names his father as the measure: Heracles came to Troy for the horses of Laomedon with only six ships and sacked the city (5.633–646). Sarpedon reads the same story the other way. Heracles sacked Troy because of the folly of Laomedon, who “rebuked him with evil words though he had done him good, and did not give him the horses” (5.648–651), and the story proves nothing against him. He kills the man who told it (5.652–659; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]], [[tlepolemus]] and [[sarpedon]]).

**Athena's Tydeus.** Athena gives Diomedes the paradigm Agamemnon gave him in Book 4, in her own words: “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself. Tydeus, you know, was small in build, but he was a fighter” (5.800–801). When he went alone as a messenger to Thebes, she told him to feast quietly in the halls, but he challenged the young Cadmeians and beat them all, “such a helper was I to him” (5.802–808; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[small-but-a-fighter]]). The detail Agamemnon did not know, her command to feast quietly, is the point: Tydeus fought against her orders, and his son is holding back because of hers. Diomedes answers with her own words (5.819–821 ~ 5.130–132), and she releases him, repeating her phrase, “such a helper am I to you” (5.828; see [[epitarrhothos]]). Agamemnon's paradigm was answered by Sthenelus and by silence (4.401–418); Athena's is answered by Diomedes, and then by the wounding of Ares.
