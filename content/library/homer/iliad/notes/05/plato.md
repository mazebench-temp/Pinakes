---
summary: The Symposium makes two goddesses of love from the Aphrodite whose mother is Dione in Book 5 and Hesiod's Aphrodite born of Uranus; the Republic sets the helmet of Hades, which hides Athena from Ares, beside the ring of Gyges; and a dialogue handed down among Plato's works makes Athena's clearing of Diomedes' eyes a figure for teaching.
---

- The elder Aphrodite, motherless daughter of Uranus, and the younger, daughter of Zeus and Dione (*Symposium* 180d–e): [[plato-two-aphrodites]].
- The soul must be just “whether it has the ring of Gyges or not, and, besides such a ring, the helmet of Hades” (*Republic* 10.612b): [[plato-helmet-of-hades]].
- As Athena took the mist from Diomedes' eyes, a teacher must first take the mist from Alcibiades' soul (*Alcibiades II* 150d–e): [[mist-from-the-eyes]].
- The *Republic* bans “all the battles of the gods that Homer composed” (*Republic* 2.378d), and Book 5 ends with one, Athena against Ares. See [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]] and [[longinus-wounded-gods]].
- **The sons of Aloeus.** In Aristophanes' speech in the *Symposium*, the round people of the first age, strong and proud, attacked the gods, and what Homer says of Ephialtes and Otus is said of them: that they tried to climb up to heaven (*Symposium* 190b–c). The ascent is the Odyssey's story, in which the brothers meant to pile Ossa on Olympus and Pelion on Ossa (*Odyssey* 11.305–320); in Book 5 the same brothers bind Ares in a bronze jar (5.385–391). See [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]].
