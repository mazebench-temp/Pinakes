summary: Lycia is named four times. Pandarus set out from Lycia, and no one there claims to be his better; Sarpedon left his wife and son in far-off Lycia on the Xanthus, and Tlepolemus tells him that coming from Lycia will not save the Trojans.
grc: [Λυκίη, Λυκίηθεν]
---

Two Lycias may be meant. Pandarus, whose men from Zeleia under Ida the Catalogue calls Trojans (2.824–827), says that Apollo roused him “when I set out from Lycia” (5.105), and Aeneas tells him that “no one in Lycia claims to be better than you” (5.173). Sarpedon's Lycia is the far country of the south: “for Lycia is far away, on the eddying Xanthus” (5.479; see [[xanthus-of-lycia]]), where he left his wife, his infant son and his possessions (5.480–481). Tlepolemus tells him that coming “from Lycia” will make him no defense for the Trojans (5.644–646).

Strabo took Pandarus' men for Lycians of the Troad. Homer, he says, sets “the Lycia under Pandarus, in which is Zeleia” next to Aeneas' Dardania, and calls its people both Lycians and Trojans (12.4.6, 13.1.7). He adds that a Pandarus was honored at Pinara in Lycia proper, “perhaps a namesake of the Trojan” (14.3.5). The poem itself does not explain the name. See [[pandarus]], [[zeleia]] and [[lycians]].
