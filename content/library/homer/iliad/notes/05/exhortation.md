**A false report.** The book's first exhortation rests on a mistake. Pandarus, thinking he has killed Diomedes, calls on the Trojans: “Rise up, great-hearted Trojans, goaders of horses; the best of the Achaeans is hit” (5.102–103). The narrator corrects him in the next line but one (5.106; see [[pandarus]]).

**A god in disguise.** Ares, “in the likeness of Acamas, the swift leader of the Thracians”, calls to the sons of Priam: how long will they let their people be killed? until the fighting reaches the gates? Aeneas lies fallen, a man they honored as highly as Hector, “but come, let us save our brave companion from the din” (5.461–469; see [[acamas-of-thrace]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). A god gives the reasons a mortal would give: the enemy at the gates, the fallen friend. The closing verse, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (5.470), returns for Hera at the end of her rebuke (5.792) and eight times more in the poem (6.72, 11.291, 13.155, 15.500, 15.514, 15.667, 16.210, 16.275; see [[menos]] and [[thymos]]).

**The rebuke.** Sarpedon, an ally, shames his host: “Hector, where has the might gone that you used to have?” Hector's brothers and brothers-in-law cower like dogs around a lion, while the allies, who have nothing at Troy to lose, do the fighting; Hector should be urging the others to stand and defend their wives, and begging the allies to hold on (5.471–492; see [[dogs-around-a-lion]] and [[the-net]]). The words “stung Hector to the heart”, and he leaps from his chariot and goes through the army urging it on (5.493–496; see [[sarpedon]] and [[hector]]).

**“Friends, be men.”** Agamemnon's exhortation is the shortest in the book and the plainest:

{{quote:5eab5b38-2112-58ad-b538-a73e0e747a2e}}

ἀνέρες ἔστε, “be men”, opens Hector's whole-line exhortation, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (6.112). The reason is shame before one another, and it is put as a maxim: when men feel shame, more are saved than are killed (see [[aidos]] and [[shame-saves-more]]). Ajax speaks the same four verses, with a change in the first, in the battle at the ships (15.561–564).

**Retreat.** Diomedes, who can now see gods, recognizes Ares beside Hector and exhorts the Achaeans to give way: “keep your faces turned toward the Trojans and give ground always backward, and do not be eager to fight in force against gods” (5.605–606). An exhortation to retreat, and the army obeys it: the Argives neither flee to the ships nor charge, but keep giving ground (5.699–702; see [[man-at-the-river]]).

**Shame, Argives.** Hera takes the likeness of Stentor, who could shout as loud as fifty men, and gives the rebuke that Agamemnon will repeat in Book 8: “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (5.787 = 8.228). While Achilles fought, the Trojans never came out of their gates; now they fight by the ships (5.787–791; see [[stentor]] and [[ships]]).

**A goddess's paradigm.** Athena rebukes Diomedes with the example Agamemnon used in Book 4: Tydeus was small, but a fighter (5.800–813; see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[small-but-a-fighter]]). This time Diomedes answers her, and she sends him against Ares. The goddesses exhort each other too, in a hero's words: “let us two also think of our furious courage”, says Hera to Athena (5.718), in the verse with which Diomedes answered Sthenelus (4.418).
