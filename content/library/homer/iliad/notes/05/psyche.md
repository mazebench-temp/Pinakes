**At death.** When Diomedes' spear cuts through Pandarus' face and he falls from the chariot, “there his soul and his strength were loosed” (λύθη ψυχή τε μένος τε, 5.296). The ψυχή goes with the μένος that drove him (see [[menos]]). Before his duel with Tlepolemus, Sarpedon promises him that he will give “triumph to me, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (εὖχος ἐμοὶ δώσειν, ψυχὴν δʼ Ἄϊδι κλυτοπώλῳ, 5.654). The verse is a formula of the victor's boast; Odysseus and Meriones use it too (11.445, 16.625). The dead man pays twice, a boast to his killer and a soul to the god below (see [[euchomai]] and [[hades]]).

**And back.** Sarpedon wins the duel but is carried off with Tlepolemus' spear in his thigh. Under the oak of Zeus his companion Pelagon pushes it out:

{{quote:7b8205dc-9953-54e9-b121-1866d5ba6a91}}

The ψυχή leaves him, and yet he is not dead, and the scene shows what the word means. The ψυχή is the breath of life, related to ψύχω, “breathe, blow”, and a faint is a breath lost for a while. Sarpedon faints, “breathes again” (ἐμπνύνθη), and the breath (πνοιή) of the North Wind brings back the spirit he had gasped away. When Andromache sees Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot, she faints in the same way: “she gasped out her ψυχή”, and then “breathed again” (22.466–475). See [[achlys]], [[thymos]] and [[sarpedon]].
