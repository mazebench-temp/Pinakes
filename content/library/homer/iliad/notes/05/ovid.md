---
summary: Ovid's Diomedes tells how Venus avenged her wound and his companions became birds, and twice Ovid the love poet measures himself against the son of Tydeus who struck a goddess.
---

- Venus' revenge and the birds of Diomedes (*Metamorphoses* 14.457–511): [[birds-of-diomedes]].
- **The lover who struck his mistress.** Ovid, who has struck his girl in anger, calls the son of Tydeus the worst example of the crime: he was the first to strike a goddess, and the poet is the second. Yet Tydides did less harm, for he was savage to an enemy, and Ovid has hurt the woman he claimed to love (*Amores* 1.7.31–34). See [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]].
- **Not Tydides.** At the start of the *Remedies for Love*, Ovid tells Cupid not to fear him. He is not the son of Tydeus, from whom Cupid's wounded mother went back to the clear upper air with the horses of Mars (*Remedia Amoris* 5–6). In Book 5 Aphrodite borrows Ares' horses and chariot to reach Olympus (5.355–369).
