summary: Hector never speaks in Book 5; others speak of him. Stung by Sarpedon's rebuke, he rallies the Trojans, charges with Ares at his side, passes the wounded Sarpedon without a word, and with the god kills six men.

**Spoken of.** Hector has no speech in Book 5. He is the measure the others use. Pandarus led his men to Troy “to do a favor to brilliant Hector” (5.211); Ares, in the likeness of Acamas, tells the sons of Priam that Aeneas, who lies fallen, was honored “as highly as brilliant Hector” (5.467); Sarpedon reproaches him, and Diomedes explains why he must give ground before him (5.471–492, 5.601–606).

**Stung.** Sarpedon's rebuke begins with a boast of Hector's own, that he would hold the city “without the people and the allies, alone, with your brothers-in-law and your brothers” (5.473–474), and ends with his duty to the allies (5.490–492; see [[sarpedon]]). Hector does not answer in words:

{{quote:54dbe5b0-da81-5d91-a14a-d574f09c23f3}}

The last four lines are Hector's rally. They return when Helenus sends him through the army (6.103–106) and, all but the first word, when Iris brings him the message of Zeus (11.211–214). In Book 3 Hector was the one who rebuked (3.38–57); here an ally rebukes him, the first of several such reproaches (16.538–547, 17.142–168; see [[trojan-allies]]).

**With Ares.** When Menelaus and Antilochus have killed Pylaemenes and his charioteer, Hector charges, and the gods go with him: “Ares led them, and lady Enyo”, and Ares “ranged now in front of Hector, now behind him” (5.590–595; see [[enyo]] and [[ares]]). Diomedes can see the god, since Athena has taken the mist from his eyes (5.127–128; see [[achlys]]), and he gives ground:

{{quote:ce25b833-31dc-54b6-8817-bf3cbf4c98d8}}

The praise, αἰχμητήν τʼ ἔμεναι καὶ θαρσαλέον πολεμιστήν, is a formula: the dying Sarpedon uses the same words to Glaucus (16.493). From this book on, Hector is often compared to Ares. He has “the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.349), he is “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (11.295, 13.802), and he rages like spear-wielding Ares or a fire in the mountains (15.605–606). Here he kills two men in one chariot, Menesthes and Anchialus (5.608–609; see [[menesthes]] and [[anchialus]]).

**Passing Sarpedon.** When Odysseus is killing the Lycians, “great Hector of the gleaming helmet” is “quick to see it” (5.680; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]] and [[would-have-had-not]]) and comes through the champions, “bringing terror to the Danaans” (5.682). Sarpedon begs him not to leave him as prey for the Danaans (5.684–688):

{{quote:3753f24c-87d2-5fa4-bc78-bd9c88a88f70}}

He does what Sarpedon's rebuke asked of him and drives the Argives back, but he does not answer the ally who asked it. In Book 17 Glaucus will charge him with leaving Sarpedon, “your guest and companion”, to be prey and spoil for the Argives (17.150–151).

**Whom first, whom last.** The Argives give ground “before Ares and Hector armed in bronze” (5.699), and the narrator asks:

{{quote:6c1cf391-330c-51a1-bdc5-b3f11578b218}}

The same question introduces Hector's killings in Book 11 and Patroclus' in Book 16 (11.299–300, 16.692–693; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]). Here the poem does not divide the dead between the man and the god (see [[teuthras]], [[orestes-the-achaean]], [[trechus]], [[oenomaus]], [[helenus-son-of-oenops]] and [[oresbius]]). Seeing the slaughter, Hera calls Athena to arms (5.711–718).
