**The wounder of gods.** Dione comforts her wounded daughter with the gods who have suffered at the hands of men (see [[dione]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Two of her three examples are the work of Heracles:

{{quote:3f215c34-dcb1-5b22-a7e3-b8c18354bcb9}}

A few lines give him both his fathers: “the mighty son of Amphitryon” (5.392), his mother's husband (see [[amphitryon]]), and “the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.396). Homer tells of the wound of Hera only here. Hades was shot “in Pylos among the dead”, or, as the same letters can be read, “at the gate”, the gate of the underworld; the first points to Heracles' war on Pylos (11.690–693), the second to his descent for the dog of Hades (8.366–369; see [[pylos-or-the-gate]] and [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). Dione's judgment follows: σχέτλιος, “hard, relentless”, and ὀβριμοεργός, “doer of violent deeds”, the word Priam will use of Achilles (22.418; see [[doer-of-violent-deeds]]). For Diomedes, Heracles is both a precedent and a warning: a mortal who wounded gods, and a reminder that “the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.407). Even Heracles died (18.117–119).

**The sacker of Troy.** Tlepolemus measures Sarpedon against the sons of Zeus of former times, and above all against his own father:

{{quote:7400d433-c36f-5e27-9b06-3e4f3c1393ad}}

βίην Ἡρακληείην is literally “the Heraclean might” (see [[might-of-heracles]]). The two epithets that follow stand together only for him, here and in the Odyssey (Od. 11.267): θρασυμέμνων, “bold in endurance”, and θυμολέων, “lion-hearted”, which is used of Achilles too (7.228; see [[bold-in-endurance]] and [[lion-hearted]]). Sarpedon grants the fact and changes its meaning:

{{quote:d6044c66-71e8-5a17-93fe-509749bb8127}}

Homer does not say what good Heracles did Laomedon, or which horses he was promised. Elsewhere he tells that the Trojans and Athena built Heracles a wall to escape the sea monster (20.144–148), and in this book that Zeus gave Tros a breed of horses in recompense for Ganymede, from which Anchises stole a strain “without Laomedon's knowledge” (5.265–269; see [[tros]], [[laomedon]] and [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). Laomedon cheated gods as well: Poseidon and Apollo served him for a year for a wage he refused to pay (21.441–457). In Sarpedon's version Troy fell the first time for its king's bad faith, as the Achaeans now expect it to fall for the broken oaths (4.155–168; see [[oath]]).
