---
summary: In Book 5 μοῖρα is death and the power that leads men to it. Hypsenor is seized by “purple death and mighty fate”, fate leads Amphius to Troy and rouses Tlepolemus against Sarpedon, and it is not fated for Odysseus to kill the son of Zeus.
grc: [μόρσιμον]
en: [fated]
---

**Death.** When Eurypylus shears off Hypsenor's arm, “his eyes were seized by purple death and mighty fate” (πορφύρεος θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, 5.82–83; see [[purple-death]] and [[thanatos]]). The pair θάνατος and μοῖρα is a formula for the end of a life: the event and the portion that decreed it.

**A power that leads.** Twice in Book 5 μοῖρα moves a man toward his death. Amphius lived in Paesus, rich in possessions and grainland, “but his fate led him to be an ally to Priam and his sons” (5.612–614; see [[amphius-son-of-selagus]]), and Ajax kills him. Tlepolemus, the son of Heracles, “was roused against godlike Sarpedon by mighty fate” (5.628–629), and dies at his hand. The duel is between a son and a grandson of Zeus (5.631), and its outcome is fixed before it begins.

**Fated.** μόρσιμος, “fated”, from the same root as μόρος and μοῖρα, marks the limit in the other direction. When Odysseus wonders whether to pursue the wounded Sarpedon, “it was not fated for great-hearted Odysseus to kill the mighty son of Zeus with the sharp bronze” (5.674–675), and Athena turns his heart against the other Lycians. Sarpedon is saved here for a death that belongs to another man, Patroclus, in Book 16 (see [[sarpedon]]).
