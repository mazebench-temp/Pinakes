summary: Book 5 is the first long book of killing. Its dead often come in pairs, brothers or the two men of one chariot; their obituaries look back to fathers, priests and homes; their wounds are told exactly; and one man is killed by a god.

**In pairs.** Many of Book 5's dead die two by two. Diomedes goes after Abas and Polyidus, the sons of Eurydamas, then Xanthus and Thoon, the sons of Phaenops, then two sons of Priam “who were in one chariot”, Echemmon and Chromius (5.148–165). Aeneas kills the twin sons of Diocles, who fall together like tall fir trees (5.541–560). Hector kills two men “who were in one chariot”, Menesthes and Anchialus (5.608–609), and Menelaus and Antilochus kill Pylaemenes and his charioteer (5.576–589). The pairs come from the chariot, which carries a fighter and his driver, sometimes two brothers; they also double the grief, since each pair of brothers leaves a father (see [[bereaved-parents]] and [[chariot]]).

**The history of the dead.** The obituaries give the dead a place and a past: a priest's sons (5.9–11, 5.76–78), a hunter whom Artemis taught (5.49–54), the builder of Alexander's ships (5.59–64), a bastard reared by his father's wife (5.69–71), a rich man of Paesus whom fate led to Troy (5.612–614), a man of Hyle by the Cephisian lake, intent on his wealth (5.707–710). See [[the-war-in-small]] and [[the-beginning-of-evils]].

**The wounds.** The wounds are told with the exactness of Book 4. The spear goes through Phereclus' buttock under the bone into the bladder (5.66–67); it goes in at the back of Pedaeus' head and cuts under his tongue, and he “bit the cold bronze with his teeth” (5.73–75); it goes through Pandarus' nose and teeth and comes out under the chin (5.290–293). Mydon, struck from his chariot, lands head first in deep sand and stands there until his own horses kick him over (5.585–588). The formulas close each death: he fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes (see [[fell-with-a-thud]], [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[purple-death]]).

**A god kills.** When Athena and Diomedes drive at Ares, he is stripping Periphas, “by far the best of the Aetolians”, whom he has just killed (5.842–848; see [[periphas]]). A god has killed a man with his own hands, and a man then wounds the god. When Hector and Ares advance together, the narrator asks the question he asks at the height of great fights, “Then whom first, whom last did they slay, Hector, the son of Priam, and bronze Ares?” (5.703–704), and gives six names. See [[androktasia]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].
