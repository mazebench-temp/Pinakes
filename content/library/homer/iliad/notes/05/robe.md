**The peplos.** Book 5 names the robe with its other word, πέπλος: a large piece of woven cloth, a woman's or a goddess's robe, pinned at the shoulders with brooches (see [[brooch]]). It could also serve as a cover. Pandarus' eleven chariots at home have πέπλοι spread over them (5.194; see [[chariot]]).

**A shield of cloth.** When Diomedes' stone brings Aeneas down, Aphrodite throws her white arms around her son, “and in front of him she spread a fold of her shining robe as a shelter from missiles, for fear one of the Danaans of the swift horses might strike the bronze into his chest and take away his life” (5.314–317). πτύγμα, “fold”, occurs only here. ἕρκος, “barrier, shelter”, is the word for armor: Menelaus' band was “a barrier against javelins” (4.137). A goddess's robe is made to do a shield's work, and it fails. Diomedes pursues her, and his spear pierces her hand “through the ambrosial robe that the Graces themselves had made for her” (5.337–338). The Graces serve Aphrodite; in the Odyssey they bathe and anoint her at Paphos and dress her in lovely clothes (Odyssey 8.362–366; see [[graces]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**Athena's robe.** Arming against Ares, Athena “let her soft robe fall on her father's floor, richly worked, which she herself had made and labored at with her hands” (5.734–735). ἑανός, “soft”, is the adjective discussed under [[robe]]; ποικίλος, “richly worked”, is cloth with patterns woven in. Athena is the goddess of weaving, who made Hera's ambrosial robe as well (14.178–179; see [[weaving]]). She puts off the robe of her own making and puts on the tunic of Zeus, and the goddess of the loom becomes the goddess of war (5.736–737; see [[tunic]] and [[arming-scene]]).

The two robes answer each other. Aphrodite's, made for her by others, is brought into the war and pierced; Athena's, made by her own hands, is left on the floor before she goes to war. Zeus puts the difference into words: the works of war are not Aphrodite's, but “the lovely works of marriage” (5.428–430). In Book 6 the Trojan women will lay a robe on the knees of Athena's statue and pray to her, and she will refuse them (6.286–311).
