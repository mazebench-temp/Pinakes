summary: Pandarus' arrow goes through Diomedes' shoulder, and he boasts too soon; Sthenelus draws it out clean through, and Pandarus blames a god for turning it. In Dione's stories Heracles' arrows wound Hera and Hades, and Hades goes up to Olympus with the arrow still in his shoulder.

**The arrow in the shoulder.** Pandarus draws his curved bow and hits Diomedes on the right shoulder, “and the bitter arrow flew through it, and held straight on” (5.97–100; see [[corselet]] and [[bitter]]). He shouts that the best of the Achaeans is hit and will not “hold out long against the mighty shaft” (5.103–104), and the narrator answers him at once: “but the swift shaft had not overcome him” (5.106). Diomedes asks Sthenelus to draw the arrow out, and Sthenelus “drew the swift shaft clean through and out of his shoulder; and the blood spurted up through the plaited tunic” (5.110–113). The arrow had gone right through, and διαμπερές, “clean through”, suggests that he drew it out on the far side rather than back the way it came (see [[healing]]). At the end of the book the wound is still there: Diomedes is cooling “the wound where Pandarus had hit him with his arrow” (5.795).

**A god turned it.** Pandarus cannot understand why his arrow did not kill. Some god, he tells Aeneas, stands beside Diomedes, wrapped in cloud, “who turned my swift shaft aside as it reached him” (5.185–187); and yet he hit him, straight through the corselet (5.188–191). He has hit two chieftains and drawn real blood from both, and only roused them (5.206–208). Athena did turn his arrow in Book 4 (4.127–133). This time the arrow went in, and it was not a god but Sthenelus who drew it out (see [[pandarus]]).

**Missiles.** βέλος is any missile. Aphrodite spreads her robe before Aeneas “as a shelter from missiles” (ἕρκος … βελέων, 5.316), and Ajax is “hard pressed by missiles”, the spears the Trojans shower on him (5.622). Diomedes' βέλος is his spear, at 5.18 and at 5.290, where “Athena guided the shaft” into Pandarus' face (see [[spear]]).

**Heracles' arrows.** Dione's stories put the bow in the hands of the greatest hero of the past. Heracles shot Hera in the right breast “with a three-barbed arrow” (ὀϊστῷ τριγλώχινι, 5.393), from γλωχίς, “point”; Paris wounds Machaon with a three-barbed arrow in Book 11 (11.507). He shot Hades with “a swift arrow”, and the god went up to the house of Zeus “pierced through with pains; for the arrow had been driven into his massive shoulder, and it troubled his spirit” (5.395–400), until Paeëon healed him (see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]], [[hades]] and [[paeeon]]).
