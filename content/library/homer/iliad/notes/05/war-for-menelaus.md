---
summary: In Book 5 the cause of the war is named in an obituary and by the gods: the twin sons of Diocles came to Troy “seeking satisfaction for the sons of Atreus” (5.550–553), and Hera speaks of the promise that Menelaus would sack Ilios and sail home (5.714–718). On the other side Sarpedon fights with nothing of his own at stake.
passages:
  - 5.550-553 | The sons of Diocles come to Troy
  - 5.714-718 | Hera: the promise to Menelaus
---

**The obituary.** Aeneas kills the twin sons of Diocles, and the narrator tells why they were at Troy.

{{quote:a8ac00c9-cd1e-5152-8526-580c1450ba04}}

τιμὴν … ἀρνυμένω, “seeking satisfaction”, is Achilles' phrase in Book 1, where the whole army came “to win satisfaction for Menelaus” (τιμὴν ἀρνύμενοι, 1.159). There it was a charge against Agamemnon; here it is the reason two young men from Pherae sailed to Troy and died there (see [[crethon]], [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]] and [[two-lions]]). The debt of honor owed to the sons of Atreus is paid by other men's sons.

**The gods' promise.** When Hera sees Ares killing the Argives beside Hector, she tells Athena that “it was an empty word we promised Menelaus, that he would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home, if we let baneful Ares rage like this” (5.715–717). The promise is not reported anywhere else in the Iliad. The two goddesses who would not have the peace that Zeus proposed in Book 4 (4.20–29) have bound themselves to Menelaus' cause, and their descent to the battle in Book 5 is made to keep their word (see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

**A war with no stake.** On the Trojan side Sarpedon says what the allies fight for. He has come from far-off Lycia, leaving his wife, his infant son and his wealth, “and yet there is nothing of mine here that the Achaeans could carry off or drive away” (5.480–484). It is Achilles' argument in Book 1 from the other side of the war: the Trojans never drove off his cattle or his horses, and mountains and sea lie between (1.152–157). Neither man has a quarrel of his own at Troy, and both fight. Pandarus came, he says, “to do a favor to brilliant Hector” (5.211). See [[sarpedon]] and [[trojan-allies]].
