---
summary: Book 5 explains the gods' name: they are bloodless and “are called immortals” because they eat no bread and drink no wine. The word marks the line that Diomedes crosses and is warned not to cross.
---

The word is at the center of the book's question, whether a man may fight the gods. Athena forbids Diomedes to fight “face to face with the immortal gods,” except Aphrodite (5.130–132). Pandarus suspects that “someone of the immortals stands near him, his shoulders wrapped in cloud” (5.185–186). Aphrodite complains to Dione that “now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.380), and Dione answers that “the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.407; [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Apollo's warning sets the two races apart: “never alike is the race of immortal gods and of men who walk on the earth” (5.441–442; [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). At the end Ares complains that Athena has let Diomedes loose “to rage madly against the immortal gods” (5.882).

The narrator explains the name at the moment of the first wound:

{{quote:22e0313e-ee00-5cfb-8120-bb9d50179d2e}}

The gods are ἀθάνατοι because they are ἀναίμονες, without blood, and bloodless because they do not eat bread or drink wine, the food of men ([[ichor]], [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]). The reasoning ties mortality to the food of men.
