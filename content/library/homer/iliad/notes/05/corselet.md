**Through the plate.** Pandarus hits Diomedes “on the right shoulder, on the plate of his corselet; and the bitter arrow flew through it, and held straight on, and the corselet was spattered with blood” (5.98–100). γύαλον is a hollow, here the curved plate of the corselet, shaped to the body; Meges' corselet is “fitted with curved plates” (15.530; see [[corselet]]). Pandarus repeats the detail when he complains to Aeneas that he hit Diomedes “straight through the plate of his corselet” and did not kill him (5.188–191). The same half-verse comes back in Book 13 with one word changed. Helenus shoots Menelaus “on the plate of his corselet”, and the bitter arrow flies off it, ἀπὸ δʼ ἔπτατο, where Pandarus' flew through, διὰ δʼ ἔπτατο (13.586–587 ~ 5.99).

**Held.** In the second exchange Pandarus throws his spear, and it goes through Diomedes' shield and “reached his corselet” (θώρηκι πελάσθη, 5.282). Pandarus shouts that he has hit him “clean through the flank” and has won a great triumph (5.284–285), but Diomedes answers, “You missed; you did not hit me” (5.287). The corselet has held, and Pandarus' next moment is his last (see [[pandarus]] and [[shield]]).

**Arming.** The verb θωρήσσω, “put on the corselet”, is the verb of arming as a whole. Athena lets her robe fall, puts on the tunic of Zeus, and “armed herself in armor for tearful war” (5.736–737). She puts on no corselet; the tunic of her father takes its place, and the aegis, the helmet and the spear complete her arming (see [[tunic]], [[aegis]] and [[arming-scene]]).
