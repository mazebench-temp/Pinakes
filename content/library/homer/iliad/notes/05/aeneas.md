**The partner.** Aeneas enters the action when Diomedes is “wreaking havoc on the ranks of men” (5.166). He goes looking for Pandarus in the verses with which Athena went looking for him in Book 4 (5.168–169 ~ 4.88–89), and like her he wants an arrow:

{{quote:ac904230-eb3b-5ca0-9b42-bf16c1fcecf3}}

Athena offered the archer thanks and gifts; Aeneas appeals to his fame (κλέος) and tells him to pray, and he is the first to wonder whether the man is a god “in wrath over sacrifices” (see [[menis]] and [[kotos]]). When Pandarus answers with his regrets (see [[pandarus]]), Aeneas takes charge: “Do not talk this way … mount my chariot, so that you may see what the horses of Tros are like” (5.218–222; see [[tros]]). He offers either task, to drive or to fight, and Pandarus takes the spear, leaving the reins to the driver whose voice the horses know (5.226–238). Pandarus calls him “counselor of the bronze-armored Trojans” (5.180; see [[counselor]]), as Deiphobus and Apollo will (13.463, 20.83).

**A claim.** Sthenelus names the pair as they come. Aeneas “claims that he was born the son of noble Anchises, and his mother is Aphrodite” (5.247–248). εὔχεται is the verb of a proud declaration of birth (see [[euchomai]]), and Aeneas will make the claim in almost these words himself when he faces Achilles (20.208–209). The divine mother is his distinction and, in this book, his protection.

**The stone.** When Pandarus falls, Aeneas stands over the body like a lion until Diomedes lifts a stone that two men of today could not carry (5.297–304; see [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[men-of-today]]):

{{quote:7b631d9b-cf15-58e3-ac88-372d61d54a2a}}

The narrator names the joint, the κοτύλη or “cup”. The last two verses return when Diomedes' spear, striking Hector's helmet, stuns him (5.309–310 ~ 11.355–356). The two Trojans who come nearest to death at his hands are the two on whom, the seer Helenus says, the toil of the Trojans rests (6.77–79).

**Two rescues.** “And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have perished there” (5.311; see [[would-have-had-not]]). ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν belongs almost only to Agamemnon; this book gives it to Aeneas and, forty lines earlier, to his father (5.268; see [[lord-of-men]]). His mother wraps him in her robe and is wounded (see [[aphrodite]]); Apollo takes him up in a cloud, shields him from Diomedes' charges, and sets him down in his temple on Pergamus, where Leto and Artemis heal him and “gave him glory” (5.343–448; see [[apollo]] and [[divine-rescue]]). His horses are lost. Sthenelus drives them to the ships (5.319–327), and Diomedes will drive them again, the horses “I took them once from Aeneas” (8.108; see also 23.291–292).

**Honored.** While the armies fight over his phantom, Ares in the likeness of Acamas rallies the sons of Priam to save “a man … whom we honored as highly as brilliant Hector, Aeneas, son of great-hearted Anchises” (5.467–468; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]]). Aeneas himself thinks otherwise: in Book 13 he resents Priam, because, “brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.460–461).

**Return.**

{{quote:bd4f8496-d5dd-5a1c-a8e5-f7b7b0b87abd}}

The Trojans will welcome Hector back from his duel with Ajax in the same verse, “alive and unhurt” (5.515 = 7.308). Aeneas answers the death of his companion Deicoon (5.533–540; see [[deicoon]]) by killing the twin sons of Diocles, who fall “like tall fir trees” (5.541–560; see [[crethon]], [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]] and [[two-lions]]). Ares urges Menelaus on, “intending that he should be brought down at the hands of Aeneas” (5.563–564): the death that Agamemnon feared would send the army home (4.169–175). But Antilochus takes his stand beside Menelaus, and Aeneas, “swift warrior though he was”, gives way before two men (5.571–572). The book does not name him again.
