summary: The same words, “so he spoke, εὐχόμενος”, close Pandarus' boast and Diomedes' prayer fifteen lines apart. Book 5 uses the verb for boasts that fail, for prayers that are heard, and for claims of birth.
grc: [ἐπεύχεται]
en: [boasts, triumph]
passages:
  - 5.95-106 | Pandarus boasts
  - 5.114-121 | Diomedes prays to Athena
---

**Boast and prayer.** Pandarus hits Diomedes in the shoulder and shouts to the Trojans that the best of the Achaeans is hit and will not hold out long; “So he spoke, boasting” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος, 5.106). Diomedes has the arrow drawn out and prays to Athena to let him kill the man “who struck me first, and boasts over me” (ἐπεύχεται, 5.119); “So he spoke in prayer” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος, 5.121). The Greek verse is the same. The boast is answered at once by the narrator, “but the swift shaft had not overcome him” (5.106), and the prayer by the goddess, “and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121). The one verb covers both, since both are solemn declarations; the translation must choose (see [[prayer]]).

**Triumph given.** The noun εὖχος is the triumph a killer claims from his victim. Pandarus claims it too soon a second time, “you have given me a great triumph” (5.285), and dies in the next lines (5.290–296). Sarpedon claims it before his duel with Tlepolemus, “you will give me triumph, and your soul to Hades” (5.654; see [[psyche]]), and wins it.

**Claims of birth.** εὔχομαι also states who a man is. No one in Lycia “claims to be better” than Pandarus with the bow (5.173). Sthenelus names the two men driving at them: Pandarus “claims to be the son of Lycaon”, and Aeneas “claims that he was born the son of noble Anchises, and his mother is Aphrodite” (5.246–248). The claim is not a doubt. It is the form in which a hero's lineage is declared (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).
