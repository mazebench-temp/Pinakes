“Then in turn Sarpedon sharply rebuked brilliant Hector” (μάλα νείκεσεν, 5.471). The νεῖκος that follows is one of the Iliad's model rebukes. It opens with a question that is an accusation, “Hector, where has the might gone that you used to have?” (5.472); it sets the rebuker's own sacrifice against the other's idleness, a wife and infant son left in far Lycia while Hector's kinsmen cower (5.473–486); it warns of the result, the city sacked (5.487–489); and it ends with what Hector ought to do (5.490–492). The rebuke works: “his words stung Hector to the heart” (δάκε δὲ φρένας Ἕκτορι μῦθος, 5.493), and he leaps from his chariot and rouses the army (5.494–496). See [[sarpedon]], [[hector]] and [[exhortation]].

Book 5 has two more rebukes without the word. Athena rebukes Diomedes as Agamemnon did, with his father's example (5.800–813; see [[small-but-a-fighter]]), and Zeus rebukes Ares (5.889–898). Tlepolemus and Sarpedon exchange insults before their duel (5.633–654), the flyting that is the rebuke's companion in battle (see [[kertomia]]).
