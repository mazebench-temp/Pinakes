summary: In Dione's story Hera was once shot in the breast by Heracles, and in the present she joins Athena in mocking the wounded Aphrodite. When Ares rages beside Hector, she harnesses her chariot, wins Zeus' leave to drive Ares from the battle, and shouts to the Argives in the likeness of Stentor; at the end she goes home with Athena, the work done.

**Shot by Heracles.** Dione's second example of a god who suffered at a mortal's hands is Hera:

{{quote:3c8be4a2-6c06-5a7b-b5bc-0a572450dc2b}}

The wound is told nowhere else. Elsewhere in the Iliad Hera is Heracles' persecutor, not his victim: she tricked Zeus on the day of his birth (19.95–133), and when he sailed home from sacking Troy she drove him off course to Cos, for which Zeus hung her from the sky (14.249–262, 15.18–30). See [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]], [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]] and [[amphitryon]].

**The chariot.** With Athena she provokes Zeus over the wounded Aphrodite (5.418–425; see [[athena]]). Later, seeing Ares and Hector cut down the Argives, she turns to Athena: the goddesses' promise to Menelaus, that he would sack Ilios and sail home, will come to nothing “if we let baneful Ares rage like this” (5.711–718). The promise is not told elsewhere. She harnesses the horses herself, “Hera, the august goddess, daughter of great Cronus” (5.721; see [[cronus]]), while Hebe puts on the wheels (5.722–723; see [[hebe]]). The chariot is of gold, silver, bronze and iron (5.722–732; see [[chariot]]). She drives out through the gates of heaven that the Hours keep (5.748–752; see [[hours]]).

**Leave from Zeus.** She finds Zeus sitting apart on the topmost peak of Olympus and asks whether he does not blame Ares for “these violent deeds” (5.753–763). Her question begins as Athena's mockery of Aphrodite began, “Father Zeus, will you be angry with me” (5.762 ~ 5.421). He tells her to rouse Athena against Ares (5.765–766; see [[zeus]]). Her horses cover at a bound as much of the hazy sea as a man sees from a lookout (5.770–772; see [[the-lookout]] and [[longinus-horses-of-the-gods]]). She halts where the Simoeis joins the Scamander, hides the horses in mist, and the river sends up ambrosia for them (5.773–777; see [[simoeis]]). The two goddesses go on foot, “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]).

**Stentor's voice.** Among the Argives she shouts in the likeness of Stentor, who could shout as loud as fifty men (5.784–786; see [[stentor]]):

{{quote:bad7358b-c74f-5eb4-ae0f-51461f82bc5b}}

The first line is Agamemnon's in Book 8 (5.787 = 8.228; see [[aidos]]). The last overstates the case. Here the Trojans are fighting on the plain; the line comes true when it returns in Book 13, with the Trojans at the ships (5.791 = 13.107). Her point is the absence of Achilles, which Apollo has already told the Trojans (4.512–513) and which Achilles himself will state in Book 9 (9.352–355).

**His mother.** Zeus tells Ares that he has “your mother's fury … Hera's”, and that he suffers by her promptings (5.892–894; see [[ares]]). The god she asked Zeus to punish is her own son. She goes home with Athena, “Argive Hera” (5.908; see [[argive]]), having stopped Ares from his slaughter of men (5.909).
