**Falling from the chariot.** Many of the dead of Book 5 are killed in their chariots or as they mount them: Phegeus, Odius, Phaestus, two sons of Priam “who were in one chariot”, and two men whom Hector kills in one chariot (5.19, 5.39, 5.46, 5.160, 5.609). The fall has its own half-verse, ἤριπε δʼ ἐξ ὀχέων, “he fell from the chariot” (5.47, 5.294). Mydon, Pylaemenes' charioteer, falls headfirst and stands a long while with his head in deep sand, until his own horses kick him flat (5.580–589).

**Driver and fighter.** A chariot carries a driver and a fighter (see [[chariot]]). Aeneas asks Pandarus to mount his car and offers him the choice: take the whip and the reins while Aeneas gets down to fight, or face Diomedes himself while Aeneas minds the horses (5.221–228). Pandarus chooses to fight. The horses will draw the car better under the driver they know, he says, and without Aeneas' voice they might take fright and balk (5.230–238). The driver keeps the team close, the fighter meets the enemy, and the team is the way out. Diomedes, who fights on foot, will not even mount: “I am loath to mount the chariot” (5.255).

**Away from the din.** Diomedes tells Sthenelus to tie their own reins to the chariot rail and rush at Aeneas' horses (5.259–264), and Sthenelus does it. He “held his own single-hoofed horses away from the din, and tied the reins fast to the chariot rail”, drove off Aeneas' team, and gave it to Deipylus to take to the ships (5.319–327; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). The ἄντυξ is the curved rail around the front of the car; Hera's chariot has two (5.728).

**Eleven chariots at home.** Pandarus came to Troy on foot. In his father's halls stand eleven chariots, “beautiful, newly built, just finished”, under cloths, with a pair of horses beside each (5.192–196; see [[lycaon]] and [[horses]]). πρωτοπαγής, “newly built”, and νεοτευχής, “just finished”, occur only here. They are like the Myrmidons' chariots, covered in the huts while their master keeps out of the war (2.777).

**The chariot of Hera.** The fullest description of a chariot in Homer is of a god's:

{{quote:d1461fdd-bf7f-5425-b312-031d6572f09c}}

It names the parts in order, from the wheels to the yoke. The wheels (κύκλα) are of bronze and have eight spokes (ὀκτάκνημα, found only here), on an axle of iron (see [[iron]]); their rims (ἴτυς) are of gold, with tires (ἐπίσσωτρα) of bronze fitted over them; the hubs (πλῆμναι) are silver. The car is strung with straps of gold and silver and has two rails; the pole (ῥυμός) is silver, and at its end Hera binds a golden yoke and golden breast straps (λέπαδνα). The parts are those of a real chariot, whose wheel rim was bent from green poplar (4.482–487); the metals are the gods' (see [[gold-and-silver]], [[hebe]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

**The oaken axle.** At the end of the book a goddess mounts a mortal's car. Athena pulls Sthenelus down and takes his place, “and the oaken axle groaned loudly under the weight; for it carried a dread goddess and the best of men” (5.838–839). φήγινος, “of oak”, occurs only here; Hera's axle was iron. She takes the whip and the reins and drives straight at Ares, who lunges “over the yoke and the horses' reins” (5.840–851; see [[athena]]). The book's first chariot scene turned on who would drive and who would fight; in its last, a goddess drives and the man fights.
