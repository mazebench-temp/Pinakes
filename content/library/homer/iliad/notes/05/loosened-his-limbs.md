---
summary: Aeneas says of Diomedes that “he has loosened the knees of many brave men” (5.176), and Pandarus dies in a variant of the formula, “there his soul and his strength were loosed” (5.296).
grc: ["γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν", "λύθη ψυχή τε μένος τε"]
en: [loosened the knees, were loosed]
---

**The knees.** Aeneas, looking for Pandarus, describes the man who is routing the Trojans: whoever he is, he “has done much harm to the Trojans, since he has loosened the knees of many brave men” (5.175–176). γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν, “loosened the knees”, puts the killing blow in the knees, on which a man stands (see [[gounata]]). The same verse-end is used of Patroclus when Sarpedon goes to meet him, “since he has loosened the knees of many brave men” (16.425), and the knees of the dying are loosened in the same words in the later battles (11.579, 13.412).

**Soul and strength.** Pandarus falls from the chariot with his armor clattering, and his horses shy aside, “and there his soul and his strength were loosed” (τοῦ δʼ αὖθι λύθη ψυχή τε μένος τε, 5.296). The verb is the same, but what is loosened is not the limbs: it is the ψυχή, the life that leaves the body at death, and the μένος, the might that drove it (see [[psyche]] and [[menos]]). The verse comes back twice in Book 8, each time for a charioteer of Hector (8.123, 8.315). See [[androktasia]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]].
