Book 5 tells the Iliad's first stories of Laomedon, and both are about horses. Diomedes, telling the pedigree of Aeneas' pair, says that Zeus gave the breed to Tros in recompense for Ganymede, and that Anchises “stole a strain without Laomedon's knowledge, putting his mares to them” (5.265–269; see [[tros]] and [[anchises-steals-the-horses]]). Laomedon, Tros' grandson and Priam's father (20.236–237), owned the stallions.

Later in the book horses bring down his city. Tlepolemus, a son of Heracles, boasts that his father “once came here for the sake of the horses of Laomedon with only six ships and fewer men, and sacked the city of Ilios, and widowed its streets” (5.640–642). Sarpedon grants the fact and turns its meaning:

{{quote:d6044c66-71e8-5a17-93fe-509749bb8127}}

The city fell “through the folly of a man” (ἀφραδίῃσιν): a king who took a service and would not pay for it. ἀγαυοῦ, “noble”, is an epithet of rank, not a judgment. The Iliad does not say that these horses were of the breed of Tros, or what the service was, but it knows the sea monster: in Book 20 the gods sit on “the high wall of godlike Heracles”, which the Trojans and Athena built for him to escape the monster (κῆτος, 20.144–148). In Book 21 Poseidon tells how Laomedon cheated him and Apollo of their wages (21.441–457). Book 5 gives the first stroke of the portrait: the Trojan king who did not pay what he promised, told in the book that follows the breaking of the oaths (see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).
