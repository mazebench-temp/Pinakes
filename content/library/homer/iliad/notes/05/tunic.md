**The plaited tunic.** Pandarus' arrow has gone through the plate of Diomedes' corselet (5.99), and when Sthenelus draws it out, “the blood spurted up through the plaited tunic” (αἷμα δʼ ἀνηκόντιζε διὰ στρεπτοῖο χιτῶνος, 5.113). The tunic is the layer under the corselet (see [[corselet]]). στρεπτός, “twisted, plaited”, is said of a tunic here and once more, of the young Trojans whom Achilles takes alive at the river and binds with the belts they wore “on their plaited tunics” (21.30–31). What kind of tunic it was is not certain: one woven of twisted thread, or one made to protect the body under the armor.

**The tunic of Zeus.** Athena arms in her father's house: she lets her own robe fall on his floor, “and putting on the tunic of Zeus the cloud-gatherer she armed herself in armor for tearful war” (5.734–737). The tunic takes the place of the corselet in her arming, and it is her father's, like the aegis she throws over her shoulders next (see [[aegis]], [[arming-scene]] and [[zeus]]). The goddess who wove her own robe goes to war dressed by her father (see [[robe]]). The same verses arm her again in Book 8 (8.385–388).
