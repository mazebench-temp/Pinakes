The narrator stops to say what flows in the gods, and why. They eat and drink differently from men, and so they are made differently: bloodless, and therefore immortal (see [[ichor]], [[immortals]], [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). The rest of the book shows what that means. The gods can be wounded and feel pain, but they do not die: Paeëon heals Hades and Ares, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.402, 5.901; see [[paeeon]]).

Two gods in Book 5 explain the gods' wounds by the gods themselves. Dione says that “many of us who have our homes on Olympus have endured cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another” (5.383–384; see [[dione]] and [[homes-on-olympus]]). Ares says to Zeus, “We gods are forever enduring the most chilling things by one another's will, when we do favors for men” (5.873–874). A mortal wounds a god because another god sends him, and the gods' favors to men are what set them against one another. See [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]], [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]].

Over them all is Zeus. “For all the others, all the gods there are on Olympus, obey you, and each of us is subject to you,” Ares tells him (5.877–878), and Zeus answers that of all the gods who hold Olympus, Ares is the most hateful to him (5.890; see [[zeus]]). Between the gods and men stands Apollo's rule: “never alike is the race of immortal gods and of men who walk on the earth” (5.441–442; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]).

## Outside Homer

“Longinus” admired Homer's wounded and quarreling gods for their grandeur, but called them impious unless they are read as allegory (*On the Sublime* 9.6–8; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]]). Alexander, wounded by an arrow, told his friends that what flowed from him was blood, not ichor (Plutarch, *Alexander* 28; see [[blood-not-ichor]]).
