---
summary: Enyo, “sacker of cities,” is named with Athena as the goddesses who rule over the war of men, and whom Aphrodite is not.
---

Diomedes goes after Aphrodite “knowing that she was a feeble god, and not one of those goddesses who rule over the war of men, neither Athena nor Enyo, sacker of cities” (5.331–333; [[enyo]], [[pitiless-bronze]]). The title of the great destroyers, Odysseus and Achilles among men, is given to a war goddess, beside Athena. Enyo comes into the battle later with Ares, leading Tumult (5.592–593; [[queenly]], [[shameless-in-combat]]). The feeble goddess and the two warlike ones are set apart in one sentence, and the book turns on the difference: Diomedes wounds Aphrodite, and at the end of the book Athena, one of the two, rides beside him against Ares.
