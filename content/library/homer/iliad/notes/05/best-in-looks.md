Hera, in the likeness of Stentor ([[bronze-voiced]]), begins with an insult built like Hector's to Paris, εἶδος ἄριστε (3.39): εἶδος ἀγητοί, “admirable in looks,” and nothing more. ἀγητός (“admired, admirable”) is a word of praise, and the insult lies in its limit: they are fine to look at and cowards in battle ([[eidos]], [[elenchos]], [[aidos]]). Agamemnon uses the same verse to shame the army in Book 8 (Il. 8.228). Elsewhere εἶδος ἀγητός is simple praise: of Hector's body, which the Achaeans run up to admire when he is dead (Il. 22.369–371), and of the disguised Hermes, in Priam's greeting to him (Il. 24.376).
