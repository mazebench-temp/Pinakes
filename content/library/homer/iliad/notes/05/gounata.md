---
summary: The knees in Book 5 are where strength fails, “he has loosened the knees of many brave men”, and where a child belongs: Aphrodite falls on her mother's knees, and Dione says that the man who fights the gods will have no children at his knees.
grc: [γούνατʼ, γούνασι]
---

**Strength.** Aeneas describes Diomedes to Pandarus as a man who “has loosened the knees of many brave men” (πολλῶν τε καὶ ἐσθλῶν γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν, 5.176). To loosen a man's knees is to kill him; the knees hold up a fighter, and when they give way he falls (see [[loosened-his-limbs]]).

**A child's place.** Wounded, Aphrodite goes to Olympus and “fell on the knees of Dione, her mother”, and Dione takes her in her arms, strokes her, and asks who has done this to her (5.370–374). A goddess takes refuge in her mother's lap like a child. In the battle of the gods Artemis, beaten by Hera, sits weeping on her father's knees, and Zeus asks her the same question in the same two verses (21.505–510 = 5.373–374; see [[dione]]). Dione's answer ends with the other side of the picture: the man who fights with the immortals does not live long, “and his children do not call him papa at his knees when he comes home from war” (ποτὶ γούνασι παππάζουσιν, 5.408–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). In Book 1 the knees were the suppliant's, clasped by Thetis; in Book 5 they are the parent's.
