**Routed.** The book opens with the Trojans in retreat. Athena leads Ares out of the battle, “and the Danaans turned the Trojans back” (5.37), and each Achaean leader kills his man (5.37–83). Diomedes sweeps across the plain like a river in flood, and the close-packed battalions of the Trojans “did not stand against him, many though they were” (5.93–94; see [[river-in-flood]]). Their epithets are the familiar ones: “great-hearted” (5.27, 5.102), “goaders of horses” (5.102; see [[goaders-of-horses]]), “bronze-armored” (5.180; see [[bronze-armored]]) and “proud” (5.623; see [[proud]]).

**The rally.** The turn comes from a god and an ally. Apollo calls on Ares, and Ares, in the likeness of the Thracian Acamas, “went among the ranks of the Trojans and urged them on” (5.461–470; see [[acamas-of-thrace]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). Sarpedon's rebuke sends Hector through the army, “and they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans” (5.497; see [[sarpedon]] and [[hector]]). The Achaeans hold, white with the dust of the horses' hooves as the heaps of chaff grow white under the winnowers (5.499–505; see [[winnowing]]). Ares shrouds the battle in night “to help the Trojans”, on the orders of Apollo, now that Athena, “the Danaans' helper”, is gone (5.506–511).

**With the gods.** When Hector charges, “Ares led them, and lady Enyo” (5.592; see [[enyo]] and [[tumult]]). Diomedes tells the Achaeans to give ground with their faces to the Trojans, for a god is with them (5.601–606), and the Argives give way, “when they learned that Ares was among the Trojans” (5.702). Hector and Ares kill six men (5.703–710; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]).

**By the ships.** Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, measures how far the Trojans have come:

The fighting is still on the plain, but Hera speaks as if it had reached the ships (see [[hera]] and [[stentor]]). She measures Achilles' absence by how far the Trojans dare to come, and Achilles will measure it in the same way: while he fought, Hector would come out only as far as “the Scaean gates and the oak” (9.352–355; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).
