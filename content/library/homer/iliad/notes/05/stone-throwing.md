**Diomedes' stone.** Diomedes has thrown his spear and killed Pandarus, and Aeneas stands over the body with spear and shield, shouting terribly (5.297–302; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Diomedes takes up a stone:

{{quote:b5c86ba6-9bcf-503e-ab9c-b9e88ab96f20}}

The measure, “such as mortals are now”, is the narrator's, spoken to listeners who are such mortals (see [[men-of-today]]). The verse returns for the stones of Hector and of Aeneas himself (12.449, 20.287), and a variant for Ajax's (12.383; see [[stone-throwing]]). Then the wound, told with a word explained for the listener: the stone strikes Aeneas on the hip, “where the thigh turns in the hip joint, and men call it the cup” (5.305–306). κοτύλη is a cup, the little cup that a pitying man holds out to an orphan in Andromache's lament (22.494), and here the socket of the hip. The stone crushes the socket, breaks both tendons and tears away the skin, and Aeneas sinks to his knees, leaning on the earth with his hand, and black night covers his eyes (5.307–310; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). He does not die. His mother carries him off (5.311–318).

**The stone returned.** In Book 20 Aeneas faces Achilles, picks up a stone in the same three verses, and would have thrown it, had not Poseidon seen and taken him out of the fight (20.285–291). The man crushed by a stone in Book 5 lifts the same stone against a greater enemy.

**Antilochus.** Mydon, Pylaemenes' charioteer, is turning his horses when Antilochus hits him “with a stone full on the elbow”; the reins, white with ivory, fall from his hands into the dust, and Antilochus finishes him with a sword stroke to the temple (5.580–584; see [[mydon]] and [[ivory]]).
