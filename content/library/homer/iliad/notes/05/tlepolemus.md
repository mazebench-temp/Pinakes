{{quote:84a00dac-6223-5807-b1eb-181ae4c95d79}}

The introduction repeats the Catalogue's description of him, “son of Heracles, tall and brave” (2.653), and names his death before he speaks: “mighty fate” (μοῖρα κραταιή) sends him (see [[moira]]). As they come close (see [[when-they-came-close]]), the narrator sets the two men side by side by their descent from Zeus, and descent is the whole subject of Tlepolemus' speech. The line also makes them kin: Sarpedon, son of Zeus, is a half-brother of Tlepolemus' father.

**The taunt.**

{{quote:a0188dab-f3f1-5ffb-a0af-25d737543d6b}}

He gives Sarpedon a title, “counselor of the Lycians” (see [[man-of-counsel]]), and then the verb πτώσσειν, “cower”, which Sarpedon had just used of Hector's brothers (καταπτώσσουσι, 5.476). The measure of a son of Zeus is the heroes of former times, and one of them was his own father, who came for the horses of Laomedon with six ships and sacked Ilios (5.638–642; see [[heracles]] and [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). Then the present: “yours is a coward's heart, and your people are dwindling away” (5.643), and a threat, that Sarpedon will “pass the gates of Hades” (5.646). Sarpedon grants the sack but gives it another cause, the bad faith of Laomedon, and turns the threat back on the speaker (5.648–654).

Comparisons of sons with their fathers run through Books 4 and 5. Agamemnon shamed Diomedes with the deeds of Tydeus (4.370–400), and Athena will tell Diomedes that “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Tlepolemus claims his father's greatness and is killed; Sarpedon's father saves his son, for now.

**The death.**

{{quote:da27dfae-f97f-5f76-99ee-67debeb250f0}}

The spears fly “at the same moment” (ἁμαρτῇ), and each finds its man; Tlepolemus' spear goes through Sarpedon's thigh (5.660–662). “Murky night covered his eyes” (5.659; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The Achaeans carry his body out of the fighting, as the Lycians carry out Sarpedon (5.663–669), and Odysseus, seeing it, turns his anger on the Lycians (5.669–678; see [[odysseus]]).

The leader of the Rhodians, whose flight overseas the Catalogue told (2.653–670; see [[rhodians]] and [[the-flight-of-tlepolemus]]), dies in his only fight in the poem and is not named again. Another Tlepolemus, son of Damastor, is among the men Patroclus kills in Book 16 (16.416).
