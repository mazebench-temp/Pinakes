summary: Book 5 begins with its purpose: Athena gives Diomedes might and daring so that he may “win noble fame”. He means to win it by taking the horses of Aeneas, Aeneas taunts Pandarus with the fame of his bow, and Agamemnon tells the army that flight wins no fame at all.
passages:
  - 5.1-3 | Athena gives Diomedes might and daring
  - 5.528-532 | Agamemnon's exhortation
---

**The purpose.** The book's first sentence gives the reason for everything that follows. Athena gives Diomedes might and daring, “so that he would stand out among all the Argives and win noble fame” (κλέος ἐσθλὸν ἄροιτο, 5.2–3). The aristeia is a machine for making κλέος: a hero made conspicuous (ἔκδηλος) by a god, so that men will speak of him (see [[the-aristeia]]).

**Fame in things.** Diomedes uses the same phrase of the horses of Aeneas, the breed that Zeus gave to Tros: “If we could take these two, we would win noble fame” (5.273). Fame can be carried off, as spoils are. Sthenelus drives the horses to the ships (5.319–327), and in the chariot race of Book 23 Diomedes wins with them (23.290–292, 23.499–513).

**Fame lost.** Aeneas finds Pandarus and asks him, “where are your bow and your feathered arrows and your fame?”, the fame in which no man in Lycia claims to be better than him (5.171–173). The bow has failed him twice, and within a hundred lines he is dead (5.290–296; see [[pandarus]]). Agamemnon states the rule for everyone: “when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage” (5.532; see [[shame-saves-more]]).
