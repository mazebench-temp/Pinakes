summary: Horses are the prize of Book 5. Diomedes drives off the teams of the men he kills, Pandarus regrets the horses he left at home, and Sthenelus takes the horses of Aeneas, of the breed Zeus gave to Tros for Ganymede and Anchises stole from Laomedon. The gods' horses fly between earth and sky and graze on ambrosia.

**Spoils.** Horses are the richest spoils of the field. Diomedes drives off the teams of the sons of Dares and of the sons of Priam and gives them to his companions to take to the ships (5.25–26, 5.165), and Antilochus whips off the team of Mydon, whom he has killed (5.589; see [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**Horses left at home.** Pandarus came to Troy with a bow and no chariot. At home, beside each of his father's eleven chariots, a pair of horses stands “munching white barley and spelt”; his father told him to lead the Trojans from the chariot, “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—sparing the horses, for fear they would go short of fodder with men penned in, when they were used to eating their fill” (5.195–203). He spared the horses and lost his life on foot (see [[pandarus]] and [[lycaon]]).

The breed was Zeus' gift to Tros for his son Ganymede, “the best of all horses under the dawn and the sun”; Anchises stole a strain from it by putting his mares to Laomedon's stallions, reared four of the six foals and gave two to Aeneas, μήστωρε φόβοιο, “masters of rout” (see [[tros]], [[ganymede]], [[anchises]], [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[anchises-steals-the-horses]]). Horses have a lineage here, as heroes do. The breed was in Laomedon's keeping, and Laomedon's horses were the prize for which Heracles came to Troy and which Laomedon refused him (5.640–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). Apollodorus makes them the same horses, the mares Zeus gave in recompense for Ganymede (*Library* 2.5.9).

**Taken.** Sthenelus drives off “the fine-maned horses of Aeneas” while Aphrodite carries Aeneas away (5.323–327). They serve Diomedes afterward. In Book 8 he invites Nestor into his chariot in the words Aeneas spoke to Pandarus, “so that you may see what the horses of Tros are like” (8.105–108 ~ 5.221–223), and adds that he took them from Aeneas; he drives them in the chariot race for Patroclus (23.290–292, 23.377–378).

**Knowing the driver.** Pandarus fears that Aeneas' horses, “missing your voice”, might balk under another driver (5.231–234). When Pandarus falls, his horses shy aside (5.295–296).

**The gods' horses.** Ares' horses rest in a mist beside his spear, on the left of the battle (5.355–356). Aphrodite begs them from her brother, “the horses with golden headbands”, and Iris drives them to Olympus and feeds them ambrosial fodder (5.358–369). Hera's horses, also with golden headbands, are harnessed by Hera herself, “eager for strife and the war cry” (5.720, 5.732). They pass through the gates of heaven, and each bound of these “high-neighing horses of the gods” covers as much ground as a man on a lookout can see over the wine-dark sea (5.770–772; see [[the-lookout]] and [[longinus-horses-of-the-gods]]). At the rivers of Troy she unyokes them, hides them in mist, and the Simoeis makes ambrosia spring up for them to graze (5.775–777; see [[ambrosia]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

**Epithets.** The horses are “single-hoofed”, “fine-maned” and “strong-hoofed” (5.236, 5.323, 5.329), and the gods' horses have golden headbands, are driven by the goad and neigh on high (see [[single-hoofed]], [[fine-maned]], [[strong-hoofed]], [[with-golden-headbands]], [[goad-driven]] and [[high-neighing]]). Men and places are named from horses too: the Trojans “goaders of horses”, Diomedes “tamer of horses”, Ilios “of the fine horses” (εὔπωλος, 5.551), and Hades “of the famous horses” (κλυτόπωλος, 5.654; see [[goaders-of-horses]], [[tamer-of-horses]], [[of-the-fine-horses]] and [[of-the-famous-horses]]).
