**His father's son.** Book 4 brought him into the poem with a name and a killing, but no father (see the Book 4 note). Here he is Ἀντίλοχος μεγαθύμου Νέστορος υἱός, “Antilochus, the son of great-hearted Nestor” (5.565; see [[nestor]] and [[great-hearted]]), the verse by which he will be named again (13.400, 17.653, 23.541).

**Beside Menelaus.** Ares is urging Menelaus on against Aeneas, meaning him to die (5.563–564). Antilochus runs through the champions, “for he feared greatly for the shepherd of the people, that he might come to harm, and make all their toil in vain” (5.566–567): if Menelaus fell, the war would lose its cause. He does not attack; he stands “close beside” Menelaus (5.570), and that is enough. Aeneas, swift warrior though he is, will not face two men standing together (5.571–572). The two drag the dead sons of Diocles back to the army and put them in their companions' arms (5.573–574; see [[crethon]]). The bond between the young man and Menelaus runs through the poem. In Book 15 Menelaus spurs him on as the youngest and swiftest of the Achaeans (15.568–571); in Book 17 Menelaus sends him to tell Achilles that Patroclus is dead (17.679–701); in the chariot race of Book 23 Antilochus forces past Menelaus and then gives way to him (23.402–447, 23.566–611).

**Mydon.** Menelaus spears Pylaemenes, and Antilochus takes the charioteer, Mydon, son of Atymnius, as he is turning the horses (5.576–581). A stone to the elbow makes him drop the reins, “white with ivory”, and a sword stroke to the temple throws him headfirst into deep sand, where he stays standing until the horses kick him flat (5.582–588; see [[mydon]]). Antilochus whips the horses off to the Achaean army (5.589). In Book 4 the man who tried to strip Antilochus' victim died in the attempt (4.463–469); this time he takes his prize, as he will again when he drives off the horses of Asius' charioteer (13.400–401).
