**The prayer.** Diomedes' first appeal to Athena in Book 5 rests on his father. Wounded by Pandarus, he prays: “if ever in friendship you stood by my father and me in destructive war, now be my friend in turn” (5.116–117). She answers by giving him his father's might, “such as the horseman Tydeus, wielder of the shield, used to have” (5.125–126; see [[wielder-of-the-shield]] and [[tydeus]]). In Book 10 he will pray to her again in the name of Tydeus' embassy to Thebes (10.284–294).

**The retelling.** At the end of the book Athena finds Diomedes resting beside his chariot, wiping the blood from Pandarus' wound, and gives him Agamemnon's reproach in her own voice.

{{quote:cf050df9-3e88-5a9d-92e1-9c511c8e4374}}

The story is the one Agamemnon told (4.384–390), with three changes. The goddess speaks in the first person: Agamemnon's “such a helper was Athena to him” (4.390) becomes “such a helper was I to him” (5.808; see [[epitarrhothos]]). She adds that she had tried to hold Tydeus back, telling him to feast at his ease, and that he fought anyway. And she leaves out the ambush of the fifty on his way home, which Agamemnon told at length (4.391–398). What she keeps is the contests and the small man who won them: “Tydeus, you know, was small in build, but he was a fighter” (5.801; see [[small-but-a-fighter]]). She ends with the charge of Book 4 put more sharply: if weariness or fear holds Diomedes back, he is no son of Tydeus (5.811–813).

**The answer.** In Book 4 Diomedes heard Agamemnon in silence (4.401–402). Here he answers, and his answer turns the paradigm round. Tydeus fought when the goddess told him not to; Diomedes is holding back because she told him to. “You would not let me fight face to face with the blessed gods”, he says, except Aphrodite, and he has recognized Ares (5.815–824). Athena accepts the answer, calls him “dear to my heart” (see [[dear-to-my-heart]]), and promises him what she gave his father: “such a helper am I to you” (5.826–828). Then she takes the reins herself and drives him at Ares (see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).
