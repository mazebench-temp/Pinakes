summary: Book 5 gives the first ambrosia of the Iliad, and it is fodder: the Simoeis makes it spring up for Hera's horses to graze (5.777), and Ares' horses are given “ambrosial fodder” on Olympus (5.369). Aphrodite's robe is “ambrosial” (5.338), and the book explains that the gods are bloodless and immortal because they eat no bread and drink no wine (5.341–342).

**The ambrosial robe.** Diomedes' spear pierces Aphrodite's hand “through the ambrosial robe that the Graces themselves had made for her” (5.338; see [[robe]] and [[graces]]). ἀμβρόσιος, “immortal, belonging to the gods”, marks the robe as a god's, as it marked the locks of Zeus when he nodded in Book 1 (1.529).

**What the gods eat.** Then the narrator explains the blood that flows from the wound:

{{quote:22e0313e-ee00-5cfb-8120-bb9d50179d2e}}

The gods are what they eat. They have ichor instead of blood because they do not eat bread or drink wine (see [[ichor]]). The poem names only what they do not eat; their own food is ambrosia and their drink nectar, as the Odyssey shows when Calypso's servants set ambrosia and nectar before her while Odysseus is given the food that mortals eat (Odyssey 5.196–199; see [[nectar]]). Men are the other side of the line. When Diomedes meets Glaucus in Book 6 he asks whether he is a god or one of the mortals “who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142). That the gods drink no wine raised a question for later readers, since Ganymede pours wine for Zeus (see [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]]).

**Ambrosial fodder.** The gods' horses eat the gods' food. On Olympus Iris unyokes the horses of Ares and sets ambrosial fodder (ἀμβρόσιον εἶδαρ) before them (5.369), in the verse that feeds Poseidon's horses in Book 13 (13.35). When Hera halts her horses where the Simoeis and the Scamander meet, “the Simoeis made ambrosia spring up to graze on” (5.777). The noun ἀμβροσίη appears here for the first time in the Iliad, as a plant that a river of Troy makes grow for the gods' horses (see [[simoeis]] and [[chariot-journey]]). Later in the poem ambrosia keeps bodies from harm: Apollo anoints the dead Sarpedon with it (16.670, 16.680), and Thetis drips it with nectar into the nostrils of the dead Patroclus (19.38–39).
