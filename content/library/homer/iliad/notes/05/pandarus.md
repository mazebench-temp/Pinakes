**The arrow.** The first check to Diomedes comes from him. As Diomedes sweeps across the plain like a river in flood, “the splendid son of Lycaon” shoots him through the plate of his corselet in the right shoulder, and shouts to the Trojans that “the best of the Achaeans is hit”, “if truly the lord, the son of Zeus, roused me when I set out from Lycia” (5.95–105). The son of Zeus is Apollo, the giver of his bow (2.827; see [[apollo]]). “So he spoke, boasting” (εὐχόμενος; see [[euchomai]]), but the shaft had not overcome him (5.106). Sthenelus draws it out, and Diomedes prays to Athena for the death of the man “who struck me first, and boasts over me” (5.119). She answers at once (5.121–132).

**Regrets.** Aeneas finds him in the verses with which Athena found him in Book 4 (5.168–169 ~ 4.88–89) and asks for another arrow (5.171–178; see [[aeneas]]). Pandarus answers with the longest speech he makes (5.180–216). He knows the man by his shield, his helmet and his horses, but not whether he is a god: “someone of the immortals stands near him” (5.185–186). Then he tells what he left at home, the chariots and horses in his father's halls, and the advice he ignored (5.192–203; see [[lycaon]]): “but I did not listen—it would have been far better” (5.201). The verse will be Hector's, of Polydamas' advice, before he meets Achilles (22.103), and Odysseus', of the Cyclops' cave (*Odyssey* 9.228). He has shot two chieftains, “the son of Tydeus and the son of Atreus”, and only roused them (5.206–208). So he curses the bow:

{{quote:b9f71a4e-b189-549b-9890-98239fb8be55}}

The bow was Apollo's gift, the one distinction the Catalogue gave him (2.827; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]] and [[bow]]). Now it is “empty wind” (ἀνεμώλια; see [[anemolios]]). He says he took it up to do Hector a favor (χάριν, 5.211); in Book 4 Athena had promised him thanks (χάριν) from all the Trojans (4.95). The oath hangs on his homecoming (see [[nostos]]), and its self-curse is one that Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, will swear (5.214 = *Odyssey* 16.102). The man who broke the truce with an arrow now swears an oath against his own bow. He never comes home, to burn the bow or to pay Apollo the hecatomb he vowed (4.119–121).

**The spear.** He mounts Aeneas' chariot and takes the spear, leaving Aeneas the reins (5.230–240). Sthenelus names him as he comes, “Pandarus, and he claims to be the son of Lycaon” (5.246). His throw pierces Diomedes' shield and reaches the corselet, and he boasts a second time too soon: “you have given me a great triumph” (5.285). “You missed; you did not hit me,” Diomedes answers (5.287), and throws:

{{quote:ed66106b-5424-5802-a541-597394cf008d}}

βέλος δʼ ἴθυνεν Ἀθήνη: the goddess who guided his arrow to Menelaus' belt (4.132) guides the spear into his face, and it cuts off the tongue that boasted. The archer who came to Troy on foot, for want of horses, falls from another man's chariot. Aeneas stands over his body (5.297–302; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]), and the poem does not mention it again.

His arrow outlives him. Late in the book Athena finds Diomedes by his chariot “cooling the wound where Pandarus had hit him with his arrow”, the sweat under his shield strap wearing him out (5.795–798). See [[diomedes]].
