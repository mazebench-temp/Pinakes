summary: Thirty-nine men die by name in Book 5, twenty-seven Trojans and allies and twelve Achaeans, and the book uses every form of the killing scene. Six Achaean leaders take a man each, each victim with a history; Diomedes kills brothers in pairs; there are rapid lists of names, chains of pity and revenge, the first boasts of the poem, and a god who kills and strips a man.

**Each leader takes his man.** Athena leads Ares out of the battle, “and the Danaans turned the Trojans back; and each of the leaders took his man” (5.37–38). Six single kills follow, each a small biography: Odius, leader of the Halizones; Phaestus from deep-soiled Tarne; Scamandrius, a hunter taught by Artemis; Phereclus, who built Alexander's ships; Pedaeus, a bastard son of Antenor whom Theano raised as her own; Hypsenor, son of the priest of the Scamander (5.38–83; see [[odius]], [[phaestus-son-of-borus]], [[scamandrius]], [[phereclus]], [[pedaeus]] and [[hypsenor]]). Four of the six are struck from behind as they turn or run (5.40, 5.56, 5.65–66, 5.80). Each life is a skill or a bond that does not save the man: the hunter's art, the builder's craft and Athena's love, the stepmother's care, the father's priesthood (see [[the-war-in-small]] and [[no-help-to-you]]). The blows are exact, from the spear through the back and out at the chest, in the same verse for two men (5.41 = 5.57), to the spear under the tongue, so that Pedaeus “bit the cold bronze with his teeth” (5.75). Each death has its formula (see [[fell-with-a-thud]], [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[purple-death]]).

**Pairs.** Diomedes kills eight men in four pairs (5.144–165). Three pairs are brothers, and the narrator turns from the killer to the fathers: Eurydamas, the old dream-reader, and Phaenops, who has no other son, so that distant kinsmen divide his wealth (5.148–158; see [[bereaved-parents]] and [[phaenops]]). The last pair are sons of Priam, pulled from one chariot like a heifer and a cow by a lion (5.159–165; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). The book began with brothers, the sons of Dares (5.9–26), and it goes on in pairs: Aeneas kills the twin sons of Diocles, whose line runs back to the river Alpheus, and Hector two men in one chariot (5.541–560, 5.608–609; see [[crethon]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]]).

**Lists.** Two quick lists give only names. Odysseus kills seven Lycians in two verses (5.677–678). Hector and Ares kill six, introduced by the narrator's question “Then whom first, whom last did they slay?”, and only the last, Oresbius, is given a home and a life (5.703–710; see [[whom-first-whom-last]] and [[oresbius]]).

**Pity and revenge.** Menelaus pities the fallen twins and goes through the champions, and the fight brings the deaths of Pylaemenes and his charioteer Mydon (5.561–589); Ajax pities Menesthes and Anchialus and kills Amphius (5.610–615). The verse is the same for both, τὼ δὲ πεσόντʼ ἐλέησε, “pitied them as they fell”. Amphius' body brings the fight over the body (5.617–626; see [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**The boast.** Book 4 had no boast over the fallen, and the first boasts of the poem go wrong. Pandarus twice shouts over Diomedes that he will not last, and Diomedes lives to kill him (5.101–105, 5.283–289). The third shout is Diomedes' own, over a goddess he has wounded (5.347–351). The formula is the same for all three: τῷ δʼ ἐπὶ μακρὸν ἄϋσε, “and over him … shouted aloud” (see [[euchomai]]).

**Gods in the list.** The narrator names Ares with Hector as a killer, “Hector, the son of Priam, and bronze Ares” (5.704), and at the end of the book the god is stripping Periphas, whom he has killed himself (5.842–848; see [[periphas]]). The killing scene, the most mortal thing in the poem, has a god for its subject, and in the same scene the god is wounded (see [[the-aristeia]]).
