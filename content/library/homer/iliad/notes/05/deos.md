summary: Athena asks Diomedes whether “heartless fear” holds him, and he answers in her words that it does not. Elsewhere in Book 5 fear comes from gods and from Hector, and δειλός is used in its other sense, “wretched”, of two dead brothers.

**Fear denied.** Athena finds Diomedes resting by his chariot and offers him two explanations, weariness or fear: “or heartless fear has hold of you” (ἤ νύ σέ που δέος ἴσχει ἀκήριον, 5.812). He takes up her words to deny them: “No heartless fear has hold of me, nor any shrinking” (οὔτέ τί με δέος ἴσχει ἀκήριον, 5.817). What holds him back is obedience to her own command (5.818–824). She answers, “do not fear Ares for that” (μήτε σύ γʼ Ἄρηα τό γε δείδιθι, 5.827), and drives the chariot at the god herself (see [[small-but-a-fighter]]).

**Fear from a god.** When Diomedes' spear goes into Ares' flank, the god bellows as loud as nine or ten thousand men, “and trembling seized them, Achaeans and Trojans alike, in their fear” (5.862–863; see [[ten-thousand-men]]). Hector, with Ares beside him, goes through the champions “bringing terror to the Danaans” (δεῖμα φέρων Δαναοῖσι, 5.682). Ajax, who has killed Amphius and tries to strip him, is driven off because “he feared the strong ring of the proud Trojans around the body” (5.623). The fears of mortals in this book are reasonable.

**The other δειλός.** δειλός can mean “cowardly”, but also “wretched, poor”, a word of pity. Menelaus and Antilochus drag the bodies of the twin sons of Diocles back to the Achaean army, “and they laid the two poor men (δειλώ) in the arms of their companions” (5.573–574; see [[bereaved-parents]]).
