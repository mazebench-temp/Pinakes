summary: Book 5 is full of rescues. Hephaestus hides Idaeus in night; Aphrodite wraps Aeneas in her robe and is wounded for it; Apollo takes him up in a dark cloud, sets him down in his temple and leaves a phantom in his place; Zeus keeps death from Sarpedon; and Athena turns Ares' spear away from Diomedes.

**Hephaestus.** The first rescue of the book is the smallest. Idaeus flees from Diomedes, and “he himself would not have escaped black doom either, but Hephaestus protected him, and saved him, covering him in night” (5.22–23). The god saves the son of his own priest, “so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief” (5.24), and he hides him in darkness as Aphrodite hid Paris in mist (3.381; see [[dares]] and [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Aphrodite.** The rescue of Paris is repeated, and this time it fails. “And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have perished there, had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (5.311–312), in the verse that saved Paris (3.374). She throws her arms around her son, spreads a fold of her robe in front of him as a shelter from missiles, and carries him out of the fighting (5.314–318; see [[robe]]). There is no mist this time, and the enemy has been given eyes to see gods: Diomedes pursues her, wounds her hand, and she drops her son (5.330–343; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**Apollo.** Apollo finishes what Aphrodite began. He “took him up in his arms and protected him in a dark cloud, for fear one of the Danaans of the swift horses might strike the bronze into his chest and take away his life” (5.344–346). The last two verses are the verses of Aphrodite's rescue (5.316–317): the same fear, in a stronger god. Diomedes attacks three times the man he knows Apollo is shielding, and at the fourth the god warns him off (5.432–444; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Then Apollo sets Aeneas down “in sacred Pergamus”, where Leto and Artemis heal him (5.445–448; see [[temple]]), and leaves in his place a phantom “like Aeneas himself, and in armor such as his”, over which the armies go on fighting (5.449–453; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]] and [[eidolon]]). The pattern has its snatching away and its setting down in safety, and a new step: a likeness left behind.

**Not a rescue.** At 5.680 the rescue verse is used of a man. Odysseus would have killed more Lycians, “had not great Hector of the gleaming helmet been quick to see it” (5.679–680); Hector drives the Achaeans back, and no one is snatched away. The line is not counted here.

**Keeping ruin away.** Zeus does not snatch his son out of the battle, but when Tlepolemus' spear goes through Sarpedon's thigh, “his father still kept ruin away” (5.662). In Book 16 he will think of saving him again and let him die (16.431–461; see [[sarpedon]]). Diomedes sees the same protection around Hector: “always at his side is one of the gods, who keeps ruin away” (5.603).

**Athena.** The last rescue is Athena's. Ares lunges at Diomedes with his spear, and the goddess “caught it in her hand and pushed it up and away from the chariot, to fly in vain” (5.853–854), as she turned Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus in Book 4 (4.127–133). See [[athena]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]].
