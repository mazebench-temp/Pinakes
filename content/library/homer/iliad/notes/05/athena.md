summary: Athena makes Book 5 the day of Diomedes. She fills him with might, takes the mist from his eyes and lets him wound Aphrodite, and she mocks the wounded goddess before Zeus. At the end she arms with the aegis, rides beside Diomedes in the helmet of Hades, and drives his spear into Ares.

**Might and sight.** The book opens with her gift:

{{quote:55bc383e-6419-5b33-8227-16c2972361f7}}

She kindles fire from his helmet and shield like the star of late summer (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]] and [[the-aristeia]]). When Pandarus' arrow strikes him, Diomedes prays to her by her old friendship with his father (5.115–120), and she answers with light limbs, fresh might, and a new sight:

{{quote:85a7e4df-a946-5a51-9baa-282144c09f8b}}

With the sight comes a rule: he is to fight no god face to face except Aphrodite (5.129–132). He keeps it. He wounds Aphrodite, gives way at Apollo's warning, and when he recognizes Ares beside Hector he draws back and tells the Achaeans to do the same (5.330–351, 5.440–444, 5.596–606). See [[achlys]], [[seeing-the-gods]], [[mist-from-the-eyes]] and [[diomedes]].

**The other god of war.** At the start she takes Ares by the hand and persuades him that both of them should leave the mortals to fight, “and avoid the wrath of Zeus” (5.29–35). He sits down by the Scamander; she does not stay away. When Apollo sees “that Pallas Athena was gone”, he sends Ares back into the fighting (5.510–511). Zeus himself pairs them: war is “the business of swift Ares and Athena” (5.430). The book ends with the two face to face.

**Mockery.** On Olympus she and Hera provoke Zeus “with cutting words” (5.419; see [[kertomia]]). Cypris, Athena suggests, has been coaxing some Achaean woman to follow the Trojans, and has scratched her hand on the woman's golden brooch (5.421–425). The joke has Helen in it. Zeus smiles and sends Aphrodite back to the works of marriage (5.426–430; see [[zeus]]).

**The arming.**

{{quote:c90d7453-60d3-5c6d-b532-400fda69025b}}

The goddess of weaving lets fall a robe of her own making and puts on her father's tunic (see [[robe]] and [[tunic]]). Then come the aegis, with Rout, Strife, Courage, Onslaught and the Gorgon's head, a golden helmet “fitted with the foot soldiers of a hundred cities”, and the spear “with which she overpowers the ranks of men, of heroes, against whom she, the daughter of the mighty father, bears resentment” (5.738–747; see [[aegis]], [[gorgon]], [[helmet]], [[helmet-of-a-hundred-cities]] and [[kotos]]). When the two goddesses arm again in Book 8, the robe, the tunic, the chariot and the spear return in the same words (8.384–391), but not the aegis and the helmet (see [[arming-scene]]).

**Beside Diomedes.** She finds him cooling his wound and taunts him with his father, who was small but a fighter (5.793–813; see [[tydeus]] and [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). He answers that he is keeping her command (5.815–824), and she releases him against Ares: “such a helper am I to you” (5.828; see [[epitarrhothos]]). She pushes Sthenelus from the chariot and takes his place:

{{quote:65c6d2fe-f1be-5395-b221-4bf9113f70a7}}

She takes the whip and the reins and puts on the helmet of Hades, so that Ares will not see her (5.840–845; see [[helmet-of-hades]]). She turns his spear aside and drives Diomedes' spear into his flank (5.853–859). The goddess who guided Pandarus' arrow to Menelaus' belt (4.132) and Diomedes' spear into Pandarus' face (5.290) now drives a mortal's spear into a god. See [[diomedes-wounds-ares]].

**Ares' complaint.** On Olympus Ares blames Zeus for “that senseless daughter … whose mind is always on lawless deeds”, who has let Diomedes loose against the gods (5.875–882). He has not forgotten in Book 21, where she fells him with a stone (21.391–414). She returns to the house of Zeus with Hera, “Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae” (5.908; see [[of-alalcomenae]]).
