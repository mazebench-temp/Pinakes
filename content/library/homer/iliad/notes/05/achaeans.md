summary: The Achaeans drive the Trojans back while Diomedes rages, stand firm like motionless clouds when the Trojans rally, and give ground, still facing the enemy, once Ares fights for Hector, until Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, shames them. Athena's jest about "the Achaean women" is the only mention of Achaean women in the book.

**The course of the battle.** With Ares led away, "the Danaans turned the Trojans back" and each leader killed his man (5.37–38). When Hector rallies the Trojans, the Achaeans "stood their ground like clouds" that Zeus sets motionless on the mountain tops in windless weather (5.522–527; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]). Once they learn that Ares is among the Trojans they neither flee to the ships nor charge, "but kept giving ground backward" (5.699–702), as Diomedes had told them to do, keeping their faces toward the enemy (5.605–606). Hera, in Stentor's likeness, calls them "wretched disgraces, admirable in looks", and reminds them that while Achilles fought the Trojans never came out of their gates (5.787–791; see [[stentor]]).

**The Achaean women.** Athena mocks the wounded Aphrodite before Zeus: surely Cypris has been urging "one of the Achaean women" to follow the Trojans she loves, and scratched her hand on a golden brooch while she caressed "one of these well-robed Achaean women" (5.422–425; see [[aphrodite]]). The jest turns the wound Diomedes gave her into an accident of seduction, the work of the goddess who once led Helen from Sparta. Ἀχαιϊάδες (5.422, 5.424) is the feminine of the people's name; compare Thersites' insult Ἀχαιΐδες, "Achaean women", at 2.235.
