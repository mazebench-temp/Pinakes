---
summary: Book 5 asks what a god is and how to tell one. Athena lets Diomedes tell god from man; the Trojans suspect a god behind him; the Achaeans suspect one behind Hector; and the gods themselves, wounded, complain that they suffer for men.
grc: [θεοῦ, θεοὺς]
passages:
  - 5.121-132 | Athena takes the mist from Diomedes' eyes
  - 5.596-606 | Diomedes recognizes Ares
---

**God or man.** Athena takes the mist from Diomedes' eyes “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (ὄφρʼ εὖ γιγνώσκῃς ἠμὲν θεὸν ἠδὲ καὶ ἄνδρα, 5.128), and the book keeps asking the question (see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Pandarus, seeing him rage, does not know whether he is a god; if he is a man, “he does not rage like this without a god” (οὐχ ὅ γʼ ἄνευθε θεοῦ τάδε μαίνεται, 5.183–185). Later Diomedes says the same of Hector: “always at his side is one of the gods, who keeps ruin away” (5.603). Each side explains the other's best man by a god, and each is right (see [[divine-intervention]]).

**A god who can be fought.** θεός is used of goddesses as well as gods, and the book plays on it. Diomedes goes after Aphrodite “knowing that she was a feeble god (θεός), and not one of those goddesses (θεάων) who rule over the war of men” (5.331–332). Dione says of Heracles that he “brought sorrow on the gods (θεούς) who hold Olympus” (5.404). Ares, wounded, complains that “we gods are forever enduring the most chilling things by one another's will, when we do favors for men” (5.873–874). Apollo states the rule that holds even so: the race of immortal gods and the race of men are never alike (5.440–442; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]).

**The wrath of a god.** Aeneas' maxim puts the word at its heaviest: “the wrath of a god is hard to bear” (χαλεπὴ δὲ θεοῦ ἔπι μῆνις, 5.178; see [[menis]]).
