**The death.** The Catalogue's “Pylaemenes of the shaggy heart” (2.851) dies in four verses. He is ἀτάλαντον Ἄρηϊ, “the equal of Ares”, the title the Catalogue gave Meges (2.627; see [[equal-of-ares]]), and “leader of the great-hearted shield-bearing Paphlagonians” (see [[paphlagonians]]). The dual ἑλέτην, “the two of them killed”, gives the kill to Menelaus and Antilochus together; Menelaus does the spearing, “as he stood” (ἑσταότα), at the collarbone (5.578–579), while Antilochus kills his charioteer Mydon (5.580–589; see [[menelaus]], [[antilochus]] and [[mydon]]).

**Alive again.** In Book 13 Harpalion, “son of king Pylaemenes”, who had followed his dear father to the war, attacks Menelaus, fails to pierce his shield, and is shot by Meriones as he withdraws. The Paphlagonians lay him in a chariot and take him to Troy, grieving, “and his father went with them, letting tears fall, / and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.643–659; see [[poine]]). The father is named at the start of the passage (13.643), so there is no doubt that the poem means the same Pylaemenes.

The contradiction was noticed in antiquity. It has been explained as two men of the same name, as a slip of the kind a long poem made for performance allows, and as a sign of different poets (see [[pylaemenes-dead-and-alive]]). The poem gives no sign of a second Paphlagonian king. It does have other doublets among its minor warriors: Hector kills two Phocian leaders named Schedius, sons of different fathers (15.515–516, 17.306–307). Whatever its origin, the pairing of the two books is close. Meriones kills the son with the wound he gave Phereclus in this book (13.650–652 ~ 5.66–67; see [[phereclus]] and [[meriones]]), and Harpalion dies after attacking the man who killed his father here.
