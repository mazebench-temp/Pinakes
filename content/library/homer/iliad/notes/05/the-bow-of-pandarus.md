---
summary: In Book 5 the bow fails Pandarus: he regrets that he left his father's horses at home and came to Troy trusting in it, and swears to break it and throw it in the fire if he ever gets home (5.192–216). He fights Diomedes with a spear instead and is killed.
passages:
  - 5.192-216 | Pandarus regrets his bow
---

**Where is your bow?** Aeneas finds Pandarus in the throng and calls on the archer's skill: “Pandarus, where are your bow and your feathered arrows and your fame? In this no man here can rival you, and no one in Lycia claims to be better than you” (5.171–173). Let him pray to Zeus and shoot at the man who is killing so many Trojans (5.174–178).

**The horses left at home.** Pandarus answers that he has already shot him, through the shoulder, and it did him no harm (5.180–191). He should have come by chariot. His father Lycaon has eleven new chariots in his halls, each with its pair of horses eating white barley and spelt, and told him to lead the Trojans from a chariot; but Pandarus, sparing the horses, afraid that they would go hungry in a besieged city, left them and came to Ilios on foot, “trusting in my bow” (5.192–205; see [[lycaon]] and [[horses]]).

{{quote:b9f71a4e-b189-549b-9890-98239fb8be55}}

The bow has done what the archer asked of it. It wounded Menelaus and broke the truce (4.105–147; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]), and it hit Diomedes. But it drew blood and killed no one, and now Pandarus calls it ἀνεμώλια, “empty wind” (5.216; see [[anemolios]]). The vow to burn it, if he ever sees his home again, is the last we hear of the bow that Apollo gave him (2.827). He never goes home, and he never pays Apollo the hecatomb he vowed in Book 4 (4.119–121).

**The spear.** Aeneas takes him into his chariot, and Pandarus chooses the spear: “I will meet him with my sharp spear as he comes on” (5.238). His spear goes through Diomedes' shield and stops at his corselet, and he boasts too soon, as he boasted of his arrow (5.280–285, 5.102–105). Diomedes' spear, guided by Athena, goes through his face and kills him (5.290–296; see [[pandarus]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]).
