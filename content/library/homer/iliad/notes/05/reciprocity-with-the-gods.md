**If ever.** Wounded by Pandarus' arrow, Diomedes prays in the form of Book 1, with its “if ever” (εἴ ποτε):

{{quote:f99e2fe8-fa76-5acb-981e-28d00b5daea7}}

His claim is not a gift of his own, a temple roofed or thigh-bones burned, as Chryses claimed (1.39–41). It is the goddess's own past favor to his family: she stood by his father, so let her stand by him. A god's help is a bond that can be called on again, as Chryses' second prayer called on the god who had heard him once before (1.453–456; see [[philos]]). The answer is immediate: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him”, and she makes his limbs light (5.121–122; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[epitarrhothos]]).

**Wrath over sacrifices.** Aeneas, not knowing who is sweeping the field, wonders whether it is “some god who bears resentment against the Trojans, in wrath over sacrifices” (5.177–178; see [[menis]]). A sacrifice withheld is the first cause a Homeric hero thinks of when a god is angry. Pandarus, in Book 4, vowed Apollo a hecatomb of lambs when he came home to Zeleia (4.119–121); he dies in Book 5 without going home.

**Priests.** Hephaestus saves Idaeus, the son of his priest Dares, “so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief” (5.9–24; see [[dares]] and [[priesthood]]). Dolopion, the priest of the Scamander, “was honored by the people like a god”, but the river does not save his son Hypsenor (5.76–83). Service to a god does not buy a son's life.
