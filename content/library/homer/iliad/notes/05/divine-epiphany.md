**Eyes that see gods.** In Book 1 Athena appeared to Achilles alone, and none of the others saw her (1.198). In Book 5 she gives a man the power to see gods. Answering his prayer, she stands near him and speaks, in the verse with which she spoke to Pandarus in Book 4 (5.123 = 4.92):

{{quote:85a7e4df-a946-5a51-9baa-282144c09f8b}}

The gift has a use in battle: he may wound Aphrodite, but he must not fight any other god, and so he must know them when he sees them (5.129–132; see [[seeing-the-gods]], [[achlys]] and [[mist-from-the-eyes]]).

**Recognitions.** γιγνώσκω, “know, recognize”, is the verb of what follows. Diomedes goes after Cypris “knowing that she was a feeble god” (5.331), and attacks Aeneas, though “he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him” (5.433). He sees Ares beside Hector “in the likeness of a mortal man”, shudders, and tells the Achaeans to give ground (5.596–606). When Athena comes to him at the end of the book, he knows her at once: “I know you, goddess, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.815), and he explains his retreat, “for I recognize Ares ruling over the battle” (5.824). There is no astonishment (θάμβος) such as seized Achilles and Helen (1.199, 3.398); the goddess is his companion, “such a helper am I to you” (5.828; see [[epitarrhothos]]).

**Not knowing.** Pandarus has no such eyes. He likens the man who is routing the Trojans to Diomedes, “but I do not know for sure whether he is a god”, and if it is Diomedes, some immortal stands beside him, “his shoulders wrapped in cloud” (5.181–187). Aeneas had asked the same: the man might be “some god who bears resentment against the Trojans” (5.177). They are wrong about the man and right about the god (see [[pandarus]]).

**Gods in disguise.** The gods go among men in borrowed shapes. Ares rouses the sons of Priam “in the likeness of Acamas, the swift leader of the Thracians” (5.462; see [[acamas-of-thrace]]), and Hera shouts to the Argives “in the likeness of great-hearted Stentor with the voice of bronze” (5.785; see [[stentor]]). Athena comes to Diomedes in her own form, lays her hand on the yoke of his horses and speaks (5.799). When she mounts his chariot to fight Ares, she puts on the helmet of Hades, “so that mighty Ares should not see her” (5.845): a god hides from a god, and the man she drives against him sees her (see [[helmet-of-hades]]).
