**A gift.** The book opens with it: Athena gave Diomedes “might and daring” (μένος καὶ θάρσος, 5.2; see [[tharsos]]). When he is wounded and prays, she gives it again, and names its source:

{{quote:e7ee1a16-de29-5f9a-b27f-e96a9326fb71}}

The μένος is πατρώϊον, “his father's”: Athena gives the son what she once stood beside in the father (see [[tydeus]] and [[gennaios]]). Its effect is immediate. “Three times as great a fury seized him” (τρὶς τόσσον ἕλεν μένος, 5.136), and he goes back into the fight like a lion wounded by a shepherd (see [[wounded-lion]]). When Sthenelus urges retreat, Diomedes answers from what the goddess gave him: “my might is still steadfast” (ἔτι μοι μένος ἔμπεδόν ἐστιν, 5.254).

Other gods give it too. Apollo sends Aeneas back from his temple, healed, and “put might into the breast of the shepherd of the people”, and his companions see him “with his might unimpaired” (5.513–516). Ares, in the likeness of Acamas, urges on “the might and spirit of every man” (ὄτρυνε μένος καὶ θυμὸν ἑκάστου, 5.470), and Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, does the same for the Argives in the same verse (5.792). A god's gift can be meant for harm. Ares urges on the μένος of Menelaus, “intending that he should be brought down at the hands of Aeneas” (5.563–564).

**Lost.** μένος can also go. Sarpedon asks Hector, “where has the might gone that you used to have?” (5.472), and Hector's answer is to rally the army. When Diomedes' spear goes through Pandarus' face, “there his soul and his strength were loosed” (λύθη ψυχή τε μένος τε, 5.296): the μένος that drove him leaves him with the ψυχή (see [[psyche]]), as the bronze took it from the lambs of the oath (3.294).

**Not only men.** The winds have it: the clouds on the mountains stand still “while the might of the North Wind sleeps” (5.524). At the end of the book Zeus finds it in his own son: “You have your mother's fury, uncontrollable and unyielding, Hera's” (μητρός τοι μένος, 5.892–893). Diomedes' μένος came from his father, Ares' from his mother. The verb formed from the noun, μενεαίνω, “be furiously eager”, marks the limit that Book 5 sets on Diomedes' might: he springs at Aeneas under Apollo's hands “eager to kill him” (μενεαίνων, 5.436), and after he recognizes Ares he tells the Achaeans not “to be eager to fight in force against gods” (μενεαινέμεν, 5.606).
