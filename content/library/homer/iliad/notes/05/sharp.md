Athena gives Diomedes leave to wound one goddess: “if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the war, her you may wound with the sharp bronze” (5.131–132), and he carries it out “with his sharp spear” (5.336; [[daughter-of-zeus]]). He repeats her words at the end of the book (5.820–821). The formulas of the sharp weapon are everywhere else in the killing: Menelaus kills Scamandrius “with his sharp spear” (5.50), Meges strikes Pedaeus with his (5.73), and it was not fated for Odysseus to kill Sarpedon “with the sharp bronze” (5.675). The simile of the two lions ends with them “killed at the hands of men with the sharp bronze” (5.558; [[two-lions]]).

Book 5 has the adjective in two new shapes. ὀξυόεις, a longer form meaning “sharp-pointed,” which the translation renders “sharp,” describes Menelaus' spear (5.50) and the spears of Menelaus and Aeneas held out against each other (5.568). The plural ὀξέα describes Hector's spears as he goes through the army (5.495) and the spears the Trojans shower on Ajax, “sharp and gleaming” (5.619; [[gleaming-all-over]]). The adverb ὀξύ, “quick to see,” at 5.312 and 5.680 is not the epithet. Nor is ὀξέα κεκλήγων in Book 2, where Thersites screams out his “shrill insults” (2.222).
