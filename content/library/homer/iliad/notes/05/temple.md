**On Pergamus.** When Apollo has saved Aeneas and warned off Diomedes, he “set Aeneas apart from the throng in sacred Pergamus, where his temple had been built” (5.445–446). Pergamus is the citadel of Troy, and Apollo's seat there: in Book 4 he looked down from it and shouted to the Trojans, and here, after sending Ares into the battle, he sits down “on the height of Pergamus” (4.508, 5.460; see [[pergamus]] and [[apollo]]). It is the first temple in Troy that the Iliad names. Athena's, on the same citadel, comes in Book 6, where the priestess Theano lays the robe of the Trojan women on the goddess's knees (6.297–304; see [[theano]]).

**The inner shrine.** Inside is the ἄδυτον, the inner room, literally the place “not to be entered” (ἀ-, “not”, and δύω, “enter”). The word occurs only in these two passages of Homer. There the god's mother and sister heal the man he has saved: “There Leto and Artemis of the showering arrows healed him in the great inner shrine and gave him glory” (5.447–448; see [[healing]], [[leto]] and [[artemis]]). κυδαίνω, “give glory”, is more than healing: the goddesses restore his standing along with his body (see [[kydos]]). When the battle turns, Apollo sends him “out of the rich inner shrine” and puts might in his breast (5.512–513). πίων, “rich”, is the word for Athena's “rich temple” at Athens, where sacrifices are offered as the years come round (2.549–551).

**A house of the god.** νηός, from ναίω, “dwell”, is the god's dwelling, and Apollo's household lives in it: his mother and his sister, who work the healing while he goes back to the fight. The same form νηός is also “of a ship” (see [[temple]] and [[ships]]).
