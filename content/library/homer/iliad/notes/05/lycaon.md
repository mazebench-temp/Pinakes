Lycaon is named ten times in Book 5, eight of them as the father in his son's title (5.95, 5.101, 5.169, 5.179, 5.229, 5.246, 5.276, 5.283; see [[pandarus]]). He appears in his own right once, in the speech in which Pandarus regrets what he left at home:

{{quote:e4417fec-ae55-5d8f-857a-cd21fce73f8c}}

γέρων αἰχμητά, “the old spearman” (5.197): αἰχμητά is an old nominative in -α, like ἱππότα (“horseman”) and νεφεληγερέτα (“cloud-gatherer”). The house is rich. The Catalogue called its people wealthy (ἀφνειοί, 2.825; see [[zeleia]] and [[wealthy]]), and Lycaon keeps eleven new chariots under their cloths, each with a pair of horses beside it. The verse of the horses at their barley and spelt returns for the Trojan horses by the watch-fires (5.196 ~ 8.564).

The scene is one of the Iliad's parting instructions, given by a father to a son as he sets out (ἐπέτελλε, 5.198). Hippolochus told Glaucus “always to be the best” (6.207–208), Peleus told Achilles to keep his great heart in check and Menoetius told Patroclus to guide Achilles with advice (9.252–259, 11.783–789). Those sons keep or forget their fathers' words; Pandarus confesses that he did not listen (5.201), and dies in the same book. His reason, to spare the horses short rations “with men penned in” (5.202–203), was a careful owner's. The archer who came on foot dies falling from Aeneas' chariot (5.294).
