---
summary: Diomedes' prayer to Athena has every step of the scene, and it is answered at once and in person (5.114–132). It is the first prayer since Book 1 to be granted as asked, and the answer gives more than was asked.
refs: [5.114-122]
passages:
  - 5.114-122 | Diomedes prays to Athena
---

1. **Setting and gesture.** Diomedes has drawn back to his chariot, and Sthenelus has drawn the arrow from his shoulder; the blood is still spurting through his tunic (5.107–113). No gesture is named: “Then Diomedes, good at the war cry, prayed” (δὴ τότʼ ἔπειτʼ ἠρᾶτο, 5.114).
2. **Invocation.** “Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone” (5.115; see [[hear-me]] and [[atrytone]]). The same invocation opens Odysseus' prayer before the night raid, and Penelope's and Odysseus' prayers to Athena in the Odyssey (10.278; Odyssey 4.762, 6.324).
3. **Claim.** “If ever in friendship you stood by my father and me in destructive war” (5.116–117). The claim is εἴ ποτε, “if ever”, as in Chryses' prayers (1.39–41), but what it recalls is not a temple or a sacrifice. It is the goddess's friendship to his father, the story Agamemnon told in Book 4 (see [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). φίλα φρονέουσα, “in friendship”, asks to be answered in kind: νῦν αὖτʼ ἐμὲ φῖλαι, “now be my friend in turn”.
4. **Request.** “Grant that I may kill this man, and that he come within the cast of my spear, who struck me first, and boasts over me, and says that I shall not look much longer on the bright light of the sun” (5.118–120). The request answers the boast, and the boast is quoted in it: Pandarus said the best of the Achaeans would not last long (5.103–104; see [[prayer]] and [[while-i-live]]).
5. **Answer.** “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121), in the verse of Chryses' prayers with a new name (1.43, 1.457). No prayer in Books 2 to 4 was granted: Zeus refused Agamemnon's (2.419–420) and did not fulfill the prayers at the oath (3.302), and the poem does not say that Apollo heard Pandarus (4.119–126). Athena answers at once. She makes his limbs light, stands near him, and gives him more than he asked: his father's might and eyes to see the gods, with a limit on what he may do with them (5.122–132). The request itself is granted to the letter. Pandarus comes within the cast of his spear, and Athena guides the throw (5.290–296; see [[pandarus]]).

The gesture of prayer appears once more in the book without a prayer. Aeneas tells Pandarus to “lift up your hands to Zeus and let fly a shaft at this man” (5.174), and Pandarus answers with a complaint about his bow (5.180–216).
