**The mother.** Iris brings Aphrodite to Olympus in Ares' chariot (5.353–369), and she falls “on the knees of Dione, her mother” (5.370–371). Dione takes her in her arms, strokes her and “called her by name” (5.372; see [[called-him-by-name]]). She supposes that a god has done it: “Who of the heavenly gods has done such things to you, dear child, for no reason, as if you were caught doing some wrong openly?” (5.373–374). The answer is a man. Aphrodite names Diomedes and gives the news that the book is about: “now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.380). See [[mothers-and-sons]].

**Endurance.**

{{quote:28c4e5f8-1fd1-5180-8a36-31fc934d5c8c}}

The first line is the one with which Hephaestus comforted his mother Hera in Book 1, with “my child” in place of “my mother” (τέτλαθι μῆτερ ἐμή, 1.586). In both speeches a story from the gods' own past follows, and both teach endurance. Dione's is built on the verb: τέτλαθι, “be patient”, and τλῆμεν, “have endured” (5.382–383), then τλῆ μὲν Ἄρης … τλῆ δʼ Ἥρη … τλῆ δʼ Ἀΐδης, “Ares endured … Hera endured … Hades endured” (5.385, 5.392, 5.395; see [[anaphora]]). Ares was bound by Otus and Ephialtes in a bronze jar for thirteen months, until Hermes stole him away (5.385–391; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]]). Heracles shot Hera in the breast with a three-barbed arrow, and Hades in the shoulder “in Pylos among the dead” (5.392–402; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). None of these stories is told anywhere else in Homer (see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]).

The examples do more than console. The gods suffer at the hands of men, Dione says, “bringing them on one another” (5.384), and she applies it at once: “against you the gray-eyed goddess Athena set this man on” (5.405). A mortal holds the spear, and a god sends him. Ares will make the same complaint to Zeus at the end of the book (5.873–875; see [[olympians]]).

**The threat.** The comfort turns into a curse on Diomedes:

{{quote:eb6af802-d7ab-5073-997a-ba8d195879e8}}

Let him beware of meeting someone better than Aphrodite, or his wife [[Aegialeia]] will wake the household with her wailing for “the best of the Achaeans” (5.410–415; see [[diomedes]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). The Iliad does not fulfill the threat. Diomedes goes on to wound Ares himself, and in the Odyssey he comes home safely to Argos (Od. 3.180–182). Dione states the rule, and the book shows the exception: a man whom Athena protects. See [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].

**The healing.**

{{quote:4b64ff01-7c19-5d30-ac3d-4eebfc8a215e}}

No drug is needed. Hades and Ares are healed by Paeëon's pain-killing drugs (5.401, 5.900; see [[paeeon]]); Aphrodite is healed by her mother's hands (see [[ichor]] and [[healing]]). Athena and Hera look on and mock, and Zeus sends his daughter back to the works of marriage (5.418–430; see [[zeus]]).
