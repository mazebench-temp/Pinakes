**Through the body.** The poem follows the point where it goes (see [[androktasia]]). It enters the back between the shoulders and comes out through the chest, in the same verse for Odius and for Scamandrius (5.40–41, 5.56–57). It goes in at the right buttock and passes under the bone into the bladder (5.66–67). It strikes Pedaeus on the back of the head and cuts through under the tongue, so that he “bit the cold bronze with his teeth” (5.73–75). Diomedes' spear strikes Pandarus beside the eye, passes through the teeth, cuts off the tongue at the root, and comes out under the chin (5.290–293). ἀκωκή, “point”, is the word for the part that does the work (5.16, 5.67).

**Thrown and thrust.** A spear is thrown from a distance, as Phegeus throws first and misses over Diomedes' left shoulder (5.15–17), or thrust at close quarters, as Idomeneus stabs Phaestus as he mounts his chariot (νύξʼ, 5.46). Diomedes' answer to Phegeus “did not fly from his hand in vain” (5.18): the translation calls it “the shaft”, βέλος, the word for any missile (see [[arrows]]).

**Guided by a goddess.** In Book 4 Athena guided Pandarus' arrow to Menelaus' belt (ἴθυνεν, 4.132). In Book 5 “Athena guided the shaft” of Diomedes' spear into Pandarus' face (βέλος δʼ ἴθυνεν Ἀθήνη, 5.290; see [[pandarus]]). At the end she catches Ares' spear in her hand and pushes it up and away from the chariot, and she drives Diomedes' spear home into Ares' flank (ἐπέρεισε, 5.853–857): a goddess puts her weight behind a mortal's throw (see [[athena]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

**The gods' spears.** Ares' spear rests in a mist beside his horses while he sits out the fighting (5.356); in battle he “wielded a huge spear in his hands” (5.594). Athena's spear is “heavy, huge, and massive, with which she overpowers the ranks of men” (5.746), the spear she took up on stepping into Hera's chariot. Hera, rebuking the Argives, recalls how the Trojans dreaded the “heavy spear” of Achilles (5.790).

**A spear in the wound.** Tlepolemus and Sarpedon throw at the same moment, and their long spears fly together (5.655–657). Sarpedon's goes through Tlepolemus' neck; Tlepolemus' goes through Sarpedon's left thigh, grazing the bone (5.657–662). The long ash spear drags at the wounded man as his companions carry him off, and no one thinks to draw it out, until Pelagon pushes it through and out under the oak of Zeus (5.663–667, 5.692–695; see [[healing]] and [[sarpedon]]).

**A heel on the body.** Ajax, driven back from Amphius, sets his heel on the body to draw out his spear (λὰξ προσβάς, 5.620–621). Hector frees his spear from Patroclus' body with the same step (16.862–863; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).
