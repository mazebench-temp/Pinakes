summary: Odysseus, seeing Sarpedon carried off, ponders “in his mind and in his heart” whether to pursue the son of Zeus or kill more of the Lycians, and Athena turns his heart against the Lycians, because it was not fated for him to kill Sarpedon (5.671–676).
grc: ["μερμήριξε δʼ ἔπειτα"]
en: [pondered in his mind and in his heart]
passages:
  - 5.669-676 | Odysseus ponders
---

Sarpedon has killed Tlepolemus and is carried off wounded, and Odysseus sees it, “and his heart raged within him” (5.668–670). Then comes the formula of deliberation, with its two alternatives, ἢ … ἦ, “whether … or”:

“and then he pondered in his mind and in his heart whether he should pursue further the son of loud-thundering Zeus, or rather take the lives of more of the Lycians” (5.671–673).

μερμήριξε, “he pondered”, is the verb of Achilles' choice in Book 1 (1.189), and κατὰ φρένα καὶ κατὰ θυμόν, “in his mind and in his heart”, the phrase of its other formula (1.193; see [[phrenes]] and [[thymos]]). In Book 1 Athena came and stopped Achilles' hand. Here the choice is taken out of the man's hands in two ways at once: “But it was not fated for great-hearted Odysseus to kill the mighty son of Zeus with the sharp bronze; so Athena turned his heart against the multitude of the Lycians” (5.674–676). The narrator states what was not fated, and the goddess makes it so by working on the man's heart (see [[moira]], [[decision-scene]] and [[divine-intervention]]). Sarpedon's death is kept for Patroclus (16.419–507). Odysseus kills seven Lycians, and would have killed more had Hector not seen him (5.677–680; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[odysseus]]).
