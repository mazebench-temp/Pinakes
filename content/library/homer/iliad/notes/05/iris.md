summary: Iris leads the wounded Aphrodite out of the battle and drives her to Olympus in the chariot of Ares, where she unyokes the horses and feeds them ambrosia.

---

In Books 2 and 3 Iris came with a message, in a mortal's likeness. Here she comes as herself, and the poem does not say that anyone sent her. She takes the wounded goddess out of the throng, “weighed down with pain” (5.353–354; see [[aphrodite]]), and when Ares lends his horses, she drives:

{{quote:f5d3bfa8-50b3-5bb8-ada6-2de1240c0305}}

Her epithet is still ποδήνεμος, “wind-footed” (5.353, 5.368; see [[wind-footed]]), but she travels by chariot, takes the reins and the whip, and is the one who unyokes the horses at the end of the journey. “And whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling” is the regular verse of a chariot setting out, used again of Hera's horses in this book (5.366 ~ 5.768; 8.45). The gods' horses eat the gods' food. Iris sets ambrosial fodder before those of Ares on Olympus, the Simoeis makes ambrosia spring up for Hera's by the rivers of Troy (5.775–777), and the Hours tie Hera's horses at ambrosial mangers (8.433–434; see [[ambrosia]] and [[horses]]).
