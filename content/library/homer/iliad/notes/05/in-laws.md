---
summary: Sarpedon taunts Hector that he once boasted he could hold Troy without the allies, with only his brothers and his brothers-in-law (γαμβροί).
grc: [γαμβροῖσι]
en: [brothers-in-law]
---

Rebuking Hector, Sarpedon recalls a boast: “You said, I think, that you would hold the city without the people and the allies, alone, with your brothers-in-law and your brothers” (5.473–474). γαμβρός looks the other way from the old words of this entry: it is the man who marries a daughter of the house, and so a son-in-law or a brother-in-law. Priam's daughters lived in their father's palace with their husbands (6.247–250), and those husbands are Hector's γαμβροί. Sarpedon's point is that Hector's defense of Troy was to be a family affair, and now the family has vanished and the allies do the fighting: “they cower like dogs around a lion, and we are the ones who fight, we who are only allies among you” (5.476–477; see [[trojan-allies]] and [[dogs-around-a-lion]]).

The same word names the kinsmen who fight for Troy later. Alcathous, whom Idomeneus kills, was a γαμβρός of Anchises, married to his eldest daughter (13.427–433).
