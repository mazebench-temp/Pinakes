---
summary: Book 5 tells of a sack of Troy that has already happened and warns of the one to come. Tlepolemus boasts that Heracles sacked Ilios with six ships (5.640–642), Sarpedon warns Hector that the Achaeans will soon sack his city (5.487–489), and Hera recalls the gods' promise that Menelaus would sack well-walled Ilios (5.715–716).

**The first sack.** Troy has fallen before. Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon that his father Heracles came for the horses of Laomedon “with only six ships and fewer men, and sacked the city of Ilios, and widowed its streets” (5.641–642). Sarpedon answers that the city fell through Laomedon's folly (5.648–651). The Iliad's only account of a fall of Troy is of the first one, in a generation before the war (see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]).

**A warning.** Sarpedon ends his rebuke of Hector with the danger that the Trojans will be caught as if in an all-catching net, “and they will soon sack your well-peopled city” (5.489; see [[the-net]]). Ares, in the likeness of Acamas, asks the sons of Priam how long they will let their people die: “Until they are fighting around the well-built gates?” (5.466). Both speak to rouse the Trojans, and both put the sack into the future. The ships of Alexander, built by Phereclus, were “an evil for all the Trojans” (5.63; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]).

**A promise.** On the other side Hera speaks of a promise she and Athena made to Menelaus, “that he would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home”, which will prove empty if Ares is allowed to rage (5.715–717; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, reminds the Argives that the Trojans are fighting “far from the city, by the hollow ships” (5.791). The sack is promised by the gods, feared by the Trojans, and for the moment further away than ever.
