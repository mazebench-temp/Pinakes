summary: Menelaus kills the hunter Scamandrius when the Trojans give way. Later he pities the twin sons of Diocles, who had come to Troy for his sake, and goes out against Aeneas, as Ares wishes; Antilochus stands beside him, Aeneas gives way, and Menelaus spears Pylaemenes, leader of the Paphlagonians.

**The hunter.** Menelaus is the third of the six leaders who kill a man each when the Trojans give way (5.37–83). His man is Scamandrius, a hunter whom Artemis herself had taught to shoot, and whose skill does not save him (5.49–58; see [[scamandrius]]). Menelaus, “famed for his spear” (δουρικλειτός, 5.55; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]), stabs him in the back as he flees, “between the shoulders”, with the verse just used of his brother's kill (5.57 = 5.41; see [[agamemnon]]).

**Pity.** Aeneas comes back healed and kills the twin sons of Diocles, Crethon and Orsilochus. They had come to Troy “seeking satisfaction for the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus” (5.552), in the words with which Achilles summed up the whole expedition, τιμὴν ἀρνύμενοι Μενελάῳ (1.159; see [[crethon]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). The man for whose sake they died sees them fall:

{{quote:89465a86-f822-5d7e-af35-21fed00cd9ba}}

ἐλέησεν, “pitied”: pity for the fallen sends him forward. The same verse, with another name, sends Telamonian Ajax against Amphius fifty lines later (τὼ δὲ πεσόντʼ ἐλέησε, 5.561, 5.610; see [[ajax]]), and it returns twice in Book 17 (17.346, 17.352). “Dear to Ares” is his standing epithet (ἀρηΐφιλος; see [[dear-to-ares]]), and here the god himself urges on his might, “intending that he should be brought down at the hands of Aeneas” (5.564). The god who fights for Troy wants the man the war is fought for dead.

**Antilochus.** Nestor's son sees the danger and runs to his side, “for he feared greatly for the shepherd of the people, that he might come to harm, and make all their toil in vain” (5.566–567; see [[antilochus]] and [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). If Menelaus fell, the war would lose its cause. Agamemnon said as much over his wounded brother in Book 4: if Menelaus died, the Achaeans would remember their native land and leave Helen to Priam (4.169–175). Aeneas, “swift warrior though he was”, will not face two men standing side by side (5.571–572). The two drag the twins back to the Achaean army and give them to their companions (5.573–574).

**Pylaemenes.** “There they killed Pylaemenes, the equal of Ares” (5.576): ἑλέτην is dual, the work of the pair. Menelaus spears the Paphlagonian leader at the collarbone as he stands, and Antilochus kills his charioteer (5.576–589; see [[pylaemenes]] and [[mydon]]). The victim returns alive in Book 13, where his son Harpalion attacks Menelaus and is killed in retreat by Meriones (13.643–659).

**Named, not seen.** Twice more in the book Menelaus is spoken of in his absence. Pandarus, counting his wasted arrows, says he has drawn blood from “the son of Tydeus and the son of Atreus” (5.207), that is, from Menelaus in the broken truce (4.127–147). And Hera, seeing Ares rage, tells Athena that “it was an empty word we promised Menelaus, that he would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home” (5.715–716; see [[hera]]).
