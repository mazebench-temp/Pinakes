summary: Book 5 measures sons against fathers. Athena gives Diomedes his father's might and then tells him he is little like his father; the son and the grandson of Zeus fight a duel over the name of their fathers; Pandarus regrets ignoring his; and Zeus heals the son he hates because he is his own.

**The son of Tydeus.** Diomedes' aristeia is his answer to Agamemnon's charge in Book 4, that he is worse than his father in battle (4.399–400; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]). His prayer claims his father's friendship with Athena: “if ever in friendship you stood by my father and me” (5.116). Athena answers by putting into him “your father's might, undaunted, such as the horseman Tydeus, wielder of the shield, used to have” (5.125–126), and he refuses to retreat because “it is not in my blood” (5.253; see [[gennaios]]). Late in the book the goddess measures him against his father again, and finds him short: “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800; see [[small-but-a-fighter]] and [[tydeus]]). The father's example is both a gift and a reproach.

**The sons of Zeus.** Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, and Sarpedon, son of Zeus, meet as “the son and the grandson of Zeus the cloud-gatherer” (5.631), and they fight first with their fathers' names. Tlepolemus says that those who call Sarpedon a son of Zeus are lying, since he falls far short of the men born of Zeus in the old days, like “mighty Heracles, my father”, who sacked Troy with six ships (5.633–646; see [[pseudos]] and [[men-of-today]]). Sarpedon answers that Heracles sacked Troy because of the folly of its king Laomedon, who cheated him of the horses he was promised (5.648–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). The father's glory does not save the son. Sarpedon kills Tlepolemus, and “his father still kept ruin away” from him (5.662).

**Advice and inheritance.** Pandarus tells Aeneas that his old father Lycaon told him to bring horses and chariots, and that he did not listen (5.197–205; see [[peitho]]). Aeneas' horses came from his father Anchises, who stole a strain of the breed of Tros (5.268–272). Sthenelus names both his adversaries by their fathers (5.246–248; see [[euchomai]]).

**A father's anger.** At the end Zeus rebukes Ares as Agamemnon rebuked Achilles, in the same words: “strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles” (5.891; see [[strife-is-always-dear]]). He hates him, and he heals him anyway, “for you are of my stock, and it was to me your mother bore you” (5.896). Had Ares been any other god's son, he would long ago have been lower than the sons of Uranus (5.897–898).

The bereaved fathers of the obituaries, Dares, Eurydamas, Phaenops and Diocles, are gathered under [[bereaved-parents]].
