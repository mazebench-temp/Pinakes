**Diomedes' prayer.** Sthenelus has drawn the arrow from his shoulder, and Diomedes prays:

{{quote:4c36aaba-a0a1-5f4f-971c-32e9bff41e40}}

It has the three parts of a prayer (see [[prayer-type-scene]]). The invocation is “Hear me” (κλῦθί μευ), with the goddess's titles, “child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone” (see [[hear-me]], [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]] and [[atrytone]]). The claim is past favor, as in Chryses' prayers, but it is claimed through his father: “if ever in friendship you stood by my father and me in destructive war” (see [[tydeus]] and [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). The request is a death: “grant that I may kill this man, and that he come within the cast of my spear, who struck me first, and boasts over me”. The answer comes in the verse that closed Chryses' prayers, with Athena's name for Apollo's: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (5.121; 1.43, 1.457). The goddess answers at once and in person: she makes his limbs light, stands beside him and speaks (5.122–132). In Book 10 Diomedes prays to her again in the same terms, “stand by me as you stood by my father Tydeus”, and the closing verse is repeated in the plural (10.284–295).

**Prayer and boast.** Fifteen lines earlier, the same words close Pandarus' shout over the wounded Diomedes: “So he spoke, boasting” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος, 5.106). εὔχομαι means to declare something solemnly. Said to a god it is a prayer, said over an enemy it is a boast, and said of oneself it is a claim (see [[euchomai]]). The narrator lets one phrase carry both, and adds his verdict to each: the boaster is wrong, “but the swift shaft had not overcome him” (5.106), and the man who prays is heard. The request of the prayer answers the boast: Diomedes asks to kill the man “who boasts over me” (ἐπεύχεται, 5.119). Elsewhere in the book εὔχομαι is a claim: no one in Lycia “claims to be better” than Pandarus (5.173), and Pandarus and Aeneas claim their fathers (5.246–248). εὖχος is the “triumph” that Pandarus thinks he has won and that Sarpedon promises himself (5.285, 5.654). These lines are not counted here.

**The gesture.** Aeneas tells Pandarus to “lift up your hands to Zeus and let fly a shaft at this man” (Διὶ χεῖρας ἀνασχών, 5.174). The gesture of prayer comes before the shot, as Athena told Pandarus to pray to Apollo before his shot in Book 4 (4.101–103). This time Pandarus does not pray; he complains of his bow (5.180–216; see [[bow]]).
