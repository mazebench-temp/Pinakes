---
summary: Aeneas stands over Pandarus like a lion “trusting in his courage”; Diomedes knows Aphrodite for a god without courage and taunts her with deceiving women who have none; Agamemnon tells the army that flight wins no courage; and on the aegis Courage herself is a goddess.
grc: [ἀλκὶ, ἀνάλκιδας, ἄλκαρ]
en: [feeble, defense]
---

**The lion's courage.** When Pandarus falls, Aeneas bestrides the body “like a lion trusting in his courage” (λέων ὣς ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, 5.299). The phrase belongs to the lion: the Odyssey uses it of the mountain lion to which Odysseus is compared as he comes out of the thicket (*Odyssey* 6.130), and Hector, fighting the Ajaxes for the body of Patroclus, is “trusting in his courage” (18.155–158). See [[lion]].

**The goddess without it.** Diomedes goes after Aphrodite “knowing that she was a feeble god” (ἄναλκις, 5.331), not one of the goddesses who rule in war, Athena and Enyo. When he has wounded her, he taunts her: “is it not enough that you deceive feeble women?” (γυναῖκας ἀνάλκιδας, 5.349; see [[eperopeuo]]). The word that Odysseus used of the commoner who ran for the ships (2.201) is used here of a god and of women.

**The army's.** Agamemnon ends his exhortation with it: “when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage” (5.532; see [[shame-saves-more]]). Hera uses a line that Diomedes spoke to Sthenelus in Book 4, “let us two also think of our furious courage” (5.718 = 4.418), when she proposes to Athena that they go down to the battle. Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon that he will be no ἄλκαρ, “defense”, to the Trojans (5.644).

**Courage in person.** On Athena's aegis, around which Rout runs, are Strife, “and on it Courage (Ἀλκή), and on it chilling Onslaught” (5.739–740). The goddess is a separate entry (see [[courage]]); this word does not claim her name.
