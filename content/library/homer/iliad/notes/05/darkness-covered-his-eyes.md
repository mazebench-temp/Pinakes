**Darkness and death.** The half-verse of Book 4 is not used; Book 5 varies it. Phaestus is stabbed in the right shoulder as he mounts his chariot, “and he fell from the chariot, and hateful darkness seized him” (στυγερὸς δʼ ἄρα μιν σκότος εἷλε, 5.46–47). The darkness is the same, but it “seizes” rather than covers, and it is στυγερός, “hateful”. Phereclus falls to his knees with a groan, “and death enfolded him” (θάνατος δέ μιν ἀμφεκάλυψε, 5.68): death itself does the covering, all around (ἀμφι-). Hypsenor's eyes are seized by purple death and mighty fate (5.82–83; see [[purple-death]]).

**Night.** When Sarpedon's spear goes through Tlepolemus' neck, “murky night covered his eyes” (τὸν δὲ κατʼ ὀφθαλμῶν ἐρεβεννὴ νὺξ ἐκάλυψε, 5.659; see [[tlepolemus]]). ἐρεβεννός, “murky”, belongs with Ἔρεβος, the darkness under the earth.

**Fainting.** Night covers the eyes of men who do not die. Aeneas, his hip crushed by Diomedes' stone, sinks to his knees, “and black night covered his eyes” (5.310), and his mother carries him off. Sarpedon faints when Pelagon pushes the spear from his thigh: “his soul left him, and a mist was poured over his eyes; but he breathed again” (5.696–697; see [[achlys]] and [[psyche]]). The same half-verse of mist ends a life in Book 16, in a killing built on the killing of Phaestus: Meriones stabs Acamas “as he was mounting his chariot” on the right shoulder, and he falls from the chariot, and the mist is poured over his eyes (16.342–344 ~ 5.45–47). The same words serve for dying and for fainting, as they do later for Hector struck by a stone and for Andromache when she sees him dragged (14.438–439, 22.466). See [[androktasia]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]].
