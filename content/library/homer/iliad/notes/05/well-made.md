---
summary: The chariot is “well-made,” εὐεργής, in the verse of a charioteer's fall, when Antilochus kills Mydon.
grc: [εὐεργέος]
---

εὐεργής = εὖ (“well”) + ἔργον (“work”): “well-worked.” The translation renders it as it renders εὔτυκτος, “well-made.” Antilochus hits Mydon, the charioteer of Pylaemenes, with a stone and then with his sword, “and gasping he fell from the well-made chariot headfirst in the dust” (5.585–586; [[mydon]], [[antilochus]]). He stays planted in deep sand until the horses kick him flat, and Antilochus drives them off to the Achaeans (5.587–589). The same verse returns in Book 13, when Antilochus kills the charioteer of Asius and again takes the horses (Il. 13.396–401), and Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, falls from the well-made chariot when Patroclus' stone strikes him (Il. 16.743). In the Odyssey the word describes ships, gold and a cloak (Od. 8.567, 9.202, 13.224).
