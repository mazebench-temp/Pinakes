**The gods in the battle.** In most books the gods act from a distance or in disguise. In Book 5 they are on the field. Athena rides in Diomedes' chariot, Ares fights at Hector's side and strips a man he has killed (5.842–848), Enyo brings Tumult, Aphrodite throws her arms round her son, and Apollo carries him off and makes a phantom for the armies to fight over (5.311–318, 5.445–453, 5.592–595). Aphrodite states the change: “it is no longer the dread din of battle between Trojans and Achaeans; now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.379–380). See [[divine-intervention]] and [[seeing-the-gods]].

**What divides them.** The book states the difference between gods and men in three ways. The first is the body: a god bleeds ichor, not blood, because the gods eat no bread and drink no wine, “and so they are bloodless, and are called immortals” (5.339–342; see [[ichor]]), and a god's wound heals at once, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.402, 5.901). The second is kind, in Apollo's words to Diomedes, “never alike is the race of immortal gods and of men who walk on the earth” (5.441–442; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). The third is time: the man who fights the immortals “does not live long” (5.407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Sight is a fourth difference. Mortal eyes carry a mist that must be taken away before a man can tell god from man (5.127–128; see [[achlys]]).

**What binds them.** The book also shows how the two orders are tied together. The gods have sons and grandsons among the fighters: Aeneas is Aphrodite's son, Sarpedon Zeus' son, Tlepolemus his grandson (5.247–248, 5.631), and their parents save them when they can (see [[bereaved-parents]]). The gods fight one another through men, and Dione says so:

Ares says the same to Zeus: “we gods are forever enduring the most chilling things by one another's will, when we do favors for men” (5.873–874). The gods' pains come from each other, carried by mortal weapons (see [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]).

**The difference at the end.** The book ends on Olympus. Paeëon heals Ares as fast as fig juice curdles milk, Hebe bathes him and dresses him in lovely clothes, and he sits down beside Zeus, “exulting in his glory” (5.899–906). The man he killed in his last hour on the field, Periphas, “by far the best of the Aetolians”, lies where he left him (5.842–848; see [[periphas]]). Zeus tells Aphrodite that the works of war are not given to her (5.428–430; see [[the-works-of-war]]); to the gods they are a province, and to the men below they are death. The contrast is the one Jasper Griffin saw at the heart of the Iliad's gods: their ease makes the seriousness of human suffering visible (*Homer on Life and Death*, 1980; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).
