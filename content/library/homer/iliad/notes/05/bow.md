summary: Pandarus' bow fails him. He has hit two of the best Achaeans and only roused them, so he swears to break the bow and burn it if he ever gets home (5.204–216), takes up the spear instead, and dies. The other archers of the book are divine: Artemis, who taught the hunter Scamandrius, Apollo of the silver bow, and Heracles, whose arrows wounded Hera and Hades.

**The bow that failed.** Aeneas finds Pandarus and asks, “Pandarus, where are your bow and your feathered arrows and your fame?” (5.171–172). The bow was his fame, the gift of Apollo in the Catalogue (2.827), and the weapon that broke the truce in Book 4 (see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). His answer is a lament for it. He came to Troy on foot, “trusting in my bow; but it was not going to help me after all”: he has shot two of the chieftains, Diomedes and Menelaus, and drawn real blood from both, and only roused them (5.204–208).

{{quote:b9f71a4e-b189-549b-9890-98239fb8be55}}

The bow hung on a peg at home, as Odysseus' bow hangs on its peg until Penelope takes it down for the contest (Odyssey 21.53). ἀνεμώλια, “empty wind”, is his last word on it (see [[anemolios]]). Then he gives it up for the spear: “now I will try with the spear” (5.279). His spear reaches Diomedes' corselet and stops there, and Diomedes' spear, guided by Athena, kills him (5.280–296). He never comes home, and the vow to burn the bow, like his vow of a hecatomb to Apollo in Book 4, is never kept (see [[pandarus]]).

**Curved bows.** The bow is τόξα, a plural for one weapon, and it is “curved”: καμπύλα τόξα when Pandarus draws it at Diomedes (5.97), ἀγκύλα τόξα when he takes it from its peg (5.209). See [[curved-bow]].

**The archer gods.** Artemis taught Scamandrius “to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains”, but “Artemis of the showering arrows was no help to him then, nor his far-shooting, in which he had excelled before” (5.51–54; see [[artemis]], [[scamandrius]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). Apollo is “god of the silver bow” three times (5.449, 5.517, 5.760; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]).

**Heracles.** The greatest archer of the book is in Dione's stories. Heracles shot Hera in the right breast “with a three-barbed arrow”, and Hades with a swift arrow “in Pylos among the dead” (5.392–397; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). Dione's verdict is a verse against the bow: “Hard man, doer of violent deeds, who cared nothing for the evil he did, who with his bow brought sorrow on the gods who hold Olympus!” (5.403–404; see [[heracles]]). The Odyssey keeps the memory. Odysseus will not compete with the men of old, Heracles and Eurytus, who competed with the gods in archery, and Eurytus died young because he challenged Apollo (Odyssey 8.223–228); Heracles' shade in Hades still holds his bow bare, an arrow on the string (Odyssey 11.606–608). The man in Book 5 who wounds gods does it with the spear.
