**The gift.** Ancient readers called the book “the aristeia of Diomedes” (see [[the-aristeia]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). It begins with a goddess's gift:

{{quote:55bc383e-6419-5b33-8227-16c2972361f7}}

μένος (“might”) and θάρσος (“daring”) are the gods' to give (see [[menos]] and [[tharsos]]), and ἔκδηλος, “standing out”, is found only here in Homer. Fire plays on his helmet and shield like the star of late summer, the star to which Priam will liken Achilles (5.4–7, 22.26–31; see [[the-autumn-star]]), and in Book 18 Athena kindles the same “tireless fire” (ἀκάματον πῦρ) over Achilles' head (18.225–227). Diomedes has often been read as a stand-in for the absent Achilles, whom Hera recalls just before Athena seeks Diomedes out (5.787–791).

**The wound and the prayer.** He sweeps across the plain like a winter torrent, so that you could not have told which side he was on (5.85–92; see [[river-in-flood]] and [[apostrophe]]). Pandarus' arrow goes through the plate of his corselet into his right shoulder, and the archer shouts that “the best of the Achaeans is hit” (5.103; see [[pandarus]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Sthenelus draws it out (see [[sthenelus]]), and Diomedes prays to Athena by her old favor to his father (5.115–120; see [[prayer-type-scene]]). She makes his limbs light, puts his father's might in his breast, and gives him a new sight:

{{quote:85a7e4df-a946-5a51-9baa-282144c09f8b}}

With the gift comes a rule: he is to fight no god but Aphrodite (5.129–132; see [[achlys]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). From here the book turns on what he knows. He goes after Aphrodite “knowing that she was a feeble god” (5.331), attacks Aeneas “though he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him” (5.433), and sees Ares beside Hector (5.604).

**The rampage.** His fury triples, like that of a lion grazed by a shepherd (5.136–143; see [[wounded-lion]]). He kills in pairs: the sons of the priest Dares, one of whom Hephaestus saves; then Astynous and Hypeiron; Abas and Polyidus, the sons of the dream-reader Eurydamas; Xanthus and Thoon, the only sons of old Phaenops; and two sons of Priam in one chariot (5.9–26, 5.144–165). Three of the four fathers are given lines of their own (see [[dares]], [[eurydamas]], [[phaenops]] and [[androktasia]]).

**Pandarus and Aeneas.** When the two drive at him in one chariot, Sthenelus urges retreat, and Diomedes answers him “looking at him darkly”, as in Book 4 (5.251; 4.411):

{{quote:c04a7601-6a9f-59f3-a261-40eeb7c51bb9}}

γενναῖον (“in my blood”) occurs only here in Homer: the son of Tydeus answers from his birth (see [[gennaios]]). He foresees, rightly, that at most one of the two will escape (5.257–258). Athena guides his spear into Pandarus' face (5.290–296), and Sthenelus drives off the horses of Aeneas (5.319–327; see [[tros]]). He crushes Aeneas' hip with a stone “which two men could not carry, such as mortals are now” (5.302–304; the same lines describe Aeneas' stone at 20.285–287; see [[men-of-today]]).

**Aphrodite.** He wounds the goddess in the hand as she carries her son away, and ichor flows (5.330–342; see [[ichor]] and [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]). Then he taunts her:

{{quote:1b4e8496-e3fb-5283-82cb-29c70f2de4ad}}

ἠπεροπεύεις (“you deceive”) is the verb behind Hector's insult to Paris, ἠπεροπευτά (“seducer”, 3.39; see [[eperopeuo]]). Aphrodite and then Apollo say that he “would fight even with father Zeus” (5.362, 5.457). Dione warns that the man who fights the gods does not live long, and pictures his wife Aegialeia wailing for him (5.406–415; see [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[aegialeia]]). The warning is never fulfilled: the Odyssey brings him home to Argos (Od. 3.180–182).

**Apollo and Ares.**

{{quote:c6f743f1-bf92-5222-9e94-25279a5e608e}}

He draws back “a little way”, avoiding the god's wrath (5.443–444). The same pattern stops Patroclus at the wall of Troy, and at a later fourth onset Patroclus dies (16.702–711, 16.784–787; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). When he recognizes Ares beside Hector, he shudders like a man stopped at a flooding river, and tells the Achaeans to give ground with their faces to the enemy (5.596–606; see [[man-at-the-river]]).

**Athena's rebuke.** Athena finds him by his chariot, cooling his wound under the shield strap, and rebukes him with his father (5.793–813; see [[tydeus]]). Agamemnon's rebuke he bore in silence (4.401–402). This one he answers:

{{quote:3a405d16-eec7-5421-a8d3-0c4344226fe7}}

He gives the goddess back her own command, two lines of it word for word (5.820–821 = 5.131–132; see [[message-repeated]]). In her story Tydeus fought although she told him to feast quietly (5.802–807); the son holds back because he obeys her. She releases him with the words she used of his father: “such a helper am I to you” (5.828; 5.808; see [[epitarrhothos]]).

**The chariot.**

{{quote:7e6320af-9af1-5bbb-8481-e2ba307b3012}}

Athena drives, wearing the helmet of Hades so that Ares cannot see her. She turns his spear aside and drives Diomedes' spear into his flank, and Ares bellows like ten thousand men (5.840–863; see [[helmet-of-hades]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). On Olympus he complains that Athena “has let loose the son of Tydeus, overbearing Diomedes, to rage madly against the immortal gods”, and repeats Apollo's words about him (5.881–884; 5.458–459). A mortal has wounded two gods in one day (see [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).
