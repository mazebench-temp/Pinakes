summary: Book 5 repeats words inside speeches. Diomedes answers Athena with her own command (5.819–821 ~ 5.130–132); Ares tells Zeus in Apollo's words how Diomedes wounded Aphrodite and then attacked a god (5.883–884 = 5.458–459); and Aphrodite and Apollo both say that Diomedes “would fight even with father Zeus” (5.362, 5.457).

**Athena's command.** Athena told Diomedes, when she took the mist from his eyes, “do not fight face to face with the immortal gods, with any of the others; but if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the war, her you may wound with the sharp bronze” (5.130–132). When she comes back and rebukes him for giving ground, he answers with her own words: “You would not let me fight face to face with the blessed gods, with any of the others; but if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, comes into the war, her I may wound with the sharp bronze” (5.819–821). The command has become a report, and “immortal” has become “blessed”. The quotation is his defense: he is not afraid, he is obeying her (see [[diomedes]] and [[athena]]).

**Apollo's words in Ares' mouth.** Apollo, rousing Ares against Diomedes, describes him: “First he came close to Cypris and wounded her hand at the wrist, and then he rushed at me myself, like a god” (5.458–459). Ares, wounded, tells Zeus the same two verses (5.883–884). In Apollo's mouth “me myself” was Apollo; in Ares' it is Ares, whom Diomedes has now wounded. The words were true of both gods, and by repeating them the poem tells the two assaults as one story (see [[ares]] and [[apollo]]).

**The same charge.** Aphrodite, begging Ares for his horses, says that Diomedes “now would fight even with father Zeus” (5.362), and Apollo, in the next scene with Ares, says it again (5.457). The gods have one verse for the man who has gone past the limits set for mortals (see [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]).
