Zeus sends Aphrodite back to “the works of marriage”.

**Wives left at home.** The men of Book 5 carry their wives in their speeches. Pandarus swears by his homecoming, to see “my native land and my wife and my great high-roofed house” (5.212–213), and does not see them. Sarpedon reminds Hector that he left “my dear wife and my infant son” in far Lycia (5.480), and says the same words wounded, fearing he will never go home to gladden them (5.688). He tells Hector that he should be urging the Trojans “to stand firm and defend their wives” (ὤρεσσι, 5.485–486), with a rare word for wife, ὄαρ. See [[nostos]] and [[nepios]].

**Wives in the obituaries and the threats.** Theano, the wife of Antenor, reared her husband's bastard son Pedaeus “like her own dear children, to please her husband” (5.70–71; see [[posis]] and [[theano]]). Dione's threat against Diomedes is his wife's grief: Aegialeia, the wise daughter of Adrastus, will wake the household with her wailing, longing for her wedded husband (5.412–415; see [[aegialeia]] and [[pothe]]). The Iliad tells nothing more of her; she is the widow a goddess's threat imagines.

**The works of marriage.** On Olympus Athena mocks the wounded Aphrodite: she must have been urging some Achaean woman to go off with the Trojans she loves, and scratched her hand on the woman's brooch (5.421–425). The joke is Helen's story in miniature, an Achaean woman lured to Troy by the goddess of desire. Zeus answers it by sending his daughter back to her province: “you attend to the lovely works of marriage” (5.428–430; see [[the-works-of-war]] and [[marriage]]). Zeus' own marriage appears at the end of the book: he tells Ares that he can hardly keep Hera down with words (5.893).
