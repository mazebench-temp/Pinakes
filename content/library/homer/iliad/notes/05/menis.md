summary: In Book 5 μῆνις is the wrath of gods only. Athena leads Ares out of the battle to avoid the wrath of Zeus, Aeneas warns that the wrath of a god is hard to bear, and Diomedes gives ground before the wrath of Apollo.
grc: [μῆνις, μηνίσας]
passages:
  - 5.29-36 | Athena leads Ares away
  - 5.440-444 | Apollo's warning
---

Achilles is absent from Book 5, and his μῆνις is not mentioned. The word belongs here to the gods, three times, and each time it is something to be avoided.

**The wrath of Zeus.** Athena takes Ares by the hand and proposes that the two of them withdraw and let the mortals fight it out, “and avoid the wrath of Zeus” (Διὸς δʼ ἀλεώμεθα μῆνιν, 5.34). Zeus has forbidden nothing yet; he will forbid the gods to help either side only at the start of Book 8 (8.5–27; see [[zeus]]). Athena's reason serves her purpose. With Ares gone, the Danaans turn the Trojans back (5.37), and she herself will be back in the battle before the day is over, with Zeus' leave (5.765–766).

**The wrath of a god.** Aeneas, seeing an unknown man sweep the field, wonders whether he is a god angry with the Trojans:

{{quote:a99287a6-1854-530d-bcd3-8068ba4defa2}}

μηνίσας ἱρῶν, “in wrath over sacrifices”, names the classic cause, a god who has not had his offerings, as Artemis sent the boar on Calydon because Oeneus left her out of a sacrifice (9.533–538). Apollo's wrath in Book 1 was of the other kind: “It is not for a vow that he finds fault, nor for a hecatomb, but because of his priest” (1.93–94). Aeneas' maxim, χαλεπὴ δὲ θεοῦ ἔπι μῆνις, “the wrath of a god is hard to bear”, is the lesson the whole poem teaches from its first line. See [[kotos]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].

**The wrath of Apollo.** At the fourth charge Apollo warns Diomedes that gods and men are never alike, and Diomedes “drew back a little way, avoiding the wrath of Apollo who shoots from afar” (5.443–444; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). The verse returns when Patroclus draws back from the same god at the wall of Troy (16.710–711). Diomedes heeds it and survives; Patroclus goes on and dies.
