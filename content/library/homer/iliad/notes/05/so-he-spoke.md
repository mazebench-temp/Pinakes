summary: The closing formulas of Book 5 turn speech into action. The same words close a boast and a prayer with opposite results, “He spoke, and” leads straight into a throw, the goddesses obey each other in one verse, and a new line, “So they spoke such words to one another”, moves the story from one place to another.
grc: ["ὣς εἰποῦσʼ", "ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντες", "ὣς οἳ μὲν τοιαῦτα πρὸς ἀλλήλους ἀγόρευον", "ὣς φάμενος", "ὣς φαμένη"]
en: [So they spoke such words to one another]
---

The closings of Book 5: 5.35, 106, 111, 121, 133, 239, 274, 280, 290, 352, 363, 416, 426, 431, 443, 460, 470, 493, 533, 607, 655, 689, 719, 767, 792, 835, 899. New forms are the feminine ὣς εἰποῦσʼ and ὣς φαμένη, “so saying”, for Athena and Hera (5.133, 5.792, 5.835), and ὣς φάμενος for Diomedes (5.290).

**Boast and prayer.** ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος closes Pandarus' boast, “So he spoke, boasting”, and fifteen lines later Diomedes' prayer, “So he spoke in prayer” (5.106, 5.121). The rest of each verse gives the result: the shaft had not overcome him; Athena heard him (see [[prayer]] and [[euchomai]]).

**Into the deed.** Often there is no gap between word and act. “He spoke, and” (ἦ ῥα καί, ἦ καί) leads straight into Pandarus' spear-cast, Dione's wiping away of the ichor, and Agamemnon's throw (5.280, 5.416, 5.533). Diomedes answers Pandarus' boast with three verses and then “So saying, he let fly” (5.290).

**Obeying.** Hera proposes that the two of them arm, “So she spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena did not disobey” (5.719); Zeus gives Hera leave, and “the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey” (5.767). The line of obedience serves both goddesses (see [[litotes]]).

**The effect.** The second half of the verse gives what the words did. Diomedes “drew back a little way” at Apollo's warning (5.443); Sarpedon's rebuke “stung Hector to the heart” (5.493); Zeus “smiled” at Athena's joke (5.426; see [[smiling-at-him]]); and he “told Paeëon to heal” the son he had just rebuked (5.899). Once the effect is silence. Sarpedon begs Hector to save him, “and Hector of the gleaming helmet said nothing to him”, but rushed past to drive back the Argives (5.689–691).

**Changing the scene.** ὣς οἳ μὲν τοιαῦτα πρὸς ἀλλήλους ἀγόρευον, “So they spoke such words to one another”, closes a conversation and turns to what was happening meanwhile elsewhere. It moves the story from Diomedes and Sthenelus to the chariot of Aeneas bearing down on them (5.274–275), and from Olympus, where Zeus has spoken to Aphrodite, back to the battlefield (5.431–432). The verse is used often in both poems (7.464, 8.212, 13.81; Odyssey 4.620).
