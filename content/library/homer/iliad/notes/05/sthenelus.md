**The arrow.** Wounded by Pandarus, Diomedes calls on him by his father's name: ὄρσο πέπον Καπανηϊάδη, “Up, dear son of Capaneus” (5.109). Sthenelus leaps down and draws the shaft “clean through and out of his shoulder”, and the blood spurts up through the tunic (5.111–113). No healer comes, as Machaon came to Menelaus in Book 4 (4.210–219). Diomedes prays, and Athena answers with might rather than a cure: late in the day he is still cooling the wound (5.114–132, 5.795–798; see [[healing]]).

**Caution.** When Aeneas and Pandarus drive at them together, Sthenelus sees them first:

{{quote:f9f0426f-1faa-5ff5-b768-823a1a1f5b9d}}

It is the second time he speaks up beside Diomedes, and the second time Diomedes answers him “looking at him darkly” (5.251; 4.411; see [[looking-darkly]]). In Book 4 he spoke too boldly for the king's presence; here he speaks too cautiously for Diomedes, who will not give ground and gives him orders instead (5.252–273; see [[diomedes]]).

**The horses.** He carries out the orders in the words in which they were given. Diomedes told him to hold their own horses, tying the reins to the chariot rail, and to rush at the horses of Aeneas and drive them to the Achaeans (5.261–264). The narrator tells that he did it in almost the same lines (5.321–324; see [[message-repeated]]), for he did not forget his instructions (5.319). συνθεσίαι, “compacts, instructions”, occurs elsewhere in the Iliad only once, of the compacts the army made when it sailed: “What will become of our compacts and our oaths?” (2.339).

{{quote:d41be128-2a41-5121-b838-14ee3cdabd18}}

He gives the horses to his companion Deipylus to drive to the ships and mounts his own chariot again to follow Diomedes (5.325–330; see [[deipylus]]). They are of the breed Zeus gave to Tros, and Diomedes drives them in the chariot race of Book 23 (23.290–292; see [[tros]]).

**Replaced by a goddess.** At the end of the book Athena pushes him from the chariot, “pulling him back with her hand”, and he springs down quickly (5.835–836). She takes his place, the reins and the whip, and the oaken axle groans under “a dread goddess and the best of men” (5.838–840; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).
