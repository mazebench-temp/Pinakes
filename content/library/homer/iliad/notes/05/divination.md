The one diviner of Book 5 is known only through his sons. After killing Astynous and Hypeiron, Diomedes “went after Abas and Polyidus, sons of Eurydamas, the old interpreter of dreams; but the old man read no dreams for them when they set out, and mighty Diomedes slew them and stripped them” (5.148–151; see [[eurydamas]], [[abas-son-of-eurydamas]] and [[polyidus-son-of-eurydamas]]).

ὀνειροπόλος is “one who busies himself with dreams”, from ὄνειρος, “dream”, and πολέω, “go about, tend”. He is one of the three experts Achilles names in Book 1: “some seer, or a priest, or even an interpreter of dreams—for a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (1.62–63; see [[onar]] and [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]). His verb is κρίνω, “sort, judge”: ἐκρίνατʼ ὀνείρους, he “judged” the dreams, deciding what they meant.

The line about him is compressed and has been read in more than one way. The translation takes it that he did not read the dreams that would have warned his sons when they left for the war. Either way the point is the same: the man whose craft was the future did not see his own sons' death. The Catalogue made the same point twice. The seer Merops of Percote, who “knew the art of prophecy” above all men, forbade his sons to go to war, “for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.831–834; Diomedes kills them in 11.328–334; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]); and the augur Ennomus fought for Troy, “but his birds did not save him from black doom” (2.858–859). In Book 5 the same irony is spread over the killing lists: the hunter Artemis taught is killed by the spear, the builder of Alexander's ships knew nothing of the oracles of the gods, and of the priests' sons only one is saved by his father's god (5.9–83; see [[prophecy]] and [[priesthood]]).
