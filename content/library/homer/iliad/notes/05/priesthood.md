summary: Two Trojan priests lose sons in Book 5. Dares, the wealthy priest of Hephaestus, sees one son killed by Diomedes while the god hides the other (5.9–24); Dolopion, priest of the Scamander, “honored by the people like a god”, loses his son Hypsenor to Eurypylus (5.76–83).

**Dares.** The first men Diomedes kills are a priest's sons. “There was among the Trojans one Dares, wealthy and noble, a priest of Hephaestus; and he had two sons” (5.9–10). ἱρεύς is the Ionic form of ἱερεύς (see [[priesthood]]). Diomedes kills Phegeus, and the other son, Idaeus, would have died too, “but Hephaestus protected him, and saved him, covering him in night, so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief” (5.23–24). Chryses in Book 1 reminded Apollo of his service and asked for a return (1.39–42). Dares asks for nothing, and the god he serves pays him back unasked, and in part: one son is saved and one is lost (see [[dares]], [[phegeus]], [[idaeus-son-of-dares]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**Dolopion.** Eurypylus kills Hypsenor, “son of high-hearted Dolopion, who had been made priest of the Scamander and was honored by the people like a god” (5.76–78). Dolopion is an ἀρητήρ, as Chryses was (1.11); the word means “one who prays”. A river has a priest because a river is a god: the Trojans sacrifice bulls to the Scamander and throw live horses into its eddies (21.130–132), and Peleus vowed to the Spercheius a hecatomb and his son's hair at the river's precinct and altar (23.144–148). This priest's son gets no god's help. Eurypylus shears off his arm, and “purple death and mighty fate” seize his eyes (5.79–83; see [[dolopion]], [[hypsenor]], [[scamander]] and [[purple-death]]).

**Honored like a god.** θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ, “was honored by the people like a god”, is a formula of rank. It is used of two priests, Dolopion and Onetor, the priest of Zeus on Ida, whose son Laogonus Meriones kills (16.603–605), and of two kings, Agamemnon and Thoas of Aetolia (10.33, 13.218). The priest's honor and the priest's grief come in the same sentence, and both priests' sons die. See [[the-war-in-small]] and [[androktasia]].
