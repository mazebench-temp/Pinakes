summary: Book 5 heals mortals and gods. Sthenelus draws the arrow from Diomedes' shoulder, Dione wipes the ichor from Aphrodite's hand, Leto and Artemis heal Aeneas in Apollo's temple, and Pelagon pushes the spear out of Sarpedon's thigh. Paeëon, the healer of the gods, spreads pain-killing drugs on the wounds of Hades and of Ares, and heals Ares as fast as fig juice curdles milk.

**Sthenelus.** The first healing of the book is a companion's work in the field. Wounded by Pandarus, Diomedes draws back to his chariot and asks Sthenelus to “draw the bitter arrow out of my shoulder”; Sthenelus leaps down, “and standing beside him drew the swift shaft clean through and out of his shoulder; and the blood spurted up through the plaited tunic” (5.109–113; see [[sthenelus]] and [[arrows]]). There is no healer and no drug. Diomedes prays, and Athena “made his limbs light, his feet and his hands above” (5.114–122). The wound stays open: at the end of the book he is cooling it, lifting the strap of his shield to wipe away the dark blood (5.794–798; see [[shield]]).

**Dione.** Aphrodite, wounded by Diomedes, is healed by her mother's hands. Dione “with both hands wiped the ichor from her daughter's hand; the hand was healed, and the heavy pains were eased” (5.416–417). No drug is needed (see [[dione]] and [[ichor]]).

**Leto and Artemis.** Apollo takes Aeneas out of the battle to his temple on Pergamus. “There Leto and Artemis of the showering arrows healed him in the great inner shrine and gave him glory” (5.445–448; see [[temple]], [[leto]] and [[artemis]]). He comes back to his companions “alive and unhurt and with his might unimpaired”, and they have no time to ask him how (5.514–518).

**Pelagon.** Sarpedon is carried out of the fighting with Tlepolemus' spear in his thigh, and in their haste “no one noticed it or thought to draw the ash spear from his thigh, so that he could walk” (5.664–667). Under the oak of Zeus his companion Pelagon pushes it out through the wound (5.692–695), and Sarpedon faints: “his soul left him, and a mist was poured over his eyes; but he breathed again, and the breath of the North Wind blowing on him revived his spirit” (5.696–698). The removal of the spear nearly kills him, and the wind brings him back (see [[sarpedon]], [[pelagon-of-lycia]] and [[psyche]]).

**Paeëon.** The gods have a healer of their own. In Dione's story Hades goes up to Olympus with Heracles' arrow in his shoulder, “and Paeëon spread pain-killing drugs on it and healed him; for he was not made to be mortal” (5.401–402). The same two verses heal Ares when Zeus has told Paeëon to cure him (5.899–901), and a simile gives the speed of it:

{{quote:d0888b8c-9694-5632-856a-1642df4162c9}}

ὀδυνήφατα φάρμακα are drugs that “kill pain” (ὀδύνη, “pain”, and the root of φόνος, “slaying”; see [[fig-juice]] and [[paeeon]]). Then Hebe bathes Ares and dresses him in lovely clothes, and he sits down beside Zeus, “exulting in his glory” (5.905–906).

**Gods and men.** The two healings of Paeëon give their reason: “for he was not made to be mortal”. A god's wound closes at once, and a mortal's is drawn out, cooled and endured. Book 5 has three verbs for healing, ἀκέομαι (5.402, 5.448, 5.901), ἰάομαι (5.899, 5.904) and ἄλθομαι (5.417), and it uses them most for the gods, whom mortals have wounded (see [[gods-and-mortals]]).
