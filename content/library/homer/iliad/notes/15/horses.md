summary: In Book 15 the Trojan chariots reach the ships. Apollo makes a road for the horses across the ditch, and Hector, running back to the battle like a stallion that has broken its halter, urges on the horsemen; Cleitus' horses shy and rattle the empty chariot when he falls; and Ajax strides over the ships like a rider leaping from horse to horse (15.258–270, 15.352–386, 15.445–457, 15.679–684).

**Ares' horses.** When Hera tells the gods that his son Ascalaphus has been killed, Ares orders Terror and Rout to yoke his horses and puts on his armor, to go and avenge him, until Athena takes his helmet, shield and spear from him and sits him down on his throne (15.110–142; see [[ares]], [[ascalaphus]], [[terror]] and [[rout]]). The gods' chariots are yoked for war in the Iliad as men's are, and Terror and Rout, who go with Ares into battle (4.440), are his grooms here; Rout is his son (13.299).

**The road for the horses.** In Book 12 Hector's horses would not cross the ditch, and the Trojans left their chariots at its edge (12.50–59, 12.84–85). Now Apollo, having revived Hector, tells him to urge on his many horsemen “to drive their swift horses against the hollow ships; and I will go on ahead, and the road for the horses I will make smooth all the way” (15.258–261). Hector runs back like a stalled horse that breaks its halter, urging on the horsemen (ἱππῆας, 15.263–270; see [[the-stallion]]). He drives his own team with the whip, and the Trojans all shout and drive their ἐρυσάρματας ἵππους, “chariot-drawing horses”, after him, while Apollo kicks the banks of the ditch into it and makes a causeway as long as a spear's cast (15.352–360; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). They drive their horses in among the ships and fight from their chariots against the Achaeans on the decks (15.385–387). ἱππεῖς, “horsemen”, are chariot-fighters; the Iliad has no cavalry (see [[chariot]]).

**Cleitus' horses.** Teucer's arrow strikes Cleitus, the companion of Polydamas, in the neck from behind, as he holds the reins and drives the horses where the battalions are most confused, “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans”. He falls from the chariot, and “the horses shied back, rattling the empty chariot” (κείνʼ ὄχεα κροτέοντες, 15.445–453; see [[cleitus]]), as the teams of Agamemnon's dead victims rattled their empty chariots across the field in Book 11 (κείνʼ ὄχεα κροτάλιζον, 11.159–162). Polydamas is the first to reach them, gives them to Astynous, son of Protiaon (see [[astynous-son-of-protiaon]]), and tells him to keep them close, “with his eyes on him” (15.453–457; see [[polydamas]]). A team without a driver is a prize, and a charioteer's death puts it at risk, as when Hector's charioteer Eniopeus was killed in Book 8 and the horses stood without a driver (8.121–127).

**The rider.** Ajax, striding from deck to deck with his long sea pike, is likened to a man skilled in riding who has yoked four horses together and leaps from one to another as they fly along the road to a great city (15.679–684; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). It is the only rider of horseback in the Iliad's similes.

The book's other horses are in its epithets and its armor: “horse-pasturing Argos” (15.30; see [[horse-pasturing]]), “the Danaans of the swift horses” (15.320; see [[of-the-swift-horses]]), Teucer's helmet “with a horsehair crest” (15.481) and Dolops' horsehair plume, which Meges cuts off with his spear (15.535–538; see [[helmet]]).
