**Zeus' account.** Zeus ends his forecast of the war with the day on which it began:

{{quote:6cfbea40-e5c1-51f4-98ae-909f60485019}}

He tells the scene of Book 1 in its own words. Thetis “clasped my knees” (ἥψατο γούνων, 15.76), as she clasped them and held on while he sat in silence (1.512). “As I promised him at first, and nodded my head to it” (ὑπέστην … ἐπένευσα κάρητι, 15.75) answers her second plea, “Promise me truly, and nod your assent” (ὑπόσχεο καὶ κατάνευσον, 1.514), and his own nod, which could not be taken back (1.524–527; see [[neuo]]). Whom he “promised” (οἱ, 15.75) is not clear: Achilles, or Thetis herself, to whom he nodded (see the translation's note on 15.56–77). The last verse is the one with which Athena told the same scene in Book 8 (15.77 = 8.372), and Zeus has nothing of the kiss that she added (see [[zeus]] and [[athena]]).

**Whose wish.** What Thetis asked, Zeus calls “the wish of the son of Peleus” (Πηλεΐδαο … ἐέλδωρ, 15.74). ἐέλδωρ is her word, “fulfill for me this wish” (1.504), and the wish was Achilles' before it was hers: he sent her to ask that the Achaeans be penned in among their ships' sterns and slaughtered, so that Agamemnon might learn his blind folly (1.407–412; see [[achilles]]). Until it is fulfilled, Zeus will not cease from his anger or let any other god defend the Danaans (15.72–74). In the same speech he has named its price: Patroclus will be killed by Hector, and Achilles will kill Hector (15.64–68; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The prayer in full.** At the height of the battle the narrator gives the same purpose. Zeus wishes to hold out glory to Hector, “so that on the curved ships he might cast wondrous fire, / tireless fire, and fulfill the excessive prayer of Thetis / in full” (15.596–599). ἀρή is “prayer” here, and ἐξαίσιος, “excessive”, formed from ἐξ αἴσης, “beyond what is due”, is the narrator's own judgment of what she asked (see [[aisa]] and the translation's note on 15.598). Walter Leaf took it as “unreasonable”, and heard the poet speak here “explicitly as a Greek partisan” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 15.598). ἐπικρήνειε, “fulfill”, is from the verb of her plea in Book 1, κρήηνον, “fulfill” (1.504; see [[fulfill-this-wish]]), and the prayer is fulfilled “in full” (πᾶσαν) only when a ship burns. Zeus waits to see it, and then the tide will turn (15.599–602).
