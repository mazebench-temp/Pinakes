Dolops, son of Lampus, rushes at Meges and stabs the middle of his shield (15.525–529; see [[meges]] and [[dolops-son-of-lampus]]). The narrator stops for the corselet that saved him (see [[corselet]]):

{{quote:28a3931d-6ba8-5008-9828-6f5d07b0eb04}}

The gift has the shape of the guest-gifts of earlier books. Agamemnon's corselet came from Cinyras of Cyprus “to be a guest-gift” (11.19–23), and the helmet of boar's tusks went as a guest-gift from Amphidamas to Molus, and from Molus to his son Meriones (10.266–271; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). Here the giver is named as ξεῖνος, the guest-friend, and the gift is chosen for its use, “to wear into war, a defense against enemy men” (15.533). Phyleus had gone off to live on Dulichium, “angry with his father” (2.629; see [[phyleus]]); where he met Euphetes, and when, the poem does not say. The bond outlives him in its gift: “and then too it kept destruction from his son's body” (15.534). As Glaucus and Diomedes are guest-friends through their grandfathers (6.215–231; see the note on Book 6), Meges is protected by his father's friend. Strabo places this Ephyra in Elis, on the river Selleis that flows from Pholoe (*Geography* 8.3.5; see [[selleis-ephyra]] and [[euphetes]]).
