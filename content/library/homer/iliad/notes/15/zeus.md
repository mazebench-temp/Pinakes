summary: Zeus wakes on Ida beside Hera, sees the Trojans routed and Hector struck down, and takes the war back into his own hands. He threatens Hera, foretells the deaths of Sarpedon, Patroclus and Hector and the fall of Ilios, orders Poseidon out of the battle, sends Apollo with the aegis to revive Hector, and drives the Trojans on to the ships, waiting to see one burn.

**Waking.** “And Zeus awoke / on the peaks of Ida, beside Hera of the golden throne” (15.4–5; see [[ida]] and [[golden-throned]]). He sees what has happened while he slept: the Trojans in turmoil, the Argives driving them, “and among them lord Poseidon”, and Hector lying on the plain, gasping and vomiting blood, “for it was not the feeblest of the Achaeans who had hit him” (15.6–11; see [[poseidon]] and [[hector]]). “And seeing him, the father of men and gods pitied him” (15.12; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]]), and he turns on Hera, looking at her “darkly and terribly” (15.13; see [[looking-darkly]]). Her trick is κακότεχνος, “evil-working” (15.14; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).

**The punishment, remembered.** He threatens to lash her with blows (πληγῇσιν ἱμάσσω, 15.17) and reminds her of the day he hung her from the sky, with two anvils on her feet and a golden bond on her hands, while the gods chafed and could not free her, and any god he caught he hurled from the threshold until he reached the earth (15.18–24; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]] and [[hera]]). So Hephaestus fell to Lemnos when he tried to defend his mother (1.590–594; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]), and in Book 14 Sleep remembered Zeus waking that day and “flinging the gods about the house” (14.256–257; see [[sleep]]). The cause was Heracles: Hera had driven him over the sea to Cos with the North Wind's storms, and Zeus rescued him and brought him back to Argos (15.24–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]] and [[heracles]]). He ends with a taunt at the trick of Book 14: “so that you may see whether love and the bed will help you, / in which you lay with me, coming away from the gods, and deceived me” (15.32–33; see [[apate]]).

**The forecast.** Hera swears that Poseidon acts of his own will, and “the father of men and gods smiled” (15.47), the verse in which he smiled at Athena's mockery of Aphrodite (5.426). Then he sets out the course of the war. Iris is to send Poseidon home, and Apollo to revive Hector and drive the Achaeans back in flight “among the many-benched ships / of Achilles” (15.54–64). Achilles will rouse Patroclus; Hector will kill Patroclus “before Ilios”, after Patroclus has killed many, “and among them my son, brilliant Sarpedon”; Achilles will kill Hector; and from then on Zeus will bring about “a counterattack from the ships”, until the Achaeans take Ilios “through the counsels of Athena” (15.64–71; see [[plan-of-zeus]], [[sarpedon]], [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]). Until then he will not cease from his anger, nor let any other god defend the Danaans, until the wish of the son of Peleus is fulfilled, “as I promised him at first, and nodded my head to it, / on the day when the goddess Thetis clasped my knees” (15.72–77; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[thetis]]). It is the fullest forecast in the poem, and it runs past its end to the fall of the city. Not every detail comes true as he says it: the Achaeans are driven back to the ships, but the ship that Hector seizes is that of Protesilaus (15.704–706; see the translation's note on 15.56–77).

**The elder brother.** On Gargarus, “and a fragrant cloud was wreathed around him” (15.152–153; see [[gargarus]]), he sends Iris to order Poseidon out of the war, threatening to come and fight him, “for I say I am far stronger than he in strength, / and the elder by birth” (15.158–167; see [[iris]]). In Book 13 the narrator gave the same reason for Poseidon's caution: “Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355). Poseidon answers that the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot and are equal in honor, and that Zeus should keep his threats for his own children (15.185–199; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). He gives way, and Zeus tells Apollo that it was better so for both of them, “for surely others too would have heard of our fighting, / even the gods below, who are around Cronus”, and “it would not have been settled without sweat” (15.224–228; see [[cronus]] and [[titans]]).

**Apollo and the aegis.** Zeus gives Apollo the tasseled aegis to rout the Achaeans and bids him rouse Hector until they reach the ships and the Hellespont: “From then on I myself will devise both deed and word, / so that the Achaeans may draw breath again from their toil” (15.229–235; see [[apollo]], [[aegis]], [[tasseled]] and [[hellespont]]). It was Hephaestus who gave Zeus the aegis “to carry, to put men to rout” (15.309–310; see [[hephaestus]]). Hector has already sat up, “since the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus had roused him” (15.242; see [[aegis-bearing]]), and Apollo tells him that “the son of Cronus” has sent him a helper from Ida (15.254–255). Thoas sees the god behind it: “not without / loud-thundering Zeus does he stand forth as champion” (15.292–293; see [[thoas]] and [[loud-thundering]]).

**Thunder for Nestor.** At the ships Nestor prays to “Father Zeus” by the thighs that men once burned to him in Argos, praying for their return, “and you promised and nodded your assent”: “remember these things, Olympian, and ward off the pitiless day” (15.372–376; see [[nestor]], [[father-zeus]], [[olympian]] and [[prayer]]). “Zeus the counselor thundered loudly, / hearing the prayers of the old son of Neleus” (15.377–378; see [[counselor]]); but it is the Trojans who take heart at the thunder and spring on the Argives (15.379–380). The sign answers the prayer, and the battle goes the other way.

**The guard of Hector.** When Teucer draws on Hector, “he did not escape the shrewd mind of Zeus, who was guarding / Hector”, and Zeus breaks the bowstring (15.461–465; see [[teucer]] and [[bow]]). Hector reads the sign aloud. He has seen the missiles of the Achaeans' best man “foiled by Zeus” (Διόθεν, “from Zeus”, 15.489), and “Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus” (15.488–493; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). Zeus rouses the Trojans again when the Achaeans fence the ships with bronze (15.567).

**Waiting for the fire.** The Trojans rush on the ships like lions, “and fulfilled the commands of Zeus” (15.593), who rouses them and bewitches the hearts of the Argives:

{{quote:596b54c2-d0b5-54ee-a9e1-2fe42639f605}}

“For his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector” is the verse that told why Zeus would not listen to Asius (12.174). ἐξαίσιον ἀρήν is Thetis' prayer of Book 1, “excessive”, beyond what is fitting (15.598; 1.503–510; see the translation's note on 15.598). The burning of a ship is the term Zeus has set himself: once it is reached, he will turn the battle (see [[plan-of-zeus]]). He stands behind Hector, “for from the upper air he himself was his defender, / Zeus, who, though Hector was alone among many men, / honored him and gave him glory. Short-lived he was to be” (15.610–612), and the Achaeans flee “before Hector and father Zeus” (15.637).

**His hand.** When Hector swoops on a dark-prowed ship like an eagle (see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]] and [[dark-prowed]]), “Zeus pushed him on from behind / with his very great hand, and urged on the army with him” (15.694–695). Elsewhere the hand of Zeus is held over a people to shelter it, as Achilles says “far-seeing Zeus has held / his hand over” Ilios (9.419–420; compare 4.249); here it drives a man forward. Hector takes hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire: “Now Zeus has given us a day worth all the rest”; if the elders held him back before, it was because “far-seeing Zeus was harming our minds then”, and “now he himself is urging us on and commanding us” (15.719–725; see [[far-seeing]]). The book ends before the fire takes.
