---
summary: Hector falls on the Achaeans “like a deadly lion coming upon cattle” in a great marsh.
---

“But he, like a deadly lion coming upon cattle / that graze in the low wet ground of a great marsh, / countless, and among them a herdsman who does not yet well know / how to fight a wild beast over the slaughter of a cow with curving horns; / he walks always with the first and the last of the cattle, / keeping in step with them, but the lion leaps into the middle / and devours a cow, and they all flee in fear: so then the Achaeans / fled in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus” (15.630–637; [[hector]], [[lion]], [[cattle]], [[with-curving-horns]], [[father-zeus]], [[wondrous]]). The lion's deadly mind is the will to kill that the simile sets against the herdsman's inexperience. In the next verses Hector kills Periphetes of Mycenae (15.638–652; [[periphetes-of-mycenae]]). The word's first use in the Iliad was of the water snake that bit Philoctetes (2.723; [[water-snake]]).
