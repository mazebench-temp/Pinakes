summary: The deception ends when Zeus wakes on Ida and sees what Hera's trick has done. He calls it an evil-working trick, threatens her with blows and reminds her of the anvils; Hera swears that Poseidon acted of his own will, and Zeus smiles and sets his plan going again (15.4–77).

  - 15.34-52 | Hera's oath and Zeus' smile
---

**The waking.** “And Zeus awoke” (ἔγρετο δὲ Ζεύς) comes in the middle of a verse, as the routed Trojans halt beside their chariots (15.4). On the peaks of Ida, beside Hera, Zeus springs up and sees all that she has done: the Trojans in turmoil, the Argives driving them, Poseidon among the Argives, and Hector lying on the plain, gasping and vomiting blood, “for it was not the feeblest of the Achaeans who had hit him” (15.4–11; see [[hector]] and [[ajax]]). He pities Hector (15.12). In Sleep's story he had woken once before to find what Hera had done, and raged through the house (14.256–258; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]).

**The trick named.** His first words name the deception: “Truly, Hera, there is no dealing with you: your evil-working trick / has stopped brilliant Hector from battle and put the army to rout” (κακότεχνος … δόλος, 15.14–15; see [[hera]]). He calls it her “grievous mischief” (κακορραφίης ἀλεγεινῆς, 15.16), and he ends with the noun and the verb of deception itself:

{{quote:bb40dd28-5ee3-56dd-897a-9ad365e5854a}}

ἀπατάων, “deceits”, and ἀπάτησας, “deceived”, are the words of the episode's traditional name, Διὸς ἀπάτη, “the Deception of Zeus” (see [[apate]] and [[title-of-book-14]]). φιλότης τε καὶ εὐνή, “love and the bed”, turns her own words on her. Her false story to Aphrodite and to Zeus was of Oceanus and Tethys, who had long kept away “from the bed and from love” (εὐνῆς καὶ φιλότητος, 14.207 = 14.306), and Zeus slept “overcome by sleep and love” (14.353; see [[philotes]]).

**The threat.** Between the two, Zeus threatens her. He does not know whether she will not again be the first to reap the fruit of her mischief, when he lashes her with blows (15.16–17), and he reminds her how he once hung her from the sky with two anvils on her feet, for her storm against Heracles (15.18–30; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). He says nothing of Sleep, who lulled him then and now (14.252–253, 14.354–360; see [[sleep]]).

**The oath.** Hera shudders, and swears by Earth and Heaven, by the water of Styx, by his head and by “the wedded bed of us two”, that it is not through her will that Poseidon harms the Trojans (15.34–44; see [[hera]] and [[oath]]). The oath is true to the letter, for it was Sleep who told Poseidon to help the Danaans “while he still sleeps” (14.354–360), and it says nothing of what she did herself (see the translation's note on 15.41–46). Zeus smiles (μείδησεν, 15.47), takes her at her word, “if you are speaking truly and exactly” (15.53), and sends her to fetch Iris and Apollo. The plan that the deception interrupted goes on as before (15.49–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). On Olympus Hera laughs “with her lips”, but her brow is not warmed (15.100–103).
