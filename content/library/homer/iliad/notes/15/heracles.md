**Zeus' grief.** In Book 14 Hera told Sleep that Zeus had been enraged for “Heracles, his own son” (14.266). Zeus' own words bear her out. Even after he had hung Hera from the sky, the pain would not let go of his heart, ἀζηχὴς ὀδύνη Ἡρακλῆος θείοιο, “the ceaseless pain for divine Heracles” (15.24–25; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]] and [[divine]]). The genitive is objective: the pain is for Heracles, whom Zeus himself rescued from Cos and brought back to Argos (15.29–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]] and [[cos]]). Zeus does not call him his son here.

**Many labors.** The account ends with the shape of Heracles' life: καὶ πολλά περ ἀθλήσαντα, “though he had suffered many labors” (15.30). ἀθλέω is to toil at an ἆθλος, a contest or labor, and the noun is the word for the labors that Eurystheus set him (ἀέθλων, 8.363, 19.133; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]). Poseidon uses the verb of himself and Apollo, who toiled to build the wall for Laomedon (ἀθλήσαντε, 7.453).

**Eurystheus' messenger.** Near the end of the book Hector kills Periphetes of Mycenae, “the dear son of Copreus, who used to go for lord Eurystheus / with messages to mighty Heracles” (15.638–640; see [[periphetes-of-mycenae]], [[copreus]], [[eurystheus]] and [[might-of-heracles]]). The labors are seen here from Mycenae: Eurystheus sent his orders by a herald, and οἴχνεσκε, “used to go”, makes the errands many. The narrator judges the father by the son: “From him, a far worse father, was born a better son” (15.641).
