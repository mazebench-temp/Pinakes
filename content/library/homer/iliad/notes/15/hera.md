summary: Zeus wakes beside Hera, sees through her trick and threatens her with the punishment of the past. She swears that Poseidon acts of his own will, goes back to Olympus with his orders, and there, laughing with her lips alone, tells the gods that they are fools to rage against him and gives Ares the news of his son's death; then she sends Apollo and Iris to Ida.

**Found out.** Zeus wakes “beside Hera of the golden throne” (15.5; see [[golden-throned]]), sees Poseidon among the Achaeans and Hector struck down, and looks at her darkly: “Truly, Hera, there is no dealing with you: your evil-working trick / has stopped brilliant Hector from battle and put the army to rout” (15.14–15; see [[zeus]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). He threatens to lash her with blows and reminds her of the day he hung her from the sky with anvils on her feet, after she drove Heracles to Cos (15.16–30; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]] and [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). The love and the bed in which she deceived him will not help her now (15.31–33). “So he spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera shuddered” (15.34; see [[ox-eyed]]).

**The oath.** She swears by Earth and Heaven, by the water of Styx, “the greatest / and most dread oath for the blessed gods”, by Zeus' own head and by their marriage bed (15.36–40; see [[oath]], [[styx]], [[earth]] and [[sky]]). What she swears is true to the letter:

{{quote:0b156239-6533-548e-a516-caac2d8cc576}}

Poseidon came to the battle unasked, pitying the Achaeans and indignant with Zeus (13.15–16; see [[poseidon]]), and it was Sleep, not Hera, who told him to help them while Zeus slept (14.354–360; see [[sleep]]). The oath says nothing of what she did herself, and her “perhaps” (που, 15.43) keeps her clear (see the translation's note on 15.41–46). She ends by calling Zeus κελαινεφής, “lord of the dark clouds”, and promising to advise Poseidon to follow his lead (15.45–46; see [[lord-of-dark-clouds]]). Zeus smiles and takes her at her word: if she were to sit among the immortals “thinking as I do”, Poseidon would soon turn his mind to follow theirs (15.47–52). Let her go to the gods and call Iris and Apollo (15.53–55).

**Among the gods.** “The white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey” (15.78; see [[white-armed]]), and she flies from the mountains of Ida to Olympus as swiftly as the thought of a traveler who wishes himself in one place and another (15.79–83; see [[the-travelers-thought]]). The gods spring up and greet her with their cups, and she takes the cup of Themis, who asks whether the son of Cronus has frightened her (15.84–91; see [[themis-the-goddess]]). Hera will not tell: “you know yourself / what kind of heart he has, overbearing and harsh” (15.93–94; see [[overbearing]]), and the others will hear at the feast what evil deeds Zeus is making known (15.95–99).

{{quote:6f38de51-c8d9-5349-8dc5-2ca368e4e404}}

**Fools.** She speaks among them all: “Fools, we who rage against Zeus in our folly!” (15.104). No word and no force will stop him; he sits apart, does not care, and says that he is far the best of the gods in power and strength, “So accept whatever evil he sends to each of you” (15.105–109; see [[olympians]]). She counts herself among the fools (μενεαίνομεν, “we rage”), but she has news to give: a bane has been made already for Ares, “for his son has perished in battle, the dearest of men to him, / Ascalaphus” (15.110–112; see [[ares]] and [[ascalaphus]]). Ares leaps up to avenge him, and only Athena stops him from bringing Zeus' anger down on all of them (15.113–142; see [[athena]]). The poem does not say whether Hera meant to provoke him. Athena speaks of her as the one who “has just now come from Olympian Zeus” (15.130–131).

**The messengers.** Hera calls Apollo out of the house, and Iris, “who is the messenger among the immortal gods”, and passes on Zeus' order in three verses: go to Ida, look upon the face of Zeus, “do whatever he urges and bids you” (15.143–148; see [[apollo]] and [[iris]]). Then she sits down on her throne (15.149–150). On Gargarus Zeus is not angry with the two, “since they had quickly obeyed the words of his dear wife” (15.155–156; see [[gargarus]]). Hera does not appear again in the book; Poseidon names her among the gods who want Ilios sacked, with Athena, Hermes and Hephaestus (15.213–214; see [[hermes]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**Her titles.** Book 15 gives her χρυσόθρονος, “of the golden throne” (15.5), βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “ox-eyed queen Hera” (15.34, 15.49), θεὰ λευκώλενος Ἥρη, “the white-armed goddess Hera” (15.78, 15.92, 15.130), and πότνια Ἥρη, “queen Hera” (15.83, 15.100, 15.149; see [[queenly]]). Zeus calls her φίλη ἄλοχος, “his dear wife” (15.156).
