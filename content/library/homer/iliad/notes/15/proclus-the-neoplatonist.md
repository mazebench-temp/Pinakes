---
summary: Proclus reads the gods of Book 15 as figures of the divine order. Hera hung with two anvils at her feet is the air, with the two heaviest elements below it; the three sons of Cronus are three forms of Zeus, among whom the whole is divided; their lots are eternal, as Poseidon's “to dwell in forever” shows; and Poseidon, though he claims to be Zeus' equal, calls him father.
---

- **Hera and the anvils.** Plato's Socrates suggests that the namegiver called the air, ἀήρ, Hera (*Cratylus* 404c; see [[plato]]). Proclus, commenting on the passage, makes the air a symbol of the soul, as fire is an image of mind, water of the nature that nourishes the world, and earth of body, and goes on: “that is why Homer, hinting at this, makes Hera hang, and under her feet two anvils; for the air has been allotted the two heaviest elements below itself” (*On the Cratylus* 170, on 15.18–21). The same note finds Hera as the air in the deep mist that she spreads before the fleeing Trojans (21.6–7) and in the sun she sends down unwilling to the streams of Ocean, which Proclus takes for the thickest mist, raised by Hera; and since the voice is struck air, he gives her the voice that she lends to Achilles' horse (18.239–240, 19.407). See [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]] and [[hera]].
- **Three forms of Zeus.** In the notes on the *Cratylus* Zeus, the head of the demiurgic order, is twofold: one transcendent, ranked with Cronus, and one counted among the sons of Cronus, of whom the Homeric Poseidon says, “For we are three brothers, born of Cronus” (15.187). This Zeus governs a third of the whole, as in Poseidon's “All things have been divided in three” (15.189). He is called by one name, Zeus; the second brother by two, “Zeus of the sea” and Poseidon; the third by three, “Zeus of the underworld”, Pluto and Hades. The first preserves, fashions and gives life to the highest things, the second to the second, and the third to the last, which is why he carries off Kore, to give soul with her to the extremities of the whole (*On the Cratylus* 148). The title “Zeus of the underworld” is Homer's own for Hades (9.457; see [[zeus-of-the-underworld]] and [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]).
- **The father.** Homer, following Orpheus, Proclus says, celebrates Zeus as the father of gods and men alike, and the gods of the world turn toward him; even the most universal of the other gods, who seem in some way his equals because they proceed from the same causes, call him father. Hera pleads with him as one of the same rank, for Cronus bore her the eldest (4.58–59), and Poseidon claims the same: “For we are three brothers, born of Cronus, whom Rhea bore, / Zeus and I, and the third is Hades” (15.187–188). Yet both call him father (*On the Cratylus* 99). In the Iliad they do: Hera addresses him as “Father Zeus” (5.757), and so does Poseidon (7.446).
- **The lots of the gods.** Commenting on the *Timaeus*, where Athena is said to have received Athens and Sais by lot (*Timaeus* 23d), Proclus asks what it is for gods to receive by lot, and how they are said to divide the whole. If there were only one father of the whole, one providence and one law, there would be no need of lots or of a divine distribution; but after the one comes a triad, and after the single providence the manifold. So, after the division of the whole into two, heaven and becoming, “the triad divided the whole, of which Homer's Poseidon speaks”, and Proclus quotes the three lots of the sea, the sky and the misty darkness (*On the Timaeus* I 136 Diehl, on 15.190–192). The lots are eternal: “the ancient theology shows the everlasting existence of the lots, as when Homer says, ‘I got by lot the gray sea, to dwell in forever’; for ‘forever’ signifies eternity” (I 141 Diehl).
- **The wide sky.** When Plato calls the whole world both heaven and cosmos, Proclus notes that the ancients used these names in several senses: some called only the region below the moon cosmos and what is above it heaven, while others gave the name heaven even to the uttermost parts of becoming, as in “Zeus got the wide sky, in the upper air and the clouds” (*On the Timaeus* I 272 Diehl, on 15.192).
