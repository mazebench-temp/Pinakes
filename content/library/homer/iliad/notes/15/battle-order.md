**The best in front.** When the Achaeans see Hector restored, Thoas proposes the order of a defense: “Let us order the multitude to go back to the ships; / but let us ourselves, all of us who claim to be the best in the army, / stand firm” (15.295–297; see [[thoas]] and [[commoners]]). The leaders around Ajax and Idomeneus, Teucer, Meriones and Meges “called the best men and set the battle in order / against Hector and the Trojans; but behind them / the multitude went back to the ships of the Achaeans” (15.303–305; see [[aristos]]). The champions, πρόμαχοι, fight in front, and the book follows several of them in and out: Deiochus is struck from behind as he flees among them (15.341–342), Polydamas goes back to mingle with them (15.457) and is saved there by Apollo (15.521–522), and Antilochus leaps out from them to throw his spear (15.573; see [[antilochus]]).

**In battalions.** The two armies meet “in a body” (ἀολλέες, 15.306, 15.312). Hector goes along the ranks of men (15.279) and calls to the Trojans “along the ranks” as he drives at the ditch (15.353), and over the path that Apollo bridges “they poured forward in battalions” (φαλαγγηδόν, 15.360; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). Teucer's victim Cleitus drives his horses “where by far the most battalions were thrown into confusion” (15.448; see [[cleitus]]).

**The ranks hold.** At the ships the order holds for a time. The Achaeans cannot push the Trojans away, “nor could the Trojans ever break the battalions of the Danaans” (15.408–409; see [[the-chalk-line]]). Hector, raging, “wanted to break the ranks of men, testing them, / where he saw the greatest throng and the best armor; / but even so he could not break them … / for they held, fitted together in close order like a tower” (ἴσχον γὰρ πυργηδὸν ἀρηρότες, 15.615–618), like a rock by the sea that withstands the winds and the waves (15.618–622; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]). πυργηδόν, “like a tower”, is the word for the formation that checked Hector in Book 13 (13.152), and for the hunters who stand against a boar or a lion in a simile of Book 12 (12.43; see [[hector]]). Only when Hector falls on them like a wave on a ship does the line give way, and the Achaeans are driven back to their huts (15.623–658).
