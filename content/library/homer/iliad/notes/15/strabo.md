---
summary: Strabo reads Book 15 as a geographer. Otus of Cyllene, a leader of the Epeians, shows that Homer's Cyllene is the port of Elis, not the Arcadian mountain, and the Epeians who came from Elis to live under Meges explain why Otus is the companion of Phyleus' son; Meges' corselet from Ephyra on the Selleis places a fourth Ephyra in Elis; and Melanippus' herds at Percote show that Priam's rule reached along the coast of the Hellespont.
---

- **Otus of Cyllene.** Describing the coast of Elis, Strabo comes after Cape Araxus to Cyllene, the port of the Eleans: “Homer too mentions this Cyllene, saying ‘Otus of Cyllene, a leader of the Epeians’; for he would not have made a man from the Arcadian mountain the leader of the Epeians.” In his own day, he adds, it was a village of moderate size, with an ivory Asclepius by Colotes that was a marvel to see (*Geography* 8.3.4). His verse joins the beginning of 15.518 to the end of 15.519. See [[otus-of-cyllene]], [[cyllene-elis]], [[cyllene]] and [[epeians]].
- **The Epeians of Dulichium.** Against those who made Cephallenia the same as Dulichium, Strabo argues from the poet. Dulichium and the other Echinades were under Meges, and those who lived there were Epeians who had come from Elis, “which is why he calls Otus of Cyllene ‘companion of the son of Phyleus, and a leader of the great-hearted Epeians’”, while the Cephallenians were Odysseus' men (*Geography* 10.2.14, quoting 15.519 and 2.631). See [[meges]], [[phyleus]], [[dulichium]] and [[cephallenians]].
- **Meges' corselet.** Between Cape Chelonatas and Cyllene, Strabo says, the Peneius flows into the sea, and so does the river the poet calls the Selleis, which flows from Pholoe. On it stands Ephyra, a fourth city of that name beside the Thesprotian, the Thessalian and Corinth, on the road to Lasion, either the same as Boenoa, that is Oenoe, or near it, a hundred and twenty stades from the city of the Eleans. From it, he holds, came the mother of Tlepolemus, of whom the Catalogue says “he had brought her from Ephyra, from the river Selleis” (2.659), since Heracles' campaigns were in those parts and no river Selleis flows by the other Ephyras; from it came Meges' corselet: “Phyleus had once / brought it from Ephyra, from the river Selleis” (15.530–531); and from it the man-killing drugs that Odysseus went to Ephyra to seek, and that the suitors fear Telemachus will fetch from there (*Odyssey* 1.259–262, 2.328–329). Nestor's tale of the war with the Epeians, he adds, makes Augeas' eldest daughter a sorceress, “who knew all the drugs that the wide earth nourishes” (11.741; *Geography* 8.3.5). See [[ephyra]], [[selleis-ephyra]], [[corselet]], [[tlepolemus]], [[augeas]] and [[agamede]].
- **Melanippus at Percote.** Those who have studied the matter, Strabo says, infer from the poet that the whole coast was subject to the Trojans, divided into nine dynasties but under Priam at the time of the war. Going through them, he comes to the country between the Aesepus and Abydos. Asius ruled about Abydos, but at Abydos a son of Priam, Democoon, kept horses, plainly his father's (4.499–500), and at Percote the son of Hicetaon herded cattle, and these too not another man's. He quotes the verses in which Hector calls on his kinsmen, “and first he rebuked the son of Hicetaon, / mighty Melanippus. For a time he grazed shambling cattle / in Percote” (15.546–548). So Percote too was Trojan land, and Priam ruled it all (*Geography* 13.1.7). See [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]], [[percote]], [[hicetaon]], [[democoon]], [[asius]] and [[priam]].
- **Ida.** Strabo's account of Ida, its headlands Lectum and Zeleia and its summit Gargarus, quotes Homer's verse on the arrival at Ida as “They came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts”, word for word 15.151, where Iris and Apollo come to Zeus on Gargarus, and goes on with 14.284, where Hera and Sleep leave the sea at Lectum (*Geography* 13.1.5): see the Book 14 note, [[lectum]], [[gargarus]] and [[ida]].
