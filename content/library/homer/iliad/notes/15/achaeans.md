---
summary: The book turns the Achaeans from pursuers into the pursued. Zeus wakes to see them driving the Trojans, and forecasts both their flight to the ships of Achilles and their final victory; Poseidon leaves them, Apollo's aegis routs them, and at the end of the day they fight at the sterns of their ships, thinking they will not escape (15.6–8, 15.56–77, 15.218–235, 15.326, 15.699–702).
---

**Driving, then driven.** The book opens on the Trojans fleeing across the ditch, and Zeus, waking on Ida, sees “the Trojans and the Achaeans, / the one side in turmoil, and the others driving them in confusion from behind” (15.6–7), with Poseidon among them (see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). His plan reverses it. Apollo is to turn the Achaeans back, “rousing a craven panic among them, / so that in their flight they tumble among the many-benched ships / of Achilles, son of Peleus” (15.62–64), and Apollo is to rouse Hector “until the Achaeans / in their flight reach the ships and the Hellespont” (15.232–233; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The forecast reaches past the poem: after Hector's death Zeus will bring about a counterattack from the ships “until the Achaeans / take steep Ilios through the counsels of Athena” (15.70–71; see [[the-fall-of-troy]]). Poseidon leaves the Achaean army, “and the Achaean heroes missed him” (15.218–219; see [[poseidon]] and [[heros]]).

**Routed.** When Apollo shakes the aegis in their faces, “the Achaeans fled in rout, their courage gone” (15.326; see [[aegis]], [[apollo]] and [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]), and the Trojans kill eight of them in a list, before they plunge across the ditch and go inside the wall “under compulsion” (15.328–345; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). At the ships they pray, Nestor above all, that Zeus “not let the Achaeans be so beaten down by the Trojans” (15.367–376; see [[nestor]]). When Hector falls on them like a wave on a ship, “the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (15.629; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]), and like cattle before a lion “the Achaeans / fled in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus” (15.636–637; see [[hector]], [[zeus]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]).

**At the sterns.** The last movement of the book is a battle at the ships, and the narrator gives both sides' thoughts in one sentence: “the Achaeans / thought they would not escape from the evil, but would perish, / and the heart in the breast of each of the Trojans hoped / to set fire to the ships and kill the Achaean heroes” (15.699–702; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). Around the ship of Protesilaus “the Achaeans and Trojans / were cutting one another down hand to hand” (15.707–708; see [[protesilaus]]). Ajax alone will not stand back with “the other sons of the Achaeans” (15.675; see [[ajax]] and [[sons-of-the-achaeans]]). Hector, speaking to the Trojans, imagines them gone: a man who dies for his country leaves his family safe, “if the Achaeans / are gone with their ships to their dear native land” (15.498–499).
