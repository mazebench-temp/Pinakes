---
summary: Zeus sends Apollo down from Ida with the aegis to revive Hector and rout the Achaeans. He breathes might into Hector, goes before the Trojans wrapped in cloud, shakes the aegis in the Danaans' faces, bridges the ditch and throws down the Achaean wall as a boy scatters a sandcastle.

**Sent for.** Zeus has Hera call “Apollo famed for his bow” (κλυτότοξον, 15.55; see [[famed-for-his-bow]]) so that he may rouse Hector, breathe might into him, make him forget his pains, and turn the Achaeans back with a craven panic (15.59–62; see [[zeus]] and [[hector]]). Hera calls him out of the house on Olympus with Iris, and the two fly to Ida (15.143–156; see [[hera]] and [[iris]]). Zeus' ban of Book 8 has kept the gods out of the battle (8.10–12), and Apollo now comes back to it at Zeus' own command.

**To Hector.** When Poseidon has gone into the sea, Zeus speaks to Apollo as φίλε Φοῖβε, “dear Phoebus”, and ἑκατηβόλε, “you who shoot from afar” (15.221, 15.231; see [[far-shooter]]): let him take the tasseled aegis and rout the Achaean heroes with it (see [[tasseled]]), and let Hector be his own care, until the Achaeans reach the ships and the Hellespont (15.220–235; see [[aegis]] and [[hellespont]]). “Apollo did not fail to heed his father” and goes down from the mountains of Ida like a hawk, the swift dove-killer (15.236–238; see [[like-a-hawk]] and [[hawk]]). He finds Hector sitting up, no longer lying down, and asks why he sits apart (15.239–245). Hector, feebly, does not know him: “Who are you, good sir, among the gods, who question me face to face?” (15.247). The god names himself:

{{quote:39998c85-88d8-5de2-812d-8fae98ea0d5a}}

χρυσάορος, “of the golden sword”, is given to Apollo only here and at 5.509 (see [[of-the-golden-sword]]). He promises to go ahead of the horses and make their road smooth, “and turn back the Achaean heroes”, and he breathes great might into the shepherd of the people (15.258–262), who runs to the battle like a stallion that has broken its halter, “once he had heard the voice of the god” (15.263–270; see [[the-stallion]]).

**The aegis.** Apollo goes before Hector, “his shoulders wrapped in cloud, and he held the furious aegis, / dreadful, shaggy all around, conspicuous, which the smith / Hephaestus gave to Zeus to carry, to put men to rout” (15.307–310; see [[aegis]], [[shaggy-all-around]] and [[hephaestus]]). As long as he holds it still, the missiles of both sides strike home; when he looks the Danaans in the face and shakes it, and shouts aloud, he bewitches their hearts and they forget their furious courage (15.318–322). They scatter like cattle or sheep before two beasts in the dead of night, “for Apollo / sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (15.323–327; see [[two-beasts-in-the-night]], [[rout]] and [[kydos]]). Agamemnon foretold that Zeus himself would “shake his dark aegis over them all” in resentment at the Trojans' deceit (4.166–168); now it is shaken against the Achaeans.

**The wall.** As the Trojans drive their horses at the ditch, Apollo kicks down its banks “easily” and throws them into the middle, bridging a path as long as a spear cast; then, “holding the aegis, highly prized”, as Athena held it among the Achaeans in Book 2 (2.447; see [[precious]]), he throws down the wall itself (15.355–361; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The narrator turns to address him:

{{quote:2accf9fe-8f2e-5f0e-b472-b39cc2fef944}}

ἤϊε Φοῖβε, “archer Phoebus”, is a title of uncertain meaning, used again at 20.152 (see the translation's note on 15.365, [[ieios]] and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* uses both of this book's rare titles in its story of his birth, ἤιε Φοῖβε and χρυσάορα (*Hymn to Apollo* 120, 123). The wall that the Achaeans built with long labor (7.436–441) is thrown down in a moment; after the war Apollo will wash its remains away with the rivers of Ida, with Poseidon (12.17–33).

**In the rest of the book.** Ajax reminds Teucer of “the bow that Phoebus Apollo gave you” (15.440–441; see [[teucer]] and [[bow]]), but it is Zeus who breaks its string (15.461–465). Apollo keeps the son of Panthous from death when Meges springs at him (15.520–522; see [[polydamas]]).
