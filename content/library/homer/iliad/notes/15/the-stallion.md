{{quote:91afe33b-be29-5d3d-b7c7-8b9f0c78a181}}

The verses of the simile are those of Book 6 word for word (15.263–268 = 6.506–511); the frame is new. In Book 6 they followed Paris out of his chamber, where he had sat handling his armor while others fought, and he came down from Pergamus laughing aloud (6.503–514). Here they follow a god's healing. Ajax's stone had struck Hector down, and Zeus, waking on Ida, saw him lying on the plain, “held by a grievous gasping, senseless at heart, vomiting blood” (15.9–11). Apollo finds him sitting up and gathering his spirit, tells him who he is, and “breathed great might into the shepherd of the people” (15.239–262; see [[apollo]] and [[hector]]).

The details that judged Paris in Book 6 now fit a man restored. The tether that the horse breaks, δεσμὸν ἀπορρήξας, is Hector's wound and his helplessness. The horse holds its head high, ὑψοῦ δὲ κάρη ἔχει, and the man who lay on the ground stands up again. The horse is εἰωθὼς λούεσθαι ἐϋρρεῖος ποταμοῖο, “used to bathing in the fair-flowing river”, and Hector was carried in Book 14 to the ford of the fair-flowing river, πόρον … ἐϋρρεῖος ποταμοῖο, the Xanthus, where his companions laid him down and poured water over him (14.433–436; see [[scamander]]). The horse runs μετά τʼ ἤθεα καὶ νομὸν ἵππων, “to the haunts and pasture of the horses”, and Hector runs to the horsemen: Apollo had told him to urge on his many horsemen to drive their swift horses against the hollow ships (15.258–259).

The tenor gives the change: ὣς Ἕκτωρ λαιψηρὰ πόδας καὶ γούνατʼ ἐνώμα / ὀτρύνων ἱππῆας, ἐπεὶ θεοῦ ἔκλυεν αὐδήν, “so Hector plied his nimble feet and knees, urging on the horsemen, once he had heard the voice of the god” (15.269–270). Paris' run was his own; Hector's is the god's doing, and the verse says so. Hector's horsemen are the chariot-fighters (ἱππῆες), and the road for their horses is the one Apollo will make smooth across the ditch (15.260–261, 15.352–360; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]).

The Achaeans see him and lose heart, as hunters do when a lion stands in their path (15.271–280; see [[the-hunters-and-the-lion]]). The verse of Hector's running, λαιψηρὰ πόδας καὶ γούνατʼ ἐνώμα, returns in Book 22 for Achilles, who runs toward Troy like a prize-winning horse, to the race around the walls in which Hector is the prize (22.21–24, 22.159–166).
