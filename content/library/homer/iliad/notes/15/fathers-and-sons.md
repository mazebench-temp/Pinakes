summary: A god grieves for his son, and a son outdoes his father. Ares, told that Ascalaphus has fallen, would defy Zeus to avenge him; Zeus foretells the death of his own son Sarpedon; and Periphetes of Mycenae, the son of Copreus, Eurystheus' messenger, is “a better son” of “a far worse father”.
passages:
  - 15.638-652 | Periphetes, son of Copreus
---

**Divine fathers.** Ares learns from Hera that his son Ascalaphus, “the dearest of men to him”, has perished in battle (15.110–112), and strikes his thighs and calls for his horses; Athena tells him that better men than his son have died and will die (15.113–141; see [[ares]], [[ascalaphus]] and [[poine]]). Zeus, laying out his plan, names among Patroclus' victims “my son, brilliant Sarpedon” (15.67; see [[sarpedon]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The fathers among the gods can lose their sons as mortal fathers do.

**A better son.** The book's one long obituary is of a son who surpassed his father. Periphetes of Mycenae was “the dear son of Copreus, who used to go for lord Eurystheus / with messages to mighty Heracles” (15.639–640; see [[copreus]] and [[eurystheus]]), and “From him, a far worse father, was born a better son / in every kind of prowess, both in running and in fighting, / and in mind he was among the first of the Mycenaeans” (15.641–643; see [[periphetes-of-mycenae]] and [[arete]]). It is the rare case. Sthenelus claimed that the sons had surpassed their fathers at Thebes (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]), and in the Odyssey Athena, as Mentor, tells Telemachus that few sons are like their fathers, most are worse, and few are better (*Odyssey* 2.276–277). Periphetes is one of the few, and his merit gives Hector the glory: he trips on his shield and is killed (15.644–652).
