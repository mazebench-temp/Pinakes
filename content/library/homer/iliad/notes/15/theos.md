---
summary: Book 15 begins among the gods, gathered and troubled in the house of Zeus, who claims to be far the best of them in power and strength. On the battlefield men name the gods as the cause of what they see: “one of the gods has rescued and saved / Hector”, “a god has confounded them”, and the ships “came here against the will of the gods”.
passages:
  - 15.84-112 | The gods in the house of Zeus
---

**The gods together.** When Zeus hung Hera from the sky, “the gods chafed throughout high Olympus” and could not free her (15.21–22; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). Now Hera comes from Ida to find “the immortal gods, in the house of Zeus” gathered at a feast, and “throughout the house of Zeus the gods were troubled” at what she brings (15.85, 15.101; see [[feast]]). Her speech names their position: “Fools, we who rage against Zeus in our folly!”, for he says “that among the immortal gods / in power and in strength he is far the best” (15.104–108; see [[nepios]] and [[kratos]]). Athena, “terribly afraid for all the gods”, stops Ares before Zeus' anger falls on them all (15.123; see [[ares]]). Zeus sends Poseidon “among the tribes of the gods, or into the shining sea” (15.161, 15.177; see [[phylon]]), and is glad that their quarrel ended without a fight that “even the gods below, who are around Cronus” would have heard of (15.224–225; see [[cronus]] and [[titans]]).

**Gods named by men.** Hector, revived, does not know which god stands before him: “Who are you, good sir, among the gods, who question me face to face?” (15.247; see [[divine-epiphany]]). Thoas sees the revival and draws the conclusion of every man in the poem who sees the impossible: “But once again one of the gods has rescued and saved / Hector” (15.290–291; see [[thoas]]). When Teucer's string breaks, Ajax says that “a god has confounded them, begrudging the Danaans” (15.473; see [[daimon]]). The Achaeans at the ships pray “to all the gods” (15.368; see [[prayer]]). Hector, at the stern of Protesilaus' ship, says that the ships came “against the will of the gods / and brought many banes upon us” (15.720–721); the poem has shown Hera, Athena and Poseidon on the Achaeans' side from the start, and Hector's claim says more of his confidence than of the gods (see [[hector]]).
