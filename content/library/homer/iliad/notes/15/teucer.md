**Called to the bow.** Teucer is one of the leaders who stand with Ajax, Idomeneus, Meriones and Meges when Thoas has the best men meet Hector (15.301–303; see [[thoas]] and [[battle-order]]). At the ships, when Hector's spear has killed Lycophron, Ajax turns to him: “Where now are your arrows / of swift death, and the bow that Phoebus Apollo gave you?” (15.440–441; see [[ajax]] and [[lycophron]]). ἰοὶ ὠκύμοροι, “arrows of swift death”, is said again of Odysseus' arrows in the hall (see the translation's note on 15.441). The bow was Apollo's gift, as Pandarus' was (2.827; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). “So he spoke, and the other understood, and ran and stood beside him, / holding in his hand the back-bent bow and the quiver / that holds the arrows” (15.442–444; see [[back-bent]], [[quiver]], [[arrow-holding]] and [[bow]]).

**Cleitus.** His first arrow strikes “Cleitus, the splendid son of Peisenor, / the companion of Polydamas”, as he drove his horses where the battalions were thickest, “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans” (15.445–449; see [[cleitus]], [[peisenor]] and [[polydamas]]). “For the arrow of many groans fell on his neck from behind” (15.451): πολύστονος, said elsewhere of Strife and of griefs, is given to an arrow only here (11.73, 1.445). The horses shy back, “rattling the empty chariot”, and Polydamas catches them and hands them to Astynous, son of Protiaon (15.452–457; see [[astynous-son-of-protiaon]]). So it went in Book 8, when the arrows Teucer aimed at Hector killed Gorgythion and then Hector's charioteer Archeptolemus (8.300–315; see [[gorgythion]] and [[archeptolemus]]).

**The string.**

{{quote:9a6fec0d-6e56-5d12-bb98-4e43a3d5fac4}}

The narrator says what would have happened, and why it did not (see [[would-have-had-not]]): Zeus, “who was guarding / Hector”, took the triumph away from the archer, εὖχος ἀπηύρα (see [[euchomai]] and [[zeus]]). Τελαμώνιον, “son of Telamon”, is the title that usually names Ajax (see [[telamonian]], [[telamon]] and [[nothos]]). The last words, “and the bow fell from his hand”, are those of Book 8, when Hector's stone broke his string at the collarbone (τόξον δέ οἱ ἔκπεσε χειρός, 8.329 = 15.465). Twice the best archer of the Achaeans draws on Hector, and twice his string breaks, the first time by Hector's hand, the second by Zeus'.

**A god.** Teucer does not know which god it was:

{{quote:b5870d81-6760-5a71-b4a2-3d6de5313e78}}

νεόστροφον, “newly twisted”, and πρώϊον, “this morning”, occur only here; the narrator called the string ἐϋστρεφέα, “well-twisted” (15.463). The archer's care, which the poem lets him tell, shows that no fault of his broke it. Ajax too speaks only of “a god” (15.473), while Hector, from the other side, has seen “the missiles of their best man foiled by Zeus” (15.488–489; see [[hector]], [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]] and [[divine-intervention]]). Hector calls Teucer ἀνδρὸς ἀριστῆος, “their best man”. In Book 16 the narrator gives Teucer's words to Ajax's own understanding, when Hector lops the head off his spear and he knows that Zeus is cutting short the plans of their battle (16.119–121).

**The spear.** Ajax tells him to let “your bow and your thick-flying arrows / lie” and to fight with a long spear and a shield (15.472–477); βιὸς καὶ ταρφέες ἰοί, “your bow … nor your thick-flying arrows”, was Diomedes' scorn for Paris' weapons (11.387; see [[paris]]). “So he spoke, and the other put the bow away in the huts, / and around his shoulders he put a shield of four layers, / and on his mighty head he set a well-made helmet / with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it; / and he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze” (15.478–482; see [[arming-scene]], [[shield]], [[helmet]] and [[spear]]). The verses of the helmet are those of Paris, Agamemnon and Patroclus arming (3.336–337, 11.41–42, 16.137–138), and the whole passage returns in the Odyssey when Odysseus' arrows are spent in the hall, and he too leans his bow against the doorpost and takes up shield, helmet and spears (*Odyssey* 22.119–125). The archer becomes a spearman, as he fought in Book 13, when he killed Imbrius with his long spear (13.170–181; see [[imbrius]]). “And went his way, and running very swiftly stood beside Ajax” (15.483), and the poem does not name him again in the book.

**Apollo's bow.** Three times a god turns the archery of the man who shoots with Apollo's gift: Apollo turned his arrow aside from Hector in Book 8 (8.311), Zeus breaks his string here, and at the funeral games Apollo begrudges him the bird, because he made no vow (μέγηρε, 23.865; see [[apollo]]). Ajax uses the same word here: the god has confounded the arrows “begrudging the Danaans” (μεγήρας, 15.473).
