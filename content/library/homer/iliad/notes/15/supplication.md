**Thetis at Zeus' knees.** Zeus tells Hera that he will not cease from his anger until Achilles' wish is fulfilled, “as I promised him at first, and nodded my head to it, / on the day when the goddess Thetis clasped my knees, / begging me to honor Achilles, sacker of cities” (15.75–77; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[neuo]]). The supplication of Book 1 still binds him, for the promise he nodded to cannot be taken back (1.500–527). The narrator returns to it at the height of Hector's attack, when Zeus rouses him so as to fulfil “the excessive prayer of Thetis” (15.598; see [[prayer]]).

**Nestor's supplication.** When the Achaeans have been driven back to their huts, Nestor “begged each man by his parents, imploring him” (λίσσεθʼ ὑπὲρ τοκέων γουνούμενος, 15.660; see [[nestor]]). The verbs are those of supplication, and the construction is the suppliant's “by”, ὑπέρ with the genitive, with which Hector, dying, will beg Achilles “by your life and your knees and your parents” not to give his body to the dogs (22.338). Nestor clasps no knees, and he asks for no mercy. He begs the men to remember their children, wives, possessions and parents, the living and the dead, and ends: “for their sake, though they are not here, I implore you / to stand firm, and not to turn to rout” (τῶν ὕπερ … γουνάζομαι οὐ παρεόντων, 15.665–666; see [[aidos]] and [[exhortation]]). In the Odyssey the soul of Elpenor begs Odysseus for burial in the same words, “by those you left behind, who are not here”, his wife, his father and Telemachus (*Odyssey* 11.66–68). An old man supplicates the young to save themselves.
