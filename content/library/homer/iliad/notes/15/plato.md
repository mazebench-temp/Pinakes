---
summary: Plato opens the myth of judgment in the Gorgias with Poseidon's division of the world among the sons of Cronus, “as Homer says”, but without lots and with Pluto for Hades; the Critias has the gods divide the earth by lot without strife. Among the stories banned from the city in the Republic is the fall of Hephaestus “when he was about to defend his mother being beaten”, and Book 15 tells of a punishment of Hera in which Zeus hurls from the threshold the gods who would free her.
---

- **The division of the rule.** Socrates introduces the myth that closes the *Gorgias* as a true account, though Callicles will take it for a tale. “As Homer says, Zeus and Poseidon and Pluto divided the rule, when they took it over from their father” (*Gorgias* 523a). Then comes the law about men that held under Cronus and holds still among the gods: the just go at death to the Isles of the Blessed, the unjust to the prison of punishment, Tartarus. Under Cronus, and still early in Zeus' reign, living judges judged the living on the day they were to die, and judged badly, deceived by fine bodies, birth and wealth; Pluto and the overseers from the Isles of the Blessed came to Zeus to complain that men were reaching both places undeserving. So Zeus ordered that men be judged dead and stripped, by judges who were dead too, and made three of his sons judges, Minos, Rhadamanthus and Aeacus, in the meadow where the road divides, one way to the Isles of the Blessed and the other to Tartarus (523a–524a; see [[minos]], [[rhadamanthus]], [[aeacus]] and [[tartarus]]). Homer's Poseidon told the division to make himself the equal of Zeus and to keep Zeus to his third share (15.185–195; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Plato says nothing of lots, calls the third brother Pluto, and has the lord of the dead bring his complaint to Zeus, who orders the judgment of the dead in his brother's realm. Homer is called in again before the myth ends: he too, Socrates says, bears witness that the incurable are kings and rulers, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Tityus, punished forever in Hades, and Minos judges with a golden scepter, “as Homer's Odysseus says he saw him” (525d–e, 526c–d; *Odyssey* 11.569; see [[plato-thersites]]).
- **Lots without strife.** In the *Critias* the gods once divided the whole earth among themselves region by region, “not by strife”, for it would not be reasonable for gods not to know what befits each of them, or knowing it to try to win by quarrels what belongs to others; “receiving by the lots of justice what was dear to them, they settled their lands” (*Critias* 109b), and Athena and Hephaestus received Attica as their single share (109c). In Book 15 the lot is the ground of a quarrel, which Poseidon gives up only at Iris' warning (15.200–217; see [[lots]]).
- **Hephaestus and his mother.** Among the stories that must not be told in the city, “whether composed with hidden meanings or without”, Socrates names “Hera's bonds from her son, and Hephaestus' hurling by his father when he was about to defend his mother being beaten, and all the battles of the gods that Homer composed” (*Republic* 2.378d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]). The fall is Hephaestus' own story in Book 1: once, when he was eager to defend her, Zeus seized him by the foot and hurled him from the threshold (1.586–594; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). Book 15 puts the beating and the hurling side by side. Zeus, waking, threatens to lash Hera with blows (15.17), and reminds her how, when he hung her from the sky, he seized any god who tried to free her and hurled him from the threshold, in the words of Hephaestus' fall (15.18–24; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). Homer does not say that the two falls are one.
- **Hera and the air.** Socrates' derivation of Hera's name from ἀήρ, “air” (*Cratylus* 404c; see the Book 14 note), is the starting point of Proclus' reading of her hanging: [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]].
