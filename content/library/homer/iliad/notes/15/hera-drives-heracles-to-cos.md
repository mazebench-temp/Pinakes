**Two tellings.** Sleep told the story to refuse Hera; Zeus tells it to threaten her, when he wakes on Ida and sees what her new trick has done (15.14–33; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). One verse is the same in both, “and then you carried him off to well-peopled Cos” (15.28 = 14.255; see [[cos]]). Around it each speaker tells what he knows. Sleep gave the occasion, the voyage home from the sack of Troy (14.250–251; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]), and his own part: he lulled the mind of Zeus while Hera roused the winds (14.252–254). Zeus gives Hera's means. She won over the storm winds “with the help of the North Wind” (ξὺν Βορέῃ ἀνέμῳ, 15.26; see [[boreas]]), and sent Heracles over the barren sea, “devising evil” (κακὰ μητιόωσα, 15.27). And Zeus tells the end, which Sleep did not know or did not say: he rescued his son and brought him back to Argos (15.29–30; see [[heracles]] and [[argos]]), and he punished Hera by hanging her from the sky (15.18–24; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). In Sleep's version Zeus' rage fell on the gods in his house and above all on Sleep, who escaped only by fleeing to Night (14.256–261); in Zeus' version it falls on Hera and on the gods who tried to free her, and neither Sleep nor Night is named.

**Hera's forecast.** Hera told Sleep that Zeus would not help the Trojans “as much / as he was enraged for Heracles, his own son” (14.265–266; see [[hera]]). The event bears her out only in part. Zeus wakes in anger, pities Hector, threatens Hera with blows and reminds her of the anvils (15.12–30). But he does not punish her now: her oath makes him smile, and he sends her to fetch Iris and Apollo (15.34–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).
