summary: Zeus wakes to find Poseidon leading the Achaeans and sends Iris to order him out of the war as the stronger and the elder. Poseidon answers that the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot and are equal in honor; then he yields, with a threat of anger beyond healing if Zeus spares Ilios, and goes down into the sea.

**Seen from Ida.** When Zeus wakes he sees the Argives driving the Trojans, “and among them lord Poseidon” (15.8). Hera swears that it is not through her will that Poseidon “harms the Trojans and Hector and helps the others”; perhaps his own heart urges him on, “and he saw the Achaeans hard pressed at the ships and pitied them” (15.41–44; see [[hera]] and [[oath]]). Zeus answers that if Hera thought as he did, Poseidon would quickly turn his mind to follow theirs, and sends Iris to bid him “cease from war and go to his own house” (15.51–58).

**The order.** On Gargarus Zeus gives Iris the message: let Poseidon go among the tribes of the gods or into the shining sea, or else consider whether he dares to await Zeus when he comes, “for I say I am far stronger than he in strength, / and the elder by birth” (15.158–167; see [[zeus]], [[iris]] and [[shining-sea]]). Iris repeats it to “the famous shaker of the earth” almost word for word, turning the threat into the third person (15.173–183; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]]). In Book 13 the narrator gave the same reason for Poseidon's secret help: the brothers were of one race, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.354–355).

**Equal in honor.** “Deeply troubled”, Poseidon answers as he answered Hera in Book 8, when he would not fight against Zeus, “since he is far the stronger” (15.184; 8.208–211). Now he claims equality. Brave as Zeus is, “he has spoken arrogantly, / if he will hold me back by force against my will, when I am his equal in honor” (ὁμότιμον, 15.185–186; see [[time]]), for the three brothers drew lots:

{{quote:bacffc5f-a805-5f8a-818e-1919b31d69f4}}

The world was divided in three, and the earth and Olympus are still common to all (see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]], [[lots]], [[hades]] and [[cronus]]). “So I will not live by the mind of Zeus”: let him stay at ease in his third share and keep his threats for his own daughters and sons, who must obey him even under compulsion (15.194–199). It is the only telling of the division in Homer (see the translation's note on 15.185–195).

**The elder.** Iris asks whether she is really to carry back so harsh a word: “The hearts of good men can be turned. / You know how the Erinyes always follow the elder” (15.201–204; see [[the-hearts-of-good-men-can-be-turned]], [[the-erinyes-follow-the-elder]] and [[erinyes]]). Poseidon gives way at once: “this word you have spoken is fair and right; / it is a good thing, too, when a messenger knows what is due” (15.206–207; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]). He still feels the insult, when one “who has an equal lot, ordained to the same portion as himself” is rebuked with angry words (15.208–210; see [[moira]] and [[aisa]]), “But now, for all my indignation, I will give way” (15.211; see [[nemesis]]).

**The threat.** He yields with a warning. If Zeus spares steep Ilios against the will of Poseidon himself and of Athena, Hera, Hermes and Hephaestus, “let him know this, that between us two there will be incurable anger” (15.212–217; see [[troy]]). The five gods he names are the ones who go to the ships when Zeus lets the gods join the battle in Book 20 (20.33–37; see [[athena]], [[hermes]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**Into the sea.** “So saying, the shaker of the earth left the Achaean army, / and went and plunged into the sea; and the Achaean heroes missed him” (15.218–219; see [[sea]]). Zeus tells Apollo that it was better so for both of them: otherwise even the gods below would have heard of their fighting, and “it would not have been settled without sweat” (15.222–228; see [[cronus]]). Poseidon's help, which began at the start of Book 13 (13.10–38; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]), is over, and the way is clear for Apollo and Hector.

**His titles.** Book 15 gives him most of his formulas: Ποσειδάων ἐνοσίχθων, “Poseidon the earth-shaker” (15.41, 15.205; see [[earth-shaker]]), κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος, “the famous shaker of the earth” (15.173, 15.184), γαιήοχε κυανοχαῖτα, “dark-haired earth-holder”, twice in Iris' mouth (15.174, 15.201; see [[earth-holder]] and [[dark-haired]]), γαιήοχος ἐννοσίγαιος, “the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth”, in Zeus' (15.222), and Ποσειδάωνα ἄνακτα, “lord Poseidon” (15.8, 15.57, 15.158).
