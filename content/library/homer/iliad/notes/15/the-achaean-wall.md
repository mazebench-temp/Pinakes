The Trojans pour over it to the ships, and Ajax tells the Achaeans that no stronger wall is left behind them (15.1–2, 15.343–366, 15.384, 15.395, 15.735–736).

**Across the ditch, both ways.** The book begins after the rout of Book 14, “when they had crossed the stakes and the ditch / in their flight”, the Trojans driven back over it (15.1–2). Apollo's aegis turns the battle, and the ditch is crossed the other way: “the Achaeans meanwhile, / plunging into the ditch they had dug and the stakes, / fled this way and that, and went inside the wall under compulsion” (15.343–345; see [[achaeans]]). ἐνιπλήξαντες, “plunging into”, is the word Polydamas used at the wall when he warned the Trojans of being driven back into the ditch (ἐνιπλήξωμεν, 12.72; see [[polydamas]] and the translation's note on 12.67–74). His fear is the Achaeans' fate today.

**Thrown down.** Hector orders the Trojans to make for the ships and leave the spoils, and they drive their chariots on behind him (15.346–355; see [[hector]]):

{{quote:9997ff75-c535-5181-b2ce-315c778c6204}}

κάπετος, “trench”, is another name for the ditch (see the translation's note on κάπετος and τάφρος). The banks the Achaeans dug, which Book 12 described as too steep for horses to cross (12.52–59), go into the middle at a kick, and the god bridges a road for the chariots, “as far as the cast of a spear / goes, when a man throws it to test his strength” (see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). The wall had been built to stand “as long as Hector was alive and Achilles kept up his wrath” (12.10–12); now it falls “very easily”, as a boy beside the sea, with his feet and hands, scatters the sand he has heaped up to play with. The comparison makes the god's work child's play, and the Achaeans' wall a heap of sand (see [[the-boy-and-the-sand]] and [[apollo]]). “So you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery / of the Argives” (15.365–366; see [[argives]]): κάματος, the labor of the building, is what the god undoes. Apollo is one of the two gods who will sweep the wall away after the war with the rivers and the rain (12.17–33), and the builder, with Poseidon, of the wall of Troy that Poseidon feared the Achaean wall would make men forget (7.451–453; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The poem has called the wall thrown down once already, in Nestor's sight at the start of Book 14 (14.13–15, 14.55–56); here the god's own hand brings it down.

**Over the wall.** The Trojans go down over the wall “with a great shout”, like a great wave over the side of a ship, and fight at the sterns (15.381–389; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). Patroclus, in Eurypylus' hut, sees them “rushing at the wall” and leaves for Achilles (15.395; see [[patroclus]]).

**No stronger wall.** At the end of the book Ajax, on the deck of the last ship, takes the wall away in words: “Do we think there are helpers behind us, / or some stronger wall, that could ward off ruin from men? / There is no city near, fitted with towers” (15.735–737; see [[ajax]]). The army that built a wall to hold back the Trojans has nothing left between itself and the sea but the ships.
