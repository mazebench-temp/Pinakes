summary: Found lying on the plain, gasping and vomiting blood, Hector is revived by Apollo at the command of Zeus and runs back to the battle like a stalled horse. With Apollo and the aegis before him he drives the Achaeans over the ditch and the wall to their ships. Zeus breaks Teucer's bowstring to save him and gives him glory because his life is to be short; raging like Ares, he kills Periphetes of Mycenae, lays hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire.

**Lying on the plain.** When Zeus wakes on Ida, he sees Hector lying among his companions, “held by a grievous gasping, senseless at heart, / vomiting blood” (15.10–11), as Book 14 left him at the ford of the Xanthus (14.436–439). Zeus pities him and turns on Hera: “your evil-working trick / has stopped brilliant Hector from battle and put the army to rout” (15.14–15; see [[zeus]], [[hera]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Then he foretells Hector's part in what is to come. Apollo is to urge him into battle, “and breathe might into him again, and make him forget the pains / that now wear him down in his mind” (15.60–61); of Patroclus, once he has killed Sarpedon, “shining Hector will kill him with the spear / before Ilios” (15.65–66); “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68; see [[plan-of-zeus]], [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]).

**Revived.** Zeus sends Apollo with the aegis: “let shining Hector be your own care, you who shoot from afar; / rouse great might in him for so long, until the Achaeans / in their flight reach the ships and the Hellespont” (15.231–233; see [[apollo]], [[aegis]] and [[hellespont]]). In Book 11 Zeus promised him power to kill until he reached the ships and the sun went down (11.191–194). Apollo finds him “sitting up, no longer lying down” (15.240), gathering his spirit and knowing his companions again, “since the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus had roused him” (15.242). The verse that brings the god to him, “He found the son of wise-hearted Priam, brilliant Hector” (15.239), brought Iris to him with the promise of Book 11, when he stood among his horses and chariot (11.197–198). He does not know the god:

{{quote:84f0645f-7a37-5546-9911-e0f5539513ed}}

τίς δὲ σύ ἐσσι φέριστε is how Diomedes began to Glaucus, “Who are you, good sir, among mortal men?” (6.123). ὀλιγοδρανέων, “feebly” (15.246), comes back only twice: for the dying Patroclus answering Hector, and, in this same verse, for the dying Hector answering Achilles (16.843, 22.337). Apollo names himself, “Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who before now too / have protected you” (15.256–257). He set Hector on his feet when Ajax's stone laid him on his back in Book 7 (7.272), and in Book 11 Diomedes cried, “now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you” (11.363). “So saying, he breathed great might into the shepherd of the people” (15.262; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]): with the same verse Apollo will send Aeneas against Achilles (20.110).

**Back in the battle.** He runs to the battle like a stalled horse that breaks its halter, in the six verses that brought Paris down from Pergamus in Book 6 (15.263–268 = 6.506–511; see [[the-stallion]] and [[paris]]): “so Hector plied his nimble feet and knees, / urging on the horsemen, once he had heard the voice of the god” (15.269–270). The Danaans, who were pursuing like hunters after a stag, fall back as if a lion had appeared in their path (see [[the-hunters-and-the-lion]]): “when they saw Hector going along the ranks of men, / they were afraid, and the hearts of all sank to their feet” (15.279–280). Thoas marvels “how he has risen up once more, escaping the fates” (15.287), when they had hoped he was dead at Ajax's hands, and sees a god at work: “for not without / loud-thundering Zeus does he stand forth as champion, so eager” (15.292–293; see [[thoas]]).

**To the ships.** He leads the Trojans “with long strides” (15.307), the stride of Ajax coming out to meet him in Book 7 (7.213), and Apollo goes before him wrapped in cloud, holding the aegis. When the god shakes it, the Achaeans forget their courage and scatter like cattle before two beasts at night (see [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]), “for Apollo / sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (15.326–327). Hector kills Arcesilaus, a leader of the Boeotians, and Stichius, the companion of Menestheus (15.329–331; see [[arcesilaus]] and [[stichius]]). When the Achaeans flee inside the wall, he calls out:

{{quote:f830afc8-ccf0-5100-ab91-b14326ddc8db}}

The threat to the laggard, ὃν δʼ ἂν ἐγὼν ἀπάνευθε … νοήσω (15.348), takes up Agamemnon's threat before the first battle, “But any man I see wishing to hang back from the fighting” (2.391). The penalty is the one he asked the Achaeans to spare him when he challenged them in Book 7: if he fell, his body was to be given back, so that “the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (7.80). Before Achilles he will ask it again in the same verses, and be refused (22.342–354; see [[cremation]] and [[dogs]]). He drives his horses on “with the whip from the shoulder” (15.352), and the Trojans follow with “a wondrous din” (15.355) across the causeway that Apollo makes over the ditch and through the wall the god throws down (15.355–366; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]).

**One ship.** At the ships the battle is stretched as evenly as a carpenter's line makes a timber straight (15.410–413; see [[the-chalk-line]]), “and Hector went straight at glorious Ajax” (15.415). “The two were toiling over one ship” (15.416), and neither can drive the other off, “since a god had brought him there” (15.418). When Ajax kills Caletor, son of Clytius, as he brings fire to the ship, Hector sees “his cousin” fall (15.419–423; see [[caletor]]), calls on the Trojans and Lycians to save the body, and throws at Ajax; the spear misses and kills Ajax's attendant, Lycophron of Cythera (15.424–435; see [[lycophron]]). His call is the verse of his exhortations in Books 8, 11 and 13, “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand” (15.425, again at 15.486; see [[exhortation]]). ἀνεψιός, “cousin”: Caletor's father Clytius is a brother of Priam, as are Lampus and Hicetaon, whose sons Hector also loses in this book (20.237–238; see [[clytius]], [[dolops-son-of-lampus]] and [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]]).

**Zeus guards him.** Teucer kills Polydamas' charioteer Cleitus (15.445–457; see [[cleitus]]) and takes another arrow “for Hector armed in bronze” (15.458). He would have stopped him from battle, “if he had hit him while he was the best in the fighting” (15.460). “But he did not escape the shrewd mind of Zeus, who was guarding / Hector” (15.461–462): Zeus snaps the well-twisted string as Teucer draws, and the bow falls from his hand (15.463–465; see [[teucer]] and [[bow]]). In Book 8 Apollo made one of Teucer's arrows at Hector swerve, and Hector's own stone broke Teucer's bowstring, “and the bow fell from his hand” (8.311, 8.328–329). Now Zeus does it for him.

**The help of Zeus.** Hector sees that Teucer's missiles have been foiled, and tells his men: “Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus” (15.490; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]), who now “diminishes the might of the Argives, and helps us” (15.493). Then:

{{quote:e9eaf871-821c-5591-9639-fce169a74de1}}

ἀμυνομένῳ περὶ πάτρης, “fighting in defense of his native land” (15.496), takes up his answer to Polydamas, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]). Begging Achilles for the body, Priam will say that Achilles killed Hector as he fought in defense of his native land, ἀμυνόμενον περὶ πάτρης (24.500). In Book 6 Hector foresaw Andromache led away in tears by an Achaean when the city fell (6.454–463; see [[hector-and-andromache]]); here a fallen man's wife and children are safe if the Achaeans go home (see [[the-city-and-the-household]]). Ajax answers from the other side: “Truly it is not to a dance that he bids them come, but to fight” (15.508; see [[ajax]] and [[not-to-a-dance-but-to-fight]]).

**His kinsmen.** He kills Schedius, son of Perimedes, leader of the Phocians (15.515–516; see [[schedius-son-of-perimedes]]). When Menelaus kills Dolops, son of Lampus, Hector calls on all his kinsmen and first rebukes Melanippus, son of Hicetaon: “Are we to hold back like this, Melanippus? Is your dear heart / not moved at all, now that your cousin is killed?” (15.553–554). They must fight on “until either we kill them, or from top to bottom / they take steep Ilios and kill its citizens” (15.557–558), the words of his cry to Paris in Book 13, “Now from top to bottom all of steep Ilios / has perished” (13.772–773). Melanippus follows him and is killed by Antilochus; Hector comes running through the combat, and Antilochus flees before him like a beast that has done some harm (15.568–591; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]], [[antilochus]] and [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]).

**The purpose of Zeus.** The narrator gives the reason for his success: “For his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector, / son of Priam, so that on the curved ships he might cast wondrous fire” (15.596–597), and so fulfill the prayer of Thetis; Zeus waits only to see a ship burn before he turns the battle (15.598–602; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). The first verse said the same at the wall (12.174). Then Hector himself:

{{quote:aec12477-8d84-5d26-a2da-263559f77b07}}

μαίνετο, “He raged” (15.605), like spear-brandishing Ares or a deadly fire in the mountains (see [[like-huge-ares]] and [[forest-fire]]). Hera said that he “rages past all bearing” (8.355), Odysseus that he “rages terribly, trusting in Zeus” (9.238), and in Book 21 the narrator remembers this as the day when shining Hector raged, ὅτε μαίνετο φαίδιμος Ἕκτωρ (21.5). The foam at his mouth, ἀφλοισμός, is named only here; his eyes blazed with fire when he leapt through the gate (12.466; see [[eyes-like-fire]]). Zeus honors him because he is μινυνθάδιος, “Short-lived” (15.612), the word with which Achilles began his complaint to Thetis, “since you bore me to be short-lived” (1.352; see [[short-lived]]). Athena is already rousing his “fated day” (15.613): in Book 22 his day of fate sinks in the scales of Zeus, Apollo leaves him, and Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, brings him to stand against Achilles (22.209–213, 22.226–231, 22.294–299; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[athena]]). In Book 17, when he has put on Achilles' armor, Zeus will give him great might for the same reason, since he will not come home from the battle (17.198–208).

**Against the ranks.** He tries to break the ranks where the throng is greatest, but the Danaans hold, “fitted together in close order like a tower, like a rock” (15.618; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]), the close order that in Book 13 he was sure would give way before his spear (13.152–153). He falls on them like a wave on a ship and like a lion among cattle (15.623–636; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]), and they flee “before Hector and father Zeus” (15.637), “but he killed only Periphetes of Mycenae” (15.638), the better son of a worse father, who trips on the rim of his own shield. “He it was who then put the higher glory into Hector's hands” (15.644): the higher glory that Zeus gave him at the gate (12.437) and that, he has just said, shows whom Zeus helps (15.491). Hector “was quick to see it” and kills him close to his companions, who fear him too much to help (15.649–652; see [[periphetes-of-mycenae]]).

**The stern.** When Athena takes the mist from the Achaeans' eyes, they see “Hector, good at the war cry, and his companions” (15.671). He does not stay in the throng but swoops on a dark-prowed ship like an eagle on a flock of birds, “and Zeus pushed him on from behind / with his very great hand” (15.694–695; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]). Then “Hector took hold of the stern of a seafaring ship” (15.704), the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and did not take him home (15.705–706; see [[protesilaus]] and [[stern]]). The ships of Protesilaus and Ajax were drawn up where Hector first broke in at the wall (13.679–684).

{{quote:9c91b84e-8066-5f3b-b172-bfecd44f8f77}}

Odysseus told Achilles that Hector vowed “to hack the high sternposts from the ships, / and to burn the ships themselves” (9.241–242). Now he holds the stern ornament, the ἄφλαστον (only here), and calls for the fire he promised in Book 8 (8.180–182). He blames “the cowardice of the elders” (15.721) for holding him back before, which the poem has not shown; Achilles told the embassy that while he fought, Hector would not rouse the battle far from the wall (9.352–354; see [[trojan-elders]]). Ajax falls back to the seven-foot bench and keeps off with his spear every Trojan who brings fire “to do a favor to Hector who urged them on” (15.744; see [[ajax]]), and twelve die in front of the ships.

**Afterward.** In Book 16 Hector hews off the head of Ajax's spear with his sword, Ajax gives way, and the Trojans throw tireless fire on the ship, which burns from the stern (16.114–124). Patroclus drives them from the half-burned ship (16.293–294); Hector, seeing that the victory has turned, is carried from the field by his horses (16.362–368), and before Ilios he kills Patroclus, as Zeus foretold (16.818–857; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

His epithets in the book are δῖος, “brilliant” (15.15, 15.239, 15.583, 15.652), φαίδιμος, “shining” (15.65, 15.231), κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (15.246, 15.504), χαλκοκορυστής, “armed in bronze” (15.221, 15.458), μεγάθυμος, “great-hearted” (15.440), βοὴν ἀγαθός, “good at the war cry” (15.671), and ποιμὴν λαῶν, “shepherd of the people” (15.262); he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (15.597, 15.604; see [[brilliant]], [[phaidimos]], [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]], [[armed-in-bronze]], [[great-hearted]], [[good-at-the-war-cry]], [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
