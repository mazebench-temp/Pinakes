summary: The book opens with the Trojans routed beyond the ditch and turns when Apollo, holding the aegis, leads them back behind Hector. They kill eight Achaeans as the battle scatters, cross the ditch and the wall, and fight from their chariots against the Achaeans on the decks; like lions that eat raw flesh they fulfill the commands of Zeus, until Hector holds the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire, and Ajax kills every Trojan who brings it.

**Routed.** The book begins where Book 14 ended. The Trojans have crossed the stakes and the ditch in flight, and “they halted beside their chariots and stood their ground, / pale with fear, in their rout” (15.3–4). Zeus wakes and sees “the one side in turmoil, and the others driving them in confusion from behind, / the Argives” (15.7–8; see [[zeus]]), the verse with which Nestor saw the Achaeans driven by the Trojans at the start of Book 14 (14.14–15). He sends Apollo to turn them back (15.59–62, 15.220–235; see [[apollo]]).

**Behind Apollo.** “And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them” (15.306), with Apollo going before them holding the aegis. When he shakes it, the Achaeans scatter like cattle before two wild beasts at night, for he “sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (15.327; see [[aegis]] and [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]). As the battle scatters, six Trojan leaders kill eight Achaeans: Hector, Aeneas, Polydamas, Polites, Agenor and Paris (15.328–342; see [[hector]], [[aeneas]], [[polydamas]], [[polites]], [[agenor]] and [[paris]]), as eight Trojans fell to the Achaean leaders at the end of Book 14 (14.511–519). Hector forbids them to stop for the spoils (15.346–351), and they drive their chariots “with a wondrous din” over the causeway that Apollo makes across the ditch: “There they poured forward in battalions” (15.355–360). The god throws down the wall as a boy scatters his sand (15.361–366; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]).

**Over the wall.** The Achaeans halt beside the ships in the verse with which the Trojans halted beside their chariots at the start of the book (15.367 ≈ 15.3; as at 8.345). Nestor prays, and Zeus thunders in answer, but it is the Trojans whom the thunder rouses: “And the Trojans, when they heard the thunder of aegis-bearing Zeus, / sprang on the Argives more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (15.379–380; see [[nestor]]). They go over the wall like a great wave over the side of a ship and fight at the sterns, they from their chariots, the Achaeans from the decks with their long spears for fighting at sea (15.381–389; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]] and [[stern]]).

**An even fight.** They cannot break through: “nor could the Trojans ever break the battalions of the Danaans / and force their way among the huts and the ships” (15.408–409), and the battle is stretched as evenly as a carpenter's line makes a timber straight (15.410–413; see [[the-chalk-line]]). Twice Hector calls on the “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand”, to save the body of Caletor and, when Zeus has broken Teucer's bowstring, to fight at the ships for their native land (15.424–428, 15.485–499; see [[lycians]], [[dardanians]] and [[exhortation]]).

**Like lions.** “And the Trojans, like lions that eat raw flesh, / rushed upon the ships, and fulfilled the commands of Zeus” (15.592–593), who roused them and bewitched the Argives, to give Hector the glory of the fire (15.594–604; see [[eating-raw-flesh]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The half-verse is said of the Achaeans standing around Diomedes in Book 5 and of Ajax and Hector falling on each other in Book 7 (5.782, 7.256; see [[lion]]).

**At the ship.** The thoughts of both armies are told: the Achaeans think they will die, “and the heart in the breast of each of the Trojans hoped / to set fire to the ships and kill the Achaean heroes” (15.701–702), the hope with which they followed Hector at the start of Book 13 and his vow of Book 8 (13.41–42, 8.181–182). They fight hand to hand at the ship of Protesilaus with axes and swords, “and the black earth ran with blood” (15.715), and when Hector seizes the stern and calls for fire, “they sprang more fiercely on the Argives” (15.726; see [[protesilaus]]). Falling back to the seven-foot bench, Ajax stabs every Trojan who brings fire, and kills twelve (15.727–746; see [[ajax]]). His last words tell the difference between the armies: the Achaeans have no city near to defend them, “but here in the plain of the close-armored Trojans / we sit with our backs to the sea, far from our native land” (15.739–740; see [[close-armored]] and [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]).
