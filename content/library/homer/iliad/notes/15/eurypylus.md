τόφρʼ ὅ γʼ ἐνὶ κλισίῃ ἀγαπήνορος Εὐρυπύλοιο, “so long sat in the hut of manly Eurypylus” (15.392; see [[patroclus]], [[hut]] and [[manly]]). Eurypylus was shot in the thigh by Paris as he stripped Apisaon, and Patroclus, meeting him limping from the battle, cut out the arrow and laid a bitter root on the wound (11.575–584, 11.806–848; see [[healing]]). Book 12 began with Patroclus still tending him (12.1–2), and through the whole fight at the wall Patroclus has sat with him, cheering him with talk and spreading healing drugs on the wound (15.390–394). When he sees the Trojans storm the wall, he leaves him: “Eurypylus, I can no longer, much as you need me, / stay here beside you; for a great quarrel has arisen. / But let your attendant cheer you” (15.399–401). The attendant is not named. Patroclus' words to Achilles follow at the start of Book 16, and he names Eurypylus there among the wounded, in the verse Nestor used in Book 11 (16.27 = 11.662; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Eurypylus does not appear again in this book.
