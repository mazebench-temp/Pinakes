---
summary: Dogs in Book 15 are hunting dogs in the similes, turned back by a lion and darting at a wounded fawn (15.272, 15.579), and a dog killed by a wild beast (15.587); and they are the eaters of the dead in Hector's threat against any Trojan who hangs back from the ships (15.348–351).
---

**In the similes.** The dogs of the hunt are twice the Achaeans. In the first simile they chase a stag or a wild goat with the country men until a bearded lion, Hector restored, appears in their path and turns them all back (15.271–276; see [[the-hunters-and-the-lion]]); the verse ἐσσεύαντο κύνες τε καὶ ἀνέρες ἀγροιῶται is the one in which dogs and country men chased a lion from the farmyard in Book 11 (11.549). Then the dog is a single man. Antilochus springs on the dying Melanippus “like a dog that darts at a wounded fawn”, which a hunter has hit as it leapt from its lair (15.579–581; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]), and when Hector comes at him he flees like a wild beast that has killed a dog or a herdsman beside the cattle (15.586–588; see [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]). κύων ὥς, “like a dog”, occurs only here in Homer.

**The eaters of the dead.** Hector, sending the Trojans on against the ships, threatens his own men: whomever he sees away from the ships, on the other side, he will kill on the spot, and his kinsmen and kinswomen will not give him his portion of fire, “but dogs shall tear him in front of our city” (15.346–351; see [[hector]] and [[cremation]]). The threat that the enemy's dead will be left to the dogs is turned on the Trojans' own: a man who will not fight is to be treated as an enemy. Dogs and birds at Troy are the fate of the unburied from the proem on (1.4–5), and Hector will mean to give Patroclus' body to the Trojan dogs (17.125–127).
