---
summary: Deer in Book 15 are the quarry of two hunting similes: an antlered stag that dogs and country men chase until a rock or a forest saves it and a lion turns them back (15.271–276), and a wounded fawn on which a dog darts, as Antilochus darts on the dying Melanippus (15.579–581).
grc: [νεβρῷ]
---

**The stag that escapes.** ἢ ἔλαφον κεραὸν ἢ ἄγριον αἶγα, “an antlered stag or a wild goat” (15.271), is the pair of beasts that the hungry lion of Book 3 finds and devours (3.24; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[antlered]]). Here the deer is the quarry of a hunt that fails: a lofty rock or a shady forest shelters it, “and it is not their lot to catch it”, and a lion appears and turns the hunters back (15.271–276; see [[the-hunters-and-the-lion]]). The Danaans had been pursuing the routed Trojans, and the quarry that escapes them is answered in Thoas' words: a god has rescued Hector once again (15.286–293).

**The wounded fawn.** νεβρός, the fawn, is the helpless young of the deer, and in Book 15 it is already down. Antilochus springs on Melanippus “like a dog that darts at a wounded fawn, which, as it leapt from its lair, a hunter has hit with his shot” (15.579–581; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). The fawn here is masculine, as the words that refer to it show (βλημένῳ, τόν). In Book 22 a dog rouses a fawn from its lair and tracks it down, as Achilles pursues Hector (22.189–193).
