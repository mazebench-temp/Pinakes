summary: Book 15 is the book of shame at the ships. Ajax opens one rally with the cry “Shame, Argives!” and in another repeats Agamemnon's lines on feeling shame before one another; the narrator says that shame and fear held the Argives together by their huts; and Nestor asks each man to feel shame before other men and to remember his children, his wife and his parents. Among the gods, Athena tells Ares that his mind is gone, and his sense of shame.

**A god without shame.** When Hera tells the gods that Ares' son Ascalaphus has fallen, Ares strikes his thighs and calls for his horses, ready to go down to the ships against Zeus' command (15.110–120; see [[ares]] and [[ascalaphus]]). Athena takes the helmet from his head and the spear from his hand: “Madman, crazed in your mind, you are lost! Truly it is for nothing / that you have ears to hear; your mind is gone, and your sense of shame” (νόος δʼ ἀπόλωλε καὶ αἰδώς, 15.128–129; see [[athena]] and [[noos]]). The αἰδώς Ares has lost is the regard owed to the stronger, here to Zeus, who would come to Olympus and seize “the one to blame and the one who is not” (15.137; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). He had asked the gods not to be indignant with him (μὴ … νεμεσήσετʼ, 15.115; see [[nemesis]]): shame is what the man feels who fears that indignation, and Ares feels none.

**The cry.** At the ships the Trojans have crossed the wall, and Hector tells his men that it is no disgrace to die for their native land (15.486–499; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]]). Ajax answers with the rally-cry of Hera, Agamemnon and Poseidon, αἰδὼς Ἀργεῖοι, “Shame, Argives!” (15.502; 5.787, 8.228, 13.95; see the notes on Books 5, 8 and 13). He goes on not with insults but with the stakes: “Now it is sure: either we perish / or we are saved and thrust the evil away from the ships” (15.502–503). If Hector takes the ships, no one will walk home (15.504–505); Hector is calling his men “not to a dance … but to fight” (15.508; see [[not-to-a-dance-but-to-fight]]); and it is better to die at once than to be drained away slowly “by worse men than we are” (15.511–513; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). The shame of the last verse is the shame of being beaten by inferiors.

**Shame before one another.** When Hector calls on his kinsmen over the body of Dolops, Ajax rallies the Argives a second time, with the lines Agamemnon spoke in Book 5 and a new first verse:

{{quote:ebc5fc39-66d2-530f-8022-67f65cd52ca3}}

The verse “and set shame in your hearts” (καὶ αἰδῶ θέσθʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ) replaces Agamemnon's “and take a valiant heart” (5.529); the rest is his (5.530–532; see [[shame-saves-more]]). This time the words take hold at once: “they laid his word to heart, and fenced the ships / with a bulwark of bronze” (15.566–567): the bronze-armed men themselves stand round the ships as a fence.

**Shame and fear.** Driven back from the first row of ships, the Argives stand by their huts “all together, and did not scatter through the camp; for shame held them / and fear; for they called to one another ceaselessly” (ἴσχε γὰρ αἰδὼς / καὶ δέος, 15.657–658; see [[deos]]). The narrator names the two feelings that keep a line together, the shame of being seen to run and the fear of the enemy, and the shouting by which each man makes the others see and be seen.

**Before other men.** Then Nestor, the guardian of the Achaeans, “begged each man by his parents, imploring him” (15.659–660; see [[supplication]] and [[nestor]]):

{{quote:38f3daa7-082d-54f7-98a9-b9dc44c804e4}}

His first verse is Ajax's (15.661 = 15.561), but the shame he asks for is not shame before the comrades who stand beside a man. It is shame “before other men” (ἄλλων ἀνθρώπων, 15.662), the judgment of people in general, and it reaches home: the children, wives, possessions and parents, living or dead, for whose sake he begs them to stand. Hector has told the Trojans, in sight of their city, that their families will be safe if they die (15.496–499); the Achaeans' families are across the sea, and Nestor asks the men to see them, though “they are not here” (οὐ παρεόντων, 15.665). The speech ends with the formula of the rally, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (15.667; see [[exhortation]]), and Athena then clears the mist from the Achaeans' eyes (15.668–673; see [[achlys]]).
