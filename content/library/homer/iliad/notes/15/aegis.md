summary: Zeus gives Apollo the tasseled aegis to rout the Achaeans. **Given to Apollo.** Zeus, awake, sends Apollo to Hector: “But take the tasseled aegis in your hands, / and shake it hard over them, and put the Achaean heroes to flight” (αἰγίδα θυσσανόεσσαν, 15.229–230; see [[apollo]] and [[zeus]]). The aegis has been Athena's in the battles of the poem (2.446–451, 5.738–742); here Zeus lends it to the god who fights for the Trojans.

**What it is.** The narrator describes it as Apollo goes before the Trojans:

{{quote:85d0b959-a253-57a4-a38a-a4bcf70efd33}}

Four epithets stand in a row: θοῦρις, “furious”, the word of furious courage, which it shares with a shield (11.32; see [[furious]]); δεινή, “dreadful”, as in Athena's arming (5.739); ἀμφιδάσεια, “shaggy all around”, found only here, of the goatskin or of its fringe of tassels (see the translation's note on 15.309); and ἀριπρεπής, “conspicuous”. Then its maker and purpose: the smith Hephaestus gave it to Zeus to carry “to put men to rout” (ἐς φόβον ἀνδρῶν, 15.310; see [[hephaestus]] and [[rout]]). It is the only account of its origin in Homer.

**Still and shaken.** The aegis works only when it moves. “As long as Phoebus Apollo held the aegis motionless in his hands, / so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell” (15.318–319): the battle is even. “But when he looked the Danaans of the swift horses in the face / and shook it, and himself shouted very loudly besides”, he bewitched their hearts, “and they forgot their furious courage” (15.320–322; see [[thymos]]), and they fled like cattle or sheep before two beasts in the dead of night (15.323–327; see [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]). Zeus will shake it himself on Ida in Book 17, with thunder and lightning, and turn the battle to the Trojans (17.593–596).

**Throwing down the wall.** “Holding the aegis, highly prized” (αἰγίδʼ ἔχων ἐρίτιμον, 15.361), the half-verse of Athena in Book 2 (2.447), Apollo kicks in the banks of the ditch and throws down the Achaean wall as a boy scatters sand (15.355–366; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). Elsewhere in the book Zeus is “aegis-bearing” in the verse-ends of his messages and his thunder (15.175, 15.242, 15.379; see [[aegis-bearing]]).
