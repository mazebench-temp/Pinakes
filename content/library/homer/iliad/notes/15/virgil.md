---
summary: The Aeneid takes four things from Book 15. Neptune claims the sea as his “by lot”, as Poseidon does; Turnus runs out to battle like the stallion that breaks its halter; Latinus and Mezentius stand like the Danaans' rock by the sea; and Turnus calls for fire to burn the Trojan ships, as Hector does at the stern of Protesilaus' ship.
---

- **The sea by lot.** Neptune sends Aeolus' winds home with a message for their king: not to him were the rule of the sea and the fierce trident given, *sed mihi sorte datum*, “but to me, by lot” (*Aeneid* 1.138–139). It is the claim Poseidon makes to Iris, “I got by lot the gray sea, to dwell in forever” (15.190): [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]] and [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]].
- **The stallion.** Turnus runs down from the citadel to battle like a horse that breaks its bonds and makes for the pastures and the river (*Aeneid* 11.486–497). Homer gives the simile to Paris (6.506–511) and, in this book, to Hector, when Apollo has breathed might into him (15.262–270): [[virgils-stallion]] and [[the-stallion]].
- **The rock by the sea.** Hector cannot break the Danaans, “for they held, fitted together in close order like a tower, like a rock, / lofty and great, close to the gray sea, / which withstands the swift paths of the shrill winds / and the swollen waves that belch against it” (15.618–621; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]). Virgil gives the rock twice, each time to one man. When the Latins clamor for war against the omens, King Latinus resists them “like a rock that stands unmoved in the sea, like a rock of the sea when a great crash comes, which holds itself fast by its mass while many waves bark around it; the crags and the foaming reefs roar about it in vain, and the seaweed dashed against its side is flung back” (*Aeneid* 7.586–590). And Mezentius, against whom the whole Etruscan army rushes, is “like a cliff that juts out into the vast sea, facing the fury of the winds and exposed to the deep, which endures all the force and the threats of sky and sea, itself remaining unmoved” (*Aeneid* 10.693–696). Homer's rock is a whole army in close order; Virgil's stands alone. And Homer's holds only for a moment, until Hector leaps into the throng (15.623–629), while Latinus does not hold at all: when he cannot prevail he gives up the reins of government and shuts himself in his palace (*Aeneid* 7.591–600).
- **Fire for the ships.** At the end of Book 15 Hector holds the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls, “Bring fire” (15.716–718). In the ninth book of the *Aeneid* Turnus, finding no way into the Trojan camp, turns on the fleet that lies beside it, fenced by earthworks and the river. He calls for fire (*incendia poscit*), fills his own hand with a blazing pine, and his men arm themselves with black torches (*Aeneid* 9.67–76). Then the poet asks the Muses what god turned such fires from the Trojans (*Aeneid* 9.77–79), as Homer, early in the next book, asks the Muses how fire first fell on the ships of the Achaeans (16.112–113). Homer's ship burns until Patroclus puts out the fire (16.122–124, 16.293–294); Virgil's ships, built from the sacred pines of Ida, are saved by the mother of the gods, who turns them into nymphs of the sea (*Aeneid* 9.80–122).
