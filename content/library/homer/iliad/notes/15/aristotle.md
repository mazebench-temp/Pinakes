---
summary: Aristotle quotes two verses of Book 15 in the Rhetoric among Homer's metaphors that make lifeless things alive, the spears longing for flesh and the spear point raging through Dolops' chest. The Nicomachean Ethics gives Hector, as the commander who drives his men by threats, words close to his threat at the ditch; the History of Animals names the dove-killer among the hawks; and the Metaphysics reports the oath by the Styx that Hera swears here. The treatise On the Cosmos, handed down with his works, quotes Poseidon's verse on the sky that fell to Zeus.
---

- **Lifeless things alive.** “And as Homer often does, making lifeless things alive through metaphor” (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1411b31–32): two of his six examples are verses of Book 15. The spears that fall short “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (*Rhetoric* 1412a1), a verse found at 15.317 and at 11.574; and “the spear point sped through his chest, raging” (*Rhetoric* 1412a2) is 15.542, where Menelaus kills Dolops: [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]].
- **Courage under compulsion.** Below the courage of citizens, which comes from shame, Aristotle ranks that of men whom their commanders compel, who act from fear and flee what is painful, not what is base; his example is a threat that he gives to Hector, “whomever I see cowering apart from the battle, he shall not be sure of escaping the dogs” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, 1116a29–b3): [[aristotle-civic-courage]]. The words are nearest to Agamemnon's in Book 2 (2.391–393), but the Hector who threatens his own men is the Hector of this book, who drives the Trojans over the ditch with the warning that whomever he sees away from the ships will die where he stands, unburied, and dogs will tear him before the city (15.346–351; see [[hector]]). In the same book the Achaeans hold together by the huts, “for shame held them / and fear” (15.657–658; see [[aidos]] and [[deos]]): the two motives that Aristotle keeps apart, joined in one sentence.
- **The dove-killer.** Apollo goes down from Ida “like a hawk, / a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (15.237–238). In the *History of Animals* the φασσοφόνος is a kind of hawk: the strongest of the hawks is the triorchis, the second the aesalon, the third the circus, while the asterias, the dove-killer and the pternis are of other kinds (*History of Animals* 9.36); and the cymindis is the size of “the hawk called the dove-killer” (*History of Animals* 9.12; see the Book 14 note). Homer's epithet is Aristotle's name for a species (see [[dove-killer]] and [[hawk]]).
- **The oath by the Styx.** Hera swears by Earth and Heaven and “the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest / and most dread oath for the blessed gods” (15.36–38; see [[styx]]). Aristotle reports that some found Thales' doctrine of water in the old poets, who made the oath of the gods water, the Styx, “for what is oldest is most honored, and an oath is the most honored thing” (*Metaphysics* Α 3, 983b30–33): [[aristotle-oceanus-and-tethys]].
- **Zeus' lot.** The treatise *On the Cosmos*, which has come down among Aristotle's works but is generally thought not to be his, places god not in the middle, where the earth and this turbid region are, but above, pure in a pure place, which we call heaven and Olympus; it quotes the Odyssey's Olympus, which no wind shakes and no rain or snow touches (*Odyssey* 6.42–45). All men stretch out their hands to the sky when they pray, it goes on, and so this verse too has been well spoken: “Zeus got the wide sky, in the upper air and the clouds” (*On the Cosmos* 6, 400a, quoting 15.192; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]] and [[sky]]).
