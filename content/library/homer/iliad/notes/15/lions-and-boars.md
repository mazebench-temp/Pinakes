---
summary: The first half of the couplet returns without the boars: after Antilochus' escape, the Trojans rush upon the ships “like lions that eat raw flesh”, fulfilling the commands of Zeus (15.592–595).
grc: ["λείουσιν ἐοικότες ὠμοφάγοισι"]
en: [like lions that eat raw flesh]
---

Τρῶες δὲ λείουσιν ἐοικότες ὠμοφάγοισι / νηυσὶν ἐπεσσεύοντο, Διὸς δʼ ἐτέλειον ἐφετμάς: “And the Trojans, like lions that eat raw flesh, rushed upon the ships, and fulfilled the commands of Zeus” (15.592–593). The half-verse is the one of Book 5 and Book 7, where it opened the couplet of lions and wild boars (5.782, 7.256); here it stands alone, and ὠμοφάγοισι, before a consonant, lacks its final ν (see [[eating-raw-flesh]]). In Book 5 the beasts were the best of the Argives crowded around Diomedes, and in Book 7 the two champions closing in their duel. Here they are a whole army on the attack, and the beasts' hunger is Zeus' purpose: he keeps rousing great might in the Trojans and bewitching the hearts of the Argives, for he wishes to give glory to Hector, so that he may cast fire on the ships and fulfill the prayer of Thetis (15.593–600; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

The half-verse follows hard on two animal similes for Antilochus, who sprang on the dying Melanippus like a dog on a wounded fawn and then fled before Hector like a beast that has done harm (15.579–591; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]] and [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]). The single beast that runs before the throng gathers is followed by a pack of lions that do not run.
