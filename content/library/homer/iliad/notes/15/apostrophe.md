The address to Apollo closes the simile of the boy who builds sand castles by the sea and scatters them in play: “so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery / of the Argives, and roused panic among them” (15.365–366; see [[apollo]] and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). It is one of only two places where the narrator addresses Apollo, both with the obscure vocative ἤϊε (the other is 20.152; see [[ieios]] and the translation's note on 15.365), and it comes at the moment of the god's greatest ease. The address to Melanippus falls between his death and the stripping that does not happen: Antilochus leaps on him like a dog on a wounded fawn, “so at you, Melanippus, Antilochus, steadfast in battle, leapt / to strip your armor” (15.582–583; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]] and [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). The listener is called on at the height of the fight at Protesilaus' ship, as in the first battle (4.429): “you would have said that unwearied and unyielding they met one another / in war, so furiously did they fight” (15.697–698).
