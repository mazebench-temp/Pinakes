summary: Ajax holds the ships. Over one ship he kills Caletor as he brings fire and loses his attendant Lycophron to Hector's spear; when Zeus breaks Teucer's bowstring he sends his brother to fight with spear and shield; he shames the Argives twice and kills Laodamas, son of Antenor; and when the Trojans reach the first ships he strides from deck to deck with a spear for fighting at sea, falls back at last to the seven-foot bench, and stabs twelve men who come with fire (15.415–483, 15.501–517, 15.560–567, 15.674–746).

**The stone remembered.** The book opens on what Ajax did at the end of the last: Zeus wakes to see Hector lying on the plain, gasping and vomiting blood, “for it was not the feeblest of the Achaeans who had hit him” (15.9–11; see [[hector]]). Hector tells Apollo that “Ajax, good at the war cry, struck me / on the chest with a stone” (15.249–250), and Thoas, seeing him back on his feet, says that every Achaean had hoped he had died “at the hands of Ajax, son of Telamon” (15.288–289; see [[thoas]], [[telamon]] and [[good-at-the-war-cry]]). On Thoas' advice the best men stand together against him: “Those around Ajax and lord Idomeneus, / Teucer and Meriones and Meges, the equal of Ares, / called the best men and set the battle in order” (15.301–303; see [[battle-order]]). Which Ajax is not said; with Teucer named next, it is the son of Telamon (see the translation's note on 15.301). The line breaks when Apollo shakes the aegis (15.318–327; see [[aegis]]).

**Over one ship.** At the ships the battle is stretched as evenly as a carpenter's chalk line makes a timber straight (15.410–413; see [[the-chalk-line]]), “and Hector went straight at glorious Ajax. / The two were toiling over one ship, and could not, / the one drive the other off and burn the ship with fire, / nor the other thrust him back, since a god had brought him there” (15.415–418; see [[glorious]] and [[ships]]). Their duel in Book 7 was parted at nightfall (7.279–282; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]), and their meeting in Book 14 ended with Hector felled by Ajax's stone (14.409–420); here neither can move the other. The fire that Hector has threatened since Book 8 (8.180–183) is now carried to a ship, and Ajax stops it: he spears Caletor, son of Clytius, Hector's cousin, “as he brought fire to the ship”, “and the brand dropped from his hand” (15.419–421; see [[caletor]]). Hector, calling on the Trojans to save the body, throws at Ajax and misses, and the spear kills Lycophron of Cythera, Ajax's attendant (15.422–435; see [[lycophron]]).

**Lycophron.** “And Ajax shuddered, and spoke to his brother” (15.436):

{{quote:91838e64-8329-505a-a163-34de5ed4fead}}

The dead man was an exile who had come to the brothers' house after a killing, and they honored him “like our own dear parents” (see [[lycophron]], [[cythera]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). Ajax answers the death with his brother's bow, as in Book 8, when Teucer shot from under the great shield (8.266–272; see [[teucer]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

**The broken string.** Teucer kills Polydamas' charioteer Cleitus, but when he draws on Hector, Zeus, “who was guarding / Hector”, breaks the string, and the bow falls from his hand (15.445–465; see [[cleitus]] and [[zeus]]). Teucer blames “a god” who cuts short the plans of their battle (15.467–470), and Ajax answers him:

{{quote:f67e22bc-047a-5075-b115-df9f4e5d94bb}}

Teucer puts the bow away in the huts, arms, and runs to stand beside his brother (15.478–483; see [[arming-scene]]). The narrator names Zeus; the brothers speak only of “a god” (δαίμων, 15.468; θεός, 15.473), and it is Hector, watching from the other side, who says that he has seen “the missiles of their best man foiled by Zeus” (15.489). Teucer's words come back in Book 16. When Hector lops the head off Ajax's spear, Ajax knows in his heart the works of the gods (ἔργα θεῶν), that Zeus is cutting short the plans of their battle, πάγχυ μάχης ἐπὶ μήδεα κεῖρε, in the words Teucer uses here (16.119–121; 15.467); he gives way, and the first ship burns. ἀσπουδί, “without a struggle” (15.476), is Hector's word too: let the Achaeans not board their ships without a struggle (8.512), and let him not die without a struggle and without glory (22.304).

**Shame, Argives!** Hector tells the Trojans that the help of Zeus is plain to see, and that a man who dies defending his native land leaves his wife and children safe (15.486–499; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]] and [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]]). “And Ajax on the other side called to his companions”:

{{quote:96fcee39-db70-5d80-8e07-ce4e36ae79cc}}

αἰδὼς Ἀργεῖοι, “Shame, Argives!”, is the cry of Hera in the likeness of Stentor, of Agamemnon and of Poseidon (5.787, 8.228, 13.95; see [[aidos]] and [[exhortation]]). Ajax gives it reasons of his own. The ships are the only road home: no man will walk back to his country if Hector takes them (ἐμβαδόν, “on foot”, only here in Homer). Hector has called his army “not to a dance, … but to fight” (15.508; see [[not-to-a-dance-but-to-fight]] and [[dance]]). And a quick end is better than a slow one, βέλτερον ἢ ἀπολέσθαι ἕνα χρόνον ἠὲ βιῶναι, “Better to perish once and for all, or to live” (15.511; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]); in the Odyssey Eurylochus uses the same reckoning when he urges the hungry companions to take the Sun's cattle, preferring to die at once in the waves rather than waste away on a desert island (*Odyssey* 12.350–351). The verse that closes Hector's speech closes his: “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (15.500 = 15.514). Then Hector kills Schedius, Ajax kills Laodamas, “leader of the foot soldiers, the splendid son of Antenor” (15.515–517; see [[schedius-son-of-perimedes]] and [[laodamas]]), and Polydamas kills Otus. It is the second son of Antenor to die by Ajax's spear in two books, after Archelochus (14.463–468; see [[antenor]] and [[archelochus]]).

**Set shame in your hearts.** When Hector rebukes Melanippus, “great Telamonian Ajax urged on the Argives” again (15.560):

{{quote:ebc5fc39-66d2-530f-8022-67f65cd52ca3}}

The verses are Agamemnon's exhortation in Book 5 with a new first verse (5.529–532; see [[shame-saves-more]]), and Nestor will open his own appeal with that first verse (15.661). This time the men respond: “they laid his word to heart, and fenced the ships / with a bulwark of bronze” (15.566–567).

**On the decks.** When the Trojans reach the first ships and Athena clears the mist from the Achaeans' eyes, Ajax will no longer stand back with the others:

{{quote:372a1ec9-1d97-516c-8eb9-621eb7c68469}}

ξυστὸν μέγα ναύμαχον, “a great spear for fighting at sea”: one of the long spears made for fighting from ships, which the Achaeans have already taken up against the Trojans' chariots (15.386–389), made in lengths joined with bands, and twice as long as Hector's spear of eleven cubits (6.319, 8.494; see [[spear]] and the translation's note on 15.389, 15.677–678). μακρὰ βιβάσθων, “with long strides”, is his walk when he went out to fight Hector in Book 7 and when he challenged him in Book 13 (7.213, 13.809); in this book it is also Hector's (15.307). A simile follows that the Iliad uses only here: Ajax goes from deck to deck as a trick rider, driving four horses along the road to a city, leaps from one to another while men and women watch (15.679–686; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). “His voice reached the upper air, / and always shouting terribly he ordered the Danaans / to defend the ships and the huts” (15.686–688). Hector, like an eagle on a flock of birds, darts at a ship, and Zeus pushes him on with his hand (15.688–695; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]).

**At the seven-foot bench.** Hector seizes the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls for fire (15.704–725; see [[protesilaus]] and [[stern]]). “And Ajax no longer stood his ground; for he was hard pressed by missiles; / but he drew back a little way, thinking he would die, / to the seven-foot bench, and left the deck of the balanced ship” (15.727–729). θρῆνυς is a footstool everywhere else in Homer; on the ship it is a bench, a thwart or the steersman's seat (see the translation's note on 15.729). From there, on the watch, he keeps off with his spear every Trojan who brings fire (15.730–731), and he speaks once more:

{{quote:001ed965-6c58-5589-bc75-ccf76276dcaf}}

The address is the army's (see [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]), the verse with which Agamemnon and Nestor speak to all the Danaans (2.110, 6.67, 19.78; see [[attendants-of-ares]]), and the second verse is the one Hector has just used to his Trojans (15.487 = 15.734). The speech takes away every refuge in turn: no helpers behind, no stronger wall, now that Apollo has thrown down theirs (15.361–366; see [[the-achaean-wall]]), no city with towers and a people to turn the battle (see the translation's note on 7.26). The besiegers sit with their backs to the sea, far from home, and their only city is the ships. φόως, “light”, is the light of rescue, as in Agamemnon's hope that Teucer may become “a light to the Danaans” (8.282; see the translation's note on 15.741). When the gods left the battle in Book 6, Ajax was the first to break a battalion of the Trojans, “and he brought light to his companions” (6.5–6).

**Twelve.** “He spoke, and in his fury pressed on with his sharp spear” (15.742). μαιμώων, “in his fury”, is the word for Menelaus' spear point as it sped through Dolops' chest (μαιμώωσα, 15.542). Every Trojan who rushes at the ships with fire, “to do a favor to Hector who urged them on”, Ajax waits for and stabs, “and twelve he stabbed hand to hand in front of the ships” (15.743–746). The book ends with him there. Teucer's arrow had killed Cleitus as he drove his horses “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans” (15.449); the brothers kill the men who serve Hector's favor. In Book 16, worn out under the missiles, he holds on until Hector cuts the head from his spear, and the fire falls on the ship (16.102–123).
