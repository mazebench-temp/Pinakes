χερσὶ δὲ μή τί με πάγχυ κακὸν ὣς δειδισσέσθω: “And let him not try at all to frighten me with his hands, as if I were a coward” (15.196). δειδίσσομαι is “frighten”, and the same words, κακὸν ὣς δειδίσσεσθαι, are in Odysseus' rebuke to the kings who run for the ships in Book 2, where they are taken as “to panic like a coward” (2.190). The coward is the man whom threats can move. Zeus has threatened to come and fight Poseidon face to face, since he is far stronger and the elder by birth (15.162–167, 15.176–183), and Poseidon refuses the part: he is Zeus' equal in honor, one of three brothers who drew lots for the sky, the sea and the misty darkness, and let Zeus stay in his third share (15.185–195; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Threats, he says, are for Zeus' own children, “who will listen to him when he urges them, even under compulsion” (15.197–199).

He gives way all the same. Iris asks whether she is to carry the answer just as it is, and reminds him that the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.200–204); Poseidon says that she has spoken rightly, and “for all my indignation, I will give way” (15.211), with a threat of his own if Zeus spares Troy (15.212–217). He is not frightened like a coward; he yields to the right of the elder.
