{{quote:aec12477-8d84-5d26-a2da-263559f77b07}}

μαίνετο δʼ ὡς ὅτʼ Ἄρης ἐγχέσπαλος ἢ ὀλοὸν πῦρ / οὔρεσι μαίνηται βαθέης ἐν τάρφεσιν ὕλης: “He raged as when spear-brandishing Ares, or deadly fire, rages in the mountains, in the thickets of the deep forest” (15.605–606). The simile has two vehicles and one verb. μαίνομαι is to rage, to be beside oneself, and it is said here of the man, of the god and of the fire alike. Others have said it of Hector before: Hera, that he “rages past all bearing” (8.355), and Odysseus, that he “rages terribly, trusting in Zeus” (9.238); and the narrator looks back on this day in Book 21 as the day when shining Hector raged (ὅτε μαίνετο φαίδιμος Ἕκτωρ, 21.3–5). Ares is ἐγχέσπαλος, “spear-brandishing” (see [[spear-brandishing]] and [[ares]]), and the fire ὀλοόν, “deadly” (see [[deadly]]). The τάρφη are the thickets, the dense growth where a fire finds its fuel, as in the uncut woodland of Agamemnon's simile (11.155–157).

What follows is a description, not a simile, and it keeps the fire in view: “and foam formed around his mouth, and his two eyes blazed under his grim brows, and his helmet shook terribly around his temples as he fought” (15.607–609). ἀφλοισμός, “foam”, is found only here. λαμπέσθην, “blazed”, is a dual, for the pair of eyes, and the brows are βλοσυραί, “grim”, the word for Ajax's face as he goes out to his duel (7.212) and, in a compound, for the Gorgon on Agamemnon's shield (11.36). Hector's eyes blazed with fire when he leapt through the gate of the wall (12.466; see [[eyes-like-fire]]); now the whole man rages like fire. A few verses later he leaps into the throng “blazing all over with fire” (λαμπόμενος πυρὶ πάντοθεν, 15.623).

The narrator names the cause and the end. Zeus himself is Hector's defender from the upper air, and honors him though he is one man among many. “Short-lived he was to be; for already she was rousing his fated day against him, Pallas Athena, under the strength of the son of Peleus” (15.610–614; see [[hector]] and [[short-lived]]). The fire is lit by Zeus, who wants to see a ship burning (15.596–600; see [[plan-of-zeus]]), and it will burn out at Achilles' hands.

Fire in the forest has been the gleam of bronze (2.455–458), the destruction of fleeing men (11.155–159) and a roar (14.396–397). Here, for the first time, it is a hero's frenzy, and the next fire simile, for Achilles, is a frenzy too: wondrous fire rages through the deep glens of a dry mountain, the deep forest burns, and the wind drives the flame on every side (20.490–494). Ares as a likeness for the onset of a single warrior is in the simile of Ajax going out to the duel (7.208–212; see [[like-huge-ares]]), and the short comparison “like a flame” is given above all to Hector (13.53, 13.688; see [[like-a-flame]]).
