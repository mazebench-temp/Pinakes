---
summary: At the stern of Protesilaus' ship Hector blames the elders for the years the army spent behind the wall: “through the cowardice of the elders” they held him back when he wanted to fight at the ships (15.719–723).
refs: [15.721]
---

Holding the stern ornament of Protesilaus' ship, Hector calls for fire and tells the Trojans that Zeus has given them “a day worth all the rest”, to take the ships, which “brought many banes upon us, through the cowardice of the elders, / who, when I wanted to fight at the sterns of the ships, / held me back, and restrained the army” (15.719–723). κακότητι γερόντων, “through the cowardice of the elders”, is Hector's charge; κακότης is the word for cowardice in battle (2.368). The γέροντες are the old men of Troy, the “elders of the people” who sat with Priam at the Scaean gates, “done with war now because of their age, but excellent / speakers” (3.149–151). Three of them, Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon (3.147), lose sons at the ships in this book: Caletor (15.419), Dolops (15.525–526) and Melanippus (15.546–547; see [[caletor]], [[dolops-son-of-lampus]] and [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]]).

The poem has not shown the elders restraining him. Achilles remembers only that while he fought, Hector would not rouse the battle far from the wall, “but came only as far as the Scaean gates and the oak” (9.352–354). Hector at once offers a second cause: “but if far-seeing Zeus was harming our minds then, / now he himself is urging us on and commanding us” (15.724–725; see [[hector]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).
