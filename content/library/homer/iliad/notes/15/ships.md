summary: Book 15 is the battle at the ships. Zeus foretells that the Achaeans will fall among the ships of Achilles and that the counterattack from the ships will follow; the Trojans cross the wall and fight at the sterns, the Achaeans from the decks with long sea-spears; the first row of ships is lost; and Hector takes hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire.

**The plan.** Zeus' forecast gives the ships their place in the story: Apollo is to drive the Achaeans back “so that in their flight they tumble among the many-benched ships / of Achilles, son of Peleus”, and from then on Zeus will bring about “a counterattack from the ships” until Ilios is taken (παλίωξιν παρὰ νηῶν, 15.63–71; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The narrator repeats the phrase when the moment comes near: Zeus is waiting “to see with his eyes the blaze of a burning ship”, for from then on he will turn the counterattack against the Trojans (15.599–602). The ancient title of the book is taken from it (see [[title-of-book-15]]).

**At the sterns.** Apollo throws down the wall, and the Trojans go over it like a wave over a ship's side (15.381–384; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]]). They drive their horses in “and fought at the sterns / hand to hand with double-pointed spears, the one side from their chariots, / the others from the black ships, having climbed up high, / with the long spears that lay ready for them on the ships, / for fighting at sea, jointed, clad in bronze at the tip” (15.385–389; see [[stern]] and [[spear]]). Neither side can break the other, and the battle is stretched as evenly as a chalk line makes a ship's timber straight (15.405–413; see [[the-chalk-line]]). Hector and Ajax toil over a single ship, the one unable to drive the other off and burn it, the other unable to thrust him back (15.415–418; see [[ajax]] and [[hector]]).

**Fire.** The Trojans' aim is the fire. Caletor dies bringing fire to the ship (15.419–421; see [[caletor]]); Ajax tells the Argives that Hector “is eager to set fire to the ships” (15.507); Zeus wishes Hector to “cast wondrous fire, / tireless fire” on the curved ships (15.597–598); and the Trojans' hope is “to set fire to the ships and kill the Achaean heroes” (15.702). If Hector takes the ships, Ajax asks, do the Achaeans expect to walk home (15.504–505)? The ships are their only way home, and the wall built to defend them is gone (see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[nostos]]).

**The first row.** The Achaeans are driven back until they face their own ships: “the outermost ships, / all those drawn up first, hemmed them in” (15.653–654). These are the ships beached first and farthest toward the plain (14.30–36; see the note on Book 14). “And the Argives gave way from the ships, under compulsion, / from the first ones”, and stood by the huts, held together by shame and fear (15.655–658; see [[hut]] and [[aidos]]). Ajax strides up and down the decks (ἴκρια, 15.676, 15.685), going from ship to ship like a rider leaping from horse to horse (15.679–686; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]).

**The ship of Protesilaus.** Hector makes for “a dark-prowed ship” like an eagle at the birds (15.690–693), and lays hold of the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and did not take him home (15.704–706; see [[protesilaus]]); around it the two armies fight with axes and swords (15.707–715; see [[axe]]). Hector will not let go of the stern ornament, and calls for fire: Zeus has given them a day worth all the rest, “to take the ships, which came here against the will of the gods / and brought many banes upon us” (15.716–721). The ship burns at the start of the next book (16.122–124).

**In similes.** The sea and its ships fill the similes of the battle: the wave over a ship's side (15.381–383), the carpenter's line on a ship's timber (δόρυ νήϊον, 15.410), and the wave that falls on a swift ship, so that the sailors tremble at heart, carried out from under death by only a little (15.624–628; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]). The Achaeans at their ships are in the sailors' place.
