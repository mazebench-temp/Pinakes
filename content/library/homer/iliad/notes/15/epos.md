---
summary: The gods' quarrel is a quarrel of words: Hera asks whether the gods can stop Zeus “by word or by force”, Poseidon resents being rebuked “with angry words” by an equal and tells Zeus to keep his “terrible words” for his children, and Iris' word turns him. At the ships the Argives lay Ajax's word to heart.
---

ἔπος is set against force twice: Hera asks whether the gods are still eager to stop Zeus “by word or by force” (ἢ ἔπει ἠὲ βίῃ, 15.106), and Zeus promises to devise “both deed and word” for the Achaeans (15.234; see [[word-or-deed]]). Zeus' order to Poseidon threatens what words cannot do: if Poseidon “will not obey my words” (ἐπέεσσʼ, 15.162 = 15.178), let him reckon with Zeus' strength (see [[peitho]]). Poseidon answers that Zeus should rebuke his own sons and daughters “with terrible words” (ἐκπάγλοις ἐπέεσσιν, 15.198), and that grief comes on him whenever Zeus wishes to rebuke an equal “with angry words” (χολωτοῖσιν ἐπέεσσιν, 15.210; see [[cholos]] and [[neikos]]). Iris' answer is a word of a different kind, which he calls “fair and right” (τοῦτο ἔπος κατὰ μοῖραν, 15.206; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]). Athena approaches Ares “with words” to stop him (ἐπέεσσι καθάπτετο, 15.127; see [[ares]]). In the battle a speech takes effect when men lay it to heart: after Ajax's appeal to shame the Argives “laid his word to heart, and fenced the ships” (ἐν θυμῷ δʼ ἐβάλοντο ἔπος, 15.566; see [[shame-saves-more]]). The verse “and lamenting he spoke a word” introduces both Ares' grief for his son and Patroclus' leave-taking of Eurypylus (15.114 = 15.398).
