summary: Patroclus, who has stayed in Eurypylus' hut to tend his wound, sees the Trojans storm the wall and leaves for Achilles, taking Nestor's words for his own: “Who knows if, with a god's help, I might stir his heart / by persuading him? Good is the persuasion of a companion.”
passages:
  - 15.390-405 | Patroclus leaves Eurypylus
---

Patroclus has been in Eurypylus' hut since Nestor sent him back to Achilles in Book 11, cheering the wounded man with talk and spreading healing drugs on his wound (15.390–394; 11.806–848; see [[patroclus]], [[eurypylus]] and [[healing]]). When he sees the Trojans rushing at the wall and hears the shouting and rout of the Danaans, he groans and strikes his thighs “with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word” (15.397–398), the verses with which Ares heard of his son's death at the start of the book (15.113–114). “A great quarrel has arisen” (μέγα νεῖκος ὄρωρεν, 15.400; see [[neikos]]), as Poseidon said when he roused the Achaeans in Book 13 (13.122), and he leaves Eurypylus to his attendant.

His last two verses are Nestor's, with one change: Nestor said “you might stir his heart” (ὀρίναις, 11.792), and Patroclus says “I might stir” (ὀρίνω, 15.403). The old man's counsel has become the companion's purpose. “So he spoke, and his feet carried him off” (15.405), and the narrator does not follow him until he stands weeping before Achilles at the start of Book 16 (16.2–4).
