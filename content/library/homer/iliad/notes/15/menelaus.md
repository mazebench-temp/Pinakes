**Dolops.** Dolops, son of Lampus, has struck through Meges' shield, and Meges has sheared the plume from his helmet (15.525–538; see [[dolops-son-of-lampus]] and [[meges]]):

{{quote:ed876c34-b82e-5db6-96a9-8d30328732ec}}

Μενέλαος ἀρήϊος, “warlike Menelaus” (see [[warlike]]), ἦλθεν ἀμύντωρ, “came to his defense”: the half-verse in which Polydamas came to the defense of Satnius' body in Book 14 (14.449; see [[polydamas]]). στῆ δʼ εὐρὰξ σὺν δουρὶ λαθών, “stood off to one side with his spear, unseen”, is the half-verse in which Coon, son of Antenor, came at Agamemnon and wounded him in the arm (11.251; see [[coon]] and [[agamemnon]]): the blow from the side that wounded the elder brother is now dealt by the younger. αἰχμὴ δὲ στέρνοιο διέσσυτο μαιμώωσα, “and the point sped through his chest in its fury” (15.542): Aristotle quotes the verse, with “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (15.317 = 11.574), as Homer's way of making lifeless things alive by metaphor, for “being shameless and raging and the rest are activity” (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1412a1–4; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]] and [[aristotle]]). The verse with στέρνοιο, “chest”, is this one; at 5.661 the point of Tlepolemus' spear speeds “in its fury” through Sarpedon's thigh. “The two hurried to strip the bronze-fitted armor from his shoulders” (15.544; see [[stripping-the-dead]]), and Hector calls his kinsmen to the body (15.545–559).

**Antilochus.** When Ajax has shamed the Argives a second time, “Menelaus, good at the war cry, urged on Antilochus” (15.568; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]] and [[exhortation]]):

{{quote:5c717cfd-9560-5f70-a7fa-73656b9ebaa2}}

The praise is a portrait: youth, speed and valor, the gifts the poem gives Nestor's son (see [[antilochus]]). εἴ … βάλοισθα, “if only you would … strike”, is a wish that serves as a command, and Menelaus goes back to his own place while the young man leaps out (15.572–573). Antilochus kills Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, and then runs from Hector (15.573–591; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]]). The two are paired elsewhere in the poem. In Book 5 Antilochus came to Menelaus' side, fearing for him, when he faced Aeneas, and Aeneas would not stand against the two together (5.561–572; see [[aeneas]]); in Book 17 Menelaus will send Antilochus to tell Achilles that Patroclus is dead (17.651–701); and at the funeral games the young man's driving will anger him, until Antilochus gives way to the elder (23.566–611).
