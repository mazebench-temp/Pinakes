---
summary: Meges' corselet was a gift from his father's guest-friend, “a defense against enemy men,” and Melanippus grazed his cattle in Percote “while the enemy were still far away.”
---

As a noun, δήϊοι are “the enemy,” and both uses in the book are of this kind. Dolops' spear does not get through Meges' corselet, which Phyleus brought from Ephyra: “For his guest-friend, Euphetes, lord of men, had given it to him / to wear into war, a defense against enemy men; / and then too it kept destruction from his son's body” (15.532–534; [[meges]], [[phyleus]], [[euphetes]], [[corselet]], [[xenia]], [[lord-of-men]]). And Melanippus, before the war, “grazed shambling cattle / in Percote, while the enemy were still far away” (15.547–548; [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]], [[percote]], [[shambling]]). One gift of a guest-friend protects a son a generation later; one herdsman's life is divided by the coming of the enemy ([[curving]]).
