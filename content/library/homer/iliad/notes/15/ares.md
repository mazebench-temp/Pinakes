summary: On Olympus Hera tells Ares that his son Ascalaphus has been killed. He strikes his thighs, tells Terror and Rout to yoke his horses and arms to avenge him, even at the risk of Zeus' thunderbolt, until Athena takes his helmet, shield and spear and sits him down. In the battle he is a measure of the fighters and of Hector's rage.

**The news.** Sitting among the gods in the house of Zeus, Hera ends her complaint with news meant for one of them: “For already now, I think, a bane has been made for Ares; / for his son has perished in battle, the dearest of men to him, / Ascalaphus, whom mighty Ares says is his own” (15.110–112; see [[hera]], [[ascalaphus]] and [[obrimos]]). Deiphobus killed him in Book 13, while Ares sat on Olympus under golden clouds and did not know (13.518–525).

**His grief.**

{{quote:d4640ba3-9b9e-51e2-be41-8277ce2a522d}}

Striking the thighs is the gesture of despair: Asius strikes his when the Lapiths hold the gate against him and he calls Zeus a lover of lies (12.162–166), and Patroclus strikes his in the same two verses when he sees the Trojans over the wall (15.397–398; see [[patroclus]] and [[lament]]). The god imagines himself lying “with the dead among the blood and the dust”, as he told Zeus in Book 5 that he would have suffered among the grim heaps of the dead if his feet had not carried him away (5.885–887). Terror and Rout, who drove the armies on with him in Book 4 (4.439–440), are his grooms here (see [[terror]], [[rout]] and [[ares-and-rout]]); in a simile of Book 13 Rout is his “dear son”, who goes to war with him from Thrace (13.298–303).

**Disarmed.** Athena, afraid that Zeus' anger will fall on all the gods, runs out from her throne, takes the helmet from his head, the shield from his shoulders and the spear from his hand, and calls him a madman: if he goes, Zeus will come back to Olympus and seize them one after another, the guilty and the innocent, and many a better man than Ascalaphus has been killed or will be (15.121–141; see [[athena]] and [[zeus]]). “So saying, she sat furious Ares down on his throne” (15.142; see [[furious]]). It is the second time that she has kept him from the battle: in Book 5 she led him out of it by the hand, so that the two of them might avoid the wrath of Zeus (5.29–36).

**In the battle.** On the field Ares is the measure of warriors and of war. Meges is “the equal of Ares” (15.302; see [[equal-of-ares]] and [[meges]]), and Hector at the ships “raged as when spear-brandishing Ares, or deadly fire, / rages in the mountains” (15.605–606; see [[spear-brandishing]] and [[hector]]). Ajax calls the Danaans “attendants of Ares” as he holds the deck alone (15.733; see [[attendants-of-ares]] and [[ajax]]).
