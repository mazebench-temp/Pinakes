---
summary: Birds in Book 15 are hunters and prey in two similes. Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, the dove-killer, “the swiftest of winged birds” (15.237–238), and Hector falls on the ships like a tawny eagle swooping on a flock of geese, cranes and swans feeding by a river (15.690–692). No bird of omen flies in the book; Zeus gives his sign by thunder (15.377).
---

**Hunters.** πετεηνά, “winged ones”, are the birds of both similes. Apollo, sent to revive Hector and to rout the Achaeans with the aegis, goes down from the mountains of Ida “like a hawk, a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]] and [[hawk]]). Near the end of the book Hector makes for the ships “as a tawny eagle swoops on a tribe of winged birds as they feed beside a river” (15.690–691; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]] and [[eagle]]). Both are birds of prey, and both are on the Trojan side.

**Prey.** The flock the eagle falls on is the flock of Book 2, “geese or cranes or long-necked swans” (15.692 = 2.460), which there pictured the Achaean army pouring onto the plain (2.459–466; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]], [[geese]], [[cranes]] and [[swans]]). The hawk's prey is in its name: φασσοφόνος, “dove-killer”, from φάσσα, the wood pigeon (see [[doves]]).

**No omens.** In Books 8, 12 and 13 eagles flew over the armies as signs (8.247–252, 12.200–209, 13.821–823). In Book 15 the sign is thunder: Zeus thunders loudly in answer to Nestor's prayer, and the Trojans, hearing it, spring on the Argives more fiercely (15.377–380; see [[prayer]]).
