summary: Twenty-seven speeches in Book 15 close with the formula, and nearly all turn at once to the effect, among the gods in feelings and obedience, among men in the action of the battle. Three rallies end in the same verse, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man”; Patroclus' speech ends with his feet carrying him off; and the last closing of the book is Ajax's: “He spoke, and in his fury pressed on with his sharp spear”.
grc: ["ὣς εἰπόντα"]
---

**Among the gods.** The book opens with a chain of speeches on Olympus and Ida, and the closing verses register how each is taken: “So he spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera shuddered” (15.34), “So she spoke, and the father of men and gods smiled” (15.47; see [[smiling-at-him]]), “So she spoke, and Ares struck both his sturdy thighs” (15.113). Obedience is told in a formula of its own: Hera “did not disobey” (15.78), swift Iris “did not disobey” (15.168), and “Apollo did not fail to heed his father” (15.236; see [[peitho]]). Poseidon's speech ends in a departure, ὣς εἰπών (15.218), and he plunges into the sea.

**Among men.** In the battle the formula leads straight into the act: “So saying, he threw his shining spear at Ajax” (15.429), or into the obedience of the many, “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (15.300), the verse with which the council and the army obeyed Diomedes and Poseidon in Book 14 (14.133, 14.378). Three exhortations end in the verse of the rally, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man”: Hector's (15.500), Ajax's (15.514) and Nestor's (15.667; see [[exhortation]]). After Nestor's prayer the answer comes in the closing verse itself: “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor thundered loudly” (15.377; see [[prayer]]).

**Two endings.** Patroclus leaves Eurypylus in a verse found only here, “So he spoke, and his feet carried him off” (τὸν μὲν ἄρʼ ὣς εἰπόντα πόδες φέρον, 15.405; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]): the speaker is carried out of the story while the narrator stays at the wall. The half-verse returns when Antilochus' feet carry him, weeping, to Achilles with the news of Patroclus' death (τὸν μὲν δάκρυ χέοντα πόδες φέρον, 17.700). The book's last speech is Ajax's, and its closing is the short form, ἦ καί, “He spoke, and in his fury pressed on with his sharp spear” (15.742; see [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]). Hector's last speech before it, at the stern of Protesilaus' ship, ends with the Trojans: “So he spoke, and they sprang more fiercely on the Argives” (15.726).
