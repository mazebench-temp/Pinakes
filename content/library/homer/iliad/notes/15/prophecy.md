summary: Zeus foretells to Hera the course of the war from the death of Patroclus to the fall of Ilios (15.56–77), a forecast that ancient editors rejected as unlike Homer. The narrator foretells Hector's death at the height of his glory (15.610–614), and the men on both sides read the will of Zeus in what they see.

**Zeus' forecast.** In Book 8 Zeus told Hera only the next stage of the war, that Hector would not stop until Achilles was roused beside the ships over the dead Patroclus, “for so it is decreed” (8.470–477; see [[thesphaton]]). In Book 15 he tells the whole of it, step by step, in the future tense: Achilles will rouse Patroclus, Hector will kill Patroclus after Patroclus has killed many, among them Sarpedon, and Achilles will kill Hector (κτενεῖ … κτενεῖ, 15.64–68). Then the verb changes to the optative with ἄν, “I would bring about a counterattack from the ships”, to last until the Achaeans take Ilios (15.69–71; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The poem fulfills each step but the last. Patroclus kills Sarpedon (16.480–491); Hector kills Patroclus, and the narrator says of him what Zeus said, that he had killed many (πολέας ὀλέσαντʼ, 15.66; πολέας πεφνόντα, 16.827); Achilles kills Hector (22.326–363). The fall of Troy lies beyond the poem (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]).

**Doubted.** The event does not follow the words in every point. The Achaeans are not driven onto the ships of Achilles, for Hector seizes the ship of Protesilaus (15.63–64, 15.704–706); and it is Patroclus who begs Achilles to send him out (16.36–45), though Achilles in the end bids him rise (ὄρσεο, 16.126). Walter Leaf reports that Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus athetized the whole passage (15.56–77), and that Zenodotus left out 15.64–77, saying that the verses were “like a Euripidean prologue”. Leaf thought the objections to 15.64–71 decisive: such a forecast of the course of the war is not Homer's practice, and it does not agree with the event (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 15.56; see [[aristarchus]] and [[zenodotus]]). The translation keeps the verses and notes the same departures (see its note on 15.56–77).

**Short-lived.** The narrator foretells Hector's death in the moment of his greatest glory, with Zeus himself defending him from the upper air: “Short-lived he was to be; / for already she was rousing his fated day against him, / Pallas Athena, under the strength of the son of Peleus” (15.612–614; see [[short-lived]] and [[hector]]). μόρσιμον ἦμαρ, “the fated day”, is set in motion by a goddess, and the forecast names her part. In Book 22 Athena comes to Hector in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus and draws him to stand against Achilles (22.226–231), and Achilles tells him that Pallas Athena will soon subdue him by his spear (22.270–271).

**Reading the signs.** The men in the battle read the will of Zeus from what they see, and read it rightly for the day. Thoas sees Hector risen, “escaping the fates”, and knows that “one of the gods has rescued and saved / Hector”, for “not without / loud-thundering Zeus does he stand forth as champion” (15.286–293; see [[thoas]]). When Teucer's bowstring snaps, he says a god is cutting short their plans, and Ajax agrees that “a god has confounded them, begrudging the Danaans” (15.467–473; see [[teucer]] and [[ajax]]). Hector reads the same sign the other way: “Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus” (15.488–493). The poem leaves one sign open. Zeus thunders, “hearing the prayers of the old son of Neleus”, who has begged him to ward off the pitiless day; the Trojans hear the thunder too, and spring on the Argives more fiercely (15.370–380; see [[nestor]]).
