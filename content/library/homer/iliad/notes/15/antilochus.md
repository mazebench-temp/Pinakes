**Spurred on.** “Antilochus, no other of the Achaeans is younger than you, / nor swifter of foot, nor as valiant as you in fighting” (15.569–570; see [[menelaus]]). Menelaus' praise names what the poem gives him, youth and speed. He leaps out from the champions and throws, “glancing around him; and the Trojans shrank back / from the man as he threw; and he did not throw the shaft in vain” (15.573–575). The verses are those in which Odysseus, avenging Leucus, threw at the Trojans and killed Democoon, Priam's bastard son from Abydos (15.573–575 ≈ 4.496–498; see [[odysseus]] and [[democoon]]). The spear strikes Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, “as he was going into the war, in the chest beside the nipple. / He fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes” (15.577–578; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]], [[fell-with-a-thud]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Melanippus was Hector's cousin, whom Priam honored like his own children; Hector had just rebuked him and led him back into the fight (15.545–559). Strabo pairs the two men whom these verses kill, Melanippus who grazed his cattle at Percote and Democoon who came from Abydos, to show that Priam's rule reached along the coast of the Hellespont (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[percote]] and [[strabo]]).

**The dog and the fawn.** “And Antilochus sprang at him like a dog that darts / at a wounded fawn, which, as it leapt from its lair, / a hunter has hit with his shot, and loosened its limbs” (15.579–581; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). Then the narrator turns to the dead man: “so at you, Melanippus, Antilochus, steadfast in battle, leapt / to strip your armor” (15.582–583). The address in the second person is given elsewhere to Menelaus and Patroclus, and earlier in this book to Apollo (15.365); here it goes to a Trojan at the moment of his death (see [[apostrophe]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). μενεχάρμης, “steadfast in battle”, is Antilochus' epithet (13.396, 23.419).

**The flight.** But Hector comes running through the combat to meet him (15.583–584; see [[hector]]):

{{quote:31288f9f-4d1e-5f72-b8c7-16c3b541f417}}

“Swift warrior though he was” (θοός περ ἐὼν πολεμιστής): in Book 5 the same words told how Aeneas would not stand against Antilochus and Menelaus together (5.571); the young man who helped to make Aeneas give way now gives way to Hector. The hunter's dog of the first simile has become the beast that kills the dog and must run before the herdsmen gather (see [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]). The last verse is the one in which Ajax, at the end of his slow retreat in Book 11, turned and stood when he reached his companions (11.595 = 15.591; see [[ajax]]). As he runs, the narrator names him by his father, Νεστορίδης, “the son of Nestor” (15.589; see [[nestor]]). He is not named again in the book. In Book 16 he kills Atymnius and fights beside his brother Thrasymedes (16.317–329; see [[thrasymedes]]).
