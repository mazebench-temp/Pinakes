---
summary: Zeus foretells that Achilles will send Patroclus out, that he will kill many and Sarpedon among them, and that Hector will kill him before Ilios (15.64–67). Patroclus, still tending Eurypylus, sees the Trojans storm the wall, strikes his thighs, and runs to persuade Achilles, with Nestor's words, “Good is the persuasion of a companion” (15.390–405).

**Foretold.** In the speech in which he lays out the rest of the war, Zeus says that Apollo will turn the Achaeans back to the ships of Achilles, “and he will rouse up his companion, / Patroclus; and shining Hector will kill him with the spear / before Ilios, after Patroclus has destroyed many young men, / others besides, and among them my son, brilliant Sarpedon” (15.64–67; see [[plan-of-zeus]], [[sarpedon]] and [[hector]]). Zeus had spoken in Book 8 of a fight at the sterns of the ships “over Patroclus dead” (8.475–476); here he gives the killer, the victims and the cause, and the next verse gives the consequence: “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68; see [[achilles]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The listener knows Patroclus' end before he leaves the hut where he is tending a wounded man (see the translation's note on 15.56–77).

**In the hut.** The book takes up the scene left at the start of Book 12, when Patroclus was “tending wounded Eurypylus” (12.1–2):

{{quote:b2929c93-3b8a-5bf5-a53a-2b31210b1de2}}

The whole fight at the wall and among the ships, Books 12 to 14, has passed while he sat there (see [[eurypylus]], [[the-fight-at-the-wall]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). λόγοις, “with talk”, is the only instance of λόγος in the Iliad. φάρμακʼ ἀκέσματʼ, “healing drugs” (ἄκεσμα only here), continue the treatment begun with the bitter root (11.844–848; see [[healing]]).

**He sees the wall stormed.** “But when he saw them rushing at the wall, / the Trojans, and among the Danaans there rose shouting and rout” (15.395–396): the two verses in which the Lapiths at the gate saw the same thing (12.143–144). “He groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs / with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word” (15.397–398): the gesture is Ares' when he hears of his son's death earlier in this book (15.113–114), and Achilles will make it when he sees fire on the ships (16.125). Then he speaks:

{{quote:ea5ab210-2aea-5610-9181-1078a33ca96a}}

νεῖκος, “quarrel”, is the battle itself (see [[neikos]]). The attendant who is to cheer Eurypylus in his place takes up the word for Patroclus' own care, ποτιτερπέτω after ἔτερπε (15.401, 15.393). The last two verses are Nestor's, with the person changed: “who knows if, with a god's help, you might stir his heart” has become “I might stir his heart” (15.403–404 ≈ 11.792–793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]] and [[nestor]]). “So he spoke, and his feet carried him off” (15.405). The narrator follows him only at the start of the next book, where he comes to Achilles weeping like a spring of dark water (16.2–4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]). The persuasion he hopes for is the one Nestor planned, that Achilles send him out in his armor (11.794–803), and Zeus' forecast at the start of this book has already told where it ends.
