---
summary: Zeus foresees that Apollo will rouse “a craven panic” among the Achaeans, and when Apollo shakes the aegis they forget their furious courage and flee, “their courage gone”. Hector names the help that comes from Zeus with the same word, ἀλκή, and Ajax repeats that when men flee “there rises neither fame nor any courage”.
---

ἀλκή and its negative frame the rout. Zeus foretells that Apollo will turn the Achaeans back, “rousing a craven panic among them” (ἀνάλκιδα φύζαν, 15.62; see [[plan-of-zeus]]); when Apollo shakes the aegis in their faces, “they forgot their furious courage” (λάθοντο δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 15.322), and “the Achaeans fled in rout, their courage gone” (ἀνάλκιδες, 15.326; see [[aegis]]). Hector had used the formula of himself: Ajax's stone “stopped me from my furious courage” (15.250). Hector's maxim gives ἀλκή its other sense, the protecting strength of a god: “Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus” (Διὸς … ἀλκή, 15.490; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). On the Achaean side the word ends Ajax's appeal to shame, “but when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage” (15.564; see [[shame-saves-more]]), and Hector and Ajax both call on their men to “remember your furious courage” (15.487, 15.734; see [[exhortation]]).
