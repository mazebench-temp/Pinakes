---
summary: The three gods who once wished to bind Zeus all yield to him in Book 15. Zeus reminds Hera how he bound her and hung her from the sky, Poseidon claims equal honor and then gives way, and Athena restrains Ares for fear of Zeus' anger against all the gods.
passages:
  - 15.18-24 | Hera hung from the sky
  - 15.121-142 | Athena restrains Ares
---

**The plotters.** Achilles told how Hera, Poseidon and Pallas Athena once “wished to put him in bonds”, until Thetis summoned the hundred-handed Briareus to sit beside Zeus (1.396–406). In Book 15 the three meet Zeus' power one after another, and each gives way. When Poseidon threatens incurable anger if Zeus spares Ilios, the gods he names as wanting it sacked are himself, Athena, Hera, Hermes and Hephaestus (15.212–217): the three plotters stand at the head of the party against Troy.

**Hera.** Zeus reminds Hera of the day when he bound her: “around your hands I cast a bond / of gold, unbreakable”, and she hung from the sky with two anvils on her feet (15.18–21; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). In Book 1 Thetis came “and freed him from his bonds” (ὑπελύσαο δεσμῶν, 1.401); here the gods “could not stand beside you and set you free” (λῦσαι δʼ οὐκ ἐδύναντο, 15.22), and any god who tried was hurled from the threshold. The poem does not say whether the hanging came before or after the plot, or whether the one had anything to do with the other.

**Poseidon.** Ordered out of the battle, Poseidon claims that he is Zeus' equal in honor, one of three brothers who divided the world by lot, and says that Zeus should stay in his third share (15.185–199; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Then he yields to Iris' warning, and goes down into the sea (15.206–219; see [[poseidon]]). Zeus is glad of it: a fight between them would not have been settled “without sweat” (15.226–228).

**Athena.** When Ares arms to avenge his son Ascalaphus, Athena, “terribly afraid for all the gods”, takes his helmet, shield and spear and sits him down again (15.113–142; see [[athena]] and [[ares]]). Zeus, she says, will leave the war and come to Olympus “to throw us into tumult, / and he will seize us one after another, the one to blame and the one who is not” (15.136–137). It is what he did when Hera hung from the sky: any god he caught he seized and hurled from the threshold (15.22–24). Hera has drawn the same lesson for the gods: “Fools, we who rage against Zeus in our folly! / Are we still eager to stop him by going near him, / by word or by force?” (15.104–106).
