---
summary: Death is in view on both sides: Hector bids any Trojan who is struck “let him die”, for it is not unseemly to die for one's native land, and threatens death without burial to any who hang back; the Achaeans at the ships think they will not escape, but will perish, and Ajax draws back “thinking he would die”.
grc: [τεθνάτω, τεθνάμεν, θανέειν, θανόντα]
en: [let him die, to die, had died, when he is dead]
---

Hector's exhortation makes death the cost and not the end: “whoever of you / is hit or struck and meets his death and his fate, / let him die” (θάνατον καὶ πότμον … τεθνάτω, 15.494–496), for it is not unseemly “to die” for one's native land (τεθνάμεν, 15.497; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]]). His threat at the ditch sets another death against it: whoever he sees away from the ships, “there and then I will devise his death”, and he will not be given his portion of fire “when he is dead” (15.349–350; see [[cremation]]). On the other side the Achaeans had hoped that Hector “had died at the hands of Ajax” (15.288–289), and at the end they fight thinking “they would not escape from the evil, but would perish” (15.700); Ajax himself gives ground “thinking he would die” (15.728). In the simile of the wave on the ship, the sailors “are carried out from under death by only a little” (15.628; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]).
