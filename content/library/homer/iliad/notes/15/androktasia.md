summary: The killings of Book 15 come in two lists and a run of single scenes. Twice the narrator lists the Trojans' victims in a few verses each, with a history for some of them (15.328–342, 15.515–524); between and after come the deaths of Caletor, Lycophron, Cleitus, Dolops, Melanippus and Periphetes, each with its lineage, its blow and its fall.
passages:
  - 15.328-342 | Man killed man
  - 15.515-524 | Schedius, Laodamas, Otus and Croesmus
---

**The lists.** After Apollo's rout, “man killed man, as the battle scattered” (15.328; see [[polyptoton]]), and eight Achaeans die in fifteen verses: Hector kills Arcesilaus and Stichius, Aeneas Medon and Iasus, Polydamas Mecisteus, Polites Echius, Agenor Clonius, and Paris strikes Deiochus from behind as he flees (15.329–342; see [[hector]], [[aeneas]] and [[paris]]). Two of them are given a history. Medon, the bastard son of Oileus, lived in Phylace as an exile for killing a kinsman of his stepmother, and Iasus was a leader of the Athenians, “the son of Sphelus, son of Bucolus” (15.333–338; see [[medon]], [[iasus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The second list, after the exhortations of Hector and Ajax, sets killing against killing: Hector kills Schedius, Ajax Laodamas, Polydamas Otus of Cyllene, and Meges, missing Polydamas, kills Croesmus (15.515–524; see [[schedius-son-of-perimedes]], [[laodamas]], [[otus-of-cyllene]] and [[croesmus]]).

**The single scenes.** Between and after the lists, each death is told with the parts of the type-scene (see the article above). The victim's place and kin: Caletor is Hector's cousin, son of Clytius (15.419–422); Lycophron, son of Mastor, an exile from Cythera who lived in Ajax's house (15.430–439); Cleitus, son of Peisenor, the companion of Polydamas (15.445–446); Dolops, “the strongest son that Lampus, / son of Laomedon, fathered” (15.525–527); Melanippus, who grazed his cattle in Percote and lived beside Priam (15.546–551); and Periphetes of Mycenae, the son of Copreus, Eurystheus' messenger, and “a far worse father … a better son” (15.638–643; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). The blow and the fall are told in the formulas: “He fell with a thud” (15.421, 15.524, 15.578; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]), “and his limbs were loosened” (15.435; see [[loosened-his-limbs]]), “and darkness covered his eyes” (15.578; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Almost every death brings a fight for the body or the armor (see [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).
