summary: Cattle in Book 15 are mostly in the similes of the fight at the ships: a herd scattered by two beasts in the night, a herdsman killed beside his cattle, and a countless herd in a marsh on which a lion falls (15.323–326, 15.586–588, 15.630–636). The Trojan Melanippus once grazed cattle at Percote (15.547–548), and Nestor prays by the thigh pieces of oxen and sheep burned to Zeus in Argos (15.372–375).
grc: ["βοῶν ἀγέλην"]
---

**In the similes.** The herd is the Achaean army, and each time something falls on it that it cannot resist. When Apollo shakes the aegis, the Achaeans flee “as a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep is thrown into confusion by two wild beasts in the dead of black night, coming on them suddenly when the herdsman is not there” (15.323–325; see [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]). When Hector falls on them at the ships, they flee like countless cattle grazing in a marsh, whose young herdsman does not yet know how to fight a wild beast “over the slaughter of a cow with curving horns”; the lion leaps into the middle and devours one cow, and the rest all flee (15.630–636; see [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[with-curving-horns]]). Once the herdsman himself is the victim: Antilochus flees before Hector like a wild beast that has killed a dog or a herdsman beside his cattle (βουκόλον ἀμφὶ βόεσσι, 15.586–588; see [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]).

**The herdsman of Percote.** Of Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, the narrator says: “For a time he grazed shambling cattle in Percote, while the enemy were still far away” (15.547–548). When the Achaean ships came he went back to Ilios and lived beside Priam (15.549–551; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]], [[percote]] and [[shambling]]). The princes of the Troad herd their cattle in the Iliad, and the war finds them at it or calls them home from it: Andromache's seven brothers were killed among their cattle and white sheep (6.421–424), Anchises was tending cattle when Aphrodite bore him Aeneas (5.313), and Apollo himself once herded the cattle of Laomedon in the glens of Ida (21.448–449). Antilochus kills Melanippus, and the beast of the next simile has killed a herdsman (15.576–588).

**Sacrifice.** Nestor's prayer recalls the sacrifices of home: “Father Zeus, if ever anyone in Argos rich in wheat, burning to you the fat thigh pieces of ox or sheep, prayed that he might return” (15.372–374; see [[thigh-pieces]], [[sheep]] and [[prayer]]). Zeus thunders, and the Trojans take the thunder for themselves (15.377–380).

**Ox-eyed.** βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “ox-eyed queen Hera”, shudders at Zeus' threat at the beginning of the book, and Zeus, answering her oath, addresses her by the same title (15.34, 15.49; see [[ox-eyed]]).
