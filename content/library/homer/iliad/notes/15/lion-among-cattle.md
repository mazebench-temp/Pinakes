summary: Hector falls on the Achaeans like a deadly lion on countless cattle grazing in a marsh, whose young herdsman keeps to the front and the rear of the herd; the lion leaps into the middle and devours one cow, and the rest all flee. So the Achaeans flee before Hector and Zeus, and he kills only Periphetes (15.630–652).

{{quote:e4ee404b-1988-5007-95a0-e0036b2ff9e2}}

αὐτὰρ ὅ γʼ ὥς τε λέων ὀλοόφρων βουσὶν ἐπελθών, / αἵ ῥά τʼ ἐν εἱαμενῇ ἕλεος μεγάλοιο νέμονται / μυρίαι: “But he, like a deadly lion coming upon cattle that graze in the low wet ground of a great marsh, countless” (15.630–632). ὀλοόφρων is “with deadly mind”. The εἱαμενή is low meadow by a river or marsh, and the words ἐν εἱαμενῇ ἕλεος μεγάλοιο are those of the place where the poplar of Simoeisius grew (4.483; see [[the-poplar]]). The herd is μυρίαι, “countless”, and among them is a herdsman, νομεὺς οὔ πω σάφα εἰδὼς / θηρὶ μαχέσσασθαι ἕλικος βοὸς ἀμφὶ φονῇσιν, “who does not yet well know how to fight a wild beast over the slaughter of a cow with curving horns” (15.632–633; see [[with-curving-horns]]). He is young, or new to the work: οὔ πω, “not yet”.

The herdsman walks “always with the first and the last of the cattle, keeping in step with them” (ὁμοστιχάει, found only here), “but the lion leaps into the middle and devours a cow, and they all flee in fear” (15.634–636). In Book 11 the lion routed a whole herd at night, and Agamemnon, whom it pictured, kept killing the hindmost (11.172–178); this lion goes where the herdsman is not, into the middle, and takes one beast while the rest scatter.

The tenor: ὣς τότʼ Ἀχαιοὶ / θεσπεσίως ἐφόβηθεν ὑφʼ Ἕκτορι καὶ Διὶ πατρὶ / πάντες, ὃ δʼ οἶον ἔπεφνε Μυκηναῖον Περιφήτην, “so then the Achaeans fled in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus, all of them; but he killed only Periphetes of Mycenae” (15.636–638). The vehicle and the tenor are matched number for number: the whole herd flees and one cow is eaten; all the Achaeans flee and one man dies. Periphetes, son of Copreus, the herald of Eurystheus, a far better man than his father, trips on the rim of his shield as he turns, falls on his back, and Hector runs up and fixes his spear in his chest, close to his companions, who cannot help him, “for they themselves feared brilliant Hector greatly” (15.638–652; see [[periphetes-of-mycenae]], [[copreus]] and [[shield]]). The companions stand as the herdsman stands, near and useless.

The simile is the last of three in quick succession for Hector's onset at the ships. The Danaans first hold like a rock by the sea (15.618–622; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]); Hector falls on them like a wave on a ship, and their hearts are torn (15.624–629; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]); and now they flee like cattle before a lion. In three likenesses the Danaans go from standing firm to rout. See [[lion]] and [[cattle]].
