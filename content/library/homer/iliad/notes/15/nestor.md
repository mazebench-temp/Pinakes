His son Antilochus kills Melanippus and runs from Hector (15.568–591).

**The prayer.** Apollo has thrown down the wall, and the Achaeans halt by the ships, calling to one another and lifting their hands to all the gods; “and above all Nestor, the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans, / prayed, stretching out his hands to the starry sky” (15.367–371; see [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]], [[starry]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]):

{{quote:250a1c90-5d8d-5fc8-803e-1dc152f49345}}

“Father Zeus, if ever” opens Thetis' prayer to Zeus in Book 1 (1.503), and the argument is Chryses': remember what was given, and give in return (1.39–41; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The gifts Nestor names are not his own but those of every man who, back in “Argos rich in wheat”, burned the fat thigh pieces and prayed to come home (see [[argos]], [[rich-in-wheat]], [[thigh-pieces]] and [[nostos]]). ὑπέσχεο καὶ κατένευσας, “you promised and nodded your assent” (15.374), is the phrase in which Agamemnon complained of the promise Zeus had broken, that he would sack Ilios and sail home (2.112–113 = 9.19–20). Nestor himself told the army in Book 2 that Zeus nodded his assent, flashing lightning on the right, on the day they embarked (2.350–353). Now he asks only that the god “ward off the pitiless day” (νηλεὲς ἦμαρ; see [[olympian]]).

**The thunder.** “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor thundered loudly, / hearing the prayers of the old son of Neleus” (15.377–378; see [[counselor]], [[son-of-neleus]] and [[neleus]]). The narrator says that Zeus heard. But it is the Trojans who act on the sign: “And the Trojans, when they heard the thunder of aegis-bearing Zeus, / sprang on the Argives more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (15.379–380; see [[charme]]). The answer to Nestor's prayer is held back. Zeus has resolved to give Hector glory until a ship burns, and from then on to bring about the counterattack from the ships (15.596–602; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Implored by their parents.** The Trojans reach the first ships, and the Argives give way to the huts but do not scatter, “for shame held them / and fear” (15.653–658; see [[aidos]] and [[deos]]). “And above all Gerenian Nestor, the guardian of the Achaeans, / begged each man by his parents, imploring him” (15.659–660):

{{quote:38f3daa7-082d-54f7-98a9-b9dc44c804e4}}

The first verse is the one with which Ajax has just urged on the Argives (15.561 = 15.661; see [[ajax]] and [[exhortation]]). Nestor's appeal goes further. λίσσεθʼ … γουνούμενος, “begged … imploring”, and γουνάζομαι, “I implore you” (15.660, 15.665), are the words of supplication, used by the old man to his own soldiers (see [[supplication]]). Hector has told the Trojans that a man who dies defending his land leaves his wife and children safe, and his house and allotment unharmed (15.494–499; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]]); Nestor's men are to stand for wives, children, wealth and parents across the sea, “both he whose parents are living and he whose parents are dead” (15.664; see [[the-city-and-the-household]]). τῶν ὕπερ … οὐ παρεόντων, “for their sake, though they are not here” (15.665): in the Odyssey the ghost of Elpenor begs Odysseus in the same words, by his wife, his father and his son, who are absent (*Odyssey* 11.66–68). The verse that closes the appeal, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (15.667), has closed Hector's and Ajax's speeches before it (15.500, 15.514). Athena then thrusts the mist from the Achaeans' eyes, and Ajax goes up on the decks (15.668–678; see [[athena]]).

**His son.** Antilochus, “the son of Nestor” (Νεστορίδης, 15.589), is spurred on by Menelaus, kills Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, and runs from Hector like a beast that has killed a dog or a herdsman (15.568–591; see [[antilochus]]).
