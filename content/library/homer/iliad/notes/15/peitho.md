---
summary: Obedience among the gods: Hera and Iris “did not disobey” Zeus, Iris and Apollo quickly obeyed Hera's summons, and Poseidon is warned what follows if he “will not obey” — until Iris persuades him. Among men Thoas' counsel is obeyed at once, and Patroclus goes to Achilles trusting in the persuasion of a companion.
grc: [πεπιθοῦσα, πιθέσθην, ἐπιπείσεται, ἐπιπείσεαι]
en: [winning over, had quickly obeyed]
---

**Among the gods.** The book's divine chain of command is a chain of obedience. Hera, bidden to summon Iris and Apollo, “did not disobey” (οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε, 15.78), and when the two come at her word Zeus is not angry, “since they had quickly obeyed the words of his dear wife” (πιθέσθην, 15.156; see [[cholos]]). Iris, sent to Poseidon, “did not disobey” (15.168), and Apollo “did not fail to heed his father” (15.236). Poseidon is the test: “if he will not obey my words, but disregards them” (ἐπιπείσεται, 15.162 = 15.178; see [[epos]]), let him consider Zeus' strength. He does not obey the threat; he yields to Iris' persuasion (15.200–211; see [[the-hearts-of-good-men-can-be-turned]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). Zeus remembers how Hera once won over the storms against Heracles (πεπιθοῦσα θυέλλας, 15.26; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]).

**Among men.** Thoas ends his counsel “let us all do as I say” (πειθώμεθα, 15.294), and “they listened closely to him and obeyed” (15.300; see [[thoas]]). Patroclus hopes to stir Achilles “by persuading him” (παρειπών, 15.404; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). In the simile of the stallion the horse runs “trusting in his splendor” (ἀγλαΐηφι πεποιθώς, 15.267; see [[the-stallion]]), the perfect of the verb.
