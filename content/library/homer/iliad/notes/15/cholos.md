summary: Zeus' anger now governs the war: he will not cease from it until Achilles' wish is fulfilled, and the gods fear a greater anger from him if Ares goes to avenge his son. Athena bids Ares let go his anger; Poseidon withdraws “avoiding the sheer anger” of Zeus, but promises “incurable anger” if Troy is spared; and Zeus foretells the anger of Achilles for Patroclus, in which he will kill Hector.
grc: [ἐχολώσατο]
---

**The anger of Zeus.** Zeus tells Hera that after Hector's death he will bring a counterattack from the ships until the Achaeans take Ilios, “But until then I do not cease from my anger” (οὔτʼ … παύω χόλον, 15.69–72), nor will he let any immortal defend the Danaans, until the wish of the son of Peleus is fulfilled (15.72–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The gods know what it means. Had Athena not stopped Ares, “another, still greater and more grievous, / anger and wrath would have come from Zeus upon the immortals” (χόλος καὶ μῆνις, 15.121–122; see [[menis]] and [[would-have-had-not]]), and she tells Ares, “So now I bid you let go your anger for your own son” (15.138; see [[ares]] and [[poine]]). When Iris and Apollo come quickly at Hera's word, Zeus “was not angry in his heart” (οὐδέ … ἐχολώσατο θυμῷ, 15.155).

**Between the brothers.** Poseidon resents being rebuked “with angry words” (χολωτοῖσιν ἐπέεσσιν, 15.210) and gives way with a threat: if Zeus spares Ilios, “there will be incurable anger” between them (ἀνήκεστος χόλος, 15.217; see [[the-hearts-of-good-men-can-be-turned]]). Zeus, for his part, says that Poseidon has gone into the sea “avoiding the sheer anger / of mine” (χόλον αἰπύν, 15.223–224).

**The anger to come.** Zeus' forecast ends in another anger: Hector will kill Patroclus, “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (τοῦ δὲ χολωσάμενος, 15.68; see [[achilles]]). The anger of Achilles against Agamemnon, which began the poem, will be replaced by an anger for his companion.
