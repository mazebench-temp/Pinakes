---
summary: Plutarch quotes Zeus' threat on waking as the poet's own lesson that a husband's favor won by drugs and guile turns to anger, and Poseidon's “All things have been divided in three” against Pompey and Caesar, two men for whom the Roman empire was not enough. In the Table Talk the spears that long for flesh are an example of the poets' metaphors of motion.
---

- **Zeus' threat.** In *How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* Plutarch rejects the allegorists who made Hera's adornment for Zeus and the enchantments of the strap a cleansing of the air, for the poet gives the solution himself. In the story of Hera he shows that favor and intercourse won from husbands by drugs and witchcraft and guile are not only short-lived, cloying and unstable, but turn to enmity and anger when the pleasure fades; for such are Zeus' threats to her: “so that you may see whether love and the bed will help you, / in which you lay with me, coming away from the gods, and deceived me” (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 19e–20b, quoting 15.32–33, with ἴδῃς for ἴδῃ): [[plato-zeus-deceived]] and the Book 14 note.
- **The threefold division.** When Crassus perished among the Parthians, Plutarch writes, the great obstacle to civil war was gone, for fear of him had kept Pompey and Caesar to fair terms; once fortune had removed the third contestant, each at once began to oil himself and dust his hands against the other, as the comic poet says. “So small a thing is fortune compared with human nature. It does not satisfy its desire, since so great a depth of empire and extent of territory could not contain two men; though they heard and read that ‘all things have been divided in three’ among the gods, ‘and each has his share of honor’, they did not think the Roman empire enough for themselves, who were two” (*Pompey* 53.6–7, quoting 15.189). In the Iliad the division is the ground of a quarrel, which Poseidon gives up only at Iris' warning (15.185–217); Plutarch holds it up as a picture of contentment. See [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]] and [[callimachus-against-the-lots]].
- **The spears that long for flesh.** In the *Table Talk* Ammonius divides dancing into movement, pose and indication, and compares the poets: they name things plainly, but for vividness and imitation they use words that imitate sounds, and metaphors, saying that broken streams “babble” and “plash”, that missiles fly “longing to glut themselves on flesh”, and that an evenly balanced battle “had its heads level” (*Table Talk* 9.15; the verses are 15.317 = 11.574 and 11.72): [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]].
