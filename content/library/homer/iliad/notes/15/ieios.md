summary: When Apollo has thrown down the Achaean wall as easily as a boy scatters sand, the narrator turns to the god: “so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery / of the Argives.”
---

“And he threw down the wall of the Achaeans / very easily, as when a boy beside the sea throws down the sand, / who, when he has made playthings in his childishness, / scatters them again with his feet and hands in play: / so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery / of the Argives, and roused panic among them” (15.361–366; [[apollo]], [[phoebus]], [[the-achaean-wall]], [[apostrophe]]). The address comes at the end of the simile: the god who plays with the wall as a child with sand is spoken to directly, in the moment of his ease. The wall, the long labor of the Argives (7.436–441), is undone by a god in a few verses, as Poseidon and Apollo will wash it away after the war (12.17–33).
