ἀλλʼ ὥς τʼ ὀρνίθων πετεηνῶν αἰετὸς αἴθων / ἔθνος ἐφορμᾶται ποταμὸν πάρα βοσκομενάων / χηνῶν ἢ γεράνων ἢ κύκνων δουλιχοδείρων: “but as a tawny eagle swoops on a tribe of winged birds as they feed beside a river, geese or cranes or long-necked swans” (15.690–692). The words ὀρνίθων πετεηνῶν open both similes, and the third verse is the second verse of Book 2 (2.460). In Book 2 the many tribes of birds were the Achaean army itself, flying over the meadow by the Cayster and settling with loud cries as the men poured out onto the plain of the Scamander (2.459–466). Here the flock is a single ἔθνος, feeding by a river, and the eagle that falls on it is Hector, making for the ships with Zeus' hand at his back (15.693–695; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]). The army that went out to war like the birds of the Asian meadow is now the flock under the eagle.
