summary: Hera sends Iris to Ida with Apollo, and Zeus sends her on to order Poseidon out of the battle. She gives the order in Zeus' own words, and when Poseidon answers that he is Zeus' equal, she asks whether she is really to carry back so harsh a word, since the Erinyes always follow the elder; Poseidon yields and praises a messenger who knows what is due.

**Called to Ida.** Zeus has Hera call “Iris to come” with Apollo, so that she may go among the Achaeans and tell Poseidon to cease from war and go home (15.55–58; see [[zeus]] and [[poseidon]]). Hera calls her out of the house on Olympus as the one “who is the messenger among the immortal gods” (μετάγγελος, 15.144; of Iris again at 23.199), and the two fly to Ida and stand before Zeus on Gargarus, who is not angry, “since they had quickly obeyed the words of his dear wife” (15.143–156; see [[hera]], [[apollo]] and [[gargarus]]).

**The order.** Zeus speaks to her first: “Go, swift Iris, to lord Poseidon, / take him all this message, and do not be a false messenger” (15.158–159). His command opens as it does each time he sends her (8.399, 11.186, 24.144), and ψευδάγγελος, “false messenger”, occurs only here. “So he spoke, and swift Iris, wind-footed, did not disobey” (15.168; see [[wind-footed]]), the verse of her errand to Hector (11.195), and she flies down from the mountains of Ida to Ilios as swiftly as snow or hail flies from the clouds under the North Wind (15.169–172; see [[snow-and-hail]] and [[boreas]]).

**Word for word.** She gives Poseidon the message as Zeus spoke it, turning “I” into “he”: “A message for you, dark-haired earth-holder, / I have come here bringing, from aegis-bearing Zeus” (15.174–175), and then the order and the threat, ending with Zeus' claim to be the stronger and the elder (15.176–183 ≈ 15.160–167; see [[message-repeated]] and [[messenger-scene]]). So in Book 8 she gave Hera and Athena Zeus' threat in his own words (8.413–422).

**Her counsel.** Poseidon answers that he is Zeus' equal in honor and will not live by his mind (15.185–199; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Iris does not carry the answer back at once:

In Book 8 she added an insult of her own to Zeus' message (8.423–424); here she adds advice. The hearts of good men can be turned as, in Phoenix's words, “even the gods themselves can be turned” (9.497; see [[the-hearts-of-good-men-can-be-turned]] and [[litai]]), and the Erinyes, who fulfill a parent's curse, side with the elder brother (see [[the-erinyes-follow-the-elder]] and [[erinyes]]). Poseidon accepts the rebuke: “Goddess Iris, this word you have spoken is fair and right; / it is a good thing, too, when a messenger knows what is due” (ὅτʼ ἄγγελος αἴσιμα εἰδῇ, 15.206–207; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]] and [[aisa]]). He yields, with a threat of his own, and goes down into the sea (15.211–219). The poem does not tell her return; Zeus' next words are to Apollo (15.220).
