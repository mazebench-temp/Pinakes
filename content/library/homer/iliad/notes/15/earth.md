summary: Hera calls Earth to witness her oath to Zeus, with Heaven and the water of Styx; in Poseidon's account of the lots the earth belongs to all three brothers alike; and at the ships it is the native land the fighters long for and the ground that runs black with blood.
# γῆν (15.24) is the only accusative of the short form γῆ in Homer.
grc: [γῆν]
---

**A witness.** “Let Earth now be witness to this, and the wide Heaven above, / and the down-flowing water of Styx” (15.36–37; see [[styx]], [[sky]], [[oath]] and [[hera]]). Earth and Heaven are capitalized here in the digitized Iliad, as powers called on by name (see the translation's note on 15.36–38). Agamemnon called on the rivers and the earth to witness the truce (3.276–280), and in Book 14 Hera swore to Sleep with one hand on “the bountiful earth” (14.271–274). Calypso swears the same words to Odysseus (*Odyssey* 5.184–186).

**Hurled to earth.** When Zeus hung Hera from the sky, any god he caught he would hurl from the threshold, “until he reached / the earth with little strength left” (15.23–24; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). γῆν, the short form of the word, is rare in Homer beside γαῖα; elsewhere in the Iliad it is Earth called on in oaths, Γῆ (3.104, 19.259), and the life-giving earth that holds down even the strong (21.63).

**Common to all.** When the sons of Cronus drew lots, Poseidon got the sea, Hades the misty darkness and Zeus the sky, “but the earth is still common to all, and high Olympus” (15.190–193; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]], [[poseidon]] and [[olympus]]). The battlefield is part of no brother's lot, and Poseidon claims his right to be there.

**Ground and native land.** Below the gods the earth is the ground of the fighting. Spears thrown short “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (15.316–317), a verse that Aristotle cites as Homer's way of making lifeless things alive (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1412a1; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]]). Medon lived “away from his native land” after a killing (15.335), and Protesilaus' ship “did not take him back again to his native land” (15.705–706; see [[protesilaus]]). Hector tells the Trojans that a man who dies defending his country leaves his wife and children safe, if the Achaeans are gone “to their dear native land” (15.494–499; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]] and [[hector]]); Ajax asks the Achaeans whether they expect to walk home to their native land if Hector takes the ships, and reminds them that they sit with their backs to the sea, “far from our native land” (15.504–505, 15.739–740; see [[ajax]] and [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]). When the armies meet at the stern of Protesilaus' ship, “the black earth ran with blood” (15.715; see [[black-earth]]).
