---
summary: Zeus reminds Hera how she won over the storms with the help of the North Wind to drive Heracles to Cos, and Iris flies down from Ida like snow or hail under the rush of the North Wind, “born of the clear sky”.
---

**Hera's storm.** Among the wrongs Zeus throws at Hera is the storm she raised against Heracles on his way home from Troy: “whom you, winning over the storms with the help of the North Wind, / sent over the barren sea, devising evil, / and then you carried him off to well-peopled Cos” (15.26–28; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]], [[heracles]] and [[barren-sea]]). The last verse is Sleep's (14.255), but in Sleep's telling Hera roused “blasts of grievous winds over the sea” (14.254) and no wind is named. Only Zeus gives the North Wind a part in it. πεπιθοῦσα, “winning over”, makes the storms persons whom Hera persuades (see [[peitho]]).

**The simile.** Sent from Ida to Poseidon, Iris goes down to Ilios, and the poet compares her flight to the North Wind's snow (see [[snow-and-hail]]):

{{quote:27f1a045-0559-5b9d-9c77-8e22fc6dfc9f}}

αἰθρηγενής, “born of the clear sky”, is the North Wind's epithet here and in a simile of Book 19 (see [[born-of-the-clear-sky]]), where the Achaeans pour out from the ships like thick snowflakes driven by the North Wind (19.357–358); the Odyssey has αἰθρηγενέτης (*Odyssey* 5.296). The cold north wind clears the sky as it blows. The swiftness is Iris' own, “so swiftly swift Iris flew in her eagerness” (15.172; see [[iris]]), the words used of Hera's flight from Ida a little before (15.83).
