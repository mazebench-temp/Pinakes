summary: The gods decide that the truce shall be broken, and a Trojan, persuaded, breaks it. Agamemnon lays the guilt on the Trojans and expects Zeus to punish them, and Sthenelus says that the fathers who died at Thebes died by their own recklessness.

**Both at once.** Book 4 gives the plainest case in the poem of an act that belongs to a god and to a man. On Olympus Hera asks and Zeus agrees that Athena shall “contrive that the Trojans … be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.64–72). On the plain Athena finds Pandarus and offers him glory and gifts, and the narrator judges him: “she persuaded the mind of the fool” (τῷ δὲ φρένας ἄφρονι πεῖθεν, 4.104). The goddess supplies the persuasion, and the man supplies the folly. The poem does not choose between them (see [[divine-intervention]]). Plato did choose, and would not let the poets of his city say that the breach came about through Athena and Zeus (see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).

**The Trojans' guilt.** The men on the plain know only half. Agamemnon says “the Trojans have shot you and trampled on the trusty oaths” (4.157), and that Zeus will shake the aegis over them “in resentment at this deceit” (4.168; see [[apate]]). The Achaeans fight the rest of the book on that ground: the Trojans “first did harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.236, 4.271). Pandarus pays in the next book, and the city in the end (see [[a-day-will-come]]). The god whom Agamemnon expects to punish the deceit is the god who allowed it.

**Their own recklessness.** Sthenelus gives the other answer. The fathers who died at Thebes “perished through their own recklessness” (σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, 4.409), in the words the Odyssey uses for Odysseus' companions and that its Zeus uses for mortals who blame the gods for what they bring on themselves (*Odyssey* 1.7, 1.32–34; see [[atasthalie]]). In his account the sons won because they trusted the gods' signs, and the fathers were ruined by their own fault; Agamemnon's story has just told of the ill-omened signs Zeus showed them (4.381).
