**The gods' honors.** Zeus honored Ilios above all cities because its altar never lacked its share: “libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us” (4.46–49; see [[time]] and [[geras]]). The honor of the gods is paid in sacrifice, and a city that pays it is honored in return. It does not save the city.

**The full cup.** Agamemnon's praise of Idomeneus lists the signs of honor: in war, in any other work, and at the feast, where the other Achaeans drink a measured portion but Idomeneus' cup “always stands full, as mine does, to drink whenever your heart bids you” (4.257–263). The honor is a claim on its holder: “rouse yourself for war, and be the man you have always boasted you are” (4.264).

**First at the feast.** The same logic makes the rebuke of Menestheus and Odysseus. “It would be fitting for you two to stand among the foremost and meet the blazing battle; for you two are the first to hear my call to the feast”, where they are glad to eat roast meat and drink honey-sweet wine, “but now you would gladly watch, even if ten columns of Achaeans were fighting in front of you” (4.341–348). In Book 12 Sarpedon says the same to Glaucus, in the same words: kings are honored in Lycia with seats, meat and full cups, and so they must “stand among the foremost and meet the blazing battle” (12.310–321; the line is 4.342 = 12.316). Honor is paid in advance, and repaid in the front rank.

**Disgrace.** The other side of honor is what others will say. Agamemnon fears coming to Argos “in utter disgrace” and imagines the Trojan who will mock him on his brother's tomb (4.171–182; see [[elenchos]]). And honor between generations is disputed: Sthenelus asks the king never to put the fathers “in equal honor” with their sons (4.410; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]). See [[praise-and-blame]].
