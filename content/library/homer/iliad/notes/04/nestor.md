**The battle order.** Agamemnon finds “the clear-voiced orator of the Pylians” (4.293; see [[clear-voiced-orator]]) doing what he advised in Book 2, putting the army in order (2.362–368). Nestor sets his companions around five captains, [[Pelagon]], [[Alastor]], [[Chromius]], [[Haemon]] and [[Bias]], the only Pylian officers the poem names (4.295–296), and arranges them:

{{quote:fe6f576a-3f4e-5d1b-8468-0e04ebfd1e8a}}

The horsemen and chariots go in front, the foot soldiers, “many and brave”, behind them as a bulwark, and the cowards in the middle, where they cannot help fighting. Then he instructs the charioteers (4.301–309). They are to keep their horses in hand and not get tangled in the throng. No one, trusting in his horsemanship and his manhood, is to fight the Trojans alone ahead of the others, or fall back, “for you will be the weaker”; whoever reaches an enemy chariot from his own is to thrust with his spear. “This is how the men of old sacked cities and walls” (4.308). The narrator adds his verdict: “So the old man urged them on, long skilled in wars” (4.310).

Readers have long asked what kind of fighting this is. Elsewhere in the Iliad the chariot mostly carries a warrior to the fight and waits while he fights on foot. Nestor imagines charioteers keeping a line and fighting from their cars, and he ascribes the method to “the men of old” (οἱ πρότεροι). Some have seen in it a memory of Bronze Age chariot warfare, others an old man's lore or the poet's own invention, and the question is still open. What is clear is the lesson: the line matters more than the single champion. The Catalogue said that no one could rival Menestheus at marshaling horses and men except Nestor, “for he was older” (2.553–555); Agamemnon goes from Nestor at work to Menestheus standing idle (4.327). See [[battle-order]], [[chariot]] and [[pylians]].

**The prize of the old.** Agamemnon rejoices at the sight. He wishes that Nestor's knees could keep pace with his spirit, and that old age, “the common lot”, belonged to some other man (4.313–316). Nestor answers:

{{quote:e11a5ff3-2647-523f-8a92-c6c75fdc877b}}

He names the one deed that measures what he has lost, the killing of the Arcadian champion [[Ereuthalion]], whose story he tells in Book 7 (7.132–156; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). He accepts the gods' division of a life, youth then and old age now, for they “never give men all things at once” (4.320); Polydamas turns the same thought against Hector, who cannot have everything at once (13.726–734). And he claims what is left: he will give orders “with counsel and with words” (βουλῇ καὶ μύθοισι), “for that is the prize of the old” (τὸ γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ γερόντων, 4.323). γέρας (“prize”) and γερόντων (“of the old”) sound alike, and the play makes counsel the honor that belongs to age. The spears belong to the younger men (4.324–325). See [[geras]] and [[old-age-and-memory]].

Agamemnon's wish, βίη δέ τοι ἔμπεδος εἴη, “and your strength be steadfast” (4.314), becomes Nestor's own. When he tells the tales of his youth in later books, he wishes in almost the same words that he were young again and his strength steadfast (7.157, 11.670, 23.629).

**The son.** The spears belong to the younger men, and within a hundred and fifty lines one of them is Nestor's son. In the first battle Antilochus makes the first kill of the poem, striking Echepolus on the ridge of his helmet (4.457–462; see [[antilochus]]). Book 4 does not say whose son he is.
