Achilles is named once in Book 4. Agamemnon reviews the whole army without mentioning him, and the Achaeans fight their first battle without a word about their best man. It is Apollo, calling from Pergamus, who names him, as a reason for the Trojans to take heart: the Argives are not made of stone or iron, and Achilles is not fighting (see [[apollo]]). His absence has become the enemy's hope. That is what he asked Thetis to win from Zeus: that the Achaeans be penned in at the ships and slaughtered, so that Agamemnon might learn “his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans” (1.408–412). See [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[achilles-withdraws]].

χόλον θυμαλγέα πέσσει, “he broods over his heart-grieving anger”: πέσσω is to cook or ripen, and so to nurse a feeling and keep it. χόλος is the poem's ordinary word for anger, used beside the μῆνις of the proem (see [[cholos]] and [[menis]]). Phoenix will use the same words of Meleager, who lay beside his wife nursing his heart-grieving anger while enemies attacked his city (9.565; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). The place is the one where Book 1 left him, “sitting beside his swift-sailing ships” (1.488), and where the Catalogue left him lying idle (2.688). See [[thetis]].
