summary: Athena guides Pandarus' arrow to where Menelaus' belt was clasped and his corselet “met it in a double layer”, and the arrow forces its way through the corselet in the verse that pierced Paris' in Book 3 (4.132–136). The armies arm again, and the first battle is fought by “men in bronze corselets”.
grc: [θώρηξ, θωρήσσοντο]
en: [arming]
passages:
  - 4.132-140 | The arrow goes through belt, corselet and band
---

**Double.** Athena guides the arrow “to where the golden clasps of the belt held it together, and the corselet met it in a double layer” (διπλόος ἤντετο θώρηξ, 4.132–133). What was double is not clear. The belt may have lain over the corselet, so that the arrow met two layers; or the corselet's front and back plates may have overlapped at that point; or the corselet itself may have been doubled there. Homer speaks of the γύαλον, the curved plate, of a corselet (5.99, 13.507), and Meges' corselet is “fitted with curved plates” (γυάλοισιν ἀρηρότα, 15.530). A corselet of front and back plates, like the bronze one from a warrior's grave at Argos, would have met in overlapping edges (see [[corselet]]). The same words return when Achilles' spear strikes Polydorus in the back (20.414–415).

**Through.** “Through the elaborate belt it was driven, and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet” (4.135–136). The second verse is 3.358, where Menelaus' spear went through Paris' borrowed corselet; now an arrow does the same to Menelaus'. Even so, the band beneath “protected him most”, and the arrow only grazed the skin (4.137–139; see [[band]]).

**Where is the corselet?** When Menelaus reassures his brother, he names what stopped the arrow: “the gleaming belt … and underneath the kilt and the band” (4.186–187), and Machaon loosens the same three (4.215–216). The corselet the arrow went through is not named. Some take the ζῶμα, “kilt”, to be the corselet or its lower part; others a separate garment worn beneath it (see [[kilt]] and [[belt]]).

**Arming.** The verb θωρήσσω, “put on the corselet”, is the verb of arming. The Cretans are “arming around wise-hearted Idomeneus” when Agamemnon comes to them (θωρήσσοντο, 4.252; see [[the-review]]). The Achaeans who meet the Trojans are χαλκεοθώρηκες, “in bronze corselets” (4.448; see [[armored-in-bronze]]), and the Trojan Antiphus is αἰολοθώρηξ, “of the flashing corselet” (4.489; see [[of-the-flashing-corselet]]). As Diomedes leaps down from his chariot, “the bronze on the breast of the lord rang terribly as he moved” (4.420–421).
