**The archer's god.** Apollo gave Pandarus his bow (2.827; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). Athena, in the likeness of a Trojan, tells Pandarus to shoot, and to “pray to Apollo, the Lycian-born, famed for his bow, that you will offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia” (4.101–103). He prays in her words (4.119–121). Λυκηγενής is taken as “born in Lycia”, or connected with λύκη (“light”) or λύκος (“wolf”); κλυτότοξος is “famed for the bow” (see [[lycian-born]] and [[famed-for-his-bow]]). In Book 1 the narrator said at once that Apollo heard his priest (1.43). Of Pandarus' prayer he says nothing: the goddess who put it in his mouth guides the arrow herself (4.127–133), and Pandarus will die without coming home to pay his vow (5.290–296). See [[pandarus]] and [[hecatomb]].

Agamemnon names him with Zeus and Athena in a wish, as in Book 2: if only all his men had the spirit of the two Ajaxes (4.288–289; 2.371).

It is Apollo's first speech in the Iliad; in Book 1 he acted without speaking. νεμέσησε: he is indignant, as at something that ought not to happen (see [[nemesis]]). He speaks from Pergamus, the citadel of Troy, where he has his temple (5.445–448; see [[pergamus]]), and from there he looks down on the battle again in Book 7 (7.21). The speech is an exhortation (see [[exhortation]]) with two reasons for courage. The Argives are only flesh, not “stone or iron to withstand the flesh-cutting bronze” (ταμεσίχροα; see [[flesh-cutting]]). And their best man is away: “Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, is not fighting, but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (4.512–513). The god tells the Trojans what the plan of Zeus depends on (see [[achilles]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The narrator calls him δεινὸς θεός, “the dread god” (4.514).

Apollo and Athena work the two sides of the field at once: he shouts from the city, and she goes through the throng rousing the Achaeans (4.514–516; see [[athena]]). In Book 7 the two meet by the oak and agree to end the day's fighting with a duel (7.17–43).
