summary: No sacrifice is performed in Book 4, but the book gives the gods' own view of it. Zeus loved Troy because his altar there never lacked its share, the savor of fat that is the gods' prize, and Pandarus vows Apollo a hecatomb of lambs that he will not live to offer.

**The gods' share.** Zeus gives up Troy to Hera with regret, for it was the city he honored most, and he gives the reason in the language of sacrifice:

{{quote:0400527c-ca17-5edc-9e1a-5a8092362062}}

The altar (βωμός), the “equal share of the feast” (δαὶς ἐΐση), the libation (λοιβή) and the κνίση, the savor of burning fat that rises with the smoke, are what the gods receive, and Zeus calls them γέρας, “the prize allotted to us”, the word for the prize of honor a warrior receives from the spoils. The gods' part of a sacrifice is their honor, as a hero's prize is his. Zeus says the same two lines of Hector's offerings in Book 24 (24.69–70), and Hector's burnt thigh-pieces on Ida and on the citadel of Troy are what Zeus remembers when he pities him (22.170–172). See [[altar]], [[equal-feast]], [[geras]], [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[zeus]].

**A vow.** Athena tells Pandarus to pray to Apollo and to vow “that you will offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia” (4.101–103), and he prays in the same words (4.119–121). ῥέζω, “do”, is the verb of sacrificing, as in Book 2 (see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[hecatomb]]). The sacrifice is promised for the future, in return for success; in Book 5 Pandarus dies before he can go home (5.290–296).

**Not a sacrifice.** The lambs of the oath are named once more, when Agamemnon says that “the blood of lambs” is not in vain (4.158). They were killed to seal an oath, not offered to the gods (see [[oath]]).
