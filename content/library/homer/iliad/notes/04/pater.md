**Tydeus and his son.** Agamemnon's rebuke of Diomedes is a story about his father: Tydeus never cowered, came to Mycenae as a guest, went alone to Thebes as an envoy, and killed fifty men in an ambush; “but the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.370–400). Diomedes does not answer. Sthenelus does: “We boast that we are far better than our fathers (πατέρων); … so never put our fathers (πατέρας) in equal honor with us” (4.405–410). See [[better-than-our-fathers]], [[tydeus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]].

**The father of Telemachus.** Answering Agamemnon's rebuke, Odysseus names himself by his son: the king will see “the dear father (πατέρα) of Telemachus mingling with the champions of the horse-taming Trojans” (4.354). He swore the same way in Book 2, “may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus” (2.260). The Iliad names Telemachus only in these two lines. See [[telemachus]].

**A father's gift.** Machaon sucks the blood from Menelaus' wound and spreads on it soothing drugs, “which Chiron once gave to his father in friendship” (τά οἵ ποτε πατρὶ φίλα φρονέων πόρε Χείρων, 4.219). The healer's art passes from the Centaur to Asclepius and from Asclepius to his son. See [[machaon]], [[asclepius]], [[chiron]] and [[healing]].

**Father Zeus.** Zeus is “father” to gods and men alike: Athena sulks “at father Zeus” (4.23), Zeus is “the father of men and gods” (4.68), Agamemnon promises that “father Zeus will be no helper to liars” (4.235) and prays “father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (4.288). See [[father-zeus]] and [[father-of-men-and-gods]].
