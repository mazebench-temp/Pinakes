**Encouragement and rebuke.** Agamemnon's review begins with the two kinds of exhortation side by side (4.232–249; see [[the-review]]). To the eager:

{{quote:1115d2c5-c473-5637-a7a5-eb7acaf756eb}}

The reasons are the broken oath and its penalty. Zeus does not help liars, the oath-breakers will be food for vultures, and their wives and children will be carried off in the Achaean ships: the curse of Book 3 turned into a promise of victory (see [[no-helper-to-liars]] and [[oath]]). To the slack, a rebuke: “Argives, you arrow-fighters, you disgraces, have you no shame?” (see [[arrow-fighters]]). They stand dazed like fawns that have run themselves out, and wait for the Trojans to reach the ships, “to see if the son of Cronus will hold his hand over you” (4.242–249; see [[like-fawns]]).

**Praise as exhortation.** To Idomeneus the king recalls the honor he pays him at the feast of the elders, and ends “But rouse yourself for war, and be the man you have always boasted you are” (4.257–264). The Ajaxes need no urging: “to you two I give no orders, since it is not fitting to urge you on” (4.286). Honor received at the table is a debt paid in the front line, and the same reasoning turns into rebuke against Menestheus and Odysseus, the first to be called to the feast and the last to fight (4.338–348; see [[feast]]).

**Instruction and example.** Nestor's words to his horsemen are orders of tactics, closed with the example of “the men of old” (4.303–309; see [[battle-order]]). Agamemnon's rebuke of Diomedes is an exhortation by paradigm: his father Tydeus fought far ahead of his companions (4.370–400; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Diomedes accepts it, and turns it on Sthenelus: “But come, let us two also think of our furious courage” (4.418).

**Silence.** When the armies advance, “to his own men each one of the leaders gave orders; and the rest went in silence” (4.428–429). The Trojans' allies shout in many tongues (4.433–438).

**The gods.** Ares rouses the Trojans and Athena the Achaeans (4.439). When the Trojans give ground, Apollo shouts to them from Pergamus:

{{quote:c687b0f3-a0ae-5cfd-8a55-a1bda23e67e2}}

He gives a god's reasons: the Argives are flesh, not stone or iron, and Achilles is not fighting. Athena answers by going through the throng, “wherever she saw them holding back”, without a word reported (4.514–516).
