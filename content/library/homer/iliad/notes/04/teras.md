**The star.** Athena darts down from Olympus like the star that Zeus sends “as a portent (τέρας) for sailors or for a wide army of men, a bright one, and many sparks fly from it” (4.75–77). The men who see her take her as a sign, and ask what it means: war again, or friendship (4.79–84). The simile has told the audience what the armies do not know. The goddess herself is the portent, and she is coming to break the truce. See [[shooting-star]] and [[divine-epiphany]].

**Signs at Thebes.** Agamemnon's story of Tydeus is a story of signs heeded and ignored. When Tydeus and Polynices came to Mycenae for allies, “Zeus turned them back, showing ill-omened signs” (παραίσια σήματα, 4.381). After the ambush, Tydeus let one man go home, Maeon, “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 4.398). Sthenelus draws the lesson for his own generation: the Epigonoi took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (τεράεσσι θεῶν καὶ Ζηνὸς ἀρωγῇ, 4.408), while the fathers perished by their own recklessness (4.409; see [[atasthalie]]). The poem does not say what the portents were. See [[tydeus-at-thebes]], [[the-epigonoi]] and [[prophecy]].
