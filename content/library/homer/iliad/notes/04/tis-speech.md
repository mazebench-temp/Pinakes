summary: When Athena comes down like a shooting star, one voice speaks for both armies and asks whether Zeus will bring war again or friendship (4.81–85). Agamemnon imagines another voice, a Trojan who will leap on Menelaus' tomb and mock the Achaeans (4.176–182), the first speech in the poem of what “someone will say”.

**Both armies.** Athena darts down from Olympus like a star, and amazement holds the watchers on both sides:

{{quote:a5bc81d4-8007-531e-a5fb-a80188b56f9c}}

The opening verse is the whole formula (4.81 = 2.271), and the closing verse joins the singular close, ὣς ἄρα τις εἴπεσκεν, to the phrase that made the voice at the oath belong to both armies, “of Achaeans and Trojans alike” (3.297, 3.319; see [[tis-speech]]). The voice reacts to a sign and puts the choice that Zeus himself put to the gods a few lines earlier, war or friendship, in almost his words (4.15–16, 4.82–83). The armies do not know that the gods have already chosen, or that the star is Athena going down to break the truce (see [[shooting-star]] and [[zeus]]).

**Someone will say.** Over his wounded brother Agamemnon pictures the future. If Menelaus dies, the army will go home, his bones will rot in Troy, “and one of the overweening Trojans will say, leaping on the tomb of glorious Menelaus: ‘May Agamemnon fulfill his anger like this against all, as now he has led an army of the Achaeans here in vain, and has gone home to his dear native land with empty ships, leaving brave Menelaus behind.’ So someone will say one day; then may the wide earth gape for me” (4.176–182).

The frame is the tis-speech put into the future: καί κέ τις ὧδʼ ἐρέει, “and someone will say thus”, and to close, ὥς ποτέ τις ἐρέει, “so someone will say one day”. Hector uses the same closing half-line twice: imagining what a man will say when he sees Andromache weeping as a slave (6.459–462), and what a sailor will say of the tomb of the man Hector has killed (7.87–91). Agamemnon's is the first such speech in the Iliad. It imagines shame, where Hector's imagine grief and glory; and it is spoken by an enemy on a grave (see [[tomb]] and [[agamemnon]]).
