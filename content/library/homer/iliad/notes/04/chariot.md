**On foot.** Before his review Agamemnon “left his horses and his chariot, inlaid with bronze” (ἅρματα ποικίλα χαλκῷ, 4.226), and told his attendant Eurymedon to keep the snorting horses close, for when weariness should take his limbs (4.227–230; see [[eurymedon]]). The chariot follows the fighter and waits for him, ready to carry him out of danger (see [[the-review]]).

**Nestor's order.** “The horsemen he set in front with their horses and chariots” (σὺν ἵπποισιν καὶ ὄχεσφι, 4.297), and Nestor gives them their orders: keep in line, do not charge ahead alone or fall back, and “whoever from his own chariot reaches an enemy's chariot, let him thrust with his spear” (ἀπὸ ὧν ὀχέων ἕτερʼ ἅρμαθʼ, 4.306–307). Fighting from the chariot is what “the men of old” did (4.308); elsewhere in the Iliad the chariot mostly carries a hero to the fighting and away. See [[battle-order]].

**Diomedes.** Agamemnon finds Diomedes “standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot” (ἅρμασι κολλητοῖσι, 4.366). κολλητός, “glued, well-joined”, is a word for fitted woodwork (see [[well-joined]]). After the rebuke he “leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor” (ἐξ ὀχέων, 4.419), in the words used of Menelaus leaping down to meet Paris (3.29).

**The chariot-maker.** In the simile of Simoeisius' fall, a poplar is felled by “a chariot-maker” (ἁρματοπηγὸς ἀνήρ, 4.485), who cuts it down with iron “to bend a wheel rim for a beautiful chariot” (ἴτυν … περικαλλέϊ δίφρῳ, 4.486), and it lies drying by the river. The simile shows a wheel in the making, its rim of poplar cut green and left to dry before it is bent (see [[the-poplar]], [[simoeisius]] and [[iron]]).
