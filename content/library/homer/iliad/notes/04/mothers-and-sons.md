**A goddess as mother.** When the arrow flies, Athena brushes it from Menelaus' flesh “as a mother brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). The simile gives a war goddess a mother's care for a grown warrior. The care is real and it is limited: she lets the arrow through where the armor is double, and it draws blood (4.132–140).

**A mortal mother.** Simoeisius' mother appears in his story only at his birth. She bore him by the banks of the Simoeis “coming down from Ida, when she had gone with her parents to watch over the flocks; therefore they called him Simoeisius” (4.474–477). The next lines give his death, and his parents' loss: he did not repay them for his rearing (4.477–479; see [[threptra]]).

**The absent son.** At the end of the book Apollo gives the Trojans a reason to fight: “Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, is not fighting, but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (4.512–513). The goddess's son is where his mother's plea to Zeus put him, out of the war until the Achaeans honor him (1.503–510). See [[meter]], [[thetis]] and [[achilles]].
