**Silent anger.** Zeus names her “Athena of Alalcomenae” (Ἀλαλκομενηΐς, 4.8), from the Boeotian town or, as others explained it in antiquity, “the Protectress”, from ἀλαλκεῖν, “ward off” (see [[of-alalcomenae]]). When Zeus proposes peace, the two goddesses answer in different ways:

{{quote:30e756f6-6e4e-57fd-b97c-ff195ca6727d}}

Hera argues with Zeus; his daughter says nothing and holds her anger in. The bargain is made without a word from her, and she is sent to carry it out, “eager already” (πάρος μεμαυῖαν, 4.73).

**The shooting star.** She goes “darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (4.74), as she did at Hera's bidding in Book 2 (2.167). The same two lines send her down when Zeus gives up Hector (4.73–74 = 22.186–187).

{{quote:4e819775-d173-5e7c-a5cd-d2cac0a65191}}

The star is a τέρας, a portent that “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus” sends to sailors or to an army, and the watchers take it as one. Amazement (θάμβος) holds both armies, and each man asks his neighbor whether Zeus is bringing war again or setting friendship between them (see [[thambos]] and [[tis-speech]]). They see a sign from Zeus, not the goddess, who comes down “into their midst” and at once takes another shape. See [[shooting-star]] and [[divine-epiphany]].

**Laodocus.** She slips into the Trojan throng in the likeness of Laodocus, a son of Antenor (4.86–87; see [[laodocus]]), finds Pandarus, and offers him thanks and glory from the Trojans, gifts from Alexander, and a prayer to Apollo (4.93–103). “So spoke Athena, and she persuaded the mind of the fool” (4.104). The goddess who hates Troy sends the Trojan archer to pray to Troy's own god. See [[pandarus]] and [[apollo]].

**The arrow turned.** The narrator turns to Menelaus himself:

{{quote:90ce77e5-b5e5-516b-bda1-7e6075564a18}}

She wards off the shaft, and she “herself guided it” (αὐτὴ δʼ αὖτʼ ἴθυνεν). The simile is of a mother's care, and it also measures the gesture: τόσον … ὡς, she keeps the arrow off only as far as a mother keeps a fly from her sleeping child (see [[mother-and-fly]]). She lets it through belt, corselet and band until it grazes “the outermost flesh of the man” (4.139), and the dark blood runs down his thighs (4.140–147). Her errand needed a wound and not a death. If Menelaus died, the army would remember home, as Agamemnon at once foresees (4.169–182); a wound proves that the Trojans have broken the oaths. She is ἀγελείη here, “driver of spoil” (4.128), a title also explained as “leader of the host” (see [[driver-of-spoil]]). In Book 5 she will guide the spear of Diomedes into Pandarus' face, with the same verb (ἴθυνεν, 5.290).

**The Achaeans roused.** When the armies meet, “Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those” (4.439): each side has its god (see [[ares]]). After Apollo's call from Pergamus, “the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia” goes through the throng and rouses the Achaeans “wherever she saw them holding back” (4.514–516). The words are those of Agamemnon's review, where he rebuked “those whom he saw holding back from hateful war” (οὕς τινας αὖ μεθιέντας ἴδοι, 4.240): the goddess finishes the king's work. Τριτογένεια is used here for the first time in the poem, and its meaning is uncertain (see [[tritogeneia]]). The book ends with an imagined man walking through the battle unhurt, with Pallas Athena leading him by the hand and keeping off the missiles; even he could not make light of the fighting (4.539–544).

**Tydeus.** Agamemnon's story of Tydeus at Thebes gives her an older favorite. Tydeus won every contest easily, “such a helper was Athena to him” (4.390). In Book 5 she reminds his son Diomedes of it in almost the same words: “such a helper was I to him” (τοίη οἱ ἐγὼν ἐπιτάρροθος ἦα, 5.808). See [[tydeus]].

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates will not let it be said that the breaking of the oaths by Pandarus came about through Athena and Zeus (*Republic* 2.379e; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).
