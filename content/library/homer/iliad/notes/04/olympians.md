ἠγορόωντο, “were holding assembly”: the gods have their agora, as the Achaeans and the Trojans do (see [[assembly]]). It is also a feast. Hebe pours the nectar, and they pledge one another in golden cups (see [[hebe]], [[nectar]] and [[feast]]). And they are spectators, “looking out upon the city of the Trojans”. Zeus' first words are about two goddesses who “sit apart and look on and take their pleasure” (4.9–10).

Only Zeus and Hera speak. Hera speaks for the rest twice. “Not all of us other gods approve”, she warns (4.29), and when she and Zeus have agreed, “the other gods will follow, the immortals” (4.63–64). What the gods have in common is their share of worship: the altar's portion of feast, libation and savor, “for that is the prize allotted to us” (4.49; see [[geras]]).

On the field the gods are seen from below. When Pandarus shoots, “the blessed gods did not forget” Menelaus, “and first of all the daughter of Zeus” (4.127–128; see [[blessed-gods]]). Nestor says that “the gods never give men all things at once” (4.320), and Agamemnon ends his quarrel with Odysseus hoping that “the gods make it all blow away” (4.363).
