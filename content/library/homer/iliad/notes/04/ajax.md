**The two Ajaxes.** Agamemnon comes to them “going through the crowd of men”, as he came to the Cretans (4.273, 4.251). They are arming, “and with them followed a cloud of foot soldiers” (4.274). The cloud becomes a simile. A goatherd on a lookout sees a cloud coming over the sea before the West Wind, blacker than pitch and bringing a great storm, and he shudders and drives his flocks into a cave; so the battalions move into war with the two Ajaxes, “dark, bristling with shields and spears” (4.275–282; see [[storm-cloud]]). The dual runs through the scene, in the verb of τὼ δὲ κορυσσέσθην (4.274) and in Agamemnon's address, Αἴαντʼ … ἡγήτορε, σφῶϊ and αὐτώ (4.285–287). Here, as in Book 2 (2.406), Αἴαντε are Ajax son of Telamon and Ajax son of Oileus (see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

To Idomeneus the king said “rouse yourself”; the Ajaxes get no orders, “since it is not fitting”, because they urge on the people themselves. His wish repeats the one he made over Nestor's counsel in Book 2 with a different measure. There he wanted ten counselors like Nestor; here he wants the Ajaxes' spirit in every breast, and the city's fall follows in the same two lines (4.290–291 = 2.373–374). Neither Ajax answers. See [[agamemnon]] and [[the-review-of-the-army]].

**Simoeisius.** In the first battle Telamonian Ajax makes the second Achaean kill of the poem, after Antilochus'. He strikes the young [[Simoeisius]] in the chest beside the right nipple, the spear goes through the shoulder, and the boy falls in the dust like a poplar cut down by a chariot-maker (4.473–489; see [[the-poplar]]). The narrator gives the victim a birth on the banks of the Simoeis and parents he never repaid for his rearing, and gives the killer his name and two epithets, “great-hearted” (4.479) and “sprung from Zeus” (4.489). Then Antiphus, son of Priam, throws at Ajax, misses, and kills Odysseus' companion Leucus (4.489–493; see [[antiphus-son-of-priam]] and [[leucus]]).
