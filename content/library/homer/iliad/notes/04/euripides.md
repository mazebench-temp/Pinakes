---
summary: In the Suppliants, Adrastus praises the dead Tydeus as a man of deeds rather than words, the same father whom Agamemnon holds up to Diomedes in Book 4.
---

When Theseus has brought back from Thebes the bodies of the Argive leaders, he asks Adrastus to tell the young men of Athens what kind of men they were, and Adrastus praises five of them in turn. Tydeus' praise is short: he had an ambitious spirit, and his pride showed in deeds and not in words (*Suppliants* 901, 907–908). The lines between, which call him “not brilliant in speech, but a formidable master of the shield” (902–906), are rejected by some editors as an interpolation. Agamemnon's Tydeus is the same man, a fighter rather than a speaker: his son, he says, is “worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.400).
