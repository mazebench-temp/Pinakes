Diomedes keeps silent under the rebuke; his companion will not. Sthenelus stands beside him at the chariot, named by his father, Καπανήϊος υἱός (4.367), and he begins by calling the king a liar: ψεύδεʼ, “lies”, from a king who knows “how to speak plainly” (4.404; see [[pseudos]]). Then he turns Agamemnon's story around. The king told of a father greater than his son. Sthenelus boasts (εὐχόμεθʼ; see [[euchomai]]) that the sons are “far better than our fathers” (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]), and the proof is Thebes. The fathers, Tydeus and his own father Capaneus among them, perished before its walls; the sons took “the seat of seven-gated Thebes” with a smaller army against a stronger wall (4.406–407; see [[thebes]] and [[the-epigonoi]]).

It is a rare claim in Homer, where the fathers usually set the standard. Glaucus was sent to Troy with the charge not to shame the race of his fathers (6.207–209), and Hector prays that someone may one day say of his son that he is far better than his father (6.479–480). In the Odyssey Athena, in the guise of Mentor, tells Telemachus that few sons are like their fathers, most are worse, and few are better (Od. 2.276–277).

Sthenelus makes his case from the king's own words. In Agamemnon's story Tydeus spared Maeon “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 4.398); the sons, says Sthenelus, won “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (πειθόμενοι τεράεσσι θεῶν, 4.408). The fathers pressed on in spite of the ill-omened signs Zeus showed at Mycenae (4.381), and “perished through their own recklessness” (4.409; see [[atasthalie]] and [[capaneus]]). “So never put our fathers in equal honor with us” (ὁμοίῃ … τιμῇ, 4.410; see [[time]]).

Diomedes turns on him, “looking at him darkly”, and tells him to sit in silence and obey (4.411–418; see [[diomedes]]). Sthenelus says nothing more in Book 4. In the next book he draws an arrow out of Diomedes' shoulder (5.108–113) and drives off the horses of Aeneas for him (5.319–327).
