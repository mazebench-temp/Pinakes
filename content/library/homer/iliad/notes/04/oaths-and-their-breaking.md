summary: Book 4 is the breaking of the oaths. The gods decide that the Trojans shall be the first to break them, and Pandarus' arrow does it; Agamemnon declares that Zeus fulfills an oath even late, the breakers paying with their heads, their wives and their children, and he makes the broken oath the army's reason to fight.

**The curse chooses its victims.** The curse spoken over the wine in Book 3 named no one: “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths” (3.299). In Book 4 the gods choose the side. Zeus offers peace (4.14–19). Hera refuses, and asks him to send Athena “and contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans, be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (ἄρξωσι πρότεροι ὑπὲρ ὅρκια δηλήσασθαι, 4.66–67). Zeus gives the order in the same words (4.71–72). They are the words of the curse (3.299), and of Menelaus' demand that Priam swear because his sons could not be trusted (3.106–107). The goddess is sent to make the curse fall on Troy.

**The breach.** Athena does not break the truce herself. She takes the likeness of a Trojan, Laodocus, and finds a man who will: the archer Pandarus, whom she tempts with thanks and glory from the Trojans and gifts from Alexander, and tells to vow a hecatomb to Apollo (4.86–103). “She persuaded the mind of the fool” (4.104). He strings his bow behind his companions' shields and shoots at Menelaus (4.105–126). The god's plan and the man's choice meet in one shot, and the poem holds both responsible (see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[plato-broken-oaths]]).

**Not in vain.** Holding his wounded brother by the hand, Agamemnon begins with the rite of Book 3. The oaths he “cut” were his brother's death, and the Trojans have “trampled on the trusty oaths” (4.155–157).

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

The line on the libations and right hands is the one Nestor spoke in Book 2, when the army was about to abandon its own oaths and sail home (2.341). What the rite pledged is still in force: the oath, the blood of lambs, the unmixed wine. Then Agamemnon states the doctrine: “For even if the Olympian has not fulfilled it at once, he fulfills it in the end, even late, and they pay a great price, with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.160–162; see [[fulfilled-late]] and [[poine]]). The payment is the one the curse named: their brains poured out like the wine, their children's, and their wives subdued by others (3.300–301). And he knows the end:

{{quote:2f616469-d39c-5c58-b7ba-997c641df646}}

Zeus will shake his dark aegis over them all, “in resentment at this deceit” (4.166–168; see [[apate]] and [[kotos]]). In Book 3 the narrator said that Zeus “was not yet” going to fulfill the prayer (3.302). Agamemnon's word is “late” (ὀψέ). See [[a-day-will-come]].

**The army's reason.** In the review the broken oath becomes the motive for the fighting. Agamemnon tells the eager: “father Zeus will be no helper to liars, but those who first did harm in defiance of the oaths, vultures will eat their tender flesh, and we will lead away their dear wives and little children in our ships, when we take the citadel” (4.235–239; see [[no-helper-to-liars]]). Idomeneus answers the king's praise in the same terms: “the oaths have been confounded by the Trojans; for them there will be death and sorrows hereafter, since they were the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.269–271). The formula of the curse has now been spoken by the armies (3.299), by Hera and by Zeus (4.66–67, 4.71–72), by Agamemnon (4.236) and by Idomeneus (4.271). The men know only the Trojans' guilt; the audience has seen the gods decide it. The first part of Book 4 was known in antiquity as “the confounding of the oaths” (see [[titles-of-book-4]]).
