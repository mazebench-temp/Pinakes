**The wound.** When Pandarus' arrow grazes Menelaus and the dark blood flows, the narrator turns to a craft. A Maeonian or Carian woman stains ivory with purple to make a cheekpiece for horses; many horsemen pray to carry it, but it lies in a chamber as a delight for a king, “both an ornament for the horse and a glory to the driver”: “so, Menelaus, your thighs were stained with blood, your shapely thighs, and your shins, and your fine ankles beneath” (4.141–147). The wound is seen as a precious thing, and the body as beautiful in it. The narrator speaks to Menelaus directly, as he did when the arrow flew (4.127, 4.146; see [[apostrophe]] and [[ivory-stained-with-purple]]).

**The young man.** Simoeisius is “the young man in his prime” (ἠΐθεον θαλερόν, 4.474), and he falls like a poplar grown in the low wet ground of a marsh, “smooth, but with branches growing at the very top”, which a chariot-maker cuts down with the gleaming iron to bend into a wheel rim, and leaves to dry by the river (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]). The tree's smoothness is the young body's, and it is cut because it is fine. In Book 22 Priam says that everything is beautiful in a young man killed in battle (22.71–73). Book 4 shows it first. See [[death-in-battle]] and [[simoeisius]].
