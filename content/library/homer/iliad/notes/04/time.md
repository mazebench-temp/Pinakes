Book 4 gives three kinds of honor, divine, royal and ancestral.

**A god's honor.** Of all the cities under the sun and the starry sky, “sacred Ilios was the most honored (τιέσκετο) in my heart”, Zeus tells Hera, “and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (4.44–47). The iterative form, τιέσκετο, is the honor of long habit, and the reason is an exchange: the city's altar never lacked its share of the feast (4.48–49; see [[geras]]). Zeus gives the city up all the same (4.37–43).

**A king's honor.** Agamemnon's praise of Idomeneus is a statement of honor and its signs: “I honor (τίω) you above the Danaans of the swift horses, both in war and in any other work, and at the feast”. When the elders drink, the others have a measured portion, but his cup, like the king's own, always stands full (4.257–263). The full cup is honor made visible. The honor carries a debt, and Agamemnon names it in the next line: “rouse yourself for war, and be the man you have always boasted you are” (4.264). In Book 12 Sarpedon draws the same conclusion from the same signs: kings are honored with seats, meat and full cups, and so they must fight in the front rank (12.310–321). See [[honor-and-dishonor]].

**The honor of the dead.** Sthenelus ends his retort: “So never put our fathers in equal honor (ὁμοίῃ … τιμῇ) with us” (4.410). Agamemnon had honored the dead Tydeus above his living son; Sthenelus claims the higher place for the sons, who took Thebes (see [[better-than-our-fathers]]).
