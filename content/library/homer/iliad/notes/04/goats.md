In Book 3 the wild goat was a carcass that a hungry lion finds (3.23–24). In Book 4 it is a hunter's trophy. Pandarus uncovers his polished bow, made from the horns of a wild goat, ἰξάλου αἰγὸς ἀγρίου, “a leaping wild goat”. ἴξαλος occurs only here in Homer, and “leaping, bounding” is a guess from the context. He had lain in wait for it (δεδεγμένος ἐν προδοκῇσι, “waiting in ambush”), hit it under the breast as it came out from a rock, and it fell backward on the rock (4.105–108).

The horns “grew from its head sixteen palms long” (ἑκκαιδεκάδωρα, from δῶρον, the breadth of a palm), well over a meter each. A craftsman who worked in horn (κεραοξόος τέκτων) dressed them and fitted them together, smoothed the whole and set a golden tip on it (4.109–111). How the horns made a bow, as the whole of it or as part of a bow built of horn, wood and sinew, is disputed. See [[bow]] and [[the-bow-of-pandarus]].

The wild goats of the Odyssey are also shot with bows. On the island off the land of the Cyclopes, the nymphs start up the mountain goats, and Odysseus' men take their curved bows and long hunting spears from the ships and kill enough for a feast (Odyssey 9.154–158).

The goats of the book's second picture are a working flock. A goatherd on a lookout sees a storm cloud coming over the sea, shudders, and drives his flocks (μῆλα) into a cave (4.275–279; see [[storm-cloud]]). The goatherds of Book 2 sorted out their mingled flocks as the leaders marshaled the army (2.474–477; see [[goatherds-sorting-their-flocks]]).
