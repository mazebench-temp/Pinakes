Two mothers appear in Book 4, one in a simile and one in a young man's story.

**The mother in the simile.** When Pandarus' arrow flies, Athena stands in front of Menelaus and brushes it from his flesh, “as a mother (μήτηρ) brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131). The goddess who arranged the shot is its gentlest guard, and she protects by half: she guides the arrow to where the belt and corselet are doubled, and it draws blood (4.132–140). The mother does the same small, sure thing as the goddess, and the simile makes the scene tender for a moment. See [[mother-and-fly]].

**The mother of Simoeisius.** Telamonian Ajax kills a young man in his prime, “Simoeisius, whom once his mother bore beside the banks of the Simoeis, coming down from Ida, when she had gone with her parents to watch over the flocks; therefore they called him Simoeisius” (4.474–477). The mother appears only in his birth, and the next lines turn to his death: he did not repay his parents for his rearing, and his life was short (4.477–479; see [[threptra]]). See [[simoeisius]] and [[death-in-battle]].

Apollo names the one great mother of the poem when he tells the Trojans that “Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair”, is not fighting (4.512; see [[mothers-and-sons]]).
