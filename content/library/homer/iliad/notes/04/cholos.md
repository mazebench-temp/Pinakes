summary: Book 4 begins with the anger of goddesses and ends with the anger of Achilles. Hera cannot contain hers, and Zeus says only eating Priam raw would heal it; Odysseus is angered by a rebuke and by a companion's death; and Apollo tells the Trojans that Achilles is brooding over his anger by the ships.

**Held and spoken.** Zeus' proposal of peace angers both goddesses, in two ways. “Athena kept silent and said nothing, sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her; but Hera's breast could not contain her anger, and she spoke” (4.22–24). Achilles in Book 1 did both, holding back his sword and speaking his mind.

**Healed by eating.** Zeus answers Hera with a picture of her anger: if she could go in through the gates and eat Priam raw, and his sons, and the other Trojans, “then you might heal your anger” (χόλον ἐξακέσαιο, 4.34–36). The wish to eat an enemy raw returns at the height of the poem's hatreds: Achilles says it to the dying Hector (22.346–347), and Hecuba says it of Achilles (24.212–213). Zeus then speaks of his own anger, which Hera must not hinder when he wants to sack a city she loves (4.40–42).

**A king's anger mocked.** Agamemnon imagines a Trojan leaping on Menelaus' tomb: “May Agamemnon fulfill his anger (χόλον τελέσειʼ) like this against all” (4.178). The taunt turns Calchas' theory of royal anger, kept until it is fulfilled (1.81–82), into mockery of an anger that achieves nothing.

**Men's anger.** In the review Agamemnon rebukes the slack “with angry words” (χολωτοῖσιν ἐπέεσσιν, 4.241). Odysseus answers him angrily, and the king, “when he saw that he was angry”, takes back his word (4.349–357). In Agamemnon's story the Cadmeians, beaten at the games, “were angry” (χολωσάμενοι) and set an ambush (4.391). In the first battle anger becomes vengeance: Odysseus, “very angry at heart” (χολώθη) at the killing of Leucus, kills Democoon, “in his anger for his companion” (4.494, 4.501).

**Achilles.** Apollo ends the book by telling the Trojans that Achilles is not fighting, “but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (χόλον θυμαλγέα πέσσει, 4.513). πέσσω, “digest, brood over”, is the verb of Calchas' warning: a king may swallow down (καταπέψῃ) his anger for the day (1.81). Achilles' anger is still being digested. See [[achilles]], [[heart-grieving]] and [[menis]].
