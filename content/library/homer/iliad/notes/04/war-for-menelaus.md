---
summary: In Book 4 the cause of the war is weighed in heaven and on earth. Zeus grants that the victory is Menelaus' and proposes that he take Helen home, and Hera will not have it. When Pandarus wounds Menelaus, Agamemnon fears that his brother's death would end the war and leave Helen to the Trojans as a thing to boast of.
passages:
  - 4.13-19 | Zeus: the victory is Menelaus'
  - 4.169-182 | If Menelaus dies
---

In Book 3 the cause of the war was put to the test in a duel, and Agamemnon claimed the victory and what the oath promised (3.455–461). Book 4 opens with Zeus agreeing, and ends the book with the war begun again.

**The victory granted.** “The victory belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares”, Zeus tells the gods (4.13). If they wished it, the city of lord Priam would still be inhabited, “and Menelaus would take Argive Helen back home” (4.18–19). Zeus names Helen only: the possessions and the satisfaction of the oath are forgotten (3.281–291). The duel would have done what it was fought for. Hera and Athena refuse, and the gods agree instead that the Trojans shall break the oaths (4.20–72; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

**The price of the shot.** Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, tempts Pandarus with thanks and glory from the Trojans, and above all with splendid gifts from Alexander, if he sees Menelaus laid on the pyre (4.93–99). The man who began the quarrel is to pay for its end (see [[paris]] and [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]).

**If Menelaus dies.** Holding his wounded brother by the hand, Agamemnon sees at once what his death would mean.

The war depends on the husband. Helen would be left to Priam and the Trojans as “a thing to boast of” (εὐχωλήν, 4.173), in the words Hera and Athena used in Book 2 when the army ran for the ships (2.160–162, 2.176–178; see [[argive-helen]]). Agamemnon imagines the boast itself: a Trojan will leap on the tomb of Menelaus and wish that Agamemnon may always end his anger so, having led an army here in vain and gone home with empty ships (4.176–182). Menelaus answers calmly that the arrow found no deadly place (4.183–187; see [[menelaus]]).

**Wives for a wife.** Rousing the army, Agamemnon promises the payment in kind that Nestor promised in Book 2, when he told the men not to go home before they had lain with the wife of some Trojan (2.354–356). The vultures will eat the flesh of the oath-breakers, “and we will lead away their dear wives and little children in our ships, when we take the citadel” (4.237–239; see [[vultures]]). Diomedes, silencing Sthenelus, puts the war in Agamemnon's hands: the glory will be his if the Achaeans take sacred Ilios, and the great grief his if they are cut down (4.415–417).

**A second wrong.** The broken truce changes the cause. Menelaus prayed in Book 3 for vengeance on the man who wronged his host (3.351–354). After Book 4 the Trojans owe for a broken oath as well: they “trampled on the trusty oaths”, and Zeus will make them pay with their heads and their wives and children (4.157–162, 4.235–239, 4.269–271; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).
