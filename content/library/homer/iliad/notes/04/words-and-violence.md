**Words that start a war.** The book begins with a tease. Zeus provokes Hera “with cutting words, speaking with sly intent” (4.5–6; see [[kertomia]]), and the quarrel that follows ends with Athena sent to break the truce. The violence of the rest of the book begins as a conversation at a feast.

**Words as weapons.** Agamemnon fears the words of the enemy as much as their arrows. If Menelaus dies, a Trojan will leap on his tomb and mock him: “May Agamemnon fulfill his anger like this against all” (4.176–182; see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[tis-speech]]). In the review his own words are his weapons: praise to the eager, rebuke “with angry words” to the slack (4.233, 4.241; see [[praise-and-blame]]).

**Words answered with words.** The men he rebukes fight back with speech. Odysseus: “what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth! … what you say is empty wind” (4.350–355). Agamemnon takes his word back (4.356–363; see [[anemolios]]). Sthenelus: “Son of Atreus, do not lie” (4.404). Diomedes stops the exchange with an order, “sit in silence, and obey my word” (4.412), and leaps from his chariot in his armor (4.419–421). The argument ends when the speaker goes to fight.

**No words.** When the armies meet, the only sounds are the din of shields and spears and “cries of pain and cries of triumph together from men killing and being killed” (4.446–451). No one taunts an enemy before he strikes or boasts over him after, as fighters will in later books. The first battle is fought without a speech, except for Apollo's shout from the citadel (4.509–513). See [[death-in-battle]] and [[euchomai]].
