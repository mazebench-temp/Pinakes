**The instigator.** In Book 4 Hera asks Zeus to send Athena to make the Trojans break the oaths (4.64–72). In the *Aeneid* Juno, watching from the Alban hill, urges Turnus' sister, the nymph Juturna, to stir up war and break the treaty they are about to swear (*Aeneid* 12.134–160).

**The disguise.** Athena goes among the Trojans in the likeness of Laodocus, son of Antenor, and persuades one man, Pandarus (4.86–104). Juturna takes the likeness of Camers, a warrior of famous family, and rouses the whole army against the treaty (*Aeneid* 12.222–237).

**The sign and the first missile.** Both armies watch Athena come down like a shooting star and wonder whether it means war or friendship (4.75–85). Juturna sends a false portent: an eagle seizes a swan, and the other birds mob it until it drops its prey. The augur Tolumnius reads it as a sign for the Rutulians and throws the first spear, which kills one of nine brothers, the sons of the Arcadian Gylippus (*Aeneid* 12.244–276).

**The arrow.** Aeneas, bareheaded and unarmed, tries to hold his men back, and an arrow strikes him. No one knew whose hand had shot it, or whether chance or a god had brought the Rutulians such glory; the glory of the deed was suppressed, and no one boasted of wounding Aeneas (*Aeneid* 12.311–323). Homer's archer was promised thanks and glory (4.95), and Agamemnon grants him fame even without his name: “fame for him, and grief for us” (4.197). Virgil denies his archer both the name and the fame.

**The healing.** Machaon, son of Asclepius, draws out the arrow, sucks out the blood and spreads on the wound the drugs that Chiron gave his father (4.210–219). Aeneas is tended by Iapyx, whom Apollo loved and offered his own arts; Iapyx chose medicine, but he cannot draw out the arrow until Venus secretly brings dittany from Cretan Ida and mixes it in the water. Then the arrow comes away of itself, and Iapyx says that this is not the work of human skill: “a greater god is at work” (*Aeneid* 12.391–429).

The augur who broke the truce dies in the fighting that follows (*Aeneid* 12.460–461), as Pandarus dies in Iliad 5 (5.290–296). See [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]] and [[healing]].
