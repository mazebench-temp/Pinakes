---
summary: Zeus proposes to the gods that they “put friendship between the two sides”, now that Menelaus has won; below, the armies see Athena come down and wonder whether war or friendship is coming. She brings war.
passages:
  - 4.14-19 | Zeus proposes peace
  - 4.79-85 | The armies see the sign
---

The truce of Book 3 was sworn as friendship and trusty oaths (3.73, 3.94, 3.256), and before the duel both armies prayed that “friendship and trusty oaths may be ours” (3.323). Book 4 names the friendship twice more, as a choice that is about to close.

On Olympus Zeus puts the choice to the gods: “let us consider how these things are to be: whether we shall rouse evil war again and the dread din of battle, or put friendship (φιλότητα) between the two sides” (4.14–16). Friendship would mean that Priam's city is still inhabited and Menelaus takes Helen home (4.17–19). Hera and Athena mutter against it, and the choice is made for war (4.20–72).

Below, the armies see Athena come down like a shooting star, and a man says to his neighbor: “Surely evil war and the dread din of battle will come again, or else friendship (φιλότητα) is set between the two sides by Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men” (4.82–84). The men ask the gods' question in the gods' own words (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]] and [[tis-speech]]), without knowing it has already been answered. The next thing the star brings is Pandarus' arrow.

The friendship of the two armies does not come again. What the poem gives instead is a friendship of two men: after their duel in Book 7, Hector and Ajax exchange gifts and part “in friendship, reconciled” (7.302). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[shooting-star]].
