summary: With Achilles out of the fighting, Book 4 keeps measuring who is best. Agamemnon honors Idomeneus above the Danaans, says that Tydeus was above all others and that his son is worse in battle but better in assembly; Sthenelus claims that the sons are far better than their fathers; and Apollo tells the Trojans that Achilles is not fighting.

**Measures.** The review is a series of judgments of worth. Idomeneus is honored “above the Danaans of the swift horses, both in war and in any other work, and at the feast” (4.257–259). Tydeus, by report, “was above all others” (4.375). His son is measured by two scales at once: “worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.400). The two scales are the ones Nestor tried to balance in Book 1, the best in battle and the greatest in rule. Achilles will weigh himself on them in Book 18, in nearly the same words: no Achaean is his equal in war, “but in the assembly others are better” (ἀγορῇ δέ τʼ ἀμείνονές εἰσι καὶ ἄλλοι, 18.105–106; compare ἀγορῇ δέ τʼ ἀμείνω, 4.400). Sthenelus rejects the verdict: the sons are “far better” (μέγʼ ἀμείνονες) than their fathers (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]] and [[aristos]]).

**The absent best.** The man the Iliad calls best is not there. Apollo, rousing the Trojans, tells them what the Achaeans lack: “Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, is not fighting, but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (4.512–513). His absence is the condition of everything the others do in the book, as Achilles meant it to be when he swore that the Achaeans would miss him (1.240–244). The Catalogue named Telamonian Ajax the best “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769), and in the first battle it is Ajax who kills Simoeisius (4.473–489).
