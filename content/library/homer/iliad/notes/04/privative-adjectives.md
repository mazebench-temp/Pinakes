---
summary: The book's one privative pair describes the imagined onlooker of the first battle, “unhit and unwounded” (4.540). Its single privatives gather around the arrow, the oath and the war's unfinished work.
grc: ["ἄβλητος καὶ ἀνούτατος"]
en: [unhit and unwounded]
---

**The pair.** The first battle ends with a man imagined walking through it, ὅς τις ἔτʼ ἄβλητος καὶ ἀνούτατος ὀξέϊ χαλκῷ, “any man who, still unhit and unwounded by the sharp bronze”, might go through the middle led by Athena (4.540). ἄβλητος, “not hit by a missile” (from βάλλω, “throw, hit”), and ἀνούτατος, “not stabbed” (from οὐτάω, “stab, wound”), name the two ways a man is wounded in battle, at a distance and hand to hand; both occur only here in the Iliad. See [[apostrophe]] and [[androktasia]].

**Singles.** Pandarus' arrow is ἀβλής, “never shot before” (4.117), a word found only here (see [[arrows]]). The libations of the broken oath were ἄκρητοι, “unmixed” (4.159; see [[libation]]). Hera will not have her labor ἀτέλεστον, “unfulfilled” (4.26, 4.57), Agamemnon promises that the oath's penalty will not be ἀτέλεστα (4.168), and he fears that Menelaus will lie in Troy with his work ἀτελεύτητον, “unfinished” (4.175; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). Zeus gives up Troy “willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (ἑκὼν ἀέκοντί γε θυμῷ, 4.43; see [[with-an-unwilling-heart]]), the privative set against its opposite. The narrator calls Pandarus ἄφρων (4.104), “the fool”, a man without φρένες, and the stone that crushes Diores' ankle is ἀναιδής, “shameless” (4.521), the epithet of the stone Sisyphus rolls in the underworld (Odyssey 11.598; see [[shameless-stone]]).
