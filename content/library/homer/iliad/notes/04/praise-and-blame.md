summary: Agamemnon's review of the army is the poem's fullest sequence of praise and rebuke. He praises Idomeneus, the Ajaxes and Nestor, and rebukes the slack, Menestheus and Odysseus, and Diomedes; the men rebuked answer with anger, with silence and with a retort, and the king takes back one rebuke and lets another stand.

**The narrator's praise.** The review opens with the poet's own judgment of the king: “Then you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing, nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight, but very eager for the battle where men win glory” (4.223–225). The negatives name the faults he will rebuke in others: he will ask Menestheus and Odysseus why they stand cowering (καταπτώσσοντες, 4.340), the word used of him here (4.224), and Diomedes why he cowers (4.371). The king who rebukes has first been cleared of the charge.

**Two speeches.** Agamemnon has one speech for the eager and one for the slack. To the eager he gives a reason to fight: Zeus will not help the oath-breakers, and their wives and children will be taken (4.234–239). The slack he calls “you arrow-fighters, you disgraces” and asks whether they feel no shame, standing like fawns (4.242–249; see [[sebas]], [[elenchos]], [[obscure-iomoroi]] and [[like-fawns]]). In Book 6 Hector's rebuke of Paris uses the same phrase, “holding back from hateful war” (6.330, 4.240).

**Praise.** Idomeneus is praised with the signs of honor, a full cup at the elders' feast, and reminded of his boasts (4.257–264); he answers with loyalty (4.266–271). The Ajaxes need no orders at all, since they urge on the people themselves (4.285–291). Nestor is praised with a wish that he were young, and turns the praise into a reflection on age (4.313–325; see [[prize-of-the-old]]).

**Rebuke answered with anger.** Menestheus and Odysseus are rebuked as the first at feasts and the last to fight (4.338–348). Odysseus answers “looking at him darkly”: what the king says is empty wind (4.349–355). Agamemnon smiles, takes back his word, and promises to make it good (4.356–363; see [[anemolios]] and [[smiling-at-him]]).

**Rebuke answered with silence.** Diomedes is rebuked at length with the deeds of his father (4.370–400). He says nothing, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.402; see [[aidos]]). Sthenelus answers for him with a boast, and Diomedes silences his companion: the king has the right to urge the army, since the glory and the grief will be his (4.404–418; see [[better-than-our-fathers]] and [[nemesis]]). Agamemnon does not take this rebuke back, and does not answer Sthenelus. Diomedes will answer it himself, first by fighting in Book 5, and then in words in the assembly of Book 9 (9.32–36).

The two answers are the two ways the poem allows a man to take blame: to reject it at once, or to bear it and disprove it by deeds. See [[the-review-of-the-army]], [[the-review]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].
