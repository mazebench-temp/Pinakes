Book 4 opens with the gods at their feast, “looking out upon the city of the Trojans” (4.4), and it is about the fate of a city. If Zeus' proposal of peace were accepted, “the city of lord Priam would still be inhabited” (4.18). Hera will not have it, and Zeus asks her what Priam and his sons have done to her, that she rages to sack “the well-built citadel of Ilios” (4.31–33).

The bargain that follows speaks of cities as things the gods own and exchange. Zeus will let Hera have Troy, but whenever he is eager to sack a city “where men dear to you were born”, she must not hinder him (4.40–42). He names what he is giving up: “For of all the cities (πόληες) under the sun and the starry sky where men on earth have their dwelling, sacred Ilios was the most honored in my heart” (4.44–46). Hera answers with her own list: “three cities (πόληες) are by far the dearest to me, Argos and Sparta and wide-streeted Mycenae; sack them whenever they become hateful to your heart” (4.51–53). The cities the gods love are the cities they are willing to give up. Whether the poet had the later decline of the three cities in mind is disputed (see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

Among men the city is what the war is for. Agamemnon wishes that all his men had the Ajaxes' spirit: “Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head, taken and sacked beneath our hands” (4.290–291), the lines he spoke to Nestor in Book 2 (2.373–374). Nestor teaches his horsemen the tactics with which “the men of old sacked cities (πόλεας) and walls” (4.308). At the end of the book Apollo speaks “from the city” (ἀπὸ πτόλιος, 4.514), the god of the citadel looking down on the fighting. See [[troy]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]].
