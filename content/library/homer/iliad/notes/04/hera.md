summary: At the council of the gods Hera's anger overrules Zeus' offer of peace. She has toiled to gather the army against Priam, and to win Troy's destruction she gives up to Zeus her three dearest cities, Argos, Sparta and Mycenae, claiming the rank of his sister and his wife.

**Argive Hera.** Zeus names her by her city, Ἥρη Ἀργείη, “Argive Hera” (4.8; see [[argive]]), as Hesiod does, “lady Hera of Argos, who walks in golden sandals” (*Theogony* 11–12). Her great sanctuary stood in the plain of Argos (see [[argos]]). His taunt is that she and Athena, Menelaus' helpers, only sit and watch (4.7–12).

**Her protest.** Athena says nothing, but “Hera's breast could not contain her anger” (4.24). The same five lines return in Book 8, when Zeus has turned the two goddesses back from the battle and mocks them (4.20–24 = 8.457–461).

{{quote:e671a048-6dff-52cb-9ab8-0f4621599875}}

Her first line is the one with which she answered Zeus in the quarrel of Book 1 (1.552; see [[most-dread]]). She speaks of the war as her own work: her labor (πόνος), her sweat, her horses worn out “as I gathered the army, to bring evils on Priam and his sons” (λαὸν ἀγειρούσῃ). The poem says nothing else of her part in the muster. Her last line is the formula with which a god disputes a decision of Zeus (4.29 = 16.443, 22.181; see [[zeus]]).

**What have they done to you?** Zeus asks what great evils Priam and his sons have done to her, that she rages to sack Ilios. If she could go in through the gates and “eat Priam raw, and the sons of Priam, and the other Trojans”, then she might heal her anger (4.31–36). She does not answer. The Iliad gives a cause only in its last book, “the blind folly of Alexander”, who insulted Hera and Athena when they came to his steading and preferred Aphrodite (24.25–30), lines that [[aristarchus|Aristarchus]] rejected (see [[paris]]). There Ilios, Priam and his people are said to have become “hateful” (ἀπήχθετο) to her, the verb she uses here of her own cities, which Zeus may sack “whenever they become hateful to your heart” (ἀπέχθωνται, 4.53). Her hatred lasts to the end: in Book 20 she says that she and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, not even when all Troy burns (20.313–317). Zeus' picture of a goddess eating her enemies raw is matched later by mortals: Achilles wishes he could eat Hector's flesh raw (22.346–347), and Hecuba wishes she could eat Achilles' liver (24.212–213).

**Three cities.** Zeus sets a condition: when he wants to sack a city “where men dear to you were born”, she must not stand in his way (4.40–43). She answers with three:

{{quote:b9f6ebb9-3565-53c5-bd48-647d61afbfa9}}

They are the cities of the kings she fights for. In the Catalogue Argos is Diomedes' city, Mycenae Agamemnon's, and Sparta one of the towns of Menelaus (2.559, 2.569, 2.582). Mycenae is εὐρυάγυια, “wide-streeted”, the word the Achaeans use of the Troy they mean to take (2.12, 2.141, 2.329; see [[wide-streeted]]). And she offers what she could not defend in any case: “even if I grudge them and will not let you sack them, I accomplish nothing by grudging, since you are far the stronger” (4.55–56).

Some readers have seen in the lines a memory of real destructions: the burning of the Mycenaean palaces around 1200 BC, or, as a prophecy after the event, the sack of Mycenae by Argos in the 460s (Diodorus 11.65), long after the poem was composed. Neither fits the three names well. Argos and Sparta flourished long after the Bronze Age, and the Iliad never shows Zeus taking up the offer. Most critics read it within the scene: Hera answers the city Zeus gives up with three of her own, and the price shows how much Troy's fall is worth to her. See [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]].

**Sister and wife.** She then claims her standing:

{{quote:2762619c-bbe3-534d-bb6f-96b7aab7e4bd}}

πρεσβυτάτην can mean “eldest” or “most senior”, and she claims it both by birth and by marriage. She makes the same claim in Book 18, repeating these two lines (4.60–61 = 18.365–366), when she tells Zeus why she, “the best of goddesses”, must stitch evils for the Trojans (18.364–367). Elsewhere she is “Hera, senior goddess, daughter of great Cronus” (Ἥρη πρέσβα θεά, 5.721, 14.194), and the narrator calls her Zeus' “sister and wife” (κασιγνήτην ἄλοχόν τε, 16.432); among the sons of Cronus, Zeus is the elder (13.355). In Hesiod, Hestia is Cronus' first-born child and Hera the third (*Theogony* 453–454). She grants that Zeus is “lord among all the immortals” and asks for a partner's share: “let us give way to each other in this, I to you and you to me” (4.62–63). See [[cronus]].

**Her plan.** Then she tells Zeus what to do. Athena is to go and contrive that the Trojans “be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.64–67), and Zeus sends her in Hera's own words (4.70–72). The curse sworn over the truce fell on whichever side first did harm (3.299). Hera wants Troy destroyed, and wants the Trojans to have earned it. See [[oath]], [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[athena]].
