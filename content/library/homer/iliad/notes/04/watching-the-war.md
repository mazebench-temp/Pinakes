summary: Book 4 opens with the gods looking down on Troy from their feast and closes with an imagined onlooker led through the battle by Athena. Between them, two goddesses sit and watch, the armies watch a goddess fall like a star, Agamemnon rebukes men who would rather watch than fight, and the similes give the battle its distant watchers.

**Gods watching.** Book 4 begins with the gods seated on the golden floor, drinking to one another from golden cups, “looking out upon the city of the Trojans” (Τρώων πόλιν εἰσορόωντες, 4.4). Zeus' first taunt is about watching: Hera and Athena, Menelaus' helpers, “sit apart and look on and take their pleasure” (εἰσορόωσαι τέρπεσθον, 4.9–10), while Aphrodite acts. At the end of the book Apollo is watching too, “looking down from Pergamus”, and does not like what he sees (4.507–508).

**Men watching a god.** When Athena comes down like a star, “amazement held those who watched, the horse-taming Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans” (4.79–80), the lines that described the armies watching the duel (3.342–343). The watchers ask each other what the sign means (4.81–85). See [[shooting-star]] and [[divine-epiphany]].

**Watching instead of fighting.** In the review, watching is a fault. Agamemnon tells Menestheus and Odysseus that they would gladly watch (ὁρόῳτε) “even if ten columns of Achaeans were fighting in front of you” (4.347–348), and asks Diomedes, “why do you stare at the bridges of war?” (ὀπιπεύεις, 4.371; see [[obscure-gephyrai]]). The place for a leader is in front, not looking on.

**The poet's watchers.** The narrator speaks to the listener as to an onlooker: “you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing” (4.223); “you would not have said that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts” (4.429–430). Two similes of the book have a watcher inside them. A goatherd sees from his lookout a cloud coming over the sea, blacker than pitch, and shudders and drives his flocks into a cave (4.275–279; see [[storm-cloud]]); a shepherd far off in the mountains hears the roar of two torrents meeting (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]). Each time the battle is seen, or heard, by a single man at a distance, who knows what it means for him.

**The onlooker.** The book ends with an imagined spectator:

{{quote:ff39fce3-97be-5887-ad7f-98daf22c24d4}}

Only a man whom Athena led by the hand through the fighting, unhit and unwounded, could see the whole of it, and even he could not make light of the work. What he would see is the book's last line: the dead of both armies lying face down in the dust beside one another. See [[death-in-battle]] and [[the-first-battle]].
