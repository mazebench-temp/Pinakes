**War or friendship.** Zeus' question to the gods, “whether we shall rouse evil war (πόλεμόν τε κακόν) again and the dread din of battle, or put friendship between the two sides” (4.15–16), is repeated by the men below when they see Athena come down: “Surely evil war and the dread din of battle will come again, or else friendship is set between the two sides by Zeus, who is the dispenser of war (ταμίης πολέμοιο) for men” (4.82–84). A ταμίης is a steward, the servant who gives out the stores of a house; war is something Zeus hands out. The line returns in Book 19 (19.224; see [[dispenser-of-war]] and [[dread-din-of-battle]]). Plato's Socrates, who would not let the poets make a god the cause of evil, rejects a verse that made Zeus the dispenser of good and evil (see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).

**Eager or holding back.** The review sorts men by their relation to war. Agamemnon himself is “very eager for the battle where men win glory” (4.225); he encourages the eager and rebukes those he sees “holding back from hateful war” (μεθιέντας … στυγεροῦ πολέμοιο, 4.240). Odysseus throws the charge back: “How can you say that we hold back from war (πολέμοιο μεθιέμεν)?” (4.351). Tydeus came to Mycenae “not in war” (ἄτερ πολέμου, 4.376) but as a guest; Nestor puts the cowards in the middle “so that even an unwilling man would have to fight” (πολεμίζοι, 4.300), and the old man is “long skilled in wars” (4.310). At the end of the book Athena goes through the throng, “wherever she saw them holding back” (4.516). See [[the-review-of-the-army]] and [[obscure-gephyrai]].
