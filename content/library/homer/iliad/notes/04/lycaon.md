---
summary: Lycaon is named only in his son's title, when Athena finds “the son of Lycaon” (4.89) and when she opens her temptation with “wise-hearted son of Lycaon” (4.93).
---

The narrator names Pandarus once (4.88) and then calls him by his father (4.89), and Athena, in the likeness of a Trojan, addresses him the same way: “Will you listen to me now, wise-hearted son of Lycaon?” (4.93). The father's name gives the request its formality, and the epithet δαΐφρων (“wise-hearted”; see [[wise-hearted]]) its flattery. Eleven lines later the narrator calls the son a fool (4.104; see [[pandarus]]). This is the father of Pandarus, not Priam's son Lycaon, whose corselet Paris wore in the duel (see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]).
