summary: Book 4 opens with the gods at their feast bargaining over whole cities and closes with men dying in the dust. The gods' labor, their love for a city and their anger all fall on mortals, and Apollo reminds the Trojans that the Argives' flesh is not stone or iron.

**At ease.** The gods sit on the golden floor with Zeus, Hebe pours the nectar, and they pledge one another in golden cups, “looking out upon the city of the Trojans” (4.1–4). What for men is a war is for them a spectacle and a subject of conversation (see [[watching-the-war]]). Their quarrel over it is settled among themselves, by an exchange.

**Mortal cities as gods' possessions.** Zeus will let Hera destroy Troy, the city he honored most of all those “where men on earth have their dwelling”, and she lets him destroy any of hers he likes: Argos, Sparta, Mycenae (4.40–56). The whole bargain is conducted in terms of what the gods feel, anger, love and hate, and its price will be paid by people who know nothing of it. Hera's labor is real, and it is labor for destruction: “the sweat I sweated in my toil; my horses were worn out as I gathered the army, to bring evils on Priam and his sons” (4.26–28). Zeus asks her what harm Priam has done her, and answers that she would have to eat him raw to heal her anger (4.31–36). See [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]].

**Equals.** Hera claims her standing among the gods as a hero claims his among men, by birth and by marriage: “for I too am a god, and my birth is from the same stock as yours” (4.58–61). Among the gods, as among men, the stronger decides, and she says so: she cannot stop Zeus, “since you are far the stronger” (4.56). See [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]].

**Flesh.** At the end of the book Apollo gives the Trojans a reason to stand: the Argives' “flesh is not stone or iron to withstand the flesh-cutting bronze when they are struck” (4.510–511; see [[not-stone-or-iron]]). The god knows what the men know too, that they can be cut. The last lines imagine a man led through the battle by Athena, unhit and unwounded, and give him the sight of the dead (4.539–544): a mortal made safe for a moment, as a god always is, in order to see what mortality is.
