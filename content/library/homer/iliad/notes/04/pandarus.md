**Found.** Both armies watch Athena come down like a shooting star, and wonder whether Zeus will bring war again or set friendship between them (4.75–85; see [[shooting-star]]). She slips into the Trojan throng in the likeness of [[Laodocus]], “looking for godlike Pandarus” (4.88), and finds “the son of Lycaon, noble and strong” standing among the shield-bearers who “had followed him from the streams of the Aesepus” (4.89–91). The poem does not say why she chooses him. The Catalogue gave him one distinction, a bow from Apollo (2.827), and an arrow can come out of a crowd unannounced.

**The temptation.**

{{quote:505dae43-d440-50b7-a29d-c9332c98490b}}

She offers what a hero wants, thanks (χάρις) and glory ([[kydos|κῦδος]]) from all the Trojans, and gifts from “king Alexander”, who has most to gain from Menelaus' death (see [[paris]]). She makes the killing vivid, Menelaus “brought down by your arrow and laid on the grievous pyre” (4.99), and tells him how to pray: to Apollo, “the Lycian-born, famed for his bow”, with the vow of a hecatomb of firstborn lambs when he comes home to [[Zeleia]] (4.101–103; see [[lycian-born]] and [[hecatomb]]). She began with πίθοιο (“will you listen to me”, 4.93); the narrator ends with the effect:

{{quote:8f4da89a-3c3e-5014-9cc0-38cc6ee5ce83}}

φρένας ἄφρονι: she persuaded “the mind” (φρένες) of a man “without mind” (ἄφρων), whom she had just called δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (4.93). Hector's taunt over the dying Patroclus ends in the same words (σοὶ δὲ φρένας ἄφρονι πεῖθε, 16.842). The goddess persuades, but what she works on is the man's own wish for glory and gifts. See [[phrenes]], [[peitho]] and [[who-is-to-blame]].

**The bow.** It is made from the horns of a wild goat that he shot himself from ambush; they “grew from its head sixteen palms long”, and a horn-worker joined and smoothed them and set on a golden tip (4.105–111). The hunter who shot from ambush now shoots from behind the shields his companions hold in front of him (4.113–115). On the bow, and on how it can also be Apollo's gift, see [[bow]], [[goats]] and [[the-bow-of-pandarus]].

**The shot.** He takes “an arrow, feathered, never shot before, a source of black pains” (4.116–117), and prays as he was told. The three lines of the prayer are Athena's, with one word changed, εὔχεο (“pray”) to εὔχετο (“prayed”): compare 4.101–103 with 4.119–121. Then he draws:

{{quote:4df5ff6c-adce-5c77-b61c-9b5901fca372}}

The poem does not say that Apollo heard. Athena, who sent the arrow, turns it from a mortal wound (4.127–140; see [[menelaus]]).

**The oaths.** Zeus sent Athena to see that the Trojans would “be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.67, 4.72), and one archer does it for all of them. The Achaeans at once make the deed the city's: “the Trojans have shot you and trampled on the trusty oaths” (4.157), and “father Zeus will be no helper to liars” (4.235). The curse of Book 3 falls on Troy (3.298–301), and Agamemnon foresees that “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.164; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[a-day-will-come]]). He does not know the archer's name: “someone well skilled with the bow … one of the Trojans or Lycians: fame for him, and grief for us” (4.196–197; see [[kleos]]). Athena promised him glory; his victim's brother grants him fame, in the same breath as the Achaeans' grief.

Pandarus says nothing in Book 4. His speeches come in Book 5, where his bow fails him. There the goddess who “guided” (ἴθυνεν) his arrow to Menelaus' belt (4.132) guides the spear of Diomedes into his face (βέλος δʼ ἴθυνεν Ἀθήνη, 5.290), and he dies without coming home to pay his vow (5.290–296).
