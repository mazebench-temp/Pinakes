**A victory granted.** At the end of Book 3 Agamemnon claimed the duel for his brother: “victory clearly belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (3.457). No Trojan answered him. At the start of Book 4 Zeus says it among the gods, almost in the same words: “But the victory (νίκη) belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (4.13). If the gods accepted it, the terms of the oath would follow: the city would stand and Menelaus would take Helen home (4.17–19). The gods' judgment agrees with the king's claim, and the gods set it aside. See [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**A victory resented.** Tydeus, alone at the Cadmeians' feast, challenged them to contests, “and won them all easily; such a helper was Athena to him” (πάντα δʼ ἐνίκα ῥηϊδίως, 4.389–390). The Cadmeians are angered by their guest's victories, and they answer them with an ambush (4.391–398). See [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[contests]].
