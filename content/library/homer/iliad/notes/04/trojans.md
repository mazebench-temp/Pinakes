**The oath-breakers.** On Olympus Hera asks, and Zeus orders, that Athena “contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans, be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.66–67, 4.71–72). The words echo the curse sworn in Book 3 on “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths” (πρότεροι ὑπὲρ ὅρκια, 3.299; see [[oath]]). The gods name a people, and one man does the deed: Pandarus, whom Athena, in the likeness of the Trojan Laodocus, tempts with “thanks and glory from all the Trojans” (4.95; see [[pandarus]] and [[laodocus]]). From then on the Achaeans speak of the Trojans as a people forsworn. “The Trojans have shot you and trampled on the trusty oaths”, Agamemnon tells his wounded brother (4.157), and Zeus will make them pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.162). Rousing the army, he promises that vultures will eat the flesh of “those who first did harm in defiance of the oaths”, and that the Achaeans will carry off their wives and children (4.236–239). Idomeneus agrees: the oaths “have been confounded by the Trojans; for them there will be death and sorrows hereafter” (4.269–271). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**Imagined Trojans.** Agamemnon also imagines the Trojans' view of the war if Menelaus dies. They will keep Argive Helen as “a thing to boast of”, and “one of the overweening Trojans” will leap on Menelaus' tomb and mock the expedition that came for nothing (4.173–182; see [[tis-speech]]). The Trojans themselves say nothing in Book 4. Their standing epithet, here as in Book 3, is ἱππόδαμοι, “horse-taming” (4.80, 4.333, 4.352, 4.355, 4.509; see [[tamer-of-horses]]).

**Many tongues.** When the armies move, the Achaeans go in silence, in fear of their commanders (4.427–432; see [[silence]]). The Trojans are like sheep:

{{quote:8e90d374-4124-5071-8bdb-c4ae78bdeca9}}

The noise has a reason: the army is made of many peoples who do not share a language, as Iris warned Hector in Book 2 (2.803–804; see [[trojan-allies]]). The contrast of Trojan clamor and Achaean silence goes back to the opening of Book 3, where the Trojans come on like cranes (3.2–9; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). Ares rouses the Trojans, and Athena the Achaeans (4.439).

**The first battle.** The Trojans lose the first man killed in the poem, Echepolus, then Simoeisius, and then Democoon, a son of Priam; their side kills Elephenor, Leucus and Diores (see [[the-first-battle]]). When Democoon falls, “the champions and shining Hector gave ground” (4.505), and the Argives drive forward, until Apollo calls to the Trojans from the citadel:

{{quote:c687b0f3-a0ae-5cfd-8a55-a1bda23e67e2}}

The god's argument is plain: the Achaeans are flesh, not stone or iron, and Achilles is away (see [[apollo]] and [[pergamus]]). The book ends with the two peoples together: “many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another” (4.543–544).
