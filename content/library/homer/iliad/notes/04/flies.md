---

The flies of Book 2 were a swarm, as many as the Achaeans on the plain (2.469–473; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). The fly of Book 4 is one, and harmless. A mother keeps it off her sleeping child with a wave of the hand, and so Athena keeps the arrow off Menelaus: what could kill a king is, to a goddess, a fly (see [[mother-and-fly]]).

The fly returns to Menelaus. In Book 17, over the body of Patroclus, Athena puts into his breast “the daring of a fly”, which keeps trying to bite however often it is driven from a man's skin (17.570–572). The words of Book 4 come back with it. There Athena “kept” the arrow “from his flesh” (ἔεργεν ἀπὸ χροός, 4.130); in Book 17 the fly is “kept off” (ἐργομένη) from a man's skin (χροός, 17.571). The pest the goddess brushed away has become the image of the man she protected.
