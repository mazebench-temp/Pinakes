---

σημάντωρ, “one who gives the signal, commander”, is formed from σημαίνω, “give orders”, the verb Achilles refuses to Agamemnon (1.295–296). When the Achaean battalions move to war like waves breaking on a shore, “to his own men each one of the leaders gave orders; and the rest went in silence, and you would not have said that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts, silent, in fear of their commanders” (σιγῇ δειδιότες σημάντορας, 4.428–431). The commanders speak and the army obeys, and the obedience is heard as silence. The Trojans, whose allies speak many tongues, bleat like ewes in a fold (4.433–438).

Plato's Socrates approved of the line and quoted it, joined to the silent advance of Book 3, as an example of the obedience that makes for self-control (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]). See [[silence]], [[deos]] and [[the-army-and-its-leaders]].
