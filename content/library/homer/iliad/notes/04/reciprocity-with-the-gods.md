**What the god received.** Zeus explains his love for Troy in the terms of exchange: “For my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast, libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us” (4.48–49). Troy has paid its dues faithfully, and it was “the most honored in my heart” (4.46). He gives it up in the next lines to keep peace with Hera (4.37–43). The exchange explains a god's love; it does not bind him. In the last book Zeus speaks the same lines of Hector's sacrifices (24.69–70). See [[geras]] and [[equal-feast]].

**What the archer vowed.** Athena tells Pandarus to pray to Apollo, “the Lycian-born, famed for his bow”, and vow a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs “when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia” (4.101–103). He makes the vow as he draws (4.119–121). The prayer is a bargain in the form of *da ut dabo*: a success now for an offering later. Apollo gives him neither the kill nor the homecoming. See [[euchomai]], [[prayer]] and [[hecatomb]].

**What the oath pledged.** Agamemnon's confidence that the Trojans will pay rests on the rite of Book 3: “the oath is in no way vain, and the blood of lambs, and the libations of unmixed wine” (4.158–159). The victims and the wine were given to the gods as witnesses, and the gods owe their punishment in return, even if it comes late (4.160–161; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[fulfilled-late]]).
