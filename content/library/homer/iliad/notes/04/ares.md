**The Trojans' god.**

{{quote:c99a1945-6921-5957-8d4d-c000d5479a6a}}

τοὺς μέν … τοὺς δέ, “these … those” (4.439): the Trojans, just described shouting in many tongues (4.433–438), and the Achaeans, marching in silence (4.427–432). It is the first time the poem shows Ares at work in the fighting, and he is on the Trojan side, as he will be in Book 5. There Apollo calls on him, and he rouses the Trojans in the likeness of Acamas, leader of the Thracians (5.455–470), until Athena and Diomedes wound him (5.855–898). The two gods of war face each other from the first clash, and in the battle of the gods Athena knocks him down with a stone (21.391–414). See [[athena]].

With them come Terror and Rout (see [[terror]] and [[rout]]), and Strife, his sister, who grows from small to vast and casts the quarrel into the midst of both armies (4.440–445; see [[strife]]). ἀνδροφόνος, “man-slaying”, is his epithet here (see [[man-slaying]]).

**War itself.** Odysseus, stung by Agamemnon's rebuke, asks how he can be said to hold back “whenever we Achaeans rouse sharp Ares against the horse-taming Trojans” (4.351–352). The phrase is Nestor's from Book 2 (ἐγείρομεν ὀξὺν Ἄρηα, 2.440): Ares is the fighting itself. His name also lives in the epithets. Menelaus is “dear to Ares” (ἀρηΐφιλος, 4.13, 4.150; see [[dear-to-ares]]) and “warlike” (ἀρήϊος, 4.98, 4.115, 4.195, 4.205; see [[warlike]]), and the Achaeans are “the warlike sons of the Achaeans” (4.114).
