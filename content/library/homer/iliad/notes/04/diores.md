**Fate.** His death begins with fate: μοῖρα πέδησε, “fate fettered” him (4.517). πεδάω is to bind with fetters (πέδη). The same verb is used at the greatest death of the poem, when “deadly fate fettered” Hector to stay outside the walls (Ἕκτορα δʼ αὐτοῦ μεῖναι ὀλοιὴ μοῖρα πέδησεν, 22.5). See [[moira]].

**The stone.** Peirous hits him beside the ankle with a χερμάδιον, a stone for throwing, and “the shameless stone” (λᾶας ἀναιδής) crushes tendons and bones. The stone that Sisyphus rolls in the underworld is called shameless in the same words (Od. 11.598): a thing that has no regard for anyone (see [[anaideia]]).

**The fall.** He falls on his back “stretching out both his hands to his dear companions, breathing out his life” (4.523–524). The gesture is an appeal, and no one comes in time. The same words tell the fall of Thoon, killed by Antilochus in Book 13 (4.522–523 ~ 13.548–549). Peirous runs up and spears him beside the navel, and his bowels pour out on the ground (4.524–526).

**Side by side.** Thoas the Aetolian kills Peirous at once, but cannot strip him (4.527–535; see [[thoas]] and [[peirous]]). The two leaders lie together:

{{quote:9a1b2ec7-1dfc-5a47-ad89-927cfdc3f48d}}

Diores was one of the four Epeian leaders of the Catalogue, each with ten ships (2.615–624; see [[epeians]]). The last lines of the book widen the picture of the two men to the whole battle: “many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another” (4.543–544).
