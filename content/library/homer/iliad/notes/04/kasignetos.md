**Dear brother.** When the black blood runs from Menelaus' wound, both brothers shudder, in the same words (ῥίγησεν, 4.148, 4.150). Agamemnon takes Menelaus by the hand and speaks to him as to a dead man.

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

φίλε κασίγνητε, θάνατόν νύ τοι ὅρκιʼ ἔταμνον: “dear brother, it was your death, then, that I swore in the oaths” (4.155). The verb is the one for cutting the victims' throats: the oath he cut was his brother's death. He had set his brother out alone to fight for the whole army (4.156). His grief is a brother's and a king's at once. If Menelaus dies, the army will go home, and Agamemnon will come to Argos in disgrace, leaving his brother's bones to rot in Troy (4.169–182; see [[elenchos]]). Menelaus answers him as the stronger of the two: “Take heart, and do not frighten the army of the Achaeans” (4.184). Agamemnon's reply begins “dear Menelaus” (φίλος ὦ Μενέλαε, 4.189), and he sends for the healer. See [[agamemnon]], [[menelaus]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**Strife.** Among the powers that drive the armies together is Eris, “the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares” (Ἄρεος ἀνδροφόνοιο κασιγνήτη ἑτάρη τε, 4.441). War has a family: Terror and Rout go with her, and in Book 13 Rout is Ares' son (13.299). See [[strife]], [[terror]] and [[rout]].

**Sons of Priam.** In the first battle two of Priam's sons appear. Antiphus, of the flashing corselet, throws at Ajax and kills Leucus instead (4.489–493); Odysseus, in his anger, kills Democoon, “a bastard son of Priam”, who had come from Abydos (4.499–504). The Iliad will show these brothers again: Antiphus dies in Book 11 in one chariot with Isus, “one a bastard and one born in wedlock” (11.101–104). See [[antiphus-son-of-priam]], [[democoon]] and [[nothos]].
