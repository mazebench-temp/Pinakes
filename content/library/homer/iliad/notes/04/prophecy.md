summary: Agamemnon foretells the fall of Troy: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish”, since Zeus fulfills an oath even late. The book's signs, the star of Athena's descent, the ill-omened signs at Mycenae and the portents Tydeus obeyed, are all signs heeded or ignored, and its language is the language of fulfillment.

**Fulfillment.** The verb τελέω and its family run through the first half of Book 4. Hera will not have her labor left “unfulfilled” (ἀτέλεστον, 4.26, 4.57). Agamemnon's prophecy rests on the certainty that Zeus “fulfills” an oath: “even if the Olympian has not fulfilled it at once (οὐκ ἐτέλεσσεν), he fulfills it (τελεῖ) in the end, even late … These things will not go unfulfilled” (οὐκ ἀτέλεστα, 4.160–168). He fears that his brother will lie in Troy “with your work unfinished” (ἀτελευτήτῳ ἐπὶ ἔργῳ, 4.175), and imagines a Trojan mocking him: “May Agamemnon fulfill (τελέσειʼ) his anger like this against all” (4.178). See [[fulfilled-late]].

**Signs.** Athena comes down like the star that Zeus sends “as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men” (4.75–77), and the men read her descent as a sign (4.79–85). In Agamemnon's story Zeus turns the Mycenaeans back with “ill-omened signs” (4.381), and Tydeus spares Maeon “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.398). Sthenelus says the sons took Thebes trusting in the portents, and the fathers perished by their own recklessness (4.408–409). See [[teras]] and [[atasthalie]].
