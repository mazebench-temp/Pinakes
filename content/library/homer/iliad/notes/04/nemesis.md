---
summary: Diomedes, rebuked by Agamemnon, will not hold it against him: “I do not blame Agamemnon.” When the Argives drive the Trojans back, Apollo is indignant and calls the Trojans to fight.
passages:
  - 4.411-418 | Diomedes silences Sthenelus
  - 4.507-513 | Apollo rouses the Trojans
---

**Refused.** Agamemnon has told Diomedes that he is a lesser man than his father (4.370–400). Diomedes says nothing, and when Sthenelus answers for him, he tells his companion to sit in silence: “I do not blame (οὐ … νεμεσῶ) Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, for urging the well-greaved Achaeans to fight” (4.413–414). νέμεσις is the indignation at what is improper, and Diomedes judges that there is nothing improper here. A king who will have the glory of victory and the grief of defeat may drive his army as he likes (4.415–417). The refusal is the reverse of the quarrel of Book 1, where Achilles answered Agamemnon's words with indignation that lasted. See [[aidos]], [[diomedes]] and [[praise-and-blame]].

**Felt.** The god's indignation comes at the end of the book. After Odysseus kills Democoon, the Trojan champions and Hector give ground, and the Argives drag away the dead and drive far forward. “But Apollo was indignant (νεμέσησε), looking down from Pergamus” (4.507–508), and shouts to the Trojans to stand. What angers him is the reversal: his people giving way. See [[apollo]] and [[not-stone-or-iron]].
