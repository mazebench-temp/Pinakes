---
summary: Book 4 decides the sack on Olympus and foretells it on earth. Zeus gives up sacred Ilios, the city he honored most, to Hera's anger, and Agamemnon, over his wounded brother, says that a day will come when it will perish.
passages:
  - 4.31-49 | Zeus gives up Troy
  - 4.160-168 | A day will come
---

**On Olympus.** Zeus asks what Priam and his sons have done to Hera, that she rages “to sack the well-built citadel of Ilios” (4.31–33). If she could, she would eat Priam raw (4.35). He gives way, “willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (4.43), though of all the cities under the sun sacred Ilios was the most honored in his heart (4.44–47). In return Hera gives up to him any of her own cities he may wish to sack (4.51–56). The fall of Troy is settled as a bargain between the gods. See [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]].

**On earth.** Agamemnon does not know what the gods have agreed. Over Menelaus' wound he says that the Olympian fulfills an oath, “even late”, and then says what he knows “in my mind and in my heart” (4.160–163):

{{quote:2f616469-d39c-5c58-b7ba-997c641df646}}

Zeus himself, he goes on, will shake his dark aegis over the Trojans “in resentment at this deceit” (4.166–168; see [[aegis]] and [[apate]]). The three lines 4.163–165 are the most famous of the book. Hector speaks them to Andromache in Book 6, of his own city and with the same certainty, and prays to be dead and buried before he hears her cry as she is dragged away (6.447–449, 6.464–465). See [[a-day-will-come]].

**Promises.** The rest of the book speaks of the sack as a reward. The oath-breakers' wives and children will be led away in the ships “when we take the citadel” (4.238–239). With men like the two Ajaxes the city of lord Priam “would soon bow its head, taken and sacked beneath our hands” (4.290–291), as Agamemnon said of ten counselors like Nestor in Book 2 (2.372–374). Diomedes speaks of the glory that will follow Agamemnon if the Achaeans “take sacred Ilios” (4.415–416).

**A city already taken.** One sack is told in Book 4 as done. Sthenelus boasts that he and Diomedes took “the seat of seven-gated Thebes”, trusting the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus, where their fathers perished (4.405–409; see [[the-epigonoi]]). Two of the Achaean leaders have already taken a great walled city. The poem sets their victory beside Troy's future fall and leaves the fall itself beyond its end.
