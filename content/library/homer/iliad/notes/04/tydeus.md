**The measure of the son.** Agamemnon's rebuke of Diomedes is the longest speech of the review, and most of it is about Tydeus. It begins with the comparison:

{{quote:dfb1d8cb-0468-58c8-ab94-3ad316d5a24f}}

Tydeus fought “far ahead of his dear companions”, as a front-fighter; the son, Agamemnon says, cowers. The king's authority is hearsay. He never met Tydeus, and knows him from those who saw him “at his work” (πονεύμενον): the story is κλέος, the report that passes from mouth to mouth (see [[kleos]] and [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). The son does not remember him either. Tydeus left him when he was small, “when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223), and when Diomedes boasts of his father's spearmanship he appeals to what the Achaeans “must have heard” (14.124–125).

**The story.** It has four parts (see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]).

1. *Mycenae* (4.376–381). Tydeus came to Agamemnon's own city “not in war”, a guest (ξεῖνος) with [[Polynices]], to raise allies for the war against Thebes. The Mycenaeans were willing, but Zeus turned them back with ill-omened signs.
2. *The embassy* (4.382–384). When the army reached the [[Asopus]], “the Achaeans”, as Agamemnon calls the Argives of that generation, sent Tydeus ahead as their envoy (see [[embassy]]).
3. *The feast* (4.385–390). He found the [[Cadmeians]] feasting in the house of [[Eteocles]]. A stranger alone among many, he was not afraid. He challenged them to contests and won them all easily, “such a helper was Athena to him” (see [[contests]] and [[xenia]]).
4. *The ambush* (4.391–398). The angry Cadmeians set fifty young men to wait for him on his way back, under [[Maeon]] and [[Polyphontes]]. Tydeus killed them all except Maeon, whom he sent home “obeying the portents of the gods” (see [[ambush]]).

The moral takes one line: “Such was Tydeus the Aetolian; but the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400).

**What the story shows.** It serves Agamemnon less well than he thinks. Its first episode is the refusal of his own Mycenae to join the war, on a sign from Zeus. Its hero is a guest who turns a feast into a contest and a lone envoy who kills fifty men, and its turning point is an act of obedience to the gods. Sthenelus seizes on this: the fathers perished “through their own recklessness”, while the sons obeyed the portents and took the city (4.405–410; see [[sthenelus]]). Diomedes accepts neither verdict. He says nothing, and then he fights (see [[diomedes]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Tydeus elsewhere in the Iliad.** The story returns in pieces. Athena tells Diomedes that she bade Tydeus feast quietly at Thebes, but he challenged the young Cadmeians and beat them all, “such a helper was I to him” (5.800–808). Diomedes prays to her to stand by him as she stood by his father, when Tydeus left the Achaeans at the Asopus, carried a gentle message to the Cadmeians, and on his way back did grim deeds with her help (10.284–290). And Diomedes tells his descent: Tydeus, son of Oeneus, left Calydon for Argos and married a daughter of Adrastus, and the heaped earth covers him at Thebes (14.113–125). Homer never tells how he died. For the later tradition see [[statius-tydeus]].
