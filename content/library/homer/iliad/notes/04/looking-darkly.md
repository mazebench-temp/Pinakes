---
summary: Twice in the review a man answers with a glare: Odysseus to Agamemnon's unjust rebuke (4.349), and Diomedes to Sthenelus, who has contradicted the king (4.411).
passages:
  - 4.349-355 | Odysseus answers Agamemnon
  - 4.411-418 | Diomedes silences Sthenelus
---

**Odysseus.** Agamemnon has accused Odysseus and Menestheus of cowering at the back while others fight, though they are the first to be called to his feasts (4.338–348). “Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said” (4.349): what a word has escaped the barrier of the king's teeth; he will see “the dear father of Telemachus” among the Trojan champions; what he says is empty wind (4.350–355; see [[what-a-word]]). The glare answers a breach of the kind J. P. Holoka took the formula to mark: the king has shamed a leader in front of his men without cause. For once the glare works at once. Agamemnon smiles, sees that he is angry, and takes his word back (4.356–363; see [[smiling-at-him]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Diomedes.** Agamemnon has rebuked Diomedes with his father's deeds, and Diomedes has said nothing, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.401–402). Sthenelus answers for him: do not lie, “we boast that we are far better than our fathers” (4.404–410). Diomedes turns on his own companion:

This time the glare is turned not against the king but in his defense. Sthenelus has overstepped by answering a king who spoke to someone else, and Diomedes, who would not answer for himself, silences his friend with τέττα, “friend”, a word of affection found only here (see [[tetta]], [[diomedes]], [[sthenelus]] and [[aidos]]).

In Book 1 the glare passed between Achilles and Agamemnon, and in Book 2 from Odysseus to Thersites. In Book 4 it answers the king once and defends him once.
