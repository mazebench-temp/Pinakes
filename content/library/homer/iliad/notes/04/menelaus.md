**The victory.** On Olympus Zeus says what Agamemnon claimed at the end of Book 3 (3.457): “the victory belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (4.13). He teases Hera and Athena, the two “helpers of Menelaus” (4.7), who sit apart and watch while Aphrodite saves Paris (4.7–12). If the gods chose peace, Priam's city would stand, and “Menelaus would take Argive Helen back home” (4.19). For a moment the war could end. Hera refuses, and his two helpers arrange for him to be shot (4.20–72; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

**The wound.** The narrator turns to him: “But you, Menelaus, the blessed gods did not forget” (4.127). Athena, who sent the arrow, stands in front of him and brushes it away “as a mother brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). She guides it to the golden clasps of his belt, where the corselet is doubled, and it passes through belt, corselet and band and “grazed the outermost flesh of the man” (4.132–139; see [[belt]] and [[band]]). She wants the oaths broken, not Menelaus dead. It is the first wound that an enemy gives in the poem, and it falls on the man for whom the war is fought.

Homer speaks to Menelaus in the second person twice here (4.127, 4.146) and five times later (7.104, 13.603, 17.679, 17.702, 23.600); only Patroclus is addressed this way more often. Whether this shows the narrator's sympathy or only the convenience of the vocative in the verse is disputed.

**Ivory and purple.**

{{quote:dae55fa8-05d3-5e31-a538-75e6553ebeb3}}

Skin and blood become ivory and purple dye, the work of a Maeonian or Carian woman, and the wound becomes a thing of price: a cheekpiece kept for a king, “both an ornament for the horse and a glory to the driver” (4.145). The eye follows the blood down, from thighs to shins to ankles. See [[ivory-stained-with-purple]], [[ivory]] and [[purple]].

**His answer.** Both brothers shudder at the blood, until Menelaus sees that “the binding and the barbs were outside” and his spirit gathers again (4.148–152). Agamemnon, holding him by the hand, laments him as already dead: his bones will rot in Troy, and a Trojan will leap on his tomb (4.153–182; see [[agamemnon]]). Menelaus answers:

{{quote:6cf7fb06-150e-586f-9a4c-64c3509a6f96}}

Twenty-eight lines get four. He thinks first of the army, which must not be frightened, and then of the facts: the arrow “did not stick in a deadly place”, because belt, kilt and band stopped it (see [[kilt]]). He is the speaker Antenor remembered, “few words but very clear” (3.214).

**Healed.** Machaon draws out the arrow, loosens belt, kilt and band, sucks out the blood and spreads on the wound the drugs of Chiron (4.210–219; see [[machaon]] and [[healing]]). While they are “busy about Menelaus, good at the war cry” (4.220), the Trojan ranks come on.

In Book 4 he is “dear to Ares” (4.13, 4.150), “warlike” (4.98, 4.115, 4.195, 4.205), “glorious” (κυδάλιμος, 4.100, 4.177), “fair-haired” (4.183, 4.210) and “good at the war cry” (4.220). See [[dear-to-ares]], [[warlike]], [[fair-haired]] and [[good-at-the-war-cry]].
