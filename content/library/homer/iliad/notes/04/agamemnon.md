summary: Agamemnon mourns his wounded brother as dead, foretells the fall of Troy for the broken oaths, and sends for a healer. Then he goes through the army on foot, praising Idomeneus, the Ajaxes and Nestor and rebuking Menestheus, Odysseus and Diomedes, and he takes back his words when Odysseus answers in anger.

**The lament.** When Agamemnon sees the black blood run down his brother's thighs, he shudders (4.148–149). He takes Menelaus by the hand and groans, and the companions groan with him (4.153–154):

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

ὅρκια τάμνειν, “to cut oaths”, is the phrase for making a truce over the victims' blood (3.73, 3.252; see [[oath-formulas]]). He cut them, he says, for his brother's death. Grief turns at once to certainty. What the Olympian does not fulfill at once he fulfills late, and the breakers will pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.162), as the curse of Book 3 asked (3.298–301). Then comes the prophecy:

{{quote:2f616469-d39c-5c58-b7ba-997c641df646}}

These are the words in which Zeus named the city he loved (4.46–47), and Hector will speak them to Andromache in grief (4.163–165 = 6.447–449; see [[troy]] and [[a-day-will-come]]). Zeus, Agamemnon goes on, will shake his aegis over the Trojans “in resentment at this deceit” (4.168). The audience has seen what he cannot. The breach was planned on Olympus, where Zeus gave Troy up to Hera and sent Athena to make the Trojans break the oaths (4.37–72). Agamemnon sees Zeus avenging an oath; the audience has seen him bargain away a city he loved. Both lead to the same end. See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**The king's fear.** The rest of the lament is about himself. If Menelaus dies, Agamemnon will come to “thirsty Argos” in disgrace, “for at once the Achaeans will remember their native land” and leave Helen to Priam as a thing to boast of (4.169–175). He has seen it happen: the army ran for the ships when he tested it, and his words are almost those of Hera and Athena in that crisis (4.173–174 ~ 2.160–161, 2.176–177). He imagines a Trojan leaping on his brother's tomb and mocking the king who brought an army in vain and sailed home with empty ships (4.176–181; see [[tis-speech]]), and ends, “then may the wide earth gape for me” (4.182). The wounded man has to comfort the king (4.183–187; see [[menelaus]]). Agamemnon sends [[Talthybius]] for Machaon, and his message counts the cost in glory: “fame for him, and grief for us” (4.197; see [[kleos]] and [[penthos]]).

**On foot.** As the Trojans come on, the narrator turns to the listener and speaks up for him (see [[apostrophe]]):

{{quote:e54d1847-9556-5821-b657-133af625beab}}

The three denials name the faults he is about to find in others. He will ask Menestheus and Odysseus why they stand “cowering” (καταπτώσσοντες, 4.340, the narrator's word at 4.224), and Diomedes why he “cowers” (πτώσσεις, 4.371). He leaves his chariot with [[Eurymedon]], to be kept close for the moment when weariness takes him “as he went commanding so many” (4.230), and goes through the ranks on foot, “like a master” (κοιρανέων, 4.250), as Odysseus went through the army in Book 2 (2.207; see [[koiranos]]). The verb for his round, ἐπεπωλεῖτο (“ranged through”, 4.231, 4.250), gave this part of the book its ancient title, Ἀγαμέμνονος ἐπιπώλησις, “Agamemnon's review” (see [[titles-of-book-4]]). He has encouragement for the eager and “angry words” for the slack (4.232–249; see [[exhortation]] and [[argives]]). See [[the-review]] and [[the-review-of-the-army]].

**Praise and blame.** Then he goes from leader to leader. He finds Idomeneus, the Ajaxes and Nestor ready, and praises them; he finds Menestheus, Odysseus and Diomedes waiting, and rebukes them (see [[idomeneus]], [[ajax]], [[nestor]], [[menestheus]], [[odysseus]] and [[diomedes]]). The coin of both praise and blame is the feast. Idomeneus' cup “always stands full, as mine does” (4.262–263); Menestheus and Odysseus are “the first to hear my call to the feast” (4.343), and so they should be the first to “meet the blazing battle” (4.342). Sarpedon will state the same bargain in the same words: kings honored with seats, meat and full cups must stand among the foremost and meet the blazing battle (12.310–316). See [[feast]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]].

His judgment is quick, and not always just. The narrator has already explained why the Athenians and the Cephallenians are standing still: their men had not yet heard the war cry, and they were waiting for another column to begin the charge (4.331–335). Agamemnon rebukes before he asks. When Odysseus answers him “looking at him darkly” (4.349), he gives way:

{{quote:c6915534-a83a-59c7-9bf5-533b3ab46e6e}}

He smiles, as Zeus smiles when he softens his harsh words to Athena (8.38–40; see [[smiling-at-him]]), gives Odysseus his full titles, and promises to set things right later. Diomedes gets no such amends. He hears the rebuke and the long story of his father in silence (4.370–402; see [[tydeus]]), and Sthenelus answers instead. Agamemnon says nothing more. Diomedes remembers: in the assembly of Book 9 he reminds the king that he called him unwarlike and a coward before the Danaans, and tells him that Zeus gave him the scepter but not courage (9.32–39).

The review ends with Diomedes leaping down in his armor (4.419–421), and the Danaan battalions move to war in silence, “in fear of their commanders” (4.431). Unjust in part, the king's round has done its work.
