ξεῖνος comes twice in Agamemnon's story of Tydeus, once in each of its senses.

**A guest.** Tydeus came to Mycenae “not in war, a guest (ξεῖνος), with godlike Polynices, gathering an army” (4.376–377). The two exiles came as guests to ask for allies, and their hosts were willing, until Zeus turned them back with ill-omened signs (4.379–381). The host who receives a guest owes him help, and here only a god's sign releases him. Agamemnon did not see any of this himself; he tells it as he heard it from those who saw Tydeus (4.374–375).

**A stranger.** Sent ahead as an envoy, Tydeus found the Cadmeians feasting in the house of Eteocles, “and there, though he was a stranger (ξεῖνός περ ἐών), horse-driving Tydeus was not afraid, alone among the many Cadmeians” (4.387–388). A guest is under the protection of Zeus, and an envoy above all. Tydeus challenged his hosts to contests and won them all, with Athena's help, and the Cadmeians answered with an ambush of fifty men on the road home (4.389–395). Tydeus killed them all but one (4.396–398). The hosts broke the bond by trying to kill an envoy; in Troy too, Antimachus once urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came as an envoy with Odysseus (11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]).

In Book 6 Diomedes meets Glaucus in battle, learns that their grandfathers were guest-friends, and refuses to fight him. There he says that he does not remember Tydeus, who left him when he was little, when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes (6.215–231).
