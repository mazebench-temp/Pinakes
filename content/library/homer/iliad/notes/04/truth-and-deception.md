**A hidden intent.** Zeus speaks to Hera “with sly intent” (παραβλήδην, 4.6): he proposes peace in words meant to provoke. Whether he wants peace or wants it refused, the poem does not say (see [[kertomia]]).

**A goddess in disguise.** Athena goes among the Trojans “in the likeness of a man, Laodocus, son of Antenor, a strong spearman” (4.86–87). Pandarus hears a comrade's advice, not a goddess' command, and takes it (4.93–104). The disguise is the first deceit of the book, and it produces the second. See [[laodocus]] and [[divine-intervention]].

**Liars.** The shot fired in a truce turns the oaths into a lie. Agamemnon calls it “this deceit” (τῆσδʼ ἀπάτης, 4.168) and tells the army that father Zeus will be no helper to liars (ψευδέσσι, 4.235; see [[apate]], [[pseudos]] and [[no-helper-to-liars]]). The men who break an oath are liars whatever the gods did to bring it about.

**A false judgment.** Sthenelus opens his retort to Agamemnon, “do not lie, when you know how to speak plainly” (4.404). The charge is not that the king invented the story of Tydeus but that he judged it wrongly. ψεύδομαι covers both.
