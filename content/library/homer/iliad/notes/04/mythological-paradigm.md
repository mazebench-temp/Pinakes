summary: Agamemnon rebukes Diomedes with the story of his father Tydeus at Thebes (4.370–400), the longest paradigm of the book; Nestor appeals to “the men of old” and to his own youth (4.308–309, 4.318–319); and Sthenelus answers that the sons are better than their fathers (4.404–410).

**Tydeus.** Agamemnon's rebuke is built as a ring. It begins with a question, “why do you cower, why do you stare at the bridges of war?” (4.371), and a denial, “Tydeus was not one to cower like this” (4.372); it tells the story; and it ends with the lesson: “Such was Tydeus the Aetolian; but the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400; see [[ring-composition]]). Between comes the story, told from hearsay, “so said those who saw him at his work; for I myself never met him or saw him” (4.374–375): the visit to Mycenae with Polynices, the ill-omened signs, the embassy from the Asopus, the contests won at the Cadmeians' feast, the ambush of fifty and the one man sent home (4.376–398; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]], [[embassy]] and [[contests]]).

The story is shaped for its hearer. It shows a man alone among many, fearless, and helped by Athena, which is what Diomedes will be in Book 5. Athena tells it again to rouse him there, with a detail of her own, that she had told Tydeus to feast quietly (5.800–813), and Diomedes recalls it in his prayer to her in Book 10 (10.284–294). As M. M. Willcock argued, a paradigm is fitted to the moment, and here one story serves three speakers (see [[tydeus]] and [[diomedes]]).

**Nestor.** Nestor closes his orders to the horsemen with the authority of the past: “This is how the men of old sacked cities and walls, keeping this purpose and this spirit in their breasts” (4.308–309; see [[battle-order]]). To Agamemnon's wish that he were young he answers with a memory he does not tell: he would like to be as he was “when I killed brilliant Ereuthalion” (4.319). The story itself comes in Book 7 (7.132–156; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

The fathers perished “through their own recklessness” (ἀτασθαλίῃσιν), and the sons trusted the portents of the gods. The example of the past is denied its authority (see [[the-epigonoi]] and [[better-than-our-fathers]]). Diomedes silences him, and accepts the paradigm in silence (4.411–418; see [[looking-darkly]]).
