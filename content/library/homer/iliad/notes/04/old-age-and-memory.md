Agamemnon's story of Tydeus is memory of another kind, a report of what others saw.

**The old man's army.** Agamemnon finds Nestor marshaling his men around his captains (4.293–296). He puts the horsemen in front, the best of the foot soldiers behind as a bulwark, and the cowards in the middle, “so that even an unwilling man would have to fight” (4.297–300), and he gives his horsemen their orders out of the past: “This is how the men of old sacked cities and walls, keeping this purpose and this spirit in their breasts” (4.308–309). The narrator adds, “So the old man urged them on, long skilled in wars” (4.310). See [[battle-order]].

**The wish and the answer.** Agamemnon is glad, and wishes him young: “Old man, I wish that, as the spirit is in your breast, so your knees might follow, and your strength be steadfast. But old age, the common lot, wears you down” (4.313–315; see [[gounata]]).

{{quote:e11a5ff3-2647-523f-8a92-c6c75fdc877b}}

Nestor wishes it too, and names the day he would choose, when he killed Ereuthalion; the story is told in full in Book 7 (7.132–156; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). He does not complain. The gods do not give men everything at once; he was young then, and is old now; he will give orders “with counsel and with words, for that is the prize of the old”, and leave the spears to the younger men who trust in their strength (see [[all-things-at-once]], [[prize-of-the-old]] and [[geron]]).

**Memory by report.** Agamemnon's own memory of the past is borrowed. He tells Diomedes the deeds of Tydeus at Thebes, “so said those who saw him at his work; for I myself never met him or saw him” (4.374–375). Nestor speaks from what he did; Agamemnon from what he heard. Sthenelus does not accept the old story as a measure: the sons did better (4.404–410; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]).
