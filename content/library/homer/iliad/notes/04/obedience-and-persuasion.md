**Persuaded.** Athena asks Pandarus, “Will you listen to me now?” (ἦ ῥά νύ μοί τι πίθοιο, 4.93), and the narrator ends the scene: “she persuaded the mind of the fool” (4.104). Persuasion here is the goddess's weapon, and it works on a man with little sense. See [[peitho]] and [[phrenes]].

**Obeyed.** The narrator's formula of compliance comes twice: the father of men and gods “did not disobey” Hera (4.68), and the herald Talthybius “did not disobey” Agamemnon (4.198). It is unusual for Zeus to be on the obeying side.

**Compelled.** Nestor does not rely on persuasion. He drives the cowards into the middle of his formation, “so that even an unwilling man would have to fight” (ἀναγκαίῃ, “by necessity”, 4.299–300). The Achaean army as a whole marches “silent, in fear of their commanders” (4.431; see [[semaino]]).

**Obeying the gods.** In Agamemnon's story Tydeus spares one of his ambushers, “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.398), and Sthenelus says that the sons took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (4.408). Obedience to the gods' signs is what wins (see [[teras]]).

**Obeying the king.** Diomedes does not answer Agamemnon's rebuke, out of awe for the king (4.401–402), and when Sthenelus answers it he orders him, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (4.412), in the words with which Zeus silenced Hera (1.565). Plato's Socrates took the line as a model of the obedience to rulers that makes for self-control (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]). See [[silence]] and [[tetta]].
