**Chosen.** “Then he removed the lid of his quiver, and took out an arrow, feathered, never shot before, a source of black pains” (4.116–117; see [[quiver]]). ἀβλής, “not yet shot”, occurs only here. πτερόεις, “feathered”, is the adjective that makes words “winged” (see [[feathered]] and [[winged-words]]). ἕρμα is a prop or support: the props under a beached ship are ἕρματα (1.486), and Sarpedon was the ἕρμα of his city (16.549). The arrow is the thing on which black pains rest, their source. The phrase returns when Agamemnon hopes for a healer's drugs “that will stop the black pains” (4.191; see [[black-pains]] and [[healing]]), and three times the arrow is πικρός, “bitter”, sharp to the flesh (4.118, 4.134, 4.217; see [[bitter]]).

**Its parts.** The poem names them as they are used. The γλυφίδες, “notches” (4.122), are the nock cut in the butt of the shaft to take the string; Odysseus too draws “the string and the notches” (Odyssey 21.419). The head is iron (σίδηρον, 4.123; see [[iron]]). It was fastened to the shaft with a binding of sinew (νεῦρον), and it had barbs (ὄγκοι), which made it hard to draw out (4.151, 4.214). The arrow is ὀξυβελής, “sharp-pointed”, and “eager to fly into the throng” (4.126; see [[sharp]] and [[bow]]).

**The wound.** Athena turns the arrow to the belt, and it goes through belt, corselet and band and “grazed the outermost flesh of the man” (4.132–139; see [[belt]], [[corselet]] and [[band]]). ἐπέγραψε, “grazed”, is from γράφω, “scratch”, the verb of the baneful signs scratched on Bellerophon's folded tablet (6.168–169) and, in later Greek, of writing. The brothers shudder at the blood, until Menelaus sees “that the binding and the barbs were outside” (4.151): the head had not gone in past its barbs, and the wound was shallow. When Machaon draws the arrow out of the belt, “the sharp barbs were broken back” (4.213–214). An arrow that had gone deep had to be cut out, as Patroclus cuts the arrow from Eurypylus' thigh (11.844–845).

**Words.** ὀϊστός and ἰός are the arrow, and to shoot one is ὀϊστεύω (4.100, 4.196, 4.206). βέλος is any missile. In Book 4 it is Pandarus' arrow, “brought down by your arrow” (βέλεϊ, 4.99), “the piercing shaft” (βέλος ἐχεπευκές, 4.129, the phrase of Apollo's arrows at 1.51), “the sharp shaft” (4.185); but at 4.498 it is the spear Odysseus throws, and βέλεα are the missiles of every kind that fly over the fighting (4.465, 4.542). ἰόν at 4.278 is not an arrow but a participle, “going”, from εἶμι.

The Odyssey knows arrows smeared with a man-killing drug (Odyssey 1.261–262). No arrow in the Iliad is said to be poisoned.
