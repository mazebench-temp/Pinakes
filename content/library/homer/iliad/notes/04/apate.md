summary: Agamemnon foresees Zeus shaking his dark aegis over the Trojans “in resentment at this deceit”, the breaking of the truce. The audience has seen the deceit arranged on Olympus and carried out by a goddess in disguise.
grc: [ἀπάτης]
passages:
  - 4.155-168 | Agamemnon on the broken oath
---

Agamemnon ends his speech over the wounded Menelaus with a prophecy: a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish, “and Zeus, son of Cronus, throned on high, who dwells in the upper air, will himself shake his dark aegis over them all, in resentment at this deceit (τῆσδʼ ἀπάτης κοτέων)” (4.166–168). The deceit is the shot fired in a truce, which turned the oaths into a lie (see [[pseudos]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

Agamemnon lays it on the Trojans. The audience knows more. The deceit was decided on Olympus, where Hera asked and Zeus agreed that Athena should contrive that the Trojans be the first to break the oaths (4.64–72), and it was carried out by deceit: the goddess went among the Trojans in the likeness of Laodocus, son of Antenor, to persuade Pandarus (4.86–104). The god whom Agamemnon expects to punish the deceit is the god who allowed it. The poem lets both stand. Pandarus chose to shoot, and the narrator calls him a fool for it (4.104); the gods arranged it. See [[who-is-to-blame]], [[truth-and-deception]], [[kotos]] and [[plato-broken-oaths]].
