**Pandarus.** The prayer is given twice, as an instruction and as a deed, in the same three verses (4.101–103, 4.119–121; see [[message-repeated]]).

1. **Setting and gesture.** None. He prays in the middle of shooting, with the arrow already on the string (4.118), behind the shields his companions hold before him (4.113–115).
2. **Invocation.** “Apollo, the Lycian-born, famed for his bow” (Λυκηγενέϊ κλυτοτόξῳ), titles that suit an archer's god and an archer's home (see [[lycian-born]] and [[famed-for-his-bow]]). There is no “hear me” (see [[hear-me]]).
3. **Claim.** None from the past. Chryses could remind Apollo of a temple roofed and thigh-pieces burned (1.39–41); Pandarus offers a vow, a claim on the future.
4. **Request.** Not spoken. The vow says it: a “glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs” if he comes home to Zeleia (see [[hecatomb]]).
5. **Answer.** None is told. The arrow flies and Athena turns it aside (4.127–133). The poem does not say that Apollo heard, and the vow is never paid.

The prayer is reported in indirect speech, where Chryses' prayers were quoted in full. The man who prays is doing what a goddess told him to do, and the narrator has just called him a fool (4.104). See [[prayer]] and [[pandarus]].

**Agamemnon.** Before the Ajaxes he prays in the form of a wish: “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, there were such a spirit in the breasts of all!” (4.288–289). The three gods are invoked, the wish follows, and the second half repeats his wish for ten counselors in Book 2, “Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head, taken and sacked beneath our hands” (4.290–291 = 2.373–374). It is praise in the shape of a prayer, and no god is asked to answer it (see [[if-only-father-zeus]]).
