summary: Twice Agamemnon weighs the Trojan archer's fame against “grief for us”, and at the end of the review Diomedes weighs Agamemnon's glory if Troy falls against the “great grief” that will be his if the Achaeans are cut down.
passages:
  - 4.193-197 | Agamemnon sends for Machaon
  - 4.412-418 | Diomedes silences Sthenelus
---

In Book 4 πένθος is always the other side of a balance. Agamemnon's message to Machaon ends, “fame for him, and grief for us” (τῷ μὲν κλέος, ἄμμι δὲ πένθος, 4.197), and Talthybius repeats it (4.207; see [[fame-for-him-grief-for-us]] and [[kleos]]). One man's success is the other side's mourning, and the line gives each in a half-verse.

Diomedes uses the same shape when he refuses to resent Agamemnon's rebuke. The king has the right to drive the army on, “for glory will go with him if the Achaeans cut down the Trojans and take sacred Ilios, and great grief (μέγα πένθος) will be his if the Achaeans are cut down” (4.415–417). Here the two halves belong to one man. The king who takes the glory of the army's victory also takes the grief of its defeat, and so he may urge it on as he likes. See [[kydos]], [[nemesis]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].

Agamemnon's own grief over his brother has another word: “I will have dread grief (ἄχος) for you, Menelaus, if you die” (4.169; see [[achos]]).
