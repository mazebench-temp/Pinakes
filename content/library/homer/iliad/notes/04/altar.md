---
summary: Zeus gives up Troy with regret, for his altar there “never lacked an equal share of the feast, libation and the savor of the burnt fat” (4.48–49).
grc: [βωμὸς]
---

The altar is named once in Book 4, by Zeus, in the lines that give his reason for loving Troy above all cities (4.44–49; see [[zeus]] and [[troy]]). The altar stands for the whole of a city's worship: the sacrifices and feasts it offers, the libations poured and the savor of the burnt fat that rises from it, which the gods receive as their “prize” (γέρας). The same two lines return in Book 24, when Zeus explains why the gods honored Hector (24.69–70), and when Zeus pities Hector in Book 22 he remembers the thigh-pieces of oxen Hector burned for him on the peaks of Ida and on the citadel of Troy (22.170–172). Zeus loved the city whose altar fed him, and yields it all the same. See [[sacrifice]], [[equal-feast]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].
