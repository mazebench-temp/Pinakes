summary: Athena tells Pandarus to vow Apollo a hecatomb of firstborn lambs, and he vows it as he shoots (4.101–103, 4.119–121). Agamemnon says that the blood of the lambs of the oath is not in vain (4.158), and in a simile the ewes of a rich man's fold bleat when they hear their lambs (4.435).

Book 4 has three kinds of lambs.

**Promised to a god.** Athena tells Pandarus to pray to Apollo, the Lycian-born, “that you will offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia” (4.101–103), and he repeats the vow word for word as he fits the arrow to the string (4.119–121). ἀρνῶν πρωτογόνων, “firstborn lambs”, are the first lambs of the season. A ἑκατόμβη was once a sacrifice of a hundred oxen; here it is a great offering of lambs (see [[hecatomb]] and [[lycian-born]]). The vow depends on the archer coming home, and Pandarus dies in the next book (5.290–296). See [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]].

**Killed for an oath.** Over his wounded brother Agamemnon recalls the lambs of Book 3.

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

The lambs were killed on the plain, and their blood showed what would happen to the man who broke the oath (3.292–301; see [[oath]] and [[like-this-wine]]). Now Agamemnon holds the blood to its meaning: if the Olympian has not fulfilled the curse at once, he will fulfill it late (4.160–162). The second line of his words repeats Nestor's outburst in Book 2, when the army was running for the ships: “the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted” (2.341, 4.159).

**Calling their mothers.** In the simile of the Trojan advance, the ewes in a rich man's fold bleat without pause “as they hear the voices of their lambs” (4.435; see [[sheep-in-the-fold]]).
