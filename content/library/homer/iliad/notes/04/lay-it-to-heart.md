---
summary: Zeus uses the line to set a condition on his gift of Troy to Hera: when he wants to destroy a city dear to her, she must not stand in his way (4.39–42).
grc: ["ἐνὶ φρεσὶ βάλλεο σῇσιν"]
---

“And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart” (4.39 = 1.297). Zeus has given way to Hera's hatred of Troy, and the formula turns his speech from the concession to its price: “whenever I too am eager to sack a city, one I choose, where men dear to you were born, do not try to hinder my anger, but let me be” (4.40–42). The warning is also a bargain, and Hera accepts it at once with her three cities (4.51–56; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

In Book 1 Achilles used the line to draw his limit before Agamemnon: the girl he would give up, nothing more (1.297–303). Here too it draws a line, between what Zeus gives and what he keeps for himself. The line is used seventeen times in Homer, and it often marks the turn to a warning or a prophecy.
