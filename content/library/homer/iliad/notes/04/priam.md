**On Olympus.** The gods speak of Priam more than the men do. If peace were made, “the city of lord Priam would still be inhabited” (4.18). Hera toiled to gather the army “to bring evils on Priam and his sons” (4.28). Zeus asks her what evils Priam and his sons have done to her, and imagines her going in through the gates to “eat Priam raw, and the sons of Priam, and the other Trojans” (4.31–36). Then he names what he loved above all the cities of men: “sacred Ilios … and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (4.46–47). ἐϋμμελίης, “of the good ash spear”, from μελίη, the ash tree and the spear made from it, is Priam's epithet in these lines (see [[with-good-ash-spear]]). Zeus names him as he gives him up.

The words of Zeus' love become the words of Troy's doom (see [[troy]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Agamemnon also fears that if Menelaus dies the army will go home and “leave to Priam and the Trojans a thing to boast of, Argive Helen” (4.173–174), as Hera and Athena warned in Book 2 (2.160–161, 2.176–177). He wishes that “the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head” (4.290), the wish he made in Book 2 (2.373).

**His sons.** Two sons of Priam fight in the first battle. Antiphus, “son of Priam” (Πριαμίδης), throws at Ajax and kills Odysseus' companion Leucus (4.489–493; see [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]). Democoon, a bastard son “who had come to him from Abydos, from his swift mares”, is killed by Odysseus in revenge (4.499–504; see [[democoon]]). He is the first of Priam's sons to die in the poem.
