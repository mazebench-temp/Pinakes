---
summary: Agamemnon imagines his brother dead and buried in Troy, and a Trojan leaping on his tomb and mocking the Achaeans who sailed home (4.174–182).
passages:
  - 4.169-182 | A Trojan leaps on Menelaus' tomb
---

If Menelaus dies, Agamemnon says, the Achaeans will think at once of home and leave Helen to the Trojans; Menelaus' bones will rot in the earth of Troy, “with your work unfinished”, and one of the overweening Trojans will leap on his tomb (τύμβῳ ἐπιθρῴσκων) and say: “May Agamemnon fulfill his anger like this against all, as now he has led an army of the Achaeans here in vain” (4.169–181). The tomb that should keep a man's fame becomes the Trojans' monument of Achaean failure, and the imagined speaker jumps on it.

Hector will imagine the opposite. The tomb of the man he kills in single combat will stand by the Hellespont, and a sailor in years to come will say “This is the mound of a man who died long ago … shining Hector killed”, and Hector's fame will never perish (7.84–91). Both speeches picture what “someone will say” in the future (see [[tis-speech]]). Agamemnon's is a fear, Hector's a boast. See [[agamemnon]] and [[menelaus]].
