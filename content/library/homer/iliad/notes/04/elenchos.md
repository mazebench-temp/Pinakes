---
summary: Agamemnon fears that he will go home “in utter disgrace” if Menelaus dies of his wound, and in the review he calls the Argives who hold back “you disgraces”.
passages:
  - 4.169-182 | Agamemnon fears the return
  - 4.240-249 | Agamemnon rebukes the laggards
---

The word comes twice in Book 4, both times from Agamemnon, and it points first at himself and then at his army.

**The king's fear.** Holding his wounded brother by the hand, Agamemnon imagines Menelaus dead and the army gone home: “And I would come in utter disgrace (ἐλέγχιστος) to thirsty Argos” (4.171). The Achaeans will remember their native land, Helen will be left to the Trojans as a thing to boast of, and Menelaus' bones will rot in Troy with his work unfinished (4.172–175). The disgrace is what others will say, and he imagines a Trojan leaping on his brother's tomb to say it (4.176–182). In Book 2 Odysseus had said the same of the army's flight: the Achaeans wanted to make Agamemnon “the most despised (ἐλέγχιστον) of all mortal men” (2.284–285). See [[agamemnon]], [[euchomai]] and [[may-the-earth-gape]].

**The army's disgrace.** In the review Agamemnon turns the word on the men who hold back: Ἀργεῖοι ἰόμωροι ἐλεγχέες, οὔ νυ σέβεσθε; “Argives, you arrow-fighters, you disgraces, have you no shame?” (4.242). The king who fears disgrace for himself uses it as a goad on others. See [[sebas]], [[arrow-fighters]] and [[praise-and-blame]].
