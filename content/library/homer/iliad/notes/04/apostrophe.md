**Menelaus.** Pandarus' arrow flies, and the narrator turns to its target: “But you, Menelaus, the blessed gods did not forget” (οὐδὲ σέθεν Μενέλαε θεοὶ μάκαρες λελάθοντο, 4.127). The story continues in the third person, with Athena turning the arrow and the blood running from the wound, and at the end of the ivory simile the address returns: “so, Menelaus, your thighs were stained with blood” (τοῖοί τοι Μενέλαε μιάνθην αἵματι μηροί, 4.146). The two addresses frame the wound. They are the first time the narrator of the Iliad speaks to one of his characters, and Menelaus will be addressed five times more (see [[menelaus]], [[ivory-stained-with-purple]] and [[mother-and-fly]]).

**The listener.** At the start of the review the narrator speaks to his hearer: “Then you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing, nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight, but very eager for the battle where men win glory” (4.223–225). The negatives rule out, one by one, what an onlooker might have feared from the king who had just lamented his brother (see [[the-review]] and [[litotes]]). As the Achaeans advance, he speaks again: “you would not have said that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts” (4.429–430), and the silence of the army is described as it would have struck a witness (see [[silence]]).

**The onlooker.** The book ends with a man imagined walking through the fighting unhurt, led by Athena by the hand and kept safe from the missiles. Even he “could make light of it” no longer, “for many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another” (4.539–544). The first battle of the poem is judged by a witness who cannot be hurt, and found grievous.
