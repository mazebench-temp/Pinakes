---
summary: Pandarus takes the lid off his quiver and draws out an arrow “never shot before” (4.116–117).
grc: [φαρέτρης, πῶμα]
en: [lid]
---

“Then he removed the lid of his quiver, and took out an arrow, feathered, never shot before, a source of black pains” (4.116–117). The lid (πῶμα) is what made Apollo's quiver “close-covered” (ἀμφηρεφής, 1.45), and it kept the arrows in and dry. The Cyclops closes his cave with its door-stone as easily as a man puts the lid on a quiver (Odyssey 9.313–314).

The verb is the one Homer uses for stripping armor from the dead, συλάω. Pandarus “uncovered” his bow (ἐσύλα, 4.105) and “removed” the quiver's lid (σύλα, 4.116), as Elephenor, later in the book, is eager “to strip off” the armor of the man he drags away (συλήσειε, 4.466). The bow too had a cover: Penelope takes Odysseus' bow from its peg “with the case in which it lay” (γωρυτός, Odyssey 21.53–54), and Pandarus says that he took his bow from its peg the day he led his men to Troy (5.209–211).

The quiver is ἰοδόκος, “arrow-holding”, in the Iliad's and the Odyssey's other scenes of archery: Teucer's (15.443–444) and Odysseus' (Odyssey 21.11–12). See [[arrows]] and [[bow]].
