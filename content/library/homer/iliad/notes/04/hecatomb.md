In Book 1 the hecatomb was the price of Apollo's favor, owed and paid at Chryse (1.93–100, 1.430–474). In Book 4 it is promised. Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, tells Pandarus how to pray:

{{quote:62046cc7-8f34-5a58-853b-51bbda20bdcd}}

He does as he is told, in the same three verses with one word changed, εὔχεο, “pray”, to εὔχετο, “prayed” (4.119–121). The vow is a bargain with the god, the prayer's claim put in the future: if the shot succeeds and he comes home, Apollo will have his hecatomb (see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

The victims are lambs, ἀρνῶν πρωτογόνων, “firstborn lambs”, the first young of the season (see [[lambs]]). A hecatomb, “a hundred oxen” in name, is never a hundred and need not be oxen: in Book 1 it was bulls and goats, or lambs and goats (1.66, 1.316). κλειτός, “glorious, renowned”, is a word for what men will talk of. And the place is fixed, “when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia” (see [[zeleia]]). The vow is not paid. The arrow wounds but does not kill, and in Book 5 Pandarus dies before he can go home (5.290–296; see [[pandarus]]).
