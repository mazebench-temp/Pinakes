---
summary: Agamemnon ends his prophecy of Troy's fall with an assurance, “These things will not go unfulfilled” (4.168). The language of fulfillment runs through the book, from Hera's labor, which must not be left unfulfilled, to the oath that Zeus fulfills late.
grc: ["ἔσσεται οὐκ ἀτέλεστα"]
en: [will not go unfulfilled]
---

**The assurance.** Agamemnon has foretold the day when Ilios will perish and Zeus will shake the aegis over the Trojans “in resentment at this deceit”, and he ends: τὰ μὲν ἔσσεται οὐκ ἀτέλεστα, “These things will not go unfulfilled” (4.168). It is the strong form of the formula turned into a denial of its opposite (see [[litotes]]), and it follows a claim of certain knowledge: “For I know this well in my mind and in my heart” (4.163; see [[a-day-will-come]] and [[oath]]).

**The language of fulfillment.** As in Books 1 and 2, τελέω (“bring to its end, fulfill”) and its negatives run through the book.

- Hera asks Zeus how he can wish to make her labor “vain and unfulfilled” (ἅλιον … ἀτέλεστον, 4.26), and insists that “my labor too must not be left unfulfilled” (οὐκ ἀτέλεστον, 4.57).
- Agamemnon says that even if the Olympian “has not fulfilled” the oath at once (οὐκ ἐτέλεσσεν), “he fulfills it in the end, even late” (ἔκ τε καὶ ὀψὲ τελεῖ, 4.160–161). ὀψέ, “late”, recalls the sign at Aulis, “late in coming, late in fulfillment” (ὀψιτέλεστον, 2.325).
- If Menelaus dies, he will lie in Troy “with your work unfinished” (ἀτελευτήτῳ ἐπὶ ἔργῳ, 4.175), the word Zeus used of his nod, which cannot be “unfulfilled” (1.527).
- The imagined Trojan on Menelaus' tomb says: “May Agamemnon fulfill his anger like this against all” (χόλον τελέσειʼ, 4.178).

The gods speak of fulfilling their labor against Troy, the king of fulfilling an oath's penalty, and the enemy of fulfilling a king's anger in failure. See [[plan-of-zeus]].
