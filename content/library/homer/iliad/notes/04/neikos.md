summary: On Olympus Zeus gives way so that the quarrel over Troy may not become a cause of strife between him and Hera. In the review Agamemnon rebukes the slack, Menestheus and Odysseus, and Diomedes, and takes back the rebuke that angers Odysseus; in the battle Strife casts “the quarrel, common to all” among the armies.

**Avoided.** Zeus yields Troy to Hera rather than quarrel: “Do as you wish; let this quarrel (νεῖκος) not become hereafter a great cause of strife (ἔρισμα) between you and me” (4.37–38). νεῖκος and ἔρις stand side by side, as in Agamemnon's complaint that Zeus throws him “into fruitless strife and quarrels” (2.376). On Olympus a quarrel is settled by an exchange of cities (4.40–56). See [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]].

**Spoken.** Agamemnon's review of the army is framed by the verb. “Those whom he saw holding back from hateful war, these he would rebuke (νεικείεσκε) with angry words” (4.240–241), and he rebukes (νείκεσσεν) Menestheus and Odysseus (4.336) and then Diomedes (4.368). A king's rebuke is part of his office, and it is a risk. Odysseus answers with anger, and Agamemnon smiles and takes it back: “I do not rebuke you beyond measure (οὔτέ σε νεικείω περιώσιον), nor give you orders” (4.359). Diomedes answers with silence, “in awe of the rebuke (ἐνιπήν) of the king he revered” (4.402). See [[praise-and-blame]].

**Cast.** When the armies close, Strife, the sister of Ares, “then too cast the quarrel, common to all (νεῖκος ὁμοίιον), into their midst, as she went through the throng, increasing the groaning of men” (4.444–445). The translation keeps the two words apart: Strife is the power, the quarrel is what she throws, like a missile, between the two armies. ὁμοίιος is either “common to all” or “grievous”; both explanations are ancient. See [[strife]] and [[eris]].
