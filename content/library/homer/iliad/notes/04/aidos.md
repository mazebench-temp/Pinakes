Agamemnon has told Diomedes that his father Tydeus never cowered and that he himself is worse in battle, though better in assembly (4.370–400). The line that follows gives Diomedes' answer, which is no answer. αἰδεσθείς, “in awe”, is the verb of αἰδώς, and αἰδοῖος, “revered”, is the king to whom αἰδώς is due, as Priam is to Helen (3.172). The word ἐνιπή, “rebuke”, names what Diomedes bears. Respect for the king's position outweighs the injury to his own honor, and he keeps silent.

He explains himself only when Sthenelus answers for them both. He does not blame Agamemnon, since the glory and the grief of the war will be the king's (4.412–417; see [[nemesis]]). His answer to the charge itself comes later and at the proper time. In the assembly of Book 9, “as is right, my lord, in the assembly” (ἣ θέμις ἐστίν, ἄναξ, ἀγορῇ, 9.33), he reminds Agamemnon that he was the first to insult his courage before the Danaans (9.32–36). Plato's Socrates chose Diomedes' next words, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (4.412), as an example of the self-control the young should hear (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]).
