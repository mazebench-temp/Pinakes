**The glory of a shot.** Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, promises Pandarus that he would win “thanks and glory (χάριν καὶ κῦδος) from all the Trojans, and most of all from king Alexander”, if he killed Menelaus (4.95–96; see [[charis]]). κῦδος is the shine of a success, the standing a man has in the moment of victory, and a king's brother brought down by an arrow would give it. The shot succeeds only by half. Menelaus lives, and the man who grieves over him does not even know the archer's name (see [[kleos]]).

**A glory to the driver.** The simile that describes Menelaus' wound turns the blood into an ornament. A Maeonian or Carian woman stains ivory with purple to make a cheekpiece for horses; many horsemen pray to carry it, but it lies in a chamber as a delight for a king, “both an ornament for the horse and a glory (κῦδος) to the driver” (4.141–145). So Menelaus' thighs are stained with blood (4.146–147). The cheekpiece is κῦδος that can be seen and carried, a thing that marks its owner. See [[ivory-stained-with-purple]].

**The king's glory.** Diomedes answers Sthenelus that Agamemnon may drive the army on as he likes: “for glory (κῦδος) will go with him if the Achaeans cut down the Trojans and take sacred Ilios, and great grief will be his if the Achaeans are cut down” (4.415–417). The glory of the army's victory belongs to its king, as Zeus gives κῦδος to kings (1.279). See [[penthos]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].

**The family.** Hera calls the Achaeans ὑπερκύδαντες, “exultant”, when she asks that the Trojans be made to break the oaths against them (4.66), and Zeus repeats her words (4.71): the Achaeans have won the duel and are glorying in it. κυδάλιμος, “glorious”, is said of Menelaus (4.100, 4.177) and of Capaneus (4.403). Athena is κυδίστη Τριτογένεια, “most glorious Tritogeneia” (4.515; see [[tritogeneia]]), and battle is κυδιάνειρα, “where men win glory” (4.225; see [[where-men-win-glory]]).
