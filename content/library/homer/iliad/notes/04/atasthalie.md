---
summary: Sthenelus answers Agamemnon's praise of Tydeus by saying that the sons took Thebes trusting in the portents of the gods, while their fathers “perished through their own recklessness”.
passages:
  - 4.404-410 | Sthenelus answers Agamemnon
---

{{quote:962da432-0690-53a3-9676-5c0e0b672ce4}}

Agamemnon has held up Tydeus to his son: a guest at Mycenae, a lone envoy among the Cadmeians, the killer of fifty men in an ambush (4.370–400). Sthenelus, whose father Capaneus also died at Thebes, answers for both sons. The Epigonoi took the city with a smaller army against a stronger wall, “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (4.408); the fathers died by their own fault, κεῖνοι δὲ σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο (4.409).

Sthenelus does not say what the recklessness was. Agamemnon's own story has just supplied an answer. When Tydeus and Polynices came to Mycenae for allies, “Zeus turned them back, showing ill-omened signs” (παραίσια σήματα, 4.381), and the Seven marched on Thebes all the same. By Sthenelus' account the sons obeyed the signs and the fathers did not.

The half-line is the one the Odyssey uses in its first lines for Odysseus' companions (*Odyssey* 1.7). Sthenelus makes it serve a boast: see [[better-than-our-fathers]]. Diomedes will not have it. He tells his companion to sit in silence and takes the rebuke as fair (4.411–418), and in Book 14 he will himself claim descent from “a good father”, Tydeus (14.113–114).
