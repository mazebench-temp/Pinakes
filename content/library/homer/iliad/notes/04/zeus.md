**The provocation.** The gods sit with Zeus in assembly on the golden floor, drinking nectar and looking out at Troy (4.1–4; see [[olympians]]). “At once the son of Cronus set about provoking Hera with cutting words, speaking with sly intent” (4.5–6). κερτόμιος is the word for taunts (see [[kertomia]]). παραβλήδην, “with sly intent”, is explained either as “with a side-thrust” or as “by comparison”, and a comparison follows: Menelaus has two goddesses to help him, Hera and Athena, but they sit apart and watch, while Aphrodite always stands by Paris and has just saved his life (4.7–12; see [[aphrodite]]). Then the offer:

{{quote:474927d2-1f6f-5867-b037-55b0e578471a}}

These are the terms of the duel: the victory is Menelaus', so Helen goes home and Troy is spared (see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Commentators have asked whether Zeus means it. The narrator calls it provocation, and Zeus has promised Thetis to honor Achilles by giving the Trojans the upper hand, which needs the war to go on (1.508–510, 1.524–530, 2.3–4). The offer makes Hera ask for the war herself. On the plain, the men who watch Athena come down put the same choice in almost the same words, “evil war and the dread din of battle” or “friendship”, and leave it to “Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men” (4.82–84, echoing 4.15–16). They do not know that it has already been made.

**The city he honored most.** Hera will not have her labor wasted (4.25–29). Zeus answers “deeply troubled” (μέγʼ ὀχθήσας; see [[deeply-troubled]]), with a question she never answers: what have Priam and his sons done to her, that she would eat them raw to heal her anger (4.31–36)? Then he gives way, so that the quarrel will not become “a great cause of strife between you and me” (4.37–38; see [[eris]]), and sets a condition:

{{quote:d9eb6c4b-2749-5d81-9161-86d4cfc81488}}

ἑκὼν ἀέκοντί γε θυμῷ, “willingly, though with an unwilling heart”: the gift is his own choice, made against his heart. His reason for loving Troy is its worship. Its altar never lacked “an equal share of the feast, libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us” (γέρας, 4.48–49), and in the last book he gives the same two lines as his reason for honoring Hector (24.69–70; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[geras]]). The verb is past, τιέσκετο, “was (always) honored”. And the words in which he names what he loved, “sacred Ilios … and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (4.46–47), are the words in which Agamemnon, a hundred and twenty lines later, foretells that they will perish (4.164–165; see [[troy]]).

**The bargain.** Hera offers Argos, Sparta and Mycenae, claims equal birth and rank as his sister and his wife, and asks that each give way to the other (4.51–63; see [[hera]]). “So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey” (4.68). οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε is the formula of obedience, used when Achilles obeys Athena (1.220) and Athena obeys Hera (2.166); here the father of men and gods obeys his wife. He passes her orders to Athena almost word for word: the Trojans are to “be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.67, 4.72). The words are those of the curse sworn over the truce in Book 3, on whichever side should first do harm (3.299). The gods keep the peace among themselves by trading the cities of men. Troy's fall is settled on Olympus, and the guilt for it is to be Troy's own. See [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**The same choice, twice more.** The scene has two later twins. Zeus pities his son Sarpedon and wonders whether to snatch him from death (16.431–438); he pities Hector and asks the gods to consider whether to save him (22.168–176). Each time a goddess answers with the line Hera speaks here, “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (4.29 = 16.443 = 22.181), and each time Zeus gives way (16.458, 22.183–185). In Book 22 the lines that follow are the ones that send Athena down here (22.186–187 = 4.73–74). He has the power to save and does not use it against the other gods.

**Zeus in men's mouths.** Agamemnon, over his wounded brother, is sure that if the Olympian does not fulfill an oath at once he fulfills it late, and that Zeus “will himself shake his dark aegis over them all, in resentment at this deceit” (4.160–168; see [[aegis]] and [[apate]]). He tells the army that “father Zeus will be no helper to liars” (4.235). The audience knows what Agamemnon does not: the deceit began with Zeus, who sent Athena to contrive it. His signs also run through the Theban stories of the review. He turned the Mycenaeans back from the war of the Seven, “showing ill-omened signs” (4.381), and the sons of the Seven took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (Ζηνὸς ἀρωγῇ, 4.408).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates will not let it be said that the breaking of the oaths by Pandarus came about through Athena and Zeus (*Republic* 2.379e; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).
