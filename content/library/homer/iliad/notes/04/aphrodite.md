---
summary: Aphrodite is named once in Book 4, in Zeus' taunt to Hera and Athena. “Laughter-loving Aphrodite” always stands by Paris and has just saved him, while Menelaus' two goddesses sit apart and watch.
refs: [4.11-12]
---

{{quote:18f5310e-e5a9-548f-a0ae-e1d7df330f61}}

Zeus opens the council of the gods by setting the goddesses side by side. Menelaus has two helpers, Hera and Athena, who “sit apart and look on and take their pleasure”. Paris has one, who “is always at his side and wards off doom from him” (παρμέμβλωκε … κῆρας ἀμύνει). “And now she has saved him, when he thought that he would die” (4.12): this is the rescue from the duel, when she broke his helmet strap and carried him off in a mist (3.373–382; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). φιλομειδής, “laughter-loving”, is her standing epithet (see [[laughter-loving]]). The comparison is meant to provoke Hera and Athena, and it does: they mutter against his proposal of peace (4.20). See [[zeus]].

Zeus judges that the rescue changed nothing: “the victory belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (4.13). The goddess who kept Paris alive did not win him the duel. In Book 5 she tries to save her son Aeneas in the same way and is wounded by Diomedes (5.311–351).
