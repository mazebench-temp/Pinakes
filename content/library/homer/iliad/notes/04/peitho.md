The verb takes all its senses in Book 4: persuade, trust, obey.

**Persuaded.** Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, begins “Will you listen to me now, wise-hearted son of Lycaon?” (ἦ ῥά νύ μοί τι πίθοιο, 4.93), and the narrator closes the scene with his judgment: “So spoke Athena, and she persuaded the mind of the fool” (τῷ δὲ φρένας ἄφρονι πεῖθεν, 4.104). The goddess persuades, and the man who lets himself be persuaded is a fool. See [[pandarus]] and [[phrenes]].

**Trusted.** The perfect πέποιθα means “trust, rely on”. Agamemnon names the pledges of the truce, “the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted” (ᾗς ἐπέπιθμεν, 4.159), in the line Nestor spoke in Book 2 when the army was about to abandon its own oaths (2.341). Nestor warns his horsemen: “Let no one, trusting in his horsemanship and his manhood, be eager to fight the Trojans alone” (4.303), and he leaves the spears to the younger men, who “trust in their strength” (4.325).

**Obeyed.** The Theban story turns on signs obeyed. Tydeus killed all the ambushers but one, and let Maeon go home, “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 4.398). Sthenelus says that the sons took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (πειθόμενοι, 4.408), while the fathers perished by their own recklessness (see [[atasthalie]] and [[teras]]). Diomedes ends the argument with a command: “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (ἐμῷ δʼ ἐπιπείθεο μύθῳ, 4.412), the half-line with which Zeus silenced Hera in Book 1 (1.565). And twice the narrator notes that someone “did not disobey”: Zeus, when Hera asks him to send Athena (4.68), and Talthybius, when Agamemnon sends him for Machaon (4.198). See [[obedience-and-persuasion]].
