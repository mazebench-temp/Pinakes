---
summary: Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, tempts Pandarus with “thanks and glory” from all the Trojans and splendid gifts from Alexander, if he will shoot Menelaus.
passages:
  - 4.93-103 | Athena tempts Pandarus
---

Athena does not order Pandarus to shoot. She offers him a bargain (4.93–103). He would win “thanks and glory (χάριν καὶ κῦδος) from all the Trojans, and most of all from king Alexander”, and Alexander would give him splendid gifts before anyone else, “if he should see warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus, brought down by your arrow and laid on the grievous pyre” (4.95–99). Then she tells him to vow Apollo a hecatomb of firstborn lambs when he comes home to Zeleia (4.101–103).

The temptation is made of reciprocity, with men and with a god. Pandarus would do the Trojans a service and be owed their gratitude; he would do Alexander a greater one and be paid in gifts; he would pay Apollo for the shot with a sacrifice. The narrator calls him a fool for being persuaded (φρένας ἄφρονι πεῖθεν, 4.104). None of the returns comes. The arrow wounds Menelaus and does not kill him, and Pandarus never goes home to Zeleia: Diomedes kills him in the next book (5.290–296). There he says that he came to Troy “bringing a favor (χάριν) to brilliant Hector” (5.211). See [[pandarus]], [[kydos]], [[nostos]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].
