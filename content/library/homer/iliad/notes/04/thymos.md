**Life.** In Book 4 the θυμός is often simply a man's life, and the book shows it going and coming back. Menelaus shudders at his own black blood, but when he sees that the binding and the barbs of the arrow are outside the flesh, “his spirit was gathered back into his breast” (ἄψορρόν οἱ θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἀγέρθη, 4.151–152), as a man's does when he comes to himself. In the first battle it goes and does not come back. Elephenor is stabbed in the side, “so his spirit left him” (λίπε θυμός, 4.470); Diores falls stretching out his hands to his companions, “breathing out his life” (θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, 4.524); Thoas strikes Peirous in the belly “and took away his life” (ἐκ δʼ αἴνυτο θυμόν, 4.531). The translation renders the word “spirit” or “life” as the context asks. See [[death-in-battle]].

**Will.** The θυμός is also where a man's will and courage sit. Zeus gives Troy up “willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (ἑκὼν ἀέκοντί γε θυμῷ, 4.43; see [[with-an-unwilling-heart]]). Agamemnon wishes that such a spirit as the Ajaxes' were in the breasts of all the army (4.288–289), and Nestor tells his horsemen that the men of old sacked cities keeping “this purpose and this spirit in their breasts” (4.309). In the old man the θυμός has outlasted the body: Agamemnon wishes that Nestor's knees could follow the spirit in his breast (4.313–314; see [[gounata]]).

The compound μεγάθυμος, “great-hearted”, is said of the Abantes, of Agenor and of Ajax (4.464, 4.467, 4.479; see [[great-hearted]]).
