**The plan.** Zeus opens the book by asking whether the gods should rouse war again or “put friendship between the two sides” (4.14–16), the friendship the oath was sworn to make (3.73, 3.94; see [[philotes]]). Hera will not have peace, and her bargain with Zeus ends in an order: Athena is to go down and “contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans, be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.66–67), and Zeus repeats the words to her (4.71–72). They are the words of the curse sworn over the wine, “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths” (3.299). The gods do not break the oath themselves. They see to it that the Trojans do, so that the curse falls on Troy (see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

**The breach.** Athena persuades Pandarus, and he shoots Menelaus in the truce from behind his companions' shields (4.86–126; see [[pandarus]]). One man shoots, and the Achaeans lay the deed on the city. Plato would not let poets say that Athena and Zeus caused it (see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).

**“The oath is in no way vain.”** Holding his brother's hand, Agamemnon begins his lament:

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

θάνατόν νύ τοι ὅρκιʼ ἔταμνον is literally “it was death for you that I cut as oaths”: τάμνω, “cut”, is the verb of the oath sacrifice (see [[oath-formulas]]), and the throats he cut for a truce have become his brother's death. The ὅρκια that were carried through the city in Book 3 (3.245) are now trampled underfoot (4.157). He names what made the oath: the blood of the lambs, the libations of unmixed wine, and the right hands in which they trusted. The last verse is Nestor's from Book 2, where it named the pledges of the expedition (4.159 = 2.341); the rite of Book 3 had no handclasp (see [[libation]]).

Then comes the certainty. “For even if the Olympian has not fulfilled it at once, he fulfills it in the end, even late, and they pay a great price, with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.160–162; see [[fulfilled-late]]). The curse of Book 3 asked that the oath-breakers' brains pour out like the wine, “theirs and their children's, and may their wives be subdued by others” (3.300–301). The narrator had answered that Zeus “was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302), and Agamemnon's “even late” (ὀψέ) takes up that “not yet”. He knows how it will end:

{{quote:2f616469-d39c-5c58-b7ba-997c641df646}}

Zeus himself will shake his dark aegis over them all, “in resentment at this deceit”; “these things will not go unfulfilled” (4.166–168; see [[aegis]], [[apate]], [[kotos]] and [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). Hector speaks the same three verses to Andromache in Book 6, as a foreboding from inside the city (6.447–449 = 4.163–165; see [[a-day-will-come]]).

**A war cry.** Going through the army, Agamemnon tells the eager that “father Zeus will be no helper to liars”: vultures will eat the oath-breakers, and the Achaeans will carry off their wives and children (4.235–239; see [[no-helper-to-liars]], [[exhortation]] and [[pseudos]]). The curse has become the army's plan of war.

**Idomeneus.** Agamemnon praises Idomeneus, and he answers with his own good faith: he will be the king's “trusty companion”, as he promised at the start and nodded his assent (4.266–267). The promise kept is set beside the oaths broken:

{{quote:b5e8908c-7852-53de-a1ce-09e76777e0d8}}

σύν … ἔχευαν, “have been confounded”, is literally “poured together”, from συγχέω. From the verb comes the noun of the book's traditional title, Ὁρκίων σύγχυσις, “the confounding of the oaths” (see [[titles-of-book-4]]). The formula of the curse is heard once more: “since they were the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.271; so 4.67, 4.72, 4.236).

**Afterwards.** In Book 7 Antenor tells the Trojans that they are fighting “having played false with the trusty oaths” (ὅρκια πιστὰ ψευσάμενοι), and proposes to give Helen back (7.348–353). Hector, earlier that day, had given the Trojan view: Zeus “did not fulfill our oaths” (7.69). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

## Outside Homer

Agamemnon's certainty that the gods punish late but in full became a commonplace of Greek thought about divine justice. Hesiod says that the perjurer's family is left the weaker after him (*Works and Days* 282–285). Solon says that one man pays at once and another later, and that if a man escapes, his innocent children or his descendants pay (fragment 13.29–32 West). The Pythia told Glaucus of Sparta, who asked whether he might deny a deposit on oath, that Oath has a nameless son who pursues the perjurer until he destroys his whole house (Herodotus 6.86).
