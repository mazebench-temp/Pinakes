summary: The first deaths in battle come at the end of Book 4. Echepolus is the first man killed; Elephenor dies dragging his body; the young Simoeisius falls like a poplar; revenge follows revenge; and the book ends with Trojans and Achaeans lying face down in the dust side by side.

**The first.** “Antilochus was the first to kill a helmeted man of the Trojans, brave among the champions, Echepolus, son of Thalysias” (4.457–458). The spear goes into his forehead and through the bone, “and darkness covered his eyes, and he fell like a tower in the fierce battle” (4.461–462). The first death of the poem is quick, exact and told in formulas that will be used again and again (see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[like-a-tower]]).

**The body.** At once there is a fight over the corpse. Elephenor seizes it by the feet to strip the armor, and as he stoops his side shows beside his shield, and Agenor stabs him; “so his spirit left him, and over him there was work, grievous work, of Trojans and Achaeans” (4.463–471). The next pair repeats the pattern. Leucus, dragging away the body of Simoeisius, is killed in his turn (4.491–493).

**The young man.** Simoeisius is the first of the dead to be given a history:

{{quote:6912aca5-7766-5232-a212-c4e195edfdf6}}

He is named for the river by which his mother bore him; he did not repay his parents for his rearing; his life was short (see [[threptra]] and [[simoeisius]]). He falls “like a poplar that has grown in the low wet ground of a great marsh”, cut down by a chariot-maker to bend into a wheel rim, and left drying by the river (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]). The tree is smooth, with branches only at the top, like the young man's body, and it lies by a river as his does by the Simoeis.

**Revenge.** Odysseus, angry at the death of Leucus, kills Democoon, a bastard son of Priam, through both temples (4.494–504). Peirous crushes Diores' ankle with a stone and spears him as he lies stretching out his hands to his companions; Thoas kills Peirous, and cannot strip him because the Thracians close around the body (4.517–535). Every death in the battle is answered by another. No one speaks over the dead: the only words in the fighting are a god's (4.509–513).

**Side by side.** The book ends with the dead of both armies together. “So the two lay stretched in the dust beside each other, one of the Thracians, the other of the bronze-armored Epeians, both of them leaders; and many others were killed around them” (4.536–538). A man led through the battle unhurt by Athena could not make light of the work, “for many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another” (4.539–544). The first battle has no winner. It has the dead. See [[the-first-battle]], [[androktasia]] and [[watching-the-war]].
