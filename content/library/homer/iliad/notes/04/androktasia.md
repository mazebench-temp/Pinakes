summary: The first battle of the Iliad (4.457–544) has seven killings, the first of the poem. They show every part of the scene, from the victim's history and the path of the weapon to the fight over the body, and they are linked in chains of attempt and revenge.

The armies meet (4.446–456; see [[the-armies-clash]]), and the narrator turns from the mass to single men.

**The first killing.** “Antilochus was the first to kill a helmeted man of the Trojans, brave among the champions, Echepolus, son of Thalysias” (4.457–458). The killer is named first, then the victim with his father's name; the spear goes through the ridge of the helmet into the forehead and the bone, “and darkness covered his eyes, and he fell like a tower in the fierce battle” (4.459–462). Blow, death formula and simile follow one another in four lines. See [[antilochus]], [[echepolus]], [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[like-a-tower]].

**The body.** Elephenor, leader of the Abantes, seizes the dead man by the feet and drags him out of range, “in eager haste to strip off his armor”, and Agenor stabs him in the side his shield has left bare as he stoops, “and loosened his limbs” (4.463–469). The attempt to strip a body costs the stripper his life. The fight over him is “grievous work”, and the men leap on one another “like wolves” (4.470–472; see [[elephenor]], [[agenor]], [[loosened-his-limbs]] and [[like-wolves]]).

**Simoeisius.** Ajax kills the son of Anthemion, and the scene stops for his life: born beside the Simoeis when his mother came down from Ida with her parents to see the flocks, named for the river, he did not repay his parents for his rearing (θρέπτρα; see [[threptra]]), “and short was his life” (4.473–479). Ajax's spear goes in beside the right nipple and out through the shoulder, and he falls in the dust like a poplar cut down by a chariot-maker (4.480–487; see [[simoeisius]] and [[the-poplar]]). The same lines on the unrepaid rearing are said in Book 17 of Hippothous, whom Ajax also kills.

**The chain.** Antiphus, a son of Priam, throws at Ajax and misses, and hits Leucus, Odysseus' companion, in the groin as he drags the body away (4.489–493). Odysseus, angry for his friend, throws and kills Democoon, a bastard son of Priam (νόθος; see [[nothos]]), through both temples: “darkness covered his eyes, and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him” (4.494–504; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]). Later Peirous, leader of the Thracians, crushes Diores' ankle with a stone and stabs him by the navel as he lies stretching out his hands to his companions, and Thoas the Aetolian kills Peirous in turn with spear and sword (4.517–531). Thoas cannot strip him, for the Thracians close round the body with their long spears and push him back (4.532–535). A missed throw that kills another man, a friend avenged, a body saved: each is a regular part of Homeric battle (see [[antiphus-son-of-priam]], [[leucus]], [[democoon]], [[diores]], [[peirous]] and [[thoas]]).

**The men who die.** Four Trojans die and three Achaeans. Three of the dead, Elephenor, Diores and Peirous, were named as leaders in the Catalogues (2.540–541, 2.622, 2.844), and 4.464 repeats Elephenor's line from the Catalogue (2.541). The others enter the poem only to die. There is no boast over the dead in this battle. It ends with a picture of the whole field, the two leaders lying side by side in the dust, and many others, “for many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another” (4.536–544). See [[the-first-battle]] and [[death-in-battle]].
