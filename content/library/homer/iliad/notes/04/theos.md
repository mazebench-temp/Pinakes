Book 4 begins and ends among gods. Zeus opens it by teasing: “Two of the goddesses (θεάων) are helpers of Menelaus”, and they sit and watch while Aphrodite saves Paris (4.7–12; see [[kertomia]]). Hera answers with a claim of standing: “for I too am a god (θεός), and my birth is from the same stock as yours” (4.58); Cronus fathered her “the most senior”, by birth and because she is the wife of Zeus (4.59–61). The masculine θεός serves for a goddess too, and Hera uses it to put herself on Zeus' level.

Among men the gods are named as the givers of what men have and lack: “the gods never give men all things at once” (4.320; see [[all-things-at-once]]), and Agamemnon asks that the gods make his rebuke blow away (4.363). At the end Apollo, “the dread god” (δεινὸς θεός, 4.514), shouts to the Trojans from the city. See [[gods-and-mortals]].
