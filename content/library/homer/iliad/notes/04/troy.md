**Watched and bargained for.** The gods look out “upon the city of the Trojans” as they drink (4.4). Zeus offers them a choice in which the city “would still be inhabited” (οἰκέοιτο, 4.18), the only picture of Troy at peace in the book. Hera refuses, and Zeus gives the city up:

{{quote:0400527c-ca17-5edc-9e1a-5a8092362062}}

Of all the cities of men “under the sun and the starry sky”, Ilios was dearest to Zeus, and the reason is its worship: the altar that never lacked its share of feast, libation and savor (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). ἱρή, “sacred”, is Troy's standing epithet (see [[hieros]]). In return Hera gives up Argos, Sparta and Mycenae (4.51–56; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

**A day will come.** Agamemnon, over his wounded brother, is sure the oath will be avenged:

{{quote:2f616469-d39c-5c58-b7ba-997c641df646}}

These are the words in which Zeus named what he loved (4.46–47). In the last book the same formula names what the gods hate: “sacred Ilios, and Priam, and the people” became hateful (ἀπήχθετο) to Hera, Poseidon and Athena because of the blind folly of Alexander (24.27–28). Zeus himself, Agamemnon goes on, will shake his aegis over the Trojans “in resentment at this deceit” (4.166–168). Hector will say the same two lines to Andromache (6.448–449). In the review Agamemnon pictures the fall: vultures eating the oath-breakers' tender flesh, and their wives and children led away in the ships, “when we take the citadel” (4.237–239). For the Trojans, Idomeneus says, there will be “death and sorrows hereafter” (4.270). See [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

**Before the walls.** The first battle of the poem is fought on the plain below the city (4.422–544). Apollo calls to the Trojans “from the city” (4.514), from its citadel, Pergamus (4.508; see [[pergamus]]). Menelaus, if he died, would lie “in Troy, with your work unfinished” (4.175), in his brother's words.

## Outside Homer

Scipio Aemilianus, watching Carthage burn in 146 BC, spoke 4.164–165 and told Polybius that he feared the same fate for Rome (Polybius 38.21–22; Appian, *Punica* 132; see [[scipio-at-carthage]]).
