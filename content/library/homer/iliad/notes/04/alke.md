summary: The word marks the stages of Agamemnon's review. He tells the eager not to let go of their “furious courage” and asks the laggards why they stand like fawns with no courage in their hearts; Idomeneus is like a boar in courage; and Diomedes, rebuked, answers only “let us two also think of our furious courage”.

  - 4.251-254 | Idomeneus like a boar
  - 4.411-418 | Diomedes silences Sthenelus
---

**Held and let go.** Going through the ranks on foot, Agamemnon speaks to two kinds of men. To the eager he says, “Argives, do not yet let go of your furious courage” (μή πώ τι μεθίετε θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 4.234), and gives the reason: Zeus will not help the oath-breakers (4.235–239; see [[no-helper-to-liars]]). The men he rebukes are the ones “holding back” (μεθιέντας, 4.240), the same verb: they have let go already. He asks them why they stand like fawns worn out from running, “and there is no courage in their hearts” (4.243–245; see [[like-fawns]]). In Book 1 Achilles had given Agamemnon himself “the heart of a deer” (1.225).

**The boar.** At the next contingent the image turns. Idomeneus stands among the champions “like a boar in courage” (συῒ εἴκελος ἀλκήν, 4.253; see [[like-a-boar]]). The fawn and the boar are the two ends of the scale: the animal that runs and the animal that turns and stands.

**Diomedes' answer.** Agamemnon's longest rebuke goes to Diomedes, whose father Tydeus never cowered (4.370–400). Diomedes says nothing, in awe of the king (4.401–402). When Sthenelus answers for him, he tells him to sit in silence and grants that Agamemnon has the right to urge the army on, since the glory and the grief will be his (4.412–417). His last words take up the king's own phrase: “But come, let us two also think of our furious courage” (4.418). Then he leaps from his chariot, and the bronze rings on his chest (4.419–421). The charge of cowardice is answered in the next book, by fighting. In the assembly of Book 9 Diomedes reminds Agamemnon that he was the first to insult his ἀλκή before the Danaans, and that the Argives, young and old, know whether it was true (9.34–36).
