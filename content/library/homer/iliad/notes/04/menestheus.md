**Waiting.** From Nestor, who is marshaling his men, Agamemnon comes to the one man the Catalogue ranked with Nestor as a marshal of horses and fighters (2.553–555). He finds “the son of Peteos, Menestheus, driver of horses” standing still, with the Athenians around him and Odysseus and the Cephallenians nearby (4.327–330; see [[driver-of-horses]] and [[athenians]]). The narrator gives the reason before the king speaks: their men had not yet heard the war cry, since the battalions were only now beginning to move, and they were waiting for another column of Achaeans to charge first (4.331–335).

**The rebuke.**

{{quote:7e48bc0b-a377-5c41-9e37-b742e3bf7c3f}}

Agamemnon addresses Menestheus with honor to his father, “the king whom Zeus has nurtured” (see [[peteos]]), and Odysseus with an insult. Then he speaks to both in the dual. They are the first to be called to the feast of the elders, and the last to fight; they would gladly watch ten columns of Achaeans fight in front of them. The feast that made Idomeneus' praise (4.257–263) makes their blame. See [[feast]] and [[agamemnon]].

**Silence.** Menestheus does not answer. Odysseus does, angrily, and Agamemnon takes back his words, to Odysseus alone (4.349–363; see [[odysseus]]). Book 4 gives Menestheus neither a word nor a deed. He is left with the distinction the Catalogue gave him, which the Athenians of a later age would cite (see [[herodotus-menestheus]]).
