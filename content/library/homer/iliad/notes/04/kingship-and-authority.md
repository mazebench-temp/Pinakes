**The king on foot.** When the Trojans come on, the narrator praises Agamemnon as he seldom does: “you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing, nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight” (4.223–225). He leaves his horses and chariot with his attendant and goes through the army on foot, “commanding so many”, “like a master” (κοιρανέων, 4.226–231, 4.250). The words are those used of Odysseus when he went through the army with the king's scepter in Book 2 (2.207; see [[koiranos]]). Now the king does it himself. See [[the-review-of-the-army]].

**A rebuke withdrawn.** A king's rebuke can be answered. Odysseus answers Agamemnon's with anger, and the king smiles, takes back his word and promises to make it good (4.349–363). Zeus' word cannot be taken back (1.526); a king's among men can, and taking it back costs Agamemnon nothing here. In Book 1 he would not do it. See [[mythos]] and [[smiling-at-him]].

**A rebuke defended.** Diomedes will not answer. He takes the rebuke “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.402), and when Sthenelus protests he gives the reason: “I do not blame Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, for urging the well-greaved Achaeans to fight; for glory will go with him if the Achaeans cut down the Trojans and take sacred Ilios, and great grief will be his if the Achaeans are cut down” (4.413–417). The king's right to command rests on his share in the outcome. It is the reverse of Achilles' complaint in Book 1, that the king takes the best share while others do the fighting (1.163–168). See [[aidos]] and [[nemesis]].

**Zeus' authority.** On Olympus the question is simpler, and Hera states it: she cannot stop Zeus, “since you are far the stronger” (4.56), and “you are lord among all the immortals” (4.61). Yet the strongest yields. Zeus gives up the city he loves rather than quarrel with his wife, “willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (4.43; see [[with-an-unwilling-heart]]), and “did not disobey” her (4.68).
