Three fears, of three kinds. The first is a fear to be avoided. Agamemnon has mourned his brother as dead in front of the companions, and Menelaus stops him: “Take heart, and do not frighten (μηδέ τί πω δειδίσσεο) the army of the Achaeans at all” (4.184). A king's despair would spread through the army.

The second is a fear that holds an army together. The Achaeans advance on the Trojans without a sound, “silent, in fear of their commanders” (σιγῇ δειδιότες σημάντορας, 4.431), and Plato's Socrates praised the line as a lesson in obedience (see [[plato-silent-achaeans]], [[semaino]] and [[silence]]).

The third is the fear a warrior causes. When Diomedes leaps from his chariot in his armor, “the bronze on the breast of the lord rang terribly as he moved; fear (δέος) would have seized even a stout-hearted man” (4.420–421). The next book is his. In Agamemnon's story his father Tydeus is the man who feels none: alone among the many Cadmeians, “though he was a stranger, horse-driving Tydeus was not afraid” (οὐδὲ … τάρβει, 4.387–388).
