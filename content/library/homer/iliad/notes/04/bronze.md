---
summary: The bronze of Book 4 is the bronze of the first battle: the bronze point that pierces the bone, the flesh-cutting bronze, the bronze that rings on Diomedes' chest. Apollo tells the Trojans that the Argives' flesh cannot withstand it, and the band that saved Menelaus was the work of bronze-smiths.
grc: [χαλκείη, χαλκῆες]
en: [bronze-smiths]
---

**The weapon.** χαλκός stands for the blade and the point. The first two killings of the poem end in the same half-verse, “the bronze point” (αἰχμὴ χαλκείη, 4.461, 4.503; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]); Agenor's spear is “bronze-fitted” (χαλκήρεϊ, 4.469), Ajax's is “the bronze spear” (4.481), and when Thoas strikes Peirous “the bronze stuck in his lung” (4.528). Agamemnon rebukes Menestheus and Odysseus for watching others fight “with the pitiless bronze” (4.348; see [[pitiless-bronze]]), and the imagined onlooker of the book's end is “unwounded by the sharp bronze” (4.540).

**Flesh and bronze.** Apollo gives the Trojans a reason to fight: the Argives' “flesh is not stone or iron to withstand the flesh-cutting bronze when they are struck” (4.510–511). ταμεσίχρως, “flesh-cutting”, is the bronze's own epithet (see [[flesh-cutting]]), and the comparison sets the metal of weapons against the metal of hardness (see [[not-stone-or-iron]] and [[iron]]).

**Armor.** The Achaeans are “bronze-armored” (4.199, 4.285, 4.537) and fight “in bronze corselets” (4.448; see [[bronze-armored]] and [[armored-in-bronze]]). Odysseus goes through the champions “armored in gleaming bronze” (4.495), and as Diomedes leaps down from his chariot “the bronze on the breast of the lord rang terribly as he moved” (4.420). Agamemnon's chariot is “inlaid with bronze” (4.226).

**The smiths.** Menelaus and the narrator both say that the band which saved him was made by “bronze-smiths” (χαλκῆες … ἄνδρες, 4.187, 4.216). The χαλκεύς is named for the metal he works; in the Odyssey the word is used even of a goldsmith (Odyssey 3.432). See [[band]].
