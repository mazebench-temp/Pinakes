summary: The breaking of the truce repeats the first crime of the war, a bond sworn under Zeus broken by a Trojan, and the man who would reward the archer is Alexander. Agamemnon foresees the end of Troy, the review names the Achaean leaders a third time, and the first battle gives the war its first dead.

**The crime again.** The war began when a guest broke the bond of hospitality in Menelaus' house. The truce of Book 3 was meant to end it, and in Book 4 a Trojan breaks it, in a second offense against Zeus' protection (see [[xenia]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). The man who would reward the breach is Alexander: Athena promises Pandarus gifts from him “if he should see warlike Menelaus … brought down by your arrow and laid on the grievous pyre” (4.96–99). The man who took Helen would pay for her husband's death.

**The end foreseen.** Agamemnon's lament over his brother looks to both ends of the war. If Menelaus dies, the army will go home, leaving Helen to the Trojans and Menelaus' bones in Troy, his work unfinished (4.171–181): the war abandoned as it nearly was in Book 2 (2.155–162). But he knows the other end too: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.164–165; see [[a-day-will-come]]). In the review he promises the army the captives of a sacked city, the Trojans' wives and children (4.238–239).

**The leaders named again.** The Catalogue of Ships named the Achaean leaders in Book 2, and Helen named some of them from the wall in Book 3. The review names them a third time, now in action, contingent by contingent: Idomeneus and Meriones, the two Ajaxes, Nestor and his captains, Menestheus, Odysseus, Diomedes and Sthenelus (4.251–421). See [[the-review-of-the-army]].

**Older wars.** The book also looks back past the war to the wars of the fathers. Nestor recalls the day he killed Ereuthalion (4.318–319), and Agamemnon and Sthenelus quarrel over the war against Thebes, which the fathers lost and the sons won (4.370–410; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[the-epigonoi]]).

**The first dead.** Then the armies meet, and for the first time in the poem men are killed (4.446–544; see [[death-in-battle]]).
