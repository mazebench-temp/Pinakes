**The rebuke.** Agamemnon finds Diomedes “standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot” (4.366), with Sthenelus beside him. Nothing in the narrative suggests fear, but the king rebukes him as a coward:

{{quote:acaec77a-37a6-5a0c-b330-b01dfd6bc683}}

He addresses him through his father, in the frame the Dream used to greet Agamemnon himself, “son of wise-hearted …, tamer of horses” (4.370, 2.23; see [[wise-hearted]] and [[tamer-of-horses]]). The “bridges of war” are obscure; the phrase is usually taken for the lanes between the ranks or for the open ground between the armies (see [[obscure-gephyrai]]). Then comes the story of Tydeus at Thebes, told to shame the son (4.372–398; see [[tydeus]] and [[tydeus-at-thebes]]), and its verdict: “the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400).

**Silence.**

{{quote:ccc0e6f4-41c5-5ee1-84ba-5e1f37dabfa2}}

αἰδεσθεὶς βασιλῆος ἐνιπὴν αἰδοίοιο: the respect is said twice, in the participle and in the adjective. αἰδώς is what a man owes a king (see [[aidos]]), and Diomedes pays it by saying nothing to an unjust charge.

**The answer to Sthenelus.** His companion answers for him: the king lies, and the sons are better than their fathers, for they took Thebes where the fathers perished (4.404–410; see [[sthenelus]]). Diomedes turns on him, “looking at him darkly” (4.411):

{{quote:3360dd1a-17c4-5c7e-b6da-e2253f32be96}}

τέττα (“Friend”) is an address found only here in Homer, and it softens the order. Diomedes does not blame (νεμεσῶ) the king, for the glory of victory and the grief of defeat will both be his (see [[nemesis]]). The man who will not defend himself defends the king's right to rebuke, and turns to the fight: “let us two also think of our furious courage”. He leaps from his chariot in his armor, and the bronze on his chest rings so terribly that “fear would have seized even a stout-hearted man” (4.421; see [[stout-hearted]]).

**The father's measure.** Agamemnon has made Tydeus the measure of the son, and the next book takes the measure up. Athena gives Diomedes might and daring (5.1–3) and puts into his breast the fearless might of his father, the horseman Tydeus (5.125–126). When he holds back later, she rebukes him as Agamemnon did, and begins where the king ended: Tydeus “fathered a son little like himself” (γείνατο, 5.800; 4.400). Tydeus was small, but a fighter, and a man who holds back is “no offspring of Tydeus” (5.800–813). Nor has Diomedes forgotten the rebuke. In the assembly of Book 9, when Agamemnon proposes to sail home, Diomedes speaks first, “as is the custom, my lord, in the assembly” (ἀγορῇ, 9.33). The king once reproached his courage before the Danaans and called him unwarlike and a coward, and all the Argives know it; Zeus gave Agamemnon the scepter, but not courage, which is the greatest power (9.32–39). Nestor praises him as the best in council of all the men of his age (9.53–58). The son whom Agamemnon called better in assembly proves it against him. See [[fathers-and-sons]].
