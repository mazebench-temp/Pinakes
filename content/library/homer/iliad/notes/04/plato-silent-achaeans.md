---
summary: Plato's lesson in discipline draws twice on Book 4, with Diomedes' command to Sthenelus, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word”, and the Achaeans who march “silent, in fear of their commanders”.
passages:
  - 4.411-418 | Diomedes silences Sthenelus
  - 4.427-432 | The Achaeans march in silence
---

{{quote:a53ecf87-fd81-530b-bb9f-9dd428ae776f}}

Socrates quotes Diomedes' line exactly as our text has it: τέττα, σιωπῇ ἧσο, ἐμῷ δʼ ἐπιπείθεο μύθῳ, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (4.412). τέττα, “friend”, is an affectionate address found nowhere else in Homer. Diomedes has just kept silent himself under Agamemnon's rebuke, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.401–402), and now he tells his companion to do the same. Plato leaves out the reasons Diomedes gives, which are generous to the king: the glory will be Agamemnon's if the Achaeans take Troy, and the grief his if they are cut down (4.413–418).

Plato then adds “what goes with these”, the Achaeans going “breathing fury, in silence, fearing their commanders” (*Republic* 3.389e). The first half is 3.8; the second is 4.431. “What goes with these” suits Book 4, where the silent march comes less than twenty lines after Diomedes' command, as the Achaean battalions move forward like waves on the shore (4.422–432). In Book 4, too, the silence is set against the Trojans, who clamor like ewes bleating in a rich man's fold, in many tongues (4.433–438). Plato keeps the discipline and drops the contrast.
