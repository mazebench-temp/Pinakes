summary: Signs from Zeus decide the Theban wars in the stories of Book 4: ill-omened signs kept the Mycenaeans from joining the Seven (4.381), Tydeus spared Maeon “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.398), and the sons took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods” (4.408). Athena's descent is compared to a star sent as a portent (4.75–77).

**The Seven.** Tydeus and Polynices came to Mycenae to gather allies for the war on Thebes, and the Mycenaeans were willing, “but Zeus turned them back, showing ill-omened signs” (παραίσια σήματα φαίνων, 4.381). παραίσιος, “ill-omened”, perhaps “against what is fated” (παρά + αἶσα), occurs only here in Homer. The poem does not say what the signs were. Mycenae stayed out of a war that the gods had doomed (see [[the-seven-against-thebes]] and [[mycenae]]).

**The portents obeyed.** Tydeus killed the fifty men of the ambush and let one go home, “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 4.398). Sthenelus uses the same words of the sons: they took Thebes with a smaller army against a stronger wall, “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus”, while their fathers “perished through their own recklessness” (πειθόμενοι τεράεσσι θεῶν, 4.406–409). The difference between the two generations, as Sthenelus tells it, is that one read the signs and obeyed them (see [[the-epigonoi]], [[teras]] and [[better-than-our-fathers]]).

**A star.** Athena comes down from Olympus like the star that the son of Cronus sends “as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men” (τέρας, 4.75–77). The watching armies read her descent as a sign, and wonder whether it means war or friendship (4.79–84; see [[shooting-star]] and [[tis-speech]]).
