**Helpers.** Book 4 names the gods as helpers. Zeus calls Hera and Athena Menelaus' “helpers” (ἀρηγόνες) who sit and watch, while Aphrodite stands at Paris' side and wards off doom from him (4.7–12). In the review Agamemnon promises that Zeus will be no “helper” (ἀρωγός) to liars (4.235). In his story Athena was such a helper (ἐπίρροθος) to Tydeus that he won every contest at Thebes (4.390), and Sthenelus says the sons took the city with “the help of Zeus” (4.408).

**The goddess at work.** Athena's part in the breach is told in every kind of divine action the poem has. She comes down from Olympus like a star, a sign seen by both armies (4.73–85; see [[shooting-star]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). She goes among the Trojans in the likeness of a man, Laodocus, son of Antenor (4.86–88), and persuades Pandarus with promises (4.93–104). When the arrow flies she stands before Menelaus and brushes it aside “as a mother brushes a fly from her child”, and guides it herself to where the belt and the corselet are doubled, so that it draws blood and does not kill (4.127–140; see [[mother-and-fly]]). She makes the wound, and she makes it slight. Pandarus' choice is his own all the same: the narrator calls him a fool (4.104). See [[who-is-to-blame]].

**Gods in the battle.** When the armies close, the gods drive them: “Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those, and Terror and Rout, and Strife, raging without end”, who grows from small to huge and casts the quarrel among them (4.439–445; see [[strife]]). When the Trojans give ground, Apollo shouts to them from Pergamus, and Athena goes through the Achaean throng “wherever she saw them holding back” (4.507–516). The last lines imagine one more intervention: a man led through the battle by Athena, holding him by the hand and keeping off the missiles (4.539–542; see [[watching-the-war]]).
