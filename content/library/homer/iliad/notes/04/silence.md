**Anger kept in.** Zeus' proposal of peace angers Hera and Athena. “Athena kept silent (ἀκέων) and said nothing, sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her” (4.22–23). Her silence is not assent. It is anger held in, set against Hera's, which her breast cannot contain (4.24).

**Respect.** Diomedes says nothing to Agamemnon's rebuke, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.401–402; see [[aidos]]). His silence is a choice: he will answer later, by fighting, and in the assembly of Book 9 (9.32–36).

**Silence imposed.** When Sthenelus answers for him, Diomedes orders him to be quiet: “Friend, sit in silence (σιωπῇ), and obey my word” (4.412; see [[tetta]]). The second half-line is Zeus' command to Hera in Book 1, “Sit down in silence, and obey my word” (1.565). Plato's Socrates chose Diomedes' line as an example of the obedience the young should learn (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]).

**The silent army.** The Achaeans advance into the first battle without a sound except their leaders' orders.

{{quote:a53ecf87-fd81-530b-bb9f-9dd428ae776f}}

“You would not have said that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts.” The Trojans, by contrast, are like ewes in a rich man's fold, bleating ceaselessly at the voices of their lambs, for their allies speak many tongues (4.433–438; see [[sheep-in-the-fold]]). The same contrast opened Book 3, where the Trojans came on with clamor like cranes and the Achaeans in silence (3.2–9; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). In both books silence belongs to the side that is disciplined and one. See [[semaino]] and [[the-army-and-its-leaders]].
