---
summary: Pity comes in pairs. Two men pity a fallen companion and charge to avenge him, Lycomedes for Leiocritus and Asteropaeus for Apisaon, in the same verse (17.346, 17.352); and Zeus pities twice, the weeping horses of Achilles and the weeping Ajax (17.441, 17.648), after speaking to the doomed Hector with the same pitying address he uses to the horses, “Ah, wretch” (17.201).
---

**Men.** “And Lycomedes, dear to Ares, pitied him as he fell” (17.346), and “warlike Asteropaeus pitied him as he fell” (17.352): the verse is the same on both sides of the body, and pity in the battle is the start of a charge. Lycomedes kills Apisaon; Asteropaeus cannot break through the ring of shields (17.347–355; see [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[hetairos]]).

**Zeus.** The god's pity is for tears. “And the son of Cronus saw them weeping and pitied them” (17.441): the horses weep for their charioteer, and Zeus answers with a saying about men and a gift of might (17.443–456; see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]). Ajax weeps as he prays for light, “and the father pitied him as he shed tears”, and Zeus scatters the mist (17.648–650; see [[prayer]]). The soliloquies over Hector and over the horses open alike, Zeus shaking his head and speaking to his own heart, with ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch”, and ἆ δειλώ, “Ah, wretched pair” (17.200–201, 17.442–443; see [[wretch]]): the man who does not know that his death is close, and the deathless horses who know that their charioteer is dead. Pity does not change what Zeus has decided. Hector has his great power only for the day, and the Trojans keep their glory until sunset (17.206–208, 17.453–455; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).
