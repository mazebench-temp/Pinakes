---
summary: Nestor is not in the battle, but his orders keep his sons Thrasymedes and Antilochus fighting apart from the body, so that they do not know that Patroclus is dead; it is the “son of great-hearted Nestor” whom Menelaus looks for and sends to Achilles (17.377–383, 17.651–701).
---

Nestor has not been in the fighting since he carried the wounded Machaon from the battle in Book 11 (11.597–643). His command still reaches the field: his two sons are fighting apart, watchful against the death and the panic of their companions, “since so Nestor had commanded / urging them on to war from the black ships” (17.381–383; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[antilochus]]). Walter Leaf, who thought the tactical advice given to Nestor in the poem “as usual … prosy and suspicious”, reads the verses as preparing for Antilochus' errand (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.377 and 17.381).

Antilochus is twice named as his son when Menelaus is sent to find him: “Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor, still alive” (17.653), and “to see if somewhere they might find the son of Nestor still alive” (17.681; see [[great-hearted]] and [[menelaus]]). The Pylians, his people, feel the loss when Antilochus runs off with the news, and Menelaus sends them his other son, Thrasymedes (17.702–705; see [[pylians]]).
