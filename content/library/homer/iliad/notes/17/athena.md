summary: Athena comes down late in the fight for Patroclus' body, sent by Zeus to rouse the Danaans. Wrapped in a purple cloud, like the rainbow that Zeus stretches out as a portent, she enters the Achaean ranks, and in the likeness of Phoenix urges Menelaus to defend the body; when he prays to her first of all the gods, she puts strength in his shoulders and knees and the daring of a fly in his breast.

**Not even Athena.** Around the body the fight is such that “not Ares, who rouses armies, nor Athena / would have made light of it when they saw it, not even if great anger came upon them” (17.398–399; see [[ares]] and [[who-rouses-armies]]). Walter Leaf takes the two as the war gods of the two sides, so that each side would have won the favor of its own patron (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.399), as when the armies first met: “Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those” (4.439). The words are those of Book 13, where the battalions around the two Ajaxes were such that “not even Ares could have made light of” them, “nor Athena who rouses armies” (13.127–128), and of the end of Book 4, where no man could make light of the fighting even if Pallas Athena led him by the hand (4.539–544).

**Sent down.** When Automedon has killed Aretus, the battle over Patroclus is stretched tight again:

{{quote:02e5fa35-5e89-5f46-8fb2-64689ea30524}}

The words “for far-seeing Zeus had sent her” are made like those of Book 1, where Athena came down to Achilles, “for the white-armed goddess Hera had sent her” (1.195). But Zeus had forbidden the gods to help either side (8.7–12), and he had said that he would let no other immortal defend the Danaans until Achilles' wish was fulfilled (15.72–77). Walter Leaf reports that 17.545 was athetized in antiquity, and takes 545–546 for an interpolation meant to explain how Athena comes to break the command of Book 8; Zeus' design does not change, for he goes on helping the Trojans (17.593–596), and he is on Ida, not in heaven (on 17.545; see [[zeus]] and the translation's note on 17.545–546). In Book 15 she thrust the mist from the Achaeans' eyes without a word from Zeus (15.668–669; see the note on Book 15).

Her coming is likened to a sign of Zeus, as it was in Book 4, when she darted down from Olympus like a star that the son of Cronus sends “as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men” (4.75–78; see [[shooting-star]]). Here it is the rainbow that he stretches out from heaven “to be a portent either of war / or of chilling winter” (17.548–549; see [[like-rainbows]], [[teras]] and [[iris]]), and she comes wrapped in a cloud of the same color. Leaf takes πορφύρεος, “purple”, as lurid and dark rather than bright, and the rainbow as part of the storm-cloud, with no associations of hope (on 17.547; see [[purple]] and the translation's note on 17.547). In Book 19 she will dart down from heaven like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey, to put nectar and ambrosia into Achilles' breast (19.349–354).

**As Phoenix.** She speaks first to Menelaus, who is nearest, “in the likeness of Phoenix in form and in tireless voice” (17.555; see [[phoenix]]): the verse with which Poseidon came to the Ajaxes as Calchas (13.45) and with which she will come to Hector as Deiphobus (22.227). Walter Leaf takes the mention of Phoenix as clear evidence that the whole passage is late, and finds ἀτειρέα, “tireless”, hardly the epithet for a very old man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.555). Phoenix, Achilles' old companion and one of the five leaders of the Myrmidons (16.196), speaks for Achilles' honor: “To you, Menelaus, it will be a shame and a reproach / if the trusted companion of noble Achilles / is torn by swift dogs under the wall of the Trojans” (17.556–558; see [[oneidos]] and [[dogs]]). Menelaus answers the old man with his old title, “Phoenix, old father, aged one” (17.561; see the translation's note on 9.607), and wishes that Athena “would give me strength, and keep off the force of the missiles” (17.562), the help that she gives the imagined man at the end of Book 4, leading him by the hand and keeping off the force of the missiles (4.541–542; Leaf on 17.561). He does not know that she is the one he speaks to.

**The daring of a fly.** Menelaus has prayed without knowing it, and the goddess answers:

{{quote:735a2793-7697-597a-a730-8dff95de2b8a}}

She is glad to be prayed to first of all the gods, as in the Odyssey she is glad when Peisistratus hands her the cup before he hands it to Telemachus (*Odyssey* 3.52–53). Leaf compares that passage, and the words of Aphrodite in [[euripides|Euripides']] *Hippolytus*, that the race of gods rejoices when it is honored by men (*Hippolytus* 7–8; on 17.567). Her gifts are strength in the limbs, as when she made Diomedes' limbs light at his prayer (5.121–122), and daring, θάρσος (see [[tharsos]] and [[the-daring-of-a-fly]]). The fly has been Menelaus' simile before: when Pandarus shot him, Athena brushed the arrow away from his flesh, “as a mother / brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). Now the fly's persistence is his; it keeps on biting, “and human blood is sweet to it” (17.572). The poem's other flies come in swarms: the Achaeans massing on the plain like the flies around a shepherd's milk pails (2.469–473), and the men thronging around Sarpedon's body like flies around the brimming pails (16.641–644; Leaf on 17.570). In the battle of the gods Ares will call Athena herself κυνάμυια, “dog-fly” (21.394). Filled with that daring, Menelaus stands over Patroclus and kills Podes, Hector's dear companion (17.574–581; see [[menelaus]]), and Apollo at once comes to Hector (17.582–590; see [[apollo]]).
