summary: As Hector drags the stripped body of Patroclus away, Ajax comes near “carrying his shield like a tower”, the verse's third use, and Hector gives ground and leaps into his chariot (17.125–131).
passages:
  - 17.125-139 | Ajax comes to the body of Patroclus
---

Αἴας δʼ ἐγγύθεν ἦλθε φέρων σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον: “And Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower” (17.128). The verse brought Ajax to his duel with Hector in Book 7 and to the rescue of the wounded Odysseus in Book 11 (7.219, 11.485; see the notes on those books). The third time it brings him to a dead man. Hector has taken the armor from Patroclus and is dragging the body away, “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127). When the tower comes near, Hector gives ground into the throng of his companions and springs into his chariot (17.129–130), as the Trojans scattered before Ajax in Book 11. The great shield then becomes a cover: “And Ajax covered the son of Menoetius with his broad shield / and stood like a lion over his young” (17.132–133; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

The tower here is not the tower of Ilios, though the Greek has the same word; the entry for the tower of Ilios excepts the verse (see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). Ajax is the defender of the body for the rest of the book, the man whom Glaucus says Hector dared not face (17.166–168; see [[ajax]] and [[glaucus]]).
