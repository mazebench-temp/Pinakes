summary: When the Trojans are about to be driven back into Ilios, Apollo, in the likeness of the old herald Periphas, shames Aeneas; he knows the god, rallies Hector and the Trojans, and kills Leiocritus. He goes with Hector after the horses of Achilles and withdraws before the two Ajaxes, and at the end of the book he and Hector press hardest on the men who carry Patroclus' body.

**The herald's likeness.** Ajax has killed Hippothous and Phorcys, and the Trojans “would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice”, had not Apollo urged on Aeneas (17.319–323; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[apollo]]). The god comes “in form like Periphas, / the herald, son of Epytus, who beside Aeneas' old father / had grown old as a herald, a man whose mind was kindly toward him” (17.323–325; see [[periphas-son-of-epytus]], [[anchises]] and [[heralds]]): a servant of his father's house, whom Walter Leaf calls “an attached retainer of the family” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.325). Apollo rouses two Trojans in this book, Hector twice and Aeneas once.

{{quote:5cb5169f-d2c5-5385-a425-c815a450b798}}

ὑπερδέα δῆμον, “a people above fear” (17.330), has been explained in more than one way since antiquity (see the translation's note on 17.330). Walter Leaf calls all the ancient explanations “virtually impossible” and adopts the conjecture ὑπὲρ Δία, “in defiance of Zeus”, which answers ὑπὲρ θεόν, “in defiance of a god” (17.327), and sets the other men against “but Zeus wills far rather for us” (17.331); he suggests that the words were changed because they seemed impious in the mouth of a god (on 17.330).

**He knows the god.** “So he spoke, and Aeneas knew Apollo who shoots from afar / as he looked him in the face” (17.333–334; see [[hekatebolos]]). He shouts to Hector and the other leaders of the Trojans and the allies that it is αἰδώς, “a shame”, to be driven back into Ilios (17.335–337; see [[aidos]]), and tells them without naming him that “one of the gods has come and stood close beside me, and says / that Zeus, the highest counselor, is still our helper in the battle” (17.338–339; see [[zeus]]). He leaps out far in front of the champions, and the Trojans rally and stand facing the Achaeans (17.342–343). His spear kills Leiocritus, son of Arisbas, “the brave companion of Lycomedes”, and Lycomedes, in pity, kills the Paeonian Apisaon in return (17.344–349; see [[leiocritus]], [[arisbas]] and [[lycomedes]]).

**The horses.** When Automedon gets down from the chariot to fight, Hector turns to Aeneas, who is near: “Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-armored Trojans, / I see the two horses of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus here, / come out into the battle with poor charioteers” (17.485–487; see [[hector]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Pandarus addressed him with the same verse when the two went out together against Diomedes (17.485 = 5.180; see [[pandarus]]), and it was Diomedes who took the horses of Aeneas then (5.319–327). “So he spoke, and the brave son of Anchises did not disobey” (17.491): ἐῢς πάϊς Ἀγχίσαο is the Catalogue's name for him (2.819; again 12.98). The two go straight on with oxhide shields over their shoulders, and Chromius and Aretus with them (17.492–497; see [[chromis]] and [[aretus]]). Automedon calls the Ajaxes and Menelaus, for “here they are bearing down through the tearful war, / Hector and Aeneas, who are the best of the Trojans” (17.512–513; see [[automedon]]). Helenus named the same two as the men on whom the toil of the Trojans and the Lycians rests, “because you are the best / in every undertaking, both in fighting and in counsel” (6.77–79). Automedon kills Aretus, Hector's spear misses, and before the two Ajaxes “Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius” give ground and leave Aretus lying (17.516–536; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]).

**The retreat.** At the end of the book the Trojans follow the men carrying Patroclus, “and two among them most of all, / Aeneas, son of Anchises, and shining Hector” (17.753–754), and the young Achaeans flee “before Aeneas and Hector” like starlings or jackdaws before a falcon (17.755–759; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]). Αἰνείας τʼ Ἀγχισιάδης begins the verse again when he and Achilles meet between the armies (17.754 = 20.160; see [[achilles]]).
