---
summary: The fight over the body of Patroclus is a fight over where it will go: the Trojans try to drag it into Ilios, to win back Sarpedon or to win glory, and they themselves would have been driven back into the city had not Apollo roused Aeneas. Achilles does not yet know of the death, because the battle is far off under the wall of Troy.
# Unnamed: the city (ἄστυ, πόλις, πτολίεθρον) in 17.131, 17.144, 17.147, 17.152, 17.160, 17.191, 17.287, 17.407, 17.419.

---

**Into the city.** Hector sends Patroclus' armor “to the city, to be great fame for him” (17.131), and his own armor “to sacred Ilios” when he puts on that of Achilles (17.191–194; see [[hector]], [[kleos]] and [[hieros]]). Glaucus bids him consider “how you will save your city and your town / alone, with the people who were born in Ilios” (17.144–145), threatens that if the Lycians go home “sheer destruction will appear for Troy” (17.155), and wants Patroclus dragged “into Ilios”, to be exchanged for the armor and body of Sarpedon (17.159–163; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]). The Trojans around the body have it most in mind “to drag him to their own city and win glory” (17.287), and an Achaean says that it would be better for the earth to gape for them all than to let them do it (17.416–419; see [[trojans]]).

**Back into the city.** When Ajax has killed Hippothous and Phorcys, the Trojans “would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice” (17.319–320; see [[would-have-had-not]]), had not Apollo shamed Aeneas: “Aeneas, how could you protect, even in defiance of a god, / steep Ilios?” (17.327–328; see [[apollo]], [[aeneas]] and [[steep]]). Aeneas calls it a shame “to go up into Ilios, beaten by our own cowardice” (17.337).

**Far from the ships.** Achilles does not know that Patroclus is dead, for they are fighting “far away from the swift ships, / under the wall of the Trojans” (17.403–404; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[achilles]]). He expected Patroclus to come back alive after pressing close to the gates, “since he did not expect at all / that he would sack the citadel without him, nor with him either” (17.405–407; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Athena, as Phoenix, warns Menelaus against the shame of letting dogs tear Achilles' companion “under the wall of the Trojans” (17.557–558; see [[athena]]).

Its epithets in the book are ἱρή, “sacred” (17.193), and αἰπεινή, “steep” (17.328), and it is “the great city of lord Priam” (17.160; see [[priam]]).
