---
summary: The book turns on who knows that Patroclus is dead. Thrasymedes and Antilochus, fighting apart, do not know (17.377–383); Achilles does not know, for the battle is far from the ships, and Thetis, who often told him the purpose of Zeus, did not tell him this (17.401–411); the horses know first, and weep (17.426–428). Hector does not know that his own death is close, and Zeus does (17.198–208).
---

**Not yet known.** “But two men had not yet learned, / two glorious men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus, / that noble Patroclus was dead, but they still thought / that he was alive and fighting the Trojans in the front of the throng” (17.377–380). “Nor yet at all / did brilliant Achilles know that Patroclus was dead” (17.401–402): he expected him back from the gates alive, since he did not expect him to sack the city without him, or with him either (17.404–407; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]), and his mother had not told him (17.408–411; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). The horses had known from the first, “ever since they first learned that their charioteer / had fallen in the dust” (17.427–428; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The rest of the book carries the knowledge to Achilles (17.640–701; see [[messenger-scene]]).

**Hector.** “Ah, wretch, death is not on your mind at all, / which is now close to you” (17.201–202): Zeus sees what Hector does not, and gives him great power for the day, “since you will not come home from the battle” (17.206–208; see [[poine]]). The narrator calls the Trojans who hope to drag away the body “the fools” (17.236), and Chromius and Aretus, who hope to take the horses, “the fools; they were not to come back without bloodshed” (17.497; see [[nepios]]).
