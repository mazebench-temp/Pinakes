summary: Over the body of Patroclus each army speaks through one nameless voice, and the two voices answer each other. An Achaean says the black earth should rather gape for them all than the Trojans drag the body to their city; a Trojan, that no one must hang back, even if all must die beside it (17.414–423). The speeches do the work of an exhortation: “So a man would say, and would rouse the might of each.”
grc: ["τις αὖ Τρώων", "τις εἴπεσκε"]
en: [a man of the great-hearted Trojans would speak]

The verse that brings in the Achaean speech has a new second half, Ἀχαιῶν χαλκοχιτώνων, “of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (17.414), and the Trojan answer has a verse of its own, ὣς δέ τις αὖ Τρώων μεγαθύμων αὐδήσασκεν, “And so in turn a man of the great-hearted Trojans would speak” (17.420), with αὐδήσασκεν formed like εἴπεσκεν. Elsewhere such speeches react to something the crowd has seen (see the article above); here the two answer each other across the body (see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Walter Leaf quotes a scholium that Zenodotus did not have 17.404–425 and that Aristarchus athetized only 17.420, and notes that without that verse the Trojan's two verses would be read as part of the Achaean speech (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.404 and 17.420; see the translation's note on 17.404–425).
