---
summary: The book is full of men giving way, and each retreat is argued or judged. Menelaus reasons that no Danaan will blame him for yielding to a man the gods help, and goes back step by step like a lion driven from a farmyard (17.91–113); Hector gives ground before Ajax and is called a runaway (17.129, 17.143), answers that the mind of Zeus is stronger (17.176–178), and is taunted by Apollo for shrinking back before Menelaus (17.586–588). When Zeus turns the battle, Peneleos is the first to flee, Leitus falls back in fear, and Idomeneus drives to the ships (17.597–625).
---

Menelaus' retreat is the one the poem argues out (see [[decision-scene]] and [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]), and the lion simile gives its manner: he goes “turning back again and again”, ἐντροπαλιζόμενος, and “unwillingly” (17.109–112), as he leaves the body a second time “very unwillingly” to look for Antilochus, like a lion that goes off at dawn after a night of javelins and torches (17.657–667; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). Hector's withdrawals are judged by others: Glaucus calls him φύξηλις, “a runaway” (17.143; see [[kleos]]), and Apollo, as Phaenops, asks who of the Achaeans will fear him now (17.586–590). At the end the whole army gives way, “and there was no respite from the war” (17.755–761; see [[phobos]]).
