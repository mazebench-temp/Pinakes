---
summary: In How the Young Man Should Study Poetry Plutarch takes three lessons from Book 17. Patroclus, who “knew how to be kind to all”, had learned his kindness, as Hector had learned to be brave; Hector's reproach to Glaucus, whom he had thought the wisest of the Lycians, goes with the lesson that the wise do not blame others beyond their desert; and “Hector, best in looks”, like the insult to Paris, makes good looks without excellence a reproach.
---

- **Patroclus' kindness.** Plutarch leaves the grammarians their quibbles, the wordplay of Cleanthes and Chrysippus, and tells the young reader to press instead on what is both useful and persuasive. He sets side by side Hector's “nor does my heart bid me, since I have learned to be brave” (6.444) and Menelaus' praise of Patroclus, “for he knew how to be kind to all” (17.671). In the first, he says, the poet shows courage to be a thing learned, and in the second he holds that to keep company with men in a friendly and gracious way comes from knowledge and reason; so he urges the young not to neglect themselves but to learn what is noble and attend to their teachers, since clumsiness and cowardice are want of learning and ignorance (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 31e–f). The verb that carries the lesson is ἐπίστατο, “knew how”, and it is said of the ἐνηείη, the “gentleness”, of Patroclus (17.670–671; see [[eneeie]] and [[patroclus]]).
- **Hector's answer to Glaucus.** Next the young are to notice that Homer makes good conduct the work of wisdom: Nestor “will not tell a lie, for he is very wise” (*Odyssey* 3.20, 3.328); Menelaus rebukes Antilochus, who has cheated him in the chariot race, as one who was wise before (πρόσθεν πεπνυμένε, 23.570); and Hector asks Glaucus why, being the man he is, he has spoken so arrogantly, when Hector thought him wiser than all the others. The point, Plutarch says, is that the wise neither lie, nor fight unfairly in contests, nor accuse others beyond their desert (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 32a–b, quoting 17.170–171); the last is Glaucus' fault. Plutarch's text of the second verse begins ὦ πέπον, “my friend”, where ours has ὢ πόποι, “ah”. Glaucus had called Hector a runaway who left Sarpedon to the Argives (17.142–168; see [[glaucus]] and [[hector]]).
- **Best in looks.** Homer spoke very well, Plutarch says, in “Evil Paris, best in looks” (3.39) and “Hector, best in looks” (17.142): he shows that a man who has no good thing finer than his good looks deserves blame and reproach. The lesson should be applied to the like, to humble those proud of worthless things and to teach the young to take “best in money” or “best at dinners” for a reproach, since preeminence should be sought in noble things (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 34e–35a; see [[best-in-looks]] and [[praise-and-blame]]).
