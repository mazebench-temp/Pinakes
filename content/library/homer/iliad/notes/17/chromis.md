**Chromis and Chromius.** The Catalogue's Mysians “were led by Chromis and Ennomus the augur” (2.858; see [[mysians]] and [[ennomus]]). In Book 17 Hector urges on “Phorcys and Chromius and Ennomus the augur” (17.218), and Walter Leaf, noting that the names of these verses are mostly taken from the Catalogue (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.216), says that “Chromis is called Chromios in 17.218, 494, 534” (on 2.858). The pairing with Ennomus, in the Catalogue's words, makes the identification likely; the poem does not say that the Chromius who goes after the horses later in the book is the same man, but it gives him no other description. Four other men in the Iliad are called Chromius: a Pylian captain, a son of Priam, a Lycian and a Trojan killed by Teucer (4.295, 5.160, 5.677, 8.275; see [[chromius]], [[chromius-son-of-priam]], [[chromius-of-lycia]] and [[chromius-killed-by-teucer]]).

**After the horses.** When Hector and Aeneas go after the horses of Achilles, “with them went Chromius and godlike Aretus, / both of them; and their hearts had high hopes / of killing the two men and driving off the horses with high-arched necks, / the fools” (17.494–497; see [[hector]], [[aeneas]], [[aretus]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Automedon kills Aretus, and before the two Ajaxes “Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius” give ground and leave Aretus lying (17.533–536; see [[automedon]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). θεοειδής, “godlike”, ends the verse for him at 17.534 as it did for Aretus at 17.494 (see [[godlike]]).
