---
summary: The iron din of the battle goes up “through the barren upper air to the bronze sky”: the one place where the epithet of the sea is given to the upper air.
---

“So they fought on, and the iron din / went up through the barren upper air to the bronze sky” (17.424–425). ἀτρύγετος, the sea's epithet, is said here of the αἰθήρ, the bright upper air above the mist that covers the body (17.366–377; see the translation's note on 17.371). The din of the battle rises through it to the sky, while the horses of Achilles stand weeping apart (17.426–440; [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf takes the iron of the din as “inflexible, i.e. indomitable, unwearying” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.424; see the translation's note on 17.424). See [[sea]].
