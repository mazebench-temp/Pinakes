summary: Around the body of Patroclus the mist is so thick that “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon”, while the rest of the battle is fought in the sharp light of the sun. Zeus repeats the term of the day, the setting of the sun, and when he scatters the mist at Ajax's prayer, the sun shines out over the whole battle.

**Sun and moon.** Zeus has poured mist about the helmets of the Achaeans who stand over Patroclus (17.268–273; see [[zeus]]), and in that part of the battle the fight goes on in darkness:

{{quote:a68777d0-332c-5bb7-a85b-3e47f4bf082c}}

The mist (ἀήρ) is so thick that sun and moon seem to have perished, as on the night when Odysseus' ships came to the island off the land of the Cyclopes, and mist lay deep around the ships and the moon gave no light from heaven (*Odyssey* 9.143–145). Elsewhere the armies fight at their ease “under the upper air” (ὑπʼ αἰθέρι, 17.371), and Ajax will pray for αἴθρη, “clear sky” (17.646); the translation keeps the two words apart (see the translation's note on 17.371). Walter Leaf reports that Naber would read αἴθρῃ here, after the cloudless clear sky of Olympus in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 6.44–45), but keeps αἰθέρι, which the roar that reached “the upper air and the rays of Zeus” supports (13.837), and takes αἰθήρ as simply the air of heaven (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.371). The sharp light of the sun (αὐγὴ ἠελίου ὀξεῖα, 17.371–372) is the light that Zeus called “the sharpest of all for seeing” when he promised Hera that not even the Sun could see through his golden cloud (14.344–345; see [[the-golden-cloud]] and the note on Book 14).

**The term.** Pitying the horses of Achilles, Zeus repeats to himself the term of the day: he will give the Trojans glory to kill “until they come to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (17.454–455). He set it for Hector in the same words in Book 11 (11.193–194, 11.208–209; see the note on Book 11), and the day that began at 11.1 does not end until Hera sends the unwilling sun down into the streams of Oceanus (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[oceanus]]). The sun had passed the middle of the sky before Patroclus died (16.777–780; see the note on Book 16).

**The sun shines out.** Ajax, who cannot see a man to send to Achilles, prays that Zeus may “make clear sky, and grant us to see with our eyes” (17.646; see [[ajax]]). Zeus scatters the mist, “and the sun shone out, and all the battle was seen” (17.650).
