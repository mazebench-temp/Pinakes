summary: The Trojans fight for the body of Patroclus, which Hector means to give to the Trojan dogs. They push the Achaeans back and are scattered by Ajax, and would have fled into Ilios had not Apollo roused Aeneas; around the body they fight in mist and pull it this way and that. When Zeus thunders from Ida they rout the Achaeans, and at the end they press after the men who carry the body to the ships.

**The Trojan dogs.** Hector, having stripped Patroclus, was dragging him “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127; see [[hector]] and [[dogs]]). Menelaus calls on the Achaean leaders to be indignant “that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.255), a phrase that Iris will repeat to Achilles (18.179), and Zeus pours mist around the body because he “hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, / the Trojan dogs” (17.272–273; see [[zeus]]). The adjective is Τρῳός, the form that otherwise names the Trojan women, Τρῳαί, and once the Trojan mares (16.393; see [[trojan-women]]).

**Charge and rout.** “And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them” (17.262), with a shout like a great wave roaring against the stream at the mouth of a river (17.263–266; see [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]]). “And the Trojans first pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans” (17.274 = 16.569), but they kill no one and only drag at the corpse, and Ajax scatters “the battalions of the Trojans / who stood around Patroclus and had it most in mind / to drag him to their own city and win glory” (17.275–287; see [[ajax]] and [[kydos]]). Then they would have gone up into Ilios, “beaten by their own cowardice”, had not Apollo roused Aeneas (17.319–323; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[apollo]]). Aeneas calls it αἰδώς, “a shame”, to be driven into the city (17.336–337; see [[aeneas]] and [[aidos]]), and the Trojans rally and stand facing the Achaeans (17.342–343). The dead of the Trojans and their mighty allies fall heaped together with the Danaans, but far fewer of the Danaans perish, since they keep close and defend one another (17.360–365; see [[trojan-allies]]).

**Around the body.** In the mist over the body the best men fight, while “the other Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans / fought at their ease under the upper air” (17.370–371). Both sides pull the body this way and that, as men stretch a hide, the Trojans hoping to drag it “to Ilios”, the Achaeans to the ships (17.389–397; see [[the-stretched-hide]]). What a man of each side would say is given in turn: an Achaean would rather the black earth gaped for them all, and a man of the great-hearted Trojans would say, “Friends, even if it is our fate to be brought down beside this man, / all of us alike, let no one yet hang back from the war” (17.414–422; see [[tis-speech]]). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus rejected the verse that brings in the Trojan (17.420); without it, as Leaf observes, the two verses that follow must be read as part of the Achaean's speech (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.420; see the translation's note on 17.404–425).

**The victory.** When Zeus shakes the aegis on Ida, he gives “victory to the Trojans” (17.596; see [[aegis]]). Ajax knows that “father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans” (17.630; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]), and Menelaus tells Antilochus that “victory is the Trojans'” (17.689; see [[antilochus]]). When Menelaus and Meriones lift the body, “the Trojan army” shouts and charges like hounds at a wounded boar, but whenever the two Ajaxes turn and stand, “their color changed, and no one dared / to dart forward and fight for the body” (17.722–734; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]]). They follow still, Aeneas and Hector most of all, and the book ends at the ditch: “and there was no respite from the war” (17.753–761).

Their epithets in the book are ἱππόδαμοι, “horse-taming” (17.230, 17.418), ὑπέρθυμοι, “high-hearted” (17.276), μεγάθυμοι, “great-hearted” (17.420), φιλοπτόλεμοι, “war-loving” (17.194), and χαλκοχίτωνες, “bronze-armored” (17.485; see [[tamer-of-horses]], [[high-hearted]], [[great-hearted]], [[war-loving]] and [[bronze-armored]]).
