summary: The fly of Book 17 is the image of Menelaus' courage: Athena puts in his breast “the daring of a fly”, which keeps on trying to bite however often it is driven from a man's skin, because human blood is sweet to it (17.570–572).
grc: [μυίης]
en: [fly]
---

καί οἱ μυίης θάρσος ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἐνῆκεν, “and put in his breast the daring of a fly” (17.570; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]] and [[menelaus]]). In Book 2 the flies were a swarm, the measure of an army, and in Book 16 they were the fighters crowding around the body of Sarpedon (2.469–473, 16.641–643; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). Here the fly is alone, and what it gives the likeness is persistence: driven off again and again, it always comes back to bite. Athena gives the fly's boldness to the man from whose skin, in Book 4, she brushed Pandarus' arrow as a mother brushes a fly from her sleeping child (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). In Book 19 the flies are the eaters of the dead, and Achilles fears that they will breed worms in the wounds of Patroclus (19.23–27).
