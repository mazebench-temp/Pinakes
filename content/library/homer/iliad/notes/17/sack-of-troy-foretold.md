---
summary: In Book 17 the fall of Troy is what the Trojans fear and what Achilles knows will not come yet. Glaucus warns that sheer destruction will appear for Troy if the Lycians go home (17.144–155); the Argives would have driven the Trojans into Ilios “beyond the portion of Zeus”, had not Apollo shamed Aeneas (17.319–341); and Achilles never expected Patroclus to sack the city, without him or with him, for he had often heard the purpose of Zeus from his mother (17.404–409).

**Not without him, nor with him.** Achilles does not know that Patroclus is dead. He expects him to come back alive “after pressing close to the gates”, “since he did not expect at all / that he would sack the citadel without him, nor with him either; / for often he had learned this from his mother, hearing it in secret, / who would report to him the purpose of great Zeus” (17.405–409; see [[achilles]], [[thetis]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The fall of Troy is part of the purpose of Zeus that his mother reported: the city is not to fall to Patroclus, and not to Achilles either. Apollo has told Patroclus as much at the wall: it was not his portion that the city should be sacked by his spear, “nor by Achilles, who is far better than you” (16.707–709; see the note on Book 16). Walter Leaf takes αὐτῷ in “nor with him either” as reflexive, Achilles himself, parallel to ἕθεν, “without him”, and finds the other construction, that Achilles would not take the city with Patroclus, harsh (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.407). He objects that Achilles would hardly have expected Patroclus to reach the gates after the charge he had given him not to lead the way toward Ilios (16.87–96; on 17.404). The man who wishes to sack Troy will not live to see it fall (see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**Beyond the portion of Zeus.** When Ajax has killed Hippothous and Phorcys, the Trojans nearly fall back into the city: “Then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares, / would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice, / and the Argives would have won glory even beyond the portion of Zeus / by their own power and strength” (17.319–322; see [[would-have-had-not]]). ὑπὲρ Διὸς αἶσαν is one of the phrases with which the poem allows that a thing may happen, or nearly happen, beyond what is fated (see the translation's note on 6.487). In Book 16 the Achaeans “would have taken high-gated Troy / at the hands of Patroclus” (16.698–699); here the threat is smaller, the Trojans' flight into their own walls, and Apollo prevents it again. In the likeness of the herald Periphas he asks Aeneas, “how could you protect, even in defiance of a god, / steep Ilios?” Other men have saved their cities by their own strength, but the Trojans tremble, though Zeus wills them the victory (17.327–332; see [[apollo]] and [[aeneas]]). Leaf takes ὑπὲρ θεόν, “in defiance of a god”, as the same thought as ὑπὲρ Διὸς αἶσαν (on 17.327; see the translation's note on 17.327, 17.321). Aeneas tells the Trojans that it would be a shame “to go up into Ilios, beaten by our own cowardice” (17.336–337), and they stand.

**Sheer destruction.** Glaucus rebukes Hector for leaving Sarpedon to the Argives: “Consider now how you will save your city and your town / alone, with the people who were born in Ilios”; if the Lycians listen to him, “we will go home, and sheer destruction will appear for Troy” (17.144–155; see [[glaucus]] and [[lycians]]). Hector answers the allies that he brought them from their cities “so that the wives of the Trojans and their little children / you might protect with a ready heart from the war-loving Achaeans” (17.223–224; see [[trojan-allies]] and [[captive-women]]).
