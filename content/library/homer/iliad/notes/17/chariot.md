summary: The chariot of Achilles is the book's other prize. Its horses will not move for Automedon's whip and words, and stand weeping like a pillar on a tomb (17.426–440); Zeus sends them back into the battle, but Automedon, alone in “the sacred chariot”, cannot both drive and fight (17.456–465), and gives Alcimedon the whip and the reins (17.479–483). Hector, who wants the horses, sees “poor charioteers” and comes for them with Aeneas (17.483–490).

**The driver and the fighter.** A chariot carries a driver and a fighter, and Automedon alone is both: “for there was no way, alone as he was in the sacred chariot, / to attack with the spear and hold in the swift horses” (17.464–465). Walter Leaf suggests that the chariot is “sacred” as drawn by horses of divine origin (see the translation's note on 17.464). Alcimedon takes the reins, Automedon gets down, and the two keep their roles: Automedon tells him to keep the horses breathing on his back (17.501–502), and kills Aretus on foot (17.516–524). Hector sees the horses come out “with poor charioteers” and hopes to take them (17.486–488), but Zeus has already sworn that Hector will not ride behind them (17.448–449; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[automedon]]).

**Other chariots.** Hector springs into his chariot when Ajax comes (17.130), and Idomeneus, who had come on foot, is saved by the chariot of Meriones, whose charioteer Coeranus is killed in his place and “let the reins fall to the ground” (17.609–619); Meriones picks them up and tells Idomeneus to drive to the ships (17.620–625).
