summary: The son of Oileus is the first to answer when Menelaus calls the leaders to the body, running through the combat to meet him (17.256–257). After that he is one of “the two Ajaxes”, who part Automedon from Hector and Aeneas and, at the end of the book, hold back the Trojans while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body away (17.507–535, 17.668–672, 17.707–761).

**The first to come.** Menelaus, standing over the body with Ajax, shouts to the leaders of the Argives to come of their own accord and be indignant that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs (17.246–255; see [[menelaus]]). “So he spoke, and swift Ajax, son of Oileus, heard him clearly; / and he was the first to come running through the combat to meet him” (17.256–257), and Idomeneus and Meriones come after him (17.258–259; see [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]). ὀξὺ δʼ ἄκουσεν, “heard him clearly”, is found only here; the speed is his own, ταχύς, “swift”, the mark of his name from the Catalogue on (2.527; see [[swift]]). He is not named alone again in the book.

**One of the pair.** From here on he is present in the dual and the plural. When Hector and Aeneas come against Automedon and the immortal horses, Automedon calls “the two Ajaxes and Menelaus” to keep the pitiless day from him and Alcimedon, and the two Ajaxes come through the throng at his call and part the fighters before they close with swords (17.507–534; see [[automedon]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Menelaus, sent to find Antilochus, charges the Ajaxes and Meriones to remember the gentleness of Patroclus (17.668–672), and when he comes back tells them that Achilles cannot fight without armor (17.707–714). Telamonian Ajax answers for both: Menelaus and Meriones will carry the body, “and behind you / we two will fight the Trojans and brilliant Hector, / with equal spirit, of one name” (17.718–720; see [[ajax]]). ὁμώνυμοι, “of one name”, is said only here in Homer, and only of these two (see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). Whenever the pair turn and stand, the Trojans' color changes (17.732–734), and they hold back the battle as a wooded ridge holds back floodwater (17.747–753; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]).

At the wall in Book 12 the great Ajax went to help Menestheus and left the son of Oileus to hold their place (12.364–369); in this book they fight together to the end.
