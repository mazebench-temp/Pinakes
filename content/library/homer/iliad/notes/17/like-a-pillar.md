{{quote:8d39b080-7566-5c93-8aba-0cbd71a80611}}

ἀλλʼ ὥς τε στήλη μένει ἔμπεδον, ἥ τʼ ἐπὶ τύμβῳ / ἀνέρος ἑστήκῃ τεθνηότος ἠὲ γυναικός, / ὣς μένον ἀσφαλέως περικαλλέα δίφρον ἔχοντες, “but as a pillar stays fixed that stands on the tomb / of a man who has died, or of a woman, / so they stood unmoving, holding the beautiful chariot” (17.434–436). The pillar is the one set on a grave mound: the burial that Hera and Zeus would give Sarpedon is “a tomb and a pillar” (16.457, 16.675), and Walter Leaf compares the pillar on the tomb of Ilus behind which Paris shoots at Diomedes (11.371; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.435; see [[tomb]]). μένει ἔμπεδον, “stays fixed”, and μένον ἀσφαλέως, “they stood unmoving”, put the same verb on the stone and on the horses. Automedon has tried the whip, soothing words and threats, and the pair will go neither back to the ships by the Hellespont nor into the battle (17.429–433; see [[automedon]]).

The point of likeness is stillness, as in the simile of Alcathous, who stood under Poseidon's spell like a pillar or a tree (13.437). But this pillar stands on a grave, and the stillness is mourning. The next verses show it: the horses bow their heads to the ground, hot tears run from their eyelids, and their manes are soiled in the dust (17.437–440; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). They stand over their dead charioteer as a stone stands over the dead, and the verse that adds “or of a woman” makes the stone any grave's. In Book 23 Achilles describes the same pair as they stand mourning Patroclus with their manes resting on the ground (23.280–284).
