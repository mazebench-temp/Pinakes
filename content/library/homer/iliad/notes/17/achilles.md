summary: Achilles never appears in the book, and does not know that Patroclus is dead. Hector puts on his armor, the gods' gift to Peleus, in which the son will not grow old; his immortal horses weep for their charioteer; the fight is for a body that the Achaeans mean to bring back to him. Ajax wishes for a messenger, and Menelaus sends Antilochus with the news, but tells the Ajaxes that Achilles cannot come out without armor (17.75–78, 17.186–214, 17.401–411, 17.426–458, 17.640–655, 17.685–711).

**The armor.** Hector has stripped Patroclus of the armor Achilles lent him, and puts it on apart from the battle: “the immortal armor / of Achilles, son of Peleus, which the heavenly gods / gave to his dear father; and he granted it to his son / when he grew old; but the son did not grow old in his father's armor” (17.194–197; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]], [[exchange-of-armor]] and [[peleus]]). The last words look forward to his early death (see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Zeus, seeing Hector in it, calls Achilles “the best of men, before whom others too tremble” (17.203; see [[zeus]]), and Hector goes among the allies “flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.214). Glaucus, too, measures Patroclus by his master: “the attendant of such a man has been slain, who is by far the best / of the Argives beside the ships” (17.164–165; see [[glaucus]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and the narrator measures Ajax: the best of the Danaans “after the noble son of Peleus” (17.280; see [[ajax]]).

**The horses.** Apollo turns Hector from chasing the horses of Achilles, which are hard to master “for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (17.75–78; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[thetis]]). They stand apart from the battle weeping for Patroclus, and Zeus pities them and regrets that the gods gave them to Peleus, a mortal (17.426–447; see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]). Automedon fears that Hector will not stop until he mounts “behind the fine-maned horses of Achilles” (17.504; see [[automedon]]).

The battle is far from the ships, under the wall of Troy, and Achilles expects Patroclus back alive, since he did not expect him to sack the city without him, “nor with him either” (17.403–407); his mother used to tell him in secret the purpose of Zeus, but not this (17.408–411; see [[thetis]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The passage stands between the stretching of the hide and the iron din that goes up to the sky (17.389–401, 17.424–425). Walter Leaf notes that the prophecy Achilles recalls in the next book, that the best of the Myrmidons would die while he lived, “almost directly contradicts” the last two verses (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.408; 18.9–11; see the translation's notes on 17.404–425 and 17.408–411).

**The news.** The Achaeans fight for the body in his name. Menelaus would drag it away “for Achilles, son of Peleus” and bring “at least his corpse to Achilles” (17.105, 17.121; see [[menelaus]]), and Athena, in Phoenix's form, calls Patroclus “the trusted companion of noble Achilles” (17.557). When Zeus helps the Trojans, Ajax wishes for a messenger: “Would that there were some companion to take the news quickly / to the son of Peleus, since I do not think he has even heard / the grievous news, that his dear companion has perished” (17.640–642). Menelaus finds Antilochus and sends him: “But run quickly to the ships of the Achaeans, to Achilles, / and tell him, in the hope that he may quickly bring the corpse safe to his ship, / naked” (17.691–693; see [[antilochus]]), and Antilochus runs off weeping “to bring the evil news to Achilles, son of Peleus” (17.701). But Menelaus does not expect him to come: “I do not think / that he will come now, however angry he is with brilliant Hector; / for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans” (17.709–711). In the next book, unarmed, he shows himself at the ditch, and his shout saves the body (18.203–233).
