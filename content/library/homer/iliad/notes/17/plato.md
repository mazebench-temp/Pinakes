---
summary: Plato takes Apollo's taunt, “a soft spearman”, for Homer's own judgment of Menelaus in the Symposium, and in the Republic makes it the end of a man whose spirit too much soft music has melted away.
---

- Menelaus, “a soft spearman”, who comes unbidden to the feast of his better (*Symposium* 174b–c, on 17.588 and 2.408): [[plato-menelaus-uninvited]].
- **The soft spearman of the Republic.** Socrates is discussing how music and gymnastics should tune the two parts of the guardians' nature, the spirited and the philosophic. When a man lets music pipe over him and pour into his soul through his ears, as through a funnel, the sweet, soft and mournful modes, and spends his whole life humming and delighted with song, the first effect is good: whatever spirit he had is softened like iron and made useful instead of useless and hard. But if he keeps on and does not let up, the music melts and dissolves the spirit, until it has melted it away and cut the sinews, as it were, out of his soul, and made him “a soft spearman” (μαλθακὸν αἰχμητήν, *Republic* 3.411a–b). A man spirited by nature becomes irritable instead, quick to flare up at trifles and quick to die down (3.411b–c). Plato does not name Menelaus here. The words are Apollo's, when the god, in the likeness of Phaenops, taunts Hector for shrinking back before Menelaus, “who before now at least / was a soft spearman” (17.587–588; see [[menelaus]] and [[apollo]]). In the *Symposium* Socrates takes them for the poet's own judgment; Walter Leaf reports that Aristonicus answered that they are an enemy's words, since the poet himself calls Menelaus ἀρηΐφιλος, “dear to Ares” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.588; see [[title-of-book-17]]).
