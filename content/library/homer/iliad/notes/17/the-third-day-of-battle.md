---
summary: Book 17 is the afternoon of the third day of battle, a whole book without a mark of the hour. Around the body of Patroclus the fighters are in mist while the rest of the battle goes on under a clear sun, until Zeus scatters the mist at Ajax's prayer; Zeus renews to the Trojans the promise he sent Hector in the morning, glory to kill until they reach the ships and the sun goes down (17.453–455; 17.454–455 ≈ 11.193–194); and the book ends in rout at the ditch, with the sun still up.

**No hour.** The poem marked the hours of this day at its beginning, the morning and the woodcutter's meal (11.84–90), and at its turn, when the sun “stood astride the middle of the sky” and then moved “toward the time for unyoking oxen” (16.777–780). Book 17 marks none. It speaks instead of the whole day. Hector asks Glaucus to watch “whether I shall be a coward all day long” (πανημέριος, 17.180; see [[hector]] and [[glaucus]]), and around the body “all day long the great quarrel of strife went on” (πανημερίοις, 17.384); the toil over Patroclus is the one that Zeus stretched tight “that day” (ἤματι τῷ, 17.400–401). Agamemnon had said in the morning that Zeus did not allow him “to fight the Trojans all day long” (11.278–279). Walter Leaf found πανημερίοις at 17.384 “meaningless here”, since it can mean neither “all day” nor “all the rest of the day” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.384).

**Sun and mist.** The light of the afternoon falls unevenly. Around the body the best men fight in a mist so thick that “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–369), while the other Trojans and Achaeans fight “at their ease under the upper air, and the sharp light / of the sun was spread over them” (17.370–377; see [[helios]] and [[zeus]]). When Ajax prays for light, Zeus scatters the mist, “and the sun shone out, and all the battle was seen” (17.645–650; see [[ajax]]). The sun is still high, and the day is far from over (see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]).

**The promise renewed.** Zeus, pitying the horses of Achilles, gives the Trojans again the term of the day:

{{quote:7eb408a2-5e9d-5807-a82d-6120465c4a73}}

The last two verses are the promise that Zeus sent to Hector by Iris in the morning (11.186–194, 11.200–209): power “to kill, until he comes to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194). Now Zeus speaks it in his own thought, and of the Trojans, “them” and “they” for “him” and “he” (see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Its first part has been fulfilled once: Hector reached the ships (13.1, 15.704–725). Patroclus has driven the Trojans back to their walls (16.684–711), and the promise begins again. Walter Leaf thought the verses borrowed from the earlier stage of the fighting, and found in this the reason for their inconsistency with Book 18, where the Trojans do not reach the ships or even the wall but are stopped at the ditch some distance off (on 17.454). The Achaeans flee to the ships and the Hellespont, Achilles' shout from the ditch turns the Trojans back, and Hera sends the unwilling sun down to the streams of Ocean (18.148–150, 18.215–231, 18.239–242).

**The turn of the afternoon.** The battle turns once more in the book. Zeus takes up the aegis, covers Ida in clouds and gives the Trojans the victory (17.593–596; see [[aegis]]), and Ajax and Menelaus see “that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle” (17.626–627). The book ends with the Achaeans in flight around the ditch, “and there was no respite from the war” (17.761 = 16.302).
