---
summary: Menelaus is “dear to Ares” in the first verse of the book and twice more; the Achaeans are “dear to Ares” in the verse of a rout averted, as in Book 6, and in Aeneas' rebuke; and Lycomedes, “dear to Ares,” avenges Leiocritus.
---

**Menelaus.** “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares” (17.1); Euphorbus “spoke to Menelaus, dear to Ares” (17.11; [[euphorbus]]); and while Ajax stands over Patroclus, “on the other side the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / stood, nursing great grief in his breast” (17.138–139). The epithet that belonged to him in his duel with Paris in Book 3 is his again in his book ([[menelaus]]).

**The Achaeans.** “Then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares, / would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice” (17.319–320) repeats the verses of Book 6 (6.73–74), where Helenus' advice turns the rout; here Apollo does it, rousing Aeneas ([[would-have-had-not]], [[aeneas]]). Aeneas then turns the words back on the Trojans: “this now is a shame, before the Achaeans dear to Ares / to go up into Ilios, beaten by our own cowardice” (17.336–337; [[aidos]]).

**Lycomedes.** “And Lycomedes, dear to Ares, pitied him as he fell” (17.346; [[lycomedes]], [[leiocritus]], [[pity]]): his companion Leiocritus has fallen to Aeneas' spear, and he kills Apisaon in return ([[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]).
