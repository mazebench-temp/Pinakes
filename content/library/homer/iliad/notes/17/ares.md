summary: Ares does not come to the battle in person. When Zeus fits the armor of Achilles to Hector, Ares, “dread Enyalius”, enters into him; elsewhere his name is war itself. Hector and Automedon are “the equal of swift Ares”, Meriones “the equal of Enyalius”, not even Ares would have made light of the toil over the body, and the two Ajaxes have withstood “sharp Ares” side by side.

**Ares in Hector.** Zeus has foretold Hector's death and promised him great power for a time, and he nods (17.198–209; see [[zeus]]). Then:

{{quote:2a2a34d0-22a7-50ef-b0b8-62ac7c10810d}}

δῦ δέ μιν Ἄρης, “and Ares entered into him”, is found only here. What enters a man elsewhere is anger, as it entered Meleager (ἔδυ χόλος, 9.553) and enters Achilles at the sight of his new armor (19.16; see [[cholos]]). The god's other name follows in apposition, printed in lowercase: Walter Leaf calls ἐνυάλιος here “a mere epithet of Ares”, the only such use in Homer, and thinks the word, like the name of Enyo, probably not Greek (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.211); the translation renders it as the name, as everywhere else in the Iliad (see [[enyalius]], [[enyo]] and the translation's note on 17.210–211). Hector's limbs are filled “with courage and strength” (ἀλκῆς καὶ σθένεος), and he goes among the allies with a great cry, “flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.211–214; see [[hector]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). Automedon, praying to Zeus, is filled with the same courage and strength (17.498–499; see [[automedon]]).

**The equal of Ares.** Apollo rouses Hector against Menelaus, “the equal of swift Ares” (θοῷ ἀτάλαντον Ἄρηϊ, 17.72; see [[apollo]]), and Automedon, stripping the armor from Aretus, is “the equal of swift Ares” (17.536; see [[equal-of-ares]]), the title of Patroclus as he charged the Trojans three times before his death (16.784). Meriones comes to Menelaus' call, “the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men” (17.259; see [[meriones]]): the whole verse is his title in the Catalogue and twice more (2.651, 7.166, 8.264; see [[enyalius]]).

**Ares as war.** The fight around the body is such that “not Ares, who rouses armies, nor Athena / would have made light of it when they saw it” (17.398–399; see [[athena]]). λαοσσόος, “who rouses armies”, is given to Athena (13.128), to Strife (20.48) and to Apollo (20.79), and only here to Ares (see [[who-rouses-armies]]). Hector hopes that the charioteers of Achilles' horses will not dare “to stand and fight against us face to face in the work of Ares” (17.490), where the dative Ἄρηϊ is battle itself. When Hector's spear misses Automedon and sticks quivering in the ground, “then mighty Ares let its might go” (17.528–529; see [[obrimos]]), the verse with which Idomeneus' spear came to rest in the heart of Alcathous and Aeneas' spear in the ground beside Meriones (13.444, 16.613; see the translation's note on 13.443–444). And the two Ajaxes, who will hold off the Trojans while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body, say that they “have withstood sharp Ares, standing side by side” (17.721; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]); ὀξὺς Ἄρης is the war that leaders on both sides call on their men to rouse (2.440, 8.531).

**Dear to Ares.** The book opens with “the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares” (17.1; see [[menelaus]]), and the epithet returns for him (17.11, 17.138), for Lycomedes (17.346) and for the Achaeans (17.319, 17.336; see [[dear-to-ares]]).

**A variant.** Where Apollo rouses Hector in the likeness of Phaenops (17.582), Zenodotus wrote that Ares came and roused him. Walter Leaf reports the scholium that asks how Ares could suddenly be there, and finds the question just, since Ares has not been on the battlefield since the end of Book 5 and was kept from going back in Book 15 (15.121–142; Leaf on 17.582; see [[apollo]] and [[zenodotus]]).
