summary: Glaucus reproaches Hector for leaving Sarpedon, his guest-friend and companion, as prey for the Argives, and proposes to win back his armor and his body by dragging Patroclus' body into Troy. Glaucus does not know that Apollo and Sleep and Death have already carried the body home to Lycia.
# Unnamed: Sarpedon in Glaucus' rebuke (17.151-153) and "his body" (17.163).
refs: [17.151-153, 17.163]
---

**Glaucus' reproach.** “How would you save a lesser man in the throng, / hard man, when you left Sarpedon, at once your guest-friend and your companion, / to become the prey and spoil of the Argives, / who was of great use to you, to the city and to yourself, / while he lived? And now you did not dare to keep the dogs from him” (17.149–153; see [[glaucus]] and [[hector]]). ξεῖνον καὶ ἑταῖρον, “at once your guest-friend and your companion” (17.150; see [[xenia]] and [[hetairos]]): when he fell the Trojans grieved for him as “a prop of their city, / foreigner though he was” (16.549–550). Dying, he begged Glaucus to keep the Achaeans from stripping him (16.492–501), and in Book 5 he begged Hector not to leave him “as prey for the Danaans” (5.684); Glaucus now uses the word of Hector's failure, ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα, “the prey and spoil” (17.151).

**The exchange.** If the Trojans could drag Patroclus into the city, Glaucus says, “the Argives would quickly release the fine armor of Sarpedon, / and we could bring his body into Ilios” (17.162–163), for Patroclus was the attendant of the best of the Argives (17.164–165; see [[patroclus]]). The Achaeans had stripped the armor and Patroclus had given it to his companions to carry to the ships (16.663–665), but the body had been taken from the field by Apollo, washed and anointed, and carried home to Lycia by Sleep and Death (16.666–683; see [[apollo]], [[sleep]] and [[death]]). αὐτόν (17.163) is the body as opposed to the arms, and “Evidently Glaukos knows nothing of the rescue of the body by Apollo”, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.151 and 17.163; see the translation's note on 17.163). The armor stays with the Achaeans: Achilles sets it out as a prize at Patroclus' games (23.798–800; see [[contests]]).
