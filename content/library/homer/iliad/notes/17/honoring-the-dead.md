---
summary: What the fight is for. The Trojans want Patroclus' body to behead and give to the dogs of Troy, or to trade for Sarpedon's; the Achaeans want to bring it, naked as it is, to Achilles. The fear of the poem's first lines, that the dead will be the spoil of dogs and birds, is spoken again and again over one body, and Zeus himself hates that Patroclus should become a spoil for the enemy's dogs.
---

**Dogs and birds.** The poem opened with the dead made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5). Here Hector means to make it true of one body, and every voice on the Achaean side speaks the fear. Hector drags the body off “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127); Ajax fears less for the corpse of Patroclus, “which will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans”, than for his own head (17.240–242); Menelaus calls the leaders to be indignant “that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.255; see [[nemesis]]); and Athena, as Phoenix, warns Menelaus of the shame if the trusted companion of Achilles “is torn by swift dogs under the wall of the Trojans” (17.558; see [[katepheie]]). Zeus shares the fear: he had not hated Patroclus while he lived, “and he hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, / the Trojan dogs” (17.272–273; see [[zeus]] and [[dogs]]). Glaucus turns the same charge on Hector for Sarpedon: “And now you did not dare to keep the dogs from him” (17.153).

**For Achilles.** The Achaean aim is a burial by his friends. Menelaus' first thought is to drag the body away “for Achilles, son of Peleus” (17.104–105), and his word to Ajax is “if we may bring at least his corpse to Achilles, / naked” (17.120–122); the message to Achilles asks that he “may quickly bring the corpse safe to his ship, / naked” (17.692–693; see [[messenger-scene]]). The Trojans think of a trade. Glaucus says that if they dragged Patroclus into Ilios, the Argives would give back Sarpedon's armor and they could bring his body into the city (17.159–163), not knowing that Apollo has already carried Sarpedon home to Lycia for burial (16.666–683; see the translation's note on 17.163 and [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]).

**The other dead.** The book's other bodies go to whoever holds the field. The Argives drag away Hippothous and Phorcys and strip them (17.317–318), and Menelaus drags the body of Podes into the crowd of his companions (17.580–581). Euphorbus' parents are left with their lament; their son had hoped to end it by bringing them Menelaus' head and armor (17.36–40; see [[lament]] and [[bereaved-parents]]).
