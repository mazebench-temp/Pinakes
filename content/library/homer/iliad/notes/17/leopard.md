summary: Menelaus names the leopard first of three fierce beasts whose might is less than the pride of the sons of Panthous: “Neither is the leopard's might so great, nor the lion's, / nor the deadly wild boar's” (17.20–23).
en: [leopard]
---

οὔτʼ οὖν παρδάλιος τόσσον μένος οὔτε λέοντος / οὔτε συὸς κάπρου ὀλοόφρονος, “Neither is the leopard's might so great, nor the lion's, / nor the deadly wild boar's” (17.20–21). The leopard stands with the lion and the boar as the beasts of greatest μένος, “might” (see [[menos]], [[lion]] and [[boar]]). Menelaus' point is a taunt: the sons of Panthous think more of themselves than any beast of prey, and he has killed one of them already (17.19–32; see [[euphorbus]] and [[hyperenor]]). Walter Leaf takes the particle οὖν here as close to an ironical ἄρα (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.20). The beast whose might Menelaus names is the one whose dappled skin he wore on the night of Book 10 (10.29–30).
