---
summary: “Great-hearted” names Ajax three times, Patroclus as Hippothous lets fall his foot, Achilles in the armor Hector wears, the fathers of Schedius, Leitus and Antilochus, and the Trojans; Menelaus speaks to his own proud heart.
---

**Ajax.** Glaucus says that Hector could not bring himself to stand “before great-hearted Ajax” (17.166; [[glaucus]]); Hippothous dies “brought down by the spear of great-hearted Ajax” (17.303), in the verse that ended the death of Simoeisius in Book 4 (4.479; [[short-lived]]); and “Nor did it escape great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus / that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle” (17.626–627; [[that-turns-the-battle]]), as the book opened, “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus” (17.1).

**Patroclus and Achilles.** Hippothous “let the foot of great-hearted Patroclus fall to the ground” (17.299; [[hippothous]]), and Hector went among the allies “flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.214; [[son-of-peleus]]).

**Fathers.** Schedius is the “son of great-hearted Iphitus” (17.306; [[schedius]], [[iphitus]]), Leitus “the son of great-hearted Alectryon” (17.602; [[leitus]], [[alectryon]]), and Antilochus “son of great-hearted Nestor” (17.653; [[nestor]]).

**The Trojans and the heart.** “And so in turn a man of the great-hearted Trojans would speak” (17.420; [[tis-speech]]). Menelaus, deeply troubled, “spoke to his own proud heart” (17.90; [[deeply-troubled]]), the verse that opens a soliloquy in the Iliad (11.403, 22.98).
