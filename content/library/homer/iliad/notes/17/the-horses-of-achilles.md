summary: Hector chases the immortal horses in vain, and Apollo tells him that no mortal but Achilles can master them. They stand apart from the battle weeping for Patroclus, as still as a pillar on a tomb, until Zeus pities them and breathes might into them. Automedon drives them back into the fight, and when Hector and Aeneas try to take them they fail (17.75–78, 17.426–542).

**Hector's chase.** When Patroclus fell, the horses carried Automedon away from Hector's spear, and Hector went after them (16.864–867). Apollo, in the likeness of Mentes, turns him back: “Hector, now you are running like this, chasing what cannot be caught, / the horses of the wise-hearted grandson of Aeacus; but they are hard / for mortal men to master and to drive, / for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (17.75–78; see [[apollo]] and [[hector]]). The last three verses are Odysseus' words to the captured Dolon, to whom Hector had sworn the same horses (10.402–404; see the note on Book 10). Walter Leaf held that they were interpolated here from Book 10, where they suit the context far better (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.76). Hector's oath was that no other Trojan would ride behind them (10.330–331). In this book Zeus decides that Hector will not ride behind them either (17.448–449).

**The weeping.**

{{quote:202afbb7-20ab-5cae-b07a-09eb5f65f48a}}

ἵπποι δʼ Αἰακίδαο μάχης ἀπάνευθεν ἐόντες / κλαῖον, ἐπεὶ δὴ πρῶτα πυθέσθην ἡνιόχοιο / ἐν κονίῃσι πεσόντος ὑφʼ Ἕκτορος ἀνδροφόνοιο, “But the horses of the grandson of Aeacus, standing apart from the battle, / were weeping, ever since they first learned that their charioteer / had fallen in the dust at the hands of man-slaying Hector” (17.426–428). The charioteer is Patroclus, who rode the chariot into battle with Automedon at the reins and leapt down from it to fight Hector (16.733); Leaf supposes that the horses had been kept close behind him while he fought (on 17.427). The verbs are in the dual, πυθέσθην, ἠθελέτην, ἐνισκίμψαντε: the two grieve as one team. Automedon tries the whip, soothing words and threats, and they will go neither back to the ships nor into the battle (17.429–433; see [[automedon]]). They stand “as a pillar stays fixed that stands on the tomb / of a man who has died, or of a woman” (17.434–435; see [[like-a-pillar]]), οὔδει ἐνισκίμψαντε καρήατα, “bowing their heads to the ground”, a strong and picturesque phrase in Leaf's judgment (on 17.437). Their tears are the hot tears of men, δάκρυα … θερμά, shed ἡνιόχοιο πόθῳ, “in longing for their charioteer” (17.437–439; see [[hot-tears]] and [[tears]]). Their manes, falling from under the yoke-cushion on both sides of the yoke, are soiled in the dust (17.439–440). The verse of the manes returns when Xanthus bows his head to speak to Achilles (19.406; Leaf on 17.440), and at the funeral games Achilles says that the pair stand mourning the charioteer who used to wash their manes and pour oil on them, with their manes resting on the ground (23.280–284).

**Zeus' pity.** Zeus sees them and pities them, shakes his head, and speaks to his own heart, as he spoke over Hector arming in the armor of Achilles (17.441–442, 17.200; see [[zeus]] and [[pity]]): “Ah, wretched pair, why did we give you to lord Peleus, / a mortal, while you are ageless and immortal? / Was it so that you should have sorrows among unhappy men?” (17.443–445). ἀγήρω τʼ ἀθανάτω, “ageless and immortal”, is in the dual, the pair's condition and the gods'; Sarpedon used the same words, in the same number, for what he and Glaucus could never be (12.322–323; Leaf on 17.444; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The gods' gift to Peleus at his wedding has brought immortal creatures into mortal grief (see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]), and Zeus gives the reason: “For there is nothing, I think, more miserable than man / of all the things that breathe and creep upon the earth” (17.446–447; see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]).

{{quote:c3336a80-0a3d-58d3-a262-2ba6a8bb956f}}

Then the promise. Hector will not ride behind them; it is enough that he has the armor and boasts of it in vain (17.448–450; see the translation's note on 17.448–449). Zeus will put might into their knees and hearts to bring Automedon safe to the ships, for he will still give the Trojans glory to kill until the sun goes down (17.451–455, nearly the words of 11.193–194; see the translation's note on 17.455). “So saying, he breathed noble might into the horses. / And the two shook the dust from their manes to the ground, / and lightly bore the swift chariot among the Trojans and the Achaeans” (17.456–458).

**Automedon, Alcimedon and Hector.** Automedon fights from the chariot, swooping with the horses “like a vulture after geese”, but alone he cannot both drive and strike, and he kills no one (17.459–465; see [[like-vultures]]). Alcimedon, son of Laerces, rebukes him and takes the whip and the reins. Automedon's answer is a lament for Patroclus as a horseman: no other of the Achaeans is Alcimedon's equal “at keeping in hand the taming and the might of immortal horses, / except Patroclus, a counselor equal to the gods, / while he lived” (17.466–483; see [[alcimedon]] and [[patroclus]]). Hector sees the change and calls Aeneas: “I see the two horses of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus here, / come out into the battle with poor charioteers” (17.483–490; see [[aeneas]]). Leaf remarks that we are left to guess how Hector reconciles this hope with what Apollo told him (on 17.488). Chromius and Aretus go with them, hoping to kill the two men and drive off “the horses with high-arched necks”, “the fools” (17.494–498). Automedon prays to Zeus and tells Alcimedon to keep the horses close behind him, “breathing on my back”, for Hector will not stop until he mounts behind “the fine-maned horses of Achilles” or is killed among the foremost (17.498–506). Automedon kills Aretus, Hector's spear misses, the two Ajaxes come up, and the Trojans fall back (17.507–536; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]). Automedon strips the dead man and mounts the chariot again, bloody like a lion that has devoured a bull (17.537–542; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Zeus' word holds: the horses do not appear again in the book, and Hector never rides behind them.

Virgil gave the weeping to a mortal war-horse in the funeral procession of Pallas (*Aeneid* 11.89–90; see [[virgils-weeping-horse]]).
