---
summary: The traditional title calls Book 17 the aristeia of Menelaus, and it is an aristeia turned to defense. Menelaus has the prayers and the god's gift of strength (17.46, 17.561–573) and kills Euphorbus and Podes, but the great opponent, Hector, he gives way to rather than fight (17.91–113), and his deeds are standing over a body and carrying it out of the battle (see [[menelaus]]).
---

Parts of the pattern fall to others. Ajax, who “in looks and in deeds stood out above the rest / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (17.279–280), scatters the Trojans like a boar and kills Hippothous and Phorcys (17.281–318; see [[ajax]] and [[like-a-boar]]). Automedon has a small aristeia of his own with the immortal horses: he swoops through the throng like a vulture after geese, prays to Zeus, and kills Aretus (17.459–542; see [[like-vultures]] and [[automedon]]). On the Trojan side Hector's arming in the armor of Achilles has the shining start of an aristeia, with Ares entering into him and the armor flashing (17.192–214; see [[exchange-of-armor]]), and Zeus sets its limit at the start, great power for the day and no homecoming (17.206–208, 17.453–455; see [[poine]]). The lion similes follow Menelaus: a lion devouring a cow while dogs and herdsmen keep their distance, a bearded lion driven from a farmyard, a lion going off at dawn with grieving heart (17.61–69, 17.109–113, 17.657–667; see [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).
