summary: The cattle of Book 17 are in its similes. Menelaus stands over Patroclus as a cow stands over her first calf (17.4–6), and over the dead Euphorbus like a lion over the best cow of a herd (17.61–67); the armies pull at the body like men stretching the hide of a great bull (17.389–395); Aretus falls like an ox struck behind the horns with an axe (17.520–523); and Menelaus leaves the body like a lion kept from the fattest of the cattle (17.657–664).

**The mother.** The book's first simile is a cow: Menelaus “bestrode him, as a mother stands over her calf, / one that has borne her first, lowing plaintively, and never knew birth before” (17.4–5; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]]). The cow is not named; she is μήτηρ, “mother”, and her calf a πόρταξ, a word found only here.

**The herd and the lion.** The cow is the lion's prey in three similes of the book. A mountain lion snatches “the cow that is the best” from a grazing herd and devours her while the herdsmen keep their distance, as no Trojan dares come against Menelaus (17.61–69; see [[lion-among-cattle]]); a lion that men keep from “the fattest of the cattle” all night goes off at dawn, as Menelaus leaves the body of Patroclus (17.657–664; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]); and Automedon mounts his chariot bloody, like a lion that has devoured a bull (17.542; see [[bulls]]).

**Hide and axe.** Two similes take their picture from the work done with cattle. The armies pull the body this way and that like men stretching the hide of a great bull soaked in fat (17.389–395; see [[the-stretched-hide]]), and Aretus leaps forward and falls as an ox of the fields leaps forward and falls when a young man strikes it behind the horns with a sharp axe (17.520–523; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]] and [[of-the-fields]]). Hector and Aeneas come against Automedon with their shoulders wrapped in oxhides, “dry and tough”, their shields (17.492–493; see [[shield]]).
