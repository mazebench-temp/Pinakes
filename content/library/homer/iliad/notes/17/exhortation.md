summary: The book's exhortations are calls to stand over a body. Hector rouses the Trojans with the verse of his rallies, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (17.185), and the allies with reasons and rewards; Menelaus calls the Achaean leaders to come of themselves; Aeneas cries that it is a shame to be driven into Ilios; Ajax keeps the ring of shields closed; and Menelaus' last charge asks the Ajaxes and Meriones to remember the gentleness of Patroclus.
passages:
  - 17.220-232 | Hector urges the allies
  - 17.248-255 | Menelaus calls the leaders
---

**Hector.** Before he arms, Hector calls to the Trojans, Lycians and Dardanians “who fight hand to hand” with the verse of his rallies, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (17.184–185; 6.112, 8.174, 11.287, 15.487), and with his reason: he is going to put on the armor of Achilles (17.186–187). Armed, he goes to the leaders of the allies one by one (17.215–219; see [[trojan-allies]]), and his speech to them is the fullest of the book. He did not gather them for their numbers, but to protect the Trojans' wives and children, and for that he wears down his own people with gifts and food (17.220–226; see [[doron]]). Each man is to turn straight at the enemy and either perish or be saved, “for that is the intimate talk of war” (17.227–228; see [[oaristys]]). Whoever drags Patroclus to the Trojans and makes Ajax give way will have half the spoils, and fame as great as Hector's own (17.229–232; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[kleos]]).

**Menelaus and Aeneas.** At Ajax's word Menelaus shouts to the Achaean leaders, those “who beside the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus, / drink at the public cost” (17.248–250; see [[feast]]), and asks for no one by name: he cannot pick them out in the battle, so “let each come of himself, and be indignant in his heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.252–255; see [[nemesis]] and [[dogs]]). Swift Ajax, Idomeneus and Meriones come first, and others whose names the narrator cannot tell (17.256–261). On the other side Aeneas, rebuked by Apollo in the likeness of the herald Periphas, calls to Hector and the leaders that “this now is a shame”, to be driven into Ilios by their own cowardice when a god has said that Zeus is still their helper (17.335–341; see [[aidos]] and [[epitarrhothos]]).

**Holding the ring.** Over the body the orders are to stand, not to charge. Ajax goes among them all and lets no one draw back from the body or fight out in front of the others, “but bade them stand firm around it and fight at close quarters” (17.356–359; see [[battle-order]]). The nameless voices of both armies urge the same: the Achaeans would rather the earth gaped for them all, and the Trojans will not hang back even if all must die beside the man (17.414–423; see [[tis-speech]] and [[may-the-earth-gape]]). Athena, as Phoenix, gives Menelaus a leader's charge, “But hold firm, and urge on the whole army” (17.559; see [[katepheie]]).

**Remembering Patroclus.** The last exhortation asks for memory, not courage. Menelaus, leaving the body to look for Antilochus, tells the Ajaxes and Meriones, “now let each remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus” (17.669–672; see [[eneeie]]). The rally bids men remember their courage (μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 17.185); here μνησάσθω has the dead man for its object. Apollo's report of Euphorbus' death had used the phrase of the rally the other way, of a courage ended: Menelaus has killed him “and stopped him from his furious courage” (17.81; see [[alke]]).
