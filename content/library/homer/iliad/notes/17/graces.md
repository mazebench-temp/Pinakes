summary: When Menelaus kills Euphorbus, the young man's hair, “hair like the Graces'”, braided with gold and silver, is drenched with his blood.
---

**Hair like the Graces'.** Menelaus' spear goes through Euphorbus' neck, and he falls with a thud: “His hair was drenched with blood, hair like the Graces', / and his braids, which were bound tight with gold and silver” (17.51–52; see [[euphorbus]], [[menelaus]] and [[hair]]). Χαρίτεσσιν ὁμοῖαι is literally “like the Graces”, meaning like their hair. Walter Leaf compares the Odyssey's “thoughts like Penelope”, that is, like hers (ὁμοῖα νοήματα Πηνελοπείῃ, *Odyssey* 2.121), and notes the effect of the asyndeton, for the verse begins with no connective (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.51). He reports that Zenodotus is said to have read χαρίτεσσι μέλαιναι, and quotes a scholium according to which the Macedonians and Cypriots called χάριτες the twisted, curly myrtles used in garlands (on 17.51; see [[zenodotus]]). The Graces are the measure of beauty in Homer: they made Aphrodite's ambrosial robe (5.338), and Nausicaa's maids have their beauty from them (*Odyssey* 6.18). The beauty is that of the young man who struck Patroclus first (16.806–815), and the next simile shows it cut down, an olive sapling torn from its trench by a storm (17.53–58).
