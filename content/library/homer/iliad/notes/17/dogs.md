summary: The dogs of Book 17 are the fate that threatens the body of Patroclus. Hector drags it away to give it to “the Trojan dogs”, Menelaus calls the Achaean leaders to be indignant that it should become “a plaything for the Trojan dogs”, and Zeus will not let it be a spoil for the dogs of its enemies (17.127, 17.255, 17.272–273). In the similes dogs keep the lion from the herd and are scattered by the boar, and at the end the Trojans themselves are hounds at a wounded boar (17.725–729).

**The Trojan dogs.** Hector, having stripped Patroclus, “was dragging him, to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127; see [[hector]]). The threat is said three times in the book with the same adjective, Τρῳῇσι(ν) … κυσί(ν), “the Trojan dogs” (17.127, 17.255, 17.273; see [[trojans]]). Glaucus reproaches Hector for leaving Sarpedon to the Argives: “And now you did not dare to keep the dogs from him” (17.153; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]). Ajax fears less for the body, which “will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans”, than for his own head and Menelaus' (17.240–242; see [[birds]]). Menelaus calls on the leaders to “be indignant in his heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.254–255). μέλπηθρα, “plaything”, is a thing to make sport of; Poseidon wished the same end on any Achaean who held back from the fight, to “become a plaything for dogs” (13.233), and Iris will repeat the verse to Achilles (18.179). Zeus, who had not hated Patroclus while he lived, “hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, / the Trojan dogs” (17.272–273; see [[zeus]]), and Athena, as Phoenix, tells Menelaus that it will be a shame to him if “swift dogs” tear Achilles' trusted companion under the wall of Troy (17.556–558; see [[swift-dogs]]). The fear is the one named in the first verses of the poem, where the wrath of Achilles made the men themselves “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5). The body is saved: Menelaus and Meriones carry it out of the fight (17.715–761).

**In the similes.** Dogs and herdsmen shout at a lion from far off and dare not come near it (17.65–67; see [[lion-among-cattle]]), dogs and men drive the lion from the steading (17.110, 17.658; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]), and the boar scatters the dogs and the young men in the mountains (17.282; see [[like-a-boar]]). At the end of the book the likeness is turned on the Trojans: they charge the men carrying the body “like hounds that at a wild boar / that has been hit dart ahead of the young hunters”, and scatter when it turns on them (17.725–729; see [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]]). The men who wanted to give Patroclus to their dogs have become the dogs.
