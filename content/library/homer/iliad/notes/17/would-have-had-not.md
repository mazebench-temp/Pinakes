summary: Four times the narrator stops the fight over the body at the edge of another outcome. Menelaus would easily have carried off Euphorbus' armor, had not Apollo begrudged it (17.70–71); the Trojans would have been driven into Ilios and the Argives would have won glory “even beyond the portion of Zeus”, but Apollo roused Aeneas (17.319–323); Automedon and Hector would have fought with swords, had not the two Ajaxes parted them (17.530–531); and Idomeneus, on foot, would have given the Trojans great power, had not his charioteer Coeranus driven up and died in his place (17.612–616).
grc: ["ἔνθά κε ῥεῖα φέροι", "καί κε Τρωσὶ μέγα κράτος"]
en: [would easily have carried off]
---

**Apollo.** Two of the four turns are Apollo's, and both keep the Trojans in the fight. The second repeats two verses of Book 6, where the Trojans would have gone up into Ilios beaten by their own cowardice, had not Helenus spoken to Aeneas and Hector (6.73–76; 17.319–320). Here the next verses go further: the Argives would have won glory “even beyond the portion of Zeus / by their own power and strength” (17.321–322; see [[aisa]] and [[kydos]]), and in place of εἰ μή comes ἀλλʼ αὐτὸς Ἀπόλλων, “but Apollo himself” (17.322). The outcome prevented would have run against Zeus' plan, as in Book 2 the Argives would have had their homecoming “against fate” (ὑπέρμορα, 2.155; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Apollo, in the likeness of the herald Periphas, tells Aeneas that Zeus wills victory for the Trojans (17.323–332; see [[divine-epiphany]]); the translation's note on 17.327 gives Walter Leaf's view that ὑπὲρ θεόν, “in defiance of a god”, is the same thought as “beyond the portion of Zeus”.

**Men.** The other two are turned back by men. The Ajaxes, called by Automedon, part him from Hector and Aeneas before they can fight hand to hand with swords (17.530–534), and Coeranus, Meriones' charioteer, drives the horses up to Idomeneus, who had come on foot: “he came as a light to him and kept off the pitiless day, / but he himself lost his life at the hands of man-slaying Hector” (17.615–616). The words of Idomeneus' danger, κράτος ἐγγυάλιξεν, “he would have put great power in the Trojans' hands” (17.613), are the words of Zeus' promise to Hector, “now at least I will put great power in your hands” (17.206; see [[kratos]]).
