summary: The narrator speaks to Menelaus twice as he searches for Antilochus and goes back to the body, the fifth and sixth of his seven addresses to him (17.679, 17.702). Twice he turns to the listener: in the mist over the body “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–367), and not Ares or Athena, seeing the fight, would have made light of it (17.398–399).

**Menelaus.** The first address closes the simile of the eagle, which, they say, has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds and sees the hare crouching under a leafy bush: “So then, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, your shining eyes / roved everywhere through the crowd of your many companions, / to see if somewhere they might find the son of Nestor still alive” (17.674–681; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]] and [[eyes]]). The second comes when he has sent Antilochus off with the news: “Nor did your heart, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, wish / to help the hard-pressed companions from whom he had gone” (17.702–703); he leaves the Pylians to Thrasymedes and goes back to stand over Patroclus (17.704–706; see [[thrasymedes]]). Both come while he is away from the body on Ajax's errand, looking for a messenger to Achilles (17.651–706; see [[messenger-scene]]). In Book 16 the narrator spoke to Patroclus on the day of his death; here he speaks to the man who stands over his body.

**The listener.** The address to the listener comes with the mist: the best men fought around the body “like blazing fire; and you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon; / for they were covered in mist” (17.366–368; see [[war-and-night]]). The imagined spectators of the tug-of-war over the body are gods: “not Ares, who rouses armies, nor Athena, / would have made light of it when they saw it, not even if great anger came upon them” (17.398–399), as of the battalions around the two Ajaxes at the ships (13.127–128; see [[watching-the-war]]).
