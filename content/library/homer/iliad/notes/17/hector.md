summary: Apollo, in the likeness of Mentes, turns Hector from the horses of Achilles against Menelaus; Hector strips Patroclus and drags the body to cut off the head, but gives way before Ajax. Rebuked by Glaucus, he puts on the immortal armor of Achilles, and Zeus, who foresees his death, fits it to him and gives him great power for a time. He leads the allies against the body, goes after Achilles' horses with Aeneas, and, taunted by Apollo as Phaenops and backed by Zeus' thunder, routs the Achaeans and presses the men who carry Patroclus back to the ditch.

**Apollo sends him.** At the end of Book 16 Hector went after Automedon with his spear, but the immortal horses carried Automedon away (16.864–867; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). He is still chasing them when Apollo comes to him “in the likeness of a man, Mentes, the leader of the Cicones” (17.73; see [[mentes]] and [[apollo]]): “Hector, now you are running like this, chasing what cannot be caught” (17.75). The horses obey no one but Achilles, and meanwhile Menelaus has killed Euphorbus, “the best of the Trojans” (17.76–81; see [[euphorbus]] and [[menelaus]]). “And dread grief clouded Hector's heart, dark on every side” (17.83; see [[achos]]). He sees Menelaus taking the armor and the dead man on the ground, and comes through the champions “shouting shrilly, like the flame of Hephaestus, / unquenchable” (17.84–89; see [[like-a-flame]]). Menelaus does not wait for him. No Danaan will blame him “if I give way before Hector, since he fights with help from the gods” (17.101), for “When a man is willing, against a god's will, to fight with a man / whom a god honors, soon a great bane rolls down on him” (17.98–99). He gives ground, step by step, and goes to look for Ajax (17.106–113).

**The body.**

{{quote:6a794336-df3e-505a-b260-f600bef631ab}}

In Book 16 Zeus weighed whether Hector should kill Patroclus over Sarpedon “and take the armor from his shoulders” (16.650; see [[zeus]]), and Hector boasted over the dying man that “here the vultures will eat you” (16.836; see [[vultures]]). Now he has the armor (“for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor”, Menelaus tells Ajax, 17.122, and Antilochus, 17.693), and he means to cut off the head and give the corpse to the Trojan dogs (see [[trojans]] and [[dogs]]); ancient defenders of Achilles' treatment of Hector's body cited this purpose (see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Iris will tell Achilles that Hector is the most eager of all to drag the body away, and that his heart bids him cut the head from the tender neck and fix it on the stakes (18.175–177). Ajax comes near “carrying his shield like a tower” (17.128; see [[ajax]]), and Hector draws back into the throng of his companions, springs into his chariot and gives the armor to the Trojans to carry to the city, “to be great fame for him” (17.129–131; see [[kleos]]).

**Glaucus' rebuke.** Glaucus, “looking at Hector darkly” (17.141; see [[looking-darkly]]), calls him a runaway, φύξηλις (17.143), and accuses him of leaving Sarpedon, his guest-friend and companion, to the Argives, and of not daring to stand against Ajax (17.142–168; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]). His first words, “Hector, best in looks” (17.142), are those with which Hector himself began his rebukes of Paris, “Evil Paris, best in looks” (3.39 = 13.769; see [[best-in-looks]] and [[paris]]). Hector answers, “Looking at him darkly” (17.169), in the verse with which Odysseus scorned Agamemnon's counsel, “but now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said” (17.173 = 14.95; see [[odysseus]]): he does not shudder at battle or at the din of horses, “but the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger” (17.175–176; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The narrator said the same three verses of Patroclus when he forgot Achilles' warning (17.176–178 ≈ 16.688–690; see [[patroclus]]). Then he bids Glaucus stand beside him and watch “whether I shall be a coward all day long, as you say” (17.179–182).

**The armor of Achilles.** He calls to the “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand” (17.184), the rallying cry of earlier books (8.173, 11.286, 13.150, 15.425, 15.486; see [[lycians]] and [[dardanians]]), to hold “while I put on the armor of noble Achilles, / the fine armor I stripped from mighty Patroclus when I killed him” (17.186–187). He runs after the men who are carrying it to the city, changes his armor apart from the battle, and gives his own to the Trojans to take to Ilios (17.188–194; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). It is “the immortal armor / of Achilles, son of Peleus, which the heavenly gods / gave to his dear father” (17.194–196; see [[peleus]]), and the narrator adds that “the son did not grow old in his father's armor” (17.197; see [[achilles]]). Zeus sees him:

{{quote:534e6117-e327-53d3-a9f1-c90b924824f2}}

ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch” (17.201; see [[wretch]]), are the words with which Hector mocked the dying Patroclus (16.837), and “which is now close to you” (17.202) answers Patroclus' last words to him, that “already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (16.852–853; see [[mighty-fate]] and [[prophecy]]). Hector will die in this armor: when Achilles looks for a place to strike, the bronze covers all his body, and the narrator names it in Hector's own words here, the fine armor he had stripped from mighty Patroclus when he killed him (22.322–323 ≈ 17.186–187). The recompense (ποινή, 17.207; see [[poine]]) is for a homecoming that will not come: Andromache will never take the armor of the son of Peleus from his hands (17.207–208; see [[andromache]] and [[nostos]]). Zeus fits the armor to Hector's body, “and Ares entered into him, / dread Enyalius, and his limbs within were filled / with courage and strength” (17.210–212; see [[ares]], [[enyalius]] and the translation's note on 17.210–211). Walter Leaf reports the scholia's remark that the divine armor seems to fit Peleus, Achilles, Patroclus and Hector alike, and suggests that it had no corselet to fit (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.210). He goes among the allies “with a great cry; and he appeared to them all / flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.213–214).

**The allies.** He goes to each of the leaders of the allies, ten of them named in three verses (17.215–218; see [[trojan-allies]]), and tells them that he did not gather them from their cities “in search of numbers”, but so that they might protect the wives and little children of the Trojans (17.221–224). For this he wears down his own people with gifts and food (17.225–226), and this is the moment to perish or be saved, “for that is the intimate talk of war” (17.228; see [[oaristys]]). Whoever drags Patroclus among the Trojans and makes Ajax give way shall have half of the spoils, “and his fame will be as great as mine” (17.229–232; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[kleos]]). The narrator calls the men who charge with such hopes “the fools” (νήπιοι, 17.236; see [[nepios]]).

**In the fight.** “And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them” (17.262; = 13.136, 15.306). When Ajax kills Hippothous, Hector throws at him and kills Schedius, the leader of the Phocians (17.304–311; see [[schedius]] and [[hippothous]]); when Ajax kills Phorcys, “The champions and shining Hector gave ground” (17.316; = 4.505, 16.588; see [[phorcys]]). Apollo, as the herald Periphas, rouses Aeneas, and Aeneas shouts to “Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and the allies” that it is a shame to be driven back into Ilios (17.334–341; see [[aeneas]] and [[periphas-son-of-epytus]]).

**The horses.** Zeus will not let Hector have the horses of Achilles: “not behind you … / will Hector, son of Priam, ride” (17.448–449; see [[son-of-priam]]), and “Is it not enough that he has the armor too and boasts of it for nothing?” (17.450). ἐποχήσεται, “ride”, is the verb of Hector's oath to Dolon, “that no other man shall ride behind those horses” (10.330; see [[dolon]]). When Alcimedon takes the reins and Automedon gets down to fight, Hector sees the two horses “come out into the battle with poor charioteers” (17.487) and asks Aeneas to go after them with him (17.483–490); Chromius and Aretus go too (17.494; see [[chromis]] and [[aretus]]). Automedon foresees that Hector will not hold back “before he mounts behind the fine-maned horses of Achilles / when he has killed us both” (17.504–505). Automedon kills Aretus; Hector's spear misses Automedon in the verses of Aeneas' throw at Meriones (17.525–529 ≈ 16.610–613), and the two Ajaxes part them before they can close with swords (17.530–532; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). Hector, Aeneas and Chromius draw back and leave Aretus lying (17.533–536).

**Phaenops.** For the third time Apollo comes to Hector in a man's likeness: as his uncle Asius at the Scaean gates (16.715–725; see [[asius-son-of-dymas]]), as Mentes (17.73), and now as “Phaenops, son of Asius, who of all / his guest-friends was dearest to him, and had his home at Abydos” (17.583–584; see [[phaenops-son-of-asius]], [[abydos]] and [[xenia]]). The god shames him:

{{quote:6b49c58d-2fef-5301-9d1b-104baeb33e79}}

Podes, son of Eetion, was “rich and brave”, and “Hector honored him most / of all the people, since he was his dear companion at the feast” (17.575–577; see [[podes]]). “So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him” (17.591): the verse returns when Achilles learns that Patroclus is dead (18.22). Then Zeus takes up the aegis on Ida, thunders, “and gave victory to the Trojans, and put the Achaeans to flight” (17.593–596; see [[aegis]] and [[ida]]).

**The rout.** Hector stabs Leitus, son of Alectryon, on the wrist, “and stopped him from battle” (17.601–604; see [[leitus]]). Idomeneus hits him on the corselet, but the spear snaps at the socket (17.605–607; see [[idomeneus]]). Hector's answering throw misses Idomeneus and kills Coeranus of Lyctus, Meriones' charioteer, who had driven up the horses to save Idomeneus, “but he himself lost his life at the hands of man-slaying Hector” (17.608–619; see [[coeranus-of-lyctus]] and [[man-slaying]]). Ajax sees that Zeus is helping the Trojans, and that the Achaeans at the ships believe “the might and the irresistible hands of man-slaying Hector” will no longer be held back (17.637–639; see [[irresistible-hands]]).

**The retreat.** Menelaus, who has sent Antilochus with the news, does not think that Achilles will come “now, however angry he is with brilliant Hector; / for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans” (17.710–711; see [[achilles]] and [[antilochus]]). The two Ajaxes will hold off “the Trojans and brilliant Hector” while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body (17.717–721). At the end of the book “Aeneas, son of Anchises, and shining Hector” press hardest on them, and the young men of the Achaeans flee before them like starlings or jackdaws before a falcon, “and forgot the joy of battle” (17.753–759; see [[aeneas]] and [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]).

His epithets in the book are κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (17.96, 17.122, 17.169, 17.188, 17.693), φαίδιμος, “shining” (17.316, 17.483, 17.754), ἀνδροφόνος, “man-slaying” (17.428, 17.616, 17.638), and δῖος, “brilliant” (17.710, 17.719); he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (17.449, 17.503), and θοῷ ἀτάλαντος Ἄρηϊ, “the equal of swift Ares” (17.72; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]], [[phaidimos]], [[brilliant]] and [[equal-of-ares]]).
