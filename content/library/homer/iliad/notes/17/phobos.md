---
summary: Rout is given by the gods. Apollo casts “a heaven-sent rout” among Ajax's men (17.118); Zeus, says Hector, “puts even a valiant man to flight” (17.177); and when Zeus shakes the aegis and thunders from Ida, he “put the Achaeans to flight”, and Peneleos the Boeotian “was the first to begin the rout” (17.593–597). Euphorbus had named the stakes at the start: the fight would end “in courage or in rout” (17.42).
---

The rout of the end of the book is told in its last simile: the young men of the Achaeans flee before Aeneas and Hector like starlings or jackdaws before a falcon, “screaming a cry of death”, and forget the joy of battle (17.755–759; see [[charme]]). Menelaus' fear for Patroclus' body is the fear of such a rout, that the Achaeans “in grievous rout would leave him as a prey to the enemy” (17.666–667).
