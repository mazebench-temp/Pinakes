---
summary: The Works and Days has Menelaus' proverb in another form, “the fool learns by suffering”, and two half-verses that Homer uses only in Book 17: the houses that dwindle, in Hesiod's unjust city struck by Zeus and in Homer's city on fire, and the glory that attends, on wealth in Hesiod and on kings by the gift of Zeus in Homer. A fragment of the Astronomy calls the Hyades “nymphs like the Graces”, the words for Euphorbus' hair.
---

- **The fool learns.** Menelaus ends his warning to Euphorbus with the proverb ῥεχθὲν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (17.32 = 20.198). Hesiod ends a warning to his brother Perses with παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “the fool learns it by suffering”: Justice wins over Outrage when she comes to the end (*Works and Days* 217–218). See [[a-fool-learns-once-it-is-done]].
- **The houses dwindle.** In the simile of the fire that rushes on a city of men, μινύθουσι δὲ οἶκοι, “the houses dwindle / in the great blaze” (17.738–739; see [[the-burning-city]]). Homer has the half-verse nowhere else. Hesiod has it in his picture of the unjust city, on which Zeus brings famine and plague together: the people waste away, οὐδὲ γυναῖκες τίκτουσιν, μινύθουσι δὲ οἶκοι, “the women do not bear, and the houses dwindle”, by the counsels of Olympian Zeus (*Works and Days* 242–245). A few lines later the gods dwindle the house of the man who wins wealth by force or by lying (μινύθουσι δὲ οἶκον, 325). Homer's houses go down in fire; Hesiod's are emptied by the anger of the gods.
- **Glory attends.** The leaders whom Menelaus calls to the body drink at the public cost beside the sons of Atreus, and each gives orders to his people, ἐκ δὲ Διὸς τιμὴ καὶ κῦδος ὀπηδεῖ, “and from Zeus come honor and glory” (17.249–251). Homer has the verse-end only here. In the *Works and Days* it is wealth that excellence and glory attend, πλούτῳ δʼ ἀρετὴ καὶ κῦδος ὀπηδεῖ (313), in the praise of work. See [[kydos]] and [[time]].
- **Like the Graces.** Euphorbus' hair, drenched with his blood, is κόμαι Χαρίτεσσιν ὁμοῖαι, “hair like the Graces'” (17.51); the phrase is found only here in Homer. A fragment of the *Astronomy* handed down under Hesiod's name, quoted in a scholium on Aratus, calls the Hyades Νύμφαι Χαρίτεσσιν ὁμοῖαι, “nymphs like the Graces”, and names them Phaesyle, Coronis, Cleeia, Phaeo and Eudora (*Astronomy* fr. 2 Evelyn-White). See [[graces]] and [[euphorbus]].
