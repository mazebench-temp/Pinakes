summary: Hippothous, the Pelasgian leader, is named among the allies whom Hector urges on (17.217). He ties a strap around the ankle of Patroclus' body and drags it by the foot to please Hector and the Trojans, until Ajax drives his spear through his helmet; he falls face down on the body, far from Larisa. Phorcys, standing over him, dies next, and the Achaeans strip them both.

**The strap.** He is the seventh of the ten leaders of the allies whom Hector, in Achilles' armor, urges on by name, and Hector has promised half the spoils and equal fame to the man who drags Patroclus among the Trojans (17.217, 17.229–232; see [[hector]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Hippothous tries it, “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans” (χαριζόμενος, 17.291; see [[charis]]). ποδὸς ἕλκε, “was dragging him by the foot”, is the half-verse in which Idomeneus dragged off the dead Othryoneus (17.289 ≈ 13.383; see [[othryoneus]]). He has bound the body with a τελαμών, a strap such as a shield or a sword hangs from (17.290), and it is “the son of Telamon”, υἱὸς Τελαμῶνος, who rushes at him (17.293; see [[telamon]] and [[ajax]]).

**His death.** Ajax strikes him at close quarters through the bronze-cheeked, horsehair-crested helmet, and the brain spurts from the wound “along the spear's socket” (17.293–298; see the translation's note on 17.297). He lets Patroclus' foot fall to the ground, “and he fell face down beside him on the body” (17.299–300), as the Myrmidon Epeigeus fell on the body of Sarpedon (16.579; see [[epeigeus]]). The narrator closes his life in the verses of Simoeisius, the first man Ajax killed in the poem, a young man born by the Simoeis who did not repay his parents for his rearing (4.477–479; see [[simoeisius]]), but begins them with the distance of his home:

{{quote:c52a9d41-a9ae-5b85-adae-0df1d2404b99}}

Larisa is the town of the Pelasgians in the Catalogue (2.840–841; see [[larisa]] and [[pelasgians]]). Phorcys stands over him and is killed in turn, “and the Argives shouted aloud, and dragged away the dead, / Phorcys and Hippothous, and loosed the armor from their shoulders” (17.317–318; see [[phorcys]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). His brother Pylaeus, who led the Pelasgians with him (2.842–843; see [[pylaeus]]), is not named again.
