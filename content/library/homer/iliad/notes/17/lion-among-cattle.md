**Menelaus over Euphorbus.**

{{quote:b3c9f809-8445-52f4-b47b-6418f1d99b2f}}

Euphorbus has fallen like an olive tree torn up by a storm (17.53–60; see [[the-olive-sapling]]). Then: ὡς δʼ ὅτε τίς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος ἀλκὶ πεποιθὼς / βοσκομένης ἀγέλης βοῦν ἁρπάσῃ ἥ τις ἀρίστη, “And as when a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage, / snatches from a grazing herd the cow that is the best” (17.61–62). ὀρεσίτροφος, “mountain-bred” (see [[mountain-bred]]), and ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in his courage”, are said together of the lion to which Odysseus is likened when he comes out of the bushes before Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 6.130). The next two verses are those of the lion that routs the cattle at night in Book 11 (17.63–64 = 11.175–176; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.63): “he seizes her in his strong teeth and breaks her neck / first, and then gulps down her blood and all her entrails” (17.63–64).

δῃῶν· ἀμφὶ δὲ τόν γε κύνες τʼ ἄνδρές τε νομῆες / πολλὰ μάλʼ ἰύζουσιν ἀπόπροθεν οὐδʼ ἐθέλουσιν / ἀντίον ἐλθέμεναι· μάλα γὰρ χλωρὸν δέος αἱρεῖ, “mauling her; and around him the dogs and the herdsmen / raise a great clamor from far off, but they are not willing / to come against him; for pale fear takes hold of them” (17.64–67). ἰύζω is to shout, as men shout after the eagle that carries off a goose in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 15.162); Leaf notes that it fits the men, and that the dogs are joined to it only loosely and then forgotten (on 17.66). χλωρὸν δέος, “pale fear”, is the fear that seizes men at a portent of Zeus (7.479, 8.77; see [[pale-fear]]).

The tenor: ὣς τῶν οὔ τινι θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἐτόλμα / ἀντίον ἐλθέμεναι Μενελάου κυδαλίμοιο, “so no one of them had the heart in his breast to dare / to come against glorious Menelaus” (17.68–69). The lion stands over its kill as Menelaus stands over the dead Euphorbus and begins to strip his armor (17.60), and the Trojans keep their distance as the herdsmen do; ἀντίον ἐλθέμεναι, “to come against”, is said of both (17.67, 17.69). The lion's moment does not last. Apollo sends Hector against him, and Menelaus gives ground like a lion driven from a farmyard by dogs and men (17.70–113; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]] and [[menelaus]]).

**Automedon over Aretus.**

{{quote:db9544e0-b01a-5527-af4b-b802c0a36ab3}}

Automedon has killed Aretus and stripped him, boasting that he has eased his heart a little for Patroclus, though the man he has killed is a lesser one (17.535–539; see [[automedon]]). ὣς εἰπὼν ἐς δίφρον ἑλὼν ἔναρα βροτόεντα / θῆκʼ, ἂν δʼ αὐτὸς ἔβαινε πόδας καὶ χεῖρας ὕπερθεν / αἱματόεις ὥς τίς τε λέων κατὰ ταῦρον ἐδηδώς, “So saying, he took the bloody spoils and put them in the chariot, / and mounted it himself, his feet and his hands above / bloody, like a lion that has devoured a bull” (17.540–542). The point of likeness is the blood. In the Odyssey the old nurse finds Odysseus among the dead suitors spattered with blood and gore like a lion that comes from devouring an ox of the fields, and he too is bloody πόδας καὶ χεῖρας ὕπερθεν, “his feet and his hands above” (*Odyssey* 22.401–406). The death of Aretus is told with two likenesses from the herd: he falls like an ox struck behind the horns with an axe (17.520–523; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]), and his killer leaves him like a lion from a bull.
