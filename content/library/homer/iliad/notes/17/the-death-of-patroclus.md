summary: Book 17 is the aftermath of the death. Euphorbus, Hector and Zeus each tell the killing in his own way; the body is fought over all day; and the news moves slowly through the battle, to the horses, to Ajax and to Antilochus, who runs weeping to Achilles, while Achilles himself does not know (17.401–411).

**Told again.** No one in Book 17 tells the killing as the narrator told it in Book 16. Euphorbus claims the first blow: “none of the Trojans or their famous allies before me / hit Patroclus with the spear in the fierce battle” (17.14–15; see [[euphorbus]]). It is true of the spear, for Apollo struck Patroclus' back and shoulders “with the flat of his hand” (16.791–792). Hector claims the killing, “the fine armor I stripped from mighty Patroclus when I killed him” (17.187), a verse the narrator repeats of him in Book 22 (22.323; see [[hector]]). Zeus says the same: “you have killed his companion, gentle and strong, / and the armor, against all order, from his head and shoulders / you took” (17.204–206), though in Book 16 it was Apollo who struck the helmet from Patroclus' head and loosened his corselet (16.793–804; see the translation's note on 17.205). The horses weep “ever since they first learned that their charioteer / had fallen in the dust at the hands of man-slaying Hector” (17.427–428; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Apollo's part is named only by the dying Patroclus (16.844–850), by Thetis in Book 18 (18.454–456), and in Book 19 by the horse Xanthus, who tells Achilles that it was not their slowness that let the Trojans take the armor from Patroclus' shoulders, but the son of Leto, who killed him and gave Hector the glory (19.411–414; 19.414 = 18.456). Walter Leaf takes 17.125 to show that Patroclus had not already been stripped of the armor, as the lines at the end of Book 16, which he suspected, relate (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.125).

**The body and the armor.** Hector has the armor and is dragging the body away when Ajax comes up (17.125–131), and the fight for the body fills the rest of the book (see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). The armor passes into the story of its own: Hector puts it on, and Zeus, watching, foresees Hector's death (17.183–214; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). γυμνός, “naked”, is said both of the stripped body, “naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (17.122 = 17.693), and of Achilles, who has no armor to fight in: “for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans” (17.711). Walter Leaf thought the verse interpolated at 17.122, where Menelaus says that Hector has the armor before the narrator has told that he took it (on 17.122).

**Who knows.** The book is also the story of the news. Menelaus is the first to see that Patroclus has fallen (17.1–2), and the horses learn it and weep (17.426–428). Thrasymedes and Antilochus, fighting apart on Nestor's orders, have not yet learned it (17.377–383; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[nestor]]); Leaf takes the lines as preparing for Antilochus' errand (on 17.377). Achilles does not know, and the narrator says why:

His mother often told him the purpose of Zeus, but not this (see [[thetis]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]), and the death that Zeus foretold to Hera in Books 8 and 15 comes to Achilles as news (see [[plan-of-zeus]]). In Book 18 he will remember a prophecy of hers that the best of the Myrmidons would die while he still lived (18.9–11; see the translation's note on 17.408–411). Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus did not write 17.404–425 at all, and that 17.411 is marked with an obelus in one manuscript, which may mean that Aristarchus rejected it (on 17.404 and 17.411; see [[zenodotus]] and [[aristarchus]]). Leaf himself found the passage “a painfully conscientious endeavour to explain just so much of the situation as is already quite clear” (on 17.404).

**The messenger.** Ajax wishes that some companion would take the news to Achilles, “since I do not think he has even heard / the grievous news, that his dear companion has perished” (17.640–642), but the mist hides everyone, and his prayer for light is also a prayer to find a messenger (17.643–655; see [[ajax]]). Menelaus finds Antilochus, the son of Nestor, whom Leaf takes to be chosen as a friend of Achilles and a good runner (on 17.653, citing *Odyssey* 4.202; compare 23.555–556), and tells him that “the best of the Achaeans has been slain, / Patroclus, and great longing for him has come upon the Danaans” (17.689–690; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Antilochus is struck speechless, his eyes fill with tears, and his voice is checked (17.694–696), in the two verses with which Penelope hears that Telemachus has sailed for Pylos and that the suitors mean to kill him (*Odyssey* 4.704–705; see [[tears]]). He gives his armor to Laodocus and runs from the war in tears, “to bring the evil news to Achilles, son of Peleus” (17.697–701; see [[antilochus]] and [[laodocus-companion-of-antilochus]]). λυγρῆς ἀγγελίης, “the grievous news”, is Ajax's phrase and Menelaus' (17.642, 17.686), and Antilochus brings it in the same words in Book 18: Patroclus lies dead, they are fighting over the naked corpse, and Hector has the armor (18.18–21).
