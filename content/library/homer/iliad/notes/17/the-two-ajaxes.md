summary: The two Ajaxes come at Automedon's call and part him from Hector and Aeneas. At the end of the book Telamonian Ajax calls the pair ὁμώνυμοι, “of one name”, and they hold back the Trojans like a wooded ridge holding back floodwater while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body of Patroclus toward the ships (17.507–535, 17.668–672, 17.707–761).
# The dual and plural (Αἴαντε, Αἴαντʼ, Αἰάντεσσι) are claimed by ajax; the pair takes its lines by refs.
refs: [17.507-515, 17.531-533, 17.668-672, 17.707-714, 17.719-721, 17.732-734, 17.747-752]
passages:

**At Automedon's call.** When Hector and Aeneas come to take Achilles' horses, Automedon “called to the two Ajaxes and Menelaus” (17.507; see [[automedon]] and [[menelaus]]). He greets the pair with the first half of Agamemnon's verse in the review, “Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives” (17.508; 4.285, 12.354), and asks them to leave the body to the best men and keep off the pitiless day from him and his driver (17.509–511). The pair come, and the narrator gives them the turn of the fight: “And now they would have rushed at each other hand to hand with swords, / had not the two Ajaxes parted them, eager as they were, / who came through the throng at their companion's call” (17.530–532; see [[would-have-had-not]]). Hector, Aeneas and Chromius draw back in fear of them (17.533–534).

**Of one name.** Menelaus charges the pair and Meriones in the same address, “Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Meriones”, to remember the gentleness of Patroclus (17.668–672; see [[meriones]] and [[patroclus]]), and when he has sent Antilochus to Achilles he comes back to them (17.707–714). Telamonian Ajax answers him and gives the pair its part:

{{quote:897a6ac1-3d1e-5d40-9d5a-54ef283855f9}}

ὁμώνυμοι, “of one name”, occurs only here in Homer; the dual Αἴαντε names them together without telling them apart (see [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). ἶσον θυμὸν ἔχοντες, “with equal spirit”, was said of them as two oxen yoked to one plow, where they stood side by side against Hector at the ships (13.704; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]). Ajax's “we who even before now / have withstood sharp Ares, standing side by side” (17.720–721) can recall the towers and the ships, where the poem showed them so (12.265–277, 13.701–708).

**The rearguard.** The Trojans charge like hounds that dart ahead of the young hunters at a wounded boar, until the boar turns on them (17.725–729; see [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]]), “but whenever the two Ajaxes turned round / and stood against them, their color changed, and no one dared / to dart forward and fight for the body” (17.732–734; see [[dogs]]). Menelaus and Meriones carry the corpse, “And behind them / the two Ajaxes held the enemy back, as a ridge holds back water” (17.746–747), a wooded ridge running across the plain that turns aside the streams of mighty rivers and is not broken by them (17.748–751; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]): “so the two Ajaxes kept holding back the battle / of the Trojans” (17.752–753). Hector and Aeneas press on, and the young men of the Achaeans flee before them (17.753–761). In the next book the pair drive Hector from the body three times, and cannot frighten him away, until Achilles shows himself at the ditch (18.155–164).
