summary: The birds of Book 17 are the eaters of the dead and the creatures of its similes. Ajax fears that the body of Patroclus will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans (17.241); the eagle has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven (17.674–675); and the falcon brings slaughter to small birds, the starlings and jackdaws to which the fleeing Achaeans are likened (17.755–757).
grc: [ὀρνίθεσσιν]
en: [birds]
---

**The eaters of the dead.** Ajax does not fear so much for the corpse of Patroclus, “which will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans”, as for his own head and Menelaus' (17.240–242; see [[dogs]]). οἰωνοί are the large birds that feed on the unburied, as in the first verses of the poem (1.4–5).

**The birds of the similes.** Automedon swoops through the battle with Achilles' horses like a vulture after geese (17.460; see [[like-vultures]], [[vultures]] and [[geese]]). Menelaus looks for Antilochus like the eagle, which men say “has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven” and which sees the hare from on high (17.674–678; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]] and [[eagle]]). At the end the young Achaeans flee before Aeneas and Hector like a cloud of starlings or jackdaws screaming at the sight of a falcon, “which brings slaughter to small birds” (17.755–757; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]], [[starlings]], [[jackdaws]] and [[falcon]]). In Book 16 the Achaeans had the hawk: Patroclus scattered the Trojans like a hawk that puts jackdaws and starlings to flight (16.582–583; see [[like-a-hawk]]).
