{{quote:c0cc6a7b-9c72-576e-9e3f-2a0504ab09a1}}

τοῖσι δʼ ἐπʼ Αὐτομέδων μάχετʼ ἀχνύμενός περ ἑταίρου / ἵπποις ἀΐσσων ὥς τʼ αἰγυπιὸς μετὰ χῆνας, “And behind them Automedon fought, grieving though he was for his companion, / swooping with his horses like a vulture after geese” (17.459–460). ἵπποις is “with his horses”, charging together with them (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.460). The swoop is the horses' speed: Zeus has just breathed might into the immortal pair, and they have shaken the dust from their manes and carried the chariot back into the battle (17.456–458; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The αἰγυπιός of the similes is a bird of prey that comes down on smaller birds (see [[vultures]]), and its quarry here is a flock of geese, the quarry of the tawny eagle that Hector was likened to in Book 15 (15.690–692; see [[geese]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]). The next verses give the swoops again and again, with verbs of repeated action: “for easily he would escape out of the din of the Trojans, / and easily he would charge, pursuing through the great throng” (17.461–462).

But this vulture does not kill: “But he killed no men whenever he rushed in pursuit; / for there was no way, alone as he was in the sacred chariot, / to attack with the spear and hold in the swift horses” (17.463–465). A chariot needs a driver and a fighter, and Automedon, alone, can be only one of them (see [[chariot]] and the translation's note on 17.464). The likeness holds for the speed and the swoop, and the narrative at once takes back the kill it promised. Alcimedon sees him and asks which god has taken away his wits, and Automedon gives him the whip and the reins and gets down to fight (17.466–483; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]).

The simile follows the vulture of Book 16, where Sarpedon and Patroclus rushed at each other like two vultures screaming on a high rock (16.428–430). Then Patroclus was alive and Automedon was his charioteer; now Automedon drives the same horses alone and grieves for him.
