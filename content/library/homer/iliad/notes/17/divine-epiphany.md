summary: Four times a god comes into the fight over the body in the likeness of a man the hearer trusts. Apollo comes to Hector as Mentes, leader of the Cicones (17.70–82), to Aeneas as the old herald Periphas, who served his father (17.322–341), and again to Hector as Phaenops, his dearest guest-friend (17.582–590); Athena comes to Menelaus as Phoenix (17.543–573). Only Aeneas knows the god.
refs: [17.73, 17.82, 17.323-326, 17.333-334, 17.555, 17.582-585]

**The likenesses.** Each disguise is chosen for the hearer. Periphas, son of Epytus, had grown old as a herald beside Anchises, “a man whose mind was kindly toward him” (17.324–325; see [[heralds]]); Phaenops, son of Asius, was the dearest of Hector's guest-friends and had his home at Abydos (17.583–584; see [[xenia]]); Phoenix was the old man who brought up Achilles (9.485–495; see [[phoenix]]), and Athena takes his likeness “in form and in tireless voice” (17.555). Mentes, “the leader of the Cicones”, is named only here in the Iliad, where the Catalogue gives the Cicones another leader, Euphemus (17.73; 2.846). The words, too, fit the voice. As Mentes, Apollo tells Hector that he is running after horses no one but Achilles can drive, while Menelaus kills the best of the Trojans (17.75–81); as Periphas he tells Aeneas that Zeus wills victory for the Trojans, yet they tremble and will not fight (17.327–332); as Phaenops he asks who among the Achaeans will fear Hector now, when he has shrunk back before Menelaus, “a soft spearman” (17.586–590).

**Recognition.** Hector does not know the god either time, any more than he knew Apollo as Asius in Book 16 (16.715–725); he goes at once where he is sent. Aeneas knows him: “So he spoke, and Aeneas knew Apollo who shoots from afar / as he looked him in the face” (17.333–334), and he says so to Hector, “one of the gods has come and stood close beside me” (17.338; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Menelaus answers Athena as Phoenix, and his wish for her help, spoken to the old man, reaches the goddess herself (17.560–568; see [[prayer]]). Athena's coming is marked for the listener alone: she goes down among the Achaeans wrapped in a purple cloud, like the rainbow Zeus stretches out as a portent (17.544–552; see [[like-rainbows]], [[purple]] and [[teras]]). Mentes' departure is the verse of Apollo's departure as Asius: “So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men” (17.82 = 16.726; see [[ponos]]).
