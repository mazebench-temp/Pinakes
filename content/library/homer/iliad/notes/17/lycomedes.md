---
summary: When Aeneas kills Leiocritus, “the brave companion of Lycomedes”, Lycomedes pities him and avenges him on the Paeonian Apisaon, son of Hippasus, the best of the fighters after Asteropaeus (17.344–351).
refs: [17.347-349]
---

ἔνθʼ αὖτʼ Αἰνείας Λειώκριτον οὔτασε δουρὶ / υἱὸν Ἀρίσβαντος Λυκομήδεος ἐσθλὸν ἑταῖρον, “Then Aeneas stabbed Leiocritus with his spear, / the son of Arisbas, the brave companion of Lycomedes” (17.344–345; see [[aeneas]], [[leiocritus]] and [[arisbas]]). Apollo, in the likeness of the herald Periphas, has just roused Aeneas, and the Trojans stand against the Achaeans again (17.319–343; see [[apollo]]).

“And Lycomedes, dear to Ares, pitied him as he fell, / and came up close and stood and threw his shining spear, / and hit Apisaon, son of Hippasus, shepherd of the people, / in the liver beneath the midriff, and at once loosened his knees” (17.346–349; see [[dear-to-ares]], [[pity]] and [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). The throw and the wound are told in the verses in which Eurypylus killed another Apisaon, the son of Phausius, in Book 11, with only the father's name changed (17.347–349 ≈ 11.577–579; see [[apisaon]] and the translation's note on 13.412–423). The dead man had come from Paeonia, “and after Asteropaeus he was the best in the fighting” (17.350–351; see [[paeonians]]). The same verse that sent Lycomedes forward sends Asteropaeus: “And warlike Asteropaeus pitied him as he fell” (17.352), but he can do nothing against the ring of shields that Ajax has ordered around the body (17.352–359; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[ajax]]).

In Book 12 Lycomedes held the wall with the son of Oileus when Telamonian Ajax went to help Menestheus (12.364–369; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
