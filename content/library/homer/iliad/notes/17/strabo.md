---
summary: Strabo uses Hippothous' death “far from deep-soiled Larisa” to decide which Larisa was the Pelasgians': not the one near Hamaxitus, in sight of Ilium, but the one near Cyme, a thousand stades away, where the Aeolian settlers found the Pelasgians worn down by the war. He also sets Abydos, the home of Phaenops son of Asius, under Asius, finds Asteropaeus' father in the old name of the Paeonians, shows a tomb of Schedius in Phocis, and names Lyctus and Panopeus.
---

- **Hippothous' Larisa.** Strabo places the Pelasgians next to the Cilicians of the Troad and the people of Eurypylus, “from what Homer says and from the rest of history”, and quotes the Catalogue: Hippothous led “the tribes of the Pelasgians, fighters with the spear, / those who lived in deep-soiled Larisa” (2.840–843). From this, he says, the poet shows that the Pelasgians were many, for he said “tribes”, not “tribe”, and that they lived at Larisa. There are many Larisas, but one must take one of those near by, and most rightly the one near Cyme. Of the three, the one at Hamaxitus is in full view of Ilium and very near, about two hundred stades away, “so that Hippothous could not plausibly be said to have fallen in the struggle over Patroclus ‘far from Larisa’, from that one at least”, but rather from the one near Cyme, about a thousand stades off. The third, a village in the territory of Ephesus in the plain of the Caÿster, once a city with a temple of Apollo of Larisa, lies nearer Tmolus than Ephesus and so would belong to the Maeonians, and nothing shows that it, or Ephesus either, existed at the time; for the Larisa near Cyme the whole Aeolian history, a little after the Trojan War, is witness (*Geography* 13.3.2, on 17.301). In his list of the many Larisas he names in Asia the Phryconis near Cyme, the one at Hamaxitus in the Troad, and the Ephesian Larisa (*Geography* 9.5.19). Homer says that Hippothous fell on the body “far from deep-soiled Larisa”, and did not repay his parents for his rearing (17.300–302; see [[hippothous]] and [[larisa]]).
- **The Pelasgians after the war.** The Aeolian history Strabo means is the founding of Cyme. Settlers from Phricium, the Locrian mountain above Thermopylae, put in where Cyme now stands and found the Pelasgians “worn down by the Trojan War” (κεκακωμένους ὑπὸ τοῦ Τρωικοῦ πολέμου), but still holding Larisa, about seventy stades from Cyme. They built the fort still called the New Wall thirty stades from Larisa, took Larisa, founded Cyme and settled the survivors there; from the mountain both Cyme and Larisa are called Phryconis, and Larisa was deserted in Strabo's day. The Lesbians, he adds, say that they were ranged under Pylaeus, whom the poet names as a leader of the Pelasgians, and that a mountain of theirs is still called Pylaeum after him (*Geography* 13.3.3; see [[pylaeus]] and [[pelasgians]]). In Larisa Phryconis, he goes on, Piasus was honored, a leader of the Pelasgians who violated his daughter Larisa and was drowned by her in a jar of wine (13.3.4). In his account of Italy Strabo quotes the same verses of the Catalogue to show that Homer made the Pelasgians neighbors of the Cilicians of the Troad (*Geography* 5.2.4).
- **Abydos and Asius.** Phaenops, son of Asius, whose likeness Apollo takes to rouse Hector, “had his home at Abydos” (17.582–584; see [[phaenops-son-of-asius]]). Strabo does not name him, but he sets Abydos in the realm of Asius son of Hyrtacus. The coast from the Aesepus to Abydos, he says, was Trojan, “for the places about Abydos are under Asius”, though a bastard son of Priam kept his father's horses at Abydos (*Geography* 13.1.7, on 4.499–500), and he quotes the Catalogue's verses on the men of Percote, Practius, Sestus, Abydos and Arisbe, who “were led in turn by the son of Hyrtacus, Asius” (2.835–839; *Geography* 13.1.20). See [[abydos]] and [[asius]].
- **Paeonia.** Apisaon, son of Hippasus, “had come from deep-soiled Paeonia” (17.350; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). In the excerpts that preserve the lost end of his seventh book, Strabo reports that some make the Paeonians colonists of the Phrygians and others their founders, that Paeonia once reached to Pelagonia and Pieria, and that Pelagonia was formerly called Orestia; so it is not unreasonable that Asteropaeus, one of the leaders who came from Paeonia to Ilium, is called the son of Pelegon, since the Paeonians themselves were called Pelagonians (*Geography* 7, fr. 38, 39). Asteropaeus is the Paeonian whom Apisaon followed in prowess, and who tries in vain to avenge him (17.351–355; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[paeonia]]).
- **The tomb of Schedius.** Daphnus, Strabo says, now razed, was once a city of Phocis on the Euboean sea, dividing the Epicnemidian Locrians in two, and the proof is the Schedieion in it, which they say is the tomb of Schedius (*Geography* 9.3.17). Homer has two Phocian leaders of that name, both killed by Hector: Schedius son of Perimedes (15.515–516) and Schedius son of Iphitus, “by far the best of the Phocians, who in famous Panopeus / had his home” (17.306–308; 2.517–518). Strabo does not say which is meant. See [[schedius]] and [[phocians]].
- **Panopeus and Lyctus.** Panopeus, Schedius' home, is in Strabo's day called Phanoteus; it borders on the country about Lebadeia, is the fatherland of Epeius, and the story of Tityus is placed there (*Geography* 9.3.14; see [[panopeus]]). Lyctus, from which Coeranus followed Meriones (17.611), is the Cretan city “which the poet called Lyctus”, Lyttus in later Greek, a hundred and twenty stades from Cnossus (*Geography* 10.4.7; see [[lyctus]]).
