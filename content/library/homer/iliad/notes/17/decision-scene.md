summary: Menelaus, alone over the body as Hector comes on, debates with his own heart. If he leaves the armor and Patroclus, the Danaans will be indignant; if he stands alone, he will be surrounded. He breaks off in the verse of Odysseus' debate and decides by a rule about the gods: “When a man is willing, against a god's will, to fight with a man / whom a god honors, soon a great bane rolls down on him” (17.98–99). He gives ground, to find Ajax and come back.
passages:
  - 17.89-113 | Menelaus debates with his heart
---

**Two evils.** The speech has the shape of Odysseus' when he is cut off in Book 11 (11.403–410; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]). It opens with the same verse, “and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (17.90; see [[deeply-troubled]]), and sets two courses side by side, εἰ μέν … εἰ δέ. “Ah me, if I leave behind the fine armor / and Patroclus, who lies here to win satisfaction for me, / I fear that any of the Danaans who sees it will be indignant with me” (17.91–93; see [[nemesis]] and [[time]]). “But if I fight Hector and the Trojans alone / out of shame, I fear they may surround me, many against one” (17.94–95; see [[aidos]]). Then comes the verse that breaks off every such debate in the poem, “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (17.97 = 11.407, 21.562, 22.122, 22.385).

**The rule.** Odysseus answered his question with a rule for the brave, and stood. Menelaus answers with a rule about the gods. Hector fights “with help from the gods” (ἐκ θεόφιν), and to fight a man whom a god honors is to bring a great bane on oneself; so no Danaan will blame him if he gives way (17.98–101; see [[daimon]]). Shame is weighed against the gods and yields to them, and the debate finds a third course: if he could find Ajax, the two of them would come back “even against a god's will” and drag the body away for Achilles, “of evils that would be the best” (17.102–105). As with Odysseus, the enemy ends the debate: “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart, / the ranks of the Trojans came on” (17.106–107 ~ 11.411–412; see [[pondering-two-ways]]), and Menelaus gives ground step by step, like a lion driven from a farmyard (17.108–113; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]] and [[retreat]]).
