summary: Automedon cannot move Achilles' horses, which stand weeping for Patroclus, until Zeus puts might into them. Then he swoops through the battle with them like a vulture after geese, but alone in the chariot he cannot both drive and fight; he gives the reins to Alcimedon, kills Aretus when Hector and Aeneas come for the horses, and strips him, saying he has eased his grief for Patroclus a little (17.426–542).

**The weeping horses.** The horses of Achilles stand apart from the battle, weeping since they learned that their charioteer had fallen. “Truly Automedon, the valiant son of Diores, / often touched them, striking them with the swift whip, / and often spoke to them with soothing words, and often with threats” (17.429–431; see [[diores-father-of-automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), but they will go neither back to the ships nor into the battle, and stand as still as a pillar on a tomb, their heads bowed and their manes trailing in the dust (17.432–440). Zeus pities them and puts might into their knees and hearts, “so that you may bring Automedon too safe out of the war / to the hollow ships” (17.451–453; see [[zeus]]). The horses shake the dust from their manes and carry the chariot into the battle (17.456–458).

**Alone in the chariot.** “And behind them Automedon fought, grieving though he was for his companion, / swooping with his horses like a vulture after geese” (17.459–460; see [[like-vultures]]). He can escape the Trojans easily and charge them easily, “But he killed no men whenever he rushed in pursuit; / for there was no way, alone as he was in the sacred chariot, / to attack with the spear and hold in the swift horses” (17.463–465; see the translation's note on 17.464 and [[chariot-fighters]]). His companion Alcimedon sees him and asks which god has taken away his wits, that he fights alone in the front when his companion is dead and Hector wears the armor (17.466–473; see [[alcimedon]]). Automedon answers that no Achaean could match Alcimedon at holding in the immortal horses, “except Patroclus, a counselor equal to the gods, / while he lived? But now death and fate have overtaken him” (17.475–478; see [[patroclus]]), gives him the whip and the reins, and leaps down to fight on foot (17.479–483).

**Aretus.** Hector sees the horses “with poor charioteers”, and he and Aeneas, with Chromius and Aretus, come to take them (17.483–497; see [[hector]], [[aeneas]] and [[aretus]]). Automedon prays to Zeus, and his heart is filled with courage and strength (17.498–499; see [[prayer]]). He tells Alcimedon to keep the horses close, “breathing on my back” (17.501–506), and calls the two Ajaxes and Menelaus to keep the pitiless day from the two of them, ending with a maxim: “But these things lie on the knees of the gods; / for I too will throw, and all this will be the care of Zeus” (17.507–515; see [[the-two-ajaxes]], [[menelaus]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). His spear goes through Aretus' shield and belt into the lower belly, and Aretus leaps forward and falls on his back like an ox struck behind the horns with an axe (17.516–524; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]). Hector's spear misses him: “but he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear; / for he bent forward” (17.526–527), and the spear sticks quivering in the ground behind him (17.527–529), in the four verses of Meriones' escape from Aeneas in the fight over Sarpedon (17.526–529 = 16.610–613; see [[meriones]]). The two Ajaxes part them before they close with swords (17.530–534).

**For Patroclus.** “Automedon, the equal of swift Ares” (17.536; see [[equal-of-ares]]), a title the poem gives Hector and Meriones and, at his last charge, Patroclus (8.215, 13.295, 16.784), strips Aretus and speaks in triumph: “Truly, a little at least, for the dead son of Menoetius / I have eased my heart of grief, though it is a lesser man I have killed” (17.538–539; see [[euchomai]]). He puts the bloody spoils in the chariot and mounts it, “his feet and his hands above / bloody, like a lion that has devoured a bull” (17.540–542; see [[lion]]).
