summary: Menelaus recalls Hyperenor to warn off his brother Euphorbus: he scorned Menelaus and stood up to him, and never went home to his wife and parents. Euphorbus answers that Menelaus widowed his brother's new bride and left his parents in mourning, and tries to avenge him.

# Unnamed: Menelaus' account of him (17.25-28) and Euphorbus' (17.35-37).
refs: [17.25-28, 17.35-37]
---

{{quote:ebd330c3-f011-5af4-95a5-21cd25769709}}

**Menelaus' account.** Menelaus names him with a periphrasis, βίη Ὑπερήνορος, “mighty Hyperenor” (17.24; see [[might-of-hyperenor]]). Book 14 tells his death in four verses, with no word spoken between the two men (14.516–519). Here Menelaus gives the killing a cause: Hyperenor scorned him and stood up to him, and called him “the most despised warrior among the Danaans” (17.26; see [[menelaus]]). Walter Leaf observes that the killing is told in Book 14 “in a merely cursory manner, with nothing to explain the allusion here” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.24). ἔφατο may be taken as “thought”, he adds, but even so Book 14 gives no hint of how Hyperenor showed his contempt, “unless Menelaos means to assume it for rhetorical purposes here”. The lesson Menelaus draws is for the brother: “So surely I will loose your strength too” (17.29; see [[euphorbus]]).

**His brother's account.** Euphorbus tells the same death from his family's side. “Now, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, now you will surely pay / for my brother, whom you killed, and you boast of it as you speak; / and you widowed his wife in the inmost part of her new bridal chamber, / and brought accursed lamentation and grief upon his parents” (17.34–37; see [[marriage]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). Walter Leaf suggests that the words may allude to the common family house, which grew by new chambers rather than new houses (on 17.36), as the married sons of Priam sleep with their wives in fifty chambers of polished stone within his palace (6.242–246). The wife whom Menelaus says Hyperenor never went home to gladden (17.28) is a bride; the Catalogue tells the same of Protesilaus, whose wife was left behind “her cheeks torn in mourning” and his house half-finished (2.700–701; see [[protesilaus]]). ἀρητόν, “accursed” (17.37), returns in Andromache's lament for Hector (24.741; see the translation's note on 17.37). In Book 14, when Acamas killed Promachus to avenge his brother, he said that a man prays “to leave a kinsman in his halls, to defend him against disaster” (14.483–485; see [[acamas]] and [[poine]]). Euphorbus is that kinsman, γνωτός (17.35), and he dies trying (17.43–60).
