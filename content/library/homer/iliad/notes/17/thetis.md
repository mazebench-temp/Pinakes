summary: Thetis is not named, but the narrator lays Achilles' ignorance of Patroclus' death to her: she used to tell him in secret the purpose of Zeus, and this time she did not tell him. Apollo speaks of Achilles as the son “whom an immortal mother bore”, and the gods' gifts to her mortal husband, the armor and the horses, are remembered.
# Unnamed: "whom an immortal mother bore" (17.78 = 10.404) and "his mother" who reported Zeus' purpose (17.408-411).

**What she did not tell.** Achilles does not yet know that Patroclus is dead, for the fight is far from the ships, under the wall of Troy (17.401–404; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]):

{{quote:36f09dc7-dffa-590e-beef-050925117ee2}}

What he had learned from her was that he would not sack the city, either without Patroclus or with him (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). She has told him that his life is short (1.352, 1.416–418) and that two fates are carrying him to his death (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf takes νόσφιν as “in secret”, comparing Patroclus' question whether his lady mother had told him something from Zeus (16.36–37), and observes that the idea of a continued prophetic communication from her is found only here (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.408). Achilles told Patroclus that she had told him nothing (16.50–51; see the note on Book 16 and the translation's note on 11.794–795). In Book 18, when he sees Antilochus coming, he will remember that his mother once told him that the best of the Myrmidons would leave the light of the sun at the hands of the Trojans while he still lived (18.9–11). Leaf finds that this “almost directly contradicts” 17.410–411, and puts the discrepancy down to a difference of authorship (on 17.408; see the translation's note on 17.408–411).

**The immortal mother.** Apollo, as Mentes, tells Hector that the horses of Achilles are hard to master “for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (17.78 = 10.404; see [[apollo]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The horses and the armor were the gods' gifts to Peleus, her mortal husband (see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). Hector puts on the immortal armor “which the heavenly gods / gave to his dear father” (17.195–196), and Zeus asks the weeping horses why the gods gave them “to lord Peleus, / a mortal” (17.443–444; see [[peleus]] and [[zeus]]). In Book 18 Achilles will tell her that the gods gave Peleus the armor on the day they cast her into the bed of a mortal man (18.84–85), and she will tell Hephaestus that she endured that marriage much against her will (18.432–434; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]).
