**Before Hector.**

{{quote:65337198-aa3e-5256-a471-869bb5a3af46}}

Alone over the body, Menelaus has debated with his own heart whether to fight Hector and the Trojans, and has decided to give way and look for Ajax (17.90–105). αὐτὰρ ὅ γʼ ἐξοπίσω ἀνεχάζετο, λεῖπε δὲ νεκρὸν / ἐντροπαλιζόμενος ὥς τε λὶς ἠϋγένειος, “And he gave ground backward, and left the body, / turning back again and again, like a bearded lion” (17.108–109). ἐντροπαλίζομαι, “keep turning back”, is said of Ajax when Zeus puts him to flight just before the full simile in Book 11 (11.547), and of Andromache going home from Hector (6.496). λίς is the rarer of the two words for the lion (see [[lion]]), and ἠϋγένειος, “bearded”, is its epithet (see [[bearded]]). ὅν ῥα κύνες τε καὶ ἄνδρες ἀπὸ σταθμοῖο δίωνται / ἔγχεσι καὶ φωνῇ· τοῦ δʼ ἐν φρεσὶν ἄλκιμον ἦτορ / παχνοῦται, ἀέκων δέ τʼ ἔβη ἀπὸ μεσσαύλοιο, “that dogs and men drive from the steading / with spears and shouting; and the valiant heart within him / grows chill, and he goes unwillingly from the farmyard” (17.110–112). παχνόομαι, “be chilled, frozen”, from πάχνη, “hoarfrost”, is found only here in Homer. Walter Leaf compares Hesiod's ἐπάχνωσεν φίλον ἦτορ, of a theft that “chills the heart” of the man robbed (*Works and Days* 360), and calls it the opposite of ἰαίνομαι, the warming of the heart (23.598; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.112).

The tenor: ὣς ἀπὸ Πατρόκλοιο κίε ξανθὸς Μενέλαος, “so fair-haired Menelaus went from Patroclus” (17.113). The simile is a shorter form of the one in Book 11: the dogs and men, the farmyard, the courage that will not carry the lion through, the unwilling retreat (11.548–557). Menelaus finds Ajax, and the two come back to the body (17.114–139; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]] and [[menelaus]]).

**To find Antilochus.**

{{quote:46b13c12-ef9c-5f17-9b7f-ea63dff6c4a3}}

When the mist has lifted, Ajax asks Menelaus to find Antilochus and send him to Achilles with the news (17.651–655). βῆ δʼ ἰέναι ὥς τίς τε λέων ἀπὸ μεσσαύλοιο, / ὅς τʼ ἐπεὶ ἄρ κε κάμῃσι κύνας τʼ ἄνδρας τʼ ἐρεθίζων, “and he went, like a lion from a farmyard, / when he grows weary of provoking the dogs and the men” (17.657–658). The next six verses are those of Book 11, word for word but for ἀπονόσφιν, which the text prints as one word here and as two there (17.659–664 = 11.550–555; see the translation's note on 17.664): the men keep watch all night and will not let the lion take the fattest of the cattle, it charges and gains nothing, javelins and blazing torches fly at it, and at dawn it goes off with grieving heart. The tenor ends as Ajax's ended: ὣς ἀπὸ Πατρόκλοιο βοὴν ἀγαθὸς Μενέλαος / ἤϊε πόλλʼ ἀέκων, “so Menelaus, good at the war cry, went from Patroclus / very unwillingly” (17.665–666), as Ajax “went back from the Trojans / very unwillingly” (11.556–557). Ajax feared for the ships; Menelaus fears for the body, “that the Achaeans / in grievous rout would leave him as a prey to the enemy” (17.666–667).

Leaf thought the borrowed simile pointless here, since Menelaus is not driven back by his enemies but goes of his own will at Ajax's request; he reports that Fick and others reject the whole passage (17.657–673), and he judged the eagle simile that follows much more apt (on 17.657; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]). What the two retreats share is the unwillingness, πόλλʼ ἀέκων: the lion leaves the cattle it wants, Ajax the ships he defends, and Menelaus the body he has guarded since the first verse of the book.
