{{quote:02e5fa35-5e89-5f46-8fb2-64689ea30524}}

“And again over Patroclus the fierce battle was stretched tight, / grievous and tearful, and Athena roused the quarrel, / coming down from heaven” (17.543–545). Zeus has sent her, “for his mind had now turned” (17.545–546); Walter Leaf reports that the couplet was athetized in antiquity, and judges it an interpolation, since Zeus does not change his design and sits on Ida, not in heaven (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.545; see the translation's note on 17.545–546).

ἠΰτε πορφυρέην ἶριν θνητοῖσι τανύσσῃ / Ζεὺς ἐξ οὐρανόθεν τέρας ἔμμεναι ἢ πολέμοιο / ἢ καὶ χειμῶνος δυσθαλπέος, ὅς ῥά τε ἔργων / ἀνθρώπους ἀνέπαυσεν ἐπὶ χθονί, μῆλα δὲ κήδει, “As when a purple rainbow is stretched out for mortals / by Zeus from heaven, to be a portent either of war / or of chilling winter, which from their work / makes men cease upon the earth, and troubles the flocks” (17.547–550). Zeus “stretches” the rainbow, τανύσσῃ, the verb of the hide in the simile of the tug over the body and of the battle that is stretched tight over Patroclus (17.390–393, 17.401, 17.543; see [[the-stretched-hide]]). The rainbow is a τέρας, a sign sent by the god, as were the rainbows that Zeus set in the cloud in Book 11 (11.27–28; see [[teras]]). δυσθαλπής, “hard to warm, chilling”, is found only here in Homer: a winter storm that stops men's work in the fields and harms the flocks (see [[sheep]]).

πορφύρεος is the color of the rainbow and of the cloud in which the goddess wraps herself. Leaf, following Monro, takes it as dark and troubled rather than bright: to the Greeks, he says, the rainbow had no associations of hope; it is part of the storm cloud against which it is seen and brings thoughts of gloom and disaster, and the point of the simile is that the cloud in which Athena wrapped herself was as lurid as the rainbow (on 17.547; see [[purple]] and the translation's note on 17.547).

The tenor: ὣς ἣ πορφυρέῃ νεφέλῃ πυκάσασα ἓ αὐτὴν / δύσετʼ Ἀχαιῶν ἔθνος, ἔγειρε δὲ φῶτα ἕκαστον, “so she, wrapping herself in a purple cloud, / entered the crowd of the Achaeans, and roused each man” (17.551–552). The goddess comes as a portent of war, and war follows. She speaks first to Menelaus, in the likeness of Phoenix, and puts the daring of a fly in his breast (17.553–573; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]] and [[athena]]).

Athena's descent in Book 4 had a likeness of the same kind: she darted down like a star that Zeus sends as a portent to sailors or to an army (4.75–79; see [[shooting-star]]). Both times the goddess comes as a sign, and both times the fighting begins again. Iris, the messenger of the gods, has the rainbow's name, but Homer never shows her as a rainbow (see [[iris]]).
