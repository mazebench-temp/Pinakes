summary: Zeus watches the fight over Patroclus from Ida and decides it. He sees Hector put on the armor of Achilles, foretells his death and nods to give him power for a time; he pours mist around the body, which he will not leave to the Trojan dogs; he pities the weeping horses of Achilles and keeps them from Hector; at last he shakes the aegis over Ida and gives the Trojans the victory, and at Ajax's prayer he scatters the mist.

**Zeus in their mouths.** Zeus has watched the battle from the peaks of Ida since he woke there in Book 15 (15.4–6, 15.152–153; see [[ida]]), and in the fight for the body of Patroclus the men on both sides speak of him as the power behind it (see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Menelaus answers Euphorbus' threat with “Father Zeus, it is no fine thing to boast so overweeningly” (17.19), and strikes him “praying to father Zeus” (17.46; see [[menelaus]] and [[euphorbus]]). The verses are those of his duel with Paris (17.44–46 = 3.348–350, with δεύτερος for δεύτερον): then Zeus failed him and his sword shattered (3.361–368), now the spear goes through the young man's neck. When Hector comes on, Menelaus gives way, “since he fights with help from the gods” (17.101). Hector answers Glaucus' rebuke with the words in which the narrator explained Patroclus' folly: “but the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger” (17.176–178 ≈ 16.688–690; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). Menelaus reminds the Achaean leaders that “from Zeus come honor and glory” (17.251), as Odysseus told the kings in Book 2 that “a king's honor comes from Zeus” (2.197; see [[time]]). The narrator says that the Argives would have won glory “even beyond the portion of Zeus”, had Apollo not roused Aeneas (17.321–322; see [[aisa]] and the translation's note on 6.487). Apollo tells Aeneas that “Zeus wills far rather for us than for the Danaans / victory” (17.331–332), and Aeneas tells the Trojans that “Zeus, the highest counselor, is still our helper in the battle” (17.339; see [[epitarrhothos]]), with the title that Zeus gave himself when he dared the gods to pull him down with a golden rope (ὕπατος μήστωρ; 8.22; see [[the-golden-rope]]). Automedon prays to father Zeus and is filled “with courage and strength” (17.498–499), the words that told how Hector was filled when Ares entered him (17.211–212); before he throws he says, “and all this will be the care of Zeus” (17.515; see [[automedon]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). And Menelaus says of Hector, “for Zeus grants him glory” (17.566), as Nestor said of him in Book 8 (8.141; see [[kydos]]).

**The armor.** Hector puts on “the immortal armor / of Achilles, son of Peleus, which the heavenly gods / gave to his dear father” (17.194–196; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]], [[olympians]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]), and Zeus sees him:

{{quote:534e6117-e327-53d3-a9f1-c90b924824f2}}

The verse “he shook his head and spoke to his own heart” is used in the Iliad only of Zeus, and only in this book, here and over the horses of Achilles (17.200, 17.442); in the Odyssey it is Poseidon's, as he watches Odysseus on his raft (*Odyssey* 5.285, 5.376). ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch”, is what Hector said over the dying Patroclus (16.837). When Apollo struck the helmet of Achilles from Patroclus' head, Zeus gave it to Hector to wear, “and his destruction was near him” (16.799–800; see [[helmet]]); now he tells him that death “is now close to you” (17.202), and calls the man he killed “gentle and strong” (17.204; see [[eneeie]]). The armor was taken “against all order” (οὐ κατὰ κόσμον, 17.205; see [[kosmos]]): Walter Leaf notes that it was Apollo who struck the helmet from Patroclus' head and loosened his corselet (16.793–804), and suggests that the wrong may lie in its divine origin (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.205; see the translation's note on 17.205). Since Hector will not come home for Andromache to take the armor from him, Zeus will put great power in his hands in recompense (ποινή; μέγα κράτος ἐγγυαλίξω, 17.206–208; see [[poine]], [[kratos]], [[andromache]] and [[hector]]): the power he promised him in Book 11, “to kill, until he comes to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down” (11.192–194). In Book 15 the narrator gave the same reason for Zeus' favor: Hector was to be short-lived (15.610–614; see [[short-lived]]). Then Zeus nods, in the verse of his promise to Thetis (17.209 = 1.528; see [[neuo]] and [[dark-brows]]); Leaf calls the verse “evidently inappropriate here, as Zeus only nods to himself” (on 17.209). He fits the armor to Hector's body, “and Ares entered into him, / dread Enyalius” (17.210–211; see [[ares]] and the translation's note on 17.210–211).

**The mist.** When the Trojans charge, the Achaeans stand around the son of Menoetius “with one heart” (17.267), fenced with their shields, and Zeus covers them:

{{quote:39072ab5-e6c9-5231-9aa3-60f12ff1e176}}

Hera and Zeus “poured a thick mist around” their horses when they stopped them (5.776, 8.50), and over the body of Sarpedon Zeus stretched night, “so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.567–568; see the note on Book 16). The narrator gives the reason in two parts: Zeus had not hated Patroclus while he lived as Achilles' attendant, and he would not have him become a spoil for “the Trojan dogs” (17.270–273; see [[dogs]] and [[patroclus]]), to whom Hector had meant to give the body (17.126–127). The dead of the proem were “the spoil of dogs” (1.4). μισέω, “hate”, is found nowhere else in Homer. Walter Leaf thinks the passage hardly genuine: the Achaeans, for whose benefit the darkness is meant, are the first to beg for its removal (17.645–647), and he takes the darkness of Ajax's prayer for the dust of the struggle on the sandy plain; ὀμίχλη, “fog”, which Zeus thrusts away with the mist (17.649), is “a great mist of dust” raised by storm winds in a simile of Book 13 (13.336; on 17.268, 17.272 and 17.644).

**The toil.** In the poem as it stands the mist stays around the body. The best men fight in it, so that “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–367), while the rest of the armies fight at their ease under a clear sky (17.370–377; see [[helios]]). The fight is Zeus' work as well as his protection: “such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus / stretched tight that day, an evil toil” (17.400–401). ἐτάνυσσε is the verb with which the son of Cronus stretched the battle even (11.336) and the strife over Sarpedon tight (16.662), and the men around the body pull it this way and that like a hide stretched with fat (17.389–395; see [[the-stretched-hide]] and [[the-rope-of-war]]).

**The purpose of Zeus.** The narrator explains why Achilles does not know of the death. His mother, “who would report to him the purpose of great Zeus” (Διὸς μεγάλοιο νόημα, 17.409), had not told him this (17.408–411; see [[thetis]] and [[noos]]).

**The horses.** Achilles' horses stand apart from the battle and weep for their charioteer (17.426–440; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and Zeus sees them:

{{quote:1136d14f-3b27-5cb6-91d7-ad0ad5d8b14f}}

He pities them (ἐλέησε, 17.441; see [[pity]]) as he pitied Hector vomiting blood on the plain (15.12) and his son Sarpedon and Patroclus as they met (16.431), and the verse returns when he pities Achilles and the elders who mourn with him (19.338–340). The horses were “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381, 16.867; see [[peleus]]); here Zeus says “we”. ἀγήρω τʼ ἀθανάτω τε, “ageless and immortal”, is the formula for the condition of the gods (see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The second verse of his judgment on man (see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]) ends Odysseus' warning to the suitor Amphinomus, after a first verse of his own, that the earth nurtures nothing feebler than man (*Odyssey* 18.130–131; 17.447 = *Odyssey* 18.131); and Achilles will tell Priam that the gods have spun it for wretched mortals to live in grief, while they themselves are without care (24.525–526; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). Then Zeus decides: “But not behind you, nor in your intricately worked chariot, / will Hector, son of Priam, ride; I will not allow it” (17.448–449). ἐποχήσεται is the verb of Hector's oath to Dolon that no other Trojan would ride behind these horses (10.330; see [[dolon]] and the translation's note on 17.448–449). The armor is enough for Hector, who “boasts of it for nothing” (17.450). The horses will carry Automedon safe to the ships, “for I will still hold out glory to them / to kill, until they come to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (17.453–455): the promise made to Hector in Book 11 (11.193–194), now made to the Trojans, whom σφισι, “to them”, must mean though they have not been named since 17.420, as Walter Leaf notes (on 17.453). “So saying, he breathed noble might into the horses” (17.456).

**His mind turned.** Athena comes down from heaven to rouse the Achaeans, “for far-seeing Zeus had sent her / to rouse the Danaans; for his mind had now turned” (17.545–546; see [[far-seeing]] and [[athena]]). Walter Leaf reports that the first verse was athetized in antiquity, and judges the couplet an interpolation meant to explain how Athena could act against Zeus' command of Book 8 (8.7–12): there is no change in Zeus' design, for he goes on helping the Trojans (17.593–596), and he is on Ida, not in heaven (on 17.545; see the translation's note on 17.545–546 and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The simile that brings her down is Zeus' own sign: “As when a purple rainbow is stretched out for mortals / by Zeus from heaven, to be a portent either of war / or of chilling winter” (17.547–549; see [[teras]], [[purple]] and [[iris]]), like the rainbows to which the serpents on Agamemnon's corselet are compared, “that the son of Cronus / sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28; see [[like-rainbows]]).

**The aegis.** When Apollo has sent Hector back into the fight (17.582–592; see [[apollo]]), Zeus himself turns the battle:

{{quote:8c14ffd9-b19a-59fb-b868-2fbc8e162cf6}}

In Book 15 he lent the aegis to Apollo to rout the Achaeans (15.229–230, 15.318–322); now he shakes it himself (see [[aegis]]), with the thunder and lightning of Book 8, when he thundered loudly from Ida and sent a blazing flash among the Achaeans (8.75–77), and thundered three times from the mountains of Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.170–171; see [[ida]]). Agamemnon had promised that Zeus would shake his dark aegis over the Trojans in resentment at their deceit (4.166–168); it is shaken over the Achaeans. Peneleos is the first to flee (17.597).

**Ajax's prayer.** Ajax and Menelaus see “that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle” (17.627; see the translation's note on 7.26). “Even one who is an utter fool / would know that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans”, says Ajax (17.629–630; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]): all their missiles strike home, “Zeus guides them all the same” (17.632), and the Achaeans' fall to the ground in vain. The mist hides every man who might carry the news to Achilles (17.640–644), and he prays:

{{quote:ce21374f-2c42-55c1-9694-17ec50f656dd}}

“So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears” (17.648) is the verse in which Zeus pitied Agamemnon's tears in Book 8 and nodded that his people would be saved (8.245–246; see [[tears]] and [[agamemnon]]). Ajax asks for light to see by, and to die in if he must; Zeus gives him the light and keeps the victory for the Trojans (see [[ajax]]). In the light Ajax sends Menelaus to find Antilochus, who will carry the news to Achilles (17.651–701; see [[antilochus]]). For the prayer in later criticism, see [[longinus-ajaxs-prayer]].
