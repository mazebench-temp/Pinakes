Apollo, in the likeness of Mentes, has turned Hector from his vain chase of Achilles' horses and told him that Menelaus has killed Euphorbus (17.70–81; see [[apollo]]). “And dread grief clouded Hector's heart, dark on every side” (17.83). He sees the one man taking the armor and the other lying in his blood (17.84–86). βῆ δὲ διὰ προμάχων κεκορυθμένος αἴθοπι χαλκῷ / ὀξέα κεκλήγων φλογὶ εἴκελος Ἡφαίστοιο / ἀσβέστῳ, “He went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze, / shouting shrilly, like the flame of Hephaestus, / unquenchable” (17.87–89). The half-verse φλογὶ εἴκελος was Hector's in Book 13 (13.53, 13.688), and here it is filled out. The flame is Hephaestus', fire itself, as when the hogs at Achilles' feast “were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus” (9.467–468; see [[hephaestus]]). It is ἄσβεστος, “unquenchable”, like the fire that the Trojans threw on Protesilaus' ship, when “at once an unquenchable flame poured over it” (16.122–123). ὀξέα κεκλήγων, “shouting shrilly”, is the scream of birds and of men in an onset, which the young Achaeans will utter as they flee at the end of the book (12.125, 16.430, 17.756; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]).

The first verse of the passage is said three times in this book: of Menelaus coming to stand over Patroclus (17.3), of Hector here, and of Hector again when a black cloud of grief covers him at Apollo's taunt (17.591–592). The flame finds the man it came for: “nor did his shrill shout escape the son of Atreus” (17.89). Menelaus, deeply troubled, debates with his own heart and gives ground like a lion driven from a farmyard (17.90–113; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]] and [[menelaus]]). Later he tells Athena why he cannot stand alone: “But Hector has the dread might of fire” (17.565; see [[hector]]).
