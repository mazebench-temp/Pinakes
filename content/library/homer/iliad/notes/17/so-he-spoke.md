---
summary: Every closing of Book 17 turns at once to the effect. A speech fails to persuade (17.33) or is obeyed (17.246, 17.491, 17.656), or it strikes the hearer: Aeneas knows Apollo, Athena is glad, a black cloud of grief covers Hector, Zeus pities Ajax's tears, Antilochus shudders at the news. Zeus closes his own soliloquy with the nod of Book 1 (17.209 = 1.528).
---

**The nod.** Zeus' words over Hector in Achilles' armor end “He spoke, and the son of Cronus nodded with his dark brows” (ἦ καὶ κυανέῃσιν ἐπʼ ὀφρύσι νεῦσε Κρονίων, 17.209), the verse with which he granted Thetis' prayer in Book 1 (1.528; see [[neuo]] and [[brows]]). There the nod confirmed a promise to a goddess. Here no one hears it, and it confirms what he has just said to his own heart: great power for Hector now, and no homecoming (17.206–208; see [[poine]]).

**Persuasion.** The first speech of the book fails: “So he spoke, but he did not persuade him; and answering he said” (17.33; see [[peitho]]), and Euphorbus dies for it (see [[a-fool-learns-once-it-is-done]]). The others are obeyed in the verse of obedience, ὣς ἔφατʼ, οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε: Menelaus twice does what Ajax asks (17.246, 17.656), and Aeneas joins Hector against the horses of Achilles (17.491).

**Effects.** The rest name what the words did. “So he spoke, and he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Ajax” (17.123); “So he spoke, and Aeneas knew Apollo who shoots from afar / as he looked him in the face” (17.333–334; see [[seeing-the-gods]]); “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena was glad / that he had prayed to her first of all the gods” (17.567–568; see [[prayer]]); “So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears” (17.648; see [[pity]]). Two carry bad news. When Apollo tells Hector that Menelaus has killed his dear companion Podes, “So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him” (17.591; see [[achos]]), the verse that will close Antilochus' news to Achilles (18.22); and when Menelaus tells Antilochus that Patroclus is dead, “So he spoke, and Antilochus shuddered when he heard the word” (17.694; see [[messenger-scene]]). The last closing of the book is an act: “So he spoke, and they took the body up from the ground in their arms” (17.722; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).
