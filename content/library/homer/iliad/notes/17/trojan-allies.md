summary: Hector, in the armor of Achilles, goes among the leaders of the allies, names ten of them, and tells them why he brought them to Troy: not for their numbers, but to protect the wives and children of the Trojans, for whose sake he wears down his own people with gifts and food. He promises half the spoils and equal fame to the man who drags Patroclus' body among the Trojans; Hippothous and Phorcys die in the attempt.
# Unnamed: the allies whom Hector urges on and their charge (17.213, 17.215-219, 17.221-236).
refs: [17.213, 17.215-219, 17.221-236]
passages:
  - 17.212-236 | Hector urges on the allies

**Ten leaders.** Hector goes “among the famous allies, / with a great cry” (17.212–213; see [[hector]]), and urges on their leaders one by one: “Mesthles and Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus / and Asteropaeus and Deisenor and Hippothous / and Phorcys and Chromius and Ennomus the augur” (17.216–218). Walter Leaf notes that the names are mostly taken from the Catalogue (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.216, citing 2.848–864): Mesthles led the Maeonians (2.864), Glaucus the Lycians with Sarpedon (2.876), Hippothous the Pelasgians (2.840), Phorcys the Phrygians (2.862), and Chromis, as the Catalogue calls him, and Ennomus the augur the Mysians (2.858). Asteropaeus, the Paeonian, is first named at the Achaean wall (12.102). Medon, Thersilochus and Deisenor are named only here, though a Paeonian Thersilochus dies in Book 21 (21.209). See [[mesthles]], [[glaucus]], [[medon-ally-of-the-trojans]], [[thersilochus]], [[asteropaeus]], [[deisenor]], [[hippothous]], [[phorcys]], [[chromis]] and [[ennomus]]. Leaf finds the mention of Glaucus among the men urged on “strange” (on 17.216); Glaucus has just rebuked Hector (17.140–168).

**Why they are at Troy.**

{{quote:d15a637d-ef21-5c1e-8c63-d5af0c869bc7}}

περικτιόνων, “who dwell around us” (17.220): Leaf notes that the word is found elsewhere only in passages of what he calls the ὁπλοποιία, the making of the arms (18.212, 19.104, 19.109), and once in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 2.65; on 17.220). Hector's words answer the complaint that Glaucus has just made, that the allies fight for a city that gives them no thanks (17.146–148; see [[charis]]), as Sarpedon made it in Book 5 (5.472–492; see [[sarpedon]]): they are there to protect the wives and little children of the Trojans (17.223–224; see [[children-and-widows]]), and the city pays for them, “With this in mind I wear down with gifts and with food / my people” (17.225–226). In Book 18 he will say that the treasures of Priam's city, once famous for its gold and bronze, have been sold off to Phrygia and Maeonia (18.288–292). The choice between dying and being saved is “the intimate talk of war” (17.227–228; see [[oaristys]]), and the reward for dragging Patroclus among the Trojans is half the spoils and fame as great as Hector's (17.229–232; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[kleos]]).

**The charge.** “So he spoke, and they went straight at the Danaans with all their weight, / holding up their spears; and their hearts had high hopes / of dragging the body away from under Ajax, son of Telamon, / the fools” (17.233–236; see [[ajax]] and [[nepios]]). Hippothous ties a strap to the dead man's ankle and is killed as he drags him, and Phorcys dies standing over Hippothous (17.288–318). Later the Paeonian Apisaon falls to Lycomedes, and Asteropaeus cannot break through the Achaeans' shields (17.348–355; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). Around the body “the dead of the Trojans and their mighty allies” fall heaped together with the Danaans (17.361–363). Euphorbus, at the start of the book, had claimed the spoils because “none of the Trojans or their famous allies” had hit Patroclus before him (17.14; see [[euphorbus]]), and Aeneas calls on “Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and the allies” to turn back (17.335; see [[aeneas]]).
