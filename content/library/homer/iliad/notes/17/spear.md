summary: Spears fail and succeed in pairs. Euphorbus' point bends back on Menelaus' shield, and Menelaus' goes through his neck (17.43–50); Automedon's long-shadowed spear goes through Aretus' shield and belt, and Hector's, thrown back, misses and sticks quivering in the ground (17.516–529); Hector's spear, thrown at Ajax, kills Schedius instead (17.304–311); and Idomeneus' spear snaps at the socket on Hector's corselet, while Hector's, thrown at Idomeneus, kills Coeranus (17.605–619).

**The throws.** Twice a man avoids a spear in the verse of Meriones' escape from Aeneas, “but he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear” (17.305, 17.526 = 16.610), and each time another pays: Hector's spear, missing Ajax, strikes Schedius under the collarbone (17.304–311), and missing Automedon, it sticks in the ground behind him with the butt quivering, “and then mighty Ares let its might go”, in four verses told of Aeneas' spear in Book 16 (17.526–529 = 16.610–613; see [[ares]]). The wounds are close and exact: Ajax's spear splits Hippothous' helmet so that the brain spurts along the socket, παρʼ αὐλόν (17.295–297; see the translation's note on 17.297), and Hector's spear strikes Coeranus under the jaw and the ear and pushes out his teeth “by the roots” (17.617–618; see the translation's note on 17.617–618).

**Over the body.** The defenders hold their spears in front of them, Menelaus first, “eager to kill whoever came against him” (17.7–8), and then the ring of Achaeans, who “held their spears before them” (17.355; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The Trojans following the bearers stab at them “with swords and double-pointed spears” (17.731; see [[double-pointed]]).
