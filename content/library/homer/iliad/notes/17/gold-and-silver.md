---
summary: Euphorbus falls with his hair drenched in blood, “hair like the Graces', / and his braids, which were bound tight with gold and silver” (17.51–52). Walter Leaf explains the braids as hair pinched into locks by little spirals of gold or silver, such as have been found beside skulls in ancient graves in Greece, in Etruria and at Hissarlik (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.52).
---

Gold worn to battle is the mark of a young man's death. Leaf compares Nastes the Carian, who “came to the war wearing gold, like a girl, / the fool”, and whose gold Achilles carried off (2.872–875; see [[nastes]]). Euphorbus' gold goes nowhere: Menelaus begins to strip him and is driven off by Hector (17.60–108; see [[graces]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).
