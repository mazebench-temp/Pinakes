---
summary: The Achaeans close around the body of Patroclus “with one heart”, are pushed back and rallied by Ajax, and hold it inside a ring of shields; one of them says that the earth should rather gape for them all than let the Trojans drag it away. When Zeus shakes the aegis they are routed, and the book ends with their young men fleeing to the ditch (17.266–287, 17.356–365, 17.414–419, 17.593–625, 17.753–761).
---

**One heart.** As the Trojans come on with a shout like a wave at a river's mouth, “the Achaeans / stood around the son of Menoetius with one heart, / fenced with their bronze-fitted shields” (17.266–268; see [[patroclus]] and [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]]), and Zeus pours mist about their helmets (17.268–273; see [[zeus]]). The Trojans push “the bright-eyed Achaeans” back from the body, though they kill none of them, “yet only for a short while were the Achaeans / to be away from it” (17.274–278; see [[bright-eyed]]), for Ajax rallies them (17.278–287; see [[ajax]]). After Apollo has roused Aeneas, Ajax lets no one draw back from the body “nor any of the Achaeans fight out in front of the others” (17.356–359; see [[battle-order]]).

**The voice of the army.** While the two sides pull the body this way and that, the narrator gives each army a voice: “and this is what a man would say, of the bronze-armored Achaeans: / ‘Friends, it is no glorious thing for us to go back / to the hollow ships; rather let the black earth here / gape for us all’” (17.414–417; see [[tis-speech]], [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[bronze-armored]]). Better that, he says, than to let the Trojans drag the body to their city and win glory (17.417–419).

**Routed.** Zeus shakes the aegis over Ida, “and gave victory to the Trojans, and put the Achaeans to flight” (17.596; see [[aegis]]). Meriones tells Idomeneus that “the power is no longer with the Achaeans” (17.623), and Ajax prays to Zeus to “rescue the sons of the Achaeans from under the mist” (17.645; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]]). The Trojan army shouts “when they saw the Achaeans lifting the corpse” (17.724), and at the end “the young men of the Achaeans / went screaming a cry of death, and forgot the joy of battle” before Aeneas and Hector, like starlings and jackdaws before a falcon (17.755–759; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]] and [[charme]]). Twice they are “the Achaeans dear to Ares”, in the narrator's verse and in Aeneas' echo of it (17.319, 17.336; see [[dear-to-ares]]).
