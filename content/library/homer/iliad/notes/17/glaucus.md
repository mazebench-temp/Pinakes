---
summary: Glaucus rebukes Hector more bitterly than in Book 16. Hector left Sarpedon, his guest-friend and companion, to the Argives and dared not face Ajax; the Lycians will go home unless the Trojans drag Patroclus' body into Ilios and win back Sarpedon's armor and body in exchange. Hector answers that the mind of Zeus is always stronger, and names him among the allies he urges on (17.216). It is Glaucus' last part in the poem.

**The rebuke.** He comes in with the verse that brought him in to kill Iphinous in Book 7, “And Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, leader of the Lycian men” (17.140 = 7.13), and speaks “looking at Hector darkly” (17.141; see [[looking-darkly]]). εἶδος ἄριστε, “best in looks”, is the abuse with which Hector himself began his rebukes of Paris (3.39 = 13.769; see [[best-in-looks]] and [[hector]]), and φύξηλις, “a runaway” (17.143), is found only here. Walter Leaf, noting that the word recurs in Greek only in Lycophron and other learned imitators, hears in its feminine ending the sting of “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (2.235, 7.96; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.143). Much of what he says has been said before. Sarpedon mocked Hector in Book 5 for thinking that he would hold the city “alone, with your brothers-in-law and your brothers” (5.473–474), and warned the Trojans that they might “become the prey and spoil of hostile men” (5.488; see [[sarpedon]]); Glaucus bids Hector consider how he will save the city “alone, with the people who were born in Ilios” (17.145), and says that he left Sarpedon “to become the prey and spoil of the Argives” (17.151), the fate that the wounded Sarpedon begged Hector to spare him: “do not leave me as prey for the Danaans / to lie here” (5.684–685). The complaint of the Lycians, “since there was, it seems, no thanks / for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever” (17.147–148), is in the words of Achilles' refusal to the embassy (9.316–317; see [[achilles]] and [[charis]]).

**Sarpedon.** Glaucus calls Sarpedon Hector's ξεῖνος and ἑταῖρος, “at once your guest-friend and your companion” (17.150; see [[xenia]] and [[hetairos]]), and reproaches him: “And now you did not dare to keep the dogs from him” (17.153; see [[dogs]]). He does not know that Apollo has washed the body and that Sleep and Death have carried it home to Lycia (16.666–683): Walter Leaf notes on the line that “Glaukos knows nothing of the rescue of the body by Apollo” (on 17.151; see the translation's note on 17.163). So he proposes an exchange. If the Trojans dragged Patroclus into Ilios, the Argives would give back Sarpedon's fine armor, “and we could bring his body into Ilios” (17.156–163), for Patroclus was the attendant of the man who “is by far the best / of the Argives beside the ships” (17.164–165; see [[patroclus]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). Otherwise, “if any of the Lycian men will listen to me, / we will go home, and sheer destruction will appear for Troy” (17.154–155; see [[lycians]]). In Book 16 he reproached Hector for forgetting the allies (16.538–540); now he threatens to take his own men away. Last, Hector did not dare to stand against Ajax, “since he is stronger than you” (17.166–168; see [[ajax]]).

**Hector's answer.** “Glaucus, why have you, such a man as you are, spoken arrogantly?” (17.170). ὑπέροπλον ἔειπες, “spoken arrogantly”, is what Poseidon said of Zeus' order to leave the battle (15.185; see [[poseidon]]). Hector had thought that Glaucus “surpassed in wisdom all others / of those who live in deep-soiled Lycia” (17.171–172; see [[lycia]] and [[deep-soiled]]), and he answers that he does not fear battle, but the mind of Zeus is always stronger (17.175–178; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]); let Glaucus stand by him and watch his work (17.179–182). He goes to put on the armor of Achilles, and when he comes back among the allies he names Glaucus second of the ten leaders he urges on, “Mesthles and Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus” (17.216; see [[trojan-allies]]). Walter Leaf finds the mention of Glaucus among the men who are urged on “strange” (on 17.216).

The poem does not name him again. The armor of Sarpedon that he hoped to win back is set out by Achilles as a prize at Patroclus' funeral games (23.798–800; see [[contests]]).
