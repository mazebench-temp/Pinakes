---
summary: Menelaus looks about for Antilochus like the eagle, which, men say, has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven: even from high up it sees the hare crouching under a leafy bush, swoops on it and takes its life (17.673–681).
---

πάντοσε παπταίνων ὥς τʼ αἰετός, ὅν ῥά τέ φασιν / ὀξύτατον δέρκεσθαι ὑπουρανίων πετεηνῶν, “looking about everywhere like an eagle, which, they say, / has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven” (17.674–675; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]). The eagle's sight is one of its superlatives, with the surest of omens and the greatest strength and speed (8.247, 21.253). The simile is about seeing: Menelaus' eyes rove through the crowd of his companions, and they find Antilochus “on the left of all the battle” (17.679–682; see [[menelaus]] and [[antilochus]]). The kill belongs to the bird alone. The hare that the eagle sees from on high is named πτώξ (17.676), “the cowerer” (see [[hare]]).
