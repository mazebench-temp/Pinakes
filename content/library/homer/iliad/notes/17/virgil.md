---
summary: Virgil gives Pallas' funeral a weeping war-horse, Aethon, as the horses of Achilles weep for Patroclus, and when Turnus takes Pallas' sword-belt the poet speaks over the victor's head as Zeus speaks over Hector's when Hector puts on the armor of Achilles: the spoils will cost the man who wears them his life.
---

- The war-horse Aethon weeping in Pallas' funeral procession (*Aeneid* 11.89–92, on 17.426–440): [[virgils-weeping-horse]].
- **The spoils and the doom.** When Hector puts on Achilles' armor, Zeus, speaking to his own heart, addresses him: “Ah, wretch, death is not on your mind at all, / which is now close to you”; he will give him great power for the day, since Hector will not come home for Andromache to receive the armor from him (17.198–208; see [[hector]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). When Turnus has stripped Pallas' sword-belt and exults in the spoil, Virgil speaks in his own voice in the same way: “O mind of men, ignorant of fate and of the lot to come, and of keeping measure when raised up by good fortune! A time will come for Turnus when he will wish he had bought Pallas untouched at a great price, and will hate those spoils and that day” (*Aeneid* 10.500–505). Both speak over the killer's head at the moment he takes the arms of a young man, and both foretell that the arms will cost him his life. Hector dies in the armor he took from Patroclus (22.322–323); Turnus dies when Aeneas sees Pallas' belt on his shoulder (*Aeneid* 12.938–952). Alcimedon's complaint that Hector “wears the armor of the grandson of Aeacus on his own shoulders and glories in it” (17.473) has its counterpart in the Turnus who “exults in the spoil” (*ovat spolio*, *Aeneid* 10.500) and wears “on his shoulders the enemy's badge” (*umeris inimicum insigne gerebat*, *Aeneid* 12.944). Pallas' death as the death of Patroclus is described in the Book 16 note.
