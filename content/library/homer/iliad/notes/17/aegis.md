summary: On Ida Zeus takes up the tasseled aegis himself, covers the mountain in clouds, flashes his lightning and thunders, shakes the aegis, and gives the Trojans the victory. Hector calls him “aegis-bearing” when he answers Glaucus.

**Zeus shakes the aegis.** After the death of Podes, Apollo sends Hector back into the fight (17.582–592; see [[apollo]]), and Zeus himself turns the battle:

{{quote:8c14ffd9-b19a-59fb-b868-2fbc8e162cf6}}

αἰγίδα θυσσανόεσσαν, “the tasseled aegis”, is the phrase in which Zeus gave it to Apollo in Book 15, “and shake it hard over them, and put the Achaean heroes to flight” (15.229–230; see [[tasseled]]); Apollo shook it in the Danaans' faces and bewitched their hearts (15.318–322; see the note on Book 15). Here it is also μαρμαρέη, “gleaming”, the word for the sea in Sleep's terms for Hera's oath (14.273) and, in the next book, for the rim of Achilles' new shield (18.480; see [[marmareos]]). Now Zeus shakes it himself, and it comes with cloud, lightning and thunder (see [[zeus]] and [[ida]]). In τὴν δὲ τίναξε, “and shook it”, the pronoun is the aegis in the manuscripts; Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus read γῆν, “and shook the earth”, which he thinks the better sense, τήν being very weak (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.595). It does what Hephaestus made it for, “to put men to rout” (15.310; see [[hephaestus]] and [[rout]]): Zeus “put the Achaeans to flight” (17.596), and Peneleos is the first to begin the rout (17.597). Agamemnon had promised that Zeus would “himself shake his dark aegis over them all, / in resentment at this deceit” (4.167–168), meaning the Trojans who broke the oaths; here it is shaken over the Achaeans.

**Aegis-bearing.** Hector tells Glaucus that “the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger” (17.176; see [[aegis-bearing]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]), in a verse that the narrator had ended in Book 16 with “than that of men” (16.688).
