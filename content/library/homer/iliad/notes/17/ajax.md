summary: Ajax is the body's bulwark. He comes up with his shield like a tower and Hector gives way; he stands over Patroclus like a lion over its young, kills Hippothous, who was dragging the body by a strap at the ankle, and Phorcys, and keeps the Achaeans packed close around it. When Zeus hides the field in mist he weeps and prays for light, even to die in, and with the son of Oileus he holds back the Trojans while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body away (17.114–139, 17.237–245, 17.274–365, 17.626–655, 17.715–761).

**The shield like a tower.** Menelaus, giving ground from the body, finds him “on the left of all the battle”, encouraging his companions, whom Apollo has put to rout (17.114–118; see [[menelaus]]), and his words stir “the heart of wise-hearted Ajax” (17.123; see [[wise-hearted]]). Hector has stripped Patroclus and is dragging him away to cut off his head and give the body to the Trojan dogs, when “Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower” (17.125–128; see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). The verse is the one with which he came to fight Hector in Book 7 and to rescue Odysseus in Book 11 (7.219, 11.485; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[like-a-tower]]). Hector gives ground at once and springs into his chariot (17.129–131; see [[hector]]). “And Ajax covered the son of Menoetius with his broad shield / and stood like a lion over his young” (17.132–133; see [[patroclus]]): the lion that hunters meet as it leads its cubs through the forest, and that draws down the skin of its brow over its eyes (17.133–136; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]).

**Stronger than Hector.** Glaucus throws it in Hector's face that he could not bring himself to stand against “great-hearted Ajax”, “since he is stronger than you” (17.166–168; see [[glaucus]]). Hector rejects the charge that he “did not stand up to huge Ajax”: he does not shudder at battle, “but the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger” (17.174–176; see [[huge]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]); and he promises half the spoils and fame equal to his own to the ally who drags Patroclus among the Trojans and makes Ajax give way (17.229–232). The allies charge, hoping to drag the body “from under Ajax, son of Telamon, / the fools; truly over it he took the lives of many” (17.235–236; see [[nepios]] and [[telamon]]). In Book 7 the heralds parted Ajax and Hector at nightfall, and in Book 14 Ajax's stone laid Hector in the dust (7.273–282, 14.409–420; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**Fear for their heads.** Alone with Menelaus, Ajax says what he fears: “My dear Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, no longer do I expect / that we two by ourselves will come back from the war” (17.238–239; see the translation's note on 17.239). He fears less for the corpse, “which will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans”, than for his own head and Menelaus', since Hector, “a cloud of war”, covers everything around (17.240–244). In Book 13 Hector had promised that fate to Ajax himself: “and you will glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans” (13.831). Walter Leaf thought the “naïve confession of fear” not unworthy of a Homeric hero: it heightens the glory of what follows (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.241). “But come, call the best of the Danaans, if any may hear” (17.245), and Menelaus calls them (17.246–261).

**After the son of Peleus.** The Trojans push the Achaeans back and drag at the body, but only for a short while, “for very quickly he rallied them, / Ajax, who in looks and in deeds stood out above the rest / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (17.278–280; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). The two verses are the ones with which Odysseus describes Ajax's shade in the underworld (Od. 11.550–551), and the Catalogue had already called him the best of the men “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769). He makes straight through the champions like a wild boar that wheels about in the glens and scatters dogs and young men (17.281–285; see [[like-a-boar]]).

**Hippothous and Phorcys.** Hippothous, son of Lethus the Pelasgian, has tied a strap round the tendons by Patroclus' ankle and is dragging him by the foot, “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans; but quickly upon him / came the evil that no one could keep from him, eager though they were” (17.288–292; see [[hippothous]] and [[pelasgians]]). The verses were last said of Teucer's victim Cleitus, Polydamas' charioteer (15.449–450; see [[cleitus]]). “The son of Telamon, rushing at him through the throng, / struck him at close quarters through the bronze-cheeked helmet” (17.293–294; see [[bronze-cheeked]]), and the brain spurts out along the spear (17.297; see the translation's note on 17.297). The strap (τελαμών, 17.290) and the father's name (Τελαμῶνος, 17.293) stand three verses apart. Hippothous falls on the body, “far from deep-soiled Larisa”, and his end is told in the verses that ended Simoeisius, the first man Ajax kills in the poem: “and he did not repay / his dear parents for his rearing, and short was his life, / brought down by the spear of great-hearted Ajax” (17.300–303; compare 4.477–479; see [[simoeisius]] and [[larisa]]).

Hector's spear, thrown at Ajax, misses narrowly and kills Schedius, the best of the Phocians (17.304–311; see [[schedius]]). Ajax answers by striking Phorcys, son of Phaenops, as he stands over Hippothous; the spear breaks the plate of his corselet and lets out his bowels, and he clutches the earth in his palm, in the verses of Idomeneus' kill of Oenomaus (17.312–315; 17.314–315 = 13.507–508; see [[phorcys]] and [[oenomaus-the-trojan]]). The champions and Hector give ground, and the Argives drag away the two dead men and strip them (17.316–318).

**The ring of shields.** When Apollo has rallied the Trojans, Asteropaeus charges and can do nothing, for the Achaeans stand fenced with their shields around Patroclus (17.352–355; see [[asteropaeus]]). It is Ajax's order: “For Ajax went among them all with many commands; / he would let no one draw back from the body / nor any of the Achaeans fight out in front of the others, / but bade them stand firm around it and fight at close quarters” (17.356–359; see [[battle-order]]). “So huge Ajax commanded” (17.360). The earth is wet with blood, but far fewer of the Danaans die, “for they always remembered / to ward off sheer slaughter from one another in the throng” (17.364–365).

**The prayer for light.** When Zeus shakes the aegis over Ida and routs the Achaeans, “Nor did it escape great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus / that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle” (17.626–627). Ajax speaks first: “Ah, by now even one who is an utter fool / would know that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans” (17.629–630; see [[o-popoi]], [[even-a-fool-can-see]] and [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). Their missiles strike home, “coward or brave man”, while the Achaeans' “fall idly to the ground in vain” (17.631–633). He proposes that they plan both to save the body and to come back alive to their companions, who say that Hector's hands will “be held back” no longer, “but will fall upon the black ships” (17.634–639), the verse of the Trojans' boast that Odysseus reported on the night of the embassy (17.639 = 9.235). He wishes for a messenger to the son of Peleus, “since I do not think he has even heard / the grievous news, that his dear companion has perished” (17.640–642; see [[achilles]]), but the mist covers men and horses alike (17.643–644). Then he prays:

{{quote:90c4b2d4-04db-5ff5-8cf8-9567bbbe92ad}}

He asks for αἴθρη, “clear sky” (17.646; see the translation's note on 17.371), and for death in the light rather than in the mist; Walter Leaf renders the thought “(so it be but) in light, even slay us” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.647; see [[longinus-ajaxs-prayer]]). “So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears, / and at once he scattered the mist and thrust away the fog, / and the sun shone out, and all the battle was seen” (17.648–650; see [[zeus]], [[pity]] and [[shedding-tears]]). In the light he sends Menelaus to look for Antilochus, and to have him tell Achilles “that by far the dearest of his companions has perished” (17.651–655; see [[antilochus]]).

**Of one name.** When Menelaus comes back and says that Achilles cannot fight without armor, Ajax gives the orders for the retreat: “All this you have said fairly, famous Menelaus; / but you and Meriones, getting under it very quickly, / lift the body and carry it out of the toil” (17.716–718; see [[meriones]]). He and the son of Oileus will fight behind them, “with equal spirit, of one name, we who even before now / have withstood sharp Ares, standing side by side” (17.720–721; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Whenever the two turn and stand, the Trojans' color changes and no one dares to fight for the body (17.732–734), and they hold back the battle as a wooded ridge holds back the streams of rivers in flood (17.747–753; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]). Walter Leaf reports a scholium that the cyclic poets imitated the passage in the death of Achilles, where Ajax carries the body and Odysseus covers the retreat (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.719).
