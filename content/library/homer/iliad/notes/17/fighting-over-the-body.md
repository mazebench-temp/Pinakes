summary: The whole book is the fight for Patroclus' body, and it has every part of the type-scene at full length. Menelaus and then Ajax stand over the body; Euphorbus, Hector and Hippothous try to take it, to behead it and to drag it off; the Achaeans hold it in a ring of shields under Zeus' mist while both sides pull it this way and that; and at the end Menelaus and Meriones carry it toward the ships while the two Ajaxes hold back the Trojans.

**Standing over.** Menelaus is the first. He bestrides the body “as a mother stands over her calf, / one that has borne her first, lowing plaintively, and never knew birth before” (17.4–5; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]]), and holds his spear and shield in front of it, “eager to kill whoever came against him”, in the verses of Aeneas over Pandarus (17.7–8 = 5.300–301). He gives way when Hector comes on and comes back with Ajax, who covers the son of Menoetius with his broad shield and stands “like a lion over his young”, with Menelaus on the other side (17.120–139; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]], [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[ajax]]). Twice more Menelaus goes back to stand over the body: after Athena has put the daring of a fly in him, and after he has sent Antilochus to Achilles (17.574, 17.706). On the Trojan side Phorcys is killed as he stands over the fallen Hippothous (17.312–315).

**The attempts.** The book counts the ways a body is taken. Euphorbus claims the body and the bloody spoils because he struck Patroclus first (17.12–17; see [[euphorbus]]). Hector strips the armor and drags the body off, “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.125–127; see [[honoring-the-dead]], [[dogs]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Glaucus wants it as an exchange: if the Trojans dragged it into Ilios, the Argives would give back Sarpedon's armor and body (17.159–163; see [[glaucus]]). Hector offers half the spoils and fame equal to his own to the man who drags it to the Trojans (17.229–232). Hippothous ties a strap around the tendons at the ankle and drags it by the foot through the battle, “to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans”, until Ajax's spear splits his helmet and he falls face down on the body (17.288–303; see [[hippothous]] and [[helmet]]). Each attempt costs a life or a retreat.

**The ring of shields.** The defense becomes a formation. The Achaeans stand around the son of Menoetius “with one heart, / fenced with their bronze-fitted shields” (17.267–268), and Ajax lets no one draw back from the body or fight out in front of the others (17.356–359; see [[battle-order]]). The earth is wet with blood, but fewer Achaeans fall, “for they always remembered / to ward off sheer slaughter from one another in the throng” (17.360–365). Zeus pours mist over the helmets of the men around the body, since he would not let Patroclus become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, and in the mist only the best men fight, while the rest of the battle goes on under a clear sky (17.268–273, 17.366–377; see [[war-and-night]]).

**Both ways.** In the middle of the book the fight is a pull on the body itself. As men stand in a circle and stretch a bull's hide soaked in fat until the fat sinks in, “so they kept pulling the corpse this way and that in a little space, / both sides”, the Trojans hoping to drag it to Ilios and the Achaeans to the ships (17.389–397; see [[the-stretched-hide]]). “Such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus / stretched tight that day, an evil toil” (17.400–401; see [[the-rope-of-war]] and [[ponos]]). The nameless voices of the two armies speak over it, the Achaeans ready to have the earth gape for them rather than give it up, the Trojans ready to die beside it (17.414–423; see [[tis-speech]]).

**The carrying.** At the end the body is lifted. Ajax sends Menelaus and Meriones to get under it and carry it out of the toil while the two Ajaxes fight behind them (17.715–721; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]), and they lift it “very high, with a great effort” (17.722–723). The Trojans charge like hounds at a wounded boar and scatter whenever the Ajaxes turn on them (17.725–734; see [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]]); the bearers go on like mules dragging a beam down a mountain path, with the Ajaxes behind them like a wooded ridge that holds back the streams of a plain, while the war blazes around them like a fire in a city (17.735–753; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]], [[the-wooded-ridge]] and [[the-burning-city]]). The book ends at the ditch, with the young Achaeans fleeing like starlings or jackdaws before a falcon and fine armor falling around the trench (17.755–761; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]). The body is not yet safe: the Achaeans draw it out of the missiles only in the next book, when Achilles has shouted from the trench (18.148–152, 18.231–233). The story of the whole fight is told under [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]].
