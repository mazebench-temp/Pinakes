summary: Patroclus is dead from the second verse, and the whole book is fought over his body. Euphorbus claims it, Hector strips it and drags it off to behead it, Glaucus wants it to ransom Sarpedon's armor, Hippothous drags it by a strap at the ankle, and the two armies stretch it between them like a hide, while Zeus covers the fighters around it in mist; at the end Menelaus and Meriones carry it toward the ships. Zeus, Automedon and Menelaus speak of him as gentle and kind to all (17.1–17, 17.125–163, 17.268–273, 17.288–303, 17.389–401, 17.669–672, 17.717–761).

**The prize.** “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / that Patroclus had been brought down by the Trojans in the combat” (17.1–2). From the second verse he is a body, and the book is the fight for it (see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]], [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Everyone who comes to it has a claim. Euphorbus wants the spoils as the man who struck him first: “for none of the Trojans or their famous allies before me / hit Patroclus with the spear in the fierce battle” (17.14–15; see [[euphorbus]]). Hector, who has stripped him of Achilles' armor, “was dragging him, to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.125–127; see [[hector]] and [[dogs]]), until Ajax comes up with his shield. Glaucus wants the body as a pledge for his own dead: if the Trojans could drag Patroclus into Ilios, “the Argives would quickly release the fine armor of Sarpedon, / and we could bring his body into Ilios” (17.159–163; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]), not knowing that Apollo has already carried Sarpedon home to Lycia (see the translation's note on 17.163). Hector offers half the spoils and equal fame to the ally who drags him among the Trojans (17.229–232). Hippothous ties a strap round the tendons by his ankle and drags him by the foot, until Ajax kills him and he falls face down on the body (17.288–303; see [[hippothous]] and [[ajax]]).

Twice he is left. Menelaus gives ground before Hector and goes to find Ajax, “if we may bring at least his corpse to Achilles, / naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (17.108–122; see [[menelaus]]), and the Achaeans are pushed back from him for a short while, until Ajax rallies them (17.274–280). Ajax stands over him like a lion over its young, and Menelaus on the other side (17.132–139).

**The mist and the hide.** As the Achaeans close around him with their shields, Zeus covers their shining helmets in a thick mist, “since he had not hated the son of Menoetius before, / while he was alive and was the attendant of the grandson of Aeacus; / and he hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, / the Trojan dogs; so he roused his companions to defend him” (17.268–273; see [[zeus]] and [[son-of-menoetius]]). In the mist the best men fight around “the dead son of Menoetius”, while the rest of the battle goes on under a clear sky (17.366–377). The two sides pull the corpse “this way and that in a little space”, as men stand round in a circle and stretch the hide of a bull soaked in fat (17.389–395; see [[the-stretched-hide]]), “such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus / stretched tight that day, an evil toil” (17.400–401). One Achaean says it would be better for the black earth to gape for them all than to give up “this man” to the Trojans, and a Trojan that no one must hang back, even if they all must die “beside this man” (17.414–423; see [[tis-speech]]).

**Not known to his friends.** Two men fighting apart, Thrasymedes and Antilochus, “still thought / that he was alive and fighting the Trojans in the front of the throng” (17.377–383; see [[antilochus]] and [[thrasymedes]]). Achilles does not know either: the battle is far from the ships, and he expects him back alive, for his mother had not told him “that by far the dearest of his companions had perished” (17.401–411; see [[achilles]] and [[thetis]]). Ajax wishes that someone could take him the news (17.640–642), and Menelaus sends Antilochus with it: “the best of the Achaeans has been slain, / Patroclus, and great longing for him has come upon the Danaans” (17.689–690; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Gentle and kind.** The book remembers him in the words of those who loved him. Zeus, watching Hector put on the armor, calls him the “companion, gentle and strong” of the best of men, whose armor Hector took “against all order” (17.203–205; see the translation's note on 17.205). The horses of Achilles weep “in longing for their charioteer” (17.437–439; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Automedon tells Alcimedon that no other Achaean could hold the immortal horses as he could, “except Patroclus, a counselor equal to the gods, / while he lived? But now death and fate have overtaken him” (17.477–478; see [[automedon]]), and when he has killed Aretus he says he has eased his heart a little for “the dead son of Menoetius” (17.538–539). Menelaus tells Athena, in Phoenix's form, that “his death has touched my heart deeply” (17.564), and before he leaves the body he charges the Ajaxes and Meriones:

The last verse is Automedon's (17.672 = 17.478). ἐνηείη, “gentleness”, is found only here (see [[eneeie]]); ἐνηής, “gentle”, Zeus' word for him (17.204), is said of him again by Lycaon and at the gathering of his bones (21.96, 23.252). Πατροκλῆος δειλοῖο, “of poor Patroclus”, ends the verse three times more in Book 23, of his ghost and of the night of mourning for him (23.65, 23.105, 23.221). Walter Leaf, who doubted the passage, thought the gentleness beside the point, since it is a point of honor to rescue the body, and the idea borrowed from 23.252 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.657). Elsewhere in the book he is “noble Patroclus” (17.10, 17.379; see [[blameless]]), “great-hearted” (17.299), “the hero Patroclus” (17.137, 17.706; see [[heros]]), “the trusted companion of noble Achilles” (17.557), and “the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (17.388 = 16.165; see [[grandson-of-aeacus]]).

**Carried.** At the end Ajax sets Menelaus and Meriones to carry him while the two Ajaxes fight behind (17.715–721), “and they took the body up from the ground in their arms / and lifted it very high, with a great effort”, and the Trojans shout when they see it lifted (17.722–724). They carry him toward the ships as mules drag a beam down a mountain path, with the Trojans pressing after them (17.735–761; see [[meriones]], [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[mules-dragging-a-beam]]). In the next book Achilles' shout from the ditch lets the Achaeans draw him out of the missiles and lay him on a bier (18.228–233).
