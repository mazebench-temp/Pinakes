summary: Idomeneus comes with Meriones when Menelaus calls the leaders to the body. In the rout after Zeus thunders from Ida he strikes Hector on the corselet and his spear snaps; Hector's spear, thrown back at him, kills Coeranus, Meriones' charioteer, who had driven up to rescue him, and Idomeneus flees to the ships in fear (17.258–259, 17.605–625).

**At the call.** When Menelaus calls the leaders of the Argives to the body, the son of Oileus comes first, “and after him Idomeneus and Idomeneus' squire, / Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men” (17.258–259; see [[menelaus]], [[meriones]] and [[enyalius]]). The verses are those in which the two came forward to face Hector in Book 7 and followed Diomedes across the ditch in Book 8 (7.165–166, 8.263–264), with τὸν δὲ μετʼ, “after him”, for τοῖσι δʼ ἐπʼ.

**Driven to the ships.** When Zeus shakes the aegis over Ida and gives the victory to the Trojans, the Achaean leaders are struck one after another (17.593–604; see [[zeus]], [[peneleos]] and [[leitus]]). As Hector rushes after Leitus, Idomeneus “hit him on the corselet, on the chest beside the nipple; / but the long spear snapped at the socket” (17.605–607; see [[hector]]), as Meriones' spear snapped on the shield of Deiphobus in Book 13 (13.162; see the translation's note on 13.162, 13.608). Hector throws back at him as he stands in his chariot, and misses him by a little (17.608–609). The spear kills the man who had saved him: Idomeneus had come from the ships on foot, and Coeranus, Meriones' squire and charioteer, had driven up the horses in time, “and he came as a light to him and kept off the pitiless day” (17.610–619; see [[coeranus-of-lyctus]] and the translation's note on 17.612–616). Meriones picks up the fallen reins and tells him to drive for the ships: “you know yourself that the power is no longer with the Achaeans” (17.620–623). “So he spoke, and Idomeneus lashed the fine-maned horses / toward the hollow ships; for fear had fallen on his heart” (17.624–625; see [[deos]] and [[fine-maned]]).

It is his last act in the book. In Book 13, his great day at the ships, the narrator said that his legs were no longer steady, “So in close fighting he kept off the pitiless day, / but his feet no longer carried him nimbly to flee out of the war” (13.514–515); now that he has come into the fight on foot, it is Coeranus' horses that keep the pitiless day from him (17.615), and they carry him to the ships. Meriones stays to carry the body (17.715–746).
