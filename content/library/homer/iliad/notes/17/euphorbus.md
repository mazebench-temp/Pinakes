summary: Euphorbus claims Patroclus' body and spoils from Menelaus as the man who struck him first, and threatens to take Menelaus' head and armor home to his parents in revenge for his brother Hyperenor. His spear bends back on Menelaus' shield, and Menelaus drives his own spear through his neck; he falls like a young olive tree torn up by a storm, his braided hair drenched with blood. Apollo calls him the best of the Trojans, and his death brings Hector back to the body.

**The claim.** “Nor did the son of Panthous, of the good ash spear, neglect / noble Patroclus when he fell” (17.9–10; see [[panthous]]). Walter Leaf notes that ἐϋμμελίης, “of the good ash spear”, is said in this passage of the sons of Panthous (17.9, 17.23, 17.59), and elsewhere in the Iliad only of Priam, in the genitive (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.9; see [[with-good-ash-spear]]). He stands close by the body and speaks to Menelaus, who bestrides it:

{{quote:b49ed2df-61e9-5aab-bc78-5776db2d8e56}}

The claim is true as far as it goes. He was the first to wound Patroclus, from behind, when Apollo had stripped him of his armor (16.806–815; see the note on Book 16), but it was Hector who killed him, and Hector who took the armor (16.818–828, 17.125).

**The quarrel.** Menelaus answers with an appeal to Zeus, “Father Zeus, it is no fine thing to boast so overweeningly” (17.19; see [[menelaus]]): neither leopard nor lion nor boar has such pride “as the pride of the sons of Panthous, of the good ash spear” (17.20–23). He reminds him of Hyperenor, who scorned him and did not go home on his own feet to gladden his wife and parents (17.24–28; see [[hyperenor]]), and warns him off: “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (17.32; see [[nepios]]). Euphorbus answers that Menelaus will now pay “for my brother, whom you killed” (γνωτὸν ἐμόν, 17.35), whose wife he widowed “in the inmost part of her new bridal chamber” and whose parents he left in mourning (17.34–37; see [[marriage]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). His revenge is to be a gift to them: “Surely I would put an end to the lamentation of those wretched ones / if I brought back your head and your armor / and laid them in the hands of Panthous and of brilliant Phrontis” (17.38–40; see [[phrontis]] and [[lament]]). It is the only time the poem names his mother.

**The death.** He stabs at Menelaus' shield, “balanced on every side”, but the bronze does not break through and its point is bent back (17.43–45; see [[balanced-on-every-side]]). Then Menelaus rushes “second with the bronze, / … praying to father Zeus” (17.45–46): the verses of his duel with Paris, when Zeus did not grant him the kill (17.44–46 = 3.348–350; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). This time the spear goes in at the base of the throat as Euphorbus gives ground, “and straight through the soft neck went the point” (17.47–49), “and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him” (17.50; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]).

**The hair.** “His hair was drenched with blood, hair like the Graces', / and his braids, which were bound tight with gold and silver” (17.51–52; see [[hair]], [[graces]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus is said to have read χαρίτεσσι μέλαιναι, and a scholium that the Macedonians and Cypriots give the name χάριτες to twisted, curly myrtle (on 17.51). He explains the braids, after Helbig, as locks pinched together by little spirals of gold or silver, such as have been found beside the skull in graves in Etruria, in Greece (Olympia, Boeotia, Mycenae) and at Hissarlik, and compares the Carian of the Catalogue who “came to the war wearing gold, like a girl” (2.872; on 17.52; see [[like-a-girl]]). The young man falls like a flourishing olive sapling, reared with care in a lonely place, white with blossom, which a sudden storm wrenches from its trench and stretches on the ground (17.53–58; see [[the-olive-sapling]]): “such was the son of Panthous, Euphorbus of the good ash spear, / whom Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed, and began to strip of his armor” (17.59–60).

**The best of the Trojans.** Menelaus “would easily have carried off the glorious armor of the son of Panthous” (17.70) had not Apollo roused Hector, telling him that Menelaus had killed “the best of the Trojans, / Euphorbus, son of Panthous” (17.80–81; see [[apollo]] and [[hector]]). Hector sees Menelaus taking the armor, “and the other on the ground, / lying; and the blood was flowing from the stab wound” (17.85–86): κατʼ οὐταμένην ὠτειλήν, the words of Hyperenor's death, when his soul rushed out through the wound (14.518). The armor is not taken. Menelaus gives way before Hector and leaves Patroclus' body as well (17.106–113), and the poem says no more of Euphorbus.
