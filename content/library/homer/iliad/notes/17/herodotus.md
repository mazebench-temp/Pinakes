---
summary: Herodotus has the Paeonians of the Strymon tell Darius that they are colonists of the Teucrians from Troy, and brings Xerxes from Priam's Pergamon to Abydos, the home of Phaenops son of Asius, where the king watches his army and fleet, calls himself blessed, and weeps for the shortness of human life.
---

- **Paeonians from Troy.** Apisaon, son of Hippasus, came “from deep-soiled Paeonia”, and after Asteropaeus was the best in the fighting (17.350–351; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]] and [[asteropaeus]]). Herodotus tells how two Paeonians, Pigres and Mantyes, who wished to rule their people, came to Sardis with their tall and handsome sister and sent her past Darius carrying water on her head, leading a horse and spinning flax all at once. When Darius asked who the Paeonians were, they told him that Paeonia lay on the river Strymon, not far from the Hellespont, and that they were colonists of the Teucrians from Troy (Τευκρῶν τῶν ἐκ Τροίης ἄποικοι, *Histories* 5.12–13), and Darius had Megabazus move the Paeonians to Asia (5.14). Among the great expeditions of the past he also counts one of the Mysians and Teucrians before the Trojan War, who crossed into Europe by the Bosporus, subdued all the Thracians and came down to the Ionian Sea and south as far as the river Peneus (*Histories* 7.20.2; see the Book 13 note). In the Iliad the Paeonians fight for Troy as allies who have come from the Axius (2.848–850; see [[paeonians]] and [[paeonia]]).
- **Xerxes at Abydos.** Phaenops, son of Asius, “had his home at Abydos” (17.584; see [[phaenops-son-of-asius]]). Herodotus brings Xerxes there by way of Troy. When the army reached the Scamander, the first river that ran dry for it, Xerxes went up to the Pergamon of Priam, wishing to see it, learned the story of each thing there, sacrificed a thousand cattle to Athena of Ilium, and the Magi poured libations to the heroes; that night a panic fell on the camp. At daybreak he marched on, with Rhoeteum, Ophryneum and Dardanus, which borders on Abydos, on his left, and the Gergithae Teucrians on his right (*Histories* 7.43). At Abydos he sat on a throne of white stone set up for him on a hill and watched his land army and his ships, and a race of the ships. When he saw the whole Hellespont hidden by ships and the shores and plains of Abydos full of men, he called himself blessed, and then wept: pity had come over him, he told Artabanus, as he reckoned how short all human life is, for not one of so many men would be alive in a hundred years (7.44–46). Compare Zeus' pity for the horses of Achilles and his words on the misery of men (17.441–447; see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]). See [[abydos]] and [[troy]].
