---
summary: Glaucus, “leader of the Lycian men”, threatens to take the Lycians home, since Hector left the body of their king to the Argives (17.140, 17.146–155), and Hector calls on the “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians” to hold while he puts on the armor of Achilles (17.184). After this book the poem does not name the Lycians again.
---

Glaucus speaks for them: “for none of the Lycians at least will go out to fight the Danaans / for the sake of the city, since there was, it seems, no thanks / for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever” (17.146–148), and “So now, if any of the Lycian men will listen to me, / we will go home, and sheer destruction will appear for Troy” (17.154–155; see [[glaucus]] and [[trojan-allies]]). The grievance is Sarpedon's body, which Hector left “to become the prey and spoil of the Argives” (17.150–153; see [[sarpedon]]). In Book 16 the Lycians themselves fled from their dead king when Zeus turned Hector to flight (16.659–661), and Glaucus' complaint about thanks is in the words of Achilles' refusal to fight for Agamemnon (9.316–317; see [[achilles]] and [[charis]]). When he has answered Glaucus, Hector rallies the army with the cry of earlier books, “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand” (17.184; see [[hector]] and [[dardanians]]), and he names Glaucus among the leaders of the allies whom he urges on (17.216). The threat to go home is not taken up again. The Lycians are not named in the rest of the poem, nor Glaucus, nor Lycia.
