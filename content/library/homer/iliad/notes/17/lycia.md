---
summary: Hector tells Glaucus that he had thought him wiser than all who live in “deep-soiled Lycia” (17.172). It is the last time the poem names the land.
---

τῶν ὅσσοι Λυκίην ἐριβώλακα ναιετάουσι, “of those who live in deep-soiled Lycia” (17.172; see [[deep-soiled]]): Hector turns Glaucus' rebuke back on him, “Ah, truly I thought that you surpassed in wisdom all others / of those who live in deep-soiled Lycia; / but now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said” (17.171–173; see [[glaucus]] and [[hector]]). Glaucus had threatened to take the Lycians home (17.154–155; see [[lycians]]). He does not know that Sarpedon is already there, set down by Sleep and Death “in the rich land of wide Lycia” in Book 16 (16.673–683; see [[sarpedon]]). After this book the Iliad does not name Lycia again.
