summary: Antilochus and his brother Thrasymedes, fighting apart as Nestor had ordered, do not know that Patroclus is dead. When Zeus has cleared the mist, Ajax sends Menelaus to find him; Menelaus gives him the news and bids him run to Achilles, and he stands speechless, his eyes full of tears, then leaves his armor with Laodocus and runs weeping to the ships (17.377–383, 17.651–704).

**Not yet told.** In the mist around the body the best men are worn down, “but two men had not yet learned, / two glorious men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus, / that noble Patroclus was dead” (17.377–379; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[patroclus]]). They are fighting apart, on the watch against the death and the panic of their companions, “since so Nestor had commanded / urging them on to war from the black ships” (17.381–383; see [[nestor]] and the translation's note on 17.383, 17.457, 17.579, 17.750). Walter Leaf took the verses as meant to prepare the way for his errand later in the book (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.377).

**The messenger.** When Zeus has scattered the mist, Ajax tells Menelaus to look for Antilochus, “son of great-hearted Nestor, still alive, / and urge him to go quickly to wise-hearted Achilles / and tell him that by far the dearest of his companions has perished” (17.653–655; see [[ajax]] and [[menelaus]]). Leaf reasons that Antilochus is chosen as a friend of Achilles and a good runner, as the Odyssey remembers him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.653; Od. 4.202). Menelaus finds him “on the left of all the battle, / encouraging his companions and urging them on to fight” (17.682–683), the verses in which he found Ajax (17.116–117), and tells him: “Antilochus, come here, nurtured by Zeus, so that you may hear / grievous news, which I wish had never been” (17.685–686). Patroclus, “the best of the Achaeans”, has been slain, and Antilochus is to run to Achilles so that he may save the body, “naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (17.689–693; see [[messenger-scene]]).

**Speechless.** “So he spoke, and Antilochus shuddered when he heard the word; / and for a long time speechlessness took him, and his two eyes / filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked” (17.694–696; see [[tears]]). The last two verses are Penelope's in the Odyssey, when she learns that Telemachus has sailed for Pylos (Od. 4.704–705), and the second returns in Book 23 for Eumelus, thrown from his chariot in the race (23.397). “But even so he did not neglect the command of Menelaus” (17.697; see [[ephemosyne]]): he leaves his armor with his companion Laodocus, who is wheeling his horses close by, and runs (17.698–699; see [[laodocus-companion-of-antilochus]]). “And his feet carried him out of the war, shedding tears, / to bring the evil news to Achilles, son of Peleus” (17.700–701; see [[achilles]] and [[shedding-tears]]).

**Missed by the Pylians.** The narrator turns to Menelaus: his heart did not wish to help the hard-pressed companions “from whom he had gone, / Antilochus, and great longing for him came upon the Pylians” (17.703–704; see [[pylians]]). μεγάλη δὲ ποθή, “great longing”, is what Menelaus has just said the Danaans feel for Patroclus (17.690). Menelaus sends Thrasymedes to lead them and goes back to the body, and tells the Ajaxes that he does not think Achilles will come (17.705–711). In the next book Antilochus reaches Achilles, “shedding hot tears”, and gives him the news in Menelaus' words, the grievous news that ought never to have been and the naked body whose armor Hector has (18.16–21; 17.686, 17.693).
