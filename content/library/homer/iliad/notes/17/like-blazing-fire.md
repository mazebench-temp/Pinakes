---
summary: “So they fought on like blazing fire” closes the struggle around the body of Patroclus and turns to the mist that covers it: “and you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–369).
passages:
  - 17.366-383 | Mist over the body, sun over the battle
---

ὣς οἳ μὲν μάρναντο δέμας πυρός, οὐδέ κε φαίης / οὔτέ ποτʼ ἠέλιον σῶν ἔμμεναι οὔτε σελήνην, “So they fought on like blazing fire; and you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–367). The verse of transition is changed here. Elsewhere it is whole, δέμας πυρὸς αἰθομένοιο, and its μέν looks to a δέ that turns the story away from the fighting (11.596, 13.673; see the notes on those books). Here the epithet gives way to a new thought, and before the story turns to the rest of the battle (οἳ δʼ ἄλλοι, 17.370) it looks more closely at the fight around the body, a fire that burns in the dark. The narrator speaks to the listener, οὐδέ κε φαίης, “you would not have said”, as if he could have stood there, and gives the reason: “for they were covered in mist, in all that part of the battle where the best men / stood around the dead son of Menoetius” (17.368–369; see the translation's note on 17.368 and [[zeus]]).

The mist holds only around the body. The rest of the Trojans and Achaeans “fought at their ease under the upper air, and the sharp light / of the sun was spread over them” (17.371–372), until Zeus scatters the mist at Ajax's prayer “and the sun shone out, and all the battle was seen” (17.645–650; see [[helios]]). In the next book the whole verse returns at the start, as Antilochus runs to Achilles with the news (18.1–2).
