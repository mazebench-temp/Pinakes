---
summary: Aeacus is named six times through his grandson Achilles, always in the genitive Αἰακίδαο, “of the grandson of Aeacus”: of his horses, of his attendant Patroclus, and of his armor on Hector's shoulders (17.76, 17.271, 17.388, 17.426, 17.473, 17.486).
---

Αἰακίδαο, “of the grandson of Aeacus”, is always Achilles (see [[grandson-of-aeacus]] and [[achilles]]). Three times it names the owner of the immortal horses: Apollo calls them “the horses of the wise-hearted grandson of Aeacus” (17.76), the narrator turns to them weeping, “But the horses of the grandson of Aeacus, standing apart from the battle” (17.426), and Hector sees “the two horses of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” come into the battle with poor charioteers (17.486; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Twice it is Patroclus' master: Zeus had not hated Patroclus “while he was alive and was the attendant of the grandson of Aeacus” (17.271), and the armies fight “around the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (17.388 = 16.165; see [[patroclus]]). Once it is the armor: Hector “wears the armor of the grandson of Aeacus on his own shoulders and glories in it” (17.473; see [[alcimedon]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]).
