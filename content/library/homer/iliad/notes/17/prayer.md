summary: Four prayers, all answered. Menelaus prays to Zeus as he thrusts at Euphorbus, and Automedon as Hector and Aeneas come at him, and each kills his man (17.46–50, 17.498–524); Menelaus' wish for Athena's help is heard by Athena as a prayer to her first of all the gods (17.561–573); and Ajax prays to Zeus to lift the mist, and Zeus pities his tears and does it (17.645–650).
# εὐχόμενος (17.537): Automedon speaks "in triumph" over Aretus, not in prayer.

**Before the throw.** Menelaus answers Euphorbus' spear “praying to father Zeus” (ἐπευξάμενος Διὶ πατρί, 17.46), and his spear goes through the young man's neck (17.47–50; see [[euphorbus]]). Automedon, with Hector and Aeneas coming to take the horses, “prayed to father Zeus, / and his heart, dark on every side, was filled with courage and strength” (17.498–499); his spear kills Aretus (17.516–524). Neither prayer is spoken. What they asked is shown by what follows, and Automedon's triumph over the dead man is a speech of another kind (17.537–539; see [[euchomai]]).

**Athena.** Athena, in the likeness of Phoenix, tells Menelaus to stand firm over Patroclus (17.555–559; see [[katepheie]]). He answers with a wish, not addressed to her: “Phoenix, old father, aged one, if only Athena / would give me strength, and keep off the force of the missiles!” (17.561–562). The goddess “was glad / that he had prayed to her first of all the gods” (17.567–568), and puts strength into his shoulders and knees and the daring of a fly into his breast (17.569–573; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]], [[tharsos]] and [[gounata]]). A wish naming a god is heard as a prayer, and the god who hears it is the one standing beside him unknown.

**Ajax.** The prayer for light is the last in the book. It has no claim on the god, no promise of sacrifice and no reminder of past favors, only the request, and a request to die where men can see:

{{quote:90c4b2d4-04db-5ff5-8cf8-9567bbbe92ad}}

Zeus “pitied him as he shed tears” (17.648), scatters the mist, and the sun shines on the whole battle (17.649–650; see [[pity]] and [[war-and-night]]). The answer is all that was asked: the light is given, and the Trojans keep the victory (see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). For the prayer in later criticism, see [[longinus-ajaxs-prayer]].
