summary: Book 17 is Menelaus' aristeia, and its prize is the body of Patroclus. He is the first to stand over it and kills Euphorbus; he gives way to Hector and comes back with Ajax; he calls the leaders to the body, and with the daring of a fly that Athena puts in him kills Podes; he sends Antilochus to Achilles with the news, and with Meriones carries the body out of the battle while the two Ajaxes hold off the Trojans (17.1–139, 17.237–261, 17.553–590, 17.651–746).

**Over the body.** “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / that Patroclus had been brought down by the Trojans in the combat” (17.1–2; see [[dear-to-ares]], [[patroclus]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). He is the first to reach the body, “and bestrode him, as a mother stands over her calf, / one that has borne her first, lowing plaintively, and never knew birth before” (17.4–5; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]], the translation's note on 17.4–5 and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The next two verses, the spear and the shield held out in front, “eager to kill whoever came against him” (17.7–8), are those of Aeneas over the body of Pandarus (5.300–301; see [[aeneas]]); but Aeneas bestrode his friend “like a lion trusting in his courage” (5.299), and Menelaus is given a mother's anxious care.

**Euphorbus.** Euphorbus, son of Panthous, who struck Patroclus first, claims the body and the spoils as his own and tells him to “draw back, and leave the body, and let the bloody spoils be” (17.9–17; see [[euphorbus]] and [[panthous]]). He greets him with the full address, Ἀτρεΐδη Μενέλαε διοτρεφὲς ὄρχαμε λαῶν, “Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people” (17.12), which the Iliad uses only here; in the Odyssey Telemachus and Peisistratus use it to him in his house at Sparta (Od. 4.156, 4.291, 4.316, 15.64, 15.87). “Deeply troubled” (17.18; see [[deeply-troubled]]), Menelaus cries out to Zeus, “Father Zeus, it is no fine thing to boast so overweeningly” (17.19), and sets the pride of the sons of Panthous above the might of leopard, lion and wild boar (17.20–23; see [[leopard]], [[lion]] and [[boar]]). Then he names the brother he killed: “Nor indeed did mighty Hyperenor, tamer of horses, / have joy of his youth, when he scorned me and stood up to me / and said that I was the most despised warrior among the Danaans” (17.24–26; see [[hyperenor]]). The killing was told in four verses in Book 14, and nothing there tells of the scorn (14.516–519); Walter Leaf allows that Menelaus may “assume it for rhetorical purposes” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.24). The end of his warning, from “no, I bid you draw back” to the proverb “a fool learns a thing once it is done”, Achilles will say to Aeneas in the same words (17.30–32 ≈ 20.196–198; see [[a-fool-learns-once-it-is-done]] and [[nepios]]); Hesiod has the proverb as παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “having suffered, the fool learns” (*Works and Days* 218; see [[hesiod]]).

Euphorbus, threatening to bring Menelaus' head and armor home to his parents, stabs at his shield, and the point bends back (17.34–45). The next verses are those of the duel with Paris in Book 3, when Paris' point bent back in the same shield: “And then he rushed second with the bronze, / Menelaus, son of Atreus, praying to father Zeus” (17.44–46 ≈ 3.348–350; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]] and [[prayer]]). This time the prayer is not reported, and the stroke goes home. He stabs Euphorbus at the base of the throat, “and leaned his own weight on it, trusting in his heavy hand” (17.47–48), the verse of Iphidamas' thrust at Agamemnon's belt (11.235). Euphorbus falls “with a thud”, his hair like the Graces' wet with blood, like an olive sapling a storm has torn from its trench (17.50–58; see [[fell-with-a-thud]], [[graces]] and [[the-olive-sapling]]), and Menelaus begins to strip him (17.60). The Trojans keep away from him as dogs and herdsmen keep off a lion that has seized the best cow of a herd, “so no one of them had the heart in his breast to dare / to come against glorious Menelaus” (17.61–69; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

**Against a god.** “Then he would easily have carried off the glorious armor of the son of Panthous, / he, the son of Atreus, had not Phoebus Apollo begrudged him that” (17.70–71; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[apollo]]). Apollo turns Hector from his chase of Achilles' horses with the news that “warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus, / standing over Patroclus, has killed the best of the Trojans” (17.79–80; see [[warlike]] and [[hector]]), and Hector comes through the champions “shouting shrilly, like the flame of Hephaestus, / unquenchable” (17.88–89; see [[like-a-flame]]). Menelaus debates with his own heart:

{{quote:3f2a538d-68b0-568c-a0cc-224195019bef}}

He weighs blame against shame. If he leaves the armor and Patroclus, “who lies here to win satisfaction for me” (ἐμῆς ἕνεκʼ … τιμῆς, 17.92), the Danaans will be indignant (see [[nemesis]]); the war is fought to win τιμή for him from the Trojans (1.159; see [[time]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). If he fights alone “out of shame” (αἰδεσθείς, 17.95; see [[aidos]]), he will be surrounded. The verse that breaks off the debate, “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (17.97), comes in four other speeches of decision: Odysseus', alone among the Trojans, who decides to stand (11.407), Agenor's and Hector's before Achilles (21.562, 22.122), and Achilles' over the dead Hector, when he turns from attacking the city to the unburied Patroclus (22.385–387; see [[decision-scene]]). Menelaus decides the other way from Odysseus, by a maxim: no one fights a man whom a god honors without a great bane rolling down on him, and no Danaan will blame him for giving way to Hector, “since he fights with help from the gods” (17.98–101; see [[retreat]] and [[daimon]]). The best of evils is to find Ajax and drag away the body for Achilles “even against a god's will” (17.102–105).

**The bearded lion.** He gives ground “turning back again and again, like a bearded lion” that dogs and men drive from the steading, until his valiant heart grows chill (17.108–113). Walter Leaf compares a painted plate from Rhodes in the British Museum, on which Menelaus and Hector fight over the body of Euphorbus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.108). ἐντροπαλιζόμενος, “turning back again and again”, is the word of Ajax's slow retreat in Book 11 (11.547; see [[ajax]]). He finds Ajax “on the left of all the battle” (17.116), and asks him to come, “if we may bring at least his corpse to Achilles, / naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (17.121–122). Hector, who was dragging the body “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127), gives way to Ajax's shield, and the two stand over Patroclus, Ajax like a lion over its young, “And on the other side the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / stood, nursing great grief in his breast” (17.128–139; see [[penthos]]).

**The call to the leaders.** When Hector brings the allies on, Ajax tells him that he fears less for the corpse than for their own heads, and asks him to call the best of the Danaans (17.237–245). “And he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Danaans: / ‘Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives’” (17.247–248): the two verses are the wounded Agamemnon's and Eurypylus' calls for help in Book 11 (11.275–276 = 11.586–587). He names the leaders as men who “beside the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus, / drink at the public cost” (17.249–250; see [[agamemnon]] and the translation's note on 17.249–251). He cannot find each man in the strife, “but let each come of himself, and be indignant in his heart / that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.254–255; see [[dogs]]). The son of Oileus comes first, then Idomeneus and Meriones, and others whose names no one could tell (17.256–261; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]).

**The daring of a fly.** When the mind of Zeus turns, Athena comes down in a purple cloud and goes first to Menelaus, “for he was close to her”, “in the likeness of Phoenix in form and in tireless voice” (17.544–555; see [[athena]] and [[phoenix]]). “To you, Menelaus, it will be a shame and a reproach / if the trusted companion of noble Achilles / is torn by swift dogs under the wall of the Trojans. / But hold firm, and urge on the whole army” (17.556–559). Her words are the dying Sarpedon's to Glaucus: “For I will be a shame and a reproach to you hereafter” and “But hold firm, and urge on the whole army” (16.498, 16.501; see [[sarpedon]], [[katepheie]] and [[oneidos]]). Menelaus answers the old man: “Phoenix, old father, aged one, if only Athena / would give me strength, and keep off the force of the missiles!” (17.561–562). Patroclus' death “has touched my heart deeply” (17.564), but Hector has the might of fire, “for Zeus grants him glory” (17.566). The goddess is glad “that he had prayed to her first of all the gods” (17.568): without knowing it, he has prayed to the goddess in front of him (see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

She puts strength in his shoulders and knees, “and put in his breast the daring of a fly, / which, however much it is driven from a man's skin, / keeps on trying to bite, and human blood is sweet to it” (17.570–572; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]] and [[tharsos]]). In Book 4 the same goddess kept Pandarus' arrow from his flesh “as a mother / brushes a fly from her child” (4.130–131); the verb is the same, ἔεργεν there and ἐργομένη here (see [[mother-and-fly]]). Now the fly is in him. He goes to stand over Patroclus, and his spear strikes Podes, son of Eetion, Hector's “dear companion at the feast”, on the belt “as he darted into flight”; he drags the body “into the crowd of his companions” (17.574–581; see the translation's note on 17.383, 17.457, 17.579, 17.750). Apollo, in the likeness of Phaenops, taunts Hector with it: “now that you have shrunk back before Menelaus, who before now at least / was a soft spearman?” (17.587–588). μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής, “a soft spearman”, is found only here in Homer. It is an enemy's word, and the ancient critic Aristonicus noted that the poet himself calls Menelaus “dear to Ares”; Walter Leaf, reporting the note, adds that Menelaus is often spoken of slightingly, “though in action he always proves better than his reputation” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.588). The narrator himself judged Hector “far the mightier” when Menelaus offered to face him in Book 7 (7.104–105). Plato's Socrates took the phrase as Homer's own judgment: Homer made Menelaus “a soft spearman”, and then had him come unbidden to the feast of his better, Agamemnon (*Symposium* 174b–c; 2.408; see [[plato-menelaus-uninvited]]).

**The search for Antilochus.** “Nor did it escape great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus / that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle” (17.626–627; see [[that-turns-the-battle]]): the verse that opened the book with Menelaus alone now joins him to Ajax. Ajax tells him that someone must take the news to Achilles, and when Zeus has scattered the mist, sends him to look for Antilochus (17.628–655; see [[ajax]]). “So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war cry, did not disobey” (17.656 = 17.246), “and he went, like a lion from a farmyard” (17.657). The rest of the simile is Ajax's in Book 11, the lion that wants the fattest of the cattle and goes off at dawn, held back by the watchers' javelins and torches (17.659–664 = 11.550–555; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]), and so is the verse that follows it, “very unwillingly; for he feared greatly” (17.666; compare 11.557): Ajax feared for the ships, Menelaus for the body, that the Achaeans “in grievous rout would leave him as a prey to the enemy” (17.666–667).

Before he goes he charges the Ajaxes and Meriones: “now let each remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus; / for he knew how to be kind to all / while he lived; but now death and fate have overtaken him” (17.670–672; see [[patroclus]]). ἐνηείη, “gentleness”, occurs only here in Homer (see [[eneeie]]); Zeus called Patroclus ἐνηής, “gentle”, when Hector put on the armor (17.204), and the adjective is given to him again in later books (21.96, 23.252). Walter Leaf doubted the whole passage (17.657–673): the simile is borrowed from Book 11 and is pointless for a man who goes at Ajax's request and is not driven off, and the gentleness of Patroclus seemed to him beside the point, the idea taken from 23.252 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.657). He goes “looking about everywhere like an eagle, which, they say, / has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven” (17.674–675; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]), and the narrator turns to him: “So then, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, your shining eyes / roved everywhere through the crowd of your many companions” (17.679–680). He is addressed so seven times in the poem, twice in this book (17.679, 17.702; see [[apostrophe]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]).

He finds Antilochus “on the left of all the battle”, in the verses in which he found Ajax (17.682–683 = 17.116–117), and gives him the news:

{{quote:83961bf1-7db7-59cb-9237-b0f9f2b6e809}}

The message names Patroclus “the best of the Achaeans” (17.689; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and ends as his first plea to Ajax ended, with the naked body and Hector in its armor (17.693 = 17.122). “Great longing for him” is what Menelaus feared for Odysseus when he was cut off in Book 11, “and that great longing for him may come upon the Danaans” (11.471). Antilochus is struck speechless and runs to Achilles weeping, “But even so he did not neglect the command of Menelaus” (17.694–701; see [[antilochus]] and [[ephemosyne]]).

**The body carried.** The narrator turns to him again: “Nor did your heart, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, wish / to help the hard-pressed companions from whom he had gone” (17.702–703). He sends Thrasymedes to the Pylians and goes back to stand over Patroclus (17.705–707; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[pylians]]). Achilles will not come, he tells the Ajaxes, “for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans” (17.711), and he repeats Ajax's words, “But let us ourselves consider the best plan, / both how we may drag away the body” (17.712–713 ≈ 17.634–635). “All this you have said fairly, famous Menelaus” (17.716): the vocative ἀγακλεές, “famous”, is given in Homer only to him and to Hephaestus (21.379; see [[famous]]). Ajax sets him and Meriones to carry the body while the two Ajaxes fight behind (17.717–721; see [[meriones]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]), “and they took the body up from the ground in their arms / and lifted it very high, with a great effort” (17.722–723). They carry it toward the ships as mules drag a beam or a ship's timber down a mountain path, sweating under the load, while the war burns around them like a fire in a city (17.735–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]] and [[the-burning-city]]). The book ends before they reach the ships; in the next, Achilles' shout from the ditch lets the Achaeans draw the body out of the missiles and lay it on a bier (18.228–233).

**His aristeia.** The book's traditional title is Μενελάου ἀριστεία, “the aristeia of Menelaus” (see [[the-aristeia]] and [[title-of-book-17]]). It has the prayers and the god's gift of strength (17.46, 17.561–573), but only two kills, Euphorbus and Podes, and twice he leaves the body. His work is the body: he stands over it, leaves it and comes back, calls others to it, sends the news of it, and carries it. His titles are his usual ones: ξανθός, “fair-haired”, seven times (17.6, 17.18, 17.113, 17.124, 17.578, 17.673, 17.684; see [[fair-haired]]), βοὴν ἀγαθός, “good at the war cry” (17.237, 17.246, 17.560, 17.651, 17.656, 17.665; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]]), ἀρηΐφιλος, “dear to Ares” (17.1, 17.11, 17.138), and in address διοτρεφής, “nurtured by Zeus” (17.12, 17.34, 17.238, 17.652, 17.679, 17.702).
