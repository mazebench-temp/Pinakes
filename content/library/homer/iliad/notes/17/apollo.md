---
summary: Apollo keeps the Trojans in the fight for the body of Patroclus. Three times he comes to them in the likeness of a man they trust, and three times they come on again: as Mentes, leader of the Cicones, he turns Hector from the horses of Achilles back to Menelaus; as the old herald Periphas he shames Aeneas, who knows him; as Phaenops, son of Asius, he taunts Hector for shrinking from Menelaus.

**As Mentes.** Menelaus has killed Euphorbus, and no Trojan dares come against him: “Then he would easily have carried off the glorious armor of the son of Panthous, / he, the son of Atreus, had not Phoebus Apollo begrudged him that” (17.70–71; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[euphorbus]] and [[menelaus]]). ἄγαμαι is “wonder at”, and of a god “begrudge”, as when Menelaus in the Odyssey says that a god must have begrudged Odysseus his homecoming (*Odyssey* 4.181–182), and Penelope that the gods begrudged her and Odysseus their youth together (*Odyssey* 23.210–212). Apollo rouses Hector “in the likeness of a man, Mentes, the leader of the Cicones” (17.73; see [[mentes]] and [[cicones]]). The Catalogue names Euphemus as leader of the Cicones (2.846), and Walter Leaf reports that some therefore wrote Peiros, the Thracian of 4.520 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.73); in the Odyssey Athena comes to Telemachus in the likeness of another Mentes, leader of the Taphians (*Odyssey* 1.105). Hector is “chasing what cannot be caught, / the horses of the wise-hearted grandson of Aeacus” (17.75–76), and the god warns him in the words of Odysseus to Dolon, who had been promised the same horses: they are “hard / for mortal men to master and to drive, / for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (17.76–78 = 10.402–404; see [[dolon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Menelaus meanwhile has killed “the best of the Trojans, / Euphorbus, son of Panthous” (17.80–81). “So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men” (17.82), the verse with which Poseidon left Idomeneus (13.239) and Apollo left Hector in Book 16 (16.726). Hector, in dread grief, turns on Menelaus, shouting “like the flame of Hephaestus” (17.83–89; see [[hector]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**A rout.** When Menelaus looks for Ajax, he finds him on the left of the battle encouraging his companions, “for Phoebus Apollo had cast a heaven-sent rout among them” (17.118; see [[ajax]] and [[phobos]]). The rout is not told; it is given as the reason for Ajax's work. In Book 15 the god routed the Achaeans with the aegis, “for Apollo / sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (15.326–327; see [[aegis]]), and in Book 16 he “sent evil turmoil among the Argives” when he had roused Hector against Patroclus (16.729).

**As Periphas.** After Ajax has killed Hippothous and Phorcys, the Trojans would have gone back up into Ilios, and the Argives would have won glory “even beyond the portion of Zeus” (17.319–322; see [[aisa]]), “but Apollo himself / urged on Aeneas, in form like Periphas, / the herald, son of Epytus” (17.322–324; see [[periphas-son-of-epytus]], [[epytus]] and [[aeneas]]). The likeness is chosen for the man addressed: Periphas had grown old as a herald beside Anchises, “a man whose mind was kindly toward him” (17.324–325; see [[anchises]] and [[heralds]]). “In his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him” (17.326) is the verse with which he spoke to Hector as Asius (16.720) and will speak to Aeneas as Lycaon (20.82; see [[son-of-zeus]]):

{{quote:5cb5169f-d2c5-5385-a425-c815a450b798}}

Other men, he says, have defended their cities even against a god, trusting in their own strength and numbers; the Trojans have Zeus on their side, and still they do not fight. Walter Leaf takes ὑπὲρ θεόν, “in defiance of a god”, as the narrator's ὑπὲρ Διὸς αἶσαν, “beyond the portion of Zeus” (on 17.327; see the translation's notes on 17.327, 17.321 and on 17.330). “So he spoke, and Aeneas knew Apollo who shoots from afar / as he looked him in the face” (17.333–334; see [[hekatebolos]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The lesser Ajax knew Poseidon in the likeness of Calchas from behind, by his feet and legs as he went away (13.66–72; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]); Aeneas knows the god face to face. To the Trojans he does not name him: “one of the gods has come and stood close beside me” (17.338), and he passes on the god's word, that Zeus is still their helper (17.339; see [[zeus]]). The Trojans rally and stand facing the Achaeans (17.342–343).

**As Phaenops.** Filled by Athena with the daring of a fly, Menelaus kills Podes, son of Eetion, Hector's dear companion at the feast, and drags his body away (17.567–581; see [[athena]]). “And Apollo stood close beside Hector and urged him on, / like Phaenops, son of Asius, who of all / his guest-friends was dearest to him, and had his home at Abydos” (17.582–584; see [[phaenops-son-of-asius]], [[abydos]] and [[xenia]]). This time the god taunts:

{{quote:6b49c58d-2fef-5301-9d1b-104baeb33e79}}

μαλθακός, “soft”, is found nowhere else in Homer. “So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him” (17.591), the verse that tells Achilles' grief when Antilochus brings him the news (18.22; see [[achos]]). Hector goes through the champions again (17.592), and Zeus takes up the aegis and gives the Trojans the victory (17.593–596; see [[zeus]]). Walter Leaf reports a scholium according to which Zenodotus wrote that Ares, not Apollo, came and roused Hector here, and which asks how Ares came to be there so suddenly; Leaf finds the objection just, since Ares has not been on the battlefield since the end of Book 5 and was kept from returning in Book 15 (15.142), while Apollo has been in the fighting again and again, most recently at 17.322–332 (on 17.582; see [[zenodotus]]). The verse with which the god speaks, repeated from 17.326, is missing in several manuscripts and was unknown to Zenodotus (on 17.585).

**The Trojans' god.** Apollo strikes no one in this book. He speaks, each time in the likeness of a man close to the one he rouses, as he came to Hector as his uncle Asius in Book 16 (16.715–720; see [[asius-son-of-dymas]]) and will come to Aeneas as Priam's son Lycaon (20.79–82; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Each time the Trojans have given way: from the body of Euphorbus, back toward their city, and before Menelaus over Podes. Only Aeneas sees through the likeness. The Achaeans feel the god's hand without knowing it: Menelaus gives way to Hector, “since he fights with help from the gods” (17.101).
