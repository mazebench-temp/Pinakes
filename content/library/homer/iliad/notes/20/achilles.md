summary: The Achaeans arm around him, and Zeus sends the gods into the battle for fear that, fighting alone, he may sack the wall against fate. He meets Aeneas between the armies like a wounded lion and taunts him with his flight from Ida; Aeneas' spear is stopped in the shield of Hephaestus, his own passes over Aeneas' back, and Poseidon pours a mist over his eyes and carries Aeneas away. Then he calls on the Achaeans and goes in among the Trojans. He kills Iphition, Demoleon, Hippodamas and Polydorus, meets Hector, whom Apollo hides in mist, and kills ten more, raging like a fire on a mountain while his horses trample the dead (20.1–46, 20.75–78, 20.156–503).

**Around you, son of Peleus.** The book begins with the army arming “around you, son of Peleus, insatiable of battle, the Achaeans” (20.2). It is the only place in the poem where the narrator turns to Achilles and speaks to him (see [[apostrophe]] and the translation's note on 20.2). μάχης ἀκόρητον, “insatiable of battle”, agrees with σέ, “you”; Walter Leaf preferred the less supported ἀκόρητοι, of the Achaeans, since it is Achilles' men, not he, who might be supposed to have had a surfeit of battle (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.2; see [[insatiable]]).

**Zeus' fear.** Zeus calls the gods to assembly and lets them go down to help either side as each of them wishes (20.4–25; see [[zeus]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). His reason is Achilles:

{{quote:29e95b82-4cad-527d-aa64-93e5f051a400}}

οἶος, “alone”, means without the gods; Leaf remarks that Zeus seems to forget that Achilles will gain rather than lose by their coming, since the gods on the Achaean side are the stronger (on 20.26). The anger is for Patroclus: ἑταίρου is the genitive of the cause, “because of his friend”, as Poseidon is angry “because of the Cyclops” (Od. 1.69; Leaf on 20.29; see [[patroclus]] and [[hetairos]]). The fear of ὑπέρμορον, “against fate” (20.30), returns later on this day (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]] and the translation's note on 20.30); lines recorded in the T scholia were put in place of 29–30, Leaf thinks, because an omniscient god ought not to fear what might happen (on 20.30).

**He had appeared.** While the gods are still apart from men, “the Achaeans exulted greatly, because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (20.42–43): the verse and a half that gave the reason for the Trojans' fear in their assembly at nightfall and for the whole army's gathering to his assembly at dawn (18.247–248, 19.45–46). The next verse is the Trojans' trembling when Ajax came out against Hector (20.44 = 7.215; Leaf on 20.42). Now they tremble “when they saw the swift-footed son of Peleus / flashing in his armor, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (20.45–46). τεύχεσι λαμπόμενον, “flashing in his armor”, was said of Hector in Achilles' old armor, “flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.214; see [[exchange-of-armor]]); now Achilles flashes in the armor Hephaestus made (see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” is twice said of Hector (11.295, 13.802; see [[ruin-of-mortals]] and [[like-huge-ares]]).

**Hector.**

{{quote:b17d75fb-960b-5670-9cb3-40b567ace685}}

He keeps the vow he made to his mother: he would not live unless Hector were first struck by his spear (18.91–93), and “Now I will go, so that I may find the destroyer of that dear life, / Hector” (18.114–115; see [[hector]]). The last verse ends Diomedes' answer to Pandarus and Aeneas, that the two of them would not stop before one had fallen and glutted Ares with blood (20.78 = 5.289; see [[with-the-oxhide-shield]]).

**What gods and men say of him.** Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, rouses Aeneas against him, and Aeneas answers that he has faced Achilles once before, on Ida, when Achilles came upon his cattle and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus (20.79–96; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]] and [[achilles-raids]]). No man can fight him face to face, “for always at his side is one of the gods, who keeps ruin away” (20.98), the verse in which Diomedes said the same of Hector (5.603), and his weapon flies straight; but if a god stretched the outcome evenly he would not easily win, “not even if he boasts that he is all of bronze” (20.102). παγχάλκεος, “all of bronze”, means invulnerable (Leaf on 20.101; see [[bronze]]). Apollo answers that Aeneas is the son of the greater goddess, “and he is from a lesser goddess; / for one is the daughter of Zeus, and the other of the Old Man of the Sea” (20.106–107; see [[aphrodite]], [[thetis]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). Hera would have one of the gods stand by Achilles, “so that he may know that the best of the immortals love him” (20.122), and says that they have all come down “so that he may suffer nothing among the Trojans / today; hereafter he shall suffer whatever his fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (20.126–128; see [[aisa]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). The words are nearly those of Alcinous about Odysseus (Od. 7.196–198), and Hecuba will say of Hector that mighty Fate spun his thread at his birth (24.209–210; Leaf on 20.125, 20.128). Aristarchus athetized 125–128 as contradicting Zeus, who fears that Achilles will be too strong, not that he will suffer (see the translation's note on 20.125–128). If Achilles does not learn from the voice of the gods that they are with him, Hera goes on, he will be afraid when a god comes against him, “for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (20.129–131; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The lion.** “And the son of Peleus rushed against him from the other side like a lion” (20.164), a ravager that a whole people has gathered to kill. When a young man's spear strikes it, it crouches with gaping jaws, lashes its ribs and hips with its tail, and rushes straight on, “to see if he will kill / one of the men, or perish himself in the front of the throng” (20.172–173), “so his might and his proud spirit urged Achilles on / to go to meet great-hearted Aeneas” (20.174–175). Leaf agrees with those who call it the most finished simile in Homer (on 20.164; see [[like-a-lion]] and [[lion]]). The two came together between the armies “by far the best” (20.158–160; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]).

**The taunt.** “brilliant swift-footed Achilles spoke to him first” (20.177; see [[podarkes]]). Does Aeneas hope to rule the Trojans with Priam's honor? Priam has sons, “and he is steady, and not light of mind” (20.180–183; see [[priam]]). Or have the Trojans marked out a domain for him if he kills Achilles, as the Lycians did for Bellerophon (20.184–186; 6.194–195)? Aristarchus athetized 180–186 as mean in style and thought and unworthy of Achilles; Leaf answers that the same could be urged against the whole dialogue, and that Aeneas' claims to the throne are explained by his genealogy (on 20.180; see the translation's note on 20.180–186). Book 13 told of Aeneas' grudge against Priam, who did not honor him (13.460–461; see [[aeneas]]).

Then Ida. Achilles put him to flight with his spear (20.187), in the words Aeneas used of the same day to Apollo (20.90): he drove him alone from his cattle down the mountains of Ida, and Aeneas fled to Lyrnessus, “but I / sacked it, going after you with Athena and father Zeus, / and took away the day of freedom from the women, and led them off / as captives; but Zeus and the other gods rescued you” (20.191–194; see [[lyrnessus]] and [[eleutheros]]). Heyne, as Leaf reports, remarked how lightly the names of the gods are used here, when within three lines Zeus both helps the attack and saves its victim (on 20.194). The verse of the women's day of freedom is the one in which Hector mocked the dead Patroclus for hoping to take it from the Trojan women (20.193 = 16.831), and among the women of Lyrnessus was Briseis (2.689–691, 19.60; see [[briseis]]). The last verses, ending “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (20.198), are Menelaus' words to Euphorbus (17.30–32). Aristarchus athetized 195–198: Menelaus wanted only Patroclus' body and could let Euphorbus go, but Achilles has come for vengeance and should not let his first opponent escape (Leaf on 20.195; see [[a-fool-learns-once-it-is-done]]).

**Aeneas' answer.** Aeneas will not be frightened with words “as if I were a child” (20.200). He knows his opponent's parents: “They say that you are the offspring of noble Peleus, / and that your mother is Thetis of the lovely tresses, daughter of the sea” (20.206–207; see [[peleus]] and [[thetis]]), and “Of these, one pair or the other will weep for their dear son / today” (20.210–211). Then he tells his own descent from Zeus and makes an end of words (20.213–258; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[dardanus]]).

**The fool.** Aeneas' spear strikes the shield, and it groans loudly around the point. “And the son of Peleus held the shield away from him with his strong hand / in fear” (20.261–262), thinking that the spear would pass through it, “the fool, nor did he consider in his mind and in his heart / that the glorious gifts of the gods are not easy / for mortal men to master, nor do they give way” (20.264–266; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). He has just told Aeneas that “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (20.198); now the narrator gives him the word, νήπιος, that he gave Agamemnon trusting his dream and Patroclus begging for his own death (2.38, 16.46; see [[nepios]]). The gold, the god's gift, holds the point (20.267–268).

**The cast.** “Then second in his turn Achilles let fly his long-shadowed spear, / and struck Aeneas on his shield, balanced on every side” (20.273–274). The duel keeps the order of Hector's with Ajax in Book 7. Hector's spear went “Through six layers … but in the seventh hide it stopped. Then second in his turn / Ajax, sprung from Zeus, let fly his long-shadowed spear, / and struck the son of Priam on his shield, balanced on every side” (7.247–250; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). Achilles' spear, the Pelian ash, goes through the rim of Aeneas' shield and over his back as he crouches, and stands fixed in the ground (20.275–283; see [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[shield]]).

**The sword.** “But Achilles / sprang at him eagerly, drawing his sharp sword, / shouting terribly” (20.283–285; see [[sword]]). σμερδαλέα ἰάχων, “shouting terribly”, is the cry with which he went along the shore to call the assembly (19.41), and he gives it three times in this book (20.285, 20.382, 20.443). Aeneas lifts a stone “which two men could not carry, / such as mortals are now” (20.286–287), in the verses of the stone in Book 5, with the names changed: there Aeneas was the one shouting terribly, and Diomedes lifted the stone against him (20.285–287 = 5.302–304; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[men-of-today]]). The stone would have struck Achilles' helmet or his shield, “and the son of Peleus would have taken his life with his sword at close quarters, / had not Poseidon the earth-shaker been quick to see it” (20.290–291; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[divine-rescue]]).

**Poseidon's mist.** Poseidon grieves for Aeneas, who will soon go down to Hades “brought down by the son of Peleus” (20.294), and fears the anger of Zeus “if Achilles / kills this man” (20.301–302), since Aeneas is fated to survive (see [[poseidon]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). He goes to the two: “At once he poured a mist over the eyes / of Achilles, son of Peleus” (20.321–322; see [[achlys]]), draws Achilles' spear out of Aeneas' shield and sets it before his feet, and sends Aeneas flying over the ranks to the edge of the battle (20.322–329). In Book 5 Athena took the mist from Diomedes' eyes, “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (5.127–128). To Aeneas Poseidon speaks of Achilles as “at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals” (20.334), and bids him draw back from him: “But when Achilles has met his death and his fate, / then take heart, and fight among the foremost; / for no other of the Achaeans will slay you” (20.337–339; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**The wonder.** “And at once he scattered the mist from the eyes of Achilles, / the heaven-sent mist” (20.341–342), and Achilles “stared hard with his eyes, / and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (20.342–343; see [[deeply-troubled]]):

{{quote:f5753c38-6771-555d-b640-d32efddb924f}}

The first verse is Poseidon's when he rallies the Achaeans at the ships, and Thoas' when he sees Hector back in the battle (13.99, 15.286); Achilles will speak the same two verses again when he finds Lycaon come back from Lemnos (21.53–54 = 20.343–344; see [[o-popoi]]). φίλος ἀθανάτοισι θεοῖσιν, “dear to the immortal gods” (20.347), takes up, without his knowing it, Poseidon's φίλτερος ἀθανάτοισιν (20.334). εὐχετάασθαι, “boasted”, is the verb with which Aeneas claimed his descent from Anchises and Aphrodite and from Zeus (εὔχομαι, 20.209, 20.241; see [[euchomai]]). ἐρρέτω, “Let him go to his ruin!” (20.349), is what he said of Agamemnon in the embassy (9.376–377).

**Man against man.** He leaps along the ranks and calls on the Achaeans:

{{quote:2303a731-9de0-565d-be9b-fd798a62f5a8}}

ὑσμίνης στόμα, “the mouth of so great a battle” (20.359), Leaf takes as hardly more than a periphrasis for the battle, perhaps a figure from the bit and bridle, since ἐφέπειν is used of driving horses (on 20.359; see [[exhortation]]). Hector answers him from the other side: “Nor will Achilles put fulfillment to all his words, / but one he will fulfill, and another he will cut short halfway. / I will go against him, even if his hands are like fire, / if his hands are like fire, and his might like gleaming iron” (20.369–372; see [[hands-like-fire]], [[hector]] and [[iron]]). The repetition of a whole phrase is found again only at 22.128 and 23.642 (Leaf on 20.371). But Apollo sends Hector back into the crowd, lest Achilles “hit you with a spear, or strike you with his sword at close quarters” (20.378; see [[apollo]]).

**The first killings.** “And Achilles leapt among the Trojans, clothed in courage in his heart, / shouting terribly, and first he killed Iphition” (20.381–382; see [[iphition]]). Leaf thought that the first verse may well have opened Achilles' career in the original poem of the Wrath, the Μῆνις, and that the Iphition episode which follows betrays a later origin (on 20.381; see the translation's note on 20.381–395). Over Iphition he boasts, calling him “most terrible of all men” (20.389), the words with which Agamemnon and Iris addressed Achilles himself (1.146, 18.170) and which Leaf finds meaningless of an unknown warrior (on 20.381; see [[most-terrible-of-men]]); and he tells the dead man where his father's domain lies, by the Gygaean lake (20.390–392; see [[gygaean-lake]]). He stabs Demoleon, son of Antenor, through the helmet (20.395–400; see [[demoleon]]), and Hippodamas in the back as he leaps from his chariot, so that he bellows as he dies like a bull dragged around the Heliconian lord (20.401–406; see [[hippodamas]] and [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]]). Then Polydorus, Priam's youngest and dearest son, whom his father would not let fight, rushes through the champions to show off the speed of his feet, and “Brilliant swift-footed Achilles hit him in the middle with a javelin / in the back as he darted past” (20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]).

**Hector again.** When Hector sees his brother fall he comes against Achilles “like a flame” (20.423; see [[like-a-flame]]), and Achilles leaps up and speaks in triumph:

{{quote:dc047cbe-ef7f-5805-9ff0-65c162fefeed}}

The companion is Patroclus (see [[patroclus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]); for “the bridges of war” see [[obscure-gephyrai]]. The last verse is Diomedes' to Glaucus (20.429 = 6.143; see [[looking-darkly]]). Hector answers in Aeneas' words, “Son of Peleus, do not think that with words, as if I were a child, / you will frighten me” (20.431–432 = 20.200–201), but grants that Achilles is the better man: “And I know that you are brave, and I am far worse than you” (20.434), a frankness in which Leaf sees the dignity of Hector's character (on 20.434). Athena turns Hector's spear back from Achilles with a breath (20.438–441; see [[athena]]). Achilles springs at him, eager to kill him, “shouting terribly; but Apollo snatched him away / very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist” (20.443–444), in the verses with which Aphrodite saved Paris from Menelaus (3.380–381; see [[divine-rescue]]). “Three times then brilliant swift-footed Achilles sprang at him / with his bronze spear, and three times he struck the deep mist” (20.445–446), and “when for the fourth time he rushed on like a god” (20.447) it is Achilles who shouts; in the same pattern it was Apollo who shouted at Diomedes and at Patroclus (5.438–439, 16.705–706; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Leaf, finding that here the fourth onset leads to nothing, thinks 447 interpolated from the parallels (on 20.444; see the translation's note on 20.447). The taunt:

{{quote:01bdcf72-c94c-5dfc-ac4a-52ce7f6242bc}}

These are Diomedes' words to Hector in Book 11 (11.362–367), and Leaf thinks them in their own place here and copied there (on 20.449; see [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]). Achilles will meet Hector again before the Scaean gates (see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

**The rampage.** He goes after the others, “whomever I can catch” (20.454): Dryops, Demuchus, son of Philetor, Laogonus and Dardanus, the two sons of Bias, Tros, son of Alastor, Mulius, Echeclus, son of Agenor, Deucalion, and Rhigmus from Thrace with his attendant Areithous (20.455–489; see [[dryops]], [[demuchus]], [[laogonus-son-of-bias]], [[dardanus-son-of-bias]], [[tros-son-of-alastor]], [[mulius-killed-by-achilles]], [[echeclus-son-of-agenor]], [[deucalion-the-trojan]], [[rhigmus]], [[areithous-the-attendant]] and [[androktasia]]). Tros comes to clasp his knees, hoping to be taken alive, “the fool, and he did not know that the other would not be persuaded; / for he was no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted, / but in a great fury” (20.466–468; see [[supplication]]). Leaf suspects 463–468, finding the poet's own judgment on Achilles wholly alien to the epic style (on 20.463; see the translation's note on 20.463–468). In the next book Achilles tells Lycaon that before Patroclus died he spared many Trojans, taking them alive and selling them, but now none whom a god puts into his hands will escape death (21.99–105).

**Fire and the threshing floor.** As a wondrous fire rages through the valleys of a dry mountain, “so he rushed everywhere with his spear, like a god, / chasing the men he was killing; and the black earth ran with blood” (20.490–494; see [[forest-fire]] and [[like-a-god]]); a fire falling on uncut woodland was the simile for Agamemnon's slaughter (11.155–159; Leaf on 20.490). Then his horses trample corpses and shields as bulls tread out barley on a threshing floor, until the axle and the rails of his chariot are spattered with blood (20.495–502; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]], [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[chariot]]), and the book ends with him: “And he was eager to win glory, / the son of Peleus, and his irresistible hands were spattered with gore” (20.502–503; see [[kydos]] and [[irresistible-hands]]). The verses of the chariot are those of Hector's in Book 11 (20.499–502 = 11.534–537), where Hector “was eager to plunge into the throng” (11.537), and the last is Agamemnon's, with Πηλεΐδης for Ἀτρεΐδης (20.503 = 11.169). Several editors rejected 495–503, because the verses are borrowed and because Achilles, who has fought on foot, is suddenly in his chariot; Leaf keeps them (on 20.495; see the translation's note on 20.495–503).
