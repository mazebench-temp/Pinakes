---
summary: Cicero will not listen to Homer, “who says that Ganymede was carried off by the gods for his beauty, to serve Jupiter his drink”. Homer, he says, invented such things and gave the gods what is human, when he would rather have given men what is divine.
---

- **Ganymede.** In the first book of the *Tusculan Disputations* Cicero argues that the powers of the mind, memory and invention, are divine. He does not think that the gods delight in ambrosia and nectar, or in Juventas serving their cups, “nor do I listen to Homer, who says that Ganymede was carried off by the gods for his beauty, to serve Jupiter his drink” (*nec Homerum audio, qui Ganymeden ab dis raptum ait propter formam, ut Iovi bibere ministraret*); that was no just cause for so great a wrong to Laomedon. “Homer invented these things and transferred what is human to the gods; I would rather he had transferred what is divine to us” (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.65). The verses are Aeneas': “and the gods snatched him up to pour wine for Zeus, / for the sake of his beauty” (20.234–235). In the Iliad Ganymede is the son of Tros, and Zeus gave Tros his horses “in recompense for his son Ganymede” (5.265–266; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]); Cicero's wrong to Laomedon follows the version that made Ganymede Laomedon's son (see [[ganymede]], [[cupbearing]] and [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]]).
