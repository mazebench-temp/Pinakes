---
summary: In Aeneas' genealogy the gods snatch up Ganymede, son of Tros, “to pour wine for Zeus, / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (20.234–235).
---

The gods drink nectar, not wine, and Walter Leaf calls οἰνοχοεύειν a mixture of metaphor, like the cupbearing of Hephaestus, who “poured for all the other gods, / drawing sweet nectar from the mixing bowl” (οἰνοχόει γλυκὺ νέκταρ, 1.597–598; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.221, 20.234). Aristotle explains the word as one kept by the custom of the language (*Poetics* 25, 1461a29–31; see [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 20.234). In the Hymn to Aphrodite it is Zeus who carries Ganymede off for his beauty, to pour for the gods in his house, “drawing red nectar from a golden mixing bowl” (χρυσέου ἐκ κρητῆρος ἀφύσσων νέκταρ ἐρυθρόν, *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–206; see [[ganymede]] and [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]).
