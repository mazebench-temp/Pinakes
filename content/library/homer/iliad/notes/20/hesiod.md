---
summary: Hesiod's war of the gods against the Titans ends a verse as the battle of the gods does, “as the gods came together in strife”, and his dank places at the ends of the earth are, like the dwellings of Hades, places “which even the gods hate”; at Zeus' fight with Typhoeus, Hades trembles below. A Hesiodic fragment says of swift Iphiclus what Aeneas says of the foals of Erichthonius' mares, and the Works and Days has a saying close to Aeneas' on speaking and hearing.
---

- **The din of the gods.** The battle of the gods rises to “so great was the din that rose as the gods came together in strife” (τόσσος ἄρα κτύπος ὦρτο θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, 20.66). At the height of the war against the Titans, when the noise is as if earth and wide heaven fell together, Hesiod has “so great a din arose as the gods came together in strife” (τόσσος δοῦπος ἔγεντο θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, *Theogony* 700–705). In his war too the boundless sea roars, the earth crashes, the wide heaven groans, tall Olympus is shaken from its base, and the quaking reaches misty Tartarus (*Theogony* 678–682): [[the-battle-of-the-gods]], [[title-of-book-20]] and [[titans]].
- **Hated by the gods.** Aidoneus fears that his dwellings will be laid bare, “terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (σμερδαλέʼ εὐρώεντα, τά τε στυγέουσι θεοί περ, 20.65). Hesiod's verse on the springs and ends of the earth, of Tartarus, the sea and the sky is the same but for its first word, ἀργαλέʼ εὐρώεντα, τά τε στυγέουσι θεοί περ, “grievous and dank, which even the gods hate” (*Theogony* 739 = 810), and the Titans lie hidden “in a dank place” (χώρῳ ἐν εὐρώεντι, 731). Walter Leaf cites these verses, with others, for εὐρώεις (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.64): [[hades]] and [[dank]].
- **Hades trembles.** When Zeus thunders against Typhoeus, and the earth, the wide heaven, the sea and Tartarus resound and great Olympus quakes under the feet of its lord, “Hades trembled, lord of the dead below, and the Titans under Tartarus around Cronus” (*Theogony* 839–852). In Book 20 Zeus' thunder and Poseidon's earthquake make Aidoneus, “lord of those below”, leap from his throne (20.56–63): [[hades]] and [[typhoeus]].
- **Running on the ears.** The foals of Erichthonius' mares “would run on the very tips of the ripe ears and not break them” (ἄκρον ἐπʼ ἀνθερίκων καρπὸν θέον οὐδὲ κατέκλων, 20.227). A Hesiodic fragment says the same of Iphiclus, the swift-footed son of Phylacus and Clymene, in nearly the same words: “he would run over the tips of the ears and not break them, but sped with his feet over the wheaten awns and did no harm to the grain” (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 84 Evelyn-White; see the translation's note on 20.227): [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]].
- **Speaking and hearing.** Aeneas' “Whatever kind of word you speak, such will you hear back” (20.250) is close to the *Works and Days*: εἰ δὲ κακὸν εἴποις, τάχα κʼ αὐτὸς μεῖζον ἀκούσαις, “if you speak ill, you may soon hear worse yourself” (*Works and Days* 721; Leaf on 20.250): [[such-will-you-hear-back]].
