summary: Poseidon comes up out of the sea to the gods' assembly and asks Zeus his plan. He goes to the ships with the Achaeans' gods and shakes the earth and Ida, so that Aidoneus fears the house of the dead will be laid bare, and Apollo is set against him. When Hera would turn Aeneas back he counsels the gods to sit apart and leave the war to men, and leads his party to the wall of Heracles. But when Achilles is about to kill Aeneas he grieves for him, declares that the lineage of Dardanus is fated to survive and that Aeneas and his children's children will rule the Trojans, and with Hera's leave carries him out of the battle under a mist, warning him to keep away from Achilles until Achilles is dead. In a simile he is the Heliconian lord, around whom young men drag a bellowing bull, and the earth-shaker is glad of them.

**At the assembly.** Themis calls the gods to the house of Zeus, “nor did the earth-shaker / fail to heed the goddess, but came up out of the sea to join them, / and sat down in their midst, and asked Zeus his plan” (20.13–15; see [[themis-the-goddess]], [[earth-shaker]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Walter Leaf reports a scholium that the special mention of Poseidon may allude to the bad terms on which he last parted from Zeus, when Zeus ordered him out of the war (15.173–218); he answers that Poseidon was the chief rival of Zeus, and that his obedience to a summons would not always be a matter of course (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.13). His question is the occasion for Zeus' release of the gods: “You know, shaker of the earth, the plan in my breast” (20.20; see [[zeus]] and [[shaker-of-the-earth]]).

**To the ships.** “Hera went to the gathering of the ships, and Pallas Athena, / and Poseidon the earth-holder, and the great helper / Hermes”, and Hephaestus went with them (20.33–36; see [[earth-holder]], [[hera]], [[athena]], [[hermes]] and [[hephaestus]]). They are the gods he named in Book 15, when he gave way to Zeus with a threat: if Zeus spared steep Ilios “against my will and that of Athena, driver of spoil, / of Hera and Hermes and lord Hephaestus”, there would be incurable anger between them (15.213–217; see the note on Book 15).

**The earthquake.** When the gods come into the throng and Zeus thunders from above, Poseidon shakes the world below:

{{quote:97106a6c-0f13-520e-8940-87a3efb12a97}}

ἐνοσίχθων, “the earth-shaker” (20.63), is here what his title says. The mountains and the woods trembled under his feet when he strode down from the peak of Thracian Samos in Book 13 (13.12–19; see [[samothrace]]); now he shakes “the boundless earth” itself, which by the division of the world between the three sons of Cronus is common to them all (15.193; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]), and the third brother, the lord of the dead, fears that he will break it open (20.61–65; see [[hades]]). The critic of *On the Sublime* quotes 61–65 for the terror of the battle of the gods, and runs 20.60 into his quotation of Poseidon's advance in Book 13 (*On the Sublime* 9.6, 9.8; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]] and [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]).

**Against Apollo.** “For against lord Poseidon / stood Phoebus Apollo with his feathered arrows” (20.67–68; see [[apollo]]). They are the two gods who built the wall of Troy for Laomedon (7.452–453; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[laomedon]]). When they meet in Book 21, Poseidon bids Apollo begin, as the younger, and reminds him how Laomedon cheated them of their wage, while Poseidon built the wall and Apollo herded the king's cattle on Ida; Apollo answers that he would not be sound of mind if he fought Poseidon for the sake of mortals, who flourish and fade like leaves, and turns away, ashamed to come to blows with his father's brother (21.435–469).

**Sit apart.** When Hera sees Aeneas going to face Achilles and asks Poseidon and Athena whether to turn him back or stand by Achilles (20.112–131; see [[hera]] and [[aeneas]]), Poseidon will not have the gods fight:

{{quote:5af9602f-b8e7-5518-944d-a371c8f4095e}}

The words of 134–135 are those with which, in Book 8, he refused Hera's plan to fight Zeus: “I would not wish that we should fight against Zeus, son of Cronus, / we others, since he is far the stronger” (8.210–211). There Zeus was the stronger; here “we” are. Leaf notes that the evidence of the manuscripts shows the line to have been adapted from 8.211 at a very late date (on 20.135). “War will be the concern of men” is Hector's half-verse to Andromache, where men are set against women (6.492; Leaf on 20.137; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). Then “the dark-haired god led the way / to the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles” (20.144–145; see [[dark-haired]] and [[the-wall-of-heracles]]), and there he sat with the other gods of his side, wrapped in “an unbreakable cloud” (20.149–150), while the gods of the Trojans sat on the brows of Callicolone (20.151–152; see [[callicolone]]).

**The fated escape.** In the duel Achilles would have killed Aeneas with his sword, “had not Poseidon the earth-shaker been quick to see it” (20.291; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). The half-verse is the one with which Aphrodite saw her son in danger from Diomedes in Book 5 (εἰ μὴ ἄρʼ ὀξὺ νόησε, 5.312). He speaks among the gods:

{{quote:b29576d8-205f-52bd-9ee6-e4949fee0ec3}}

Aeneas is “not to blame” (ἀναίτιος, 20.297; see [[aitios]]). For ἀλλοτρίων ἀχέων, “others' griefs” (20.298), Leaf, finding the expression strange, records Bentley's ἀτέων (on 20.298; see [[achos]]). The argument from gifts (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]) is one that Hera once turned against Poseidon himself, when he would not help the Danaans: “Yet they bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae, / many and pleasing” (8.203–204), and in Book 24 Zeus will say it of Hector (24.66–70). Then Poseidon gives the reason that settles it: Aeneas is fated to escape, so that the lineage of Dardanus may not perish without seed, since Zeus has come to hate the lineage of Priam, “and now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children, who will be born hereafter” (20.300–308; see [[moira]], [[genee]], [[dardanus]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Leaf finds the speech and the rescue glaringly inconsistent with Poseidon's attitude in the rest of the Iliad and with his own counsel a little earlier (20.133–143): Aeneas should have been saved by Apollo, who urged him on, or by Aphrodite, as in Book 5, and the episode may have been composed not only for the glory of Aeneas but to explain a cult of Poseidon among the families who claimed descent from him (on 20.293). Hera leaves it to him, since she and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (20.309–317; see [[hera]] and [[oath]]).

**The rescue.** He goes through the battle and pours a mist over the eyes of Achilles, draws the ash spear out of Aeneas' shield and sets it before Achilles' feet, “and lifting Aeneas high he sent him flying from the ground”, over many ranks of heroes and horses, to the far edge of the war, “where the Caucones were arming themselves for war” (20.318–329; see [[achlys]], [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[caucones]]). Aristarchus athetized 322–324, since Achilles' spear had gone through the shield and stood in the ground (20.276–280; see the translation's note on 20.322–324). Then he speaks to Aeneas: “Aeneas, which of the gods bids you so recklessly / fight face to face with the high-hearted son of Peleus, / who is at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals?” (20.332–334). ἀτέοντα, “recklessly”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 20.332). Aeneas is to draw back whenever he meets Achilles, “lest you come to the house of Hades even beyond your portion” (20.336; see [[hades]] and [[moira]]); “But when Achilles has met his death and his fate, / then take heart, and fight among the foremost; / for no other of the Achaeans will slay you” (20.337–339; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Then he scatters “the heaven-sent mist” from Achilles' eyes (20.341–342), and Achilles sees his spear on the ground and no man: “Truly Aeneas too was dear to the immortal gods” (20.347; see [[achilles]]).

**The Heliconian lord.** When Achilles stabs Hippodamas in the back, the dying man bellows “as when a bull / bellows as he is dragged around the Heliconian lord / by young men dragging him; and the earth-shaker is glad of them” (20.403–405; see [[heliconian-poseidon]], [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]] and [[sacrifice]]). The god of the earthquake and of the rescue is here the god of a cult, pleased with the sacrifice. Strabo reports that some took the lines for the Ionians' sacrifice to Heliconian Poseidon at the Panionia, and others for the sacrifice at Helice (*Geography* 8.7.2; see [[helice-or-the-panionia]]).

**His titles.** The book calls him Ποσειδάων ἐνοσίχθων, “Poseidon the earth-shaker” (20.63, 20.132, 20.291, 20.318, 20.330), and ἐνοσίχθων alone (20.13, 20.405); ἐννοσίγαιε, “shaker of the earth”, in the mouths of Zeus and Hera (20.20, 20.310); γαιήοχος, “the earth-holder” (20.34); Ποσειδάωνος ἄνακτος, “lord Poseidon” (20.67); and κυανοχαίτης alone, “the dark-haired god” (20.144), a word which, of the stallion whose likeness the North Wind takes, is rendered “dark-maned” (20.224; see [[boreas]]).
