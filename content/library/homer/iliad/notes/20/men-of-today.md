---
summary: For the last time in the Iliad the narrator measures a hero's stone by men “such as mortals are now”: Aeneas lifts it against Achilles (20.285–287). A little earlier Aeneas has looked back on his own forebears, the richest and the most beautiful “of mortal men”.
---

{{quote:a2d87747-c276-51f4-a599-e2a86129cd57}}

**The last stone.** It is the fourth stone that the poem measures by the men of the narrator's day, and the last (5.304, 12.383, 12.449, 20.287; see [[stone-throwing]]). The verses are those of Diomedes' stone, which crushed Aeneas' hip in Book 5 (5.302–310). Now Aeneas lifts it himself, against a man stronger than he, and never throws it (20.288–291; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]).

**The men of his house.** In his answer to Achilles, Aeneas looks back as the narrator does. He knows his own parents and Achilles' from “the tales mortal men have told of old” (20.204), and he measures his forebears against all mortals in one form of verse: Erichthonius “was the richest of mortal men”, and Ganymede “the most beautiful of mortal men” (ὃς δὴ … γένετο θνητῶν ἀνθρώπων, 20.220, 20.233; see [[erichthonius]], [[ganymede]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The narrator sets his heroes above the men who listen to him; the heroes set the men of their house above all mortals (see [[aeneas]]).
