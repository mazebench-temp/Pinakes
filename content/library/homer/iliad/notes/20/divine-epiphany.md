---
summary: Apollo comes to Aeneas in the likeness of Priam's son Lycaon, and Aeneas does not know him (20.79–111); after the rescue Poseidon comes close to Aeneas and speaks to him as himself (20.330–340); and Apollo stands beside Hector, who knows the god's voice and draws back in fear (20.375–380).
refs: [20.79-82, 20.103, 20.330-331, 20.375, 20.380]
---

Apollo's disguise is chosen to shame Aeneas: Lycaon is a son of Priam, and he reminds Aeneas of the boasts he made over his wine (20.81–85; [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[son-of-zeus]]). Aeneas answers him as “Son of Priam” (20.87; [[son-of-priam]]). Hera has warned the gods of the Achaean side that “gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (20.131; [[gods-are-hard-to-face]], [[seeing-the-gods]]). Hector, unlike Aeneas, knows the god at once, “when he heard the voice of the god as he spoke” (20.380). Poseidon's coming ends with the god's departure, “So saying, he left him there, when he had made all this plain to him” (20.340). See [[apollo]], [[poseidon]] and [[divine-rescue]].
