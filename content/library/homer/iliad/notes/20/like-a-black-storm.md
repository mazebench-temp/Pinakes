---
summary: When the gods come into the throng of men, Athena shouts for the Achaeans from the ditch and the shore, and Ares answers from the other side “like a dark storm”, calling on the Trojans from the topmost height of the city and running beside the Simoeis on Callicolone (20.47–53).
---

αὖε δʼ Ἄρης ἑτέρωθεν ἐρεμνῇ λαίλαπι ἶσος / ὀξὺ κατʼ ἀκροτάτης πόλιος Τρώεσσι κελεύων, / ἄλλοτε πὰρ Σιμόεντι θέων ἐπὶ Καλλικολώνῃ: “And Ares on the other side shouted, like a dark storm, / shrilly calling on the Trojans from the topmost height of the city, / and now running beside the Simoeis on Callicolone” (20.51–53). The half-verse is the one of the Lycians climbing onto the Achaean battlements (ἐρεμνῇ λαίλαπι ἶσοι, 12.375), with ἶσος, singular, for one fighter, as in Nestor's κελαινῇ λαίλαπι ἶσος (11.747). In those books men were like the storm in their onset; here a god is like it, and the likeness is of his voice. Ares' cry has had its own simile before: when Diomedes wounded him, he bellowed as loud as nine or ten thousand men shout in war, and he went up to the sky like a murky mist out of the clouds (5.859–867; see [[ten-thousand-men]] and [[dark-mist-from-the-clouds]]).

The storm answers Athena. αὖε δʼ Ἀθήνη, “and Athena shouted”, standing now beside the ditch outside the wall and now on the loud-resounding shores; αὖε δʼ Ἄρης ἑτέρωθεν, “and Ares on the other side shouted”, now from the height of the city and now beside the Simoeis (20.48–53; see [[aute]], [[athena]], [[ares]], [[shore]], [[simoeis]] and [[callicolone]]). Each god has two stations, and between them lies the whole field, from the Achaean ditch to the river under the city. Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus began the main clause with αὖε δʼ Ἀθήνη rather than with ὦρτο δʼ Ἔρις, which sets the two shouts against each other a little more sharply (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.48; see the translation's note on 20.48). Strife has risen between them, “who rouses armies” (20.48; see [[strife]]). The storm is followed by thunder and earthquake: Zeus thunders from on high, and Poseidon shakes the boundless earth beneath (20.56–58; see [[earth]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).
