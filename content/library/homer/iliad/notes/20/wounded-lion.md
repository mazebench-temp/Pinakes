It goes its way heedless of them, but when a young man hits it with a spear it crouches with gaping jaws, lashes its flanks with its tail, and rushes straight on in its fury, to kill one of the men or perish itself (20.164–175).

{{quote:c3ea1a5f-003f-596a-a973-3ac85be91967}}

Πηλεΐδης δʼ ἑτέρωθεν ἐναντίον ὦρτο λέων ὣς / σίντης, ὅν τε καὶ ἄνδρες ἀποκτάμεναι μεμάασιν / ἀγρόμενοι πᾶς δῆμος: “And the son of Peleus rushed against him from the other side like a lion, / a ravager, whom men are eager to kill, / gathered together, a whole people” (20.164–166). The half-verse is the one with which Agamemnon rushed against the sons of Antimachus (11.129; see [[like-a-lion]]); here it opens the longest lion simile given to Achilles. σίντης, “ravager”, makes the lion a robber of herds, like the lion that a god brings upon the jackals (11.481; see [[ravager]]), and the καί that follows carries on its stress, “and so”, “consequently”: being a ravager, it is a beast that men want dead (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.165). δῆμος is the whole community, a village, Leaf thinks (on 20.166). The men are many, as the herdsmen of Sarpedon's simile guard the sheep with dogs and spears against a mountain lion that will not go back untried (12.299–306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]).

The lion's first answer is contempt: ὃ δὲ πρῶτον μὲν ἀτίζων / ἔρχεται, “and at first he goes his way / heedless of them” (20.166–167). ἀτίζω, “disregard”, is found only here in Homer (Leaf on 20.166). Then a spear changes it: “but when one of the young men swift in war / hits him with a spear” (20.167–168; see [[swift-in-war]]). The wound is not said to be grave. As with the lion of Book 5, which the shepherd grazed but did not kill, it rouses the beast (5.136–139), and what follows is the lion's fury told part by part. It crouches, ἐάλη, with gaping jaws; foam gathers about its teeth; its valiant heart groans within it (see [[kradie]] and [[etor]]); it lashes its ribs and hips on both sides with its tail, and urges itself on to fight; and γλαυκιόων, “glaring”, a word found only here in Homer, it rushes straight on in its fury, “to see if he will kill / one of the men, or perish himself in the front of the throng” (20.168–173). Leaf takes γλαυκιόων as “glaring”, with bright eyes (on 20.172), and reports from the B scholia an old belief that the lion has a black sting under its tail, like a little horn, with which it lashes itself and is goaded into greater fury (on 20.170). ἐάλη, “crouches”, is the verb of Aeneas himself a hundred verses later, when he crouches under Achilles' spear (Αἰνείας δʼ ἐάλη, 20.278).

The end is left open, kill or be killed. The lion of Patroclus was struck in the chest, and its own courage destroyed it (16.752–753); the mountain lion of Sarpedon's simile either seizes a sheep or is struck among the foremost (12.305–306). Achilles' lion is struck and turns to fight, and whether it dies is not said.

The tenor: ὣς Ἀχιλῆʼ ὄτρυνε μένος καὶ θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ / ἀντίον ἐλθέμεναι μεγαλήτορος Αἰνείαο, “so his might and his proud spirit urged Achilles on / to go to meet great-hearted Aeneas” (20.174–175; see [[menos]], [[thymos]] and [[proud]]). The lion “urges himself on to fight” (ἑὲ δʼ αὐτὸν ἐποτρύνει, 20.171), and Achilles is urged on by his own might and spirit: what the beast does to itself, the hero's spirit does to him. θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ is the lion's spirit in Sarpedon's simile, which bids it go for the sheep (12.300), and in Book 24 Apollo will say that Achilles, like a lion, yields to his great might and proud spirit (24.41–43). Nothing in the tenor answers the young man's spear. In the narrative it is Aeneas who strode out first, threatening, tossing his helmet and shaking his spear (20.161–163; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]).

Leaf agrees with those who have called this the most finished simile in Homer, “as it is certainly one of the finest”, and finds that it stands out from its context as some of the remarkable similes of Book 12 do (on 20.164). The lion of the Hesiodic *Shield* lashes its flanks with its tail and glares in the same words (see Outside Homer in the article).
