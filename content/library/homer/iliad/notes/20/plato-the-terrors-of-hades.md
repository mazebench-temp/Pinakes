Plato does not say where the verses come from. In the poem they are not a description of Hades but the god's fear. Zeus thunders from on high and Poseidon shakes the earth from below, and the lord of those below leaps from his throne and cries out, lest the earth be broken open, “and his dwellings be laid bare to mortals and immortals, / terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (20.61–65; see [[hades]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Plato quotes these two verses as they stand in our text, among the verses that make death fearful to men who must be brave (*Republic* 3.386d). εὐρώεντα, “dank”, from εὐρώς, “mould”, is said of the house of Hades in the Odyssey too (*Odyssey* 10.512, 23.322; see [[dank]]).

Proclus names these verses in his list of what the poet says of Hades, “the fearful dwellings, which the gods hate” (*On the Republic* I 118 Kroll). He answers that the poet should not be blamed for calling those places “terrible and dank, which even the gods hate”. They strike souls with terror by their variety and by the apparitions of the powers set over them, but they are many, fitted to the different states of the souls carried there, and they lie farthest from the gods, the last of all things, full of the disorder of matter and without a share in the rays of the sun (I 122 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

*On the Sublime* quotes all five verses as an image of the terror of the battle of the gods (*On the Sublime* 9.6; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]]).
