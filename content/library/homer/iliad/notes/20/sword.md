---
summary: When Aeneas has escaped his spear, Achilles springs at him “drawing his sharp sword”, and would have killed him with it had Poseidon not been quick to see (20.283–291); Apollo warns Hector that Achilles may strike him with the sword at close quarters (20.378). In the rampage Achilles kills Demuchus, Dardanus, Tros, Echeclus and Deucalion with the sword (20.459, 20.462, 20.469, 20.475–476, 20.481–482).
---

**Against Aeneas.** “But Achilles / sprang at him eagerly, drawing his sharp sword, / shouting terribly” (20.283–285): ἐρυσσάμενος ξίφος ὀξύ, “drawing his sharp sword”, ends the verse when Leonteus draws on Antiphates and Peneleos on Ilioneus (12.190, 14.496), and again when Achilles kills Lycaon (21.116). Aeneas takes up a stone, and Achilles “would have taken his life with his sword at close quarters” but for Poseidon (20.290–291; see [[achilles]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). The sword is the silver-studded sword of bronze that he slung on when he armed (19.372–373); Hephaestus did not make it (see the note on Book 19).

**At close quarters.** σχεδὸν ἄορι, “with his sword at close quarters”, recurs: Apollo warns Hector to keep back, lest Achilles “hit you with a spear, or strike you with his sword at close quarters” (20.378; see [[hector]] and [[apollo]]), and of the two sons of Bias, Achilles struck down the one with his spear “and striking the other with his sword at close quarters” (20.462; see [[dardanus-son-of-bias]]), in the same words, τύψῃ and τύψας.

**The rampage.** Demuchus he stopped with a spear in the knee and then, “stabbing him with his great sword he took away his life” (20.459; see [[demuchus]]); Tros, son of Alastor, was trying to clasp his knees when “the other stabbed him with his sword in the liver” (20.469; see [[tros-son-of-alastor]]). Echeclus “he struck full on the head with his hilted sword, / and the whole sword grew warm with blood” (20.475–476; see [[hilted]] and [[echeclus-son-of-agenor]]); the last two verses of his death are those of Cleobulus, whom the son of Oileus killed with the sword (20.476–477 = 16.333–334; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.475). Deucalion, his arm pierced, waited for him, “seeing death before him; and he struck his neck with his sword / and sent his head flying far, helmet and all” (20.481–482; see [[deucalion-the-trojan]] and [[helmet]]), as Peneleos struck off Ilioneus' head “helmet and all” (14.497–498).
