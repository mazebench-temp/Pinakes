summary: Athena goes to the ships with Hera and Poseidon, and when the gods come into the throng she shouts by the ditch and on the shore, as Ares shouts from the city; Enyalius is set against her. Aeneas remembers that she went before Achilles at Lyrnessus. She is named in Hera's counsel and Hera's oath against Troy, and with the Trojans she built the wall of Heracles, where her party sits. When Hector hurls his spear at Achilles she turns it back with a breath.

**Her shout.** She goes to the ships with Hera, “and Pallas Athena” (20.33; see [[pallas]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]), and when the gods come into the throng of men, “mighty Strife rose up, who rouses armies, and Athena shouted, / standing now beside the ditch they had dug, outside the wall, / and now on the loud-resounding shores she shouted aloud” (20.48–50; see [[strife]], [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[loud-resounding]]). Ares shouts back from the other side, from the height of the city and along the Simoeis (20.51–53; see [[ares]]). On the evening before, when Achilles stood at the ditch and shouted, she cried out apart from him, and untold tumult rose among the Trojans (18.217–218). Aristarchus began the main clause at αὖε δʼ Ἀθήνη, “and Athena shouted”, taking the δέ there as redundant (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.48; see the translation's note on 20.48).

**Against Enyalius.** In the pairs, “against Enyalius the gray-eyed goddess Athena” (20.69; see [[enyalius]] and [[gray-eyed]]). She had driven Diomedes' spear home into the flank of Ares in Book 5 (5.855–859), and in Book 21 Ares, reminding her of it, strikes her aegis with his spear, and she fells him with a stone that men of old had set as a boundary mark of a field, and then strikes down Aphrodite, who is leading him away (21.391–426).

**At Lyrnessus.** Aeneas tells Apollo that when Achilles drove him from Ida he would have been brought down “at the hands of Achilles and of Athena, / who went before him and brought him light, and bade him / slay the Leleges and the Trojans with his bronze spear” (20.94–96; see [[aeneas]], [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]] and [[leleges]]). φάος, “light”, is salvation, as Leaf notes, comparing 6.6 and 16.39 (on 20.95). Achilles says the same: he sacked Lyrnessus “with Athena and father Zeus” (20.192).

**With Hera and Poseidon.** Hera turns to “Poseidon and Athena” when she sees Aeneas go out against Achilles (20.115; see [[hera]] and [[poseidon]]), and when Poseidon would save him she reminds him of the oaths that she and Athena have sworn, “I and Pallas Athena”, never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (20.313–317). Poseidon leads their party to the wall “which the Trojans and Pallas Athena / had made for him”, for Heracles, against the sea monster (20.146–148; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[heracles]]); in Book 8 she complained that Zeus forgot how often she had saved his son in his labors (8.362–369). Achilles, rallying the Achaeans, names her with Ares as gods who could not deal alone with so great a battle: “not even Ares, though he is an immortal god, nor Athena / could deal with the mouth of so great a battle and toil through it” (20.358–359).

**The breath.** Hector, facing Achilles after the death of Polydorus, hurls his spear, “and Athena / turned it back from glorious Achilles with a breath, / blowing very gently; and it came back to brilliant Hector, / and fell right there before his feet” (20.438–441; see [[hector]] and [[achilles]]). Leaf notes that a very gentle breath from a goddess' mouth is enough to drive back the spear, the same idea as in 444, where Apollo snatches Hector away “very easily, as a god can” (on 20.439). So she brushed Pandarus' arrow away from Menelaus, “as a mother / brushes a fly from her child” (4.130–131).
