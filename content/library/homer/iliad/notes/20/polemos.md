summary: Zeus “roused relentless war”, and the gods “set out to go to war, their hearts divided” (20.31–32); Poseidon would leave the war to men (20.137), and neither party of gods will begin “harsh war” (20.154). Among men the book is a series of challenges to fight face to face: Aeneas had promised “that you would fight face to face with Achilles” (20.85), says that “no man can fight face to face with Achilles” (20.97), and does; Achilles bids his men “let man go against man, and let him be eager to fight” (20.355); and Priam “would not let him fight at all”, his youngest son (20.408).
grc: [πολεμίξειν, μαχεῖται]
en: [fights]

μαχεῖται (20.26), a contracted future found nowhere else in Homer, Walter Leaf takes as a sign of lateness (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.26; see the translation's note on 20.26); πολεμίξειν (20.85) is the future that is usual after a verb of promising (Leaf on 20.85). Poseidon says that the battle and war “have blazed up at very close quarters” (20.18), a phrase Leaf finds odd at the one pause in the fighting since Book 11 (on 20.18; see the translation's note on 20.18).
