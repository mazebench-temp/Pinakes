summary: Tros, son of Alastor, comes to clasp Achilles' knees and beg to be taken alive, in the hope that Achilles will pity a man of his own age; but Achilles is “no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted”, and stabs him in the liver while he is still trying to reach the knees (20.463–472).
# λίσσεσθʼ (20.469) occurs only here.

{{quote:1e1c9dc9-0970-585f-a7ee-7c030d470e8f}}

**The gesture.** Tros comes ἀντίος … γούνων, “to meet him, to clasp his knees” (20.463), and “he was trying to clasp his knees with his hands, / eager to beg him” (20.468–469; see [[tros-son-of-alastor]] and [[gounata]]). Walter Leaf, after Hentze, takes the imperfect ἥπτετο as an attempt that fails, and λαβών in the next verse as “taking him prisoner”, not “taking him by the knees” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.464). The suppliant never makes the contact on which his claim rests, as Dolon was struck down while he reached for Diomedes' chin (10.454–457; see [[dolon]]), and as Menelaus pushed Adrastus away from him before Agamemnon killed him (6.62–65; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]).

**The plea.** What Tros hopes is told by the narrator, not spoken: that Achilles would take him and spare him and let him go alive, “and not kill him, pitying a man of his own age” (20.464–465; see [[pity]]). He offers no ransom, though Adrastus, Dolon and the sons of Antimachus had all offered one (6.46–50, 10.378–381, 11.131–135; see [[apoina]]). The narrator answers the hope at once: νήπιος, “the fool”, did not know that Achilles would not be persuaded, “for he was no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted, / but in a great fury” (20.466–468; see [[nepios]], [[sweet-tempered]] and [[gentle-hearted]]). Leaf suspects the verses (on 20.463; see the translation's note on 20.463–468).

**Spare.** πεφίδοιτο, the verb of sparing in Tros' hope (20.464), is the word of the next book, when Achilles tells Lycaon, who holds his knees, that before Patroclus died it was dearer to him to spare Trojans (πεφιδέσθαι) and sell them, but now none will escape (21.99–105; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]); and of the last, when Zeus sends word to Priam that Achilles “with great care … will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (24.158, 24.187; see [[priam]] and [[achilles]]). Every man who begs for his life on the battlefield of the Iliad is killed (see [[supplication-type-scene]]); the suppliant whom Achilles spares comes by night to his hut.
