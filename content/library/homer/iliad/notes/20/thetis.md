summary: Apollo tells Aeneas that his mother Aphrodite is the greater goddess, for Achilles' mother is only the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, and Aeneas names Achilles' parents in his reply, Peleus and “Thetis of the lovely tresses, daughter of the sea”. Hera speaks of the fate spun for Achilles when his mother bore him.
# Unnamed: "a lesser goddess ... the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea" (20.106-107), and "when his mother bore him"
# (20.128).
refs: [20.106-107, 20.128]
---

**The lesser goddess.** Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, urges Aeneas against Achilles with an argument from their mothers: “for they say that you too were born of Zeus' daughter Aphrodite, / and he is from a lesser goddess; / for one is the daughter of Zeus, and the other of the Old Man of the Sea” (20.105–107; see [[apollo]], [[aeneas]], [[aphrodite]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). Thetis is not named; she is the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, as she was when she sat beside her aged father in the depths in Book 1 (1.358; see [[nereids]]).

**Aeneas' answer.** In his reply to Achilles, Aeneas sets the parents side by side: “They say that you are the offspring of noble Peleus, / and that your mother is Thetis of the lovely tresses, daughter of the sea; / but I claim that I was born the son of great-hearted Anchises, / and my mother is Aphrodite. / Of these, one pair or the other will weep for their dear son / today” (20.206–211; see [[peleus]], [[anchises]] and [[of-the-lovely-tresses]]). ἁλοσύδνης, “daughter of the sea”, is found again only in the *Odyssey*, where the seals are the brood of “the lovely ἁλοσύδνη” (*Odyssey* 4.404); Walter Leaf takes it as “daughter of the salt water” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.207; see [[daughter-of-the-sea]] and the translation's note on 20.207). Aristarchus athetized 205–209 as needless, since both men already know each other's lineage (see the translation's note on 20.205–209).

**At his birth.** Hera says that the gods have come down so that Achilles may suffer nothing among the Trojans that day; “hereafter he shall suffer whatever his fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (20.127–128; see [[hera]], [[achilles]] and [[aisa]]).
