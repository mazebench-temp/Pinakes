summary: Aeneas' spear strikes the shield, and it groans loudly around the point; Achilles holds it away from him in fear, not considering that the gifts of the gods do not give way, and the gold, the god's gift, holds the spear back (20.259–268). Four verses that make the five layers metals, two of bronze, two of tin and one of gold, with the spear stopped in the gold, were athetized by Aristarchus (20.269–272). The narrator calls it the shield “that had kept grim destruction from him” (20.289).
# Unnamed: the shield struck and its layers (20.259-272), and the shield "that had kept grim destruction from him" (20.289).
refs: [20.259-272, 20.289]
---

**The god's gift.**

{{quote:f9b2a2c1-416a-569a-b568-89f1416b85be}}

δεινῷ σάκει … σμερδαλέῳ, “the dread shield, / the terrible shield” (20.259–260): Aristarchus read σάκεϊ ἔλασʼ, and Walter Leaf thinks σμερδαλέῳ weak in its emphatic place and would consider the adverb σμερδαλέον (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.259–260; see the translation's note on 20.259–260). The noise is the shield's: Leaf takes μέγα with μύκε, “groaned loudly”, and the larger body, not the point, as the source of the sound (on 20.260). Achilles fears the spear, “the fool” (20.264; see [[achilles]] and [[nepios]]), because he does not consider what the shield is. The thought joins two earlier verses: θεῶν ἐρικυδέα δῶρα, “The glorious gifts of the gods”, are what Paris would not cast aside (3.65), and “for mortal men to master” was said of Achilles' horses (10.403; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). χρυσὸς γὰρ ἐρύκακε, δῶρα θεοῖο, “for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god” (20.268), will be said again of Asteropaeus' spear (21.165). Leaf takes the gold, the most precious part, to stand for the whole metal facing of the shield, and the plural δῶρα for its several pieces (on 20.268; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**Five layers.**

{{quote:09c7ab0a-9fb5-5980-89bb-3b3678c68053}}

The words follow the duel of Hector and Ajax. Hector's spear “struck Ajax's dread shield of seven oxhides / on the outermost bronze, which was the eighth layer upon it. / Through six layers went the unyielding bronze, cutting its way, / but in the seventh hide it stopped” (7.245–248; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]): δεινὸν σάκος, διὰ πτύχας and σχέτο are the dread shield, the layers and the stopping of 20.259, 20.269 and 20.272. But Hephaestus' five πτύχες were not said to be metal when he made them (18.481; see [[shield]]), and Aristarchus athetized the four verses. Leaf calls them spurious, made by someone who took the five layers of 18.481 for the different metals, whereas they were surely of hide; even if they were metal, the gold would be hidden in the middle, where it would be neither useful nor ornamental, and 268, as 21.165 shows, needs no expansion (on 20.269; see the translation's note on 20.269–272, [[bronze]] and [[tin]]). Aristarchus' own reason, as Leaf reports it, was that the verses had been made up by someone who wished to raise a problem, and that they contradicted the genuine verses, by which what Hephaestus made cannot be pierced. So that he might not seem to have athetized them for want of a solution, he explained them as they stand: the outer layer stopped the point and only bent back the layers behind it, so that there was a hollow and not a wound. The place of the gold was a favorite problem; Porphyry put it in the middle, Aristarchus on the outside (Leaf on 20.269).

Aristotle quotes the last half-verse with χάλκεον, “bronze”, where the Iliad has μείλινον, “ash”: when a word seems to signify a contradiction, one must ask in how many ways it can mean it, as in “there the bronze spear was stopped” (*Poetics* 1461a31–35; see [[the-bronze-spear-stopped]] and the translation's note on 20.272). Leaf finds the passage of the Poetics practically unintelligible, but thinks that it probably points to a solution of the same kind (on 20.269).

**What it had done.** Aeneas' stone would have struck Achilles “on helmet or on the shield that had kept grim destruction from him” (20.289). τό οἱ ἤρκεσε λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον, with the aorist and no κε, is best taken, Leaf thinks, as a clause describing the shield, which had saved him at 268, rather than as part of what would have happened (on 20.289; see the translation's note on 20.289).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* Cycnus strikes Heracles' shield with his spear, οὐδʼ ἔρρηξεν χαλκόν· ἔρυτο δὲ δῶρα θεοῖο, “and did not break the bronze; the gifts of the god protected him” (*Shield* 413–415), a verse Leaf compares with 20.268 (on 20.268; see [[shield-of-heracles]]).
