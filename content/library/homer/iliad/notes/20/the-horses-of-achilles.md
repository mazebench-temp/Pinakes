# Unnamed: "his single-hoofed horses" and their trampling (20.498-502); horses claims ἵπποι and ἱππείων.
refs: [20.498-502]
---

The horses are not named in the book, and Achilles, who has fought on foot since he met Aeneas, is in his chariot only at its end. They come in a simile (see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]] and [[bulls]]):

{{quote:68c4ea42-acff-57a1-a117-8e7d383edaad}}

μώνυχες ἵπποι, “single-hoofed horses” (see [[single-hoofed]]), is the phrase with which Achilles drove them out among the foremost at the end of Book 19 (19.424). The four verses of the blood are those of Hector's horses in Book 11, “trampling on corpses and shields” as Cebriones drove them into the fight (20.499–502 = 11.534–537, with στεῖβον for στείβοντες; see [[chariot]] and [[the-horses-of-hector]]). Some editors rejected 495–503 for the borrowing and because Achilles, who has fought on foot, is suddenly in his chariot; Walter Leaf answers that heroes kept the chariot close at hand and mounted and dismounted as the moment required, without the poem's saying so, and that Achilles, having killed the chief men on foot, now drives over the rank and file (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.495; see the translation's note on 20.495–503).

The horses that stood weeping for Patroclus (17.426–440), and that promised through Xanthus to save Achilles “still, this time at least” (19.408; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]), here trample the Trojan dead under his chariot (see [[achilles]]).
