The river stands last in the pairs of gods that the battle sets against each other: “and against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river, / whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (20.73–74; see [[scamander]], [[hephaestus]] and [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). A few lines earlier it has gone to the Trojan side under its divine name alone, among the gods who favor Troy, “and Leto and Xanthus and laughter-loving Aphrodite” (20.40). Socrates describes it by the fight that follows: “the river at Troy, which fought in single combat with Hephaestus, ‘whom the gods,’ he says, ‘call Xanthus, and men Scamander’” (ὃς ἐμονομάχει τῷ Ἡφαίστῳ, *Cratylus* 391e). The single combat is told in Book 21, where Hera calls her son against the river and Hephaestus' fire overcomes it (21.328–382).

Aristotle's explanation of the divine name is the river's color: the Scamander is thought to make sheep yellow, “and that is why they say Homer calls it Xanthus instead of Scamander” (*History of Animals* 3.12; see [[aristotle]]). Proclus quotes the verse with the bird's when he argues, after the *Cratylus*, that the gods' names show the whole being of the things they name, while men's names touch them only in part (*On the Timaeus* I 273 Diehl; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
