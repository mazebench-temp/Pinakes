---
summary: The gods of the Achaean side sit on the wall that the Trojans and Athena built for Heracles, so that he could escape the sea monster when it chased him from the shore toward the plain.
---

Poseidon leads the gods of his party “to the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles, / the high wall, which the Trojans and Pallas Athena / had made for him, so that he might slip away and escape the sea monster / whenever it chased him from the shore toward the plain” (20.145–148; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]], [[heracles]] and [[poseidon]]). It is the one sea monster of the Iliad (the κήτεα of Book 13 are the beasts of the sea that know Poseidon as their lord, 13.27–28), and the poem does not tell where it came from or how it ended. τὸ κῆτος, with the article, is “the” sea monster, as of something well known: Walter Leaf notes that this use of the article is very rare in Homer except with a few nouns, and confined to late passages of the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.147). μιν, “him”, is Heracles, and the monster is the subject of σεύαιτο, “chased” (on 20.148). The monster comes out of the sea onto the shore and pursues Heracles inland, and the wall shelters him from it.

In Apollodorus the monster was Poseidon's, sent against the people of the plain when Laomedon cheated him and Apollo of their wages for fortifying Pergamum, and Heracles killed it to save Laomedon's daughter Hesione (*Library* 2.5.9; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]], [[hesione]], [[laomedon]] and [[apollodorus]]). If the Iliad knew that story, Poseidon sits in Book 20 on a wall built against his own monster; the poem does not say.
