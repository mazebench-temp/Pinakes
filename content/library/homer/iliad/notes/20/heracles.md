---
summary: Poseidon leads the gods of the Achaean side to the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles, which the Trojans and Athena built for him so that he could escape the sea monster.
---

“So he spoke, and the dark-haired god led the way / to the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles, / the high wall, which the Trojans and Pallas Athena / had made for him, so that he might slip away and escape the sea monster / whenever it chased him from the shore toward the plain” (20.144–148; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]], [[sea-beasts]] and [[poseidon]]). The poem tells the episode as something its hearers know: the monster is τὸ κῆτος, “the” monster (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.147), and the wall stands on the plain. The Trojans, whose city Heracles sacked because Laomedon would not give him the horses he had earned (5.638–642, 5.648–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]] and [[laomedon]]), built the wall with Athena, who in Book 8 complains that Zeus forgets how often she saved his son in his labors (8.362–363; see [[athena]]).

θείοιο, “divine”, is said of other heroes too, of Thoas of Lemnos (θείοιο Θόαντος, 14.230) and of Odysseus in the *Odyssey* (θείου Ὀδυσῆος, *Odyssey* 2.259; see [[divine]]). Leaf reports the legend from the A scholia and Apollodorus, after Hellanicus: Laomedon exposed his daughter Hesione to the monster that Poseidon sent, and promised his horses to whoever killed it, and Heracles killed it with the protection of a wall that Pallas built for him, but was given mortal horses instead of the immortal ones (on 20.145; see [[hesione]]). Apollodorus, as it has come down, tells the monster and Hesione but not the wall (*Library* 2.5.9; see [[apollodorus]]).
