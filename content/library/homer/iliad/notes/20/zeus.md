summary: Zeus has Themis call the gods to his house, and when Poseidon asks his plan he lets them go down and help either side, for the Trojans could not hold Achilles alone, and he fears Achilles may sack the wall against fate; he himself will sit in a fold of Olympus and watch. He thunders as the gods come together, and sits on high while both parties hold back. In the speeches he is the father of Aphrodite and of Dardanus, the god who rescued Aeneas on Ida and who increases and diminishes the prowess of men, and Poseidon says that he has come to hate the lineage of Priam.

**The assembly.** “And Zeus bade Themis call the gods to assembly / from the summit of many-folded Olympus” (20.4–5; see [[themis-the-goddess]], [[assembly]] and [[many-folded]]). They come “to the house of Zeus the cloud-gatherer” and sit down “in the polished colonnades, which for father Zeus / Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill” (20.10–12; see [[hephaestus]] and [[with-cunning-skill]]). Zeus last called the gods together on the morning of the second day of battle, when he held an assembly “on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus” and forbade any god to help either the Trojans or the Danaans, on pain of coming back to Olympus beaten (8.2–12; see the note on Book 8). Poseidon, come up out of the sea, asks him what he means: “Why, lord of the gleaming thunderbolt, have you called the gods to assembly again? / Are you pondering something about the Trojans and the Achaeans?” (20.16–17; see [[poseidon]] and [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]]).

**The gods released.** Zeus answers:

{{quote:1d3aebc3-b3f2-58ca-a81e-2283bd4889f0}}

μέλουσί μοι ὀλλύμενοί περ, “I care for them, even as they perish” (20.21): Walter Leaf notes that a god could say ὀλλύμενοι of either side, but takes it to show that the Trojans are uppermost in Zeus' thoughts (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.21). In Book 4 he told Hera that of all the cities under the sun “sacred Ilios was the most honored in my heart, / and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear” (4.46–47). His care is a spectator's. He will stay “in a fold of Olympus” (πτυχὶ Οὐλύμποιο), where the gods have their houses “in the folds of Olympus” (11.77), and “look on and delight my heart” (20.22–23; see [[olympus]] and [[watching-the-war]]). Leaf finds the phrase rather inconsistent with line 21, and sees it carried to excess in Book 21 (on 20.23), where Zeus, sitting on Olympus, laughs for joy when he sees the gods come together in strife (21.388–390; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

The release undoes the ban of Book 8: the gods are now to “help either side, whichever way each of you has a mind” (20.25). The reason is Achilles. If he fights alone, the Trojans will not hold him “even for a little while”, and Zeus fears “he may even sack the wall against fate” (20.26–30; see [[achilles]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). ὑπέρμορον, “against fate”, is what Zeus fears, not what he wills (see the translation's note on 20.30). Leaf reports that some substituted other lines, preserved in the T scholia, for 29–30, because the god who knows everything ought not to fear what might happen; and he objects that Zeus seems to forget that Achilles stands to gain from the gods' intervention, since the gods of the Achaean side are the stronger (on 20.26, 20.30), as Poseidon himself says (20.135). At the end of the battle of the gods Apollo goes into Ilios for the same reason, for the wall of the city is his care, lest the Danaans sack it beyond fate (ὑπὲρ μόρον) that day (21.515–517). “So spoke the son of Cronus, and roused relentless war” (20.31; see [[son-of-cronus]] and [[relentless]]).

**Thunder from on high.** When the gods have come into the throng, “terribly the father of men and gods thundered / from on high; and below, Poseidon shook / the boundless earth and the steep peaks of the mountains” (20.56–58; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]] and [[poseidon]]). The brothers who drew the sky and the sea as their lots mark the two ends of the world, and the third, Aidoneus, is afraid beneath them (20.61–65; see [[hades]] and [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Then both parties of gods sit apart, loath to begin harsh war, “though Zeus, sitting on high, bade them” (20.155). Leaf, with Hentze, takes κέλευε absolutely, “was supreme over them”, since Zeus had not told the gods to fight one another (on 20.155; see the translation's note on 20.155).

**In the speeches.** Aeneas tells Apollo that when Achilles drove him from Ida and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus, “Zeus / rescued me, who roused my might and my nimble knees” (20.92–93; see [[aeneas]] and [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). Achilles answers that he sacked Lyrnessus “with Athena and father Zeus”, “but Zeus and the other gods rescued you” (20.192–194; see [[father-zeus]]). Apollo's argument is that Aeneas' mother is “Zeus' daughter Aphrodite”, and Achilles' mother only the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea (20.105–107; see [[aphrodite]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). Aeneas begins his lineage with Zeus: “first Zeus the cloud-gatherer fathered Dardanus” (20.215; see [[dardanus]] and [[cloud-gatherer]]), and of Ganymede he says that “the gods snatched him up to pour wine for Zeus” (20.234; see [[ganymede]] and [[cupbearing]]). He ends with a maxim: “But it is Zeus who increases and diminishes the prowess of men, / as he wishes; for he is the mightiest of all” (20.242–243; see [[arete]]). Leaf takes it as an answer to Achilles' sarcasm about Aeneas' flight at Lyrnessus (on 20.242).

**The lineage of Dardanus.** When Aeneas is about to die, Poseidon fears “lest the son of Cronus too be angry, if Achilles / kills this man”, for he is fated to escape, so that the lineage of Dardanus may not perish, “whom the son of Cronus loved above all the children / who were born to him from mortal women. / For already the son of Cronus has come to hate the lineage of Priam” (20.301–306; see [[genee]], [[priam]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Leaf takes the aorist ἔχθηρε as petulant, “has come to hate”, with particular reference to Zeus' recent decision to let the war take its course; the imperfect ἤχθαιρε, an ancient variant, would contradict his conduct too flagrantly (on 20.306). He finds the words as inconsistent with the attitude of Zeus as Poseidon's rescue is with Poseidon's (on 20.293). In Book 24 Zeus will still call Hector the dearest to the gods of all mortals in Ilios, for his gifts never failed (24.66–70).
