---
summary: When the gods come into the throng of men, mighty Strife rises up, rouser of armies, and Athena and Ares shout on either side.
---

“But when the Olympians had come into the throng of men, / mighty Strife rose up, who rouses armies, and Athena shouted” (20.47–48; see [[olympians]], [[athena]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). ὦρτο δʼ Ἔρις κρατερὴ λαοσσόος: λαοσσόος, “who rouses armies”, is said of Strife here, of Apollo when he rouses Aeneas (20.79), of Athena (13.128) and of Ares (17.398; see [[who-rouses-armies]] and [[krateros]]). Walter Leaf takes this as the start of the main clause, where Aristarchus began it at αὖε δʼ Ἀθήνη, “and Athena shouted” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.48; see the translation's note on 20.48).

The scene is the one of Book 4, where the armies first met: “Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those, / and Terror and Rout, and Strife, raging without end, / the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares” (4.439–441). Here too Strife rises between Athena and Ares, and the two gods shout to the armies, Athena by the ditch and on the shore, Ares from the city and along the Simoeis (20.48–53; see [[ares]]). Then the common word takes over: the gods “made heavy strife break out among them” (ἔριδα, 20.55), and the din rose “as the gods came together in strife” (ἔριδι, 20.66; see [[eris]]). Poseidon will not drive the gods together in strife (ἔριδι, 20.134). In Book 21 strife falls upon the gods themselves, heavy and grievous (ἔρις … βεβριθυῖα / ἀργαλέη, 21.385–386), and Zeus laughs to see them come together in strife (21.390).
