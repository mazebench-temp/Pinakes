summary: Aeneas and Achilles both remember the sack before their duel, from opposite sides. Aeneas tells Apollo how Achilles drove him from his cattle on Ida and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus, while Zeus saved him and Athena led Achilles on (20.89–96); Achilles reminds Aeneas how he fled to Lyrnessus, and how the gods saved him there, though the town was sacked and its women led away (20.187–194).

**Aeneas' memory.** Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, has asked where Aeneas' threats have gone. Aeneas answers that he has stood against Achilles before:

{{quote:8e267afe-66cd-552f-b380-928c2e1075ad}}

“Our cattle”: Aeneas was with the herds on Ida, where Aphrodite bore him to Anchises “as he tended his cattle” (5.313; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]] and [[ida]]). In his telling the sack is the gods' work on both sides. Zeus saved him, rousing his might and his knees, and Achilles had Athena before him, who “brought him light” (τίθει φάος, 20.95), the light that a helper brings in battle, as Ajax “brought light to his companions” (6.6); Walter Leaf explains the word here as “salvation” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.95; see [[athena]]). He names two towns and a people: Lyrnessus, Pedasus and the Leleges (see [[lyrnessus]], [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]] and [[leleges]]).

**Achilles' memory.** Achilles tells it to Aeneas' face, to shame him:

{{quote:afdca81b-f543-5e85-87f5-9fb41c83d352}}

His version adds what Aeneas left out: that Aeneas was alone, that he ran down the mountains and “never turned around as you fled” (μετατροπαλίζεο, only here in Homer, 20.190), that he took refuge in Lyrnessus, and that Achilles sacked the town in pursuit of him and led away its women (see [[captive-women]] and [[eleutheros]]). It leaves out what Aeneas said, Pedasus and the Leleges. And where Aeneas set Zeus against Athena, Achilles has Zeus on both sides: he sacked the town “with Athena and father Zeus”, and “Zeus and the other gods rescued you” (20.192–194). Heyne remarked, as Leaf reports, on the lightness with which the gods' names are used here (on 20.194; see [[achilles]]). Leaf takes Aeneas' answer, “it is Zeus who increases and diminishes the prowess of men” (20.242), for a reply to this taunt about his flight (on 20.242).

**The same sack.** Both men speak of one raid. The Catalogue told it from the victor's side, as the labor that won Achilles Briseis (2.689–693), and Briseis from inside the city (19.291–300). Book 20 adds the herds on Ida, the flight and the escape of a Trojan prince, and shows the two men remembering the day as they face each other (see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). Strabo quotes Achilles' lines in his account of Demetrius of Scepsis, who used them to place the royal seat of Aeneas (*Geography* 13.1.53; see [[lyrnessus]] and [[strabo-on-the-rule-of-aeneas]]).
