summary: Hera leads the gods who go to the ships, and Artemis is set against her. When Aeneas goes to face Achilles she calls on Poseidon and Athena to turn him back, or to stand by Achilles so that he may suffer nothing that day, whatever his fate spun for him at his birth; Poseidon checks her. When Poseidon would save Aeneas she leaves it to him, for she and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, even when all Troy burns.

**To the ships.** Hera is the first of the gods named when they go down to the war: “Hera went to the gathering of the ships” (20.33; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). In the pairs that follow, “against Hera stood she of the golden distaff, the clamorous / Artemis of the showering arrows, sister of the Far-Shooter” (20.70–71; see [[artemis]]). The two meet in Book 21, where Hera seizes Artemis by both wrists and beats her about the ears with her own bow, smiling, until she flees in tears (21.479–496).

**Her counsel.** “Nor did the son of Anchises escape the notice of white-armed Hera / as he went to face the son of Peleus through the crowd of men” (20.112–113; see [[white-armed]] and [[aeneas]]). She gathers the gods, who here are only Poseidon and Athena, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.114), and speaks:

{{quote:94799ae7-f569-59be-affb-49fb1bc4e3f6}}

She would have Achilles know “that the best of the immortals love him, / and that those are empty wind” who have helped the Trojans (20.122–123; see [[achilles]]), and her warning ends with a maxim, “for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (20.131; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). Aristarchus athetized 125–128 as contradicting Zeus, who fears that Achilles will be too strong, not that he will suffer (20.26–30); Leaf answers that πάντες, “all”, means only the gods of the Achaean side (on 20.125; see the translation's note on 20.125–128). The thread spun at birth is the image of 24.209–210 and of the *Odyssey* (7.197–198; Leaf on 20.128; see [[aisa]]). Her care for Achilles is the care of Book 18, when she sent Iris to bid him show himself to the Trojans at the ditch (18.165–198; see [[iris]]), and of Book 19, when she gave his horse a human voice (19.407; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]).

**Checked.** Poseidon answers her: “Hera, do not rage beyond good sense; you have no need to” (20.133; see [[poseidon]] and the translation's note on 20.133). In Book 8 he called her “reckless in speech” when she proposed that the gods of the Danaans should hold off Zeus (8.209), and he refuses her now in almost the same words (20.134–135 ≈ 8.210–211): the gods are not to drive one another into strife, but to sit apart and leave the war to men.

**Her oath.** When Poseidon grieves for Aeneas and proposes to save him, “Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him” (20.309; see [[ox-eyed]] and [[queenly]]): let him decide for himself whether to rescue Aeneas,

{{quote:24878767-45b7-5cb1-b628-a5ad85a5f96e}}

The poem tells of the oath only here. Leaf takes the plural ὅρκους to mean oaths by many objects, that is, of the most solemn kind (on 20.313; see [[oath]]). In Book 21 the river Xanthus, beaten by the fire of Hephaestus, swears in the same words never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (21.373–376 = 20.315–317; see [[scamander]] and the translation's note on 20.315–317). Leaf reports that some editors reject 316–317 here, and finds them perhaps more in place in Book 21 (on 20.315). Her enmity is Athena's too: “I and Pallas Athena” (20.314; see [[athena]]). In Book 21 it is Hera who sets Hephaestus on the river, ἐμὸν τέκος, “my child” (21.328–341; see [[hephaestus]]).
