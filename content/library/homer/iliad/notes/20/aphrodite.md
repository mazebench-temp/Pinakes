---
summary: Aphrodite goes to war with the gods of the Trojans, but she is the one god without an opponent when the others stand paired, and she does nothing in the book. Her son's birth is her part in it. Apollo sends Aeneas against Achilles because his mother is the daughter of Zeus and Achilles' only the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, and Aeneas names her as his mother when he faces Achilles (20.40, 20.104–109, 20.209).
# Unnamed: "one is the daughter of Zeus" (20.107).
refs: [20.107]
---

**On the Trojan side.** She goes down to the war with Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto and Xanthus, “and laughter-loving Aphrodite” (20.38–40; see [[laughter-loving]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). φιλομειδής is printed here with one μ, as at 3.424, 4.10 and 14.211 (see the translation's note on 14.211). When the gods take their stand against one another, five pairs are named, and she is not in any of them (20.67–74). In the next book, when she leads the wounded Ares away, Athena strikes her to the ground (21.416–426).

**The greater goddess.** Apollo, sending her son against Achilles, makes her rank his argument:

{{quote:2db11768-7cc4-59e8-a43c-8187d380adba}}

He ranks the mothers by their fathers. Aphrodite is “Zeus' daughter”, as she is in the verse of her rescues, “Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus” (3.374, 5.312; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]); Thetis is the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea (see [[thetis]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). The fight does not bear the argument out. In Book 5 Zeus told her that the works of war were not given to her (5.428–430; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]), and Poseidon, saving her son, tells him that Achilles is “at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals” (20.334). In the next book Achilles makes the same kind of argument against Asteropaeus, grandson of a river, over his body: Zeus is stronger than rivers, and so the race of Zeus is stronger than the race of a river (21.184–191).

**“My mother is Aphrodite.”** Aeneas claims her when he answers Achilles: “but I claim that I was born the son of great-hearted Anchises, / and my mother is Aphrodite” (20.208–209), the words in which Sthenelus named him in Book 5, now in his own mouth (5.247–248; see [[aeneas]], [[anchises]] and [[euchomai]]). He sets her beside Thetis, “Thetis of the lovely tresses, daughter of the sea”, and one of the two pairs of parents, he says, will weep for a son today (20.206–212). Neither does. Poseidon, not Aphrodite, takes Aeneas out of the fight (20.318–329; see [[divine-rescue]]). Walter Leaf thinks that he would naturally have been saved by Apollo, who sent him, or by his mother, as in Book 5 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.293; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]).
