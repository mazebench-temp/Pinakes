summary: Achilles longs above all to meet Hector. Hector tells the Trojans that he will go against Achilles even if his hands are like fire, but Apollo bids him wait in the crowd, and he goes back, afraid. When Achilles kills his brother Polydorus he can hold back no longer; he answers Achilles' taunt that he knows he is the worse man, but these things lie on the knees of the gods. Athena turns his spear back with a breath, and Apollo snatches him away in a thick mist, which Achilles strikes three times in vain.

**Achilles' wish.** While the gods go to meet one another, “Achilles / longed above all to plunge into the throng to meet Hector, / son of Priam”, to glut Ares with his blood (20.75–78; see [[achilles]] and [[son-of-priam]]). It is the vow of Book 18, that Achilles would not live unless Hector were first struck by his spear (18.91–93). Aeneas, telling his lineage, ends with the two branches of the house of Tros: “and Anchises fathered me, and Priam brilliant Hector” (20.240; see [[genealogy-in-battle]], [[aeneas]] and [[priam]]).

**To the Trojans.** When Achilles has called on the Achaeans, “shining Hector called to the Trojans / with a shout, and said that he would go against Achilles” (20.364–365; see [[phaidimos]] and [[trojans]]):

{{quote:92856e34-ddb0-56c6-916d-a81bc6504387}}

He answers Achilles' boast that not even Ares or Athena could deal with so great a battle (20.356–359): he too could fight the immortals in words, but not with the spear, “since they are far stronger than we are” (20.368), the half-verse in which Odysseus told Nestor that a god could easily give even better horses (10.556–557; see the translation's note on 10.557). Walter Leaf thinks the future κολούσει, “will cut short”, necessary if the verse is to stand, or else that the verse was a saying about Zeus, interpolated here (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.370; see the translation's note on 20.370). “If his hands are like fire” is said twice, a repetition of a whole phrase that Leaf finds again only at 22.128 and 23.642 (on 20.371; see [[iron]]). The resolve is the one he made in the assembly of the night before, that he would not flee from Achilles but stand face to face (18.307–308). The Trojans raise their spears, and the war cry rises (20.373–374).

**Apollo's warning.**

{{quote:7a58eab6-8776-5da9-b713-7db39ff3729d}}

προμάχιζε, “step out as a champion”, is the verb of Paris stepping out before the Trojans in Book 3 (3.16; see [[apollo]]). ἐκ φλοίσβοιο, “out of the din”, Leaf takes as the surging mass of the warriors behind the front fighters, the οὐλαμός, “crowd”, into which Hector withdraws (on 20.377). Achilles bade Aeneas do the same, “draw back / and go into the crowd” (20.196–197), and Poseidon told Aeneas, “draw back whenever you meet him” (20.335; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]): of the two champions of the Trojans, the gods save one and hold back the other. ταρβήσας, “afraid” (20.380): Hector obeys the voice of the god.

**Polydorus.** Achilles kills Iphition, Demoleon and Hippodamas, and then Polydorus, the youngest of Priam's sons (20.381–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]).

{{quote:df6876a6-7af8-526e-94b7-90c1317b8f44}}

κασίγνητον, “his brother” (see [[kasignetos]]): Polydorus was Priam's son by Laothoe, not by Hecuba (21.84–91, 21.95). “A mist was poured over his eyes”, κέχυτʼ ἀχλύς, is said of men at the point of death or of fainting (5.696, 16.344; see [[achlys]] and the translation's note on 20.421). Apollo's warning is undone: οὐδʼ ἄρʼ ἔτʼ ἔτλη, “and he could no longer bear” to keep away (20.421–422). The same words will say the opposite in Book 22, when Achilles comes on and trembling seizes Hector, “and he could no longer bear” to stay where he stood, and he runs (22.136–137). He comes on “like a flame” (20.423; see [[like-a-flame]]).

**Face to face.** It is the first time in the poem that the two men meet. Achilles leaps up in triumph at the sight of “the man … / who killed my honored companion” (20.425–427; see [[patroclus]] and [[obscure-gephyrai]]), and taunts him with the verse with which Diomedes met Glaucus, “Come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (20.429 = 6.143; see [[looking-darkly]]). He answers οὐ ταρβήσας, “unafraid” (20.430), where at the god's warning he went back ταρβήσας, “afraid” (20.380); the half-verse introduces Diomedes' answers to Pandarus and to Paris (5.286, 11.384; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]).

{{quote:aa378b47-dc43-56b1-b6d0-6f6aadaaf6b4}}

The first three verses are Aeneas' answer to Achilles' taunt (20.431–433 = 20.200–202; see [[kertomia]] and [[nepios]]). “And I know that you are brave, and I am far worse than you” (20.434): Leaf says that the scholiasts lose the whole significance of the line by taking it as ironic, and sees in it the dignity of a man who faces with heroic frankness a contest he admits to be unequal (on 20.434). The outcome he leaves to the gods, in the verse of Automedon in Book 17 (20.435 = 17.514; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). πάροιθεν (20.437) is taken in the translation as “at the point”; the T scholia and Eustathius took it as “before now”, with a reference to the death of Patroclus (Leaf on 20.437; see the translation's note on 20.437).

**The rescue.** Athena turns his spear back with a breath, and it falls at his feet (20.438–441; see [[athena]]). As Achilles springs at him, Apollo snatches him away “very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist” (20.443–444), the verses in which Aphrodite saved Paris from Menelaus (3.380–381; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[apollo]]). Achilles strikes the mist three times and shouts after him, in the words Diomedes had used when Apollo's helmet saved Hector in Book 11, “Once again you have escaped death now, you dog” (20.449–454 = 11.362–367; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[dogs]]). Phoebus Apollo is the god “to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins” (20.451). “I will surely finish you off when I meet you later” (20.452): the next meeting is before the Scaean gates (see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). The only earlier meeting of the two that the poem mentions, in Achilles' words to the envoys, was at the same gates, where Hector waited for him alone and “barely escaped my charge” (9.352–355; see [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]).

His epithets in the book are φαίδιμος, “shining” (20.364), δῖος, “brilliant” (20.240, 20.428, 20.440), and κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (20.430); he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (20.77; see [[phaidimos]], [[brilliant]], [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
