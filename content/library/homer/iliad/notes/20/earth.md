---
summary: When the gods join the war, Zeus thunders from on high and Poseidon shakes the boundless earth from below, and Aidoneus leaps from his throne in fear that the earth will be broken open and the house of the dead laid bare (20.56–66). The earth rumbles under the armies' feet (20.157), receives the spears and the dead of the book, and at the end runs black with blood (20.494).
---

**The earthquake.** δεινὸν δὲ βρόντησε πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε / ὑψόθεν· αὐτὰρ νέρθε Ποσειδάων ἐτίναξε / γαῖαν ἀπειρεσίην ὀρέων τʼ αἰπεινὰ κάρηνα, “and terribly the father of men and gods thundered / from on high; and below, Poseidon shook / the boundless earth and the steep peaks of the mountains” (20.56–58). ἀπειρέσιος, “boundless”, is found only here in the Iliad (see the translation's table). The feet and peaks of Ida, the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans all shake (20.59–60; see [[ida]]), and beneath the earth Aidoneus, lord of those below, leaps from his throne and cries out, “lest above him / Poseidon the earth-shaker should break the earth open, / and his dwellings be laid bare to mortals and immortals, / terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (20.62–65; see [[hades]] and [[poseidon]]). The three sons of Cronus are at their three levels: Zeus above, Poseidon on and in the earth, Hades beneath it. In Book 15 Poseidon told how the three had divided all things by lot, the sky to Zeus, the gray sea to himself, the misty darkness to Hades, “but the earth is still common to all, and high Olympus” (15.187–193). Here the common earth is where the battle of the gods is felt: struck from above, shaken from below, and in danger of breaking open onto the world beneath (see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]] and [[earth-shaker]]). The author of *On the Sublime* quotes Aidoneus' fear among the terrors of the battle of the gods (9.6; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]]). When the Achaean army marched out in Book 2, the earth groaned beneath its feet as when Zeus lashes the earth around Typhoeus (2.780–784; see [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]); in Book 13 the high mountains and the woods trembled under Poseidon's feet as he strode down from the peak of Samothrace (13.17–19).

**Under the armies.** “and the earth rumbled beneath their feet / as they rushed on together” (20.157–158). κάρκαιρε, “rumbled”, is an imitative word found only here in Greek (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.156; see the translation's note on 20.157).

**Plowland and grain.** Achilles taunts Aeneas with a domain “of orchard and plowland” that the Trojans might mark out for him (ἀρούρης, 20.185), in the words of the domains of Bellerophon and of Sarpedon in Lycia (6.194–195, 12.313–314). The foals of Erichthonius' mares ran “over the grain-giving earth” and did not break the ears (20.226–227; see [[grain-giving]] and [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]]).

**The ground of the fighting.** Achilles' spear passes over Aeneas' back and “stood fixed in the ground, still straining” (20.279–280), as it will over Lycaon's in the next book (21.69–70). Poseidon lifts Aeneas high “from the ground” and sends him over the ranks, and Achilles, the mist taken from his eyes, finds his spear “lying on the ground” and no man to be seen (20.325, 20.344–346; see [[divine-rescue]]). The dead go down to it: Polydorus sinks “to the ground” holding his bowels (20.420; ποτὶ γαίη, printed without the iota of the dative: see the translation's note on 20.420), and Deucalion “lay stretched out on the ground” (20.483). At the end of Achilles' rampage “the black earth ran with blood” (20.494; see [[black-earth]], [[the-armies-clash]] and [[forest-fire]]).
