summary: Book 20 has two rescues from Achilles. Poseidon, a god of the Achaean side, saves Aeneas because he is fated to survive: he pours a mist over Achilles' eyes and sends Aeneas flying over the ranks to the edge of the battle (20.288–340). Apollo snatches Hector away in a mist, in the verses of Aphrodite's rescue of Paris (20.443–444). Both men have been saved before, Aeneas by Zeus at Lyrnessus, as he and Achilles remember it, and Hector by Apollo in Book 11.

**“Had not.”** “Then Aeneas would have hit him with the stone as he rushed on, / on helmet or on the shield that had kept grim destruction from him, / and the son of Peleus would have taken his life with his sword at close quarters, / had not Poseidon the earth-shaker been quick to see it” (20.288–291). The last verse is the verse of Aphrodite's rescues of Paris and of Aeneas himself, with Poseidon's name (3.374, 5.312; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**A council before the rescue.** The rescue is debated before it is made. Poseidon puts the case to the gods of his side: pity for the man, his innocence and his gifts, the anger of Zeus, and fate. Aeneas is fated to escape, so that the lineage of Dardanus may not perish (20.293–308; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Hera leaves him to decide, “whether you will rescue Aeneas” (20.311), since she and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (20.313–317). In Book 16 Zeus asked whether to save Sarpedon, and Hera talked him out of it: Sarpedon was a mortal “long since ordained to his portion” (16.440–449). Aeneas' portion is to live.

**The rescue.**

{{quote:02d3fee9-6348-51bd-8172-21dd64c3b9fb}}

The mist goes over the eyes of the attacker, not around the man who is saved, as when Aphrodite hid Paris in thick mist and Apollo covered Aeneas in a dark cloud (3.381, 5.344–345; see [[achlys]]). Poseidon gives Achilles back his spear, drawn out of Aeneas' shield, in three verses that Aristarchus athetized, because the spear had passed through the shield and stood in the ground (20.322–324, 20.276–280; see the translation's note on 20.322–324). Then he sends Aeneas flying over the battle. There is no place of safety at the end of the flight, as there was for Paris in his bedroom and for Aeneas in Apollo's temple (3.382, 5.445–448), only the far edge of the war, where the Caucones are arming (see [[caucones]]). The god warns the man he has saved, as Apollo warned the attacker in Book 5 (5.440–442): Aeneas is to keep away from Achilles until Achilles is dead (20.332–339).

**The attacker.** Poseidon scatters the mist, and Achilles sees his spear on the ground and no man. “Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes” (20.344), he says, in the words of Thoas when he saw Hector back in the battle, “once again” saved by one of the gods (15.286–291). He concludes that Aeneas too was dear to the gods, and lets him go (20.347–352; see [[achilles]] and [[achilles-and-aeneas]]).

**Hector.** Later in the book Hector comes against Achilles. Athena turns his spear back from Achilles with a breath, as she turned Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus (20.438–440; 4.127–133; see [[athena]]), and the spear comes back to Hector:

The two verses of the rescue are the verses of Aphrodite's rescue of Paris, with Apollo's name (20.443–444 ≈ 3.380–381). Achilles' three charges at the mist are Diomedes' at Aeneas, three times thrown back by Apollo (5.436–438; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]), and what he shouts after Hector is what Diomedes shouted when Apollo's helmet saved him in Book 11: “Once again you have escaped death now, you dog” (20.449–454 = 11.362–367; see [[hector]]).

**Saved before.** The book remembers an older rescue. Aeneas tells Apollo that when Achilles sacked Lyrnessus, “Zeus / rescued me” (20.92–93); Achilles tells Aeneas that “Zeus and the other gods rescued you”, and that “they will not rescue you now” (20.194–195; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). The verb is ἐρύομαι, “rescue”, the verb of Apollo's rescue of Aeneas in Book 5 (ἐρύσατο, 5.344), of Hera's question to Poseidon (ἐρύσσεαι, 20.311) and of Achilles' words to Hector, “now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you” (ἐρύσατο, 20.450). Aeneas himself thinks that Achilles has such a god always at his side, “who keeps ruin away” (20.98), the words Diomedes spoke of Hector (5.603).
