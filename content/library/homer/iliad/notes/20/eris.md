---
summary: Strife among the gods opens the book: they bring the armies together “and made heavy strife break out among them” (20.55), and the din rises “as the gods came together in strife” (20.66). Poseidon would not “drive the gods together in strife” (20.134). Aeneas asks Achilles “what need is there for strife and quarrels between us two” and likens them to women quarreling “in their anger over heart-devouring strife” (20.251–253).
---

Strife herself rises with the gods (20.48; see [[strife]]). ῥήγνυντο, “made … break out”, is a phrase found only here, and Walter Leaf understands ἐν δʼ αὐτοῖς as “among men”, though “among themselves” seems more natural (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.55; see the translation's note on 20.55). The strife of the gods breaks out only to be put off: both parties sit apart and neither will begin (20.133–155; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Aristarchus athetized Aeneas' comparison as untimely after 20.244; Leaf finds it the most vigorous passage in the speech, but thinks ἔριδος πέρι θυμοβόροιο almost a travesty of the phrase used of Hector and Ajax (7.301; Leaf on 20.251; see [[women-quarreling-in-the-street]], [[heart-devouring]] and [[neikos]]).
