Aeneas, giving his lineage to Achilles, names the three sons of Tros, “Ilus and Assaracus and godlike Ganymede, / who was the most beautiful of mortal men; / and the gods snatched him up to pour wine for Zeus, / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (20.232–235; see [[ganymede]], [[tros]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The critics' problem was the verb. The gods drink no wine, as Book 5 says when Aphrodite bleeds ichor (5.341), and yet Ganymede is carried off Διὶ οἰνοχοεύειν, “to pour wine for Zeus”. Aristotle quotes the two words as they stand in the verse and answers by the custom of the language: as men who work iron are still called bronze-smiths, so Ganymede is said to pour wine for Zeus, though the gods do not drink wine; or else the word is a metaphor (*Poetics* 25, 1461a29–31). Walter Leaf notes Aristotle's remark on this slight extension of the verb to nectar (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.234; see [[cupbearing]]).

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* uses the same verb in the same story. Zeus snatched fair-haired Ganymede for his beauty, “so that he might be among the immortals”, as Book 20 also says (ἵνʼ ἀθανάτοισι μετείη), “and pour wine for the gods in the house of Zeus” (θεοῖς ἐπιοινοχοεύοι), “drawing red nectar from a golden mixing bowl” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–206; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[nectar]]). In the Hymn it is Zeus who takes him, in the Iliad the gods (θεοί, 20.234).
