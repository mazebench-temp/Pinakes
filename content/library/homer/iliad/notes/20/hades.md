**The lord below.** Zeus thunders from on high and Poseidon shakes the earth and the mountains below, and the third brother is afraid beneath them (see [[zeus]], [[poseidon]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]):

{{quote:45bbf05c-e345-54a7-a320-d04489228937}}

ἄναξ ἐνέρων Ἀϊδωνεύς, “Aidoneus, lord of those below”, gives him the title of the division of the world in Book 15, where Poseidon names him “Hades, lord of those below” (Ἀΐδης ἐνέροισιν ἀνάσσων, 15.188; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). There the earth was left common to all three brothers (15.193); here the brother who holds the sea shakes it so that the brother below fears for his house. Ἀϊδωνεύς, the longer form of the name, is found in Homer only here and at 5.190, where Pandarus thought he would hurl Diomedes “down to Aidoneus”. εὐρώεντα, “dank”, is from εὐρώς, “mould”, as the house of Hades is dank in the *Odyssey* (10.512, 23.322); some ancient readers took it as “spacious”, from εὐρύς, which Walter Leaf thinks weakens every passage where it occurs (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.64; see [[dank]] and the translation's note on 20.61–65). The critic of *On the Sublime* quotes these lines for the terror of the battle of the gods: the earth torn open, Tartarus laid bare, the whole world overturned (*On the Sublime* 9.6; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]]). Plato's Socrates quotes the last two among the verses that make the house of Hades fearful, which he would strike out of poetry (*Republic* 3.386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).

**In Hesiod.** The *Theogony* says almost the same words of the realm below, with ἀργαλέα, “grievous”, for σμερδαλέα, “terrible”: the sources and limits of earth and Tartarus, of sea and sky, are ἀργαλέʼ εὐρώεντα, τά τε στυγέουσι θεοί περ, “grievous and dank, which even the gods hate” (*Theogony* 739 = 810; see [[hesiod]]). And when Zeus fights Typhoeus, the earth, the sky and the sea boil and an unquenchable quaking arises, “and Hades trembled, lord of the dead below”, with the Titans beneath Tartarus around Cronus (*Theogony* 847–852). Leaf compares Virgil's “pale realms, hateful to the gods” (on 20.64): when Hercules tears open the cave of Cacus, it is as if the earth gaped and unlocked the seats below, and the shades trembled at the light let in (*Aeneid* 8.243–246; see [[virgil]]).

**The house of Hades.** Poseidon grieves for Aeneas, who “will soon go down to Hades, brought down by the son of Peleus” (20.294; see [[aeneas]]), and warns him to draw back from Achilles, “lest you come to the house of Hades even beyond your portion” (20.336; see [[moira]] and [[house]]).
