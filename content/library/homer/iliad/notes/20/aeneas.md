summary: Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, sends Aeneas against Achilles, and he goes unwillingly, remembering how Achilles once drove him from his cattle on Ida. Facing Achilles, he answers the taunt that he hopes for Priam's honor with his birth and his lineage from Zeus and Dardanus. His spear is stopped in the god-made shield, Achilles' passes over his back, and as Achilles comes at him with the sword he lifts a stone. Poseidon carries him out of the battle, because he is not to blame and is fated to survive: he and his children's children will rule the Trojans (20.79–352).

**Sent against Achilles.**

{{quote:6de47a0a-8e07-56b1-840e-c9902ac8137e}}

In Book 17 Apollo roused him in the likeness of the old herald Periphas, with the same verse (20.82 = 17.326; see [[periphas-son-of-epytus]]); now he takes the voice of a son of Priam, and Aeneas answers him as one, “Son of Priam” (20.87; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The god calls him by the title that Pandarus and Deiphobus gave him, “counselor of the Trojans” (20.83; 5.180, 13.463; see [[counselor]]), and asks where his threats have gone, the promise he made to the kings of the Trojans over his wine that he would fight Achilles face to face (20.83–85; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, put the same question to Idomeneus: “Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have your threats gone” (13.219).

**Unwilling.** “Son of Priam, why do you bid me do this, though I am unwilling” (20.87). Aeneas has met Achilles before, and run. Achilles came upon his cattle on Ida, drove him off and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus, and Aeneas lived because Zeus “roused my might and my nimble knees” (20.89–93; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). He weighs his chances. Some god is always at Achilles' side to keep ruin away, and even without one his weapon flies straight; but “if a god / were to stretch the outcome of war evenly, he would not very easily / win, not even if he boasts that he is all of bronze” (20.97–102; see [[divine-rescue]]). Apollo answers with Aeneas' birth: his mother is a daughter of Zeus, and Achilles' is only the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea (20.104–109; see [[aphrodite]]). Then the god “breathed great might into the shepherd of the people” (20.110), as in Book 5, when he sent Aeneas back healed from his temple, he “put might into the breast of the shepherd of the people” (5.512–513; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[apollo]]).

**Out in front.** “Nor did the son of Anchises escape the notice of white-armed Hera” (20.112): she calls Poseidon and Athena to turn him back (20.112–131; see [[hera]]). When the armies meet, “Aeneas strode out first, threatening” (ἀπειλήσας, 20.161), and the threats Apollo asked about are made in the field. He comes “tossing his heavy helmet” (20.162; see [[heavy-helmet]]); νευστάζων, “tossing”, is found only here in the Iliad. μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, is his epithet in this book, four times (20.175, 20.263, 20.293, 20.323; see [[great-hearted]]), and once he is δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (20.267; see [[wise-hearted]]).

**His answer.** Achilles taunts him with hoping to rule the Trojans with the honor of Priam, and with his flight from Ida (20.178–198; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). The taunt touches the grudge of Book 13, when Aeneas stood at the back of the throng, nursing his wrath against Priam, who did not honor him (13.460–461). He does not answer it directly. He answers with his birth:

{{quote:ac527758-a530-58d9-852d-3f202b4481a4}}

He knows Achilles' parents only by report, “hearing the tales mortal men have told of old” (20.204), and he claims his own in the words with which Sthenelus named him to Diomedes in Book 5, now in his own mouth and with “great-hearted” Anchises for “noble” (20.208–209 = 5.247–248; see [[anchises]] and [[euchomai]]). Then he gives his lineage, from Zeus through Dardanus, Erichthonius and Tros, and through Assaracus, Capys and Anchises down to himself, beside Priam's line through Ilus and Laomedon (20.213–241; see [[dardanus]], [[assaracus]], [[capys]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The rest of the speech turns from words to the spear (20.242–258; see [[zeus-increases-and-diminishes]], [[the-tongue-of-mortals]] and [[women-quarreling-in-the-street]]).

**Fear.** His spear is stopped in the shield of Hephaestus (20.259–272; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). When Achilles' spear comes through the rim of his own shield, “Aeneas crouched, and held the shield up away from him / in fear” (20.278–279), as Achilles had held his shield away from Aeneas' spear (20.261–262), and the spear passes over his back and stands in the ground: “and countless grief was poured over his eyes, / in fear that the shaft had stuck so near him” (20.282–283; see [[achos]]). ἐάλη, “crouched”, is the verb of the wounded lion in the simile (20.168) and of Idomeneus under his shield (13.408). Then, as Achilles comes at him with the sword, he lifts a stone, in the verses of the stone that crushed his own hip in Book 5 (20.285–287 = 5.302–304; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[men-of-today]]).

**Why the gods save him.** Poseidon sees it and speaks among the gods:

{{quote:b29576d8-205f-52bd-9ee6-e4949fee0ec3}}

ἀναίτιος, “not to blame” (see [[aitios]]): the griefs for which he suffers are others' (20.298). Walter Leaf reports a scholium of Aristonicus that explained Priam's suspicion of Aeneas (13.460–461) in the same way, not by his claim to the crown but by his having no stake of his own in the war of the sons of Priam (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.298). Then Poseidon gives the reason of fate: Aeneas is fated to escape, so that the lineage of Dardanus may not perish, and “now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children, who will be born hereafter” (20.300–308; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]], [[might-of-aeneas]] and [[poseidon]]).

**Carried off and warned.** Poseidon lifts him from the ground, and Aeneas, “springing from the hand of the god”, flies over the ranks of men and horses to the far edge of the war, where the Caucones are arming (20.325–329; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[caucones]]). There the god's warning undoes Apollo's encouragement. Apollo told him that his mother was the greater goddess (20.105–107); Poseidon asks “which of the gods bids you so recklessly” fight a man who is “at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals” (20.332–334). He is to draw back whenever he meets Achilles, and fight among the foremost when Achilles is dead, for no other Achaean will kill him (20.335–339; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Achilles, when the mist lifts, grants that “Aeneas too was dear to the immortal gods” (20.347; see [[achilles]]).

**His last fight.** Aeneas does not fight again in the Iliad. He is named once more, in the chariot race, as the man whose horses Diomedes took in Book 5, though Apollo saved the man himself (23.290–292; see [[tros]]). The poem leaves him alive, with Poseidon's word that he will rule the Trojans.

**In Virgil.** Neptune recalls this rescue in the *Aeneid*: he snatched Aeneas from Achilles in a hollow cloud (*Aeneid* 5.803–811; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]).
