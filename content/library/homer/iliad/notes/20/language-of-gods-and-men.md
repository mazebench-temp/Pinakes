---
summary: The river of Troy, set against Hephaestus in the battle of the gods, is the river “whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander”.
---

“and against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river, / whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (20.73–74; see [[scamander]] and [[hephaestus]]). It is the last of the four places in the Iliad where a thing is given a name among the gods and another among men, after Briareus, “whom the gods call Briareus, but all men / call Aegaeon” (1.403–404), the mound that “men call Batieia, / but the immortals, the tomb of much-bounding Myrine” (2.813–814), and the bird that “the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis” (14.291; see [[briareus]], [[batieia]] and [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). The form of the verse is the one of Book 1: ὃν … καλέουσι θεοί, ἄνδρες δέ, “whom the gods call …, and men”. Here, unlike Briareus and the bird, both names are used in the poem's own narrative: the Xanthus between whose streams and the Simoeis' the battle surges (6.4), and the Scamander by whose banks Hector fights (11.497–499). Walter Leaf suggests that both may be attempts to render a foreign name hard to pronounce, something like *Ksamnd-* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.74, referring to his note on 1.403).

Plato's Socrates takes this verse first among Homer's names of gods and men: the river at Troy “which fought in single combat with Hephaestus”, whom the gods call Xanthus and men Scamander, and he asks whether it is not a solemn thing to know why it is more correct to call it Xanthus (*Cratylus* 391e–392a; see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]] and [[plato]]).
