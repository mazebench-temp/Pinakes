summary: The Trojans arm on the rise of the plain and tremble at the sight of Achilles, and Zeus sends the gods into the war for fear that Achilles alone will sack the wall against fate; Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, Xanthus and Aphrodite go to their side. Hera and Athena have sworn never to ward off their evil day, and Poseidon foretells that Aeneas and his children's children will rule them. Hector calls on them not to fear the son of Peleus, but Achilles leaps among them and kills fourteen men by name.

**Before the battle.** The Achaeans arm around Achilles, “and the Trojans on the other side, on the rise of the plain” (20.3), the verse in which they were drawn up in Book 11, here without its verb (11.56; see the translation's note on 20.3). Poseidon asks Zeus whether he is pondering something about the Trojans and the Achaeans (20.17), and Zeus answers, “I care for them, even as they perish” (20.21; see [[zeus]]). Walter Leaf notes that a god might say ὀλλύμενοι, “perishing”, of either side, but takes the word to show that the Trojans are uppermost in Zeus' thoughts (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.21). For if Achilles fights alone against the Trojans, “they will not hold the swift-footed son of Peleus even for a little while”; they used to tremble at the sight of him before, and now Zeus fears that he may sack the wall against fate (20.26–30; see [[achilles]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]).

**The gods on their side.** “And to the Trojans went Ares of the gleaming helmet, and with him / Phoebus of the unshorn hair, and Artemis of the showering arrows, / and Leto and Xanthus and laughter-loving Aphrodite” (20.38–40; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]], [[ares]], [[apollo]], [[artemis]], [[leto]], [[scamander]] and [[aphrodite]]). While the gods still keep apart, “a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan” when they see Achilles in his armor (20.44–46), the verse of their fear when Ajax came out against Hector (7.215; see [[phobos]]). Then Ares shouts to the Trojans from the topmost height of the city and along the Simoeis (20.51–53), and Poseidon's earthquake shakes “the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (20.60; see [[poseidon]]). The wall of Heracles, where Poseidon's party sits apart, was made by “the Trojans and Pallas Athena” (20.145–148; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]]).

**Their fate.** On the other side the gods speak of the Trojans' end. Hera calls “empty wind” the gods who “have warded off the war and the combat from the Trojans” (20.123–124; see [[hera]] and [[anemolios]]), and tells Poseidon that she and Athena have sworn “never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, / not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (20.315–316; see [[evil-day]] and [[athena]]). Achilles mocks Aeneas for hoping to rule “the horse-taming Trojans” with the honor of Priam, or to be given a domain by the Trojans (20.179–186; see [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[aeneas]]). Poseidon, saving Aeneas, says that Zeus has come to hate the lineage of Priam, “and now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children, who will be born hereafter” (20.306–308; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]] and [[priam]]). In Aeneas' genealogy the people's eponym appears: Erichthonius fathered Tros, “lord of the Trojans” (20.230; see [[tros]]).

**Hector's call.** Achilles bids the Achaeans stand no longer far from the Trojans, and says that “no man / of the Trojans will rejoice, whoever comes near my spear” (20.354, 20.362–363). Hector answers him from the other side: “High-hearted Trojans, do not fear the son of Peleus” (20.366; see [[hector]] and [[high-hearted]]). “So he spoke, urging them on, and they raised their spears to face them, / the Trojans; and their might mingled together, and the war cry rose” (20.373–374; see [[exhortation]] and [[aute]]). But Apollo sends Hector back into the crowd (20.375–380), “And Achilles leapt among the Trojans, clothed in courage in his heart” (20.381). He kills fourteen men by name, Trojans and allies: Iphition, born beneath Tmolus, Demoleon, son of Antenor, Hippodamas, Polydorus, son of Priam, Dryops, Demuchus, Laogonus and Dardanus, the sons of Bias, Tros, son of Alastor, Mulius, Echeclus, Deucalion, and the Thracian Rhigmus with his attendant Areithous (20.381–489; see [[iphition]], [[demoleon]], [[hippodamas]], [[polydorus-son-of-priam]], [[dryops]], [[demuchus]], [[laogonus-son-of-bias]], [[dardanus-son-of-bias]], [[tros-son-of-alastor]], [[mulius-killed-by-achilles]], [[echeclus-son-of-agenor]], [[deucalion-the-trojan]], [[rhigmus]] and [[areithous-the-attendant]]). He rages through them like a fire in the mountains, and his horses trample the dead (20.490–503; see [[forest-fire]] and [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]).
