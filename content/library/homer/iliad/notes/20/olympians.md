**Two parties.** Zeus has the gods called to his house, and lets them go and “help either side, whichever way each of you has a mind” (20.25; see [[zeus]], [[assembly]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]):

{{quote:56640ffd-b9c1-518c-8714-9b67722470cb}}

The five gods who go to the ships are the ones Poseidon counted on against Zeus in Book 15 (15.213–214; see [[poseidon]]), and the six who go to the Trojans are the gods who have helped them in the earlier books: Apollo, who sent Ares among their ranks in Book 5 (5.454–462), Aphrodite, who saved Paris and Aeneas (3.380–382, 5.311–318), and Leto and Artemis, who healed Aeneas (5.445–448), with the river of the plain (see [[scamander]]). δίχα θυμὸν ἔχοντες, “their hearts divided”, is how the gods are divided again when they fall upon one another in Book 21 (δίχα δέ σφιν ἐνὶ φρεσὶ θυμὸς ἄητο, 21.386).

**Among men.** “Now as long as the gods were apart from mortal men, / so long the Achaeans exulted greatly” (20.41–42), but “when the Olympians had come into the throng of men”, Strife rose up and Athena and Ares shouted (20.47–53; see [[strife]], [[athena]] and [[ares]]). “So the blessed gods urged both sides on / and brought them together, and made heavy strife break out among them” (20.54–55; see [[blessed-gods]] and [[eris]]). Walter Leaf takes ἐν δʼ αὐτοῖς, “among them”, of the men, though the gods themselves would suit what follows (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.55; see the translation's note on 20.55). Zeus thunders, Poseidon shakes the earth, “so great was the din that rose as the gods came together in strife” (20.66), and they are set against one another in pairs (20.67–74).

**Sitting apart.** Hera tells Poseidon and Athena, “We have all come down from Olympus to take our part / in this battle” (20.125–126); Leaf takes “all” to mean the gods of the Achaean side (on 20.125). Poseidon would rather that the gods sit apart, and if the others begin, he expects to send them “back to Olympus, to the gathering of the other gods” (20.142). The two parties sit on either side of the plain, on the wall of Heracles and on Callicolone, “devising / their plans” (20.144–155; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[callicolone]]). In Book 21, when the fighting is done, the other gods go back to Olympus, some angry and some exulting, and sit down beside their father (21.518–520).

**The gods and men.** Apollo bids Aeneas pray to “the everlasting gods” (20.104; see [[gods-who-live-forever]]), and Poseidon pleads that he “always gives pleasing / gifts to the gods who hold the wide sky” (20.298–299; see [[wide-sky]]). When Poseidon has carried Aeneas away, Achilles sees that “Aeneas too was dear to the immortal gods” (20.347; see [[immortals]]).
