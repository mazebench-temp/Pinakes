{{quote:c9efdbc4-6ae4-517e-a043-be7f9cdce473}}

ὡς δʼ ἀναμαιμάει βαθέʼ ἄγκεα θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ / οὔρεος ἀζαλέοιο, βαθεῖα δὲ καίεται ὕλη, / πάντῃ τε κλονέων ἄνεμος φλόγα εἰλυφάζει: “As a wondrous fire rages through the deep valleys / of a dry mountain, and the deep forest burns, / and the wind, driving it everywhere, whirls the flame” (20.490–492). ἀναμαιμάω, “rage through”, is found only here; the simple μαιμάω is to quiver with eagerness, as a spear's point speeds through a man “in its fury” (5.661, 15.542), and Walter Leaf compares the compound used of Scylla as she searches eagerly round her rock for fish (σκόπελον περιμαιμώωσα, *Odyssey* 12.95; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.490). θεσπιδαής, “wondrous”, is the fire the Trojans meant to throw on the ships (12.441, 15.597; see [[wondrous]]). ἄγκεα are valleys, the valleys through which the grieving lion of Book 18 hunts for the man who stole its cubs (18.321); the mountain is ἀζαλέος, “dry”, the word of the dry oaks that a flooding river carries down (11.494), and a dry mountain is fuel for the fire. εἰλυφάζω, “whirl”, is found only here, but the wind that drove Agamemnon's fire was εἰλυφόων, “whirling” (11.156; see the translation's table).

The tenor: ὣς ὅ γε πάντῃ θῦνε σὺν ἔγχεϊ δαίμονι ἶσος / κτεινομένους ἐφέπων· ῥέε δʼ αἵματι γαῖα μέλαινα, “so he rushed everywhere with his spear, like a god, / chasing the men he was killing; and the black earth ran with blood” (20.493–494). πάντῃ, “everywhere”, is said first of the wind and then of the man (20.492, 20.493): Achilles goes where the wind drives the fire. δαίμονι ἶσος, “like a god”, was said of him a few verses before, when he made his fourth rush at Hector in the mist (20.447; see [[like-a-god]]). Leaf finds κτεινομένους, “those who were being slain”, a curious participle, and compares Agamemnon, who pursued the Trojans αἰὲν ἀποκτείνων, “always killing” (11.177–178; on 20.494). The verse-end ῥέε δʼ αἵματι γαῖα μέλαινα, “the black earth ran with blood”, is said of whole armies elsewhere (15.715; 4.451 and 8.65 without μέλαινα; see [[the-armies-clash]] and [[black-earth]]); here one man's killing makes it so.

This is the fifth forest fire of the poem. It was the gleam of the army's bronze on the march (2.455–458), the Trojans falling before Agamemnon (11.155–159), a roar that the shout of the armies outdoes (14.396–397), and Hector's frenzy at the ships (15.605–606). Achilles' fire is closest to Agamemnon's. Both come in a long run of killings (11.91–162; 20.455–489; see [[the-aristeia]]), both are followed by chariots and the dead (11.159–162; 20.495–502), and the same half-verse sums up both men, their irresistible hands spattered with gore (11.169; 20.503; see [[irresistible-hands]]). A second simile follows at once: Achilles' horses trample the dead as bulls tread out barley on a threshing floor (20.495–503; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]). Earlier in the book Hector had said that Achilles' hands were like fire (20.371–372; see [[hands-like-fire]]), and he himself had come against him like a flame (20.423; see [[like-a-flame]]).
