Aeneas' cast opens the duel. Achilles holds his shield away from him in fear, “the fool”, not knowing that the glorious gifts of the gods are not easy for mortal men to master (20.259–266). The spear does not break the shield, “for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god; / but it drove through two layers, and there were still three, / since the crook-footed god had beaten out five layers, / two of bronze, and two of tin inside, / and one of gold, and in that the ash spear was stopped” (20.268–272; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). The verse ends τῇ ῥʼ ἔσχετο μείλινον ἔγχος, “in that the ash spear was stopped”; Aristotle quotes it as τῇ ῥʼ ἔσχετο χάλκεον ἔγχος, “there the bronze spear was stopped” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a33–34).

The difficulty that Aristotle's rule answers is in the lines themselves. If the gold lay in the middle, the spear could not have passed two layers and been stopped by it; and the layers of metal disagree with Book 18, where the five layers are of the shield itself (18.481). Aristarchus athetized 269–272 (see the translation's note on 20.269–272). Walter Leaf, who calls the lines spurious, quotes Aristarchus' reason, that they were worked up by someone who wished to make a problem and that they clearly conflict with the genuine verses, since the works of Hephaestus are held to be invulnerable. The order of the metals, Leaf says, was a favorite problem, discussed at length by Porphyry and probably by Aristotle in his *Homeric Problems*. Porphyry held that the gold was the middle layer, Aristarchus that it was the outermost; and Aristarchus, so as not to seem to have athetized the lines for want of a solution, explained them as they stand: the spear was stopped by the outer layer without piercing it, but bent in the next two, so that there was a hollow and not a wound. Leaf thinks that Aristotle's obscure sentence points to a solution of the same kind (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.269).
