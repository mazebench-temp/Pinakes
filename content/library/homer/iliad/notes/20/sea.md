---
summary: Poseidon comes up out of the sea to Zeus' assembly (20.13–14); Apollo calls Achilles' mother the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, and Aeneas calls her “daughter of the sea” (20.107, 20.207); and the foals of Erichthonius' mares run over the broad back of the sea on the crests of the breakers (20.228–229).
---

**Poseidon.** οὐδʼ ἐνοσίχθων / νηκούστησε θεᾶς, ἀλλʼ ἐξ ἁλὸς ἦλθε μετʼ αὐτούς, “nor did the earth-shaker / fail to heed the goddess, but came up out of the sea to join them” (20.13–14). The sea is his share of the world, which he got by lot (15.190), and his house is in its depths at Aegae (13.21–22); in Book 13, too, he “had come up out of the sea” to watch the war from Samothrace (ἐξ ἁλός, 13.15; see [[poseidon]]). Walter Leaf reports the scholiast's view that he is named here because of the bad terms on which he last parted from Zeus (15.173–218), and answers that Poseidon was Zeus' chief rival, whose coming at a summons could not be taken for granted (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.13). Once the gods are in the battle, the god of the sea shakes the earth (20.57–60; see [[earth]]).

**The sea's daughter.** Apollo, urging Aeneas on, says that his mother is the daughter of Zeus and Achilles' “the daughter … of the Old Man of the Sea” (ἐξ ἁλίοιο γέροντος, 20.107; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]] and [[aphrodite]]). Aeneas calls Thetis ἁλοσύδνη, “daughter of the sea”, a word found again only in the *Odyssey*, of the goddess whose brood are the seals (20.207; *Odyssey* 4.404; see [[daughter-of-the-sea]], [[thetis]] and the translation's note on 20.207).

**Over the sea.** The twelve foals that the North Wind fathered on the mares of Erichthonius run over the earth on the tips of the ears of grain, “but when they bounded over the broad back of the sea, / they would run on the very crests of the breakers of the gray sea” (20.228–229; see [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]], [[broad-back-of-the-sea]] and [[gray-sea]]). ῥηγμίν, elsewhere the shore where the sea breaks, is here the surf itself, without the shore, Leaf thinks (on 20.229; see the translation's table). Poseidon's own horses fly over the waves in Book 13, so lightly that the bronze axle is not wetted beneath (13.29–30).
