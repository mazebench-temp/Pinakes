# Λαομέδων (20.237): the nominative occurs in the Iliad only here and at 21.452; the entry's stem catches Λαομέδοντα (20.236).

ἀμύμονα Λαομέδοντα, “noble Laomedon” (20.236): the genealogy gives him the epithet of its verse, which Idomeneus' lineage gives to Deucalion (13.451; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[blameless]]). Elsewhere in the poem he is the king who refused Heracles the horses for which he had come, and cheated the gods of their wages (5.640–651, 21.441–457; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]); here he is only a link between Ilus and Priam (see [[ilus]] and [[priam]]).

His sons are named in two verses: “and Laomedon fathered Tithonus and Priam, / Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, offshoot of Ares” (20.237–238). The second is the verse that named three of them among the elders at the Scaean gates (3.147; see [[tithonus]], [[lampus]], [[clytius]], [[hicetaon]] and [[trojan-elders]]). Walter Leaf thinks it may have been borrowed here to systematize the genealogy of the house of Priam, and notes that Book 15 carries the idea further by giving each of the three brothers a son (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.238; 15.419, 15.526, 15.546). Bucolion, his eldest son, whose mother bore him in secret, is not in the list (6.23–24; see [[bucolion]]).

Laomedon is not named at the wall of Heracles, to which Poseidon leads the gods of the Achaean side, “the high wall, which the Trojans and Pallas Athena / had made for him, so that he might slip away and escape the sea monster” (20.146–147; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]]). The poem does not tell why the monster came. Leaf reports the legend from the A scholia and Apollodorus, after Hellanicus: Poseidon, cheated by Laomedon of his hire for building the walls of Troy, sent the monster; Heracles killed it, protected by a wall that Pallas built for him, for the immortal horses that Laomedon had promised, and Laomedon cheated him with mortal ones (on 20.145).
