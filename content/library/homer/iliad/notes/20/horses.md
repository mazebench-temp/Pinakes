---
summary: Men and horses fill the plain as the armies come on (20.156–157). Aeneas tells of the three thousand mares of Erichthonius and the twelve foals that the North Wind fathered on them in the likeness of a stallion (20.221–229). Achilles' killings are fought among chariots, and at the end of the book his own single-hoofed horses trample the dead (20.394–502).
---

**The plain.** “And the whole plain was filled with them, and it gleamed with bronze, / with men and horses; and the earth rumbled beneath their feet” (20.156–157; see [[earth]]). When Poseidon throws Aeneas clear of Achilles, he leaps “over many ranks of heroes, and many ranks of horses” (20.326).

**The mares of Erichthonius.** In his lineage Aeneas tells of Erichthonius, “the richest of mortal men”: “three thousand horses of his were pastured along the marshland, / mares, glorying in their tender foals” (20.220–222). βουκολέοντο, “were pastured”, is properly the herdsman's verb for cattle, and Walter Leaf calls it a mixture of metaphor, like Hephaestus' οἰνοχόει γλυκὺ νέκταρ, sweet nectar poured with the verb for pouring wine (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.221; 1.598). The North Wind fell in love with the mares as they grazed and lay with them “in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion”, and they bore twelve foals that ran over the ears of grain without breaking them and over the crests of the breakers of the sea (20.223–229; see [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]], [[erichthonius]] and [[boreas]]). Achilles' own horses were foaled to a wind, the West Wind, by the Harpy Podarge (16.149–151; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Leaf notes that the belief that mares could conceive by the wind was widespread in antiquity, but sees here no more than a mythical way of saying how fast the foals ran (on 20.223).

**The chariots.** The men Achilles kills fall among horses. The horses of the Achaeans tear the dead Iphition with the tires of their wheels (20.394–395); Hippodamas is killed as he leaps down from his chariot, καθʼ ἵππων, literally from his horses (20.401, the verse of Chersidamas at 11.423); Laogonus and Dardanus are thrust from their chariot to the ground (ἐξ ἵππων, 20.461); and Rhigmus' attendant Areithous is stabbed in the back “as he turned the horses back”, and the horses are thrown into confusion (20.487–489; see [[chariot]]).

**Achilles' horses.** “so beneath great-hearted Achilles his single-hoofed horses / trampled corpses and shields alike” (20.498–499), as bulls tread out barley on a threshing floor, and the drops thrown up “from the horses' hooves” spatter the axle and the rails of his chariot (20.501; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]], [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[single-hoofed]]). The verses are those of Hector's horses in Book 11 (11.534–537).
