summary: Achilles' aristeia begins in earnest. He meets his two great opponents, Aeneas and Hector, and a god takes each from him; he charges three times at the mist that hides Hector and at the fourth shouts after him; and from his leap among the Trojans to the end of the book he kills fourteen men, while the similes of the lion, the forest fire and the threshing floor mark his progress (20.156–503).
# The rout, from the leap among the Trojans to the end of the book (20.381-503).
refs: [20.381-503]
---

**The great opponents.** The pattern brings the hero against an enemy of rank, with the gods taking part, and Achilles has two in this book. Aeneas, son of a goddess, meets him “by far the best” of the two armies (20.158), and Poseidon carries him off (20.318–329; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[divine-rescue]]); Hector comes against him to avenge his brother, and Apollo hides him in mist (20.419–444; see [[hector]]). Walter Leaf remarks that the meeting with Aeneas is brought in as if it were a chance encounter, as though Apollo had not prepared it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.158). The help of the gods is promised to Achilles too: Hera would have one of them stand by him (20.120–122), Athena turns Hector's spear back with a breath (20.438–440; see [[athena]]), and Achilles himself speaks of “any god who is a helper to me as well” (20.453).

**Three times and a fourth.** Three times he springs at Hector with his bronze spear and strikes the deep mist (20.445–446), and when “for the fourth time he rushed on like a god” (20.447), it is not a god who speaks, as with Diomedes and Patroclus (5.438–439, 16.705–706), but Achilles, with a taunt (20.448–454; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[like-a-god]]). Leaf, finding that the fourth onset leads to nothing, thinks 447 added from the parallels (on 20.444; see the translation's note on 20.447).

**The rout.** “And Achilles leapt among the Trojans, clothed in courage in his heart, / shouting terribly” (20.381–382). Leaf thought that this verse may well have begun Achilles' career in the original poem of the Wrath (on 20.381). He kills Iphition, Demoleon, Hippodamas and Polydorus (20.381–418), and after Hector's escape Dryops, Demuchus, the two sons of Bias, Tros, Mulius, Echeclus, Deucalion, Rhigmus and Areithous (20.455–489): fourteen men named in the book, in the manner of the [[androktasia]], some in a single verse and others with a story, a birthplace or a plea (see [[achilles]]).

**The similes.** The lion marks his advance against Aeneas (20.164–175; see [[like-a-lion]]), and the end of the rout has two similes in succession: the fire raging through a dry mountain forest (20.490–494; see [[forest-fire]]) and the bulls treading barley on the threshing floor, with his horses trampling the dead (20.495–502; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The end of the book takes its colors from Book 11: the fire in the woods of Agamemnon's aristeia (11.155–159; Leaf on 20.490), Hector's chariot spattered with blood (20.499–502 = 11.534–537), and Agamemnon's hands spattered with gore (20.503 = 11.169; see [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]). The aristeia goes on into the river in Book 21 and ends with the death of Hector in Book 22 (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]).
