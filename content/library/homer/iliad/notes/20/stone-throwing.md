**The verses of Book 5.** The three verses are those of Diomedes' stone, with Aeneas' name where the son of Tydeus stood (5.302–304). The shout that begins them has changed sides. In Book 5 it was Aeneas who stood over Pandarus like a lion, “shouting terribly”, and Diomedes who took up the stone against him (5.299–304); here Achilles comes on like a lion and draws his sword “shouting terribly”, and Aeneas takes up the stone (20.164, 20.283–285; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[men-of-today]]). The same verse, σμερδαλέα ἰάχων· ὃ δὲ χερμάδιον λάβε χειρί, begins Hector's charge at Teucer, whose bowstring his stone breaks (8.321–329; see [[hector]] and [[teucer]]).

**A stone not thrown.** “Then Aeneas would have hit him with the stone as he rushed on, / on helmet or on the shield that had kept grim destruction from him, / and the son of Peleus would have taken his life with his sword at close quarters” (20.288–290), had not Poseidon seen it (20.291; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[divine-rescue]]). The narrator's verdict is that the stone would not have saved Aeneas. It would have struck the arms of Hephaestus, and the shield had just stopped his spear (20.267–272; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf takes the clause τό οἱ ἤρκεσε λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον in this way, of the shield that had saved Achilles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.289; see the translation's note on 20.289). In the duel of Hector and Ajax the stones came after the spears, and Ajax's knocked Hector down (7.264–272); here the narrator gives the fight to the sword, and a god ends it.
