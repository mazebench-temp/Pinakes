summary: He is not named in the book, but he is the cause of what Achilles does in it. Zeus fears that Achilles, “terribly enraged at heart over his companion”, may sack the wall against fate (20.29–30), and Achilles, seeing Hector, rejoices to meet “the man … who killed my honored companion” (20.425–426).
# Unnamed: "his companion" (20.29) and "my honored companion" (20.426).
refs: [20.29, 20.426]
---

**Zeus' reason.** Zeus sends the gods into the battle because Achilles is fighting again, “and now that he is terribly enraged at heart over his companion, / I fear he may even sack the wall against fate” (20.29–30; see [[zeus]] and [[achilles]]). ἑταίρου, “over his companion”, is a genitive of the cause, as Poseidon is angry because of the Cyclops (Od. 1.69; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.29; see [[hetairos]]).

**Hector.** When Hector comes against him, Achilles leaps up and says, “Here near me is the man who has touched my heart most deeply, / who killed my honored companion” (20.425–426; see [[hector]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). τετιμένον, “honored”, is a participle that has become a mere adjective, Leaf notes (on 20.426). It is the vow Achilles made when he heard the news, to find “the destroyer of that dear life, / Hector” (18.114–115). The T scholia and Eustathius, Leaf reports, took Hector's answer, that his weapon too is sharp πάροιθεν, “at the point” (20.437), as “before now”, a reminder that his spear had killed Patroclus (on 20.437; see the translation's note on 20.437).

In the next book Achilles takes twelve young men alive from the river as ποινή, “the penalty for Patroclus” (21.26–28; see [[poine]]).
