---
summary: Book 20 is the second stage of the day, from the arming of the armies to the beginning of Achilles' slaughter, and it marks no hour. Zeus releases the gods to the war, Achilles meets Aeneas and then Hector and a god saves each from him, and the book ends with the earth running with blood; the river and the gates follow in Books 21 and 22 without a break.
# "Today" (σήμερον, 20.127, 20.211), the book's only mentions of the day itself.
refs: [20.127, 20.211]
---

**No hour.** Book 20 takes the day up where Book 19 left it, with the armies arming, the Achaeans by the ships around Achilles and the Trojans “on the rise of the plain” (20.1–3; see [[achilles]]). The verse of the Trojans is the one that set them on the rise of the plain at the beginning of the third day of battle (20.3 = 11.56; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and the translation's note on 20.3). The book marks no hour, and its only mentions of the day itself are σήμερον, “today”: the gods have come down so that Achilles “may suffer nothing among the Trojans / today” (20.126–127), and Aeneas tells Achilles that one pair of parents or the other “will weep for their dear son / today” (20.210–211).

**The pause.** The fighting has not yet begun when the gods meet. Poseidon asks Zeus whether he is pondering something about the two armies, “For now their battle and war have blazed up at very close quarters” (20.18; see [[poseidon]]). Walter Leaf found the words singularly inappropriate in the only pause in the battle since the beginning of Book 11, and explained them by supposing that this prologue belongs with the battle of the gods at the end of Book 21, which comes, he says, just as the death of Hector is being accomplished on earth (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.18; see the translation's note on 20.18).

**The gods in the field.** Zeus sends the gods down because of Achilles (20.20–30; see [[zeus]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). While they keep apart the Achaeans exult and the Trojans tremble (20.41–46); when they come into the throng Strife rises, the gods shout on both sides, Zeus thunders and Poseidon shakes the earth (20.47–66). But after taking sides they sit apart, the Achaean party on the wall of Heracles and the Trojan on Callicolone, and “to begin harsh war / both sides were loath” (20.144–155; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[callicolone]]). Their fight waits for Book 21 (21.385–520).

**Two rescues.** The gods take both of his great opponents from Achilles: Aeneas, whom Poseidon lifts over the ranks, because he is fated to survive and rule the Trojans (20.302–308, 20.318–329; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]), and Hector, whom Apollo hides in mist (20.443–444; see [[divine-rescue]]). Achilles promises Hector that he will finish him off “when I meet you later” (20.452), and the meeting comes on this same day, before the Scaean gates (22.5–6; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

**Into Book 21.** The book ends in the slaughter, “and the black earth ran with blood” (20.494), and with Achilles' hands spattered with gore (20.503). The next book goes on without a break, as the fleeing Trojans reach the ford of the Xanthus (21.1–4; see [[scamander]]). In this index's count the whole book is Day 27.
