Πηλεΐδης δʼ ἑτέρωθεν ἐναντίον ὦρτο λέων ὣς: “And the son of Peleus rushed against him from the other side like a lion” (20.164). In Book 11 the same words, ἐναντίον ὦρτο λέων ὣς, ended a verse and a comparison: Agamemnon rushed against the two sons of Antimachus when their horses bolted, and killed them as they begged for their lives (11.129–147; see [[sons-of-antimachus]]). Here the comparison runs on for nine verses more. The lion is a ravager that a whole people has gathered to kill; it goes its way heedless of them until a young man's spear strikes it, and then it turns on them in its fury, to kill or be killed (20.165–175; see [[wounded-lion]] and [[lion]]).

Aeneas and Achilles have come out between the two armies, “by far the best” (20.158–160), and Aeneas strode out first, threatening, with his shield before his chest and his bronze spear shaking (20.161–163; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). Achilles answers him as Agamemnon answered the sons of Antimachus, with a lion's rush. The gods sit apart on two hills, loath to begin the war themselves (20.144–155; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).
