---
summary: Achilles does not ask Aeneas who he is; he taunts him. Aeneas answers that each knows the other's lineage from the tales of men, and gives his own all the same, in the verses Glaucus used, from Zeus and Dardanus down through two branches to Hector and himself; then he turns from lineage to the power of Zeus, and from words to spears (20.176–258).
---

**The meeting.** Achilles and Aeneas come together “between the two armies, eager to fight”, and “when they had come close, advancing against each other”, Achilles speaks first (20.159, 20.176–177 = 6.120–122; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[when-they-came-close]]). He does not ask who Aeneas is, as Diomedes asked Glaucus (6.123). He knows him: he once drove him from his cattle down the mountains of Ida, and he taunts him with the honor of Priam, which he will never win (20.178–198; see [[priam]]).

**Known by report.** Aeneas answers that he too can speak taunts, and that lineage is no news between them: “We know each other's lineage, and we know each other's parents, / hearing the tales mortal men have told of old; / but by sight you have never yet seen mine, nor I yours” (20.203–205; see [[genee]] and the translation's note on 20.204). Aristarchus athetized 205–209 as needless for that reason, since both men know each other's lineage; Walter Leaf finds this insufficient ground (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.205). Then Aeneas offers the lineage anyway, with Glaucus' couplet: “But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well / our lineage—and many men know it—” (20.213–214 = 6.150–151).

**The lineage.** It runs from Zeus through seven generations, Dardanus, Erichthonius, Tros, Assaracus, Capys, Anchises and Aeneas, the longest genealogy in the Iliad (20.215–240; see [[dardanus]], [[erichthonius]], [[tros]], [[assaracus]], [[capys]] and [[anchises]]). Each generation has its verse, and the verses are made alike: “And Dardanus in turn fathered a son, king Erichthonius”, “And Ilus in turn fathered a son, noble Laomedon” (20.219, 20.236), as in Idomeneus' “and Minos in turn fathered a son, noble Deucalion” (13.451). It follows both branches of the house of Tros, his own and Priam's, and ends with the two in one verse: “and Anchises fathered me, and Priam brilliant Hector” (20.240; see [[ilus]], [[laomedon]] and [[priam]]). Two stories stand in it, the mares of Erichthonius and the carrying off of Ganymede (20.221–229, 20.232–235; see [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]] and [[ganymede]]). It closes with Glaucus' verse, “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (20.241 = 6.211; see [[euchomai]]). Leaf takes the genealogy for the kernel of the episode, to which the rest of the speech was written up (on 20.215).

**After the lineage.** Glaucus' lineage ended the fight before it began (6.212–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]); Aeneas' ends in a claim that lineage does not decide it: “But it is Zeus who increases and diminishes the prowess of men, / as he wishes; for he is the mightiest of all” (20.242–243; see [[zeus-increases-and-diminishes]]). Leaf hears in it an allusion to Achilles' taunt about Aeneas' flight to Lyrnessus (on 20.242). Then Aeneas turns on the talk itself: “But come, let us no longer talk like this, like children” (20.244); there are insults enough for a ship of a hundred benches, the tongue of mortals is a twisting thing, and “Whatever kind of word you speak, such will you hear back” (20.246–250; see [[the-tongue-of-mortals]] and [[such-will-you-hear-back]]); they need not quarrel like women in the street (20.251–255; see [[women-quarreling-in-the-street]]). The speech ends in the challenge, “let us taste of each other with our bronze-fitted spears” (20.258).
