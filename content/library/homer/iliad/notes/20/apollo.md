summary: Apollo goes to the Trojans with Ares, Artemis, Leto, Xanthus and Aphrodite, and is set against Poseidon. In the likeness of Priam's son Lycaon he rouses Aeneas against Achilles, telling him that his mother is the greater goddess, and breathes great might into him; Poseidon blames him when Aeneas is about to die. He sits with Ares on Callicolone. When Achilles turns on the Trojans he warns Hector not to meet him in front, and when Achilles charges Hector he snatches him away in thick mist.

**With the Trojans.** “Phoebus of the unshorn hair” goes to the Trojans with Ares, his sister Artemis, his mother Leto, Xanthus and Aphrodite (20.38–40; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). ἀκερσεκόμης, “of the unshorn hair”, is found only here in Homer (see [[of-the-unshorn-hair]] and the translation's note on 20.38–39). In the pairs, “against lord Poseidon / stood Phoebus Apollo with his feathered arrows” (20.67–68; see [[poseidon]], [[arrows]] and [[feathered]]), and Artemis is “sister of the Far-Shooter” (20.71; see [[artemis]] and [[hekatos]]). Walter Leaf notes that ἰά, for ἰούς, “arrows”, occurs only here in Homer (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.67). When the two meet in Book 21, Apollo will not fight his father's brother for the sake of mortals (21.461–469).

**Aeneas roused.** “But Apollo, who rouses armies, roused Aeneas straight / to face the son of Peleus, and put noble might into him; / and he made his voice like that of Priam's son Lycaon” (20.79–81; see [[who-rouses-armies]], [[aeneas]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The next verse, “in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him” (20.82; see [[son-of-zeus]]), is the one with which he urged Aeneas on in the likeness of the herald Periphas in Book 17 (17.326). He asks where Aeneas' threats have gone, which he made to the kings of the Trojans over the wine (20.83–85; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]), and when Aeneas answers that a god always stands at Achilles' side, Apollo turns the argument: “Come, hero, you too pray to the everlasting gods; / for they say that you too were born of Zeus' daughter Aphrodite, / and he is from a lesser goddess” (20.104–106; see [[aphrodite]], [[thetis]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). “So saying, he breathed great might into the shepherd of the people” (20.110), the verse with which he sent Hector back into battle in Book 15 (15.262). Hera sees that “Phoebus Apollo set him on” (20.118), and Poseidon foresees Aeneas' death “because he obeyed the words of Apollo the Far-Shooter” (20.295). Leaf objects that if Aeneas was to be saved, it should naturally have been by Apollo, who urged him on and is still in the field, or by Aphrodite, as in Book 5 (on 20.293), where Apollo took Aeneas from Aphrodite and covered him in a dark cloud, and set him in his temple on Pergamus (5.344–346, 5.445–448; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[pergamus]]).

**On Callicolone.** Poseidon advises the Achaeans' gods to sit apart, unless Ares or Phoebus Apollo begins the battle (20.138), and the gods of the Trojans sit “on the brows of Callicolone, / around you, archer Phoebus, and Ares, sacker of cities” (20.151–152; see [[callicolone]], [[ares]] and [[ieios]]). The poet turns to address him, as he did when Apollo threw down the wall of the Achaeans as easily as a boy by the sea scatters the playthings he has made of sand: “so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery” (15.361–365; see [[apostrophe]] and the translation's note on 15.365).

**Hector warned.** When Achilles has called on the Achaeans and Hector on the Trojans, “Phoebus Apollo stood beside Hector and said: / ‘Hector, no longer step out at all as a champion against Achilles, / but wait for him in the crowd and out of the din, / lest he hit you with a spear, or strike you with his sword at close quarters’” (20.375–378; see [[hector]]). Hector goes back into the crowd, “afraid, when he heard the voice of the god as he spoke” (20.380).

**Hector saved.** But when Hector sees his brother Polydorus killed, he comes against Achilles; Athena turns back his spear, and as Achilles charges, “Apollo snatched him away / very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist” (20.443–444; see [[divine-rescue]]). The words are those in which Aphrodite snatched Paris away from Menelaus (3.380–381). Three times Achilles strikes the deep mist, and then he shouts: “now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you, / to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins” (20.450–451; see [[achilles]] and [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]). The taunt is the one Diomedes shouted after Hector in Book 11, when the helmet that Phoebus Apollo had given him turned Diomedes' spear (11.352–353, 11.362–367). In Book 15 Apollo told Hector that he had protected him before, “both you yourself and your steep citadel” (15.254–257).
