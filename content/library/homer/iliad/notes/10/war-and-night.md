summary: Book 10 is the only action of the Iliad fought at night. The night of Book 9 goes on, the chiefs who cannot sleep hold a council beyond the ditch, both sides send out spies, and two men kill thirteen sleepers and bring back the horses of Rhesus in the last third of the dark.

**The same night.** The night that fell at the end of Book 8 has not ended. The Trojan fires still burn on the plain, and Agamemnon, looking out at them, “marveled at the many fires that burned before Ilios, at the sound of flutes and pipes and the din of men” (10.11–13; see [[trojans]]). The embassy has failed and the kings have gone to sleep (9.712–713); the king cannot. Day 26 dawns only at the start of Book 11 (11.1–2). See [[the-second-day-of-battle]].

**Night as a field of war.** Battles in the Iliad are fought by day (see the article above); Book 10 shows what the dark makes possible. Agamemnon fears that the enemy, camped close by, “may be eager to fight even by night” (10.100–101), and Nestor wakes Diomedes because the Trojans sit “on the rise of the plain near the ships, and only a little space still holds them off” (10.160–161). The kings cross the ditch and sit “in a clear space” where Hector turned back from killing the Argives “when night covered all around” (10.198–201; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The night of Book 8 stopped a battle; this one is used for what cannot be done by daylight: spying, a capture, a killing of sleepers and a theft of horses (see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[the-doloneia]]).

**The words of the night.** The book has its own phrases for the dark. Men go “through the ambrosial night” (10.41, 10.142; see [[ambrosial]]), “through the dark night” (νύκτα διʼ ὀρφναίην, 10.83, 10.276, 10.386), and “through the swift black night” (10.394, 10.468; see [[black-night]]). ὀρφναῖος, “dark, murky”, is found in the Iliad only in this book, and once in the Odyssey, of the moonless night of the landing on the island of goats (*Odyssey* 9.143). The sentries keep watch “through the evil night” (10.188), and the spies go “like two lions through the black night, through the slaughter, through the corpses, through the armor and the black blood” (10.297–298; see [[lion]]).

**Sound instead of sight.** In the dark the poem listens. Athena's heron is not seen, “but they heard it cry” (10.274–276; see [[heron]]). The sentries are turned toward the plain “to hear if the Trojans were coming on” (10.189). Dolon stops at the sound behind him, hoping for friends (10.354–356), Odysseus whistles to Diomedes as a signal (10.502), and Nestor is the first to hear the hoofbeats of the returning horses: “shall I be wrong, or speak the truth?” (10.532–535). Seeing, when it happens, is the spy's skill: Odysseus sees Dolon coming and sees Rhesus first (10.339–340, 10.476).

The night is divided by the stars into three portions (see [[moira]]); the Odyssey twice marks “the third part of the night” in the same way, “and the stars had moved on” (*Odyssey* 12.312, 14.483). The verse puzzled ancient readers, since if more than two parts had passed a whole third could not remain, and Aristotle gave it as a case of ambiguity (*Poetics* 25, 1461a; see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]). Whatever the arithmetic, the raid fills the last third of the night: the two set out, take Dolon, kill Rhesus, and are back at the ships, bathed and fed, before the dawn of 11.1. The *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, sets its whole action in this night and gives its sentries “the fourfold watch of the night” (τετράμοιρον νυκτὸς φυλακήν, *Rhesus* 5; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).

**The night's outcome.** At the start of Book 9 Nestor said that “this night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.78), and in this book he puts it in another image: everything stands “on the edge of a razor” (10.173–174; see [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]] and [[on-the-razors-edge]]). The night ends with a success, horses “like the rays of the sun” brought in (10.547), a libation to Athena, and no sleep (10.572–579), but it does not decide the war: the next day drives the Achaeans back to their ships.
