summary: The closings of Book 10 turn words into deeds at night: a dressing, a crossing of the ditch, an arming, a spear-cast, an oath. The same verse of silence ends the call for a volunteer in both camps, and two speeches are never closed, Dolon's last plea and Nestor's fear for the spies, which the spies themselves cut short.
grc: ["ὣς εἰπόνθʼ", "ὣς ἄρʼ ἐφώνησεν", "οὔ πω πᾶν εἴρητο"]
en: [Not yet had his whole word been spoken]
---

The closings of Book 10: 10.72, 131, 148, 162, 177, 194, 218, 227, 240, 254, 295, 313, 328, 332, 349, 372, 454, 465, 482, 512, 540, 564.

**Two councils.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence” closes Nestor's call for a man to go among the Trojans and Hector's call for a man to go to the Achaean ships (10.218, 10.313). It is the verse that met Agamemnon's proposal to flee earlier the same night (9.29, 9.693; see [[silence]]). In each camp one man breaks the silence and volunteers, Diomedes and Dolon (10.219–226, 10.314–327).

**Into the deed.** “So saying” (ὣς εἰπών) leads straight into action: Nestor puts on his tunic, hurries across the ditch, and Odysseus at the end drives the horses across it (10.131, 10.194, 10.564). In the dual it turns the spies' last words into their arming, ὣς εἰπόνθʼ, “So saying, the two put on their dread armor” (10.254; see [[arming-scene]]). “He spoke, and” (ἦ ῥα καί) is followed by Diomedes' spear-cast over Dolon's shoulder (10.372), and ἦ, καί by Dolon's last gesture (10.454). After his prayer with the spoils, “So he spoke” (ὣς ἄρʼ ἐφώνησεν) is followed by the spoils lifted onto the tamarisk (10.465).

**The effect.** The second half of the verse tells what the words did: “and many were willing to follow Diomedes” (10.227), “and he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.240), “and swore an oath that would prove false” (10.332; see [[oath]]), “and gray-eyed Athena breathed might into him” (10.482), “and he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke” (10.512 = 2.182; see [[divine-epiphany]]). The prayers are closed with the goddess's hearing, in the plural: “So they spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard them” (10.295; see [[hear-me]]).

**Two for one.** “So the two spoke” (ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντε, 10.349) closes a speech that Odysseus alone has made. The dual takes in both men, who act on it together, lying down among the dead (see the translation's note on 10.349).

**Speeches cut off.** Two speeches of the night are not closed by the formula. Dolon's last plea is never spoken: his head is mingled with the dust “while he was still speaking” (10.457; see [[supplication]]). And Nestor, hearing the sound of horses, is still speaking his fear for the spies when they arrive: “Not yet had his whole word been spoken when they came themselves” (10.540). The Odyssey uses the half-verse when the disguised Odysseus, hearing footsteps at Eumaeus' hut, is still saying that someone is coming, and Telemachus stands in the doorway (Od. 16.4–12).
