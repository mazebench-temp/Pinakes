---
summary: Every killing of the night is done with the sword. Thrasymedes lends Diomedes a two-edged sword, his own having been left at his ship; with it Diomedes cuts through Dolon's neck and kills twelve sleeping Thracians and their king, “turning this way and that” (10.255–256, 10.454–457, 10.483–497).
grc: [ἄορι]
---

**A borrowed sword.** Diomedes' own sword “had been left behind at his ship”, and Thrasymedes gives him “a two-edged sword” (φάσγανον ἄμφηκες, 10.255–256; see [[two-edged]]); Meriones gives Odysseus another (ξίφος, 10.261; see [[arming-scene]]). ἄμφηκες is said of a sword once more in the Iliad, of the one Achilles drives into Lycaon (21.118).

**Dolon.** Diomedes strikes the suppliant “full in the middle of the neck, darting at him with his sword, and sheared through both the tendons” (10.455–456; see [[supplication]]). φασγάνῳ ἀΐξας, “darting at him with his sword”, is the half-verse of the blow with which Eurypylus cuts off Hypsenor's arm (5.81).

**The Thracians.** Then “he killed, turning this way and that; and an ugly groaning rose from the men struck by the sword, and the earth grew red with blood” (10.483–484), while Odysseus, “whenever the son of Tydeus stood over a man and struck him with his sword”, drags the body aside (10.489–490). ἄορ is a third word for the sword, beside ξίφος and φάσγανον. The two verses of the killing return when Achilles leaps into the river with only his sword and kills the Trojans there, with “water” for “earth” (21.17–21), and in the Odyssey of the slaughter of the suitors in Odysseus' hall (Od. 22.307–309, 24.183–185). They tell a killing of men who cannot fight back. In the night the one spear thrown is thrown to stop a man, not to kill him (10.372–374; see [[spear]]), and every death is by the sword.
