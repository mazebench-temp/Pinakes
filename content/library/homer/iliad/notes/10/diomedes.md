summary: Diomedes is the spear of the night raid. Woken by Nestor's heel, he volunteers to spy on the Trojans, asks for a companion and chooses Odysseus, prays to Athena as his father's helper, runs down Dolon and kills him as he begs, and kills twelve sleeping Thracians and then their king Rhesus, over whose head he stands as “an evil dream”, until Athena sends him back to the ships.

**Woken by the heel.** Nestor and Odysseus find him asleep outside his hut with his armor, on an oxhide among his sleeping companions, whose spears stand upright on their butt-spikes and shine far off like Zeus' lightning (10.150–156; see [[like-lightning]]). Nestor stirs him with his heel, λὰξ ποδὶ κινήσας, as Telemachus wakes Nestor's own son in the Odyssey (10.158; *Odyssey* 15.44–45), and rebukes him: the Trojans are camped “near the ships, and only a little space still holds them off” (10.159–161). The youngest of the kings answers the oldest:

{{quote:574276be-374c-5db8-a080-219497aef49f}}

The complaint is a tribute to the old man who never stops (see [[nestor]]). Nestor grants it, but all stands “on the edge of a razor” (10.173–174; see [[on-the-razors-edge]]), and he sends the young man to rouse swift Ajax and Meges, “for you are younger, if you have pity on me” (10.176). Diomedes throws on a lion's skin, described in nearly the words used for Agamemnon's, and goes (10.177–179; 10.23–24; see [[lion-skin]]).

**The volunteer.** Nestor's call for a spy meets silence, and Diomedes breaks it, as he broke the two silences of Book 9 (10.218–219; 9.30–31, 9.693–696; see [[silence]] and [[the-doloneia]]). “Nestor, my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.220) is the verse with which Dolon will volunteer to Hector (10.319; see [[dolon]]). Diomedes will not go alone: “When two go together, one sees before the other / how the advantage lies” (10.224–225; see [[when-two-go-together]]). Agamemnon tells him to take the best man and not to choose by birth, “not even if he is more kingly” (10.239), the word of his own demand that Achilles submit to him (βασιλεύτερος, 9.160); “he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.240; see [[agamemnon]]). Diomedes names Odysseus, whom Athena loves, “since he is wise beyond all others” (10.242–247; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Thrasymedes arms him, with a skullcap of bull's hide (10.255–259; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[arming-scene]]).

**His father's helper.** Diomedes prays second, to “Atrytone”: “go with me, as when you went with my father, brilliant Tydeus, / to Thebes”, and he tells her the story of Tydeus' embassy (10.284–291; see [[tydeus]] and [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Agamemnon told that story to shame him (4.370–400), and Athena to rouse him (5.800–813); the son tells it as a claim on the goddess, and vows her a yearling heifer with gold poured around her horns, in the three verses of Nestor's vow at Pylos (10.292–294 = *Odyssey* 3.382–384; see [[sacrifice]]). The night repeats the father's errand among the enemy, with Athena at his side. Tydeus “devised very grim deeds” with her help (10.289), and when the Trojans wake they gaze at “the grim deeds / the men had done” (μέρμερα ἔργα, 10.524–525).

**Dolon.** In the chase Diomedes is the spear (10.348). As Dolon nears the Achaean sentries, Athena puts might into him, “so that none of the bronze-armored Achaeans / might boast that he struck him first, and he himself come second” (10.366–368; see [[menos]]). He throws, and “missed the man on purpose”: the point sticks in the ground over Dolon's shoulder, and Dolon stops (10.369–376). After the questioning, “looking at him darkly” (10.446; see [[looking-darkly]]), he refuses the plea for ransom: “Do not put any thought of escape in your heart, Dolon, / good though your news is” (10.447–448). His reason is practical: let go, Dolon would come back to spy or to fight, and dead he will never again be “a bane to the Argives” (10.449–453). Agamemnon, refusing the suppliant Adrastus, wanted no Trojan left alive, not even the boy in his mother's womb (6.55–60; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]). As Dolon reaches for his chin to beg, Diomedes cuts through his neck, and his head is in the dust while he is still speaking (10.454–457; see [[supplication]]). The Odyssey gives Odysseus the same killing, in many of the same words, when he refuses the suppliant Leodes: “looking at him darkly”, the sword through the middle of the neck, the head in the dust in mid-speech (*Odyssey* 22.320–329).

**The Thracians.** Odysseus points him to the sleepers: “bring out your strong might” (10.479). Athena breathes might into him, and he kills “turning this way and that”, an ugly groaning rising from the men struck by the sword (10.482–484), in verses that return, a little changed, for Achilles in the river (21.20–21) and for the killing of the suitors (*Odyssey* 22.308–309). Like a lion falling on flocks with no herdsman he kills twelve (10.485–488; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]), and then the king:

{{quote:bf31520c-0daa-5b09-9018-98f86bbca9a0}}

Οἰνεΐδαο πάϊς, “the son of Oeneus' son”: only here is Diomedes named by his grandfather (see [[oeneus]]). He stands over the sleeper's head as dreams stand over sleepers' heads (2.20, 2.59, 23.68; see [[onar]] and [[rhesus]]), unless a real dream is meant (see the translation's note on 10.494–497).

**The most shameless thing.** When Odysseus has the horses and whistles, Diomedes stays, pondering “what was the most shameless thing he could do”: drag off the chariot with its inlaid armor, or kill more Thracians (10.503–506). Athena tells him to think of his return, “if some other god should wake the Trojans too” (10.509–511), and “he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke”, as Odysseus knew it in Book 2 (10.512; 2.182). He mounts the horses and rides (10.513; see [[horses]]). In Book 5 Apollo himself drove him back from Aeneas (5.432–444); here the god can only wake the Thracians after he has gone (10.515–522; see [[apollo]]).

**Home.** At the place where Dolon died he leaps down to hand Odysseus the spoils, then whips the horses on (10.528–531). Odysseus' report gives him the king and the twelve, “killed by brave Diomedes” (10.559–560). The Thracian horses are tied at his manger beside his own (10.566–569), presumably the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas (5.319–327, 8.105–108). In the chariot race of Book 23 he drives those, and Rhesus' horses are never mentioned again (23.290–292; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]] and [[the-doloneia-question]]). He bathes with Odysseus and pours wine to Athena (10.572–579; see [[bathing]]), and the next day an arrow of Paris drives him from the field (11.369–400).
