---
summary: Book 10 counts the night in three portions, its spies in pairs, and its dead in twelve and a thirteenth. Odysseus' report to Nestor counts the thirteenth twice, once for Rhesus and once for Dolon.
passages:
  - 10.251-253 | The night two parts gone
  - 10.482-497 | The killing of Rhesus
  - 10.555-563 | Odysseus' report
---

**Three portions.** Odysseus measures the hour by the stars: “the greater part of the night has gone, two portions of it, and a third portion still remains” (10.252–253; see [[moira]] and [[war-and-night]]). The arithmetic puzzled ancient readers, since a whole third cannot remain if more than two are gone; the sense is that the two portions are the greater part (see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]).

**Two.** The book is a story of pairs. Diomedes asks for a companion, “when two go together” (10.224; see [[when-two-go-together]]), the two arm (ἐδύτην, 10.254, 10.272) and go out “like two lions through the black night” (λέοντε δύω, 10.297), and they chase Dolon like “two sharp-toothed hounds” after a fawn or a hare (10.360). Greek has a dual number for things that come in twos, and the narrative of the raid is full of dual verbs and pronouns for the pair (see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). The Thracians' horses stand beside each man as a yoked pair (δίζυγες ἵπποι, 10.473), and their armor lies in three rows (τριστοιχί, 10.473), an adverb found only here, though Scylla's teeth stand in three rows in the Odyssey (τρίστοιχοι, *Odyssey* 12.91). Dolon is “the only son among five sisters” (10.317; see [[dolon]]).

**Twelve and a thirteenth.** Diomedes kills among the Thracians “until he had killed twelve” (10.488), and then the king, “him, the thirteenth” (τὸν τρισκαιδέκατον, 10.495; see [[rhesus]]). Twelve is one of the poem's round numbers, and twelve men of the best die at once again when Achilles shouts from the trench (18.230). In his report to Nestor, Odysseus counts differently: “their lord was killed by brave Diomedes, and beside him twelve companions, all of the best. A thirteenth, a spy, we took close to the ships” (10.559–561). The same word, τὸν τρισκαιδέκατον, now names Dolon. The count holds if the king is set apart and Dolon is added to the twelve companions (see the translation's note on 10.559–561, and [[the-evil-dream]]). τρισκαιδέκατος occurs nowhere else in the Iliad.
