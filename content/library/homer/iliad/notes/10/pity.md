summary: The one plea for pity in Book 10 is an old man's, spoken with affection: Nestor asks Diomedes to go and wake the others, “for you are younger, if you have pity on me”. Dolon, begging for his life, never asks for pity, and the only word of the family in his speeches is the “pitiless bond” he asks to be tied with.
passages:
  - 10.169-179 | Nestor sends Diomedes
  - 10.442-445 | Dolon's plea
---

**An old man's plea.** Diomedes has told Nestor that he is a hard man who never rests, and that younger men could do the waking (10.164–167). Nestor agrees, explains the need, and then turns the young man's argument back on him: “But go now, and rouse swift Ajax and the son of Phyleus; for you are younger, if you have pity on me” (εἴ μʼ ἐλεαίρεις, 10.175–176). The verb is the one Andromache used of Hector's lack of pity for his child and wife (6.407) and Apollo of Athena's for the dying Trojans (7.27). Here it is a request between friends, and Diomedes goes at once (10.177–179; see [[nestor]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**No pity asked.** The suppliants of the later books ask for respect and pity in a formula: “I clasp your knees; respect me and pity me” (σὺ δέ μʼ αἴδεο καί μʼ ἐλέησον), says Lycaon to Achilles (21.74), and Hecuba, baring her breast, asks Hector in the same words to respect it and pity her (22.82). Dolon's plea has none of these words. He asks to be taken alive for ransom (10.378–381), and at the end he asks to be bound “with a pitiless bond” (νηλέϊ δεσμῷ, 10.443) until the two have tested his story. νηλής, “pitiless”, is the negative of ἔλεος. Diomedes does not pity him, and the narrator does not say that anyone did (see [[supplication]], [[aidos]] and [[apoina]]).
