The history of the helmet that Meriones puts on Odysseus' head runs through three kinds of passing. Autolycus stole it from the house of Amyntor at Eleon, breaking in; he gave it at Scandeia to Amphidamas of Cythera; “and Amphidamas gave it to Molus to be a guest-gift, and Molus gave it to his son Meriones to wear” (10.266–270; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]], [[autolycus]], [[amphidamas]] and [[molus]]). Theft, gift between friends, inheritance: by the time it reaches Odysseus the helmet carries the bond of guest and host along with its story.

Guest-gifts in Homer are often things of this kind, kept and handed down. Diomedes left at home the golden cup that Bellerophon gave his grandfather Oeneus when Oeneus entertained him for twenty days (6.216–221; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). The next morning Agamemnon arms in a corselet that Cinyras of Cyprus had given him “to be a guest-gift”, in the same half-verse (11.19–20). In the Odyssey the great bow with which Odysseus kills the suitors was a guest-gift from Iphitus, never taken to war but kept at home “as a memorial of a dear guest-friend” (*Odyssey* 21.11–41). The thief at the start of the story, Autolycus, is Odysseus' own grandfather in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 19.394–398), though the Iliad does not say so here: the gift comes back, through other hands, to his grandson.
