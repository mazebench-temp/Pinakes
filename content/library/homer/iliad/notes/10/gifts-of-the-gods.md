Paris' maxim in Book 3 is that the glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside, since no one could take them by his own will (3.65–66). Book 10 is about one such gift and the wish to have it. Dolon asks for the horses of Achilles, and Odysseus answers that they “are hard for mortal men to master and to drive, for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (10.402–404). The poem later says what makes them so: they are immortal, the gods' glorious gift to Peleus (16.380–381, 16.866–867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). In Book 20 the narrator joins the two thoughts in one sentence, when Aeneas fears that Achilles' spear will pierce his shield, not knowing “that the glorious gifts of the gods are not easy for mortal men to master or to make yield” (20.265–266). The first half-verse is Paris' (θεῶν ἐρικυδέα δῶρα, 3.65), the second Odysseus' to Dolon (ἀνδράσι γε θνητοῖσι δαμήμεναι, 10.403).

At the end of the night Nestor, seeing the horses of Rhesus, “terribly like the rays of the sun”, asks whether some god met the two men and gave them, and decides that one did, since Zeus and Athena love them both (10.545–553). Odysseus corrects him without contradicting the principle: “a god, if he wished, could easily give horses even better than these, since the gods are far stronger than we are. But these horses you ask about, old sir, are newly come; they are Thracian” (10.556–559). What a god could give, men have taken. See [[doron]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].
