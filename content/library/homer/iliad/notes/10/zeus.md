summary: Zeus does nothing in the night of Book 10, but men speak of him throughout. Agamemnon groans as thick as his lightning and believes that his mind has turned to Hector, Hector swears by him an oath that will prove false, and at the end Nestor says that Zeus loves Odysseus and Diomedes.
# πόσις Ἥρης, "the husband of Hera", names Zeus without his name at 10.5 (also 13.154, 16.88).

**Lightning.** The book's first simile is the god's weather. Agamemnon groans in his breast as thick and fast as when “the husband of Hera of the lovely hair flashes his lightning, / making ready a heavy, endless rain, or hail, / or snow … or somewhere the great mouth of bitter war” (10.5–8; see [[lightning-before-the-storm]] and [[hera]]). The god who makes ready the storm makes ready the war as well. When Agamemnon looks at his own army, he tears out his hair “to Zeus on high” (10.15–16), in appeal or in reproach (see the translation's note on 10.15–16). Later the spears of Diomedes' sleeping companions, driven upright into the ground, shine far off “like the lightning of father Zeus” (10.153–154). There is no other lightning in this night. Zeus does nothing in Book 10, and his weapon appears only in likenesses.

**The mind of Zeus.** Agamemnon tells Menelaus that they need counsel, “since the mind of Zeus has turned. / He has set his heart more on the sacrifices of Hector” (10.45–46; see [[agamemnon]]). Hector is “dear to Zeus”, though he is “the dear son of no goddess and of no god” (10.49–50; see [[dear-to-zeus]]). Agamemnon explains the god's favor by offerings, as Zeus himself will when he says that Hector never failed him in sacrifice (22.170–172, 24.66–70; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The poem has given another cause, the nod to Thetis (1.524–530; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Nestor answers with a hope: “surely Zeus the counselor will not fulfill for Hector all the plans / that he now hopes for” (10.104–105; see [[counselor]]), and Hector will have more sorrows if Achilles gives up his anger (10.106–107).

**Misery from birth.** Agamemnon tells his brother not to stand on his rank: “let us toil ourselves as well; so, it seems, / at our birth Zeus laid heavy misery on us” (10.70–71). To Nestor he calls himself the man “whom above all men / Zeus has plunged into toils without end” (10.88–89). Helen says the like of herself and Paris, “on whom Zeus has set an evil fate” (6.357), and Achilles will tell Priam that the gods have spun a life of grief for wretched mortals, and that Zeus gives from two jars, of evils and of blessings (24.525–533).

**The oath.** Hector takes the scepter in his hands and swears to Dolon: “Now let Zeus himself be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera, / that no other man shall ride behind those horses, / no other Trojan” (10.329–331; see [[oath]] and [[hector]]). Agamemnon closed his oath at the truce for the dead with the same half-verse (7.411; see [[loud-thundering]]). The narrator adds that Hector “swore an oath that would prove false” (10.332), without saying that he meant to deceive: Dolon never comes back to claim the horses (10.336–337). ἐπίορκος is elsewhere the oath that the powers below the earth punish (3.278–279; 19.258–260); here no god is said to punish it.

**The favor of Zeus.** When the two spies ride in, Nestor wonders whether a god gave them the horses, “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both, / and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena” (10.552–553; see [[cloud-gatherer]] and [[athena]]). The night that began with Agamemnon's fear of Zeus' favor for Hector ends with Zeus' love for the Achaean pair, and the narrator has already called Odysseus “dear to Zeus” on the ride back (10.527). Odysseus answers that a god could easily give better horses, “since the gods are far stronger than we are” (10.556–557; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). The half-verse is the one in which Achilles says the same of the gods (20.368), and Nestor of Zeus (8.144; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]).
