**The vow.** Diomedes ends his prayer before the raid with a promise:

{{quote:22403eed-4545-5df6-a313-cfcc580aa9d1}}

The three verses are those of Nestor's prayer to Athena at Pylos in the Odyssey, after the goddess has left his feast in the likeness of a bird (Od. 3.371–384). Here the prayer follows the heron she has sent (10.274–277; see [[heron]] and [[prayer]]). At Pylos the vow is kept the next morning, and the gilding is told step by step: a goldsmith is sent for, Nestor gives the gold, and the smith works it round the cow's horns “so that the goddess might rejoice when she saw the offering”, and Athena comes to meet the sacrifice (Od. 3.425–438). The heifer is ἦνις, “yearling”, and ἀδμής, “unbroken”, one “which no man has yet led under the yoke” (see [[yearling]] and [[unbroken]]): a victim that has never served men. ἦνις describes the twelve cows Hecuba is told to promise Athena, “yearlings, never touched by the goad” (6.93–94, 6.308–309).

**Not told.** The Iliad does not tell Diomedes' sacrifice. The night ends with Dolon's spoils laid in a ship's stern “until they could make ready an offering to Athena” (ἱρόν, 10.570–571; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]), and with a libation to her (10.578–579; see [[libation]]). Hecuba's vow is not fulfilled either: Athena refuses the prayer (6.311).

**Hector's sacrifices.** Agamemnon explains the defeat by the god's preference: Zeus “has set his heart more on the sacrifices of Hector” (Ἑκτορέοις … ἱεροῖσιν, 10.46; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Zeus himself will say later that Hector burned him many thighs of oxen, and that his altar never lacked its share (22.170–172, 24.66–70). The poem has given another cause for the Trojans' success, Zeus' promise to Thetis (1.524–530; see the translation's note on 10.46).
