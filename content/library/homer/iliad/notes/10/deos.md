summary: The night is framed by fear: Menelaus is “terribly afraid” that no one will go, Agamemnon “terribly afraid for the Danaans”, Nestor at the end “terribly afraid” for the two who went. Agamemnon fears for his brother, and the captured Dolon is “pale with fear”, trembling as the king trembled at the start.

**Terribly afraid.** Three speakers say αἰνῶς, “terribly”, with a verb of fear, at the beginning, the middle and the end of the Achaean night. Menelaus is “terribly afraid” that no one will undertake to spy on the Trojans (δείδω, 10.38–39). Agamemnon tells Nestor, “I am terribly afraid for the Danaans” (περιδείδια, 10.93), and describes the fear in his body: his heart is not steady, it leaps out of his breast, and his shining limbs tremble beneath him (10.93–95; see [[kradie]]). Nestor, hearing hoofbeats at the end, hopes it is Odysseus and Diomedes, “but I am terribly afraid at heart that the best men of the Argives have come to some harm” (δείδοικα, 10.538–539). The book opened with Agamemnon's mind trembling within him (10.10), and with “trembling” holding Menelaus (τρόμος, 10.25; see [[menelaus]]).

**Fear for a brother.** After telling Diomedes to choose the best companion and not the most kingly, “So he spoke, and he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (ὣς ἔφατʼ, ἔδεισεν δὲ περὶ ξανθῷ Μενελάῳ, 10.240). The verse begins with a formula that elsewhere follows a threat and gives the fear of the one threatened, who then obeys: Chryses, Hera, Helen, and Priam twice (1.33, 1.568, 3.418, 24.571, 24.689; see [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). Here the one afraid is the speaker himself, and his fear is for another: the narrator uses the line to show the motive behind Agamemnon's rule of merit (see [[aidos]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**The spy's fear.** The fear Agamemnon described in himself is seen in the enemy. When Diomedes' spear flies past him, Dolon “stood still, terrified, stammering, and there was a chattering of teeth in his mouth, pale with fear” (χλωρὸς ὑπαὶ δείους, 10.374–376); he answers Odysseus while “his limbs trembled beneath him” (ὑπὸ δʼ ἔτρεμε γυῖα, 10.390), as the king's “tremble beneath me” (τρομέει δʼ ὑπὸ φαίδιμα γυῖα, 10.95). χλωρός, “pale, greenish”, is the color of fear: “pale fear” takes the Achaeans at Zeus' thunder (7.479, 8.77; see [[pale-fear]]), and the routed Trojans are “pale with fear” when Zeus wakes on Ida (15.4). Even the horses are thought of: Odysseus drags the dead aside so that Rhesus' horses will not “tremble in their hearts” at treading on them (10.491–493).
