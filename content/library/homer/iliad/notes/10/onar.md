---
summary: Diomedes kills Rhesus in his sleep, “for an evil dream stood over his head that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena”: the killer takes the place of the dream that stands over a sleeper's head.
passages:
  - 10.494-497 | The evil dream
---

Rhesus is the thirteenth man Diomedes kills among the sleeping Thracians, robbed of his life “as he lay gasping; for an evil dream stood over his head that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena” (κακὸν γὰρ ὄναρ κεφαλῆφιν ἐπέστη … Οἰνεΐδαο πάϊς, 10.496–497; see [[rhesus]] and [[diomedes]]). The verses are usually read as a metaphor in apposition: Diomedes, standing over the sleeper, is himself the evil dream. Dreams in Homer stand over the head of the one they visit. The Dream sent to Agamemnon “stood above his head” in the likeness of Nestor (2.20, 2.59), and so do the ghost of Patroclus (23.68) and the phantom that Athena sends to Penelope (*Odyssey* 4.803). The verses have also been taken of a real dream in which the dying king saw his killer, and the Alexandrian editors are reported to have doubted the second verse (see [[the-evil-dream]]).

The two readings agree on what the dream is. At the start of Book 2 Zeus, sleepless, sent a “baneful Dream” to a sleeping king by his own design (οὖλον ὄνειρον, 2.6; see [[agamemnons-dream]]); here Athena's design sends an “evil dream” to another sleeping king, and it kills him. In the last book Hermes will stand over a sleeping king's head in the night, in the same verse as the dreams, to wake Priam and bring him safely out of the Achaean camp (24.682). ὄναρ itself is rare: in the Iliad it occurs only here and in Achilles' reminder that “a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (1.63; see [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]).

The *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, gives the dream to Rhesus' charioteer, who saw in his sleep wolves mounted on the backs of the horses (*Rhesus* 780–788; see [[euripides-rhesus]] and [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).
