summary: In the night after Hector's day the Achaean leaders try to read the plan. Agamemnon thinks that “the mind of Zeus has turned” toward Hector's sacrifices (10.45–46); Nestor answers that Zeus will not fulfill all of Hector's hopes, and that Hector will have worse sorrows if Achilles turns from his anger (10.103–107), the term that Zeus himself has set.

**The mind of Zeus has turned.** Agamemnon tells Menelaus that they need shrewd counsel to save the Argives and the ships, “since the mind of Zeus has turned. / He has set his heart more on the sacrifices of Hector” (10.45–46). He reads the defeat as a change in the god's mind and gives it a cause, Hector's offerings (Ἑκτορέοις … ἱεροῖσιν). The poem has given another: the nod to Thetis (1.524–530), and the prophecy Zeus spoke to Hera at nightfall, that Hector will not cease from war until Achilles is roused beside the ships (8.473–477; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Hector's sacrifices are real enough; Zeus himself will remember the thighs of oxen Hector burned to him on Ida and in the citadel (22.170–172), and he will call Hector the dearest to the gods of all the men in Ilios, because he never failed in the gifts they love (24.66–70). But in the verses of 8.548 and 8.550–552, known only from the Second Alcibiades, the gods take no share of the Trojans' hecatombs on this very night (see the translation's notes on 10.46 and 8.548, 8.550–552, and [[the-refused-sacrifice]]). In Book 9 Agamemnon read the plan rightly, that Zeus “has honored this man and beaten down the army of the Achaeans” (9.118); in the dark of Book 10 he reads it from the Trojan side, as favor won by sacrifice (see [[agamemnon]] and [[zeus]]).

**Toil and honor.** Agamemnon's complaint borrows the end of an Achilles verse. Zeus has plunged him “into toils without end, as long as breath / stays in my breast and my knees have strength to move” (10.89–90). Achilles had used the same words of the honor he has from Zeus, “the portion of Zeus, / which will keep me beside the curved ships, as long as breath / stays in my breast and my knees have strength to move” (9.608–610). From the same god one man has honor and the other toil. He had already drawn the moral for his brother: let them toil like the rest, “so, it seems, / at our birth Zeus laid heavy misery on us” (10.70–71).

**The term.** Nestor answers: “surely Zeus the counselor will not fulfill for Hector all the plans / that he now hopes for, I suppose; but I think he will labor / under sorrows still greater, if Achilles / turns his dear heart from its grievous anger” (10.104–107). He does not know the plan, but he names its term, the one Zeus set (8.473–477) and Achilles repeated to the embassy, that he would not fight until Hector reached the Myrmidons' ships (9.650–655; see [[longing-for-achilles]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Nestor's “if” leaves the time open; Zeus has already named the price, the death of Patroclus (8.476). Meanwhile the night belongs to the Achaeans. When the two come back with Rhesus' horses, Nestor says it plainly: “Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both” (10.552), and Odysseus answers that a god could easily give better horses still, “since the gods are far stronger than we are” (10.556–557; see [[the-doloneia]]).
