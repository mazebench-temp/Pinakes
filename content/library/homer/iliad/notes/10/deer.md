summary: The deer of Book 10 is the quarry of the chase. Diomedes and Odysseus run Dolon down as two hounds press “a fawn or a hare” through wooded country (10.360–364); κεμάς, the word for this young deer, occurs nowhere else in Homer.
grc: ["κεμάδʼ"]
---

ἢ κεμάδʼ ἠὲ λαγωόν, “a fawn or a hare” (10.361). κεμάς is a young deer, rendered “fawn” as νεβρός is elsewhere (4.243, 8.248), and it is found only in this verse of Homer. The animal is chosen for its speed and its fear. Dolon, “swift-footed” (10.316), runs like the fawn ahead of two hounds, crying out, until he is cut off near the Achaean sentries; then he stands still, his teeth chattering, pale with fear (10.360–376; see [[two-hounds]], [[hare]] and [[dolon]]).

The fawn is the hounds' quarry in other similes too. Antilochus springs on a Trojan like a dog on a fawn that a hunter has wounded (15.579–581), and Achilles chases Hector around the walls as a dog chases a fawn through the glens and ravines (22.188–193). The deer is also the coward's beast. Achilles told Agamemnon that he had “the heart of a deer” (1.225; see [[dog-eyes-deer-heart]]), and Agamemnon asked his own men why they stood dazed like fawns (4.243; see [[like-fawns]]). The swift and frightened spy of Book 10 is both.
