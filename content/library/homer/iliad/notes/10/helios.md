---
summary: The sun is named once in Book 10, in a likeness. Nestor, seeing Rhesus' horses brought in through the dark, finds them “terribly like the rays of the sun” (10.547).
---

The night has no sun, and its one sun is a comparison. When Odysseus and Diomedes ride in, Nestor asks how they took the horses, or whether some god met them and gave them: “They are terribly like the rays of the sun” (10.544–547; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]] and [[nestor]]). Dolon called them “whiter than snow, and like the winds in running” (10.437). ἀκτίς, “ray”, occurs only here in the Iliad. In the Odyssey it is always the rays of “the shining sun” that never reach a place: the thicket where Odysseus sleeps on Scheria (*Odyssey* 5.479), the boar's lair on Parnassus (*Odyssey* 19.441, nearly the same verse), and the land of the Cimmerians (*Odyssey* 11.16).

The likeness goes with Nestor's guess that the horses are a god's gift (10.546, 10.551). Elsewhere the sun is the measure of shining bronze (6.513, 19.398, 22.134–135; see [[like-the-sun]]), of Hera's veil (14.185), and of the halls of Menelaus and Alcinous (*Odyssey* 4.45, 7.84). Only here is it the measure of horses. In the Odyssey the horses that draw Dawn and bring light to men are named Lampus and Phaethon, “Bright” and “Shining” (*Odyssey* 23.244–246; see [[eos]]).
