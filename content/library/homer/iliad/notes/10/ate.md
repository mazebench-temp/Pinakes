summary: The captured Dolon blames his errand on Hector: “With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense.” The blindness is laid on a man, not a god, and it is the promise of Achilles' horses.
grc: [ἄτῃσι]
en: [blind follies]
passages:
  - 10.390-399 | Dolon blames Hector
---

Dolon, his teeth chattering, answers Odysseus' question why he came: “With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense” (πολλῇσίν μʼ ἄτῃσι παρὲκ νόον ἤγαγεν Ἕκτωρ, 10.391; see [[noos]]). The plural is rare. The only other plurals of ἄτη in Homer are Agamemnon's “my blind folly”, ἐμὰς ἄτας, when he admits his fault before the elders (9.115), and Achilles' cry when the quarrel is over, “Father Zeus, truly you give men great blind follies” (19.270). Achilles names Zeus as the giver, and Agamemnon too laid his blindness on Zeus (9.18; 19.86–89); Dolon names a man, and says how it was done. Hector “nodded his assent” to give him the horses and chariot of Achilles (κατένευσε, 10.392–393), the verb of Zeus' nod to Thetis (1.524–527), and sent him through the night (10.394–399). The blinding was a promise, and Hector's oath on it “would prove false” (10.332; see [[oath]]).

The two senses of the word, the blindness and the ruin that follows it, meet in the scene as they do in Phoenix's allegory (9.502–512). The folly that sent Dolon out is followed a few lines later by his death, when Diomedes kills him as he reaches to supplicate (10.454–457; see [[dolon]] and [[supplication]]).
