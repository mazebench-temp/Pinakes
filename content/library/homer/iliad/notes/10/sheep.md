**The black ewe.** Nestor asks whether any man will go among the Trojans and learn their plans. He would win fame under heaven, and a gift as well:

{{quote:961a13c3-52d3-5c07-9336-fde4e5bccf75}}

ὄϊν … μέλαιναν / θῆλυν ὑπόρρηνον: “a black ewe, / with a lamb at her udder”. ὄις is a sheep of either sex, and θῆλυς, “female”, says outright what the feminine μέλαιναν already shows; ὑπόρρηνος, “with a lamb under her”, occurs only here. It is built on ῥήν, “lamb”, which is also in πολύρρηνες, “rich in flocks”, the men of the seven cities Agamemnon offers Achilles (9.154; see [[rich-in-flocks]] and [[lambs]]). A ewe is a modest gift. Its worth is in the giving, one from every chief who commands a ship, and in what goes with it, a place at every feast; “no possession is like it” (10.216; see the translation's note on 10.215–217). Why the ewes are black is not said. Black victims go to the powers below: the black female lamb for Earth at the oaths of Book 3 (3.103–104), and the black ewe that Odysseus offers to the dead (*Odyssey* 10.527). The Trojan volunteer asks for a richer prize, the horses and chariot of Achilles (10.322–323; see [[doron]]). The poem does not say that the ewes were ever given.

**The flock in the similes.** The Achaean sentries keep watch like dogs “around the sheep in a fold” (περὶ μῆλα, 10.183) when they hear a beast coming down through the woods (10.183–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). In the Thracian camp the flock has no guard. Diomedes kills the sleepers as a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman, “on goats or sheep” (μήλοισιν ἀσημάντοισιν … αἴγεσιν ἢ ὀΐεσσι, 10.485–486; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]] and [[goats]]). μῆλα are the small livestock, sheep and goats together. The first flock is kept, the second is not.
