summary: The volunteers on both sides answer in the same verse, “my heart and my proud spirit urge me”, and Odysseus' heart “was always daring”. Odysseus asks Dolon whether his own heart sent him, and finds that it “was reaching for great gifts”. The θυμός is also the life that Diomedes takes from the sleeping Thracians, and the heart of the horses that must not shy.
grc: [μεγαθύμους, καταθύμιος]
en: [on your mind]

**What urges a man.** Nestor asks for a man who would trust “his own daring heart” (θυμῷ τολμήεντι, 10.205), and both answers begin in the same words, with only the name changed: “Nestor, my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.220) and “Hector, my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.319; see [[kradie]] and [[proud]]). The pairing κραδίη καὶ θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ is the poem's phrase for the impulse that drives a man, or that must be restrained: Ajax uses it of the man who accepts the blood-price for a brother and restrains his heart and proud spirit (9.635–636), and in the Odyssey Telemachus says it to the disguised Odysseus before his fight with the beggar Irus (*Odyssey* 18.61). Diomedes says it of Odysseus, “whose heart and proud spirit are eager above all others in every toil” (10.244), and the narrator says of him among the volunteers, “the heart in his breast was always daring” (θυμὸς ἐτόλμα, 10.232; see [[tharsos]]).

**What urged Dolon.** Odysseus asks the captured spy whether Hector sent him out, “or did your own heart send you?” (ἦ σʼ αὐτὸν θυμὸς ἀνῆκε, 10.389). Dolon blames Hector (10.391), and Odysseus answers with a smile, “Truly your heart was reaching for great gifts” (10.401; see [[doron]]). He had told him a moment before, “do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383), with καταθύμιος, “in the heart”, a word of the same family. Diomedes ends the questioning: “Do not put any thought of escape in your heart, Dolon” (10.447).

**Life.** θυμός is also the life that a blow takes away. Diomedes tells Dolon what will happen if he loses his life (ἀπὸ θυμὸν ὀλέσσῃς, 10.452), robs Rhesus of “his honey-sweet life” (μελιηδέα θυμὸν ἀπηύρα, 10.495; see [[honey-sweet]]), and wonders whether to “take the lives of still more of the Thracians” (10.506). The phrase of 10.495 recurs when Menelaus threatens Euphorbus (17.17) and when the ghost of Odysseus' mother tells him that longing for him took her “honey-sweet life” (*Odyssey* 11.203).

**The horses' heart.** Animals have a θυμός too. Odysseus drags the dead out of the way so that Rhesus' horses might pass easily and “not tremble in their hearts at treading on the dead” (10.491–493), and after the raid they fly to the ships “not unwilling …, for there their hearts were glad to go” (10.530–531; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).
