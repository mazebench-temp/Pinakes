summary: The night armings of Book 10 are made of skins and leather. Agamemnon and Diomedes throw on lion skins, Menelaus a leopard's skin, Nestor a cloak; the two spies are armed by others with borrowed weapons and caps of hide without ridge or crest, and the Trojan spy Dolon in a wolf's skin and a ferret-skin cap. No one puts on greaves or a corselet until Agamemnon arms in bronze at dawn (11.15–46).

**Arming at night.** Six men arm in Book 10, in animal skins, caps of hide and borrowed weapons (see [[lion-skin]], [[leopard-skin]] and [[wolf-skin]]), and none of them puts on the bronze of a full arming, greaves, corselet, sword, shield, helmet and spear (3.330–338; see [[arming-scene]]).

**The brothers.** Agamemnon puts on his tunic and, in the verse of his dressing in Book 2, his sandals; but where he threw a great mantle around him then, he now puts on “the blood-red skin of a lion, a great tawny lion, reaching to his feet”, and takes up his spear (10.21–24; 2.42–44). A dressing scene ends as an arming (see [[dressing-scene]]). Menelaus arms in three verses: “First he covered his broad back with a leopard's skin, dappled, and lifting a helmet of bronze he set it on his head, and took up a spear in his strong hand” (10.29–31). “First” (πρῶτα) begins the greaves verse of a full arming (3.330). Menelaus finds his brother “putting his fine armor about his shoulders” and asks why he is arming (κορύσσεαι, 10.34–37); the poem does not say what Agamemnon put on besides the skin and the spear.

**Nestor.** His armor lies beside his bed, “a shield and two spears and a shining helmet”, with the belt he girded on whenever he armed for war (10.75–79; see [[belt]]). He dresses in Agamemnon's two verses, pins on a cloak (see [[cloak]]), and takes only the spear: “he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze” (10.131–135). The verse recurs when Nestor arms again at the start of Book 14, with the shield of his son Thrasymedes, “for his son had his father's” (14.9–12), and when Teucer gives up his broken bow for the spear (15.479–482).

**Odysseus and Diomedes.** Odysseus goes into his hut and “put his inlaid shield about his shoulders” (10.149), the words of Teucer's arming (15.479; see [[shield]]). Diomedes has slept “with his armor”, and he arms with the end of Agamemnon's verses, the lion's skin and the spear (10.151, 10.177–178 ≈ 10.23–24).

**The spies.** When the two have been chosen, their arming is framed by the same half-verse at its beginning and end, “the two put on their dread armor” (10.254, 10.272; see [[ring-composition]]). ὅπλα, “arms”, is used of armor elsewhere in the Iliad only of the arms Hephaestus makes for Achilles (18.614, 19.21). Every piece is someone else's:

{{quote:5fdcd4f3-31a4-5510-af52-c501071559f3}}

Meriones gives Odysseus a bow, a quiver, a sword, and a leather helmet faced with a boar's white tusks (10.260–265; see [[bow]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). The order is that of a full arming without its first two pieces, sword, shield, helmet; there are no greaves and no corselets. The helmet of a full arming has a crest that “nodded terribly” (3.337), and Agamemnon's at dawn will have two ridges (ἀμφίφαλον, 11.41). Diomedes' cap is ἄφαλόν τε καὶ ἄλλοφον, “without ridge or crest” (10.258), words found only here (see [[helmet]]).

As in a full arming, one piece has a history. Agamemnon's corselet will be a guest-gift from Cinyras (11.19–23); Odysseus' helmet passed from the thief Autolycus to Meriones, and Amphidamas gave it to Molus δῶκε ξεινήϊον εἶναι, “to be a guest-gift” (10.266–271), the half-verse of Cinyras' gift (11.20; see [[autolycus]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**Dolon.** The Trojan spy arms in the same manner:

{{quote:687fd5f6-ec7f-5a23-b2ad-5f2bffa248b1}}

His bow, skin, cap and throwing spear answer the Achaeans' pieces, and his killers take them off him, “the cap of ferret skin, and the wolfskin, and the back-bent bow, and the long spear” (10.458–459): the arming undone from the head down, with the spear last as before (see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Rhesus' armor of gold, which Dolon calls fit for the gods, is never put on (10.438–441).

**Dawn.** The next morning Agamemnon arms in full, greaves, Cinyras' corselet, sword, the shield with the Gorgon, the crested helmet, two spears, “and far off the bronze shone from them up into the sky” (11.15–45). By night the sleepers' spear points shone in the same words, “far off the bronze shone like the lightning of father Zeus” (10.153–154; see [[spear]]). The night armings are among the things that set Book 10 apart. They have been taken as a sign that the book was composed separately, and as the gear of a traditional kind of story, the ambush, as Casey Dué and Mary Ebbott argue (*Iliad 10 and the Poetics of Ambush*, 2010; see [[ambush]] and [[the-doloneia]]).
