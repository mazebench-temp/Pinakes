βάν ῥʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέοντε δύω διὰ νύκτα μέλαιναν / ἂμ φόνον, ἂν νέκυας, διά τʼ ἔντεα καὶ μέλαν αἷμα: “they went like two lions through the black night, / through the slaughter, through the corpses, through the armor and the black blood” (10.297–298). The comparison is half a verse, and the dual λέοντε δύω, “two lions”, is the dual of the simile of Book 5, where the twin sons of Diocles fell like two young lions killed by men (5.554–560). Those lions were the hunted; these go out to hunt. The second verse fills the comparison with the place they cross, three times “through”: the plain between the camps is covered with the dead of the day's battle, which the chiefs avoided when they sat down “in a clear space” beyond the ditch (10.198–200). When the two go on from Dolon's body toward the Thracians, the end of the verse returns, “through the armor and the black blood” (10.469).

Lions hunt by night in the similes, and men and dogs stay awake to keep them off (11.548–555), and two beasts throw a herd into confusion “in the dead of black night” when the herdsman is away (15.323–325; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]). The two men set out in lion's character as well as in lion's skin, for Diomedes put on a lion's skin when Nestor woke him (10.177–178; see [[lion]]). The book changes the beast as the night goes on. In the chase of Dolon the pair are two hounds (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]]), and among the sleeping Thracians Diomedes is again a lion, alone, among flocks without a herdsman (10.485–488).
