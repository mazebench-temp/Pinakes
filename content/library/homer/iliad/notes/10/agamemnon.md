summary: On the night of the embassy Agamemnon cannot sleep. He groans at the Trojan fires, tears his hair and goes in a lion's skin to wake Nestor; he sends Menelaus to call the chiefs by their fathers' names, tells Nestor of his fear, and bids Diomedes choose his companion for the raid by merit, “not even if he is more kingly”, fearing for his brother.

**Sleepless.** The book opens as Book 2 opened, the others asleep all night and one awake, with the king in the place of Zeus (10.1–4; 2.1–4; see [[hypnos]] and [[a-man-of-counsel-should-not-sleep]]). The pattern returns in the last book, where Hermes alone lies awake, planning how to bring Priam from the ships (24.677–681; 24.677 = 2.1, 24.678 = 10.2).

{{quote:dcd22b52-4b02-5038-98b5-9b7c0d8635f4}}

His groans come as thick and fast as the flashes of Zeus' lightning (10.5–10; see [[lightning-before-the-storm]]). When he looks toward Troy he marvels at the fires and at the flutes and pipes, which the Iliad mentions elsewhere only on the shield of Achilles (10.11–13; 18.495, 18.526; see [[trojans]]). When he looks at his own army he tears out his hair “to Zeus on high” (10.14–16), a mourner's gesture (22.77–78, 22.406), made to the god whose mind, he believes, has turned against him (see [[hair]] and the translation's note on 10.15–16).

**The brothers.** Menelaus, awake too, puts on a leopard's skin and finds his brother arming by his ship, in a lion's skin (10.23–35; see [[menelaus]], [[lion-skin]] and [[leopard-skin]]). The army needs “counsel, shrewd counsel”, Agamemnon says, “since the mind of Zeus has turned. He has set his heart more on the sacrifices of Hector” (10.43–46). The poem has given another cause, Zeus' nod to Thetis (1.524–530), though Zeus himself will say that Hector never failed him in sacrifice (22.170–172, 24.66–70; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Then he gives his orders:

{{quote:ef53b333-2446-5b5a-baef-03cd820785e6}}

Every man is to be called by his father's name and honored, and the kings must toil themselves (see [[kings]] and [[kydos]]). To Nestor he calls himself the man whom Zeus “has plunged into toils without end, as long as breath stays in my breast and my knees have strength to move” (10.89–90). The closing words are Achilles', from the same night, where they measure how long “the portion of Zeus” that honors him will keep him beside the ships (9.608–610; see [[achilles]] and [[aisa]]).

**The fear.** Then he tells Nestor what keeps him awake:

{{quote:c4afaae2-3bf5-5fe4-9627-fec92c2d56cd}}

ἀλαλύκτημαι, “I am distraught”, occurs only here (see the translation's note on 10.94). In 49 BC, when news came that Pompey and the consuls had left Italy, Cicero gave these words to Atticus for his own distress (*Letters to Atticus* 9.6.4; see [[cicero]]). The fear has a practical end: the sentries may have fallen asleep (10.96–101; see [[night-watch]]). When Nestor blames Menelaus for sleeping, the king defends his brother, who often hangs back “not giving way to sloth or to thoughtlessness of mind, but looking to me and waiting for me to lead” (10.120–125).

**The choice.** When many volunteer to go with Diomedes, Agamemnon tells him to choose the best, and not to take a worse man, “giving way to respect, looking to his lineage, not even if he is more kingly” (10.235–239; see [[aidos]]). “So he spoke, and he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.240). The man who has just told his brother to name each man “by his father and his lineage” (πατρόθεν ἐκ γενεῆς, 10.68) now forbids Diomedes to choose by lineage (ἐς γενεὴν ὁρόων, 10.239), and βασιλεύτερος, “more kingly”, the word of his demand that Achilles submit (9.160), has become a claim that must give way to merit. In Book 7 he held Menelaus back from fighting Hector (7.104–119). Diomedes chooses Odysseus (10.242–247; see [[diomedes]]).

**His ship.** Dolon means to go through the camp “to Agamemnon's ship, where I suppose the chiefs are taking counsel” (10.325–327; see [[dolon]]). Ἀγαμεμνονέην, “of Agamemnon”, is an adjective made from the name, as Ἑκτορέοις is from Hector's (10.46). The elders had feasted in his hut that night (9.89–90), but the chiefs are now sitting beyond the ditch. When the spies return, Nestor questions them, and the king is not named again in the book. The next morning he arms in bronze and is the first to kill (11.15–46, 11.91–92).
