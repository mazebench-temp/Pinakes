**The price.** Hector offers the man who will spy on the ships “a chariot and two horses with high-arched necks, / the best there are at the swift ships of the Achaeans” (10.305–306; see [[hector]]). The best are Achilles' (2.769–770), and Dolon names them: “the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze / that carry the noble son of Peleus” (10.322–323; see [[dolon]]). His words echo the end of the Catalogue's praise, ἵπποι θʼ οἳ φορέεσκον ἀμύμονα Πηλεΐωνα, “and so were the horses that carried the noble son of Peleus” (2.770). Hector swears by Zeus “that no other man shall ride behind those horses, / no other Trojan” (10.330–331), and the narrator calls it an oath “that would prove false” (10.332; see [[oath]]). Captured, Dolon says that Hector led him astray “with many blind follies” when he nodded his assent to give him “the single-hoofed horses of the noble son of Peleus” (10.391–393; see [[ate]]).

**Odysseus' answer.**

{{quote:1be7fc5b-bf47-54bb-97af-a9d3e4586cb0}}

They are the gods' horses, and only a goddess's son can master them (see [[achilles]] and [[thetis]]). Apollo, in the likeness of Mentes, will say the same three verses to Hector when Hector is chasing them (17.76–78 ≈ 10.402–404), and Zeus will resolve that Hector, too, shall not ride behind them (17.448–449). In the end Hector's oath holds for the Trojan who swore it.

Dolon went out for the best horses at the Achaean ships, and the two Achaeans come back with the best horses in the Trojan camp (see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).
