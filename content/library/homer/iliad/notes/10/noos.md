summary: The night turns on seeing. Diomedes asks for a companion because of two men one “sees” first and a man alone has a slower mind, and chooses Odysseus, who “is wise beyond all others”; yet Odysseus forgets the whip. Nestor trusts that Zeus will not fulfill all Hector's plans, and Dolon says that Hector led him “astray from my good sense”.

**Hector's plans.** Agamemnon has said that Zeus favors Hector (10.45–46). Nestor answers with a hope: “surely Zeus the counselor will not fulfill for Hector all the plans that he now hopes for” (πάντα νοήματα, 10.104–105). A νόημα is what a man has in mind, an intention or a plan, and the hope is that only the god's will be fulfilled (see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Achilles will say the same of himself after Patroclus' death: “Zeus does not bring all men's plans to fulfillment” (ἀλλʼ οὐ Ζεὺς ἄνδρεσσι νοήματα πάντα τελευτᾷ, 18.328).

**Thoughtlessness.** Nestor blames Menelaus for sleeping while his brother toils; Agamemnon defends him. Menelaus often hangs back, “not giving way to sloth or to thoughtlessness of mind” (ἀφραδίῃσι νόοιο, 10.122), but because he looks to his brother to lead (10.120–123; see [[menelaus]]). The same noun returns with Dolon, who runs past the hidden Achaeans “in his thoughtlessness” (ἀφραδίῃσιν, 10.350).

**Seeing first.** Diomedes' maxim is a statement about νόος. Of two men going together “one sees before the other” (ἐνόησεν), while the man alone, “even if he sees it” (νοήσῃ), has a slower mind (νόος, 10.224–226; see [[when-two-go-together]]). νοέω is the moment of seeing and understanding together, and Diomedes chooses Odysseus because “he is wise beyond all others” (περίοιδε νοῆσαι, 10.247), literally “he knows beyond others how to see”. The night bears this out: Odysseus notices Dolon coming (10.339–340) and sees Rhesus first (10.476). But the poem gives him one lapse. When he drives off the Thracian horses he strikes them with his bow, “since he had not thought of” the shining whip, to take it from the inlaid chariot (οὐ … νοήσατο, 10.500–501). Nestor, who has seen many horses in a long life, “never yet” saw “or noticed” horses like these (οὐδὲ νόησα, 10.550).

**Beyond his mind.** Dolon, captured, blames his errand on Hector: “With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense” (παρὲκ νόον ἤγαγεν, 10.391). παρὲκ νόον is “beyond (good) sense”, the phrase Poseidon uses when he tells Hera not to be angry “beyond reason” (παρʼ ἐκ νόον, 20.133). The promise of Achilles' horses took Dolon out of his mind, and Odysseus answers with a smile that those horses are hard for any mortal but Achilles to master (10.400–404; see [[ate]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
