summary: The verb comes three times, all in Nestor's mouth, as he goes round the camp at night. He will rebuke Menelaus “even if you blame me for it”, says that no Argive will then blame Menelaus or disobey him, and asks the woken Odysseus not to be indignant.
passages:
  - 10.114-130 | Nestor and Agamemnon on Menelaus
  - 10.141-147 | Nestor wakes Odysseus
---

**Blame for blaming.** Nestor tells Agamemnon that he will rebuke Menelaus for sleeping while his brother toils, “even if you blame me for it” (εἴ πέρ μοι νεμεσήσεαι, 10.115). To rebuke a king's brother before the king is to risk the king's indignation, and Nestor names the risk and takes it (see [[neikos]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). Agamemnon answers without anger: Menelaus is already awake, and out calling the chiefs (10.120–127).

**Blame and authority.** Nestor's reply turns the word round: “In that case no one will blame him or disobey him among the Argives, whenever he urges anyone on and gives him an order” (οὔ τίς οἱ νεμεσήσεται οὐδʼ ἀπιθήσει, 10.129–130). A man who does his share can give orders without resentment. νέμεσις is what others feel when someone takes more than his due, and a leader who toils is safe from it (see [[kingship-and-authority]]).

**Do not be indignant.** Woken by Nestor's call, Odysseus comes out of his hut and asks why they wander the camp at night (10.141–142). Nestor's first words are an apology: “do not be indignant; such grief has overpowered the Achaeans” (μὴ νεμέσα, 10.145). To wake a chief in the dark is a thing a man might resent, and the old man asks pardon for it by naming the need (see [[achos]]). Diomedes, woken next by a nudge of Nestor's heel, does protest: “You are a hard man, old sir” (10.164), and Nestor grants that all he says is “fair and right” (10.169; see [[moira]]).
