summary: Rhesus has come to Troy with a chariot worked in gold and silver and golden armor “fit for the immortal gods”, as Dolon describes them. Diomedes vows Athena a heifer with gold poured round her horns, and Dolon offers the ransom of gold, bronze and iron that captives offer.

**Armor for gods.** Dolon tells his captors what the newly come Thracian king has brought: the finest horses he has seen, “and his chariot is finely worked with gold and silver; and armor of gold, huge, a wonder to see, he brought with him; it is not fit at all for mortal men to wear, but for the immortal gods” (10.438–441; see [[rhesus]]). Gold is the metal of the gods' things, of Hera's chariot and Zeus' scales (5.722–731, 8.69), and πελώρια θαῦμα ἰδέσθαι, “huge, a wonder to see”, is the phrase with which Achilles will describe the armor the gods gave his father, which Hector stripped from Patroclus (18.83–84). Glaucus' golden armor, exchanged for Diomedes' bronze, was worth a hundred oxen against nine (6.234–236; see [[gold-for-bronze]]). Rhesus' gold is never taken. Diomedes thinks of carrying off the chariot “where the inlaid armor lay”, but Athena sends him back to the ships with the horses alone (10.503–511; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**Gilded horns.** Diomedes' vow to Athena promises the costliest victim: “a yearling heifer, broad of brow, unbroken, … with gold poured around her horns” (10.292–294). The three verses are Nestor's at Pylos in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 3.382–384), where the goldsmith comes with his anvil and tongs, and Nestor gives him the gold to work around the horns before the sacrifice (*Odyssey* 3.425–438; see [[sacrifice]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).

**The ransom.** Dolon, “a man rich in gold, rich in bronze” (πολύχρυσος πολύχαλκος, 10.315; see [[rich-in-gold]]), offers the ransom of a rich man's house: “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” (10.379). The verse is Adrastus' (6.48) and will be the sons of Antimachus' (11.133); in the Odyssey it names the treasure in the storeroom where Odysseus' bow is kept (*Odyssey* 21.9–10; see [[apoina]]). Apollo, who wakes the Thracians, is “god of the silver bow” (ἀργυρότοξος, 10.515; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]).
