summary: The exchange with Athena runs through the night and works. The two spies pray to her, Odysseus by her constant help and Diomedes by her help to his father, with the vow of a gilded heifer; Odysseus offers her Dolon's spoils with a promise and a further request; and the night ends with wine poured to her.

**Two prayers.** Athena sends a heron on the right, heard in the dark, and the two men pray to her before setting out. Odysseus claims a lasting bond: “Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, you who always stand by me in all my toils, and whatever I do you see it; now again love me above all, Athena”, and asks for a safe return with glory (10.278–282). Diomedes claims an inherited one and adds a payment:

{{quote:344b6a5d-115b-52f6-b654-bb81184fa49e}}

Between them the two prayers repeat Diomedes' prayer of Book 5, “Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone: if ever in friendship you stood by my father and me in destructive war, now be my friend in turn, Athena” (5.115–117; see the note on Book 5). Odysseus takes its opening and its “love me”, Diomedes its title Atrytone and its appeal to her help for his father (see [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[philos]]). Only Diomedes promises anything, and he says it in the language of exchange: “And I in turn will sacrifice to you” (σοὶ δʼ αὖ ἐγώ, 10.292). The three verses of the vow are Nestor's at Pylos in the Odyssey, where the sacrifice is made and the goldsmith gilds the horns (*Odyssey* 3.382–384, 3.425–438; see [[sacrifice]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The Iliad does not tell whether Diomedes paid. “So they spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard them” (10.295).

**The spoils for the goddess.** After Dolon is killed, Odysseus holds up his cap, wolfskin, bow and spear to “Athena, goddess of the spoil” (ληΐτιδι, 10.460, only here), and prays: “Rejoice in these, goddess; for you, first on Olympus of all the immortals, we will call upon; but once again guide us to the horses and the beds of the Thracian men” (10.462–464). The gift answers a favor received and asks for the next. ἐπιδωσόμεθα is taken here as “we will call upon”, as θεοὺς ἐπιδώμεθα, “let us call the gods (to witness)” (22.254); it has also been read as “we will give (gifts) to you”, a promise of more (see the translation's note on 10.463). He sets the spoils on a tamarisk and marks the place (10.465–468), takes them up again on the way back (10.527–529), and at the ships lays them in a ship's stern “until they could make ready an offering to Athena” (ὄφρʼ ἱρὸν ἑτοιμασσαίατʼ Ἀθήνῃ, 10.570–571; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]). The offering itself is not told.

**The libation.** The book ends with the goddess's share: after the bath and the meal, “to Athena, from the full mixing bowl, they drew honey-sweet wine and poured it out” (10.578–579; see [[libation]]). In Book 7 the army poured to Zeus in fear of his thunder (7.480–481), and in Book 9 the kings poured before they slept (9.712). Here the libation is given to one god, by name, for a night in which she gave what she was asked. Nestor has already said why: “Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both, and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena” (10.552–553; see [[athena]]).
