---
summary: The rebukes of this night are spoken among friends. Nestor means to rebuke Menelaus for sleeping, and rebukes Diomedes to his face as he wakes him; Diomedes answers with a complaint that is half praise, and Odysseus asks Diomedes neither to praise him nor to rebuke him.
grc: [νεικέσω, νείκεσε]
passages:
  - 10.114-127 | Nestor and Agamemnon on Menelaus
  - 10.157-176 | Nestor wakes Diomedes
---

νεικέω, “rebuke”, is used three times in Book 10, and none of the rebukes is a quarrel. Nestor tells Agamemnon that he will rebuke Menelaus, “dear and respected though he is”, for sleeping while his brother toils (νεικέσω, 10.114–116; see [[aidos]] and [[nemesis]]); Agamemnon answers that Menelaus is already awake and out calling the chiefs. Nestor wakes Diomedes with his heel, “and urged him on and rebuked him to his face” (ὄτρυνέ τε νείκεσέ τʼ ἄντην, 10.158), with a question that echoes the Dream's reproach in Book 2, “Why do you sleep the whole night through?” (10.159; see [[hypnos]]). In the Odyssey the same half-verse introduces a real insult, the young Phaeacian Euryalus' taunt that Odysseus looks like no athlete (*Odyssey* 8.158).

Diomedes answers in kind: “You are a hard man, old sir; you never cease from toil” (σχέτλιός ἐσσι γεραιέ, 10.164). σχέτλιος, “hard, relentless”, is the word Ajax used of Achilles in Book 9 (9.630), but here the reproach is close to praise, and Nestor takes it so (10.169). In the Odyssey Eurylochus begins the same way when Odysseus would drive his tired men past the island of the Sun at nightfall: “You are a hard man, Odysseus; your strength is beyond the common, and your limbs never tire”, and goes on to complain that his companions are “worn out by weariness and by sleep”, the words of Agamemnon's fear for the sentries (*Odyssey* 12.279–281; 10.98). The last rebuke is refused before it is spoken: Odysseus asks Diomedes, “do not praise me too much, nor rebuke me at all” (μήτέ τι νείκει, 10.249; see [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]] and [[praise-and-blame]]).
