---
summary: The same verse of silence meets Nestor's call for a spy and Hector's, “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence”; Diomedes breaks it on one side and Dolon on the other. In the dark a silent approach is a threat, and the raid itself is done without words, until the Trojan camp wakes in uproar.
passages:
  - 10.82-85 | Nestor challenges the visitor
  - 10.218-226 | Diomedes volunteers
  - 10.313-320 | Dolon volunteers
---

**A silent approach.** Nestor, lying awake, hears someone coming through the camp and challenges him: “Who is this, coming alone along the ships through the camp through the dark night …? Speak, and do not come at me in silence. What need brings you?” (φθέγγεο, μηδʼ ἀκέων ἐπʼ ἔμʼ ἔρχεο, 10.82–85). At night a man who comes without a word may be an enemy. The chiefs who go round the camp do it aloud: Agamemnon tells his brother to “call out wherever you go” (φθέγγεο, 10.67), and Nestor wakes Odysseus “calling out” (10.138–139; see [[hypnos]]).

**Two silences.** Nestor's call for a man to spy on the Trojans is met with the verse that met Hector's challenge in Book 7 and Agamemnon's proposal to flee in Book 9: “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence” (10.218; see the note on Book 9). Across the plain Hector's call is met with the same verse (10.313), the only time the Iliad uses it of the Trojans alone. On both sides it is the silence of men weighing a dangerous task, and on both sides one man breaks it with the same words, “my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.220, 10.319; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). For the Achaeans it is Diomedes, who broke the silence in Book 7 and twice in Book 9 (7.399, 9.31, 9.696; see [[diomedes]]); for the Trojans it is Dolon.

**Silent work.** The raid is done with few words. The two lie down among the dead to let Dolon pass (10.349–350); Odysseus signals to Diomedes with a whistle (ῥοίζησεν, 10.502), a verb found only here in Homer, though the Cyclops drives his flock to the mountain with the noun, “with much whistling” (*Odyssey* 9.315); Diomedes kills the sleeping Thracians while “an ugly groaning rose from the men struck by the sword” (10.483–484). Hector meanwhile holds his council “away from the din” (νόσφιν ἀπὸ φλοίσβου, 10.416). The silence ends when Apollo wakes Hippocoon, who groans aloud and calls his companion's name, and “a clamor and an untold tumult rose among the Trojans” (10.522–523; see [[tumult]]).
