summary: Both leaders promise a gift to the man who will spy. Nestor offers a black ewe from every chief, and Hector a chariot and the best horses at the Achaean ships, which Dolon turns into a demand for the horses of Achilles. Arms change hands by gift, the boar's-tusk helmet among them, and at the end Nestor guesses that a god gave the horses of Rhesus.

**Two rewards.** Nestor promises the volunteer fame and a gift: “he will have a fine gift” (δόσις ἐσθλή), a black ewe with a lamb at her udder from every chief who commands a ship, “no possession is like it”, and a place at every feast (10.212–217; see [[kleos]] and [[sheep]]). A ewe is a small thing; the worth of the gift is that every chief gives it (see the translation's note on 10.215–217). δόσις, “a giving”, recurs only in the Odyssey's saying that a gift to strangers and beggars is “small and welcome” (*Odyssey* 6.208, 14.58). Hector's offer is bigger and vaguer: “Who would undertake this task for me and accomplish it for a great gift? His reward will be sure”, a chariot and the two best horses at the Achaean ships (δώρῳ ἔπι μεγάλῳ· μισθὸς δέ οἱ ἄρκιος ἔσται, 10.303–306). μισθός is a wage, the pay of hired work: the wage Laomedon refused Poseidon and Apollo (21.444–457), the pittance a spinning woman earns for her children (12.433–435), the two talents of gold for which Aegisthus' watchman kept his year's watch (*Odyssey* 4.524–526). In the Odyssey the suitor Eurymachus mocks the disguised Odysseus by offering him farm work with Hector's half-verse, “your wage will be sure” (*Odyssey* 18.358).

**Great gifts.** Hector promises horses that are not yet his. Dolon makes the offer specific: Hector must swear on his scepter to give him the horses and chariot of Achilles (10.321–323; see [[oath]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). When he tells this to his captors, Odysseus answers with a smile, “Truly your heart was reaching for great gifts” (μεγάλων δώρων, 10.401), the horses no mortal but Achilles can master (10.402–404).

**Gifts that arm.** The two Achaeans set out in arms given for the night. Thrasymedes gives Diomedes a sword and shield and a leather cap, and Meriones gives Odysseus a bow, a quiver, a sword and the boar's-tusk helmet (10.255–261), whose history is told as a chain of gifts: Autolycus “gave” it to Amphidamas, Amphidamas “gave” it to Molus as a guest-gift, Molus “gave” it to Meriones (10.268–270; see [[xenia]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]).

**A god's gift.** The reward that comes home is neither Nestor's nor Hector's. Nestor, seeing the horses of Rhesus, asks whether the two took them from the Trojan throng “or did some god meet you and give them to you?”, and decides that a god did (10.545–553). Odysseus corrects him with a compliment to the gods: “a god, if he wished, could easily give horses even better than these, since the gods are far stronger than we are” (δωρήσαιτʼ, 10.556–557; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). Whether Nestor's ewes were ever given is not told.
