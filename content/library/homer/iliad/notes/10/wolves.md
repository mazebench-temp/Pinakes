ἕσσατο δʼ ἔκτοσθεν ῥινὸν πολιοῖο λύκοιο, “and put on over it the skin of a gray wolf” (10.334). Dolon slings his curved bow about his shoulders and puts the skin over it, sets a cap of ferret skin on his head, and takes a sharp javelin (10.333–335; see [[ferret]] and [[dolon]]). πολιός, “gray”, is the wolf's color here, as it is the color of the sea and of old men's hair. After his death the skin is called λυκέη, “the wolf's”, the adjective standing for the noun, and Odysseus holds it up to Athena with the cap, the bow and the spear before setting the spoils on a tamarisk (10.458–468; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]).

This is the only wolf in the Iliad outside the similes. The chiefs of Book 10 go out into the night in the skins of beasts, lions for Agamemnon and Diomedes and a leopard for Menelaus (10.23–24, 10.29, 10.177–178; see [[lion]] and [[leopard-skin]]), and the Trojan spy in a wolf's. Homer does not call it a disguise; it is his dress, as theirs is. But the wolf of the similes is the enemy of the flock (16.352–355, 22.263; see [[sheep]]), and the Achaean sentries that night keep watch like dogs at the fold (10.183–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). Dolon in his wolf's skin gets nowhere near them. The two Achaeans run him down as two hounds run down a fawn or a hare (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]]).

In the tragedy *Rhesus* the skin is a disguise. Dolon will fasten the wolf's hide on his back, set the beast's gaping jaws over his head, fit his hands to its forefeet and go on all fours along the ditches, a wolf hard for the enemy to find, walking upright only where no one is about (*Rhesus* 208–215). Later in the play the charioteer of Rhesus dreams that wolves have mounted the horses' backs (780–788; see [[the-doloneia]]).
