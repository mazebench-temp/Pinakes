**Her favorite.** Diomedes chooses Odysseus because his heart is eager in every toil, “and Pallas Athena loves him” (10.245; see [[odysseus]] and [[diomedes]]). Odysseus' prayer greets her as “you who always / stand by me in all my toils” (10.278–279), and in the Odyssey she says it of herself in the same words (ἐν πάντεσσι πόνοισι παρίσταμαι, *Odyssey* 13.300–301).

**The heron.** As the two set out, she sends them a sign:

{{quote:0d6200f6-05d1-51cc-84da-b9cf38bded24}}

It comes on the right, the side of good omens (see [[heron]] and [[divination]]). Priam asks Zeus for a bird on the right so that he may see it with his own eyes (24.310–312); in the dark the two spies only hear theirs.

**Two prayers.** Odysseus asks for her love and a return “with glory” (10.278–282). Diomedes asks the “child of Zeus, Atrytone” (10.284; see [[atrytone]]) to go with him as she went with his father to Thebes, when she stood by Tydeus “with a ready heart” (10.285–290; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Between them they share out Diomedes' prayer of Book 5 (5.115–117): Odysseus takes its opening, κλῦθί μευ αἰγιόχοιο Διὸς τέκος (“hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus”), and its plea, φῖλαι (“love me”, 10.280, as 5.117); Diomedes takes its appeal to his father, and adds a vow of a yearling heifer with gold poured around her horns (10.292–294). “So they spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard them” (10.295) is, in the plural, the verse that answers Diomedes in Book 5 (5.121), Odysseus in the footrace (23.771), and Nestor at Pylos after the same vow in the same three verses (*Odyssey* 3.382–385). There the heifer is sacrificed the next morning, with Athena present (*Odyssey* 3.430–463); Book 10 does not tell the sacrifice (see [[prayer]] and [[sacrifice]]).

**Might.** She works through Diomedes. When Dolon nears the sentries, “Athena put might / into the son of Tydeus, so that none of the bronze-armored Achaeans / might boast that he struck him first, and he himself come second” (10.366–368; see [[menos]]). Her care is for his glory: Achilles forbids the Achaeans to shoot at Hector for the same reason, in the same half-verse (ὃ δὲ δεύτερος ἔλθοι, 22.205–207). Among the Thracians “gray-eyed Athena breathed might into him” (10.482), and Rhesus dies while “an evil dream stood over his head / that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena” (10.496–497; see [[metis]] and [[onar]]). Diomedes stands over the sleeper as dreams do (2.20, 23.68), unless a real dream is meant (see the translation's note on 10.494–497); in the Odyssey Athena sends such a dream to Penelope and comes as one to Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 4.795–803, 6.20–21).

**Goddess of the spoil.** When Dolon is dead, Odysseus holds up his arms to her:

{{quote:1e661555-4f48-54ad-92fb-d7ede870b515}}

ληΐτις, from ληΐς (“booty”), occurs only here (see [[of-the-spoil]]); her usual title of the kind is ἀγελείη, “driver of spoil” (4.128, 5.765, 6.269; see [[driver-of-spoil]]). Odysseus sets the spoils on a tamarisk and marks the place (10.465–468), receives them back from Diomedes on the way home (10.526–529), and lays them in a ship's stern “until they could make ready an offering to Athena” (10.570–571; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]). The offering itself is not told.

**The warning.** While Diomedes ponders whether to take Rhesus' chariot or kill more Thracians, Athena “came and stood near”: “Think now of your return … if some other god should wake the Trojans too” (10.507–511). “He knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke” (10.512), as Odysseus knew it in Book 2, in the same verse (2.182); in the dark her voice is enough. The other god is Apollo, who sees “Athena attending the son of Tydeus” (10.516) and wakes the Thracians in resentment at her (see [[apollo]]). The narrator's ἕπουσαν, “attending”, is the active of the verb of Diomedes' prayer, σπεῖό μοι, “go with me” (10.285).

**Thanks.** Nestor guesses that a god gave them the horses, “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both, / and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena” (10.552–553; see [[zeus]]). The last offering of the night is hers: “to Athena, from the full mixing bowl, / they drew honey-sweet wine and poured it out” (10.578–579; see [[libation]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Rhesus*, the tragedy of this night transmitted among Euripides' plays (its authorship is disputed; see [[euripides]]), Athena comes on stage. She turns Odysseus and Diomedes from Hector to Rhesus, describes his white horses, deceives Paris in the likeness of Aphrodite, and sends the two back to the ships (*Rhesus* 595–674). At the end Rhesus' mother, a Muse, blames Athena for his death: Odysseus and the son of Tydeus “did nothing in doing it” (938–940).
