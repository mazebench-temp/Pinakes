summary: The night is a contest of plans. Agamemnon goes to Nestor to “devise some flawless plan” with him, the chiefs call in younger men “to take counsel with them”, Diomedes chooses Odysseus “of many wiles” because a man alone has “thin” counsel, and Rhesus dies “by the design of Athena”.

**A plan devised together.** Agamemnon's first resolve in the sleepless night is to go to Nestor, “in the hope that he might devise with him some flawless plan that would ward off evil from all the Danaans” (σὺν μῆτιν ἀμύμονα τεκτήναιτο, 10.19–20). τεκταίνομαι is the verb of the builder (τέκτων): a plan is made as a joiner makes a thing, and it is made “with” another. Nestor's μῆτις is famous; in Book 7 he “began to weave a plan” (7.324), and in Book 9 the same verses introduced the plan of the embassy (9.93–94). Agamemnon measures the enemy by the same word. He never heard, he says, that one man “devised so many grim deeds in a single day” as Hector (μητίσασθαι, 10.48), and Nestor answers that “Zeus the counselor” will not fulfill all Hector's plans (μητίετα Ζεύς, 10.104; see [[counselor]] and [[noos]]).

**Counsel in pairs.** The book keeps returning to planning done together. The kings call Meriones and Nestor's son to the council “to take counsel with them” (συμμητιάασθαι, 10.197), a compound found nowhere else in Homer. Each side's spy is sent to learn what the enemy “are planning among themselves” (μητιόωσι μετὰ σφίσιν, 10.208, repeated by Odysseus to Dolon at 10.409; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). Diomedes asks for a companion because the man alone, “even if he sees it, his mind is slower, and his counsel thin” (λεπτὴ δέ τε μῆτις, 10.226; see [[when-two-go-together]]). The same half-verse returns once, when Antilochus asks pardon of Menelaus: a young man's mind is quicker, but his counsel thin (23.587–590).

**Many wiles.** The companion Diomedes chooses is the man of μῆτις. Nestor wakes him first, “Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel” (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντον, 10.137), the phrase Helenus used of Hector (7.47; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]), and Odysseus is πολύμητις, “of many wiles”, six times in the book: four times in the verses that introduce his speeches, once as he goes into his hut for his shield (10.148), and once at the height of the killing, as he drags the dead out of the horses' way (10.488; see [[of-many-wiles]]). Nestor calls him πολυμήχανος, “of many devices”, in the same scene (10.144; see [[of-many-devices]]). Dolon, by contrast, runs past the hidden pair “in his thoughtlessness” (10.350).

**The design of Athena.** The last μῆτις of the night is a god's. Rhesus dies “as he lay gasping; for an evil dream stood over his head that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena” (διὰ μῆτιν Ἀθήνης, 10.496–497; see [[onar]] and [[athena]]). The goddess who sends the heron and hears the prayers also plans the killing. In the Odyssey she tells Odysseus that he is the best of mortals in counsel and she is famed among the gods for μῆτις (*Odyssey* 13.297–299; see [[kerdos]]); in this night her design and his wiles serve the same end.
