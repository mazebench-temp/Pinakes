summary: Caught by Odysseus and Diomedes, Dolon begs to be taken alive for ransom in the words of Adrastus, is questioned and given hope, and is killed by Diomedes as he reaches for his chin to beg again (10.377–381, 10.446–457). Like every plea for life on the battlefield of the Iliad, his fails.
grc: [γενείου, ζωγρεῖτʼ]
en: [Take me alive, take him by the chin]

**The plea.** When the two catch Dolon they “seized his hands” (10.377), and the hands of the suppliant, which should reach for the knees of the man he begs, are held. He bursts into tears and offers a ransom:

{{quote:4f3f31e2-ee9a-523a-bae9-dd9ce1bd8d8d}}

The last three verses are Adrastus' plea to Menelaus, with “you” in the plural (6.48–50), and the sons of Antimachus will repeat them to Agamemnon (11.133–135; see [[apoina]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]). ζωγρεῖτʼ, “take me alive”, is Adrastus' ζώγρει in the plural, for two captors (6.46).

**Hope.** Odysseus answers, “Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383), and questions him. The words promise nothing. The phrase returns once in the Iliad, when Zeus watches Hector put on the armor of Achilles: “Ah, wretch, death is not on your mind at all, and it is coming close to you” (17.201–202). Dolon tells all he is asked and more, and at the end asks to be taken to the ships, or left bound until his word has been tested (10.442–445).

**The refusal.** Diomedes, “looking at him darkly” (see [[looking-darkly]]), gives a soldier's reason: “if we take ransom and release you now, or let you go, surely later too you will come to the swift ships of the Achaeans, either to spy, or to fight face to face” (10.449–451). Agamemnon refused Adrastus for the wrong done in Menelaus' house (6.55–60), and Achilles will refuse Lycaon for the death of Patroclus (21.99–113). Diomedes refuses a spy.

**Too late.** Dolon makes the gesture only at the end:

{{quote:6875253e-0325-5834-9dd7-dc780f9ab8cf}}

The chin is touched after the knees: Thetis clasped Zeus' knees and took him beneath the chin (1.500–501; 8.371). Dolon is killed before he can touch it, and his head falls into the dust “while he was still speaking” (see [[aristotle-the-speaking-head]]). The Odyssey ends the plea of the seer Leodes, who clasps Odysseus' knees among the suitors, with the same two verses: Odysseus refuses him “looking at him darkly” and strikes him in the middle of the neck with a sword, and his head is mingled with the dust while he is still speaking (Od. 22.310–329).

**Every plea refused.** No man who begs for his life in the fighting of the Iliad is spared (6.37–65, 11.130–147, 20.463–472, 21.64–135). Dolon's death has most in common with Lycaon's. In both scenes a spear flies over the man and sticks in the ground (10.372–374; 21.67–70), the man begs, and he is killed with a sword, a two-edged one for Lycaon, like the one Thrasymedes lent Diomedes (21.116–118; 10.256). Diomedes' slaughter of the Thracians that follows is told in the verses of Achilles' slaughter in the river just before Lycaon comes to him (10.483–484 ≈ 21.20–21; see [[sword]]). Readers have judged the killing of Dolon in different ways: as the hard logic of a night raid, in which a spy let go is a spy again; as a breach of the suppliant's claim; or as the darkest case of the pattern the poem keeps, in which no plea in battle is granted (see [[dolon]] and [[diomedes]]).
