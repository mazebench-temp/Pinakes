Sleep is named in some twenty-five lines of Book 10, more than in any other book of the Iliad, and almost always as something absent, lost or dangerous.

**One awake.** The book begins in the shape of the opening of Book 2: all the others asleep, one awake.

{{quote:dcd22b52-4b02-5038-98b5-9b7c0d8635f4}}

Its second verse returns word for word on the night when Hermes brings Priam out of Achilles' camp (10.2 = 24.678). In Book 2 the one awake was Zeus, planning how to honor Achilles at the Achaeans' cost (2.1–4); now it is the king, who lies awake because the plan has worked. The Dream once told him that “a man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through” (2.24), and now he does not (see [[a-man-of-counsel-should-not-sleep]]). His brother is awake too, “for neither on his eyelids would sleep settle” (10.25–26), and Agamemnon tells Nestor that he wanders because “sweet sleep does not settle on my eyes” (10.91–92; see [[sweet-sleep]]). Nestor, in his soft bed, is awake as well: “since sleep does not come to you either”, Agamemnon says to him (10.75, 10.96).

**Sleep as a danger.** What Agamemnon fears is that the sentries, “worn out by weariness and by sleep”, have lain down to rest and forgotten their watch (10.98–99; see [[night-watch]]). The phrase καμάτῳ ἀδηκότες, “worn out by weariness”, occurs four times in this book and once in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 12.281), and in Book 10 it moves from fear to fact: the Achaeans fear it for their sentries (10.98), Hector hopes it of the Achaeans (10.312, repeated by Dolon, 10.399), and it is true of the Thracians (10.471). The sentries are awake after all, and the simile of the dogs round the fold says what it costs them: “sleep is lost to them” (ἀπό τέ σφισιν ὕπνος ὄλωλεν, 10.186), and “so sweet sleep was lost from their eyelids” (10.187), with the verb of perishing. Nestor's charge is “let no one be taken by sleep, so that we do not become a joy to our enemies” (10.192–193). Across the plain Hector keeps his own men awake: “Nor did Hector let the proud Trojans sleep” (10.299–300).

**The sleeper woken.** Diomedes is the one chief found asleep, outside his hut among his sleeping companions, their spears planted upright (10.150–156; see [[spear]]). Nestor stirs him with his heel and gives him the Dream's rebuke in his own words: “Wake up, son of Tydeus! Why do you sleep the whole night through?” (τί πάννυχον ὕπνον ἀωτεῖς, 10.159). The verb ἀωτέω occurs elsewhere only when Odysseus wakes his men on Circe's island, “no longer sleep sweet sleep” (*Odyssey* 10.548). Diomedes “sprang up out of sleep very quickly” (10.162), in the words that will be used of Hippocoon at the end of the night (10.519).

**The sleep of the enemy.** In the second half of the book sleep changes sides. Odysseus asks Dolon about “the watches and the sleeping places” of the Trojans (10.408), and learns that the Trojans keep watch at their fires while the allies sleep, since their wives and children are far away (10.418–422; see [[trojan-allies]]). The Thracians “were sleeping, worn out by weariness”, and Rhesus “was sleeping in the middle” (10.471, 10.474). Diomedes kills twelve of them and then Rhesus, “as he lay gasping; for an evil dream stood over his head that night” (10.495–497; see [[onar]] and [[rhesus]]). Only a god breaks the sleep: Apollo, who “did not keep a blind watch”, wakes Hippocoon, who “started up from sleep” to find the horses gone (10.515–522; see [[apollo]] and [[hippocoon]]), as Athena had feared that “some other god should wake the Trojans” (10.511).

The book does not end, as Books 7 and 9 do, with the army taking “the gift of sleep” (7.482, 9.713). The two men bathe, eat and pour wine to Athena (10.572–579), and Book 11 begins with the dawn (11.1–2). See [[war-and-night]].
