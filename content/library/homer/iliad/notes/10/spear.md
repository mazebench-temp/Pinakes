summary: The spears of the night stand upright on their butt-spikes beside sleeping men and shine like lightning (10.152–154). Diomedes throws his over Dolon's shoulder on purpose, to stop him (10.372–374), and Dolon's javelin is taken from his body as “the long spear”.
grc: [σαυρωτῆρος, ἄκοντα, δουρηνεκὲς]
en: [spears, butt-spikes, javelin, spear's throw]
---

**Upright in the ground.** Four of the night's armings end with taking up a spear, Agamemnon's, Menelaus', Nestor's and Diomedes' (10.24, 10.31, 10.135, 10.178; see [[arming-scene]]), and Dolon's with a javelin (10.335). Diomedes' companions sleep with their spears “driven upright on their butt-spikes, and far off the bronze shone like the lightning of father Zeus” (10.152–154; see [[like-lightning]]). σαυρωτήρ, the butt-spike, is named only here; elsewhere the butt is the οὐρίαχος (13.443, 16.612). Critics found fault with the custom, and Aristotle answered that it was the custom of the time, “as the Illyrians still do” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a; see [[aristotle-upright-spears]]).

**Thrown wide.** When the spies have run Dolon down, Diomedes throws, “and missed the man on purpose; over his right shoulder the point of the polished spear stuck fast in the ground” (10.372–374; see [[polished]]). The spear is cast to stop a man, not to kill him. When Achilles casts at Lycaon, the suppliant ducks under it and the spear stands in the ground over his back (21.67–70; see [[supplication]]).

**Dolon's javelin.** The Trojan spy takes “a sharp javelin” (ὀξὺν ἄκοντα, 10.335), and when he is stripped it is “the long spear” (δόρυ μακρόν, 10.459; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). The spear also measures a distance: Dolon knows his pursuers for enemies “when they were a spear's throw away, or even less” (δουρηνεκές, 10.357), a word found only here.
