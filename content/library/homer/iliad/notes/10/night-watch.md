summary: Book 10 is the night of the watch. Agamemnon fears that his sentries have fallen asleep, and Nestor finds them awake in their arms like dogs round a fold; Dolon tells his captors that the Trojans keep watch only at their own fires, while the allies sleep and the Thracians lie unguarded (10.96–101, 10.180–193, 10.408–422, 10.471–475).

**The sacred company.** The sentries who went out at the start of the night (9.80–88) are the king's first concern. He will ask Nestor “to go to the sacred company of the sentries and give them orders”, since Nestor's son and Meriones command them: “to them above all we entrusted it” (10.56–59; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[meriones]]). τέλος is a body of men, as in “the company of the Thracian men” (10.470); whether ἱερός here means “sacred”, the watch being in the gods' keeping, or “strong” is disputed (see the translation's note on 10.56). Menelaus is to bid every man stay awake as he goes (10.67), the order of the watch in Priam's mouth and Hector's (7.371, 18.299).

**The fear.** Agamemnon's fear is practical:

{{quote:7aec518f-2271-5fee-8b6e-6cb0562a8a1e}}

His last words answer Hector's, who kept his fires burning “in case even by night the long-haired Achaeans set out to flee” (μή πως καὶ διὰ νύκτα, 8.510). καμάτῳ ἀδηκότες, “worn out by weariness”, returns like a refrain. Hector sends his spy to learn whether the ships “are guarded as before”, or whether the beaten Achaeans are unwilling “to keep watch through the night, worn out by dreadful weariness” (10.308–312), and Dolon repeats the question to his captors (10.396–399; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). At the end of the night it is the Thracians who sleep “worn out by weariness” (10.471).

**The rounds.** The chiefs are to meet “before the gates among the sentries” (10.126–127; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The leaders of the sentries are not asleep, “but they were all sitting awake with their arms”, like dogs that keep a hard watch over a fold when they hear a beast in the woods, always turned toward the plain to hear the Trojans coming (10.180–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). Nestor is glad, and speaks to them as a father: “Keep watch like this now, dear children, and let no one be taken by sleep, so that we do not become a joy to our enemies” (10.192–193; see [[nestor]] and [[hypnos]]). The watch does its work. When Dolon, chased by Odysseus and Diomedes, is “just about to fall in with the sentries”, Diomedes stops him (10.365–371; see [[dolon]]).

**The Trojan watch.** Odysseus asks Dolon “how are the watches and the sleeping places of the other Trojans” (10.408), and Dolon answers:

{{quote:0f3070c0-f36b-5e1d-bd2d-dd16e221769e}}

The Trojans watch because their wives and children are in the city behind them; the allies, whose families are far away, sleep (see [[trojans]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Hector had asked for more: fires all night on the plain, and in the city boys and old men on the towers and “a steadfast watch” against ambush (8.508–522; see [[ambush]]).

**No watch at all.** The Thracians, newly come and camped apart at the end of the line, keep no watch. They sleep in three rows with their armor beside them and a yoke of horses by each man, with Rhesus in the middle (10.434, 10.471–475; see [[thracians]] and [[rhesus]]), and Diomedes kills twelve men and the king before anyone wakes. On the Trojan side the only watcher is a god: “Nor did Apollo, god of the silver bow, keep a blind watch”, and he wakes Hippocoon, who finds the place where the horses stood empty (10.515–522; see [[apollo]] and [[hippocoon]]). The phrase οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε is used only of gods, of Poseidon twice (13.10, 14.135) and in the Odyssey of Ares (Od. 8.285).

**Outside Homer.** The *Rhesus*, the tragedy transmitted among Euripides' plays and set in this same night, has a chorus of Trojan “sentries of the army” (φύλακες στρατιᾶς, 15), who keep “the watch of the night in four parts” (τετράμοιρον νυκτὸς φυλακήν, 5) and come to wake Hector in his bed (1–10; see [[euripides]]).
