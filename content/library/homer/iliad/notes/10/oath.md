**The oath asked.** Dolon volunteers on a condition: “But come, hold up your scepter for me, and swear to me that you will give me the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze that carry the noble son of Peleus” (10.321–323). He asks for the gesture as well as the words, an oath on the raised scepter (see [[scepter]]). Achilles swore his great oath “by this scepter” (1.233–239), and Agamemnon lifted his scepter to all the gods when he granted the truce for the dead (7.411–412).

**The oath sworn.**

{{quote:eb8ca461-10ef-5e52-b297-7e7e43eaa2a4}}

ἴστω, “let him be witness”, is the word of oaths. Agamemnon granted the truce with “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (7.411), with the same titles for the god, and he will swear that he never touched Briseis with “Now let Zeus be witness first” (19.258). What Hector swears to is not his to give: the horses belong to Achilles, and they are immortal (see [[horses]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

**False.** The narrator's verdict is ἐπίορκον ἐπώμοσε (10.332). ἐπίορκος is elsewhere the false oath that the powers below the earth punish, in the prayer at the oath-sacrifice of Book 3 and in Agamemnon's oath of Book 19 (3.278–279; 19.259–260). The narrator does not say that Hector meant to deceive. The oath is false because it will not come true: Dolon sets out at once, “but he was not to come back from the ships and bring word to Hector” (10.336–337). In Book 17, when Hector himself is chasing the horses, Zeus says that Hector “shall not ride behind” them (17.448–449), in the verb of the oath, ἐποχήσεται, which Homer uses nowhere else (10.330; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

**A nod.** Dolon, captured, tells the oath as a nod: Hector “nodded his assent to give me” the horses and the chariot, and led him astray “with many blind follies” (10.391–393; see [[ate]]). κατανεύω, “nod assent”, is the gesture of a binding promise, and Zeus' nod is the greatest pledge among the gods (1.524–527; see [[neuo]]). The words recur once, of another Trojan promise. Othryoneus, newly come to Troy, asked Priam for Cassandra without a bride-price and promised to drive the Achaeans from Troy, and Priam “promised him and nodded his assent / to give her” (13.365–369); trusting the promise, he fought and was killed by Idomeneus. Like Dolon, he dies for a prize that was promised with a nod.
