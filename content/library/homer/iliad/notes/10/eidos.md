---
summary: Dolon is introduced as “ill-favored to look at, but swift-footed”: the Iliad's usual weighing of looks against worth, turned the other way. Aristotle took the phrase of an ugly face, not a misshapen body.
passages:
  - 10.314-317 | Dolon
---

The narrator introduces Hector's volunteer with four facts: he is the son of the divine herald Eumedes, rich in gold and bronze, the only son among five sisters, and “he was ill-favored to look at, but swift-footed” (ὃς δή τοι εἶδος μὲν ἔην κακός, ἀλλὰ ποδώκης, 10.316; see [[dolon]]). The verse weighs looks against another gift, as the poem often does, but in reverse. Nireus was the handsomest man who came to Ilios after Achilles, “but he was a weakling” (2.673–675), and Hector taunts Paris that the Achaeans took him for a champion for his looks, “but there is no strength in his heart” (3.44–45); Dolon's looks are poor and his feet are fast. In the Odyssey Odysseus makes the same balance a rule: one man is plainer in looks, but a god crowns his words with beauty (*Odyssey* 8.169–170; see the article above).

ποδώκης, “swift-footed”, is used of no other man in the Iliad but Achilles (2.860, 8.474 and often), besides fast horses (2.764, 23.376; see [[swift-footed]]), and the swift-footed spy asks for Achilles' horses as his reward (10.322–323). The poem's other Trojan scout, Polites, kept watch from a tomb “trusting in his speed of foot” (2.792; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). Dolon's speed takes him past the hidden Achaeans and almost to the ships, but the two cut him off from his own lines and run him down (10.346–371).

**Outside Homer.** Aristotle, answering objections to Homer's language, explains εἶδος … κακός as an ugly face, “not a misshapen body, for the Cretans call a handsome face εὐειδές” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a; see [[aristotle-dolons-face]]).
