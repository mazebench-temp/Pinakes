summary: Odysseus is the eyes and the voice of the night raid. Woken by Nestor's call, he is the companion Diomedes chooses because Athena loves him; wearing the helmet his grandfather once stole, he sees Dolon first, lays the ambush, questions him with a smile, offers his spoils to Athena, shows Diomedes the sleeping Rhesus, and drives the Thracian horses home with his bow.

**Awake at a word.** Nestor wakes him first, “the equal of Zeus in counsel” (10.137; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]), by calling out, “and at once the cry came around his mind” (10.139). He comes out of his hut and asks what need has brought them “through the ambrosial night” (10.141–142). Nestor answers with the full verse of his titles, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (10.144; see [[son-of-laertes]]), and he takes up his shield and follows (10.148–149). Diomedes, a few verses later, needs the old man's heel (10.158; see [[diomedes]]).

**Chosen.** “And enduring Odysseus was willing to slip into the throng / of the Trojans; for the heart in his breast was always daring” (10.231–232). τλήμων, “enduring”, can also mean “daring”, and the next verse leans that way (see the translation's note on 10.231 and [[enduring]]). Diomedes chooses him with a verse that Zeus speaks of him to Athena at the first council of the gods in the Odyssey, “how then could I forget divine Odysseus” (10.243 = *Odyssey* 1.65), because Athena loves him and “he is wise beyond all others” (10.245–247; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Odysseus cuts the praise short, “for you are saying this among Argives who know it”, and turns to the stars: two parts of the night are gone (10.249–253; see [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]] and [[war-and-night]]).

**His grandfather's helmet.** Meriones gives him a bow, a quiver, a sword and the leather helmet set with a boar's white tusks, and the narrator tells its history: Autolycus stole it from Amyntor's house at Eleon, and it passed by gift to Amphidamas, Molus and Meriones, “and now it covered the head of Odysseus” (10.260–271; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). The Iliad does not say that Autolycus was Odysseus' grandfather; the Odyssey does (*Odyssey* 19.394–412; see [[autolycus]]). The bow is the only one the Iliad gives him, and he never shoots it; he drives the horses with it (10.500, 10.513–514). In the Odyssey he says that at Troy only Philoctetes was a better archer (*Odyssey* 8.219–220; see [[bow]]).

**The heron.** Athena sends a heron on the right, heard but not seen, and “Odysseus rejoiced at the bird” (10.274–277; see [[heron]]). He prays to her as the goddess “who always / stand by me in all my toils”, as she says of herself in the Odyssey (10.278–279; *Odyssey* 13.300–301), and asks to come back “with glory, having done a great deed that the Trojans will rue” (10.281–282; see [[athena]] and [[prayer]]).

**Dolon.** He sees Dolon first, guesses that he is a spy or a stripper of corpses, and lays the ambush: let him pass, then cut him off and drive him toward the ships (10.339–348; see [[ambush]]). When Diomedes' spear has stopped him and Dolon offers ransom, it is Odysseus who answers: “Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383). He promises nothing. His questions come in the Odyssey's verse of inquiry, “But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account” (10.384, 10.405), found thirteen times in the Odyssey and elsewhere in the Iliad only in Book 24 (24.380, 24.656). The same verse brings in his answer to the suppliant Medon in the Odyssey, whom he spares (*Odyssey* 22.371). Dolon he leaves to Diomedes. He asks him, in Nestor's own verses, what Nestor wanted to know (10.409–411 = 10.208–210), and Dolon tells him that and more, down to the Thracian king and his horses (10.413–445; see [[dolon]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**Spoils for Athena.** He holds up Dolon's cap, wolfskin, bow and spear to “Athena, goddess of the spoil”, and asks her to guide them to the Thracians (10.458–464; see [[of-the-spoil]]). He sets the spoils on a tamarisk and marks the place with reeds and branches, so that they will find it in the dark (10.465–468; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]).

**The horses.** Among the sleeping Thracians he sees Rhesus first and gives out the work: Diomedes may untie the horses, “or else you kill the men, and the horses will be my care” (10.476–481). While Diomedes kills, Odysseus drags each body aside by the foot, “with this thought in his heart: that the fine-maned horses / might pass through easily” (10.488–493). He unties the team and drives it out with his bow, “since he had not thought of the shining whip” (10.498–501), νοήσατο, the verb of Diomedes' praise, περίοιδε νοῆσαι (10.247). Then he whistles to Diomedes (ῥοίζησεν, 10.502), a verb found nowhere else in Homer (see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).

**The return.** Nestor greets him as Agamemnon greeted him after the embassy, “much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans” (10.544 = 9.673; see [[much-praised]] and [[nestor]]). Then he brought back a refusal; now he brings horses “like the rays of the sun” (10.547). A god could give better horses, he answers, “since the gods are far stronger than we are” (10.556–557; see [[gods-and-mortals]]); he gives the killing to Diomedes and counts Dolon “a thirteenth” (10.558–563; see the translation's note on 10.559–561). He drives the horses across the ditch “laughing aloud” (καγχαλόων, 10.565), and sets Dolon's spoils in the stern of a ship “until they could make ready an offering to Athena” (10.570–571); the poem does not say whose ship.

**Near the Odyssey.** No book of the Iliad brings him closer to the hero of the Odyssey: the night, the stealth, the questioning, the gods' favor, the helmet from Autolycus' house, and the verses shared with the Odyssey (10.243, 10.400, 10.457; see [[the-doloneia]]). Helen remembers in the Odyssey how he went into Troy disguised as a beggar, killed many Trojans and brought back much knowledge (*Odyssey* 4.242–258), and in the beggar's tale he leads a night ambush under Troy and wins a freezing comrade a cloak by a trick (*Odyssey* 14.468–502).
