**One sees before the other.** Diomedes asks for a companion because two see more than one: “When two go together, one sees before the other / how the advantage lies” (10.224–225; see [[when-two-go-together]]). The one who sees first is Odysseus. He sees Dolon coming and plans the ambush (10.339–348), and he sees the sleeping Rhesus and points him out (10.476–477). Diomedes chose him for his mind, “since he is wise beyond all others” (10.247), and Odysseus does the talking of the night: he questions Dolon, prays over the spoils, and answers Nestor (10.382–445, 10.460–464, 10.555–563; see [[odysseus]]).

**The work divided.** Diomedes does the killing: Dolon, cut down as he begs (10.454–457), and the twelve Thracians and their king (10.482–497; see [[diomedes]]). Odysseus gives out the parts himself:

{{quote:4a7c9dbf-f7a7-5302-a623-9dc3cb83d246}}

He takes the horses. Behind Diomedes he drags each dead man aside by the foot, so that the horses, not yet used to corpses, will not shy at treading on them; then he unties the team, drives it out of the throng with his bow, and whistles to Diomedes (10.488–502; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). Diomedes lingers, pondering “what was the most shameless thing he could do”, until Athena sends him off (10.503–511; see [[athena]]). On the way back, at the place where they killed Dolon, Odysseus holds in the horses and Diomedes leaps down to put the bloody spoils into his hands (10.526–529).

**Together.** The verbs of the night are duals: the two put on their armor (ἐδύτην, 10.254, 10.272), set out and leave the others behind (λιπέτην, 10.273), lie down among the dead (κλινθήτην, 10.350), run after Dolon (ἐπεδραμέτην, 10.354), catch him and seize his hands (κιχήτην, ἁψάσθην, 10.376–377), and at the end sit down to their meal (ἐφιζανέτην, 10.578). The similes make them a pair of beasts, two lions in the black night and two hounds after a fawn or a hare (10.297, 10.360–364; see [[two-lions]] and [[two-hounds]]). Odysseus' report gives the killing of the king and his twelve companions to “brave Diomedes” (10.559), and Nestor's welcome takes them together: “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both, / and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena” (10.552–553). They wash in the sea, bathe, anoint themselves, and pour wine to Athena (10.572–579; see [[libation]] and [[the-doloneia]]).
