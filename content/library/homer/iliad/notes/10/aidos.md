**Respect overruled.** Many chiefs offer to go with Diomedes, Menelaus among them (10.227–232). Agamemnon gives him the choice, with a warning:

{{quote:83fcabd8-ca6a-5750-9e3c-f2a242b79298}}

αἰδώς is the regard owed to those whose standing matters, and here it is the deference that birth and rank command: Diomedes might take “a worse one with you, giving way to respect, looking to his lineage” (see [[genee]] and [[basileus]]). Agamemnon tells him to set it aside and take “the best” (see [[aristos]]). The narrator adds the reason in a single verse: “So he spoke, and he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.240; see [[deos]] and [[menelaus]]). The rule of merit is spoken out of a brother's fear. The construction “do not, out of respect …” (μηδὲ … αἰδόμενος) recurs in the Odyssey, when Telemachus asks Nestor and then Menelaus not to soften the truth about his father “out of respect or pity” (*Odyssey* 3.96, 4.326). αἰδοῖ εἴκων, “giving way to respect”, matches Agamemnon's defense of Menelaus a hundred lines earlier, “not giving way to sloth” (ὄκνῳ εἴκων, 10.122): respect, like sloth, is a thing a man can yield to.

**Respected and rebuked.** Nestor speaks of Menelaus with both feelings at once: “Menelaus, dear and respected though he is, I will rebuke” (φίλον περ ἐόντα καὶ αἰδοῖον, 10.114–115; see [[neikos]]). αἰδοῖος, “one to be respected”, is the word for those who command reverence, elders, hosts and guests, and the same pair, φίλος and αἰδοῖος, describes a guest-friend in the tale the disguised Odysseus tells Penelope (*Odyssey* 19.191). The old man's respect does not prevent the rebuke, and Agamemnon answers it by defending his brother (10.120–127; see [[praise-and-blame]]).

**Respect withheld.** The suppliant's gestures appeal to αἰδώς, and Dolon makes them. He weeps, offers ransom, and at the end, as he is about to take Diomedes by the chin, is killed in mid-word (10.377–381, 10.454–457; see [[supplication]] and [[apoina]]). The word is not used; the refusal is.
