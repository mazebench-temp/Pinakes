summary: Agamemnon, who “ruled mightily over all the Argives, and was honored by the people like a god”, spends the night toiling like any man and tells his brother to honor every chief by name. The reward Nestor offers the spy is honor in a small gift and a place at every feast.
passages:
  - 10.32-35 | Menelaus finds his brother arming
  - 10.211-217 | Nestor's reward
---

**Honored like a god.** When Menelaus goes to wake his brother, the narrator gives Agamemnon his full standing: he “ruled mightily over all the Argives, and was honored by the people like a god” (θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ, 10.32–33). The half-verse is used of the priest Dolopion (5.78), of Thoas, who ruled the Aetolians (13.216–218), and of the priest Onetor (16.604–605); only here is it said of the king of all the Achaeans. It comes at the point of his lowest fortune, on the night after his defeat, as he arms to go round the camp. His instructions to Menelaus follow: wake the chiefs by name, “giving glory to them all; and do not be proud in your heart, but let us toil ourselves as well” (10.69–70; see [[kydos]] and [[ponos]]). The honor of the king does not exempt him from the night's work.

**Honor in a gift.** Nestor's reward for the spy is a black ewe with a lamb from every chief, “no possession is like it” (10.215–216), “and he will always have a place at feasts and banquets” (10.217). The value of the ewe lies in the honor of a gift from all the chiefs, and the place at feasts is honor of the kind Hector once mocked Diomedes for losing: “the Danaans of the swift horses honored you above others with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (8.161–162). Sarpedon names the same honors as the reason why the Lycian kings must fight in the front (12.310–321; see [[doron]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).
