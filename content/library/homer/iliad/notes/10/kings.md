summary: The kings of Book 10 are the chiefs roused in the night and called to a council beyond the ditch, with two captains of the watch beside them (10.166, 10.194–197). Agamemnon tells Diomedes not to choose his companion by rank, “not even if he is more kingly” (10.239).

**Roused.** Nestor goes from hut to hut waking the chiefs himself, and Diomedes protests that “other, younger sons of the Achaeans” could “wake each one of the kings” (10.165–167; see [[nestor]] and [[diomedes]]). Agamemnon has sent Menelaus to call them, naming each man by his father and “giving glory to them all” (10.67–69; see [[menelaus]]).

**The council in the field.** “The kings of the Argives, all who had been called to the council” follow Nestor across the ditch, and with them two who are not kings, Meriones and Nestor's son, the captains of the watch, “for the kings themselves had called them to take counsel with them” (10.194–197; see [[meriones]], [[thrasymedes]] and [[boule]]). συμμητιάασθαι, “to take counsel together”, occurs only here. They sit on open ground, clear of corpses, where Hector turned back at nightfall (10.198–202; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Their councils earlier in the poem were held among the ships, at Nestor's ship in Book 2 and in Agamemnon's hut earlier this night (2.53–54, 9.89–90; see [[elders]]). Nestor's reward for the volunteer is to come from the kings: each of “all the best men who hold command over the ships” will give a black ewe with her lamb (10.214–216; see [[sheep]]). When the spies come back he greets the same company as “leaders and rulers of the Argives” (10.533; see [[hegemon]]).

**More kingly.** When many volunteer, Agamemnon tells Diomedes to take the best man, not to give way to respect and choose a worse one, “looking to his lineage, not even if he is more kingly” (10.237–239; see [[aidos]] and [[agamemnon]]). The night before, βασιλεύτερος, “more kingly”, was the word of his demand that Achilles submit, and Achilles threw it back at him (9.160, 9.392; see [[basileus]]). Now it names a claim that must give way to merit, and the narrator gives the king's reason: “he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.240).

**The Thracian king.** βασιλῆα at 10.494 is Rhesus, the king of the Thracians (Ῥῆσος βασιλεύς, 10.435), whom Diomedes kills as the thirteenth (see [[rhesus]] and [[thracians]]).
