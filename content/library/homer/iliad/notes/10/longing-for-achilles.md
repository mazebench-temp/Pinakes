---
summary: Achilles is absent from Book 10, and the night measures the army's need of him. Nestor hopes that Hector will have worse sorrows if Achilles turns his heart from its anger (10.106–107), and he says of the night's crisis that “a need has come that is no longer bearable” (10.118), the words in which Achilles, the next day, imagines the Achaeans begging at his knees (11.609–610).
passages:
  - 10.103-118 | Nestor's answer to Agamemnon
---

**Nestor's hope.** When Agamemnon comes to him in the night, Nestor answers his fear with a hope: Zeus will not fulfill all of Hector's plans, “but I think he will labor / under sorrows still greater, if Achilles / turns his dear heart from its grievous anger” (10.105–107; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). No other Achaean speaks of Achilles' anger that night. The embassy has failed, and the hope is Nestor's, not a plan.

**The need.** Nestor goes on to blame Menelaus for sleeping: he should be toiling among all the chiefs, “beseeching them; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (λισσόμενος· χρειὼ γὰρ ἱκάνεται οὐκέτʼ ἀνεκτός, 10.117–118). The next day, watching the rout from the stern of his ship, Achilles calls Patroclus and says: “Now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees begging; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (λισσομένους· χρειὼ γὰρ ἱκάνεται οὐκέτʼ ἀνεκτός, 11.609–610). The verse is the same but for one word. In Nestor's mouth the need sends a king to beg his fellow chiefs; in Achilles' it brings the army to beg him. Achilles had sworn that the longing for him would come when many fell at Hector's hands (1.240–244); Book 10 is the night when the Achaeans feel the need and do not yet ask him again (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**His horses.** What the Trojans want of Achilles is his team. Hector promises his spy the best horses at the ships, and Dolon asks for the horses and the chariot “that carry the noble son of Peleus” (10.305–306, 10.322–323). In the Catalogue the Myrmidons' horses stood idle beside their chariots, munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley, while their master nursed his wrath (2.771–777). Odysseus tells Dolon that Achilles' horses are hard for any mortal to master, “for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore” (10.402–404; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[achilles-withdraws]]). The night's prize is another king's horses, and the need remains (see [[the-doloneia]]).
