---
summary: Menelaus cannot sleep for fear for the Argives who came to Troy for his sake. He puts on a leopard's skin, goes to his brother and is sent to call Ajax and Idomeneus; Nestor blames him for sleeping and Agamemnon defends him, and when he volunteers for the raid his brother takes care that he is not chosen (10.25–72, 10.114–130, 10.230–240).

**Sleepless for the Argives.** “And trembling held Menelaus likewise”: the verse takes up Agamemnon's trembling (τρομέοντο, 10.10; τρόμος, 10.25). His reason is his own. He fears for “the Argives, who for his sake had come over the wide waters to Troy” (10.25–28; see [[argives]]). He said as much when he accepted Paris' challenge, “since you have suffered many evils for the sake of my quarrel” (3.99–100), and the twin sons of Diocles died at Troy “seeking satisfaction for the sons of Atreus” (5.552; see [[atreidae]]). He covers his back with a dappled leopard's skin, as Paris did when he stepped out as champion of the Trojans (10.29–30; 3.17; see [[leopard-skin]] and [[paris]]), sets a bronze helmet on his head, and takes a spear (10.29–31).

**With his brother.** He finds Agamemnon arming by his ship, “and his coming was welcome to him” (10.35). He speaks first, and guesses the plan: “Why are you arming like this, dear brother? Will you urge one of the companions to go and spy on the Trojans?” No one will undertake it, he fears; “he would have to be very bold of heart” (10.37–41; see [[dear-brother]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). In Book 2 he came to his brother's sacrifice “unbidden, for he knew in his heart how his brother was laboring” (2.408–409). Here too he comes unasked, and he is given a task: to run along the ships and call Ajax and Idomeneus (10.53–54; see [[ajax]] and [[idomeneus]]). He asks the practical question, whether to wait with them or come back, and is told to stay, to call out to each man by his father's name and lineage, and not to be proud (10.61–71; see [[agamemnon]]).

**Blamed and defended.** Nestor, who does not know that Menelaus is already out, says that he will rebuke him, “dear and respected though he is”, for sleeping and leaving the toil to his brother (10.114–118; see [[nestor]]). Agamemnon answers for him: Nestor might well blame him at other times, for he often hangs back, though not from sloth or thoughtlessness, but because he waits for his brother to lead; tonight he woke first (10.120–125).

**The volunteer.** When Diomedes asks for a companion, “the son of Atreus was willing, Menelaus famed for his spear” (10.230; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]). Agamemnon tells Diomedes to choose the best man, “not even if he is more kingly”, and the narrator gives the reason: “he feared for fair-haired Menelaus” (10.239–240; see [[fair-haired]]). In Book 7 Agamemnon held him back from fighting Hector, “since he was far the mightier” (7.104–119). This time the king does not name him, and Diomedes chooses Odysseus. Menelaus is not mentioned again in the book.
