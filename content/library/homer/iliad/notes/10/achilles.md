---
summary: Achilles is absent from the night of Book 10, but the book keeps him in view. Nestor hopes that he may yet turn from his anger, and the Trojans covet his immortal horses, which Dolon asks for as the price of his spying and which, Odysseus says, no mortal but Achilles can master.
passages:
  - 10.104-107 | Nestor's hope
  - 10.400-404 | Odysseus on the horses of Achilles
---

**Absent.** The night goes on from his refusal of the embassy (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Nestor answers Agamemnon's fear of Hector with a hope: Zeus will not fulfill all Hector's plans, and Hector “will labor / under sorrows still greater, if Achilles / turns his dear heart from its grievous anger” (10.105–107; see [[longing-for-achilles]] and [[hector]]). It is the only time he is named in the Achaean councils of the night.

**His horses.** The Trojans name him more often, for his horses. Dolon asks for “the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze / that carry the noble son of Peleus” (10.322–323), and, captured, says that Hector promised him “the single-hoofed horses of the noble son of Peleus” (10.392; see [[dolon]] and [[son-of-peleus]]). Odysseus answers with Achilles' descent:

{{quote:1be7fc5b-bf47-54bb-97af-a9d3e4586cb0}}

The horses are the gods' gift to his father, and only the son of a goddess can drive them (see [[the-horses-of-achilles]], [[grandson-of-aeacus]] and [[thetis]]). Apollo will say the same verses to Hector (17.76–78).

**Swift-footed.** The Trojan who wants the horses is himself ποδώκης, “swift-footed” (10.316), the epithet of Achilles in the Catalogue and in Zeus' prophecy (2.860, 8.474; see [[swift-footed]]).
