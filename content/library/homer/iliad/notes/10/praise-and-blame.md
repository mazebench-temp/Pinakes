---
summary: In the night the chiefs praise and rebuke one another without anger. Nestor means to rebuke Menelaus and is answered by Agamemnon's defense of him; he rebukes Diomedes and is called a hard man in return; Diomedes praises Odysseus, who refuses praise and blame alike, and is greeted on his return as “much-praised”.
passages:
  - 10.114-130 | Nestor and Agamemnon on Menelaus
  - 10.157-176 | Nestor wakes Diomedes
  - 10.241-253 | Diomedes chooses Odysseus
---

**Blame answered.** Nestor tells Agamemnon that he will rebuke Menelaus, even at the risk of the king's displeasure, for sleeping while his brother toils (10.114–118; see [[neikos]] and [[nemesis]]). The rebuke is never delivered. Agamemnon first grants that at other times he would bid Nestor blame him, and then defends him: Menelaus often hangs back, “not giving way to sloth or to thoughtlessness of mind, but looking to me and waiting for me to lead”, and tonight he woke first (10.120–127; see [[menelaus]]). Nestor withdraws at once: in that case no one will blame him or disobey him (10.129–130). In Book 9 a rebuke of the king had to be justified as “the custom … in the assembly” (9.33; see the note on Book 9); here a rebuke of the king's brother is turned aside by a word of praise.

**Blame returned.** Nestor wakes Diomedes and “rebuked him to his face”: why does he sleep the whole night through, with the Trojans so near (10.158–161)? Diomedes answers with a reproach that is also a compliment: “You are a hard man, old sir; you never cease from toil … there is no dealing with you, old sir” (10.164–167). Nestor grants that it is all “fair and right” (10.169; see [[moira]]) and sends him off anyway.

**Praise by name.** The king's instructions to his brother are a lesson in courtesy: call out to each man “naming each man by his father and his lineage, and giving glory to them all; and do not be proud in your heart” (10.68–69; see [[kydos]] and [[genee]]). Nestor praises the sentries for their watch: “Keep watch like this now, dear children” (10.192).

**Praise refused, and given.** Chosen as Diomedes' companion and praised before the chiefs as the man Athena loves and the one who would bring them both home out of blazing fire (10.242–247), Odysseus answers, “do not praise me too much, nor rebuke me at all; for you are saying this among Argives who know it” (10.249–250; see [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]]). He comes back with the horses of Rhesus, and Nestor greets him with praise in a name: “Come, tell me, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans” (πολύαινʼ Ὀδυσεῦ, 10.544; see [[much-praised]]). On the Trojan side there is only blame: the captured Dolon lays his errand on Hector's promises (10.391; see [[ate]]).
