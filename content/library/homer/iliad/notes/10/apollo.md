summary: Apollo acts once in Book 10. Seeing Athena with Diomedes, he goes down into the Trojan throng in resentment at her and wakes Hippocoon, Rhesus' cousin, too late to save the Thracians or their king's horses.

{{quote:40b20b27-8c70-58d1-8cd9-b4b3afbd2f4f}}

Athena has just told Diomedes to go back to the ships “if some other god should wake the Trojans too” (10.511; see [[athena]]), and the other god comes at once. οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε, “nor … keep a blind watch”, is said only of gods who watch another's moves: of Poseidon, when Zeus turns his eyes away from Troy (13.7–10) and when the wounded kings go back to the battle (14.133–136), and in the Odyssey of Ares, when he sees Hephaestus leave his house (*Odyssey* 8.285–286). Apollo watches Athena. On the first day of battle, when he saw that she was gone, he had Ares rouse the Trojans (5.508–511), and when he saw her coming down from Olympus, he went to meet her at the oak (7.17–22). His resentment here, τῇ κοτέων, is at her, not at the man she helps (see [[kotos]]). In the chariot race it is the son of Tydeus he resents (κοτέσσατο, 23.383): he knocks the whip from Diomedes' hand, and Athena, who sees it, gives it back (23.383–390; see [[diomedes]]).

He wakes Hippocoon, “a counselor of the Thracians, / the brave cousin of Rhesus” (10.518–519; see [[hippocoon]] and [[rhesus]]), who sees the empty place where the horses stood and the men gasping in the slaughter, and calls his companion by name (10.519–522). The Trojans rush together in uproar (10.523–525; see [[thracians]]). The god comes too late. The two spies are already riding for the ships, and the Trojans can only gaze at “the grim deeds / the men had done” (10.524–525). The book names him only here, with his title ἀργυρότοξος, “god of the silver bow” (10.515; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]).
