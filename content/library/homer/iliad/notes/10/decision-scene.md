**Agamemnon.** “But the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, was not held by sweet sleep, as he pondered many things in his mind” (10.3–4). The book opens in the frame of Zeus' night at the start of Book 2: the others slept all night long, but one did not (2.1–4; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[hypnos]]). The resolution comes in Zeus' verse, “And this plan seemed to him in his heart the best” (10.17 = 2.5), and the plan is to go to Nestor, “in the hope that he might devise with him some flawless plan that would ward off evil from all the Danaans” (10.17–20; see [[metis]]). Zeus' plan was a lying dream; Agamemnon's is to ask for counsel.

**Diomedes.** When Odysseus has driven off the horses and whistled, Diomedes stays:

{{quote:909e6eec-5b07-5616-8066-f492c01689ff}}

The alternatives are three, not two (ἢ … ἢ … ἦ), and the deed he is weighing is κύντατον, “the most shameless thing he could do”, the superlative of the dog-word, of a deed of reckless daring (see the translation's note on 10.503). “While he was pondering this in his mind, just then Athena came and stood near and spoke to brilliant Diomedes” (10.507–508). Achilles' deliberation in Book 1 was cut off in the same way: “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart, and was drawing his great sword from its sheath, Athena came” (1.193–194). In both scenes the goddess stops a man in the middle of killing, and in both she is obeyed at once: Diomedes “knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke” and mounted the horses (10.512–513; see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[athena]]).
