summary: Back at the ships, Odysseus and Diomedes wash off their sweat in the sea, step into polished bathtubs, anoint themselves with oil, and sit down to eat and pour wine to Athena (10.572–579). It is the Iliad's only bath of living men, told in the order and partly in the verses of the Odyssey's welcome of a guest.

**Sea and tub.** The washing comes in two stages. First the two wade into the sea and wash off the heavy sweat from their shins, necks and thighs, until the wave has washed it from their skin and “their hearts were refreshed” (ἀνέψυχθεν φίλον ἦτορ, 10.572–575). Then they step into polished bathtubs and bathe, and anoint themselves richly with olive oil (10.576–577). The bathtubs (ἀσάμινθοι) are the only ones in the Iliad, and the verbs are middle: no servant bathes them, and no clean clothes are brought, as they are for a guest in the Odyssey (see [[bathing]]). In the Odyssey the bath takes the weariness from the limbs (Od. 10.363); here it ends a night's toil.

**A guest's welcome.** The order is the Odyssey's. When Telemachus and Peisistratus arrive at Sparta, their sweating horses are unyoked and tied at the mangers, and the guests step into polished tubs, in the verse of 10.576, are bathed and anointed, and sit down to a meal (Od. 4.39–58). Here the horses of Rhesus have just been tied at Diomedes' manger (10.566–569; see [[horses]]), and the bath, the anointing and the meal follow, with a libation to Athena (see [[feast]] and [[libation]]). The two come home to their own camp as guests come to a king's house.

**The only bath.** No other living man is bathed in the Iliad. Hector's bath is heated while he lies dead (22.442–446), and Achilles refuses his until Patroclus is burned (23.39–47).
