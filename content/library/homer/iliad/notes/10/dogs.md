summary: The dogs of Book 10 are in two similes and one word. The Achaean sentries keep watch like dogs around the sheep in a fold (10.183–189), Diomedes and Odysseus run Dolon down like two hounds after a fawn or a hare (10.360–364), and Diomedes ponders “the most shameless thing he could do”, κύντατον, a superlative formed from the dog (10.503).
grc: [κυνῶν, κύνε, κύντατον]
en: [hounds, most shameless]
passages:
  - 10.183-189 | The watchdogs
  - 10.360-364 | Two hounds
---

**Watchdogs.** The leaders of the sentries are awake in their arms, like dogs that keep a troubled watch around the sheep in a fold when they hear a fierce beast coming down through the woods; a great din rises against it “of men and dogs” (ἀνδρῶν ἠδὲ κυνῶν), and sleep is lost to them (10.183–186; see [[the-watchdogs]] and [[night-watch]]). These are the dogs of the farm, the guardians of the herd that the lion similes set against the beast (11.548–555, 12.299–306). The verb δυσωρέομαι, “keep a hard watch”, occurs only here and is built on the root of θυραωροί, the dogs that guard Priam's door (22.69).

**Hounds.** In the chase of Dolon the dogs are hunters. Two sharp-toothed hounds, εἰδότε θήρης, “skilled in the hunt”, press a fawn or a hare through wooded country (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]], [[deer]] and [[hare]]). κύνε, the dual of κύων, occurs only here, and the dual makes the two Achaeans one pair of hounds. In Book 8 Hector was a single hound at the heels of a boar or a lion (8.338–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]); here the hounds' quarry is the weakest kind, and the spy who went out in a wolf's skin is run down by dogs (10.334; see [[wolves]]).

**The most shameless.** When Odysseus has driven out the horses, Diomedes stays “and pondered what was the most shameless thing he could do” (ὅ τι κύντατον ἕρδοι, 10.503): to take the chariot, or to kill more Thracians (10.503–506). κύντατος is the superlative of the word that Zeus threw at Hera in Book 8, “there is nothing more shameless than you” (κύντερον, 8.483). The dog is the beast of shamelessness (see [[anaideia]]), and here the word is used of the boldest outrage open to a man in an enemy camp, until Athena tells him to go (10.507–511; see the translation's note on 10.503).

**Caps.** κυνέη, the cap or helmet, was once a cap of dogskin (see [[helmet]]). In Book 10 the word is used of a skullcap of bull's hide, of a leather helmet faced with a boar's tusks, and of Dolon's cap of ferret skin (10.257–258, 10.261–265, 10.335; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]] and [[ferret]]).
