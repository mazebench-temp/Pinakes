summary: The Trojans spend the night on the plain beside their fires, while their leaders sit in Hector's council. Dolon tells the Achaean spies that only the Trojans keep watch, each man at his own fire, while the allies sleep; at the end of the night they wake to the slaughter in the Thracian camp.

**The fires.** The book opens on the Trojans as the Achaeans see them. Agamemnon looks out over “the Trojan plain” and marvels “at the many fires that burned before Ilios, / at the sound of flutes and pipes and the din of men” (10.11–13): the thousand fires of their camp (8.553–563; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]). Flutes and pipes are heard nowhere else in the Iliad but on the shield of Achilles, at a wedding and among herdsmen piping in the fields (18.495, 18.526). The Trojans are camped “on the rise of the plain / near the ships” (10.160–161), and Agamemnon fears that they “may be eager to fight even by night” (10.100–101; see [[war-and-night]]).

**Two councils.** Hector does not let “the proud Trojans” sleep (Τρῶας ἀγήνορας, 10.299; see [[proud]]): he calls their leaders and rulers to council, and asks for a spy, and they fall silent as the Achaean chiefs fell silent at Nestor's question (10.313 = 10.218; see [[hector]] and [[the-doloneia]]). Each side wants to know what the other means to do. Nestor's question, whether the Trojans mean to stay by the ships or go back to the city (10.208–210), Odysseus puts to Dolon in the same verses (10.409–411).

There is no “picked guard” (κεκριμένη, 10.417), only the men at the hearth-fires, “those who must”, keeping one another awake (see the translation's note on 10.418–419). The Trojans' wives and children are in the city behind them, and the allies', far away; so the Trojans watch and the allies sleep (see [[trojan-allies]] and [[night-watch]]). They fight, the poem said of them, “for their children and for their wives” (8.57).

**The waking.** Athena warns Diomedes to leave before “some other god should wake the Trojans too” (10.511), and Apollo goes down “into the great throng of the Trojans” and wakes Hippocoon (10.517–519; see [[apollo]] and [[hippocoon]]). Then “a clamor and an untold tumult rose among the Trojans / as they came rushing together; and they gazed at the grim deeds / the men had done” (10.523–525; see [[grim-deeds]]). κυδοιμός, “tumult”, is here the common noun; at 5.593 it is Tumult, whom Enyo brings into battle (see [[tumult]]). At the ships Nestor fears that the best of the Argives may have come to harm “under the din of the Trojans” (10.539), the phrase with which Odysseus urged Achilles to save the Achaeans (9.247–248).

Their epithets in the book are “proud” (10.299), “great-hearted” (10.205), “horse-taming” (10.424) and “noble” (10.563; see [[great-hearted]], [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[noble]]).
