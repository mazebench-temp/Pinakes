Book 10 opens as Book 2 does: everyone sleeps but one, who lies awake with his cares, Zeus there, Agamemnon here (10.1–4; 2.1–4; see [[agamemnon]] and [[hypnos]]). Aristotle's example of a metaphor sets the opening of Book 2, quoted with “all” for “the other”, beside a line and a half of Book 10: “whenever he looked out over the Trojan plain … the din of flutes and pipes”. He runs 10.11 into the beginning of 10.13, so that the din, which in our text is “the din of men”, becomes a din of flutes and pipes. The difficulty is plain: if all slept, how could Agamemnon gaze at the plain, and who was playing? Aristotle answers that “all” is put for “many” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a16–20). In our text both books say “the others” (ἄλλοι), and the music comes from the Trojan camp, where Hector had told his men to keep their fires burning all night (8.508–509, 8.553–565).

Modern readers have found the opening awkward for other reasons. Walter Leaf called its first two lines “a merely formal tag”, since none of the chiefs can have had much sleep that night, and asked how Agamemnon, lying in his hut behind the wall, could look out over the plain at all; the scholia, he notes, offer various prosaic solutions (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.1 and 10.11). The translation renders the lines as they stand (see [[the-doloneia-question]]).
