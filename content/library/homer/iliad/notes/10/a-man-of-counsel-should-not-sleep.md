summary: In the night of Book 10 the Dream's maxim is kept and broken. Agamemnon lies awake while the others sleep; Nestor wakes Diomedes with a question that echoes the Dream, “Why do you sleep the whole night through?”; and Hippocoon, “a counselor of the Thracians”, sleeps until a god wakes him too late.

The Dream told the sleeping Agamemnon that “a man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through, a man to whom the people are entrusted, and who has so much to care for” (2.24–25). The opening of Book 10 shows the king doing what it asked: the others sleep all night, “but the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, was not held by sweet sleep, as he pondered many things in his mind” (10.3–4; see the article above and [[hypnos]]). His first thought is to go to the man of counsel above all, Nestor, and the old man is awake when he comes (10.17–20, 10.80–85).

Nestor repeats the maxim's reproach to the young man whom he praised in Book 9 as best in counsel among his age-mates (9.53–54). He finds Diomedes asleep outside his hut and wakes him with his heel: “Wake up, son of Tydeus! Why do you sleep the whole night through?” (τί πάννυχον ὕπνον ἀωτεῖς, 10.159), with πάννυχος, the shorter form of the Dream's παννύχιος (2.24). Diomedes springs up and protests that the old man never rests (10.162–167; see [[neikos]]).

On the other side of the plain the maxim's own word, βουληφόρος, “man of counsel”, names a man who slept through the night's danger. Apollo, angered to see Athena with Diomedes, “roused Hippocoon, a counselor of the Thracians, the brave cousin of Rhesus; and he started up from sleep”, to find the horses gone and his companions gasping in their blood (10.517–522; see [[hippocoon]] and [[man-of-counsel]]). Hector, meanwhile, holds his council awake “with all those who are counselors” (βουληφόροι, 10.414; see [[boule]]).
