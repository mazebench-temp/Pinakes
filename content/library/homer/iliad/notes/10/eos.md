---
summary: Book 10 is a night book and has no dawn of its own. Dawn is only near: when Odysseus and Diomedes set out, Odysseus reckons that two of the night's three portions are gone, “and dawn is near” (10.251).
passages:
  - 10.248-253 | The night two parts gone
---

Odysseus cuts short Diomedes' praise of him: “But let us go; for the night is far spent, and dawn is near; / the stars have moved on, and the greater part of the night has gone, / two portions of it, and a third portion still remains” (10.251–253; see [[odysseus]] and [[moira]]). The Odyssey reckons the night by the same thirds and the same stars: “when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had moved on” (τρίχα νυκτὸς ἔην, μετὰ δʼ ἄστρα βεβήκει, *Odyssey* 12.312 = 14.483). If more than two portions had gone, a whole third could not remain. Aristotle gives the verse as a case of ambiguity, “for πλέω is ambiguous” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a), and the translation takes πλέων as “the greater part”, made up of the two portions (see the translation's note on 10.251–253).

In ἐγγύθι δʼ ἠώς, ἠώς is the dawn that is coming, and the index counts the line to Dawn, as it counts “the twelfth dawn” (1.493), “When it was not yet dawn” (7.433), and the dawn's spread in the speeches of Poseidon and Zeus (7.451, 7.458). Only 8.66 is excepted, where ἠώς is the morning, the part of the day before midday (see the translation's note on 8.66).

The night began when the light of the sun fell into Oceanus at the end of the second day of battle (8.485–486), and it holds the embassy of Book 9 and the raid (see [[war-and-night]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). The two spies are back at the ships, bathed and fed, before the dawn comes (10.572–579). It rises from the bed of Tithonus at the start of Book 11 (11.1–2).
