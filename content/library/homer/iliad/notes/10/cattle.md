summary: Cattle in Book 10 are a hide, a vow and a measure. Diomedes sleeps on the hide of an ox of the fields (10.155); he vows Athena a yearling heifer, unbroken, with gold poured around her horns, in the verses of Nestor's prayer at Pylos (10.292–294 = Odyssey 3.382–384); and mules, the narrator says, plow better than oxen (10.351–353).

**The oxhide.** Nestor finds Diomedes asleep outside his hut among his companions, “and under him was spread the hide of an ox of the fields” (ῥινὸν βοὸς ἀγραύλοιο), with a shining rug under his head (10.155–156; see [[of-the-fields]]). An oxhide is a soldier's bed in the field. In the Odyssey Odysseus, a beggar in his own house, sleeps in the porch on an untanned oxhide with fleeces over it (*Odyssey* 20.1–3).

**The vow.** As the two set out, Diomedes prays to Athena to stand by him as she stood by his father Tydeus at Thebes, and he makes her a promise:

{{quote:22403eed-4545-5df6-a313-cfcc580aa9d1}}

βοῦν ἦνιν εὐρυμέτωπον / ἀδμήτην: “a yearling heifer, broad of brow, / unbroken”. ἦνις, “yearling”, is the word of the twelve cows the Trojan women promise Athena in Book 6 (6.93–94; see [[yearling]]), and ἀδμής, “unbroken”, is spelled out in the relative clause: no man has yet led her under the yoke. An animal that has never worked is fit for a god. The three verses are Nestor's in the Odyssey, when he prays to Athena at Pylos (*Odyssey* 3.382–384), and there the sacrifice is told: the goldsmith Laerces is sent for, Nestor gives him the gold, and he works it around the heifer's horns, “so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (*Odyssey* 3.425–438). Both times the goddess hears the prayer (10.295; *Odyssey* 3.385), as she refused the Trojan women's (6.311). The poem does not tell whether Diomedes paid his vow (see [[sacrifice]], [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[athena]]).

**Oxen and mules.** The start that Dolon has on his pursuers is measured by mules, “for they are better than oxen / at dragging the jointed plow through the deep fallow” (αἱ γάρ τε βοῶν προφερέστεραί εἰσιν, 10.352–353; see [[mules]]). The plow is drawn by oxen in the other two places where Homer names the jointed plow: the two Ajaxes stand side by side like two wine-dark oxen straining at it in the fallow (13.703–707), and in the Odyssey a man longs for his supper after his two oxen have dragged it all day through the field (*Odyssey* 13.31–34).
