**The counselor.** Agamemnon, sleepless, decides that the best plan is “to go first of all men to Nestor, son of Neleus, in the hope that he might devise with him some flawless plan” (10.18–19; see [[agamemnon]] and [[metis]]). Νηλήϊον, “Neleian”, is the adjective that serves as a patronymic, as in the Dream's disguise (Νηληΐῳ υἷι, 2.20; see [[neleus]]). Earlier this same night it was Nestor who “first of all began to weave a plan” (9.93), and the sentries “would heed him above all, for his son commands the sentries” (10.57–58; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[night-watch]]).

**In his bed.** Agamemnon finds him “beside his hut and his black ship, in a soft bed”, with a shield, two spears and a helmet beside him (10.74–76; see [[bed]]).

{{quote:02a776f6-c40b-5e7d-a066-d2ae96d2f76c}}

παναίολος, “gleaming”, is also the epithet of the belt that stopped Pandarus' arrow at Menelaus' waist (4.186; see [[belt]] and [[panaiolos]]). The old man who granted in Book 4 that “now old age is upon me” (4.321) still keeps his war-belt by his bed (see [[old-age-and-memory]]). He raises himself on his elbow and challenges the figure in the dark: “Are you looking for one of your mules, or one of your companions?” (10.82–85). A strayed mule has seemed a strange errand for a king, and οὐρήων has also been taken as “guards” (see the translation's note on 10.84, [[mules]] and [[aristotle-mules]]).

**His answer.** To Agamemnon's fear he answers with a hope: “surely Zeus the counselor will not fulfill for Hector all the plans that he now hopes for”, and Hector will suffer more “if Achilles turns his dear heart from its grievous anger” (10.104–107; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The embassy has failed only hours before, and he still counts on Achilles. He names the men to wake, and turns on Menelaus, “dear and respected though he is”, for sleeping while his brother toils (10.108–118; see [[menelaus]]). When Agamemnon answers that Menelaus is already out calling the chiefs, Nestor yields at once (10.119–130).

**The round.** He dresses in the verses of Agamemnon's tunic and sandals (10.131–132 ≈ 10.21–22), but where the king put on a lion's skin, Nestor fastens on “a crimson cloak, double and wide, with a thick woolly nap upon it” (10.133–134; see [[lion-skin]], [[cloak]] and [[dressing-scene]]). He wakes Odysseus with a call, for a council “whether to flee or to fight” (10.146–147), the words Dolon will use of the chiefs he means to spy on (10.327; see [[odysseus]]). Diomedes he finds asleep outside his hut and wakes “stirring him with his heel” (λὰξ ποδὶ κινήσας, 10.158), the verse with which, in the Odyssey, Telemachus wakes Nestor's son Peisistratus (Od. 15.45). The young man protests:

{{quote:574276be-374c-5db8-a080-219497aef49f}}

“Yes, my friend, all that you have said is fair and right” (10.169) is the verse with which Diomedes answered Nestor on the battlefield, “Yes, old man” (8.146), returned with φίλος, “my friend”, in place of γέρον. The need is too great to send anyone else, for now it stands “on the edge of a razor” whether destruction comes on the Achaeans, or life (10.170–174; see [[on-the-razors-edge]]). He sends Diomedes for swift Ajax and Meges, “for you are younger, if you have pity on me” (10.175–176). In Book 9 he told Diomedes that he could be his youngest son (9.57–58; see [[diomedes]]).

**The council.** Among the sentries, who are awake and armed, “the old man was glad when he saw them”, and calls them “dear children” (10.190–193). He leads the kings across the ditch (10.194–202; see [[kings]]) and asks for a man who would go among the Trojans, catch a straggler or overhear their plans, and come back unharmed (10.204–211; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). The reward is fame “under heaven” and a black ewe with her lamb from every chief who commands a ship (10.212–217; see [[kleos]] and [[sheep]]). Silence follows, as it follows Hector's richer offer to the Trojans (10.218 = 10.313; see [[silence]]), until Diomedes answers him by name (10.220).

**The return.** “Nestor was the first to hear the sound” (10.532), and he doubts himself in Helen's words when she recognizes Telemachus, “shall I be wrong, or speak the truth?” (10.534 = Od. 4.140). He asks first, as Agamemnon asked when the embassy came back, and in the same verse: “Come, tell me, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans” (10.543–544 = 9.672–673; see [[much-praised]]). The horseman's eye is on the horses, “terribly like the rays of the sun”: he is always among the Trojans, “old warrior though I am”, but has never seen their like, and he thinks a god gave them, “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both” (10.547–553; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]] and [[gerenian-horseman]]). It is the verse with which Idaeus parted Hector and Ajax (7.280). Odysseus answers with the address Agamemnon used in the dark, “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans” (10.555 = 10.87; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]), and calls him “old sir” (10.558). Nestor does not speak again in the book.
