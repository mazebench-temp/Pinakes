**Newly come.** Dolon, having told the spies where every other ally lies, points them to the Thracians: “here are the Thracians, apart, newly come, the farthest out of all; / and among them is Rhesus, their king, the son of Eioneus” (10.434–435; see [[rhesus]], [[newly-come]] and [[trojan-allies]]). They are not the Thracians of the Catalogue, whom Acamas and Peirous led from the shores of the Hellespont (2.844–845); both of those leaders are already dead (4.517–538, 6.5–11; see [[peirous]] and [[acamas-of-thrace]]). The poem does not say from what part of Thrace Rhesus came (see [[thrace]]).

**Asleep.** Odysseus prays to Athena to “guide us to the horses and the beds of the Thracian men” (10.464), and the two come to “the company of the Thracian men” (Θρῃκῶν ἀνδρῶν τέλος, 10.470). The Thracians are sleeping, “worn out by weariness”, with their fine armor laid on the ground beside them “in good order, / in three rows”, and beside each man a yoke of horses (10.471–473). No one keeps watch: the allies leave the watch to the Trojans (10.420–421; see [[night-watch]]).

**The killing.** Athena breathes might into Diomedes, and he kills “turning this way and that”; an ugly groaning rises from the men struck by the sword, and the earth grows red with blood. As a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman, “so the son of Tydeus went at the Thracian men, / until he had killed twelve” (10.482–488; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]] and [[diomedes]]). Odysseus drags each man aside, and the thirteenth is the king (10.488–497). When Odysseus has the horses out, Diomedes still ponders whether to take the chariot or the lives of “still more of the Thracians” (10.503–506), until Athena sends him away (see [[athena]]).

**The waking.** Apollo wakes Hippocoon, “a counselor of the Thracians”, who finds the horses gone and the men “gasping in the grievous slaughter” (10.518–522; see [[hippocoon]] and [[apollo]]). At the ships Odysseus tells Nestor that the horses are “newly come; / they are Thracian”, and that Diomedes killed their lord “and beside him twelve companions, all of the best” (10.558–560; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). The Thracians who came to fight for Troy never fight. In the *Rhesus* their king's charioteer, wounded, tells how they slept without a watch, and, unlike Homer's Thracians, without their armor laid out in order (*Rhesus* 762–769; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).
