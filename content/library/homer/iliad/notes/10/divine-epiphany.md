summary: The goddess of the night is known by sound. Her heron is heard and not seen, and when Athena comes and stands near Diomedes among the dead Thracians, he knows her by her voice and obeys at once (10.274–277, 10.507–513). Apollo, who keeps no blind watch, sees her with him (10.515–517).

Athena “came and stood near and spoke to brilliant Diomedes” (ἐγγύθεν ἱσταμένη, 10.508), as she stood near Odysseus by his ship in Book 2 (ἀγχοῦ δʼ ἱσταμένη, 2.172). Her speech is short and urgent: “Think now of your return, son of great-hearted Tydeus, to the hollow ships, or you may get there in flight, if some other god should wake the Trojans too” (10.509–511). “So she spoke, and he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke” (10.512), the verse in which Odysseus knew her in Book 2 (2.182; see [[athena]]). As in Book 2 there is no astonishment and no reply. Diomedes mounts the horses at once (10.513; see [[decision-scene]]).

The poem does not say whether Diomedes sees her. Before the raid the two know Athena's heron only by its cry: “they did not see it with their eyes through the dark night, but they heard it cry” (10.275–276; see [[heron]]). The god who sees is Apollo. He does not “keep a blind watch” (10.515), and when he sees Athena attending Diomedes he goes down into the Trojan throng and wakes Hippocoon (10.515–519; see [[apollo]] and [[hippocoon]]). ἀλαοσκοπιή, “blind watch”, is used in this way only of gods, who watch keenly (13.10, 14.135; Od. 8.285).
