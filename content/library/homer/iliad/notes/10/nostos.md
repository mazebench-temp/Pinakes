**Coming home from the night.** Elsewhere in the Iliad νόστος is the return to Greece, the homecoming the army runs for in Book 2 and Achilles weighs against fame in Book 9 (2.155, 9.413; see the article above). In Book 10 the words are used of a short way back, from the enemy's camp to one's own. Diomedes gives his reason for choosing Odysseus: “If he goes with me, even out of blazing fire we would both come home (ἄμφω νοστήσαιμεν), since he is wise beyond all others” (10.246–247). The verse sets the homecoming beside the knowing that ensures it, νοστήσαιμεν and νοῆσαι, two words whose possible kinship is discussed under [[noos]]. At the Thracian camp, when Diomedes lingers over what more to do, Athena breaks in: “Think now of your return, son of great-hearted Tydeus, to the hollow ships, or you may get there in flight, if some other god should wake the Trojans too” (νόστου δὴ μνῆσαι, 10.509–511; see [[athena]]). The genitive νόστου is found only here in the Iliad.

**The spy who did not return.** Every plan for the night includes the way back. Nestor's spy is to learn the Trojans' plans “and come back to us unharmed” (10.211–212), and Odysseus prays that they “come back to the ships with glory” (10.281; see [[kleos]]). As soon as Dolon sets out, the narrator says that he “was not to come back from the ships and bring word to Hector” (10.336–337; see [[dolon]]). The two Achaeans come back on Rhesus' horses, and the Odyssey's man of homecoming is the one who brings his companion home (see [[odysseus]]).
