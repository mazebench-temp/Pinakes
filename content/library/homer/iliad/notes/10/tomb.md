summary: Hector holds his council “by the mound of divine Ilus”, away from the din of the camp (10.414–416). The old king's grave mound is a meeting place on the plain, as old tombs are lookouts and mustering places elsewhere in the Iliad; the next day Paris shoots Diomedes from behind its pillar.
grc: [σήματι]
---

Dolon tells Odysseus that “Hector, with all those who are counselors, is holding council by the mound of divine Ilus, away from the din” (10.414–416; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[ilus]]). The grave mound of Ilus, son of Tros and grandfather of Priam (20.231–237), is a place to meet on the plain, away from the fires and the noise of the Trojan camp. Old tombs serve the living elsewhere in the Iliad. Polites, “trusting in his speed of foot”, kept watch for the Trojans from the top of the tomb of old Aesyetes (2.791–794; see [[spies-and-scouts]]), and the Trojans and their allies were marshaled at the mound that men call Batieia and the gods the tomb of Myrine (2.811–815).

The next day the tomb of Ilus is a landmark of the rout, as the Trojans flee past it toward the city (11.166–168), and a hiding place. Paris, leaning against the pillar on it, shoots Diomedes in the foot as he strips a dead man, and leaps out “from his ambush” to boast (11.369–379; see [[ambush]]). Priam passes it on his night journey to Achilles (24.349–351).

σῆμα occurs twice in Book 10, in two senses: the mound that marks a grave (10.415) and the mark that Odysseus sets beside Dolon's spoils so that he can find them in the dark (10.466; see [[dedication-of-spoils]]). Only the first is counted here.
