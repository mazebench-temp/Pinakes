---
summary: Horses are the prize of the night. Hector promises his spy the best team at the Achaean ships, Dolon asks for the immortal horses of Achilles, and Odysseus and Diomedes bring back instead the snow-white horses of the Thracian king Rhesus, riding them to the ships as no one else in the Iliad rides.

**The price of a spy.** Hector offers his spy “a chariot and two horses with high-arched necks, the best there are at the swift ships of the Achaeans” (10.305–306). Dolon names the best: Hector must swear to give him “the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze that carry the noble son of Peleus”, and Hector swears that no other Trojan “shall ride behind those horses” (10.322–331; see [[oath]]). Odysseus smiles at the prize:

{{quote:507f07f3-85f1-5108-935c-ee6116dfa412}}

They are immortal, a gift of the gods to Peleus (16.866–867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). In Book 17 Apollo tells Hector the same thing in the same three verses, when Hector himself is chasing those horses (17.75–78), and Zeus, pitying them as they weep for Patroclus, declares that Hector “shall not ride behind” them (17.441–449).

**The Thracian teams.** Dolon describes the horses of Rhesus: “His are the finest horses I have seen, and the biggest: whiter than snow, and like the winds in running” (10.436–437; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). White horses are named nowhere else in Homer. The Thracians sleep with “a yoke of horses” beside each man (10.473), the words used of Pandarus' horses standing idle at home (5.195), and Rhesus' team is tied by straps to the end of his chariot rail (10.474–475; see [[chariot]]). Odysseus divides the work: “Untie the horses; or else you kill the men, and the horses will be my care” (10.480–481), the words in which Aeneas offered Pandarus the choice of driving or fighting (5.228). While Diomedes kills, Odysseus drags each dead man aside by the foot, so that the horses “might pass through easily, and not tremble in their hearts at treading on the dead; for they were not yet used to them” (10.491–493). ἀήθεσσον, “they were not yet used to”, occurs nowhere else in Homer. The horses are as new to the war as their master, “newly come” (10.434, 10.558).

**Driven with a bow.** Odysseus unties the horses, ties them together with the straps, and drives them out of the throng, “striking them with his bow, since he had not thought of the shining whip, to take it in his hands from the inlaid chariot”; then he whistles to Diomedes (10.498–502).

**Riding.** At Athena's warning Diomedes “mounted the horses” (ἵππων ἐπεβήσετο), Odysseus struck them with his bow, “and they flew toward the swift ships of the Achaeans” (10.513–514). Elsewhere ἵππων ἐπιβαίνειν is to mount a chariot, the horses standing for team and car together (5.46, 5.255, 5.328), but the chariot has been left behind (10.503–511), and the two ride. Where they killed Dolon, Odysseus holds the horses in while Diomedes leaps down for the spoils and mounts again (10.526–529), and at the ships the two get down “onto the ground” (10.541). Riding appears elsewhere in the Iliad only in a simile, of a trick rider who leaps from horse to horse of four galloping along the road to a city (15.679–684). In the Odyssey, Odysseus sits astride a beam of his wrecked raft “like a man riding a horse” (Od. 5.371). The ride itself is told in the verses of a chariot journey, “and he whipped the horses”, though no whip has been taken (10.530–531; see [[chariot-journey]]).

**At the ships.** Nestor hears them first: “The sound of swift-footed horses is beating about my ears” (10.535). The old horseman of Pylos has never seen horses like these, “terribly like the rays of the sun”, and thinks a god must have given them (10.547–553; see [[nestor]] and [[like-the-sun]]). Odysseus answers that a god could give better ones, and that these are Thracian and newly come (10.556–559). He drives them across the ditch, laughing (10.564–565), and they are tied at Diomedes' hut:

{{quote:b3bfb500-be21-54e3-94bf-2af5fe2c13dd}}

Diomedes' own horses are the horses of Tros he took from Aeneas (5.319–327, 8.105–108). Wheat for horses is mentioned once more in the Iliad: Andromache set it before Hector's team (8.188).

**Afterward.** The horses of Rhesus are not mentioned again in the Iliad, and in the chariot race of Book 23 Diomedes drives the horses of Tros (23.290–292). The silence is one of the old arguments that Book 10 was not part of the poem (see [[the-doloneia]]).
