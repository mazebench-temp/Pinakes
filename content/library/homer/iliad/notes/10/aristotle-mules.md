οὐρήων (10.84) is the genitive plural of οὐρεύς, the word that Aristotle thought might mean “guards” at 1.50. The alternative rests on οὖρος, “warden”, which is Nestor's own title, “guardian of the Achaeans” (8.80, 11.840, 15.370, 15.659; *Odyssey* 3.411). Here it would make Nestor ask whether the king is looking for one of the sentries, on a night when the sentries are much on the chiefs' minds (10.56–59, 10.97–101, 10.180–193; see [[night-watch]]). Walter Leaf reports from the scholia that Aristarchus rejected the verse, because he took οὐρεύς here to mean φύλαξ, “guard” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.84).

Leaf himself kept the mules. The word is too common in that sense to bear another, and a man roused in the night might well ask an intruder whether he wants a friend or a strayed mule, of which the camp had many (1.50; 23.111). He cites an earlier comparison with a night in Xenophon's *Anabasis*, when a panic ran through the Greek camp and Clearchus had a herald proclaim a talent of silver for whoever would name the man who had let the ass loose among the arms (*Anabasis* 2.2.20). The translation keeps “mules”, as at 1.50 (see the translation's note on 10.84 and [[mules]]). Mules appear in Book 10 once more, as a measure of distance: Dolon has run “as far off as the furrow-length / of mules” before his pursuers break cover (10.351–352).
