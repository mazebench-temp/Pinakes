---
summary: In the spring of 49 BCE Cicero wrote to Atticus in the words of Book 10, Agamemnon's distress for his own and Diomedes' “when two go together” for the counsel he lacked. He set Agamemnon tearing his hair among the foolish customs of grief, named Rhesus as the son of a Muse, and credited Peisistratus with putting Homer's books in order.
---

- **Agamemnon's distress.** When Pompey and the consuls had left Italy, Cicero wrote that he was no longer anxious but burning with grief, and quoted Agamemnon: οὐδέ μοι ἦτορ ἔμπεδον, ἀλλʼ ἀλαλύκτημαι, “my heart / is not steady, but I am distraught” (*Letters to Atticus* 9.6.4, quoting 10.93–94; see [[agamemnon]]).
- **Two together.** Later in the same letter: if Atticus had been well, they would have been together, and counsel would not have been lacking: σύν τε δύʼ ἐρχομένω, “when two go together” (*Letters to Atticus* 9.6.6): [[when-two-go-together]].
- **Agamemnon's hair.** In the third book of the *Tusculan Disputations*, on grief, Cicero traces the ugly forms of mourning, squalor, the tearing of cheeks, the beating of breast, thigh and head, to the belief that grief is right and a duty: “hence that Homeric Agamemnon”, and Accius' Agamemnon, “tearing his unshorn hair again and again in grief”. Here, he adds, belongs Bion's jest, that the most foolish king tore out his hair in his sorrow, as if baldness relieved grief (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.62). In Homer Agamemnon tears out his hair only once, at the sight of the ships and the army in the night, “to Zeus on high” (10.15–16; see [[agamemnon]]).
- **Rhesus, son of a Muse.** Arguing that divine birth cannot make a god, Cicero's Cotta says that if Achilles is a god because his mother was a goddess, then Orpheus and Rhesus are gods, born of a Muse (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.45). The Rhesus who is the son of a Muse is the Rhesus of the tragedy; in Homer he is the son of Eioneus (10.435; see [[euripides-rhesus]] and [[rhesus]]).
- **Peisistratus.** He is said to have been the first to arrange the books of Homer, until then in disorder, as we have them now (*On the Orator* 3.137): [[the-doloneia-question]].
