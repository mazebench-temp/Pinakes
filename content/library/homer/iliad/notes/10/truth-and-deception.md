---
summary: A night of spies is a night of true and false words. Hector swears an oath that will prove false; Odysseus tells the captured Dolon to take heart and asks for “an exact account”, and Dolon gives one and offers to be tested; the report proves true, and he is killed. Nestor, hearing hoofbeats, asks whether he will be wrong or speak the truth.

---

**An oath that proves false.** Hector swears to Dolon by Zeus that no other Trojan will ride behind Achilles' horses, “but I say that you shall glory in them forever”, and the narrator adds, “So he spoke, and swore an oath that would prove false” (ἐπίορκον ἐπώμοσε, 10.330–332). The oath is false because it will not be kept, since Dolon never comes back (10.336–337); the poem does not say that Hector meant to deceive (see the translation's note on 10.330–332, and [[oath]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). ἐπίορκος is elsewhere the false oath that the powers below punish (3.278–279, 19.259–260).

**An exact account.** Odysseus begins his questioning with comfort, “Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383; see [[tharsos]]), and goes on with the formula of inquiry, “But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account” (ἀτρεκέως κατάλεξον, 10.384, again at 10.405). Dolon answers in kind, “Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly” (10.413, 10.427), and does: where Hector holds council, how the watch is kept, where each of the allies lies, and where the Thracians and their king sleep (10.414–441). He even offers himself as security for his words, asking to be held until they have tested “whether I have spoken to you fairly, or not” (10.444–445; see [[aisa]]). The formula guarantees nothing by itself; in the Odyssey Odysseus promises his father “I will tell you all this very exactly” and tells him a false tale about himself (*Odyssey* 24.303–314). Here the account is true, and it is proved at the Thracian camp: “Here is the man, Diomedes, and here are the horses that Dolon, whom we killed, told us about” (10.477–478). Whether Odysseus' “take heart” was meant to deceive is not said; it promised Dolon nothing, and it was Diomedes who killed him (10.446–457; see [[dolon]]).

**Wrong or true?** When the two ride back in the dark, Nestor is the first to hear the hoofbeats: “Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives, shall I be wrong, or speak the truth? But my heart bids me speak” (ψεύσομαι, ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω; 10.533–534; see [[pseudos]]). The verse is Helen's in the Odyssey, when she recognizes Telemachus by his likeness to his father (*Odyssey* 4.140). Nestor's guess is right, and Odysseus' report to him is plain and true (10.555–563).
