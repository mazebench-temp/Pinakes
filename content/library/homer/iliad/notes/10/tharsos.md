---
summary: The night mission needs daring. Menelaus says the spy would have to be “very bold of heart”, Diomedes says that a companion would bring “more boldness”, and Nestor cheers the sentries. Odysseus' “Take heart” is said to the captured Dolon, who is killed a few lines later.
passages:
  - 10.37-41 | Menelaus on the spy
  - 10.220-232 | The volunteers
  - 10.382-389 | Odysseus questions Dolon
---

**Boldness wanted.** Menelaus doubts that anyone will undertake to go out alone and spy on the enemy through the night: “he would have to be very bold of heart” (μάλα τις θρασυκάρδιος ἔσται, 10.41). θρασυκάρδιος, a compound of θρασύς, the other form of the root of θάρσος, recurs only once, of the man who could look on the toil of battle with joy and not grieve (13.343–344; see [[bold-hearted]]). Nestor asks for a man who will trust “his own daring heart” (θυμῷ τολμήεντι, 10.205), and of the volunteers the narrator says that Odysseus' “heart in his breast was always daring” (θυμὸς ἐτόλμα, 10.232), with τόλμα, a second word for daring.

**Boldness in company.** Diomedes' heart urges him to go, but he asks for a companion: “there would be more comfort, and more boldness” (μᾶλλον θαλπωρὴ καὶ θαρσαλεώτερον, 10.223). θάρσος, which Homer usually describes as a god's gift (see the article above), comes here from a friend at one's side (see [[when-two-go-together]]). θαλπωρή, “comfort, warmth”, is the word Andromache used of the comfort she would lose with Hector (6.411–412).

**Encouragement.** Nestor finds the sentries awake and “cheered them with his words” (θάρσυνε μύθῳ, 10.190), praising them as “dear children” (10.192; see [[night-watch]]). The imperative θάρσει, “take heart”, the poem's ordinary word of encouragement, is spoken once in the book, by Odysseus to the trembling Dolon: “Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all” (θάρσει, μηδέ τί τοι θάνατος καταθύμιος ἔστω, 10.383). The words reassure without promising, and they are followed by questions (see [[truth-and-deception]]). The only other place where death is καταθύμιος, “on the mind”, is Zeus' pity for Hector putting on Achilles' armor: “Ah, wretched man, death is not on your mind at all, though it is near you” (17.201–202). In the Odyssey the verse that introduces Odysseus smiling at Dolon (10.400) introduces his θάρσει to a suppliant he spares, the herald Medon: “Take heart, since this man has saved you” (*Odyssey* 22.371–372). Dolon is killed by Diomedes while he is still speaking (10.454–457; see [[dolon]]).
