summary: The formula ποδάρκης δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς comes seven times: twice “Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles thought of another thing,” the lock of hair and the prayer to the winds (23.140, 23.193); in Nestor's words about the turning post (23.333); and as Achilles pities Eumelus, smiles at Antilochus, is remembered as Eetion's killer, and speaks to Agamemnon (23.534, 23.555, 23.828, 23.889).
---

ἔνθʼ αὖτʼ ἄλλʼ ἐνόησε, “Then … thought of another thing,” is in the Odyssey the verse of Athena's plans (*Odyssey* 2.382, 2.393, 4.795, 6.112, 18.187); Walter Leaf calls it “a characteristically Odyssean phrase,” found in the Iliad only in these two verses (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.140). Each time it gives Achilles a new thought for the dead: he cuts the hair he had grown for the Spercheius (23.141–151; [[hair]]), and he prays to the North Wind and the West Wind when the pyre will not burn (23.192–198; [[prayer]]). At the games the formula marks the moments of Achilles the host: his pity for Eumelus (23.534), his smile at Antilochus, “rejoicing in Antilochus, since he was his dear companion” (23.555–556), and his gift to Agamemnon of the last prize without a contest (23.889–894).
