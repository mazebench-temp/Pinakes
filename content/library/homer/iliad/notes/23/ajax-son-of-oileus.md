summary: The son of Oileus quarrels with Idomeneus over which horses lead the chariot race, until Achilles stops them (23.473–498). In the footrace he leads Odysseus almost to the finish, until Athena makes him slip in the dung of the oxen slain for Patroclus; he takes the second prize, the ox, spitting out the dung and complaining that the goddess stands by Odysseus like a mother, and the Achaeans laugh at him (23.754–792).

**The quarrel.** When Idomeneus says that the leading horses are no longer Eumelus' mares, “swift Ajax, son of Oileus, rebuked him shamefully” (23.473; see [[idomeneus]] and [[aischros]]):

{{quote:bdd3648d-a447-5603-80d8-3276f61cc828}}

λαβρεύομαι, “bluster”, and λαβραγόρης, “blusterer”, occur only in this speech in Homer. Walter Leaf derives them from λάβρος, used of rushing wind and water, a torrent of words; and he reports the remark of the scholia that the man who accuses Idomeneus of bluster was himself ruined by a boast, when he said that he had escaped the sea in spite of the gods and Poseidon drowned him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.474; Od. 4.499–511). He is wrong about the race. Idomeneus calls him “best in quarreling, ill-counseling”, whose mind is harsh, and offers to wager a tripod or a cauldron with Agamemnon as arbiter; Ajax rises in anger to answer, and Achilles stops them both: “No longer now answer each other with harsh words, / Ajax and Idomeneus” (23.482–498; see [[neikos]] and [[achilles]]). Diomedes, not Eumelus, comes in first (23.499).

**The footrace.** He is the first to rise for the footrace, with Odysseus and Antilochus after him, and at once he takes the lead (23.754–759). Odysseus runs as close behind him as the rod is to a weaving woman's breast, treading in his footprints before the dust can settle and pouring his breath on his head, while the Achaeans cheer Odysseus on (23.759–767; see [[odysseus]] and [[the-weaving-womans-rod]]). In the last stretch Odysseus prays to Athena, and she makes his limbs light (23.768–772; see [[athena]] and [[prayer]]). Then:

{{quote:8d73e07c-d52b-577a-9865-2bdc32336462}}

The dung is from the sacrifice of the funeral, the oxen killed for Patroclus (see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[cattle]]). Odysseus takes the silver mixing bowl, and Ajax the second prize, the ox: “and shining Ajax took the ox” (23.778–779; see [[mixing-bowl]]). He stands holding it by the horn, which Leaf takes for a sign of possession (on 23.780), spits out the dung, and speaks among the Argives: “Ah, truly the goddess hindered my feet, she who always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (23.780–783). “So he spoke, and they all laughed heartily at him” (23.784). The man who had no equal in pursuit on foot (14.520–522) loses the race only to a goddess, and Antilochus, who comes in last, tells the Achaeans that “Ajax is only a little older than I”, and that the gods honor older men (23.787–792; see [[antilochus]]). Leaf reports that Aristarchus athetized 23.772, since if Athena had made Odysseus' limbs light he would have won without Ajax's fall, and agrees (on 23.772).
