summary: The fullest account of games in Homer. After the burial Achilles keeps the army, makes them sit “in a wide gathering”, and brings prizes out of the ships (23.257–261); he holds eight contests, the chariot race, the boxing, the wrestling, the footrace, the armed duel, the throw of a lump of iron, the archery and the javelin, with prizes set out for each (23.262–897). Nestor, given a prize without a contest because he is too old to compete, recalls the games at Buprasium, where he won everything but the chariot race (23.618–642).

**The rite.** The games follow the burial in the same place and on the same day: the army is going back from the mound when Achilles stops it (23.257; see the translation's note). ἀγών is the gathering, the circle of seated spectators; the translation renders it as the contest itself only once (ἐν ἀγῶνι, “in a contest”, 23.531; see its key-word row for ἀγών). The prizes come first. They are ἄεθλα, set out before each contest in order of rank; the competitors “make trial of this prize” (ἀέθλου πειρήσεσθαι, 23.707, 23.753, 23.831), and ἀεθλεύειν is to hold games or to compete (23.274, 23.737). Again and again Achilles sets out the prizes, stands up and speaks among the Argives, and calls on the men to rise (see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

**Prizes for all.** In most contests there are as many prizes as competitors: five for the five drivers, down to a two-handled bowl for the last (23.262–270), a cup for the loser of the boxing (23.656), a woman worth four oxen for the loser of the wrestling (23.704–705), and half a talent of gold for the last runner (23.751). The lump of iron, one prize for four men, is the exception (23.826–849). Achilles adds prizes of his own: a corselet for Eumelus, who came in last through no fault of his driving (23.536–565), and half a talent for Antilochus' praise (23.795–796; see [[ainos]]). Nestor and Agamemnon are given prizes without a contest, the one for his age, the other as the acknowledged best (23.615–623, 23.890–897; see [[prize-of-the-old]]).

**Rules and disputes.** Lots decide the places at the start (23.352–357; see [[lots]]), and Phoenix is set by the turning post as a lookout, to mark the running and report the truth (23.359–361; see [[turning-post]]). When Antilochus is accused of fouling Menelaus, Menelaus asks the leaders to judge between them and then puts Antilochus to an oath “as is the custom” (23.566–585; see [[themis]] and [[oath]]). Twice a contest ends with equal prizes: Achilles stops the wrestling (“victory is for both”), and the Achaeans, afraid for Ajax, stop the armed duel (23.735–737, 23.822–823; see [[nike]]). The spectators quarrel too, and Idomeneus proposes a wager of a tripod or a cauldron, with Agamemnon as arbiter (23.485–487; see [[istor]] and [[eris]]).

**The canon.** Achilles names the four contests that Nestor can no longer enter, boxing, wrestling, the javelin and the footrace (23.621–623), and Nestor answers with the five he entered at the funeral games of Amarynceus: boxing, wrestling, the footrace and the spear, which he won, and the chariot race, which he lost to the sons of Actor (23.629–642; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). Walter Leaf takes the two lists as an epic pentathlon, and notes that the Phaeacians' list has leaping in place of the javelin and no chariot race (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.621; *Odyssey* 8.103). This book adds three contests of its own, the armed duel, the lump of iron and the archery. Of the duel Leaf says that “we now leave poetry for patchwork”, and he finds the archery even more confused and obscure (on 23.798, 23.850). Euryalus' father Mecisteus had won the games at the funeral of Oedipus (23.677–680; see [[mecisteus]]).
