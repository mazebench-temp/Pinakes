summary: At Patroclus' funeral games Telamonian Ajax wins no contest outright. He wrestles Odysseus to a draw and is thrown by a trick when he lifts him (23.708–739); in the armed duel the Achaeans, afraid for him, stop his fight with Diomedes (23.811–825); and with the lump of iron he throws beyond Epeius and Leonteus but is outthrown by Polypoetes (23.836–849).

**The wrestling.** “So he spoke, and then great Telamonian Ajax rose, / and up stood Odysseus of many wiles, who knew crafty ways” (23.708–709; see [[telamonian]] and [[odysseus]]): the great man against the cunning one. They lock together like the rafters of a high house, and neither can throw the other, “but the strong force of Odysseus held” (23.710–720; see [[the-rafters-of-a-high-house]]). When the struggle begins to vex the watching Achaeans, Ajax proposes a plainer trial, in a speech of two verses:

{{quote:e398c742-000d-5798-bcb4-ec839f7f7ef5}}

He lifts Odysseus, and Odysseus, who “did not forget his trickery”, kicks him behind the knee and throws him on his back; when Odysseus in turn tries to lift him he can barely move him, and the two fall together (23.725–732). Achilles stops them before a third fall: “victory is for both; take equal prizes” (23.733–737; see [[achilles]]).

**The armed duel.** For the armor of Sarpedon he rises again in the same verse, “So he spoke, and then great Telamonian Ajax rose”, against Diomedes (23.811 = 23.708; see [[diomedes]] and [[sarpedon]]). Three times they rush together. His spear strikes Diomedes' shield and is stopped by the corselet beneath, and Diomedes over the great shield keeps reaching for his neck with the point of his spear (23.813–821; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). “And then the Achaeans, terribly afraid for Ajax, / bade them stop and take equal prizes”, and Achilles gives the sword of Asteropaeus to Diomedes (23.822–825; see [[asteropaeus]]). His duel with Hector in Book 7 was stopped too, by the heralds with their scepters, before either man had won (7.273–282; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). Walter Leaf finds the whole contest patched together from verses borrowed from other passages (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.798 and 23.811).

**The weight throw.** He rises with Polypoetes, Leonteus and Epeius to throw the lump of iron that Eetion used to hurl (23.826–838; see [[iron]] and [[eetion]]). Epeius throws first, and the Achaeans laugh; Leonteus throws second; “and third great Telamonian Ajax hurled it / from his strong hand, and overshot the marks of all” (23.839–843). Leaf reports that 23.843 was athetized in antiquity as borrowed from the Odyssey, where Odysseus' discus “flew beyond the marks of all” among the Phaeacians (on 23.843; Od. 8.192); he takes the σήματα for pegs marking each man's throw. Then Polypoetes throws beyond the whole gathering, as far as a herdsman's throwing stick flies over the herd, and his companions carry the prize to the ships (23.844–849; see [[polypoetes]] and [[the-herdsmans-throwing-stick]]).
