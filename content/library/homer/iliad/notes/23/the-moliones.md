οἴοισίν μʼ ἵπποισι παρήλασαν Ἀκτορίωνε, “Only with horses did the two sons of Actor drive past me” (23.638; see [[actor-of-elis]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). The dual names them as in Nestor's other tale, “the two sons of Actor, the Moliones” (Ἀκτορίωνε Μολίονε, 11.750). In the war of his youth they were “still boys”, whom their father Poseidon hid from his spear in a thick mist (11.709–710, 11.750–752; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[poseidon]]); at Buprasium they are grown, and they win the contest that Nestor did not. Nestor names every other rival; the twins he calls only by their father's name.

**By their numbers.** They got in front πλήθει, “by their numbers”, “grudging me the victory, / because the greatest prizes were left there” (23.639–640). Walter Leaf thinks the couplet “defies interpretation”, and reports the scholia's explanations: that they entered more than one chariot, that most of the starters conspired to give them the advantage, or, as Aristarchus held, citing Hesiod, that they were not twins like the Dioscuri but a double being, two bodies grown together, whom the people allowed to drive as one against Nestor's protest (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.639; see the translation's note on 23.639–640, [[aristarchus]] and [[hesiod]]). He also mentions Verrall's suggestion, that the twins, resenting Nestor's four victories to their one, held that the greatest prize had stayed with them after all.

**Twins.** “They were twins: the one held the reins steadily, / held the reins steadily, and the other urged them on with the whip” (23.641–642). δίδυμοι, “twins”, is found only here in the Iliad, which elsewhere calls twins διδυμάονε (the sons of Diocles, 5.548; the sons of Bucolion, 6.26) and gives the word to Sleep and Death (16.672); the repeated half-verse (ἔμπεδον ἡνιόχευεν, / ἔμπεδον ἡνιόχευʼ) makes the pair visible: one driver who never lets go of the reins, because another works the whip. A scholium explains ἔμπεδον as “firmly and safely”, and Leaf adds that the driver's attention was not divided between reins and whip (on 23.641). In Patroclus' race each man drives his own team, and the race turns on one driver's skill, as Nestor told his son (23.313–318; see [[nestor]]).
