**The race.** He rises third, “the son of Atreus, fair-haired Menelaus, / sprung from Zeus”, and yokes his own horse Podargus beside Agamemnon's mare Aethe (23.293–300; see [[aethe-and-podargus]] and [[agamemnon]]). He draws the third place, “Menelaus famed for his spear”, and on the way back drives behind Diomedes (23.355, 23.401; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]). Antilochus means to pass him where the road narrows. Menelaus is afraid and calls to him to hold in his horses, for the road will soon be wider, and when Antilochus drives on as if he had not heard, “he himself of his own will let up on the driving, / lest the single-hoofed horses collide in the road / and overturn the well-woven chariots” (23.425–437; see [[antilochus]] and [[chariot]]). He calls Antilochus the most baneful of mortals, tells him that he will not have the prize without an oath, and urges his own horses on: “for both of them are lacking in youth” (23.439–445). He comes in third, close behind Antilochus, and would have passed him if the course had been longer, for the strength of Agamemnon's mare was growing (23.514–527; see [[the-horse-and-the-wheel]]). Meriones is behind “famous Menelaus” by the cast of a spear (23.528–529).

**The charge.** When Achilles has given Eumelus a prize of his own and left the mare to Antilochus, Menelaus stands up, “grieving in his heart, / angry without end at Antilochus”, and a herald puts the scepter in his hand and bids the Argives be silent (23.566–569; see [[scepter]]). It is the form of an assembly or a trial, in which the scepter is carried by those who give judgment (1.238; 18.505).

{{quote:67a3dd5d-619a-55d2-9a25-fd9268d90704}}

He would not win the mare by his rank. Walter Leaf explains the parataxis: men would say that Menelaus takes the prize because, though his horses were beaten, he himself had the advantage in rank and power (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.577; see [[arete]]). So he proposes a judgment of his own that no Danaan can fault, “for the judgment will be straight”: let Antilochus stand before his horses and chariot, with the whip in his hand, and swear by Poseidon that he did not hamper Menelaus' chariot by a trick (23.579–585; see [[oath]]). Leaf takes δικάσω as “I myself will decide”, by bringing the matter to the test of an oath (on 23.579).

**The reply.** Antilochus will not swear; he pleads his youth and gives up the mare (23.586–597). Menelaus' heart is warmed, “as when there is dew around the ears / of a growing crop”, and the narrator turns to him: “so, Menelaus, was the spirit in your heart warmed” (23.597–600; see [[dew-on-the-growing-crop]] and [[apostrophe]]). He gives way in turn:

{{quote:87879209-0dea-52b5-ba67-d386faed31b2}}

In Book 3 Menelaus himself said that “the minds of younger men are always flighty” (3.108); here he excuses the young man as one who was “never flighty or light of mind / before” (23.603–604), and Leaf compares the passage (on 23.603). The suffering that Antilochus and Nestor and his brother have borne for his sake is the war, which is fought for Menelaus; Antilochus once came through the champions to stand beside him when he faced Aeneas, “for he feared greatly for the shepherd of the people” (5.565–572; see [[nestor]] and [[thrasymedes]]). The last words, “overbearing and harsh”, are the words in which Hera spoke of the heart of Zeus (15.94; Leaf on 23.611). Menelaus gives the mare to Noemon, Antilochus' companion, and takes the third prize, the cauldron (23.612–613; see [[noemon-companion-of-antilochus]], [[cauldron]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
