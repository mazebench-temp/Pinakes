summary: Hair cut for the dead. Achilles swears not to wash until he has cut his hair for Patroclus; the Myrmidons cut theirs and cover the body with it; and Achilles cuts the fair hair he had grown for the river Spercheius, as Peleus vowed for his homecoming, and puts it in his dead friend's hands (23.46, 23.135–153). The manes of Achilles' horses, which Patroclus used to wash, rest on the ground in mourning (23.282–284).

**The vow.** No water will come near Achilles' head, he swears, until he has laid Patroclus on the fire, heaped up a mound “and cut my hair” (23.44–46; see [[bathing]]). Walter Leaf takes the cut hair as the sign of mourning (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.46).

**The Myrmidons.** In the procession “they covered the whole corpse with their hair, which they cast on it / as they cut it off” (23.135–136). καταείνυσαν is “clothed”, a hyperbole (Leaf on 23.135; see the translation's note on 23.135). Leaf explains the custom as an offering of part of the living body, by which the mourner gives himself as an escort to the dead (on 23.135). In the Odyssey Peisistratus calls it the only γέρας, the word of 23.9, left to miserable mortals, “to cut one's hair and let a tear fall from one's cheeks” (*Odyssey* 4.197–198; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]), and the Danaans cut their hair at the death of Achilles (*Odyssey* 24.46).

**Achilles' lock.**

{{quote:4f39aadb-b40a-5106-bbc9-d5d7b067b052}}

ξανθὴν … χαίτην, “his fair hair” (23.141), is the hair by which Athena caught him in the first book, ξανθῆς … κόμης (1.197). He had grown it for the river of his country, and Peleus had vowed it with a hecatomb for his son's homecoming; since he will not go home, he gives it to Patroclus “to take with him”, and puts it in his hands (23.144–153; see [[spercheius]] and [[peleus]]). Leaf explains that a lock grown for a river was cut at coming of age, and that Achilles still wears it because he left home young; he compares Orestes in Aeschylus, who offers one lock to the river Inachus for his nurture and a second for mourning at his father's tomb (on 23.141; *Libation Bearers* 6–7; see [[libation-bearers]]). The custom lasted. Pausanias describes by the Cephisus a votive statue of a boy cutting his hair for the river, and takes Peleus' vow in Homer as proof that all the Greeks had done so from of old (Pausanias 1.37.3; see [[the-lock-for-the-spercheius]]). Plato's Socrates would not have it believed that Achilles gave the hair consecrated to the Spercheius to a dead man (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato]]).

**Manes.** χαίτη is also a horse's mane. Achilles' horses have lost the charioteer “who very often poured liquid olive oil / on their manes, after washing them with clear water”, and they stand mourning him, “and their manes / rest on the ground” (23.281–284; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). So in Book 17, as they wept for him with their heads bowed to the ground, “their rich manes were stained”, until Zeus breathed might into them and “the two shook the dust from their manes to the ground” (17.439–440, 17.457). In the race the manes of the teams stream in the wind (23.367; see [[horses]]).
