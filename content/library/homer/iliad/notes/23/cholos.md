---
summary: Achilles cuts the throats of twelve Trojans “in my anger over your killing” (23.23); the ghost remembers that Patroclus killed a boy “in anger over the knucklebones” (23.88); and at the games anger is roused and checked, in Diomedes' tears, in the quarrel of Idomeneus and the son of Oileus, and in Menelaus' charge against Antilochus.
---

The book begins with Achilles' anger. He vows at the bier to kill twelve Trojan children before the pyre, “in my anger over your killing” (23.22–23), and keeps his word (23.175–176; see [[human-sacrifice]]); the kings bring him to Agamemnon's hut “with difficulty, for he was angry at heart for his companion” (23.37). The ghost's own story begins with anger: Patroclus came to Peleus' house as an exile, having killed the son of Amphidamas, “a foolish child, not meaning to, in anger over the knucklebones” (23.88; see [[amphidamas-of-opus]]).

At the games the anger is smaller, and the games contain it. Diomedes weeps “in his anger” when Apollo strikes the whip from his hands (23.385). Idomeneus answers the son of Oileus “in anger”, and the son of Oileus rises “in anger, to answer him with harsh words”, until Achilles stops them (23.482, 23.488–498). Menelaus stands up “angry without end at Antilochus” (23.567), and yields to him “for all my anger” (23.602–603; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
