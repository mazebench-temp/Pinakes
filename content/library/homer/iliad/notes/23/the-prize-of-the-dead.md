---
summary: Achilles keeps the Myrmidons by the body of Patroclus with their horses and chariots, to “weep for Patroclus; for that is the prize of the dead” (23.9): the only time in the Iliad that the half-verse is said of lament, and the only time in the poem that it begins with ὅ.
passages:
  - 23.6-11 | Let us weep for Patroclus
---

**The lament as the prize.** In Book 16 the prize of the dead was a tomb and a pillar for Sarpedon (16.457, 16.675); here it is the weeping of his companions, before the burning and the mound (see [[lament]], [[myrmidons]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). The Myrmidons drive their horses three times around the body, and their tears wet the sands and their armor (23.12–16). The rest of the prize follows in the book, the burning, the mound and the games (see [[cremation]], [[tomb]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

**ὅ for τό.** The half-verse has τό five times, here ὅ, as again only once in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 24.190). Walter Leaf finds it difficult not to believe that ὅ was meant to be the same as τό, since the relative and the demonstrative stems were easily confused once the article as a relative had grown archaic (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.9; see the translation's note on 23.9).

**The prize of mourners.** In the Odyssey the young Peisistratus calls weeping the one γέρας left to wretched mortals, to cut the hair and let a tear fall from the cheeks for a man who has died (*Odyssey* 4.196–198; see [[hair]]). The Myrmidons do both for Patroclus, the tears at the start of the book and the hair in the procession (23.15–16, 23.135–136).
