**The chariot race.** The race is told at length, from the prizes to the last of them (23.262–652). The drivers rise one by one, Nestor teaches his son how to win with slow horses, and the race is followed both on the course, where the gods take a hand and Antilochus forces past Menelaus, and among the spectators, where Idomeneus and the son of Oileus quarrel over who is leading (23.287–498; see [[antilochus]], [[idomeneus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The finish leads to two disputes over the second prize, the mare: Antilochus' with Achilles, who means to give her to Eumelus, and Menelaus' with Antilochus, who won her by a trick (23.534–613; see [[eumelus]] and [[menelaus]]). There are five prizes for five drivers, and since Eumelus has been given something else the last is left over, to be given to Nestor (23.615–623; see [[nestor]]).

**The pattern.** The later contests are set out in much the same words. Achilles places the prizes in the gathering, stands, and speaks: “And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives” (23.657, 23.706, 23.752, 23.801, 23.830; first at 23.271 for the chariot race), and twice more he calls the contestants with “Rise up, you who will make trial of this prize too” (23.753, 23.831; for two, 23.707). The contestants rise, the contest is told, and the prizes are taken away. The spectators answer each time: they fall silent before Epeius' boast (23.676), gaze and are amazed at the wrestling and the archery (23.728 = 23.881), shout for Odysseus as he runs (23.766–767), laugh at Ajax with the dung in his mouth and at Epeius' throw (23.784, 23.840), fear for Ajax in the armed duel (23.822) and shout at Polypoetes' throw and Teucer's shot (23.847, 23.869).

**The contests.** When Achilles gives Nestor his prize, he names the contests the old man can no longer enter: boxing, wrestling, the javelin and the footrace (23.621–623), and Nestor names the same with the chariot race in his memory of the games of Amarynceus (23.634–638; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). Walter Leaf takes the two lists for an epic pentathlon, with the chariot race above the contests of bodily strength (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.621). The armed duel, the weight throw and the archery are not in either list, and Leaf did not count them among the older games: at the armed duel “we now leave poetry for patchwork”, and the archery, with a prize set beforehand for cutting the cord, he calls “the extreme of absurdity” (on 23.798, 23.850). Of the javelin, foretold in Achilles' words to Nestor (23.622), he allows “a certain dignity” in the way Agamemnon is honored without a contest in which he could not be allowed to lose (on 23.884).

**Before the games.** Leaf suggests that before the games were added, 23.257, “and when they had heaped up the mound they were going back. But Achilles”, ran on into the first verses of Book 24 (on 23.257). Aristarchus and Aristophanes athetized the list of prizes that Achilles brings out of the ships (23.259–261), which Leaf thinks they took for tautologous (on 23.259).
