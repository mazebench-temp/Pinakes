summary: Honor at the games is reckoned and spoken of. A tripod is “valued … at twelve oxen” and a skilled woman “at four oxen” (23.703–705); Nestor thanks Achilles for remembering “the honor with which it is fitting that I be honored among the Achaeans” (23.649); and Antilochus, beaten by Odysseus and Ajax, says that “still, even now, / the immortals honor older men” (23.787–788).
grc: [τιμῶσι]
en: [valued]
---

τίω is first “value” (23.703, 23.705), and the value is reckoned in cattle, as Glaucus' golden armor was worth a hundred oxen and Diomedes' bronze nine (6.235–236; see [[cattle]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). Walter Leaf thinks four oxen a low price for an accomplished woman, since Laertes gave twenty for Eurycleia (*Odyssey* 1.430–431), and supposes that captive women were plentiful in the camp (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.705). The cauldron of the javelin is “worth an ox” (23.885; see [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]).

Nestor's couplet is “another very obscure” one for Leaf, who takes τιμῆς as a genitive of price: Achilles remembers him at the value at which he ought to be valued (on 23.648; see the translation's note on 23.648–649). The prize that called it forth is honor without a contest, given to an old man who can no longer compete (23.618–623; see [[prize-of-the-old]], [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[geron]]).

Antilochus turns his own defeat into a compliment to his elders: Ajax is only a little older than he, Odysseus is of an earlier generation, and none but Achilles could match him in running (23.787–792). Leaf explains ἔτι καὶ νῦν, “still, even now”: as the gods honored them when they were young, so they honor them when they are old (on 23.787).
