summary: The chariot race is measured in throws. Antilochus' horses run ahead of Menelaus' mares by the range of a discus that a young man throws to test his strength, Menelaus had been left behind by the range of a discus before he caught up, and Meriones finishes behind Menelaus by the cast of a spear (23.429–437, 23.523, 23.529).

ὅσσα δὲ δίσκου οὖρα κατωμαδίοιο πέλονται, / ὅν τʼ αἰζηὸς ἀφῆκεν ἀνὴρ πειρώμενος ἥβης, / τόσσον ἐπιδραμέτην: “As far as the range of a discus swung from the shoulder carries, / which a man in his prime lets fly, testing his young strength, / so far they ran ahead” (23.431–433). οὖρα is a measured stretch, as in the furrow-length of mules by which Odysseus and Diomedes let Dolon get ahead of them (10.351–352; see the translation's note on 10.351–353). κατωμάδιος, “swung from the shoulder”, occurs only here; Walter Leaf compares κατωμαδόν, the stroke of the whip from the shoulder with which Hector drives on his horses (15.352), and points to the earlier throws for the next verse (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.431). πειρώμενος ἥβης, “testing his young strength”, is the third form of the phrase: the spear of Apollo's causeway is thrown σθένεος πειρώμενος, “to test his strength” (15.359), and the javelin of Book 16 πειρώμενος ἢ ἐν ἀέθλῳ, “trying his strength, either in a contest” or in war (16.590). The discus is a sport of the camp: the Myrmidons, while Achilles kept from the war, “amused themselves throwing the discus and the javelin” on the shore (2.773–774; see [[discus]]).

The measure is of ground taken by a trick. Antilochus has seen a narrow place where winter water broke away the road, drives off the road beside Menelaus, and will not hold in when Menelaus shouts that they will both come to grief; Menelaus lets up of his own will, for fear that the chariots will collide and overturn, and Antilochus' horses run on by the range of a discus while Menelaus' mares hang back (23.417–437; see [[antilochus]] and [[menelaus]]). Menelaus makes up nearly all of it before the finish: “At first, it is true, he was left behind as far as the range of a discus, / but he quickly caught up with him” (23.523–524), and he comes in as close behind as a horse is to the wheel of its own chariot (23.517–522; see [[the-horse-and-the-wheel]]). δίσκουρα, written as one word, occurs only here; Leaf notes that some ancient critics, offended by the compound, read δίσκου οὖρα, and he compares δημογέρων, “elder of the people”, for the form (on 23.523).

Then the spear: Meriones “was behind famous Menelaus by the cast of a spear” (δουρὸς ἐρωήν, 23.529). The words that measured Apollo's causeway and Achilles' leap from the river (15.358, 21.251) are here a plain measure of distance, without a simile, and the slowest team finishes a spear's cast behind. The games measure their distances by their own contests: the discus, the spear, and at the end the herdsman's throwing stick, by which Polypoetes outthrows them all (23.845–847; see [[the-herdsmans-throwing-stick]]).
