summary: Iris hears Achilles' prayer to the winds and, sent by no god, carries it to the house of the West Wind, where the winds are feasting. She will not sit with them, since she is on her way back to the Ethiopians' sacrifices by the streams of Oceanus, but gives them Achilles' request and promise, and they rise and go to the pyre.

**Unsent.** The pyre of Patroclus will not burn, and Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind, pouring libations from a golden cup (23.192–198; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[prayer]]). “And swiftly Iris, / hearing his prayers, went as messenger to the winds” (23.198–199). μετάγγελος, “messenger”, is said of her once more in Homer, when Hera calls out Apollo “and Iris, who is the messenger among the immortal gods”, and sends them to Zeus on Ida (15.143–148; see [[messenger-scene]]). Here no god sends her: Walter Leaf notes that this is the only errand Iris runs without being sent by a god (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.198). Once before she acted unbidden, when she led the wounded Aphrodite out of the throng (5.353). She carries a man's prayer to other gods.

**At the feast.** She finds the winds “all together in the house of the stormy West Wind, / feasting at a banquet”, and when she stands on the stone threshold they all spring up and each calls her to sit by him (23.200–203; see [[feast]]). Leaf finds an unmistakable touch of humor in the winds' party and their behavior to the goddess, perhaps a relief from the gloom of the funeral, perhaps the hand of an interpolator (on 23.200; see the translation's note on 23.200). She will not sit:

{{quote:1b141032-8c45-58bc-89fe-8d07c0818e7e}}

Patroclus refused a seat in nearly the same words when Nestor would have kept him in his hut, “But Patroclus on his side refused, and spoke a word: / ‘There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus’” (11.647–648); he too had an errand. Leaf supposes that Iris heard Achilles from the land of the Ethiopians and has come from there (on 23.206; see [[ethiopians]], [[oceanus]] and [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]); she is going back to share in their sacrifices (see [[hecatomb]] and [[sacrifice]]). Her message gives Achilles' prayer in his own terms, the request to come and the promise of “fine sacrifices” (ἱερὰ καλά, 23.195, 23.209; see [[message-repeated]]), and turns at the end to the winds themselves, “so that you may rouse the pyre to burn” (23.210). “So saying she went away, and they rose” (23.212): her usual parting verse ends “So saying, swift-footed Iris went away” (8.425, 11.210, 18.202; the same Greek at 24.188), but here the verse turns to the winds, who rise with a wondrous din and come to the pyre (23.212–216).
