summary: Glory in the race is a god's gift. Athena gives Diomedes back his whip, “put might into his horses and set glory on him” (23.400), and Antilochus, who has seen it, will not race the team to which Athena “has now held out speed, and has set glory on him” (23.406). After the footrace Antilochus' speech ends with a compliment to Achilles, and the narrator says that he “gave glory to the swift-footed son of Peleus” (23.793).
grc: [κύδηνεν]
en: [gave glory]
---

ἐπʼ αὐτῷ κῦδος ἔθηκε, “set glory on him”, occurs only in these two verses (23.400, 23.406). Elsewhere a god “holds out” κῦδος (κῦδος ὀρέξαι, 5.33, 5.260, 11.79, 17.453), and Antilochus' ὤρεξε τάχος, “has now held out speed”, gives the verb to the speed that wins it. The ancient critics athetized 405–406, asking how Antilochus could know of Athena's part; Walter Leaf finds the grounds insufficient (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.405; see [[athena]], [[divine-intervention]] and [[diomedes]]).

κυδαίνω, “give glory”, was Zeus' verb in Book 13, when he “wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector, / to give glory to Achilles, swift of foot” (13.347–348). At the games a young man does it with words: having lost the footrace to Odysseus, Antilochus says that no one but Achilles could beat him in running, and Achilles answers that the praise shall not be spoken in vain (23.787–796; see [[ainos]] and [[antilochus]]).
