summary: While Aphrodite keeps the dogs from Hector's body, Apollo brings a dark cloud down over it so that the sun does not parch it. In the chariot race he bears Diomedes a grudge and strikes the whip from his hands, and Athena undoes his work; Achilles names him as the god who grants a boxer the strength to outlast his man; and in the archery he begrudges the bird to Teucer, who has promised him nothing, while Meriones promises him a hecatomb of firstborn lambs and hits it.

**Hector's body.** Achilles has said that he will give Hector “to the dogs” (23.183; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Aphrodite keeps the dogs from him by day and by night and anoints him with oil (23.184–187; see [[aphrodite]]), and Apollo keeps the sun from him:

{{quote:7a5229af-2089-5510-8776-c088f36eb84a}}

κυάνεον νέφος, “a dark cloud”, is what he wrapped around Aeneas when he carried him out of the battle in Book 5: “and Phoebus Apollo took him up in his arms and protected him / in a dark cloud” (5.344–345; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[aeneas]]). Here it covers a dead man, and the danger is “the might of the sun” (23.190; see [[helios]]). Fäsi and Düntzer rejected 184–191 as anticipating Book 24. Walter Leaf reports von Christ's defense, that the emphatic Πατρόκλου of 192 implies another subject before it; he adds that Aphrodite acts here with Apollo because the two are the leading patrons of Troy, and that the cloud may be a way of saying that Phoebus, as sun-god, keeps the sun from the body. He finds the case against the lines weak except against 187, but thinks that they interrupt the story and would be better away (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.184; see the translation's note on 23.184–191). In Homer the Sun is a god of his own, invoked beside Zeus in the oaths of Book 3 (3.277; see [[helios]]); it was the allegorist Heraclitus, much later, who took Apollo for the sun (see [[allegory-of-apollo]]). In Book 24 Apollo alone guards the body: pitying the man even in death, he keeps every disfigurement from it and covers it with the golden aegis, so that Achilles may not tear it as he drags it (24.18–21). That last clause, ἵνα μή μιν ἀποδρύφοι ἑλκυστάζων, ends 23.187 too.

**The horses of Tros.** Diomedes yokes for the chariot race “the horses of Tros, of which he had once robbed / Aeneas, though Apollo saved the man himself” (23.291–292; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[diomedes]]): it was Apollo who took Aeneas from the field in Book 5 while Diomedes drove off his horses (5.319–327, 5.344–346, 5.445–446). ὑπεξεσάωσεν, “saved”, is found only here in Homer.

**The chariot race.** On the way back the horses of Tros press so close behind the mares of Eumelus that Diomedes “would have passed him or left the race in doubt, / had not Phoebus Apollo borne a grudge against the son of Tydeus, / and struck the shining whip from his hands” (23.382–384; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[eumelus]]). Diomedes weeps with anger as his horses run on “without the goad” (23.385–387). The poem gives no reason for the grudge. Leaf finds one in the mares, which Apollo had reared himself: “Apollo, god of the silver bow, had reared them in Pereia” (2.766; Leaf on 23.383; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]] and the translation's note on 23.383). So, he adds, the horse is at least as closely connected with Apollo as with Poseidon (see [[poseidon]]). Diomedes had met the god himself in Book 5, when he sprang three times at Aeneas, “though he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him”, and was warned off (5.432–444). κοτέσσατο, “bore a grudge”, is found in this form only here in Homer; Athena, going after Eumelus, is κοτέουσα, “in resentment” (23.391; see the translation's key-word row κότος, κοτέω). Apollo's trick does not stand: “Nor did Apollo escape Athena's notice as he cheated / the son of Tydeus”, and she gives the whip back (23.388–390; see [[athena]] and [[divine-intervention]]). ἐλεφηράμενος, “cheating”, recurs in Homer only in the *Odyssey*, of the dreams that come through the gate of ivory and cheat with words that are never fulfilled (ἐλεφαίρονται, *Odyssey* 19.564–565; Leaf on 23.388; see the translation's note on 23.388 and [[dream]]).

**The boxer's god.** For the boxing Achilles sets out a mule, to be led away by “the one whom Apollo / grants to outlast the other” (23.660–661; see [[contests]]). δώῃ καμμονίην, “grants to outlast”, is said again in Homer only by Hector before his duel with Achilles, “if Zeus grants me / to outlast you” (22.256–257). Leaf reports that the scholia make Apollo the god of boxing by his victory over Phorbas, king of the Phlegyans, who beset the road to Delphi and challenged the gods; he thinks that legend post-Homeric, and takes Apollo to be named as the god who brings young men to their strength. He compares Penelope's words, that Telemachus is now a man “by the will of Apollo” (Ἀπόλλωνός γε ἕκητι, *Odyssey* 19.86), and Hesiod's daughters of Tethys, who with lord Apollo and the Rivers bring boys to manhood (ἄνδρας κουρίζουσι σὺν Ἀπόλλωνι ἄνακτι / καὶ Ποταμοῖς, *Theogony* 346–348; Leaf on 23.660; see the translation's note on 23.660).

**The archers.** In the archery the god of the bow decides the contest:

{{quote:5c2ec00d-bc7b-5cf7-8f20-0f3a3eed78bc}}

That ἄνακτι, “the lord”, is Apollo we learn only from the name two verses later (Leaf on 23.863). ἠπείλησεν is a declaration made aloud, here a vow (see the translation's note on 23.863). Teucer's arrow cuts the cord, the dove flies up toward the sky, and Meriones, who has been holding an arrow ready, snatches the bow from his hand, for the two shoot with one bow (23.866–871; Leaf on 23.870; see [[teucer]] and [[meriones]]). “And at once he promised far-shooting Apollo / that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs” (23.872–873; see [[far-shooter]] and [[hecatomb]]), and he strikes the dove under the wing as she circles high under the clouds (23.874–876). The vow is the one that Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, bade Pandarus make before he shot at Menelaus, “and pray to Apollo, the Lycian-born, famed for his bow, / that you will offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs” (4.101–102; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]). Leaf takes the hecatomb of lambs for Apollo's fixed price for a successful shot (on 23.864). Antilochus had said as much of the chariot race: Eumelus “should have prayed / to the immortals” (23.546–547; see [[prayer]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Teucer has missed through Apollo before. When he shot at Hector in Book 8, “he missed again, for Apollo made it swerve” (8.311); in Book 15 it was Zeus who broke his string (15.461–465).
