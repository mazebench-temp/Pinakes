---
summary: At the funeral the speeches close in lament and obedience, “So he spoke, and they groaned aloud in a body” (23.12), “So he spoke, and in them all he roused the longing for lament” (23.108); at the games they close in the acts of the contest: “So he spoke, and then … rose” brings up the contestants (23.708, 23.811, 23.836, 23.859), and “So saying, he put it in his hands, and he received it with joy” hands over a prize (23.624, 23.797).
---

**Lament and obedience.** Achilles' first speech, to the Myrmidons, ends with their groaning, “and Achilles led them” (23.12); his oath to the kings ends with obedience, “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (23.54, again at 23.738), and his answer to the soul with an embrace that fails (23.99–100). “So he spoke, and in them all he roused the longing for lament” (23.108) is the verse with which Menelaus' memory of Odysseus moves his house to tears in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 4.183; [[the-longing-for-lament]]); its second half returns when Achilles puts his hair in the dead man's hands (23.152–153). “So he spoke, threatening” (23.184) closed Asteropaeus' challenge (21.161); here it closes Achilles' threat that Hector will go to the dogs, and the same verse denies it: “but no dogs busied themselves about him” (23.184–187; [[dogs]]).

**The horses obey.** Antilochus' horses and Menelaus' answer their masters' shouts with the verse of the Lycians who feared Sarpedon's rebuke, “So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke” (23.417, 23.446; 12.413), and both teams “ran harder” (23.418, 23.447).

**The games.** Achilles' call to each contest is answered by “So he spoke, and then … rose” for the wrestlers, the armed fighters, the throwers and the archers (23.708, 23.811, 23.836, 23.859), and by “So he spoke, and at once … rose” for Epeius and for the runners (23.664, 23.754); the same words bring the son of Oileus to his feet in anger at Idomeneus (23.488; [[contest-scene]]). Prizes change hands with “So saying, he put it in his hands, and he received it with joy” (23.624, 23.797; the verse of the corselet, 23.565, is shorter). The crowd answers in the closing verses: “So he spoke, and they all approved what he bade” (23.539), “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence” after Epeius' boast (23.676), and “So he spoke, and they all laughed heartily at him” after the son of Oileus' complaint (23.784), as the Achaeans laughed at Thersites (2.270). The last speech of the book ends with the verse of Agamemnon's obedience to Nestor in Book 2, “So he spoke, and Agamemnon, lord of men, did not disobey” (23.895; 2.441).

**ἦ ῥα καί.** “He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector” (23.24) repeats the verse of the defilement (22.395; [[brilliant]]). ἦ ῥα καί also runs Achilles' answer into his order to Automedon (23.563) and the two speeches of the reconciliation into the giving of the mare (23.596, 23.612; [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
