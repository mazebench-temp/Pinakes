summary: Achilles pities Eumelus, who comes in last “dragging his fine chariot” (23.533–534), and proposes to give him the second prize; Antilochus, who would lose it, answers: “But if you pity him and he is dear to your heart, / there is much gold in your hut” (23.548–549). Earlier the soul of Patroclus asks for Achilles' hand “in my grief” (23.75), and the Myrmidons weep at dawn around “the pitiable corpse” (23.110).

οἰκτίρω, the verb of both verses, is rare in the Iliad, and its last use is Achilles' pity for Priam's gray head in the next book (οἰκτίρων, 24.516). Here the pity costs Achilles nothing, since the prize is another man's, and Antilochus' answer puts the cost where it belongs: if Achilles pities Eumelus, let him give him something from his own gold, bronze, flocks, slave women and horses, so that the Achaeans may praise him (23.548–552; see [[ainos]]). Achilles smiles, “rejoicing in Antilochus”, and gives Eumelus the corselet he took from Asteropaeus (23.555–565; see [[eumelus]], [[antilochus]] and [[asteropaeus]]). The pity and its answer are the start of the dispute over the second prize (see [[themis]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

The soul's ὀλοφύρομαι is “I lament” or, as most take it, “I lament to you”, that is, “I beg you”; Aristarchus read it as a subjunctive, “let me lament” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.75; see the translation's note on 23.75 and [[psyche]]).
