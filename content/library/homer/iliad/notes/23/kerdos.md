summary: The chariot race is won by κέρδεα. Nestor tells his son that “he who knows crafty ways, though he drives worse horses”, keeps close to the turning post (23.322–325), and Antilochus comes in second “by crafty ways, not by speed, having got in ahead of Menelaus” (23.515). In the wrestling Odysseus stands up, “who knew crafty ways” (23.709).
grc: [κέρδεα, κέρδεσιν]
en: [crafty ways]
---

The plural κέρδεα is found in the Iliad only in these three verses. In the Odyssey it is the craft of Odysseus and of Penelope, “who knows crafty ways beyond all” (*Odyssey* 2.88), and Athena uses it of herself and Odysseus together, “since we both know crafty ways”, she famed among the gods “for cunning and for crafty ways” (*Odyssey* 13.296–299; see [[metis]]).

The craft Nestor teaches is a driver's: watch the post, turn close, hold the horses in from the start and keep an eye on the man in front (23.319–325; see [[turning-post]] and [[cunning-not-strength]]). What Antilochus does is something else. He forces his way past Menelaus where the road narrows, and Menelaus gives way rather than see both chariots overturned (23.415–437). The narrator's phrase, κέρδεσιν, οὔ τι τάχει γε (23.515), sets craft against speed, as Nestor set cunning against strength (23.315); Menelaus calls it δόλος, “a trick”, and asks for an oath (23.585; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

Odysseus' κέρδεα in the wrestling are a trick of the same kind. Lifted by Ajax, “Odysseus did not forget his trickery”: he struck the hollow of his knee and threw him (23.725–727; see [[odysseus]]). Walter Leaf takes δόλος here as Odysseus' own craft (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.725).
