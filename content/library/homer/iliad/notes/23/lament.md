summary: The lament for Patroclus is the men's. The Myrmidons weep around his body and Thetis rouses in them the longing for lament; Achilles leads it with his hands on his companion's breast; the soul of Patroclus itself comes lamenting; after the ghost and after the hair Achilles rouses the longing for lament in them all, until he asks Agamemnon to end it for the work of the pyre; and all night at the pyre he mourns as a father burning the bones of his son (23.9–17, 23.97–110, 23.152–157, 23.222–225).

**The prize of the dead.** Achilles keeps the Myrmidons by the body to “weep for Patroclus; for that is the prize of the dead”, and only “when we have taken our fill of deadly lament” will they unyoke and eat (23.9–11; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[myrmidons]]). τεταρπώμεσθα γόοιο is a phrase of this book, the next and the Odyssey; Walter Leaf notes that the word expresses satiety, but has not lost the sense of satisfaction (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.10; see the translation's note on 23.10). Achilles asks the ghost for the same, an embrace “and take our fill of deadly lament” (23.97–98), as Odysseus asks his mother's soul in the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.210–212; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]).

**The longing.** As the Myrmidons drive around the body, “Thetis roused among them the longing for lament” (23.14; see [[thetis]]), and Achilles leads the lament in the verses of Book 18, “laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion” (23.17–18 = 18.316–317; see [[achilles]]). After the ghost, and again after he has put his hair in the dead man's hands, “in them all he roused the longing for lament” (23.108, 23.153; see [[the-longing-for-lament]] and [[himeros]]). The soul of Patroclus too came “lamenting and weeping”, γοόωσά τε μυρομένη τε (23.106; see [[psyche]]). The lament is cut short only for the burial: “And now the light of the sun would have set on them as they mourned”, had not Achilles asked Agamemnon to send the army to its meal; “they may have their fill of lament, it is true”, but not now (23.154–158; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[agamemnon]]). Leaf compares Odysseus' argument for food in Book 19 (on 23.157; 19.155–172; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]). The verse of the setting sun is said in the Odyssey of the weeping of Odysseus and his son, and of Odysseus and his herdsmen (*Odyssey* 16.220, 21.226).

**At the pyre.** All night, as the winds blow on the pyre, Achilles mourns:

{{quote:f7714e69-14c6-5437-b3fc-b44b2abc24d9}}

ὀδύρεται and ὀδύρετο, “mourns” and “mourned”, set the father's lament and Achilles' side by side (see [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]). In this book it is men who lament, the Myrmidons, Achilles and the soul of Patroclus; the women's lament for Patroclus was Briseis', in Book 19 (19.282–302; see [[briseis]]). The book begins with the Trojans groaning in the city for Hector (23.1), and the last book ends with the women's laments over him, led by Andromache, Hecuba and Helen (24.719–776; see [[hector]]).
