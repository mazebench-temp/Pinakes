summary: The mules of Book 23 haul the wood for the pyre of Patroclus down from Ida, and are among the prizes of the games: the mare that is the second prize in the chariot race carries a mule foal, and the prize for the boxing is a hard-working mule, which Epeius claims before anyone dares to fight him (23.111–123, 23.260, 23.265–266, 23.653–669).
# οὐρῆες (23.115) and ἡμίονον, ἡμιόνου occur only in this book; ἡμιόνους (23.260) returns in Book 24.
grc: [οὐρῆες, ἡμιόνους, ἡμίονον, ἡμιόνου]
en: [mule]

**The wood.** At dawn Agamemnon “urged on mules and men to bring wood / from all the huts” (23.111–112), and the men set out with woodcutting axes and ropes, “and the mules went before them” (23.114–115). “Many a time they went uphill and downhill, sideways and aslant” (23.116): the four adverbs are formed for the occasion, and Walter Leaf notes that the line has become famous, perhaps beyond its merits, as an imitation of the sound of stamping feet (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.116; see the translation's note). On the slopes of Ida the men fell the oaks, split them, and bind them behind the mules, “and the mules tore up the ground with their feet, / longing for the plain, through the thick brushwood” (23.120–122). δατεῦντο, “tore up”, is the verb of the horses that tore a dead man apart with the tires of their wheels (20.394); Leaf takes it of the violence of their efforts, and ἐλδόμεναι πεδίοιο as their eagerness to reach the plain (on 23.121–122). The wood is thrown down on the shore where Achilles has planned the barrow, and the pyre is built of it (23.123–139; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[pyres]]). In Book 17 Menelaus and Meriones carried the body of Patroclus out of the battle as mules that have put their strong might into the work drag a beam or a ship's timber down a rocky mountain path (17.742–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]]); now the mules drag the timber for his pyre.

**The prizes.** Among the prizes that Achilles brings out of the ships are “horses and mules and the strong heads of oxen” (23.260). The second prize of the chariot race is a mare, “six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal in her womb” (23.266; see [[horses]]); βρέφος, “foal”, occurs only here in Homer. The prize of the boxing is the mule itself: Achilles “led in a hard-working mule and tethered it in the gathering, / six years old, unbroken, the hardest to tame” (23.654–655). Leaf takes the last words to mean that six years is the hardest age for breaking (on 23.266; see the translation's note on 23.266). ταλαεργός, “hard-working”, is the mule's epithet, three times in this contest (23.654, 23.662, 23.666); in the Odyssey the mares of Noemon and of Iphitus have hard-working mules beneath them, unbroken (*Odyssey* 4.635–637, 21.22–23). The value of the mule is shown by the order of the prizes: it goes to the winner, and the loser takes a two-handled cup (23.656, 23.663).

**Epeius' mule.** Before anyone else stands up Epeius lays hold of the mule and claims it: “Let him come near, whoever will carry off the two-handled cup; / but the mule, I say, no other of the Achaeans will lead away / by winning at boxing, since I claim to be the best” (23.666–669; see [[epeius]]). Only Euryalus faces him, and Epeius fells him with one blow (23.677–699; see [[the-leaping-fish]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
