---
summary: The sun would have set on the mourners around the pyre had Achilles not asked Agamemnon to send the army to its meal, and Apollo covers the place where Hector's body lies with a cloud, lest the might of the sun parch the skin.
---

“And now the light of the sun would have set on them as they mourned, / had not Achilles quickly stood beside Agamemnon and said” (23.154–155; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[sunset-lines]]). The verse comes twice more in Homer, both times in the *Odyssey*, of Odysseus weeping with his son and with his herdsmen (*Odyssey* 16.220, 21.226). That morning Dawn had appeared to the same mourners as they wept (23.109; see [[eos]]).

Apollo brings a dark cloud down over Hector's body, “and covered all the place / that the corpse lay on, lest the might of the sun should first / parch the skin around his sinews and limbs” (23.189–191; see [[apollo]] and [[aphrodite]]). μένος ἠελίοιο, “the might of the sun”, is said again in Homer only in the *Odyssey*, of the heat that drives a stag down from the woods to drink at the river (*Odyssey* 10.158–160). Walter Leaf suggests that the cloud may be a way of saying that Phoebus, as sun-god, keeps the sun from the body (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.184); but in Homer the Sun is a god of his own, who sees all things and hears all things (3.277; see the article above).
