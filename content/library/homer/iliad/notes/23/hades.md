---
summary: The god does not appear; his house is the place Patroclus cannot yet enter. Achilles greets the dead man “even in the house of Hades” at the start and at the end of the funeral rites. The ghost asks to be buried quickly so that it may pass the gates of Hades, since the souls keep it beyond the river and it wanders through the wide-gated house, and once it has had its portion of fire it will never come back from Hades. Achilles, waking, knows that even in the house of Hades there is some soul and phantom; at the pyre he sends his companion to Hades, and he bids the Achaeans keep the bones until he himself is hidden in Hades.
---

**Rejoice.** Achilles lays his hands on the breast of the dead: “Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the house of Hades” (23.19), and he says it again when the twelve Trojans are burning on the pyre (23.179; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]). καί, “even in Hades, where there is but little rejoicing indeed”, as Walter Leaf puts it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.19; see the translation's note on 23.19). εἰν Ἀΐδαο δόμοισι, “in the house of Hades”, is the phrase of Priam's fear for his sons, “already dead and in the house of Hades” (22.52), and it comes a third time in this book, when Achilles wakes from the ghost's visit (23.103).

**The gates and the river.** The ghost of Patroclus stands over the sleeping Achilles and asks: “Bury me as quickly as may be, so that I may pass the gates of Hades. / The souls keep me far away, the phantoms of the dead, / and they do not yet let me mingle with them beyond the river, / but I wander aimlessly through the wide-gated house of Hades” (23.71–74; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]], [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]). The gates of Hades are a byword in the poem: Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon that “brought down by me you will pass the gates of Hades” (5.646), and Achilles hates a man who hides one thing in his heart and says another “as the gates of Hades” (9.312). That the dead need burial to enter Hades is not the belief of the *Odyssey*, where the unburied Elpenor is among the souls (*Odyssey* 11.51–54) and the souls of the suitors go down while their bodies still lie uncared for (*Odyssey* 24.186–190). Nitzsch was inclined to athetize 72–74 for that reason; Leaf will not so manipulate the evidence, and sees in the difference the poems' differences of age and place (on 23.72). The river he takes to be presumably the Styx (8.369), though it may be Oceanus (on 23.73; see [[styx]] and [[oceanus]]). How a soul that has not passed the gates can wander through the house of Hades is not easy to see; Leaf takes εὐρυπυλὲς Ἄϊδος δῶ, “the wide-gated house of Hades”, which recurs in Homer only in the *Odyssey* (11.571), to mean no more than the threshold of the realm (on 23.74; see the translation's note on 23.71–74).

**No return.** “For never again / shall I come back from Hades, once you have given me my portion of fire” (23.75–76; see [[cremation]]). νίσομαι, “come back”, is the verb of a happy homecoming, and Leaf notes the pathos of it here (on 23.76; see [[nostos]]). The soul goes “beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering” (23.100–101), and Achilles springs up: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104; see [[o-popoi]], [[phrenes]] and the translation's note on 23.103–104).

**To Hades.** At the pyre Achilles holds the head of the body, “grieving; for he was sending his noble companion to Hades” (23.137). Ἄϊδος δέ is the suffix of direction printed as a separate word, Ἄϊδόσδε, as when the souls of Patroclus and Hector go to Hades (16.856, 22.362; see the translation's note on 22.309, 22.362, 22.456). When the bones have been gathered, he bids the Achaeans put them in a golden bowl “until I myself am hidden in Hades” (23.243–244; see [[the-golden-urn]] and [[tomb]]). Elsewhere in Homer the dative Ἄϊδι is the god who receives the dead, as when the wrath “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls” (1.3) or a victor claims “your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (5.654); here it is a place, “in Hades”, and Leaf takes the local use as a sign of later origin (on 23.244). The dead man goes “beneath the misty darkness” (23.51), ζόφον ἠερόεντα, the share that fell to Hades when the sons of Cronus divided the world, “and Hades got the misty darkness” (15.191; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]).
