summary: The fullest account of a burning in Homer, from the mourning to the mound. Patroclus' ghost asks for his “portion of fire”; the Myrmidons carry him out in armor and cover him with their hair; on a pyre a hundred feet each way Achilles lays him with the fat of sheep and cattle, jars of honey and ointment, four horses, two dogs and twelve Trojans; the pyre will not burn until the winds come at his prayer; and at dawn the fire is quenched with wine, the bones gathered into a golden bowl and a mound heaped around the pyre (23.45–257).

**The order of the rite.** Achilles names the parts of it in his oath, that no water will touch his head “until I have laid Patroclus on the fire and heaped up a mound / and cut my hair” (23.45–46), and in his request to Agamemnon that the wood be brought, “so that the tireless fire may burn him away / the sooner from our eyes” (23.52–53). The ghost of Patroclus asks for it as his due: he cannot pass the gates of Hades, “once you have given me my portion of fire” (23.71–76; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). πυρὸς λελάχητε is the verb of Hector's terms for his own body, that the Trojans might give him his portion of fire (7.79–80, 22.342–343). The book then tells the burial in order: the lament and the feast, the wood, the procession, the hair, the pyre, the offerings, the burning, the quenching, the bones, the mound (see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

**The procession.** The Myrmidons arm and mount their chariots, the horsemen in front and the foot soldiers behind, and carry the body between them (23.128–134). In the Odyssey the Achaeans move in armor around the pyre of Achilles, footmen and horsemen (*Odyssey* 24.68–70). They had already driven three times around the body the day before (23.13); Walter Leaf compares the driving of Hector three times around Patroclus' tomb in the last book (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.13; 24.14–18). The mourners cut their hair and cover the corpse with it, and Achilles adds his own lock (23.135–153; see [[hair]]).

**The pyre and the offerings.** The pyre is a hundred feet each way, and the body is laid on its top (23.164–165; see [[pyres]]). Before it the mourners flay many sheep and cattle, and Achilles takes the fat from them all and covers the corpse with it from head to foot, and heaps the carcasses around it (23.166–169). He sets against the bier two-handled jars of honey and ointment; κλίνων, “leaning”, implies to Leaf the pointed jar meant to be set in the ground or against a wall, a practice he finds surviving in the small pointed lekythoi set around the bier at Athens (23.170–171; on 23.171; see [[honey]]). Achilles' own body was burned in the clothes of the gods, with much ointment and sweet honey (*Odyssey* 24.67–68). Then the living: four horses, two of the nine table dogs, and twelve sons of the Trojans, killed with the bronze (23.171–176; see [[horses]], [[dogs]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). The horses and men lie at the pyre's edge, and Patroclus in the middle, so that his bones can be told apart (23.239–242).

**The burning.** Achilles lets in “the iron might of the fire, to feed on them” (23.177), and greets Patroclus: “all these the fire is eating along with you” (23.182). But the pyre will not burn until he prays to the North Wind and the West Wind and promises them sacrifices, so that “the dead might blaze with fire as quickly as may be, / and the wood be quick to burn” (23.192–198; see [[boreas]] and [[zephyrus]]). The winds come and fall on the pyre, “and the wondrous fire roared loud”, and they toss its flame all night, while Achilles pours wine on the ground and mourns (23.212–225; see [[libation]] and [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]). The fire dies at the rising of the morning star (23.226–228). In the Odyssey the mother of Odysseus tells him that this is the way of mortals when they die: the might of the fire destroys flesh and bones, and the soul flies off like a dream (*Odyssey* 11.218–222).

**The quenching and the bones.** At Achilles' bidding the leaders put out the pyre with sparkling wine, as far as the flame had come, gather the white bones into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, and lay them in the huts under a soft linen cloth (23.236–254; see [[the-golden-urn]] and [[wine]]). Leaf, citing Cicero and Pliny, says that quenching the pyre with wine was a practice of Rome as of Greece, and finds it in the Twelve Tables' rule against a costly sprinkling, ne sumptuosa respersio (on 23.237; Cicero, *On the Laws* 2.60). The fat, he explains, was a double layer to keep out air and moisture (on 23.243). Hector's pyre is quenched in the same words, and his bones are gathered into a golden chest (24.791–795); Achilles' bones were gathered at dawn in unmixed wine and ointment (*Odyssey* 24.72–73).

**The mound.** Then “they marked out the circle of the mound and laid its foundations / around the pyre” (23.255–256): τορνώσαντο, “drew the circle”, and θεμείλια, which Leaf takes for a ring of upright stones facing the foot of the mound, like the circle of stones around the graves at Mycenae; he compares Herodotus' tomb of Alyattes, with a base of great stones and the rest a mound of earth (on 23.255; Herodotus 1.93.2; see [[tomb]]). The mound is heaped around the pyre, as Nestor proposed for the war dead in Book 7 (ἀμφὶ πυρήν, 23.256; 7.336). The bones are not in it, and Leaf calls it a cenotaph (on 23.254).

**The feast and the games.** The burial is framed by meals and contests. The Myrmidons have their funeral feast beside the body before the burning, τάφος being in Homer the funeral, never the tomb (23.29; Leaf on 23.29; see [[feast]]), and when Hector is buried his people feast in Priam's house (24.802–803). After the mound Achilles holds games, as the sons of Amarynceus did for their father and as Thetis did for her son (23.257–897, 23.630–631; *Odyssey* 24.85–92; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[contests]]).
