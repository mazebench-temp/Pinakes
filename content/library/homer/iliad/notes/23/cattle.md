summary: The cattle of Book 23 are slaughtered for the funeral feast and before the pyre, set out as prizes, and used as the measure of the prizes' worth; in the footrace the son of Oileus slips in the dung of the oxen killed for Patroclus, and stands with the horn of his prize ox in his hand, spitting out the dung (23.30, 23.166, 23.260, 23.703–705, 23.750, 23.773–784, 23.885).

**The funeral.** On the evening after Hector's death Achilles gives the Myrmidons a funeral feast: “Many sleek oxen bellowed around the iron / as their throats were cut, and many sheep and bleating goats” (23.30–31; see [[sheep]], [[goats]] and [[boar]]). ἀργοί is rendered “sleek”, not “white”: Walter Leaf reports the T scholia, who note that white victims are not offered to the dead and suppose that these oxen were killed for the feast of the living, not for Patroclus, and he takes ἀργός of oxen as “glistening” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.30). ὀρέχθεον occurs only here, and its sense is uncertain: the ancient explanations were “bellowed”, “stretched themselves out” in death, and “were cut up” (see the translation's note on 23.30). Leaf notes that this and 18.34 are the only places where a knife of iron is mentioned (on 23.30; see [[iron]]). At the pyre the next day “many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns / they flayed before the pyre and were busy over them”, and Achilles covers the dead man with their fat from head to foot (23.166–169; see [[cremation]]). The verse is the one in which Phoenix's kinsmen slaughtered their cattle to keep him at home (9.466).

**The prizes.** Achilles brings out of the ships “horses and mules and the strong heads of oxen” (23.260). The ox measures what the prizes are worth: the tripod for the wrestling “the Achaeans valued among themselves at twelve oxen”, and the woman skilled in handiwork at four (23.703–705; see [[tripod]]), and the cauldron set out for the javelin is “worth an ox” (βοὸς ἄξιον, 23.885; see [[cauldron]]). So Glaucus gave golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.236), Lycaon was sold in Lemnos for the worth of a hundred oxen (21.79), and in the Odyssey Laertes paid twenty oxen for Eurycleia (*Odyssey* 1.430–431); Leaf finds four oxen a low price for a woman so accomplished (on 23.705). The second prize of the footrace is an ox itself, “great and rich with fat” (23.750). The straps that Diomedes gives Euryalus for the boxing are “the well-cut straps of an ox of the fields” (23.684).

**The dung.** As the runners come to the prize, Athena makes the son of Oileus slip “where the dung was spread from the loud-bellowing oxen that were slain, / which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus; / and his mouth and nostrils were filled with the dung of oxen” (23.774–777; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[athena]]). ἐρίμυκοι, “loud-bellowing”, are the oxen that tread out the barley on a threshing floor in the simile of Achilles' chariot (20.495–497; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]). Odysseus takes the mixing bowl, “and shining Ajax took the ox. / And he stood holding in his hands the horn of the ox of the fields, / spitting out the dung” (23.779–781); Leaf compares Epeius laying hold of his mule (on 23.780; 23.666). He blames the goddess who stands by Odysseus like a mother, and all the Achaeans laugh at him (23.782–784; see [[like-a-mother]]).

**The herd.** In the last simile of the book Polypoetes outthrows the gathering by as far as a herdsman hurls his throwing stick, which flies spinning through the cattle of the herd (23.845–846; see [[the-herdsmans-throwing-stick]]).
