summary: Eumelus is the first to rise for the chariot race, with the mares that Apollo reared. He leads on the way back, with Diomedes close behind, until Athena breaks his yoke and he is thrown; he comes in last, dragging his chariot. Achilles pities him and would give him the second prize, but Antilochus will not give it up, and Achilles gives Eumelus the corselet he took from Asteropaeus (23.288–289, 23.354, 23.375–397, 23.480–481, 23.532–565).

**The mares.** He is the first to rise for the chariot race, “Eumelus, lord of men, / the dear son of Admetus, who excelled in horsemanship” (23.288–289; see [[admetus]] and [[lord-of-men]]), with the mares that the Catalogue called the best horses at Troy, reared by Apollo in Pereia (2.763–767; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]]). He draws the second place, “powerful Eumelus” (23.354). When the horses turn for home, “the swift-footed mares of the grandson of Pheres took the lead”, and the horses of Tros come so close behind them that their breath warms his back and shoulders (23.375–381; see [[pheres]], [[diomedes]] and [[horses]]).

**Apollo and Athena.** Diomedes “would have passed him or left the race in doubt”, had not Apollo, who bore a grudge against Diomedes, struck the whip from his hand (23.382–384; see [[apollo]]). Walter Leaf explains the god's partiality: he had reared Eumelus' mares himself (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.383). Athena saw it, gave Diomedes back the whip and put might into his horses, and then turned on Eumelus (23.388–390; see [[athena]] and [[divine-intervention]]):

{{quote:5de1fb28-d1f3-57e7-a468-11b3caeae967}}

The last verse is the one that told how Antilochus took the news of Patroclus' death, speechless, his eyes full of tears (17.695–696; see [[antilochus]]). Leaf notes that the horses are harnessed only by the yoke, without traces (on 23.393; see [[chariot]]): when it breaks, nothing holds them to the chariot.

**Seen from afar.** In the gathering Idomeneus cannot see the mares that led at the turn and guesses that their driver lost the reins and was thrown and broke his chariot; the son of Oileus answers that the mares in front are still “those of Eumelus, and he stands in the chariot holding the reins” (23.459–468, 23.480–481; see [[idomeneus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Idomeneus is nearer the truth.

**Last.** “And the son of Admetus came in last of all the others, / dragging his fine chariot, driving his horses in front of him” (23.532–533). Achilles pities him, and judges him by his skill, not by his place: “The best man is driving in his single-hoofed horses last. / But come, let us give him a prize, as is fitting, / the second” (23.534–538; see [[achilles]] and [[pity]]). Antilochus will not give up the second prize, the mare: Eumelus should have prayed to the immortals, and if Achilles pities him, let him give him something from his own store (23.543–554). Leaf notes that prayer is answered in the later contests, for Odysseus and for Meriones (on 23.546; 23.770–772, 23.872–876). So Achilles gives him the corselet he took from Asteropaeus, “of bronze, around which a casting of shining tin / runs in a circle; it will be worth much to him”, and Automedon fetches it from the hut and puts it in Eumelus' hands (23.558–565; see [[asteropaeus]], [[corselet]] and [[automedon]]). The last verse, “and he received it with joy” (23.565), is the verse of Nestor receiving his prize (23.624), and Leaf thinks it interpolated from there, as the manuscripts show (on 23.565). Eumelus does not speak in the Iliad.
