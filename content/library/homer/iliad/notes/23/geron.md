summary: Youth and age are measured against each other at the games. Achilles gives Nestor a prize without a contest, “for already grievous old age presses upon you” (23.623), and Nestor answers, “now let younger men take on / such deeds; and I must yield to grievous old age” (23.643–644). Antilochus asks Menelaus' pardon because “I am much younger / than you” (23.587–588), and after the footrace he says that “the immortals honor older men”, since Odysseus, who beat him, is “in green old age” (23.788–791).

**The young.** Antilochus is the young man of the book. His father begins his advice with “young as you are” (νέον περ ἐόντα, 23.306), and the narrator says that he “beat all the young men in running” (23.756), where Walter Leaf takes αὖτε to set the champion of the young against the veterans (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.756). His excuse to Menelaus is the young man's mind, quick and thin in counsel (23.589–590; see [[metis]] and [[noos]]), as Menelaus himself had said in Book 3 that “The minds of younger men are always flighty” (3.108). Menelaus answers in kind, “this time youth has overcome your mind” (23.604), where νεοίη is a strange form and the alliteration νόον νίκησε νεοίη, as Leaf reports from Monro, suggests a proverb (on 23.604; see [[nike]]). The taunts of the spectators turn on age too: Ajax, son of Oileus, tells Idomeneus that he is not by so much the youngest of the Argives, nor the sharpest of sight (23.476–477), and Leaf recalls that Idomeneus was “half gray” (μεσαιπόλιος, 13.361; on 23.476). Even horses age: Menelaus tells his own that Antilochus' horses “are lacking in youth” (23.445).

**The old.** γέρων is Peleus, “the old man” whose prayer for his son's homecoming went unanswered (23.149; see [[nostos]]), and Nestor, whom Achilles addresses as γέρον (23.618). Old age “presses upon” Nestor (ἐπείγει, 23.623), and he must “yield” to it (γήραϊ λυγρῷ πείθεσθαι, 23.644–645); Leaf compares Achilles' “let us yield to the hateful feast” (23.48; on 23.48). Nestor's wish, εἴθʼ ὣς ἡβώοιμι, “If only I were young like that” (23.629), begins his stories of youth in Book 7 and Book 11 as well (7.157, 11.670), and in the Odyssey the disguised Odysseus uses it to begin and to end a story of his own (*Odyssey* 14.468, 14.503; see [[old-age-and-memory]], [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]] and [[ainos]]).

**Green old age.** ὠμογέρων, “in green old age” (23.791), is found only here in Homer. Leaf takes it as early old age, a metaphor from unripe fruit, and compares ἐν ὠμῷ γήραϊ in the Odyssey, where it must mean premature old age (*Odyssey* 15.357; on 23.791; see the translation's note on 23.791). Antilochus' compliment makes Odysseus the oldest of the three runners and the winner, and Achilles rewards it (23.787–796; see [[time]] and [[kydos]]).
