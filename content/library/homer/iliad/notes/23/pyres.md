---
summary: The pyre of Patroclus, the greatest in the poem, a hundred feet each way, built on the shore from oaks cut on Ida. The body is laid on its top with the offerings around it; it will not burn until the North and West Winds fall on it, and it burns all night; at dawn it is quenched with wine, and the mound is heaped around it (23.110–257).
# πυρῇ / πυρὴ (23.165, 23.172, 23.174, 23.192, 23.216, 23.241), πυρκαϊὴν (23.225, 23.237, 23.250) and πυρκαϊὴ (23.228)
# are found in Homer only in this book and of Hector's pyre (24.787, 24.791).
grc: [πυρῇ, πυρκαϊὴν, πυρκαϊή]

**The wood.** At dawn Agamemnon sends mules and men to the slopes of Ida, and they cut high-leafed oaks with their axes, split them and bind the logs behind the mules (23.110–122; see [[ida]], [[mules]] and [[axe]]). Walter Leaf thinks it safer to see in the oaks no more than the forests of the Troad, which are almost all of oak (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.118). The wood is thrown down on the shore, “where Achilles / had planned a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself” (23.125–126; see [[tomb]]), and after the procession those who care for the dead heap it up (23.138–139, 23.163).

**A hundred feet.** ποίησαν δὲ πυρὴν ἑκατόμπεδον ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα, “and made a pyre a hundred feet this way and that”, “and on the top of the pyre they laid the body, grieving at heart” (23.164–165). Leaf notes that the temple at Athens that preceded the Parthenon was called the Hecatompedon (on 23.164). The first words of the second verse are said again of Hector's pyre, ἐν δὲ πυρῇ ὑπάτῃ νεκρὸν θέσαν (24.787). Around the body the carcasses of the victims are heaped, the jars set against the bier, and the horses, dogs and men thrown on the pyre (23.166–176; see [[cremation]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). Achilles twice stands “apart from the pyre” to act alone, to cut his hair and to pray to the winds (23.141, 23.194; see [[hair]] and [[prayer]]).

**The burning.** “But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not burn” (23.192), until the winds, at Iris' word, came and “fell on the pyre, and the wondrous fire roared loud” (23.216; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[iris]]). All night they tossed its flame, and Achilles dragged himself beside it (23.217–225; see [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]); “then the pyre was dying down, and the flame ceased” at the rising of the morning star (23.226–228). The bones of Patroclus lay in its middle, those of the horses and men at its edge (23.241–242).

**Quenched and covered.** At Achilles' word the leaders put the pyre out with sparkling wine, as far as the fire had reached (23.237–238, 23.250–251), and when the bones are gathered they draw the circle of the mound and heap the earth “around the pyre” (23.255–256; see [[tomb]]). So the Achaeans had heaped one mound around the pyre of their dead in Book 7 (7.336, 7.435), and so the Trojans quench Hector's pyre in the last book (24.791–792).
