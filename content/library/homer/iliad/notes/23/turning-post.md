---
summary: Nestor describes the turning post to his son, a dry stump of oak or pine with two white stones leaning against it, perhaps the mound of a man long dead or a post set up by earlier men, and tells him how to drive close around it (23.309, 23.323–345). Achilles marks it and sets Phoenix beside it as a lookout (23.358–361); Idomeneus guesses that the leader came to grief there (23.462–466); and the runners of the footrace start from the post (23.757–758).
# The τέρμα forms are claimed here, within the Iliad: τέρματʼ at Od. 8.193 are the marks of a discus throw, and τέρματα
# (22.162) is claimed by the simile racehorses-at-the-turning-post. Unnamed: the stump and its stones (23.327-331), and
# the drive around it (23.334-337, 23.339-341).

**The sign.** Antilochus knows “how to wheel around the turning posts”, his father says, but his horses are the slowest in the race, and he must win by cunning: the good driver “always watches the turning post and turns close” (23.309–310, 23.323; see [[antilochus]], [[nestor]] and [[metis]]). Then Nestor gives him a sign by which to know it:

{{quote:d2da01d9-2800-51c1-a286-f8dc32947c14}}

σῆμα is used twice, of the sign Antilochus is to note (23.326) and of a grave mound (23.331; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Walter Leaf calls the passage “hopelessly obscure”: Nestor seems to know where Achilles will set the post, and that the ground around it is smooth (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.326). He reports Heyne's remark that there is no evidence in antiquity for wooden posts as grave monuments (on 23.331). The stump that “does not rot in the rain” (23.328) was a problem for ancient critics (see [[the-stump-that-rots]]). Whatever it was, a mound of the dead or a post of earlier men, Achilles makes it the turning post of the race for his own dead companion (23.333; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

**Around the post.** The turn is to the left. Antilochus is to drive close, lean a little to the left in the chariot, goad on the right-hand horse with a shout and give it rein, and let the left-hand horse press close to the post, “so that the hub may seem to graze its edge, / the hub of the well-made wheel; but avoid touching the stone, / lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot” (23.334–341; see [[chariot]]). Leaf notes that ἐγχρίμπτω, “press close”, implies bringing near, not touching (on 23.334). If he gets past the others at the post, no one will catch him, not even with Arion or the horses of Laomedon (23.344–348; see [[arion-the-horse]] and [[laomedon]]). Antilochus drew the first lot, and Leaf takes him to have had the inside place at the start (on 23.358).

**The lookout.** When the drivers stand in a row, “Achilles pointed out the turning post / far off on the smooth plain”, and set Phoenix beside it “so that he might mark the running and report the truth” (23.358–361; see [[phoenix]]). Leaf reports that critics rejected 23.360, or all of 23.359–361, since the lookout is never heard of again, not even when fair play is disputed (on 23.360).

**The turn unseen.** The narrative passes over the turn: the next thing told is the last stretch back toward the sea (23.373–375; Leaf on 23.373). From his lookout Idomeneus says that he saw the mares that led “first swinging around the turning post”, and wonders whether their driver “could not / hold them well around the turning post, and failed to wheel” (23.462–466; see [[idomeneus]]). Leaf reports that Kurtz and many others reject 23.462–464, since the scene assumes that the far turn could not be seen (on 23.462). What had happened was told already: Athena broke the yoke of Eumelus on the way back (23.391–397; see [[eumelus]]).

**The footrace.** The same verse sets out the runners: “And they stood in a row; and Achilles pointed out the turning post” (23.757 ≈ 23.358), and they start from the νύσσα, here the line from which they run (23.758; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). Leaf reports that Aristarchus marked 23.757 as transferred from the chariot race (on 23.757).
