**The wrestling.** For the wrestling “up stood Odysseus of many wiles, who knew crafty ways” against great Telamonian Ajax (23.708–709; see [[ajax]], [[of-many-wiles]] and [[kerdos]]). They grip each other like the rafters of a high house, their backs creak and the welts rise red on their ribs and shoulders, but neither can throw the other, until they begin to vex the watching Achaeans and Ajax proposes that each lift the other in turn (23.710–724; see [[the-rafters-of-a-high-house]]).

{{quote:398793e3-97ef-5d0b-a7b4-eb5836f7d6c3}}

Walter Leaf takes δόλου, “his trickery”, for Odysseus' own cunning, not a trick of Ajax: lifted off the ground, he kicks Ajax behind the knee (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.725). When it is his turn he can barely move Ajax from the ground, and they fall together; Achilles stops them before a third fall and gives the victory to both, “take equal prizes” (23.729–737). Leaf observes that Odysseus, who put his man on his back, might have been thought the winner, and that it is not said how the tripod and the woman were divided (on 23.736). In the Odyssey Menelaus remembers how Odysseus once wrestled Philomeleides on Lesbos and threw him hard, and all the Achaeans rejoiced (Od. 4.341–344).

**The footrace.** He rises second for the footrace, “Odysseus of many wiles” again (23.755), and runs close behind the son of Oileus, as close as the rod to a weaving woman's breast, treading in his footprints before the dust can settle, “and all the Achaeans shouted / for him as he strove for victory” (23.759–767; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-weaving-womans-rod]]). In the last stretch he prays in his heart to Athena, “Hear me, goddess, come as a good helper to my feet”, and she makes his limbs light, his feet and his hands above (23.768–772; see [[athena]], [[prayer]] and [[hear-me]]). Ajax slips in the dung, and “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus took up the mixing bowl, / since he came in first” (23.773–779; see [[mixing-bowl]]). Ajax complains that the goddess “stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (23.783), as she came to him in the assembly of Book 2 to stop the flight to the ships (2.166–182). In the Odyssey he tells the Phaeacians that only in running does he fear that one of them might pass him, so badly has the sea worn him down (Od. 8.230–233).

**In green old age.** Antilochus, who comes in last, says that the gods honor older men: Ajax is only a little older than he, “but this man is of an earlier generation and of earlier men; / they say he is in green old age; and it is hard / for the Achaeans to contend with him in running, except for Achilles” (23.787–792; see [[antilochus]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). ὠμογέρων, “in green old age”, occurs only here; Leaf takes it as “in early old age”, a metaphor from unripe fruit (on 23.791).
