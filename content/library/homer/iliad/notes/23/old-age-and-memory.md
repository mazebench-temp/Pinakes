---
summary: The games make old age visible. Achilles gives Nestor a prize without a contest because “grievous old age presses upon you”, and Nestor answers with his last memory of youth, the games of Amarynceus, and yields to age (23.618–650). Antilochus excuses himself to Menelaus as the younger man, and when the old Odysseus beats him in the footrace he says that “the immortals honor older men” (23.587–590, 23.787–792).

  - 23.618-650 | Nestor's prize and his memory

**The old man's prize.** The fifth prize of the chariot race is left over, and Achilles gives it to Nestor “just as it is”, without a contest: “for you will not fight with fists, nor wrestle, / nor enter the javelin throw any more, nor run / with your feet; for already grievous old age presses upon you” (23.621–623; see [[nestor]] and [[contests]]). χαλεπὸν … γῆρας, “grievous old age”, is a weight that presses (ἐπείγει). In Book 4 Nestor called counsel “the prize of the old” (4.323); here the old man is given a prize in the literal sense, for his years (see [[prize-of-the-old]] and [[geras]]).

**The memory.** Nestor grants all of it: his limbs are no longer steady, his arms no longer dart out lightly from his shoulders (23.626–628). Then he wishes himself young, with the verse that belongs to all his tales of youth (23.629; 7.157, 11.670), and remembers the games of Amarynceus, where he won every contest Achilles has just named and lost only the chariot race (23.630–642; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). The tale ends in the present, with an old man's acceptance: “So I was once; but now let younger men take on / such deeds; and I must yield to grievous old age, / but then I stood out among heroes” (23.643–645). In Book 11 the same kind of ending carried a doubt, “Such was I among men, if that was ever so” (11.762; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]]); here it carries none. The memory is not used to persuade anyone, as it was in Books 7 and 11 (see [[mythological-paradigm]]); it is the old man's answer to being honored.

**Being remembered.** Memory runs both ways in the scene. The old man remembers his youth, and he is glad that the young man remembers him: “my heart rejoices / that you always remember me, a gentle friend, and I am not forgotten by you, / nor the honor with which it is fitting that I be honored among the Achaeans” (23.647–649; see [[time]]). The bowl itself is a memorial, μνῆμα, of the funeral of Patroclus (23.619). Achilles, who will not grow old, honors the old man and stays to hear “all the praise of the son of Neleus” (23.651–652).

**Young and old.** The quarrel after the race is settled by an appeal to age. Antilochus gives way to Menelaus as the younger man: “for I am much younger / than you, lord Menelaus, and you are the elder and the better. / You know what the transgressions of a young man are like; / for his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin” (23.587–590; see [[antilochus]] and [[menelaus]]). Menelaus accepts the excuse: “this time youth has overcome your mind” (23.604). In the footrace “the son of Nestor, / Antilochus; for he, for his part, beat all the young men in running” (23.755–756) runs against Odysseus and the son of Oileus; Walter Leaf takes αὖτε to set the champion of the young against the veterans (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.756). Odysseus wins with Athena's help, and Antilochus, last, turns his defeat into praise of age (see [[odysseus]] and [[athena]]):

ὠμογέρων, “in green old age” (23.791), is found only here in Homer; Leaf explains it as “in early old age”, a metaphor from unripe fruit (on 23.791; see the translation's note on 23.791). Antilochus' praise ends with Achilles, whom no Achaean but he can match in running, and Achilles rewards it with half a talent of gold, “your praise shall not be spoken in vain” (23.792–796; see [[achilles]]); he had listened to the end to the praise of Antilochus' father (23.652).
