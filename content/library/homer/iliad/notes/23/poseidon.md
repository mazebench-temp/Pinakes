---
summary: Each time Poseidon is named in the book, it is with horses. Achilles' immortal horses were his gift to Peleus; with Zeus he loved Antilochus and taught him horsemanship; and Menelaus bids Antilochus swear by the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth, with his hand on his horses, that he did not hamper Menelaus' chariot by a trick, an oath Antilochus declines to take. Walter Leaf denies that Homer knows Poseidon as a god of horses, and explains the oath by Poseidon's descent to Antilochus' house.
---

**The gift to Peleus.** Achilles will not drive in his own games: “For you know how far my two horses surpass the rest in prowess; / for they are immortal, and Poseidon gave them / to my father Peleus, and he in turn put them into my hands” (23.276–278; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[peleus]]). Elsewhere the poem says only that the gods gave them, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381 = 16.867), and Zeus himself grieves over them, “Ah, wretched pair, why did we give you to lord Peleus, / a mortal” (17.443–444). Walter Leaf calls this the only passage in Homer where Poseidon is brought into any special relation with the horse (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.277; see the translation's note on 23.277). Poseidon does have horses of his own: in Book 13 he yokes his bronze-footed team and drives it over the waves (13.23–31), and in Book 8 he unyokes the horses of Zeus on Olympus (8.440; see the notes on Books 8 and 13).

**Antilochus' teachers.** Nestor tells his son: “they have loved you, / Zeus and Poseidon, and have taught you horsemanship / of every kind” (23.306–308; see [[antilochus]], [[nestor]] and [[zeus]]). Aristarchus preferred the singular verb, ἐδίδαξε, on the ground that Poseidon alone had to do with horsemanship. Leaf calls that untenable: Poseidon, in his view, has no special connection with the horse in Homer, and teaches the race of Neleus useful arts because he is its ancestor (on 23.307). Of Apollo, who reared the mares of Eumelus, Leaf says that the horse is at least as closely connected with him as with Poseidon (on 23.383; see [[apollo]]).

**The oath.** When Antilochus has taken the second prize by a trick, Menelaus demands the oath that is “the custom” (ἣ θέμις ἐστί, 23.581; see [[themis]] and [[oath]]): “stand in front of your horses and chariot, and hold the whip / in your hands, the slender one with which you drove before, / and touching the horses, by the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth, / swear that you did not willingly hamper my chariot by a trick” (23.582–585; see [[menelaus]], [[earth-holder]] and [[shaker-of-the-earth]]). The pair of titles is found six times more in the Iliad, always in the narrator's words except once in Zeus' (15.222): so the envoys go along the shore to Achilles' hut, “praying earnestly to the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth” (9.182–183; see [[embassy]]; also 13.43, 13.59, 13.677, 14.355). Leaf reports that the passage has often been quoted to show that Poseidon was for Homer, as for later Greece, the god of horses, but finds the other signs of it too doubtful to rest anything on. For him Poseidon is the natural god for Antilochus to swear by, as the god of his house and the father of his grandfather Neleus (on 23.584; see the translation's note on 23.584 and [[neleus]]). The *Odyssey* tells how “the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth” took the likeness of the river Enipeus and lay with Tyro, and she bore Pelias and Neleus (*Odyssey* 11.235–257). Antilochus does not swear. He gives up the mare rather than “fall from your heart and be an offender against the gods” (23.594–595), and Leaf takes ἀλιτρός, “offender”, as a confession that he cannot take the oath (on 23.595; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and the translation's note on 23.595).
