---
summary: At dawn Agamemnon sends mules and men under Meriones to the slopes of Ida “of the many springs”, where they fell high-leafed oaks for the pyre of Patroclus and bring the logs down to the shore (23.110–126).
---

Achilles had asked for the wood the night before: “and at dawn urge them on, Agamemnon, lord of men, / to bring wood” (23.49–50; see [[agamemnon]]). At dawn the woodcutters set out with Meriones in charge (23.110–113; see [[meriones]]): “And they went holding woodcutting axes in their hands / and well-plaited ropes; and the mules went before them. / Many a time they went uphill and downhill, sideways and aslant; / but when they reached the slopes of Ida of the many springs, / at once with the long-edged bronze they cut the high-leafed oaks / in haste” (23.114–119; see [[axe]] and [[mules]]). Walter Leaf calls the three adverbs ἄναντα κάταντα πάραντα nonce-words formed for the jingle, and the line famous, “perhaps beyond its merits”, as an imitation of stamping feet (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.116).

κνημοί, “slopes”, literally “shins”, are Ida's spurs, as in the phrase Ἴδης ἐν κνημοῖσι, “on the slopes of Ida” (2.821, 11.105, 21.449), and πολυπῖδαξ, “of the many springs”, is the mountain's standing epithet (8.47, 14.157, 14.283, 14.307, 15.151, 20.59, 20.218; see [[of-the-many-springs]]). On the oaks, Leaf reports Gerlach's suggestion of a ritual use of the wood, and thinks it safer to see in them no more than the forests of the Troad, which, to judge from modern times, were almost all of oak (on 23.118). The oaks fall “with a great crashing”, the Achaeans split them and bind them behind the mules, and the mules tear up the ground, “longing for the plain, through the thick brushwood” (23.119–122), until the logs are thrown down on the shore where Achilles has planned the barrow (23.125–126; see [[shore]], [[pyres]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

The wood comes from the mountain of the Trojans, where Zeus has his precinct and altar (8.47–48; see [[zeus]]). In the last book the Trojans too must fetch wood for Hector's pyre from the mountain far from the city, and they bring it in for nine days with oxen and mules (24.662–663, 24.782–784).
