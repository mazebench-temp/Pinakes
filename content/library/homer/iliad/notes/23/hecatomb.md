---
summary: Three hecatombs are named in the book, and none is offered in it: the holy hecatomb that Peleus vowed the river Spercheius for his son's safe return, the hecatombs the Ethiopians are offering to the immortals, to which Iris is going back, and the glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs that Teucer fails to promise Apollo and Meriones promises.
---

**Peleus' vow.** Achilles tells the river Spercheius that his father had prayed in vain “that when I came home there to my dear native land / I would cut my hair for you and offer a holy hecatomb, / and would sacrifice fifty uncut rams right there / into your waters” (23.145–148; see [[spercheius]], [[prayer]] and [[sacrifice]]). ἱερὴν ἑκατόμβην, “a holy hecatomb”, is elsewhere in the Iliad the hecatomb that Odysseus brings to Chryse for Apollo (1.99, 1.431, 1.443, 1.447); here it is one gift beside the hair and the fifty rams. The homecoming never comes, and the hair goes to Patroclus instead (23.150–151; see [[hair]]).

**At the edge of the world.** Iris will not sit with the winds, for she is going back to the land of the Ethiopians, “where they are offering hecatombs / to the immortals, so that I too may feast among them on the sacrifices” (23.206–207; see [[ethiopians]] and [[iris]]). In the *Odyssey* Poseidon is away among the Ethiopians to receive a hecatomb of bulls and rams (*Odyssey* 1.22–26; see [[poseidon]]).

**Lambs for Apollo.** In the archery the verse is used twice: Teucer “did not promise the lord / that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs” and misses the dove, and Meriones “promised far-shooting Apollo” the same and brings it down (23.863–864, 23.872–873; see [[apollo]], [[lambs]] and [[firstborn]]). It is the verse of Athena's advice to Pandarus and of his vow (4.102, 4.120), and the hecatomb is of lambs, not oxen (see the article above). Walter Leaf takes the hecatomb of lambs as Apollo's fixed price for a successful shot, and notes that good manuscripts omit 864, which the sense cannot spare, while none omits the same verse at 873 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.864).
