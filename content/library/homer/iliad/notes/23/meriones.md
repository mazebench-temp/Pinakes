---
summary: Meriones leads the men and mules who bring wood from Ida for the pyre (23.112–124). At the games he comes in fourth in the chariot race with the slowest horses, wins the archery by shooting the dove after a vow to Apollo, and is given the spear when Achilles awards Agamemnon the javelin without a contest (23.351–356, 23.528–531, 23.614–615, 23.859–883, 23.887–896).
# Unnamed: "a brave man watched over them" (23.112), his slow horses (23.530-531), and his shot (23.871-881).

**The wood.** At dawn Agamemnon sends mules and men from all the huts to bring wood for the pyre, “and a brave man watched over them, / Meriones, the attendant of manly Idomeneus”; they cut oaks on the slopes of Ida, and the woodcutters carry the logs down to the shore, “for so he bade them” (23.110–124; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]], [[ida]] and [[idomeneus]]). The verse that names him is said twice, at the start and at the end of the work (23.113 = 23.124).

**The chariot race.** He is the fifth and last to make ready his horses, and draws the fourth place (23.351, 23.356). He comes in fourth, “behind famous Menelaus by the cast of a spear; / for his fine-maned horses were the slowest, / and he himself was the least skilled at driving a chariot in a contest” (23.528–531; see [[menelaus]] and [[fine-maned]]). Nestor said the same of his own son's horses, “the slowest at running” (23.309–310), and Walter Leaf remarks drily that the honor was given to Antilochus' team before (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.530). Meriones does not dispute anything. He takes the fourth prize, the two talents of gold, “fourth, as he had driven in” (23.614–615; see [[gold-and-silver]]).

**The archery.** For the archery he stands up against Teucer, “the best of the Achaeans / at archery” (13.313–314; see [[teucer]]): “and up rose Meriones, the good attendant of Idomeneus” (23.859–860). Teucer draws the first lot, shoots without promising a hecatomb to Apollo, misses the bird, and cuts the cord (23.861–869). Then:

As the text stands, Leaf says, it can only mean that the two men shoot with one bow, as Aristarchus held, and that Meriones snatches it from Teucer's hand, having long held his own arrow ready; but he finds the idea absurd (on 23.870). The dove settles on the mast, lets her neck hang down, and falls dead far off, “and the people gazed and were amazed”; Meriones takes all ten double axes, and Teucer the single axes (23.877–883; see [[doves]], [[axe]] and [[prayer]]). He has shot before in the poem: he gave Odysseus his bow for the night raid, and he killed Harpalion with an arrow as he went back to his companions (10.260, 13.650–652).

**The javelin.** When Achilles sets out a spear and a cauldron for the javelin, Agamemnon and Meriones stand up, “Meriones, the good attendant of Idomeneus” again (23.884–888). Achilles gives Agamemnon the prize without a contest, “and let us give the spear to the hero Meriones” (23.893), and Agamemnon gives him the bronze spear (23.896; see [[agamemnon]] and [[spear]]).
