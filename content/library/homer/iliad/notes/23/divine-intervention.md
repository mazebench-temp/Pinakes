summary: Before the games the gods guard the dead and serve the living. Aphrodite and Apollo keep Hector's body from the dogs and the sun, and Iris, sent by no god, brings the winds to kindle Patroclus' pyre. In the games they take sides among the Achaeans. Apollo strikes the whip from Diomedes' hands, and Athena gives it back and wrecks Eumelus' chariot; Athena answers Odysseus' prayer in the footrace and makes Ajax slip; Apollo denies the dove to Teucer, who made him no vow, and grants it to Meriones, who did. The men, watching, see accidents where the narrator has shown a god, and explain a defeat by a prayer not made.

**Gods among the Achaeans.** In the battles the gods take sides between the armies; in the games they take sides among the Achaeans. In the chariot race Apollo bears Diomedes a grudge and strikes the whip from his hands, and Athena, seeing it, gives it back, puts might into his horses and breaks the yoke of Eumelus' mares (23.382–400; see [[apollo]], [[athena]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). In the footrace Odysseus prays to Athena, and she makes his limbs light and makes Ajax, son of Oileus, slip in the dung (23.768–777). In the archery Teucer shoots without a vow, “He missed the bird; for Apollo begrudged him that”, and Meriones, who promises Apollo a hecatomb, brings it down (23.862–876; see [[hecatomb]]). The other contests are left to men. Achilles names Apollo as the god who grants a boxer the strength to outlast his opponent (23.660–661), and Ajax in the wrestling leaves the outcome to Zeus (23.724; see [[zeus]]), but it is Achilles who stops the wrestlers, “victory is for both” (23.736), and the Achaeans who stop the armed duel, “terribly afraid for Ajax” (23.822). Before the games, Aphrodite and Apollo keep Hector's body from the dogs and from the sun (23.184–191; see [[aphrodite]]), and Iris, unsent, brings the North Wind and the West Wind to the pyre in answer to Achilles' prayer (23.198–218; see [[iris]], [[boreas]] and [[zephyrus]]).

**What the men see.** The narrator says what the gods did; the men watching see only its effects. Idomeneus, from his lookout, misses the mares that led on the way out, “the mares must have come to grief / somewhere there on the plain”, and guesses that the reins escaped the charioteer at the turning post (23.460–466; see [[idomeneus]] and [[turning-post]]). Antilochus, claiming the second prize against Eumelus, speaks of a mishap and of a duty neglected: “thinking that his chariot and swift horses came to grief, / and he himself, good as he is; but he should have prayed / to the immortals” (23.545–547; see [[antilochus]] and [[eumelus]]). The verb is the one the narrator uses for the gods' work: Diomedes' horses were “hindered” by Apollo (ἐβλάφθησαν, 23.387), Athena “hindered” Ajax (βλάψεν, 23.774), and Ajax says so himself, “Ah, truly the goddess hindered my feet” (ἔβλαψε, 23.782); the men's “came to grief” is ἔβλαβεν and βλάβεν (23.461, 23.545; see the translation's key-word row βλάπτω). Some of the men know more. Antilochus tells his horses that Athena has given speed to Diomedes' team (23.405–406), verses athetized in antiquity because he could not know it, which Walter Leaf defends (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.405). Ajax knows who tripped him, and the Achaeans laugh (23.782–784); and Antilochus, who came in last, puts Odysseus' victory down to the gods: “the immortals honor older men” (23.787–788; see [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**Prayer answered.** Leaf observes that the book proves the power of a contestant's prayer, citing Odysseus and Meriones, and Pandarus in Book 4 (on 23.546). Odysseus prays and wins; Meriones vows and hits; Teucer makes no vow and misses; and Eumelus, by Antilochus' account, should have prayed (see [[prayer]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Aristarchus marked 23.772, Athena's gift of light limbs, since with it Odysseus would have won without Ajax's fall, and Leaf agrees (on 23.772; see the translation's note on 23.772); the poem has the goddess answer the prayer and trip the rival as well.
