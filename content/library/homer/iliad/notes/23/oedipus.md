ὅς ποτε Θήβας δʼ ἦλθε δεδουπότος Οἰδιπόδαο / ἐς τάφον, “who once came to Thebes, when Oedipus had fallen, / for his funeral” (23.679–680; see [[mecisteus]], [[thebes]] and [[cadmeians]]). Οἰδιπόδαο is the genitive of Οἰδιπόδης, the epic form of the name; Οἰδίπους, Walter Leaf notes, is not a Homeric form (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.679). ἐς τάφον goes with ἦλθε, “came for the funeral”: τάφος in Homer is the funeral, not the tomb (see the translation's note on 23.29).

**Fallen.** δεδουπότος is the perfect of δουπέω, “fall with a thud”, the verb of a warrior's fall in battle (δούπησεν δὲ πεσών, “he fell with a thud”; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]). Leaf reports that the glossographers took it simply as “dead”, while Aristarchus held that it could be used only of a death in battle, with the clang of armor, though no legend tells of a war in which Oedipus died. The only similar use in Homer, he notes, is Idomeneus' readiness “himself to fall with a thud, warding off ruin from the Achaeans” (13.426; Leaf on 23.679; see the translation's note on 23.679).

**A funeral at Thebes.** The verse gives Oedipus a funeral at Thebes, with games at which a stranger from Argos could win against the Cadmeians. The Odyssey tells the rest of the story in a few lines: his mother Epicaste married her own son, who had killed his father; the gods soon made it known, she hanged herself, and he went on ruling the Cadmeians in Thebes, “suffering pains” (*Odyssey* 11.271–280; see [[jocasta]]). Neither poem speaks of blinding or exile. The games for Oedipus are recalled when Euryalus boxes at the games for Patroclus, shortly after Nestor's memory of the games for Amarynceus (23.629–642; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

## Outside Homer

Pausanias used this verse against Sophocles. In the sanctuary of the goddesses whom the Athenians call the August he saw a monument to Oedipus, whose bones, he found, had been brought from Thebes; and of the account of Oedipus' death in Sophocles' play he writes, “I am prevented from believing by Homer, who says that after the death of Oedipus Mecisteus came to Thebes and took part in the funeral games” (*Description of Greece* 1.28.6–7; see [[pausanias]]). Leaf cites the passage for this early form of the legend (on 23.679).
