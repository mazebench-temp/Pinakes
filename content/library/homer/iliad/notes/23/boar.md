---
summary: The swine of Book 23 are the herd's hogs, not wild boars: many white-tusked hogs, rich with lard, are singed over the fire for the funeral feast that Achilles gives the Myrmidons (23.32–33).
# ὕες (23.32), the only instance of this form in the Iliad.
grc: [ὕες]
---

πολλοὶ δʼ ἀργιόδοντες ὕες θαλέθοντες ἀλοιφῇ / εὑόμενοι τανύοντο διὰ φλογὸς Ἡφαίστοιο, “and many white-tusked hogs, rich with lard, / were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus” (23.32–33). The two verses are those of the feast with which Phoenix's kinsmen tried to keep him at home, where the hogs are σύες (9.467–468; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.32). ἀργιόδους, “white-tusked”, is the epithet of the wild boar that Artemis sent against Calydon (9.539) and of the boar whose tusks covered Meriones' helmet (10.263–264); here it is given to the hogs of the herd, as in the Odyssey to the swine that Eumaeus keeps (*Odyssey* 14.416, 14.532). The hogs are singed, εὑόμενοι, the bristles burned off over the fire, and “the flame of Hephaestus” is the fire itself (see [[hephaestus]]).

They are part of the funeral feast, with the oxen, the sheep and the goats, while the blood runs everywhere around the corpse, “enough to be drawn in cups” (23.29–34; see [[cattle]] and [[sheep]]). The feast is for the living. No swine are thrown on the pyre the next day, where Achilles puts sheep and cattle, horses, dogs and men (23.166–177; see [[cremation]]).
