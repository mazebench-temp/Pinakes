**The chariot race.** Achilles “brought prizes out of the ships, cauldrons and tripods” (23.259; see [[tripod]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). The third prize of the race is “a cauldron untouched by fire, / a fine one, holding four measures, still white as it was” (23.267–268). ἄπυρος, “untouched by fire”, is a vessel never set on the fire, like the seven tripods among Agamemnon's gifts (9.122), and λευκὸν ἔτʼ αὔτως is, in Walter Leaf's words, “in its natural brightness” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.267 and 23.268). At the lookout Idomeneus offers to wager “a tripod or a cauldron” on which team is in front (23.485; see [[idomeneus]]). Menelaus, who came in third, gives the mare back to Antilochus, “and then he took the all-gleaming cauldron” (23.612–613; see [[menelaus]] and [[antilochus]]). The same half-verse, λέβηθʼ ἕλε παμφανόωντα, is used in the Odyssey when the old nurse takes the gleaming basin to wash the stranger's feet (*Odyssey* 19.386).

**The javelin.** For the last contest Achilles “brought a long-shadowed spear / and a cauldron untouched by fire, worth an ox, flowered, / and set them down in the gathering” (23.884–886). ἀνθεμόεις, “flowered”, is said of a basin in the Odyssey too (*Odyssey* 3.440), and Leaf notes that flowers and rosettes were favorite motives of Mycenaean and archaic decoration (on 23.885). The value is counted in oxen, like the wrestling prizes, the tripod at twelve and the woman at four (23.703–705). Achilles gives Agamemnon the prize without a contest and the spear to Meriones, and Agamemnon gives “the beautiful prize to the herald Talthybius” (23.890–897; see [[agamemnon]], [[meriones]] and [[talthybius]]). Leaf thinks the spear can hardly be worth more than the cauldron, so that the second prize is named first, and reports Jordan's suggestion that the spear was the weapon of the contest, given to Meriones as an afterthought (on 23.884; see [[spear]]).
