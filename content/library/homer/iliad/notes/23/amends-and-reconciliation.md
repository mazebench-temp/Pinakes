summary: The dispute over the second prize of the chariot race ends with both men giving way. Antilochus, accused of a trick and challenged to swear, yields the mare and excuses himself by his youth; Menelaus, his heart warmed, gives her back, “so that these men too may know / that my heart is never overbearing and harsh” (23.566–613). Achilles ends the other disputes of the games with words and gifts.
# Unnamed: the apology and the answer (23.587-595, 23.602-611).

**The charge.** Menelaus takes the scepter and accuses Antilochus of shaming his prowess by throwing worse horses in front of his own, and he will not take the mare by his rank or let anyone say he won her with lies: let Antilochus swear by Poseidon that he did not hamper his chariot by a trick (23.566–585; see [[menelaus]] and [[oath]]).

**The apology.** Antilochus answers without anger:

{{quote:0fd94e5c-44d1-5713-8189-f349a704ee26}}

He grants Menelaus the elder's place, “the elder and the better”, and calls what he did the transgression of a young man, whose counsel is thin (see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[metis]]). He gives up the prize and offers something greater from his own store, as amends are made with gifts (see [[apoina]]), but he does not admit the trick; he would rather give than lose Menelaus' friendship and offend the gods. Walter Leaf quotes the scholia: Antilochus keeps his claim to the victory while he gives up the prize, for in saying “I myself will give” he implies that the mare is his to give (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.591).

**The answer.** Menelaus' heart is warmed “as when there is dew around the ears / of a growing crop” (23.597–599; see [[dew-on-the-growing-crop]]), and he gives way in turn: Antilochus was never flighty before, he and his father and his brother have suffered much for Menelaus' sake, and so he will yield to his plea and give him the mare, “though she is mine, so that these men too may know / that my heart is never overbearing and harsh” (23.602–611). Each man yields to the other, and each keeps his honor: Antilochus has the mare, and Menelaus has had his right acknowledged and takes the cauldron, the third prize (23.612–613; see [[time]]). Leaf quotes the scholia again, on the love of honor that Homer shows in young and old alike: with how many words Menelaus claims the victory, and says that he gives up his own prize willingly (on 23.610).

**Achilles' settlements.** The other disputes of the games are ended by Achilles. He stops the quarrel of Idomeneus and the son of Oileus with a rebuke (23.490–498), gives Eumelus a corselet of his own when Antilochus will not give up the mare (23.555–565), gives Nestor the prize that is left over, and adds half a talent of gold to Antilochus' prize for his praise (23.615–623, 23.795–797). At the end he honors Agamemnon as the best of all and gives him the prize without a contest (23.890–894; see [[achilles]], [[agamemnon]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
