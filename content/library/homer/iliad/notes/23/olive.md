**Aphrodite's oil.** Achilles has threatened to give Hector to the dogs, “for Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, kept the dogs from him / by day and by night, and anointed him with rose-sweet oil, / ambrosial, so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him” (23.185–187; see [[aphrodite]], [[hector]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). The oil is a god's, ambrosial like the oil with which Hera anointed herself before she went to Zeus (14.171–172), and it keeps the dead man's skin whole. Walter Leaf suggests that ῥοδόεις, “rose-sweet”, may mean no more than “fragrant”, or may allude to the ancient idea that oil of roses kept things from decay (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.186).

**The jars on the pyre.** On the pyre Achilles sets “two-handled jars of honey and of ointment, / leaning them against the bier” (23.170–171; see [[honey]] and [[cremation]]). ἄλειφαρ is the word of the “ointment nine years old” with which the Myrmidons filled the wounds of Patroclus when they washed and anointed his body (18.350–351); Leaf takes it for oil, apparently improved by keeping (on 18.351; see [[bathing]]).

**The horses' manes.** Achilles will not race, for his horses mourn their charioteer, “a kind one, who very often poured liquid olive oil / on their manes, after washing them with clear water” (23.281–282; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). ὑγρὸν ἔλαιον, “liquid olive oil”, is the oil that Nausicaa's mother gives her in a golden flask for the bath (*Odyssey* 6.79–80): the horses were washed and anointed as men are after the bath. Leaf compares the care that Andromache gave Hector's horses, with honey-sweet wheat and mixed wine (8.185–190; on 23.281).
