summary: The race in which Sophocles' Orestes is said to have died is built on the chariot race of this book: the lots and the shout at the start, the dust, the breath of the horses behind, the driver who keeps close to the post and gives the right-hand horse its head, the wrecked chariots, and the crash at the post that Nestor warns his son against. Walter Leaf calls it the other famous locus classicus on the chariot race.

**The report.** Orestes' old attendant, posing as a messenger from Phocis, tells Clytemnestra and Electra how Orestes died at the Pythian games. On the first day he won every contest that the judges proclaimed; on another day, at sunrise, came the race of the swift horses, with ten chariots: an Achaean, a Spartan, two Libyans, Orestes fifth with Thessalian mares, an Aetolian with chestnut colts, a Magnesian, an Aenian with white horses, an Athenian and a Boeotian (*Electra* 680–708; see [[electra-play]] and [[pythian-games]]). In the Iliad five chariots run (23.287–351; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

**Homer's race in Sophocles.** The appointed judges place the chariots by lot (*Electra* 709–710), as Achilles shakes the lots for the places (23.352–357); Walter Leaf sets the two side by side (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.352). At the sound of the bronze trumpet they start, and all together they shout to their horses and shake the reins in their hands (*Electra* 711–713), as Homer's drivers “all together … raised their whips over their horses, / and struck them with the straps, and shouted at them with words” (23.362–363); Leaf reads ἅμα, “together”, in Homer's line, as Sophocles has it (on 23.362). The course fills with the clatter of the chariots, and the dust flies up (*Electra* 713–715), as Homer's dust “rose and stood like a cloud or a storm” (23.365–366; see [[like-a-cloud-or-a-storm]]). The breath of the horses behind foams on the backs and the wheels of those in front (*Electra* 718–719), as the breath of Diomedes' horses warms Eumelus' back and broad shoulders (23.379–381; Leaf on 23.381).

**At the post.** Orestes keeps under the very edge of the post, always brings his hub close, and, giving rein to the right-hand trace-horse, holds back the horse on the inside (ὑπʼ αὐτὴν ἐσχάτην στήλην ἔχων / ἔχριμπτʼ ἀεὶ σύριγγα, δεξιὸν δʼ ἀνεὶς / σειραῖον ἵππον εἶργε τὸν προσκείμενον, *Electra* 720–722). This is Nestor's lesson to Antilochus: “Pressing close to it, drive your chariot and horses near, … but the right-hand horse / goad on with a shout, and let the reins go with your hands. / And at the post let your left-hand horse press close” (23.334–338; see [[turning-post]] and [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]). Leaf calls Sophocles' race “the other famous locus classicus on the chariot race”, and compares its ἔχριμπτε with Homer's ἐγχρίμψας, a verb that implies bringing near, not touching (on 23.334, 23.336).

**The wrecks.** Then the Aenian's hard-mouthed colts bolt as they come out of the turn from the sixth lap into the seventh, and dash their heads against the chariot of the man from Barca; from that one mishap the chariots break and fall on one another, and the whole plain of Crisa is filled with the wrecks (*Electra* 724–730). The Athenian, a skilled driver, pulls aside and waits while the surge goes by (731–733), as Menelaus holds back his horses in the narrow road rather than collide with Antilochus, lest the chariots overturn and both men fall in the dust (23.433–437; see [[menelaus]] and [[antilochus]]). Orestes, driving last, has held his mares back, trusting to the finish; now he goes after the Athenian, and the two drive yoke to yoke, now one head in front and now the other (*Electra* 734–740). Nestor's driver who knows crafty ways, though his horses are worse, “keeps them steady and watches the man in front” (23.322–325).

**The fall.** Through all the other laps Orestes stood upright in an upright chariot. Then, slackening the left rein as the horse made the turn, he struck the edge of the post without seeing it, broke the axle-box in the middle, slid over the rail and was tangled in the reins; as he fell, his horses scattered over the course (*Electra* 741–748). It is the crash that Nestor warns against: “but avoid touching the stone, / lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot; / that would be a joy to the others, and a disgrace to yourself” (23.340–342), and Leaf says that this verse “is well illustrated by the catastrophe of the race in the Electra” (on 23.340). In Homer the fall is Eumelus': Athena breaks his yoke, his mares run off the road, the pole hits the ground, and he tumbles out beside the wheel, the skin torn from his elbows, mouth and nose (23.391–397; see [[eumelus]]). Sophocles' Orestes is dragged along the ground until the other drivers stop his horses and free him, so bloody that no friend would know him; and men of Phocis bring home his ashes, “a great body, in a little bronze”, to win a tomb in his fatherland (*Electra* 749–760).
