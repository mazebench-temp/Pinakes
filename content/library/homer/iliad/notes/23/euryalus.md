summary: Euryalus alone stands up to Epeius in the boxing. The poet names his father Mecisteus, who once beat all the Cadmeians at the funeral games of Oedipus; Diomedes girds him and wishes him victory, but Epeius knocks him senseless with one blow, and his companions lead him away and fetch the loser's cup (23.677–699).
# Unnamed: the beaten boxer (23.681-684, 23.687-699).

**The challenger.** After Epeius' boast the Achaeans fall silent, and one man stands up:

{{quote:8a8e061a-1536-57cb-ab90-f11c81a0883d}}

ἰσόθεος φώς, “a man equal to the gods”, and the father and grandfather are the Catalogue's words for him (2.565–566; see [[equal-to-the-gods]], [[mecisteus]] and [[talaus]]). The poet adds the father's deed: at the funeral of Oedipus at Thebes, Mecisteus “defeated all the Cadmeians” (23.679–680; see [[oedipus]], [[cadmeians]] and [[thebes]]). The son enters the boxing with his father's victory at other funeral games behind him, and loses.

**His second.** “And the son of Tydeus, famed for his spear, busied himself about him, / encouraging him with words, and he greatly wished him victory” (23.681–682): Diomedes puts the loincloth about him and gives him the well-cut straps of oxhide (23.683–684; see [[diomedes]] and [[well-cut]]). Euryalus is the third of the Argive leaders after Diomedes and Sthenelus (2.563–566). Walter Leaf explains Diomedes' care by kinship: Euryalus' father Mecisteus and Adrastus were brothers, sons of Talaus, and Diomedes was a grandson of Adrastus through his mother and had married Adrastus' daughter Aegialeia (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.677; see [[adrastus]] and [[aegialeia]]). The Iliad itself gives the two marriages, Tydeus' to “one of the daughters of Adrastus” and Diomedes' to Aegialeia (14.121, 5.412); that Adrastus was a son of Talaus is later tradition (see [[talaus]]).

**The fall.** Epeius strikes him on the cheek “as he peered about; and he did not / stand long; for there his shining limbs gave way beneath him”, and he leaps up under the blow like a fish under the North Wind's ripple (23.690–694; see [[epeius]] and [[the-leaping-fish]]). Epeius sets him on his feet, and his companions lead him through the gathering “with trailing feet, / spitting thick blood, his head lolling to one side”, set him down among them, “his wits wandering”, and go back themselves to fetch the two-handled cup, the loser's prize (23.695–699; see [[cup]]). It is the last time he is seen in the poem. His only other deeds are four Trojans killed in the first battle (6.20–28).
