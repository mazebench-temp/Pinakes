summary: In this book Achilles twice renews his vow to give Hector to the dogs, and lays the body face down in the dust beside the bier; the gods keep it whole, Aphrodite anointing it so that the dragging will not tear the skin. The slaughter of the captives at the pyre, which Plato names beside the draggings, is in this book too.

**The vow renewed.** Over the body of Patroclus Achilles tells the dead man that he is now fulfilling his promise, “to drag Hector here and give him raw to the dogs to divide among them” (23.20–21), and he “devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector, / stretching him face down beside the bier of the son of Menoetius / in the dust” (23.24–26). At the pyre he says it again: the fire is eating the twelve Trojans with Patroclus, “but Hector, / the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs” (23.182–183). The vow of the dogs answers what Hector had meant for Patroclus, to give his body to the Trojan dogs (17.126–127), on which the defenders built their case. Walter Leaf takes the “shameful deeds” of 23.24 to mean no more than leaving the body naked and neglected, “somewhat of an anticlimax after what has preceded” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.24), and denies that the “evil deeds” of the pyre pass any moral judgment on Achilles (on 23.176; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]).

**The gods' answer.** The narrator answers the threat at once: “no dogs busied themselves about him, / for Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, kept the dogs from him / by day and by night”, and anointed him with rose-sweet ambrosial oil, “so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him”; and Apollo brought a dark cloud over the place where the body lay, lest the sun parch it (23.184–191; see [[aphrodite]], [[apollo]] and [[hector]]). The dragging around the tomb that Plato would not have believed is foreseen here. The half-verse of 23.187, ἵνα μή μιν ἀποδρύφοι ἑλκυστάζων, ends the passage of Book 24 in which Apollo covers the body with the golden aegis as Achilles drags it three times around the mound (24.14–21). Leaf reports that Fäsi and Düntzer rejected 23.184–191 because they anticipate the next book; he finds the case strong only against 187, “which is indefensible”, but thinks the lines would be better away (on 23.184).

**Plato and the defenders.** Plato names the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus together with the slaughters of the captives at the pyre (*Republic* 3.391b). The dragging belongs to Book 24, after the mound of this book has been heaped (23.255–257); the slaughter is told in this book, and Proclus' answer to it, that the twelve were attendants set beside the soul of Patroclus, is given under [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]] (*On the Republic* I 151–153 Kroll). With the Thessalian custom of dragging the killers around the tomb, Proclus reports the ancients' view that the dragging and the slaughter alike “were taken as completing the rites due to Patroclus” (I 150 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
