summary: When the pyre of Patroclus will not burn, Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind and promises them fine sacrifices. Iris fetches them from a feast in the West Wind's house; they come over the sea with a wondrous din, fall on the pyre and blow on the flames all night, and at the morning star they go home over the Thracian sea. Later, in a simile of the boxing, a fish leaps up under the North Wind's ripple.

**The prayer.** “But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not burn” (23.192). Achilles thinks of another thing: “standing apart from the pyre he prayed to two winds, / the North Wind and the West Wind, and promised them fine sacrifices; / and pouring many libations from a golden cup he entreated them / to come” (23.194–197; see [[prayer]], [[libation]], [[sacrifice]] and [[pyres]]). The two are the winds of the simile that opens Book 9, “the North Wind and the West Wind, which blow from Thrace” (9.5; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]] and the note on Book 9). Iris hears the prayer and finds them feasting in the house of the West Wind (23.198–211; see [[iris]] and [[zephyrus]]).

**The night on the pyre.** They rise “with a wondrous din, driving the clouds in confusion before them. / And swiftly they came to the sea to blow, and a wave rose / under the shrill wind; and they came to deep-soiled Troy, / and fell on the pyre, and the wondrous fire roared loud” (23.213–216; see [[troy]]). ἀήμεναι, “to blow”, is best taken as “so as to blow upon it” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.214; see the translation's note on 23.214). θεσπιδαές, “wondrous”, is a standing epithet of fire (12.177, 15.597, 20.490, and of Hephaestus' fire at 21.342), and Leaf denies that it marks here the divine power that raised the flames (on 23.216; see the translation's key-word row θεσπιδαής). “And all night long, together, they tossed the flame of the pyre, / blowing shrilly” (23.217–218), while Achilles pours wine on the ground and calls on the soul of Patroclus (23.218–225; see [[achilles]] and [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]). ἄμυδις, “together”, may mean that they beat on the flame from different sides (Leaf on 23.217).

**Home.** At the morning star the pyre dies down, “And the winds went back again to go home / over the Thracian sea; and it groaned, raging with its swell” (23.226–230; see [[eos]] and [[thrace]]): on their way, as Leaf puts it, to their home in Thrace (on 23.230). οἴδματι θύων, “raging with its swell”, was said of the river Scamander when it rushed on Achilles (21.234; see [[scamander]]).

**The ripple.** In the boxing Euryalus, struck on the cheek by Epeius, leaps up “as when under the ripple of the North Wind a fish leaps up / on a shore full of seaweed, and a dark wave hides it” (23.692–693; see [[the-leaping-fish]], [[euryalus]] and [[epeius]]). φρίξ, “ripple”, was the West Wind's in the simile of Book 7 (7.63; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]).
