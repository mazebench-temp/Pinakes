---
summary: The dogs of Book 23 belong to the funeral. Achilles tells the dead Patroclus that he will give Hector raw to the dogs, throws two of his own nine table dogs on the pyre, and swears again that the dogs, not the fire, shall devour Hector; but Aphrodite keeps the dogs from the body day and night (23.19–23, 23.173–174, 23.179–187).
# τραπεζῆες (23.173): the table dogs, as τραπεζῆας (22.69).
grc: [τραπεζῆες]
---

**The promise.** Achilles lays his hands on the breast of Patroclus and leads the lament: “Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the house of Hades; / for now I am fulfilling for you all that I promised before, / to drag Hector here and give him raw to the dogs to divide among them, / and before your pyre to cut the throats of twelve / splendid children of the Trojans” (23.19–23; see [[achilles]] and [[lament]]). The promise he made over the body in Book 18 was of Hector's armor and head and the twelve Trojans (18.334–337); the dogs he promised Hector himself, “the dogs and the birds will divide you all among them” (22.354; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). ὠμά, “raw”, has no noun with it (see the translation's note on 23.21–23, and [[eating-raw-flesh]]). Then he stretches Hector face down in the dust beside the bier (23.24–26).

**The table dogs.** On the pyre, after the sheep and the cattle, the jars of honey and ointment and four horses: “The lord had nine dogs that fed at his table, / and he cut the throats of two of them and threw them on the pyre” (23.173–174; see [[cremation]] and [[horses]]). τραπεζῆες κύνες are the dogs a lord keeps in his house and feeds from his table. Priam foresaw that his own table dogs would drink his blood at his doors when the city fell (22.66–71), and in the Odyssey Odysseus wonders whether his old dog Argos was a hunter or one of the table dogs that masters keep for show (*Odyssey* 17.306–310). Achilles' dogs go with their master's friend into the fire, with the twelve Trojans after them (23.175–177; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

**Not the fire but the dogs.** At the pyre Achilles calls Patroclus by name again: the twelve sons of the Trojans the fire is eating along with him, “but Hector, / the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs” (23.182–183). The verb, δαπτέμεν, “devour”, is the one Hector used when he begged him, “do not let the dogs devour me beside the ships of the Achaeans” (καταδάψαι, 22.339). Fire and dogs are the two ends of a body: the one gives it its due, the other dishonors it. The narrator answers at once: “So he spoke, threatening; but no dogs busied themselves about him, / for Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, kept the dogs from him / by day and by night, and anointed him with rose-sweet oil, / ambrosial, so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him” (23.184–187; see [[aphrodite]]), and Apollo covers the place with a dark cloud, lest the sun parch his skin (23.188–191; see [[apollo]]). The poem began with the dead made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5); for Hector the threat is not carried out. Some critics rejected these verses as anticipating the next book, where the gods' care for the body is told again (see the translation's note on 23.184–191).
