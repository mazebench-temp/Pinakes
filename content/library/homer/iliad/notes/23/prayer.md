summary: The book is full of prayers and vows. Achilles tells how Peleus vowed his son's hair, a hecatomb and fifty rams to the river Spercheius for his safe return, a prayer the god did not fulfill. At the pyre Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind with libations and a promise of sacrifices, and Iris hears and carries his prayer. In the games Antilochus says that Eumelus should have prayed to the immortals; Odysseus prays to Athena in his heart, and she hears him; and of the two archers, the one who vows a hecatomb to Apollo hits the dove.

**Peleus' vow.** Before the pyre is lit Achilles cuts off the hair that he had grown for the river Spercheius, and speaks to the river: “Spercheius, it was in vain that my father Peleus prayed to you / that when I came home there to my dear native land / I would cut my hair for you and offer a holy hecatomb, / and would sacrifice fifty uncut rams right there / into your waters, where you have a precinct and a fragrant altar. / So the old man prayed, but you did not fulfill what he had in mind” (23.144–149; see [[spercheius]], [[hair]], [[hecatomb]] and [[sacrifice]]). It is a vow, a gift promised for a favor not yet granted, and the god has not granted it. ἄλλως, “in vain” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.144; see the translation's note on 23.141–151). Achilles' own prayer for Patroclus in Book 16 was half refused: “And the father granted him one thing, and refused him the other” (16.250; see [[zeus]]). Now the hair goes to the dead: “I would grant my hair to the hero Patroclus to take with him” (23.151).

**To the winds.** When the pyre will not burn, Achilles, “standing apart from the pyre he prayed to two winds, / the North Wind and the West Wind, and promised them fine sacrifices; / and pouring many libations from a golden cup he entreated them / to come” (23.194–197; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[libation]]). He calls, promises and pours. “And swiftly Iris, / hearing his prayers, went as messenger to the winds” (23.198–199; see [[iris]]), and gives them his request and his promise in her own words, “But Achilles prays the North Wind and the clamorous West Wind / to come, and promises fine sacrifices” (23.208–209). The winds come; the poem does not say whether the sacrifices were paid. All night, while the pyre burns, Achilles pours wine on the ground, “calling on the soul of poor Patroclus” (23.218–221; see [[psyche]]).

**He should have prayed.** When Achilles proposes to give the second prize to Eumelus, whose chariot was wrecked, Antilochus objects: Eumelus is a good man, “but he should have prayed / to the immortals; then he would not have come in last of all, driving” (23.546–547; see [[antilochus]], [[eumelus]] and [[euchomai]]). The narrator has shown Athena breaking his yoke (23.391–393; see [[athena]]).

**Odysseus' prayer.** In the footrace, at the last stretch, “Odysseus / prayed in his heart to gray-eyed Athena: / ‘Hear me, goddess, come as a good helper to my feet.’ / So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (23.768–771; see [[hear-me]]). The prayer is nothing but the call and the request, with no claim on the goddess. ὃν κατὰ θυμόν, “in his heart”: Odysseus prays so once more in Homer, to the river of Scheria as he swims for the shore (*Odyssey* 5.444–445). The answer is the verse of the answered prayer, said of Apollo hearing Chryses (1.43, 1.457) and of Athena hearing Diomedes (5.121); this verse and the next are Book 5's word for word (23.771–772 = 5.121–122).

**The archers' vows.** Teucer shoots at once, “but did not promise the lord / that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs”, and misses the bird, “for Apollo begrudged him that” (23.863–865); Meriones “promised far-shooting Apollo” the same hecatomb and brings the dove down (23.872–876; see [[apollo]] and [[hecatomb]]). ἠπείλησεν is a declaration made aloud, here a vow (see the translation's note on 23.863). The vow is the one that Athena bade Pandarus make in Book 4, and that he made before his shot (4.101–103, 4.119–121; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]). Leaf notes that the book proves the power of a contestant's prayer by Odysseus and Meriones, as Book 4 proved it by Pandarus (on 23.546; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).

**Not prayers.** When Epeius says εὔχομαι εἶναι ἄριστος, it is a claim, “since I claim to be the best” (23.669; see [[epeius]] and [[euchomai]]), and the line is excepted from this entry. Nestor's thanks to Achilles end in a wish, not a prayer: “And for this may the gods give you thanks in return, to satisfy your heart” (23.650; see [[charis]] and [[nestor]]).
