---
summary: The silver mixing bowl of the footrace had been the price of Lycaon: Euneus gave it to Patroclus when Lycaon was sold into Lemnos (23.746–747).
---

“and as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam, it was given / to the hero Patroclus by Euneus, son of Jason” (23.746–747; see [[euneus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The sale is the one told at the river: Achilles had taken Lycaon by night in his father's orchard and sold him “into well-built Lemnos, / taking him there in his ships, and the son of Jason paid the price” (21.40–41; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]] and [[lemnos]]). Book 21 does not name Patroclus in the sale, and Walter Leaf finds it natural enough to make him an agent in the transaction (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.746). Lycaon told Achilles that he had fetched him “the worth of a hundred oxen” (21.79), and Leaf takes the hundred oxen for the value of this bowl (on 21.79).

Achilles' answer to Lycaon at the river ties the sale to Patroclus: “for before Patroclus met his fated day, / until then it was somehow dearer to my heart to spare / Trojans, and many I took alive and sold” (21.100–102; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). Now Lycaon is dead, killed with the sword after his ransom and return (21.116–119), and the price once paid for him is set out as a prize at the funeral of the man who received it (23.748; see [[patroclus]]).
