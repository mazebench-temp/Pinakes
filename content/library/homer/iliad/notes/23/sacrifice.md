summary: The book's sacrifices are promised to a river, to the winds and to Apollo, and its slaughter is for the dead. Peleus had vowed the Spercheius a hecatomb and fifty rams sacrificed into its waters; Achilles promises the winds fine sacrifices; Iris goes back to share the hecatombs of the Ethiopians; and Meriones vows Apollo a hecatomb of firstborn lambs. At the funeral feast and at the pyre the Achaeans kill oxen, sheep, goats and hogs, and the corpse is wrapped in their fat; in the footrace Ajax slips in the dung of the oxen killed for Patroclus.
# ἱερεύσειν (23.147) and ῥέζουσʼ (23.206) occur only here in the Iliad; ἱρῶν "sacrifices" (23.207) also catches
# 5.178, where Aeneas wonders whether Diomedes is a god angry with the Trojans, "in wrath over sacrifices".

**Vows and promises.** Peleus had vowed the river Spercheius, for his son's safe return, that he would “offer a holy hecatomb, / and would sacrifice fifty uncut rams right there / into your waters, where you have a precinct and a fragrant altar” (23.146–148; see [[prayer]], [[spercheius]], [[hecatomb]] and [[altar]]). ἔνορχα, “uncut”, are males without blemish. ἱερεύσειν ἐς πηγάς, “sacrifice into your waters”, may mean, Walter Leaf allows, that the victims were dropped into the river, as the Trojans threw horses alive into the eddies of the Scamander (21.131–132), but need mean no more than that their blood ran into it; he compares Odysseus cutting the throats of his sheep over the pit (*Odyssey* 11.35–36; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.147; see the translation's note on 23.141–151). At the pyre Achilles promises the winds “fine sacrifices” (ἱερὰ καλά, 23.195, 23.209; see [[boreas]] and [[zephyrus]]), and Iris is on her way back to the Ethiopians, “where they are offering hecatombs / to the immortals, so that I too may feast among them on the sacrifices” (23.206–207; see [[ethiopians]] and [[iris]]). μεταδαίσομαι ἱρῶν is to share the sacrificial feast (Leaf on 23.207): the god comes to eat with the men who offer (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). In the archery Meriones vows Apollo “a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs”, and Teucer, who did not, misses (23.863–873; see [[apollo]] and [[lambs]]).

**For the dead.** At the funeral feast “Many sleek oxen bellowed around the iron / as their throats were cut, and many sheep and bleating goats”, and hogs were singed over the fire, “and everywhere around the corpse the blood ran, enough to be drawn in cups” (23.30–34; see [[feast]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). The T scholia, which Leaf quotes, note that white victims are not offered to the dead, and suppose the oxen were killed for the feast of the living; Leaf himself thinks it more natural that the blood was caught in cups and poured out as a gift to the dead, as blood is given to the shades in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 10.535–537, 11.96, 11.153; on 23.30, 23.34; see the translation's notes on 23.30 and 23.34). At the pyre, “many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns / they flayed before the pyre and were busy over them; and from them all / great-hearted Achilles took the fat and covered the corpse with it / from head to foot, and heaped the flayed carcasses around it” (23.166–169; see [[cremation]], [[pyres]], [[sheep]] and [[cattle]]). The first of those verses is the kinsmen's feast in Phoenix's story (23.166 = 9.466). The words recall the sacrifice to the gods, where the thigh bones are “wrapped in fat, / making a double fold” (κνίσῃ ἐκάλυψαν / δίπτυχα ποιήσαντες, 1.460–461): here it is the corpse that is wrapped in fat (δημὸν … ἐκάλυψε, 23.168), and the bones of Patroclus will be laid in a golden bowl in “a double fold of fat” (δίπλακι δημῷ, 23.243; see [[the-golden-urn]]), which Leaf explains as a covering to keep out air and moisture (on 23.243). After the sheep and cattle come the jars of honey and ointment, the four horses, two of the dogs, and the twelve Trojans (23.170–177; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[dogs]]). In the footrace Ajax, son of Oileus, slips “where the dung was spread from the loud-bellowing oxen that were slain, / which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus” (23.775–776; see [[athena]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
