summary: He keeps the Myrmidons to mourn Patroclus, lays Hector face down beside the bier and gives them a funeral feast; he swears that no water will touch his head until Patroclus is burned, and in his sleep the soul of Patroclus asks him for burial and for one urn for their bones. He gives Patroclus the hair he had grown for the Spercheius, burns him with sheep and cattle, horses, dogs and twelve young Trojans, prays to the winds and pours wine all night, and has the bones gathered and a mound heaped, to be made wide and high after his own death. Then he holds the games: he sets out the prizes, will not race his own horses, makes peace between the quarrelers, pities Eumelus, honors Nestor and gives the last prize to Agamemnon without a contest (23.4–257, 23.257–897).

**The mourning.** When the army scatters to the ships, Achilles keeps back the Myrmidons, “Myrmidons of the swift horses, my trusty companions” (23.6; see [[myrmidons]], [[of-the-swift-horses]] and [[trusty]]): they are to drive near the body with their chariots and weep for Patroclus, “for that is the prize of the dead” (23.7–9; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]), and only then unyoke and eat. Three times they drive around the body, “and Achilles led them” (23.12–13). The two verses in which he leads the lament, “laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion”, are those of the night when the body was brought back (23.17–18 = 18.316–317; see [[man-slaying]] and [[lament]]). Then he speaks to the dead:

{{quote:2f46b3dc-c628-5646-8539-6ed4f6ee53b5}}

χαῖρε, “Rejoice”, has its full force: even in Hades, Walter Leaf comments, where there is little rejoicing indeed (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.19; see the translation's note on 23.19). Leaf takes τελέω as a present, “now I am fulfilling”: the dragged body is an earnest of the whole promise, made over the corpse and kept in the river and over the dying Hector (on 23.20; 18.334–337, 21.27–32, 22.354; see [[human-sacrifice]]). “He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector” (23.24), the verse of the dragging (22.395), and he stretches Hector face down in the dust beside the bier (23.24–26; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Then, beside the ship of “the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (23.28; see [[grandson-of-aeacus]]), he gives the countless Myrmidons “a funeral feast to satisfy the heart” (23.29; see [[feast]]), and the blood of the victims runs around the corpse (23.30–34; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

**No water for his head.** The kings bring him to Agamemnon's hut, “having won him over with difficulty, for he was angry at heart for his companion” (23.37), and have the heralds set a tripod over the fire, in the verse with which he once had Patroclus washed (23.40–41 ≈ 18.344–345; see [[bathing]] and [[tripod]]).

{{quote:aa62eb60-8fd1-5846-9ce4-581b8a05dbc3}}

Leaf quotes Monro's view that the oath is an old formula, meaning “Zeus, or by whatever name the highest of the gods is to be called” (on 23.43; see [[oath-formulas]] and [[zeus]]). The burning, the mound and the cut hair that he names are the order of the funeral to come (see [[cremation]] and [[hair]]). He does not refuse food this time, as he did in Book 19 (19.205–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]]): “But now let us yield to the hateful feast” (23.48). Leaf finds the verb peculiarly apt in his mouth, food being a power that must be obeyed (on 23.48; see the translation's note on 23.48). He asks Agamemnon to have wood brought at dawn, “so that the tireless fire may burn him away / the sooner from our eyes, and the army may turn to its tasks” (23.49–53; see [[agamemnon]] and [[tireless]]).

**The ghost.** The others go to their huts, but Achilles “lay groaning heavily among the many Myrmidons” on the shore of the loud-roaring sea (23.59–61; see [[groaning-heavily]] and [[loud-roaring-sea]]), until sleep took him, “loosening the cares of his heart” (23.62), his limbs weary from chasing Hector to the walls (23.63–64; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). The soul of Patroclus comes to him in his sleep, reproaches him for forgetting the dead, asks to be buried quickly, and tells him that it is his fate too “to die beneath the wall of the wealthy Trojans”; their bones are to lie together in one urn (23.65–92; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). He answers as the living answer the dead: he will do it all, “I / will accomplish them all and obey as you bid me”, and asks for one embrace (23.93–98). His hands close on nothing, the soul goes beneath the earth like smoke, “and Achilles sprang up in amazement”, struck his hands together, and cried that even in the house of Hades there is a soul and a phantom, but no mind in it at all (23.99–107; see [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]).

**The barrow and the hair.** The wood is thrown down on the shore where he “had planned a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself” (23.125–126; see [[tomb]]). He sends out the Myrmidons in armor, and holds the head of the body as they carry it, grieving, “for he was sending his noble companion to Hades” (23.128–137). At the pyre, “Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles thought of another thing” (23.140): he cuts off the fair hair he had grown for the river Spercheius, and “deeply troubled he spoke, looking out over the wine-dark sea” (23.143; see [[deeply-troubled]] and [[wine-dark]]). Peleus vowed the hair for his homecoming, with a holy hecatomb and fifty rams sacrificed into the river's waters; “But now, since I am not going home to my dear native land, / I would grant my hair to the hero Patroclus to take with him” (23.144–151; see [[spercheius]], [[peleus]] and [[nostos]]). He made the choice when he resolved to avenge Patroclus at the cost of his own life (18.95–126): of the two fates his mother told him of, a homecoming with a long life or death at Troy with imperishable fame, his is the second (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Leaf explains that the lock grown for a river was cut at coming of age, and that Achilles still wears it because he left home young (on 23.141; see [[hair]]).

**At the pyre.** He bids Agamemnon send the army away, “we to whom most of all / the corpse is dear” will see to the rest (23.155–160). He covers the body with fat, sets jars of honey and ointment against the bier, throws four horses on the pyre “groaning loudly”, kills two of the nine table dogs, and cuts down twelve sons of the Trojans with the bronze, “and he devised evil deeds in his mind” (23.168–176; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[dogs]]). The half-verse was said of him as he leapt into the river with his sword (21.19); Leaf denies that κακά passes a moral judgment, the work being ill only for its victims (on 23.176). He lets in “the iron might of the fire” (23.177), and greets Patroclus again in the verses of the morning: the fire is eating the twelve with him, “but Hector, / the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs” (23.179–183). Leaf would read at 180 a variant in which the promise is now fulfilled, which the moment seems to demand (on 23.179). The narrator corrects him at once: Aphrodite keeps the dogs from Hector and anoints him, “so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him”, and Apollo shades the body from the sun (23.184–191; see [[aphrodite]] and [[apollo]]).

**The winds.** The pyre will not catch. Standing apart, he prays to the North Wind and the West Wind, promises them fine sacrifices, and “pouring many libations from a golden cup he entreated them” (23.193–197; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[prayer]]). Iris hears him and carries the message: “But Achilles prays the North Wind and the clamorous West Wind” (23.199, 23.208–209; see [[iris]]). All night, while the winds toss the flame, he draws wine from a golden mixing bowl and pours it on the ground, calling on the soul of Patroclus, and mourns as a father mourns who burns the bones of his son, “dragging himself beside the pyre, groaning without ceasing” (23.218–225; see [[libation]] and [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]).

**The bones and the tomb.** At dawn he lies down beside the dying pyre, “and sweet sleep leapt upon him” (23.231–232; see [[sweet-sleep]]), until the leaders gather and wake him. He tells them to quench the pyre with wine and to gather the bones of Patroclus, which are easy to tell apart, since he lay in the middle and the horses and men at the edge (23.236–242), and goes on:

{{quote:27784d32-0893-5ee1-ad71-4aceb7e25524}}

Leaf glosses τοῖον, “just such”, as “as seemly as you will, but not extravagant”, and ἔπειτα as “after my death” (on 23.246; see the translation's note on 23.246). Everything that he orders for Patroclus waits for his own end. The ghost foretold it (23.80–81), the barrow was planned for both (23.126), the hair goes with Patroclus because its owner will not go home (23.150–151), and now the bones are kept in the huts and the mound is left for the survivors to finish (23.243–257; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and [[the-golden-urn]]). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells him how it was done: his bones and Patroclus' lie together in a golden amphora under a great tomb on a headland by the Hellespont (*Odyssey* 24.72–84).

**Host and judge.** The mound is heaped, and the army is going back, “But Achilles / kept the army there and made them sit in a wide gathering” (23.257–258), and brings out the prizes for the games he gives in honor of Patroclus (see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[contests]]). He will not race himself: “If now we Achaeans were holding games in honor of another, / truly I would take the first prize and carry it to my hut” (23.274–275), but his immortal horses stand mourning the charioteer who washed and oiled their manes (23.276–284; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). He shakes the lots, shows the turning post and sets Phoenix beside it as a lookout (23.352–361; see [[phoenix]]), and he stops the quarrel of Idomeneus and Ajax with a reproof that both would make to another man (23.490–498; see [[idomeneus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). “And seeing him, brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him” (23.534): Eumelus comes in last, dragging his chariot, in the words that told Patroclus' pity for the wounded Eurypylus (11.814; see [[eumelus]] and [[pity]]). When Antilochus will not give up the second prize, “brilliant swift-footed Achilles smiled, / rejoicing in Antilochus, since he was his dear companion” (23.555–556; see [[antilochus]]), the only time in the poem that he smiles, and he gives Eumelus the corselet he took from Asteropaeus (23.558–565; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[automedon]]).

**The gifts.** The fifth prize of the chariot race, left over, he carries to Nestor, “to be a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus”, since old age keeps him from the contests (23.616–623; see [[nestor]] and [[prize-of-the-old]]); Nestor answers with the story of his own victories, bids him “But go, and likewise honor your companion with funeral games” (23.646), and Achilles goes only “when he had listened to all the praise of the son of Neleus” (23.652). He sets the prizes for each contest and stands to announce them (23.653–663, 23.700–707, 23.740–753, 23.798–810, 23.826–835, 23.850–858). He ends the wrestling before a third bout, “victory is for both; take equal prizes” (23.736), and adds half a talent of gold to Antilochus' prize when Antilochus says that it is hard for the Achaeans to race Odysseus, “except for Achilles” (23.785–796). Among the prizes are his spoils: the bowl that Euneus gave Patroclus as the price of Lycaon, “This too Achilles set as a prize in honor of his companion” (23.741–748; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), the sword of Asteropaeus and the armor Patroclus took from Sarpedon (23.798–808; see [[sarpedon]]), and the lump of iron that Eetion used to throw, “but him brilliant swift-footed Achilles killed” (23.826–829; see [[eetion]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). Last, when Agamemnon rises for the javelin, Achilles gives him the first prize without a contest: “Son of Atreus, for we know how far you are ahead of all, / and how far you are the best in strength and in throwing” (23.884–894; see [[agamemnon]]). Leaf finds a certain dignity in Agamemnon's being honored without a contest that his rank would not let him lose, and adds that here Achilles “is at least a human being”, after the wooden figures of the contests since 23.797 (on 23.884).
