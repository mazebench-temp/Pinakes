At the games he remembers how Patroclus used to wash the manes of his horses with clear water (23.281–282).

**The bath refused.** The verses are those in which Achilles, on the night Patroclus was brought back, bade his companions heat water to wash the clotted blood from the dead man: ἀμφὶ πυρὶ στῆσαι τρίποδα μέγαν, and ἄπο βρότον αἱματόεντα (23.40–41 ≈ 18.344–345; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.40; see [[tripod]] and [[patroclus]]). Andromache had a tripod set on the fire for Hector's bath in the same words (22.443; see [[andromache]]). The bath that was given to the dead man is offered to the living one, and he refuses it with an oath: “it is not lawful that water for washing should come near my head / until I have laid Patroclus on the fire and heaped up a mound / and cut my hair” (23.44–46; see [[achilles]] and [[themis]]). λοετρά, “water for washing”, was the warm bath that Nestor promised the wounded Machaon and the “hot bath” Andromache made ready for Hector (14.6, 22.444). Achilles refuses the bath as he refused food in Book 19 (19.205–214; see [[fasting]]); this time he agrees to eat (23.48). The poem does not say when he washes.

**The manes.** The charioteer whom Achilles' horses mourn used to pour olive oil on their manes “after washing them with clear water” (23.281–282; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). λοέσσας is from λούω, the verb of the bath, as when Hebe bathes Ares (5.905); and the horse of the simile runs to the river where he is “used to bathing” (6.508 = 15.265; see [[the-stallion]]).
