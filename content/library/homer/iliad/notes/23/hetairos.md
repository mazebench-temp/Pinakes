---
summary: The companion of the book is Patroclus. Achilles lays his hands on “the breast of his companion” (23.18), holds his head in the procession, “for he was sending his noble companion to Hades” (23.137), and mourns over the bones as a father over a son's: “so Achilles mourned as he burned the bones of his companion” (23.224). In the games a prize is set out “in honor of his companion” (23.748), and Achilles smiles at Antilochus' protest, “since he was his dear companion” (23.556).
---

The soul of Patroclus names what death has ended: “no longer, alive, apart from our dear companions, / shall we sit and take counsel together” (23.77–78). It asks to share Achilles' urn as they shared his father's house, where Peleus received the boy and named him Achilles' attendant (θεράπων, 23.84–92; see [[patroclus]] and [[peleus]]). His companions carry him in the procession (23.134), and the Myrmidons, Achilles' “trusty companions” (23.6), are the first mourners (23.4–16; see [[myrmidons]]).

The simile at the pyre makes the companion a son, “newly married, whose death has grieved his wretched parents” (23.222–225; see [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). When the bones are gathered they are those of “their gentle companion” (ἑτάροιο ἐνηέος, 23.252), the word Menelaus had used when he asked each man to “remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus” (17.670).

Antilochus is the other companion of the book. In the Odyssey he lies in the same mound as Achilles and Patroclus, though his bones are apart from theirs, and the shade of Agamemnon calls him the one Achilles honored most of all his companions after the dead Patroclus (*Odyssey* 24.76–79; see [[antilochus]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
