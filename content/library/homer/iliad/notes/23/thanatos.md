---
summary: The noun θάνατος does not occur in the book, but the dead do: “the prize of the dead” (23.9), the reproach of Patroclus' soul, “You were not careless of me while I lived, but you are now that I am dead” (23.70), “the pyre of dead Patroclus” (23.192), and the turning post that may be “the mound of some mortal who died long ago” (23.331).
grc: [θανὼν]
---

The participles carry the theme. θανόντων, “of the dead”, is the dead as a class, owed their prize (23.9; see [[geras]]); θανόντος, “now that I am dead”, sets the dead man against the living one (ζώοντος, 23.70; see [[honoring-the-dead]]); and κατατεθνηῶτος, “who died long ago”, ends the verse of a tomb as it ended Hector's verse of the tomb of his victim (23.331; 7.89; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). In the simile of the pyre the son is “newly married, whose death has grieved his wretched parents” (θανών, 23.223; see [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]] and [[bereaved-parents]]).

The immortals are the other side. Achilles' horses are ἀθάνατοι, Poseidon's gift to Peleus, and they stand mourning a mortal driver (23.276–284; see [[immortals]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
