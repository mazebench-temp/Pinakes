**Out of the race.** Achilles has set out the prizes for the chariot race, and says that if the games were in honor of anyone else he would win the first prize himself, for his horses surpass the rest in prowess, ἀρετή (23.274–276; see [[achilles]] and [[arete]]). They are immortal, the gift of Poseidon to Peleus (23.277–278; see [[peleus]] and [[poseidon]]); elsewhere the gods together gave them (16.381, 17.443–444). The best horses of the army after them were the mares of Eumelus, which lead the race until Athena breaks their yoke (2.763–770, 23.375–397; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]] and [[eumelus]]).

**Mourning.** κλέος … ἡνιόχοιο, the “noble fame of such a charioteer”, is to Walter Leaf the glory that consists in a charioteer, and he notes a well-supported variant σθένος (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.280). The charioteer is Patroclus, “a kind one”, who washed their manes and oiled them (23.280–282; see [[patroclus]] and [[bathing]]); Leaf compares the care that Andromache gave Hector's horses (on 23.281; 8.185–190). πενθείετον, “mourn”, is found only here in Homer, and for the rest of the couplet Leaf compares the scene in Book 17, where the horses stood weeping for Patroclus with their heads bowed to the ground and their manes stained (on 23.283; 17.436–440). ἐρηρέδαται, “rest”, is said again of the two white stones that lean against the turning post (23.329; see [[hair]]). The horses do not appear again in the book; in the next book Achilles yokes his swift horses each dawn to drag Hector around the tomb (24.14–15; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).
