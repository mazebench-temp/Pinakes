summary: The Myrmidons drive their chariots around the body of Patroclus and ride out in them behind his bier (23.6–14, 23.130–133). At the games the chariot race comes first, with the richest prizes: Nestor teaches his son how to take the turning post without breaking his chariot, Eumelus' yoke is broken and his pole dashed to the ground, Menelaus holds back rather than collide, and Diomedes' chariot, overlaid with gold and tin, leaves hardly a track in the dust (23.262–652).

**The funeral.** Achilles will not let the Myrmidons unyoke: “but with the horses themselves and the chariots let us go near / and weep for Patroclus”, and they drive three times around the body (23.7–14; see [[myrmidons]]). At dawn they arm, yoke their horses and mount their chariots, “fighters and charioteers”, for the procession to the pyre (23.130–133; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). παραιβάτης, the fighter who stands beside the driver, occurs only here in Homer.

**The lesson.** Nestor tells his son how a chariot is driven around the turning post. He must lean a little to the left in the chariot, goad on the right-hand horse with a shout and give it rein, and let the left-hand horse press close to the post, “so that the hub may seem to graze its edge, / the hub of the well-made wheel; but avoid touching the stone, / lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot” (23.334–341; see [[turning-post]] and [[antilochus]]). ἐϋπλέκτῳ, “well-woven”, is said of the chariot only here and of the chariots Menelaus fears to overturn (23.335, 23.436). Walter Leaf thinks it may refer to a woven floor, the usual epithet being ἐΰξεστος, “well-polished”, which shows that the body was of wood (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.335); Plato's text of Nestor's verse has ἐυξέστῳ (*Ion* 537a; 16.402). The two horses are yoked to the pole without traces, so that when Athena breaks Eumelus' yoke, his mares run apart off the road, the pole is dashed to the ground and he is thrown out beside the wheel (23.392–394; Leaf on 23.393).

**Whip and goad.** The drivers raise their whips over the horses, strike them with the straps and shout at them (23.362–363). Apollo knocks the whip from Diomedes' hand, and his horses run on “without the goad” until Athena gives it back (23.384–390); Antilochus urges his horses on “with the goad” (23.430); Diomedes drives in “with the whip from the shoulder”, and at the finish leans the whip against the yoke (23.500, 23.510); Menelaus asks Antilochus to take an oath holding “the whip / in your hands, the slender one with which you drove before” (23.582–583). Leaf takes the κέντρον, “goad”, for the same thing as the μάστιξ, “whip”: a long pliant rod with points at the end, such as could be leaned against the yoke (on 23.387). μάστι (23.500) is a rare form, which Leaf finds again only in the Odyssey's μάστιν (on 23.500; Od. 15.182).

**Wheels.** Menelaus keeps his place on the broken road, “shunning a clash of wheels” (ἁματροχιάς, 23.422), and warns Antilochus that he may “run into my chariot and bring us both to harm” (23.428); he holds back “lest the single-hoofed horses collide in the road / and overturn the well-woven chariots, and they themselves / fall in the dust in their eagerness for victory” (23.435–437). Idomeneus imagines the leading driver thrown out at the turn and his chariot broken (23.465–467). Diomedes' chariot, “overlaid with gold and tin”, runs so lightly behind his horses that there is no deep “track of the tires” (ἁρματροχιή, 23.503–506; see [[tin]]); Leaf calls the line hyperbolical (on 23.505). The narrator measures the gap between Antilochus and Menelaus at the finish by the space between a horse and the wheel of the chariot it draws, whose tire the tips of its tail hairs touch (23.517–521; see [[the-horse-and-the-wheel]]). Eumelus comes in last, “dragging his fine chariot” (23.533).

**The drivers.** In the race each man drives his own chariot. The drivers, ἐλατῆρες, stand in their chariots with pounding hearts (23.369–370), and Ajax, son of Oileus, insists that Eumelus still “stands in the chariot holding the reins” (εὔληρα, 23.481), a word Leaf finds again only in Quintus of Smyrna (on 23.481). In Nestor's memory of the games of Amarynceus, the twin sons of Actor drove one chariot together: “the one held the reins steadily, / held the reins steadily, and the other urged them on with the whip” (23.641–642; see [[the-moliones]]).
