summary: The tomb of Patroclus, which is to be Achilles' too. Achilles swears not to wash until he has heaped the mound, plans on the shore “a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself”, and asks for a tomb no greater than is fitting, for the Achaeans who outlive him to make wide and high; the mound is heaped around the pyre, while the bones are kept in the huts. In the chariot race the turning post may be the mound of a man who died long ago (23.45, 23.126, 23.243–257, 23.331).
# ἠρίον "barrow" (23.126) is found only here in Homer.
grc: [ἠρίον]

**Three words.** The book has three words for the grave mound: σῆμα, “mound”, the marker that shows where a man lies (23.45, 23.255, 23.257, 23.331); τύμβος, “tomb” (23.245); and ἠρίον, “barrow”, found only here in Homer, though later prose and poetry use it; its derivation is unknown (23.126; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.126; see the translation's row for τύμβος / σῆμα). σῆμα keeps its other senses in the same book: the “sign” that Nestor gives his son and the white “mark” on a horse's forehead (23.326, 23.455; see [[teras]]).

**For two.** The wood for the pyre is thrown down on the shore, “where Achilles / had planned a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself” (23.125–126). The ghost of Patroclus has asked that their bones lie together (23.83–92; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[the-golden-urn]]), and when the pyre is out Achilles gives his orders for the tomb:

{{quote:27784d32-0893-5ee1-ad71-4aceb7e25524}}

τοῖον, “just such”, is to Leaf “as seemly as you will, but not extravagant”, and ἐμεῖο δεύτεροι, “after me”, means those who are left behind when he is dead (on 23.246, 23.247; see the translation's note on 23.246). The tomb is begun for Patroclus and left for the Achaeans to finish for Achilles (see [[achilles]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). In the Odyssey they do: over the bones of the two the army heaps a great and noble tomb on a headland by the broad Hellespont, to be seen far out at sea by men now living and to come (*Odyssey* 24.80–84). Hector had imagined such a tomb by the Hellespont for the man he would kill, which sailors would point out in years to come (7.86–91; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[hellespont]]).

**The mound heaped.** The leaders “marked out the circle of the mound and laid its foundations / around the pyre; and at once they heaped up the loose earth, / and when they had heaped up the mound they were going back” (23.255–257). Leaf takes the θεμείλια for a ring of upright stones facing the foot of the mound, like the circle that surrounds the graves at Mycenae, and compares the tomb of Alyattes in Herodotus, a base of great stones under a mound of earth (on 23.255; Herodotus 1.93.2; see [[cremation]]). The bones are not in it: they stay in the huts in the golden bowl, and Leaf concludes that the mound is a cenotaph, the bones being kept to go home with those of Achilles (on 23.254). Hector's bones, in his own country, are laid in a grave under stones and the mound heaped over them (24.795–801).

**An old mound.** In his advice to Antilochus Nestor describes the turning post of the chariot race, a dry stump with two white stones leaning against it, “either the mound of some mortal who died long ago, / or it was set as a post in the time of earlier men” (23.331–332; see [[turning-post]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[nestor]]). Old tombs stand in the Trojan plain as landmarks, as the tomb of Ilus does (10.415, 11.166; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]).

In the last book Achilles drags Hector each dawn three times around the mound of Patroclus, σῆμα Μενοιτιάδαο θανόντος (24.14–17; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Strabo records memorials of Patroclus and Antilochus near the shrine and memorial of Achilles at Sigeum, to which the people of Ilium make offerings (*Geography* 13.1.32; see [[strabo]]).
