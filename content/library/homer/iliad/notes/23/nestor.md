summary: Nestor stands beside Antilochus before the chariot race and teaches him to win with slower horses by cunning, describing the turning post and how to round it (23.304–350). When the race is over, Achilles gives him the unclaimed fifth prize as a memorial of Patroclus' funeral, since old age keeps him from the contests, and Nestor accepts it with the last of his tales of youth, the funeral games of Amarynceus (23.615–652).

**The father at the chariot.** Antilochus is the fourth to make ready his horses, “the splendid son of Nestor, the high-hearted lord, / the son of Neleus; and horses born in Pylos, / swift-footed, drew his chariot; and his father, standing close beside him, / spoke to him for his good, a wise man to one who understood it himself” (23.302–305; see [[antilochus]], [[neleus]] and [[pylos]]). Zeus and Poseidon have loved the young man and taught him horsemanship, so there is little need to teach him; but his horses are “the slowest at running” (23.306–310; see [[zeus]] and [[poseidon]]). The horses are “swift-footed” (ὠκύποδες, 23.304) six verses before they are the slowest, an ornamental epithet that Walter Leaf finds harsh here (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.304).

**Cunning.** The remedy is μῆτις, “cunning”, which Nestor names four times in six verses (see [[metis]] and [[cunning-not-strength]]):

{{quote:85322e0f-ae78-5a8b-91e1-25cd7bed41cd}}

The driver who trusts in his horses and chariot wheels heedlessly wide, while “he who knows crafty ways, though he drives worse horses, / always watches the turning post and turns close” and keeps his horses in hand from the start (23.319–325; see [[kerdos]]).

**The post.** Then Nestor gives his son “a sign, very easy to see”: a dry stump of oak or pine a fathom high, with two white stones against it, at the narrowing of the road, “either the mound of some mortal who died long ago, / or it was set as a post in the time of earlier men, / and now brilliant swift-footed Achilles has made it the turning post” (23.326–333; see [[turning-post]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[the-stump-that-rots]]). He is to drive close, lean a little to the left, goad the right-hand horse and give it rein, and let the left-hand horse press so close to the post that the hub seems to graze its edge, but avoid the stone, “lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot” (23.334–343; see [[chariot]]). If he passes there, no one could catch him, “not even if he drove brilliant Arion behind you, / the swift horse of Adrastus, who was of the stock of the gods, / or the horses of Laomedon” (23.344–348; see [[arion-the-horse]], [[adrastus]] and [[laomedon]]). “So saying, Nestor, son of Neleus, sat down again in his place, / since he had told his son the sum of each thing” (23.349–350).

**The lesson in the race.** The race does not go by the plan. Antilochus threatens his father's horses with their master: “there will be no keep for you two with Nestor, shepherd of the people, / but he will kill you at once with the sharp bronze”, if they come in with a worse prize (23.411–413; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). He does not pass Menelaus at the post but at a narrow place in the road, where winter water has broken it, against Menelaus' warning (23.417–437; see [[menelaus]]), and he comes in second “by crafty ways, not by speed” (κέρδεσιν, 23.515), the narrator's word for the skill his father praised (κέρδεα, 23.322). Menelaus' cry, “Antilochus, you drive heedlessly” (ἀφραδέως, 23.426), gives him the word Nestor used of the driver who trusts in his horses and wheels heedlessly wide (23.320). When Menelaus demands an oath, the son answers with the other side of his father's lesson: a young man's mind is quicker, “and his counsel thin” (λεπτὴ δέ τε μῆτις, 23.590), and he gives up the mare. The narrator names him by his father both when he claims the prize and when he yields it, “Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor” (23.541, 23.596; see [[great-hearted]]), and Menelaus forgives him for the sake of all that he, “and your brave father and your brother”, have suffered and toiled for him (23.607–608).

**The fifth prize.** Meriones takes the fourth prize, and the fifth, the two-handled bowl, is left over; “Achilles gave it to Nestor, / carrying it through the gathering of the Argives, and stood beside him and said” (23.614–617; see [[achilles]] and [[phiale]]):

{{quote:6d158ef9-8f88-5ac1-af5d-942e6bd9d067}}

The bowl is a κειμήλιον, “a treasure” to keep, and a memorial of Patroclus' funeral (μνῆμα, 23.619; see [[patroclus]]), given αὔτως, “just as it is”, without a contest (see the translation's note on 23.621–623). Only one other prize of the games is given so: at the end, Achilles gives Agamemnon the prize for the javelin without a throw, since “we know how far you are ahead of all” (23.884–897; see [[agamemnon]]). Achilles honors the oldest of the kings for his age, and the first of them for being ahead of all.

**The answer.** Nestor receives the bowl “with joy” (23.624) and answers with the verse with which Agamemnon once answered him, “Yes, my child, all that you have said is fair and right” (23.626), where Agamemnon had said “old man” (γέρον, 1.286); Leaf thinks that Aristarchus probably omitted the verse as interpolated from Book 1 (on 23.626). His limbs are no longer steady, nor do his arms dart out lightly from his shoulders (23.627–628). Then comes the wish to be young, and the last of his tales of youth: the funeral games of Amarynceus at Buprasium, where he won at boxing, wrestling, the footrace and the spear, every contest Achilles has just named, and lost only the chariot race, to the twin sons of Actor (23.629–645; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). He ends with thanks:

{{quote:fd125784-43dc-558f-88c6-b37e7a894d05}}

ἐνηέος, “a gentle friend” (23.648), is said elsewhere in the Iliad only of Patroclus (17.204, 21.96, 23.252; ἐνηείη, “gentleness”, 17.670); Leaf calls the couplet 23.648–649 “another very obscure couplet”, and takes ἐνηέος with μευ: Achilles remembers the old man as kindly disposed toward him (on 23.648; see the translation's note on 23.648–649). The honor due to him among the Achaeans is τιμή (23.649; see [[time]]), and the thanks he asks of the gods is χάρις (23.650; see [[charis]]). Achilles goes back through the throng “when he had listened to all the praise of the son of Neleus” (αἶνον, 23.651–652; see the translation's note on 23.652).

**His son in the footrace.** Antilochus, “the son of Nestor”, runs in the footrace too, “for he, for his part, beat all the young men in running” (23.755–756), and comes in last of three behind Odysseus, “in green old age”, and the son of Oileus (23.778–792; see [[odysseus]]).
