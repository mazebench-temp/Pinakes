---
summary: Nestor's advice to Antilochus before the chariot race is a lesson in cunning. The other horses are faster, “but they themselves / do not know how to devise more than you yourself. / But come, my friend, put cunning into your heart” (23.311–313), and three verses beginning “By cunning” set the woodcutter, the helmsman and the charioteer side by side (23.315–318). Antilochus, asking Menelaus' pardon, blames his youth: “his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin” (23.590).
grc: [μήτι]
en: [cunning]

{{quote:042695b5-2205-5283-9910-f6a70086099f}}

Nestor's three examples go from the forest to the sea to the racecourse, and each begins with μήτι, “by cunning” (23.315–318; see [[cunning-not-strength]]); the first sets it against βίη, “strength”. Then the lesson becomes practical: the driver who trusts his horses wheels wide, but the one who knows crafty ways watches the post and turns close, and Nestor shows his son the post itself (23.319–343; see [[kerdos]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). Antilochus' plan in the race is his own: “this I myself will contrive and devise” (23.415), and Menelaus' word for it is δόλος, “a trick” (23.585; see [[truth-and-deception]]).

Antilochus' excuse uses a half-verse of Diomedes in the night of Book 10, where a man who goes alone sees less than two together: “his mind is slower, and his counsel thin” (10.226). Here the counsel is thin because the mind is quick, the mind of a young man (23.587–590; see [[geron]] and [[noos]]); Walter Leaf compares Menelaus' words in Book 3, “The minds of younger men are always flighty” (3.108; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.590 and 23.603).

The epithet of Odysseus, πολύμητις, “of many wiles”, comes twice in the games: when he stands up for the wrestling, “Odysseus of many wiles, who knew crafty ways” (23.709), and for the footrace (23.755; see [[odysseus]]).
