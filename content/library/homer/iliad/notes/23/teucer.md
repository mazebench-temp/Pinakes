**The first shot.** “So he spoke, and then mighty lord Teucer rose” (βίη Τεύκροιο ἄνακτος, 23.859), the hero named by his strength, as Heracles is βίη Ἡρακληείη. Meriones rises against him, they shake the lots in a bronze-fitted helmet, and Teucer draws the first (23.860–862; see [[meriones]] and [[lots]]):

{{quote:5c2ec00d-bc7b-5cf7-8f20-0f3a3eed78bc}}

The vow that Teucer leaves out is a whole verse, ἀρνῶν πρωτογόνων ῥέξειν κλειτὴν ἑκατόμβην, “that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs” (23.864, 23.873). The Iliad has it only twice more, when Athena tells Pandarus to pray to Apollo with that promise before he shoots at Menelaus, and Pandarus does so (4.101–103, 4.119–121; see [[pandarus]] and [[hecatomb]]). The Achaeans' best archer forgets what the Trojan archer remembered, and the god of the bow, who gave Teucer his own (15.441; see [[apollo]]), denies him the bird.

**The cord.** “But he hit the cord beside its foot, where the bird was tied; / and the bitter arrow cut the cord clean through” (23.866–867). The dove darts up toward the sky, the cord hangs loose, and the Achaeans shout in applause (23.868–869; see [[doves]]). Meriones, who has long been holding an arrow ready, snatches the bow from Teucer's hand, makes the vow, and strikes the bird high under the clouds (23.870–881). Aristarchus took it that the bow was common to the competitors, like the discus; Walter Leaf, who reports his view and compares the one lump of iron that all the throwers used, thinks the idea absurd (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.870; see the translation's note on 23.870–871). Achilles had set a prize beforehand for hitting the cord and missing the bird (23.857–858), and Teucer wins it: “and Teucer carried the single axes to the hollow ships” (23.883; see [[axe]]). Leaf thinks a prize set beforehand for so unlikely a feat “the extreme of absurdity” (on 23.850).

**Apollo and Teucer.** A god turns Teucer's arrows for the third time. In Book 8 Apollo made his arrow swerve from Hector (8.311), in Book 15 Zeus broke his bowstring as he drew on Hector (15.461–465), and here Apollo “begrudged him” the bird (μέγηρε, 23.865; see [[divine-intervention]]).
