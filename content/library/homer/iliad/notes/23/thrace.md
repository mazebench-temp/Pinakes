“And the winds went back again to go home / over the Thracian sea; and it groaned, raging with its swell” (23.229–230). Their home is Thrace, as in the simile that opens Book 9, where the North Wind and the West Wind “blow from Thrace” (9.5; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]), and Walter Leaf takes the line so, “on their way to their home in Thrace” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.230). Iris had found them feasting together in the house of the stormy West Wind (23.200–201; see [[zephyrus]], [[boreas]] and [[iris]]), and Achilles had prayed to the two at once (23.194–195), the pair that the simile sets blowing together. Hera's road to Lemnos in Book 14 led over the same northern waters: she sped over the snowy mountains of the Thracians and stepped from Athos onto the billowing sea (14.227–230; see [[lemnos]]). The half-verse οἴδματι θύων is said also of the Scamander in flood, as he rushes on Achilles, “raging with his swell” (21.234; see [[scamander]]).

The prize for the armed duel is Thracian too: “this silver-studded sword, / a fine Thracian one, which I took from Asteropaeus” (23.807–808; see [[sword]], [[asteropaeus]] and [[thracians]]). Helenus kills Deipyrus with “a great Thracian sword” (13.576–577), and in the last book Priam's finest cup is a gift that Thracian men gave him on an embassy (24.234–235): Thracian work was prized on both sides of the war.
