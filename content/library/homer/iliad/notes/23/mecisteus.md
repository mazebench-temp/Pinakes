# Μηκιστῆος (23.678) occurs only here; Μηκιστέος (2.566) is claimed by the entry. Unnamed: ὅς, "who" (23.679).
grc: [Μηκιστῆος]
refs: [23.679-680]
---

{{quote:8a8e061a-1536-57cb-ab90-f11c81a0883d}}

The verse that names him is the Catalogue's, “son of lord Mecisteus, son of Talaus” (2.566; see [[talaus]] and [[euryalus]]), with the genitive Μηκιστῆος for Μηκιστέος. Here the poet adds the one deed told of him. He went to Thebes for the funeral of Oedipus, and there beat all the Cadmeians (23.679–680; see [[oedipus]], [[cadmeians]] and [[thebes]]). πάντας ἐνίκα, “he defeated all”, recalls Tydeus, who went to Thebes as an envoy, challenged the Cadmeians feasting in the house of Eteocles “to contests, and won them all” (πάντα δʼ ἐνίκα, 4.389; again 5.807; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]); in later tradition both men fell at Thebes in the war of the Seven, and Herodotus names the Theban Melanippus as the man who killed Adrastus' brother Mecisteus and his son-in-law Tydeus (5.67.3; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]] and [[herodotus]]). The relative ὅς, “who”, is Mecisteus, as Leaf notes, not his son (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.679). Pausanias cites Mecisteus at the funeral games of Oedipus as the reason, from Homer, why he cannot believe Sophocles' account of Oedipus' death (*Description of Greece* 1.28.7; see [[oedipus]] and [[pausanias]]).

So the boxer goes out with a father who won at funeral games, and is beaten at Patroclus' (23.689–699; see [[epeius]]), as Nestor, shortly before, remembered winning at the games of Amarynceus (23.629–637; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). Achilles' games are full of the memory of older games.

The other Mecisteus of the Iliad, son of Echius, is an Achaean of the poem's own generation (8.333, 13.422, 15.339; see [[mecisteus-son-of-echius]]).
