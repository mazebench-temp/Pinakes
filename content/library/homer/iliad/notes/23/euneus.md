---
summary: The silver mixing bowl, first prize of the footrace, had been given by Euneus, son of Jason, to Patroclus as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam (23.746–747).
---

“and as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam, it was given / to the hero Patroclus by Euneus, son of Jason” (23.746–747). Ἰησονίδης Εὔνηος is the name and title under which he sent ships of wine from Lemnos in Book 7 (7.468; see [[jason]] and [[lemnos]]). The verse-end ὦνον ἔδωκε, “paid the price”, is that of the line in which Achilles sold Lycaon into Lemnos, “and the son of Jason paid the price” (21.41; see the note on Book 21). Patroclus is not named there, and Walter Leaf finds it natural enough that he should have acted in the sale (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.746).

Leaf takes the price of 21.41 to be this bowl, and the “worth of a hundred oxen” that Lycaon says he fetched (21.79) to be the value of the bowl, not the substance of the payment (on 21.41 and 21.79). By that reckoning it was worth far more than the great tripod of the wrestling, which the Achaeans valued at twelve oxen, or the woman beside it, valued at four (23.703–705), the lines to which Leaf refers (see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[trade]]).

The bowl had come to Euneus from Thoas, king of Lemnos, to whom Phoenician traders gave it (23.744–745; see [[thoas-of-lemnos]] and [[phoenicians]]). Achilles sets it out “in honor of his companion” (23.748), the dead man who had received it, and Odysseus wins it in the footrace (23.778; see [[patroclus]] and [[odysseus]]).
