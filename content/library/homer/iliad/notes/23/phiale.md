summary: The bones of Patroclus are gathered into a golden bowl, wrapped in a double fold of fat, to wait for those of Achilles (23.243–254); and a two-handled bowl, never set on the fire, is the fifth prize of the chariot race, which Achilles gives to Nestor as a memorial of the funeral (23.270, 23.615–619).

# Iliad only: in the Poetics and the Rhetoric φιάλη is the classical bowl of Aristotle's metaphor.

**The bones.** When the pyre has been quenched, Achilles bids the Achaeans gather the bones of Patroclus, “And let us put them in a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, / until I myself am hidden in Hades” (23.243–244). They do so: “the white bones of their gentle companion / they gathered into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, / and laid them in the huts and covered them with a soft linen cloth” (23.252–254; see [[cremation]] and [[patroclus]]). δίπλακι δημῷ, Walter Leaf explains, is a double layer of fat to keep out air and moisture (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.243). The bowl is to wait for Achilles' own bones, as the soul of Patroclus had asked that one urn should hold the bones of both (23.83–92; see [[the-golden-urn]] and [[achilles]]), and Leaf thinks the mound heaped up over the pyre a cenotaph, the bones staying in the hut to go home with those of Achilles (on 23.254; see [[tomb]]). He takes the bowl for the same vessel as the golden two-handled jar of 23.92, a verse athetized in antiquity (on 23.92 and 23.243); in the Odyssey the bones of the two lie mingled in a golden two-handled jar, the gift of Thetis (*Odyssey* 24.72–77).

**The prize.** The fifth prize of the chariot race is “a two-handled bowl untouched by fire” (23.270). Five teams ran, but Eumelus, who came in last, was given the corselet of Asteropaeus instead (23.558–565; see [[eumelus]] and [[corselet]]), “And the fifth prize was left, / the two-handled bowl; Achilles gave it to Nestor” (23.615–616), with the words “Here now, old man, let this be a treasure for you too, / to be a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus” (23.618–619; see [[nestor]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). μνῆμα, “memorial”, is found only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey Helen gives Telemachus a robe as a memorial of her own hands, and Odysseus kept the bow of Iphitus at home as a memorial of a dear guest-friend (*Odyssey* 15.126, 21.40). The vessel of the bones and the vessel of remembrance bear the same name.
