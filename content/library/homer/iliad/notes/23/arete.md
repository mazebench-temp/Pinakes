---
summary: The word is used of horses and of a driver. Achilles stays out of the race because his immortal horses “surpass the rest in prowess” (23.276), and on the last stretch “the prowess of each / showed itself” (23.374–375). Menelaus, passed by a trick, tells Antilochus, “You have shamed my prowess”, and will not have it said that he took the mare because “he himself was the greater in prowess and strength” (23.571, 23.578).
---

{{quote:67a3dd5d-619a-55d2-9a25-fd9268d90704}}

For Walter Leaf the four uses are four senses: speed at 276 and 374, as in Polydorus' “prowess of his feet” (20.411); skill at 571; and at 578 dignity or rank, as in Phoenix's list of what the gods have, “prowess and honor and strength” (9.498) (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.571). The translation keeps “prowess” for all four (see its note on 23.571).

The race is a test in which ἀρετή is seen, as Idomeneus said of the ambush, “where most of all the prowess of men is seen clearly” (13.277). On the way back toward the sea the prowess of each team “showed itself” (φαίνετο), and “the horses' running was strained to the full” (23.374–375; see [[horses]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). Achilles' own horses would have won, since they are immortal, Poseidon's gift to Peleus (23.276–278), but they stand mourning their charioteer (see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

Menelaus' complaint turns on the word. Antilochus has passed him with worse horses, by a trick, so the race has not shown his ἀρετή but shamed it (ᾔσχυνας, 23.571; see [[aischros]]). If he took the mare by his standing, someone would say that he had overpowered Antilochus with lies, his horses being far worse, “but he himself was the greater in prowess and strength” (23.575–578). Leaf reads the two clauses as parataxis: Menelaus takes the prize because, though his horses were beaten, he had the advantage in rank and power (on 23.577). So Menelaus asks for an oath instead (23.579–585; see [[themis]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
