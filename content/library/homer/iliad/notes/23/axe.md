**The woodcutters.** For the wood of the pyre the men “went holding woodcutting axes in their hands / and well-plaited ropes” (23.114–115), and felled the oaks of Ida with the long-edged bronze (23.117–119; see [[ida]]). ὑλοτόμος, “woodcutting”, is found in Homer only here, of the axes and of the men who carry them (23.114, 23.123). In Nestor's lesson to his son the woodcutter, δρυτόμος, is the first example of skill: “By cunning, far more than by strength, is a woodcutter better” (23.315; see [[cunning-not-strength]] and [[metis]]).

**The archery.** “Then for the archers he set out dark iron: / he set down ten double axes and ten single axes” (23.850–851; see [[iron]]). The man who hits the dove tied to the mast is to “take up all the double axes and carry them home”, and the one who hits the cord, “since he is the lesser”, the single axes (23.855–858; see [[mast]] and [[doves]]). Teucer cuts the cord and Meriones kills the bird, “And Meriones took up all ten double axes, / and Teucer carried the single axes to the hollow ships” (23.882–883; see [[meriones]] and [[teucer]]). Walter Leaf takes the iron to be the axe-heads themselves, and reports that the scholia explain πελέκεις and ἡμιπέλεκκα as weights of iron. He thinks such a standard by no means impossible: if iron, like gold and silver, was bartered in wedges of known weight, they might well be called “axes” and “half-axes”, and the prize would not then be named first by its material. Aristarchus compared the axes of the trial of the bow in the Odyssey, through which Odysseus shoots (*Odyssey* 19.572–575, 21.120–122), a comparison Leaf calls “quite unintelligible” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.850).
