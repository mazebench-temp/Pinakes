summary: Two of Plato's charges come from this book, the hair sacred to the Spercheius that Achilles gave to the dead Patroclus, in a half-verse that Plato quotes, and the slaughter of the captives at the pyre. Proclus answered that the hair was owed to the river only on a homecoming that would never come, and read the whole work of the pyre, the winds, the night's libation and the twelve Trojans, as a rite for the soul of Patroclus.

**The hair.** Plato's charge quotes the poem. Achilles, Socrates says, gave the hair sacred to the other river, the Spercheius, to Patroclus the hero, saying Πατρόκλῳ ἥρωϊ κόμην ὀπάσαιμι φέρεσθαι, though he was dead (*Republic* 3.391b). The half-verse is 23.151 as it stands in our text: “I would grant my hair to the hero Patroclus to take with him.” In the poem Achilles, standing apart from the pyre, cuts the fair hair “which he had grown thick and long for the river Spercheius”, and speaks to the river: his father Peleus had prayed that when his son came home he would cut his hair for the river and sacrifice fifty uncut rams into its waters, where it has a precinct and an altar; “So the old man prayed, but you did not fulfill what he had in mind.” Since he is not going home, he gives the hair to Patroclus, and places it in his companion's hands (23.141–153; see [[spercheius]], [[peleus]], [[hair]] and [[the-lock-for-the-spercheius]]). What Plato counts as defiance of a god is in the poem the end of a vow for a homecoming that Achilles knows he will not have (see [[nostos]]).

**The captives.** Plato's last charge, “the slaughters of the captives at the pyre” (*Republic* 3.391b), is the end of a vow. Achilles took twelve young men alive out of the river “as the penalty for Patroclus” (21.26–32), having vowed over the body to cut their throats before the pyre (18.336–337), and at the start of this book he tells the dead man that he is now fulfilling the vow (23.19–23). At the pyre he throws on four horses and two of his dogs, “and twelve brave sons of the great-hearted Trojans, / cutting them down with the bronze; and he devised evil deeds in his mind” (23.171–176), and calls to his companion that he is fulfilling all he promised: the fire is eating the twelve along with him, but Hector he will give to the dogs (23.179–183; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Walter Leaf denies that κακά here passes any moral judgment on Achilles: “The word means only that what he did was ill work for his victims”, as there is no condemnation of Zeus when he devises κακά in Book 7 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.176; 7.478).

**Proclus on the hair.** Proclus answers that the first and preferred good for Achilles was to cut his hair for the Spercheius on his return, as promised. But when he had given up hope of returning, persuaded by his mother's prophecy, “for straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.96), it was surely necessary, as the second best, to cut it in honor of his friend; so Socrates himself, in the *Second Alcibiades*, accepts and puts on the garlands that Alcibiades was bringing to the god. Besides, the hair had not yet become the river's: the man who had promised to dedicate it after his return, when he lost the return, lost the dedication with it (*On the Republic* I 149 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**Proclus on the pyre.** On the captives Proclus gives two answers. By what appears, Achilles completed his honors to Patroclus with them and did nothing crueler than if he had killed them, like the others, at the river; they fared better, burned whole, than if they had been torn by beasts, as Lycaon was left to the fishes (I 151–152 Kroll; 21.122–123). Then, “if we must recall the more secret teaching of our master”, the whole business of that pyre imitates the theurgists' immortalizing of the soul, leading the soul of Patroclus up to the life apart from the body. That is why Achilles stands before the pyre and calls on the winds, Boreas and Zephyrus, so that the visible vehicle may be tended through their visible motion and the diviner one be purified unseen and restored to its own place, drawn up by the rays of the air, the moon and the sun; and why he is said to pour libation all night long, “taking a two-handled cup … from a golden mixing bowl, / … calling on the soul of poor Patroclus” (Proclus quotes 23.219 and 23.221). The poet all but proclaims, Proclus says, that Achilles' work was for the soul of his friend and not only for what was seen, and that everything in it is a symbol: the golden mixing bowl the source of souls, the libation its outflow, which channels a better life into the single soul, and the pyre the undefiled purity that can lead the soul from bodies to the unseen (I 152–153 Kroll; 23.192–225; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[libation]]). The twelve slain at the pyre were set beside the soul of Patroclus as its attendants, and Achilles chose their number as the most fitting for those who are to follow, the number given over to the complete processions of the gods. He did these things not from savagery but by priestly laws laid down for the souls of those who died in war, some aiming at justice, some by the law of war, some by sacred rites; and in all of them the poet kept the measures of imitation (I 153 Kroll).
