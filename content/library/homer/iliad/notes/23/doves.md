---
summary: The dove of Book 23 is the archers' mark. Achilles ties a timid dove by the foot to the mast of a ship set up on the sand; Teucer's arrow cuts the cord and she flies free, and Meriones, vowing a hecatomb to Apollo, shoots her high under the clouds, and she settles on the mast and dies like a warrior (23.850–883).
---

**The mark.** For the archery Achilles sets out ten double axes and ten single axes, “and he set up the mast of a dark-prowed ship / far off on the sand, and to it he tied a timid dove / by the foot with a thin cord” (23.851–854; see [[axe]] and [[mast]]). Whoever hits the dove takes the double axes home; whoever hits the cord, missing the bird, is the lesser shot and takes the single axes (23.855–858). Walter Leaf thinks it the extreme of absurdity to set a prize beforehand for so unlikely a shot as cutting the cord (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.850; see the translation's notes on 23.850–883 and 23.855–858). τρήρων, “timid”, is the dove's standing epithet (23.853, 23.855, 23.874; see [[timid]]). The dove of the similes is the prey of hawks and falcons: Artemis fled from Hera like a dove from a hawk, and Achilles chased Hector as a falcon swoops on a timid dove (21.493–496, 22.139–142; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]] and [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]). Here the hunter is an archer, and the bird is tied.

**The shots.** The lot falls first to Teucer. He shoots with force but makes no vow to Apollo, and the god begrudges him the bird: the arrow strikes the cord beside her foot and cuts it, “Then the bird darted up toward the sky, and the cord / hung loose toward the ground; and the Achaeans shouted in applause” (23.862–869; see [[teucer]] and [[apollo]]). Meriones snatches the bow, vows a hecatomb of firstborn lambs, “And high up under the clouds he saw the timid dove; / and there, as she circled, he struck her under the wing, in the middle, / and the shaft went straight through” (23.870–876; see [[meriones]] and [[lambs]]). The arrow falls back and sticks in the ground in front of his foot. Leaf finds the description hopelessly confused: a shot from the side could hardly fall at the archer's feet, and a bird struck right overhead could hardly fly to the far-off mast (on 23.875).

**The death.** “and the bird, / settling on the mast of the dark-prowed ship, / let her neck hang down, and her thick feathers drooped. / And swiftly the spirit flew from her limbs, and far from him / she fell” (23.877–881). The bird dies in the words of men and beasts killed in battle. λίασθεν, “drooped”, is the verb of a warrior sinking face down when a spear has gone through him (15.543; Leaf on 23.879), and the θυμός that flies from her limbs is the spirit that flew from the horse Pedasus when Sarpedon's spear struck him (ἀπὸ δʼ ἔπτατο θυμός, 16.469; see [[thymos]]). Whether she falls far from the mast or far from Meriones is not clear (see the translation's note on 23.880). The people gaze and are amazed (23.881), in the half-verse that was said of the wrestlers (23.728).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] copies the contest in his games for Anchises, where a ship's mast is raised and a fluttering dove is hung from it by a cord as the mark (*erigit et volucrem traiecto in fune columbam, / quo tendant ferrum, malo suspendit ab alto*, *Aeneid* 5.488–489). Leaf notes that Virgil lets the bird die outright (on 23.875): struck high in the clouds, she falls lifeless, leaving her life among the stars of the upper air, and brings back the arrow fixed in her as she falls (*decidit exanimis vitamque reliquit in astris / aetheriis fixamque refert delapsa sagittam*, *Aeneid* 5.517–518).
