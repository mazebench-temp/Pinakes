**At the feast and the pyre.** At the funeral feast “Many sleek oxen bellowed around the iron / as their throats were cut” (23.30–31): the iron is the slaughterer's blade. On the pyre Achilles “let loose in it the iron might of the fire, to feed on them” (23.177), where Walter Leaf glosses σιδήρεον as “relentless”, comparing the iron din of battle and the heart of iron that Hector found in Achilles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.177; 17.424, 22.357; see [[not-stone-or-iron]]).

**The lump of iron.** Among the prizes brought from the ships are “fair-belted women, and gray iron” (23.261), the goods Achilles once said he would take home (9.366). For the weight throw he sets out a prize with a history:

{{quote:d558e540-52a3-57d5-af85-6823325cbac4}}

σόλον αὐτοχόωνον is to Leaf most naturally a mass of iron as it came from the furnace; others took it for meteoric iron, which he thinks unfit for making farm tools, and he notes that the oldest iron found at Hissarlik was two lumps that Schliemann dug up in 1890, much too small to serve as a discus (on 23.826). Eetion is Andromache's father, whom Achilles killed when he sacked Thebe; she says that he did not strip him of his armor but burned it with him (6.414–419; see [[eetion]]), and the lump came away “with the rest of the possessions” (23.829; see [[possessions]]). Leaf finds the speech of the prize obscure, and the picture of shepherd and plowman supplied with iron from a raw lump a suspiciously deliberate attempt to represent an archaic stage, when in Homer the smith is already a specialist (on 23.832 and 23.835). The lump is thrown like a discus, and Polypoetes, who throws it as far as a herdsman throws his stick, carries off “the king's prize” (23.839–849; see [[discus]], [[polypoetes]] and [[the-herdsmans-throwing-stick]]).

**The archers' iron.** “Then for the archers he set out dark iron” (23.850), ten double axes and ten single (see [[axe]]). ἰόεις, “dark”, stands here in place of the usual epithets πολιός, “gray”, and αἴθων, “gleaming”; Leaf reports that Aristarchus explained it instead as iron fit for arrows, ἰοί, a prize suited to archers (on 23.850).
