---
summary: The might of fire, of the sun and of horses. Achilles lets loose “the iron might of the fire” on the twelve Trojans (23.177), and Apollo shades Hector's body “lest the might of the sun should first / parch the skin” (23.190–191). In the race Athena “put might into his horses” for Diomedes (23.390, 23.400), the mares of Eumelus swerve off “when fury seized their hearts” (23.468), and “the noble might” of Agamemnon's mare grows on the last stretch (23.524–525).
---

The translation's key-word row for μένος sets out the senses: “might” is what a god puts into men and horses, “fury” the rage of runaway mares. Athena's gift to Diomedes' horses is the gift she gave Diomedes himself in Book 5, “might and daring” (5.2), given now in a race (see [[athena]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

Walter Leaf glosses σιδήρεον, of the fire's might, as “relentless” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.177; see the translation's note on 23.177), and πυρὸς μένος returns when the pyre is quenched, “as far as the might of the fire has reached” (23.238; see [[cremation]]). With a genitive the word makes a periphrasis for a man: “the strong might of godlike Leonteus” rises for the weight throw (κρατερὸν μένος, 23.837; see [[leonteus]]), as “the great strength of Eetion” once threw the same lump (μέγα σθένος, 23.827).
