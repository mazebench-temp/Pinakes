summary: The book is his funeral. The Myrmidons drive around his body and weep, and Achilles promises him Hector and twelve Trojans; his soul comes to Achilles in his sleep, asks for burial, tells how he came to Peleus' house as a boy who had killed another, and asks that their bones lie in one urn. He is carried out under his companions' hair, given Achilles' lock, and burned on a pyre a hundred feet each way; his bones are gathered into a golden bowl and a mound is heaped. At the games he is remembered in Achilles' horses, which mourn their kind charioteer, and in the prizes, among them the spoils he won (23.9–257, 23.280–284, 23.619–620, 23.747–748, 23.776, 23.800).

**Mourned.** “Let us go near / and weep for Patroclus”, Achilles tells the Myrmidons (23.8–9), and they drive their horses three times around his body; their armor is wet with tears, “such a master of rout did they long for” (23.13–16; see [[myrmidons]] and [[master-of-rout]]). The title is Diomedes' and Hector's in the battle books (6.97, 12.39). Walter Leaf notes that here the body lies out on the shore, where the Myrmidons and Achilles sleep around it, though in Book 19 it lay in the hut (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.13; 19.211–212). Achilles lays his hands on his breast and tells him that he is fulfilling his promises, and he stretches Hector face down beside “the bier of the son of Menoetius” (23.18–25; see [[son-of-menoetius]], [[achilles]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**The soul.** In the night “the soul of poor Patroclus” comes to Achilles in his own likeness and speaks: Achilles has forgotten him dead, who did not neglect him living; he must be buried quickly, for the souls keep him from crossing the river into Hades; the two will never again sit apart from their companions and take counsel, and Achilles too is fated to die at Troy (23.65–81; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[psyche]]). Then he speaks of their boyhood:

The poem tells his coming to Peleus' house only here. He had killed the son of Amphidamas over a game of knucklebones, “a foolish child, not meaning to” (23.88), and Menoetius brought him to Peleus, who reared him and made him Achilles' θεράπων, “attendant” (23.85–90; see [[amphidamas-of-opus]], [[menoetius]], [[peleus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The word was the narrator's for him in the books of his death: “the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.165, 17.388), “the godlike attendant” (16.865), “the attendant of Achilles” (18.152). Leaf thinks the exile was told to reconcile the Patroclus who lived in Peleus' house when the army gathered (11.765–790) with the promise to bring him home to Opus (18.324–327; on 23.87; see [[opus]]). He was the elder of the two (11.786–787), and Achilles greets the soul as ἠθείη κεφαλή, “dear heart”, which the scholia, as Leaf reports, call the address of a younger man to an elder (23.94; on 23.94; see [[dear-heart]]). Their bones are to share one urn, the golden jar that Thetis gave her son (23.91–92; see [[the-golden-urn]]).

**To the pyre.** In the morning his companions carry him out “in the middle” of the armed Myrmidons, cover him with their hair, and Achilles holds his head, sending “his noble companion” to Hades (23.134–137; see [[hair]]). At the pyre Achilles puts in his hands the lock he had grown for the Spercheius, “to the hero Patroclus to take with him” (23.151–152; see [[spercheius]]); ἥρως is said of him again when the prizes are set out, the bowl given to “the hero Patroclus” as the price of Lycaon (23.747). He is laid on the top of the pyre, wrapped in the fat of the victims from head to foot, with jars of honey and ointment against his bier, four horses, two dogs and twelve young Trojans (23.165–176; see [[cremation]], [[pyres]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). “But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not burn” (23.192), until the winds come at Achilles' prayer and Iris' word, to the pyre “on which lies / Patroclus, over whom all the Achaeans groan” (23.210–211; see [[iris]]). All night Achilles calls on his soul and pours wine on the ground (23.218–221; see [[libation]]).

**The bones.** At dawn his bones are easy to know, “for he lay in the middle of the pyre, and the others apart / were burned at its edge, horses and men all mingled together” (23.239–242). His companions gather “the white bones of their gentle companion” into a golden bowl, lay them in the huts and heap the mound (23.250–257; see [[tomb]]). ἐνηής, “gentle”, takes up the noun ἐνηείη, “gentleness”, with which Menelaus called for him when the fight for his body was at its height, “now let each remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus; / for he knew how to be kind to all” (17.670–671; see [[eneeie]]). Leaf takes the mound for a cenotaph, the bones being kept for the journey home with those of Achilles (on 23.254); in the Odyssey they lie mingled with Achilles' in a golden amphora under the tomb on the Hellespont (*Odyssey* 24.76–84).

**Remembered at the games.** Achilles will not race his horses, for they have lost “such a charioteer, / a kind one” (ἠπίου), who used to wash their manes and pour oil on them, and they stand mourning him (23.280–284; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The prizes keep his name. Nestor's bowl is “a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus; for you will not see him / among the Argives any more” (23.619–620; see [[nestor]]); the Sidonian mixing bowl was Euneus' price for Lycaon, paid to Patroclus (23.741–748; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[euneus]]); the armor set out for the duel is Sarpedon's, “which Patroclus had taken from him” (23.800; see [[sarpedon]]); and the son of Oileus slips in the dung of the oxen Achilles killed “in honor of Patroclus” (23.775–776; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
