**The hut and the wood.** The kings lead Achilles, still grimed with blood, “to brilliant Agamemnon” and have the heralds set a tripod over the fire for a bath (23.35–41; see [[heralds]] and [[bathing]]). Achilles refuses it on oath and asks a service of the commander: “and at dawn urge them on, Agamemnon, lord of men, / to bring wood” (23.49–50; see [[lord-of-men]]). At dawn “powerful Agamemnon / urged on mules and men to bring wood / from all the huts”, under the eye of Meriones (23.110–113; see [[powerful]], [[mules]] and [[meriones]]).

**At the pyre.** When the light would have set on their lament, Achilles turns to him again: “Son of Atreus, for it is your words above all that the army of the Achaeans / will obey” (23.156–157). He is to send the army to its meal and leave the pyre to those who care for the dead, with the leaders. Agamemnon does it at once (23.155–163; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). In the assembly of the first book the two quarreled over Achilles' demands (1.101–187); now Agamemnon does what Achilles asks without a word (see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). At dawn the leaders gather around him, and their din wakes Achilles, who addresses them through him: “Son of Atreus, and you others, the best of all the Achaeans” (23.233–236). The prizes of the games are announced to him first in the same way, “Son of Atreus, and you other well-greaved Achaeans” (23.272, 23.658).

**In the games.** Menelaus drives Agamemnon's mare Aethe with his own Podargus; Echepolus, son of Anchises, a rich man of Sicyon, had given her to Agamemnon “so that he might not have to follow him to windy Ilios” (23.295–299; see [[echepolus-son-of-anchises]] and [[menelaus]]). In the race the son of Atreus is Menelaus, and the mare that Antilochus mocks, “Aethe, female though she is”, is his brother's (23.409, 23.525; see [[antilochus]]). Idomeneus, quarreling with the son of Oileus over who leads the race, proposes that they wager a tripod or a cauldron and make “Agamemnon, son of Atreus, the arbiter” (23.485–487; see [[idomeneus]]).

**The javelin.** The last contest of the book is his. When Achilles sets out a spear and a cauldron worth an ox, “up rose the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon”, and Meriones with him (23.884–888; see [[wide-ruling]]). Achilles does not let them throw:

{{quote:1dba1509-eeec-540a-8f2f-c07e6a13afb6}}

“So he spoke, and Agamemnon, lord of men, did not disobey” (23.895). He gives the bronze spear to Meriones and the cauldron to his herald Talthybius (23.896–897; see [[talthybius]] and [[cauldron]]). Walter Leaf finds a certain dignity in his being recognized without a contest in which his rank would not let him be beaten (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.884; see the translation's note on 23.884–886). The book that brought Achilles to Agamemnon's hut as a guest ends with Achilles giving him honor before the army (see [[achilles]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
