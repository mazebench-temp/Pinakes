summary: Two of the passages that Plato would strike out are in this book, the soul of Patroclus going beneath the earth “like smoke, gibbering”, and Achilles' cry that there is some soul and phantom even in the house of Hades, but no mind in it. In the poem they are one scene, the vanishing of the ghost; Proclus read the cry as Homer's own distinction of soul, image and mind.

**Plato's verses.** Plato quotes Achilles' cry, “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104), and, a little after it, the soul that “went beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering” (23.100–101), which he sets beside the souls of the suitors, gibbering like bats (*Republic* 3.386d–387a; *Odyssey* 24.6–9). He does not say where the verses come from. The second passage he begins after οὐδʼ ἔλαβε, “but did not take hold of it”, and ends before Achilles springs up, so that nothing in it shows whose soul it was. In the poem they belong to one scene. The soul of Patroclus has stood over the sleeping Achilles, like him in stature, eyes and voice, and has asked to be buried; Achilles reaches out to embrace it, “but did not take hold of it”, and the soul goes beneath the earth; he springs up, strikes his hands together and cries out (23.62–104; see [[patroclus]], [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]). His next words give his reason: “for all night long the soul of poor Patroclus / stood over me, lamenting and weeping, / and charged me with each thing, and it was wondrously like him” (23.105–107).

**τις or τι.** Plato's text of the cry reads ἦ ῥά τις, “there is some soul and phantom”, as ours does. Walter Leaf prefers the well-attested τι, which gives “the soul is something even in Hades”, not wholly destroyed, and says that it must have been the reading that Propertius had before him when he wrote “sunt aliquid manes”, “the shades are something” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.103; see the translation's note on 23.103–104 and [[propertius-the-ghost-of-cynthia]]).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus names among Homer's sayings about Hades “the image and the soul carried about bereft of mind” and “the smoke and the gibbering” (*On the Republic* I 118 Kroll). He finds in them an inspired teaching. Homer distinguishes the soul, its image and the mind of the soul: the soul uses the image, and the mind is more divine than both; the image and the soul can in some way be known, and they are still held by the body, show themselves its guardians and long for its care even when it is not given, but the mind cannot be grasped by the motions of our imagination. “That is why Achilles too, when he saw Patroclus speaking about the burial of his body, believed that in Hades there is a soul and an image, but that there is no mind in them, nor any wisdom using them”: the workings of the irrational led him to grant the first two, but he could not confirm the presence of the soul's intellect from what he saw in a dream (I 120–121 Kroll). The gibbering is true as well. Souls that leave the body unwillingly use shadowy, heavy and earthy vehicles, and give out a voice without meaning, a material sound, “which poetry called gibbering”; as the vehicles of souls led upward give out a harmonious voice, so the sound of those under the earth is like gibbering, the image of a life of appetite and imagination alone (I 121 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
