**What Aeschines makes of it.** Before the verses are read, Aeschines tells the jury what is in them. As Achilles slept by the pyre, “as the poet says”, the image of Patroclus stood over him and spoke of such things, and charged him with such things, that one may well weep at them and envy their virtue and their friendship: warning him that he too was not far from the end of his life, he charged him to arrange beforehand, if he could, that as they had been reared and had lived in the same place, so when they died their bones should lie in the same coffer. And lamenting, and going through the times they had spent together, he said that no longer, as before, would they sit alone together, apart from their other friends, and take counsel about the greatest things; for, Aeschines adds, he thought such faith and goodwill the thing most to be longed for (*Against Timarchus* 146–147). Then he bids the clerk read “what Patroclus says in sleep about their being buried together” (149). In the poem Achilles is asleep on the shore among the Myrmidons, and the pyre is built only the next day (23.59–65, 23.110–165); he sleeps beside the pyre after the burning (23.231–232). Aeschines' “alone together, apart from their other friends” is his reading of “apart from our dear companions” (23.77).

**The verses he had read.** His text differs from ours more than his verses of Book 18 do. It begins οὐ γὰρ ἔτι, “for no longer”, for our οὐ μὲν γάρ (77). It has Τρώων εὐηγενέων, “of the well-born Trojans”, where ours has εὐηφενέων, “wealthy” (81), and Leaf notes that the reading with γ is as old as Aeschines. After the prophecy that Achilles will die beneath the wall of the Trojans it adds a verse, μαρνάμενον δηίοις Ἑλένης ἕνεκʼ ἠυκόμοιο, “fighting with the enemy for the sake of fair-haired Helen”, which Leaf takes for a reminiscence of Achilles' words in Book 9 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.81; 9.327, 9.339). The next verse ends σὺ δʼ ἐνὶ φρεσὶ βάλλεο σῇσιν, “and lay it to heart”, as the formula ends elsewhere (see [[lay-it-to-heart]]), where ours has “and charge you with it, if you will obey me” (82). After “do not lay my bones apart from yours, Achilles” (83) come two verses that ours lacks, “but so that the same earth may hide you too, / in the golden two-handled jar that your lady mother gave you”, and then ὡς ὁμοῦ ἐτράφομέν περ, “as we were reared together”, for our ἀλλʼ ὁμοῦ ὡς ἐτράφημεν (84). The speech then runs as ours to “so too let one urn enclose the bones of us both” (91), and stops there: the golden jar, our last verse (92), has been read already.

**Leaf's judgment.** Leaf calls the quotation “the longest from H. in any classical writer”, and its large variations “a valuable glimpse into the popular texts of his day”. In place of 84 Aeschines has three verses “which are in all respects worse”: “grammar and thought are alike confused, and ἀμφιφορεῖ is a non-Epic form”. The quotation, he says, may console us for the loss of such corrupt texts (on 23.77). Yet at 84, where the manuscripts' ἐτράφημεν gives an augment without parallel in Homer, Buttmann's conjecture ὡς τράφομέν περ, which Leaf thinks almost certainly right, finds some support in Aeschines' reading (on 23.84). Verse 92 was athetized in antiquity as brought in from the Odyssey, where Thetis gives the golden jar for Achilles' bones after his death (*Odyssey* 24.73–77); Leaf agrees, and observes that the jar had entered the text by Aeschines' day (on 23.92; see the translation's notes on 23.84 and 23.91–92).

**The request in the poem.** The ghost asks for one urn as the last claim of a life shared since boyhood, when Menoetius brought him from Opus to Peleus' house after he had killed the son of Amphidamas over the knucklebones (23.83–90; see [[menoetius]], [[opus]] and [[amphidamas-of-opus]]). Achilles promises to do it all (23.95–96), and has the bones gathered into a golden bowl “until I myself am hidden in Hades” (23.243–244). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles that his white bones lie in the golden jar that his mother gave, mixed with the bones of Patroclus (*Odyssey* 24.73–77). Aeschines reads the request as part of the evidence for a love that Homer, he says, leaves unnamed (*Against Timarchus* 142; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]).
