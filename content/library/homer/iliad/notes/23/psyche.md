---
summary: The soul of Patroclus comes to Achilles in his sleep, “like him in all things” (23.66), asks to be buried so that it may pass the gates of Hades, and goes “beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering” when Achilles reaches for it (23.100–101). Achilles cries out that “even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104), and at the pyre he pours wine all night, “calling on the soul of poor Patroclus” (23.221).

{{quote:99962af3-b84d-52c3-a71a-320e97d226e2}}

**The visit.** ἦλθε δʼ ἐπὶ ψυχή, “there came to him the soul” (23.65), is the phrase with which the shades come up one after another to Odysseus in the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.84, 11.90, 11.387, 11.467). Walter Leaf finds ἐπί more significant there, of each soul following the last; here it is the unexpected approach (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.65). The soul comes as dreams do, standing above the sleeper's head (see [[dream-scene]] and [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]), and it is the man as he was, in stature, eyes, voice and clothes (23.66–67; see [[eidolon]]).

**The soul kept out.** Until the body is burned, the soul cannot join the others. “The souls keep me far away, the phantoms of the dead”, it says; they will not let it mingle with them beyond the river, and it wanders “through the wide-gated house of Hades” until it has been given “my portion of fire” (23.71–76; see [[hades]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). πυρὸς λελάχητε is the verb of Hector's terms for his own body in Book 7, that the Trojans may give him his portion of fire (λελάχωσι, 7.79–80). The translation's note on 23.71–74 sets out how this differs from the Odyssey, where the unburied Elpenor is not kept from the shades (*Odyssey* 11.51–83), and how Leaf explains the difference.

**The soul and the mind.** Achilles reaches out and takes nothing; the soul goes under the earth like smoke, τετριγυῖα, “gibbering”, the sound the suitors' souls make as they follow Hermes down, like bats in a cave (*Odyssey* 24.6–10). Leaf takes the thin voice to be as weak a copy of the living voice as the phantom is of the man (on 23.101). Achilles' cry draws the conclusion: there is something even in Hades, a soul and a phantom, but no φρένες in it (23.103–104; see [[phrenes]]). Monro–Allen print τίς, “there is some soul”; Leaf prefers τι, “the soul is something even in Hades” (on 23.103; see the translation's note on 23.103–104).

**Poor Patroclus.** The phrase Πατροκλῆος δειλοῖο, “of poor Patroclus”, follows the soul three times: when it comes, when Achilles tells of its visit, and when he calls on it at the pyre, drawing wine from a golden mixing bowl all night (23.65, 23.105, 23.218–221; see [[libation]]). Its one use before this book is Menelaus' call to the Achaeans, as they fight over the body, to “remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus” (17.670).

In the Odyssey the soul of Patroclus comes up to Odysseus with the souls of Achilles, Antilochus and Ajax (*Odyssey* 11.467–470; again 24.15–18), and the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles that his bones lie mixed with those of Patroclus in a golden jar (*Odyssey* 24.76–77; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
