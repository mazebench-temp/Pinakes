---
summary: Achilles plans one mound for Patroclus and for himself. He has a “great barrow” in mind from the start (23.126), and over the bones he asks for no great tomb yet, “but one just such as is fitting; and afterwards, you Achaeans, / make it wide and high, you who after me / are left behind among the many-benched ships” (23.246–248). In the race the turning post may be “the mound of some mortal who died long ago” (23.331), and the bowl that Achilles gives Nestor is to be “a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus” (23.619).

**One mound for two.** The barrow (ἠρίον, only here in Homer; see the translation's note on 23.126) is planned “for Patroclus and for himself” (23.126), and the soul of Patroclus has asked that their bones lie together in one urn (23.82–92; see [[the-golden-urn]]). So the mound that the Achaeans heap over the pyre is a first stage, made modest on purpose: τοῖον, in Walter Leaf's paraphrase, “as seemly as you will — but not extravagant” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.246), and the men who outlive Achilles are to make it wide and high (23.246–248). The bones are not laid in it but kept in the hut under a linen cloth (23.252–254), and Leaf calls the mound a cenotaph (on 23.254; see [[tomb]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). The Odyssey tells the end. The bones of Achilles lie mixed with those of Patroclus in a golden jar, and the army heaped over them a great and noble tomb on a headland by the Hellespont, to be seen far out at sea by men now and hereafter (*Odyssey* 24.76–84).

**The old mound.** The turning post that Nestor describes, a dry stump with two white stones leaning against it, is either “the mound of some mortal who died long ago” or a post set up “in the time of earlier men” (23.326–333): a tomb whose owner no one can name. Leaf calls the passage “hopelessly obscure” (on 23.326), and reports Heyne's remark that there is no evidence in antiquity for wooden posts as grave monuments (on 23.331; see [[turning-post]] and [[the-stump-that-rots]]). The same book holds both kinds of tomb: the one Achilles wants raised high for men to come, and one that is already only a landmark.

**Memorials.** The prizes keep the dead man's memory too. The fifth prize of the chariot race is left over, since Eumelus, who came in last, has been given a corselet instead, and Achilles gives it to Nestor, “to be a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus” (μνῆμʼ, 23.618–619). μνῆμα occurs only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it names the robe that Helen gives Telemachus, “a memorial of Helen's hands”, and the bow that Iphitus gave Odysseus (*Odyssey* 15.126, 21.40). Fame belongs to the dead charioteer as well: the horses of Achilles stand mourning because “they have lost the noble fame of such a charioteer” (23.280; see [[kleos]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
