summary: Idomeneus watches the chariot race from a lookout outside the gathering. He is the first to make out the leading horse and guesses that Diomedes is ahead; Ajax, son of Oileus, mocks his eyes and his bluster, and he offers to wager a tripod or a cauldron on it, until Achilles stops the quarrel (23.448–498). Diomedes comes in first. His attendant Meriones brings the wood for the pyre and competes in three contests.

**The lookout.** While the Argives sit in the gathering watching the horses, Idomeneus, “leader of the Cretans”, sits outside it, “highest of all, at a lookout”, and is the first to make them out (23.448–451; see [[cretans]]). He hears from far off the man who shouts at the horses and knows him, and he sees a horse in the lead, bay, with a white mark on its forehead, “round like the moon” (23.452–455; see [[horses]]). He stands and speaks:

{{quote:138f963e-fc87-501f-a3c2-ff60dcceb124}}

He saw the mares that led “first swinging around the turning post”, and now cannot see them: did their driver lose the reins at the turn and break his chariot? The man in front, he thinks, is “an Aetolian by lineage”, Diomedes (23.462–472; see [[turning-post]] and [[diomedes]]). Walter Leaf supposes that the far part of the course was hidden from the gathering by the lie of the plain, so that even Idomeneus, from his height, could not see the turn (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.451). He reports that Kurtz and many others rejected 23.462–464, which make the turn visible, and that 23.471 was athetized in antiquity (on 23.462, 23.471).

**The quarrel.** “And swift Ajax, son of Oileus, rebuked him shamefully” (23.473; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[aischros]]). Why does he bluster as he always has? He is not by so much the youngest of the Argives, nor are his eyes the sharpest; he need not be a blusterer, “for there are others here, better men”; the mares in front are still Eumelus' (23.474–481). Leaf recalls that Idomeneus is “half gray” (on 23.476; 13.361). Idomeneus answers in anger:

{{quote:39e8b65b-1368-5a57-bcb1-060ceb0dcb71}}

ἴστωρ, “arbiter”, is the word of the trial on the shield of Achilles, where two men “were eager to win a decision at the hands of an arbiter” (18.501; Leaf on 23.486). Leaf finds Idomeneus' anger cooling as he speaks, so that he ends with a wager instead of an insult (on 23.483). The son of Oileus rises at once to answer, “and now the strife between the two would have gone further still, / had not Achilles himself stood up and spoken” (23.488–491; see [[eris]]). Achilles bids them stop: they would be indignant with another man who did such things; let them sit and watch, and they will see whose horses are in front (23.492–498; see [[achilles]] and [[nemesis]]). Leaf reports that Heyne rejected 23.493, where Ajax and Idomeneus are named together (on 23.493). Diomedes comes in first, as Idomeneus said (23.499–513).

**His attendant.** At dawn Meriones, “the attendant of manly Idomeneus”, led the men who brought wood for the pyre (23.113 = 23.124), and in the games he is three times “the good attendant of Idomeneus” (23.528, 23.860, 23.888; see [[meriones]]).
