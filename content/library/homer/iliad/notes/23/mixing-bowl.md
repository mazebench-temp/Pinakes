**The golden bowl.** While the winds fan the pyre, Achilles, “taking a two-handled cup, drew wine from a golden mixing bowl / and poured it on the ground, and soaked the earth” (23.219–220; see [[cup]], [[wine]] and [[libation]]). The bowl of the feast here serves the dead, and the wine is not drunk but poured out, all night long, as he calls on the soul of Patroclus (23.218–221).

**The silver bowl.** The first prize of the footrace has the longest history of any prize of the games.

{{quote:8a94f5a4-7b52-530d-aefc-309fd91a94e7}}

Achilles sets it out “in honor of his companion, / for whoever should prove the nimblest with his swift feet” (23.748–749). τετυγμένον, “well wrought”, means adorned, not plain, as the tripod of the wrestling is ποιητός, “well-made” (23.718; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.741), and the same words, κρητῆρα τετυγμένον, begin the verse in which Menelaus promises Telemachus a silver bowl with a golden rim, the work of Hephaestus and a gift of the king of the Sidonians (*Odyssey* 4.615–619; see [[sidonians]]). Leaf notes that this is the only place where the subject of νικᾶν, here “surpassed”, is a concrete thing (on 23.742). The bowl passed from its Sidonian makers to Phoenician traders, to Thoas of Lemnos, and to Euneus, who gave it to Patroclus as the price of Lycaon (23.743–747; see [[phoenicians]], [[thoas-of-lemnos]], [[euneus]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]); Leaf takes the “worth of a hundred oxen” that Lycaon says he fetched (21.79) for its value (on 21.79). The second prize is an ox, “great and rich with fat”, and the last half a talent of gold (23.750–751; see [[talent]]). Odysseus, helped by Athena when Ajax slips in the dung, “took up the mixing bowl, / since he came in first” (23.778–779; see [[odysseus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
