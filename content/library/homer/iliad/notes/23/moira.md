---
summary: The soul of Patroclus tells Achilles, “it is your fate too, Achilles, like the gods, / to die beneath the wall of the wealthy Trojans” (23.80–81); and Nestor, thanking Achilles for his prize, says, “Yes, my child, all that you have said is fair and right” (23.626).
---

The prophecy comes from the dead, in the night before the burning (see [[psyche]], [[ker-doom]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]), and it is the ground of the request that follows, one urn for the bones of both (23.82–92; see [[the-golden-urn]]). Achilles accepts it when he gives his orders for the mound, which those who outlive him are to finish (23.243–248; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).

Nestor's verse is Agamemnon's answer to him in Book 1, “Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right” (1.286), with τέκος, “my child”, in place of γέρον, “old man”. There it put aside his advice; here the old man accepts a gift. Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus probably left out 23.626 as taken from 1.286 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.626; see [[geron]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).
