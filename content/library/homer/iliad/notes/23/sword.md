---
summary: The prize of the armed duel is the silver-studded Thracian sword that Achilles took from Asteropaeus, and he gives it to Diomedes with its sheath and its well-cut strap (23.807–808, 23.824–825).
# φάσγανον (23.807, 23.824) is claimed by the entry, ἐϋτμήτῳ τελαμῶνι (23.825) by its note on Book 7.
---

For the armed duel Achilles sets out the armor of Sarpedon, to be shared by the two fighters, and promises a sword to the one who first reaches the other's flesh through his armor (23.805–806): “to him I will give this silver-studded sword, / a fine Thracian one, which I took from Asteropaeus” (23.807–808; see [[silver-studded]], [[thracians]] and [[asteropaeus]]). Achilles had killed Asteropaeus at the river with his own sword and stripped him of his armor (21.179–183). Walter Leaf finds it far from clear how Sarpedon's armor is to be held in common, and the sudden addition of the sword unexpected (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.808).

Ajax and Diomedes fight until the Achaeans, terribly afraid for Ajax, bid them “stop and take equal prizes” (23.822–823); “But the hero gave the great sword to the son of Tydeus, / bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut strap” (23.824–825; see [[diomedes]], [[sheath]] and [[well-cut]]). The second verse is the one with which Hector gave Ajax his silver-studded sword at the end of their duel (7.303–304; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]): both combats end undecided, with the gift of a sword. Leaf takes κῦρε (23.821) as “kept lighting on his neck”, touching without wounding, so that Diomedes met the terms and won the sword; if it means only “kept aiming at”, there is no reason for the award, and Aristarchus and Aristophanes athetized 824–825. He finds the whole narrative “as self-contradictory as it is obscure” (on 23.821).
