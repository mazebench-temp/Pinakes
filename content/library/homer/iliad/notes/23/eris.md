summary: Strife among the spectators and rivalry among the competitors. The quarrel of Idomeneus and Ajax, son of Oileus, would have gone further, “had not Achilles himself stood up” (23.490–491). Antilochus tells his horses not to “strive against” the horses of Diomedes (23.404), and says after the footrace that it is hard for the Achaeans “to contend with” Odysseus in running, “except for Achilles” (23.791–792).
grc: [ἐριζέμεν, ἐριδήσασθαι]
en: [strive against, contend]
---

The games are rivalry under rules, and the book shows how near it lies to quarrel. The spectators' strife is in words (23.473–498; see [[neikos]] and [[nemesis]]); the competitors' rivalry is the will to win, and twice a contest is stopped with equal prizes before it turns to harm (23.735–737, 23.822–823; see [[nike]] and [[contests]]). Antilochus will not strive with the horses to which Athena has given speed and glory, but with those of Menelaus (23.403–409; see [[kydos]]), and the quarrel he starts there is settled without an oath (23.566–613; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

ἐριδήσασθαι is found only here in Homer, and some ancient commentaries read ἐριζήσασθαι. Walter Leaf finds the line ill expressed, since it would naturally mean that it is hard to match the Achaeans in speed, and the contracted dative Ἀχιλλεῖ without parallel in Homer (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.792; see [[geron]]).
