summary: The soul of Patroclus tells Achilles that “The souls keep me far away, the phantoms of the dead” (23.72), and Achilles, when it has slipped from his arms, says that there is in the house of Hades “soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.104).
en: [phantoms]
---

ψυχαὶ εἴδωλα καμόντων, “the souls … the phantoms of the dead”, is the Iliad's only use of the word for the dead; its other εἴδωλον is the phantom of Aeneas that Apollo makes in Book 5 (5.449). The same words name the dead in the Odyssey's second underworld, where the suitors' souls come to the meadow of asphodel (ψυχαί, εἴδωλα καμόντων, *Odyssey* 24.14), and the shade of Achilles calls the dead “phantoms of worn-out mortals” (βροτῶν εἴδωλα καμόντων, *Odyssey* 11.476). καμόντες, “those who have toiled”, are the dead in the oath of Book 3 too, where the powers below the earth punish “dead men, whoever has sworn a false oath” (3.278–279; see [[oath-formulas]]).

The likeness is exact. The soul is “like him in all things” (πάντʼ αὐτῷ … ἐϊκυῖα, 23.66), and Achilles says that “it was wondrously like him” (ἔϊκτο δὲ θέσκελον αὐτῷ, 23.107). αὐτός is the man himself, as Apollo's phantom was “like Aeneas himself” (αὐτῷ τʼ Αἰνείᾳ ἴκελον, 5.450) and the bodies of the proem are “the men themselves” (1.4; see [[psyche]]). The voice is a copy too: the soul goes “gibbering” (τετριγυῖα, 23.101), and Walter Leaf takes the thin voice to be as weak a copy of the living voice as the εἴδωλον is of the man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.101).

In the Odyssey the first shade to come to Odysseus is also an unburied companion's: the εἴδωλον of Elpenor asks to be burned and given a mound by the sea (*Odyssey* 11.51–83; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).
