The truce of the *Aeneid* follows Book 7, but some details of the funerals that it allows come from this book. On the shore the Trojans, “girt in shining arms”, run three times around the kindled pyres and three times ride around the mournful fire, wailing; “the earth is sprinkled with tears, and sprinkled are the arms” (*spargitur et tellus lacrimis, sparguntur et arma*, *Aeneid* 11.188–191). At the start of this book the Myrmidons, with Achilles leading them, “three times … drove their well-maned horses around the body, / weeping”, and “The sands were wet, and wet was the armor of the men / with tears” (23.12–16; see [[myrmidons]] and [[lament]]). Walter Leaf sets Virgil's verse beside Homer's (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.15). Virgil's verse repeats its verb as Homer's does, δεύοντο … δεύοντο, “were wet … wet was”.

Then many cattle are slaughtered around the pyres for Death, and bristly swine and sheep driven in from the fields are killed over the flames, and the Trojans watch their companions burn and do not leave the half-burnt pyres until night has turned the sky (*Aeneid* 11.197–202). Before Patroclus' pyre many sheep and cattle are flayed and their fat laid over the body, and all night long Achilles pours wine and calls on the soul of his friend, mourning beside the pyre until the morning star (23.166–169, 23.217–228; see [[cremation]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).
