σκοπός, “lookout”, is a man posted to watch, as Polites sat as a lookout for the Trojans, trusting in his speed of foot, and as two lookouts sat apart from the army in the ambush on the shield of Achilles (2.792; 18.523; see [[turning-post]]). Phoenix is to keep the running in mind and report the truth (see [[truth-and-deception]]). The old man is called ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (see [[antitheos]]), and ὀπάων πατρὸς ἑοῖο, “his father's squire”: Peleus took him in, made him rich and set him over the Dolopians, and sent him to Troy with his son to teach him “to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.438–443, 9.480–484; see [[peleus]]). He is “the old horse-driving Phoenix” in the embassy, the marshaling of the Myrmidons and the mourning for Patroclus (9.432, 16.196, 19.311; see [[horse-driving]]), and so a fitting watcher of a chariot race.

He does nothing more. When Menelaus disputes the result, no one asks the lookout what he saw; Menelaus asks the leaders to judge, and then proposes an oath (23.566–585). Walter Leaf reports that critics rejected 23.360, or all of 23.359–361, for that reason, and that if, with Aristarchus, Phoenix is to count the laps (δρόμους), there seems to have been only one (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.360). Phoenix is not named again in the Iliad.
