---
summary: Gold serves the dead and pays the living. Achilles pours libations “from a golden cup” and draws wine “from a golden mixing bowl” at the pyre (23.196, 23.219), and the bones of Patroclus are gathered “into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat” (23.253). In the games two talents of gold are the fourth prize of the chariot race and half a talent the last of the footrace (23.269, 23.751), while the first prize of the footrace is “a mixing bowl of silver, well wrought” (23.741).
---

Three golden vessels serve the dead man: the cup of the libations, the mixing bowl from which the wine is drawn and poured on the ground, and the bowl that is to hold the bones until Achilles' own are laid with them (23.196, 23.219, 23.243–244, 23.253; see [[libation]], [[the-golden-urn]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). The verse about the golden two-handled jar that Thetis gave her son (23.92) was rejected in antiquity as taken from the Odyssey, where Thetis gives a golden amphora for the bones of Achilles and Patroclus (*Odyssey* 24.73–77; see the translation's note on 23.91–92). Hector's bones too will be laid in a golden chest (χρυσείην ἐς λάρνακα, 24.795).

As a prize, gold ranks low. Two talents of gold, the sum set in the middle of the trial on the shield (18.507), are here only the fourth prize, below a cauldron untouched by fire, and Meriones, fourth home, takes them (23.267–269, 23.614–615; see [[cauldron]] and [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]); Walter Leaf refers to the small value of the Homeric talent (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.269). Half a talent is the prize of the last runner, and Achilles gives another half to Antilochus for his praise (23.751, 23.795–796; see [[ainos]]). When Antilochus tells Achilles to give Eumelus a prize of his own, the first thing he names is “much gold in your hut” (23.549; see [[pity]]).

Silver comes twice, in the prize of the footrace, the Sidonian bowl with its long history (23.741–747; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[sidonians]]), and in the silver-studded sword of Asteropaeus that Diomedes wins in the armed duel (φάσγανον ἀργυρόηλον, 23.807–808, 23.824; see [[sword]] and [[asteropaeus]]). Diomedes' chariot, “overlaid with gold and tin”, runs so close behind his horses that it leaves hardly a track in the dust (23.503–506; see [[chariot]]).
