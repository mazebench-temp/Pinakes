summary: Book 23 is the book of horses. The Myrmidons drive their teams three times around the dead Patroclus, four horses are thrown on his pyre, and Achilles' immortal horses stand mourning their charioteer; then five teams run the chariot race, in which the gods take a hand, the best mares are wrecked, and the slowest horses come in second by a trick (23.6–14, 23.171–172, 23.276–284, 23.287–652).
# ἱπποσύνας (23.307) and ἵππου (23.525) occur only here in the Iliad. "stallion" also links 20.224 (ἵππῳ).

**The funeral.** The Myrmidons are ταχύπωλοι, “of the swift horses” (23.6; see [[of-the-swift-horses]]), and Achilles will not let them unyoke until they have mourned with the horses and the chariots: “And three times they drove their well-maned horses around the body” (23.13). Only then do they loose “their high-neighing horses” (23.27; see [[high-neighing]]). At dawn they yoke again for the procession to the pyre, “the horsemen in front, and behind them followed a cloud of foot soldiers” (23.133). On the pyre, after the sheep and the cattle and the jars of honey and ointment, Achilles throws “four horses with high-arched necks” (πίσυρας δʼ ἐριαύχενας ἵππους, 23.171), then two of his nine table dogs and the twelve Trojans (23.171–177; see [[cremation]] and [[dogs]]). When the fire is out, the bones of Patroclus are easy to tell apart, because the others, “horses and men all mingled together”, burned at the edge of the pyre (23.240–242). Achilles' own horses do not run. They are immortal, Poseidon's gift to Peleus, and they stand mourning the charioteer who used to wash their manes and pour olive oil on them, “and their manes / rest on the ground” (23.276–284; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), as they wept for him on the battlefield with their heads bowed to the ground and their rich manes stained (17.426–440).

**The teams.** Five drivers yoke for the race (23.287–351; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[chariot]]). Eumelus drives the mares of the grandson of Pheres, the best horses at Troy, which Apollo had reared in Pereia (2.763–767; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]] and [[swift-as-birds]]). Diomedes leads under the yoke the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas (23.291–292; 5.319–327, 8.105–108; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]); the race calls them his ἄρσενες ἵπποι, “stallions” (23.377). Menelaus yokes his own Podargus, a name that one of Hector's horses also bears (8.185), beside Agamemnon's mare Aethe, “straining hard for the race” (23.293–300); Echepolus of Sicyon had given her to Agamemnon so as not to follow him to Troy (see [[aethe-and-podargus]]). Antilochus drives “horses born in Pylos” (23.303), and Meriones' horses are the slowest of all (23.530). Nestor tells his son what Diomedes told Nestor in Book 8, “your horses are slow” (8.104): the Pylian horses “are the slowest at running” (23.309–310). When the poem brought them on it called them ὠκύποδες, “swift-footed” (23.304), an ornamental epithet that Walter Leaf calls very harsh in this place (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.304). What the horses lack the driver must make up. Nestor shows him how to take the turn: “but the right-hand horse / goad on with a shout, and let the reins go with your hands. / And at the post let your left-hand horse press close” (23.336–338; see [[metis]] and [[cunning-not-strength]]). He ends with the famous horses of the past, “brilliant Arion, / the swift horse of Adrastus, who was of the stock of the gods”, and “the horses of Laomedon, the good ones that were reared here” (23.346–348; see [[arion-the-horse]] and [[laomedon]]).

**The race.** The drivers raise their whips, strike the horses with the straps and shout at them (23.362–364). The dust rises beneath the horses' chests like a cloud or a storm, “and their manes streamed in the blasts of the wind” (23.365–367; see [[like-a-cloud-or-a-storm]]). “And the chariots at one moment skimmed the bountiful earth, / and at another bounded high in the air” (23.368–369), and the horses “flew on, raising the dust of the plain” (23.372; again 23.449). On the way back the mares of Eumelus take the lead, and the horses of Tros come so close behind that “they always seemed about to mount the chariot, / and with their breath Eumelus' back and broad shoulders / grew warm; for they flew with their heads laid on him” (23.379–381). Then the gods take a hand. Apollo strikes the whip from Diomedes' hand, and his horses are hindered, “running without the goad”; Athena gives it back and “put might into his horses”, and she breaks the yoke of Eumelus' mares, which run apart off the road while he is thrown out beside the wheel (23.383–397; see [[apollo]] and [[athena]]).

**Horses spoken to.** The drivers talk to their horses, as Hector and Achilles did to theirs (8.184–197, 19.399–403; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]). Antilochus “called to the horses of his father” (23.402), in the half-verse with which Achilles called to his father's horses before he went out to battle (19.399). “You two as well, step out! Go at full stretch, as fast as you can” (23.403). He does not ask them to race the horses to which Athena has given speed, only not to be shamed by “Aethe, female though she is. Why are you lagging, good sirs?” (23.409). θῆλυς ἐοῦσα is said once more in the Iliad, of Hera, who, “female though she was”, deceived even Zeus (19.97). And he threatens them: “there will be no keep for you two with Nestor, shepherd of the people, / but he will kill you at once with the sharp bronze” (23.411–412). They run harder, “fearing their lord's rebuke” (23.417). Menelaus, who has held back his mares in the narrow place, calls on them in turn: “Do not hold back, do not stand still, grieving at heart. / Their feet and knees will grow weary sooner / than yours; for both of them are lacking in youth” (23.443–445). The next verse is the one that told the answer of Antilochus' horses (23.446 = 23.417).

**Seen from afar.** The Argives sit in the gathering and watch the horses fly on in the dust (23.448–449). Idomeneus, who sits outside it, highest of all, at a lookout, is the first to make out the leader, “a horse, conspicuous, in the lead, / which was bay in all the rest, but on its forehead / there was a white mark, round like the moon” (23.453–455; see [[like-the-moon]] and [[idomeneus]]). φοῖνιξ, “bay”, describes a horse only here in Homer; elsewhere it is the purple with which a woman stains ivory (4.141). Leaf notes that a round mark must be the full moon (on 23.455). The quarrel that follows is about whose horses are in front; for the son of Oileus they are still Eumelus' mares, “the high-stepping mares, speeding over the wide plain” (23.475; see [[high-stepping]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**The finish.** Diomedes comes in driving with the whip from the shoulder, “and his horses / lifted themselves high as they swiftly made their way”. The drops of dust strike the charioteer, and the chariot runs so lightly that it leaves no deep track in the fine dust (23.499–506). At the finish “much sweat poured / from the horses, from their necks and from their chests to the ground” (23.507–508), and Sthenelus unyokes them (23.513). Antilochus is second, “by crafty ways, not by speed” (23.515; see [[kerdos]]), and Menelaus is as close behind him as a horse is to the wheel of its own chariot (23.517–523; see [[the-horse-and-the-wheel]]), “for the noble might was growing / of Agamemnon's mare, fine-maned Aethe” (23.524–525; see [[fine-maned]]). Meriones' “fine-maned horses were the slowest” (23.530). Eumelus comes in last of all, “dragging his fine chariot, driving his horses in front of him” (23.533).

**The mare.** The second prize is a mare, “six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal in her womb” (23.265–266; see [[mules]]), and she is the prize the book fights over. Achilles would give her to Eumelus, Antilochus will not give her up, Menelaus claims her, and in the end Antilochus yields her to Menelaus and Menelaus gives her back (23.536–613). Leaf thinks that ἀδμήτη, “unbroken”, implies that an unbroken spirit was a recommendation to the heroic horseman (on 23.266). Menelaus asks Antilochus to stand in front of his horses and chariot with the whip in his hand, “and touching the horses, by the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth, / swear that you did not willingly hamper my chariot by a trick” (23.582–585; see [[oath]]). Poseidon gave Peleus his horses (23.277–278) and taught Antilochus horsemanship with Zeus (23.307); Leaf calls this the only passage in Homer that brings Poseidon into any special relation with the horse (on 23.277, with 23.307 and 23.584; see [[poseidon]]).

**Nestor's memory.** At the games for Amarynceus, Nestor says, he won every contest but one: “Only with horses did the two sons of Actor drive past me” (23.638). They were twins, and one held the reins while the other whipped the horses on (23.641–642; see [[the-moliones]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). The old man has no more chariot races to run, and Achilles gives him the fifth prize without a contest (23.615–623; see [[nestor]]).
