summary: When Achilles threatens to give Hector to the dogs, Aphrodite keeps the dogs from the body by day and by night and anoints it with rose-sweet, ambrosial oil, so that the dragging may not tear the skin; Apollo covers it with a cloud against the sun.
# Unnamed: her anointing of the body (23.186-187).
refs: [23.186-187]
passages:
  - 23.184-187 | Aphrodite keeps the dogs from Hector
---

Achilles ends his second address to the dead Patroclus with a threat: Hector, “the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs” (23.183; see [[achilles]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). The narrator answers him at once:

{{quote:909d5ddc-fa57-582a-91ad-5aeb384c0faa}}

ἄλαλκε, “kept … from”, takes back Achilles' own words to the dying Hector: “there is no one who will keep the dogs from your head” (ἀπαλάλκοι, 22.348; see [[the-death-of-hector]] and [[dogs]]). Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “daughter of Zeus” (see [[daughter-of-zeus]]). The oil is ἀμβρόσιος, “ambrosial”, the oil with which Hera anoints herself before she goes to Zeus, “ambrosial and sweet” (14.171–172; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]), and the body is kept sound as Thetis kept that of Patroclus, when she dripped ambrosia and red nectar through his nostrils, “so that his flesh might be sound” (19.38–39; see [[thetis]]). ῥοδόεις, “rose-sweet”, occurs only here in Homer. Walter Leaf allows that it may mean no more than “fragrant”, or allude to the old belief that oil of roses preserves, and notes that Homer has the adjective but never names the rose (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.186; see the translation's note on 23.184–191). The flower first appears, as he says, in the *Hymn to Demeter*, among those that Persephone was picking (ῥόδα, *Hymn to Demeter* 6).

Apollo covers the body with a dark cloud against the sun (23.188–191; see [[apollo]]). Fäsi and Düntzer rejected 184–191, among other reasons because Aphrodite nowhere else shows Hector any special regard; Leaf answers that her coming implies no special regard: she acts with Apollo because the two are the leading patrons of Troy (on 23.184). In Book 24 it is Apollo alone who keeps the body from all disfigurement and covers it with the golden aegis (24.18–21).
