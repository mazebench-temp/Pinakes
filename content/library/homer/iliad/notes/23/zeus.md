---
summary: Zeus takes no part in the funeral or the games, and the book has no scene on Olympus. He is named in Achilles' oath, “by Zeus, who is highest and best of the gods”, that he will not wash until Patroclus is burned; as the father of Aphrodite; as the god who gave Echepolus the wealth with which he bought himself out of the war; with Poseidon as Antilochus' teacher in horsemanship; and in the wrestling, when Ajax leaves the outcome to him and Achilles stops the bout.
---

**The oath.** The kings bring Achilles to Agamemnon's hut and have a tripod set over the fire for his bath, “But he refused stubbornly, and swore an oath besides: / ‘No, by Zeus, who is highest and best of the gods, / it is not lawful that water for washing should come near my head / until I have laid Patroclus on the fire and heaped up a mound / and cut my hair’” (23.42–46; see [[achilles]], [[oath-formulas]], [[bathing]] and [[themis]]). The second half of the oath's first verse is Agamemnon's at the reconciliation in Book 19, “Now let Zeus be witness first, highest and best of the gods” (19.258; see the note on Book 19 and [[oath]]), and Odysseus' when he swears to Penelope (*Odyssey* 19.303). ὅς τίς τε, with a definite person as its antecedent, is almost without parallel. Walter Leaf quotes Monro, who took the verse for an old formula, “Zeus, or by whatever name the highest of the gods is to be called”, and compared the chorus of Aeschylus, Ζεύς, ὅστις ποτʼ ἐστίν, “Zeus, whoever he is” (*Agamemnon* 160); Leaf adds Euripides' Ζεύς, ὅστις ὁ Ζεύς (*Heracles* 1263) and Hecuba's prayer to Zeus, “whoever you are, hard to know” (*Trojan Women* 885–886; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.43; see the translation's note on 23.43).

**Father and giver.** Aphrodite, who keeps the dogs from Hector, is “daughter of Zeus” (23.185; see [[aphrodite]] and [[daughter-of-zeus]]). Echepolus, son of Anchises, gave Agamemnon his mare Aethe “so that he might not have to follow him to windy Ilios, / but might stay at home and take his pleasure; for great wealth / had Zeus given him” (23.297–299; see [[sicyon]] and [[agamemnon]]). So in the Catalogue the son of Cronus “poured wondrous wealth” on the Rhodians (2.670; see [[rhodes]]). Nestor tells Antilochus that “they have loved you, / Zeus and Poseidon, and have taught you horsemanship / of every kind” (23.306–308; see [[antilochus]] and [[nestor]]). Aristarchus preferred the singular, “taught”, as though Poseidon alone had to do with horsemanship; Leaf thinks that Poseidon teaches the race of Neleus as its ancestor, and that Zeus is joined with him as the ultimate source of all gifts from the gods (on 23.307; see [[poseidon]]).

**Nurtured by Zeus.** In the dispute over the second prize, Menelaus calls Antilochus διοτρεφές, “nurtured by Zeus”, as he bids him swear (23.581), and Antilochus answers with the same word as he gives way (23.594; see [[zeus-nurtured]], [[menelaus]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). Aristarchus athetized 581 because the title of honor is out of place in anger; Leaf finds the ground inadequate, since such titles are used even by enemies (on 23.581; see the translation's note on 23.581). Menelaus and Odysseus are each called διογενής, “sprung from Zeus” (23.294, 23.723; see [[sprung-from-zeus]]).

**The business of Zeus.** In the wrestling neither Ajax nor Odysseus can throw the other, and the Achaeans grow weary of watching, until Ajax proposes a change: “either lift me, or I you; and all the rest will be the business of Zeus” (23.724; see [[ajax]], [[odysseus]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). Odysseus throws Ajax by a trick, and in the second bout both fall together; when they would wrestle a third time, Achilles stands up and stops them: “victory is for both; take equal prizes” (23.734–736; see [[contests]]). The decision Ajax left to Zeus is made by the man who gives the games.
