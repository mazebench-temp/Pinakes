# “sparkling wine”.
---

**Poured for the dead.** All night, while the winds blow on the pyre, Achilles, “taking a two-handled cup, drew wine from a golden mixing bowl / and poured it on the ground, and soaked the earth, / calling on the soul of poor Patroclus” (23.219–221; see [[libation]], [[cup]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[psyche]]). The words are those of the libation at the oath in Book 3, “And drawing wine from the mixing bowl in cups / they poured it out” (3.295–296), where the wine poured “on the ground”, χαμάδις, was the image of the oath-breakers' brains (3.300; see [[oath]]). Here it is drunk by no one: the earth is soaked for the dead.

**Quenching the pyre.** At dawn Achilles bids the leaders “first put out the pyre with sparkling wine, / all of it, as far as the might of the fire has reached” (23.237–238), and they do so, “as far as the flame had come, and the ash had fallen deep” (23.250–251; see [[pyres]] and [[cremation]]). Walter Leaf notes that the practice was common in Rome as in Greece (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.237). The same verse is used of the pyre of Hector at the end of the poem (24.791), and in the Odyssey the soul of Agamemnon tells Achilles that the Achaeans gathered his white bones at dawn “in unmixed wine and ointment” (*Odyssey* 24.72–73).
