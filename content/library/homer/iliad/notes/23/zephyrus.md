**His house.** Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind to make the pyre burn (23.192–198; see [[boreas]] and [[prayer]]), and Iris finds the winds “all together in the house of the stormy West Wind, / feasting at a banquet” (23.200–201; see [[iris]] and [[feast]]). Ζεφύροιο … ἔνδον, “in the house of the West Wind”, is said as Διὸς ἔνδον, “within the house of Zeus” (20.13; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.200). δυσαής, “stormy” (see [[stormy]]), is his epithet in the *Odyssey* too (*Odyssey* 5.295, 12.289). Leaf observes that the West Wind of Homer is always a stormy and dangerous wind except in the Elysian plain, where Oceanus sends up his breezes to cool men (*Odyssey* 4.567; on 23.200). The breath that ripens the fruit in the orchard of Alcinous is another exception (*Odyssey* 7.118–119; see the article above). When Iris comes, the winds all spring up and each calls her to sit by him (23.202–203), and Leaf finds a touch of humor in the scene (on 23.200; see [[iris]] and the translation's note on 23.200).

**Clamorous.** In Iris' message Achilles prays to “the North Wind and the clamorous West Wind” (23.208). κελαδεινός, “clamorous”, is elsewhere in the Iliad an epithet of Artemis, “the clamorous” (16.183, 20.70, 21.511; see [[clamorous]], [[artemis]] and the translation's key-word row κελαδεινός); in the *Odyssey* the West Wind is κελάδων, “roaring”, when Athena sends it as a fair wind for Telemachus' ship (*Odyssey* 2.420–421).

**On the pyre.** The winds come with a wondrous din, blow on the pyre all night, and at the morning star go home over the Thracian sea (23.212–230; see [[boreas]], [[thrace]] and [[pyres]]). The West Wind is the father of Achilles' immortal horses, whom the Harpy Podarge bore to him (16.149–151; see the note on Book 16); in the games they stand mourning their charioteer, their manes resting on the ground (23.283–284; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
