summary: Antilochus drives his father's slow horses in the chariot race. Taught by Nestor to win by cunning at the turning post, he forces his way past Menelaus where the road narrows and comes in second “by crafty ways, not by speed”; he claims the second prize when Achilles would give it to Eumelus, and then yields it to Menelaus rather than swear that he won it fairly. In the footrace he comes in last, and wins half a talent with his praise of Achilles (23.301–613, 23.755–797).

**Nestor's son.** He is the fourth to make ready, “the splendid son of Nestor, the high-hearted lord, / the son of Neleus”, and his horses were born in Pylos (23.301–304; see [[pylos-born]]). His father stands beside him and gives him the longest speech of the games. Zeus and Poseidon have taught him horsemanship, and he knows how to wheel around the turning posts, but his horses are the slowest in the race; he must win by μῆτις, “cunning”, as the woodcutter, the helmsman and the charioteer do, and Nestor shows him how to drive close around the post (23.305–348; see [[nestor]], [[metis]] and [[turning-post]]). Walter Leaf explains Poseidon's part as that of the ancestor of the house, the father of Neleus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.307 and 23.584). His lot leaps out first, and Leaf takes him to have had the inside place (23.352–354; on 23.358).

**The narrow road.** On the way back he urges on his father's horses, not against Diomedes, whose horses Athena has made swift, but against Menelaus' mare: “and this I myself will contrive and devise, / to slip past him in the narrow road, and it will not escape me” (23.403–416; see [[aethe-and-podargus]]). Where winter water has broken away part of the road and Menelaus is driving, “shunning a clash of wheels”, Antilochus turns his horses off the road and presses on after him (23.418–424). Menelaus shouts, “Antilochus, you drive heedlessly; hold in your horses!”, but Antilochus drives on harder, “as if he had not heard”, and Menelaus holds back rather than let the chariots collide and overturn (23.426–437; see [[menelaus]] and [[chariot]]). Leaf takes the road for a gully that serves as a watercourse in winter, with room for one chariot, so that Antilochus forces Menelaus to give way or crash (on 23.420). Menelaus' rebuke is the first word of the dispute that follows:

{{quote:b3ed2838-b9dc-55d6-9016-24e53652d1f7}}

πεπνῦσθαι, “to be wise”, is the verb of the epithet πεπνυμένος, “wise”: Menelaus will call him “who were wise before” (23.570), and the narrator gives him the epithet as he answers (23.586).

**Second.** “And after him Antilochus, of the line of Neleus, drove in his horses, / by crafty ways, not by speed, having got in ahead of Menelaus” (23.514–515; see [[kerdos]]). Νηλήϊος, rendered here “of the line of Neleus” (23.514), is said elsewhere of Nestor (10.18, 23.349), of Nestor's horses (11.597) and of Pylos (11.682), and Leaf compares Αἰακίδης, the name Achilles takes from his grandfather (on 23.514; see [[neleus]]). Menelaus comes in so close behind him that the narrator measures the gap by a horse and the wheel it draws, and he would have passed Antilochus if the course had been longer (23.516–527; see [[the-horse-and-the-wheel]]).

**The claim.** When Eumelus comes in last, Achilles pities him and proposes to give him the second prize, the mare, and the Achaeans approve; but Antilochus stands up and answers Achilles “claiming his right” (δίκῃ, 23.534–542):

{{quote:25c4569b-8f9c-581b-ba8c-ac01f6320841}}

His words recall Achilles' own to Agamemnon in Book 1: “and now you threaten to take away my prize yourself” (1.161; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). Leaf takes δίκῃ for a formal appeal to the custom, not “with justice”, since Antilochus, though in the right against Eumelus, is in the wrong toward Menelaus (on 23.542). This time the quarrel ends at once: “So he spoke, and brilliant swift-footed Achilles smiled, / rejoicing in Antilochus, since he was his dear companion”, and he gives Eumelus the corselet he took from Asteropaeus (23.555–565; see [[achilles]] and [[eumelus]]). It is the only time Achilles smiles in the Iliad.

**The oath refused.** Menelaus takes the scepter and accuses him of throwing his worse horses in front and so hindering his own, and asks him to stand before his horses with the whip in his hand and swear by Poseidon that he did not do it by a trick (23.566–585; see [[oath]]). Antilochus does not swear. He pleads his youth, “for I am much younger / than you”, and gives up the mare, offering something greater from his store besides rather than “fall from your heart and be an offender against the gods” (23.587–595; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). Leaf reads ἀλιτρός, “an offender”, as a confession that he cannot take the oath (on 23.595). Menelaus answers in kind: Antilochus was “never flighty or light of mind / before; but this time youth has overcome your mind”, and since “you have suffered much and toiled much, / you and your brave father and your brother, for my sake”, he gives him back the mare, though she is his (23.602–611). His brother is not named; the only other son of Nestor at Troy in the Iliad is Thrasymedes (see [[thrasymedes]]). Antilochus' companion Noemon leads her away (23.612–613; see [[noemon-companion-of-antilochus]]).

**The footrace.** He rises third for the footrace, after the son of Oileus and Odysseus, “for he, for his part, beat all the young men in running” (23.755–756), and comes in last. He takes the half talent of gold “smiling” and speaks:

{{quote:15c18ba6-5620-521d-b3c9-6d6a2c61c32e}}

“So he spoke, and gave glory to the swift-footed son of Peleus” (23.793), and Achilles answers, “Antilochus, your praise shall not be spoken in vain”, and adds another half talent of gold (23.795–797; see [[odysseus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The young man who forced his way past an elder in the chariot race ends his part in the games by giving way to his elders, and is paid for it (see [[old-age-and-memory]]).
