summary: The book performs what the dead are owed. The Myrmidons drive three times around the body and weep, “for that is the prize of the dead” (23.9); Achilles will not wash until Patroclus is on the fire, the mound heaped and his hair cut (23.43–47); and the soul of Patroclus asks, “Bury me as quickly as may be, so that I may pass the gates of Hades” (23.71). The pyre, the bones and the mound follow, and then games in the dead man's honor, while Hector's body lies unburied, kept from the dogs by Aphrodite and Apollo (23.182–191).

**The dues.** Achilles names them in his oath: the fire, the mound, the shorn hair, and “all that is fitting / for a dead man to have when he goes beneath the misty darkness” (23.44–51; see [[oath]], [[bathing]] and [[hair]]). The first half of the book performs them in order: the lament around the body (23.9–17), the funeral feast (τάφος, in Homer always the funeral and never the tomb, as Walter Leaf notes: *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.29), the wood from Ida (23.110–126), the procession, with the companions' hair and then Achilles' own laid on the corpse (23.127–153), the pyre and its offerings (23.163–183), the quenching, the gathering of the bones into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, and the mound (23.236–257; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]], [[lament]], [[cremation]], [[pyres]] and [[tomb]]).

**The dead man's claim.** The dead has his own voice in the book. The soul of Patroclus reproaches Achilles: “You were not careless of me while I lived, but you are now that I am dead” (23.70). Until he is buried the souls will not let him pass, and once he has had his portion of fire he will not come back (23.71–76; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[psyche]]). Elpenor's ghost asks the same of Odysseus in the Odyssey, a burning and a mound by the sea (*Odyssey* 11.71–78). Achilles promises to do all of it (23.95–96), and at the pyre he greets the dead man again with the verses of the morning: he is fulfilling all that he promised (23.19–20 = 23.179–180).

**Hector.** The same rites are refused the other dead man. Achilles' promise was “to drag Hector here and give him raw to the dogs” (23.21), and over the burning pyre he says, “but Hector, / the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs” (23.182–183). The gods give the body what he refuses it. Aphrodite keeps the dogs off by day and night and anoints it with rose-sweet oil, and Apollo covers the place where it lies with a dark cloud, lest the sun parch it (23.184–191; see [[aphrodite]], [[apollo]] and [[dogs]]). The lines look ahead to the last book, where the body is ransomed and burned (see the translation's note on 23.184–191).

**Games for the dead.** Nestor, thanking Achilles for his prize, bids him “likewise honor your companion with funeral games” (23.646). The verb κτερεΐζω, like κτερίζω, is used of funeral rites (κτεριοῦσι, 11.455, 22.336; κτεριῶ, 18.334), and Leaf explains καί, “likewise”: as men of old honored their dead with games, so let Achilles honor his friend (on 23.646). The prize of the footrace is set out “in honor of his companion” (23.748), the bowl given to Nestor is to be “a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus” (23.619), and the dung in which the son of Oileus slips comes from the oxen “which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus” (23.776; see [[contests]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
