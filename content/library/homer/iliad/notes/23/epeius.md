summary: Epeius, son of Panopeus, lays hold of the mule set for the boxing, boasts that he will crush whoever faces him, though he owns that he falls short in battle, and knocks out Euryalus with one blow to the cheek, then lifts him to his feet (23.664–699). He throws first in the contest of the lump of iron, and all the Achaeans laugh (23.836–840).
# Unnamed: the boxer who seizes the mule and boasts (23.666-675), who fights (23.685-688) and strikes (23.690).

**The challenge.** For the boxing Achilles sets out a hard-working mule, six years old and unbroken, for the winner, and a two-handled cup for the loser, and calls for two men to “put up their fists and strike hard”, the winner to be the one whom Apollo grants to outlast the other (23.653–663; see [[mules]], [[cup]] and [[apollo]]). At once “a man rose, tall and brave, / skilled in boxing, the son of Panopeus, Epeius; / and he laid hold of the hard-working mule and spoke” (23.664–666; see [[panopeus-father-of-epeius]]). He takes the prize in hand before anyone has fought for it; Leaf compares the Roman gesture of laying a hand on what one claims (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.666). Then he speaks:

He turns Achilles' last words back on the field, “whoever will carry off the two-handled cup”, the loser's prize (23.663, 23.667), and Leaf thinks the repetition “evidently meant to be ironical” (on 23.667). κηδεμόνες, “those who care for him” (23.674), is the word for “those who cared for the dead” and stayed to build Patroclus' pyre (23.163; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]); Leaf finds the sarcasm evident, “let him have his family mourners ready” (on 23.674). “Is it not enough that I fall short in battle?” (23.670) is the only thing the poem says of his part in the war. Leaf thinks the couplet 23.670–671 may have been added to excuse the fact that Epeius has not been named before, and asks why a man should excuse bad fighting by being a good boxer (on 23.670; see the translation's note on 23.670–671). The maxim that closes it, that no man can be skilled in every kind of work, is said by the man whom the Odyssey makes the builder of the wooden horse (*Odyssey* 8.493).

**Euryalus.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence” (23.676), the verse with which the Achaeans met Hector's challenge (7.92). Euryalus alone stands up, and Diomedes encourages him, puts the loincloth about him and gives him the oxhide straps (23.677–684; see [[euryalus]] and [[diomedes]]). The fight is short. There was “a terrible grinding of jaws” and sweat flowed from all their limbs, “and brilliant Epeius rushed on, / and struck him on the cheek as he peered about; and he did not / stand long” (23.688–691). Euryalus leaps up under the blow like a fish under the North Wind's ripple and falls (23.692–694; see [[the-leaping-fish]]); “but great-hearted Epeius / took him in his hands and set him upright” (23.694–695). His companions lead him away, spitting blood, his head lolling, his wits wandering, and come back for the cup (23.695–699). The boaster who promised to tear flesh and crush bones (23.673) is the first to pick up the beaten man.

**The lump of iron.** For the throw with the lump of iron that Eetion used to hurl, four men rise, Polypoetes, Leonteus, Telamonian Ajax and “brilliant Epeius” (23.836–838; see [[eetion]] and [[iron]]). “And they stood in order, and brilliant Epeius took the lump, / and whirled it and threw; and at this all the Achaeans laughed” (23.839–840). The poem does not say why they laughed. Leaf, taking Epeius for the representative of brute strength, thinks admiration more probable than derision (on 23.840; see the translation's note on 23.840); Leonteus throws next, Ajax outthrows them both, and Polypoetes wins (23.841–849; see [[leonteus]], [[ajax]] and [[polypoetes]]).
