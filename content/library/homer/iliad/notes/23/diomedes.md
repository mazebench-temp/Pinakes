summary: Diomedes races the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas. Apollo strikes the whip from his hand, but Athena gives it back, puts might into his horses and breaks the yoke of Eumelus, and he wins the chariot race; Sthenelus takes the first prizes. In the boxing he seconds his kinsman Euryalus, and in the armed duel he fights Telamonian Ajax until the Achaeans stop them, and Achilles gives him the sword of Asteropaeus (23.290–292, 23.357, 23.377–400, 23.499–513, 23.681–684, 23.811–825).

**The horses of Tros.** He rises second for the chariot race, “and he led under the yoke the horses of Tros, of which he had once robbed / Aeneas, though Apollo saved the man himself” (23.290–292; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[aeneas]]). He took them in Book 5, when Sthenelus drove them off as he had been told (5.319–327; see [[sthenelus]]), and he drove them against Hector with Nestor beside him in Book 8 (8.105–108). Walter Leaf notes that the wound Paris gave him in Book 11 is forgotten from here on (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.291; 11.369–400); in Book 19 he still came limping to the assembly with Odysseus (19.47–49). He draws the last place, “by far the best” (ὄχʼ ἄριστος, 23.357).

**Apollo and Athena.** On the way back his stallions come so close behind Eumelus' mares that they seem about to mount the chariot, and he would have passed Eumelus or left the race in doubt, “had not Phoebus Apollo borne a grudge against the son of Tydeus, / and struck the shining whip from his hands” (23.377–384; see [[apollo]] and [[eumelus]]). Tears of anger pour from his eyes as he sees the mares draw away and his own horses slow, running without the goad (23.385–387). Athena sees the god's trick, gives him back the whip, puts might into his horses, breaks Eumelus' yoke, “and set glory on him” (23.388–400; see [[athena]], [[divine-intervention]] and [[kydos]]). Antilochus, behind him, says the same to his own horses: Athena has given speed to the horses of the son of Tydeus (23.404–406). In Book 5 Apollo kept Diomedes from killing Aeneas, and Athena drove his chariot against Ares (5.432–444, 5.835–863); here the two gods take sides over the same horses in a race.

**Seen from afar.** Idomeneus hears him shouting to his horses and guesses who is leading: “an Aetolian by lineage, and he is lord among the Argives, / the son of Tydeus, tamer of horses, mighty Diomedes” (23.452–472; see [[idomeneus]]). His father Tydeus was the son of Oeneus of Calydon, and settled in Argos (14.113–121; see [[tydeus]] and [[aetolians]]). Leaf reports that 23.471 was athetized in antiquity, since the description belongs to the poet, not to a character (on 23.471).

**First.** He comes in driving his horses with the whip from the shoulder, while they lift themselves high and the drops of dust strike him, and the chariot, overlaid with gold and tin, leaves hardly a track behind it (23.499–506; see [[chariot]] and [[horses]]). He stops in the middle of the gathering, leaps down and leans the whip against the yoke, and Sthenelus quickly takes the prizes and gives the woman and the tripod to their companions, and the horses are unyoked (23.507–513; see [[sthenelus]] and [[tripod]]). Achilles, when he proposes to give Eumelus the second prize, says, “and let the son of Tydeus carry off the first” (23.538).

**The boxing.** When Euryalus, son of Mecisteus, stands up against Epeius, Diomedes “busied himself about him, / encouraging him with words, and he greatly wished him victory”, and puts the loincloth about him and gives him the well-cut straps of oxhide; Epeius knocks him down (23.677–699; see [[euryalus]], [[epeius]], [[kilt]] and [[well-cut]]). In the Catalogue Euryalus is the third leader of the men of Argos, after Diomedes and Sthenelus (2.563–566). Leaf explains Diomedes' care for him by kinship: Adrastus and Mecisteus were brothers, sons of Talaus, and Diomedes is the grandson of Adrastus and has married his daughter Aegialeia (on 23.677; 14.121, 5.412–415; see [[mecisteus]] and [[adrastus]]). The Iliad itself calls Mecisteus a son of Talaus (2.566, 23.678), but does not name Adrastus' father.

**The armed duel.** He rises with Telamonian Ajax to fight in armor. The arms of Sarpedon are for both of them, and a silver-studded sword for the one who first reaches the other's flesh through his armor (23.798–812; see [[ajax]] and [[sarpedon]]). Three times they rush together; Ajax's spear is stopped by Diomedes' corselet, and Diomedes keeps reaching for Ajax's neck over the great shield, until the Achaeans, afraid for Ajax, bid them stop and take equal prizes. Achilles gives Diomedes the great sword, which he took from Asteropaeus, with its sheath and strap (23.807–808, 23.813–825; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[sword]]). Leaf takes κῦρε, “kept reaching”, to mean that the spear point lighted on Ajax's neck without wounding him, so that Diomedes met the terms of the contest; Aristarchus and Aristophanes, taking it as “kept aiming”, athetized the award (on 23.821).
