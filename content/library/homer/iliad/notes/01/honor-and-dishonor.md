summary: Book 1 is a chain of injuries to honor, each repaid in suffering. [[Agamemnon]] dishonors [[Chryses]] and Apollo strikes the army; Agamemnon takes [[Achilles]]' prize and Achilles asks Zeus to make the Achaeans pay.
---

Book 1 is built as a chain of three injuries to honor. Each is answered by suffering spread over a whole community, and each is settled, or not, by a payment.

## Three dishonors

{{quote:7383d2b4-8a47-5a2d-829d-b2f524c89584}}

The narrator's first explanation is that Agamemnon dishonored (ἠτίμασεν) the priest Chryses. He refused the ransom, and with it the [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“respect”) that the whole army wished to show. Apollo repays the insult to his priest on the army. When the girl is returned and the hecatomb offered, Chryses describes the plague as an honor done to him.

{{quote:82b55497-3e79-5a69-8a32-056d9bf6f9c0}}

The second injury is Agamemnon's own. To give back Chryseis is to lose a [[geras|γέρας]] (“prize”), and he will not be the only Argive without one (1.118–120). His remedy is to take another man's prize. Honor, on this view, is a fixed quantity, and one man's gain is another's loss.

{{quote:4fee66c9-5b1c-5c9e-b45f-705cf34865e9}}

The third is Achilles'. He has done most of the fighting for the smaller prize (1.163–168), and now even that is taken. His complaint to his mother states the logic of the whole poem: because his life is short, Zeus owes him honor at least.

## Honor paid in suffering

Achilles does not ask for his prize back. He asks that the Achaeans be penned against the sea and killed, so that they may “have joy of their king” and Agamemnon may learn his [[ate|ἄτη]] (“blind folly”) in not honoring the best of the Achaeans (1.407–412). Thetis carries the request to Zeus with the words of honor five times in six lines (1.505–510), and she makes it her own case too: she wants to know how far she is “the most dishonored goddess of all” (1.516). Hera sums up what Zeus has granted.

{{quote:4d39b909-0618-5d24-968f-a07d86d2dfe2}}

To honor Achilles is to destroy many Achaeans. The same logic governed the plague. The god's honor was restored at the army's cost, and Achilles' will be.

## Honor as payment

Honor can also be counted. Achilles says the Achaeans came to Troy to win τιμή, “satisfaction”, for Menelaus from the Trojans (1.159; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). He offers Agamemnon repayment “three and four times over” when Troy falls (1.128). Athena promises Achilles three times as many gifts for the outrage (1.213–214; see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]). The poem will test whether any payment can make good an injury to honor. In Book 9 Achilles refuses the gifts.

The book ends with a restored distribution. At Chryse and on Olympus alike, “no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast” (1.468, 1.602). A book about unequal shares closes twice on an equal one.
