---
summary: The gods act in Book 1 at every turn. [[Apollo]] sends the plague, [[Hera]] puts the assembly into Achilles' mind, [[Athena]] stops his sword, and [[Zeus]] nods to [[Thetis]]. Yet each divine act meets a human choice.
---

The proem asks which of the gods set Achilles and Agamemnon to quarrel (1.8), and answers at once: Apollo. From then on the gods take part at every turn of Book 1. They do so in different ways, and never simply instead of the human actors.

## Apollo: the god in person

{{quote:2dcda65d-12f3-5df4-9e33-86e73b78faf1}}

Apollo answers his priest's prayer in person. He comes down from Olympus in anger, the arrows rattling on his shoulders, “like the night”, and shoots into the camp for nine days (1.48–53). The plague is at once a sickness and a god's archery. When his anger is appeased, he sends the ship home with a following wind (1.479). See [[wrath-of-apollo]].

## Hera: a thought put into the mind

{{quote:819624de-b17d-59ce-8f76-1d73ca0fee39}}

Hera acts unseen. She “put it in the mind” of Achilles to call the assembly, because she cared for the Danaans and saw them dying. Achilles gives no sign of knowing it; he calls the assembly as his own decision. Albin Lesky called this “double motivation”: the act is fully the man's and fully the god's at once.

## Athena: the epiphany

{{quote:793bfcc2-3317-5553-b4d9-cfd33ac1a4fe}}

Athena's intervention is the most famous in the poem. Sent by Hera, she comes while Achilles is drawing his sword, stands behind him, takes him by his fair hair, and is seen by him alone. He knows her at once, and terrible eyes blaze (whose, the Greek does not say; see [[whose-eyes-blaze]]).

The goddess does not simply stop his hand. Achilles was already weighing whether to “check his anger” (1.192), and she gives him reasons: cease from strife, use words, and receive three times the gifts later (1.210–214). His answer is a choice to obey: “whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen” (1.218). Readers since antiquity have asked whether Athena is a real goddess or a picture of Achilles' second thoughts. The text keeps both. She is visible to him alone, but she is also a goddess who goes back to Olympus (1.221–222). See [[athena-restrains-achilles]].

## Gifts of the gods

The gods are also the source of what men are. Calchas' prophecy is Apollo's gift (1.72). Agamemnon belittles Achilles' strength as the gift of a god (1.178; see [[strength-is-a-gift]]) and asks whether, if the gods made him a spearman, his insults must therefore rush forth to be spoken (1.290–291).

## Zeus: the nod

{{quote:477c1411-8b92-55a4-b1d7-c768ab2754fb}}

Zeus' intervention in Book 1 is a promise. His nod to Thetis shakes Olympus and sets the plan of the rest of the poem in motion (see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The next book opens with Zeus awake while gods and men sleep, pondering how to honor Achilles and destroy many Achaeans (2.1–4).
