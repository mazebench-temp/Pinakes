The poem asks the goddess to sing a μῆνις and at once counts its cost. The epithet οὐλομένη (“ruinous”, from ὄλλυμι, “destroy”) opens a list of the dead: countless [[algos|ἄλγεα]] (“sorrows”), souls hurled to Hades, bodies left for dogs and birds. Only then does the sentence name the plan of Zeus and the moment it all began, the [[eris|ἔρις]] (“strife”) between the son of Atreus and Achilles.

{{quote:d0cf62c3-5c7a-58e4-a72f-4a05bb27bad3}}

The first wrath in the story is not Achilles' but Apollo's. The narrator has said only that the god was angry (χολωθείς, 1.9). It is the seer [[Calchas]] who calls it μῆνις. The two wraths have the same cause. Agamemnon dishonors someone, and the whole army pays. Apollo's wrath is ended within the book, by the return of [[Chryseis]], a hecatomb, and the paean sung at [[Chryse]] (1.430–474). It shows how a μῆνις can be appeased. Achilles' will not be appeased so easily.

{{quote:203760cc-180a-59bd-8219-35b0738ed561}}

At the height of the quarrel the narrator sets the two men side by side. Achilles throws down the scepter and sits; on the other side (ἑτέρωθεν) Agamemnon ἐμήνιε. For one line the king's anger is described with the hero's word.

{{quote:53bf26a2-6e2b-557b-96d6-82ef9b3f3e49}}

[[Thetis]] tells her son to sit by the ships and keep up his wrath against the Achaeans while she goes to Zeus. The wrath now has a direction, against his own side, and a purpose: to show the Achaeans what they have lost.

{{quote:cef1c3dc-8f3f-56a0-9738-48a316b66da8}}

Some seventy lines later the narrator reports that he does as she said, in nearly her words (νηυσὶ παρήμενος ὠκυπόροισι, 1.421 and 1.488). The wrath takes the form of absence: no assembly, no war. The verbs with -σκ- (πωλέσκετο, φθινύθεσκε, ποθέεσκε) describe what he did day after day. It is a wrath that also consumes its owner. He wastes away his own heart and longs for the battle he refuses.
