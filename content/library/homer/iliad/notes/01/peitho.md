---
summary: The verb runs through every scene of Book 1, and the book is a study in whom people obey and why.
---

Every scene of the book turns on who obeys whom, and why.

{{quote:a9c48215-7151-5ef4-9d33-f0df529c0e81}}

The first obedience is fear. Chryses is afraid and obeys Agamemnon's word. The Achaeans obey Agamemnon as a matter of course (1.79). The god, Calchas says, will be appeased and “won over” only by the girl's return and a hecatomb (1.100).

Agamemnon refuses to be won over by Achilles (1.132). Achilles asks how any Achaean can obey such a king (1.150).

{{quote:bd4a0149-6797-5f52-9e28-4f7e893b0a17}}

The second obedience is to the gods. Athena comes to stop Achilles' fury “if you will obey”, and bids him obey. He does, with a principle: whoever obeys the gods, the gods listen to him (see [[whoever-obeys-the-gods]]). He did not disobey the word of Athena (1.220).

{{quote:dac371f9-8e1a-5207-af03-c8fd20cf0db5}}

The third is persuasion by age and memory. Nestor opens with “obey me” (πίθεσθʼ, 1.259) and closes with the same appeal: the great men of old obeyed my word, “so you too obey, since it is better to obey” (see [[better-to-obey]]). Neither man does.

The quarrel ends with refusal on both sides. Agamemnon says there is one who will not obey Achilles (1.289); Achilles says he will obey Agamemnon no longer (1.296). Only Patroclus obeys, because Achilles is his dear companion (1.345).

On Olympus Thetis expects to persuade Zeus (1.420, 1.427), and Zeus nods “so that you may be certain” (πεποίθῃς, 1.524). Hera is told to sit in silence and obey (1.565), and like Chryses at the start of the book, she is afraid and does so (1.568–569).
