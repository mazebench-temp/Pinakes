---
summary: He returns from the [[Ethiopians]], grants [[Thetis]] her plea with the most famous nod in literature, and silences [[Hera]].
---

Zeus is behind the poem from its fifth line. The first thing said about him is that his will was being fulfilled in all that suffering:

{{quote:1b4c453c-646c-5e94-a341-f61e540648db}}

What that plan was is left open; see [[plan-of-zeus]].

He is the source of kingship and of justice. Nestor tells Achilles that Zeus gives glory to the scepter-bearing king, and the judges who carry the scepter keep "the ordinances that come from Zeus":

{{quote:5c15789f-ab27-518c-a4d8-f127771989af}}

{{quote:6800bbc5-7be5-5618-9ea0-a284361f8aad}}

Dreams come from him (1.63), heralds are his messengers (1.334), and Agamemnon counts on his honor above all (1.175).

For eleven days of the book he is away. Thetis tells her son why she cannot go to Olympus at once:

{{quote:9d8e7067-6d84-59d8-b0ca-65fb72d34720}}

When he returns she finds him sitting apart on the peak, clasps his knees, and asks honor for Achilles. He says nothing for a long time. Then he agrees, knowing it will cost him a quarrel with Hera, and seals it with a nod:

{{quote:7589640c-96ff-51c5-a34b-314daf332e28}}

This nod is the hinge of the Iliad. Everything the Achaeans suffer until the death of Patroclus follows from it. It was also the model for [[phidias-zeus|Phidias' statue of Zeus at Olympia]].

On Olympus Zeus rules by force as well as by right. Hera's suspicion is met first with a lordly refusal to share his counsels, then with a threat:

{{quote:12ae56a3-8ec3-5809-a85b-5f2e39e57c55}}

His son Hephaestus warns the gods that he is "by far the strongest" (1.581) and remembers being thrown out of heaven by him (1.590–594). The book ends with Zeus going to bed, with Hera beside him.
