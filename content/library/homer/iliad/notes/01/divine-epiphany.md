---
summary: Athena comes down from the sky to stop Achilles' sword and is seen by him alone (1.194–222). Thetis rising from the sea to comfort him is a gentler version of the scene.
---

{{quote:107d25e0-78cc-5551-87d0-029244305196}}

Athena comes “from the sky” (οὐρανόθεν), sent by Hera, at the moment Achilles is drawing his sword. She comes in her own form. She stands behind him (στῆ δʼ ὄπιθεν) and seizes him by his fair hair ([[fair-haired]]), “appearing to him alone; none of the others saw her” (οἴῳ φαινομένη). The assembly is all around, and the scene is private.

Achilles is astonished (θάμβησεν), turns, and knows her at once. “Her eyes blazed terribly”, though the Greek does not say whose eyes ([[whose-eyes-blaze]]). He speaks first, with [[winged-words|winged words]]. Her reply repeats the narrator: “I came … down from the sky; the white-armed goddess Hera sent me” ({{ref:Iliad 1.207-209}}) takes up “Athena came down from the sky; for the white-armed goddess Hera had sent her” ({{ref:Iliad 1.194-196}}). Then come the order and the promise.

{{quote:cedfa88f-f711-5c61-bcc8-4a6861fd5e26}}

Achilles obeys. The departure is told in the pluperfect: she “had gone” (βεβήκει) to Olympus ({{ref:Iliad 1.221-222}}). By the time the sword is back in its sheath, the goddess is gone.

Achilles' other divine visitor in Book 1 comes the opposite way. Thetis rises from the sea, not down from the sky, “like a mist” ([[like-a-mist]]). She sits before him instead of standing behind, strokes him instead of seizing him, and asks why he weeps ({{ref:Iliad 1.357-363}}). See [[athena-restrains-achilles]] and [[divine-intervention]].
