---
summary: The hecatomb Calchas prescribes, carried to Chryse in a single ship with the returned girl, is the price of Apollo's favor.
---

The word occurs in ten lines, and it follows the story. Achilles asks whether Apollo is angry over a vow or a hecatomb left unpaid ({{ref:Iliad 1.65}}). Calchas answers that he is not ({{ref:Iliad 1.93}}): the god will end the plague only when the girl is returned “unbought, unransomed”, and a holy hecatomb is led to Chryse ({{ref:Iliad 1.99-100}}).

{{quote:5140278a-5a5a-5d0c-babd-12f45ffa893c}}

Agamemnon orders a hecatomb put aboard a ship ({{ref:Iliad 1.142}}), and it sails with Chryseis ({{ref:Iliad 1.309}}). Meanwhile the army offers τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας, “perfect hecatombs”, of bulls and goats on the shore of the camp ({{ref:Iliad 1.315-316}}). τελήεις, “complete, perfect”, probably means victims without blemish. At Chryse the holy hecatomb is brought ashore ({{ref:Iliad 1.431}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.438}}), offered by Odysseus on behalf of the Danaans ({{ref:Iliad 1.443}}), and set around the altar for the [[sacrifice|sacrifice]] ({{ref:Iliad 1.447}}).

Four times it is a “holy hecatomb” (ἱερὴν ἑκατόμβην), a set phrase that follows it from Calchas' prescription to the altar ({{ref:Iliad 1.99}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.431}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.443}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.447}}). It always travels with the girl: Apollo's price is Chryseis returned without ransom and the hecatomb together, a gift to the god in place of the gifts the father offered.

The size is modest. The hecatomb for Chryse travels in one ship with twenty oarsmen ({{ref:Iliad 1.309}}), and the victims are “bulls and goats” ({{ref:Iliad 1.316}}) or “lambs and goats” ({{ref:Iliad 1.66}}). What the name promises is scale, not number.
