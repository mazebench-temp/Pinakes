---
summary: Whom do people obey, and why? In Book 1 they obey out of fear, out of reverence for the gods, or out of friendship. Persuasion by argument, [[Nestor]]'s art, fails, and on Olympus obedience is secured by a threat.
---

Book 1 asks, scene by scene, what it is that persuades.

## Fear

{{quote:bd17205d-2d0f-59d2-a5aa-848404f76ee2}}

The first obedience in the poem is fear. Chryses, threatened, obeys Agamemnon's word and goes off in silence. The book's last quarrel ends the same way: Hera, threatened by Zeus, is afraid and sits in silence (1.568–569). The heralds sent for Briseis go “unwilling” (1.327) and stand before Achilles “in fear and awe” (1.331).

## The gods

{{quote:601dcd40-a9cd-5f86-8c96-6877243ba7bf}}

Athena comes to stop Achilles' fury “if you will obey”. She gives reasons and a promise of gifts, and ends with a command: “hold back, and obey us”. Achilles obeys, and states the principle: “whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen” (see [[whoever-obeys-the-gods]]). He did not disobey the word of Athena (1.220). Obedience to the gods is itself a claim on them (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).

The gods can be persuaded too. Calchas says that with the girl and a hecatomb “we might appease him and win him over” (1.100). Thetis says of Zeus, “I think I will persuade him” (1.427), and he nods “so that you may be certain” (πεποίθῃς, 1.524).

## Argument

{{quote:42ed4021-f0c8-502c-aa8a-39d973f398fc}}

Nestor is the book's master of persuasion. His voice flows sweeter than honey (1.249), and he argues from age and memory: the great men of his youth heeded his counsels and obeyed his word, “so you too obey, since it is better to obey” (see [[better-to-obey]]). He fails with both men. Agamemnon grants that everything he said was “fair and right” and refuses in the same breath (1.286–289).

## Refusal

{{quote:e94b45a2-33e0-59cb-9e7e-9f7357d5b8d8}}

The quarrel ends in a formal refusal. Agamemnon complains that Achilles wants to give orders to all, and that there is one who will not obey him (1.289). Achilles answers that Agamemnon may give orders to others, but “I, I think, will obey you no longer”. Earlier he asked how any Achaean could obey such a king (1.150), and Agamemnon told him, “You will not outwit me or win me over” (1.132).

The one willing obedience among men is friendship. Patroclus obeys “his dear companion” without a word (1.345).

## On Olympus

Zeus does not argue. He orders Hera to sit in silence and obey his word, and threatens force (1.565–567). It is Hephaestus who persuades, and he persuades toward submission: approach Zeus “with gentle words”, and he will be gracious (1.582–583). The quarrel of the gods ends in laughter because one of them is strong enough that obedience is not in question.
