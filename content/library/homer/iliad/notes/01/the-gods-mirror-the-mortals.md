summary: The quarrel on Olympus repeats the quarrel in the camp: a ruler, a challenger with a grievance, a peacemaker who remembers the past, and the same threats in the same words. On Olympus it ends in laughter.
---

Book 1 contains two quarrels, one among men and one among gods, and the second is built to recall the first. The same roles, the same arguments and some of the same lines appear in both. The difference is in how they end.

## Threat and silence

{{quote:2211709d-a223-53d6-b017-fbf681c81618}}

{{quote:4e9c3da3-31da-5597-ad57-3a38678c7333}}

Agamemnon warns Chryses that the god's scepter and fillet may not protect him (μή νύ τοι οὐ χραίσμῃ). Zeus warns Hera that all the gods on Olympus may not protect her (μή νύ τοι οὐ χραίσμωσιν). The response is the same too.

{{quote:b1a73627-5012-51be-8c4b-69b358f28aa3}}

{{quote:36ae7fbd-3732-5f98-9c42-e4ab1e143327}}

“The old man was afraid (ἔδεισεν) and obeyed,” and went off in silence (ἀκέων). “Ox-eyed queen Hera was afraid (ἔδεισεν),” and sat in silence (ἀκέουσα). In the book's first quarrel and in its last, a stronger power silences a weaker one with the same threat.

## Ruler, challenger, peacemaker

Each quarrel has a ruler who insists on his superiority. Agamemnon takes Briseis so that Achilles may know “how much greater (φέρτερος) I am” (1.186); Zeus, Hephaestus says, is “by far the strongest” (φέρτατος, 1.581). Each has a challenger with a grievance, who attacks with words: Achilles with insults, Hera with “cutting words” (1.539).

Each has a peacemaker. Nestor, “sweet of speech”, recalls the heroes of his youth and advises the stronger man to yield to the one who rules over more (1.254–284). Hephaestus recalls his own fall from heaven and advises his mother to yield to the strongest (1.573–594). Both use the past to persuade. The same phrase comes from both sides: “for all your grief, you will be powerless to help” (1.241–242), “for all my grief, I will be powerless to help you” (1.588–589).

Even the settings echo each other. Achilles sits apart from his companions by the sea (1.348–350); Thetis finds Zeus sitting apart from the other gods on the highest peak (1.498–499).

## Two endings

Nestor fails. The assembly breaks up (1.305), Briseis is taken, and Achilles withdraws. Hephaestus succeeds. Hera smiles, the gods laugh, and the feast goes on till sunset (1.595–604). The gods can let a quarrel go because nothing is at stake for them: it is a quarrel “for the sake of mortals”, and “worse things prevail” (1.574–576). Among men the same quarrel costs lives.
