---
summary: Seers, oracles and predictions in Book 1. [[Calchas]] names the cause of the plague at his own risk, gods and heroes foretell what the poem will bring, and the verb τελέω, “fulfill”, runs through the book.
---

When the plague has lasted nine days, Achilles proposes the obvious remedy: ask someone who knows the will of the gods. Book 1 then shows what it costs to know, and to say.

## Kinds of seer

{{quote:787ba520-59b5-56bd-8f29-5e009f6b03ae}}

Achilles names three kinds of expert: a μάντις (“seer”), a ἱερεύς (“priest”), and an ὀνειροπόλος (“interpreter of dreams”), “for a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (see [[onar|ὄναρ]] and [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]).

{{quote:ec9ea2ff-7282-54c4-a357-ba60f717a50f}}

The seer who answers is Calchas, “by far the best of the bird-seers” (οἰωνοπόλων). He knows past, present and future, in the words Hesiod uses of the Muses (see [[calchas-and-the-muses]]). He guided the fleet to Ilios by the art of prophecy that Apollo gave him (see [[calchas-guides-the-fleet]]). His knowledge is Apollo's, and so is the plague he must explain.

## The danger of prophecy

Calchas knows what his knowledge will cost. He asks Achilles to swear to protect him before he speaks (1.76–83), and Achilles swears by Apollo that no one will touch him, “not even if you mean Agamemnon” (1.90). Only then does he speak the [[theopropion|θεοπρόπιον]], the oracle.

{{quote:db40a413-5ba0-505b-a1c4-b0a1c8722e7f}}

Agamemnon's answer is an attack on the seer. He has never prophesied anything good for him, and it is always dear to his heart to prophesy evil. Ancient readers heard in this an allusion to Aulis (see [[calchas-evil-prophecies]]).

## Fulfillment

Prophecy in Book 1 is not only the seer's business. Achilles predicts Agamemnon's end (1.204–205), Athena the gifts to come (1.212–214), Achilles the Achaeans' longing for him (1.240–244), Thetis her son's short life (1.415–418), and Zeus confirms his promise with a nod.

{{quote:9ed696b2-1b14-5933-b27f-75f3c53719bd}}

The verb τελέω, “fulfill, bring to pass”, runs through these scenes. In the proem the plan of Zeus “was being fulfilled” (ἐτελείετο, 1.5). Calchas' king keeps his grudge until he “fulfills” it (1.82). Agamemnon complains that the seer never spoke a good word “or brought to pass” (1.108). Achilles thinks his prediction “will be fulfilled” (1.204), and Athena says hers “will surely come to pass” (1.212). Agamemnon's threat “has now been carried out” (1.388). Zeus promises to “to bring them to fulfillment” (1.523), and no word he nods to can go “unfulfilled” (ἀτελεύτητον, 1.527).
