---
summary: [[Chryses]] brings a boundless ransom for his daughter and [[Agamemnon]] refuses it; the god then demands her back “unbought, unransomed”.
---
{{quote:1fd8ebb8-0483-5133-a62e-328319840bc4}}

The Iliad begins with a father coming to the enemy's ships with “boundless ransom” (ἀπερείσια ἄποινα) and the insignia of his god. The army shouts its approval: respect the priest and accept the splendid ransom (1.22–23). Agamemnon alone refuses.

{{quote:19736985-c0ec-5df5-b925-c7019dd8829b}}

His reason, given later, is personal. He would rather keep the girl, whom he prefers to his wife.

{{quote:4b9e28fc-e241-5977-a501-24abedce56af}}

The refusal changes the terms. Calchas says Apollo will not relent until the girl is given back ἀπριάτην ἀνάποινον, “unbought, unransomed”, and a hecatomb is taken to Chryse. ἀπριάτην is formed from πρίαμαι (“buy”) with the negative prefix; ἀνάποινον is ἄποινα negated. The ransom that Agamemnon could have received becomes a gift he must give, to the god.

Achilles tells the whole story again to his mother, in the same words (1.370–379).

The pattern recurs. Agamemnon refuses ransom again when the Trojan Adrastus begs Menelaus for his life (6.46–60), and when two sons of Antimachus beg him in Book 11 (11.131). The poem ends where it began, with an old father in the enemy's camp bringing ransom for his child. In Book 24 Priam comes to Achilles for Hector's body, and this time the ransom is accepted (24.137, 24.139).
