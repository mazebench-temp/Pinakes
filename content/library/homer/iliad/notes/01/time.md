---
summary: The verbs τίω and τιμάω (“honor”) and ἀτιμάζω, ἀτιμάω (“dishonor”) trace the chain of injuries that drives Book 1, from [[Chryses]] to [[Achilles]] to [[Thetis]], “the most dishonored goddess of all”.
---
The book is a chain of dishonors, each answered by suffering.

{{quote:7383d2b4-8a47-5a2d-829d-b2f524c89584}}

The narrator's explanation of the plague: Agamemnon had dishonored (ἠτίμασεν) the priest Chryses. Calchas repeats the charge (1.94). To dishonor a god's priest is to dishonor the god, and Apollo repays it on the whole army. Chryses later describes the plague the other way round, as a gift to himself: “you honored me (τίμησας) and struck the army of the Achaeans hard” (1.454).

{{quote:0096b580-5be9-5593-b60c-1174cf069218}}

In the quarrel both men speak the language of honor. Achilles will not stay at Troy ἄτιμος, piling up wealth for Agamemnon (1.171). Agamemnon answers that others will honor him, and Zeus above all (1.175). Achilles swears that the king will one day regret that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans (1.244), and asks Thetis for exactly that recognition (1.412).

{{quote:353696e0-9042-5bf6-8809-a5f29743cf0c}}

Alone by the sea, Achilles states the logic of the whole poem. Because his life will be short, Zeus owes him honor at least. Instead Zeus has not honored him “even a little”, for Agamemnon has dishonored him and taken his prize.

{{quote:449cde3d-ba6c-5ae9-a133-f6d9e813d45f}}

Thetis turns the complaint into a prayer, and the words of honor come five times in six lines: honor my son; he has been dishonored; honor him; until the Achaeans honor him and increase him with honor. When Zeus says nothing, she asks him to promise or refuse, so that she may know how far she is “the most dishonored goddess of all” (ἀτιμοτάτη, 1.516). Hera guesses the answer. Zeus has promised to honor Achilles and to destroy many Achaeans beside the ships (1.558–559). Honor for one man will be paid for with the lives of others.
