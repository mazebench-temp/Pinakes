---
summary: The language of swearing in Book 1: Achilles' oath by Apollo to protect Calchas (1.86) and his great oath by the scepter (1.233–244).
---

Calchas asks Achilles to swear to protect him (καί μοι ὄμοσσον, {{ref:Iliad 1.76}}), and Achilles swears a negative oath by Apollo:

{{quote:9ce775c7-5b9b-50b3-b0c5-fc3db8c4f390}}

No one will lay a hand on the seer, “not even if you mean Agamemnon” ([[dear-to-zeus]], [[while-i-live]]).

The great oath by the scepter is the most elaborate speech act in the book:

{{quote:02bbefd5-c74b-5a6f-916e-1fd754d9e5e5}}

Achilles announces the oath (ἐπὶ μέγαν ὅρκον ὀμοῦμαι), names its object with ναὶ μά, and describes it at length: a staff cut from a tree, stripped by the bronze, never to put out leaves again, carried by the judges who guard the laws of Zeus. Then he declares it a great oath (239) and states its content: the Achaeans will one day long for him ({{ref:Iliad 1.240-244}}; [[man-slaying]]). Last he flings the scepter to the ground ([[studded-with-golden-nails]]). The dead wood that will never grow again guarantees a decision that will not be undone.

Other acts in the book have the force of oaths. Achilles makes the heralds witnesses (μάρτυροι) before gods and men ({{ref:Iliad 1.338-340}}). And Zeus has an oath of his own: the nod of his head, which nothing can revoke ({{ref:Iliad 1.524-527}}; [[dark-brows]]).
