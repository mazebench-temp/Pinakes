# Μενεσθῆος μεγαθύμου (12.373 = 15.331). Μενέσθην (5.609) is Menesthes, another man.

**At his tower.** Sarpedon and Glaucus have just set out at the head of the Lycians (12.330; see [[sarpedon]] and [[lycians]]).

{{quote:2f959ed8-c344-5ba9-8a75-e7c31716b8ad}}

The tower is his own, “his tower” and “the tower of great-hearted Menestheus” (12.332, 12.373; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[battlements]]); ἀνὰ πύργον Ἀχαιῶν (12.333) is the towered wall as a whole, “the rampart of the Achaeans”, along which he looks. The Catalogue praised him above all men at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men (2.553–554). Here he does what a commander can: he sees the danger, looks for a leader “who might ward off disaster from his companions” (12.334), and sends for one. He cannot shout to the Ajaxes, so great is the din of shields and helmets and of the gates, all shut now, which the enemy are trying to break (12.337–341; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).

**The message.** “Go, brilliant Thootes, run and call Ajax, / both of them, rather” (12.343–344; see [[thootes]]). The two Ajaxes would be best, “since sheer destruction will soon be wrought here”; the leaders of the Lycians “have been violent in the fierce battles” (12.345–347; see [[violent]]). If the Ajaxes are hard pressed too, “then at least let valiant Telamonian Ajax come alone, / and let Teucer follow with him, well skilled with the bow” (12.349–350; see [[ajax]] and [[teucer]]). These eight verses are the only words the poem gives him: in Book 4 he stood silent under Agamemnon's rebuke (4.327–364). Thootes repeats them to the Ajaxes, naming the sender as “the dear son of Peteos, whom Zeus has nurtured” (12.355; see [[peteos]], [[zeus-nurtured]] and [[message-repeated]]).

**Help arrives.** Ajax and Teucer come “to the tower of great-hearted Menestheus” as the Lycian leaders climb onto the battlements (12.373–377; see [[great-hearted]]). The poem does not show Menestheus in the fight that follows; Ajax kills Epicles, Teucer wounds Glaucus, and the two drive Sarpedon back (12.378–407). He leads the Athenians again in Book 13 (13.195–196, 13.689–691; see [[athenians]]).
