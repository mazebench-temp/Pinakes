**The fifth column.** When the Trojans form five columns on foot at the ditch, each with three leaders, the allies come last (12.86–104; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]] and [[trojan-allies]]):

{{quote:76087caf-0c52-57c8-9e75-397bce1ecd75}}

ἔπρεπε καὶ διὰ πάντων, “he stood out even among them all” (12.104): the narrator sets him above the two men he judged the best (see [[aristos]], [[glaucus]] and [[asteropaeus]]). In the Catalogue he and Glaucus led only the Lycians (2.876).

**Roused by his father.** The narrator says that the Trojans and Hector would not yet have broken the gates “had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon, against the Argives, like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[counselor]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]). Hector will break the gate (12.445–462), but not without Sarpedon. Holding before him his shield of beaten bronze, its oxhides stitched “with golden wires” (12.294–297; see [[shield]]), he advances “like a mountain-bred lion” that has long gone without meat and makes for the sheep in a guarded fold, and so his spirit sends “godlike Sarpedon” against the wall (12.299–308; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]], [[mountain-bred]] and [[antitheos]]). The simile ends with two outcomes: “either he springs on one and seizes it, or he himself / is hit among the foremost by a javelin from a swift hand” (12.305–306). The speech that follows puts the same choice to men.

**The speech.** “And at once he spoke to Glaucus, son of Hippolochus” (12.309):

{{quote:87df0494-0d3d-531e-b8fc-3efce64447b9}}

The honors come first: a seat of honor, meat and full cups, the regard of all “as gods” (12.312), and a domain (τέμενος) by the Xanthus (12.311–314; see [[feast]], [[geras]], [[time]] and [[xanthus-of-lycia]]). The verse of the feast is the one in which Hector taunted Diomedes with the honors he would lose (12.311 = 8.162), and the domain is described in the words used of the one the Lycians gave Bellerophon, Sarpedon's grandfather (12.314 ≈ 6.195; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). The honors are a debt. So that a Lycian may say that their kings rule “not without glory” (12.318; see [[kleos]]), the two must take their stand “among the foremost Lycians” (12.315, 12.321; see [[lycians]]).

Then the argument turns from what is owed to what is certain. Sarpedon would neither fight nor send Glaucus “into the battle where men win glory” (12.325; see [[where-men-win-glory]]) if escaping the war meant being “ageless and immortal forever” (12.323), the condition of the gods for which Hector wished (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). But the fates of death stand over them, “countless, which no mortal can flee or escape” (12.327; see [[ker-doom]]), and what is left to choose is who wins the triumph (εὖχος, 12.328; see [[euchomai]]). The narrator has just named the men who fought at Troy “the race of men half-divine” (12.23; see [[hemitheos]]). The son of Zeus counts himself among the mortals (βροτόν, 12.327). See [[sarpedons-speech]], [[honor-and-dishonor]], [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[death-in-battle]].

**At Menestheus' tower.** “Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey” (12.329), and the two lead “the great host of the Lycians” against the tower of Menestheus, who sends his herald for the Ajaxes: “the leaders of the Lycians have pressed on so hard, who even before now / have been violent in the fierce battles” (12.346–347 = 12.359–360; see [[menestheus]], [[thootes]] and [[violent]]). Ajax's first kill is “Sarpedon's companion, great-hearted Epicles” (12.379; see [[epicles]]), and Teucer's arrow sends Glaucus back from the wall (12.387–391). Sarpedon's grief (ἄχος, 12.392; see [[achos]]) does not make him “forget the joy of battle” (12.393; see [[charme]]): he spears Alcmaon, son of Thestor (12.394–396; see [[alcmaon]]), and turns to the battlement (see [[battlements]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]):

{{quote:e6c4024d-1194-5e16-b65d-b4b383f63d4d}}

**Zeus' reprieve.** In Book 5 “his father still kept ruin away” (5.662). Here Zeus wards off the fates from him, and the narrator says where he is not to die: “at the sterns of the ships” (12.403). Zeus will foretell his death at Patroclus' hands (15.66–67), and when the hour comes he thinks of snatching him out of the battle and lets him die (16.431–461). Glaucus then complains that Zeus “does not defend his own son” (ὃ δʼ οὐ οὗ παιδὸς ἀμύνει, 16.522), in the words with which the narrator says here that he did (Ζεὺς κῆρας ἄμυνε / παιδὸς ἑοῦ, 12.402–403; see [[zeus]] and [[loigos]]).

**The call to the Lycians.** Ajax's spear drives him back a little from the battlement, but “he did not altogether / draw back, since his heart hoped to win glory” (12.406–407; see [[kydos]]). Wheeling round on “the godlike Lycians” (12.408), who share his epithet, he asks: “Lycians, why do you let go of your furious courage like this?” (12.409; see [[furious]] and [[alke]]). He has made “a path for many” (κέλευθον, 12.399), but he cannot make one to the ships alone: “the work of more men is better” (12.412; see [[the-work-of-many]]). They bear down harder “around their lord, the counselor” (12.414), but neither side can drive the other from the wall (12.415–436), until Zeus gives the higher glory to Hector, “who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.438; see [[hector]]).

**Afterward.** In Book 16 he calls on “the godlike Lycians” in nearly the same verse, this time to stop their flight from Patroclus (16.421 ≈ 12.408), and the man who came on like a mountain lion dies like a bull under a lion's jaws (16.487–491). Over his body Patroclus calls him “the man who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans, Sarpedon” (16.558–559), the narrator's verse for Hector here (12.438). His armor, stripped by the Achaeans, becomes a prize of the armed combat at Patroclus' games, beside the sword Achilles took from Asteropaeus (16.663–665, 23.798–808; see [[contests]]).
