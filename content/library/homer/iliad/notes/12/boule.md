**Polydamas' counsel.** Polydamas' first advice, to leave the chariots at the ditch and cross on foot, pleases Hector (12.60–83), and the other Trojans and their allies “obeyed the counsel of blameless Polydamas” (12.108–109); Asius alone would not leave his horses (12.110–115; see [[polydamas]], [[asius]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). His second, to turn back at the sign of the eagle, opens with a complaint: it is not fitting for a man of the people to speak against Hector, “neither in council / nor ever in war” (ἐνὶ βουλῇ, 12.213–214; see [[demos]]). In Book 18 the narrator will say of the assembly that rejects his last advice that they praised Hector, who counseled evil, and no one praised Polydamas, “who gave good counsel” (ἐσθλὴν φράζετο βουλήν, 18.312–313), the words Polydamas uses of himself here (ἐσθλὰ φραζομένῳ, 12.212).

**The counsels of Zeus.** Hector answers that Polydamas would have him forget “loud-thundering Zeus' / counsels, which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to”, and obey birds: “But let us obey the counsel of great Zeus, / who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.235–242; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). Διὸς βουλή is the phrase of the proem, the plan of Zeus that was being fulfilled (1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Hector knows the part of it that Iris brought him, power to kill until he reaches the ships (11.200–209). He does not know the part that Zeus told Hera, that he will not stop until Achilles rises beside the ships in the fight over the dead Patroclus (8.470–477; see [[prophecy]]).

**What Zeus wishes.** The narrator gives Zeus' will in the verb: when Asius complains, Zeus' heart “wished to hold out glory to Hector” (ἐβούλετο, 12.174), as in Book 11 the gods blamed him “because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (11.79; see [[kydos]]).

**The counselor.** At the end of Sarpedon's call the Lycians press harder around “their lord, the counselor” (βουληφόρον, 12.414; see [[counselor]] and [[the-work-of-many]]).
