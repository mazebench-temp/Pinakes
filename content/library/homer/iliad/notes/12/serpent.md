summary: The serpent of Book 12 is the portent at the ditch. Blood-red, monstrous and still alive in an eagle's talons, it strikes the bird and is dropped among the Trojans, who shudder at “the writhing snake” (12.200–209); the narrator calls it by the only ὄφις in Homer.

φοινήεντα δράκοντα φέρων ὀνύχεσσι πέλωρον / ζωὸν ἔτʼ ἀσπαίροντα: “carrying in its talons a blood-red serpent, monstrous, alive and still struggling” (12.202–203). φοινήεις, “blood-red”, occurs only in this verse and its repetition (12.220; see [[blood-red]]); the serpent of Aulis was δαφοινός, “blood-red on its back” (2.308). πέλωρον, “monstrous”, is a word for portents: the serpent and the sparrows were δεινὰ πέλωρα θεῶν, “the dread portents of the gods” (2.321). The serpent is given a warrior's phrase. It “had not yet forgotten the joy of battle” (οὔ πω λήθετο χάρμης, 12.203), as Sarpedon on the wall “did not forget the joy of battle” (12.393); it bends back (ἰδνωθεὶς ὀπίσω) and strikes the eagle on the breast beside the neck (12.204–205).

When it falls, the narrator uses another word: Τρῶες δʼ ἐρρίγησαν ὅπως ἴδον αἰόλον ὄφιν, “And the Trojans shuddered when they saw the writhing snake” (12.208). ὄφις, the ordinary word for a snake in later Greek, occurs in Homer only here, for the same creature as the δράκων of six verses before, and this entry takes both words. αἰόλος, “quick-moving, writhing”, is also the word for the wasps' supple waists (12.167). Lying in the midst of the Trojans, the snake is “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.209; see [[teras]]).

In Polydamas' reading the serpent is the Achaeans, who will cut the Trojans down “fighting in defense of the ships” (12.227; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). At Aulis the serpent was the devourer and the birds its prey; here it is the prey that fights its way free (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).
