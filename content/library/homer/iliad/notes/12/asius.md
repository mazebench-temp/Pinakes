summary: Asius, son of Hyrtacus, leads the third company with Helenus and Deiphobus, and alone of the Trojans will not leave his horses at the ditch. He drives for the open gate on the left of the ships, is held there by the two Lapiths, and calls Zeus a lover of lies; the narrator foretells his death by Idomeneus' spear.

**The Catalogue's verses.** He is the third leader of the third company, with Helenus and Deiphobus, “two sons of Priam” (12.94–95; see [[helenus]] and [[deiphobus]]), and he is introduced as the Catalogue introduced him, his name doubled across the verse: Ἄσιος ἥρως / Ἄσιος Ὑρτακίδης, “Asius, the hero, / Asius, son of Hyrtacus, whom his horses carried from Arisbe, / great and tawny horses, from the river Selleis” (12.95–97; 12.96–97 = 2.838–839; see [[hyrtacus]], [[arisbe]], [[selleis-arisbe]] and [[tawny]]). The horses are what the book remembers him by.

**The fool.** All the others obey Polydamas and leave their chariots at the ditch (12.108–109; see [[polydamas]]), but not he:

{{quote:9bd01e70-43d9-51d2-bf9d-618f6314cbed}}

νήπιος, “the fool!”, is the narrator's word for a man who does not see what is coming (see [[nepios]]); the narrator gives it to Asius' followers too (12.127). μοῖρα δυσώνυμος, “fate of evil name”, enfolds him before he can come home (12.116; see [[moira]] and [[of-evil-name]]). The prophecy is kept in Book 13. Asius comes on foot in front of his horses, which his charioteer holds so close that they breathe on his shoulders; Idomeneus' spear goes through his throat, and he falls like an oak or a poplar or a tall pine that carpenters fell for a ship's timber, and lies stretched out “in front of his horses and chariot” (13.384–393; see [[idomeneus]] and [[deucalion]]). Antilochus kills the charioteer and drives the horses off to the Achaeans (13.394–401).

**The open gate.** He makes for the left of the ships, where the Achaeans came in from the plain with their chariots, and finds the gate held open to save companions fleeing from the battle (12.118–123; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). His men follow “with shrill cries”, sure that the Achaeans will no longer hold out (12.124–126). At the gate stand the Lapiths Polypoetes and Leonteus, like oaks rooted in the mountains, who “awaited great Asius as he came on, and did not flee” (12.127–136; see [[polypoetes]], [[leonteus]] and [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). Five men are named around him: Iamenus, Orestes, his son Adamas, Thoon and Oenomaus (12.139–140; see [[iamenus]], [[orestes-the-trojan]], [[adamas]], [[thoon-companion-of-asius]] and [[oenomaus-the-trojan]]).

**A lover of lies.** As the stones fly, he groans and strikes both his thighs, the gesture of Ares when he hears of his son's death and of Patroclus when he sees the Trojans storm the wall (15.113, 15.397), and speaks in anger:

{{quote:c73de222-c52e-5558-b6e6-df3c72f06b95}}

φιλοψευδής occurs only here (see [[lover-of-lies]] and [[father-zeus]]). His complaint holds a simile, rarer in speeches than in the narrative: the two Lapiths are wasps or bees that will not leave their house by the road but fight off the hunters for their young (see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]). “So he spoke, but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus” (12.173–174; see [[zeus]]).

**Afterward.** The book does not say what Asius did next; Leonteus kills Iamenus and Orestes (12.193). In Book 13 Deiphobus avenges him by killing Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, and boasts that Asius, on his way to Hades, will be glad of the escort (13.402–416); Meriones kills his son Adamas (13.560–575). When Hector looks for “Adamas, son of Asius, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus” among the foremost, he does not find them alive (13.758–764, 13.771). Every man named with him at the gate is dead by the end of Book 13, if the Thoon and the Oenomaus killed there are his companions (13.506, 13.545).
