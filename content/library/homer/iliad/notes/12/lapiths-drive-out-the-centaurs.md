summary: The two Lapiths who hold the gate of the Achaean wall are the sons of the story. Polypoetes is the son of Peirithous, born on the day his father drove the Centaurs from Pelion, and Leonteus the grandson of Caeneus; Homer calls them “high-hearted sons of Lapith spearmen” (12.127–130).
passages:
  - 12.127-136 | The sons of the Lapiths at the gate
---

The Iliad names the Lapiths only in this book: υἷας ὑπερθύμους Λαπιθάων αἰχμητάων, “high-hearted sons of Lapith spearmen” (12.128), and σὺν δʼ ἔβαλον Λαπίθαι πόλεμον καὶ δηϊοτῆτα, “And the Lapiths joined in the war and the combat” (12.181; see [[lapiths]]). The sons are named by their fathers' generation, the spearmen of the war with the Centaurs. The Catalogue gave the descent of both (2.740–746): Polypoetes was born to Peirithous and Hippodameia “on the day when he took vengeance on the shaggy beasts and drove them from Pelion”, and Leonteus is the son of Coronus, son of Caeneus. Peirithous and Caeneus are among the men whom Nestor named as the mightiest he had ever seen, who fought “the beasts who lived in the mountains, and destroyed them terribly” (1.262–268; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). Book 12 calls Polypoetes “the son of Peirithous” twice, at his entrance and at his first kill (12.129, 12.182; see [[peirithous]]).

The story itself is not told again, but the sons of the men who drove the beasts out of their mountain are given images of the mountains. They stand before the gates like oaks in the mountains (οὔρεσιν, 12.132), and fight like two wild boars in the mountains (ἐν ὄρεσσιν, 12.146; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]] and [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]). They hold the gate against Asius and bring down eight Trojans (12.127–194; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]).
