---
summary: The wall was built “against the will of the gods, the immortals”; Hector calls Zeus lord over “all mortals and immortals”; and Sarpedon imagines that he and Glaucus might be “ageless and immortal forever.”
---

**Against the will of the gods.** The book opens with the reason the wall will not last: “It was built against the will of the gods, the immortals; and so it did not stand firm for long” (12.8–9). The Achaeans had given the gods no hecatombs for it (12.6; [[hecatomb]]), the fault that Poseidon put before Zeus on the day it was built, when he asked whether any mortal would still tell his mind to the immortals (7.446–450; [[the-building-of-the-wall]], [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Mortals and immortals.** Hector answers Polydamas' reading of the eagle with the god's rank: “But let us obey the counsel of great Zeus, who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.241–242; [[mortal-men]], [[boule]]).

**Ageless and immortal.** Sarpedon reasons from the life the gods have and men do not: “My friend, if, once we had escaped this war, we two were to be ageless and immortal forever, then neither would I myself fight among the foremost, nor would I send you into the battle where men win glory” (12.322–325; [[sarpedons-speech]]). Hector wished to be “immortal and ageless all my days” (8.539; [[immortal-and-ageless]]); Sarpedon draws the opposite lesson from the same pair of words: because no mortal can escape the fates of death, the two of them must go forward (12.326–328; [[gods-and-mortals]]).
