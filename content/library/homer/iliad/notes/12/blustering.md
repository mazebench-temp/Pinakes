---
summary: In the simile of the stones a “blustering wind,” ἄνεμος ζαής, drives the shadowy clouds and pours down the snow.
grc: [ζαὴς]
---

“And the stones fell to the ground like snowflakes, that a blustering wind, driving the shadowy clouds, pours down thick upon the bountiful earth” (12.156–158; [[a-snow-of-stones]], [[shadowy-mountains]], [[bountiful-earth]]). The word is ζαής, not ὑπεραής: ζα- (“very,” as in [[violent]], ζαχρηής) + the root of ἄημι (“blow”), “blowing hard,” from the same root as ὑπεραής, the word of the storm wind in Book 11 (11.297), and the translation gives both the same English. Homer uses ζαής three times, always of wind: here; in a simile of the Odyssey, as a strong wind scatters a heap of chaff when the wave breaks Odysseus' raft (Od. 5.368–370); and of the gale Zeus stirs up against Odysseus' men on Thrinacia (Od. 12.313). Hector fought “like a storm wind” at the start of the book (12.40; [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]); here the wind of the simile carries the stones that both sides throw. The form is added to the entry.
