summary: On Polydamas' advice the Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch, in the verses of the Achaean order at dawn, and go against the wall on foot in five companies, each under three leaders (12.75–107). The Achaeans hold their battalions behind the battlements, and Sarpedon tells Glaucus that a king's place is among the foremost.

**On foot.** Polydamas' plan is the order the Achaeans took at dawn: “let the attendants hold the horses at the ditch, / and we ourselves, on foot, armed in our armor, / all follow Hector in a body” (12.76–78). When it is carried out, “each man gave orders to his own charioteer / to hold the horses in good order there at the ditch” (12.84–85), the two verses of the Achaean order (11.47–48), and 12.77 is 11.49 (see [[chariot]] and [[polydamas]]). The Achaeans chose to fight on foot, far ahead of their horsemen (11.51–52); the Trojans leave their chariots because they cannot drive them across the ditch (12.50–66).

**Five companies.** “And they, parting and arraying themselves, / marshaled in five companies, followed with their leaders” (12.86–87). πένταχα, “in five parts”, occurs only here. Each company has three leaders (see the translation's note on 12.86–104):

1. Hector, Polydamas and Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, who goes on foot because Hector has left another, weaker man with the chariot (12.88–92; see [[cebriones]]);
2. Paris, Alcathous and Agenor (12.93; see [[alcathous]]);
3. Helenus and Deiphobus, two sons of Priam, with Asius (12.94–97; see [[deiphobus]] and [[asius]]);
4. Aeneas with the two sons of Antenor, Archelochus and Acamas (12.98–100; see [[aeneas]]);
5. Sarpedon at the head of the allies, with Glaucus and Asteropaeus (12.101–104; see [[sarpedon]] and [[asteropaeus]]).

Hector's company is “the most and the best” (12.89, 12.197), as the Trojan Catalogue said of the men who armed with him (2.817–818). Two of the verses are the Catalogue's own: Asius' horses from the river Selleis (12.96–97 = 2.838–839) and the sons of Antenor, “well skilled in every kind of fighting” (12.100 = 2.823; see [[trojan-catalogue]]). The companies lock themselves together “with their well-made oxhides” and go straight at the Danaans (12.105–107; see [[shield]]). Only Asius keeps his chariot (12.108–115).

**In close order.** The simile that brings Hector to the ditch uses the language of formation. The hunters face the boar or lion “arraying themselves in close order like a tower” (πυργηδὸν σφέας αὐτοὺς ἀρτύναντες, 12.43), and the beast turns again and again, “testing the ranks of men, / and wherever he charges, there the ranks of men give way” (12.47–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). Hector uses the phrase of the Achaeans in Book 13, “though they have arrayed themselves in close order like a tower” (13.152), and πυργηδόν describes them again when they hold against him at the ships (15.618).

**Behind the wall.** The defenders fight from the towers and behind the battlements (12.154–155, 12.263–264; see [[battlements]]), and the two Ajaxes range along them, rousing every rank, “outstanding, or middling, / or worse” (12.269–270; see [[not-all-alike-in-war]]). When the Lycians come, “the Argives on the other side strengthened their battalions / inside the wall” (12.415–416), and neither line can drive the other back (12.417–420).

**The foremost.** Sarpedon gives the leader's place in front its reason. Because the kings of Lycia are honored with the best seat, meat and wine, “So now we must take our stand among the foremost Lycians / and meet the blazing battle” (12.315–316), the verses with which Agamemnon reproached Menestheus and Odysseus for standing back, the second of them unchanged (12.315–316 ≈ 4.341–342; see [[feast]] and [[sarpedons-speech]]). The kings are to be seen fighting “among the foremost Lycians” (12.321), and only if they were to be ageless and immortal would Sarpedon not fight “among the foremost” (12.322–324).
