The narrator leaves the Lapiths at the left gate to say that the battle is everywhere:

{{quote:161a026a-e2d6-581c-9902-80fb52513ea5}}

ἀργαλέον δέ με ταῦτα θεὸν ὣς πάντʼ ἀγορεῦσαι: the narrator speaks of himself, με, as elsewhere only when he calls on the Muses (2.484–493, 11.218, 14.508, 16.112; see the translation's note on 12.175–181). The thought is the invocation's. The Muses “are present, and know all things, / but we hear only the report, and know nothing” (2.485–486; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]]), and the singer could not tell the multitude “not even if I had ten tongues and ten mouths” (2.489; see [[ten-tongues]]). Only a god could see the whole battle at once and tell it; here the narrator asks no god for help, and tells what a mortal can, one gate and its two defenders (12.181–194; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]). What he does say of the rest is strange: “everywhere a wondrous fire had risen around the wall / of stone”, either the wall of stone or a fire of stone, the hail of missiles, since λάϊνον can go with either noun; and he speaks of other gates, where until now the story has followed one (see the translation's note on 12.175–181, and [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).

When the fighting reaches the ships, the first verse of the passage (12.175) returns with ships in place of gates: “others were fighting their battle around other ships” (15.414).
