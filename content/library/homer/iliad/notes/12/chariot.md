**The lash of Zeus.** The Argives are “beaten down by the lash of Zeus” (Διὸς μάστιγι, 12.37), the driver's whip in the god's hand; Ajax uses the words again in Book 13, “by the evil lash of Zeus we Achaeans were beaten down” (13.812; see [[zeus]]).

**At the ditch.** Hector urges his companions across, “but his horses / did not dare, swift-footed though they were, and neighed loudly, standing at the very / edge” (12.50–52). The ditch is wide, its banks overhang, and stakes are set close along it, so that “There a horse drawing a well-wheeled chariot could not easily / go in” (12.58–59; see [[the-achaean-wall]], [[horses]] and [[well-wheeled]]). In Book 8 Hector had boasted that “the horses will easily leap over the ditch they have dug” (8.179). Polydamas explains why chariots are useless here: beyond the stakes stands the wall, “where there is no way to get down and fight / for horsemen; for it is narrow” (12.65–66), and if the Achaeans turn and counterattack, the Trojans will be caught in the ditch (12.71–74; see [[polydamas]]).

**Down from the chariots.** His counsel pleases Hector, “and at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor” (12.81), the verse of the warrior who dismounts to fight (3.29, 4.419, 5.494, 6.103, 11.211). “Nor did the other Trojans gather on their chariots, / but all sprang down” (12.82–83), and each tells his charioteer to hold the horses in good order at the ditch (12.84–85, in the verses of the Achaean order, 11.47–48; see [[battle-order]]). Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, goes on foot as a leader of the first company, and Hector leaves “another man, weaker than Cebriones” beside the chariot (12.91–92; see [[cebriones]]).

**Asius.** “But the son of Hyrtacus, Asius, leader of men, would not / leave his horses there, and his charioteer and attendant” (12.110–111). The narrator calls him a fool: “glorying in his horses and chariot”, he will never drive back from the ships to windy Ilios, for Idomeneus' spear awaits him (12.113–117; see [[asius]] and [[nepios]]). He drives to the left of the ships, “where the Achaeans / were coming back from the plain with their horses and chariots”, and finds the gate held open for fugitives (12.118–123; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). The builders had made the gates “a way for driving horses” (7.340), and the one Trojan who kept his chariot makes for it; but two Lapiths stand in front of it, and he gets no farther (12.127–174; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]). In Book 13 he dies on foot in front of his horses, and the narrator's word comes true: Idomeneus' spear strikes him in the throat, he lies stretched out “in front of his horses and chariot”, his charioteer is killed, and Antilochus drives the horses off to the Achaeans (13.384–401). The horses he would not leave are taken from him.

**The way back.** Polydamas' fear for the chariots comes true in Book 16, when the Trojans are driven back over the ditch: many teams break their chariots at the pole in it and leave them behind, while Achilles' immortal horses leap it (16.366–381; see [[divination]]).
