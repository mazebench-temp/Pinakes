---
summary: The fighting at the wall is “a wondrous fire,” θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ, in the narrator's aside and in Hector's call to burn the ships, and the Trojans follow Hector “with a wondrous din.”
grc: [θεσπιδαὲς]
---

**The wondrous fire.** The narrator breaks off his account of the gate: “and it is hard for me to tell all these things like a god; for everywhere a wondrous fire had risen around the wall of stone” (12.176–178; [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). θεσπιδαής joins the first part of θεσπέσιος to the root of δαίω (“kindle, burn”): “wondrously blazing.” The fire is the fighting itself, as battle and cries “blazed” around the wall at the start (δεδήει, 12.35); whether λάϊνον, “of stone,” goes with the wall or with the fire is uncertain. At the breach Hector turns the figure into a command: “throw the wondrous fire on the ships” (12.441; [[tamer-of-horses]]). The phrase θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ always ends the verse: of the fire Zeus waits to see on the ships (15.597), of a forest fire in a simile (20.490), of Hephaestus' fire against the river (21.342, 21.381), and of Patroclus' pyre (23.216). Its forms are added to the entry's.

**The wondrous din.** “So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him with a wondrous din” (12.251–252; [[delighting-in-thunder]]). ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ was the noise with which the Trojans poured missiles on the fleeing Diomedes in Book 8 (8.158–159), and it will be the noise with which they follow Apollo over the ditch when he breaks down the wall (15.355). See [[hector]].
