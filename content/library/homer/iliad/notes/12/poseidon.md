summary: Poseidon takes no part in the fight at the wall. Before the battle the narrator tells how, after the sack of Troy, he and Apollo will turn the rivers of Ida against the Achaean wall, and how he himself, trident in hand, will sweep its foundations into the sea and leave the shore smooth, as Zeus allowed him in Book 7.

  - 12.17-34 | Poseidon and Apollo destroy the wall

**The wall's end.** As the Trojans come against the wall, the narrator looks past the end of the poem to its destruction: “then Poseidon and Apollo took counsel / to bring the wall to nothing, leading against it the might of the rivers” (12.17–18). It will come when the best of the Trojans are dead, the city is sacked and the Argives have sailed home (12.13–16). Apollo turns the rivers against the wall, Zeus rains (12.24–26; see [[apollo]] and [[zeus]]), and Poseidon leads:

**Zeus' leave.** He does what Zeus allowed him when he complained of the wall in Book 7: when the Achaeans have gone home, “break down the wall and sweep it all into the sea, / and cover the great beach over again with sand” (7.461–462). 12.31 repeats 7.462 almost word for word. Zeus ended, “so that the great wall of the Achaeans may be brought to nothing” (7.463), and his verb, ἀμαλδύνω, occurs again only in this account (12.18, 12.32). The narrator gives Poseidon's grievance in Poseidon's own words: the Achaeans “did not give glorious hecatombs to the gods” (12.6; 7.450; see [[hecatomb]], [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]], [[the-building-of-the-wall]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The trident.** τρίαινα, the trident, occurs in the Iliad only here (12.27). In the Odyssey Poseidon takes it up to raise the storm against Odysseus (Od. 5.291–292) and to split the rock on which the Locrian Ajax boasted that he had escaped the sea “against the will of the gods” (ἀέκητι θεῶν, Od. 4.499–511). The Achaean wall was built “against the will of the gods” (12.8); θεῶν ἀέκητι recurs in the Iliad only at 15.720, of the ships themselves, which Hector says came to Troy against the gods' will. Both times Poseidon undoes what men did, or boasted of, against the gods' will. He works as the sea god: he sends the foundations “out on the waves”, leaves the shore of the Hellespont smooth, and covers the beach with sand (12.28–31; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[hellespont]]).

**The two builders.** Poseidon told Zeus that he and Phoebus Apollo built the wall of Troy for Laomedon (7.452–453); in Book 21 he says that he built it while Apollo herded Laomedon's cattle (21.446–449; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). Here the two gods of Troy's wall take down the Achaeans' wall together. In the battle of the gods they will not fight each other, for Apollo is ashamed to come to blows with his father's brother (21.461–469).

**Not in the battle.** In the fight itself Poseidon has no part, and Zeus' ban still holds (8.10–12). In the next book he watches the battle from the peak of Samothrace (13.10–14). He pities the Achaeans, is mightily indignant with Zeus (13.15–16), and goes down to rouse them at the ships in the likeness of Calchas (13.43–45). The god who will wash the wall away fights for its defenders.
