summary: Helmets lie with the shields in the dust by the Simoeis, ring dry under the stones at the wall, and fail the men who wear them: Polypoetes' spear goes through Damasus' bronze-cheeked helmet, and Ajax's boulder crushes Epicles' four-ridged one (12.22, 12.160, 12.183–186, 12.339, 12.384).
grc: [τρυφάλειαι, κόρυθες, κόρυς, τρυφαλειῶν]
passages:
  - 12.183-186 | Damasus' helmet
---

**Shields and helmets.** The Simoeis is the place “where many oxhide shields and helmets / had fallen in the dust, and the race of men half-divine” (12.22–23): the arms stand for the men who wore them (see [[simoeis]], [[shield]] and [[hemitheos]]). At the wall “the helmets rang dry all around”, as stones like millstones struck them (12.160–161; see the translation's note), and at Menestheus' tower the din rises from shields and “helmets with horsehair crests” (ἱπποκόμων τρυφαλειῶν, 12.339; see [[horsehair-crested]]).

**Through the helmet.** Polypoetes “struck Damasus with his spear through the bronze-cheeked helmet; / nor did the bronze helmet hold it, but right through / the bronze point broke the bone, and the brain / inside was all spattered” (12.183–186; see [[damasus]], [[polypoetes]] and [[bronze-cheeked]]). The last three verses are those of Oileus' death in Book 11, with κόρυς in place of στεφάνη (12.184–186 ≈ 11.96–98), and they return for Demoleon, killed by Achilles (20.397–400). Two of Homer's four words name one helmet here: it is a κυνέη, once a cap of dogskin and now any helmet, with cheek-pieces of bronze, and in the next verse a χαλκείη κόρυς (see the translation's note on 12.183).

**Four ridges.** Ajax's boulder “crushed the four-ridged helmet, and smashed the bones / of the head all together” (12.384–385; see [[epicles]] and [[four-ridged]]). τετράφαλος is formed from φάλος, the ridge on the crown of a helmet (3.362), and means “with four ridges”; it is said again only of Achilles' helmet (22.315). τετραφάληρος (5.743 = 11.41) is another word, “with four plates” (see the translation's note on 12.384). In Book 11 a helmet saved Hector from Diomedes' spear (11.349–353); in Book 12 no helmet saves the man who wears it.
