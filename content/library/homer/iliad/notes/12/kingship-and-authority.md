summary: Sarpedon defines what kings owe for their honors. Polydamas complains that Hector's authority leaves no room for counsel, and Hector threatens with death the man who turns another from the fight. Hector bids the Trojans obey Zeus, “who is lord over all mortals and immortals”, and the Lycians press on “fearing their lord's rebuke”.

passages:
  - 12.310-321 | The honors of the Lycian kings
---

**What kings owe.** Sarpedon's speech is the Iliad's clearest account of what a king's honors are for. The Lycians give their kings the best seat, meat and wine, and land, and the kings owe them the front rank, so that a Lycian may say, “Truly not without glory are they who rule over Lycia, / these kings of ours” (12.310–321; see [[sarpedons-speech]], [[basileus]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]). The verb is κοιρανέουσιν, “rule” (12.318; see [[koiranos]]), and the judge is a common soldier.

**What a commander may demand.** Hector's authority is another matter. Polydamas says that a man of the people may not speak against him, “neither in council / nor ever in war, but always to increase your power” (12.213–214; see [[kratos]] and [[demos]]). Hector answers with a threat: if Polydamas holds back from the combat, or turns another man away from the war, “at once, struck by my spear, you will lose your life” (12.248–250). Agamemnon had made the same threat to his whole army in Book 2: “any man I see wishing to hang back from the fighting / and linger beside the curved ships, for him there will be / no hope then of escaping the dogs and the birds” (2.391–393; see [[aristotle-death-in-my-hand]]). A commander in the field can threaten death; in the assembly he can be answered.

**The lord of all.** Hector sets one authority above his own and Polydamas' alike: “But let us obey the counsel of great Zeus, / who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.241–242; see [[zeus]], [[anax]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]).

**A lord's rebuke.** When Sarpedon calls the Lycians to follow him, “they, fearing their lord's rebuke, / bore down the harder around their lord, the counselor” (12.413–414; see [[the-work-of-many]] and [[counselor]]). ἄναξ twice in two verses: the king who has argued for his honors to Glaucus is obeyed by his men from fear.
