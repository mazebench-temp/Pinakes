---
summary: Plato's Ion is the oldest witness to the book's name, placing the eagle and the serpent “at the Teichomachia” among the passages a seer should judge. The Republic rewards its brave men with the honors that Sarpedon says the Lycians give their kings, the Seventh Letter turns Hector's retort to Polydamas against Dionysius, and the Apology and the Cratylus call the heroes demigods, Homer's ἡμίθεοι of 12.23.
---

- The eagle and the serpent, recited in full as a passage for the seer to judge, “at the Teichomachia” (*Ion* 538e–539d): [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]] and [[title-of-book-12]].
- **The honors of the brave.** In the *Republic* the city will honor its brave men at sacrifices and on all such occasions with hymns, and “with seats of honor and meats and fuller cups” (*Republic* 5.468d–e). The verse is the one in which Sarpedon names the honors of the Lycian kings, “with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (12.311), and Hector the honors the Danaans gave Diomedes (8.162; see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]). Plato's city gives the honors as a reward for courage; Sarpedon reasons from the honors to the duty: because the Lycians give them, their kings must stand among the foremost (12.315–321; see [[sarpedons-speech]]).
- **The gods destroyed his wits.** Hector answers Polydamas at the ditch with the verse with which Paris answered Antenor in Book 7, ἐξ ἄρα δή τοι ἔπειτα θεοὶ φρένας ὤλεσαν αὐτοί, “then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits” (12.234 = 7.360). The *Seventh Letter*, handed down among Plato's works, turns it against any man who has put what he takes most seriously into writing, and so against Dionysius of Syracuse and his treatise on first principles: then surely “not the gods, but mortals” destroyed his wits (θεοὶ μὲν οὔ, βροτοὶ δέ, 344c–d). Whether Plato wrote the letter is disputed.
- **Demigods.** Homer calls the men who fell by the Simoeis “the race of men half-divine” (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, 12.23), the only ἡμίθεος in his poems. In the *Apology* Socrates speaks of “the demigods who died at Troy”, above all the son of Thetis, who thought so little of danger compared with disgrace (28c–d), and hopes to meet in Hades Minos, Rhadamanthys, Aeacus, Triptolemus and “the other demigods who were just in their own lives” (41a). In the *Cratylus* he asks Hermogenes whether he does not know that the heroes are demigods, all born of a god's love for a mortal woman or a mortal's for a goddess, and derives ἥρως, “hero”, from ἔρως, “love” (398c–e). See [[hemitheos]] and [[heros]].
