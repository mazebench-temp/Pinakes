summary: Dawn is named once in Book 12, as a quarter of the sky. Birds on the right go “toward the dawn and the sun”, and Hector does not care which way they go (12.239).
# πρὸς ἠῶ τʼ ἠέλιόν τε (12.239), "toward the dawn and the sun", is the east, the place where the dawn
# rises. The index counts ἠώς where it is the dawn, its spread over the earth included (7.451, 7.458),
# and leaves out only the time of day, "morning" (8.66, 11.84). The phrase also catches ὑπʼ ἠῶ τʼ ἠέλιόν
# τε, "under the dawn and the sun" (5.267), where no pattern of the entry counted the accusative ἠῶ
# before; the Sun already counts both lines. The Odyssey has the phrase at 9.26 and 13.240.

Hector answers Polydamas' reading of the eagle that he does not care whether birds go “to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, / or to the left, toward the misty darkness” (12.239–240; see [[helios]], [[divination]] and [[misty]]). ἠῶ here is the dawn as a place, the east where it comes up. The index counts it to Dawn, as it counts the dawn that “spreads” over the earth in the speeches of Poseidon and Zeus (ἐπικίδναται ἠώς, 7.451, 7.458), and it counts the same phrase in Book 5, where the horses of Tros are the best “under the dawn and the sun” (5.267). The Odyssey uses the pair for the east and the west (*Odyssey* 9.25–26, 13.239–241).

Book 12 has no dawn of its own. It belongs to the third day of battle, which dawned when Dawn rose from the bed of Tithonus (11.1–2) and ends only at the sunset of Book 18 (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[tithonus]]).
