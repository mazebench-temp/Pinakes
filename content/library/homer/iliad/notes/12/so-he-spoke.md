summary: The closings of Book 12 tell what each speech achieved. Polydamas' safe counsel pleases Hector, Asius does not persuade the mind of Zeus, Glaucus does not turn away, the herald and Ajax do not disobey, the Lycians fear their lord's rebuke, and the Trojans hear Hector's call “with their ears”.
grc: ["ὣς τώ γε προβοῶντε"]
en: [So the two]
---

The closings of Book 12: 12.80, 173, 251, 277, 329, 351, 364, 370, 413, 442.

**Persuaded or not.** “So spoke Polydamas, and his safe counsel pleased Hector, / and at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor” (12.80–81): the closing gives the verdict, and the next verse the deed. Both verses return when Hector takes Polydamas' counsel again in Book 13 (12.80–81 = 13.748–749; see [[polydamas]]). Asius' complaint gets the other verdict: “So he spoke, but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this; / for his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.173–174; see [[asius]] and [[zeus]]). The narrator answers the speech with what the speaker cannot know.

**Left open.** Polydamas' second counsel has no closing. Hector's answer follows at once, “Looking at him darkly” (12.230; see [[looking-darkly]]), and his own speech closes with action: “So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him / with a wondrous din” (12.251–252). The same verse and a half close his answer to Ajax at the end of Book 13 (12.251–252 ≈ 13.833–834).

**Obedience.** Three closings are of obedience. “So he spoke, and Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey” (12.329) has a verb more than the usual verse, ἀπετράπετʼ, “turned away”: Glaucus neither shrinks from the fight nor refuses his friend (see [[glaucus]]). “So he spoke, and the herald heard him and did not disobey” (12.351) is the verse of Talthybius' errand (4.198; see [[heralds]]), and “So he spoke, and great Telamonian Ajax did not disobey” (12.364) is the verse used of Athena, Hera, Nestor and Iris (2.166, 5.767, 8.112, 11.195; see [[ajax]]). Ajax's own instructions close as he leaves: “So he spoke, and Telamonian Ajax went away” (12.370), with the half-verse of Hector's closing (ὣς ἄρα φωνήσας, 12.251).

**The two.** The joint speech of the two Ajaxes closes in the dual: “So the two, shouting out in front, urged on the battle of the Achaeans” (ὣς τώ γε προβοῶντε, 12.277; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]), as the plea of the two sons of Antimachus closed in Book 11 (11.136).

**Fear and hearing.** Sarpedon's call to the Lycians closes, “So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke, / bore down the harder around their lord, the counselor” (12.413–414; see [[sarpedon]] and [[counselor]]); the first verse returns, with “their father's” for “their lord's”, when Priam's sons fear his rebuke (24.265). Hector's call to the Trojans closes, “So he spoke, urging them on, and they all heard him with their ears” (12.442; see [[exhortation]]). His shout was raised to be heard (12.439), where Menestheus' could not be (12.337–338).
