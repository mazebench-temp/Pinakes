summary: The one bird-sign of Book 12 is an eagle on the left that drops a wounded serpent among the Trojans. Polydamas reads it as a prophet would, a warning that they will not come back from the ships in good order, and Hector answers that he trusts the counsels of Zeus and cares nothing for birds (12.195–250).

**The sign.** The young men around Hector and Polydamas are hesitating at the ditch when “a bird had come to them as they were eager to cross, / an eagle flying high, on the left, skirting the army” (12.200–201). It carries a blood-red serpent, still alive, which strikes it on the breast beside the neck; the eagle drops it in the middle of the throng and flies off screaming, and the Trojans shudder at “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.202–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]], [[eagle]], [[serpent]] and [[teras]]). The left is the side of ill omen. The good signs of the poem come on the right: Zeus' lightning when the fleet sailed (2.353), the heron Athena sends to Odysseus and Diomedes (10.274–277), and the lightning with which Zeus encourages the Trojans (9.236–237).

**The reading.** Polydamas is no seer, but he reads the sign as Calchas read the serpent and the sparrows at Aulis, by likeness (2.308–330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). The eagle let its prey go “before it reached its own home, / and did not finish carrying it to give to its young” (12.221–222), and so it will be with the Trojans:

ὑποκρίνομαι, “interpret”, is the verb for reading an omen. In the Odyssey an eagle carries a goose past the horses of Telemachus as he leaves Sparta, and while Menelaus ponders “how he might interpret it rightly” (ὑποκρίναιτο), Helen reads it as Odysseus coming home to take vengeance on the suitors (Od. 15.160–178). θεοπρόπος, “prophet”, is a title of Calchas (13.70; see [[calchas]] and [[theopropion]]). Polydamas names two marks of the true prophet: he knows portents “clearly in his heart”, and “the people would obey” him. The second is aimed at Hector, for Polydamas has begun by complaining that it is not fitting “for a man of the people to speak against you” (12.211–214; see [[polydamas]] and [[demos]]).

**Hector's answer.** Hector, “looking at him darkly” (12.230; see [[looking-darkly]]), sets the counsels of Zeus, “which he himself promised me”, against the bird. Polydamas, he says, would have him obey long-winged birds, and he cares nothing whether they fly to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, or to the left, toward the misty darkness (12.235–240), that is, to the east or to the west. “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). οἰωνός, a bird six verses earlier, here means the omen itself; in the singular Homer uses the word only of a bird of omen (12.243; 13.823; 24.292, 24.310; Od. 15.532, 17.160). Hector does not deny that Zeus sends signs; he trusts another, the promise brought by Iris that he would kill until he reached the ships (11.200–209; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). In the next verses Zeus sends a storm of dust from Ida against the Achaeans. The Trojans attack “trusting in his portents and in their own strength” (12.256), in the words of the old stories of men who obeyed the portents of the gods (4.398, 4.408, 6.183).

**The outcome.** The poem bears Polydamas out. The Trojans break the wall and reach the ships, but when Patroclus drives them back, “they did not cross back in good order” (οὐδὲ κατὰ μοῖραν πέραον πάλιν), and many chariots are left broken in the ditch (16.366–371). The narrator later says of Polydamas that “he alone saw before and after” (ὃ γὰρ οἶος ὅρα πρόσσω καὶ ὀπίσσω, 18.250).

**Later.** Plato's Socrates quotes the eagle and the serpent (12.200–207) as a passage for the seer, not the rhapsode, to judge, “as at the Teichomachia” (*Ion* 539b–d; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]).
