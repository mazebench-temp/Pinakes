summary: Book 12 is the battle for the wall. The narrator first tells how Poseidon and Apollo will wash it away after the war; then the Trojans leave their chariots at its ditch, tear at its towers and battlements, and Hector breaks one of its gates with a stone and leaps inside (12.3–34, 12.49–87, 12.257–264, 12.437–471).

**Its end, told first.** Before the battle the narrator looks past the poem. The wall had been built “to protect their ships” (12.5) and without hecatombs, as Poseidon complained (12.6 = 7.450); it was built “against the will of the gods, the immortals; and so it did not stand firm for long” (12.8–9; see [[hecatomb]]). Its time is measured by the war:

{{quote:11409b89-db0d-524b-a020-d04e3b530c6a}}

After the sack, Poseidon and Apollo bring the rivers of Ida against it, and Zeus rains, “so that he might the sooner make the walls float out to sea” (12.26); Poseidon sends out on the waves its foundations “of logs and stones that the Achaeans had laid with toil” (12.29), and covers the beach again with sand (12.17–33; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]], [[poseidon]] and [[apollo]]). ἁλίπλοος, “floating on the sea”, is found only here. In the poem the wall outlasts its defeat. The Trojans break through it in this book and Apollo throws part of it down in Book 15 (15.355–366), yet it still has its towers, its ditch and its barred gates when Hermes brings Priam through them (24.443–446). Its end comes only after the sack.

**The ditch.** In Book 8 Hector boasted that the horses would easily leap the ditch (8.179). Now they stand neighing at its edge (12.50–52). It is wide, “not to be leapt across from close at hand, nor crossed with ease”; “overhanging banks” stand all along it on both sides, and along the top are sharp stakes, “close-set and great, a defense against enemy men”, planted by the sons of the Achaeans (12.53–57; the stakes of 7.441). Polydamas reads the ground. The stakes stand in the ditch and the wall is close behind them, so horsemen have no room to get down and fight; and if the Achaeans turned, the Trojans would “plunge into the ditch they have dug”, and no one would get back to the city (12.62–74; see [[polydamas]]). The Trojans leave their horses at the ditch in the verses used of the Achaeans that morning (12.84–85 = 11.47–48). In Book 15 it is the Achaeans who plunge into the ditch and the stakes as they flee (τάφρῳ καὶ σκολόπεσσιν ἐνιπλήξαντες, 15.344), the fate Polydamas feared for the Trojans (ἐνιπλήξωμεν, 12.72).

**Gates and towers.** The wall has several gates (12.175), and by the time the Lycians attack, “all of them had been shut” (12.340); ἐπώχατο, found only here, is taken as “had been shut”, though some read “were assailed” (see the translation's note on 12.340). The one on the left of the ships, where the chariots came back from the plain, stands open for fugitives when Asius drives at it (12.118–123), and the one Hector breaks has double doors with two bars and a bolt (12.453–456; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). The towers are framed in wood, “the timbers of the towers” that ring under the missiles (12.36), and from them the defenders throw stones (12.154–155). The two Ajaxes range along them (12.265). One belongs to Menestheus, and Sarpedon brings the Lycians against it (12.331–333, 12.373–377; see [[menestheus]]); from its height Epicles falls “like a diver” (12.385–386). πύργος can also stand for the whole towered wall: Menestheus looks “along the rampart of the Achaeans” for help (ἀνὰ πύργον Ἀχαιῶν, 12.333). In his own aside the poet says that “a wondrous fire had risen around the wall of stone” (12.177–178). λάϊνον, “of stone”, may go with the wall or with the fire, a storm of stones (see the translation's note on 12.175–181).

**The battlements.** The Trojans tear at the copings (κρόσσαι), throw down the battlements (ἐπάλξεις) and pry out the “jutting buttresses, which the Achaeans had set first in the ground to be the props of the towers”, while the Danaans fence the battlements with oxhides and shoot from behind them (12.258–264; see [[battlements]]). Sarpedon comes “to rush at the wall and break through the battlements” (12.308). He pulls one away, and “the wall was laid bare, and he made a path for many” (12.397–399; see [[sarpedon]]). Yet neither side can force the path or close it (12.417–420). The battlements part the armies as a boundary parts two men in a common field (12.421–424; see [[the-boundary-dispute]]), and towers and battlements are spattered with blood (12.430–431).

**The breach.** Zeus gives the higher glory to Hector, “who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.438). The same words are said of Sarpedon when he lies dead (16.558). The Trojans climb onto the copings (12.444), Hector breaks the gate with a stone, and “some climbed over the wall, and others poured in through the well-built gates themselves” (12.469–470; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]] and [[hector]]). In the next book they are the men “who have climbed over the great wall in a throng” (13.50, 13.87), and in Book 14 Nestor comes out of his hut to find “the wall of the Achaeans thrown down” (14.13–15).
