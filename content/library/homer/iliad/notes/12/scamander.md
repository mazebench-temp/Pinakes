δῖός τε Σκάμανδρος, “and brilliant Scamander” (12.21; see [[brilliant]]). The river along whose banks so many of the poem's dead have fallen (7.329–330, 11.497–499) is one of the eight that bring the Achaean wall to nothing, and with the Simoeis it closes the list: the rivers of the battlefield come last (12.17–33; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[simoeis]]). Hesiod's catalogue of the rivers that Tethys bore to Ocean also ends with the Scamander, θεῖόν τε Σκάμανδρον, “and divine Scamander” (*Theogony* 345; see [[hesiod]]). [[strabo|Strabo]], reporting Demetrius of Scepsis, says that the Scamander rises on Cotylus, a height of Ida, from a single spring and runs west, while the Granicus and the Aesepus, rising close by, run north to the Propontis (13.1.43; see [[granicus]] and [[aesepus]]).

The Xanthus by whose banks Sarpedon and Glaucus hold their domain (Ξάνθοιο παρʼ ὄχθας, 12.313) is the river of Lycia, not the Trojan river that the gods call Xanthus (see [[xanthus-of-lycia]]).
