---
summary: Polydamas twice comes to Hector's side at the ditch. His first counsel, to leave the chariots and attack the wall on foot, pleases Hector and is obeyed by all but Asius; his second, reading the eagle that drops a serpent as a sign that the Trojans will not come back from the ships in good order, brings Hector's rebuke and a threat on his life.

passages:
  - 12.80-109 | The Trojans obey Polydamas
---

**The first counsel.** “Then Polydamas came and stood beside bold Hector and said” (12.60): the verse returns at 12.210, and nearly the same at 13.725 (see [[bold]]). He speaks to “Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and the allies” (12.61). The ditch is hard to cross, with its sharp stakes and the wall close behind them, and there is no room for horsemen to get down and fight (12.62–66). If Zeus means to destroy the Achaeans utterly, he would wish it now, that they perish here “nameless, far from Argos”; but if they turn and a counterattack comes from the ships, and the Trojans are caught in the ditch, “not even a messenger will get back / to the city” (12.67–74; see the translation's note). His plan:

{{quote:924074cf-a993-5ed8-aa57-ae95d70e0295}}

It is the order the Achaeans took at dawn. Their charioteers held the horses “in good order there at the ditch” while the men went forward on foot in their armor (11.47–49), and the same verses now tell of the Trojans (12.77, 12.84–85; see [[battle-order]] and [[chariot]]). “Let us all do as I say” is the verse with which Agamemnon puts his proposals to the army (2.139, 9.26); in Book 18 Hector will use it to overrule Polydamas (18.297).

**Obeyed.** “So spoke Polydamas, and his safe counsel pleased Hector” (12.80): μῦθος ἀπήμων, counsel that brings no harm (see the translation's note). Hector leaps down, and the rest after him (12.81–83). Polydamas leads the first company with Hector and Cebriones, “noble Polydamas” (ἀμύμων, 12.88; see [[blameless]] and [[cebriones]]), and “the other Trojans and their far-famed allies / obeyed the counsel of blameless Polydamas” (12.108–109). ἀμώμητος, “blameless”, occurs only here in Homer. Only Asius will not leave his horses, and the narrator calls him a fool (12.110–117; see [[asius]] and [[boule]]).

**The second counsel.** At the ditch, while his men are eager to cross, the eagle comes on the left and drops the serpent among them (12.195–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). He comes to Hector again (12.210) and begins with a grievance:

{{quote:db02b4bd-dab5-538d-9097-6984733eda2b}}

δῆμον ἐόντα, “a man of the people”, is literally “being the δῆμος”: the word for the people as a whole is used of a single man only here (see the translation's note and [[demos]]). Polydamas is no commoner. His father Panthous sits among the elders with Priam (3.146; see [[panthous]] and [[trojan-elders]]), and Book 18 makes him Hector's companion, born on the same night (18.251). The complaint is bitter, and the poem has shown no such rebuke: Hector took his first counsel at once (12.80). Then he reads the sign. The eagle let its prey go before it reached its nest, and never brought it to its young (12.217–222):

{{quote:116458fc-6db9-5c18-ba33-3dce7509e248}}

He claims no gift of his own; he says how a θεοπρόπος, “a prophet”, would read it (see [[prophecy]], [[teras]] and [[divination]]). The Trojans' best bird-seer, Helenus, is leading another company (12.94; see [[helenus]] and [[best-of-bird-seers]]).

**Hector's answer.** Hector looks at him darkly, answers in the words with which Paris put off Antenor (12.231–234 = 7.357–360), sets the counsels of Zeus against “long-winged birds”, and calls him a coward who has no fear of dying because he will not fight; if he holds back, or turns another man from the war, Hector's spear will kill him (12.230–250; see [[hector]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]). Polydamas does not answer, and the book does not name him again.

**Borne out.** Both warnings come true in Book 16. When Patroclus drives the Trojans from the ships, “they crossed back not in good order”: Hector's horses carry him away, the ditch holds back the Trojan people, and many chariots are left broken in it (16.366–371). Hector heeds Polydamas once more at the ships, where the verses of 12.80–81 return (13.723–753; 13.748–749). After Achilles' return he rejects him with the same two verses of introduction and reply as here (18.284–285 ≈ 12.230–231), and before the gates in Book 22 he remembers that refusal (22.99–103).
