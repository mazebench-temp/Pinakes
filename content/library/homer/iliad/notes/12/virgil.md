---
summary: The Aeneid returns to Book 12 again and again. Aeneas in the storm wishes he had died where Sarpedon lies and the Simois rolls the shields and helmets of the dead; Venus shows him Neptune shaking the foundations of Troy with his trident; Pandarus and Bitias hold an open gate like the two Lapiths; and Homer's eagle with a serpent in its talons becomes a simile in the eleventh book and a false omen in the twelfth.
---

- **The Simois.** In the storm that opens the Aeneid, Aeneas wishes he had died before Troy, where huge Sarpedon lies, and “where the Simois seizes and rolls beneath its waves so many shields and helmets of men and brave bodies” (*Aeneid* 1.99–101). The shields, the helmets and the men are the three things that Book 12 says fell by the Simoeis: “where many oxhide shields and helmets / had fallen in the dust, and the race of men half-divine” (12.22–23; see [[simoeis]] and [[hemitheos]]).
- **Neptune's trident.** On the night of the sack Venus takes the cloud from Aeneas' eyes and shows him the gods at work: where he sees blocks torn apart and stones wrenched from stones, Neptune shakes the walls and the foundations, heaved up by his great trident, and tears the whole city from its seat (*Aeneid* 2.604–612). In Book 12 it is the Achaean wall whose foundations Poseidon, “holding his trident in his hands”, sends out on the waves (12.27–29), and Theodore Buckley's notes to Pope's Iliad set Dryden's version of Virgil's lines beside the passage. Homer's Poseidon says that he built the wall of Troy for Laomedon, with Apollo in Book 7 and alone in Book 21 (7.452–453, 21.446–447; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]).
- Pandarus and Bitias at the open gate, like twin oaks, and Turnus shut inside (*Aeneid* 9.672–818): [[virgil-pandarus-and-bitias]].
- **The eagle and the snake.** When the Etruscan Tarchon snatches Venulus from his horse and carries him off, Virgil compares him to a tawny eagle that carries off a snake high in the air, its feet entwined and its talons fixed, while the wounded serpent writhes its coils, raises its bristling scales and hisses, rearing up, and the eagle presses it no less with its hooked beak and beats the air with its wings (*Aeneid* 11.751–758). The simile recalls the eagle of Book 12, flying high with a serpent “alive and still struggling” in its talons (12.200–207; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). Homer's eagle is bitten and lets its prey fall among the enemy; Virgil's keeps it, as Tarchon keeps his.
- **The false omen.** In the last book the nymph Juturna sends a sign to break the truce. Jupiter's tawny bird, harrying the shore birds, swoops and seizes a splendid swan, until the other birds turn on it in a cloud and force it to drop its prey into the river and flee. The augur Tolumnius hails the augury as the one he has often prayed for, calls on the Rutulians to fight, and hurls the spear that breaks the truce (*Aeneid* 12.244–269; see [[virgil-duel-for-lavinia]]). As at the Achaean ditch, an eagle loses its prey and a reader of birds declares what it means. But Juturna's sign, the narrator says, deceived the Italians, and the augur who trusts it is wrong, where Hector, who scorns the birds, scorns a true sign (12.237–243; see [[divination]]).
