---
summary: The gods take no part in the fight at the wall. The narrator says that the wall was built against their will and tells how three of them will destroy it after the war; in the battle the gods who help the Danaans can only grieve, and when Hector leaps through the gate no one but the gods could have stopped him.
refs: [12.6, 12.8-9, 12.179-180, 12.234, 12.242, 12.466]
passages:
  - 12.175-181 | The gods who help the Danaans grieve
---

**Against their will.** The narrator lays the wall's short life to the gods. The Achaeans built it, “and they did not give glorious hecatombs to the gods” (12.6), the complaint Poseidon made in Book 7 in the same Greek (7.450). So “It was built against the will of the gods, / the immortals; and so it did not stand firm for long” (12.8–9; see [[hecatomb]], [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[immortals]]). In Book 7 the gods sat beside Zeus and gazed at the work (7.443–444). After the war three of them undo it: Poseidon and Apollo with the rivers, and Zeus with his rain (12.17–33; see [[poseidon]], [[apollo]], [[zeus]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

**Like a god.** In the middle of the battle the narrator speaks in his own voice: “it is hard for me to tell all these things like a god” (12.176). The fighting goes on at every gate at once (12.175), and only a god could tell it all, as the Muses, whom he asks for the Catalogue, “are present, and know all things” (2.485; see [[muses]] and [[invocation-of-the-muses]]).

**The helpers of the Danaans.** Then he looks to the gods themselves: “the gods were grieved at heart, / all who were the helpers of the Danaans in battle” (12.179–180). They are the party Hera spoke of, “all of us who help the Danaans” (8.205), and in the battle of the gods they will be Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes and Hephaestus (20.33–37). Zeus' ban keeps them out of the war (8.10–12). In Book 11 they sat in their halls and blamed him (11.75–79); here they grieve. In the next book Ares and “the other immortal gods” are still sitting on Olympus, “shut out from the war” (13.523–525; see [[ares]]). ἐπιτάρροθος, “helper”, is Athena's word for herself beside Diomedes: “such a helper was I to him” (5.808; see [[epitarrhothos]]).

**In men's mouths.** Hector tells Polydamas that “the gods themselves have destroyed your wits” (12.234), the verse with which Paris answered Antenor (7.360), and calls Zeus the one “who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.242; compare 2.669). Sarpedon says that in Lycia “all look on us as gods” (12.312), and that if he and Glaucus could be “ageless and immortal forever”, he would not fight (12.322–325; see [[sarpedons-speech]], [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**Except the gods.** When Hector breaks the gate, “no one could have held him back, meeting him, / except the gods” (12.465–466; see [[hector]]). No god comes yet. In the next book Zeus turns his eyes away from Troy, and Poseidon goes among the Achaeans in secret; he will not help them openly, because Zeus is the elder and knows more (13.1–45, 13.351–357; see [[poseidon]]).
