---
summary: The boars of Book 12 are in two similes of the fight at the wall. Hector goes through the throng like a boar or a lion that turns among hounds and hunters (12.41–50), and the two Lapiths fight in front of the gates like two wild boars that await the din of men and dogs in the mountains (12.145–153).
grc: [κάπριος, σύεσσιν]
passages:
  - 12.41-50 | A boar or a lion
  - 12.145-153 | Two wild boars
---

**Hector.** κάπριος ἠὲ λέων, “a boar or a lion” (12.42): Hector among the Achaeans is the beast at bay that turns on the hunters, never fears or flees, “and it is his pride that kills him” (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). The pair of beasts was the quarry in Hector's own similes of Books 8 and 11. He drove the Achaeans like a hound that catches at “a wild boar or a lion” from behind (8.338), and earlier on this same day he set the Trojans on them as a hunter sets his hounds on a wild boar or a lion (11.292–295). At the wall the hunter has become the quarry.

**The Lapiths.** The two Lapiths dart out from the gate ἀγροτέροισι σύεσσιν ἐοικότε, “like two wild boars” (12.146), that await the din of men and dogs in the mountains, dart sideways and break the wood around them, “and a gnashing of tusks goes on, until someone hits them and takes their life” (12.146–150; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]). The gnashing of tusks is the half-line of Odysseus' boar at bay in Book 11 (11.417; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]). Both similes give the boar its usual part in the Iliad, the beast that stands and fights (see [[like-a-boar]]), and both put it beside the hunters' dogs (see [[dogs]]).
