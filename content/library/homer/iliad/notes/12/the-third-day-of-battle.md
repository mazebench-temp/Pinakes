---
summary: Book 12 is the second stage of the day, from the ditch to the breach. The Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch and storm the wall on foot, the battle hangs level until Zeus gives Hector the higher glory, and the book ends with Hector through the gate and the Danaans in flight among the ships.
---

**Without a break.** Book 12 has no dawn and marks no hour. It takes up the day where Book 11 left it, with Patroclus tending Eurypylus in his hut (12.1–2; 11.842–848), “and they fought on, the Argives and Trojans, in a mass” (12.2–3). Patroclus stays there, out of the poem's sight, while the wall is stormed. The poem comes back to him only in Book 15, and with the words used here of the Lapiths at the gate: he leaves when he sees the Trojans rushing at the wall and the Danaans in rout (15.395–396 = 12.143–144, with the verb in the singular; see [[patroclus]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

**Zeus' promise.** From Ida Zeus sent Hector his promise: “I will put power in his hands to kill, until he comes to the well-benched ships, and the sun goes down” (11.192–194). Book 12 brings him to the wall and through it. Zeus' heart “wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.174), his wind from Ida “granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (12.255), and the level battle ends when “Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector” (12.437; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Hector holds to the promise against the bird-sign: Polydamas, he says, would have him forget “loud-thundering Zeus' counsels, which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to” (12.235–236; see [[hector]]). At the end of the book he calls on the Trojans to “throw the wondrous fire on the ships” (12.441). He will lay hold of a ship's stern only in Book 15 (15.704–705), and the first ship burns in Book 16 (16.122–124).

**The level battle.** Before the gate breaks, the fighting at the wall stands even: “so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight” (12.436). Earlier in the day Zeus himself “stretched the battle evenly”, looking down from Ida (11.336–337), and the verse of 12.436 returns when the Achaeans hold the Trojans at the ships (15.413). Here the balance is the scales of an honest working woman, weighing wool for her children's meager wage (12.433–435; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]).
