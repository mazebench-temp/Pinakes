summary: The fight at the wall is fought with stones. The defenders hurl them from the towers as thick as snow, Ajax kills Epicles with a boulder that lay by the battlement, and Hector breaks the gate with a stone that two of the best men of today could not have levered onto a wagon (12.154–161, 12.287–289, 12.378–386, 12.445–462).

**From the towers.** The Lapiths fight in front of the gate “trusting in the men above them” (12.153), and these, “with stones, from the well-built towers / kept shooting, defending themselves and the huts” (12.154–155). The stones fall like snowflakes driven by the wind, “so the missiles poured from their hands, from the Achaeans / and from the Trojans too”, and helmets and bossed shields ring dry “as men were struck with stones like millstones” (12.156–161; see [[a-snow-of-stones]] and [[arrows]]). μυλάκεσσι occurs only here (see the translation's note on 12.160–161). After the two Ajaxes have roused the defenders, the stones fly from both sides as thick as the snow when Zeus covers the mountains and fields, “and over the whole wall a thudding rose” (12.278–289; see [[the-great-snowfall]]).

**Ajax's boulder.** Telamonian Ajax kills Sarpedon's companion Epicles at Menestheus' tower:

{{quote:5607d46e-b39a-5ebe-8aa6-793d3996545a}}

μάρμαρος is a gleaming boulder, not marble in the later sense, and ὀκριόεις, “jagged”, is the stone of Peirous and of Hector's blow at Teucer (4.518, 8.327; see [[jagged]] and the translation's note on 12.380–383). The boulder lay ready inside the wall, “uppermost beside the battlement” (12.381; see [[battlements]]). It is measured against the men of the listeners' day: a man could not easily hold it in both hands, “not even one in his prime, / such as mortals are now” (12.382–383; see [[men-of-today]] and [[epicles]]).

**Hector's stone.** At the gate the measure is doubled:

{{quote:4c1df323-eb59-5e63-b5a0-ffff21194647}}

Two men, “the best of the people”, could not lever it onto a wagon (see [[demos]]). The verse that says he wielded it easily alone is the verse of Diomedes' stone (12.449 = 5.304), and yet the next verse gives the lightness to Zeus (see the translation's note on 12.450). He carries it as a shepherd carries a ram's fleece in one hand (12.451–452; see [[the-shepherd-and-the-fleece]]), plants his feet apart “so that his cast might not be weaker”, hits the doors in the middle and breaks both hinges; “the stone fell inside / with its weight”, and the doors split apart “under the rush of the stone” (12.453–462; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]] and [[hector]]). In Book 7 Ajax's stone laid Hector on his back, and in Book 8 Hector's stone felled Teucer (7.268–272, 8.321–329); here a stone breaks the wall open.
