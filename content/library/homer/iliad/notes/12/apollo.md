summary: Apollo has no part in the fight at the wall. He is named only in the narrator's account of the wall's end, where after the war he turns the mouths of the rivers of Ida together and for nine days sends their stream against it.
refs: [12.18, 12.25]
passages:
  - 12.17-34 | Poseidon and Apollo destroy the wall
---

**The rivers.** With Poseidon he plans the wall's destruction (12.17–18), and the work with the rivers is his:

{{quote:6f942593-7512-5eaa-bc88-759743c2e54a}}

The rivers are the eight that run from Ida to the sea, from the Rhesus to the Simoeis (12.19–22; see [[rhesus-river]], [[scamander]], [[simoeis]] and [[ida]]). Poseidon leads the waves and Zeus sends the rain (see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]). ἐννῆμαρ, “for nine days”, is the span of Apollo's first work in the poem: “For nine days the arrows of the god ranged through the army” (1.53; see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

**Builder and destroyer.** Poseidon names Apollo as his partner in building the wall of Troy for Laomedon (7.452–453); in Book 21 Apollo herds the king's cattle while Poseidon builds (21.446–449; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). Apollo destroys the Achaean wall twice. After the war he does it with the rivers. In the war itself Zeus sends him to Hector with the aegis (15.220–235). He promises to make the whole way smooth for the horses (πᾶσαν λειανέω, 15.260–261), as Poseidon will make all smooth beside the Hellespont (λεῖα δʼ ἐποίησεν, 12.30). Then he tramples the banks of the ditch into a causeway and throws the wall down “very easily”, as a boy by the sea scatters the sand he has heaped up in play (15.355–366; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Sand is in both pictures: the great beach covered again with sand (12.31), and the boy's heap of sand (ψάμαθον, 15.362). On the wall of Troy he does the opposite: standing on a tower, he thrusts Patroclus back three times from its angle (16.700–703).

**Not in the battle.** Apollo is the Trojans' god, but he takes no part in Book 12, for Zeus' ban still holds (8.10–12). The wound that Teucer gives Glaucus in the bared arm (12.387–391) is one he will heal. In Book 16, with Sarpedon dead, Glaucus prays to him, and the god stops the pain, dries the blood and puts might in his heart (16.508–531; see [[glaucus]]). The prayer recalls this book. Teucer hit him “as he rushed at the high wall, warding off disaster from his companions” (16.511–512), in the words of Teucer's shot (12.388) and of Menestheus' search for a leader who might “ward off disaster from his companions” (12.334).
