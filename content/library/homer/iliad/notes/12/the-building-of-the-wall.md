---
summary: The end that Zeus granted Poseidon in Book 7 is told at the start of Book 12. When Troy has fallen and the Achaeans have gone home, Poseidon and Apollo turn the rivers of Ida against the wall, Zeus rains, and Poseidon sweeps it into the sea and covers the beach with sand (12.3–34).
passages:
  - 12.3-34 | The destruction of the wall
---

**Zeus' word kept.** In Book 7 Zeus told Poseidon what he might do when the Achaeans “are gone with their ships to their dear native land”: “break down the wall and sweep it all into the sea, and cover the great beach over again with sand, so that the great wall of the Achaeans may be brought to nothing” (7.459–463). The narrator tells it done, in Zeus' words:

{{quote:a4fae4eb-149e-53ed-9dfc-4eaecf4e9666}}

The Argives “had gone in their ships to their dear native land” (12.16; 7.460). Poseidon sends the foundations “out on the waves” (12.28), where Zeus said “into the sea” (7.461). He “covered the great beach over again with sand” (12.31), the verse of 7.462 with the verb changed from command to deed. And the wall is “brought to nothing”, ἀμαλδύνω, the verb of the last line of Zeus' answer (7.463), found in Homer only there and twice in this passage (12.18, 12.32). The grievance is Poseidon's own, in his own verse: when they built the wall, “they did not give glorious hecatombs to the gods” (12.6 = 7.450; see [[poseidon]] and [[hecatomb]]).

**What Book 12 adds.** Zeus spoke to Poseidon alone. In Book 12 two gods “took counsel” (12.17). Apollo, whom Poseidon named as his fellow builder of Laomedon's wall (7.452–453), turns the rivers of Ida against it for nine days; Zeus rains without ceasing; and Poseidon, trident in hand, leads (12.17–29; see [[apollo]], [[zeus]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). The rivers and the rain are not in Zeus' words, and with them comes the list of the eight rivers (12.19–22; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Poseidon feared that the fame of the Achaean wall would reach “as far as the dawn spreads” and that men would forget his own (7.451–453). When he has done, he has “made all smooth beside the strong-flowing Hellespont” (12.30; see [[hellespont]]), and there is nothing left to be famous.

**In time to come.** “So Poseidon and Apollo were to do in time to come” (ὄπισθε, 12.34). Inside that frame the narrator tells the death of the best of the Trojans, the sack of Troy “in the tenth year” and the Argives' homecoming as things done (12.13–16), and then turns back to the battle that is going on “now” (τότε, 12.35). From that later time the men who fell by the Simoeis are “the race of men half-divine” (12.23; see [[hemitheos]] and [[simoeis]]).
