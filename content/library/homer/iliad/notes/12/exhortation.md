**At the ditch.** The book's first exhortation has no words. Hector goes through the throng and “begged his companions, / urging them to cross the ditch” (12.49–50), and the horses will not go (see the translation's note on 12.49, and [[chariot]]).

**The two Ajaxes.** While the Trojans tear at the battlements, the two Ajaxes range along the towers “calling out orders”, “urging on the might of the Achaeans” (κελευτιόωντʼ, μένος ὀτρύνοντες, 12.265–266; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). They use both kinds of speech: “One with soothing words, another with harsh words / they rebuked, whomever they saw holding back from the battle altogether” (12.267–268; see [[soothing-words]] and [[neikos]]). Their speech is given once, for both:

The address takes in every rank, the outstanding, the middling and the worse (μεσήεις, “middling”, occurs only here; see [[not-all-alike-in-war]]). The reasons are few: there is work for all, they know it themselves, and Zeus may grant them to drive the enemy back to the city. The enemy is ὁμοκλητήρ, “the man who shouts at us”, Hector, whose shouting must turn no one back to the ships (12.273; see the translation's note). “So the two, shouting out in front, urged on the battle of the Achaeans” (12.277). When Telamonian Ajax leaves for Menestheus' tower, he gives the task to the son of Oileus and Lycomedes: “stand and urge the Danaans to fight with all their might” (12.366–367; see [[lycomedes]]).

**Sarpedon.** His speech to Glaucus gives the reason from honor its fullest form: the kings of Lycia are honored at the feast and with land, and must earn it among the foremost; and since no mortal can escape the fates of death, “let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (12.310–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]], [[feast]], [[ker-doom]] and [[euchomai]]). It is an exhortation in the first person plural, spoken to himself as much as to his friend. When the Lycians hang back at the battlement he calls on them in four verses: the address, a rebuke in the formula of the exhortation, “why do you let go of your furious courage like this?”, the reason, that he cannot break through alone, and the order with a maxim, “come on with me; the work of more men is better” (12.409–412; see [[furious]] and [[the-work-of-many]]). “They, fearing their lord's rebuke, / bore down the harder” (12.413–414; see [[so-he-spoke]]).

**Hector.** Hector's own exhortation in the book begins as a threat. If Polydamas holds back from the fighting or turns another man from the war, “at once, struck by my spear, you will lose your life” (12.248–250; see [[polydamas]]), as Agamemnon threatened the laggard with the dogs and birds before the first battle (2.391–393). When Zeus gives him the glory, “he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Trojans” (12.439), the verse in which Agamemnon called to the Danaans (8.227, 11.275), with his own people in the place of theirs: “Rise up, horse-taming Trojans, break through the wall / of the Argives, and throw the wondrous fire on the ships” (12.440–441; see [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[ships]]). The Trojans “all heard him with their ears” and rushed at the wall in a body (12.442–443), and once he is through the gate he calls on them, “wheeling around through the throng, / to climb over the wall; and they obeyed his urging” (12.467–468). Menestheus, on the other side, could make no one hear him over the din (12.337–338; see [[heralds]]).
