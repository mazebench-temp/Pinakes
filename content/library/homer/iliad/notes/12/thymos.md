---
summary: In Book 12 the θυμός wishes, grieves, knows and sends men on. Zeus' heart wishes to give glory to Hector, the gods grieve at heart, a prophet knows portents “clearly in his heart”, Sarpedon's spirit sends him at the wall like a lion's proud spirit, and his heart, hoping for glory, keeps him there; θυμός is also the life that leaves Epicles' bones.
---

**The heart of Zeus and the gods.** When Asius blames Zeus, the narrator answers that Zeus' θυμός “wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.174; see [[kydos]] and [[zeus]]). The gods who help the Danaans “were grieved at heart” (ἀκαχήατο θυμόν, 12.179; see [[achos]]).

**Knowing.** Polydamas would have his reading of the eagle taken as a prophet's, “one who clearly in his heart / knew of portents” (σάφα θυμῷ εἰδείη, 12.228–229; see [[theopropion]] and [[teras]]).

**The spirit that sends.** In the simile the mountain lion's “proud spirit bids him / make an attempt on the sheep” (θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, 12.300–301), and the man follows the lion: “so then his spirit sent godlike Sarpedon / to rush at the wall” (12.307–308; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]] and [[sarpedon]]). When Ajax drives him back from the battlement, his θυμός holds him: “he did not altogether / draw back, since his heart hoped to win glory” (12.406–407).

**Life.** θυμός is also the life a man has and loses. The hunter in the Lapiths' simile “takes their life” from the boars (12.150; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]); Hector threatens Polydamas that “struck by my spear, you will lose your life” (12.250); and Epicles, hit by Ajax's boulder, “like a diver, / fell from the high tower, and his spirit left his bones” (12.385–386; see [[epicles]] and [[death-in-battle]]). The verse returns when Cebriones falls from his chariot (16.742–743), and the Odyssey has it of a helmsman struck by a falling mast (*Odyssey* 12.413–414).
