---
summary: Sheep in Book 12 are the prey of Sarpedon's lion, which goes for the sheep in a fold guarded by herdsmen and dogs (12.299–306), and the fat sheep that the kings of Lycia eat (12.319); the ram's fleece in Hector's simile is a sheep's too (12.451).
grc: ["μήλων πειρήσοντα", "πίονα μῆλα"]
---

μῆλα are the small livestock, sheep and goats together. Sarpedon's lion is bidden μήλων πειρήσοντα, “make an attempt on the sheep”, in their close-built fold, where herdsmen are “keeping watch over the sheep” with dogs and spears (περὶ μῆλα, 12.301–303; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). A few verses later the sheep are the kings' food. A Lycian is to say that the kings who rule over Lycia “eat fat sheep and choice wine, honey-sweet”, but that there is strength in them too (12.318–321; see [[sarpedons-speech]]). The lion that goes hungry into the fold and the king who eats fat sheep at home are the same man.

Sheep give two more measures at the end of the book. Hector carries the stone against the gate as easily as a shepherd carries a ram's fleece in one hand (12.451–452; see [[the-shepherd-and-the-fleece]] and [[ram]]), and the battle hangs as level as the scales of a working woman weighing wool (εἴριον, 12.434; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]).
