summary: Hector takes Polydamas' counsel to leave the chariots at the ditch and leads the first company on foot; when an eagle drops a serpent among his men, he rejects Polydamas' reading of the sign, trusts the promise of Zeus, and threatens to kill him. Zeus gives him the higher glory, and he breaks the gate with a stone and leaps through, “his face like swift night”.

**The wall's term.** Looking past the end of the poem, the narrator measures the wall by Hector's life and speaks of his death as past: “As long as Hector was alive and Achilles kept up his wrath, / and the city of lord Priam was unsacked, / so long the great wall of the Achaeans also stood firm” (12.10–12; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

**The beast at bay.** The Argives are penned in beside their ships “in fear of Hector, the mighty master of rout” (12.39), Diomedes' title in Book 6 (6.97, 6.278; see [[master-of-rout]]), “and he fought as before, like a storm wind” (12.40, as at 11.297; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]). He turns through the throng as a boar or a lion turns among hounds and hunters, “exulting in his strength”; they stand against him in close order like a tower and hurl their spears, but his heart never fears or flees, “and it is his pride that kills him” (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνων, “exulting in his strength”, was said of Hector himself in Books 8 and 9 (8.337, 9.237; see [[exulting-in-strength]]). In Book 8 he was the hound at the heels of a boar or a lion, and in Book 11 the hunter who sets his hounds on one (8.338–342, 11.292–295; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]] and [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). Now he is the beast. The word for its fatal pride, ἀγηνορίη, comes back only when Andromache fears that Achilles has ended “the grievous manliness that possessed him” (22.457–459).

**On foot.** He goes through the throng and begs his companions to cross the ditch (12.49–50; ἐλλίσσεθʼ, “begged”: see the translation's note), but his own horses neigh at the edge and will not go (12.50–52). When Polydamas advises leaving the chariots, “his safe counsel pleased Hector, / and at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor”, and the other Trojans leap down “when they saw brilliant Hector” (12.80–83; see [[polydamas]] and [[brilliant]]). He leads the first company with Polydamas and his charioteer Cebriones, “the most and the best” (12.89), as in the Catalogue (2.817); another, weaker man stays with the chariot (12.88–92; see [[cebriones]] and [[battle-order]]). While Asius fails at the left gate, Zeus pays him no heed, “for his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.173–174; see [[zeus]] and [[kydos]]).

**The omen.** When an eagle drops a serpent among his hesitating men, Polydamas reads it as a warning (12.195–229; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). Hector answers “looking at him darkly” (12.230; see [[looking-darkly]]), and his first four verses are those with which Paris refused Antenor's proposal to give Helen back (12.231–234 = 7.357–360; see [[paris]] and [[antenor]]). Then he turns to the sign:

{{quote:390f4e53-ec32-5e6c-b60e-3e6e82713603}}

Iris brought him the promise (11.200–209), and he read Zeus' nod in the thunder of Book 8 (8.175); Agamemnon had used the same words, ὑπέσχετο καὶ κατένευσε, of a promise of Zeus that proved a deceit (2.112, 9.19; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The last verse became a proverb (see [[one-omen-is-best]] and [[divination]]). He ends with a charge of cowardice, “for your heart is not staunch against the enemy, nor eager for battle”, and a threat: if Polydamas holds back, or turns another man from the war, “at once, struck by my spear, you will lose your life” (12.244–250).

**The gate.** He leads, and the Trojans follow “with a wondrous din”, while Zeus sends a storm of dust from Ida and grants “glory to the Trojans and Hector” (12.251–255). Even so they would not then have broken the gates and the long bar, the narrator says, had not Zeus roused Sarpedon (12.290–293; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[sarpedon]]). The fight hangs level “until Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector, / son of Priam, who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.437–438; see [[son-of-priam]]). He shouts to the Trojans to break through and “throw the wondrous fire on the ships” (12.440–441), the fire he promised in Book 8 (8.180–182). He snatches up a stone that two of the best men, “such as mortals are now”, could not heave onto a wagon, in the verse that measured the stones of Diomedes and Aeneas (12.445–449; 12.449 = 5.304 = 20.287; see [[men-of-today]] and [[stone-throwing]]). This time a god shares the feat: “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus made it light for him” (12.450). He carries it as easily as a shepherd carries a ram's fleece (12.451–452; see [[the-shepherd-and-the-fleece]]), plants his feet, hits the double doors in the middle and breaks off both hinges, and the doors split apart (12.453–462; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).

{{quote:1544ecda-2a1f-5e97-81b8-ea63107cef70}}

The night in his face recalls Apollo coming down from Olympus “like the night” to bring the plague (1.47; see [[like-night]] and [[eyes-like-fire]]). Hector wheels through the throng and calls the Trojans over the wall, and the Danaans flee among the ships (12.467–471).

**Afterward.** Poseidon tells the Achaeans that Hector “has broken the gates and the long bar” (13.124), nearly the words of 12.291, and Hector's next charge stops against the close ranks around the two Ajaxes (13.126–148). In this book the Trojans go up to the wall “trusting in his portents and in their own strength” (12.256); alone before the gates in Book 22, Hector fears that men will say, “Hector, trusting in his own strength, destroyed the people” (22.107; see [[polydamas]]).

His epithets in the book are θρασύς, “bold” (12.60, 12.210), δῖος, “brilliant” (12.83), κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (12.230), and φαίδιμος, “shining” (12.290, 12.462), and he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam”, at the breach (12.438; see [[bold]], [[brilliant]], [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]], [[phaidimos]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
