---
summary: Book 12 turns on two counsels of Polydamas. The first, to leave the chariots at the ditch, pleases Hector and is obeyed by all but Asius; the second, to turn back at the eagle's sign, Hector refuses, telling Polydamas to obey not birds but Zeus. Asius does not persuade Zeus; Glaucus, the herald and Ajax do not disobey; and at the end the Trojans obey Hector's call to climb the wall.

**The first counsel.** Polydamas proposes the crossing on foot with the line of the assembly speaker, “Come then, let us all do as I say” (πειθώμεθα πάντες, 12.75), the line of Agamemnon and of Diomedes before him (2.139, 9.26, 9.704). “His safe counsel pleased Hector” (12.80), and the Trojans and their allies “obeyed the counsel of blameless Polydamas” (12.108–109; see [[polydamas]] and [[boule]]). One man would not: Asius kept his horses and his chariot, and drove them at the gate (12.110–115; see [[asius]] and [[nepios]]).

**The second.** Polydamas' second counsel begins with a complaint about the ground of obedience: a man of the people may not speak against Hector, neither in council nor in war (12.211–214; see [[demos]]). He ends by wishing his reading had a prophet's authority, a prophet “whom the people would obey” (12.229; see [[theopropion]]). Hector's answer turns on the same verb: “and you tell me to obey long-winged birds … But let us obey the counsel of great Zeus” (πείθεσθαι, πειθώμεθα, 12.237–241; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). The question is whom to obey, a sign or a promise. In Book 18 Hector rejects Polydamas' last counsel with the line Polydamas used here, “let us all do as I say” (18.297).

**Persuading Zeus.** Asius complains to Zeus that the god has deceived him, “but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this” (12.173; see [[pseudos]] and [[zeus]]).

**Not disobeying.** The narrator marks prompt obedience with the usual formula. “Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey” Sarpedon (12.329); Thootes the herald “heard him and did not disobey” Menestheus (12.351); and Telamonian Ajax “did not disobey” the herald (12.364; see [[sarpedons-speech]] and [[thootes]]). The Lycians press on around Sarpedon “fearing their lord's rebuke” (12.413), and at the end the Trojans obey Hector: he called to them to climb over the wall, “and they obeyed his urging” (12.467–468; see [[hector]]).
