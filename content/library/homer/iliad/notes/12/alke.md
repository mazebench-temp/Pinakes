---
summary: Sarpedon, driven back from the battlement, wheels on the Lycians: “why do you let go of your furious courage like this?”
---

Teucer's arrow and Ajax's spear have driven Sarpedon back a little from the battlement he tore down, and he turns to his men: “Lycians, why do you let go of your furious courage like this?” (12.409; see [[sarpedon]] and [[lycians]]). μεθίετε θούριδος ἀλκῆς is the language of the leaders' rebukes. Agamemnon goes along the Argive ranks calling, “Argives, do not yet let go of your furious courage” (4.234), and in the next book Poseidon reproaches the Achaeans for letting it go (οὐκ ἔτι καλὰ μεθίετε θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 13.116). The other formula of the rally asks men to remember it: “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (6.112, 15.487; see the article above and [[furious]]). Sarpedon's rebuke ends in a maxim, “the work of more men is better” (12.412; see [[the-work-of-many]]), and the Lycians press on.
