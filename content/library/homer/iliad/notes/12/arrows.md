**Missiles.** βέλος is any missile (see the entry), and at the wall the missiles are stones. They fall from the towers like snowflakes, “so the missiles poured from their hands, from the Achaeans / and from the Trojans too” (βέλεα, 12.159–160; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[a-snow-of-stones]]), and Hector's stone at the gate is his βέλος: he stands with his legs well apart, “so that his cast might not be weaker” (12.458).

**The arrows of Zeus.** In the simile of the snowfall Zeus sets out “to snow, showing men these arrows of his” (πιφαυσκόμενος τὰ ἃ κῆλα, 12.280; see [[the-great-snowfall]]). κῆλα are Apollo's arrows of plague in Book 1 (1.53, 1.383); here the god's shafts are the snowflakes, or his weather (see the translation's note on 12.280, 12.286).

**Teucer's arrows.** Teucer shoots from the wall at Menestheus' tower:

{{quote:15fc586a-8ecb-5201-971c-4659a010ad88}}

The arrow takes Glaucus out of the fight, and Sarpedon grieves to see him go (12.392; see [[glaucus]] and [[achos]]). The wound returns in Book 16. When Sarpedon falls, Glaucus cannot help him, for his arm still pains him, “the wound which Teucer had struck with an arrow as he rushed at the high wall, / warding off disaster from his companions” (16.511–512); he prays to Apollo, and the god stops the pain and dries the blood (16.513–529). The phrase “ward off disaster from his companions” is the one in which the narrator told what help Menestheus was looking for along the wall (12.334; see the translation's note on 12.334): Teucer was that help. Teucer's next arrow hits “the shining strap around his chest” of Sarpedon's shield, but Zeus wards off the fates from his son (12.400–403; see [[shield]] and [[sarpedon]]).
