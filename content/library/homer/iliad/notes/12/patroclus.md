“So in the hut the valiant son of Menoetius / was tending wounded Eurypylus” (12.1–2). The first verse names him only by his father, in the title Book 11 gave him three times (Μενοιτίου ἄλκιμος υἱός, 11.605, 11.814, 11.837; see [[son-of-menoetius]] and [[menoetius]]); his own name does not occur in the book. The scene is the one on which Book 11 ended, Patroclus cutting the arrow from Eurypylus' thigh and laying on the bitter root (11.842–848; see [[eurypylus]] and [[healing]]). Then the narrator turns to the battle, and the fight at the wall is told while he sits there (see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

Book 15 comes back to the hut. As long as the Achaeans and Trojans fought around the wall, Patroclus sat with Eurypylus, cheering him with talk and laying drugs on the wound; but when he saw the Trojans rushing at the wall and the Danaans in rout, he groaned, struck his thighs, and ran to Achilles with Nestor's words, “Good is the persuasion of a companion” (15.390–404; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). The two verses of his seeing are the verses of the Lapiths at the gate (15.395–396 = 12.143–144), and the groan and the blow to the thighs are Asius' when the gate holds against him (15.397 ≈ 12.162).
