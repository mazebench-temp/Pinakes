**What Zeus wishes.** Asius, held at the gate by the two Lapiths, calls Zeus a lover of lies, “but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this”:

{{quote:1895e2eb-ae40-5262-bc2a-adcce8f53dcb}}

The verse is nearly the one with which the gods blamed Zeus in Book 11, “because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (ἐβούλετο κῦδος ὀρέξαι, 11.79; see the note on Book 11 and [[boule]]). κῦδος ὀρέξαι, “hold out glory”, is the phrase of a god's gift (see [[pseudos]] and [[zeus]]).

**Given in stages.** With the storm of wind he sends from Ida, carrying the dust against the ships, Zeus bewitches the minds of the Achaeans and “granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (12.252–255). The Lycians and the Danaans then fight to a standstill, the battle hanging as level as the scales of an honest working woman weighing wool (12.433–436; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]), “until Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector, / son of Priam, who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.437–438; see [[hector]]). κῦδος ὑπέρτερον, “the higher glory”, decides an even contest, as the golden scales of Zeus do (8.69–74; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]).

**Hoped for and won.** Glory is also what a man takes. Sarpedon, driven back a little from the battlement, “did not altogether / draw back, since his heart hoped to win glory” (κῦδος ἀρέσθαι, 12.406–407; see [[sarpedon]]). In his speech to Glaucus the battle is κυδιάνειρα, “where men win glory” (12.325; see [[where-men-win-glory]]), and his last word puts εὖχος, “triumph”, where κῦδος would stand: men hold it out to one another, and either side may give it (12.328; see [[sarpedons-speech]] and [[euchomai]]).

**A glorious heart.** In the simile with which the battle begins, Hector is like a boar or a lion among hounds and hunters: “his glorious heart / never fears or flees, and it is his pride that kills him” (κυδάλιμον κῆρ, 12.45–46; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]] and [[kydalimos]]).
