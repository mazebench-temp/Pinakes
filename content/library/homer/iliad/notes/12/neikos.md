passages:
  - 12.265-277 | The two Ajaxes along the towers

**Rebuke.** The two Ajaxes go along the towers and rebuke whomever they see holding back from the battle, “one with soothing words, another with harsh words” (12.267–268; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[not-all-alike-in-war]]). νείκεον is the verb of rebuke, and here it can be gentle. When Agamemnon went along the ranks in Book 4, the rebuke was kept for the slack: the eager he encouraged, and “those whom he saw holding back from hateful war, / these he would rebuke with angry words” (νεικείεσκε, 4.240–241; see [[exhortation]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). The Ajaxes rebuke every man who holds back, in whichever tone will move him.

**The quarrel.** Their call ends in a hope: that Zeus may let them “thrust back the quarrel and drive the enemy to the city” (νεῖκος ἀπωσαμένους, 12.276). The quarrel is the fighting itself, pushed back like an attacker. When the armies first met, Strife “cast the quarrel, common to all, into their midst” (4.443–444; see [[eris]] and [[strife]]).

**Toil, war, and quarrel.** Menestheus, with the Lycians at his tower, sends the herald Thootes to call both Ajaxes, or at least Telamonian Ajax, “if over there too toil and quarrel have arisen” (πόνος καὶ νεῖκος, 12.348). Thootes repeats the message almost word for word, but where Menestheus said “toil and quarrel” he says “war and quarrel” (πόλεμος καὶ νεῖκος, 12.361), and where Menestheus said “over there” (κεῖθι) he says “here” (ἐνθάδε), for he now speaks where the Ajaxes stand (see [[thootes]], [[message-repeated]] and [[ponos]]). Neither pair is found elsewhere. νεῖκος ὄρωρεν, “a quarrel has arisen”, is said elsewhere of the war itself, as when Hector proposes the duel of Paris, “for whose sake the quarrel arose” (3.87).
