summary: Sarpedon ends his speech to Glaucus with “let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us”; Glaucus, wounded, leaps back from the wall unseen, so that no Achaean “should see him hit and boast over him with words”. Neither word is a prayer.
passages:

  - 12.387-391 | Glaucus wounded
---

**Triumph.** εὖχος is the thing a victor can claim. In Book 11 Hector told the Trojans that “a great triumph has been given me / by Zeus” (11.288–289; see the note on Book 11). Sarpedon's triumph has two possible owners. Since no mortal can escape the fates of death, “let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (12.328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]). εὖχος ὀρέξαι is made like κῦδος ὀρέξαι, “hold out glory”, the phrase of a god's gift (12.174; see [[kydos]]); here men give it to one another, and the one who gives it is the man who falls. The half-verse is said again by Idomeneus to Meriones (13.327), and Hector, waiting for Achilles, gives the gift back to the god, wanting to see to which of the two of them the Olympian will hold out triumph (ὁπποτέρῳ κεν Ὀλύμπιος εὖχος ὀρέξῃ, 22.130).

**Boast.** Teucer's arrow hits Glaucus in the bared arm as he rushes at the wall, “and he leapt back from the wall unseen, so that none of the Achaeans / should see him hit and boast over him with words” (12.390–391; see [[glaucus]] and [[teucer]]). εὐχετάομαι is a longer form of εὔχομαι, and the boast is the one a man shouts over an enemy he has struck, as Paris over the wounded Diomedes (11.379–383; see the note on Book 11). To be seen hit is to give the enemy his εὖχος, and Glaucus would rather vanish than give it.

The two words are not prayers, and the [[prayer]] entry leaves both lines out (12.328, 12.391).
