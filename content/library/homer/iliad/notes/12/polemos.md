---
summary: Book 12 is a battle at a wall, and its words for war mark the stages of the day: “battle and cries blazed around / the well-built wall”; Hector asks Polydamas why he is afraid of “the war and the combat”; the two Ajaxes remind the Achaeans that men are not all alike “in war”; Sarpedon would not fight if escaping “this war” made him immortal; and in the end “their battle and war” are stretched as evenly as a balance.
grc: [μαχέσθην, μάχονται, μαχοίμην, πτόλεμος]
---

**Blazing.** The narrator turns from the wall's future to the day's fighting with a verse of fire: “but now battle and cries blazed around / the well-built wall” (12.35–36; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The image returns when he cannot tell the fighting at every gate, “for everywhere a wondrous fire had risen around the wall / of stone” (12.177–178; see the translation's note on 12.175–181), and in Sarpedon's “blazing battle” (μάχης καυστείρης, 12.316; see [[blazing]]).

**War and combat.** πόλεμος καὶ δηϊοτής, “the war and the combat”, is the pair for the fighting as a whole. The Lapiths join in it (12.181), and Hector throws it at Polydamas: “Why are you afraid of the war and the combat?” If Polydamas turns any other man away from the war, he will die by Hector's spear (12.244–250; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). The two Ajaxes call on all ranks, “since men are by no means all alike / in war” (12.270–271; see [[not-all-alike-in-war]]), and Menestheus sees them standing, “insatiable of war” (12.335; see [[insatiable]]).

**This war.** Sarpedon's speech sets the war against immortality: “if, once we had escaped this war, / we two were to be ageless and immortal forever”, he would not fight among the foremost (μαχοίμην, 12.322–324); but the Lycians must be able to say that their kings “fight among the foremost” (μάχονται, 12.321), and the battle is the place “where men win glory” (12.325; see [[sarpedons-speech]]).

**Toil and war.** The herald Thootes repeats Menestheus' message almost word for word, but what was “toil and quarrel” in Menestheus' mouth is “war and quarrel” in his (12.348, 12.361; see [[neikos]] and [[ponos]]).

**Stretched even.** At the battlements neither side can drive the other back, and the battle hangs like the scales of a working woman weighing wool: “so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight” (ἐπὶ ἶσα μάχη τέτατο πτόλεμός τε, 12.436 = 15.413; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]). The image is the one of 11.336, where “the son of Cronus stretched the battle evenly for them” (ἐτάνυσσε, from τανύω, a verb close to τείνω), and in the next book Zeus and Poseidon pull the rope of strife and war tight over both armies (13.358–360). Only Zeus' gift of glory to Hector breaks the balance (12.437; see [[kydos]]).
