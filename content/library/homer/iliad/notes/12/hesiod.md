---
summary: Hesiod's poems set Book 12 in a larger frame. The Theogony counts seven of its eight rivers of Ida among the children of Ocean and Tethys; the Works and Days names the race of heroes “demigods”, Homer's word at 12.23, and sends some of them to the Islands of the Blessed; the Catalogue of Women makes Sarpedon a son of Europa. The Works and Days ends by blessing the man who works “judging the birds”, where Hector will not heed them.
---

- **The rivers.** Poseidon and Apollo turn against the wall all the rivers that flow from Ida to the sea, “the Rhesus and Heptaporus, the Caresus and Rhodius, / the Granicus and Aesepus, and brilliant Scamander, / and the Simoeis” (12.20–22). Hesiod's catalogue of the rivers that Tethys bore to Ocean, among the Nile, the Alpheus, the Ister and the other great rivers of the world, has seven of the eight: the Rhesus (*Theogony* 340), the Rhodius and the Heptaporus (341), the Granicus, the Aesepus and “divine Simoeis” (342), and “divine Scamander” (345). Only the Caresus is missing. Hesiod's verse 342 has the shape of Homer's 12.21, Γρήνικόν τε καὶ Αἴσηπον θεῖόν τε Σιμοῦντα beside Γρήνικός τε καὶ Αἴσηπος δῖός τε Σκάμανδρος: the same two rivers in the same places of the verse, with θεῖος for δῖος and the Simoeis, in Hesiod's form Σιμοῦς, for the Scamander. The rivers of the Troad are named together only here in Homer, and how the two lists are related is not known. See [[rhesus-river]], [[heptaporus]], [[caresus]], [[rhodius]], [[granicus]], [[aesepus]], [[scamander]] and [[simoeis]].
- **The demigods.** By the Simoeis, the narrator says, fell shields and helmets “and the race of men half-divine” (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, 12.23), the only ἡμίθεος in Homer. In the *Works and Days* Zeus makes a fourth race, better and juster than the bronze, “the godlike race of hero-men, who are called demigods (ἡμίθεοι), the race before ours on the boundless earth”. War destroyed them, some at seven-gated Thebes fighting for the flocks of Oedipus, some at Troy, where ships had brought them over the sea for the sake of Helen; to some Zeus gave a life apart from men at the ends of the earth, and they live free of care in the Islands of the Blessed (156–173). Hesiod wishes that he had not been born among the fifth race, the men of iron (174–176), and the narrator of Book 12 twice measures the heroes against “such as mortals are now” (12.383, 12.449). See [[hemitheos]], [[men-of-today]] and [[heros]].
- **Sarpedon.** Zeus rouses “his own son, Sarpedon” against the Argives (12.292). In Homer his mother is Laodameia, Bellerophon's daughter (6.198–199). The ancient commentary on 12.292 tells the other story, how Zeus, in love with Europa, the daughter of Phoenix, turned himself into a bull breathing saffron, carried her to Crete and lay with her, and then gave her as wife to Asterion, king of the Cretans; she bore Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys, and “the story is in Hesiod and Bacchylides” (Hesiod, *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 19 Evelyn-White). A papyrus of the *Catalogue*, much damaged, continues the story of Europa's sons; as its editors restore it, it went on to Sarpedon's rule in Lycia and his coming to Troy (fr. 19A). See [[sarpedon]].
- **The Lapiths.** The two who hold the gate against Asius are “high-hearted sons of Lapith spearmen” (υἷας ὑπερθύμους Λαπιθάων αἰχμητάων, 12.128), the only time Homer uses the phrase. The *Shield of Heracles*, ascribed to Hesiod, has the same verse-end for the battle of the Lapiths and Centaurs on Heracles' shield, ἐν δʼ ἦν ὑσμίνη Λαπιθάων αἰχμητάων, where Caeneus, Dryas, Peirithous and their companions, worked in silver with golden armor, fight the Centaurs (*Shield* 178–190). See [[lapiths]] and [[lapiths-drive-out-the-centaurs]].
- **The language of Zeus.** Hector trusts the counsel of “great Zeus, / who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.241–242). The phrase is not found elsewhere in Homer; in the *Theogony* Zeus, trusting in the thunder and lightning that the Cyclopes gave him, “is lord over mortals and immortals” (θνητοῖσι καὶ ἀθανάτοισιν ἀνάσσει, 506), and Typhoeus would have ruled over both, had Zeus not been quick to see it (837–838). In the snow simile Zeus shows men “these arrows of his”, κῆλα (12.280); Hesiod calls the thunder, the lightning and the thunderbolt “the shafts of great Zeus”, κῆλα Διὸς μεγάλοιο (*Theogony* 707–708). And the verse with which the Argives strengthen their battalions inside the wall, Ἀργεῖοι δʼ ἑτέρωθεν ἐκαρτύναντο φάλαγγας (12.415, as 11.215), is the verse with which the Titans strengthen theirs for the last battle with the gods, Τιτῆνες δʼ ἑτέρωθεν ἐκαρτύναντο φάλαγγας (*Theogony* 676).
- **Judging the birds.** The *Works and Days* ends by calling happy the man who knows all these things and works, blameless before the immortals, “judging the birds” (ὄρνιθας κρίνων) and avoiding transgressions (826–828). Some added to these verses a *Divination by Birds*, which Apollonius of Rhodes rejected as not Hesiod's (Proclus on *Works and Days* 828). Hector at the ditch will not heed long-winged birds, whether they fly to the right or the left (12.237–240; see [[divination]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]).
