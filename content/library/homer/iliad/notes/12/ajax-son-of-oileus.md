**One of the pair.** For most of the book he is present only in the dual. He is one of the two Ajaxes who range along the towers and rouse the Achaeans with soothing and harsh words (12.265–277; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]), one of the pair whom Menestheus sees “insatiable of war” and asks for, “both of them, rather” (12.335, 12.344), and one of the “Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives” whom the herald Thootes addresses (12.354; see [[thootes]]).

**Left behind.** Menestheus will take Telamonian Ajax alone if both cannot come, and the great Ajax obeys (12.349, 12.364). Then:

{{quote:aacaa6e6-5d8b-55bd-8eb8-6bbe58d2f206}}

Ὀϊλιάδης, “son of Oileus”, appears here for the first time, and in Homer it always names this Ajax (13.203, 13.712, 14.446, 16.330, 23.759; see [[oileus]]). Until now the poem has named him by his father only in the phrase “swift Ajax, son of Oileus” (2.527; see [[swift]]). Telamonian Ajax calls him by the name they share, Αἶαν, as the son of Oileus calls the son of Telamon in Book 13 (13.68). His task is the pair's own, to stand and urge on the Danaans (12.367), as Agamemnon said the two did without being told (4.287). The other man left with him, “mighty Lycomedes” (12.366), was one of the captains of the night watch (9.84; see [[lycomedes]]).

He is not named again in Book 12. When Poseidon comes to the ships in Book 13, the two Ajaxes are together again, and the son of Oileus is the first to know the god (13.43–72; see [[poseidon]]).
