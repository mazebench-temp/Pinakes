summary: When Poseidon has swept the Achaean wall into the sea, he “made all smooth beside the strong-flowing Hellespont” and covered the great beach over again with sand (12.30–31).
grc: [ἀγάρροον]
---

{{quote:a4fae4eb-149e-53ed-9dfc-4eaecf4e9666}}

ἀγάρροον, “strong-flowing” (12.30), is the epithet that the Catalogue gives the Hellespont (ἀγάρροος, 2.845), and Homer uses it nowhere else (see [[broad-hellespont]]). The shore of the Hellespont is the beach of the camp, where the ships were drawn up in rows because the beach could not hold them all, with the wall along their sterns (14.30–36). λεῖα, “smooth” (12.30): when Poseidon has done, nothing shows where the wall stood, and the beach is covered again with sand (12.31; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

The same shore was to keep other works of men. Hector imagined the mound of the man he would kill heaped up “beside the broad Hellespont”, for sailors to point out (7.86–91), and the Achaeans built their wall against the single mound over their dead (7.336–338, 7.435–437). In the Odyssey the tomb of Achilles and Patroclus stands on a headland above the same water, to be seen far out at sea (Od. 24.80–84). The wall is to leave no trace (see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).
