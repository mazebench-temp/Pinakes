---
summary: The birds of Book 12 are the omen at the ditch, "an eagle flying high, on the left", that drops a wounded serpent among the Trojans (12.200–209, 12.218–221), and the "long-winged birds" that Hector refuses to obey (12.237–243).
passages:
  - 12.200-209 | The eagle and the serpent
  - 12.237-243 | Hector scorns the birds
---

**The bird at the ditch.** As Hector's men hesitate to cross, “a bird had come to them as they were eager to cross” (ὄρνις, 12.200): an eagle flying high, on the left, with a blood-red serpent in its talons, which bites it at the neck and is dropped in the middle of the throng (12.200–209; see [[eagle]], [[serpent]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). Polydamas repeats the description almost word for word when he reads it (12.218–220): the eagle that let its prey go before it reached its own home is the Trojans, who will break the wall but not come back in good order (12.221–227; see [[divination]]).

**Long-winged birds.** Hector answers that he will not obey οἰωνοῖσι τανυπτερύγεσσι, "long-winged birds", and does not care whether they fly to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, or to the left, toward the darkness (12.237–240). Then he turns the same noun into his own maxim: “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (εἷς οἰωνὸς ἄριστος, 12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). οἰωνός is both the bird and the sign it gives; Hector keeps the second sense and refuses the first. In Book 8 the army needed no seer to read Zeus' eagle (8.247–252); here the eagle comes on the left, and its reader is a Trojan whom Hector will not hear.
