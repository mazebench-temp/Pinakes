**The message.** Menestheus, whose tower the Lycians are attacking, cannot make himself heard over the din, and sends the herald Thootes (12.331–342; see [[heralds]], [[thootes]] and [[menestheus]]):

{{quote:5ced9413-0c37-542f-9b2f-ef522be85ca9}}

Thootes runs along the wall, stands beside the Ajaxes and speaks at once (12.352–353). His ten verses keep five of the eight word for word (12.344, 12.346–347, 12.349–350 = 12.357, 12.359–360, 12.362–363) and two more with a word or two changed. The changes are the ones delivery requires, and one more:

1. **Frame.** He begins with an address and the sender's name: “Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives, / the dear son of Peteos, whom Zeus has nurtured, bids you” (12.354–355; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[peteos]]). The address is the verse with which Agamemnon hailed the pair in the review of Book 4, where he went on, “to you two I give no orders, since it is not fitting to urge you on” (4.285–286). The herald brings them an order.
2. **The order.** Menestheus' command to the herald, “run and call Ajax” (12.343), becomes a verse addressed to the Ajaxes themselves, which Menestheus did not speak: “go there, so that you may face the toil, even for a little while” (12.356). With μίνυνθά περ, “even for a little while”, the herald asks no more of them than a short help.
3. **Here and there.** The places turn with the speaker. Menestheus' “sheer destruction will soon be wrought here” (τῇδε, 12.345) becomes “there” (κεῖθι, 12.358), and his “if over there too toil and quarrel have arisen” (κεῖθι, 12.348) becomes “if here too war and quarrel have arisen” (ἐνθάδε, 12.361). In the same verse πόνος, “toil”, has become πόλεμος, “war”; the toil has moved up into the herald's own verse (πόνοιο, 12.356). The last two verses are the same in the Greek, and only the English turns “come” into “go” (12.349, 12.362).

**The choice.** The message offers two things, both Ajaxes or, if they are needed where they are, Telamonian Ajax alone with Teucer, “well skilled with the bow” (12.350; see [[teucer]]). Ajax takes the second: he tells the son of Oileus and Lycomedes to stay and urge on the Danaans, and goes with his brother (12.364–372; see [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). He judges that the fighting where he stands needs the other two, and the message's second choice becomes his answer.

**Book 4.** The errand repeats the one Talthybius ran for Agamemnon in Book 4, when he carried the king's call to Machaon in the king's words, with the sender named and one title changed (4.193–197, 4.204–207). The verse that sends both heralds off is the same (4.198 = 12.351; see [[talthybius]]).
