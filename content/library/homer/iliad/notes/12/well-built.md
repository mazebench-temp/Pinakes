summary: The translation's “well-built” renders ἐΰδμητος of the Achaean wall and its towers and ποιητός of the gates the Trojans pour through; in Sarpedon's simile πυκινὸς δόμος is the lion's “close-built fold.”
grc: [ἐΰδμητον, ἐϋδμήτων, ποιητὰς]
en: [close-built]
---

**The wall.** ἐΰδμητος = εὖ (“well”) + δμητός, from δέμω (“build”), the verb of the wall's building (τεῖχος ἔδειμαν, 7.436). It is the wall's epithet as the battle reaches it: “but now battle and cries blazed around the well-built wall” (12.35–36), Asius' men come “straight for the well-built wall” (12.137), and the defenders throw stones “from the well-built towers” (12.154; [[the-achaean-wall]], [[swift-sailing-ships]]). The Iliad used the word once before, of the altar at Chryse (1.448); later it describes the towers and the city of Troy (16.700, 21.516, 22.195). Its forms are added to the entry's.

**The gates.** When Hector has broken in, “some climbed over the wall, and others poured in through the well-built gates themselves” (12.469–470; [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). ποιητός, “made,” is the word the translation also renders “well-built” at 5.198 and 5.466, the second time of the gates of Troy.

**The fold.** In Sarpedon's simile the lion goes “even into the close-built fold” (ἐς πυκινὸν δόμον, 12.301; [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]), the phrase of Amyntor's house in Book 10 (10.267). The translation renders it “close-built” here, and that English is added to the entry's.
