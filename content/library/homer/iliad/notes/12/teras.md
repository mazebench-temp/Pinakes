**The portent.** An eagle flying high on the left of the Trojan army, carrying a blood-red serpent still alive, is struck on the breast by its prey and drops it in the middle of the throng, and the Trojans shudder to see the writhing snake lying among them, “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (Διὸς τέρας αἰγιόχοιο, 12.200–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]], [[eagle]] and [[serpent]]). The phrase named the Gorgon's head on the aegis in Book 5 (5.742; see the article above). Two portents on the Achaean side lie behind this one. At Aulis a blood-red serpent darted from under the altar, ate the sparrow and her young, and was turned to stone by Zeus, and Calchas read in it the years of the war (2.308–330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). In Book 8 Zeus sent an eagle carrying a fawn, which it dropped beside his altar, and when the Achaeans saw that the bird had come from Zeus, “they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (8.247–252). Here the serpent fights back: it “had not yet forgotten the joy of battle” (12.203; see [[charme]]), and the eagle loses its prey.

**The reading.** Polydamas reads the sign as the Trojans' own story. The eagle let its prey go before it reached its home, and the Trojans, even if they break the gates and the wall, will not come back from the ships in good order (12.217–227). He ends: “So would a prophet interpret it, one who clearly in his heart / knew of portents, and whom the people would obey” (12.228–229; see [[theopropion]] and [[divination]]). τεράων is his word for what a seer knows. Hector answers that he cares nothing for birds, and that “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).

**Trusting the portents.** Then Zeus rouses a storm of wind from Ida that carries the dust against the ships, and the Trojans go at the wall, “trusting in his portents and in their own strength” (τοῦ περ δὴ τεράεσσι πεποιθότες, 12.256). The portents now are the storm and the dust, signs of Zeus' favor that need no seer to read them. The Trojans trust them as Tydeus sent Maeon home and Bellerophon killed the Chimaera, “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 4.398, 6.183). The bird's warning comes true later, when the Trojans cross the ditch again in disorder before Patroclus (16.367–371).
