summary: On Polydamas' advice the Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch and attack the wall on foot in five companies. They shudder at the eagle's portent but follow Hector, tear at the battlements in the dust storm Zeus sends, and when Hector breaks the gate they pour over the wall and through the gates.

**On foot.** The book's first battle verse joins the two armies, “the Argives and Trojans, in a mass” (12.3), and the narrator looks past the poem to the day when “the best of the Trojans had died” (12.13; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). At the ditch the horses balk, but “the men on foot were eager to see if they could accomplish it” (12.59). Polydamas speaks to Hector and the other “leaders of the Trojans and the allies”, and when Hector leaps from his chariot the other Trojans do the same (12.61–83; see [[polydamas]] and [[hector]]). Each man gives his horses to his charioteer to hold at the ditch, in the verses that told the Achaeans' order at dawn (12.84–85 = 11.47–48; see [[battle-order]]), and they form five companies (12.86–104; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

**Fifteen leaders.** Five of the leaders are sons of Priam (Hector, Cebriones, Paris, Helenus and Deiphobus), three are sons of Antenor (Agenor, Archelochus and Acamas), and two belong to the house of Anchises (Aeneas and Alcathous, the husband of Aeneas' sister). Polydamas is the son of the elder Panthous, Asius comes from the towns on the Hellespont, and Sarpedon leads the allies with Glaucus and Asteropaeus (12.88–104; see [[sons-of-priam]], [[antenor]], [[anchises]], [[asius]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Hector's company has “the most and the best” (12.89), as his Trojans had in the Catalogue (2.817). Locking their oxhide shields together, they go straight at the Danaans, sure that the enemy “would no longer / hold out, but would fall among the black ships” (12.105–107). All obey Polydamas except Asius, who drives on with his chariot to the open gate on the left, where the Lapiths stop him (12.108–194).

**The portent.** The eagle drops the serpent in the middle of the throng, “and the Trojans shuddered when they saw the writhing snake / lying in their midst, the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.208–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]] and [[teras]]). Polydamas reads it as their own fate: “we shall leave many of the Trojans behind, whom the Achaeans / will cut down with the bronze” (12.226–227). Hector refuses the sign, and they follow him “with a wondrous din” (12.251–252; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).

**At the wall.** With Zeus' storm of dust blowing at the ships, “trusting in his portents and in their own strength”, they tear at the copings, throw down the battlements and pry out the buttresses (12.252–261; see [[zeus]] and [[battlements]]). The stones fly from both sides, “some at the Trojans, and some from the Trojans at the Achaeans” (12.287–288; see [[the-great-snowfall]]), the Lycians come at Menestheus' tower (12.330–429; see [[lycians]]), and the towers and battlements are spattered with blood “on both sides, from Trojans and Achaeans” (12.430–431).

**Through the gate.** Hector calls them by their epithet: “Rise up, horse-taming Trojans, break through the wall / of the Argives, and throw the wondrous fire on the ships” (12.440–441; see [[tamer-of-horses]]).
