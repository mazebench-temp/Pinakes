---
summary: The narrator calls Asius “the fool!” as he drives his chariot at the gate, and his men “the fools!” for saying that the Achaeans would no longer hold out. What they expect comes at the end of the book, by other hands.
passages:
  - 12.108-127 | Asius drives for the gate
---

**Asius.** Asius alone will not leave his horses at the ditch, and drives them at the gate on the left, which the Achaeans hold open for men fleeing from the plain (12.108–123; see [[asius]]). The narrator breaks into the account to pass sentence: “the fool! He was not going to escape the evil fates / and, glorying in his horses and chariot, from the ships / go back home to windy Ilios” (12.113–115; see [[ker-doom]] and [[moira]]). νήπιος stands first in its verse, as in the narrator's other judgments of men who do not see their end: Agamemnon believing the Dream, “the fool, who did not know what deeds Zeus was planning” (2.38), and Nastes, who came to war wearing gold, “the fool, and it did nothing to keep off grim destruction” (2.873; see the article above). Asius glories in the horses that the others have left behind on Polydamas' advice (12.76–85; see [[polydamas]]).

**His men.** They follow him with shrill cries, saying that the Achaeans would no longer hold out but would fall among the black ships, “the fools! At the gates they found two men, the best”, the Lapiths Polypoetes and Leonteus (12.124–130; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]). This time the answer comes in the same verse. What the fools expected comes at the end of the book, by other hands: when Hector breaks the gate, “the Danaans fled in rout / among the hollow ships” (12.470–471; see [[hector]] and [[rout]]).

**The word returned.** In Book 8 Hector called the Achaeans who built the wall “Fools” (8.177), and boasted that his horses would leap the ditch; here the horses balk at its edge (12.50–59; see the note on Book 8). The narrator gives the word to the Trojans twice in this book, and once more when they applaud Hector against Polydamas' second counsel (18.310–313; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).
