---
summary: The sun is named once in Book 12, as a quarter of the sky. Hector does not care whether birds go to the right, “toward the dawn and the sun”, or to the left, “toward the misty darkness” (12.239–240).
---

Polydamas reads the eagle that came on the left as a warning, and Hector answers that he heeds no birds, “whether they go to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, / or to the left, toward the misty darkness” (12.239–240; see [[hector]], [[divination]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). The right is the east, where the dawn and the sun come up, and signs on the right are good (10.274, 24.320). The left is the west, the misty dark that is also Hades' share of the world (15.191; see [[misty]]).

The Odyssey divides the world by the same words. Ithaca lies “toward the darkness”, and the other islands apart from it “toward the dawn and the sun” (*Odyssey* 9.25–26); and Ithaca is known both to those who live toward the dawn and the sun and to those who live behind, toward the misty darkness (*Odyssey* 13.239–241). In the Iliad the phrase also measures the whole earth: the horses of Tros are the best of all “under the dawn and the sun” (5.267).

The index counts the line to the Sun, as it counts every sun in the poem, whether rising, setting, at midday or in a likeness, and as it counts 5.267. It counts the line to Dawn as well (see [[eos]]). The sun of Book 12 does not set. The book belongs to the third day of battle, which ends only when Hera sends the sun down into the streams of Oceanus (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).
