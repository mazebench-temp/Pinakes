---
summary: Strabo uses Book 12 for the Troad. Following Demetrius of Scepsis he traces the rivers that Poseidon and Apollo turn against the wall and names what each was called in his day; he reports Aristotle's view that the poet who invented the wall made it vanish, cites the sack of Priam's city “in the tenth year” against the Ilians' claim that it was never wholly destroyed, and takes Hector's birds that fly “toward the dawn and the sun” or “toward the misty darkness” as proof that Homer divided the world into a northern and a southern side.
---

- **The rivers of Ida.** Ida is called “of many springs”, Strabo reports, because of the number of rivers that flow from it (13.1.43). From Demetrius of Scepsis, “a man of the place”, he reports that the Scamander, the Granicus and the Aesepus all rise on Cotylus, a hill of Ida about a hundred and twenty stades above Scepsis, the Granicus and Aesepus running north to the Propontis, the Scamander west from a single spring (13.1.43). Of the rivers of 12.20, the Rhesus “is now called Rhoeites, unless it is the one that flows into the Granicus”; the Heptaporus, also called Polyporus, is crossed seven times on the road from the Fair Pine to the village of Melaenae and the Asclepieum; the Caresus flows from Malus, between Palaescepsis and Achaeium, into the Aesepus, and has given its name to the district of Caresene and to a town Caresus, now deserted; and the Rhodius flows from Cleandria and Gordus into the Aenius (13.1.44–45). Elsewhere Strabo has the Rhodius reach the sea between Abydus and Dardanus, opposite the Cynossema, the “Bitch's Tomb”, on the Chersonese, “which they say is the tomb of Hecuba”, though others say that it flows into the Aesepus; “it too is one of the rivers named by the poet”, and he quotes the verse (13.1.28). Near Zeleia the Tarsius is crossed twenty times on one road, “like the Heptaporus, which the poet names” (13.1.10). See [[rhesus-river]], [[heptaporus]], [[caresus]], [[rhodius]], [[granicus]], [[aesepus]], [[scamander]] and [[ida]].
- **Two men named Rhesus, two named Asius.** Among the many names that Thracians and Trojans share, Strabo counts the river Rhesus in the Troad and Rhesus the Thracian king, and he notes that the poet has a second Asius, Hecuba's brother, the son of Dymas, who lived in Phrygia on the Sangarius (13.1.21; 16.717–719; see [[rhesus]] and [[asius]]).
- **Arisbe and the Selleis.** Asius' horses brought him “from Arisbe … from the river Selleis” (2.838–839 = 12.96–97). Strabo infers from the verse that Arisbe was Asius' royal seat and that the Selleis flows near it, though the places were so obscure in his day that writers did not agree about them (13.1.20–21; see [[arisbe]] and [[selleis-arisbe]]).
- **The wall.** Strabo argues with Demetrius that the naval station lies too near the Ilium of his own day for that to be Homer's Troy. If it were, one would have to wonder at the recklessness of the Achaeans, who left their camp unwalled so long so near the city, for the poet says the wall was built only lately, “or it never existed, but the poet who invented it made it vanish, as Aristotle says”; and at the cowardice of the Trojans, who, once the wall was built, “fought at the wall” (ἐτειχομάχουν), broke into the camp and fought at the ships, but never dared to attack it while it was unwalled. The plain before the city, he adds, is all silt brought down by the rivers (13.1.36): [[aristotle-and-the-wall]] and [[title-of-book-12]].
- **The sack.** The Ilians of Strabo's day said that their city had not been wholly destroyed when the Achaeans took it, and had never been deserted. Strabo answers that Homer explicitly speaks of its destruction, and among other verses he quotes “and the city of Priam was sacked in the tenth year” (13.1.40–41, quoting 12.15; see [[troy]]).
- **The quarters of the sky.** Hector will not heed birds, “whether they go to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, / or to the left, toward the misty darkness” (12.239–240). Strabo quotes the two verses three times as evidence for Homer's knowledge of the regions of the world. He takes ζόφος, “darkness”, as the north, and “the dawn and the sun” as the whole southern side, along the path of the sun, as when Ithaca lies “toward the darkness” and the other islands “toward the dawn and the sun” (*Odyssey* 9.25–26; Strabo 1.2.20, 1.2.28, 10.2.12). The translation takes Hector's two directions as east and west (see its note on 12.237–243, and [[divination]]).
