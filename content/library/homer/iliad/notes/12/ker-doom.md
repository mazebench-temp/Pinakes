summary: The narrator says that Asius “was not going to escape the evil fates”; Sarpedon tells Glaucus that “the fates of death stand over us, / countless”; and at the wall Zeus “warded off the fates / from his son”.
passages:
  - 12.110-117 | Asius' fate foretold
  - 12.322-328 | The fates of death
  - 12.400-403 | Zeus saves Sarpedon on the battlement
---

**Asius.** When Asius alone of the Trojans drives his chariot at the open gate, the narrator stops to pass sentence: “the fool! He was not going to escape the evil fates / and, glorying in his horses and chariot, from the ships / go back home to windy Ilios” (12.113–115; see [[asius]] and [[nepios]]). κακὰς ὑπὸ κῆρας ἀλύξας is a phrase of escape, and the Odyssey uses it of Odysseus, who alone of his companions escaped the evil fates (*Odyssey* 23.332). Here it is negated, and the next verses tell the death as already done: “fate of evil name enfolded him / through the spear of Idomeneus” (12.116–117; see [[moira]]). It comes in the next book (13.384–393).

**Countless.** Sarpedon's reason for fighting in front is that “the fates of death stand over us, / countless, which no mortal can flee or escape” (12.326–327; see [[sarpedons-speech]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). The κῆρες here are the many deaths that wait for any man, not the two that Zeus sets on his scales for two armies or two men (8.70, 22.210), nor the two fates between which Achilles says he may choose (9.411; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). ἐφεστᾶσιν, “stand over us”, is a perfect: they are already there.

**Warded off.** Teucer's arrow hits the shining strap of Sarpedon's shield, “but Zeus warded off the fates / from his son, so that he should not be beaten down at the sterns of the ships” (12.402–403; see [[zeus]] and [[sarpedon]]). What Sarpedon has just said no mortal can do for himself, his father does for him, for now. In Book 16 Zeus wonders aloud whether to snatch him from the battle, and lets the fate come (16.431–461).
