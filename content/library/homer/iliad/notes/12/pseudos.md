---
summary: When the two Lapiths hold the gate, Asius calls Zeus a “lover of lies” (φιλοψευδής), because the day's success had made the Trojans believe the Achaeans would not hold out. The narrator answers that Zeus meant the glory for Hector.
grc: [φιλοψευδὴς]
en: [lies]
---

Asius has driven his chariot at the open gate, and his men have shouted that the Achaeans would no longer hold out (12.110–126). Two men stop them, and Asius groans, strikes his thighs, and blames the god: “Father Zeus, so you too, it seems, have become a lover of lies / through and through; for I did not think the Achaean heroes / would hold back our might and our irresistible hands” (12.162–166; see [[asius]] and [[lover-of-lies]]). φιλοψευδής is found only here. The lie he means is no word of Zeus to him, for Zeus has promised him nothing. It is the day itself, which had seemed to promise that the Achaeans would not hold out (see the translation's note on 12.164).

The narrator answers him at once: “So he spoke, but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this; / for his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.173–174; see [[kydos]] and [[zeus]]). The promise is Hector's, given by Iris (11.200–209), and the glory goes to Hector at the end of the book (12.437). Agamemnon brought the charge with more reason. Zeus “once promised me and nodded his assent / that I would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home; / but now he has devised an evil deceit” (2.112–114 = 9.19–21), and in Book 2 the dream that Zeus sent him was a lie (see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[apate]]). Hector speaks of the promise that he trusts in the same words, “which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to” (12.236; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).
