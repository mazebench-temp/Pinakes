summary: Zeus governs the fight at the wall from Ida. Asius' complaint does not move him, for he means to give Hector glory; the eagle with the serpent is his portent, and he sends a storm of dust that bewitches the Achaeans, rouses his son Sarpedon against the wall and keeps death from him, and at last gives Hector the higher glory and makes light the stone that breaks the gate.

**The rain to come.** When Poseidon and Apollo turn the rivers of Ida against the wall after the sack, “Zeus rained / without ceasing, so that he might the sooner make the walls float out to sea” (12.25–26). He gave Poseidon the wall in Book 7 (7.455–463) and adds his rain to the work. Whether Zeus or Apollo is the subject of θείη, “make”, is not certain (see the translation's note on 12.26 and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The lash.** In the battle the Argives, “beaten down by the lash of Zeus”, are penned in beside their ships (12.37–38). Διὸς μάστιξ returns only in Ajax's taunt to Hector (13.812). Polydamas allows that the day may be the god's doing: “if, meaning them evil, he is destroying them utterly, / Zeus who thunders on high” (12.67–68; see [[thunders-on-high]] and [[polydamas]]). Hector said the same of Zeus when the truce failed, κακὰ φρονέων (7.70).

**A lover of lies.** When the Lapiths hold the gate, Asius strikes his thighs and cries, “Father Zeus, so you too, it seems, have become a lover of lies / through and through” (12.164–165; see [[asius]], [[father-zeus]] and [[lover-of-lies]]). Asius took the day's success for a sign that the Achaeans would not hold out (12.125–126), but Zeus promised him nothing; he told Thetis that no word he nods to “can be taken back, or prove false” (1.526). Zeus is not moved:

{{quote:1895e2eb-ae40-5262-bc2a-adcce8f53dcb}}

In Book 11 the gods blamed him “because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (11.79). Now the glory is Hector's. The verse returns at the ships, where the narrator gives its purpose: Hector is to throw fire on the ships, and so all of Thetis' prayer will be fulfilled (15.596–600; see [[kydos]], [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The portent.** The eagle that drops the serpent among the Trojans is “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.209), as the Gorgon's head on the aegis is (5.742; see [[teras]], [[aegis-bearing]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). Polydamas reads it as a warning, and Hector refuses to forget “loud-thundering Zeus' / counsels, which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to”: “let us obey the counsel of great Zeus, / who is lord over all mortals and immortals” (12.235–242; see [[boule]], [[neuo]], [[loud-thundering]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]). The promise was real, but Iris gave it a term, the going down of the sun (11.200–209), and the nod is the one Hector read in the thunder (8.175). Hector sets one sign of Zeus against another, and the poem bears out both. Zeus gives him the wall, and when Patroclus drives the Trojans back, they do not cross the ditch in good order (16.366–371). Against Polydamas' last counsel Hector will again claim the glory that “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus” has given him (18.293–294).

**The storm from Ida.** Zeus acts for Hector at once:

{{quote:af82bd7c-b575-560a-a315-0b111466dc15}}

θέλγω, “bewitch”, is a god's work: Poseidon bewitches the eyes of Alcathous (13.435), Apollo the Achaeans' hearts with the aegis (15.321–322), and Zeus does it again at the ships (15.594). The Trojans attack “trusting in his portents and in their own strength” (12.256). The Achaeans look to him too: the two Ajaxes hope that “Zeus may grant it, the Olympian lord of the lightning”, that they drive the enemy to the city (12.275–276; see [[lord-of-the-lightning]] and [[olympian]]).

**The snow.** The stones fly as thick as snowflakes “when Zeus the counselor has set out / to snow, showing men these arrows of his” (12.279–280). κῆλα are Apollo's arrows in Book 1 (1.53, 1.383), and Hesiod calls thunder, lightning and the thunderbolt κῆλα Διὸς μεγάλοιο, “the shafts of great Zeus” (*Theogony* 707–708). The simile ends with “the rain of Zeus” (12.286), in the half-verse of the river in flood (5.91; see [[counselor]] and [[the-great-snowfall]]).

**His son.** The gate would not have fallen “had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon” (12.292; see [[sarpedon]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). When Teucer and Ajax strike at Sarpedon on the battlement, his father saves him:

{{quote:833f6514-96e9-57f8-8ac2-98ae50cfae38}}

He did the same in Book 5, when Tlepolemus' spear grazed Sarpedon's bone, “but his father still kept ruin away” (5.662; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). This rescue is bounded by place: not at the sterns of the ships. Sarpedon has just said that no mortal can escape the fates of death (12.326–327; see [[ker-doom]] and [[sarpedons-speech]]). He will die at Patroclus' hands, after Zeus has thought of snatching him away, given way to Hera, and shed drops of blood in his honor (16.431–461). Glaucus, whom Teucer wounds in this book (12.387–391), will say then that Zeus “does not defend his own son” (ὃ δʼ οὐ οὗ παιδὸς ἀμύνει, 16.522), with the verb of 12.402.

**The higher glory.** The fight hangs level like the honest working woman's scales, “until Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector” (12.436–437; 12.436 = 15.413; compare [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Two men of today could not heave Hector's stone onto a wagon, “but he wielded it easily even alone. / The son of crooked-counseling Cronus made it light for him” (12.449–450). The first verse was said of Diomedes' stone, with no god named (5.304); here the feat is Hector's and the god's at once (see [[crooked-counseling]] and [[stone-throwing]]). When Hector leaps through the gate, no one could have stopped him “except the gods” (12.466; see [[olympians]]).

**Afterward.** Book 13 begins with Zeus. Having brought Hector and the Trojans to the ships, he turns his shining eyes away from Troy, expecting no god to help either side (13.1–9), and Poseidon comes to the Achaeans (13.10–45; see [[poseidon]]).
