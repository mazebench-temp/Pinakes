**Seat, meat and cups.** Sarpedon asks Glaucus “why is it that we two are honored most / with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups / in Lycia, and all look on us as gods” (12.310–312; see [[sarpedons-speech]] and [[sarpedon]]). The second verse is the one with which Hector taunted Diomedes, whom the Danaans had honored with the same three things and would now dishonor (12.311 = 8.162). They are the honors of the feast: the first seat, the larger portion of meat, and the cup kept full, as Agamemnon keeps Idomeneus' cup full like his own (4.257–263; see [[time]]). The Lycians add land, a great domain by the Xanthus, “a fair one, of orchard and wheat-bearing plowland” (12.313–314), the words in which they once marked out a domain for Bellerophon (12.314 ≈ 6.195; see [[xanthus-of-lycia]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]).

**Paid for in battle.** The honors bring a duty: “So now we must take our stand among the foremost Lycians / and meet the blazing battle” (12.315–316). The verses are close to those with which Agamemnon shamed Menestheus and Odysseus in the review of Book 4, and there the feast was named as the reason (12.315–316 ≈ 4.341–342):

Agamemnon throws the feast in the faces of men who hang back; Sarpedon makes it his own reason to go forward. A few verses after his speech the Lycians attack the tower of the same Menestheus (12.331–332; see [[menestheus]]).

**What the people say.** The honors must be seen to be earned. Sarpedon imagines one of the close-armored Lycians saying of their kings that they rule “not without glory”, “and they eat fat sheep / and choice wine, honey-sweet; but there is strength in them too, / noble strength, since they fight among the foremost Lycians” (12.318–321; see [[kleos]]). The fat sheep and honey-sweet wine are the roast meat and “cups / of honey-sweet wine” of Agamemnon's feast for the elders (4.345–346). The people's word sets the kings' feasting beside their fighting, and the one pays for the other. When Menelaus calls the Argive leaders to defend the body of Patroclus, he names them as the men “who drink at the public cost” (δήμια πίνουσιν) beside the sons of Atreus (17.248–251).
