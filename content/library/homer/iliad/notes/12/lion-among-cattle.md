---
summary: Zeus rouses his son Sarpedon against the Argives “like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293), a half-verse that opens the full simile of the mountain lion and the fold.
grc: ["λέονθʼ ὣς βουσὶν ἕλιξιν"]
---

εἰ μὴ ἄρʼ υἱὸν ἑὸν Σαρπηδόνα μητίετα Ζεὺς / ὦρσεν ἐπʼ Ἀργείοισι λέονθʼ ὣς βουσὶν ἕλιξιν: “had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon, against the Argives, like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293). ἕλιξ, “twisting”, is a standing epithet of cattle (εἰλίποδας ἕλικας βοῦς, 9.466), usually taken of their curving horns, by some of their rolling gait (see [[with-curving-horns]]). The half-verse belongs to the narrator's judgment that comes before the long simile: not even then would the Trojans and Hector have broken the gates of the wall and the long bar, without Sarpedon (12.290–293; see [[sarpedon]]). The verb is Zeus' own, ὦρσεν, “roused”: the lion is set on the herd by his father.

When Sarpedon goes, the herd of the half-verse becomes a fold of sheep, guarded by herdsmen with dogs and spears (12.299–306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). At his death in Book 16 the image of the lion among cattle is turned against him: Patroclus kills him as a lion kills a tawny, great-hearted bull among the cattle (16.487–491). See [[lion]] and [[cattle]].
