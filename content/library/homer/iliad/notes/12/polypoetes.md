**At the gate.** Asius alone of the Trojans keeps his chariot and drives for the gate on the left of the ships, held open for men fleeing from the plain, and his men follow him, sure that the Achaeans will no longer hold out (12.110–126; see [[asius]] and [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). “The fools!” (12.127; see [[nepios]]):

{{quote:537bd8aa-05ca-50af-b8a4-ab70dbc49059}}

They are the two leaders of the Lapiths in the Catalogue (2.738–747; see [[lapiths]] and [[leonteus]]), and Polypoetes is the son of [[Peirithous]], who drove the Centaurs from Pelion on the day the boy was born (2.740–744; see [[lapiths-drive-out-the-centaurs]]). The Catalogue called him “steadfast in battle” (2.740; see [[steadfast-in-battle]]); here he is κρατερός, “mighty” (12.129, 12.182; see [[krateros]]). The oaks rooted against wind and rain show men who will not be moved (see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]).

**Before the gate.** The narrator goes back to tell how the two had stood inside at first, rousing the Achaeans, and darted out when they saw the Trojans rushing at the wall (12.141–145). Before the gate they are like two wild boars that face the din of men and dogs (12.146–150; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]), and the bronze rings on their chests as they are hit, “for they fought very fiercely, / trusting in the men above them and in their own strength” (12.151–153), while the stones fall from the towers like snowflakes (12.154–161; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]). Asius' complaint to Zeus pays them an enemy's tribute: like wasps or bees defending their young, “these men, though they are only two, will not give way from the gates / until they have either killed or been taken” (12.167–172; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]).

**His victims.**

{{quote:04e81115-9d6c-5a59-b445-1b4070fb3827}}

The killing of Damasus is told in stock verses: the brain “inside was all spattered; and he beat him down for all his eagerness” ends Agamemnon's killing of the Trojan [[oileus-the-trojan|Oileus]] (12.185–186 ≈ 11.97–98), and Achilles kills Demoleon through his bronze-cheeked helmet in nearly the same four verses (20.397–400; see [[helmet]] and [[androktasia]]). Pylon and Ormenus get only their names (see [[damasus]], [[pylon]] and [[ormenus-killed-by-polypoetes]]). Asius is not among the dead: the narrator has given his death to Idomeneus (12.113–117; see [[idomeneus]]).
