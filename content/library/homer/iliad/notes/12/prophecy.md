summary: The narrator prophesies twice in Book 12, foretelling the wall's destruction after the war and Asius' death by the spear of Idomeneus. Polydamas reads the eagle as a prophet would, and Hector rejects the reading for the promise of Zeus; the reading comes true in Book 16.

**The narrator's forecasts.** The book opens with a forecast that reaches beyond the end of the poem: after Hector's death, the sack of Troy and the Argives' return, Poseidon and Apollo will wash the wall away (12.3–35; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). It fulfills what Zeus promised Poseidon in Book 7 (7.459–463; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]), and it is told in the past tense of a thing already done, closed by “So Poseidon and Apollo were to do in time to come” (12.34). A few verses later he foretells a single death: Asius will not escape the evil fates, for “fate of evil name enfolded him / through the spear of Idomeneus” (12.113–117; see [[asius]], [[moira]] and [[ker-doom]]). It comes true in the next book (13.384–393).

**Polydamas' reading.** Polydamas reads the eagle that drops the serpent as a sign about the Trojans: “For this is how I think it will end, if truly / this bird came to the Trojans” (12.217–218). The eagle never brought its prey home to its young, and the Trojans, even if they break the wall, will not come back from the ships in good order (12.217–227). He ends with what a prophet would say, “one who clearly in his heart / knew of portents, and whom the people would obey” (12.228–229; see [[theopropion]], [[divination]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]).

**Hector's answer.** Hector sets against the sign the promise of Zeus, “which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to” (12.235–243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). Both are partly right. The promise holds, and Hector breaks the gate the same day (12.437–471); but the Trojans come back across the ditch in disorder before Patroclus (16.367–371), and Hector remembers before his death that he did not obey Polydamas (22.99–103; see [[polydamas]] and [[hector]]). His own forecast in this book, that Polydamas would die by his spear if he held back (12.248–250), is a threat and not a prophecy, and the poem never makes him carry it out.
