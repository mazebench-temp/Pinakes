summary: Telamonian Ajax ranges along the towers with the son of Oileus, rousing the Achaeans. Called by Menestheus, he leaves the lesser Ajax and Lycomedes to hold their place, goes with Teucer to the tower the Lycians are storming, kills Epicles with a boulder that no man of today could easily hold, and with Teucer drives Sarpedon back from the battlement.

**On the towers.** Ajax first appears in the book as one of a pair. While the Trojans tear at the battlements, “the two Ajaxes, calling out orders, ranged along the towers / in every direction, urging on the might of the Achaeans”, and together they speak the exhortation to every rank, “outstanding, or middling, / or worse” (12.265–277; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[not-all-alike-in-war]]).

**Sent for.** Menestheus sees the Lycians coming at his tower and looks along the wall for a leader to ward off disaster. He sees the two Ajaxes, with Teucer close by, but cannot make them hear over the din of shields, helmets and gates (12.331–341), and sends his herald “to Ajax” (12.342): “run and call Ajax, / both of them, rather”, and if there is toil and quarrel there too, “then at least let valiant Telamonian Ajax come alone, / and let Teucer follow with him, well skilled with the bow” (12.343–350; see [[menestheus]] and [[thootes]]). If only one can come, it is to be the great Ajax, as he was the man Menelaus called to save Odysseus (11.464–465). Thootes repeats the message almost word for word, “and great Telamonian Ajax did not disobey” (12.364; see [[message-repeated]]).

**Leaving his namesake.** He tells the son of Oileus to stay: “Ajax, you two stay here, you and mighty Lycomedes, / and stand and urge the Danaans to fight with all their might; / but I will go over there and face the war, / and I will come back again quickly, when I have helped them well” (12.366–369; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[lycomedes]]). He goes along the inside of the wall with Teucer, “his brother by the same father”, and Pandion, who carries Teucer's bow (12.370–374; see [[teucer]], [[pandion]] and [[kasignetos]]). The brothers who fought as shield and bow in Book 8 (8.266–272) go together again. They arrive as the leaders of the Lycians climb onto the battlements “like a dark storm” (12.375; see [[like-a-black-storm]]).

**Epicles.**

{{quote:5607d46e-b39a-5ebe-8aa6-793d3996545a}}

The boulder lay inside the wall, “uppermost beside the battlement” (12.381; see [[stone-throwing]], [[battlements]] and [[epicles]]). “Such as mortals are now” is the narrator's measure for the stones of Diomedes and Aeneas (5.302–304, 20.285–287) and for the stone with which Hector will break the gate (12.445–449; see [[men-of-today]]). Ajax's boulder is one that a single man of today, even in his prime, could not easily hold in both hands; Hector's, one that two of the best could not easily heave onto a wagon. The jagged boulder and the fall “like a diver” come together again when Patroclus kills Cebriones (16.735–743; see [[like-a-diver]]). Ajax felled Hector in their duel with “a stone like a millstone” (7.270), and will fell him again with a stone at the ships (14.409–420).

**Sarpedon.** When Sarpedon has dragged a battlement away and opened a path for many (12.397–399), “Ajax and Teucer came at him together” (12.400). Teucer's arrow hits the strap of his shield, but Zeus wards off the fates from his son; “and Ajax leapt on him and stabbed his shield; but not right through / went the spear, and it drove him back as he pressed on” (12.401–405; see [[sarpedon]]). Sarpedon gives ground a little from the battlement, but no more (12.406–407). Neither side can then drive the other back (12.417–420; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]), and the breach comes when Hector smashes a gate with a stone (12.445–471; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]). When Poseidon comes to the ships in Book 13, the two Ajaxes are together again (13.46–82).
