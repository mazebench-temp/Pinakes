Glaucus goes with him against Menestheus' tower until Teucer's arrow strikes his bared arm, and he leaps back from the wall unseen, so that no Achaean may boast over him.

**Chosen.** Glaucus has not been named since he killed Iphinous in Book 7 (7.13–16). He returns as one of the two men whom Sarpedon judges the best of the allies after himself:

{{quote:76087caf-0c52-57c8-9e75-397bce1ecd75}}

The other, the Paeonian Asteropaeus, will meet Achilles with the question Glaucus put to Diomedes, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (21.153 ≈ 6.145; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**The listener.** The speech in which Sarpedon explains why the kings of Lycia must fight among the foremost is spoken to Glaucus alone, and in the dual: νῶϊ, “we two” (12.310), the pair who might escape the war (φυγόντε, 12.322) and be “ageless and immortal” (ἀγήρω τʼ ἀθανάτω, 12.323; see [[sarpedon]] and [[sarpedons-speech]]). In the Catalogue Glaucus comes second (2.876); in the speech the cousins share the seat of honor, the domain by the Xanthus and the title of “these kings of ours” (12.311–319; see [[xanthus-of-lycia]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). His father sent him to Troy “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (6.208; see [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[hippolochus]]); Sarpedon tells him what the honors of the best require. Glaucus answers with no words: “So he spoke, and Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey” (12.329). The two lead the Lycians against Menestheus' tower (12.330–332; see [[lycians]]).

**The wound.** When Telamonian Ajax and Teucer come to the tower, Ajax kills Epicles (12.378–386; see [[epicles]]), and Teucer shoots Glaucus:

{{quote:15fc586a-8ecb-5201-971c-4659a010ad88}}

He goes λαθών, “unseen” (12.390), and the narrator gives the reason: no Achaean is to see him hit and boast over him (εὐχετόῳτο, 12.391; see [[euchomai]] and [[teucer]]). “And grief came upon Sarpedon as Glaucus went away” (12.392; see [[achos]]).

In Book 16 the grief runs the other way: “grief came” upon Glaucus (ἄχος γένετο, 16.508) as he heard the dying Sarpedon call him “Glaucus, my friend” (Γλαῦκε πέπον, 16.492), as Sarpedon called him “my friend” here (ὦ πέπον, 12.322). He is still wounded. The narrator tells the wound again in the verses of this book, adding that Teucer shot him ἀρὴν ἑτάροισιν ἀμύνων, in the words of Menestheus, who looked for a leader “who might ward off disaster from his companions” (16.511–512 ≈ 12.387–388, 12.334). Glaucus prays to Apollo, who stops the pain and dries the blood, and he rallies the Lycians to fight over Sarpedon's body (16.513–533; see [[apollo]]). When he rebukes Hector for leaving the body to the Argives, Hector answers him with the opening words of Sarpedon's speech, Γλαῦκε τί ἢ (17.170 ≈ 12.310; see [[hector]]).
