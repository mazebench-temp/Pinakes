summary: The city's fate frames the wall's. The Achaean wall stood firm while “the city of lord Priam was unsacked”, and its end comes after the city “was sacked in the tenth year” (12.10–16). In the battle Troy is the home behind the Trojans, to which Asius will never drive back (12.114–115).
refs: [12.11, 12.15, 12.74, 12.276]
---

**The sack, told as past.** The narrator measures the life of the Achaean wall by the city's. It stood firm as long as Hector lived, Achilles kept up his wrath, and “the city of lord Priam was unsacked” (12.11); ἀπόρθητος, “unsacked”, occurs in Homer only here. Then he tells the fall itself as a thing done: “when the best of the Trojans had died … and the city of Priam was sacked in the tenth year” (12.13–15; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). The tenth year is the year of Calchas' sign: “and in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (2.329; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**The city behind the army.** In the battle Troy is the home the Trojans mean to return to. Polydamas fears that if the Achaeans turn on them at the ditch, “not even a messenger will get back to the city” (12.73–74; see [[polydamas]]); Asius, glorying in his chariot, will never “go back home to windy Ilios” (12.114–115; see [[asius]] and [[windy]]); and the two Ajaxes hope to “drive the enemy to the city” (12.276; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]).
