---
summary: The Trojans fight at the wall in five columns under fifteen leaders, and the book tests what leaders can do. Polydamas speaks to Hector as “a man of the people”; the two Ajaxes call on every rank of fighter; Menestheus looks along the wall for a leader who can save his men; and Sarpedon tells the Lycians that the work of more men is better.
refs: [12.84-107, 12.211-214, 12.265-276, 12.331-337, 12.346-347, 12.354, 12.376, 12.408-414]
passages:
  - 12.84-107 | The five columns
---

**Five columns.** On Polydamas' advice each Trojan tells his charioteer to hold the horses at the ditch, and the army, “marshaled in five companies, followed with their leaders” (12.84–87; see [[battle-order]]). Each company has three leaders: Hector, Polydamas and Cebriones; Paris, Alcathous and Agenor; Helenus, Deiphobus and Asius; Aeneas with the two sons of Antenor, Archelochus and Acamas; and Sarpedon with Glaucus and Asteropaeus, at the head of the allies (12.88–104; see the translation's note on 12.86–104). Hector's company is “the most and the best” (12.89). One leader breaks the order: Asius will not leave his horses (12.110–115; see [[asius]]).

**Leaders and people.** Polydamas calls himself δῆμον ἐόντα, “a man of the people”, who may not speak against Hector (12.211–214; see [[demos]] and [[kratos]]), though he leads the first column beside him. The prophet he would like to be is one “whom the people would obey” (12.229).

**Every rank.** The two Ajaxes, whom Thootes will hail as “leaders of the bronze-armored Argives” (12.354, the words of Agamemnon at 4.285), go along the towers and call on every man, “outstanding, or middling, / or worse”: now there is work for all (12.265–276; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[not-all-alike-in-war]]).

**A leader for the tower.** When the Lycians come at his tower, Menestheus “peered along the rampart of the Achaeans, to see whether he could catch sight of / one of the leaders who might ward off disaster from his companions” (12.333–334; see [[menestheus]]); he cannot make himself heard, and sends a herald (12.335–350; see [[heralds]]). The enemy is described by its leaders, “the leaders of the Lycians”, “violent in the fierce battles” (12.346–347), “the mighty leaders and rulers of the Lycians” (12.376; see [[lycians]]).

**A leader alone.** Sarpedon, driven back from the battlement, tells his men that he cannot break through alone, and they press on around him, “fearing their lord's rebuke” (12.408–414; see [[the-work-of-many]]).
