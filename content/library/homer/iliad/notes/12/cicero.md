---
summary: Cicero ends a letter to Atticus, on whether to resist Caesar's land law, with Hector's “one omen is best” in Greek; in his poem on Marius an eagle, bitten by a snake, kills it and flies toward the east, a lucky sign made from Homer's unlucky one; and in On Divination he notes that the Romans count the left the lucky side and the Greeks the right.
---

- **One omen is best.** Late in 60 BCE, before Caesar's consulship, Cicero wrote to Atticus about the choice before him: to resist Caesar's land law, which meant a struggle but one full of glory, to stay away, or to support it, as Caesar was said to expect. Support promised close ties with Pompey and even with Caesar, reconciliation with his enemies, peace with the people and a quiet old age. But he was moved by the close of the third book of his own poem on his consulship, where the Muse Calliope bids him hold to the course he had followed from his youth and as consul, and increase his fame and the praise of good men; and so, he wrote, “I do not think there can be any doubt that for me always εἷς οἰωνὸς ἄριστος ἀμύνεσθαι περὶ πάτρης” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.3.3–4). See [[one-omen-is-best]] and [[aristotle-one-omen]].
- **The eagle and the snake.** In the first book of *On Divination* Cicero's brother Quintus, arguing for augury, quotes Cicero's own poem *Marius*. Jupiter's winged servant, wounded by a snake's bite, seizes the snake from a tree trunk, transfixing it with its savage talons as it darts its mottled neck; it tears and bloodies it with its beak, and, its anger sated and its pain avenged, flings it down into the water, lacerated, and turns from the setting sun toward the bright east. Marius, the augur of the divine will, marks the sign as a promise of his glory and his return, and the father of heaven himself thunders on the left (*On Divination* 1.106). Homer's eagle, bitten, lets the serpent fall among the Trojans and flies off screaming, and the sign warns them (12.200–209); Cicero's eagle kills its snake, and the sign is good. See [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]].
- **Left and right.** Arguing against augury in the second book, Cicero points out that the augurs themselves disagree. By Roman custom thunder on the left is lucky, as in Ennius' verse, while Homer's Ajax, as Cicero calls him, tells Achilles that Jupiter shows the Trojans favorable signs with lightning on the right: “so to us the left seems better, to Greeks and barbarians the right” (*On Divination* 2.82). The verse he means is spoken by Odysseus in the embassy (9.236–237). In Book 12 the eagle comes on the left, the unlucky side (12.201), and Hector scorns birds on either side, the right toward the dawn and the left toward the darkness (12.238–240; see [[divination]]).
