summary: As the narrator foretold at the wall, Asius dies by Idomeneus' spear. Coming on foot to defend Othryoneus' body, with his horses breathing on his shoulders, he is struck in the throat and falls like an oak or a pine felled for ship-timber; Antilochus drives off his horses, Deiphobus avenges him, and his son Adamas dies too.

**The prophecy kept.** At the wall the narrator said that Asius, “glorying in his horses and chariot”, would not go home from the ships, for “fate of evil name enfolded him / through the spear of Idomeneus” (12.113–117). Now Idomeneus drags away the body of Othryoneus, and Asius comes to defend it (see [[othryoneus]] and [[idomeneus]]):

{{quote:900bed96-9c61-5133-ad6c-6e0ea53ef583}}

He comes “on foot in front of his horses”, which the charioteer holds so close behind him that they breathe on his shoulders: the horses that the Catalogue gave him and that kept him from obeying Polydamas at the ditch (2.838–839, 12.110–115) stand at his back to the end. The five verses of his fall return for the death of Sarpedon (13.389–393 = 16.482–486; see [[the-felled-tree]] and [[sarpedon]]). His charioteer, struck out of his wits, does not turn the horses, and Antilochus kills him and drives the horses to the Achaeans (13.394–401; see [[antilochus]]).

**Avenged.** Deiphobus, Ἀσίου ἀχνύμενος, “grieving for Asius”, throws at Idomeneus and kills Hypsenor instead, and boasts that Asius will be glad on his way to the house of Hades, “since I have given him an escort” (13.402–416; see [[deiphobus]] and [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]]). His son Adamas dies at Meriones' hands (13.560–575; see [[adamas]]), and of the others named with him at the gate an Oenomaus and a Thoon die in this book (13.506, 13.545; see [[oenomaus-the-trojan]] and [[thoon-companion-of-asius]]). When Hector looks for “Adamas, son of Asius, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus”, he finds them dead at the sterns of the ships, and he asks Paris for them by the same names (13.759–763, 13.771; see [[hyrtacus]] and [[hector]]).
