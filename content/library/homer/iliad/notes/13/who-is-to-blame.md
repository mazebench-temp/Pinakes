summary: Poseidon, as Calchas, grants that Agamemnon may be wholly to blame for the army's plight and denies that this excuses anyone; Idomeneus says that no man is to blame and lays the defeat on Zeus' pleasure; Menelaus tells Zeus that all things come from him; Paris refuses Hector's blame; and the narrator shows the two sons of Cronus making sorrows for the heroes.

Book 13 puts the question of blame in four mouths, two Achaean and two Trojan, and the narrator answers it in his own way.

**The leader.** Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, names a cause: the Trojans are at the ships “through the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army, / who in their strife with him are not willing to defend / the swift-sailing ships” (13.108–110; see the translation's note on 13.108–114). He grants more than the army would: “even if in very truth the one wholly to blame is / the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, / because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (13.111–113; see [[agamemnon]] and [[aitios]]). It is the charge Achilles made in Book 1 and Nestor in Book 9 (1.335, 9.106–111; see the note on Book 9), now made by a god in a seer's shape, as Calchas in Book 1 named Agamemnon's dishonoring of Apollo's priest (1.94). The god's point is that blame is no excuse. “For us at least there is no way to hold back from war” (13.114); the hearts of good men can be healed (13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]).

**No man.** Poseidon, now as Thoas, puts the question to Idomeneus, and Idomeneus clears the army: “no man now is to blame, as far as I / know; for all of us know how to make war”, and no one shrinks from it; “but so, I think, / it must be the pleasure of the son of Cronus, supreme in might, / that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (13.222–227; see [[idomeneus]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). The last verse is spoken three times in three books. Polydamas, in Book 12, wished it might happen at once, if Zeus meant to help the Trojans (12.67–70); Idomeneus here lays it on Zeus' pleasure; and Agamemnon will say it in the next book, as proof that Zeus has turned against the Achaeans (14.69–73). The Achaean answer moves the blame from men to Zeus. Ajax says it to Hector at the end of the book: “We are not without knowledge of battle at all, / but by the evil lash of Zeus we Achaeans were beaten down” (13.811–812; compare 12.37).

**All from Zeus.** Menelaus, over Peisander, blames the Trojans and asks Zeus to account for them: “Father Zeus, they say that you surpass in wisdom all others, / men and gods; and all these things come from you. / How you favor men who are arrogant” (13.631–633; see [[hybris]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]). The god of guests is to punish the Trojans (13.624–625), and the same god is the source of their success. Menelaus does not reconcile the two; in Book 3, when his sword broke on Paris' helmet, he had simply called Zeus the most baneful of the gods (3.365).

**Not to blame.** The Trojans have the question too. Hector, finding his leaders dead or wounded, rebukes Paris, and Paris answers: “Hector, since your heart is set on blaming one who is not to blame” (ἀναίτιον αἰτιάασθαι, 13.775; see [[paris]] and [[beyond-ones-strength]]). The phrase is the one Patroclus used of Achilles, a dread man who “would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.654), and Paris has the facts on his side: he has fought without rest, and the men Hector asks about have been killed (13.778–783).

**The narrator.** Between the speeches the narrator says what none of the speakers knows. Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans to give glory to Achilles, but not the destruction of the Achaeans; Poseidon helped the Achaeans in secret. “The two mighty sons of Cronus, with their minds set on different sides, / were making grievous sorrows for the heroes” (13.345–346), and between them they stretched the rope of strife and war over both armies, “which loosened the knees of many” (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Idomeneus was right that it was Zeus' pleasure, and wrong about its end; Menelaus was right that it all came from Zeus, and did not know why.
