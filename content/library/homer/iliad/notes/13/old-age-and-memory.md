summary: Idomeneus, “half-gray”, fights the battle of an aging man. He fears Aeneas for his youth, “the greatest power”, and wishes they were of an age; when he has killed Oenomaus his legs are no longer steady enough to strip the body, and he gives ground step by step, in a verse that the old Nestor will speak of himself.

**Half-gray.** When the rope of war is stretched over the armies, “then, though he was half gray, calling on the Danaans, / Idomeneus leapt among the Trojans and roused rout among them” (13.361–362; see [[idomeneus]]). μεσαιπόλιος, “half gray, grizzled”, is found only here (see [[half-gray]]). περ, “though”, makes his age a thing to be overcome. He is no Nestor, but the book measures him by his years, and in his long day of fighting it tells what they cost.

**Youth, the greatest power.** As Aeneas comes on to defend Alcathous, Idomeneus calls his companions: “I am alone, and terribly afraid / of Aeneas, swift of foot … / and he has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power. / For if we were of the same age, with the spirit we have now, / soon either he would carry off the great mastery, or I would” (13.481–486; see [[kratos]] and [[aeneas]]). The wish is Nestor's kind, the old man's wish to be young again, which Agamemnon first voiced for him and Nestor took up with the maxim that the gods never give men all things at once (4.313–325; see [[all-things-at-once]]); Nestor wishes it again before his stories of Ereuthalion and the Epeians (7.132–157, 11.668–762; see the note on Book 11). ὁμηλικίη, “of the same age”, is the word of the young: Hippodameia, Anchises' daughter, “surpassed all the girls of her age” (13.431). Idomeneus' spirit is young; his body is not.

**Legs no longer steady.** The fight goes as he feared. Aeneas misses him, and he kills Oenomaus, but he draws his spear from the body and cannot strip the armor, “for he was hard pressed by missiles. / For his legs were no longer steady when he rushed, / to dart after his own spear or to avoid a missile. / So in close fighting he kept off the pitiless day, / but his feet no longer carried him nimbly to flee out of the war” (13.509–515). He goes away step by step, and Deiphobus throws at him again (13.516–518; see [[kotos]] and [[retreat]]). οὐ γὰρ ἔτʼ ἔμπεδα γυῖα, “for his limbs were no longer steady”, is the half-verse with which Nestor, at Patroclus' funeral games, accepts a prize given him because old age keeps him from the contests: “for my limbs are no longer steady, my friend, my feet, nor do my hands dart out lightly from my shoulders”, and he wishes he were young and his strength steady, as when he won the games at Buprasium (23.618–643). Agamemnon had wished in Book 4 that Nestor's knees might follow his heart and his strength be steady (ἔμπεδος, 4.313–314), and Diomedes told him in Book 8 that his strength was loosened and hard old age was on him (8.102–104). Idomeneus is not yet old as Nestor is, but the same words take hold of him. Stand his ground he still can; he cannot run.

**The old men at home.** Two old men in the book are fathers who speak of what is to come: “the old man Priam”, who promised Cassandra to Othryoneus (13.368), and “the good old man Polyidus”, the seer, who told his son his two fates (13.666; see [[geron]] and [[bereaved-parents]]).
