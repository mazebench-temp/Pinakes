summary: Poseidon wishes that a god may put it in the minds of the two Ajaxes to stand firm, and tells the Achaean leaders to set shame and indignation in their minds; the close ranks set their minds straight ahead. Asius' charioteer is struck out of his wits; Hippodameia surpassed the girls of her age in beauty, handiwork and wits; Menelaus tells Zeus that men say he surpasses all in wisdom; and the two sons of Cronus have their minds set on different sides.
grc: [φρόνεον, φρονέοντε, φρονέοντι]
en: [wisdom]
---

**Put in the mind.** Poseidon's appeals go to the φρένες. To the two Ajaxes: “But for you two, may some god put it in your minds / to stand firm yourselves and to urge the others” (ἐνὶ φρεσὶ ποιήσειεν, 13.55–56; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). To the leaders, after the maxim that “the hearts of good men can be healed” (φρένες, 13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]): “But let each of you set in his mind / shame and indignation” (ἐν φρεσὶ θέσθε, 13.121–122; see [[aidos]] and [[nemesis]]). The rally ends in the close ranks around the two Ajaxes, where “the men set their minds straight ahead, and were eager to fight” (ἰθὺς φρόνεον, 13.135; see [[battle-order]]). The phrase was used in Book 12 of Asius, who drove his horses at the open gate “Straight there, with his mind set on it” (ἰθὺς φρονέων, 12.124; see [[asius]]).

**Out of his wits.** When Idomeneus kills Asius in front of his horses, his charioteer “was struck out of the wits he had before, / and he did not dare to escape from the hands of the enemy / by turning the horses back” (πλήγη φρένας, 13.394–396; see [[antilochus]]), and Antilochus spears him and drives off the horses (13.396–401). The phrase comes once more in Homer, of Thestor, whom Patroclus finds crouching in his chariot, “for he was struck out of his wits, and the reins darted from his hands”; Patroclus spears him through the jaw and hauls him over the rail like a fisherman landing a fish (16.401–410). Each time it is the man with the reins who loses his wits, and he is speared where he sits.

**Wits.** Hippodameia, Anchises' eldest daughter, “surpassed all the girls of her age / in beauty and handiwork and wits” (κάλλεϊ καὶ ἔργοισιν ἰδὲ φρεσί, 13.431–432; see [[hippodameia-daughter-of-anchises]]), “and so / the best man in wide Troy had married her” (13.432–433; see [[aristos]]). These are the measures of a wife: Agamemnon says that Chryseis is not inferior to Clytemnestra, “not in form or stature, not in mind or in handiwork” (1.113–115). Menelaus begins his complaint to Zeus with the god's wits: “Father Zeus, they say that you surpass in wisdom all others, / men and gods; and all these things come from you” (περὶ φρένας ἔμμεναι ἄλλων, 13.631–632; see [[satiety-of-all-things]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). The praise is a reproach, for the god who surpasses all in wisdom favors the arrogant Trojans (13.633–635; see [[hybris]]). Hector uses the same words to reproach Glaucus in Book 17: he had thought that Glaucus surpassed in wits all who live in Lycia, and now he finds fault with his wits for what he has said (17.170–173).

**Minds set apart.** The two sons of Cronus, “with their minds set on different sides” (ἀμφὶς φρονέοντε, 13.345), were making grievous sorrows for the heroes: Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans, and Poseidon helped the Achaeans in secret (13.345–357; see [[the-rope-of-war]] and [[nike]]). ἀμφὶς φρονέοντε is found only here in Homer. Among the men the verb is used of thought before action. Deiphobus, torn two ways after Idomeneus' challenge, decides “as he pondered” to fetch Aeneas (φρονέοντι, 13.455–459; see [[kerdos]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]), and Antilochus “took aim in his mind” whether to throw or to close in (13.558–559). At the end of the book Paris, answering Hector's rebuke, “persuaded his brother's mind” (13.788; see [[peitho]]).
