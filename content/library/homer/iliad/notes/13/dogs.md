---
summary: Dogs in Book 13 are the sharp-toothed dogs robbed of their goat by two lions (13.198) and the hunters' dogs that Idomeneus' boar waits for (13.475); they eat the unburied, in Poseidon's curse on a shirker and Hector's threat to Ajax (13.233, 13.831); and Menelaus calls the Trojans “you evil dogs” (13.623).
---

**In the similes.** The dogs that guard the goat are the Trojans around the body of Imbrius, and the lions take it from them (13.198; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]] and [[sharp-toothed]]). The dogs and men that come at Idomeneus' boar are the Trojans following Aeneas (13.475; see [[the-bristling-boar]]).

**The eaters of the dead.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, wishes that any man who holds back from fighting this day may never return home from Troy, “but stay here and become a plaything for dogs” (κυνῶν μέλπηθρα, 13.232–234). μέλπηθρα is a thing to play with, from μέλπω, “sing and dance, sport”; the phrase returns with “Trojan dogs” in the fight for Patroclus' body (17.255, 18.179). Hector ends his answer to Ajax with the threat of the proem: “you will glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans with your fat and your flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans” (13.831–832; see [[birds]]).

**Abuse.** Menelaus, standing over Peisander, calls the Trojans κακαὶ κύνες, “you evil dogs”, who insulted him and carried off his wife (13.622–627). The feminine adjective sharpens the insult (see the translation's note on 13.623 and [[lobe]]).
