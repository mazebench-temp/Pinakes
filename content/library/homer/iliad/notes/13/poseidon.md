summary: While Zeus looks away, Poseidon watches the battle from Samothrace, drives over the sea to the ships, and rallies the Achaeans in secret, in the likeness first of Calchas and then of Thoas. Angry for his grandson Amphimachus, he fights on unseen, binding Alcathous for Idomeneus' spear and guarding Antilochus, until the glory nearly passes to the Achaeans.

**No blind watch.** Zeus has brought Hector to the ships and turned his eyes away, sure that no god will come “to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.9), nearly the words of his own ban (8.11; see [[zeus]]). The next verse proves him wrong.

{{quote:acadcc9f-8855-5637-b022-836c6c09ca56}}

The verse of the blind watch brings in Apollo when he sees Athena at Diomedes' side (10.515) and Ares when he sees Hephaestus leave his house (*Odyssey* 8.285): each time one god acts on what another is doing. Hesiod says it of Cronus watching for his children (*Theogony* 466). From Samothrace Poseidon sees what Zeus watched in Books 8 and 11, “the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (8.52, 11.82; see [[samothrace]] and [[watching-the-war]]). His motive is pity for the Achaeans and indignation with Zeus; the second verse returns when the narrator explains his help (13.15–16; 13.16 = 13.353; see [[pity]] and [[nemesis]]).

**The journey.** “The high mountains and the woods trembled / under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went” (13.18–19; see [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]). With the fourth stride he reaches Aegae, where his house stands “in the depths of the sea, / golden, gleaming, imperishable forever” (13.20–22; see [[aegae]]); the Odyssey sends him to the same house (*Odyssey* 5.381). He yokes his horses in the verses with which Zeus yoked his for Ida (13.23–26 ≈ 8.41–44) and drives over the waves, while the sea beasts frolic around him, knowing “their lord”, and the sea parts for joy (13.27–31; see [[chariot-journey]] and [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]]). In a cave between Tenedos and Imbros he unyokes the horses and sets ambrosial fodder before them, in the verses of Iris tending Ares' horses on Olympus (13.34–35 ≈ 5.368–369), and hobbles them with gold, “unbreakable, not to be loosed” (13.36–37; see [[imbros]]): the words of the chains Hephaestus forged for Ares and Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.275).

**As Calchas.** He comes to the army “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (13.45), the verse of Athena's disguises as Phoenix and as Deiphobus (17.555, 22.227; see [[calchas]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). He warns the two Ajaxes against Hector, “who claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.54), strikes them with his staff, and makes their limbs light, as Athena made Diomedes' (13.59–61; 13.61 = 5.122). He darts off like a hawk (13.62–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]). The son of Oileus knows him from behind by the movements of his feet: “gods are easy to know” (13.71–72; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The rebuke.** He rouses the weary men in the rear, who weep to see the Trojans over the wall (13.83–125). He opens with the cry of Hera in Stentor's likeness and of Agamemnon, “Shame, Argives” (13.95; 5.787, 8.228; see [[aidos]]), blames “the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army” (13.108), and grants that Agamemnon may be wholly to blame, “because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (13.113). The disguise suits the charge: in Book 1 Calchas named Agamemnon's dishonor of Apollo's priest (1.94; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). His maxim, “the hearts of good men can be healed” (13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]), Iris will turn on him in Book 15: “the minds of the good can be turned” (15.203). The battalions close around the two Ajaxes (13.126–135; see [[battle-order]]).

**For his grandson.** When Hector kills Amphimachus, son of Cteatus, “Poseidon was angry with all his heart / for his grandson” (13.206–207), since the Moliones are his sons (11.751; see [[amphimachus]] and [[the-moliones]]). He meets Idomeneus “making his voice like that of Thoas” (13.216), as Iris took Polites' voice (2.791; see [[thoas]] and [[idomeneus]]), and asks where the Achaeans' threats have gone (13.219–220). Idomeneus blames no man, only the will of Zeus (13.222–227). He wishes that whoever holds back may become “a plaything for dogs” (13.233), and leaves: “So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men” (13.239), the verse with which Apollo leaves Hector after speaking as Asius and as Mentes (16.726, 17.82).

**The elder brother.** The narrator gives the motive and the limit:

{{quote:2018589c-4fed-5861-a440-daab6c48ff18}}

In Book 15 Zeus, through Iris, claims to be “elder by birth” (15.166, 15.182); Poseidon answers that the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot and are equal in honor (15.185–193; see [[fall-of-cronus]]), and Iris reminds him that the Erinyes follow the elder (15.204). In the battle of the gods he says the narrator's words of himself, to the younger Apollo: “since I was born first and know more” (21.440). Hesiod makes Zeus the youngest (*Theogony* 478). The two brothers stretch over both armies the rope of strife and war (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).

**His hand in the battle.** He beats down Alcathous at Idomeneus' hands, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (13.434–435; see [[alcathous]]); elsewhere it is fate that fetters a man (4.517, 22.5). He guards Antilochus among the missiles and makes Adamas' spear-point powerless, the “dark-haired” god “who begrudged him the other's life” (13.554–565; see [[antilochus]], [[adamas]] and [[dark-haired]]). Nestor will say that Zeus and Poseidon loved his son (23.306–307). At last “the glory would have been the Achaeans'; / so hard did the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth, / urge on the Argives, and himself defended them with his strength” (13.676–678; see [[would-have-had-not]]). His help goes on in Book 14, first in an old man's likeness and then openly while Hera keeps Zeus asleep, until Zeus wakes and sends Iris (14.135–152, 14.352–401, 15.158–219; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).
