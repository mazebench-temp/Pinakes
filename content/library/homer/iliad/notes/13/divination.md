**The seer's likeness.** Poseidon comes to the Achaeans “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (13.45; see [[calchas]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). A seer is a fitting shape for a god's word, and Calchas has spoken for the gods before, on the plague and on the portent at Aulis (1.68–100, 2.299–330). The son of Oileus sees through the likeness: “this is not Calchas, the prophet and augur” (θεοπρόπος οἰωνιστής, 13.70; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). θεοπρόπος is the word Polydamas used of the prophet who would read the eagle at the ditch (12.228); οἰωνιστής, “augur”, a reader of birds, is used elsewhere only of the Mysian leader Ennomus (2.858, 17.218; see [[ennomus]]).

**Polyidus' prophecy.** Euchenor of Corinth, son of the seer Polyidus, “had gone on board his ship knowing well his deadly fate; / for often the good old man Polyidus had told him / that he would perish of a grievous sickness in his own halls, / or be beaten down by the Trojans among the ships of the Achaeans” (13.665–668; see [[euchenor]] and [[polyidus-the-seer]]). He chose the war, and so escaped both the sickness and the fine the Achaeans laid on a man who stayed at home (13.669–670). The seer's knowledge gives his son a choice of deaths, as Thetis' gives Achilles a choice of fates (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). The two sons of Merops, a seer who “above all men / knew the art of prophecy”, went to the war against their father's word and died (2.831–834 = 11.329–332; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]).

**The eagle on the right.** Ajax has just told Hector that he will soon flee to the city, praying for horses swifter than hawks (13.815–820):

{{quote:259a6d03-f03e-5830-8af6-82104b0a430a}}

The right is the side of good omen. The eagle of Book 12 came on the left and dropped its serpent among the Trojans, and Hector said that he cared nothing whether birds went to the right or to the left (12.200–209, 12.237–240; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]], [[one-omen-is-best]] and [[eagle]]). Now an eagle on the right flies for his enemies, and the Achaeans read it without a seer. The verse ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις returns twice in the Odyssey, each time for a bird that answers a speaker's words: an eagle carrying a goose as Telemachus takes leave of Menelaus, which Helen reads as Odysseus' homecoming and revenge (Od. 15.160–178), and a hawk plucking a dove, which Theoclymenus reads as a sign that Telemachus' line will rule in Ithaca (Od. 15.525–534).
