---
summary: Menelaus, over the body of Peisander, reminds the Trojans of “the other insult and shame” with which they insulted him, when they carried off his wife and his goods; the god of guests, he says, will make the city pay.
grc: [λώβης, λωβήσασθε]
en: [insulted]
---

Menelaus' taunt over Peisander turns from the battle at the ships to the cause of the war. The Trojans, he says, are “not lacking in the other insult and shame / with which you insulted me, you evil dogs” (ἄλλης μὲν λώβης τε καὶ αἴσχεος … ἣν ἐμὲ λωβήσασθε κακαὶ κύνες, 13.622–623; see [[aischros]] and the translation's note on 13.623). “The other insult” is the one before the war: they carried off his wedded wife and many possessions after they had been welcomed in his house (13.626–627; see [[xenia]] and [[hybris]]). The noun and the verb stand together. The verb is the one with which Achilles stopped short of the sword in Book 1, “otherwise, son of Atreus, this insult would be your last” (λωβήσαιο, 1.232), and an insult calls for payment: Achilles would not be reconciled until Agamemnon had paid back the whole heart-grieving insult (9.386–387; see the article above). Menelaus names the payment: Zeus, god of guests, “will one day destroy your steep city” (13.624–625; see [[menis]]).
