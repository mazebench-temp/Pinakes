summary: Idomeneus, half-gray, fears Aeneas because he has “the flower of youth, which is the greatest power”, and wishes they were of an age, so that one of them might carry off “the great mastery”; Polydamas will fall on the ships only “if a god is willing to give us the power”.
passages:
  - 13.481-486 | Idomeneus calls his companions
---

**The greatest power.** Idomeneus has stood his ground like a boar, but as Aeneas comes on he calls his companions, Ascalaphus, Aphareus, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus, and tells them why: “I am alone, and terribly afraid / of Aeneas, swift of foot, coming on … / and he has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power” (13.481–484; see [[idomeneus]] and [[aeneas]]). The verse-end ὅ τε κράτος ἐστὶ μέγιστον is the one Agamemnon used of Zeus, “for his power is the greatest” (2.118 = 9.25), and with which Diomedes answered Agamemnon in Book 9: Zeus gave him the scepter, “but courage he did not give you, and that is the greatest power” (9.39; see [[alke]]). Courage was the greatest power for Diomedes; for the aging Idomeneus it is youth. ἥβης ἄνθος, “the flower of youth”, is found only here in Homer (see [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**The great mastery.** He goes on: “For if we were of the same age, with the spirit we have now, / soon either he would carry off the great mastery, or I would” (13.485–486). κράτος here is the victory in a single fight, carried off like a prize (φέροιτο). Hector will put it the same way when he resolves to meet Achilles: he will stand against him, “whether he carries off the great mastery, or I” (18.307–308). In the fight that follows Idomeneus neither wins nor loses it. Aeneas misses him and he kills Oenomaus, but his legs are no longer quick enough to strip the body, and he gives ground step by step (13.502–516).

**The god's to give.** Polydamas proposes that the best men consider together “whether we should fall upon the many-benched ships, / if a god is willing to give us the power, or after that / come away from the ships unharmed” (αἴ κʼ ἐθέλῃσι θεὸς δόμεναι κράτος, 13.741–744; see [[polydamas]] and [[boule]]). He names no god. Hector has trusted the promise that Zeus would put power in his hands until he reached the ships (11.192–194, 11.207–209; see the note on Book 12); at the ships Polydamas makes the power conditional again. Zeus' own word for what he gives the Trojans in this book is victory (νίκην, 13.347; see [[nike]] and [[kydos]]).
