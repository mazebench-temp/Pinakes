**The arrogant.** Menelaus' speech over Peisander gathers the vocabulary of outrage. He calls the Trojans ὑπερφίαλοι, “overbearing” (13.621), the word with which he asked in Book 3 that Priam himself swear the oaths, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted” (3.105–106; see the note on Book 3 and [[overbearing]]). He calls what they did to him λώβη and αἶσχος, “insult and shame” (13.622–623; see [[lobe]] and [[aischros]]). Then he turns to Zeus: “How you favor men who are arrogant, / the Trojans, whose might is always reckless” (ὑβριστῇσι … ἀτάσθαλον, 13.633–634; see [[atasthalie]]).

ὑβριστής, the man who commits ὕβρις, is found only here in the Iliad. The Odyssey has it in a question that Odysseus asks three times when he comes to an unknown land: are the people “arrogant and wild and not just, / or hospitable, with a god-fearing mind?” (ὑβρισταί … ἦε φιλόξεινοι, *Odyssey* 6.120–121, 9.175–176, 13.201–202). There the arrogant are the opposite of the good host, and that is Menelaus' charge: the Trojans came to his house as guests and left with his wife and his goods (13.626–627; see [[xenia]] and [[menis]]). The same two words, ὑβρισταί and ἀτάσθαλοι, describe the suitors who hold Odysseus' land (*Odyssey* 24.282), and the Iliad has joined the verb and the adjective once before, in Nestor's story of the Epeians, who “treated us with outrage and devised reckless deeds” (11.694–695; see the note on Book 11).

**Favored by Zeus.** What Menelaus cannot understand is that Zeus “favors” such men (χαρίζεαι, 13.633; see [[charis]]), though “they say that you surpass in wisdom all others, / men and gods; and all these things come from you” (13.631–632; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). He does not doubt the punishment, for the god of guests “will one day destroy your steep city” (13.625); he complains of its delay, as he complained in Book 3 when his sword broke on Paris' helmet, “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365). The arrogance he sees in the Trojans has a last mark, an appetite for war that is never satisfied (13.634–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]).
