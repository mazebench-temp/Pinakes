---
summary: The two sons of Cronus stretch over the armies the rope “of mighty strife and of war common to all,” and Menelaus says that the Trojans cannot have their fill of “war common to all.”
---

The narrator's image of the gods' work in the battle uses the epithet: “And the two of them took the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all, / and knotting it crosswise they stretched it tight over both sides, / unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (13.358–360; [[the-rope-of-war]], [[eris]], [[krateros]]). The rope lies over both sides at once (ἐπʼ ἀμφοτέροισι), and the war it stands for is common to both. The same pairing, a word for battle called “mighty” with the war common to all, returns at the end of the day that began in Book 11, when the sun sets and the Achaeans cease from φυλόπιδος κρατερῆς καὶ ὁμοιΐου πολέμοιο, the mighty din of battle and the war common to all (18.241–242; [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

Menelaus, over the body of Peisander, uses the line-end formula: the Trojans, “whose might is always reckless, and who cannot / have their fill of the din of battle, of war common to all” (13.634–635; [[menelaus]]). The next verse begins his saying about satiety ([[satiety-of-all-things]]).
