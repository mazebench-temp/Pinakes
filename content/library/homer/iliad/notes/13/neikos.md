---
summary: The battle at the ships is a quarrel. Poseidon tells the Achaean leaders that “a great quarrel has arisen”, Meriones says that he takes his stand among the foremost “whenever the quarrel of war arises”, and at the sterns of the ships “the quarrel stood even”.
---

**A great quarrel.** Poseidon ends his rebuke of the Achaean leaders with the reason they must set shame and blame in their minds: “for a great quarrel has arisen. / Hector, good at the war cry, is fighting beside the ships” (δὴ γὰρ μέγα νεῖκος ὄρωρεν, 13.122–123; see [[aidos]] and [[nemesis]]). The quarrel is the battle, not the dispute between Agamemnon and Achilles that the god has just set aside (13.111–115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]). The same half-verse sends Patroclus from Eurypylus' hut in Book 15, when he sees the Trojans rushing at the wall: he cannot stay, “for a great quarrel has arisen”, and runs to rouse Achilles (15.399–404).

**The quarrel of war.** Meriones tells Idomeneus that he fights “among the foremost, in the battle where men win glory, … whenever the quarrel of war arises” (ὁππότε νεῖκος ὀρώρηται πολέμοιο, 13.270–271; see [[meriones]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). νεῖκος πολέμοιο, “the quarrel of war”, comes only here in the Iliad; the Odyssey has the fuller phrase “the quarrel of war common to all”, when Penelope recalls Odysseus' parting words, that the Trojans are fighting men “who most quickly decide the great quarrel of war common to all” (*Odyssey* 18.257–264), and when Athena stops the fighting on Ithaca at the close of the poem (*Odyssey* 24.541–544).

**Even.** When the Trojans see Idomeneus and come at him, “the quarrel stood even among them at the sterns of the ships” (ὁμὸν ἵστατο νεῖκος, 13.333). The image of the next lines is a storm of dust on the roads (13.334–338; see [[the-dust-storm]]), and soon the narrator stands the two gods above it, stretching “the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all” over both armies (13.358–360; see [[eris]] and [[the-rope-of-war]]). In Book 17 the two words of this book are joined: around Patroclus' body “the great quarrel of strife arose” all day long (ἔριδος μέγα νεῖκος, 17.384–385).
