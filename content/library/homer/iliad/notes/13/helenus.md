summary: The seer of Book 6 fights here with sword and bow. Helenus kills Deipyrus with a great Thracian sword; when he and Menelaus strike at the same moment, his arrow glances off Menelaus' corselet and Menelaus' spear pins his hand to his bow, and Agenor binds the wound with a sling.
# Ἑλένῳ (13.582) and Ἑλένοιο (13.758, 13.770, 13.781) occur only here; Helen is Ἑλένη, Ἑλένης.
grc: [Ἑλένῳ, Ἑλένοιο]
refs: [13.577, 13.583-600, 13.761, 13.764, 13.782-783]
passages:
  - 13.576-600 | Helenus, Deipyrus and Menelaus
---

**The sword.** Helenus strikes Deipyrus on the temple at close quarters with “a great Thracian sword” and knocks off his helmet, which rolls among the feet of the fighters until an Achaean picks it up, “and murky night covered his eyes” (13.576–580; see [[deipyrus]], [[sword]] and [[helmet]]). The prize sword that Achilles took from Asteropaeus is Thracian too (23.807–808; see [[asteropaeus]]). The man whom the poem first named as “by far the best of the bird-seers” (6.76; see [[best-of-bird-seers]]) here kills like any warrior.

**The bow.** Grief seizes Menelaus, who goes at “Helenus, the hero and lord” with threats, brandishing his spear, while Helenus draws his bow; the two strike at the same moment (13.581–585; see [[menelaus]]). “The son of Priam” hits him in the chest, but the arrow glances off the plate of his corselet like dark beans or chickpeas leaping from a winnowing shovel (13.586–592; see [[son-of-priam]], [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]] and [[corselet]]). Menelaus' spear goes through the hand that holds the polished bow and into the bow itself, and Helenus draws back among his companions, avoiding doom, “with his hand hanging at his side; and the ash spear trailed along with it” (13.593–597; see [[bow]]).

σφενδόνη, “sling”, occurs only here in Homer; the Locrians, who carry no spears, trust in the same “well-twisted sheep's wool” (13.716; see [[sling]], [[locrians]], [[agenor]] and [[healing]]).

**Mighty lord Helenus.** Three times in Hector's search, in the narrator's verse, in Hector's question and in Paris' answer, he is named with a periphrasis, βίη θʼ Ἑλένοιο ἄνακτος, “mighty lord Helenus” (13.758, 13.770, 13.781), as Heracles is βίη Ἡρακληείη, “mighty Heracles” (see [[might-of-heracles]]). He is not dead: he and Deiphobus have gone away “struck by long spears / in the hand; but the son of Cronus kept off death from them” (13.781–783; see [[deiphobus]] and [[hector]]). He does not fight again in the poem.
