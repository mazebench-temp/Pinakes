grc: [νοστήσειεν, νοστήσαντα]

passages:
  - 13.231-238 | Poseidon as Thoas to Idomeneus
---

**A curse.** Idomeneus has told the disguised god that no Achaean shrinks from the war. Poseidon answers with a curse on any man who does: “Idomeneus, may that man never return home / from Troy, but stay here and become a plaything for dogs, / whoever on this day of his own will holds back from fighting” (μὴ κεῖνος ἀνὴρ ἔτι νοστήσειεν, 13.232–234; see [[retreat]] and [[dogs]]). The homecoming that every man at Troy hopes for is the thing the coward is to lose, and the death he would escape by holding back is to come to him in its worst form, unburied.

**No return.** Harpalion, son of the Paphlagonian king Pylaemenes, “had followed his dear father to make war / at Troy, and did not come back again to his native land” (οὐδʼ αὖτις ἀφίκετο πατρίδα γαῖαν, 13.644–645; see [[harpalion]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). The narrator tells the end of his life before he tells his death, as he will tell of Protesilaus' ship in Book 15, “which brought Protesilaus / to Troy, and did not take him back to his native land” (15.705–706; see [[protesilaus]]). Neither man's homecoming is ever in question; the verse closes it before the story reaches it.

**The god's return.** At the start of the book Poseidon leaves his horses in a cave of the sea between Tenedos and Imbros, hobbled with golden hobbles, “so that they might stay there steadfast / until their lord returned” (νοστήσαντα ἄνακτα, 13.37–38; see [[poseidon]] and [[chariot-journey]]). The god's return is certain; it is the men's that the book takes away.
