**The god from behind.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, strikes the two Ajaxes with his staff and darts away like a hawk (13.43–65; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The lesser Ajax sees through the disguise:

{{quote:838178da-bd0d-5175-996d-65f28e89b9cd}}

What he knew the god by is disputed. ἴχνια (13.71) is elsewhere a track or a footprint, and the words can mean that he knew the god by the prints he left; with κνημάων, “of the legs”, the translation takes them of the god's gait, “the movements of his feet and legs” (see the translation's note on 13.71). Either way he knows him “from behind, as he went away”, as in the Odyssey Telemachus knows Athena only when she flies off like a bird, and Nestor when she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle (Od. 1.319–323, 3.371–379; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). ἀρίγνωτοι δὲ θεοί περ, “gods are easy to know”, is said of a god who has just come and gone in a seer's likeness. In the Odyssey Odysseus says the opposite: no one could see a god going this way or that against his will (Od. 10.573–574), and Athena is hard for a mortal to know, since she takes every likeness (Od. 13.312–313).

The god's gift is in his next words. Poseidon “made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above” (13.61), and the son of Oileus feels it: “my feet below and my hands above are quivering” (13.75). The same verse tells how Athena lightened Diomedes in answer to his prayer (5.122), and how in the footrace of Book 23 she lightens Odysseus, who passes this Ajax when he slips in the dung of the sacrificed cattle (23.772–777).

**The head of Imbrius.** When the two Ajaxes have carried off and stripped the body of Imbrius, whom Teucer killed (13.197–202; see [[imbrius]] and [[teucer]]), the lesser Ajax acts alone: “and his head from his tender neck / the son of Oileus cut off, in his anger for Amphimachus, / and with a swing he sent it flying like a ball through the throng; / and it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet” (13.202–205). Hector has just killed Amphimachus, a leader of the Epeians and Poseidon's grandson (13.183–187, 13.206–207; see [[amphimachus]]); the head falls at the feet of the killer. σφαιρηδόν, “like a ball”, occurs only here in Homer (see [[like-a-ball]]); in the next book Peneleos holds up the head of Ilioneus on his spear (14.496–500).

**The yoke.** Where Hector broke in, “Ajax, the swift son of Oileus, no longer stood at all / apart from Telamonian Ajax, not even a little”, and the two strain together like oxen at one plow (13.701–708; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]] and [[ajax]]). The phrase Ὀϊλῆος ταχὺς υἱός, “the swift son of Oileus”, returns in Book 14, when he kills the most Trojans in the rout, “for there was none like him to pursue men in flight” (14.520–522; see [[swift]]). His Locrians do not follow him into the close fight, “for their hearts did not stand firm in close battle” (13.713): with bows and slings instead of helmets, shields and spears, they shoot from behind the armed men (13.712–722; see [[locrians]] and [[sling]]). The leader fits the Catalogue, which made him small and gave him a linen corselet, “but with the spear he surpassed all the Panhellenes and Achaeans” (2.529–530; see [[with-linen-corselet]]); he fights in front while his men shoot from behind. The ships “of Ajax” beside which they fight (13.681) may be his; the poem does not say, and the critics whom Strabo reports took them for Telamonian Ajax's (Strabo 9.1.10; see [[ajax]]).

**His brother.** His bastard brother Medon, “the brother of Ajax” (13.695), leads the Phthians with Podarces in the same defense (13.693–697; see [[medon]], [[oileus]] and [[phthians]]).
