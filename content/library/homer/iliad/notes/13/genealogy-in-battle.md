**Unasked.** Idomeneus has killed his third man, Alcathous, and he shouts to Deiphobus: “Deiphobus, shall we then think it a fair exchange, / three killed for one, since you boast like this? / Strange one, come and stand against me yourself” (13.446–448). In the meetings of Books 6, 20 and 21 one man asks who the other is, and the other answers with his lineage before they fight (see the entry). Here no one asks, and the genealogy is a challenge and a boast in one (see [[androktasia]]). The reckoning of three for one answers Deiphobus' boast over Hypsenor, that Asius goes to Hades with an escort (13.414–416; see [[deiphobus]]). Then the lineage:

**From Zeus.** Like the genealogies of Glaucus and Aeneas, Idomeneus' runs down from its source, here in three verses, a generation to each, each with the verb “fathered” (τέκε, τέκετο, τίκτε): from Zeus through Minos, “a guardian over Crete”, and Deucalion to Idomeneus himself (13.450–452; see [[zeus]], [[minos]], [[deucalion]] and [[crete]]). The last words bring the line to Troy, where the ships have brought him “to be an evil to you and to your father and to the other Trojans” (13.453–454; see [[idomeneus]]). Tlepolemus told Sarpedon that men lie when they call him “the offspring of aegis-bearing Zeus” (Διὸς γόνον αἰγιόχοιο, 5.635); Idomeneus claims the descent, three generations back. In the Odyssey the disguised Odysseus claims the same line, calling himself Aethon, a younger son of Deucalion and brother of Idomeneus (Od. 19.178–184).

**No answer.** Deiphobus does not reply. He ponders whether to fight Idomeneus alone or to find a companion, and goes for Aeneas (13.455–467; see [[pondering-two-ways]], [[decision-scene]] and [[aeneas]]).
