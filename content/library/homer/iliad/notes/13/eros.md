---
summary: In Menelaus' complaint to Zeus, ἔρος is the desire that a man would rather “put away”, as at the end of a meal, for sleep, love, song and dancing than for war.
---

The formula that closes a Homeric meal, “when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (ἐξ ἔρον ἕντο, 1.469 and often; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]), is turned to a new use in Menelaus' complaint over Peisander. Men have their fill of sleep and love, of sweet song and flawless dancing, “of which a man would rather have his fill / than of war” (τῶν πέρ τις καὶ μᾶλλον ἐέλδεται ἐξ ἔρον εἷναι / ἢ πολέμου, 13.638–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]). To put away the desire is to have had enough. ἐέλδεται, “wishes, longs”, adds a second word of wanting: a man longs to come to the end of his desire for these pleasures sooner than for war, and the Trojans never come to the end of theirs (see [[himeros]]).

Once more in the Iliad the phrase is used outside the meal. Priam, setting out for Achilles' hut, says that Achilles may kill him if he will, once he has held his son in his arms, “when I have put away my desire for lamentation” (γόου ἐξ ἔρον εἵην, 24.226–227).
