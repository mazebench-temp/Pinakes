summary: Zeus turns his eyes from Troy, sure that no god will break his ban, and Poseidon breaks it in secret. In the likeness of men he rouses the Achaeans, strikes the two Ajaxes with his staff and makes their limbs light, binds Alcathous for Idomeneus' spear, guards Antilochus and breaks Adamas' spear-point; Zeus, from afar, keeps death from two Trojans and rouses the allies from Ascania.

**The ban, and its breach.** Zeus, having brought Hector to the ships, turns his shining eyes to the lands of the Thracians and the Mysians, “for he did not expect in his heart that any of the immortals / would come to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.7–9). The last verse repeats the terms of his ban at the start of Book 8, against any god who goes “to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (8.10–11; see the note on Book 8). Poseidon, watching from Samothrace, breaks it, and the narrator gives the reason he does so in secret: “Zeus was born first and knew more. / Therefore he shrank from giving help openly, / but secretly he kept rousing the army, in the likeness of a man” (13.355–357; see [[poseidon]], [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[nemesis]]). The help stays hidden until, in the next book, Sleep comes to tell Poseidon that Zeus is asleep and to help the Danaans with a ready heart while he sleeps (14.354–360), and the god leads the Achaeans himself (14.362–387); when Zeus wakes, he sends Iris to order him out of the battle (15.157–219).

**In a man's likeness.** Poseidon comes first as Calchas, “in form and in tireless voice” (13.45), and later in the voice of Thoas (13.216), and the poem shows what each disguise allows. As the seer he speaks to the two Ajaxes and to the leaders in the rear with a seer's authority (13.47–58, 13.95–124); as Thoas, an Aetolian lord, he speaks to Idomeneus as one fighter to another (13.219–238). Only the son of Oileus sees through him, by his gait as he leaves (13.66–72; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**Strength put in.** He strikes the two Ajaxes with his staff, fills them with strong might, “and made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above” (13.59–61; see [[menos]]). The verse is Athena's gift to Diomedes when she answers his prayer (5.122), and she will give it again to Odysseus in the footrace (23.772). The god's touch meets men who are ready already. The narrator says that the Ajaxes were “already eager themselves” when he spoke to them (μεμαῶτε καὶ αὐτώ, 13.46), and that Idomeneus, when the god met him, “was still eager to meet / the war” (13.214–215): the god urges, and the men go where they were going (see the article above).

**Hands on the fighting.** Three times the god acts in the fighting itself. He beats Alcathous down “at the hands of Idomeneus, / bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs”, so that he can neither flee nor dodge, and stands like a pillar for the spear (13.434–438; see [[alcathous]] and [[like-a-pillar]]); elsewhere it is μοῖρα that “fetters” a man for death (πέδησε, 4.517; see [[moira]]). He guards Antilochus among the missiles (13.554–555), and when Adamas' spear strikes Antilochus' shield he makes its point powerless, “begrudging him the other's life”, so that half of it stays in the shield like a fire-hardened stake (13.560–565; see [[antilochus]] and [[adamas]]). And late in the day he urges on the Argives so hard, “and himself defended them with his strength”, that the glory would have been theirs (13.677–679; see [[kydos]]).

**Zeus at a distance.** Zeus acts from afar, and the mortals name him. Hector is sure that the husband of Hera has roused him (13.153–154), and Poseidon, as Calchas, says that the Olympian himself may be rousing him (13.58). Paris tells Hector that the son of Cronus kept off death from Deiphobus and Helenus (13.783), and the narrator says that Zeus roused the men from Ascania to fight (13.794). Zeus' counsels keep Ares and the other gods on Olympus, shut out from the war, so that Ares does not know his son has fallen (13.521–525; see [[ares]]). The narrator joins the two gods in one image: the brothers stretch the rope of strife and war over both armies (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).
