summary: Poseidon fills the two Ajaxes “with strong might”, and Ajax feels it rise; the Achaeans speak three times of “the might and the hands” of fighting men; Idomeneus does not let his great might slacken; mighty Ares lets the might of a spear go; and Menelaus calls the might of the Trojans “always reckless”.
grc: [μενεαίνετε]
---

**Put in by a god.** Poseidon strikes the two Ajaxes with his staff, “and filled them with strong might, / and made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above” (πλῆσεν μένεος κρατεροῖο, 13.59–61; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The son of Oileus knows the god by his gait and feels his own heart eager and his feet and hands quivering (13.66–75); Telamonian Ajax answers that his hands quiver around his spear, “and my might has risen” (καί μοι μένος ὤρορε, 13.77–80). The might that a god puts in comes over a man as his own (see [[divine-intervention]]).

**Might and hands.** μένος καὶ χεῖρες, “might and hands”, is the measure of a fighting man, and Book 13 uses it three times. The Trojans, Poseidon says, would not await “the might and the hands of the Achaeans” before this (13.105); in the ambush no one could make light of Meriones' “might and … hands” (13.287); and Hector will find it a steep climb “to overcome their might and their irresistible hands”, the hands of the two Ajaxes and Teucer, and burn the ships (13.317–320). The last turns round Asius' complaint in Book 12, that the Achaeans would not hold back “our might and our irresistible hands” (12.166; see the note on Book 12 and [[irresistible-hands]]). The phrase comes elsewhere when a man escapes another's might and hands (6.502, 7.309), when the Achaeans fear that they cannot hold back Hector's (17.637–639), and when Agamemnon says that Zeus has bound their own (14.73).

**Unslackened.** Idomeneus, after Hypsenor falls, “did not let his great might slacken, but was eager always / either to cover some one of the Trojans in murky night / or himself to fall with a thud, warding off ruin from the Achaeans” (οὐ λῆγε μένος μέγα, 13.424–426; see [[idomeneus]] and [[loigos]]).

**The might of a spear.** When Idomeneus kills Alcathous, the spear stands fixed in his heart, and the heart, still beating, shakes its butt; “and then mighty Ares let its might go” (ἀφίει μένος ὄβριμος Ἄρης, 13.442–444). In the other two places of the verse a thrown spear sticks quivering in the ground (16.612–613, 17.528–529), so that the μένος is the spear's force, which Ares, the power of war in the weapon, at last lets go; some take it as the dying man's strength (see the translation's note on 13.443–444 and [[ares]]).

**Reckless might.** Menelaus, standing over Peisander, complains to Zeus that he favors men who are arrogant, “the Trojans, whose might is always reckless” (τῶν μένος αἰὲν ἀτάσθαλον, 13.633–634; see [[atasthalie]] and [[hybris]]), and he reproaches them for being eager now, “among the seafaring ships, / to throw deadly fire” (μενεαίνετε, 13.628–629). The verb belongs with the noun, as in the earlier books (see the note on Book 3).
