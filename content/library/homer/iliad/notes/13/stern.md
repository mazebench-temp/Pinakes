summary: The day of battle that Zeus foretold “at the sterns of the ships” has come. When Idomeneus and Meriones reach the left, “the quarrel stood even among them at the sterns of the ships” (13.333), and Hector finds some of his leaders lying dead “at the sterns of the ships of the Achaeans” (13.762).
grc: ["=πρυμνῇσι", "=πρυμνῇσιν"]
---

The ships were beached with their sterns toward the plain, so to fight at the sterns is to fight for the ships themselves (see the entry and [[ships]]). In Book 8 Zeus foretold the fight “at the sterns” (8.475), and in Book 12 he kept his son Sarpedon from dying there (12.403). In Book 13 the Trojans have crossed the wall, and the phrase marks where the battle is. When the Trojans see Idomeneus and Meriones and all come at them, “the quarrel stood even among them at the sterns of the ships” (ἐπὶ πρυμνῇσι νέεσσιν, 13.333; see [[battle-order]] and [[the-armies-clash]]). When Hector goes looking for his leaders, he finds that “some at the sterns of the ships of the Achaeans / lay dead at the hands of the Argives, having lost their lives” (νηυσὶν ἔπι πρυμνῇσιν, 13.762–763; see [[hector]]).

Here πρυμνός is the adjective, “hindmost”, with the ships: “at the hindmost parts of the ships”, the sterns. The same adjective gives the top of Deiphobus' arm (πρυμνοῖο βραχίονος, 13.532) and the roots of the oxen's horns in the simile of the two Ajaxes (πρυμνοῖσιν κεράεσσι, 13.705). The phrase returns in Books 14, 15 and 19, as the battle at the sterns goes on (14.51, 14.65, 15.248, 15.722, 19.135).
