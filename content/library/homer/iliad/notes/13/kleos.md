summary: Othryoneus had come to Troy “at the report of the war”, as Iphidamas came at the report of the Achaeans; each is bound to a bride he will not live with, and each is killed by an Achaean king. Idomeneus fears the opposite of fame for the Achaeans, that they will perish at Troy “nameless”.
passages:
  - 13.363-382 | Othryoneus and Cassandra
---

**At the report.** Idomeneus' first victim on the left is Othryoneus of Cabesus, “who had come not long before at the report of the war” (ὅς ῥα νέον πολέμοιο μετὰ κλέος εἰληλούθει, 13.364; see [[othryoneus]] and [[cabesus]]). κλέος here is report, the news that travels (see the article above). The phrase is used once more, of Iphidamas, son of Antenor, whom his grandfather married to his own daughter in Thrace and who left the bridal chamber and “came at the report of the Achaeans” with twelve ships (μετὰ κλέος ἵκετʼ Ἀχαιῶν, 11.221–228; see [[iphidamas]] and the translation's note on 11.221–228). The two stories have the same shape. Each young man came to Troy because of what he heard, each is bound to a bride he will not live with, and each is killed by an Achaean king, Iphidamas by Agamemnon and Othryoneus by Idomeneus. They differ in the price. Iphidamas had given a hundred cattle for his wife and promised a thousand goats and sheep, and died “far from his wife, … / his wedded wife, from whom he had seen no joy, though he had given much” (11.241–245); Othryoneus asked for Cassandra “without bride-price”, promising instead to drive the Achaeans from Troy (13.365–369; see [[cassandra]] and [[bride-price]]).

**Nameless.** The opposite of κλέος is namelessness. Idomeneus, asked by Poseidon in Thoas' likeness where the Achaeans' threats have gone, answers that it must be the pleasure of Zeus “that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (νωνύμνους, 13.227; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). The verse is Polydamas' wish in Book 12 and Agamemnon's fear in Book 14 (12.70, 14.70). To perish at Troy without a name is what the Achaeans fear; in the Odyssey Athena tells Telemachus that the gods have not made his line “nameless hereafter”, since Penelope bore such a son (*Odyssey* 1.222–223; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
