summary: Poseidon bids the two Ajaxes remember their courage and reproaches the Achaean leaders for letting go of it; Meriones says that he has not forgotten his, Idomeneus stands his ground like a boar “trusting in its courage”, and Paris claims that his mother did not bear him wholly without it. The book ends with the Argives, who “did not forget their courage”.

en: [valiant]

**Remembered and let go.** Poseidon's first words to the two Ajaxes set the choice in two genitives: “you two will save the army of the Achaeans / if you remember your courage, and not chilling rout” (ἀλκῆς μνησαμένω, μὴ δὲ κρυεροῖο φόβοιο, 13.47–48; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The rally-cry of the poem is “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (6.112, 8.174, 15.487 and often); here it is spoken in the dual, and its alternative is named. To the leaders in the rear he says the reverse, “you no longer do well to let go of your furious courage, / all of you, the best men in the army” (13.116–117), as Agamemnon told the Argives not to let it go (4.234) and Sarpedon rebuked the Lycians for doing so (12.409; see the note on Book 12 and [[furious]]). The book keeps the account. Meriones answers Idomeneus that he too has not forgotten his courage (οὐδʼ ἐμέ φημι λελασμένον ἔμμεναι ἀλκῆς, 13.269); the Trojans, shot at from behind by the Locrians, “remembered nothing” of the joy of battle (13.721–722; see [[charme]]); and the last lines leave the Argives who “did not forget / their courage, but awaited the best of the Trojans as they came on” (13.835–836). In the next books it is forgotten: when Apollo shakes the aegis, the Achaeans “forgot their furious courage” (15.322), and so do the Trojans when Patroclus drives them from the ships (16.357).

**The deer and the boar.** Poseidon's contempt for the Trojans of old is a simile. They were like deer, the prey of jackals, leopards and wolves, “straying aimlessly, without courage” (ἀνάλκιδες, 13.102–104; see [[timid-deer]] and [[deer]]). Idomeneus, waiting for Aeneas, is the opposite beast: “he stood his ground like a boar in the mountains, trusting in its courage” (ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, 13.471; see [[the-bristling-boar]]). The half-verse goes with the lions and boars of similes (5.299, 17.61, 17.728; *Odyssey* 6.130), and once with Hector holding his ground over Patroclus' body (18.158). The likeness is an old one for Idomeneus. In Book 4 Agamemnon found him among the champions “like a boar in courage” (συῒ εἴκελος ἀλκήν, 4.253); here the Trojans see him come “like a flame in his courage” (φλογὶ εἴκελον ἀλκήν, 13.330; see [[idomeneus]]).

**The coward and the valiant.** In the ambush Idomeneus describes, “the coward and the valiant man are shown for what they are” (ὅ τε δειλὸς ἀνὴρ ὅς τʼ ἄλκιμος, 13.278; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[deos]]). ἄλκιμος, “valiant”, is the adjective of ἀλκή, and δειλός, “cowardly”, the adjective of fear.

**Not wholly without courage.** ἄναλκις is an insult, and the man who hears it appeals to his birth. When Hector rebukes him, Paris answers, “my mother did not bear me wholly without courage either” (ἐπεὶ οὐδʼ ἐμὲ πάμπαν ἀνάλκιδα γείνατο μήτηρ, 13.777), as Diomedes, rising in the next book, says that no one can call his race “base and without courage” (κακὸν καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, 14.126). Paris ends with a promise and a limit: “I say we will not fall short / in courage, as far as our strength allows” (13.785–786; see [[beyond-ones-strength]] and [[paris]]).
