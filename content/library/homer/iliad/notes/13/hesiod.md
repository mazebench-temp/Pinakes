---
summary: Hesiod's poems set Book 13 against another account of the gods and heroes: the Theogony makes Zeus the youngest of Rhea's children, where Poseidon defers to him as first-born, uses the words of Poseidon's “blind watch” of Cronus watching for his children, and makes Rout a son of Ares and Aphrodite. The Catalogue of Women gives the Moliones Poseidon for their father, sends Idomeneus, “son of Deucalion, offspring of Minos”, to woo Helen, and names the mare-milking Scythians and the milk-drinkers whom Strabo set beside Homer's.
---

- **Born first.** Poseidon helps the Achaeans only in secret because Zeus “was born first and knew more” (13.355; see [[poseidon]] and [[proclus]]). The *Theogony* reverses the order. Rhea bore to Cronus Hestia, Demeter and Hera, Hades, the loud-crashing Earth-shaker, and Zeus (453–458). Cronus swallowed each child as it was born, and Rhea, about to bear “the youngest of her children”, great Zeus (ὁπλότατον παίδων, 478), was sent to Crete to hide him; when Cronus was tricked into bringing up his children again, he vomited first the stone “which he had swallowed last” (497). The Iliad makes Zeus the elder elsewhere too (15.165–166, 15.182; the Erinyes follow the elder, 15.204), and there Poseidon says that the three sons of Cronus divided everything by lot (15.187–193); in the *Theogony*, after the war with the Titans, the gods urge Zeus at Earth's prompting to be their king, and he divides their honors among them (881–885). Homer's Zeus is the elder and “knew more”. Hesiod's gets his wisdom by swallowing his first wife, Metis, “who knew most of gods and mortal men”, so that the goddess might counsel him on good and evil (886–900; see [[metis]]).
- **No blind watch.** “But the powerful earth-shaker kept no blind watch” (οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε, 13.10). Homer uses the verse also of Apollo (10.515), of Poseidon again (14.135) and of Ares (*Odyssey* 8.285), each time of a god who sees what another god is doing. The *Theogony* has the same words, unjoined, of Cronus waiting for his children to be born: “therefore he kept no blind watch (οὐκ ἀλαὸς σκοπιὴν ἔχεν), but watched closely and swallowed his children” (466–467). The ancient commentary explains the compound as “a vain watch, for ἀλαός in dialect is the blind man” (scholia on 13.10).
- **Rout.** Idomeneus and Meriones go out like Ares and Rout (Φόβος), “his dear son, strong and fearless”, who routs even the steadfast (13.298–303; see [[ares-and-rout]]). Homer does not name Rout's mother. In the *Theogony* Aphrodite bore to Ares Rout and Terror (Φόβον καὶ Δεῖμον), dread gods who throw the close ranks of men into confusion in chilling war, with Ares, sacker of cities, and Harmonia besides (933–937; see [[rout]] and [[terror]]).
- **Poseidon's grandson.** Poseidon is angry “for his grandson” when Hector kills Amphimachus, son of Cteatus (13.206–207; see [[amphimachus]]). The poem calls Cteatus and his brother “of Actor's line” (2.621, 13.185), yet when Nestor nearly killed them it was “their father, the wide-ruling earth-shaker” who saved them (11.750–752). Hesiod, a scholium on 11.750 reports, gave them the same double parentage: he made the Moliones sons of Actor and Molione in name, but of Poseidon by birth (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 9 Evelyn-White; see [[the-moliones]]).
- **The house of Minos.** Idomeneus boasts that Zeus fathered Minos, “a guardian over Crete”, Minos fathered Deucalion, and Deucalion fathered him (13.449–453; see [[minos]] and [[deucalion]]). In the *Catalogue* Europa bears Minos to Zeus (fr. 19 Evelyn-White), and in its list of Helen's suitors “from Crete the great might of Idomeneus wooed her, son of Deucalion, offspring of far-famed Minos”; he sent no go-between, but went himself in his black ship over the sea (fr. 68 Evelyn-White, from a papyrus, the name partly restored). “The great might of Idomeneus”, μέγα σθένος Ἰδομενῆος, is the periphrasis of 13.248, σθένος Ἰδομενῆος, “mighty Idomeneus” (see [[idomeneus]]).
- **The mare-milkers.** Strabo answers Apollodorus' claim that Homer invented the far peoples of 13.5–6 with two Hesiodic verses: “Ethiopians and Ligurians and mare-milking Scythians”, which Eratosthenes had quoted (*Geography* 7.3.7; *Catalogue of Women* fr. 40 Evelyn-White), and, from Ephorus, the verse of the so-called *Circuit of the Earth* in which the Harpies carry Phineus “to the land of the milk-drinkers, who have wagons for their homes” (*Geography* 7.3.9; fr. 39). A papyrus of the *Catalogue* has the first verse with “Libyans” in place of “Ligurians”, in the flight of the Harpies round the ends of the earth (Oxyrhynchus Papyrus 1358, fr. 2, in Evelyn-White's appendix). See [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]] and [[abii]].
