summary: The contradiction was already argued over in antiquity. Aristophanes of Byzantium rejected the verse of the father's tears; Aristarchus would either reject it or take the father for another Pylaemenes, a king where the dead man of Book 5 was a leader; others read “and their father did not go with them”, or made the mourner the father's ghost.
passages:
  - 13.643-659 | Harpalion's death
---

The ancient commentators knew the problem well. A critical sign in the margin of 13.643, where Harpalion is called “son of king Pylaemenes”, pointed to “the inquiry about Pylaemenes” (scholium A on 13.643), and their notes on the passage record four answers.

- **Reject the verses.** Aristophanes of Byzantium rejected 13.658, the father's tears, and Aristarchus said that one must either reject it or take the father for another man of the same name (scholium T on 13.658). In the Venetus A both 13.658 and 13.659 are marked as spurious. Someone, the note explains, misled by “who had followed his dear father” (13.644), added them so that the father too should mourn his son; but that verse means that Harpalion followed his father when he first came from home, as the words that follow show, “to make war / at Troy, and did not come back again to his native land” (13.644–645; scholium A on 13.658–659). Some rejected 13.657 as well, since Homer has no other dead man carried off on a chariot; others answered that a chariot was needed because the fighting was at the ships, far from the city (scholia on 13.657).
- **Two men of one name.** “If these verses remain,” the same note goes on, “one must understand a homonymy”: the poet has two Pylaemenes, leaders of the Paphlagonians, as he has two Ajaxes and two heralds named Eurybates, Agamemnon's and Odysseus' (scholium A on 13.658–659). Other notes give a longer list, the two Phocian leaders called Schedius, sons of Perimedes and of Iphitus, both killed by Hector, the article's own example; two charioteers called Eurymedon, Agamemnon's and Nestor's; three Trojans called Adrastus, three called Thoon and three called Melanippus; and two called Peisander, killed by Agamemnon and by Menelaus. And they tell the two men apart by their titles: the Pylaemenes of Book 5 is a leader (ἀρχὸν Παφλαγόνων, 5.577), this one a king (βασιλῆος, 13.643). Besides, if Harpalion had been the son of the man killed in Book 5, it would have been most unreasonable for him not to be seen fighting over his father (scholia on 13.643; see [[schedius]], [[eurybates]] and [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]]).
- **Change the text.** “Some plausibly rewrite: μετὰ δʼ οὔ σφι πατὴρ κίε δάκρυα λείβων”, “and their father did not go with them, letting tears fall” (scholium A on 13.658–659). A single negative turns the contradiction into a pointed absence.
- **The father's ghost.** Some took the mourner for the father's ψυχή, his shade (scholium T on 13.658).

The same note observes that the poet, rightly for the moment, gives the father no words here, and keeps a father's lament over a dead son for another place, for Hector (scholium T on 13.658). Our text prints both verses, and the translation renders them as they stand (see the translation's note on 13.643–659, [[pylaemenes]] and [[harpalion]]).
