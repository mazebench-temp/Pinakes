summary: Book 13 is Idomeneus' great day. Roused by Poseidon in the likeness of Thoas, he arms and goes out with Meriones, whom he tells how the brave man shows himself in an ambush; on the left of the ships he kills Othryoneus, Asius and Alcathous, mocks Othryoneus' bargain and tells Deiphobus his descent from Zeus, and he stands against the younger Aeneas until his legs fail him and he gives ground step by step.

**Roused.** Poseidon, angry for his grandson Amphimachus, goes along the huts and ships (13.206–209; see [[poseidon]], [[amphimachus]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Idomeneus, “famed for his spear” (13.210; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]), is coming from a companion “struck behind the knee by the sharp bronze”; he has given orders to the healers and is going to his hut, “for he was still eager to meet the war” (13.211–215; see [[healing]]). The god speaks to him in the voice of Thoas: “Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have your threats gone, the threats the sons of the Achaeans used to make against the Trojans?” (13.219–220; see [[thoas]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). Idomeneus answers that “no man now is to blame, as far as I know” (13.222–223; see [[who-is-to-blame]]): it must be the pleasure of Zeus that the Achaeans perish here, “nameless, far from Argos” (13.226–227), the verse of Polydamas' wish (12.70) and of Agamemnon's fear (14.69–70). The god curses any man who holds back and bids him arm: “we two would know how to fight even against brave men” (13.232–238; see [[joined-prowess]]).

**The lightning.** He arms and goes out “like the lightning that the son of Cronus takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus, showing a sign to mortals” (13.240–245; see [[like-lightning]] and [[arming-scene]]). It is the shining start of his aristeia, the only one the Iliad gives him (see [[the-aristeia]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]); to the Trojans he is “like a flame in his courage” (13.330), as Hector was a flame in Poseidon's words (13.53; see [[like-a-flame]]).

**The ambush.** Meriones meets him near the hut, going for a spear (13.246–258; see [[meriones]]). Idomeneus has “one or twenty” Trojan spears, “which I take from the men I kill; for I do not mean to fight standing far off from hostile men” (13.260–265; see [[spear]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). When Meriones protests that he too fights among the foremost, Idomeneus cuts him short, “I know what you are in prowess” (13.275), and says where it would show:

{{quote:8a049d42-8348-5f14-b532-485942aedbc4}}

The ambush is where Achilles said Agamemnon never dared to go with the best of the Achaeans (1.226–228; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]], [[ambush]] and [[arete]]). A missile would strike Meriones in front, “as you pressed forward to the intimate talk of the champions” (13.288–291; see [[oaristys]]).

**To the left.** The two go out like Ares and his son Rout (13.295–305; see [[ares-and-rout]]). The two Ajaxes and Teucer hold the middle, and Telamonian Ajax would not give way even to Achilles in the standing fight; so “steer us this way, to the left of the army” (13.312–327; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[ajax]]).

**Three for one.** “Then, though he was half gray, calling on the Danaans, Idomeneus leapt among the Trojans and roused rout among them” (13.361–362). μεσαιπόλιος, “half gray”, is found only here (see [[half-gray]]). He kills Othryoneus, Cassandra's suitor, and mocks the bargain: the Achaeans will give him Agamemnon's most beautiful daughter if he will sack Ilios with them, “for we are not stingy with bride-gifts” (13.363–382; see [[othryoneus]], [[cassandra]] and [[bride-price]]). Asius, coming to the rescue on foot in front of his horses, takes the spear in the throat, the death the narrator foretold (12.113–117), and falls like a tree felled for ship-timber (13.383–393; see [[asius]] and [[the-felled-tree]]). Deiphobus' spear flies over Idomeneus' round shield and strikes down Hypsenor (13.402–416; see [[deiphobus]] and [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]]). Poseidon fetters Alcathous, who stands like a pillar while Idomeneus spears him through the heart (13.427–444; see [[alcathous]] and [[like-a-pillar]]). Then he answers Deiphobus' boast in the verse that brought it in (13.445 ≈ 13.413): is it a fair exchange, “three killed for one”? Let Deiphobus come and face him:

{{quote:0867d904-6ed6-5d4f-8f46-6c7e228e7a77}}

Meriones has called him Δευκαλίδη, “son of Deucalion” (13.307); now he gives the whole line (see [[minos]], [[deucalion]], [[crete]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**The younger man.** Deiphobus fetches Aeneas (13.455–469; see [[aeneas]]). “But flight did not seize Idomeneus as if he were a darling child” (13.470): he waits like a boar in the mountains (13.471–476; see [[the-bristling-boar]] and [[like-a-boar]]), and calls on Ascalaphus, Aphareus, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus (13.477–480):

{{quote:6342534f-b32b-578e-8dd2-224d171977fa}}

He fears a younger man, and says so (see [[kratos]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). Aeneas' spear misses; Idomeneus kills Oenomaus but cannot strip him under the missiles, for “his legs were no longer steady when he rushed, to dart after his own spear or to avoid a missile” (13.502–515; see [[oenomaus-the-trojan]]). He goes away “step by step”, and Deiphobus' last spear at him kills Ascalaphus (13.516–520; see [[ascalaphus]]). The book does not name him again.

**His titles.** δουρικλυτός, “famed for his spear” (13.210, 13.467, 13.476); Κρητῶν βουληφόρε, “counselor of the Cretans” (13.219, 13.255; see [[man-of-counsel]]); Κρητῶν ἀγός, “leader of the Cretans” (13.221, 13.259, 13.274, 13.311; see [[cretans]]); σθένος Ἰδομενῆος, literally “the might of Idomeneus”, rendered “mighty Idomeneus” (13.248); with Aeneas, ἀτάλαντοι Ἄρηϊ, “the equals of Ares” (13.500; see [[equal-of-ares]]).

**Afterward.** Hector asks Paris, “And where is Othryoneus?” (13.772; see [[hector]]). In Book 17 he comes into the fight on foot; Meriones' charioteer Coeranus drives up to save him and is killed by Hector's spear, and Idomeneus whips the horses to the ships, “for fear had fallen on his heart” (17.605–625). At the funeral games the son of Oileus tells him that he is not the youngest of the Argives, and that his eyes are not the sharpest (23.476–477; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
