---
summary: Grief in Book 13 is the feeling that moves men to fight. The Achaeans in the rear grieve and weep until Poseidon rouses them; Deiphobus throws at Idomeneus grieving for Asius; Deiphobus' boast grieves the Argives, and Antilochus, grieving, covers his companion; grief seizes Menelaus when Deipyrus falls; and the narrator imagines a man bold enough to watch the battle and not grieve.
grc: [ἀκάχοιτο]
---

**Grief into action.** The Achaeans in the rear, worn out, feel grief come into their hearts as they watch the Trojans over the wall, and weep (13.86–89; see [[tears]]); Poseidon's rebuke turns the grief to battle (13.95–135; see [[aidos]]). On the other side Deiphobus comes close to Idomeneus “grieving for Asius” and throws his spear (Ἀσίου ἀχνύμενος, 13.402–403; see [[deiphobus]]). His boast over Hypsenor answers it: “So he spoke, and grief came upon the Argives at his boasting, / and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Antilochus; / but even in his grief he did not neglect his companion” (13.417–419; see [[antilochus]] and [[euchomai]]). The first of those verses comes twice more in the next book, after the boasts of Polydamas and Acamas (14.458, 14.486). When Helenus kills Deipyrus, “grief seized the son of Atreus, Menelaus, good at the war cry”, and he goes at Helenus with threats (13.581–582; see [[menelaus]] and [[helenus]]). Last, the Paphlagonians carry Harpalion's body to Ilios “grieving” (ἀχνύμενοι, 13.656–658; see [[bereaved-parents]]).

**Not to grieve.** When the two armies close, bristling with spears and blinding with the gleam of bronze, the narrator imagines a watcher: “he would be very bold of heart / who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (οὐδʼ ἀκάχοιτο, 13.343–344; see [[watching-the-war]] and [[ponos]]). The sight is one that no man could see without grief. The same form comes once more in the Iliad, when Hera tells Poseidon that if the gods who help the Danaans would drive the Trojans back and hold off Zeus, he would sit grieving there alone on Ida (8.205–207).
