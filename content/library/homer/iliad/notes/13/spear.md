summary: Spears break in Book 13. Meriones' snaps at the socket on Deiphobus' shield, and he goes to the huts for another, where Idomeneus keeps Trojan spears, “one or twenty”, taken from men he has killed; Peisander's breaks on Menelaus' shield, and Poseidon makes Adamas' point powerless. A spear fixed in Alcathous' heart shakes with its beating, and Menelaus' spear pins Helenus' hand to his bow (13.159–168, 13.246–297, 13.442–444, 13.560–565, 13.593–609).

**Broken spears.** Meriones' cast at Deiphobus is good: it strikes the shield “and did not miss”, but long before it can drive through the bull's hide “the long spear snapped at the socket” (ἐν καυλῷ ἐάγη, 13.159–162; see [[deiphobus]] and [[shield]]). The καυλός is the place behind the point where the head is fitted to the shaft (16.115); a spear breaks there again when Peisander stabs Menelaus' shield (13.606–609) and in Book 17, nearly in the words of 13.162 (17.607; see the translation's note on 13.162, 13.608). Meriones is angry “on both counts, for the victory and for the spear that he had shattered”, and goes along the huts and ships to fetch a long spear he had left in his hut (13.165–168).

**Idomeneus' spears.** Meeting Idomeneus near the huts, Meriones explains that he is going for a spear, since he broke the one he had on Deiphobus' shield (13.255–258). Idomeneus answers:

{{quote:f3bf326d-6464-50be-b9e1-b56a0c9661df}}

καὶ ἓν καὶ εἴκοσι is “one or twenty”, as many as he likes (see the translation's note on 13.260). The spears are Τρώϊα, taken from men he has killed, the trophies of a man who fights close: “for I do not mean / to fight standing far off from hostile men” (13.262–263; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[hut]]). Meriones has spoils too, “but they are not near enough for me to take one” (13.268). After Idomeneus has spoken of the ambush, Meriones takes up a bronze spear from the hut and follows him (13.294–297; see [[ambush]]). Idomeneus himself took “two spears” when he armed (δοῦρε, 13.241; see [[arming-scene]]).

**Double-pointed.** The Achaeans in the close ranks stab at Hector “with swords and double-pointed spears” (ἔγχεσιν ἀμφιγύοισιν, 13.147). ἀμφίγυος is explained as “with a limb at either end”, a spear with a point at the head and a spike at the butt (σαυρωτήρ, 10.153), or as “pliant at both ends” (see the translation's note on 13.147). The phrase returns in the fighting at the ships and over Patroclus (14.26, 15.278, 15.386, 15.712, 16.637, 17.731).

**Hearts and hands.** Telamonian Ajax tells the son of Oileus, “my irresistible hands quiver around my spear” (13.77). Idomeneus stabs Alcathous in the middle of the chest, and the heart, still beating, shakes the butt of the spear fixed in it (οὐρίαχος), “and then mighty Ares let its might go” (13.442–444; see [[alcathous]]). The verse returns twice, each time after a spear has stuck quivering in the ground (16.612–613, 17.528–529), and so the might that Ares lets go is usually taken as the spear's: its force spent, it comes to rest (see the translation's note on 13.443–444 and [[ares]]). Menelaus' spear goes right through Helenus' hand into his bow, and Helenus draws back “with his hand hanging at his side; and the ash spear trailed along with it”, until Agenor draws it out (13.593–598; see [[helenus]] and [[sling]]).

**Poseidon and the point.** Adamas stabs the middle of Antilochus' shield, “but his spear-point was made powerless / by dark-haired Poseidon, who begrudged him the other's life”; half of the spear stays in the shield “like a fire-hardened stake”, and half lies on the ground (13.561–565; see [[adamas]], [[antilochus]] and [[poseidon]]). σκῶλος πυρίκαυστος, a stake hardened in the fire, occurs only here.

**Long spears.** The battle “bristled with the spears, / long and flesh-cutting, that they held” (13.339–340; see [[bronze]]), and the spears of the book are long, from Teucer's (13.177) to Hector's, “my long spear, which will tear your lily-white skin” (13.830). The Locrians, who fight with bows and slings, have no “ash spears” (μείλινα δοῦρα, 13.715; see [[locrians]]).
