---
summary: Zeus leaves the Trojans and Achaeans at the ships “to have toil and misery without ceasing” and turns his eyes away; Poseidon, after speaking to Idomeneus in the likeness of Thoas, “went back again into the toil of men”; and the narrator says that only a man very bold of heart could have rejoiced at the sight of the toil and not grieved.
passages:
  - 13.1-9 | Zeus turns his eyes away
---

**Left to toil.** The book opens with Zeus looking away. When he had brought the Trojans and Hector close to the ships, “he left them beside the ships to have toil and misery / without ceasing, and he himself turned his shining eyes away” (πόνον τʼ ἐχέμεν καὶ ὀϊζὺν / νωλεμέως, 13.2–3; see [[zeus]]), toward the lands of the Thracians, the Mysians, the mare-milkers and the Abii, for he did not expect that any god would come to help either side (13.4–9; see [[abii]]). The half-verse comes once more in Homer, in the Odyssey's simile for Odysseus weeping at Demodocus' song of Troy: he weeps like a woman who falls on the body of her husband, killed before his city, while the enemy beat her back and shoulders with their spears and lead her off into slavery “to have toil and misery” (*Odyssey* 8.523–530).

**Into the toil of men.** But Poseidon “kept no blind watch” (13.10), and he comes to the Achaeans in secret. When he has spoken to Idomeneus in the likeness of Thoas, “So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men” (ἔβη θεὸς ἂμ πόνον ἀνδρῶν, 13.239; see [[poseidon]], [[eidos]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The verse comes twice more, both times of Apollo, after he has roused Hector in the likeness of a man, of Asius, Hector's uncle, and of Mentes, leader of the Cicones (16.715–726, 17.71–82). In all three places a god disguised as a man has spoken his piece and goes back into the fighting.

**Toiling.** Idomeneus tells Meriones that if he were hit “as you toiled” (πονεύμενος, 13.288), the missile would strike him in front (see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). The verb is the fighter's work, as when Agamemnon, rebuking Diomedes, recalls what was said of his father Tydeus by “those who saw him at his work” (πονεύμενον, 4.370–375).

**The sight of the toil.** When the armies close, bristling with spears, the narrator imagines an onlooker: “he would be very bold of heart / who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (ἰδὼν πόνον, 13.343–344; see [[achos]] and [[watching-the-war]]). The god who left the armies to their toil turned his eyes away; the narrator says that no one with an ordinary heart could watch it and rejoice.
