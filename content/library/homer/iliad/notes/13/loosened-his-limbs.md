summary: The rope of war that Zeus and Poseidon stretch over the armies “loosened the knees of many” (13.360), and Deiphobus' spear “at once loosened” Hypsenor's knees (13.412); weariness loosens the limbs of the men in the rear (13.85); and Poseidon reverses the formula, fettering the shining limbs of Alcathous so that he cannot flee (13.435).
grc: ["γούνατʼ ἔλυσε", "γυῖα λέλυντο"]
en: [limbs were loosened]
---

**The rope.** The rope of strife and war that the two sons of Cronus stretch over both armies is “unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (ἄρρηκτόν τʼ ἄλυτόν τε, τὸ πολλῶν γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν, 13.360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). The verse turns on the verb: what cannot be loosed (ἄλυτον) loosens (ἔλυσεν) the knees. In the Odyssey the same words are said of the causes of the war: of Helen, “since she loosened the knees of many men”, and of the journey to Troy that Zeus devised, “which loosened the knees of many men” (Od. 14.68–69, 14.235–236).

**Hypsenor.** Deiphobus' spear strikes Hypsenor “in the liver beneath the midriff, and at once loosened his knees” (13.412), the verse of the death of Apisaon in Book 11 (11.579) and of another Apisaon in Book 17 (17.349). Yet two companions carry him off groaning, and whether he dies is not certain (13.420–423; see [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]] and the translation's note on 13.412–423).

**Weariness.** The men in the rear sit by the ships: “Their limbs were loosened by grievous weariness” (13.85), as the rowers' limbs are in a simile of Book 7 (7.6). Poseidon's staff makes the limbs of the two Ajaxes light (13.61), and the men of the rear take heart again at his rebuke (13.83–125; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Telamonian Ajax's companions take his shield when weariness and sweat come over his knees (13.709–711).

**Bound.** For Alcathous the god reverses the formula. Poseidon “beat down” Alcathous at the hands of Idomeneus, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (θέλξας ὄσσε φαεινά, πέδησε δὲ φαίδιμα γυῖα, 13.434–435), so that he could neither flee nor swerve, but stood motionless like a pillar or a high-leafed tree (13.436–438; see [[alcathous]] and [[like-a-pillar]]). The limbs that death loosens are first bound. πεδάω, “fetter”, is the verb of the golden hobbles that Poseidon cast around his horses' feet (πέδας, 13.36; see [[chariot-journey]]).
