---
summary: περὶ κῆρι, “with all the heart”, measures three strong feelings in Book 13, Poseidon's indignation with the Achaean leaders, his anger for his grandson, and the love of Anchises and his wife for their daughter; and the Locrians, who have no armor, have no heart for close battle.
---

**With all the heart.** The phrase περὶ κῆρι gives a feeling its full measure. Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, tells the Achaean leaders, “with you I am indignant with all my heart” (νεμεσσῶμαι περὶ κῆρι, 13.119; see [[nemesis]]); when his grandson Amphimachus falls, “then indeed Poseidon was angry with all his heart” (περὶ κῆρι … ἐχολώθη, 13.206; see [[cholos]] and [[amphimachus]]); and Hippodameia was the daughter “whom her father and lady mother loved with all their hearts / in their hall” (13.430; see [[fathers-and-daughters]]). In Book 4 Zeus used it of the city he loved most, “sacred Ilios was the most honored in my heart” (4.46), and Hera of her own cities, which he may sack “whenever they become hateful to your heart” (4.53; see the note on Book 4). In the last book it measures the gods' love for Peleus and for Hector (24.61, 24.423).

**No heart for it.** The Locrians follow the son of Oileus to Troy with bows and slings, and do not stand with him in the front, “for their hearts did not stand firm in close battle; / for they had no bronze-fitted helmets with horsehair crests, / nor had they well-rounded shields and ash spears” (οὐ γάρ σφι σταδίῃ ὑσμίνῃ μίμνε φίλον κῆρ, 13.713–715; see [[locrians]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The want of heart is explained by the want of armor, and the narrator does not blame them: shooting from behind, they break the Trojan battalions (13.716–722; see [[sling]] and [[bow]]).
