summary: Idomeneus thinks the Achaeans' defeat must be the pleasure of “the son of Cronus, supreme in might,” and arms like his lightning, and Paris says that he kept death from Deiphobus and Helenus; in the middle of the book the narrator names Zeus and Poseidon together as “the two mighty sons of Cronus.”
grc: [Κρόνου υἷε]
en: [sons of Cronus]
---

**Idomeneus.** Answering Poseidon in Thoas' likeness, Idomeneus finds no man to blame: “but so, I think, it must be the pleasure of the son of Cronus, supreme in might, that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (13.225–227; [[supreme-in-might]], [[aitios]]). When he has armed, he goes out “like the lightning that the son of Cronus takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus” (13.242–243; [[like-lightning]], [[gleaming-olympus]]), and he tells Meriones that Hector will not burn the ships against the Ajaxes and Teucer “unless the son of Cronus himself casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships” (13.319–320; [[swift-ships]]).

**The two brothers.** “And the two mighty sons of Cronus, with their minds set on different sides, were making grievous sorrows for the heroes” (13.345–346; [[zeus]], [[poseidon]], [[cronus]]): Zeus wills victory to the Trojans, Poseidon helps the Argives in secret (13.347–360; [[the-rope-of-war]]). The dual Κρόνου υἷε, which names the brothers as sons of one father, is added to the entry's forms; the next lines say which of them is the elder (13.354–355).

**Paris.** Paris tells Hector that Deiphobus and Helenus have gone away wounded in the hand, “but the son of Cronus kept off death from them” (13.781–783; [[paris]], [[deiphobus]], [[helenus]]).
