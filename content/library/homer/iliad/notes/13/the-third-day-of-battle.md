---
summary: Book 13 is the third stage of the day, the first part of the battle at the ships. It has no dawn and marks no hour; Zeus, having brought Hector to the ships, looks away, Poseidon rallies the Achaeans in secret, and the book ends with the two armies shouting at each other, the battle still going on.
---

**Zeus' promise, half kept.** “Now when Zeus had brought the Trojans and Hector close to the ships” (13.1): the first part of the promise sent to Hector by Iris, power to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships” (11.193), is fulfilled, and Zeus turns his eyes away. He expects no god to help either side (13.8–9), and the second verse of his reason is nearly the verse in which he forbade it at the start of the second day of battle, “to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.9 ≈ 8.11; see [[zeus]]). The other gods are still held on Olympus “by the counsels of Zeus” (13.524–525; see [[ares]]). The narrator states the purpose of the day at the center of the book: Zeus wants victory for the Trojans and Hector, “to give glory to Achilles”, but not the destruction of the Achaean army (13.347–350; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Poseidon's help keeps the Achaeans from that destruction while Zeus is not looking (see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]).

**Yesterday and today.** The book counts back to the second day of battle. Polydamas fears that the Achaeans may “pay back yesterday's / debt” (13.745–746; see [[polydamas]]), their rout in Book 8, and the men of Ascania had come as relief “on the morning before” (13.793–794; see [[ascania]]). Hector answers Ajax's challenge with the end of the wish he made on the evening of that day, when he spoke of what the next sunrise would bring: then he wished to be “immortal and ageless”, now the son of Zeus and Hera, “as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (13.825–828 ≈ 8.538–541; see [[hector]]).

**Into Book 14.** The book ends with the shout of both armies reaching “the upper air and the rays of Zeus” (13.837), and the next book begins with Nestor hearing the din in his hut (14.1). The narrative does not pause: Zeus sleeps and wakes on Ida within the same day (14.352–353, 15.4–5).
