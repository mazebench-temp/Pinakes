summary: Protesilaus, dead since the landing, is named in Book 13 by his ships. Hector holds on where he first broke in, “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were”, below the lowest stretch of the wall, and Protesilaus' brother Podarces leads his men in their defense (13.679–684, 13.693–700).
passages:
  - 13.679-684 | Where Hector broke in
---

The dead man's ships keep his name. Hector, not knowing that his men are being cut down on the left, holds on where he first leapt in at the gates and the wall (13.674–680):

{{quote:2ee8bd5c-9bed-5dc1-a342-497d29cf0b31}}

The ships are drawn up θῖνʼ ἔφʼ ἁλὸς πολιῆς, “on the shore of the gray sea”; the man who led them there was killed as he leapt from his ship at the landing, “by far the first of the Achaeans” (2.701–702; see [[the-death-of-protesilaus]]). Which Ajax's ships stand beside his is not said (see [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). His men are there to defend them: Podarces, “the son of Iphiclus, the son of Phylacus”, his brother, leads them with Medon among the Phthians (13.693–700; see [[podarces]] and [[phthians]]). The place is where the battle at the ships comes to its crisis. Hector lays hold of the stern of “the fair, swift ship that had brought Protesilaus to Troy and did not take him home again” (15.704–706); fire falls on the ship from which Ajax is driven, and Patroclus' first spear flies “by the stern of the ship of great-hearted Protesilaus”, and the ship is left half-burned (16.112–124, 16.284–294; see [[patroclus]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]).
