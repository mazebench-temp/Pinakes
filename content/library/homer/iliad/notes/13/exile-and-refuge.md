**Medon.** The Achaeans who hold Hector off where he first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, are named contingent by contingent, and in front of the Phthians are “Medon and Podarces, steadfast in battle” (13.693; see [[phthians]], [[medon]] and [[podarces]]). The narrator stops for Medon: “One was the bastard son of divine Oileus, / Medon, the brother of Ajax; but he lived / in Phylace, away from his native land, since he had killed a man, / a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, whom Oileus had as wife” (13.694–697; see [[nothos]], [[eriopis]] and [[phylace]]). The Catalogue gave him the command of Philoctetes' men while their leader lay wounded on Lemnos (2.726–728). Here the poem tells why a son of Oileus, the brother of the Locrian leader, fights among the men of Thessaly: a killing among his father's kin by marriage, and the flight that the article above describes. Like Phoenix in Peleus' Phthia, who was made rich and given a people to rule (9.480–484), he has a place of command in the land that took him in. The four verses return word for word when Aeneas kills him (15.332–336), the obituary of an exile.

**Imbrius.** Teucer's first victim had a different history. Imbrius, son of Mentor, lived in Pedaeum before the Achaeans came, and had as his wife Medesicaste, a bastard daughter of Priam; when the Achaean ships came, “he came back to Ilios, and stood out among the Trojans, / and lived beside Priam; and Priam honored him as he honored his children” (13.172–176; see [[imbrius]], [[medesicaste]] and [[pedaeum]]). He is no exile; he left his town for his wife's father's city when the war came. But the welcome is the one the pattern gives the refugee: Peleus loved Phoenix “as a father loves his son” (9.481), and Priam honored Imbrius as his own children. Imbrius dies in this book and Medon in Book 15, both far from the towns where they were born (see [[death-in-battle]]).
