“And he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him” (13.187): the whole line ends Amphimachus, struck in the chest by the spear Hector meant for Teucer (see [[amphimachus]]). The first half alone follows the blow for Othryoneus, “And he fell with a thud; and Idomeneus boasted over him and spoke” (13.373), and for Alcathous, “And he fell with a thud, and the spear was fixed in his heart” (13.442; see [[othryoneus]] and [[alcathous]]).

Imbrius, felled like an ash tree, ends in a variant of the second half: “so he fell, and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze” (ἀμφὶ δέ οἱ βράχε τεύχεα ποικίλα χαλκῷ, 13.181; see [[imbrius]] and [[the-felled-ash]]). The verse ended Alcmaon's fall in Book 12 (12.396) and will end Hector's under Ajax's stone in Book 14 (14.420). βράχε, “rang”, is the sound of the bronze, as ἀράβησε, “clattered”, is in the whole line.

Idomeneus turns the verb into a wish. He was eager always “either to cover some one of the Trojans in murky night / or himself to fall with a thud, warding off ruin from the Achaeans” (13.425–426; see [[idomeneus]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The infinitive δουπῆσαι occurs only here: the thud of the fall stands for a man's own death.
