summary: Peisander fights Menelaus with “a fine axe of good bronze” on a long olive-wood handle, taken from under his shield when his spear has broken; it strikes the ridge of Menelaus' helmet, and Menelaus kills him (13.601–619). In a simile the builders' newly sharpened axes fell the trees to which Asius is compared (13.389–391).

**Peisander's axe.** Peisander goes straight for Menelaus, led, the narrator says, by an evil fate “to be beaten down by you, Menelaus” (13.601–603; see [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]] and [[apostrophe]]). His spear breaks at the socket on Menelaus' broad shield (13.606–609; see [[spear]]). Menelaus draws his silver-studded sword, in the verse with which he drew it against Paris in Book 3 (13.610 = 3.361), and Peisander arms himself again:

{{quote:6eb91165-45ae-5e22-a3ab-ab93d7b705d1}}

ἀξίνη, the battle-axe, appears again only in the fight at Protesilaus' ship (15.711). εὔχαλκος, “of good bronze”, is said once more, of Achilles' ash spear (20.322), and the handle, πέλεκκον, only here. The axe comes “from under his shield” (ὑπʼ ἀσπίδος): it was carried behind the shield and comes out when the spear has failed (see [[shield]]).

The two men strike together. The axe falls on the ridge of Menelaus' horsehair-crested helmet, “up under the crest itself”, and Menelaus, with the sword he has drawn, strikes Peisander on the forehead above the base of the nose; the bones crack and the two eyes fall bloody at his feet in the dust (13.614–618; see [[helmet]] and [[eyes]]). In Book 3 the same sword, drawn in the same verse, shattered on the ridge of Paris' helmet (3.361–363). Here Menelaus' helmet holds under the axe, and his sword does its work. He sets his heel on Peisander's chest, strips him, and speaks against the Trojans' insolence and their insatiable love of battle (13.618–639; see [[menelaus]], [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]).

**The woodcutters' axes.** Asius falls “as an oak falls, or a white poplar, / or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders / cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391). The verses return for the fall of Sarpedon (16.482–484; see [[the-felled-tree]] and [[asius]]). The tool of the shipwright, which Paris made the image of Hector's heart (3.60–63; see [[heart-like-an-axe]]), here gives the image of a warrior's death.
