**One race, and the elder.** The narrator explains why Poseidon helps the Achaeans in disguise: “Both indeed were of one race and of one father's line, / but Zeus was born first and knew more. / Therefore he shrank from giving help openly” (ἦ μὰν ἀμφοτέροισιν ὁμὸν γένος ἠδʼ ἴα πάτρη, / ἀλλὰ Ζεὺς πρότερος γεγόνει καὶ πλείονα ᾔδη, 13.354–356; see [[poseidon]], [[zeus]] and [[cronus]]). γένος is the stock they share; πάτρη, elsewhere “native land”, is here descent from one father (see the translation's note on 13.345–357). Birth decides between equals: the brother born first knows more, and the younger defers. Zeus says so himself in Book 15, in the message that Iris carries to Poseidon: he is “far greater in strength and elder by birth” (βίῃ πολὺ φέρτερος … καὶ γενεῇ πρότερος, 15.165–166, 15.181–182), and Iris adds that the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.204; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]). Men claim the same right in the same words. Odysseus tells Achilles to let him advise, “since I was born before you and know more” (19.219), and Poseidon says it to Apollo in the battle of the gods (21.440). In Hesiod the order is reversed: Zeus is the youngest of Rhea's children, born last after Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and the Earth-shaker (*Theogony* 453–458, 478; see [[hesiod]]).

**Offspring of Zeus.** Idomeneus, having killed Alcathous, dares Deiphobus to stand against him, “so that you may see what kind of offspring of Zeus I am, who have come here” (οἷος Ζηνὸς γόνος, 13.449), and recites his descent in three generations: Zeus fathered Minos, “a guardian over Crete”, Minos fathered Deucalion, and Deucalion fathered Idomeneus, “a lord over many men / in wide Crete” (13.450–453; see [[idomeneus]], [[minos]], [[deucalion]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The lineage is a boast (see the article above). Its form is the form of Aeneas' recital to Achilles in Book 20, which begins “Zeus the cloud-gatherer first fathered Dardanus” and goes from father to son in the same verbs (Δάρδανον αὖ πρῶτον τέκετο … Ζεύς, 20.215–240; compare ὃς πρῶτον Μίνωα τέκε, 13.450). Idomeneus reaches Zeus in three generations; Priam, in Aeneas' list, is the sixth from Zeus, and his son Deiphobus the seventh. The boast ends with what the line has brought to Troy: “to be an evil to you and to your father and to the other Trojans” (13.454).

## Outside Homer

The Suda, explaining the Athenian φρατρία, “phratry”, cites 13.354 as Homer's word for the same group of kin: “Homer, at any rate, calls the same thing πάτρη too: 'Both indeed were of one race and of one father's line'” (φ 694); elsewhere it glosses the word, in the form πάτρης, as “descent from the father” (π 789).
