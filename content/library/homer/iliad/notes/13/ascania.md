---
summary: The men who come to Troy as relief “on the morning before” have come “from deep-soiled Ascania” (13.793). Strabo takes this as the Mysian Ascania near Nicaea, nearer to Troy than the “far-off” Phrygian Ascania of the Catalogue (2.863).
---

οἵ ῥʼ ἐξ Ἀσκανίης ἐριβώλακος ἦλθον ἀμοιβοί, “who had come from deep-soiled Ascania as relief” (13.793; see [[deep-soiled]]). Palmys, Ascanius and Morys, son of Hippotion, are named before the verse (see [[ascanius-the-mysian]], [[palmys]] and [[morys]]). The Catalogue's Ascania was τῆλʼ, “far off” (2.863), the home of the Phrygians.

[[Strabo]] distinguishes the two. The land around the Ascanian lake was called by the lake's name, he says, and part of it was Phrygian and part Mysian, the Phrygian part farther from Troy. The Catalogue's “far-off Ascania” is the Phrygian one, “since there is another, nearer Ascania, the Mysian, by the present Nicaea”, and it is this one that the poet names in Book 13, where Strabo's text has an extra verse calling these men's leader a leader of the Mysians (*Geography* 12.4.5; see [[mysians]] and [[phrygians]]). Nicaea lay on the Ascanian lake (*Geography* 12.4.7), and the Suda calls Ascania “a lake at Nicaea” (α 4160).
