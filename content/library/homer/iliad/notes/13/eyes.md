---
summary: The book begins with Zeus turning his shining eyes away from Troy. Poseidon, who keeps watch, sees with his eyes the wonder of the Trojans at the ships; the gleam of bronze blinds the fighters; the god bewitches Alcathous' bright eyes so that he cannot flee; the eyes of Idomeneus' boar blaze with fire; and Peisander's eyes fall bloody at his feet (13.3–7, 13.99, 13.340, 13.435, 13.474, 13.616–617).
grc: [ὀφθαλμὼ, ὀφθαλμῶν]
---

**Eyes turned away.** Zeus has brought Hector to the ships, “and he himself turned his shining eyes away” (13.3), to the horse-herding Thracians, the Mysians and the most just Abii. “And to Troy he no longer turned his shining eyes at all” (13.7; see [[zeus]] and [[abii]]): the two verses enclose the far peoples between the turning of the eyes and their not turning back. Poseidon, who “kept no blind watch” (13.10), sees what Zeus has stopped looking at (see [[poseidon]] and [[watching-the-war]]).

**Seeing.** In Calchas' likeness Poseidon tells the men in the rear, “Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes” (13.99; see [[o-popoi]]). The son of Oileus knows the god not by his face but from behind, by the movements of his feet and legs (13.71–72; see [[divine-epiphany]]). When the armies meet on the left, “eyes were blinded / by the gleam of bronze” (13.340–341; see [[bronze]]).

**Bewitched.** Poseidon beats Alcathous down at Idomeneus' hands, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (13.435), so that he stands still like a pillar and is killed (see [[alcathous]] and [[loosened-his-limbs]]). Zeus' “shining eyes” and Alcathous' “bright eyes” are the same words, ὄσσε φαεινώ and ὄσσε φαεινά (13.3, 13.435).

**Fire and blood.** The boar of Idomeneus' simile “bristles up along its back; / and its two eyes blaze with fire” (13.473–474; see [[the-bristling-boar]]). Menelaus strikes Peisander above the base of the nose: “the bones cracked, and his two eyes / fell bloody at his feet on the ground in the dust” (13.616–617; see [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]]). Death itself is told through the eyes: darkness covers Adamas' eyes, and murky night Deipyrus' (κατʼ ὀφθαλμῶν, 13.575, 13.580; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]).
