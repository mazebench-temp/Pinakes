---
summary: Menelaus calls the Trojans “overbearing” over the body of Peisander, as in Book 3 he called the sons of Priam overbearing and not to be trusted. The book also has the adverb, “overbearingly”.
grc: [ὑπερφιάλως]
en: [overbearingly]
---

“So, I think, you will leave the ships of the Danaans of the swift horses, you overbearing Trojans, insatiable of the dread war cry” (13.620–621; [[menelaus]], [[of-the-swift-horses]], [[insatiable]]). It is Menelaus' word for the enemy. At the oath in Book 3 he asked that Priam himself come to swear, since his sons “are overbearing and not to be trusted” (3.106). Here the rest of the speech gives the charge its content: the Trojans carried off his wedded wife and his possessions after they had been welcomed by her, and did not fear the wrath of Zeus, god of guests; and Zeus, Menelaus complains, favors “men who are arrogant, the Trojans, whose might is always reckless” (13.622–634; [[hybris]], [[xenia]], [[who-thunders-loud]]). See [[trojans]].

The adverb ὑπερφιάλως comes earlier in the book. Idomeneus tells Meriones that they must not stand talking like children, “lest someone be overbearingly indignant” (13.293; see [[nemesis]]). In the Odyssey the suitors are “overbearingly” indignant at the words of Odysseus (Od. 17.481, 21.285).
