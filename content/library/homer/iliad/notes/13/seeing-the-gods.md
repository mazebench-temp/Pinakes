summary: Poseidon comes among the Achaeans in the likeness of Calchas and then of Thoas. The son of Oileus knows him from behind, by the movements of his feet and legs as he goes, and declares that “gods are easy to know”; no one else in the book sees through him, and Hector can only infer that a god has roused him.

**Known from behind.** Poseidon comes to the two Ajaxes “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (13.45), speaks, strikes them with his staff and darts away like a hawk rising from a rock (13.46–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]] and [[divine-intervention]]). Then:

{{quote:838178da-bd0d-5175-996d-65f28e89b9cd}}

ἴχνια is elsewhere a track or a footprint, and the god's tracks are the phrase of the Odyssey's guided walks, where a man “followed in the footsteps of the god” (*Odyssey* 2.406, 3.30, 5.193, 7.38). Here, with κνημάων, “of the legs”, it is taken as the movement of feet and legs, the god's gait seen from behind as he went away; read as “tracks”, the son of Oileus knew him by the prints he left (see the translation's note on 13.71). Either way the recognition comes, as it usually does, at the moment of departure (see the article above). Telemachus knows that Mentes was a god when he flies off like a bird, and Nestor knows Athena when she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle (*Odyssey* 1.319–323, 3.371–379).

**Easy to know.** ἀρίγνωτοι δὲ θεοί περ, “and gods are easy to know”, states the opposite of what the poem usually shows. Hera says that gods are hard to face when they appear in their own form (20.131), and in Book 5 Diomedes needs Athena's gift to tell god from man (5.127–128). ῥεῖα δʼ ἀρίγνωτος, “easily known”, is a formula: Hector, when Teucer's bowstring breaks, says that “easily known to men is the help that comes from Zeus” (15.489–490), and in the Odyssey the phrase is used of Artemis among her nymphs and of the houses of Alcinous and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 6.108, 6.300, 17.265). The son of Oileus' confidence is not tested again. Telamonian Ajax does not answer the point; he feels his own hands quiver and his might rise (13.76–80; see [[menos]]).

**Unknown.** No one else in the book knows the god. As Thoas, he speaks to Idomeneus in Thoas' voice (εἰσάμενος φθογγήν, 13.216), and Idomeneus answers him as Thoas (13.222–230); the narrator calls the disguise “the likeness of a man” (13.357). On the other side Hector, stopped at the close ranks, can only reason from his success: the Achaeans will give ground, “if truly / the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.153–154). The god at work among the Achaeans he does not see, and in the next book Poseidon himself leads them, holding a terrible sword like lightning (14.362–387).
