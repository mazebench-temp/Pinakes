---
summary: In Book 13 the θυμός is roused and shared: a god casts the joy of battle into the hearts of the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus' companions stand by him “with one spirit”, and the two Ajaxes pull together like oxen “with equal spirit”. It is also the life that Harpalion breathes out and that leaves Euchenor's limbs, and even the dead Asius, Deiphobus boasts, will be glad in his heart.
---

**Roused.** The heart is where a god's urging is felt. When Poseidon has struck the two Ajaxes with his staff, the son of Oileus says that “the heart in my own breast / is more eager than before to make war and to fight” (13.73–74), and the narrator says that they rejoiced “in the joy of battle that the god had cast into their hearts” (χάρμῃ … τήν σφιν θεὸς ἔμβαλε θυμῷ, 13.82; see [[charme]] and [[divine-intervention]]). Hector's call ends with the formula of the rally, “he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (13.155; see [[menos]] and [[exhortation]]). Deiphobus' plea stirs the heart of Aeneas (13.468), and at the end of the book Hector, testing the Achaean battalions, “did not confound the spirit in the breasts of the Achaeans” (13.808).

**One spirit.** Idomeneus calls on his companions as Aeneas comes on, “and they all, with one spirit in their hearts, / stood close beside him, leaning their shields against their shoulders” (ἕνα φρεσὶ θυμὸν ἔχοντες, 13.487–488; see [[idomeneus]]). ἕνα θυμὸν ἔχοντες, “having one spirit”, is said of men who fight as one: the Achaeans and Trojans who fight hand to hand around Protesilaus' ship (15.707–712), Patroclus and Automedon arming in front of the Myrmidons (16.218–219), and the Achaeans who stand around Patroclus' body (17.266–268). The two Ajaxes, side by side where Hector first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, are like two oxen pulling the plow “with equal spirit” (ἶσον θυμὸν ἔχοντε, 13.703–704; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]); Telamonian Ajax will use the phrase of the pair in Book 17: “we two namesakes, with equal spirit”, who have long stood by each other in battle (17.719–721).

**Fear and anger.** The coward in ambush cannot keep his heart quiet: “the heart in his breast cannot be held to sitting still” (13.280; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). Deiphobus “feared in his heart” the spear of Meriones (13.163), and Paris “was very angry at heart” over Harpalion's death (13.660; see [[cholos]]). Paris tells Hector that “your heart is set on blaming one who is not to blame” (13.775), and bids him lead “wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (13.784).

**Life.** θυμός is also the life. Harpalion sits down in his companions' arms, “breathing out his life, like a worm on the ground” (θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, 13.654; see [[like-a-worm]]), the half-verse of Diores' death in Book 4 (4.524); Paris' arrow strikes Euchenor under the jaw and the ear, “and quickly his spirit / went from his limbs, and hateful darkness seized him”, two verses that return when Meriones kills Laogonus in Book 16 (13.671–672 = 16.606–607; see [[death-in-battle]]). Deiphobus, boasting over Hypsenor, gives the dead Asius a heart still: “even as he goes to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper, / he will be glad in his heart, since I have given him an escort” (13.415–416; see [[hades]] and [[euchomai]]).
