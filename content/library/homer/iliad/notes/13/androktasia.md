summary: Book 13 tells sixteen killings, ten Trojans and six Achaeans if Hypsenor dies, most of them on the left, where Idomeneus kills four men. Their obituaries are full ones, Imbrius the son-in-law of Priam, Othryoneus the suitor of Cassandra, Alcathous the husband of Hippodameia, Harpalion whose father follows his body, Euchenor who chose his death; spears thrown at one man kill another, killings answer killings, and four end in a boast (13.170–672).

**The killings.** Teucer kills Imbrius, and Hector Amphimachus (13.170–187). Then, on the left, Idomeneus kills Othryoneus, Asius, Alcathous and Oenomaus, and Antilochus Asius' charioteer; Deiphobus' spears, thrown at Idomeneus, kill Hypsenor and Ascalaphus; Aeneas kills Aphareus, Antilochus Thoon, Meriones Adamas, and Helenus Deipyrus (13.361–580). Menelaus kills Peisander, Meriones Harpalion, and Paris Euchenor (13.601–672). Whether Hypsenor dies is not certain: the verse of a killing strikes him, but he is carried off “groaning heavily” and not named again (13.412–423; see the translation's note). Deiphobus and Helenus are wounded in the arm and the hand and leave the field (13.528–539, 13.593–600). See [[the-battle-at-the-ships]].

**The victims.** Five of the dead have histories. Imbrius had married a bastard daughter of Priam and lived beside the king, who honored him as he honored his own children (13.171–176; see [[imbrius]]). Othryoneus had come to Troy for Cassandra, offering a deed for a bride (13.363–369; see [[othryoneus]] and [[bride-price]]). Alcathous had married Anchises' eldest daughter, the best of the girls of her age, and reared Aeneas in his house (13.427–433, 13.465–466; see [[alcathous]] and [[marriage]]). Harpalion had followed his father to Troy “and did not come back again to his native land”, and the father follows the body in tears (13.643–659; see [[harpalion]] and [[pylaemenes-dead-and-alive]]). Euchenor sailed knowing the two deaths his father the seer had foretold, and chose death in war (13.663–672; see [[euchenor]] and [[divination]]). Ascalaphus' history is his father: he is “the son of Enyalius”, and Ares does not yet know that he has fallen (13.518–525; see [[ascalaphus]]).

**Chains.** Three spears meant for one man kill another: Hector's at Teucer kills Amphimachus, and Deiphobus' at Idomeneus kills Hypsenor and then Ascalaphus (13.183–187, 13.402–412, 13.516–520). Each death calls for another. The son of Oileus cuts off Imbrius' head “in his anger for Amphimachus”, and Poseidon is angry for his grandson (13.202–209); Asius comes to defend Othryoneus' body and dies; Deiphobus, “grieving for Asius”, kills Hypsenor (13.384–412); grief seizes Menelaus at Deipyrus' death (13.581); and Paris, angry for his guest-friend Harpalion, kills Euchenor (13.660–672).

**The blows.** The places are told exactly: below the ear (13.177), the chest (13.186), the belly through the corselet, twice in the same verses (13.371–372, 13.397–398), the throat beneath the chin (13.388), the liver beneath the midriff (13.412), the heart, whose beating shakes the spear (13.438–444), the shoulder (13.519), the throat again (13.542), the vein that runs up the back to the neck (13.546–547), between the genitals and the navel, “where most of all / Ares is painful for miserable mortals” (13.568–569), the temple (13.576), the forehead, so that the eyes fall out (13.615–617), the buttock into the bladder (13.651–652), under the jaw and the ear (13.671).

**The falls.** Imbrius falls like an ash tree cut on a mountain peak, Asius like an oak, a poplar or a pine felled for ship-timber (13.178–181, 13.389–393; see [[the-felled-ash]] and [[the-felled-tree]]); Adamas writhes around the spear like a roped ox (13.570–573; see [[the-roped-ox]]); Harpalion lies stretched out like a worm on the ground (13.654–655; see [[like-a-worm]]); Thoon falls on his back, “stretching out both his hands to his dear companions” (13.548–549). The formulas of death are many: [[fell-with-a-thud]] (13.187, 13.373, 13.442), the clutching of the earth (ἕλε γαῖαν ἀγοστῷ, 13.508, 13.520), [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and its variants (13.544, 13.575, 13.580, 13.672), and [[loosened-his-limbs]] (13.412).

**Boasts.** Idomeneus mocks Othryoneus' bargain (13.374–382) and offers Deiphobus a genealogy, three killed for one (13.446–454; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]); Deiphobus boasts that Asius will be glad of an escort to Hades (13.414–416); and Menelaus, over Peisander, speaks against the Trojans' insatiable love of battle (13.620–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]).
