summary: Book 13 closes its speeches with the usual formulas, often turning at once to the speech's effect, and twice in less usual words: “So with his orders the earth-holder roused the Achaeans” (13.125), and “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right” (13.821), where the close of Ajax's challenge opens an omen.
grc: ["ὥς ῥα κελευτιόων", "ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι"]
en: [So with his orders, As he spoke]
---

The common forms close most of the book's speeches: ὣς φάτο (13.295, 13.328, 13.455, 13.468, 13.748), ὣς εἰπών (13.155, 13.239, 13.383, 13.640, 13.788), ὣς ἔφατʼ (13.417, 13.487) and ἦ καί (13.59, 13.754). The conversation of the two Ajaxes ends “So they spoke such words to one another” (13.81). Often the same verse names the effect: “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (13.155), “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast” (13.468), “So spoke Polydamas, and his safe counsel pleased Hector” (13.748), “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind” (13.788).

Two closings are less usual. Poseidon's long rebuke ends “So with his orders the earth-holder roused the Achaeans” (ὥς ῥα κελευτιόων, 13.125), where the participle names the speech an order (see [[exhortation]] and [[poseidon]]). Ajax's challenge to Hector ends with a sign: “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right, / an eagle flying high” (ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις, 13.821–822). The verse returns twice in the Odyssey, each time for a bird that answers the words just spoken (Od. 15.160, 15.525; see [[divination]]).
