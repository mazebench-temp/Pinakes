---
summary: Honor in Book 13 is given and withheld by kings and peoples. Agamemnon “dishonored” Achilles; Priam honored Imbrius like his own children but did not honor Aeneas, who stands apart in anger; Thoas was honored by his people like a god; and Hector wishes to be honored as Athena and Apollo are.
grc: [ἀπητίμησε, τίεσκεν]
passages:
  - 13.108-115 | Even if Agamemnon is to blame
  - 13.459-461 | Aeneas at the back of the throng
  - 13.824-832 | Hector answers Ajax
---

**Dishonored.** Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, grants that Agamemnon may be wholly to blame for the army's plight, “because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (οὕνεκʼ ἀπητίμησε, 13.113; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[agamemnon]]). ἀπατιμάω, “dishonor”, a compound of ἀτιμάω, is found only here. The seer whose likeness the god has taken named the first dishonor of the poem in the same way: Apollo's anger was for the priest “whom Agamemnon dishonored” (ἠτίμησʼ, 1.94).

**Honored like his children, and not at all.** Imbrius, who came back to Ilios when the Achaeans landed, “lived beside Priam; and Priam honored him as he honored his children” (τίεν ἶσα τέκεσσι, 13.176; see [[imbrius]] and [[priam]]). The three verses of his homecoming are said again of Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, in Book 15 (13.174–176 = 15.549–551), and in Book 5 Deicoon was a man “whom the Trojans, as they did the sons of Priam, / honored” (5.535–536). Aeneas has the opposite complaint. Deiphobus finds him at the back of the throng, “for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, / because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (οὔ τι τίεσκεν, 13.460–461; see [[aeneas]] and [[menis]]). The king's honor makes a man stand out among the Trojans or stand apart from them; Achilles stands apart for the same reason (1.244, 1.412).

**Honored like a god.** Poseidon takes the voice of Thoas, “who in all Pleuron and steep Calydon / was lord of the Aetolians, and was honored by the people like a god” (θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ, 13.217–218; see [[thoas]]). The verse-end is said of Dolopion, priest of the Scamander (5.78), of Agamemnon (10.32–33) and of Onetor, priest of Idaean Zeus (16.604–605): priests and kings, the men the people treat as gods. Hector goes further and wishes it of himself. Answering Ajax, he says that he wishes he were the son of Zeus and Hera “all my days, … / and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored, / as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (13.825–828; see [[hector]]). The verse about the honor of Athena and Apollo is the one of his wish in Book 8, where he wished to be immortal and ageless (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and the translation's note on 13.825–828). His certainty is measured by an impossible honor.
