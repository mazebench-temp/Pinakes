summary: Brought to the ships by Zeus, Hector is stopped at the close ranks around the two Ajaxes, kills Poseidon's grandson Amphimachus, and holds the breach unaware that his men are dying on the left. He takes Polydamas' counsel, finds his brothers and allies dead or wounded, rebukes Paris, and answers Ajax's challenge with the wish to be the son of Zeus and Hera.

**Brought to the ships.** The book begins with him. “Now when Zeus had brought the Trojans and Hector close to the ships” (13.1), the god turns his eyes away (see [[zeus]]). To Poseidon, speaking as Calchas, he is the danger: “that frenzied man leads them, like a flame, / Hector, who claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.53–54; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). λυσσώδης, “frenzied”, occurs only here (see [[frenzied]]), but Teucer called him “this mad dog” (8.299), and Odysseus spoke of the “mighty frenzy” that had entered him (9.239, 9.305). Hector has made no such claim; at the end of the book he wishes it (13.825–826).

**Checked.** He leads the Trojan charge:

{{quote:173580e2-2a01-5926-b2c1-bbaeed86e5af}}

The close ranks around the two Ajaxes stop the boulder (see [[the-rolling-boulder]], [[battle-order]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). His call to the “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand” is the verse of his exhortations in Books 8 and 11 (13.150 = 8.173, 11.286; see [[exhortation]]). In Book 8 he said, “I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me” (8.175); now the Achaeans, drawn up “in close order like a tower”, will give ground before his spear, “if truly / the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.152–154; see [[husband-of-hera]]).

**Amphimachus.** When Teucer kills Imbrius, Priam's son-in-law, Hector throws at him, misses, and kills Amphimachus, son of Cteatus (13.182–187; see [[imbrius]] and [[amphimachus]]). Rushing to snatch the helmet, he is covered so wholly “in terrible bronze” that Ajax's spear finds no skin; it strikes the boss of his shield and pushes him back from both bodies (13.188–194; see [[ajax]]). The son of Oileus throws Imbrius' head into the dust at his feet (13.202–205), and Poseidon, angry for his grandson, goes to rouse Idomeneus (13.206–209; see [[poseidon]]).

**At the breach.** For a while the Achaeans only speak of him. Idomeneus leaves him to the two Ajaxes and Teucer, “even if he is very strong”: it will be a steep climb for him to burn the ships, “unless the son of Cronus himself / casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships” (13.312–320; see [[idomeneus]]). Zeus wants victory for the Trojans and for him, the narrator explains, to give glory to Achilles (13.347–350; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). “But Hector, dear to Zeus, had not heard” that his men were being cut down on the left (13.674–676; see [[dear-to-zeus]]). He holds on where he first leapt in at the gates and the wall, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, and five peoples can hardly hold off “brilliant Hector, like a flame” (13.679–688; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[like-a-flame]]).

**Polydamas' counsel.** The Trojans would have fallen back to Ilios had not Polydamas spoken (13.723–725; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[polydamas]]). He begins with a reproach, “Hector, there is no dealing with you, to make you heed persuasion” (13.726): because a god has given Hector the works of war, he wants to know more than others in counsel too (13.727–734; see [[different-gifts]]). This time “his safe counsel pleased Hector”, in the verses that accepted the first counsel at the ditch (13.748–749 = 12.80–81). He leaves Polydamas to hold the best men and rushes off “like a snowy mountain” (13.754; see [[like-a-snowy-mountain]]).

**The search.** He looks among the champions for Deiphobus, Helenus, Adamas and Asius, and finds them dead at the sterns of the ships or wounded inside the wall (13.758–764). On the left he finds Paris:

{{quote:1452eee8-10c9-559d-9f79-30cc96478242}}

The first verse is his rebuke of Book 3 (13.769 = 3.39; see [[evil-paris]]). In Books 3 and 6 Paris accepted the reproach, “Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (3.59, 6.333). Now he answers that Hector blames one who is not to blame, and Hector, persuaded, goes with him; the verse of persuasion is the one with which Agamemnon turned Menelaus from fighting Hector (13.775–789; 13.788 = 7.120; see [[paris]]).

**The challenge.** He leads the Trojans “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (13.802 = 11.295; see [[equal-of-ares]]), testing the battalions “as he stepped forward under cover of his shield” (13.807), as Deiphobus came on at the start (13.158), but he does not shake them (13.808). Ajax tells him that the Achaeans were beaten down by “the evil lash of Zeus” (13.812, 12.37), and that he will soon pray for horses swifter than hawks to carry him home (13.810–820). An eagle flies on the right, where the eagle of Book 12 came on the left (13.821–823; 12.200–209; see [[eagle]]). Hector answers:

{{quote:a1854acf-024b-5bb2-81a5-b3653daffc41}}

ἁμαρτοεπής and βουγάϊος are insults found nowhere else in the Iliad (see [[blundering-in-speech]], [[braggart]] and [[what-a-word]]). The oath is that of 8.538–541, with Zeus and Hera for parents in place of being immortal and ageless (see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The impossible wish measures his certainty; it is no claim to divine birth (see the translation's note on 13.825–828). The threat that Ajax will glut the dogs and birds of the Trojans “fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans” turns against an Achaean the words that Athena spoke in Book 8 of a Trojan dead at the ships (13.831–832 ≈ 8.379–380).

**Afterward.** Ajax answers with a stone, and Hector is carried toward the city “groaning heavily”, as Deiphobus was in this book (14.402–439; 14.432 = 13.538). He returns to lay hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship (15.704–706).

His epithets include ἄμοτον μεμαώς, “raging without end” (13.80), βοὴν ἀγαθός, “good at the war cry” (13.123), rare for him, and χαλκοκορυστής, “armed in bronze” (13.720); he is Πριαμίδης at 13.40, 13.80, 13.316 and 13.803 (see [[raging-without-end]], [[good-at-the-war-cry]], [[armed-in-bronze]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
