summary: The gods keep out of the battle of Book 13, as Zeus' ban requires, all but Poseidon. Zeus looks away, sure that no immortal will help either side; Ares and the other gods sit on Olympus, “shut out from the war”; and the son of Oileus knows that “one of the gods who hold Olympus” has spoken to him in Calchas' shape.

**Shut out.** Zeus' ban of Book 8 still holds: a god who goes “to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” will come back to Olympus beaten, or be hurled into Tartarus (8.10–16; see [[zeus]]). Zeus trusts it and turns his eyes away, “for he did not expect in his heart that any of the immortals / would come to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.8–9). The gods obey. When his son Ascalaphus falls, Ares is sitting “on the peak of Olympus beneath golden clouds, / held back by the counsels of Zeus, where the other / immortal gods were also, shut out from the war” (13.523–525; see [[ares]] and [[olympus]]). In Book 11 they sat in their halls and blamed Zeus (11.75–79); in Book 12 the helpers of the Danaans grieved (12.179–180).

**One god among men.** Poseidon alone breaks the ban, and only in a man's likeness (13.10–45, 13.351–357; see [[poseidon]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). The son of Oileus knows that “one of the gods who hold Olympus” has spoken to him in the likeness of the seer, “and gods are easy to know” (13.68–72; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). The verse-end is the one in which Dione named the gods whom Heracles' bow brought to grief, and in which Zeus told Ares that he was the most hateful of them (5.404, 5.890).

**In men's words.** Ajax tells Hector that he will soon “pray to father Zeus and the other immortals” for swift horses (13.818), as Hecuba once bade him pour a libation (6.259); and Hector wishes to be “honored as Athena and Apollo are honored” (13.827; see [[athena]] and [[apollo]]).
