---
summary: Poseidon, in Thoas' voice, and Meriones address Idomeneus as “counselor of the Cretans,” and Deiphobus calls Aeneas “counselor of the Trojans” when he needs him to defend Alcathous.
---

**Idomeneus.** Poseidon, “making his voice like that of Thoas, son of Andraemon” (13.216), meets Idomeneus as he comes from a wounded companion: “Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have your threats gone, the threats the sons of the Achaeans used to make against the Trojans?” (13.219–220; [[poseidon]], [[thoas]], [[idomeneus]], [[cretans]], [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). In Book 20 the same verse, with Aeneas and the Trojans in it, is Apollo's taunt to Aeneas, when the god has taken the voice of Lycaon, son of Priam (20.79–85): both times a god in a man's voice asks a leader where the threats have gone. Meriones gives Idomeneus the fuller verse, “Idomeneus, counselor of the bronze-armored Cretans” (13.255; [[bronze-armored]], [[meriones]]), Pandarus' address to Aeneas with the people changed (5.180).

**Aeneas.** Deiphobus, who has decided not to face Idomeneus alone, finds Aeneas standing at the back of the throng, angry with Priam, and begins: “Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans, now you must indeed defend your brother-in-law, if any grief for your kin touches you” (13.463–464; [[deiphobus]], [[aeneas]], [[alcathous]]). The title of a leader in council is given to a man who, the narrator says, was always nursing wrath against Priam because Priam did not honor him (13.459–461; [[time]], [[menis]]). Hector will use the fuller verse when he proposes to Aeneas that they take the horses of Achilles (17.485–490). See [[boule]].
