---
summary: Anger for the dead drives three deeds in Book 13: the son of Oileus, angry for Amphimachus, cuts off Imbrius' head and sends it rolling to Hector's feet; Poseidon, angry for his grandson, goes to rouse the Danaans; and Paris, angry for his guest-friend Harpalion, shoots Euchenor. Meriones is enraged at a broken spear.
grc: [χώσατο, ἐχολώθη]
---

**Anger for the dead.** Hector kills Amphimachus, and the two Ajaxes carry off Imbrius' body; “and his head from his tender neck / the son of Oileus cut off, in his anger for Amphimachus, / and with a swing he sent it flying like a ball through the throng; / and it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet” (κεχολωμένος Ἀμφιμάχοιο, 13.202–205; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[imbrius]] and [[like-a-ball]]). The next verse gives the same anger to a god: “And then indeed Poseidon was angry with all his heart / for his grandson, fallen in grim combat” (περὶ κῆρι Ποσειδάων ἐχολώθη, 13.206–207; see [[poseidon]] and [[amphimachus]]), and he goes along the ships to urge on the Danaans and make sorrows for the Trojans. On the Trojan side, when Meriones kills Harpalion, “Paris was very angry at heart over his killing; / for he had been his guest-friend”, and “in his anger for him he let fly a bronze-fitted arrow” that kills Euchenor (13.660–662; see [[paris]], [[harpalion]] and [[xenia]]). Anger for a fallen companion moves men to kill again, and it is the motive that will bring Achilles back to the war when Patroclus falls.

**Anger at a broken spear.** Meriones, whose spear snapped at the socket against Deiphobus' shield, drew back, “and was terribly enraged / on both counts, for the victory and for the spear that he had shattered” (χώσατο δʼ αἰνῶς / ἀμφότερον, νίκης τε καὶ ἔγχεος, 13.165–166; see [[meriones]], [[nike]] and [[spear]]). He goes to his hut for another, and the errand brings him to Idomeneus (13.167–168, 13.246–258).
