summary: Idomeneus goes out armed like the lightning that the son of Cronus brandishes from Olympus, “showing a sign to mortals”; at the end of the book an eagle flies on the right, and the Achaeans shout, emboldened by the omen.
---

**A sign in a simile.** Idomeneus arms in his hut and goes out “like the lightning that the son of Cronus / takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus, / showing a sign to mortals; and its flashes are plain for all to see; / so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran” (δεικνὺς σῆμα βροτοῖσιν, 13.242–245; see [[like-lightning]] and [[idomeneus]]). The lightning in the simile is a σῆμα, a sign from Zeus (see the article above), and the hero who is likened to it carries its meaning into the battle. Athena came down to the Trojan plain in Book 4 like the star that the son of Cronus sends “as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men” (4.75–79), and the serpents on Agamemnon's corselet were like the rainbows that the son of Cronus “sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28; compare 17.547–550). Here the sign is not seen by any character; it is the narrator's.

**The eagle on the right.** At the end of the book, as Ajax tells Hector that he will soon pray for horses swifter than hawks to carry him to the city, “a bird flew by on the right, / an eagle flying high; and the army of the Achaeans shouted out, / emboldened by the omen” (13.821–823; see [[eagle]], [[divination]] and [[tharsos]]). In Book 12 an eagle flying on the left dropped a serpent among the Trojans, and Hector refused to heed it (12.200–250; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]). Now the bird comes on the Achaeans' right, and they read it at once. Hector answers Ajax with abuse and a wish sworn on the day's outcome: if only he were the son of Zeus and Hera, as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives (13.824–832; see [[hector]]).
