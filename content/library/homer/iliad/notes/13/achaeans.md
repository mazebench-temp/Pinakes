---
summary: Poseidon pities the Achaeans, beaten down and weeping by their ships, and rallies them in secret, since Zeus wants victory for the Trojans but not the Achaean army's destruction. Formed in close ranks around the two Ajaxes, they stop Hector, and the book ends with their shout at an eagle on the right (13.15–16, 13.83–90, 13.126–148, 13.347–350, 13.821–823).
---

**Pitied.** From Thracian Samos Poseidon sees “the city of Priam … and the ships of the Achaeans”, “and he pitied the Achaeans / as they were beaten down by the Trojans, and he was fiercely indignant with Zeus” (13.14–16; see [[poseidon]] and [[pity]]). The narrator repeats the reason when he explains why the god helps them in secret (13.352–353). Zeus' purpose lies between the two gods: he “wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector, / to give glory to Achilles, swift of foot; but he did not at all / wish the Achaean army to perish before Ilios” (13.347–349; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**In the rear.** The men Poseidon finds behind the line are worn out. “Their limbs were loosened by grievous weariness, / and grief came into their hearts as they watched / the Trojans, who had climbed over the great wall in a throng. / As they looked at them they let tears fall from beneath their brows; / for they thought they would not escape from the evil” (13.85–89; see [[tears]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The last verse returns almost word for word when the Achaeans despair at the ships in Book 15 (15.699–700). Poseidon's rebuke to their leaders begins “Shame, Argives, young men!” (13.95; see [[argives]] and [[aidos]]).

**The close ranks.** Around the two Ajaxes “the best men, / chosen out, awaited the Trojans and brilliant Hector”, so close that “shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man” (13.128–131; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[battle-order]]). When Hector comes on like a rolling boulder, “the sons of the Achaeans, facing him, / stabbing at him with swords and double-pointed spears, / pushed him away from them” (13.146–148; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and [[the-rolling-boulder]]). He says that they “have arrayed themselves in close order like a tower” (13.152), the words used in Book 12 of the hunters who face the boar or lion in the simile that made Hector the beast (12.43; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]).

**A fine and a debt.** The book shows the army from outside too. Euchenor of Corinth sailed to Troy knowing that he would die there, and so “avoided at once the grievous fine of the Achaeans / and the hateful sickness” (13.669–670): the army laid a penalty on the man who stayed at home (see [[euchenor]]). Polydamas fears “that the Achaeans may pay back yesterday's / debt” (13.745–746), their defeat of the day before (see [[polydamas]]).

**The glory that nearly was.** Hector has not heard that his men are being cut down on the left of the ships, and “soon the glory would have been the Achaeans'”, so hard did Poseidon urge on the Argives (13.674–678; see [[kydos]]). At the end of the book Ajax speaks for the army: “We are not without knowledge of battle at all, / but by the evil lash of Zeus we Achaeans were beaten down” (13.811–812; see [[ajax]]). An eagle flies on the right as he speaks, “and the army of the Achaeans shouted out, / emboldened by the omen” (13.822–823; see [[eagle]] and [[divination]]).
