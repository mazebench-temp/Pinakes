---
summary: Hector answers Ajax's challenge with an impossible wish, to be “the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” as surely as the day brings evil to the Argives.
---

Ajax has told Hector that the time is near when he will pray for his horses to be swifter than hawks, and an eagle has flown by on the right (13.809–823; [[high-flying]]). Hector answers with a wish: “If only I were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus / all my days, and queen Hera had borne me, / and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored, / as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (13.825–828; [[hector]], [[queenly]]). The verses take the form of his wish at nightfall in Book 8, when he asked to be “immortal and ageless all my days” (8.538–541; [[immortal-and-ageless]]); here descent from Zeus and Hera takes the place of immortality. The wish cannot come true, and it is not meant to: it measures his certainty that the day belongs to the Trojans.

Earlier in the book Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, said that Hector “claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.54; [[eristhenes]]). Hector makes the claim only as a wish. The epithet usually names Zeus as a father, as when Hera calls Athena “child of aegis-bearing Zeus” (2.157; [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]), and Hector wishes for the honor of the two children of Zeus he names, Athena and Apollo.
