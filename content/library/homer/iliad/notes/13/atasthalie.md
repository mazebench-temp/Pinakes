---
summary: Menelaus asks Zeus how he can favor the Trojans, “whose might is always reckless”, setting the word of self-made ruin beside ὕβρις.
grc: [ἀτάσθαλον]
en: [reckless]
---

In his complaint to Zeus over Peisander, Menelaus calls the Trojans “men who are arrogant, / the Trojans, whose might is always reckless” (ὑβριστῇσι / Τρωσίν, τῶν μένος αἰὲν ἀτάσθαλον, 13.633–634; see [[hybris]] and [[menos]]). The adjective belongs to judgments that foresee or explain a ruin by the victims' own conduct (see the article above), and Menelaus' speech has just foretold it: the god of guests “will one day destroy your steep city” (13.625). ὕβρις and ἀτασθαλίη go together. The Iliad joined the verb and the adjective in Nestor's story of the Epeians (ὑβρίζοντες ἀτάσθαλα μηχανόωντο, 11.695; see the note on Book 11), and in the Odyssey the suitors are “arrogant and reckless men” (ὑβρισταὶ … καὶ ἀτάσθαλοι ἄνδρες, *Odyssey* 24.282). Hector, waiting for Achilles, will use the noun of himself, as the man who destroyed his people “by my own recklessness” (22.104).
