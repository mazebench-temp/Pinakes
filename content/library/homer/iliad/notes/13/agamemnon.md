summary: Agamemnon, wounded, does not appear in Book 13, but Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, grants that he may be wholly to blame for dishonoring Achilles, and Idomeneus mocks a Trojan suitor with the offer of the most beautiful of his daughters (13.108–115, 13.378–382).
# Ἀτρεΐδην (13.581), Ἀτρεΐδης (13.593, 13.605, 13.610) and Ἀτρεΐδαο (13.646) are Menelaus. Ἀτρεΐδαο at
# 13.378 is Agamemnon, whose daughters Idomeneus offers. ἡγεμόνος … κείνῳ (13.108–109) is Agamemnon.

**To blame.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, explains the Trojans' presence at the ships by “the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army, / who in their strife with him are not willing to defend / the swift-sailing ships” (13.108–110; see the translation's note on 13.108–114). Then he grants more:

{{quote:d45e520a-a6ed-5294-8afc-72321f5a3443}}

The seer's likeness speaks as the seer did in Book 1, when Calchas named Agamemnon's dishonoring of Apollo's priest as the cause of the plague (ἠτίμησʼ, 1.94; see [[calchas]]), and ἀπητίμησε, “dishonored”, repeats the charge Thetis brought to Zeus, that Agamemnon had dishonored her son (ἠτίμησεν, 1.507; see [[achilles]] and [[time]]). αἴτιος, “to blame” (13.111; see [[aitios]]), is the word Agamemnon will refuse when he makes peace with Achilles: “I am not to blame, but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in mist” (19.86–87). In Book 9 he admitted that he had been blinded (ἀασάμην, 9.116, 9.119; see [[ate]]). Poseidon leaves the question open. Whoever is to blame, the army may not hold back from war: “the hearts of good men can be healed” (13.114–115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]).

**His daughters.** Idomeneus kills Othryoneus, who had asked Priam for “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam, / Cassandra, without bride-price”, promising to drive the Achaeans from Troy (13.363–369; see [[othryoneus]], [[cassandra]] and [[idomeneus]]). Over the body he mocks the bargain with an offer from the other side:

{{quote:c11cf530-7db5-5f74-82a6-4ee0e576c89c}}

Agamemnon had made such an offer in earnest. To win Achilles back he offered any of his three daughters, Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa, “without bride-price” (ἀνάεδνον, 9.144–148 = 9.286–290; see [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]] and [[bride-price]]), the word in which Othryoneus asked for Cassandra (13.366), and Achilles refused to marry the daughter of the son of Atreus even if she rivaled Aphrodite in beauty (9.388–391). “Bringing her from Argos” (13.379): the daughters live in his hall at home (9.144; see [[argos]]). The taunt costs nothing: the king is absent, wounded earlier in the day, and returns to the story only in Book 14 (11.251–283, 14.27–29).
