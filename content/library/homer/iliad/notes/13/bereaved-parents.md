**A father who followed.** Harpalion, son of king Pylaemenes, “had followed his dear father to make war / at Troy, and did not come back again to his native land” (13.644–645; see [[harpalion]] and [[pylaemenes]]). Meriones' arrow kills him as he draws back, and he lies stretched out like a worm, breathing out his life in his companions' arms (13.650–655; see [[like-a-worm]]). The Paphlagonians lift him into a chariot and take him to Ilios, “grieving; and his father went with them, letting tears fall, / and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.656–659; see [[tears]] and [[poine]]). The father who brought his son to the war walks behind his body. Menelaus killed Pylaemenes, leader of the Paphlagonians, in Book 5 (5.576–579), and how he can walk behind his son's body here is an old puzzle (see [[pylaemenes-dead-and-alive]]).

**A father who foretold.** Euchenor's father was Polyidus, a seer of Corinth, who told him often that he would perish of a grievous sickness in his own halls, or be beaten down by the Trojans among the ships; Euchenor knew his fate and chose the war (13.663–670; see [[euchenor]], [[polyidus-the-seer]] and [[nousos]]). The poem has met seer-fathers before. Merops of Percote, who knew prophecy above all men, would not let his two sons go to war, and they did not obey him (2.831–834 = 11.329–332), and Eurydamas, the interpreter of dreams, did not read dreams for his sons when they went (5.148–151; see the note on Book 5). Polyidus told his son, and his son went with the knowledge.

**Gods and their offspring.** Among the gods the book shows grief without the power to save. Poseidon is “angry with all his heart / for his grandson, fallen in grim combat”, Amphimachus, son of Cteatus, and goes along the ships to rouse the Danaans (13.206–209; see [[amphimachus]] and [[the-moliones]]). Ares does not even know. When Deiphobus kills Ascalaphus, “the son of Enyalius”, the narrator says that loud-voiced Ares “had not yet heard at all / that his son had fallen in the fierce battle”, for he sat on the peak of Olympus beneath golden clouds, held back by the counsels of Zeus (13.518–525; see [[ascalaphus]] and [[ares]]). When Hera tells him, he beats his thighs and arms to avenge his son, and Athena stops him (15.110–142). The article above sets the gods, who can save their sons for a time, against mortal fathers, who cannot; Book 13 shows two gods who could not.

**Spared.** Two of Priam's sons are wounded and live: Deiphobus is led from the battle by his brother Polites, and Agenor binds up Helenus' pierced hand, and Paris tells Hector that the son of Cronus kept off death from them (13.533–539, 13.593–600, 13.781–783; see [[deiphobus]] and [[helenus]]). Idomeneus' boast to Deiphobus had named the old king among those he came to Troy to harm: “to be an evil to you and to your father and to the other Trojans” (13.454; see [[priam]]).
