summary: Deiphobus breaks Meriones' spear on his shield, kills Hypsenor with a spear meant for Idomeneus to avenge Asius, fetches Aeneas to defend Alcathous, and kills Ascalaphus, son of Ares, with a second spear meant for Idomeneus, until Meriones wounds him in the arm and his brother Polites leads him from the battle.

**Behind the shield.** “Among them Deiphobus strode in high spirits, / the son of Priam” (13.156–157), stepping lightly forward under cover of his round shield (ὑπασπίδια προποδίζων, 13.158; see [[son-of-priam]] and [[balanced-on-every-side]]). Meriones hits the bull's-hide shield, and his long spear snaps at the socket; Deiphobus holds the shield away from him “and feared in his heart / the spear of wise-hearted Meriones” (13.159–164), and Meriones, “terribly enraged … for the victory and for the spear”, goes back for another (13.165–168; see [[meriones]], [[shield]] and [[spear]]). Telling Idomeneus of it, Meriones calls him ὑπερηνορέων, “overweening” (13.258). The broken spear brings Meriones to Idomeneus' hut and to their talk of courage (13.246–294; see [[idomeneus]]).

**For Asius.** When Idomeneus has killed Asius, Deiphobus comes close, “grieving for Asius”, and throws (13.402–403; see [[asius]]). Idomeneus crouches under his shield, and the spear flies over it with a dry ring; “yet Deiphobus did not let it go in vain from his heavy hand”, and it strikes Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, in the liver (13.404–412; see [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]]). He boasts aloud:

{{quote:7556c548-64ea-5402-aa73-a828488da333}}

“The strong gatekeeper” is Hades (πυλάρταο, 8.367; see [[gatekeeper]] and [[hades]]). The boast grieves the Argives and stirs Antilochus, who stands over Hypsenor and covers him with his shield (13.417–420; see [[antilochus]]).

**To Aeneas.** Idomeneus kills Alcathous and answers the boast in its own terms: “Deiphobus, shall we then think it a fair exchange, / three killed for one, since you boast like this?” (13.446–447). He calls him δαιμόνιε, “strange one”, and bids him stand and learn his descent from Zeus, Minos and Deucalion, come to be an evil “to you and to your father and to the other Trojans” (13.448–454; see [[minos]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Deiphobus is torn two ways, whether to take a companion or to try it alone, and judges “the better way” to be to fetch Aeneas, whom he finds standing at the back of the throng (13.455–461; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). He appeals to kinship: Alcathous, “being your brother-in-law, reared you in his house when you were small” (13.463–467; see [[aeneas]] and [[alcathous]]). Aeneas calls to his side Deiphobus first, with Paris and Agenor, “who were leaders of the Trojans with him” (13.489–491).

**Ascalaphus.** As Idomeneus gives ground step by step, Deiphobus throws again, “for he had always kept a steady grudge against him” (13.516–517; see [[kotos]]). The poem does not say what began the grudge. He misses again and kills Ascalaphus, son of Ares, whose father sits on Olympus and does not yet know (13.518–525; see [[ascalaphus]] and [[ares]]). As he snatches the dead man's helmet, Meriones leaps on him and stabs his arm, the helmet falls clanging to the ground, and Meriones, leaping in again “like a vulture”, pulls out the spear (13.526–533; see [[like-vultures]]). Polites, “his own brother”, puts his arms around his waist and leads him out of the battle to his horses, and they carry him toward the city, “groaning heavily / in his distress”, the blood running down from his arm (13.533–539; see [[polites]] and [[kasignetos]]). The same verse carries Hector from the field in Book 14 (13.538 = 14.432).

**Gone.** When Hector looks for him among the champions, Paris answers that he and Helenus have gone away, “both struck by long spears / in the hand; but the son of Cronus kept off death from them” (13.758–783; see [[hector]] and [[helenus]]). Meriones' spear struck his upper arm (βραχίων, 13.529, 13.532); χείρ, the word of 13.539 and 13.783, can mean the arm as well as the hand. He does not fight again in the poem; his next part is the shape that Athena takes to deceive Hector before his death (22.226–247).
