---
summary: The Aeneid draws on Book 13 for Neptune's drives over the sea, for Turnus rushing like a rock torn from a mountaintop and Aeneas towering like the snowy Apennine, for the close ranks in which foot presses on foot and man on man, and for the mountain ash cut down by farmers, Virgil's image of the fall of Troy.
---

- Neptune's chariot over the sea (*Aeneid* 1.124–156, 5.816–826): [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]].
- **The rock and the mountain.** In the last book of the *Aeneid* Turnus leaps from his chariot and rushes through the ranks toward the city like a rock that comes crashing headlong from a mountaintop, torn away by the wind, or washed out by a stormy rain, or loosened by the creeping years. The “shameless” mass (*mons improbus*) is carried down the steep and bounds over the ground, rolling woods, herds and men along with it, and Turnus breaks through the scattered ranks to the walls (*Aeneid* 12.684–690). Hector came on “like a rolling boulder from a rock”, which a river in winter flood thrusts down from a crest, “breaking with its untold rain the props of the shameless rock” (ἀναιδέος … πέτρης); it leaps up high and flies, and the forest crashes beneath it (13.137–141; see [[the-rolling-boulder]] and [[hector]]). Virgil keeps the rain, the shameless rock, the leap and the forest, but his rock does not stop. Hector's was halted on level ground, as Hector was at the close ranks (13.141–148); Turnus reaches the walls. A few lines later Aeneas, hearing Turnus' name, comes on as great as Athos, as great as Eryx, as great as father Apennine himself when he roars with his quivering holm oaks and rejoices to lift his snowy head to the sky (*Aeneid* 12.697–703). Hector, when he had agreed to Polydamas' counsel, rushed off “like a snowy mountain” (13.754; see [[like-a-snowy-mountain]]).
- **The close ranks.** When the Trojan and Latin lines meet in Book 10, *haeret pede pes densusque viro vir*, “foot clings to foot, and man close to man” (*Aeneid* 10.361). Around the two Ajaxes “shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man” (13.131, again at 16.215; see [[battle-order]]).
- **The felled ash.** On the night of the sack Aeneas sees all Ilium sink into the fire and Neptune's Troy overturned from its foundations, as when farmers on the mountaintops vie to bring down an ancient mountain ash, hacked with iron and blow after blow of the axe; it nods and threatens, until, overcome by its wounds, it groans its last and falls from the ridge (*Aeneid* 2.624–631). Imbrius falls “like an ash tree / that on the peak of a mountain seen from afar on every side / is cut by the bronze and brings its tender leaves to the ground” (13.178–180; see [[the-felled-ash]] and [[imbrius]]). Virgil's tree is a city.
