summary: Menelaus names Zeus “god of guests” in the only place the Iliad gives him the title, and charges the Trojans with carrying off his wife and possessions after they had been welcomed in his house; on the other side, Paris kills Euchenor in anger for Harpalion, who “had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians”.

  - 13.660-672 | Paris avenges his guest-friend

**Zeus, god of guests.** Menelaus, his heel on the dead Peisander's chest, speaks to the Trojans as the wronged host (13.618–627; see [[menelaus]]):

{{quote:358333ad-21bc-5def-b8bf-b445dd469eb2}}

Ζηνὸς … ξεινίου, “Zeus, god of guests”, comes only here in the Iliad. The Odyssey has the title twice, in Odysseus' words to the Cyclops, that Zeus is “the avenger of suppliants and guests, god of guests, who goes with guests who are to be respected” (*Odyssey* 9.270–271), and in his false tale of the Egyptian king who spared him for fear of “the wrath of Zeus, god of guests” (*Odyssey* 14.283–284; see [[menis]]). The charge is exact. ἐπεὶ φιλέεσθε παρʼ αὐτῇ, “after you had been welcomed by her”, uses φιλέω, the verb of a host's welcome, as Antenor “received as guests and welcomed” Menelaus and Odysseus in Troy (ἐξείνισσα καὶ … φίλησα, 3.207; see [[philotes]]). The wife who received the guests left with them, and the possessions with her. Menelaus' prayer before the duel in Book 3 asked Zeus for vengeance on “the man who first wronged me”, so that men born hereafter might shudder “to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (3.351–354); here the punishment is foretold for the whole city, which Zeus “will one day destroy” (13.625). In the rest of the speech the offense against the host becomes the Trojans' ὕβρις and their insatiable appetite for war (13.631–639; see [[hybris]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]).

**Paris and his guest-friend.** Twenty lines later the bond holds on the Trojan side. Meriones kills Harpalion, son of the Paphlagonian king Pylaemenes, with an arrow as he withdraws (13.643–655), “and Paris was very angry at heart over his killing; / for he had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians” (ξεῖνος γάρ οἱ ἔην, 13.660–661; see [[harpalion]] and [[paris]]). He lets fly an arrow in his anger and kills Euchenor of Corinth (13.662–672; see [[euchenor]]). The man whom Menelaus accuses of betraying a host keeps faith with a guest, and in the only way left to him: no penalty was paid for the dead son (13.659; see [[poine]]), and Paris answers the death with another. Where Paris came to be Harpalion's ξεῖνος among the Paphlagonians the poem does not say.
