summary: Zeus opens Book 13 by turning his eyes away from Troy, sure that no god will break his ban, and Poseidon breaks it at once. He does nothing more in the book, but his will runs through it, and men on both sides speak of his favor, his thunder, his lash and his wrath.

**Looking away.** The book begins with his name.

{{quote:faaba23f-3a21-5855-9503-2f01e20555a0}}

His gaze goes far off, to the Thracians, the Mysians, the mare-milkers and the Abii, “the most just of men” (13.4–6; see [[thracians]], [[mysians]], [[abii]] and [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]). In Books 8 and 11 he sat “looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (8.52, 11.82); now he looks away, trusting his ban, whose words the narrator echoes: no god is to come “to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.9; 8.11; see [[watching-the-war]]). Poseidon “kept no blind watch” and comes at once (13.10; see [[poseidon]]). Hera will have Sleep close those shining eyes (14.236), and over Sarpedon's body Zeus never turns them from the fight (16.644–645).

**His will.** He has looked away, but the battle still goes as he wills. The narrator sets the two sons of Cronus side by side: Zeus “wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector, / to give glory to Achilles”, but not the destruction of the Achaean army, while Poseidon helps the Argives only in secret, because “Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.345–357; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Together they stretch the rope of war over both armies (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). His counsels keep Ares and the other gods on Olympus, “shut out from the war” (13.523–525; see [[olympians]]); he rouses the allies newly come from Ascania (13.794); and, Paris says, he kept death from Deiphobus and Helenus (13.783).

**In men's mouths.** Hector trusts that “the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.153–154; see [[husband-of-hera]]). Idomeneus thinks “it must be the pleasure of the son of Cronus, supreme in might, / that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (13.226–227; see [[supreme-in-might]]), the verse of Polydamas at the ditch (12.70) and of Agamemnon in despair (14.70). The narrator has just said the opposite (13.348–349). The ships will burn, Idomeneus says, only if “the son of Cronus himself / casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships” (13.319–320); Zeus will wait for the blaze of a burning ship (15.596–600). And Idomeneus traces his line from him: “Zeus first fathered Minos, a guardian over Crete” (13.450; see [[minos]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

Menelaus names him twice. The Trojans, he says, did not fear “the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests, who will one day destroy your steep city” (13.624–625; see [[who-thunders-loud]] and [[xenia]]). ἐριβρεμέτης is found only here in Homer, and ξείνιος only here in the Iliad. Then he turns to the god: “Father Zeus, they say that you surpass in wisdom all others, / men and gods; and all these things come from you. / How you favor men who are arrogant” (13.631–633; see [[father-zeus]] and [[hybris]]).

Ajax tells Hector that the Achaeans were beaten down “by the evil lash of Zeus” (13.812; 12.37), and that Hector will soon “pray to father Zeus and the other immortals” for swift horses (13.818). Hector answers: “If only I were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus / all my days, and queen Hera had borne me” (13.825–826; see [[aegis-bearing]]), the form of his wish in Book 8 to be immortal and ageless (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). Poseidon, as Calchas, had said that Hector “claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.54).

**The last word.** In the narrator's similes Zeus brandishes the lightning, “showing a sign to mortals” (13.242–244; see [[like-lightning]]), and a storm wind falls on the sea “under the thunder of father Zeus” (13.795–797). The book that began with his name ends with the roar of both armies reaching “the upper air and the rays of Zeus” (13.837). Διὸς αὐγάς, “the rays of Zeus”, occurs only here.
