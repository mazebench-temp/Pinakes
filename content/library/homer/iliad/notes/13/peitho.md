summary: Men in Book 13 trust and are persuaded. Othryoneus fights “trusting in the promises” Priam made him, Idomeneus stands like a boar trusting in its courage, and the Locrians follow their leader trusting in bows and slings; Polydamas tells Hector that he cannot be made to heed persuasion, and Paris, at the end, persuades him.

**Trusting.** The perfect πέποιθα, “trust, be confident”, runs through the book. Poseidon tells the Achaean leaders, “I for my part am confident / that if you fight you will save our ships” (13.95–96; see [[aidos]]). Othryoneus, promised Cassandra by Priam, “fought, trusting in the promises” (ὑποσχεσίῃσι πιθήσας, 13.369; see [[othryoneus]]), and Idomeneus kills him and mocks the bargain (13.370–382). Idomeneus himself stands his ground like a boar in the mountains, “trusting in its courage” (ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, 13.471; see [[alke]]). The Locrians have no helmets, shields or spears, “but trusting in bows and in well-twisted sheep's wool / they had followed him to Ilios”, and with these they break the Trojan battalions from behind (13.716–718; see [[locrians]] and [[sling]]). Each man trusts in what he has; the promise is the one thing that fails. The Trojans of Book 12 went at the wall, “Trusting in his portents and in their own strength” (12.256), and in this book they are held at the ships.

**Not to be persuaded.** Polydamas begins his counsel with a complaint: “Hector, there is no dealing with you, to make you heed persuasion” (ἀμήχανός ἐσσι παραρρητοῖσι πιθέσθαι, 13.726; see [[polydamas]] and [[different-gifts]]). παράρρητος, “to be swayed by words”, is found elsewhere only in Phoenix's account of the heroes of old: “they could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (9.524–526). This time Hector is persuaded; Polydamas' “safe counsel pleased Hector” (13.748), and he goes to gather the leaders (see [[obedience-and-persuasion]]).

**Persuaded.** Hector finds his leaders dead or wounded and rebukes Paris, and Paris answers him with the facts and a promise of courage (13.765–787; see [[beyond-ones-strength]]). “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind” (ὣς εἰπὼν παρέπεισεν ἀδελφειοῦ φρένας ἥρως, 13.788). The verse is the one with which Agamemnon persuaded Menelaus not to meet Hector in Book 7 (7.120). Then the brother held back from the fight; now the two go together where the battle is thickest (13.789).
