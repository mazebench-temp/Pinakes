**Not unavenged.** Deiphobus throws at Idomeneus “grieving for Asius” (13.403), misses, and strikes Hypsenor; then he boasts: “Asius does not lie unavenged after all; no, I tell you, / even as he goes to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper, / he will be glad in his heart, since I have given him an escort” (οὐ μὰν αὖτʼ ἄτιτος κεῖτʼ Ἄσιος, 13.414–416; see [[deiphobus]], [[asius]] and [[euchomai]]). ἄτιτος, “unpaid, unavenged”, belongs with τίνω, “pay” (see the article above). A death in battle is a debt, and the dead man's side pays it back with another death. Idomeneus answers in the same terms after he has killed Alcathous, “shall we then think it a fair exchange, / three killed for one, since you boast like this?” (13.446–447; see [[kotos]]). In the next book the Trojan Acamas states the rule: he has killed Promachus “so that the penalty for my brother / may not long remain unpaid; that is why a man prays / to leave a kinsman in his halls to avenge his ruin” (14.482–485).

**No penalty.** Harpalion, son of the Paphlagonian king Pylaemenes, is killed by Meriones' arrow and carried to Ilios, “and his father went with them, letting tears fall, / and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (ποινὴ δʼ οὔ τις παιδὸς ἐγίγνετο τεθνηῶτος, 13.658–659; see [[harpalion]], [[pylaemenes]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). The verse-end is Ajax's in Book 9, where a man “accepts the blood-price for his dead son” from the killer and lets him stay among his people (ποινὴν … παιδὸς … τεθνηῶτος, 9.632–636). Between enemies at war no such price is offered. What follows is a death of another kind: Paris, angry over Harpalion, his guest-friend, shoots Euchenor (13.660–672; see [[xenia]] and the translation's note on 13.643–659).
