summary: Taunted by Idomeneus, Deiphobus “was torn two ways in thought”, whether to draw back and take one of the Trojans as his companion or to try Idomeneus alone, and he chose to go for Aeneas (13.455–459).
refs: [13.456-457]
---

“So he spoke, and Deiphobus was torn two ways in thought, / whether to take as his companion one of the great-hearted Trojans, / drawing back, or to try it even alone” (13.455–457; see [[deiphobus]]). The two courses are set out in the usual double question, ἤ … ἦ, “whether … or”. Idomeneus has just challenged him to come and stand against him (13.448), and the choice is between meeting the challenge alone and finding help. Deiphobus finds help: “as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way: / to go for Aeneas” (13.458–459; see [[decision-scene]] and [[aeneas]]). The poem does not count it as cowardice. Idomeneus himself calls his companions when Aeneas comes against him, afraid of the younger man (13.477–486), and Diomedes chose a companion for the night raid because “when two go together, one sees before the other” (10.224; see [[when-two-go-together]]). Menelaus will weigh the same choice over Patroclus' body, between standing alone against Hector and finding Ajax, and will give ground and look for Ajax (17.90–116).

A fighter's choice is framed the same way when Antilochus, never still under Poseidon's guard, “took aim in his mind / either to throw at someone, or to rush at him close at hand” (13.558–559; see [[antilochus]]).
