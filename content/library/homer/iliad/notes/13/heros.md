---
summary: ἥρως in Book 13 is a title for fighting men on both sides, Achaean and Trojan alike: Peneleos, Agamemnon, Idomeneus and Meriones, and Alcathous, Helenus and Paris.
grc: [ἥρωʼ]
---

The word is spoken of men on both sides, and often at the moment of a deed. Idomeneus is “the hero” as he drags Othryoneus by the foot and as he stabs Alcathous (13.384, 13.439), and Meriones as he draws back in anger at his broken spear and as he draws his spear out of Adamas (13.164, 13.575); on the other side Helenus is “the hero and lord” when Menelaus goes at him (13.582), and Paris “the hero” when he persuades his brother's mind (13.788). Alcathous, about to be killed, is “the hero Alcathous”, son-in-law of Anchises (ἥρωʼ Ἀλκάθοον, 13.428; see [[alcathous]]). Among the Achaeans the title goes also to Peneleos, whom Poseidon urges on (13.92), and to Agamemnon, in the god's grudging concession that “the hero, son of Atreus” may be to blame (13.112; see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

The plural names the whole fighting class. The two sons of Cronus “were making grievous sorrows for the heroes” (ἀνδράσιν ἡρώεσσιν, 13.346), and Menelaus says that the Trojans are eager to burn the ships “and to kill the Achaean heroes” (13.629; see the article above).
