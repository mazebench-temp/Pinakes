---
summary: Love stands beside sleep, song and dancing in Menelaus' list of the pleasures of which a man has his fill sooner than of war, “but the Trojans are insatiable of battle”.
---

φιλότης appears once in Book 13, in its sense of love, in Menelaus' complaint to Zeus: “Of all things there is satiety, of sleep and of love (καὶ ὕπνου καὶ φιλότητος), / of sweet song and of flawless dancing” (13.636–637; see [[satiety-of-all-things]] and [[hypnos]]). The love of the list is the pleasure that the formula “to mingle in love” names (see the article above). A few lines earlier Menelaus has spoken of the other bond the word covers, the welcome of host and guest: the Trojans carried off his wife and possessions “after you had been welcomed by her” (φιλέεσθε, 13.627; see [[xenia]]).

In the next book the word returns beside sleep. Hera asks Aphrodite for “love and longing” (φιλότητα καὶ ἵμερον, 14.198), and Zeus falls asleep on the peak of Gargarus “overcome by sleep and love” (ὕπνῳ καὶ φιλότητι δαμείς, 14.352–353), leaving Poseidon free to help the Achaeans (see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Sleep and love stand together in one verse only in these two places in Homer.
