summary: Book 13 is a book of exhortations. Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, puts heart into the two Ajaxes and shames the men in the rear; Hector calls on the Trojans to stand by him; Poseidon, in Thoas' voice, stirs Idomeneus; Idomeneus and Aeneas call their companions to the fight over Alcathous; and Hector rebukes Paris, while Ajax's challenge tells Hector that the Achaeans too have hands (13.47–58, 13.95–124, 13.150–154, 13.219–238, 13.463–490, 13.765–820).

**The two Ajaxes.** Poseidon's first speech is an exhortation in the form of a prophecy: “Ajaxes, you two will save the army of the Achaeans / if you remember your courage, and not chilling rout” (13.47–48). The danger is where Hector leads, and “may some god put it in your minds / to stand firm yourselves and to urge the others” (13.55–56). Then the god strikes them with his staff and fills them with might (13.59–61; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[divine-epiphany]]).

**Shame.** To the men in the rear, who weep with weariness as they watch the Trojans come over the wall, Poseidon speaks the book's longest exhortation (13.95–124). It has the parts of the pattern (see the entry): the address, “Shame, Argives, young men!” (αἰδὼς Ἀργεῖοι, 13.95; see [[aidos]]); the stake, “if you fight you will save our ships” (13.96); the enemy changed, once like deer quick to flee and now fighting by the ships (13.99–110; see [[timid-deer]] and [[o-popoi]]); the blame set aside, for even if Agamemnon is wholly to blame for dishonoring Achilles, that is no reason to hold back (13.111–114; see [[who-is-to-blame]]); a maxim, “the hearts of good men can be healed” (13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]); the rebuke, “Weaklings” (13.120), and the charge to set in their minds “shame and blame” (αἰδῶ καὶ νέμεσιν, 13.121–122; see [[nemesis]]); and the danger, Hector, who “has broken the gates and the long bar” (13.123–124). “So with his orders the earth-holder roused the Achaeans” (13.125), and the battalions form around the two Ajaxes (13.126–135; see [[battle-order]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

**Hector.** Checked at the close ranks, Hector shouts: “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand, / stand by me!” The Achaeans will give ground before his spear, “if truly / the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.150–154; see [[hector]] and [[husband-of-hera]]). “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (13.155), the verse that ends his rallies in Books 5, 6 and 11 (5.470, 6.72, 11.291).

**Thoas' voice.** Poseidon, in Thoas' voice, asks Idomeneus where the Achaeans' threats have gone (13.219–220; see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). Idomeneus answers that no man is to blame, and bids “Thoas” go on calling on every man, as he always urges on another whom he sees holding back (13.222–230). The god curses the man who holds back of his own will and calls him to arms: “we must hurry at this together”, for “Even the prowess of very wretched men counts when it is joined” (13.232–238; see [[the-work-of-many]] and [[idomeneus]]).

**Calls for help.** Deiphobus calls on Aeneas to defend his brother-in-law (13.463–467). Idomeneus, alone and afraid of the younger man, calls his companions by name, “Come here, friends, and defend me” (13.481–486), and Aeneas on the other side calls his (13.489–490; see [[aeneas]]). The exhortation becomes an appeal, and both sides gather around the body of Alcathous (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

**Rebuke and challenge.** Hector finds Paris on the left, “encouraging his companions and urging them on to fight” (13.767), and rebukes him in the words of his first rebuke in Book 3 (13.769 = 3.39); Paris answers that he has not held back, and leads him into the thick of the battle (13.765–789; see [[paris]]). Ajax's challenge turns the exhortation outward: “We are not without knowledge of battle at all”, he tells Hector, “we too have hands ready to defend” the ships (13.810–814; see [[ajax]] and [[single-combat]]).
