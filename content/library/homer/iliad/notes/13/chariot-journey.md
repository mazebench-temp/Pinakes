summary: Poseidon's journey from Samothrace to the Achaean camp has every step of the pattern on a grander scale: four strides to Aegae, the yoking in the verses of Zeus' in Book 8, a drive over the waves while the sea beasts frolic and the sea parts for joy, and, in a cave between Tenedos and Imbros, the unyoking, the ambrosial fodder and golden hobbles that cannot be broken or loosed (13.17–38).

**Strides.** Poseidon comes down from the peak of Samothrace, and the mountains and the woods tremble under his feet: “Three strides he took, and with the fourth he reached his goal, / Aegae” (13.17–21; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]], [[samothrace]] and [[aegae]]). There, at his golden house in the depths of the sea, the journey by chariot begins:

{{quote:9e5e9bae-3cec-5ac8-9da8-2e9e019ef718}}

**The yoking.** The first four verses are those of Zeus' yoking in Book 8, with the opening words changed (13.23–26 = 8.41–44): the bronze-footed horses with their golden manes, the god putting on gold, the golden whip, the mounting (see [[bronze-footed]], [[arming-scene]] and [[chariot]]).

**The drive.** Zeus' horses flew “between earth and the starry sky” (8.46); Poseidon's run over the waves. The sea beasts frolic around him “out of their lairs, and did not fail to know their lord”, the sea parts for joy, and the bronze axle is not wetted beneath (13.27–30; see [[sea-beasts]]). The creatures of the sea know their god at once, as the son of Oileus will know him from behind by the movements of his feet and legs (13.66–72; see [[divine-epiphany]]). Longinus quotes the drive among his examples of the sublime (see [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]).

**The cave and the hobbles.** “There is a wide cave in the depths of the deep sea, / between Tenedos and rocky Imbros” (13.32–33; see [[tenedos]] and [[imbros]]). There Poseidon halts his horses, unyokes them and sets ambrosial fodder before them, in the verses of Iris' halt in Book 5 (13.34–35 ≈ 5.368–369; see [[ambrosia]]), and casts golden hobbles around their feet, “unbreakable, not to be loosed, so that they might stay there steadfast / until their lord returned” (13.36–38), in the verse with which Hephaestus forges the bonds for Ares and Aphrodite in the Odyssey (13.37 = Od. 8.275). Hera and Zeus hide their horses in mist at the end of a journey (5.775–776, 8.49–50); Poseidon stables his under the sea. The two adjectives of the hobbles, ἀρρήκτους ἀλύτους, return for the rope of war that Zeus and Poseidon stretch over both armies, “unbreakable and not to be loosed” (13.360; see [[the-rope-of-war]] and [[privative-adjectives]]).

**On foot.** The journey ends where Zeus' did not. Zeus drove to Ida to sit and watch (8.47–52); Poseidon leaves his chariot under the sea, “and he went to the army of the Achaeans” (13.38), on foot and in the likeness of Calchas (13.43–45; see [[poseidon]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). He had sat watching on Samothrace before he set out (13.10–16; see [[watching-the-war]]).
