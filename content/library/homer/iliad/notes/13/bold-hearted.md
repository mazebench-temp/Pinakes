---
summary: He would be “very bold of heart,” says the narrator, who could have looked on the battle line bristling with spears and glaring with bronze, and rejoiced.
---

When Idomeneus and Meriones reach the left, the battle comes together like a storm of dust on the roads, and the narrator describes the line: “And the battle, destroyer of mortals, bristled with the spears, / long and flesh-cutting, that they held; and eyes were blinded / by the gleam of bronze from the shining helmets / and the newly burnished corselets and the bright shields / as they came on together; he would be very bold of heart / who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (13.339–344; [[destroyer-of-mortals]], [[long-spears]], [[flesh-cutting]], [[the-dust-storm]]). The bold-hearted man is imagined, as in Menelaus' doubt in Book 10 (10.41). Here he is an onlooker, like the man the narrator imagines in the first battle, who came into the fighting unhurt, led by Athena, and “could make light of it” no longer (4.539–542; [[apostrophe]]). Earlier in the book the battalions around the two Ajaxes were such that “not even Ares could have made light of” them (13.127–128).
