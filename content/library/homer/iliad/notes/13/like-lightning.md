{{quote:a25c50fb-4567-5d71-8127-5f34618abdb6}}

βῆ δʼ ἴμεν ἀστεροπῇ ἐναλίγκιος, ἥν τε Κρονίων / χειρὶ λαβὼν ἐτίναξεν ἀπʼ αἰγλήεντος Ὀλύμπου / δεικνὺς σῆμα βροτοῖσιν· ἀρίζηλοι δέ οἱ αὐγαί· / ὣς τοῦ χαλκὸς ἔλαμπε περὶ στήθεσσι θέοντος: “and went on his way like the lightning that the son of Cronus takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus, showing a sign to mortals; and its flashes are plain for all to see; so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran” (13.242–245). The half-verse of Books 10 and 11 gave the flash alone (λάμφʼ ὥς τε στεροπή, 10.154, 11.66). Here ἀστεροπή, the fuller form of στεροπή, is drawn in full: the god takes it in his hand and shakes it from Olympus (see [[gleaming-olympus]] and [[son-of-cronus]]), and ἀρίζηλοι, “very clear”, are its αὐγαί, its flashes. The bronze about Idomeneus' chest is what shines, as the bronze of the planted spears and of Hector's armor shone before.

The lightning is a σῆμα, “a sign”. Zeus' lightning on the right was a sign of good fortune for the Achaeans as they sailed (2.353), and for the Trojans on the night before this day (9.236–237). The sign Idomeneus gives is the sign of his coming. Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, has just rebuked him and told him to arm (13.215–239; see [[poseidon]]); he goes to his hut, puts on his armor and takes two spears (13.240–241; see [[arming-scene]]), and Meriones meets him at the door (13.246; see [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]).

The last two verses return for Achilles running across the plain to Troy in Book 22. Priam sees him shining like the star of late summer, the dog of Orion, whose “flashes are plain for all to see” among the stars, and which is made an evil sign; “so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran” (22.26–32, with 22.27 and 22.32 in the words of 13.244–245; see [[the-autumn-star]]).
