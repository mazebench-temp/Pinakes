summary: Almost every death of Book 13 becomes a fight for the body. Hector is driven from Amphimachus' body and the Achaeans carry off both dead; the two Ajaxes carry off Imbrius like lions with a goat; Idomeneus drags Othryoneus by the foot, and Asius dies coming to his defense; Antilochus stands over the fallen Hypsenor; and the two sides fight hand to hand over Alcathous and then over Ascalaphus (13.182–205, 13.383–423, 13.463–539).

**Amphimachus and Imbrius.** Teucer rushes to strip Imbrius, and Hector's spear, thrown at him, kills Amphimachus. Hector rushes to snatch Amphimachus' helmet, and Ajax's spear on the boss of his shield pushes him back “from both the dead, and the Achaeans dragged them away” (13.182–194; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[amphimachus]]). Stichius and Menestheus, the leaders of the Athenians, carry Amphimachus back to the army, and the two Ajaxes lift Imbrius high, like two lions carrying off a goat in their jaws from the dogs (13.195–202; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The rescue ends in an outrage: the son of Oileus cuts off the head and sends it rolling through the throng to Hector's feet (13.202–205; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**Othryoneus and Asius.** Idomeneus kills Othryoneus, mocks him, and “dragged him by the foot through the fierce battle” (13.383; see [[othryoneus]]). Dragging by the foot is the attacker's move, as when Elephenor took Echepolus by the feet in the first battle (4.463), and Hippothous will drag Patroclus by the foot in the same half-verse (17.289). Asius “came to his defense” (ἐπαμύντωρ), on foot in front of his horses, and Idomeneus kills him too (13.384–393; see [[asius]]).

**Hypsenor.** When Deiphobus' spear strikes Hypsenor, Antilochus, grieving, “did not neglect his companion, / but ran and stood over him, and covered him with his shield”, and two companions carry him to the ships (13.418–423). The verses are those with which Ajax stood over the wounded Teucer in Book 8 (8.330–334; see [[antilochus]] and [[shield]]).

**Alcathous and Ascalaphus.** Deiphobus calls on Aeneas to defend his brother-in-law: “let us defend Alcathous, who in former days, / being your brother-in-law, reared you in his house when you were small” (13.465–466; see [[aeneas]] and [[alcathous]]). Idomeneus' companions stand close around him, the Trojans follow Aeneas like sheep after the ram, “and around Alcathous they rushed at one another hand to hand / with their long spears” (13.487–497; see [[battle-order]]). When Ascalaphus falls, the fight moves to his body in the same verse, “And around Ascalaphus they rushed at one another hand to hand” (13.526): Deiphobus snatches the helmet, and Meriones wounds him and makes him drop it (13.526–539; see [[ascalaphus]] and [[helmet]]). Idomeneus, too slow now to strip Oenomaus under the missiles, gives ground step by step (13.509–516; see [[idomeneus]]).

**Under the god's guard.** Antilochus strips Thoon while the Trojans stand around him stabbing at his shield, and Poseidon keeps the bronze from his flesh (13.550–559; see [[poseidon]]). On the other side the Paphlagonians take Harpalion's body to Ilios, and no penalty is paid for him (13.656–659; see [[harpalion]]).
