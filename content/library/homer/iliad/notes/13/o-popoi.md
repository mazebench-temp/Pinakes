---
summary: Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, opens the heart of his rebuke with the full exclamation, “Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes” (13.99), a verse used three more times in the Iliad for a sight the speaker cannot believe.
---

“Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes, / a terrible one, which I used to say would never come to pass: / the Trojans advancing against our ships” (13.99–101; see [[poseidon]] and [[exhortation]]). The cry comes after the opening appeal to shame (13.95–98) and introduces the wonder itself: the Trojans, who used to be like deer quick to flee, are fighting by the ships (13.101–110; see [[timid-deer]]). The god speaks as a mortal and an Achaean would, of “our ships”.

The verse returns three times. Thoas, the Aetolian whose likeness Poseidon takes later in this book, uses it when he sees Hector back in the fight after Ajax's stone (15.286; see [[thoas]]); Achilles, when his spear lies on the ground and Aeneas has vanished (20.344); and Achilles again, when he sees Lycaon, whom he had sold to Lemnos, back on the bank of the river (21.54). Each time the wonder is a reversal the speaker thought impossible.
