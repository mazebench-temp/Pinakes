---
summary: Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, opens his rebuke of the Achaean leaders with the cry “Shame, Argives, young men!” and ends it by bidding each of them set in his mind “shame and blame”, the only place in Homer where the two words stand together.
passages:
  - 13.95-124 | Poseidon rebukes the Achaean leaders
---

**The cry.** The Achaeans in the rear are worn out and weep as they watch the Trojans come over the wall (13.83–89). Poseidon comes among them in the likeness of Calchas and goes first to the leaders, Teucer, Leitus, Peneleos, Thoas, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus (13.90–94; see [[poseidon]]). His first word is the rally-cry αἰδὼς Ἀργεῖοι, “Shame, Argives”, with which Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, and then Agamemnon on Odysseus' ship roused the army (5.787, 8.228; see the note on Book 8). They went on κάκʼ ἐλέγχεα, εἶδος ἀγητοί, “wretched disgraces, admirable in looks”; Poseidon says κοῦροι νέοι, “young men”, an address found nowhere else in Homer, and speaks as the old seer whose shape he has taken. His speech also borrows Hera's argument. She told the Achaeans that the Trojans never came out before the gates while Achilles fought, “but now they are fighting far from the city, by the hollow ships” (5.788–791); the god speaks that verse again (13.107), after a longer account of the Trojans as they were before, deer that would not face the might and the hands of the Achaeans (13.101–106; see [[timid-deer]]). The cry comes once more at the ships, from Ajax (15.502), and Sarpedon turns it on his own men when Patroclus routs them, “Shame, Lycians!” (16.422).

**Shame and blame.** The speech closes with a pair of nouns: “let each of you set in his mind / shame and blame; for a great quarrel has arisen” (αἰδῶ καὶ νέμεσιν, 13.121–122; see [[nemesis]] and [[neikos]]). αἰδώς is felt by the man who might hold back, νέμεσις by those who see him do it (see the article above). Poseidon asks each man to hold both: his own shame, and the indignation he would rouse in others. He has just shown them his own: a wretch who held back from war he would not quarrel with, “but with you I am indignant with all my heart” (13.118–119). The words stand together only here in Homer; in Hesiod Aidos and Nemesis leave mankind together at the end of the age of iron (*Works and Days* 197–201). In the fighting at the ships the appeal comes back in another form. Ajax and then Nestor call on the Achaeans to “set shame in your hearts” (αἰδῶ θέσθʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 15.561, 15.661), and the narrator says that the Argives, driven back to their huts, stayed together and did not scatter, “for shame and fear held them” (15.657–658; see [[deos]]).

**Shame is for the best.** The rebuke is addressed to the leaders, “all of you, the best men in the army” (13.117; see [[aristos]]), and its burden is μεθημοσύνη, the slackness of holding back (13.108, 13.121; see the translation's table). Poseidon grants that Agamemnon may be wholly to blame for dishonoring Achilles, and denies that this gives anyone leave to hold back from war (13.111–114; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]). It works. The battalions take their stand around the two Ajaxes, shield pressed on shield and man on man (13.125–135; see [[battle-order]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]).
