**Poseidon's staff.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, speaks first to the two Ajaxes, “who were already eager themselves”: he fears only the place where Hector leads the Trojans “like a flame”, and he asks them to stand firm and urge on the others (13.43–58; see [[poseidon]], [[calchas]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). He strikes them both with his staff, fills them with might and is gone like a hawk (13.59–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]). The son of Oileus knows the god (13.66–75; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Telamonian Ajax speaks only of himself:

{{quote:9b0fb1bf-fffc-5567-a6f4-74e928603128}}

Poseidon has just said that he does not fear “the irresistible hands of the Trojans” (13.49); Ajax claims the epithet for his own (13.77), and Idomeneus will grant it to the Ajaxes and Teucer (13.318; see [[irresistible-hands]]). His wish to fight Hector “even alone” (καὶ οἶος, 13.79) recalls their duel, which he opened by telling Hector that he would now learn, “man to man”, what chiefs the Danaans had (οἰόθεν οἶος, 7.226; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). The two speak “rejoicing in the joy of battle that the god had cast into their hearts” (13.82; see [[charme]]).

**The close ranks.** The battalions that stop Hector form “around the two Ajaxes”, the best men, chosen out, shield pressed on shield (13.126–135; see [[battle-order]]). Hector comes on like a rolling boulder and is pushed back, reeling (13.136–148; see [[the-rolling-boulder]] and [[hector]]).

**Over the dead.** When Hector has killed Amphimachus and rushes to tear the helmet from his head, Ajax thrusts at him; Hector is so covered in bronze that “none of his skin showed”, and Ajax stabs the boss of his shield and pushes him back from both the dead (13.188–194; see [[amphimachus]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Then the two Ajaxes carry off Imbrius, Teucer's victim, like two lions with a goat snatched from the dogs, and strip him (13.197–202; see [[imbrius]] and [[two-lions-and-a-goat]]).

**Idomeneus' measure.** Choosing where to fight, Idomeneus leaves “the middle of the ships” to “the two Ajaxes and Teucer” (13.312–313), and says of the great Ajax:

{{quote:8d2de3f6-1818-5c27-95c6-56ad940e1f3b}}

Ajax is mortal and can be wounded, but no mortal could make him give way, and in the standing fight not even Achilles (see [[demeter]], [[achilles]] and [[breaker-of-men]]). The Catalogue made him the best of the men while Achilles kept up his wrath (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and in Book 7 he measured himself by Achilles, “breaker of men” (7.228). Idomeneus leaves Achilles only his speed (see the translation's note on 13.325).

**The ships of Ajax.** Hector holds on where he first broke in, “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were”, below the lowest stretch of the wall (13.679–684; see [[protesilaus]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The poem does not say which Ajax. Telamonian Ajax's ships stood at one end of the camp (8.224–226 = 11.7–9), and in Book 15 he defends Protesilaus' ship from the decks (15.674–746); but the Locrians of the son of Oileus fight here (13.686, 13.712–722; see the translation's note on 13.681). The critics whom Strabo reports took the ships for Telamonian Ajax's. Lying at the end of the line beside those of Protesilaus' Thessalians, not beside the Athenians, they told against the verse in which Ajax stations his men from Salamis with the Athenian battalions (2.558), a verse that some said Solon or Peisistratus had added (Strabo 9.1.10; see [[solon-and-salamis]]). The Athenians, all the same, fight at this place (13.689–691).

**The yoke.** There the two Ajaxes stand together: “Ajax, the swift son of Oileus, no longer stood at all / apart from Telamonian Ajax, not even a little”, and they strain side by side like two oxen at one plow (13.701–708; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]). Then the poem tells them apart by their men. “But with the son of Telamon there followed many brave / men, his companions, who would take over his shield / whenever weariness and sweat came over his knees” (13.709–711), while the Locrians do not follow the son of Oileus (13.712–722; see [[locrians]]). The great shield needs relief (see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). In Book 16, under the missiles at the ships, his left shoulder tires of holding it steady and the sweat pours from all his limbs (16.102–111).

**The challenge.** After Polydamas' counsel Hector leads the Trojans back into the fight like Ares, testing the battalions behind his shield, but he does not shake the Achaeans (13.802–808).

{{quote:beef1251-366a-5741-9d79-13d4126ddad2}}

He comes “with long strides” (μακρὰ βιβάσθων), as he went out to meet Hector in Book 7 (μακρὰ βιβάς, 7.213) and as he will stride along the decks of the ships (15.676, 15.686). The Achaeans, he says, were beaten “by the evil lash of Zeus” (13.812), the lash that penned them in at the start of Book 12 (12.37; see [[zeus]] and [[strange-one]]). He ends with a prophecy: soon Hector, in flight, will pray to Zeus that his horses be “swifter than hawks” to carry him back to the city (13.817–820; see [[hawk]]). An eagle flies by on the right as he speaks, and the army shouts, emboldened by the omen (13.821–823; see [[eagle]] and [[divination]]).

**Hector's answer.** Hector answers with abuse, “Ajax, blundering in speech, you braggart, what a thing you have said!” (13.824; see [[what-a-word]] and the translation's note), and swears by an impossible wish, to be the son of Zeus and Hera, that Ajax, if he dares to face his spear, will glut the dogs and birds of the Trojans with his fat and flesh (13.825–832). The spear, he says, will tear Ajax's “lily-white skin” (13.830), a word used elsewhere in Homer only of the voice of the cicadas (λειριόεις, 3.152). The meeting comes in Book 14: Hector's spear stops where the two straps cross on Ajax's chest, and Ajax fells him with a stone, as in their duel (14.402–420; 7.268–272).
