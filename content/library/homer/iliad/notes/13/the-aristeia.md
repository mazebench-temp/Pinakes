---
summary: Book 13 holds the aristeia of Idomeneus, the old Cretan king's great day. Roused by Poseidon, he arms and goes out like the lightning of Zeus and like Ares with Rout, kills Othryoneus, Asius and Alcathous in turn and boasts his descent from Zeus, then stands against Aeneas like a boar at bay, until his legs fail him and he gives ground step by step (13.206–516).

**The shining start.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, rouses Idomeneus as he comes from a wounded companion (13.210–238; see [[divine-epiphany]]). He arms in a single verse “and went on his way like the lightning that the son of Cronus / takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus, / showing a sign to mortals” (13.242–244; see [[arming-scene]] and [[like-lightning]]). With Meriones he goes into the war as Ares goes with his son Rout (13.298–305; see [[ares-and-rout]]), and the Trojans see him “like a flame in his courage” (13.330).

**The killings.** “Then, though he was half gray, calling on the Danaans, / Idomeneus leapt among the Trojans and roused rout among them” (13.361–362; see [[half-gray]]). He kills Othryoneus and mocks his bargain for Cassandra (13.363–383), Asius, whose death “through the spear of Idomeneus” the narrator foretold (13.384–393; 12.115–117), and Alcathous, whom Poseidon binds for him (13.427–444), and over Alcathous he boasts his descent from Zeus (13.445–454; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[androktasia]]). The god's help comes at the start and at the third killing; there is no wound and no prayer.

**The great opponent.** Deiphobus fetches Aeneas (13.455–467). Idomeneus does not flee “as if he were a darling child”, but stands like a boar in the mountains that awaits the hunters (13.470–477; see [[the-bristling-boar]] and [[darling]]). Yet he calls his companions to him, afraid, for Aeneas “has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power”, and if they were of an age, one or the other would soon carry off the mastery (13.481–486; see [[kratos]]). The two men, “the equals of Ares”, fight over the body; Aeneas' spear misses, and Idomeneus kills Oenomaus (13.499–508).

**The end.** The day ends not with a wound but with age. Idomeneus cannot strip Oenomaus under the missiles, “For his legs were no longer steady when he rushed, / to dart after his own spear or to avoid a missile”; he can still keep off the pitiless day in close fighting, but his feet no longer carry him nimbly out of the war, and he goes “step by step away” (13.509–516; see [[idomeneus]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). Deiphobus throws at him once more and kills Ascalaphus instead (13.516–520), and Idomeneus is not named again in the book. The episode is commonly called the aristeia of Idomeneus, though no ancient title names it; the ancient title of the book is the battle at the ships (see [[title-of-book-13]]).
