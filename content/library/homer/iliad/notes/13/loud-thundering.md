---
summary: Hector, driven back by the close ranks, says that “the loud-thundering husband of Hera” has roused him.
---

Hector has given ground before the close ranks around the Ajaxes, and he shouts to the Trojans and their allies that the Achaeans will not hold him back for long, “if truly / the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.153–154; [[hector]], [[husband-of-hera]]). The formula ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, often in oaths and wishes, here carries Hector's confidence. In Book 12 he set the counsels of “loud-thundering Zeus” against the bird of omen (12.235–236). In Book 13 the god's thunder and lightning appear only in comparisons: Idomeneus goes out like the lightning of the son of Cronus (13.242–245; [[like-lightning]]), and the Trojans come on like a blast of wind “under the thunder of father Zeus” (13.795–796; [[the-storm-and-the-waves]]). Zeus himself has turned his eyes from Troy (13.1–9). See [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]].
