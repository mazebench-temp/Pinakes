The word appears three times in Book 13, once granting blame and twice refusing it, as it is refused most often in the poem (see the article above and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**Granted.** Poseidon, in the likeness of the seer Calchas, concedes the charge in the strongest terms the Iliad has: “even if in very truth the one wholly to blame is / the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, / because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (εἰ δὴ καὶ πάμπαν ἐτήτυμον αἴτιός ἐστιν, 13.111–113; see [[agamemnon]] and [[time]]). The concession is made in order to be set aside: “for us at least there is no way to hold back from war” (13.114; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]).

**Refused.** Idomeneus answers the god's question, where the Achaeans' threats have gone, by clearing his own people: “Thoas, no man now is to blame, as far as I / know” (οὔ τις ἀνὴρ νῦν γʼ αἴτιος, 13.222–223), and putting the defeat down to the pleasure of Zeus (13.226–227; see [[idomeneus]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). The form of the denial is the one of Books 1 and 3, “not to blame”, and as there it moves the blame elsewhere, here to a god.

**Blaming the blameless.** When Hector rebukes Paris for the losses on the left, Paris answers, “Hector, since your heart is set on blaming one who is not to blame” (ἀναίτιον αἰτιάασθαι, 13.775; see [[paris]] and [[beyond-ones-strength]]). The phrase is the one Patroclus used of Achilles, who “would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.654; see the note on Book 11), and in the Odyssey Eurycleia uses it to Telemachus, who suspects his mother of neglecting the stranger: “do not blame her, child, who is not to blame” (*Odyssey* 20.135). The man who was at fault in Book 3, when he admitted that Hector's rebuke was fair (3.59), is not at fault here, and says so.
