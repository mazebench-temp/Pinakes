---
summary: Menelaus' complaint begins “Father Zeus,” Ajax foretells that Hector will pray “to father Zeus” in flight, and the storm of a simile comes “under the thunder of father Zeus.”
---

Menelaus, over the body of Peisander, turns from the Trojans to the god: “Father Zeus, they say that you surpass in wisdom all others, / men and gods; and all these things come from you. / How you favor men who are arrogant, / the Trojans” (13.631–634; [[menelaus]], [[hybris]]). As in his reproach after the duel, “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365; [[deadly]]), the address of prayer opens a complaint, and it leads into his saying that the Trojans are insatiable of battle (13.636–639; [[satiety-of-all-things]]).

Ajax tells Hector that the time is near when, in flight, “you will pray to father Zeus and the other immortals / that your fine-maned horses may be swifter than hawks” (13.817–819; [[fine-maned]]), in the words with which Hecuba bade Hector pour a libation “to father Zeus and the other immortals” (6.259). And in the simile of the Trojans' advance, the blast of wind “comes down to the plain under the thunder of father Zeus” (13.795–796; [[the-storm-and-the-waves]], [[zeus]]).
