---
summary: Zeus turns his eyes from Troy to the far north, to the Abii, “the most just of men”; the book adds the genitive plural δικαιοτάτων.
grc: [δικαιοτάτων]
---

When he has brought Hector to the ships, Zeus turns his shining eyes away, “looking out far off on the land of the horse-herding Thracians and of the Mysians, who fight hand to hand, and of the proud mare-milkers who live on milk, and of the Abii, the most just of men” (13.4–6; [[zeus]], [[abii]], [[horse-herding]], [[thracians]], [[mysians]], [[noble]]). The superlative that set Chiron apart from the other Centaurs (11.832) closes a list of peoples at the edge of the earth, set against the battle that Zeus no longer watches. Whether Ἀβίων is a name or an epithet was debated in antiquity. Strabo reports that Posidonius took the ἄβιοι for men who live without women (*Geography* 7.3.3). He himself takes them as men without hearths, living in wagons, and argues that men who live frugally on little are rightly called most just, since wrongs arise mostly over contracts and property (*Geography* 7.3.4; see the translation's note on 13.4–6 and [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]).
