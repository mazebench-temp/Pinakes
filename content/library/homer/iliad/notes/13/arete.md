summary: The word comes three times in a hundred lines, all around Idomeneus: Poseidon, as Thoas, tells him that “even the prowess of very wretched men counts when it is joined”, and Idomeneus tells Meriones that he knows his prowess, and that an ambush is where the prowess of men is seen most clearly.
passages:
  - 13.232-238 | Poseidon as Thoas to Idomeneus
  - 13.266-294 | Idomeneus and Meriones at the huts
---

**Joined.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, bids Idomeneus arm and come with him, for the two of them may be of some use: “Even the prowess of very wretched men counts when it is joined, / and we two would know how to fight even against brave men” (συμφερτὴ δʼ ἀρετὴ πέλει ἀνδρῶν καὶ μάλα λυγρῶν, 13.237–238; see [[joined-prowess]]). The maxim gives ἀρετή even to the worst of men, so long as they stand together; the excellence of a fighter is not only a hero's possession.

**Seen.** Prowess must be shown, and someone must see it. Meriones, who has come to the huts for a spear, tells Idomeneus that he too fights among the foremost: “Some other of the bronze-armored Achaeans, perhaps, / may not notice me fighting, but you yourself, I think, know it” (13.272–273; see [[meriones]]). Idomeneus answers, “I know what you are in prowess; why do you need to tell of it?” (οἶδʼ ἀρετὴν οἷός ἐσσι, 13.275), and names the place where it shows best, an ambush, “where most of all the prowess of men is seen clearly” (ἔνθα μάλιστʼ ἀρετὴ διαείδεται ἀνδρῶν, 13.277; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[ambush]]). The verb is the one with which Hector, in the word's first use in the poem, said that Diomedes would “learn his own prowess” the next day (διαείσεται, 8.535; see the article above). There the test was one man against another; here it is a man against his own fear, and the proof is in his body, his color, his heart and his teeth (13.279–286).

The praise is given and then stopped. Idomeneus will not have them go on talking so, “like children, / standing here” (13.292–293; see [[nemesis]]), and the proof of Meriones' prowess is left to the battle, where he wounds Deiphobus and kills Adamas and Harpalion (13.528–533, 13.567–575, 13.650–652).
