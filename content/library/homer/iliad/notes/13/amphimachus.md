**The death.** Teucer has killed Imbrius and rushes to strip him; Hector throws at him, Teucer sees the spear coming and dodges, and it strikes Amphimachus in the chest “as he was going into the war” (13.182–187; see [[teucer]] and [[hector]]). Κτεάτου υἷʼ Ἀκτορίωνος, “son of Cteatus, of Actor's line” (13.185): he is one of the two cousins who led the first division of the Epeians in the Catalogue (2.620–621; see [[cteatus]], [[thalpius]] and [[epeians]]). The poem gives him no deed of his own.

**Over the body.** Hector rushes to snatch “the helmet, fitted close to the temples, / from the head of great-hearted Amphimachus” (13.188–189), but Ajax stabs the boss of his shield and drives him back from both the dead, and Stichius and Menestheus, the leaders of the Athenians, carry the body back to the army (13.190–196; see [[ajax]], [[stichius]], [[menestheus]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The son of Oileus, “in his anger for Amphimachus”, cuts off the head of Imbrius and sends it flying to Hector's feet (13.202–205; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**Poseidon's grandson.** “And then indeed Poseidon was angry with all his heart / for his grandson, fallen in grim combat” (13.206–207), and he goes along the huts and ships to rouse the Danaans; in the likeness of Thoas he sends Idomeneus into the battle (13.208–245; see [[poseidon]] and [[idomeneus]]). υἱωνός is a son's son, as Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, is the υἱωνός of Zeus (5.631), so the god is the father of Cteatus. Nestor said as much of the two Moliones, whom “their father, the wide-ruling earth-shaker” hid in mist from his spear (11.750–752; see [[the-moliones]] and the translation's note on 13.206–207). The patronymic makes Cteatus Actor's son; the god's anger makes him Poseidon's.
