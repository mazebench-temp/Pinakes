summary: The narrator says that Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans and Hector, to give glory to Achilles. Idomeneus judges that victory at the ships will be a steep climb for Hector. Two spears break at the socket on a shield: Meriones, whose spear it was, is enraged for the victory he has lost, and Peisander rejoices and hopes for victory, and Menelaus kills him.

---

**Willed.** Victory in the book is Zeus' to give, and the narrator says to whom: “Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector, / to give glory to Achilles, swift of foot” (Τρώεσσι καὶ Ἕκτορι βούλετο νίκην, 13.347–348; see [[kydos]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]), while Poseidon, his mind set on the other side, helps the Achaeans in secret (13.345–357; see [[phrenes]] and [[divine-intervention]]). βούλετο νίκην is the phrase of a god's partisanship: Apollo wished for victory for the Trojans in Book 7 (7.21), and when Hector cuts through the spear of Telamonian Ajax, Ajax knows that Zeus wishes it for them, and the Trojans throw fire on the ship (16.114–123; see the note on Book 8). Idomeneus, choosing where to fight, judges that the victory will not come easily. In the middle the two Ajaxes and Teucer will give Hector his fill of war, and it will be “a steep climb for him, for all his eagerness to fight, / to overcome their might and their irresistible hands / and burn the ships, unless the son of Cronus himself / casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships” (νικήσαντι, 13.312–320; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[teucer]]).

**Lost and hoped for.** Two spears in the book break at the socket on a shield, and the two men take it in opposite ways. Meriones' spear snaps on Deiphobus' shield of bull's hide, and he draws back “and was terribly enraged / on both counts, for the victory and for the spear that he had shattered” (νίκης τε καὶ ἔγχεος, 13.159–166; see [[meriones]] and [[cholos]]); he goes to his hut for another. Peisander's spear breaks on the shield of Menelaus, “and he rejoiced in his heart and hoped for victory” (ἐέλπετο νίκην, 13.605–609). Menelaus draws his sword and kills him (13.610–618; see [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]] and [[menelaus]]). The hope comes once more in the Iliad, and again Menelaus ends it: Dolops, his crest struck off by Meges, fights on “and still hoped for victory”, until Menelaus comes up unseen and strikes him from behind (15.535–543).
