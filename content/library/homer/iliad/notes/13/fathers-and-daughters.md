summary: Three fathers' daughters stand behind the men who die in Book 13. Priam had given a bastard daughter to Imbrius and promised Cassandra, “the most beautiful in form of his daughters”, to Othryoneus; Idomeneus mocks the bargain with an offer of Agamemnon's most beautiful daughter; and Alcathous had married Hippodameia, Anchises' eldest, whom her father and mother loved above all.

**Priam's daughters.** The first Trojan to die in the book, Imbrius, “had as his wife a bastard daughter of Priam, Medesicaste”, and when the Achaeans came he lived beside Priam, “and Priam honored him as he honored his children” (13.173–176; see [[medesicaste]], [[imbrius]] and [[nothos]]). Othryoneus, newly come to Troy at the report of the war, asked for “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam, / Cassandra, without bride-price”, promising in return to drive the Achaeans from Troy, “and the old man Priam promised him and nodded his assent / to give her” (13.363–369; see [[othryoneus]], [[cassandra]] and [[bride-price]]). The father bargains with his daughter for the city's rescue. θυγατρῶν εἶδος ἀρίστην, “most beautiful in form of the daughters”, was said of Laodice in Books 3 and 6 (3.124, 6.252); here the title goes to Cassandra, whom the Iliad names only twice, here and when she first sees her father bringing Hector's body home (24.697–706).

**Agamemnon's daughter.** Idomeneus kills Othryoneus and mocks the bargain over the body: “we would give you the most beautiful in form of the daughters of the son of Atreus, / bringing her from Argos, to marry her, if with us / you would sack the well-peopled citadel of Ilios … for we are not stingy with bride-gifts” (13.378–382; see [[idomeneus]]). The joke has a real offer behind it. In Book 9 Agamemnon offered Achilles any of his three daughters “without bride-price”, with a dowry such as no man ever gave with his daughter (9.144–148; see the note on Book 9 and [[agamemnon]]), and Achilles refused her, “not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty / and equaled gray-eyed Athena in handiwork” (9.388–390). Idomeneus offers Othryoneus the bride Achilles would not take, on Priam's terms reversed.

**Anchises' daughter.** Alcathous, the next man Idomeneus kills, “was the son-in-law of Anchises, / and had married the eldest of his daughters, Hippodameia, / whom her father and lady mother loved with all their hearts / in their hall; for she surpassed all the girls of her age / in beauty and handiwork and wits; and so / the best man in wide Troy had married her” (13.428–433; see [[hippodameia-daughter-of-anchises]], [[anchises]] and [[alcathous]]). The praise of a daughter takes the measures that Agamemnon gave Chryseis, “not in form or stature, not in mind or in handiwork” (1.115), and that Achilles named for Agamemnon's daughter (9.389–390). The parents' love is spoken of at the moment their son-in-law dies. The husband is “the best man in wide Troy”, and the best of the daughters has been given to him; the next lines tell how Poseidon bound him for Idomeneus' spear (13.434–444).
