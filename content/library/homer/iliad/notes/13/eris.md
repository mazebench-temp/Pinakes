summary: Poseidon blames the army's slackness on its strife with Agamemnon, in the verb of the proem's quarrel; Idomeneus says that no one can contend with Achilles in speed; and the two sons of Cronus stretch over both armies “the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all”.

en: [contend]
passages:
  - 13.358-360 | The rope of strife and war
---

**Strife with the leader.** Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, tells the Achaean leaders that the Trojans are at the ships through the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army, “who in their strife with him are not willing to defend / the swift-sailing ships” (οἳ κείνῳ ἐρίσαντες, 13.108–110; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and the translation's note on 13.108–114). The participle is the proem's. The Iliad's story began “from the time when first the two of them stood apart in strife” (διαστήτην ἐρίσαντε, 1.6), Agamemnon and Achilles; now the strife has passed from the two men to the army, which holds back from the war because of it. The god asks them to heal it (13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]).

**Rivalry.** Ajax would give way to no mortal man, Idomeneus tells Meriones, not even to Achilles, breaker of men, “in the standing fight at least; but in speed no one can contend with him” (ποσὶ δʼ οὔ πως ἔστιν ἐρίζειν, 13.324–325; see [[ajax]], [[achilles]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). ἐρίζω here is the rivalry of a contest, as when Calypso tells Odysseus that it is not fitting for mortal women to vie with goddesses in form and looks (*Odyssey* 5.211–213; see the article above).

**The rope of strife.** The narrator gives the battle its image when he has told the purposes of the two sons of Cronus: “And the two of them took the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all, / and knotting it crosswise they stretched it tight over both sides, / unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (ἔριδος κρατερῆς καὶ ὁμοιΐου πτολέμοιο / πεῖραρ, 13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). ἔρις and πόλεμος are named as one thing, and it is the gods who stretch it. Strife herself, the goddess, does not appear in the book; in Book 4 she “cast the quarrel, common to all, into their midst” (4.444), and in Book 11 Zeus sent her to the ships to begin this day of battle (11.3–14; see [[strife]]). See [[neikos]].
