---

After the plague of Book 1, sickness appears in the Iliad only in the story of Euchenor. His father Polyidus, the seer, had often told him “that he would perish of a grievous sickness in his own halls, / or be beaten down by the Trojans among the ships of the Achaeans; / so he avoided at once the grievous fine of the Achaeans / and the hateful sickness, so that he would not suffer sorrows in his heart” (13.667–670; see [[euchenor]], [[polyidus-the-seer]] and [[ker-doom]]). The two deaths are set side by side as the choice of a life: a slow death at home, and a quick one in battle. Euchenor chose the battle, and Paris' arrow kills him at once (13.671–672).

The sickness is “grievous” and “hateful”, ἀργαλέη and στυγερή. In the Odyssey the second word goes with the wasting illness that takes most men at home: Odysseus' mother, in the underworld, tells him that no sickness came on her, “which most often takes the life from the limbs with hateful wasting”, but longing for him (*Odyssey* 11.198–203), and on Eumaeus' island of Syrie no hateful sickness comes upon wretched mortals, but Apollo and Artemis kill the old with their gentle arrows (*Odyssey* 15.403–411). The θωή, “fine”, that Euchenor also avoided is a penalty the Achaeans laid on a man who would not go to the war; the word comes elsewhere in Homer only when a suitor threatens old Halitherses with a fine (*Odyssey* 2.192; see the translation's note on 13.663–672).
