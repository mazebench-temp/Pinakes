---
summary: As Ajax ends his challenge to Hector, an eagle flying high passes on the right, and the Achaean army shouts, emboldened by the omen (13.821–823).
passages:
  - 13.809-823 | Ajax's challenge and the eagle on the right
---

ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις / αἰετὸς ὑψιπέτης· ἐπὶ δʼ ἴαχε λαὸς Ἀχαιῶν / θάρσυνος οἰωνῷ: “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right, an eagle flying high; and the army of the Achaeans shouted out, emboldened by the omen” (13.821–823). The eagle comes on the right, the side of good omen, as Ajax finishes speaking: he has just told Hector that the time is near when he will pray, in flight, for horses swifter than hawks (13.817–820). It is the eagle of Book 12 reversed. That bird, “an eagle flying high” in the same words, came on the left of the Trojans and dropped its prey among them, and Hector refused to heed it (12.200–209, 12.237–243; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]] and [[high-flying]]). No seer reads this one; the army takes heart at once.

Hector does not answer the sign. He calls Ajax a blundering braggart and wishes he were the son of Zeus and Hera, as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives (13.824–832; see [[hector]]). The two armies shout, and the din reaches the upper air and the rays of Zeus (13.833–837).

The first verse of the omen returns twice in the Odyssey, for an eagle carrying a goose as Telemachus leaves Sparta and for a hawk tearing a dove as he lands in Ithaca (*Odyssey* 15.160–161, 15.525–526; see [[hawk]] and [[birds]]).
