summary: Rout appears in Book 13 only in the simile for Idomeneus and Meriones, as Ares' “dear son, strong and fearless”, who goes to war with his father from Thrace and “routs even a warrior of enduring heart”. The common noun runs through the book as the rout of an army.

**Ares' son.** Idomeneus and Meriones, leader and attendant, go into the battle like Ares, “and Rout, his dear son, strong and fearless, follows with him, / who routs even a warrior of enduring heart” (13.299–300; see [[ares-and-rout]], [[ares]] and [[meriones]]). The relative clause explains the name by its verb: Φόβος ἐφόβησε, “Rout routs”. Only here in Homer is Rout called Ares' son. Elsewhere he is one of the powers that rouse the armies with Terror and Strife (4.439–440), a figure on the aegis and on Agamemnon's shield (5.739, 11.37), and in Book 15 he yokes Ares' horses with Terror when Ares learns that his son Ascalaphus is dead (15.119–120; see [[terror]]). ἀταρβής, “fearless”, and ταλάφρων, “of enduring heart”, occur nowhere else in Homer. Hesiod makes Rout and Terror the children of Ares and Aphrodite, “who drive the close ranks of men in confusion” in war beside their father (*Theogony* 933–936).

**The common noun.** In Poseidon's first words to the two Ajaxes the army will be saved if they remember their courage, “and not chilling rout” (13.48; see [[poseidon]]). Idomeneus, half gray, leaps among the Trojans and “roused rout among them” (13.362), and when Aeneas comes against him, “flight did not seize Idomeneus as if he were a darling child” (13.470; see [[idomeneus]]). The translation keeps “rout” for an army and “flight” for one man.
