---
summary: Strabo uses Book 13 all through the Geography. Its opening peoples start a long argument about the Mysians, the mare-milkers and the Abii; he places its Thracian Samos, Aegae, Ephyri and Phlegyes, Locrian slingers and Ascanians on the map; and he quotes it to show that Homer knew Cassandra only as a maiden and seems to make Minos a native of Crete, for the story of Aeneas' grudge against Priam, for the station of Ajax's ships, and for the Ionians and the Phthians, these last fighting “in the battle at the ships”.
---

- **The far peoples.** Zeus' gaze at the Thracians, the Mysians “who fight hand to hand”, the mare-milkers and the Abii (13.4–6) gives Strabo a long argument with Posidonius, Apollodorus and Ephorus over who these peoples were and whether Homer knew them (*Geography* 7.3.2–10; also 1.1.6 and 12.3.26–27): [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]].
- **A figure of speech.** The book's first line, in which Zeus brings “the Trojans and Hector” to the ships, is one of Strabo's examples of a familiar figure, naming the whole and then a part of it: so the Odyssey names the Phoenicians and then the Sidonians (*Odyssey* 4.83–84), and the Catalogue the sons of Oeneus and then Meleager (2.641–642; *Geography* 1.2.33). The ancient commentary on 13.1 makes the same comparison with Meleager (scholia on 13.1).
- **Thracian Samos.** Homer calls Samothrace Samos, Strabo says, and keeps it apart from the Ionian Samos either by the epithet, “high up on the topmost peak of wooded Samos, / the Thracian” (13.12–13), or by naming it with its neighbors, “to Samos and Imbros” (24.753) and “between Samos and rocky Imbros” (24.78). So Strabo argues in *Geography* 10.2.17; Apollodorus had used the same verses to show how the poet keeps places of the same name apart (*Geography* 8.3.6). See [[samothrace]].
- **Aegae.** Strabo takes the Aegae of Poseidon's golden house (13.21) for the town in Euboea, from which the Aegean was probably named, not for the Achaean town that Hera names with Helice (8.203). In his quotation the verse runs on into the first words of 13.34, “there Poseidon halted his horses”, which in Homer belong to the cave between Tenedos and Imbros (*Geography* 8.7.4; 9.2.13). See [[aegae]].
- **Ephyri and Phlegyes.** On the simile of Ares and his son Rout arming in Thrace against the Ephyri or the Phlegyes (13.301–302), Strabo reports that the people of Gyrton were once called Phlegyae, after Phlegyas, the brother of Ixion, and those of Crannon Ephyri, so that it is hard to say whom the poet means; he quotes the verse with the plural θωρήσσοντο for Homer's dual (*Geography* 9.5.21; 7, fr. 14 and fr. 16). See [[ares-and-rout]].
- **Minos.** Some said that Minos was a stranger in Crete, others a native, and “the poet seems rather to favor the second view”, Strabo writes, when he says that “Zeus first fathered Minos, a guardian over Crete” (13.450; *Geography* 10.4.9). Ephorus, Strabo reports, made Minos the imitator of an older Rhadamanthys: every nine years he went up to the cave of Zeus and came back with laws that he called the god's commands, and so the poet calls him the king who reigned “nine years”, the familiar of great Zeus (*Odyssey* 19.178–179; *Geography* 10.4.8). See [[minos]].
- **Cassandra.** Against the Ilians' claim that their city was never wholly destroyed, Strabo quotes the verses on Othryoneus (13.363–366) to show that Homer knew Cassandra as a maiden and nothing of her violation (*Geography* 13.1.40): [[strabo-on-cassandra]].
- **Aeneas' grudge.** Strabo reports the story that Aeneas survived the war because of his enmity to Priam, “for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, / because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.460–461), as the sons of Antenor and Antenor himself were spared for Menelaus' stay in his house; Sophocles, in his *Capture of Ilion*, had a leopard skin hung before Antenor's door as a sign that it was to be left unsacked. Then he follows the stories of Aeneas' wanderings, to Macedonian Olympus, to Arcadia, to Sicily and to Latium (*Geography* 13.1.53). See [[aeneas]] and [[antenor]].
- **Ajax's ships.** The critics who denied that Ajax brought his ships to stand beside the Athenians (2.558) asked why, then, Ajax's ships are drawn up at the end of the line, not with the Athenians but with Protesilaus' Thessalians: “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were” (13.681; *Geography* 9.1.10; see [[solon-and-salamis]]). They took this Ajax for Telamonian Ajax. A note on the verse in the Venetus A says that the poet means the Locrian, whose ships were drawn up near Protesilaus' (scholium A on 13.681; see [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
- **The Ionians and the Phthians.** Attica was once called Ionia and Ias, Strabo says, and when the poet says “there the Boeotians and the Ionians” he means the Athenians (13.685; *Geography* 9.1.5). Some, he reports, explained the name of the Curetes from girlish dress, and pointed among other things to the “Ionians with trailing tunics” (*Geography* 10.3.8). He explains the Phthians of 13.686–700 as the men of Philoctetes and Protesilaus, who fight “in the battle at the ships” while Achilles' own Phthians stay idle (*Geography* 9.5.7). See [[ionians]], [[phthians]] and [[title-of-book-13]].
- **The Locrians.** The Euboeans, Strabo says, were good at the standing fight hand to hand, with spears held out (2.543–544); “of the Locrians he says the opposite”, that close battle was not their work, and that they followed to Ilios trusting in bows and in “well-twisted sheep's wool”. He quotes the verses freely, but with οἰὸς ἀώτῳ, the reading now printed at 13.716 (*Geography* 10.1.13, from 13.713–717). See [[locrians]] and [[sling]].
- **Ascania.** Strabo distinguishes a far Phrygian Ascania (2.862–863) from a nearer Mysian one by Nicaea, and so two leaders called Ascanius. He quotes 13.792–793 with an extra verse, Μυσῶν ἀγχεμάχων ἡγήτορα, “leader of the Mysians who fight hand to hand” (*Geography* 12.4.5). See [[ascania]] and [[ascanius-the-mysian]].
