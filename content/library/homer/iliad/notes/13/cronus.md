summary: Cronus is named in Book 13 in his son's title, Κρονίων, four times, and once in his own name, when the narrator sets “the two mighty sons of Cronus”, Zeus and Poseidon, at odds over the battle.
refs: [13.354]
passages:
  - 13.345-360 | The two sons of Cronus
---

**The son of Cronus.** Four times Zeus is Κρονίων, “the son of Cronus” (see [[son-of-cronus]] and [[zeus]]). Idomeneus thinks that it must please “the son of Cronus, supreme in might” that the Achaeans perish nameless far from Argos (13.226–227; see [[supreme-in-might]]); he goes out like the lightning that the son of Cronus brandishes from Olympus (13.242–244; see [[like-lightning]]); the ships will burn only if “the son of Cronus himself / casts a blazing firebrand” on them (13.319–320); and Paris says that the son of Cronus kept off death from Deiphobus and Helenus (13.783).

**Two sons.** Once the father is named. The narrator turns from the battle to the gods behind it: “And the two mighty sons of Cronus, with their minds set on different sides, / were making grievous sorrows for the heroes” (13.345–346). Only here does Homer give Cronus two sons in the dual. The brothers are “of one race and of one father's line”, ἴα πάτρη, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.354–355; see [[poseidon]]). In Book 15 Poseidon names the third: “for we are three brothers from Cronus, whom Rhea bore, / Zeus and I, and the third is Hades, who rules the dead”, and the world was divided among them by lot (15.187–193; see [[fall-of-cronus]], [[hades]] and [[lots]]).

In Hesiod Rhea bears to Cronus Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon and last Zeus, “the youngest of her children”, whom Cronus does not swallow (*Theogony* 453–458, 478). The Iliad's Zeus is the first-born (13.355; 15.166, 15.182, 15.204). See [[the-rope-of-war]].
