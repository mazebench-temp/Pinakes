**Urged on.** Among the weary men in the rear whom Poseidon rouses are “Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war cry” (13.93; see [[masters-of-the-war-cry]] and [[antilochus]]). Idomeneus calls on the pair in the same verse when Aeneas comes at him (13.479).

**The broken spear.** Deiphobus advances under cover of his round shield. Meriones' spear strikes it and snaps at the socket, though Deiphobus holds the shield away from him in fear of “the spear of wise-hearted Meriones” (13.156–164; see [[deiphobus]], [[wise-hearted]] and [[spear]]). Meriones draws back, “terribly enraged on both counts, for the victory and for the spear that he had shattered”, and goes to his hut for another (13.165–168).

**At the hut.** On the way he meets Idomeneus, whose θεράπων ἐΰς, “good attendant”, he is (13.246). Earlier books called him Idomeneus' ὀπάων, “squire” (7.165, 8.263, 10.58); θεράπων ἐΰς returns as his title at the funeral games (23.528, 23.860, 23.888). Idomeneus greets him as “Meriones, son of Molus, swift of foot, dearest of my companions” (13.249; see [[molus]] and [[hetairos]]). Only here is Meriones “swift of foot”, Achilles' epithet, and the Iliad has φίλταθʼ ἑταίρων, “dearest of companions”, only once more, when Achilles mourns Patroclus (19.315). “Wise” in both his answers (13.254, 13.266; see [[wise]]), Meriones says that he has shattered his spear on the shield of “overweening Deiphobus” (13.255–258; see [[overweening]]). Offered one of the Trojan spears in Idomeneus' hut, he claims spoils of his own, and courage:

Idomeneus answers that he knows his prowess, and that it would show in an ambush, where the coward's color changes and the brave man's does not (13.275–294; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[idomeneus]]).

**Ares and Rout.** Meriones, “the equal of swift Ares”, takes a bronze spear and goes “after Idomeneus” (13.295–297), and the two go to war like Ares and his son Rout, who follows his father (13.298–305; see [[ares-and-rout]] and [[rout]]). θοῷ ἀτάλαντος Ἄρηϊ is said of him three times in this book (13.295, 13.328, 13.528), and elsewhere of Hector, Patroclus and Automedon (8.215, 16.784, 17.536; see [[equal-of-ares]]). It is Meriones who asks where to go into the throng, “On the right of the whole army, or through the middle, or on the left?” (13.307–310), and who leads the way to the left (13.328–329).

**Deiphobus wounded.** When Deiphobus has killed Ascalaphus and snatched his helmet, Meriones leaps on him and strikes his arm with the spear, so that the helmet falls with a clang; “leaping in again like a vulture”, he pulls the spear from the top of the arm and draws back among his companions (13.526–533; see [[ascalaphus]] and [[like-vultures]]). Polites leads his brother out of the battle (13.533–539; see [[polites]]). The man whose shield broke Meriones' spear leaves the field with a wound from his spear.

**Adamas.** Adamas, son of Asius, whose spear Poseidon has made powerless against Antilochus' shield, is drawing back when Meriones follows and strikes him “between the genitals and the navel, where most of all Ares is painful for miserable mortals” (13.567–569). He writhes around the spear like an ox that herdsmen have bound with ropes, “for a little while, not long at all”, until Meriones pulls it out (13.570–575; see [[adamas]] and [[the-roped-ox]]). Idomeneus killed his father (13.384–393; see [[asius]]).

**Harpalion.** Harpalion, son of the Paphlagonian king, stabs Menelaus' shield and draws back, “glancing all around”; Meriones shoots him in the right buttock, and the arrow passes under the bone into the bladder (13.643–652; see [[harpalion]] and [[menelaus]]), nearly the words of his kill of Phereclus in Book 5 (5.66–67). Meriones is an archer: he lent Odysseus his bow (10.260), and he wins the archery at the funeral games (23.859–883; see [[bow]]). Paris answers with “a bronze-fitted arrow” of his own and kills Euchenor (13.650, 13.662; see [[euchenor]]).
