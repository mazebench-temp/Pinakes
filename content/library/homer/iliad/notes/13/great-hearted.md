---
summary: The epithet goes to Achaeans and Trojans alike, to the Paphlagonians and the Phthians, and in a simile to the Phlegyes; the book adds the accusative μεγαλήτορας.
grc: [μεγαλήτορας]
---

Book 13 uses the two words of the entry nine times, on both sides. Of Achaeans: Hector rushes to snatch the helmet “from the head of great-hearted Amphimachus” (13.188–189; [[amphimachus]]); Antilochus is “son of great-hearted Nestor” as he drives off Asius' horses (13.400; [[antilochus]], [[nestor]]), in the half-verse that named him in Book 5 (5.565); Medon and Podarces fight “armed in front of the great-hearted Phthians” (13.699; [[phthians]]); and “the Locrians did not follow the great-hearted son of Oileus” into the close fighting (13.712; [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[locrians]]). Of Trojans and their allies: Deiphobus wonders whether to take “one of the great-hearted Trojans” as his companion (13.455–456; [[deiphobus]]); “great-hearted Agenor” draws the spear from Helenus' hand (13.598; [[agenor]]); “the great-hearted Paphlagonians” tend the dying Harpalion (13.656; [[paphlagonians]]); and Polydamas tells Hector that “the great-hearted Trojans,” having crossed the wall, are scattered among the ships (13.737–739; [[polydamas]]).

The ninth use is in a simile. Ares and his son Rout arm in Thrace to go out “to the Ephyri / or to the great-hearted Phlegyes” (13.301–302; [[ares-and-rout]]), peoples named only here in Homer. The accusative μεγαλήτορας is new to the entry.
