---
summary: A god casts the joy of battle into the hearts of the two Ajaxes; the deer of Poseidon's simile have none in them; and the Trojans, shot at from behind by the Locrians, remember nothing of it.
---

The book uses the word three times, and the three uses make a course: the joy of battle given, lacking, and forgotten.

**Given.** The two Ajaxes, struck by Poseidon's staff, speak of their eagerness, “rejoicing in the joy of battle that the god had cast into their hearts” (χάρμῃ γηθόσυνοι, τήν σφιν θεὸς ἔμβαλε θυμῷ, 13.81–82; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The narrator names the giver. Elsewhere the joy of battle is something an army remembers of itself (see the article above); here it is cast in from outside, and the two men feel it as their own.

**Lacking.** The deer to which Poseidon likens the Trojans of old have “no joy of battle in them” (οὐδʼ ἔπι χάρμη, 13.104; see [[timid-deer]] and the translation's note on 13.104). The god's point is that the Trojans have changed: the deer now fight at the ships (13.105–110; see [[alke]]).

**Forgotten.** Where Hector first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, the armored Achaeans fight in front and the Locrians shoot from behind, unseen, “and of the joy of battle / the Trojans remembered nothing; for the arrows threw them into confusion” (οὐδέ τι χάρμης / Τρῶες μιμνήσκοντο, 13.721–722; see [[locrians]], [[bow]] and [[sling]]). Rallying, the Achaeans “remembered the joy of battle” (4.222, 8.252); the negative is the phrase of a rout. This time the rout is not caused by a god or a champion but by missiles from men no one can see, and it would have sent the Trojans back to Ilios, had not Polydamas spoken (13.723–725; see [[polydamas]]).
