summary: When the Trojans are about to fall back from the ships, Polydamas comes to Hector a third time: a god gives one man the works of war and another a good mind, and Hector should call the best men together, for the Achaeans may repay yesterday's debt while “a man insatiable of war” waits by the ships. Hector takes the counsel.
# The phrase: Πανθοΐδης alone is also his brother Euphorbus (16.808, 17.81).

**The third counsel.** “Then wretchedly from the ships and the huts / the Trojans would have retreated to windy Ilios, / had not Polydamas come and stood beside bold Hector and said” (13.723–725; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[bold]]). The last verse brought him to Hector's side twice at the ditch (12.60, 12.210); there it began δὴ τότε, “then”, and here εἰ μή, “had not”. He begins where his second counsel ended, with Hector's refusal to listen (12.211–214, 12.230–250):

{{quote:388cb19a-b94b-528b-8fed-67bc1fa7d0a1}}

παραρρητός, “open to persuasion”, is found elsewhere only in Phoenix's story of the heroes of old, who “could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (9.526). The gnomic verses on the gifts of the gods are a saying in their own right (see [[different-gifts]]). They leave unsaid what Book 18 says plainly, that Polydamas and Hector were born on one night, “but the one excelled far in words, the other with the spear” (18.252; see [[polydamas]]).

**The plan.** “All around you the crown of war is blazing” (13.736). The Trojans who came over the wall stand apart with their arms or fight “fewer against more, scattered among the ships” (13.737–739). Let Hector draw back and call the best men, and decide whether to fall on the ships, “if a god is willing to give us the power”, or come away unharmed (13.740–744; see [[kratos]]). He fears that the Achaeans “may pay back yesterday's / debt, since beside the ships there waits a man insatiable of war” (13.745–747). The debt is the Achaean defeat of the day before, the day of Book 8, and the Achaeans may repay it in kind (see the translation's note on 13.745–746). He does not name Achilles. ἆτος πολέμοιο, “insatiable of war”, is Ares' epithet (5.388, 5.863, 6.203), given here to a man (see [[insatiable-of-war]] and [[achilles]]).

**Taken.** “So spoke Polydamas, and his safe counsel pleased Hector” (13.748): the two verses with which Hector took the first counsel at the ditch return whole (13.748–749 = 12.80–81). Hector tells him to keep the best men there while he gives his orders, and the Trojans hurry “to the son of Panthous, manly Polydamas” (13.751–757; see [[panthous]] and [[manly]]). The council does not take place. Hector finds his brothers dead or wounded, rebukes Paris, and goes back into the fight, where it is thickest “around Cebriones and noble Polydamas” (13.758–790; see [[hector]], [[cebriones]] and [[blameless]]).

**Borne out.** The fear comes true before the day is over. Achilles shows himself at the ditch, and at his shout the Trojans and their allies are thrown into confusion and twelve of their best men die (18.203–231). That night Polydamas counsels a retreat to the city, and Hector refuses him (18.249–313).
