---
summary: The “Mysians, who fight hand to hand” are among the far peoples on whom Zeus turns his eyes away from Troy (13.5); Strabo, after Posidonius, took them for the Mysians of Europe, by the Danube, not the Mysian allies of the Trojans.
---

Μυσῶν τʼ ἀγχεμάχων, “and of the Mysians, who fight hand to hand” (13.5). ἀγχέμαχος, “fighting at close quarters”, is said elsewhere of Achilles' companions and attendants (16.248, 16.272 = 17.165); the Dardanians and others are ἀγχιμαχηταί (13.150). Zeus looks at them “far off” (νόσφιν, 13.4), in a list that goes on to the mare-milkers and the Abii (see [[thracians]] and [[abii]]).

The Mysians who fight for Troy are allies from Asia: Chromis and the augur Ennomus led them (2.858), Dolon places them toward Thymbre (10.430; see [[trojan-allies]]), and in the next book Ajax kills their leader Hyrtius (14.511–512). Strabo, agreeing with Posidonius, argued that the poet must mean other Mysians, the Mysians of Europe, beside the Thracians: Zeus turns his eyes “away” (πάλιν) from Troy, and the Mysians of Asia, who border the Troad, would not be “far off”; to name them with the Thracians across the Hellespont would be to confuse the continents (*Geography* 7.3.2). Posidonius proposed reading Μοισῶν, “Moesians”, in the thirteenth book; Strabo thought it needless to change a reading so long accepted, since the people had more likely been called Mysians from the first, and noted that in his own day Aelius Catus had moved fifty thousand Getae from beyond the Danube into Thrace, where they were called Moesians (7.3.3–4, 7.3.10; see [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]).
