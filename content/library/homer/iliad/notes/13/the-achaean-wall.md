**Over the wall.** Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, tells the two Ajaxes that he does not fear the Trojans elsewhere, “who have climbed over the great wall in a throng” (13.50), and the narrator says it again of the weary men at the rear, who weep as they watch the Trojans inside (13.86–88). ὑπερκατέβησαν, “climbed over”, is found only in these two verses. Poseidon ends his rebuke with the breach: Hector “has broken the gates and the long bar” (13.124), in the words in which the narrator foretold it (12.291; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). The Achaeans now fight among their ships with no wall before them, and the close ranks around the two Ajaxes take its place: Hector, checked against them, says that the Achaeans “have arrayed themselves in close order like a tower” (13.152), the words used at the wall of the hunters who stand against a boar or a lion, in the simile for Hector himself (12.41–48; see [[battle-order]]).

**Where it was lowest.** When the losses on the left go unnoticed, the narrator brings the reader back to the place of the breach:

{{quote:11e5dda5-d487-55f4-87fd-de60aeaf26fc}}

χθαμαλώτατον, “lowest”, occurs only here in the Iliad, and the poem does not say why the wall was built lower there. Which Ajax's ships stood there is not said either. Telamonian Ajax had drawn up his ships at one end of the line (11.7–9), but the Locrians of the son of Oileus fight in this place (13.712–722; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The ship of Protesilaus is the one Hector will seize when the Trojans reach the ships again (15.704–706; see [[protesilaus]]).

**Inside.** Polydamas tells Hector that “the great-hearted Trojans, now that they have come down over the wall”, are scattered among the ships, some standing apart, others fighting, fewer against more (13.737–739; see [[polydamas]]). The wall that kept the Trojans out now lies between them and their city. In the next book Nestor sees that it has been thrown down (14.13–15), and in Book 15 Apollo throws it down again before the Trojans, as a child scatters sand by the sea (15.355–366; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]).
