**Glory for Achilles.** The narrator sets out the two brothers' purposes. Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector, “to give glory to Achilles, swift of foot”, but did not wish the Achaean army to perish before Ilios, “but was giving glory to Thetis and her stout-hearted son” (κυδαίνων, κύδαινε, 13.347–350; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[zeus]]). The verb is the gift of κῦδος (see the article above), and its object here is not the side that wins. Hector's victories are the means by which Zeus does honor to the man who is not fighting, as Thetis asked in Book 1 (1.505–510; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Zeus will do the same for Hector himself in Book 15, honoring and glorifying him “for he was to be short-lived” (τίμα καὶ κύδαινε, 15.610–614).

**Given to one side.** Idomeneus and Meriones go into battle like Ares and his son Rout, who arm in Thrace to go against the Ephyri or the Phlegyes, “and they do not / listen to both sides, but give glory to one or the other” (ἑτέροισι δὲ κῦδος ἔδωκαν, 13.298–303; see [[ares-and-rout]] and [[rout]]). The simile puts in the gods' hands the decision that the poem elsewhere gives to Zeus, as when the narrator asks whom Hector killed first and whom last, “when Zeus gave him glory” (11.299–300; compare 8.216; see the note on Book 12).

**Where men win glory.** Meriones tells Idomeneus where he stands: “among the foremost, in the battle where men win glory” (μάχην ἀνὰ κυδιάνειραν, 13.270–271; see [[where-men-win-glory]] and [[meriones]]).

**The glory that was not.** Hector does not know that on the left of the ships his men are being cut down, “And soon the glory would have been the Achaeans'”, so hard did Poseidon urge them on; but Hector held on where he had first broken in at the gates (13.674–680; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Glory is the outcome of a day of battle, and the narrator weighs it before it is given.

**Glorious Menelaus.** κυδάλιμος, “glorious”, is the epithet of Menelaus three times in his fight with Helenus and Peisander (13.591, 13.601, 13.606; see [[kydalimos]] and [[menelaus]]).
