---
summary: Book 13 is a book about holding back and giving ground. Poseidon rebukes the slackness of those who hold back from war and curses any man who does so; Hector, stopped by the close ranks, gives ground reeling; three Trojans draw back “avoiding doom”, and two are killed as they go; Idomeneus, too slow to run, gives ground step by step; and Paris, rebuked as a coward, says that he may hold back some other time, not now.

**Holding back.** The word of the book is μεθίημι, “let go, hold back from”, and its noun μεθημοσύνη, “slackness”. Poseidon tells the Achaean leaders that the day of defeat is in sight “if you hold back from wretched war”, that “for us at least there is no way to hold back from war”, that they no longer do well to let go of their courage, and that a wretch who held back he would not quarrel with, but with them he is indignant (13.97–119; see [[aidos]] and [[nemesis]]); the Trojans are at the ships through “the slackness of the army” (13.108, 13.121). As Thoas he curses the man who holds back: “may that man never return home / from Troy, but stay here and become a plaything for dogs, / whoever on this day of his own will holds back from fighting” (13.232–234; see [[dogs]]). Idomeneus answers that no one now shrinks from the war (13.224–225). The curse on the man who leaves the fighting is the counterpart of Odysseus' rule in Book 11, that it is cowards who leave it (11.408–410; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]).

**Giving ground.** Hector, coming on like a boulder from a crest, is stopped at the close-packed battalions, “and he gave ground, reeling” (χασσάμενος πελεμίχθη, 13.146–148; see [[the-rolling-boulder]] and [[battle-order]]), and again, struck on the shield by Ajax, “he gave ground backward / from both the dead” (13.193–194). Three Trojans in the book draw back into the crowd of their companions “avoiding doom”, Adamas, Helenus and Harpalion, and Meriones kills two of them as they go (13.566–575, 13.596, 13.648–655; see [[ker-doom]]). The same verse without the doom tells how Meriones himself withdrew, to fetch a spear and after wounding Deiphobus (13.165, 13.533). And the Trojans as a whole “would have retreated to windy Ilios”, had not Polydamas spoken (13.723–725; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Too slow to run.** Idomeneus does not flee. When Aeneas comes on, “flight did not seize Idomeneus as if he were a darling child”, and he stands his ground like a boar (13.470–475; see [[the-bristling-boar]]). But after killing Oenomaus his legs are no longer steady, “so in close fighting he kept off the pitiless day, / but his feet no longer carried him nimbly to flee out of the war”, and he goes away step by step, βάδην (13.512–516; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). It is the retreat Diomedes ordered before Ares, keeping the face to the enemy (5.605–606), and it is forced on him by age.

**The coward's name.** In the ambush that Idomeneus describes, the coward's fear shows in his body, and the brave man's wounds are in front, never on the back of his neck (13.276–291; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). Hector calls Paris by the names of Book 3, “Evil Paris, best in looks, mad for women, seducer” (13.769 = 3.39), and Paris answers that he may hold back from war at some other time, “since my mother did not bear me wholly without courage either” (13.776–777; see [[alke]] and [[beyond-ones-strength]]).
