**Roused.** He is the last of the leaders Poseidon urges on in the rear, with Meriones, “masters of the war cry” (13.93; see [[masters-of-the-war-cry]] and [[meriones]]), and Idomeneus calls on the same pair in the same verse against Aeneas (13.479).

**The charioteer.** When Idomeneus has killed Asius in front of his horses, the charioteer is struck out of his wits and does not dare to turn the horses; Antilochus, μενεχάρμης, “steadfast in battle” (13.396), spears him through the middle, in the verses of Idomeneus' kill of Othryoneus: “the bronze corselet that he wore did not protect him, and he fixed it in the middle of his belly” (13.397–398 = 13.371–372). “And gasping he fell from the well-made chariot” (13.399), the verse with which Pylaemenes' charioteer fell in Book 5 (5.585), and as then, Antilochus drives off the horses to the Achaeans (13.400–401; 5.589; see [[asius]], [[mydon]] and [[chariot]]).

**Hypsenor.** Deiphobus' boast over Hypsenor stirs the heart “of wise-hearted Antilochus” most of all, “but even in his grief he did not neglect his companion, but ran and stood over him, and covered him with his shield” (13.417–420; see [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]] and [[wise-hearted]]). The verses are those in which Ajax covered his fallen brother Teucer, with “companion” in place of “brother” (13.419–423 ≈ 8.330–334), and Mecisteus and Alastor carry the man to the ships as they carried Teucer (see [[mecisteus-son-of-echius]] and [[alastor-who-carries-teucer]]). The poem says only that Hypsenor was his ἑταῖρος (13.419; see [[hetairos]]).

**Thoon and Adamas.** Antilochus watches for Thoon as he turns and cuts through the vein that runs up the back to the neck; Thoon falls on his back, “stretching out both his hands to his dear companions”, and Antilochus begins to strip him, “looking about him” (13.545–551; see [[thoon-companion-of-asius]]). The Trojans stab at his broad shield from every side, but cannot reach his flesh:

{{quote:1eb8123d-a47c-5622-a123-620c1ce17290}}

Adamas, son of Asius, stabs the middle of his shield, but “dark-haired Poseidon, who begrudged him the other's life”, makes the point powerless; half of the spear stays in the shield “like a fire-hardened stake”, half lies on the ground, and Adamas draws back to die by Meriones' spear (13.560–575; see [[adamas]], [[poseidon]] and [[like-a-fire-hardened-stake]]).

**Poseidon's favor.** The poem does not say why the god guards him. Nestor will tell his son that “Zeus and Poseidon loved you, young as you are, and taught you every kind of horsemanship” (23.306–308), and in the Odyssey Nestor's father Neleus is Poseidon's son (Od. 11.235–254; see [[nestor]] and [[neleus]]). Poseidon has just been angered by the death of his grandson Amphimachus (13.206–207; see [[amphimachus]]).
