Μηριόνης δʼ ἐξ αὖτις ἐπάλμενος αἰγυπιὸς ὣς / ἐξέρυσε πρυμνοῖο βραχίονος ὄβριμον ἔγχος: “And Meriones, leaping in again like a vulture, pulled the heavy spear out from the top of his arm” (13.531–532). The comparison is half a verse, as at Book 7, where it was the gods who sat “like birds, like vultures” (7.59). There the bird watched from a height; here it swoops. ἐπάλμενος, “leaping on”, is said of Meriones twice: he leapt on Deiphobus to strike his arm as Deiphobus snatched the shining helmet from the dead Ascalaphus (13.527–530), and leaps in again to take back his spear (13.531). Then he draws back into the crowd of his companions (13.533), as a bird of prey strikes and is gone. Polites leads his wounded brother to the chariot, and Deiphobus is carried toward the city (13.533–539; see [[deiphobus]] and [[meriones]]).

The αἰγυπιός of the later similes is a fighter that swoops: Sarpedon and Patroclus rush at each other like two vultures with crooked talons fighting on a high rock (16.428–430), Automedon drives among the Trojans like a vulture among geese (17.460), and in the Odyssey Odysseus and his men fall on the suitors like vultures that swoop from the mountains on smaller birds (*Odyssey* 22.302–306).
