summary: Twice, in the same two verses, a bronze corselet fails to protect its wearer, Othryoneus and Asius' charioteer, and the spear is fixed in his belly. Idomeneus breaks the plate of Oenomaus' corselet and the bronze “tunic” of Alcathous, but the plate of Menelaus' corselet turns Helenus' arrow like beans from a winnowing shovel (13.371–372, 13.397–398, 13.439–441, 13.507, 13.586–592).
grc: [θωρήκων, θώρηκες, θωρήσσεσθον]
refs: [13.439-441]
---

**It did not protect him.** Idomeneus' spear strikes Othryoneus as he comes striding high, “and the bronze corselet / that he wore did not protect him, and he fixed it in the middle of his belly” (13.371–372; see [[othryoneus]]). The same two verses tell, a few lines later, how Antilochus kills Asius' charioteer as he sits stunned in the chariot (13.397–398; see [[antilochus]] and [[chariot]]). οὐδʼ ἤρκεσε θώρηξ is the narrator's verdict on the armor: the corselet was worn to keep off just such a blow.

**The plate.** γύαλον is a curved plate of the corselet. Idomeneus strikes Oenomaus in the middle of the belly “and broke the plate of his corselet, and the bronze let out / his bowels” (13.506–508; see [[oenomaus-the-trojan]]); the verses return when Ajax kills Phorcys (17.314–315). Helenus' arrow strikes Menelaus in the chest “on the plate of his corselet, and the bitter arrow flew off” (13.586–587; see [[helenus]] and [[menelaus]]). It is the verse with which Pandarus' arrow struck Diomedes in Book 5, but there “the bitter arrow flew through it” (5.99). Here it glances far away from the corselet, like dark beans or chickpeas leaping from a broad winnowing shovel on the threshing floor (13.588–592; see [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]]).

**A tunic of bronze.** When Idomeneus stabs Alcathous, whom Poseidon has fettered where he stands, he “broke the tunic around him, / of bronze, which before had kept destruction from his body; / and then it rang dry as it was torn about the spear” (13.439–441; see [[alcathous]]). The χιτὼν χάλκεος is the corselet under another name, as the Achaeans and the Cretans are χαλκοχίτωνες, “bronze-armored” (13.255, 13.272), a word made of χαλκός, “bronze”, and χιτών, “tunic” (see [[bronze-armored]] and [[tunic]]). The dry sound of the torn bronze (αὖον ἄϋσεν) is the sound of Idomeneus' shield grazed by a spear (13.409) and of the helmets struck by stones at the wall (12.160; see the translation's note on 13.409, 13.441).

**Gleaming corselets.** In Idomeneus' hut the spoils include “corselets shining brightly” (13.265; see [[hut]]), and in the clash on the left the eyes are blinded by “the newly burnished corselets” (13.342; see [[bronze]]). The verb of the corselet, θωρήσσω, is the verb of arming: Ares and his son Rout “arm themselves” to go out from Thrace (θωρήσσεσθον, a dual, 13.301; see [[ares-and-rout]]), and the Phthians fight “armed” in front of their people (θωρηχθέντες, 13.699). The Locrians, whose leader wears a linen corselet in the Catalogue (λινοθώρηξ, 2.529), have no armor for the close fight at all (13.712–716; see [[sling]]).
