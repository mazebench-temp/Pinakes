---
summary: Poseidon comes among the Achaeans twice in disguise. As Calchas, in form and voice, he rouses the two Ajaxes and the men in the rear, and the son of Oileus knows him from behind as he goes, “and gods are easy to know”; as Thoas, in voice alone, he rouses Idomeneus, who does not know him. The narrator explains why: Poseidon helps the Achaeans secretly, “in the likeness of a man”, because Zeus was born first (13.43–82, 13.206–239, 13.351–357).

**As Calchas.** Poseidon comes up out of the sea “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (13.45; see [[calchas]] and [[divination]]). He tells the two Ajaxes that they will save the army “if you remember your courage” (13.47–48), strikes them with his staff, fills them with might and makes their limbs light, and darts away like a hawk rising from a sheer rock (13.59–65; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]).

**Recognition.** The son of Oileus is the first to know him:

{{quote:838178da-bd0d-5175-996d-65f28e89b9cd}}

He knows the god as he goes away, from behind (μετόπισθε), by ἴχνια, taken as “the movements” of feet and legs; read as “tracks”, he knew him by the footprints he left (see the translation's note on 13.71). And he knows him in his own body, in the eagerness of his heart and the quivering of his feet and hands (13.73–75; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Telamonian Ajax does not take up the claim; he answers only that his hands too quiver around his spear (13.76–80). There is no astonishment (θάμβος), no prayer, no reply to the god, who is already gone. ἀρίγνωτοι δὲ θεοί περ, “and gods are easy to know”, is not always borne out: Helen knew Aphrodite by her neck, her breasts and her flashing eyes (3.396–398), but Pandarus never learned who had spoken to him in Laodocus' shape (4.86–104), and in the Odyssey “the gods do not appear plainly to all” (Od. 16.161). See [[seeing-the-gods]].

**The men in the rear.** Poseidon then “came easily among them” and roused the leaders, Teucer, Leitus, Peneleos, Thoas, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus, with a speech of shame (13.90–124; see [[exhortation]]). μετεισάμενος is from εἶμι, “go”, not from εἴδομαι, “be like”, and the poem does not say whether he still wears the shape of Calchas.

**As Thoas.** For Idomeneus he changes only his voice, “making his voice like that of Thoas, son of Andraemon, / who in all Pleuron and steep Calydon / was lord of the Aetolians, and was honored by the people like a god” (13.216–218; see [[thoas]]). The last half-verse is a formula for a man honored at home (5.78, 10.33, 11.58). Idomeneus answers him by name, “Thoas” (13.222, 13.228), and never learns otherwise: “So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men” (13.239; see [[idomeneus]]).

**In the likeness of a man.** The narrator gives the reason for the disguises. The two sons of Cronus were of one race and one father's line, “but Zeus was born first and knew more. / Therefore he shrank from giving help openly, / but secretly he kept rousing the army, in the likeness of a man” (13.355–357; see [[poseidon]], [[zeus]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). Here the epiphany is a way of hiding: the god must look like a man so that Zeus will not see him. In Book 14 he comes once more in disguise, “like an old man”, to Agamemnon (14.135–146); only when Sleep has told him that Zeus is sleeping does he lead the Achaeans openly, with a terrible sword in his hand “like the lightning” (14.354–387).
