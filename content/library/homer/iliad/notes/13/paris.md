summary: Paris stands with Aeneas in the fight over Alcathous, and in anger for his guest-friend Harpalion he shoots the Corinthian Euchenor. When Hector finds him on the left and rebukes him in the words of Book 3, he answers that he has fought without rest and is not to blame, and goes with his brother into the thickest of the fight.

**With Aeneas.** In the fight over Alcathous, Aeneas calls to his side “Deiphobus and Paris and brilliant Agenor, / who were leaders of the Trojans with him” (13.490–491; see [[aeneas]] and [[alcathous]]). Paris had led the second company against the wall with Alcathous and Agenor (12.93).

**For Harpalion.** Menelaus, standing over Peisander, has just reproached the Trojans for the wife they carried off “wantonly, after you had been welcomed by her”, without fear of “Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests” (13.624–627; see [[menelaus]], [[helen]], [[who-thunders-loud]] and [[xenia]]). Paris' next deed is done for a guest-friend. When Meriones kills Harpalion, son of Pylaemenes, “Paris was very angry at heart over his killing; / for he had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians” (13.660–661; see [[harpalion]] and [[paphlagonians]]). The poem does not say how the bond began. He shoots Euchenor, the rich son of the seer Polyidus, who had chosen death at Troy over sickness at home, under the jaw and the ear (13.662–672; see [[euchenor]] and [[polyidus-the-seer]]). Euchenor is the first man he has killed since Menesthius (7.8–10); in Book 11 his arrows only wounded (see [[bow]]).

**Hector's rebuke.** Hector finds him “on the left of the tearful battle, / brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair, / encouraging his companions and urging them on to fight” (13.765–767; see [[husband-of-helen]]), and greets him with the verse of Book 3, “Evil Paris, best in looks, mad for women, seducer” (13.769 = 3.39; see [[evil-paris]] and [[best-in-looks]]). Then he asks for Deiphobus, Helenus, Adamas, Asius and Othryoneus: “now sheer destruction is sure for you” (13.770–773; see [[hector]]). Paris answers:

In Books 3 and 6 he accepted the reproach as fair, “not beyond what is fair” (3.59, 6.333; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Here he rejects it. Hector is set on “blaming one who is not to blame” (ἀναίτιον αἰτιάασθαι), as Patroclus says Achilles “would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.654; see [[aitios]]). His mother did not bear him ἀνάλκιδα, “without courage” (13.777; see [[alke]]); since Hector roused the battle at the ships he has fought without rest, and the men Hector asks about are dead, except Deiphobus and Helenus, who have gone away wounded (13.778–783). His last verse is a maxim: “But beyond his strength no man can fight, however eager he is” (13.787; see [[beyond-ones-strength]]).

“So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind” (13.788): the verse in which Agamemnon persuaded Menelaus not to face Hector (7.120). The two go together where the battle is thickest, around Cebriones and Polydamas (13.789–790; see [[cebriones]]). The epithets of the scene are the old ones, δῖος, “brilliant” (13.766), and θεοειδής, “godlike” (13.774; see [[brilliant]] and [[godlike]]).
