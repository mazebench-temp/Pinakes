**Tears before the battle.** The Achaeans whom Poseidon finds in the rear are worn out, “and grief came into their hearts as they watched / the Trojans, who had climbed over the great wall in a throng. / As they looked at them they let tears fall from beneath their brows; / for they thought they would not escape from the evil” (13.86–89; see [[achos]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). These are tears of despair, shed for no one dead, by men who see their own end coming. They are an appeal too, and the god answers it, not with comfort but with rebuke: “Shame, Argives, young men!” (13.95; see [[aidos]] and [[poseidon]]). ὑπʼ ὀφρύσι δάκρυα λεῖβον, “they let tears fall from beneath their brows”, is found once more in Homer, with the participle λείβων, of Odysseus at the Phaeacians' feast, weeping in secret behind his cloak (*Odyssey* 8.83–86; see the article above).

**Tears behind the body.** Harpalion, killed by Meriones' arrow, is lifted into a chariot by the Paphlagonians and taken to Ilios, “grieving; and his father went with them, letting tears fall, / and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.656–659; see [[harpalion]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). They are the only tears in the book shed for a dead man, and they go with the body to the city; on the field the next lines turn to Paris' anger and another death (13.660–672).

**The tearful battle.** When Hector goes to look for his leaders he finds Paris “on the left of the tearful battle” (μάχης ἐπʼ ἀριστερὰ δακρυοέσσης, 13.765; see [[tearful]]). The epithet was given to war when Athena armed in Books 5 and 8 (5.737, 8.388) and to the onslaught that Achilles watches from his ship in Book 11 (ἰῶκά τε δακρυόεσσαν, 11.599–601); it will describe the battle again when Zeus wonders whether to snatch Sarpedon “alive out of the tearful battle” (16.436).
