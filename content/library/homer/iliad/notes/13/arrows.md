summary: Two arrows kill in Book 13, Meriones' “bronze-fitted arrow” through Harpalion and Paris' through Euchenor, told in the same half-verse; Helenus' “bitter arrow” glances from Menelaus' corselet; and the Locrians' arrows throw the Trojans into confusion. βέλος is any missile: Idomeneus asks whether a missile's point is wearing Meriones down, and Poseidon guards Antilochus among many missiles (13.251, 13.511–513, 13.555, 13.585–592, 13.650–662, 13.722).

**Missiles.** βέλος, from βάλλω, “throw, shoot”, is any missile, arrow, spear or stone (see the entry). Idomeneus asks Meriones whether he has left the fight because he has been hit, and “the point of a missile” is wearing him down (βέλεος … ἀκωκή, 13.251; see [[meriones]]). The brave man, he says, is hit in the chest or the belly, and the missile never falls on the back of his neck (13.288–291; see [[ambush]]). Idomeneus himself is now too slow on his feet to dart after his own spear or to avoid a missile, and as he tries to strip Oenomaus he is “hard pressed by missiles” (βελέεσσιν, 13.509–513; see [[idomeneus]]). Poseidon guards Antilochus “even among many missiles” (13.555; see [[antilochus]] and [[poseidon]]).

**The bitter arrow.** Helenus shoots at Menelaus “with an arrow” (ἰῷ, 13.586), and it strikes the plate of his corselet; “the bitter arrow flew off” (ἀπὸ δʼ ἔπτατο πικρὸς ὀϊστός, 13.587), and like beans from a winnowing shovel it “glanced far away and flew off” (13.592; see [[corselet]] and [[helenus]]). The bitter arrow is Pandarus' in Books 4 and 5 (4.118, 4.134, 5.99) and Teucer's in Book 8 (8.323). Pandarus' arrow went through the plate of Diomedes' corselet in the same half-verse (διὰ δʼ ἔπτατο, 5.99); Helenus' flies off.

**Two deaths.** Meriones lets fly “a bronze-fitted arrow” at Harpalion as he draws back (ἵει χαλκήρεʼ ὀϊστόν, 13.650), and Paris, angry for the death of his guest-friend, lets fly another at Euchenor (προΐει χαλκήρεʼ ὀϊστόν, 13.662; see [[harpalion]], [[euchenor]] and [[bronze-fitted]]). The two half-verses set the killing and the revenge side by side. Harpalion dies “like a worm on the ground” (13.654; see [[like-a-worm]]), and Euchenor, who knew his fate, dies at once (13.671–672).

**The Locrians.** The book's last arrows are the Locrians', shot from behind the men in armor: “and of the joy of battle / the Trojans remembered nothing; for the arrows threw them into confusion” (13.721–722; see [[locrians]], [[bow]] and [[sling]]).
