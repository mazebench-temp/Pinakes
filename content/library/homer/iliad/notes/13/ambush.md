summary: Idomeneus tells Meriones that an ambush is where a man's prowess is seen most clearly: the coward's color changes, he cannot sit still, his heart pounds and his teeth chatter, while the brave man is not much afraid once he has taken his place, and prays to close with the enemy quickly (13.275–287).

Meriones, his spear broken on Deiphobus' shield, meets Idomeneus by the huts and is quick to say that he too takes his stand among the foremost. Idomeneus answers that he knows it, and gives the proof, the Iliad's fullest account of an ambush (13.246–294; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]], [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]):

**The custom.** The speech tells how an ambush was laid. The best men are picked for it (λεγοίμεθα, “were being chosen”, 13.276), as Achilles' taunt took for granted when he said that Agamemnon had never dared go on ambush “with the best of the Achaeans” (1.227), and as the king of Lycia chose the best men of his country to lie in wait for Bellerophon (6.188–189). They take their places and sit hidden (ἧσθαι, ἐσίζηται λόχον, 13.280, 13.285) until the enemy comes, and then they must close with him at once (13.286). In the open fight a man stands in the ranks; in the ambush there is nothing to do but wait, and fear has time to show in the body. The coward cannot keep still, and the brave man's color does not change (13.279–285).

**Elsewhere.** The poem tells only one ambush at length, the night ambush of Book 10, whose victim shows the coward's signs (10.374–376). In the Odyssey the wooden horse is “a close ambush” (πυκινὸν λόχον, Od. 11.525), the test that Neoptolemus passed without turning pale (Od. 11.523–532), and the disguised Odysseus, posing as a Cretan, as Idomeneus is, boasts that whenever he chose the best men for an ambush his heart never foresaw death (Od. 14.216–221).
