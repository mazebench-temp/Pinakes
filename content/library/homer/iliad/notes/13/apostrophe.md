**Menelaus.** “And Peisander went straight for glorious Menelaus; / and an evil fate was leading him toward the end of death, / to be beaten down by you, Menelaus, in grim combat” (13.601–603; see [[menelaus]] and [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]]). In Books 4 and 7 the narrator turned to Menelaus where Menelaus himself was in danger (4.127, 4.146, 7.104). Here the death named is the other man's, and it is named before the fight begins; then the two come close, Menelaus' spear misses, and Peisander's axe falls on his helmet (13.604–615; see [[when-they-came-close]] and [[axe]]). The later addresses to Menelaus come in the fight for Patroclus' body and at the games (17.679, 17.702, 23.600).

**The onlooker.** When the armies meet on the left, the narrator imagines a man watching: “he would be very bold of heart / who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (13.343–344; see [[the-armies-clash]]). The first battle of the poem ended with such a man, one who, coming into the work unwounded, “could make light of it” no more (4.539–542).
