---
summary: Proclus quotes the verse that explains Poseidon's secret help, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355), four times in his commentaries on Plato, and reads it as a statement of rank among the gods, not of age.
---

Homer's verse gives Poseidon's reason for helping the Achaeans only in secret, “in the likeness of a man”: the two gods are “of one race and of one father's line”, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.354–357; see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]). The same words close Odysseus' claim to advise Achilles, ἐπεὶ πρότερος γενόμην καὶ πλείονα οἶδα (19.219), and Poseidon's challenge to Apollo in the battle of the gods (21.440). Proclus quotes the verse in two of his commentaries on Plato.

- **On the Cratylus.** Plato's Socrates derives Poseidon's name three ways: from the sea, which stopped the god as he walked and became a “foot-bond” to him; from “knowing many things” (πολλὰ εἰδότος); and from “the shaker” (*Cratylus* 402e–403a). Proclus explains the three by the god's lot, the sea, which answers to the world of becoming; by his communion with the god above him; and by his activity outward, which he shares with Pluto. For the second he cites the Homeric verse, said “by way of comparison” of Zeus, for such a Zeus is the intelligible next above Poseidon (Proclus, *On the Cratylus* 151). Poseidon knows many things; Zeus, in Homer's comparative, knows more.
- **On the Timaeus.** Three times in the *Timaeus* commentary. Plato says that the god did not make the soul younger than the body, since he would not let the elder be ruled by the younger (*Timaeus* 34b–c). The rule of the elder over the younger, Proclus answers, reaches down from the gods themselves: among the intellectual gods the father, Zeus, is called the eldest of all the sons of Cronus, “but Zeus was born first and knew more”, for everywhere the elder is the sign of a life more intellective, more universal and more unified (*On the Timaeus* II 115 Diehl). Where Plato says that what is eternal becomes neither older nor younger through time (37e–38a), Proclus finds an elder in the intelligible world, “but Zeus was born first”, and a younger, Hebe, who pours the gods' nectar (4.2–3), though neither comes about through time (III 47 Diehl; see [[hebe]]). And when the Demiurge hands the newly sown souls to “the young gods” (42d), he suggests that the gods within the cosmos are called young by comparison with the elder, invisible creation: “for among the gods the more intellective is elder; ‘but Zeus was born first and knew more,’ says Homer” (III 310 Diehl).

Proclus' Zeus is first-born in rank, not in time. The ancient commentary on 13.355 read the verse more simply: Homer always makes the elder the more experienced, and Poseidon, whom he makes great in strength, yields to Zeus in honor, not in might (scholia on 13.355). In Hesiod, Zeus is the youngest of Rhea's children (see [[hesiod]]).
