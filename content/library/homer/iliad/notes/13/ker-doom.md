summary: Three Trojans in one book draw back into the crowd of their companions “avoiding doom”, and two of them die at once; the coward in ambush sits “foreseeing the fates”; and Euchenor goes on board his ship “knowing well his deadly fate”, one of two his father had foretold.

**Avoiding doom.** “And he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom” (ἂψ δʼ ἑτάρων εἰς ἔθνος ἐχάζετο κῆρʼ ἀλεείνων) is a verse of retreat that the Iliad gives to Paris before Menelaus (3.32), to the wounded Eurypylus (11.585), to Hector when his spear has failed against Ajax, who strikes him with a stone as he withdraws (14.402–412) and to Euphorbus after he has wounded Patroclus (16.817). Book 13 uses it three times, of three Trojans. Adamas, whose spear Poseidon has broken in Antilochus' shield, draws back, but Meriones follows him and spears him between the genitals and the navel, “where most of all / Ares is painful for miserable mortals” (13.560–575; see [[adamas]]). Helenus, his hand pinned to his bow by Menelaus' spear, draws back and lives (13.593–600; see [[helenus]]). Harpalion, whose spear has failed against Menelaus' shield, draws back “glancing all around, lest someone graze his flesh with the bronze”, and Meriones' arrow kills him as he goes (13.646–655; see [[harpalion]]). The verse says what the man intends; twice in this book the doom he avoids catches him in the act. Meriones himself twice draws back into the crowd of his companions in the book, without the doom (13.165, 13.533).

**Foreseeing the fates.** In Idomeneus' picture of the ambush the coward's heart pounds against his chest “as he foresees the fates” (κῆρας ὀϊομένῳ, 13.283; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). The κῆρες here are the deaths that wait for any man, which Sarpedon called “countless, which no mortal can flee or escape” (12.326–327; see the note on Book 12). The brave man does not think of them; the coward can think of nothing else. Achilles' insult to Agamemnon was the same: to go to ambush with the best of the Achaeans “seems to you death” (κήρ, 1.227–228).

**Two fates.** Euchenor, son of the seer Polyidus, “had gone on board his ship knowing well his deadly fate” (εὖ εἰδὼς κῆρʼ ὀλοήν, 13.665; see [[euchenor]] and [[polyidus-the-seer]]). His father had often told him that he would perish of a grievous sickness in his own halls, or be beaten down by the Trojans among the ships, “so he avoided at once the grievous fine of the Achaeans / and the hateful sickness, so that he would not suffer sorrows in his heart” (13.666–670; see [[nousos]]). Achilles told the envoys of Book 9 that his mother had told him of “two different fates” (διχθαδίας κῆρας), a long life at home without fame or a short one at Troy with imperishable fame (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Euchenor's choice has the same shape and other reasons: not fame, but escape from a fine and from a slow death at home. Paris' arrow takes him under the jaw, “and hateful darkness seized him” (13.671–672).
