**Aeneas' wrath.** Deiphobus, challenged by Idomeneus, looks for a companion and finds Aeneas “at the very back of the throng, / standing there; for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, / because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.459–461; see [[aeneas]] and [[priam]]). ἐπιμηνίω, “nurse wrath against”, is found only here, and it is the one place where the Iliad gives the μῆνις family to a mortal other than Achilles and, once, Agamemnon (1.247; see the article above). The complaint is in Achilles' terms. Achilles withdrew because Agamemnon “paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans” (1.244, 1.412; see [[time]]); Aeneas stands apart because Priam does not honor him, “brave as he was”. The cause is not told (see the translation's note on 13.459–461). Achilles will later taunt Aeneas with hoping to rule the Trojans with Priam's honor, which Priam, who has sons, will not put in his hand (20.178–183), and Poseidon will say that Zeus has come to hate the race of Priam and that Aeneas and his children's children will rule the Trojans (20.306–308).

The Trojan wrath ends as the Achaean one does not. Deiphobus appeals to kinship: “now you must indeed / defend your brother-in-law, if any grief for your kin touches you”, Alcathous, who reared him in his house when he was small and now lies dead (13.463–467; see [[alcathous]] and [[in-laws]]). The words stir his heart, and he goes after Idomeneus at once (13.468–469). Phoenix had reminded Achilles that he too had reared him, on his knees (9.485–495), and was refused.

**The wrath of Zeus.** Standing over Peisander, Menelaus charges the Trojans with having insulted him without fearing “the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests, who will one day destroy your steep city” (Ζηνὸς ἐριβρεμέτεω χαλεπὴν ἐδείσατε μῆνιν / ξεινίου, 13.624–625; see [[xenia]] and [[who-thunders-loud]]). A god's μῆνις is what men fear and keep clear of: Athena leads Ares out of the battle, to “avoid the wrath of Zeus” (5.34), and Diomedes and Patroclus give ground, “avoiding the wrath of Apollo” (5.444, 16.711). In the Odyssey the same god guards the same bond. In Odysseus' false tale the king of Egypt spares him, though his men want him dead, “for he feared the wrath of Zeus, god of guests, who above all resents evil deeds” (*Odyssey* 14.283–284). The Trojans' fault, in Menelaus' eyes, is that they did not fear it, and the sack of Troy will be its sanction (see [[hybris]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]).
