---
summary: Herodotus describes the Scythian nomads who milk their mares and live in wagons, the peoples later readers found in 13.5–6; he has Homer's word for Hector's rolling boulder in an oracle about Cypselus and in the siege of the Acropolis; and he tells how the Athenians came to be called Ionians, and how Mysians crossed into Europe before the Trojan War.
---

- **Mares' milk.** The Scythians blind all their slaves for the sake of the milk they drink. Some blow into the mares through bone tubes like pipes while others milk, saying that the blowing fills the veins and lets down the udder; then they pour the milk into hollow wooden vessels, set the blind men around them to stir it, and skim off the top as the more valuable part, “for they are not plowmen but nomads” (*Histories* 4.2.1–2). They have no cities or walls, but carry their houses with them, all of them mounted archers, living not from the plow but from their herds, with their dwellings on wagons; how could such men not be invincible and impossible to engage? (*Histories* 4.46.3). Ancient readers took Homer's “proud mare-milkers / who live on milk” (13.5–6) for these nomads: see [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]] and [[abii]].
- **The rolling boulder.** Hector comes on “like a rolling boulder from a rock” (ὀλοοίτροχος, 13.137), the only use of the word in Homer (see [[the-rolling-boulder]]). Herodotus has it twice. When Eetion of Corinth went to Delphi to ask about children, the Pythia greeted him with the verses: “Eetion, no one honors you, though you are worthy of much honor. Labda is with child, and she will bear a rolling boulder, which will fall on the men who rule alone” (*Histories* 5.92β.2). The child, saved from the Bacchiads in a chest (κυψέλη) and named Cypselus after it, became tyrant of Corinth (5.92ε.1–2). And when Xerxes besieged the Acropolis, the Athenians inside rolled boulders (ὀλοιτρόχους) down on the Persians as they came up to the gates (*Histories* 8.52.2).
- **The Ionians.** The Athenians, Herodotus says, were Pelasgians called Cranai, then Cecropidae under Cecrops, Athenians under Erechtheus, and Ionians when Ion son of Xuthus became their commander (*Histories* 8.44.2); elsewhere he says that the Athenians, like the other Ionians, shunned the name, and that most of them seemed to him ashamed of it (1.143.3). The only Ionians in Homer are “the Ionians with trailing tunics” of 13.685, whom the poem seems to pick up as “the picked men of the Athenians” (13.689; see [[ionians]] and [[athenians]]).
- **Mysians in Europe.** Among the great expeditions of the past Herodotus counts one of the Mysians and Teucrians before the Trojan War, who crossed into Europe by the Bosporus, subdued all the Thracians, and came down to the Ionian Sea and south as far as the river Peneus (*Histories* 7.20.2); the Bithynians said that the Teucrians and Mysians had driven them from their homes on the Strymon (7.75.2). Strabo, after Posidonius, took the Mysians “who fight hand to hand” of 13.5 for Mysians of Europe, from whom he thought the Mysians of Asia had come (see [[mysians]] and [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]).
