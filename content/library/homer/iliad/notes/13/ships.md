**The goal.** Zeus has brought the Trojans and Hector “close to the ships” (13.1), and the battle of the book is fought among them (see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[title-of-book-13]]). The Trojans “hoped to take the ships of the Achaeans / and kill all the best men there beside them” (13.41–42), and Hector threatened to go through to the sea “through the huts and ships of the Achaeans, / killing” (13.143–145). Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, calls it “a great wonder” that the Trojans, who used to be like deer quick to flee, are now fighting “far from the city, by the hollow ships” (13.99–107; see [[hollow-ships]]). The ships are the army's only way home: Idomeneus fears it must be Zeus' pleasure that the Achaeans “should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (13.227).

**Fire.** Idomeneus tells Meriones that it will be a steep climb for Hector to overcome the Ajaxes and burn the ships, “unless the son of Cronus himself / casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships” (13.317–320). Menelaus, over Peisander's body, accuses the Trojans of being eager, “among the seafaring ships, / to throw deadly fire, and to kill the Achaean heroes” (13.628–629). Hector promised the fire in Book 8 and called for it at the wall (8.181–183, 12.440–441). In Book 15 Zeus waits to see the gleam of a burning ship, the fulfillment of Thetis' prayer (15.596–600), and Hector lays hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire (15.704–718; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**At the sterns.** The ships were drawn up with their sterns to the plain, and the fighting is at the sterns (see [[stern]]): the quarrel stands even “at the sterns of the ships” (13.333), and Hector finds some of his leaders lying dead “at the sterns of the ships of the Achaeans” (13.762).

**The ships of Ajax and Protesilaus.** Hector holds on where he first leapt in, “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were, / drawn up on the shore of the gray sea; and above them / the wall had been built lowest” (13.681–683; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Which Ajax is meant is not said (see the translation's note on 13.681). Protesilaus, the first of the Achaeans to leap from his ship, was killed at the landing (2.698–702; see [[protesilaus]]); his ship is the one Hector seizes in Book 15, the ship “that brought Protesilaus / to Troy, and did not take him back to his native land” (15.704–706).

**The ships in men's stories.** Imbrius came back to Ilios “when the curving ships of the Danaans came” (13.174; see [[imbrius]]). Idomeneus tells Deiphobus that “the ships have brought me here / to be an evil to you” (13.453–454), and Euchenor “had gone on board his ship knowing well his deadly fate” (13.665; see [[euchenor]]). Meriones keeps his spoils “beside my hut and my black ship” (13.267; see [[hut]]), and Idomeneus invites the dead Othryoneus to the “seafaring ships” to settle the terms of his marriage (13.381; see [[bride-price]]). Polydamas asks whether the Trojans should “fall upon the many-benched ships” or come away from them unharmed (13.742–744; see [[many-benched]]).
