---
summary: Troy is the city behind the battle at the ships. Zeus turns his eyes away from it, Poseidon sees it from Samothrace, the wounded and the dead are carried back to it, and it is named in reproach and in threat, when Hector tells Paris that “now from top to bottom all of steep Ilios has perished” and Ajax tells Hector that “much sooner would your well-peopled city be taken and sacked” (13.772–773, 13.815–816).
grc: ["Πριάμοιο πόλις"]
refs: [13.107, 13.538, 13.625, 13.815, 13.820]
---

**Seen from afar.** The book opens with two views of the city. Zeus “no longer turned his shining eyes at all” to Troy (13.7), but from Samothrace Poseidon sees Ida, “and the city of Priam could be seen, and the ships of the Achaeans” (13.14; see [[samothrace]] and [[ida]]). Zeus, the narrator says, did not want the Achaean army to perish “before Ilios” (13.349).

**Far from the city.** Poseidon counts it a wonder and a shame that the Trojans, who once would not face the Achaeans, are now fighting “far from the city, by the hollow ships” (13.107). The city is where the dead and wounded go back: Deiphobus, wounded, is carried “toward the city” (13.538), and the Paphlagonians take Harpalion's body “to sacred Ilios”, his father weeping behind (13.656–658; see [[harpalion]]). The Trojans would have fallen back “to windy Ilios” but for Polydamas (13.723–725; see [[polydamas]]). When Hector finds his brothers and allies dead or wounded, he cries out to Paris: “Now from top to bottom all of steep Ilios / has perished; now sheer destruction is sure for you” (13.772–773; see [[paris]]). Some of the wounded, he finds, are “within the wall” (13.764), most naturally the city's, as Deiphobus will be “within the wall” of Troy when Hector looks for him in vain (22.299).

**The city at stake.** Men came to Troy for the war. Imbrius came back to Ilios when the Achaeans came, and Priam honored him like his own children (13.174–176; see [[imbrius]]); Othryoneus came “at the report of the war”, promising to drive the Achaeans from Troy for the hand of Cassandra (13.363–369), and Idomeneus mocks him with a better offer, if he will help sack “the well-peopled citadel of Ilios” (13.380; see [[othryoneus]]). Menelaus warns that Zeus, god of guests, “will one day destroy your steep city” (13.625; see [[menelaus]] and [[xenia]]), and Ajax tells Hector that the Trojan city would fall long before the ships, and that Hector will soon pray for swift horses to carry him “to the city” (13.815–820; see [[ajax]]). Harpalion followed his father to Troy “and did not come back again to his native land” (13.645); nearly the same words are said of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and did not bring him home (15.705–706; see [[protesilaus]]).
