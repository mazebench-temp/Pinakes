---
summary: Idomeneus greets Meriones as “dearest of my companions”, the address Achilles will use to the dead Patroclus; Ares goes to war with “his dear son” Rout; and men die with their hands stretched to their “dear companions”.
grc: [φίλταθʼ]
---

**Dearest of companions.** Meeting Meriones near the huts, Idomeneus calls him “Meriones, son of Molus, swift of foot, dearest of my companions” (φίλταθʼ ἑταίρων, 13.249; see [[meriones]], [[idomeneus]] and [[hetairos]]). Meriones is his θεράπων, his attendant (13.246, 13.331), and the superlative names the closeness of a leader and the man who fights at his side. Achilles uses the same words when he mourns Patroclus, “you too, ill-fated, dearest of my companions”, remembering how he used to set a meal before him in the hut (19.315–316; see [[patroclus]]).

**Dear kin, dear companions.** The adjective marks the bonds the book's similes and deaths are made of: Ares goes into war with Rout, “his dear son” (φίλος υἱός, 13.299; see [[ares-and-rout]]); Harpalion had followed “his dear father” to Troy (13.644); Thoon falls stretching out his hands “to his dear companions”, and Harpalion dies “in the arms of his dear companions” (13.549, 13.653). Idomeneus calls his own men to him with it, “Come here, friends, and defend me” (δεῦτε φίλοι, 13.481). Hector is “dear to Zeus” in the verse that says he did not know that his men were being cut down on the left (Διῒ φίλος, 13.674; see [[dear-to-zeus]]).
