---
summary: Hera takes no part in Book 13 and is named twice by Hector: as the wife of Zeus, “the loud-thundering husband of Hera”, who he trusts has roused him, and as the mother he wishes he had, “if only … queen Hera had borne me”.
---

**The husband of Hera.** Pushed back from the close ranks around the two Ajaxes, Hector tells his men that the Achaeans will give ground, “if truly / the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.153–154; see [[hector]], [[loud-thundering]] and [[husband-of-hera]]). The formula names Zeus in oaths and wishes that call on him to make something true (7.411, 10.329). Here Hector stakes his attack on it.

**The mother he wishes for.** At the end of the book Ajax tells him that the Achaeans can still defend their ships, and Hector answers with a wish: “If only I were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus / all my days, and queen Hera had borne me, / and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored” (13.825–827; see [[queenly]]). The wish has the form of the one he made in Book 8, to be immortal and ageless (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The pair whose son he would be are the parents of Ares (5.892–896), and Hera is the goddess who, Zeus says, would eat Priam and his sons raw if she could (4.34–36; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]).

Hera herself does not act in the book, but the next one is hers. Watching from Olympus, she sees Poseidon at work among the Achaeans and rejoices, sees Zeus on Ida and hates him, and sets out to deceive him; while Zeus sleeps, Poseidon leads the Achaeans openly (14.153–165, 14.352–360; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). In Book 15 she tells Ares that his son Ascalaphus, who dies in this book, is dead (15.110–112; see [[ares]]).
