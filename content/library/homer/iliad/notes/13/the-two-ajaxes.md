summary: The two Ajaxes are the first men Poseidon rouses, striking them both with his staff, and the close ranks that stop Hector form around them. They carry off the body of Imbrius like two lions with a goat, and where Hector broke in they stand side by side like two oxen yoked to one plow (13.46–82, 13.126, 13.197–202, 13.701–708).

**The god's first call.** Poseidon comes up out of the sea in Calchas' likeness and “first he spoke to the two Ajaxes, who were already eager themselves” (13.46; see [[poseidon]] and [[calchas]]). He addresses them in the dual, Αἴαντε σφώ, “Ajaxes, you two” (13.47): “Ajaxes, you two will save the army of the Achaeans / if you remember your courage, and not chilling rout” (13.47–48). Elsewhere the Achaeans will hold; here Hector leads the Trojans “like a flame” (13.53), and the god asks the pair “to stand firm yourselves and to urge the others” (13.56). It has been their work since the review, where Agamemnon gave them no orders, “for you yourselves bid the people fight with all their might” (4.287), and on the towers of Book 12 (12.265–277). Then he strikes them both with his staff and fills them with might (13.59–61). The son of Oileus knows the god, and Telamonian Ajax feels his hands quiver around his spear: “So they spoke such words to one another, / rejoicing in the joy of battle that the god had cast into their hearts” (13.66–82; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[ajax]]).

**At the center.** “And around the two Ajaxes the battalions took their stand” (13.126), the close-packed ranks against which Hector's charge breaks (13.126–148; see [[battle-order]]). Idomeneus leaves the middle of the ships to “the two Ajaxes and Teucer” (13.312–318; see [[teucer]] and [[idomeneus]]). The plural with δύω, Αἴαντές τε δύω (13.313), serves the pair as the dual does.

**Two lions.** “And Imbrius the two Ajaxes, eager with furious courage, carried off” (13.197). Simile and story keep the dual. As two lions snatch a goat from the dogs and carry it high in their jaws, “so the two Ajaxes, helmeted men, held him high / and stripped his armor” (13.198–202; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]] and [[imbrius]]). In Book 5 two lions were the twin sons of Diocles, killed by Aeneas (5.554–560; see [[two-lions]]); here the lions win. Then the son of Oileus, alone, beheads the body (13.202–205).

**The yoke.** Where the wall was lowest and Hector held on, the two stand closer than ever:

βόε, “two oxen”, is dual too. ἶσον θυμὸν ἔχοντε, “with equal spirit” (13.704), comes back in Book 17, when Telamonian Ajax speaks for the pair: the two of them, “of one name”, will fight the Trojans and Hector “with equal spirit”, as they have long awaited sharp Ares standing beside each other (17.719–721). Only the yoke keeps the oxen apart, but the poet parts the men at once by their followers: companions take over Telamonian Ajax's shield when he tires, and the Locrians, bowmen and slingers, do not follow the son of Oileus into close battle (13.709–722; see [[locrians]]).
