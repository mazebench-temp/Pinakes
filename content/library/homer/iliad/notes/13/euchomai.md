---
summary: Book 13 is a book of boasts. Hector “claims” to be the son of Zeus and at the end wishes it; four victors boast over the dead, Idomeneus over Othryoneus and Alcathous, Deiphobus over Hypsenor, Menelaus over Peisander; and Idomeneus chooses the left to see “whether we will hold out triumph to another, or another to us”.

**A claim and a wish.** Poseidon, as Calchas, warns the two Ajaxes of the place “where that frenzied man leads them, like a flame, / Hector, who claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (ὃς Διὸς εὔχετʼ ἐρισθενέος πάϊς εἶναι, 13.53–54; see [[hector]] and [[frenzied]]). εὔχομαι is to declare what one is, and the god reports it as a boast. At the end of the book Hector says it of himself as a wish, with an oath's certainty: “If only I were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus / all my days, and queen Hera had borne me, … / as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (13.825–828; see [[time]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**Triumph.** Idomeneus tells Meriones to steer for the left, “so that as quickly as may be / we may see whether we will hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (ἠέ τῳ εὖχος ὀρέξομεν, ἦέ τις ἡμῖν, 13.326–327; see [[idomeneus]]). The half-verse is Sarpedon's to Glaucus (12.328; see the note on Book 12): εὖχος, “triumph”, is what the victor claims and the fallen man gives.

**Boasts over the dead.** Four victors speak over the men they have killed, and the speeches answer one another. Idomeneus “boasted over him and spoke” over Othryoneus, mocking the bargain for Cassandra (13.373–382; see [[othryoneus]] and [[fathers-and-daughters]]). Deiphobus, avenging Asius, “boasted terribly, shouting aloud”, that Asius would go to Hades glad of the escort he had given him (13.413–416; see [[poine]] and [[aute]]), “and grief came upon the Argives at his boasting” (13.417; see [[achos]]). Idomeneus kills Alcathous and answers in the same verse, “Deiphobus, shall we then think it a fair exchange, / three killed for one, since you boast like this?” (εὔχεαι, 13.445–447; see [[genee]] and [[kotos]]). And Menelaus, his heel on Peisander's chest, “spoke in triumph” (εὐχόμενος, 13.619), but his triumph turns into a complaint to Zeus about the Trojans' outrage (13.620–639; see [[hybris]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]).
