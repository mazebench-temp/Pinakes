**The bargain.** Idomeneus' first victim on the left is an ally who had come “not long before at the report of the war” (13.363–364; see [[othryoneus]] and [[cabesus]]):

{{quote:3f14f8c1-7747-54a3-b307-b2935355eb54}}

Cassandra is “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam”, the words used of her sister Laodice (13.365; 3.124, 6.252; see [[cassandra]]). A deed takes the place of the price: ἀνάεδνον, “without bride-price”, as Agamemnon offered his daughter to Achilles (9.146), and in return “a great deed”. Priam “promised him and nodded his assent / to give her” (13.368–369), in the words of Zeus' promises to Agamemnon and to Hector (2.112, 12.236) and of Hector's promise of Achilles' horses to Dolon, another reward promised for a deed to a man who did not live to claim it (δωσέμεναι κατένευσε, 10.393; see [[dolon]]). In the Odyssey Menelaus “promised and nodded his assent to give” his daughter Hermione to the son of Achilles at Troy, and the gods brought the marriage about (Od. 4.5–7). Othryoneus “fought, trusting in the promises” (13.369; see [[priam]]).

**The taunt.** Idomeneus spears him in the belly and boasts over him:

{{quote:c11cf530-7db5-5f74-82a6-4ee0e576c89c}}

The taunt turns Othryoneus' terms around. The bride is “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of the son of Atreus”, in the words of Cassandra's verse (13.378 ≈ 13.365), and she is to be brought from Argos, where Agamemnon's offer in Book 9 was to be made good (9.141–144); the deed is the opposite one, to sack Ilios. The meeting at the ships to settle the marriage (ἀμφὶ γάμῳ) is the place to which Idomeneus now drags the body by the foot (13.383; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). ἐεδνωταί occurs only here: the Achaeans speak as the bride's kin, and “not bad” ones, generous with the ἔεδνα. Whether these are the suitor's gifts, which the bride's kin would generously spare him, or gifts that go with the bride, like Agamemnon's dowry (9.147–148), is disputed (see the translation's note on 13.382). Either way the joke turns on the custom that a suitor gives for his bride: Othryoneus offered a deed for Cassandra, and paid with his life. Hector names him among the dead when he rebukes Paris: “And where is Othryoneus?” (13.772; see [[idomeneus]] and [[marriage]]).
