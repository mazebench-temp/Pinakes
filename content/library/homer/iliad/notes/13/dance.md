---
summary: Dancing is twice named in Book 13 as the opposite of war. Menelaus counts it among the pleasures of which men have their fill, “of sweet song and of flawless dancing”, while the Trojans cannot have their fill of battle (13.636–639), and Polydamas among the gifts a god gives to different men, “to another dancing, to another the lyre and song” (13.730–731).
grc: [ὀρχηθμοῖο, ὀρχηστύν]
---

**Satiety.** Over Peisander's body Menelaus speaks of the Trojans' appetite for war:

“Of all things there is satiety, of sleep and of love, / of sweet song and of flawless dancing, / of which a man would rather have his fill / than of war; but the Trojans are insatiable of battle” (13.636–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]] and [[menelaus]]). Song and dance are the pleasures of peace and of the feast: in the Odyssey “song and dancing” are “the ornaments of a feast” (μολπή τʼ ὀρχηστύς τε, Od. 1.152). Menelaus' second verse returns whole when Odysseus, having killed the suitors, has the bard rouse his household to dance, so that passers-by will think a wedding is being held (Od. 23.143–145).

**A gift.** Polydamas tells Hector that one man cannot take everything at once: “For to one man a god has given the works of war, / to another dancing, to another the lyre and song, / and in the breast of another far-seeing Zeus puts a mind / that is good” (13.730–733; see [[different-gifts]], [[lyre]] and [[polydamas]]). The dancer is set against the fighter here as in Aphrodite's words of Paris, who looked as if he were going to a dance and not coming from a fight (3.392–394; see the entry), but without scorn: each skill is a god's gift. The scorn comes later, when Aeneas mocks Meriones as a dancer (ὀρχηστήν, 16.617) and Priam abuses his surviving sons as liars and dancers (24.261).
