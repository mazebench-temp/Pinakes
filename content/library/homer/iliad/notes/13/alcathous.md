summary: Alcathous, Anchises' son-in-law and “the best man in wide Troy”, cannot flee when Poseidon bewitches his eyes and fetters his limbs, and Idomeneus spears him through the heart as he stands like a pillar. Deiphobus brings Aeneas, whom Alcathous had reared, and the two sides fight over his body.

**The best man in Troy.** The narrator names him and turns aside to his wife, Hippodameia, the eldest and best-loved of Anchises' daughters, “and so / the best man in wide Troy had married her” (13.427–433; see [[hippodameia-daughter-of-anchises]] and [[anchises]]). The sentence comes back to him only for his death.

**Bound by a god.** “Him then Poseidon beat down at the hands of Idomeneus, / bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (13.434–435; see [[poseidon]] and [[idomeneus]]). θέλγω, “bewitch”, is a god's work, as when Zeus bewitched the Achaeans' minds with a storm of dust (12.254–255). He can neither run back nor swerve, and stands motionless “like a pillar or a tree with high foliage” (13.436–438; see [[like-a-pillar]]). The spear breaks the bronze tunic that had kept destruction from him, and “it rang dry as it was torn about the spear”; it stands in his heart, and the heart, still beating, shakes its butt, “and then mighty Ares let its might go” (13.439–444; see [[corselet]] and the translation's note on 13.443–444). Idomeneus counts him in his boast: “shall we then think it a fair exchange, / three killed for one” (13.446–447; see [[deiphobus]]).

**His foster son.** Deiphobus calls on Aeneas to defend “Alcathous, who in former days, / being your brother-in-law, reared you in his house when you were small” (13.465–466; see [[aeneas]]). “And around Alcathous they rushed at one another hand to hand” (13.496), while Aeneas and Idomeneus, the equals of Ares, try for each other with the bronze (13.496–505; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The poem does not say what became of the body; the fight moves on to Ascalaphus (13.516–526).
