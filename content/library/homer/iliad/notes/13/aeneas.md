summary: Aeneas stands at the back of the throng, nursing his wrath against Priam, until Deiphobus calls on him to defend Alcathous, the brother-in-law who reared him. Idomeneus, afraid of his youth, calls his companions; Aeneas leads the Trojans like a ram before the flock, misses Idomeneus, and kills Aphareus.

**Wrath against Priam.** Deiphobus finds him “at the very back of the throng, / standing there; for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, / because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.459–461; see [[priam]] and [[time]]). ἐπεμήνιε, “nursing wrath”, belongs to the family of μῆνις, the word of Achilles' wrath (see [[menis]]). The cause is not told. Achilles will taunt him with hoping to rule the Trojans with the honor of Priam, who has sons of his own (20.178–183), and Poseidon will foretell that, since Zeus has come to hate Priam's line, the might of Aeneas and his children's children will rule the Trojans (20.306–308; see the translation's note on 13.459–461).

**Kinship.** Deiphobus calls him “counselor of the Trojans” (13.463), as Pandarus did (5.180), and appeals to kinship: “defend your brother-in-law, if any grief for your kin touches you” (13.464). Alcathous, the husband of his eldest sister, Hippodameia, had reared him in his house when he was small, and Idomeneus has killed him (13.465–467; see [[alcathous]], [[hippodameia-daughter-of-anchises]] and [[anchises]]). The words stir his heart, and he goes after Idomeneus (13.468–469).

**Feared.** Idomeneus waits like a boar at bay and calls his companions: he is “terribly afraid / of Aeneas, swift of foot”, who “has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power”; if they were of an age, one of them would soon carry off the mastery (13.470–486; see [[idomeneus]], [[the-bristling-boar]] and [[kratos]]). ἥβης ἄνθος, “the flower of youth”, occurs only here in Homer.

**The ram.** Aeneas calls on Deiphobus, Paris and Agenor, the leaders at his side, and the army follows him “as sheep follow the ram / from the pasture to drink, and the shepherd is glad at heart; / so the heart of Aeneas rejoiced in his breast” (13.489–495; see [[sheep-following-the-ram]]). The simile makes him both ram and shepherd. He and Idomeneus, “the equals of Ares”, rush at each other (13.499–501; see [[equal-of-ares]]). His spear misses and goes “quivering into the earth, / since it had sped in vain from his strong hand” (13.502–505); the two verses return when he throws at Meriones in Book 16 (13.504–505 = 16.614–615).

**Aphareus.** He kills Aphareus, son of Caletor, with a spear in the throat as the man turns toward him, “and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (13.541–544; see [[aphareus]], [[caletor-father-of-aphareus]] and [[that-destroys-the-spirit]]).
