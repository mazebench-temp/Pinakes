summary: Achilles takes the victims he vowed over Patroclus' body. When his hands are weary with killing in the river, he chooses twelve young men alive “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died”, binds their hands with their own straps and sends them to the ships, to be killed at the pyre (21.26–32; 18.336–337, 23.175–176).
# The twelve taken alive, bound and sent to the ships (21.27-32).

**Alive.** ζωούς, “alive” (21.27), comes back three times in the book. Before Patroclus died, Achilles tells Lycaon, he took many Trojans alive and sold them (ζωοὺς ἕλον, 21.102); the Trojans have thrown horses alive into the river's eddies (21.132; see [[rivers]]); and the river saves the living from Achilles, hiding them in his eddies (ζωοὺς δὲ σάω, 21.238; see [[scamander]]). These twelve he takes alive in order to kill them. ποινή, “the penalty” (21.28), is the price paid for a killing (see [[poine]]), and Achilles means all the Trojans to pay it: they will perish “until all of you / pay for the slaughter of Patroclus” (21.133–134; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]).

**The vow and the deed.** The twelve are κοῦροι, “young men” (21.27). In the vow over the body they were “twelve / splendid children of the Trojans” (18.336–337; see the note on Book 18), and at the pyre they are twelve noble sons of the great-hearted Trojans, whom he kills with the bronze (23.175–176). The poem speaks of them again when Achilles repeats the vow at the start of the funeral (23.22–23), and, after the killing, when he tells the dead man that the fire is eating the twelve with him (23.181). Walter Leaf takes the straps that bind them for the leather belts of their tunics, and remarks that they evidently wore no corselets (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.31; see [[well-cut]] and [[belt]]). They are led out “dazed, like fawns” (21.29; see [[like-fawns]]).

**Evil deeds.** As he leaps into the river with his sword, “he devised evil deeds in his mind” (κακὰ δὲ φρεσὶ μήδετο ἔργα, 21.19), and the half-verse is said once more in the poem, when he kills the twelve at the pyre (23.176). Leaf, setting the two lines side by side, denies that κακά passes a moral judgment on Achilles, which would be against the epic style: the work was ill for his victims, as when Zeus “devised evil” for the Achaeans all night, with no condemnation of Zeus (7.478; on 21.19, 23.176).

**Plato.** Plato's Socrates, purging the poets, will not let it be said that Achilles disobeyed the river, though it was a god, and was ready to fight it, nor that he dragged Hector around Patroclus' tomb or slaughtered the captives at the pyre (τὰς τῶν ζωγρηθέντων σφαγὰς εἰς τὴν πυράν): none of this, he says, was truly told (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato]]). ζωγρηθέντων, “of those taken alive”, is the word for the twelve. Proclus' answer to the charge is told under [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]].
