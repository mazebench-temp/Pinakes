summary: The river of Troy is Achilles' adversary in Book 21. Achilles fills his streams with the Trojan dead; the river speaks to him from an eddy, rises against him, calls on his brother the Simoeis, and nearly drowns him, until Hera sends Hephaestus against him. Burned and boiling, he yields and swears never again to save the Trojans.

**The ford.** The book begins at the river: “But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river, / the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (21.1–2), the verses with which the Trojans brought the stunned Hector to its water (14.433–434; see [[fair-flowing]] and [[eddying]]). Walter Leaf notes that the ford is named only in this formal couplet and takes it for a poetical invention, unknown to the older battle scenes, though he observes that the scene answers to the modern river, the Mendere, which can be forded in two places and elsewhere is deep enough to drown a man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.4 and 21.8). Achilles splits the Trojans there. Half of them flee across the plain, and half are penned into “the deep-streaming, silver-eddying river” (21.8; see [[deep-streaming]] and [[silver-eddying]]); they fall in with a great crash and swim this way and that among the eddies, like locusts driven into a river by fire (21.9–16; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]]). So “deep-eddying Xanthus' / sounding stream was filled with horses and men, all mingled together” (21.15–16; see [[deep-eddying]]). Achilles leaves his spear against the tamarisks on the bank and leaps in with his sword, and “the water grew red with blood” (21.17–21; see [[tamarisk]]); the Trojans cower under the banks like small fish fleeing a dolphin (21.22–26; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]), and he takes twelve young men alive out of the river for Patroclus (21.26–32; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

**The god of the Trojans.** Achilles kills Lycaon and throws him into the river: “Scamander / will carry you, eddying, into the broad bosom of the sea” (21.124–125; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The river will not save them, he tells the Trojans, “to whom you have long sacrificed many bulls, / and thrown single-hoofed horses alive into its eddies” (21.130–132; see [[rivers]], [[bulls]] and [[horses]]). Didymus reports that Aristophanes athetized the six verses of this taunt (21.130–135), as added by readers who did not see why the river is angry, when the poet gives the reason a few lines later (see the translation's note on 21.130–135). “So he spoke, and the river grew all the more angry in his heart” (21.136): he puts might into Asteropaeus, “since he was angry for the young men slain in battle, / whom Achilles was cutting down along the stream” (21.145–147; see [[asteropaeus]]). Asteropaeus is the grandson of another river, the Axius, and over his body Achilles boasts that “the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's”, though “here beside you is a great river” (21.184–199; see [[axius]] and [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]); Leaf notes that the river beside him would seem to be the Scamander, though the context suggests the Axius (on 21.192).

**The river speaks.** When Achilles turns on the Paeonians, the river can bear no more:

{{quote:db5abc47-d418-5c3d-9d4d-bc55127b8d9d}}

The god takes a man's likeness, as gods do when they speak to men (see [[divine-epiphany]]), and calls Achilles ὄρχαμε λαῶν, “leader of the people” (21.221). His complaint is the river's own: his lovely streams are full of the dead, and he cannot pour his water into the sea. Achilles answers, “So it shall be, Scamander, nurtured by Zeus, as you bid”, but he will not stop killing Trojans until he has penned them in the city and met Hector (21.222–226). What he grants is not plain. Leaf weighs two readings, a ruse of the river to lure Achilles into his power, which reads a great deal into the text, and irony answering irony, as Monro read it, which makes severe demands on a hearer's imagination (on 21.223). The river then reproaches Apollo, “child of Zeus”, for not keeping the plan of the son of Cronus, who charged him to stand by the Trojans until evening (21.228–232); Leaf defends the speech, often condemned because Apollo is not there to hear it, as a reproach rather than a cry for help (on 21.229; see [[apollo]]).

**The river rises.** Achilles leaps from the bank into the middle of the stream:

{{quote:4b49e0dc-c977-5b51-9649-bf8a332006e9}}

The river fights as a river: he stirs up his streams and casts the dead out onto the land, bellowing like a bull (21.237; see [[like-a-bull]]), and hides the living Trojans in his eddies, his own side in the war of the gods (20.40). Leaf compares the wave that hid Poseidon and Tyro at the mouth of the river Enipeus (on 21.239; *Odyssey* 11.243–244). The wave stands around Achilles and the stream beats on his shield; he catches hold of an elm, which falls, tears away the bank, and bridges the river with its branches (21.240–246; see [[elm]]). He leaps out and runs over the plain, but “the great god did not stop” (21.248), ἀκροκελαινιόων, “darkening at the crest” (21.249), a word found only here. Achilles springs away with the swoop of an eagle, and the river comes after him “with a great din” (21.251–256; see [[like-an-eagle]]); the wave overtakes him as water outruns the man who leads it along a channel, “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.257–264; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]), and as often as he turns to stand, “the great wave of the river fallen from Zeus” beats on his shoulders and eats the ground from under his feet (21.265–271; see [[fallen-from-zeus]]). Achilles cries out to Zeus that he will die a wretched death, “trapped in a great river, like a swineherd boy / whom a torrent sweeps away as he crosses it in winter” (21.272–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]] and [[achilles]]). Poseidon and Athena come to him in the likeness of men and promise that “it is not your fate to be brought down by a river, / but he will soon subside” (21.284–297; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]). The whole plain is full of water now, with armor and corpses floating in it, but the broad-flowing river cannot hold him back, for Athena has put great strength in him (21.299–304).

**The call to the Simoeis.** “Nor did Scamander abate his fury” (21.305). The river makes his wave rear its head and calls on his brother to fill his streams from his springs and raise a great wave of logs and stones, so that they may stop “this savage man, / who now prevails, and is bent on being the equal of the gods” (21.305–315; see [[simoeis]]). Then he tells what he will do:

{{quote:34b912ad-4270-510d-a8c0-1a8ba7855900}}

λίμνη, “lake” (21.317), is the flood now spread over the plain, as Leaf notes (on 21.246, 21.317). The threat is to deny Achilles a burial: the river will be his tomb. Friedländer, as Leaf reports, thought 320–321, where the Achaeans cannot find the bones, and 322–323, where they hold his funeral, two versions joined together; Leaf finds the suggestion unnecessary (on 21.320; see the translation's note on 21.317–323). The Simoeis does not answer, as Apollo did not; Leaf calls the mention of him perfunctory (on 21.307). The river rushes on Achilles, “seething with foam and blood and corpses” (21.324–325), and “the surging wave of the river fallen from Zeus” stands over him (21.326–327). πορφύρεον δʼ ἄρα κῦμα are the first words of the wave that rose over Poseidon and Tyro at the Enipeus (*Odyssey* 11.243).

**Fire.** Hera sends Hephaestus against him, as the gods' pairing had set them in Book 20, “against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river, / whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (20.73–74; see [[hephaestus]] and [[fire]]). Hephaestus burns the dead on the plain and dries it, and turns the flame on the river: the trees and plants of his banks burn, the eels and the fishes in his eddies are in distress, “And the force of the river was burning” (21.342–356). The river gives way at once. No god can match Hephaestus, he says, and he will not fight him; let Achilles drive the Trojans out of their city, “what have I to do with strife and help?” (21.357–360). His streams boil like a cauldron (21.361–365; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]), and he begs Hera to call off her son:

{{quote:ea279537-4176-5c87-8e7b-f8cccf95cbd4}}

He swears in the words of the oath of Hera and Athena (20.313–317; see [[oath]]). The god who saved the living Trojans in his eddies now swears to stand aside when Troy burns (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Hera stops Hephaestus, “and the wave rushed back down along its fair streams” (21.381–382). “But when the might of Xanthus had been overpowered”, the two gods ceased (21.383–384). The river is not seen to act again. At the end of the book Apollo, in the likeness of Agenor, draws Achilles away from the city, “turning him along the deep-eddying river Scamander” (21.602–603).

**Two names.** The book uses both of the river's names, and in its speeches each speaker uses his own. The narrator says Xanthus (21.2, 21.15, 21.146, 21.383) and Scamander (21.305, 21.603); Achilles, a man, says Scamander (21.124, 21.223); Hera, a goddess, says Xanthus (21.332, 21.337). So the river is named as it was named in Book 20, “whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (20.74; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Its epithets in the book are those of a great river: “fair-flowing and silver-eddying” (21.130), “deep-streaming” (21.8), “deep-eddying” (21.15, 21.212, 21.228, 21.329, 21.603), “eddying” (21.2, 21.125, 21.206, 21.332), “fallen from Zeus” (21.268, 21.326) and “broad-flowing” (21.304); Achilles calls it “nurtured by Zeus” (21.223), and the first verses make it a son of Zeus (21.2).
