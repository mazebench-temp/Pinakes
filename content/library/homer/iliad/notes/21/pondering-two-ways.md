**Agenor.** Apollo has set Agenor against Achilles and stands beside him hidden in mist (21.544–549). When Agenor sees Achilles, “his heart heaved with many thoughts as he waited” (21.551). πόρφυρε, “heaved”, is said of a heart elsewhere in Homer only in the Odyssey, where Menelaus and Odysseus go to the ships or to Circe's house with their hearts heaving (πολλὰ δέ μοι κραδίη πόρφυρε κιόντι, *Odyssey* 4.427, 4.572, 10.309), and of the sea once, in the simile of Nestor's indecision (14.16; see the translation's note on 14.16 and [[the-heaving-sea]]). Then “deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (21.552; see [[deeply-troubled]]):

**Three ways.** The first course is flight with the rest, and it is dismissed at once: Achilles would catch him even so, and cut his throat as a coward (21.553–555). ἀνάλκιδα, “a coward”, carries all the contempt of the word (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.555; see the translation's note on 21.555). The second is a longer way round: let the others be driven, run from the wall over the plain to Ida, and come back at evening (21.556–561; see [[ida]] and [[the-ilean-plain]]). Like the third, it is set out as a supposition with no conclusion, εἰ δʼ ἂν … ἐάσω, “But what if I let…”, as Leaf notes (on 21.556, 21.567). The turning verse breaks it off, ἀλλὰ τί ἤ μοι ταῦτα φίλος διελέξατο θυμός; “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (21.562), and a reason follows: Achilles would see him leave the city and catch him with his swift feet, “for he is far too strong, beyond all men” (21.563–566). The third course is to go out to meet him in front of the city, and the argument for it is that Achilles too is mortal: “For surely his flesh too can be pierced by the sharp bronze, / and there is one life in him” (21.567–569). He crouches and waits (21.571; see [[agenor]] and [[the-leopard-and-the-hunter]]).

**The four monologues.** Agenor's is the third of the four monologues in which a warrior alone before a stronger enemy speaks to his own heart: Odysseus' (11.403–410), Menelaus' (17.90–105), Agenor's, and Hector's in the next book (22.98–130). Each opens with the verse of 21.552 and ὤ μοι ἐγώ, “Ah me”, and each turns with the verse of 21.562 (11.407 = 17.97 = 22.122). Leaf finds the scheme of Agenor's closest to Hector's: in both, direct flight is rejected at once, a more roundabout escape is weighed and dropped, and the man decides to stand with some faint hope (on 21.552). Hector's roundabout way is to lay down his arms and go to Achilles with an offer of Helen and the treasures (22.111–121), and his supposition begins as Agenor's does, εἰ δέ κεν (22.111; Leaf on 21.556). Odysseus and Menelaus are interrupted by the enemy while they ponder (11.411–412, 17.106–107); Agenor and Hector finish their debates and wait. Agenor's decision rests on what men say of Achilles, that he is mortal (21.569–570). His spear finds the god-made greave, and he survives only because the god beside him snatches him away (21.590–598; see [[decision-scene]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]).

**Achilles waits for Lycaon.** Achilles too speaks to his own proud heart in this book, in the same verse, when he sees Lycaon come out of the river (21.53). His speech is wonder, not debate: the Trojans he has killed will be rising from the dark, if this man has come back from Lemnos, and his spear will show whether the earth holds him back (21.54–63; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). “So he pondered as he waited” (21.64): ὣς ὅρμαινε μένων are the words that close Hector's monologue in the next book, as Achilles comes near (22.131; see [[achilles]]).

**The river ponders.** After Achilles' boast over Lycaon, the river “pondered in his heart how he might stop from his toil / brilliant Achilles, and ward off ruin from the Trojans” (21.137–138; see [[scamander]]). It is a pondering with no alternatives, a search for a means, like Zeus' at the start of Book 2 and Hera's in Book 14 (2.3–4, 14.159–160). His means is Asteropaeus, into whose heart he puts might (21.144–147; see [[asteropaeus]]).
