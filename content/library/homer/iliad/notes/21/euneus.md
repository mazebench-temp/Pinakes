summary: Euneus is not named in Book 21, but he is “the son of Jason” who paid the price when Achilles sold Lycaon, son of Priam, into Lemnos (21.40–41).
# υἱὸς Ἰήσονος (21.41) is Euneus, as Ἰησονίδης Εὔνηος names him at 7.468 and 23.747; the phrase is found only here.
grc: ["υἱὸς Ἰήσονος"]
---

ἀτὰρ υἱὸς Ἰήσονος ὦνον ἔδωκε, “and the son of Jason paid the price” (21.41; see [[trade]]). The narrator does not name him, but the son of Jason in the Iliad is Euneus, whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, and who sends ships of wine to the Achaean camp (7.467–471; see [[jason]] and [[hypsipyle]]). Walter Leaf thinks that he bought Lycaon as a slave (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.41; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Leaf identifies the price with the silver mixing bowl that Achilles sets out at Patroclus' games (on 21.41), which “as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam”, Euneus gave to Patroclus (23.746–747; see [[mixing-bowl]]). Lycaon puts its worth at a hundred oxen, “I fetched you the worth of a hundred oxen” (21.79), and Leaf takes the hundred oxen for the value of the bowl, not the substance of the payment (on 21.79). Bergk rejected 21.41 as interpolated from the legend of the Argonauts, which is found in the Iliad only in late passages (Leaf on 21.41).

In Book 21 it is Achilles who sells Lycaon, and in Book 23 Euneus pays Patroclus; Leaf finds it natural that Patroclus should have acted in the sale, though Book 21 does not name him there (on 23.746). It names him instead in Achilles' answer to Lycaon: “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.107; see [[patroclus-died-too]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). A guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, later ransomed Lycaon from Lemnos (21.42–43; see [[eetion-of-imbros]] and [[lemnos]]).
