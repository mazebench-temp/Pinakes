---
summary: Achilles leaps at him in the river, and the angry river puts might in him. Asked who he is, he answers with Glaucus' question and his lineage from the Axius; throwing two spears at once, he grazes Achilles' right forearm, the only wound Achilles takes in the poem, but he cannot pull Achilles' spear out of the bank, and dies by his sword. Achilles boasts over him that the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's, and leaves him to the eels and the fishes (21.139–208).

**The river's champion.** Achilles has just killed Lycaon and thrown him into the river, with a taunt that the river will not save the Trojans, though they have long sacrificed bulls to it and thrown horses alive into its eddies (21.120–135; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). “So he spoke, and the river grew all the more angry in his heart” (21.136), and pondered how to stop Achilles. At that moment Achilles leaps at Asteropaeus, and the narrator gives his line in three verses: the Axius “of the broad streams” begot his father Pelegon on Periboea, the eldest of the daughters of Acessamenus (21.139–143; see [[pelegon]], [[periboea]], [[acessamenus]] and [[axius]]). Asteropaeus comes up out of the water to meet him, and the river does what it was pondering:

{{quote:be6772ef-20d1-5c6f-8cd1-234dcb9169bb}}

The grandson of one river fights as the champion of another. The Scamander will soon rise against Achilles himself (21.211–327; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

**The question.** “And when they had come close, advancing against each other”, Achilles speaks first (21.148–149 = 20.176–177; see [[when-they-came-close]]); Walter Leaf thinks the first verse hardly in place after 21.144, where the two have already met (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.148). Achilles' question and his threat are those of Diomedes to Glaucus: “Who are you among men, and from where, that you have dared to come against me? / It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (21.150–151; 6.123, 6.127; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]). Leaf calls the whole dialogue evidently modeled on that of Glaucus and Diomedes (on 21.153; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). “Then the shining son of Pelegon spoke to him in turn” (21.152), in the verse with which “the shining son of Hippolochus” answered (6.144; see [[phaidimos]]), and his first words are Glaucus' own, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (21.153 ≈ 6.145; see [[genee]]). Glaucus went on to the generations of leaves; Asteropaeus answers the question.

{{quote:52ea27a2-1231-5b70-9d5f-431fd1329ad1}}

**Where he comes from.** He has come from Paeonia, “which lies far away”, leading the Paeonians “with their long spears” (21.154–155; see [[paeonia]] and [[paeonians]]). δολιχεγχέας, “with their long spears”, is found only here; in the Catalogue the Paeonians are archers, and Pyraechmes leads them (2.848; see [[pyraechmes]] and [[with-curved-bows]]). “This is now / the eleventh dawn since I came to Ilios” (21.155–156): Lycaon has just told Achilles that this dawn is the twelfth since he came home to Ilios from Lemnos, and Leaf calls the verse an echo of Lycaon's (21.80–81; on 21.156). Leaf reports from the scholia that by a strict reckoning of the days Asteropaeus must already have been at Troy when the Catalogue was made, and that a verse naming him was added after 2.848 in many copies, Πηλεγόνος θʼ υἱὸς περιδέξιος Ἀστεροπαῖος, with the epithet that this book gives him (on 21.154; on 2.848).

**His lineage.** It begins from the river, in the words with which the Catalogue praised the Axius: “But my lineage is from the broad-flowing Axius, / the Axius, who sends the fairest water over the earth” (21.157–158 ≈ 2.849–850; see [[axius]] and [[broad-flowing]]). Leaf thinks 158 a late adaptation of 2.850 (on 21.158). Then the river “begot Pelegon, famed for his spear; and he, they say, / was my father” (21.159–160). Like the genealogies of Glaucus and Aeneas, it runs down from its source, but it is short, two generations in four verses (6.152–211; 20.215–241). It ends, like Aeneas' speech, in the call to fight: “Now let us fight, shining Achilles” (21.160).

**Two spears.** Achilles raises the Pelian ash, but Asteropaeus throws first (see [[the-pelian-ash]]):

{{quote:70e1543f-0017-5662-a2bd-7ebebf5cf75b}}

He throws both spears at once, “for he could use either hand” (ἐπεὶ περιδέξιος ἦεν, 21.163). περιδέξιος is found only here (see [[ambidextrous]]). Leaf takes it to mean ambidextrous, a sense that ἀμφιδέξιος would give more naturally but the hexameter would not admit, and rejects the alternative reported in the scholia, very skillful, since Homer never uses δεξιός of cleverness (on 21.163). The spear that strikes the shield is stopped by the gold, in the verse that told how Aeneas' spear was stopped (21.165 = 20.268; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The other grazes his right forearm and draws the dark blood (see [[dark-blood]]): it is the only wound Achilles takes in the Iliad. The arm, Leaf explains, was raised for the cast, so that the spear touched it and flew on over his body (on 21.166; see the translation's note on 21.166–167). It stuck in the ground “longing to glut itself on flesh” (21.168), like the spears that stood in the ground “longing to glut themselves on flesh” in the battles of Books 11 and 15 (11.574, 15.317; see [[spear]]).

**The spear in the bank.** Achilles throws his “straight-flying ash” in his turn and misses, and the spear sticks halfway into the high bank (21.169–172; see [[straight-flying]]). He draws his sword and leaps at Asteropaeus, who “could not draw Achilles' ash spear / out of the bank with his strong hand” (21.174–175; see [[strong-hand]]). Three times he shakes it, three times he gives up, and the fourth time he means to bend and break “the ash spear of the grandson of Aeacus” (21.176–178; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[grandson-of-aeacus]]). It is the spear that no other Achaean could wield, which Chiron gave to Peleus (16.140–144; see [[the-pelian-ash]]). The two verses of his attempts are found again in the Odyssey, where Telemachus tries to string his father's bow (21.176–177 ≈ *Odyssey* 21.125–126; see the translation's notes on 21.176 and 21.177).

**The death.** “But before that Achilles took his life with his sword at close quarters” (21.179): he struck him in the belly beside the navel, and all his bowels poured out on the ground, “and darkness covered his eyes / as he gasped” (21.180–182; see [[sword]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The verses are those of the death of the Epeian Diores at the hands of Peirous in the first battle (4.525–526; Leaf on 21.180; see [[diores]] and [[peirous]]). Achilles springs on his chest, strips off his armor, “and spoke in triumph” (21.182–183), the verse of Menelaus over Peisander (13.619; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[euchomai]]).

**The boast.** Achilles answers the lineage with his own. “Lie there as you are”, he begins (21.184), as he began over Lycaon, “Lie there now among the fishes” (21.122), and a book earlier over Iphition, whose birthplace was far away by the Gygaean lake (20.389–392; see [[iphition]]). It is hard, he says, for one sprung from a river to strive with the children of Zeus: “You say that you are of the lineage of a broad-flowing river, / but I claim to be of the lineage of great Zeus” (21.186–187). His line goes up through Peleus and Aeacus to Zeus, and as Zeus is mightier than rivers, so is his lineage mightier than a river's; not even Achelous can fight Zeus, nor Oceanus, the source of all waters, who fears his thunderbolt (21.188–199; see [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]], [[aeacus]], [[achelous]], [[oceanus]] and [[thunderbolt]]).

**The eels and the fishes.** Achilles pulls the bronze spear out of the bank, as Asteropaeus could not, and leaves him “lying in the sand, and the dark water lapped over him” (21.200–202):

{{quote:3f818ef2-b618-54a7-9ccf-30b403289f8e}}

The fishes do to him what Achilles promised Lycaon, “some fish, to eat the white fat of Lycaon” (21.127; see [[fish]] and [[eels]]). Leaf takes ἀμφεπένοντο, a word for tending the wounded, as ironical here, and notes that the eels are set apart from the fishes (on 21.203; see the translation's note on 21.203–204).

**His people.** The Paeonians were in rout along the river, “when they saw their best man in the fierce battle / brought down by force at the hands of the son of Peleus and by his sword” (21.207–208; see [[aristos]]), and Achilles killed seven of them, until the river spoke (21.209–213; see [[paeonians]]). He had been the best of them before. Sarpedon chose him with Glaucus for the best of the allies after himself (12.101–104); Hector named him among the allied leaders he urged on (17.217); and when Lycomedes killed Apisaon, who “after Asteropaeus … was the best in the fighting”, he pitied him and charged the Achaeans around Patroclus' body, but could not break through their shields (17.348–355; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]] and [[lycomedes]]). Over Patroclus his charge stopped at a wall of shields; at the river his spear stopped at the gold of the shield the god made.
