summary: The words of buying and selling gather in Lycaon's story: Achilles “sold him into well-built Lemnos”, “the son of Jason paid the price”, and Lycaon “fetched you the worth of a hundred oxen” (21.40–41, 21.58, 21.78–79); before Patroclus died, Achilles says, he took many Trojans alive “and sold” them (21.102). Poseidon remembers that Laomedon threatened to sell him and Apollo “off to islands far away” (21.454).

**The words.** ἐπέρασσε, “sold” (21.40), is the verb of selling across the sea. Walter Leaf notes that it is found in the Iliad only in this book (21.40, 21.58, 21.78, 21.102, 21.454) and in the Odyssey only in the tales of kidnapping and sale in the fourteenth and fifteenth books; it is to be kept apart from περάω, “cross”, whose aorist is ἐπέρησα, though the two are evidently akin through the sense “make to pass over” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.40). ὦνος, “price” (21.41), returns once in the Iliad, of the same sale (23.746). ἦλφον, “I fetched” (21.79), is found only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey one of the suitors would ship the beggar and Theoclymenus to the Sicels, “where they would fetch you a worthy price” (*Odyssey* 20.382–383).

**What a man is worth.** Lycaon puts a price on himself twice: he fetched Achilles “the worth of a hundred oxen” when he was sold, and he was ransomed for three times as much (21.79–80; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[apoina]]). ἑκατόμβοιος is the value of Glaucus' golden armor, “the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine” (6.236; see [[cattle]]). The price that Euneus paid was a silver mixing bowl of Sidonian work, which Achilles sets out as a prize at Patroclus' games (23.740–747; see [[euneus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]), and Leaf takes the hundred oxen for the bowl's value, not for what was handed over (on 21.79). Leaf thinks too that Lycaon names the ransom to show Achilles how much he will be worth if he is sold again (on 21.80).

**Sold or killed.** “Trojans, and many I took alive and sold” (21.102): before Patroclus died, Achilles says, he spared his captives and sold them, but now none shall escape death (21.100–105; see [[slavery-and-freedom]], [[achilles-raids]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]).

**The gods for sale.** Poseidon reminds Apollo how Laomedon, when the year of their service was over, robbed them of their wage and threatened to bind their feet and hands “and to sell us off to islands far away” (21.453–454; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]] and [[laomedon]]). In the next book Priam laments the sons whom Achilles killed or sold “to islands far away” in the same words, περνὰς νήσων ἔπι τηλεδαπάων (22.45; Leaf on 22.45).
