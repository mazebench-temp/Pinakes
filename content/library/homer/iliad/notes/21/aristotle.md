summary: Aristotle's black eagle, the hare-killer, is the smallest of the eagles and the strongest, and Walter Leaf compares it with the eagle of Achilles' leap from the river, “at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds”; a reading “black-boned” for the eagle's epithet was ascribed to Aristotle, Leaf reports, but more probably belongs to Democritus. Aristotle also uses the adjective of the book's title, calling the elephant an animal of the riverside.
---

- **The black eagle.** Achilles springs away from the river “with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter, / which is at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds” (21.252–253). Aristotle, setting out the kinds of eagle, describes one black in color, smallest in size and strongest of them, which lives in mountains and forests and is called the black eagle and the hare-killer; it is swift to strike, fearless and warlike (*History of Animals* 9.32). Walter Leaf compares the passage. He reports that the scholia ascribe to Aristotle the reading μελανόστου, “black-boned”, for μέλανος τοῦ, and blame him for not knowing that epithets should not be drawn from what cannot be seen; but Porphyry's note on 24.315 makes it more probable that the reading was Democritus' (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.252): [[like-an-eagle]] and [[eagle]].
- The adjective παραποτάμιος, “by the river”, of the elephant, “an animal of the riverside, not of the river” (*History of Animals* 9.46): [[title-of-book-21]].
