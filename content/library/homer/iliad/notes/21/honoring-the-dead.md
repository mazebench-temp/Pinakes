---
summary: The book denies the dead their rites. Achilles tells Lycaon, “nor will your mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you” (21.123–124), and throws him to the fishes; he leaves Asteropaeus in the sand for the eels and the fishes (21.200–204); and the river promises Achilles that the Achaeans will not find his bones to gather (21.320–321).
refs: [21.120-127, 21.200-204, 21.316-323]
---

The fishes that “will lick the blood / from your wound” (21.122–123) take the part that dogs and birds play in the proem (1.4–5). Walter Leaf reports two readings of ἀκηδέες: “careless of thy fate”, or, with Monro, “without mourning rites” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.123; see the translation's note on 21.122–123). The river casts the bodies out onto the dry land, and Hephaestus' fire burns the dead on the plain (21.235–238, 21.343–349; see [[death-in-battle]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
