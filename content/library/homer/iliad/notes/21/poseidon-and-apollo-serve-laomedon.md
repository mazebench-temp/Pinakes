**The occasion.** Poseidon tells the story to make Apollo fight. It would be shameful to go back to Olympus without a blow, he says, and Apollo, as the younger, should begin; and does he not remember what they suffered at Ilios, “we two alone of the gods”? (21.436–443; see [[poseidon]], [[apollo]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). The story ends in a reproach: “It is to his people that you now do a favor”, instead of helping to destroy the overbearing Trojans with their children and their wives (21.458–460; see [[charis]] and [[overbearing]]). Apollo answers with contempt for mortals, not with the grievance, and turns away (21.461–469; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]).

**The words of service.** θητεύσαμεν, “served for hire” (21.444), makes the gods θῆτες, men who work for another man's pay. Walter Leaf notes that the verb recurs in Homer only in the *Odyssey*, of the hired laborer the shade of Achilles would rather be than king of the dead, and of the work for a wage that Eurymachus offers the beggar Odysseus, which shows that the word means hired service (*Odyssey* 11.489, 18.357–358; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.444). μισθός, “wage”, comes four times in thirteen verses: they served “for a fixed wage” (μισθῷ ἔπι ῥητῷ, 21.445), and the term of the wage came, and Laomedon robbed them of the whole wage, which he had promised and did not pay (21.450–457; see [[misthos]], [[laomedon]] and the translation's row on μισθός). ὃ δὲ σημαίνων ἐπέτελλεν, “he was our master and gave us our orders” (21.445): σημαίνω is the word of a master's commands (Leaf on 21.445; see [[semaino]]). πὰρ Διὸς ἐλθόντες, “we came from Zeus” (21.444): the poem gives no reason, and Leaf notes that the words might be thought to imply a punishment, and that later mythologists said it was to show Laomedon's insolence in full (on 21.442; see [[zeus]] and [[hybris]]). The seasons that brought the term of the wage are πολυγηθέες, “joyful”, a general epithet, Leaf thinks, not the joy of their release (on 21.450).

**The two versions.** Here Poseidon builds the wall alone, and Apollo herds the cattle “on the slopes of wooded, many-folded Ida” (21.446–449; see [[the-walls-of-troy]], [[ida]] and [[cattle]]); in Book 7 the two of them built the wall together (7.452–453), and Leaf notes that the discrepancy was one of the grounds on which Aristarchus rejected 7.443–464 (on 21.446; see the translation's note on 7.443–464). Of the threats, the binding of “our feet, and our hands above” (21.453), Leaf reads σύν, “together”, against the σοί of the printed vulgate, which would single Apollo out for a punishment that both were to share (on 21.453).
