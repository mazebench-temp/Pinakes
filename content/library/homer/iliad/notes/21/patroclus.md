---
summary: Patroclus is dead, and what Achilles does in the book is done for him. Achilles takes twelve young men alive from the river “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (21.27–28); he tells Lycaon that before Patroclus met his fated day he spared Trojans and sold them, but now he spares none, for “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.100–107); and the Trojans will perish until all of them pay “for the slaughter of Patroclus” (21.133–134).
# Unnamed: "your companion, gentle and strong", in Lycaon's plea (21.96).
refs: [21.96]
---

**The penalty.** ποινὴν Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο θανόντος, “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (21.28; see [[poine]], [[human-sacrifice]] and [[son-of-menoetius]]). Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο fills the middle of the verse again when Achilles bids the kings gather the bones of Patroclus, son of Menoetius, from the pyre (23.239), and the whole verse-end returns in the Odyssey, where the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles that his bones lie in the golden jar mixed with those of “Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (*Odyssey* 24.76–77).

**Gentle and strong.** Lycaon asks Achilles not to kill him, since he is not of the same womb as Hector, “who killed your companion, gentle and strong” (21.95–96; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[hetairos]]). ἑταῖρον … ἐνηέα τε κρατερόν τε are the words with which Zeus, speaking to his own heart, addressed Hector as Hector put on Achilles' armor: “and you have killed his companion, gentle and strong” (17.204). ἐνηής, “gentle”, is Menelaus' word for him over the body, “now let each remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus” (17.670; see [[meilichos]]), and the Achaeans will weep over the bones of their gentle companion (23.252). Walter Leaf, who thinks Bayfield right to condemn 21.94–96, finds the praise out of place in an enemy's mouth (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.94; see the translation's note on 21.94–96).

**Before and after.** Achilles answers that “before Patroclus met his fated day” it was dearer to his heart to spare Trojans, and many he took alive and sold, but now no one shall escape death whom a god throws into his hands (21.100–105; see [[achilles]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Then: “No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this? / Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). Over the body in the river he tells the Trojans that they will perish “until all of you / pay for the slaughter of Patroclus and the ruin of the Achaeans, / whom you killed beside the swift ships while I was away” (21.133–135). The ruin he names is the one that fell on the Achaeans while he sat apart, which Patroclus went out to stop (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
