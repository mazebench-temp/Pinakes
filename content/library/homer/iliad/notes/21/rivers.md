---
summary: Book 21 is the book of the river. Achilles mocks the Trojans for the bulls and live horses they give their river, kills a grandson of the river Axius with the boast that Zeus is mightier than any river, and is nearly drowned by the Scamander, a god who speaks, fights, calls on his brother the Simoeis and, burned by Hephaestus, swears an oath. Two of its similes are of everyday water, a channel led through a garden and a torrent in winter.

**Bulls and horses.** Achilles throws Lycaon's body into the river and tells the Trojans that it will not save them:

{{quote:bf31cccd-1534-5355-92ee-9c41a2e77d04}}

The sacrifices are a standing custom: δηθά with the presents ἱερεύετε, καθίετε, “you have long sacrificed … and thrown” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.131). A bull is a river's victim elsewhere in the Iliad too, when Nestor's Pylians offer “a bull to Alpheus, and a bull to Poseidon” (11.728; see [[alpheus]] and [[bulls]]), and Leaf connects the offering with the common figure of a river as a bull or a bull-headed man (see [[like-a-bull]]). Horses thrown alive into a river have no parallel in Homer; Leaf suggests that Achilles names the custom with contempt, as a barbarous one, and compares the white horses that the Persian Magi offered to the Strymon (on 21.131). Herodotus says that the Magi slaughtered them, seeking good omens, and so worked their charms on the river (σφάζοντες ἵππους λευκούς, *Histories* 7.113.2–7.114.1; see [[herodotus]]): the horses were killed, not thrown in alive (see [[horses-for-the-rivers]]). The Scamander has a priest among the Trojans, Dolopion, “honored by the people like a god” (5.77–78; see [[dolopion]]), and the Spercheius a precinct and an altar at its springs, where Peleus vowed Achilles' hair, a hecatomb and fifty rams for his safe return (23.144–151; see [[spercheius]]). Aristophanes athetized the six verses of Achilles' taunt, as added by readers who did not see why the river was angry (see the translation's note on 21.130–135).

**The lineage of rivers.** Asteropaeus is the grandson of a river: the Axius “of the broad streams” lay with Periboea and begot his father Pelegon, and he names his lineage from “the Axius, who sends the fairest water over the earth” (21.141–143, 21.157–159; see [[axius]] and [[asteropaeus]]). Achilles answers him over his body:

{{quote:03336f39-3200-50d1-8bf6-b3c3ca11f525}}

ἁλιμυρήεντες, “that murmur to the sea” (21.190), recurs in Homer only at *Odyssey* 5.460. Not even lord Achelous can match Zeus, nor deep-flowing Oceanus, from whom all rivers and all the sea flow (21.194–197; see [[achelous]], [[oceanus]] and [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]).

**A river at war.** The Scamander shows what a river can do when it fights. He speaks in the likeness of a man from a deep eddy (21.213), complains that his streams are choked with the dead and cannot reach the sea (21.218–220), and calls Achilles to account; he rises with his wave, casts out the dead and hides the living (21.234–239), and floods the plain (21.300–302). As a god he has a brother, the Simoeis, whom he calls on to raise a great wave “with water from your springs” (21.308–313; see [[simoeis]]); and as a god he can be beaten and swear (21.356–376; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). Twice he is “the river fallen from Zeus” (21.268, 21.326; see [[fallen-from-zeus]]), as the Spercheius is (16.174).

**Everyday water.** Two similes set common water beside the god. As the wave overtakes Achilles, it is like the water that a man leads from a spring of dark water along a channel among his plants and gardens, which runs down murmuring and “outruns even the man who leads it” (21.257–262; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]); and Achilles fears to die like a swineherd boy “whom a torrent sweeps away as he crosses it in winter” (21.282–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]]). The river is a place of rest at the end of the book, when Agenor thinks of fleeing to the slopes of Ida “and then in the evening, when I have bathed in the river, / and let the sweat dry from me”, going back to the city (21.558–561).
