---
summary: Achilles speaks of his death twice in the book. To Lycaon he says that “death and mighty fate” hang over him too, and that a morning or an evening or a midday will come when someone kills him with a spear or an arrow (21.110–113); trapped in the river he says that his mother told him he would perish under the wall of the Trojans by the swift arrows of Apollo (21.277–278). Poseidon assures him that it is not his fate to be brought down by a river (21.291).
refs: [21.108-113, 21.276-278, 21.291]

**The hour and the weapon.** He tells Lycaon nothing of the hour, a morning or an evening or a midday (21.111). δείλη, “evening”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 21.111); the scholia put a stop after ἔσσεται, “there will come”, and made the three times a parenthesis, which, Walter Leaf says, spoils the sentence (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.111). The weapon too is left open, “either with a spear or with an arrow from the string” (21.113). θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, “death and mighty fate” (21.110), are the words with which the dying Patroclus told Hector that his end stood close beside him (16.853), and Thetis will say the same to her son in the last book (24.132; see [[mighty-fate]], [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

**His mother's word.** In the river he blames his mother, who “told me that under the wall of the armored Trojans / I would perish by the swift arrows of Apollo” (21.277–278; see [[thetis]] and [[achilles]]). No earlier book reports this prophecy. In the first book Thetis lamented that his portion of life was brief (1.416–418); in the embassy he told of the two fates she had told him (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]); after Patroclus' death she told him that his own fate was ready straight after Hector's (18.96); and his horse Xanthus said that he was fated “to be brought down by force by a god and a man” (19.417; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]). Now the god is Apollo and the place is the wall; the dying Hector will name the man too, Paris, and the place, the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[apollo]], [[paris]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Leaf compares Hector's prophecy, and finds the slight discrepancy from 21.113, where the weapon may be a spear, hardly worth notice (on 21.278).

**Not by a river.** What he fears in the river is a wretched death by drowning, “like a swineherd boy / whom a torrent sweeps away as he crosses it in winter” (21.281–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]]). Poseidon, coming with Athena, answers that fear: “so it is not your fate to be brought down by a river” (21.291; see [[poseidon]]). The river threatens him with a death without a grave: he will wrap him in sand and silt, so that the Achaeans will not know how to gather his bones, and he will have no need of a heaped-up tomb (21.318–323; see [[scamander]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). The Odyssey tells what the Achaeans did: they gathered his bones at dawn into a golden jar that his mother gave, mixed with the bones of Patroclus, and heaped a great mound over them on a headland by the Hellespont (*Odyssey* 24.72–84).

**Mortal.** At the end of the book Agenor, resolving to stand against him, tells himself that Achilles' flesh too can be pierced by the sharp bronze, “and there is one life in him, and men say that he / is mortal” (21.568–570; see [[agenor]]). Leaf notes that the invulnerability of Achilles is a purely post-Homeric legend (on 21.568).
