---
summary: In the simile of the fire on the plain, Hephaestus dries the flooded plain as quickly as the North Wind of late summer dries a newly watered orchard, to the joy of the man who tends it (21.345–349).
passages:
  - 21.342-349 | The fire on the plain
---

“As when the North Wind of late summer a newly watered orchard / quickly dries, and whoever tends it rejoices” (21.346–347; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]). The North Wind is the drying wind, “born of the clear sky” (15.171, 19.358), and ὀπωρινός, “of late summer”, sets it in the season of ripening fruit. The *Odyssey* has ὀπωρινὸς Βορέης again, carrying thistles over a plain as the winds toss Odysseus' raft (*Odyssey* 5.328–330). Hera has promised the West and the South Wind to drive the fire against the Trojans (21.334–337; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]); the North Wind comes into the episode only in the simile, as the likeness of Hephaestus' fire (see [[hephaestus]] and [[fire]]). In Book 23 it is the North Wind and the West Wind, called by Achilles' prayer, that make the pyre of Patroclus burn (23.192–230).
