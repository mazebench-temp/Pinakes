summary: The lotus grows in plenty along the banks of the Xanthus with the rushes and the galingale, and it burns when Hephaestus sets the river on fire (21.351–352).

{{quote:ac3d0b83-6290-5fac-861a-ed50a5daeb5e}}

The lotus of the Iliad is a plant of damp ground: Achilles' idle horses munch it with marsh-grown parsley (2.776), and the earth sends it up “dewy” under Zeus and Hera on Ida (14.348). Here it grows where the ground is wettest, on the river's banks, with the rushes and the galingale (see [[rushes]] and [[galingale]]); the *Odyssey* too names lotus and galingale together, on Menelaus' plain (*Odyssey* 4.603). Hephaestus' fire burns the trees of the bank, then these low plants, then the fish in the water (21.350–355; see [[fire]], [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).
