---
summary: The river speaks to Achilles “in the likeness of a man” (21.213); Poseidon and Athena come to him “in form like men” and take his hand (21.284–298); and Apollo, “in the likeness of Agenor himself in every way,” draws him away from the city (21.600–605).
---

**Poseidon and Athena.** The scene has the parts of the type: arrival, “very swiftly Poseidon and Athena / came and stood near him, in form like men” (21.284–285); a gesture, “and taking his hand in their hands they pledged their faith with words” (21.286); a speech in which Poseidon names himself and his companion (21.288–297; [[pallas]]); and a departure, “So saying, the two went away among the immortals” (21.298). The verse that names them was athetized because a god in disguise should not name himself; Walter Leaf answers that Achilles must know the help is divine (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.290).

**The river and Apollo.** The river god takes a man's likeness to plead (21.213), and Apollo takes Agenor's to deceive (21.600). Before that Apollo had stood beside Agenor unseen, “leaning against the oak; and he was hidden in thick mist” (21.549; [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The deceived Achilles learns the truth only at the start of the next book (22.7–20). See [[truth-and-deception]], [[scamander]], [[poseidon]] and [[apollo]].
