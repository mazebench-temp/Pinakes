summary: Lycaon says that “deadly fate” has put him in Achilles' hands again (21.83); Achilles answers that “death and mighty fate” hang over him too (21.110), and that the Trojans will all “perish by an evil fate” (21.133). In the river “it was fated” that he die a wretched death (21.281); Apollo guards the wall “lest the Danaans sack it against fate that day” (21.517); and Agenor tells Achilles, “you will meet your fate here” (21.588).
grc: [εἵμαρτο]
---

Apollo's fear is the one Zeus voiced at the start of Book 20, that Achilles “may even sack the wall against fate” (20.30): the poem lets the gods fear what it will not let happen (see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). μοῖρα κραταιή, “mighty fate”, ends the verse of death that closed a killing in Book 20 (20.477; see [[mighty-fate]]). Walter Leaf notes that Agenor's future, πότμον ἐφέψεις, “you will meet your fate”, is with *Odyssey* 24.471 the only future of the phrase (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.588). Poseidon tells Achilles that “it is not your fate” to die in the river, and the dove that flies from the hawk had no fate to be caught (21.291, 21.495; see [[aisa]]).
