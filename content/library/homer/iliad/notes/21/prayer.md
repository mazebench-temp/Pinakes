summary: No one prays in the book. The nearest thing to a prayer is Achilles' cry in the river: he “groaned aloud, looking up into the wide sky”, and tells Zeus that “no one of the gods has undertaken to save me in my misery / from the river” (21.272–274); Poseidon and Athena come at once, “with the consent of Zeus” (21.284–290).
# The other forms of εὔχομαι in the book are claims and boasts (euchomai keeps them): Achilles speaks "in triumph" over
# Asteropaeus and "claims" the lineage of Zeus (21.183, 21.187); the two gods grant him "a triumph" (21.297); Athena
# "claims" to be stronger than Ares (21.411); Artemis taunts Apollo with the "triumph" he gave away and with his old
# "boasting" (21.473, 21.476); Hermes tells Leto to "boast" that she beat him (21.501).

The verse that opens the cry opened Menelaus' reproach to Zeus when his sword broke on Paris' helmet, with only the name changed (3.364 = 21.272), and Achilles' words too are a complaint, not a request: he blames his mother, who “bewitched me with lies” (21.276), and wishes that Hector had killed him. He asks for nothing in so many words, and he is answered at once, as Athena heard Menelaus' wish as a prayer in Book 17 (17.561–568). Walter Leaf compares “After that I could suffer anything” (21.274) with Ajax's prayer to die in the light (17.647; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.274).

The Trojans have long sacrificed bulls to the river and thrown live horses into its eddies, and Achilles tells them that it will not protect them (21.130–132; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[rivers]]). See [[euchomai]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]].
