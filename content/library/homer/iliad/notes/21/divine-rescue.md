---
summary: Apollo saves Agenor, the man he has set against Achilles. He stands beside him hidden in mist to ward off death, and when Achilles rushes at him, snatches him away in thick mist and sends him out of the war; then, in Agenor's likeness, he leads Achilles away from the city (21.544–605). Earlier in the book Achilles, overwhelmed by the river, complains that no god will save him, and Poseidon and Athena come to him (21.273–297).

**“Had not.”** The narrator's “would have … had not” is here said of the city: “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy, / had not Phoebus Apollo set brilliant Agenor on” (21.544–545), as it was said when Patroclus reached the wall (16.698–700; see [[would-have-had-not]]). The god does not save a man from danger; he puts a man in the way to save the city, and stands by him, hidden in mist, “to ward off the heavy hands of Death” (21.547–549; see [[agenor]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).

**The rescue.** Agenor throws and hits Achilles' greave, and Achilles rushes at him:

{{quote:01f47685-93e1-586e-8a53-afc1ccdd189b}}

ἐξήρπαξε, “snatched him away”, is found in Homer only in the three rescues of this type: Aphrodite's of Paris and Apollo's of Hector and of Agenor (3.380, 20.443, 21.597). κάλυψε δʼ ἄρʼ ἠέρι πολλῇ, “and hid him in thick mist”, ends the verse as in the other two (3.381, 20.444; see [[achlys]]). There is no place of safety at the end, no bedroom or temple (3.382, 5.445–448). Apollo simply sends him out of the war, ἡσύχιον, “in peace”, an adjective found in Homer only here (21.598).

**The attacker.** In Book 20 Achilles was left with an empty field: he saw his spear on the ground and no man when Poseidon took Aeneas away, and he struck three times at the mist that hid Hector (20.341–352, 20.445–448; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). Here Apollo gives him a man to chase. The god who has hidden Agenor takes his shape and runs ahead of Achilles, always a little out of reach, until the Trojans are safe inside the walls (21.599–611; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). In the next book Apollo tells him that he will not kill him, since he is not fated to die, and Achilles complains that the god has robbed him of great glory and saved the Trojans easily (22.13, 22.18–19). “Apollo no longer allowed him to win glory” (21.596): the rescue is told as a theft of κῦδος (see [[kydos]]).

**Achilles' own rescue.** Earlier in the book the roles are turned around. Achilles, pressed by the river, cries out to Zeus: “how no one of the gods has undertaken to save me in my misery / from the river!” (21.273–274). Poseidon and Athena come at once, in the form of men, and promise that it is not his fate to be brought down by a river (21.284–297; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]), and Hera sends Hephaestus against the river with fire (21.328–382; see [[achilles-and-the-river]] and [[hephaestus]]). Achilles, from whom the gods snatch his enemies, is himself saved by gods, though not snatched away.
