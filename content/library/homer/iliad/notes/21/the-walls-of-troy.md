summary: Poseidon reminds Apollo that he built the wall of Troy for Laomedon. When Achilles drives the Trojans back to the city, Apollo goes in, mindful of the wall; Priam has the gates opened, and the army flees “straight for the city and the high wall”. Agenor, waiting outside, thinks of running away from the wall, and the rest crowd inside and dare not wait for one another “outside the city / and the wall” (21.446–611).

**The builder.** In the battle of the gods Poseidon reminds Apollo of the year they served Laomedon for a wage: “I built a wall around the city for the Trojans, / wide and very beautiful, so that the city might be unbreakable” (21.446–447), while Apollo herded his cattle on Ida (21.448–449; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]], [[laomedon]] and [[ida]]). ἄρρηκτος, “unbreakable”, is the wall's purpose. In Book 7 Poseidon claimed the building for both gods (7.452–453), and Walter Leaf notes that the discrepancy was one of Aristarchus' grounds for athetizing 7.443–464 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.446; see the translation's note on 21.441–457). Achilles, in the river, remembers that his mother foretold his death “under the wall of the armored Trojans” by Apollo's arrows (21.277–278; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]), and Poseidon and Athena promise him that he will pen the army “within the glorious walls of Ilios” (21.295; see [[troy]]).

**Apollo and the wall.** When the gods' battle is over, the builder's partner goes in to guard it:

{{quote:0eed2a5b-5c74-5a61-9d93-7202787eab68}}

The fear is the fear that sent the gods into the war, when Zeus said that Achilles might “sack the wall against fate” (20.30; see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]). Apollo stood on the wall's tower once before, when Patroclus three times set foot on its angle (16.698–711).

**The gates opened.** Priam has the gatekeepers hold the gates open until the army is inside the wall and then shut the closely fitted doors, “for I fear that this baneful man may leap inside the wall” (21.526–536; see [[priam]]).

{{quote:0863e9da-dc2c-526b-93ec-f9c027133e2d}}

φάος, “a light”, is safety for the fugitives, as when Ajax “brought light to his companions” and Achilles “was no light at all to Patroclus” (6.6, 18.102; Leaf on 21.538; see the translation's note on 21.538). Leaf reports an ancient note that Zenodotus athetized the verses, thinking it absurd that a city open to the sky should be lit through a gate; he doubts that this silly reason was really Zenodotus', and finds it more in the style of Zoilus (on 21.538; see [[zenodotus]]). καρχαλέοι, “parched”, is found only here before Apollonius (Leaf on 21.541).

**Outside and inside.** Agenor, waiting before the gates, thinks of running on his feet “away from the wall” to Ida, fears that Achilles would see him going “away from the city toward the plain”, and decides to meet him “in front of the city” (21.557–567; see [[agenor]] and [[the-ilean-plain]]). When Apollo has drawn Achilles off, the Trojans pour in, and “Nor did they dare any longer to wait for one another outside the city / and the wall” (21.608–609; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). In the next book they rest on the battlements, and Hector alone stays outside the wall (22.1–6; see [[hector]]).
