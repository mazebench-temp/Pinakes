summary: With Athena, both in the form of men, Poseidon comes to Achilles in the river, promises him with the consent of Zeus that he will not be overcome by a river, and bids him pen the Trojans in the city and kill Hector. In the battle of the gods he challenges Apollo to begin, reminding him of the year they served Laomedon for an unpaid wage; Apollo will not fight his father's brother, and Artemis taunts him for yielding Poseidon the victory.

**Help in the river.** Poseidon and Athena answer Achilles' cry from the river, “in form like men”, take his hand and pledge their faith with words (21.284–286; see [[athena]] and [[achilles]]). “And among them Poseidon the earth-shaker began to speak” (21.287; see [[earth-shaker]]): τοῖσι, “among them”, comes from a familiar verse, Walter Leaf notes, though Poseidon speaks to Achilles alone (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.287). He tells him not to fear, for two gods are his helpers, “with the consent of Zeus” (21.290; see [[zeus]]): it is not his fate to be brought down by a river, which will soon subside, and he is not to stop fighting until he has penned the Trojan army in the city and taken Hector's life (21.288–297). The promise ends, “we grant that you win a triumph” (21.297; see [[euchomai]]). In Book 20 Poseidon told Aeneas to keep away from Achilles until Achilles had met his death (20.335–339; see [[aeneas]]); here he promises Achilles the death of Hector.

The last verse is the one in which Odysseus claimed the right to counsel Achilles (21.440 = 19.219), and the precedence of Zeus over Poseidon was put in the same terms: “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355; Leaf on 21.440). Leaf finds this bellicose Poseidon hardly the god who advised his party in Book 20 to sit apart and leave the war to men (on 21.436; 20.133–143). He goes on to remind Apollo of the year the two of them served Laomedon for a fixed wage, he building the wall and Apollo herding the cattle on Ida, and of the king who cheated them and threatened to sell them and lop off their ears (21.441–457; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]] and [[laomedon]]). “I built a wall around the city for the Trojans” (21.446; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]): in Book 7 he called it the wall that he and Apollo had built together (7.452–453), and Leaf notes that the discrepancy was one of Aristarchus' grounds for rejecting that passage (on 21.446). His conclusion is a reproach: Apollo now does a favor to Laomedon's people, instead of helping to destroy the overbearing Trojans with their children and wives (21.458–460).

Apollo answers him as “Shaker of the earth” (21.462; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]]): he would not be sound of mind if he fought for mortals, and he turns away, ashamed “to come to blows with his father's brother” (21.462–469; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). Artemis taunts her brother for yielding Poseidon “the whole victory”, and for his old boast that he would fight “face to face against Poseidon” (21.472–477; see [[artemis]]).
