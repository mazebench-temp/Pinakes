---
summary: Achilles, seeing Lycaon back from Lemnos, and Agenor, waiting for Achilles under the walls, each speak “deeply troubled … to his own proud heart” (21.53, 21.552).
---

The whole verse, ὀχθήσας δʼ ἄρα εἶπε πρὸς ὃν μεγαλήτορα θυμόν, introduces the soliloquies of the Iliad: Odysseus left alone (11.403), Menelaus over Patroclus (17.90), Achilles at the sight of the rout (18.5), Achilles when Aeneas vanishes (20.343), and Hector before the gates (22.98). Book 21 has it twice. Achilles' speech is wonder that turns to a threat: the dead seem to rise again, so let Lycaon taste his spear and see whether the earth will hold him (21.54–63; [[o-popoi]], [[life-giving-earth]]). Agenor's is a choice between flight and a stand, introduced by the verse of Odysseus' and built like his speech (21.552 = 11.403; 21.553–570; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.552; [[pondering-two-ways]], [[decision-scene]]).
