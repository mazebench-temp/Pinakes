summary: The book opens at “the ford of the fair-flowing river” (21.1), and Achilles tells the dead Lycaon that the river, “fair-flowing and silver-eddying though it is,” will not protect the Trojans (21.130–131); the river's own water is “fair streams” seven times.
grc: [ἐΰρροος, "καλὰ ῥέεθρα"]
en: [fair streams]
---

**The ford.** “But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river, / the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (21.1–2) is the couplet with which the Trojans carried the fallen Hector to the river (14.433–434), and with which Priam will cross it on his way home (24.692–693). Walter Leaf notes that the ford is named only in this formal verse (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.4; see the translation's note on 21.1–2 and [[eddying]]).

**Achilles' taunt.** ἐΰρροος, the epithet the Scamander had in Book 7 (7.329), comes back in Achilles' boast over Lycaon's body, which he has flung into the river: “Not even the river, fair-flowing and silver-eddying though it is, / will protect you, to whom you have long sacrificed many bulls” (21.130–131; [[silver-eddying]], [[rivers]]).

**Fair streams.** The book's own phrase for the river's water is καλὰ ῥέεθρα, “fair streams,” seven times, four of them at the end of the verse. In them the river saves the living Trojans (21.238), the fallen elm holds them back (21.244), the plants by them burn and the fishes tumble in them (21.352, 21.354), and they bubble, blaze and boil under Hephaestus' fire, until the wave runs back down along them (21.361, 21.365, 21.382). The Odyssey uses the phrase once, of the river Enipeus (*Odyssey* 11.240). See [[scamander]] and [[hephaestus]].
