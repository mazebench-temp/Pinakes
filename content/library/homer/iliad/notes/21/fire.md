---
summary: Book 21 sets fire against water. It opens with a simile of locusts driven into a river by fire, and at its center Hera sets Hephaestus against the river, whose fire burns the dead on the plain, sets the trees and the water ablaze and makes the river boil, until the river swears never to save the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire”.
passages:
  - 21.12-16 | The locusts and the fire
  - 21.342-349 | The fire on the plain
  - 21.356-365 | The river burns
---

**The locusts.** The book's first simile is a fire. When Achilles drives half the Trojans into the river, they are like locusts that flutter up “under the rush of fire” and flee to a river, while “the tireless fire burns, / springing up suddenly” and they cower in the water (21.12–16; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]] and [[tireless]]). ῥιπῆς πυρός, “the rush of fire” (21.12), is made like the rush of the North Wind, ῥιπῆς … Βορέαο (15.171), and ὄρμενον ἐξαίφνης, “springing up suddenly” (21.14), is said of the fire that falls on a city in a simile of Book 17 (17.738; see [[the-burning-city]]). The simile is the battle by the river in small: the fire behind, the water ahead.

**Hera's orders.** When the river is about to overwhelm Achilles, Hera calls on her son (21.328–341; see [[hera]] and [[hephaestus]]). He is to let his great flame shine forth; she will raise the West Wind and the brightening South Wind from the sea, a hard storm carrying “the evil blaze” to burn away the heads and the armor of the Trojans (21.333–337; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]); he is to burn the trees along the banks of Xanthus “and set the river himself on fire” (21.338), and not to let words or threats turn him back, until she calls out: “then hold back the tireless fire” (21.341). φλέγμα κακόν, “the evil blaze” (21.337), occurs only here in Homer. Walter Leaf notes that Hera takes no steps to fulfil her promise of the winds (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.335), and he compares ἐν δʼ αὐτὸν ἵει πυρί, “set the river himself on fire”, with the setting of Patroclus on his pyre (on 21.338; 23.45).

**The plain and the river.** “So she spoke, and Hephaestus made ready a wondrous fire” (21.342; see [[wondrous]]). It burns first on the plain, among the dead whom the river had cast out of his streams (21.235–238, 21.343–344), and dries the plain as the North Wind dries a newly watered orchard (21.345–349; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]). Then the god turns on the river “the flame, gleaming all over” (21.349), φλόγα παμφανόωσαν, the flame that Athena kindled from the head of Achilles (18.206; see [[gleaming-all-over]]). The elms, willows and tamarisks burn, and the lotus, the rushes and the galingale on the banks (21.350–352; see [[elm]], [[willow]], [[tamarisk]], [[lotus]], [[rushes]] and [[galingale]]); the eels and the fishes tumble in the eddies (21.353–355; see [[eels]] and [[fish]]); “And the force of the river was burning” (21.356).

The river speaks “burning with fire” (21.361), and his streams boil like a cauldron that melts a hog's fat on a great fire (21.362–365; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]] and [[cauldron]]). Fire against water was the pairing of these two gods when the gods went to war (20.73–74), and Leaf calls “the symbolic allusion to the enmity of fire and water” obvious (on 21.332; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

**The burning of Troy.** To end his torment the river swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire, / burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans are burning it” (21.373–376). The words are those of the oath that Hera and Athena swore, as Hera told Poseidon in Book 20 (20.313–317; see [[oath]] and [[ravening]]). The burning that the Iliad will not tell is sworn to by a god who is himself on fire (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Hera calls her son off, and “Hephaestus quenched the wondrous fire” (21.381).

**Smoke.** Later in the book Achilles brings toil and sorrow on the Trojans as smoke goes up from a burning city “and the wrath of the gods has sent it up” (21.522–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]).
