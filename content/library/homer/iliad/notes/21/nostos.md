summary: Lycaon has had his homecoming. Ransomed from Lemnos, he “escaped and came to his father's house”, and “For eleven days he delighted his heart with his friends, / after he came from Lemnos” (21.44–46), until a god threw him into Achilles' hands. Agenor plans to slip away to Ida and come back in the evening, “I might go back to Ilios” (21.561), and Apollo gives him a safe return: he “sent him away to go out of the war in peace” (21.598).

The same verb sends Lycaon the other way. Achilles was “to / send him on his way to the house of Hades against his will” (21.47–48), and Walter Leaf counts the line among the few in which νέεσθαι has lost the Homeric sense of a happy return, the sense of its cognate νόστος (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.48). Achilles mocks the first homecoming: the dead Trojans will rise up again, and the spear will show “whether he will come back from there just the same” (21.62), from the world below as from Lemnos (21.54–63; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Agenor weighs his escape and gives it up, fearing that Achilles will see him go and catch him (21.556–566; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).
