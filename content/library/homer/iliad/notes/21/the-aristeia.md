summary: Achilles' aristeia goes on through the book and takes in the parts of the pattern that Book 20 lacked. He is wounded, when Asteropaeus grazes his forearm (21.166–167), and he cries to Zeus and is helped, when Poseidon and Athena come to him in the river and Athena puts great strength in him (21.272–304). The rout fills the river with the dead, the river god himself fights him, and at the end a god takes his last opponent from him, as Aeneas and Hector were taken (21.1–33, 21.211–327, 21.595–611).
# The rout at the ford and in the river (21.1-33), the wound (21.166-167), the cry and the gods' help (21.272-304),
# and the rout to the gates (21.520-543).
refs: [21.1-33, 21.166-167, 21.272-304, 21.520-543]
---

**The wound.** In the pattern the hero is hit, often by an archer: Diomedes by Pandarus, Agamemnon by Coon. Achilles is hit once in the poem, by Asteropaeus, who throws two spears at once: one is held by the gold of the shield, and the other grazes the forearm of his right arm, “and the dark blood spurted” (21.166–167; see [[asteropaeus]]). It does not stop him; he kills Asteropaeus with the sword (21.179; see [[achilles]]).

**Prayer and help.** Diomedes, wounded, prayed to Athena, and she made his limbs light (5.114–122). Achilles, overwhelmed by the river, cries to Zeus that no god has undertaken to save him (21.272–283; see [[prayer]]), and Poseidon and Athena come at once and promise that the river will not bring him down; “for Athena had put great strength in him” (21.284–304; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]).

**Against the gods.** At the height of his success the hero goes past the limit set for men. Achilles fights the river god himself, who calls him a man “bent on being the equal of the gods” (21.315), as Apollo warned Diomedes not to think himself equal to the gods (5.440–442), and only Hephaestus' fire saves him (21.233–382; see [[achilles-and-the-river]] and [[scamander]]).

**The rout.** At the ford he cuts the Trojans in two and kills them in the river with the sword until his hands are weary (21.1–26), kills seven of the Paeonians (21.205–210), and at the end drives the Trojans to the gates, “and a mighty frenzy / held his heart unceasingly, and he was eager to win glory” (21.520–543; see [[kydos]]). Similes mark his progress: the fire that drives locusts into the water, the dolphin among the fishes, the eagle's swoop as he flees the river, the water that outruns the gardener, and smoke rising from a burning city (21.12–16, 21.22–26, 21.251–256, 21.257–264, 21.522–525; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]], [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]], [[like-an-eagle]], [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]] and [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]).

**The opponent taken.** As Poseidon took Aeneas and Apollo took Hector from him in the last book, Apollo snatches Agenor away in mist and, in Agenor's likeness, draws Achilles away from the city (21.595–611; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The aristeia ends with the death of Hector in Book 22 (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]).
