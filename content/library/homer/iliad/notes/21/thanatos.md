summary: Achilles answers Lycaon with death as the common lot: “No, friend, you too must die” and “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107), and “over me too hang death and mighty fate” (21.110). In the river he fears “a wretched death” (21.281); Apollo stands by Agenor to ward off “the heavy hands of Death” (21.548), and Agenor reminds himself that Achilles too “is mortal” (21.569–570).
grc: [κάτθανε, θανάτῳ, ἔθανʼ]
# θανάτοιο at 21.548 is Death in person, “the heavy hands of Death”: death's Book 21 note claims the phrase.
except: [21.548]
---

Lycaon wished above all “to escape evil death and black doom” (21.66), and Achilles tells him that no Trojan whom a god throws into his hands will escape death (21.103–105). At 21.548 all the manuscripts have χεῖρας, “hands”, and Walter Leaf accepts the bold personification of Death, which hardly goes beyond Sleep, “the brother of Death” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.548; 14.231; see the translation's note on 21.548); the line goes to [[death]], as Death in person does in Book 16. The book ends with the Trojans crowding into the city, too frightened to wait outside and learn “who had escaped / and who had died in the war” (21.609–610; see [[patroclus-died-too]], [[death-in-battle]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).
