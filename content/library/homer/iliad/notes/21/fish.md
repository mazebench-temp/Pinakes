summary: Book 21 is full of fish. The Trojans cower before Achilles like fishes before a dolphin; he throws Lycaon's body into the river to be eaten by the fishes and leaves Asteropaeus to the eels and the fishes; and when Hephaestus burns the river, the eels and the fishes tumble in distress in its eddies.
# ἰχθύες (21.22, 21.203, 21.353) and ἰχθύς (21.127), first met in this book; ἰχθύσιν (21.122) is claimed in the Book 19 note.
grc: [ἰχθύες, ἰχθύς]
---

**The dolphin.** In the second simile of the book, the Trojans under the banks are the fishes that flee before a huge-bellied dolphin into the recesses of a harbor, “for he devours greedily whatever he catches” (21.22–26; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]). The poet counts the dolphin among the fishes, ἰχθύες ἄλλοι, “the other fishes”, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.22).

**Lycaon.** The likeness becomes fact. Achilles throws the body of Lycaon into the river and boasts over it (21.120–127; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]):

{{quote:058cf532-fdc5-525f-b8e3-b8bb364e2617}}

There will be no mother to mourn him on a bier, only the river to carry him out to sea and the fish to feed on him. Leaf takes the picture to be of the body floating on the surface after a time, and the fish darting up from beneath the dark ripple to eat it; he reports a variant, ὑπαλύξει, which Philetas and Callistratus explained as the fish's escaping the cold by eating the fat, an interpretation he calls incredible (on 21.126; see the translation's note on 21.126–127). The fish of the Iliad feed on the dead, as they were given the boar of Agamemnon's oath, thrown into the sea (19.266–268).

**Asteropaeus.** Achilles leaves Asteropaeus lying on the sand with the dark water lapping over him, “And the eels and the fishes busied themselves about him, / tearing at the fat about his kidneys and nibbling it away” (21.203–204; see [[eels]] and [[asteropaeus]]).

**In the fire.** When Hephaestus turns his flame on the river, “the eels and the fishes in the eddies were in distress, / which tumbled this way and that in the fair streams, / distressed by the breath of Hephaestus of many wiles” (21.353–355; see [[hephaestus]], [[fire]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). The fishes that fed on the dead suffer with their river.
