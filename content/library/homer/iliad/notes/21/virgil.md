---
summary: Virgil gives Aeneas, raging after the death of Pallas, Achilles' revenge for Patroclus: captives taken alive for the pyre, a suppliant refused his ransom, and a boast that the fishes will lick the dead man's wounds. His Neptune recalls the rivers filled with Achilles' dead, when the Xanthus could not find its way to the sea, and Walter Leaf hears “the deep of the gray sea” in Pallas' speech to his men.
---

- Aeneas' captives for Pallas' pyre, the supplication of Magus and the death of Tarquitus (*Aeneid* 10.510–560, on 21.26–32 and 21.64–127): [[virgils-lycaon]].
- **The river filled with the dead.** Neptune reminds Venus that he saved Aeneas from the son of Peleus at the time when Achilles drove the breathless Trojan columns against their walls and gave many thousands to death, and the rivers groaned, filled, and the Xanthus could not find its way and roll out to the sea (*Aeneid* 5.803–810; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]). The words are the river's own complaint: “my lovely streams are full of corpses, / and I can find no way to pour my stream into the shining sea, / choked with corpses” (21.218–220). In Homer, Poseidon's rescue of Aeneas comes before the battle by the river, in Book 20 (20.318–329): [[scamander]] and [[aeneas]].
- **The Simois.** In the storm that opens the *Aeneid*, Aeneas wishes he had died at Troy, where the Simois rolls beneath its waves so many shields and helmets of men and brave bodies (*Aeneid* 1.94–101). Book 12 gives the shields and helmets to the Simoeis (12.22–23); in this book it is the Scamander's flood on which “much fine armor of young men slain in battle” floats with the corpses (21.301–302), and the Scamander calls on the Simoeis to help him bury Achilles (21.307–323): [[simoeis]].
- **The sea that shuts us in.** Pallas, rallying his Arcadians, tells them that no gods press them: they are mortals pressed by a mortal enemy, with as many lives and hands as he; the sea shuts them in with its great barrier, and there is no land left for flight (*Aeneid* 10.375–378). Walter Leaf takes *maris magna claudit nos obice pontus* for an imitation of πόντος ἁλός, “the deep of the gray sea, which holds back many against their will”, in Achilles' words on Lycaon's return (21.59; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.59). Pallas' mortal enemy may be compared with Agenor's thought as he waits for Achilles: “and there is one life in him, and men say that he / is mortal” (21.569–570; see [[agenor]]).
