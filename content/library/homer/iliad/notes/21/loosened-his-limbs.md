summary: Athena's stone strikes Ares on the neck, “and loosened his limbs” (21.406); and twice knees and heart are loosened, Lycaon's at Achilles' answer (21.114) and Aphrodite's at Athena's blow (21.425).
grc: ["λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ"]
en: [knees and dear heart were loosened]
---

**A god felled.** The half-verse of a killing blow is used of a god who cannot die: Athena “struck furious Ares on the neck, and loosened his limbs. / He covered seven plethra as he fell, and grimed his hair with dust” (21.406–407; [[furious]], [[stone-throwing]], [[fell-with-a-thud]]).

**Knees and heart.** The other formula, λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ, “knees and dear heart were loosened,” is not death but collapse (see the translation's table of formulas). Lycaon hears Achilles' refusal: “So he spoke, and the other's knees and dear heart were loosened where he was; / he let go of the spear, and sat down, stretching out both / his hands” (21.114–116). Aphrodite takes Athena's blow on the breast: “and her knees and dear heart were loosened where she was” (21.425). The Iliad has the verse only in this book; the Odyssey uses it seven times, of fear, dismay and sudden joy (*Odyssey* 4.703, 5.297, 5.406, 22.68, 22.147, 23.205, 24.345).
