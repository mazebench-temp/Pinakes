summary: When the river is about to drown Achilles, Hera sends Hephaestus against the river. His fire burns the dead on the plain, sets the river's trees and water ablaze and makes the water boil, until the river yields and swears never to help the Trojans; at his mother's word Hephaestus quenches the fire. It is the first of the gods' fights, fire against water. Twice in the book the gold and tin of the arms he made hold back a Trojan spear.

**Hera's call.** The river has risen over Achilles and is bringing him down (21.324–327; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]), and Hera, afraid for him, calls on her son (see [[hera]]):

{{quote:d0dc0430-6187-5fa9-a42a-e8731857a0af}}

κυλλοπόδιον, “crook-footed one”, is the vocative of κυλλοποδίων, the name by which the poem calls him at his forge and over the shield (18.371, 20.270; see [[crook-footed]]). The A scholia record an athetesis of the verse, since Hera should not name her son's lameness when she asks a favor of him; Walter Leaf calls the line indispensable and gives Cobet's explanation, that a scribe took the mark that begins a speech for the obelus of an athetesis (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.331). ἠΐσκομεν, “we judged”, refers to the pairing of the gods in Book 20, “and against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river” (20.73); Leaf takes the imperfect as ironical, “it seems we were wrong”, and calls the allusion to the enmity of fire and water obvious (on 21.332; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Hera's part, the winds that will carry the blaze to the Trojans, is never carried out (21.334–337; Leaf on 21.335; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]).

**The fire.** “So she spoke, and Hephaestus made ready a wondrous fire” (21.342). He burns the dead on the plain and dries it, then turns the flame on the river; the trees and plants of the banks burn, and the eels and the fishes are in distress, “distressed by the breath of Hephaestus of many wiles” (21.343–355; see [[fire]]). The god and his fire are one. The fish suffer under his breath (πνοιῇ, 21.355), and “the blast of the strength / of Hephaestus, of much wisdom” wears the river down (ἀϋτμὴ / Ἡφαίστοιο βίηφι, 21.366–367), as the fire of a sacrifice and of a feast is “the flame of Hephaestus” (2.426, 9.468). His two epithets here are words of the mind. πολυμήτιος, “of many wiles” (21.355), is otherwise in Homer the epithet of Odysseus (see [[of-many-wiles]]); πολύφρων, “of much wisdom” (21.367), is said of Hephaestus again in the *Odyssey*, where the cunning bonds of πολύφρονος Ἡφαίστοιο catch Ares and Aphrodite and the gods laugh at his craft (*Odyssey* 8.296–298, 8.326–327; see [[of-much-wisdom]]).

**The river yields.** The river speaks to him by name: “Hephaestus, no one of the gods can match himself against you, / nor would I fight with you, blazing with fire as you are” (21.357–358). He gives up the Trojans, and when his streams boil like a cauldron (21.362–365; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]) he begs Hera: “Hera, why has your son attacked my stream to trouble it / above all others?” (21.369–370). He swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (21.373–376; see [[scamander]]).

**Hera calls him off.**

{{quote:42cb505c-de46-5f22-a4d8-cbd2d2569af7}}

The reason Hera gives, βροτῶν ἕνεκα, “for the sake of mortals” (21.380), is the reason Apollo gives a little later for refusing to fight Poseidon (21.462–463), and the reason Hera herself gave in Book 8, when she would no longer fight Zeus for mortals (8.427–428). Hephaestus himself had said it on Olympus, when Zeus and Hera quarreled over Thetis: “if you two quarrel like this for the sake of mortals” (1.574; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). The fight of the two gods ends as soon as it is won. στυφελίζειν, “batter” (21.380), is Artemis' word for what Hera does to her later in the book (21.512).

**The gifts of the god.** Hephaestus' work is at Achilles' side through the book. Asteropaeus' first spear strikes the shield, but “the gold held it back, the gifts of the god” (21.164–165 = 20.268), and Agenor's javelin rings off the greave of newly wrought tin, “for the gifts of the god held it back” (21.591–594; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[greaves]]).

**Readings.** Proclus reads the pair of Hephaestus and the river as the opposite principles of bodily matter, heat and dryness against cold and wet (*On the Republic* I 95 Kroll; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]).
