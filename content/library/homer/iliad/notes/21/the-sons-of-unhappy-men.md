summary: Achilles, meeting Asteropaeus at the river, asks him who he is and threatens him with Diomedes' line, “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (21.151). Asteropaeus answers, like Glaucus, with his lineage, and the threat is carried out.

**Diomedes' threat in Achilles' mouth.** Diomedes said the line to a stranger who had come out in front of everyone to face him, praising his daring, σῷ θάρσει, before he asked whether he was a god (6.123–129; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). Achilles says it to the man who has come up out of the river to stand against him with two spears (21.144–151; see [[asteropaeus]]). His question is shorter and harder than Diomedes': not whether the stranger is a god, but who he is, where he comes from, and how he has dared, ἔτλης, to come against him (21.150). Asteropaeus answers, as Glaucus did, with his lineage (21.152–160; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). In Book 6 the answer turned the fight into a friendship; here the threat is carried out, and the father it names as unhappy is Pelegon, the son of the river Axius (see [[pelegon]]).

**Sons and children.** When Asteropaeus is dead, Achilles takes up the word of his threat in his boast. Those who meet his might are “the sons of unhappy men”, δυστήνων … παῖδες (21.151); it is hard for a river's offspring to strive with “the children of the mighty son of Cronus”, ἐρισθενέος Κρονίωνος / παισίν (21.184–185). The sons of unhappy fathers are set against the children of Zeus (see [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]).
