---
summary: Ida is named twice, in a memory and in a hope. Poseidon reminds Apollo that while he built the wall of Troy for Laomedon, Apollo herded the king's cattle “on the slopes of wooded, many-folded Ida” (21.448–449); and Agenor, waiting for Achilles, thinks of running to “the slopes of Ida” and hiding in the brushwood until evening (21.556–561).
---

**Apollo the herdsman.** Φοῖβε σὺ δʼ εἰλίποδας ἕλικας βοῦς βουκολέεσκες / Ἴδης ἐν κνημοῖσι πολυπτύχου ὑληέσσης, “and you, Phoebus, herded the shambling cattle with curving horns / on the slopes of wooded, many-folded Ida” (21.448–449; see [[many-folded]], [[wooded]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). Ἴδης ἐν κνημοῖσι, “on the slopes of Ida”, is the phrase of Aeneas' birth to Aphrodite and Anchises (2.821) and of the place where Achilles caught two sons of Priam herding their sheep (11.104–106; see [[sons-of-priam]]). Ida's slopes are pasture for the herds of Troy's princes, and in this memory a god herds there for a mortal king's wage (see [[laomedon]] and [[apollo]]).

**Agenor's refuge.** Agenor thinks of letting the others be driven in confusion before Achilles while he runs the other way, “toward the Ilean plain, until I reach / the slopes of Ida and slip down into the brushwood” (21.558–559; see [[the-ilean-plain]] and [[agenor]]). ῥωπήϊα, “the brushwood”, is the thick undergrowth through which two lions carry off a goat in a simile of Book 13 (13.198–200). At evening he would bathe in the river, let the sweat dry from him, and go back to Ilios (21.560–561). He gives the plan up, since Achilles would see him going and catch him with his swift feet (21.563–566; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). Ida was where Achilles once caught Aeneas alone with his cattle and drove him down the mountains on swift feet (20.188–191; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). The plain on the way is the puzzle. Walter Leaf rejects Crates' reading, “the plain of Ida”, since Ida is twenty miles from Troy across hilly country and no plain near the city could bear its name, and favors a conjecture by which Agenor would cross the level plain to the glades and the spurs of Ida, a plan he finds perfectly intelligible (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.558; see the translation's note on 21.558).
