---
summary: Old Priam stands “on the divine tower” and sees Achilles driving the Trojans before him; with a groan he comes down from the tower and bids the gatekeepers hold the gates open until the army is inside (21.526–536).
grc: ["θείου ἐπὶ πύργου", "οἰμώξας ἀπὸ πύργου"]
en: [divine tower, came down from the tower]
---

θείου, “divine”: Walter Leaf thinks the tower is called so because Poseidon built it, since θεῖος is not a mere synonym of ἱερός, the common epithet of citadels, and he compares “the god-built towers” of 8.519 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.526). Poseidon tells of the building in this book: “I built a wall around the city for the Trojans” (21.446; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). In Book 3 Priam sat on the same tower with the elders above the Scaean gates and watched the Achaeans (3.146–153); here he watches the rout, and the gates he has opened are presumably the Scaean gate, the only one of which the poem speaks on the side toward the plain (Leaf on 21.531; see [[priam]] and [[scaean-gates]]).
