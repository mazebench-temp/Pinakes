---
summary: Ares begins the battle of the gods. He springs at Athena, calls her a dog-fly, reminds her of the spear she drove into him through Diomedes, and stabs at her aegis; she fells him with a boundary stone, and he covers seven plethra as he falls. Aphrodite leads him away groaning. In Achilles' words to Lycaon his name stands for war.
# Unnamed: his attack and his words to Athena (21.392-400), his fall and Athena's boast over him (21.407-417), and the
# two lying on the earth (21.426).

**War itself.** Achilles tells Lycaon that he too will die, when someone takes his life “in the work of Ares” (Ἄρῃ, 21.112), with a spear or with an arrow (see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and the translation's note on 21.112).

**The attack.** When strife falls on the gods, “Ares began it, / the piercer of shields” (21.391–392; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]): Poseidon had said in Book 20 that the gods of his side would fight only if Ares or Apollo began (20.138). ῥινοτόρος, “piercer of shields”, is found only here (see [[piercer-of-shields]]). He springs at Athena, the goddess set against him in the pairs (20.69), with abuse (ὀνείδειον … μῦθον, 21.393; see [[oneidos]]):

{{quote:22c2d59a-76a5-5375-9b6b-a6a5efe4bff4}}

κυνάμυια, “dog-fly”, joins the dog's shamelessness to the fly's boldness (see [[dog-fly]]), and θάρσος ἄητον, “your gale of daring” (21.395), is a phrase of uncertain sense (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.395; see the translation's note on 21.395). The memory is of Book 5, when Athena drove Diomedes' spear into his flank, wearing the helmet of Hades so that he should not see her (5.844–845, 5.855–859; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]); now he says that she took the spear herself, “the spear seen by all” (21.397; see [[seen-by-all]]). He stabs at the aegis, which not even the thunderbolt of Zeus overpowers (21.400–402; see [[aegis]] and [[blood-stained]]).

**The fall.** Athena takes up a boundary stone and strikes him on the neck (21.403–406; see [[boundary-stones]] and [[stone-throwing]]): “He covered seven plethra as he fell, and grimed his hair with dust, / and his armor clattered about him” (21.407–408). Leaf compares Tityos, who lies over nine plethra in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 11.577), and finds no picture of a god's size as exaggerated except Ares' own cry in Book 5, “as loud as nine thousand or ten thousand men” (5.860; on 21.407). Athena laughs and boasts over him: he has not yet learned how much stronger she is, and he is paying for his mother's curses, because he deserted the Achaeans and helps the Trojans (21.408–414; see [[athena]] and [[erinyes]]). Leaf takes the Erinyes for no more than Hera's curses, and recalls Athena's words in Book 5, that Ares had declared to her and to Hera “that he would fight the Trojans and help the Argives” (5.832–834; on 21.412; see [[hera]]).

**Led away.** “and Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took him by the hand and led him away, / groaning thick and fast; and hardly did he gather his spirit again” (21.416–417; see [[aphrodite]]). The last words are those of Hector after Ajax's stone, “he was just gathering his spirit again” (15.240). Hera calls him “Ares, ruin of mortals” as she sends Athena after them (21.421; see [[ruin-of-mortals]]); Athena strikes Aphrodite down, so that “the two of them lay on the bountiful earth”, and mocks her for coming “as an ally to Ares” (21.426–431). In Book 5 he went up to Olympus, complained to Zeus and was healed, and sat beside his father “exulting in his glory” (5.868–906); the poem does not say how he rises from the plain this time.
