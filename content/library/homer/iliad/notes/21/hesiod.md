---
summary: Hesiod's Tethys bears to Oceanus the eddying rivers, among them the silver-eddying Achelous, the Strymon, the Simois and the Scamander. His battle of the Titans makes the wide heaven groan, as the battle of the gods makes the great sky trumpet; Rhea plans vengeance for the Erinyes of a father, as Athena tells Ares that he pays for those of his mother; and the dolphins of the Hesiodic Shield hunt the fishes in a harbor, as in Homer's simile.
---

- **The rivers.** Tethys bore to Oceanus the eddying Rivers, the Nile, the Alpheus, the Strymon, the Phasis, the Rhesus and Ἀχελώιόν τʼ ἀργυροδίνην, the silver-eddying Achelous, and with them the Aesepus, divine Simois and divine Scamander (*Theogony* 337–345). Achilles' boast over Asteropaeus names lord Achelous beside Oceanus, “from whom all rivers and all the sea / and all springs and deep wells flow” (21.194–197); Hesiod's epithet for the Achelous is the one the poem gives the Scamander, “silver-eddying” (21.8, 21.130). Walter Leaf notes that Achelous is named in Homer only here, and in Hesiod at *Theogony* 340 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.194): [[achelous]], [[oceanus]] and [[rivers]].
- **The sky in the battle of the gods.** When the gods fall upon each other, “the wide earth roared, / and the great sky trumpeted around” (21.387–388). In Hesiod's war of the Titans the boundless sea roared, the earth crashed loudly, the wide heaven groaned as it was shaken, and tall Olympus shook from its base (*Theogony* 678–680). Walter Leaf compares the verses, and takes the trumpet of the sky for the din of the fighting echoed from the vault of heaven, not for thunder (on 21.388): [[the-battle-of-the-gods]] and [[titans]].
- **The Erinyes of a parent.** Athena tells the fallen Ares, “So you may pay in full for the Erinyes of your mother” (21.412). In the *Theogony* Rhea, about to bear Zeus, begs her parents to help her bear the child unseen, so that vengeance might be taken for the Erinyes of the father and of the children whom great Cronus swallowed (τίσαιτο δʼ ἐρινῦς πατρὸς ἑοῖο, *Theogony* 468–473). Walter Leaf compares the verse, and takes the Erinyes in Athena's words for little more than a mother's curses (on 21.412): [[erinyes]] and [[ares]].
- **Dolphins in a harbor.** Among the scenes of the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* is a harbor with good anchorage, where two silver dolphins devour the mute fishes and the bronze fishes tremble beneath them (*Shield* 207–213). The dolphin of Book 21 drives the other fishes in fear into the recesses of “a harbor with good anchorage” (21.22–24). Walter Leaf compares the picture (on 21.22): [[shield-of-heracles]] and [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]].
