summary: When Hephaestus has mastered the river, strife falls on the other gods; the earth roars, the great sky trumpets, and Zeus, sitting on Olympus, laughs for joy. Ares strikes Athena's aegis and she fells him with a boundary stone, and strikes down Aphrodite, who leads him away; Apollo will not fight Poseidon for the sake of mortals; Hera beats Artemis about the ears with her own bow; Hermes yields to Leto. Artemis weeps on her father's knees, Apollo goes into Ilios, and the other gods go back to Olympus.

**The first pair.** Of the five pairs set against one another in Book 20 (20.67–74), the first to meet is the one in which Walter Leaf saw the only principle of the pairing, fire against water (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.67). Hera sends Hephaestus against the Xanthus: “we judged eddying Xanthus to be matched in battle”, she tells him (21.332), an imperfect that Leaf takes as ironical, “it seems we were wrong” (on 21.332). The fire dries the plain and boils the river until it yields and swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (21.328–384; see [[hephaestus]], [[hera]], [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

**Strife falls.** Then the other gods come to blows:

{{quote:b71f0578-b9b8-57db-aa7d-59a01815955e}}

The verse of the clash is the verse of the Trojans tumbling into the river at the start of the book: σὺν δʼ ἔπεσον μεγάλῳ πατάγῳ, “and they fell upon each other with a great crash” (21.387), answers ἐν δʼ ἔπεσον μεγάλῳ πατάγῳ, “and they fell in with a great crash” (21.9), and in both the next words are βράχε δʼ, “roared”. ἄητο (21.386), the heart “blown” two ways, Leaf takes of a heart blown about by gusts of passion (on 21.386). σάλπιγξεν, “trumpeted” (21.388), is found only here, and the trumpet itself only in a simile (18.219). Aristarchus noted that the poet knows the trumpet but never shows his heroes using it; Leaf takes the sound for the din of the fighting echoing from the vault of the sky, not for thunder, since Zeus the thunderer takes no part, and compares Hesiod's battle of the Titans, where the wide sky groaned as it was shaken (ἐπέστενε δʼ οὐρανὸς εὐρὺς / σειόμενος, *Theogony* 679–680; on 21.388; see the translation's note on 21.388 and [[longinus-wounded-gods]]). The last words, θεοὺς ἔριδι ξυνιόντας, “the gods coming together in strife” (21.390), recall the din of Book 20, “as the gods came together in strife” (20.66), and Hesiod ends the din of the Titans' war with the same words (θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, *Theogony* 705).

**Zeus laughs.** He had said that he would stay on Olympus, “where I shall look on and delight my heart” (20.23; see [[zeus]] and [[watching-the-war]]), and now his heart laughs for joy. Leaf notes that Homer's Zeus goes beyond a smile only here and at 508, when Artemis comes to him in tears, and thinks that he “appears to have a just appreciation of the whole combat as a parody of serious fighting” (on 21.390; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

**Athena and Ares.** Ares begins, as Poseidon had foreseen in Book 20 (20.138), “the piercer of shields” (ῥινοτόρος, 21.392, only here; see [[piercer-of-shields]]). He calls Athena a dog-fly who drives the gods together in strife (21.394; see [[dog-fly]]), the very words in which Poseidon had refused to begin, “I would not wish that we should drive the gods together in strife” (20.134). He reminds her of Book 5, when she set Diomedes on him and drove the spear home herself (21.396–399; 5.855–859; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]), and stabs at her aegis, which not even the thunderbolt of Zeus overpowers (21.400–402; see [[aegis]]). She gives ground and takes up a stone:

{{quote:9af59755-c5e5-55ee-b59d-70a3631f7381}}

The first two verses are those of Hector's stone against Ajax, with the goddess in the man's place (7.264–265; Leaf on 21.403; see [[stone-throwing]]), and the third makes the stone a landmark, “the boundary stone of a field” (21.405; see [[boundary-stones]]). A plethron is about a hundred feet. Leaf finds no picture of a god's size as exaggerated as these seven plethra except Ares' own cry in Book 5, “as loud as nine thousand or ten thousand men” (5.860), and Tityos lying over nine plethra in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 11.577); Homer's gods are tall, “as gods should be” (18.518), but not such monsters as this, and he takes all these passages for late (on 21.407). Athena laughs, calls Ares “Little fool” (21.410; see [[nepios]]), and tells him that he is paying for his mother's curses, because he deserted the Achaeans (21.409–414; see [[athena]] and [[ares]]).

**Aphrodite.** Aphrodite, the one god without an opponent in the pairs (20.67–74), takes Ares by the hand and leads him away, groaning (21.416–417; see [[aphrodite]]). Hera sends Athena after her in the verses with which she sent her against Ares in Book 5 (21.419–420 = 5.713–714): “there again that dog-fly is leading Ares, ruin of mortals” (21.421; see [[ruin-of-mortals]]). Athena strikes her on the breast, and “So the two of them lay on the bountiful earth” (21.426). Her second boast wishes that all the Trojans' helpers were “so bold and hardy, as Aphrodite was” (21.430): then the war would long be over (21.428–433). “So she spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera smiled” (21.434), the verse with which Hera smiled at Hephaestus' plea for peace among the gods in Book 1 (1.595); Leaf thinks it interpolated from there (on 21.434).

**Poseidon and Apollo.** The earth-shaker challenges Apollo: it would be the more shameful to go back to Olympus without a fight, and Apollo, as the younger, should begin. He reminds him of the year they served Laomedon for a wage that was never paid (21.435–460; see [[poseidon]], [[laomedon]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). Leaf finds this bellicose Poseidon hardly the god who advised his party in Book 20 to sit apart and leave the war to men (on 21.436; 20.133–143). Apollo answers that he would not be “sound of mind” if he fought for mortals, who flourish and waste away like leaves, “and let them fight it out themselves” (21.462–467; see [[apollo]], [[the-generations-of-leaves]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]); “for he was ashamed / to come to blows with his father's brother” (21.468–469; see [[aidos]]). His reason is the one Hera has just given for sparing the river, that it is not fitting “to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.380), and the one Hephaestus gave in Book 1, “if you two quarrel like this for the sake of mortals” (1.574).

**Hera and Artemis.** Artemis, “the mistress of wild beasts” (21.470), rebukes her brother for giving Poseidon the triumph “for nothing” (21.473), and Apollo makes no answer; Hera does. She calls her “you shameless dog” (21.481), a lion only to women, who would do better to kill wild beasts on the mountains than to fight her betters (21.481–488; see [[shameless-dog]] and [[a-lion-to-women]]). The last sentence breaks off, and the deed supplies the rest, as Nicanor explained (Leaf on 21.487): she catches both of Artemis' wrists in her left hand, takes the bow from her shoulders with her right and beats her about the ears with it, smiling, while the arrows fall out; Artemis flees in tears like a dove into a rock, and leaves the bow behind (21.489–496; see [[artemis]] and [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]).

**Hermes and Leto.** Hermes, set against Leto in Book 20 (20.72), will not fight her: “it is a hard thing / to trade blows with the wives of Zeus the cloud-gatherer”, and she may boast among the gods that she beat him (21.497–501; see [[hermes]] and [[leto]]). Leto gathers up her daughter's bow and arrows from the dust and follows her (21.502–504).

**The end.** Artemis goes up to the bronze-floored house of Zeus, sits weeping on her father's knees, and he laughs and asks her the question that Dione asked Aphrodite when Diomedes had wounded her (21.505–510; 21.509–510 = 5.373–374; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]). Leaf thinks the whole scene founded on memories of the wounding of Aphrodite (on 21.509). She names Hera, “from whom strife and quarrel have fastened upon the immortals” (21.513; see [[eris]] and [[neikos]]): the strife that fell on the gods at the beginning (ἔρις, 21.385) is laid at Hera's door at the end. Apollo goes into Ilios, for the wall is on his mind, “lest the Danaans sack it against fate that day” (21.515–517; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]), the fear for which Zeus had let the gods go to war (20.30); the others go back to Olympus, “some of them angry, and some exulting greatly”, and sit down beside their father (21.518–520; see [[olympus]]).

**The outcome.** Every fight goes to a god of the Achaean side, the gods whom Poseidon had called “far the stronger” in Book 20 (20.135): Hephaestus masters the river, Athena fells Ares and Aphrodite, Hera beats Artemis. The two who decline are Apollo, on the Trojan side, and Hermes, on the Achaean. No god bleeds. In Book 5 the gods wounded by Diomedes shed ichor and were healed on Olympus (5.339–342, 5.900–906; see [[ichor]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]); here Ares lies in the dust and groans, Aphrodite falls, and Artemis weeps. Zeus laughs at the beginning and at the end (21.389, 21.508), Athena laughs over Ares (21.408), Hera smiles twice (21.434, 21.491), and below them Achilles goes on killing Trojans and their horses (21.520–521; see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). Leaf, who took the episode for a late composition, found borrowings in it made mechanically (on 21.502) and called Apollo's leaves a medley that only deliberate parody could explain (on 21.464). The fight takes place on the day of Achilles' return, between his struggle with the river and the rout of the Trojans into the city (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]).
