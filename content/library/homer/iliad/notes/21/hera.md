summary: Hera spreads a mist before the fleeing Trojans. When the river is about to sweep Achilles away, she shouts in fear for him and sends her son Hephaestus against it with fire; when the burned river begs her and swears never to help the Trojans, she calls Hephaestus off, for it is not fitting to batter an immortal god for the sake of mortals. In the battle of the gods she sets Athena on Aphrodite and smiles at the result, and she beats Artemis about the ears with her own bow; Artemis blames her for the gods' strife.

**The mist.** As the Trojans flee across the plain toward the city, “Hera spread / a deep mist before them to hold them back” (21.6–7; see [[trojans]]). Walter Leaf notes that the mist is forgotten at once, and that the poem does not say whom it is to hold back, probably the Trojans; Düntzer struck the words out (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.6; see the translation's note on 21.6–7).

**Hephaestus sent.** When the wave of the river is bringing Achilles down, “Hera gave a great shout, terribly afraid for Achilles” (21.328; see [[achilles]]), and calls on her son (see [[achilles-and-the-river]]):

{{quote:446c8a0e-43aa-59b4-a032-9976fa2867ad}}

κυλλοπόδιον, “crook-footed one” (21.331), is the vocative of a title of Hephaestus (18.371, 20.270; see [[hephaestus]] and [[crook-footed]]). An ancient athetesis of the verse held that Hera should not name her son's lameness when she asks his help; Leaf calls the verse indispensable (on 21.331; see the translation's note on 21.331). In Book 18 Hephaestus remembered his “dog-faced mother, who wished / to hide me away because I was lame” (18.396–397); here she calls him “my child” in the same breath as his lameness. ἠΐσκομεν, “we judged” (21.332), looks back to the pairing of Book 20, which set the river against Hephaestus (20.73–74), and Leaf takes the imperfect as ironical (on 21.332). She promises to rouse the West Wind and the brightening South Wind from the sea to carry the blaze against the Trojans (21.334–337; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]), but the winds never come (see the translation's note on 21.335). Her orders end with a signal: he is not to be turned back by soothing words or threats, and is to stop only when she cries out (21.338–341).

**The river's oath.** Burned and boiling, the river begs her: why has her son attacked his stream above all others, when he is not so much to blame as all the others who help the Trojans? He will cease if she bids him, and he swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (21.369–376; see [[scamander]] and [[oath]]). The three verses of the oath are those in which Hera told Poseidon of the oaths she and Athena had sworn (21.374–376 = 20.315–317): the river swears to her what she has sworn herself. Then:

{{quote:42cb505c-de46-5f22-a4d8-cbd2d2569af7}}

Her reason, that an immortal god is not to be battered “for the sake of mortals” (21.380), is the one Apollo will give a little later for refusing to fight Poseidon (21.463; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). ἐρύκακε χωομένη περ, “angry though she was” (21.384): she holds the two of them back before her anger is spent.

**Athena and Aphrodite.** When Athena has felled Ares and Aphrodite is leading him away, Hera sees her and sets Athena on her: “Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone, / there again that dog-fly is leading Ares, ruin of mortals, / out of destructive war, through the turmoil; go after her!” (21.420–422; see [[athena]], [[aphrodite]] and [[dog-fly]]). The first two verses of her speech are those with which she called Athena out against Ares himself in Book 5 (21.419–420 = 5.713–714; Leaf on 21.419; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). When Athena has struck Aphrodite down and boasted over her, “So she spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera smiled” (21.434; see [[white-armed]]), the verse with which she smiled at Hephaestus' plea for peace among the gods in Book 1 (1.595); Leaf thinks it interpolated from there (on 21.434).

**Artemis.** Set against Hera in Book 20 (20.70–71), Artemis does not attack her; she taunts her brother for yielding to Poseidon (21.470–477; see [[artemis]] and [[apollo]]). It is Hera who turns on her, “the revered wife of Zeus, in anger” (21.479), with abuse: “you shameless dog”, an archer whom Zeus has made “a lion to women”, who should hunt wild beasts on the mountains and leave fighting to those stronger than she (21.481–488; see [[shameless-dog]] and [[a-lion-to-women]]). The sentence breaks off, and the deed supplies the rest (Nicanor, as Leaf reports on 21.487):

{{quote:fb2b67db-9d61-5c98-9e0c-e466a4ccf9fd}}

μειδιόωσα, “smiling” (21.491): she smiled at Athena's victory over Aphrodite, and she smiles at her own. The variant ἐντροπαλιζομένη, which would make Hera the one who twists this way and that, Leaf calls inferior, since there is no reason why Hera should turn aside (on 21.492). Artemis flees in tears like a dove from a hawk (21.493–496; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]), and complains to her father: “It was your wife who battered me, father, white-armed Hera, / from whom strife and quarrel have fastened upon the immortals” (21.512–513; see [[zeus]] and [[eris]]). στυφελίζω, “batter”, is the verb of Hera's own words to Hephaestus (21.380). Hermes, declining to fight Leto, speaks of “the wives of Zeus the cloud-gatherer” (21.499), a plural in which Leaf hears an allusion to Hera's triumph (on 21.499; see [[hermes]] and [[leto]]).
