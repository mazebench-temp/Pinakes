---
summary: Strabo uses Book 21 for the Troad and for the north. He counts the Leleges of Altes as the third of the Trojan dynasties and rejects the reading “under the Satnioeis”, cites the bowl that Euneus gave for Lycaon, explains Asteropaeus' father Pelegon by the Paeonians' old name, Pelagonians, and finds the muddy Axius hard to square with its fairest water. He tells of Aethiopians who catch locusts with smoke, as in the simile of the locusts, places the oak beside which Apollo stands near the old site of Ilium, and reports that rivers were likened to bulls for their roaring and their bends.
---

- **Altes and the Leleges.** Following those who conjecture from the poem that the whole coast was Trojan, divided into nine dynasties under Priam, Strabo counts the Leleges as the third, quoting “Altes, who rules over the war-loving Leleges”, whose daughter bore Priam Lycaon and Polydorus (*Geography* 13.1.7, on 21.85–88). Later he places their city, Pedasus, on the Satnioeis, where its deserted site is still shown; some write ὑπὸ Σατνιόεντι, “under the Satnioeis”, wrongly, he says, as if the city lay under a mountain, but there is no mountain of that name there, only the river, a large torrent (*Geography* 13.1.50, quoting 21.86–87). Walter Leaf reports Strabo's remark for his reading ἐπί (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.87): [[leleges]], [[altes]], [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]] and [[satnioeis]].
- **The bowl for Lycaon.** Praising the skill of the Sidonians, Strabo cites the mixing bowl that Euneus gave in exchange for Lycaon, and quotes the verses on its beauty (*Geography* 1.2.33, on 23.741–744). In this book Lycaon tells Achilles that he was sold into Lemnos, where the son of Jason paid the price (21.40–41, 21.76–79): [[euneus]], [[lemnos]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]].
- **Pelegon and the Pelagonians.** Some, Strabo says, make the Paeonians colonists of the Phrygians, others their founders, and Paeonia is said to have reached as far as Pelagonia and Pieria; so Asteropaeus, one of the Paeonian leaders who came to Ilium, is not without reason called the son of Pelegon, since the Paeonians themselves were called Pelagonians (*Geography* 7 fr. 38–39, on 21.141, 21.159): [[pelegon]], [[asteropaeus]] and [[paeonians]].
- **The fairest water.** The Axius, Strabo says, flows muddy, while Homer calls its water the fairest; perhaps, he suggests, because of the spring called Aea, which pours very pure water into it and shows the reading now current in the poet to be faulty (*Geography* 7 fr. 21). Asteropaeus repeats the Catalogue's praise of the Axius, “who sends the fairest water over the earth” (21.158; compare 2.850): [[axius]].
- **Locusts and fire.** Walter Leaf, on the simile of the locusts that flee the fire into a river (21.12–14), reports that the scholia call the practice Cypriot, and that Strabo tells of it among the locust-eaters of Aethiopia (on 21.12). Strabo describes them as blacker and shorter than their neighbors and very short-lived; they live on the locusts that the strong spring winds drive into their country, and they throw smoky wood into the ravines and set it alight, so that the locusts flying over the smoke are blinded and fall (*Geography* 16.4.12): [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]].
- **The oak.** Apollo takes his stand beside Agenor “leaning against the oak” (21.549). Strabo, with those who think that the old Ilium stood at the Village of the Ilians, places the wild fig tree below the old site and far from the city of his day, and the oak a little below the wild fig tree, quoting Achilles' words on the Scaean gates and the oak (*Geography* 13.1.35, on 9.352–354): [[the-oak-of-zeus]].
- **Rivers like bulls.** Explaining the story of Heracles' fight with the Achelous for Deianira, Strabo reports how some read the myth. Achelous was said to be like a bull, as the other rivers are, from their roaring and from the bends of their streams, which people call horns, and like a serpent for his length and windings; Heracles subdued the river with embankments and channels (*Geography* 10.2.19). In this book the Scamander casts out the dead “bellowing like a bull” (21.237), and Achilles names lord Achelous in his boast (21.194): [[achelous]], [[rivers]] and [[heracles]].
