summary: Aphrodite, who had no opponent in the pairs of gods, takes the fallen Ares by the hand and leads him away. Hera calls her a dog-fly and sends Athena after her, and Athena strikes her on the breast, so that she falls beside Ares, and mocks her as the boldest of the Trojans' helpers.

**Ares' helper.** In Book 20 she went down with the gods of the Trojans and was set against no one (20.40, 20.67–74; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Her part in the battle is to help the god who lent her his horses in Book 5, when Diomedes had wounded her hand (5.355–363; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]): “and Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took him by the hand and led him away, / groaning thick and fast” (21.416–417; see [[ares]] and [[daughter-of-zeus]]). Walter Leaf reports the argument of the ancient Separatists, that the poet of the Iliad made Aphrodite the companion of Ares and the poet of the *Odyssey* the wife of Hephaestus, and the answer in the scholia, that the two unions did not belong to the same time (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.416; see the translation's note on 21.416). In the Iliad the wife of Hephaestus is Charis (18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]).

**Struck down.** Hera sees her and calls her “that dog-fly” (21.421; see [[dog-fly]] and [[hera]]), and Athena runs after her:

Her knees give way in the words used of Lycaon before Achilles, λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ (21.425, 21.114; see the translation's row on that formula). Athena mocks her: would that all who help the Trojans were “so bold and hardy, as Aphrodite was / when she came as an ally to Ares, to meet my might” (21.430–431; see [[athena]]). τλήμονες, “hardy”, is the second sense of τλήμων, which is “enduring” of Odysseus (see the translation's note on 10.231). In Book 5 Zeus told her that the works of war were not given to her (5.428–430); she has gone among them again, with the same result. “So she spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera smiled” (21.434).
