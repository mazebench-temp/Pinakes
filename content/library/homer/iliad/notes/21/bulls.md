summary: The Trojans have long sacrificed many bulls to their river, Achilles says, and thrown live horses into its eddies (21.130–132); when the river rises against him, it casts out the dead “bellowing like a bull” (21.237).
# ταύρους (21.131), only here in the Iliad.
grc: [ταύρους]
---

**Bulls for the river.** “Not even the river, fair-flowing and silver-eddying though it is, / will protect you, to whom you have long sacrificed many bulls” (21.130–131; see [[rivers]] and [[scamander]]). A bull is a river's victim in Nestor's story too, “a bull to Alpheus, and a bull to Poseidon” (11.728; see [[alpheus]]). Walter Leaf connects the offering with the common figure of a river as a bull or a bull-headed man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.131).

**The river bellows.** When Achilles leaps into the river, the river rises, stirs up his streams, and thrusts the dead out onto the land, μεμυκὼς ἠΰτε ταῦρος, “bellowing like a bull” (21.237; see [[like-a-bull]]). Leaf takes the half-verse to explain why rivers were so often pictured as bulls (on 21.237). μεμυκώς is said of the bull dragged off by two lions on the Shield (18.580), and the dying Hippodamas bellowed like a bull dragged around the Heliconian lord, Poseidon (20.403–404; see [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]]).
