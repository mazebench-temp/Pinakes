---
summary: Apollo stands beside Agenor “leaning against the oak”, hidden in thick mist, to ward off death from him as Achilles comes on (21.547–549). Walter Leaf takes it for the oak by the Scaean gate.
---

φηγῷ κεκλιμένος, “leaning against the oak” (21.549): the god who will save Agenor stands at his side, unseen, “to ward off the heavy hands of Death” (21.547–549; see [[apollo]], [[agenor]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The oak is not described. Walter Leaf takes it for the oak by the Scaean gate of Book 5 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.549; see the translation's note on 21.549), and the gates that Priam has just had opened for the army are taken for the Scaean gate too (Leaf on 21.531; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[priam]]). Apollo has been at this tree before: he and Athena met beside it and sat in it in the likeness of vultures to watch the duel of Hector and Ajax (7.22, 7.58–61).

In the rout of Book 11 the Trojans “came to the Scaean gates and the oak” and there halted and waited for one another (11.170–171). In this book they do not wait: they pour into the city, not daring “to wait for one another outside the city / and the wall” (21.608–609; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]).
