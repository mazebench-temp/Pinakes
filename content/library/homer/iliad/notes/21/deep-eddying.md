---
summary: The Scamander is deep-eddying from the first rout to the last chase: “so before Achilles deep-eddying Xanthus' / sounding stream was filled” (21.15–16); he speaks to Achilles in anger and reproaches Apollo (21.212, 21.228); Hera fears that he will sweep Achilles away (21.329); and Apollo leads Achilles off “along the deep-eddying river Scamander” (21.603). Once the epithet is the Axius', the father of Pelegon (21.143).
---

The verse-end ποταμὸς βαθυδίνης comes four times in the book (21.143, 21.212, 21.228, 21.329). Its last use is the verse that set the river against Hephaestus when the gods were paired: “lest the great deep-eddying river sweep him away” (μέγας ποταμὸς βαθυδίνης, 21.329, as 20.73), and at once Hera calls Hephaestus (21.330–341). The river that speaks to Achilles “from a deep eddy” (21.213) is deep-eddying in the verse before; Heyne suspected the line, Walter Leaf reports, partly for the repetition of βαθυδίνης and δίνης (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.213).

The Axius is the other deep-eddying river. He lay with Periboea and fathered Pelegon, Asteropaeus' father, and in the duel that follows Achilles sets the lineage of rivers below the lineage of Zeus (21.141–143, 21.184–199; [[axius]], [[broad-flowing]]).
