summary: Agenor's great scene. As Achilles drives the Trojans to the gates, Apollo sets him against Achilles and stands beside him hidden in mist. Alone before the city, Agenor weighs flight against standing his ground and decides to stand; he waits like a leopard for the hunter, challenges Achilles, and hits his greave, which the god's gift makes proof against the bronze. When Achilles rushes at him, Apollo snatches him away and takes his likeness (21.544–600).

**Set on by Apollo.** The Achaeans are about to take the city when the god sends one man out against Achilles:

{{quote:d800bf21-e6a2-5363-8137-9948fdde699d}}

The first verses are those of Patroclus at the same wall, when Apollo himself stood on the tower and drove him back (21.544–545 ≈ 16.698–700; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). This time the god acts through a man. ἀνῆκε, “set … on”, is the verb of Apollo sending Aeneas against Achilles in the last book (20.118; see [[aeneas]]). φῶτʼ Ἀντήνορος υἱὸν ἀμύμονά τε κρατερόν τε, “a man, the son of Antenor, noble and strong” (21.546; see [[son-of-antenor]] and [[blameless]]), is built like the verse that names Machaon, “the man who is the son of Asclepius” (4.194; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.544). Apollo puts θάρσος, “daring”, into his heart (see [[tharsos]]) and stands beside him “to ward off the heavy hands of Death” (21.548; see [[death]] and the translation's note on 21.548), leaning against the oak, which Leaf takes for the oak by the Scaean gate (on 21.549; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). It is the god, not the man, who is hidden in the mist.

**The debate.** When Agenor sees Achilles, “his heart heaved with many thoughts as he waited” (21.551), and he speaks to his own heart in the verse of Odysseus, Menelaus and Hector (21.552; see [[deeply-troubled]]). He sets out three ways. He could flee where the others are fleeing, and be caught and have his throat cut as a coward (21.553–555). He could let the others be driven before Achilles and run the other way, from the wall over the plain to the slopes of Ida, hide in the brushwood, and come back to the city at evening, washed and cool (21.556–561; see [[ida]] and [[the-ilean-plain]]). Or he could go out to meet Achilles in front of the city (21.567). He breaks off the second with the verse that ends every such debate, “But why does my heart debate these things with me?”, sees that Achilles would notice him and catch him on the plain, and chooses the third:

{{quote:62658e93-5548-5f99-8edb-0d2b3da72ae1}}

Leaf notes that Achilles' invulnerability is a legend later than Homer (on 21.568): here his flesh can be pierced like any man's. Verse 570 was athetized in antiquity, Leaf reports from the scholia, as added by someone who thought that the sentence wanted a verb, and because “but Zeus, son of Cronus, grants him glory” undoes the hope just spoken; Leaf finds the objection well founded, and notes that the verse comes from 8.141 (on 21.570; see the translation's note on 21.568–570). The plan of the speech is closest to Hector's in the next book: flight is rejected at once, a roundabout escape is weighed and dropped, and the man decides to stand with a faint hope (Leaf on 21.552; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[decision-scene]]).

**The leopard.** “So saying, he crouched and waited for Achilles, and his valiant heart / within him was eager to make war and to fight” (21.571–572). He is like a leopard that comes out of a deep thicket to face a hunter and does not give up even when pierced with a spear, “until she either closes with him or is brought down” (21.573–580; see [[the-leopard-and-the-hunter]] and [[leopard]]).

**The challenge.** He holds his shield before him, takes aim and shouts:

{{quote:13421695-4559-592c-be9c-aa369388651c}}

His words answer Apollo's to Patroclus at the wall, “It is not your portion / that the city of the proud Trojans be sacked by your spear, / nor by Achilles” (16.707–709): πόλιν πέρσειν Τρώων ἀγερώχων (21.584) repeats πόλιν πέρθαι Τρώων ἀγερώχων (16.708; see [[proud]]). νηπύτιʼ, “you little fool” (21.585), is the address of the gods' taunts in this book, here in a mortal's mouth (21.410, 21.441, 21.474; see [[nepios]] and the translation's note on 21.410). The Trojans within the city guard Ilios in front of their parents and wives and sons (21.586–588; see [[troy]]); οἳ καί, “who”, is Aristarchus' reading for the manuscripts' οἵ κε (Leaf on 21.587).

**The throw.** “He spoke, and hurled the sharp javelin from his heavy hand, / and hit him on the shin below the knee, and did not miss” (21.590–591). The greave of newly wrought tin rang, and the bronze sprang back, “for the gifts of the god held it back” (21.592–594): Hephaestus made Achilles “greaves of soft tin” (18.613; see [[greaves]], [[tin]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). Leaf thinks that 594 was added to recall the making of the arms, and notes that the manuscript A omits it (on 21.594; see the translation's note on 21.594).

**Snatched away.** “Then the son of Peleus rushed at godlike Agenor / in his turn” (21.595–596; see [[antitheos]]), but Apollo would not let him win glory. He snatched Agenor away, hid him in thick mist, and “sent him away to go out of the war in peace” (21.596–598; see [[divine-rescue]]). Then the god took his shape, “in the likeness of Agenor himself in every way” (21.600), and led Achilles away from the city while the Trojans crowded in (21.599–611; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). Agenor is not named again in the poem. In the last book Achilles killed his brother Demoleon (20.395–400; see [[demoleon]] and [[antenor]]).
