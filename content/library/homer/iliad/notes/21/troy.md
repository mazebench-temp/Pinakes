---
summary: Troy is the goal of Achilles' rout and the thing the gods fear for. He means to pen the Trojans in the city, Poseidon and Athena promise that he will pen them “within the glorious walls of Ilios”, and the river swears never to help them, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire”. At the end of the book the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy, had Apollo not set Agenor against Achilles, and the Trojans crowd into the city (21.225–611).
# Ἰλιόφι occurs only at 21.295.
grc: [Ἰλιόφι]
refs: [21.4, 21.225, 21.309, 21.446-447, 21.516-517, 21.532, 21.540, 21.563, 21.567, 21.584-586, 21.606-611]
---

**Penned in the city.** Achilles drives half of the Trojans across the plain “toward the city” and half into the river (21.3–8). He tells Lycaon that the Trojans will perish “until we reach the city of sacred Ilios” (21.128; see [[hieros]]), and the river that he will not stop slaying them “until I have penned them in the city and made trial of Hector” (21.224–225). Poseidon and Athena promise him the same: he is not to stop “until you have penned the army within the glorious walls of Ilios” (21.295). Ἰλιόφι, “of Ilios”, is found only here. Walter Leaf takes it as a genitive after τείχεα, and so as a false archaism, made when the old locative or instrumental force of the ending -φι had been forgotten; in so late a passage, he thinks, the mistake is likelier than the old genitive Ἰλίοο that L. Meyer would restore, and the phrase Ἰλιόθι πρό, “before Ilios” (8.561), may have helped it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.295; see the translation's note on 21.295 and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The river, calling on his brother Simoeis, fears that Achilles “will sack the great city of lord Priam” (21.309; see [[scamander]] and [[priam]]).

**Troy burning.** Beaten by Hephaestus' fire, the river swears “never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, / not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire, / burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans are burning it” (21.374–376), the oath that Hera says she and Athena have sworn (20.313–317; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Athena, boasting over Ares and Aphrodite, says that if all the Trojans' helpers were like them, “then long ago we would have ceased from war, / having sacked the well-built citadel of Ilios” (21.428–433; see [[athena]]). The book's simile of the smoke that goes up from a burning city, sent up by the wrath of the gods, is told of Achilles as he drives the Trojans toward Troy (21.522–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]).

**The wall and its builder.** Poseidon reminds Apollo of the evils they suffered “around Ilios” when they served Laomedon for a wage: he built the wall around the city for the Trojans, so that the city might be unbreakable, while Apollo herded cattle on Ida (21.441–449; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).

**The day the city nearly fell.** After the gods' battle Apollo goes into “sacred Ilios”, for the wall of the well-built city is on his mind, “lest the Danaans sack it against fate that day” (21.515–517; see [[apollo]]). “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy” (21.544), as they would have taken it at the hands of Patroclus (16.698), but Apollo sets Agenor against Achilles (21.545; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[high-gated]] and [[agenor]]). Agenor thinks of running away from the city and coming back to Ilios at evening, and stands instead in front of the city; he tells Achilles that he hoped to sack the city of the proud Trojans that day, but that many valiant men “guard Ilios” in front of their parents and wives and sons (21.556–588). Apollo leads Achilles away in Agenor's shape, and the Trojans pour into the city (21.599–611; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]).

**Ilios in the speeches.** Lycaon is on his twelfth day since he came home “to Ilios” from Lemnos (21.80–81), and Asteropaeus has been there eleven days, in a verse that echoes Lycaon's (21.155–156; Leaf on 21.156). Achilles says that no one shall escape death “whomever a god / throws into my hands before Ilios” (21.103–104).
