---
summary: Lycaon, clasping Achilles' knees, claims a suppliant's standing because he once ate at Achilles' side: “for with you first I tasted the grain of Demeter / on the day when you took me in the well-built orchard” (21.76–77).
---

{{quote:81c59bbe-7787-5834-8d0a-8c5b6aa74732}}

Δημήτερος ἀκτή, “the grain of Demeter” (21.76), is bread, the goddess's gift, and the phrase is found in Homer only here and in Idomeneus' praise of Ajax, who would give way to no man, “a man who was mortal and ate the grain of Demeter” (13.322; see the note on Book 13). To eat it is to be mortal. Lycaon makes it a bond as well: he ate it first at Achilles' side, as his captive, before he was sold, and so he is “as good as a revered suppliant” (21.75; see [[supplication]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Walter Leaf explains the claim by the custom that to have broken bread under a man's roof gives the standing of a suppliant, and takes πρώτῳ, “first”, to mean first among the Greeks (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.75–76). Achilles does not answer the claim (21.99–113; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]).
