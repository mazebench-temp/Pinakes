# The leaves of Apollo's answer (21.464-465). φύλλοισιν ἐοικότες alone would also catch Iris' "like leaves or like
# the sands" (2.800).

{{quote:2fc87026-a898-51f1-83dc-9246b007bb4e}}

Apollo joins two phrases of Diomedes' meeting with Glaucus in Book 6. ἀρούρης καρπὸν ἔδοντες, “eating the fruit of the field” (21.465), is Diomedes' word for mortals, “the mortals who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142; see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]), and the leaves are Glaucus' answer to him (6.146–149; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). But Glaucus' leaves are the generations, one growing as another passes away; Apollo's are mortal lives, which now flourish and now fade. ζαφλεγέες, “blaze with life” (21.465; ζα-, “very”, and φλέγω, “blaze”), found only here, puts fire into the flourishing, and ἀκήριοι, “lifeless” (21.466), is the word of Menelaus' curse on the Achaeans who sit “spiritless” rather than face Hector (7.100). Walter Leaf calls the passage an obvious reminiscence of Glaucus' simile, spoiled by the incongruous eating and by leaves that are full of fire, a medley that he thinks only deliberate parody could have produced (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.464; see the translation's note on 21.464–466).

The god draws a conclusion that Glaucus did not: mortals are not worth a quarrel among the gods, “and let them fight it out themselves” (21.467; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]], [[apollo]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Glaucus drew none, and went on to tell his lineage.
