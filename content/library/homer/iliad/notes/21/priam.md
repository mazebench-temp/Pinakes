summary: Old Priam stands on the divine tower and sees the Trojans routed before Achilles; he comes down groaning and orders the gatekeepers to hold the gates open until the army is inside, and then to shut them, for fear that Achilles may leap inside the wall (21.526–536). Earlier in the book his son Lycaon, taken by Achilles in his father's orchard, tells of his mother Laothoe, one of Priam's many wives, and Achilles swears to spare none of the Trojans, “and above all of the sons of Priam” (21.34–105).

**On the tower.** When the gods have gone back to Olympus and Achilles is killing Trojans and horses alike on the plain, the narrative turns to the city:

{{quote:9c67cd04-6574-54fc-b1e3-927538854381}}

θείου ἐπὶ πύργου, “on the divine tower” (21.526; see [[divine]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]): Walter Leaf takes θεῖος as “built by a god”, probably by Poseidon, since it is not a mere synonym of ἱερός, the common epithet of citadels, and compares the “god-built towers” of 8.519 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.526; see [[god-built]]). Earlier in the book Poseidon has reminded Apollo that he built the wall around the city for Laomedon (21.446–447; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[laomedon]]). Priam watched the war from the tower beside the Scaean gates with the elders and Helen in Book 3 (3.145–244; see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]); now he watches a rout. He sees Achilles πελώριον, “huge” (21.527; see [[huge]]), and the Trojans before him “at once driven in confusion, in panic” (21.528; see the translation's note on 21.6–7), and he comes down from the tower “with a groan” (21.529).

**The orders.** The gatekeepers are ἀγακλειτοί, “famous” (21.530; see [[famous]]), and Leaf takes the gates for the Scaean gate, the only one on the side toward the plain of which the poem tells (on 21.531; see [[scaean-gates]]). The gates are to stand open “until the people / come to the city in panic” and are then to be shut again with “the closely fitted doors” (21.531–535; see [[closely-fitted]]). ἐπανθέμεναι, “put … back”, is Aristarchus' reading; some of the city texts had ἐπʼ ἂψ θέμεναι (Leaf on 21.535; see the translation's note on 21.535). Polydamas had said that the gates and their doors would guard the city if the Trojans stayed inside (18.273–276; see [[polydamas]]); the day after, Priam orders them shut behind the routed army. What Priam fears is Achilles himself, οὖλος ἀνήρ, “this baneful man” (21.536; see [[baneful]]), the epithet of Ares and of the lying Dream (5.461, 2.6). “So he spoke, and they opened the gates and pushed back the bars” (21.537), and Apollo leapt out through them to meet the fleeing army (21.538–539; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]).

**His sons.** In this book Achilles kills Lycaon, “a son of Priam, descendant of Dardanus” (21.34; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[dardanus]]), whom he once took by night in “his father's orchard” and sold into Lemnos (21.35–41). Back for eleven days, he has come again into Achilles' hands. He tells of his mother: Laothoe, daughter of Altes, king of the Leleges, “His daughter Priam had for a wife, and many others too” (21.85–88; see [[laothoe]], [[altes]] and [[sons-of-priam]]); his full brother Polydorus has already been killed (21.89–91, 20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). Leaf notes that ἔχε, “had”, is imperfect, though Laothoe is still alive in the next book, because it goes with the time of her bearing him (on 21.88; 22.48–53). Achilles answers that he will spare no Trojan whom a god throws into his hands before Ilios, “no one of all the Trojans, and above all of the sons of Priam” (21.104–105; see [[achilles]]). The river, calling on the Simoeis to help him, fears that Achilles will soon sack “the great city of lord Priam” (21.309; see [[scamander]] and [[troy]]).

**What he does not know.** The Trojans crowd in without waiting to learn who has escaped and who has died (21.608–610). In the next book Priam, looking at the army inside the walls, cannot see Lycaon and Polydorus among them, and hopes to ransom them with the gold and bronze that old Altes gave with his daughter, if they are alive (22.46–53).
