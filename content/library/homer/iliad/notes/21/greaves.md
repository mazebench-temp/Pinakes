**The god's greaves.** These are the greaves Hephaestus made in the night, “of soft tin” (18.613; see [[tin]], [[heanos]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]), and which Achilles put on first when he armed (19.369–370). νεοτεύκτου, “newly wrought”, is found only here in Homer. σμερδαλέον κονάβησε, “rang terribly”, is the sound the bronze on his chest made as he fled the river, σμερδαλέον κονάβιζεν (21.254–255; see [[achilles]]). The last half-verse, θεοῦ δʼ ἠρύκακε δῶρα, takes up what was said of the shield, χρυσὸς γὰρ ἐρύκακε δῶρα θεοῖο, “for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god” (21.165 = 20.268; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). Walter Leaf thinks 21.594 an addition meant to recall the making of the arms, and notes that the manuscript A omits it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.594; see the translation's note on 21.594).

**Aristotle.** Aristotle quotes the half-verse of the greave in the *Poetics*, among words used by the custom of the language: mixed wine is still called wine, and so the poet composed “a greave of newly wrought tin” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a27–28; see [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]]).
