summary: The book states the difference three times. The narrator: “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.264). Hera: it is not fitting “to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.380). Apollo, refusing to fight Poseidon “for the sake of mortals, / wretched creatures, who, like leaves, at one time / blaze with life, eating the fruit of the field, / and at another waste away lifeless” (21.463–466).
refs: [21.264, 21.315, 21.379-380, 21.462-467, 21.568-570]
---

Apollo's leaves recall Glaucus' to Diomedes (6.146–149), and Walter Leaf, who finds the mixture of the two images a medley, thinks it hard to believe that any poet wrote it except in deliberate parody (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.464; see the translation's note on 21.464–466 and [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). The river, a god, fights a mortal and is beaten by a god for him (21.233–384; see [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]), and Agenor sets out the mortal's case against Achilles himself: “there is one life in him, and men say that he / is mortal” (21.569–570; see [[immortals]], [[mortal-men]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]).
