summary: Hermes, set against Leto in the pairs of gods, will not fight her, since it is a hard thing to trade blows with the wives of Zeus, and tells her to boast that she beat him. Leto gathers up the bow and arrows that Hera has beaten out of Artemis' hands and goes after her daughter.

In the pairs of Book 20, “against Leto stood strong Hermes, the great helper” (20.72; see [[hermes]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]), and the pair never fight. ἀλόχοισι Διός, “the wives of Zeus” (21.499): Leto is one of the women Zeus names to Hera among his loves, “nor when I loved glorious Leto” (14.327; see [[zeus]]), and Walter Leaf takes the plural as a general truth that also glances at Hera's triumph a moment before (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.499). She makes no answer, and picks up her daughter's weapons. καμπύλα τόξα, “the curved bow” (21.502), here takes in the arrows too, and μετὰ στροφάλιγγι κονίης, “in the whirl of dust” (21.503), comes from the death of Cebriones (16.775): a mechanical use of a standing epithet and a tasteless borrowing, in Leaf's judgment (on 21.502; see [[bow]] and [[arrows]]). Then she “went back after her daughter” (21.504; see [[artemis]]), a genitive that Leaf finds hard to construe (on 21.504). As in Book 5, where she and Artemis healed Aeneas in Apollo's temple on Pergamus (5.445–448; see [[pergamus]]), she stands by her children (see [[apollo]]).
