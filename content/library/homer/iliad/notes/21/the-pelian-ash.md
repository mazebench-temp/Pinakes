summary: Achilles raises the Pelian ash against Asteropaeus (21.161–162). His cast misses and drives it halfway into the high bank, where Asteropaeus three times tries to draw it out and the fourth time means to bend and break it, until Achilles kills him with the sword and pulls it free (21.169–200). It is the spear he leaves on the bank when he leaps into the river, lifts against Lycaon, and carries toward the city (21.17, 21.67–72, 21.542).

**The cast into the bank.** “So he spoke, threatening, and brilliant Achilles raised up / the Pelian ash” (21.161–162; see [[achilles]] and [[asteropaeus]]). The verse ends as when he lifted his long spear against Lycaon, ἀνέσχετο δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς (21.67). Πηλιάδα μελίην is the spear's full name, always in the accusative (16.143, 19.390, 21.162; 22.133 later) but once, in the cast at Aeneas (Πηλιὰς … μελίη, 20.277). Asteropaeus throws first, two spears at once; then Achilles “let fly his straight-flying ash” (21.169). ἰθυπτίων, “straight-flying”, is found only here; Walter Leaf explains it by Aeneas' words about Achilles, “his weapon flies straight” (20.99), and reports Zenodotus' ἰθυκτίωνα, said to mean “straight-grained”, whose derivation he calls impossible (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.169; see the translation's note on 21.169). The spear misses and strikes the high bank, “and drove the ash spear halfway into the bank” (21.171–172): μεσσοπαγές, “fixed halfway”, where Aristarchus read μεσσοπαλές, which Leaf rejects (on 21.172; see the translation's note on 21.172 and [[ash-spear]]).

**Asteropaeus and the spear.** Achilles draws his sword and leaps at him, “and he could not draw Achilles' ash spear / out of the bank with his strong hand” (21.174–175). Three times he shakes it and three times he “held back from the effort”, in the verses that tell of Telemachus trying to string his father's bow in the Odyssey, from which Bekker restored βίης, “from the effort”, as Leaf approves (21.176–177; *Odyssey* 21.125–126; Leaf on 21.177; see the translation's notes on 21.176 and 21.177). The fourth time he means “to bend and break the ash spear of the grandson of Aeacus” (21.178; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[grandson-of-aeacus]]), but Achilles kills him first with the sword (21.179). No other Achaean could wield the spear (16.141–142, 19.388–389), and the man who tries to break it dies before he can. His boast spoken, Achilles “drew the bronze spear out of the bank” (21.200).

**Earlier and later in the book.** He leaves his spear on the bank, “leaning against the tamarisks”, when he leaps into the river with his sword (21.17–18), and yet he has it when he meets Lycaon (21.67), a discrepancy Leaf puts down to a different author for 21.1–33 (on 21.67). “But come, the point of my spear too / he shall taste”, he says of Lycaon (21.60–61). He lifts it to stab him; it passes over Lycaon's back and stands in the ground, “straining to glut itself on the flesh of a man” (21.68–70), and Lycaon, clasping Achilles' knees with one hand, holds the spear with the other “and would not let it go” (21.72), until Achilles' answer loosens his knees and he lets it fall (21.114–115; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). “Meanwhile the son of Peleus, holding his long-shadowed spear” (21.139), goes against Asteropaeus, and at the end of the book he presses on toward the city “fiercely with his spear” (21.542). In this book the narrator calls it δόρυ, ἔγχος and ἐγχείη, “spear”, and μελίη, “ash” (21.169, 21.174), and gives it its full name once (see [[spear]]).
