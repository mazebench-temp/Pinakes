---
summary: Lycaon's plea to Achilles has all four parts of the scene: he runs in under the spear and clasps Achilles' knees, makes his appeal with one hand on the knees and the other on the spear, and is refused (21.64–119).
passages:
  - 21.64-119 | Lycaon supplicates Achilles
---

**Approach and gesture.** “and the other came near him, dazed, / eager to clasp his knees” (21.64–65); Achilles lifts his spear, but Lycaon runs in under it and takes his knees, “and with the other he held the sharpened spear and would not let it go” (21.68–72; [[sharp]]).

**Appeal.** The verb of the scene opens the speech: “I implore you, Achilles; respect me and pity me” (21.74). The claim is a past bond: Lycaon has eaten at Achilles' table, and so is “as good as a revered suppliant” (21.75–76; see the translation's note on 21.75). A ransom, a genealogy and a plea follow (21.79–96; [[lay-it-to-heart]]).

**Answer.** The refusal is the fullest in the poem: before Patroclus died Achilles spared and sold, “but now there is no one who shall escape death” (21.99–113; [[patroclus-died-too]]). Lycaon lets go of the spear and sits with his arms spread, and Achilles kills him (21.114–119; [[loosened-his-limbs]]). See [[supplication]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]].
