# Unnamed: his orders, his robbery and his threats (21.445, 21.451, 21.453-458).

Poseidon tells the story to shame Apollo, who now helps “his people”, the Trojans (21.458; see [[poseidon]], [[apollo]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). Laomedon's titles here are hostile: ἀγήνωρ, “proud” (21.443; see [[proud]]), and ἔκπαγλος, “that terrible man” (21.452), where Aeneas' genealogy called him “noble” (ἀμύμονα, 20.236) and Poseidon in Book 7 “the hero” (ἥρῳ, 7.453). He was the gods' master: ὃ δὲ σημαίνων ἐπέτελλεν, “he was our master and gave us our orders” (21.445), with σημαίνω, the word of a master's commands (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.445; see [[semaino]]). His threats are a slave-dealer's: to bind their feet and their hands, to sell them off to islands far away, and to lop off their ears (21.453–455; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[trade]]). περάαν, “to sell us off” (21.454), is the verb of Achilles' sale of Lycaon into Lemnos (21.40, 21.58; see the translation's note on 21.40); for τηλεδαπάων, “far away”, the city editions read θηλυτεράων, which Leaf thinks was taken to mean “fertile” (on 21.454); and ἀπολεψέμεν, “lop off”, is Aristarchus' reading for the vulgate ἀποκοψέμεν, “cut off”. Leaf thinks it the more vigorous, as if the gods' bodies were trunks to be lopped (on 21.455), the verb of the scepter that the bronze has stripped of leaves and bark (1.236). The wage he promised and did not pay (21.457; see [[misthos]]) is the fault the poem remembers in him, as with the horses he promised Heracles and did not give (5.649–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]).
