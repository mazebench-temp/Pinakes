summary: Lycaon, unarmed, runs in under Achilles' spear, clasps his knees with one hand and holds the spear with the other, and makes the longest plea for life on the battlefield of the Iliad; Achilles kills him (21.64–119). Later the river, burning, begs Hera to call off her son, and is heard (21.367–382).
# ἐλλίσσετο (21.71), γουνοῦμαι (21.74) and ἱκέταο (21.75) occur in the Iliad only here.

**The gesture held.** No one who begs for his life in the fighting of the Iliad is spared, and the gesture often fails before the words. Menelaus pushes Adrastus away from his knees (6.62–63), Dolon is struck down as he reaches for Diomedes' chin (10.454–457), the sons of Antimachus beg from their chariot (11.130), and Tros never reaches Achilles' knees at all (20.463–469; see [[supplication-type-scene]]). Lycaon runs in under the spear and holds the knees with one hand and the spear with the other, “and would not let it go” (21.72), through a plea of twenty-three verses (21.74–96; see [[gounata]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). He lets go of both only when Achilles has answered him: “he let go of the spear, and sat down, stretching out both / his hands” (21.115–116; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]).

**The plea.** “I implore you, Achilles; respect me and pity me” (21.74; see [[aidos]] and [[pity]]): the verse returns in the Odyssey with Odysseus' name, spoken by the seer Leodes, whom Odysseus kills, and by the singer Phemius, whom he spares (*Odyssey* 22.312, 22.344). Lycaon claims to be “as good as a revered suppliant” (21.75), and gives the reason: “for with you first I tasted the grain of Demeter” (21.76; see [[demeter]]). Walter Leaf explains that to have broken bread under another man's roof was claim enough to a suppliant's standing, though Achilles had never accepted him as one (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.75). He names the price he once fetched and the ransom that freed him (21.79–80), not the treasure in his father's house that Adrastus, Dolon and the sons of Antimachus offered (6.46–50, 10.378–381, 11.131–135), and Achilles hears in it an offer of ransom: “do not offer me ransom” (21.99; see [[apoina]]). The answer is death, and the reason Achilles gives is Patroclus' death and his own (21.99–113; see [[patroclus-died-too]]).

**A god's plea.** Later in the book a god begs a goddess, and is heard. The river, burning in Hephaestus' fire, speaks to Hera, “begging her earnestly” (21.368): he will cease if she bids him, and he will swear never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, not even when all Troy burns. Hera calls off her son, for “it is not fitting / to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.369–380; see [[scamander]], [[hera]] and [[hephaestus]]).
