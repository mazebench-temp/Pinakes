summary: The river of Troy is eddying in the book's first verses (21.2), in Achilles' boast over Lycaon, “but Scamander / will carry you, eddying, into the broad bosom of the sea” (21.124–125), as the Paeonians flee “along the eddying river” (21.206), and in Hera's call to Hephaestus (21.332).
grc: [δινήεντα, δινήεις]
---

The book begins with the couplet that brought the fallen Hector to the ford in Book 14: “But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river, / the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (21.1–2 = 14.433–434; again 24.692–693). The ford is named only in this verse (see the translation's note on 21.1–2 and [[fair-flowing]]).

The book is full of the river's eddies. The Trojans whirl around in them (21.11), the horses thrown in alive are sunk in them (21.132), the river hides the living in them (21.239) and speaks out of one (21.213), and the fishes suffer in them when Hephaestus' fire comes (21.353). Its epithets are made from the same word: eddying, [[deep-eddying]] six times, and [[silver-eddying]] twice. Hera's words, “we judged eddying Xanthus to be matched in battle” (21.332), recall the pairing of fire and water in the battle of the gods (20.73–74). See [[scamander]] and [[rivers]].
