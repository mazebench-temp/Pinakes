summary: Lycaon asks for it, “I implore you, Achilles; respect me and pity me” (21.74), and Achilles, who once spared Trojans, has none to give: “No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this?” (21.106). The narrator says it of him in the river, where he cut down the young men “and did not pity them” (21.147), and Achilles himself, trapped by the river, complains that no god has undertaken “to save me in my misery” (21.273).

Before Patroclus died, Achilles says, it was “dearer to my heart to spare / Trojans” (21.101–102), and many he took alive and sold (see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). The φίλος of his answer, “friend”, was heard by the scholia as mockery, and by Walter Leaf as a sudden real pity for his victim (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.106; see the translation's note on 21.106–107 and [[philos]]). The river is angry for the young men whom Achilles did not pity (21.146–147; see [[cholos]] and [[supplication]]).
