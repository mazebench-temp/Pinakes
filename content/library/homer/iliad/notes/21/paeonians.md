# Unnamed: those who fled when they saw their best man fall (21.206-208), and the seven he killed (21.209-210).

**Spearmen and charioteers.** Asteropaeus tells Achilles that he has come from Paeonia “leading the Paeonian men with their long spears” (21.155; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[paeonia]]). In the Catalogue they were archers, the Paeonians “with their curved bows”, and Pyraechmes led them (2.848; see [[with-curved-bows]] and [[pyraechmes]]). Walter Leaf notes the difference, and that δολιχεγχέες is found only here, though the Thracians too hold long spears (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.154; 4.533; see [[thracians]]). When Achilles turns on them they are Παίονας ἱπποκορυστάς, “the Paeonians who fight from chariots” (21.205), as when Patroclus killed Pyraechmes (16.287).

**The rout.** Leaf notes that these Paeonians have not been named before in the book, and presumes that they were with their leader, and among those whom Achilles drove into the river at its beginning (on 21.206; 21.7–11). When Pyraechmes fell, “his companions around him fled in rout, / the Paeonians” (16.290–291). So now, at the death of their second leader, they are in rout along the river, “when they saw their best man in the fierce battle / brought down by force at the hands of the son of Peleus and by his sword” (21.207–208; see [[aristos]]).

**Seven names.** Achilles kills seven of them, named in two verses without a father, a wound or a word (21.209–210; see [[thersilochus]], [[mydon-the-paeonian]], [[astypylus]], [[mnesus]], [[thrasius]], [[aenius]] and [[ophelestes-the-paeonian]]). The verses are built like Odysseus' killing of seven Lycians in Book 5, two verses of names followed by the same verse of what would have been: “And brilliant Odysseus would have killed still more of the Lycians” (5.677–679), “and swift Achilles would have killed still more of the Paeonians” (21.211; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[androktasia]]). There Hector was quick to see it (5.680); here the river, in anger, speaks to Achilles from a deep eddy, and the slaughter of the Trojans and their allies in his streams becomes his complaint (21.212–221; see [[scamander]]).
