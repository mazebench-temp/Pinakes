---
summary: Achilles would have killed still more Paeonians, “had not the deep-eddying river spoken to him in anger” (21.211–212); and the Achaeans would have taken Troy, “had not Phoebus Apollo set brilliant Agenor on” (21.544–545).
---

**The river.** “and swift Achilles would have killed still more of the Paeonians, / had not the deep-eddying river spoken to him in anger” (21.211–212). The turn brings in a god, as so often, but this god speaks and pleads before he fights (21.214–221; [[deep-eddying]], [[swift-footed]]).

**The city.** “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy, / had not Phoebus Apollo set brilliant Agenor on” (21.544–545). The verses are those of Patroclus' assault, “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy / … had not Phoebus Apollo” (16.698–700; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.544). The same god stops the city's fall at the hands of Patroclus, standing on the well-built tower, and at the hands of Achilles, this time through a man: Agenor stands, and Apollo, in his likeness, draws Achilles away until the Trojans are inside (21.545–611; [[agenor]], [[high-gated]], [[apollo]]).
