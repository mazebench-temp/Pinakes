Apollo's answer to Poseidon is the other half of his warning to Diomedes. In Book 5 he told a mortal not to think himself the gods' equal (5.440–442); in Book 21 he tells a god that mortals are not worth fighting for: “Shaker of the earth, you would not call me sound of mind / if I should fight with you for the sake of mortals” (21.462–463; see [[apollo]] and [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). The two scenes are one thought: men are not the gods' equals, and they are not worth a quarrel between gods. Hera has just given Hephaestus the same reason for sparing the river: “it is not fitting / to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.379–380; see [[hera]]), and Hephaestus gave it in Book 1 to make peace between his parents, “if you two quarrel like this for the sake of mortals” (1.574).

The book also states the warning in its own terms. When the wave keeps overtaking Achilles, the narrator explains, “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.264; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]), and the river calls on Simoeis to stop the man who now prevails and “is bent on being the equal of the gods” (21.315; see [[scamander]]): μέμονεν δʼ ὅ γε ἶσα θεοῖσι, the thing that Apollo forbade Diomedes, ἶσʼ ἔθελε φρονέειν (5.441). Achilles comes out of the river because two gods stand by him (21.284–297; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]), not because he is their equal.
