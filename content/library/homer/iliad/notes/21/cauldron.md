summary: The cauldron of Book 21 is in a simile, on the fire: burned by Hephaestus, the river's streams boil like a cauldron that melts a hog's fat over a great fire (21.362–365).
# λέβης, the nominative (21.362), only here in the Iliad.
grc: [λέβης]
en: [cauldron]
---

“As a cauldron boils within, pressed by a great fire, / as it melts the fat of a tender-fed hog” (21.362–363; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad the cauldron is above all a treasure, counted with tripods and gold: Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles include “twenty gleaming cauldrons” (9.123; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]), and the prizes of the funeral games include a cauldron “untouched by fire”, new and never set over a flame (ἄπυρον, 23.267, 23.885). Here it is in use, over “dry sticks” on a hearth, as the tripod is when water is heated on the blazing fire to wash the body of Patroclus (21.364; 18.344–348; see [[tripod]]). The *Odyssey* uses the same likeness for Charybdis seething “like a cauldron on a great fire” (λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ, *Odyssey* 12.237). The vessel of the kitchen measures the god's defeat: Hephaestus is the fire under the pot, and the river, “burning with fire”, is the water that boils (21.361; see [[hephaestus]], [[fire]] and [[scamander]]).
