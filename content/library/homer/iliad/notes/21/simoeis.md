summary: The Scamander, unable to hold Achilles back, calls on the Simoeis as his “dear brother” to fill his streams and raise a great wave of logs and stones against him (21.305–323). The Simoeis does not answer.
passages:
  - 21.305-327 | The river calls on the Simoeis
---

When Athena has put great strength into Achilles and he rushes on against the stream, the Scamander does not give up (21.299–306; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]):

{{quote:dcb958d3-e8e4-5b46-a304-ca7a6e9a90be}}

φίλε κασίγνητε, “Dear brother” (21.308; see [[kasignetos]]). The two rivers of the Trojan plain join their streams in front of the city (5.773–774), and they are named together as the bounds of the fighting, “between the streams of the Simoeis and the Xanthus” (6.4), and among the rivers of Ida that will wash away the Achaean wall (12.21–22). Hesiod makes them brothers in fact, as children of Tethys and Oceanus, naming “divine Simoeis” and “divine Scamander” in the same catalogue of rivers (*Theogony* 337–345; see [[hesiod]] and [[rivers]]).

The Scamander asks his brother to bring all his water: to fill his streams “with water from your springs, and spur on all your torrents” (21.312). ἔναυλος is a gully or the torrent that runs down it (16.71; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.283), and Achilles has just pictured his own death as a swineherd boy's, swept away by such a torrent in winter (21.282–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]]). With logs and stones in the flood the two rivers would stop “this savage man”, and bury him where he falls (21.314–323).

The Simoeis does not come. Leaf finds the mention of him perfunctory, as we should expect from his rare appearances in the Iliad, and notes that the appeal goes unanswered, like the river's appeal to Apollo (on 21.307; 21.229–232). The Simoeis was not among the gods who went down to the war in Book 20, where only the Xanthus is named (20.40; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Hera sends Hephaestus against the Scamander instead, and the river that called for a greater flood is burned (21.328–384; see [[hephaestus]]).
