---
summary: Ares remembers his wound when he attacks Athena: “Do you not remember when you set Diomedes, son of Tydeus, on / to stab me, and you yourself took the spear seen by all / and drove it straight at me, and tore through my fair skin?” (21.396–398). Now the gods wound one another with no mortal between them: Athena fells Ares with a stone and strikes Aphrodite down with her hand (21.403–425), and Hera beats Artemis about the ears with her own bow (21.489–492).
refs: [21.391-399, 21.403-426, 21.489-496]
---

Ares remembers the blow as Athena's own. In Book 5 Diomedes thrust and Athena drove the spear home, and it “tore through the fair skin” (5.855–858); here she took the spear herself, and it was “seen by all”, where in Book 5 she had made herself invisible to him (5.844–845; see the translation's note on 21.397). His threat that she will pay for it ends with his own fall (21.399–407; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

A mortal fights a god in this book too, and neither is wounded. Achilles leaps into the river, and Scamander, who speaks to him “in the likeness of a man” (21.213), cannot drown him while Poseidon and Athena stand by (21.233–304); the river says that Achilles “is bent on being the equal of the gods” (21.315), the thought that Apollo forbade Diomedes, “and do not wish / to think yourself equal to the gods” (5.440–441). The god who suffers is the river, burned by Hephaestus until Hera calls it unfitting “to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.380; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). The beaten goddess is comforted as Aphrodite was: Artemis sits on her father's knees, and he asks her Dione's question, “Who of the heavenly gods has done such things to you, dear child” (21.509 = 5.373; see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).
