# The question (21.150), the lineage (21.157-160) and Achilles' answering lineage (21.187-189).

**The meeting.** The scene follows the meeting of Diomedes and Glaucus step by step (see the entry). The two come close and Achilles speaks first, in the verses of his meeting with Aeneas (21.148–149 = 20.176–177; see [[when-they-came-close]]); his question and threat are Diomedes' (6.123, 6.127 = 21.151; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]); and Asteropaeus answers in the verse of Glaucus' answer, with “son of Pelegon” for “son of Hippolochus”, and questions the question in Glaucus' words, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (21.152–153 ≈ 6.144–145; see [[genee]]). Walter Leaf calls the dialogue evidently modeled on that of Glaucus and Diomedes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.153). τίς πόθεν εἰς ἀνδρῶν, “Who are you among men, and from where”, joins two questions in one, as in the Odyssey's question to a stranger (*Odyssey* 1.170), and Leaf thinks it possible that πόθεν asks not from what place but of what father (on 21.150).

**The answer.** Glaucus turned first to the generations of leaves (6.146–149), and Aeneas said that each knew the other's lineage already (20.203–205). Asteropaeus answers at once: where he comes from, how long he has been at Troy, and his descent from the river (21.154–160; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[axius]]). It is the shortest of the three genealogies, two generations in four verses, against the six generations of Glaucus' and the seven of Aeneas' (6.152–206, 20.215–240), and it is told in the words of other river lineages: ἐμοὶ γενεὴ ἐξ Ἀξιοῦ εὐρὺ ῥέοντος … ὃς τέκε Πηλεγόνα (21.157–159), as Diocles' γένος was ἐκ ποταμοῖο Ἀλφειοῦ, ὅς τʼ εὐρὺ ῥέει … ὃς τέκετʼ Ὀρτίλοχον (5.544–546; see [[pelegon]] and [[diocles]]). Like Aeneas' speech, it ends in the call to fight: “Now let us fight, shining Achilles” (21.160; 20.257–258).

**The answering lineage.** In Book 6 the lineage ended the fight before it began, and in Book 20 it was followed by the spears. Here the fight is short, and Achilles answers the lineage over the dead man: “You say that you are of the lineage of a broad-flowing river, / but I claim to be of the lineage of great Zeus” (21.186–187; see [[euchomai]]). His own line runs up in two verses, through Peleus and Aeacus to Zeus, in the language of genealogies, τίκτέ μʼ ἀνὴρ πολλοῖσιν ἀνάσσων Μυρμιδόνεσσι (21.188), as Idomeneus said, “and Deucalion fathered me, a lord over many men” (13.452; see [[idomeneus]], [[aeacus]] and [[myrmidons]]). The two claims are alike: Idomeneus too was a great-grandson of Zeus, and gave his line unasked over a man he had killed (13.446–454). Achilles draws from his the rule that settles the contest of lineages, as Zeus is mightier than rivers, so the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's (21.190–191; see [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]).

**Turned to war.** In the battle of the gods Hera takes the first verse of Glaucus' and Aeneas' couplet, “But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well / our lineage” (6.150–151 = 20.213–214), and turns it on Artemis: “But if you wish to learn of war, so that you may know well / how much stronger I am, since you match your might with mine…” (21.487–488; see [[hera]], [[artemis]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). The lesson is not a lineage but a beating: she takes the bow from Artemis' shoulders and beats her about the ears with it (21.489–492).
