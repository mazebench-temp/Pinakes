summary: At the ford of the Xanthus he splits the Trojans, leaps into the river with only his sword, and takes twelve young men alive as the penalty for Patroclus. He kills Lycaon, son of Priam, who clasps his knees, telling him that he too must die, and Asteropaeus, grandson of a river, boasting of his descent from Zeus. The river, choked with his dead, rises against him and chases him over the plain; he cries to Zeus that he will die a wretched death, Poseidon and Athena promise him that the river will not bring him down, and Hera sends Hephaestus' fire against it. Priam sees him from the tower and opens the gates; Agenor's javelin rings on his greave, and Apollo, in Agenor's likeness, draws him away from the city (21.1–611).

**At the ford.** When the fleeing Trojans reach the ford of the Xanthus, “there he split them, and some he drove across the plain / toward the city, where the Achaeans had been fleeing in terror / the day before, when shining Hector raged” (21.3–5; see [[scamander]] and [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]). The other half are penned into the river, and “so before Achilles deep-eddying Xanthus' / sounding stream was filled with horses and men, all mingled together” (21.15–16), as locusts driven by a fire cower down into the water (21.12–14; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]]). Twice the Catalogue said of a Trojan ally that he was “brought down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus / in the river” (2.860–861, 2.874–875; see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]); neither man is named in this book.

**Only his sword.**

{{quote:fbd41494-d6d6-5af0-9bd8-a551779a459c}}

He leaps in “like a god” (δαίμονι ἶσος, 21.18), as he rushed on the mist that hid Hector and through the Trojans like a fire (20.447, 20.493), and as he will rush on them again when he has answered the river (21.227; see [[like-a-god]]). The half-verse κακὰ δὲ φρεσὶ μήδετο ἔργα, “he devised evil deeds in his mind” (21.19), is said once more in the poem, when he kills the twelve young men at Patroclus' pyre (23.176). Walter Leaf, whose note here refers to that line, denies that κακά passes a moral judgment on Achilles, which would be against the epic style: the word means only that what he did was ill work for his victims, and no one condemns Zeus when “all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil for them” (7.478; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.19, 23.176). The next two verses are those of Diomedes killing the sleeping Thracians, with “struck” for “killed” and “water” for “earth” (21.20–21 = 10.483–484; see [[the-doloneia]] and [[sword]]). As the other fishes flee a huge-bellied dolphin into the recesses of a harbor, the Trojans cower under the banks (21.22–26; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]).

**The twelve.** “And when his hands grew weary with killing, / he chose twelve young men out of the river alive, / as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (21.26–28; see [[poine]] and [[patroclus]]). He vowed them over Patroclus' body: “before your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve / splendid children of the Trojans, in my anger over your killing” (18.336–337), and he will kill them at the pyre (23.175–176; see [[human-sacrifice]]). He leads them out “dazed, like fawns” (21.29), in the words with which Agamemnon reproached the Argives who hung back (4.243; see [[like-fawns]]), binds their hands behind them with the straps they wore on their own tunics, and gives them to his companions to take to the ships (21.30–32; see [[well-cut]]). “But he sprang at them again, eager to cut men down” (21.33).

**Lycaon.** Then he meets Lycaon, son of Priam, whom he once caught by night in his father's orchard cutting shoots from a wild fig tree for the rails of a chariot, and sold into Lemnos. Ransomed, Lycaon has been home eleven days, and on the twelfth “a god threw him into the hands of Achilles” (21.34–48; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Achilles sees him unarmed and spent, and speaks to himself in the two verses with which he wondered at Aeneas' escape (21.53–54 = 20.343–344): the Trojans he has killed will rise again from the darkness, now that this man has come back from Lemnos. “But come, the point of my spear too / he shall taste”, to learn whether the earth will hold him, “which holds back even the strong” (21.60–63). κεῖθεν, “from there” (21.62), is the world of the dead, by a euphemism (Leaf on 21.62).

He lifts his spear to stab him, and Lycaon runs in under it and clasps his knees (21.67–72; see [[supplication]]). At 21.17 Achilles left his spear on the bank; Leaf takes the discrepancy for a sign that 21.1–33 are by another hand (on 21.67). Lycaon begs, “for with you first I tasted the grain of Demeter” (21.76), and he is the son of Laothoe, not of Hector's mother: “do not kill me” (21.74–96). He describes himself in Achilles' own word, μινυνθάδιον, “to be short-lived” (21.84), with which Achilles spoke to Thetis in the first book, “Mother, since you bore me to be short-lived” (1.352; see [[short-lived]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Achilles answers:

{{quote:ceddbe41-0dee-5425-8a1b-b5db243d83d6}}

Before Patroclus died he took captives and sold them. Lycaon himself he sold into Lemnos (21.40, 21.78–79); Isus and Antiphus he “had bound with pliant willow shoots on the slopes of Ida” and released for a ransom (11.104–106), and Andromache's mother he set free, “taking a boundless ransom” (6.427); Hecuba will say that he sold her sons beyond the sea, to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos (24.751–753; see [[achilles-raids]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[apoina]]). In the last book Tros, son of Alastor, came to clasp his knees in the hope of being taken alive, and died by his sword (20.463–469). Now: “No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this? / Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). In φίλος, “friend”, the scholia saw mockery of Lycaon's claim to hospitality; Leaf sees a sudden change of mood to real pity for his victim, “a far finer touch” (on 21.106). He sets his own death beside Lycaon's. He is handsome and tall, the son of a brave father and of a goddess, “yet over me too hang death and mighty fate”, and a morning or an evening or a midday will come when someone takes his life, “striking me either with a spear or with an arrow from the string” (21.108–113; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, “death and mighty fate”, ends the verse in which the dying Patroclus foretold Hector's death (16.853), and Thetis will say it to her son in the last book (24.132; see [[mighty-fate]]).

Lycaon lets go of the spear and sits with both hands spread, and Achilles, “drawing his sharp sword, / struck him on the collarbone beside the neck” (21.116–117; see [[sword]]). He throws the body into the river by the foot and boasts over it: the fishes will lick the blood from the wound, no mother will lay him on a bier and lament, and Scamander will carry him into the sea (21.120–127), as Hecuba fears that she will not weep for Hector on a bier (22.86–87; see [[lament]]). The river will not save the Trojans, though they have long sacrificed bulls to it and thrown horses alive into its eddies, and they will perish until all of them “pay for the slaughter of Patroclus and the ruin of the Achaeans, / whom you killed beside the swift ships while I was away” (21.130–135; see [[rivers]]). Aristophanes athetized the six verses as added by readers who did not see why the river grows angry (see the translation's note on 21.130–135). The river, all the more angry, ponders how to stop him (21.136–138).

**Asteropaeus.** “Meanwhile the son of Peleus, holding his long-shadowed spear, / leapt at Asteropaeus, eager to kill him” (21.139–140; see [[asteropaeus]]). His question ends in Diomedes' verse to Glaucus: “Who are you among men, and from where, that you have dared to come against me? / It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (21.150–151; 21.151 = 6.127), and Asteropaeus' answer begins as Glaucus' did (21.153; 6.145). Leaf calls the dialogue evidently modeled on that of Glaucus and Diomedes (on 21.153; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]). Asteropaeus throws two spears at once. One strikes the shield and does not break it, “for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god” (21.165 = 20.268; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]); the other grazes the forearm of his right arm, “and the dark blood spurted” (21.166–167). It is the only wound Achilles takes in the poem (see [[the-aristeia]]). His own cast misses and drives the ash spear halfway into the high bank (21.169–172; see [[the-pelian-ash]]). He draws his sword “from beside his thigh” (21.173), as in the quarrel he pondered whether to draw it on Agamemnon (1.190), and while Asteropaeus tries three times to pull the spear from the bank and the fourth time means to bend and break it, “Achilles took his life with his sword at close quarters” (21.179), as he would have taken Aeneas' had Poseidon not been quick to see (20.290).

He springs on the dying man's chest, strips him, and speaks in triumph (21.182–183; 21.183 = 13.619). It is hard to strive with the children of Zeus, even for one sprung from a river: Asteropaeus claims the lineage of a broad-flowing river, “but I claim to be of the lineage of great Zeus. / The man who begot me is lord over many Myrmidons, / Peleus, son of Aeacus; and Aeacus was the son of Zeus” (21.187–189; see [[peleus]], [[aeacus]] and [[myrmidons]]). It is the only place in the poem where he traces his line back to Zeus, and the only place where Aeacus is named in his own person. Zeus is mightier than rivers, and not even Achelous or deep-flowing Oceanus can match him; even Oceanus fears his thunderbolt (21.190–199; see [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]], [[oceanus]] and [[thunderbolt]]). He draws the bronze spear out of the bank and leaves the body in the sand for the eels and the fishes (21.200–204).

**The river speaks.** He kills seven of the Paeonians, who have fled at the sight of their leader's death (21.205–210; see [[paeonians]]), until the river, “in the likeness of a man”, calls to him from a deep eddy: “Achilles, beyond all men you are strong, and beyond men you do evil; / for the gods themselves are always there to defend you” (21.213–215; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). Aeneas told Apollo the same of him: “for always at his side is one of the gods, who keeps ruin away” (20.98). Let him do his grim deeds on the plain, the river says, for its streams are full of corpses (21.216–221). Achilles answers, “So it shall be, Scamander, nurtured by Zeus, as you bid”, but he will not stop killing Trojans “until I have penned them in the city and made trial of Hector / face to face, whether he will bring me down or I him” (21.223–226). What he grants is not plain. Leaf weighs two readings: that the river sets a trap to get him into the water and he falls into it, which reads a great deal into the text, and that the river speaks in irony and Achilles answers irony with irony, which makes severe demands on a hearer's imagination; he looks for the explanation elsewhere (on 21.223; see the translation's note on 21.223). “So saying, he rushed on the Trojans like a god” (21.227). Plato's Socrates counts it among the things not to be believed of Achilles that he disobeyed the river, though it was a god, and was ready to fight it (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]).

**The river attacks.** When the river has reproached Apollo (21.228–232), “Achilles, famed for his spear, leapt into the midst of him, / springing down from the bank” (21.233–234; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]). The river throws out the dead onto the dry land, “bellowing like a bull”, and hides the living in his eddies (21.235–239; see [[bulls]]); his wave falls on Achilles' shield and pushes him back, and he cannot keep his footing (21.240–242). He catches an elm, which tears away the bank and bridges the river, leaps out of the eddy and runs over the plain in fear, and the great god rises after him, “darkening at the crest, so that he might stop from his toil / brilliant Achilles” (21.242–250). He springs away “with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter, / which is at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds”, the bronze ringing terribly on his chest (21.251–256; see [[like-an-eagle]]), but the wave overtakes him as water outruns the man who leads a channel through his garden, “nimble though he was; for the gods are stronger than men” (21.257–264; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]). As often as he turns to stand against it “and learn whether all / the immortals were putting him to flight”, the wave beats on his shoulders and the river eats the ground from under his feet (21.265–271).

**The cry to Zeus.**

{{quote:1767ee48-388e-5e57-8083-fbcbc24190f5}}

The first verse is Menelaus' when his sword broke on Paris' helmet, with the son of Peleus for the son of Atreus, and both men go on, “Father Zeus” (21.272 = 3.364; see [[zeus]] and [[prayer]]). τι πάθοιμι, “I could suffer anything” (21.274), is the Attic “die”: Leaf explains, let come what may, if only he is saved from a shameful death by drowning (on 21.274; see the translation's note on 21.274). He blames his mother, “who bewitched me with lies” (21.276), for her word that he would perish under the wall by the arrows of Apollo (21.277–278; see [[thetis]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). ἔθελγεν, “bewitched”, comes back at the end of the book, when Apollo draws him away from the city: “for by a trick Apollo was bewitching him” (21.604). He would rather Hector had killed him, “the best man bred here” (21.279). The next verse, “But now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (21.281), is the last of Odysseus' lament in the storm at sea, where he wishes that he had died on the day the Trojans hurled their spears at him over the dead son of Peleus, for then he would have had funeral rites and the Achaeans would have spread his fame (*Odyssey* 5.306–312; 21.281 = *Odyssey* 5.312). Both men, facing death in the water, would have chosen death in battle (see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Achilles ends with a swineherd boy swept away by a winter torrent (21.282–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]]).

**Poseidon's promise.** “So he spoke, and very swiftly Poseidon and Athena / came and stood near him, in form like men, / and taking his hand in their hands they pledged their faith with words” (21.284–286; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]).

{{quote:5d8420dc-2677-5dfe-8aaa-15391cb38186}}

The verse in which Poseidon names himself and Athena (21.290) was athetized, because a god in a man's shape should not say who he is; Leaf answers that Achilles must know that the help is divine, and that a god takes a man's form because his own brightness is dangerous to mortal eyes, “for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (on 21.290; 20.131; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]] and the translation's note on 21.290). The counsel answers what Achilles told the river, that he would not stop “until I have penned them in the city and made trial of Hector” (21.225): the god bids him not stop “until you have penned the army within the glorious walls of Ilios”, and “when you have taken Hector's life, / go back to the ships” (21.295–297). εὖχος ἀρέσθαι, “that you win a triumph” (21.297), is what the army asked of Zeus for Ajax before his duel with Hector, “grant victory to Ajax, and that he win a splendid triumph” (7.203; see [[euchomai]]). The command of the gods drives him on, “and his knees leapt high / as he rushed straight on against the stream, nor did the broad-flowing river / hold him back; for Athena had put great strength in him” (21.302–304).

**The river's threat.** Scamander calls on his brother Simoeis to help him stop “this savage man, / who now prevails, and is bent on being the equal of the gods” (21.314–315; see [[simoeis]]), the thing Apollo forbade Diomedes: “do not wish / to think yourself equal to the gods” (5.440–441; Leaf on 21.315). Neither his strength nor his looks nor his fine armor will help him (21.316–317), as Hector told Paris that his lyre, his hair and his looks would be no help in the dust (3.54–55). The river will wrap him in sand and silt, so that the Achaeans will not know how to gather his bones: “Right there his mound shall be made, and he will have no need / of a heaped-up tomb, when the Achaeans hold his funeral” (21.322–323; see [[tomb]]). Leaf reports Friedländer's view that 320–321 and 322–323 are two variants joined together, and Monro's reading of the last words, that he will need no mound because he will have no burial (on 21.320, 21.323). The wave rears up and is bringing him down (21.324–327), and Hera cries out, “terribly afraid for Achilles, / lest the great deep-eddying river sweep him away” (21.328–329; see [[hera]]), and sends Hephaestus, whose fire dries the plain and boils the river until it yields and asks that “brilliant Achilles at once / drive them out of their city” (21.330–384, 21.359–360; see [[hephaestus]]). Achilles has no part in the battle of the gods that follows (21.385–513; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

**Before the gates.** “But Achilles / was killing the Trojans themselves and their single-hoofed horses alike” (21.520–521), and his slaughter is likened to the smoke of a burning city: “so Achilles brought toil and sorrows on the Trojans” (21.522–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]). Old Priam on the divine tower “caught sight of huge Achilles” (21.527; see [[priam]]): πελώριος, “huge”, is the word for Ajax and for Ares (3.229, 7.208; see [[huge]]), and Achilles is πελώριος again when Hector waits for him (22.92). Priam has the gatekeepers hold the gates open, “for I fear that this baneful man may leap inside the wall” (21.536); οὖλος, “baneful”, is said of Ares (5.461, 5.717; see [[baneful]]). Achilles presses on fiercely with his spear, “and a mighty frenzy / held his heart unceasingly, and he was eager to win glory” (21.542–543; see [[kydos]]). λύσσα, “frenzy”, is what Odysseus said had entered Hector in the night of the embassy (9.239, 9.305). “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy” (21.544), the verse said of Patroclus at the wall (16.698), had Apollo not set Agenor on (21.545–546; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Agenor.** Agenor waits for him and weighs flight, but he will be caught, “for he is far too strong, beyond all men” (21.566). He takes heart: “For surely his flesh too can be pierced by the sharp bronze, / and there is one life in him, and men say that he / is mortal; but Zeus, son of Cronus, grants him glory” (21.568–570; see [[agenor]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]). Leaf notes that the invulnerability of Achilles is a purely post-Homeric legend (on 21.568); Aeneas said that Achilles would not easily win, “not even if he boasts that he is all of bronze” (20.102). Agenor's taunt calls him “you little fool” for hoping to sack the city that day (21.583–585; see [[nepios]]). His javelin strikes Achilles' shin below the knee, “And around him the greave of newly wrought tin / rang terribly” (21.592–593), and “the gifts of the god held it back” (21.594; see [[greaves]] and [[tin]]).

**Apollo's trick.** “Then the son of Peleus rushed at godlike Agenor / in his turn; but Apollo no longer allowed him to win glory” (21.595–596). Apollo snatches Agenor away in thick mist, as he snatched Hector from him in the last book (21.597; 20.443–444; see [[divine-rescue]]), and in Agenor's likeness “by a trick he kept the son of Peleus away from the people” (21.599), running a little ahead of him along the Scamander, “so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet” (21.605), while the Trojans crowd into the city (21.606–611; see [[apollo]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). At the start of the next book the god turns and asks why a mortal chases an immortal, and Achilles answers that Apollo has robbed him of great glory (22.7–20).
