summary: When Hera sends Hephaestus against the river, she promises to raise the West Wind and the brightening South Wind from the sea, a hard storm to carry the fire against the heads and the armor of the Trojans (21.334–337). The storm is never sent.

---

{{quote:c5c56b9b-c8aa-535e-8660-47f10bab8cb4}}

Hera's part in the fight with the river is to be the wind behind the fire: Hephaestus is to burn the trees of the banks and the river himself, and the West and the South Wind are to carry the blaze over the Trojans (21.331–341; see [[hera]], [[hephaestus]] and [[fire]]). The two winds are the pair of the simile of Book 11, where the West Wind batters “the clouds / of the brightening South Wind” (11.305–306; see [[notus]] and [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]]). εἴσομαι, “I will go”, is taken by Walter Leaf from the verb of hastening, ϝίεμαι; Zenodotus understood it as “I shall know”, reading “whether” in the next verse (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.335; see the translation's note on 21.335). Leaf notes that Hera takes no steps to fulfil her promise. The only wind in the episode is the North Wind of a simile, drying an orchard as the fire dries the plain (21.346–349; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]] and [[boreas]]).

Winds and fire meet again at the pyre of Patroclus, which will not burn until Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind; Iris fetches them from a feast in the house of Zephyrus, and they blow on the pyre all night (23.192–230; see [[pyres]]).
