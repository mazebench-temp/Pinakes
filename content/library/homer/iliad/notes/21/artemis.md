summary: Artemis taunts her brother for yielding to Poseidon. Hera, calling her a shameless dog and a lion to women, seizes her wrists and beats her about the ears with her own bow, until she flees in tears like a dove and leaves the bow behind. Leto gathers it up, and Artemis goes up to Olympus and sits weeping on her father's knees; Zeus laughs and asks who has done this, and she blames Hera.

**The taunt.** Set against Hera in Book 20 (20.70–71), Artemis turns first on her own brother:

{{quote:449fda94-786e-577c-a4bb-6f352e94c58e}}

πότνια θηρῶν, “the mistress of wild beasts”, is said only here in Homer (see [[queenly]], [[wild]] and the translation's row on πότνια θηρῶν, Ἄρτεμις ἀγροτέρη). Aristonicus reports that the second verse was athetized as superfluous, since no other hunting goddess could be meant; Walter Leaf thinks it may be a gloss, but quite inoffensive (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.471). ἀνεμώλιον, “as empty as the wind” (21.474), is the word of Pandarus' complaint that his bow “goes with me as empty wind” (ἀνεμώλια, 5.216; Leaf on 21.473; see [[bow]]), and μέλεον, “for nothing” (21.473), is an adverb (see the translation's note on 21.473). Apollo says nothing (21.478; see [[apollo]]).

**Hera's answer.** “but the revered wife of Zeus, in anger, / rebuked her of the showering arrows with abusive words” (21.479–480; see [[hera]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]); Leaf notes that the second verse was unknown to Eustathius and is adapted from 2.277 (on 21.480). Hera calls her “you shameless dog” (21.481; see [[shameless-dog]]), an archer whom Zeus has made a lion only to women, with leave to kill whichever of them she wishes (21.483–484; see [[a-lion-to-women]]), and who would do better “to slay wild beasts on the mountains / and wild deer” (21.485–486; see [[deer]]). The *Hymn to Aphrodite* gives her the same pleasures, the bow and the killing of wild beasts in the mountains (τόξα καὶ οὔρεσι θῆρας ἐναίρειν, *Hymn to Aphrodite* 18); Aristarchus remarked that ἐναίρειν, literally to despoil a slain man of his arms (ἔναρα), is not properly used of killing beasts (Leaf on 21.485). Then Hera breaks off and acts: she catches both wrists in her left hand, takes the bow from her shoulders with her right and beats her about the ears with it, smiling, “as she twisted this way and that; and the swift arrows fell out” (21.487–492). ἐντροπαλιζομένην, “as she twisted”, is Artemis turning her head from the blows (Leaf on 21.492). The archer who came to the war “of the showering arrows” (20.71) is disarmed with her own weapons, and flees in tears “like a dove / that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock”, leaving the bow behind (21.493–496; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]).

**On her father's knees.** Leto picks up the bow and arrows and follows her daughter (21.502–504; see [[leto]]), and Artemis comes to Olympus:

{{quote:7eebefae-5667-5c1d-9ec2-1122dcf63bf2}}

She is “the well-crowned, the clamorous goddess” (21.511; see [[well-crowned]] and [[clamorous]]), κελαδεινή standing alone for her name (Leaf on 21.511). Zeus' question is Dione's to Aphrodite, wounded by Diomedes (21.509–510 = 5.373–374; see [[aphrodite]], [[dione]] and [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]); Leaf thinks the whole scene founded on the wounding of Aphrodite, and notes that almost all the manuscripts omit 510 here (on 21.509). In Book 5 Aphrodite fell on her mother's knees, and Dione took her in her arms, comforted her and wiped the ichor from her hand (5.370–417); here the daughter sits on her father's knees, and the father laughs (21.506–508; see [[zeus]]). Her answer gives Hera the blame for the whole battle, “from whom strife and quarrel have fastened upon the immortals” (21.513; see [[eris]], [[neikos]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).
