summary: Achilles meets him at the river. Taken once by night in Priam's orchard, sold into Lemnos and ransomed by Eetion of Imbros, he has been home eleven days; unarmed, he clasps Achilles' knees and pleads the bread he ate at Achilles' side, the price he fetched, and his mother Laothoe, whose other son Achilles has killed. Achilles answers that Patroclus died too, kills him with the sword, and throws him into the river for the fishes (21.34–135).

**Taken in the orchard.**

{{quote:931b172d-a8a3-5c59-bcbd-9120fb059754}}

He is “a son of Priam, descendant of Dardanus” (21.34; see [[son-of-priam]] and [[dardanus]]), and the narrator begins with the first time Achilles met him. ἐννύχιος προμολών, “coming out by night” (21.37): Achilles came on him in his father's orchard (ἀλωή, 21.36, 21.77), cutting young shoots from a wild fig “to be the rails of a chariot” (21.38; see [[chariot]]). Walter Leaf calls ἐρινεὸν … ὄρπηκας a figure of the whole and the part, and reports Agar's conjecture ἐρινεοῦ, since Lycaon could hardly have been cutting the branches off the tree by night, only shaping them; young branches were chosen for the rails because they bend (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.37; see the translation's note on 21.37–38). The tree is not the wild fig on the plain under the wall (see [[the-fig-tree]]). ἀνώϊστον κακόν, “an evil he never foresaw” (21.39): the adjective is found only here in Homer. The capture is one of the raids of the war's first years, like the raid in which Achilles caught Isus and Antiphus, two other sons of Priam, herding sheep on Ida, and bound them with willow shoots (11.104–106; see [[achilles-raids]], [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]).

**Sold, ransomed and home.**

{{quote:5f93538f-d089-5447-8760-d28ae3d95425}}

ἐπέρασσε, “sold” (21.40), is the aorist of the verb of selling across the sea, properly “make to pass over”, and Leaf notes that the Iliad uses it only in this book (on 21.40; 21.58, 21.78, 21.102, 21.454; see [[trade]]). The buyer was the son of Jason, Euneus of Lemnos, who sends wine to the Achaean camp in Book 7 (7.467–471; see [[euneus]] and [[lemnos]]), and Leaf thinks he bought Lycaon as a slave (on 21.41; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). In Book 23 the price is named: a silver mixing bowl of Sidonian work, which Euneus gave to Patroclus as the price of Lycaon, and which Achilles sets out as a prize at Patroclus' funeral games (23.740–747; see [[mixing-bowl]]). Book 21 does not name Patroclus in the sale, but Leaf finds it natural that he should have had a part in it (on 23.746). A guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, bought Lycaon back and sent him to Arisbe on the Hellespont, and he escaped home from there (21.42–44; see [[eetion-of-imbros]], [[imbros]] and [[arisbe]]); Leaf reads in ὑπεκπροφυγών that he was meant to be kept in custody at Arisbe for his own safety (on 21.44).

The narrator counts his days at home, “For eleven days he delighted his heart with his friends, / after he came from Lemnos” (21.45–46), and Lycaon counts them again, “this dawn / is the twelfth since I came to Ilios” (21.80–81; see [[eos]]). He was led away from the orchard “against his will” (21.36), and Achilles is to send him to the house of Hades “against his will” (21.48). νέεσθαι, “on his way”, is one of the few places where the verb has lost the sense of a happy return home (Leaf on 21.48; see [[nostos]]).

**Unarmed.** Achilles sees him “naked, without helmet or shield, and he had no spear either” (21.50): he had flung them to the ground, worn down by sweat and weariness as he fled out of the river (21.51–52). The sentence begun at 21.49 breaks off to explain how Achilles saw that he was unarmed, and resumes only at 21.53 (Leaf on 21.50; see the translation's note on 21.49–53). Achilles speaks to his own heart in the two verses with which he wondered at Aeneas' escape (21.53–54 = 20.343–344; see [[o-popoi]]): the Trojans he has killed will rise up again “from under the misty darkness”, the realm that fell to Hades (21.56; 15.191), now that this man has come back from Lemnos. The sea did not hold him; let him taste the spear, to see whether “the life-giving earth will hold him back, which holds back even the strong” (21.59–63; see [[life-giving-earth]]). κεῖθεν, “from there” (21.62), is the world of the dead, by a euphemism (Leaf on 21.62), and the T scholia found “life-giving” an unfitting epithet for the earth that holds the dead (Leaf on 21.63).

**At Achilles' knees.** Lycaon comes near “dazed” (21.64), the word for the twelve young men whom Achilles has just led out of the river, “dazed, like fawns” (21.29), to be killed at Patroclus' pyre (see [[human-sacrifice]]). Achilles lifts his long spear to stab him; Lycaon runs in under it and clasps his knees, and the spear passes over his back and stands in the ground, “straining to glut itself on the flesh of a man” (21.67–70), as Achilles' own spear passed over Aeneas' back in the last book (20.279–280). Leaf notes that the spear seems to be thrown, though Aristarchus held that οὐτάμεναι, “to stab”, is used only of thrusting, and that at 21.17 Achilles had left his spear on the bank (on 21.67–68; see the translation's note on 21.67–70). “And with one hand he took hold of his knees and begged him, / and with the other he held the sharpened spear and would not let it go” (21.71–72; see [[supplication]] and [[gounata]]).

**The plea.**

{{quote:81c59bbe-7787-5834-8d0a-8c5b6aa74732}}

γουνοῦμαι, “I implore you” (21.74): the whole verse returns in the Odyssey with Odysseus' name in Achilles' place, in the mouths of the seer Leodes and the singer Phemius (*Odyssey* 22.312, 22.344; see [[aidos]] and [[pity]]). “I am as good as a revered suppliant to you” (21.75): Leaf explains that to break bread under another man's roof gives the standing of a suppliant; Lycaon is not truly one, since Achilles has not accepted him, but claims to be as good as one, with ἀντί as in the Odyssey's “a guest and a suppliant are as good as a brother” (on 21.75; *Odyssey* 8.546). πρώτῳ, “first” (21.76), first among the Greeks, makes the tie personal, as Odysseus tells Nausicaa that he has come to her first (Leaf on 21.76; *Odyssey* 6.175–176). The grain of Demeter is bread (see [[demeter]]). Then come the prices. ἑκατόμβοιον, “the worth of a hundred oxen” (21.79), is the word for Glaucus' golden armor (6.236), and Leaf takes it for the value of Euneus' bowl (on 21.79); “three times as much” was the ransom (21.80). Leaf thinks that Lycaon means to show Achilles how much he will be worth if he is sold again (on 21.80), and Achilles hears an offer of ransom (21.99; see [[apoina]]).

**Fate, Zeus and a mother.** Lycaon puts his capture down to “deadly fate” and to Zeus: “I must be hateful to father Zeus, / who has given me to you again” (21.83–84; see [[moira]] and [[zeus]]), as the narrator said that “a god threw him into the hands of Achilles” (21.47), and as he says again, “a god has brought me here” (21.93; see [[daimon]]). Then he names his mother:

{{quote:f8cb359e-0def-5157-870e-3dc4c5ad1497}}

μινυνθάδιον, “to be short-lived” (21.84), is the word Achilles used of himself to his own mother in the first book, “Mother, since you bore me to be short-lived” (1.352; see [[short-lived]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). His mother is Laothoe, daughter of old Altes, king of the Leleges at Pedasus on the Satnioeis (see [[laothoe]], [[altes]], [[leleges]], [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]] and [[satnioeis]]). His full brother is Polydorus, whom Achilles killed earlier that day (20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). δειροτομήσεις, “you will cut the throats of” (21.89), is the verb of Achilles' vow to cut the throats of twelve Trojans before Patroclus' pyre (18.336), and Leaf glosses it as the slaughter of a helpless victim at the altar (on 21.89; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

**Not Hector's brother.**

{{quote:038af10d-f0c1-5916-9fca-80ce0054804a}}

The last argument is his birth. He is not ὁμογάστριος, “of the same womb” (21.95), as Hector, Hecuba's son (see [[hecuba]] and [[kasignetos]]); the word returns once, of a brother lost (24.47), and Zenodotus read ἰογάστριος. Leaf rejects the inference, once drawn from the line, that kinship was reckoned through the mother: in a house with many wives the children of one mother would feel a closer tie among themselves, and all that Lycaon can urge is that he is not so close to Hector as he might have been (on 21.95). The verse that introduces the argument is Achilles' own in the quarrel of the first book, “And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart” (21.94 = 1.297; see [[lay-it-to-heart]]), and the last ends with Zeus' words of Patroclus as he watches Hector put on Achilles' armor, “gentle and strong” (21.96 = 17.204; see [[eneeie]] and [[patroclus]]). Bayfield condemned the three verses, as Leaf reports: two are borrowed, the praise of Patroclus is out of place in an enemy's mouth, the plea comes oddly after the reminder of Polydorus' death, and Achilles makes no answer to it (on 21.94; see the translation's note on 21.94–96).

**The answer.** “So the shining son of Priam spoke to him, / begging with words; but the voice he heard would not be soothed” (21.97–98). The second verse ends as the sons of Antimachus heard Agamemnon's voice (11.137; see [[implacable]]). Achilles calls him νήπιε, “Fool” (21.99; see [[nepios]]), and answers:

{{quote:ceddbe41-0dee-5425-8a1b-b5db243d83d6}}

“No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this? / Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). Achilles sets his own death beside Lycaon's: he too, handsome and tall and the son of a goddess, will die, on some morning or evening or midday, by spear or arrow (21.108–113; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**The sword.**

{{quote:5ac36b5a-9203-5648-b69f-08d9b4eabefb}}

He lets go of the spear that he has held since he clasped Achilles' knees (21.72, 21.115). His death is told in verses used of others: he sits stretching out both his hands as Ilioneus did before Peneleos' sword (21.115–116 ≈ 14.495–496), and lies stretched out with the dark blood soaking the earth as Harpalion lay (21.119 = 13.655; Leaf on 21.115; see [[ilioneus]] and [[harpalion]]). The two-edged sword goes in at the collarbone beside the neck (21.117–118; see [[sword]] and [[two-edged]]).

**Among the fishes.** “And Achilles took him by the foot and flung him into the river to be swept away” (21.120), and boasted over him:

{{quote:058cf532-fdc5-525f-b8e3-b8bb364e2617}}

The body is given to the river, not to burial. Leaf compares Odysseus' words over the beaten beggar Irus, ἐνταυθοῖ νῦν ἧσο, “sit there now” (*Odyssey* 18.105; on 21.122). ἀκηδέες, rendered “care nothing for you” (21.123), may also mean, as Monro took it, without the rites of mourning (Leaf on 21.123; see the translation's note on 21.122–123). His mother will not lay him on a bier and lament him (see [[laothoe]] and [[lament]]), and in the next book Priam, on the wall, looks for Lycaon and Polydorus among the Trojans who have fled into the city, not knowing that both are dead (22.46–53). Leaf suspects the text of 21.126, and reports the variant ὑπαλύξει with the explanation of Philetas and Callistratus, that a fish fattened on the fat would escape the cold (on 21.126; see the translation's note on 21.126–127 and [[fish]]). From the dead man Achilles turns to all the Trojans and to the river that will not save them (21.128–135; see [[scamander]] and [[rivers]]), and the river grows angry (21.136–138; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

**After his death.** The bowl that paid for him becomes the first prize of the footrace at Patroclus' games, and Odysseus wins it (23.740–747, 23.778; see [[euneus]]). When Hermes wakes Priam in Achilles' hut, he tells him that he has ransomed his son, “and given much”, and that his sons left behind would give three times as much ransom for him alive (24.685–686), in the words of Lycaon's ransom, when Eetion “ransomed him, and paid much” (21.42) and Lycaon was ransomed “paying three times as much” (21.80; see [[apoina]]).
