---
summary: Book 21 is the third stage of the day, without a break from Book 20: Achilles in the river, the battle of the gods, and the flight of the Trojans to the gates. The narrator looks back to “the day before, when shining Hector raged” (21.5); Apollo goes into Ilios lest the Danaans sack it against fate “that day” (21.516–517), and Agenor taunts Achilles with his hope of sacking the city “on this day” (21.584). The book ends with the Trojans crowding into the city while Apollo draws Achilles away, and the day goes on in Book 22.
# The day named: "the day before" (21.5), "the evening, late in its setting" (21.232), "that day" (21.517) and "on this
# day" (21.584).
refs: [21.5, 21.232, 21.517, 21.584]
---

**The day before.** The Trojans flee across the plain toward the city, “where the Achaeans had been fleeing in terror / the day before, when shining Hector raged” (21.4–5; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[hector]]). ἤματι τῷ προτέρῳ, “the day before”, ties this day to the last: the rout of the Achaeans to their ships has turned into the rout of the Trojans to their city. Walter Leaf notes that there is no record of the Achaeans having crossed the ford in the earlier battles, and that the ford itself is named only in the formal couplet that begins this book, found also in Books 14 and 24 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.4; 21.1–2 = 14.433–434, 24.692–693; see [[scamander]] and the translation's note on 21.1–2).

**The river.** The first half of the book belongs to Achilles and the river. He splits the Trojans at the ford, kills them in the water, takes twelve alive for Patroclus' pyre, and kills Lycaon and Asteropaeus (21.1–204; see [[achilles]], [[human-sacrifice]], [[the-death-of-lycaon]] and [[asteropaeus]]). The river, choked with the dead, rises against him, Poseidon and Athena promise that it will not bring him down, and Hera sends Hephaestus, whose fire forces the river to yield (21.205–384; see [[achilles-and-the-river]] and [[scamander]]).

**Evening.** The river reproaches Apollo for not standing by the Trojans, as Zeus charged him, “until there comes / the evening, late in its setting, and darkens the deep-soiled earth” (21.231–232; see [[apollo]]). Leaf takes δείελος as perhaps the evening star or the setting sun (on 21.232; see the translation's note on 21.229–232). No evening comes in the book.

**The gods.** The gods who went down to the war at the beginning of the day (20.4–74) fall on one another in strife, and Zeus laughs to see it (21.385–390); when it is over, “the other gods who live forever went to Olympus” (21.518), and Apollo goes into the city (21.515; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

**Against fate.** Apollo is anxious for the wall, “lest the Danaans sack it against fate that day” (21.517), the fear for which Zeus sent the gods into the battle (20.30), and “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy” had he not set Agenor against Achilles (21.544–546; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Agenor taunts Achilles that he hoped “that on this day you would sack the city of the proud Trojans” (21.584; see [[agenor]]).

**Into Book 22.** The book ends with the Trojans in the city, “all whose feet and knees had saved them” (21.611), and with Achilles drawn away along the river by Apollo (21.599–605; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The next book begins with them drying their sweat on the walls (22.1–2) while Hector waits outside the Scaean gates (22.5–6). In this index's count the whole book is Day 27.
