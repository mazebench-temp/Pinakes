**A dream.** Zeus begins at a distance. To honor Achilles he sends a baneful Dream to Agamemnon in the likeness of Nestor, with a false promise of victory (2.1–40; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[dream]]). He does not need to appear; the king does the rest. Rumor, “the messenger of Zeus”, then blazes through the gathering army (2.93–94; see [[ossa]]).

**A homecoming against fate.** When the test sends the army running for the ships, the narrator stops at the edge of another story.

{{quote:2ee51c7c-6e2d-51bf-81a6-2731f4973478}}

“Would have …, had not” (κεν … εἰ μή) is the Iliad's way of bringing the story to the brink and letting a god turn it; Aphrodite snatches Paris from Menelaus in the same construction (3.373–374). ὑπέρμορα, “beyond what is fated”, names what is at stake: Troy is fated to fall, and a homecoming now would break the pattern. Hera sends Athena, and Athena finds Odysseus already “standing still”, not touching his ship, “for grief had come upon his heart and spirit” (2.169–171). He knows her voice at once and runs (2.182). As with Achilles in Book 1, the goddess meets a man whose mind is half made up, and tells him what to do with it.

**In disguise.** Athena stands beside Odysseus “in the likeness of a herald” to silence the crowd (2.279–282). At the end of the book Zeus sends Iris to Troy, where she speaks in the voice of Polites; Hector knows “the goddess's word” and sends the Trojans out to battle (2.786–808). See [[truth-and-deception]].

**Signs.** Odysseus and Nestor remind the army of two signs Zeus sent at the start of the war: the serpent at Aulis, which the Olympian “had sent into the light” and then turned to stone (2.308–320), and the lightning on the right as the fleet set sail (2.350–353; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[the-departure-from-aulis]]). Nestor also names the question that every intervention raises. Marshal the men by tribes and clans, he tells Agamemnon, and “you will know whether it is by the will of heaven (θεσπεσίῃ) that you do not sack the city, or by the cowardice of men and their ignorance of war” (2.367–368). Agamemnon's reply holds both answers at once: Zeus “throws me into fruitless strife and quarrels”, and yet “I was the first to grow angry” (2.375–378). In Book 19 he will blame Zeus, Fate and the Erinys for the quarrel with Achilles (19.86–87).

**Refusal.** A god may take the gift and refuse the favor. Agamemnon prays that the sun may not set before he has burned Priam's hall.

See [[prayer]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].

**Within the men.** Athena goes through the ranks with the aegis and “roused strength in each man's heart to go to war without ceasing and to fight”, and at once war is sweeter to them than going home (2.446–454; see [[aegis]]). The flight of the morning is undone from inside. Zeus then makes Agamemnon “conspicuous among many” (2.482–483), and the Catalogue recalls older gifts of the gods, such as Apollo's mares for Eumelus and his bow for Pandarus (2.766, 2.827).
