---
summary: Book 2 uses the epithet four times, in two pairs of lines, as the army is first held back and then summoned to war.
---

Two of the uses are in a repeated line. Agamemnon, having dressed and taken the scepter, “went along the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans” ({{ref:Iliad 2.47}}); Odysseus, having received the same scepter from him, does the same in the same words ({{ref:Iliad 2.187}}).

The other two are in commands. Hera tells Athena to go “through the army of the bronze-armored Achaeans” and hold them back ({{ref:Iliad 2.163}}). When Athena repeats the command to Odysseus, she changes the line: “Go now through the army of the Achaeans, and hang back no longer” ({{ref:Iliad 2.179}}). The epithet gives way to the urgency of the order, a small example of how a formula can be adapted to a new speaker. Nestor uses the phrase in his advice to call the army to battle: “let the heralds of the bronze-armored Achaeans make proclamation” ({{ref:Iliad 2.437}}).
