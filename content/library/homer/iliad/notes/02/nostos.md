**The promise withdrawn.** Agamemnon's test begins with a homecoming promised and taken back. Zeus once promised that he would sack Ilios “and then sail home” (ἀπονέεσθαι), and now bids him go back to Argos in disgrace (2.112–115). He ends by proposing flight: “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (2.140). See [[the-test-of-the-army]].

**Against fate.** The army takes him at his word, and its shout reaches the sky “as they longed for home” (οἴκαδε, 2.154).

{{quote:2ee51c7c-6e2d-51bf-81a6-2731f4973478}}

This is the first time the noun νόστος occurs in the poem. It names a homecoming that nearly happens ὑπέρμορα, “beyond what was fated”, and is stopped by a goddess (see [[divine-intervention]]). Hera and Athena ask in the same words whether the Argives will flee home (οἶκον δὲ) to their dear native land and leave Helen to Priam (2.158–162, 2.174–178).

**Thersites and Odysseus.** Thersites says aloud what the test proposed: “let us go home with our ships” (οἴκαδέ περ σὺν νηυσὶ νεώμεθα, 2.236), close to Achilles' threat to go home to Phthia with his ships (1.169–170). Odysseus tells him to stop watching for his homecoming (νόστον, 2.251), for “we do not yet clearly know how these things will turn out, whether we sons of the Achaeans shall return well or badly” (νοστήσομεν, 2.252–253); his own return is the subject of the *Odyssey*. To the army he turns the promise round. The Achaeans themselves promised Agamemnon, when they left Argos, that he would sack Ilios before he sailed home.

{{quote:6829645b-0fe0-5f11-82d9-60928be5bbb3}}

He grants that they wail like children or widows about going home, and that nine years is enough to make any man weary (2.289–297; see [[children-and-widows]]). But it is shameful to stay so long and go home empty-handed (2.298; see [[shameful-to-stay-long]]).

**Nestor.** Nestor makes the homecoming a threat. Let no one hurry to go home before he has lain with a Trojan wife and paid them back for Helen (2.354–356); and let any man desperate to go home lay hands on his ship, so that he may meet his death before the others (2.357–359).

**Sweeter than going home.** When Athena passes through the ranks with the aegis, the longing is reversed.

{{quote:334597ca-f56a-57fb-bd9c-b5fc10074569}}

The words with which Agamemnon proposed flight, “to their dear native land” (φίλην ἐς πατρίδα γαῖαν, 2.140), close the episode (2.454). See [[polemos]].
