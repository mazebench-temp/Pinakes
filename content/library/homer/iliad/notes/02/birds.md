Book 2 uses both Homeric words for a bird: οἰωνός, the large bird of prey and of omen, and ὄρνις, the common word for any bird.

**Carrion.** Agamemnon warns that a man who hangs back from the battle will have no hope of escaping “the dogs and the birds” (κύνας ἠδʼ οἰωνούς, 2.393), the pair of the proem (1.4–5; see [[dogs]]).

**The sign at Aulis.** The birds of Odysseus' story are small ones, a sparrow and her nestlings, and no one reads their flight. The sign is the serpent that eats them, and Calchas, the bird-seer of Book 1, reads it by counting the victims (2.308–329; see [[sparrow]], [[serpent]] and [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

**The flock.** As the army pours out onto the plain, it is compared to “the many tribes of winged birds” (ὀρνίθων πετεηνῶν ἔθνεα πολλά), geese and cranes and long-necked swans on the Asian meadow.

{{quote:d65fb152-00f9-5b63-bd8c-3537efdc1c0b}}

The birds give the army its number, its restless motion “here and there”, and its noise (see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]], [[geese]], [[cranes]] and [[swans]]). Birds return at the end of the Catalogue as a measure of speed: Eumelus' mares were “swift-footed like birds” (ποδώκεας ὄρνιθας ὥς, 2.764; see [[swift-as-birds]] and [[horses]]).

**The augur.** In the Trojan catalogue the Mysians are led by Chromis and “Ennomus the augur” (οἰωνιστής, “reader of birds”).

{{quote:81a9365c-5c70-5d61-918e-9d23ebee7634}}

“His birds did not save him from black doom”: Achilles was to kill him in the river (see [[ennomus]] and [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). His art could not save him. Earlier in the Trojan catalogue another seer, Merops of Percote, “knew the art of prophecy” above all men and forbade his sons to go to the war; they did not obey him, “for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.831–834; see [[merops]]).
