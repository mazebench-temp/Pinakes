**Her name in the lie.** The false reason the Dream gives for victory is Hera: “she has bent them all, Hera, with her pleading, and sorrows hang over the Trojans” (2.14–15). The words are heard three times, from Zeus, from the Dream and from Agamemnon (2.15, 2.32, 2.69). They sound true because her hatred of Troy is known; Zeus complained in Book 1 that she is forever quarreling with him and says he helps the Trojans (1.518–521). The lie borrows her real wishes to make its promise believable. See [[dream]].

**The rescue.**

{{quote:2ee51c7c-6e2d-51bf-81a6-2731f4973478}}

ὑπέρμορα (“beyond fate”, “against fate”): the Achaeans would have gone home contrary to what was fated. It is one of the poem's “almost” moments. The narrator says what would have happened “if not” (εἰ μή) for a god, and the story is turned back onto its course. Zeus himself fears that Achilles may sack Troy “beyond fate” (20.30). See [[moira]] and [[nostos]].

Hera speaks at once, to Athena, not to Zeus. Her argument is Argive Helen and the Achaean dead: if the army goes home, the Trojans will keep Helen as a trophy, and the many who died for her will have died in vain (2.157–162; see [[helen]]). Athena is to hold back every man with gentle words (σοῖς ἀγανοῖς ἐπέεσσιν, 2.164). She does, through Odysseus.

**Hera and Zeus.** In Book 1 Hera quarreled with Zeus over his promise to Thetis and was cowed into silence (1.536–569). In Book 2 she acts on her own, in the pattern of Book 1, where she sent Athena to stop Achilles' sword (1.194–195): Hera sends, and Athena goes. The rescue also serves Zeus. His plan needs the Achaeans to stay and fight, and lose, until Achilles is honored. Hera saves the army for the war that Zeus means to turn against it. See [[athena]] and [[divine-intervention]].
