**Calchas at Aulis.** Odysseus asks the army to stay “so that we may learn whether Calchas' prophecy is true or not” (μαντεύεται, 2.300). The prophecy was the reading of a sign. As the Achaeans sacrificed at Aulis, a serpent came from beneath the altar, ate eight sparrow chicks and their mother, and was turned to stone (2.308–320; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[teras]]). The army stood silent, and Calchas “at once spoke out, prophesying” (θεοπροπέων, 2.322; see [[theopropion]]):

{{quote:61cce681-3bc9-50a0-907b-080ef3797900}}

The reading is a count: nine birds, nine years, and in the tenth the city. The sign is “late in coming, late in fulfillment” (ὄψιμον ὀψιτέλεστον, 2.325), and Odysseus adds, “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330). In Book 1 Agamemnon called Calchas a “prophet of evils” who had never told him anything good (1.106–108). In Book 2 the same seer's prophecy is the army's reason to stay. See [[calchas]] and [[prophecy]].

**The lightning.** Nestor adds a sign of his own. On the day the Argives embarked, Zeus “flashed his lightning on our right, showing signs of good omen” (2.353). The right is the side of good omens, and lightning is the sign of Zeus (see [[the-departure-from-aulis]]).

**The Dream.** Achilles had said that “a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (1.63; see [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]). Nestor weighs Agamemnon's dream by the dreamer: from anyone else “we would call it a lie”, but this man claims to be the best of the Achaeans (2.80–82). The dream did come from Zeus, and it was a lie (see [[dream]] and [[onar]]).

**Seers who could not save their own.** Merops of Percote “above all men knew the art of prophecy (μαντοσύνας), and would not let his sons go into man-destroying war; but they would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.831–834; see [[merops]] and [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Ennomus the augur (οἰωνιστής, “reader of birds”) leads the Mysians, “but his birds did not save him from black doom”; he fell at the hands of Achilles in the river (2.858–861; see [[ennomus]] and [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). The art that reads the will of the gods for others does not protect the seer or his house.
