---
summary: Book 2 has two privative pairs, the commoner “unwarlike and weak” and the aegis “ageless and immortal”, and a great many single privatives, which gather around failure, disorder and what does not end.
grc: [ἀπτόλεμος καὶ ἄναλκις, ἀγήρων ἀθανάτην]
en: [unwarlike and weak, ageless and immortal]
---

**Two pairs.** Odysseus tells a man of the people who has run for the ships that he is ἀπτόλεμος καὶ ἄναλκις, “unwarlike and weak”, without war (πτόλεμος) and without strength (ἀλκή), and never counted for anything in war or in council (2.201–202). The pair returns in Book 9, where Diomedes reminds Agamemnon that he once called him “unwarlike and weak” before the Danaans, and asks whether he thinks the sons of the Achaeans are so (9.34–35, 9.40–41).

Athena goes through the ranks holding the aegis, “highly prized, ageless and immortal” (ἐρίτιμον ἀγήρων ἀθανάτην τε, 2.447). Elsewhere the pair belongs to the gods and to what mortals cannot have. Sarpedon imagines that he and Glaucus might be “ageless and immortal” forever (12.323), and Calypso says she offered to make Odysseus so (Odyssey 5.135–136). Here it describes a thing, the aegis that Hephaestus the smith gave to Zeus (15.308–310). The gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made for Alcinous are likewise “immortal and ageless all their days” (Odyssey 7.91–94). See [[aegis]].

**Failure.** Book 2 is full of single words in ἀ-, and many gather around failure. In the test speech Agamemnon calls the war ἄπρηκτον (“a war that comes to nothing”, 2.121) and the work at Troy ἀκράαντον (“unfinished”, 2.138), and later he speaks of ἀπρήκτους ἔριδας (“fruitless strife”, 2.376). ἀκράαντος is formed from κραίνω (“accomplish”), and a compound of that verb comes back after Agamemnon's prayer: Zeus “was not yet going to fulfill” it (ἐπεκραίαινε, 2.419), and he made the toil ἀμέγαρτον, “a toil none would envy” (2.420).

**Disorder.** Thersites is described by negations: ἀμετροεπής (“of unmeasured words”), ἄκοσμα (“disorderly” words), οὐ κατὰ κόσμον (“against all order”), and in Odysseus' mouth ἀκριτόμυθος (“reckless in speech”) (2.212–214, 2.246; see [[of-unmeasured-words]] and [[reckless-in-speech]]). After the blow he looks ἀχρεῖον, “helpless” (2.269).

**What does not end.** The scepter, also the work of Hephaestus (2.101), is ἄφθιτον αἰεί (“imperishable forever”, 2.46, 2.186; see [[imperishable]]). Athena rouses strength to fight ἄλληκτον (“without ceasing”, 2.452). The poet would need a voice ἄρρηκτος (“that could not break”) to name the multitude (2.490). Other privatives are plain description: libations of unmixed wine (ἄκρητοι, 2.341), logs stripped of their leaves (ἀφύλλοισιν, 2.425), a destroying fire in a vast forest (ἀΐδηλον, ἄσπετον, 2.455). Twice the Catalogue says that a contingent was not ἄναρχοι (“without a leader”, 2.703, 2.726), a privative inside a [[litotes]].
