**The Dream.** Zeus chooses to honor Achilles by deceiving Agamemnon. The [[dream|Dream]] finds him asleep, rebukes him in Nestor's shape, and promises that he can take Troy “now”. He believes it, and the narrator's judgment follows at once:

{{quote:130425b2-40e3-5355-8921-905c49fc817a}}

“That very day” (ἤματι κείνῳ) is his own addition; the Dream said only “now”. He wakes, dresses, and takes up “the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever” (2.46). See [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[dressing-scene]].

**The test.** He calls the elders to Nestor's ship, tells them the dream in the Dream's own words, and then announces something the Dream never asked for:

{{quote:5c269b4c-6904-58ac-9ae6-b48cdbc739b4}}

The poem gives no reason beyond “as is the custom” (ἣ θέμις ἐστί), and does not say why he takes the risk on the morning of promised victory. See [[peira]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]].

Leaning on the scepter whose descent from Zeus the poet has just traced (2.100–108; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]), he tells the army that the war is lost. His words are truer than he knows:

{{quote:539c222b-74c7-5d91-bbdd-a2b7f903c1c9}}

Zeus has indeed deceived him, with the promise of victory rather than the order to go home. The feigned speech is persuasive in earnest: disgrace in the eyes of men to come, the Achaeans' numbers (“many a ten would go without a wine-pourer”, 2.128), the rotting ships and the waiting wives, and at the end, “let us flee”. The men take him at his word and run for the ships (2.142–154). The elders, who were to hold them back, are not heard. Hera, Athena and [[Odysseus]], with Agamemnon's own scepter, turn the army round. Agamemnon is silent through it all, even while [[Thersites]] abuses him to his face (2.225–242).

**The admission.** Nestor's counsel gives him back his voice. He praises the old man, “If only … I had ten such counselors” (2.371–372), and then says what he never said in Book 1:

{{quote:0c5f3124-263c-53cc-abb0-a656385f67c5}}

He still blames Zeus for the “fruitless strife”, but he admits that he began the quarrel, and he imagines a day when he and Achilles are of one mind. In Book 9 he will call his conduct ἄτη (“blind folly”, 9.115–120), and in Book 19 he will blame Zeus, Fate and the Erinys (19.86–90). See [[ate]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]. Then he threatens any man who hangs back by the ships with the dogs and the birds (2.391–393). Aristotle quotes the threat with a further phrase that our text lacks, πὰρ γὰρ ἐμοὶ θάνατος, “for death is in my hands” (*Politics* 3.14, 1285a10–14).

**The prayer.** With the elders around him he sacrifices a five-year-old ox to Zeus.

{{quote:e7ee5ec4-5e3e-5deb-8295-b58b728f5a85}}

He asks for everything before sunset: Priam's hall thrown down, its doors burned, Hector's tunic torn on his chest. Zeus accepts the offering and refuses the prayer. In Book 1 Apollo heard both prayers of his priest Chryses (1.43, 1.457); the first prayer of the king in the poem is refused. See [[prayer-type-scene]].

**Like Zeus, Ares and Poseidon.** As the leaders marshal the army, Zeus makes Agamemnon stand out:

{{quote:b6efa94c-330b-5f7b-a0a2-a43c951fede3}}

Three gods lend him their parts, and then he is a bull among the cattle. Zeus makes him so “on that day” (ἤματι κείνῳ, 2.482), the words in which Agamemnon had expected to take Priam's city (2.37). Zeus grants him splendor that day, and nothing more. See [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]] and [[bull-above-the-herd]].

**The Catalogue.** His is the largest contingent, a hundred ships from [[mycenae|Mycenae]], wealthy Corinth, Sicyon and the towns of the Achaean coast (2.569–580). He stood out among the heroes, the poet says, “because he was the best, and led by far the most men” (2.580). Achilles scorned his claim to be “the best of the Achaeans” (1.91); the Catalogue grants it by the measure of his army, and later gives the title among the fighters to Telamonian Ajax, “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). His realm does not include Argos, which the Catalogue gives to [[Diomedes]] (2.559–568), although the scepter makes him lord “of many islands and of all Argos” (2.108). Thucydides took that line as evidence that Agamemnon's power rested on a fleet (1.9.4). See [[thucydides-and-the-catalogue]] and [[catalogue-of-ships]].
