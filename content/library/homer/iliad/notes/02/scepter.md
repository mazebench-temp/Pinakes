Book 1 showed the scepter of the judges, a stripped branch that Achilles swore by and threw to the ground (1.234–246). Book 2 shows the scepter of a king: who gave it, who holds it, and what it can be used for.

## The scepter of his fathers

When Agamemnon wakes from the Dream, he dresses, and the last thing he takes up is the scepter, “of his fathers, imperishable forever” (σκῆπτρον πατρώϊον ἄφθιτον αἰεί). It is where a warrior arming for battle would take up his spear (see [[dressing-scene]]). With it he goes along the ships to the council.

{{quote:e33c248a-9e1b-596b-9d6c-bac21690432c}}

At the assembly he rises holding it, and the narrator stops to tell its history. [[Hephaestus]] made it and gave it to [[Zeus]]; Zeus gave it to [[Hermes]], Hermes to [[Pelops]], Pelops to [[Atreus]]; Atreus left it at his death to [[Thyestes]], and Thyestes left it to Agamemnon “to carry, to be lord of many islands and of all Argos” (see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]).

{{quote:778cced3-e225-5437-8786-4ee584223e25}}

The pedigree answers Book 1. Achilles' scepter was a branch cut by bronze and carried by the judges of the Achaeans; Agamemnon's is a god's work, a gift of Zeus, and an inheritance. Nestor had said that a scepter-bearing king has his glory from Zeus (1.278–279). Here the claim is told as a story. Then Agamemnon leans on the scepter (τῷ … ἐρεισάμενος, 2.109) and uses its authority for a lie: Zeus has deceived him, and they must all go home (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

## In the hands of Odysseus

When the army stampedes to the ships, Athena sends Odysseus to stop it. He goes straight to Agamemnon and “received from him the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever”, and with it he goes along the ships. The scepter keeps its half-line from the dressing scene, and the next verse is the one used of the king himself (2.187 = 2.47). The staff makes him the king's deputy.

He uses it in two ways. A king or a man of note he holds back with gentle words; a man of the people he strikes with the scepter and rebukes (σκήπτρῳ ἐλάσασκεν, 2.199). The iterative verbs say that he did this again and again. His words to the commoners end with the scepter itself: let there be one king, “to whom the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given the scepter and the ordinances” ([[themis|θέμιστες]]; see [[one-ruler]]).

{{quote:a6e5efd3-f944-5871-af60-228c9844d8cf}}

## The blow

Only [[Thersites]] goes on shouting. Odysseus threatens to strip him and drive him out of the assembly with shameful blows, and then strikes him on the back and shoulders. A bloody welt rises “under the golden scepter” (σκήπτρου ὕπο χρυσέου; see [[gold-and-silver]]), and the army laughs (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

{{quote:8d88137d-0792-5d3c-a5c4-435ddf466f85}}

Then Odysseus stands “holding the scepter” (2.279) and speaks, while Athena, in the likeness of a herald, silences the crowd. The staff that has just been a weapon marks the speaker again.

In Book 1 the scepter stood for a justice that had failed, and Achilles threw it down. In Book 2 it is the king's authority itself: a gift of Zeus, lent to the man who can use it, and laid on a man's back. See [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[words-and-violence]].
