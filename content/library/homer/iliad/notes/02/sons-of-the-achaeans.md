---
summary: Book 2 uses the formula eleven times, most often in speeches about whether the army will fight or go home.
grc: [κοῦροι Ἀχαιῶν]
en: [young men of the Achaeans]
---

In Book 1 the sons of the Achaeans were the givers of prizes. In Book 2 they are the army whose will is in question. Agamemnon and Nestor end their speeches to the council with the same line: “Come then, let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans” ({{ref:Iliad 2.72}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.83}}). Agamemnon says the sons of the Achaeans outnumber the Trojans ten to one ({{ref:Iliad 2.129}}). Odysseus warns the kings that Agamemnon may strike or harm the sons of the Achaeans ({{ref:Iliad 2.193}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.195}}), and Thersites accuses him of bringing them to grief ({{ref:Iliad 2.234}}). Odysseus answers that no one knows “whether we sons of the Achaeans shall return well or badly” ({{ref:Iliad 2.253}}).

The formula also names the army as a crowd, the nearest and farthest of the sons of the Achaeans who must hear Odysseus ({{ref:Iliad 2.281}}), and as the men who left Philoctetes on Lemnos ({{ref:Iliad 2.722}}).

The variant κοῦροι Ἀχαιῶν, “the young men of the Achaeans,” names the men of Aegina and Mases in the Catalogue ({{ref:Iliad 2.562}}). The Catalogue uses the same turn for other peoples too, κοῦροι Βοιωτῶν and κοῦροι Ἀθηναίων ({{ref:Iliad 2.510}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.551}}).
