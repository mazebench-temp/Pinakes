Men speak of him throughout the book as the source of kingship, of signs and of their troubles. He accepts Agamemnon's sacrifice but not his prayer, and sends Iris to bring the Trojans out.

**The sleepless plan.**

{{quote:f27e722e-0793-5781-bd01-1ef76081bef8}}

Book 1 ended with Zeus going to bed beside Hera (1.609–611). Book 2 begins with him awake while the other gods and men sleep, pondering how to keep his promise to Thetis: to honor Achilles and destroy many Achaeans at the ships. “This plan seemed to him in his heart the best” is the formula for a decision reached after thought, used of gods and men alike (see [[decision-scene]]). βουλή (“plan”) is also the word of the proem, “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5). Here the plan becomes an act. See [[plan-of-zeus]].

**The lying Dream.** Zeus tells the Dream to report his words “exactly” (ἀτρεκέως, 2.10). The message is to be delivered word for word, and what it says is false: Troy may be taken now, the gods are no longer divided, Hera has won them over (2.11–15). None of it is true of Zeus himself, who is planning the opposite. The narrator gives the real plan at once: Zeus “was yet to lay more sorrows and groaning on Trojans and Danaans alike” (2.39–40). It is the first time in the poem that a god deceives a mortal, and ancient readers found it hard to accept. Plato's Socrates refuses to praise it (*Republic* 2, 383a), and Aristotle records a defense of the passage by a change of accent (*Poetics* 25, 1461a21–23). See [[dream]], [[plato-lying-dream]] and [[aristotle-and-hippias]].

**Zeus in men's mouths.** The rest of the book shows the Achaeans living under Zeus. The scepter was made for him, and he gave it to Hermes (2.101–103; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). A king's honor “comes from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves him” (2.197), and the one king is the man to whom “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus” has given the scepter and the ordinances (2.205–206). The portent at Aulis was sent by “the Olympian himself”, who turned the serpent to stone (2.309, 2.318–319), and Calchas read it as a sign from “Zeus the counselor” (2.324). Zeus flashed his lightning on the right when the fleet set sail (2.350–353). The years of the war are “nine years of great Zeus” (2.134).

The men also blame him. Agamemnon, feigning despair, calls him σχέτλιος (“hard”) and charges him with “an evil deceit” (2.111–115). In earnest, later, he says that Zeus has given him pains and thrown him into strife (2.375–376). Both charges are truer than he knows.

**The prayer refused.**

{{quote:dcc1fe5e-205b-5baf-b9fe-5a78e31a6144}}

Agamemnon has asked to burn Priam's hall before sunset (2.412–418). Zeus takes the sacrifice and makes the toil greater. The narrator's “not yet” (οὐδʼ ἄρα πώ) keeps Troy's fall in view while putting it off. See [[prayer-type-scene]].

**The day of battle.** As the army is marshaled, Zeus makes Agamemnon stand out, like Zeus himself “in eyes and head” (2.477–483). In the similes his power is weather and earthquake. The winds rush down “from the clouds of father Zeus” (2.146), and the earth groans under the marching army as it groans under Zeus when he lashes the ground around Typhoeus (2.780–785; see [[zeus-and-typhoeus]]). Last, he sends Iris to the Trojans with “grievous news” (2.786–787), for his plan needs them out on the plain. The book that began with his sleepless plan ends with both armies moving toward the battle he has prepared. See [[iris]].
