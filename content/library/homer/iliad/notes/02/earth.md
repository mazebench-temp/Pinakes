summary: The earth groans under the army in the assembly and on the march, as it groans when Zeus lashes the ground around Typhoeus. It is also the “dear native land” the men long for, the black earth that holds Protesilaus, and the mother of Erechtheus.
grc: [γαῖα, χθὼν, ἄρουρα, αἴης, αἶαν]

**The groaning earth.** The army's weight is heard in the ground. As the people sit down in the first assembly, “the earth groaned” (ὑπὸ δὲ στεναχίζετο γαῖα, 2.95). When they pour out onto the plain of the Scamander, “the earth beneath rang terribly under the feet of the men and of their horses” (ὑπὸ χθὼν σμερδαλέον κονάβιζε, 2.465–466). After the Catalogue, as they cross the plain, they go “as if the whole land (χθών) were being devoured by fire”, and the earth groans under them as it groans under Zeus when in his anger he lashes the earth around Typhoeus among the Arimi (2.780–784). The simile begins and ends with the groaning earth (γαῖα δʼ ὑπεστενάχιζε … στεναχίζετο γαῖα). See [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]] and [[zeus-and-typhoeus]].

**The native land.** γαῖα is also a country. The formula for home is “to our dear native land” (φίλην ἐς πατρίδα γαῖαν), used four times, from Agamemnon's pretended call to flee to the moment war becomes sweeter than going home (2.140, 2.158, 2.174, 2.454). Hera and Athena also use αἶα, a poetic word for the earth: many Achaeans have died in Troy “far from their dear native land” (φίλης ἀπὸ πατρίδος αἴης, 2.162, 2.178). See [[philos]] and [[nostos]].

**The earth that holds and bears.** Agamemnon prays that Hector's companions may “fall headlong in the dust and bite the earth” (2.418). Of Protesilaus, killed as he landed, the Catalogue says that “by now the black earth held him fast” (2.699; see [[the-death-of-protesilaus]]). The earth also gives birth: Erechtheus was born of “the grain-giving earth” (ζείδωρος ἄρουρα, 2.548), ἄρουρα being the tilled land (see [[grain-giving]] and [[athena-and-erechtheus]]). And the Axius is the fairest water “that spreads over the earth” (αἶαν, 2.850).
