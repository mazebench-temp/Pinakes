**The men of Protesilaus and Philoctetes.** Two contingents in the Catalogue have lost their leaders. Protesilaus was killed as he leapt ashore, the first of the Achaeans (2.698–702), and Philoctetes lies on Lemnos with the wound of a water snake (2.721–723). Of both the Catalogue says, “Nor were they without a leader, though they longed for their leader” (πόθεόν γε μὲν ἀρχόν, 2.703, 2.726), and Podarces and Medon marshal them (see [[hegemon]]). Of Protesilaus' men it says it again, with a change: they “did not lack a leader, though they longed for the good man they had lost” (πόθεόν γε μὲν ἐσθλὸν ἐόντα, 2.708–709). They have a leader; what they miss is the man.

{{quote:232c9280-b4e1-5bd3-aaaa-9f5368961817}}

The Catalogue tells the absence of Philoctetes in the words it uses of Achilles. Philoctetes “lay on an island” (ἐν νήσῳ κεῖτο, 2.721) as Achilles lay among his ships (κεῖτο … ἐν νήεσσι, 2.688; 2.771–772). Each “lay grieving” (κεῖτʼ ἀχέων), and each line ends with what was soon to come: of Achilles, “soon he was to rise up” (2.694); of Philoctetes, “soon they were to remember” him, the Argives beside their ships (2.724–725; see [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]).

**The Myrmidons.** Achilles' men have no one in his place: “there was no one to lead them into the ranks” (2.687). When the Catalogue is over, the poet returns to them. Achilles lies among his ships nursing his wrath; his men play on the shore with discus, javelin and bow; the horses stand idle, and the chariots lie covered in the huts; “and the men, longing for their leader, dear to Ares (ἀρχὸν ἀρηΐφιλον ποθέοντες), wandered here and there through the camp, and did not fight” (2.778–779).

{{quote:2c0a391c-d115-5778-8cb8-7da83a12508f}}

In Book 1 Achilles swore that a longing (ποθή) for him would one day come upon all the sons of the Achaeans (1.240), and he himself longed for the war cry and for battle (1.492). Book 2 shows the first of that longing, among his own men. The rest of the army does not miss him yet: its best man is Telamonian Ajax, “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769).
