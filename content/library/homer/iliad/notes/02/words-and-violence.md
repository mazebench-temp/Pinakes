summary: Book 2 moves from the assembly to the battlefield, and its one blow is struck in the assembly. Odysseus holds back the kings with words and the commoners with the scepter, beats Thersites into silence, and Nestor, Agamemnon and Iris turn the talk to war.

**Words and the scepter.** Agamemnon tests the army “with words” (ἔπεσιν), and the elders are to hold it back “with words” (2.73–75). When the test goes wrong, Odysseus uses two instruments. A king he holds back “with gentle words”; a man of the people “he would strike him with the scepter and rebuke him with a word” (2.189, 2.199). The staff that marks the right to speak becomes a stick (see [[scepter]]).

**Thersites.** Thersites is all words: “a man of unmeasured words, whose mind was full of words, many and disorderly, for quarreling with the kings” (2.212–214; see [[of-unmeasured-words]]). He was always abusing (νεικείεσκε) Achilles and Odysseus, and now he screams insults (ὀνείδεα) at Agamemnon (2.220–224). His speech ends on a line of Achilles: “Otherwise, son of Atreus, this insult would be your last” (2.242 = 1.232). In Book 1 the line took the place of the sword Athena had stopped. In Thersites' mouth it is a threat with nothing behind it.

Odysseus answers word for word, then with his hand. He calls Thersites the worst of all mortals at Troy, forbids him to “have the kings in your mouth”, and swears on his own head and on his name as the father of Telemachus that he will strip him and beat him out of the assembly (2.246–264).

The army's verdict is that the blow has stopped a “word-slinging slanderer” (λωβητῆρα ἐπεσβόλον), whose spirit will never again drive him “to rail at the kings with abusive words” (2.275–277; see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]). The one blow of the book silences a speaker, and the crowd laughs.

**From words to war.** The rest of the book leaves talk behind. Nestor scolds the assembly for talking like children: “we go on quarreling like this with words, and can find no remedy” (2.337–343). Agamemnon says that he and Achilles “fought for the sake of a girl with hostile words” (μαχεσσάμεθʼ … ἀντιβίοις ἐπέεσσιν, 2.377–378), the narrator's own words for the quarrel of Book 1 (1.304), and sends the men to their meal “so that we may join in the clash of Ares” (2.381). After the sacrifice Nestor says, “let us not sit here talking any longer” (2.435).

The Trojans hear the same lesson from a goddess. Iris, in the likeness of Polites, tells Priam: “endless words are always dear to you, as once in time of peace; but relentless war has come” (2.796–797). Her ἄκριτοι (“endless”, from κρίνω, “separate, decide”) echoes the word Odysseus threw at Thersites, ἀκριτόμυθος (“reckless in speech”, 2.246). Hector breaks up the assembly, and the men rush to arms (2.808).
