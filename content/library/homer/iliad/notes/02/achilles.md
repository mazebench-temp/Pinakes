**Zeus' plan.** The book opens with his name. Zeus lies awake pondering “how Achilles he might honor, and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans” (2.3–4). Everything that follows, the Dream, the test and the march to battle, comes from the promise made to Thetis. See [[plan-of-zeus]].

**In Thersites' mouth.**

{{quote:6e9cc963-7c75-593c-8598-2eb519a7eceb}}

[[Thersites]] takes over Achilles' case against Agamemnon. Two of these lines are Achilles' own: “he has taken my prize and keeps it, having seized it himself” is what Achilles told Thetis (1.356), and “this insult would be your last” is what he said to Agamemnon's face (1.232). Achilles is “a far better man” than the king, the claim of his oath in Book 1 (1.244). But Thersites mocks the absent hero too: there is “no anger at all in Achilles' heart; he lets it pass”. The audience knows better. Thersites is “most hateful of all to Achilles and to Odysseus” (2.220), and even his defense of Achilles is an insult.

**Agamemnon's admission.** Answering Nestor, Agamemnon says it in the assembly: “Achilles and I fought for the sake of a girl with hostile words, and I was the first to grow angry” (2.377–378). He hopes that they will one day be of one mind (2.379–380). See [[agamemnon]].

**The Catalogue.**

{{quote:8dabd452-362d-5346-b996-1374b97efce9}}

The Catalogue gives Achilles fifty ships, from Pelasgian Argos, Phthia and Hellas (2.681–685), and then turns his entry into a story of absence. His men have no one to lead them into the ranks, because he lies idle among the ships, angry over Briseis. The poet adds what Book 1 did not say: he took her at Lyrnessus, when he sacked it and the walls of Thebe and killed Mynes and Epistrophus. The entry ends with a promise, “but soon he was to rise up”. The entry of Philoctetes, left behind on Lemnos, ends the same way: “there he lay grieving; but soon they were to remember” him (2.724). Two great fighters lie apart, and both will return. See [[myrmidons]], [[briseis]], [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]], [[philoctetes]] and [[achilles-withdraws]].

**While Achilles kept up his wrath.**

{{quote:7a36300d-342c-59c6-9f8f-a6b668b1f744}}

When the poet asks the Muse who was best, the answer is qualified. Telamonian Ajax was best “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (ὄφρʼ Ἀχιλεὺς μήνιεν), and Achilles' horses were better than any. The poem's first word, μῆνις, returns as a verb, μήνιεν, and again two lines later, ἀπομηνίσας (“nursing his wrath”, 2.772). See [[menis]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]].

{{quote:59741a90-1c33-5381-b0fe-b6be01b9bd7a}}

While the other armies march, his men pass the time on the shore with the discus, the javelin and the bow. Their horses graze on lotus and parsley, their chariots stand under covers, and the men wander through the camp “longing for their leader, dear to Ares”. It is a picture of peace inside the war, and it answers the end of Book 1, where Achilles himself sits by the ships longing for the war cry (1.488–492). See [[longing-for-achilles]].

**Deaths foretold.** The Trojan Catalogue looks ahead to his return. Ennomus the augur, and the Carian leader who came to war wearing gold like a girl, will be “brought down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus in the river” (2.858–861, 2.872–875). The patronymic Αἰακίδης (“grandson of Aeacus”) is heard here for the first time, and the river battle of Book 21 is foretold long before Achilles returns to the fighting. See [[the-river-battle-foretold]] and [[aeacus]].

He is also the measure of beauty: Nireus was the handsomest man who came to Ilios “after the noble son of Peleus” (2.673–674).
