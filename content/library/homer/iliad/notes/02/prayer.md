**Two prayers before battle.** When the army scatters to its meal, each man sacrifices to one god or another, “praying to escape death and the toil of Ares” (2.401). At the same hour Agamemnon prays for victory.

{{quote:e9e44cde-e73c-57fb-9ec1-19f624604c58}}

He calls on Zeus with a string of titles, “most glorious, greatest, lord of the dark clouds, who dwell in the upper air”, and claims nothing; the sacrifice under way stands in place of a claim. His request is large and has a deadline. Priam's hall is to be thrown down and burned, Hector's tunic torn on his chest, and his companions are to bite the dust, all before the sun goes down. It is the Dream's promise, that he could take Troy “now” (2.29), turned into a request.

{{quote:dcc1fe5e-205b-5baf-b9fe-5a78e31a6144}}

Both of Chryses' prayers in Book 1 closed with “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (1.43, 1.457). This one closes with a refusal. ἐπεκραίαινε, “fulfill”, is the verb of the petitioner's “fulfill for me this wish” (κρήηνον ἐέλδωρ, 1.41; see [[fulfill-this-wish]]), and here the narrator uses it to say that Zeus would not. The refusal is for now: οὐδʼ … πω, “not yet”. Zeus takes the offering, and Troy will fall in its time, but not today. The men prayed to escape the toil of war; Zeus makes the toil greater. See [[prayer-type-scene]].

**Wishes and boasts.** Agamemnon's cry after Nestor's speech, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, I had ten such counselors among the Achaeans!” (2.371–372), has the form of a prayer, three gods invoked and a wish, and serves as praise of the old man (see [[if-only-father-zeus]]). εὔχομαι and εὐχωλή also keep their sense of claiming and boasting. The man who saw the dream “claims (εὔχεται) to be by far the best of the Achaeans”, says Nestor, in nearly the words Achilles used in Book 1 (2.82; 1.91). If the army flees, it will leave Helen to the Trojans as “a thing to boast of” (εὐχωλή, 2.160, 2.176). Thamyris “boasted” (εὐχόμενος) that he would win even if the Muses sang against him (2.597).
