---
summary: Book 2 uses the epithet of the kings as holders of authority from Zeus, and extends it to warriors of the past and to the father of a Thracian ally.
grc: [διοτρεφέες βασιλῆες, διοτρεφέων αἰζηῶν, διοτρεφέος]
---

The kings' epithet marks each stage of the army's return to order. The heralds shout to make the people listen “to the kings whom Zeus has nurtured” ({{ref:Iliad 2.98}}). Odysseus warns a fleeing king that the leaders' anger is dangerous:

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

The lines spell out what the epithet means: a king's honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus loves him ([[kingship-and-authority]], [[counselor]]). When the army is called to war, the Zeus-nurtured kings marshal it around Agamemnon ({{ref:Iliad 2.445}}).

The Catalogue uses the adjective more loosely. Heracles sacked “many cities of young men whom Zeus had nurtured” (διοτρεφέων αἰζηῶν, {{ref:Iliad 2.660}}), and the Thracian leader Euphemus is the son of Troezenus, “whom Zeus had nurtured” ({{ref:Iliad 2.847}}).
