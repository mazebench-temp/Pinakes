---
summary: The verb κοτέω occurs once in Book 2, for the whole army's resentment as Thersites rails at Agamemnon; the Greek leaves open which of the two men the army resents.
grc: [κοτέοντο]
en: [resented]
---

{{quote:4d45c4f3-8ae1-50ae-a681-446c5dcb9f4c}}

As Thersites screams his insults at Agamemnon, “the Achaeans resented him terribly, and were indignant in their hearts” (ἐκπάγλως κοτέοντο νεμέσσηθέν τʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 2.223). The Greek says only τῷ, “with him” (2.222), and the pronoun has been read both ways. The nearest name is Agamemnon's (2.221), and an army angry with its king would explain why Thersites dares to speak. On the other reading the army resents Thersites, which suits the “but he” that follows (αὐτὰρ ὃ, 2.224) and the laughter at his beating (2.270). The translation's “him” keeps the ambiguity.

In Book 1 κότος was the grudge of one great man: the king's grudge that Calchas feared (1.80–83; see [[a-kings-grudge]]), and Achilles' resentment, which Agamemnon said he did not mind (1.181). Here a whole army feels it, and beside it stands νεμεσάω (“be indignant”), the anger at something that should not be done (see [[nemesis]]). A little earlier Odysseus warned the kings in Calchas' spirit: great is the spirit of kings whom Zeus has nurtured, and Agamemnon in his anger may do the army harm (2.195–197). See [[thersites]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].
