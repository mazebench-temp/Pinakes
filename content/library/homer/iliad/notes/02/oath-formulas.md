---
summary: No one in Book 2 swears by a god, but Odysseus stakes his head and his name as the father of Telemachus on his threat to Thersites (2.259–260), a curse on himself that has the force of an oath.
refs: [2.259-260]

---

Book 2 has no ναὶ μά or οὐ μά and no great oath. Its one oath-like speech is Odysseus' threat to Thersites. An oath calls down harm on the one who swears if his word proves false, and Odysseus does the same without the words of swearing.

{{quote:9f242ea9-2026-5f64-b415-010ee8baaf42}}

The pledge is his own person: his head, and his name as a father. He speaks of himself first in the third person, “the head of Odysseus”, as if naming the thing he pledges, and then in the first, “may I no longer be called”. The condition follows: “if I do not take you and strip off your own clothes” (2.261). The formula of certainty introduces the whole (2.257; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). Telemachus is named in the Iliad only here and at 4.354, and both times Odysseus is speaking of himself as “the father of Telemachus” (see [[telemachus]]).

The oaths of ritual appear in Nestor's rebuke: “What will become of our compacts and our oaths? Into the fire, then, with the counsels and plans of men, the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted!” (2.339–341). These are the sworn agreements of the expedition, made with libations and handclasps (see [[oath]] and [[libation]]). Agamemnon, too, imagines the Achaeans and the Trojans swearing “a trusty truce” (ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμόντες, 2.124) so that both armies could be counted. The phrase recurs in Book 3, when a truce is sworn in earnest.
