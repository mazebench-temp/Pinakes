summary: In the assemblies of Book 2 μῦθος is the word that carries weight: the command the Dream obeys, the rebuke Odysseus gives the commoners, the speech the army cheers. At the end Iris tells Priam that the time for endless μῦθοι is past.

**Commands.** The first μῦθος of the book is Zeus' order to the Dream: “the Dream went, when he had heard the command” (τὸν μῦθον ἄκουσε, 2.16). The Dream's message to Agamemnon is a μῦθος too (2.59), and when the army has run for the ships, Hera's μῦθος to Athena stops a homecoming against fate (2.155–156). Each is an order that moves the story.

**Gentle words and a μῦθος.** Odysseus goes through the camp with Agamemnon's scepter. A king or a man of note he holds back “with gentle words” (ἀγανοῖς ἐπέεσσιν, 2.189), the phrase of Hera's and Athena's instructions (2.164, 2.180). A man of the people he strikes with the scepter and rebukes “with a word” (ὁμοκλήσασκέ τε μύθῳ, 2.199), and tells him to sit still and “listen to the word (μῦθον) of others who are better than you” (2.200). The kings are given ἔπεα (see [[epos]]); the commoner is given a blow and a μῦθος, and his part is to listen.

[[Thersites]] takes the word for himself: he “abused Agamemnon in these words” (νείκεε μύθῳ, 2.224). Odysseus answers “with harsh words” (χαλεπῷ … μύθῳ, 2.245) and calls him ἀκριτόμυθος, “reckless in speech”, a man whose words have no judgment in them (2.246; see [[reckless-in-speech]]). Then Athena, in the likeness of a herald, silences the crowd so that all may hear Odysseus' μῦθος (2.280–282), and the army shouts its approval, “as they praised the words of divine Odysseus” (2.335).

**Beginning and telling.** After Agamemnon's sacrifice Nestor “began to speak” (μύθων ἦρχε, 2.433); the same two lines bring him to speech after the feast at Pylos (Odyssey 3.67–68). The poet uses the verb for the telling he cannot do alone: “the multitude I could not tell (μυθήσομαι) or name” (2.488; see [[ten-tongues]] and [[invocation-of-the-muses]]).

**Endless words.** The last μῦθοι of the book are Trojan. Iris, in the likeness of Polites, finds Priam holding assembly and tells him: “Old man, endless words (μῦθοι … ἄκριτοι) are always dear to you, as once in time of peace; but relentless war has come” (2.796–797). Thersites' epithet ἀκριτόμυθος is made of the same two words, and “always dear to you” is the reproach of Book 1's quarrels (1.107, 1.177; see [[philos]]). Nestor had told Agamemnon the same thing in his own camp: “let us not sit here talking any longer” (2.435). In both camps Book 2 ends the time for talk.
