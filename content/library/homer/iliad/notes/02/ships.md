In Book 1 the ships were the camp, the way home, and the trap Achilles wished on the army. Book 2 nearly puts them to sea.

## The launch that nearly happens

Agamemnon's test speech makes the ships an argument for going home. Nine years have passed, “and the timbers of our ships have rotted, and the ropes have come loose” (δοῦρα σέσηπε νεῶν καὶ σπάρτα λέλυνται). δοῦρα is δόρυ in its old sense, “wood” (see [[spear]]).

{{quote:ad37b500-9392-5d64-a4fb-6bf0d32191a7}}

He ends, “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (2.140), and the men take him at his word.

{{quote:5b5cf69b-f2b3-5737-a0d6-1a1445f8fa3c}}

The launch is told in its order. They lay hold of the ships to drag them down to the sea, clear out the launching-trenches (οὐροί), the channels in the sand along which a ship was hauled, and pull away the [[props]] (ἕρματα) that held the beached hulls upright. “Then the Argives would have had their homecoming against fate” (2.155; see [[nostos]]), had Hera not sent Athena: “do not let them drag their curving ships to the sea” (2.165, 2.181). Odysseus alone had not laid a hand on his ship (2.170–171), and he turns the army back (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

The speeches that follow keep the ships in view. Odysseus grants the men their longing: a man kept from his wife for a single month by winter storms chafes beside his ship, and they have waited nine years (2.292–297). He reminds them of the ships gathering at [[aulis|Aulis]] (2.303). Nestor turns the ship into a threat: whoever is desperate to go home, “let him lay hands on his black, well-benched ship, / so that before the others he may meet his death and his fate” (2.358–359). Agamemnon promises the dogs and the birds to any man who lingers “beside the curved ships” (2.391–393). The way home has become the coward's place.

## The ships counted

The poet announces the Catalogue as a list of ships: “I will tell the leaders of the ships, and all the ships” (ἀρχοὺς αὖ νηῶν ἐρέω νῆάς τε προπάσας, 2.493). Each entry gives its number of ships, most often in one of two verses, “and with him followed forty black ships” (2.534 and often) and “thirty hollow ships were ranged in line” (2.516 and elsewhere). Twice the crews are given: a hundred and twenty young men in each Boeotian ship (2.509–510), and fifty rowers, all archers, in each of Philoctetes' seven (2.719–720). Thucydides worked from these figures when he estimated the size of the fleet (see [[thucydides-and-the-catalogue]]). The Arcadians, an inland people, sailed in ships that Agamemnon gave them:

{{quote:4dfff20e-2a16-553c-b456-a32d9b85d1a6}}

For the numbers and the verses, see [[catalogue-of-ships]]. Book 2 also adds new epithets of the ships: [[many-benched]], [[well-benched]], [[curving]], and “with red-painted prows” ([[red-painted]]).

At the end of the list Achilles lies idle “among his curved, seafaring ships” (2.771; also 2.688), where Thetis told him to sit (1.421, 1.488). On the Trojan side Polites keeps watch from the tomb of Aesyetes for the moment the Achaeans set out “from their ships” (ναῦφιν, 2.794), an old case form in -φι.
