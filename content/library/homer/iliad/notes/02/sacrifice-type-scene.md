**In brief.** The pattern takes a single line at Aulis, where the army “were offering perfect hecatombs to the immortals” (2.306), a variant of the line for the sacrifice on the shore in Book 1 (1.315). It takes two when the men scatter to their meal before battle: “each man sacrificed to one or another of the everlasting gods, praying to escape death and the toil of Ares” (2.400–401).

**In full.** Agamemnon's sacrifice to Zeus follows the sacrifice at Chryse step by step.

{{quote:38fbdd6d-1083-523c-92e9-115e34157683}}

Most of it is the same verse. The killing and the god's portion (2.421–424 = 1.458–461) and the tasting, roasting and feast (2.427–432 = 1.464–469) repeat Book 1 word for word. The differences come where the occasion differs.

1. **Preparation.** At Chryse the victims were set in order around the altar and the men washed their hands (1.448–449). Here the kings “stood around the ox and took up the barley grains” (2.410). The half-line with the barley is the same; there is no washing, and no altar is named. See [[barley-grains]] and [[hand-washing]].
2. **Prayer.** Chryses prayed “lifting up his hands” (1.450). Agamemnon speaks “praying among them” (2.411), with no gesture. See [[prayer-type-scene]].
3. **Answer.** “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (1.457) becomes a refusal: “the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill his prayer; he accepted the offerings, but made the toil greater” (2.419–420). As at Chryse, the answer is told before the victim is killed. The audience knows, and the kings do not, that the rest of the rite is performed for a prayer already refused.
4. **Burning.** At Chryse “the old man” burned the thigh-pieces on split wood and poured wine over them, while young men held the five-pronged forks (1.462–463). Here there is no priest and no wine. “They” burn the thigh-pieces “on split logs stripped of their leaves”, and spit the entrails and hold them over “the flame of Hephaestus” (2.425–426). See [[five-pronged-forks]] and [[libation]].
5. **Ending.** At Chryse the meal led to wine, a day of song, and sleep (1.470–476). Here the line that ends the eating (2.432) is followed by Nestor's call to arms, and the heralds summon the army to war (2.433–444; see [[feast-type-scene]]).

The changes fit the occasion. Chryses was Apollo's priest, and his sacrifice was a reconciliation, told at leisure. Agamemnon is a king sacrificing on the morning of battle, and the scene is cut short at both ends. What the two scenes share matters as much. The rite is done correctly and the offering is accepted; the difference lies in the god's plan. At Chryse the sacrifice restored the Achaeans' bond with Apollo. Here a correct sacrifice and a proper prayer meet the plan of Zeus, who has nodded assent to Thetis' request to give the Trojans strength until the Achaeans honor her son (1.508–510, 1.524–527; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

One word runs through the scene. Menelaus comes because he knows “how his brother was laboring” (ἐπονεῖτο, 2.409); Zeus makes “the toil” (πόνον) greater (2.420); and the kings eat “when they had ceased from the work” (πόνου, 2.430). The work of the sacrifice is done. The toil of war is still to come.
