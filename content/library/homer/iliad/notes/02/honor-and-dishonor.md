**Achilles' honor.** Book 2 opens with Zeus awake, pondering “how Achilles he might honor, and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans” (2.3–4). The words are Hera's from Book 1 (1.559). The honor Thetis asked for will be paid in Achaean lives, and the lying Dream is the first installment (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[agamemnons-dream]]). Achilles himself stays out of sight. The Catalogue shows him lying idle by his ships, “angry over the girl” (2.688–694), and gives the title of best man to Telamonian Ajax “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]).

**The king's disgrace.** In the test Agamemnon speaks as a man about to lose face. Zeus bids him go back to Argos δυσκλέα, “in disgrace”, and it is “a shameful thing, even for men to come to hear of”, that so great an army should achieve nothing (2.115–122; see [[kleos]] and [[aischros]]). Hera and Athena fear the same loss for the whole army: fleeing, the Argives would leave Helen behind as “a thing to boast of” for Priam and the Trojans (2.160–162, 2.176–178; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Honor is measured against the enemy. What one side gives up, the other boasts of.

**Thersites.** Thersites turns Achilles' grievance into a general one. The Achaeans give Agamemnon the choicest women “first of all, whenever we take a citadel” (2.226–228), and he keeps what others win.

{{quote:6e9cc963-7c75-593c-8598-2eb519a7eceb}}

The second line is the one Achilles spoke to Thetis and Thetis to Zeus (1.356, 1.507), and the last is Achilles' threat in the quarrel (1.232; see [[thersites]]). Odysseus concedes the fact: the Danaans “give him so very much” (2.255). His answer is to dishonor the speaker. He threatens to strip off the clothes “which cover your nakedness” (τά τʼ αἰδῶ ἀμφικαλύπτει) and drive him from the assembly “with shameful blows” (ἀεικέσσι πληγῇσιν, 2.261–264), and the army laughs at the beaten man (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

**Honor from Zeus.** To the kings Odysseus states the principle behind Agamemnon's rank.

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

Agamemnon claimed as much in Book 1: others would honor him, “and above all Zeus the counselor” (1.175). Book 2 bears him out and undercuts him on the same day. Zeus has deceived him with the Dream, yet makes him “conspicuous among many and preeminent among heroes” at the marshaling (2.482–483), and the Catalogue says he stood out “because he was the best, and led by far the most men” (2.579–580).

**The army's shame.** For the army, honor means not going home empty-handed. Odysseus tells Agamemnon that the Achaeans want to make him “the most despised of all mortal men” (ἐλέγχιστον) by breaking their promise to him (2.284–288). He grants that nine years is long to wait, “but all the same, it is shameful to stay so long and go home empty-handed” (2.297–298; see [[shameful-to-stay-long]]). Nestor names the payment the army is owed: no one should go home before he has made the Trojans pay (τίσασθαι) for the struggles and groans for Helen's sake (2.354–356), and the Catalogue gives Menelaus the same wish (2.590). The quarrel of Book 1 was over one man's honor; here the honor of the whole army is at stake.
