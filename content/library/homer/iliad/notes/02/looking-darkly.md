summary: Odysseus turns the glare on Thersites (2.245), and the verse names the kind of speech that follows. He does not simply speak; he “rebuked him with harsh words.”
passages:
  - 2.244-247 | Odysseus looks darkly at Thersites
---

In Book 1 the glare passed between the two greatest men in the army. In Book 2 it goes down the scale, from a king to a man to whom the poem gives no father and no city, and it answers exactly the offense that J. P. Holoka took the formula to mark: a breach of status. Thersites has abused the king in the king's own assembly, and Odysseus' first words name the fault. He must not “try all alone to quarrel with kings” (2.247), nor “have the kings in your mouth as you harangue us” (2.250).

The frame is not the usual one. The reply formula is τὸν δʼ ἄρʼ ὑπόδρα ἰδὼν προσέφη, with a plain verb of speaking (1.148). Here the verse runs καί μιν ὑπόδρα ἰδὼν χαλεπῷ ἠνίπαπε μύθῳ, “and looking at him darkly rebuked him with harsh words” (2.245). ἠνίπαπε, from ἐνίπτω (“rebuke, reproach”), and χαλεπῷ μύθῳ (“with a harsh word”) say at once what kind of speech is coming. Nor is it a reply. Thersites spoke to Agamemnon and to the army (2.225–242), and Odysseus answers for the king. See [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

Two more looks follow within a few lines. Beaten, Thersites wipes away his tear “with a helpless look” (ἀχρεῖον ἰδών, 2.269), and the army comments on the beating “looking at his neighbor” (ἰδὼν ἐς πλησίον ἄλλον, 2.271; see [[tis-speech]]). The glare of the king, the helpless look of the beaten man, the glance the crowd exchanges: the scene is told in looks as well as in words.
