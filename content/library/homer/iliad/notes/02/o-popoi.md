---
summary: Three speeches in Book 2 open with the cry, Hera's to Athena (2.157), the crowd's comment on the beating of Thersites (2.272) and Nestor's rebuke of the assembly (2.337). It carries dismay, admiring surprise and reproach.
---

**Hera.** Watching the Argives run for the ships, Hera turns to Athena: “Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone, is it so, then, that home to their dear native land the Argives will flee” (2.157–159). The cry is dismay at a homecoming against fate (2.155). Here it is followed not by ἦ but by an address and an indignant question, οὕτω δή (“is it so, then”). When Athena passes the words on to Odysseus, she keeps the question and drops the cry (2.173–174; see [[message-repeated]]).

**The crowd.** When Odysseus has beaten Thersites, “a man” says to his neighbor: “Ah, truly Odysseus has done countless good deeds” (2.272). The cry is neither grief nor anger but surprise and approval, and it leads, as it usually does, into ἦ (“truly”). See [[tis-speech]].

**Nestor.** Nestor began his plea to the two kings with the cry in Book 1 (1.254). He begins his rebuke of the army with it in Book 2.

{{quote:008866c5-f953-520f-b78f-646b2ecc723a}}

The crowd's comment and Nestor's rebuke open with the same words, ὢ πόποι ἦ δή (“ah, truly now”, 2.272, 2.337), and the same opening serves opposite moods. The crowd is pleased with Odysseus; Nestor is angry with the whole assembly.
