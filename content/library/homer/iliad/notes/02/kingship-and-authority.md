**Council and assembly.** The Dream defines the king's office: “A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through, a man to whom the people are entrusted” (2.24–25; see [[a-man-of-counsel-should-not-sleep]]). Book 2 then shows the machinery of command. Agamemnon first seats a council of elders beside Nestor's ship; then nine heralds seat the people, so that they may listen “to the kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (2.53–55, 2.96–98; see [[elders]], [[assembly]] and [[heralds]]). Nestor accepts the dream because of who dreamed it, the man who “claims to be by far the best of the Achaeans” (2.82). Achilles said it in scorn in Book 1 (1.91); Nestor says it in earnest.

**The scepter and the test.** Agamemnon rises holding the scepter that came down from Zeus through the house of Pelops, and uses its authority to give an order he hopes will be refused (see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The army obeys at once. His word carries all the weight the scepter gives it, and he has turned it against himself.

**One ruler.** Odysseus restores order with the king's scepter in his hand, and states the doctrine twice. To the kings: “a king's honor comes from Zeus” (2.197). To the men of the people:

Then he goes through the army “like a master” (κοιρανέων, 2.207; see [[one-ruler]] and [[koiranos]]). Thersites offers another idea of the leader's office: “It is not right for one who is their leader to bring the sons of the Achaeans to grief” (2.233–234). Odysseus tells him not to quarrel with kings or “have the kings in your mouth” (2.247–250), and beats him with the scepter (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

**Counsel.** Nestor tells Agamemnon to lead the Argives “as before, holding to your steadfast purpose”, but also to “take good counsel yourself, and listen to another” (2.344–345, 2.360). If the Achaeans obey him, marshaled by tribes and clans, he will learn which of his leaders are cowards (2.362–368; see [[phylon]]). Agamemnon answers with rare generosity. He wishes for ten such counselors, and says of the quarrel, “I was the first to grow angry” (2.370–378). Then he gives orders, and ends with a threat of death for any man who hangs back by the ships (2.391–393; see [[aristotle-death-in-my-hand]]).

**Preeminent.** At the marshaling Zeus makes him “conspicuous among many and preeminent among heroes”, like a bull above the herd (2.480–483; see [[bull-above-the-herd]]). The Catalogue names the ground of his rank: a hundred ships, “because he was the best, and led by far the most men” (2.576–580). It is Nestor's argument from Book 1, that he “rules over more” (1.281). The Catalogue also names the limit. The best of the men was Telamonian Ajax while Achilles kept up his wrath, “for he was by far the strongest” (2.768–769), the words Hephaestus used of Zeus (1.581).

**Troy.** The Trojans take their orders from a goddess. Iris, in the likeness of Polites, gives her charge to Hector rather than to Priam: the allies speak many tongues, so “let each man give commands to those he leads” (2.802–806). The Achaeans have been told to accept one king. The Trojan army is a coalition, each part under its own leader (see [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]).
