---
summary: In Book 2 the word is not the war cry but the clamor of the assembly and the shout of men rushing to launch their ships for home, until the army shouts its approval of Odysseus' call to stay.
grc: [=ἀϋτῆς, =ἀϋτὴ, ἀϋσάντων]
en: [clamor, shout, the shouting]
---

**The clamor.** The army pours into the assembly and the earth groans under it. Nine heralds shout to make the men hold back from their ἀϋτή (“clamor”) and listen to the kings (2.96–98).

{{quote:9af04649-5dbb-5633-8a14-d186df2b660a}}

**The shout for home.** Agamemnon's test works too well. The men set off “with a shout” (ἀλαλητῷ, another word for the battle cry, 2.149) and run for the ships, calling to one another to drag them down to the sea, and “their shout reached the sky as they longed for home” (ἀϋτὴ δʼ οὐρανὸν ἷκεν / οἴκαδε ἱεμένων, 2.153–154). The cry that should carry men into battle carries them away from it. In Book 1 Achilles, kept from the fighting, “longed for the war cry and for battle” (1.492). Here the whole army raises the cry and longs for home. See [[the-test-of-the-army]].

{{quote:5b5cf69b-f2b3-5737-a0d6-1a1445f8fa3c}}

**The shout turned.** After Odysseus has recalled the portent at Aulis, the Argives shout aloud, and all around them the ships echo terribly “with the shouting of the Achaeans” (ἀϋσάντων, from the verb ἀΰω, “shout”) as they praise his words ({{ref:Iliad 2.333-335}}). The army that shouted for home now shouts for staying. Its next shout, at the end of Agamemnon's call to battle, is compared to a wave breaking on a jutting crag (2.394–397; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]).
