summary: Troy is the prize the Dream promises and Agamemnon prays for, “wide-streeted”, “well-walled”, the great city of Priam. At the end of the book the poem goes inside it for the first time, and the Trojans pour out of its gates onto the plain.

**Two names.** Book 2 uses both, Ἴλιος (2.113, 2.133, 2.216, 2.230, 2.249, 2.288, 2.492, 2.673) and Τροίη (2.141, 2.162, 2.178, 2.237). Several times the army is described as all those “who came to Ilios” (2.216, 2.249, 2.492, 2.673): the men are counted by where they went.

**The city promised.** The Dream tells Agamemnon that he “may take the wide-streeted city (πόλιν εὐρυάγυιαν) of the Trojans” (2.12–13). In the test he tells the army the opposite: “we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (2.141). Zeus once promised him, he says, that he would sack “well-walled Ilios” and sail home (2.113), and Odysseus reminds the army that it made him the same promise on its way from Argos (2.286–288). Calchas foretold that “in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (2.329). See [[wide-streeted]], [[well-walled]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]].

**Priam's city.** Agamemnon prays to throw down Priam's hall, blackened with smoke, and burn its doors (2.414–415). In his test he sets the Trojans “who have hearths in the city” against the allies from many cities, who are the real obstacle (2.123–133). The city holds men of many tongues, Iris tells Hector, and each must be led by his own commander (2.803–806).

**Inside the walls.** At the end of the book the poem enters Troy. The Trojans are holding an assembly at Priam's doors when Iris comes (2.788). Hector breaks it up, all the gates are opened, and the army pours out to form up at the mound called Batieia on the plain before the city (2.807–815). See [[trojans]], [[priam]] and [[batieia]].
