**Honor for Achilles.** The book begins with honor. Zeus lies awake, pondering how he might honor Achilles (τιμήσῃ) and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans (2.3–4). The second line repeats, almost word for word, what Hera guessed he had promised Thetis (1.559). The honor Thetis asked for her son (1.505–510) will be paid for with Achaean lives.

{{quote:f27e722e-0793-5781-bd01-1ef76081bef8}}

**Nestor's shape.** The Dream comes in the likeness of Nestor, “whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders” (τῖʼ, 2.21). The king's regard for his best counselor is the opening the god uses (see [[agamemnons-dream]]).

**A king's honor.** Odysseus reminds the kings he turns back that “a king's honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves him” (2.197). The line restates Nestor's scepter-bearing king, “to whom Zeus has given glory” (1.278–279), and Agamemnon's boast that others will honor him, “and above all Zeus the counselor” (1.175). The reader knows what Odysseus does not: at this moment Zeus is honoring Achilles at Agamemnon's cost. See [[kingship-and-authority]].

**Achilles' complaint.** Thersites charges that Agamemnon “has dishonored” (ἠτίμησεν) Achilles, a far better man than himself (2.239–240).

{{quote:6e9cc963-7c75-593c-8598-2eb519a7eceb}}

The second line is Achilles' complaint to Thetis (1.356), which Thetis repeated to Zeus (1.507). What was said privately by the sea and on Olympus is now shouted in the assembly by the ugliest man who came to Ilios (2.216). See [[thersites]] and [[geras]].

**Worth and payment.** The aegis that Athena carries is ἐρίτιμος, “highly prized”, and each of its hundred golden tassels is worth a hundred oxen (2.447–449): τιμή as value, reckoned in cattle. Twice the book speaks of paying the Trojans back “for the struggles and groans for Helen's sake” (τίσασθαι, 2.356, 2.590). That verb is τίνω, “pay, pay back”, not τίω, but it answers the τιμή for Menelaus that Achilles said the army came to win (1.159; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). The test is full of the language of shame: going home “in disgrace” (δυσκλέα, 2.115), “a shameful thing” for men to come to hear of (2.119). See [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[aischros]].
