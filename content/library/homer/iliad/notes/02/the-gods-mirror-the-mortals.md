summary: In Book 2 gods and men plan and deceive in parallel. Zeus plans a deception in the night and Agamemnon one at dawn; Hera sends Athena and Athena sends Odysseus, as kings send heralds; and gods walk among men in the shapes of a counselor, a herald and a prince.

**Two plans.** Zeus lies awake, and “this plan seemed to him in his heart the best”: a lying Dream for Agamemnon (2.5–6; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). At dawn Agamemnon seats a council of elders and “laid out a shrewd plan” of his own (πυκινὴν ἀρτύνετο βουλήν, 2.55): he will test the army with words, telling it to flee (2.73–75; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The god deceives the king, and the king deceives his army. The army believes its king, as the king believed the god.

**A council of gods.** In the Dream's message the gods themselves are an assembly. The immortals “are no longer of two minds; she has bent them all, Hera, with her pleading” (2.13–15). It is a lie, but it pictures Olympus in the terms of a human council, divided until one speaker bends the rest.

**Messengers.** Hera sends Athena, and Athena passes the message to Odysseus in the same words (2.157–181; see [[message-repeated]]). Odysseus runs to Agamemnon, receives the king's scepter and goes through the camp as his deputy, while his own herald picks up the cloak he has thrown off (2.182–187). The chain of command runs unbroken from Olympus to the ships: goddess to goddess, goddess to man, king to deputy, master to herald. Rumor, “the messenger of Zeus”, drives the army to the assembly, “urging them on” (ὀτρύνουσʼ ἰέναι, 2.93–94), as Athena with the aegis will later drive it to war in the same words (2.451), while nine human heralds shout to hold it back (2.96–97).

**Gods in disguise.** Three times a god takes a mortal's place in the human order: the Dream as Nestor, the counselor (2.20–22), Athena as a herald calling for silence (2.279–282), and Iris as Priam's son Polites, the Trojans' lookout (2.791–795). Each borrows an office as well as a face (see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Two kings.** Zeus “rules over gods and men” (2.669). At the marshaling Agamemnon is made to look like Zeus, Ares and Poseidon at once (2.477–479; see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]). The poem sets its human king beside its divine one on the very day the god is deceiving him.
