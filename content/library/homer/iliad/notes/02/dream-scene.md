---
summary: The Dream that Zeus sends to Agamemnon is the first dream in the Iliad, told in full by the narrator (2.16–41) and then again by Agamemnon to the council (2.56–71). Every part of the scene serves a deception.
---

**The visit.** The Dream finds Agamemnon asleep in his hut, “and ambrosial sleep was poured around him” (2.19). It stands above his head in the likeness of Nestor.

{{quote:778f87ad-7b57-521e-aef3-1117ebe920b5}}

Each part of the pattern is turned to the purpose of the lie. The likeness is of Nestor, “whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders” (2.21): the false message comes in the shape of the one counselor the king trusts. The opening reproach, “You are asleep”, becomes a maxim of kingship, that a man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through (2.23–25; see [[a-man-of-counsel-should-not-sleep]]). The Dream names its sender, “I am a messenger to you from Zeus”, and claims for him a care he never expressed (2.26–27). Then it delivers Zeus' message word for word (see [[message-repeated]]) and ends with a warning not to forget it when sleep lets him go (2.33–34).

**The departure and the waking.** The Dream goes, and the narrator speaks before Agamemnon wakes.

{{quote:130425b2-40e3-5355-8921-905c49fc817a}}

“He woke from his sleep, and the divine voice was poured around him” (θείη δέ μιν ἀμφέχυτʼ ὀμφή, 2.41). Sleep was “poured around” him when the Dream came (κέχυθʼ, 2.19); now the voice is. The dream is over, but its voice is still there, around him and outside him, as Dodds' account of the Homeric dream would lead one to expect. He dresses and goes to act on it (see [[dressing-scene]]).

**The dream retold.** At dawn Agamemnon tells the elders at Nestor's ship what he saw, and tells it as a visit, in the words of the scene. The Dream “came to me in my sleep through the ambrosial night”, it was most like Nestor “in looks and stature and build”, and “he stood above my head” (2.56–59). He ends with its going: it “flew away and was gone, and sweet sleep let me go” (2.71; see [[retelling]]). Nestor accepts the dream because of who saw it. From anyone else, “we would call it a lie” (2.80–82; see [[pseudos]]). See [[agamemnons-dream]], [[dream]] and [[onar]].
