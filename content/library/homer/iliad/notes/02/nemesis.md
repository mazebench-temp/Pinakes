passages:
  - 2.220-224 | Thersites abuses Agamemnon
  - 2.291-297 | I do not blame the Achaeans
---

Book 2 has the verb twice: once of the army's indignation, once of indignation withheld.

**At Thersites.** Thersites, who usually abuses Achilles and Odysseus, turns on Agamemnon.

{{quote:4d45c4f3-8ae1-50ae-a681-446c5dcb9f4c}}

The Achaeans “resented him terribly, and were indignant in their hearts” (ἐκπάγλως κοτέοντο νεμέσσηθέν τʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 2.223). κοτέοντο is anger stored up ([[kotos]]); νεμέσσηθεν is the sense that a line has been crossed. The pronoun τῷ, “at him”, is usually taken of Thersites, who is shouting insults at the king in the assembly. Some readers have referred it to Agamemnon, the last man named, and so made Thersites the voice of the army's anger. The rest of the scene favors the usual reading: when Odysseus beats Thersites, the army laughs and praises the blow (2.270–277). See [[thersites]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

**Blame withheld.** Later Odysseus stands with the scepter and speaks to the whole army. He grants the men their longing before he answers it.

{{quote:1c16b0ee-9de9-5474-a1e4-8dad82efbb6a}}

A man away from his wife even for a month chafes by his ship when the winter storms keep him in, and this is the ninth year. “So I do not blame the Achaeans” (τὼ οὐ νεμεσίζομʼ Ἀχαιούς) for chafing beside the ships. The concession is real, and it is the first step of the argument. The next line turns it: “but all the same, it is shameful to stay so long and go home empty-handed” (2.298; see [[shameful-to-stay-long]] and [[aischros]]). Odysseus will not blame the men for wanting home, but going home would earn them the blame of others. Agamemnon had used the same thought in the test: it is a shameful thing “even for men to come to hear of” (2.119).
