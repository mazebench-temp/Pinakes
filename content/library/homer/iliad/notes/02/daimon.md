---
summary: Odysseus begins both of his speeches to the fleeing army with δαιμόνιε, “strange one”, to a king and to a man of the people alike.
grc: [δαιμόνιʼ]
---

Odysseus goes through the camp with Agamemnon's scepter to stop the flight to the ships. He speaks to kings and to men of the people in turn, and both speeches begin with the same word, δαιμόνιʼ (for δαιμόνιε), “strange one” (2.190, 2.200). What follows differs. A king hears that it is not right for him to panic like a coward, and is asked to sit down and seat the others. A commoner gets a blow of the scepter and an order: sit still, and listen to your betters (see [[kings]] and [[commoners]]). The word implies that the man is not himself, as if some δαίμων (“divine power”) were driving him; what drives him here is the panic. In Book 1 it passed between husband and wife (1.561). See [[strange-one]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]].
