summary: “A shameful thing”, says Agamemnon in the test, and Odysseus says it again in earnest: after so long, to go home empty-handed is a disgrace. Between the two stands Thersites, “the ugliest man who came to Ilios”.
passages:

The word occurs three times in Book 2, and it frames the book's argument about going home.

**A shameful thing to hear of.** In the test speech Agamemnon gives a reason not to go home, in the middle of arguing that they must.

{{quote:98a05387-8986-53ad-ba9e-69b79c016a16}}

It is αἰσχρόν, “even for men to come to hear of” (καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι), that so fine and so great an army should wage a war that comes to nothing against fewer men. The shame is measured by what later generations will hear, the dark side of [[kleos|κλέος]]. The argument sits oddly in a speech that ends “let us flee” (2.140), and the army hears only the ending (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

**The ugliest man.** The narrator uses the superlative once, of Thersites.

{{quote:84632ffd-0ab5-5371-b980-4a4b1dabcf30}}

αἴσχιστος comes directly after the account of his speech, disorderly words spoken to make the Argives laugh (γελοίϊον, 2.215). The body is then described defect by defect. His looks match his standing, and Odysseus measures him on the same scale: there is no mortal worse than he “of all who came with the sons of Atreus to Ilios” (2.248–249). See [[thersites]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

The frame of the line, “the … man who came to Ilios”, returns once more, for Nireus: “the most handsome man who came to Ilios of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (2.673–674). The handsomest after Achilles is “a weakling, and few people followed him” (2.675; see [[nireus]]). The two superlatives stand far apart in the book. Thersites' ugliness matches his worth; Nireus' beauty does not.

**Shameful to stay long.** Odysseus takes up Agamemnon's argument and makes it his own, after granting the men their longing (2.291–297; see [[nemesis]]).

{{quote:117dd526-3442-5a8b-bb75-ee019e5d8b9d}}

The argument was a pretense in the king's mouth. In Odysseus' it persuades, and after the story of Aulis that follows, the Argives shout their approval (2.333–335). The length of the stay is what makes an empty return disgraceful. See [[shameful-to-stay-long]].
