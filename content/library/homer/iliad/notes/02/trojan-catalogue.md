---
summary: At the end of Book 2 the poem turns to the other side. Iris sends the Trojans out to meet the Achaeans, and the poet names their contingents, foretelling the deaths of several of their leaders.
---

**The call to arms.** The Achaeans are crossing the plain when Iris comes to the Trojans “with grievous news” from Zeus (2.786–787). They are holding an assembly at Priam's doors, “as once in time of peace” (2.797), and she speaks in the voice of Polites, the Trojans' lookout (2.791–795). The Achaeans are coming “like leaves or like the sands” (2.800; see [[like-leaves-or-sand]]); let each leader of the allies marshal his own countrymen (2.802–806). Hector knows the goddess's word, breaks up the assembly, and the army pours out of the gates (2.807–810). It is marshaled at a mound in the plain that men call Batieia and the immortals the tomb of Myrine (2.811–815; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). The Achaeans' ships have just been counted; the Trojans are named, not counted.

**The commanders.** The list opens with Hector, and his Trojans are described in the words used of Agamemnon's men: with him “by far the most and the best” (2.817–818; compare 2.577–578). Aeneas comes second, with his birth: Aphrodite bore him to Anchises on the slopes of Ida, “a goddess who lay with a mortal” (2.820–821; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]).

**Deaths foretold.** Three entries look ahead to deaths, and in each the narrator knows what the man does not. Merops of Percote, a seer, would not let his two sons go to the war, “but they would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.832–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Ennomus the augur led the Mysians, “but his birds did not save him from black doom”: he fell at Achilles' hands in the river (2.858–861). And one of the Carian leaders came to the war wearing gold, “like a girl”. The narrator calls him νήπιος (“the fool”), the word he used of Agamemnon when he believed the Dream (2.38; see [[nepios]]).

The two deaths in the river are told in the same verse (2.860 = 2.874; see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). The Catalogue of Ships also looks ahead, but to returns: Achilles “was to rise up” soon (2.694), and the Argives were soon to remember Philoctetes (2.724–725). The Trojan list looks ahead to deaths.

**Far away.** The allies come from farther and farther away, and the narrator marks it (2.849, 2.857, 2.863). The book ends with the most distant of them.

**Many tongues.** Iris' warning about the allies' many languages (2.804) is borne out by one word: the Carians are βαρβαρόφωνοι, “of barbarous speech” (2.867; see [[barbarophonos]]). Agamemnon had told the army that the Achaeans outnumbered the men of Troy many times over, but that allies “from many cities” kept him from taking it (2.130–133). The catalogue shows who they are.
