The words for a sign from the gods cluster in the speeches of Odysseus and Nestor (2.284–368). The army has just tried to sail home, and the two men argue it back into the war with signs from its own past.

**At Aulis.** Odysseus' story begins with the word σῆμα: “there a great sign appeared” (ἔνθʼ ἐφάνη μέγα σῆμα, 2.308). A serpent, sent “into the light” by Zeus himself, ate eight sparrow chicks and their mother, and the god who had brought it to light made it ἀρίζηλον, “a sign for all to see”, turning it to stone (2.318–319). An ancient variant read ἀΐζηλον, “unseen”. See [[the-portent-at-aulis]], [[serpent]] and [[sparrow]]. Then the story sums up what they saw, and Calchas names it.

{{quote:61cce681-3bc9-50a0-907b-080ef3797900}}

Three words name the event, and each catches a different side of it. σῆμα is what appears and is seen. πέλωρα, “dread portents” (δεινὰ πέλωρα θεῶν, 2.321), is the thing as a terror and a wonder: the army “stood there and marveled” (2.320). τέρας is the sign as a message from Zeus, which Calchas reads: “late in coming, late in fulfillment, whose fame shall never perish” (2.325; see [[kleos]]). The verb φαίνω, “show, bring to light”, runs through the passage (ἐφάνη 2.308, ἔφηνε 2.318, 2.324). The sign is shown by the god, and read by the seer. Nine birds are nine years, and in the tenth year Troy will fall. Odysseus draws the conclusion for the army: “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330).

**On the day of sailing.** Nestor adds a second sign from the same time.

{{quote:7440031a-789f-5696-b741-28c4914b88a7}}

Zeus nodded his assent and flashed his lightning on the right, “showing signs of good omen” (ἐναίσιμα σήματα φαίνων). The right is the side of good omen. Nestor uses the sign to bind the army: no one should go home before he knows whether Zeus' promise “is a lie or not” (see [[pseudos]] and [[the-departure-from-aulis]]). In Book 9, with the Trojans camped by the ships, Odysseus tells Achilles that Zeus now flashes his lightning on the right for them (9.236–237).

**A tomb.** The last σῆμα of the book is a grave. The mound before Troy that men call Batieia the immortals call “the tomb of much-bounding Myrine” (σῆμα, 2.814; see [[tomb]], [[myrine]], [[batieia]] and [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Here the word is the mark a grave leaves on the land, not a sign from the gods.
