**Counted by tens.** Agamemnon's speech in the test contains the book's first sum.

{{quote:5ddcff68-3c54-55eb-a231-009d914f2402}}

If Achaeans and Trojans swore a truce and were counted (ἀριθμηθήμεναι), with the Achaeans ranged in tens and each ten choosing a Trojan to pour its wine, “many a ten would go without a wine-pourer”. The count is a picture of a feast and a boast: the Achaeans outnumber the Trojans of the city more than ten to one. Yet numbers do not win the war, he goes on, because Troy has allies from many cities (2.130–133). The Trojan Catalogue at the end of the book names them, but gives no numbers.

**Nine years.** Numbers measure the length of the war. “Nine years of great Zeus have gone by” (2.134); “for us this is the ninth year come round”, while a man chafes at “a single month” away from his wife (2.292–295). At Aulis the serpent ate eight chicks, “and the mother who bore them was the ninth”, and Calchas read the birds as years: “in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (2.313, 2.326–329; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). The pattern of nine and a tenth, which timed the plague in Book 1 (1.53–54), here measures the whole war.

**Counted for something.** To be counted is to matter. The man of the people is “never counted for anything (ἐναρίθμιος) in war or in council” (2.202). Agamemnon wishes for “ten such counselors” as Nestor (2.372). At the sacrifice the narrator counts the elders: Nestor “first of all”, then Idomeneus, the two Ajaxes and Diomedes, “and sixth, Odysseus”; Menelaus comes unbidden (2.405–408). Wealth is counted too: each of the hundred golden tassels of the aegis is “worth a hundred oxen” (2.448–449; see [[gold-and-silver]]).

**The uncountable.** When the army marches out, counting fails. The men stand in the meadow “numberless” (μυρίοι), as many as the leaves and flowers in their season, as the flies around the milk pails (2.468–473). The poet could not tell or name the multitude even with ten tongues and ten mouths, and chooses what he can count: “I will tell the leaders of the ships, and all the ships” (2.488–493; see [[ten-tongues]]). The Trojans hear the same from Iris: the Achaeans are coming “like leaves or like the sands, far too many” (2.800).

**The ships.** The Catalogue keeps the promise. Every contingent is given its number of ships, most often in a closing formula (“and with him followed forty black ships”), from three for Nireus to a hundred for Agamemnon (2.671, 2.576). Twice it gives the men aboard: a hundred and twenty young Boeotians in each ship (2.509–510), and fifty rowers, all archers, in each of Philoctetes' seven (2.719–720). The Epeians have four leaders with ten ships apiece (2.618–619). Crete is “of the hundred cities” (ἑκατόμπολις, 2.649), where the Odyssey gives it ninety (Odyssey 19.174). For the totals see [[catalogue-of-ships]]; for what Thucydides made of the figures, see [[thucydides-and-the-catalogue]].
