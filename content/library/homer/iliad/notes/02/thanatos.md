**The dead of nine years.** Atreus “when he died” (θνῄσκων) left the scepter to Thyestes (2.106; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). Odysseus calls the army to witness the sign at Aulis, “all whom the fates of death (κῆρες … θανάτοιο) have not carried away” (2.302): the survivors of nine years are the ones who remember (see [[ker-doom]]).

**Before the battle.** Nestor tells any man desperate to go home to lay hands on his ship, “so that before the others he may meet his death and his fate” (2.359), and Agamemnon promises the dogs and the birds to any man who hangs back (2.391–393). Then each man sacrifices to one of the gods, “praying to escape death and the toil of Ares” (2.401). The fighting that follows is the first in the poem.

**In the Catalogue.** Deaths explain some of the leaders. Thoas leads the Aetolians because the sons of Oeneus are no more, nor Oeneus himself, “and fair-haired Meleager was dead” (θάνε, 2.641–643; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). Protesilaus led his men “while he lived; but by now the black earth held him fast” (2.699; see [[the-death-of-protesilaus]]). Other deaths are foretold. Ennomus the augur, and the Carian leader, Nastes or Amphimachus, who came to war wearing gold like a girl, will be killed by Achilles in the river (2.858–861, 2.872–875; see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). Merops of Percote, who knew the art of prophecy above all men, would not let his sons go to war, “but they would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.832–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). The fates of death that carried away the Achaean dead (2.302) are leading the sons of Merops to theirs.
