**The invocation.**

{{quote:6d988018-c781-5748-8a0a-4485685324a9}}

The poet asks for help at the hardest point in the poem. The Muses are goddesses, present everywhere and knowing everything, while men “hear only the report (κλέος), and know nothing”. He will name the leaders and the ships. The multitude he could not name even with ten tongues and ten mouths, an unbreakable voice and a heart of bronze, unless the Olympian Muses, daughters of Zeus, “were to remind me” (μνησαίατο). The verb is the verb of memory: the Muses are the source of what the singer remembers. It is the first time the poet calls on them by name. The proem asked only a “goddess” to sing (1.1). See [[invocation-of-the-muses]], [[we-hear-only-the-report]], [[ten-tongues]] and [[kleos]].

**Tell me, Muse.** At the end of the Catalogue the poet turns to her again, in the singular: “But who was the best of them, tell me, Muse, of the men and of the horses” (2.761–762). The answer is the mares of Eumelus and, while Achilles kept up his wrath, Telamonian Ajax. See [[best-of-the-achaeans]].

**Thamyris.** In Nestor's part of the Catalogue, at Dorium, the Muses met Thamyris the Thracian as he came from Eurytus of Oechalia. He had boasted that he would win even if the Muses themselves sang against him, and in their anger they maimed him (πηρόν), took away his wondrous song, and made him forget his lyre-playing (2.594–600). The poem that has just depended on the Muses shows what becomes of a singer who claims to rival them. πηρός later came to mean “blind”, but Homer does not say what the Muses did to his body. See [[thamyris-and-the-muses]] and [[thamyris]].
