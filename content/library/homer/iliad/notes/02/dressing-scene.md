---
summary: Woken by the Dream, Agamemnon dresses in the fixed order and takes up the scepter of his fathers where a warrior would take his spear (2.41–47). Later in the book Odysseus throws off his cloak to run, and threatens to strip Thersites of his.
---

{{quote:e33c248a-9e1b-596b-9d6c-bac21690432c}}

The scene follows straight on the waking, and its haste is the Dream's: “arm the long-haired Achaeans with all speed” (2.11, 2.28). Each item has its mark of quality. The tunic is soft, fine and new-made (νηγάτεον, a rare word, “new-made” or “glossy”; see [[tunic]]); the cloak is great, the feet shining, the sandals fine, the sword silver-studded (see [[silver-studded]]). They are a king's clothes. The last thing he takes up is not a spear but “the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever” (σκῆπτρον πατρώϊον, 2.46), and with it he goes along the ships. When Achilles arms for his return to battle, the last thing he takes is his father's spear (πατρώϊον … ἔγχος, 19.387). Agamemnon dresses for the council and the assembly, where the scepter is a king's weapon (see [[scepter]] and [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]).

The last two verses return when Odysseus goes to Agamemnon “and received from him the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever, and with it went along the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (2.186–187, repeating 2.46–47 with a new verb). The king's going out becomes his deputy's.

Two other passages of Book 2 turn on clothes. When Athena sends him through the camp, Odysseus sets off running “and threw off his cloak” (ἀπὸ δὲ χλαῖναν βάλε), and his herald picks it up (2.183–184): a man in a hurry undresses. And his threat to Thersites is a dressing scene undone. He will strip off “your own clothes, your cloak and your tunic, which cover your nakedness” (2.261–262; see [[aidos]]). The king is dressed for authority; the commoner is threatened with nakedness and shame. See [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].
