summary: The city in Book 2 is what the Dream promises and the test takes away: “the wide-streeted city” that Agamemnon is told he can take that very day. Troy is a city held by men from many cities, and the Catalogue fills the book with the towns of both sides.

**The promise.** Zeus sends the Dream to tell Agamemnon that he may now take “the wide-streeted city of the Trojans” (πόλιν εὐρυάγυιαν), and the line is spoken three times, by Zeus, by the Dream and by Agamemnon (2.12, 2.29, 2.66; see [[wide-streeted]] and [[message-repeated]]). Agamemnon wakes thinking he will take the city of Priam that very day (2.37). In the test he says the opposite: Zeus, who “has brought down the heights of many cities and will bring down more”, now bids him go home, and “we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (2.116–118, 2.141). Calchas had foretold the city's fall in the tenth year (2.329), and Odysseus asks the army to stay “until we take the great city (ἄστυ) of Priam” (2.332).

**A city of many cities.** Agamemnon's count of the enemy turns on the word. The Trojans “who live in the city” (κατὰ πτόλιν) are so few that many companies of ten Achaeans would go without a Trojan to pour their wine; but allies “from many cities” keep him from sacking “the well-peopled citadel of Ilios” (2.123–133). At the end of the book Iris says the same from the other side: there are many allies “throughout the great city of Priam” (κατὰ ἄστυ, 2.803), and the Achaeans are coming across the plain “to fight against the city” (2.801). The Trojans form up on a mound “in front of the city” (προπάροιθε πόλιος, 2.811; see [[batieia]]), and the Trojan Catalogue lists the peoples and towns that sent them.

{{quote:809ad908-2b5f-522f-b7a2-1865426b47a4}}

With ten counselors like Nestor, Agamemnon says, “the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head (ἠμύσειε), taken and sacked beneath our hands” (2.373–374). The verb is the one used of the field of grain that bows under the West Wind in the simile of the assembly (ἠμύει, 2.148).

**The towns of the Catalogue.** The Achaean Catalogue is a list of towns, several of them a “well-built citadel” (ἐϋκτίμενον πτολίεθρον, 2.501, 2.505, 2.546, 2.569; see [[well-built]]). A town can be named by its lord: Cos is “the city of Eurypylus” (2.677), and Oechalia “the city of Eurytus” (2.730). Phaestus and Rhytium are “well-peopled cities” (πόλεις, 2.648) in “Crete of the hundred cities” (2.649). And cities are what heroes destroy. Heracles won the mother of Tlepolemus after he “had sacked many cities (ἄστεα) of young men whom Zeus had nurtured” (2.660); Thersites reminds Agamemnon of the women the army gives him “whenever we take a citadel” (2.228); Odysseus is πτολίπορθος, “sacker of cities” (2.278; see [[sacker-of-cities]]).

Book 2 is the first to use ἄστυ, “town”, the other word for a city. For Priam's city the two are interchangeable: “the great city (ἄστυ) of Priam” (2.332, 2.803) is also “the city (πόλις) of lord Priam” (2.373).
