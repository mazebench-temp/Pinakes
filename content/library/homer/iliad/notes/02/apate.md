summary: Opening the test of the army, Agamemnon says that Zeus “has devised an evil deceit” and bids him go home in disgrace. He is pretending, but a deceit has been practiced on him: the Dream that sent him to the assembly.
passages:
  - 2.111-115 | An evil deceit

{{quote:539c222b-74c7-5d91-bbdd-a2b7f903c1c9}}

The word occurs once in Book 2, at the start of Agamemnon's test of the army (see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[peira]]). His charge against Zeus is a charge of broken faith. Zeus “once promised me and nodded his assent” (ὑπέσχετο καὶ κατένευσεν) that he would sack Ilios and sail home. “Now” he has devised (βουλεύσατο) an evil deceit, and bids (κελεύει) him go back to Argos δυσκλέα, “in disgrace”, with an ill name (see [[kleos]]), having lost so many men.

Agamemnon means the pretended order to go home, and he does not believe a word of it. The audience knows of a real deceit, devised by Zeus as the best plan (βουλή, 2.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). It too came with an order: the Dream said that Zeus “bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans” (κέλευσε, 2.28). The complaint is false of the order Agamemnon invents and true of the order he obeys. The narrator has already called him νήπιος, “the fool”, for believing it (2.38; see [[nepios]]).

Three lines earlier the speech joins deceit to [[ate|ἄτη]], “blind folly”: “Zeus, son of Cronus, has bound me fast in heavy blind folly” (2.111). And it turns Zeus' own words against him. In Book 1 Zeus told Thetis that nothing he confirms with a nod can prove ἀπατηλόν, “false” (1.526). Agamemnon's pretended complaint is that Zeus' nod to him has done exactly that. Nestor takes up the point later in the day: the army must stay until it knows whether Zeus' promise “is a lie or not” (2.349; see [[pseudos]]).

In Book 9, after the Trojans have driven the Achaeans back to their ships, Agamemnon speaks these lines again, and this time he believes them (9.18–22).
