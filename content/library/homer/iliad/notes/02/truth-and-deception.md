**“Exactly as I command.”** Zeus sends the Dream with an order to tell Agamemnon “everything exactly (ἀτρεκέως) as I command” (2.10). The message is exact, and false: now he can take Troy, for the gods are no longer divided (2.11–15). The Dream takes the shape of Nestor, “whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders” (2.20–22), the one counselor he would believe, and adds that Zeus “cares greatly for you and pities you” (2.27). The narrator leaves the listener in no doubt. These were “things that were not to be fulfilled”, and Agamemnon is a fool (2.36–38; see [[agamemnons-dream]], [[pseudos]] and [[plato-lying-dream]]). Ancient critics who thought a lie unworthy of Zeus tried to move it from the god to the Dream (see [[aristotle-and-hippias]]).

**A lie inside the lie.** Agamemnon adds a deception of his own. He will first test the men with words (πειρήσομαι, 2.73; see [[peira]]) by telling them to flee. In the assembly he says that Zeus “has devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην) and bids him go home in disgrace (2.114–115; see [[apate]]). He means it as a pretense, and it is true: Zeus has deceived him, though not in the way he says. See [[the-test-of-the-army]].

**Who is believed.** Nestor states the rule by which the dream is judged: “if any other of the Achaeans had told us of this dream, we would call it a lie” (2.80–81). The dreamer's rank makes it credible. Odysseus tells the kings what the people do not know: “you do not yet clearly know the mind of the son of Atreus; now he is testing them” (2.192–194). The truth is shared in the council and kept from the assembly.

**Disguises.** Three times in the book a god takes a mortal's shape: the Dream as Nestor, “very close to him in looks and stature and build” (2.57–58), Athena as a herald (2.280), and Iris with the voice of Priam's son Polites (2.791–795). Odysseus knows Athena by her voice (2.182), and Hector knows “the goddess's word” (2.807). Agamemnon does not see through the Dream.

**Tests of truth.** Signs are judged by time. Odysseus asks the army to stay “so that we may learn whether Calchas' prophecy is true or not” (ἢ ἐτεόν, 2.300). Nestor says that the deserters would go home before they know “whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus is a lie or not” (εἴ τε ψεῦδος, 2.348–349). The listener, who knows that Troy will fall, knows that both signs are true, and that the Dream was false.

**The Muses.** Only the Muses are beyond these doubts.

{{quote:27b81b71-cf39-5bf1-8a91-a574030d4fc1}}

They are present at everything and know all things; mortals hear only report. The Catalogue that follows rests its truth on their knowledge, not on any mortal witness (see [[we-hear-only-the-report]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]).
