grc: [ἐριδαίνομεν, ἔριδας, ἔριζεν]

Thersites' mind, the narrator says, was full of disorderly words “for quarreling with the kings” (ἐριζέμεναι βασιλεῦσιν, 2.214), and Odysseus tells him not to try, all alone, to quarrel with kings (2.247). The words are Nestor's to Achilles in Book 1: “nor you, son of Peleus, seek to strive with a king” (ἔθελʼ ἐριζέμεναι βασιλῆϊ, 1.277). The strife that the proem set between two chiefs (1.6) has passed to the ugliest man in the army. See [[thersites]] and [[basileus]].

Nestor rebukes the whole assembly: “we go on quarreling (ἐριδαίνομεν) like this with words, and can find no remedy” (2.342–343), while the oaths and pledges of the war's beginning go into the fire (2.339–341).

{{quote:0c5f3124-263c-53cc-abb0-a656385f67c5}}

Agamemnon answers with the quarrel of Book 1. Zeus “throws me into fruitless strife and quarrels” (ἀπρήκτους ἔριδας καὶ νείκεα, 2.376). He and Achilles fought over a girl “with hostile words” (ἀντιβίοις ἐπέεσσιν), as the narrator said of them at the end of their quarrel (1.304), and he admits that he began it. He calls the strife ἄπρηκτος, “fruitless”, the word he used of the war itself in the test (ἄπρηκτον πόλεμον, 2.121). See [[neikos]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]].

Once the verb has its other sense, rivalry in excellence. No man on earth had yet been born the equal of Menestheus at marshaling horses and men: “Nestor alone could rival him (ἔριζεν), for he was older” (2.553–555; see [[menestheus]]).
