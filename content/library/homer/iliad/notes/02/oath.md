**An imagined truce.** Agamemnon's test speech pictures Achaeans and Trojans “cutting trusty oaths” (ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμόντες, 2.124) so that both armies could be counted. ὅρκια are the victims or the rite of an oath, and to “cut” them is to kill the animals over which it is sworn. Here the truce is a figure of speech, a way of measuring the armies (see [[cupbearing]]).

**The army's promise.** The oaths that matter in Book 2 are the Achaeans' own. Odysseus tells Agamemnon:

{{quote:6829645b-0fe0-5f11-82d9-60928be5bbb3}}

The promise (ὑπόσχεσις) was made on the way from Argos: to stay until Ilios was sacked. Nestor names the same commitments in harder words, “compacts and oaths”, “the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted”, and asks what will become of them if the army goes home (2.339–341; see [[libation]]). Neither speaker describes the swearing itself. Odysseus also calls on the army as witnesses (μάρτυροι) of the portent at Aulis (2.301–302), the word Achilles used of the heralds in Book 1 (1.338), here for men who saw a sign.

**The promise of Zeus.** Both sides of the argument appeal to a nod of Zeus. In his feigned despair Agamemnon says that Zeus “once promised me and nodded his assent” (κατένευσεν) that he would sack Ilios, and has now devised “an evil deceit” (2.112–114). Nestor answers that the son of Cronus did nod assent (κατανεῦσαι) on the day the Argives embarked, and flashed his lightning on the right (2.350–353), and that no one should go home before learning whether “the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus” is a lie (2.348–349). A nod is not an oath, but in Book 1 Zeus says that what he nods to cannot be taken back or prove false (1.526–527). Book 2 sets that word beside a real deceit, the Dream. See [[neuo]], [[apate]] and [[the-departure-from-aulis]].

**A self-curse.** Odysseus threatens Thersites in the form of an oath, with his own head and name as the pledge: “may the head of Odysseus no longer sit on his shoulders, and may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus”, if he does not strip and beat him (2.259–264; see [[oath-formulas]]).

**The oath of the gods.** The Titaressus flows on top of the Peneius like olive oil, “for it is a branch of the water of Styx, the dread oath” (ὅρκου … δεινοῦ, 2.755). A branch of the river the gods swear by does not mingle with an ordinary river. See [[styx]], [[titaressus]] and [[like-olive-oil]].
