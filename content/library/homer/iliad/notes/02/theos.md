**The voice of a goddess.** Two leaders recognize a goddess by her voice. When Athena, sent by Hera, stands by Odysseus and speaks, “he knew the voice of the goddess (θεᾶς ὄπα) as she spoke” (2.182), and he runs to Agamemnon for the scepter. At the end of the book Iris comes to the Trojan assembly in the likeness of Polites, and “Hector did not fail to know the goddess's word” (οὔ τι θεᾶς ἔπος ἠγνοίησεν, 2.807). Agamemnon knew that his dream was divine (θεῖος, 2.56) but not that it lied (2.38).

**Goddesses who know.** The poet asks the Muses for the names of the leaders, “for you are goddesses (θεαί), and are present, and know all things, but we hear only the report, and know nothing” (2.485–486). Here what makes a goddess is knowledge: the gods are present, and mortals depend on κλέος, “report” (see [[kleos]], [[memory-and-the-muses]] and [[we-hear-only-the-report]]).

{{quote:27b81b71-cf39-5bf1-8a91-a574030d4fc1}}

**The god unnamed.** As in Book 1, “the god” can stand without a name for the one at work. At Aulis “the god who had brought him to light” made the serpent a sign (2.318), and Odysseus names him in the next line, the son of Cronus. After the sacrifice Nestor urges Agamemnon not to put off “the work which the god is putting into our hands” (2.436).

**Gods and men.** The book opens with gods and men asleep while Zeus alone lies awake (2.1–2), and in the Catalogue Zeus is the one “who rules over gods and men” (2.669). In the Trojan Catalogue Aphrodite bore Aeneas to Anchises, “a goddess who lay with a mortal” (θεὰ βροτῷ εὐνηθεῖσα, 2.821; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).
