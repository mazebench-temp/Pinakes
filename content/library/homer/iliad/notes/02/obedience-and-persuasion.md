summary: Book 2 turns obedience inside out. Agamemnon gives an order he wants refused, and the army obeys it; Odysseus restores order with gentle words for the kings and blows for the rest; and in the end the king himself obeys Nestor.

**Messengers.** Among the gods an order is carried out at once. The Dream goes “when he had heard the command” (2.16). Athena “did not disobey” Hera (2.166), in the formula that marked Achilles' obedience to Athena in Book 1 (1.220). When Iris brings Zeus' message to Troy, Hector “did not fail to know the goddess's word” and breaks up the assembly (2.807–808).

**An order meant to be refused.** Agamemnon's test depends on disobedience. He will order the men to flee, and the elders are to hold them back “with words” (2.73–75; see [[peira]]). He ends his speech with the formula of command, “let us all do as I say” (πειθώμεθα πάντες).

The men “who had not heard the council” (2.143) do exactly as they are told and run for the ships. The king is obeyed too well.

**Two ways of persuading.** Hera tells Athena, and Athena tells Odysseus, to hold back every man “with your gentle words” (ἀγανοῖς ἐπέεσσιν, 2.164, 2.180). Odysseus does so with the kings. He gives them reasons: they do not know the king's mind, and a king's anger is to be feared (2.188–197). With a man of the people he uses the scepter and a command: “sit still, and listen to the word of others who are better than you” (2.199–202). The kings are persuaded; the rest are told to obey. The one man who will not sit down, Thersites, is beaten until he does (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

**Argument.** Once the assembly is quiet, argument works. Odysseus reasons from shame, from the army's promise and from the sign at Aulis, and the Argives shout “as they praised the words of divine Odysseus” (2.284–335). Nestor asks the king to “listen to another” (πείθεό τʼ ἄλλῳ), promises that his word “will not be one to cast aside”, and makes his plan depend on obedience: “If you do this, and the Achaeans obey you” (2.360–364). In Book 1 Agamemnon called Nestor's advice fair and right and went his own way (1.286–289). Now he praises the old man (2.370–372), and when Nestor bids him summon the army, “Agamemnon, lord of men, did not disobey” (2.441).

**A father unheeded.** The Trojan Catalogue ends the theme with a warning. Merops of Percote “above all men knew the art of prophecy”, and would not let his sons go to war, “but they would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (οὔ τι πειθέσθην, 2.831–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Against that, no persuasion works.
