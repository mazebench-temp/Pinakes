δάκρυ χέων is not used in Book 2. Its only tears fall after Odysseus strikes Thersites with the scepter.

{{quote:8d88137d-0792-5d3c-a5c4-435ddf466f85}}

Both the words and the weeping differ from Book 1. There the formula went with speech and with a listener: Achilles prayed to his mother shedding tears, she sat before him as he shed them, and she answered him weeping (1.357, 1.360, 1.413). Thersites does not pour out tears, and no one answers them. “A big tear fell from his eye” (θαλερὸν δέ οἱ ἔκπεσε δάκρυ, 2.266), and a few lines later “he wiped away the tear” (ἀπομόρξατο δάκρυ, 2.269). The cause is not grief for a loss but the pain of a blow, and the tear is part of what makes him ridiculous: “troubled though they were, they laughed heartily at him” (2.270).

Heroes in Homer weep without shame. What sets Thersites' tear apart is its cause and its audience. Achilles weeps for his honor, and a goddess answers him; Thersites weeps from a blow, and the army laughs. See [[odysseus-and-thersites]] and [[tears]].

Each of the two half-lines occurs once more in Homer, both times in the Odyssey and with a different weight. A big tear falls from Eumaeus' eyes as he kisses Telemachus, safely back from his journey (θαλερὸν δέ οἱ ἔκπεσε δάκρυ, Odyssey 16.16); and Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, turns his face aside at the sight of his old dog Argos and wipes away a tear (ἀπομόρξατο δάκρυ, 17.304). In the Odyssey the words are given to joy and to hidden grief; in the Iliad, to a beaten man.
