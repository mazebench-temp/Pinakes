summary: The wives waiting at home are one of Agamemnon's reasons for going home and Odysseus' excuse for the army; Nestor promises every man a Trojan wife; and Protesilaus' wife mourns in a half-finished house.
grc: [ἄλοχοι, ἀλόχοιο, ἄλοχος]
en: [wives]
---

**Wives at home.** Agamemnon's test speech ends its case for going home with the families who wait: “our wives, I suppose, and our little children sit in our halls and wait for us” (2.136–137). Odysseus turns the same longing into an excuse for the army. He compares the men to “young children or widowed women” wailing to go home (2.289–290; see [[children-and-widows]]), and then grants them their reason:

{{quote:1c16b0ee-9de9-5474-a1e4-8dad82efbb6a}}

A month away from one's wife is hard enough, and this is the ninth year. See [[nostos]].

**Trojan wives.** Nestor's answer is to take other men's wives:

{{quote:485361a5-ef0f-52ca-9e60-3af29173d4d1}}

The war is fought over a wife taken from her husband, and its reward is to be the wives of the enemy, taken as captives. See [[helen]] and [[captive-women]].

**Protesilaus.** The Catalogue tells of a marriage cut short. Protesilaus, killed as he leapt from his ship far ahead of the other Achaeans, left behind in Phylace “his wife, her cheeks torn in mourning, … and his house half-finished” (ἀμφιδρυφὴς ἄλοχος … καὶ δόμος ἡμιτελής, 2.700–701). Homer does not name her. See [[protesilaus]] and [[the-death-of-protesilaus]].
