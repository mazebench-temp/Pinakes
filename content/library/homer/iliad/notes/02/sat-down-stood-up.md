---
summary: The line is used once in Book 2, in the council, where Agamemnon sits and Nestor rises (2.76). In the assembly that follows, sitting down and standing up become the signs of order lost and restored.
passages:
  - 2.76-78 | Nestor rises in the council
---

**In the council.** Agamemnon has told the elders his dream and his plan to test the army, and the procedure runs as it did in Book 1: he sits, and Nestor rises (2.76 = 1.68 = 1.101). The next verse names the new speaker with his title, and the one after vouches for him (see [[with-good-intent]]).

{{quote:f8b2b44c-271a-5d52-b238-2ed56a862b5d}}

The council ends as orderly as it ran. Nestor leads the way out, and the scepter-bearing kings “rose up after him and obeyed” (ἐπανέστησαν, 2.85).

**In the assembly.** The full assembly shows what the formula takes for granted. The people can hardly be made to sit: nine heralds shout for silence, and “with difficulty the people were seated and held in their places” (2.96–100). Then Agamemnon “stood up, holding the scepter” (ἀνὰ … ἔστη σκῆπτρον ἔχων, 2.100–101). His speech sends the army to the ships, and Odysseus has to seat it again, man by man: “Sit down yourself, and make the rest of the people sit” (2.191); “Strange one, sit still” (2.200). When the men are back, the narrator marks it with a verse that echoes 2.99: “The others sat down and were held in their places” (2.211). Thersites sits only after the blow, “terrified” (2.268).

Then Odysseus rises as Agamemnon did, in the same words, “holding the scepter” (2.278–279), and Athena in the likeness of a herald silences the crowd (see [[scepter]]). The two halves of the formula have come apart. To sit is to be brought back to order; to stand with the scepter is to have the right to speak. The assembly ends as Book 1's did, with everyone on their feet: “They stood up and hurried off, scattering among the ships” (ἀνστάντες, 2.398; compare 1.305). See [[assembly-type-scene]].
