**Above the herd.** The run of similes that brings the army onto the plain ends with a bull.

{{quote:771472fc-8d29-5183-8bff-5bf79aedab0b}}

The Greek names the animal twice: first βοῦς, the general word for an ox (see [[cattle]]), then, at the head of the next line, ταῦρος, “a bull”. The bull “stands out” (ἔξοχος) and “is conspicuous” (μεταπρέπει) among the gathered cattle, and the application gives Agamemnon the same words, “conspicuous among many and preeminent among heroes” (ἐκπρεπέʼ … ἔξοχον, 2.483). It is Zeus who makes him so “on that day” (see [[bull-above-the-herd]]). The bull follows directly on the comparison of Agamemnon with three gods, Zeus in eyes and head, Ares in waist and Poseidon in chest (2.477–479; see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]).

**For Erechtheus.** In the Athenian entry of the Catalogue, Athena set Erechtheus down in her rich temple, and there the young men of the Athenians seek his favor with bulls and rams (ταύροισι καὶ ἀρνειοῖς) as the years come round.

{{quote:92894c35-1783-5479-be06-28d0b03ac23f}}

ἱλάονται (“they seek his favor”) is the language of [[propitiation]], and the rite is yearly. Whom it honors is not certain: μιν may be Erechtheus, as the translation takes it, or Athena (see [[athena-and-erechtheus]] and [[erechtheus]]). Bull and ram are an old pair of victims. Poseidon feasts on a hecatomb “of bulls and rams” among the Ethiopians (Odyssey 1.25), and Tiresias tells Odysseus to offer him a ram, a bull and a boar (Odyssey 11.130–131). In Book 1 the bull's companion was the goat (1.41, 1.316).
