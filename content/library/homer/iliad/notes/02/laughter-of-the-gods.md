Book 2 has no laughter among the gods. Its one laugh is the army's.

{{quote:fda9a5f1-b0db-55dc-9c11-c6c0d27f40e2}}

**The laugher laughed at.** Thersites set out to amuse: he said “whatever he thought would make the Argives laugh” (γελοίϊον, 2.215). He gets his laugh in the end, as its object. Beaten by Odysseus, he sits down in tears, and the army laughs “heartily at him” (ἐπʼ αὐτῷ ἡδὺ γέλασσαν, 2.270). They laugh “troubled though they were” (ἀχνύμενοί περ): the laughter comes out of the distress of the failed test, and releases it (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

**A human counterpart?** The scene can be read as the human counterpart of the end of Book 1 (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). There the gods laughed at Hephaestus, “lame in both legs” (ἀμφιγυήεις, 1.607; see [[lame-in-both-legs]]), as he bustled among them pouring the nectar. Here the army laughs at Thersites, “lame in one foot” (χωλὸς δʼ ἕτερον πόδα, 2.217). In both scenes a crowd laughs at a lame figure who has put himself forward, and the laughter ends a quarrel: Hera's with Zeus, Thersites' with the kings. The differences are as telling. Hephaestus takes the cupbearer's part to make peace, and his mother smiles; Thersites is beaten into silence, and weeps. The gods laugh at no cost to themselves; the army laughs at one of its own. The poem does not draw the parallel, and readers give it more or less weight (see [[thersites]]).

**The same laugh.** The half-line recurs when the Achaeans laugh at Ajax son of Oileus, fallen in the dung in the footrace at the games (23.784), and twice in the Odyssey, when the suitors laugh at the seer Theoclymenus and at Telemachus, not long before their own deaths (Odyssey 20.358, 21.376).
