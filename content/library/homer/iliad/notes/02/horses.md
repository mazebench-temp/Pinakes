Book 1 has no chariots, and its horses are only plunder that the Trojans never took (1.154). Book 2 prepares a battle, and the horses come out with the chariots. It begins with “the men who fight from chariots” (ἀνέρες ἱπποκορυσταί) asleep (2.1; see [[chariot-fighters]]), and it ends with the Trojans pouring from their gates, “footmen and horsemen” (πεζοί θʼ ἱππῆές τε, 2.810).

**Before battle.** Agamemnon's orders put the horses beside the weapons: let each man sharpen his spear and make his shield ready, give his swift-footed horses their feed, and look his chariot over (2.382–384). The day will be long, and “many a horse will sweat as it strains at the polished chariot” (2.390; see [[chariot]]).

**On the plain.** As the army pours onto the plain of the Scamander, the earth rings “under the feet of the men and of their horses” (2.466). In the Catalogue no man on earth was the equal of the Athenian [[menestheus|Menestheus]] “at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men”, except [[Nestor]] (2.553–555).

**The best horses.** When the Catalogue is done, the poet asks the Muse who was the best “of the men and of the horses” (2.761–762).

{{quote:3d3eb9d9-432c-5e1a-8b5b-b5977726e84c}}

The best were mares. ἵππος is both masculine and feminine, and the feminine words around it (ἄρισται, θηλείας) make the point. [[Eumelus]], grandson of Pheres, drove them. They were swift as birds, alike in coat and in age, their backs as level as a builder's line, and [[Apollo]] had reared them in [[pereia|Pereia]]. They carried “the panic of Ares” (φόβον Ἄρηος) into battle (see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]] and [[swift-as-birds]]). In the chariot race of Book 23 they lead until Athena breaks their yoke (23.375–397).

Better still were the horses of Achilles, and they are out of the war.

{{quote:2c0a391c-d115-5778-8cb8-7da83a12508f}}

While their master nurses his wrath, the horses stand idle, each beside its chariot, munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley, and the chariots lie covered in the huts. λωτός here is a fodder plant of the meadows, as at Odyssey 4.603, not the fruit of the Lotus-eaters (Odyssey 9.84). The idle teams are the wrath made visible (see [[achilles-withdraws]] and [[myrmidons]]).

**Asius.** In the Trojan catalogue one leader comes with his team: Asius, “whom his horses carried from Arisbe, great and tawny (αἴθωνες) horses, from the river Selleis” (2.838–839; see [[asius]]). The same two lines bring him back in Book 12 (12.96–97), where he alone will not leave his horses and chariot at the ditch (12.108–115).

**Epithets.** Horses run through the epithets of the book as well: “tamer of horses” (ἱππόδαμος) of Atreus and the Trojans (2.23, 2.230), “driver of horses” (πλήξιππος) of Pelops (2.104), “horse-pasturing” (ἱππόβοτος) of Argos (2.287), and Nestor “the horseman” (ἱππότα, 2.336). See [[tamer-of-horses]], [[driver-of-horses]], [[horse-pasturing]] and [[gerenian-horseman]].
