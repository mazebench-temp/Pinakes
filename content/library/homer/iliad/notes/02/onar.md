Achilles said in Book 1 that “a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (1.63; see [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]). Book 2 bears him out in the worst way. Zeus sends Agamemnon a dream, and it lies. In Book 2 the Dream is also a person, who takes a message, stands above the sleeper's head and flies away; it has its own entry ([[dream]]). This note follows the word for the dream as such.

Agamemnon tells the council that “a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” (ἐνύπνιον, “in sleep”, 2.56), and repeats its message almost word for word (see [[message-repeated]]). Nestor, whose likeness the Dream had taken, weighs it.

{{quote:7364e943-ed47-5eb2-9b39-9b5b6b982d37}}

If any other Achaean had told them of this dream (τὸν ὄνειρον), they would call it a lie (ψεῦδος) and turn away from it; but the man who saw it claims to be the best of the Achaeans (2.80–82). Nestor tests the dream by the rank of the dreamer, not by its content, and no interpreter of dreams is asked, though Achilles had named one (1.62–63). See [[agamemnons-dream]], [[pseudos]] and [[aristos]].

Plato's Socrates, much as he praises Homer, will not praise the sending of this dream, since a god does not lie (*Republic* 2, 383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]).
