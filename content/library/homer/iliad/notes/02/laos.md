summary: Book 2 is the book of the λαοί, the people entrusted to a king, who run for the ships and are brought back, then are marshaled, counted in the Catalogue, and follow their leaders out to war.
grc: [λαός]
passages:
  - 2.119-122 | A shameful thing
  - 2.576-580 | The most and the best
---

**Entrusted to a king.** The Dream defines Agamemnon by his people: he is a man “to whom the people are entrusted” (ᾧ λαοί τʼ ἐπιτετράφαται, 2.25, 2.62), and so he should not sleep. At the assembly the λαοί come streaming on (2.86), and the earth groans as they sit down (2.95–99).

**Lost and shamed.** In the test Agamemnon speaks of the λαός as a loss and a shame. Zeus bids him go home in disgrace “when I have lost so many men” (ἐπεὶ πολὺν ὤλεσα λαόν, 2.115), and it is shameful that “so fine and so great an army” (τοιόνδε τοσόνδε τε λαόν) should fight in vain against fewer men.

{{quote:98a05387-8986-53ad-ba9e-69b79c016a16}}

At the end of the book Iris, in the likeness of Polites, uses the same words of the Achaean army from the other side: she has never seen “so fine and so great an army” (2.799).

**Turned back.** Hera sends Athena, and Athena sends Odysseus, “through the army of the Achaeans” (κατὰ λαὸν Ἀχαιῶν, 2.163, 2.179). Odysseus tells each king to sit down and “make the rest of the people sit” (ἄλλους ἵδρυε λαούς, 2.191): a king answers for his λαοί. Athena, as a herald, silences the λαός so that Odysseus may speak (2.280). Nestor's advice to separate the men by tribes and clans is meant to show “which of the leaders is a coward, and which of the people” (2.365; see [[phylon]]). The heralds gather the λαός, and Athena speeds through it with the aegis (2.438, 2.450). Agamemnon remains the “shepherd of the people” (ποιμένα λαῶν), even in the line that ends Thersites' abuse of him (2.243; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]).

**Following their leaders.** In the Catalogue the λαοί are those who follow (ἕποντο). Agamemnon's contingent is the largest.

{{quote:4d6d85aa-2bbf-5c97-bfe8-855eff55cfda}}

He stood out among the heroes “because he was the best, and led by far the most men” (2.580; see [[aristos]]), and Hector's Trojans are given the same line (2.817–818). Nireus, the most handsome man at Troy after Achilles, was a weakling, “and few people followed him” (παῦρος δέ οἱ εἵπετο λαός, 2.675). Tlepolemus gathered a large λαός and went into exile over the sea (2.664–665). The people of Protesilaus had a leader, though they longed for the one they had lost (2.708–709), and the people of Achilles amuse themselves on the shore (2.773; see [[menis]]).
