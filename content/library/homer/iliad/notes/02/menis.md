**Zeus' plan.** The book begins where Book 1 left the wrath. While gods and men sleep, Zeus lies awake, pondering how he might honor Achilles and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans (2.3–4). The second line is Hera's guess from the end of Book 1 (1.559), now told by the narrator: she guessed right. The wrath has become Zeus' business, and his first move is the lying Dream (see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

{{quote:f27e722e-0793-5781-bd01-1ef76081bef8}}

**Talked about in the camp.** The word is not spoken in the camp, but the quarrel is. Nestor trusts the Dream because the man who saw it “claims to be by far the best of the Achaeans”, the words Achilles used with contempt (2.82, 1.91). Thersites repeats Achilles' complaint and then sneers that there is no anger in him at all: “he lets it pass” (2.239–242; see [[thersites]] and [[cholos]]). The poem has shown the opposite (1.488–492). Agamemnon admits before the army that he was the first to grow angry (2.375–380).

**In the Catalogue.** The Myrmidons come with fifty ships, but “they gave no thought to grim-sounding war”, for no one led them into the ranks: Achilles lay idle among the ships, “angry over the girl, Briseis of the lovely hair” (2.686–689). The passage ends with a look ahead: “For her sake he lay grieving; but soon he was to rise up” (2.694).

At the end of the Catalogue the poet asks the Muse who was the best, and the answer is qualified by the wrath. The best man was Telamonian Ajax, “while Achilles kept up his wrath (ὄφρʼ Ἀχιλεὺς μήνιεν); for he was by far the strongest” (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]).

{{quote:2c0a391c-d115-5778-8cb8-7da83a12508f}}

Achilles lies by his ships ἀπομηνίσας, “nursing his wrath” against Agamemnon (2.772). Ajax uses the same couplet when he challenges Hector in Book 7 (7.229–230). The rest of the passage shows the wrath from outside, as idleness: men throwing the discus and the javelin and shooting their bows on the shore, horses grazing beside the chariots, chariots covered in the huts, and men wandering through the camp. In Book 1 Achilles longed for the war cry and for battle (ποθέεσκε, 1.492). Now his men long for him (ποθέοντες, 2.778). See [[achilles-withdraws]] and [[pothe]].
