In Book 1 the chest was where a king kept his grudge and where Achilles' heart deliberated (1.83, 1.189). In Book 2 it holds the heart only once: Agamemnon's call to flee “stirred the heart (θυμός) in the breast” of every man in the crowd who had not heard the council (2.142).

Otherwise the chest in Book 2 is the body. [[Thersites]], the ugliest man who came to Ilios, had shoulders “rounded and hunched together over his chest” (2.218). On the eve of battle the chest is where armor sits and where blows are aimed: many a man will sweat through the strap of his shield “across his chest” (2.388), Agamemnon prays to tear the tunic of Hector “around his chest” with the bronze (2.416), and the Abantes are eager to tear the corselets “around the chests of their enemies” (2.544; see [[corselet]]).

{{quote:993e67fc-8077-58b8-969b-3b3789d5e77c}}

When the army is marshaled, Agamemnon is “in eyes and head like Zeus who delights in thunder, in waist like Ares, and in chest like Poseidon” (2.478–479). The word for his chest is a different one, στέρνον, “breast”. Book 2 thus describes two bodies part by part: the ugliest man at Troy, and the king who is like three gods (see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]).
