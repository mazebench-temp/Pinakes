**Sent by Hera.** Hera calls her “child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone” (2.157). Ἀτρυτώνη is usually taken as “the unwearied one”, from ἀ- (“not”) and τρύω (“wear out”), but the sense is not certain. See [[atrytone]]. Athena “did not disobey”, and darts down from the peaks of Olympus (2.166–168), as she came down at Hera's bidding to stop Achilles' sword in Book 1 (1.194–195). In both books Hera sends and Athena goes.

**Odysseus.** She finds Odysseus standing grieved by his ship and gives him Hera's words, turned from “the Argives” to “you”, with small changes. The Achaeans will flee “tumbling into your many-benched ships” (2.175), and she adds, “hang back no longer” (2.179). He knows her voice at once (2.182). Her instrument is the hero she favors most, the goddess who, as the lesser Ajax complains, “always stands by Odysseus like a mother” (23.782–783). See [[odysseus]] and [[message-repeated]].

**The herald.** When Odysseus rises to speak, she stands beside him in the likeness of a herald and orders the army to be silent, so that the nearest and the farthest may hear (2.278–282). A goddess does the work of the assembly's herald. See [[heralds]].

**The aegis.**

{{quote:8043fe94-871f-5509-a302-0d0eb8e2bf9d}}

Here the aegis is described for the first time in the poem: highly prized, ageless and immortal, with a hundred golden tassels, each worth a hundred oxen. With it she rouses strength in every man's heart, and “at once war became sweeter to them than to go home”. The line reverses the morning, when the men ran for the ships “as they longed for home” (2.154). What Odysseus' blows and Nestor's counsel began, the goddess finishes. See [[aegis]] and [[divine-intervention]].

**Athena and Erechtheus.** The Catalogue remembers her as the foster mother of Erechtheus. The grain-giving earth bore him, Athena raised him and set him down in her rich temple at Athens, and there the young Athenians honor him with bulls and rams (2.546–551). With the Odyssey's “strong house of Erechtheus”, which Athena enters at Athens (Odyssey 7.80–81), it is one of the two Homeric glimpses of her cult in the city. Whether μιν in 2.550 is Erechtheus or Athena herself is disputed. See [[athena-and-erechtheus]], [[erechtheus]] and [[athens]].

**In prayer.** Agamemnon names her with Zeus and Apollo in his wish for ten counselors like Nestor (2.371). The same three gods are called on in wishes elsewhere in the poem (4.288, 7.132, 16.97).
