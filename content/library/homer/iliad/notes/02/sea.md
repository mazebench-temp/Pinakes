summary: In Book 2 the sea is the way home the army nearly takes, and an image of the army itself: in three similes the assembly heaves like the sea under the winds, crashes like surf on the beach, and roars like waves on a headland.
grc: [πόντου, πόντος]
passages:

  - 2.149-154 | The rush to the ships
  - 2.207-210 | Like the surf on the beach

**The assembly as sea.** The army gathers “along the front of the deep shore” (2.92), and the similes of the assembly come from the sea beside it. When Agamemnon bids the men go home, the assembly is stirred “like the long waves of the sea on the Icarian deep” (πόντου Ἰκαρίοιο), raised by the East Wind and the South Wind (2.144–146; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]] and [[icarian-sea]]). When Odysseus drives the men back, they come “with a roar, as when a wave of the loud-roaring sea crashes on the long beach, and the sea (πόντος) resounds” (2.209–210; see [[surf-on-the-beach]]). When they shout their approval of Agamemnon's call to battle, the shout is like a wave that the South Wind drives against a jutting crag (2.394–397; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]). The three similes follow the crowd from disorder to assent.

**The way home.** At the end of the test the men rush to the ships and call to one another “to lay hold of the ships and drag them down to the shining sea” (2.152; see [[shining-sea]]). Hera sees the Argives about to flee “over the broad back of the sea” (2.159; see [[broad-back-of-the-sea]]), and she and Athena say, “do not let them drag their curving ships to the sea” (2.165, 2.181). Odysseus pleads for the men: a man who stays even a month away from his wife chafes beside his ship “when the winter storms and the surging sea keep him penned in” (2.292–294), and they have waited nine years.

**In the Catalogue.** The Catalogue is a list of fleets, but the sea itself is seldom named. Agamemnon gave the Arcadians ships “to cross the wine-dark sea … since they cared nothing for the works of the sea” (θαλάσσια ἔργα, 2.612–614): an inland people with no ships of their own (see [[arcadians]] and [[wine-dark]]). Dulichium and the Echinae are “islands that lie across the sea, opposite Elis” (2.626). Tlepolemus, after killing Licymnius, built ships and “went fleeing over the sea” to Rhodes (2.664–667; see [[the-flight-of-tlepolemus]]).

**By the sea.** While Achilles nurses his wrath, his Myrmidons, “along the shore of the sea” (παρὰ ῥηγμῖνι θαλάσσης), amuse themselves with the discus, the javelin and the bow (2.773–775; see [[achilles-withdraws]] and [[shore]]). In Book 1 Achilles himself sat on the shore and wept, looking out over the sea (1.348–350).
