The serpent of Book 2 lives in a memory. [[Odysseus]] has turned the army back from the ships and asks it to stay until it learns whether [[Calchas]] prophesied truly (2.299–300). They are all witnesses, he says, to what happened at [[aulis|Aulis]], when the fleet was gathering and they were offering hecatombs at the altars around a spring, under a [[plane-tree|plane tree]] (2.301–307; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

**A great sign.** The creature is announced as a sign before it is named: μέγα σῆμα, “a great sign” (see [[teras]]). Three words then describe it: δράκων; δαφοινός (“blood-red”, from the intensive prefix δα- and φοινός, “red”), with the color “on its back” (ἐπὶ νῶτα); and σμερδαλέος (“terrible”). The Olympian “himself” sent it into the light (φόως δέ), and it darts out from beneath the [[altar]] where the offerings are being made.

**The kill.** It makes for the plane tree, where the nestlings cower on the topmost branch, and eats them as they cheep. Then, coiling itself (ἐλελιξάμενος), it catches the mother by the wing as she flies shrieking around it (see [[sparrow]]). Of the serpent the story tells only what it does; the cries belong to its victims.

**Stone.** “The god who had brought him to light made him ἀρίζηλον”, clear to see, for the son of Cronus turned him to stone (λᾶαν, 2.318–319). The sign is made to last. An ancient variant read ἀΐζηλον, “unseen”: the god who had revealed the snake made it vanish. The translation follows ἀρίζηλον. The same words, λᾶαν ἔθηκε, tell how Poseidon turned to stone the Phaeacian ship that brought Odysseus home (Odyssey 13.163).

**The reading.** Calchas reads the serpent's meal, not the serpent: nine victims, nine years of war, and the city taken in the tenth (2.326–329). But his sentence sets “this one” (οὗτος) beside “we” (ἡμεῖς): as the serpent devoured, so shall we make war. The serpent stands on the Achaean side of the comparison, the helpless young on the side of Troy. The stone suits what Calchas says of the sign, that it is “late in coming, late in fulfillment”, and that its fame will never perish (2.325).
