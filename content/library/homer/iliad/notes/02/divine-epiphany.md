In Book 1 Athena came in her own form and was seen by Achilles alone (1.194–200). In Book 2 the gods come in disguise, and the question is whether the mortal knows the god.

**The Dream as Nestor.** The first divine visitor of the book comes in the shape of a man.

{{quote:778f87ad-7b57-521e-aef3-1117ebe920b5}}

The shape is chosen to be trusted: Nestor is the elder “whom Agamemnon honored most”. Agamemnon knows that he has had a divine visitor, for “the divine voice was poured around him” when he woke (2.41), and he describes the likeness to the council (2.57–58). He recognizes a god but not a lie. See [[dream]] and [[dream-scene]].

**Athena and Odysseus.** Athena comes down from Olympus, finds Odysseus standing grieved by his ship, stands near him and speaks (2.166–181). The poem says nothing of how she looks. Odysseus knows her by her voice:

{{quote:a7de6061-53b7-5852-b1f0-e7d8ed3bf145}}

ξυνέηκε θεᾶς ὄπα φωνησάσης, “he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke”. There is no astonishment, as there was for Achilles (θάμβησεν, 1.199), and no reply. He runs, throwing off his cloak.

**Athena as a herald.** When Odysseus rises to speak, Athena stands beside him “in the likeness of a herald” (εἰδομένη κήρυκι) and orders the people to be silent (2.279–282). This is help in disguise, not an appearance: no one is said to know her. Her passage through the ranks with the aegis is told the same way, as an act without a witness (2.446–452; see [[aegis]]).

**Iris and Hector.** Iris comes to the Trojan assembly “in the likeness” (ἐεισαμένη) of Priam's son Polites, with his voice (2.791–795). “Hector did not fail to know the goddess's word” (2.807). As with Odysseus, a goddess speaks in a shape not her own, the hero knows, and he acts at once: he breaks up the assembly (2.808). The words may mean that he saw through the disguise, or only that he heeded the message. See [[iris]] and [[polites]].
