---
summary: Book 2 names hecatombs only in Odysseus' memory of Aulis, where a portent broke in upon the “perfect hecatombs” the army was offering; the ox Agamemnon sacrifices before battle is not called one.
---

The word occurs twice, both times at Aulis. The Achaeans were offering τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας, “perfect hecatombs”, to the immortals at the holy altars, in the phrase used of the army's sacrifice on the shore in Book 1 (2.305–306; 1.315), when “the dread portents of the gods broke in upon the hecatombs” (2.321; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[sacrifice]]). The sacrifice before battle is smaller. Agamemnon offers Zeus a single ox, fat and five years old (2.402–403), and the poem does not call it a hecatomb.

The parts of the word appear once more in their literal sense. Each of the hundred golden tassels of Athena's aegis is ἑκατόμβοιος, “worth a hundred oxen” (2.449; see [[aegis]]). There the hundred oxen are a price. Homer reckons value in cattle: Glaucus gives Diomedes golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.236).
