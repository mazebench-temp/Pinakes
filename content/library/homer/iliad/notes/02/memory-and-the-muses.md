summary: Book 2 is the Iliad's book of memory. The Dream warns Agamemnon not to forget, Odysseus calls the army to witness what it remembers of Aulis, the poet asks the Muses to remind him of all who came to Troy, and the Muses punish a singer by making him forget.

**Do not forget.** The Dream ends its message with a command to remember: “keep this in your mind, and do not let forgetfulness (λήθη) take hold of you when honey-sweet sleep lets you go” (2.33–34). Agamemnon obeys, and repeats the message to the elders almost word for word (2.56–70; see [[message-repeated]]). What he remembers so faithfully is a lie.

**Witnesses.** When Odysseus argues for staying, his evidence is the army's own memory.

{{quote:2e79eb4b-16fd-560d-9477-73317eb56fac}}

The men still alive are witnesses (μάρτυροι), and after nine years the day at Aulis is “yesterday, it seems, or the day before” (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). Memory binds as well as proves. Odysseus reminds the army of the promise it made on the way from Argos (2.286–288), and Nestor of “our compacts and our oaths”, “the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted” (2.339–341). And what the army does now will be remembered: to fail would be “a shameful thing, even for men to come to hear of” (2.119; see [[kleos]]).

**Remind me.** Before the Catalogue the poet turns from the army's memory to his own.

{{quote:6d988018-c781-5748-8a0a-4485685324a9}}

The Muses are present at everything and know all things; mortals hear only the report (see [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). The multitude is beyond any mortal voice, unless the Muses “were to remind me (μνησαίαθʼ) of all who came to Ilios”. The singer's memory is not his own: the Muses remind, and he tells. What follows, the Catalogue of Ships, is the longest feat of remembering in the poem, and at its end the poet asks again, “tell me, Muse” (2.761; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]] and [[catalogue-of-ships]]).

**Forgetting.** In the middle of that feat the poet tells of a singer the Muses punished. Thamyris the Thracian boasted that he would win even if the Muses themselves sang against him. In their anger they maimed him, took away his song, “and made him forget (ἐκλέλαθον) his lyre-playing” (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). The goddesses who give memory can take it away.

**Remembered and forgotten men.** The Catalogue uses the language of memory for two absent leaders. With Achilles idle by his ships, the Myrmidons “gave no thought to grim-sounding war” (ἐμνώοντο, from μνάομαι, “be mindful”, a verb of the same family, 2.686). Philoctetes, left on Lemnos with his wound, lies grieving, “but soon they were to remember (μνήσεσθαι), the Argives beside their ships, lord Philoctetes” (2.724–725). The army that forgot him will need him (see [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]).
