---
summary: Odysseus uses the masculine δαιμόνιε twice as he stops the flight, once to a king and once to a commoner, with very different words to follow.
grc: [δαιμόνιʼ]
---

The vocative opens both of Odysseus' short speeches to the fleeing men. To a king or a man of note he says, “Strange one, it is not right for you to panic like a coward,” and goes on with reasons: the kings did not all hear what Agamemnon said in the council, and his anger is to be feared ({{ref:Iliad 2.190-197}}). To a man of the people he says, “Strange one, sit still, and listen to the word of others who are better than you,” and strikes him with the scepter:

{{quote:a6e5efd3-f944-5871-af60-228c9844d8cf}}

The same word of reproach begins both speeches, and in Book 1 it opened Zeus' rebuke of Hera ({{ref:Iliad 1.561}}). It is the tone that follows that divides the two classes of men ([[gentle-words]], [[kings]], [[commoners]]).
