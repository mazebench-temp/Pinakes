**A war that comes to nothing.** Book 1 has no battle; Book 2 ends with two armies on the plain. The first war it names is Agamemnon's pretense: it is shameful that so great an army should “wage a war that comes to nothing, and fight” (ἄπρηκτον πόλεμον πολεμίζειν ἠδὲ μάχεσθαι) against fewer men.

{{quote:98a05387-8986-53ad-ba9e-69b79c016a16}}

**Arguing for war.** Odysseus tells a man of the people that he is “unwarlike (ἀπτόλεμος) and weak, never counted for anything in war or in council” (2.201–202), and the crowd praises Odysseus for good counsel and for marshaling the battle (2.273). He reminds the army that Calchas foretold nine years of war at Aulis (πτολεμίξομεν, 2.328). Nestor calls the assembly children “who care nothing for the works of war” (πολεμήϊα ἔργα, 2.338), and proposes an order of battle that will show whether the city stands by the will of heaven “or by the cowardice of men and their ignorance of war” (2.368). Agamemnon admits that he and Achilles “fought” for a girl with words (μαχεσσάμεθʼ, 2.377), as they “fought it out with hostile words” in Book 1 (1.304). Then he sends the men to their meal before battle and threatens any man who hangs back from the fighting (2.381–393). Ares himself stands for war here: “so that we may join in the clash of Ares” (ξυνάγωμεν Ἄρηα, 2.381), “try our strength in the hateful work of Ares” (2.385), “rouse sharp Ares” (2.440; see [[ares]]).

**From assembly to war.** The book's two summonses differ by one word. At dawn the heralds called the Achaeans to the assembly (ἀγορὴν δὲ, 2.51); now they call them to war (πόλεμον δὲ, 2.443).

{{quote:de3f8fde-e590-577f-96a6-7a6bf3172e2a}}

Athena rouses strength in each man's heart “to go to war without ceasing and to fight” (ἄλληκτον πολεμίζειν ἠδὲ μάχεσθαι, 2.452). The test's words return reversed: the war that came to nothing (ἄπρηκτον) has become war without ceasing (ἄλληκτον; see [[privative-adjectives]]).

{{quote:334597ca-f56a-57fb-bd9c-b5fc10074569}}

**In the Catalogues.** The Arcadians are “well skilled in war” (2.611), though they cared nothing for the works of the sea (2.614). The Myrmidons “gave no thought to grim-sounding war” (πολέμοιο δυσηχέος, 2.686) and “did not fight” (2.779), while Achilles, who longed for war in Book 1 (1.492), lies by his ships (see [[menis]]). Iris tells Priam that endless talk belongs to peace: “relentless war has come” (πόλεμος δʼ ἀλίαστος, 2.797). The sons of Merops go into “man-destroying war” against their father's warning (2.831–834), and Nastes, or Amphimachus, comes to the war wearing gold like a girl (2.872; see [[like-a-girl]]).
