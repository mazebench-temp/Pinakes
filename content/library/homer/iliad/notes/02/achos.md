**Odysseus' grief.** The army is running for the ships to go home. Athena finds Odysseus standing still. He has not touched his ship, “for grief had come upon his heart and spirit” (ἄχος κραδίην καὶ θυμὸν ἵκανεν). In Book 1 ἄχος came upon Achilles, at an insult to himself (1.188). In Book 2 it comes upon Odysseus, at the army's flight. The phrase returns in speeches as “this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit”, and each time in the Iliad the speaker's standing is at stake. Diomedes fears that Hector will boast of making him run (8.147–149), Poseidon resents orders from Zeus, his equal (15.208–209), and Achilles remembers the seizure of his prize (16.52–54). See [[kradie]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]].

**Troubled, they laughed.** When Odysseus has beaten Thersites with the scepter, the army laughs at him, ἀχνύμενοί περ, “troubled though they were” (2.270). The concessive participle is the one of Book 1 (ἀχνύμενός περ, “for all your grief”, 1.241, 1.588). The Greek does not say what troubles them. Perhaps it is the homecoming they had just rushed to and have now lost; the laughter does not give it back. See [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

**Two absent leaders.** The Catalogue names two leaders who are not in the fighting, and it says the same thing of each with ἀχέων, the participle of ἀχέω (“grieve”), a verb of the same family. Achilles lies idle among his ships, angry over Briseis: τῆς ὅ γε κεῖτʼ ἀχέων, τάχα δʼ ἀνστήσεσθαι ἔμελλεν, “for her sake he lay grieving; but soon he was to rise up” (2.694). Philoctetes lies on Lemnos, in agony from the wound of a water snake: ἔνθʼ ὅ γε κεῖτʼ ἀχέων· τάχα δὲ μνήσεσθαι ἔμελλον, “there he lay grieving; but soon they were to remember” him (2.724–725). Each line looks past its present with τάχα (“soon”). When Thetis tells Hephaestus of her son's grief for Briseis, she uses the same words, τῆς ἀχέων (18.446). See [[achilles-withdraws]], [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]] and [[water-snake]].
