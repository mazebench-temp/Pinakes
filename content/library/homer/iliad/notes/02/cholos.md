**The king's anger.** When the army runs for the ships, Odysseus holds back the kings with a warning about Agamemnon: “Beware that in his anger (χολωσάμενος) he does not harm the sons of the Achaeans” (2.195).

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

This is Calchas' account of royal anger from Book 1, that a king is the stronger when he is angry with a lesser man (1.80–83; see [[a-kings-grudge]]), now used to keep the chiefs in their places. The king's anger is great because his spirit is great and his honor comes from Zeus (2.196–197; see [[thymos]], [[time]] and [[basileus]]).

**No anger in Achilles.** Thersites ends his attack on Agamemnon with a sneer.

{{quote:6e9cc963-7c75-593c-8598-2eb519a7eceb}}

“There is no anger (χόλος) at all in Achilles' heart; he lets it pass.” μεθήμων (“slack, one who lets things go”) belongs with μεθίημι, “let go”, the verb of Nestor's plea that Agamemnon let go his anger against Achilles (μεθέμεν χόλον, 1.283). The narrator shows the opposite. When the Catalogue reaches the Myrmidons, Achilles lies idle by his ships, “angry over the girl, Briseis” (χωόμενος, 2.689), as Thetis left him in Book 1 (1.429; see [[menis]]). Agamemnon, for his part, admits that he was the first to grow angry in the quarrel, though with another verb (χαλεπαίνων, 2.378).

**Anger in the Catalogue.** Two short stories turn on it. The Muses, angry (χολωσάμεναι) at Thamyris for boasting that he would win even if they sang against him, maimed him and took away his song (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). Phyleus went off to live in Dulichium, “angry with his father” (πατρὶ χολωθείς, 2.629; see [[phyleus-and-augeas]]); his anger, too, ended in withdrawal.

**Zeus.** The account of the Achaean army closes with a simile of divine anger. The earth groans under the marching men as it groans under Zeus, “in his anger (χωομένῳ), when he lashes the earth around Typhoeus” (2.781–783; see [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]).
