**The houses of the gods.** The Dream's message says that “the immortals who have their homes on Olympus” (Ὀλύμπια δώματʼ ἔχοντες) are no longer of two minds, and the line is spoken three times (2.13, 2.30, 2.67). The poet calls on the Muses with the same phrase (2.484; see [[homes-on-olympus]]).

**The house at home.** In the test of the army Agamemnon reminds the men of what they left: nine years have passed, the timbers of the ships have rotted, “and our wives, I suppose, and our little children sit in our halls (ἐνὶ μεγάροις) and wait for us” (2.134–138).

{{quote:ad37b500-9392-5d64-a4fb-6bf0d32191a7}}

The word for going home is οἶκόνδε, “homeward” (printed in this text as οἶκον δέ), the accusative of οἶκος with the suffix -δε, “toward”. The army is to flee “home to their dear native land” (2.158, 2.174); the men “wail to one another about going home” (2.290); Nestor tells them not to hurry home before each has lain with a Trojan wife, and threatens any man who is “desperate to go home” (2.354, 2.357). See [[nostos]].

**Protesilaus' house.** The Catalogue tells of one house left behind. Protesilaus, the first of the Achaeans to leap ashore, was killed by a Dardanian, and “his wife, her cheeks torn in mourning, had been left behind in Phylace, and his house half-finished” (δόμος ἡμιτελής, 2.700–701). The phrase may mean that the building was left unfinished, or that the household was incomplete, a wife without husband or children; the sense is disputed. See [[the-death-of-protesilaus]].

{{quote:18c19717-694e-5eba-ac7d-260fa39432fa}}

**Other houses.** Astyoche bore two sons of Ares “in the house of Actor” (δόμῳ, 2.513). Tlepolemus grew up “in the well-built hall” (ἐνὶ μεγάρῳ εὐπήκτῳ, 2.661), then killed his father's uncle and fled over the sea. The Perrhaebians “had made their homes (οἰκία) around wintry Dodona” (2.750), and the Paphlagonians had “famous homes” by the river Parthenius (2.854).
