**Sleep.** Book 1 ended with the gods going to bed, and Zeus lying down with Hera beside him (1.605–611). Book 2 begins with gods and men alike asleep, with one exception: “sweet sleep did not hold Zeus” (2.1–2; see [[all-the-gods-slept]]). Gods and men share the same sleep. Only the king of the gods lies awake, planning the deaths of men.

**Care from afar.** The Dream tells Agamemnon that Zeus, “though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you” (2.26–27). It is a lie told in the language of divine care, and Agamemnon believes it (see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Prayer.** Before the battle the whole army turns to the gods.

{{quote:a976b3b0-4bea-57a2-b9cf-3c8573056ef3}}

The gods are αἰειγενέται, “everlasting”, and the men who pray to them ask to escape death for one more day. Agamemnon asks Zeus for Troy by nightfall; Zeus accepts the offering and refuses the prayer (2.419–420; see [[divine-intervention]]).

**Knowledge.** The invocation of the Muses states the difference in terms of knowledge. The goddesses are present and know all things; mortals “hear only the report, and know nothing” (2.485–486; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]). The Catalogue then tells of Thamyris, a mortal singer who boasted that he would win even if the Muses sang against him. They maimed him and took his song away (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). A mortal who claims to equal the gods is punished.

**Sons of gods.** At the marshaling Agamemnon is likened to three gods at once, Zeus, Ares and Poseidon (2.477–479; see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]). The Catalogues that follow are full of divine parentage. Ares lay in secret with Astyoche, “a modest maiden”, and she bore him the two sons who lead the men of Aspledon and Orchomenus (2.511–515). Athena reared Erechtheus, whom the earth bore, and set him in her temple (2.547–551). Immortal Zeus begot Peirithous (2.741), and Aphrodite, “a goddess who lay with a mortal”, bore Aeneas to Anchises on the slopes of Ida (2.820–821). Zeus, “who rules over gods and men”, loved the Rhodians and poured wealth upon them (2.668–670). The heroes of the war stand a generation or two from the gods, and the war will not spare them for it.

**Two names.** The mound before Troy “which men call Batieia, but the immortals, the tomb of much-bounding Myrine” (2.813–814) has a name in each language, as Briareus had in Book 1 (1.403–404; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]).
