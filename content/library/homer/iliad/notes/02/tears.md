**Thersites' tear.** Odysseus threatens to beat Thersites out of the assembly and send him back to the ships “howling” (κλαίοντα, “weeping”, 2.263), and the blow makes the threat good at once: “a big tear fell from his eye”, and soon he “wiped away the tear” (2.266, 2.269). These are the only tears in Book 2, and they are unlike those of Book 1. Chryses, Achilles and Thetis wept in grief and in appeal, and a god or a mother answered them. Thersites weeps from the pain of a blow, and the army laughs at him (2.270; see [[shedding-tears]], [[odysseus-and-thersites]] and [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

**Other griefs.** Elsewhere the book's grief is spoken of rather than wept. Odysseus compares the Achaeans who long for home to young children or widowed women who “wail to one another” (ὀδύρονται, 2.289–290; see [[children-and-widows]]). At Aulis the mother sparrow flutters round her young, “lamenting” them (ὀδυρομένη, 2.315). In the Catalogue, Achilles and Philoctetes both “lay grieving” (κεῖτʼ ἀχέων, 2.694, 2.724; see [[achos]]).

**A widow.** The Catalogue has one mourner. Protesilaus, the first of the Achaeans to leap ashore, was killed by a Dardanian man, and “his wife, her cheeks torn in mourning, had been left behind in Phylace, and his house half-finished” (2.700–701). ἀμφιδρυφής, “torn on both cheeks”, names the gesture of lament, the nails drawn down the face; Briseis tears her face in the same way over Patroclus (19.284–285). See [[the-death-of-protesilaus]].
