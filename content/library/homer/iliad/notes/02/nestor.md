**In the Dream's shape.** Zeus' Dream comes to Agamemnon in the likeness of “the son of Neleus, Nestor, whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders” (2.20–21). The lie wears the face of the man Agamemnon trusts most. Agamemnon tells the council that the Dream was “most like brilliant Nestor, very close to him in looks and stature and build” (2.57–58). See [[dream]].

**In the council.** Nestor is the only elder who answers.

{{quote:7364e943-ed47-5eb2-9b39-9b5b6b982d37}}

He judges the dream by the dreamer, not by what it says. From any other man it would be a lie (ψεῦδος); from the man who claims to be the best of the Achaeans it must be acted on. He says nothing about the Dream having borrowed his own shape. Then he leads the way out of the council, and the kings follow (2.84–86). See [[pseudos]].

**In the assembly.** After Odysseus has turned the army back, Nestor speaks more harshly than he did in Book 1. The Achaeans talk like children who care nothing for war (2.337–338), and their compacts, oaths and libations might as well go into the fire (2.339–341). He tells Agamemnon to lead as before, reminds the army of Zeus' lightning on the right when the fleet set sail (2.350–353; see [[the-departure-from-aulis]]), and promises each man a Trojan wife in payment for Helen (2.354–356; see [[helen]]). Any man desperate to go home may lay hands on his ship, and die before the rest (2.357–359).

**Tribes and clans.** Then comes the advice that shapes the rest of the book:

{{quote:febf4877-61cf-557a-a68a-821006ee6bd7}}

κατὰ φῦλα κατὰ φρήτρας (“by tribes and by clans”): the army is to fight in its natural groups, so that kin help kin, and so that Agamemnon may learn which leaders and which men are brave, and whether Troy still stands by the will of the gods or by the cowardice of his men. Why the army should be ordered this way only in the tenth year the poem does not say. The marshaling that follows (2.446–483) and the Catalogue, contingent by contingent, carry the advice out. See [[phylon]] and [[catalogue-of-ships]].

Agamemnon answers with praise: “Once again, old man, you surpass the sons of the Achaeans in the assembly,” and he wishes he had ten such counselors (2.370–372).

**After the sacrifice.** Nestor is the first of the elders Agamemnon calls to his sacrifice (2.405). When they have eaten, he urges the muster at once: “let us no longer put off the work which the god is putting into our hands” (2.435–436). The god's work is the victory the Dream promised, and Nestor, like Agamemnon, trusts it. Agamemnon obeys without a word (2.441).

**The Catalogue.** Nestor leads ninety ships, the second largest contingent after Agamemnon's hundred, from Pylos and the towns of the southwestern Peloponnese (2.591–602). His entry holds the story of Thamyris, whom the Muses maimed at Dorium (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). The Catalogue has already named him as the one man who could rival Menestheus of Athens in marshaling horses and men, “for he was older” (2.553–555). The adviser on marshaling is also its master. See [[pylos]] and [[pylians]].

His formula Γερήνιος ἱππότα Νέστωρ, “the Gerenian horseman Nestor”, is first heard in Book 2 (2.336, 2.433, 2.601). See [[gerenian-horseman]].
