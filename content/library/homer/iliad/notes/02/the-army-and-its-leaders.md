**Entrusted.** The Dream calls the king a man “to whom the people are entrusted” (λαοί τʼ ἐπιτετράφαται, 2.25). The book shows what the trust involves. The plan is made in a council of elders. The people, pouring to the assembly like swarming bees, are seated only with difficulty (2.87–99; see [[swarming-bees]]). Agamemnon's speech stirs “every man in the crowd who had not heard the council” (2.143), and they run for the ships.

**Kings and commoners.** Odysseus turns them back in two ways. Kings and men of note he holds back with reasons; a man of the people he strikes with the scepter and tells to listen to his betters (see [[kings]], [[commoners]] and [[one-ruler]]).

**Thersites and the crowd.** When the rest are seated, one man goes on shouting. Thersites speaks for the ranks: the Achaeans give the king the choicest women, the captives are brought in by “I or another of the Achaeans”, and “let us go home with our ships” (2.226–236). Odysseus beats him with the scepter, and the crowd gives its verdict.

{{quote:fda9a5f1-b0db-55dc-9c11-c6c0d27f40e2}}

The one man who spoke for the ranks is disowned by them, and the army's anonymous voice praises the beating (see [[tis-speech]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

**Tribes and clans.** Nestor's advice puts the army in order: “Separate the men by tribes and by clans, Agamemnon, so that clan may help clan, and tribe tribe.” Then “you will know which of the leaders is a coward, and which of the people” (2.362–365; see [[phylon]] and [[hegemon]]). Order makes leaders and men visible, to each other and to the king.

**Marshaling.** The kings rush about marshaling the men (κρίνοντες, 2.446), and the leaders sort them “as goatherds sort out the wide flocks of goats” (διεκόσμεον, 2.474–476; see [[goatherds-sorting-their-flocks]]). The Catalogue praises Menestheus: no man on earth had yet been born his equal “at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men”, and only Nestor could rival him (2.553–555).

**Leaders, not men.** The poet asks the Muses for “the leaders and lords of the Danaans”; the multitude he cannot name (2.487–493). So the Catalogue names leaders and counts ships, and the men appear as those who “followed”. Where a leader is missing, the Catalogue says what his men feel. The men of Protesilaus, and again those of Philoctetes, are not leaderless: “Nor were they without a leader, though they longed for their leader” (πόθεόν γε μὲν ἀρχόν, 2.703, 2.726). Achilles' Myrmidons had no one to lead them into the ranks, and “longing for their leader, dear to Ares, wandered here and there through the camp, and did not fight” (2.686–687, 2.778–779; see [[pothe]]).

**The Trojans.** Troy's army is a coalition of many tongues. Iris tells Hector to “let each man give commands to those he leads, and let him marshal his own countrymen and lead them out” (2.804–806). The Trojans and their allies are marshaled at the tomb of Myrine (2.815), and their Catalogue, too, names only the leaders.
