Book 2 opens with “the other gods and the men who fight from chariots” asleep (ἀνέρες ἱπποκορυσταί, 2.1). The word ἅρμα itself first occurs in the poem in Agamemnon's orders before battle. Every man is to feed his swift-footed horses “and look carefully over his chariot” (ἅρματος ἀμφὶς ἰδών), and “many a horse will sweat as it strains at the polished chariot” (ἐΰξοον ἅρμα, 2.383–390). ἐΰξοος, “well-planed”, from ξέω (“scrape, plane”), is a word for worked wood.

{{quote:b905eac7-92ec-5d74-a6cb-4f92cb4fac83}}

The Catalogue counts ships, not chariots, but it keeps the horses in view. [[menestheus|Menestheus]] has no equal at marshaling “horses and shield-bearing men” (2.554), and after the list the poet asks the Muse for the best of the horses as well as of the men: the mares of [[eumelus|Eumelus]], reared by Apollo, and the horses of Achilles (2.761–770; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]]).

Achilles' own chariots stand unused. While he lies by his ships nursing his wrath, his men amuse themselves on the shore; the horses stand idle beside the chariots, munching lotus and parsley, and “the chariots of their masters lay well covered in the huts” (ἅρματα δʼ εὖ πεπυκασμένα, 2.777).

{{quote:2c0a391c-d115-5778-8cb8-7da83a12508f}}

The covered chariots are a sign of the hero's absence (see [[achilles-withdraws]]). On the Trojan side the army pours out of the gates, “footmen and horsemen” (πεζοί θʼ ἱππῆές τε, 2.810). A ἱππεύς in Homer is a man who fights from a chariot, not a rider.
