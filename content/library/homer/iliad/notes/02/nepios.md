summary: The narrator calls Agamemnon νήπιος for believing the Dream, and at the end of the book the Carian leader who came to war in gold. In between, the word names the little children waiting at home, the sparrow's helpless young, and an army that talks like children.
passages:
  - 2.35-40 | Things not to be fulfilled
  - 2.337-343 | Nestor: like children
  - 2.872-875 | Like a girl
---

The word occurs five times in Book 2. The first and the last are the narrator's comment on a man who does not know what is coming; the three between are about children.

**Agamemnon.** The Dream has gone, and it has left Agamemnon pondering its promise before he wakes.

{{quote:130425b2-40e3-5355-8921-905c49fc817a}}

He thinks he will take Priam's city “that very day”. He is νήπιος, “who did not know what deeds Zeus was planning”: sorrows and groaning for Trojans and Danaans alike. The word sets what the narrator knows, the plan of Zeus ([[plan-of-zeus]]), against what the king believes. It is the poem's first νήπιος, and one of its sharpest. The commander of the army is a child in the hands of Zeus. See [[agamemnons-dream]].

**Children.** In the test speech Agamemnon counts among the reasons for going home the wives and “little children” (νήπια τέκνα, 2.136) who wait in their halls. In the portent at Aulis the sparrow's nestlings are νήπια τέκνα, “helpless young”, eaten with their mother (2.311; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[sparrow]]). Then Nestor turns the word on the assembly.

{{quote:008866c5-f953-520f-b78f-646b2ecc723a}}

They talk “like children, foolish little ones” (παισὶν … νηπιάχοις), who care nothing for the works of war. The two senses of the word meet. The army that ran for the ships behaved like children longing for home, and Odysseus has just compared the men to “young children or widowed women” wailing to one another (2.289–290; see [[children-and-widows]]).

**The man in gold.** The Carian entry of the Trojan Catalogue ends with a νήπιος.

{{quote:23b85784-5f14-5a0e-a864-4125ff74640c}}

One of the two Carian leaders, Nastes or Amphimachus (the Greek does not say which; see [[nastes]] and [[amphimachus-of-caria]]), came to war wearing gold “like a girl”. The narrator calls him νήπιος: the gold did nothing to keep off death at the hands of Achilles “in the river”, and Achilles carried it off (see [[like-a-girl]] and [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). As with Agamemnon, the word looks past the moment; here it looks to a death the man did not foresee.
