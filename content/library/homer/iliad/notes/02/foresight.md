**The fool.** The Dream leaves Agamemnon pondering “things that were not to be fulfilled”.

{{quote:130425b2-40e3-5355-8921-905c49fc817a}}

The narrator knows what Zeus is planning and says so: more sorrows and groaning, for Trojans and Danaans alike. Agamemnon thinks he will take Troy “that very day” (ἤματι κείνῳ), and the poem calls him νήπιος, “the fool”, the word for a man who cannot see what is coming (see [[nepios]]). In Book 1 Achilles made the charge: Agamemnon does not know how to look before and after (1.343). Here the narrator makes it himself.

**No one knows yet.** The wiser speakers of Book 2 claim no more than they know. Odysseus tells the kings that they “do not yet clearly know the mind of the son of Atreus” (2.192), and tells Thersites: “We do not yet clearly know how these things will turn out, whether we sons of the Achaeans shall return well or badly” (2.252–253). His case for staying is a case for waiting, “so that we may learn whether Calchas' prophecy is true or not” (2.299–300). Nestor says that the deserters would go home before they know “whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus is a lie or not” (2.348–349), and offers Agamemnon a way to know: marshal the army by tribes and clans, and “you will know” which men are cowards, and whether the city stands by heaven's will or by men's failings (2.365–368; see [[phylon]]). Foresight here is patience, and knowledge comes from testing.

**Late in fulfillment.** Calchas' sign at Aulis was “late in coming, late in fulfillment” (ὀψιτέλεστον, 2.325): nine years of war, and Troy in the tenth. Odysseus tells the army that “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[prophecy]]). Agamemnon's prayer to burn Priam's hall before sunset meets the same slow clock: “the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill his prayer” (2.419). The “not yet” (οὐδʼ ἄρα πω) looks past the refusal. Troy will fall, but not that day, and not within the Iliad.

**The Catalogue's future.** The Catalogue lists what was, but the narrator looks ahead from it. Achilles lies grieving by his ships, “but soon he was to rise up” (2.694). Philoctetes lies on Lemnos, “but soon they were to remember, the Argives beside their ships, lord Philoctetes” (2.724–725), a return that falls after the end of the poem (see [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]). Among the Trojan allies, Merops the seer foresaw his sons' death and could not stop them (2.831–834). The birds of Ennomus the augur did not save him from black doom, and the Carian who came to war wearing gold, “the fool”, was killed by Achilles in the river (2.858–861, 2.872–875; see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). A seer and an augur, men whose art is foresight, are among the doomed. The listener, told of each death before the fighting begins, sees further than any of them.
