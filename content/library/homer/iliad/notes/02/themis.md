{{quote:5c269b4c-6904-58ac-9ae6-b48cdbc739b4}}

Agamemnon tells the council that he will first test the army with words, ἣ θέμις ἐστί, “as is the custom”. The phrase means what is right or customary, and it is used of what a man may properly do: Diomedes claims the right to speak against the king in the assembly, “as is θέμις” (9.33). What custom Agamemnon has in mind is not said. It may be the testing of the men, or only the king's right to address them first; the Greek allows both. See [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[peira]].

{{quote:a08bf104-57fa-51e0-9d0d-32ada468bbf2}}

Odysseus' rebuke to the commoners ends with a theory of kingship: one king, “to whom the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given the scepter and the ordinances (σκῆπτρόν τʼ ἠδὲ θέμιστας), so that he may take counsel for them” (2.205–206). Nestor says nearly the same line to Agamemnon in Book 9 (9.99). In Book 1 the θέμιστες were guarded by the δικασπόλοι, who carry the scepter when they give judgment (1.238–239). Here they belong to one king, and the scepter in Odysseus' hand is Agamemnon's own, which came down from Zeus (2.100–108, 2.186). See [[one-ruler]], [[scepter]], [[boule]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].
