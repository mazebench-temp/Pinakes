---
summary: Book 2 names Agamemnon by his father in eighteen lines and both brothers in two, and it traces the patronymic to its source in the history of the scepter.
grc: [Ἀτρεΐωνος, Ἀτρέος υἱὲ]
---

The patronymic is used by every kind of speaker in the book. The Dream wakes Agamemnon with it, in its longer form Ἀτρέος υἱέ (“son of wise-hearted Atreus, tamer of horses,” {{ref:Iliad 2.23}}; [[wise-hearted]]). Odysseus warns the kings that they do not yet know “the mind of the son of Atreus” (Ἀτρεΐωνος, {{ref:Iliad 2.192}}). Thersites opens and closes his abuse with the vocative Ἀτρεΐδη ({{ref:Iliad 2.225}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.242}}), and Odysseus opens his speech to the army with it ({{ref:Iliad 2.284}}). Nestor uses the full formal address, Ἀτρεΐδη κύδιστε ([[most-glorious]], {{ref:Iliad 2.434}}). The plural names the two brothers together: “all who came with the sons of Atreus to Ilios” ({{ref:Iliad 2.249}}) and the men and horses “who followed the sons of Atreus” ({{ref:Iliad 2.762}}; [[atreidae]]).

The history of the scepter shows what the patronymic means:

{{quote:21df9459-3371-5238-840d-d6c99e6c8d57}}

Agamemnon's authority is an inheritance. The son of Atreus holds the scepter his father held, and when he stands out among the leaders, it is Zeus who makes “the son of Atreus” conspicuous ({{ref:Iliad 2.482}}).
