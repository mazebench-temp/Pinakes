**Council and assembly.** Agamemnon has the heralds summon the army; “but first” he seats a council (βουλή) of the elders beside Nestor's ship (2.50–55). The two bodies hear different things. The council hears the dream and the plan to test the army; the crowd does not. Agamemnon's proposal to go home stirs “every man in the crowd who had not heard the council” (2.143), and Odysseus reminds the kings, “Not all of us heard what he said in the council” (2.194). The test depends on the difference. See [[boule|βουλή]], [[elders]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]].

**The gathering.** The men pour out like bees, and Rumor blazes among them (2.87–94; [[swarming-bees]], [[ossa]]).

{{quote:9af04649-5dbb-5633-8a14-d186df2b660a}}

The assembly has its seats (ἕδραι), and order means sitting in them in silence until the kings speak. It takes nine heralds to bring this about (see [[heralds]]). Then the speaker rises with the scepter (2.100–109; see [[scepter]]).

**Broken and restored.** Agamemnon's feigned proposal breaks up the assembly with no word of dismissal. It is stirred like the sea and like a field of grain (2.144–149; [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]], [[wind-in-the-wheat]]), and the men run for the ships. Odysseus brings them back, persuading the kings and beating the commoners. A man of the people is to “sit still, and listen to the word of others who are better than you”, since he counts for nothing “in war or in council” (2.200–202). The men rush back to the assembly “with a roar” and are seated again (2.207–211).

**Who may speak.** Thersites alone keeps on, quarreling with the kings “against all order” (οὐ κατὰ κόσμον, 2.214), and Odysseus threatens to beat him “out of the assembly” (ἀγορῆθεν, 2.264; see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]). The crowd calls this Odysseus' best deed, “to stop this word-slinging slanderer from haranguing us” (ἀγοράων, 2.275). Speech in the assembly belongs to the kings and elders. Nestor rebukes the army for talking in the assembly “like children” (ἀγοράασθε, 2.337), and Agamemnon answers him, “Once again, old man, you surpass the sons of the Achaeans in the assembly” (2.370). The assembly ends with an order to eat and arm, and the men “stood up and hurried off, scattering among the ships” (2.398).

**The Trojan assembly.** At Troy the people are in assembly “at Priam's doors, all gathered together, young and old” (2.788–789), when Iris comes with news of the Achaean advance. She rebukes the old king: “endless words are always dear to you, as once in time of peace; but relentless war has come” (2.796–797). Hector breaks up the assembly at once, and the Trojans rush to arms (2.807–808; see [[iris]]). On both sides the book ends by leaving the place of speech for the field of battle.
