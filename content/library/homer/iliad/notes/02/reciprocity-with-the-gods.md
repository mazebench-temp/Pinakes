summary: In Book 2 the exchange fails. Zeus accepts Agamemnon's ox and refuses his prayer, the army sacrifices "praying to escape death", and a singer who claimed he could outsing the Muses is punished.
---

**The refused prayer.** Agamemnon sacrifices a fat five-year-old ox to Zeus with the chief kings and prays that the sun will not set before Priam's hall is burned (2.402–418). Zeus takes the gift and not the prayer:

{{quote:dcc1fe5e-205b-5baf-b9fe-5a78e31a6144}}

In Book 1 the formula of a prayer answered was "and Phoebus Apollo heard him" (1.43, 1.457). Here the god receives the offerings (δέκτο μὲν ἱρά) and adds to the toil. Sacrifice puts a god in the worshiper's debt, but it does not bind Zeus when his plan runs the other way (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**Promises from Zeus.** The book is full of divine promises and of doubt about them. Agamemnon believes the Dream's "now you may take the city" and feigns belief that Zeus has broken an old promise (2.111–115). Nestor recalls the true sign given when the fleet sailed (2.350–353). See [[truth-and-deception]].

**Cult.** The Catalogue's entry for Athens gives the Iliad's clearest picture of a regular civic cult. There, "the young men of the Athenians, as the years come round", seek the favor of Erechtheus with bulls and rams:

{{quote:92894c35-1783-5479-be06-28d0b03ac23f}}

**Rivalry with the gods.** Thamyris the Thracian boasted that he would win even if the Muses themselves sang against him. They maimed him and took away his song (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). The gods give song, and they can take it back.
