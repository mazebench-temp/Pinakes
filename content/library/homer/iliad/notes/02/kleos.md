summary: The word first appears in Book 2, twice: the portent at Aulis is a sign “whose fame shall never perish”, and before the Catalogue the poet says that mortals “hear only the report”.
passages:
  - 2.321-329 | Calchas reads the sign
  - 2.484-493 | The invocation of the Muses
---

κλέος does not occur in Book 1. Book 2 uses it twice, in two senses, and both uses point to the poem itself.

{{quote:61cce681-3bc9-50a0-907b-080ef3797900}}

**A fame that will not perish.** At Aulis, Calchas calls the serpent and the sparrows a great sign “late in coming, late in fulfillment, whose fame shall never perish” (ὅου κλέος οὔ ποτʼ ὀλεῖται, 2.325). The sign will be talked of forever, and it already is: nine years later Odysseus retells it to the army at Troy, quoting Calchas' own words, and tells them that “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). The same half-line serves Hector when he imagines his own lasting name (7.91).

{{quote:27b81b71-cf39-5bf1-8a91-a574030d4fc1}}

**Only the report.** In the invocation before the Catalogue, κλέος is the other side of knowledge. The Muses were present and know all things; mortals hear only the κλέος and know nothing (see [[invocation-of-the-muses]] and [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). The poet presents what he knows of the war as hearsay, made trustworthy only by the Muses' memory. Epic is made of κλέος in both senses: the report that comes down to the singer and the fame his song gives.

**Fame and shame.** Two related words show the other side. In his test speech Agamemnon says that Zeus now bids him go back to Argos δυσκλέα, “in disgrace”, having lost so many men (2.115), and that it is “a shameful thing, even for men to come to hear of” that so great an army should achieve nothing (2.119; see [[aischros]]). Future hearers are the measure of shame as well as of fame. In the Catalogue, Sthenelus is the son of “famous Capaneus” (ἀγακλειτοῦ, 2.564), one of the Seven against Thebes. The phrase δουρὶ κλυτός, “famed for his spear”, belongs to the epithets (see [[famed-for-his-spear]]).
