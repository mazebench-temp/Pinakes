**Agamemnon's dream.** At dawn Agamemnon tells the elders what the audience has just heard. He tells it beside Nestor's ship (2.54), and he begins by describing the dream as a visit: it came to him “through the ambrosial night”, it was “most like brilliant Nestor, very close to him in looks and stature and build”, and “he stood above my head” (2.56–59; see [[dream-scene]]). Then he quotes it, and for ten lines his words are the Dream's (2.60–69 = 2.23–32; see [[message-repeated]]). He stops before its warning not to let forgetfulness take hold of him “when honey-sweet sleep lets you go” (2.33–34), and says instead what happened: the Dream “flew away and was gone, and sweet sleep let me go” (2.71). The warning has become his story. What the narrator added, that the promise was “not to be fulfilled” (2.36), he cannot know. And what the Dream never said, he adds himself: first he will test the army with words (2.72–75).

**Thersites and Achilles.** Thersites retells the quarrel of Book 1 from the army's side, and two of his lines are Achilles' own.

{{quote:6e9cc963-7c75-593c-8598-2eb519a7eceb}}

“He has taken his prize and keeps it, having seized it himself” (2.240) was said by Achilles to Thetis (1.356) and repeated by Thetis to Zeus (1.507). Now it reaches the assembly in the mouth of the ugliest man at Troy. “Otherwise, son of Atreus, this insult would be your last” (2.242) is what Achilles said to Agamemnon's face (1.232). Between the two Thersites puts a line that the poem has shown to be false: there is no anger in Achilles' heart (2.241). See [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

**Odysseus and Aulis.** Odysseus tells the sign at Aulis as something the whole army saw: “you are all witnesses” (2.301–302). Inside his story he quotes Calchas, and the seer begins by repeating the sign in the words Odysseus has just used for it: the serpent “devoured the sparrow's young and the mother too, eight of them, and the mother who bore them was the ninth” (2.326–327, from 2.317 and 2.313). Then Calchas turns the count into years: nine years of war, and the city taken in the tenth (2.328–329). Odysseus draws the conclusion for the present, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330). See [[the-portent-at-aulis]].

**Nestor and the lightning.** Nestor recalls a second sign from the sailing: Zeus “nodded his assent” (κατανεῦσαι) and flashed his lightning on the right (2.350–353). Agamemnon had used the same verb in his pretended despair, of a promise that Zeus had broken (ὑπέσχετο καὶ κατένευσεν, 2.112). Nestor retells the promise as a fact and draws the opposite conclusion: no one should go home before he has lain beside the wife of some Trojan (2.354–356). See [[the-departure-from-aulis]].
