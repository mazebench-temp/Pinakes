**Kings and people.** The βασιλῆες are a group within the army. They meet in council beside the ship of Nestor, “the king born in Pylos” (2.54), and rise after him, “the scepter-bearing kings”, while the people stream on (2.86). The heralds shout to make the people listen to “the kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (2.98). See [[kings]], [[elders]], [[scepter-bearing]] and [[zeus-nurtured]].

**Odysseus' two speeches.** When the army runs for the ships, Odysseus treats the two ranks differently. “Whenever he came upon a king or a man of note” (βασιλῆα καὶ ἔξοχον ἄνδρα), he stood beside him and held him back with gentle words.

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

His reason is Calchas' account of royal anger in Book 1 (1.80–83): the king's anger is to be feared, because “great is the spirit of kings whom Zeus has nurtured; a king's honor comes from Zeus” (2.196–197; see [[cholos]] and [[time]]). A man of the people (δήμου ἄνδρα) he struck with the scepter and told to sit still and listen to his betters (2.198–202; see [[commoners]]). Then he states a principle.

{{quote:a08bf104-57fa-51e0-9d0d-32ada468bbf2}}

“We Achaeans shall not all be kings here” (βασιλεύσομεν, 2.203). The rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler, “one king” (εἷς βασιλεύς), to whom Zeus has given the scepter and the ordinances. In Book 1 the word belonged to many men and was graded by degree, the one who rules over more being the greater (1.277–281). Here it is claimed for one. See [[one-ruler]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].

**Quarreling with kings.** Thersites' head is full of disorderly words “for quarreling with the kings” (ἐριζέμεναι βασιλεῦσιν, 2.214). Odysseus tells him not to quarrel with kings all alone (2.247), and not to have the kings in his mouth (2.250); the crowd hopes he will never again abuse the kings (2.277). The warning is Nestor's to Achilles in Book 1, “nor you, son of Peleus, seek to strive with a king” (1.277), now given to Thersites. See [[eris]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

**Marshaling the army.** When the heralds call the men to war, the kings nurtured by Zeus rush about marshaling them around the son of Atreus (2.445). In the Catalogue the verb is used once, of the past: Sicyon, “where Adrastus was king at first” (ἐμβασίλευεν, 2.572; see [[adrastus]]).
