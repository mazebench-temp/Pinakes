---
summary: Odysseus, driving the men back to the assembly, tells them that the rule of many is not good, “let there be one ruler”, and goes through the army “like a master”. The Catalogue is framed as a list of “the leaders and lords of the Danaans”.
passages:
  - 2.203-207 | Let there be one ruler
  - 2.484-487 | The leaders and lords of the Danaans
---

The word and its family occur five times in Book 2, each time where command is asserted or counted.

**One ruler.** Odysseus goes through the camp with Agamemnon's scepter. The men of the people he strikes and orders to sit and listen to their betters (2.198–202; see [[commoners]]). His last words to them are the book's plainest statement about who should rule.

{{quote:a08bf104-57fa-51e0-9d0d-32ada468bbf2}}

In context πολυκοιρανίη, “the rule of many”, is less a constitution than the state the army has just been in: every man giving orders, as when they “called to one another” to drag the ships down to the sea (2.151–152). Odysseus does not deny that there are many kings. “We Achaeans shall not all be kings here” (2.203) grants that some are. What he insists on is that command be single: one κοίρανος, one βασιλεύς ([[basileus]]), the man to whom Zeus has given the scepter and the θέμιστες, “the ordinances” ([[themis]]), “so that he may take counsel for them” (see [[boule]]). The saying has its own entry, [[one-ruler]].

**Like a master.** The narrator follows with the verb: ὣς ὅ γε κοιρανέων δίεπε στρατόν, “so, like a master, he went through the army” (2.207). The one who acts as κοίρανος here is not the king but his deputy, carrying the king's scepter (see [[scepter]]). Agamemnon, the one ruler of the maxim, says nothing from the end of his test speech until Nestor has spoken (2.142–368).

**Leaders and lords.** The poet asks the Muses to tell “who were the leaders and lords of the Danaans” (οἵ τινες ἡγεμόνες Δαναῶν καὶ κοίρανοι ἦσαν, 2.487). After the last Achaean contingent he closes the list with the same line: “These, then, were the leaders and lords of the Danaans” (2.760). The Catalogue is a catalogue of κοίρανοι, each with his own people and ships, many lords under one commander (see [[hegemon]], [[catalogue-of-ships]] and [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). The multitude under them the poet will not name (2.488–490; see [[ten-tongues]]).
