**At Aulis.** Odysseus reminds the army of the day the fleet gathered at Aulis, when they were sacrificing by a spring beneath a plane tree:

{{quote:2e79eb4b-16fd-560d-9477-73317eb56fac}}

The offering is told in a variant of the line for the army's sacrifice on the shore in Book 1: ἕρδομεν ἀθανάτοισι τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας, “were offering perfect hecatombs to the immortals”, beside ἕρδον δʼ Ἀπόλλωνι τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας (1.315). A serpent darted out from beneath the altar, and “the dread portents of the gods broke in upon the hecatombs” (2.310, 2.321). The gods answered at the altar itself. See [[the-portent-at-aulis]], [[altar]] and [[hecatomb]].

**Before battle.** Agamemnon sends the army to its meal, and each man sacrifices to a god of his own:

{{quote:a976b3b0-4bea-57a2-b9cf-3c8573056ef3}}

ἕρδω and ῥέζω are the ordinary verbs of doing, and Book 2 uses them in both senses. The crowd says that Odysseus has done (ἔρεξεν) his best deed in silencing Thersites (2.274), and Iris tells Hector to “do (ῥέξαι) as I say” (2.802). Sacrifice is the deed done for the gods.

Agamemnon's own sacrifice is told in full. He sacrifices (ἱέρευσε, a verb related to ἱερός, “holy”) a fat ox, five years old, to Zeus, and calls the chief men of the army to it; Menelaus comes unbidden.

{{quote:86067180-92bd-5759-ba87-c71a5f01e797}}

The victim is a single ox in its prime, not a hecatomb. The rite runs in the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse (see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]), but without a priest: the kings do everything themselves. There is no hand-washing. At Chryse the men washed their hands and then took up the barley grains (1.449); here they simply “stood around the ox and took up the barley grains” (2.410; see [[hand-washing]]). Where the old man of Book 1 burned the thigh-pieces and poured wine over them, here “they burned” them “on split logs stripped of their leaves”, and spitted the entrails “and held them over the flame of Hephaestus” (2.425–426). The fire is called by the name of its god. No wine is poured (see [[libation]]).

Zeus “accepted the offerings (ἱρά), but made the toil greater” (2.420). The sacrifice is received; the prayer is not (see [[prayer]]). In Book 1 the same gestures ended Apollo's anger, and the god was glad as he listened (1.472–474). Here they cannot buy what the king asks for, Troy's fall before sunset, a hope that rests on a lying Dream (see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**At Athens.** The Catalogue names one rite that recurs every year. Athena set Erechtheus down in her own rich temple at Athens, “and there with bulls and rams they seek his favor, the young men of the Athenians, as the years come round” (2.549–551). The translation takes μιν (“him” or “her”) as Erechtheus; it could also be Athena who receives the sacrifices. See [[propitiation]] and [[athena-and-erechtheus]].
