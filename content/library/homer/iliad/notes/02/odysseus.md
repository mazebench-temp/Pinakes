summary: Odysseus saves the expedition. Sent by Athena, he takes Agamemnon's scepter, turns back the kings with reasons and the commoners with blows, beats Thersites into silence, and holds the army with the memory of the portent at Aulis. In the Catalogue he leads the Cephallenians in twelve ships.

**The goddess and the grief.** When the army runs for the ships, Athena finds Odysseus standing still. He has not touched his ship, “for grief had come upon his heart and spirit” (2.169–171), and he is the only leader the poem shows grieving at the flight. She calls him by his full titles:

{{quote:0462bfbd-d0ab-5278-9d34-e47bbd209fe9}}

It is the first time the Iliad names him as son of Laertes and πολυμήχανος (“of many devices”). He knows the goddess by her voice (2.182), as Achilles knew her by her eyes (1.199–200). He throws off his cloak for his herald to pick up, and runs. See [[athena]], [[son-of-laertes]], [[of-many-devices]] and [[eurybates-of-ithaca]].

**The scepter.** He goes first to Agamemnon and takes from him “the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever”, in nearly the same lines that described Agamemnon taking it up (2.46–47, 2.186–187). With the king's scepter in his hand he speaks for the king. See [[scepter]].

**Kings and commoners.** He does not treat all men alike.

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

To a king or a man of note he gives reasons: Agamemnon is only testing them, and a king's anger is dangerous. A man of the people he strikes with the scepter and tells to sit and listen to his betters, for “the rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler, one king” (2.198–206). Both speeches begin with the same word, δαιμόνιε (“strange one”), and both end in the authority of Zeus. “So, like a master (κοιρανέων), he went through the army” (2.207). See [[kings]], [[commoners]], [[one-ruler]] and [[koiranos]].

**Thersites.** When only Thersites is still shouting, Odysseus does not answer his charges. He rebukes him, “looking at him darkly” (2.245), and swears by his own head and by his name as “the father of Telemachus” that he will strip him and beat him out of the assembly (2.258–264). Then he strikes him with the golden scepter (2.265–269). The army laughs, and calls it the best thing Odysseus has ever done (2.272–277). See [[thersites]], [[telemachus]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]].

**The speech on Aulis.** He stands with the scepter, and Athena, in the likeness of a herald, silences the crowd (2.278–282). He begins with Agamemnon's shame and the promise the army made him on its way from Argos, then concedes what the men feel:

{{quote:d02f0256-2a52-566e-9fda-e23ca130db43}}

Then he reminds them of the sign at Aulis. A serpent darted from under the altar, ate eight sparrow chicks and their mother, and was turned to stone, and Calchas read it as nine years of war and Troy taken in the tenth (2.303–329). “And now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330). The speech moves from shame through sympathy to the memory of a god's promise, and the Argives roar their approval of “divine Odysseus” (2.333–335). See [[the-portent-at-aulis]] and [[calchas]].

**The Catalogue.** He leads the Cephallenians of Ithaca, Neritum, Crocyleia, Aegilips, Zacynthus, Samos and the mainland opposite, in twelve ships with red-painted prows (2.631–637). It is one of the smallest contingents of the great leaders, the same size as Ajax's (2.557). The Catalogue calls him again “the equal of Zeus in counsel” (2.636), as the narrator did when Athena found him (2.169) and when Agamemnon called him to the sacrifice (2.407). See [[cephallenians]], [[ithaca]] and [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]].
