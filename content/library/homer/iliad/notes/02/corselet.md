---
summary: The corselet itself is named once in Book 2, as what the Abantes' spears will tear; but its verb θωρήσσω, “arm”, runs through the book, from the Dream's command to the arming of the Trojans.
passages:
  - 2.542-544 | Spearmen of Euboea
---

The θώρηξ appears once in Book 2, and as a target. The Abantes of Euboea follow Elephenor, “spearmen, eager with outstretched ash spears / to tear the corselets around the chests of their enemies” (θώρηκας ῥήξειν δηΐων ἀμφὶ στήθεσσι).

{{quote:24a7e911-4372-5505-98fd-4d3980647084}}

The verb made from it, θωρήσσω, “arm” (literally “put on the corselet”), gives the book its plot. Zeus tells the Dream to bid Agamemnon “arm the long-haired Achaeans with all speed”, and the command comes again in nearly the same words from the Dream and from Agamemnon himself (2.11, 2.28, 2.65). Agamemnon and Nestor end their speeches to the elders with the same line: “let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans” (2.72, 2.83). The test nearly sends the army home instead, and only after the sacrifice do the heralds summon it to war (2.441–444).

Then the Catalogue shows the contingents arming. The Phocians “armed close beside the Boeotians, on their left” (2.526), Menelaus' men “armed apart” (2.587), and Agamemnon himself “put on the gleaming bronze” (2.578). The book ends with the Trojans and their allies, “eager with their spears”, arming in their turn (2.817–818). By the end of Book 2 both armies are armed.

Two leaders wear a corselet of another kind, λινοθώρηξ, “with linen corselet”: the lesser Ajax, small but the best spearman in the army (2.529–530), and the Trojan ally Amphius (2.830; see [[with-linen-corselet]]).
