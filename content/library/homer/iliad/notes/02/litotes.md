---
summary: Book 2 uses litotes for prompt obedience (“did not disobey”), for Nestor's confidence in his counsel, for the contingents of the Catalogue whose leaders are gone, and for Hector's recognition of a goddess.
refs: [2.166, 2.204, 2.361, 2.441, 2.703, 2.709, 2.726, 2.807]
---

**“Did not disobey.”** The line “So he spoke, and … did not disobey” (ὣς ἔφατʼ, οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε) follows a request or an order more than twenty times in the Iliad. In Book 1 the words marked Achilles' reluctant obedience to Athena (1.220). In Book 2 they mark prompt action. Athena does not disobey Hera, and goes “darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (2.166–167). Agamemnon does not disobey Nestor, and “at once” he orders the heralds to summon the army (2.441–442). In Book 1 he would not take the old man's advice (1.286–291). See [[obedience-and-persuasion]].

**“Not one to cast aside.”** Nestor introduces his counsel with a denial: οὔ τοι ἀπόβλητον ἔπος ἔσσεται ὅττί κεν εἴπω, “the word I speak will not be one to cast aside” (2.361). The adjective occurs once more in Homer, in the same kind of denial: Paris tells Hector that the gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside (3.65). See [[epos]].

**“Nor were they without a leader.”** Twice the Catalogue comes to a contingent whose leader is gone, and twice it uses the same line: οὐδὲ μὲν οὐδʼ οἳ ἄναρχοι ἔσαν, πόθεόν γε μὲν ἀρχόν, “nor were they without a leader, though they longed for their leader” (2.703, 2.726). The first time Protesilaus is dead and his brother Podarces leads the men; the second time Philoctetes lies on Lemnos and Medon leads them. The people of Protesilaus, the Catalogue says again, “did not lack a leader” (2.709). The denials say that the men were led, and the second half of each line says what they had lost. ἄναρχος (“leaderless”) occurs in Homer only in this line. See [[privative-adjectives]].

**“Did not fail to know.”** When Iris speaks to the Trojans in the likeness of Polites, “Hector did not fail to know the goddess's word” (οὔ τι θεᾶς ἔπος ἠγνοίησεν, 2.807), and at once he broke up the assembly. The verb is the one used of Hera, who “was not unaware” that Thetis had been making plans with Zeus (1.536–538), and of Calypso, who knew Hermes at sight (Odyssey 5.77–78). In each case the understatement means a full and immediate recognition. See [[iris]].

Odysseus' maxim οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη, “the rule of many is not good” (2.204), may be counted too. It says “bad” without saying it, and the flat denial gives the maxim its weight. See [[one-ruler]].
