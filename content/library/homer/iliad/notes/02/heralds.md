**The summons.** At dawn Agamemnon orders “the clear-voiced heralds” (κήρυκες λιγύφθογγοι; see [[clear-voiced]]) to summon the army to the assembly.

{{quote:2e65d0a3-c87c-5f94-83e7-d14332bd53d0}}

κηρύσσω, “proclaim, summon”, is the herald's own verb, formed from κῆρυξ. The summons and the gathering fill a single line: “They made the summons, and the men gathered very quickly” (2.52).

**Order in the assembly.** The men gather in uproar, and it takes nine heralds, shouting, to restrain them, “if they would ever hold back from their clamor and listen to the kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (2.96–98; see [[assembly]]). Later a goddess does the herald's work. When Odysseus rises with the scepter, Athena stands beside him in a herald's likeness and orders silence:

{{quote:7c812926-3bba-5fbf-b80f-b2c11a9ff52c}}

The office is so much a part of the assembly that a goddess takes its shape to do its work. No one is said to see through the disguise (see [[divine-epiphany]]).

**A leader's man.** When Odysseus runs to stop the flight to the ships, he throws off his cloak, and “the herald Eurybates of Ithaca, who attended him” picks it up (2.183–184). A herald served a leader as well as the army. This Eurybates is not Agamemnon's herald of the same name (1.320; see [[eurybates-of-ithaca]]).

**The summons to war.** After the sacrifice Nestor tells Agamemnon to let the heralds “make proclamation and gather the people along the ships” (2.437–438). The king does it at once, in the words of the morning's summons:

{{quote:de3f8fde-e590-577f-96a6-7a6bf3172e2a}}

One word changes. κηρύσσειν ἀγορὴν δέ (2.51) becomes κηρύσσειν πόλεμον δέ (2.443): the heralds who called the army to the assembly now call it to war. In Book 2 the heralds carry no messages between men, as Talthybius and Eurybates did in Book 1. The messengers of this book are divine: the Dream, Rumor and Iris (see [[messenger-scene]]).
