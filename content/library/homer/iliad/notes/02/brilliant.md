Book 2 shows how general the epithet is. It names Nestor, when Agamemnon tells the council that the Dream was “most like brilliant Nestor” ({{ref:Iliad 2.57}}); Agamemnon himself, as Thersites screams abuse at him ({{ref:Iliad 2.221}}); Odysseus, as he steps up to silence Thersites ({{ref:Iliad 2.244}}); and Achilles, lying idle by his ships, in the full formula ποδάρκης δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς ({{ref:Iliad 2.688}}; [[podarkes]]).

It is also given to women and to places. Aphrodite is δῖ’ Ἀφροδίτη, the mother of Aeneas ({{ref:Iliad 2.820}}). Alcestis is δῖα γυναικῶν, “brilliant among women”:

{{quote:45774d85-3917-5018-9f00-2ec27fb17279}}

The river Cephisus ({{ref:Iliad 2.522}}) and the towns of Elis ({{ref:Iliad 2.615}}) and Arisbe ({{ref:Iliad 2.836}}) are δῖος too, and there the translation renders it “shining,” as it does for the sea ([[shining-sea]]). The same adjective praises a king under insult and a river in a list of places. This is the clearest sign in the book that δῖος has become a general mark of excellence, with little to say about any one bearer.

The feminine δῖα and the elided δῖ’ must be matched with their accents, since without them they look like the preposition διά (“through”) and the accusative Δία (“Zeus”).
