In Book 1 the aegis was only a word inside an epithet. Book 2 keeps the epithet: Zeus is “aegis-bearing” six times, in the mouths of Hera (2.157), Nestor (2.348) and Agamemnon (2.375), and in the narrator's (2.491, 2.598, 2.787). Then, as the army musters for battle, the aegis itself appears, in Athena's hands.

{{quote:f2807638-11ec-5655-b4a0-f115cea8c7ff}}

The description gives its worth rather than its look. It is ἐρίτιμος, “highly prized”, and “ageless and immortal” (ἀγήρων ἀθανάτην τε), the words that set the gods apart from men (compare Sarpedon, 12.322–323). A hundred tassels of gold hang from it (θύσανοι παγχρύσεοι), “all finely woven, and each worth a hundred oxen” (ἑκατόμβοιος). Value is counted in cattle, as when Glaucus gives golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.235–236).

With it Athena “sped flashing” (παιφάσσουσα) through the army, urging the men on, and roused strength in each man's heart to fight without ceasing. Hephaestus gave Zeus the aegis “to put men to flight” (15.308–310), but here it turns the Achaeans to eagerness, and at once: “war became sweeter to them than to go home in the hollow ships” (2.453–454). The morning began with a stampede to the ships (2.142–154); the goddess with the aegis reverses it (see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The marching army then gleams like a forest fire (2.455–458; see [[forest-fire]]).
