{{quote:4d45c4f3-8ae1-50ae-a681-446c5dcb9f4c}}

Thersites was most hateful to Achilles and Odysseus, “for those two he was always abusing” (νεικείεσκε, with the -σκ- of habit). Now he turns on Agamemnon, and the narrator frames his speech with the verb: he “abused Agamemnon in these words” (νείκεε, 2.224), and “so he spoke, abusing Agamemnon, shepherd of the people” (νεικείων, 2.243). After the beating the crowd hopes that his proud spirit will never again drive him “to rail at the kings with abusive words” (νεικείειν βασιλῆας ὀνειδείοις ἐπέεσσιν, 2.277). In Book 1 the verb described Hera's quarreling with her husband; here it is the public abuse of a king, and it is answered with the scepter. See [[thersites]], [[odysseus-and-thersites]] and [[oneidos]].

The noun comes once, from Agamemnon: Zeus throws him “into fruitless strife and quarrels” (ἔριδας καὶ νείκεα, 2.376), by which he means his quarrel with Achilles (see [[eris]]).
