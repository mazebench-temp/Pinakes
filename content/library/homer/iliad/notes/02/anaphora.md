**In the speeches.** Odysseus ends his rebuke of a man of the people on the number one: εἷς κοίρανος ἔστω, / εἷς βασιλεύς, “let there be one ruler, one king” (2.204–205). The repeated εἷς (“one”) answers the πολυ- (“many”) of πολυκοιρανίη, “the rule of many”, in the same line. See [[one-ruler]].

Agamemnon's orders before the battle run on εὖ (“well”): εὖ μέν τις δόρυ θηξάσθω, εὖ δʼ ἀσπίδα θέσθω, / εὖ δέ τις ἵπποισιν δεῖπνον δότω … / εὖ δέ τις ἅρματος ἀμφὶς ἰδὼν πολέμοιο μεδέσθω (2.382–384). Let each man sharpen his spear well, set his shield well, feed his horses well, and look his chariot over well. Four clauses and four tasks make up the whole preparation, as the four lines beginning with ἐκ (“out”) made up the landing at Chryse in Book 1 (1.436–439). Then two lines begin with the same verb, ἱδρώσει (“will sweat”): the strap of many a man's shield will sweat, and many a horse at the chariot (2.388, 2.390).

The poet's own plea to the Muses doubles the number ten: δέκα μὲν γλῶσσαι, δέκα δὲ στόματʼ, “ten tongues and ten mouths” (2.489; see [[ten-tongues]]).

**Nireus.**

{{quote:6e50452b-d36f-5b47-87f0-80316249e217}}

Nireus brings three ships, the fewest in the Catalogue, and his name leads three lines. The repetition gives a minor figure a weight his contingent does not have, and the last line takes it back: he was a weakling, and few people followed him. Aristotle cites the passage for its amplification. When much is said about a man, his name is repeated; so when his name is repeated, much seems to be said, and Homer makes Nireus memorable though he never mentions him again (*Rhetoric* 3.12, 1414a2–7; see [[aristotle-nireus]] and [[nireus]]).

**In the Catalogue.** The Catalogue's entries often run as a chain of relative clauses, each beginning with οἵ (“those who”), as in the entry for the Boeotians (2.496–508), and once three times in a single line: οἵ τʼ Ἄλον οἵ τʼ Ἀλόπην οἵ τε Τρηχῖνα νέμοντο, “who lived in Alus and Alope and Trachis” (2.682). Close to anaphora is the name taken up again at the head of the next line, sometimes called epanalepsis (“taking up again”): Ἄσιος … / Ἄσιος (2.837–838), Ἀξιοῦ … / Ἀξιοῦ (2.849–850), Νάστης … / Νάστης (2.870–871). The second mention adds a line of description, as with Nireus. See [[catalogue-of-ships]] and [[trojan-catalogue]].
