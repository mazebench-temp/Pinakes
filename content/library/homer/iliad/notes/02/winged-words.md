---
summary: The formula occurs once in Book 2, when Zeus gives the Dream its orders (2.7), in the same line that brought in Achilles' words to Athena in Book 1.
---

Having decided that the best plan is to send a Dream to Agamemnon, Zeus speaks to it at once: “And he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words” ({{ref:Iliad 2.7}}). The whole line is 1.201, where it brought in Achilles' first words to Athena. There the one addressed was a goddess; here it is the Dream, which is spoken to like a person and obeys like one (see [[dream]] and [[dream-scene]]).

Book 2 has eighteen speeches, and this is the only one the formula introduces. The others are brought in by plainer verbs, or by lines that describe the speaker's standing or manner: Agamemnon “leaning on” the scepter (2.109), the adviser who speaks “with good intent” (2.78, 2.283; [[with-good-intent]]), Odysseus “looking darkly” at Thersites (2.245; [[looking-darkly]]). If the formula marks speech that bursts out on a strong feeling, this is a different case. The words are not an outburst but an order, given as soon as a sleepless night has produced the plan (2.1–6; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).
