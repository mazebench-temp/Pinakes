summary: Before the first battle Agamemnon tells each man to make his shield ready and foresees the sweat on the strap of the “man-covering shield”; in the Catalogue, Menestheus has no equal at marshaling horses and “shield-bearing men”.
passages:
  - 2.381-393 | Prepare for war
---

The shield first appears when Agamemnon sends the army to its meal before battle. Each man is to sharpen his spear and “make his shield ready” (εὖ δʼ ἀσπίδα θέσθω, 2.382), feed his horses and look over his chariot, for the fighting will last all day.

{{quote:b905eac7-92ec-5d74-a6cb-4f92cb4fac83}}

The day's labor is foreseen in sweat: “Many a man will sweat through the strap across his chest / of his man-covering shield” (ἱδρώσει μέν τευ τελαμὼν ἀμφὶ στήθεσφιν / ἀσπίδος ἀμφιβρότης). The τελαμών is the strap from which the shield hung, and ἀμφιβρότη, from ἀμφί (“around”) and βροτός (“man”), describes a shield that covers the whole man. The picture comes true in Book 5, when Athena finds Diomedes worn down by the sweat under the broad strap of his round shield, and his hand weary (ἱδρὼς γάρ μιν ἔτειρεν ὑπὸ πλατέος τελαμῶνος / ἀσπίδος εὐκύκλου· τῷ τείρετο, κάμνε δὲ χεῖρα, 5.796–797).

In the Catalogue, [[menestheus|Menestheus]] of Athens has no equal “at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men” (κοσμῆσαι ἵππους τε καὶ ἀνέρας ἀσπιδιώτας, 2.554), and only Nestor could rival him. Horses and shield-bearers are the two parts of the army, the men in chariots and the men on foot (see [[chariot]]).

Book 2 uses only ἀσπίς; the other word for the shield, σάκος, does not occur in it.
