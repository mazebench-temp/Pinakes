At Aulis, while the Achaeans were sacrificing beneath a plane tree, a serpent darted from under the altar, ate eight sparrow chicks and their mother, and was turned to stone. Calchas explained the sign at once:

{{quote:61cce681-3bc9-50a0-907b-080ef3797900}}

θεοπροπέων ἀγόρευε (“spoke out, prophesying”, 2.322) echoes the words Agamemnon threw at Calchas in Book 1: “and now among the Danaans you prophesy and declare” (θεοπροπέων ἀγορεύεις, 1.109). See [[theopropion]]. Calchas reads the sign by counting: eight chicks and a mother are nine years, and the tenth brings the city's fall. It is the sign of a war that is “late in coming, late in fulfillment” (ὄψιμον ὀψιτέλεστον, 2.325). See [[teras]] and [[the-portent-at-aulis]].

Odysseus stakes the army's patience on the seer: “stay a little while, so that we may learn whether Calchas' prophecy is true or not” (2.299–300). His conclusion is that “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330), for this is the tenth year. In Book 1 Calchas' truth earned him Agamemnon's anger (1.106–108). In Book 2 his old prophecy is the army's best reason to stay. See [[prophecy]] and [[calchas-guides-the-fleet]].
