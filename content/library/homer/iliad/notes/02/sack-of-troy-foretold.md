---
summary: The sack is promised for today by the Dream, abandoned in pretense in the test, foretold for the tenth year by Calchas, and prayed for before sunset by Agamemnon. Zeus accepts his offering and refuses the prayer.
passages:
  - 2.321-332 | Calchas' prophecy recalled
  - 2.410-420 | Agamemnon's prayer refused
---

Book 2 speaks of the fall of Troy again and again, and every mention is a promise, a hope or a pretense.

**Promised for today.** The Dream tells Agamemnon to arm the army, “for now he may take the wide-streeted city of the Trojans” (2.12–13, repeated at 2.29–30 and 2.66–67). He wakes thinking “that he would take the city of Priam that very day”, and the narrator calls him a fool (2.37–38; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[nepios]]).

**Given up, in pretense.** In the test Agamemnon says that Zeus promised him he would sack well-walled Ilios and return, and now sends him home; “we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (2.112–113, 2.141). Odysseus turns the promise against the army: on their way from Argos the Achaeans promised Agamemnon that he would sack Ilios before he sailed home, and now they will not keep their word (2.284–288; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

**Foretold.** Odysseus recalls Calchas' reading of the sign at Aulis: nine years of war, “and in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (2.329). It is the tenth year, and he ends: stay “until we take the great city of Priam” (2.332; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). Nestor puts the sack in harsher terms: let no one go home before he has lain with the wife of some Trojan (2.354–356). The fate of Troy's women, which Andromache will foresee in Book 24, is here a promise to the soldiers.

**Prayed for, and refused.** With ten counselors like Nestor, Agamemnon says, Priam's city would soon “bow its head” (2.372–374). At the sacrifice before battle he asks Zeus for all of it at once.

The hall of Priam burned, its doors on fire, Hector's tunic torn around his chest, his companions biting the dust: the prayer is a picture of the last night of Troy, asked for on the first day of fighting in the poem. Zeus “was not yet going to fulfill” it (οὐδʼ ἄρα πώ, 2.419). The “not yet” keeps the sack in the future, where the Iliad leaves it (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]).
