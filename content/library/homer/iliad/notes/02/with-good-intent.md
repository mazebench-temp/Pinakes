---
summary: The line vouches twice for a counselor in Book 2, for Nestor in the council (2.78) and, for the first time, for Odysseus in the assembly (2.283). This time both are heeded.
---

**Nestor in the council.** As in Book 1, the line follows the change of speakers (2.76–78; see [[sat-down-stood-up]]), and the credentials are short: Nestor “was king of sandy Pylos” (2.77). His advice is short too. If any other Achaean had told them this dream, they would call it a lie; but the man who saw it claims to be the best of the Achaeans, so let them arm the army. Of the test he says nothing. The formula vouches for his good will, not for his judgment.

{{quote:7364e943-ed47-5eb2-9b39-9b5b6b982d37}}

**Odysseus in the assembly.** In Book 1 the line brought in the seer and the old counselor. In Book 2 it is given to Odysseus, when he rises with the scepter after the beating of Thersites. His credentials are the scepter in his hand and a goddess at his side. Athena, in the likeness of a herald, orders the people to be silent, so that the nearest and the farthest of the Achaeans alike “might hear his words and take thought for his counsel”.

{{quote:7c812926-3bba-5fbf-b80f-b2c11a9ff52c}}

The speech that follows shames the army into staying and reminds it of the sign at Aulis (2.284–332; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

**Heeded.** Both men whom the line introduced in Book 1 were rebuffed. In Book 2 both are followed. The kings rise after Nestor and obey him (2.84–86), and the Argives shout “as they praised the words of divine Odysseus” (2.333–335).
