The word occurs once in the book, at the start of Agamemnon's speech to the assembly. He has told the elders that he will test the army by ordering it to flee (2.72–75; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]), and he begins with the reason. Zeus “has bound me fast (ἐνέδησε) in heavy blind folly”: he promised that Agamemnon would sack Ilios and sail home, and now he has devised an evil deceit (κακὴν ἀπάτην) and bids him go back to Argos in disgrace. ἄτη is pictured as a bond that Zeus has tied on him.

Agamemnon means it as a pretense, and it is truer than he knows. Zeus has deceived him that very night, with a Dream that promised he could take Troy (see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[apate]]), and the narrator has already called him νήπιος, “the fool” (2.38; see [[nepios]]). In Book 1 Achilles asked that Agamemnon might learn his ἄτη through the Achaeans' defeat (1.412). The next morning the king names it himself, as a ruse.

In Book 9, after a day of defeat, Agamemnon stands weeping before the assembly and repeats the lines, from the blind folly to the call to flee (9.18–28, repeating 2.111–118 and 2.139–141 with one word changed: τότε, “then”, for πρίν, “once”, at 9.19). This time he means them. In Book 19 he goes further and blames ἄτη, sent by Zeus, Moira and the Erinys, for the quarrel itself (19.86–89).
