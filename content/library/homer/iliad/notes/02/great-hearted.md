---
summary: Book 2 uses the epithet seven times, of the elders, of peoples of the Catalogue, and of the fathers of its heroes, and adds the synonym μεγαλήτωρ.
grc: [μεγαθύμων, μεγαθύμου, μεγαθύμους, μεγαλήτορος]
---

μεγαλήτωρ = μέγας (“great”) + ἦτορ (“heart”): “great-hearted.” It means the same as μεγάθυμος and fits the verse in other cases; the translation renders both “great-hearted.”

In Book 1 the great-hearted Achaeans were the army as givers of prizes. In Book 2 the epithet spreads to groups and to men of the past. Agamemnon seats a council of “great-hearted elders” beside Nestor's ship ({{ref:Iliad 2.53}}). In the Catalogue it describes whole peoples, the Abantes of Euboea ({{ref:Iliad 2.541}}) and the Cephallenians of Odysseus ({{ref:Iliad 2.631}}), and above all fathers: Iphitus, father of the Phocian leaders ({{ref:Iliad 2.518}}); Erechtheus, the earthborn king of Athens ({{ref:Iliad 2.547}}); and Oeneus of Calydon:

{{quote:bb48ee81-128e-54a9-832a-a7ddff93ab4d}}

Several of those so praised are dead or absent. Oeneus and his sons are gone, and so Thoas leads the Aetolians. Protesilaus, “great-hearted” in the description of his younger brother ({{ref:Iliad 2.706}}), was the first Achaean to die at Troy. The epithet of spirit often marks a man the Catalogue remembers rather than one who is present.
