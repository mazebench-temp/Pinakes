summary: Book 2 adds gold as wealth and spoil to gold as the mark of gods and kings. Thersites taunts Agamemnon with the gold of ransom, the aegis has golden tassels each worth a hundred oxen, and a Carian who came to war in gold loses it to Achilles.

**The king's silver and gold.** Agamemnon dresses for the assembly in tunic, cloak and sandals, slings on his “silver-studded” sword (ξίφος ἀργυρόηλον, 2.45), and takes up the scepter of his fathers (see [[dressing-scene]] and [[silver-studded]]). The scepter's gold is named only once in the book, in the line where it raises a welt on Thersites' back: the welt rose “under the golden scepter” (σκήπτρου ὕπο χρυσέου, 2.268; see [[scepter]]).

**Gold as ransom.** Thersites asks Agamemnon whether he wants “still more gold, which someone will bring out of Ilios, one of the horse-taming Trojans, as ransom for his son” (2.229–231). Gold is the price of a life (see [[apoina|ἄποινα]]), and Thersites' charge is that the king takes the ransom while others take the prisoners. In Book 1 the ransom Chryses brought for his daughter began the whole quarrel (1.13).

**The aegis.** Athena goes through the army holding the aegis, and from it “a hundred tassels hang floating, all of gold, all finely woven, and each worth a hundred oxen” (2.448–449; see [[aegis]]). The ornament of a god is priced in cattle, the measure of wealth in Homer. The same word, ἑκατόμβοιος (“the worth of a hundred oxen”), values Glaucus' golden armor (6.236).

**Silver in the Catalogue.** Apollo, “god of the silver bow”, reared the mares of Eumelus (2.766). The Peneius is ἀργυροδίνης, “silver-eddying” (2.753), and the Titaressus runs over it like oil (see [[like-olive-oil]]). The Halizones come “from far away, from Alybe, where silver has its birth” (ὅθεν ἀργύρου ἐστὶ γενέθλη, 2.857), a rare notice in Homer of where a precious metal comes from (see [[alybe]]).

**Gold like a girl.** The last gold in the book belongs to a Carian leader.

{{quote:23b85784-5f14-5a0e-a864-4125ff74640c}}

He came to the war wearing gold, “like a girl, the fool”, and it did nothing to keep off destruction. Achilles killed him in the river and “carried off the gold”. The Greek does not say whether he was Nastes or Amphimachus (see [[nastes]], [[amphimachus-of-caria]] and [[like-a-girl]]). In Book 1 gold marked what belonged to gods and kings. Here it is vanity, and then spoil.
