**The enemy in the Achaean camp.** Until the last hundred lines the Trojans are the object of Achaean plans and speeches. Zeus' Dream tells Agamemnon that "sorrows hang over the Trojans" (2.15, 2.32), a lie that will lead the Achaeans into defeat. In his speech to test the army Agamemnon counts them. If both sides made a truce and were numbered, there would not be a Trojan to pour the wine for every ten Achaeans; it is the allies who keep Troy standing (2.123–133). See [[trojan-allies]].

{{quote:809ad908-2b5f-522f-b7a2-1865426b47a4}}

Hera and Athena will not leave "to Priam and the Trojans a thing to boast of" (2.160, 2.176). Thersites imagines "one of the horse-taming Trojans" ([[tamer-of-horses]]) bringing gold out of Ilios to ransom his son (2.229–231). Nestor tells the army that no one should go home before he has slept with the wife of a Trojan (2.354–356). To the Achaeans the Trojans are a people to be counted, ransomed and despoiled.

**The Trojans at home.** Then the scene changes. Iris comes to the Trojans holding their own assembly at Priam's doors (2.786–789), and Hector breaks it up and leads them out (2.807–810). A book taken up with the Achaean council and assembly ends with a Trojan assembly, briefly told, and with both armies on the plain.

{{quote:a84a77ea-69ec-5a2d-a46d-865ef648689c}}

**The Trojan catalogue.** The Trojans themselves head the catalogue, under Hector, "by far the most and the best" (2.816–818). The men of Zeleia under Pandarus are called Trojans too (2.826), and Aeneas' Dardanians are their near kin (2.819). Everyone after them is an ally. The last mention of the Trojans in the book looks far ahead, to Achilles killing them in the river (2.861; [[the-river-battle-foretold]]).
