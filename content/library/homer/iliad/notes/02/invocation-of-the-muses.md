---
summary: The longest invocation in the Iliad introduces the Catalogue of Ships: the Muses are present and know everything, while mortals hear only report. A shorter appeal follows the Catalogue.
passages:
  - 2.484-493 | The invocation of the Muses
  - 2.760-762 | Tell me, Muse, who was the best
---

The run of similes that shows the army pouring onto the plain ends with Agamemnon standing out like a bull among the herd (2.480–483). Then the poet stops, and asks for help.

{{quote:6d988018-c781-5748-8a0a-4485685324a9}}

**Knowing and hearing.** The Muses are goddesses, they “are present” (πάρεστε), and they “know all things” (ἴστε πάντα). Mortals “hear only the report” (κλέος οἶον ἀκούομεν) “and know nothing” (οὐδέ τι ἴδμεν). The verb of knowing, οἶδα, belongs to the root of seeing: to know is to have seen. The poet knows the war only as κλέος, what is heard (see [[kleos]] and [[we-hear-only-the-report]]).

**The multitude.** The question is who the leaders and lords were. The crowd itself, the πληθύς, he could not tell or name, not with ten tongues and ten mouths, an unbreakable voice and a heart of bronze (see [[ten-tongues]]), unless the Muses were to remind him (μνησαίατο) of all who came to Ilios. What the Muses give is memory (see [[memory-and-the-muses]]).

**The program.** The last line says what the poet will do instead: “the leaders of the ships, and all the ships” (2.493). That is exactly what follows (see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). The similes before it have shown the army as uncountable, like leaves and flowers or swarming flies (2.468–473); the Catalogue counts it by its leaders and its ships (see [[numbers-and-counting]]).

**Thamyris.** In the middle of the Catalogue the Muses appear once more. They met the Thracian singer Thamyris, who boasted that he would beat them even if they sang themselves, and in their anger they maimed him and took away his song (2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). The singer who asks them for help, and the singer who challenged them, stand in the same book.

**After the Catalogue.** A shorter appeal, to one Muse, asks who was the best of the men and horses that followed the sons of Atreus (2.761–762). The answer is Eumelus' mares and Telamonian Ajax, “while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.763–770; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). The Muse's knowledge brings the poem back to its subject.
