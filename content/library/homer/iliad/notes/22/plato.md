---
summary: The Republic strikes out four passages of this book, Zeus' grief for Hector pursued, Priam rolling in the dung, Achilles' words to Apollo and the soul that flies to Hades lamenting, and denies the draggings of Hector; its rule that soldiers must not treat a dead enemy's body as the enemy bears on this book too. The Apology and the Republic use the proverbial oak and rock of Hector's monologue, the Cratylus finds Homer's reason for the name Astyanax in Andromache's lament, and the Ion names Achilles rushing at Hector, and the pitiful things about Andromache, Hecuba and Priam, among the passages with which a rhapsode most moves his audience.
---

- The soul that flies from the limbs to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (*Republic* 3.386d, quoting 16.856–857 = 22.362–363): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]].
- Priam “rolling in the dung, calling each man by his name”, and Zeus' grief for the man dear to him whom he sees pursued around the city (*Republic* 3.388b–c, quoting 22.414–415 and 22.168–169): [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]].
- Achilles' words to Apollo, “You have foiled me, Far-Worker, most baneful of all the gods”, and his threat of vengeance (*Republic* 3.391a, quoting 22.15 and 22.20): [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]].
- “The draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus” (*Republic* 3.391b), which are those of Book 24 (24.14–18); the dragging of this book goes from the wall to the ships (22.395–404): [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]].
- **The ransom.** Just before, Socrates will not have it believed that Achilles was so fond of money as to take gifts from Agamemnon, and again to take a price and release a corpse, but otherwise to refuse (*Republic* 3.390e–391a). The ransom is taken in Book 24. In this book Achilles refuses any ransom at all, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold” (22.349–354): [[apoina]] and [[achilles]].
- **The body of the enemy.** In the fifth book Socrates would have his soldiers give up stripping the dead of anything but their arms, and give up hindering the taking up of the dead: to think the body of a dead man an enemy, when the enemy has flown away and left behind that with which he fought, is the mark of a small mind, like dogs that are angry with the stones thrown at them and do not touch the thrower (*Republic* 5.469c–e). He does not name Homer. In this book the Achaeans stab Hector's body where it lies, and Achilles, calling the dying man “you dog”, refuses to give it back (22.344–354, 22.369–375): [[title-of-book-22]] and [[stripping-the-dead]].
- **The oak and the rock.** Socrates, refusing to bring his family before the jury, says that he too, “in Homer's very words”, is not sprung “from an oak or from a rock” but from human beings, and has kinsmen and three sons (*Apology* 34d); in the *Republic* he asks whether constitutions come “from an oak or a rock”, and not from the characters of the citizens (*Republic* 8.544d). Plato's sense, of birth, is Penelope's in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 19.163). In this book Hector uses the phrase of idle talk: “There is no way now, from an oak or from a rock, / to talk intimately with him” (22.126–127): [[words-and-violence]] and [[oaristys]].
- The reason for the name Astyanax, “for he alone guarded their city and long walls” (*Cratylus* 392d–e, on 22.506–507): [[plato-astyanax]].
- **The rhapsode.** Socrates asks Ion whether, when he recites well and most astonishes his audience, singing of Odysseus leaping onto the threshold, or of Achilles rushing at Hector, or something of the pitiful things about Andromache or Hecuba or Priam, he is in his right mind or beside himself; Ion answers that at a pitiful thing his eyes fill with tears, and at a fearful or terrible one his hair stands on end and his heart leaps (*Ion* 535b–c). Achilles rushes at Hector in this book (22.312), and Priam, Hecuba and Andromache lament him at its end (22.405–515): [[title-of-book-22]] and [[lament]].
