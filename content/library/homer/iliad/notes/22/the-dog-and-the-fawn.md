{{quote:a7bce12b-334b-518d-a538-bb14dba8b3b3}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε νεβρὸν ὄρεσφι κύων ἐλάφοιο δίηται / ὄρσας ἐξ εὐνῆς διά τʼ ἄγκεα καὶ διὰ βήσσας: “As when in the mountains a dog chases the fawn of a deer, / having roused it from its lair, through the valleys and through the glens” (22.189–190). The fawn of Book 15 had already been hit by a hunter as it leapt from its lair, and the dog only darted at it (15.579–581). This one has been roused unhurt from its bed, εὐνή, the word of that lair too (ἐξ εὐνῆφι, 15.580), and the dog must run it down. δίημαι, “chase”, returns in Andromache's fear that Achilles has cut Hector off alone from the city “and is chasing him toward the plain” (πεδίον δὲ δίηται, 22.456). ἄγκεα and βῆσσαι are the valleys and glens of the mountains, the country of the hunt: the lion whose cubs a deer hunter has taken “goes through many valleys, searching out the man's tracks” (18.321), in the simile of Achilles' grief for Patroclus (see [[lion]]).

τὸν δʼ εἴ πέρ τε λάθῃσι καταπτήξας ὑπὸ θάμνῳ, / ἀλλά τʼ ἀνιχνεύων θέει ἔμπεδον ὄφρά κεν εὕρῃ: “and even if it escapes his notice, cowering under a bush, / yet he runs on, tracking it steadily, until he finds it” (22.191–192). καταπτήξας, “cowering”, is the crouch of the hare, πτώξ, “the cowerer”, which lies under a leafy bush and cannot hide from the eagle's eye (θάμνῳ ὑπʼ ἀμφικόμῳ, 17.676–677; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]] and [[hare]]). ἀνιχνεύων, “tracking”, from ἴχνος, “a track”, occurs only here in Homer. The lion of Book 18 searched out the hunter's tracks (ἴχνια) as this dog tracks the fawn; the hunter there is Hector, who killed Patroclus, and here the hunt has found him.

The tenor: ὣς Ἕκτωρ οὐ λῆθε ποδώκεα Πηλεΐωνα, “so Hector did not escape the notice of the swift-footed son of Peleus” (22.193). λήθω, “escape notice”, joins the two similes of the dog and the fawn. In Book 15 the dog was Antilochus, and he “did not escape brilliant Hector's notice” (οὐ λάθεν Ἕκτορα δῖον, 15.583); now Hector is the fawn, and cannot escape. The point of likeness is persistence. The falcon of the first simile of the chase had speed (22.139–144; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]); the dog has the hunter's tirelessness, and the fawn has nowhere to hide. Then the narrator turns to the one thing that keeps Hector alive: whenever he makes for the gates, Achilles is there before him and turns him back, and the chase becomes a dream in which neither man can do what he strives for (22.194–201; see [[as-in-a-dream]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

The book began with the Trojans in the city “in panic like fawns” (22.1; see [[like-fawns]] and [[deer]]). Hector, the one man left outside, is the fawn of the chase.
