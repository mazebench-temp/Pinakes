summary: The dying Hector names the day of Achilles' death: Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy him at the Scaean gates (22.358–360). Achilles answers the dead man that he will accept his doom whenever Zeus and the other gods are willing (22.365–366). Earlier in the book Zeus sets a fate of death for each of them in his scales, and Hector's sinks (22.209–213).

**The man and the place.** No earlier prophecy is so full. Thetis told Achilles that his own fate was ready straight after Hector's (18.96); the horse Xanthus said that he was fated to be brought down “by a god and a man” (19.416–417; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]); and in the river Achilles remembered his mother's word, that he would perish under the wall of the Trojans by the arrows of Apollo (21.277–278). Hector names the man, Paris, and the place, the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[paris]], [[apollo]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Patroclus, dying, told Hector that he would fall at the hands of Achilles (16.851–854), and Hector, dying, tells Achilles how he will fall (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[the-death-of-hector]]). At the beginning of the book Apollo told Achilles, “You will not kill me, since I am not fated to fall to you” (22.13); the god will help to kill him.

**The gods' wrath.** Hector's warning comes after Achilles has refused to give back his body: “Take care now, lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you” (22.358). μήνιμα, “a cause of the gods' wrath”, is found again only in Elpenor's plea to Odysseus not to leave him unwept and unburied (*Odyssey* 11.72–73; see the translation's note on 22.358 and [[menis]]). In the last book the threat begins to come true: Apollo tells the gods that they may be angry with Achilles, good man though he is, for what he does to the body, and Zeus sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry with him and Zeus most of all (24.53–54, 24.112–115; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**The answer.** “Lie dead; and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (22.365–366). Except for τέθναθι, “Lie dead”, the words are those with which Achilles chose his death in Book 18, when he resolved to find Hector (18.115–116; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and [[ker-doom]]). He spoke them then of the man he would kill; he speaks them now to the man he has killed.

**The scales.** When for the fourth time the two came to the springs, Zeus set in his scales “two fates of death that lays men low, / one of Achilles, and one of Hector, tamer of horses”, and Hector's day sank (22.208–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The poem weighs the two deaths together, and tells only one of them.
