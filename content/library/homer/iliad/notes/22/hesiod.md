---
summary: Hesiod uses the proverbial oak and rock of Hector's monologue, as Hector does, of talk, when he breaks off his account of how the Muses made him a poet.
---

- **The oak and the rock.** Hector, waiting for Achilles, knows that there is no way now “from an oak or from a rock” to talk with him as a young man and a girl talk together (ἀπὸ δρυὸς οὐδʼ ἀπὸ πέτρης, 22.126–128). Hesiod, having told how the Muses gave him a staff of laurel and a divine voice and bade him sing of the blessed gods, breaks off with the same pair: “But what is this to me, about an oak or a rock?” (ἀλλὰ τί ἦ μοι ταῦτα περὶ δρῦν ἢ περὶ πέτρην; *Theogony* 35). In both passages the phrase goes with talk; in Penelope's question to the stranger it is a matter of birth (*Odyssey* 19.163): [[words-and-violence]] and [[oaristys]].
