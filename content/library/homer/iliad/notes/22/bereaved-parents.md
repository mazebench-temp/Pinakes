summary: The parents of the poem's greatest loss watch it happen. Priam has already been made “bereft of many brave sons” (22.44), and does not know that Lycaon and Polydorus are dead: if they are, “it is sorrow to my heart and to their mother”, but the people's sorrow will be shorter, “unless you die too” (22.46–55). When Hector falls, Priam mourns him above all the sons Achilles “has killed in their bloom” (22.423–426), and Hecuba asks, “Why should I live?” (22.431).

εὖνις, “bereft”, is found in Homer only here and once in the Odyssey, where Odysseus wishes he could make the Cyclops “bereft” of his life (22.44; *Odyssey* 9.524). Priam's reckoning sets the parents' grief apart from the people's: the loss of two sons belongs to their father and mother, the loss of Hector to everyone (22.52–55; see [[laothoe]], [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[polydorus-son-of-priam]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). His second speech puts Achilles' father beside himself, “Peleus, who begot him and reared him to be a bane / to the Trojans” (22.421–422), and wishes that Hector had died in his arms, so that “we would have had our fill of weeping”, his mother and he (22.426–428; see [[fathers-and-sons]], [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[lament]]).

The book also counts the parents of the slayer. Hector begs Achilles by his parents (22.338), and Achilles, refusing, tells him that his own mother will not lay him on a bier (22.352–353). At the end Andromache names the opposite loss, a child without a father (22.484–507; see [[chere]] and [[childhood]]).
