summary: Virgil's Andromache faints, as she does in this book, at a sight from Troy, and asks whether Aeneas comes as a true messenger, where in this book no true messenger had come to her. She honors Hector at an empty tomb, the kind of cenotaph that Walter Leaf finds hinted at in her lament here, where she says she will burn his clothes in place of his body.

**The faint.** In the Iliad Andromache is weaving in the inner part of the house, “for no true messenger had come and told her / that her husband was staying outside the gates” (22.437–439; see [[weaving]]). When she sees him dragged, “murky night covered her eyes, / and she fell backward, and breathed out her soul”, and only after a time comes to herself and laments (22.466–476; see [[andromache]]). Virgil's Andromache, seeing Aeneas and the Trojan arms around him, stiffens, the warmth leaves her bones, she falls, and after a long time she speaks: “Is it a true face, are you a true messenger (*verus … nuntius*) who comes to me, son of a goddess? Are you alive? Or, if the kindly light has left you, where is Hector?” (*Aeneid* 3.306–312). Her words recall the Iliad's “true messenger”, ἐτήτυμος ἄγγελος (22.438), and her question ends with Hector.

**The empty tomb.** Aeneas finds her pouring the yearly offerings to the ashes and calling the shade “to the tomb of Hector, which she had consecrated, empty, of green turf, and two altars, a cause for tears” (*Hectoreum ad tumulum, viridi quem caespite inanem / et geminas, causam lacrimis, sacraverat aras*, *Aeneid* 3.301–305). In this book Andromache believes that the worms will eat Hector beside the ships once the dogs have had their fill, and she will burn the clothes laid up in the halls instead, “of no use to you, since you will not lie in them, / but to be your fame before the Trojans and the Trojan women” (22.508–514; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Walter Leaf, discussing funeral rites performed without the body, says that such a cenotaph “is not mentioned in H., but naturally follows from the importance attached to the formalities of burial”, and that it is hinted at in these verses (22.512–514) and at *Odyssey* 1.291 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.323). In the Iliad the body comes back and is buried in Book 24; Virgil's Andromache, far from Troy, keeps a tomb without it.
