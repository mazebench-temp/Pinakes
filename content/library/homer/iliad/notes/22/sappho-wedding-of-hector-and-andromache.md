The memory comes at the moment the marriage ends. Andromache has run to the wall and seen Hector dragged toward the ships; night covers her eyes, she falls backward, and the bindings fly from her head, the headband, the cap, the plaited band, and the veil, κρήδεμνον, “that golden Aphrodite had given her / on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet took her / from the house of Eetion, after giving a countless bride-price” (22.466–472; see [[andromache]], [[veil]] and [[bride-price]]). In her lament she goes back further, to her birth: “we were both born, it seems, to one fate, / both of us, you in Troy in the house of Priam, / and I in Thebe under wooded Placus” (22.477–479; see [[thebe]] and [[eetion]]). The Iliad tells no more of the wedding day than these words. There the gifts are the bridegroom's (μυρία ἕδνα, 22.472); the bride's own wealth appears in the poem only in the epithet ἄλοχος πολύδωρος, “richly dowered wife” (6.394, 22.88; see [[richly-dowered]]).
