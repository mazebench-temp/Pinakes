summary: The book opens on the difference. Apollo asks Achilles why he pursues him, “you who are mortal, me, an immortal god”, and tells him, “You will not kill me, since I am not fated to fall to you”; Achilles answers, “Truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength” (22.8–20). On Olympus Athena calls Hector “A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion” (22.179); and the Trojans, Achilles says, “prayed as to a god” to the man he has just killed (22.394).
refs: [22.8-20, 22.41-42, 22.179-180, 22.297-303, 22.394, 22.434-436]
---

Achilles' complaint is that the god has nothing to fear: “you have no fear at all of vengeance hereafter” (22.19), a vengeance a mortal could not take (see [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]). The words for mortals come in pairs: θνητὸς … θεὸν ἄμβροτον, “mortal … an immortal god” (22.9), and twice the verse-end δειλοῖσι βροτοῖσιν, “to wretched mortals”, for the star that brings fever and for the old man's body given to the dogs (22.31, 22.76; see [[immortals]] and [[mortal-men]]).

Hector learns the difference last. Zeus and Apollo “before now / protected me with ready hearts”, and now they have let him go: “the gods have called me to my death” (22.297–303; see [[divine-intervention]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). His mother remembers him as one whom the Trojans “greeted … as a god” (22.434–435), and Priam had wished Achilles as dear to the gods as he was to him, so that the dogs might eat him (22.41–42; see [[philos]]).
