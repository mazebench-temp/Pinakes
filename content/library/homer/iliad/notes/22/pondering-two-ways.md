summary: Hector, alone before the Scaean gates, speaks to his own heart in the last and longest of the Iliad's four battlefield monologues (22.98–130). He rejects the way into the city for shame, weighs going to Achilles unarmed with terms, breaks off with the turning verse, and resolves to fight; then Achilles comes, and he runs. Later in the book Achilles breaks off a plan of his own with the same turning verse (22.385).

**The form.** The monologue has the frame of the three before it, Odysseus' (11.403–410), Menelaus' (17.90–105) and Agenor's (21.552–570). It opens with the verse “and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (22.98 = 11.403 = 17.90 = 21.552; see [[deeply-troubled]]) and ὤ μοι ἐγών, “Ah me!”, and with Menelaus' very words it sets out the first course as a condition, ὤ μοι ἐγὼν εἰ μέν κε, “Ah me, if I …” (22.99; 17.91). It turns with “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (22.122 = 11.407 = 17.97 = 21.562). It closes as Achilles' musing over Lycaon closed, “So he pondered as he waited” (22.131 = 21.64; see [[achilles]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Walter Leaf finds its scheme closest to Agenor's: in both, the plainest escape is rejected at once, a more roundabout one is weighed and then dropped, and the man decides to stand with some faint hope (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.552).

**Into the city.** The plain way out is through the gates, and shame shuts it. Polydamas told him to lead the Trojans into the city on the night when Achilles rose up, and he did not listen; he has destroyed the army by his own recklessness, and a baser man will say so (22.99–107; see [[polydamas]], [[aidos]] and [[hector]]). Against that he puts the alternative of the brave, the one Odysseus put in his monologue, “whether he is struck or strikes another” (11.410): “either to face Achilles, kill him, and come home, / or to perish gloriously at his hands before the city” (22.109–110).

Like Agenor's second and third courses, it is a supposition left without a conclusion, εἰ δέ κεν, “Or what if I …” (22.111; Leaf on 21.556, 21.567). The offer is Antenor's of Book 7, Helen and the possessions with her to the sons of Atreus (7.350–351), with half of the city's wealth added, the terms that Walter Leaf finds on the shield for the besieged city (on 18.511; see [[helen]], [[possessions]] and [[oath]]).

**The turn.** The turning verse breaks the supposition off, and a reason follows, as it does for Odysseus, Menelaus and Agenor (11.408–410, 17.98–101, 21.563–566). Hector's reason is Achilles himself: “I fear that I may go up to him, and he will not pity me, / nor respect me at all, but will kill me naked, / just like a woman, once I have taken off my armor” (22.123–125; see [[pity]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). “There is no way now, from an oak or from a rock, / to talk intimately with him” (22.126–127; see [[oaristys]]). The phrase about the oak and the rock is proverbial, and its sense is not certain. Penelope uses it when she asks the stranger for his family, “for you are not from an oak of old story, nor from a rock” (*Odyssey* 19.163), and Hesiod breaks off the first part of his proem with it: ἀλλὰ τί ἦ μοι ταῦτα περὶ δρῦν ἢ περὶ πέτρην; (*Theogony* 35), word for word “but why to me these things about oak or rock?” Hesiod's verse begins with the words that begin Hector's turning verse, ἀλλὰ τί ἤ μοι ταῦτα, “But why … these things”, and ends with the oak and the rock that come four verses after it. The decision is to fight at once and leave the outcome to Zeus: “Better to drive together in strife as quickly as may be; / let us see to which of us the Olympian will hold out triumph” (22.129–130). Idomeneus said the like to Meriones on the way into battle, “so that as quickly as may be / we may see whether we will hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (13.326–327; see [[decision-scene]]).

**The decision undone.** Odysseus decided to stand and stood; Menelaus decided to give way and gave way; Agenor decided to stand and threw his spear (11.411–488, 17.106–108, 21.571–594). Of the four, Hector alone does not do what he has decided. When Achilles comes near, “trembling seized him; and he could no longer bear / to stay there” (22.136–137; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Menelaus had put the rule in his own monologue, when the man coming on was Hector: “When a man is willing, against a god's will, to fight with a man / whom a god honors, soon a great bane rolls down on him” (17.98–99). When Hector at last stands, after the deceit of Athena, he has no choices left to weigh. His second speech to himself has no alternatives, only the knowledge that the gods have called him to his death and a wish for the manner of it: “Let me not die without a struggle and without glory” (22.297–305; see [[the-death-of-hector]]).

**Achilles breaks off.** The turning verse comes once more in the book, and for the only time outside a monologue. Achilles, standing among the Achaeans over Hector's body, proposes that they make trial of the city to learn whether the Trojans will leave it or stay, and breaks off: “But why does my heart debate these things with me? / There lies beside the ships a corpse unwept, unburied, / Patroclus” (22.381–387; see [[patroclus]]). The plan is dropped, and they go back to the ships singing the paean (22.391–394).
