---
summary: Answering Plato's charges, Proclus defends this book's Achilles against impiety: his words to Apollo, “most baneful of all the gods”, were spoken to the daemon of Apollo's line who guarded Hector, and the same power, with Paris, was to kill him. He also explains the laments of Zeus for Hector and of Priam in the dung, and the dragging of Hector.
---

- Achilles' words to Apollo (22.15, 22.20), spoken not to the god but to the lower power of Apollo that guarded Hector, the Apollo who “left him” when his day sank (22.213) and who with Paris would destroy Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360) (*On the Republic* I 146–148 Kroll): [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]].
- The laments that Plato would strike out: Priam rolling in the dung, and the greatest of the gods lamenting Hector pursued (22.414–415, 22.168–169). The heroes, he answers, live the life of action and grieve as men of action do, and the gods' tears are the poets' sign of their care for mortal things (*On the Republic* I 122–125 Kroll): [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]].
- The soul that leaves the body “leaving manhood and youth”, which Proclus gives to Patroclus, though the verses are said of Hector too (22.362–363; *On the Republic* I 119–120 Kroll): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]].
- The dragging of Hector, defended as a Thessalian custom and as the due penalty for what Hector meant to do with the body of Patroclus (*On the Republic* I 150–151 Kroll, on 24.14–21): [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]].
