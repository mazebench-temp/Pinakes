**Athena.** The scene with Achilles has the parts of the type: the descent from Olympus (22.187; see [[olympus]]), the arrival, the speech with its order, “But you stand still now and catch your breath” (22.215–223), and the hero's obedience, “So spoke Athena, and he obeyed, and rejoiced in his heart” (22.224; see [[athena]]). In Book 1 she came to the same man to stop him, “appearing to him alone; none of the others saw her” (1.198; see [[athena-restrains-achilles]]). Then she goes to Hector in another's shape (22.226–227; see [[deiphobus]]). The same verse brings her to each, ἀγχοῦ δʼ ἱσταμένη ἔπεα πτερόεντα προσηύδα, “and standing near him she addressed him with winged words” (22.215, 22.228), and in the Iliad it is always a goddess coming to a man: Athena to Pandarus in the likeness of Laodocus (4.92), Athena to Diomedes (5.123), Iris to Achilles (18.169). No departure is told. Hector learns that she has gone when he calls for a spear and his brother is not there (22.294–299; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Apollo.** Apollo's epiphany runs the other way. A god who has been seen as a man tells the man who has chased him that he is a god, and does not say which; Achilles names him, “Far-Worker” (22.15; see [[far-worker]], [[apollo]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). There is no astonishment, only anger: “You have foiled me” (22.15).
