**Before the death.** Hecuba's plea already speaks of the rites: “For if he kills you, I shall never / weep for you on a bier, dear scion, whom I bore myself, / nor will your richly dowered wife” (22.86–88; see [[hecuba]] and [[bed]]). The body laid on its bed and wept over by the women of the house is the first of the honors of the dead, and the dogs by the ships will take its place (see [[honoring-the-dead]]). Achilles, refusing Hector's last plea, says it again in the words he spoke over Lycaon: “not even so shall your lady mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you, whom she bore herself” (22.352–353; 21.123–124; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Hector's own soul goes to Hades “lamenting its fate” (ὃν πότμον γοόωσα, 22.363), as Patroclus' did (16.857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]). Priam's grief begins before his son's death: when he sees Achilles coming, “the old man groaned aloud, and beat his head with his hands” (22.33; see [[priam]]).

**The city wails.**

{{quote:dce66aad-384e-521b-a0e5-98217615b396}}

The gestures are a mourner's: Hecuba tears her hair and flings away her head covering when she sees her son dragged (see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[hair]] and [[veil]]). κωκύω, “wail” (see the translation's table of key words), is in Homer always the cry of women, of Thetis for her son, of Briseis over Patroclus, of Hecuba here (18.37, 19.284, 22.407); οἰμώζω, “groan”, and οἰμωγή, “groaning”, are always men's, as when Priam “groaned pitifully” (22.408). Andromache, at her loom, hears both from the tower, “the wailing and the groaning” (22.447; see [[andromache]]). The narrator likens the cry to a whole city smoldering with fire from top to bottom (22.410–411; see [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]]), the fall that Hector's death foretells (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**Three laments.** Each of Hector's family speaks in turn, and each is answered. Priam, held back from the gates, mourns the son for whom “sharp grief will bring me down to the house of Hades”, and wishes he had died in his arms, “then we would have had our fill of weeping and shedding tears”; “So he spoke, weeping, and the citizens groaned with him” (22.416–429; see [[bereaved-parents]] and [[tears]]). Hecuba “led the unceasing lamentation” among the Trojan women: “My child, wretched that I am! Why should I live …?” (22.430–436; see [[trojan-women]]). Andromache, come to herself among her husband's sisters and his brothers' wives, laments “with deep sobs” (ἀμβλήδην, 22.476, found only here), and speaks to Hector of their one fate, of her widowhood and the orphan, and of his body naked by the ships while she burns his clothes to be his fame (22.477–514; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]], [[chere]] and [[kleos]]); “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” (22.515 = 19.301). Over the body in the last book the laments come again, after the singers of the dirge, from Andromache, Hecuba and Helen, answered by the women and at the last by the whole people (24.719–776). Here the father speaks first, and the wife last, since she has not yet heard (22.437–439).
