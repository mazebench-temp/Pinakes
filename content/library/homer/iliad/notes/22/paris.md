---
summary: Paris is named twice, both times by Hector: as Alexander, who brought Helen's possessions to Troy, in the terms Hector thinks of offering Achilles (22.115–116), and as Paris, who with Phoebus Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates, in Hector's dying words (22.359–360).
---

**Alexander.** Hector thinks of giving back “all, as many as Alexander in his hollow ships / brought to Troy, which was the beginning of the quarrel” (22.115–116; see [[helen]] and [[possessions]]). The verse and a half are the words in which the herald Idaeus reported Paris' own offer to the Achaeans in Book 7: he would give back the possessions but not the woman, and the herald added his own wish that Paris had perished first (7.362–364, 7.389–391; see [[idaeus]]). Menelaus called the war his quarrel, “and because Alexander began it” (3.100; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]). Hector puts the thought away (22.122–130; see [[hector]]).

**Paris.** Hector's last words are a warning to Achilles: “Take care now, lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you, / on that day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo / destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates” (22.358–360). The horse Xanthus told Achilles that he was fated “to be brought down by force by a god and a man” (19.417), and Achilles remembers his mother saying that he would die under the wall of the Trojans “by the swift arrows of Apollo” (21.277–278). Hector names the man, and the place (see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]], [[apollo]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Paris fights with the bow: from behind the pillar on the tomb of Ilus he wounded Diomedes in the foot, and later Machaon and Eurypylus (11.369–383, 11.505–507, 11.581–584; see [[diomedes]], [[machaon]] and [[eurypylus]]). Hector has called him Δύσπαρι, “Evil Paris”, and wished that he had never been born (3.39–40; again 13.769; see [[evil-paris]]). In his last words he gives him the killing of Achilles. The Iliad does not tell it. Paris is named again only in the last book: by the narrator, who traces the gods' hatred of Troy to his folly, by Priam among the sons he abuses, and by Helen in her lament for Hector (24.28–30, 24.249, 24.763).
