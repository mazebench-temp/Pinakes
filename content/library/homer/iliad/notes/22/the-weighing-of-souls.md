---
summary: The weighing of two men, the form that tragedy and epic took up, is in this book: at the springs, on the fourth round of the chase, Zeus sets the fates of Achilles and Hector in his golden scales, and Hector's day sinks. Virgil's Jupiter weighs Aeneas and Turnus as their duel begins, before the flight; in Homer the scales end the chase.
refs: [22.209-213]
---

{{quote:b3ca717f-55ad-5165-89a1-d8c459771e51}}

In Book 8 the scales weigh two armies; here they weigh two men, “one of Achilles, and one of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.211), and the verses are those of Book 8, with the two men in place of the two armies and Hector's day in place of the Achaeans' (22.209–212; 8.69–72; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The weighing comes when the chase reaches the springs for the fourth time, and it ends it: “and Phoebus Apollo left him”, and Athena goes to Achilles (22.208–214; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

Virgil keeps the two men but moves the moment. His Jupiter weighs the fates of Aeneas and Turnus as they clash, and Turnus' sword breaks and his flight begins (*Aeneid* 12.725–746); in Homer the flight comes first and the scales end it. The rest of Virgil's duel follows this book closely (see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]).
