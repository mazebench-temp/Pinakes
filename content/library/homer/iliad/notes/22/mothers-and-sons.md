summary: Hecuba begs her son from the wall with her breast in her hand: “Hector, my child, respect this, and pity me / myself, if ever I gave you the breast that makes care forgotten” (22.82–83). If Achilles kills him, she says, “I shall never / weep for you on a bier, dear scion, whom I bore myself” (22.86–87); she sees him dragged, tears her hair and wails (22.405–407), and leads the women's lament, “My child, wretched that I am! Why should I live …?” (22.431).

Her fear comes back from Achilles' mouth. He tells the dying Hector, “not even so shall your lady mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you, whom she bore herself” (22.352–353): the end of the verse is hers (ὃν τέκον αὐτή, 22.87; ὃν τέκεν αὐτή, 22.353, the only two places in Homer), and the rest is what he told Lycaon over the river (21.123–124; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[lament]]). The gesture of the breast is described only here in Homer, in the verbs of loosening and lifting (22.80; see [[hecuba]] and [[aidos]]), and the breast returns in the last book, when Hera sets Hector, a mortal who sucked a woman's breast, against Achilles, the son of a goddess (24.58–59; see [[thetis]]). Priam, after the death, names her “his mother, most ill-fated, who bore him” (22.428).

The book has another mother at its end. Andromache imagines the orphan coming back in tears “to his widowed mother” (22.499; see [[andromache]], [[chere]] and [[childhood]]). Laothoe, the mother of Lycaon and Polydorus, is named in Priam's plea, with the sorrow that her sons' death would bring “to my heart and to their mother” (22.48–53; see [[laothoe]] and [[bereaved-parents]]).
