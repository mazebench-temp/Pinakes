summary: The book's two dead are given opposite fates. Over Hector Achilles says, “you the dogs and the birds / will tear shamefully, but him the Achaeans will give burial rites” (22.335–336); Hector asks once more for his body to be given back, as in his challenge of Book 7, and Achilles refuses it, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold” (22.338–354). Patroclus lies by the ships, “a corpse unwept, unburied” (22.386), and Hector is dragged there behind the chariot.

**The terms refused.** Before the fight Hector offers what he offered in Book 7, with the gods as witnesses: he will not defile Achilles if he kills him, but will strip the armor and give the body back to the Achaeans, “and you do the same” (22.254–259; 7.76–86). Achilles will have no agreements (22.261–267; see [[oath]]). The verb of Hector's promise, “I will not defile you” (ἀεικιῶ, 22.256), returns when Achilles has done the opposite: Zeus “gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (ἀεικίσσασθαι, 22.403–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**Unwept, unburied.** Achilles' words for Patroclus are the words of Elpenor's ghost in the Odyssey, who begs Odysseus not to leave him ἄκλαυτον ἄθαπτον, lest he become θεῶν μήνιμα, and asks for a mound καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι (*Odyssey* 11.72–76); Hector's last words use the other two phrases, “a cause of the gods' wrath” and “even for men to come to hear of” (22.358, 22.305; see the translation's note on 22.358 and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Patroclus is unburied by Achilles' own vow, to give him no burial rites until he brings back Hector's armor and head (18.333–335).

{{quote:9849cf11-227b-5a8d-a3f8-ca792d7c71f3}}

**The mourners.** The book's first wish for an unburied body is Priam's, for Achilles' own: if he were as dear to the gods as to Priam, “soon the dogs and vultures would eat him / as he lay” (22.42–43), and Walter Leaf takes κείμενον, “as he lay”, as “unburied” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.43). Hecuba knows the cost before the fight: if Achilles kills him, “I shall never / weep for you on a bier”, nor will his wife (22.86–88). Priam wishes that Hector had died in his arms, “then we would have had our fill of weeping and shedding tears” (22.426–427). Andromache, who cannot have the body, will burn the clothes laid up for it, “of no use to you, since you will not lie in them, / but to be your fame” (22.510–514); Walter Leaf sees in these lines, and in Athena's advice to Telemachus to heap a mound for his father if he is dead (*Odyssey* 1.291), a hint of a cenotaph, which Homer never mentions (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.323; see the translation's note on 22.512–514 and [[lament]]).
