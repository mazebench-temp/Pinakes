ὣς φαμένη μεγάροιο διέσσυτο μαινάδι ἴση / παλλομένη κραδίην· ἅμα δʼ ἀμφίπολοι κίον αὐτῇ, “So saying, she rushed through the hall like a maenad, / with her heart leaping; and her handmaids went with her” (22.460–461). μαινάς, “maenad”, the raving woman of the rites of Dionysus in later Greek, occurs only here in Homer. The comparison takes two words, μαινάδι ἴση, “equal to a maenad”, as the comparison of Book 6 took two, μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα, “like a woman out of her mind” (6.389), and both words come from μαίνομαι, “rage, be mad”, the verb of the god himself in the story of the nurses of “raving Dionysus”, whom Lycurgus drove down the Nysean mount (6.132–133; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and [[dionysus]]). παλλομένη κραδίην, “with her heart leaping”, takes up her own words to her women a moment before: “in my own / breast my heart leaps up to my mouth, and beneath me my knees / are frozen” (22.451–453).

The scene is that of Book 6, with the end changed. There she had gone to the great tower of Ilios when she heard that the Trojans were hard pressed, with the nurse carrying the child, and she came running to meet Hector at the Scaean gates, alive (6.386–395; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). Here she is at her loom in the inner part of the high house, weaving a double-folded purple cloth and working flowers into it, and she has told her handmaids to set a great tripod over the fire for Hector's bath when he comes home from the battle; no messenger has told her that he stayed outside the gates (22.437–446; see [[weaving]] and [[bathing]]). She hears the wailing from the tower, her limbs shake, the shuttle falls to the ground, and she goes out with two of her women (22.447–461; see [[handmaids]]). From the wall she sees him “being dragged in front of the city; and the swift horses / were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans” (22.464–465), and murky night covers her eyes (22.466–467; see [[andromache]]).

The point of likeness is the wildness of her running, out of the house and through the city. In Book 6 the madness was the housekeeper's word for her hurry, and she found her husband at the gate. In Book 22 the narrator gives her the maenad's frenzy, and she finds him dead.
