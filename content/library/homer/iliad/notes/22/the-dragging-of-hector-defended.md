summary: The first dragging of Hector is in this book, from the wall to the ships. Plato's charge and the Thessalian custom that the defenders cite concern the later draggings around the tomb of Patroclus; and in this book it is Hector who offers to give back the loser's body, and Achilles who refuses, with Patroclus' stripping in his mind.
refs: [22.395-404]
---

The dragging of this book is not around a tomb. Achilles pierces the tendons of Hector's feet, ties him to the chariot with oxhide straps, and drives to the ships with the head trailing in the dust, while the city watches; the poem calls it “shameful deeds” (ἀεικέα … ἔργα, 22.395) and says that Zeus gave Hector to his enemies “to defile in his own native land” (22.395–404, 22.463–465; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). The tomb comes later: Patroclus is burned and his mound heaped up in Book 23, and only in Book 24 does Achilles drag Hector three times around it, day after day (24.14–18). Plato's “draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus” (*Republic* 3.391b), and the Thessalian custom of dragging bodies around tombs that the defenders cite, belong to that book.

The defenders' other answer, that Hector began the outrage, rests on Books 17 and 18, on what Hector meant to do with Patroclus' head and body. In this book the roles are the other way around. Before the duel Hector offers an agreement that the winner should strip the loser's armor and give back his body, and Achilles refuses it: there are no oaths between lions and men (22.254–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). When Hector falls, Achilles' first words are of Patroclus: “Hector, you thought, I suppose, when you were stripping Patroclus, / that you would be safe” (22.331–332); the dogs and birds will tear Hector, “but him the Achaeans will give burial rites” (22.335–336). The poem gives Achilles' reason as revenge for his friend, not as a penalty for what Hector intended (see [[hector]] and [[patroclus]]).
