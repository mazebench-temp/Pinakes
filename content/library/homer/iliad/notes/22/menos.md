---
summary: The two men's might is measured through the book. Hector waits “with unquenchable might” (22.96), Apollo “roused his might and his nimble knees” for the last time (22.204), and Hector accuses Achilles of lying “so that in fear of you I might forget my might and my courage” (22.282). Achilles, charging, “filled his heart with savage / fury” (22.312–313), and wishes that “my fury and my heart” would let him eat Hector raw (22.346–347). Andromache remembers a husband who “ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might” (22.459).
---

ἄσβεστον μένος, “unquenchable might”, is said only here in Homer; the adjective belongs to laughter, the battle cry, flame and fame (see [[unquenchable-laughter]] and [[unquenchable-cry]]). Apollo's verse is the one in which Aeneas told how Zeus saved him from Achilles on Ida, “who roused my might and my nimble knees” (20.93 = 22.204; see [[apollo]] and [[nimble]]). Andromache's verse is the one in which Odysseus, in the Odyssey, tells the shade of Achilles how his son Neoptolemus fought at Troy (22.459 = *Odyssey* 11.515), and she gives Hector's might its other name, ἀγηνορίη, “pride” (22.457; see [[andromache]]). Apollo's opening words use the verb: “you rage so relentlessly” (ἀσπερχὲς μενεαίνεις, 22.10), the half-verse with which Zeus reproached Hera's hatred of Troy (4.32; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]).
