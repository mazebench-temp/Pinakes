---
summary: Achilles kills Hector, and with him the condition of his own death, for his mother had told him that “straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.96). Zeus sets in his scales “two fates of death that lays men low, / one of Achilles, and one of Hector” (22.210–211); the dying Hector names the day “when Paris and Phoebus Apollo / destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates” (22.359–360); and Achilles answers in the words of his choice, “Lie dead; and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (22.365–366).
refs: [22.209-213, 22.358-366, 22.386-390]

{{quote:06ecc1eb-d7ce-56b6-bd11-384c1400e4fc}}

The two lines of the answer are the two Achilles spoke in Book 18, when he resolved to find Hector, “the destroyer of that dear life” (18.114–116 = 22.365–366); there they followed the resolve, here they follow the deed, and they open with τέθναθι, “Lie dead”, a perfect imperative found only here and spoken to a man already dead (22.364; see [[ker-doom]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The prophecy he answers adds Paris to the god whose arrows his mother had named (21.277–278) and gives the place, the Scaean gates; the horse Xanthus had said only “a god and a man” (19.417; see [[prophecy]]).

In the scales the fate of Achilles lies beside Hector's, and it is Hector's day that sinks (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Achilles speaks of his own life only as a measure of memory: he will not forget Patroclus “as long as I / am among the living and my knees have strength to move”, and will remember him even in the house of Hades (22.387–390; see [[hetairos]] and [[patroclus]]).
