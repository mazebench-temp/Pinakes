Mourning over Hector, Andromache foresees the life of their orphaned son. Once he ate marrow on his father's knees; “but now he will suffer much, having lost his dear father, / he whom the Trojans call by the name of Astyanax; / for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.505–507; see [[astyanax]] and [[andromache]]). In Book 6 the narrator gives the name and its reason in the third person: Hector called the boy Scamandrius, “but the others Astyanax; for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.402–403).

Socrates' proof in the *Cratylus* draws on both passages. He says that in Homer the boy is called Astyanax “by the Trojans” (ὑπὸ τῶν Τρώων, *Cratylus* 392d), which is Andromache's “whom the Trojans call” (ὃν Τρῶες ἐπίκλησιν καλέουσιν, 22.506), where Book 6 says “the others”. And the verse he quotes as Homer's reason is Andromache's with two words changed: οἶος γάρ σφιν ἔρυτο πόλιν καὶ τείχεα μακρά, “for he alone guarded their city and long walls” (392e). Our text has ἔρυσο, “you guarded”, and πύλας, “gates” (22.507). The third person is Book 6's; the long walls are this book's. Socrates draws the conclusion that the son of the city's savior is rightly called its lord, “as Homer says” (392e; see [[plato]]).
