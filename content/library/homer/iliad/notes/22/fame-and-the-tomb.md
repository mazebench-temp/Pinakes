---
summary: The man who imagined his victim's tomb in Book 7 dies with no hope of his own. Hector, knowing that the gods have called him, asks only for fame: “Let me not die without a struggle and without glory, / but having done some great thing, even for men to come to hear of” (22.304–305). His body is taken to the ships, and Andromache, who will have nothing to bury, will burn his clothes “to be your fame before the Trojans and the Trojan women” (22.510–514).

The half-line καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι, “even for men to come to hear of”, is used in the Iliad only by Agamemnon, of a disgrace (2.119), and by Hector here; in the Odyssey it closes Elpenor's request for a mound (*Odyssey* 11.75–76; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). Hector's ἀκλειῶς, “without glory”, is the word Telemachus and Eumaeus use of Odysseus, snatched away by the Harpies instead of dying at Troy, where the Achaeans would have made him a tomb (*Odyssey* 1.239–241, 14.369–371; see [[kleos]]). Earlier in the book he had weighed the other ending, “to perish gloriously at his hands before the city” (ἐϋκλειῶς, 22.110; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).

In Book 7 Hector's challenge gave the dead man's body back for a mound by the Hellespont, which a passer-by would read as Hector's fame (7.84–91). Now Achilles keeps the body (22.345–354), and there is no mound for Hector until the last lines of the poem (24.797–801). Walter Leaf sees in Andromache's burning of the clothes a hint of a cenotaph, a monument without a body, which Homer never mentions (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.323; see the translation's note on 22.512–514 and [[tomb]]).
