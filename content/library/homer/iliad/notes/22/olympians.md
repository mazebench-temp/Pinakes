**The gods together.** In Book 16 Zeus put the question of Sarpedon to Hera alone, and she spoke of the other gods (16.443–449); here he speaks to the whole company: “But come, consider, you gods, and take counsel, / whether we shall save him from death, or now at last / bring him down by Achilles, son of Peleus, brave as he is” (22.174–176; see [[zeus]]). Athena's answer speaks for the others, “not all of us other gods approve” (22.181; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]] and [[athena]]), and the decision is made without another voice. In Book 20 Zeus stayed on Olympus to look on and sent the others down into the war (20.22–25), and after their battle they went back and sat beside him (21.518–520); now they all look on (see [[watching-the-war]]).

**In men's speech.** Hector asks Achilles to “call upon the gods; for they will be the best / witnesses and overseers of agreements” (22.254–255; see the translation's note on 22.254, and [[oath]]), and Achilles refuses any oath between them (22.261–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). Hector, deceived, says that “the gods have called me to my death” (22.297), the words the narrator spoke to the dying Patroclus, “when the gods called you to your death” (16.693; see [[patroclus]]). Dying, he warns Achilles against becoming “a cause of the gods' wrath” (22.358; see [[menis]] and the translation's note on 22.358). Achilles answers that he will accept his doom whenever Zeus wills it “and the other immortal gods” (22.366; see [[immortals]]), and tells the Achaeans, “since the gods have granted us to bring down this man” (22.379). The Trojans, he says, prayed to Hector “as to a god” (22.394; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).
