---
summary: Hector's life is the prize of the race and the stake of the duel. The two men run past the springs “for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.161); Hector would “take your life” if Zeus let him (22.257); Achilles' spear finds the throat, “where the destruction of life is swiftest” (22.325); Hector begs “by your life and your knees” (22.338); and when he dies “his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades” (22.362). Andromache, at the sight of his body, “breathed out her soul” and lives (22.467).

{{quote:0103caf6-c062-562a-b146-9bb65af93207}}

The narrator sets the life against the prizes of a footrace, “no beast for sacrifice or ox-hide … such as are prizes for the feet of men” (22.159–161); περὶ ψυχῆς, “for life”, recurs in Homer only when Odysseus plans the escape from the Cyclops' cave (*Odyssey* 9.423; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). The verses of the soul's going are Patroclus' (22.362–363 = 16.856–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]] and [[hades]]).

Andromache's swoon is told in the words of death, as Sarpedon's was when “his soul left him, and a mist was poured over his eyes”, and he breathed again (5.696–697): night covers her eyes, she breathes out her ψυχή (ἀπὸ … ἐκάπυσσε, only here in Homer), and she comes to herself (ἔμπνυτο) when her spirit is gathered back (22.466–475; see [[thymos]] and [[andromache]]).
