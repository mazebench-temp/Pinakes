summary: The book is fought under the wall of Troy. The Trojans rest on its battlements while Hector alone stays outside; his parents beg him to come inside it, and shame keeps him out. Achilles chases him three times around it, and whenever Hector makes for the gates and the towers, where the men above might defend him, Achilles turns him back toward the plain. Athena deceives him in the likeness of a brother who has dared to come out of the wall, and Andromache, running to the wall, sees him dragged away.

**Inside and outside.** The book opens on the wall. The Trojans who fled into the city like fawns cool their sweat and drink, “leaning on the fair battlements” (22.1–3; see [[battlements]] and [[like-fawns]]), while the Achaeans “came nearer the wall, leaning their shields against their shoulders” (22.4). Walter Leaf notes that the same formation is made elsewhere to receive a charge (11.593, 13.488), finds it by no means clear how it could serve in an advance, and suggests that the approach to the walls may be a mere reconnaissance carried out with all defensive precautions (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.4; see the translation's note on 22.4). Hector alone stays outside, held by fate “in front of Ilios and the Scaean gates” (22.5–6; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[hector]]). Achilles, whom Apollo's trick has turned “away from the wall” (22.16; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]), strides back toward the city (22.21).

**Come inside the wall.** Priam begs his son, “But come inside the wall, my child, so that you may save / the Trojans and the Trojan women” (22.56–57; see [[priam]]), and Hecuba bids him “ward off the enemy / from inside the wall” (22.84–85; see [[hecuba]]). Hector answers in his own heart: “If I go within the gates and the walls, / Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me” (22.99–100). In Book 18 Polydamas would have had the army trust to the towers and the gates, and said that Achilles might roam beneath the city with his horses but would never rush in (18.254–283; see [[polydamas]] and the note on Book 18). Hector thinks of setting down his shield and helmet, leaning “my spear against the wall” and going to offer terms, and gives up the thought (22.111–130; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).

**Around the wall.** When Achilles comes, Hector flees “beneath the wall of the Trojans” (22.144; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]), and the two run “always out from under the wall, along the wagon road” (22.146), past the lookout, the fig tree and the springs, three times around the city (22.145–166; see [[the-lookout-at-troy]], [[the-fig-tree]], [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Zeus sees “a man dear to me, pursued around the wall” (22.168); Plato quotes the verse with περὶ ἄστυ, “around the city” (*Republic* 3.388c; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]). The wall is Hector's one hope of rescue:

{{quote:2441976e-583b-5604-8aea-a843129cc3f1}}

From above, καθύπερθεν, the men on the towers could defend him only if he came in close under them, and each time Achilles gets ahead of him and turns him back toward the plain (see [[dardanian-gates]]). Achilles will not let his own men throw at Hector either, so that no one else may win the glory (22.205–207). Strabo, following Demetrius of Scepsis, took the race as evidence that Homer's city did not stand where the Ilium of his own day stood: the present city could not be run around, because of the continuous ridge joined to it, but the old site could (*Geography* 13.1.37; see [[strabo-on-the-chase-around-troy]]).

**Out of the wall.** Athena comes to Hector in the likeness of Deiphobus, and he honors his brother, who dared “to come outside the wall, while the others stay inside” (22.236–237). When he calls for a second spear and no one is there, he understands: “but he is within the wall, and Athena has deceived me” (22.299; see [[deiphobus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**The long walls.** Andromache hears the wailing from the tower and runs to it; “she stood on the wall, looking about, and saw him / being dragged in front of the city” (22.463–464; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]], [[andromache]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). In her lament she tells Hector that the Trojans call his son Astyanax, because “you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.506–507; see [[astyanax]]). τείχεα μακρά, “long walls”, are the walls of Zeus' taunt to Hera, who would go in “through the gates and the long walls / and eat Priam raw” (4.34–35; see [[hera]]).
