summary: Zeus, watching Achilles chase Hector around the walls, grieves for the man who has burned many thighs of oxen for him “on the peaks of many-folded Ida”, and at other times on the height of the city (22.168–173).
---

“for Hector, who has burned many thighs of oxen for me / on the peaks of many-folded Ida, and at other times / on the height of the city” (22.170–172; see [[zeus]], [[hector]] and [[sacrifice]]). Ida is Zeus' mountain. On its peak Gargarus he has “a precinct and a fragrant altar” (8.48; see [[gargarus]]); on the peaks of Ida, Ἴδης ἐν κορυφῇσι, he sits with the lightning in his hands in Book 11 and wakes beside Hera in Book 15 (11.183–184, 15.5); and in the prayers at the oath in Book 3 he is “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida” (3.276, 3.320). Hector made his offerings on the god's own heights. πολύπτυχος, “many-folded”, is said of Olympus and of Ida; Poseidon gave it to Ida when he recalled Apollo herding Laomedon's cattle on its slopes (21.449; see [[many-folded]] and the note on Book 21).

Zeus' memory of the offerings is the ground of his pity, and the gods will hear it again: in the last book Apollo reproaches them that Hector burned thighs of oxen and goats for them, and that now they will not save even his body (24.33–35; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
