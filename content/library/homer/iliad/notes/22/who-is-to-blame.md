---
summary: Hector blames himself before he blames the gods. Waiting outside the gates, he admits that he did not listen to Polydamas, “since I have destroyed the army by my own recklessness” (22.103–104); deceived, he sees that “the gods have called me to my death” and that “Athena has deceived me” (22.297–299). Priam lays his sorrows on Achilles and on Peleus, “who begot him and reared him to be a bane / to the Trojans” (22.421–422), and Andromache on a common fate, “We were both born, it seems, to one fate” (22.477).

The book gives both answers to the same death. Hector names his ἀτασθαλίαι, the word the Odyssey's Zeus uses for men who suffer beyond their share by their own folly (*Odyssey* 1.32–34; see [[atasthalie]] and [[polydamas]]), and also names the first cause of the war, the goods that Alexander brought to Troy, “the beginning of the quarrel” (22.116; see [[neikos]]). The narrator gives the killing to the goddess and the man together, “gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles” (22.446), and the defilement to Zeus, who “gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.403–404; see [[divine-intervention]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

Andromache's blame turns to her own birth: “would that he had never begotten me!” (22.481), she says of her father Eetion (see [[eetion]] and [[moira]]).
