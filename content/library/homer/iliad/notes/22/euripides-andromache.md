summary: Euripides' Andromache remembers this book. She saw Hector dragged by the chariot, as the Iliad's Andromache sees him from the wall, though in Euripides the son of Thetis drags him around the walls; she left him lying in the dust; and she remembers coming from Thebe to Priam's house with golden bride-gifts.

**The dragging.** In the Iliad Andromache, warned by Hecuba's wailing, runs to the wall and sees Hector “being dragged in front of the city; and the swift horses / were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans” (22.463–465; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Euripides' Andromache counts the sight among her sorrows: she “saw” her husband dead at the hands of Achilles (*Andromache* 8–9), and later, “I who saw the slaughter of Hector, dragged by the wheels (σφαγὰς … Ἕκτορος τροχηλάτους), and Ilium pitifully burning, went aboard the Argive ships a slave, dragged by the hair” (399–402). In Homer the city burns only in a simile, on the same day, when Hector's parents and the people wail “as if all / beetling Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom” (22.410–411). Her elegiac lament gives the dragging Euripides' own course: the son of sea-born Thetis dragged him “around the walls”, driving his chariot (τὸν περὶ τείχη / εἵλκυσε διφρεύων, 107–108), and she wept as she left “the city and the chambers and my husband in the dust” (πόσιν ἐν κονίαις, 111–112). The dust is Homer's: Hector's head “lay in the dust” as he was dragged (ἐν κονίῃσι, 22.402). The walls are not; in Homer the three circuits of the walls belong to the chase (22.165).

**The bride-gifts.** The play begins with her wedding: “Glory of the land of Asia, city of Thebe, from which I came once, with the rich golden splendor of my bride-gifts (ἕδνων σὺν πολυχρύσῳ χλιδῇ), to the royal hearth of Priam, given to Hector as his wife to bear his children” (1–4). In the Iliad the ἕδνα are what Hector gave for her, “a countless bride-price” (μυρία ἕδνα, 22.472), when he took her from the house of Eetion in Thebe under Placus (22.470–472, 22.479–480; see [[bride-price]] and [[thebe]]). In Euripides the word goes with the bride.

**Paris.** Menelaus, arguing with Peleus over the captive, reminds him that Paris, “who killed your son Achilles”, was Hector's brother, and this woman Hector's wife (*Andromache* 655–656). Hector foretells the death in this book, at the hands of Paris and Phoebus Apollo at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
