---
summary: Fate holds Hector from the book's first lines: “deadly fate fettered Hector to stay where he was” (22.5). Priam fears that he will “soon meet your fate” (22.39); Hector tells Achilles that he did not “know my fate from Zeus” (22.280), and when he sees the trick he says, “now my fate has overtaken me” (22.303). His soul goes to Hades “lamenting its fate” (22.363), and Hecuba ends her lament, “now death and fate have overtaken you” (22.436).
grc: [μόρσιμός, δύσμορον, δύσμορος, αἰνόμορον]
en: [of dread fate]
---

Apollo, a god and safe from it, puts it in a word: “I am not fated to fall to you” (οὔ τοι μόρσιμός εἰμι, 22.13). Walter Leaf notes that μόρσιμος is said of a person only here and in a verse of the Odyssey; elsewhere there is only the fated day and “it was fated” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.13; *Odyssey* 16.392 = 21.162; see the translation's note on 22.13 and [[gods-and-mortals]]). Hecuba's line is the one said twice of Patroclus in Book 17, now of her son (22.436 = 17.478, 17.672); Hector had said it of himself, νῦν αὖτέ με μοῖρα κιχάνει (22.303; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]).

The family of μόρος names the mourners too. Priam calls himself δύσμορος, “ill-fated” (22.60), and Andromache her father and herself, “he ill-fated, and I of dread fate” (δύσμορος αἰνόμορον, 22.481), the two compounds that are found in the Iliad only in this book (see [[aisa]], [[most-ill-fated]] and [[ker-doom]]).
