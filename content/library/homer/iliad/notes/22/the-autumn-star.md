The star that Book 5 named only by its season (ἀστέρʼ ὀπωρινῷ, 5.5) is given its whole description here. παμφαίνονθʼ ὥς τʼ ἀστέρʼ ἐπεσσύμενον πεδίοιο, / ὅς ῥά τʼ ὀπώρης εἶσιν, “gleaming like the star, as he sped over the plain, / the star that comes in late summer” (22.26–27). παμφαίνω, “shine all over”, was the verb of the star in Book 5 too (λαμπρὸν παμφαίνῃσι, 5.6). Its rays shine out among the many stars νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ, “in the dead of night” (22.28), and men call it κύνʼ Ὠρίωνος, “the dog of Orion” (22.29), the dog of the hunter whose stars Hephaestus set on the Shield (18.486; see [[orion]]). Walter Leaf notes that the name of the dog has since passed to the whole constellation of which Sirius is the brightest star, which follows close upon Orion. ἐπίκλησιν καλέουσι, “call by the name of”, is said of the Bear, “which they also call by the name of the Wagon” (18.487); Leaf takes it there as “as a second name”, and thinks it may be so here too, if the description is enough to suggest the star's first name, Sirius (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.29).

λαμπρότατος μὲν ὅ γʼ ἐστί, κακὸν δέ τε σῆμα τέτυκται, / καί τε φέρει πολλὸν πυρετὸν δειλοῖσι βροτοῖσιν: “It is the brightest, but it is made a sign of evil, / and brings much fever to wretched mortals” (22.30–31). πυρετός, “fever”, occurs only here in Homer, and Leaf reports the scholia, which note that the word is used in its proper sense, of fever, and not, as some took it, of the burning of the air; he compares Virgil's Sirius, “bringing thirst and diseases to sick mortals” (on 22.31; *Aeneid* 10.274; see the article). He also points out a difficulty. The star's season is late summer, when it rises at dawn, the time of fever; but Sirius is seen at night only in winter and spring. Either the poet joined its brightness and its deadliness by a licence that is poetically legitimate though astronomically impossible, or νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ does not mean what it is taken to mean, and is the twilight of morning or evening, as the same difficulty with the evening star suggests (on 22.27; 22.317; see [[the-evening-star]] and the translation's note on 22.26–31).

The tenor comes back from the star to the man: ὣς τοῦ χαλκὸς ἔλαμπε περὶ στήθεσσι θέοντος, “so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran” (22.32). The verse is the one that closes the simile of Idomeneus going into battle like the lightning of Zeus, and the lightning there also shows “a sign to mortals” and has flashes ἀρίζηλοι, “plain for all to see”, in the half-verse that describes the star's rays here (13.242–245; 22.27; see [[like-lightning]]). The bronze is the armor Hephaestus made for Achilles, and its light, seen from the wall, is a sign of the evil that comes on the city. The old man groans aloud and beats his head with his hands, and begs his son to come inside (22.33–76; see [[priam]]).

The star is a dog, and the speech it provokes is full of dogs. Priam wishes that the dogs and vultures would eat Achilles, and foresees his own table dogs tearing at him at his doors (22.41–76; see [[dogs]]). Here Achilles shines like the hunter's dog in the sky; in the chase that follows he is the dog that tracks the fawn (22.189–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]).
