summary: Priam counts his sons before Hector's death and after it. Achilles “has made me bereft of many brave sons”, and Lycaon and Polydorus are missing (22.44–53); his picture of the sack begins with “my sons perishing” (22.62); and when Hector is dead, “so many sons of mine he has killed in their bloom”, but he mourns one above all (22.423–426). Andromache, hearing the wailing, fears that “some evil is near for the children of Priam” (22.453).

**Bereft.** Priam's first charge against Achilles, as he begs Hector to come inside, is the loss of his sons: “he who has made me bereft of many brave sons, / killing them, and selling them off to islands far away” (22.44–45; see [[bereaved-parents]]). Achilles told Lycaon at the river that he had once taken many Trojans alive and sold them, and that now no one would escape him, “and above all of the sons of Priam” (21.101–105; see [[achilles-raids]]). Two of them, Lycaon and Polydorus, Priam cannot see among the Trojans in the city, and he does not know that both are dead (22.46–53; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[polydorus-son-of-priam]] and [[laothoe]]). His picture of the sack begins with “my sons perishing and my daughters dragged off” (22.62; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Hector names his brothers once, to Athena in the likeness of Deiphobus: “truly before now you were by far the dearest to me / of my brothers, the sons whom Hecuba and Priam bore” (22.233–234; see [[deiphobus]] and [[hecuba]]).

**One above all.** When Hector is dead Priam counts again:

{{quote:5e1c7c43-a1f9-5b7f-b35a-691d67419960}}

τηλεθάοντας, “in their bloom” (22.423), is found once more in the Iliad, of the olive sapling that a man rears in a lonely place, “a fine, thriving tree”, until a sudden storm wind wrenches it out of its trench (17.53–58; see [[like-a-sapling]]). In the last book Priam tells Achilles that he begot the best sons in Troy and that not one of them is left: he had fifty when the Achaeans came, nineteen from one womb, and Ares has loosened the knees of most of them, and the one who guarded the city, Hector, Achilles has killed (24.493–501; see [[priam]] and [[hector]]). Andromache hears the cries from the tower and fears “some evil … for the children of Priam” (22.453; see [[andromache]]).
