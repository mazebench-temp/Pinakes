summary: No one prays to a god in the book. The prayers it names were made to a man: Achilles boasts of killing Hector, “to whom the Trojans throughout the city prayed as to a god” (22.394), and Hecuba remembers the Trojans “who greeted you / as a god” (22.434–435). Zeus, for his part, remembers Hector's offerings, “many thighs of oxen” burned to him on Ida and on the height of the city (22.170–172).
# The other words of the family in the book are not prayers (euchomai keeps them): Hector's "triumph" (22.130) and
# Hecuba's "my boast" (22.433). Priam "stretching out his hands" (22.37) begs his son, not a god.

εὐχετάομαι with θεῷ ὣς, “pray to as to a god”, is in the Odyssey a promise of thanks to a mortal: Odysseus makes it to Nausicaa, who saved his life, and Telemachus to Helen, who has foretold his father's return, “then I would pray to you there too as to a god” (*Odyssey* 8.464–468, 15.180–181; see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[euchomai]]). Priam's gesture, χεῖρας ὀρεγνύς, is the gesture of Achilles praying to his mother and of Nestor praying to the starry sky (1.351, 15.371), but he stretches out his hands to his son (22.37; see [[supplication]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

Hector calls on the gods as witnesses of an agreement, not as helpers (22.254–255; see [[oath]]), and when he sees that Athena has deceived him he knows that the gods who “before now / protected me with ready hearts” have let him go (22.301–303). Zeus' memory of his sacrifices does not save him (22.168–185; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[sacrifice]]).
