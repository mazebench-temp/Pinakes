---
summary: Priam, begging Hector to come inside, names him once more, “the old man of famous name, Altes”, who “granted much to his daughter”: with that wealth Priam would ransom Lycaon and Polydorus, if they were still alive (22.49–51).
---

**His gifts.** γέρων ὀνομάκλυτος Ἄλτης, “the old man of famous name, Altes” (22.51): ὀνομάκλυτος joins ὄνομα, “name”, and κλυτός, “famous”, and is found only here in Homer (see [[famous]]). Lycaon called him “the old man” too, γέροντος, and the poet names him only through his grandsons (21.85; see [[geron]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). πολλὰ γὰρ ὤπασε παιδί, “for … granted much to his daughter”: ὀπάζω is to give something to go with a man, as Zeus “grants glory” to Hector and Peleus gave Phoenix many people to rule (8.141, 9.483; see the translation's table of key words); here it is what a father sends with his daughter when she marries. With that wealth, kept in Priam's house, Priam would ransom her sons, if they were alive among the Achaeans (22.46–51; see [[laothoe]], [[apoina]] and [[priam]]).

**A dowry.** The poem's usual marriage runs the other way: the suitor gives gifts for his bride, as Hector gave “a countless bride-price” for Andromache (22.472; see [[bride-price]] and [[marriage]]). Walter Leaf takes the gifts, if the line is genuine, as a sign that Laothoe held no inferior place, nor one that her father disapproved of; but he thinks van Leeuwen probably right to omit the line as a late addition, since the practice of giving gifts to a daughter at her marriage dates only from the end of the Homeric period, and Priam could hardly be expected to proclaim that he looks to his wives' dowries to ransom his sons (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.51). Elsewhere in the Iliad a father gives with his daughter when Agamemnon offers Achilles a dowry “very great, such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter” (9.147–148), and when the king of Lycia gives Bellerophon “half of all his royal honor” with his (6.192–193; see [[bellerophon]]).
