---
summary: Zeus' grief for Hector is the grief of a god who has been well served: “for Hector, who has burned many thighs of oxen for me / on the peaks of many-folded Ida, and at other times / on the height of the city” (22.170–172). He asks the gods whether to save him, Athena objects, and he lets her go (22.174–187). The service is remembered, and it does not buy the man's life.

Zeus' reason is the claim a worshiper makes in prayer, the burned thighs of Chryses' first prayer (1.39–41), spoken here by the god himself; in Book 4 he said the same of the whole city, whose altar never lacked “libation and the savor of the burnt fat” (4.48–49; see [[sacrifice]] and [[prayer]]). The answer comes from fate, not from exchange: Hector is “A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion” (22.179; see [[aisa]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). Hector, deceived, says the same from below: Zeus and Apollo “before now / protected me with ready hearts; but now my fate has overtaken me” (22.302–303; see [[moira]]).

The account is reopened in the last book, where Apollo asks the gods whether Hector never burned them thighs of oxen and goats, since they will not save even his corpse, and Zeus grants that Hector was dearest to the gods of all the men in Ilios, since he never failed in his offerings (24.33–35, 24.66–70; see [[book-1-and-book-24]]).
