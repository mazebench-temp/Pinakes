---
summary: Aristotle's example of Hector's shame comes from the opening of his monologue outside the gates: if he goes inside, Polydamas, whose advice he refused, will be the first to heap disgrace on him.
refs: [22.100]
---

{{quote:3d54a03e-2f17-51e0-9603-4e13d4121ee4}}

Aristotle quotes a single verse, “Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me” (Πουλυδάμας μοι πρῶτος ἐλεγχείην ἀναθήσει, 22.100; *Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, 1116a17–29). The speech around it shows the shame he means. Polydamas had told Hector to lead the Trojans back to the city on the night when Achilles rose, and Hector would not listen (22.101–103; 18.254–283; see [[polydamas]]); now, having destroyed his army by his own recklessness, he feels shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women, lest some baser man say, “Hector trusted in his own strength and destroyed the army” (22.104–107; see [[aidos]]). It would be far better, he decides, “either to face Achilles, kill him, and come home, / or to perish gloriously at his hands before the city” (22.108–110). The shame that Aristotle counts as the courage of citizens keeps Hector outside the wall. Cicero quoted the same verse to Atticus when he feared what the leading men of Rome would say of him (see [[cicero-trojans]] and [[hector]]).
