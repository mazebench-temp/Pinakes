summary: Pity is asked for all through the book, and it saves no one. Priam begs, “And pity me too, unhappy as I am” (22.59), and Hecuba, “respect this, and pity me” (22.82); Hector fears that if he goes to Achilles unarmed “he will not pity me” (22.123); and Priam, after the death, hopes that Achilles “may respect my years and pity / my old age” (22.419–420). Zeus' heart “grieves” for Hector (22.169), and in Andromache's picture of the orphan, of “those who pity him” one holds out a cup for a moment (22.494).

{{quote:5e0ed54e-9475-58e3-b277-cfb6e5a24910}}

Priam names the most pitiable sight himself. A young man dead in battle is fair to look at, but when dogs disfigure “the gray head and the gray beard” of an old man, “this is the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals” (οἴκτιστον, 22.74–76). When Achilles at last pities Priam, the poet uses the same words, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (πολιόν τε κάρη πολιόν τε γένειον, 24.516), found only in these two places (see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). ἐλεεινά, “pitifully”, marks Priam's voice twice, when he begs his son and when he groans over his body (22.37, 22.408), as it marked the sparrow's chicks devoured by the serpent at Aulis (2.314).

The gods' pity changes nothing here. Zeus grieves and asks whether to save Hector, and lets Athena go (22.168–187; see [[zeus]] and [[watching-the-war]]). Achilles answers the plea for pity with the refusal “Do not implore me, you dog, by my knees or my parents” (22.345; see [[supplication]] and [[aidos]]).
