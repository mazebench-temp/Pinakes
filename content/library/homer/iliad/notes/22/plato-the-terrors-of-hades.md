The scene repeats the death of Patroclus line for line at its close. Patroclus, dying, tells Hector that he will not live long, but will be brought down by Achilles (16.851–854); Hector, dying, tells Achilles to beware of the day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy him at the Scaean gates (22.358–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Then the same three verses follow: “As he spoke, the end of death covered him, / and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (22.361–363 = 16.855–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]] and [[the-end-of-death]]). And each killer answers the dead man in the same half-verse, “And to him, though he was dead, … spoke” (τὸν καὶ τεθνηῶτα προσηύδα, 22.364 = 16.858). Hector asks why Patroclus prophesies his destruction, since Achilles might die first by his spear (16.859–861); Achilles accepts his doom “whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass” (22.365–366).

Plato quotes the two verses without saying whose soul they describe, and his text writes the end of the first as one word, Ἄϊδόσδε, “to Hades”, where ours prints Ἄϊδος δέ (*Republic* 3.386d). Proclus, explaining the verse, gives it to Patroclus: souls that have lived the life of action are vexed at being parted from the body that served their actions, “like the soul of Patroclus, leaving manhood and youth” (*On the Republic* I 119–120 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). He does not say that the verse is spoken of Hector too (see [[hector]] and [[psyche]]).
