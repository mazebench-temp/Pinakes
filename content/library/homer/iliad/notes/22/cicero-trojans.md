summary: Both of Cicero's Greek tags come from Hector's monologue in this book: “Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me” and “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes”. Here the shame is for a decision already taken against advice, which is what Cicero feared to be reproached with.

The second line is also Hector's answer to Andromache in Book 6 (6.442), but Cicero joins it to the first, and the two stand together only here, in the monologue that Hector speaks alone before the Scaean gates as Achilles comes on (22.99–107; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[hector]]). Writing to Atticus in 59 BCE, he gives them in the reverse order: αἰδέομαι Τρῶας καὶ Τρῳάδας ἑλκεσιπέπλους, and then, after asking what the optimates would say, Πουλυδάμας μοι πρῶτος ἐλεγχείην ἀναθήσει, with Cato for Polydamas (*Letters to Atticus* 2.5.1). In 50 BCE the two come together again: he feels shame “not only before Pompey but before the Trojans and the Trojan women”, and Polydamas follows (*Letters to Atticus* 7.1.4).

In Book 6 Hector's shame is the fear of seeming a coward if he keeps away from the fighting (6.441–443). In this book it is the shame of a man who has made a choice and been proved wrong: Polydamas told him to lead the Trojans back to the city, he did not listen, and he has destroyed the army by his own recklessness (22.100–104; see [[polydamas]] and [[aidos]]). Cicero's letters use the lines in the same way, at moments of decision, when he feared what men of his own party would say of a choice that could be taken for a betrayal.
