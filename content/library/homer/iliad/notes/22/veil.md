**Hecuba's head covering.** When Hecuba sees her son dragged before the city, she “tore her hair, and flung her shining head covering / far from her” (22.406–407; see [[hecuba]] and [[hair]]). καλύπτρη, from καλύπτω, “cover”, is kept apart in the translation as “head covering” (see the translation's note on 22.468–470); in the Odyssey it is the head covering that Calypso and Circe put on when they dress (*Odyssey* 5.232, 10.545). To throw it off, as to tear the hair, is the act of a mourner (see [[lament]]).

**Andromache's headdress.**

{{quote:00e4c77d-a850-51f1-a99b-a5cdf6992652}}

δέσματα σιγαλόεντα, “the glossy bindings”, names the whole, and the next verses name its parts (see [[glossy]]): ἄμπυξ, “the headband”, the word that gives the horses of the gods their golden headbands (χρυσάμπυκας, 5.358); κεκρύφαλος, “the cap”, a cap or net for the hair; πλεκτὴ ἀναδέσμη, “the plaited band” that binds the hair up; and last the κρήδεμνον, “the veil” (see the translation's note on 22.468–470). ἄμπυξ, κεκρύφαλος and ἀναδέσμη are found only here in Homer. The two women's gestures are told alike: Hecuba ἀπὸ … ἔρριψε … τηλόσε, “flung … far from her”, and Andromache τῆλε δʼ ἀπὸ κρατὸς βάλε, “far from her head she flung” (22.406–407, 22.468). In the Odyssey Nausicaa and her maids throw off their veils with the same verb, ἀπὸ κρήδεμνα βαλοῦσαι, to play at ball (*Odyssey* 6.99–100).

**A wedding gift.** The veil was the gift of golden Aphrodite on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet led Andromache from the house of Eetion, after giving a countless bride-price (22.470–472; see [[aphrodite]], [[bride-price]] and [[marriage]]). The bride's veil is thrown off by the widow. Hera put on her κρήδεμνον, white like the sun, to go to Zeus (14.184–185), and Achilles wished that he and Patroclus alone might “loose the sacred veil of Troy”, its crown of walls (16.100; see the translation's note on 16.97–100). Here, with the city's defender dead, his wife's veil falls from her head (see [[andromache]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).
