summary: One death fills the book. Hector's is told as Sarpedon's and Patroclus' were: decided beforehand by Zeus, ended by a dying speech and by “the end of death covered him” (22.361; 16.502, 16.855), and followed, as Patroclus' was, by the soul's flight, “and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades” (22.362 = 16.856). The wound is exact, “at the throat, where the destruction of life is swiftest”, with the windpipe left uncut “so that he might still say something to him in answer” (22.324–329).

**The pattern.** Zeus weighs Hector's fate in the scales and Apollo leaves him (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]), as Zeus decided the deaths of Sarpedon and Patroclus (16.431–461, 16.644–655). Like Patroclus, Hector dies foretelling the death of the man who kills him (22.355–360; 16.851–854; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]), and the verses of the soul's going are Patroclus' verses (22.361–363 = 16.855–857; see [[the-end-of-death]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]). Here the pattern stops. No one fights over the body: Achilles strips it, the Achaeans stab it, and he drags it to the ships (22.367–404; see [[the-death-of-hector]], [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**The body.** Hector wears the armor he took from Patroclus, and only the throat is bare (22.322–325; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The poet keeps looking at the body after death: the Achaeans gaze at its “stature and admirable looks” before they stab it, and one says that it is “much softer to handle now” than when Hector burned the ships (22.370–374); its head lies in the dust, “that once was handsome” (22.402–403); and Andromache pictures it naked, eaten by worms when the dogs are done (22.509–510).

**The young and the old.** Priam has already said what such a death is worth. For a young man “it is all fitting, / killed in battle, torn by the sharp bronze, / to lie there; all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is”; the most pitiable sight is an old man's body disfigured by dogs (22.71–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[pity]]). Hector, when he knows that the gods have called him, asks only not to die “without a struggle and without glory” (22.304–305; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[kleos]]). Priam counts his own dead, “so many sons of mine he has killed in their bloom” (22.423; see [[bereaved-parents]]).
