---
summary: Apollo begins the book by reproaching Achilles: “Truly you care nothing now for your toil against the Trojans, whom you put to flight” (22.11). Andromache ends it foreseeing her son's life without a father: “always there will be toil and sorrows for him / hereafter” (22.488–489).
---

Walter Leaf takes Τρώων πόνος as an objective genitive, the labor of slaughtering the Trojans, and notes that it cannot mean the Trojans' distress, and that the manuscripts do not give here the usual variant φόνος (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.11; see the translation's note on 22.11 and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). In Andromache's lament πόνος and κήδεα are the orphan's portion, his lands taken by others (22.488–489; see [[chere]] and [[childhood]]).
