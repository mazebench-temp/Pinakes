---
summary: The book is the end of talk between Hector and Achilles. Hector, waiting, sees that there is no way now “to talk intimately with him, as a maiden and a young man” and chooses the fight (22.126–130); Achilles refuses his terms, “do not talk to me of agreements” (22.261); Hector calls him “a glib talker and a cheat with words” (22.281); and to the dying man's plea Achilles answers, “Do not implore me, you dog”, with the wish to “carve your flesh and eat it raw” (22.345–347).

ἀπὸ δρυὸς οὐδʼ ἀπὸ πέτρης, “from an oak or from a rock”, is a proverbial phrase, found again in the Odyssey when Penelope asks the stranger his lineage, “for you are not from an oak of old story, or from a rock” (*Odyssey* 19.163); the talk Hector gives up is the easy talk of young lovers, ὀαρίζειν (22.126–128; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[eris]]). Before the duel Hector offers the words that could bind it, an agreement before the gods, and Achilles refuses them with the image of lions and men (22.254–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]] and [[oath]]). After it Achilles' words exceed what his hands will do: he wishes he could eat Hector raw, as Zeus told Hera that only if she could “eat Priam raw, and the sons of Priam” might she heal her anger (22.346–347; 4.34–36; see the translation's note on 22.346–347 and [[cholos]]).

When Hector is dead, words and blows go together. Each Achaean says, “much softer to handle now / is Hector than when he burned the ships with blazing fire”, and stabs him (22.372–375; see [[tis-speech]]), and in Andromache's picture the orphan is driven from the feast with blows and abuse (22.496–498; see [[oneidos]] and [[childhood]]).
