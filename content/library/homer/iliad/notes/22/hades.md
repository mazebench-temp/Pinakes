---
summary: The house of Hades waits through the book. Priam fears that his sons by Laothoe are already there; Hector's fated day goes off to it when it sinks in the scales of Zeus, and his soul goes there when he dies. Achilles will remember Patroclus even in the house of Hades, Priam's grief for Hector will bring him down to it, and Andromache laments that Hector is going to the house of Hades beneath the depths of the earth.
---

**The house of Hades.** The god does not appear; his house is named six times, by the narrator and by the living. Priam does not know whether Lycaon and Polydorus are alive in the camp or “already dead and in the house of Hades” (22.52; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]), in the words with which the cowherd Philoetius, in the *Odyssey*, wonders whether his master is dead (*Odyssey* 20.208).

**Hector's day and Hector's soul.** When Zeus weighs the fates, Hector's day goes there before him: “the fated day of Hector sank, / and went off to the house of Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him” (22.212–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). When he dies, “his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (22.362–363; see [[psyche]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]), the two verses of Patroclus' death (16.856–857). Ἄϊδος δέ, “to Hades”, is the suffix of direction printed apart, Ἄϊδόσδε (see the translation's note on 22.309, 22.362, 22.456).

**The living.** Achilles, who will not forget Patroclus “as long as I / am among the living”, goes further: “and even if in the house of Hades men forget the dead, / yet even there I will remember my dear companion” (22.387–390; see [[patroclus]], [[hetairos]] and the translation's note on 22.389). Priam mourns all his dead sons less than one, “for whom sharp grief will bring me down to the house of Hades” (22.425; see [[priam]] and [[achos]]). Andromache: “But now you go to the house of Hades beneath the depths of the earth” (22.482; see [[andromache]]). ὑπὸ κεύθεσι γαίης, “beneath the depths of the earth”, is said again in Homer only in the *Odyssey*, of the souls of the dead who stand talking in his house (εἰν Ἀΐδαο δόμοις, *Odyssey* 24.203–204).
