---
summary: Priam, on the wall, cannot see Lycaon and Polydorus among the Trojans who have crowded into the city, and will ransom them with bronze and gold if they are alive in the Achaean camp (22.46–53). He does not know that Achilles has killed Lycaon at the river that same day.
# Λυκάονα (22.46), the accusative, occurs only here in Homer; Λυκάονος (21.127, 23.746) is claimed in the entry.
grc: [Λυκάονα]
---

{{quote:8bf09c64-4778-5239-99c0-5350f6862e5e}}

**Sold and ransomed.** Priam names the two sons just after his charge against Achilles, “he who has made me bereft of many brave sons, / killing them, and selling them off to islands far away” (22.44–45; see [[sons-of-priam]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). Lycaon was one of those sold. Achilles took him by night in his father's orchard and sold him into Lemnos, and a guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, “ransomed him, and paid much” (21.35–44; see [[eetion-of-imbros]] and [[lemnos]]). The half-verse νήσων ἔπι τηλεδαπάων, “to islands far away”, is the one in which Poseidon recalled Laomedon's threat to bind him and Apollo and sell them (21.453–454; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).

**The ransom.** If the two are “alive in the camp”, Priam will ransom them (ἀπολυσόμεθʼ, 22.50; see [[apoina]]), with the wealth that their grandfather Altes gave with his daughter (22.51; see [[altes]] and [[laothoe]]). Lycaon has come home by ransom once already, as he told Achilles at the river, “paying three times as much” (21.80). But Achilles' answer to him was that the time of ransom was over: “before Patroclus met his fated day, / until then it was somehow dearer to my heart to spare / Trojans, and many I took alive and sold; / but now there is no one who shall escape death” (21.100–103; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). Priam's hope belongs to the war before Patroclus died.

**Not known.** The Trojans who fled into the city did not wait outside the wall to learn “who had escaped / and who had died in the war” (21.609–610), and Priam looks for his sons among them. Achilles killed Polydorus among the champions earlier in the day (20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]) and Lycaon at the river, where he threw the body into the stream for the fishes (21.116–127; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]), and their mother will not lay him on a bier (21.123–124). Priam weighs their loss against Hector's: if they are dead, the sorrow is his and their mother's, and the people's will be shorter-lived, “unless you die too, brought down by Achilles” (22.52–55; see [[priam]] and [[hector]]).
