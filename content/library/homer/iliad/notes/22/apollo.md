summary: Apollo, still in Agenor's likeness, turns and reveals himself to Achilles, who cannot kill a god; Achilles calls him the most baneful of the gods and would take vengeance if he could. In the pursuit he comes to Hector for the very last time and rouses his knees, and when Hector's day sinks in the scales of Zeus he leaves him. Hector, deceived, names him with Zeus as the gods who once protected him, and dying foretells that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates.

**The god revealed.** Apollo has drawn Achilles away from the city in the likeness of Agenor (21.599–605; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]), and he still has that likeness when he turns to speak (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.7; see the translation's note on 22.7):

{{quote:4dd8e9c7-20ee-5fa6-ae8f-3ed7059dd26c}}

He has warned men off before: Diomedes, who rushed three times at Aeneas under his protection, “Take thought, son of Tydeus, and give way” (5.440–442), and Patroclus at the wall of Troy, “Draw back, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus!” (16.707–709; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). This time there is nothing to warn against. The god has already done what he came to do, and he tells Achilles what it was: the Trojans whom he put to flight “have crowded into the city, while you have turned aside here” (22.12). The end of 22.10, ἀσπερχὲς μενεαίνεις, “you rage so relentlessly”, is what Zeus said to Hera of her hatred of Troy (4.32; Leaf on 22.10). Leaf takes Τρώων πόνος as “your toil against the Trojans”, the work of killing them (on 22.11), and μόρσιμος, said here of a person, as “predestined” (on 22.13; see the translation's notes on 22.11 and 22.13, and [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**Achilles' answer.** “You have foiled me, Far-Worker, most baneful of all the gods” (22.15; see [[far-worker]] and [[deeply-troubled]]). ἔβλαψας, “you have foiled me”, is used in this sense in Homer only of what gods do to men (9.507, 15.724), apart from wine in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 21.294; Leaf on 22.15; see the translation's note on 22.15). ὀλοώτατε, “most baneful”, says in the superlative what Menelaus said to Zeus in the comparative, “no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365; Leaf on 22.15). The god has taken great glory from him, Achilles says, and saved the Trojans “easily, since you have no fear at all of vengeance hereafter” (22.19; see [[kydos]]); Leaf compares Thetis' words to Zeus, “since you have nothing to fear” (1.515; on 22.19). He ends: “Truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength” (22.20), with the half-verse of Telemachus' complaint against the suitors (*Odyssey* 2.62; Leaf on 22.20). Plato's Socrates hesitates, for Homer's sake, to call it impious to believe that Achilles spoke so to Apollo, and quotes both verses (*Republic* 3.391a; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]).

**The last time.** Three times around the city Hector keeps ahead of Achilles. “And how could Hector have escaped the fates of death, / had not Apollo come to him for the very last time, / close by, who roused his might and his nimble knees?” (22.202–204; see [[ker-doom]] and [[nimble]]). πύματόν τε καὶ ὕστατον, “for the very last time”, comes again in Homer only in the *Odyssey*, where a slave woman at the mill prays that the suitors may feast in Odysseus' halls that day for the very last time (*Odyssey* 20.116). Apollo has stood by Hector since Zeus sent him down in Book 15, “Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who before now too / have protected you” (15.256–257); in Book 20 he warned Hector not to step out against Achilles and snatched him away from him in thick mist (20.375–378, 20.443–444; see [[divine-rescue]]). Now he gives strength but no rescue, and when Zeus' scales have sunk, “and Phoebus Apollo left him” (22.213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[phoebus]]).

Athena tells Achilles that Hector cannot get clear of them “not even if far-working Apollo should go to great lengths, / groveling before aegis-bearing father Zeus” (22.220–221; see [[athena]], [[father-zeus]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). προπροκυλινδόμενος, “groveling”, is found elsewhere only in the *Odyssey*, of a stranger who has come from Crete rolling on from place to place (*Odyssey* 17.522–525); the simple verb is Priam's, “rolling in the dung” as he begs his people to let him go to Achilles (κυλινδόμενος, 22.414).

**Hector's gods.** When Hector knows that Athena has deceived him, he names the two gods who had stood by him: “for truly this long ago was dearer / to Zeus and to the far-shooting son of Zeus, who before now / protected me with ready hearts; but now my fate has overtaken me” (22.301–303; see [[far-shooter]], [[zeus]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). εἰρύατο, “protected”, answers Apollo's own words in Book 15, ὅς σε πάρος περ / ῥύομʼ, “who before now too / have protected you” (15.256–257).

**The prophecy.** Dying, Hector warns Achilles of the day “when Paris and Phoebus Apollo / destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates” (22.359–360; see [[the-death-of-hector]], [[death-of-achilles-foretold]], [[paris]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Thetis had named Apollo's arrows (21.277–278), and the horse Xanthus “a god and a man” (19.417); Hector names the man and the place (see the translation's note on 22.359–360 and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Patroclus, dying, had named the son of Leto among his own killers and foretold Hector's death at the hands of Achilles (16.849–854; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
