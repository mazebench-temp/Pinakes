summary: Apollo, who has drawn him away from the city, mocks him, and Achilles turns back to the walls. He chases Hector three times around Troy and will not let his men throw at him; at Athena's word he stands, refuses Hector's offer of an agreement, and kills him with a spear through the throat, where the armor Hector took from Patroclus, Achilles' own, leaves him bare. He refuses all ransom for the body, answers the dying man's prophecy of his own death, strips him, bids the young Achaeans sing the paean, and drags him behind his chariot to the ships (22.7–404).

**Apollo's taunt.** At the end of the last book Apollo, in Agenor's likeness, drew Achilles away from the city along the Scamander, and now the god turns and asks why the son of Peleus, a mortal, chases an immortal god with his swift feet: “You will not kill me, since I am not fated to fall to you” (22.8–13; see [[apollo]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). Walter Leaf notes that μόρσιμος, “fated”, is used of a person only here and in one verse of the *Odyssey* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.13; *Odyssey* 16.392 = 21.162; see the translation's note on 22.13). The god who says that Achilles cannot kill him is the god by whose hand, the dying Hector will say, Achilles will fall (22.359–360).

{{quote:b097755c-28df-550d-82ec-110dc3d69e37}}

ἔβλαψας, “you have foiled me”, is used in this sense in Homer only of what gods do to men, apart from the wine of the *Odyssey* (Leaf on 22.15; see the translation's note on 22.15). Plato's Socrates will not have it believed that Achilles spoke so to a god, and quotes 22.15 and 22.20 (*Republic* 3.391a; see [[plato]]). The κῦδος, “glory”, that the god has taken from him runs through the book: Priam begs Hector “not hold out great glory / to the son of Peleus” (22.57–58); Achilles will not let his men throw at Hector, “lest someone win the glory by hitting him” (22.207); Athena promises that the two of them “will carry off great glory for the Achaeans” (22.217); and over the dead man Achilles says, “We have won great glory” (22.393; see [[kydos]]). “So saying, he strode toward the city in high spirits” (22.21), as Hector strode among the foremost on his own great day (11.296), and he runs like a prize-winning horse with its chariot (22.22–24; see [[like-a-racehorse]]).

**Seen from the wall.** Old Priam sees him first, “gleaming like the star, as he sped over the plain”, the star of late summer called the dog of Orion, “so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran” (22.25–32; see [[the-autumn-star]]). To his parents he is σχέτλιος, “the hard man!” (22.41, 22.86; see [[hard]]). Leaf takes the word as “hard of heart”, as Heracles is “Hard man, doer of violent deeds” (5.403; on 22.41), and when Hector is dead Priam calls Achilles a “doer of violent deeds” too (ὀβριμοεργόν, 22.418), the word's only other use in Homer. He wishes that the dogs and vultures would eat him, for he has made Priam bereft of many sons, killing them and selling them to islands far away (22.42–45; see [[priam]] and [[achilles-raids]]). Hector, waiting before the gates, thinks of going to him unarmed with offers of Helen and half the city's wealth, and knows the answer: “I fear that I may go up to him, and he will not pity me, / nor respect me at all, but will kill me naked” (22.111–125; see [[hector]], [[pity]] and [[aidos]]). Achilles shows him neither when he begs for his body (22.345–354); the hope of both is Priam's, who would go to the ships to beg Achilles “in the hope that he may respect my years and pity / my old age” (22.418–420).

**Like Enyalius.**

{{quote:b8cc1d24-1b92-5d8e-981b-2627d4da6eec}}

The narrator called Hector “the equal of Ares” at the height of his power (11.295, 13.802), and Patroclus came out of the hut “the equal of Ares; and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604); Achilles comes on now as the equal of Enyalius, the war god under his battle name, “the warrior of the shaking helmet” (22.132; see [[enyalius]], [[equal-of-ares]] and [[of-the-shaking-helmet]]). He brandishes the Pelian ash, the spear that Patroclus could not take (see [[the-pelian-ash]]), and the bronze of the god's armor shines around him like fire or the rising sun (see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). Hector trembles and runs, “and the son of Peleus sprang at him, trusting in his swift feet” (22.136–138; see [[swift-footed]]).

**The pursuit.** He flies after Hector as a falcon swoops on a dove (22.139–144; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]), past the lookout and the wild fig tree to the two springs of the Scamander (22.145–156; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]), “in front a brave man fled, but a far better one pursued him / swiftly”, not for a beast of sacrifice or an ox-hide, the prizes of a footrace, “but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.158–161). μέγʼ ἀμείνων, “far better”, is the narrator's word for Achilles, and Achilles will use it of himself over the dying Hector (22.333). Like racehorses rounding the turning post, the two whirl three times around the city of Priam while the gods watch (22.162–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Achilles keeps after him as a dog keeps after a fawn that hides under a bush (22.188–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]); as often as Hector makes for the Dardanian gates, hoping for help from the towers, he gets in front of him and turns him back toward the plain (22.194–198; see [[dardanian-gates]]); and the two cannot close, as in a dream (22.199–201; see [[as-in-a-dream]]).

**His men waved back.** “And brilliant Achilles shook his head at his men, / and would not let them hurl their bitter missiles at Hector, / lest someone win the glory by hitting him, and he himself come second” (22.205–207). The last words were said when Athena put might into Diomedes as he chased Dolon, “so that none of the bronze-armored Achaeans / might boast that he struck him first, and he himself come second” (10.367–368; see [[the-doloneia]]). Aristotle takes the scene for his example of what epic can carry and the stage cannot: the pursuit of Hector would seem ridiculous in the theater, “with the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back” (*Poetics* 1460a15–16; see [[epic-and-tragedy]]). His ἀνανεύων answers Homer's ἀνένευε, “shook his head” (22.205).

**Athena's word.** At the fourth time at the springs Zeus weighs “two fates of death that lays men low, / one of Achilles, and one of Hector”, and Hector's day sinks; Apollo leaves him (22.208–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Athena comes to Achilles: “Now at last I hope that we two, shining Achilles, dear to Zeus, / will carry off great glory for the Achaeans to the ships, / having cut down Hector”, and she tells him to stand and catch his breath while she persuades Hector to fight (22.214–223; see [[athena]]). “So spoke Athena, and he obeyed, and rejoiced in his heart, / and he stood, leaning on his ash spear with its point of bronze” (22.224–225). In the first book, too, Athena came to Achilles and he obeyed her, “Whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen” (1.218); there she held him back from killing Agamemnon, and here she holds him back so that Hector may be killed (see [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). The first of the two verses is said of Odysseus near the end of the *Odyssey*, when the goddess stops the fighting on Ithaca (*Odyssey* 24.545).

**No agreements.** Hector stands at last and offers terms, with the gods as witnesses: whoever wins will strip the other's armor and give back the body (22.250–259; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). “Looking at him darkly” (22.260; see [[looking-darkly]]), Achilles answers:

{{quote:0e2fcb34-b611-5dde-a73f-06719d150070}}

There will be no oaths before one of them has fallen and glutted Ares with blood (22.266–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]] and [[oath]]). Now Hector must be “a spearman and a bold warrior”: “there is no escape for you any longer, but at once Pallas Athena / will bring you down by my spear; and now you will pay back all at once / the sorrows of my companions, whom you killed raging with your spear” (22.268–272; see [[hetairos]]). Achilles knows that the goddess is with him before he throws; Hector learns it only when Deiphobus is not there (22.296–299; see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**The spears.** Achilles throws first. Hector sinks down, the spear flies over him and sticks in the ground, “but Pallas Athena snatched it up, / and gave it back to Achilles, unseen by Hector, shepherd of the people” (22.273–277; see [[divine-intervention]]). Hector, who does not know it, taunts him: “You missed!”; Achilles did not know his fate from Zeus after all, but was “a glib talker and a cheat with words” (22.279–281; see [[glib]]). Hector's own spear strikes the middle of Achilles' shield and glances far off, and he has no other (22.289–293; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]).

**The kill.** When Hector charges with his sword like an eagle (22.306–311; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]), “Achilles rushed at him, and filled his heart with savage / fury”, behind the shield “fine and intricately worked”, nodding with the four-ridged helmet whose plumes of gold Hephaestus set about the crest (22.312–316; see [[shield]] and [[helmet]]). The point of his spear shines like the evening star as he looks along Hector's body (see [[the-evening-star]]), “eyeing his fair skin, where it would best give way” (22.317–321). Hector wears the armor he stripped from Patroclus, Achilles' own, and it covers him everywhere but at the throat, where the collarbones hold the neck away from the shoulders; there Achilles drives his spear through the soft neck, but does not cut the windpipe, “so that he might still say something to him in answer” (22.322–329; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[the-death-of-hector]]).

**The boast.**

{{quote:df795f8d-d63b-526d-825c-7227d2a82ba0}}

The boast answers Hector's over the dying Patroclus, which began “Patroclus, I suppose you said that you would ravage our city”, called him a fool, and mocked him that “not even Achilles, brave as he is, has helped you” (16.830–837; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[nepios]]). Achilles names himself as the helper who was not there, ἀοσσητήρ, “a helper, a much better man” (22.333), the word for what Apollo was to Hector when Zeus sent him from Ida, “such a helper” (15.254), and Apollo has now left Hector (22.213). The burial rites he promises Patroclus are those he swore to withhold until he brought back Hector's armor and head (18.334–335; see [[patroclus]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**The ransom refused.** Hector, speaking feebly, begs by Achilles' life, his knees and his parents not to let the dogs devour him by the ships, but to take bronze and gold and give his body back, so that the Trojans may give him his portion of fire; the last two verses are those of his challenge in Book 7 (22.337–343; 22.342–343 = 7.79–80; see [[supplication]]). “Looking at him darkly”, Achilles answers:

{{quote:17ed5eb0-a116-5ee6-998b-a0c780b77af7}}

He wishes that his fury would let him eat Hector raw, as Zeus told Hera that she might heal her anger if she could “eat Priam raw, and the sons of Priam” (4.35), and as Hecuba will wish to eat Achilles' liver (24.212–213; see the translation's note on 22.346–347). The mother who will not lay her son on a bier and lament him he named over Lycaon too (22.353; 21.123–124; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). No ransom will save the body, not ten and twenty times the ransom, not Hector's weight in gold (22.349–352; see [[apoina]], [[dogs]] and [[birds]]). In the last book the gods' command brings him to accept a ransom and give the body back to Priam (24.137–140; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**The prophecy and the answer.** Hector, dying, knows him: “for truly the heart in your breast is iron”; let him take care lest Hector become “a cause of the gods' wrath against you, / on that day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo / destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates” (22.356–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Hecuba will say to Priam that his heart is iron when he resolves to go to Achilles' hut, and Achilles will say it to him in the hut (σιδήρειόν νύ τοι ἦτορ, 24.205, 24.521). To the dead man Achilles says, “Lie dead; and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (22.365–366). τέθναθι, “Lie dead”, is found only here; the rest is what he said when he chose to go back to the fighting, knowing that his own death would follow Hector's (22.365–366 ≈ 18.115–116; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and [[ker-doom]]).

**The body.** He draws the bronze spear out of the body, lays it aside, and strips the bloody armor from Hector's shoulders, while the other Achaeans run up, gaze at Hector's stature and looks, and stab him (22.367–375; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Hector too drew his spear from Patroclus' body when he had spoken over it (16.862–863).

**The paean.** “And when brilliant swift-footed Achilles had stripped him, / he stood among the Achaeans and spoke winged words” (22.376–377). He proposes that they make trial of the city in their armor, to learn whether the Trojans will leave it now that Hector has fallen (22.378–384), and then breaks off with the verse in which Odysseus, Menelaus, Agenor and Hector, each alone, break off their own debates: “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (22.385 = 11.407, 17.97, 21.562, 22.122; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). Achilles speaks it before the army. Patroclus lies by the ships “a corpse unwept, unburied”, and Achilles will not forget him even in the house of Hades (22.386–390; see [[patroclus]]).

{{quote:ef97d0c7-2f71-504c-ac6a-38b6d8b8f855}}

The Achaeans sang a paean to Apollo at Chryse to end the plague (1.472–474); this one is a song of victory, over the man whom Apollo has left (see [[paean]]). The Trojans “prayed as to a god” to Hector (22.394), and Hecuba will say the same of him in her lament, that the Trojans “greeted you / as a god; for truly you were their very great glory” (22.434–435; see [[euchomai]]).

**The dragging.** “He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector” (22.395). He pierces the tendons of Hector's feet, ties him to his chariot, lifts the glorious armor in and drives to the ships, with the head dragging in the dust (22.396–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). His mother, his father and his wife watch from the city (22.405–467). Priam would go out to beg him, “this reckless man, this doer of violent deeds”, for “he too has a father like me, / Peleus, who begot him and reared him to be a bane / to the Trojans” (22.418–422; see [[peleus]]). Hector called him the same, “for you are their greatest bane” (πῆμα, 22.288). Andromache, before she reaches the wall, is afraid “that brilliant Achilles has cut off my bold Hector / alone from the city” (22.455–456; see [[andromache]]), and the narrator has already told what she does not know, that “gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles” (22.446).
