summary: Hector asks to die “having done some great thing, even for men to come to hear of” (22.305), and Achilles bids the young Achaeans go back to the ships “singing the paean” (22.391–394). At the end Andromache, who has no body to mourn, promises to burn Hector's clothes “to be your fame before the Trojans and the Trojan women” (22.514).

Andromache is at her loom when the news comes, weaving into a double-folded purple cloth “flowers of many colors” (22.440–441). The verse is Helen's in Book 3, where she was working into such a cloth “the many trials / of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans” (3.125–127): Helen weaves the war, and Andromache weaves flowers until it reaches her (see [[weaving]], [[helens-web]] and [[andromache]]). Her lament and Hecuba's (22.430–436, 22.477–514) are sung again over the body in the last book, where the two women and Helen lead the mourning (24.723–776; see [[lament]], [[kleos]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
