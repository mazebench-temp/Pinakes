**Swift feet.** ποσὶν ταχέεσσι διώκειν, “to pursue with swift feet,” is a half-verse of the hunt: a hound goes after a boar or a lion “with swift feet” in the simile of Hector's attack in Book 8 (8.338–339). Book 22 has it three times of Achilles, from Apollo, Zeus and Athena in the likeness of Deiphobus (22.8, 22.173, 22.230), and ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι πεποιθώς, “trusting in his swift feet” (22.138), is the verse-end of Paris running through the city in Book 6 (6.505). καρπαλίμοισι πόδεσσι, “on swift feet” (22.166), closes the simile of the racehorses around the turning post, where the two men “whirled three times around the city of Priam” (22.165). The pursuer's speed avails him nothing until Athena stops Hector by a lie (22.199–201, 22.226–247; [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Swift horses.** After the killing the speed passes to Achilles' horses: “and the swift horses / were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans” (22.464–465), as Andromache sees from the wall. See [[achilles]], [[swift-footed]] and [[nimble]].
