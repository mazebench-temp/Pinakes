**The gate in the chase.**

{{quote:2441976e-583b-5604-8aea-a843129cc3f1}}

ὁσσάκι … τοσσάκι, “as often as … so often”: the attempt is made again and again, and fails each time. The gates are Hector's way back into the city, under “the well-built towers” from which the Trojans could cover him with their missiles (22.195–196; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]), and Achilles, who will not let his own men throw at Hector (22.205–207), keeps him from them (see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

**Priam at the gate.** When Hector has been killed and dragged away, the city wails, and “The people could hardly hold back the old man as he chafed, / eager to go out through the Dardanian gates” (22.412–413). He begs them to let him go alone to the ships of the Achaeans and supplicate the man who killed his sons (22.416–428; see [[priam]] and [[supplication]]).

**Two names.** The book names the Scaean gates at its beginning and in Hector's last words (22.6, 22.360; see [[scaean-gates]]), and the Dardanian gates twice between, in the chase and in Priam's grief; Hector stands before the Scaean gates and leaves them in flight (22.35, 22.137), and it is the Dardanian gates he makes for as he runs. The poem does not say whether these are two gates or one.
