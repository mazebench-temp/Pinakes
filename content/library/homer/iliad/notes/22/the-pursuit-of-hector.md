summary: Book 22 tells the chase. Hector runs when Achilles comes near, and the two run three times around the city, past the lookout, the wild fig tree and the springs, while Zeus asks the gods whether to save him; Achilles keeps him from the gates and waves back his own men, and at the fourth coming to the springs Zeus' scales end the chase (22.131–213). Athena, Hector and Andromache speak of it afterward (22.229–230, 22.250–252, 22.455–456).

# The chase told again: the false Deiphobus (22.229-230), Hector (22.250-252), Andromache's fear (22.455-456).
refs: [22.229-230, 22.250-252, 22.455-456]
passages:
  - 22.131-166 | Three times around the city
  - 22.188-213 | To the fourth coming to the springs
---

**The start.** Achilles comes near “the equal of Enyalius”, brandishing the Pelian ash, the bronze shining around him like a blazing fire or the rising sun (22.131–135; see [[enyalius]] and [[the-pelian-ash]]). Hector trembles, leaves the gates behind him and runs, “and the son of Peleus sprang at him, trusting in his swift feet” (22.136–138; see [[hector]] and [[swift-footed]]). Achilles goes after him like a falcon in the mountains swooping on a timid dove (22.139–142; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]): “so he flew straight on in his fury, and Hector fled / beneath the wall of the Trojans, and plied his nimble knees” (22.143–144; see [[nimble]]). The words were first said of Hector. When Apollo had put strength into him in Book 15, he ran back to the battle like a stalled horse that breaks his halter, “so Hector plied his nimble feet and knees” (15.263–269; see [[the-stallion]]). At the start of this book the same verse is Achilles', running toward the city like a prize-winning horse (22.21–24; see [[like-a-racehorse]]), and Hector plies his knees in flight.

**The course.** They run past the lookout and the windy wild fig tree, always out from under the wall, along the wagon road, and come to the two springs of the eddying Scamander, one warm, with smoke rising from it, the other cold as hail or snow in summer (22.145–152; see [[the-lookout-at-troy]], [[the-fig-tree]] and [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). Beside them are the stone troughs where the wives and daughters of the Trojans used to wash their clothes “in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (22.153–156). The last verse is Achilles' own in Book 9, when he set his life against all the wealth that Ilios was said to have possessed in peace: “For nothing to me is worth my life” (9.401–403; see [[psyche]]). Now the two men run past the troughs for a life.

**The race.**

{{quote:0103caf6-c062-562a-b146-9bb65af93207}}

ἐσθλός, “a brave man”, and μέγʼ ἀμείνων, “a far better one”, take up Hector's own words to Achilles at their first meeting, “And I know that you are brave, and I am far worse than you” (20.434). The prizes that the two do not run for are those of the games, and the narrator goes on to the horses that round the turning post for “a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died”, as the two whirl three times around the city on swift feet (22.162–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]] and [[contests]]). The games of the next book are for Patroclus (23.257–897).

**The spectators.** “And all the gods were watching” (22.166; see [[watching-the-war]] and [[olympians]]). Zeus grieves for the man who has burned many thighs of oxen for him, and asks the gods whether they will save him from death or bring him down by Achilles now (22.167–176; see [[zeus]]). Athena answers in Hera's words over Sarpedon, and Zeus lets her go; she darts down from the peaks of Olympus (22.177–187; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]] and [[tritogeneia]]).

**The gates.** “And swift Achilles pressed on after Hector, relentlessly driving him on” (22.188), as a dog tracks down a fawn that has hidden from him under a bush (22.189–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]).

{{quote:2441976e-583b-5604-8aea-a843129cc3f1}}

The men on the towers might have covered Hector with their missiles; Achilles keeps between him and the wall, on the side of the city, and turns him back to the plain (see [[dardanian-gates]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The narrator gives the chase the likeness of a dream, in which neither the one who flees can escape nor the one who pursues can catch him (22.199–201; see [[as-in-a-dream]]).

**Apollo and the Achaeans.** “And how could Hector have escaped the fates of death, / had not Apollo come to him for the very last time, / close by, who roused his might and his nimble knees?” (22.202–204; see [[apollo]] and [[ker-doom]]). The narrator asks it as a question, and the god's help is the last. On the other side Achilles keeps the Achaeans out of the chase: “And brilliant Achilles shook his head at his men, / and would not let them hurl their bitter missiles at Hector, / lest someone win the glory by hitting him, and he himself come second” (22.205–207; see [[kydos]]). δεύτερος, “second”, is the word of the games, where the prizes go to the first and to the second (23.265). This is the gesture that Aristotle thought would be ridiculous on the stage, “with the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back” (*Poetics* 1460a15–16; see [[aristotle]]).

**The fourth time.** “But when for the fourth time they came to the springs, / then the father held out his golden scales” (22.208–209). Hector's day sinks toward the house of Hades, “and Phoebus Apollo left him” (22.212–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Athena comes to Achilles: “Now there is no way for him to get clear of us any longer” (22.219), and she tells him to stand still and catch his breath (22.222; see [[athena]]).

**Told again.** The false Deiphobus begins with the chase, in Zeus' words: “truly swift Achilles is pressing you hard, / pursuing you with swift feet around the city of Priam” (22.229–230 ≈ 22.173; see [[deiphobus]]). Hector, turning at last, begins with it too: “I will no longer flee from you, son of Peleus, as before / I fled three times around the great city of Priam, and never dared / to wait for you as you came on” (22.250–252). And Andromache, hearing the wailing from the tower, fears that Achilles “has cut off my bold Hector / alone from the city and is chasing him toward the plain” (22.455–456; see [[andromache]]). When she reaches the wall, the chase is long over, and she sees him dragged (22.463–465).
