---
summary: Polydamas is not seen in Book 22, but Hector remembers him. Waiting alone for Achilles before the gates, he fears that “Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me”, who bade him lead the Trojans into the city on the night when Achilles rose up, and he did not listen (22.99–103). It is the last time the poem names him.

**The counsel remembered.** Hector's words take up the counsel and his answer to it in the assembly of the night before (18.254–313; see [[hector]]). ἐκέλευε, “bade me” (22.101), answers Polydamas' κέλομαι γὰρ ἔγωγε / ἄστυδε νῦν ἰέναι, “for I bid you / go now to the city” (18.254–255), and ἀλλʼ ἐγὼ οὐ πιθόμην, “But I did not listen” (22.103), his πίθεσθέ μοι, “do as I say” (18.266). νύχθʼ ὕπο τήνδʼ ὀλοήν, “during this deadly night” (22.102), is the night just past, between the day on which Achilles shouted at the ditch and this one, the day of his return (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]). That night Hector called Polydamas a fool and forbade the Trojans to obey him, and the narrator called the Trojans fools for applauding Hector (18.295–296, 18.310–313; see [[nepios]]).

**Borne out.** What Polydamas foresaw has happened. He told the Trojans that Achilles would not stay on the plain but “will fight for our city and our women” (18.261–265), and that if Achilles found them still on the plain, whoever escaped would be glad to reach Ilios (18.268–271). The routed Trojans came “gladly to the city” (21.607), and now they rest along the battlements while the Achaeans come up to the wall (22.1–4). Hector's κέρδιον, “far better”, comes twice in a few verses: it would have been far better to listen, and it would now be far better to face Achilles, to kill him or to die gloriously before the city (22.103, 22.108–110).

**Disgrace.** ἐλεγχείην ἀναθήσει, “to heap disgrace on me” (22.100; see [[elenchos]]): Polydamas will be “the first”, and others will follow, “another man, baser than I”, saying that Hector trusted in his own strength and destroyed the army (22.106–107). It was Polydamas who once complained that Hector always rebuked him in the assemblies when he gave good counsel (12.211–214); now Hector fears his rebuke. Aristotle took the verse for the voice of the courage of citizens, who face danger for fear of reproach (see [[aristotle-civic-courage]]).
