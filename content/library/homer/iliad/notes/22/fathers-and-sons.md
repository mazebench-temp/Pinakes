summary: A father begs his son from the wall and loses him. Priam, “stretching out his hands”, begs “Hector, dear child” to come inside and to “pity me too, unhappy as I am” (22.37–59), but “he did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.78). When Hector is dead the old man wants to go alone to the ships and beg the killer, since “he too has a father like me, / Peleus” (22.416–421), and he wishes that his son had died in his arms (22.426).

Priam's plea rests on his age. Zeus will destroy him “on the threshold of old age” (22.60), and dogs will tear the old man's body at his own doors (22.66–76; see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[geron]]). His plan after the death is the plan of the last book, where he goes to Achilles and asks him to remember his father, an old man on the same threshold (24.486–487; see [[book-1-and-book-24]] and [[supplication]]).

Parents are named on both sides of the duel. Hector begs Achilles “by your life and your knees and your parents” and offers “the gifts that my father and my lady mother will give you” (22.338–341); Achilles refuses him “by my knees or my parents”, even if Priam should bid them “weigh out your own body in gold” (22.345–352; see [[apoina]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

At the end of the book Hector is the father lost. Andromache foresees their son going to “his father's companions” and driven from the feast with the words “Your father does not feast among us”, the boy who once sat “on his father's knees” (22.492–500; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]], [[astyanax]] and [[pater]]).
