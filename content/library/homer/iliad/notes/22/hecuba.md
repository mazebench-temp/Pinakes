summary: Hecuba stands beside Priam on the wall, loosens the fold of her robe and holds up her breast, and begs Hector by the breast that nursed him to fight from inside the wall; if Achilles kills him, she will never weep for him on a bier (22.79–89). When she sees him dragged, she tears her hair, flings away her head covering and wails, and she leads the Trojan women's lament: “My child, wretched that I am! Why should I live …?” (22.405–407, 22.430–436).

**The breast.** When Priam has spoken, Hecuba begs from the other side of him:

{{quote:a4f2d751-0f19-51d2-b717-e3bc40e308bf}}

κόλπον ἀνιεμένη, “loosening the fold of her robe” (22.80; see the translation's table of key words), with one hand, and with the other holding up the breast: the gesture is told nowhere else in Homer (see [[mothers-and-sons]]). λαθικηδέα μαζόν, “the breast that makes care forgotten” (22.83), is found only here. She asks Hector to respect it, τάδε τʼ αἴδεο, “respect this” (see [[aidos]]), and to remember it, τῶν μνῆσαι (22.84; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). αἴδεο καί μʼ ἐλέησον, “respect … and pity me”, are the words in which Lycaon begged Achilles for his life (21.74; see [[supplication]] and [[pity]]). Her request is smaller than Priam's: let him ward off the enemy “from inside the wall, and do not stand forth as champion against this man” (22.85; see [[single-combat]]). σχέτλιος, “the hard man!” (22.86), is rendered as Priam's word for Achilles was (22.41; see [[hard]]); Walter Leaf, discussing Priam's, points to Hecuba's as an instance of the word's common tone of shocked and friendly reproach, which would make it hers to Hector, “rash” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.41). What she fears is the body lost: she will not weep for him on a bier, nor will his “richly dowered wife” (22.87–88; see [[richly-dowered]] and [[andromache]]), but the dogs will eat him by the ships (see [[dogs]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Achilles will say the same to the dying Hector, in her words, that his lady mother will not lay him on a bier and lament him, “whom she bore herself” (22.352–353; see [[lament]]). “So the two, weeping, addressed their dear son / begging him earnestly; but they did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.90–91; see [[priam]] and [[tears]]).

**Named by her sons.** Hector names her once, when he thanks the false Deiphobus as the dearest “of my brothers, the sons whom Hecuba and Priam bore” (22.234; see [[deiphobus]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). Athena, in Deiphobus' likeness, says that “our father and our lady mother” begged him to stay inside (22.239–240; see [[truth-and-deception]]); the dying Hector offers Achilles the gifts that “my father and my lady mother will give you” (22.341; see [[apoina]]).

**The head covering.** When she sees Hector dragged, “his mother / tore her hair, and flung her shining head covering / far from her, and wailed very loudly when she saw her son” (22.405–407; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[hair]] and [[veil]]). καλύπτρη, “head covering”, is kept apart in the translation from Andromache's κρήδεμνον, “veil”, which she too will fling away (22.470; see the translation's note on 22.468–470). κώκυσεν, “wailed”, is Thetis' cry when she hears her son's grief for Patroclus (18.37; see [[thetis]]). Priam, held back by the people, ends his own lament with her, “his mother, most ill-fated, who bore him, and I myself” (22.428; see [[most-ill-fated]]), and Andromache hears her wailing from the tower as “the voice of my revered mother-in-law” (αἰδοίης ἑκυρῆς, 22.451; see [[in-laws]]).

**Her lament.**

{{quote:1b3c8fd9-4707-505f-806e-0fc3b8a3e949}}

ἁδινοῦ ἐξῆρχε γόοιο, “led the unceasing lamentation” (22.430), is said of Achilles among the Myrmidons over Patroclus (18.316, 23.17), and of Hecuba again over Hector's body in the last book, where she speaks second of three (24.747). Thetis “led the lamentation” among the Nereids (18.51; see [[lament]] and [[trojan-women]]). δειλή, “wretched” (22.431; see [[wretch]]): her question, why live, is the question of the bereaved mother (see [[bereaved-parents]]). Hector was εὐχωλή, “my boast”, the word for a boast or a vow (see [[euchomai]]), and ὄνειαρ, “a blessing”, to all, the word Andromache uses when she says that Hector will be no blessing to his son, nor his son to him (22.433, 22.486). The Trojans greeted him θεὸν ὥς, “as a god” (22.434–435), as Achilles boasts that they prayed to him as to a god (22.394; see [[prayer]]), and he was their κῦδος, “glory” (22.435; see [[kydos]]). The last verse, “while you lived; but now death and fate have overtaken you”, was twice said in Book 17 of Patroclus (22.436 = 17.478, 17.672; see [[moira]] and [[thanatos]]).
