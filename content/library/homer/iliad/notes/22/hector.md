summary: Fate holds Hector alone outside the Scaean gates. He refuses his father and mother, debates with his own heart and resolves to fight, but when Achilles comes he runs, and is chased three times around the city until Zeus' scales sink for him and Apollo leaves him. Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, brings him to stand; his terms refused and his spear spent, he knows that the gods have called him to his death, charges with his sword, and dies by Achilles' spear at the throat, foretelling Achilles' death. The Achaeans stab the body, Achilles drags it in the dust, and his father, his mother and his wife lament him.

**Alone outside.** The Trojans have crowded into the city, and Hector alone stays out: “And deadly fate fettered Hector to stay where he was, / in front of Ilios and the Scaean gates” (22.5–6; see [[moira]] and [[scaean-gates]]). πέδησεν, “fettered”, is said of fate taking Diores (4.517), and of Poseidon holding Alcathous for Idomeneus' spear, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs”, so that he could neither escape backward nor swerve aside (13.434–436). Priam sees his son standing before the gates, “raging without end to fight with Achilles” (22.35–36; see [[raging-without-end]]), and begs him not to wait for this man “alone, apart from the others” (22.38–39). Achilles told the envoys in Book 9 of a time, while he still fought, when Hector waited for him at the same gates, “there once he waited for me alone, and barely escaped my charge” (9.355; see [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]). Now he waits again.

**His father and mother.** Priam begs him to come inside the wall, so as to save the Trojans and the Trojan women, and not hold out great glory to the son of Peleus and lose his own life; let him pity his old father too, whom the dogs of his own house will tear at his doors when the city falls (22.38–76; see [[priam]] and [[supplication]]). Hecuba loosens the fold of her robe and holds up the breast that nursed him: if Achilles kills him, she will never lay him on a bier and weep for him (22.79–89; see [[hecuba]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). The narrator closes each plea with the same words, “but he did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.78), “but they did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.91). He waits for Achilles like a serpent of the mountains at its hole, “with unquenchable might”, his shining shield leaning against a jutting tower (22.92–97; see [[the-serpent-at-its-hole]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]).

**Shame.** Waiting, he speaks to his own heart, in the fourth and longest of the Iliad's battlefield monologues (22.98–130; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). The way into the city is shut by Polydamas, who bade him lead the Trojans to the city on the night when Achilles rose up: “But I did not listen—it would have been far better” (22.100–103; see [[polydamas]] and [[elenchos]]).

{{quote:49a70dd7-a3f8-566f-8c58-8fe56b4795fb}}

ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ἐμῇσιν, “by my own recklessness”: everywhere else in Homer the recklessness is someone else's, as when Sthenelus says of the Seven against Thebes that “they perished through their own recklessness” (4.409), or the Odyssey says it of Odysseus' companions and of the suitors (*Odyssey* 1.7, 22.317). Hector alone uses the word of himself (see [[atasthalie]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). The next verse is the one with which he answered Andromache at the gates in Book 6, “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes” (22.105 = 6.442; see [[aidos]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]), and the shame has a voice. Twice before he imagined what someone would say of him in time to come: a stranger seeing Andromache in tears, “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting” (6.459–461), and a sailor passing the mound of a man “whom once, when he was the best in the fighting, shining Hector killed” (7.87–91; see [[hectors-epitaph]]). Now the speaker is “another man, baser than I”, and the saying is of ruin (22.106–107; see [[tis-speech]]). The suitors in the Odyssey fear the same baser man's word (*Odyssey* 21.324). Against it he sets the choice he put to the Trojans in the assembly, to stand face to face with Achilles, “and either he will carry off the great mastery, or I might” (18.307–308). ἐϋκλειῶς, “gloriously” (22.110), is found only here in Homer.

**Terms he will not offer.** He weighs another way: to set down his shield and helmet, lean his spear against the wall, and go to Achilles unarmed with the offer of Helen and all the possessions Alexander brought to Troy, and half of everything in the city besides, under an oath of the elders (22.111–121; see [[helen]], [[paris]] and [[possessions]]). It is the offer Antenor made in the Trojan assembly of Book 7, which the herald carried to the ships (7.350–351, 7.389–391), with the half of Troy added. Walter Leaf takes the dividing of the city's goods to be the terms put to the besieged city on the shield of Achilles, half left to the citizens and half taken as ransom by the besiegers (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.511; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). He puts the thought away. Achilles would not pity him or respect him, “but will kill me naked, / just like a woman, once I have taken off my armor” (22.123–125): Lycaon came to Achilles naked and begged “respect me and pity me”, and was killed (21.50, 21.74; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). “There is no way now, from an oak or from a rock, / to talk intimately with him, as a maiden and a young man” (22.126–127). The verb, ὀαρίζω, is used in the Iliad only here and once before of Hector himself, when Paris found him “just as he was about / to turn from the place where he had talked intimately with his wife” (6.515–516; see [[oaristys]]). The talk he had with Andromache at the gates is the talk he cannot have with Achilles. Better to fight at once: “let us see to which of us the Olympian will hold out triumph” (22.129–130).

**The flight.** “So he pondered as he waited” (22.131), and Achilles comes near, “the equal of Enyalius”, brandishing the Pelian ash, the bronze shining around him like fire or the rising sun (22.131–135; see [[enyalius]] and [[the-pelian-ash]]). Enyalius is the god whom Hector called impartial when he refused Polydamas' counsel, “and he kills the man who would kill” (18.309). “And when Hector saw him, trembling seized him; and he could no longer bear / to stay there, but left the gates behind him, and went off in flight” (22.136–137). οὐδʼ ἄρʼ ἔτʼ ἔτλη, “and he could no longer bear”, said the opposite in Book 20, when Hector could no longer keep away from Achilles after Polydorus fell (20.421–422), and in the assembly he had said, “I at least / will not flee from him” (18.306–307). Achilles goes after him like a falcon after a dove (22.139–144; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]). “In front a brave man fled, but a far better one pursued him” (22.158): the words ἐσθλός and μέγʼ ἀμείνων take up Hector's own at their first meeting, “And I know that you are brave, and I am far worse than you” (20.434). They run past the lookout and the wild fig tree and the springs, three times around the city (22.145–166; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). As often as he makes for the Dardanian gates, hoping that the men on the towers will cover him with their missiles, Achilles gets in front and turns him back toward the plain (22.194–198; see [[dardanian-gates]]), and Apollo comes to him “for the very last time” and rouses his might and his nimble knees (22.202–204). The chase is told under [[the-pursuit-of-hector]].

**Dear to Zeus.** The gods watch, and Zeus grieves: “Ah, truly a man dear to me, pursued around the wall, / I see with my eyes; and my heart grieves / for Hector, who has burned many thighs of oxen for me” on Ida and on the height of the city (22.168–172; see [[zeus]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). He asks the gods whether to save him. Athena answers in the words Hera used over Sarpedon, that a mortal long since ordained to his portion cannot be set free from death, “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (22.178–181 ≈ 16.440–443; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]), and Zeus lets her go. In the last book Apollo and Zeus will say again that Hector never failed in the offerings the gods love (24.33–34, 24.66–70). At the fourth coming to the springs Zeus lifts his scales, “and the fated day of Hector sank, / and went off to the house of Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him” (22.212–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[apollo]]).

**His brother.** Athena comes to him in the likeness of Deiphobus and says that she has come out to stand with him. Hector answers that Deiphobus was always by far the dearest of his brothers, the sons whom Hecuba and Priam bore, and that he will honor him even more now, since he alone dared to come outside the wall for his sake (22.226–237). The trust is the trap (see [[deiphobus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Face to face.** At last he stands. “I will no longer flee from you, son of Peleus”, he begins; his heart has sent him to stand against Achilles, “I may kill you, or be killed” (22.250–253). The alternative is the one he set out in the assembly (18.308). θυμὸς ἀνῆκε, “my heart has sent me”, is what his mother supposed when he came into the city in Book 6, that his heart had sent him there to lift up his hands to Zeus (6.254–257); now it sends him against Achilles. He asks Achilles to call the gods to witness that the victor will strip the armor and give back the body (22.254–259). The terms are those of his challenge to the Achaeans in Book 7 (7.76–86), and the verb of his promise, “I will not defile you terribly” (ἀεικιῶ, 22.256), comes back at the end of the scene, when Zeus gives him to his enemies “to defile in his own native land” (ἀεικίσσασθαι, 22.403–404). Achilles refuses: there are no trusty oaths between lions and men (22.260–272; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). The duel and the death are told under [[the-death-of-hector]].

**The spears.** He sinks down and lets Achilles' spear fly over him, and does not see Athena give it back (22.273–277). “You missed!” (ἤμβροτες, 22.279) is what Diomedes said to Pandarus before he killed him (5.287; see [[pandarus]]). Achilles was “a glib talker and a cheat with words” (22.281); ἀρτιεπής, “glib talker”, occurs only here in Homer. “You will not plant your spear in my back as I flee” (22.283) is said in the words with which Diomedes warned Odysseus not to turn his back like a coward in the throng (8.94–95). Idomeneus told Meriones that if he were hit, the missile would not fall on his back but meet his chest or his belly “as you pressed forward to the intimate talk of the champions” (13.288–291; see [[oaristys]]). His spear glances off the shield, and he is angry “that his swift missile had flown from his hand in vain” (22.291–292), in the words of his anger when his spear failed against Ajax in Book 14 (14.406–407). Then he drew back into the crowd of his companions (14.408). Now “he stood downcast, for he had no other ash spear” (22.293), and calls to Deiphobus.

**The gods have called me.**

{{quote:a9418be1-c4b4-5be4-810d-aae07352ef00}}

θάνατόνδε κάλεσσαν, “have called me to my death”, are the words in which the narrator asked Patroclus whom he killed “when the gods called you to your death” (16.693; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The gods “who before now / protected me with ready hearts” (22.302–303) are Zeus, who promised him power to kill until he came to the ships and the sun went down (11.192–194; 17.453–455), and Apollo, who saved him from Diomedes and from Achilles (11.363, 20.443–444; see [[divine-rescue]]). “But now my fate has overtaken me” (22.303): it is what Automedon and Menelaus said of the dead Patroclus, “But now death and fate have overtaken him” (17.478, 17.672), and what Hecuba will say of Hector (22.436). He asks only for the manner of his death. μὴ μὰν ἀσπουδί γε, “Let me not … without a struggle”, is the half-verse with which he urged the Trojans at the end of Book 8 not to let the Achaeans board their ships “at their ease, without a struggle” (8.512), now turned on himself; ἀκλειῶς, “without glory”, answers ἐϋκλειῶς, “gloriously”, in his monologue (22.110), and the deed is to be known “even for men to come to hear of” (22.305; see [[kleos]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).

**The death.** He draws his sword and swoops like an eagle (22.306–311; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]), and Achilles, finding his throat bare where the armor stripped from Patroclus did not cover it, drives the spear through his neck (22.312–329; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). Wounded, he speaks “feebly”, ὀλιγοδρανέων, in the verse with which he answered Apollo when Ajax's stone had felled him (22.337 = 15.246); the word is used once more, of the dying Patroclus (16.843). He begs for his body, and the last two verses of the plea are the verses of his challenge in Book 7, “so that when I am dead / the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (22.342–343 = 7.79–80; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). At the ships he had threatened his own men that if he saw any hanging back, “his kinsmen / and kinswomen shall not give him his portion of fire when he is dead, / but dogs shall tear him in front of our city” (15.349–351; see [[dogs]]). Achilles calls him “you dog” and refuses (22.345–354). His last words begin with what he knows: “Truly I know you well as I look at you, and I was not going / to persuade you; for truly the heart in your breast is iron” (22.356–357). The iron heart is Hecuba's word for Priam when he resolves to go to Achilles, and Achilles' word for Priam when he comes (24.205, 24.521; see [[iron]]). Then he foretells Achilles' death at the hands of Paris and Phoebus Apollo at the Scaean gates (22.358–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[prophecy]]), as Patroclus foretold his (16.851–854), and dies in Patroclus' verses: “and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (22.362–363 = 16.856–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]] and [[psyche]]).

**The body.** Achilles strips it, and the Achaeans run up and gaze at “the stature and admirable looks / of Hector; and not one stood beside him without stabbing him” (22.370–371). “Ah, truly much softer to handle now / is Hector than when he burned the ships with blazing fire” (22.373–374; see [[tis-speech]]), the fire he called for at the ship of Protesilaus (15.704–718, 16.112–124). Achilles leads the paean: “We have won great glory; we have killed brilliant Hector, / to whom the Trojans throughout the city prayed as to a god” (22.393–394; see [[kydos]]). He pierces the tendons of Hector's feet, ties him to his chariot and drags him, “and all his head lay in the dust, / that once was handsome; but then Zeus gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.395–404; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**Mourned.** His mother tears her hair and wails, his father groans, and the whole city wails as if Ilios were burning from top to bottom (22.405–411). Priam mourns him above all the sons Achilles has killed: “would that he had died in my arms” (22.423–428). Hecuba laments that he was her boast and the Trojans' glory, greeted “as a god” (22.431–436; see [[lament]]). Andromache, who has not yet heard, has a bath heated for him “when he came home from the battle”, the homecoming that Zeus denied him when he put on the armor (22.442–446; 17.207–208; see [[andromache]] and [[bathing]]). She fears that Achilles has cut him off from the city and put an end to “the grievous pride / that held him, since he never stayed in the crowd of men, / but ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might” (22.455–459), what he told her in Book 6, that he had learned “to fight among the foremost of the Trojans” (6.444–446). In her lament he is the man for whom the Trojans named their son: “for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.506–507; 6.402–403; see [[astyanax]]).

His epithets in the book are κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (22.232, 22.249, 22.337, 22.355, 22.471), δῖος, “brilliant” (22.226, 22.320, 22.393, 22.395), φαίδιμος, “shining” (22.274), ἱππόδαμος, “tamer of horses” (22.161, 22.211), and θρασύς, “bold” (22.455). He is μέγας, “great” (22.232, 22.249), and ποιμὴν λαῶν, “shepherd of the people” (22.277), and Athena calls him μάχης ἄατος, “insatiable of battle” (22.218; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]], [[brilliant]], [[phaidimos]], [[tamer-of-horses]], [[bold]], [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[insatiable-of-war]]).
