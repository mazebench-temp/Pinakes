summary: Andromache, lamenting Hector, foresees their son's life as an orphan: others will take his lands, and he will go begging among his father's companions and be driven from the feast; he who once ate marrow on his father's knees and slept in his nurse's arms will suffer much, “he whom the Trojans call by the name of Astyanax; / for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.484–507).

**A mere infant.** Andromache's lament turns from Hector to the child: “and the boy is still a mere infant, / whom you and I begot, both most ill-fated; neither will you be to him / a blessing, Hector, since you are dead, nor he to you” (22.484–486; see [[andromache]] and [[most-ill-fated]]). πάϊς δʼ ἔτι νήπιος αὔτως returns over Hector's body in the last book (24.726); at the gates in Book 6 he was “an infant …, quite small” (νήπιον αὔτως, 6.400; see the translation's note on 22.484–485, and [[nepios]]). There Hector prayed that his son would come home from war with bloody spoils and gladden his mother's heart (6.476–481; see [[hector-and-andromache]]); now each will be nothing to the other. Even if the boy escapes the war, “others will take away his lands” (22.487–489): ἀπουρίσσουσιν, found only here in Homer, is “move the boundaries” of his fields (see [[boundary-stones]]).

**The orphan's day.** The picture that follows is general, in the present tense of what always happens: the day of orphanhood leaves a child without friends of his own age; he tugs at his father's companions by the cloak and the tunic, one of them holds a cup to his lips for a moment, and a boy whose parents are living drives him from the feast: “Off with you! Your father does not feast among us” (22.490–498; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]], [[childhood]] and [[chere]]). Then it is her son again, “And in tears the boy goes up to his widowed mother, / Astyanax” (22.499–500):

**On his father's knees.** The knees are the child's place in Homer, where Phoenix set the infant Achilles and fed him meat and wine (9.485–491; see [[childhood]] and [[phoenix]]). μυελόν, “marrow”, and οἰῶν πίονα δημόν, “the rich fat of sheep”, are the richest food there was (22.501); δημός, “fat”, is another word than δῆμος, the land and its people (see [[demos]]). He slept “in the arms of his nurse” (22.503), as in Book 6 he came to the gates in a handmaid's arms and shrank from his father's helmet into his nurse's bosom (6.389, 6.399–400, 6.467; see [[handmaids]]).

**His name.** “he whom the Trojans call by the name of Astyanax; / for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.506–507): the poet gave the same reason when the child first appeared, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.402–403; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). ἐπίκλησιν καλέουσιν, “call by the name of”, is said earlier in the book of the star “that men call by the name of the dog of Orion” (22.29; see the translation's table of formulas). The name that honored his father now marks what he has lost. In the last book Priam speaks to Achilles of the son who alone, οἶος, guarded the city and its people (24.499–500), and Andromache foresees that the child will follow her into slavery or be hurled from the tower by some Achaean whose kin Hector killed (24.732–739; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).
