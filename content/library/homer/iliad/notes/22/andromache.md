---
summary: Andromache is not named in the book; the narrator calls her “the wife”. Weaving in the inner room, she has had her handmaids heat a bath for Hector's return when she hears the wailing from the tower; she runs to the wall “like a maenad”, sees him dragged toward the ships, faints, and flings away the veil that Aphrodite gave her on her wedding day. Held up by her husband's sisters and his brothers' wives, she laments: they were born to one fate, their son will be an orphan, and Hector's body will lie naked by the ships while she burns his clothes (22.437–515).
# She is never named in Book 22: "your richly dowered wife" (22.88), "the wife" (22.437), her scene and her lament

**Not yet told.** Hecuba, begging Hector from the wall, has named her only as “your richly dowered wife”, who will not weep over him on a bier (22.87–88; see [[richly-dowered]] and [[hecuba]]). When Hecuba has led the women's lament, the narrator turns to her without her name, as ἄλοχος, “the wife”, with Ἕκτορος at the head of the next verse (see the translation's note on 22.437–438):

{{quote:1a62f79d-0c73-5198-90a8-2ef63fc3e6a6}}

She is where Hector sent her in Book 6, at her own loom in the house, with her handmaids at their work (6.490–492; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). Her web is told in the words of Helen's, μέγαν ἱστὸν ὕφαινε, / δίπλακα πορφυρέην, “She was weaving a great web, / a double-folded purple cloth” (3.125–126), but Helen was working into hers “the many trials” of Trojans and Achaeans (3.126–127), and Andromache works flowers into hers (θρόνα, 22.441; see [[weaving]] and [[helen]]). The bath she orders is for a man coming home from battle, μάχης ἐκ νοστήσαντι (22.444), the homecoming that Zeus foretold she would never have from him (17.207–208; see [[bathing]] and [[nostos]]). The narrator calls her νηπίη, “the fool”, for not knowing (22.445; see [[nepios]]): Athena has already brought him down “far from all baths”.

**The voice from the tower.** “And she heard the wailing and the groaning from the tower; / and her limbs shook, and the shuttle fell from her to the ground” (22.447–448). She knows the voice, her mother-in-law's (22.451; see [[in-laws]]), and takes two slave women with her, as a lady goes out attended (22.449–450; see [[handmaids]]). Her fear is the fear of Book 6, that his own might would destroy him (6.407; see [[menos]]): that Achilles has cut him off from the city and “put an end to the grievous pride / that held him, since he never stayed in the crowd of men, / but ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might” (22.457–459). ἀγηνορίη, “pride”, is what kills the boar or lion of the simile that turns on the hunters, “and it is his pride that kills him” (12.41–46), and in the Odyssey the last two verses are Odysseus' praise of Neoptolemus to his father's shade (*Odyssey* 11.514–515; see [[neoptolemus]]).

**Like a maenad.** “So saying, she rushed through the hall like a maenad, / with her heart leaping; and her handmaids went with her” (22.460–461). μαινάδι ἴση is found only here in Homer. In Book 6 she ran to the tower “like a woman out of her mind” (μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα, 6.389; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]), and there she found Hector alive at the gates. Now she stands on the wall among the throng of men and sees him “being dragged in front of the city” toward the ships (22.462–465; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[the-tower-of-ilios]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). “And murky night covered her eyes, / and she fell backward, and breathed out her soul” (22.466–467): the first verse is the verse of a warrior's death (5.659, 13.580; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]), and ἀπὸ … ἐκάπυσσε, “breathed out”, is found only here. She does not die; she swoons, and “came to herself” (ἔμπνυτο, 22.475), the word for Hector reviving after Diomedes' spear struck his helmet (11.359).

**The veil.**

{{quote:00e4c77d-a850-51f1-a99b-a5cdf6992652}}

The headdress of a bride is thrown off as a mourner throws off her veil, and the day of the gift is the day Hector took her from her father's house (see [[veil]], [[aphrodite]], [[eetion]], [[bride-price]] and [[marriage]]). κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” (22.471), is the epithet of the bridegroom, and of the father whose helmet frightened their son at the Scaean gates (6.466–473; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]). Hector's sisters and his brothers' wives stand around her and hold her up (22.473–474), the women among whom he looked for her in Book 6 (6.378, 6.383; see [[in-laws]] and [[trojan-women]]).

**Her lament.** She speaks “with deep sobs” (ἀμβλήδην, 22.476; only here in Homer), among the Trojan women, and to Hector:

{{quote:1662362c-05e1-5eee-814b-e85231154012}}

The two births stand side by side, his in the house of Priam and hers in the house of Eetion, in Thebe under wooded Placus (see [[eetion]], [[thebe]] and [[placus]]). In Book 6 she foresaw widowhood: “who soon will be your widow” (6.408–409); now she is “a widow in the halls” (22.484; see [[chere]]), and the child she begged Hector not to make an orphan (6.432) will be one. The orphan's day is a general picture, of a boy shut out from the feasts of his father's companions (22.487–499; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]]), and then her own son's, Astyanax, who once ate marrow on his father's knees and slept in his nurse's arms (22.500–507; see [[astyanax]]). She ends with the body:

{{quote:9849cf11-227b-5a8d-a3f8-ca792d7c71f3}}

The worms and the dogs are the fate that Hecuba feared and Achilles promised (22.86–89, 22.335–336, 22.354; see [[dogs]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The clothes were woven by the women of the house (see [[weaving]]); burned without the body, they will be Hector's κλέος, “fame”, before the Trojans and their women (see [[kleos]]). Walter Leaf sees in these lines, as at *Odyssey* 1.291, a hint of a cenotaph, which Homer never mentions (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.323; see the translation's note on 22.512–514). The women answer her as they answered Briseis over Patroclus, “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” (22.515 = 19.301; see [[lament]]). Her last lament comes over his body, where she speaks first of three (24.723–745).
