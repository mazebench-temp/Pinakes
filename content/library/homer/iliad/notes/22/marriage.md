summary: Andromache is called in the book only Hector's wife (ἄλοχος, 22.88, 22.437), and when she sees him dead she flings away the veil Aphrodite gave her “on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet took her / from the house of Eetion, after giving a countless bride-price” (22.470–472). Priam calls Laothoe, the mother of two of his sons, “a queen among women” (22.48).

**The wife.** Hecuba warns Hector that if he dies, neither she nor his “richly dowered wife” will weep for him on a bier (22.86–88; see [[richly-dowered]]), and the narrator brings Andromache in as ἄλοχος, “the wife”, without her name, “for no true messenger had come and told her / that her husband was staying outside the gates” (22.437–439; see [[andromache]] and [[posis]]). Her lament makes them one: “We were both born, it seems, to one fate, / both of us” (22.477–478), and leaves her “a widow in the halls” (22.484; see [[chere]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**The wedding.** ἠγάγεθʼ, “took her” (22.471), is the middle of ἄγω, to lead a bride to one's own house, as Echecles took Polymele “to his house, after giving a countless bride-price” (16.190; see [[bride-price]]). The same verb is used earlier in the book of the other marriage of Priam's house: Hector thinks of giving back Helen and all the possessions that Alexander ἠγάγετο Τροίηνδʼ, “brought to Troy, which was the beginning of the quarrel” (22.114–116; see [[helen]] and [[paris]]). One son led his bride from her father's house with gifts; the other carried off another man's wife and her goods. Andromache's wedding day is remembered by Aphrodite's gift, the veil she throws off when she sees Hector dragged (22.468–472; see [[veil]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**Priam's wives.** κρείουσα γυναικῶν, “a queen among women” (22.48), is Priam's title for Laothoe, the daughter of Altes, who brought him gifts from her father (22.51; see [[laothoe]] and [[altes]]). Walter Leaf takes the word, found only here, to mean that she was a true wife, in no subordinate position: a plain case of polygamy, but among the Trojans only, which he counts among the few hints of a difference of custom between the two peoples (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.48). Hecuba is the mother of Hector (22.234; see [[hecuba]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**The wives of Troy.** The wives of the Trojans washed their clothes at the springs in time of peace (22.155–156), and Hector asks that “the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans” may give him his portion of fire (22.343); in Priam's picture of the sack his sons' wives are dragged off by the Achaeans (22.65; see [[trojan-women]] and [[captive-women]]).
