summary: Hector meets Achilles in the armor he stripped from Patroclus, and it covers all his body but the throat, where the collarbones hold the neck away from the shoulders; there Achilles drives his spear (22.321–327). When Hector is dead Achilles strips it, bloody, from his shoulders, and lifts it into his chariot before he drags the body to the ships (22.367–369, 22.376, 22.399).
# Unnamed: the armor that covers Hector (22.321-327), stripped (22.367-369, 22.376) and lifted into the chariot (22.399).

**Worn by Hector.** The second verse is the one in which Hector, about to put the armor on, spoke of it to the Trojans, “the fine armor I stripped from mighty Patroclus when I killed him” (17.187 ≈ 22.323; see [[might-of-patroclus]]). Zeus, watching him arm, said that he would not come home from the battle for Andromache to receive it from him (17.207–208), and in this book she has her handmaids heat a bath for him, “the fool, nor did she know that far from all baths / gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles” (22.442–446; see [[andromache]] and [[bathing]]).

**The gap.** Achilles, in the armor that Hephaestus made to replace this one (see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]), looks along Hector's body, “eyeing his fair skin, where it would best give way” (22.321), and finds it bare only at the throat. There the spear goes in (22.326–327; see [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[the-death-of-hector]]). Patroclus wore it until Apollo struck the helmet from his head and loosened the corselet, just before Euphorbus and Hector wounded him (16.793–822).

**Taken back.** “and laid it aside, and began to strip the armor from his shoulders, / bloody” (22.368–369); “And when brilliant swift-footed Achilles had stripped him” (22.376), he mounts his chariot, “lifting the glorious armor in” (22.399), and drags Hector to the ships (see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). He had sworn not to bury Patroclus until he brought back Hector's armor and head (18.334–335); the armor he brings back is his own, his father's gift. Hector, offering his terms, had meant to strip Achilles in the same way: “but when I have stripped you of your glorious armor, Achilles, / I will give your body back to the Achaeans” (22.258–259).
