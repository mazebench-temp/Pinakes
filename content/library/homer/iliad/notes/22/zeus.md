---
summary: Zeus watches the pursuit with the other gods and grieves for Hector, who has burned many thighs of oxen for him, and asks the gods whether to save him or let him fall; when Athena protests in Hera's words over Sarpedon, he gives way and sends her down. At the springs he holds out his golden scales, and Hector's fated day sinks. Men name him through the book as the giver of their fate and of victory, and at the end he gives Hector's body to his enemies to defile in his own land.

**The spectator.** As Achilles and Hector run around the city, “all the gods were watching; / and among them the father of men and gods began to speak” (22.166–167; see [[watching-the-war]] and [[father-of-men-and-gods]]):

{{quote:aa9cc543-1644-59b7-9411-9e4bb580da70}}

The speech has the shape of his words over Sarpedon: a cry of grief, and a choice between saving the man and letting him fall (16.433–438; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). For Sarpedon he asked Hera; here he puts the question to all the gods, φράζεσθε … καὶ μητιάασθε, “consider … and take counsel” (22.174; see [[olympians]]), and speaks of their decision as a common one, “whether we shall save him” (22.175). His reason is Hector's sacrifices, burned “on the peaks of many-folded Ida, and at other times / on the height of the city” (22.171–172; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]], [[sacrifice]] and [[many-folded]]). Zeus has a precinct and an altar on Gargarus, the peak of Ida (8.47–48; see [[gargarus]] and [[ida]]), and a priest among the Trojans, Onetor, “the priest of Zeus / of Ida” (16.604–605; see [[onetor]]). Plato's Socrates would not have the greatest of the gods lament so, and quotes the first two verses, with περὶ ἄστυ, “around the city”, where the Iliad has περὶ τεῖχος, “around the wall” (*Republic* 3.388c; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]).

**He gives way.** Athena answers him with the three verses of Hera's answer over Sarpedon (22.179–181 = 16.441–443; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]] and [[athena]]), and Zeus yields with the two verses with which he answered her in Book 8, “Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak / in full earnest” (22.183–184 = 8.39–40; see [[tritogeneia]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). He adds: “do as your mind is set, and hang back no longer” (22.185), as he told Hera in Book 4, “Do as you wish” (4.37). “So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already, / and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (22.186–187): the verses with which he sent her down to make the Trojans break the truce (4.73–74), and the first of them when he sent her to Achilles with nectar and ambrosia (19.349; see [[olympus]]).

**The scales.** “But when for the fourth time they came to the springs, / then the father held out his golden scales” (22.208–209; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The verses are those of Book 8, where the scales gave the day to the Trojans (8.69–74); here one fate is Achilles' and one Hector's, and “the fated day of Hector sank, / and went off to the house of Hades” (22.212–213; see [[aisa]] and [[hades]]). What Zeus foretold to Hera in Book 15 is done: “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**In men's mouths.** For the men below Zeus is the giver of fate and of victory. Priam fears that “the father, the son of Cronus, on the threshold of old age / will destroy” him “in a grievous fate” (22.60–61; see [[son-of-cronus]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). Hector waits to see “to which of us the Olympian will hold out triumph” (22.130; see [[olympian]] and [[euchomai]]); he calls the gods to witness an agreement and stakes it on Zeus' grant of victory (22.254–259), mocks him for claiming to know his fate “from Zeus” (22.280), and at the end knows that he was dear to Zeus and Apollo long ago, and is no longer (22.301–303; see [[apollo]]). Athena calls Achilles “dear to Zeus” (Διῒ φίλε, 22.216; see [[dear-to-zeus]]), and Achilles will accept his own doom “whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods” (22.365–366 = 18.115–116; see [[ker-doom]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

**What Zeus knew.** In Book 17, when Hector put on the armor of Achilles, Zeus pitied him and gave him great power for a time, “since you will not come home from the battle / for Andromache to receive from you the glorious armor of the son of Peleus” (17.207–208). Hector dies in that armor (22.322–323), and Andromache, who has not yet heard, has a bath made ready “for Hector when he came home from the battle” (22.444; μάχης ἐκ νοστήσαντι, as μάχης ἐκνοστήσαντι, 17.207; see [[andromache]] and [[bathing]]).

**The body.** Hector had promised not to defile Achilles' body if Zeus gave him the victory: “for I will not defile you terribly, if Zeus grants me / to outlast you” (οὐ … ἀεικιῶ, 22.256–257). When Achilles drags his body, the narrator uses the same verb of what Zeus allows: “but then Zeus gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (ἀεικίσσασθαι, 22.403–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). In the last book Zeus will have Thetis summoned, so that Achilles may take gifts from Priam and give Hector back (24.74–76).
