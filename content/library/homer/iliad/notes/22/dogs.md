summary: The dogs of Book 22 are the end that everyone foresees for the dead. Priam wishes them on Achilles and fears his own for himself; Hecuba, Achilles and Andromache foresee them for Hector by the ships, and Hector begs to be spared them; Achilles calls him “you dog”; and in the chase Achilles is a hunting dog that tracks a fawn (22.42, 22.66–76, 22.89, 22.189–193, 22.335–354, 22.509).
# τραπεζῆας (22.69), the table dogs of Priam's house, named again only at 23.173 and Od. 17.309 (τραπεζῆες).

**Priam's dogs.** Priam begs Hector from the wall not to wait for Achilles: “Would that he were as dear to the gods / as he is to me: soon the dogs and vultures would eat him / as he lay” (22.41–43; see [[vultures]]). Then he foresees his own death when the city is taken, after he has seen his sons killed and his daughters dragged away:

{{quote:aa0c2617-ac5b-5aa9-a776-0be07db1caa8}}

These are not the dogs of the battlefield but his own, τραπεζῆας θυραωρούς, “to share my table and guard my doors” (22.69). τραπεζῆες κύνες, “table dogs”, are the dogs a lord keeps in his house: Achilles had nine, and he cuts the throats of two and throws them on the pyre of Patroclus (23.173–174), and in the Odyssey Odysseus wonders whether his old dog Argos was swift in the chase or one of the table dogs that masters keep for show (*Odyssey* 17.306–310). θυραωρός, “door-guard”, occurs only here. ὠμησταὶ ἐρύουσιν, “that eat raw flesh will tear at”, are the words Odysseus used of the birds that would tear at the body of Socus (11.453–454; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]). The dogs that Priam has fed from his table will drink his blood and lie in the doorway. For a young man killed in battle, he goes on, all is fitting and all that is seen of him is fair; but when dogs disfigure the gray head and the gray beard and the nakedness of an old man, “this is the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals” (22.71–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]], [[pity]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**The threat to Hector.** Hecuba warns him that if Achilles kills him she will not weep for him on a bier, but “far away from us both, / beside the ships of the Argives, the swift dogs will devour you” (22.88–89; see [[hecuba]] and [[swift-dogs]]). Achilles, standing over him, says it as a fact: “and you the dogs and the birds / will tear shamefully, but him the Achaeans will give burial rites” (22.335–336; see [[birds]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Hector begs him by his life and his knees and his parents, “do not let the dogs devour me beside the ships of the Achaeans”, but to take bronze and gold and give his body back to be burned (22.338–343; see [[supplication]] and [[cremation]]). Achilles' answer begins with the word itself:

{{quote:17ed5eb0-a116-5ee6-998b-a0c780b77af7}}

κύον, the vocative, is the dog of abuse, the shameless creature (see [[shameless-dog]]), the word Achilles shouted after Hector when Apollo snatched him away (20.449). Andromache ends the book with the same fear: by the curved ships, far from his parents, “writhing worms will eat you, when the dogs have had their fill / of your naked body” (22.508–510; see [[maggots]]). The threat that the poem announced in its first verses, the men made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5), stands over Hector's body at the end of the book. It is not carried out. Aphrodite keeps the dogs from him day and night and anoints him with oil of roses (23.184–187), and Hermes tells Priam that neither dogs nor birds have eaten his son (24.411).

**The hunting dog.** In the simile of the chase Achilles is a dog that rouses a fawn from its lair in the mountains and tracks it down wherever it hides (22.188–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). The first dog of the book is a star: Achilles, coming over the plain, gleams like the star “that men call by the name of the dog of Orion” (22.29; see [[the-autumn-star]] and [[orion]]).
