---
summary: Achilles chases a god before he chases a man, in the same words. Apollo asks him, “Why, son of Peleus, do you pursue me with your swift feet, / you who are mortal, me, an immortal god?” and tells him, “You will not kill me” (22.8–13); then Zeus sees Achilles pursuing Hector “with swift feet around the city of Priam” (22.173), and the false Deiphobus says it again (22.230). The god cannot be caught; the man is caught and killed.
refs: [22.8-13, 22.168-187, 22.230]

{{quote:4dd8e9c7-20ee-5fa6-ae8f-3ed7059dd26c}}

The phrase ποσὶν ταχέεσσι διώκειν, “pursue with swift feet”, comes three times in the book, of the god and twice of Hector (22.8, 22.173, 22.230), and elsewhere in Homer only of a hound in a simile of Book 8, where the hound is Hector driving the Achaeans (8.338–341; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). Achilles' answer to the god states the difference plainly: “Truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength” (22.20; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

{{quote:aa9cc543-1644-59b7-9411-9e4bb580da70}}

The book also sets two fathers side by side. On the wall Priam begs “Hector, dear child” to come inside, Hecuba begs him to “pity me”, and “they did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.38, 22.82, 22.91); on Olympus Zeus grieves for the same man, “my heart grieves / for Hector” (22.169–170), and asks the gods whether to save him. When his own child objects, he yields to her in the words he used to Athena in Book 8, “Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak / in full earnest” (22.183–184 = 8.39–40), and lets her go (22.185–187; see [[fathers-and-sons]], [[priam]], [[zeus]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). The mortal father cannot move his son; the father of gods and men is moved by his daughter.
