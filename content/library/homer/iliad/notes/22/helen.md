---
summary: Helen is named once, in Hector's monologue before the gates. He thinks of going to Achilles unarmed and promising him Helen and the possessions with her, all that Alexander brought to Troy, “which was the beginning of the quarrel”, with half the wealth of the city besides, and he puts the thought away (22.111–122).
---

Waiting for Achilles, Hector weighs a way out of the fight: he would set down his arms, go to meet Achilles, “and promise him Helen, and the possessions with her, / all, as many as Alexander in his hollow ships / brought to Troy, which was the beginning of the quarrel, / to give to the sons of Atreus to take away” (22.114–117; see [[hector]] and [[possessions]]). The words are those of the Trojan assembly in Book 7. Antenor proposed, “let us give Argive Helen, and the possessions with her, / to the sons of Atreus to take away” (7.350–351; see [[antenor]] and [[argive-helen]]), and Paris refused to give up the woman, though he would give back the possessions (7.362–364; see [[paris]]). The herald Idaeus took the answer to the ships in the verses that Hector now uses of the treasure, “All the possessions that Alexander in his hollow ships / brought to Troy” (7.389–390; see [[idaeus]]). Diomedes told the Achaeans to take neither the possessions “nor Helen” (7.400–401; see [[diomedes]]). Hector would give both, and half of everything in the city besides (22.117–121).

“Which was the beginning of the quarrel” (ἥ τʼ ἔπλετο νείκεος ἀρχή, 22.116) names the cause of the war as Menelaus named it when he accepted the duel of Book 3, “for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (3.100; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[neikos]]). The thought goes no further. Achilles would kill him unarmed, and there is no talking with him now (22.122–128; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). Helen takes no part in the laments of this book; she is named next when she speaks the last of the three laments for Hector in the last book (24.761–775).
