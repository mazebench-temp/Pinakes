---
summary: The knees run through the book, from the chase to the orphan's memory. Achilles “plied his nimble feet and knees” (22.24), Hector “plied his nimble knees” in flight (22.144), and Apollo “roused his might and his nimble knees” for the last time (22.204). Achilles boasts that he has “loosened your knees” (22.335); Hector begs “by your life and your knees”, and Achilles answers, “Do not implore me, you dog, by my knees” (22.338, 22.345). Achilles will remember Patroclus while “my knees have strength to move” (22.388); Andromache's knees “are frozen” with fear (22.452–453); and Astyanax once sat “on his father's knees” (22.500).
---

λαιψηρὰ γοῦνα, “nimble knees”, is the pursuit's phrase, as in Aeneas' flight from Achilles on Ida (20.93; see [[nimble]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Achilles' “who have loosened your knees” (ὅς τοι γούνατʼ ἔλυσα, 22.335) is the phrase for a killing, γούνατʼ ἔλυσε, which the narrator uses (11.579) and others use of a great killer, of Diomedes and of Hector himself (5.176, 15.291); only here does the killer say it of himself (see [[loosened-his-limbs]]), and the suppliant's grasp of the knees is refused in words (22.338–345; see [[supplication]]). The orphan's lost place on his father's knees is a child's place in Homer (see [[childhood]]).
