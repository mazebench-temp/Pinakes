summary: The book is full of pleas, and none is granted. Priam, stretching out his hands, and Hecuba, holding up her breast, beg Hector to come inside (22.33–91); the dying Hector begs Achilles by his life, his knees and his parents to give his body back for ransom, and Achilles answers, “Do not implore me, you dog, by my knees or my parents” (22.337–354); and Priam, rolling in the dung, begs the people to let him go and supplicate the killer, “in the hope that he may respect my years and pity / my old age” (22.412–422).

**The parents.** Priam calls out “begging his dear son” (λισσόμενος, 22.35), and speaks to him “pitifully, stretching out his hands” (22.37), the gesture of a man who prays to a god, made here to a son (1.351; see [[prayer]] and [[priam]]). His plea ends with the suppliant's word, “pity me” (ἐλέησον, 22.59; see [[pity]]). Hecuba's is a suppliant's from the first verse, “Hector, my child, respect this, and pity me” (22.82), the words in which Lycaon begged Achilles for his life (αἴδεο καί μʼ ἐλέησον, 21.74), and she asks by her breast, as others ask by the knees (22.80–83; see [[hecuba]] and [[aidos]]). “So the two, weeping, addressed their dear son / begging him earnestly; but they did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.90–91): the first verse, but for its last words, is the one in which the sons of Antimachus begged Agamemnon for their lives, and he too did not listen (11.136–137; see [[tears]]).

**A false plea.** Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, tells Hector that “our father and our lady mother / begged me hard, imploring me one after the other”, and the companions too, “to stay there” (λίσσονθʼ ἑξείης γουνούμενοι, 22.239–241; see [[deiphobus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The goddess lends her lie the plea that the parents had in truth made to Hector.

**Hector's plea.** Struck in the throat, Hector begs Achilles: “I beg you by your life and your knees and your parents, / do not let the dogs devour me beside the ships of the Achaeans” (22.338–339; see [[hector]] and [[gounata]]). He offers ransom, bronze and gold, the gifts of his father and his lady mother, for his body to be taken home and burned (22.340–343; see [[apoina]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). He cannot clasp the knees he names; he lies in the dust. Achilles answers with the same words turned back, μή με κύον γούνων γουνάζεο μὴ δὲ τοκήων, “Do not implore me, you dog, by my knees or my parents” (22.345), and refuses even Hector's weight in gold (22.349–354). So he refused Lycaon, who held his knees, earlier the same day (21.64–119; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]).

**Priam's plan.** When Hector is dead Priam “entreated them all” (ἐλλιτάνευε, 22.414), the people who hold him back, and asks to go alone to the ships: λίσσωμʼ ἀνέρα τοῦτον ἀτάσθαλον ὀβριμοεργόν, “Let me beg this reckless man, this doer of violent deeds” (22.418; see [[doer-of-violent-deeds]] and [[atasthalie]]), in the hope that he will feel respect and pity for an old man, since he too has a father (22.419–422; see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). It is what Priam does in the last book. He clasps Achilles' knees and kisses the hands that killed his sons, and asks him to respect the gods and pity him, remembering his father (24.477–506; see [[book-1-and-book-24]]).
