summary: The marriage of Hector and Andromache is shown at its end and remembered from its beginning. She does not know that “her husband was staying outside the gates”, and has a bath heated for him (22.437–446); when she sees him dragged, she flings away the veil “that golden Aphrodite had given her / on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet took her / from the house of Eetion, after giving a countless bride-price” (22.468–472). Her lament begins, “We were both born, it seems, to one fate” (22.477), and leaves her “a widow in the halls” (22.484).

Hecuba had already named her, “your richly dowered wife”, who will not weep over Hector on a bier (22.87–88; see [[richly-dowered]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). The wedding day returns at the moment of widowhood: the veil was Aphrodite's gift, and the bride-price Hector's (22.470–472; see [[veil]], [[bride-price]] and [[marriage]]). The Trojan wives of the book are seen in peace, washing clothes at the springs with their daughters “in time of peace” (22.153–156), and as mourners, the women among whom Andromache laments (22.476, 22.515; see [[trojan-women]], [[andromache]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]).
