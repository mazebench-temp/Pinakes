---
summary: Book 22 answers the meeting at the Scaean gates. Hector, waiting for Achilles outside them, speaks again the verse of shame with which he answered Andromache (22.105 = 6.442); she is at the loom to which he sent her, rushes out “like a maenad” as she once went to the tower “like a woman out of her mind”, and finds him dead and dragged; the widowhood and the orphanhood she foresaw at the gates are the matter of her lament (22.437–515).
---

**The verse of shame.** At the gates Andromache begged Hector to stay on the tower, so as not to make his child an orphan and his wife a widow, and he answered that he would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women if he held back from the fighting (6.431–446). Alone before the same gates, waiting for Achilles, he gives himself the same answer, in the same verse: “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes” (22.105 = 6.442; see [[hector]], [[aidos]] and [[trojan-women]]). She had told him that his own might would destroy him (6.407; see [[menos]]); now she fears that Achilles has put an end to the pride that never let him stay in the crowd of men (22.457–459).

**The same places.** Hector sent her home to “the loom and the distaff” and to her handmaids (6.490–492), and there she is when he dies, weaving in the inner room with her handmaids heating his bath (22.440–446; see [[weaving]] and [[bathing]]). In Book 6 she had gone to the great tower “like a woman out of her mind” (μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα, 6.389), with a handmaid who carried the child (ἅμα δʼ ἀμφίπολος κίεν αὐτῇ, 6.399); in Book 22 she rushes through the hall “like a maenad” (μαινάδι ἴση), “and her handmaids went with her” (ἅμα δʼ ἀμφίπολοι κίον αὐτῇ, 22.460–461; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]] and [[handmaids]]). Then she met him alive at the gates; now she sees him from the wall, dragged toward the ships (22.462–465; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet”, the epithet of the father whose helmet frightened the child (6.466–473), is the epithet of the bridegroom of her wedding day (22.471; see [[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]).

**What she foresaw.** She had called herself the woman “who soon will be your widow” (6.408–409), and the child an orphan to be (6.432; see [[chere]]). Her lament over the dragged body comes to the widow “in the halls” and the infant, and gives the orphan's life in full (22.482–507; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]] and [[astyanax]]). Hector's own fear in Book 6 was her slavery, the day of freedom taken away (6.454–465; see [[eleutheros]] and [[doulios]]); her lament in Book 22 does not yet speak of it, and only over his body in the last book does she foresee herself carried off in the ships, and the child with her or hurled from the tower (24.729–738). At the end of Book 6 her handmaids mourned Hector in his house while he was still alive (6.497–502); in Book 22 the mourning is for the dead (22.476–515; see [[lament]] and [[andromache]]).
