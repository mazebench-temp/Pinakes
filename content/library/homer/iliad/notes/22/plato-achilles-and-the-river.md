summary: Plato's charges against Achilles begin with two verses of this book, his words to Apollo, “most baneful of all the gods”, on whom he would take vengeance if he had the strength. Proclus answered that Achilles spoke not to the god but to a lower power of Apollo's line, the guardian of Hector, who left Hector when his day sank and who would kill Achilles with Paris.

**Plato.** Socrates hesitates, for Homer's sake, to say that it is not even holy to say such things of Achilles, or to believe others who say them, “and again that he said to Apollo: ‘You have foiled me, Far-Worker, most baneful of all the gods; / truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength’” (*Republic* 3.391a). The two verses are 22.15 and 22.20, as they stand in our text; Plato leaves out the four between them, in which Achilles says what the god has taken from him: many more Trojans would have bitten the earth before they reached Ilios, but Apollo has taken great glory from him and saved them easily, “since you have no fear at all of vengeance hereafter” (22.16–19). In the poem the words answer a deception. Apollo, in the likeness of Agenor, had drawn Achilles away from the city while the Trojans fled inside (21.599–611), and now he reveals himself and mocks him: “You will not kill me, since I am not fated to fall to you” (22.7–13; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]] and [[apollo]]).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus begins his answer to the charges of impiety with these words. Achilles, he says, is of all Homer's heroes the most careful about the divine; and if he flung bolder words than he should at Apollo, we must remember that the orders of Apollo reach from on high down to the last things, some divine, some angelic, some daemonic, and these divided in many ways. Achilles is not addressing the god himself but a daemonic Apollo, and not the first of these but the one set over particular things, “and (why should I not say it plainly?) the guardian of Hector himself”. The poet says as much, when the Far-Worker stands before Achilles “in the likeness of Agenor himself in every way” (21.600–601), and again when Hector's day sinks: “and went off to the house of Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him” (22.213). ἔβλαψας, “you have foiled me”, fits such a power, which robbed Achilles of the end of his labors, and ὀλοώτατε, “most baneful”, shows the one that opposed him more than the other gods and daemons, since by keeping safe the man who had grieved him most it stood most in the way of his vengeance. Nor did the words go unpunished: Achilles is said to be killed soon after by a power of Apollo's line, “when Paris and Phoebus Apollo / destroy you” (22.359–360). So, Proclus concludes, even here poetry makes us more temperate toward the divine (*On the Republic* I 146–148 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

Proclus' Apollo of Hector has support in the book itself. It is Apollo who comes to Hector “for the very last time” and rouses his knees in the chase (22.203–204), and Hector, knowing that he is deceived, says that his death was long dearer “to Zeus and to the far-shooting son of Zeus, who before now / protected me with ready hearts” (22.301–303).
