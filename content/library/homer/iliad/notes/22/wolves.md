---
summary: The wolves of Book 22 are in Achilles' refusal of any agreement with Hector: as there are no trusty oaths between lions and men, “nor do wolves and lambs have hearts at one”, so there can be no friendship between the two of them (22.261–266).
---

{{quote:0e2fcb34-b611-5dde-a73f-06719d150070}}

The wolf and the lamb are the pair of the similes, where wolves fall on lambs and kids that a careless shepherd has let scatter (16.352–355; see [[like-wolves]] and [[lambs]]). Here they are not a likeness but a law of nature, set beside the lion and the man (see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]] and [[lion]]). ὁμόφρονα θυμὸν ἔχουσιν, “have hearts at one”, is the word of agreement: ὁμόφρων occurs only here in Homer, and in the Odyssey Odysseus wishes Nausicaa a husband with whom she may keep house ὁμοφρονέοντε, “of one mind”, nothing being better (*Odyssey* 6.180–185).
