summary: Priam pleads as an old man. **On the threshold.** Priam ends his plea to Hector with himself: “And pity me too, unhappy as I am, while I am still alive and aware, / ill-fated, whom the father, the son of Cronus, on the threshold of old age / will destroy in a grievous fate, when I have looked on many evils” (22.59–61; see [[priam]] and [[pity]]). ἐπὶ γήραος οὐδῷ, “on the threshold of old age”, is said three times in Homer, each time of an old father: of Priam here; of Peleus, when Priam in the last book asks Achilles to remember his father, a man of his own age on the baneful threshold of old age (24.486–487); and of Laertes, whom Odysseus left at home (*Odyssey* 15.348). The threshold may be the one that leads into old age, or old age itself, the last threshold of life. The Odyssey speaks of reaching it as a gift that may be withheld: Amphiaraus, whom Zeus and Apollo loved, did not reach it, and Penelope says that the gods begrudged her and Odysseus the youth they might have enjoyed together and the old age they might have come to (*Odyssey* 15.244–246, 23.210–212). The evils Priam will have looked on are those of a sacked city, his sons perishing and his daughters and his sons' wives dragged away (22.62–65; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**The old man's body.** He himself will die last of all, and the dogs he reared in his halls to share his table will tear his body at his own doors (22.66–71; see [[dogs]]). He sets the young man's death in battle against it: for a young man “it is all fitting”, and “all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is” (22.71–73; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[death-in-battle]]). An old man has no such beauty to show. What the dogs disfigure is “the gray head and the gray beard / and the nakedness of an old man who is slain” (22.74–75; see [[aidos]] and [[geron]]). The verse of the gray head and beard comes once more, in the last book, when Achilles raises the old man by the hand, pitying his gray head and gray beard (οἰκτίρων, 24.515–516). “The old man spoke, and with his hands he pulled at the gray hairs, / tearing them from his head; but he did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.77–78; see [[hair]]). The old fail to persuade the young here, as Nestor failed with Agamemnon and Achilles in the first book (1.254–303). Hecuba, too, asks her son to remember, “remember these things, dear child” (τῶν μνῆσαι, 22.84), and “they did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.91; see [[hecuba]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]).

ἡλικίην, “my years” (22.419), is Priam's time of life, not, as at 16.808, the men of one's own age (see the translation's note on 22.419). This is the argument of the last book. There Priam goes to Achilles, clasps his knees, and asks him first of all to remember his father, an old man like himself, whose neighbors may be pressing him hard with no one to ward off ruin (24.477–489; see [[supplication]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Achilles had already pictured Peleus so when he mourned Patroclus: dead by now, or barely alive “in hateful old age”, waiting for the news of his son's death (19.334–337; see [[peleus]]).
