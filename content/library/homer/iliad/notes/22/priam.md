summary: Old Priam is the first to see Achilles coming, gleaming like the star, and begs Hector from the wall to come inside: he has already lost Lycaon and Polydorus, the city's fate hangs on Hector, and he himself will be torn by his own dogs at his doors (22.25–78). When Hector is dragged before the city, Priam groans, rolls in the dung, and begs the people to let him go alone to the ships and plead with Achilles; of all his dead sons he mourns Hector most (22.405–429).

**The first to see.** “And the old man Priam was the first to see him with his eyes, / gleaming like the star, as he sped over the plain” (22.25–26): the star is the dog of Orion, brightest of all and a sign of evil (22.26–32; see [[the-autumn-star]] and [[orion]]). He watches from the wall, as he sat with the elders by the Scaean gates in Book 3 and “caught sight of huge Achilles” from the divine tower in Book 21 (3.145–244, 21.526–529; see [[the-view-from-the-wall]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). In Book 3 he went back into the city before the duel, “since I could never bear to see with my own eyes / my dear son fighting” (3.305–307); now he sees his son run, die and be dragged. “And the old man groaned aloud, and beat his head with his hands, / lifting them up high” (22.33–34): Walter Leaf supplies χεῖρας, “hands”, with ἀνασχόμενος and takes the word as a sign of the violence of the gesture (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.34; see the translation's note on 22.34). Then he speaks “pitifully, stretching out his hands” (ἐλεεινὰ … χεῖρας ὀρεγνύς, 22.37), the gesture of a man who prays, made to his son (see [[prayer]] and [[supplication]]).

**The plea.** He speaks first of Achilles, “the hard man!” (σχέτλιος, 22.41; see [[hard]]). Walter Leaf notes that the word is often a shocked and friendly reproach, and would more naturally be said of Hector, “rash”, if the verbs before and after it did not refer to Achilles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.41). Then he wishes on Achilles what Achilles will wish on Hector, to be eaten by dogs and vultures as he lay (22.42–43; compare 22.335–336, 22.354; see [[dogs]] and [[vultures]]). ἔδοιεν, “would eat”, is Aristarchus' reading, where the manuscripts have the future, ἔδονται; Leaf prefers the optative, since Priam does not mean to express any confident hope that the dogs will soon eat Achilles, and takes κείμενον, “as he lay”, to mean unburied (on 22.42–43). Then he counts his losses: the sons Achilles has killed or sold across the sea (22.44–45; see [[sons-of-priam]]), and Lycaon and Polydorus, whom he cannot see among the Trojans who have crowded into the city, and whom he would ransom if they were alive (22.46–55; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[polydorus-son-of-priam]], [[laothoe]] and [[altes]]). Hector, inside the wall, would save “the Trojans and the Trojan women”, and not hold out great glory to the son of Peleus (22.56–58; see [[trojan-women]] and [[kydos]]). Last he asks pity for himself, and pictures what an old king will see when the city falls: his sons perishing, his daughters and his sons' wives dragged off, little children dashed to the ground, and his own body torn at his doors by the dogs he fed at his table, the most pitiable sight there is (22.59–76; see [[old-age-and-memory]], [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). “The old man spoke, and with his hands he pulled at the gray hairs, / tearing them from his head; but he did not persuade Hector's heart” (22.77–78; see [[hair]]). Hecuba begs next, and the two together fail (22.79–91; see [[hecuba]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**His name in the fight.** While the two men run, Priam is named through his city: Achilles chases Hector “around the city of Priam” (22.165, 22.173, 22.230), and Hector, turning at last, says that he “fled three times around the great city of Priam” (22.251; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, lies that “our father and our lady mother / begged me hard” to stay inside (22.239–240; see [[deiphobus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]); it is what Priam and Hecuba had begged of Hector. The dying Hector offers Achilles “the gifts that my father and my lady mother will give you” for his body (22.341), and Achilles answers that he would not give it back “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold, / Priam, descendant of Dardanus” (22.351–352; see [[apoina]] and [[dardanus]]).

**The father mourns.** When Hector is dragged before the city, “his dear father groaned pitifully”, and the people wailed as if all Ilios were burning (22.408–411; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]] and [[lament]]). They can hardly hold the old man back, “eager to go out through the Dardanian gates” (22.412–413; see [[dardanian-gates]]). He rolls in the dung, κυλινδόμενος κατὰ κόπρον (22.414): in the last book Iris finds him in his courtyard, wrapped in his cloak, with the dung that he gathered with his hands as he rolled heaped on his head and neck, and he tells Achilles that he has done so ever since Hector died (24.160–165, 24.637–640). ἐξονομακλήδην, “by his name”, is found again only in the Odyssey, of the companions whom Scylla snatches, calling on Odysseus by name for the last time (22.415; *Odyssey* 12.250).

The plan is the plan of the last book, where he goes to Achilles by night, clasps his knees, and begs him to remember his father (24.477–506; see [[supplication]] and [[book-1-and-book-24]]). Now he ends with the son he has lost:

{{quote:5e1c7c43-a1f9-5b7f-b35a-691d67419960}}

“So he spoke, weeping, and the citizens groaned with him” (22.429; see [[bereaved-parents]] and [[tears]]). Plato's Socrates would not have the poets show Priam, who was born near to the gods, entreating and rolling in the dung and calling each man by name (*Republic* 3.388b; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]).
