summary: Athena does not come down to the battle of Book 11, but she acts for the Achaeans twice. With Hera she thunders in honor of Agamemnon as he arms, and she keeps Socus' spear from Odysseus' entrails; in Nestor's story of his youth she is the goddess of the Pylians, who brings them word by night, guides the quarrel and turns the army back.

**Thunder for the king.**

{{quote:ed4f156d-0b47-5858-841d-95ac2543d195}}

ἐγδουπέω, “thunder”, occurs only here. It is the verb of Zeus' epithet ἐρίγδουπος (“loud-thundering”; see [[loud-thundering]]), and elsewhere thunder is his own sign (8.75, 8.133, 8.170; see the translation's note on 11.45). The two goddesses whom Iris turned back in Book 8 (8.397–437; see [[hera]] and [[iris]]) do not come down: Strife alone of the gods is in the battle (11.73–75). They honor the king from the sky when he has armed for his day of glory (see [[arming-scene]] and [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]).

**Socus' spear.** Socus' spear goes through Odysseus' shield and corselet, “and tore all the skin from his ribs; but it was not allowed / by Pallas Athena to mingle with the man's entrails” (11.437–438; see [[socus]] and [[odysseus]]). The poem does not say how she held it back. In Book 4 she turned Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus' flesh, “as a mother / brushes a fly from her child” (4.130–131), and guided it to his belt (see [[menelaus]]). Odysseus knows at once “that the stroke had not come to a deadly place” (11.439), and kills Socus as he turns to run.

**Nestor's goddess.** In Nestor's story she brings the Pylians word to arm: “Athena / came running from Olympus as a messenger to us, to arm, / by night” (11.714–716; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). The middle verse is the one in which Iris comes running from Olympus to bid Achilles arm (11.715 = 18.167). The army she gathers is “very eager to fight” (11.717), and the young Nestor, whose father had hidden his horses, stands out among the horsemen on foot, “since so Athena guided the quarrel” (11.721; see [[neikos]] and [[nestor]]). At the Alpheus the Pylians sacrifice “to gray-eyed Athena a cow from the herd” (11.729; see [[gray-eyed]]); the printed Ἀθηναίη is read as the dative (see the translation's note on 11.729). They join battle “praying to Zeus and Athena” (11.736), and at the Olenian Rock and Alesium “Athena turned the army back” (11.758; see [[buprasium]]). In Nestor's story of Ereuthalion too, “Athena gave me triumph” (7.154; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). In the Odyssey he vows her a heifer with gilded horns, and when it is sacrificed at Pylos she comes to receive the offering (*Odyssey* 3.380–385, 3.430–438).
