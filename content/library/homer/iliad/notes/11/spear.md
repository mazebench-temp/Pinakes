summary: Agamemnon arms with two spears whose bronze shines to the sky (11.43–45). Book 11 keeps the thrust apart from the throw and the shot: Agamemnon and Odysseus are “stabbed” with spears, Diomedes and Eurypylus “shot” (11.659–662). Spears go through a helmet, a shield and a corselet, bend on a belt, glance off Hector's helmet, and stick in Ajax's shield “longing to glut themselves on flesh”.

**Two spears.** Agamemnon's arming ends with “two strong spears tipped with bronze, sharp; and far off the bronze shone from them up into the sky” (11.43–45). By night the spear points of Diomedes' sleeping companions shone in the same words, “far off the bronze shone like the lightning of father Zeus” (10.153–154). Hector, rallying the Trojans at Zeus' word, goes through the army “brandishing his sharp spears”, in the verse of his rallies in Books 5 and 6 (11.212 = 5.495 = 6.104; see [[arming-scene]]).

**Stabbed or shot.** The verbs of the book keep the weapons apart. οὐτάω and νύσσω, “stab, thrust”, are the verbs of the spear held in the hand; βάλλω, “hit, shoot”, is the verb of the thrown spear, the stone and the arrow. Nestor's count of the wounded uses them with care: “the best men lie among the ships, shot or stabbed (βεβλημένοι οὐτάμενοί τε). The son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes, has been shot, and Odysseus, famed for his spear, has been stabbed, and Agamemnon; and Eurypylus too has been shot in the thigh with an arrow” (11.658–662). Eurypylus repeats the verse of the shot and the stabbed (11.826). The two kings were wounded by the thrusting spear, Agamemnon by Coon through the forearm (νύξε, 11.252) and Odysseus by Socus through shield and corselet (οὔτησε, 11.434–438); the others by Paris' arrows (see [[bow]], [[arrows]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**Spear against armor.** Agamemnon's spear goes through Oileus' helmet and skull (11.95–98; see [[helmet]]). Iphidamas' bends like lead on Agamemnon's belt, and Agamemnon wrenches it from his hand (11.234–240; see [[belt]]). Diomedes' cast at Hector, “poising his long-shadowed spear” in the verse of Hector's cast at Ajax (11.349 = 7.244), glances off the helmet that Apollo gave Hector, and Diomedes goes after it where it has sunk into the ground (μετὰ δούρατος ᾤχετʼ ἐρωήν, 11.357–358). Socus' spear passes through Odysseus' shield and corselet, Odysseus plants his own in Socus' back between the shoulders, and then draws Socus' heavy spear out of his own flesh, and the blood spurts up (11.434–458; see [[corselet]] and [[socus]]). Agamemnon's spear is ἀνεμοτρεφές, “wind-nurtured” (11.256; see [[wind-nurtured]]), usually explained of ash grown tough on a windy height; the same word describes a wave raised by the wind (15.625). Four verses later the same spear is a ξυστόν, the “polished” shaft (from ξύω, “scrape, smooth”), “bronze-fitted”, and with it he stabs Coon (11.260; see [[bronze-fitted]]). At the ships ξυστά are the long pikes for fighting from the decks (15.388–389, 15.677–678).

**Spears in Ajax's shield.** The Trojans follow Ajax “stabbing with their spears at the middle of his shield” (νύσσοντες ξυστοῖσι, 11.565), and the spears from bold hands stick in his great shield or fall short and stand in the ground, “longing to glut themselves on flesh” (11.571–574). The spear's hunger returns when Achilles' spear stands in the riverbank “longing to glut itself on flesh” (21.168; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

**Javelins.** The ἄκων is the lighter spear for throwing: the countrymen's javelins drive the lion from the farmyard (11.552 = 17.661), and in the cattle raid of Nestor's youth Itymoneus falls “by a javelin from my hand” (11.675; see [[itymoneus]]). Diomedes taunts Hector that Apollo has saved him again, “to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins” (11.362–364). Achilles will say the same three verses to Hector when Apollo snatches him away in Book 20 (20.449–451).
