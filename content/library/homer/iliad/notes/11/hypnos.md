**The sleep of bronze.** Agamemnon strikes Iphidamas on the neck with his sword, “and loosened his limbs. So he fell there and slept the sleep of bronze, pitiable, far from his wife, whom he had wooed, fighting for his townsmen” (κοιμήσατο χάλκεον ὕπνον, 11.240–242; see [[iphidamas]] and [[death-in-battle]]). κοιμάομαι is the ordinary verb for lying down to sleep, and χάλκεος the ordinary word for the bronze of weapons; the phrase they make occurs only here. It has been taken as the sleep that the bronze brings, or as a sleep as hard and unbreakable as bronze (see the translation's note on 11.241). Homer makes Sleep the twin brother of Death, and the two carry Sarpedon's body home to Lycia (16.671–683; see the article above). Here the likeness is made in one phrase, for a young man who had just left his bride.

**Sleep in armor.** In Nestor's story the Pylians, marching against the Epeians, take their supper by companies “and lay down to sleep, each man in his armor, beside the streams of the river” (κατεκοιμήθημεν, 11.730–732; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). The fighting begins when the sun rises (11.735–736).

## Outside Homer

The Suda illustrates κοιμίσαι, “to put to sleep”, used of death, with a verse of Sophocles and with this half-verse of Homer (*Suda* κ 2547; see [[sophocles]]). Virgil's “iron sleep” (*ferreus somnus*), the death that closes a warrior's eyes, is usually compared with it (*Aeneid* 10.745–746; see [[virgil]]).
