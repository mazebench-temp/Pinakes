{{quote:6c02e05a-cd53-5539-a16e-ae83d58914ef}}

ὡς δʼ ὁπότε πλήθων ποταμὸς πεδίον δὲ κάτεισι / χειμάρρους κατʼ ὄρεσφιν ὀπαζόμενος Διὸς ὄμβρῳ: “and as when a river in flood comes down to the plain, a winter torrent down from the mountains, driven on by the rain of Zeus”. The river of Book 5 was ποταμῷ πλήθοντι … χειμάρρῳ, swollen when “the rain of Zeus falls heavy” (5.87–91), and the words are nearly the same. The flood of Book 5 broke dikes and orchard walls, the works of men; this one tears up the mountain forest, “many dry oaks and many pines”, and carries them down, casting much ἀφυσγετός into the sea (11.494–495). The word occurs only here and is explained as mud and silt or as the wreckage of trees and rubbish that a torrent sweeps along (see the translation's note on 11.495).

The tenor: “so then shining Ajax swept over the plain, driving them in confusion, cutting down horses and men” (11.496–497). κλονέων, “driving them in confusion”, is the word used of the Trojan battalions before Diomedes in Book 5 (κλονέοντο, 5.93). Ajax has just killed Doryclus, a bastard son of Priam, and wounded four more (11.489–491; see [[doryclus]] and [[ajax]]). The flood is the last Achaean advance of the book. Fifty lines later Zeus puts Ajax himself to flight (11.544; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).

The narrative turns from the river of the simile to a real one. Hector does not yet know what Ajax is doing, “since he was fighting on the left of all the battle, beside the banks of the river Scamander” (11.497–499; see [[scamander]]).
