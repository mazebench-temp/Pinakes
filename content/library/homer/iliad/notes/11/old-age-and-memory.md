**The wish.** Nestor turns from Achilles' indifference to himself:

{{quote:3dd011ed-771e-5d09-b4c2-36ba44019499}}

In Book 7 the story of Ereuthalion began with a wish to be young (7.132–133) and ended with this verse (7.157); here the verse opens the story, as it will again at the funeral games (11.670 = 7.157; compare 23.629; see the note on Book 7 and [[such-was-i-if-ever]]). The verse before it is a verse of old age: the Hymn to Aphrodite uses it of Tithonus, and in the Odyssey Odysseus, disguised as an old beggar, asks for the bow to see whether he still has strength “such as there was before in his supple limbs” (*Odyssey* 21.282–284; see Outside Homer below).

**The story.** The war with the Epeians is the longest of Nestor's tales of his youth (11.670–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). In Book 1 he held up the men who fought the Centaurs and heeded his counsel (1.260–273); in Book 7 he told how he alone faced Ereuthalion (7.132–160). Here he tells of a young man whose father hid his horses and would not let him arm, who went to war on foot all the same, killed the leader of the horsemen, and drove the enemy before him (11.717–761). It is told to a young man about a young man: Patroclus is to persuade Achilles, or to go out himself and save the army (11.790–803; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Nestor's going out ended with thanks “to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.761); Patroclus' will end in his death (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Between the story and the advice comes the old man's doubt, “Such was I among men, if that was ever so” (11.762).

**The memory of Phthia.** The second memory is of something Nestor witnessed. He and Odysseus came to Peleus' house to gather the army, and heard the charges the fathers gave their sons, Peleus' to Achilles, “always to be the best and to excel above the others”, and Menoetius' to Patroclus, to counsel Achilles (11.765–789; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]], [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[retelling]]). The memory becomes a reproach: “So the old man charged you, but you forget” (11.790), the verse with which Odysseus reminded Achilles of his father's charge in Book 9 (9.259). Odysseus, Phoenix and Nestor all remember that day, and each remembers the charge his purpose needs (see [[kindliness-is-better]]). The old man remembers for the young what they have forgotten.

**The cup.** Before the speech the narrator gives Nestor a feat of strength. His cup, studded with gold, was so heavy that another man could barely move it from the table when it was full, “but Nestor, the old man, lifted it without toil” (11.636–637; see [[the-cup-of-nestor]] and [[geron]]). The poem does not reconcile it with his complaint (see the translation's note on 11.636–637).

## Outside Homer

The verse in which Nestor says that his strength is gone, ἔσθʼ οἵη πάρος ἔσκεν ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσιν (11.669), is used in the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite of Tithonus. Dawn asked Zeus to make him immortal but forgot to ask for youth; when he grew old she shut him in a chamber, where his voice flows on endlessly, but there is no strength in him “such as there was before in his supple limbs” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 5.218–238). Book 11 begins with Dawn rising from his bed (11.1; see [[tithonus]]).
