ὡς δʼ ὅτε πῦρ ἀΐδηλον ἐν ἀξύλῳ ἐμπέσῃ ὕλῃ: “and as when destroying fire falls on thick, uncut woodland”. The opening words, πῦρ ἀΐδηλον, are those of the simile of Book 2 (2.455). ἄξυλος, used only here as an adjective, is usually explained as woodland where no timber has been cut, dense and full of fuel; as a name it belongs to Axylus, killed by Diomedes (6.12; see the translation's note on 11.155). εἰλυφόων ἄνεμος, “the whirling wind”, carries the fire everywhere, and the thickets (θάμνοι) “fall uprooted” (πρόρριζοι), pressed by its onrush. The tenor: “so under Agamemnon, son of Atreus, fell the heads of the fleeing Trojans” (11.158–159).

The fire of Book 2 was seen from far off, the gleam of an army's bronze on the march. This fire is seen close, by what it does. The likeness is the falling: thickets that fall uprooted, heads that fall under Agamemnon, πίπτουσιν, πῖπτε (11.157–158). The aftermath follows in the narrative. Horses rattle empty chariots along the bridges of war, missing their charioteers, who lie on the ground, “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.159–162; see [[vultures]] and [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]).

Later fires belong to later heroes. Hector rages like a deadly fire in the thickets of a mountain forest (15.605–606), and Achilles, at the height of his own day of killing, sweeps over the plain like a fire in the glens of a dry mountain, where the wind whirls the flame about (εἰλυφάζει, 20.490–494). The verb of the whirling wind is the one used here, and so is the half-verse that ends both men's killing, their hands spattered with gore (11.169 ≈ 20.503).
