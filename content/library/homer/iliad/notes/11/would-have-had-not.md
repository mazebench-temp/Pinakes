summary: Three times in Book 11 the story stops at the edge of another outcome. The Achaeans would have fled to their ships had not Odysseus called to Diomedes (11.310–312); they would not yet have given ground had not Paris wounded Machaon (11.504–507); and in Nestor's story, he would have killed the Moliones had not Poseidon hidden them in mist (11.750–752).

**A man turns it.** “Then there would have been ruin, and deeds past mending would have been done, and the Achaeans would have fled and fallen among their ships, had not Odysseus called to Diomedes, son of Tydeus” (11.310–312). The first verse is the one with which Zeus' thunder turned Diomedes back in Book 8 (11.310 = 8.130), and there the rescuer was “the father of men and gods” (8.132). Here it is a man, and one of the Achaeans' own: Odysseus' call begins the stand of the two (see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]).

**Turned the other way.** The second is negative and turns against the Achaeans: “and the brilliant Achaeans would not yet have drawn back from their path, had not Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair, stopped Machaon, shepherd of the people, while he was the best in the fighting” (11.504–506). What prevents the outcome is an arrow, and it leads by way of Nestor's chariot to Patroclus' errand (see [[bow]] and [[machaon]]).

**In a character's mouth.** Nestor tells his own story with the narrator's device: “And I would have slain the two sons of Actor, the Moliones, had not their father, the wide-ruling earth-shaker, saved them from the war, hiding them in thick mist” (11.750–752). As in the narrator's own uses, a god saves men whom the tradition keeps alive: the twins will beat Nestor in the chariot race at Buprasium (23.638–642), and their sons lead Epeians at Troy (2.620–621; see [[the-moliones]] and [[poseidon]]). Aphrodite saved Aeneas in the same turn of phrase (5.311–312; see [[divine-rescue]]).
