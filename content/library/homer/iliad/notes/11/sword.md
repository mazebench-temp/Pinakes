summary: Agamemnon's sword has studs of gold and hangs in a silver sheath on golden straps (11.29–31). Every sword stroke that the book tells is his: he kills Antiphus with a blow beside the ear, cuts off Hippolochus' arms and head, and strikes Iphidamas on the neck.
grc: [ἧλοι]
en: [studs]
---

**Gold and silver.** The sword verse of the other armings ends ξίφος ἀργυρόηλον, “silver-studded sword” (3.334, 16.135, 19.372; see [[silver-studded]]). Agamemnon's breaks off after ξίφος, and the poet takes the two metals apart: “and on it studs of gold shone bright, and the sheath around it was of silver, fitted with golden straps” (11.29–31; see [[sheath]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). ἧλοι, “nails, studs”, are of gold on Achilles' scepter and on Nestor's cup too (χρυσείοις ἥλοισι πεπαρμένον, 1.246, 11.633; see [[studded-with-golden-nails]]).

**The king's sword.** In Book 11 only Agamemnon is shown striking with the sword, and he uses it at the closest quarters. He strikes Antiphus beside the ear “with his sword” and throws him from the chariot (11.109); he cuts off Hippolochus' arms “with the sword” and hews through his neck, and sends the trunk rolling like a log through the throng (11.145–147); he wrenches Iphidamas' spear from his hand and strikes him on the neck with his sword (ἄορι, 11.239–240); and standing over Iphidamas he cuts off Coon's head, though the weapon is not named (11.261). Wounded, he ranges through the ranks “with spear and sword and with great stones” while the blood is warm (11.264–266; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-aristeia]]). The same two verses are given to Hector when his turn comes, but no stroke of Hector's sword is told (11.540–541 = 11.264–265).
