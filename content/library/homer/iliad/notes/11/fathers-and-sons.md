summary: Sons pay for their fathers and die in pairs: Agamemnon kills the sons of Antimachus to punish their father's proposal to murder the envoys, and the sons of Merops die as their father foresaw. In Nestor's speech the fathers protect and charge their sons: Neleus hides his son's horses, Poseidon hides his twin sons in a mist, and Peleus and Menoetius send Achilles and Patroclus to Troy with their charges.

**Paying for the father.** The sons of Antimachus offer their father's treasure for their lives, and Agamemnon answers with their father's crime: he urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came on an embassy, “now you will pay for your father's shameful insult” (11.130–142; see [[sons-of-antimachus]], [[antimachus]] and [[apoina]]). The two Trojans whose conduct toward that embassy the poem records each lose two sons to Agamemnon on this day. Antimachus urged that the envoys be killed; Antenor “received them as guests and welcomed them in my halls” (3.207), and his sons Iphidamas and Coon die at Agamemnon's hands, “There the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the king, the son of Atreus, fulfilled their fate” (11.221–263; see [[antenor]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). The poem draws no moral from the pairing.

**Fathers who could not save them.** Diomedes kills the two sons of Merops of Percote, the seer who would not let his sons go to war, in the verses that named them in the Catalogue (11.328–334 = 2.831–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]): the father's foresight is borne out. Priam loses three sons in the book, Isus and Antiphus, “one a bastard and one born in wedlock”, together in one chariot (11.101–109), and the bastard Doryclus to Ajax (11.489–490; see [[nothos]]). Odysseus tells Socus that his father and his lady mother will not close his eyes (11.452–453). Cisseus, who reared his grandson Iphidamas and gave him his daughter, loses him too (11.221–245; see [[cisseus]]).

**Fathers in Nestor's story.** Nestor's war is a son's story told among fathers. Neleus had twelve sons, and Heracles killed all but Nestor (11.692–693); when the young Nestor drove the plunder home, “Neleus was glad at heart that so much had fallen to me, young as I was when I went to war” (11.683–684), and when the Epeians came, he would not let his last son arm and hid his horses (11.717–719; see [[neleus]]). On the other side, the twin sons of Actor would have died at Nestor's hands, “had not their father, the wide-ruling earth-shaker, saved them from the war, hiding them in thick mist” (11.750–752; see [[the-moliones]] and [[poseidon]]).

**The fathers' charges.** Then Nestor remembers the day the two fathers sent their sons to Troy. “Old Peleus charged his son Achilles always to be the best and to excel above the others”, the charge Hippolochus gave Glaucus (11.783–784 = 6.208); Menoetius charged Patroclus to give Achilles counsel, and Patroclus has forgotten (11.785–790; see [[always-to-be-the-best]], [[peleus]], [[menoetius]] and [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). Patroclus' errand to Nestor began as a companion's (11.611–617); Nestor turns it into a son's duty to his father's word.
