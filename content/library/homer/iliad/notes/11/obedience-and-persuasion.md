---
summary: Iris, Nestor and Patroclus obey at once, the sons of Merops would not obey their father and die, and Nestor's war began with a son who went to battle against his father's will. Menoetius' charge makes Patroclus the one whom Achilles should obey, and Patroclus, refusing Nestor's chair with “you will not persuade me”, is persuaded all the same.
passages:
  - 11.328-335 | The sons of Merops
  - 11.785-793 | Menoetius' charge and Nestor's advice
---

**Prompt obedience.** Iris carries Zeus' message to Hector and Nestor takes up the wounded Machaon in the same formula, “and did not disobey” (οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε, 11.195, 11.516; see the article above). Patroclus answers Achilles' call and goes: “So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (11.616), the verse with which he set out the mixing bowl for the envoys in Book 9 (9.205; see [[hetairos]]). Diomedes stays when Odysseus calls him, though he knows the cost: “I will stay, and I will endure; but short will be our profit of it, since Zeus the cloud-gatherer wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us” (11.317–319; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]).

**Sons who would not obey.** Diomedes kills the two sons of Merops of Percote, “who above all men knew the art of prophecy, and would not let his sons go into man-destroying war; but they would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (11.329–332 = 2.831–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Nestor's story turns on a disobedience with the other ending. Neleus would not let his son arm and hid his horses; the young Nestor went to the battle on foot all the same, “since so Athena guided the quarrel”, and came home the hero of the day (11.717–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). The poem does not set the two stories side by side; the book holds both.

**Whom Achilles should obey.** Menoetius' charge to his son reverses the usual order of rank. Achilles is higher in lineage and far the stronger, but Patroclus is the elder, and he is to counsel and direct him, “and he will obey you, to his good” (πείσεται εἰς ἀγαθόν περ, 11.786–789; see [[genee]] and [[menoetius]]). Nestor builds his advice on it: “you might say these things to wise-hearted Achilles, in the hope that he may listen”, for “good is the persuasion of a companion” (11.791–793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]] and [[peitho]]).

**Refused and persuaded.** Patroclus will not sit in Nestor's hut: “There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, and you will not persuade me” (οὐδέ με πείσεις, 11.648). The half-verse is the one with which Hector refused to sit with Helen, “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me; you will not persuade me” (6.360), and with which Achilles will refuse his mother when she tries to keep him from the battle (18.126). Each time the refusal is a man's haste to go where he is needed. Nestor persuades him all the same: the long speech “stirred the heart in his breast” (11.804; see [[thymos]]), and he runs to carry its advice to Achilles. One plea in the book fails outright. The sons of Antimachus speak to Agamemnon “with soothing words; but the voice they heard would not be soothed” (μειλιχίοις … ἀμείλικτον, 11.136–137; see [[apoina]]).
