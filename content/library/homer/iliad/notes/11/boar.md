**Hunted and hunting.** In the hunting similes of Book 11 the boar is the quarry that fights back. Hector's hounds are set on “a wild boar or a lion” (ἀγροτέρῳ συῒ καπρίῳ, 11.293; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]), the two beasts that can kill dogs and men. Diomedes and Odysseus, making their stand against Hector, are two boars that fall in high spirits on the hunting dogs and turn the hunt around (κάπρω, a dual, 11.324–326; see [[two-boars]]). Then Odysseus, alone, is the boar at bay: it comes out of a deep thicket whetting the white tusk in its curving jaws, with a gnashing of tusks, while dogs and young men close in from all sides and stand their ground (κάπριον, 11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]). The three similes go from the boar as game to the boar as attacker to the boar surrounded. κάπρος and κάπριος are the boar; σῦς is the pig, wild or tame (see [[like-a-boar]]).

**Droves of pigs.** The boar's tame kin appear in Nestor's story. From the cattle raid on Elis the Pylians drove home fifty herds of cattle, as many flocks of sheep, “as many droves of pigs” (συῶν συβόσια), as many wide herds of goats, and a hundred and fifty mares (11.677–681; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[raiding]]). συβόσιον, “a herd of swine”, is made from σῦς and βόσκω, “feed”. The verse of the pigs and goats returns whole in the Odyssey, where Eumaeus counts the herds of his absent master on the mainland (*Odyssey* 14.100–101 = 11.678–679, but for the first words).
