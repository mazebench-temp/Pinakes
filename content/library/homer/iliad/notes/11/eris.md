**The goddess.** Book 11 begins with Strife. Zeus sends her to the Achaean ships, “grievous, holding in her hands a portent of war”; she stands on Odysseus' ship in the middle of the line and shouts, and war becomes sweeter to the Achaeans than going home (11.3–14; see [[strife]] and [[teras]]). When the armies meet she is the only god among them: “Strife of the many groans rejoiced to look on; for she alone of the gods was there among the fighters” (11.73–74). πολύστονος, “of many groans”, is what she did in the battle of Book 4, going through the throng “increasing the groaning of men” (4.445). She takes in the sight the pleasure that Agamemnon and Zeus held against Achilles and Ares, “strife is always dear to you” (1.177, 5.891; see [[strife-is-always-dear]]). The capitalized forms are the goddess's; this entry claims only the common noun.

**Evil strife.** Cebriones, seeing Ajax drive the Trojans in confusion on another part of the field, turns Hector's chariot toward the place “where most of all horsemen and foot soldiers, joining in evil strife, are destroying one another” (κακὴν ἔριδα προβαλόντες, 11.528–530; see [[cebriones]]). The cranes of the simile in Book 3 “bring on their evil strife” for the Pygmies at dawn (κακὴν ἔριδα προφέρονται, 3.7; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). In both phrases strife is a thing put forward against an enemy, by birds on the Pygmies and here by men on one another.

**Strife and quarrel.** Nestor, telling of his war with the Epeians, calls the fighting a νεῖκος, “quarrel”, not an ἔρις, “since so Athena guided the quarrel” (11.721, 11.737; see [[neikos]] and [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). In Book 4 it was Strife herself who “cast the quarrel” into the midst of the armies (4.444).
