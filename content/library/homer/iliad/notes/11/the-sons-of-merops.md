Four of these verses are the Catalogue's (11.329–332 = 2.831–834). There the brothers are named, Adrestus and Amphius, and lead the men of Adrasteia, Apaesus, Pityeia and Tereia (2.828–830; see [[adrestus]] and [[amphius]]); here they are “two men, the best of their people” (11.328), and the poem does not name them. In the Catalogue the fates of black death “were leading them on” (2.834); in Book 11 the same verse is followed by their death (11.332–334; see [[ker-doom]] and [[black-death]]). Their father's foresight, “who above all men knew the art of prophecy”, could not keep them at home (see [[merops]] and [[prophecy]]).

They die in the stand that Diomedes and Odysseus make against Hector's charge, after Thymbraeus and Molion and before Hippodamus and Hypeirochus (11.320–335; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]], [[thymbraeus]] and [[hippodamus]]). The words are duals, ἑλέτην … ἀνέρε, “they took … two men” (11.328): a pair of Achaeans takes a pair of brothers in one chariot. Diomedes alone kills them and takes their “glorious armor” (11.333–334; see [[diomedes]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).
