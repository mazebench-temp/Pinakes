summary: The Achaeans leave their chariots at the ditch and go forward on foot (11.47–52). In the rout empty chariots rattle across the field, the wounded leaders are carried off one after another in their chariots, and Cebriones drives Hector over corpses and shields until the axle runs with blood (11.531–537). In Nestor's story chariots are the prize: four racing horses with their chariot, and fifty chariots taken in one day.

**Left at the ditch.** Each Achaean orders his charioteer “to hold the horses in good order there at the ditch”, and the men go forward on foot, “far ahead of the horsemen” (11.47–52), the reverse of Nestor's order in Book 4, chariots in front and foot behind (4.297–300). Polydamas will give the Trojans the same advice at the wall, in the same words (12.76–77 ≈ 11.48–49; see [[battle-order]]).

**Driver and fighter.** The chariot carries two. Isus, the bastard son, drives, and Antiphus stands beside him (παρέβασκε) when Agamemnon kills them both (11.103–109; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]). The sons of Antimachus are together in one chariot when the glossy reins slip from their hands (11.126–130). Agastrophus dies because his attendant held his horses too far off for him to escape: he “was greatly blinded in his heart” (11.339–342; see [[agastrophus]]). In Agamemnon's rout the horses run without their drivers: “many horses with high-arched necks rattled empty chariots along the bridges of war, missing their noble charioteers; but they lay on the ground, far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.159–162). The “bridges of war” are taken as the lanes between the ranks (see the translation's note on 4.371).

**Carried out of the battle.** Zeus' sign for Hector is the moment when Agamemnon “leaps into his chariot” (εἰς ἵππους ἅλεται, 11.192), and the chariot carries the wounded leaders away one by one. Agamemnon and Diomedes spring into their chariots and order their drivers to the ships in the same two verses (11.273–274 = 11.399–400), and Agamemnon's horses fly “not unwilling”, their chests flecked with foam, carrying “the worn-out king away from the battle” (11.280–283). Odysseus is led out until his attendant drives the horses near (11.487–488). Nestor takes Machaon up into his chariot (11.511–520), and his mares, sweating, carry them past Achilles' ship (11.597–598, 11.615; see [[chariot-journey]]). At Nestor's hut Eurymedon unyokes the horses (11.620–621).

The chariot and its horses trample the dead. The same verses, from the corpses and shields to the drops from the tires, are given to Achilles' chariot in Book 20, followed there by the verse of Agamemnon's gore-spattered hands (11.534–537 ≈ 20.499–502; 11.169 ≈ 20.503; see [[cebriones]] and [[hector]]).

**Nestor's chariots.** In Nestor's story the chariot is wealth and glory. Augeas kept four prize-winning horses “with their chariot” that Neleus had sent to race for a tripod, and sent the driver away grieving (11.698–702; see [[contests]] and [[augeas]]). Neleus hid his son's horses, and Nestor fought on foot among the horsemen; he killed Mulius, leapt into his chariot, and took fifty chariots, with two men biting the ground beside each (11.717–721, 11.738–749; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]).
