---
summary: Grief comes twice: it veils the eyes of Coon when he sees his brother killed, and Nestor tells Patroclus that Achilles “knows nothing of the grief” that has risen throughout the army.
grc: [πένθεος]
passages:
  - 11.248-253 | Coon wounds Agamemnon
  - 11.656-664 | Nestor counts the wounded
---

**Grief over the eyes.** When Agamemnon kills Iphidamas, his eldest brother Coon sees it, and “strong grief veiled his eyes for his fallen brother” (κρατερόν ῥά ἑ πένθος ὀφθαλμοὺς ἐκάλυψε, 11.248–250; see [[coon]] and [[kasignetos]]). The verb is the one the poem uses of death, when darkness covers a man's eyes (4.461, 6.11), and of Hector's swoon a hundred lines later, “and black night covered his eyes” (11.356; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Grief does to Coon's eyes what death will do in a few lines more, for he wounds Agamemnon and is killed over his brother's body (11.251–261). The same image returns for the grief of the living, when “a black cloud of grief” covers Hector at the news of Podes' death and Achilles at the news of Patroclus' (ἄχεος νεφέλη, 17.591, 18.22; see [[achos]]).

**Grief in the army.** Nestor answers Achilles' question with a reproach: “Why does Achilles grieve like this for the sons of the Achaeans, as many as have been struck by missiles? He knows nothing of the grief, how great it has risen throughout the army” (11.656–658). Then he counts the wounded (11.658–664; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[pity]]). The grief he means is the army's, for its wounded and its dead (see [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]).
