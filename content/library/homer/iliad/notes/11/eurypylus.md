**At Ajax's side.** “And when the splendid son of Euaemon caught sight of him, / Eurypylus, hard pressed by thick missiles”, he comes to stand beside the retreating Ajax, and his spear strikes Apisaon, son of Phausius, “in the liver beneath the midriff” (11.575–579; see [[ajax]], [[euaemon]] and [[apisaon]]). As he strips the armor, Paris draws his bow: the arrow strikes his right thigh, “and the reed broke off, and weighed the thigh down” (11.580–584; see [[paris]] and [[arrows]]). It is the third wound of the day from Paris' bow, after Diomedes' foot and Machaon's shoulder (11.376–377, 11.507; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**The rally.** He draws back among his companions and shouts in the two verses with which the wounded Agamemnon left the field (11.586–587 = 11.275–276):

{{quote:31b14814-5fd1-50cf-8a32-a1ee47f7f09f}}

The wounded man saves the retreat. His companions form up with their shields slanted against their shoulders and their spears raised, and Ajax turns and stands among them (11.592–595). Nestor, counting the wounded at his hut, names Eurypylus too (11.662), though he left the field with Machaon before Paris shot him (11.516–520; see [[nestor]]).

**Limping.** Running back from Nestor's hut, Patroclus meets him at Odysseus' ships, where the place of assembly and the altars are, “limping out of the war”, the sweat pouring from his head and shoulders and the dark blood from his wound, “but his mind was steady” (11.806–813; see [[patroclus]]). Patroclus pities him (ᾤκτειρε, 11.814; see [[pity]]) and asks whether the Achaeans can still hold back Hector. No longer, Eurypylus answers: “they will fall among the black ships”, for the best lie wounded (11.823–827). Then he asks for help:

{{quote:524186f8-1c55-5115-8eca-26c841131ca5}}

Both healers of the army are out of reach, Machaon wounded in the huts and Podalirius on the plain (11.833–836; see [[machaon]] and [[podalirius]]).

**Tended.** Patroclus is carrying Nestor's word to Achilles, but “even so I will not leave you in your distress” (11.841). He takes the shepherd of the people under the chest, leads him to the hut, lays him on oxhides, cuts out the arrow with a knife, washes the wound with warm water and lays on a bitter, pain-killing root, “and the wound dried, and the blood stopped” (11.842–848; see [[healing]]). The half-verse is the one that brought on Agamemnon's pains (11.267); here the root has stayed them. Book 12 opens with Patroclus still tending him in the huts (12.1–2), and he stays, talking and laying on drugs, until the Trojans storm the wall; then he leaves him to his attendant and runs to Achilles with Nestor's words (15.390–404; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]).
