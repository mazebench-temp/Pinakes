summary: “And deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (11.403): the participle of vexation, without μέγα, opens Odysseus' monologue when he is left alone in the battle, in a whole verse that recurs for every speech a hero makes to his own heart.
grc: ["ὀχθήσας δʼ ἄρα εἶπε"]
en: [deeply troubled]
---

ὀχθήσας δʼ ἄρα εἶπε πρὸς ὃν μεγαλήτορα θυμόν, “and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (11.403). In the reply formula the participle is μέγʼ ὀχθήσας, and it brings in an answer to someone else. Here it brings in a speech to oneself, a monologue. The verse is used seven times in the Iliad, always of a man alone, troubled by what he sees or must decide: Odysseus cut off by the Trojans (11.403), Menelaus over the body of Patroclus (17.90), Achilles seeing the Achaeans flee (18.5), Achilles when Aeneas vanishes from before him (20.343), Achilles seeing Lycaon again (21.53), Agenor waiting for Achilles (21.552), and Hector before the Scaean gates (22.98). In the Odyssey it is Odysseus' verse in the storm, four times in one book (Odyssey 5.298, 5.355, 5.407, 5.464). See [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[thymos]].
