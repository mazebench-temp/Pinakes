summary: Nestor's story of his war with the Epeians, the longest paradigm in the poem (11.670–762), is framed by the wish to be young again and by “Such was I among men, if that was ever so”, and turned against Achilles, who “will have the profit of his prowess alone”. Its hero is a young man forbidden by his father to fight, who went out on foot and saved his people; the plan that follows sends Patroclus out in the same way.

**The frame.** Nestor begins with the wounded and with Achilles, who “does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.656–668), and then turns to himself: his strength “is not such as it was before in my supple limbs”. “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (11.668–670) is the verse with which he closed his story of Ereuthalion (11.670 = 7.157), and he will use it again before the story of the games at Buprasium (23.629; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). The story runs for ninety verses, and he closes it with a verse of his own: “Such was I among men, if that was ever so. But Achilles will have the profit of his prowess alone; and yet I think he will weep much hereafter, when the army has perished” (11.762–764; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]] and [[ring-composition]]).

**Fitted to the case.** The story is not of a duel but of a whole war: a raid on the cattle of Elis, the division of the spoil, the Epeian siege of Thryoessa, Athena's call by night, and a battle in which young Nestor kills Mulius, takes fifty chariots and drives the Epeians back to Buprasium, and all give thanks “to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.670–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Its point is put in the two verses that follow it. Nestor used his strength for his people, and Achilles keeps his to himself (ἀρετή, “prowess”, 11.763; see [[arete]]). Some details fit the present closely. Neleus would not let his son arm and hid his horses, “for he said I knew nothing yet of the works of war”, yet Nestor went out on foot and stood out among the horsemen (11.717–721): a young man held back by an elder who goes out all the same. The man listening is the one who will go out at the end of this speech, in another man's armor, with the Myrmidons behind him (11.794–803).

**For whom?** The speech is spoken to Patroclus, but its rebuke is aimed at Achilles, and its last part, the memory of Peleus' and Menoetius' charges, is aimed at Patroclus himself, who is to persuade Achilles or to go in his place (11.765–803; see [[retelling]]). Like Phoenix's story of Meleager, told to Achilles in Book 9 (9.524–605; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]), it is a long account of an old war told to move a man to fight, and it moves Patroclus, “and he went running along the ships to Achilles” (11.804–805). Whether the poet took the story from tradition or shaped it, or invented it, for this moment, as M. M. Willcock argued of other paradigms, cannot be known; Strabo, who retells it step by step, retells Homer's account (8.3.28; see [[nestor]]).
