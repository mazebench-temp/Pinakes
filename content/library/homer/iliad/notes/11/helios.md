summary: The setting of the sun is the term of Zeus' promise to Hector, who will kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194); in Nestor's story the Pylians join battle “when the shining sun rose up above the earth” (11.735).
passages:
  - 11.191-194 | The term of Zeus' promise
---

**The term.** Zeus sends word to Hector that when Agamemnon is wounded he will give him power to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships, / and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194), and Iris repeats it (11.208–209; see [[zeus]] and [[iris]]). The day that began at 11.1 is the longest of the poem. The sun that Zeus names here goes down only in Book 18, when Hera sends it unwilling into the streams of Oceanus and the Achaeans rest from the battle (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]], [[sunset-lines]] and [[oceanus]]). Zeus repeats the term during the fight over Patroclus' body (17.454–455 ≈ 11.193–194).

**Midday.** Book 8 marked the middle of its day by the sun, which “stood astride the middle of the sky” (8.68). Book 11 repeats the verses of the morning before it (11.84–85 = 8.66–67), but marks the turn of the day by the woodcutter's meal, when the Danaans break the Trojan battalions (11.86–90; see [[eos]]).

**Nestor's sunrise.** In Nestor's story the Pylians reach the Alpheus at midday (ἔνδιοι, 11.726), sacrifice, eat and sleep in their armor, and join battle at sunrise, “when the shining sun rose up above the earth” (11.735; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). ἠέλιος φαέθων, “the shining sun”, occurs only here in the Iliad and four times in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.479, 11.16, 19.441, 22.388).
