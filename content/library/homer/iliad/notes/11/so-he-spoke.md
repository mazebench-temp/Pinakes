---
summary: The closings of Book 11 turn speeches into blows: “He spoke, and” is followed again and again by a spear thrust. Iris and Nestor obey in the verse of obedience, Patroclus obeys Achilles in the verse of Books 1 and 9, and Nestor's long speech ends with the verse that once sent Machaon to heal Menelaus: “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast” (11.804).
---

The closings of Book 11: 11.143, 195, 210, 280, 291, 320, 349, 368, 396, 434, 446, 456, 472, 516, 531, 592, 616, 804, 842.

**Words into blows.** ἦ, καί, “He spoke, and”, leads straight from the speech to a killing: Agamemnon thrusts Peisander from the chariot with his spear (11.143), Diomedes thrusts Thymbraeus from his (11.320), casts his spear at Hector (ἦ ῥα, καί, 11.349), and begins to strip Agastrophus (11.368), and Odysseus plants his spear in the back of the fleeing Socus (11.446). “So saying” (ὣς εἰπών) is followed by Socus' thrust at Odysseus' shield and by Odysseus drawing Socus' spear from his own flesh (11.434, 11.456). At the end of the book the same ἦ, καί turns Patroclus' words to the wounded Eurypylus into care: “He spoke, and taking the shepherd of the people beneath the chest he led him to the hut” (11.842–843; see [[healing]]).

**Obedience.** “So he spoke, and swift Iris, wind-footed, did not disobey” (11.195), and “So he spoke, and the Gerenian horseman Nestor did not disobey” (11.516 = 8.112): the god's messenger obeys Zeus, and the old man obeys Idomeneus' call to carry Machaon away (see [[wind-footed]] and [[gerenian-horseman]]). “So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (11.616) is the verse with which he brought Briseis out in Book 1 and set out the mixing bowl in Book 9 (1.345, 9.205); this time obedience sends him to Nestor's hut (see [[patroclus]]). Hector's rally closes with the whole verse of the exhortation, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (11.291; see [[exhortation]]).

**The stirred heart.** Nestor's speech, the longest in the book, ends: “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast; and he went running along the ships to Achilles, grandson of Aeacus” (11.804–805). The verse was used in Book 4 when Talthybius summoned Machaon to heal Menelaus (4.208); here it moves the man who will end the book doing a healer's work, and who will put Nestor's plan to Achilles (see [[message-repeated]]). The same words close Aphrodite's summons to Helen in Book 3, of Helen's heart (3.395).

**Speeches left open.** Several speeches have no closing at all, and the reply follows at once: Odysseus' call to Diomedes (11.313–315), Paris' boast (11.380–383), Patroclus' refusal to sit (11.648–654), and Eurypylus' answer to Patroclus (11.823–836). The sons of Antimachus' plea is closed in the dual and turned toward its failure: “So the two, weeping, addressed the king with soothing words; but the voice they heard would not be soothed” (11.136–137; see [[supplication]]). Odysseus' monologue ends in the verse of deliberation, “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart” (11.411; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).
