---
summary: Once in Book 11 a speech is introduced with “winged words”: Patroclus, meeting the wounded Eurypylus, pities him, “and lamenting addressed him with winged words” (11.814–815).
---

Patroclus is running back to Achilles with Nestor's plan when he meets Eurypylus limping out of the battle, an arrow in his thigh and the dark blood gushing from the wound. “And seeing him the valiant son of Menoetius pitied him, and lamenting addressed him with winged words” (11.814–815). The speech is drawn out of him by what he sees and feels, as Paolo Vivante observed of the formula: “Ah, wretched leaders and rulers of the Danaans, so you were destined, far from your friends and your native land, to glut the swift dogs in Troy with your white fat” (11.816–818; see [[pity]] and [[patroclus]]). The whole verse, “and lamenting addressed him with winged words”, brought in Ares' complaint to Zeus when he showed him the immortal blood running from his wound (5.870–871), and in the Odyssey it is used three times more, for Eurylochus, the shade of Heracles and Eumaeus (Odyssey 10.265, 11.616, 16.22). None of the other speeches of Book 11 is introduced with it (see [[answering-him]] and [[so-he-spoke]]).
