**Why Nestor tells it.** Nestor has told the war of his youth and ended on Achilles, who “will have the profit of his prowess alone” (11.762–764; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Then he turns to Patroclus with the words with which Odysseus began his reminder in the embassy: “My friend, surely Menoetius charged you thus / on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon” (11.765–766; compare 9.252–253). He and Odysseus were in the hall and heard every word (11.767–768). He describes the scene they found: Peleus burning the thighs of an ox to Zeus in the courtyard and pouring wine from a golden goblet, the two young men busy with the meat, and Achilles springing up in amazement to lead the visitors in and set before them “good cheer … as is the custom with guests” (11.772–779; 11.777 = 9.193; see [[sacrifice]], [[libation]] and [[xenia]]).

**Two charges.** The fathers give their sons two different parts. Peleus tells Achilles “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (11.784), the charge Hippolochus gave Glaucus (6.208; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]). Menoetius gives Patroclus the part of the elder and the adviser:

{{quote:e062ce9f-0c28-590a-925b-a8d710d2823f}}

Menoetius' words are a speech within Nestor's speech, and the only words of his in the poem (see [[menoetius]]). Then comes the reproach of the embassy, now aimed at the son of Menoetius: “So the old man charged you, but you forget” (11.790 = 9.259).

**What it leads to.** Nestor asks Patroclus to try persuasion, “Good is the persuasion of a companion” (11.793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]), and if Achilles will not fight, to go out in his place, wearing his armor (11.794–803). The speech stirs Patroclus' heart, and he runs back toward Achilles (11.804–805). He repeats Nestor's words about persuasion when he leaves Eurypylus (15.403–404 = 11.792–793), and in Book 16 he gives Achilles the counsel his father charged him to give, with Nestor's plan (16.21–45). Achilles listens, as Menoetius said he would, and sends him out to die (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[patroclus]]). Of the three tellings of the day, Nestor's is the only one that gives Menoetius' charge, and the only one told to Patroclus (see [[peleus]]).
