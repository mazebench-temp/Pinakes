---
summary: Two errands frame Book 11. Zeus sends Iris from Ida to Hector, and she goes, finds him, delivers the message and leaves, in Iris' own verses (11.181–210). Achilles sends Patroclus to Nestor to learn the name of a wounded man; Patroclus will not sit, calls himself a messenger, and is held first by Nestor's long answer and then by the wounded Eurypylus, so that the errand is not finished in the book (11.599–848).

**Iris.** The scene keeps every step. The sender is on Ida, “with the lightning in his hands”, and “he urged on golden-winged Iris to carry a message”, the verse with which he sent her against Hera and Athena in Book 8 (11.184–185; 11.185 = 8.398; see [[golden-winged]]). He gives the message in direct speech, “Go, swift Iris” (11.186–194; see [[message-repeated]]). The messenger “did not disobey” (11.195), goes “down from the mountains of Ida to sacred Ilios”, and finds (εὗρε) Hector “standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot” (11.196–198); she stands near and speaks in Zeus' words, and leaves: “So saying, swift-footed Iris went away” (11.210 = 8.425; see [[iris]]). No answer goes back. Hector acts on the word at once, leaping from his chariot to rally the army (11.211–214).

**Patroclus.** The second errand begins with the sender watching. Achilles, standing on the stern of his ship, sees Nestor's mares carry a wounded man past, calls Patroclus out of the hut, and sends him to ask Nestor who the man is: from behind he looked like Machaon (11.599–615). Patroclus “obeyed his dear companion” and runs along the huts and ships (11.616–617), in the verse with which he obeyed Achilles in Books 1 and 9 (11.616 = 1.345 = 9.205; see [[so-he-spoke]]). He finds Nestor and Machaon drinking in the hut, and stands at the door (11.642–644). Nestor rises to take him by the hand and seat him, and he refuses: he must go back “as a messenger” (ἄγγελος, 11.652), and the man who sent him is quick to blame (11.647–654; see [[visit-scene]]). The answer he receives is the longest speech in the book, with a plan for Achilles in it (11.656–803; see [[nestor]] and [[message-repeated]]). He runs back along the ships (11.804–805), but at the ships of Odysseus he meets Eurypylus wounded, and pity holds him (11.806–841; see [[healing]]). The book ends with the messenger tending a wound; he comes to Achilles at the beginning of Book 16, weeping (16.2–3; see [[patroclus]]).

**Athena in Nestor's story.** In Nestor's war the gods' messenger is Athena: “Athena came running from Olympus as a messenger to us, to arm, by night” (ἄγγελος ἦλθε θέουσʼ ἀπʼ Ὀλύμπου θωρήσσεσθαι, 11.714–716). The verse returns when Iris comes running from Olympus to bid Achilles arm, sent secretly by Hera (18.166–168; see [[athena]]).
