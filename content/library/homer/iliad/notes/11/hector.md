summary: Zeus keeps Hector out of Agamemnon's way and sends Iris to promise him the power to kill until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down. When Agamemnon is wounded, Hector charges and kills nine Achaean leaders; stunned by Diomedes' spear on his helmet, he recovers, and for the rest of the book he avoids Telamonian Ajax.

**The baleful star.** The Trojans form up on the rise of the plain “around great Hector and noble Polydamas” (11.56–57; see [[polydamas]]), and Hector moves now among the foremost, now among the hindmost, giving orders:

{{quote:04a43c5b-c561-55f9-b041-352db5522f91}}

οὔλιος, “baleful”, occurs only here, and the star is usually taken as the Dog Star, which brings fever (see the translation's note on 11.62 and [[the-baleful-star]]). The flash of his bronze is the flash of the spears at Diomedes' hut, “like the lightning of father Zeus” (10.154). The likeness will turn against him: when Achilles comes at him in Book 22, it is Achilles' bronze that shines like the Dog Star, an evil sign (22.26–31).

**Kept out of the way.** As Agamemnon breaks the Trojan line, “Zeus drew Hector out from the missiles and from the dust, / from the slaughter of men, from the blood and from the tumult” (11.163–164). The narrator gives no reason; Zeus gives it some twenty lines later. From the peaks of Ida he sends Iris: as long as Agamemnon rages among the champions, Hector is to hold back and keep the army fighting (see [[iris]], [[message-repeated]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

{{quote:3089905e-855c-591b-bd0a-aa0c0ec6c9d8}}

Iris finds him “standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot” (11.198), as Agamemnon found Diomedes (4.366), and calls him “Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel” (11.200), the address with which Helenus brought him the gods' plan in Book 7 (7.47; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]). She gives him Zeus' words, turned to “you” (11.200–209). The promise sets the term of the day that began at dawn (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). Hector lays hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship in Book 15 (15.704–725), and the sun goes down only when Hera sends it, unwilling, to the streams of Ocean (18.239–242). After Patroclus' death Zeus makes the promise again, for the Trojans, in the same words (17.453–455).

**The charge.** Hector leaps down with his spears and rouses the army, in the four verses with which he rallied it in Books 5 and 6 (11.211–214 = 5.494–497 = 6.103–106). He keeps to Zeus' word, and the sons of Antenor, not Hector, meet Agamemnon (11.216–263; see [[iphidamas]] and [[coon]]). When he sees Agamemnon leave the field, he calls out:

{{quote:af17aaa5-0137-5cdb-ac56-63ac833d8040}}

He sets the Trojans on like a hunter setting his hounds on a boar or a lion, “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]], [[equal-of-ares]] and [[ruin-of-mortals]]), and falls on the battle like a storm wind on the sea (11.297–298; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]). The narrator asks, as he asked of Hector and Ares in Book 5 and will ask of Patroclus, “whom first, whom last did he slay” (11.299–300; 5.703–704, 16.692–693; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]), and names nine leaders of the Danaans before the multitude, whom he beats down as the West Wind batters the clouds (11.301–309; see [[asaeus]], [[autonous]], [[opites]], [[dolops]], [[opheltius-the-achaean]], [[agelaus-the-achaean]], [[aesymnus]], [[orus]], [[hipponous]] and [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]]). The Achaeans would have fallen among their ships, had Odysseus not called Diomedes to stand with him (11.310–315; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]).

**Stunned.** Hector “was quick to see them across the ranks, and rushed at them / shouting” (11.343–344), in nearly the words of his charge beside Ares (5.590–591). Diomedes' spear strikes the top of his helmet, and “bronze glanced off bronze”: the helmet was “three-layered”, and Apollo had given it to him (11.349–353; see [[helmet]], [[three-layered]] and [[apollo]]). Hector runs back a measureless way, sinks to his knees, leans on the earth, “and black night covered his eyes” (11.354–356), the swoon of Aeneas under Diomedes' stone (5.309–310; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). While Diomedes goes after his spear, Hector comes to himself and escapes in his chariot (11.357–360). Diomedes' taunt, “now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you” (11.362–367), is spoken word for word by Achilles when Apollo snatches Hector away from him in Book 20 (20.449–454). In Book 8 Hector boasted that he would learn whether Diomedes could drive him back from the ships (8.532–538); their next meeting leaves Hector on his knees.

**Away from Ajax.** He does not hear of Ajax's rout of the Trojans, for he is fighting on the left, by the Scamander, “doing grim deeds / with his spear and his horsemanship” (11.497–503; see [[grim-deeds]] and [[scamander]]). His charioteer Cebriones sees the turmoil, knows Ajax by his broad shield, and drives him there over corpses and shields (11.521–537; see [[cebriones]]). Hector ranges through the other ranks in the verses that told Agamemnon's last fighting (11.540–541 = 11.264–265), “but he avoided battle with Ajax, son of Telamon” (11.542). A verse that Aristotle quotes after it, found in no manuscript, gives the reason: “for Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (11.543; see [[aristotle-nemesis]] and [[nemesis]]). Ajax had the better of their duel (7.244–272; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**Huge Hector.** The book ends far from him, but he is in the question Patroclus puts to the wounded Eurypylus: will the Achaeans “still somehow hold back huge Hector, / or will they now perish, beaten down by his spear?” (11.820–821; see [[huge]]). πελώριος, “huge”, is elsewhere a word for Ajax and for Ares (3.229, 7.208).
