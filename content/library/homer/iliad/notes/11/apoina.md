summary: Agamemnon kills two sons of Priam whom Achilles had once captured on Ida “and had released them for a ransom”, and refuses the ransom that the sons of Antimachus offer in the verses of Adrastus and Dolon, because their father once urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus.

**Ransomed once.** Isus and Antiphus, two sons of Priam in one chariot, were men whom Achilles “had bound with pliant willow shoots on the slopes of Ida, catching them as they herded sheep, and had released them for a ransom” (ἔλυσεν ἀποίνων, 11.104–106). Agamemnon recognizes them as he strips them, for he had seen them by the ships when Achilles brought them in (11.110–112; see [[isus]], [[antiphus-son-of-priam]] and [[raiding]]). The ransom bought them their lives for a while; this time they are killed (see the translation's note on 11.101–112). In Book 21 Achilles will tell Lycaon, another son of Priam whom he once took and sold, that before Patroclus died he liked to spare the Trojans and took many alive and sold them, but that now none will escape death, and above all the sons of Priam (περὶ δʼ αὖ Πριάμοιό γε παίδων, 21.99–105).

**Ransom refused.** Peisander and Hippolochus, the sons of Antimachus, lose their reins in the rout, and Agamemnon rushes at them like a lion (11.122–129):

Their plea is made of the verses of earlier suppliants: the first and fourth lines are Adrastus' to Menelaus (11.131, 11.134 = 6.46, 6.49), and the last three Dolon's to Odysseus and Diomedes (11.133–135 ≈ 10.379–381; see [[supplication]], [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[dolon]]). The father's ransom would be ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom”, the words of the amends Agamemnon himself offered Achilles (9.120). Every one of these pleas is refused, and the man who refuses has a reason: Agamemnon would spare no Trojan, “not even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb” (6.57–60), and Diomedes would not let a spy go back to spy again (10.447–453). Here the reason is the father's. Antimachus “once in the Trojans' assembly urged that Menelaus, when he came on an embassy with godlike Odysseus, be killed on the spot”, and “now you will pay for your father's shameful insult” (11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]], [[lobe]] and [[antimachus]]). The father who took “Alexander's gold, splendid gifts” to keep Helen in Troy (11.123–125) offers gold for his sons, and it is not taken (see [[sons-of-antimachus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).
