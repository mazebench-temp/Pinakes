Nestor and Idomeneus, the first two elders whom Agamemnon called to his sacrifice (2.405), fight together on the left of the battle, by the banks of the Scamander, where the heads of men fall thickest, “and an unquenchable cry went up / around great Nestor and warlike Idomeneus” (11.499–501; see [[nestor]] and [[warlike]]). Hector is among them there, “doing grim deeds” (11.502; see [[grim-deeds]]). When an arrow of Paris strikes Machaon in the right shoulder and the Achaeans fear that the enemy may take him, Idomeneus speaks at once:

{{quote:1e9cd183-b6c1-5170-8faa-5d5a2a967fb4}}

He greets Nestor with the address that Agamemnon and Odysseus used in the night (11.511 = 10.87, 10.555; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]). The reason he gives is in two skills, to cut out arrows and to spread on soothing drugs: what Machaon did for Menelaus (4.213–219; see [[machaon]]), and what Patroclus does for Eurypylus at the end of the book (11.844–848; see [[healing]]). The verse on the healer's worth became proverbial: Plato's Alcibiades quotes it to the doctor Eryximachus at Agathon's party (*Symposium* 214b; see [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]). Nestor obeys without a word (11.516), and Idomeneus is not named again in the book. His great day comes in the battle at the ships in Book 13.
