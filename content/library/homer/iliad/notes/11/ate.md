summary: The verb comes once, of a minor Trojan. Agastrophus, wounded by Diomedes, cannot escape because his attendant is holding his horses far off: “he was greatly blinded in his heart”, the half-verse that Phoenix used of Oeneus, who forgot Artemis.
passages:
  - 11.338-342 | Agastrophus
---

Diomedes wounds Agastrophus, son of Paeon, in the hip, “and his horses were not near for him to escape; he was greatly blinded in his heart. For his attendant held them some way off, and he on foot rushed through the champions, until he lost his dear life” (11.339–342; see [[agastrophus]] and [[diomedes]]). ἀάσατο δὲ μέγα θυμῷ is the half-verse with which Phoenix explained Oeneus' failure to sacrifice to Artemis (9.537; see the note on Book 9 and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). There it named a king's forgetting of a goddess; here it names a fighter's mistake. Agastrophus went among the champions on foot and left his chariot, his way out, beyond reach (see the translation's note on 11.340). No god is said to have blinded him. The narrator gives the blindness its cause in the circumstances and its end in his death, and the word's two senses, the folly and the ruin it brings, meet in a single man (see the article above).

Diomedes strips him, and Paris, shooting from the tomb of Ilus while Diomedes is taking the corselet, wounds Diomedes in his turn (11.368–378; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).
