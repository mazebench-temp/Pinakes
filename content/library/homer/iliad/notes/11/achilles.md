summary: Achilles watches the rout from the stern of his ship, and when Nestor's chariot carries a wounded man past he sends Patroclus to ask who it is, the errand that leads to Patroclus' death. The rest of the book speaks about him: Patroclus fears his temper, Nestor charges that he neither cares for the Danaans nor pities them, remembers the young Achilles in Peleus' house, and proposes that Patroclus go out in his armor.

**At the end of the line.** Strife shouts from Odysseus' ship so that she is heard at both ends of the camp, where Ajax and Achilles “had hauled up their balanced ships … trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands” (11.5–9 = 8.222–226; see [[strife]]).

**What he spared.** Agamemnon kills Isus and Antiphus, sons of Priam whom Achilles had once caught herding sheep on Ida and released for ransom (11.101–112; see [[isus]], [[antiphus-son-of-priam]] and [[achilles-raids]]). Before Patroclus died, Achilles will tell Lycaon, he liked to spare Trojans and sell them; after, none escapes him (21.100–105). Nestor's captive Hecamede was taken “from Tenedos, when Achilles sacked it” (11.625; see [[hecamede]] and [[tenedos]]).

**The watcher.** He is seen for the first time since the embassy:

{{quote:c6473a4b-2cc8-50a2-a990-eb79462d9038}}

In Book 1 he sat by his ships and “longed for the war cry and for battle” (1.488–492); now he looks on (see [[watching-the-war]]). His first thought is of the Achaeans' need: “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees / beseeching me; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.609–610), the words in which Nestor, the night before, wanted Menelaus to go round the chiefs (10.117–118). The embassy has already brought him gifts and pleas. The verses have been taken as a sign that Book 9 was composed later, or as meaning that only now will the Achaeans come as suppliants in earnest (see the translation's note on 11.609–610 and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). His wish to know whether the wounded man is Machaon sends Patroclus to Nestor (11.611–615; see [[patroclus]] and [[machaon]]).

**As others see him.** Patroclus calls him “a dread man; he would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.654; see [[nemesis]]). Nestor says that he, “brave as he is, does not care for the Danaans or pity them”, and asks whether he is waiting for the ships to burn (11.665–667; see [[pity]]): that is what Achilles told Ajax he would wait for (9.650–655), in the answer that Odysseus left out of his report (9.677–692). He “will have the profit of his prowess alone”, Nestor says, and will weep when the army has perished (11.762–764; see [[arete]]).

**The young Achilles.** In Nestor's memory he is busy with Patroclus over the meat of his father's sacrifice. When Nestor and Odysseus stood in the doorway, “Achilles sprang up in amazement”, led them in and fed them, and was willing to go (11.776–782; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). He springs up so at the envoys in Book 9 (9.193), and once more when the ghost of Patroclus slips from his reaching hands (23.99–101). Peleus charged him “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (11.784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]); Menoetius set Patroclus beside him as counselor (11.786–789; see [[menoetius]]).

**The plan.** Nestor guesses that he may be shunning “some oracle” from his mother (11.794–795; see [[thetis]]); if so, let him at least send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons, dressed in Achilles' armor, “in the hope that, taking you for him, they may hold off from the war” (11.798–799; see [[myrmidons]]). Achilles has told the envoys of his two fates (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). When Patroclus puts the plan to him in the same words, he answers that no oracle holds him back (16.36–51).

**Chiron's pupil.** Eurypylus asks Patroclus for the drugs “which they say you have learned from Achilles, / whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs” (11.831–832; see [[chiron]] and [[healing]]). Only here does the Iliad give Achilles a healer's knowledge.
