summary: Diomedes stays when Odysseus calls, though he knows that Zeus means to give the Trojans power; he kills Thymbraeus, the sons of Merops and Agastrophus, and stuns Hector with a spear-cast to the helmet. Then Paris, shooting from the tomb of Ilus, pins his foot to the ground, and after a scornful answer to the archer he pulls out the arrow and drives back to the ships.

**“I will stay.”** When Odysseus calls him to stand (see [[odysseus]] and [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]), he answers: “I will stay, and I will endure; but short / will be our profit of it, since Zeus the cloud-gatherer / wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us” (11.317–319). In Book 8 he gave way to Zeus' thunderbolts when Nestor turned his horses, though three times he pondered turning back (8.133–171). Now he reads Zeus' will in the same way and stays (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[retreat]]).

**His kills.** He thrusts Thymbraeus from his chariot (11.320–321; see [[thymbraeus]]). He kills the two sons of the seer Merops, who would not let them go to war, as the Catalogue foretold in the same verses (11.328–334; 2.831–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). He wounds Agastrophus, son of Paeon, in the hip; his horses were held too far off for escape, and he died among the champions (11.338–342; see [[agastrophus]]).

**Hector's helmet.** Seeing Hector come on, he shudders and calls Odysseus to stand with him (11.345–348). His spear strikes the top of Hector's helmet, “but bronze glanced off bronze”, held off by the helmet that Phoebus Apollo had given him (11.349–353). Hector sinks to his knees, “and black night covered his eyes”; by the time Diomedes has gone after his spear, he has come to himself and escaped in his chariot (11.354–360; see [[hector]]). Diomedes shouts after him, “Once again you have escaped death now, you dog”, for “now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you” (11.362–363; see [[apollo]]). Achilles will shout the same six verses when Apollo snatches Hector from him in a mist (20.443–454 = 11.362–367). Diomedes ends with a hope: “I will surely finish you off when I meet you later, / if there is any god who is a helper to me as well” (11.365–366). ἐπιτάρροθος, “helper”, is the word Athena used in Book 5 of herself, as his father's helper and as his (5.808, 5.828; see [[athena]]). On this day no god helps him. Hector had boasted that at sunrise Diomedes would lie wounded among the foremost (8.535–538); he is wounded, but by Paris.

**Paris' arrow.** As he strips Agastrophus, Paris, leaning against the pillar on the tomb of Ilus, shoots him through the flat of the right foot, and leaps from his ambush laughing (11.369–383; see [[paris]], [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[ambush]]). Diomedes answers:

{{quote:a6ffc63c-4238-5917-b674-f548de2070f9}}

κέρᾳ ἀγλαέ is taken as “glorying in your bow”, or, by an explanation reported in antiquity, “proud of your curl” (see the translation's note on 11.385). The archer shoots from cover; the spearman meets his man “face to face in armor” (11.386; see [[bow]]). Yet the shaft he calls blunt ends his fighting. He sits down behind Odysseus to pull it out, “and a grievous pain went through his flesh” (11.398), and he drives to the ships in the verses of Agamemnon's withdrawal, “for his heart was distressed” (11.399–400 = 11.273–274). In Book 5 Sthenelus drew Pandarus' arrow from his shoulder and Athena sent him back into the fight (5.95–132); this time he does not go back into the fighting. The dying Hector foretells that the same archer, with Apollo, will kill Achilles (22.359–360).

**Among the wounded.** Nestor names him first: “The son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes, has been shot” (11.660; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). He comes with Odysseus and Agamemnon to meet Nestor (14.27–29), counsels the wounded kings to go to the battle and urge on the others while keeping out of range of the missiles (14.109–132), and limps with Odysseus to the assembly of Book 19 (19.47–49).
