summary: Zeus sends down bloody dew because he means to hurl “many mighty heads” to Hades, in the words of the proem. Brothers die in pairs, Iphidamas sleeps “the sleep of bronze” far from the bride he never enjoyed, nine Achaean leaders are a list of names, and Diomedes and Odysseus picture for their victims the death without burial that the proem promised.

**Many mighty heads.** Before the armies meet, Zeus sends down dewdrops wet with blood, “because he meant to hurl down to Hades many mighty heads” (11.53–55; see the translation's note on 11.53–55). The verse is the proem's, with one word changed: there the wrath hurled down “many mighty souls of heroes” (ψυχάς, 1.3), here Zeus means to hurl down κεφαλάς, “heads”. The book keeps counting its dead by heads: “so under Agamemnon, son of Atreus, fell the heads of the fleeing Trojans” (11.158–159), “so thick were the heads of the people beaten down by Hector” (11.309), and by the Scamander “most of all the heads of men were falling” (11.499–500). Two heads are cut off, Hippolochus', whose trunk Agamemnon sends rolling “like a log through the throng” (11.146–147), and Coon's, over his brother's body (11.261).

**Brothers.** Five pairs of brothers die in the book, and most of them together: Isus and Antiphus, sons of Priam, in one chariot (11.101–109); Peisander and Hippolochus, sons of Antimachus, in one chariot (11.122–147); Iphidamas and Coon, sons of Antenor, the second killed dragging the first away, “and went down into the house of Hades” (11.221–263); the sons of Merops (11.328–334); and Charops and Socus, sons of Hippasus (11.426–455). The one pair that escapes is in Nestor's story, the twin Moliones, whom their father Poseidon hid in a mist (11.750–752; see [[the-moliones]] and [[kasignetos]]).

**The sleep of bronze.** The book's fullest obituary is Iphidamas', who was reared in Thrace by his grandfather, married his aunt, and left the bridal chamber at the report of the Achaeans (11.221–231; see [[iphidamas]]):

{{quote:4ca3fb72-7bc6-5f3d-a3f6-8cd6c393667b}}

χάλκεον ὕπνον, “the sleep of bronze”, occurs only here: the sleep of death that the bronze brings, or a sleep as hard and unbreakable as bronze (see the translation's note on 11.241 and [[hypnos]]). The rest of the obituary is a marriage unenjoyed. He had given the bride-price, a hundred cattle, and promised a thousand more from his flocks, and “had seen no joy” of his wife (χάριν, 11.243; see [[marriage]] and [[pity]]). Othryoneus, who will come to Troy for the war in Book 13 and ask for Cassandra without a bride-price, dies as quickly (13.363–382; see [[kleos]]).

**A list of names.** When Hector charges, the narrator asks “whom first, whom last did he slay”, and answers with nine names and no more: Asaeus, Autonous, Opites, Dolops son of Clytius, Opheltius, Agelaus, Aesymnus, Orus and Hipponous, “these were the leaders of the Danaans he killed, and after them the multitude” (11.299–305; see [[whom-first-whom-last]] and [[androktasia]]). None of the nine is given a father's history, a home or a wound.

**Without burial.** Two boasts picture for the victim the end the proem promised, the body left to the birds (1.4–5). Diomedes tells Paris what his own shaft does to a man: “the cheeks of his wife are torn in mourning, and his children are orphans; and he, reddening the earth with his blood, rots, and there are more birds around him than women” (11.393–395). Odysseus says it over Socus, and sets his own burial against it:
