summary: Telamonian Ajax comes with Menelaus to save Odysseus, sweeps the plain like a winter torrent, and is the one man Hector avoids. When Zeus puts fear in him he gives ground step by step, like a lion driven from a farmyard and a donkey beaten out of the grain, until Eurypylus rallies the Achaeans and he turns to stand among them.

**At one end of the line.** Strife shouts from Odysseus' ship in the middle of the camp, so as to be heard at both ends, “both to the huts of Ajax, son of Telamon, / and to those of Achilles”, who had hauled up their ships at the farthest ends, “trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands” (11.7–9 = 8.224–226; see [[strife]]).

**Odysseus.** When Menelaus hears Odysseus' cry, he calls Ajax with the verse of address that Hector and Achilles used for him, “Telamonian Ajax, sprung from Zeus, lord of the people” (11.465 = 7.234 = 9.644; see [[menelaus]], [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[koiranos]]). Ajax follows, “a man equal to the gods” (11.472; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]). The Trojans press around Odysseus like jackals around a wounded stag until a god brings a lion, and the jackals scatter in fear (11.473–482). Then “Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower, / and stood beside him; and the Trojans scattered in fear” (11.485–486), with the verb that scattered the jackals (διέτρεσαν, 11.481, 11.486). The first of those verses brought him against Hector in Book 7 and returns over the body of Patroclus (11.485 = 7.219 = 17.128; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[like-a-tower]]).

**The torrent.** He kills Doryclus, a bastard son of Priam, and wounds four more (11.489–491; see [[doryclus]]), and sweeps the plain like a river in flood, a winter torrent swollen by the rain of Zeus that carries oaks and pines down to the sea (11.492–497; see [[river-in-flood]]). Hector, on the left by the Scamander, does not know of it until Cebriones sees the Trojans in turmoil: “It is Telamonian Ajax who drives them in confusion; I know him well, / for he has the broad shield around his shoulders” (11.526–527; see [[cebriones]]).

**The better man.** Hector drives into the thick of the fighting, “but he avoided battle with Ajax, son of Telamon” (11.542). The next verse is in no manuscript. Aristotle quotes it after 11.542 as an example of what rouses indignation: “For Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (11.543; *Rhetoric* 2.9, 1387a; see [[aristotle-nemesis]] and [[nemesis]]). In Book 7 the two fought, and Ajax had the better of every exchange (7.244–272; see [[hector]]).

**Zeus' fear.** Then Zeus himself turns him:

{{quote:e6f8f3bc-0876-52bf-a55f-94ef18604335}}

He swings the shield of seven oxhides round onto his back to cover his retreat, and two similes picture the withdrawal (see [[seven-oxhide]]). He goes like a lion driven from a farmyard by dogs and countrymen with javelins and torches: “so then Ajax, grieving at heart, went back from the Trojans / very unwillingly; for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans” (11.548–557). The lion returns in the same verses when Menelaus leaves the body of Patroclus (17.657–666). And he goes like a donkey that boys cannot beat out of the grain until it has had its fill, the only donkey in Homer (11.558–562; see [[donkey]]). The lion gives his unwillingness, the donkey his slow retreat under blows that do not hurt him (see [[retreat]] and [[like-a-wild-beast]]).

**Standing.** At times he turns and holds back the Trojan battalions, at times he turns to flee, but he keeps them all from the ships, “standing his ground” between the two armies (11.566–571). Spears stick in his great shield or stand in the ground short of him, “longing to glut themselves on flesh” (11.572–574). Eurypylus comes to his side, and when an arrow of Paris drives him back, Eurypylus calls on the Achaeans to stand “around great Ajax, the son of Telamon” (11.575–591; see [[eurypylus]]). They form up with their shields slanted against their shoulders, “and Ajax came to meet them, / and he turned and stood, when he reached the crowd of his companions” (11.594–595).
