---
summary: The only doves of Book 11 are golden ones. On Nestor's great cup, around each of its four handles, two golden doves are feeding (11.632–635).
grc: [πελειάδες]
passages:
  - 11.632-637 | Nestor's cup
---

δοιαὶ δὲ πελειάδες ἀμφὶς ἕκαστον / χρύσειαι νεμέθοντο: “and around each two doves of gold were feeding” (11.634–635). πελειάς is a form of πέλεια, “dove”, found only here in Homer, and νεμέθομαι, “feed, peck”, occurs only in this verse. With four handles, the cup carries eight doves. They are part of a description that has made Nestor's cup famous: studded with golden nails, with four handles and two bases beneath, so heavy when full that another man could hardly move it from the table, though Nestor, old as he was, lifted it without toil (11.632–637; see [[the-cup-of-nestor]] and [[nestor]]). How the doves and the bases were set on the cup is not said (see the translation's note on 11.624–641).

Everywhere else in Homer doves are living birds, timid and swift: the prey of the hawk and the eagle (22.139–142; *Odyssey* 20.243), the likeness of goddesses on the move (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]), the target of the archery contest at Patroclus' games (23.853–855), and the birds that carry ambrosia to Zeus past the Wandering Rocks (*Odyssey* 12.62–63). On Nestor's cup they are still and feeding, made of gold.
