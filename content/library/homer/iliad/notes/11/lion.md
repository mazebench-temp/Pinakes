summary: Book 11 has more lions than any book before it. Agamemnon's morning is told in lions, a lion in a hind's lair and a lion that routs a herd by night (11.113–121, 11.172–178); Hector sets the Trojans on like hounds on a boar or a lion, Paris says the Trojans shudder at Diomedes as goats at a lion, a lion scatters the jackals around the wounded Odysseus, and Ajax gives ground like a lion driven from a farmyard (11.293, 11.383, 11.480–481, 11.548–557).

**Agamemnon's lions.** The lion is the beast of Agamemnon's day. He kills Isus and Antiphus as a lion comes into a hind's lair and crushes her young, while the mother can only run (11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]); he rushes against the sons of Antimachus “like a lion” (11.129); he pursues the Trojans as a lion routs a herd of cattle in the dead of night and breaks the neck of one (11.172–178; see [[lion-among-cattle]]); and he drags Iphidamas' spear toward him, “eager as a lion” (11.239; see [[like-a-lion]]). λίς, used at 11.239 and 11.480, is a short form of λέων found only in comparisons. In the night before, Agamemnon had put on the skin of a great tawny lion (10.23–24).

**The quarry and the rescuer.** Other lions of the book belong to other men. Hector sets the Trojans on the Achaeans as a hunter sets his hounds on a wild boar or a lion (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]): the Achaeans are dangerous game. Paris, having wounded Diomedes, says that the Trojans shudder at him as bleating goats shudder at a lion (11.383; see [[goats-before-a-lion]]). When the Trojans close around the wounded Odysseus like jackals around a stag, a god brings a lion, σίντης, “a ravager”, and the jackals scatter: Ajax comes with his shield (11.474–486; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). The lion is the stronger beast that ends the scavengers' feast (see [[jackal]]).

**The lion driven off.** Then Zeus puts Ajax to flight, and the lion is the one that loses. Dogs and country men keep it all night from the fattest of the cattle with javelins and blazing torches, and at dawn it goes off with grieving heart (11.548–557; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The craving is still there, “craving meat”, and the courage, but the farm holds. In the morning of the book the lion took what it wanted, in the afternoon it cannot. The beast that the narrator does not name when Ajax first falls back, “glancing around like a wild beast” (θηρί, 11.546), is this lion (see [[like-a-wild-beast]]), and the hind's attacker of the first simile is called “the mighty beast” (κραταιοῦ θηρός, 11.119).
