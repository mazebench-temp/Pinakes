summary: Agamemnon drives the Trojans almost to their city, and Zeus stops him “just about to come beneath the city and its steep wall” (11.181); Iris goes down from Ida to Ilios with Zeus' word, and Nestor's plan looks to the day when fresh men will drive the Trojans back to their city again.

---

**The city in sight.** Zeus, sitting apart, looks out on “the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (11.82; see [[watching-the-war]]). Agamemnon's rout carries the Trojans past the tomb of Ilus and the fig tree, “making for the city” (11.168), to the Scaean gates and the oak (11.170; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]), and he is “just about to come beneath the city and its steep wall” when Zeus sits down on Ida (11.181–184; see [[agamemnon]] and [[ida]]). Iris goes “from the mountains of Ida to sacred Ilios” to tell Hector to hold back until Agamemnon is wounded (11.196; see [[iris]] and [[hector]]), and the battle turns away from the city toward the ships.

**Coming to Troy.** The book remembers men coming to the war. Cinyras heard in Cyprus “the great report that the Achaeans / were about to sail up to Troy in their ships” (11.21–22; see [[cinyras]] and [[cyprus]]), and Iphidamas, hearing the same report in Thrace, left his ships at Percote “but he himself had come on foot to Ilios” (11.227–230; see [[iphidamas]] and [[percote]]). At the end of the book Patroclus pities the Achaean leaders, destined “far from your friends and your native land, / to glut the swift dogs in Troy with your white fat” (11.817–818; see [[patroclus]]), and Nestor's plan hopes that the Myrmidons, fresh, may drive the worn-out Trojans “back to the city, away from the ships and huts” (11.802–803; see [[nestor]]).
