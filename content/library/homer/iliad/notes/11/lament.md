---
summary: Diomedes answers Paris that the man his own spear strikes leaves a wife whose cheeks “are torn in mourning” and children who are orphans (11.393–395).
grc: [ἀμφίδρυφοι]
en: [torn in mourning]
---

Diomedes, wounded in the foot by Paris' arrow, boasts of his own spear: whoever it strikes, “the cheeks of his wife are torn in mourning, and his children are orphans; and he, reddening the earth with his blood, rots, and there are more birds around him than women” (11.393–395). ἀμφίδρυφος, “torn on both sides”, names the mourner's gesture of tearing the cheeks with the nails, and the same word describes Protesilaus' widow: “His wife, her cheeks torn in mourning, had been left behind in Phylace” (ἀμφιδρυφής, 2.700; see [[torn-in-mourning]] and [[protesilaus]]). The widow mourns at home, far away, and the dead man lies unmourned on the plain, with birds about him instead of women (see [[death-in-battle]] and [[tomb]]).
