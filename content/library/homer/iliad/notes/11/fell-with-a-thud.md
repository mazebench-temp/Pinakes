---
summary: The half-verse of the fall ends Socus' life, and the second half is not the clatter of his armor but Odysseus' boast over him: “and he fell with a thud; and brilliant Odysseus boasted over him” (11.449).
---

Odysseus plants his spear in Socus' back as he turns to flee, between the shoulders, and drives it through his chest, “and he fell with a thud; and brilliant Odysseus boasted over him” (δούπησεν δὲ πεσών· ὃ δʼ ἐπεύξατο δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, 11.447–449; see [[socus]]). The whole verse of the first battle ends with the armor clattering on the fallen man (4.504); here the second half turns to the killer. The same turn, the thud and then the boast, is used of Idomeneus over Othryoneus and of Achilles over Iphition (13.373, 20.388), and Odysseus' boast follows: the birds will tear Socus, while Odysseus, if he dies, will have burial from the Achaeans (11.450–455; see [[tomb]]).

The book's other falls are told in other formulas. Chersidamas “fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm” (11.425), the first of five uses of the verse (13.508, 13.520, 14.452, 17.315); Mulius “fell in the dust” (ἤριπε δʼ ἐν κονίῃσιν, 11.743); and in Nestor's rout two men beside each captured chariot “bit the ground with their teeth” (11.748–749; see [[androktasia]]).
