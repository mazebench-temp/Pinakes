{{quote:a6ffc63c-4238-5917-b674-f548de2070f9}}

οὐκ ἀλέγω, ὡς εἴ με γυνὴ βάλοι ἢ πάϊς ἄφρων: “I care no more than if a woman had hit me, or a witless child” (11.389). ὡς εἰ, “as if”, makes the comparison, and ἄφρων is “senseless, witless”. In Book 7 Hector put the comparison into words himself, to deny it (7.235–236). Here Diomedes uses it as a weapon. The next verse says what kind of man shoots so: κωφὸν γὰρ βέλος ἀνδρὸς ἀνάλκιδος οὐτιδανοῖο, “for blunt is the shaft of a weakling, a nobody” (11.390). κωφός, “dull, blunt”, is said of a missile that does no harm, and ἄναλκις, “without strength”, is the word for a coward.

The retort turns Paris' own boast against him. Paris wished that the Trojans could have a breathing space from Diomedes, before whom they shudder like goats before a lion (11.380–383; see [[goats-before-a-lion]]). Diomedes calls him an archer, an insulter, and παρθενοπίπης, “ogler of girls”, a word found only here (11.385; see the translation's note on 11.385), and says that his bow would not help him if he made trial of Diomedes face to face, in armor (11.386–388). Paris is set among the women in the contempt of his enemies, as Hector set him among them in Book 3, when he said that Paris was fair to look at, mad for women, and no fighter (3.39–45; see [[paris]] and [[bow]]). Then Diomedes describes his own spear's work: the man it touches dies, his wife tears her cheeks, his children are orphans, and there are more birds around him than women (11.391–395; see [[birds]]). The women of the end of the speech are the mourners of the men Diomedes kills.

Diomedes' answer is bravado. He has Odysseus stand in front of him while he pulls out the arrow, “and a grievous pain went through his flesh”, and he leaves the battle for the ships (11.396–400; see [[diomedes]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).
