summary: In Book 11 the birds are carrion. Diomedes tells Paris that the man his spear hits rots on the ground with “more birds around him than women” (11.393–395), and Odysseus tells the dying Socus that the birds that eat raw flesh will tear at him, beating their thick wings around him (11.452–455).
grc: [οἰωνοὶ]
passages:
  - 11.393-395 | More birds than women
  - 11.452-455 | Odysseus over Socus
---

**More birds than women.** Diomedes, shot through the foot, answers Paris' boast with his own spear's work: the man it touches is quickly dead, his wife's cheeks are torn in mourning, his children are orphans, “and he, reddening the earth with his blood, / rots, and there are more birds around him than women” (11.393–395; see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]]). The women who should mourn the dead man are outnumbered by the birds that come to eat him.

**No burial for Socus.** Odysseus speaks the same threat over the man he has just killed: “Ah, wretch, your father and your lady mother / will not close your eyes in death, but the birds / that eat raw flesh will tear at you, beating their thick wings around you. / But if I die, the brilliant Achaeans will give me burial rites” (11.452–455; see [[socus]] and [[odysseus]]). ὠμησταί, “eaters of raw flesh”, is said of Priam's dogs in the same phrase, ὠμησταὶ ἐρύουσιν, “will tear” (22.66–67). The contrast between the enemy left to the birds and the self that the comrades will bury returns in Achilles' last words to Hector: the dogs and birds will maul him, while the Achaeans will give Patroclus his rites (22.335–336).

The birds of the dead are the proem's, where the wrath made the heroes “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5). In Book 11 the narrator adds the vultures, to whom the dead charioteers are “far dearer … than to their wives” (11.162; see [[vultures]]).
