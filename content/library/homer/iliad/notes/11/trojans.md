**On the plain.** For the third day of battle the Trojans form up “on the rise of the plain” (11.56), where they had camped the night before (10.160), around Hector, Polydamas, Aeneas and three sons of Antenor (11.56–60; see [[hector]], [[polydamas]], [[aeneas]] and [[antenor]]). The two armies cut each other down like reapers working toward each other through a rich man's field, “and neither side thought of deadly rout” (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]). Zeus, sitting apart, means “to hold out glory to the Trojans” (11.79; see [[kydos]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The rout.** At the hour of the woodcutter's meal the Danaans break the battalions (11.86–91), and before Agamemnon “fell the heads / of the fleeing Trojans”, as thickets fall before a forest fire (11.155–159). Their horses rattle empty chariots over the bridges of war, and the drivers lie on the ground, “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.159–162; see [[chariot]]). They run past the tomb of Ilus and the fig tree to the Scaean gates and the oak, where some halt and wait while others still flee across the plain like cattle before a lion (11.166–178; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[the-fig-tree]], [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).

**The day of Hector.** At Hector's word “they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans” (11.214). When Agamemnon leaves the field, Hector calls them with the cry of the Trojans and their allies:

{{quote:af17aaa5-0137-5cdb-ac56-63ac833d8040}}

He sets them on like a hunter setting hounds on a boar (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). Diomedes knows that Zeus “wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us” (11.319). Their ranks close around Odysseus alone like jackals around a wounded stag, and scatter when Ajax comes up with his shield like a tower (11.412–420, 11.473–486; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). Then “the high-hearted Trojans and their allies gathered from many lands” follow Ajax, stabbing at the middle of his shield (11.563–565; see [[trojan-allies]] and [[ajax]]), and by the end of the book Eurypylus can tell Patroclus that their strength “keeps rising” (11.827). Paris, wishing he had killed Diomedes, says that the Trojans “shudder at you as bleating goats shudder at a lion” (11.383; see [[goats-before-a-lion]]).

Their epithets in the book are “great-hearted” (11.294, 11.459), “high-hearted” (11.564), “shield-bearing” (11.412) and “horse-taming” (11.568; see [[great-hearted]], [[high-hearted]], [[shield-bearing]] and [[tamer-of-horses]]).
