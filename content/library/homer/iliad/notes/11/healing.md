summary: In Book 11 the healer himself is wounded: Paris' arrow takes Machaon out of the battle, and Idomeneus sends Nestor to save him because “a healer is a man worth many others” (11.504–520). The wounded kings have no healer, and the book ends with the fullest treatment of a wound in the poem, Patroclus' care of Eurypylus with the knife, warm water and a bitter root, by the skill that came to him from Chiron through Achilles (11.809–848).

**The healer wounded.** Machaon, “the son of Asclepius, the noble healer”, is hit in the right shoulder by Paris' three-barbed arrow while he is fighting among the best (11.505–507, 11.518). Idomeneus sends Nestor to carry him off, “for a healer is a man worth many others, to cut out arrows and spread on soothing drugs” (11.514–515; see [[a-healer-is-worth-many]] and [[machaon]]). The second verse names the healer's two tasks, and the end of the book performs both. Machaon's brother Podalirius is still fighting on the plain (11.833–836; see [[podalirius]]).

**The wounded kings.** None of the leaders wounded in the first part of the book is treated by a healer. Agamemnon fights on while the blood wells warm from his forearm, until “the wound dried and the blood stopped”, and then the pain drives him from the field (11.264–274). Diomedes pulls the arrow from his own foot while Odysseus covers him (11.397–398), and Odysseus draws Socus' spear from his own side, and the blood spurts up (11.456–458). Only later does Patroclus tell Achilles that the wounded kings lie at the ships, and that “healers rich in drugs tend them, healing their wounds” (16.23–29; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**In Nestor's hut.** Machaon is not treated in the book. Hecamede makes him and Nestor a drink of Pramnian wine, goat's cheese and barley meal, and they drink and talk (11.624–643). The poem does not present the drink as a remedy, but later readers did: Plato's Socrates asks whether a doctor or a rhapsode should judge Homer's verses on it (*Ion* 538b–c), and in the *Republic* he remembers it as given to Eurypylus (3.405e–406a; see [[kykeon]] and [[plato-asclepiads]]). At the start of Book 14 Nestor leaves Machaon drinking wine while Hecamede heats a bath to wash away the bloody gore (14.5–7).

**Agamede.** In Nestor's story the first man he killed in battle was the husband of Augeas' eldest daughter, fair-haired Agamede, “who knew all the drugs that the wide earth nourishes” (11.739–741; see [[agamede]] and [[mulius]]). No other woman in the Iliad is said to know drugs. In the Odyssey Helen has drugs from Egypt, where the earth bears the most (Od. 4.219–232).

**Patroclus and Eurypylus.** Eurypylus, limping from the battle with an arrow in his thigh, sweat running down and dark blood gushing from the wound, asks Patroclus to do what Idomeneus said a healer does: “cut the arrow out of my thigh, and wash the dark blood from it with warm water, and spread soothing drugs on it, good ones, which they say you have learned from Achilles, whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs” (11.809–813, 11.828–832). The steps are those of Book 4, done otherwise. Machaon drew the arrow out of Menelaus' belt and sucked the blood (4.213–218); here the arrowhead, whose reed has broken off (11.584), is cut out with a knife, and the blood is washed away with warm water (see [[knife]] and [[arrows]]). In place of the soothing drugs that Eurypylus asked for comes a root, unnamed, “bitter” and “pain-killing”, ὀδυνήφατος, the word of the drugs Paeëon spread on the wounds of Hades and Ares (5.401, 5.900; see [[pain-killing]] and [[soothing-drugs]]). Patroclus rubs it between his hands, and it stops the pain. The last verse, “and the wound dried, and the blood stopped”, is the verse of Agamemnon's wound (11.848 = 11.267): what came to the king of itself, before the pain, comes to Eurypylus by the healer's hand.

**Chiron's teaching.** The skill has a lineage. Chiron gave drugs to Asclepius, Machaon's father, “in friendship” (4.218–219), and taught Achilles, who taught Patroclus (11.830–832; see [[chiron]], [[chiron-and-asclepius]] and [[patroclus]]). Both of the poem's full treatments of a wound go back to the Centaur. Patroclus stays with Eurypylus into Book 15, cheering him with talk and laying drugs on the wound, until the Trojans storm the wall and he runs to Achilles (15.390–405; see [[eurypylus]]).
