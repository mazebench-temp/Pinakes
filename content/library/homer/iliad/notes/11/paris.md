summary: Paris is the archer of Book 11: leaning on a pillar of the tomb of Ilus, he shoots Diomedes through the foot and laughs over him, and later he wounds Machaon and Eurypylus, so that three of the five Achaean chiefs wounded in the book are hit by his arrows. Antimachus, who took his gold to keep Helen in Troy, pays for it with his sons.

**Alexander's gold.** Before Paris appears, the narrator names him as the man whose gold, “splendid gifts”, bought the Trojan Antimachus, who would not let Helen be given back to Menelaus (11.123–125). Agamemnon kills the sons of Antimachus for their father's counsel (11.122–147; see [[antimachus]] and [[sons-of-antimachus]]).

**The archer.** He is introduced, as when he shot Nestor's horse (8.82), by the verse that names him by his wife, “Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair” (11.369, 11.505; see [[husband-of-helen]]). He bends his bow at Diomedes, “leaning against a pillar on the man-made tomb / of Ilus” (11.371–372; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]), while Diomedes is stripping the dead Agastrophus, and the arrow goes through the flat of Diomedes' right foot into the ground (11.373–378; see [[agastrophus]] and [[bow]]). Then “he laughed very heartily, / and leapt out from his ambush” (11.378–379; see [[ambush]]):

{{quote:c2ace520-fde4-5413-8a2c-b81c2a23c0f1}}

The Trojans, he says, shudder at Diomedes as goats shudder at a lion (see [[goats-before-a-lion]]).

His “the shaft did not fly in vain” echoes the narrator's verse of a moment before (11.376, 11.380). Diomedes answers with contempt, “Archer, insulter, glorying in your bow, ogler of girls” (11.385; see [[ogler-of-girls]]): a scratch on the foot is no more than a woman's or a witless child's blow, “for blunt is the shaft of a weakling, a nobody”, while his own spear leaves widows and orphans and a corpse for the birds (11.385–395; see [[diomedes]] and the translation's note on 11.385). Yet the scratch ends Diomedes' fighting. Odysseus stands in front of him while he pulls the arrow from his foot, and he drives to the ships in pain (11.396–400), not to fight again in the Iliad.

**Machaon and Eurypylus.** On the left of the battle, by the Scamander, Paris stops Machaon “while he was the best in the fighting”, hitting him on the right shoulder with a three-barbed arrow, and the Achaeans fear for the healer (11.504–509; see [[machaon]], [[three-barbed]] and [[arrows]]). Later “godlike Alexander” sees Eurypylus stripping Apisaon and shoots him in the right thigh, and the reed breaks off in the wound (11.581–584; see [[eurypylus]], [[apisaon]] and [[godlike]]). Twice he hits a man bent over a corpse. Of the five chiefs wounded in the book, Agamemnon and Odysseus are stabbed with spears, and Diomedes, Machaon and Eurypylus are shot, all three by Paris; Nestor's reckoning keeps the difference, “shot or stabbed” (11.659–664; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).
