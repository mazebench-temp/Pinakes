---
summary: Odysseus, left alone, speaks “to his own proud heart” and asks why his heart debates these things with him, the first of the Iliad's monologues of choice. The θυμός is the life that the sons of Merops and Agastrophus lose, the heart that grieves in Ajax as he gives ground, and the heart that Nestor hopes Patroclus may stir in Achilles, as Nestor stirs his.
passages:
  - 11.401-411 | Odysseus alone
  - 11.804-805 | Nestor stirs Patroclus
---

**The heart as a second self.** With Diomedes gone and the Argives routed, “deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (11.403). The verse introduces each of the Iliad's monologues of choice, Menelaus' over the body of Patroclus and Agenor's and Hector's before Achilles (17.90, 21.552, 22.98), and three speeches of Achilles to himself (18.5, 20.343, 21.53; see [[deeply-troubled]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]). Odysseus weighs flight against being caught alone, and then breaks off: “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (11.407). διελέξατο, “talked through”, gives the debate to the θυμός, as if it were another speaker, and the man stops it with a rule (see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]). The narrator closes the scene with the verse of Achilles' hesitation over his sword in Book 1, “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart” (11.411 = 1.193; see [[phrenes]]).

**Life.** The θυμός is also the life a man loses. The sons of Merops are “robbed of spirit and life” (θυμοῦ καὶ ψυχῆς, 11.334; see [[psyche]] and [[the-sons-of-merops]]); Agastrophus rushed through the champions “until he lost his dear life” (11.342); Paris wishes he had struck Diomedes in the flank “and taken your life” (11.381); and Socus threatens that Odysseus “will lose your life” by his spear (11.433), a moment before he loses his own.

**Grief and gladness.** When Odysseus draws Socus' spear from his side, the blood spurts up, “and it grieved his heart” (11.458). Ajax, giving ground, is the lion that goes off at dawn “with grieving heart” (τετιηότι θυμῷ, 11.555; see [[retreat]]). Achilles calls Patroclus “dear to my heart” (κεχαρισμένε θυμῷ, 11.608).

**Stirring the heart.** Nestor ends his advice with a hope: “who knows if, with a god's help, you might stir his heart by persuading him?” (θυμὸν ὀρίναις, 11.792–793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). His own words do it to Patroclus: “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast” (11.804). The verse is the one with which Talthybius' summons sent Machaon to heal Menelaus in Book 4 (4.208). It sends Patroclus running back to Achilles, and on the way he stops to do a healer's work for Eurypylus (11.805–848; see [[patroclus]]).
