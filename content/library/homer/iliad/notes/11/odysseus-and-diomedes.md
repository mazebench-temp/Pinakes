summary: On the morning after the raid the pair stands together again. Odysseus calls Diomedes to stand with him as the Achaeans break, and they fight side by side like two boars turning on the hounds, until Paris' arrow drives Diomedes from the field and leaves Odysseus alone.

**The call.** “Then there would have been ruin, and deeds past mending would have been done” (11.310) is the verse with which, in Book 8, Diomedes all but penned the Trojans in Ilios, until Zeus' thunderbolt fell before his horses (8.130–136). Now the ruin is the Achaeans', and the two men turn it back. In Book 8 Diomedes called Odysseus to stand and Odysseus did not hear (8.92–98); now Odysseus calls:

{{quote:6316a290-600a-52a4-832c-7de56e4013c7}}

τί παθόντε, “what has happened to us”, is a dual: Odysseus speaks of the two of them. Diomedes stays, though he knows that Zeus is against them (see [[diomedes]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Side by side.** The duals go on: “the two went through the throng spreading tumult, as when two boars / fall in high spirits on the hunting dogs” (τὼ … ἰόντε, 11.324–325; see [[boar]]), “they turned again” (ὀρμένω, 11.326), “they took a chariot and two men” (ἑλέτην, 11.328). The kills come in pairs. Diomedes kills Thymbraeus and Odysseus his attendant Molion (11.320–322); Diomedes kills the two sons of Merops, and Odysseus Hippodamus and Hypeirochus (11.328–335; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). When Hector charges at them, Diomedes says to Odysseus, “Here is this bane rolling down on us, mighty Hector” (νῶϊν, “on us two”, 11.347).

**Parted.** Paris' arrow parts them. Odysseus stands in front of Diomedes while he pulls it from his foot, and when Diomedes drives back to the ships, “Odysseus, famed for his spear, was left alone” (11.396–401). Nestor names them one after the other among the wounded, Diomedes shot, Odysseus stabbed (11.660–661; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). They come to Nestor together in Book 14 (14.27–29), and together they limp to the assembly in Book 19, “two attendants of Ares” (19.47–49).
