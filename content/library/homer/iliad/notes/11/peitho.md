summary: The verb names obedience given and refused: Iris and Nestor do not disobey, Patroclus obeys his companion, and the sons of Merops would not obey their father. Menoetius promised that Achilles would obey Patroclus, “to his good”, and Nestor adds the noun παραίφασις, “persuasion”, in a maxim; Patroclus tells Nestor, “you will not persuade me”.

**Obeying.** The middle πείθομαι, “be persuaded”, is the book's word for obedience (see the article above and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). Iris and Nestor “did not disobey” (οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε, 11.195, 11.516); “Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (ἐπεπείθεθʼ, 11.616); and the sons of Merops “would not obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on” (πειθέσθην, 11.331–332; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Menoetius' charge to Patroclus ends with a promise in the future of the same verb: give Achilles counsel and direct him, “and he will obey you, to his good” (πείσεται εἰς ἀγαθόν περ, 11.789). Nestor turns the promise into a hope: “in the hope that he may listen” (αἴ κε πίθηται, 11.791). The future πείσεται is also the future of πάσχω, “suffer” (as at 20.127), so the entry claims it only in Menoetius' phrase.

**Persuading.** The active is refused once. Patroclus, offered a chair, tells Nestor, “you will not persuade me” (οὐδέ με πείσεις, 11.648), the half-verse with which Hector refused Helen's chair (6.360). Then Nestor gives persuasion its noun: “who knows if, with a god's help, you might stir his heart by persuading him? Good is the persuasion of a companion” (παρειπών … παραίφασις, 11.792–793). παραίφασις, from παράφημι, “talk round”, occurs only here and where Patroclus repeats the verse (15.404; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]).

**Trusting.** The aorist participle πιθήσας can mean “trusting”, the sense of the perfect πέποιθα: Iphidamas leans on his spear “trusting in his heavy hand”, and the point bends on Agamemnon's belt (πιθήσας, 11.235–237; see [[iphidamas]]).
