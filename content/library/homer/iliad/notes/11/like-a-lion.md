summary: Two half-verses make Agamemnon a lion on the morning of his great day: he rushes against the two sons of Antimachus “like a lion” (11.129), and he drags Iphidamas' spear toward him “eager as a lion” before he kills him (11.239).
grc: ["ὦρτο λέων ὣς", "μεμαὼς ὥς τε λίς"]
en: [rushed against them like a, eager as a]
passages:
  - 11.127-130 | Like a lion
  - 11.238-240 | Eager as a lion
---

**Against the suppliants.** The reins have slipped from the hands of the two sons of Antimachus, and their horses are thrown into confusion: ὃ δʼ ἐναντίον ὦρτο λέων ὣς / Ἀτρεΐδης, “and he rushed against them like a lion, the son of Atreus” (11.129–130). The brothers beg from the chariot to be taken alive, and the lion does not spare them (11.130–147; see [[sons-of-antimachus]]). The comparison comes a few verses after the lion of the full simile, which crushed the young of a hind in their lair (11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]).

**Eager as a lion.** Iphidamas' spear has bent on Agamemnon's belt. Agamemnon catches it and ἕλκʼ ἐπὶ οἷ μεμαὼς ὥς τε λίς, “dragged it toward him, eager as a lion”, wrenches it from his hand and strikes him on the neck (11.238–240; see [[like-lead]] and [[iphidamas]]). λίς is a short form of λέων, used in Homer only in comparisons and similes (11.480, 15.275, 17.109, 18.318). μεμαώς, “eager, in fury”, is the word of the wounded lion of Book 5 (ἐμμεμαώς, 5.142–143; see [[wounded-lion]]).

Agamemnon's morning is told in lions: two full lion similes and these two half-verses (see [[lion-among-cattle]], [[lion]] and [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]). The half-verse of 11.129 returns for Achilles in Book 20, where it opens a full simile, the wounded lion that a whole people gathers to kill (20.164).
