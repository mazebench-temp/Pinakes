---
summary: Five replies in Book 11 open with “Answering him, … said” or one of its kin. Diomedes answers Paris' taunt “unafraid”, as he answered Pandarus' in Book 5, and Eurypylus' wound takes the place of his title: “Then wounded Eurypylus answered him face to face”.
---

The replies of the book: 11.316, 384, 607, 822, 837. τὸν δʼ ἀπαμειβόμενος προσέφη, “Answering him, … said”, brings in Diomedes' answer to Odysseus' call to stand (11.316) and Achilles' answer to Patroclus' first words in the poem (11.607 = 9.643; see [[swift-footed]]). Patroclus answers Eurypylus with the other frame, “Then … spoke to him in turn” (τὸν δʼ αὖτε προσέειπε, 11.837; see [[son-of-menoetius]]).

**Unafraid.** Paris, shooting from cover, has hit Diomedes in the foot and taunts him, and “Unafraid, mighty Diomedes answered him” (τὸν δʼ οὐ ταρβήσας προσέφη, 11.384). The verse is the one with which he answered Pandarus' boast in Book 5 (5.286), and both times the speaker he answers is an archer who has hit him or claims to have (5.95–105, 5.283–285). There Pandarus had only struck his shield, and Diomedes killed him with his next throw; here the arrow has gone through his foot, and his answer is all he can return (see [[bow]] and [[diomedes]]). The verse is used once more, for Hector, unafraid before Achilles (20.430).

**Wounded.** “Then wounded Eurypylus answered him face to face” (τὸν δʼ αὖτʼ Εὐρύπυλος βεβλημένος ἀντίον ηὔδα, 11.822). βεβλημένος, “shot, wounded”, fills the place where a hero's epithet would stand, as it does when he calls on the leaders, “So spoke wounded Eurypylus” (11.592), and when Patroclus meets him, “Eurypylus met him, wounded” (11.809). The arrow in his thigh has become his title (see [[eurypylus]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).
