---
summary: Aristotle preserves a verse of Book 11 that no manuscript has, Zeus' indignation at Hector whenever he fought a better man, as his example of νέμεσις in the Rhetoric. He also quotes the spears that stand in the ground “longing to glut themselves on flesh” as a metaphor that brings lifeless things to life, and the lion's fear of the blazing torches in the History of Animals.
---

- **Zeus' indignation.** In his chapter on indignation, the feeling opposed to pity, Aristotle names among its causes the lesser man disputing with the greater, above all in the same field, and quotes 11.542–543: Hector “avoided battle with Ajax, son of Telamon; for Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (*Rhetoric* 2.9.11, 1387a). The second verse is known only from this quotation: [[aristotle-nemesis]].
- **The spears that long for flesh.** Homer is praised for metaphors that make lifeless things alive and active, and Aristotle gives five examples, among them the spears that fell short of Ajax, which “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (ἐν γαίῃ ἵσταντο λιλαιόμενα χροὸς ἆσαι, *Rhetoric* 3.11.3, 1411b). The verse is 11.574; it recurs at 15.317, of the spears thrown when Apollo leads the Trojan charge with the aegis. In Book 11 the spears are the ones the Trojans hurl at Ajax as he gives ground, some lodged in his great shield and many falling short of his white flesh (11.571–574; see [[ajax]] and [[spear]]).
- **The lion and the fire.** In the *History of Animals* Aristotle describes the character of the lion. In the hunt, as long as it is seen, it never flees or cowers; if the number of the hunters forces it to give ground, it withdraws at a walk, step by step, turning about at short intervals. And what is said of it is true, that it fears fire most of all, “as Homer too composed it: ‘and blazing torches, which he dreads, eager though he is’” (*History of Animals* 9.44, 629b, quoting καιόμεναί τε δεταί, τάς τε τρεῖ ἐσσύμενός περ). The verse belongs to the simile of the lion driven from the farmyard, which Book 11 uses of Ajax's retreat (11.548–557) and Book 17 of Menelaus' (17.657–664); 11.554 and 17.663 are the same line (see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). Aristotle's lion, giving ground at a walk and turning about as it goes, is how Book 11 pictures Ajax himself just before the simile, “turning back again and again, moving knee past knee only a little” (11.547; see [[lion]] and [[retreat]]).
- **The jackals.** In the same chapter Aristotle says that jackals (θῶες) do men no harm and make war on dogs and lions, which is why they are not found in the same places (*History of Animals* 9.44, 630a). In Homer's simile the jackals that are devouring a wounded stag scatter when a lion appears (11.474–481; see [[jackal]] and [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]).
- **The Gorgon.** The Gorgon on Agamemnon's shield, with Terror and Rout around her (11.36–37), bears on the solution that Aristotle's *Homeric Problems* offered for the Gorgon's head on Athena's aegis: [[aristotle-gorgon]].
