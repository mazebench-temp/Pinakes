summary: Odysseus holds the line when the Achaeans break: he calls Diomedes to stand with him, covers him when he is wounded, and, left alone, reasons with his own heart that the best must stand. Wounded by Socus, whom he kills, he shouts three times for help and is led out of the battle by Menelaus while Ajax covers him with his shield.

**The ship in the middle.** Strife shouts from Odysseus' “black ship, huge in the hull, which lay in the middle”, as Agamemnon did in Book 8 (11.5–9 = 8.222–226; see [[strife]] and [[ships]]); the Achaeans' place of assembly and their altars are there too (11.806–808). Agamemnon names him in his refusal of the sons of Antimachus, whose father urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came “on an embassy with godlike Odysseus” (11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]).

**The stand.** When Hector has killed nine leaders and the Achaeans are about to fall back on their ships, Odysseus calls Diomedes to stand by him (11.310–315). In Book 8 Diomedes called to him, and he “did not hear” (8.92–98; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). The two kill side by side, Odysseus taking Molion, Hippodamus and Hypeirochus (11.320–335; see [[molion]], [[hippodamus]] and [[hypeirochus]]). When Paris' arrow pins Diomedes' foot, Odysseus, “famed for his spear”, stands in front of him while he pulls it out (11.396–400; see [[famed-for-his-spear]] and [[diomedes]]).

**Alone.** Diomedes drives back to the ships, “And Odysseus, famed for his spear, was left alone, and no one / of the Argives stayed beside him, since rout had seized them all” (11.401–402). οἰώθη, “was left alone”, occurs elsewhere only of the battle itself, left to men when the gods had gone (6.1). He speaks to his own heart:

{{quote:cbced28b-1ccc-5c75-ab8a-553ee2b6f148}}

It is the first of four monologues in which a man alone in battle decides whether to stand, each brought in by the same verse (11.403) and turned by the same question, “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (11.407; Menelaus 17.90–105, Agenor 21.552–570, Hector 22.98–130; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). He knows what cowards do and what the best must do, and the verb of the best, ἀριστεύῃσι (11.409), is the verb of Peleus' charge to his son, αἰὲν ἀριστεύειν (11.784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[retreat]]). Menelaus, over Patroclus' body, decides the other way, and the narrator follows both speeches with nearly the same two verses: “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart, / the ranks of the shield-bearing Trojans came on” (11.411–412 ≈ 17.106–107).

**At bay.** The Trojans close around him as dogs and young men close around a boar that comes out of a deep thicket whetting its white tusk (11.412–420; see [[boar]]). He wounds Deiopites, kills Thoon and Ennomus, stabs Chersidamas as he leaps from his chariot, and wounds Charops, son of Hippasus (11.420–427; see [[deiopites]], [[thoon]], [[ennomus-the-trojan]], [[chersidamas]] and [[charops]]).

**Socus.** Charops' brother Socus, “a man equal to the gods”, challenges him: “Much-praised Odysseus, insatiable of tricks and toil” (11.428–433; see [[socus]]). πολύαινε is the title with which Agamemnon and Nestor greeted him (9.673, 10.544; see [[much-praised]]); δόλων ἆτε, “insatiable of tricks”, is a taunt, but in the Odyssey Athena, smiling, calls him so herself (*Odyssey* 13.287–293; see [[insatiable]]). Socus' spear goes through shield and corselet, in the verses of Menelaus' cast at Paris (11.435–436 = 3.357–358), and tears the skin from his ribs, “but it was not allowed / by Pallas Athena to mingle with the man's entrails” (11.437–438; see [[athena]]). Knowing that the stroke has not come to a deadly place (11.439), Odysseus promises Socus death that day; Socus turns to run, and Odysseus plants a spear in his back, the death of which Diomedes warned Odysseus in Book 8 (11.440–449; 8.95). Then he boasts:

{{quote:1a818b9c-d039-5af2-807c-d9aa1699534c}}

Achilles draws the same line between the dying Hector and Patroclus: “dogs and birds will drag you shamefully, but the Achaeans will give him burial rites” (22.335–336; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). Odysseus pulls Socus' spear from his own flesh, “and it grieved his heart” (11.456–458).

**The rescue.** The Trojans see his blood and come at him. He gives ground and shouts three times, “as loud as a man's head could hold”, and three times Menelaus hears and brings Ajax (11.459–472; see [[menelaus]] and [[ajax]]). They find the Trojans around him “as blood-red jackals in the mountains / press around a horned stag that is wounded”, until a god brings a lion (11.473–481). ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆα δαΐφρονα ποικιλομήτην, “around wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” (11.482), is a verse-end of the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 3.163, 7.168, 22.115, 22.202, 22.281), found in the Iliad only here (see [[wise-hearted]]). Ajax comes up “carrying his shield like a tower”, the Trojans scatter, and Menelaus leads Odysseus out by the hand until his attendant brings the horses (11.485–488; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

**Among the wounded.** Nestor counts him with the others: “Odysseus, famed for his spear, has been stabbed” (11.661; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), and Patroclus repeats the verse to Achilles (16.26). He comes with Agamemnon and Diomedes to meet Nestor (14.27–29), answers Agamemnon's new proposal of flight with anger (14.82–102), and limps to the assembly of Book 19 leaning on his spear (19.47–49).

**In Peleus' house.** Nestor recalls that he and “brilliant Odysseus” heard Menoetius' charge to his son in Peleus' house (11.767–768; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). On that day Odysseus also heard the charge of Peleus that he brought to Achilles in the embassy (9.252–259).
