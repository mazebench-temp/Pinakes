---
summary: The wounded Eurypylus asks Patroclus for the drugs he learned from Achilles, “whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs” (11.832). In Book 11 the Centaur's knowledge reaches the wounded by two lines, through Asclepius to Machaon, who is wounded himself, and through Achilles to Patroclus.
---

With Machaon, “the son of Asclepius, the noble healer” (11.518), lying wounded and Podalirius fighting on the plain, Eurypylus turns to Patroclus (11.828–836; see [[eurypylus]] and [[patroclus]]):

{{quote:e45d38e8-05bf-52ab-8edd-6ace34239600}}

The knowledge is the one Idomeneus prized in Machaon, “to cut out arrows and spread on soothing drugs” (11.515), and the drugs are the ones Machaon spread on Menelaus' wound in Book 4, “which Chiron once gave to his father in friendship” (4.218–219; see [[machaon]], [[asclepius]] and [[chiron-and-asclepius]]). Chiron's teaching has come down by two lines, through Asclepius to his son and through Achilles to his companion. In this book Machaon is wounded himself, and Patroclus takes his place: he cuts out the arrow, washes the wound and lays a bitter root on it (11.844–848; see [[healing]]). Eurypylus knows the teaching only by report (φασιν, “they say”, 11.831).

Here alone the Iliad calls Chiron a Centaur. δικαιότατος Κενταύρων, “the most just of the Centaurs”, sets him apart from the wild “beasts” whom the Lapiths fought (1.262–268, 2.742–744; see [[centaurs]]). It is also the only place where the Iliad says that he taught Achilles.
