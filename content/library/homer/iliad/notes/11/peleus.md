summary: In Nestor's memory Peleus is an old king at his sacrifice. When Nestor and Odysseus came to his house to gather the army, he was burning the thighs of an ox to Zeus in his courtyard and pouring wine from a golden goblet, and he charged his son “always to be the best and to excel above the others”.

**The house.** “We had come to the well-appointed house of Peleus, / gathering the army throughout the bountiful land of Achaea” (11.769–770). Nestor tells Patroclus of that day, when he and Odysseus found Menoetius there with the two young men (see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]], [[menoetius]] and [[phthia]]). In Book 7 he recalled a time in the same house when Peleus questioned him and “rejoiced greatly / to learn the lineage and birth of all the Argives” (7.127–128); the poem does not say whether it was this visit.

**The sacrifice.**

{{quote:4281b408-9e3e-51ff-a99f-77443d879cb1}}

γέρων ἱππηλάτα Πηλεύς, “the old horse-driving Peleus”, is his title in Nestor's lament of Book 7 and in Phoenix's memory of the same day (7.125, 9.438; see [[horse-driving]]). He burns the thigh pieces “in the enclosure of the courtyard” (αὐλῆς ἐν χόρτῳ), at home, as Laertes and Odysseus burned the thighs of oxen at the altar of Zeus of the courtyard in their house on Ithaca (*Odyssey* 22.334–336), and he pours the wine on the fire from a golden goblet (see [[sacrifice]] and [[libation]]). The god is Zeus “who delights in thunder” (τερπικέραυνος). The son pours to Zeus in his turn on the day he sends Patroclus out. Achilles takes from its chest the cup from which he pours to Zeus alone, pours wine “standing in the middle of the courtyard”, and prays that his companion may drive the battle from the ships and come back unharmed; Zeus, “who delights in thunder”, grants the first and refuses the second (16.225–252; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The charge.** “Old Peleus charged his son Achilles / always to be the best and to excel above the others” (11.783–784): the charge Hippolochus gave Glaucus, in the same verse (6.208; see [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[achilles]]). Odysseus, who heard Peleus that day, gave him other words in the embassy, “hold back your proud heart / in your breast; for kindliness is better” (9.255–256), and Phoenix said that Peleus sent him to teach the boy to be a speaker and a doer (9.438–443; see [[phoenix]]). Each speaker remembers the Peleus his purpose needs (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Afterward.** Achilles thinks of him in Book 16 as still living among the Myrmidons (16.15). When Patroclus is dead, he says that the old horse-driving Peleus will not receive him home (18.330–332), and he tells Priam that he cannot care for his father in his old age (24.538–542).
