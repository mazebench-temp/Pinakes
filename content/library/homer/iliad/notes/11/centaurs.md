summary: Eurypylus calls Chiron “the most just of the Centaurs” (11.832). It is the only time the Iliad uses the name Κένταυροι; elsewhere it calls them φῆρες, “beasts”.
grc: [Κενταύρων]
en: [Centaurs]
---

ὃν Χείρων ἐδίδαξε δικαιότατος Κενταύρων, “whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs” (11.832; see [[chiron]]). Nestor and the Catalogue call them by the old word for wild beasts, “the beasts who lived in the mountains” (φηρσὶν ὀρεσκῴοισι, 1.268) and “the shaggy beasts” (Φῆρας λαχνήεντας, 2.743), when they tell of the Lapiths' war with them (see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]] and [[lapiths-drive-out-the-centaurs]]). The name Κένταυρος occurs only here in the Iliad, and in the Odyssey only in the story of the Centaur Eurytion, whose drunkenness at the house of Peirithous began the quarrel between Centaurs and men (*Odyssey* 21.295–304). The superlative sets Chiron apart from the rest of his kind: he is a teacher and a giver of drugs, they are the beasts the Lapiths drove from Pelion. Homer never describes Chiron's body, and of the others he says only that they were shaggy (2.743).
