---
summary: Plato draws on Book 11 for medicine and for love: the Republic and the Ion use Hecamede's drink and Patroclus' care of Eurypylus, Alcibiades greets the doctor Eryximachus with “a healer is a man worth many others”, and Phaedrus rests his claim that Achilles was the younger of the two lovers on Homer's word that Patroclus was the elder. The Republic also has Achilles reared by Chiron, whom Book 11 makes his teacher, and the Laws makes Cinyras a byword for wealth.
---

- The drink of Pramnian wine and Patroclus' treatment of Eurypylus, merged into one scene (*Republic* 3.405e–406a, 3.408a–b), and the lines on the drink quoted in the *Ion* (538b–c): [[plato-asclepiads]].
- **A healer is worth many.** At Agathon's party Alcibiades, arriving drunk, has drained a wine-cooler full of wine and had it filled again for Socrates, and the doctor Eryximachus objects that they are drinking like thirsty men without speech or song. Alcibiades answers, “We must obey you, for ἰητρὸς γὰρ ἀνὴρ πολλῶν ἀντάξιος ἄλλων; so give whatever orders you like” (*Symposium* 214b). The verse is Idomeneus' as he sends Machaon out of the battle, “for a healer is a man worth many others” (11.514), unchanged; Eryximachus' order is that Alcibiades should make his speech in praise of Love. See [[a-healer-is-worth-many]] and [[machaon]].
- **Patroclus the elder.** In Phaedrus' speech in the *Symposium* the gods honored Achilles because he chose to die for his lover Patroclus; and Aeschylus “talks nonsense” in making Achilles the lover, since Achilles was more beautiful than Patroclus and than all the heroes, still beardless, “and then much the younger, as Homer says” (*Symposium* 179e–180a; see [[aeschylus-myrmidons]]). Homer's word on their ages is in Book 11. Menoetius charged his son when he sent him to Troy: “My child, in lineage Achilles is higher than you, / but you are the elder; in strength he is far the better” (11.786–787; see [[menoetius]] and [[patroclus]]). Homer does not say how much older Patroclus was, and the Iliad calls neither man a lover. Of Achilles' beauty it says that Nireus was the most handsome man who came to Ilios “after the noble son of Peleus” (2.673–674), and that Priam marveled at his stature and looks, for he was like the gods (24.629–630).
- **Chiron.** In the *Republic* Socrates will not have the young believe that Achilles, the son of a goddess and of Peleus, the most temperate of men and a grandson of Zeus, “and reared by the most wise Chiron”, was so disordered as to be both greedy and arrogant toward gods and men (*Republic* 3.391c). In the Iliad the only word of Chiron as Achilles' teacher is Eurypylus' report that Achilles learned his healing drugs from Chiron, “the most just of the Centaurs” (11.831–832); Plato's “most wise” takes the place of Homer's “most just” (see [[chiron]]).
- **Cinyras.** The Cypriot king who sent Agamemnon his corselet as a guest-gift (11.19–23) is a byword for wealth in the *Laws*. The poets, says the Athenian, should be made to teach that the just man is happy whether rich or poor, and that the unjust man is wretched “even if he is richer than Cinyras and Midas” (*Laws* 2.660e). He is reworking the poem of Tyrtaeus that he quoted by name earlier in the dialogue, on the man who has every good thing but courage in war (1.629a–b). See [[cinyras]].
