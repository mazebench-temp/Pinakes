summary: Agamemnon's corselet, the guest-gift of Cinyras of Cyprus, is the most fully described in the poem, banded with κύανος, gold and tin, with three serpents on each side like rainbows (11.19–28). In the fighting a corselet is stripped from Agastrophus and pierced on Odysseus, and in Nestor's story the verb of the corselet, θωρήσσω, arms the Pylians and the Moliones.

**Cinyras' gift.** The corselet comes second in Agamemnon's arming, in the verse of every full arming (11.19 = 3.332; see [[arming-scene]]), and then the verse breaks off to tell where it came from:

{{quote:3d3b6923-e62c-50cf-8b4b-b709a03a28ed}}

The gift is told in the half-verse of the boar's-tusk helmet's history, δῶκε ξεινήϊον εἶναι, “given him to be a guest-gift” (11.20; 10.269; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). Cinyras had heard the great report (κλέος) of the expedition as far as Cyprus, and sent the corselet “to do the king a favor” (11.21–23; see [[cinyras]] and [[cyprus]]). He is named nowhere else in Homer, and the poem does not say that he did anything more for the war.

**The decoration.** οἶμοι (only here in this sense) are bands or stripes: ten of dark κύανος, twelve of gold and twenty of tin, forty-two in all, but how they were laid out is not said (11.24–25; see [[kyanos]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The serpents are of κύανος too (κυάνεοι, 11.26). They rise toward the neck, three on each side, “like the rainbows that the son of Cronus sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28; see [[like-rainbows]]), in their arch or in their sheen: elsewhere Zeus stretches out the rainbow as a sign of war or of storm (17.547–549; see [[serpent]] and [[teras]]). The serpent returns, three-headed, on the strap of the shield (11.38–40; see [[shield]]). No surviving corselet is decorated in this way.

**In the fighting.** Corselets are put to the test, and they hold, or nearly. Iphidamas aims “at the waist, below the corselet”, and the belt stops him (11.234–237; see [[belt]]). Diomedes is stripping “the gleaming corselet of mighty Agastrophus” when Paris' arrow strikes his foot (11.373–377; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Socus' spear passes through Odysseus' shield “and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet” (11.435–436; see [[socus]]), in the two verses with which Menelaus' spear passed through Paris' corselet and Ajax's through Hector's (3.357–358, 7.251–252). Each time before, the spear only cut the tunic and the man swerved from black doom (3.359–360, 7.253–254). This time it tears the skin from Odysseus' ribs, and only Athena keeps it from his entrails (11.437–438; see [[odysseus]] and [[intricately-worked]]).

**The verb of arming.** θωρήσσω, “put on the corselet”, is the word for arming in general. The Achaeans go forward at dawn “armed in their armor” (θωρηχθέντες, 11.49), and in Nestor's story the verb carries the night before his first battle: the Moliones arm (11.709), Athena comes by night “to us, to arm” (11.715), Neleus “would not let me arm” (11.717–718), and the Pylians march armed to the Alpheus (11.725; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Both of Nestor's verses return for Achilles in Book 18: Iris comes to bid him arm in the words of Athena's coming (18.167 = 11.715), and he cannot, for his mother “would not let me arm” until she brings him new armor (18.189 ≈ 11.718; see [[achilles]]).
