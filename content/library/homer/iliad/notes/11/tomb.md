summary: The tomb of Ilus, the old landmark of the plain, is passed in the rout (11.166) and becomes Paris' cover: leaning on the pillar of the “man-made tomb”, he shoots Diomedes (11.369–379). Over the dead, the book's boasts set the honor of burial against the birds.
grc: [στήλῃ, κτεριοῦσί]
en: [pillar, give me burial rites]
---

**The mound of Ilus.** Agamemnon's rout runs “past the mound of ancient Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, through the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree” to the Scaean gates (11.166–170). The grave of Priam's grandfather is a landmark that every Trojan knows, where Hector held his night council in Book 10 (10.415; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[ilus]]).

**The pillar.** In the afternoon Paris shoots from it: “leaning against a pillar on the man-made tomb of Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, an elder of the people long ago” (στήλῃ κεκλιμένος ἀνδροκμήτῳ ἐπὶ τύμβῳ, 11.371–372). ἀνδρόκμητος, “made by men”, occurs only here (see [[man-made]]). The στήλη set on a mound marks a grave: a mound and a pillar are “the prize of the dead” (τύμβῳ τε στήλῃ τε, 16.457 = 16.675), and Achilles' horses, mourning Patroclus, stand as still as a pillar on the tomb of a dead man or woman (17.434–435). The old king's monument is the archer's ambush. Paris “leapt out from his ambush” (ἐκ λόχου, 11.379) to boast over Diomedes (see [[bow]] and [[ambush]]).

**Burial and the birds.** The boasts of Book 11 turn on what will become of the dead. The Trojans killed in Agamemnon's rout lie on the ground, “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.162). The man Diomedes hits “rots, and there are more birds around him than women” (11.395). Odysseus tells the fallen Socus that his father and mother will not close his eyes, but the birds will tear him, “but if I die, the brilliant Achaeans will give me burial rites” (κτεριοῦσί, 11.452–455; see [[socus]]). κτερίζω is to give the dead their due, the burning and the mound (24.38; Od. 1.291; see [[cremation]], [[vultures]] and [[death-in-battle]]).
