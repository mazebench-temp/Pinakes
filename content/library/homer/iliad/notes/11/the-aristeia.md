summary: Book 11 opens with the aristeia of Agamemnon, the name by which the book was known in antiquity (11.15–283): the fullest arming in the poem, a string of kills in pairs, three lion similes and a forest fire, the rout to the Scaean gates, an invocation before the two sons of Antenor, and a wound that ends it. Zeus sets its limit in advance, and Hector's turn begins when it ends. The rest of the book is a series of short aristeiai, each cut short by a wound or by Zeus.

**The shining start.** Strife's shout makes war sweet to the Achaeans (11.10–14), and Agamemnon arms in the fullest arming of the poem; the bronze of his spears shines up to the sky, and Athena and Hera thunder in his honor (11.15–46; see [[arming-scene]]). His aristeia has no blaze of fire from a god, as Diomedes' had (5.4–7), but the light of his own bronze.

**The rout.** At midday the Danaans break through, and Agamemnon “sprang in first” (πρῶτος ὄρουσε, 11.92, again at 11.217). His kills come in pairs, each pair from one chariot or one family, as Diomedes' did (5.144–165): Bianor and his companion Oileus (11.92–100; see [[bianor]] and [[oileus-the-trojan]]); Isus and Antiphus, sons of Priam, whom Achilles had once spared for ransom (11.101–112; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]); and Peisander and Hippolochus, the sons of Antimachus, whose plea he refuses (11.122–147; see [[sons-of-antimachus]] and [[supplication]]). Then he drives the mass before him, “always killing, calling on the Argives” (11.148–154). Similes mark each stage: a lion crushing the fawns of a hind (11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]), a forest fire that drops the thickets (11.155–159), a lion among cattle at night, killing the hindmost (11.172–178; see [[lion]] and [[forest-fire]]). The pursuit runs past the tomb of Ilus and the fig tree to the Scaean gates, and “his irresistible hands were spattered with gore” (11.166–170, 11.169), a verse that returns for Achilles at the height of his own aristeia (20.503).

**The limit.** Where Athena helped Diomedes, the god here sets a bound. Zeus draws Hector out of the fighting (11.163–164), and sends Iris to tell him to hold back as long as Agamemnon rages among the champions, but when he is wounded and takes to his chariot, Zeus will give Hector the power to kill until he reaches the ships and the sun sets (11.181–209; see [[message-repeated]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The audience knows from then on how the aristeia will end, and that it serves another man's day.

**The great opponent.** The narrator calls on the Muses to say who first came against Agamemnon (11.218–220; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). It is no great hero but a young man newly married, Iphidamas son of Antenor, given a history in eight verses, whose spear point bends on the king's belt; and then his elder brother Coon, who wounds Agamemnon through the forearm and is beheaded over his brother's body (11.221–263; see [[iphidamas]], [[coon]], [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[belt]]). “There the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the king, the son of Atreus, fulfilled their fate and went down into the house of Hades” (11.262–263).

**The wound and the end.** Agamemnon fights on while the blood is warm, and when it dries the pain comes on him like the pangs of a woman in labor (11.264–272; see [[the-woman-in-labor]]). He has no prayer and no god to heal him. He mounts his chariot, calls on the leaders to defend the ships, since Zeus has not allowed him to fight all day, and is carried away (11.273–283). His aristeia ends as the pattern ends, with withdrawal (see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), and Hector's begins at once, “when Zeus gave him glory” (11.284–309; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]).

**The rest of the book.** After Agamemnon, each Achaean who excels is stopped, by a wound or by Zeus. Diomedes and Odysseus stand together and kill in pairs (11.310–368), until Paris shoots Diomedes (11.369–400). Odysseus, left alone, holds off the Trojans like a boar at bay and strikes down five of them, until Socus' spear tears his side (11.401–488; see [[the-boar-at-bay]] and [[socus]]). Ajax sweeps the plain like a river in flood (11.489–497), until Zeus puts him to flight (11.544–565; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]] and [[the-donkey-in-the-field]]). Machaon is hit “while he was the best in the fighting” (ἀριστεύοντα, 11.506), and Eurypylus as he strips the man he has killed (11.575–584). Odysseus' rule, “whoever would be best in battle must by all means stand his ground strongly, whether he is struck or strikes another” (11.409–410), is the law of all of them (see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[aristos]]). Nestor's story of his youth, near the end of the book, is an aristeia told by its hero, which no wound interrupts: he kills Mulius, takes fifty chariots, and would have killed the Moliones (11.737–752; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]], [[mulius]] and [[the-moliones]]).

**The name.** In antiquity the book was called the aristeia of Agamemnon, Ἀγαμέμνονος ἀριστεία, though the king leaves the battle a third of the way through it (see [[title-of-book-11]] and [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]).
