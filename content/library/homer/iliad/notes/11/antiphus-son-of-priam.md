**The true-born son.** Antiphus is the γνήσιος, the son of Priam “born in wedlock” (11.102), and Ἄντιφος … περικλυτός, “renowned Antiphus” (11.104), who stands beside the driver to fight, while his bastard brother Isus holds the reins (see [[isus]] and [[nothos]]). In Book 4 he threw at Ajax across the throng and killed Odysseus' companion Leucus (4.489–493). Now Agamemnon spears Isus in the chest, strikes Antiphus “beside the ear with his sword, and threw him from the chariot” (11.109), and strips them both (see [[agamemnon]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**Ransomed once.** Achilles had caught the two on the slopes of Ida as they herded sheep, bound them with willow shoots, and let them go for a ransom; Agamemnon knows them, for he saw them at the ships when Achilles brought them in (11.104–106, 11.111–112; see [[achilles]], [[ida]] and [[apoina]]). The capture belongs to the raids before the poem begins, when Achilles also chased Aeneas from his cattle on Ida (20.89–92, 20.188–191) and took Lycaon by night from his father's orchard and sold him on Lemnos (21.34–42; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[raiding]]). Lycaon too was ransomed, and came back to die. Antiphus and Isus, spared once by Achilles, are not spared by Agamemnon, and the narrator likens him to a lion crushing the young of a hind in their lair while the mother, close by, cannot help them (11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]).
