---
summary: δαΐφρων describes Antimachus, Priam, Hippasus and his son Socus among the Trojans, and Odysseus and Achilles among the Achaeans.
---

**Trojans.** Peisander and Hippolochus are “the sons of wise-hearted Antimachus,” in the narrator's words and in Agamemnon's (11.123, 11.138; [[antimachus]], [[sons-of-antimachus]]). Iris finds “the son of wise-hearted Priam, brilliant Hector” (11.197). Odysseus boasts over Socus as “son of wise-hearted Hippasus, tamer of horses,” and draws out “wise-hearted Socus' heavy spear” (11.450, 11.456; [[hippasus-father-of-socus]], [[socus]]).

**Achaeans.** Odysseus is “wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” in the simile of the jackals (11.482; [[of-subtle-wiles]]). Achilles is “wise-hearted Achilles” twice, both times as the one Patroclus is to persuade: in Nestor's plea, “you might say these things to wise-hearted Achilles” (11.791), and in Patroclus' answer to Eurypylus (11.839; [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]).
