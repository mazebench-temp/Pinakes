---
summary: When Zeus rouses Ajax to flight, he stands amazed, swings his great shield behind his back, and falls back toward the throng “glancing around like a wild beast, turning back again and again, moving knee past knee only a little” (11.544–547). Two full similes follow for the same retreat, the lion and the donkey.
passages:

{{quote:e6f8f3bc-0876-52bf-a55f-94ef18604335}}

τρέσσε δὲ παπτήνας ἐφʼ ὁμίλου θηρὶ ἐοικώς: “and he fell back toward the throng in fear, glancing around like a wild beast” (11.546). The half-verse ends the line, as it does for Menelaus in Book 3 (3.449). παπτήνας is “glancing about”, the wary look of an animal, and ἐντροπαλιζόμενος, “turning back again and again”, is the word for Andromache looking back as she goes home from Hector (6.496). ὀλίγον γόνυ γουνὸς ἀμείβων, “moving knee past knee only a little”, occurs only here: a slow retreat, one step at a time (see the translation's note on 11.546–547).

In Book 3 the wild beast is hunting and has lost its prey. Here it is the one driven back, and it does not run. The beast is not named, but the next verse names it, a tawny lion driven from the cattle yard by dogs and men, and a second simile adds a sluggish donkey (11.548–565; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]] and [[the-donkey-in-the-field]]). The same participle and the same kind of lion return in Book 17, when Menelaus leaves the body of Patroclus “turning back again and again” like a bearded lion driven from the farmyard (17.108–112).
