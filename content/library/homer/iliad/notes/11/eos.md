**Tithonus.** “Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus, / to bring light to the immortals and to mortals” (11.1–2). Only here does the Iliad name Dawn's husband; elsewhere Tithonus is a son of Laomedon and a brother of Priam (20.237; see [[tithonus]] and [[noble]]). The same two verses open the fifth book of the Odyssey, where the gods sit in council and Zeus sends Hermes to free Odysseus (*Odyssey* 5.1–2). The dawn is the one the Trojans waited for at their fires, “until early-born Dawn” (8.508), and for which Hector prayed (9.240; see [[early-born]] and [[war-and-night]]). The day it brings is the third day of battle, which ends only when Hera sends the sun down into Oceanus (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[dawn-line]]).

**Dawn and morning.** Dawn's word is also the time of day. “As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing” (11.84) repeats 8.66, where the index does not count ἠώς as the goddess, and it does not count it here (see the translation's note on 8.66). Nor does it count the cry that went up “in the early dawn” (ἠῶθι πρό, 11.50), the lion that goes off “at dawn” (ἠῶθεν, 11.555), or the heralds who cried out “when dawn appeared” in Nestor's story (ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφι, 11.685), the phrase of Achilles' answers in Book 9, which the index also leaves to the time of day (9.618, 9.682). It counts the goddess who rises at 11.1, and Nestor's “brilliant Dawn”: the Pylian horsemen camped by the river Minyeios, “where we waited for brilliant Dawn” (11.723), as Phoenix waited for her in Achilles' hut (9.662; see [[minyeios]] and [[brilliant]]).
