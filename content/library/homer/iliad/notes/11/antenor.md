**Three sons on the plain.** “The three sons of Antenor”, Ἀντηνορίδας, Polybus, Agenor and Acamas, stand with Hector, Polydamas and Aeneas at the head of the Trojans (11.59–60; see [[polybus]], [[agenor]], [[acamas]] and [[son-of-antenor]]).

**Two sons dead.** When the poet asks the Muses who first came against Agamemnon, the answer is Iphidamas, Antenor's son by Theano, reared in Thrace by her father Cisseus (11.218–226; see [[iphidamas]], [[theano]] and [[cisseus]]). His elder brother Coon, “the eldest-born son of Antenor” (11.249), wounds Agamemnon to avenge him and is beheaded over his body (see [[coon]]). The narrator closes the scene with the father's name:

{{quote:e0f18f9f-1b8c-5a10-ad61-d119d15f1f8b}}

Antenor once received Menelaus and Odysseus as guests when they came to Troy as envoys (3.205–207), and in the Trojan assembly he urged that Helen be given back (7.347–353). Earlier in the same book Agamemnon killed the two sons of Antimachus, who had wanted the envoy Menelaus killed (11.122–147; see [[antimachus]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). The war takes the sons of both houses alike.

The Iliad names eleven sons of Antenor. Pedaeus has already died (5.69–75; see [[pedaeus]]); after Iphidamas and Coon, Archelochus, Laodamas and Demoleon die in later books (14.463–464, 15.516–517, 20.395–400).
