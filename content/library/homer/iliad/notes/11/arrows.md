summary: Arrows turn the second half of Book 11. Paris' shafts take Diomedes, Machaon and Eurypylus out of the battle, and the book ends with an arrowhead cut out of Eurypylus' thigh. Agamemnon's pain is likened to the sharp dart that the goddesses of childbirth send, and Zeus' terms for Hector's turn are “struck by a spear or hit by an arrow”.
grc: ["ἰὸς", "=ἰοί", "=ἰούς", δόναξ, βέλεσιν]
en: [reed, dart]
---

**Spear or arrow.** Zeus tells Hector to hold back until Agamemnon, “struck by a spear or hit by an arrow”, leaps into his chariot (ἢ δουρὶ τυπεὶς ἢ βλήμενος ἰῷ, 11.191 = 11.206; see [[message-repeated]]). It is a spear that wounds Agamemnon (11.252), and arrows that wound the others.

**The dart of labor.** When the wound dries, sharp pains come over Agamemnon, “as when the sharp dart seizes a woman in labor, the piercing dart that the Eileithyiai send, who bring hard birth” (11.269–270). βέλος is any missile, and here it is pain itself, sent by the goddesses of childbirth as the arrows of Apollo and Artemis send death (see [[the-woman-in-labor]], [[who-brings-hard-birth]], [[eileithyia]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**Paris' arrows.** The arrow that strikes Diomedes' foot “did not fly from his hand in vain”, οὐδʼ ἅλιον βέλος ἔκφυγε χειρός (11.376), the half-verse of Diomedes' own spear-cast in Book 5 and of Patroclus' in Book 16 (5.18, 16.480); Paris says it again in his boast (11.380). It passes through the foot and sticks in the ground, and Diomedes pulls “the swift shaft” out himself (11.377–378, 11.397–398). Machaon is hit in the right shoulder “with a three-barbed arrow” (ἰῷ τριγλώχινι, 11.507; see [[three-barbed]]), the kind with which Heracles once shot Hera in the right breast (5.392–394). Eurypylus is struck in the right thigh, “and the reed broke off, and weighed the thigh down” (ἐκλάσθη δὲ δόναξ, ἐβάρυνε δὲ μηρόν, 11.584): the shaft of reed breaks, and the head stays in the flesh (see [[bow]] and [[paris]]). Diomedes calls the archer's weapons βιὸς καὶ ταρφέες ἰοί, “your bow … nor your thick-flying arrows” (11.387), no match for a man in armor.

**The stag.** In the simile of Odysseus beset by the Trojans, a man has hit a horned stag “with an arrow from the string”; it runs as long as its blood is warm, “but when the swift arrow has beaten it down”, the jackals devour it, until a lion comes (11.475–481; see [[jackal]]).

**Cutting out the arrow.** A healer is worth many men, says Idomeneus, “to cut out arrows (ἰούς τʼ ἐκτάμνειν) and spread on soothing drugs” (11.514–515). Eurypylus asks Patroclus to “cut the arrow out of my thigh” (11.829), and Patroclus lays him out and with a knife “cut from his thigh the sharp, piercing arrow” (ὀξὺ βέλος περιπευκές, 11.844–845; see [[healing]] and [[knife]]). περιπευκής, “very sharp”, occurs only here; its stem is that of ἐχεπευκής, “piercing”, the epithet of Apollo's arrow and of Pandarus' (1.51, 4.129).

**Missiles.** βέλεα are missiles of every kind: they strike home on both sides all morning (11.85), Zeus draws Hector out of them (11.163), Ajax and Eurypylus are hard pressed by them (11.576, 11.589), and Nestor speaks of “as many as have been struck by missiles” (11.657).
