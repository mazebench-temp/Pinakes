summary: Menelaus brings Ajax to rescue the wounded Odysseus, and Ajax comes near “carrying his shield like a tower” and stands beside him; the Trojans scatter in fear, as the jackals of the simile scatter when the lion comes (11.485–486).
passages:
  - 11.485-488 | Ajax comes with his shield
---

Αἴας δʼ ἐγγύθεν ἦλθε φέρων σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον: “And Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower” (11.485). The verse is the one that brought Ajax to Hector for their duel in Book 7 (7.219), word for word. There it brought him to a single enemy; here it brings him to a comrade in danger. The verse comes at the end of a simile: the Trojans are pressing around the wounded Odysseus like jackals around a stag, until a god brings a lion and the jackals scatter in fear (11.474–484; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). Ajax is the lion, and the next half-verse uses the jackals' verb, Τρῶες δὲ διέτρεσαν ἄλλυδις ἄλλος, “and the Trojans scattered in fear, this way and that” (11.486). Menelaus leads Odysseus out by the hand while Ajax stands over him (11.487–488; see [[odysseus]] and [[menelaus]]).

The tower does not stand all afternoon. Sixty lines later Zeus puts Ajax to flight, and he swings his shield of seven oxhides onto his back and gives ground, while the Trojans' spears stick in it (11.544–574; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The verse returns a third time, when Ajax comes to stand over the body of Patroclus and Hector draws back (17.128–129).
