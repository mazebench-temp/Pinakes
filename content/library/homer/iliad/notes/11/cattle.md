summary: Cattle are the wealth fought over in Book 11. Nestor's war with the Epeians begins with a cattle raid, in which he kills Itymoneus defending his cows and drives off fifty herds, and Neleus takes three hundred head for himself (11.671–705); Iphidamas gave a hundred cattle for his bride (11.244); oxen and a cow are sacrificed; and in the similes the Trojans flee like cattle before a lion, and a lion is kept from the cattle yard all night (11.172–176, 11.548–555).

**The raid.** Nestor's story of his youth begins with cattle. A quarrel arose between the Eleans and the Pylians “over a cattle raid” (ἀμφὶ βοηλασίῃ, 11.672), when Nestor killed Itymoneus, “as I was driving off cattle in reprisal; and he, defending his cows (ᾗσι βόεσσιν), was struck among the foremost by a javelin from my hand” (11.673–675). βοηλασίη, “a driving of cattle”, occurs only here, and so does ῥύσια, beasts seized in reprisal or as security for a debt (see the translation's note on 11.672–674). The plunder was fifty herds of cattle (βοῶν ἀγέλας), and as many flocks of sheep, droves of pigs and herds of goats (11.677–681; see [[raiding]], [[itymoneus]] and [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). In the division Neleus took for himself “a herd of cattle and a great flock of sheep, choosing out three hundred, and their herdsmen”, in return for the four prize horses that Augeas had kept (11.696–702; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[augeas]]). What Achilles said in the embassy, that cattle can be had by raiding (9.406), Nestor's story shows being done.

**Gifts and sacrifices.** Iphidamas gave “a hundred cattle” to win his bride and promised a thousand goats and sheep (11.244–245; see [[marriage]]). At the river Alpheus the Pylians sacrificed a bull to the river and a bull to Poseidon, and to Athena “a cow from the herd” (βοῦν ἀγελαίην, 11.728–729; see [[bulls]]). In Phthia, Nestor found Peleus burning the fat thighs of an ox to Zeus (11.772–776; see [[sacrifice]]).

**Similes.** The Trojans flee across the plain “like cattle” that a lion has routed by night, and the lion breaks the neck of one (11.172–176; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). In the afternoon the herd is safe: dogs and country men keep a lion from “the fattest of the cattle” all night, and drive it off at dawn (11.548–555; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).
