The heads of the people fall as thick as the spray flies (see [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]] and [[hector]]). Two winds meet. The clouds are the South Wind's, and the West Wind drives into them with a squall and lifts the sea; the blast of 11.308 is his, “the wandering wind” (see [[notus]] and [[far-wandering]]). The same two winds are named together once more, when Hera promises to raise a storm of the West Wind and the brightening South Wind from the sea, to carry Hephaestus' fire against the Trojans (21.334–337). Here too, as in the similes of Books 2, 4 and 9, the West Wind is a wind of force, not the gentle breeze of later poetry (see the article above).
