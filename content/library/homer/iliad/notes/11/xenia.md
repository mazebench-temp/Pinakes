**A gift from afar.** The corselet Agamemnon puts on was given him by Cinyras “to be a guest-gift” (ξεινήϊον, 11.20), when the report of the expedition reached Cyprus (11.21–23; see [[cinyras]], [[kleos]] and [[corselet]]). The half-verse is the one in which Amphidamas gave the boar's tusk helmet to Molus (10.269; see the note on Book 10 and [[guest-gifts]]). The poem says nothing of a visit. Cinyras gave the gift “to do the king a favor” (χαριζόμενος, 11.23; see [[charis]]), a guest-gift sent across the sea by a king who had heard of a greater one, and Agamemnon arms in it on the day the poem first describes it (see [[arming-scene]]).

**The custom with guests.** Nestor's memory of Phthia is set in a scene of hospitality. He and Odysseus came to Peleus' house to gather the army, and found Peleus sacrificing in the courtyard and the two young men busy with the meat (11.769–776; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]):

{{quote:0e8347c3-7f9e-596a-8145-7865f00022ec}}

ξείνια are what is set before a guest, food and drink, and the translation renders them “good cheer”; θέμις is the custom that requires them (see [[themis]]). Achilles welcomed the envoys of Book 9 in the same way, when he “sprang up in amazement” and led them in and seated them (9.193–200; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). The young Achilles of Nestor's memory is the host he still is.

**The guest who will not sit.** At Nestor's hut the roles are repeated. Patroclus “stood at the door”, as Nestor and Odysseus once stood in Peleus' doorway, and the old man rose from his shining chair, “and took him by the hand and led him in, and bade him sit down” (11.644–646), in the verse of Achilles' welcome (11.646 = 11.778). Patroclus refuses: “There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, and you will not persuade me” (11.648), since the man who sent him is quick to blame (see [[nemesis]] and [[patroclus]]). Nestor keeps him all the same, with the longest speech in the book (11.656–803).
