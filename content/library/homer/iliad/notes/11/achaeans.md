---
summary: The Achaeans of Book 11 begin the day filled with Strife's strength, finding war sweeter than going home, and end it with their best men wounded. Achilles thinks they will now come to his knees, and Eurypylus that they will fall among the black ships (11.11–14, 11.609, 11.824).
---

**Strength from Strife.** Zeus sends Strife “to the swift ships of the Achaeans”, and from Odysseus' ship she “put great strength into each of the Achaeans, / in his heart, to go to war without ceasing and to fight” (11.3–12; see [[strife]]). Then: “And at once war became sweeter to them than to go home / in the hollow ships to their dear native land” (11.13–14). The verses are those of Book 2, where Athena went through the army with the aegis after the test that nearly sent it home (11.12–14 = 2.452–454; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]), and Poseidon's cry puts the same strength into them in Book 14 (14.151–152).

**The report.** Their coming was news across the sea. Cinyras heard in Cyprus “the great report that the Achaeans / were about to sail up to Troy in their ships”, and sent Agamemnon a gift (11.21–23; see [[cinyras]]); Iphidamas left his bride in Thrace to fight them, “at the report of the Achaeans” (11.227; see [[iphidamas]]).

**The ships.** They meet the Trojans like reapers in a rich man's field, break the battalions under Agamemnon, and then are driven back by Hector, until “the Achaeans would have fled and fallen among their ships” had Odysseus not called on Diomedes (11.67–71, 11.310–312; see [[agamemnon]] and [[hector]]). The ships are what the day is fought for. Zeus looks out on “the ships of the Achaeans” from his seat apart (11.82), Ajax gives ground fearing for them (11.557; see [[ajax]]), and Eurypylus says that the Achaeans “will fall among the black ships” (11.824; see [[ships]]).

**Honor and burial.** The army gives honor and burial. The Achaeans chose Hecamede for Nestor from the spoil of Tenedos, “because he was the best of all in counsel” (11.626–627; see [[hecamede]]), and Odysseus, standing over Socus, is sure that “the brilliant Achaeans will give me burial rites” if he dies (11.455).

**Seen from Achilles' ship.** Achilles, watching from his stern, says to Patroclus: “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees / beseeching me; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.609–610; see [[achilles]]). Nestor asks why Achilles grieves now “for the sons of the Achaeans”, when he does not care for the Danaans or pity them (11.656–665; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and [[nestor]]).

**An older war.** In Nestor's story the name reaches back a generation: after the rout of the Epeians, “the Achaeans / drove their swift horses back from Buprasium to Pylos” (11.759–760). These Achaeans are Nestor's Pylians (see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[pylians]]). When Nestor and Odysseus came to Peleus' house, they were gathering the army “throughout the bountiful land of Achaea” (11.770; see [[achaea]]).
