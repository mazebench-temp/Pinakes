summary: Book 11 gives Patroclus his first words and his first errand beyond Achilles' hut: sent to ask Nestor about the wounded Machaon, “and this was the beginning of evil for him”, he hears Nestor's reminder of his father's charge and the plan that he go out in Achilles' armor. Running back, he stops for the wounded Eurypylus, pities him, and cuts the arrow from his thigh.

**Called out.** From the stern of his ship Achilles sees Nestor's mares carry a wounded man past, and he calls his companion (11.597–603; see [[achilles]]).

{{quote:35174cbb-0698-5bbe-9586-4ed3c05a4d9f}}

ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, “the equal of Ares”, is the title the book has just given Hector (11.295). κακοῦ δʼ ἄρα οἱ πέλεν ἀρχή is the narrator's own first word that Patroclus is going to his death (see the translation's note on 11.604, [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Zeus foretold it to Hera alone (8.476); now the narrator tells the listener. Ares comes back at the end: three times he charges, “the equal of swift Ares”, and at the fourth onset “then, Patroclus, the end of your life appeared” (16.784–787).

**His first words.** In Books 1 and 9 he obeyed and served in silence. His first words in the poem are a question: “Why do you call me, Achilles? What need do you have of me?” (11.606). Achilles answers with the army's need: he thinks the Achaeans will now come to his knees, “for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.609–610), and he wants to know whether the wounded man is Machaon (11.611–615; see [[machaon]]). He calls him “Brilliant son of Menoetius, dear to my heart” (11.608), the address with which Sthenelus, Athena and Agamemnon speak to Diomedes (5.243, 5.826, 10.234); Briseis, mourning over his body, will call him “dearest to my heart” (19.287). “So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (11.616) is the verse of his silent obedience in Books 1 and 9 (1.345, 9.205). This time it sends him running along the ships (11.617).

**At Nestor's door.** He comes when Nestor and Machaon have drunk Hecamede's mixed drink and are talking, and stands at the door, “a man equal to the gods” (11.644; see [[equal-to-the-gods]], [[hecamede]] and [[nestor]]). The old man rises from his chair, takes him by the hand and bids him sit, in the verse with which, in Nestor's own story, the young Achilles welcomed Nestor and Odysseus at Peleus' house (11.646 = 11.778). Patroclus refuses, as Hector would not sit when Helen asked him (6.360):

{{quote:75d1c76d-0946-5306-9138-5141a9c7785f}}

νεμεσητός is “quick to blame” (see [[nemesis]]). He has no time to sit, and Nestor's answer runs to nearly a hundred and fifty verses (11.656–803), the longest speech in the book.

**The charge and the plan.** Nestor speaks of Achilles' indifference and of his own youth (11.656–762; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]), and then turns to Patroclus. In Peleus' house Menoetius charged his son to counsel Achilles, since he was the elder: “So the old man charged you, but you forget” (11.765–790; see [[menoetius]] and [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). Let him speak to Achilles now: “Who knows if, with a god's help, you might stir his heart / by persuading him? Good is the persuasion of a companion” (11.792–793; see [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). And if Achilles is shunning some oracle from his mother, let him at least send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons and give him his armor, so that the Trojans may take him for Achilles (11.794–800; see [[myrmidons]]):

{{quote:300e1e14-f2e9-5e42-b59e-da2430d479db}}

The words work on him at once. Nestor hoped that Patroclus might “stir” (ὀρίναις) Achilles' heart, and it is Patroclus' heart that he stirs: “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast” (ὄρινε, 11.804). When at last he leaves Eurypylus, he takes Nestor's words with him in the first person: “Who knows if, with a god's help, I might stir his heart by persuading him?” (15.403–404). In Book 16 he puts the plan to Achilles nearly word for word (16.36–45 ≈ 11.794–803), and the narrator calls him “great fool”, for he was begging for his own death (16.46–47; see [[nepios]]).

**Eurypylus.** Running back, by the ships of Odysseus he meets Eurypylus limping out of the battle with an arrow in his thigh, sweating and bleeding, “but his mind was steady” (11.806–813; see [[eurypylus]]).

{{quote:dba7a693-c146-554c-a08e-5fc344b8ee32}}

ᾤκτειρε, “pitied”: Nestor has just said that Achilles “does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.665), and Patroclus pities the first wounded man he meets (see [[pity]]). He asks whether the Achaeans can still hold Hector (11.819–821). Eurypylus answers that all the best lie wounded at the ships, and begs him to cut out the arrow and spread on the drugs “which they say you have learned from Achilles, whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs”, since Machaon, he thinks, lies wounded in the huts and Podalirius is fighting on the plain (11.822–836; see [[chiron]]). The errand and the wounded man pull him two ways: “How can these things be? What shall we do, hero Eurypylus?” He is on his way to Achilles with Nestor's word, “but even so I will not leave you in your distress” (11.838–841).

**The healer.** He leads Eurypylus to the hut, cuts out the arrow with a knife, washes away the blood with warm water, and lays on a bitter root that stays the pain (11.842–848; see [[healing]]). He does what Eurypylus asked, in the verses of the request (11.845–846 ≈ 11.829–830). The last verse, “and the wound dried, and the blood stopped” (11.848), is the verse of Agamemnon's wound, where the drying brought on the pains (11.267–268); here the pains have gone first. The book leaves Patroclus as a healer, using knowledge that came from Chiron through Achilles. He stays in Eurypylus' hut, cheering him with talk and spreading drugs on the wound, until the Trojans storm the wall (12.1–2, 15.390–404).
