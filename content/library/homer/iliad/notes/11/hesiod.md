---
summary: Hesiod's poems and the fragments of the Catalogue of Women fill in what Book 11 leaves untold or tells otherwise. They give Dawn and Tithonus their sons, make Eileithyia one daughter of Zeus and Hera, give the epithet “brightening” to the West Wind, describe a shield of Rout and Strife and Gorgons, and tell how Nestor alone of Neleus' sons escaped Heracles and how the Moliones were born to Molione and Poseidon.
---

- **Dawn and Tithonus.** The book opens as Dawn rises from the bed of Tithonus (11.1–2). Hesiod names their sons: “To Tithonus Dawn bore Memnon, armed in bronze, king of the Ethiopians, and lord Emathion” (*Theogony* 984–985). Homer knows Memnon only in the Odyssey, as the “glorious son of shining Dawn” who killed Antilochus (*Odyssey* 4.187–188; 11.522). See [[tithonus]], [[eos]] and [[the-tithonus-verse]].
- **Strife.** Zeus sends Strife to the ships at dawn, and she alone of the gods is present in the battle (11.3–4, 11.73–74). In the *Theogony* Night bears Strife, with Nemesis, Deceit and Old Age among her other children (223–225); the *Works and Days* knows two Strifes, one who fosters war and one who stirs men to work (11–26). See [[strife]] and [[hesiods-two-strifes]].
- **The shield.** Agamemnon's shield has a boss of dark κύανος, the grim-faced Gorgon with Terror and Rout around her, and a three-headed serpent of κύανος on its strap (11.32–40). The *Shield of Heracles*, ascribed to Hesiod, gives Heracles' shield bands of κύανος, sets Rout (Φόβος) in its middle with dread Strife hovering over his brow, stands Terror and Rout beside Ares in Homer's words (Δεῖμός τε Φόβος τε, *Shield* 195; 11.37), and shows the Gorgons, with serpents at their belts, pursuing Perseus (141–156, 191–196, 216–237). See [[shield]], [[gorgon]], [[terror]] and [[rout]].
- **The log.** Agamemnon sends the headless trunk of Hippolochus rolling through the throng “like a log” (ὅλμος, 11.147; see [[hippolochus-son-of-antimachus]]). Hesiod's ὅλμος is a mortar: the farmer is to cut “a mortar three feet long, a pestle three cubits, an axle seven feet” (*Works and Days* 423–424; see the translation's note on 11.147).
- **Eileithyia.** In the simile of Agamemnon's wound the Eileithyiai, who send the sharp pangs of birth, are “the daughters of Hera” (11.270–271). Hesiod's Eileithyia is one goddess, born to Zeus and Hera with Hebe and Ares (*Theogony* 921–923). See [[eileithyia]] and [[hera]].
- **The brightening wind.** Homer gives the epithet ἀργεστής only to the South Wind, “the brightening South Wind” whose clouds the West Wind batters (11.306; also 21.334). Hesiod gives it to the West Wind, “brightening Zephyrus”, one of the winds that Dawn bore to Astraeus (*Theogony* 378–380, 870). See [[notus]] and [[zephyrus]].
- **The sons of Neleus.** Nestor says only that Heracles came and crushed the Pylians, and that of the twelve sons of Neleus he alone was left (11.689–693). The *Catalogue of Women* told more: Heracles “killed the noble sons of steadfast Neleus, / eleven of them; the twelfth, Gerenian Nestor the horseman, / happened to be a guest among the horse-taming Gerenians”, and “Nestor alone escaped, in flowery Gerenus” (fr. 11 Evelyn-White, quoted by Stephanus of Byzantium under Gerenia). Another fragment tells how Periclymenus, to whom Poseidon gave the power to take every shape, settled on the yoke of Heracles' horses to fight him, until Athena pointed him out and Heracles shot him (fr. 10, from the scholia on Apollonius, *Argonautica* 1.156); the Odyssey names him among the sons of Neleus and Chloris, in the same words (Περικλύμενόν τʼ ἀγέρωχον, *Odyssey* 11.286). Hesiod's Gerenians give a reason for Nestor's epithet. See [[heracles-sacks-pylos]], [[neleus]], [[nestor]] and [[gerenian-horseman]].
- **The Moliones.** Nestor calls the brothers “the two sons of Actor, the Moliones”, and says that their father, Poseidon, saved them in a mist (11.750–752). Ancient commentary on 11.750, in the Venetian scholia and Porphyry's *Homeric Questions*, reports that Hesiod made them sons of Actor and Molione by name, but of Poseidon by birth, and that Aristarchus, with Hesiod as his witness, took them for “double” men, with two bodies grown together, not twins like the Dioscuri (fr. 9 Evelyn-White; on the “twins” of 23.641). See [[the-moliones]] and [[actor-of-elis]].
