summary: Book 11 opens the long day on which the plan of Zeus is carried to the ships. Zeus sends Strife, sheds dew wet with blood because he means to send many to Hades, and sits apart exulting while the other gods blame him; then he sends Iris with the terms of Hector's day, power to kill until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down (11.181–209), and the battle turns as he said.

**The proem's verse.** Zeus begins the day by sending Strife to the ships (11.3–4), and as the armies form he sends down from the upper air dewdrops wet with blood, “because he meant / to hurl down to Hades many mighty heads” (11.54–55). The verse is the proem's, “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls / of heroes” (1.3–4), with heads for souls, and two verses later the proem names the cause: “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5). The bloody dew is a sign of what the day will cost, as his bloody rain will be before Sarpedon dies (16.459–461; see [[zeus]]).

**Apart.** Strife alone of the gods is in the battle. The others sit in their halls on Olympus and blame Zeus, “because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (ἐβούλετο, 11.79), a verb of the same family as βουλή, “plan”. He pays them no heed, and sits apart, exulting in his glory, looking out on the city and the ships, “on the flash of bronze, and the slayers and the slain” (11.73–83; see [[watching-the-war]] and [[strife]]). The ban of the second day still holds (8.5–27); in Book 13 Poseidon will break it while Zeus looks away (13.1–16).

**The terms.** When Agamemnon nears the wall of Troy, Zeus sits down on Ida with the lightning in his hands and sends Iris to Hector (11.181–194; see [[iris]] and [[message-repeated]]). For the first time the plan is given a schedule. Hector is to hold back while Agamemnon rages; when the king is struck and takes to his chariot, Zeus will put power in Hector's hands to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships, and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.192–194). Each step comes to pass. Coon's spear strikes Agamemnon, and he mounts his chariot (11.252–274). Hector tells the Trojans that Zeus has given him a great triumph, and the narrator agrees, “when Zeus gave him glory” (11.288–289, 11.300). The rest of the day fills Books 12 to 18 (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**Read by men.** The men on the field know whose day it is. Agamemnon leaves because “Zeus the counselor did not allow me to fight the Trojans all day long” (11.278–279). Diomedes will stay, though “Zeus the cloud-gatherer wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us” (11.318–319), and Odysseus, alone, says that “the son of Cronus has put the other Danaans to rout” (11.406). Zeus stretches the battle evenly for a time (11.336–337), and then rouses Ajax, the last defender, to flight (11.544). A verse known only from Aristotle's quotation, printed in brackets, adds that Zeus was indignant with Hector whenever he fought a better man (11.543; see [[aristotle-nemesis]]).

**Achilles' wish and its price.** Achilles, watching from his ship, sees the plan reach the point he asked for, the Achaeans penned in among the sterns of their ships (1.408–410): “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me” (11.609–610). He does not know the price, which Zeus told Hera at the end of the second day, Patroclus dead (8.470–477). The narrator marks its first step when Patroclus comes out of the hut at Achilles' call, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Nestor guesses that Achilles holds back because of something from Zeus that his mother has told him (11.794–795); in Book 16 Achilles denies it (16.50–51).
