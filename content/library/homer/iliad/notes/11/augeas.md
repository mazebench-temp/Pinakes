**The horses.** “For to him too a great debt was owed in shining Elis, / four prize-winning horses with their chariot, / which had gone to the games; for they were going / to race for a tripod; but Augeas, lord of men, kept them there, / and sent the driver away, grieving for his horses” (11.698–702). The owner is Neleus (see [[neleus]]). Augeas is ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν, “lord of men”, the title the Iliad gives above all to Agamemnon (see [[lord-of-men]]), and the horses are ἀθλοφόροι, “prize-winning” (see [[prize-winning]], [[contests]] and [[tripod]]). A team of four is rare in Homer: Hector calls on four horses by name (8.185), and in the Odyssey four stallions draw a chariot in a simile (*Odyssey* 13.81). Neleus was “angered by these words and deeds” (11.703); what words, Nestor does not say. The wrong is one of the debts that the Epeians owed the Pylians and that Nestor's raid set out to recover (11.686–688, 11.698; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). The games were in Elis, but the poem gives them no name or place, and Homer never mentions Olympia.

**His family.** Nestor's first victim in the battle was Mulius the spearman, “the son-in-law of Augeas”, who “had his eldest daughter, fair-haired Agamede, / who knew all the drugs that the wide earth nourishes” (11.739–741; see [[mulius]] and [[agamede]]). The Catalogue knows him as the grandfather of the Epeian leader Polyxeinus through his son Agasthenes (2.623–624; see [[polyxeinus]] and [[agasthenes]]).

## Outside Homer

The Augeas who keeps another man's horses is the Augeas of later legend who withheld Heracles' wage. Pindar calls him “the guest-cheating king of the Epeians” (ξεναπάτας Ἐπειῶν βασιλεύς), and has Heracles kill the Moliones and sack his city “so that he might exact from the unwilling Augeas the mighty wage for his service” (*Olympian* 10.24–38; see [[the-moliones]]). In Apollodorus Heracles kills him before he takes Pylos (*Library* 2.7.2–3), which leaves no room for Nestor's war (see [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]).
