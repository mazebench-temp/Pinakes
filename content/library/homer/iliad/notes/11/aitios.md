**The gods blame Zeus.** The gods who are not in the battle sit in their halls on Olympus, “and they all blamed the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (ᾐτιόωντο, 11.78–79). The blame is real and it changes nothing: “the father paid them no heed”, and sat apart, exulting (11.80–83; see [[zeus]], [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[watching-the-war]]). In the Odyssey Zeus complains of the opposite habit, that mortals blame (αἰτιόωνται) the gods for evils they bring on themselves (*Odyssey* 1.32–34; see the article above).

**Blaming the blameless.** Patroclus will not sit in Nestor's hut, because the man who sent him is “quick to blame”: “You know well, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, what kind of man he is, a dread man; he would soon blame even one who is blameless” (τάχα κεν καὶ ἀναίτιον αἰτιόῳτο, 11.653–654; see [[nemesis]] and [[achilles]]). ἀναίτιος, “blameless”, appears here for the first time in the Iliad, and the pairing of the two words returns twice. Paris answers Hector's rebuke in Book 13 with it, “Hector, since your heart is set on blaming one who is not to blame” (13.775), and in the Odyssey Eurycleia tells Telemachus, who suspects his mother of neglecting a guest, not to blame her, for she is blameless (*Odyssey* 20.129–135). Paris and Eurycleia say it to the man who blames. Patroclus says it of Achilles, and to Nestor.
