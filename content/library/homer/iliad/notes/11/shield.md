summary: Agamemnon arms with a round ἀσπίς of ten bronze circles and twenty tin bosses, with the grim-faced Gorgon at its center and a three-headed serpent on its strap (11.32–40), the only shield in the poem with a single emblem. In the fighting shields are stabbed, pierced, stripped and trampled; Ajax's great σάκος holds the Trojans off; and the Achaeans stand by him with their shields sloped on their shoulders.

**Agamemnon's shield.** In the other armings the shield is taken up in a formula, “and then his shield, great and heavy” (3.335). Agamemnon's has nine verses of its own:

{{quote:546f2f8f-ee4e-5113-8a6d-31d346daf248}}

It is an ἀσπίς, and its three adjectives are given together only here. ἀμφιβρότη, “man-covering”, is the great shield that covers a man from head to foot; Agamemnon himself warned that many a man “will sweat through the strap across his chest of his man-covering shield” (2.388–389; see [[man-covering]]). πολυδαίδαλος, “intricately worked”, is the word of fine metalwork (see [[intricately-worked]]). θοῦρις, “furious”, is the epithet of courage (θοῦρις ἀλκή), and of the aegis, which Apollo holds as he leads the Trojans (15.307–308); it is given to a shield once more, the one Aeneas holds before his chest as he advances on Achilles (20.161–163; see [[furious]]). The shield is round, built of κύκλοι, “circles”, ten of bronze, with twenty ὀμφαλοί, “bosses”, of white tin and a central one of dark κύανος (11.33–35; see [[bossed]] and [[kyanos]]).

**The device.** At its center the Gorgon “was set as a crown”, ἐστεφάνωτο, the verb used of Rout around the aegis (5.739), “glaring terribly, and around her were Terror and Rout” (11.36–37; see [[gorgon]], [[terror]] and [[rout]]). On the aegis the Gorgon is only a head (5.741); here she is named, Γοργὼ βλοσυρῶπις, “the grim-faced Gorgon”, a compound found only here (see [[grim-faced]]). It is the only shield in the poem with a single emblem, the kind of device that the shields of later Greek warriors carried; a Gorgon's face is a common shield device in Greek art from the seventh century BC on. The strap (τελαμών) is of silver, and on it writhes a serpent of κύανος with three heads turned every way from one neck (11.38–40; see [[turned-every-way]] and [[serpent]]), like the serpents of the corselet (11.26–28; see [[corselet]]).

**In the fighting.** The bossed shield marks the place of a blow: Agamemnon stabs Coon “beneath the bossed shield” as he drags away his brother, and Odysseus stabs Chersidamas in the navel beneath his (11.259–260, 11.424; see [[bossed]]). Socus' spear goes through Odysseus' shining shield and his corselet, and Odysseus draws it out of his flesh and out of the bossed shield (11.434–436, 11.456–457). Diomedes strips Agastrophus' shield from his shoulders (11.374), and Hector's chariot runs over corpses and shields (11.534). Hector's own shield is “balanced on every side” (11.61; see [[balanced-on-every-side]]).

**Ajax's shield.** The second half of the battle turns on the great σάκος of Ajax, “like a tower” (11.485), the “broad shield around his shoulders” by which Cebriones knows him (11.526–527), swung behind his back as he retreats and bristling with the spears that stick in it (11.545, 11.565, 11.572; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

**Shields on the shoulders.** When Eurypylus calls on the leaders to stand by Ajax, they come “and stood close beside him, leaning their shields against their shoulders, and holding up their spears” (11.593–594). The half-verse σάκεʼ ὤμοισι κλίναντες returns when the Achaeans close up around Idomeneus (13.488) and when they come up to the wall of Troy after the rout (22.4; see [[battle-order]]).
