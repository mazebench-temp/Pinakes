**Wounded.** The Achaeans would not yet have given ground “had not Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair, / stopped Machaon, shepherd of the people, while he was the best in the fighting” (11.505–506). ἀριστεύοντα, “being the best”, is said of a healer in the front line (see [[aristos]]). A three-barbed arrow strikes his right shoulder, and the Achaeans, “breathing fury”, are terribly afraid that the enemy may take him if the battle turns (11.507–509; see [[paris]] and [[arrows]]). In Book 4 he drew Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus' belt (4.213–219). Idomeneus sends him off with Nestor, “for a healer is a man worth many others” (11.514; see [[idomeneus]] and [[nestor]]), and he mounts the old man's chariot as “the son of Asclepius, the noble healer” (11.518; 4.194; see [[asclepius]]).

**Seen from the ship.** The Neleian mares carry Nestor and Machaon past Achilles' ship, and Achilles, watching from the stern, sends Patroclus to find out who the wounded man is: “From behind, it is true, he looks in every way like Machaon, / the son of Asclepius, but I did not see the man's eyes” (11.597–615; see [[achilles]]). The errand is “the beginning of evil” for Patroclus (11.604; see [[patroclus]]). At Nestor's door Patroclus knows him at once: “I see Machaon, shepherd of the people” (11.651).

**In the hut.** The two men let the sweat dry in the breeze from the sea and sit down on chairs. Hecamede sets out a table, an onion, honey and barley, and in Nestor's great cup mixes Pramnian wine with grated goat's cheese and white barley meal (11.618–641; see [[hecamede]] and [[the-cup-of-nestor]]). The drink is for “their parching thirst”, and then they take pleasure in talk (11.642–643). Nothing is done for the wound. In Plato's *Republic* the same mixture is given to the wounded Eurypylus, and the sons of Asclepius find no fault with it (3.405e–406a; see [[plato-asclepiads]]).

**The healer without a healer.** When Eurypylus needs his arrow cut out, the army's two healers are out of reach: one, Machaon, “with a wound, in the huts, / himself in need of a noble healer”, the other, Podalirius, on the plain (11.833–836; see [[eurypylus]] and [[podalirius]]). The words are his father's title, ἀμύμονος ἰητῆρος, “the noble healer” (4.194, 11.518). Machaon says nothing in this book, and in the whole poem he never speaks. When he appears again, Nestor leaves him drinking while Hecamede heats water for a bath to wash the blood away (14.1–8).
