summary: Book 11 is Agamemnon's great day. He arms in the longest arming scene of the poem, breaks the Trojan line, refuses the sons of Antimachus their ransom and drives the Trojans to the Scaean gates, until a spear through the arm, the wound that Zeus had named as the end of his triumph, sends him back to the ships and lets Hector loose.

**Armed.** Strife's cry puts strength into every Achaean (11.10–14; see [[strife]]), and the king calls the Argives to arms, “and he himself put on the gleaming bronze” (11.15–16). His arming, the longest in the poem, is built on the verses of Paris' (11.17–19 = 3.330–332), but every piece is drawn out: the corselet that Cinyras sent from Cyprus, the great shield with the grim-faced Gorgon, Terror and Rout, the helmet of Athena's arming (11.41 = 5.743), and spears whose bronze shines up into the sky (11.17–45; see [[arming-scene]], [[cinyras]], [[corselet]] and [[shield]]). Then a sound that is elsewhere Zeus' own: “Athena and Hera thundered over him, / honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold” (11.45–46; see [[athena]], [[hera]] and [[mycenae]]).

**First in.** At the hour of the woodcutter's meal the Danaans break the battalions, “and Agamemnon / sprang in first” (11.91–92). He kills Bianor and his companion Oileus, driver of horses, and strips their tunics (11.92–100; see [[bianor]] and [[oileus-the-trojan]]). Then Isus and Antiphus, two sons of Priam in one chariot, whom Achilles had once caught herding sheep on Ida and released for ransom; Agamemnon knows them from the ships (11.101–112; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]). Achilles spared many Trojans in those days (21.100–102); Agamemnon kills both, as a lion crushes the fawns of a hind that cannot help them (11.113–121; see [[lion]]).

**No ransom.** Peisander and Hippolochus, sons of Antimachus, lose the reins and beg from their chariot in the verses with which Adrastus begged Menelaus: “Take us alive, son of Atreus, and accept a worthy ransom” (11.131–135 ≈ 6.46–50; see [[supplication]] and [[sons-of-antimachus]]). In Book 6 Agamemnon talked his brother out of mercy (6.55–62). Now he gives a reason of his own:

{{quote:63d8e970-c67a-5707-a767-485305567509}}

On that embassy Antenor received Menelaus and Odysseus as guests (3.205–207; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[antimachus]]); the insult was to the king's brother. To their soothing words “the voice they heard would not be soothed” (11.137), the half-verse that closes Lycaon's plea to Achilles (21.98; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). He cuts off Hippolochus' arms and head and sends the trunk “rolling like a log through the throng” (11.143–147).

**To the gates.** The Trojans fall before him like thickets before a forest fire, and their horses rattle empty chariots along “the bridges of war” (11.148–162; see [[forest-fire]]). Zeus draws Hector out of the fighting (11.163–164). The rout runs past the mound of Ilus and the wild fig tree, and the king follows, shouting, “and his irresistible hands were spattered with gore” (11.166–169; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]), the half-verse that ends Book 20, of Achilles (20.503). At the Scaean gates and the oak the Trojans begin to halt, and he pursues the rest, “always killing the hindmost; and they fled” (11.170–178; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]), Hector's verse in Book 8 (8.342).

**Zeus' limit.** As the king nears the wall, Zeus sends Iris from Ida to Hector: while Agamemnon rages among the champions, Hector is to give ground; but “when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow, / he leaps into his chariot”, Zeus will give Hector power to kill until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down (11.181–194, 11.200–209; see [[message-repeated]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The triumph has a limit, set before the wound comes, and only Hector is told of it (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**Iphidamas and Coon.** The narrator calls on the Muses for the first man who came against him (11.218–220; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). It is Iphidamas, son of Antenor, newly married, whose spear point bends like lead on the silver of the king's belt (11.221–237; see [[iphidamas]] and [[belt]]). Agamemnon wrenches the spear from his hand, “eager as a lion”, kills him with the sword and strips him (11.238–247). The elder brother, Coon, unseen, stabs the king through the arm below the elbow. “Then Agamemnon, lord of men, shuddered; / but not even so did he cease from battle and war” (11.254–255): he beheads Coon over his brother's body (11.248–261; see [[coon]]). Antenor had urged the Trojans to give Helen back (7.347–353), and Antimachus had kept her from being given (11.123–125); the king has killed two sons of each (see [[antenor]]).

**The wound.** While the blood still flows warm he ranges through the ranks, “with spear and sword and with great stones” (11.264–266); later the same two verses carry Hector through the fighting (11.540–541). When the wound dries, the pains come on him like the pangs that the Eileithyiai send a woman in labor (11.267–272; see [[eileithyia]]). He leaps into his chariot, as Zeus foretold, and leaves in the verses that will take Diomedes away (11.273–274 = 11.399–400). His last words are to the army:

{{quote:0f3180a9-3e69-57e0-85c6-ae204f4324e6}}

In Books 2 and 9 he said that Zeus had devised “an evil deceit” for him (2.114 = 9.21). This time he is right about Zeus without knowing it: the limit was set in a message he never heard. The horses carry “the worn-out king away from the battle” (11.283), and Hector cries, “Their best man is gone” (11.288; see [[hector]] and [[aristos]]).

**Afterward.** Nestor counts him among the wounded (11.658–661; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), as Patroclus does before Achilles (16.25–26). The wound keeps him out of the fighting for the rest of the day, but not out of command: in Book 14 he proposes flight for the third time, and after Odysseus' rebuke leads the wounded kings back to marshal the army (14.65–134, 14.379–382). At the reconciliation he comes last to the assembly, still wounded from Coon's spear (19.51–53).
