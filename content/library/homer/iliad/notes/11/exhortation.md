summary: Book 11 is full of calls to fight. Strife's shout fills the Achaeans with strength; Agamemnon, leaving the field, calls on the leaders to defend the ships; Hector urges the Trojans on with the news that the Achaeans' best man is gone and Zeus has given him the triumph; Odysseus shames Diomedes into standing; and the wounded Eurypylus calls the leaders to stand by Ajax.

**A goddess's shout.** The day begins with Strife standing on Odysseus' ship and shouting, “loud and terrible, shrill”, so that she put great strength into each of the Achaeans, “to go to war without ceasing and to fight”, and war became sweeter to them than going home (11.10–14; see [[strife]]). It is an exhortation without words, a god's war cry; the first verse of the shout is the one in which Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, stood and shouted to shame the Argives (11.10 ≈ 5.784).

**Agamemnon's farewell.** When the pain drives him from the field, Agamemnon “shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Danaans” (11.275), in the verse with which he called to them from Odysseus' ship in Book 8 (8.227): “Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives, it is for you now to ward off from the seafaring ships the grievous din of battle, since Zeus the counselor did not allow me to fight the Trojans all day long” (11.276–279). The address is the leaders' own (as 2.79, 9.17, 10.533), and the reason is the will of Zeus (see [[agamemnon]]).

**Hector's call.** The moment Agamemnon leaves, Hector does what Zeus' message told him to wait for:

The command is the whole verse of his rallies, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (11.287 = 6.112, 8.174), and the reasons are the ones the pattern supplies: the enemy's best man is gone (ὤριστος, “the best”), Zeus has given Hector a great triumph (εὖχος), and they may win a greater (see [[euchomai]] and [[aristos]]). The verse that closes the speech is the one that closes many exhortations, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (11.291; see [[so-he-spoke]]), and a simile of a hunter setting his hounds on a boar or a lion follows (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]).

**Between two men.** Odysseus' call to Diomedes is an exhortation by shame: “Son of Tydeus, what has happened to us, that we have forgotten our furious courage? Come here, my friend, and stand by me; for it will be a disgrace (ἔλεγχος) if Hector of the gleaming helmet takes the ships” (11.313–315; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Menelaus, hearing Odysseus' three shouts, calls Ajax to go with him into the throng, “it is better to defend him” (11.465–471).

**Eurypylus.** Wounded in the thigh, Eurypylus falls back among his companions and shouts in Agamemnon's two verses (11.586–587 = 11.275–276): “Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives, turn and stand, and keep the pitiless day away from Ajax, who is hard pressed by missiles; and I do not think he will escape from grim-sounding war. But stand and face them, around great Ajax, the son of Telamon” (11.587–591). The call is answered at once, and the leaders form up around Ajax (11.592–595; see [[eurypylus]] and [[battle-order]]).

**Furious courage.** θοῦρις ἀλκή, the courage the exhorters call for, is also something a man has or lacks: Ajax “at one time would remember his furious courage and turn back again” (11.566–567), and the young Moliones were “not yet well skilled in furious courage” (11.710; see [[furious]]).
