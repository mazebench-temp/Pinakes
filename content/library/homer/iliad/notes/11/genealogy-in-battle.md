---
summary: Book 11 has no exchange of lineages between two warriors, but lineage decides two of Agamemnon's fights. The narrator, answering his own question to the Muses, gives Iphidamas' family and history before the two come together (11.218–231); and the sons of Antimachus, naming their father to win their lives, learn that his name has doomed them (11.130–142).
---

**Iphidamas.** In the scenes of the pattern the one warrior asks who the other is, and the other answers with his lineage before they fight or part. Before Agamemnon meets Iphidamas the question is the narrator's, put to the Muses: “who was the first to come against Agamemnon?” (11.218–220; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). The answer is the kind of account that Glaucus and Aeneas give of themselves, told in the third person: “Iphidamas, son of Antenor, tall and brave”, reared in deep-soiled Thrace by Cisseus, his mother's father, “who fathered fair-cheeked Theano”; married to Cisseus' daughter; come from the bridal chamber with twelve ships, which he left at Percote (11.221–230; see [[iphidamas]], [[cisseus]] and [[theano]]). Then, as in the pattern, the two come together, in the verse that brings duelists face to face (11.231–232; see [[when-they-came-close]]). No word passes between them. The history is given to the reader, not to Agamemnon, and it is a history of what the young man leaves behind (11.241–245; see [[marriage]]).

**The sons of Antimachus.** The two brothers caught in their chariot plead for their lives in the name of their father's house and wealth: “there are many treasures lying in the house of Antimachus” (11.132). Agamemnon answers with the father's history: “If you two are indeed the sons of wise-hearted Antimachus, who once in the Trojans' assembly urged that Menelaus … be killed on the spot, … now you will pay for your father's shameful insult” (11.138–142; see [[antimachus]] and [[supplication]]). Where Glaucus' lineage revealed an old friendship between the families and turned a fight into an exchange of gifts (6.212–236), these sons' lineage reveals an old wrong, and the king kills them for it (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).
