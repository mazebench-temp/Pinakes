**The strong against the weak.** The Pylians were few and had been ill-used: Heracles had come and killed all of Neleus' sons but Nestor (11.689–693; see [[heracles]] and [[neleus]]). The Epeians took advantage of it. ὑπερηφανέοντες, “being haughty”, occurs nowhere else in Homer, and ὑβρίζοντες, “treating with outrage”, is the only use of the verb in the Iliad, where the noun ὕβρις comes only in Book 1, of Agamemnon (1.203, 1.214; see the article above). The translation keeps the two apart from λώβη, “insult” (see [[lobe]]). The outrage Nestor means is concrete. The Epeians owed debts to many in Pylos (11.686–688), and Augeas had kept the four prize-winning horses that Neleus sent to race for a tripod in Elis, and sent the driver home grieving (11.698–702; see [[augeas]]). Nestor's cattle raid, in which he killed Itymoneus, was a reprisal (ῥύσια, 11.674; see [[raiding]]), and the war that followed ended with the Epeians routed to Buprasium (11.706–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Their outrage is a stronger people's contempt for a neighbor in misfortune, and in Nestor's telling it is punished (see [[atasthalie]]).

**The suitors' formula.** In the Odyssey the half-verse ὑβρίζοντες ἀτάσθαλα μηχανόωνται, “treating them with outrage, they devise reckless deeds”, belongs to the suitors (*Odyssey* 17.588, 20.170, 20.370). The first man to say it is Telemachus, at Pylos and to Nestor himself: he wishes the gods had given him the strength to punish the suitors for their transgression, “who, treating me with outrage, devise reckless deeds” (*Odyssey* 3.205–207). The old man who once suffered outrage from the Epeians hears it described in his own words by a young man who suffers it now.
