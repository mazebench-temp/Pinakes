**Two lines of teaching.** In Book 4 the drugs went from Chiron to Asclepius, and Asclepius' son Machaon spread them on Menelaus' wound (4.217–219). In Book 11 Machaon, “the son of Asclepius, the noble healer” (11.518), is shot by Paris, and Idomeneus sends Nestor to carry him to the ships, “for a healer is a man worth many others” (11.514; see [[machaon]] and [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]). When Eurypylus is wounded, the healers are gone: Machaon, he thinks, lies wounded in the huts, and Podalirius is fighting on the plain (11.833–836; see [[podalirius]]). So he turns to the other line of Chiron's knowledge, the one that went from Chiron to Achilles and from Achilles to Patroclus. Here alone Homer says that Chiron taught (ἐδίδαξε) and did not only give; and Eurypylus knows it by report, “which they say you have learned from Achilles” (φασιν, 11.831).

**The cure.** Patroclus does what Eurypylus asks. He lays him on oxhides in his hut, cuts the arrow from his thigh with a knife, washes the wound with warm water, and puts on a bitter, pain-killing root, which stops the pain and the bleeding (11.842–848; see [[healing]], [[eurypylus]] and [[patroclus]]). Chiron is named in the same verse as “the most just of the Centaurs”, the only place in the Iliad where the Centaurs are called by that name (see [[chiron]] and [[centaurs]]).
