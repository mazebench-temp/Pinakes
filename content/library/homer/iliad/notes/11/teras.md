---
summary: The day of battle begins with portents. Strife comes to the ships “holding in her hands a portent of war”; the serpents on Agamemnon's corselet are like the rainbows Zeus sets in the clouds, “a portent for mortal men”; Athena and Hera thunder in his honor; and Zeus sends down dew wet with blood.
except: [11.166]
passages:
  - 11.3-4 | Strife's portent
  - 11.26-28 | The serpents like rainbows
---

**What Strife holds.** Zeus sends Strife to the ships, “grievous, holding in her hands a portent of war” (πολέμοιο τέρας, 11.3–4; see [[strife]]). The poem does not say what she holds. τέρας is a sign from a god, and the Gorgon's head on Athena's aegis is “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.742); the object has been taken as the aegis itself or as some other signal of battle (see the translation's note on 11.4 and [[aegis]]). A sign of war is what the rainbow can be, which Zeus stretches across the sky as a portent “of war or of storm” (τέρας ἔμμεναι ἢ πολέμοιο ἢ καὶ χειμῶνος, 17.547–549).

**The rainbows.** The rainbow appears in the arming that follows, on Agamemnon's corselet. Its dark blue serpents stretched up toward the neck, three on each side, “like the rainbows that the son of Cronus sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men” (11.26–28; see [[corselet]] and the translation's note on 11.19–28). The likeness may lie in the arch of the serpents or in their sheen; either way the king arms himself in the image of a portent.

**Signs of the day.** Two more signs follow, neither called a τέρας. As Agamemnon finishes arming, “Athena and Hera thundered over him, honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold” (11.45–46). The verb, ἐγδούπησαν, occurs only here, and elsewhere thunder is Zeus' sign, as when he thundered from Ida and pale fear took the Achaeans (8.75–77; see the translation's note on 11.45). And Zeus, stirring up tumult, sends down from the upper air “dewdrops wet with blood”, because he means to send many heads to Hades (11.52–55; see [[death-in-battle]]).

σῆμα at 11.166, the mound of Ilus, is a grave, not a sign (see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]).
