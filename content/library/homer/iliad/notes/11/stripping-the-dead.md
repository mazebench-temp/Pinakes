**Agamemnon.** The king's spoils are told with care. He leaves Bianor and Oileus “with their chests shining bare, for he had stripped off their tunics” (11.99–100): περιδύω, “strip off”, occurs only here, and the stripping takes even what they wore beneath their armor. He goes on to “slay and strip” Isus and Antiphus, and strips them of their fine armor in haste, “recognizing them”, for he had seen them at the ships when Achilles brought them in as captives (11.101, 11.110–112; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]). He strips Hippolochus on the ground (11.145), and when he has killed Iphidamas, “Then Agamemnon, son of Atreus, stripped him, and went carrying his fine armor into the throng of the Achaeans” (11.246–247; see [[iphidamas]]). It is then that Coon, seeing his brother fallen, stabs him, and is killed dragging Iphidamas' body away by the foot (11.248–261; see [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[coon]]).

**The verb of killing.** ἐξεναρίζω, “strip of his armor”, is often no more than “slay”: the narrator's question before Hector's victims is “whom first, whom last did he slay” (ἐξενάριξεν, 11.299), and Odysseus “slew and stripped” Hippodamus and Hypeirochus, Thoon and Ennomus in a verse each (11.335, 11.422; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]). The two sons of Merops Diomedes “robbed of spirit and life, and took their glorious armor” (κλυτὰ τεύχεʼ ἀπηύρα, 11.334), in the words used of Hector when he has stripped Patroclus (17.125; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Socus taunts Odysseus that he means to boast over both sons of Hippasus, “having killed two such men and taken their armor” (11.432).

**The danger.** A man stripping armor has his hands full and his eyes down. Diomedes “began to strip the son of Paeon” (11.368), and while he was taking the gleaming corselet from his chest, the shield from his shoulders and the heavy helmet, Paris shot him through the foot from behind the pillar on Ilus' tomb (11.369–378; see [[agastrophus]] and [[bow]]). Eurypylus kills Apisaon and “began to strip the armor from his shoulders” (αἴνυτο τεύχεʼ ἀπʼ ὤμων, 11.580), and when Paris “caught sight of him stripping the armor from Apisaon”, he shot him in the thigh (11.581–584; see [[eurypylus]] and [[apisaon]]). Nestor's advice in Book 6 was to kill first and strip the dead at leisure (6.67–71).

**In Nestor's story.** The Pylians pursue the Epeians to Buprasium, “killing them and gathering up their fine armor” (11.755), and Nestor's first kill in the battle wins him a team of horses and a chariot (11.738–743; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]).
