**The dawn of Day 26.** Books 9 and 10 are one night, spent in waiting for the morning, and the third day of battle begins with the first verse of Book 11:

{{quote:4b7554fe-d0d1-51ec-9c67-27351afe0db7}}

It is not the commonest dawn verse, “when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (1.477), but a pair of verses that give the goddess a husband and a purpose: she rises from the bed she shares with Tithonus, son of Laomedon and brother of Priam (20.237), to bring light to gods and men (see [[tithonus]] and [[eos]]). The same two verses open the fifth book of the Odyssey, the day on which the gods decide Odysseus' release (Odyssey 5.1–2), and the second returns, after a different first verse, at the dawn of Achilles' return to the battle (19.1–2). Here the light is brought to the bloodiest day of the poem: Zeus at once sends Strife to the ships (11.3–4), and the day that has begun will not end until Hera sends the unwilling sun down into Oceanus after the death of Patroclus (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**Morning and its hours.** The battle is joined “in the early dawn” (ἠῶθι πρό, 11.50), goes evenly “as long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing” (11.84, in the verse of 8.66), and turns at the hour of the woodcutter's meal (11.86–90; see [[feast]]).

**Dawns in Nestor's story.** Nestor's war has its own dawns: the heralds summon the creditors “when dawn appeared” (ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφι, 11.685), the Pylians wait for “brilliant Dawn” by the Minyeios (11.723; see [[minyeios]]), and the battle is joined “when the shining sun rose up above the earth” (εὖτε γὰρ ἠέλιος φαέθων ὑπερέσχεθε γαίης, 11.735; see [[helios]] and [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]).
