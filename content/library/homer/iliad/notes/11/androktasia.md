summary: Book 11 kills in every manner the pattern allows: Agamemnon's pairs of brothers with their histories, Hector's nine leaders in three verses, Odysseus' and Diomedes' quick lists, Ajax's five names in three verses, and the full single combats of Iphidamas and Socus with their obituaries and boasts. The word itself appears when Zeus draws Hector “from the slaughter of men” (11.164).

**The lists.** The book's killings come in lists of different lengths. Agamemnon's are told at length, in pairs from one chariot or one family, each with a few verses of history: Bianor and Oileus, Isus and Antiphus, Peisander and Hippolochus (11.91–147; see [[the-aristeia]]). Hector's are nine names in three verses, under the narrator's question “whom first, whom last” (11.299–304; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]). Diomedes and Odysseus kill six in pairs, and Diomedes a seventh (11.320–342); Odysseus, alone, wounds Deiopites, kills Thoon, Ennomus and Chersidamas, and wounds Charops, in five verses (11.420–427); and Ajax kills one and wounds four in three: “Doryclus, a bastard son of Priam, and then wounded Pandocus, and wounded Lysander, and Pyrasus, and Pylartes” (11.489–491; see [[doryclus]]).

**The victims.** The fullest history in the book is Iphidamas': his upbringing in Thrace, his marriage to his grandfather's daughter, the bride-price paid and promised, and his death “far from his wife, whom he had wooed”, sleeping “the sleep of bronze” (11.221–245; see [[iphidamas]] and [[marriage]]). Shorter ones mark others: the two sons of Merops, whose father the seer forbade them to go to war (11.328–332; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]); Agastrophus, whose attendant held his horses too far off for him to escape (11.338–342; see [[agastrophus]]); Socus, “wealthy”, brother of Charops (11.426–427; see [[socus]]); in Nestor's story Itymoneus, “who lived in Elis”, killed defending his cows (11.672–676; see [[itymoneus]]), and Mulius, the son-in-law of Augeas (11.738–741).

**The blows.** The places are told exactly: the forehead through the helmet (11.95–96), the chest above the nipple (11.108), beside the ear (11.109), the neck (11.240), the forearm below the elbow (11.252), the left nipple (11.321), the hip (11.339), the flat of the foot (11.377), the shoulder from above (11.421), the navel beneath the shield (11.424), the back between the shoulders (11.447–448), the liver beneath the midriff (11.579), the thigh (11.583). Two killings are beheadings, of Hippolochus and of Coon (11.146, 11.261).

**The falls and the deaths.** The formulas of death are varied: “loosened his limbs” (11.240, 11.260), “slept the sleep of bronze” (11.241), “he fell with a thud” (11.449), “he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm” (ἕλε γαῖαν ἀγοστῷ, 11.425), and in Nestor's rout two men beside each captured chariot “bit the ground with their teeth” (ὀδὰξ ἕλον οὖδας, 11.749). See [[loosened-his-limbs]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]].

**Boasts.** Two of the book's speeches over a fallen enemy are its harshest: Paris' over Diomedes, whom he has only wounded, and who answers that his own spear leaves widows and orphans and corpses for the birds (11.380–395), and Odysseus' over Socus, whose parents will not close his eyes (11.450–455; see [[tomb]]).

**The word.** ἀνδροκτασίη, “the slaying of men”, is used in the singular when Zeus draws Hector out of the fighting, “from the missiles and from the dust, from the slaughter of men, from the blood and from the tumult” (11.163–164).
