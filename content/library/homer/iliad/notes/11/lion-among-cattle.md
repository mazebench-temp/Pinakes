{{quote:55740238-0ef7-5d4a-8acf-df1df4c74fd9}}

οἳ δʼ ἔτι κὰμ μέσσον πεδίον φοβέοντο βόες ὥς, / ἅς τε λέων ἐφόβησε μολὼν ἐν νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ / πάσας: “but others were still fleeing in rout through the middle of the plain, like cattle that a lion has put to flight, coming on them in the dead of night, all of them” (11.172–174). νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ is of uncertain sense, usually taken as the depth of night (see the translation's note on 11.173). The herd flees as one, and the lion takes one: “to one sheer destruction appears”. He breaks her neck in his strong teeth, ἐξ αὐχένʼ ἔαξε, the verb of the lion of Book 5 (ἐξ αὐχένα ἄξῃ, 5.161), and then gulps down (λαφύσσει) her blood and all her entrails (11.174–176).

The tenor gives the pursuer and the pursued: “so powerful Agamemnon, son of Atreus, pursued them, always killing the hindmost; and they fled” (11.177–178). The verse is the one that ended the hound simile of the second day, when Hector harried the Achaeans to their ditch (8.342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). The point of likeness is a pursuit that picks off one victim at a time, and the panic of a herd that does not turn. The Trojans have just been halted at the gates (11.170–171); the simile is for those still out on the plain.

The lion is Agamemnon's beast through the morning: a lion crushes a hind's fawns, Agamemnon rushes at the sons of Antimachus “like a lion”, and he drags Iphidamas' spear “eager as a lion” (11.113–121, 11.129, 11.239; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]], [[like-a-lion]] and [[lion]]). The last two verses of this simile return whole in Book 17, for a mountain lion that seizes the best cow of a grazing herd while dogs and herdsmen shout from a distance (17.63–64 = 11.175–176).
