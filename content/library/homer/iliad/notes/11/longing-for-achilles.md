**The day of the oath.** In Book 1 Achilles swore that a longing for him would come upon all the sons of the Achaeans, “when many at the hands of man-slaying Hector / fall dying” (1.240–244). Book 11 is that day. Hector kills nine Achaean leaders and then the multitude (11.299–309), Agamemnon, Diomedes, Odysseus, Machaon and Eurypylus are wounded, and Achilles, watching from his ship, thinks that the army will now stand around his knees in its need (11.608–610; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**Nestor's charge.** Nestor answers Achilles' question with the other half of the oath, the man who will not help. Achilles grieves for the wounded now, but “brave as he is, does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.664–665; see [[pity]]). After the story of his own youth Nestor ends on the same point: Achilles “will have the profit of his prowess alone; and yet I think / he will weep much hereafter, when the army has perished” (11.762–764). The weeping comes, but for Patroclus. In Book 18 Achilles says that he was no light to Patroclus, nor to the other companions whom Hector beat down, and sits by the ships, a useless weight on the earth (18.102–104). Nestor's plan had asked Patroclus to be that light himself, “in the hope that you may become a light to the Danaans” (11.797; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**Another man missed.** The oath's word ποθή, “longing”, is used in Book 11 of another man. When Menelaus hears Odysseus shouting, alone among the Trojans, he fears that “great longing for him may come upon the Danaans” if he is lost (11.470–471; see [[odysseus]]).
