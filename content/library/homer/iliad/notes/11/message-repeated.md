**Zeus to Iris, Iris to Hector.** Zeus sits on the peaks of Ida with the lightning in his hands and sends Iris to Hector with a message of eight verses (11.186–194): as long as Hector sees Agamemnon rushing among the champions, he is to hold back and order the rest of the army to fight; but when Agamemnon, “struck by a spear or hit by an arrow”, leaps into his chariot, Zeus will put power in Hector's hands to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships, and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194). Iris delivers it in the same verses (11.202–209), with the changes that delivery requires:

1. **Frame.** She adds an address and names the sender: “Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel, father Zeus has sent me to tell you this” (11.200–201; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]).
2. **Persons.** “As long as he sees” becomes “as long as you see” (ὁρᾷ, ὁρᾷς), “until he comes” becomes “until you come” (ἀφίκηται, ἀφίκηαι), and “I will put power in his hands” becomes “he will put power in your hands” (11.192, 11.207).
3. **One verb.** “So long let him draw back” (τόφρʼ ἀναχωρείτω, 11.189) becomes “so long withdraw from the fighting” (τόφρʼ ὑπόεικε μάχης, 11.204), a different verb with the same sense.

Nothing is added or left out, and Hector obeys it exactly: he rallies the army at once, keeps out of Agamemnon's way, and charges when he sees him leave the field (11.211–217, 11.284–309; see [[messenger-scene]] and [[hector]]). The terms set the length of the day of battle. Zeus repeats the last two verses in Book 17, of the glory he will still give the Trojans (17.453–455), and the day ends only when Hera sends the sun down (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Nestor's plan.** Nestor tells Patroclus what to say to Achilles: if some oracle holds Achilles back, let him send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons, and give him his armor, so that the Trojans, taking him for Achilles, may hold off from the war and the worn-out Achaeans may draw breath (11.794–803). Patroclus delivers it five books later, in the same verses with the persons turned to his own request (11.794–803 ≈ 16.36–45). ἀλλὰ σέ περ προέτω, “let him send you out”, becomes ἀλλʼ ἐμέ περ πρόες, “send me out”; δότω, “let him give”, becomes δός, “give”; the Trojans are to take “me for you” instead of “you for him”; and ὤσαισθε, “you would drive”, becomes ὤσαιμεν, “we would drive”. The message has become his own plea, and its end is his death (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[retelling]]).
