**The equal of Zeus in counsel.** Iris begins Zeus' message with Hector's title, “Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel” (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντε, 11.200), the verse with which Helenus addressed him in Book 7 (7.47; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]] and [[hector]]). The compliment comes before a message that tells him exactly what to do and when: hold back while Agamemnon rages, attack when he is wounded (11.201–209; see [[message-repeated]]). The counsel is Zeus' own, and Hector follows it to the letter (see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Zeus the counselor.** Agamemnon, wounded, calls on the Argive leaders to defend the ships, “since Zeus the counselor did not allow me to fight the Trojans all day long” (11.278–279; see [[counselor]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). The king who said in Book 9 that Zeus had deceived him (9.18–22) puts his wound down to Zeus as well, and this time he is right: Zeus' message to Hector has made the wound the turning point of the day (11.191–194).

**Of subtle wiles.** When the Trojans close around the wounded Odysseus like jackals around a stag, he is “wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” (Ὀδυσσῆα δαΐφρονα ποικιλομήτην, 11.482; see [[of-subtle-wiles]] and [[odysseus]]). ποικιλομήτης joins μῆτις to ποικίλος, the word for intricate, many-colored work, the robe Athena wove herself (5.735) or Aphrodite's embroidered band (14.215). The Odyssey gives Odysseus the epithet five times (*Odyssey* 3.163, 7.168, 22.115, 22.202, 22.281); the Iliad only here, a few lines after Socus has hailed him, in hostility, as “insatiable of tricks and toil” (11.430; see [[much-praised]]). The cunning man is trapped, and cunning does not free him: Ajax comes with his shield, and Menelaus leads him out by the hand (11.485–488).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymns give the epithet to two gods: to Zeus, in Hera's reproach after he has brought forth Athena by himself (*Hymn to Apollo* 3.322), and to Hermes, the god of tricks (*Hymn to Hermes* 4.155, 4.514).
