summary: Zeus governs the battle of Book 11 from a distance. He sends Strife to the Achaean ships and a dew of blood from the sky, sits apart from the gods who blame him, and from Ida sends Iris to Hector with the promise that frames the long day ahead; once Agamemnon is wounded, Hector will kill until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down.

**Strife and the dew of blood.** As Dawn rises, “Zeus sent Strife forth to the swift ships of the Achaeans” (11.3; see [[strife]]). When the armies are drawn up, the son of Cronus rouses an evil tumult among them, “and from on high he sent down dewdrops / wet with blood from the upper air, because he meant / to hurl down to Hades many mighty heads” (11.53–55). The last verse is the proem's, where the wrath “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls” (1.3), with κεφαλάς, “heads”, for ψυχάς, “souls”; and the proem goes on, “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[hades]]). He sheds bloody raindrops once more, for his son Sarpedon before Patroclus kills him (16.459–461).

**Apart from the gods.** Strife alone of the gods is in the battle (11.73–74). The others sit at ease in their halls on Olympus, as his ban of Book 8 requires (8.10–12), and blame him: “they all blamed the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, / because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (11.78–79; see [[olympians]] and [[kydos]]). He pays them no heed:

{{quote:306b7f9e-024b-57d1-a647-6d1d2509e9ee}}

He sat so on Gargarus the day before, “exulting in his glory, / looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (8.51–52 ≈ 11.81–82; see [[exulting-in-glory]] and [[watching-the-war]]).

**The promise.** When the Trojans break before Agamemnon, “Zeus drew Hector out from the missiles and from the dust” (11.163; see [[hector]]). Then, as Agamemnon nears the wall, “the father of men and gods” comes down from heaven to the peaks of Ida, “with the lightning in his hands” (11.182–184; see [[ida]]), and sends Iris to Hector:

{{quote:3089905e-855c-591b-bd0a-aa0c0ec6c9d8}}

Thetis asked him to “give strength to the Trojans until the Achaeans / pay honor to my son” (1.509–510), and κράτος, “strength” there and “power” here, is the word of both (see [[iris]] and [[message-repeated]]). The promise sets the term of the third day of battle, which ends only when Hera sends the unwilling sun down into Oceanus (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]), and Zeus repeats its last verses during the fight over Patroclus' body (17.454–455 ≈ 11.193–194).

**Both sides read the sign.** Coon's spear fulfills the condition (11.248–253; see [[coon]]). Agamemnon, leaving the field, says that “Zeus the counselor did not / allow me to fight the Trojans all day long” (11.278–279; see [[counselor]] and [[agamemnon]]), and Hector tells the Trojans that “a great triumph has been given me / by Zeus, son of Cronus” (11.288–289). Diomedes stays to fight, though “Zeus the cloud-gatherer / wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us” (11.318–319; see [[diomedes]]), and Odysseus, left alone, says that “the son of Cronus has put the other Danaans to rout” (11.406; see [[odysseus]]). For a time Zeus holds the fight level: he “stretched the battle evenly for them, / looking down from Ida” (11.336–337).

**Ajax.** Hector avoids Ajax (11.542). A verse found in no manuscript, which Aristotle quotes, gives the reason: “For Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (11.543; see [[nemesis]] and [[aristotle-nemesis]]). In the next verse Zeus drives Ajax back himself: “And father Zeus, throned on high, roused Ajax to flight” (11.544; see [[high-throned]], [[ajax]] and [[retreat]]).

**In Nestor's story.** Zeus gives the Pylians their victory too. They sacrifice first to “Zeus, supreme in might” (11.727; see [[supreme-in-might]]), and when “Zeus put great power into the hands of the Pylians” (11.753), with the verb of his promise to Hector (ἐγγυάλιξε; ἐγγυαλίξω, 11.192), they drive the Epeians back to Buprasium, and “all gave thanks to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Nestor's last guess is that Achilles shuns an oracle, “something from Zeus” that his mother told him (11.794–795); Achilles will deny it in the same words (16.50–51; see [[achilles]]).
