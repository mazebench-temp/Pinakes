**The drink.** Nestor and Machaon come in from the battle to Nestor's hut, and Hecamede makes them a κυκεών, a “mixed drink”: Pramnian wine with goat's cheese grated over it and white barley meal sprinkled on it, with an onion as a relish and pale honey set out beside it (11.618–641; see [[hecamede]], [[wine]] and [[barley-grains]]). In the *Ion* Socrates asks whether a rhapsode or a doctor should judge if Homer speaks rightly “when he says that Hecamede, Nestor's concubine, gives the wounded Machaon a κυκεών to drink”, and Ion answers that it is a matter for medicine (*Ion* 538b–c). The verses Socrates recites are 11.639 and the first words of 11.640, “with Pramnian wine, he says, and on it she grated goat's cheese / with a bronze grater”, joined to the onion of 11.630 with παρὰ δέ, “beside it”, where our text has ἐπὶ δέ. He leaves out the barley meal, and he makes the drink Machaon's alone, though Hecamede mixes it for both men (τοῖσι, 11.624; σφωϊν, 11.628).

**The Republic.** Socrates' argument is that the sons of Asclepius practiced a medicine for wounds and passing illnesses, not for nursing chronic disease. He infers it from Troy: they did not blame the woman who gave the wounded Eurypylus Pramnian wine “with much barley meal sprinkled on it and cheese grated over it”, things thought to breed phlegm, nor did they rebuke Patroclus, who was treating him (*Republic* 3.405e–406a). And they prescribed no diet afterward for Menelaus or for Eurypylus, since the drugs were enough to heal men who had lived healthy and orderly lives, “even if they happened to drink a κυκεών on the spot” (3.408a–b). The word is Homer's word for Hecamede's drink (κυκειῶ, 11.624, 11.641).

**Book 11.** In Homer the drink and Eurypylus belong to different scenes. Eurypylus, shot in the thigh by Paris (11.581–584), meets Patroclus as he runs back from Nestor's hut, and asks him to cut out the arrow, wash the wound with warm water and spread soothing drugs on it, the drugs that Achilles learned from Chiron (11.809–832; see [[eurypylus]] and [[patroclus]]). Patroclus cuts out the arrow, washes away the blood and lays on a bitter root that stops the pain and the bleeding (11.842–848). No one gives Eurypylus a drink. Nor are the sons of Asclepius there to blame or praise anyone. Eurypylus turns to Patroclus because, of the two healers, one, he thinks, lies wounded in the huts, and the other is fighting on the plain (11.833–836): Machaon has been carried from the field in Nestor's chariot (11.516–520), and Podalirius is still in the battle (see [[machaon]] and [[podalirius]]). Socrates' Homer has merged the scene in Nestor's hut with Patroclus' care of Eurypylus.

What Socrates admires is the medicine that Book 11 itself praises. Idomeneus sends Machaon out of the battle because “a healer is a man worth many others, / to cut out arrows and spread on soothing drugs” (11.514–515; see [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]), and Socrates' Asclepius drove out diseases “with drugs and cuttings” (φαρμάκοις τε καὶ τομαῖς, *Republic* 3.407d). Patroclus does both for Eurypylus.
