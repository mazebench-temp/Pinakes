**The first step.** Patroclus comes out of the hut at Achilles' call “the equal of Ares”, and the narrator adds κακοῦ δʼ ἄρα οἱ πέλεν ἀρχή, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see the translation's note on 11.604). ἀρχή is the first step of a course whose end is already known. The ships that Phereclus built for Alexander were ἀρχέκακοι, the ships “that began the evil, which became an evil for all the Trojans” (5.62–63; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]), and Zeus has told Hera how this course ends: Hector will not stop fighting until Achilles is roused beside the ships, over Patroclus dead (8.473–477). Patroclus speaks here for the first time in the poem, a single verse, “Why do you call me, Achilles? What need do you have of me?” (11.606). The errand is small, to ask Nestor who the wounded man is (11.611–615), and it brings him to Nestor.

**The plan.** Nestor ends his long speech with a proposal. If Achilles is holding back because of some oracle his mother has told him, let him send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons in his own armor:

{{quote:300e1e14-f2e9-5e42-b59e-da2430d479db}}

The armor is the heart of the plan. In it Patroclus may be taken for Achilles; it will also make him Hector's target and Hector's spoil (17.125–131, 17.192–197), and Achilles will need new armor from Hephaestus (18.134–137). The verse ὀλίγη δέ τʼ ἀνάπνευσις πολέμοιο, “brief is the breathing space in war” (11.801), returns when Patroclus repeats the plan and when Iris rouses Achilles (16.43, 18.201; see [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]]).

**The delay and the request.** Running back with the plan, Patroclus meets the wounded Eurypylus, pities him, and stays to tend his wound (11.804–848; see [[eurypylus]] and [[healing]]). He is still there when the Trojans storm the wall (12.1–2, 15.390–405), and he comes to Achilles only when the fighting is at the sterns of the ships (16.1–3). Then he puts Nestor's plan to him almost word for word (16.36–45 ≈ 11.794–803), and the narrator speaks of him as he spoke when he first came out: “so he spoke in supplication, great fool; for it was his own evil death and doom that he was begging for” (16.46–47; see [[nepios]]). Achilles answers that no oracle holds him back (16.50–51), and grants the request. See [[patroclus]] and [[nestor]].
