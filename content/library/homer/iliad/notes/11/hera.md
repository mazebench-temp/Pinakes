**The king of Mycenae.** When Agamemnon has armed, “Athena and Hera thundered over him, / honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold” (11.45–46; see [[athena]] and [[agamemnon]]). Mycenae is one of the three cities Hera calls “by far the dearest to me” (4.51–52; see [[mycenae]] and [[rich-in-gold]]). The goddesses whom Zeus turned back in Book 8 (8.426–437) honor the king with a sound that is otherwise Zeus' own (see the translation's note on 11.45 and [[loud-thundering]]), and they come no nearer: Strife alone of the gods is in the battle (11.73–75).

**Her daughters.** When Agamemnon's wound dries and the pain comes, the poet likens it to childbirth: “As when the sharp dart seizes a woman in labor, / the piercing dart that the Eileithyiai send, who bring hard birth, / the daughters of Hera, who have the bitter pangs in their keeping” (11.269–271; see [[eileithyia]] and [[the-woman-in-labor]]). The Iliad names no father for them; Hesiod makes Eileithyia the daughter of Hera and Zeus (*Theogony* 921–923). In Book 19 Hera rules their work herself: to cheat Zeus, she brings Eurystheus to birth in the seventh month and holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene (19.114–119; see [[eurystheus]] and [[heracles]]). Both times that Book 11 names Hera, it does so for Agamemnon: in the honor of his arming and in the likeness of his pain.
