**Girding.** The day of battle begins with Agamemnon's order: he “bade them gird themselves” (ζώννυσθαι, 11.15), with ζώννυμι, the verb of the belt (ζωστήρ). To gird oneself is to arm, as Nestor girded on his gleaming belt whenever he armed for war (10.77–78), and as Achilles will bid the Myrmidons “gird on the bronze” for Patroclus' funeral (χαλκὸν ζώννυσθαι, 23.130; see [[arming-scene]]).

**The belt holds.** Iphidamas is the first man to face Agamemnon after the invocation. Agamemnon's spear turns aside, and Iphidamas thrusts:

{{quote:3866987f-5f06-57f4-8067-7c170d159de6}}

He aims below the corselet, where the belt covers the belly (see [[corselet]]), and puts his weight on the thrust, “trusting in his heavy hand”. The belt is παναίολος, “gleaming”, as Menelaus' and Nestor's are (4.186, 10.77; see [[panaiolos]]). μόλιβος, “lead”, occurs only here in Homer: the bronze point meets the silver and bends as if it were soft (see the translation's note on 11.237). Agamemnon seizes the spear, drags it toward him “eager as a lion”, wrenches it from Iphidamas' hand and kills him with the sword (11.238–240; see [[iphidamas]] and [[sword]]).

**Two sons of Atreus.** The belt saves both brothers. In Book 4 Pandarus' arrow went through Menelaus' belt, corselet and band, and he could say, “in front the gleaming belt stopped it” (4.135–139, 4.185–186). Agamemnon's is not even pierced. What wounds him is Coon's spear through the forearm a moment later (11.252–253).
