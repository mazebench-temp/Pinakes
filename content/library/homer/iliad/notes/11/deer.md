**The hind.** ἔλαφος is the deer, the hind as well as the stag. In the first simile of Agamemnon's killing a lion comes into the lair of “a swift hind” (ἐλάφοιο ταχείης) and crushes her young; the mother, close by, cannot help them, for “a dreadful trembling comes over her”, and she runs through the brush and the woods, “hurrying and sweating” (11.113–119; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]). Her young are the two sons of Priam whom Agamemnon kills, and her flight is the Trojans', who cannot save them. Speed and fear are the deer's marks in the Iliad. Achilles told Agamemnon that he had “the heart of a deer” (1.225), and Agamemnon asked the men who hung back why they stood dazed like fawns (4.243; see [[like-fawns]]).

**The stag.** In the second simile the deer is a horned stag, ἔλαφος κεραός, that a man has hit with an arrow. It escapes on its feet “as long as the blood is warm and its knees have strength to move”, and then the jackals pull it down and devour it, until a lion comes and takes it from them (11.474–481; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]] and [[jackal]]). The stag is Odysseus, wounded by Socus' spear and pressed by the Trojans. The horned stag is the lion's prey in Book 3 and the wolves' in Book 16 (3.24, 16.158), and dogs and huntsmen chase one in Book 15 until a lion turns them back (15.271–276).
