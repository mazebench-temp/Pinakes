summary: Zeus' indignation keeps Hector from fighting Ajax, in a verse known only from Aristotle, “Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (11.543); and Patroclus, refusing to sit in Nestor's hut, fears the indignation of the man who sent him, “revered and quick to blame” (11.649).
passages:
  - 11.540-544 | Hector avoids Ajax
  - 11.644-654 | Patroclus refuses to sit

**The god's indignation.** Cebriones has driven Hector across the field to where Ajax is routing the Trojans (11.521–539). Hector ranges through the other ranks “with spear and sword and with great stones”, in the two verses that told Agamemnon's last onslaught, while the blood still ran warm from his wound (11.540–541 = 11.264–265), and keeps away from one man:

{{quote:cf2de0b7-c4f6-5604-a45f-1b4cdff77616}}

The bracketed verse is in no manuscript of the Iliad. Aristotle quotes it after 11.542 in his chapter on indignation, which he defines as pain at good fortune that seems undeserved, the emotion opposite to pity; because what happens against desert is unjust, he says, we ascribe indignation to the gods. Among the things that rouse it is the lesser man contending with the greater, above all in the same field, and the two verses are his example (*Rhetoric* 2.9, 1387a; see [[aristotle-nemesis]]). Editors print the verse in brackets, and so does the translation (see its note on 11.543).

νεμέσασκε is iterative, “whenever”: the verse makes Hector's avoidance a rule that Zeus enforced, not a single choice. The better man is Ajax, whom the Catalogue called “the best by far … while Achilles kept up his wrath” (2.768–769), and whose stone laid Hector on his back in their duel until Apollo raised him (7.268–272; see [[ajax]] and [[hector]]). Zeus has promised Hector power to kill until he reaches the ships (11.192–194); he does not give him Ajax. In the next verse Zeus himself rouses Ajax to flight (11.544; see [[retreat]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Without the bracketed verse the passage runs straight on, from the man who avoids Ajax to the god who drives Ajax back; with it, the narrator gives the avoidance a reason, and the reason is the indignation of Zeus.

**The hero's indignation.** Patroclus comes to Nestor's hut on Achilles' errand, and the old man rises from his chair, leads him in by the hand, and bids him sit (11.644–646). He refuses:

{{quote:75d1c76d-0946-5306-9138-5141a9c7785f}}

αἰδοῖος νεμεσητός names both sides of the relation that the word describes (see the article above): Achilles is a man before whom one feels αἰδώς, “respect”, and whose νέμεσις one fears (see [[aidos]]). Helen pairs αἰδοῖος with another word when she speaks to Priam: “You are one I respect, dear father-in-law, and one I fear” (αἰδοῖός … δεινός τε, 3.172). Patroclus has the second word too, δεινὸς ἀνήρ, “a dread man” (11.654). Everywhere else in Homer νεμεσσητός describes an act. Helen will not go to Paris' bed, “it would be a thing for blame” (3.410), and Phoenix told Achilles that “before now there was no blame in your anger” (9.523; also 14.336, 19.182, 24.463; *Odyssey* 22.59, 22.489). Only here is the word said of a man, and the translation gives it the active sense that the next line spells out: Achilles “would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.654; see [[aitios]] and [[achilles]]).

The fear does not hurry Patroclus home. Nestor keeps him for the longest speech in the book (11.656–803), and on his way back Patroclus stops again, for the wounded Eurypylus (11.804–848). He comes to Achilles only at the beginning of Book 16, weeping, and his first plea there is the verse with which Nestor woke Odysseus in the night: “do not be indignant; such grief has overpowered the Achaeans” (μὴ νεμέσα, 16.22 = 10.145; see [[patroclus]]).
