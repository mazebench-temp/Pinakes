**A landmark of the rout.** Agamemnon drives the Trojans “past the mound of ancient Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, / through the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree, / making for the city” (11.166–168), until they halt at the Scaean gates and the oak (11.170; see [[the-fig-tree]], [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The night before, Hector had held his council beside it, away from the din (10.414–416; see [[hector]]).

**An archer's cover.** The book uses two words for the grave. At 11.166 it is σῆμα, “mound”, as in Book 10 (10.415); at 11.371 it is τύμβος, “tomb”, ἀνδρόκμητος, “man-made”, a word found only here, with a στήλη, “pillar”, standing on it (see [[tomb]] and [[man-made]]). A pillar on a mound is the honor of the dead that the Lycians will give Sarpedon (τύμβῳ τε στήλῃ τε, 16.457, 16.675), and a pillar on a tomb stands unmoving in the simile of Achilles' grieving horses (17.434–435). Paris leans on it to shoot, and after his shot he leaps out “from his ambush” (ἐκ λόχου, 11.379; see [[paris]], [[diomedes]] and [[ambush]]). The ancestor's monument shelters the archer who drives Diomedes out of the war.
