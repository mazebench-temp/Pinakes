summary: Nestor fights by the Scamander until Idomeneus sends him to carry the wounded Machaon to the ships. In his hut, where Hecamede mixes a drink in the great cup that he alone lifts with ease, he answers Achilles' messenger Patroclus with the second-longest speech in the Iliad: the story of his first war, against the Epeians, the day at Peleus' house, and the plan that Patroclus should go out in Achilles' armor.

**By the Scamander.** Nestor first appears where Hector is fighting, “on the left of all the battle, / beside the banks of the river Scamander”, where “an unquenchable cry went up / around great Nestor and warlike Idomeneus” (11.498–501; see [[scamander]] and [[idomeneus]]). When Paris hits Machaon with an arrow, Idomeneus calls on him by his full title, “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans”, to take the healer up and drive to the ships, “for a healer is a man worth many others” (11.511–515; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]], [[machaon]] and [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]). “The Gerenian horseman Nestor did not disobey” (11.516): the verse with which he obeyed Diomedes on the field in Book 8 (8.112). The pair “flew on not unwilling / toward the hollow ships”, as the horses of Rhesus did the night before (11.519–520 = 10.530–531; see [[chariot-journey]]).

**The mares and Achilles.** The poet returns to him in two verses: “the Neleian mares carried Nestor out of the war, / sweating, and they bore Machaon” (11.597–598; see [[neleus]]). Achilles sees the chariot from his stern, too fast to see the wounded man's face, and sends Patroclus to ask Nestor who he is (11.599–615; see [[achilles]] and [[watching-the-war]]). So the errand that is “the beginning of evil” for Patroclus leads to Nestor's hut (11.604; see [[patroclus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The hut.** Eurymedon, “the old man's attendant”, unyokes the horses, and the two men let the sweat dry in the sea breeze before they go in and sit (11.618–623; see [[eurymedon-attendant-of-nestor]] and [[hut]]). Hecamede, the captive picked out for him “because he was the best of all in counsel” (11.627; see [[hecamede]]), sets out a table and his cup, and mixes them Pramnian wine with goat's cheese and barley (11.628–641; see [[kykeon]]).

The narrator gives the old man this feat of strength a few verses before Nestor says that his strength “is not such as it was before in my supple limbs” (11.668–669; see [[the-cup-of-nestor]] and [[the-tithonus-verse]]).

**Patroclus at the door.** Nestor rises from his “shining chair”, takes Patroclus by the hand and bids him sit (11.645–646), in the verse with which Achilles once welcomed Nestor and Odysseus at Phthia (11.778; see [[throne]]). Patroclus will not: “There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus”, for the one who sent him is “quick to blame” (11.648–654; see [[zeus-nurtured]] and [[nemesis]]).

**The answer.** Nestor's reply runs to 148 verses (11.656–803), the longest speech in the poem after Phoenix's (9.434–605). Why does Achilles grieve now, when “he knows nothing of the grief”? The best men “lie among the ships, shot or stabbed”, and Nestor names them, keeping the two verbs apart: Diomedes shot, Odysseus and Agamemnon stabbed, Eurypylus shot (11.656–662; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). He left the field before Eurypylus was shot (11.516–520, 11.581–584) and could not yet know of that wound; Patroclus repeats the verses to Achilles (16.25–27). Achilles “does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.665; see [[pity]]).

**The war of his youth.** Then the wish that opens his tales, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (11.670 = 7.157), and the longest of them: the cattle raid on Elis, the debts and the division, Heracles' killing of his eleven brothers, the siege of Thryoessa, his father hiding his horses, the battle on foot, Mulius, the fifty chariots, the Moliones, the rout to Buprasium (11.670–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]). It ends with the Pylians giving thanks “to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men”, and with a doubt, “Such was I among men, if that was ever so” (11.761–762), Helen's words for her memory of Agamemnon (3.180; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]]).

**Phthia.** “But Achilles will have the profit of his prowess alone” (11.763; see [[arete]]). Nestor turns to Patroclus in the words of Odysseus' reminder to Achilles in Book 9: “My friend, surely Menoetius charged you thus / on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon” (11.765–766 ≈ 9.252–253), and “So the old man charged you, but you forget” (11.790 = 9.259). He was there. He and Odysseus, gathering the army, found Peleus sacrificing in his courtyard, were welcomed, and heard the fathers' charges: Peleus told Achilles “always to be the best and to excel above the others”, and Menoetius told Patroclus, the elder, to counsel him, “and he will obey you, to his good” (11.765–789; see [[menoetius]], [[peleus]], [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). The father in the story of his youth kept his son from the war; the fathers at Phthia sent theirs to it with their charges (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**The plan.** “Good is the persuasion of a companion” (11.793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). And if some oracle from Zeus that his mother told him keeps Achilles back (11.794–795):

The words “stirred the heart in his breast” (11.804). Patroclus stops for the wounded Eurypylus, to whom he names his sender, “Nestor … the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans” (11.840; see [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]]). When he leaves Eurypylus at last, he says Nestor's words over again (15.403–404 ≈ 11.792–793), and in Book 16 he puts the plan to Achilles almost word for word (16.36–45; see [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]]). Achilles grants it (16.64–65), and the narrator calls Patroclus “a great fool”, “for he was to beg for his own evil death” (16.46–47). The old man's counsel, given to save the army, sends Patroclus out to die.
