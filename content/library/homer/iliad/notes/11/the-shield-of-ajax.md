summary: The tower shield covers Odysseus when Ajax comes to rescue him (11.485), marks Ajax out to Cebriones (11.526–527), and, swung behind his back, covers his slow retreat when Zeus puts him to flight; the Trojans' spears stick in it or fall short (11.544–574).
refs: [11.527, 11.565, 11.572]
passages:
  - 11.544-574 | Ajax gives ground
---

**The rescue.** When Menelaus brings Ajax to Odysseus, who is beset like a wounded stag among jackals, “Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower, and stood beside him; and the Trojans scattered in fear, this way and that” (11.485–486). The first verse is the one with which he came out to fight Hector (7.219), and it will bring him to stand over the body of Patroclus (17.128). In Book 7 the shield goes out against an enemy; here and in Book 17 it covers a comrade (see [[like-a-tower]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Known by his shield.** On the other wing Cebriones sees the Trojans in turmoil and names the man who is routing them: “It is Telamonian Ajax who drives them in confusion; I know him well, for he has the broad shield around his shoulders” (11.526–527; see [[cebriones]]). So Pandarus knew Diomedes from afar, “knowing him by his shield and his helmet with its socket for the plume” (5.182).

**Behind his back.** Zeus rouses Ajax to flight, “and he stood amazed, and swung his shield of seven oxhides behind his back” (11.544–545). This is the last of the five uses of ἑπταβόειος, “of seven oxhides”, all of them of this shield (7.220, 7.222, 7.245, 7.266, 11.545; see [[seven-oxhide]]). He does not throw it away. ὄπιθεν … βάλεν is to sling it round onto his back, so that it covers him as he goes, and the Trojans following him stab “at the middle of his shield” (11.565; see the translation's note on 11.545).

**The spears.** As he turns again and again to hold them off, the shield takes their spears: “some stuck in his great shield as they sped forward, and many, too, in between, before they could reach his white flesh, stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (11.571–574). The two verses of the spears that fall short return in the fighting before the ships, when Apollo leads the Trojans with the aegis (11.573–574 = 15.316–317). Between the two similes of the retreat, the lion driven from the farmyard and the donkey that the boys beat with cudgels to no effect, it is the shield that makes the blows harmless (11.548–565; see [[ajax]] and [[retreat]]).

**Shields on the shoulders.** Eurypylus' call brings the Achaeans to stand around Ajax “leaning their shields against their shoulders”, and he turns and stands with them (11.592–595; see [[shield]] and [[eurypylus]]).
