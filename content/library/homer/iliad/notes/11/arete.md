**Their own prowess.** All morning the missiles of both sides strike home and the people fall (11.84–85). Then, “at the hour when a woodcutter makes ready his meal”, the Danaans break the Trojan line by their own prowess, σφῇ ἀρετῇ (11.86–90; see the translation's note on 11.84–90). The narrator has just said that Strife alone of the gods was in the battle, while the other gods sat in their halls and Zeus sat apart, looking on (11.73–83; see [[watching-the-war]]). No god is named in the break. The prowess is the army's, and Agamemnon leads it (11.91–92; see [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]).

**The profit of prowess.** Nestor ends the story of his youth with his own prowess, and turns from it to Achilles':

{{quote:2e8adba8-3fe8-54ff-9f4f-5c82c6986eb7}}

The young Nestor's prowess served his people, who “gave thanks to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.761; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]] and [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). Achilles' will serve no one. ἀπονήσεται, from ὀνίνημι, “profit, benefit”, is to have the good of a thing (in the Iliad the compound occurs only here), and οἶος, “alone”, leaves the army out; the next verse says what the army will have instead (11.764). Patroclus takes up the verb when he reaches Achilles in Book 16. He calls him αἰναρέτη, “dread in your prowess”, a compound of ἀρετή found nowhere else, and asks what other man, even one born later, will have any good of him (τί σευ ἄλλος ὀνήσεται), if he does not ward off ruin from the Argives (16.29–32; see the article above, [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]).
