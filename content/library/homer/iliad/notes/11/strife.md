summary: Zeus sends Strife to the Achaean ships at dawn. Standing on the ship of Odysseus in the middle of the line, she shouts and puts into every Achaean the strength to fight, so that war becomes sweeter to them than going home; in the battle that follows she alone of the gods is present, and she rejoices to look on.

**Sent by Zeus.** “Zeus sent Strife forth to the swift ships of the Achaeans, / grievous, holding in her hands a portent of war” (11.3–4; see [[zeus]]). What she holds is not described. τέρας is a sign from a god, and twenty verses later the rainbows that Zeus sets in the clouds are “a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28; see [[teras]] and the translation's note on 11.4). She stands on the ship of Odysseus, which lay in the middle of the line, so that a shout could carry to the huts of Ajax and of Achilles at the two ends (11.5–9). In Book 8 Agamemnon shouted from the same ship, in the same verses (8.222–226; see [[odysseus]]).

**The shout.** “There the goddess stood and shouted, loud and terrible, / shrill” (11.10–11). Her cry does what the cries of other gods do. She “put great strength into each of the Achaeans, / in his heart, to go to war without ceasing and to fight” (11.11–12), the verses of Athena with the aegis in Book 2 (2.451–452) and of Poseidon's shout in Book 14 (14.151–152). Then “war became sweeter to them than to go home / in the hollow ships to their dear native land” (11.13–14). The two verses ended Athena's rousing of the army in Book 2, on the day it had rushed to the ships to sail home (2.149–154, 2.453–454; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

**Alone of the gods.**

{{quote:cd06a2d2-8f77-53c4-82d3-cc1812347e24}}

Zeus' ban keeps the gods out of the battle (8.10–12), and Strife is there because he sent her. The other gods sit at home and blame him (11.78–79; see [[olympians]]); she rejoices as she looks on, and Zeus, apart from them, watches “exulting in his glory” (11.81–83; see [[watching-the-war]]). πολύστονος, “of many groans”, is given to her only here (see [[of-the-many-groans]]); elsewhere in the Iliad it is said of sorrows and of an arrow (1.445, 15.451). In Book 4 she went through the throng “increasing the groaning of men” (4.445).

**The word.** The common noun is in the book too: Cebriones sees horsemen and foot soldiers “joining in evil strife” (κακὴν ἔριδα, 11.529; see [[eris]]). The fighting of Nestor's youth is νεῖκος, “the quarrel of the Pylians and the Epeians” (11.737; also 11.671, 11.721), the word that the translation keeps apart from Strife (see [[neikos]]).
