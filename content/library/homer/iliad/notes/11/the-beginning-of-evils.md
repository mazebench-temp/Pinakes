Achilles has seen Nestor's mares carry a wounded man past his ship, and calls Patroclus from the hut to find out who it is (11.597–615). Before Patroclus says his first words in the poem, the narrator stops the scene with a judgment: κακοῦ δʼ ἄρα οἱ πέλεν ἀρχή, “this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see the article above and the translation's note on 11.604). οἱ is “for him”: the evil is what befalls Patroclus, not what he does.

The chain runs through the rest of the book. The errand brings Patroclus to Nestor, who tells him of his own youth, reminds him of Menoetius' charge, and gives him the plan that Achilles should send him out in his armor (11.656–803; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]] and [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]]). On the way back the wounded Eurypylus delays him (11.804–848; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). In Book 16 he puts Nestor's plan to Achilles nearly word for word, and the narrator speaks again: “So he spoke, beseeching, great fool; for he was to beg for his own evil death and doom” (16.46–47). The beginning is matched by an end. Here Patroclus comes out “the equal of Ares” (ἶσος Ἄρηϊ); in Book 16 he charges three times “equal to swift Ares”, and the fourth time, “like a god”, “the end of your life appeared” (βιότοιο τελευτή, 16.784–787; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[patroclus]]).
