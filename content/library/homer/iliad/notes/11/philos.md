summary: The fallen Trojan charioteers lie “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives”; two wounded leaders call the Argive chiefs “friends” in the same verse; and the word marks the bond of Achilles and Patroclus on the afternoon that sends Patroclus on his errand, “dear to Zeus” and obedient to “his dear companion”.
grc: [φίλτεροι]
passages:
  - 11.159-162 | Dearer to the vultures
  - 11.602-617 | Achilles sends Patroclus
---

**Dearer to the vultures.** As Agamemnon drives the Trojans across the plain, their horses rattle empty chariots along the bridges of war, missing their charioteers, “but they lay on the ground, far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (γύπεσσιν πολὺ φίλτεροι ἢ ἀλόχοισιν, 11.159–162; see [[vultures]]). The comparative of φίλος sets side by side the two who want the dead men, the wives who loved them and the birds that will eat them. The proem promised that the wrath would make the men themselves “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5); here the promise is put in the language of love. Diomedes makes the same comparison in his answer to Paris: the man his shaft hits lies rotting, “and there are more birds around him than women” (11.395; see [[birds]] and [[death-in-battle]]).

**Friends.** Agamemnon, leaving the field, and Eurypylus, wounded and calling for help for Ajax, address the chiefs in the same verse, “Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives” (ὦ φίλοι, 11.276, 11.587), the verse with which Nestor greeted them in the night (10.533). Hector, rallying the Trojans, calls his men friends as he tells them to be men: “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (11.287 = 8.174; see [[exhortation]]).

**Achilles and Patroclus.** Achilles calls Patroclus “dear to Zeus” (Διῒ φίλε, 11.611; see [[dear-to-zeus]]), the epithet the narrator has just given Odysseus twice as the Trojans close around him (11.419, 11.473), and sends him to Nestor. “So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (11.616): the verse is the one with which Patroclus set out the mixing bowl for the envoys in Book 9, when Achilles called them “the dearest of men” (9.202–205; see [[hetairos]] and [[patroclus]]). It now begins the errand that the narrator has called “the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]). On his way back Patroclus pities the Danaan leaders, destined to die “far from your friends and your native land” (11.817; compare 11.14 and [[dear-native-land]]).
