---
summary: Book 11 is full of retreats, and the poem allows nearly all of them. Hector draws back at Zeus' command until Agamemnon is wounded; the wounded chiefs leave the field one by one; Odysseus, left alone, argues himself into standing his ground; and Ajax, put to flight by Zeus, gives ground “very unwillingly” and turns to stand among his companions.

**By Zeus' order.** The day's first retreat is commanded. Zeus draws Hector “out from the missiles and from the dust” (11.163–164), and then sends Iris with an order: as long as Hector sees Agamemnon raging among the champions, “so long let him draw back, and order the rest of the army to fight the enemy in the fierce battle” (11.187–190, repeated by Iris at 11.202–205; see [[message-repeated]]). The withdrawal has a term. When Agamemnon is wounded and leaps into his chariot, Zeus will give Hector power to kill until he reaches the ships (11.191–194). Hector obeys to the letter, and charges only when he sees Agamemnon “going away” (11.211–217, 11.284; see [[hector]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The wounded.** The other retreats of the Achaean chiefs are forced by wounds, and the poem tells them in shared verses (see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Agamemnon springs into his chariot and bids his charioteer drive to the hollow ships, “for his heart was distressed” (11.273–274), and Diomedes, when the arrow is drawn from his foot, does the same in the same two verses (11.399–400). Odysseus is led out of the throng by the hand (11.487–488), Nestor carries Machaon to the ships (11.511–520), and Eurypylus, shot by Paris, “drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom” (11.585). Two of the verses that bring Paris and Eurypylus together are the ones in which Paris, seeing Menelaus, shrank back into the crowd in Book 3 (11.581, 11.585 = 3.30, 3.32): the man who once retreated now drives others back.

**Odysseus alone.** Between the wounds comes the poem's fullest argument about retreat, held by one man with himself. With Diomedes gone, Odysseus “was left alone, and no one of the Argives stayed beside him, since rout had seized them all” (11.401–402). He speaks to his heart:

He weighs flight against being caught alone, and answers with a rule rather than a reckoning (see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]], [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[deeply-troubled]]). The rule binds “whoever would be best in battle” (ἀριστεύῃσι, 11.409; see [[aristos]]), and its last words admit what standing costs. Odysseus stands, is surrounded like a boar at bay, kills or wounds five men, and is wounded by Socus (11.411–438). The rule forbids flight, not prudence. Wounded, Odysseus draws back to speak (11.440), and it is Socus who turns to flee and is speared in the back “between the shoulders” (11.446–448), in the verses that killed fleeing men in Books 5 and 8 (5.40–41, 8.258–259). Then Odysseus “gave ground backward, and shouted to his companions” (11.461). He shouts three times, and Menelaus hears him and brings Ajax, fearing that “great longing for him may come upon the Danaans” if he falls alone (11.462–488; see [[odysseus]]).

**Ajax gives ground.** The longest retreat of the book is Ajax's, and Zeus begins it: he “roused Ajax to flight” (11.544). Ajax swings his shield of seven oxhides behind his back and falls back toward the throng, “glancing around like a wild beast, turning back again and again, moving knee past knee only a little” (11.545–547; see [[like-a-wild-beast]] and the translation's notes on 11.545 and 11.546–547). Two similes picture the same withdrawal, a lion driven from the farmyard and a donkey that boys cannot drive out of the grain until it has eaten its fill (11.548–562; see [[lion]] and [[donkey]]), and between them the narrator names the feeling: “so then Ajax, grieving at heart, went back from the Trojans very unwillingly; for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans” (11.556–557). It is a fighting retreat. “At one time would remember his furious courage and turn back again … and at another he would turn to flee”, and still he kept the Trojans from the ships (11.566–570). When Eurypylus calls the Argives to stand around him, he turns and stands among his companions (11.586–595; see [[ajax]]).

**Who flees.** The book's true flights belong to the beaten. The Trojans run past the tomb of Ilus to the Scaean gates, some fleeing across the plain like cattle before a lion, and Agamemnon follows, “always killing the hindmost” (11.163–178), the half-verse used of Hector's pursuit of the Achaeans in Book 8 (8.342). Hector, his helmet struck by Diomedes' spear, “ran back a measureless way and mingled with the throng” before he fell to his knees, and so “avoided black doom” (11.354–360). In Nestor's tale the Epeians scatter “this way and that” when Mulius falls, and the Pylians pursue them to Buprasium until Athena turns the army back (11.744–758; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]).

**A retreat by likeness.** Nestor ends with a plan for a retreat brought about by a borrowed name. Achilles is to send Patroclus out in his armor, “in the hope that, taking you for him, they may hold off from the war, the Trojans, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath” (11.799–800; see [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
