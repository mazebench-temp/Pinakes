---
summary: In Nestor's story the war between the Pylians and the Epeians is three times a νεῖκος, a “quarrel”: it arose over a cattle raid, Athena guided it, and it broke out at sunrise by the Alpheus.
passages:
  - 11.670-676 | Nestor's cattle raid
  - 11.714-737 | The Pylians march to the Alpheus
---

The word comes three times in Book 11, all in Nestor's story, and each time it names a war between two peoples. The war began as a dispute over property: “as when a quarrel arose between the Eleans and us over a cattle raid, when I killed Itymoneus” (11.671–672; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[raiding]]). Behind the raid lay the debts the Epeians owed to many in Pylos and the horses Augeas had kept (11.686–702; see [[hybris]]). Then a goddess took charge of it: the young Nestor, forbidden to arm, “stood out among our horsemen, though I was on foot, since so Athena guided the quarrel” (ὧς ἄγε νεῖκος Ἀθήνη, 11.720–721; see [[athena]]). And at sunrise by the Alpheus the battle began: “when the quarrel of the Pylians and the Epeians began, I was the first to kill a man” (11.737–738).

The translation keeps “quarrel” for νεῖκος and “strife” for ἔρις (see its table of key words). Book 11 shows the difference. It opens with Strife herself, sent by Zeus to the ships, rejoicing as the only god in the battle (11.3–14, 11.73–74; see [[eris]] and [[strife]]); in Book 4 it was Strife who “cast the quarrel” into the midst of the armies (4.444). In Nestor's story Athena guides the quarrel instead, and it ends in a victory. The battle is a νεῖκος elsewhere too, when Poseidon tells the Achaeans that “a great quarrel has arisen” (13.122), and when Patroclus, leaving Eurypylus, uses the same words (15.400; see [[patroclus]]).
