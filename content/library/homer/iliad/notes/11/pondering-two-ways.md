summary: Left alone among the Trojans, Odysseus speaks to his own heart, weighs flight against capture, breaks off with “But why does my heart debate these things with me?”, and decides to stand (11.403–410). It is the first of the four battlefield monologues of the Iliad, which share their opening and their turning verse.

**Odysseus alone.** Diomedes has been carried off wounded, “and Odysseus, famed for his spear, was left alone, and no one of the Argives stayed beside him, since rout had seized them all” (11.401–402). “Deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (11.403):

**The two ways.** The choice is put as two evils, flight and capture: “It is a great evil if I flee, fearing the multitude; but it is worse if I am caught alone” (11.404–406). The deliberation does not weigh them to the end. The turning verse breaks it off, ἀλλὰ τί ἤ μοι ταῦτα φίλος διελέξατο θυμός; “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (11.407), and the decision comes not from the two courses but from what he knows of himself and of his kind: “For I know that it is cowards who leave the fighting, but whoever would be best in battle must by all means stand his ground strongly, whether he is struck or strikes another” (11.408–410; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]] and [[aristos]]). The verse of pondering follows, “While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart”, and as so often the world breaks in: “the ranks of the shield-bearing Trojans came on” (11.411–412; see [[decision-scene]]). The same two verses of interruption end Menelaus' monologue in Book 17 (17.106–107).

**Four monologues.** Four warriors in the Iliad, alone and facing a stronger enemy, speak to their hearts in this way: Odysseus here, Menelaus over the body of Patroclus as Hector comes on (17.90–105), Agenor waiting for Achilles before the gates (21.552–570), and Hector before his own last fight (22.98–130). Each begins with the verse “and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” (11.403 = 17.90 = 21.552 = 22.98; see [[deeply-troubled]]) and ὤ μοι ἐγώ, “Ah me”, sets out the choice, and turns with “But why does my heart debate these things with me?” (11.407 = 17.97 = 21.562 = 22.122). Menelaus decides to give way; Odysseus, Agenor and Hector decide to stand. Bernard Fenik compared the four as variations on one form (“Stylization and Variety: Four Monologues in the Iliad”, in *Homer: Tradition and Invention*, 1978). Odysseus' is the first and the shortest, and the only one whose speaker is wounded in the fight that follows and lives (11.434–488; see [[odysseus]] and [[retreat]]).
