---
summary: The Trojans fleeing from Agamemnon run past the tomb of Ilus and the wild fig tree toward the city (11.166–168).
passages:
  - 11.166-171 | The rout to the Scaean gates
---

“Through the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree, / making for the city” (11.167–168): the fig tree is the second of the landmarks of the Trojans' flight, between the tomb of Ilus in mid-plain and the Scaean gates and the oak, where they halt (11.166–171; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). It stands near the wall, where Andromache told Hector the city could most easily be scaled (6.433–434), and Achilles will chase Hector past it (22.145).

**Outside Homer.** Strabo names the fig tree first among the places of Homer's plain that were still pointed out (13.1.34). He describes it as a rough place grown with wild figs, lying below the old city rather than the Ilium of his own day, and puts the oak a little below it (13.1.35; see [[strabo]]).
