summary: The sons of Antimachus beg Agamemnon from their chariot to take them alive for ransom, in the words of Adrastus' plea in Book 6, and he kills them for their father's crime (11.122–147). Achilles, watching the rout, imagines the Achaeans standing around his knees as suppliants (11.609–610).

**The plea.** Peisander and Hippolochus are caught in one chariot when the reins slip from their hands, and Agamemnon rushes at them like a lion. “The two begged him from the chariot” (γουναζέσθην, 11.130). γουνάζομαι is to entreat by the knees, but they never reach his:

{{quote:988ef760-c43c-5515-a7fe-8ae90f1eb334}}

The plea is the one Adrastus made to Menelaus, clasping his knees (6.46–50), and Dolon's to Diomedes and Odysseus (10.378–381): the ransom stored in the father's house, bronze, gold and worked iron, and the father who will pay it if he hears his son is alive at the ships (see [[apoina]], [[boundless-ransom]] and [[sons-of-antimachus]]). The narrator answers it with a play on words: they spoke μειλιχίοις ἐπέεσσιν, “with soothing words”, and heard a voice ἀμείλικτον, one that “would not be soothed” (11.136–137). The same half-verse ends Lycaon's plea to Achilles (21.98).

**The refusal.** In Book 6 Agamemnon told Menelaus to spare no Trojan, not even the boy in his mother's womb; Menelaus pushed Adrastus away, and Agamemnon stabbed him (6.55–65). Here the king gives a reason: the suppliants' father, Antimachus, urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came to Troy as an envoy with Odysseus, “now you will pay for your father's shameful insult” (11.138–142; see [[antimachus]], [[embassy]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). He thrusts Peisander from the chariot with his spear, cuts off Hippolochus' arms and head, and sends the trunk rolling like a log through the throng (11.143–147). Every man who begs for his life on the battlefield of the Iliad is killed: Adrastus, Dolon, the sons of Antimachus, and later Tros son of Alastor and Lycaon (20.463–472, 21.34–135). The poem remembers a time when it was not so. Achilles once took Isus and Antiphus alive on Ida and released them for ransom (11.104–106), and Agamemnon now kills both (see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]).

**Around Achilles' knees.** Watching the rout from his ship, Achilles says to Patroclus: “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me (λισσομένους); for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.609–610). The army is to come to him as a suppliant comes, with its need. The embassy of Book 9 has already brought him gifts and pleas, and the verses have been taken as a sign that the embassy was composed later, or as meaning that only now will the Achaeans come in earnest (see the translation's note on 11.609–610 and [[supplication-type-scene]]).
