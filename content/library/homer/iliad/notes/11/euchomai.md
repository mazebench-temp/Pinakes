summary: Book 11 uses the verb and its nouns in all their senses. Hector claims “a great triumph” from Zeus; Paris and Odysseus boast over the men they have struck, and Socus warns Odysseus that he will either boast over two brothers or lose his life; Diomedes mocks Hector's prayers to Apollo; and in Nestor's story the Pylians pray to Zeus and Athena and at the end give thanks “to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men”.
grc: [ἐπεύξεαι, ἐπεύξατο]
en: [pray, gave thanks]

  - 11.430-455 | Socus and Odysseus

**Triumph.** When Agamemnon leaves the field, Hector calls to the Trojans: “Their best man is gone, and a great triumph has been given me by Zeus, son of Cronus”, and bids them drive at the Danaans, “so that you may win a higher triumph” (εὖχος, 11.288–290; see [[hector]] and [[kydos]]). εὖχος is the thing a victor can claim, and Hector says that Zeus has given it before the killing begins. Odysseus, wounded by Socus, promises him the same in Sarpedon's verses to Tlepolemus: “brought down by my spear you will give me triumph, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (11.443–445 = 5.652–654, with one verse changed).

**Boasts.** Paris, having shot Diomedes through the foot, “leapt out from his ambush, and spoke in triumph” (εὐχόμενος, 11.379), and Diomedes answers that he has only scratched him, “and you boast for nothing” (εὔχεαι αὔτως, 11.388; see [[paris]] and [[diomedes]]). Socus offers Odysseus a choice of two outcomes: “today either you will boast over both sons of Hippasus, having killed two such men and taken their armor, or struck by my spear you will lose your life” (ἐπεύξεαι, 11.431–433). The first comes true at once, and the narrator uses the verb Socus used: “he fell with a thud; and brilliant Odysseus boasted over him” (ἐπεύξατο, 11.449; see [[socus]] and [[odysseus]]). ἐπεύχομαι is the claim made over a beaten enemy, in the Iliad's usual sense of a boast spoken over the body (see the note on Book 10).

**Prayer.** Diomedes' taunt to Hector turns on prayer: Apollo has saved him again, “to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins” (εὔχεσθαι, 11.362–364; see [[apollo]]). In Nestor's story the Pylians joined battle with the Epeians “praying to Zeus and Athena” (εὐχόμενοι, 11.736), and when the Epeians had been driven to Buprasium, “all gave thanks to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (εὐχετόωντο, 11.761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). One verb covers the thanks to the god and the praise of the man. Achilles will use it of Hector at his death, the man “to whom the Trojans throughout the city prayed as to a god” (θεῷ ὣς εὐχετόωντο, 22.394).
