**The other speech in the assembly.** Antenor told the story from the side of the host: he received Menelaus and Odysseus as guests and heard them speak before the Trojans (3.205–224). Book 11 adds a speech against them. The narrator introduces the two sons of Antimachus as the sons of the man “who above all others, / having taken Alexander's gold, splendid gifts, / would not let Helen be given to fair-haired Menelaus” (11.123–125), and Agamemnon, hearing who they are, tells the rest:

{{quote:63d8e970-c67a-5707-a767-485305567509}}

ἀγγελίην ἐλθόντα, “when he came on an embassy”, is the phrase of Antenor's memory (σεῦ ἕνεκʼ ἀγγελίης, 3.206). The proposal was not carried out. The envoys went home, and the war came instead. Antimachus is set against Antenor: one man took the envoys into his house, and later urged that Helen be given back because the Trojans were fighting after breaking their oaths (7.347–353); the other took Alexander's gold and urged that the envoys be killed (see [[antenor]] and [[antimachus]]). Envoys and heralds were under the protection of Zeus, “messengers of Zeus and of men” (1.334, 7.274; see [[heralds]]).

**The debt paid.** The sons beg in the words of Adrastus, offering the bronze, gold and iron in their father's house (11.131–135 ≈ 6.46–50; compare 10.378–381; see [[supplication]] and [[apoina]]). Agamemnon answers that they will pay for their father's “shameful insult” (ἀεικέα … λώβην, 11.142). λώβη is the word Achilles used of what Agamemnon did to him (9.387). The sons die at once: Peisander speared in the chest, Hippolochus cut down on the ground, his arms and head cut off and his trunk sent rolling like a log through the throng (11.143–147; see [[sons-of-antimachus]] and [[like-a-log]]). An insult offered to envoys before the war is paid for in the tenth year by the sons of the man who offered it. A third son of Antimachus, Hippomachus, is killed by Leonteus at the Achaean wall (12.188–189). See also [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]].
