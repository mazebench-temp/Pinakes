---
summary: Strabo uses Book 11 chiefly for the Peloponnese. Nestor's story of his war with the Epeians is his main evidence that Nestor's Pylos lay in Triphylia, and he places its Thryoessa, Minyeios, Olenian Rock and Alesium on the ground, denies that Augeas' games were the Olympic games, and cites Augeas' daughter Agamede for the drugs of Elean Ephyra; he also quotes Cinyras' gift, the mound of Ilus, the clouds of the brightening South Wind, Cisseus, and Peleus' charge “always to be the best”.
---

- **Cinyras' gift.** Arguing that Menelaus' wanderings took him to the kings of Egypt, Ethiopia and Arabia in search of gifts, because they knew the glory of the sons of Atreus, Strabo compares what “is said of Agamemnon's corselet: ‘which Cinyras had once given him to be a guest-gift, / for he had heard in Cyprus the great report’” (*Geography* 1.2.32, quoting 11.20–21). His text has Κύπρονδε, “to Cyprus”, as one word, the reading that the translation follows where the digitized Greek prints Κύπρον δέ (see the translation's note on 11.21, [[cinyras]] and [[corselet]]).
- **The mound of Ilus.** Plato distinguished three stages of settlement after the floods, on the mountain tops, on the foothills and on the plains, and found them in Homer (*Laws* 3.681e–682b). Strabo, retelling the argument, gives the third to the time of Ilus, said to be the founder of Ilios, and thinks it likely that Ilus was buried in the middle of the plain because he was the first to dare to settle there; he quotes the rout “past the mound of ancient Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, / through the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree” (*Geography* 13.1.25, quoting 11.166–167). Even Ilus, he adds, did not build where the Ilium of his day stood, but about thirty stades further up. The fig tree and the mound of Ilus are among the landmarks still shown on the Scamandrian plain, and the fig tree and the oak, he argues, lie near the old site and far from the new (13.1.34–35; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[the-fig-tree]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).
- **The brightening South Wind.** Some held that there are only two principal winds, north and south, and cited Homer, who seems to join the Argestes to the South Wind in ἀργεστᾶο Νότοιο. Posidonius, as Strabo reports him, answered that Homer's ἀργεστής Νότος is the Leuconotus, the “white South Wind”, which brings few clouds, and that in the simile “as when the West Wind batters the clouds / of the brightening South Wind, striking with a deep squall” the West Wind scatters the feeble clouds that the Leuconotus has gathered; ἀργεστής is an epithet here (*Geography* 1.2.21, quoting 11.305–306; see [[notus]] and [[zephyrus]]).
- **Cisseus.** Among the small towns that Cassander joined into Thessalonica was Cissus, which one might suppose connected with the Cisseus “whom the poet mentions, ‘Cisseus reared him’, speaking of Iphidamas”; Iphidamas himself, Strabo adds, might have come from it, since his grandfather Cisseus reared him “in Thrace”, which is now called Macedonia (*Geography* 7, fr. 21, fr. 24, on 11.223; see [[cisseus]], [[iphidamas]] and [[thrace]]).
- **Nestor's Pylos.** Nestor's story to Patroclus is Strabo's chief evidence that Nestor's Pylos was neither the Elean nor the Messenian but the Triphylian, and he identifies its Thryoessa with the Thryum of the Catalogue and its river Minyeios with the Anigrus (*Geography* 8.3.19, 8.3.24, 8.3.28–29): [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]].
- **Agamede.** Explaining why Homer sends men to Ephyra on the Selleis, in Elis, for deadly drugs (*Odyssey* 1.259–262, 2.328–329), Strabo adds that Nestor too, in his story of the war with the Epeians, brings in the daughter of Augeas, their king, as a sorceress (φαρμακίς), and quotes the verses on Mulius and his wife, “who knew all the drugs that the wide earth nourishes” (*Geography* 8.3.5, quoting 11.738–741). He leaves out her name. See [[agamede]], [[mulius]] and [[ephyra]].
- **The games in Elis.** Homer, Strabo says, mentions none of the crowned games of later times, though some think he means the Olympic games when Augeas keeps the four prize-winning horses that came for the contests. Strabo answers that Pisatis, where Olympia lies, was not then subject to Augeas, and that the Olympic games were never held in Elis but always at Olympia; Nestor's games were held in Elis, where the debt was owed, and they were not for a crown, since the horses were to race for a tripod (*Geography* 8.3.30, quoting 11.698–700). In the same section he infers from the Catalogue that the Eleans were weak in the time of the Trojan war, humbled by the Pylians and later by Heracles, when Augeas was overthrown: they sent forty ships to Troy, Nestor ninety (2.591–624). See [[augeas]], [[contests]] and [[tripod]].
- **Buprasium, the Olenian Rock and Alesium.** The Olenian Rock, Strabo reports, was conjectured to be Mount Scollis, and Alesium, he says, is the district called Alesiaeum, by Amphidolis on the mountain road from Elis to Olympia, where the neighbors held a monthly market. He quotes Nestor's verses on the end of the pursuit with μέσφʼ for our ὄφρʼ, reads “where the hill of Alesium is named” as a displaced order, and notes that some point to a river Alesius (*Geography* 8.3.10, quoting 11.756–758). Buprasium itself, he says, had been a notable settlement of Elis that no longer stood; only the district on the road from Elis to Dyme kept the name (8.3.8). See [[buprasium]], [[olenian-rock]] and [[alesium]].
- **Always to be the best.** Strabo doubts the geography of Posidonius, a friend of Pompey, and tells the story that Pompey, setting out against the pirates, heard him lecture at Rhodes and asked as he left whether he had any instructions; Posidonius answered with the verse of Peleus' charge to Achilles, “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (*Geography* 11.1.6, quoting 11.784 = 6.208; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]).
