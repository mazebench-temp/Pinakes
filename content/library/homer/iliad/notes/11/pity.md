summary: Nestor says that Achilles “does not care for the Danaans or pity them”, though he sends to ask after the wounded; on his way back Patroclus sees the wounded Eurypylus and pities him, in the first use in the Iliad of the verb that later Greek made the common word for pity, and tends his wound. The narrator calls the dead Iphidamas “pitiable”.
grc: [ᾤκτειρε, οἰκτρὸς]
passages:

  - 11.809-818 | Patroclus pities Eurypylus

**Achilles' pity.** Nestor answers Achilles' question with a question: “Why does Achilles grieve like this for the sons of the Achaeans, as many as have been struck by missiles?” (ὀλοφύρεται, 11.656–657). He takes the errand as a show of concern and denies the concern behind it: “But Achilles, brave as he is, does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (οὐδʼ ἐλεαίρει, 11.664–665). The two words of the entry stand a few lines apart, the grief that is spoken and the pity that is not felt (see the article above, [[achilles]] and [[nestor]]). The charge of pitilessness is the one the envoys made in Book 9 (9.300–302, 9.496–497, 9.632; see the note on Book 9).

ᾤκτειρε, from οἰκτίρω, is the verb that later Greek made the common word for pity; the Iliad has it only from here on (see the article above). The half-verse τὸν δὲ ἰδὼν ᾤκτειρε, “and seeing him he pitied him”, is a form of the formula with which the gods pity men (“seeing him, he pitied him”, τὸν δὲ ἰδὼν ἐλέησε), and the poem gives it again to Achilles: when Patroclus comes to him weeping in Book 16, “brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him” (τὸν δὲ ἰδὼν ᾤκτιρε, 16.5), and at the games he pities Eumelus, last in the chariot race (23.534). The pity that Nestor denied Achilles, Patroclus shows, and it moves him to act: he takes Eurypylus to his hut and cuts out the arrow, though he is on Nestor's errand (11.838–848; see [[healing]], [[patroclus]] and [[eurypylus]]).

**Pitiable.** The narrator has his own pity. Iphidamas, killed by Agamemnon, “slept the sleep of bronze, pitiable, far from his wife, whom he had wooed, fighting for his townsmen” (οἰκτρός, 11.241–242; see [[iphidamas]] and [[death-in-battle]]). The sons of Antimachus, weeping, address Agamemnon “with soothing words”, “but the voice they heard would not be soothed” (11.136–137; see [[apoina]]). Odysseus, surrounded, “kept off the pitiless day” with his spear (νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, 11.484), and Eurypylus asks the Argives to keep the pitiless day from Ajax (11.588; see [[pitiless]]).
