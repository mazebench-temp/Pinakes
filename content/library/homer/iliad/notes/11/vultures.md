---
summary: After Agamemnon's rout the Trojan charioteers lie on the plain, “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.159–162).
grc: [γύπεσσιν]
passages:
  - 11.159-162 | Dearer to the vultures
---

πολλοὶ δʼ ἐριαύχενες ἵπποι / κείνʼ ὄχεα κροτάλιζον ἀνὰ πτολέμοιο γεφύρας / ἡνιόχους ποθέοντες ἀμύμονας· οἳ δʼ ἐπὶ γαίῃ / κείατο, γύπεσσιν πολὺ φίλτεροι ἢ ἀλόχοισιν: “and many horses with high-arched necks rattled empty chariots along the bridges of war, missing their noble charioteers; but they lay on the ground, far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.159–162). The horses miss their drivers (ποθέοντες, “longing for, missing”), and the men are “dearer” (φίλτεροι) to the birds that will eat them than to their wives. φίλος, the word of love and belonging, is turned upside down: the vultures want them now. The comparison is the narrator's, at the end of the forest-fire simile for Agamemnon's onslaught (11.155–159; see [[forest-fire]]).

γύψ is the vulture that eats the unburied dead, named in threats and curses: Agamemnon's promise that vultures will eat the oath-breakers (4.237), Hector's words over the dying Patroclus (16.836), and Priam's wish that dogs and vultures might eat Achilles (22.42). The other bird of Book 11's dead is the οἰωνός, the bird of prey of Diomedes' and Odysseus' boasts (11.395, 11.453–454; see [[birds]]). The wives of the charioteers, who will not have them back, are the wives of Diomedes' boast, who tear their cheeks in mourning while the birds gather round the body (11.393–395).
