summary: Three spectators watch the third day of battle with the same word, εἰσορόων, “looking on”. Strife, the only god in the fighting, rejoices; Zeus sits apart, exulting, while the other gods blame him; and Achilles, standing on the stern of his ship, sees a wounded man carried past and sends Patroclus to ask who he is.

**Three spectators.** The book gives the same participle, εἰσορόων, “looking on”, to three watchers. Strife “rejoiced to look on”, the only god in the battle (11.73–74; see [[strife]] and [[eris]]). The other gods sit in their halls on Olympus and blame Zeus (11.75–79), while he, “withdrawing apart, away from the others he sat, exulting in his glory, looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans, and on the flash of bronze, and the slayers and the slain” (11.80–83). The second and third verses are those that seated him on Ida in Book 8 (11.81–82 ≈ 8.51–52; see the note on Book 8 and [[kydos]]); the last is new, and it names what he sees, the killers and the killed together. The third watcher is Achilles, “for he was standing on the stern of his ship, huge in the hull, watching the sheer toil and the tearful onslaught” (11.600–601; see [[achilles]]).

**Apart.** Zeus and Achilles watch from apart, and the poem marks it with the same words. νόσφι λιασθείς, “withdrawing apart”, occurs twice in Homer: of Zeus here (11.80), and of Achilles in Book 1, when Briseis has been taken and he “withdrew from his companions, and sat apart on the shore of the gray sea, looking out over the boundless deep” (1.349–350). The god who watches the war he has turned against the Achaeans is described in the words used of the man for whose sake he turned it (see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**From Ida.** Later Zeus comes down to Ida: he “sat down on the peaks of Ida rich in springs, coming down from heaven, with the lightning in his hands”, and sends Iris to Hector (11.182–185; see [[ida]] and [[iris]]). From there he governs what he watches: “Then the son of Cronus stretched the battle evenly for them, looking down from Ida” (καθορῶν, 11.336–337).

**What Achilles sees.** Achilles' watching sets the rest of the book in motion. He sees Nestor's mares carry a wounded man past, and the sight gives him the satisfaction he has waited for: “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me” (11.608–610). But he did not see clearly. The man “looks in every way like Machaon” from behind, “but I did not see the man's eyes; for the mares darted past me” (11.613–615). To find out, he sends Patroclus, and the narrator marks the errand as “the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Patroclus comes back from it with Nestor's plan (11.794–803).

**In the field.** On the plain sight decides where men fight. Hector does not know that Ajax is routing the Trojans, “since he was fighting on the left of all the battle” (11.497–498), until Cebriones sees the turmoil and knows Ajax by his shield, “for he has the broad shield around his shoulders” (11.521–527; see [[cebriones]]). Paris watches from behind the pillar on the tomb of Ilus and shoots Diomedes from there (11.369–379; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]).
