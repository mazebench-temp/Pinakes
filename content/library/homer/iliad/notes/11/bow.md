summary: Paris, shooting from behind the pillar on the tomb of Ilus, pierces Diomedes' foot and is answered with the poem's fullest scorn of the archer: “Archer, insulter, glorying in your bow, ogler of girls” (11.369–395). He goes on to hit Machaon and Eurypylus, and the despised bow takes three Achaean leaders out of the battle.

**The shot.** While Diomedes is stripping Agastrophus, Alexander, “leaning against a pillar on the man-made tomb of Ilus”, bends his bow at him (11.369–372; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]). “The other drew back his bow by the grip and shot, and the shaft did not fly from his hand in vain” (11.375–376). The πῆχυς is the middle of the bow, where the hand holds it, and Helenus draws his bow in the same half-verse (13.583). The arrow goes through the flat of the right foot and sticks in the ground, and Paris leaps out “from his ambush” (ἐκ λόχου), laughing, and boasts that he wishes he had hit him in the lowest part of the flank (11.377–383). The archer fights from cover: Pandarus shot from behind his companions' shields (4.113–115), and Teucer from under Ajax's (8.266–272; see [[teucer]] and [[paris]]).

**The answer.**

{{quote:a6ffc63c-4238-5917-b674-f548de2070f9}}

τοξότης, “archer”, is the first word of abuse. λωβητήρ, “insulter”, is the name the Achaeans gave Thersites when Odysseus silenced him (2.275; see [[thersites]] and [[lobe]]). κέρᾳ ἀγλαέ is taken here as “glorying in your bow”, κέρας being the bow, which was made of horn, as Pandarus' was of the horns of a wild goat (4.105–111); an explanation reported in antiquity takes it of a lock of hair dressed like a horn, “proud of your curl”, which would go with παρθενοπίπης, “ogler of girls”, a word found only here (see the translation's note on 11.385 and [[ogler-of-girls]]). The archer is set against the man who fights “face to face in armor” (ἀντίβιον σὺν τεύχεσι), and his arrow against the spear: the bow and the thick-flying arrows (βιὸς καὶ ταρφέες ἰοί) strike no harder than a woman or a child (11.386–390), while Diomedes' own βέλος kills if it only grazes (11.391–395; see [[arrows]] and [[spear]]).

**What the bow does.** The scratch is enough. Odysseus stands in front of him while he draws the arrow out, “and a grievous pain went through his flesh”, and he drives off to the ships (11.396–400). Diomedes takes no further part in the battles of the poem: he comes to the assembly in Book 19 limping and leaning on his spear (19.47–50), and Achilles, sending Patroclus out, says that no spear now rages in Diomedes' hands (16.74–75). Paris' next arrows strike Machaon in the right shoulder (11.505–507) and Eurypylus in the right thigh (11.581–584). Of the five wounded leaders Nestor counts, three were shot by his bow (11.658–664; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). The same Paris will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates with Apollo, as Hector foretells with his last breath (22.359–360).
