---
summary: Thetis weeps among the sea goddesses “for the fate of her noble son” (24.85), and tells him that “death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (24.132); Hermes approves Priam's words with the formula, “all that you have said is fair and right” (24.379); Helen weeps for “my ill-fated self” (24.773). Fate in person, who spins a man's lot at his birth, is the [[fates|Fates]] of 24.49 and 24.209.
---

**Thetis and her son.** When Iris finds her, Thetis is weeping for her son's μόρος, “who was to / perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (24.85–86; see [[thetis]]), a verse that nearly repeats Sarpedon's at 16.461. Walter Leaf reports that the edition of Rhianus read ὃς τάχʼ ἔμελλεν, “who was soon to”, a plausible reading, since the nearness of the death would add to her sorrow, and he calls the athetesis of 86 unworthy of Aristarchus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.85, 24.86; see the translation's note on 24.86). Her words to Achilles in his hut are those of the dying Patroclus to Hector, “for you will not live long for me, but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (24.131–132; 16.852–853; see [[mighty-fate]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Achilles says it of himself to Priam: Peleus fathered “one son, doomed to an untimely death” (παναώριον, 24.540), a word found only here, which Leaf takes as virtually the same as μινυνθάδιος, “short-lived”, and ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated” (1.352, 1.417; on 24.540).

**Fair and right.** Hermes, as the young Myrmidon, answers Priam's thanks to the god who sent him a guide with the verse with which Agamemnon brushed aside Nestor in Book 1, “Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right” (24.379 = 1.286; see [[hermes]]). Leaf takes κατὰ μοῖραν here as the god's approval of Priam's pious words (on 24.379). Nestor had used the verse to thank Achilles for his prize, with τέκος in place of γέρον, “Yes, my child” (23.626; see [[geron]]).

**Ill-fated.** Andromache calls herself and Hector δυσάμμοροι, “both most ill-fated”, in the verse she spoke in Book 22 (24.727 = 22.485; see [[most-ill-fated]]), and Helen ends her lament weeping for herself, ἄμμορον, “ill-fated” (24.773), the word of Andromache's first plea to Hector (6.408; Leaf on 24.773; see [[helen]] and [[andromache]]).

**Spun by the gods.** Apollo says that the Fates gave men an enduring heart, and Hecuba that mighty Fate spun Hector's death for him at his birth; the editor prints both as persons (24.49, 24.209–210; see [[fates]]). Achilles gives the spinning to the gods: “for so the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals, / to live in grief” (24.525–526; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[achos]]).
