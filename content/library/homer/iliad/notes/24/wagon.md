summary: Priam's mule wagon carries the ransom out of Troy and brings Hector's body home. Zeus and Iris name it in the message (24.150, 24.179); Priam's sons bring it out, bind on its wicker body, yoke it and load it (24.189–190, 24.263–277); Idaeus drives it to the hut of Achilles, where the ransom is taken off and the body laid on (24.324–325, 24.578–590); and the women rush to it at the gates of Troy (24.697–718). At the end wagons bring in the wood for the pyre (24.782–784).

**The message.** Zeus allows Priam one companion, a herald, “an older man, to guide / the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back / the body to the city” (24.149–151), and Iris repeats the words to Priam (24.178–180; see [[idaeus]], [[mules]] and [[well-wheeled]]). Priam at once bids his sons “make ready the well-wheeled mule wagon / and bind the wicker body upon it” (24.189–190), and when he has driven the Trojans from his colonnade he turns on them: “Will you not make ready the wagon for me at once, / and put all these things on it, so that we may make our way?” (24.263–264; see [[sons-of-priam]]). Walter Leaf sets the verse beside Nausicaa's request in the Odyssey, “Daddy dear, will you not make ready the wagon for me” (*Odyssey* 6.57, in the same construction), and notes how different the tone is: whether such a question is a polite request or a command lies in the context, not in the words (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.263).

The parts are named only here in Homer, and the translation renders them by the usual explanation: πάσσαλος “peg”, ὀμφαλός “knob”, οἴηκες “guide rings”, ζυγόδεσμον “yoke band”, ἕστωρ “pin”, κρίκος “ring”, γλωχίν “hook” (see the translation's note on 24.268–274). Leaf discusses the passage in an appendix (on 24.268). Zenodotus left out 24.269, and Heyne thought it interpolated, though without obvious grounds; ζυγός, the masculine form of “yoke”, is unknown to Homer (Leaf on 24.269). The yoke is of boxwood and the band nine cubits long; ἐννεάπηχυς is said elsewhere in Homer only of Otus and Ephialtes, who at nine years old were nine cubits broad (*Odyssey* 11.311–312). πρωτοπαγής, “newly built”, is the word of the chariots that Pandarus left in his father's halls (5.194; Leaf on 24.267). Then the ransom is brought from the chamber and heaped on the polished wagon, and the strong-hoofed mules are yoked, the gift of the Mysians (24.275–278; see [[apoina]] and [[mysians]]). Leaf takes the weighing of the gold and the bringing out of the treasure (24.232) as out of place here, since the ransom stays in the chamber until it is put on the wagon at 24.275 (on 24.232; see [[talent]]).

**To the ships and back.** “In front the mules drew the four-wheeled wagon, / and wise-hearted Idaeus drove them; and behind / came the horses” (24.324–326; see [[chariot]]). τετράκυκλος, “four-wheeled”, is found in Homer only here and of the wagons in the Cyclops' cave (*Odyssey* 9.242), and Leaf notes the short vowel before τρ (on 24.324). The scholia remark that the mules are masculine at 24.278 and feminine at 24.325 (Leaf on 24.278). Hermes brings Priam into the camp “and the splendid gifts on the wagon” (24.447; see [[hermes]] and [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]); at the hut Achilles' men take the ransom “from the polished wagon”, and when the body has been washed Achilles lays it on a bier, “and his companions lifted it with him onto the polished wagon” (24.578–590; see [[achilles]] and [[bed]]). At 24.578 Leaf prints ἐϋσσώτρου, “well-rimmed”, against the vulgate ἐϋξέστου (on 24.578; see the translation's note on 24.578). At dawn “the mules carried the corpse”, and Cassandra sees him “lying on the bier, drawn by the mules” (24.697–702; see [[cassandra]]). His wife and mother tear their hair, “rushing to the well-wheeled wagon / and touching his head”, until Priam asks the people to let him pass with the mules, “and they stood apart and made way for the wagon” (24.710–718; see [[lament]] and [[troy]]).

**The wood.** For the pyre the Trojans go out of the city with wagons: “under the wagons oxen and mules / they yoked”, and for nine days they bring in a vast load of wood (24.782–784; see [[cattle]], [[pyres]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).
