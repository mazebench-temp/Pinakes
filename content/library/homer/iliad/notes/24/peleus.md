summary: Hera claims to have given Thetis to Peleus, dear above all to the immortals, and reminds the gods that they all came to the wedding (24.59–63). Priam begs Achilles to remember his father, an old man like himself, who hopes every day to see his son come home; Achilles weeps for him, and tells Priam that the gods gave Peleus every gift but one, a son who will die young and cannot care for him in his old age (24.486–512, 24.534–542). The son of Peleus is Achilles' name seven times.

**The wedding.** Hera answers Apollo that Hector and Achilles cannot have equal honor: Achilles is the offspring of a goddess, “whom I myself / nursed and cherished, and gave as wife to a man, / to Peleus, who was dear above all to the hearts of the immortals”, and all the gods came to the wedding, Apollo with his lyre among them (24.59–63; see [[hera]], [[thetis]] and [[apollo]]). Πηλέϊ, the dative with ε, is found only here; the usual form is Πηλῆϊ (24.534). Elsewhere the marriage is the gods' doing, “on the day when they put you into the bed of a mortal man” (18.84–85), or Zeus' doing, as Thetis tells Hephaestus: “Out of all the other goddesses of the sea he subdued me to a man, / Peleus, son of Aeacus” (18.432–433; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). Walter Leaf reports the later legend that Hera favored Thetis because she had refused the advances of Zeus, and notes that Pindar too speaks of the wedding feast (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.60 and 24.63, citing Pindar, *Pythian* 3.93 and *Nemean* 4.65).

**Remember your father.** Hermes bids Priam beg Achilles “by his father and his mother of the lovely hair / and his child” (24.466–467), and Priam begins with the father:

{{quote:c24b9e02-fa75-566f-bab7-122926bc8c2d}}

τηλίκου ὥς περ ἐγών, “who is of my age”, sets the two old men side by side. ἐπὶ γήραος οὐδῷ, “on the threshold of old age”, is what Priam called his own last years in his plea to Hector (22.60; see [[old-age-and-memory]]), and the neighbors who wear Peleus down are the fear that Achilles' shade voices in the Odyssey, when it asks Odysseus whether Peleus still has honor among the Myrmidons or is slighted because old age binds his hands and feet (*Odyssey* 11.494–503; Leaf on 24.488). Achilles himself has thought of his father as dead already, or grieving in hateful old age and waiting always for the grim news of his son's death (19.334–337), and knew that Peleus would not welcome him home (18.330–332; see [[nostos]]). Priam's hope for Peleus is the hope that he himself has lost: “but I am utterly ill-starred” (24.493). He ends as he began, “remembering your own father” (24.504; see [[supplication]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The words stir in Achilles “the longing for lament for his father” (24.507), and he weeps for his own father, and then for Patroclus (24.511–512; see [[achilles]] and [[the-longing-for-lament]]).

**The gods' gifts.** Achilles answers with Peleus as the example of the jars of Zeus, from which a man may receive a mixture of good and evil (24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]):

{{quote:d74229ce-88d5-5f39-8421-db35cc9f9579}}

The first verse is the one that named the immortal horses and the armor as the gods' gifts to Peleus (24.534 ≈ 16.381, 18.84; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). ὄλβῳ τε πλούτῳ τε, “in prosperity and wealth”, he excelled all men, and he ruled the Myrmidons (24.535–536; see [[myrmidons]]). The evil is that he has one son, παναώριον, “doomed to an untimely death”; Leaf compares the word with μινυνθάδιος, “short-lived”, and ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, of the first book (on 24.540; 1.352, 1.417; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). The poem names no other child of Peleus' house but a daughter, Polydora, the mother of Menesthius (16.175–176; see [[polydora]]). Achilles does not care for his father as he grows old, “since very far from my native land / I sit here in Troy, bringing sorrow to you and your children” (24.541–542). Priam is the second example: he too was blessed once, in wealth and sons (24.543–548; see [[priam]]).

**The patronymic.** Achilles is “the son of Peleus” seven times in the book: Πηλεΐωνα (24.338), Πηληϊάδεω (24.406, 24.431, 24.448), Πηλεΐωνι (24.458), Πηλεΐωνος (24.465) and Πηλεΐδης (24.572; see [[son-of-peleus]]). Six of them belong to the journey of Priam, from Zeus' order to Hermes to the moment Priam is told to clasp the knees “of the son of Peleus” (24.338–465), and the last to Achilles' leap like a lion from the door of his hut (24.572).
