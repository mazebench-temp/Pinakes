---
summary: Hecuba names Lemnos last of the three islands to which Achilles sold the sons of Priam he took alive, “to Samos and to Imbros and to misty Lemnos” (24.751–753); it is the island where he sold Lycaon (21.40–41, 21.78–79).
---

**Misty Lemnos.** ἀμιχθαλόεσσα is found in Homer only here, and the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* has the same epithet at the same place in the verse, in a line that also names Imbros: Ἴμβρος τʼ εὐκτιμένη καὶ Λῆμνος ἀμιχθαλόεσσα, “well-built Imbros and misty Lemnos” (*Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 36; see [[imbros]]). Its meaning was already uncertain in antiquity. Walter Leaf sets out two explanations. One takes it from μίγνυμι, “mix”, as “inhospitable”, for the island's harsh shores or for the evil name of its people, the Sintians (1.594; see [[sintians]]); the other from ὀμίχλη, “mist”, perhaps for the smoke of the “Lemnian fire”, and a variant ὀμιχθαλόεσσαν points that way. Leaf prefers the second, and finds that the first, though it suits the deserted island of Sophocles' *Philoctetes*, does not suit Homer's Lemnos, “well-built” in the Iliad (21.40) and a “well-built citadel” in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 8.283); he allows that the scholia's gloss, “prosperous, in the dialect of Cyprus”, may after all be right, and reports that Antimachus read μιχθαλόεσσαν (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.753). The translation renders “misty” (see the translation's note on 24.753).

**Where Priam's sons were sold.** Lemnos was the market nearest the camp. Its ships brought wine to the Achaeans, who paid with bronze, iron, hides, cattle and slaves (7.467–475; see [[euneus]]); Achilles sold Lycaon there, and Euneus, Jason's son, paid the price (21.40–41, 21.78–79; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), and the silver bowl that bought him was a prize at the games for Patroclus (23.745–747; see [[mixing-bowl]]). Hecuba's lament names the island with Samothrace and Imbros, the three islands of the northern Aegean that lie across the sea from the Troad, and sets the sons who were sold against Hector, whom Achilles killed and dragged (24.751–756; see [[hecuba]], [[samothrace]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).
