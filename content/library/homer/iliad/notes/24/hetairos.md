summary: Patroclus is still “his dear companion”, whom Achilles weeps for and around whose mound he drags Hector (24.4, 24.51, 24.416, 24.755). Automedon and Alcimus are the companions he honored most after him (24.574–575); Hera calls Apollo “friend of the wicked” (24.63); and Zeus says that Hermes loves best “to keep company with a man” (24.335).
grc: [ἕταρʼ, ἑταιρίσσαι]
en: [friend of the wicked, keep company with]
---

**The dead companion.** The book begins with Achilles weeping, “remembering his dear companion” (24.4). Apollo and Hermes call Patroclus the same when they tell of the dragging, ἑτάροιο φίλοιο, and Hecuba adds his name, “his companion / Patroclus, whom you killed” (24.51, 24.416, 24.755–756; see [[patroclus]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). When the body of Hector has been laid on the wagon, Achilles groans and “called his dear companion by name”, asking his pardon (24.591–595; see [[apoina]]).

**The living companions.** Around Achilles in his hut his companions prepare the morning meal when Thetis comes (24.123), sit apart when Priam enters (24.473), lift the body onto the wagon (24.590), prepare the sheep for the supper (24.622), and spread the beds (24.643). Two of them wait on him, Automedon and Alcimus, “whom above all / of his companions Achilles honored, after Patroclus who was dead” (24.574–575; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]); Walter Leaf compares the Odyssey's words for Antilochus, whom Achilles honored above all his other companions after Patroclus (*Odyssey* 24.78–79; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.575). At the end, Hector's brothers and companions gather his bones from the pyre (24.793), as Sarpedon's brothers and kinsmen were to bury him (16.456; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

**Friend and companion.** Hera taunts Apollo with having feasted at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis with his lyre, κακῶν ἕταρʼ, “friend of the wicked” (24.62–63; see [[hera]] and [[apollo]]); Leaf compares Apollo's lyre at the feast of the gods in Book 1 (1.603; on 24.63). Zeus sends Hermes to Priam because it is to him above all that it is dearest ἀνδρὶ ἑταιρίσσαι, “to keep company with a man” (24.334–335). The verb is found only here in Homer in the active; Leaf finds the intransitive use strange, compares the middle, “to take as his companion” (13.456), and takes the phrase to show Hermes as a particularly affable god (on 24.335; see [[hermes]]).
