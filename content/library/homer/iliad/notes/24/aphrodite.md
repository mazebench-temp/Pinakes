summary: Aphrodite is the goddess, unnamed, whom Alexander praised at the Judgment, “her who gave him his grievous lust”. At the end of the book Cassandra, who first sees her father bringing Hector home, is “like golden Aphrodite”.
# Unnamed: "her who gave him his grievous lust" (24.30).
refs: [24.30]
---

**The Judgment.** Hera, Poseidon and Athena keep their hatred of Troy for Alexander's blind folly, “who insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard, / and praised her who gave him his grievous lust” (24.29–30; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]] and [[paris]]). The goddess he praised is not named, and her gift is not Helen but μαχλοσύνη, “lust”, a word found only here in Homer. Aristarchus objected that Aphrodite gave Paris not lust but Helen, the most beautiful woman of her time (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.23; see the note on the Judgment and the translation's note on 24.23–30). The gifts of Aphrodite are what Hector threw in Paris' face in Book 3, “Your lyre would be no help to you then, nor the gifts of Aphrodite, / your hair and your looks” (3.54–55), and Paris answered, “do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite” (3.64; see [[hector]]).

**Like golden Aphrodite.** At dawn Cassandra is the first to see Priam coming home with the body: “but Cassandra, like golden Aphrodite, / who had gone up onto Pergamus, caught sight of her dear father” (24.699–700; see [[cassandra]], [[pergamus]] and [[golden]]). The likeness, ἰκέλη χρυσῇ Ἀφροδίτῃ, was said, with the uncontracted χρυσέῃ, of Briseis when she saw Patroclus dead (19.282; see [[briseis]]), and in the *Odyssey* Penelope comes out of her chamber “like Artemis or golden Aphrodite” (*Odyssey* 17.37 = 19.54). Both times in the Iliad the beauty of a woman meets a corpse. Cassandra, “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (13.365–366), wails aloud and calls the city to come and see Hector (24.703–706; see [[lament]]). In Book 23 Aphrodite herself kept the dogs from the body and anointed it (23.185–187); in the last book the gods' care for it is Apollo's (24.18–21; see [[apollo]]).
