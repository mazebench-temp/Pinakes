summary: Priam answers Hecuba that “if it is my fate / to die beside the ships” of the Achaeans, he would have it so (24.224–226), and he and Hecuba each say that the gods have cared for Hector “even in the portion of death” (24.428, 24.750). The adjectives of the family mark what is fitting and due: Achilles has “no fitting mind” (24.40), the gods' gifts are “their due” (24.425), and the stranger who meets Priam is “one of good omen” (24.376).
grc: [ἐναίσιμοι, ἐναίσιμα, αἴσιον]
en: [fitting, their due, of good omen]
---

**Priam's portion.** Hecuba begs Priam not to go to the man who killed his sons, and he answers that he has seen and heard the goddess himself; if it is his αἶσα to die at the ships, βούλομαι, “I would have it so”, once he has held his son and wept his fill (24.218–227; see [[priam]], [[boule]] and [[eros]]). Walter Leaf notes that βούλομαι is a much stronger word than ἐθέλω, “I am willing” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.226; see the translation's note on 24.226).

**Even in the portion of death.** The verse-end καὶ ἐν θανάτοιό περ αἴσῃ is said twice: by Priam, when the young stranger tells him that the body lies unharmed, “so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death” (24.428), and by Hecuba over the body, “truly while you were alive you were dear to the gods; / and they have cared for you even in the portion of death” (24.749–750). Leaf points out that περ in 749 has no concessive sense, and marks “alive” against the portion of death (on 24.749). The gods' care is their return for the gifts that Hector never failed to give (24.68, 24.425–428; see [[doron]], [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[thanatos]]).

**What is fitting.** The adjectives of αἶσα run through the book. Apollo says that Achilles has no φρένες ἐναίσιμοι, “no fitting mind”, nor any thought that can be bent (24.40–41; see [[phrenes]]). Priam says it is good to give the immortals ἐναίσιμα δῶρα, “the gifts that are their due” (24.425); the same word marked the lightning that Zeus sent as a sign “of good omen” on the day the Argives sailed for Troy (2.350–353). He calls the stranger αἴσιον, “one of good omen” (24.376), a word found only here: Leaf allows that it may rather mean “a man of moderation”, nearer the usual sense of αἶσα, and compares ἐναίσιμον, “in due time” (6.519), and the “ill-omened” signs of 4.381, παραίσια (on 24.376; see the translation's note on 24.376 and [[divination]]). The same stranger had come “in the likeness of a young prince” (24.347), αἰσυμνητῆρι, a word Leaf derives from αἶσα and μνάομαι, “one who is mindful of justice” (on 24.347; see [[hermes]]).
