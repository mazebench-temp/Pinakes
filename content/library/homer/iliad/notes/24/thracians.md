# Θρῇκες (24.234), the contracted form, is found only here in Homer; elsewhere Θρήϊκες.
grc: [Θρῇκες]
---

{{quote:eb4b3f38-d4d2-5416-a2ed-3b5b23127732}}

The cup is the last and the most precious of the ransom, named after the robes, cloaks, rugs, mantles and tunics, the ten talents of gold, the two tripods and the four cauldrons (24.229–233; see [[cup]] and [[apoina]]). It is a δέπας περικαλλές, “a beautiful cup”, the words of Nestor's cup (11.632; see [[the-cup-of-nestor]]), and a guest's gift: the Thracians gave it to Priam when he came to them on an ἐξεσίη, “an embassy”. Walter Leaf explains the word as “a sending”, and compares the embassy on which Odysseus, still a boy, was sent by his father and the elders to Messene to claim the sheep the Messenians had carried off from Ithaca (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.235; *Odyssey* 21.15–21); the Iliad tells nothing else of Priam's journey. κτέρας, “possession”, is found in the singular only here and in Nestor's promise to the spy, “no possession is like it” (10.216); elsewhere the plural is the funeral rites (on 24.235; see [[honoring-the-dead]]).

The poem's Thracians are Troy's allies from across the Hellespont, led by Acamas and Peirous (2.844–845), and Rhesus, newly come, whom Diomedes killed in his sleep with twelve of his men (10.434–502; see [[rhesus]]). Thracian work was prized on both sides: Helenus kills Deipyrus with “a great Thracian sword” (13.576–577), and Achilles offers the Thracian sword of Asteropaeus as a prize in the games (23.807–808; see [[thrace]]). Priam gives up his Thracian cup, as he gives up the rest, because “above all he wished in his heart / to ransom his dear son” (24.236–237; see [[priam]]).
