---
summary: Priam will let Achilles kill him at once, “when I have taken my son in my arms and put away my desire for lament” (24.227), and the meal in Achilles' hut ends with the formula, “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (24.628).
---

ἐπὴν γόου ἐξ ἔρον εἵην, “when I have … put away my desire for lament” (24.227), takes the formula that ends a meal, ἐξ ἔρον ἕντο (1.469; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]), and gives it to grief: lament is an appetite, and it can be satisfied. Walter Leaf explains the optative as attracted into the imagined act, “let Achilles kill me, so I might weep my fill” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.227; see [[lament]] and [[priam]]).

The book satisfies each appetite in turn. Achilles “had taken his fill of lament” when the longing for it left him (γόοιο τετάρπετο, 24.513; see [[himeros]]); the meal ends with the formula itself, in the verses that close the embassy's supper in the same hut in Book 9 (24.627–628 = 9.221–222; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[fasting]]); Priam and Achilles have their fill of looking at each other (τάρπησαν, 24.633; see [[thambos]]); and Priam asks for bed, to take his fill of sweet sleep (ταρπώμεθα, 24.636; see [[hypnos]]).
