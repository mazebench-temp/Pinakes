summary: The last book is full of mourning. Iris finds Priam's house in lament, the old man in the dung among his weeping sons and the women mourning through the halls; his family follows him to the plain lamenting, as if he were going to his death; Achilles and Priam weep together in the hut; and when the body comes home the city meets it at the gates, singers lead the dirge, and Andromache, Hecuba and Helen lament in turn, answered by the women and at last by the whole people (24.160–168, 24.327–328, 24.507–524, 24.703–776).

**The house of Priam.** When Iris comes to Priam with the message of Zeus, “she came to the house of Priam, and found there cries and lament” (24.160):

{{quote:71563cec-69a9-5f4e-bfc9-1763cf1d0c61}}

The sons weep around their father in the courtyard, and the women, his daughters and his sons' wives, mourn through the house (see [[priams-palace]], [[sons-of-priam]] and [[in-laws]]). The old man has rolled in the dung and heaped it on his head and neck, as he rolled in it in Book 22, calling each man by name, when the people held him back from going to the ships (22.414–415; see [[priam]]); he tells Achilles that since his son died he has never closed his eyes, “but always I groan and brood over countless sorrows, / rolling in the dung in the enclosure of the courtyard” (24.637–640). So Achilles poured the sooty dust over his head when he heard of Patroclus' death, lay stretched out in the dust and tore his hair (18.23–27; see [[achilles]]). Plato's Socrates would not have the poets show Priam, “near to the gods in birth”, rolling in the dung (*Republic* 3.388b; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]). ἐντυπὰς ἐν χλαίνῃ κεκαλυμμένος, “wrapped close in his cloak” (24.163), is found only here; the ancient explanation was a cloak drawn so tight that the body's outline showed through it (see the translation's note on 24.163).

**At home and on the road.** When Priam tells her that he will go to Achilles, Hecuba “wailed aloud” (κώκυσεν, 24.200), the cry with which she saw her son dragged (22.407; see [[hecuba]]), and she would have them weep for him at home: “No, let us weep for him now, apart, / sitting in the hall” (24.208–209). Priam will go, and if Achilles kills him, let it be “when I have taken my son in my arms and put away my desire for lament” (24.227): γόου ἐξ ἔρον εἵην is the phrase of a finished meal, ἐξ ἔρον ἕντο, “put away the desire” (24.628; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). He drives the Trojans from his colonnade, “Have you not / lament enough at home, that you come to trouble me?” (24.239–240; see [[trojans]]), and when he drives out of the city, “all his dear ones followed with him, / lamenting much, as if he were going to his death” (24.327–328). Hector's household lamented him “while he was still alive” in Book 6 (6.500).

**In the hut.** At Achilles' knees Priam's plea “roused the longing for lament for his father” (24.507; see [[the-longing-for-lament]]), and the two men weep, Priam for Hector and Achilles for his father and for Patroclus, until Achilles “had taken his fill of lament” (24.509–514; see [[supplication]]). Then he bids the old man let the sorrows lie quiet, “for there is no profit in chilling lament”, and not “mourn relentlessly” for his son (24.522–524, 24.549), and tells him of Niobe, who remembered food when she was worn out with shedding tears (24.602–613; see [[niobe]]). Priam may weep again when he has brought his son home, “he will be much wept for” (24.619–620), and he asks for nine days: “Nine days we would lament him in our halls” (24.664; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

**The city.** Priam and the herald drive toward the city “with cries of woe and groaning” (οἰμωγῇ τε στοναχῇ τε, 24.696; see the translation's row for οἰμωγή / στοναχή). Cassandra sees them and “wailed aloud”, and the whole city goes out to the gates, where Andromache and Hecuba “tore their hair for him” (τιλλέσθην, 24.711), as Hecuba tore hers and Priam his gray hair in Book 22 (22.77–78, 22.406; see [[hair]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). They would have mourned him before the gates until sunset, had not Priam made them give way: “afterwards / you shall have your fill of weeping, when I have brought him home” (24.716–717). κλαυθμός, “weeping”, is found in the Iliad only here. In the truce of Book 7 the same king “did not allow them to wail” as they gathered their dead (7.427).

**The dirge.** The singers are set beside the bed “to lead the dirges”, and they sing “the song of groaning” while the women groan with them (24.720–722). θρῆνος, “dirge”, and θρηνέω, “sing in lament”, are found in the Iliad only here; in the Odyssey the nine Muses sing the dirge for Achilles, answering one another with lovely voices (θρήνεον, *Odyssey* 24.60–61). στονόεις, “groaning”, is otherwise said in the Iliad of missiles, βέλεα στονόεντα, “groaning missiles” (8.159; see [[groaning]]); Walter Leaf suspects that στονόεσσαν hides a lost verb, “who groaned their lay”, and takes the singers for professional mourners whose formal dirge the women answer with their keening (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.720; see the translation's note on 24.720–722).

**The three laments.** Each woman “led the lamentation” (ἦρχε γόοιο, 24.723; ἐξῆρχε γόοιο, 24.747, 24.761), the words used of Thetis among the Nereids and of Achilles over Patroclus (18.51, 18.316, 23.17). Each speaks to the dead, ἆνερ, “Husband”, Ἕκτορ, “Hector” (24.725, 24.748, 24.762), and each speaks of herself: Andromache of her widowhood and her son's slavery or death, Hecuba of the sons whom Achilles sold, Helen of the kindness she will never find again in Troy: “So I weep for you, and for my ill-fated self” (24.773; see [[andromache]], [[hecuba]] and [[helen]]). So the women who wept with Briseis over Patroclus wept “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.302; see [[briseis]]). The answers grow wider: the women groan with Andromache (24.746), Hecuba stirs up “relentless lamentation” (24.760), and with Helen “the countless people groaned” (24.776). Andromache's lament tells the city's grief too, “and so the people mourn him throughout the city” (24.740). The last tears of the poem are those of his brothers and companions as they gather the bones (24.793–794; see [[tears]]).
