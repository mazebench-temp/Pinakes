---
summary: The Trojan Women carries out what Andromache foresees in this book, the women shared out among the Greeks and the child thrown from the walls, but for fear of his father rather than in anger for a kinsman. A line of the Meleager, that every man once dead is earth and shadow, is among Walter Leaf's parallels to Apollo's senseless earth.
---

- The death of Astyanax, thrown from the towers of Troy on Odysseus' advice, “your father's nobility will kill you” (*Trojan Women* 719–725, 740–743, 1188–1191, on 24.732–739): [[euripides-astyanax]].
- The judgment of the goddesses on Ida, the beginning of great woes, in the chorus of the *Andromache* (*Andromache* 274–292, on 24.25–30): [[the-judgment-of-paris]].
- Phaedra's two kinds of αἰδώς, the one not bad and the other a burden on houses, which Walter Leaf compares with the verse on shame athetized at 24.45 (*Hippolytus* 385–386; Leaf on 24.45): [[aidos]] and [[savage-as-a-lion]].
- “Every man once dead is earth and shadow” (*Meleager*, fr. 532 Nauck, on 24.54): [[the-senseless-earth]].
