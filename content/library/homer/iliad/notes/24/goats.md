summary: Apollo reminds the gods that Hector burned for them “thighs of oxen and unblemished goats” (24.33–34), and Priam calls his surviving sons robbers of lambs and kids (24.262).
# ἐρίφων (24.262), "kids", only here in the Iliad.
grc: [ἐρίφων]
---

**Hector's offerings.** “You are hard, you gods, and destructive. Was it not for you / that Hector burned thighs of oxen and unblemished goats?” (24.33–34; see [[apollo]] and [[sacrifice]]). αἰγῶν τελείων, “unblemished goats”, are the victims that Achilles supposed Apollo might want when the plague began, asking whether the god, “receiving the savor of lambs and unblemished goats”, might keep the ruin away (1.66). Apollo's protest echoes the first prayer of the poem, μηρίʼ ἔκηα … αἰγῶν, in which Chryses asked the god to remember “if ever I burned for you the fat thigh-pieces / of bulls and goats” (1.40–41), and the Achaeans appeased him with “perfect hecatombs / of bulls and goats” (1.315–316). Now Apollo makes the same claim for Hector, and Zeus answers that his altar never lacked an equal share of the feast from him (24.66–70; see [[zeus]] and [[cattle]]).

**Kids.** Among Priam's reproaches to his sons is that they are “robbers of lambs and kids from their own people” (24.262; see [[lambs]]). ἔριφοι, “kids”, are named elsewhere in the Iliad only in the simile of the wolves that fall on lambs or kids (ἐρίφοισι, 16.352).
