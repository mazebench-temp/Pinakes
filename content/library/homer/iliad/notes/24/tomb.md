summary: The book's mounds are two. Around the mound of Patroclus Achilles drags Hector every dawn, and Apollo and Hecuba both speak of it; and the poem ends with Hector's own, the golden chest of his bones laid in a trench under close-set stones, the mound heaped over it while lookouts watch for the Achaeans, and the funeral feast that follows (24.14–18, 24.51, 24.416, 24.755, 24.795–801).

**The mound of Patroclus.** Achilles ties Hector behind his chariot and drags him three times “around the mound of the dead son of Menoetius” (σῆμα Μενοιτιάδαο θανόντος, 24.16; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[patroclus]]): the mound heaped around Patroclus' pyre, while his bones wait in the huts for Achilles' own (23.243–257). Apollo complains that he “drags him around the mound of his dear companion” (24.51), Hermes tells Priam that the dragging has not disfigured him (24.416–418; see [[hermes]]), and Hecuba's lament remembers it, “he dragged you many times around the mound of his companion / Patroclus, whom you killed; and even so he did not raise him up” (24.755–756; see [[hecuba]]). σῆμα is “mound” throughout this book, never a sign (see [[teras]] and the translation's row for τύμβος / σῆμα). The other mound of the book is the old tomb of Ilus in the plain, which Priam passes on his way to the ships (24.349; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]).

**Hector's grave.** Priam asks for the eleventh day to “make a tomb over him” (τύμβον, 24.666; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). When the bones have been gathered into a golden chest and covered with purple robes (24.795–796; see [[cremation]]),

{{quote:3cf1e626-5301-563a-9eac-143d484192c4}}

κάπετος, “trench”, is the word for the ditch drawn around the vineyard on the shield (18.564); Walter Leaf compares a grave dug with the hands in Sophocles' *Ajax* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.795). The bones lie in the trench under the stones and the mound, in Hector's own land; Patroclus' mound, Leaf observes, is a cenotaph, his bones being kept to go with those of Achilles (on 23.254). Over Eetion, too, Achilles heaped a mound, and the nymphs planted elms around it (6.418–420). The mound is heaped in haste, with lookouts all around, “lest the well-greaved Achaeans should attack too soon” (24.799–800; see [[spies-and-scouts]]), though Achilles has promised no harm until the twelfth dawn (24.781; see [[truce]]). The half-verse that follows, χεύαντες δὲ τὸ σῆμα πάλιν κίον, “And when they had heaped up the mound they went back”, ended the burial of Patroclus (24.801 = 23.257; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).
