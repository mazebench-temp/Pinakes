---
summary: The anger in the last book is first the gods'. Hera answers Apollo “in anger” (24.55), and Zeus sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry with him, “and that I above all / the immortals am angry” (24.113–114, 24.134–135). Achilles keeps Hector's body out of Priam's sight, “lest in the grief of his heart he should not hold back his anger” (24.584). The anger of the past and of the future is told in stories: Achilles “angry with the son of Atreus” in Hermes' false tale (24.395), Apollo killing Niobe's sons “in his anger with Niobe” (24.606), and the Achaean who will throw Hector's son from the tower “in anger” (24.736).
---

**The gods' anger.** Hera's anger is at Apollo's plea for equal honor for Hector (24.55–63; see [[hera]] and [[time]]). Zeus' message joins two words for anger, σκύζεσθαι and κεχολῶσθαι, both rendered “angry” (24.113–114 = 24.134–135), and gives the anger a purpose, “in the hope that he may fear me and release Hector” (24.116; see [[zeus]], [[nemesis]] and [[apoina]]). Achilles accepts at once (24.139–140).

**Anger held back.** Achilles' anger in the hut is not named, but it is near the surface. When Priam asks for the body at once, Achilles looks at him darkly and warns him not to provoke him, or stir his heart any more (24.559–570; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[algos]]); Walter Leaf explains the outburst by the struggle Achilles is going through, and his sensitiveness to any word that implies hurry or doubt in a thing he has already decided, under compulsion, to do (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.560). He fears Priam's anger too, and has the body washed apart, lest the father, seeing his son, should not hold back his χόλος, and his own heart be stirred to kill him (24.582–586; see [[achos]] and [[etor]]). For χόλον Leaf reports the variants κότον and γόον, prefers χόλον, and thinks κατερύκοι perhaps better than ἐρύσαιτο (on 24.584; see the translation's note on 24.584); he takes 24.586 for an interpolation adapted from 24.570 (on 24.586).

**Anger in stories.** Hermes, as a Myrmidon, recalls the wrath from the Myrmidons' side: while Hector drove the Argives to the ships, Achilles would not let them fight, “being angry with the son of Atreus” (κεχολωμένος, 24.391–395; see [[menis]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). Apollo killed Niobe's sons χωόμενος Νιόβῃ, because she had compared herself with his mother (24.605–607; see [[the-children-of-niobe]] and [[apollo]]). Andromache foresees the anger of revenge: an Achaean whose brother, father or son Hector killed will throw her son from the tower χωόμενος (24.734–738; see [[astyanax]]).
