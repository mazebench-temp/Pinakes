summary: Apollo asks the gods whether Hector never burned thighs of oxen and goats for them (24.33–34); Zeus answers that Hector “never failed in the gifts I love” (24.66–70); Priam says it is good to give the gods their due, since they have remembered his son “even in the portion of death” (24.425–428), and Hecuba says the same in her lament (24.749–750).
refs: [24.33-34, 24.66-70, 24.425-428, 24.749-750]
---

**The gods' debt.** The return of Hector's body is argued as a debt the gods owe him. Apollo opens: “You are hard, you gods, and destructive. Was it not for you / that Hector burned thighs of oxen and unblemished goats?” (24.33–34; see [[apollo]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). Zeus agrees that Hector “was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios; / so he was to me, for he never failed in the gifts I love. / For my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast, / libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us” (24.67–70; see [[zeus]], [[libation]] and [[altar]]); the last two verses are his reason in Book 4 for honoring Troy above all cities (4.44–49).

**The return.** Priam, told that the body is unharmed, draws the lesson: “My child, truly it is good to give the gifts that are their due / to the immortals; for my son … / never forgot in his halls the gods who hold Olympus; / so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death” (24.425–428; see [[priam]] and [[hermes]]). Walter Leaf notes that the compound ἀπεμνήσαντο has the sense of requiting a favor, as in Hesiod's οἵ οἱ ἀπεμνήσαντο χάριν εὐεργεσιάων (*Theogony* 503; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.428). Hecuba's lament ends on the same thought: “truly while you were alive you were dear to the gods; / and they have cared for you even in the portion of death” (24.749–750; see [[hecuba]]).
