**The tomb.** In the Iliad Niobe's children lie nine days in their blood, with no one to bury them, and the gods bury them on the tenth; she remembers food when she is worn out with weeping, and now, a stone on Sipylus, “she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her” (24.610–617; see [[the-children-of-niobe]] and [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]]). Aeschylus' Niobe sits at the tomb. A papyrus preserves part of a speech, damaged at the beginnings of the lines, in which the speaker tells those present that she has sat on this tomb, “brooding over her dead children” ([τέκν]οις ἐπῳάζουσα τοῖς τεθνηκόσιν), that a mortal struck by evil is nothing but a shadow, and that mighty Tantalus will come here for her (PSI 1208); the lexicographer Hesychius quotes the same thought, ἐφημένη / τάφον τέκνοις ἐπῷζε τοῖς τεθνηκόσιν, “seated, she brooded over the tomb for her dead children” (fr. 157 Nauck). The verb is that of a bird sitting on its eggs. Homer's stone broods too, with another verb, πέσσει (24.617). In Aristophanes' *Frogs* Euripides mocks the play for keeping Niobe veiled and silent while the chorus sang on (*Frogs* 911–920; see the entry above and [[aeschylus-phrygians]]).

**Plato.** The papyrus has the two lines that Plato forbids the young to hear, “god plants a cause in mortals when he wishes to ruin a house utterly”, and Plato names their subject, “the sufferings of Niobe”, as the first of the tragic stories in which the poets must either deny that the evils are the god's work or find a reason for them (*Republic* 2.380a). They come soon after his rejection of the jars of Zeus, from Achilles' speech in this book (24.527–533; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]). A little after the draggings of Hector, which Plato also takes from this book, he quotes the play again, of Niobe's kin, “close to the gods, near to Zeus, whose altar of ancestral Zeus stands high in the air on the hill of Ida”, and in whom “the blood of divinities has not yet faded” (*Republic* 3.391e; see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Plato does not name the play there; Strabo quotes the same lines from Aeschylus' *Niobe*, and charges him with confusing Ida and Sipylus (*Geography* 12.8.21; see [[sipylus]]).
