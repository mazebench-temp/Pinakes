summary: The ψυχή is twice the life that has been taken. Priam's daughters and daughters-in-law mourn the brave men who “lay dead at the hands of the Argives, having lost their lives” (24.168), and Hecuba says that Achilles dragged Hector's body around the mound “when he had taken your life with the long-edged bronze” (24.754–755).
en: [lives]
---

The first verse is used in Book 13 of the Trojan leaders whom Hector finds dead at the sterns of the Achaean ships (13.763 = 24.168); the second has no parallel. Both set the life apart from the body that remains, and the book is about the body. Hecuba's lament goes on to it: the life is gone, but the body lies “fresh as dew and as if newly slain” in her halls (24.754–759; see [[hecuba]], [[fresh-as-dew]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). When Hector died, his ψυχή flew from his limbs and went to Hades, “lamenting its fate” (22.362–363; see [[the-death-of-hector]]).

**The corpse and the man.** Zeus speaks of the gods' quarrel “about Hector's corpse” (Ἕκτορος ἀμφὶ νέκυι, 24.108). Walter Leaf notes that νέκυς with the genitive of the dead man's name is found only here in Homer, unless at 17.240; elsewhere νέκυς stands in apposition with the name, “for in the old Epic psychology the corpse is not a part or appendage of the man, but the man himself”, as in the proem, where the heroes themselves are made the spoil of dogs while their souls go to Hades (1.3–4), and he sees in the construction a sign of developed thought (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.108).

**The dead who hear.** In Book 23, when the soul of Patroclus slipped from his arms, Achilles cried out that in the house of Hades there is “soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104). Yet when he gives up Hector's body he asks Patroclus not to be angry “if you learn, / even in the house of Hades” (24.592–593), and promises him a share of the ransom (see [[apoina]], [[patroclus]] and [[hades]]).
