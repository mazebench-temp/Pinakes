**Not seers, but the goddess.** Hecuba cries out against Priam's going, and he answers: “Do not hold me back when I wish to go, and do not yourself / be a bird of evil omen in the halls; you will not persuade me” (24.218–219; see [[hecuba]] and [[birds]]). ὄρνις here is the omen itself, as when Hector says that “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.219; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). Then he names the men who read the gods' will for others:

{{quote:a3af4add-d9f3-532e-8e78-7e61f698c961}}

Leaf takes θυοσκόοι with μάντιες, “seers who read the smoke of the sacrifice”, not with the priests, since the order of the words requires it; the word may point to divination from the smoke of incense, but the poems do not say, and the verse sets the two kinds of religious adviser side by side, those who counsel from signs and those who serve as priests (on 24.221; see [[priesthood]]). Achilles named three such experts in Book 1, “some seer, or a priest, / or even an interpreter of dreams” (1.62–63). The next verse is Nestor's on Agamemnon's dream, “we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more” (2.81): there the dream was believed because the king had seen it, and it was false; here the word is believed because Priam has seen the goddess, and it is true. Leaf, with Aristarchus, thinks the verse borrowed in Book 2 from this place (on 24.222; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[iris]]).

**The omen on the right.** Before he goes, Hecuba bids him ask Zeus for a sign: a bird of omen, the one dearest to Zeus and strongest, “on the right, so that you may see it yourself with your eyes / and, trusting in it, go to the ships” (24.292–295); and if Zeus will not send it, he should not go (24.296–298). Priam prays for it in the same words, and Zeus sends an eagle, which appears darting on the right across the city (24.310–320; see [[prayer]], [[eagle]] and [[surest-of-birds]]). The right is the lucky side, as Hector's scorn of birds in Book 12 assumes, “whether they go to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, / or to the left” (12.239–240; Leaf on 24.294 refers to his note there). The omen is read at once by everyone who sees it: “they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (24.321). No seer is needed when the omen has been asked for.

**Of good omen.** On the plain Priam meets Hermes in the likeness of a young man and, not knowing him, says that some god has sent him “such a wayfarer to meet me, / one of good omen” (24.375–376; see [[hermes]]). αἴσιον is found only here; Leaf allows either “of good omen”, as the text renders it, or “a man of moderation”, nearer the usual sense of αἶσα (on 24.376; see the translation's note on 24.376).

**Cassandra.** At dawn Cassandra is the first to see her father coming home (24.699–706; see [[cassandra]]). Leaf reports the scholia's remark that nothing here shows the gift of prophecy that the later poems gave her, and adds that nothing here is inconsistent with it (on 24.699; see the translation's note on 24.699).
