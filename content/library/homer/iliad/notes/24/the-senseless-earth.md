**Apollo's protest.** On the twelfth dawn after Hector's death Apollo speaks among the gods (24.31–54; see [[apollo]]). Hector burned the thighs of oxen and goats for them, and they will not save even his corpse for his wife and mother, his child, his father and his people to burn; they help Achilles, who has no mind that can be bent, who “knows savage ways, like a lion” and has lost all pity and shame (24.33–45). A man may lose someone dearer, a brother or a son, but when he has wept and mourned he leaves off, “for the Fates have put an enduring heart in men” (24.46–49); Achilles drags Hector around the mound of his companion, and that is not more honorable for him, nor better. The last two lines are the warning and its reason: “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.53–54; see [[nemesis]]).

**The verse.** Apollo's line takes up the narrator's. The account of the dragging ended, “So in his rage he defiled brilliant Hector” (ὣς ὃ μὲν Ἕκτορα δῖον ἀείκιζεν μενεαίνων, 24.22), and Apollo uses the same verb and the same participle, with “the senseless earth” in the place of the man (see [[aeikizo]]). Apollo himself had kept “all defilement” (ἀεικείην) from the body (24.19). κωφός is said in the Iliad of a weapon, “for blunt is the shaft of a weakling, a nobody” (11.390), and of the sea, which “heaves with a soundless swell” before the wind comes (14.16); here the dead body is earth that cannot feel. Walter Leaf renders it “the senseless clay”, refers to Menelaus' curse on the Achaeans who will not fight, “May you all turn to water and earth” (7.99; see [[water-and-earth]]), and rejects the reading that Achilles outrages the earth by dragging the body over it, “such a thought is surely not Epic” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.54). Among his parallels is a line of Euripides, “every man once dead is earth and shadow” (κατθανὼν δὲ πᾶς ἀνὴρ / γῆ καὶ σκιά, *Meleager*, fr. 532 Nauck).

**Aristotle and the poem.** Aristotle quotes the line in his account of calming anger, as the poet's way of stopping Achilles' anger against the dead man (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30; see [[praotes]]). In the poem no one says it to Achilles. Apollo says it to the gods, and the anger ends another way: Zeus sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry with him, and he agrees to give up the body for a ransom (24.103–140; see [[thetis]] and [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). The thought that the dead feel nothing returned in Aeschylus' *Phrygians*, joined to the warning of Nemesis, and in Cicero, without it (see [[aeschylus-phrygians]] and [[cicero-the-body-of-hector]]).
