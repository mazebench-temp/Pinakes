---
summary: The horses of Book 24 draw Achilles' chariot when he drags Hector around the mound at every dawn, and Priam's chariot behind the mule wagon, to the hut of Achilles and home again. Hermes takes their reins and breathes might into them, and the last word of the poem is Hector's epithet, “tamer of horses” (24.14–18, 24.51, 24.279–286, 24.322–326, 24.440–442, 24.690–697, 24.804).
---

**The dragging.** “But whenever he had yoked his swift horses under the chariot, / he would tie Hector behind the chariot to be dragged” (24.14–15). The verbs are iterative, and the dragging is repeated day after day (see the translation's note on 24.12). Achilles drags the body three times around the mound of Patroclus (24.16), as the Myrmidons drove their horses three times around his body before the funeral: “And three times they drove their well-maned horses around the body” (23.13). Apollo, protesting to the gods, says that he “ties him to his horses and drags him around the mound of his dear companion” (24.51). The team is the immortal pair that dragged Hector from the walls to the ships, Xanthus and Balius (22.399–400; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**Priam's horses.** For the journey the mules are yoked to the wagon, and then the horses to Priam's own chariot: “And for Priam they led under the yoke the horses that the old man / kept for himself and reared at the polished manger” (24.279–280). The words are those said of Anchises, who stole a strain of the horses that Zeus gave to Tros and kept four of the foals for himself: “Four of them he kept himself and reared at the manger” (5.271; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]). Walter Leaf reports that one manuscript reads Τρωούς, “the horses of Tros”, for Πριάμῳ, alluding to the famous breed that belonged to the kings of Troy, and thinks it not a bad reading: it avoids repeating Priam's name and gives point to αὐτὸς ἔχων, “keeping for his own use” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.279). ζευγνύσθην, “had the horses yoked”, is the middle of the master who gives the order, as the scholia explain (Leaf on 24.281). Hecuba stands before the horses to give Priam the cup for the libation (24.286; see [[libation]]). Then the old man mounts and drives out, the mules in front with the wagon, and behind them “came the horses, which the old man drove, urging them on with the whip, / swiftly through the city”, while all his kin followed him, lamenting as if he were going to his death (24.322–328; see [[mules]] and [[wagon]]).

**The night journey.** Past the mound of Ilus they halt the mules and the horses to drink at the river, at the ford, as darkness falls (24.349–351; see [[ilus]] and [[scamander]]). Idaeus, seeing a man coming, proposes to flee “on the horses”, on the chariot, leaving the mule wagon to its fate (24.356; Leaf on 24.356). Hermes asks where Priam is guiding his horses and mules through the night (24.362), and then takes charge of them himself: “He spoke, and the great helper, springing onto the chariot behind the horses, / quickly took the whip and the reins in his hands, / and breathed noble might into the horses and the mules” (24.440–442; see [[hermes]]). The words are those with which Zeus breathed noble might into the horses of Achilles as they stood weeping for Patroclus (17.456). At the hut Hermes steps down from the chariot (24.459), Priam leaps to the ground (24.469), and Idaeus stays outside, holding the horses and the mules (24.470–471; see [[idaeus]]), until Automedon and Alcimus loose them from the yoke (24.576).

**The return.** Before dawn Hermes yokes the horses and the mules again and drives them through the camp, and no one knows (24.690–691). He leaves at the ford of the Xanthus, and “they drove toward the city with cries of woe and groaning / the horses, and the mules carried the corpse” (24.696–697). Cassandra sees her father from Pergamus, “standing in the chariot” (24.701; see [[cassandra]]), and he speaks to the crowd at the gates from the chariot (24.715).

**Words of horsemanship.** Troilus, one of the dead sons Priam names, was ἱππιοχάρμης, “who fought from his chariot” (24.257; see [[troilus]]); in Hecuba's counsel and Priam's prayer the ships he goes to are those of the Δαναῶν ταχυπώλων, “the Danaans of the swift horses” (24.295, 24.313; see [[of-the-swift-horses]]); the ἱπποκορυσταί, “the men who fight from chariots”, sleep all night while Hermes lies awake (24.677). The poem ends with Hector's epithet: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804; see [[tamer-of-horses]]).
