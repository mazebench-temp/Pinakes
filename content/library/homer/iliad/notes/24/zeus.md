summary: Zeus ends the gods' quarrel over Hector's body. He will not have it stolen, but summons Thetis to tell Achilles that the gods are angry with him, and sends Iris to bid Priam ransom his son, with Hermes as his escort. When Priam prays to him as the god of Ida for a sign, he sends an eagle; seeing the old man on the plain, he pities him and sends Hermes to bring him unseen to Achilles. Achilles releases the body at his command, and tells Priam of the two jars on the floor of Zeus.

**The ruling.** For nine days the gods have quarreled over Hector's body, and on the twelfth dawn Apollo protests and Hera answers him in anger (24.31–63; see [[the-gods-debate]], [[apollo]] and [[hera]]). Zeus the cloud-gatherer answers her mildly (24.64; see [[cloud-gatherer]]):

{{quote:64271ca1-b2b4-5e04-a539-e20d2b1b5234}}

He grants her that the honor of the two men cannot be the same, and sets Hector's piety beside it. The last two verses are those with which, in Book 4, he gave up to Hera the city he honored most (4.48–49; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]); there they surrendered Troy, here they save the body of its defender. ἡμάρτανε δώρων, “failed in the gifts”, is a phrase more natural of the receiver: Hector never failed to render them (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.68). ἀποσκυδμαίνω, “be wholly angry”, occurs only here in Homer, and its simple verb only when Achilles asks Patroclus not to “be angry” with him (24.592; see the translation's key-word row σκύζομαι / σκυδμαίνω, ἀποσκυδμαίνω). Then Zeus refuses the theft and calls for Thetis, “so that I may tell her a shrewd word, how Achilles / may receive gifts from Priam and release Hector” (24.75–76; see [[shrewd-word]] and [[apoina]]).

**Zeus and Thetis.** Iris finds Thetis in her cave: “Rise, Thetis; Zeus calls you, whose counsels are imperishable” (24.88). ἄφθιτα μήδεα εἰδώς occurs nowhere else in Homer; Leaf finds it three times in Hesiod and in the *Hymn to Aphrodite*, and compares the *Hymn to Demeter*, where Iris calls Demeter in the same way, “Demeter, father Zeus, who knows imperishable things, calls you” (on 24.88; *Theogony* 545, 550, 561; *Hymn to Aphrodite* 43; *Hymn to Demeter* 321; see [[imperishable]]). On Olympus she finds “the far-seeing son of Cronus” among all the gods and sits down beside father Zeus (24.98–102; see [[far-seeing]] and [[olympians]]), and “the father of men and gods” speaks first of her grief (24.103; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]]), “I know it myself” (24.105), then of the gods:

{{quote:3f3b7e0e-d255-5162-a978-929bda0fdf79}}

The glory he gives Achilles is the ransom: Leaf takes κῦδος here as the receipt of gifts in return for the body, instead of yielding it unransomed (on 24.110; see [[kydos]]). Thetis is to tell her son that the gods are angry, and Zeus above all, “in the hope that he may fear me and release Hector” (24.116), a phrase of studied courtesy in Zeus' mouth, as Leaf puts it (on 24.116). He will send Iris to Priam himself (24.117–119). Thetis repeats the anger and leaves out the plan for Priam, so that Achilles knows only the end Zeus aims at and not the means, and Priam's coming will surprise him (Leaf on 24.181; see [[thetis]]). Achilles consents, “if the Olympian himself so bids with a ready heart” (24.140; see [[olympian]] and [[achilles]]).

**The message to Priam.** Zeus gives Iris the whole message: Priam is to go alone, with one older herald to drive the wagon, and to have no fear, “for such an escort will we grant him, the slayer of Argus” (24.153); Achilles “is not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender, / but with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (24.157–158; see [[iris]], [[hermes]] and [[supplication]]). Leaf reports the scholia's remark that the three adjectives cover the three causes of cruelty, stupidity, thoughtlessness and malice (on 24.157). Iris introduces herself to Priam as “a messenger to you from Zeus, / who, though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you” (24.173–174): the words of the Dream that Zeus sent to deceive Agamemnon (2.26–27; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[messengers-of-zeus]]). In Book 2 they carried a lie in Zeus' name; here they are true. Priam tells Hecuba that “an Olympian messenger has come to me from Zeus” (24.194), Διόθεν, as Achilles will tell Priam that “a messenger came to me from Zeus, / my mother” (24.561–562). Ὀλύμπιος at 24.194 is Iris' epithet, “an Olympian messenger”; it names Zeus at 24.140 and 24.175 (see the translation's note on 24.194).

**Zeus of Ida.** Hecuba bids Priam “pray then to the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds, / the god of Ida, who looks down on all of Troy” (24.290–291; see [[hecuba]], [[lord-of-dark-clouds]] and [[son-of-cronus]]). Ἰδαῖος, “of Ida”, is his title once more in the Iliad, when Meriones kills the son of Onetor, “the priest of Zeus / of Ida” (16.604–605; see [[onetor]] and [[ida]]); Zeus has a precinct and a fragrant altar on Gargarus (8.47–48; see [[gargarus]]), and Hector burned thighs of oxen to him “on the peaks of many-folded Ida” (22.170–171). Priam calls on him with the verse of the oath in Book 3, “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest” (24.308 = 3.276; see [[who-rules-from-ida]]), and asks for a bird of omen; “Zeus the counselor heard him” (24.314 = 16.249; see [[counselor]]), and sent an eagle (24.315 = 8.247; see [[prayer]], [[divination]], [[eagle]] and [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]).

**Pity.** “Seeing the old man he pitied him” (24.332): ἰδὼν ἐλέησε is the verb of Zeus' pity for Hector (15.12), for Sarpedon and Patroclus (16.431), for the weeping horses of Achilles (17.441), and for Achilles and the elders as they wept for Patroclus and for what each had left at home (19.338–340; see [[pity]]). He sends Hermes, “his dear son” (24.333, nearly *Odyssey* 5.28, where Zeus sends Hermes to Calypso; Leaf on 24.333): “Hermes, since to you above all it is dearest / to keep company with a man, and you listen to whom you will” (24.334–335). Leaf hears in the words a picture of Hermes as an especially affable god (on 24.335). He is to bring Priam to Achilles unseen by any of the Danaans (24.336–338), and when he has done so he tells Priam, “my father granted me to you as an escort” (24.461).

**The jars of Zeus.** Achilles explains Priam's sorrows by the gifts of Zeus: “For two jars stand on the floor of Zeus / of the gifts that he gives, one of evils, and the other of blessings” (24.527–528). To some men Zeus “who delights in thunder” gives a mixture, to others only evils (24.529–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[delighting-in-thunder]]). In the same speech “the heavenly gods” brought this bane on Priam, and the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals, to live in grief, while they themselves are free from care (24.525–526, 24.547; see [[olympians]]).

**The commands of Zeus.** Achilles' warning to Priam ends with Zeus: “lest I do not spare even you, old man, in my huts, / suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus” (24.569–570). Leaf takes Διὸς ἐφετμάς in a general sense, the laws of Zeus as the protector of suppliants, Ζεὺς ἱκετήσιος of the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 13.213; Leaf on 24.570). The narrator gives Achilles the same fear when the slave women wash the body out of Priam's sight (24.586), a verse that Leaf takes for an interpolation adapted from 24.570 (on 24.586; see the translation's note on 24.586).

**Other mentions.** Priam asks the Trojans whether they make light of it “that Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me sorrows” (24.241). Achilles is “dear to Zeus” (Διῒ φίλος, 24.472; see [[dear-to-zeus]]), and Achilles and Priam are each “nurtured by Zeus” (24.553, 24.635, 24.803; see [[zeus-nurtured]]). In the story of Niobe “the son of Cronus had turned the people to stones” (24.611; see [[the-children-of-niobe]]), and Hermes leaves Priam at the ford of “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (24.693 = 14.434 = 21.2; see [[scamander]]).
