**The third instance.** All the gods but three would have Hermes steal Hector's body away. Hera, Poseidon and Athena refuse, “but they held to it, as when sacred Ilios first became hateful to them, / and Priam, and the people, for Alexander's blind folly” (Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἄτης, 24.27–28; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). The next two lines say what the folly was: he “insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard, / and praised her who gave him his grievous lust” (24.29–30). The translation renders ἄτη “blind folly” here, as in Helen's words to Hector, “for Alexander's blind folly” (6.356), while at 3.100 it follows the other reading, “because Alexander began it” (see [[ate]]).

**The other reading.** Leaf notes that ἄτης here is a late form, standing for an older ἀϝάτης, and adds: “We should be justified in adopting the variant ἀρχῆς, were we sure of the antiquity of the line” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.28). He was not sure of it. Aristarchus athetized 24.25–30, holding that Homer did not know the judgment of the goddesses, since he would have mentioned it often, that νείκεσσε means to rebuke and not to judge, and that μαχλοσύνη, “lust”, is a word first used by Hesiod; Leaf finds the case decisive against the last two lines and weaker against the rest (on 24.23; see the translation's notes on 24.23–30 and 24.29). With ἀρχῆς the line would say, as Menelaus says, that Alexander began the wrong; with ἄτης it says, as Helen does, that he was blinded, and the lines that follow name the goddess who gave him his lust.
