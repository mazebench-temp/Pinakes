summary: Zeus allows Priam a single companion for his journey, a herald, “an older man”, to drive the mules and bring back the body; the herald, Idaeus, goes with him to Achilles' hut and home again, “the old man's crier” and “crier of the city” (24.149–151, 24.282, 24.325, 24.352, 24.577, 24.674, 24.689, 24.701).
# κήρυκα καλήτορα (24.577) and ἀστυβοώτην (24.701) only here; καλήτορα alone would catch Caletor (Καλήτορα, 15.419).
grc: ["κήρυκα καλήτορα", ἀστυβοώτην]
en: [crier, crier of the city]
---

**An older man.** “Let a herald go with him, an older man, to guide / the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back / the body to the city” (24.149–151 = 24.178–180; see [[idaeus]]). γεραίτερος, “older”: Walter Leaf takes the optatives as concessive, “I permit a herald to go with him to drive” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.149). It is the only companion Zeus allows, since no other man of the Trojans is to go (24.148). Heralds were under the protection of Zeus, and Talthybius and Eurybates went unharmed to Achilles' hut in Book 1 to take Briseis (1.320–348); here the herald drives the wagon for his king. Hermes counts him, with Priam, too old to ward off a man (24.368–369).

**Crier.** κήρυκα καλήτορα τοῖο γέροντος, “the herald, the old man's crier” (24.577), and κήρυκά τε ἀστυβοώτην, “and the herald, crier of the city” (24.701): καλήτωρ, “crier”, from καλέω, “call”, is also the name of two men (see [[caletor]] and [[caletor-father-of-aphareus]]), and ἀστυβοώτης is found only here, for ἀστυβοήτης, Leaf notes, with a misapplied epic lengthening; he takes it as the same title as καλήτωρ (on 24.701). In the camp the herald's voice is the one that summons the people and calls for silence (2.50–52, 2.96–97); in Troy he is the city's crier.
