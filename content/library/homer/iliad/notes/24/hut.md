summary: Achilles' hut is the scene of the book's center. Thetis finds him groaning there while his companions make ready their meal; Priam comes in unseen and clasps his knees where he sits beside his table; the body is washed in another room, out of Priam's sight; and the two men eat and sleep under one roof, Priam and the herald in the porch, Achilles in the inner part with Briseis (24.17, 24.122–125, 24.471–676). Only here is the hut drawn as a full house, with a courtyard, a porch, a colonnade and a hall.

**Achilles' quarters.** After each dragging Achilles “would rest again in his hut”, and leave Hector stretched face down in the dust (24.17; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). There his mother finds him “groaning without ceasing”, while his companions make ready their morning meal, “and a great shaggy ram was being sacrificed for them in the hut” (24.122–125; see [[thetis]] and [[sheep]]). The body lies there too, ἐν κλισίῃσι, “in the huts” (24.413, 24.554): one man's quarters are “huts” in the plural, and Achilles calls them “my huts” when he warns Priam (24.569; see the translation's row for κλισίη, κλισίαι).

**The courtyard and the bar.** The outside of the house is drawn as Hermes and Priam reach it:

{{quote:d69e6f23-2c47-57c9-b87e-f1438fc45d8b}}

The Myrmidons built it high, of fir beams, and roofed it with “a shaggy thatch” mown in the meadow (24.449–451; see [[myrmidons]] and [[anax]]). ὄροφος, Leaf holds, means simply “roofing”, though the ancients explained it as a kind of reed (on 24.451; see the translation's row for λαχνήεις ὄροφος). The great courtyard is fenced with close-set stakes (24.452–453; see [[house]]). Its door is held by a single bar of fir, ἐπιβλής, which Leaf takes for a long beam thrust from one door-post across the door into the other, and which the next verses call the great bolt of the doors, κληΐς (on 24.453 and 24.455). Three Achaeans drove it home and three drew it back, “but Achilles would drive it home even alone” (24.456). The verse ends like the verses of the heroes' stones, καὶ οἶος, “even alone”: what two men of the poet's own day could not lift, Diomedes, Hector and Aeneas wielded alone (5.302–304, 12.445–449, 20.285–287; see [[men-of-today]]). The god opens it for the old man (24.457; see [[hermes]]), and Achilles later tells Priam that no mortal could “easily push back the bar of our doors” (24.567; see [[night-watch]]). Leaf takes those words as an allusion to this description, and so as an answer to Heyne, who rejected 24.449–456 chiefly for the neglected digamma of ἄνακτι in 24.449 and 24.452 (on 24.448 and 24.449; see the translation's note on 24.448–456).

**The house where he sat.** Zeus foretells that Hermes will lead Priam “into the hut of Achilles” (24.155 = 24.184), and Priam asks the stranger to escort him “to the hut of the son of Peleus” (24.431; see [[hermes]]). The hut that the Myrmidons built for their lord, roofed with thatch and closed by a bar that three men worked, is described when they reach it (24.448–456; see [[myrmidons]]). Then the old man “went straight to the house, / where Achilles, dear to Zeus, used to sit” (24.471–472). The companions sit apart in the same room, and only Automedon and Alcimus are busy beside him; he has just finished eating and drinking, “and the table still stood beside him” (24.473–476; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]] and [[table]]). The line was athetized in antiquity on the ground that the tables were not cleared after a Homeric meal; Athenaeus, Walter Leaf reports, met the objection by putting the stop after ἔτι, “still” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.476; see the translation's note on 24.476). Priam comes in unseen by them (24.477; see [[supplication]]), and when the two men weep, “their groaning rose through the house” (24.512).

**The other wall.** Achilles springs “out of the door of the house like a lion” to take the ransom (οἴκοιο … θύραζε, 24.572), and has the body washed and anointed νόσφιν ἀειράσας, “taking him aside”: not into the hall, Leaf explains, but into another room, so that Priam should not see his son (24.582–586; Leaf on 24.583; see [[hector]]). Then he goes back into the hut and sits down “on the intricately worked chair from which he had risen, / by the other wall” (24.596–598; see [[throne]]). τοίχου τοῦ ἑτέρου is the place where he sat on the night of the embassy, facing Odysseus (9.218–219; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**The beds.** When Priam asks to be put to bed, Achilles bids his companions and the slave women set beds beneath the colonnade, throw purple blankets on them, spread rugs and put woolly cloaks on top, and the women go out from the hall with torches and spread two beds (24.643–648; see [[bed]] and [[handmaids]]). The first verse varies the one in which Patroclus had a bed spread for Phoenix in the same hut (24.643 ≈ 9.658); the other four are said in the Odyssey of the beds made for guests in the palaces of Menelaus and of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 4.297–300, 7.336–339; see [[xenia]]). Achilles puts his guest outside, “lest one of the Achaeans / come in here, a counselor”, one of those who always sit beside him and take counsel, as is the custom, and tell Agamemnon (24.650–655; see [[boule]]). Then the house sleeps:

{{quote:257706be-1b2f-573c-b5ef-1c482c15a613}}

The night of the embassy ended with the same verse, when Achilles “slept in the inner part of the well-built hut” beside Diomede, and Patroclus on the other side beside Iphis (24.675 = 9.663; 9.664–668; see [[diomede]] and [[briseis]]). In Menelaus' palace in Sparta Telemachus and Nestor's son sleep in the porch, and Menelaus in the inner part of the high house, with Helen beside him (*Odyssey* 4.302–305).

**A house in the camp.** Walter Leaf observes that the book draws Achilles' hut as a full counterpart of the Homeric house, with a forecourt, a porch (πρόδομος, 24.673), a colonnade (αἴθουσα, 24.644) and a hall (μέγαρον, 24.647), and calls the whole a house (οἶκος, 24.572; δώματα, 24.512). Elsewhere in the Iliad, even in Book 9, which passes in the same hut, there is hardly a sign of a building on this scale: Phoenix's bed is made with no word of a colonnade (9.658–659), though the inner part, μυχός, is common to both. The poet, Leaf concludes, knew the palace better than the camp (on 24.448; see the translation's note on 24.448–456). Of the verses of the beds he judges that they suit the palaces of Menelaus and Alcinous better than a soldier's hut, and that the Iliad is more likely the borrower (on 24.643; see the translation's note on 24.643–647).
