---
summary: Apollo complains that Achilles “has no fitting mind” (24.40), and Zeus' message is that he keeps Hector “in his raging heart” (24.114, 24.135). Hecuba asks Priam, “where has your mind gone” (24.201); Priam and his herald set out, and later sleep in Achilles' porch, “with shrewd counsels in their minds” (24.282, 24.674); and Achilles tells Priam, “I know you too, Priam, in my heart” (24.563).
---

**The mind that cannot be bent.** Apollo's charge is that Achilles has no φρένες ἐναίσιμοι, “no fitting mind, nor any thought / in his breast that can be bent” (24.40–41; see [[aisa]] and [[noos]]). Walter Leaf compares Phoenix's honor “that bends the minds of other men” and Iris' words to Poseidon, “The hearts of good men can be turned” (9.514, 15.203; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.41). Zeus calls Achilles' mind raging, φρεσὶ μαινομένῃσιν, since he keeps the body by the ships (24.114 = 24.135; see [[cholos]]). When Priam is in his hut, the same mind sees what has happened: Achilles tells Priam that he knows φρεσίν, “in my heart”, that a god led him to the ships, since no mortal could have passed the sentries or moved the bar of the gate (24.563–567; see [[night-watch]]).

**Priam's mind.** Iris bids him “Take heart in your mind” (24.171), as one who comes “with good intent” (ἀγαθὰ φρονέουσα, 24.173), and Zeus' message is that he should have no thought of death in his heart (24.152, 24.181; see [[thanatos]]). Priam asks Hecuba what her mind thinks of it (24.197), and she answers by asking where his mind has gone, the mind for which he was famous among strangers and his own people (24.201–202; see [[xenia]] and [[hecuba]]). The narrator says otherwise: πυκινὰ φρεσὶ μήδεʼ ἔχοντες, “with shrewd counsels in their minds”, is said of the herald and Priam when they yoke the horses in the palace and again when they lie down in Achilles' porch (24.282, 24.674); the half-line recurs in Homer only of the old nurse whom Penelope trusts (*Odyssey* 19.353; see [[idaeus]] and [[priam]]).
