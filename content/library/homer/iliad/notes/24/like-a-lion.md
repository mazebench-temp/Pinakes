Πηλεΐδης δʼ οἴκοιο λέων ὣς ἆλτο θύραζε, “And the son of Peleus sprang out of the door of the house like a lion” (24.572). The verse opens as the verse of Book 20 opens, Πηλεΐδης δʼ ἑτέρωθεν ἐναντίον ὦρτο λέων ὣς, “And the son of Peleus rushed against him from the other side like a lion” (20.164), where the comparison grew into the simile of the wounded lion that a whole people has gathered to kill (20.164–175; see [[wounded-lion]]). Here it stops at the half-verse. ἆλτο, “leapt”, is the verb of warriors who leap down from their chariots to fight on foot, “And at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor” (3.29), and of Priam earlier in the book, who “leapt from the chariot to the ground” at the door of the same hut (24.469); with a comparison before it, it is the verb of Thetis, who “like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus” with her son's new armor (18.616; see [[like-a-hawk]]). οἶκος, “house”, is said of the hut, which this book describes as a full house with a courtyard, a porch and a colonnade (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.572 and 24.448; see [[hut]]).

The half-verse comes directly after Achilles' warning. He has told Priam not to provoke him any longer, “lest I do not spare even you, old man, in my huts, / suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus”, and “the old man was afraid, and obeyed his word” (24.569–571; see [[priam]] and [[achilles]]). Apollo named the lion at the beginning of the book, the beast that gives way to its strength and goes among the flocks of men (24.41–43; see [[savage-as-a-lion]]). This one goes out, with Automedon and Alcimus, to unyoke the horses and the mules, bring in the herald and take the ransom from the wagon; then Achilles has the body washed, anointed and clothed out of Priam's sight, lifts it onto a bier with his own hands, and with his companions sets it on the wagon (24.573–590; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]).
