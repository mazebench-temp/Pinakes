summary: Two old men are set side by side, Priam, who has come to the killer of his sons, and Peleus, the killer's father. Priam begins, “Remember your father, Achilles, like the gods, / who is of my age, on the deadly threshold of old age” (24.486–487), and the memory makes Achilles weep for Peleus; he raises Priam, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.516), and answers with Peleus, whom he does not care for “as he grows old” (24.541), and with Priam's own past: “And you too, old man, we hear, were prosperous once” (24.543).

**Remember your father.** Priam's plea opens and closes with memory, μνῆσαι πατρὸς σοῖο, “Remember your father”, and μνησάμενος σοῦ πατρός, “remembering your own father” (24.486, 24.504):

{{quote:c24b9e02-fa75-566f-bab7-122926bc8c2d}}

The old man far away is Priam's likeness, “of my age”, and Priam had spoken of himself on the same threshold of old age (22.60; see [[the-evening-of-life]] and [[peleus]]). Walter Leaf notes that in the Odyssey the soul of Achilles has the same fear for his father, that the Myrmidons may dishonor Peleus because old age holds his hands and feet (*Odyssey* 11.495–503; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.488). The memory works: Achilles weeps “for his own father, and now again / for Patroclus”, while Priam weeps for Hector, “and the two remembered” (24.509–512; see [[himeros]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Peleus and Priam.** Achilles' answer sets the two old men side by side. Peleus had every gift of the gods, prosperity, wealth, rule and a goddess for a wife, but one son, doomed to an early death, who does not care for him as he grows old (24.534–542; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[threptra]]). Priam's prosperity is a thing Achilles knows by hearsay, ἀκούομεν, “we hear”, which Leaf takes as “know by report” (on 24.543): once he excelled in wealth and sons over all the lands between Lesbos, Phrygia and the Hellespont, and now there are always battles around his city (24.543–548; see [[priam]] and [[lesbos]]). The old man's past is told back to him by the young man who has killed his sons.

**The beauty of the old.** After the meal the two look at each other in wonder, Priam at Achilles, “how tall he was and how fair”, Achilles at Priam, “seeing his noble looks and hearing his words” (24.629–632; see [[thambos]]). Leaf reports Helbig's remark that here, in one of the earliest and most striking expressions of the Greek admiration for physical beauty, the beauty of the old man is recognized as vividly as that of the young (on 24.630). In Book 22 Priam had said that all is fair in a young man dead in battle, but that an old man's gray head disfigured is the most pitiable sight (22.71–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[pity]]).
