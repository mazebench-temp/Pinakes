summary: The poem ends with a burning. Apollo complains that the gods have kept Hector from the people who would burn him and give him funeral rites; Achilles asks how many days the rites will need and grants them; and when the body is home and mourned, the Trojans gather wood for nine days, burn him on the tenth, and on the eleventh quench the pyre with wine, gather the bones into a golden chest and heap his mound (24.37–38, 24.656–667, 24.777–804).

**Rites refused and granted.** Apollo's protest ends with the people “who would quickly / burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites” (24.37–38; see [[apollo]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]), the portion of fire that the dying Hector begged for and Achilles refused (22.342–354; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). In the hut Achilles himself asks the question: “for how many days are you minded to give brilliant Hector his funeral rites” (24.657), and Priam answers, “If you are willing that I should hold a funeral for brilliant Hector” (τελέσαι τάφον, 24.660), with nine days of lament, the burial and the feast on the tenth, the tomb on the eleventh (24.664–666; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]] and [[truce]]). τάφος in Homer is always the funeral, never the tomb, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.29). The first of the rites is done in Achilles' hut: the slave women wash the body and anoint it with oil, and Achilles lays it on a bier wrapped in a mantle and a tunic left from the ransom (24.580–590; see [[handmaids]] and [[bed]]), as Patroclus' body was washed, anointed and covered with linen (18.343–353).

**The order of the rite.** The burial follows the order of Patroclus' (see the note in Book 23): the lament over the body (24.719–776; see [[the-laments-for-hector]] and [[lament]]), the wood (24.777–784), the burning, “and on the top of the pyre they laid the body, and set fire to it” (24.787 ≈ 23.165; see [[pyres]]), the quenching with wine at the next dawn, the gathering of the bones, and the mound (24.788–801; see [[tomb]]); and a feast, held after the burial in Priam's house, as Achilles gave the Myrmidons theirs before the burning (24.802–803; 23.29; see [[feast]]). Leaf calls the scene almost an abstract of the funeral of Patroclus (on 24.791). What it leaves out is as plain: there are no victims, no hair cut for the dead, no prayer to the winds, and no libation poured at the pyre (23.135–153, 23.166–177, 23.192–225).

**Wine and bones.** “first they put out the pyre with sparkling wine, / all of it, as far as the might of the fire had reached” (24.791–792), the verses in which Achilles ordered the quenching of Patroclus' pyre (23.237–238); Leaf, on that verse, says the practice was common in Rome as in Greece (on 23.237; see [[wine]]). “his brothers and his companions gathered the white bones, / weeping” (24.793–794; see [[sons-of-priam]] and [[white]]), and laid them in a golden chest, λάρναξ, which answers the golden bowl, φιάλη, of Patroclus (24.795; 23.253; Leaf on 24.795; see [[the-golden-urn]]), “covering them with soft purple robes” (24.796; see [[purple]]). Studniczka, Leaf reports, thought the covering was spread over the chest, not wrapped around the bones inside it (on 24.796; see the translation's note on 24.796). In the Odyssey the bones of Achilles were gathered at dawn in unmixed wine and ointment and laid in a golden amphora (*Odyssey* 24.72–77). The last verse of the poem names the whole rite: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804).
