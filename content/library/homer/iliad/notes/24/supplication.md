summary: The poem's last supplication is foretold by Zeus, who promises that Achilles will spare a suppliant, and made at night in Achilles' hut. Priam clasps Achilles' knees, kisses the hands that killed his sons and begs him by his father; Achilles pushes him gently away, weeps with him, raises him by the hand and bids him sit, and when Priam presses him, warns him that he might not spare him, suppliant though he is (24.152–187, 24.465–570).

**Foretold.** Zeus sends Priam to the ships with a promise: Achilles will not kill him, nor let others kill him, “for he is not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender, / but with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (24.157–158 = 24.186–187; see [[zeus]] and [[achilles]]). By the river, when Idaeus sees a stranger coming, he offers Priam the two ways of the defenseless, to flee on the horses or to “clasp his knees and entreat him, in the hope that he may have pity” (24.356–357; see [[idaeus]]). Hermes, revealing himself at Achilles' gate, gives the rite its form: “go in yourself and clasp the knees of the son of Peleus, / and beg him by his father and his mother of the lovely hair / and his child, so that you may stir his heart” (24.465–467; see [[hermes]] and [[gounata]]). Walter Leaf finds well founded Düntzer's objections to 466–467: the Iliad knows no son of Achilles except in late passages, and Priam names neither the mother nor the son (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.466; see the translation's note on 24.466–467).

**The gesture.** Priam comes in unseen, “and standing close / he clasped the knees of Achilles in his hands and kissed his hands, / the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons” (24.477–479; see [[the-killer-in-exile]]). Thetis clasped Zeus' knees with her left hand and took his chin with her right (1.500–502), and Athena remembers that she “kissed his knees and took his chin in her hand” (8.371; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]); Lycaon held Achilles' knees with one hand and the spear with the other (21.71–72; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Priam kisses the killer's hands. Achilles and his companions stare at him as men stare at an exile who has killed a man in his own land and comes to the house of a rich man in another (24.480–484; see [[exile-and-refuge]] and [[thambos]]). Leaf rejects K. O. Müller's conjecture, drawn from the scholia, that the rich man is “one who purifies”: Homer knows no ritual purification for blood, only payment or exile (on 24.482; see the translation's note on 24.480–482).

**The plea.** “And Priam spoke to him, begging” (λισσόμενος, 24.485). He asks Achilles to remember his father, an old man like himself, who hopes to see his son come home (24.486–492; see [[peleus]]); tells of his fifty sons and of Hector, for whom he has come with a boundless ransom (24.493–502; see [[boundless-ransom]]); and ends with the request:

{{quote:c896769c-6e34-5817-963f-c658dcb24c47}}

αἰδεῖο θεούς, “respect the gods”, is the suppliant's appeal in the Odyssey too, when Odysseus reminds the Cyclops that Zeus avenges suppliants and strangers (*Odyssey* 9.269–271; Leaf on 24.503; see [[aidos]]). The other half of the request, “pity me”, is Lycaon's (21.74) and Priam's own to Hector on the wall (22.59; see [[pity]]). In the last verse, ποτὶ στόμα χεῖρʼ ὀρέγεσθαι is rendered with the usual reading, “to put to my lips the hand” (24.506), for he has kissed Achilles' hands (24.478). Leaf takes χεῖρʼ for χειρί, “to reach my hand to the mouth” of the killer, the suppliant's touch of the chin (on 24.506; see the translation's note on 24.506).

**Received.** The answer is given with the hands. “Taking the old man's hand he gently pushed him away” (24.508), and the two weep together; then Achilles rises from his chair and “raised the old man by the hand, / pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.515–516), and bids him sit down on a chair (24.522; see [[throne]]). In the Odyssey the same gesture accepts a suppliant: Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand, raises him from the hearth and seats him on a shining chair (*Odyssey* 7.167–171; see [[xenia]]). Lycaon and Hector had begged Achilles in vain (21.74–113, 22.338–354); this suppliant is heard.

**Suppliant though you are.** When Priam will not sit, Achilles warns him: “lest I do not spare even you, old man, in my huts, / suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus” (24.569–570). Leaf takes the commands to be the laws of Zeus who protects suppliants, Ζεὺς ἱκετήσιος in the Odyssey (on 24.570; *Odyssey* 13.213). The limit is spoken and not crossed: Achilles has the body washed out of Priam's sight, lest his own anger break out and he kill him (24.582–586; see [[cholos]]). The poem's first suppliant was a father too, Chryses, who “begged all the Achaeans” with the god's fillets in his hands and was driven away (1.14–15, 1.22–33; see [[chryses]] and [[book-1-and-book-24]]).
