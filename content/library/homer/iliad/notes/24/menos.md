---
summary: Achilles longs for Patroclus, “for his manhood and his noble might” (24.6), and drags Hector “in his rage” (24.22, 24.54). Priam tells Hecuba, “terribly my own might and heart bid me / to go there to the ships” (24.198–199); Hermes asks whether he does not fear “the Achaeans breathing fury” (24.364), and then “breathed noble might into the horses and the mules” (24.442). At the end the Trojans quench the pyre “as far as the might of the fire had reached” (24.792).
---

**Might and rage.** μένος ἠΰ, “noble might”, is what Achilles remembers of Patroclus, beside his manhood (24.6; see [[patroclus]]). μενεαίνων, “in his rage”, is the narrator's word and then Apollo's for Achilles dragging the body (24.22, 24.54; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[aeikizo]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]).

**Priam's might.** μένος καὶ θυμός, “my own might and heart”, drive Priam to the ships (24.198), as in Book 22 Achilles wished that his μένος and θυμός would drive him to eat Hector's flesh raw (22.346–347); here the old man is driven to go unarmed to his son's killer (see [[thymos]] and [[priam]]), while Hecuba wishes she could eat Achilles' liver (24.212–213; see [[hecuba]]). The Achaeans he must pass are μένεα πνείοντας Ἀχαιούς, “the Achaeans breathing fury” (24.364; see [[breathing-fury]]).

**A god's might.** Hermes, taking the reins, breathes μένος ἠΰ into the horses and the mules (24.440–442), as Zeus breathed it into the horses of Achilles mourning Patroclus (17.456; see [[hermes]], [[horses]] and [[mules]]).

**The might of the fire.** The verse with which the Trojans quench Hector's pyre is the one Achilles used when he told the Achaeans to quench Patroclus' pyre, πυρὸς μένος, “the might of the fire” (24.791–792; 23.237–238); Walter Leaf calls the funeral of Hector almost an abstract of that of Patroclus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.791; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).
