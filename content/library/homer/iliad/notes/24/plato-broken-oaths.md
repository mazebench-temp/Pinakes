summary: The first of Homer's verses that the Republic rejects for making a god the cause of evils are the two jars of Zeus, from Achilles' consolation of Priam. Plato's text of them differs from ours, and his reading, two jars, one of good and one of evil, is the one the translation follows; Proclus answered that the evils of the jar are only the hindrances of life, as Achilles' own example of Peleus shows.

**Plato's verses.** God, being good, is the cause of few things for men and not the cause of many, Socrates argues, since good things are far fewer than evils in human life; so the city must not accept from Homer or any other poet the error of saying “that two jars stand on the floor of Zeus, full of fates, the one of good, the other of bad”, and that the man to whom Zeus gives a mixture of both meets now with evil, now with good, while the man to whom he gives the one kind unmixed, “him evil ravening hunger drives over the shining earth”, nor that “Zeus is the dispenser for us of good and of evil alike” (*Republic* 2.379c–e; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). Plato's second verse, κηρῶν ἔμπλειοι, ὁ μὲν ἐσθλῶν, αὐτὰρ ὃ δειλῶν, “full of fates, the one of good, the other of bad”, is not in our text, which has “of the gifts that he gives, one of evils, and the other of blessings” (24.528). He gives 24.529 and 24.531 in prose, 24.530 as it stands, and 24.532 with τὸν δέ, “him”, for καί ἑ; he leaves out 24.533, and the line about the dispenser of good and evil is not in our Iliad at all (see the entry above). Walter Leaf calls the form in which Plato cites 24.528 “a rare instance in his works of large deviation from the vulgate” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.527).

**Two jars or three.** Leaf reports that the passage was read in two ways from ancient times. On one reading both of the “two jars” are jars of evils and the jar of blessings is a third, which agrees with what follows, since a man can at best expect a mixture and may have no good at all; on the other there are two jars only, one of evils and one of blessings. Pindar's “for one good, two woes” agrees with the first (see [[pindar-the-jars-of-zeus]]). Plato, Leaf says, took the passage in the second way, and on the authority of Plato and the construction of the sentence he prefers it (on 24.527); the translation renders it so (see the translation's note on 24.527–528).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus asks how one could defend the myths that seem to make the gods causes of evils, and quotes the jars in Plato's words. His answer is that all things in the world fall into two series, coming down from the gods themselves: health, beauty, strength, power, honors and wealth in the better series, and their opposites in the worse. The ancients called the first goods and the second evils, not meaning the injustice and licentiousness of the soul, which are truly evil, but the hindrances of action, sickness, weakness and want. The cause of both series is in the maker of the world, who gives each soul what is fitting with a view to its good; and the poet in his myth calls these two sources “jars”, πίθοι, either because the maker sets a limit on all things by the persuasion (πειθώ) of mind, or because the jars contain all the varied things he gives (*On the Republic* I 96–99 Kroll). So Plato and Homer agree. Homer shows what he means by evil in the lines that follow, on Peleus: the gods gave him splendid gifts, but “on him too the god laid evil”, that he had one son, doomed to an untimely death, who would not care for him in his old age (24.534–541; see [[peleus]]). “Does he seem to you to blame the god for true evils? Does he not call childlessness and the lack of care evils?” Such things may not rightly be called evils by true philosophers, but to those who have chosen the life of action they are hindrances to a life of virtue; and since Plato's own lawgiver in the *Laws* makes god the cause of these too, “it is not only Homer and Homer's Achilles who say these things, but Plato himself and his lawgiver” (I 99–100 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**In the hut.** Achilles speaks of the jars to Priam when the two of them have wept, the one for Hector and the other for his father and for Patroclus. The gods, he says, “have spun the thread for wretched mortals, / to live in grief; but they themselves are free from care” (24.525–526); then come the jars, and then the examples of Peleus and of Priam himself, who was once blessed in wealth and sons (24.527–548; see [[achilles]] and [[priam]]). A few lines later the *Republic* rejects the line of Aeschylus that a god plants a cause in mortals when he wishes to ruin a house utterly, and Plato names the play's subject, “the sufferings of Niobe” (τὰ τῆς Νιόβης πάθη, *Republic* 2.380a; see [[niobe-play]]). Achilles tells Niobe's story to Priam a little later in the same scene (24.602–617; see [[the-children-of-niobe]]).
