summary: The Trojans crowd into Priam's colonnade as he makes ready the ransom, and he drives them out with his staff: Hector's death has made them easier for the Achaeans to slay. When the body comes home the whole city goes out to meet it at the gates, until Priam bids them make way; they lament it, and at Priam's word they bring wood for nine days without fear of the Achaeans, burn Hector and heap his mound (24.237–247, 24.704–718, 24.777–804).

**Out of the colonnade.** Zeus' message bids Priam go to Achilles “alone; let no other man of the Trojans go with him” (24.148 = 24.177). When he has chosen the ransom, “all the Trojans / he kept away from the colonnade, rebuking them with shaming words” (24.237–238; see [[priams-palace]] and [[aischros]]):

{{quote:4a5d6cfa-f927-53d7-90e5-43eaa5a05519}}

“with shaming words” is the formula of Hector's rebukes to Paris (3.38, 6.325; see the translation's table of formulas). ὀνόσασθε, “do you make light of it”, is Aristarchus' reading for the corrupt οὔνεσθε of the vulgate; Walter Leaf would read ὤνησθε, “Are ye the gainers that grief has come upon me?” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.241; see the translation's note on 24.241). Then “he spoke, and drove the men off with his staff” (24.247): σκηπάνιον, “staff”, is found once more, in the hand of Poseidon as he fills the two Ajaxes with might (13.59), and δίεπε, Leaf explains, is “brought them into order”, as Odysseus did with the army (on 24.247; 2.207). He would rather go down to Hades than see the city sacked (24.244–246), the sack he pictured in Book 22, with his own death at his doors (22.59–76; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[priam]]).

**At the gates.** At dawn Cassandra calls “Trojans and Trojan women” to see Hector, “for he was a great joy to the city and to all the people” (24.704–706; see [[cassandra]] and [[trojan-women]]). “So she spoke, and not a man was left there in the city, / nor a woman” (24.707–708), and they meet him near the gates; the throng stands weeping, and they would have mourned him all day, until Priam bids them make way for the mules, “and they stood apart and made way for the wagon” (24.709–718; see [[the-laments-for-hector]]). After the laments, “the countless people groaned” with Helen (24.776; see [[lament]]).

**The truce.** Priam told Achilles that “the Trojans are greatly afraid” to go to the mountain for wood (24.663). Now he tells them, “Now, Trojans, bring wood into the city, and have no fear / in your hearts of a cunning ambush of the Argives”, for Achilles has promised no harm until the twelfth dawn (24.778–781; see [[truce]] and [[ambush]]). πυκινὸν λόχον, “a cunning ambush”: Leaf takes the adjective here as “cunning” rather than “close”, as at 4.392 (on 24.779; see the translation's note on 24.779). They yoke the wagons, gather before the city, bring wood for nine days, burn Hector on the tenth, and at the next dawn gather at the pyre, set lookouts around the mound “lest the well-greaved Achaeans should attack too soon”, and feast in Priam's house (24.782–803; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).
