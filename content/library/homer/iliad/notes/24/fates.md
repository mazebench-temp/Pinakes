---
summary: Apollo tells the gods that the Fates have put an enduring heart in men, so that a man who has lost a brother or a son weeps and leaves off; Hecuba tells Priam that mighty Fate spun Hector's death for him at his birth, to glut the dogs far from his parents.
---

**An enduring heart.** Apollo's protest sets the ordinary way of mourning against Achilles':

{{quote:a0f5f161-9d91-552a-8698-61d56d79ce1c}}

Μοῖραι, the Fates in the plural, are found only here in Homer. Walter Leaf counts the plural, with the active sense of τλητός, “enduring”, among the marks of a late passage: it implies a distinct personification of the Fates, which recurs in the *Odyssey*'s κλῶθες and is fully developed in Hesiod (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.49). What the Fates give is the power to bear. The book ends in the same teaching. Achilles tells Priam that “so the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals, / to live in grief” (24.525–526), bids him “Bear up, and do not mourn relentlessly in your heart” (24.549), and tells him how Niobe, when she was worn out with weeping, remembered food (24.602–613; see [[the-children-of-niobe]]).

**What Fate spun.** Hecuba will not let Priam go to Achilles. Let them weep for Hector at home, “this is how mighty Fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth, when I myself bore him, / that he should glut the swift dogs, far from his parents, / at the side of a mighty man” (24.209–212; see [[hecuba]] and [[mighty-fate]]). Leaf takes ὥς as “thus”, pointing on to what follows; Monro took it as “as”, with the sentence left unfinished, which Leaf thinks needless (on 24.209). The verse of the spinning is the one in which Hera said of Achilles that he would suffer hereafter “whatever his fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth” (20.127–128; Leaf on 24.210; see [[aisa]]): there it was αἶσα, here Μοῖρα in person. Hecuba reads the thread from what she fears, a body left to the dogs. The book proves her wrong in the end: the gods have kept the body whole, and Hermes tells Priam, “Old man, the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds” (24.411).
