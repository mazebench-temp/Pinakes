---
summary: Apollo warns the gods about Achilles: “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.53–54). Hermes, having brought Priam to the hut, will not go in himself, since “it would be a thing for blame / for an immortal god to welcome mortals so openly” (24.463–464).
---

**The gods' indignation.** Apollo's protest ends with the threat of the gods' νέμεσις against a man who has gone too far, however brave he is (24.53–54; see [[apollo]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Walter Leaf finds the line faulty as it stands, in the form νεμεσσηθέωμεν, the place of οἱ and its neglected digamma, and reads μή ϝʼ ἀγαθῷ περ ἐόντι νεμεσσηθήομεν ἡμεῖς (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.53). Hera answers in anger, and Zeus settles the matter; the indignation Apollo threatens comes to Achilles as Zeus' message that the gods are angry with him, Zeus above all (24.113–114, 24.134–135; see [[cholos]]).

**A thing for blame.** νεμεσσητὸν δέ κεν εἴη, “it would be a thing for blame”, is the half-line with which Helen refused to go to Paris' bed, and Hera to go back to Zeus' house from a bed where the gods might have seen them (3.410, 14.336; again *Odyssey* 22.489). Here the next line says what would be blamed (24.464). Leaf takes ἀγαπαζέμεν in its Odyssean sense, “to welcome as host”, with mortals as the subject, since an immortal cannot allow himself to become the guest of men, and reports that Düntzer took the line for an explanatory gloss on a half-line that elsewhere always stands alone (on 24.464; see the translation's note on 24.464 and [[xenia]]). The god who has driven Priam's chariot through the Achaean camp will not come with him into Achilles' sight (24.462–463; see [[hermes]]).
