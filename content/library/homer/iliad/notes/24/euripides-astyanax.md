summary: Andromache's lament over Hector foresees what the Trojan Women shows, the women carried off in the ships and the child thrown from the tower. In Homer an Achaean kills the boy in anger for a kinsman whom Hector killed; in Euripides the Greeks kill him for fear of what his father was. The Little Iliad, as Tzetzes quotes it, gave the deed to the son of Achilles.

**Andromache's foresight.** Andromache leads the laments when Hector's body is brought home, holding his head in her hands. The boy, she says, will not reach his youth, for the city will be sacked before that, now that its guardian is dead; the wives and little children will soon be carried off in the hollow ships, “and I among them”. The child will either go with her and labor at shameful tasks for an implacable master, “or some one of the Achaeans / will take you by the hand and hurl you from the tower to grim destruction, / in anger, because Hector killed his brother, it may be, / or his father, or his son too” (24.723–738; see [[astyanax]], [[andromache]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). The reason she gives is the father's: “For your father was not kind in grim battle” (24.739).

**Euripides.** The *Trojan Women* fulfills each part. The women are shared out among the Greeks, and Andromache is given to the son of Achilles (see the entry above). The herald Talthybius tells her that Odysseus prevailed among the Greeks, “saying that the son of the best of fathers must not be reared”, and that the boy is to be thrown from the towers of Troy (*Trojan Women* 719–725). Andromache answers with the cause that she gave in Homer, but turned: “your father's nobility will kill you, which brings safety to others” (740–743). Homer's Achaean kills the child out of anger for a kinsman whom Hector killed; Euripides' Greeks kill him for fear of what his father was. Hecuba says as much over the body, imagining the epitaph a poet might write on his tomb: “This child the Argives killed once”, “because they were afraid?” — a shameful epitaph for Greece (1188–1191).

**The Little Iliad.** Walter Leaf reports that the death of Astyanax was told in the poems of the Cycle, and quotes the lines that Tzetzes gives from the *Little Iliad* of Lesches: the glorious son of great-hearted Achilles led Hector's wife down to the hollow ships, and took the child from the bosom of his fair-tressed nurse, and seizing him by the foot hurled him from the tower; and as he fell, purple death and mighty fate seized him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.735; see [[little-iliad]] and [[neoptolemus]]).
