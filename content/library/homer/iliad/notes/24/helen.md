summary: Helen speaks the third and last lament over Hector, the last words of hers in the poem. He was the dearest of her brothers-in-law: in the twenty years since Alexander brought her to Troy he never spoke an unkind word to her, and when the others of the house rebuked her he held them back; now no one in Troy is kind to her, and all shudder at her (24.761–776).

**The third.** “And then Helen, the third, led the lamentation among them” (24.761), after Andromache and Hecuba (see [[the-laments-for-hector]] and [[lament]]). She speaks for the last time in the Iliad:

{{quote:db841fb7-340a-5910-ae5f-b64c26d8d00c}}

She begins as Hecuba began, “Hector, by far the dearest to my heart”, but of her brothers-in-law, δαέρων (24.748, 24.762; see [[hecuba]] and [[in-laws]]). δᾶερ, “brother-in-law”, was her word to him when he came to Paris' house in Book 6 (6.344, 6.355). Her husband is “godlike Alexander”, and the wish that she had died first is the wish she spoke to Priam on the wall and to Hector in his brother's house (3.173–175, 6.345–348; see [[paris]] and [[godlike]]). Walter Leaf answers those who find no connection in her words: though Paris was her husband, and she could claim no kindness from Hector, yet (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.763). The twentieth year is a startling word to him, explicable only by the legend of the first expedition that landed in Mysia by mistake and lost ten years, a story unknown to Homer but present in germ in the *Cypria*; the two verses return in the Odyssey, of Odysseus gone twenty years from home (24.765–766 ≈ *Odyssey* 19.222–223), and Leaf thinks they may come from the *Cypria*, though they add much force to the lament (on 24.765; see the translation's note on 24.765 and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]).

**His gentleness.**

{{quote:a99d2cc0-c6f4-5f74-ad6b-c611e67df4f1}}

ἀσύφηλον, “contemptuous”, is Achilles' word for the treatment he had from Agamemnon, “how he treated me with contempt among the Argives” (9.647; Leaf on 24.767). The in-laws are named from her side: the husband's brothers, sisters, brothers' wives and mother, and his father, Priam, “always kind, like a father” (24.769–770; see [[kind-as-a-father]] and the translation's row for δᾶερ, γάλοως, εἰνάτερες). So Priam called her “dear child” on the wall and told her that the gods, not she, were to blame (3.162–165; see [[priam]]). εἰ … ἐνίπτοι, “if anyone … rebuked me”, is the only optative of repeated action after εἰ in Homer, Leaf notes (on 24.768); and he defends 772, rejected in antiquity as a repetition of 771, as a pathetic touch (on 24.772; see the translation's note on 24.772). ἀγανοφροσύνη, “gentleness”, and ἀγανοῖς ἐπέεσσι, “gentle words” (24.772; see [[gentle-words]]): of the three laments Leaf gives hers the theme of Hector's gentleness (on 24.723). In Book 6 she spoke to him “with soothing words”, called herself a dog, and asked him to sit; he refused gently, “Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me” (6.343–360; see [[hector]]).

**Alone in Troy.** “So I weep for you, and for my ill-fated self, grieving at heart; / for there is no longer anyone else in wide Troy / who is kind to me or a friend, but all shudder at me” (24.773–775). ἄμμορον, “ill-fated”, is Andromache's word for herself in Book 6 (6.408; see [[andromache]]). The blame of the Trojan women was what she feared in Book 3, when she would not go to Paris' bed (3.411–412; see [[trojan-women]]). It is not the women who answer her, as they answered Andromache, but “the countless people” (24.776; see the translation's note on 24.776).
