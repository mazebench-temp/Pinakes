---
summary: At dawn Cassandra, who has gone up onto Pergamus, is the first to see her father coming back with the herald and Hector's body on the wagon, and she calls out through all the city (24.697–706).
---

“And no one else, / of the men or the lovely-girdled women, knew them first, / but Cassandra, like golden Aphrodite, / who had gone up onto Pergamus, caught sight of her dear father / standing in the chariot, and the herald, crier of the city” (24.697–701; see [[cassandra]], [[priam]] and [[idaeus]]). Pergamus is the height of the city, from which Apollo looked down on the battle and Paris came down in his shining armor (4.508, 6.512), and the first to see the return is the one who has climbed it. Walter Leaf notes that the scholia find no sign here of Cassandra's gift of prophecy, and that nothing in the words is inconsistent with it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.699; see the translation's note on 24.699). From the height she calls the Trojans and the Trojan women to come and see Hector, and the whole city goes out to meet him at the gates (24.703–709; see [[troy]] and [[lament]]).

At the beginning of the war it was from Pergamus that Apollo, looking down, called to the Trojans to fight on, since Achilles was not fighting (4.507–513); the poem's last look from the citadel is at Hector brought home dead.
