---
summary: Death in the last book is above all Priam's to face. Zeus and Iris bid him have “no thought of death” in his heart (24.152, 24.181); he tells Hecuba that if it is his fate to die at the ships, “let Achilles kill me at once” (24.226); and his household follows him out of the city “as if he were going to his death” (24.328). The dead are a body: Apollo pities Hector “though he was dead” (24.20) and calls him “the senseless earth” (24.54), and Andromache mourns that Hector did not reach out his hands to her “as you died” (24.743).
grc: [τεθνηότα, τεθνάμεναι, θνητοῦ, θνητῷ]
---

**Mortal and immortal.** The gods' quarrel over the body turns on the line between them and men. Hera will not have Achilles and Hector given equal honor: “Hector is a mortal and was suckled at a woman's breast; / but Achilles is the offspring of a goddess” (24.58–59; see [[hera]] and [[time]]). Walter Leaf takes θνητός as “a mere mortal”, with the weight on the rest of the line (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.58). Priam says of Hector that he “did not seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god” (24.258–259), and Achilles that the gods gave Peleus, “a mortal”, a goddess for a wife (24.537; see [[peleus]]). The gods spin grief for mortals, “but they themselves are free from care” (24.525–526; see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]).

**Priam and death.** Zeus' message closes with “Let him have no thought of death in his heart, and no fear” (24.152, again in Iris' mouth, 24.181), as Odysseus reassured Dolon, “Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383; Leaf on 24.152). Priam has already chosen: if it is his fate (αἶσα) τεθνάμεναι, “to die”, at the ships, he would have it so (24.224–227; see [[aisa]]), and he would rather go down to the house of Hades than see the city sacked (24.244–246). His sons and sons-in-law follow him out “as if he were going to his death” (θάνατον δέ, with the suffix of direction, θάνατόνδε, 24.328; see [[as-if-going-to-his-death]]), and the stranger he meets at the river reminds him that he is old and the Achaeans near (24.362–369; see [[priam]] and [[hermes]]).

**The dead body.** Apollo keeps all defilement from Hector's flesh, pitying the man καὶ τεθνηότα περ, “though he was dead” (24.20), the participle with which the narrator says that Achilles did not yet know that Patroclus was dead (17.402). Apollo's protest names the corpse κωφὴν γαῖαν, “the senseless earth” (24.54): Leaf renders “the senseless clay”, compares Menelaus' curse on the Achaeans, “may you all turn to water and earth” (7.99), and the dead man as earth in Sophocles and Euripides, and rejects the reading that Achilles outrages the earth by dragging the body over it, a thought “surely not Epic” (on 24.54; see the translation's note on 24.54, [[the-senseless-earth]], [[water-and-earth]] and [[aeikizo]]). Aristotle quotes the verse to show that men are not angry with the dead, who can no longer feel (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b24–30). The book ends with what is owed the dead, the lament and the pyre (see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[lament]]). Andromache's last reproach is that Hector died in battle, not in his bed: “For you did not stretch out your hands to me from your bed as you died, / nor speak to me some shrewd word” (24.743–744; see [[andromache]]).
