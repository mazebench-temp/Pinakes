**Even Niobe.** Achilles has released the body and bids Priam think of supper, “For even Niobe of the lovely hair remembered food” (24.601–602; see [[achilles]] and [[the-children-of-niobe]]). This is her only appearance in Homer (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.602; see the translation's note on 24.602). ἠΰκομος, “of the lovely hair”, is the epithet of Leto, the goddess she likened herself to (1.36, 19.413; see [[leto]]). Her offense is a mother's boast: “she said that the goddess had borne two, while she herself had borne many” (24.608). ἰσάσκετο, “she used to liken herself”, is found only here, an iterative of the verb of equality (24.607; Leaf on 24.607). Apollo warned Diomedes in the same spirit not to think himself equal to the gods, “since never alike is the race / of immortal gods and of men who walk on the earth” (5.440–442; see [[gods-and-mortals]]). Leaf reports a fragment of Sappho in which Leto and Niobe were once dear companions (on 24.607).

**She remembered food.** The example opens and closes on the same words, ἐμνήσατο σίτου, “remembered food” (24.602), and σίτου μνήσατʼ (24.613), and its point is in the second: “And she remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears.” Thetis had asked her son how long he would eat out his heart, “remembering neither food / nor bed” (24.129–130), and now he asks Priam to remember supper (24.601) and to think of food, μεδώμεθα … σίτου (24.618–619; see [[fasting]]). An ancient note on 613 marks Homer's difference from the later poets, who turn Niobe herself to stone, as Homer here does not; Leaf reconciles 613 with 617 by the later story that she went home to Sipylus and long afterwards prayed to be turned to stone (on 24.613; see the translation's note on 24.613), as Apollodorus tells it (*Library* 3.5.6).

The Alexandrians athetized 614–617 because a woman turned to stone could not have eaten, and the consolation, “eat, since Niobe too ate and was turned to stone”, seemed absurd; they also found the language Hesiodic (Leaf on 24.614; see the translation's note on 24.602). Leaf thinks the arguments insufficient. Niobe was turned to stone at her own prayer, not as a punishment, so that her grief, by the favor of the gods, was made to last in stone, and the lines are needed to the thought: eat now, and mourn your son hereafter, ἔπειτα, as Achilles goes on (24.619; on 24.614). φασί, “they say”, has its parallels in Homer (2.783; *Odyssey* 6.42), where the poet reports what is said of a place (on 24.614). θεῶν ἐκ κήδεα πέσσει is rendered “she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her”; Leaf allows this, but would rather join θεῶν ἐκ with the verb, “by the act of the gods”, as more fitting to Priam's consolation (on 24.617; see the translation's note on 24.617). πέσσει, “broods over”, is the verb Priam uses of himself a little later: “but always I groan and brood over countless sorrows” (κήδεα μυρία πέσσω, 24.639; see [[priam]]). The place is Sipylus, among the beds of the nymphs who dance around the Achelous (24.615–616; see [[sipylus]], [[achelous-of-lydia]] and [[nymphs]]). Leaf reports that all antiquity knew the stone figure of Niobe on Sipylus, which Pausanias, as he quotes him, describes as a rock that from a distance seems a weeping, downcast woman, and judges that the ancients did not mean the carved figure near Smyrna that has been called the Niobe (on 24.614).

For later tellings of the story see [[ovid-niobe]], [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]] and [[niobe-play]].
