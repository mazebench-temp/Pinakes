# πύργους τε νεῶν (24.443), "the towers of the ships": a phrase, since πύργους alone is also the towers of Troy (18.278,
# 22.195). τάφρον in the same line is caught by τάφρ*.
grc: ["πύργους τε νεῶν"]
---

“But when they came to the towers of the ships and the ditch, / the sentries had just begun to busy themselves about their supper” (24.443–444; see [[hermes]] and [[night-watch]]). πύργοι νεῶν, “the towers of the ships”, are the fortifications of the camp (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.443). The god pours sleep over the sentries, “and at once opened the gates and pushed back the bars, / and brought in Priam and the splendid gifts on the wagon” (24.446–447; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). Leaf observes that the sentries seem to stand at the gate in the wall, as if the space between the wall and the ditch were forgotten (on 24.443).

The towers and the barred gates are there, though Apollo threw the wall down in Book 15, “as when a boy beside the sea throws down the sand” (15.361–364; see the note on Book 15). The poem goes on using the wall and the ditch as the bounds of the camp: Achilles “went from the wall and stood at the ditch” (18.215), and Athena shouted “beside the ditch they had dug, outside the wall” (20.49). In the last book a god brings Priam in through the gates, and before dawn takes him out again through the camp unseen (24.443–447, 24.680–691). The wall's end, after the war, the narrator has already told (12.17–33).
