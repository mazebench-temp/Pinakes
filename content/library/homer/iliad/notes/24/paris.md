**Alexander's blind folly.** All the gods pity Hector except Hera, Poseidon and Athena, who hold to their hatred “as when sacred Ilios first became hateful to them, / and Priam, and the people, for Alexander's blind folly, / who insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard, / and praised her who gave him his grievous lust” (24.27–30; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]] and [[ate]]). Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἄτης is Helen's own phrase in Book 6, “for my sake, dog that I am, and for Alexander's blind folly” (6.356; see [[helen]]); Walter Leaf would accept the variant ἀρχῆς, “beginning”, if he were sure the verse were old, as at 3.100, “because Alexander began it” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.28; see [[arche-or-ate]]). Aristarchus rejected 25–30, and Leaf finds his case decisive against 29–30 (on 24.23; see the translation's notes on 24.23–30 and 24.29).

**One of the nine.** When Priam makes ready to ransom Hector, he rebukes “Helenus and Paris and brilliant Agathon” and the others, “liars and dancers, best at beating the dancing floor” (24.249–261; see [[sons-of-priam]]). Πάρις is the name here, Ἀλέξανδρος in the other two places. Aphrodite showed him to Helen in Book 3 as a man who looked as if he were going to the dance, or had just stopped dancing and sat down, not one who had come from fighting a man (3.391–394; see [[aphrodite]] and [[dance]]).

**Her husband.** Helen's lament begins with him: “truly my husband is godlike Alexander, / who brought me to Troy; I wish I had perished before that!” (24.763–764; see [[godlike]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). He is alive at the end of the poem. The dying Hector foretold that Paris and Apollo would kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
