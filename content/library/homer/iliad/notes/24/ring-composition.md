summary: Achilles' story of Niobe is framed by its point: “let us remember supper” and Niobe “remembered food” at the start, “she remembered food” and “let us two also think … of food” at the end (24.601–602, 24.613, 24.618–619).

The ring runs: A, the meal now (24.601); B, Niobe remembered food (24.602); C, the story of her children's death and burial (24.603–612); B′, “And she remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.613); A′, “But come, let us two also think, brilliant old sir, / of food” (24.618–619). The verses on Niobe turned to stone (24.614–617) stand between B′ and A′; Aristarchus rejected them, and Walter Leaf defends them as part of the thought (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.614; [[mythological-paradigm]]). The poem's own beginning and end answer each other in the same way, the father who comes to ransom his child (see [[book-1-and-book-24]]). See [[niobe]] and [[achilles]].
