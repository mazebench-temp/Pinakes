---
summary: In Achilles' story of Niobe, the mother turned to stone broods over her sorrows on Sipylus, “where they say the goddesses have their beds, / the nymphs who dance around the Achelous”.
---

**On Sipylus.** Achilles ends the story of Niobe with what she is now: “And now somewhere among the rocks, in the lonely mountains, / on Sipylus, where they say the goddesses have their beds, / the nymphs who dance around the Achelous, / there, though she is stone, she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her” (24.614–617; see [[niobe]], [[the-children-of-niobe]], [[sipylus]] and [[achelous-of-lydia]]). The nymphs are goddesses (θεάων … νυμφάων), and their εὐναί, “beds”, are perhaps no more than their dwellings, unlike the bed of Typhoeus beneath the earth, “where they say Typhoeus has his bed” (2.783); the Achelous of Lydia is otherwise unknown (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.615, 24.616; see the translation's note on 24.615). As the mountain nymphs planted elms around the tomb of Eetion (6.419–420), these keep their dances near a mourner turned to stone.

**The dance.** ἐρρώσαντο is “dance”. The Alexandrians athetized 614–617, among other reasons because the passage has the stamp of Hesiod, above all in ἀμφʼ Ἀχελώϊον ἐρρώσαντο. Leaf grants that the verb in this sense is Hesiodic, as when the Muses dance on Helicon (ἐπερρώσαντο δὲ ποσσίν, *Theogony* 8) and the mountain nymphs of the *Hymn to Aphrodite* “dance a fair dance among the immortals” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 261), but takes it for a natural narrowing of the Homeric sense, “move nimbly”, as of the hair of Zeus at his nod or the manes of horses (1.529, 23.367), and finds parallels for “they say” in a poet's own narrative (2.783; *Odyssey* 6.42; on 24.614; see the translation's note on 24.602 and [[dance]]).
