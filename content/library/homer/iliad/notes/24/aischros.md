---
summary: Priam drives the Trojans from his colonnade, “rebuking them with shaming words” (24.238), and Hermes tells him that Achilles drags Hector's body every dawn, “yet he does not disfigure him” (24.418).
---

**Shaming words.** αἰσχροῖς ἐπέεσσιν is the phrase of Hector's rebukes of Paris (3.38 = 6.325; also 13.768); in the last book, as ἔπεσσʼ αἰσχροῖσιν, it is the father's, as he drives the Trojans out of his colonnade: “Off with you, you wretches, you disgraces! Have you not / lament enough at home, that you come to trouble me?” (24.238–240; see [[elenchos]], [[priam]] and [[lament]]). His sons hear worse, “you wicked children, you shames of mine” (κατηφόνες, 24.253; see [[katepheie]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**A body not disfigured.** αἰσχύνω is the verb of a corpse defiled: Iris warned Achilles of the disgrace if Patroclus' body came back disfigured (ᾐσχυμμένος, 18.180), and Priam pictured the dogs disfiguring the old man killed at his doors (22.75). Hermes uses it of what Achilles cannot do: he drags Hector ruthlessly, “yet he does not disfigure him”, and the body lies fresh as dew, its wounds closed, because the gods care for it (24.416–423; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[fresh-as-dew]], [[aeikizo]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The translation keeps “defile” for ἀεικίζω and renders αἰσχύνω here “disfigure”.
