---
summary: At nightfall Priam and his herald halt to water their animals at the river beyond the mound of Ilus, where Hermes meets them; on the way back the god brings them to the ford of “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot”, and leaves them there as Dawn spreads over the earth (24.349–351, 24.692–695).
# Unnamed: the river where they halt (24.351), and "the fair-flowing river" of the ford (24.692), named in the next line.
refs: [24.351, 24.692]
---

**The river at nightfall.** “And when they had driven past the great mound of Ilus, / they halted the mules and the horses to drink / at the river; for darkness had now come over the earth” (24.349–351; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[mules]] and [[horses]]). Walter Leaf takes ἐν ποταμῷ, “at the river”, with στῆσαν, “they halted”, and supposes that they meant to cross at the ford named at the end of the night: the darkness comes just in time for them to pass into the enemy's lines (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.351; see the translation's note on 24.351). There, by the water, the herald sees a man coming, and Hermes takes Priam's hand (24.352–361; see [[hermes]]).

**The ford.** Before dawn the god drives them back through the camp, “But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river, / the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot, / then Hermes went away to high Olympus” (24.692–694; see [[fair-flowing]] and [[eddying]]). The river is named, as at its ford always, by its gods' name (see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). The couplet comes for the third time. The Trojans carried the stunned Hector to the ford and revived him with its water (14.433–436), and at the ford Achilles split the fleeing Trojans and drove half of them into the river (21.1–16; see the notes on Books 14 and 21). Leaf observes that the ford is named only in this formal couplet (on 21.4; see the translation's note on 21.1–2). Now Hector's body crosses it on the way home, and Priam drives on toward the city under the dawn (24.695–697; see [[eos]] and [[hector]]).
