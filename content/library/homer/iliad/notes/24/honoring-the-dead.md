---
summary: Apollo reproaches the gods for not saving Hector's body for his kin, who “would quickly / burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites” (24.37–38); the gods keep the body whole, Achilles has it washed and anointed and grants the days of the funeral (24.580–590, 24.656–670), and the book ends with Hector's pyre and mound (24.784–804).
refs: [24.35-38, 24.582-589, 24.656-667]
---

**The gods' demand.** Apollo's protest names what the dead are owed: Hector's body should be given back “for his wife to see, and his mother and his child, / and his father Priam and the people, who would quickly / burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites” (24.36–38; see [[apollo]] and [[the-gods-debate]]). Zeus sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry “because in his raging heart / he keeps Hector beside the curved ships and has not released him” (24.114–115). Meanwhile the gods keep the body as it was: “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is” (24.422–423; see [[aeikizo]], [[aegis]] and [[fresh-as-dew]]).

**Achilles' part.** Achilles gives the body its first rites himself. He has the slave women wash and anoint it apart, so that Priam should not see it, and dress it in a mantle and tunic left from the ransom, and he lifts it onto the bier himself (24.580–590; see [[bathing]] and [[apoina]]), as he had washed and laid out Patroclus (18.343–353). Then he asks the old man “for how many days are you minded to give brilliant Hector his funeral rites” (κτερεϊζέμεν, 24.657), and grants the truce Priam asks: nine days of lament, the burial on the tenth, the mound on the eleventh (24.664–670).

**The funeral.** The Trojans bring wood for nine days, burn the body on the tenth, quench the pyre with wine, lay the bones in a golden chest and heap the mound (24.784–801; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]], [[pyres]], [[larnax]] and [[tomb]]). The poem ends on the rite: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804).
