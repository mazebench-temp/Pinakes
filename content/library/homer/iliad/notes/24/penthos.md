---
summary: Zeus greets Thetis on Olympus, “grieved though you are, / with grief not to be forgotten in your heart” (24.104–105); when Cassandra calls the city to see Hector, “uncontrollable grief came upon them all” (24.708); and Andromache tells her dead husband, “you have brought accursed lamentation and grief upon your parents” (24.741).
---

The book has the word three times, each with an adjective of excess. πένθος ἄλαστον, “grief not to be forgotten”, is Thetis' grief for the son who is soon to die (24.104–105; see [[thetis]]), as it is Penelope's for Odysseus when the singer's song of the returns reminds her of him (*Odyssey* 1.342; see the translation's note on 24.105); the same adjective is the curse with which Achilles refused Hector's offer of agreements, “Hector, accursed one” (22.261). ἀάσχετον πένθος, “uncontrollable grief”, takes the whole city when Cassandra sees her father coming with the body (24.707–708; see [[cassandra]]), with the adjective of Zeus' word for Hera's fury, “uncontrollable and unyielding” (5.892; see the translation's note on 24.708).

Andromache's verse is the one with which Euphorbus reproached Menelaus for killing his brother, “and brought accursed lamentation and grief upon his parents” (17.37), said now to Hector of his own parents (24.741; see [[andromache]] and [[accursed]]). Walter Leaf notes the sudden change from the third person, “the people mourn him”, to the second (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.741), and doubts that ἀρητός can mean “accursed” (see the translation's note on 17.37). In the laments the grief is given form: the singers lead the dirges, the women groan in answer, and the three women of Hector's house lead the lamentation in turn (24.719–776; see [[lament]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]).
