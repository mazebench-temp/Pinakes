summary: Thetis has “griefs past measure in my heart” (24.91), and asks her son how long, “mourning and grieving”, he will eat out his heart (24.128–129). Achilles answers Priam with the lot the gods have spun for mortals, “to live in grief” (24.526): let them leave their sorrows “to lie quiet in our hearts, for all our grief” (24.523), since “you will achieve nothing by grieving for your own son” (24.550). He keeps the body out of Priam's sight, lest “in the grief of his heart” the old man should not hold back his anger (24.584), and Helen weeps for herself, “grieving at heart” (24.773).
grc: [ἀχνυμένοις, ἀχνυμένη]
---

**The mother's grief.** Thetis, sitting among the sea goddesses and weeping for her son's fate, answers Zeus' summons with shame and grief together (24.83–91; see [[thetis]] and [[aidos]]), and Zeus greets her as one who is grieved (24.104–105; see [[penthos]]). In the hut she finds him “groaning without ceasing” (24.123), and asks him how long, ὀδυρόμενος καὶ ἀχεύων, “mourning and grieving”, he will eat out his heart (24.128–129; see [[kradie]] and [[fasting]]).

**The consolation.** Achilles' answer to Priam puts grief in the order of things. “Let us leave our sorrows / to lie quiet in our hearts, for all our grief”, he says when he has raised him (24.522–523), with the verb with which he once let his anger at Agamemnon be, as done and past (ἐάσομεν, 16.60; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.523). The reason is the gods' spinning: they spun the thread for wretched mortals ζώειν ἀχνυμένοις, “to live in grief”, while they themselves are ἀκηδέες, “free from care” (24.525–526; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]); Leaf prefers the variant ἀχνυμένους, the regular Homeric construction, which has respectable support, and takes the dative for a natural corruption (on 24.526; see the translation's note on 24.526). The speech comes back to it at the end: “for you will achieve nothing by grieving for your own son, / nor will you raise him up” (24.550–551; see [[algos]]).

**Grief and anger.** Grief in Homer is close to anger, and Achilles fears both. He has the body washed out of Priam's sight, “lest in the grief of his heart he should not hold back his anger / when he saw his son, and Achilles' own heart be stirred” (24.584–585; see [[cholos]], [[kradie]] and [[etor]]). Helen's lament ends ἀχνυμένη κῆρ, “grieving at heart”, for herself as well as for Hector (24.773; see [[helen]] and [[ker-heart]]).
