summary: Priam's supplication of Achilles has every part of the scene: he comes in unseen and clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands (24.477–479), makes his appeal by Achilles' father (24.486–506), and is heard, first with tears and then with the body (24.507–595).

  - 24.468-517 | Priam supplicates Achilles
---

**Approach and gesture.** The suppliant who has a god to guide him comes into the hut unseen, stands close, and clasps the knees, and to the gesture of the scene adds his own: he kisses “the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons” (24.477–479; [[man-slaying]]). The simile that follows puts the host's amazement into the scene (24.480–484).

**Appeal.** The claim is not a past service but a likeness: “Remember your father, Achilles, like the gods, / who is of my age” (24.486–487; [[like-the-immortals]]). Hermes had told Priam to beg by Achilles' father, mother and child (24.465–467); Priam speaks of the father alone, and of his own sons, and he ends on what he has done, “to put to my lips the hand of the man who killed my sons” (24.503–506; [[upon-the-earth]]).

**Answer.** Achilles takes the old man's hand and gently pushes him away, and both weep (24.507–512); then he raises him by the hand (24.515). The suppliant is accepted, but the acceptance has its warning: Achilles will not stand to be pressed, “suppliant though you are” (24.560–570). Lycaon in Book 21 had the same gesture and was refused (21.64–119); Priam is the last suppliant of the poem, and the only one of Achilles' suppliants who is heard.
