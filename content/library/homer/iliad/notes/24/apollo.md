summary: Apollo keeps all defilement from Hector's body, pitying him though he is dead, and covers it with the golden aegis. On the twelfth day he protests to the gods that they will not save the corpse of a man who burned them so many offerings, and condemns Achilles, who has lost all pity; Hera answers him, and taunts him with the lyre he played at the wedding of Thetis. In Achilles' story of Niobe he kills her six sons with arrows from his silver bow, and Hecuba says that Hector lies like one whom Apollo has killed with his gentle arrows.

**Guarding the body.** Each day Achilles leaves Hector face down in the dust after dragging him around the mound of Patroclus (24.14–18; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]), and each day Apollo undoes the harm:

{{quote:2f9d31dc-4230-50d2-8d8c-e05a47a8e873}}

In Book 23 he brought a dark cloud over the body to keep the sun from it, while Aphrodite kept the dogs away and anointed it “so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him” (23.185–191), the clause that ends 24.21 too (see the note on Book 23 and [[aphrodite]]). The aegis is the one Zeus gave him in Book 15 to rout the Achaeans (15.229–230), which “the smith / Hephaestus gave to Zeus to carry, to put men to rout” (15.309–310), and with which he threw down the Achaean wall (15.360–361; see [[aegis]]). Only here is it golden; Athena's has a hundred tassels “all of gold” (2.448). The ancient critics athetized 24.20–21: the lines were not needed, contact with a corpse did not suit the sanctity of the aegis, a shield could not be wrapped around a body, the aegis was Zeus' and not Apollo's, and Book 23 had kept the body by other means. Walter Leaf finds the reasons sufficient, except that the aegis is a skin in Homer and could be so wrapped, and thinks the passage old enough to have been the model for 23.186–187; Aristarchus read the accusative, αἰγίδα χρυσείην (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.20; see the translation's note on 24.20–21). ἐλεαίρων … καὶ τεθνηότα περ, “pitying the man, / though he was dead”: Apollo's pity is the first of the book's (see [[pity]]), and the gods share it at once (24.23).

**The protest.** On the twelfth dawn after Hector's death Apollo speaks among the immortals (24.31–32; see [[the-gods-debate]]):

{{quote:ce7b5bb7-59f3-5f0f-8000-db9ff71babbd}}

σχέτλιοι, “hard”, is the reproach for those who will not give way, said of Zeus by Agamemnon and of Achilles by Ajax (2.112, 9.628–630); only here is it said to the gods together (see [[hard]]). δηλήμονες, “destructive”, occurs elsewhere in Homer only of King Echetus, “the maimer of all mortals”, in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 18.85, 18.116, 21.308), and Calypso begins her protest to Hermes in the same words, σχέτλιοί ἐστε, θεοί, ζηλήμονες ἔξοχον ἄλλων, “you are hard, you gods, and jealous beyond all others” (*Odyssey* 5.118); Leaf cannot say which passage imitates the other (on 24.33). The claim is that of the sacrifices, the one Chryses made in Apollo's own name, “if ever I burned for you the fat thigh-pieces / of bulls and goats” (1.40–41), and Zeus for Hector in Book 22, “who has burned many thighs of oxen for me” (22.170; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[sacrifice]]). What the gods owe Hector is burial: that his family and his people may see him and burn him and give him funeral rites (24.35–38; see [[cremation]]).

Against Hector stands “deadly Achilles”, whose mind cannot be bent, who “knows savage ways, like a lion” (24.39–43; see [[savage-as-a-lion]]), and “has lost all pity, and he has no shame” (24.44; see [[achilles]] and [[aidos]]). Leaf thinks 42–45 probably interpolated, the lion an imitation of a simile in Book 17 (on 24.42). Verse 45, on shame “which greatly harms men and helps them”, was athetized in antiquity as taken from Hesiod, and it is a verse of the *Works and Days* (318; Leaf on 24.45; see the translation's note on 24.42–45). Other men lose a brother or a son and leave off weeping, “for the Fates have put an enduring heart in men” (24.49; see [[fates]]). Apollo ends with a threat and a verdict: “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.53–54; see [[nemesis]], [[aeikizo]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Leaf renders κωφὴν γαῖαν “the senseless clay” (on 24.54).

**Hera's taunt.** Hera answers him as “god of the silver bow” (24.56; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]) and ends with his part at the wedding of Thetis: “And all you gods came to the wedding; and you among them / feasted with your lyre, friend of the wicked, faithless always” (24.62–63; see [[hera]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). The lyre is the one he played at the gods' feast at the end of Book 1 (1.603), and Leaf notes that Pindar too tells of the wedding feast (on 24.63). The taunt has a sequel in the poem: Achilles knows from his mother that he will perish by the arrows of Apollo (21.277–278), and Hector, dying, foretells that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy him at the Scaean gates (22.359–360). Aeschylus made the charge Thetis' own. In lines that Plato's Socrates quotes, and would not let teachers use in educating the young, Thetis remembers how Apollo, singing at her wedding, foretold long lives free of sickness for her children and raised the paean, and how she trusted that Phoebus' divine mouth was free of falsehood; but he who sang, who was present at the feast, who said these things, is the one who killed her son (*Republic* 2.383a–b; see [[plato]] and [[aeschylus]]). Zeus answers Hera, not Apollo, and the god says no more in the book.

**Niobe's sons.** In Achilles' story Apollo kills the six sons of Niobe, “The sons Apollo killed with arrows from his silver bow, / in his anger with Niobe” (24.605–606), and Artemis the six daughters, because Niobe compared herself with their mother Leto; “and so those two, though they were only two, destroyed them all” (24.609; see [[the-children-of-niobe]], [[artemis]] and [[leto]]). His anger, χωόμενος, is the word of his anger at the start of the poem (1.44; see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

**The gentle arrows.** Hecuba ends her lament with Apollo: Hector lies in her halls fresh as dew, “like one whom Apollo, god of the silver bow, / has come upon and killed with his gentle arrows” (24.758–759; see [[hecuba]]). The second verse is a favorite of the *Odyssey* and is found nowhere else in the Iliad (Leaf on 24.759). In the *Odyssey* Apollo's gentle arrows kill Menelaus' helmsman at the steering oar (*Odyssey* 3.279–281), and on the island of Syrie, when men grow old, Apollo comes with Artemis and kills them so (*Odyssey* 15.409–411); Artemis' arrows bring the same death to women (*Odyssey* 11.172–173, 11.198–199; see [[artemis]]). It is a sudden death without lingering sickness (*Odyssey* 11.172, 15.407–408), and Hecuba's likeness makes the body that Achilles dragged look like the body of such a death, the work of the gods' care (24.749–750). Leaf would read the generalizing subjunctive καταπέφνῃ for κατέπεφνεν (on 24.759; see the translation's note on 24.759).
