summary: Zeus sends Iris twice. She leaps into the sea between Samos and Imbros, finds Thetis in her cave among the goddesses of the sea, and leads her up to Olympus. Then she goes to Priam, who sits in the dung of his courtyard among his weeping sons, and gives him the message of Zeus softly, in Zeus' own words: he is to ransom Hector, alone with an older herald, and have no fear, for Hermes will escort him and Achilles will spare a suppliant. Priam has seen her face to face, and goes.

**Down to the sea.** Zeus has asked that one of the gods call Thetis to him (24.74–76; see [[zeus]]), and the verse of Book 8 sends Iris off: “So he spoke, and storm-footed Iris rose to carry the message” (24.77 = 8.409; again 24.159). It is the only verse in Homer with ἀελλόπος, “storm-footed” (see [[storm-footed]]). She leaps into the sea “between Samos and rocky Imbros”, Samothrace and its neighbor (24.78; see [[samothrace]] and [[imbros]]), “and the water groaned over her” (24.79). Walter Leaf takes λίμνη, beside πόντος, for the open expanse of water, and reports that ancient commentators took μείλανι πόντῳ for a proper name, the Black Gulf north of the Thracian Chersonese, which he calls absurd (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.79; see the translation's note on 24.79). She goes down “like a lead weight” on a fisherman's line (24.80–82; see [[the-lead-weight]]), and finds Thetis in a hollow cave, weeping for her son among the goddesses of the sea (24.83–86; see [[thetis]] and [[nereids]]). Her message is a single verse: “Rise, Thetis; Zeus calls you, whose counsels are imperishable” (24.88). Thetis veils herself in black and goes, and Iris leads the way, “swift Iris, wind-footed” (24.95; see [[wind-footed]] and [[swift-iris]]); the wave parts around them, and from the shore they shoot up to the sky (24.96–97). In Book 18 Thetis went up to Olympus alone, to ask Hephaestus for arms (18.145–148); only here is she summoned, and only here does Iris go down into the sea.

**To Priam.** Zeus then sends her to Priam with the whole message, the ransom, the herald, the wagon and the escort (24.143–158), and the verse sends her off again (24.159). “And she came to the house of Priam, and found there cries and lament” (24.160; see [[priams-palace]] and [[lament]]): Priam in the courtyard, wrapped close in his cloak with dung on his head and neck, his sons weeping around him, his daughters and his sons' wives mourning through the house (24.161–168; see [[priam]]).

{{quote:df0ba302-8400-5253-bd5a-863e2408cd9b}}

τυτθόν, “softly”: Leaf takes it that she lowers her divinely loud voice, and reports the scholia's remark that Priam trembles all the same, since a soul that has suffered reckons every future worse than the present (on 24.170). Her greeting borrows the Dream's in Book 2, “I am a messenger to you from Zeus, / who, though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you” (24.173–174 = 2.26–27; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[messengers-of-zeus]]); the Dream lied in Zeus' name, Iris does not. Then she gives the rest of Zeus' message nearly word for word, with “you” for “him” (24.175–187, from 24.146–158; Leaf on 24.175; see [[message-repeated]]). Leaf thinks the last seven verses wrongly repeated, since Priam later knows nothing of the promised escort and does not recognize his guide when he meets him (on 24.181; see the translation's note on 24.181–187). “So saying, swift-footed Iris went away” (24.188), her parting verse in Books 8, 11 and 18 (8.425, 11.210, 18.202).

**Face to face.** Priam tells Hecuba that “an Olympian messenger has come to me from Zeus” (24.194; see the translation's note on 24.194), and when she cries out against his going he answers that if any man had bidden him, a seer or a priest, they would have called it a lie, “but now, since I myself heard the goddess and looked on her face to face, / I will go” (24.223–224; see [[hecuba]], [[divination]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Iris came to him in her own form. In Book 2 she came to the Trojans' assembly in the likeness of his son Polites, and Hector knew the goddess's word all the same (2.786–807), and in Book 3 she called Helen to the wall in the likeness of Laodice (3.121–124). This is her last errand in the poem.
