summary: The poem's last ransom. Zeus ends the gods' quarrel over the body by deciding that Achilles “may receive gifts from Priam and release Hector” (24.76); Thetis tells her son, “accept a ransom for the body” (24.137), and he answers, “let him who brings the ransom carry off the body” (24.139). When it is done, he tells the dead Patroclus that “the ransom he gave me is not unseemly” (24.594).

**Ransom, not theft.** The gods who pity Hector urge Hermes to steal the body (24.23–24, 24.109), and Zeus sets the theft aside for a ransom (24.71–76; see [[hermes]] and [[zeus]]). What he grants Achilles in this he calls κῦδος, “glory” (24.110). Walter Leaf explains it as a solace to Achilles' honor, the receipt of gifts for the body instead of having to give it up “unbought, unransomed”, as Chryseis was given back (ἀπριάτην ἀνάποινον, 1.99), since the whole of Book 9 shows that the heroic point of honor lay in receiving a return (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.110; see [[kydos]] and [[time]]). Thetis gives the order in one line and Achilles accepts it in the next (24.137–139); Leaf takes ὃς ἄποινα φέροι as virtually “if anyone brings a ransom” (on 24.139).

**Release and ransom.** The verb of the book is λύω. In the active it is Achilles' part, to release the body (24.76, 24.115–116, 24.136–137, 24.555, 24.561, 24.593, 24.599); in the middle it is the father's, to ransom it: λύσασθαι φίλον υἱόν, “to ransom his dear son”, in Zeus' charge to Thetis and to Iris, in Iris' message, in Priam's report to Hecuba, and in the narrator's voice when Priam chooses the ransom (24.118, 24.146, 24.175, 24.195, 24.237). Priam's plea ends with Chryses' participle, λυσόμενος, “to ransom him” (24.502; 1.13; see [[boundless-ransom]] and [[book-1-and-book-24]]). Achilles puts Priam to bed outside for fear that word of him would bring “delay in the ransoming of the body” (λύσιος, 24.655); the noun is found only here in the Iliad, and in the Odyssey Odysseus seeks a “release” from death for his companions and himself (θανάτου λύσιν, *Odyssey* 9.421).

**Gifts and ransom.** Zeus' message speaks of gifts, δῶρα, that may warm Achilles' heart (24.119, 24.147, 24.176, 24.196; see [[doron]]); the narrator and Priam call them ἄποινα when they are loaded, offered and taken (24.276, 24.502, 24.579). In Book 22 Achilles had refused any ransom for the body, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold” (22.349–354; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). Here he takes it as the gods decided: Priam brings the ransom himself, and Achilles' companions take it from the wagon, leaving two mantles and a tunic to wrap the body (24.578–581; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]).

The Alexandrians athetized 594–595: Achilles gives up the body at Zeus' command, they argued, and would not have bartered his vengeance for Patroclus for gifts. Leaf finds the arguments insufficient, since Zeus' command was itself conditional on the ransom (24.119, 24.137–139), and only the ransom lets Achilles plead that the surrender is not dishonorable; how Patroclus was to be given his share is unknown, but Leaf takes Andromache's words to mean that clothing would be burned in a dead man's honor (22.510–514), and compares the pyre filled with good things that Odysseus promises the dead (*Odyssey* 11.30–31; on 24.594; see the translation's note on 24.594 and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The objection is older than the Alexandrians: Plato would not have the young hear that Achilles was so fond of money that he took a price for a corpse and released it, and would not otherwise (*Republic* 3.390e–391a, where the ransom is τιμή, “a price”; see [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]] and [[time]]).

**Three times as much.** Hermes, waking Priam before dawn, puts a price on the old man himself: “And now you have ransomed your dear son, and given much for him; / but for you, alive, three times as much ransom would be given / by your sons who are left behind” (24.685–687), if Agamemnon learned that he was in the camp (see [[hermes]]). Lycaon, ransomed for the second time, had fetched “three times as much” (21.80; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]).
