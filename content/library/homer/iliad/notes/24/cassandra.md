summary: Cassandra is the first in Troy to see Priam bringing Hector home. Like golden Aphrodite, she has gone up onto Pergamus; she sees her father in the chariot with the herald and the body on the bier, and she wails aloud and calls the city out to look on Hector (24.697–706).

**The first to see.** “And no one else, / of the men or the lovely-girdled women, knew them first” (24.697–698; see [[lovely-girdled]]). She has gone up onto Pergamus, the height of the city (see [[pergamus]]), as Andromache went up to the great tower when the Trojans were hard pressed in Book 6, and to the wall when she heard the wailing in Book 22 (6.386–389, 22.462–463; see [[andromache]]). She sees “her dear father / standing in the chariot, and the herald, crier of the city”, ἀστυβοώτης, a word found only here, which Walter Leaf takes as the same title as καλήτωρ, “crier” (24.577; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.701; see [[idaeus]] and [[heralds]]), and she sees Hector “lying on the bier, drawn by the mules” (24.702; see [[mules]]).

**Like golden Aphrodite.** ἰκέλη χρυσῇ Ἀφροδίτῃ is said of Briseis when she sees the body of Patroclus (19.282; see [[briseis]]), and in the Odyssey of Penelope, “like Artemis or golden Aphrodite” (*Odyssey* 17.37, 19.54; see [[golden]] and [[aphrodite]]). In Book 13 Cassandra is “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (13.365).

**The cry.** “and then she wailed aloud, and called out through all the city” (24.703): κώκυσεν, the cry of Hecuba when she saw her son dragged (22.407; see [[lament]]). ὄψεσθε, “Go and see”, is an aorist imperative, Leaf explains, as an appeal with “if ever” requires (on 24.704; see the translation's note on 24.704). μάχης ἐκνοστήσαντι, “when he came home … from battle” (24.705), is the homecoming for which Andromache had a bath heated in Book 22, μάχης ἐκ νοστήσαντι (22.444; see [[nostos]]); “for he was a great joy to the city and to all the people” (24.706), as Hecuba called him the glory of the Trojans and their women (22.433–435; see [[kydos]]). At her cry not a man or a woman is left in the city (24.707–708; see [[the-laments-for-hector]]).

**No seer.** The scholia remark, Leaf reports, that nothing here shows the gift of prophecy that the later epic cycle gave her. Leaf adds that nothing here is inconsistent with it either, and that the poet's choice of Cassandra, for a reason we cannot explain, to be the first to see the body may have suggested it to later poets (on 24.699; see the translation's note on 24.699). The Iliad names her only here and in Book 13 (13.366).
