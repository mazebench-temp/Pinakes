---
summary: Priam calls Hecuba into the chamber where his treasures are kept and tells her of the message, “Strange one, an Olympian messenger has come to me from Zeus” (24.194).
---

δαιμονίη is here without reproach. Walter Leaf hears in it pity for Hecuba in view of the blow that is about to fall, and finds the usual tone of remonstrance, as in Zeus' address to Hera (1.561), absent, unless Priam assumes it because he expects her to oppose him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.194; see [[hecuba]] and [[priam]]). Hector used the address to Andromache when he left her for the last time, “Strange one, do not grieve too much in your heart for me” (6.486; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). The Olympian messenger is Iris, not Zeus (see the translation's note on 24.194 and [[iris]]).

The book has no other δαίμων, but it has the unnamed god the word describes. Priam, meeting a stranger at the river, says, “But still some god has held his hand over me too” (24.374), and Achilles knows that “one of the gods led you to the swift ships of the Achaeans” (24.564); neither knows that the god is Hermes (see [[hermes]] and [[divine-intervention]]).
