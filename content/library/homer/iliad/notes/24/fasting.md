summary: Thetis asks her son how long he will eat out his heart, “remembering neither food / nor bed” (24.129–130). Achilles, who would not eat in Book 19, now urges food on Priam with the example of Niobe, who “remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.613), and Priam, after the meal, says that he has eaten for the first time since Hector's death: “before now I had tasted nothing” (24.641–642).

**The mother's advice.** The book opens with the army going to supper and to sleep, and Achilles alone without either (24.2–5; see [[hypnos]]). On the twelfth day Thetis finds his companions making ready the morning meal around him and asks how long, mourning and grieving, he will eat out his heart, “remembering neither food / nor bed” (24.123–130; see [[kradie]] and [[achos]]); it is good to lie with a woman, for his death is near (24.130–132; see [[philotes]]). For οὔτε some of the best manuscripts have οὐδέ, “taking no thought even for food”, Aristarchus' reading, required by his obelus on 130–132 (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.129; see the translation's note on 24.129–132). Achilles does not answer her about food.

**Niobe remembered food.** When the body has been released, it is Achilles who speaks for the meal, as Odysseus once spoke to him: “but now let us remember supper” (24.601). His reason is a story, the only mention of Niobe in Homer (24.602–617; see [[niobe]], [[the-children-of-niobe]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Her twelve children were killed, and they lay unburied for nine days; yet she “remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.613), and now, turned to stone on Sipylus, she still broods over her sorrows. The Alexandrians athetized 614–617 as inconsistent with her eating; Leaf defends them, and gives the connection of thought: do not abstain from food as if to eat were to slight the mourner's duty, since even Niobe ate, and her grief was afterwards made lasting in stone; so you may eat now, and mourn your son hereafter (on 24.614; see the translation's notes on 24.602 and 24.613). Achilles says it himself: “But come, let us two also think, brilliant old sir, / of food; and then you may weep again for your dear son” (24.618–619; see [[geron]]).

**Priam eats.** Achilles slaughters a sheep and serves the meat, and they eat until they have put away the desire for drink and food (24.621–628; see [[xenia]] and [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). Then Priam asks for bed, and tells why he needs it: he has not slept since his son was killed, but lay in the dung of his courtyard, “Now at last I have tasted food and let sparkling wine / go down my throat; before now I had tasted nothing” (24.637–642; see [[priam]]). πεπάσμην, “I had tasted”, is found only here; it recalls Zeus' words for Achilles in Book 19, “unfed and has tasted nothing” (ἄπαστος, 19.346). The man who would not eat while Patroclus lay unavenged is the one who gives the mourning father food.
