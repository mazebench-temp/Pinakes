summary: The book begins with sleep refused: the others think of “taking their fill of sweet sleep”, but Achilles weeps, “and sleep / that subdues all did not take him” (24.3–5). Hermes carries the wand that “wakes others who are sleeping” (24.344) and pours sleep on the sentries (24.445); Priam, fed, asks to be put to bed, “so that at last / we may lie down and take our fill of sweet sleep” (24.635–636); and while gods and men sleep all night, “sleep did not take hold of Hermes, the great helper” (24.679).
grc: [ὑπνώοντας, κοιμηθέντες]
---

**Sleepless.** While the army goes to supper and to sleep, Achilles weeps for Patroclus and tosses from side to side, then rises and wanders by the sea until dawn (24.1–13; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). πανδαμάτωρ, “that subdues all”, is said of sleep only here in the Iliad (24.5). The iterative verbs show, as Walter Leaf notes, that the sleepless nights and the dragging of Hector are repeated over several days, though the opening seems to describe a single night (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.12; see the translation's note on 24.12 and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

**The god of the night.** Hermes takes the wand “with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping” (24.343–344), lines that return in the Odyssey when he flies to Calypso and when he leads the suitors' souls to Hades (*Odyssey* 5.47–48, 24.3–4; Leaf on 24.339); here, as Leaf observes, the wand is used, when he pours sleep over the sentries at the ditch (24.445; see [[hermes]] and [[night-watch]]). His first question to Priam is why he drives through the night “when other mortals are asleep” (24.363, as 10.83; see [[when-other-mortals-are-asleep]]).

**The guest's sleep.** After the meal Priam asks to be put to bed: he has not closed his eyes since Hector died, lying in the dung of his courtyard (24.635–640; see [[fasting]] and [[priam]]). The end of his request, ὄφρα καὶ ἤδη / ὕπνῳ ὕπο γλυκερῷ ταρπώμεθα κοιμηθέντες, is spoken in the Odyssey by Telemachus to Menelaus and by Odysseus to Penelope (*Odyssey* 4.294–295, 23.254–255); Aristarchus read παυσώμεθα, “rest”, for ταρπώμεθα, “take our fill”, thinking it unseemly for Priam to speak of delight, and Leaf doubts that the verb can mean “rest” by itself (on 24.635; see the translation's note on 24.635). He and the herald sleep in the porch, Achilles in the inner part of the hut beside Briseis (24.673–676; see [[xenia]]).

**The one who wakes.** The book's last night opens as Book 2 and Book 10 open: “Now the other gods and the men who fight from chariots / slept all night long, overcome by soft sleep” (24.677–678; 2.1, 10.2; Leaf on 24.673). In Book 2 it was Zeus who lay awake, pondering how to honor Achilles, and in Book 10 Agamemnon; here it is Hermes, pondering how to bring Priam out unseen (24.679–681). He stands over Priam's head, as the Dream stood over Agamemnon's (24.682; 2.20, 2.59; see [[dream-scene]]), and wakes him: “Old man, you have no thought of any evil, so soundly do you still sleep / among enemy men” (24.683–684).
