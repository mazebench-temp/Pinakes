summary: Achilles cannot sleep for grief, and at each dawn drags Hector three times around the mound of Patroclus, until Thetis brings him the will of Zeus and he consents to give up the body for a ransom (24.1–142). When Priam comes to his hut by night and kisses his hands, he weeps with him for his own father and for Patroclus, raises him, and consoles him with the two jars of Zeus and the fortune of Peleus; he flares up when Priam will not sit, then takes the ransom, has the body washed and lays it on the bier himself, asks Patroclus' pardon, tells the story of Niobe and eats with Priam. The two men marvel at each other, and Achilles grants eleven days of truce and sleeps in his hut with Briseis beside him (24.468–676).

**The sleepless nights.** The games are over, and the army goes to supper and sleep, “but Achilles” (αὐτὰρ Ἀχιλλεύς, 24.3). Walter Leaf thinks it not unlikely that these words once followed the heaping of the mound, before the games were added (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.1; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

{{quote:e6471507-0efc-55c9-bf1e-cdb7076522b5}}

ὕπνος … πανδαμάτωρ, “sleep that subdues all”, is said only once more in Homer, of the sleep that takes the drunken Cyclops (*Odyssey* 9.372–373; see [[hypnos]] and [[sleepless]]). ἀνδροτής, “manhood”, is elsewhere only in the verse of the souls of Patroclus and Hector, “leaving manhood and youth” (16.857 = 22.363; see [[patroclus]]). The things he wound out with Patroclus, “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (24.8), are told in the Odyssey's words (*Odyssey* 8.183, 13.91, 13.264), and the Alexandrians athetized 24.6–9 as feeble; Leaf finds the case strong but not decisive, since the Odyssean color belongs to the whole book (on 24.6; see the translation's note on 24.6–9). The iterative verbs show that this is not one night but many (Leaf on 24.12; see the translation's note on 24.12). He turns on his side, on his back, face down (πρηνής), and wanders along the shore, where he sat weeping when Briseis was taken and lay groaning on the night of the ghost (1.348–350, 23.59–61; see [[shore]]).

**The dragging.** “But whenever he had yoked his swift horses under the chariot”, he would tie Hector behind it, drag him three times around the mound, rest in his hut, and leave him face down in the dust (24.14–18), as when he first brought him to the ships he stretched him face down beside the bier of Patroclus (23.24–26). Apollo keeps all defilement from the body and covers it with the golden aegis, “so in his rage he defiled brilliant Hector” (24.18–22; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[aegis]] and [[aeikizo]]).

**As the gods see him.** On the twelfth dawn Apollo speaks of him to the gods. They choose to help ὀλοῷ Ἀχιλῆϊ, “deadly Achilles”, a man with no fitting mind “nor any thought / in his breast that can be bent”, who goes after his prey like a lion yielding to his own strength (24.39–43; see [[apollo]], [[lion]] and [[savage-as-a-lion]]); Phoenix had told him that “even the gods themselves can be turned” (9.497; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]). “So Achilles has lost all pity, and he has no shame” (24.44): ἔλεος, “pity”, is found only here in Homer (see [[pity]] and [[aidos]]). Other men leave off weeping when they have lost a brother or a son, but he drags Hector around his companion's mound; “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.46–54; see [[nemesis]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Hera answers that he is θεᾶς γόνος, “the offspring of a goddess”, whose mother she herself nursed and gave to Peleus (24.56–63; see [[hera]] and [[thetis]]). Zeus grants that Hector too was dear to the gods, but the body cannot be stolen, “unknown to Achilles; for his mother / is always beside him, night and day alike” (24.72–73); let Thetis come, so that Achilles “may receive gifts from Priam and release Hector” (24.75–76; see [[zeus]]).

**His mother's message.** Iris finds Thetis in her cave, weeping for her son, “who was to / perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (24.85–86), in the verse of Sarpedon's fate (16.461; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). On Olympus Zeus tells her of the nine days' quarrel “about Hector's corpse and Achilles, sacker of cities” (24.108; see [[sacker-of-cities]]), and says: “but I bestow this glory on Achilles” (24.110). Walter Leaf takes the κῦδος to be the gifts, which let him give up the body without loss of honor (on 24.110; see [[kydos]] and [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). She is to tell him that the gods are angry, and Zeus above all, “because in his raging heart / he keeps Hector beside the curved ships” (24.112–116). She finds him in his hut, “groaning without ceasing”, while his companions make ready their morning meal (24.122–125; see [[hut]]). She sits close to him, “and stroked him with her hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (24.127), the verse with which she came to him on the shore in the first book (1.361). Her words are a mother's: “My child, how long, mourning and grieving, / will you eat out your heart, remembering neither food / nor bed? It is good even to lie with a woman / in love” (24.128–131; see [[fasting]]). Aristarchus obelized 130–132 as advice unfit for a mother (Leaf on 24.129; see the translation's note on 24.129–132). Her reason is the end she has foretold since the first book (1.417, 18.95–96; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]): “for you will not live long for me, but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (24.131–132). Patroclus said the same to Hector as he died, “you yourself will not live long, but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (16.852–853). Then she gives the message, in words the Dream once used to Agamemnon, “I am a messenger to you from Zeus” (24.133 ≈ 2.26), and he consents at once: “So be it: let him who brings the ransom carry off the body, / if the Olympian himself so bids with a ready heart” (24.139–140; see [[apoina]] and [[obedience-and-persuasion]]). She tells him the end Zeus wants but not the means, that Priam himself will come (24.117–119); so, Leaf observes, Achilles is amazed when he sees him (on 24.181; see [[the-killer-in-exile]]).

**As others see him.** Zeus' charge to Iris draws his portrait: Achilles will not kill the suppliant, nor let others kill him, “for he is not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender, / but with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (24.157–158 = 24.186–187). Leaf reports a scholiast's remark that the three adjectives name the three causes of cruelty, stupidity, thoughtlessness and malice (on 24.157). To Hecuba he is ὠμηστὴς καὶ ἄπιστος, “that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man”, who will not pity Priam or respect him (24.207–208; see [[hecuba]]). ὠμηστής is said nowhere else of a man, Leaf notes, “we might almost translate *a beast of prey*” (on 24.207; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]), and Hecuba wishes she could fix her teeth in his liver (24.212–213), as he wished he could carve Hector's flesh and eat it raw (22.346–347). In Hermes' tale he is the leader who “would not let us fight, being angry with the son of Atreus”, and the speaker his attendant (24.394–396; see [[polyctor]] and [[menis]]); he drags Hector “ruthlessly” every dawn, “yet he does not disfigure him” (24.416–418), and the young stranger fears him too much to take a gift behind his back (24.433–436; see [[hermes]]). The bar of his gate took three men to drive home, “but Achilles would drive it home even alone” (24.453–456), as he alone could wield his father's spear (16.141–142 = 19.388–389; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).

**Priam at his knees.** Priam goes straight to the house, “where Achilles, dear to Zeus, used to sit”, and finds him just finished with his meal, with Automedon and Alcimus busy beside him and the table still in its place (24.471–476; see [[dear-to-zeus]], [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]). The man who would let nothing go down his throat while Patroclus lay unavenged has eaten (19.205–214, 19.303–308). Priam clasps his knees and kisses his hands, “the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons” (24.478–479): χεῖρας … ἀνδροφόνους, the hands he laid on Patroclus' breast when he led the lament (18.317 = 23.18; see [[man-slaying]] and [[supplication]]). “So Achilles was amazed when he saw godlike Priam” (24.483; see [[thambos]]). Priam calls him θεοῖς ἐπιείκελʼ Ἀχιλλεῦ, “Achilles, like the gods” (24.486), as Phoenix, Hector and the ghost of Patroclus called him (9.485, 22.279, 23.80), and bids him remember his father (24.486–506; see [[priam]] and [[peleus]]).

{{quote:443b7a0c-5cd4-5d5e-b9eb-25cd3a50013b}}

The first verse is said in the Odyssey of Telemachus, when Menelaus' words stir in him the longing to lament his father (24.507 = *Odyssey* 4.113; see [[the-longing-for-lament]]). “Taking the old man's hand he gently pushed him away” (24.508): the suppliant's touch is broken, and the weeping begins. Achilles weeps for two men he will not see again, and Priam for the one Achilles killed (see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[tears]]).

**The consolation.** “But when brilliant Achilles had taken his fill of lament” (24.513), the satiety he asked of the ghost of Patroclus, “and take our fill of deadly lament” (23.98), he rises from his chair, raises the old man by the hand, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.515–516; see [[throne]]). The words are Priam's own, from his plea to Hector on the wall, of the old man whose head and beard the dogs disfigure (22.74; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). He begins ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch”, as Zeus spoke of Hector (24.518; 17.201; see the translation's note on 24.518), and asks how Priam dared to come “before the eyes of the man who has slain many and brave / sons of yours” (24.519–521), in Hecuba's words, but with the verb in his own person, ἐξενάριξα for her ἐξενάριξε (24.521, 24.205; see the translation's note on 24.521). “Your heart is iron”, she said of Priam, and he says it again (24.205, 24.521), as the dying Hector said of him, “for truly the heart in your breast is iron” (22.357; see [[not-stone-or-iron]]). Then he bids Priam sit: “and let us leave our sorrows / to lie quiet in our hearts, for all our grief; / for there is no profit in chilling lament” (24.522–524). ἀχνύμενοί περ, “for all our grief”, is the end of the verse in which he twice put away his anger against Agamemnon, “we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief” (18.112 = 19.65; see [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). So the gods have spun the thread for mortals, to live in grief, while they are free from care (24.525–526), and he tells of the jars on the floor of Zeus, from which he gives some men a mixture and some evils only (24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). His own father is his example. The gods gave Peleus splendid gifts, wealth, rule over the Myrmidons and a goddess for a wife, in the verse that named the horses and the armor as the gods' gifts to him (24.534 ≈ 16.381, 18.84), but only one son, παναώριον, “doomed to an untimely death”; Leaf calls the word virtually equivalent to μινυνθάδιος and ὠκύμορος, “short-lived” and “swift-fated”, the words of the first book (on 24.540; 1.352, 1.417). “Nor do I / care for him as he grows old, since very far from my native land / I sit here in Troy, bringing sorrow to you and your children” (24.540–542; see [[peleus]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Priam too was blessed once, in wealth and sons; “Bear up”, he ends, for grief will not raise Hector up, and some other evil will come first (24.543–551). Leaf denies that the last words are a threat (on 24.551).

**The warning.** Priam will not sit while Hector lies uncared for, and asks for the body at once (24.553–558). “Looking at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles said” (24.559), the verse of his answers to Agamemnon in the first book and to Hector in Book 22 (1.148; 22.260, 22.344):

{{quote:66a35e2a-02f7-5e0c-9d2c-511c6c5d822b}}

“Do not provoke me” is Agamemnon's warning to Chryses, and μή σε γέρον begins Agamemnon's threat to the old priest (1.26, 1.32; see [[book-1-and-book-24]]). Leaf finds nothing in Priam's words to account for the outburst except the struggle in Achilles himself, his sensitiveness to any word that implies hurry or doubt, and a man's natural irritation at being asked again to do what he has already, under compulsion, decided to do (on 24.560). The commands of Zeus are, in Leaf's view, the laws of Zeus who protects suppliants (on 24.570; see [[zeus]]). “So he spoke, and the old man was afraid, and obeyed his word” (24.571).

**The body released.** He springs out of the door of the house like a lion, with Automedon and Alcimus, the companions he honored most after Patroclus (24.572–576; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]). They take the ransom, leaving two mantles and a tunic for the body, and he has the slave women wash and anoint it apart, so that Priam should not see his son, “lest in the grief of his heart he should not hold back his anger / when he saw his son, and Achilles' own heart be stirred, / and he kill him, and sin against the commands of Zeus” (24.580–586; see [[hector]] and [[cholos]]). Leaf takes the last verse for an interpolation, which breaks the poet's reserve about how far Achilles' anger might go (on 24.586; see the translation's note on 24.586). “Achilles himself lifted him and laid him on a bier” (24.589); in Book 18 the Achaeans laid Patroclus on the bier, and Achilles followed weeping (18.231–236). Then he groans and speaks to his companion: let Patroclus not be angry if he hears in the house of Hades that Hector has been released, for the ransom is not unseemly, and he shall have his share (24.591–595; see [[patroclus]], [[boundless-ransom]] and [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]]).

**Niobe and the meal.** Back in the hut, on the chair by the other wall where he sat facing Odysseus on the night of the embassy (24.597–598; 9.218–219), he tells Priam that his son is released and lies on a bier, “but now let us remember supper” (24.599–601). The rule of Odysseus, that the living must eat (19.225–233; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]), is his rule now, and he gives it an example: even Niobe of the lovely hair remembered food, when Apollo and Artemis had killed her twelve children (24.602–617; see [[niobe]] and [[the-children-of-niobe]]). “But come, let us two also think, brilliant old sir, / of food; and then you may weep again for your dear son / when you have brought him into Ilios; he will be much wept for” (24.618–620). He himself slaughters the sheep, and the bread and meat are served in the verses of the embassy, with Automedon setting out the bread where Patroclus once did, “while Achilles served the meat” (24.621–626; 24.625–626 ≈ 9.216–217; see [[feast-type-scene]]). So the night ends as his mother advised: he has eaten, and he sleeps with a woman beside him (24.129–131, 24.675–676).

{{quote:c5859912-c876-567e-8646-1267cb8c16dd}}

Leaf, after Helbig, finds in these lines one of the earliest and most striking expressions of the Greek admiration for beauty, the beauty of the old man as vividly felt as that of the young (on 24.630; see [[thambos]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]).

**The truce.** He has the beds made beneath the colonnade, in the verse with which Patroclus gave the order for Phoenix's bed (24.643 ≈ 9.658; see [[hut]] and [[bed]]), and then, ἐπικερτομέων, “Taunting him”, he tells Priam to sleep outside, lest one of the counselors who sit with him come in, see him, and tell Agamemnon, “shepherd of the people”, so that there would be delay in the ransoming of the body (24.649–655). Leaf can find no taunt in the words to Priam, and takes the tone to be meant for Agamemnon, as though Achilles bitterly assumed that his enemy would thwart him at every turn (on 24.649; see the translation's note on 24.649). Then he asks how many days the funeral will take, so that he may hold back the army himself (24.656–658). He grants all that Priam asks, “for I will hold back the war for as long a time as you bid” (24.668–670; see [[truce]]), and takes the old man's right hand at the wrist, “so that he might feel no fear in his heart” (24.671–672). Leaf compares the gesture of kindliness with Odysseus taking Penelope's right hand by the wrist (on 24.671; *Odyssey* 18.258). Priam and the herald sleep in the porch, “but Achilles slept in the inner part of the well-built hut, / and beside him lay fair-cheeked Briseis” (24.673–676; see [[briseis]]).

**Last words of him.** Hermes wakes Priam with the warning that he sleeps among enemies, “since Achilles has spared you” (24.683–684). In Hecuba's lament he is the man who sold her other sons across the sea to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos, as he sold Lycaon to Lemnos (24.751–753; 21.40, 21.78–79; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[trade]]), and who dragged Hector many times around the mound of Patroclus, “and even so he did not raise him up” (24.754–756). The words answer his own to Priam, “nor will you raise him up” (24.551). Priam's last speech tells the Trojans to gather wood without fear, “for Achilles, / as he sent me from the black ships, gave orders / that they would do us no harm until the twelfth dawn comes” (24.779–781; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). That is the last mention of him in the Iliad; his death, which his mother and the dying Hector foretold, falls outside the poem (24.131–132, 22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
