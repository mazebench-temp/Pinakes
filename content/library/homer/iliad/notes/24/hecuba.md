summary: Hecuba cries out against Priam's going to the man who killed so many of her sons, an eater of raw flesh whose liver she wishes she could eat; when he will not be held back, she brings him wine in a golden cup to pour to Zeus and bids him pray for a bird of omen. When the body comes home she tears her hair at the wagon, and over it she speaks the second of the three laments: the gods cared for her son even in death, and he lies fresh as dew (24.193–227, 24.283–305, 24.710–712, 24.747–760).

**The message.** Priam goes down into the fragrant chamber of cedar and calls his wife: “Strange one, an Olympian messenger has come to me from Zeus” (24.191–194; see [[priams-palace]] and [[iris]]). δαιμονίη seems to Walter Leaf to imply pity for her, with none of the word's usual reproach (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.194; see [[strange-one]]). He asks her counsel, and tells her that his own heart bids him go (24.197–199).

**Her answer.** “So he spoke, and the woman wailed aloud and answered him” (24.200): κώκυσεν, the cry with which she saw her son dragged (22.407; see [[lament]]). Where has his mind gone, for which he was once famous among strangers and his own people? “How can you wish to go alone to the ships of the Achaeans, / before the eyes of the man who has slain many and brave / sons of yours? Your heart is iron” (24.201–205). Achilles will greet him with the same three verses when he comes (24.519–521; see [[achilles]]). ὠμηστὴς καὶ ἄπιστος ἀνήρ, “that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man” (24.207): ὠμηστής is said elsewhere in Homer of birds, dogs and fish, and Leaf notes that it is nowhere else used of a man, “we might almost translate a beast of prey” (on 24.207; 11.454, 22.67, 24.82). “he will not pity you / or respect you at all” are the words in which Hector feared to go up to Achilles unarmed (24.207–208; 22.123–124; see [[pity]] and [[aidos]]). Rather, let them weep for their son at home.

{{quote:aefc23f7-f0a6-5ada-b145-eae3d996fd75}}

The Fate who spun the thread at his birth spun it for Achilles too, in the same words: Hera said that he would suffer whatever his fate “spun for him with her thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (20.127–128; see [[fates]], [[mighty-fate]] and [[aisa]]). The wish to eat the enemy raw is Zeus' taunt to Hera, that she would eat Priam and his sons raw (4.35–36), and Achilles' own over the dying Hector (22.346–347; see [[hera]]). Leaf compares Beatrice in *Much Ado About Nothing*, who would eat her enemy's heart in the market place, and remarks that those who see in such words a trace of cannibalism must find it in Elizabethan England too (on 24.213). He would rather do without 214–216, the requital “for my son, since he did not kill him as he played the coward”, which Bekker and others reject (see the translation's note on 24.213–216).

**The wine.** Priam will not be held back: she must not be “a bird of evil omen in the halls” (24.218–219; see [[priam]]). He takes the ransom from the chests and drives the Trojans from the colonnade, and as the wagon is yoked, “Hecuba came near them with grieving heart, / holding wine sweet to the heart in her right hand / in a golden cup, so that they might pour a libation before they went” (24.283–285; see [[libation]] and [[cup]]). In the Odyssey the same verses bring Menelaus to his departing guests (24.284–286 ≈ *Odyssey* 15.148–150; Leaf on 24.284). She bids him pour to Zeus and pray to come home, and ask for a bird of omen on the right; if Zeus will not send it, she will not urge him to go (24.287–298; see [[prayer]] and [[eagle]]). Leaf reports Bergk's remark that to pray for an omen is elsewhere found only in the Odyssey; in the Iliad the signs come unasked (on 24.290). In Book 6 she offered Hector wine to pour to Zeus and drink, and he refused it, since he would not pour with unwashed hands (6.258–268). Priam answers, “Lady, I will not disobey you in this that you bid me” (24.300), washes his hands and takes the cup from his wife (24.302–305; see [[hand-washing]]).

**At the wagon.** When the body comes home, “his dear wife and his lady mother / tore their hair for him, rushing to the well-wheeled wagon / and touching his head” (24.710–712; see [[andromache]] and [[hair]]). πότνια μήτηρ, “lady mother”, is Hector's own word for her (6.264).

**Her lament.** “And among them Hecuba in turn led the unceasing lamentation” (24.747), in nearly the verse that began her lament in Book 22 (22.430; see [[the-laments-for-hector]]):

{{quote:7b991428-1dcb-5425-a823-0d2f5288a287}}

Helen will begin with the same words, of her brothers-in-law (24.762; see [[helen]]). The gods' care for the dead man is Hermes' word to Priam, “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is”, and Priam's, “so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death” (24.422–423, 24.428; see [[hermes]]); Zeus told Hera that Hector “was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios” (24.66–67; see [[zeus]]). Her other sons Achilles sold across the sea, as he sold Lycaon to Lemnos (21.40, 21.78–79; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]) and as Priam complained, “selling them off to islands far away” (22.44–45; see [[sons-of-priam]] and [[samothrace]]). But Hector he dragged around the mound of Patroclus, “and even so he did not raise him up” (24.754–756; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Leaf takes the thought to be that Achilles was harsher to Hector than to her other sons, and yet it has ended in his honor: the freshness of the body is the gods' favor (on 24.752).

{{quote:cd7b3a69-6764-530b-b625-a57a3e5cd7c6}}

ἑρσήεις, “fresh as dew”, is Hermes' word for the body (24.419; see [[fresh-as-dew]]). πρόσφατος means “fresh”: taken as “newly slain”, it would miss the point that Hector has lain twelve days (Leaf on 24.757; see the translation's note on 24.757). The gentle arrows of Apollo, and of Artemis for women, bring the sudden death without sickness of the Odyssey, where the last verse, of Apollo or of Artemis, comes four times (*Odyssey* 3.280, 5.124, 11.173, 15.411; Leaf on 24.759; see [[apollo]] and [[artemis]]). Apollo, who left Hector to his death (22.213), kept his body from harm (24.18–21). “So she spoke, weeping, and stirred up relentless lamentation” (24.760; see [[relentless]]). Of the three laments, Leaf gives hers the theme of Hector's piety and its reward (on 24.723).
