summary: For twelve days Hector's body lies in Achilles' huts, dragged each dawn around the mound of Patroclus and left face down in the dust, while Apollo keeps all defilement from it, and the gods quarrel over it (24.14–76). Zeus has Achilles give it back for a ransom; Hermes tells Priam that it lies whole and fresh as dew, its wounds closed; Achilles has it washed, anointed and dressed, and lays it on the bier himself (24.108–137, 24.411–423, 24.580–590). Priam brings it home at dawn; Cassandra sees it first, his wife and mother tear their hair over it at the gates, Andromache, Hecuba and Helen lament him, and the Trojans burn him, lay his bones in a golden chest and heap up his mound. The last line of the Iliad is his (24.697–804).

**In the dust.** Each dawn Achilles ties Hector behind his chariot, drags him three times around the mound of Patroclus, and leaves him stretched face down in the dust; Apollo, pitying the man though he is dead, keeps all defilement from his flesh and covers him with the golden aegis, “so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him” (24.14–21; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]], [[apollo]] and [[aegis]]). The last half-verse is the one in which Aphrodite anointed him in Book 23 (24.21 ≈ 23.187). “So in his rage he defiled brilliant Hector” (24.22; see [[aeikizo]] and [[brilliant]]).

**What the gods say of him.** Apollo reminds the gods that Hector burned them thighs of oxen and unblemished goats, and that they will not save even his corpse, “for his wife to see, and his mother and his child, / and his father Priam and the people, who would quickly / burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites” (24.33–38; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). What Achilles drags is κωφὴ γαῖα, “the senseless earth” (24.54; see [[the-senseless-earth]]). Hera answers that Hector is a mortal, suckled at a woman's breast (24.58), and Zeus that he was “the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios” and never failed in the gifts Zeus loves, in the two verses with which Zeus once spoke of Troy itself (24.66–70; 24.69–70 = 4.48–49; see [[zeus]]). θρασὺν Ἕκτορα, “bold Hector” (24.72), troubled an ancient reader, who would write “the corpse of Hector” instead; Walter Leaf accepts the standing epithet, even of a corpse (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.72). The gods quarrel nine days “about Hector's corpse” (Ἕκτορος ἀμφὶ νέκυι, 24.108). Leaf notes that only here, and perhaps at 17.240, is νέκυς joined to a genitive: elsewhere the corpse is the man himself, named in apposition, “for in the old Epic psychology the corpse is not a part or appendage of the man” (on 24.108; see the translation's row for νέκυς / νεκρός).

**His father and mother.** Hecuba would weep for him apart: mighty Fate spun at his birth that he should glut the dogs far from his parents; he did not die as a coward, but standing before the Trojans and the Trojan women, “with no thought of flight or of shelter” (24.208–216; see [[hecuba]] and [[moira]]). Leaf thinks 214–216 better away (on 24.213; see the translation's note on 24.213–216). To Priam he is “my best son” (24.242), and among the sons who are gone “Hector, who was a god among men, and did not seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god” (24.258–259; see [[priam]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). In the hut he is the one “who guarded the city and its people”, killed “as he fought in defense of his native land” (24.499–500), as Andromache's son was called Astyanax because Hector alone guarded Ilios (6.403); Leaf compares ἀμυνόμενον περὶ πάτρης with Hector's own words to Polydamas, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (on 24.500; 12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).

**Whole and fresh.** Hermes, as the young Myrmidon, tells Priam what the gods have done:

{{quote:ef372df7-50b9-5a9d-89b8-2c34fdb31f55}}

Twelve days the body has lain (24.413; see the translation's note on 24.413). The dogs to which Achilles promised it have not touched it (22.335–336, 22.354; see [[dogs]]), and the maggots that eat “men slain in battle” are kept from it, as Thetis kept them from Patroclus (19.24–39; see [[maggots]]). The wounds are closed, “for many drove their bronze into him” (24.421): “not one stood beside him without stabbing him” (22.371). ἐερσήεις, “fresh as dew” (24.419), comes back, as ἑρσήεις, in Hecuba's lament (24.757; see [[fresh-as-dew]]). Aristarchus obelized 24.423 as superfluous (Leaf on 24.423).

**Washed and dressed.** “Do not yet bid me sit”, Priam says, “while Hector / lies uncared for in the huts” (24.553–554). Achilles' companions take the ransom, and leave “two mantles and a well-spun tunic, / so that he could wrap the corpse and give it to be carried home” (24.580–581). Achilles has the slave women wash and anoint the body apart, lest Priam see his son and break out in anger (24.582–586; see [[achilles]]).

{{quote:c05a7cfd-9d8a-5ae2-be09-166c84e320be}}

The first two verses are the Odyssey's verses of a guest's bath, said of Odysseus among the Phaeacians and of Telemachus and Theoclymenus in Ithaca (24.587–588 ≈ *Odyssey* 8.454–455, 17.88–89; Leaf on 24.587). Patroclus' body was washed with hot water, anointed, filled with ointment and covered with linen and a white cloak (18.343–353); of the two mantles left for Hector, Leaf supposes, one covered the bier and the other was wrapped round the body (on 24.587; see the translation's note on 24.587). “Your son is released to you, old man, as you asked, / and he lies on a bier” (24.599–600; see [[bed]]), and he “will be much wept for” (πολυδάκρυτος, 24.620).

**Home.** At dawn the mules bring the corpse across the plain (24.695–697; see [[mules]]). Cassandra sees him lying on the bier, “drawn by the mules”, and calls the Trojans out to see Hector, if ever they rejoiced when he came home alive from battle, “for he was a great joy to the city and to all the people” (24.699–706; see [[cassandra]]). At the gates his wife and his mother tear their hair, rushing to the wagon “and touching his head” (24.710–712; see [[andromache]] and [[lament]]). In the house he is laid on a corded bed, with singers to lead the dirge (24.719–722).

**The laments.** Andromache holds his head and laments that he has perished young and left her a widow and their son an infant: the city will fall without “its guardian”, she and the boy will be slaves, unless an Achaean hurls the child from the tower in anger for a brother, a father or a son that Hector killed, “For your father was not kind in grim battle”; he did not die in his bed and leave her a last word (24.723–745; see [[astyanax]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]). The T scholia hear his name in ἔχες, “kept” (24.730; Leaf on 24.730; see the translation's note on 24.730), as the poem explained Astyanax's name by his father's guarding (22.507). Hecuba calls him the dearest of her children, dear to the gods even in death: dragged by Achilles, he lies fresh as dew, like a man whom Apollo has killed with his gentle arrows (24.747–759; see [[hecuba]]). Helen calls him the dearest of her brothers-in-law, who in twenty years never spoke an unkind word to her and restrained the others with his gentleness (24.761–775; see [[helen]] and [[in-laws]]). The laments are told under [[the-laments-for-hector]].

**The funeral.** For nine days the Trojans gather wood; on the tenth they carry out “bold Hector”, lay him on the top of the pyre and set fire to it; at the next dawn they quench the pyre with wine, his brothers and companions gather the white bones into a golden chest, covering them with soft purple robes, lay it in a trench under great stones, heap up the mound, and feast in Priam's house (24.784–803; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]], [[cremation]] and [[tomb]]). The verses follow the funeral of Patroclus, of which Leaf calls this one almost an abstract (on 24.791; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). The poem ends with his name: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804; see [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[the-last-line-of-the-iliad]]). A variant in the T scholia joined the last line to the opening of the *Aethiopis* (Leaf on 24.804; see the translation's note on 24.804).
