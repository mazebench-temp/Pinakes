---
summary: Hermes is “the slayer of Argus” eleven times in Book 24. The gods urge “the keen-sighted slayer of Argus” to steal Hector's body (24.24, 24.109), Zeus promises him to Priam as an escort (24.153, 24.182), and the title of the guide follows him from his consent to the sleep he pours over the sentries (24.339, 24.378, 24.389, 24.410, 24.432, 24.445).
# ἀργεϊφόντην (24.24, 24.109, 24.153): only in this book in the Iliad.
grc: [ἀργεϊφόντην]
---

**The theft refused.** Twice the title stands with [[keen-sighted]], in the gods' wish that Hermes steal the body (24.23–24) and in Zeus' report of it to Thetis (24.109). Walter Leaf notes that Homer does not know Hermes as the thievish god (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.23); Zeus will not allow it, since Thetis is always beside her son (24.71–73).

**The escort.** Zeus' promise, “for such an escort will we grant him, the slayer of Argus” (24.153), comes to Priam from Iris with the person changed, “for such an escort will go with you, the slayer of Argus” (24.182; [[message-repeated]]).

**The guide.** From his consent to Zeus' order, when “the guide, the slayer of Argus, did not disobey” (24.339), the title rarely appears without [[guide]]: four times in the verse that opens his answers to Priam, “Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to him in turn” (24.378, 24.389, 24.410, 24.432), and once as he “poured sleep over” the sentries (24.445). Between, he flies with his wand as “the mighty slayer of Argus,” κρατὺς ἀργεϊφόντης, the phrase of the story of Eudorus' birth (24.345; 16.181). At Achilles' gate he names himself, “an immortal god, / Hermes” (24.460–461), and from then on the narrator calls him Hermes, the great helper ([[great-helper]]). See [[hermes]].
