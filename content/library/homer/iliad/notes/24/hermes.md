summary: The gods want Hermes to steal Hector's body, and Zeus will not have it; instead he sends him to bring Priam unseen to Achilles. In the likeness of a young prince the god meets Priam at the river, tells him a false tale of himself and the truth about his son, drives him into the camp past the sleeping sentries, opens the gates and Achilles' door, and reveals himself before he goes; before dawn he wakes Priam and brings him back to the ford of the Xanthus.

**The theft refused.** At the start of the book the gods pity Hector “and kept urging the keen-sighted slayer of Argus to steal him away” (24.24; see [[keen-sighted]], [[argeiphontes]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). All agree but Hera, Poseidon and Athena (24.25–30). Zeus, answering Hera, sets the theft aside: “But stealing him we will let be, and there is no way to do it, / bold Hector, unknown to Achilles; for his mother / is always beside him, night and day alike” (24.71–73; see [[zeus]] and [[thetis]]); and he tells Thetis that the gods are still urging it (24.109). Aristarchus athetized 24.71–73, and Walter Leaf takes a scholion on 24.109 to show that he rejected 24.24 as well (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.23, 24.71 and 24.109; see the translation's notes on 24.23–30 and 24.71–73). Leaf adds that Homer does not otherwise know Hermes as the thievish god (on 24.23); yet in Dione's story it is Hermes who “stole Ares away” out of the bronze jar (5.390; see the note on Book 5). Instead of a theft Zeus orders a ransom, and Hermes' part in it is to guide the man who brings it (24.74–76, 24.152–158; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]).

**The escort promised.** Iris is to tell Priam to go alone with an older herald, “for such an escort will we grant him, the slayer of Argus, / who will lead him until he brings him to Achilles” (24.153–154), and she gives him the promise in the second person (24.182–183; see [[iris]] and [[priam]]). πομπός, “escort”, is the god's word for himself when he meets Priam (24.437, 24.439) and when he leaves him (24.461). Priam never speaks of the promise and does not know his guide when he meets him, and Leaf therefore takes Iris' repetition of it (24.181–187) for an addition, while keeping Zeus' words, which tell the hearer beforehand that Priam will be safe (on 24.181; see the translation's note on 24.181–187).

**Zeus' order.** When the two old men come out on the plain, Zeus sees them, pities Priam, and speaks to his son (24.331–333; see [[pity]] and [[idaeus]]):

{{quote:61f272d5-e492-5f6d-b099-ced0ce0b2acf}}

The words that bring in the speech, Ἑρμείαν υἱὸν φίλον ἀντίον ηὔδα (24.333), bring in Zeus' order to Hermes to go to Calypso (*Odyssey* 5.28). ἑταιρίσσαι, “to keep company with” (24.335), makes him, in Leaf's words, “a particularly affable god” (on 24.335; see the translation's row for ἑταιρίζω). This is the only errand on which Zeus sends Hermes in the Iliad. Elsewhere in the poem, and earlier in this book, his messenger is Iris (24.77–99, 24.143–188), and Leaf finds the passage, with its use of Hermes as messenger, “thoroughly Odyssean” (on 24.339; see also his note on 24.23).

**The sandals and the wand.**

{{quote:7a3681ef-ed27-5f34-81b0-bf4552019aa2}}

With the verse before them these are the verses of his journey to Calypso (24.339–345 = *Odyssey* 5.43–49; see [[sandals]] and [[wand]]). The sandals are also Athena's when she goes down to Ithaca (24.341–342 = *Odyssey* 1.97–98), and the wand, which charms men's eyes and wakes the sleeping, is described in the same words when he calls up the souls of the suitors (24.343–344; *Odyssey* 24.2–4); in the Odyssey he is χρυσόρραπις, “of the golden wand” (*Odyssey* 5.87, 10.277, 10.331). Leaf thinks the wand more in place here than in the Odyssey, since in his view it is used when the god pours sleep over the sentries (24.445), and he takes it for the origin of the later herald's staff, the κηρύκειον (on 24.339). κρατὺς ἀργεϊφόντης, “the mighty slayer of Argus” (24.345), is the half-verse of the story of Eudorus' birth (16.181; see the note on Book 16).

**The young prince.** He comes to Troy and the Hellespont, “and went on his way in the likeness of a young prince, / with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful” (24.346–348; see [[hellespont]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The second verse is said of him in the Odyssey too, when he meets Odysseus on Circe's island in the likeness of a young man (*Odyssey* 10.277–279). αἰσυμνητήρ, “prince”, is found only here in Homer; Leaf derives it from αἶσα and μνάομαι, “one who is mindful of justice”, and compares the αἰσυμνῆται who judge the games of the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 8.258; on 24.347). He observes that the description suits only the youthful Hermes of the great age of Greek art, since archaic art always shows the god bearded (on 24.348).

**At the river.** At nightfall Priam and the herald drive past the mound of Ilus and halt the mules and horses to drink at the river (24.349–351; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[scamander]]). The herald sees a man close by and says, “I think he will soon tear us to pieces” (24.355); they must flee on the horses, or clasp his knees (24.356–357). Priam is terrified, “and he stood amazed; but the great helper came near of himself, / and took the old man's hand” (24.360–361; see [[great-helper]]). ἐριούνιος stands alone for the god here and at 24.440, its only uses as a noun (Leaf on 24.360). His first words take the side of the travelers. Why is the old man driving “through the ambrosial night, when other mortals are asleep?” (24.363), nearly the verse with which Nestor challenged Agamemnon walking the camp at night (10.83; see [[when-other-mortals-are-asleep]]). The Achaeans are near and merciless, and what would he do if one of them saw him with so many good things, an old man with an old companion (24.364–369)? “But I will do you no harm, and I would even keep another / away from you; for you look to me like my dear father” (24.370–371). Priam answers that “still some god has held his hand over me too”, to send him so admirable a wayfarer, “one of good omen”, who must “come of blessed parents” (24.374–377). He says more than he knows. μάκαρες, “blessed”, is the poem's word for the gods (24.23, 24.422), and αἴσιος, “of good omen”, is found only here (see the translation's note on 24.376).

**The false tale.** The god approves Priam's piety, “all that you have said is fair and right” (24.379; Leaf on 24.379), and asks whether the Trojans are sending their treasures abroad or leaving Ilios in fear, “For such a man, the best, has perished, / your son” (24.384–385). Leaf defends the second verse: it is more natural that Hermes, having calmed the old man, should be the first to show that he knows him, than that Priam should give himself away (on 24.385). Asked who he is, he answers with a lie. He has seen Hector killing Argives at the ships while Achilles kept his men from the fighting, for he is Achilles' attendant, a Myrmidon, the son of Polyctor, chosen by lot to follow him; and the Achaeans will fight around the city at dawn (24.390–404; see [[polyctor]], [[myrmidons]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**The truth about Hector.** Priam asks for “the whole truth” (24.407), whether his son still lies beside the ships or Achilles has cut him limb from limb for his dogs (24.408–409), and the god tells it. “Old man, the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds” (24.411); this is the twelfth dawn, and the body does not rot, the blood is washed from it, and its wounds are closed: “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is” (24.411–423; see [[hector]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Priam rejoices. His son never forgot the gods, “so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death” (24.428).

**The gift refused.** Priam offers the stranger a fine goblet and asks for his protection as far as Achilles' hut (24.429–431; see [[cup]]). The god refuses the gift as a loyal attendant would: “You are testing me, old sir, a younger man, but you will not persuade me, / bidding me take gifts from you behind Achilles' back” (24.433–434), for he fears his lord “and feel shame above all in my heart / to rob him” (24.435–436; see [[aidos]] and [[peira]]). The escort he gives freely, “even to famous Argos”, by sea or on foot, and no one would scorn such an escort and fight him (24.437–439; see [[argos]]).

**Into the camp.** “He spoke, and the great helper, springing onto the chariot behind the horses, / quickly took the whip and the reins in his hands, / and breathed noble might into the horses and the mules” (24.440–442; see [[chariot]], [[horses]] and [[mules]]). At the towers of the ships and the ditch the sentries are busy with their supper, and “the guide, the slayer of Argus, poured sleep over them / all, and at once opened the gates and pushed back the bars, / and brought in Priam and the splendid gifts on the wagon” (24.443–447; see [[the-achaean-wall]], [[the-gates-of-the-wall]], [[night-watch]], [[hypnos]] and [[wagon]]). At the hut, whose door was held by a bar of fir that three Achaeans drove home and three drew back, though Achilles could work it alone, “then Hermes, the great helper, opened it for the old man” (24.448–457; see [[hut]]).

**The god revealed.** He brings in the gifts, steps down from the chariot, and speaks:

{{quote:c8607c3d-7848-593e-806a-cb9c4b1173b0}}

His reason for leaving is the gods' dignity: “it would be a thing for blame / for an immortal god to welcome mortals so openly” (24.463–464; see [[nemesis]]). Leaf takes ἀγαπαζέμεν in its Odyssean sense, “to welcome as host”, with the mortals as subject, since an immortal cannot become a guest of men (on 24.464; see the translation's note on 24.464). His last advice is the suppliant's (see [[supplication]]). Priam will appeal to Achilles by his father alone (24.486–506), and Leaf finds Düntzer's objections to 24.466–467 well founded, since the Iliad knows of a son of Achilles only in late passages (on 24.466; see the translation's note on 24.466–467). Then he “went away to high Olympus” (24.468; see [[olympus]]).

**The return.** “Now the other gods and the men who fight from chariots / slept all night long, overcome by soft sleep; / but sleep did not take hold of Hermes, the great helper” (24.677–679). The first verse is the first of Book 2, where sleep did not hold Zeus (2.1–2); the second is the second of Book 10, where it did not hold Agamemnon (10.1–4). The god who poured sleep on the sentries is the one who does not sleep. He ponders how to send Priam away “unseen by the sacred gatekeepers” (24.681; see [[night-watch]]), and stands above his head, in the verse with which the ghost of Patroclus came to Achilles (24.682 = 23.68; see [[dream-scene]]):

{{quote:b8305e63-fa1f-5711-bb38-5aafcfd61fea}}

The warning works (see [[apoina]] and [[agamemnon]]). The old man is afraid and rouses the herald, and Hermes yokes the horses and mules “and drove them himself swiftly through the camp, and no one knew” (24.689–691). At the ford of the Xanthus he leaves them: “then Hermes went away to high Olympus, / and saffron-robed Dawn was spreading over all the earth” (24.694–695; see [[scamander]] and [[eos]]).

**His book.** Hermes is named in this book more than in all the rest of the Iliad: his name, Ἑρμείας, nine times, against seven mentions elsewhere under any form of the name (2.104, 5.390, 14.491, 15.214, 16.185, 20.35, 20.72), and ἀργεϊφόντης eleven times, against three (2.103, 16.181, 21.497). Leaf sees in the book a conception of Hermes different from that of the rest of the Iliad, nearer to the Odyssey, where he carries Zeus' orders and guides the dead (on 24.23 and 24.339; *Odyssey* 5.28–148, 24.1–14). Here he guides a living man through his enemies and back, as the πομπός whom Zeus promised.
