---
summary: The dogs of Book 24 are the fate that Hector's parents fear for his body: Hecuba says that fate spun it for him at his birth to glut the swift dogs far from his parents, Priam asks whether Achilles has already set him before his dogs, and Hermes answers that the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds (24.209–211, 24.406–411).
---

**Hecuba's fear.** When Priam tells her that he will go to the ships, Hecuba answers that they should weep for Hector apart, in the hall, “this is how mighty Fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth, when I myself bore him, / that he should glut the swift dogs, far from his parents, / at the side of a mighty man” (24.209–212; see [[hecuba]] and [[mighty-fate]]). She said the same in Book 22, begging Hector from the wall: “far away from us both, / beside the ships of the Argives, the swift dogs will devour you” (22.88–89). ἀργίπους, “swift-footed”, is found only here in Homer; Walter Leaf takes ἀργίποδας as standing for πόδας ἀργούς, “swift of foot” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.211), and the translation renders it “swift”, as it renders the κύνες ἀργοί of the plague and of Hector's boast (1.50, 18.283; see [[swift-dogs]]). In that boast Hector had told Polydamas that Achilles would never sack Troy: “before that the swift dogs will eat him” (18.283).

**Priam's question and Hermes' answer.** Priam asks the young man he has met on the plain whether his son is still beside the ships, “or by now / has Achilles cut him limb from limb and set him before his dogs?” (24.408–409). ᾗσι κυσὶν, “his own dogs”, are the dogs of Achilles' house; he had nine table dogs, and he threw two of them on the pyre of Patroclus (23.173–174). Leaf notes that μελεϊστὶ ταμών, “cut him limb from limb”, is a phrase of the Odyssey, said of the Cyclops cutting up Odysseus' companions for his meal and in Odysseus' threat to the maid Melantho (on 24.409; *Odyssey* 9.291, 18.339). Hermes answers, “Old man, the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds” (24.411; see [[birds]] and [[hermes]]), and tells him of the body that the gods keep fresh (24.411–423; see [[fresh-as-dew]] and [[maggots]]).

The threat has stood over the dead since the first verses of the poem, where the wrath of Achilles made the heroes “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5), and over Hector since Achilles told him, “but the dogs and the birds will divide you all among them” (22.354). Aphrodite kept the dogs from his body day and night (23.184–186). Book 24 answers the fear: the body is given back, and no dog touches it.
