summary: When Priam comes in unseen and clasps his knees, “Achilles was amazed when he saw godlike Priam; / and the others were amazed too, and looked at one another” (24.483–484), as a household is amazed when a stranger who has killed a man comes to its door. After the meal the two men marvel at each other, Priam at Achilles, “how tall he was and how fair”, and Achilles at Priam, “seeing his noble looks and hearing his words” (24.629–632).

**Amazement at the suppliant.** Amazement is the point of the simile: θάμβος δʼ ἔχει εἰσορόωντας, “amazement holds those who watch” (24.482; see [[the-killer-in-exile]]), is the formula of the spectacles of Books 3 and 4, “amazement held those who watched” (3.342, 4.79), turned to the present of a simile, and the narrative answers it, θάμβησεν … θάμβησαν (24.483–484). Walter Leaf notes that this simile alone of its form adds the point of comparison as an independent clause, so that its minor touches, the killing and the exile, stand in the foreground (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.480; see [[ate]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). Priam has come in unseen, led by a god (24.477; see [[hermes]]), and the sight is of a king at the knees of the man who killed his sons (see [[supplication]]).

**Wonder at each other.** θαυμάζω, “marvel”, is θάμβος' companion word (see [[thaumaston]]). After the meal Priam marvels at Achilles' stature and beauty, “for he looked like the gods face to face”, and Achilles at Priam's looks and words (24.629–632); then they take their fill of looking at one another (24.633; see [[eros]]). Leaf reports Helbig's remark that here the beauty of the old man is recognized as vividly as that of the young (on 24.630; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). Earlier, in his false tale, Hermes said that the Myrmidons, held back from battle, “stood there and marveled” at Hector (24.394), and told Priam that he himself would gaze at how fresh his son's body lay (24.418–419; see [[honoring-the-dead]]).
