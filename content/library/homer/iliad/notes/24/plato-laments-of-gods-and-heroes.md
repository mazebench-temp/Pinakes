summary: Plato's first example of a hero's unworthy grief is Achilles at the start of this book, lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down, and then wandering by the sea. Plato's text of the lines ends with a familiar formula and a verb that is not Homer's; in the poem the sleepless grief is for Patroclus, it ends each morning in the dragging, and his own mother asks him how long it will last.

**Plato's verses.** Homer and the other poets are to be asked not to make Achilles, the son of a goddess, “lying now on his side, now again on his back, now face down, and then standing up and sailing distraught along the shore of the barren sea” (*Republic* 3.388a–b; see the entry above). Plato puts the verses into the accusative to fit his sentence, and his last verse is not ours. He reads πλωΐζοντʼ ἀλύοντʼ ἐπὶ θῖνʼ ἁλὸς ἀτρυγέτοιο, where our text has δινεύεσκʼ ἀλύων παρὰ θῖνʼ ἁλός, “he would … wander distraught along the shore of the sea”, and runs on into the next verse, “nor would the dawn / escape him as it appeared over the sea and the shores” (24.11–13). Plato's verse ends with a formula found elsewhere in Homer, θῖνʼ ἁλὸς ἀτρυγέτοιο, “the shore of the barren sea” (1.316, 1.327; *Odyssey* 8.49), and his πλωΐζοντα, “sailing”, is not a Homeric word: Hesiod uses it of his father, who sailed in ships for want of a good living (*Works and Days* 634). Proclus, stating Plato's charge in his own words, has Achilles “now lying on his back, now face down, now distraught on his side”, pouring dust on his head and lamenting like a child (*On the Republic* I 122 Kroll).

**In the poem.** The lines describe Achilles' nights after the funeral games. The others think of supper and sleep, but Achilles weeps, “longing for Patroclus, for his manhood and his noble might”, and for all that he wound out with him in war and at sea (24.3–9; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]); the verbs are iterative, and the nights are many (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.12; see the translation's note on 24.12). The grief that Plato would take out of poetry does not end in lament. At dawn Achilles yokes his horses and drags Hector three times around the mound (24.14–18; see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). The poem's own reproach comes from his mother: “My child, how long, mourning and grieving, / will you eat out your heart, remembering neither food / nor bed?” (24.128–130; see [[thetis]]). Priam, the other mourner Plato names, also rolls in the dung in this book. Iris finds him in his courtyard with dung heaped on his head and neck (24.160–165), and he tells Achilles that since Hector died he has done nothing but groan, “rolling in the dung in the enclosure of the courtyard” (κυλινδόμενος κατὰ κόπρον, 24.640), in the words that Plato quotes from Book 22 (22.414; see [[priam]]).
