summary: Iris finds Priam in his courtyard, his head and neck covered with the dung he rolled in, and bids him ransom Hector; against Hecuba's protest he chooses the ransom from his chests, drives the Trojans from his colonnade, rebukes his sons, pours a libation and prays, and sets out by night with the herald, guided by Hermes (24.159–467). In Achilles' hut he clasps Achilles' knees, kisses the hands that killed his sons and begs him by his own father; he weeps with him, will not sit while Hector lies uncared for, and is afraid when Achilles' anger flares; then he eats for the first time since Hector's death, marvels at Achilles, asks for eleven days for the funeral, and sleeps in the porch (24.468–676). Hermes brings him home with the body, and he sends the Trojans for wood (24.677–781).

**In the dung.** Iris comes to the house of Priam and finds “cries and lament”: his sons sit around their father in the courtyard soaking their clothes with tears, the daughters and the sons' wives mourn through the house, and in the midst the old man sits ἐντυπὰς ἐν χλαίνῃ, “wrapped close in his cloak”, with the dung on his head and neck that he heaped on himself as he rolled in it (24.160–168; see [[sons-of-priam]], [[priams-palace]] and [[lament]]). He rolled in it, κυλινδόμενος κατὰ κόπρον, when he saw Hector dragged before the city (22.414), and he will tell Achilles that he has groaned and rolled in the dung ever since (24.639–640). ἐντυπάς is found only here: the ancient explanation is that the cloak was drawn so close that the shape of the body showed through it (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.163; see the translation's note on 24.163). The goddess speaks softly, “and trembling seized his limbs”; she bids him take heart, for she comes from Zeus, who pities him, with the command to ransom Hector, alone but for an older herald, with Hermes to escort him and the promise that Achilles will spare a suppliant (24.169–187; see [[iris]], [[hermes]] and [[supplication]]).

**The choice.** He has his sons make ready the mule wagon and goes down into his fragrant chamber of cedar, and asks Hecuba what she thinks, for his own might and heart bid him go (24.189–199; see [[hecuba]]). She cries out that his wits are gone: Achilles, “that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man”, will neither pity nor respect him; let them weep for Hector apart, as fate spun at his birth (24.200–216). He answers that he will not be held back nor be persuaded by “a bird of evil omen”: if a seer or a priest had bidden him, he would call it a lie, but he heard the goddess and saw her face to face (24.218–224).

{{quote:24d078a4-70dd-53a8-8ed9-7ac3868985b1}}

βούλομαι, “I would have it so”, is stronger than ἐθέλω, “I am willing” (Leaf on 24.226; see the translation's note on 24.226). On the wall he had wished that Hector had died in his arms, “then we would have had our fill of weeping and shedding tears” (22.426–427). Then he opens the lids of his chests and takes out the ransom, robes and cloaks, rugs, mantles and tunics, ten talents of gold, tripods and cauldrons, and the beautiful cup that the Thracians gave him when he went to them on an embassy: “not even this / did the old man spare in his halls, for above all he wished in his heart / to ransom his dear son” (24.228–237; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]], [[apoina]] and [[cup]]).

**The Trojans and his sons.** He drives the Trojans from his colonnade with harsh words and his staff: have they not lament enough at home? They will be the easier for the Achaeans to kill now that Hector is dead, and may he go down to the house of Hades before he sees the city sacked (24.237–247; see [[trojans]]). ὀνόσασθε, “do you make light of it”, is Aristarchus' reading for a corrupt word (24.241; see the translation's note on 24.241). Then he turns on his nine sons, “Hurry, you wicked children, you shames of mine!”: he wishes them all dead in Hector's place; he fathered the best sons in wide Troy, and none of them is left, godlike Mestor, Troilus who fought from his chariot, “and Hector, who was a god among men, and did not seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god” (see [[aristotle-heroic-virtue]]); those who are left are liars and dancers and robbers of lambs and kids from their own people (24.248–264; see [[sons-of-priam]] and [[mestor]]).

**The libation and the prayer.** Hecuba brings wine in a golden cup and bids him pray to Zeus of Ida for a bird of omen on the right; if Zeus will not send it, she would not have him go (24.283–298). “Lady, I will not disobey you in this that you bid me; / for it is good to lift up one's hands to Zeus, in the hope that he may have pity” (24.300–301). He washes his hands in the pure water the housekeeper pours, stands in the middle of the enclosure, pours out the wine and prays to “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida”: “grant that I come to Achilles as one welcomed and pitied” (24.302–313; see [[libation]], [[hand-washing]] and [[prayer]]). Zeus sends an eagle, and the old man mounts his chariot in haste (24.314–322; see [[eagle]]).

**The journey.** He drives out with the herald Idaeus driving the mule wagon in front, and all his dear ones follow him, “lamenting much, as if he were going to his death” (24.322–328; see [[idaeus]]). At nightfall by the river, when the herald sees a man coming and proposes to flee or to clasp his knees, the old man's mind is confounded, his hair stands up on his supple limbs, and he stands amazed (24.349–360; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]). The stranger takes his hand and says that Priam looks to him like his own father; Priam answers that some god has held his hand over him, to send him such a guide (24.360–377). When the stranger says that he is an attendant of Achilles, Priam asks only one thing, whether his son still lies beside the ships “or by now / has Achilles cut him limb from limb and set him before his dogs?” (24.405–409). Told that the body is whole, he rejoices: “truly it is good to give the gifts that are their due / to the immortals”, for Hector never forgot the gods, “so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death” (24.424–428; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]), and he offers his guide a goblet, which the god refuses (24.429–439). The journey, Hermes' false tale and his escort through the camp, are told under [[hermes]], [[polyctor]] and [[the-ransom-of-hector]].

**At Achilles' knees.** Hermes bids him go in, clasp Achilles' knees and beg him by his father, his mother and his child (24.465–467). Priam leaps down from the chariot, leaves Idaeus to hold the horses and mules, goes straight to the house, and comes in unseen: “Great Priam”, Πρίαμος μέγας (24.468–477; see [[hut]]). He clasps Achilles' knees and kisses “the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons” (24.478–479), and Achilles and his companions stare at him as men stare at an exile who has killed a man and comes to a rich man's house (24.480–484; see [[the-killer-in-exile]], [[exile-and-refuge]] and [[thambos]]). Then he speaks:

{{quote:c24b9e02-fa75-566f-bab7-122926bc8c2d}}

The plea is the one he planned on the wall, when he would go alone and beg Achilles, “for he too has a father like me, / Peleus” (22.416–422; see [[peleus]]). ἐπὶ γήραος οὐδῷ, “on the threshold of old age”, is the phrase he used of himself in his plea to Hector (22.60; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). He goes on with his own loss: he fathered the best sons in wide Troy and none is left, fifty when the Achaeans came, nineteen of one womb; Ares has loosened the knees of most of them, “and he who was my only one, who guarded the city and its people, / him you killed the other day as he fought in defense of his native land, / Hector” (24.493–501; see [[sons-of-priam]] and [[hector]]). Leaf takes οἶος, “my only one”, to mean that beside Hector the other fifty counted for nothing (on 24.499; see the translation's note on 24.499). For Hector he has come with a boundless ransom, in the words that brought Chryses to the ships (24.501–502; 1.13; see [[boundless-ransom]] and [[book-1-and-book-24]]). The plea ends: “But respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me, / remembering your own father; I am more pitiable still, / for I have endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured, / to put to my lips the hand of the man who killed my sons” (24.503–506; see [[supplication]]).

**The tears.** “And the two remembered, the one man-slaying Hector, / and wept unceasingly, crouched before the feet of Achilles” (24.509–510; see [[achilles]] and [[tears]]). When Achilles has wept his fill he raises the old man by the hand, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.515–516), the words of Priam's own plea on the wall (22.74), and speaks to him of the jars of Zeus and of his past: he was blessed once, in wealth and sons, over all the land from Lesbos to Phrygia and the Hellespont (24.518–551; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]], [[lesbos]], [[phrygia]] and [[hellespont]]).

**Not yet a chair.** Priam will not sit: “Do not yet bid me sit on a chair, nurtured by Zeus, while Hector / lies uncared for in the huts, but quickly / release him, so that I may see him with my eyes; and accept the ransom” (24.553–555; see [[throne]]). He wishes Achilles joy of the ransom and a safe return home (24.556–558). The Alexandrians athetized 556–557 as insincere in his mouth; Leaf notes that Chryses' wish that the Achaeans sack Priam's city and come home safely raised the same question (on 24.556; 1.18–19; see the translation's note on 24.556). Achilles' anger flares: he means to release Hector himself, and knows that a god brought Priam through the camp; let him not stir his heart, lest he not spare him even as a suppliant. “So he spoke, and the old man was afraid, and obeyed his word” (24.559–571). The body is washed in another room, so that he should not see it and break out in anger (24.582–586; see [[cholos]]).

**The meal and the wonder.** He eats with Achilles, at Achilles' bidding, and Niobe is the example (24.599–628; see [[niobe]] and [[the-children-of-niobe]]). Then, “when they had put away the desire for drink and food”, he marvels at Achilles, “how tall he was and how fair; for he looked like the gods face to face”, and Achilles at him, “seeing his noble looks and hearing his words” (24.628–632; see [[thambos]] and [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). The elders at the Scaean gates said of Helen that she was “terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on” (3.158), and Priam himself spoke of Hector as one “who was a god among men” (24.258).

{{quote:d6934bf5-3e1c-5155-94bc-7be4cdd9d278}}

He has fasted and lain awake since Hector died, as Achilles fasted for Patroclus (19.205–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]] and [[sleepless]]); now at last he has eaten and drunk.

**The eleven days.** Achilles asks how many days he needs for the funeral, so that he may hold back the army (24.656–658). Priam asks for eleven days, with a reason: “For you know how we are penned within the city, and the wood is far / to bring from the mountain, and the Trojans are greatly afraid” (24.662–663). Nine days they would lament in the halls, bury Hector and feast on the tenth, make his tomb on the eleventh, “and on the twelfth we will fight, if we must” (24.664–667; see [[truce]]). Leaf takes the reason for a touch of pride, an apology for accepting the offer at all, where Peppmüller and Düntzer rejected it (on 24.662; see the translation's note on 24.662–663). Achilles promises, “old Priam”, and takes his right hand at the wrist, so that he may feel no fear (24.668–672). He sleeps in the porch with the herald, “with shrewd counsels in their minds” (24.673–674; 24.674 = 24.282; see [[idaeus]]).

**Home.** Hermes wakes him: his sons would pay three times as much for him alive, if Agamemnon found him among the enemy, “and the old man was afraid, and roused the herald” (24.682–689). Cassandra is the first to see him, standing in the chariot, from Pergamus (24.699–701; see [[cassandra]]). At the gates, when the crowd would mourn all day around the wagon, he speaks from the chariot: “Make way for me to pass through with the mules; and afterwards / you shall have your fill of weeping, when I have brought him home” (24.715–717). When the three women have lamented, he speaks for the last time in the poem, “a word among the people”: let the Trojans bring wood into the city and have no fear of an ambush, “for Achilles, / as he sent me from the black ships, gave orders / that they would do us no harm until the twelfth dawn comes” (24.777–781; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). The funeral feast is held “in the house of Priam, the king nurtured by Zeus” (24.803; see [[priams-palace]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]), in the second verse from the end of the Iliad.
