---
summary: The cattle of Book 24 are Hector's sacrifices, the “thighs of oxen” that Apollo reminds the gods of (24.34); the ox of the fields whose horn carries the fisherman's lead weight in the simile of Iris' descent (24.81); and the oxen that the Trojans yoke with mules to bring wood for Hector's pyre (24.782–784).
# βοῶν (24.34), in the phrase with αἰγῶν; the bare form is also the participle of βοάω, "shouting".
grc: ["βοῶν αἰγῶν"]
---

**Sacrifice.** Apollo begins his protest by reminding the gods that Hector “burned thighs of oxen and unblemished goats” for them (24.34; see [[goats]] and [[apollo]]). Zeus said the same of Hector in Book 22, when he pitied him in the chase: Hector “has burned many thighs of oxen for me / on the peaks of many-folded Ida, and at other times / on the height of the city” (22.170–172), and now he agrees that Hector was the dearest of mortals in Ilios to the gods, for his altar never lacked its share (24.66–70; see [[zeus]] and [[sacrifice]]).

**The ox of the fields.** In the simile of Iris' plunge into the sea, the lead weight is “mounted on the horn of an ox of the fields” (24.81; see [[the-lead-weight]] and [[of-the-fields]]). Whether the horn was a little tube over the line or a lure of horn is disputed (see the translation's note on 24.80–82).

**The wood for the pyre.** When Priam tells the Trojans to bring wood without fear, “under the wagons oxen and mules / they yoked”, and for nine days they bring in a vast load of wood (24.782–784; see [[mules]], [[wagon]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). Nestor proposed oxen and mules for bringing in the dead after the duel of Book 7 (7.332–333), and on the day of the truce both armies went out, some for the dead and others for wood, and lifted the dead onto wagons (7.417–432). For the pyre of Patroclus the wood was hauled down from Ida by mules (23.110–124).
