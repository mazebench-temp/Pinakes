---
summary: Hera, Poseidon and Athena keep their hatred of Ilios and Priam “for Alexander's blind folly, / who insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard” (24.28–29). When Priam comes into Achilles' hut, the narrator compares the amazement to that of a household when “a heavy blind folly seizes a man” who has killed a man in his own land and comes as an exile to the house of a wealthy man (24.480–482).
---

**Alexander's folly.** Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἄτης is the half-line with which Helen blamed Paris in Book 6, beside her own guilt (6.356), and an ancient variant gave it at 3.100 for ἀρχῆς, “because Alexander began it” (see the translation's note on 3.100). Here the folly is the Judgment of Paris, the poem's only allusion to it (24.29–30; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]] and [[paris]]). Walter Leaf calls ἄτης a late form, for the older ἀϝάτης, and says that he would adopt the variant ἀρχῆς here too, were he sure of the line's antiquity (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.28); Aristarchus athetized 25–30 (see the translation's note on 24.23–30).

**The manslayer's folly.** In the simile of 24.480–484 the ἄτη is a man's, and its result is a killing, exile and a sudden arrival in a rich man's house (see [[the-killer-in-exile]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The construction makes it seem that the ἄτη came upon him after the killing, and Leaf reports that Nägelsbach took it as the overwhelming effect of conscience and Göbel as the disorder of mind his plight brought on; he holds that the word can mean only the force that drove him to the deed, and that the poet, beginning “as when ἄτη seizes a man so that he kills”, let the main thought, the exile's arrival, take the principal verb (on 24.480; see the translation's note on 24.480–482). The scholia explain the rich man as one who purifies, and K. O. Müller conjectured ἀνδρὸς ἐς ἁγνίτεω; Leaf answers that Homer knows no expiation for blood but payment or exile (on 24.482). The simile turns the scene around. The suppliant who comes like a killer is the father of the killed, and the man whose knees he clasps is the killer (24.477–479; see [[supplication]] and [[thambos]]).
