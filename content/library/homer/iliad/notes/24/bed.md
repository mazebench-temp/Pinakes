summary: The last book has every kind of bed. Thetis asks her son how long he will remember “neither food / nor bed” (24.129–130); Hector is laid on a bier in Achilles' hut, and on a corded bed in his father's house (24.589, 24.600, 24.702, 24.720); Achilles has beds spread for Priam and his herald under the colonnade, with purple blankets, rugs and woolly cloaks, and himself sleeps in the inner part of the hut with Briseis beside him (24.643–648, 24.673–676); and Andromache laments that Hector did not die in his bed (24.743).

**Neither food nor bed.** Thetis sits beside her grieving son and strokes him: “My child, how long, mourning and grieving, / will you eat out your heart, remembering neither food / nor bed? It is good even to lie with a woman / in love” (24.128–131; see [[thetis]], [[achilles]] and [[philotes]]). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus read οὐδέ for οὔτε, “taking no thought even for food”, because he obelized the next three verses as unfit advice in a mother's mouth; Aristotle, Leaf notes, quotes them without offense (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.129; see the translation's note on 24.129–132). Aristotle names the bed among the natural desires that all men share: everyone who lacks food desires it, “and bed, as Homer says, the young man in his prime” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.11, 1118b10–11; see [[aristotle]]).

**The bier.** When the body has been washed, “Achilles himself lifted him and laid him on a bier” (24.589), and he tells Priam, “Your son is released to you, old man, as you asked, / and he lies on a bier” (24.599–600; see [[hector]]). Cassandra sees him “lying on the bier, drawn by the mules” (24.702), and in the house of Priam they lay him “on a corded bed” and set singers beside him (24.719–720; see [[lament]] and [[corded]]). λέχεα is the bed of the living and the bier of the dead, as for Patroclus (18.233, 18.352; see the note on Book 18). Andromache's grief is that Hector did not die at home: “For you did not stretch out your hands to me from your bed as you died, / nor speak to me some shrewd word” (24.743–744; see [[andromache]]).

**The guests' beds.** When Priam asks to be put to bed, Achilles bids his companions and the slave women “set beds beneath the colonnade, and throw fine purple / blankets on them, and spread rugs over them, / and put woolly cloaks on top for them to wear”, and the women spread two beds by torchlight (24.643–648; see [[purple]], [[cloak]], [[handmaids]] and [[hut]]). δέμνια are the bedsteads, ῥήγεα the blankets laid on them and τάπητες the rugs spread over these, with the woolly χλαῖναι on top (see the translation's row ῥῆγος / τάπης / χλαῖνα / φᾶρος). Leaf notes that τάπητες are used only in making beds or couches, and so answer to our blankets (on 24.230), and that the verses are those of the beds made for guests in the palaces of Menelaus and Alcinous, which suit a palace better than a soldier's hut (on 24.643; *Odyssey* 4.297–300, 7.336–339). The twelve rugs of Priam's ransom are such bedding (24.230; see [[apoina]]), and on the night of the embassy Phoenix's bed in the same hut was made of fleeces, a rug and fine linen (9.658–661; see the note on Book 9). Priam and the herald sleep in the porch, “but Achilles slept in the inner part of the well-built hut, / and beside him lay fair-cheeked Briseis” (24.673–676; see [[briseis]]), as on the night of the embassy he had lain beside Diomede (9.663–665).
