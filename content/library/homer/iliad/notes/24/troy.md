summary: In the last book Troy is the city from which Priam drives out with the ransom by night and to which he brings Hector home at dawn, while all the city comes out to meet them at the gates. Andromache foresees its sack now that its guardian is dead, and the poem ends inside it, with the wood brought in for nine days, the pyre and the feast in the house of Priam.

**Hateful to the gods.** The gods pity Hector, but Hera, Poseidon and Athena will not let him be stolen away: “they held to it, as when sacred Ilios first became hateful to them, / and Priam, and the people, for Alexander's blind folly” (24.27–28; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]], [[hieros]] and [[ate]]). The verse turns round the words in which Zeus named the city he honored most of all the cities of men, “sacred Ilios … and Priam, and the people of Priam” (4.46–47), and Zeus answers Hera now as he answered her then: Hector was “the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios”, for his altar never lacked its share (24.66–70, where 24.69–70 = 4.48–49; see [[zeus]] and [[hector]]).

**Out of the city and back.** Priam drives the ransom “swiftly through the city”, while his kin follow him “lamenting much, as if he were going to his death”, and turn back to Ilios when he reaches the plain (24.326–330; see [[priam]] and [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). To test him Hermes, in the likeness of a young Myrmidon, asks whether the Trojans are “all now leaving sacred Ilios / in fear”, and says that at dawn the Achaeans “will set battle in array around the city” (24.383–384, 24.401–402; see [[hermes]]). In the hut Priam tells Achilles that Hector “guarded the city and its people” (24.499), Achilles answers that “there are always battles around your city, and the killing of men” (24.548), and Priam asks for a truce because “we are penned within the city, and the wood is far / to bring from the mountain” (24.662–663; see [[achilles]]). At dawn Cassandra, who has gone up onto Pergamus, sees them first and calls out “through all the city”; “not a man was left there in the city, / nor a woman”, and the people meet the body near the gates (24.699–709; see [[cassandra]] and [[pergamus]]).

**From Troy.** Priam reminds Achilles of Peleus, who, when he hears that his son is alive, “hopes every day / to see his dear son come home from Troy” (24.491–492; see [[peleus]]). Τροίηθεν, “from Troy”, is found only here in the Iliad and six times in the Odyssey, always of the homecomings (*Odyssey* 3.257, 3.276, 4.488, 9.38, 9.259, 11.160); Walter Leaf keeps Τροίηθεν ἰόντα against the variant Τροίηθε μολόντα, since the ν of such case-forms in -θεν is never dropped, and calls the phrase Odyssean (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.492). Peleus will not see it: Achilles is to “perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (24.86; see [[deep-soiled]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**The city foretold.** Priam has wished to go down to Hades “before I see the city sacked and ravaged / with my eyes” (24.245–246). Over the body Andromache says what will come: “for before that this city from top to bottom / will be sacked; for you have perished, its guardian, who kept the city itself / safe” (24.728–730), and its wives and children will be carried off in the ships, and her son thrown from the tower (24.730–739; see [[andromache]], [[astyanax]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). For Helen Troy is the place to which Alexander brought her, where “there is no longer anyone else in wide Troy / who is kind to me” (24.764, 24.774–775; see [[helen]]). The poem ends before the fall (see [[the-fall-of-troy]]): the Trojans bring wood “into the city” for nine days, burn Hector, heap his mound with lookouts posted all around, and feast in Priam's house (24.777–804; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).
