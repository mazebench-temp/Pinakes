summary: Hecuba reminds Priam that he was once “famous among strangers and among those you rule” (24.202). In Achilles' hut the enemy's father is received as a guest: Achilles slaughters a sheep and serves the meat (24.621–628), has beds spread for him under the colonnade, and tells him, “Lie outside, dear old man” (24.643–650).

**The famous host.** Hecuba's reproach looks back to a Priam who was known abroad, among ξεῖνοι, as well as among his own people (24.201–202; see [[hecuba]] and [[priam]]). Some of the goods he now gives away came to him from strangers, the cup the Thracians gave him when he went to them on an embassy and the mules that were the Mysians' gift (24.234–235, 24.277–278; see [[doron]]).

**A god will not be a guest.** Hermes leaves Priam at Achilles' gate, since “it would be a thing for blame / for an immortal god to welcome mortals so openly” (24.463–464). Walter Leaf takes ἀγαπαζέμεν in its Odyssean sense, “to welcome as host”, with the mortals as subject: an immortal cannot let himself become the guest of men (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.464; see the translation's note on 24.464 and [[nemesis]]).

**The meal.** When the ransom has been taken and the body released, Achilles himself slaughters a sheep, his companions prepare it, and the meal is served in verses of the embassy's supper in Book 9: there Patroclus set out the bread “in beautiful baskets, while Achilles served the meat”, here it is Automedon (24.625–626 = 9.216–217, 24.627–628 = 9.221–222; Leaf on 24.623; see [[feast-type-scene]], [[desire-for-drink-and-food]] and [[automedon]]). Lycaon had claimed the right of one who had tasted bread at Achilles' side, and was killed (21.76; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[aidos]]); Priam eats at Achilles' table, for the first time since Hector died (24.641–642; see [[fasting]]).

**The bed.** The order for the beds is the verse with which Patroclus had a bed spread for Phoenix in Book 9, with Achilles now giving it himself (24.643; 9.658), and the beds, blankets, rugs and cloaks under the colonnade are those of the guests in the palaces of Menelaus and Alcinous (24.644–647; *Odyssey* 4.297–300, 7.336–339). Leaf thinks the lines better suited to those palaces than to a soldier's hut, and the Iliad more likely the borrower (on 24.643; see the translation's note on 24.643–647 and [[hut]]). The porch where Priam and his herald sleep is the guest's sleeping place in the Odyssey too (24.673; *Odyssey* 4.302; Leaf on 24.673). Achilles' reason for putting the guest outside, the counselors who might see him and tell Agamemnon, is said “taunting” (ἐπικερτομέων), which Leaf takes as his bitter tone toward Agamemnon, since there is no taunt in his words to Priam (24.649–655; on 24.649; see [[boule]]). Before they sleep the host takes his guest's right hand at the wrist, “so that he might feel no fear in his heart” (24.671–672), a sign of kindliness, as Leaf notes, in the Odyssey too (*Odyssey* 18.258; on 24.671; see [[supplication]]).
