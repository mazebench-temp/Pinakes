---
summary: Gifts bind men and gods both ways in the last book. Zeus says that Hector “never failed in the gifts I love” (24.68), and Priam, told that the body is unharmed, answers, “truly it is good to give the gifts that are their due / to the immortals” (24.425–426). The ransom goes as “gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart” (24.119), and Achilles tells Priam of the jars of Zeus, “of the gifts that he gives, one of evils, and the other of blessings” (24.528).
---

**Gifts to the gods.** Zeus answers Hera that Hector too “was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios; / so he was to me, for he never failed in the gifts I love” (24.67–68). The two verses that follow are those with which he spoke of Troy in Book 4, the altar that never lacked its share, “for that is the prize allotted to us” (24.69–70 = 4.48–49; see [[geras]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Walter Leaf takes ἡμάρτανε δώρων as “omitted to render them”, a phrase that would more naturally be used of the receiver (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.68). Priam draws the same lesson from what the young stranger tells him of the body:

{{quote:6af4cc8e-6f22-5e79-84f6-bf879eb1e6f2}}

ἐναίσιμα δῶρα are the gifts that are the gods' due (see [[aisa]]). ἀπεμνήσαντο is “remembered” in the sense of repaying: Leaf explains it as “remembered his gifts and repaid them”, the sense in which Hesiod and Thucydides use the compound of requiting a favor (on 24.428; see the translation's note on 24.428). Hecuba says the same over the body, that the gods “have cared for you even in the portion of death” (24.750).

**Gifts for the body.** The ransom is sent as gifts: Achilles is to “receive gifts from Priam” (24.76), and Zeus' message, repeated by Iris and by Priam, is to “bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart” (24.119, 24.147, 24.176, 24.196; see [[apoina]] and [[thymos]]). Some of what Priam sends had come to him as gifts: the mules “which the Mysians had once given to Priam, splendid gifts” (24.277–278), and the cup which Thracian men had given him on an embassy (24.234–235; see [[mysians]], [[thracians]] and [[cup]]). Hermes brings “the splendid gifts on the wagon” into the camp and “the glorious gifts” to Achilles (24.447, 24.458). Offered a goblet for himself, the god in his Myrmidon's disguise will not take “gifts from you behind Achilles' back” (24.429–436; see [[aidos]] and [[hermes]]).

**The gifts of the gods.** Achilles' parable gives the word to Zeus: the two jars on his floor hold the gifts he gives, evils and blessings, and a man receives a mixture or evils alone (24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]); Leaf understands from 24.526 that only the gods enjoy the good unmixed (on 24.527). Peleus is his example. “So to Peleus too the gods gave splendid gifts / from his birth” (24.534–535), ἀγλαὰ δῶρα as the Mysians' mules were: wealth, rule over the Myrmidons and a goddess for a wife. On him too the god laid evil, a single son doomed to an early death (24.536–540; see [[peleus]]). The gifts that men give the gods are remembered; the gifts the gods give men are a mixture.
