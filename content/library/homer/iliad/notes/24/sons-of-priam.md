**Around their father.** When Iris comes, “His sons sat around their father within the courtyard / and soaked their clothes with tears” (24.161–162; see [[lament]] and [[priams-palace]]). When she has gone, he bids them make ready the mule wagon and bind the wicker body on it (24.189–190; see [[wagon]]).

**The nine.** He drives the Trojans from his colonnade with his staff, and then turns on his sons, “rebuking Helenus and Paris and brilliant Agathon / and Pammon and Antiphonus and Polites, good at the war cry, / and Deiphobus and Hippothous and brilliant Agauus” (24.247–251; see [[helenus]], [[paris]], [[agathon-son-of-priam]], [[pammon]], [[antiphonus]], [[polites]], [[deiphobus]], [[hippothous-son-of-priam]] and [[agauus]]):

{{quote:684fdd94-fcc9-58fa-904e-a5c6eb5ed4ec}}

Four of the nine have fought in the poem, and Paris is the cause of the war; the other five are named only here, and the scholia could not say whether the last is Agauus or Dius (see the translation's note on 24.251). Of the dead, Mestor too is named only here, and Troilus “who fought from his chariot” is not named again in Homer (see [[mestor]] and [[troilus]]). Hector “did not seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god” (24.258–259; see [[hector]] and [[aristotle-heroic-virtue]]); Walter Leaf illustrates the words by Agamemnon's, that no man had ever devised so many grim deeds in a day as Hector, “the dear son of no goddess and of no god” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.258; 10.47–50). κατηφόνες, “you shames of mine” (24.253), Aristarchus took for a feminine, a sarcasm; Leaf takes it for an abstract, “my disgraces”, like ἐλέγχεα, “these disgraces”, in 24.260 (on 24.253; see the translation's note on 24.253 and [[elenchos]]). χοροιτυπίῃσιν ἄριστοι are to Leaf “heroes of the dance”, carpet knights, and ἐπιδήμιοι, the emphatic word, makes them plunderers of their own people instead of the enemy (on 24.261, 24.262; see [[dance]]). The best sons, Ares has destroyed (24.260; see [[ares]]). “So he spoke, and they, fearing their father's rebuke”, bring out the wagon, yoke the mules and load the ransom (24.265–278; see [[mules]] and [[apoina]]). When he sets out, his sons and his sons-in-law follow him to the plain and turn back to Ilios (24.327–331; see [[in-laws]]).

**Fifty.** To Achilles he says the verses of his rebuke again, “I fathered the best sons / in wide Troy, and of them, I say, not one is left” (24.493–494 = 24.255–256), and counts them: fifty when the Achaeans came, nineteen of one womb, the others borne to him by women in his halls, and of most of them Ares has loosened the knees (24.495–498; see [[priam]] and [[hecuba]]). Two of those other women the poem names, Laothoe, the mother of Lycaon and Polydorus, and Castianeira, the mother of Gorgythion (22.46–48, 8.302–305; see [[laothoe]] and [[castianeira]]). Payne Knight rejected 494–497 for the clash with “most of them” in 498, and Leaf would rather lose 498 (on 24.493; see the translation's note on 24.493–498). Priam has kissed “the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons”, the hand of the man ἀνδρὸς παιδοφόνοιο, “who killed my sons” (24.478–479, 24.506; see [[supplication]]), and Hecuba remembers the sons whom Achilles sold across the sea (24.751–753; see [[achilles-raids]]). Hermes warns Priam that “your sons who are left behind” would have to pay three times as much to ransom him alive (24.686–688; see [[hermes]]). At the last, “his brothers and his companions gathered the white bones” of Hector (24.793; see [[kasignetos]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus lists ten sons of Hecuba, Hector first and Troilus last, with Polydorus among them, and thirty-six by other women, among them Lycaon, Gorgythion, Cebriones, Democoon and Echemmon of the Iliad, and the Hippothous, Agathon and Mestor of Priam's lists (*Library* 3.12.5; see [[apollodorus]]).
