---
summary: Aristotle quotes this book three times, Apollo's line on the senseless earth for the end of anger, Priam's words on Hector for a virtue above the human, and the word “bed” from Thetis' reproach for the natural appetites; and he names the scene of the eagle “the going out of Priam”.
---

- Anger does not reach the dead: “for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30, quoting 24.54 in the Iliad's words): [[the-senseless-earth]] and [[the-gods-debate]].
- Hector, who “did not seem the son of a mortal man, but of a god”, as the instance of a virtue above us, heroic and divine (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.1, 1145a15–22, quoting 24.258–259): [[aristotle-heroic-virtue]].
- The eagle called plangos, duck-killer and morphnos, “which Homer too mentions in the going out of Priam” (*History of Animals* 9.32, on 24.316): [[title-of-book-24]], [[eagle]] and [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]].
- **Food and bed.** Some appetites are common to all and natural: “everyone who lacks it desires dry or wet food, and sometimes both, and bed, as Homer says, the young man in his prime” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.11, 1118b10–11). The verse of Homer that joins food and bed is Thetis' reproach to her son, who remembers “neither food / nor bed” (24.129–130). Walter Leaf, noting that Aristarchus obelized the lines that follow, on the good of lying with a woman, as unfit for a mother's mouth, says that Aristotle quotes them without offense (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.129); what Aristotle quotes is the one word εὐνῆς, “bed”: [[bed]], [[thetis]] and [[fasting]].
- The horn on the fisherman's line, which Aristotle explained, according to Plutarch, as a little tube above the hook, as Leaf reports (on 24.80): [[the-lead-weight]] and [[plato-the-fishermans-lead]].
