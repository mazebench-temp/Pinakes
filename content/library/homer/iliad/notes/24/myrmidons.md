summary: Hermes, in disguise, tells Priam that he is a Myrmidon, an attendant of Achilles and the son of Polyctor (24.396–400); the Myrmidons built Achilles' high hut of fir, thatched it and fenced its great courtyard with stakes (24.448–453); and Achilles tells Priam that his father Peleus ruled over the Myrmidons (24.536).
# Unnamed: the builders who roofed the hut and made its courtyard (24.450-452).
refs: [24.450-452]
---

**A false Myrmidon.** Asked who he is and of what parents, the god in the likeness of a young prince answers, “For I am his attendant; one well-made ship brought us; / I am one of the Myrmidons, and my father is Polyctor” (24.396–397; see [[hermes]], [[polyctor]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The tale fits what the poem has told of Achilles' men. He has seen Hector killing Argives at the ships, “and we stood there and marveled; for Achilles / would not let us fight, being angry with the son of Atreus” (24.394–395), as in the Catalogue the Myrmidons amused themselves on the shore with discus, javelin and bow while Achilles lay idle in his anger (2.771–775). Achilles had brought fifty ships, with fifty men in each (16.168–170), and the speaker was chosen by lot among his brothers to go with him (24.399–400; see [[lots]]). The disguise lets the god speak as one of Achilles' own people: he will not take a gift from Priam “behind Achilles' back” (24.434), but he will escort him “even to famous Argos” (24.437; see [[argos]]). Walter Leaf takes that Argos, in a Myrmidon's mouth, for the Pelasgian Argos of Achilles' own country (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.437; 2.681; see [[pelasgian-argos]]).

**The builders.** When Priam comes to the hut, it is “the high hut that the Myrmidons had built for their lord, / cutting beams of fir” (24.449–450). They roofed it with thatch mown in the meadow and made a great courtyard around it with close-set stakes (24.450–453; see [[hut]] and [[achilles]]). ἄνακτι, “for their lord”, is said twice in the description (24.449, 24.452; see [[anax]]); Leaf notes that in both places it neglects the digamma of ϝάναξ, and that this was Heyne's chief reason for rejecting 24.449–456 (on 24.448 and 24.449; see the translation's note on 24.448–456). Inside, Achilles' companions sit apart, and only Automedon and Alcimus are busy at his side (24.473–475; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]] and [[hetairos]]).

**Peleus' people.** Comparing his father's fortune with Priam's, Achilles says that the gods gave Peleus splendid gifts from his birth, “for he excelled all men / in prosperity and wealth, and he ruled over the Myrmidons, / and to him, a mortal, they gave a goddess as his wife” (24.535–537; see [[peleus]]). Over the body of Asteropaeus he had named his father, in his line from Zeus, “lord over many Myrmidons” (21.188; see the note on Book 21). Now he adds what the gods also gave: a single son, “doomed to an untimely death”, who is not there to care for him in his old age (24.538–542; see [[old-age-and-memory]]).
