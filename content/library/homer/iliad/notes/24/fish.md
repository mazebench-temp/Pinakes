# ἰχθύσι (24.82) occurs in the Iliad only here.
grc: [ἰχθύσι]
---

ἔρχεται ὠμηστῇσιν ἐπʼ ἰχθύσι κῆρα φέρουσα, “goes down bringing doom to the fishes that eat raw flesh” (24.82; see [[the-lead-weight]]). ὠμηστής, “that eat raw flesh”, is the epithet that the Iliad gives to the birds and dogs that tear at the unburied (11.454, 22.67; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]), and the fish of the poem have earned it: in Book 21 they were to lick the blood from Lycaon's wound and eat his white fat, and they tore at the body of Asteropaeus in the river (21.122–127, 21.201–204). Here they are the prey. The tackle of the simile, the lead weight and the horn of an ox, is the angler's, whom the Iliad pictures once before, in the simile of Patroclus and Thestor (16.406–408; see [[the-fisherman]]). Plato's Socrates chooses these verses as the part of Homer that a fisherman, and not a rhapsode, would be fit to judge (*Ion* 538c–d).
