---
summary: Priam tells Hecuba that if any mortal, seer or priest had bidden him go to the ships, “we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more” (24.222); he calls the sons he has left “liars and dancers” (24.261); and on the road he asks the young stranger, who is Hermes telling a false tale, for “the whole truth” (24.407).
grc: [ψεῦσταί]
---

**A lie, or a god.** Priam's verse is Nestor's in Book 2, where the message in question was Agamemnon's dream: “we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more” (24.222 = 2.81; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). Nestor accepted the dream because the man who saw it was the best of the Achaeans (2.82), and Priam accepts the message because he heard and saw the goddess himself (24.223; see [[iris]]). Walter Leaf, with Aristarchus, thinks the line borrowed in Book 2 from here, since it suits this context far better (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.222). The dream of Book 2 was a lie from Zeus; the message of Book 24 is true (see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Liars.** ψεῦσται, “liars”, begins the list in Priam's abuse of his nine surviving sons, “liars and dancers, best at beating the dancing floor, / robbers of lambs and kids from their own people” (24.261–262; see [[sons-of-priam]], [[elenchos]] and [[katepheie]]); Leaf compares Hera's taunt to Zeus, ψευστήσεις, “You will prove false” (19.107; on 24.261). The noun is found only here in Homer.

**The false tale.** The stranger who meets Priam at the river says that he is an attendant of Achilles, one of the Myrmidons, the son of Polyctor (24.396–400; see [[polyctor]] and [[hermes]]). Priam asks him for πᾶσαν ἀληθείην, “the whole truth”, about his son (24.406–409); Leaf notes that ἀληθείην κατάλεξον is an Odyssean phrase (on 24.407). What the god tells him of the body is true, and he names himself only when he leaves him at Achilles' door, “I who have come to you am an immortal god, / Hermes” (24.460–461).
