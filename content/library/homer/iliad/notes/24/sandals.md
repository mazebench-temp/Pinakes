---
summary: Sent by Zeus to bring Priam to Achilles, Hermes binds on his golden, ambrosial sandals, which carry him over sea and land as fast as the wind, and takes up his wand (24.339–345).
# πέδιλα (24.340) is the entry's; the half-verse ἐδήσατο καλὰ πέδιλα is claimed by dressing-scene.
---

“At once he bound under his feet his fine sandals, / ambrosial, golden, which carried him over the waters / and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind” (24.340–342; see [[hermes]], [[ambrosial]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The verse ends as the dressing verse of Agamemnon and Nestor ends, “and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals” (2.44, 10.22; of Hera, 14.186), ἐδήσατο καλὰ πέδιλα (see [[dressing-scene]]), but it begins with αὐτίκʼ ἔπειθʼ, “at once”, and leaves out the “shining feet”: the god binds on the means of his journey, not his dress, and then takes his wand (24.343–345; see [[wand]]).

Walter Leaf notes that the passage recurs whole in the Odyssey, when Hermes sets out for Calypso's island (*Odyssey* 5.43–49), that the three verses of the sandals are said there of Athena as well (*Odyssey* 1.96–98), and that the sending of Hermes as messenger is thoroughly Odyssean (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.339). The sandals carry him over sea and land, but the poem says nothing of wings; he flies, “holding it in his hands”, and comes quickly “to Troy and the Hellespont” (24.345–346; see [[hellespont]]).
