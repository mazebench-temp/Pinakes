summary: The last book turns on pity. Apollo keeps the body from defilement, “pitying the man, / though he was dead” (24.19–20), and complains that Achilles “has lost all pity, and he has no shame” (24.44); Hecuba fears that Achilles “will not pity you” (24.207); Priam prays to come to him “as one welcomed and pitied” (24.309), and Zeus, “seeing the old man”, pities him (24.332). Priam's plea ends, “I am more pitiable still” (24.504), and Achilles raises him from the ground, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.516).
grc: [ἔλεον, ἐλεεινότερός, οἰκτίρων]
en: [pitying, pitied, pities, more pitiable, lamenting]

**The gods' pity.** For twelve days Apollo keeps all defilement from Hector's flesh, “pitying the man, / though he was dead”, and the blessed gods watch and keep pitying him, the iterative ἐλεαίρεσκον of a pity felt day after day (24.18–23; see [[apollo]], [[watching-the-war]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Their pity proposes a theft, and Zeus turns it into a ransom (24.24, 24.71–76; see [[apoina]]). Apollo's complaint names what Achilles has lost, ἔλεος and αἰδώς, pity and shame (24.44; see [[savage-as-a-lion]]); ἔλεον is the noun's only occurrence in Homer, and the two words are the two claims of a suppliant (see [[aidos]] and [[supplication]]). Iris tells Priam that Zeus “cares greatly for you and pities you” (24.174), the verse with which the lying Dream began its message to Agamemnon (2.27); Walter Leaf finds it “quite in place here” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.174; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). When Priam reaches the plain, Zeus sees him and pities him, and sends Hermes (24.331–338; see [[zeus]] and [[hermes]]).

**Hoping for pity.** Hecuba's fear for her husband, that Achilles “will not pity you / or respect you at all” (24.207–208), is Hector's fear for himself before the duel, “he will not pity me, / nor respect me at all” (22.123–124), as Leaf notes; he adds that ὠμηστής, “eater of raw flesh”, is said of a man nowhere else in Homer (on 24.207; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]). Twice the hope is spoken as a condition, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ, “in the hope that he may have pity”: by Priam of Zeus, when he agrees to pray, and by the herald of the stranger who meets them at the river, “clasp his knees and entreat him” (24.301, 24.357; see [[prayer]] and [[idaeus]]). Priam's prayer asks that he may come to Achilles φίλον ἠδʼ ἐλεεινόν, “as one welcomed and pitied” (24.309), the words of Odysseus' prayer to come to the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 6.327; Leaf on 24.306). His household follows him out of the city “lamenting much, as if he were going to his death” (ὀλοφυρόμενοι, 24.328; see [[as-if-going-to-his-death]]).

**The plea and the answer.** Priam's plea ends with the claim to pity:

{{quote:c896769c-6e34-5817-963f-c658dcb24c47}}

He is “more pitiable” (24.504) than Peleus, whom he has asked Achilles to remember (24.486–492; see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[peleus]]). Achilles does not answer at once. He weeps for his father and for Patroclus, and then rises from his chair and raises the old man, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.507–516; see [[achilles]] and [[priam]]). The gray head and beard are those of the old man whose death Priam had called “the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals” (22.74–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]]), and οἰκτίρων is the last use in the poem of the verb of Achilles' pity for Eumelus in the chariot race (23.534, 23.548). The speech that follows opens with ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch”, said in pity, as Zeus said it of Hector (24.518; 17.201; see the translation's note on 24.518).
