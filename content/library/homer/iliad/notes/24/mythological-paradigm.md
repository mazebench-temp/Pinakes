summary: Achilles tells Priam the story of Niobe, who lost her twelve children to Apollo and Artemis and still remembered food, to persuade him to eat (24.599–620); and in the parable of the jars he sets out Peleus and Priam as examples of men to whom the gods gave both good and evil (24.534–548).

**Niobe.** The story is told for one purpose, and its frame says so: “but now let us remember supper. / For even Niobe of the lovely hair remembered food” (24.601–602), and at the end, “But come, let us two also think, brilliant old sir, / of food” (24.618–619). Between, the story: her boast against Leto, the killing of the six sons and six daughters, the nine days unburied, the burial by the gods, and her eating, “when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.603–613; [[the-children-of-niobe]], [[niobe]]). Walter Leaf notes that this is the only appearance of the Niobe legend in Homer (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.602). He reports that Aristarchus rejected the verses on Niobe turned to stone (24.614–617), asking how she could eat if she had become stone; Leaf answers that she was turned to stone later and at her own prayer, so that the verses support the point: eat now, and mourn your son afterwards (on 24.613 and 24.614; [[lonely]]).

**Peleus and Priam.** Within the parable of the jars Achilles gives the two cases at hand: the gods gave Peleus splendid gifts and one evil, a single son doomed to die young (24.534–542), and Priam was once prosperous, and now there are always battles around his city (24.543–548; [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]], [[splendid]]). The paradigm here is not a story from the past but the speaker's father and the listener himself. See [[achilles]] and [[ring-composition]].
