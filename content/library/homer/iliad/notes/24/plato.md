---
summary: The Republic rejects four passages of this book, the two jars of Zeus, Achilles tossing sleepless and wandering by the sea, the corpse given back for a price, and the draggings around the tomb of Patroclus, and twice quotes Aeschylus' Niobe beside them. The Ion gives the simile of the lead weight to the fisherman to judge, the Cratylus' etymology of Hector has its verb in Andromache's lament, and Plato's prose has λύτρα for Homer's ransom.
---

- The two jars of Zeus, “full of fates, the one of good, the other of bad” (*Republic* 2.379d–e, quoting 24.527–532 with variants): [[plato-broken-oaths]] and [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]].
- “The sufferings of Niobe”, the subject of the lines of Aeschylus that Socrates forbids, and the kin of Tantalus, “close to the gods”, from the same play (*Republic* 2.380a, 3.391e): [[niobe-play]] and [[the-children-of-niobe]].
- Thetis' complaint in Aeschylus that Apollo, who sang at her wedding, killed her son (*Republic* 2.383a–b), the charge that Hera makes in this book, “friend of the wicked, faithless always” (24.62–63): [[plato-lying-dream]] and [[apollo]].
- Achilles lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down, and wandering distraught along the shore (*Republic* 3.388a–b, quoting 24.10–12 with variants): [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]].
- Achilles “so fond of money” as to release a corpse for a price, and not otherwise (*Republic* 3.390e–391a): [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]].
- The draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus (*Republic* 3.391b, on 24.14–18): [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]].
- The lead weight on the horn of an ox, a passage for the fisherman's art to judge (*Ion* 538c–d, quoting 24.80–82 with variants): [[plato-the-fishermans-lead]].
- Hector “the holder”, from ἔχω (*Cratylus* 393a–b), and Andromache's “kept” (ἔχες, 24.730): [[plato-astyanax]].
- The three Fates, daughters of Necessity, Lachesis, Clotho and Atropos, in the myth of Er (*Republic* 10.617c); in this book the plural Μοῖραι “have put an enduring heart in men” (24.49): [[fates]].
- Chryses coming “bringing ransom for his daughter”, λύτρα, Plato's word for Homer's ἄποινα (*Republic* 3.393d): [[title-of-book-24]].
