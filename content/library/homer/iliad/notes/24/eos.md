summary: The last book is counted in dawns. Each dawn finds the sleepless Achilles by the sea, and with each he drags Hector around the mound; on the twelfth Apollo protests to the gods. Saffron-robed Dawn spreads over the earth as Priam crosses the ford with the body; Achilles' truce runs to the twelfth dawn; on the tenth Hector is burned, and at the next, rosy-fingered Dawn sees the people gather at the pyre, in the dawn verse of the first book.
# Not counted: ἠῶθεν "at dawn" (24.401) and ἅμα δʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφιν "when dawn appears" (24.600), as in Book 11.

**Dawn after dawn.** After the games the others sleep, but Achilles tosses all night and wanders on the shore, “nor would the dawn / escape him as it appeared over the sea and the shores” (24.12–13; see [[achilles]] and [[shore]]). The iterative verbs make it many nights and many dawns (see the translation's note on 24.12), and at each dawn he drags Hector three times around the mound of Patroclus (24.14–17; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Walter Leaf refers the dawn over the sea to his note on 23.227, where Dawn spreads over the sea at the burning of Patroclus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.13; see the note on Book 23). Hermes tells Priam that Achilles drags the body “whenever brilliant Dawn appears” (24.417), a phrase found only here.

**The twelfth dawn.** “But when the twelfth dawn after that had come, / then Phoebus Apollo spoke among the immortals” (24.31–32; see [[the-gods-debate]]). The verse is that of Book 1, when the gods came back from the Ethiopians on the twelfth dawn after Thetis' visit (1.493; see [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]); here, as Leaf explains, the twelve days are counted from Hector's death, though the poem has not said so (on 24.31). Hermes counts the same way, “this is the twelfth dawn / that he has lain there” (24.413–414); Leaf finds ἠώς out of place in the evening and would read ἥδε or ἤδη (on 24.413; see the translation's note on 24.413).

**Over the ford.** Hermes drives Priam out of the camp by night and leaves him at the ford of the Xanthus, “and saffron-robed Dawn was spreading over all the earth” (24.695; see [[scamander]] and [[hermes]]). The verse opened the second day of battle, with μέν for δέ (8.1 = 24.695; Leaf on 24.692; see the note on Book 8, [[saffron-robed]] and [[dawn-line]]); here it brings Hector home. Achilles had told Priam that he would see his son “when dawn appears” (ἅμα δʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφιν, 24.600), and Priam tells the Trojans that Achilles promised no harm “until the twelfth dawn comes” (24.781): the eleven days of the truce (24.664–667).

**The last dawns.** For nine days the Trojans gather wood, “but when the tenth dawn appeared, bringing light to mortals, / then they carried out bold Hector” (24.785–786; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). φαεσίμβροτος, “bringing light to mortals”, is said of the dawn only here; in the *Odyssey* it is an epithet of the sun (*Odyssey* 10.138, 10.191), and Leaf counts it among the book's Odyssean words (on 24.784; see [[bringing-light-to-mortals]]). The next morning is the poem's last: “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / then around the pyre of famous Hector the people were astir” (24.788–789; see [[rosy-fingered]] and [[early-born]]). The verse occurs twenty times in the *Odyssey* and once before in the Iliad, at the return of Chryseis (1.477; Leaf on 24.784), so that the formula that gave the poem its first dawn gives it its last.
