summary: Socrates in the Cratylus takes Hector's name for “the holder”, from ἔχω, “hold”. Andromache's last lament has both of the verbs he plays with: Hector “kept the city itself safe, and kept its devoted wives and little children”, and an ancient note, as Walter Leaf reports, says that here the poet made an etymology of Hector's name.

**The holder.** After the two names of Astyanax, Socrates asks whether Homer named Hector too. ἄναξ, “lord”, and Ἕκτωρ mean nearly the same: “of whatever a man is lord, he is surely its holder too; for plainly he rules it and possesses it and holds it” (ἔχει, *Cratylus* 393a–b; see the entry above). In this book Andromache, holding Hector's head in her hands, says the same in verse. The city will be sacked, “for you have perished, its guardian, who kept the city itself / safe, and kept its devoted wives and little children” (24.729–730; see [[andromache]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). The second verb is ἔχες, from ἔχω. Leaf reports the note of the T scholia on it, “he etymologized the name of Hector” (ἠτυμολόγησε τὸ ὄνομα Ἕκτορος), and compares Sarpedon's taunt that Hector once said he would “hold the city” without the people and the allies (ἑξέμεν, 5.473), and the verse of Book 22 on Astyanax (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.730). The first verb, ῥύσκευ, “kept … safe”, is the other half of Socrates' argument: it is the guarding that gave the boy his name, “for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (ἔρυσο, 22.507; see [[astyanax]]), here in a form found nowhere else (Leaf on 24.730).
