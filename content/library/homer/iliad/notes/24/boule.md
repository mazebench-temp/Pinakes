summary: Apollo reproaches the gods, “it is deadly Achilles, you gods, that you choose to help” (24.39); Priam tells Hecuba that if it is his fate to die at the ships, “I would have it so” (24.226); and Achilles puts Priam to bed outside, lest one of the Achaeans come in, “a counselor, one of those who always / sit beside me and take counsel with me, as is the custom” (24.651–652).
grc: [βούλεσθʼ]
en: [choose]
---

**Choosing.** βούλομαι in Homer is often “prefer, choose one thing rather than another”. Apollo uses it of the gods' partiality, βούλεσθʼ ἐπαρήγειν, that they choose to help Achilles rather than give Hector his rites (24.39; see [[apollo]]). Priam uses it of his own death: if it is his fate to die beside the ships, βούλομαι, “I would have it so” (24.224–226), a much stronger word, Walter Leaf notes, than ἐθέλω, “I am willing” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.226; see the translation's note on 24.226 and [[aisa]]).

**Counselors.** In the hut Achilles speaks of the council that meets beside him: an Achaean βουληφόρος, “a counselor”, one of those who βουλὰς βουλεύουσι, “take counsel with me”, sitting beside him “as is the custom” (ἣ θέμις ἐστί, 24.651–652; see [[counselor]], [[themis]] and [[elders]]). If one of them saw Priam, he would tell Agamemnon, and the ransoming of the body might be delayed (24.653–655; see [[apoina]]). The verse is introduced by ἐπικερτομέων, “taunting”, which Leaf takes as Achilles' bitter tone toward Agamemnon, since there is no taunt in his words to Priam (on 24.649; see the translation's note on 24.649 and [[xenia]]).
