summary: Andromache meets the body at the gates and tears her hair with Hecuba, and in the house she leads the first of the three laments, holding Hector's head. He has died young and left her a widow and their son an infant; the city will fall without its guardian, she will be carried off in the ships, and the boy will follow her into slavery or be hurled from the tower; and Hector did not die in his bed or leave her a word to remember (24.710–712, 24.723–746).

**At the gates.** In Apollo's protest she comes first of those for whom the gods should have saved the corpse, “for his wife to see, and his mother and his child” (24.36; see [[apollo]]). When the body comes home, “First his dear wife and his lady mother / tore their hair for him, rushing to the well-wheeled wagon / and touching his head” (24.710–712; see [[hecuba]], [[hair]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). In Book 22 she flung away the veil that Aphrodite gave her on her wedding day when she saw him dragged (22.468–472; see [[veil]]).

**The head in her hands.** “And among them white-armed Andromache led the lamentation, / holding in her hands the head of man-slaying Hector” (24.723–724; see [[white-armed]] and [[man-slaying]]). So Achilles held the head of Patroclus as the Myrmidons carried him to the pyre (23.136; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). Walter Leaf notes that some read ἱπποδάμοιο, “tamer of horses”, for ἀνδροφόνοιο; both are common epithets of Hector, the second the more usual (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.724).

{{quote:42f023a9-dfb9-5979-99a9-b2f4811d1568}}

ἀπʼ αἰῶνος νέος ὤλεο, “you have perished from life while young”, is a strange expression to Leaf, since it is usually the life that is taken from the man (on 24.725; see the translation's note on 24.725). The widow in the halls and the infant son are the words of her lament in Book 22 (24.725–727 ≈ 22.483–485; see [[chere]] and [[nepios]]); Leaf finds the rest of this lament quite independent of the first (on 24.726). In Book 6 she foresaw that she would soon be his widow, and begged him not to make his child an orphan and his wife a widow (6.408–409, 6.432; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). The boy will not reach his youth, ἥβη, for the city will be sacked “from top to bottom” (κατʼ ἄκρης, 24.728) now that its ἐπίσκοπος, “guardian”, is dead (24.729; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[astyanax]]). The word is used of the gods as “witnesses and overseers of agreements” (22.255), and of the scout whom Menelaus thinks Agamemnon may send “to go and spy on the Trojans” (10.38). ῥύσκευ, “kept … safe”, is an iterative found only here (Leaf on 24.730), the guarding by which the poet explained the name of Astyanax, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.403; 22.507). In ἔχες, “kept”, the T scholia hear Hector's own name, from ἔχω, “hold” (Leaf on 24.730; see the translation's note on 24.730); Sarpedon once taunted him that he had meant to hold the city, πόλιν ἑξέμεν, without people or allies (5.473).

**The ships and the tower.** The wives and children will soon be carried off in the hollow ships, “and I among them” (24.731–732; see [[captive-women]]). Hector foresaw it in Book 6, an Achaean leading her away in tears, to weave at another woman's bidding in Argos and carry water (6.454–458), and Priam pictured the sack, his sons' wives dragged off and the little children dashed to the ground (22.62–65). The boy will go with her to shameful work, “toiling for an implacable master”, or some Achaean whose brother, father or son Hector killed will take him by the hand and hurl him from the tower to grim destruction (24.732–738; see [[astyanax]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). “For your father was not kind in grim battle” (24.739): μείλιχος, “kind”, answers ἀμείλιχος, “implacable”, five verses before (24.734; see [[meilichos]]). So the people mourn him through the city, and he has brought “accursed lamentation and grief” upon his parents (24.740–741), the verse in which Euphorbus reproached Menelaus with the parents of the brother he had killed (17.37; see [[accursed]]); Leaf notes the harsh turn from the third person to the second (on 24.741). “but to me above all will grievous sorrows be left” (24.742; see [[algos]]).

**No last word.**

{{quote:03aea889-7ef8-5e0e-821f-2d800e78b34b}}

πυκινὸν ἔπος, “shrewd word”, has here more than its usual force, Leaf notes, and he quotes Paley's comparison with Tacitus' grief that he was not present at the death of his father-in-law Agricola, to receive his last commands and words and fix them in his mind (on 24.744; Tacitus, *Agricola* 45; see [[shrewd-word]] and the translation's note on 24.744). In Book 22 she had imagined Hector naked by the ships while she burned his clothes (22.508–514); now she has the body and the head in her hands, but not the farewell. “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” (24.746 = 22.515; see [[lament]]).
