---
summary: Hermes, in the likeness of a young Myrmidon, deceives Priam with a false tale of himself, an attendant of Achilles and the son of Polyctor, and tells him the truth about his son's body; he names himself only when he leaves him at Achilles' door (24.389–423, 24.460–461). Priam, who trusts the message of a goddess he saw face to face rather than the words of seers, asks the disguised god for “the whole truth” (24.220–224, 24.407).

**A god seen face to face.** When Hecuba begs Priam not to go, he answers that if any man on earth had bidden him, “seers who read the smoke of the sacrifice, or priests, / we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more; / but now, since I myself heard the goddess and looked on her face to face, / I will go” (24.221–224; see [[pseudos]], [[iris]] and [[hecuba]]). The test of a message is who brings it. Iris had told him that the slayer of Argus would go with him as his escort (24.182), yet when the god comes, Priam does not know him (see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Walter Leaf, finding that Priam knows nothing of the promise, takes Iris' repetition of it for an addition (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.181; see the translation's note on 24.181–187).

**A false tale.** The god meets Priam in the likeness of a young prince and takes his hand (24.347–361; see [[hermes]]). Priam guesses only that “some god has held his hand over me too” (24.374), and asks him the question put to strangers, who he is and of what parents (24.387). The answer is a lie:

{{quote:f54d45b5-2d25-548f-bc4d-ad4c7f4fd403}}

The tale is made to be believed. It is true to what the poem has told of the Myrmidons, idle by their ships while Achilles kept them from the fighting (24.394–395; 2.771–775; see [[myrmidons]]), and it gives the god a father like Priam, “wealthy, and an old man, just as you are” (24.398; see [[polyctor]]). Walter Leaf reports a variant in the T scholia that presses the difficulty of the god's age, since a youth with his first beard would have been a child when Achilles sailed (on 24.400; see the translation's note on 24.400). In the Odyssey the roles are reversed. Athena meets Odysseus on Ithaca in the likeness of a young man, a herdsman, delicate as the sons of kings are, and it is the mortal who answers with a false tale of himself (*Odyssey* 13.221–223, 13.256–286); in another such tale, to Penelope, “he made the many lies he told like the truth” (*Odyssey* 19.203).

**Testing.** Twice the god turns the charge of trickery on Priam: “You are testing me, old sir” (24.390, 24.433; see [[peira]]). The first time it answers a question about Hector; the second, the offer of a goblet, which a loyal attendant could not take “behind Achilles' back” (24.429–436; see [[aidos]]). πειρᾷ, Leaf notes, is an impossible contraction in Homer; he reports Payne Knight's imperatives, “test me and ask”, which do not suit the second place, and would read another form of the verb there (on 24.390; see the translation's note on 24.390).

**The whole truth.** Priam asks the supposed attendant of Achilles to “tell me the whole truth” (24.407): is his son still beside the ships, or cut limb from limb and thrown to the dogs (24.408–409)? ἀληθείην with καταλέγω, “tell the truth”, is otherwise a phrase of the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 16.226, 17.108, 21.212, 22.420). The god who lies about himself tells the truth about the dead: the dogs and birds have not touched Hector, his flesh does not rot, his wounds are closed, “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is” (24.411–423; see [[hector]]). Priam rejoices, and draws from it the lesson that gifts to the gods are repaid (24.424–428).

**Revealed.** At Achilles' gate the god says what he is, “Old man, I who have come to you am an immortal god, / Hermes; for my father granted me to you as an escort” (24.460–461), and goes. Achilles, for his part, sees through the old man's coming at once: “one of the gods led you to the swift ships of the Achaeans” (24.564), since no mortal could have passed the sentries (24.563–567; see [[night-watch]]).
