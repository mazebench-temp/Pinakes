---
summary: The mules of Book 24 carry the ransom to Achilles and bring Hector home. Zeus names them in his message; Priam's sons yoke to the mule wagon the strong-hoofed mules that the Mysians gave him; Idaeus drives them to the hut and back; they carry the corpse into Troy; and oxen and mules bring the wood for the pyre (24.149–151, 24.189, 24.263–278, 24.324–325, 24.697–718, 24.782–784).
# ἡμιόνοις (24.442), οὐρεῦσι (24.716) and the adjective "of mules", ἡμιονείην (24.189, 24.266) and ἡμιόνειον (24.268),
# occur only in this book; the other forms of the book are claimed in the notes on Books 2, 17 and 23.
grc: [ἡμιόνοις, οὐρεῦσι, ἡμιονείην, ἡμιόνειον]
---

**The message.** Zeus sends Iris to tell Priam to go to the ships alone, and only a herald with him, “an older man, to guide / the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back / the body to the city” (24.149–151), and Iris repeats the words to Priam (24.178–180; see [[iris]] and [[idaeus]]). The mules are in the plan of Zeus from the start.

**The yoking.** Priam bids his sons make ready ἄμαξαν ἐΰτροχον ἡμιονείην, “the well-wheeled mule wagon”, and bind the wicker body upon it (24.189–190). When he has driven the Trojans from his colonnade and rebuked his nine sons, they bring it out, take down the boxwood mule yoke from its peg and bind it to the pole (24.263–274; see [[wagon]]), and then “yoked the strong-hoofed mules that work in harness, / which the Mysians had once given to Priam, splendid gifts” (24.277–278). κρατερῶνυξ, “strong-hoofed”, is an epithet of horses in the Iliad (5.329, 16.724, 16.732); the Odyssey gives it to the mules that Nausicaa yokes to her wagon (*Odyssey* 6.253; see [[strong-hoofed]]). ἐντεσιεργός, “that work in harness”, occurs only here. Walter Leaf reports that according to the scholia the Mysians were famous for their mules, and that they quote Anacreon for the Mysians as the inventors of the mating of asses with mares; the Mysians were neighbors of the Eneti, “where the breed of wild mules comes from” (2.852; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.278; see [[mysians]] and [[eneti]]). The scholia also note that the mules are masculine here (τούς, 24.278) and feminine a few verses later (τάς, 24.325).

**To the hut and back.** “In front the mules drew the four-wheeled wagon, / and wise-hearted Idaeus drove them” (24.324–325), with Priam's horses behind (see [[horses]]). They halt the mules and horses to drink at the river (24.350); Hermes takes the reins and breathes noble might into the horses and the mules (24.442; see [[hermes]]); and Idaeus stays outside the hut holding them while Priam goes in (24.470–471), until Automedon and Alcimus unyoke them and take the ransom from the wagon (24.576–579). Before dawn Hermes yokes them again and drives them through the camp (24.690–691).

**The body.** At the ford Hermes leaves them, and “they drove toward the city with cries of woe and groaning / the horses, and the mules carried the corpse” (24.696–697). ἡμίονοι δὲ νέκυν φέρον is a change of construction, the mules taken up as a new subject (Leaf on 24.697), and the words νέκυν φέρον are those of Menelaus and Meriones, who carried the body of Patroclus out of the battle as mules drag a beam down a mountain (17.735, 17.742–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]]). Cassandra sees the body “lying on the bier, drawn by the mules” (24.702; see [[cassandra]]), and when the crowd at the gates would mourn Hector there till sunset, Priam calls out from the chariot, “Make way for me to pass through with the mules” (24.716). οὐρεύς is Homer's other word for the mule, the beast that Apollo's arrows struck first in the plague (1.50); this is its last use in the Iliad.

**The wood.** After the laments Priam orders the Trojans to bring wood without fear of an ambush, “So he spoke, and under the wagons oxen and mules / they yoked”, and for nine days they bring in the wood for the pyre (24.778–784; see [[cattle]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). So mules hauled the wood for the pyre of Patroclus down from Ida (23.110–124), and Nestor once proposed that the Achaeans bring in their dead “with oxen and mules” (7.332–333).
