# μόρφνον θηρητῆρʼ and περκνόν (24.316): μόρφνος and περκνός are found only here in Homer.
grc: ["μόρφνον θηρητῆρʼ", περκνὸν]
en: [the dark hunter, the dusky eagle]
passages:
  - 24.283-321 | Priam's libation and the eagle
---

**The sign.** Hecuba tells Priam to pour a libation to Zeus and ask for “a bird of omen, his swift messenger, the one that to him / is dearest of birds, and whose strength is greatest, / on the right” (24.292–294; see [[birds]] and [[messengers-of-zeus]]). The eagle is not named in her words or in Priam's prayer, which repeats them (24.310–313), but it is the bird that the poem calls “at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds” (21.253). “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him, / and at once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds, / the dark hunter, which men also call the dusky eagle” (24.314–316). The second verse is the verse of the eagle that Zeus sent to Agamemnon in Book 8 (8.247; see [[surest-of-birds]]).

**Its names.** μόρφνος and περκνός are found only here in Homer. Walter Leaf takes μόρφνος as a noun, the name of a kind of eagle, as the accent that Aristarchus approved shows; its etymology and meaning are unknown. περκνός describes the color, “dappled, spotted with dark”, as ripening grapes ὑποπερκάζουσιν, “begin to darken”, in the orchard of Alcinous (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.316; *Odyssey* 7.126). The translation renders them “dark” and “dusky” (see the translation's note on 24.316). θηρητήρ, “hunter”, is the eagle's name in Book 21 too, where Achilles springs away from the river “with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter” (21.252; see [[like-an-eagle]]), and Leaf compares those verses. [[aristotle|Aristotle]] names a second kind of eagle, next in size and strength to the first, which lives in glens and ravines and by lakes and is also called the duck-killer and the μορφνός, “which Homer too mentions in the going out of Priam” (*History of Animals* 9.32; see [[title-of-book-24]]). [[hesiod|Hesiod]] fletches the arrows of Heracles with the wings of a dark eagle, μόρφνοιο φλεγύαο (*Shield* 134). The Suda glosses μορφνόν as “dark”, and calls the μορφνός a kind of eagle, the only one, it says, that does not hunt but feeds on dead bodies (Suda μ 1268–1269).

**The wings.** “As wide as the door of the high-roofed chamber / of a wealthy man is made, well fitted with bolts, / so wide were its wings on either side” (24.317–319; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]). It darts on the right across the city, “and when they saw it / they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (24.320–321), and Priam sets out at once (24.322). The sign comes true: Hermes brings him through the camp to Achilles, and he comes home with his son's body (24.331–467, 24.677–697; see [[hermes]]).

Zeus sends the eagle twice in the Iliad, both times in answer to a prayer: to Agamemnon in the Achaeans' need (8.245–252) and to Priam before his journey. The eagle that flew on the left of the Trojans at the ditch was a warning that Hector would not heed (12.200–250; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]). Priam asks for his sign and trusts it.
