---
summary: Priam would rather go down into the house of Hades than see Troy sacked. Achilles, releasing Hector's body, asks Patroclus not to be angry if he learns of it, even in the house of Hades.
---

**Priam's wish.** Driving the Trojans from his colonnade, Priam ends: “But as for me, / before I see the city sacked and ravaged / with my eyes, may I go down into the house of Hades” (24.244–246; see [[priam]]). δόμον Ἄϊδος εἴσω ends the verse in the curses and prayers of earlier books: that whoever began the war might die “and go down into the house of Hades” (3.322), and Nestor's picture of Peleus praying that his spirit might go “down into the house of Hades” for shame at the Achaeans (7.131). Priam's wish is for himself. In Book 22 he foresaw his own death at the doors of his house when the city fell (22.59–76), and said that his grief for Hector would bring him down to the house of Hades (22.425; see the note on Book 22).

**Even in the house of Hades.** When the body has been washed and lifted onto the wagon, Achilles groans and calls Patroclus by name: “Do not be angry with me, Patroclus, if you learn, / even in the house of Hades, that I have released brilliant Hector / to his dear father, since the ransom he gave me is not unseemly” (24.592–594; see [[patroclus]] and [[apoina]]). He greeted the dead man in the same words at the beginning and at the end of the funeral, “Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the house of Hades” (23.19, 23.179; see the note on Book 23), and he had promised that “even if in the house of Hades men forget the dead, / yet even there I will remember my dear companion” (22.389–390). Here he supposes that the dead can hear what the living do, and he promises Patroclus a share of the ransom (24.595; see the translation's note on 24.594).
