---
summary: Proclus answers Plato on three passages of this book: the jars of Zeus, whose evils are the hindrances of the active life, as Achilles' example of Peleus shows; the ransom, which may have been the custom of the time, or a general's policy; and the dragging, which Achilles makes good by laying the body on the bier with his own hands. He also restates Plato's charge against Achilles tossing on his bed.
---

- The two jars, “full of fates”, as the two series of things that the maker of the world gives to souls, and Peleus' evil, childlessness and the lack of care in old age (*On the Republic* I 96–100 Kroll, quoting 24.527–528 in Plato's form and 24.534–541): [[plato-broken-oaths]].
- Achilles “now lying on his back, now face down, now distraught on his side”, pouring dust on his head (*On the Republic* I 122 Kroll, on 24.10–12): [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]].
- The ransom for Hector's body, perhaps a custom of the time, “to accept ransom for the bodies of enemies”, or a general's policy of cutting down the enemy's resources (*On the Republic* I 143–145 Kroll): [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]].
- The draggings around the tomb, the Thessalian custom, and Achilles lifting the washed body onto the bier himself (*On the Republic* I 150–151 Kroll, quoting 24.587–589): [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]].
