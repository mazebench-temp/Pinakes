summary: γέρων is Priam's word in the last book, said of him more than forty times: “the old man” in the narrative, “old man” and “old sir” in the mouths of Hermes and Achilles. Zeus sends with him a herald, “an older man” (24.149); Hermes tells him, “You are not young yourself, and this man who goes with you is old” (24.368), and claims a father who is “an old man, just as you are” (24.398); Achilles calls him “brilliant old sir” (24.618) and “dear old man” (24.650).
grc: [γεραίτερος]
en: [older]
---

**The old man.** The narrator names Priam by his age as he sits in the dung of his courtyard, as he chooses the ransom and drives out the Trojans, as he mounts his chariot, on his way home, and when he orders the wood for the pyre (24.162–164, 24.236, 24.248, 24.322, 24.715, 24.777; see [[priam]]). Seven times a speech of his is introduced by a verse with γέρων Πρίαμος θεοειδής, “the old man, godlike Priam”, a formula found only in this book (24.217, 24.372, 24.386, 24.405, 24.552, 24.634, 24.659; see [[godlike]]). Zeus' plan provides the old man with an older companion, the herald who is to drive the mules, κῆρυξ … γεραίτερος (24.149, 24.178; see [[idaeus]]); γεραίτερος is found elsewhere in the Iliad only when Nestor claims the right to speak after Diomedes as the older man (9.60).

**Old age as weakness.** The stranger who meets Priam at the river points to it at once: neither Priam nor the herald is young enough “to ward off a man, when someone is the first to grow angry” (24.368–369; see [[hermes]]), a verse that Walter Leaf finds less natural here than in the Odyssey, where it recurs (*Odyssey* 16.73, 21.133; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.369). The god's false father, Polyctor, is “wealthy, and an old man, just as you are” (24.398; see [[polyctor]]), so that the young Myrmidon, like Achilles, has an old father at home (see [[old-age-and-memory]]). Achilles' mother is the daughter of another old man, the Old Man of the Sea (24.562; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]]).

**Old sir, old man.** γεραιέ, “old sir”, is Hermes' address twice, in his half-line “You are testing me, old sir” (24.390, 24.433), and Achilles' once, δῖε γεραιέ, “brilliant old sir”, as he bids Priam eat (24.618; see [[fasting]]); elsewhere in the Iliad the vocative is said to Nestor, by Diomedes, Odysseus and Patroclus, and to Phoenix (10.164, 10.558, 11.648; 9.607, 17.561). γέρον, “old man”, is the address of Agamemnon's threat to Chryses at the poem's beginning (1.26); in the last book Achilles uses it in warning, “Do not provoke me any longer, old man” (24.560, 24.569), in report, “And you too, old man, we hear, were prosperous once” (24.543), and in kindness, “dear old man” and “old Priam” (24.650, 24.669; see [[book-1-and-book-24]] and [[xenia]]).
