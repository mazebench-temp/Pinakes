summary: Apollo complains that Achilles “has lost all pity, and he has no shame” (24.44). Thetis feels shame to go among the gods in her grief (24.90), and Zeus keeps her “respect and your friendship” (24.111); Hecuba fears that Achilles will not “respect you at all” (24.208); Hermes, in his disguise, fears Achilles and feels shame to rob him (24.435); and Priam's plea is “But respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me” (24.503).
grc: [αἰδεῖο]
---

**Apollo's line on shame.** Apollo's verse on αἰδώς goes on, “which greatly harms men and helps them” (24.45). The line was athetized in antiquity as taken from Hesiod, where it follows “shame that is no good attends a needy man” (*Works and Days* 317–318), and Walter Leaf agrees that it is senseless here, since the αἰδώς that does harm is no reproach to Achilles; the double character of shame is a commonplace, and he compares Phaedra's two kinds of αἰδώς, the one not bad and the other a burden on houses (Euripides, *Hippolytus* 385–386; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.45; see the translation's note on 24.42–45). Leaf thinks 42–45 probably all interpolated (on 24.42; see [[savage-as-a-lion]]).

**Among the gods.** Thetis, summoned to Olympus, answers Iris, “I feel shame / to mingle with the immortals, and I have griefs past measure in my heart” (24.90–91; see [[thetis]] and [[achos]]); she goes in a black veil (24.93–94). Zeus receives her “keeping your respect and your friendship for the time to come” (24.111): Leaf takes τεήν as either her respect for him or his for her, and the phrase as built on the familiar pair αἰδοίη τε φίλη τε, with which Thetis was greeted in Hephaestus' house (18.386; on 24.111; see [[philotes]] and [[zeus]]).

**The suppliant's words.** Hecuba's fear, that Achilles “will not pity you / or respect you at all” (24.207–208), repeats Hector's for himself before the duel (22.123–124; see [[pity]]). Priam asks for respect not for himself but for the gods, and pity for himself (24.503), where Lycaon had asked Achilles, “respect me and pity me”, and Hecuba her son, “respect this, and pity me” (21.74, 22.82; see [[supplication]]). αἰδεῖο is an irregular form, found again only in Odysseus' appeal to the Cyclops to respect the gods, since he and his men are suppliants (*Odyssey* 9.269); Leaf takes it as an early instance of αἰδέομαι, the verb that later replaced αἴδομαι, and would not alter it (on 24.503). Zeus had said, and Iris told Priam, that Achilles “will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (24.158, 24.187).

**Fear and shame.** Offered a goblet, the young Myrmidon refuses to take gifts behind his master's back: “I fear him and feel shame above all in my heart / to rob him, lest some harm come to me afterward” (24.435–436; see [[hermes]] and [[doron]]). Fear and αἰδώς go together, as in the heralds who stood before Achilles “in fear and awe of the king” (1.331).
