---
summary: The ransom is to bring Achilles gifts “that may warm his heart” (24.119), and when Zeus' eagle appears over Troy, “the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (24.321). Apollo says that “the Fates have put an enduring heart in men” (24.49); Hermes bids Priam beg Achilles “so that you may stir his heart” (24.467), and Achilles warns him, “So now do not stir my heart any more among its sorrows” (24.568). Hecuba and Helen begin their laments with Hector, “by far the dearest to my heart” (24.748, 24.762).
---

**The warmed heart.** θυμὸν ἰήνῃ, “that may warm his heart”, ends the message of Zeus each time it is given, to Thetis, to Iris, by Iris to Priam, and by Priam to Hecuba (24.119, 24.147, 24.176, 24.196; see [[doron]] and [[apoina]]). The heart of all who see the omen is warmed (θυμὸς ἰάνθη, 24.321; see [[eagle]]), as Menelaus' was when Antilochus gave him the mare (23.597–600).

**The enduring heart.** Apollo's τλητὸν … θυμόν, “an enduring heart”, is what lets a man who has lost a brother or a son weep and leave off (24.46–49; see [[fates]]). Walter Leaf notes that τλητός is active in sense here, “tolerant”, a use with few Homeric parallels and a suspicious likeness to Attic, and counts it, with the plural Μοῖραι, among the marks of late origin (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.49). Priam has such a heart: he has endured, ἔτλην, what no other mortal has, and Achilles asks how he dared, ἔτλης, to come alone (24.505, 24.519; see [[supplication]]). Achilles' answer speaks to the heart: “truly you have endured many evils in your heart” (ἄνσχεο σὸν κατὰ θυμόν, 24.518), and, at the end of his consolation, “Bear up, and do not mourn relentlessly in your heart” (24.549).

**The stirred heart.** Hermes' advice is that Priam should beg by Achilles' father, mother and child, ἵνα οἱ σὺν θυμὸν ὀρίνῃς, “so that you may stir his heart” (24.465–467; see [[supplication]]), and the plea does stir it, to weep for his father (24.507; see [[himeros]]). Achilles then fears the other stirring: “do not stir my heart any more among its sorrows” (24.568), and he has the body washed apart, lest his own heart be stirred at Priam's anger (ὀρινθείη φίλον ἦτορ, 24.585; see [[etor]] and [[cholos]]). After the meal he takes Priam's hand at the wrist, “so that he might feel no fear in his heart” (24.671–672; see [[xenia]]).

**Heart and might.** Priam's own μένος and θυμός bid him go to the ships (24.198; see [[menos]]); Hecuba, though against it, brings him wine to pour, “with grieving heart” (24.283), and tells him to pray, “since your heart / urges you to the ships” (24.288–289; see [[hecuba]] and [[prayer]]). The θυμός is life too: Priam has not slept since his son “lost his life at your hands” (ὤλεσε θυμόν, 24.638; see [[hypnos]]).
