summary: The last book tells the dragging as a custom of grief. Each dawn Achilles ties Hector behind his chariot, drags him three times around the mound of Patroclus and leaves him face down in the dust, while Apollo keeps the body from all defilement; Apollo tells the gods that Achilles defiles the senseless earth, Hermes tells Priam that the dragged body is not disfigured, and Hecuba remembers in her lament that it did not raise Patroclus up (24.12–22, 24.50–54, 24.416–421, 24.754–756).

**Each dawn.** When the games are over and Achilles cannot sleep, the dragging becomes the work of his mornings:

{{quote:b8a02f3f-fd97-5230-aa9e-2f3bbe6b1e7c}}

The iterative forms, δινεύεσκε and λήθεσκεν of his nights (24.12–13) and δησάσκετο, παυέσκετο and ἔασκεν of the dragging (24.15–17), with the optative ζεύξειεν, “whenever he had yoked”, make it a thing done day after day; Walter Leaf counts five iteratives in six lines, and finds that they agree with the twelve days of 24.31, though they sit awkwardly after a passage that seemed to tell of a single night (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.12; see the translation's note on 24.12). τρίς, “three times”, is the number of the circuits that the Myrmidons drove around Patroclus' body with their chariots (23.13), and of the circuits of the city in the chase (22.165; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). προπρηνέα, “face down” (24.18), is the posture in which Achilles first laid him in the dust beside Patroclus' bier (πρηνέα, 23.25; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

**The aegis.** Apollo, “pitying the man, / though he was dead”, keeps πᾶσαν ἀεικείην, “all defilement”, from his flesh, and covers him with the golden aegis, “so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him” (24.18–21; see [[apollo]] and [[aegis]]). ἀεικείη, the noun of ἀεικίζω, “defile”, is found only here in Homer (see [[aeikizo]]), and ἑλκυστάζων, “dragging”, only here and in the verse of Aphrodite's oil in Book 23 (24.21 ≈ 23.187), where Aphrodite and Apollo had already kept the dogs and the sun from the body (23.184–191). The ancients athetized 24.20–21: the aegis is Zeus', not Apollo's, contact with a corpse does not suit its sanctity, and Book 23 has given another means of keeping the body; Leaf thinks the reasons enough, but takes the passage for older than 23.186–187 and its model (on 24.20; see the translation's note on 24.20–21). “So in his rage he defiled brilliant Hector” (24.22): ἀείκιζεν μενεαίνων, the words Apollo will use (24.54).

**What the gods say.** The blessed gods pity him as they look on, and urge Hermes to steal him away (24.23–24; see [[hermes]]). On the twelfth dawn Apollo puts the dragging before them: other men have lost a brother or a son, and leave off weeping, “But this man, when he has robbed brilliant Hector of his dear life, / ties him to his horses and drags him around the mound of his dear companion; / and that is not more honorable for him, nor better” (24.50–52); “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.53–54; see [[nemesis]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Leaf takes κωφὴ γαῖα as “the senseless clay”, the dead body, and rejects the reading that Achilles outrages the earth by dragging the body over it: “such a thought is surely not Epic” (on 24.54; see the translation's note on 24.54). Aristotle quotes the line to show that anger ends with the death of its object (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30). Zeus decides that the body shall be ransomed, not stolen (24.71–76; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]).

**Not disfigured.** Priam asks the young stranger whether Achilles has already “cut him limb from limb and set him before his dogs” (24.408–409; see [[priam]]). Hermes answers that the body lies whole: “Truly he drags him around the mound of his dear companion / ruthlessly, whenever brilliant Dawn appears, / yet he does not disfigure him” (24.416–418; see [[hector]]). ἀκηδέστως, “ruthlessly”, is said only here and of the dragging that Andromache saw from the wall, “were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans” (22.465; see [[andromache]]). οὐδέ μιν αἰσχύνει, “yet he does not disfigure him”, answers Priam's fear on the wall, that the dogs would disfigure an old man's gray head (αἰσχύνωσι, 22.74–75; see [[aischros]]).

**Hecuba's memory.** In her lament Hecuba sets Hector against her other sons, whom Achilles sold across the sea: “but when he had taken your life with the long-edged bronze, / he dragged you many times around the mound of his companion / Patroclus, whom you killed; and even so he did not raise him up” (24.754–756; see [[hecuba]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]). ῥυστάζεσκεν, “he dragged”, is the iterative of a verb that the Odyssey uses of the suitors, who drag the serving women shamefully through the house (*Odyssey* 16.108–109, 20.318–319). Leaf takes the thought to be that Achilles' treatment of Hector, harsher than of her other sons, has ended only to his honor, since the body lies fresh as dew, a sign of the gods' favor (on 24.752). οὐδʼ ὧς, “even so”, answers Achilles' own words to Priam: grief will not raise up Hector either, οὐδέ μιν ἀνστήσεις, “nor will you raise him up” (24.551; see [[achilles]]).
