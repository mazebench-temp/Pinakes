summary: In the last book the gods turn the story from Achilles' anger to the ransom. Apollo keeps Hector's body from harm; the gods debate, and Zeus sends Thetis to Achilles and Iris to Priam; he answers Priam's prayer with an eagle and sends Hermes, who meets him in disguise, puts the sentries to sleep, opens the gates, reveals himself and goes, and returns at night to lead him out. Achilles releases the body by Zeus' command and by his own mind together, and knows that a god brought Priam; Priam and Hecuba see the gods' care in the body that lies fresh as dew.

**The body kept.** The first act of a god in the book is Apollo's: he keeps all defilement from Hector's flesh and covers it with the golden aegis, so that the dragging may not tear it (24.18–21; see [[apollo]] and [[aegis]]). The men see only the result. Hermes, still in disguise, tells Priam that the body lies fresh as dew after twelve days, its wounds closed, “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is, since he was dear above all to their hearts” (24.422–423); Priam answers that the gods have remembered Hector's gifts “even in the portion of death” (24.428; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 24.428), and Hecuba says the same over the body (24.749–750, 24.757–759; see [[olympians]]).

**The will of Zeus.** The gods debate what to do, and Zeus decides: not theft but ransom (24.22–119; see [[the-gods-debate]] and [[zeus]]). His will reaches the two men by messengers, Thetis to her son and Iris to Priam (24.120–187; see [[thetis]] and [[iris]]), and Priam, who has seen the goddess face to face, goes because a god has spoken (24.220–224). When Hecuba bids him ask for a sign, he prays and Zeus sends an eagle on the right (24.290–321; see [[prayer]], [[divination]] and [[eagle]]).

**The god in disguise.** Zeus sends Hermes to guide Priam, and he goes “in the likeness of a young prince, / with the first down on his lip” (24.347–348; see [[hermes]], [[divine-epiphany]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Priam does not know him, but sees a god's hand in the meeting: “But still some god has held his hand over me too, / who sent such a wayfarer to meet me, / one of good omen” (24.374–376). Hermes breathes might into the horses and mules, pours sleep over the sentries, opens the gates and pushes back the bars (24.440–447), and only at the door of Achilles' hut does he say who he is: “Old man, I who have come to you am an immortal god, / Hermes” (24.460–461). He will not go in, since “it would be a thing for blame / for an immortal god to welcome mortals so openly” (24.463–464; see [[nemesis]]). At night he comes back while the other gods sleep, wakes Priam, yokes the animals, drives them through the camp unseen, and leaves them at the ford of the Xanthus as dawn spreads over the earth (24.677–695).

**Two causes.** Achilles gives the release two causes in one breath: “I have it in mind myself / to release Hector to you; for a messenger came to me from Zeus, / my mother” (24.560–562). The god's command and the man's own mind work together, as they do throughout the poem. Walter Leaf reads his sudden anger at Priam's haste as the sign of a struggle that he must work out in his own way and at his own time: he is irritated at prayers to do what he has already decided, under compulsion, to do (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.560). He sees the god's hand, too, in what no man could have done: “one of the gods led you to the swift ships of the Achaeans. / For no mortal would dare to come, not even one in his prime, / into the camp; for he would not escape the sentries, nor would he / easily push back the bar of our doors” (24.564–567). The narrator has shown both, the sentries asleep and the bar drawn back by Hermes (24.445–446, 24.457).
