The Little Iliad told the deed, and Walter Leaf quotes the lines that Tzetzes gives from it, in which the son of Achilles leads Hector's wife to the ships and throws the child from the tower by the foot; the lines are made of Homer's own phrases.

**The lines.** On Andromache's “will take you by the hand and hurl you from the tower to grim destruction” (24.735), Walter Leaf writes that the story of the death of Astyanax “was handled by the Cyclics”, and quotes from Tzetzes five lines of the *Little Iliad* of Lesches (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.735; see the translation's note on 24.735):

> αὐτὰρ Ἀχιλλῆος μεγαθύμου φαίδιμος υἱὸς
> Ἑκτορέην ἄλοχον κάταγεν κοίλας ἐπὶ νῆας,
> παῖδα δʼ ἑλὼν ἐκ κόλπου ἐϋπλοκάμοιο τιθήνης
> ῥῖψε ποδὸς τεταγὼν ἀπὸ πύργου· τὸν δὲ πεσόντα
> ἔλλαβε πορφύρεος θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή.

“But the glorious son of great-hearted Achilles led Hector's wife down to the hollow ships, and took the child from the bosom of his fair-tressed nurse, and seizing him by the foot hurled him from the tower; and as he fell, purple death and mighty fate seized him.” The poem has the son of Achilles do both things that Andromache foresees in this book, the wives carried off in the hollow ships, “and I among them” (24.731–732), and the child thrown from the tower (24.734–735; see [[andromache]], [[astyanax]] and [[neoptolemus]]).

**Homer's phrases.** The lines are built of the Iliad's. The nurse's bosom is the one into which the boy shrank back at the sight of his father's helmet, “into the bosom of his fair-belted nurse” (6.467; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). “Seizing him by the foot hurled him” is said word for word by Hephaestus of Zeus, who “seized me by the foot and hurled me from the divine threshold” (ῥῖψε ποδὸς τεταγὼν, 1.591; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). The last verse is a formula of death in battle, “purple death and mighty fate” (5.83, 16.334, 20.477). Where Andromache says that the Achaean will take the boy “by the hand” (χειρὸς ἑλών, 24.735), the *Little Iliad* has him seized by the foot. The death is told again in Euripides' *Trojan Women*, where the Greeks decide on it in assembly (see [[euripides-astyanax]]).
