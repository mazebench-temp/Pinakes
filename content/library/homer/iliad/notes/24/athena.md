**The gray-eyed maiden.** When the gods would have Hermes steal Hector's body, “This pleased all the others, but never Hera, / nor Poseidon, nor the gray-eyed maiden” (24.25–26; see [[the-gods-debate]], [[hera]] and [[poseidon]]). γλαυκώπιδι κούρῃ, “the gray-eyed maiden”, is the only place in the Iliad where she is named so, without Ἀθήνη; κούρη Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus”, is her title beside the name (5.733, 8.384; see [[gray-eyed]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]). The three hold to the hatred they conceived for Ilios when Alexander “insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard, / and praised her who gave him his grievous lust” (24.29–30; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). Athena is one of the two goddesses whom he passed over. The poem has shown her hatred before without giving its cause: she shook her head at the prayer and the robe of the Trojan women (6.311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]), she protested when Zeus thought of saving Hector, and she brought him down at the hands of Achilles (22.177–181, 22.446). In the last book she takes no part in the debate.

**Making way.** When Iris brings Thetis up to Olympus, “she sat down beside father Zeus, and Athena made way for her” (24.100; see [[thetis]]). Walter Leaf explains that Athena sits next to Zeus as his favorite daughter, and cites a fragment of Pindar, quoted by the scholia, in which she sits nearest to her father's right hand (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.100). She gives her place to the mother of the man she has helped through the poem, from the quarrel of Book 1 to the death of Hector (1.194–222; 22.214–247; see [[athena-restrains-achilles]] and [[achilles]]).
