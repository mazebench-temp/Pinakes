The poem's own judgment is given to Apollo and to Hecuba.

**The draggings.** Plato's plural, “the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus” (τὰς Ἕκτορος ἕλξεις περὶ τὸ σῆμα τὸ Πατρόκλου, *Republic* 3.391b), fits the book. Its verbs are iterative: whenever Achilles had yoked his horses, he would tie Hector behind the chariot, drag him three times around the mound of the son of Menoetius, rest in his hut and leave him face down in the dust (24.14–18; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). Walter Leaf counts five such verbs within six lines, which show that the passage “really belongs to a period of several days” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.12), the days until the twelfth dawn after Hector's death (24.31; see the translation's note on 24.12). Plato's words are Homer's too: περὶ σῆμα, “around the mound”, is said of the dragging four times in the book, by the narrator, by Apollo, by Hermes and by Hecuba (24.16, 24.51, 24.416, 24.755).

**The note on 24.15.** The ancient note that Gigon prints among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems* is on the second line of the passage, in which Achilles ties Hector behind his chariot (fr. 389 Gigon). Its question is the one this book raises, why Achilles dragged Hector around the tomb, against the customs concerning the dead; its first answer looks back to Books 17 and 18, and its second, the Thessalian custom of dragging bodies around tombs, to the practice of its own day (see the entry above and [[aristotle]]). Proclus gives the custom in a verse of Callimachus, “still, as of old, the Thessalian man drags the slayers around the tomb of the dead” (*On the Republic* I 150 Kroll). Callimachus' verb, ῥυστάζω, “drag about”, is the one Hecuba uses in this book of Achilles dragging Hector around the mound, and its only use in the Iliad: “he dragged you many times around the mound of his companion / Patroclus, whom you killed” (πολλὰ ῥυστάζεσκεν ἑοῦ περὶ σῆμʼ ἑτάροιο, 24.755–756; see [[callimachus]] and [[hecuba]]).

**The poem's judgment.** Book 24 does not leave the question to its later readers. Apollo, protesting among the gods, says that Achilles “ties him to his horses and drags him around the mound of his dear companion; / and that is not more honorable for him, nor better”, and warns that the gods may grow indignant with him (24.51–54; see [[apollo]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Hermes tells Priam that Achilles “drags him around the mound of his dear companion / ruthlessly, whenever brilliant Dawn appears, / yet he does not disfigure him” (24.416–418): the gods keep the body fresh as dew, its wounds closed (24.18–21, 24.411–423; see [[hermes]]). Hecuba ends the matter in her lament, “and even so he did not raise him up” (24.756). The defenders answer Plato; none of them answers Apollo.

**Proclus' proof.** Proclus ends his defense with the end of the story. Serving the will of the higher powers, Achilles plans gentler things for Hector, and cares for the body with his own hands: “And when the slave women had washed him and anointed him with oil, / and put a fine mantle and a tunic around him, / Achilles himself lifted him and laid him on a bier” (24.587–589, quoted at *On the Republic* I 151 Kroll). So, Proclus concludes, every act toward the dead was given its proper measure: Achilles honored his friend above all by exacting from the enemy the penalty for his unholy intention, and paid back the enemy's outrage with his kindness to Priam and the last care of the body (I 151 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). In the poem Achilles has the body washed out of Priam's sight, lest Priam see his son and fail to hold back his anger, and Achilles' heart be stirred to kill him (24.582–586; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]).
