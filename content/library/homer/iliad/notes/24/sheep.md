summary: The sheep of Book 24 are the meat of two meals in Achilles' hut, the great shaggy ram that is being sacrificed for the morning meal when Thetis comes (24.124–125) and the silver-white sheep that Achilles slaughters for his supper with Priam (24.621–627); in Apollo's simile they are “the flocks of men” on which the lion feeds (24.43).
# μῆλα βροτῶν (24.43), the flocks of the lion simile. The "ram" of 24.125 renders ὄϊς.
grc: ["μῆλα βροτῶν"]
en: [great shaggy ram]
---

**The morning meal.** Thetis comes to her son's hut and finds him groaning without ceasing, while his companions “were busy in haste and making ready their morning meal; / and a great shaggy ram was being sacrificed for them in the hut” (24.124–125). ὄϊς is a sheep of either sex, and the masculine adjectives, λάσιος μέγας, make this one a ram (see [[ram]]). λάσιος, “shaggy”, is said in the Iliad only of the hairy breast and heart of a man (1.189, 2.851, 16.554); in the Odyssey it is the shaggy belly of the Cyclops' ram, under which Odysseus escapes from the cave (*Odyssey* 9.433). ἱέρευτο, “was being sacrificed”, is the verb of the slaughter of a beast for a meal as for an offering; Walter Leaf discusses the form and settles on the imperfect (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.125; see the translation's note on 24.125). Thetis asks her son how long he will eat out his heart, “remembering neither food / nor bed” (24.129–130).

**The supper.** When the body has been laid on the wagon, Achilles tells Priam that they too must think of food, as Niobe remembered food after her twelve children were killed (24.599–620; see [[niobe]]). “He spoke, and springing up swift Achilles slaughtered a silver-white sheep” (24.621). ἄργυφος, “silver-white”, is said again in Homer only of the flocks that shepherds graze in the land of the Laestrygonians (*Odyssey* 10.85; see [[silver-white]]). His companions flay it and cut it up, spit it and roast it (24.622–624), in verses of the feast in Ajax's honor in Book 7 (24.623–624 = 7.317–318; see [[feast-type-scene]]); Automedon sets out the bread and Achilles serves the meat (24.625–626), in the verses in which Patroclus served the bread and Achilles the meat to the envoys in Book 9 (9.216–217); Automedon has taken Patroclus' place. Priam eats for the first time since Hector's death (24.641–642).

**The lion's flocks.** In Apollo's simile the lion that gives way to its strength “goes among the flocks of men to take his feast” (εἶσʼ ἐπὶ μῆλα βροτῶν, 24.43; see [[savage-as-a-lion]]). μῆλα are the small flocks, sheep and goats together (see [[goats]]); Leaf finds βροτῶν, “of mortals”, strange, but possible in the mouth of a god (on 24.42).
