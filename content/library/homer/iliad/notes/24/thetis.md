summary: The gods cannot steal Hector's body unknown to Achilles, Zeus says, since his mother is always beside him; Iris summons her instead. She comes up from her cave in a black veil, ashamed for her grief to mingle with the immortals; Hera gives her a golden cup, and Zeus tells her to say to her son that the gods are angry with him. She sits close beside Achilles, bids him eat and sleep and lie with a woman, since his death is near, and gives him the message that he must accept a ransom for the body. Achilles later tells Priam that the messenger from Zeus was his mother.

**Always beside him.** When the gods would have Hermes steal Hector's body, Zeus answers that it cannot be done unknown to Achilles, “for his mother / is always beside him, night and day alike” (24.72–73; see [[the-gods-debate]] and [[zeus]]). Aristarchus athetized 71–73 because Thetis is not always with her son; Walter Leaf calls the reason futile, since a goddess who can hear from afar and come in a moment may fairly be said to stand always beside him, as Aphrodite, in the same verb (παρμέμβλωκε), “is always at his side” with Paris (4.11; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.71; see the translation's note on 24.71–73). Zeus would rather have her called to him, to give her son his word (24.74–76).

**In the cave.** Iris finds her in a hollow cave with the goddesses of the sea around her, as in Book 18 they gathered to her in the depths when she heard Achilles groan (18.35–51; see [[nereids]] and [[iris]]); she is weeping “for the fate of her noble son, who was to / perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (24.85–86). The second verse is the one in which Zeus wept tears of blood for Sarpedon, whom Patroclus “was to / kill in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (16.460–461; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]): here the verb is turned to “perish”. Aristarchus athetized it as superfluous, a criticism Leaf calls unworthy of him (on 24.86), and Leaf reports Rhianus' reading “who was soon to perish”, plausible because the nearness of the death would add to her sorrow (on 24.85; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). Her answer:

{{quote:435499bb-372b-5534-9df4-db17e2727c6a}}

αἰδέομαι, “I feel shame”: her grief keeps her from the company of the gods (see [[aidos]]). κάλυμμα, “veil”, is found only here in Homer; Leaf takes it for the head covering called καλύπτρη, which Hecuba flings away when she sees Hector dragged (22.405–406), and κρήδεμνον, with which Hera covers herself (14.184). Only here in Homer is black a sign of mourning, and Leaf compares Demeter in her hymn, who in grief for her daughter threw a dark veil over both her shoulders (κυάνεον δὲ κάλυμμα, *Hymn to Demeter* 42; on 24.93; see [[veil]] and the translation's note on 24.93). δῖα θεάων, “the brilliant goddess” (see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]).

**On Olympus.** Iris leads her up, and she sits down beside father Zeus, among all the gods; Athena makes way for her, and Hera puts a golden cup in her hand (24.95–102; see [[athena]] and [[hera]]). Zeus begins with her grief: “You have come to Olympus, goddess Thetis, grieved though you are, / with grief not to be forgotten in your heart; I know it myself” (24.104–105). πένθος ἄλαστον, “grief not to be forgotten”, is Penelope's grief for Odysseus in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 1.342; see the translation's note on 24.105 and [[penthos]]). The glory he gives Achilles is given “keeping your respect and your friendship for the time to come” (24.111): the friendship that made him grant her prayer in Book 1 (1.503–530; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Leaf takes the phrase from αἰδοῖός τε φίλος τε, “respected and dear”, as Charis greets Thetis in the house of Hephaestus (18.386), and leaves open whether the respect is hers for Zeus or his for her (on 24.111).

**With her son.** “So he spoke, and the goddess silver-footed Thetis did not disobey, / and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (24.120–121; see [[silver-footed]]), the verse of Athena's descents (2.167, 4.74, 22.187). She finds Achilles groaning in his hut while his companions make ready a meal, sits down close to him, “his lady mother, / and stroked him with her hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (24.126–127), the verse with which she comforted him at the beginning of the poem (1.361; see [[achilles]]). Her speech has two parts. The first is her own: “My child, how long, mourning and grieving, / will you eat out your heart, remembering neither food / nor bed? It is good even to lie with a woman / in love; for you will not live long for me, but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (24.128–132; see [[mighty-fate]]). The last two verses are Patroclus' prophecy to Hector (16.852–853). Aristarchus obelized 130–132 on moral grounds, as advice unfit for a mother; Leaf notes that Aristotle quotes them without offense (Aristotle names only the bed, “as Homer says”, *Nicomachean Ethics* 3.11, 1118b10–11), and that 131–132 seem to be taken from Book 16 (on 24.129; see the translation's note on 24.129–132). The second part is the message: “But listen to me quickly: I am a messenger to you from Zeus” (24.133), almost the Dream's verse in Book 2 (2.26; see [[messengers-of-zeus]]), and Zeus' anger in Zeus' words, with “come, release him, and accept a ransom for the body” (24.134–137). She does not tell him that Priam himself will come (see [[zeus]]). Achilles consents, and “mother and son / spoke many winged words to one another” (24.141–142).

**In others' words.** Hera tells the gods that she reared Thetis and gave her to Peleus, and that all the gods came to the wedding (24.59–63; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). Hermes bids Priam beg Achilles by his father, “his mother of the lovely hair / and his child” (24.466–467), but Priam speaks only of the father (see the translation's note on 24.466–467). Achilles tells Priam that the gods gave Peleus, a mortal, “a goddess as his wife” (24.537; see [[peleus]]), and that “a messenger came to me from Zeus, / my mother, who bore me, the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea” (24.561–562; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]]). It is the last mention of her in the Iliad. In the *Odyssey* the soul of Agamemnon tells how, when Achilles died, his mother came up out of the sea with the immortal goddesses of the sea when she heard the news, and a wondrous cry went over the water (*Odyssey* 24.47–49).
