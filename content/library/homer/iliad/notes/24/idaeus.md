summary: Priam's herald is the one companion Zeus allows him, an older man to drive the mule wagon and bring back the body. He drives the mules across the plain, sees Hermes coming in the dusk and counsels flight or supplication, waits with the animals while Priam goes in to Achilles, is led into the hut and given a chair, sleeps in the porch, and drives home at dawn, “crier of the city”, beside her father when Cassandra sees them (24.149–151, 24.281–282, 24.322–357, 24.470–471, 24.577–578, 24.674–701).

**The one companion.** Zeus bids Priam go alone, but allows him one man: “Let a herald go with him, an older man, to guide / the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back / the body to the city” (24.149–151 = 24.178–180; see [[heralds]] and [[zeus]]). The herald is with Priam as the horses are yoked, “the herald and Priam, with shrewd counsels in their minds” (24.281–282), a verse said of them again as they lie down to sleep in Achilles' porch (24.674). In front the mules draw the four-wheeled wagon, “and wise-hearted Idaeus drove them” (24.324–325; see [[mules]] and [[wagon]]); δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (see [[wise-hearted]]): in Book 7 he was “wise” and “knew wise counsels” (7.276, 7.278).

**The stranger.** Beyond the tomb of Ilus, as the animals drink at the river in the dusk, “the herald, seeing him close by, made out / Hermes, and he spoke to Priam and said: / ‘Take thought, descendant of Dardanus; this is work for a prudent mind. / I see a man, and I think he will soon tear us to pieces. / But come, let us flee on the horses, or else / clasp his knees and entreat him, in the hope that he may have pity’” (24.349–357; see [[hermes]], [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[supplication]]). φραδέος νόου, “a prudent mind”, is found only here (see the translation's note on 24.354). He counsels flight or supplication, and Priam's mind is confounded with fear (24.358–360). Hermes sees it too: “You are not young yourself, and this man who goes with you is old, / to ward off a man” (24.368–369).

**At Achilles' hut.** Priam leaps down from the chariot “and left Idaeus there; and he stayed, holding / the horses and the mules” (24.470–471). When the ransom is taken, Automedon and Alcimus unyoke the animals “and led in the herald, the old man's crier, / and sat him on a chair” (24.576–578; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]). Hermes wakes Priam before dawn, and the old man “roused the herald” (24.689), and at dawn Cassandra sees “her dear father / standing in the chariot, and the herald, crier of the city” (24.700–701; see [[cassandra]]). καλήτωρ, “crier”, and ἀστυβοώτης, “crier of the city”, are titles found only here, and Walter Leaf takes them for the same (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.701). In Book 7 Idaeus carried the Trojans' offer to the Achaean assembly at the ships, and brought back the truce for the burning of the dead (7.372–420; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]); here he goes with his king to the hut of Achilles, and brings back the body.
