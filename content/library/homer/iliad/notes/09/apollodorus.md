---
summary: The Library tells in full several stories that Book 9 only alludes to. Its Phoenix is blinded by his father on a false charge and healed by Chiron; Achilles is hidden on Scyros at the court of Lycomedes; Idas fights Apollo for Marpessa, who chooses Idas; and Meleager dies in two versions, one of which keeps the plot of Phoenix's story.
---

- **Phoenix.** Amyntor blinded his son on the false charge of seduction brought by his concubine Phthia; Peleus took him to Chiron, who restored his sight, and made him king of the Dolopians (3.13.8). In Homer Phoenix did lie with the concubine, at his mother's urging, and was cursed, not blinded (9.449–457): [[phoenix]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]].
- **Scyros.** When Calchas declared that Troy could not be taken without Achilles, Thetis, knowing he was fated to die if he went, dressed him as a girl and entrusted him to Lycomedes; at the court he had a son, Pyrrhus, later called Neoptolemus, by Lycomedes' daughter Deidamia (3.13.8). The Iliad knows only that Achilles took Scyros, where he won Iphis (9.667–668), and that his son was being reared there (19.326–327): [[scyros]].
- **Idas and Marpessa.** Idas carried off Marpessa in a winged chariot that Poseidon gave him; her father Evenus, unable to catch them, killed his horses and threw himself into the river Lycormas, which was afterward called Evenus. Apollo met Idas and would have taken the girl, until Zeus parted them and let her choose, and she chose Idas, fearing that Apollo would desert her when she grew old (1.7.8–9). Phoenix alludes to the quarrel in a few lines (9.557–564): [[idas-and-apollo]], [[marpessa]] and [[evenus-father-of-marpessa]].
- **Meleager.** In the first version Meleager's life hangs on a brand that the Fates foretold, and his mother burns it when he has killed her brothers over the boar's skin. “But some say that Meleager did not die in that way”: in the second, the sons of Thestius claimed the skin, war broke out between the Curetes and the Calydonians, Althaea cursed her son for killing some of her brothers, and he stayed at home in anger until, with the enemy at the walls and the citizens begging him, he yielded to his wife, went out, killed the rest of the brothers and fell fighting (1.8.2–3). The second version follows Phoenix's story and adds the death that Homer does not tell: [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[the-death-of-meleager]].
