---
summary: Book 9 counts women among the gifts, seven women of Lesbos chosen from Achilles' own spoils, Briseis returned under oath, and twenty Trojan women from a city not yet taken. Achilles answers that he loved Briseis as a man loves his wife; Ajax answers that the quarrel is over one girl; and the night ends with Achilles and Patroclus asleep beside women taken in Achilles' raids.
passages:

  - 9.663-668 | The night in Achilles' hut
---

**Women as gifts.** Agamemnon's list moves from tripods, gold, cauldrons and horses to women without a change of tone. He will give “seven women skilled in flawless handiwork, women of Lesbos, whom, when he himself took well-built Lesbos, I chose out for myself, who surpassed the tribes of women in beauty” (9.128–130). The women were spoils of Achilles' own raid, and Agamemnon took them as his share; the offer gives back to Achilles what he won, as his complaint about the spoils of the twenty-three cities will say (9.328–333; see [[lesbos]] and [[achilles-raids]]). With them will come Briseis, returned with an oath that Agamemnon never lay with her (9.131–134; see [[briseis]] and [[oath]]). When Troy falls Achilles may choose twenty Trojan women, “the most beautiful after Argive Helen” (9.139–140; see [[trojan-women]]): the women of the city are given away before it is taken (see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

**A wife won by the spear.** Achilles answers in the language of marriage. Agamemnon “keeps the wife dear to my heart” (ἄλοχον θυμαρέα, 9.336), and the war itself is for a wife: “Was it not for the sake of Helen of the lovely hair? Are they the only ones of mortal men who love their wives, the sons of Atreus?” He loved Briseis “from my heart, though she was won by my spear” (δουρικτητήν, 9.339–343; see [[husbands-and-wives]]). The captive is a γέρας and a wife at once, and the contradiction is his grievance (see [[geras]]). He means to take home what he won by lot, among it “fair-belted women” (9.366). Ajax hears only the arithmetic: all this “for the sake of a girl, one girl; and now we offer you seven, by far the best” (9.637–638).

**The night.** The book's last scene in the hut is told in six lines. Achilles sleeps in the inner part of the hut, and beside him lies Diomede, daughter of Phorbas, “whom he had brought from Lesbos”; beside Patroclus lies Iphis, whom Achilles gave him “when he took steep Scyros” (9.663–668; see [[diomede]] and [[iphis]]). Briseis is still in Agamemnon's huts. The poem lays the man who has just spoken of his love for her beside another captive, a woman of Lesbos, the island whose women Agamemnon has just offered him, and does not comment.

**Women in the stories.** Phoenix's quarrel with his father began with a concubine (παλλακίς), whom his father loved while he dishonored his wife, and with his mother's plea that he lie with the girl first (9.449–453; see [[concubines]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). Meleager is moved at last by his wife's account of what happens to the women of a taken city: “strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (9.593–594; see [[women-and-war]]).
