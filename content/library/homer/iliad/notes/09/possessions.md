**What can be acquired.** The book's words for wealth belong to one verb, κτάομαι, “acquire”. A man who had Agamemnon's gifts would not be ἀκτήμων, “without possessions”, of gold (9.126, 9.268); Achilles would enjoy at home “the possessions (κτήμασι) that the old man Peleus acquired (ἐκτήσατο)” (9.400); and Peleus loved Phoenix like an only son, “his darling, heir to great possessions” (κτεάτεσσι, 9.482). Achilles counts his own: the treasures (κειμήλια) he took from twenty-three cities and gave to Agamemnon (9.330–333), the “great many things” he left at home, and the gold, red bronze, fair-belted women and gray iron that he won by lot and will carry back (9.364–367; see [[division-of-spoils]]).

**No price.** Against the gifts he sets all the wealth he can name, ten and twenty times what Agamemnon has, all that comes into Orchomenus and into Egyptian Thebes, “where the most treasures lie stored in the houses” (κτήματα), and as much as the sand and the dust (9.379–385; see [[thebes-in-egypt]] and [[orchomenus]]). Then he measures it against his life. Nothing is worth it, not all that Ilios possessed “in former days, in time of peace”, nor the treasures shut within the stone threshold of Apollo at Pytho (9.401–405; see [[pytho]] and [[threshold]]), the riches of a city and of a god's sanctuary.

The verses turn on the verb: cattle and sheep are ληϊστοί, “to be had by raiding”, tripods and horses κτητοί, “to be acquired”, but a man's soul is neither λεϊστή nor ἑλετή once it has passed the teeth (9.406–409; see [[psyche]], [[tripod]], [[horses]] and [[raiding]]). κτητοί and ἐκτῆσθαι occur only here in Homer.
