summary: At night, after the rout, Agamemnon has the heralds call each man to assembly by name, without shouting; he proposes in earnest the flight he proposed as a test in Book 2, Diomedes claims the speaker's right to oppose him, and Nestor turns the meeting to supper, sentries and a council of the elders.
passages:

  - 9.32-51 | Diomedes' answer
  - 9.52-79 | Nestor's counsel
---

**The summons.** “And the son of Atreus, stricken at heart with great grief, went to and fro, ordering the clear-voiced heralds to summon every man by name to the assembly, but not to shout” (9.9–12; see [[heralds]]). The verse of the order echoes his summons in Book 2 (9.10 ≈ 2.50), where nine heralds had to shout down the din (2.96–98). This assembly meets in the dark, in grief, and quietly. κλήδην, “by name”, is found only here; later the same night Agamemnon tells Menelaus to wake the leaders “naming each man by his father and his lineage” (10.68). The men sit, and Agamemnon stands up weeping (9.13–16; see [[assembly-type-scene]]).

**The proposal.** Agamemnon tells the army that Zeus has deceived him and proposes flight, in the verses of his test in Book 2, now in earnest (9.17–28 = 2.111–118, 2.139–141; see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[agamemnon]]). In Book 2 the army ran for the ships. Here it sits in silence (9.29–30; see [[silence]]).

**The speaker's right.** Diomedes breaks the silence and claims a right of the assembly: “Son of Atreus, I will fight with you first, for your folly, as is the custom, my lord, in the assembly; and do not be angry at all” (9.32–33). The custom (θέμις) is the right to speak against the king in the place where all may speak, and Diomedes uses it to answer a reproach made on the battlefield, Agamemnon's taunt that he was unwarlike (9.34–36; 4.370–400; see [[themis]] and [[diomedes]]). The army shouts its approval in the verses that greeted his refusal of Paris' offer in Book 7 (9.50–51 = 7.403–404).

**Nestor.** Nestor stands up to praise Diomedes and to finish what he began: “you have not reached the end of the matter” (9.52–56; see [[nestor]]). He turns the meeting from flight to the night's work, supper for the army, sentries outside the wall, and a feast and council of the elders in Agamemnon's hut: “This night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.65–78; see [[feast]], [[night-watch]] and [[boule]]). “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (9.79), nearly the verse with which the Trojans obeyed Priam in their assembly in Book 7 (7.379). The meeting is not dismissed; the sentries go out (9.80).

**Two night assemblies.** At nightfall of the same day Hector had gathered the Trojans on the plain, away from the ships, and they had shouted applause at his orders for the fires and the dawn (8.489–542; see [[hector]]). The Achaeans meet in the camp in grief, and their assembly ends with orders for a guard.

**Where men become distinguished.** Phoenix reminds Achilles that when Peleus sent him to Troy he knew nothing “of war, common to all, nor of assemblies, where men become distinguished”, and that Phoenix was sent to teach him (9.440–443; see [[phoenix]]). The phrase recalls “the assembly, where men win glory”, which Achilles has not entered since the quarrel (1.490–491; see [[where-men-win-glory]]).
