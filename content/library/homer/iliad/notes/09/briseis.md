summary: Briseis is at the center of the gifts and of the refusal. Agamemnon offers to give her back with an oath that he never lay with her; Achilles calls her “the wife dear to my heart”, though she was won by his spear; and Ajax reproaches him for holding out “for the sake of a girl, one girl”.

**Nestor's reproach.** Nestor dates his disapproval from the day “when you, sprung from Zeus, took the girl Briseis / and went from the hut of Achilles in his anger” (9.106–107; see [[taking-of-briseis]] and [[nestor]]). His next words, “for you took his prize and keep it” (9.111), turn on Agamemnon the complaint that Achilles made to Thetis, Thetis to Zeus, and Thersites to the army: “he has taken my prize and keeps it” (1.356; 1.507, 2.240; see [[geras]]).

**A gift with an oath.** Agamemnon puts her among the gifts that “shall be at hand at once”: with the seven women of Lesbos “will be the one I took from him then, / the daughter of Briseus; and I will swear a great oath besides / that I never went up into her bed or lay with her” (9.131–133), and Odysseus repeats it to Achilles (9.273–275; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]], [[oath]] and [[lesbos]]). Agamemnon names her by her father, κούρη Βρισῆος, as Achilles did when he told Thetis of the girl “whom the sons of the Achaeans gave me” (1.392; see [[briseus]]). The oath is sworn in Book 19, over a boar, and she is led out of Agamemnon's hut as the eighth of the women (19.245–246, 19.258–265).

**“The wife dear to my heart.”**

{{quote:1b69306b-0186-52ec-a5b8-423edd5528d5}}

Achilles calls her ἄλοχος, “wife”, the word for the wives of the sons of Atreus in the next lines, and θυμαρής, “dear to the heart”, the phrase the Odyssey uses of Penelope in Odysseus' arms (ἄλοχον θυμαρέα, Od. 23.232). δουρικτητή, “won by the spear”, occurs only here in Homer. The war is fought for one wife, Helen (9.339), and Achilles sets his own loss beside Menelaus'. In Book 19 Briseis says that Patroclus promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife and take her to Phthia (19.297–299; see [[captive-women]] and [[helen]]).

**One girl.** The gifts do not move him; his prize was taken “in his arrogance” by the very man who gave it (9.367–368). Ajax measures the refusal against the blood-price that a man accepts for a brother or a son: the gods have given Achilles an unrelenting heart “for the sake of a girl, / one girl; and now we offer you seven, by far the best” (9.632–638; see [[ajax]] and [[poine]]). That night Achilles sleeps beside Diomede, a captive from Lesbos, and in the last book Briseis lies beside him again, in the same verses (9.663–665, 24.675–676; see [[diomede]]).
