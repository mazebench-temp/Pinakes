**The envoys.** The envoys come to Achilles as guests. They are seated and fed (9.199–221; see [[xenia]]), and no one clasps his knees or takes his chin, as Thetis did when she begged Zeus (1.500–502; see [[supplication-type-scene]]). Yet the book gives their errand the suppliant's words. Agamemnon “has sent the best men to entreat you” (λίσσεσθαι, 9.520), Phoenix says, and Achilles must not put their words to shame, “or their coming” (9.522–523). Ajax ends with the claim of the roof: “respect your own roof; we are here beneath your roof” (αἴδεσσαι δὲ μέλαθρον, 9.640; see [[aidos]]). Diomedes closes the night with the same verb: “I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus, offering countless gifts” (9.698–699). In Book 18 Thetis will tell Hephaestus how the embassy came in the words of Phoenix's story, τὸν δὲ λίσσοντο γέροντες, the elders begged him (18.448 = 9.574; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**Phoenix's suppliants.** Phoenix's speech is a string of entreaties. His own story begins with one: his mother “kept begging me by my knees” to lie with his father's concubine (λισσέσκετο γούνων, 9.451), a mother supplicating her son (see [[gounata]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). His kinsmen, “begging me hard”, kept him at home for nine nights (9.464–465). Men turn even the gods with sacrifice and prayer, “entreating them, whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (9.499–501). Then come the Prayers, λιταί, the daughters of Zeus. Whoever respects them when they draw near is helped; whoever refuses them, “then they go to Zeus, son of Cronus, and pray that Blind Folly may follow him” (9.508–512; see [[litai]] and [[ate]]). In the allegory a refused entreaty is itself a wrong that Zeus punishes.

**Meleager.** The story of Meleager is told as a series of supplications, each by someone closer to him than the last. The elders of the Aetolians “begged him” and sent the best priests of the gods (9.574–575). His father Oeneus stood on the threshold of his chamber, “shaking the jointed doors, imploring his son” (9.581–583). His sisters and his mother begged him, and then his companions (9.584–587), and at last his wife (9.590–591). γουνούμενος, from γουνοῦμαι, “clasp the knees”, is said of a father who stands outside a shut door: the verb of the gesture has come to mean only “implore”. Meleager refuses them all until the enemy is on the towers, and then yields to his wife's account of a city taken (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[meleager]]).

Achilles refuses the three envoys, though each answer gives a little more (see [[achilles]]). Agathe Thornton read the whole Iliad through its supplications (*Homer's Iliad: Its Composition and the Motif of Supplication*, 1984).
