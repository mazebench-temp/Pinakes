summary: Book 9 argues the quarrel of Book 1 again with the two men apart. Agamemnon admits his blind folly but still demands that Achilles submit to the more kingly and the elder; Achilles, through the envoys, repeats every charge he made in the assembly and adds new ones, and by the end of the night the army's counselors blame him.

**Apart.** In Book 1 the two men quarreled face to face in the assembly. In Book 9 they never meet. Agamemnon speaks in his hut and Achilles in his, and envoys carry the words between them, Odysseus each way (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[message-repeated]]). Neither gives way.

**Agamemnon.** Nestor, who tried to reconcile them in Book 1 (1.247–284), tells Agamemnon that he gave way to his proud heart and dishonored the best of men against all advice (9.106–111). Agamemnon admits it, “I was blinded” (9.116; see [[ate]]), but his offer ends where the quarrel began:

{{quote:07e672bc-3b87-50b2-8055-78166e3440b0}}

In Book 1 he took Briseis “so that you may know well how much greater I am than you” (1.185–186), and Nestor told Achilles that Agamemnon “is the greater, since he rules over more” (1.281). Now Agamemnon says it of himself, in two comparatives, “more kingly” (βασιλεύτερος) and “the elder by birth” (see [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[agamemnon]]). Odysseus leaves the lines out when he repeats the offer (9.260–306).

**Achilles.** Achilles' answer to Odysseus takes up the charges of Book 1 one by one, and sharpens them. He did the fighting and Agamemnon took the spoil (9.328–336; 1.163–168; see [[achilles-raids]]). The war is fought for the wife of one of the Atreidae, and his own wife was taken (9.337–343; 1.152–160; see [[war-for-menelaus]] and [[taking-of-briseis]]). The insults of the assembly return. Agamemnon is “clothed forever in shamelessness” (9.372), as he was “clothed in shamelessness” in Achilles' first reproach (ἀναιδείην ἐπιειμένε, 1.149), and a dog: “dog though he is”, he would not dare to look Achilles in the face (κύνεός περ ἐών, 9.373), as he had “the eyes of a dog” (1.225; see [[clothed-in-shamelessness]], [[dogs]] and [[anaideia]]). There are new charges: Agamemnon is a deceiver (9.344, 9.371, 9.375–376), and “Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (9.377). Achilles answers the demand that Odysseus did not repeat, as if he had heard it. Let Agamemnon choose another son-in-law, “one who befits him and who is more kingly” (9.392). In Book 1 he threatened to go home to Phthia (1.169–171); now he names the day: he will sail tomorrow and reach Phthia on the third day (9.356–363).

**Blame.** By the end of the night the blame has begun to shift. Phoenix grants that Achilles' anger was blameless until now (9.523), but warns that the man who refuses the Prayers is visited by Blind Folly in his turn (9.510–512; see [[litai]]). Ajax calls him savage, hard and pitiless (9.628–632), and Diomedes tells Agamemnon that he should never have entreated him, “he is proud enough as it is” (9.698–699). The quarrel ends only in Book 19, when Achilles renounces his anger and Agamemnon lays the blame for his act on Zeus, Fate and the Erinys, and on Ate herself (19.56–144; see [[ate]]).
