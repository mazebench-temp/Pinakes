---
summary: In Agamemnon's offer beauty is a measure of value: seven women of Lesbos who surpassed all women in beauty, twenty Trojan women the most beautiful after Helen, a daughter of the king. Achilles refuses the daughter even if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty, and asks whether the war itself was not fought for Helen of the lovely hair.
passages:
  - 9.128-140 | The women in the offer
  - 9.388-392 | Achilles refuses Agamemnon's daughter
---

**Beauty priced.** The women in the offer are valued as the horses and tripods are, by their excellence. The seven from Lesbos “surpassed the tribes of women in beauty” (κάλλει ἐνίκων, 9.130), and the twenty Trojan women Achilles may choose will be “the most beautiful after Argive Helen” (9.139–140; see [[captive-women]]). Helen is the standard against whom the women of Troy are counted, as she was the cause of the war in which they will be taken (see [[helen]]).

**Beauty refused.** Achilles refuses the king's daughter in the measures of a goddess: “not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty and equaled gray-eyed Athena in handiwork; not even so will I marry her” (9.389–391; see [[aphrodite]] and [[athena]]). Beauty and skill, the two things Agamemnon praised in Chryseis (1.113–115), are named only to be set aside. The same speech asks what the war was for: “Was it not for the sake of Helen of the lovely hair?” (9.339).

**In the stories.** Phoenix's world is full of the same words. His father's concubine was “fair-haired” (καλλικόμοιο, 9.449); Meleager's wife is “the beautiful Cleopatra” (9.556), and her mother Marpessa is “of the lovely ankles”, the bride for whom Idas drew his bow against Apollo (9.557–560; see [[idas-and-apollo]]). The woman who lies beside Achilles at the end of the night is “fair-cheeked Diomede” (9.665; see [[diomede]]).
