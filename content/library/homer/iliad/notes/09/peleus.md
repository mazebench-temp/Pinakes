**The charge.** Odysseus ends his appeal with the words of Achilles' father:

{{quote:4fc6a8eb-4665-57e8-8cf9-e26fb24f762c}}

φιλοφροσύνη, “kindliness”, occurs only here in Homer. Odysseus heard the charge himself, when he and Nestor came to Peleus' house to gather the army, and in Book 11 Nestor remembers another: Peleus told his son “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (11.783–784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]). Each speaker gives Peleus the words his purpose needs (see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Phoenix recalls the same day in the same verse, “on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon” (9.439 = 9.253): “the old horse-driving Peleus” sent him with the boy to teach him to be “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.438–443; see [[phoenix]] and [[horse-driving]]).

**The house of Peleus.** Agamemnon offers Achilles one of his daughters, to be led “to the house of Peleus” without bride-price (9.146–147, 9.288–289; see [[marriage]]). Achilles refuses: “Peleus himself will surely seek out a wife for me then” among the daughters of the chiefs in Hellas and Phthia, and his heart had long been set on marrying and enjoying “the possessions that the old man Peleus acquired” (9.393–400; see [[hellas]] and [[phthia]]). The son who speaks of going home to his father's house tells in the next lines of the fate that will keep him from it (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). In Book 24 he tells Priam that he will not be there to care for Peleus in his old age (24.538–542).

**Phoenix's Peleus.** When Phoenix fled his father Amyntor, he came to Phthia, and Peleus took him in:

{{quote:0a17f63e-0109-59c3-9bae-48536b65dd1d}}

The king of Phthia takes in an exile and makes him a son, as he took in the boy Patroclus when Menoetius brought him from Opus after a killing (23.84–90; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]], [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]], [[amyntor]] and [[dolopians]]). Phoenix, who could have no son of his own, raised Peleus' son as his (9.485–495).

The patronymic frames the night. Nestor sends the envoys “to the hut of Achilles, son of Peleus” (9.166), in the words with which Agamemnon sent his heralds to take Briseis (1.322), and at the end Diomedes wishes that Agamemnon had never entreated “the noble son of Peleus” (9.698; see [[son-of-peleus]]).
