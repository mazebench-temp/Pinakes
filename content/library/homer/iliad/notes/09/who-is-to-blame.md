summary: In the assembly Agamemnon blames Zeus for his defeat; in the council, faced with Nestor, he blames himself, “I was blinded; I do not deny it”, and the gods are named only as the proof. Phoenix grants that Achilles' anger has been blameless until now, and warns that refusing the gifts will make it blameworthy.
passages:
  - 9.17-25 | Zeus has bound me fast in blind folly
  - 9.104-120 | Nestor's charge and Agamemnon's admission
  - 9.515-523 | If he were not bringing gifts
---

**Zeus, then himself.** Agamemnon speaks twice of the disaster, and gives two answers. Before the army he repeats the complaint of Book 2: Zeus “has bound me fast in heavy blind folly”, promised him Troy and deceived him (9.18–22; see [[ate]] and [[apate]]). Before the elders Nestor names the man to blame, Agamemnon, who took the prize “not at all as we would have it” (9.106–111). The king accepts it without any plea: “Old man, it is no lie, your account of my blind folly. I was blinded; I do not deny it myself” (9.115–116). He was blinded “obeying my wretched mind” (9.119), and Zeus appears only as the one who loves Achilles and has beaten down the army (9.116–118; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]). In Book 19, before the whole army and with Achilles present, he will say “I am not to blame” and name Zeus, Moira and the Erinys (19.86–89). The council in Book 9 hears the plainest admission he ever makes.

**Blame for refusing.** The envoys move the question from Agamemnon to Achilles. Phoenix concedes that “before now there was no blame in your anger”, but now the king gives gifts and has sent the men dearest to him (9.515–523; see [[nemesis]]). The Prayers make the same point in an allegory: the man who refuses them is followed by Blind Folly (9.510–512; see [[litai]]). Ajax lays the hardness of Achilles' heart on the gods: “in you the gods have put a heart that is unrelenting and evil” (9.636–637). Achilles answers with the charge he has made since Book 1, that the wrong is Agamemnon's, who “deceived me and did me wrong” (9.375), and with the memory of the insult (9.646–648).

**A father's curse.** Phoenix's own story gives the gods a share in a family's wrong. His father's curse was fulfilled by “Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.456–457), and in the bracketed lines it was a god who checked his own hand from killing his father (9.458–461; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]).
