---
summary: Book 9 adds two words of the same root: ἐραννός, “lovely,” of Calydon, twice in Phoenix's story, and ἐπήρατος, “lovely,” of the feast in Odysseus' speech.
grc: [ἐραννῆς, ἐπηράτου]
---

**Calydon.** “The Aetolians defending lovely Calydon” (9.531), and “where the plain of lovely Calydon was richest” (9.577; [[calydon]], [[aetolians]], [[perikalles]]). ἐραννός, from ἔραμαι (“love, desire”), the root of ἐρατεινός, is used in Homer only here and once in the Odyssey, of the city of the Phaeacians (Od. 7.18). Calydon was “rocky” in the Catalogue (2.640; [[rocky]]); in Phoenix's story, a city under siege, it is lovely.

**The feast.** Odysseus begins his speech by setting the feast aside: “But the business of the lovely feast is not our concern” (9.228; [[feast]]). ἐπήρατος, “lovely,” from the same root, describes a feast here, a citadel and its wealth on the shield of Achilles and in Hector's thoughts before he faces Achilles (18.512, 22.121), and Ithaca, “lovelier than horse-pasturing land,” in Telemachus' praise of his island (Od. 4.606). The translation renders all three words “lovely.”
