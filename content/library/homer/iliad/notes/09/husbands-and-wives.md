summary: Achilles claims a husband's love for Briseis and asks whether the sons of Atreus alone love their wives. Agamemnon offers him a royal marriage, which he refuses for a wife of his father's choosing; in Phoenix's stories a father's neglect of his wife starts a family's ruin, and Meleager's wife is the one who moves him to fight.

**A captive called a wife.** Achilles names Briseis ἄλοχος, “wife”, and claims for his love of her what the Atreidae claim for Helen: “For any man who is good and sensible loves his own and cares for her, as I too loved her from my heart, though she was won by my spear” (9.341–343). The war was fought because one man's wife was taken; his was taken by the commander of that war (see [[captive-women]] and [[briseis]]). In Book 19 Briseis says that Patroclus promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife (19.297–299).

**A marriage refused.** Agamemnon offers him a better match, any of three daughters, without bride-price and with a dowry such as no father ever gave (9.144–148; see [[marriage]] and [[fathers-and-daughters]]). Achilles refuses her even if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty and gray-eyed Athena in handiwork, the two measures of a wife (9.388–391; see [[beauty-and-its-price]]). If he comes home, Peleus will seek out a wife for him, and of the chieftains' daughters in Hellas and Phthia he will take whichever he wishes, “a wedded wife, a fitting mate”, and enjoy his father's possessions (9.393–400). The marriage he imagines belongs to the long life at home that he says he may still choose (9.414–416).

**Husbands and wives in the stories.** Phoenix's family broke apart over a husband's neglect: Amyntor loved his concubine “and he dishonored his wife, my mother”, and she begged her son to take the girl first, so that she would turn from the old man (9.449–452). In the story of Meleager the wife is the last and strongest appeal. Meleager lies beside “his wedded wife, the beautiful Cleopatra” while the Curetes storm the city (9.556), and when friends and kin have failed, “his fair-belted wife begged Meleager, weeping” (9.590–591), and he goes (see [[cleopatra]]). Her own mother Marpessa was a bride fought over by a man and a god (9.557–564; see [[idas-and-apollo]]).
