summary: Agamemnon offers Achilles one of his three daughters without bride-price, with a dowry such as no man ever gave and seven cities; Achilles refuses the marriage, says that Peleus will find him a wife at home, and calls Briseis his wife. In Phoenix's speech a father's concubine breaks a marriage, and Meleager lies apart beside his wedded wife.

**The offer.** After the gifts that are “at hand at once” and the spoil of Troy, Agamemnon offers a marriage: “And if we come to Achaean Argos, the udder of the plowland, he would be my son-in-law; and I would honor him equally with Orestes” (9.141–143). Achilles may take whichever of the three daughters he will, Chrysothemis, Laodice or Iphianassa, “as his own, without bride-price, to the house of Peleus; and I will give a dowry besides, very great, such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter”, and seven cities near Pylos (9.144–153; see [[chrysothemis]], [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]], [[iphianassa]] and [[the-seven-cities]]). ἀνάεδνος is “without ἕδνα”, the gifts a suitor gives for his bride (16.178, 22.472). Othryoneus asks Priam for Cassandra on the same terms, promising a great deed instead (13.365–369). μείλια, found only in these two verses, are the gifts the father sends with her (see the translation's note on 9.146–147). A marriage to the king's daughter, with land, is how a king binds a great stranger to him: the king of Lycia kept Bellerophon so (6.191–195; see [[bellerophon]]). Here the father pays the price, and the son-in-law is to know his place: “let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.160; see [[message-repeated]]).

**The refusal.** Achilles answers the offer last of all the gifts:

{{quote:fb15c0fd-f82f-56d7-a9c6-cc08bd15f7b1}}

He will marry at home. “For if the gods keep me safe and I come home, Peleus himself will surely seek out a wife for me then” (9.393–394), one of the daughters of the chieftains of Hellas and Phthia, “to marry a wedded wife, a fitting mate, and enjoy the possessions that the old man Peleus acquired” (9.395–400; see [[peleus]] and [[hellas]]). In Book 13 Idomeneus taunts the dying Othryoneus with the same offer: the Achaeans too would give him the most beautiful of Agamemnon's daughters, brought from Argos, if he helped them sack Ilios (13.374–382).

**Briseis.** Achilles calls the captive his wife. “Are they the only ones of mortal men who love their wives, the sons of Atreus? For any man who is good and sensible loves his own and cares for her, as I too loved her from my heart, though she was won by my spear” (9.340–343; see [[briseis]] and [[captive-women]]). Briseis will say that Patroclus promised to make her his wedded wife (19.297–299).

**In Phoenix's story.** Phoenix's father “dishonored his wife, my mother” for a concubine, and the quarrel drove the son from the house (9.449–452; see [[concubines]]). Meleager in his anger “lay beside his wedded wife, the beautiful Cleopatra” (μνηστῇ ἀλόχῳ, 9.556), and it is his wife (παράκοιτις) who at last moves him to fight (9.590–596; see [[cleopatra]]). Her mother Marpessa was a bride (νύμφη) for whom Idas drew his bow against Apollo (9.557–560; see [[marpessa]] and [[idas]]).
