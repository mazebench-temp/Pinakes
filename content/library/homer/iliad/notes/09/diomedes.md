---
summary: Diomedes speaks first and last among the Achaeans in Book 9. When Agamemnon proposes flight he throws the king's old reproach back at him and swears to fight on with Sthenelus; when the envoys bring back Achilles' refusal he breaks the same silence, tells the king he should never have begged, and bids the army eat, sleep and fight at dawn.

**The silence broken.** “For a long time the sons of the Achaeans were silent in their grief; at last Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them” (9.30–31). The verse that brings him in brought him in when Idaeus offered Paris' treasures in Book 7 (7.399), and it brings him in again at the end of this book (9.696). Each time he breaks a silence, and each time he speaks for fighting on (see [[silence]] and [[good-at-the-war-cry]]).

**The answer to the king.**

{{quote:bf458942-aa28-5abd-af9c-5d3898d0bde0}}

In Book 4 he heard the king's rebuke in silence, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered”, and silenced Sthenelus when he answered for them (4.401–418). Now he claims the speaker's right in the assembly (9.33; see the translation's note on 9.32–33). The charge he remembers is his own summary, “unwarlike and a coward” (ἀπτόλεμον καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, 9.35); Agamemnon had asked why he cowered and called him worse than his father in battle (4.370–400; see [[tydeus]]). He gives the reproach back in the same word: Zeus gave Agamemnon the scepter, but not ἀλκή, “courage” (9.34, 9.39; see [[alke]] and [[scepter]]). Then, calling him “Strange one” (δαιμόνιε, 9.40; see [[strange-one]]), he sends him home with the words of the king's own taunt to Achilles, “Run away, then, if your heart so urges you” (1.173): “If your own heart is so eager to go home, go”, for his ships from Mycenae stand by the sea (9.42–44; see [[agamemnon]] and [[mycenae]]).

**The two of us.** If the rest go too, “the two of us, I and Sthenelus, will fight until we find the end of Ilios; for we came here with a god” (9.48–49). νῶϊ, “we two”, is the dual with which he answered Sthenelus in Book 4, “let us two also think of our furious courage” (4.418; see [[sthenelus]]); the two of them had taken Thebes, as Sthenelus boasted, “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (4.408). τέκμωρ Ἰλίου, “the end of Ilios”, is the phrase of Apollo's plan in Book 7 (7.30–31). Achilles will answer it from his hut: “you will no longer find the end of steep Ilios” (9.418–419), and Odysseus carries those words back to the kings (9.685–686). The army shouts its approval in the verses that greeted his answer to Idaeus (9.50–51 = 7.403–404), and Nestor praises him as the best in counsel of all the men of his age (9.53–58; see [[nestor]]).

**The last word.** When Odysseus has reported the refusal, the evening's silence falls again, in nearly the same verses, and Diomedes speaks again (9.693–696):

{{quote:8f413868-483d-5094-bb4a-ef1c5286a29b}}

ἀγήνωρ and ἀγηνορίη, “proud” and “pride”, are the words for a man's high spirit, here gone too far. Diomedes is the only speaker of the book who wishes the gifts had never been offered (see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). His forecast comes true in its way: Achilles goes back to war when Patroclus' death has moved his heart and Iris, sent by Hera, bids him rise (ὄρσεο, 18.170), the verb of “a god rouses him” (ὄρσῃ, 9.703). Then he gives the orders of the night. “Come then, let us all do as I say” (9.704) is the verse with which Agamemnon began to propose flight (9.26 = 2.139); Diomedes uses it to send the army to food, wine and sleep, “for in that is might and courage” (9.705–706; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]), and to send the king, at dawn, to “fight yourself among the foremost” (9.707–709). “All the kings approved, admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses” (9.710–711; see [[kings]] and [[tamer-of-horses]]). The verse of approval is the one that closed Nestor's counsel of the wall (7.344).
