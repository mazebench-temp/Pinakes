summary: Hades is named three times in Book 9 and once called “Zeus of the underworld”. Agamemnon makes him the image of the unbending, “implacable and untamable”; Achilles hates the man who hides his mind “as the gates of Hades”; and in Phoenix's two stories Hades and Persephone fulfill Amyntor's curse on his son and are called on in Althaea's curse on hers.
# Ζεύς τε καταχθόνιος (9.457) is Hades; zeus excepts the line, and the epithet zeus-of-the-underworld claims καταχθόνιος.
grc: [Ἀΐδην, "Ζεύς τε καταχθόνιος"]

**The unbending god.** Agamemnon ends his offer with a demand:

{{quote:07e672bc-3b87-50b2-8055-78166e3440b0}}

δμηθήτω, “let him be tamed”, and ἀδάμαστος, “untamable”, come from one root, and ἀμείλιχος, “implacable”, is the negative of the word for the “soothing words” Nestor asked for (μειλιχίοισι, 9.113; see [[untamable]] and [[implacable]]). Hades cannot be soothed or tamed, and that is why mortals hate him most; Agamemnon's point is that a man who will not be moved is like him. Odysseus leaves the lines out when he repeats the offer (see [[odysseus]]).

**The gates.** Achilles uses the god's name for his own hatred: “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.312–313; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). He has not heard Agamemnon's lines, yet he answers their word: Hades was ἔχθιστος, “most hateful”, to mortals (9.159), and the double-dealer is ἐχθρός, “hateful”, as the gates of Hades. The gates are the way of the dead into his house (5.646, 23.71). In the Odyssey the disguised Odysseus speaks the same verse of a man who lies because he is poor (Od. 14.156–157).

**Zeus of the underworld.** Amyntor's curse on Phoenix is fulfilled by “Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.457; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]], [[zeus-of-the-underworld]] and [[persephone]]). Ζεὺς καταχθόνιος, the Zeus below the earth, is Hades; καταχθόνιος occurs only here in Homer. Hesiod calls Hades “the god of the underworld” (θεοῦ χθονίου, *Theogony* 767–768), and his plowman prays to Ζεὺς χθόνιος, “Zeus of the earth”, and Demeter (*Works and Days* 465). In the story of Meleager his mother beats the earth with her hands, “calling on Hades and dread Persephone … to give death to her son”, and the Erinys hears her (9.569–572; see [[erinyes]], [[curses]] and [[the-death-of-meleager]]). The two powers below the earth who punish perjurers in Agamemnon's oath of Book 3 are usually taken to be the same pair (3.278–279).
