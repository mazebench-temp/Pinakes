---
summary: Book 9 is the book of gifts. Agamemnon names them and Odysseus repeats them; Phoenix argues that heroes of old could be won with gifts and that Meleager lost his by waiting; Achilles answers that “his gifts are hateful to me”, and Diomedes wishes they had never been offered. The night ends with the gift of sleep.
grc: [δώροισίν, δωτίνῃσι, δωρητοί, δώρων]
passages:
  - 9.121-157 | Agamemnon's offer
  - 9.600-605 | While the gifts are offered
---

**Gifts and words.** Nestor names the means of winning Achilles back: “kindly gifts” and “soothing words” (δώροισίν τʼ ἀγανοῖσιν ἔπεσσί τε μειλιχίοισι, 9.113). Agamemnon supplies the first: “before you all I will name the glorious gifts” (9.121), and the list runs from tripods and gold to a daughter and seven cities (9.122–157; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Nestor judges them “no longer to be scorned” (9.164). Phoenix later gives the pair its rule: the heroes of old “could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (δωρητοί τε πέλοντο παράρρητοί τʼ ἐπέεσσι, 9.526), two verbal adjectives, “giftable” and “persuadable” (see [[peitho]]). δωρητός occurs nowhere else in Homer.

**Gifts like a god's.** The men of the seven cities will honor Achilles “with gifts like a god” (δωτίνῃσι, 9.155 = 9.297). δωτίνη is a gift owed by custom: in the Odyssey it is what a host owes a stranger (*Odyssey* 9.268) and what Alcinous will give his guest (*Odyssey* 11.352). Here it is what subjects bring their king, as worshippers bring gifts to a god (see [[time]] and [[the-seven-cities]]).

**Hateful gifts.** Odysseus foresees the refusal: “if the son of Atreus is too hateful to your heart, himself and his gifts” (9.300–301), pity the rest of the Achaeans. Achilles takes up his words: “His gifts are hateful to me” (ἐχθρὰ δέ μοι τοῦ δῶρα, 9.378), and not ten or twenty times as much, not the wealth of Orchomenus or Egyptian Thebes, not gifts as many as the sand and the dust, would persuade him (9.379–387; see [[at-a-hairs-worth]] and [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]).

**Gifts on terms.** Phoenix's last argument is about timing:

{{quote:a5f8f90f-db92-530c-b3a9-2d914c47f515}}

ἐπὶ δώρων, “on the terms of the gifts”, against ἄτερ δώρων, “without the gifts” (see the translation's note on 9.597–603). The elders of the Aetolians had promised Meleager “a great gift” (μέγα δῶρον, 9.576), a domain from the richest of the plain; he fought at last of his own accord, “but they no longer paid him the gifts” (9.598; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). At the end of the night Diomedes regrets the whole offer: “I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus, offering countless gifts; he is proud enough as it is” (9.698–699).

**The gifts later.** The gifts are not forgotten. Thetis tells Hephaestus that the elders begged her son “and named many glorious gifts” (18.448–449), and Agamemnon offers again all that Odysseus promised in the hut (19.140–144). Achilles answers that he may give them or keep them, as he likes (19.146–148). Plato would not have poets praise Phoenix for advising Achilles to take gifts before giving up his wrath (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[apoina]]).

**The gift of sleep.** The book ends: “and there they lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep” (ὕπνου δῶρον, 9.713), the verse that ended Book 7 (7.482; see [[hypnos]]). It is the one gift of the night that no one refuses.
