---
summary: Achilles' “imperishable fame,” κλέος ἄφθιτον, the reward of the first of his two fates, is the most famous use of the epithet.
---

{{quote:47e1e08a-eeb7-5938-9759-5a184966aa85}}

“If I stay here and fight around the city of the Trojans, my homecoming is lost, but my fame will be imperishable” (9.412–413). κλέος ἄφθιτον occurs only here in Homer. Elsewhere in the Iliad the epithet describes things that belong to the gods or are made by them: a scepter made by Hephaestus, the wheels of Hera's chariot, Poseidon's golden house, the plans of Zeus (2.46, 5.724, 13.22, 24.88). Here a mortal's fame shares the gods' freedom from decay, at the price of his life. The second fate answers it phrase for phrase: if he goes home, “my noble fame is lost, but my life will be long” (9.415), and the verb ὤλετο, “is lost,” stands against ἄφθιτον, “that does not perish.”

The phrase has a famous parallel in the Vedic hymns of India, where words of the same origin, *śrávas* and *ákṣitam*, “unfailing fame,” are joined. The likeness is one of the chief pieces of evidence that Greek epic inherited part of its diction from an older poetry of praise ([[kleos]], [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Achilles sings “the famous deeds of men” to his lyre when the envoys arrive (9.189); he describes here the fame he would win for himself ([[clear-toned]]). See [[achilles]].
