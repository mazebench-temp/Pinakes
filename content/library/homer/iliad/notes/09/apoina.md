---
summary: Agamemnon calls his amends to Achilles ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom”, the phrase for the price of a captive's life. The man who refused Chryses' ransom in Book 1 now offers one himself, and it is refused.
passages:
  - 9.119-122 | A boundless ransom
---

**The king's ransom.** Agamemnon ends his admission of blind folly with an offer: “I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, 9.120; see [[ate]]). The line-end ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα is the Iliad's phrase for the price of a life: what Chryses brought for his daughter (1.13), what the Trojan Adrastus promised that his father would pay for him (6.49), what Achilles took for Andromache's mother (6.427), and what Priam will bring for Hector's body (24.502; see [[boundless-ransom]]). Only here and in the repetition of the verse in Book 19 (19.138) is it the price of an insult.

The word fits the case oddly, and perhaps on purpose. Ransom buys back a person; what Agamemnon would buy back is Achilles' help, and his own standing with him. The translation keeps the fixed English “ransom” so that the echo stays audible (see the translation's note on 9.115–120). The poem's first ransom was Agamemnon's to accept, and he refused it, with ruin for the army (1.11–32); now he is the one offering, and the gifts he names are a ransom's goods, tripods, cauldrons, gold, horses and women (9.122–134; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).

**Ransom refused.** Achilles will not take it: “His gifts are hateful to me” (9.378; see [[at-a-hairs-worth]]). Phoenix's argument is that the heroes of old “could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (9.526), and Ajax's is that a man accepts a price even for a brother or a son (9.632–636). That price has its own name, ποινή, the word to which ἄποινα belongs (see [[poine]]). In the last book Achilles accepts ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα from Priam for Hector (24.502, 24.579), the ransom that ends the poem.
