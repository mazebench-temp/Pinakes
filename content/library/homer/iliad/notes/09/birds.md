summary: The birds of Book 9 are two mothers in comparisons. Achilles likens himself to a bird that brings every morsel to her unfledged chicks (9.323–324), and Phoenix tells how Marpessa wept with the lot of the halcyon, the bird of many sorrows (9.563).
passages:
  - 9.321-327 | The bird and her chicks
  - 9.561-564 | The halcyon
---

**The mother bird.** In the earlier books birds are carrion and omens (1.4–5, 8.247–252) or flocks in the similes of the army (2.459–466, 3.2–7). In Book 9 ὄρνις, the common word for a bird, appears once, in Achilles' answer to Odysseus: “As a bird brings to her unfledged chicks each morsel she takes, while it goes hard with her herself, so I too spent many sleepless nights” (9.323–325; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]). The bird is a mother working for her young, and Achilles takes her part for himself. The poems' other parent birds lose their young: the sparrow at Aulis, whose eight nestlings the serpent eats before her (2.311–316; see [[sparrow]]), and the sea eagles or vultures whose young the country people have taken from the nest (*Odyssey* 16.216–218).

**The halcyon.** Phoenix names the one bird of the book that has a name. Cleopatra's parents called her Alcyone because her mother Marpessa, carried off by Apollo, wept “having the lot of the halcyon bird of many sorrows” (9.561–564; see [[halcyon]] and [[marpessa]]). It is the only halcyon in Homer.

**No birds of omen.** In Book 8 Zeus answered Agamemnon's prayer with an eagle (8.245–252; see [[eagle]]). In Book 9 his signs are lightning, and they are for the Trojans (9.236–237).
