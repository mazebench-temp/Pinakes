---
summary: Achilles tells the envoys that his mother spoke of “two different fates” (διχθαδίας κῆρας) carrying him toward death. The plural, which elsewhere sets one man's doom against another's, here sets two possible deaths of one man side by side.
passages:
  - 9.410-416 | Achilles' two fates
---

“For my mother, the goddess silver-footed Thetis, tells me that two different fates are carrying me toward the end of death” (9.410–411). Each man has his κήρ, the doom that waits for him; Sarpedon sees them standing over all men, “countless”, which no mortal can escape (12.326–327). When the poem shows two at once, they belong to two parties and Zeus weighs them against each other, the fates of the Trojans and the Achaeans in Book 8 (δύο κῆρε, 8.70) and of Hector and Achilles in Book 22 (22.210; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Here the two belong to one man, and the choice between them is his (see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]).

The two κῆρες “carry” him, φερέμεν, as the fates of death carry men off elsewhere (“all whom the fates of death have not carried away”, 2.302; “borne along by the fates”, 8.527). The end is the same, τέλος θανάτοιο; only the road and the time differ. One road is short and leads to imperishable fame, the other long and leads home (9.412–416; see [[kleos]] and [[nostos]]). In Book 18 Thetis names the doom that is now his: “your own death is ready straight after Hector's” (18.96), and Achilles answers that he will accept his κήρ “whenever Zeus wishes to bring it to pass” (18.115–116).
