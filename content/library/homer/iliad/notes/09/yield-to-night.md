---
summary: Nestor says the formula in the Achaean assembly on the night after the second day of battle, “But now let us yield to black night”, and turns it into a program for the night: supper, a watch at the ditch, and a council of the elders, since this night will shatter the army or save it.
passages:
  - 9.65-78 | Nestor's counsel for the night
---

Hector yielded to black night on the plain, with fires and a watch so that the Achaeans could not slip away (8.497–522). In the Achaean camp Nestor says the same verse, ἀλλʼ ἤτοι νῦν μὲν πειθώμεθα νυκτὶ μελαίνῃ, “But now let us yield to black night”, and as always supper follows (δόρπα, 9.65–66). Agamemnon has just proposed flight, in the words of his false proposal of Book 2 (9.26–28). Nestor's verse answers him: the night is for staying, not for running. To yield to night is to stop fighting; it is not to leave the field.

The rest of his speech fills the night that he has yielded to. Sentries are to lie by the ditch outside the wall, the elders are to feast in Agamemnon's hut and give counsel, and the king is to follow the best of it (9.66–75; see [[night-watch]] and [[boule]]). The watch goes out as he asked: seven leaders, his son Thrasymedes among them, each with a hundred young men, sit down between the ditch and the wall, kindle fires and make their supper (9.80–88), as the Trojans do on the plain. He ends with the reason the night matters: “This night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.78; see [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]). The same verse in the mouth of Eurylochus in the Odyssey leads Odysseus' men to land on the island of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.291–292). Nestor's night leads to the embassy. See [[war-and-night]].
