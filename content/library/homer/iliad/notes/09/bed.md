summary: Agamemnon will swear that he never went up into Briseis' bed, and Achilles answers, “let him lie beside her”; Achilles offers Phoenix a soft bed and has one made for him; and the night ends with Achilles and Patroclus each lying beside a woman, as Meleager, in Phoenix's story, lay beside his wife in his anger.
grc: ["τῆς εὐνῆς ἐπιβήμεναι"]
passages:
  - 9.658-668 | The beds in Achilles' hut
---

**Briseis' bed.** The oath that goes with the gifts is about a bed: Agamemnon “never went up into her bed or lay with her” (9.133 = 9.275; see [[oath]]). Achilles answers it with scorn, “he keeps the wife dear to my heart; let him lie beside her and take his pleasure” (9.336–337; see [[briseis]]).

**Phoenix's bed.** “Stay here and lie down in a soft bed”, Achilles tells Phoenix (9.617–618), and he nods to Patroclus to spread “a thick bed” for him, a sign to the others that it is time to go (9.620–622). The bed is made as the envoys leave: “fleeces and a rug and the fine nap of linen” (9.658–661; see [[handmaids]]). “There the old man lay down and waited for brilliant Dawn” (9.662), in the verse with which Odysseus lies down in his hall in the Odyssey (Od. 19.50). The bed keeps Phoenix with Achilles, as Achilles intended (9.427–429).

**The last beds.** Achilles sleeps in the inner part of the hut, and “beside him lay a woman” (παρκατέλεκτο), Diomede, daughter of Phorbas, whom he had brought from Lesbos; Patroclus lies on the other side with Iphis (9.663–668; see [[diomede]], [[iphis]] and [[hut]]). The verb is the one Phoenix used of Meleager a hundred verses before: in his anger at his mother he lay beside his wedded wife Cleopatra, “Beside her he lay, brooding on his heart-grieving anger” (τῇ ὅ γε παρκατέλεκτο, 9.556, 9.565). The word occurs in Homer only in these two verses. In the last book Achilles sleeps in the same place, and Briseis lies beside him (24.675–676).
