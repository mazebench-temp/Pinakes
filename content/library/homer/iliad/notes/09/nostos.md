summary: Agamemnon proposes the homecoming in earnest, Achilles threatens it and weighs it against fame, and to Phoenix he puts it off until dawn. The only homecoming of the night is the envoys' walk back along the shore.

**Flight.** Agamemnon ends his speech to the assembly with the verses of the test in Book 2: “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land; for we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (9.27–28 = 2.140–141; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). In Book 2 the army ran for the ships. This time nobody moves, and after a long silence Diomedes answers: “If your own heart is so eager to go home, go: the road is open to you” (9.42–43). In Book 1 Agamemnon told Achilles, “Run away, then, if your heart so urges you” (φεῦγε μάλʼ εἴ τοι θυμὸς ἐπέσσυται, 1.173); Diomedes turns the words on the king (θυμὸς ἐπέσσυται, 9.42). The rest will stay, and if they too sail, Diomedes and Sthenelus will fight on alone (9.46–49; see [[retreat]]).

**Achilles' voyage.** To Odysseus Achilles describes his own departure as if it were already under way: sacrifice to Zeus and all the gods, the ships loaded and drawn down to the sea, the Hellespont “very early”, the rowers eager, and, if Poseidon grants fair sailing, “on the third day I would reach deep-soiled Phthia” (9.357–363; see [[phthia]]). “If the gods keep me safe and I come home”, Peleus will find him a wife, and he will enjoy his father's possessions (9.393–400). He advises the others to sail home too (9.417–420).

**Homecoming or fame.** Then he names what the voyage would cost. His mother has told him of two fates, and the first clause of the prophecy sets νόστος against κλέος: “my homecoming is lost, but my fame will be imperishable” (9.413; see [[kleos]] and [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Homecoming here is the long life that lies on the other side of the choice.

**Put off.** Phoenix begins with the word: “If it is homecoming, shining Achilles, that you are turning over in your mind” (9.434). To him Achilles softens his answer: “when dawn appears we will consider whether to go home to our own or to stay” (9.618–619). He nods to Patroclus to spread a bed for Phoenix, “so that as soon as possible the others might think of going home from the hut” (νόστοιο, 9.621–622). The word that named the voyage to Phthia now names the envoys' walk back to Agamemnon's hut. To Ajax he does not speak of sailing at all (9.650–655). Odysseus reports only the first answer, the launching at dawn (9.682–687; see [[message-repeated]]), and Diomedes takes it as “whether he goes or stays” (9.701–702).

**Never.** Achilles does not sail. Thetis already knows that she will not receive him “returning home to the house of Peleus” (18.59–60 = 18.440–441), and in Book 19 he tells his horse Xanthus that his own fate is to die at Troy, “far from my dear father and mother” (19.421–422).
