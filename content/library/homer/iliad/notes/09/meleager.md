summary: Phoenix's story makes Meleager a pattern for Achilles. The slayer of the Calydonian boar withdrew from the war in anger at his mother's curse, refused every plea and the domain he was offered, and saved Calydon at his wife's entreaty only when it was burning, without the gifts (9.543–599).

**The hunter.** The Catalogue named him as one of the dead, “fair-haired Meleager” (2.642); Phoenix shows him alive. He is the son of Oeneus who killed the boar Artemis sent, “gathering huntsmen from many cities / and dogs” (9.543–545; see [[the-calydonian-boar]] and [[oeneus]]). His name is usually explained as “he who cares for the hunt”, from μέλω, “be a care to”, and ἄγρα, “hunt”. Euripides made a darker pun on it, “Meleager, you hunt a wretched hunt” (μελέαν … ἄγραν, *Meleager* fr. 517 Nauck). In the war that follows the hunt he is ἄρηι φίλος, “dear to Ares” (9.550; see [[dear-to-ares]]), and while he fights the Curetes cannot stay outside the wall.

**The anger.**

{{quote:34c6a26d-c1a8-5725-bc15-007fb428199f}}

Phoenix gives the anger a general rule: it “swells the mind in the breasts of others too, even of men who think shrewdly” (9.553–554). οἰδάνω, “swell”, occurs in Homer only here and in Achilles' answer to Ajax at the end of the embassy: “my heart swells with anger whenever I remember” how Agamemnon treated him (9.646–647). Meleager lies beside his wife “brooding on his heart-grieving anger” (9.565), the words Apollo used of Achilles beside the ships (4.513); it is the anger Odysseus asked Achilles to “let go” (9.260; see [[cholos]] and [[heart-grieving]]). His quarrel is with his mother, whose curse comes out a few lines later (9.566–572; see [[althaea]] and [[the-death-of-meleager]]). Phoenix never says that Meleager killed her brother, but the curse implies it.

**The refusal.** Meleager never speaks in the story. Elders and priests beg him and promise a domain; old Oeneus shakes the doors of his chamber; his sisters and mother beg him, and “he refused all the more” (μᾶλλον ἀναίνετο, 9.585); his dearest companions beg him and do not persuade him (9.574–587). Phoenix has just used the same verb of the man who refuses the Prayers (ἀνήνηται, 9.510; see [[litai]]), and Odysseus will use it in his report: Achilles “rejects you and your gifts” (ἀναίνεται, 9.679). Unlike Meleager, Achilles answers each of his suppliants, and each time differently (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**The return.** Only when his chamber is being struck and the Curetes are on the towers setting fire to the city does his wife move him (9.588–595; see [[cleopatra]]). He arms and goes out, “yielding to his own heart” (εἴξας ᾧ θυμῷ, 9.598), and saves the Aetolians; but by then “they no longer paid him the gifts, / many and pleasing” (9.598–599; see [[aetolians]]). Phoenix draws the lesson for Achilles: come while the gifts are offered (9.600–605). Achilles answers that he has no need of such honor (9.607–610). Phoenix does not tell the rest: the Erinys had heard Althaea's curse, and Meleager was dead before the Aetolians sailed for Troy (2.641–642; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). His wife and his chamber have a counterpart at the end of the book. παρκατέλεκτο, “lay beside”, is used in Homer only of Meleager beside Cleopatra (9.565) and of Achilles, who sleeps with Diomede beside him when the envoys have gone (9.664; see [[diomede]]).
