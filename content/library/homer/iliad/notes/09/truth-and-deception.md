---
summary: Book 9 is built on repeated messages, and on what each repetition leaves out. Odysseus repeats the offer without Agamemnon's demand and reports Achilles' first answer without the other two; Achilles opens by cursing the man who hides one thing in his mind and says another, and accuses the king of deceiving him.
passages:
  - 9.158-161 | Agamemnon's demand that Achilles yield
  - 9.307-313 | Hateful to me as the gates of Hades
  - 9.677-692 | Odysseus reports the refusal
---

**What is left out.** Agamemnon ends his offer with a demand: let Achilles be tamed, like Hades, and submit to the more kingly man and the elder (9.158–161). Odysseus repeats the offer almost word for word, changing only the persons, and stops before the demand; in its place he puts the army's pity and the glory of killing Hector (9.264–306; see [[message-repeated]]). The omission is the most famous in the poem, and nothing in the text calls it a lie. It is the envoy's tact, and the audience knows what Achilles does not.

**The man who hides.** Achilles begins his answer by promising to speak “without regard for anyone”, as he means it, and by cursing the man “who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.309–313; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]). He then accuses Agamemnon: “he has taken my prize from my hands and deceived me”; the envoys must report everything “openly”, in case the king hopes to deceive anyone else; “never again will he beguile me with words” (9.344–345, 9.369–376; see [[apate]]). He does not accuse the envoys, and Odysseus does not answer the charge.

**What is reported.** Back in Agamemnon's hut, Odysseus reports the refusal. What he says is true: Achilles will not quench his anger, he rejects the gifts, he will launch his ships at dawn and advises the rest to sail home, and Phoenix has stayed to go with him (9.677–692). It is not the whole truth. Achilles told Phoenix that they would decide at dawn whether to go or stay, and told Ajax that he would not fight until Hector reached the Myrmidons' ships (9.618–619, 9.650–655). Odysseus reports only the first answer, though Ajax, who heard the third, is named as his witness (9.688–689). Whether he keeps back the softer answers by design, or the poem lets the first answer stand for all, is debated (see the translation's note on 9.356–363 and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**True speech.** The book's one admission of truth comes early. Agamemnon tells Nestor, “Old man, it is no lie, your account of my blind folly” (οὔ τι ψεῦδος, 9.115; see [[pseudos]] and [[ate]]). Plato's Hippias would make Achilles the true man and Odysseus the liar on the strength of this book; Socrates answers that the man who said one thing to Odysseus and another to Ajax was not simple either (*Hippias Minor* 364e–371e; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]).
