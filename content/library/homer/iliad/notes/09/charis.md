summary: Achilles' first grievance is that there was “no thanks” (χάρις) for fighting without rest; later he forbids Phoenix to do Agamemnon a favor (χάριν) by weeping for him. Meleager's gifts, “many and pleasing”, were never paid.
grc: [χάρις]
passages:
  - 9.315-320 | No thanks for fighting
---

**No thanks.** Achilles' answer to Odysseus begins with a broken exchange: he will not be persuaded, “since there was, it seems, no thanks for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever” (9.316–317). χάρις is the return that a service earns, and his service has earned none. The rest of the speech fills in the account: the spoils of twenty-three cities handed over, the prize taken back (9.328–336; see [[geras]]). The particle ἄρα, “it seems, after all”, marks a discovery: he had expected a return. In Book 17 Glaucus says the same two lines to Hector, of the Lycians who fight for Troy and get no thanks for it (17.147–148); the complaint is the allies' as well as Achilles'.

**A favor to the enemy.** At the end of his answer to Phoenix Achilles turns the word round: “do not confuse my heart with weeping and grieving to do a favor to the hero son of Atreus” (φέρων χάριν, 9.612–613). To plead Agamemnon's case is to do him a χάρις, and a χάρις makes a bond. Achilles wants Phoenix's love for himself alone: “you have no need to love him, or you will make yourself hateful to me, who love you” (9.613–614; see [[philos]]).

**Gifts not given.** The Aetolians promised Meleager a domain of fifty acres if he would come out and fight. He came out too late, “but they no longer paid him the gifts, many and pleasing” (πολλά τε καὶ χαρίεντα, 9.598–599). χαρίεις, “pleasing, gracious”, belongs with χάρις: the gifts were meant as a favor that would bind him, and he fought without them (see [[doron]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Hera uses the same words in Book 8 of the gifts the Achaeans bring [[poseidon]] at Helice and Aegae, “many and pleasing; so you should will them victory” (8.203–204): pleasing gifts ask for a return. Phoenix's point is that Achilles too will lose the gifts, and the honor with them, if he waits (9.600–605; see [[time]]).
