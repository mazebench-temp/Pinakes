**At Agamemnon's table.** The elders' feast has only the core of the pattern: the meal set before them, “And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them”, and the desire for drink and food put away (9.90–92; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). As at the feasts of the kings in Books 2 and 7, what follows is not song but counsel, and the verses that bring Nestor in are those of the feast after the duel (9.92–95 = 7.323–326; compare 2.432–433). No sacrifice is told, and the wine comes from Agamemnon's own stores (9.71–72; see [[feast]]).

**In Achilles' hut.** The second meal is told step by step.

1. **Seats.** Achilles leads the envoys in and seats them “on chairs with purple rugs” (9.199–200; see [[throne]]).
2. **Wine.** He tells Patroclus to set out a larger mixing bowl, mix the wine stronger and make ready a cup for each, “for these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof” (9.202–204; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[wine]]).
3. **Meat.** On a chopping block in the firelight go the back of a sheep and of a fat goat and the chine of a hog; Automedon holds them, and Achilles carves, cuts the meat up and threads it on spits (9.206–210). The last verse is the verse of the sacrifice, μίστυλλον … καὶ ἀμφʼ ὀβελοῖσιν ἔπειραν (1.465, 2.428), turned to the singular (see [[spits]]).
4. **Fire.** Patroclus makes the fire blaze; when it has burned down, the embers are spread, the spits stretched above them and sprinkled with “divine salt” (9.211–214).
5. **Serving.** The meat is heaped on platters; Patroclus sets out bread “in beautiful baskets”, and Achilles serves the meat (9.215–217).
6. **The gods' share.** Achilles sits facing Odysseus and bids Patroclus “sacrifice to the gods”, and he throws the offerings into the fire (9.218–220; see [[sacrifice]]).
7. **Eating and enough.** The two closing verses of the elders' feast (9.221–222 = 9.91–92).

No animal is killed, for the cuts of meat are already in the hut, and the gods' share is the portions thrown into the fire, not the burned thigh-pieces of a sacrifice. The song that might follow a feast has been heard already: Achilles was singing to his lyre when the envoys came (9.186–191; see [[lyre]]). After the meal comes the errand. Ajax nods to Phoenix, Odysseus sees it and raises his cup (9.223–224; see [[visit-scene]]).

**The meal for Priam.** In the last book Achilles feeds Priam in the same hut. He kills a white sheep himself, and his companions flay and dress it, cut it up, spit it and roast it, in the verses of the feast after the duel (24.621–624; 24.623–624 = 7.317–318). Then Automedon sets out the bread in beautiful baskets and Achilles serves the meat, in the verses that told of Patroclus' bread, and the two closing verses follow (24.625–628; 24.626 = 9.217, 24.627–628 = 9.221–222). The meal for the envoys who failed and the meal for the king who succeeds end in the same verses, and Patroclus' part in the first is Automedon's in the second.

“And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them” is found in the Iliad only at these three meals (9.91, 9.221, 24.627). In the Odyssey it is the verse of meals in a house, often a guest's: the suitors in Odysseus' hall, Menelaus' guests in Sparta, Calypso and Odysseus, Eumaeus and his guest (Od. 1.149, 4.67, 5.200, 14.453, and seven more). Calypso's meal has Achilles' verse too: “she herself sat facing divine Odysseus” (Od. 5.198 ≈ 9.218).
