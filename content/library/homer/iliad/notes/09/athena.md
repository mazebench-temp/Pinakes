**Strength.** Odysseus reminds Achilles of his father's parting words: “Athena and Hera will give you strength, / if they will” (9.254–255; see [[hera]], where the passage is quoted, and [[peleus]]). Athena gives it in the end. She throws the aegis around Achilles' shoulders when he shows himself at the ditch (18.203–206), drips nectar and ambrosia into his breast at Zeus' bidding before he fights (19.341–354), and in the last duel comes to his side and gives him back his spear (22.214–216, 22.276–277).

**Handiwork.**

{{quote:fb15c0fd-f82f-56d7-a9c6-cc08bd15f7b1}}

The two goddesses measure the two excellences of a bride, beauty and skill in women's work. ἔργα are above all the work of the loom, and Athena is its teacher (see [[weaving]]). She made her own robe (5.734–735) and Hera's (14.178–179). In the Odyssey she gives the Phaeacian women their skill (*Odyssey* 7.110–111), and when the goddesses raise the orphaned daughters of Pandareus, Aphrodite feeds them, Hera gives them beauty and good sense, Artemis stature, and Athena teaches them to make glorious works (ἔργα … κλυτὰ ἐργάζεσθαι, *Odyssey* 20.68–72). The same measures judge mortal women. Agamemnon set Chryseis above Clytemnestra, “not in form or stature, not in mind or in handiwork” (1.115), and Hippodameia surpassed all the girls of her age in beauty, handiwork and wits (κάλλεϊ καὶ ἔργοισιν ἰδὲ φρεσί, 13.432). The seven women of Lesbos in Agamemnon's offer are “skilled in flawless handiwork” (9.128). Achilles will not take a wife from Agamemnon even if she were the equal of both goddesses (see [[aphrodite]], [[marriage]] and [[gray-eyed]]).
