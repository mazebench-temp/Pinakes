summary: The book is framed by two silences in the same verses, after Agamemnon's proposal to flee and after Odysseus' report, each broken “at last” by Diomedes (9.29–31, 9.693–696). Between them Achilles' refusal silences the envoys until Phoenix weeps and speaks; Patroclus sits silent while Achilles sings; and the elders keep holy silence for the prayer before the embassy.
grc: [εὐφημῆσαί]

**The frame.** Agamemnon proposes flight, and “they all fell hushed in silence. For a long time the sons of the Achaeans were silent in their grief; at last Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them” (9.29–31). Odysseus reports Achilles' refusal, and the same three verses return, with the line between them that the gods' silence had in Book 8, “amazed at his word, for he had spoken very forcefully” (9.693–696; 9.694 = 8.29). The night's debate begins and ends in the same silence, broken by the same man, and both times Diomedes speaks against the despair: let Agamemnon go home if he wishes, and let Achilles stay or go as he pleases (9.32–49, 9.697–709; see [[diomedes]], [[so-he-spoke]] and [[ring-composition]]). After the report Agamemnon says nothing more.

**The envoys' silence.** Achilles' long answer to Odysseus ends in the same verse: “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, amazed at his word, for he had refused very forcefully” (9.430–431). It is Phoenix who speaks “at last”, “bursting into tears, for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans” (9.432–433; see [[phoenix]] and [[shedding-tears]]).

**In the hut.** When the envoys arrive, “Patroclus alone sat facing him in silence, waiting for the grandson of Aeacus to leave off his singing” (9.190–191; see [[patroclus]] and [[lyre]]). At the end Achilles answers Phoenix and gives his orders without words: “He spoke, and nodded to Patroclus with his brows in silence to spread a thick bed for Phoenix” (9.620–621; see [[bed]]).

**Holy silence.** Before the embassy sets out Nestor asks for water “and bid them keep holy silence, so that we may pray to Zeus” (9.171–172). εὐφημῆσαι, to speak only words of good omen, and so to keep silent, is found only here in Homer; the prayer that follows is not given (see [[prayer]] and [[hand-washing]]). The assembly that began the night was called the same way, quietly: the heralds were to summon each man by name, “but not to shout” (9.11–12; see [[assembly]]).
