summary: Agamemnon offers Achilles any of his three daughters, without bride-price and with a dowry greater than any father has given, and Achilles refuses them. The women of the night are named as daughters: Briseis, Diomede daughter of Phorbas, and Cleopatra, daughter of Marpessa, whom her father Idas fought a god to keep.

---

**A daughter offered.** Agamemnon names his daughters, Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa, in a single verse, and gives Achilles the choice: he may lead home whichever he likes, “without bride-price” (ἀνάεδνον), and Agamemnon will add a dowry, “very great, such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter” (9.144–148; see [[chrysothemis]], [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]], [[iphianassa]] and [[marriage]]). The king who in Book 1 refused a father's ransom for his daughter (1.11–32) now offers his own daughter as part of a payment. Achilles' refusal treats her as a piece of the offer: he will not marry her if she rivaled Aphrodite in beauty and Athena in handiwork; let Agamemnon choose a son-in-law “who is more kingly” (9.388–392). Odysseus repeats the verse of the three names unchanged (9.287).

**Daughters by name.** The women of the night are named by their fathers. Briseis is “the daughter of Briseus” (κούρη Βρισῆος, 9.132, 9.274; see [[briseus]]), and the woman who lies beside Achilles is Φόρβαντος θυγάτηρ Διομήδη, “the daughter of Phorbas, fair-cheeked Diomede” (9.665; see [[diomede]] and [[phorbas-father-of-diomede]]). In Phoenix's story Cleopatra is the daughter of Marpessa, “daughter of Evenus”, and of Idas, who drew his bow against Apollo for the sake of his bride (9.556–560; see [[marpessa]] and [[idas-and-apollo]]). Her parents named her Alcyone for her mother's grief (9.561–564). In the story a man fights a god for his bride; in the camp a king gives his daughter away as part of a payment.
