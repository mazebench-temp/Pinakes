**Twenty-three cities.**

{{quote:97a367a6-4876-59e3-b2af-6ac66611c283}}

In Book 1 Achilles spoke of the raids without numbers: the greater part of the fighting was his, and the greater prize was Agamemnon's (1.163–168). To the envoys he counts. Twelve cities fell to him “with my ships”, from the sea, and eleven “on foot throughout deep-soiled Troy”, in the country around the city (9.328–329). The spoil went to Agamemnon, who stayed behind beside the ships and gave out a little and kept much, while the prizes he gave the other chiefs and kings they still hold (9.330–336; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[geras]]). The verbs are iterative, δόσκον, δασάσκετο, ἔχεσκεν, “I would give”, “he would share out”, “he would keep”: this was the habit of nine years. The account is Achilles' answer to Odysseus' plea that he pity the army (9.300–303): he has already fed it, as a bird feeds her chicks while it goes hard with her (9.323–327; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]).

**Three names.** Book 9 names three of the conquests. Achilles took “well-built Lesbos”, and from its women Agamemnon chose seven for himself, now offered back (9.128–130, 9.270–272); and Achilles sleeps with Diomede, “whom he had brought from Lesbos” (9.664–665; see [[lesbos]] and [[diomede]]). He gave Iphis to Patroclus “when he took steep Scyros, the citadel of Enyeus” (9.667–668; see [[scyros]], [[iphis]] and [[enyeus]]). Scyros lies far from Troy, off Euboea, and elsewhere Achilles' son Neoptolemus is being reared there (19.326–327; *Odyssey* 11.509); the poem does not say how the two fit together (see the translation's note on 9.664–668). And the lyre that Achilles plays came “from the spoils when he destroyed the city of Eetion” (9.188; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). Elsewhere the Iliad names Lyrnessus and Pedasus among his conquests (2.690–691, 20.92; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]).

**What raiding is worth.** The raids also give Achilles his measure of value. What can be raided can be replaced: “cattle and fat sheep can be had by raiding, and tripods can be won, and the tawny heads of horses, but a man's soul cannot come back again; it cannot be taken by raiding or caught” (9.406–409; see [[raiding]] and [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]]). ληϊστοί, “to be had by raiding”, and λεϊστή are the words of plunder (ληΐς, “booty”). The gold and bronze, the fair-belted women and the gray iron that he will take home are “all that I won by lot” (9.365–367), his own share. The woman he has lost was “won by my spear” (9.343; see [[taking-of-briseis]] and [[captive-women]]).
