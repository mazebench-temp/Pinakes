---
summary: Phoenix builds an argument on the exchange between men and gods: if sacrifice, vows and libation turn the gods when a man has done wrong, Achilles should accept Agamemnon's gifts. His own story begins with the exchange broken, when Oeneus forgot Artemis at the harvest offering while the other gods feasted on hecatombs.
passages:
  - 9.497-501 | Even the gods can be turned
  - 9.533-537 | Oeneus forgets Artemis
---

**Gifts that turn the gods.** Phoenix's argument takes the exchange of Book 1 a step further. Chryses' prayers asked for a return on gifts already given; Phoenix speaks of offerings that buy pardon after a wrong. Men turn the gods aside “with burnt offerings and gentle vows, with libation and the savor of the burnt fat … entreating them, whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (9.499–501; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]). The gods' share of the feast, the libation and the savor that Zeus called their privilege (4.48–49), here pays for an offense. Agamemnon's gifts are the human version, and Achilles is asked to be as easily turned as a god. Plato would later object that such lines teach men that the gods can be bought (*Republic* 2.364d–e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).

**The offering forgotten.** In the story that follows, the exchange fails from the other side. Oeneus made harvest offerings to all the gods but one: “the other gods feasted on hecatombs, but to her alone, the daughter of great Zeus, he made no sacrifice. Either he forgot, or did not think of it; and he was greatly blinded in his heart” (9.535–537). Artemis' answer is the boar, and the boar brings the war (9.538–549; see [[artemis]], [[oeneus]] and [[ate]]). The gods feast together on men's offerings, and to leave out one of them is enough. Whether the neglect was forgetting or not thinking of it makes no difference to the goddess.

**Before sailing.** Achilles, planning to sail home, will first make sacrifice “to Zeus and all the gods” (9.357), and hopes that Poseidon will grant him fair sailing (9.362). The night ends with the libations the kings pour before they sleep (9.712; see [[libation]]).
