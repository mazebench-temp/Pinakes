---
summary: The whole of Book 9 is one night, the night after the Achaean rout, with the Trojan fires burning on the plain. It is filled with what the day leaves no room for: an assembly, a supper, a watch, a council, an embassy and a refusal, and it ends in sleep, with dawn and battle ahead.
passages:
  - 9.1-8 | The Achaeans in despair
  - 9.65-88 | The watch is set
  - 9.704-713 | Eat and sleep, and fight at dawn
---

**The night of the fires.** The book begins with the Trojans keeping their watch on the plain and the Achaeans held by “heaven-sent Panic” (9.1–2), the night that ended Book 8 with a thousand Trojan fires (8.553–563; see [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). The fires are the night's constant threat: “the enemy close by the ships are burning many fires”, Nestor says (9.76–77), and Odysseus tells Achilles that the Trojans have camped by the wall and kindled “many fires throughout the army” (9.232–234). Hector, meanwhile, “prays for brilliant Dawn to appear with all speed” (9.240).

**What the night is for.** The Achaeans fill the dark with counsel and watching. Nestor yields to night (9.65; see [[yield-to-night]]), sets seven captains with a hundred men each between the ditch and the wall, where they kindle their own fires and make their suppers (9.66–88; see [[night-watch]]), and calls the elders to a feast and a council (9.70–75). “This night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.78; see [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]). The embassy goes along the dark shore and is received with a meal in Achilles' hut (9.182–221). The book's last image of the hut is a night scene, a bed spread for Phoenix, who “waited for brilliant Dawn”, and Achilles and Patroclus asleep with their women (9.658–668).

**Nights remembered.** Achilles' complaint counts the nights of his service: “so I too spent many sleepless nights, and passed through bloody days in battle” (9.325–326). Phoenix remembers nine nights under guard in his father's house, fires burning in the court and in the porch, and the tenth murky night on which he broke out (9.470–476).

**Toward dawn.** Every answer looks to the morning. Achilles will sail at dawn (9.357–361), or decide at dawn (9.618–619), and Odysseus reports that he will launch his ships “when dawn appears” (9.682). Diomedes ends the debate by sending the kings to sleep and to battle when “fair rosy-fingered Dawn appears” (9.705–709; see [[dawn-line]]). They “lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep” (9.713). The night goes on in Book 10, and Day 26 dawns at 11.1.
