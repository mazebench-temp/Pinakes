grc: [ἀπομηνίσαντος]
en: [nurse my wrath apart]
passages:
  - 9.515-523 | Phoenix: cast aside your wrath
---

**The envoys' word.** Book 9 is about Achilles' wrath from beginning to end, but the speakers seldom call it by its name. Agamemnon offers the gifts “if he ceased from his anger” (μεταλήξαντι χόλοιο, 9.157), Odysseus repeats it twice and tells him to “let go your heart-grieving anger” (9.260–261, 9.299), and at the end Agamemnon asks, “does anger still hold his proud heart?” (9.675). χόλος is the ordinary word, the anger that can be swallowed, checked or let go (see [[cholos]]), and the offer treats Achilles' as anger of that kind, which a fitting payment can end.

**Phoenix's word.** Phoenix alone uses the noun:

{{quote:da391da9-d61c-5972-ad44-2dc6fc77ceb6}}

μῆνιν ἀπορρίψαντα, “cast aside your wrath”, is the first time anyone uses the noun to ask Achilles to give up his wrath. Phoenix grants that the wrath was right until now, “before now there was no blame in your anger” (9.523; see [[nemesis]]); the gifts change the case. The Trojans will use his verb when they see Patroclus in Achilles' armor and think that Achilles “had cast away his wrath and chosen friendship” (μηνιθμὸν μὲν ἀπορρῖψαι, φιλότητα δʼ ἑλέσθαι, 16.282). The wrath is renounced in Book 19, without gifts and for another reason: Thetis tells her son to call an assembly, “renouncing your wrath against Agamemnon” (19.35), and the army rejoices at the words (19.74–75).

The story Phoenix tells is about anger of the other kind. Meleager's is χόλος, the anger “that swells the mind in the breasts of others too, even of men who think shrewdly” (9.553–554), and he lies beside his wife “brooding on his heart-grieving anger” (χόλον θυμαλγέα πέσσων, 9.565). Phoenix offers a story of χόλος to a man whose anger the poem calls μῆνις (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]).

**Achilles' word.** Achilles uses the compound ἀπομηνίω, “keep up one's wrath apart, withdraw in wrath”. The Achaeans must find a better plan, since the one they devised is not ready to their hand “while I nurse my wrath apart” (ἐμεῦ ἀπομηνίσαντος, 9.426). The narrator used the same verb of him in the Catalogue (2.772), and Ajax repeated it to Hector (7.230). Achilles uses it once more, in the same form, when he gives up his wrath. He wishes that Artemis had shot the girl among the ships on the day he took Lyrnessus; then so many Achaeans would not have bitten the dust “while I nursed my wrath apart” (19.59–62). In Book 16 he remembers what he told Ajax in Book 9: he said he would not end his wrath (μηνιθμόν) until the war reached his own ships (16.60–63; 9.650–655).
