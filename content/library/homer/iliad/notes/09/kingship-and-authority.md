---
summary: The night tests Agamemnon's kingship from three sides. Diomedes tells him that Zeus gave him the scepter but not courage; Nestor, that the scepter and the ordinances bind him to take counsel and act on it; and the king's offer to Achilles, a kingdom of seven cities, ends in a claim to be “more kingly”, which Odysseus suppresses and Achilles throws back.
passages:
  - 9.32-49 | Diomedes answers Agamemnon
  - 9.96-102 | The scepter and the ordinances
  - 9.157-161 | Agamemnon's demand that Achilles yield
---

**The scepter without courage.** Agamemnon's proposal to flee is answered by a younger king, in the assembly, by right of custom (9.32–33; see [[themis]]). Zeus gave Agamemnon “only half”: honor for his scepter, but not courage, “and that is the greatest power” (9.37–39; see [[strength-is-a-gift]] and [[alke]]). The line echoes Odysseus' defense of the one king in Book 2, to whom the son of Cronus gave “the scepter and the ordinances” (2.205–206), and splits it. The army shouts its approval (9.50–51), and the king does not answer.

**The scepter and its duty.** Nestor makes the same point in the council, with the respect the younger man did not show. He addresses the king as a hymn addresses a god, “with you I shall end, and with you I shall begin”, and reminds him why the scepter was given:

{{quote:20041419-f7ee-50ca-bce4-a1cc3bb0bb3d}}

The king is to speak, and to listen, and to carry out another man's good counsel (see [[boule]]). Nestor states the king's duty plainly, and Agamemnon acts on it at once. He calls Nestor's charge “no lie” and offers amends (9.115–120; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

**A kingdom offered.** The offer gives Achilles a kingdom of his own: seven cities “near the sea”, whose men will honor him with gifts like a god and bring his ordinances to rich fulfillment “under his scepter” (9.149–156; see [[the-seven-cities]]). It ends with the price: “let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.160–161; see [[basileus]]). Odysseus leaves the demand out (9.264–299). Achilles answers it anyway, telling Agamemnon to choose a son-in-law “who is more kingly” (9.392), and gives Phoenix an equal share of his own kingship (9.616). He sends his answer, through the envoys, to “the chiefs of the Achaeans” and “the other kings”, as if Agamemnon were one among them (9.346, 9.421).

**At dawn.** Diomedes has the last word. Let the king eat and sleep, and at dawn draw up the army and “fight yourself among the foremost” (9.707–709). The kings approve (9.710). The night that began with a king proposing flight ends with his peers telling him to lead (see [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]).
