**The prayer on the shore.**

{{quote:f7d7c6f1-39fe-5e85-b6e7-34c67304dd62}}

The poem does not say why the envoys pray to Poseidon, when Nestor had proposed a prayer to Zeus before they set out (9.171–172). They are walking beside his sea, and the verse that sets them on their way, παρὰ θῖνα πολυφλοίσβοιο θαλάσσης, is the one in which Chryses went along the shore before he prayed to Apollo (1.34–36; see [[loud-roaring-sea]]). Poseidon is also the Achaeans' friend among the gods. Earlier that day he refused Hera's plea to fight Zeus for them (8.198–211), but in Books 13–15 he helps them against Zeus' will, pitying them as they are beaten at the ships (13.10–16). The participle is dual, εὐχομένω, “the two praying”, like the other verbs of the walk and the welcome (9.182–198; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

γαιήοχος, “earth-holder”, is given to Poseidon here for the first time in the poem, and to no one else. It comes mostly in the formula γαιήοχος ἐννοσίγαιος, “the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth” (9.183; 13.43, 13.59, 13.677, 14.355, 15.222; *Odyssey* 11.241; see [[earth-holder]] and [[shaker-of-the-earth]]).

**Fair sailing.** Achilles' answer turns the prayer around. The envoys asked Poseidon to help them keep Achilles at Troy, and Achilles names him as the god who could take him home: “if the famous shaker of the earth grants me fair sailing, / on the third day I would reach deep-soiled Phthia” (9.362–363). εὐπλοΐη, “fair sailing”, occurs nowhere else in Homer. Odysseus reports the threat to sail at dawn, but not the god's name (9.682–683; see [[achilles]] and [[phthia]]).
