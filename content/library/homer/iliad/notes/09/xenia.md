**The host.** Achilles receives the envoys before he knows what they want. He springs up, greets them as “the dearest of the Achaeans”, leads them in and seats them “on chairs with purple rugs”, tells Patroclus to set out a larger bowl and mix the wine stronger, “for these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof” (9.193–204). He carves the meat himself, Patroclus brings bread in baskets, and they sacrifice to the gods before they eat (9.205–221; see [[feast-type-scene]]). Nothing is said of their errand until they have eaten (9.222–225). The same verses serve Achilles' meal with Priam in the last book, with Automedon in Patroclus' place: “Automedon took bread and set it out on the table in beautiful baskets, while Achilles served the meat” (24.625–626). The lines are 9.216–217 with one name changed.

**The guests' claim.** Odysseus' toast turns the meal into an opening: “We do not lack an equal share of the feast”, in Agamemnon's hut or here, “but the business of the lovely feast is not our concern” (9.225–228). Ajax closes the embassy by appealing to the hospitality Achilles has given: “respect your own roof; we are here beneath your roof” (αἴδεσσαι δὲ μέλαθρον· ὑπωρόφιοι δέ τοί εἰμεν, 9.640). Achilles' own word, μέλαθρον (9.204), comes back as an obligation: a host owes his guests αἰδώς (see [[aidos]]). Achilles does not answer that part of the plea. He keeps one guest, Phoenix, for the night, and the bed is spread for him with fleeces, a rug and linen (9.620–622, 9.658–661).

**Hospitality at home.** The king's huts, Nestor says, are full of wine brought daily from Thrace, and “you have all the means of entertaining” (πᾶσά τοί ἐσθʼ ὑποδεξίη, 9.73): a king's duty is to feast the elders. In Phoenix's story the kinsmen who kept him at home slaughtered sheep, cattle and hogs, drank the old man's wine and watched him for nine nights (9.464–473), and when he escaped he came to Peleus, “and he received me gladly” (ὑπέδεκτο, 9.480), with the verb of ὑποδεξίη. Peleus made the fugitive wealthy and gave him a people to rule (9.481–484). Phoenix speaks as a guest who became a son, to the son of the house that received him (see [[exile-and-refuge]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]).
