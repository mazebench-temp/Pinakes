summary: Achilles turns Hector's comfort into a complaint: the coward and the brave are held in one honor, and the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much. In Hector's mouth the rule was a reason not to fear; in Achilles' it is a reason not to fight.

Hector told Andromache that no man, “no coward and no brave man”, has escaped his fate once he has been born (6.488–489), so that going out to fight would not kill him before his day. Achilles names the same pair, κακός and ἐσθλός, “the coward and the brave”, to opposite effect. They share the same honor while they live and the same death when they die: κάτθανʼ ὁμῶς, “the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much” (9.320). The aorist is gnomic, a thing that always happens (see the translation's note on 9.318–320). For Hector the equality of death frees a man to fight; for Achilles it is the last item in an account that shows fighting earns nothing. “There was, it seems, no thanks for fighting” (9.316–317; see [[charis]]), the shares of the spoils are the same (see [[moira]]), the honor is the same (see [[time]]), and death is the same.

Sarpedon will draw the third conclusion. Since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, “which no mortal can flee or escape”, let them go forward and win glory or give it to another (12.322–328). The same fact that makes Achilles withdraw sends Sarpedon to the front.
