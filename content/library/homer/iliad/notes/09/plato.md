---
summary: Plato returns to Book 9 in six dialogues. Hippias and Socrates argue over Achilles' answers to Odysseus and Ajax in the Hippias Minor; the Republic rejects Phoenix's verses on gods turned by offerings and his advice to take the gifts, and the Laws answer them; Socrates dreams Achilles' line about reaching Phthia on the third day; the Symposium makes Achilles' choice of fates a choice made for love; and the Laws cite Amyntor's curse on Phoenix as proof that the gods hear parents.
---

- Achilles, true or false, in his answers to Odysseus and to Ajax (*Hippias Minor* 364e–371e): [[plato-hippias-minor]].
- Phoenix on gods turned by offerings, and on the gifts (*Republic* 2.364d–e, 3.390e; *Laws* 10.885b–e, 906d–e): [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]].
- “On the third day you would reach deep-soiled Phthia” (*Crito* 44a–b): [[plato-socrates-dream]].
- **The two fates.** In the *Symposium* Phaedrus says that Achilles learned from his mother that he would die if he killed Hector, and that if he did not, he would go home and die an old man, and that he chose to die for Patroclus; and he scolds Aeschylus for making Achilles the lover rather than the beloved (179e–180a): [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[aeschylus-myrmidons]].
- **“In the Prayers.”** Hippias and Cratylus both cite Achilles' answers as spoken ἐν Λιταῖς, the oldest name of the book; Cratylus quotes Achilles' words to Ajax, “all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart”, as a compliment to Socrates (*Hippias Minor* 364e; *Cratylus* 428c, on 9.644–645): [[title-of-book-9]].
- **Amyntor's curse.** In the *Laws* the Athenian argues that the gods hear the curses of parents against their children, and most justly. Oedipus, dishonored, cursed his sons, and everyone sings that the gods fulfilled it; “and Amyntor in his anger cursed his own son Phoenix, and Theseus Hippolytus, and countless others”. So a father who is honored by his children, and prays for their good, is surely heard as well (*Laws* 11.931b–d). In Homer the gods fulfill Amyntor's curse of childlessness, “Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.453–457; see [[amyntor]], [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[curses]]).
