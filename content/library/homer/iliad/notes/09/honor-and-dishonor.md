## The wrong admitted

Book 1 ended with Achilles dishonored and Zeus pledged to honor him at the army's cost (1.505–510; see the note on Book 1). Book 9 begins with the cost paid: the army routed, the Trojans camped by the ships, the king in tears. Nestor names the cause in Achilles' own words: “the best of men, whom the immortals themselves honored, you dishonored; for you took his prize and keep it” (9.110–111; 1.356). Agamemnon admits his blind folly and grants that Zeus “has honored this man and beaten down the army of the Achaeans” (9.115–118; see [[ate]] and [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]). The injury is no longer in dispute. The question of the night is what will make it good.

## The payment

Agamemnon's answer is compensation on a scale the poem never matches: treasure and horses at once, Briseis returned untouched under oath, a shipload of Troy's spoil and twenty Trojan women when the city falls, a daughter without bride-price and with a dowry, and seven cities whose people will honor Achilles “with gifts like a god” (9.121–157; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-seven-cities]]). It is also an assertion of rank. Achilles is to become the king's son-in-law, honored “equally with Orestes”, his son (9.142), and the offer ends with its condition: “let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” and the elder (9.160–161; see [[basileus]] and [[genee]]). Odysseus repeats the gifts and leaves the condition out (9.264–299; see [[message-repeated]]). In its place he offers the army's gratitude: pity the Achaeans, “like a god they will honor you”, and kill Hector for glory (9.300–306; see [[kydos]]). The envoys' word for what Achilles will receive is always the same, τιμή: from the seven cities, from the army, “as equal to a god” in Phoenix's last promise (9.155, 9.303, 9.603; see [[time]]).

## The refusal

Achilles' answer to Odysseus takes the system of honor apart. Fighting has brought him “no thanks”; the man who stays behind and the man who fights hardest get the same share, “the coward and the brave are held in a single honor”, and they die alike (9.316–320; see [[charis]], [[moira]] and [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). He has brought in the spoils of twenty-three cities, and Agamemnon gave prizes to the others and took back his alone (9.328–336; see [[geras]]). The gifts are hateful, and the king is worth “a hair” to him (9.378; see [[at-a-hairs-worth]]). No quantity of goods will persuade him “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.387; see [[lobe]]), and he does not say what payment would. He refuses the marriage that would make him the king's inferior: let Agamemnon find a son-in-law “who is more kingly” (9.392). Last, he sets life against honor. Goods can be raided or won, but not a life (9.401–409; see [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]]), and his mother has told him that fame will cost him his (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]).

How new this logic is has been debated. Adam Parry argued that Achilles sees through the heroic code but has no language except its own in which to say so (“The Language of Achilles”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 87, 1956); others, among them M. D. Reeve (*Classical Quarterly* 23, 1973), answered that his language is the common language of the poem, used with uncommon force. The words are those of every other speaker, τιμή, γέρας, δῶρα; the conclusions are his own.

## Honor from Zeus

Phoenix's warning is that honor depends on timing. If Achilles comes into the fighting “without the gifts”, he will not be held “in the same honor”, as Meleager fought at last and was never paid (9.598–605; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Achilles answers with a different source of honor:

The honor Thetis asked of Zeus (1.505–510) is enough; the honor the Achaeans would pay in gifts he does not need (see [[aisa]]). He gives honor instead, offering Phoenix half his own (9.616). To Ajax, who reproaches him for disregarding the friends who honored him above all (9.630–631), he answers with the wound itself: Agamemnon treated him “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (9.648; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]).

## Afterward

The refusal does not end Achilles' concern with honor. When he sends Patroclus out, he still asks him to win “great honor and glory” from the Danaans, so that they will send back the girl “and give splendid gifts besides” (16.84–86), as if no gifts had been offered. The lines are one of the passages that have been used to argue that the embassy was added to the poem late; others read them as Achilles' state of mind (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). In Book 19 he takes the gifts with indifference (19.146–148), and in Book 23 he is the giver of prizes, honoring old Nestor with a gift for which there was no contest (23.615–650).
