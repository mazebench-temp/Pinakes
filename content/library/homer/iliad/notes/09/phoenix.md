**Named first.** Phoenix first appears in Agamemnon's hut, where the elders have feasted, when Nestor chooses the envoys: “First of all let Phoenix, dear to Zeus, lead the way, and after him great Ajax and brilliant Odysseus” (9.168–169; see [[nestor]] and [[embassy]]). The poem does not say why Achilles' own man is sitting in Agamemnon's council. ἡγησάσθω, “let him lead the way”, seems to put him at the head of the embassy. Yet on the walk along the shore and in Achilles' greeting the verbs are dual, “the two” (9.182–198), and it is Odysseus who leads them in (9.192). Whether Phoenix went ahead, or is not counted among the envoys because he belongs to Achilles' household, or was added to an older story of two envoys, has long been debated (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**The nod.** After the meal “Ajax nodded to Phoenix; and brilliant Odysseus saw it” (9.223), and Odysseus spoke first. The nod suggests that Phoenix was expected to begin. When Achilles has refused Odysseus, he sets Phoenix apart from the others: “But let Phoenix stay here with us and lie down to sleep, so that he may follow me in the ships to our dear native land tomorrow, if he wishes; but I will not take him by force” (9.427–429).

**The speech.** “At last the old horse-driving Phoenix spoke among them, bursting into tears, for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans” (9.432–433). His speech (9.434–605) is the longest in the Iliad. He begins with himself: how could he be left behind, when Peleus sent him with the boy?

The verse that dates the sending, “on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon”, is the one Odysseus used to bring in Peleus' charge (9.439 = 9.253; see [[peleus]]). Speaking and doing are the two halves of a hero's worth, as in the pairing “by word or deed” (see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]], [[word-or-deed]] and [[phoenix-the-model-teacher]]). Phoenix could not be parted from Achilles even if a god made him young again, as he was when he fled his father's house (9.444–484; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]), and he reminds Achilles of the child who would eat only on Phoenix's knees and sputtered wine over his tunic: “it was you, Achilles, like the gods, that I made my son, so that one day you might ward off shameful ruin from me” (9.485–495). The old man who reared him asks for the return a son owes (see [[threptra]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

Then he turns from his own case to the gods. “Even the gods themselves can be turned” by sacrifice and prayer (9.497–501), and the Prayers, the lame daughters of Zeus, follow behind Blind Folly and heal the harm she does; whoever refuses them, they ask Zeus to send her after him (9.502–512; see [[litai]] and [[ate]]). Agamemnon is giving gifts and has sent the men dearest to Achilles, and “before now there was no blame in your anger” (9.515–523). Last come “the famous deeds of the men of old” (9.524): Meleager withdrew in anger and refused the elders and priests, his father, his sisters and mother and his companions, and fought at last when the city was burning, without the gifts (9.527–599; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[meleager]]). The lesson is spelled out: “While the gifts are offered, come; for the Achaeans will honor you as equal to a god” (9.602–603).

**Achilles' answer.** Achilles calls him “Phoenix, old father, nurtured by Zeus” and wants none of the honor he offers: “I think I am honored by the portion of Zeus” (9.607–608; see [[aisa]]). He tells Phoenix not to confuse his heart with weeping and grieving as a favor to Agamemnon, “or you will make yourself hateful to me, who love you”, and answers Agamemnon's seven cities with an offer of his own: “Be king equally with me, and take half my honor” (9.612–616). Then, for the first time, he leaves the sailing open: “when dawn appears we will consider whether to go home to our own or to stay” (9.618–619). The answer is softer than the one he gave Odysseus, and Odysseus will not report it (see [[achilles]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The bed.** Achilles nods to Patroclus to spread a bed for Phoenix, a sign for the others to leave (9.620–622); before the speeches, Ajax had nodded to Phoenix (9.223). When the envoys have gone, Patroclus has his companions and the slave women spread the bed with fleeces, a rug and fine linen: “There the old man lay down and waited for brilliant Dawn” (9.658–662). In Agamemnon's hut Odysseus reports that Phoenix has stayed, in Achilles' own words (9.690–692 ≈ 9.427–429).
