---
summary: In Book 9 the short life becomes a choice. Achilles tells the envoys that his mother has given him two fates, a short life with imperishable fame or a long one at home without it, and he sets life above any gift; in Book 1 the shortness of his life was his claim to honor, and here it is his reason to refuse honor's price.
refs: [9.401-416]
passages:
  - 9.401-416 | Nothing is worth my life
---

**The argument reversed.** In Book 1 Achilles reasoned from his short life to honor: since his mother bore him “short-lived”, Zeus owed him honor at least (1.352–356). The honor has now been offered in the greatest quantity the poem knows, and he reasons the other way. Nothing is worth his life, not the wealth of Ilios or of Pytho, for cattle and tripods can be won, but a man's soul cannot be raided back once it has passed his teeth (9.401–409; see [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]] and [[psyche]]). A short life could be paid for with honor while honor was denied him; once honor is offered as goods, the life is worth more than the goods.

**A long life.** Then he names the alternative that Book 1 did not mention. If he goes home, “my noble fame is lost, but my life will be long, and the end of death will not come on me quickly” (9.415–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]], [[kleos]] and [[ker-doom]]). αἰών, “life, lifetime”, the word for the long years at home, is the word the poem used for the life of Simoeisius, “short was his life” (4.478). For one night the short life is not a fact but a road he may refuse, and in this speech he says he will refuse it and sail home at dawn (9.356–363, 9.417–420; see [[nostos]]).

**A whole life.** The same night gives the beginning of that life as well as its two possible ends. Phoenix remembers the child who would eat with no one else, sat on his knees and fed with meat cut small, who spat the wine out over his tunic (9.485–491). The old man who reared him as his own son, since the gods gave him none, so that one day he might ward off ruin from him (9.492–495), is speaking to a man who will not grow old.

Achilles does not sail. When Patroclus is dead he takes the short road knowingly, and Thetis tells him that his death is ready straight after Hector's (18.95–96).
