summary: Three libations mark the night: the elders pour and drink before the envoys set out (9.174–177), the envoys pour from two-handled cups as they leave Achilles (9.656–657), and the kings pour before they go to their huts to sleep (9.712). Phoenix counts libation among the offerings with which men turn the gods (9.499–501).
grc: [σπεῖσάν, σπείσαντες, λοιβῇ]
passages:

  - 9.710-713 | The kings pour libations and sleep
---

**Before the embassy.** Nestor's call for water and holy silence before the prayer to Zeus (9.171–172) is carried out in a fixed sequence of verses: “At once the heralds poured water over their hands, and the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink, and served it out to all, first pouring drops for the libation into the cups. But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired, they set out” (9.174–178; see [[hand-washing]] and [[cupbearing]]). The same verses return in the Odyssey, at Pylos when Athena, as Mentor, bids the company pour to Poseidon and the other gods and think of bed, and in Odysseus' hall before the contest of the bow (Od. 3.338–342, 21.270–273). The last of them, “when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired”, occurs in the Iliad only here; in the Odyssey it comes six times, most often at the close of an evening (Od. 3.342, 3.395, 7.184, 7.228, 18.427, 21.273). The libation goes with the prayer that the narrator does not quote, that Zeus “will have pity” (9.172; see [[prayer]] and [[embassy]]).

**Leaving Achilles.** When Achilles has given his last answer, “each man took a two-handled cup and poured libation, and they went back along the ships” (9.656–657; see [[cup]]). The guests pour before they go, as the Phaeacians in the Odyssey pour to Hermes last of all before they think of bed, and as Odysseus asks Alcinous to pour a libation and send him home (Od. 7.136–138, 13.38–39).

**Before sleep.** The book ends with a libation: “And then they poured libations and went each to his hut, and there they lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep” (9.712–713). The suitors close a day in the same way in the Odyssey, pouring to the gods and going “each to his own house” (Od. 18.418–428). At the end of Book 7 the wine went on the ground for fear of Zeus' thunder (7.478–482); at the end of Book 9 it is poured after a night of failed persuasion, before a dawn of battle (see [[dawn-line]]).

**Turning the gods.** In Phoenix's speech libation is one of the means by which men win back an angry god: “with burnt offerings and gentle vows, with libation and the savor of the burnt fat, men turn them aside, entreating them, whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (9.499–501). λοιβή and κνίση together are the gods' share in Zeus' words of Book 4 (λοιβῆς τε κνίσης τε, 4.49; see [[sacrifice]] and [[propitiation]]).
