summary: Book 9 opens and closes with Diomedes' blame of the king, both times applauded; between them Nestor praises and corrects, Achilles abuses Agamemnon as a shameless dog, and Ajax turns blame on Achilles himself. The fear of men's reproaches holds Phoenix back from his worst deed.
passages:
  - 9.32-51 | Diomedes answers Agamemnon
  - 9.628-642 | Ajax's appeal
  - 9.696-711 | Diomedes' last word
---

**The rebuke returned.** Diomedes answers Agamemnon's proposal to flee with a rebuke, and claims the right to it: it is “the custom, my lord, in the assembly” (9.32–33; see [[themis]]). The king once reproached his courage before the Danaans (9.34–36; see [[oneidos]] and [[alke]]); in Book 4 Diomedes kept silent, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.401–402), and let Sthenelus answer. Now he pays it back, and the army shouts its approval, “admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses” (9.50–51). The verse returns at the end of the night, when all the kings approve his last speech (9.710–711).

**Praise with a correction.** Nestor's answer is a model of praise that leads somewhere. Diomedes is mighty in war, best in counsel among his age-mates, and no one will find fault with his word; “but you have not reached the end of the matter”, and the old man will finish it (9.53–62; see [[genee]]). To the king he speaks plainly as well: Agamemnon dishonored the best of men against Nestor's advice (9.104–111). Agamemnon takes the blame on himself (9.115–120; see [[ate]]).

**Abuse.** Achilles' blame of the king has no restraint left. Agamemnon is “clothed forever in shamelessness”, a “dog” who would not dare to look him in the face; Zeus has taken away his wits; let him go to his ruin (9.372–377; see [[anaideia]] and [[dog-face]]). These are the insults of Book 1 (1.149, 1.159, 1.225), now said to the king's envoys to be reported “openly”, so that the army may share his indignation (9.369–370; see [[nemesis]]).

**Blame turned on Achilles.** Phoenix told Achilles gently that he had no need of a pitiless heart (9.496–497). Ajax's blame is sharper. He speaks of him to Odysseus as σχέτλιος, “hard man”, who does not regard the friendship of his companions, and νηλής, “pitiless one” (9.628–632; see [[philotes]]), and then to Achilles himself: the gods have put in him “a heart that is unrelenting and evil, for the sake of a girl” (9.636–637). Achilles grants that Ajax has spoken after his own heart (9.645). At the end Diomedes blames the whole embassy: “I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus” (9.698).

**Praise in a name.** The man who brings back the refusal is greeted by the king as πολύαινος, “much-praised”, from αἶνος, “praise” (9.673; see [[much-praised]] and the translation's note on 9.673). In Phoenix's bracketed lines the reverse of praise is what keeps a man from crime: a god put in his heart “the talk of the people and the many reproaches of men” (9.460; see [[aidos]]).
