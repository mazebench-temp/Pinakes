summary: The verb's two senses meet in Book 9. Nestor and Agamemnon each “claim” seniority, Nestor to speak and Agamemnon to be obeyed; the envoys “pray” to Poseidon on the shore, and in Phoenix's plea vows turn the gods and the Prayers hear the man who prays.

**Claims.** Twice a speaker stakes himself on his age with the same verse-end, εὔχομαι εἶναι, “I claim to be”. Nestor, praising Diomedes, takes over his argument: “let me, who claim to be older than you, speak out and go through it all; and no one will dishonor my word, not even powerful Agamemnon” (γεραίτερος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 9.60–62). Agamemnon ends his offer with the same claim turned into a demand: let Achilles submit to him, “inasmuch as I claim to be the elder by birth” (προγενέστερος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 9.161; see [[genee]]). The old man claims the right to be heard; the king claims the right to be obeyed. The prayer note sets these lines aside as claims (see [[prayer]]); this entry takes both senses together.

**Prayers.** The envoys walk along the shore “praying earnestly” to Poseidon that they may win Achilles over (εὐχομένω, 9.183). The verb is dual, one of the duals of the embassy (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Phoenix's argument for yielding rests on what prayer does to the gods: men turn them “with burnt offerings and gentle vows” (εὐχωλῇς ἀγανῇσι, 9.499; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]), and whoever respects the Prayers is helped, and they “hear him when he prays” (ἔκλυον εὐχομένοιο, 9.509; see [[litai]]). εὔχομαι is the general word, λίσσομαι the word of the suppliant who begs (λισσόμενοι, 9.501), and Phoenix uses both: the gods listen to the man who prays, and the man who will not listen to entreaty is not heard.
