summary: The envoys ask Achilles for pity when gifts and honor are not enough: Odysseus asks him to pity the worn-down army, Phoenix tells him he has no need of “a pitiless heart”, and Ajax calls him pitiless when he refuses. The elders' prayer before the embassy asks the same of Zeus.
passages:
  - 9.300-306 | Odysseus: pity the Achaeans
  - 9.496-501 | Even the gods can be turned
  - 9.628-632 | Achilles does not regard his companions
---

**Pity asked of Zeus.** Nestor sends the envoys off with a prayer: “so that we may pray to Zeus, son of Cronus, if he will have pity” (αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ, 9.172). Earlier that day Zeus pitied Agamemnon's tears and sent an eagle (8.245–250); the elders ask him again, before they ask Achilles.

**Pity asked of Achilles.** Odysseus ends his speech with pity as the last resort: if Agamemnon and his gifts are hateful to him, “yet the rest, all the Achaeans, worn down throughout the army, pity them” (ἐλέαιρε, 9.300–302). The army, not the king, is the object, and the reward is the army's honor and the glory of killing Hector (9.302–306; see [[kydos]]). Phoenix asks him to tame his great spirit, since he has no need “to have a pitiless heart” (νηλεὲς ἦτορ, 9.496–497), when even the gods can be turned (see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]). Ajax, when Achilles has refused twice, calls him νηλής, “pitiless one”, the word of the bronze that kills (9.632; see [[pitiless]] and [[philotes]]).

**Pity refused.** Achilles answers none of these appeals directly. He bids the others sail home and save themselves (9.417–420), and to Ajax he says that he will not think of war until Hector reaches the Myrmidons' ships (9.650–655). In Book 16 Patroclus comes to him weeping like the spring of dark water, and “brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him when he saw him” (16.2–5); Patroclus calls him νηλεές, “pitiless”, all the same (16.33), and Achilles sends him out in his place. Pity for Priam comes only in the last book (see the article above).
