---
summary: Phoenix's story is the Iliad's fullest account of a father and son: Amyntor cursed his son never to have a son of his own, Peleus received the fugitive and loved him like an only son, and the childless Phoenix raised Achilles as his. Around it Agamemnon offers to make Achilles his son-in-law, Peleus' charges are remembered, and Oeneus begs his son in vain.
passages:
  - 9.447-457 | Amyntor's curse
  - 9.478-495 | Peleus receives Phoenix; Phoenix rears Achilles
  - 9.581-583 | Oeneus begs his son
---

**A father's curse.** Phoenix fled his father Amyntor after a quarrel over a concubine. His mother begged him to lie with the girl first, so that she would turn from the old man; he did, and his father cursed him many times and called on the Erinyes “that he would never set upon his knees a dear son born of me”, and the gods fulfilled the curse (9.447–457; see [[amyntor]], [[curses]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). In the four lines that the manuscripts lack, the son's answer was worse: he planned to kill his father, until a god put in his heart the fear of being called a father-killer (9.458–461; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]). His kinsmen kept him at home for nine nights, and on the tenth he broke out and fled (9.462–478).

**A second father.** Peleus received him “and loved me as a father loves his son, his only son, his darling, heir to great possessions”, made him wealthy, and gave him the Dolopians to rule (9.480–484; see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]] and [[darling]]). The curse took away a son; the refugee found a father.

**A foster son.** Phoenix gave Peleus' son what Peleus had given him. He raised Achilles “loving you from my heart”: the child would eat with no one else, and sat on Phoenix's knees to be fed, and spat his wine over Phoenix's tunic (9.485–491). Then he names the curse again and its remedy: “the gods would never bring to birth a child of my own; but it was you, Achilles, like the gods, that I made my son, so that one day you might ward off shameful ruin from me” (9.493–495). The knees on which Amyntor's grandson would never sit held Achilles instead. Phoenix calls him “dear child” (φίλον τέκος, 9.437, 9.444), and Achilles answers “Phoenix, old father” (ἄττα γεραιέ, 9.607; see [[old-father]] and [[phoenix]]).

**Peleus' charges.** Two envoys remember the day Peleus sent his son to Troy (see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). Odysseus recalls the charge to hold back his proud heart, “for kindliness is better” (9.252–259; see [[kindliness-is-better]]); Phoenix, that Peleus sent him with the boy to make him “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.438–443; see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]). Achilles thinks of his father as the one who would find him a wife at home and leave him his possessions (9.394–400; see [[peleus]]).

**Sons-in-law and sons.** Agamemnon offers to make Achilles his γαμβρός, “son-in-law”, and to honor him “equally with Orestes, who is reared as my darling son” (9.141–143; see [[orestes]] and [[in-laws]]); Achilles refuses to marry into the house (9.388–392). Nestor tells Diomedes that he might be his son, the youngest (9.57–58). Ajax's blood-price is accepted even “for his own son who is dead” (9.633; see [[poine]]).

**A father's plea.** In the story of Meleager, old Oeneus stands on the threshold of his son's chamber, shaking the jointed doors, “imploring his son” (9.581–583), and is refused; his mother had cursed him (see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Phoenix, the father who took a son, tells Achilles of a father whose son would not listen.
