summary: Twelve prize-winning horses are among the gifts Agamemnon offers at once, and their winnings are his measure of wealth; Achilles names horses among the things that can be won, unlike a man's life; and Diomedes ends the book with an order to draw up the army and the horses before the ships.
passages:
  - 9.123-127 | The twelve horses
---

**The prize-winners.** After the tripods, the gold and the cauldrons come “twelve horses, strong prize-winners, which have won prizes with their feet”, and Agamemnon adds a boast: “No man would be landless who had as much, nor lacking in precious gold, as the prizes these single-hoofed horses have carried off for me” (9.123–127; see [[prize-winning]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The horses are counted twice, as a gift and as a source of wealth, for they have won prizes in races at games (see [[contests]]). Neleus once sent four such horses to race for a tripod in Elis (11.698–702). Odysseus repeats the verses, but where Agamemnon said “these horses … for me”, he says “Agamemnon's horses” (9.269; see [[message-repeated]]). The twelve horses are led out of Agamemnon's hut in Book 19 with the other gifts (19.243–244).

**What can be won.** Achilles sets the gifts against his life: “tripods can be won, and the tawny heads of horses” (9.407), but not a man's soul once it has passed his teeth (see [[tripod]] and [[psyche]]). His measure of untold wealth is Egyptian Thebes, where two hundred men drive out of each of its hundred gates “with horses and chariots” (9.383–384; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]).

**At dawn.** The book's last order is for battle: “quickly draw up the army and the horses before the ships”, says Diomedes, and let Agamemnon fight among the foremost (9.708–709).
