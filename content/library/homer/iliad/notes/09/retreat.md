---
summary: The night opens with the king proposing in earnest the flight he proposed as a test in Book 2, and Diomedes refuses: let Agamemnon go, the rest will stay, and if all go he and Sthenelus will fight on alone. Achilles proposes the same flight to the whole army, since Troy will not be taken; Diomedes' answer stands.
passages:
  - 9.17-28 | Agamemnon proposes flight
  - 9.32-49 | Diomedes answers Agamemnon
  - 9.417-420 | Sail away home
---

**Flight proposed.** Agamemnon tells the assembly, in the verses of his test in Book 2, that Zeus has deceived him: “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land; for we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (9.26–28 = 2.139–141; see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[nostos]]). In Book 2 the army ran for the ships. This time it sits in silence (9.29–30).

**Flight refused.** Diomedes answers that the king may go: the road is open and his ships are by the sea. The others will stay until they sack Troy; and if they too wish to flee, “the two of us, I and Sthenelus, will fight until we find the end of Ilios; for we came here with a god” (9.42–49; see [[diomedes]] and [[sthenelus]]). The army shouts its approval (9.50–51).

**Flight urged again.** In his answer to Odysseus Achilles takes up the king's proposal for himself and for the army. He will launch his ships at dawn, and he advises the rest “to sail away home, since you will no longer find the end of steep Ilios” (9.356–363, 9.417–420). The phrase is Diomedes' own, turned round: τέκμωρ Ἰλίου, “the end of Ilios”, which Diomedes swore to find and Achilles says they will not (see [[tekmor]]). Odysseus reports the advice word for word (9.684–687). Diomedes does not argue with it. He leaves Achilles to go or stay, and tells the king to draw up the army at dawn and fight among the foremost (9.701–709). No one in the hut proposes flight again.
