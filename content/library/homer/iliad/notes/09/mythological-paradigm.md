---
summary: Phoenix's story of Meleager is one of the longest paradigms in the Iliad, a tale of the famous deeds of men of old told to persuade Achilles to take the gifts while they are offered (9.524–605). Its likeness to Achilles' case is close in every part, and what it foretells comes true in a way its teller did not intend.
refs: [9.524-528, 9.600-605]
passages:

  - 9.600-605 | Phoenix's lesson for Achilles
---

**The frame.** Phoenix brings the story in as a kind: “So too we have heard the famous deeds of the men of old, the heroes, whenever furious anger came upon one of them: they could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (9.524–526). κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men”, are what Achilles was singing when the envoys came (9.189; see [[kleos]] and [[song-and-fame]]). Then he names his own memory as its source, “I remember this deed from long ago, not recent at all, how it was” (9.527–528), and at the end he turns to the listener: “But do not have such thoughts in your mind … it would be worse to defend the ships when they are already burning. While the gifts are offered, come” (9.600–603). The story is closed inside its lesson (see [[ring-composition]]).

**Fitted to the case.** Meleager fought for his people until anger entered him; he withdrew to lie beside his wife; the elders begged him and offered a great gift; the dearest came one after another; he refused until the enemy were burning the city; and he saved it at last without the gifts (9.550–599; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]], [[meleager]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]). Each part answers something in Achilles' night, and some details, the order of the suppliants and the wife's name Cleopatra, which reverses the parts of Patroclus' name, have been thought shaped or invented for the purpose (see the translation's notes on 9.524–605 and 9.556). It is the oldest telling of the story that survives, and it has neither Atalanta nor the brand of later versions.

**Lesson and forecast.** Phoenix draws the moral from the gifts: Meleager fought too late to be paid, and Achilles will not be “held in the same honor” if he fights without them (9.598–599, 9.604–605). The poem follows the story further than the lesson. Achilles waits until the fire reaches the ships, and then his dearest companion goes out in his place; when he returns to the war himself in Book 19, he lets Agamemnon give the gifts or keep them as he pleases (16.112–129, 19.146–148). Read so, the paradigm fails as advice and comes true as a forecast.

**Other examples.** Phoenix's speech holds two more kinds of example: his own life, the quarrel with his father and the curse (9.447–495; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]), and the allegory of the Prayers, which teaches by personification rather than by story (9.502–512; see [[litai]]). Ajax argues from custom instead, from the blood-price that even a brother's killer may pay (9.632–636; see [[poine]]).
