summary: The taken prize is the night's grievance. Nestor tells Agamemnon to his face that he took it and keeps it, Agamemnon offers to give Briseis back untouched, and Achilles answers that the other chiefs keep their prizes, while from him alone the man who gave his prize has taken it back.
passages:
  - 9.330-345 | From me alone he has taken mine
  - 9.367-369 | The man who gave it took it back
---

**Taken and kept.** Nestor's charge in the council ends on the prize: “for you took his prize and keep it” (9.111). The words are Achilles' complaint to his mother (1.356), and the present tense, ἔχεις, “you keep”, makes the wrong a continuing one (see [[time]]). Agamemnon's offer answers it point by point. With the seven women of Lesbos will come “the one I took from him then, the daughter of Briseus”, and he will swear a great oath that he never lay with her (9.131–134; see [[briseis]] and [[oath]]). The prize is to come back as it left.

**Who gave it.** In Book 1 the prize was the army's gift: “the sons of the Achaeans gave” her (1.162, 1.276, 1.392). Achilles tells it differently to the envoys. All the treasures of the twenty-three cities he sacked he brought to Agamemnon, who “would take them, and share out a few, and keep many. Others he gave as prizes to the chiefs and the kings” (9.331–334). The king, in this account, hands out the prizes, and the grievance is that one man was singled out: “they keep theirs safe, but from me alone of the Achaeans he has taken mine” (9.335–336). He comes back to it later in the speech: “my prize, the very man who gave it has taken back again in his arrogance” (9.367–368; see [[hybris]] and [[division-of-spoils]]). In Book 16 he says again that the sons of the Achaeans chose her out for him as his prize (16.56), and Thetis tells Hephaestus the same (18.444); the giver changes with the speaker's purpose.

**More than a prize.** The prize is also a woman. Achilles calls Briseis “the wife dear to my heart” and says that he loved her “though she was won by my spear” (9.336, 9.343), and the sons of Atreus, who went to war for a wife, are not the only men who love their own (9.339–342; see [[captive-women]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Ajax answers with arithmetic: all this “for the sake of a girl, one girl”, when seven are offered (9.637–638).

**The prize of the old.** Achilles sends the envoys back to the chiefs with his message, “for that is the prize of the old” (9.422). Nestor claimed counsel as the γέρας of age in Book 4 (4.323); here the formula gives the elders the task of devising a better plan than the one that failed (see [[prize-of-the-old]]).
