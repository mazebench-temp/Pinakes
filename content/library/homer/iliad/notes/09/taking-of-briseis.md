summary: In the night of Book 9 the taking of Briseis is told again by those it divided. Nestor makes it Agamemnon's own deed, the envoys walk the heralds' path to offer her back, and Achilles tells it as a theft and a deceit by the man who had given her.

**Nestor's version.** Nestor dates the army's trouble from the day “when you, sprung from Zeus, took the girl Briseis and went from the hut of Achilles in his anger” (9.106–107). In Book 1 Agamemnon threatened to come for her himself, “coming with more men” (1.184–187, 1.324–325), but sent his heralds Talthybius and Eurybates, and Patroclus led her out to them (1.318–348). Nestor speaks of the deed as Agamemnon's own, as Achilles does all through the book (9.336, 9.344, 9.367–368). He had argued against it in the assembly (1.275–276) and says so again (9.108–109; see [[nestor]]).

**The path walked again.** The envoys go to Achilles in the verses that took the heralds to fetch her: “the two went along the shore” and “came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons” (9.182, 9.185; compare 1.327–328). Then the heralds stood in fear and said nothing; now the envoys come to give her back, with seven women of Lesbos and Agamemnon's oath that he never lay with her (9.131–134, 9.270–276; see [[briseis]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**Achilles' version.** To Achilles the taking was a theft and a fraud. From him alone of the Achaeans Agamemnon took his prize, and keeps “the wife dear to my heart” (9.335–336); “he has taken my prize from my hands and deceived me” (9.344); “my prize, the very man who gave it has taken back again in his arrogance” (9.367–368). In Book 1 he said that the sons of the Achaeans had given her to him (1.162, 1.299). In Book 9 the giver is Agamemnon, the king who handed out prizes from the spoil that Achilles won (9.330–336; see [[achilles-raids]] and [[geras]]). Ajax gives the loss its smallest measure, “a girl, one girl” (9.637–638), and Achilles answers with the memory of the insult, “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (9.648; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]).

He tells it once more to Patroclus in the same words (16.56–59), and when he gives up his anger he wishes that Artemis had killed her on the day he took Lyrnessus (19.59–60; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]).
