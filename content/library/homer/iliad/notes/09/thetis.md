---
summary: Achilles names his mother once, as the source of what he knows about his death. She has told him that two fates carry him toward the end, imperishable fame if he stays and fights at Troy, a long life without fame if he goes home (9.410–416).
passages:
  - 9.410-416 | Achilles' two fates
---

Thetis does not appear in Book 9, but the turning point of Achilles' reply to Odysseus rests on her word. He has said that nothing is worth his life (9.401–409), and he gives his reason:

{{quote:47e1e08a-eeb7-5938-9759-5a184966aa85}}

φησι, “tells me”, is present, and she often tells him such things. In Book 17 Achilles has often heard from his mother, who reports to him the mind of great Zeus, but she never told him that his dearest companion would die (17.408–411). In Book 1 her word is simpler. His portion of life is brief (αἶσα μίνυνθα, 1.416), he is “swift-fated and wretched beyond all men” (1.417), and he calls himself short-lived (μινυνθάδιον, 1.352). In Book 18 she tells him that his death is ready right after Hector's (μεθʼ Ἕκτορα πότμος ἑτοῖμος, 18.96). Only here are there two fates and a choice between them (see the translation's note on 9.401–416). Whether Achilles has always known of the choice or the poet frames it for this speech, the poem does not say. The others suspect something of the kind: Nestor and Patroclus both wonder whether he is shunning a prophecy that his lady mother has told him from Zeus (θεοπροπίη … πὰρ Ζηνός, 11.794–795 = 16.36–37).

The nearest likeness is Euchenor of Corinth, son of the seer Polyidus, who knew the two deaths his father had often foretold, a painful sickness at home or death at the Achaean ships, and chose to sail; Paris shoots him (13.660–672). Achilles, who says here that he will sail home (9.356–363, 9.417–418), makes his choice only after Patroclus is dead. Then he will accept his death whenever Zeus wills it, νῦν δὲ κλέος ἐσθλὸν ἀροίμην, “but now may I win noble fame” (18.114–121). κλέος ἄφθιτον, “imperishable fame”, occurs only here in Homer (see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]], [[kleos]], [[nostos]], [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

The honor Achilles claims at the end of the night is also his mother's doing. He needs no honor from Agamemnon's gifts, he tells Phoenix, since he is “honored by the portion of Zeus” (9.608), and that honor is what Thetis begged of Zeus: “honor my son” (1.505; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[zeus]]).
