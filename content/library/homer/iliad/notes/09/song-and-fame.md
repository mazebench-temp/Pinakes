summary: The envoys find Achilles singing the famous deeds of men to a lyre from the spoils of Eetion's city, and Phoenix answers with a famous deed of the men of old, the wrath of Meleager. Between them Achilles names the fame that will be sung of him, κλέος ἄφθιτον, if he stays and dies at Troy.
passages:
  - 9.186-191 | Achilles sings the famous deeds of men
  - 9.524-528 | The famous deeds of the men of old
---

**The singer.** Achilles is the only hero of the Iliad who is shown singing. Withdrawn from the fighting, he sings κλέα ἀνδρῶν to a lyre he took when he destroyed Eetion's city, and Patroclus sits facing him in silence, “waiting for the grandson of Aeacus to leave off his singing” (9.186–191; see [[lyre]], [[sack-of-thebe]] and [[kleos]]). The song keeps the fame of other men while its singer has put his own at risk. The scene is described, not quoted: the poem does not say what deeds he sang.

**The teller.** Phoenix answers the singer with a tale of the same kind. “So too we have heard the famous deeds of the men of old, the heroes, whenever furious anger came upon one of them” (9.524–525), and he will tell one that he remembers “from long ago, not recent at all” (9.527). The song in the hut was for pleasure; the tale is for persuasion, with a lesson drawn at its end, “do not have such thoughts in your mind” (9.600; see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Achilles does not take it.

**The song to come.** Between the two, Achilles names his own place in such songs. If he stays at Troy he loses his homecoming, “but my fame will be imperishable” (9.413; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). The deeds of men that he sings are the kind of fame he would win by dying, and the poem in which he sings them is the song of that fame. The same words, “a clear-toned lyre” (φόρμιγγι λιγείῃ, 9.186), describe the lyre that a boy plays on the shield of Achilles as he sings the Linus song at the vintage (18.569–572), and in the Odyssey Demodocus sings, as the famous deeds of men, a quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles (*Odyssey* 8.73–75).
