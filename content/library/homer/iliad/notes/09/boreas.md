summary: The North Wind and the West Wind, blowing together “from Thrace”, are the simile for the Achaeans' despair at the opening of the book (9.4–8).

passages:
  - 9.4-8 | The two winds from Thrace
---

The book opens on the Achaeans held by Panic, and the simile gives their grief the force of two winds at once:

{{quote:63cd51ff-19a6-5536-9421-f310d712a415}}

The winds come on “suddenly”, the dark wave rears up and throws seaweed on the shore, “so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (9.6–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). The two are paired again at the pyre of Patroclus. Achilles prays to Boreas and Zephyrus to make the fire burn, Iris finds them feasting in the house of Zephyrus, they fan the flames all night, and at dawn they go home “over the Thracian sea” (Θρηΐκιον κατὰ πόντον, 23.192–230).

Thrace is the North Wind's country. In Hesiod he blows “through horse-rearing Thrace” and stirs up the wide sea (διὰ Θρῄκης ἱπποτρόφου εὐρέι πόντῳ ἐμπνεύσας ὤρινε, *Works and Days* 507–508), with the verb of the simile (ὀρίνετον, 9.4), and he is “the Thracian North Wind” (Θρηικίου Βορέου, 553). That the West Wind too should blow from Thrace has seemed odd. Seen from the coast of Asia Minor, Thrace lies north and northwest across the sea (see the translation's note on 9.4–7, [[zephyrus]] and [[thrace]]).
