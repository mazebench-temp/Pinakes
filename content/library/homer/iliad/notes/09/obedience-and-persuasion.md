---
summary: Book 9 is the poem's fullest trial of persuasion. Three envoys use every ground the poem knows, gifts, pity, glory, a father's charge, a story from the past, the example of the gods, the claims of friendship and of a host's roof, and Achilles refuses all three; yet each of his answers gives a little more ground than the last.
passages:
  - 9.307-429 | Achilles' answer to Odysseus
  - 9.606-619 | Achilles' answer to Phoenix
  - 9.643-655 | Achilles' answer to Ajax
---

**The means.** Nestor names the two instruments at the start, gifts and words: “how we may make amends to him and win him over with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.112–113; see [[peitho]] and [[doron]]). The three envoys divide the work. Odysseus brings the gifts, leaving out the demand that would offend, and adds pity for the army, the glory of killing Hector, and the memory of Peleus' charge (9.225–306; see [[kindliness-is-better]]). Phoenix brings his own life with Achilles, the example of the gods, who can be turned, the allegory of the Prayers, and a story from the past, the wrath of Meleager (9.434–605; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]], [[litai]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Ajax brings the custom of the blood-price, the friendship of the companions, and the claim of guests under a host's roof (9.624–642; see [[poine]], [[philotes]] and [[xenia]]).

**The answers.** Achilles refuses each, and each refusal is less absolute than the last. To Odysseus he says that he will launch his ships at dawn and sail home (9.356–363); to Phoenix, that at dawn they will consider whether to go or stay (9.618–619); to Ajax, that he will not think of fighting until Hector reaches the Myrmidons' huts and ships (9.650–655). Nothing that he is told persuades him to fight, and nothing persuades him to go. He grants Ajax that “all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart”, and still his heart swells with anger when he remembers (9.645–647; see [[cholos]]). Plato's Socrates would make the shift a charge of inconsistency against him (*Hippias Minor* 370a–371e; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]).

**Obedience.** Around the failed persuasion everyone else obeys. The army “listened closely to him and obeyed” when Nestor spoke (9.79), Agamemnon accepts Nestor's rebuke (9.115–120), the envoys go as they are told (9.167–181), and the kings approve Diomedes' last counsel (9.710–711). The only man on the Achaean side who cannot be persuaded is the one whom the whole night is spent persuading (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).
