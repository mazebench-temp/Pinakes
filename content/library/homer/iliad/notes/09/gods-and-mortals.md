---
summary: Book 9 is a night without gods on the scene, but every speaker measures men against them. The envoys promise Achilles honor “like a god”, Phoenix argues that even the gods can be turned, and the stories he tells are full of gods who fulfill curses, punish a forgotten sacrifice and carry off a bride.
passages:
  - 9.496-512 | Even the gods can be turned; the Prayers
  - 9.533-537 | Oeneus forgets Artemis
---

**A night of men.** No god appears in Book 9. Zeus' lightning on the right for the Trojans is reported by Odysseus, not seen (9.236–239), and the night's decisions are made by men in huts. Yet the gods are named in almost every speech. Diomedes and Sthenelus will fight on, “for we came here with a god” (9.49). The gifts depend on the gods' granting the sack of Priam's city (9.136 = 9.278); Odysseus fears that the gods may fulfill Hector's threats (9.244–245); Achilles will sail home “if the gods keep me safe” (9.393), after sacrifice “to Zeus and all the gods” (9.357), since Zeus has held his hand over Troy (9.419–420). Ajax says that the gods put the unrelenting heart in Achilles (9.636–637), and Diomedes that he will fight again when his heart bids him “and a god rouses him” (9.703). The gods are the cause men name for what they cannot control, in others and in themselves.

**Like a god.** The measure of the honor offered to Achilles is the honor of gods. The seven cities will honor him “like a god” (9.155 = 9.297), and so will the army (9.302–303); Phoenix promises that the Achaeans will honor him “as equal to a god” (9.603), and twice calls him θεοῖς ἐπιείκελος, “like the gods” (9.485, 9.494). Hector, Odysseus says, “pays no honor at all to men or gods” (9.238–239). Hades is the god most hateful to mortals, because he cannot be appeased (9.158–159; see [[hades]] and [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]).

**Gods who can be turned.** Phoenix's argument turns on the likeness of men and gods: the gods have more prowess, honor and strength, and yet sacrifice and prayer turn them when a man has done wrong; so Achilles should be turned too (9.497–501; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The Prayers who follow are Zeus' daughters, and Zeus sends Blind Folly after the man who refuses them (9.502–512; see [[litai]] and [[ate]]).

**Gods in the stories.** Phoenix's own life was shaped by gods below: “the gods fulfilled his curses, Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.456–457), and the gods never gave him a son (9.493). Not even a god could make him young again to leave Achilles (9.445–446). In the story of Meleager the other gods feasted on hecatombs while Artemis alone was forgotten, and her anger brought the boar and the war (9.533–549; see [[artemis]]); the elders sent “the best priests of the gods” to Meleager (9.575); Idas took up his bow against Apollo for his bride (9.558–560; see [[idas-and-apollo]] and [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]); and Althaea's curse was heard by the Erinys (9.571–572; see [[erinyes]]). Phoenix asks that no god turn Achilles toward Meleager's choice (9.600–601).
