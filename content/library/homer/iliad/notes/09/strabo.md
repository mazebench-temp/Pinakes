---
summary: Strabo reads Book 9 as a geographer and as a reader of speeches. He counts the Prayers among Odysseus' displays of eloquence, puts the seven cities in Messenia, then part of Menelaus' Laconia, argues over Phoenix's homeland and his Dolopians, takes Pytho's stone threshold for a treasury, and finds on the plain of Troy the oak where Hector once waited for Achilles.
---

- **The Prayers.** Odysseus shows the orator's wisdom “in the Testing, in the Prayers, in the Embassy” (*Geography* 1.2.5): [[title-of-book-9]].
- **The seven cities.** Strabo places them on the Messenian Gulf and the gulf beside it named for Asine, names the later towns identified with them, and explains how Agamemnon could offer them: in the time of the Trojan war Messenia belonged to Menelaus' Laconia, and Agamemnon would not have promised towns that belonged neither to himself nor to his brother (8.4.1, 8.4.3–5). “All lie near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos” he takes as “near the Pylian sea” (*Geography* 8.3.22). See [[the-seven-cities]].
- **Achaean Argos.** “If we come to Achaean Argos” (9.141) means the Peloponnese, which the epithet distinguishes from Pelasgian Argos in Thessaly (8.6.5; see [[argos]]).
- **Hellas and Phthia.** Homer seems to make Phthia and Hellas two places, though he does not say whether cities or regions, and later writers disputed where Hellas lay (9.5.6, on 2.683, 9.395 and 9.478–479; see [[hellas]] and [[phthia]]). “Throughout Hellas and Phthia” and “the Curetes and the Aetolians” serve Strabo as instances of the poetic habit of naming a part beside its whole (8.3.8).
- **Phoenix.** The Dolopians lived at the edge of Phthia under Peleus (9.484; 9.5.11). Homer never brings a Dolopian force into the fighting, but Pindar made Phoenix lead them to Troy, and Strabo holds that their presence is to be understood (9.5.5): [[phoenix-the-model-teacher]] and [[dolopians]]. Demetrius of Scepsis read “Ormenium” for “Hellas” at 9.447, and Crates made Phoenix a Phocian (9.5.18): [[hellas-or-ormenium]].
- **Amyntor's curse.** Strabo quotes the curse with ἐφέζεσθαι, “that a dear son should never sit upon his knees”, where our text has the causative ἐφέσσεσθαι, “set” (9.455), to show that “on the knees” means what it says, in his argument that the Athena of Homer's Ilium was a seated image (13.1.41, on 6.92; see [[amyntor]] and [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]]).
- **Pytho's treasure.** The temple at Delphi was once rich, “as Homer says”, and he quotes the stone threshold of the Archer (9.404–405). Some, he reports, took ἀφήτωρ to mean “treasure” and the Archer's threshold to be a treasury under the ground, and said that when Onomarchus' men tried to dig it up by night, great earthquakes drove them off (9.3.8; see [[pytho]] and [[aphetor]]).
- **The Curetes.** Strabo begins his account of the Curetes with Homer, argues that they were Aetolians of Pleuron, and explains the war of the sons of Thestius against Oeneus and Meleager as a dispute over land, though the poet, following the story of the boar, makes it a fight over the boar's head and hide (*Geography* 10.3.1, 10.3.6, on 9.529–549; see [[curetes]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]).
- **Achilles' raids.** In “twelve cities of men I have sacked with my ships, and eleven, I say, on foot throughout deep-soiled Troy” (9.328–329), Troy is the whole mainland that Achilles ravaged around the city, with Thebe, Lyrnessus, Pedasus and Lesbos itself (13.1.7; see [[achilles-raids]] and [[lesbos]]).
- **The oak.** The oak where Hector waited for Achilles “alone” (9.352–355) stood a little below the fig tree, near the village where the old Ilium was thought to have been and far from the Ilium of Strabo's day, one of his arguments that the later city was not Homer's (13.1.35; see [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).
- **Egyptian Thebes.** Thebes, “now called Diospolis”, is Homer's city of the hundred gates, and Strabo quotes 9.383–384 and 9.381–382 for its size and wealth (17.1.46; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]).
- **The Hellespont.** Some stretched the name Hellespont over the Aegean as far as the Thermaic Gulf, calling Homer to witness with Achilles' ships “sailing on the fish-filled Hellespont” (9.359–361); Strabo refutes them from other verses, in which the Hellespont bounds the Thracians of Aenus (7, fr. 58, on 2.845 and 4.519–520; see [[hellespont]]).
