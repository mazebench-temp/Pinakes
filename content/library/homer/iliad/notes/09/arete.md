summary: Phoenix names ἀρετή among the things in which the gods surpass men, “prowess and honor and strength”, and argues that if gods who have more of all three can be turned, so can Achilles.
grc: [ἀρετὴ]
passages:
  - 9.496-501 | Even the gods can be turned
---

The word occurs once in the book, in the hinge of Phoenix's plea:

{{quote:cb503f95-7816-5b28-b9f3-3e26da2743f1}}

ἀρετὴ τιμή τε βίη τε, “prowess and honor and strength”, are the three things a hero claims, and Phoenix gives the gods the greater share of each. The argument runs from the greater to the less. Gods are stronger and more honored than any man, and still burnt offerings, vows and libations turn them when a man has done wrong; Achilles, who has less of all three, should not have “a pitiless heart” (9.496–497; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). ἀρετή here is the excellence that makes a being great, not a moral virtue: the gods have more of it, and that is why their yielding counts. Achilles' prowess is the thing the Achaeans need, and the word is not used of it in this book; the envoys speak instead of his courage (ἀλκή, 9.231) and of his anger (see [[alke]] and [[cholos]]). Nestor names it two books later: Achilles “will have the good of his prowess alone”, and will weep for it when the army has perished (11.762–764).

When Plato's Adeimantus quotes the passage, his text leaves this line out (*Republic* 2.364d–e; see [[litai]]).
