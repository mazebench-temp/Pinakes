summary: Both of the Iliad's κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men”, are in Book 9. Achilles sings them alone to his lyre, and Phoenix draws on them to persuade him; between the two, Achilles weighs his own κλέος ἄφθιτον, “imperishable fame”, against a long life at home.

**The song.** The envoys find Achilles “delighting his heart with a clear-toned lyre”, and “he sang the famous deeds of men” (κλέα ἀνδρῶν, 9.186–189). κλέα is the plural of κλέος: the deeds that are told, and the telling that keeps them. The Odyssey uses the phrase for the song the Muse moves Demodocus to sing (*Odyssey* 8.73). The man who has withdrawn from the fighting, where κλέος is won, sings the κλέος of others to an audience of one, Patroclus, who sits waiting in silence (9.190–191; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[lyre]]).

**The choice.** Later the same night Achilles tells the envoys what his own fame will cost (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]], where the passage and the phrase κλέος ἄφθιτον are discussed). In the prophecy κλέος is weighed twice, against different things. If he stays, it is set against homecoming: “my homecoming is lost, but my fame will be imperishable” (9.413). If he goes home, it is set against a long life: “my noble fame is lost, but my life will be long” (9.415; see [[nostos]]). Fame is what a man has in place of a return and in place of years. It is also the one thing the embassy cannot offer him. Agamemnon offers goods, and Odysseus the glory of killing Hector in the army's eyes (κῦδος, 9.303; see [[kydos]]), but κλέος is what will be said of him, and it is won by staying and dying.

Here κλέα are what one has heard (ἐπευθόμεθα), the report that the poet himself relies on, since mortals “hear only the report” (2.486; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). Phoenix draws a rule from them, that the heroes of old “could be won with gifts and swayed by words”, and the story of Meleager is his proof (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). The song Achilles sings for himself and the tale Phoenix tells to move him are made of the same matter.

**Disgrace.** The negative opens the book. Agamemnon repeats that Zeus bids him go back to Argos δυσκλέα, “in disgrace” (9.22 = 2.115), and this time he proposes to do it.
