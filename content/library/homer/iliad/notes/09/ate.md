Book 9 uses the word and its verb ἀάω (“blind”) more than any other book but the nineteenth, and in three settings: a king's confession, an old man's allegory, and a story of the generation before Troy.

**In earnest.** In the night assembly Agamemnon stands weeping “like a spring of dark water” and repeats the speech with which he tested the army in Book 2: “Zeus, son of Cronus, has bound me fast in heavy blind folly” (9.18 = 2.111; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). One word is changed, τότε, “then”, for πρίν, “once” (9.19), and this time he means it. The bond is still Zeus' doing, and the speech still ends in flight (9.26–28).

**The admission.** In the council Nestor tells him to his face that he took Achilles' prize against advice (9.104–111). Agamemnon does not argue:

{{quote:d98bc87a-13d0-5b9a-89dc-6580e77a515c}}

ἐμὰς ἄτας, “my blind folly”, is plural, his acts of folly. The plural comes elsewhere in Homer only in Dolon's excuse, “With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense” (ἄτῃσι, 10.391), and in Achilles' cry when the quarrel is over, “Father Zeus, truly you give men great blind folly” (μεγάλας ἄτας, 19.270). Here Agamemnon names no god. He was blinded “obeying my wretched mind” (φρεσὶ λευγαλέῃσι πιθήσας, 9.119), and the proof is the defeat: Zeus loves Achilles and has beaten down the army (9.116–118; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]). The confession ends in payment, “I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (9.120; see [[apoina]]). In Book 19, before the whole army, he says the same verse (19.138), but the line before it changes to “but since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits” (19.137), and he declares that Zeus, Moira and the Erinys cast savage ἄτη into his mind (19.86–89). In Homer's terms the two accounts do not exclude each other: the folly can be a god's work and still be the man's to pay for (see the article above).

Achilles hears none of this; Odysseus does not report it. He reaches the same verdict in his own words: “Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (ἐκ γάρ εὑ φρένας εἵλετο, 9.377; see [[phrenes]]), and so let him “go to his ruin at his ease”. In Book 1 he asked that Agamemnon might learn his ἄτη (1.412). Now Agamemnon has learned it, and Achilles refuses the amends that follow from it.

**The Prayers.** Phoenix gives blind folly a body and a gait:

{{quote:c2831218-dcb1-500c-9bd7-7a2efa3feca8}}

The Greek prints ἄτη in lower case, and the translation capitalizes “Blind Folly” where she acts as a person (see [[litai]], where the allegory is discussed). In a few lines the word passes through both its senses. Blind Folly runs ahead “doing harm to men” (9.507): she is the blindness that makes a man give offense. At the end the Prayers ask Zeus that she follow the man who refuses them, “so that he may be harmed and pay the price” (ἵνα βλαφθεὶς ἀποτίσῃ, 9.512; see [[poine]]): she is the ruin that punishes the refusal. Phoenix gives the first to Agamemnon, whose folly the gifts and the envoys now come to heal, and holds the second over Achilles (9.513–523).

Achilles refuses. He sends Patroclus out in his place, and when Apollo strikes Patroclus, “blind folly seized his mind” (τὸν δʼ ἄτη φρένας εἷλε, 16.805). Many readers have heard Phoenix's warning fulfilled in that death; the poem does not say so.

**Oeneus.** In the story that follows, the first fault is a forgetting. Oeneus made no harvest offering to Artemis: “Either he forgot, or did not think of it; and he was greatly blinded in his heart” (9.537). The same half-verse, ἀάσατο δὲ μέγα θυμῷ, is used of Agastrophus, whose horses were held too far off for him to escape when Diomedes wounded him (11.338–342). Neglect, not insolence, brought the boar and the war on Calydon (see [[oeneus]], [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Like the quarrel at Troy, the Calydonian war begins in blind folly.
