**The dearest.** Achilles springs up at the sight of the envoys: “Welcome, both of you! Surely you come as friends; surely the need is great, you who are to me, angry as I am, the dearest of the Achaeans” (9.197–198), and to Patroclus: “these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof” (9.204). The embassy is built on the claim. Phoenix tells him that Agamemnon has chosen the men “who to you yourself are dearest of the Argives” (9.521–522), and Ajax ends by saying that they are eager above all to be “the nearest and dearest to you, of all the Achaeans” (κήδιστοί τʼ ἔμεναι καὶ φίλτατοι, 9.642). In Phoenix's story Meleager is begged in turn by the elders, the priests, his father, his sisters and his mother, and then by his companions, “who were the most loyal and dearest to him of all” (9.585–586); only his wife comes after them (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). The envoys stand where the companions stand in the story. Achilles answers Ajax that his heart agrees with him, but his anger does not (9.645–648).

**Love of wives.** Achilles uses the verb for his own loss: “Are they the only ones of mortal men who love their wives, the sons of Atreus? For any man who is good and sensible loves his own and cares for her, as I too loved her from my heart, though she was won by my spear” (9.340–343). φιλέω is the verb of a husband's love, and he gives it to his love for a captive (see [[briseis]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**A father's love.** Phoenix tells how Peleus “loved me as a father loves his son, his only son, his darling, heir to great possessions” (9.481–482; see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]]), and how he in turn raised Achilles, “loving you from my heart” (9.486). The love passed from Peleus to Phoenix and from Phoenix to Peleus' son (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Whom to love.** Achilles makes love a matter of sides. Agamemnon admitted that the man “whom Zeus loves in his heart” is worth many people (9.116–117; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]). Achilles tells Phoenix not to plead for Agamemnon:

{{quote:b2f94cac-eb8f-529b-a118-a5ff19f87ddb}}

τὸν φιλέειν, “to love him”, and μοι … φιλέοντι, “to me, who love you”: Phoenix cannot love both. “It is good for you to vex with me the one who vexes me” (9.615) states plainly the rule that friends share their friends and their enemies.
