summary: In the night after his defeat Agamemnon proposes in earnest the flight he once proposed as a test, and Diomedes throws the king's old reproach back at him. At the elders' feast, charged by Nestor, he admits his blind folly and names boundless gifts for Achilles, with a demand that Achilles submit; he hears the refusal in silence.

**Tears.** The army is held by “heaven-sent Panic” (9.2), and its king is “stricken at heart with great grief” (9.9). He has the heralds summon each man by name, without shouting, and toils among the foremost himself (9.10–12; see [[heralds]] and [[assembly]]).

{{quote:fdbf2d94-5382-55d5-bfcb-835ec9315033}}

The simile returns for Patroclus weeping before Achilles (16.3–4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]] and [[tears]]).

**Flight in earnest.** He speaks the beginning and the end of the speech with which he tested the army in Book 2: Zeus has bound him in heavy blind folly and bids him “go back to Argos in disgrace”; “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (9.18–28 = 2.111–118, 2.139–141; see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[peira]]). Then it was a pretense, and the army ran for the ships. Now it is meant, and no one moves (9.29–30; see [[silence]]).

**Diomedes.** “Son of Atreus, I will fight with you first, for your folly” (9.32). He recalls the king's reproach of Book 4 (9.34–36; 4.370–400) and turns the king's own words against him. To Achilles in Book 1 the king said, “Run away, then, if your heart so urges you” (1.173); now Diomedes tells him, “If your own heart is so eager to go home, go” (9.42–43). Of Zeus Agamemnon had said, “his power is the greatest” (9.25); Zeus gave the king the scepter, Diomedes answers, “but courage he did not give you, and that is the greatest power” (9.39). Agamemnon makes no reply (see [[diomedes]]).

**Nestor's charge.** Nestor calls him “the most kingly” (βασιλεύτατος) and bids him feast the elders, since his huts are full of wine from Thrace (9.69–73; see [[elders]], [[feast]] and [[thrace]]). At the feast the old man begins as a hymn begins with its god, “with you I shall end, and with you I shall begin”: Zeus has put into his hands “the scepter and the ordinances”, so he above all must listen as well as speak (9.96–102; see [[scepter]] and [[themis]]). Then comes the charge: he took Briseis against Nestor's advice and dishonored “the best of men, whom the immortals themselves honored” (9.106–111; see [[nestor]] and [[taking-of-briseis]]).

**The admission.**

{{quote:d98bc87a-13d0-5b9a-89dc-6580e77a515c}}

In the quarrel he said that others would honor him, “and above all Zeus the counselor” (1.175); now he grants that Zeus has honored Achilles (9.117–118; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]). The same night that he blamed Zeus' ἄτη for the war (9.18), he owns his own (see [[ate]]). In Book 19 he will say the last two verses again, with Zeus taking away his wits in place of his “wretched mind” (19.137–138; 9.119–120), which is the explanation Achilles gives tonight (9.377).

**The gifts.** The “boundless ransom” fills thirty-six verses: treasure, horses, seven women of Lesbos and Briseis at once; a shipload of Troy's spoil; and at home a daughter, a dowry and seven cities (9.121–156; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]], [[orestes]] and [[the-seven-cities]]). The seven women he “chose out for myself” when Achilles took Lesbos (9.128–130) are his share of Achilles' own spoil, which bears out Achilles' complaint that the king kept most of what he won (9.330–333). It is far more than the “three times as many splendid gifts” Athena promised Achilles in the quarrel (1.213; see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]). When they are given at last, after Patroclus' death, only the gifts at hand are carried into the assembly; the spoil, the daughter and the cities are not mentioned again (19.238–281). The offer ends in a demand:

{{quote:15c9ce5d-0fa6-5dd9-9904-e49700fd7a25}}

Hades is “the most hateful” of the gods (ἔχθιστος), the word with which Agamemnon told Achilles in Book 1 that he was the most hateful of the kings (1.176; see [[hades]]). With βασιλεύτερος, “more kingly”, he makes a claim of the title Nestor gave him, βασιλεύτατος, “the most kingly” (9.69). In Book 1 he took the prize so that Achilles might learn “how much greater I am than you” (1.185–186). Nestor passes over the demand (9.164), Odysseus leaves it out (9.300–306; see [[message-repeated]]), and Achilles answers it anyway: let Agamemnon choose a son-in-law “who is more kingly” (9.392; see [[kings]]). Agamemnon does not go to the hut himself, and there his name is spoken with contempt (9.330–336, 9.372–378; see [[achilles]]).

**The silence.** When the envoys return, he asks first: “is he willing to ward off the consuming fire from the ships, or has he refused?” (9.674–675). Odysseus reports the refusal (9.677–692), and the scene of the evening assembly is played again, mostly in its own verses: silence, a long grief, and Diomedes (9.693–696; 9.29–31). “I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus” (9.698), he says, and at dawn the king must “fight yourself among the foremost” (9.709). Agamemnon does not answer; the kings approve, pour libations and sleep (9.710–713). In Book 10 sleep does not hold him (10.3–4), and the next morning he is the first to kill (11.91–92).
