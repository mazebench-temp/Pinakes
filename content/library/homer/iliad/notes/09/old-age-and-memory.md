summary: Book 9 belongs to two old men. Nestor steers the assembly and the council, and Phoenix, “the old horse-driving Phoenix”, makes the longest speech in the Iliad out of memory: his own youth, Achilles' childhood, and a deed of the men of old that he remembers “from long ago”.
passages:
  - 9.52-78 | Nestor's counsel
  - 9.434-495 | Phoenix's story
  - 9.524-528 | The famous deeds of the men of old
---

**Nestor.** The old man of the army speaks three times, each time to set the night's course. In the assembly he praises Diomedes, tells him that he might be his son, and claims the elder's right to finish his argument (9.53–62; see [[genee]]); he sets the watch and calls the elders to council (9.65–78). In the council “the old man first of all began to weave a plan”, Nestor, “whose counsel had shown itself best before” (9.93–94), and the plan is the embassy (9.96–113). He chooses the envoys and gives them their instructions, “glancing at each of them, and at Odysseus most of all” (9.162–181; see [[nestor]] and [[boule]]).

**Phoenix.** The second old man is Achilles' own. He is γέρων ἱππηλάτα Φοῖνιξ, “the old horse-driving Phoenix”, in the formula given to Peleus and to Oeneus (9.432, 9.438, 9.581). His speech is built of memory. He remembers the day Peleus sent him with the boy (9.438–443), his own flight from his father in his youth (9.447–484), and Achilles as a child on his knees (9.485–491). Not even a god could tempt him to leave Achilles, if he promised “to scrape away my old age and make me young and in my prime, as I was when I first left Hellas” (9.445–447; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). Then he tells the story of Meleager, “this deed from long ago, not recent at all”, as the old tell the young what the men of old did (9.527–528; see [[memory-and-the-muses]] and [[men-of-today]]). Achilles answers him as a son: “Phoenix, old father” (ἄττα γεραιέ, 9.607; see [[old-father]]), and keeps him for the night (see [[phoenix]]).

**The prize of the old.** Achilles sends the envoys back with his answer to the chiefs, “for that is the prize of the old” (9.422; see [[prize-of-the-old]]). Peleus, old at home, is remembered by both envoys (9.252–259, 9.438–443), and Achilles imagines him finding his son a wife (9.394). Diomedes, whom Nestor called young enough to be his son, has the last word, and the old men let it stand (9.697–711).
