---
summary: Agamemnon repeats in earnest that Zeus “has devised an evil deceit”; Achilles says that Agamemnon has deceived him and will never beguile him again with words. The embassy itself is suspected of guile, and Odysseus, who leaves Agamemnon's last demand out of the offer, is the man Achilles is facing.
grc: [ἀπάτησε, ἐξαπατήσειν, ἐξαπάφοιτʼ]
en: [deceived, deceive, beguile]
passages:
  - 9.312-313 | Hateful to me as the gates of Hades
  - 9.344-345 | He deceived me
  - 9.369-377 | Never again will he beguile me
---

**Zeus' deceit.** The night begins with the line Agamemnon spoke in pretense in Book 2: Zeus promised that he would sack Ilios, “but now he has devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην, 9.21 = 2.114). In Book 2 Zeus' deceit was the Dream, and the king's complaint a ruse; now the king believes it (see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[ate]]).

**Agamemnon's deceit.** Achilles uses the verb three times of Agamemnon. “Since he has taken my prize from my hands and deceived me” (ἀπάτησε, 9.344), let him not try me; tell everything openly, “if he still hopes to deceive some other of the Danaans” (ἐξαπατήσειν, 9.371); “for he deceived me and did me wrong; never again will he beguile me with words” (ἀπάτησε καὶ ἤλιτεν … ἐξαπάφοιτʼ ἐπέεσσιν, 9.375–376). What the deceit was, he does not say; the wrong he names is the taking of the prize (see [[geras]]). ἐξαπαφίσκω, “beguile”, is the verb of Hera's plan to deceive Zeus with love (14.160), and the word “with words” is pointed: the man before him is Odysseus, the king's best speaker.

**The envoys' omission.** Near the start of his answer Achilles curses the man “who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.312–313; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]). The audience knows what Achilles does not: Odysseus has just repeated Agamemnon's offer without its last lines, the demand that Achilles “submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.158–161; see [[message-repeated]]). Whether his words are aimed at Odysseus, at Agamemnon, or at no one in particular is debated. Plato's Hippias took them as proof that Achilles was simple and true, and Odysseus wily and false (*Hippias Minor* 364e–365b; see [[truth-and-deception]]).
