**The elders' feast.** Nestor ends the assembly with the order Hector gave the Trojans at nightfall, “But now let us yield to black night and make ready our supper” (9.65–66 = 8.502–503; see [[yield-to-night]]), and the sentries make their fires and take their supper between the ditch and the wall (9.88; see [[night-watch]]). The kings are to eat with the king:

{{quote:479c8e5d-5512-5652-ba27-95e95c8e2200}}

ὑποδεξίη, “the means of entertaining”, occurs only here. In Book 4 Agamemnon spoke of the elders' feast as an honor he grants, “whenever we Achaeans prepare a feast for the elders” (4.343–344); Nestor makes it the king's duty, and the place where counsel is taken: “When many are gathered, you will follow the man who devises the best counsel” (9.74–75; see [[elders]] and [[boule]]). The feast itself takes three verses. Agamemnon sets “a satisfying feast” before them, they put out their hands to it, and when they have put away the desire for drink and food, Nestor begins to weave his plan, in the verses that brought him to his feet at the feast after the duel (9.90–92; 9.92–95 = 7.323–326; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). The embassy is planned at the king's table, and it is sent off with a libation and a drink (9.174–177; see [[libation]] and [[embassy]]).

**The second meal.** In his hut Achilles feeds the envoys before a word is said of their errand, and the meal is one of the fullest accounts in the Iliad of how one is made: the stronger wine, the back of a sheep and of a goat and the chine of a hog on the chopping block, the spits over the embers, the salt, the bread in baskets, and the offering thrown into the fire (9.199–221; see [[feast-type-scene]], [[spits]] and [[sacrifice]]). The heroes do the work themselves, Achilles carving while Automedon holds the meat, Patroclus making the fire and setting out the bread, and Achilles serving the meat (9.209–217; see [[automedon]] and [[patroclus]]). Then Odysseus raises his cup:

{{quote:d3a913c2-ace4-59fd-bf1b-96cf7282d6ba}}

The envoys have eaten twice in one night, and Odysseus begins by saying that they lack nothing. The equal share of the feast is the formula of the feasts of Books 1 and 2, where no heart lacked its due (1.468, 2.431; see [[equal-feast]]); in Odysseus' mouth it is a guest's courtesy to both his hosts, and it is at once put aside, “the business of the lovely feast is not our concern”. The feast is the frame of the request, and the request is war (9.229–231).

**Two feasts remembered.** When Phoenix quarreled with his father, his kinsmen and cousins tried to keep him at home with a feast: “many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns they slaughtered, and many hogs, rich with lard, were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus, and much wine was drunk from the old man's jars” (9.466–469), and they watched him for nine nights (see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[amyntor]]). The verses return at Patroclus' funeral, for the hogs of the feast that Achilles gives the Myrmidons (23.32–33) and for the sheep and cattle flayed before the pyre (23.166). The feast that should have held Phoenix in his father's house did not hold him. He also reminds Achilles that as a child he would not go to a feast or eat in the halls with anyone else, until Phoenix set him on his knees, “and fed you your fill of meat, cutting it up first, and held the wine to your lips” (9.486–489; see [[phoenix]]). The man who was to teach him to speak and to act (9.443) also fed him.

In Phoenix's story of Meleager it is the gods who feast: “the other gods feasted on hecatombs, but to her alone, the daughter of great Zeus, he made no sacrifice” (9.535–536; see [[hecatomb]] and [[artemis]]). The goddess left out of the feast sends the boar.

**Food and wine.** The book ends with Diomedes' order to the kings, “now go to sleep, when you have satisfied your hearts with food and wine; for in that is might and courage” (9.705–706). Odysseus says the second verse to Achilles in Book 19, when he urges that the army eat before it fights (19.160–161; 19.161 = 9.706), and proposes that Agamemnon make amends to Achilles “with a rich feast in his huts” (19.179–180). Achilles answers that he would send the Achaeans to fight fasting and feast at sunset, when the insult is avenged (19.205–208), and he will not eat himself (19.305–308). In Book 9 he feasts the envoys and refuses the gifts; in Book 19 he accepts the gifts and refuses the feast.
