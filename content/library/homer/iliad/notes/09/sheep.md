---
summary: Sheep in Book 9 are meat, wealth and a measure of what can be replaced. Achilles carves the back of a sheep for the envoys, Phoenix's kinsmen slaughter fat sheep to keep him at home, the men of the seven cities are rich in flocks, and Achilles says that cattle and fat sheep can be had by raiding but a man's life cannot.
grc: ["=ὄϊος"]
passages:
  - 9.206-208 | The meal for the envoys
  - 9.406-409 | What can be raided
---

**Meat.** On the chopping block of Achilles' hut lie “the back of a sheep and of a fat goat, and the chine of a fat hog, rich with lard” (νῶτον … ὄϊος καὶ πίονος αἰγός, 9.207–208; see [[goats]], [[boar]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). ὄϊος is the genitive of ὄις. The back and the chine are the best cuts, a host's honor to his guests, as Agamemnon honored Ajax with the long chine after his duel (7.321–322). In Phoenix's story the kinsmen who beg him to stay at home slaughter “many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” and many hogs (9.466–467; see [[fat-sheep]] and [[cattle]]). The verses return for the funeral feast of Patroclus, where oxen, sheep, goats and white-tusked hogs are slaughtered (23.30–33).

**Wealth.** The men of the seven cities that Agamemnon offers are πολύρρηνες πολυβοῦται, “rich in flocks and rich in cattle” (9.154 = 9.296; see [[rich-in-flocks]], [[rich-in-cattle]] and [[the-seven-cities]]); the first word is formed from ῥήν, “sheep”. Phoenix comes to “deep-soiled Phthia, mother of flocks” (9.479; see [[mother-of-flocks]]). The bed made up for him in Achilles' hut is spread with κώεα, “fleeces”, a rug and fine linen (9.661).

**What can be raided.** Achilles measures his life against the flocks: “For cattle and fat sheep can be had by raiding, and tripods can be won, and the tawny heads of horses, but a man's soul cannot come back again” (9.406–408; see [[achilles-raids]] and [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]]). ἴφια μῆλα, “fat sheep”, are the small livestock of the farm and the plunder of a raid. Achilles killed Andromache's seven brothers among their cattle and white sheep (6.421–424; see [[sack-of-thebe]]).
