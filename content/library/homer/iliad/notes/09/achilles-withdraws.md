summary: Book 9 shows the withdrawal from inside Achilles' hut for the first time. The envoys find him singing the famous deeds of men to the lyre, and his answers turn the withdrawal into a plan to sail home, then into a promise to stay but not to fight until Hector reaches his own ships.

**Inside the hut.** Book 1 left Achilles by his ships, “never going to the assembly, where men win glory, nor ever to war; but he wasted away his own heart, staying where he was, and he longed for the war cry and for battle” (1.490–492). The Catalogue and Ajax's challenge in Book 7 saw the withdrawal from outside, through his idle men and in the words “nursing his wrath against Agamemnon” (2.771–779, 7.229–230). In Book 9 the envoys go in. He is not fighting, but he is singing of fighting, the famous deeds of men, to a lyre from the spoils of Thebe, with Patroclus sitting silent opposite (9.186–191; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[lyre]]). He names the withdrawal himself with the verb of those passages: the Achaeans' plan is not ready to hand, “the one they devised now, while I nurse my wrath apart” (ἐμεῦ ἀπομηνίσαντος, 9.426; compare ἀπομηνίσας, 2.772 = 7.230; see [[menis]]).

**Terms.** The embassy forces him to give the withdrawal terms. To Odysseus he says that he has no wish to fight Hector and will sail home at dawn (9.356–363). To Phoenix he says that at dawn they will consider whether to go home or to stay (9.618–619). To Ajax he says that he will not think of war until Hector reaches the huts and ships of the Myrmidons (9.650–655). The last is the one he keeps (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). In Book 11 he watches the rout from the stern of his ship (11.599–601), and in Book 16, when the Trojans are at the ships, he recalls the limit he set and sends Patroclus out in his armor (16.60–100). He comes back himself only in Book 18, for Patroclus, and gives up his anger in Book 19 (19.56–73; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]).
