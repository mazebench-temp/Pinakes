**Without the gesture.** The pattern's second step is missing. The envoys plead as guests, seated and fed, and no one clasps Achilles' knees, though the errand is called an entreaty (λίσσεσθαι, 9.520, 9.698; see [[supplication]] and [[visit-scene]]). In Book 11 Achilles tells Patroclus that “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.609–610; see [[embassy]]).

**Phoenix's suppliants.** The speech of the old tutor is made of supplications. His mother “kept begging me by my knees” (λισσέσκετο γούνων) to lie with the concubine (9.451), and his kinsmen begged him to stay (9.465; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). Men turn even the gods by entreaty (λισσόμενοι, 9.501), and the Prayers, the daughters of Zeus, are suppliants made into goddesses: whoever respects them is helped, and against whoever refuses them they go to Zeus and pray (9.502–512; see [[litai]] and [[prayer]]).

**Meleager.** The story Phoenix tells is a series of supplications, each by people closer to the man who refuses (9.574–596; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]):

1. The elders of the Aetolians beg him and send the best priests of the gods, promising a great gift, a domain of fifty acres (9.574–580; see [[priesthood]]).
2. His father Oeneus “entreated him again and again, standing on the threshold of the high-roofed chamber, shaking the jointed doors, imploring his son” (γουνούμενος, 9.581–583; see [[oeneus]] and [[threshold]]).
3. His sisters and his mother beg him, “but he refused all the more” (9.584–585).
4. His companions, “the most loyal and dearest to him of all” (9.585–587).
5. Last, when the enemy are on the towers, his wife begs him, weeping, and tells him what happens to the people of a captured city, and he goes out to fight (9.588–596; see [[cleopatra]] and [[women-and-war]]).

J. T. Kakridis called the order “the ascending scale of affection” (*Homeric Researches*, 1949). The envoys come to Achilles in an order of their own, the comrade Odysseus, the old father Phoenix and the friend Ajax, and the plea he grants, in part, is Patroclus' in Book 16 (16.1–100). Oeneus begging at his son's door turns the usual order of supplication around, the father imploring the son; in the last book another old father begs, when Priam kisses the hands of the man who killed his son (24.477–479).
