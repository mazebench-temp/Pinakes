---
summary: The envoys argue from foresight. Odysseus warns that there is no cure for an evil once it is done, and Phoenix that it will be worse to defend the ships when they are already burning; Achilles answers with the one foresight he has been given, his mother's prophecy of two fates.
passages:
  - 9.247-251 | No cure once the evil is done
  - 9.600-605 | While the gifts are offered
---

**Before it is too late.** Odysseus' appeal is a call to look ahead: “To you yourself there will be grief hereafter, and there is no means to find a cure for an evil once it is done; but long before that consider how you will ward off the evil day from the Danaans” (9.249–251; see [[no-cure-once-done]]). Phoenix ends his story with the same warning, drawn from Meleager, who waited until the city was burning: “it would be worse to defend the ships when they are already burning” (9.601–602). Both speak of a future that the audience knows will come: Achilles will wait until the fire reaches the ships, and the grief that comes “hereafter” will be for Patroclus (16.112–129, 18.22; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**What Achilles foresees.** Achilles answers with knowledge of his own. His mother has told him of two fates (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[prophecy]]), and he speaks of the future as if he chose it freely: he will sail at dawn, or decide at dawn, or fight when Hector reaches his ships (9.356–363, 9.618–619, 9.650–655). He foresees Hector checked at his own hut and ship (9.654–655). What he does not foresee is the cost.

**Before and after.** In Book 1 Achilles said that Agamemnon could not look “before and after” (1.343; see [[before-and-after]]). In Book 9 the king looks back and admits his folly (9.115–120), and the envoys ask Achilles to look forward.
