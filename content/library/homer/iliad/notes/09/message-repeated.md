summary: Odysseus repeats Agamemnon's list of gifts to Achilles almost line for line, changing the persons and twice rebuilding a verse, but he leaves out the king's confession before it and his demand for submission after it (9.122–161, 9.262–306). Back in Agamemnon's hut he reports only Achilles' first answer, partly in Achilles' own words (9.677–692).

**The offer given.** Agamemnon names the gifts “before you all” to the elders in his hut (9.121), after a confession: “I was blinded; I do not deny it myself” (9.116; see [[ate]]). The list runs from what is “at hand at once” to the spoil of Troy and the gifts of a homecoming, a daughter and seven cities (9.122–156; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]), and it ends with a demand:

**The offer delivered.** Odysseus brings in the list as a report, “I will recount to you all the gifts that Agamemnon promised you in his huts” (9.262–263), and then gives it in Agamemnon's own thirty-six verses (9.264–299 = 9.122–157). Most of the changes are those that delivery requires. “I will give” becomes “he will give” (δώσω, δώσει, 9.128 and 9.270, 9.149 and 9.291), “when he himself took Lesbos” becomes “when you yourself took” (9.129, 9.271), and “I will swear a great oath” becomes “he will swear” (9.132, 9.274). Agamemnon's orders in the third person, “let him load his ship … let him choose … let him take” (νηησάσθω, ἑλέσθω, ἀγέσθω), become infinitives spoken to Achilles, “you may load … choose … take” (9.137 and 9.279, 9.139 and 9.281, 9.146 and 9.288). The text prints αἴ κε, “if”, in Agamemnon's verse on the twenty Trojan women and αἵ κε, “who”, in Odysseus', with the same sense (9.140, 9.282).

Two verses are rebuilt. Agamemnon's “as the prizes these single-hoofed horses have carried off for me” becomes “as the prizes Agamemnon's horses have won with their feet” (9.127, 9.269), so that the giver is named. And the oath that Briseis' bed is untouched, sworn “as is the custom of mankind, of men and women”, becomes “as is the custom, my lord, of men and of women” (9.134, 9.276). Odysseus calls Achilles ἄναξ, “lord”, in the half-verse with which Diomedes claimed his right to speak against the king (ἣ θέμις ἐστὶν ἄναξ, 9.33), and in Book 19, when he proposes that Agamemnon swear the oath before the army, he says his own version of the verse to Achilles again (19.175–177; see [[oath]]).

**What he leaves out.** Odysseus stops where the list stops. The confession before it is not passed on, and neither is the demand after it, “Let him be tamed … and let him submit to me” (9.158–161). In its place he adds an appeal of his own: if Agamemnon and his gifts are hateful to Achilles, let him pity the other Achaeans, who will honor him like a god, and win glory by killing Hector (9.300–306). Achilles answers the word he did not hear. Let Agamemnon choose another of the Achaeans for his daughter, “one who befits him and who is more kingly” (βασιλεύτερος, 9.392), the word of 9.160 (see the translation's note on 9.158–161).

**The answer reported.** Achilles sends his answer back in the formula of an exact message: “Tell him everything, just as I charge you, openly” (πάντʼ ἀγορευέμεν ὡς ἐπιτέλλω, 9.369–370), the words with which Zeus told the Dream to say “everything exactly as I command” (2.10). Odysseus' report is short. He sums up in his own words: “that man will not quench his anger, but is filled still more with fury, and rejects you and your gifts” (9.678–679). He passes on three things from the first answer: that Agamemnon must take counsel among the Argives how to save the ships and the army (9.680–681, from 9.346–347 and 9.423–424); that Achilles will drag his ships to the sea at dawn (9.682–683, from 9.356–361); and, in Achilles' own verses with “he said” for “I”, that the others should sail home too, “since you will no longer find the end of steep Ilios” (9.684–687 = 9.417–420), where the “you” now speaks to the kings themselves. He ends with Phoenix, in Achilles' verses with the persons changed (9.690–692 = 9.427–429).

Achilles' insults, his refusal of Agamemnon's daughter and the two fates are not reported, and neither are his later answers: to Phoenix, that at dawn they will consider whether to go or stay (9.618–619), and to Ajax, that he will not think of fighting until Hector reaches the huts and ships of the Myrmidons (9.650–655). Whether Odysseus keeps back the softer answers, or the poem lets the first stand for all, is debated (see the translation's note on 9.356–363). Diomedes takes up what he hears, “let us leave him alone, whether he goes or stays” (9.701–702). When Achilles himself recalls the night in Book 16, he remembers his last answer, not his first: “I said I would not end my wrath until the war cry and the fighting reached my ships” (16.61–63).

**An older message.** Odysseus also passes on the words with which Peleus sent his son to Troy (9.252–258), and Phoenix tells of the same day (9.438–443; see [[retelling]]).
