summary: Book 9 is a book of angry people, Achilles above all, but also Amyntor, the young Phoenix, Artemis, Meleager and Althaea, and every envoy asks Achilles to let his anger go. Phoenix's maxim that anger “swells the mind” even of shrewd men comes back in Achilles' own answer to Ajax, “my heart swells with anger”.

**Let it go.** The offer is made conditional on the anger's ending. Agamemnon will give the gifts “if he ceased from his anger” (9.157), and Odysseus says it twice: “stop, and let go your heart-grieving anger; Agamemnon offers you worthy gifts, if you cease from your anger” (9.260–261, 9.299). Phoenix grants that until now the anger was blameless (9.523; see [[nemesis]]), and lays down the rule of the heroes of old, who “whenever furious anger came upon one of them” could be won with gifts and words (9.525–526). At the end of the night Agamemnon asks whether anger still holds Achilles' heart, and Odysseus answers with an image of fire: he “will not quench his anger” (σβέσσαι χόλον, 9.678), but is filled with fury the more (see [[menos]]).

**Angry people.** In the assembly Diomedes tells the king not to be angry at his rebuke, since it is his right in the assembly (μή τι χολωθῇς, 9.33). Nestor recalls the day Agamemnon took Briseis from the hut of Achilles “in his anger” (9.107). Phoenix's father was “enraged” over his concubine (περιχώσατο, 9.449), the son could not stay in the halls while his father was angry (9.463), and in the four lines that the manuscripts lack Phoenix himself planned to kill him until “one of the immortals checked my anger” (παῦσεν χόλον, 9.459; see the translation's note on 9.458–461). In Book 1 Achilles pondered whether to check his anger (χόλον παύσειεν, 1.192), and Athena came “to stop your fury” (1.207). Artemis is “angry” at Oeneus' neglect and sends the boar (χωσαμένη, χολωσαμένη, 9.534, 9.538), and Meleager is “angered by his mother's curses” (κεχολωμένος, 9.566).

**Meleager's anger.** Phoenix gives anger its own maxim:

{{quote:087153e7-6169-5a94-8f0f-26b8ef19a7a6}}

οἰδάνω, “swell”, is found only twice in Homer. The second time is Achilles' answer to Ajax: “all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart; but my heart swells with anger whenever I remember how he treated me with contempt among the Argives” (οἰδάνεται κραδίη χόλῳ, 9.645–647). The maxim that Phoenix told of Meleager comes true in Achilles' own words, later the same night. Meleager lies by his wife “brooding on his heart-grieving anger” (χόλον θυμαλγέα πέσσων, 9.565); Apollo said the same of Achilles in Book 4, that “by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (χόλον θυμαλγέα πέσσει, 4.513). πέσσω is “ripen, digest”; its compound is the verb of Calchas' king, who “swallows down his anger for the day” (καταπέψῃ, 1.81). The anger of Meleager and the anger of Achilles are described in one phrase, and the story is fitted to its listener (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]).
