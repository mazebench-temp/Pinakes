---
summary: Book 9 is full of counting: seven captains with a hundred men each, gifts in sevens and tens and twenties, twelve cities sacked by sea and eleven by land, nine nights of guard and an escape on the tenth. Achilles answers the counted gifts with numbers that cannot be reached, ten and twenty times as much, as much as the sand and the dust.
passages:
  - 9.121-157 | Agamemnon's offer
  - 9.379-387 | Not for all the wealth of Orchomenus and Thebes
---

**The night's numbers.** The watch is set in sevens and hundreds: “Seven were the leaders of the sentries, and with each a hundred young men marched” (9.85–86; see [[night-watch]]). Agamemnon's gifts are counted in the poem's typical numbers: seven tripods, ten talents of gold, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven women of Lesbos, twenty Trojan women, one of three daughters, seven cities (9.122–153; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Seven is the number of the gifts in persons and places, and Ajax uses it as his argument: one girl was taken, “and now we offer you seven, by far the best” (9.637–638). In Book 19 the gifts are counted out as promised, seven tripods, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven women “and the eighth, Briseis”, ten talents of gold weighed by Odysseus (19.243–248).

**Counting a career.** Achilles answers with his own account: “Twelve cities of men I have sacked with my ships, and eleven, I say, on foot throughout deep-soiled Troy” (9.328–329; see [[achilles-raids]]). On a journey home, Phthia is three days away (9.363).

**Beyond counting.** Then he leaves numbers behind. Not ten times or twenty times as much, not what comes in to Orchomenus or to Thebes in Egypt, “which has a hundred gates, and through each two hundred men march out with horses and chariots”, not as much as the sand and the dust (9.379–385; see [[thebes-in-egypt]] and [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]). The count grows until it has no end, and the point is that no count will do.

**Nine and ten.** Phoenix's escape follows the poem's favorite pattern. His kinsmen guarded him for nine nights, “but when the tenth murky night came upon me”, he broke out (9.470–476). The Aetolians offer Meleager a domain “of fifty acres, half of it vineyard, and half bare plowland” (9.579–580).
