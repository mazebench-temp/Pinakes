**A mind that misleads.** Agamemnon gives his own φρένες the blame: he was blinded “obeying my wretched mind” (φρεσὶ λευγαλέῃσι πιθήσας, 9.119; see [[ate]]). Achilles, who does not hear the admission, puts it another way: “Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (ἐκ γάρ εὑ φρένας εἵλετο μητίετα Ζεύς, 9.377). The narrator said the same of Glaucus when he gave gold for bronze (φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, 6.234), and Agamemnon will say it of himself in Book 19, in the verse that replaces 9.119: “but since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits” (19.137). Phoenix's maxim about anger puts the danger in the same organ: anger swells “the mind in the breasts of others too, even of men who think shrewdly” (πύκα περ φρονεόντων, 9.554; see [[cholos]]).

**The great mind.** The envoys walk along the shore praying to Poseidon “that they might easily win over the great mind of the grandson of Aeacus” (μεγάλας φρένας, 9.184; see [[peitho]]). They find him “delighting his heart” with the lyre (φρένα τερπόμενον, 9.186). Odysseus fears Hector's threats κατὰ φρένα, “at heart” (9.244), and Phoenix asks whether homecoming is what Achilles turns over “in your mind” (9.434). In his answer to Phoenix, Achilles tells him to lay his words to heart (ἐνὶ φρεσὶ βάλλεο σῇσι, 9.611; see [[lay-it-to-heart]]).

**Hidden in the mind.** Near the start of his answer to Odysseus, Achilles speaks the line that Plato's Hippias took for the measure of his character: “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (ἐνὶ φρεσίν, 9.312–313; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]). The φρένες are where a man keeps what he does not say. To Phoenix Achilles says, of the honor of Zeus, “I think” (φρονέω, 9.608), and in his first answer he will speak “as I mean it” (ᾗ περ δὴ φρονέω, 9.310): his mind and his words, he claims, are the same.
