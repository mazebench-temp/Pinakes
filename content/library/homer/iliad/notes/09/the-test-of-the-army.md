summary: Agamemnon proposes in earnest the flight he proposed as a test in Book 2. Weeping, he speaks the opening and the close of that speech to an assembly called in silence (9.17–28), and this time no one runs for the ships; after a long silence Diomedes refuses to go.

**The same words.** In Book 2 the flight was a plan. Agamemnon told the elders in council that he would test the army “as is the custom” and order it to flee, and they were to hold the men back (2.72–75). In Book 9 there is no council beforehand and no plan. He has the heralds call each man by name, without shouting, stands up weeping like a spring of dark water, and speaks (9.9–16; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]] and [[agamemnon]]). His speech is the speech of Book 2 cut down to its beginning and its end. Zeus has bound him in blind folly and devised an evil deceit, Zeus who has brought down the heights of many cities and will bring down more (9.18–25 = 2.111–118, with τότε, “once”, for πρίν); “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land; for we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy” (9.26–28 = 2.139–141). The twenty verses between, on the shame of failing with so great an army, the nine years, the rotting timbers and the waiting wives (2.119–138), are left out (see the translation's note on 9.17–28). The address is the one Nestor used to the council of Book 2, “Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives” (9.17 = 2.79).

**In earnest.** Nothing marks the speech as a test, and the narrator gives Agamemnon no second purpose; he weeps and groans (9.14–16). In Book 2 his charge that Zeus had devised “an evil deceit” was a pretense that was truer than he knew (2.114; see [[apate]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Now he believes it. The ἄτη, “blind folly”, with which Zeus has bound him, is the same word in both speeches (9.18 = 2.111; see [[ate]]); before the night is over he will use it of his own act toward Achilles (9.115–120).

**The answer.** In Book 2 the army took him at his word and ran for the ships, and Athena and Odysseus had to turn it back (2.142–210). In Book 9 the men fall silent and stay where they are (9.29–31; see [[silence]]). Diomedes answers. Agamemnon may go if he likes, for the road is open and his ships stand by the sea; the other Achaeans will stay until they sack Troy, and if they too go, he and Sthenelus will fight on until they find the end of Ilios, “for we came here with a god” (9.32–49; see [[diomedes]] and [[sthenelus]]). The army shouts its approval (9.50–51). In Book 2 the army's wish to go home had to be overcome. In Book 9 the king wants to go, and a young man, young enough to be Nestor's youngest son (9.57–58), refuses. Nestor, who said nothing about the test in Book 2 (2.79–83), now turns the night toward the embassy (9.52–78; see [[nestor]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).
