summary: Patroclus is the silent host of the embassy. He sits facing Achilles as he sings, mixes the wine, kindles the fire and sets out the bread, burns the offerings for the gods, has a bed made for Phoenix, and sleeps beside Iphis, whom Achilles gave him from the spoils of Scyros.

**The listener.** When the envoys come, Patroclus alone sits facing Achilles in silence, “waiting for the grandson of Aeacus to leave off his singing” (9.190–191), perhaps to take up the song in his turn; the poem does not say. He stands up with Achilles to receive the envoys (9.195).

**The meal.** Achilles calls him by his father's name, Μενοιτίου υἱέ, “son of Menoetius”, and bids him set out a larger mixing bowl and mix the wine stronger (9.202–203; see [[son-of-menoetius]] and [[wine]]). “So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion” (9.205): the verse with which, in Book 1, he brought Briseis out of the hut and gave her to Agamemnon's heralds (1.345). Then the work of the meal is shared. Achilles carves the meat that Automedon holds and threads it on spits, while “the son of Menoetius, a man equal to the gods, made the fire blaze high” (9.211; see [[equal-to-the-gods]] and [[spits]]). When the meat is roasted, Patroclus sets out the bread in baskets and Achilles serves the meat (9.216–217). In the last book the same two verses give the bread to Automedon, when Achilles feeds Priam and Patroclus is dead (24.625–626; see [[automedon]]). At Achilles' bidding Patroclus throws the offerings for the gods into the fire (9.219–220; see [[sacrifice]] and [[feast-type-scene]]).

**The bed for Phoenix.** He never speaks in the book. When Achilles has answered Phoenix, he “nodded to Patroclus with his brows in silence / to spread a thick bed for Phoenix”, a sign for the other envoys to go (9.620–622); once they have gone, Patroclus has the companions and the slave women spread it with fleeces, a rug and fine linen (9.658–661; see [[phoenix]] and [[handmaids]]).

**Iphis.** He sleeps on the other side of the hut, “and beside him too / lay fair-belted Iphis, whom brilliant Achilles gave him / when he took steep Scyros, the citadel of Enyeus” (9.666–668; see [[iphis]], [[scyros]] and [[captive-women]]). Achilles sleeps in the inner part of the hut with Diomede, from Lesbos (see [[diomede]]).

The silence does not last. In Book 11 Achilles sends him to Nestor, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604), and Nestor reminds him of his father's charge, to give Achilles good counsel, since Patroclus is the elder (11.786–789; see [[menoetius]] and [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). In Book 16 he comes to Achilles weeping “like a spring of dark water”, the simile used of Agamemnon's tears at the opening of Book 9 (16.3–4 = 9.14–15; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]), and asks to be sent out in his armor.
