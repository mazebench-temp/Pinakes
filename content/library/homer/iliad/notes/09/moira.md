**The share.** Nestor begins by approving Diomedes: “what you have said is fair and right” (κατὰ μοῖραν, 9.59), the formula of approval for a speech that gives everything its due (see the article above). Achilles uses the noun in its first sense, a portion of what is shared out:

{{quote:ade735d2-6644-5efe-acab-ee138fb6dbd3}}

ἴση μοῖρα, “the same share”, is a complaint about the spoils: the man who stays behind and the man who fights his hardest get alike (9.318). The next lines widen it. They are held in a single honor (see [[time]]), and κάτθανʼ ὁμῶς, “the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much” (9.320). The aorist states what always happens, and the thought slides from the portion of booty to the portion that is death, the sense μοῖρα has when Hector tells Andromache that “no man, I say, has ever escaped his fate” (6.488; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). If death is the same for all, the shares of the living ought to differ, and they do not. The rest of the speech tells why: Achilles brought in the spoils of twenty-three cities, and Agamemnon “would take them, and share out a few, and keep many” (9.328–333; see [[geras]] and [[division-of-spoils]]).

**A share of honor.** μοῖρα belongs with μείρομαι, “receive one's share”. Nestor said in Book 1 that “no ordinary share of honor falls to a scepter-bearing king” (ἔμμορε τιμῆς, 1.278–279), and Poseidon will say that he, Zeus and Hades each received a share of honor when the world was divided in three (15.189). Achilles gives the verb to Phoenix: “Be king equally with me, and take half my honor” (ἥμισυ μείρεο τιμῆς, 9.616). Agamemnon offered seven cities (9.149–156); Achilles offers an equal share of his own kingship to the old man who reared him, and in the same speech claims for himself the share that Zeus allots (Διὸς αἴσῃ, 9.608; see [[aisa]]). Whether he means the offer literally is uncertain, since Peleus had already made Phoenix lord of the Dolopians (9.483–484; see the translation's note on 9.616 and [[phoenix]]).
