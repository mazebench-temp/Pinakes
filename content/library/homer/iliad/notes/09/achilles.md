summary: On the night of the embassy Achilles welcomes Odysseus, Ajax and Phoenix as the dearest of the Achaeans, refuses Agamemnon's gifts, and tells of his two fates. His three answers move from sailing home at dawn, to deciding at dawn, to staying until Hector reaches the ships of the Myrmidons.

**The singer.**

{{quote:c34c5e0a-194e-5ca3-ae31-707e13b059eb}}

The lyre came from the spoils of Eetion's city (see [[lyre]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]), and Achilles is the only hero whom the poem shows singing. κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men”, is what heroic song tells: Phoenix brings in the story of Meleager with the same words (9.524), and in the Odyssey Demodocus sings κλέα ἀνδρῶν, a quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles (Od. 8.73–75; see [[kleos]] and [[song-and-fame]]). The man who has withdrawn from winning fame sings the fame of others.

**The host.** He springs up in amazement (ταφών, 9.193), as he did when Nestor and Odysseus came to Peleus' house to fetch him for the war (11.777; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). The envoys reach him in the verses that brought Agamemnon's heralds to take Briseis (9.182–185 ≈ 1.327–328), and he greets them in the dual; who “the two” are is an old problem (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). This time the greeting is warm, “you who are to me, angry as I am, the dearest of the Achaeans” (9.198). He calls for stronger wine, “for these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof” (9.204), carves the meat that Automedon holds, and has Patroclus burn the offerings for the gods (9.199–220; see [[automedon]], [[patroclus]], [[xenia]] and [[feast-type-scene]]).

**The reply.** His answer to Odysseus begins with a vow of plain speaking: “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man / who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.312–313). He does not say whom he means, Odysseus, who has just left out Agamemnon's demand that he submit (9.158–161; see [[odysseus]]), or Agamemnon, who “deceived me” (9.344, 9.375; see [[truth-and-deception]]). Then the grievance: “the coward and the brave are held in a single honor” (9.319; see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]). He has toiled like a bird that brings every morsel to its chicks (9.323–327; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]), sacked twenty-three cities and handed the spoil to Agamemnon, who kept most of it, and “from me alone of the Achaeans / he has taken mine, and he keeps the wife dear to my heart” (9.328–336; see [[achilles-raids]] and [[briseis]]). While he fought, Hector came out no farther than the oak (9.352–355; see [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]). He will sail at dawn, “on the third day I would reach deep-soiled Phthia” (9.363; see [[phthia]]). Plato's Socrates, awaiting execution, dreams that a woman in white speaks the line to him, and takes it to mean that he will die the day after next (*Crito* 44a–b; see [[plato-socrates-dream]]).

“His gifts are hateful to me, and I honor him at a hair's worth” (9.378). Not the wealth of Orchomenus or of Egyptian Thebes, not as much as the sand and the dust, will move him “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.379–387; see [[thebes-in-egypt]], [[the-sand-and-the-dust]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Let Agamemnon find a son-in-law “who is more kingly” (9.392), the word he used of himself in the demand Odysseus left out (βασιλεύτερος, 9.160). Nothing is worth his life (9.401–409; see [[psyche]]), and his mother has told him why:

{{quote:47e1e08a-eeb7-5938-9759-5a184966aa85}}

See [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]. Adam Parry argued that here Achilles strains against the limits of the heroic language, doubting the bond of honor and reward in words that cannot quite say it (“The Language of Achilles”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 87, 1956); others answer that his words are the common ones, put to a bitter use.

**Three answers.** To Odysseus he says that he will sail at dawn, and that Phoenix may come with him (9.356–363, 9.427–429). Phoenix he calls ἄττα γεραιέ, “old father”. He needs no honor from gifts, since he is honored by the portion of Zeus; Phoenix must not love Agamemnon, but share Achilles' kingship and half his honor, and stay the night. At dawn they will consider “whether to go home to our own or to stay” (9.607–619; see [[aisa]] and [[phoenix]]). To Ajax he grants that all he has said is after his own heart, but the memory of the insult makes it swell with anger (9.644–648; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]):

{{quote:c183e4a7-f4b4-5dfe-9c2a-a3c083aef940}}

He has moved from sailing at dawn, to deciding at dawn, to staying until Hector reaches his own ships, and Odysseus reports only the first (9.677–692; see [[message-repeated]]). In Plato's *Hippias Minor* Socrates uses the change against Hippias, who had praised Achilles as the simplest and truest of men (see [[plato-hippias-minor]]).

**The night.** Achilles sleeps in the inner part of the hut beside Diomede, a captive from Lesbos, and Patroclus with Iphis, from Scyros (9.663–668; see [[diomede]] and [[iphis]]). Briseis is in Agamemnon's huts; in the last book the verse returns with her beside him (24.675–676).

**Afterward.** The third answer is the one he keeps. In Book 16 he recalls that he would not end his wrath until the war reached his own ships (16.61–63). Yet in Book 11 he says, “now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me” (11.608–610), and in Book 16 he speaks as though no gifts had been offered (16.72–73, 16.84–86). Analysts took these lines as a sign that Book 9 was added late; others read them as Achilles' own view of an offer made on Agamemnon's terms, and the poem remembers the embassy elsewhere (18.448–450, 19.140–141; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).
