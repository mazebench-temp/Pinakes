summary: Book 9 is a night of counsel. Nestor praises Diomedes as best in counsel among men of his age, tells Agamemnon to follow whoever devises the best counsel, and weaves the plan of the embassy in the elders' council; Achilles refuses to join the king “in any counsel” and sends the elders back to find a better plan.

**Good counsel.** Nestor's first words to Diomedes join the two halves of a hero's worth: “you are mighty in war above all others, and in counsel you are the best among all men of your age” (βουλῇ, 9.53–54; see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]). His advice to Agamemnon is a rule for kings: when many are gathered, “you will follow the man who devises the best counsel” (ὅς κεν ἀρίστην βουλὴν βουλεύσῃ, 9.74–75), and the army needs counsel “good and shrewd” (9.76), since the enemy's fires burn near the ships. The narrator gives Nestor the title: “whose counsel had shown itself best before” (9.94). In the council he reminds the king that Zeus gave him the scepter and the ordinances “so that you may take counsel for them” (βουλεύῃσθα, 9.99; see [[themis]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]), and that he must carry out another's good word as well as speak his own (9.100–102). The counsel that follows is the embassy.

**Zeus' counsel.** The book begins with the counsel of a god, in Agamemnon's despair: Zeus “has devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην βουλεύσατο, 9.21 = 2.114; see [[apate]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**No counsel.** Achilles refuses partnership in counsel as well as in war: “I will join him in no counsel, nor in any deed” (οὐδέ τί οἱ βουλὰς συμφράσσομαι, οὐδὲ μὲν ἔργον, 9.374). Counsel and deed are the two things a man gives his king (see [[word-or-deed]]), and Achilles withholds both. He sends the envoys back to the chiefs with his message, “for that is the prize of the old” (9.422), so that “they may think of some other, better plan in their minds” (μῆτιν ἀμείνω, 9.423; see [[prize-of-the-old]] and [[metis]]); the plan they devised, with himself in it, will not work. Odysseus repeats the instruction to Agamemnon: “He bids you yourself take counsel among the Argives” (9.680).

**A plan withheld.** In the four lines that the manuscripts lack, Phoenix tells of a counsel of his own: “Then I planned to kill him with the sharp bronze” (βούλευσα, 9.458), before a god checked his anger (see [[aidos]]). At the end of the night the counsel that prevails is Diomedes': let Achilles go or stay, eat and sleep, and fight at dawn with the king among the foremost (9.697–709). The kings approve, “admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses” (9.710–711), in the verse with which the army applauded him at the start (9.50–51). The young man whom Nestor praised for counsel has the last word.
