summary: Ajax's last argument is the blood-price. A man accepts the penalty even from the killer of his brother or his son, and the killer stays among his people; Achilles grants the argument, but his anger will take no price. In Phoenix's allegory the man who refuses the Prayers is made to “pay the price” himself.

**The custom.** Ajax has given up on Achilles and speaks of him to Odysseus, in the third person, as “hard” and “pitiless”. Then he names the custom that Achilles is breaking:

{{quote:3db83441-e9c1-5c1e-ad29-877877e2235f}}

This is the plainest statement of the custom in Homer. A killing does not have to end in vengeance or in exile. The dead man's kin may accept a ποινή, and once it is paid the killer stays “among his people” (ἐν δήμῳ), while the kinsman's heart and proud spirit “are held in check” (ἐρητύεται). πόλλʼ ἀποτίσας, “having paid much”, is the active of τίνω, “pay”. The alternative is flight: in the Odyssey a man who has killed even one man with few to avenge him “flees, leaving his kin and his native land” (*Odyssey* 23.118–120; see [[exile-and-refuge]]). On the shield of Achilles two men quarrel in the assembly over the ποινή for a man who was killed, the one claiming that he has paid it all, the other refusing to take anything (or, as some read it, denying that he has received anything), and the elders judge between them (18.497–508). Where no price is paid, kin take it in blood. Acamas kills a man “so that the penalty for my brother may not be long unpaid” (14.483–484), and Achilles takes twelve Trojan youths alive “as the penalty for Patroclus” (21.27–28). The word can also name the price that is never paid: a father follows his son's body from the field, “and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.658–659).

**Ajax's argument.** If a brother's or a son's death can be settled with a price, Achilles' grievance can be settled too, since it is “for the sake of a girl, one girl”, and seven are offered (9.637–638; see [[briseis]]). The gods have put in him a heart that is ἄληκτον, “unrelenting” (9.636): a heart that does not stop, as the kinsman's is “held in check”. Achilles concedes the point and keeps his anger: “all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart; but my heart swells with anger whenever I remember” (9.645–647; see [[cholos]]). He does not say that the gifts are too few. He says that the memory of the insult is stronger than the argument, and in his answer to Odysseus he set a condition that gifts do not meet: Agamemnon will not persuade him “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.387; see [[lobe]]). What that payment would be, he does not say.

**A killing with no price.** Phoenix's story has a kin-killing of its own. Althaea grieves “over the killing of her brother”, and her curse implies that her son was the killer, though Homer does not tell the deed. She asks no price: she beats the earth and calls on Hades and Persephone to give her son death, and the Erinys hears her (9.566–572; see [[althaea]] and [[curses]]). Within one family there is no one to pay and no one to be paid.

**Paying the price.** The verb comes back in Phoenix's allegory. The man who refuses the Prayers is followed by Blind Folly, “so that he may be harmed and pay the price” (ἵνα βλαφθεὶς ἀποτίσῃ, 9.512; see [[ate]] and [[litai]]). The one who will not accept a payment is made to pay one.
