**The instrument.** The lyre is φόρμιγξ λίγεια, “clear-toned”, “beautiful, finely wrought”, and its ζυγόν, the crossbar that joins the two arms and holds the strings, is of silver (9.186–187; see the translation's note on 9.186–189). It came “from the spoils when he destroyed the city of Eetion”, Thebe under Placus, where Andromache's father and seven brothers died at his hands (9.188; 6.414–424; see [[eetion]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). Other things from that city are with him at Troy: the horse Pedasus, which runs in the traces beside the immortal pair (16.152–154), and the lump of iron that Eetion used to throw, which Achilles sets as a prize at Patroclus' games (23.826–829).

**The player.** Achilles is the only hero the Iliad shows playing the lyre. Hector names Paris' lyre among the gifts of Aphrodite, of no use in battle (3.54–55); Achilles plays for himself, and what he sings is “the famous deeds of men”, κλέα ἀνδρῶν (9.189; see [[kleos]] and [[song-and-fame]]), the song of the fighting he has left. Patroclus sits facing him in silence, “waiting for the grandson of Aeacus to leave off his singing” (9.190–191; see [[patroclus]] and [[silence]]). When the envoys stand before him he springs up “with the lyre still in his hand, leaving the seat where he sat” (9.193–194). The lyre belongs to his parents' story too. At their wedding the gods all came, Hera reminds Apollo, “and you feasted among them, holding your lyre” (24.62–63).
