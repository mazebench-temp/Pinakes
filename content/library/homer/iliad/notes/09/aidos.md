summary: Phoenix asks Achilles to respect the Prayers, and Ajax ends the embassy asking him to “respect your own roof”, the guests under it. Achilles calls Agamemnon “clothed in shamelessness”, and in the four lines the manuscripts lack Phoenix tells how the fear of what men would say kept him from killing his father.
grc: [αἰδέσεται, αἴδεσσαι]
en: [respects]

**Respect for the Prayers.** Phoenix's allegory turns on αἰδώς: “Whoever respects the daughters of Zeus when they draw near, him they greatly help, and they hear him when he prays” (9.508–509). The Prayers come as suppliants come, lame and looking aside (see [[litai]]), and αἰδώς is what a suppliant asks for (see [[supplication]]). The man who refuses them “and stubbornly denies them” (9.510) is the one Achilles has just been, “for he had refused very forcefully” (9.431).

**Respect your own roof.** Ajax's last words make the same appeal on other ground:

{{quote:227e4af6-d22e-5027-aea8-e05c34cb50a2}}

αἴδεσσαι δὲ μέλαθρον: the roof stands for the guests beneath it. When the envoys came, Achilles welcomed them as the dearest of men, “and they are under my roof” (ἐμῷ ὑπέασι μελάθρῳ, 9.204). Ajax holds him to his own words: a host owes αἰδώς to those he has taken in (see [[xenia]]). Achilles grants that Ajax has spoken after his own heart, and still refuses (9.644–648).

**Shamelessness.** The opposite of αἰδώς is Achilles' word for Agamemnon: “clothed forever in shamelessness” (αἰὲν ἀναιδείην ἐπιειμένος, 9.372), the insult he threw at him in the quarrel (1.149; see [[anaideia]]). Yet even this shameless “dog”, Achilles says, would not dare to look him in the face (9.372–373; see [[dog-face]]).

**Shame before men.** Phoenix's story has the poem's plainest picture of what αἰδώς does, without the word. When his father cursed him, he planned to kill him with the bronze,

{{quote:c0bd48a5-9c64-5845-ab9e-70cb5b0f8e96}}

The god does not argue with him. He puts in his heart “the talk of the people and the many reproaches of men” (δήμου φάτιν καὶ ὀνείδεα, 9.460), and the thought of being called a father-killer holds him back. The four lines are in no manuscript; Plutarch quotes them and says that Aristarchus removed them out of fear (see the translation's note on 9.458–461). Helen wishes she had married a man “who felt the blame and the many reproaches of men” (6.351; see [[nemesis]] and [[oneidos]]); Phoenix felt them, and they kept him from the worst of crimes.
