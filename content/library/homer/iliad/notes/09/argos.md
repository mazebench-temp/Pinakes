---
summary: Argos is the home that frames the night's choices. Agamemnon would go back to it “in disgrace” (9.22); his offer of a daughter is for the day they reach “Achaean Argos, the udder of the plowland” (9.141, 9.283); and Odysseus fears they will die “far from horse-pasturing Argos” (9.246).
---

**In disgrace.** Agamemnon's proposal to flee repeats his test of Book 2, and with it the line “and bids me go back to Argos in disgrace, when I have lost so many men” (9.22 = 2.115; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). Argos here is his home and the army's.

**Achaean Argos.** In the offer of marriage Argos is the land where Agamemnon's son and daughters live: “if we come to Achaean Argos, the udder of the plowland, he would be my son-in-law” (9.141–142, repeated by Odysseus at 9.283). Ἄργος Ἀχαιϊκόν, “Achaean Argos”, returns when Hera hurries to Argos to bring on the birth of Eurystheus (19.115) and in the Odyssey, when Telemachus asks Nestor whether Menelaus was not in Achaean Argos when Aegisthus killed his brother (Od. 3.247–252); it is the Argolid, or the Peloponnese at large (see [[achaeans]]). οὖθαρ ἀρούρης, “the udder of the plowland”, is a metaphor found only in these two lines: the richest farmland (see [[udder-of-the-plowland]] and the translation's note on 9.141).

**Far from Argos.** Odysseus tells Achilles that he fears “that it may be our fate to perish in Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos” (9.245–246; see [[horse-pasturing]]). Argos is the homeland of all the Achaeans, as when Hector imagines Andromache weaving “in Argos” (6.456). The adjective names Helen twice in the book: the twenty Trojan women are to be the most beautiful “after Argive Helen” (9.140, 9.282; see [[argive-helen]]).
