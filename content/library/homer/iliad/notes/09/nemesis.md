---
summary: Phoenix grants Achilles that “before now there was no blame in your anger”; once the gifts are offered and his dearest friends have come to entreat him, the anger that was blameless becomes a cause for blame. Achilles, for his part, wants the army's indignation turned on Agamemnon.
passages:
  - 9.515-523 | If he were not bringing gifts
---

**No blame until now.** Phoenix ends the first part of his plea with a concession and a warning. If Agamemnon were not bringing gifts and naming more, Phoenix would not ask Achilles to give up his wrath; but now he gives much at once and has sent the men dearest to Achilles to entreat him, and “you must not put their words to shame, or their coming; before now there was no blame in your anger” (πρὶν δʼ οὔ τι νεμεσσητὸν κεχολῶσθαι, 9.522–523). νεμεσσητός, “a cause for indignation”, is the word Helen used of going to Paris' bed (3.410). Phoenix's argument is that the gifts change the moral account: the anger was just while the wrong was unanswered, and once amends are offered, to go on refusing is what others will blame (see [[cholos]] and [[menis]]). His story of Meleager makes the same point: the Aetolians offered the domain, and his refusal cost him the gifts (9.574–599).

**The army's indignation.** Achilles wants the opposite. He tells Odysseus to report everything “openly, so that the other Achaeans too may be indignant” (ἐπισκύζωνται, 9.370), if Agamemnon hopes to deceive anyone else. σκύζομαι, “be angry, show displeasure”, is the word Achilles used of himself when he greeted the envoys, “angry as I am” (σκυζομένῳ, 9.198). He means the army's indignation to fall on the king, not on him.

**Blame later.** Whose anger is blameworthy is argued over for the rest of the poem. Patroclus calls Achilles αἰδοῖος νεμεσητός, a man to be revered and quick to blame, who “would soon blame even one who is blameless” (11.649–654). In the last book Apollo, angered by the outrage to Hector's body, warns the gods “lest we be indignant with him, good as he is” (24.53).
