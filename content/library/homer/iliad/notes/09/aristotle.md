---
summary: Aristotle uses three verses of Book 9 in the Politics, Nestor's man “without clan, without law, without hearth”, Achilles' complaint that the coward and the brave are held in one honor, and his “dishonored vagrant”. The Rhetoric takes Cleopatra's plea as an example of amplification, the Poetics defends Achilles' order for stronger wine, and the History of Animals reads the boar of Calydon as a gelded boar.
---

- The man without a city (*Politics* 1.2, 1253a1–7): [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]].
- **Equal honors.** Phaleas of Chalcedon proposed that citizens should hold equal property. Aristotle answers that men fall into civil strife over honors as well as property, but in opposite ways: the many over the inequality of property, the refined (οἱ χαρίεντες) over honors, if they are equal, “whence too, ‘the coward and the brave are held in a single honor’” (*Politics* 2.7, 1266b38–1267a2, quoting 9.319). In Achilles' mouth the verse is a grievance against Agamemnon's way of sharing the spoil (9.315–336); Aristotle hears in it the complaint of the best men in a city of equals (see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]).
- **The dishonored vagrant.** A citizen in the fullest sense, Aristotle says, is the man who has a share in the honors, the offices of the city, “as Homer too composed, ‘as if I were some dishonored vagrant’; for a man who has no share in the honors is like a resident alien (μέτοικος)” (*Politics* 3.5, 1278a34–38, quoting 9.648 = 16.59). In the *Rhetoric* he quotes the same verse among the slights that cause anger (2.2; see [[aristotle-anger]] and [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]). Achilles' ἀτίμητος, “without honor”, becomes a man without public office, and his μετανάστης, the wanderer who has left his own country, a metic.
- Cleopatra's plea, an example of a thing made greater by division into its parts (*Rhetoric* 1.7.31, 1365a): [[aristotle-cleopatras-plea]].
- “Mix it stronger” means “quicker” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a14–15): [[stronger-or-quicker]].
- The argument of Plato's *Hippias Minor*, built on Achilles' answers to Odysseus and Ajax, “misleads” (*Metaphysics* 5.29, 1025a6–13): [[plato-hippias-minor]].
- **The gelded boar.** Of male wild boars, Aristotle writes, the castrated grow larger and fiercer, “as Homer too composed: ‘she reared against them a χλούνης, a wild boar; it was not like a beast that eats bread, but like a wooded peak’” (*History of Animals* 6.28). The quotation joins the boar that Artemis sent against Calydon, with θρέψεν, “reared”, for our ὦρσεν, “roused” (9.539), to the description of the Cyclops, with “beast” for “man” (*Odyssey* 9.190–191). Aristotle takes χλούνης to mean “castrated”; the explanation recorded in the translation's note on 9.539 makes it “lying in the grass” (χλόη and εὐνή), and the Suda gives both, with “solitary” among others (χ 349). See [[the-calydonian-boar]] and [[boar]].
