**Half a gift.** In the quarrel Agamemnon belittled Achilles' strength as a god's gift (1.178), and rested his own claim on another gift, the scepter and the glory that Zeus gives to kings (1.279). Diomedes answers that claim in Book 9 with the same idea:

{{quote:a56833bd-6319-5132-85f5-19c3f754de55}}

Zeus gave “only half” (διάνδιχα, 9.37). The verse is the one Odysseus used of the single king in Book 2, “to whom the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given the scepter and the ordinances” (2.205), and Diomedes cuts it in two: the scepter, yes, and the honor that goes with it; courage, no. If gifts of the gods are the measure, the king has the lesser one, since courage is “the greatest power” (see [[alke]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]).

**Strength from the gods, restraint from the man.** Peleus' parting charge, as Odysseus recalls it, divides a hero's gifts the same way: “My child, Athena and Hera will give you strength, if they will; but you, hold back your proud heart in your breast” (9.254–256; see [[kindliness-is-better]]). Strength is the gods' to give, and is given on their terms; self-command is the son's. The book tests the second half. The strength Achilles has; the restraint, on this night, he will not give.
