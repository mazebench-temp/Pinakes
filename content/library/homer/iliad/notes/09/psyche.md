summary: Achilles uses the word three times in his answer to Odysseus. He has staked his ψυχή in battle without thanks, nothing is worth his ψυχή, and a man's ψυχή, once it has passed his teeth, cannot be raided or won back.
grc: [ψυχῆς]
en: [life]
passages:
  - 9.401-409 | Nothing is worth my life
---

The translation renders the word two ways in one speech, and the change marks a turn in the argument (see the translation's note on 9.401–416).

**Life staked.** Achilles' first complaint is that he has fought for others' wives, “always staking my life in battle” (ψυχὴν παραβαλλόμενος, 9.322). παραβάλλομαι is to put down beside, to lay as a stake; the ψυχή is what the fighter risks every day, and the risk has earned him nothing (see [[charis]]). The bird that feeds its unfledged chicks and goes hungry herself is his image for it (9.323–327).

**Life valued.** Later in the same speech he sets it against all the wealth Agamemnon could offer: “For nothing to me is worth my life” (ψυχῆς ἀντάξιον, 9.401), not the wealth of Ilios in peacetime, nor the treasures within Apollo's stone threshold at Pytho (9.401–405; see [[pytho]]). ἀντάξιος is “of equal worth”, the word of an exchange in which one thing is weighed against another (a healer is a man “worth many others”, πολλῶν ἀντάξιος ἄλλων, 11.514). Agamemnon has offered goods; Achilles answers that the one thing he would spend in earning them has no price.

**The soul that goes.** Then the ψυχή becomes the thing that leaves the body:

{{quote:67da1ce3-a8a6-5127-a152-a9855848afe4}}

Here the translation says “soul”. Cattle and sheep are ληϊστοί, “to be had by raiding”, and tripods and horses κτητοί, “to be won”; the ψυχή is neither, once it has passed “the barrier of his teeth” (ἕρκος ὀδόντων), the phrase used elsewhere of words that escape a speaker (see [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]] and [[what-a-word]]). Here the ψυχή leaves through the mouth; at the deaths of Patroclus and Hector it flies “from the limbs” to Hades (16.856, 22.362). When Hector runs from Achilles around the walls, the narrator says that the two were running not for a sacrificial beast or an oxhide, the prizes of a footrace, “but for the life of Hector” (περὶ ψυχῆς, 22.159–161): the one prize that no one can win back.
