**The threshold of the Archer.** No gift could buy Achilles' life, “not all that they say / Ilios possessed … nor all that is shut within the stone threshold of the Archer, / Phoebus Apollo, in rocky Pytho” (9.401–405). The wealth of Troy in peacetime and the treasure of a god's house are the greatest hoards he can name. Cattle and sheep can be raided and tripods won, but a man's soul cannot come back (9.406–409; see [[psyche]] and [[achilles]]).

ἀφήτωρ occurs only here. It is taken as “the archer”, from ἀφίημι, “let fly”, as the translation has it; it has also been explained as “the speaker of oracles”, from φημί, which would suit Pytho (see the translation's note on 9.404 and [[aphetor]]). The stone threshold is the threshold of his temple. In the Odyssey Agamemnon stepped over the stone threshold at Pytho (ὑπέρβη λάινον οὐδόν) to consult Apollo before the war, and was told of a quarrel among the best of the Achaeans (*Odyssey* 8.75–82). In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the god lays the foundations of his temple, and Trophonius and Agamedes set the stone threshold on them (294–297); a verse of the Hymn ends, like 9.405, Πυθοῖ ἔνι πετρηέσσῃ, “in rocky Pytho” (390; see [[pytho]] and [[rocky]]).

**Idas and Apollo.** In the story of Meleager, Phoenix names the parents of Meleager's wife:

{{quote:3cb79c24-ee08-56f4-b333-21499a986205}}

Apollo had snatched Marpessa away (ἀνήρπασε, 9.564), and Idas took up his bow against the god for the sake of his bride (9.558–560). Homer does not say how the quarrel ended; the allusion assumes that the audience knows the story. In a later account Zeus parts the two, and Marpessa chooses Idas (Apollodorus, *Library* 1.7.8–9; see [[idas]], [[marpessa]], [[cleopatra]] and [[idas-and-apollo]]). Another man who matched his bow against Apollo, Eurytus of Oechalia, challenged him to a contest and was killed by him before he reached old age (*Odyssey* 8.223–228). Diomedes in Book 5 sprang three times at Aeneas while Apollo protected him, and drew back when the god shouted at him (5.432–444; see [[diomedes]]). Phoenix gives Idas no victory, only the daring of “the strongest of men on earth / in those days” (9.558–559). Here Apollo is ἑκάεργος, “far-working” (9.564; see [[far-worker]]).
