The story begins with his fault. The other gods feasted on hecatombs, but to Artemis alone Oeneus made no sacrifice, and Phoenix leaves the reason open: “Either he forgot, or did not think of it” (9.537). ἀάσατο, “he was blinded”, puts him beside Agamemnon, who has owned that he “was blinded” (9.116, 9.119; see [[ate]] and [[artemis]]). The goddess answers on his own ground. The boar did its harm to “the orchard of Oeneus” (Οἰνῆος ἀλωήν, 9.540), throwing down tall trees with their roots and blossoms (see [[the-calydonian-boar]]). The name comes from οἶνος, “wine”, and the domain later offered to his son is half vineyard (9.579; see [[calydon]]).

His second scene is a father's plea. “And the old horse-driving Oeneus entreated him again and again, / standing on the threshold of the high-roofed chamber, / shaking the jointed doors, imploring his son” (9.581–583). γουνούμενος, “imploring”, is the verb of the suppliant who clasps a man's knees (see [[supplication]]), though here a door stands between them. γέρων ἱππηλάτα, “the old horse-driving”, is said in this book of three fathers: of Phoenix, who tells the story (9.432), of Peleus, who sent him to teach Achilles (9.438), and of Oeneus (9.581; see [[horse-driving]] and [[phoenix]]). The old man begging at his son's door is the part Phoenix plays himself. Oeneus' pleas fail, and so, that night, do Phoenix's (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[meleager]]).
