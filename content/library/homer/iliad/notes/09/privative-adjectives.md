**Three in a row.** Nestor's warning against strife among the Achaeans opens with three privatives and no conjunction: “Without clan, without law, without hearth is the man who loves war among his own people, that chilling thing” (ἀφρήτωρ ἀθέμιστος ἀνέστιος, 9.63–64). Each word strips the man of one bond: the φρήτρη, the clan or brotherhood (2.362–363), the θέμιστες, the ordinances (see [[themis]]), and the ἑστίη, the hearth of a house. ἀφρήτωρ and ἀνέστιος occur only here (see the translation's note on 9.63–64 and [[nestor]]).

**Two with a conjunction.** Agamemnon's Hades is “implacable and untamable” (ἀμείλιχος ἠδʼ ἀδάμαστος, 9.158), the god no one can soothe or break, and “let him be tamed” (δμηθήτω) in the same verse uses the root of ἀδάμαστος against Achilles (see [[implacable]], [[hades]] and [[propitiation]]). Diomedes recalls the taunt that he was ἀπτόλεμον καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, “unwarlike and a coward” (9.35), and throws the pair back: does Agamemnon think the sons of the Achaeans so unwarlike and cowardly (9.41)? The pair is the one Odysseus used to the common soldier in Book 2 (ἀπτόλεμος καὶ ἄναλκις, 2.201; see [[retelling]]).

**Singles.** The gifts begin with tripods ἄπυροι, “untouched by fire” (9.122), and a man who had the prizes of Agamemnon's horses would be neither ἀλήϊος, “landless”, nor ἀκτήμων, “lacking”, in gold (9.125–126): the negatives deny poverty to praise wealth (see [[litotes]]). The daughter is offered ἀνάεδνος, “without bride-price” (9.146; see [[marriage]]). Achilles has spent “many sleepless nights” (ἀΰπνους, 9.325), and the coward is held in the same honor as the brave, the idle man (ἀεργός) dying like the man who has done much (9.320). In his answer to Ajax he recalls the insult he has not forgotten, that Agamemnon treated him “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (ἀτίμητον, 9.648), a verse he repeats to Patroclus in Book 16 (16.59). And the fame he may win is ἄφθιτον, “imperishable” (9.413; see [[kleos]]).
