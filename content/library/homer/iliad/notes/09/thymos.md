Peleus told him to hold back his proud heart, Phoenix asks him to tame it, Ajax says he has made it savage and asks him to make it gracious, and Achilles answers that no one will persuade it; at the end Diomedes leaves him to fight when his heart bids him.

---

The word comes more than twenty-five times in Book 9, more often than any other noun of the inner life, and nearly every speech to Achilles is an appeal to his θυμός.

**The proud heart.** The envoys keep returning to one phrase, μεγαλήτωρ θυμός, “the proud heart”. Peleus, as Odysseus recalls him, told his son to hold it back in his breast, “for kindliness is better” (9.255–256; see [[kindliness-is-better]]). Ajax says that Achilles “has made savage the proud heart in his breast” (ἄγριον ἐν στήθεσσι θέτο μεγαλήτορα θυμόν, 9.629), and at the end Agamemnon asks, “does anger still hold his proud heart?” (9.675). Nestor used the phrase of the king himself: Agamemnon took the girl “giving way to your proud heart” (σῷ μεγαλήτορι θυμῷ εἴξας, 9.109–110). The same participle closes the story of Meleager, who saved the Aetolians at last “yielding to his own heart” (εἴξας ᾧ θυμῷ, 9.598), and so lost the gifts. To yield to the θυμός is how the quarrel began, and how Meleager's anger ended.

**Tame it.** Phoenix asks for more than restraint: “So, Achilles, tame your great spirit; you have no need to have a pitiless heart” (δάμασον θυμὸν μέγαν, 9.496–497). δαμάζω is the verb for breaking a horse and for killing an enemy, and it is the word Agamemnon used of Achilles himself, “Let him be tamed” (δμηθήτω, 9.158), in the lines Odysseus did not repeat. Ajax says that the gods have put in him “a heart that is unrelenting and evil, for the sake of a girl” (9.636–637), and asks him to “make your heart gracious” (ἵλαον ἔνθεο θυμόν, 9.639).

**Not persuaded.** Achilles answers in the same terms. “Not even so would Agamemnon any longer persuade my heart” (θυμὸν ἐμὸν πείσειʼ, 9.386), he tells Odysseus; to Phoenix, “do not confuse my heart with weeping and grieving” (μή μοι σύγχει θυμόν, 9.612); to Ajax, “all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart” (κατὰ θυμόν, 9.645), but his heart swells with anger when he remembers (9.646; see [[cholos]] and [[kradie]]). In the story Phoenix tells, Meleager's friends could not persuade “the heart in his breast” either (9.587), until his wife's account of a captured city stirred it (9.595).

**When his heart bids him.** Diomedes ends the debate by giving the θυμός its due: Achilles “will fight again when the heart in his breast bids him and a god rouses him” (9.702–703). The heart and the god together will decide, not the gifts.
