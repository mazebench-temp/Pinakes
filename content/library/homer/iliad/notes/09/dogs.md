grc: [κύνεός]
passages:
  - 9.369-377 | Achilles' message to Agamemnon
  - 9.543-546 | Huntsmen and dogs
---

**The shameless king.** Achilles tells the envoys to report his words to Agamemnon openly, so that the other Achaeans may be indignant if he still hopes to deceive any of them, “clothed forever in shamelessness; but he would not, dog though he is, dare to look me in the face” (9.369–373). κύνεος, “doglike”, occurs only here in Homer. The dog is the beast of shamelessness, and the line joins the two: ἀναιδείην ἐπιειμένος, “clothed in shamelessness”, and κύνεός περ ἐών, “dog though he is” (see [[clothed-in-shamelessness]] and [[anaideia]]). Both insults are Achilles' words from the quarrel of Book 1, where he called Agamemnon “clothed in shamelessness” and “dog-face”, with “the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” (1.149, 1.159, 1.225; see [[dog-face]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). The eyes are the point. Agamemnon has not come himself; Achilles says that he would not dare (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**Hunting dogs.** Meleager killed the boar of Calydon by “gathering huntsmen from many cities and dogs; for it could not have been subdued by a few mortals” (9.544–545; see [[boar]] and [[the-calydonian-boar]]). It is the only boar hunt told as a story in the Iliad. Elsewhere hounds and young men surround a boar in the similes, and the boar turns on them, whetting its white tusk (11.414–418, 12.41–48, 17.725–734; see [[lions-and-boars]]). In the Odyssey the young Odysseus goes up Parnassus with the sons of Autolycus and their dogs, tracking ahead of them, to hunt the boar that gave him his scar (*Odyssey* 19.428–458).
