summary: Achilles asks why the Argives fight if not for Helen, and says he has fought for other men's wives. In Phoenix's story women begin and end a war: Artemis sends the boar and the strife over its spoils, Althaea curses her son, and his wife Cleopatra alone moves him to fight, with an account of what happens to the women of a taken city.
passages:
  - 9.337-343 | Was it not for the sake of Helen?
  - 9.588-596 | Meleager's wife moves him to fight
---

**Fighting for wives.** Achilles describes his years at Troy as nights without sleep and days of blood, “fighting with men for the sake of their wives” (9.326–327). Whose wives is left open: the enemy's, defending their own, or the Achaeans', Helen above all (see the translation's note on 9.327). A few lines later he asks the question outright: “Why did he gather the army and lead it here, the son of Atreus? Was it not for the sake of Helen of the lovely hair?” (9.338–339; see [[helen]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). If a war can be fought for one man's wife, his own loss of the woman he loved is no small thing (9.340–343; see [[captive-women]]).

**A war begun by a goddess.** In Phoenix's story the war of the Curetes and the Aetolians begins with Artemis' anger at Oeneus' forgotten offering. She sends the boar, and when Meleager has killed it she raises “a great din and shouting” between the two peoples over its head and hide (9.533–549; see [[artemis]] and [[the-calydonian-boar]]). Behind Cleopatra stands another quarrel over a woman: her father Idas drew his bow against Apollo for the sake of his bride Marpessa (9.557–564; see [[idas-and-apollo]]).

**A mother and a wife.** Meleager withdraws from the war in anger at his mother. Althaea, grieving for her brother, beat the earth and called on Hades and Persephone to give her son death (9.566–572; see [[althaea]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). He lies beside his wife while the city is attacked, and is begged in turn by elders, priests, father, sisters, mother and friends. His wife comes last, and moves him where no one else could, by telling him what the women of a city suffer when it falls: “strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (9.590–596; see [[cleopatra]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). The poem gives the war's decisive plea to a woman and the decisive argument to the fate of women.
