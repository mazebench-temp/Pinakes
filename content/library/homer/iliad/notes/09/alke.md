---
summary: Diomedes' two speeches frame the book with courage. In the first he throws back Agamemnon's old charge that he lacked it, and tells the king that Zeus gave him the scepter but not courage, “the greatest power”; in the last he tells the kings that food and wine are “might and courage”.
passages:
  - 9.32-49 | Diomedes answers Agamemnon
  - 9.704-709 | Diomedes' last word
---

**A charge returned.** Diomedes has waited five books to answer:

{{quote:a56833bd-6319-5132-85f5-19c3f754de55}}

He begins with the reproach: “It was my courage you first reproached among the Danaans, saying I was unwarlike and a coward” (ἀπτόλεμον καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, 9.34–35). In Book 4 Agamemnon asked him why he cowered and told him he was worse in battle than his father (4.370–400); Diomedes said nothing then, and Sthenelus answered for him (see [[praise-and-blame]]). Now the words come back to the king. Zeus gave Agamemnon half: the scepter, and honor for it, but not ἀλκή, “and that is the greatest power” (ὅ τε κράτος ἐστὶ μέγιστον, 9.39). The half-line is the one Agamemnon had just spoken of Zeus himself, “for his power is the greatest” (τοῦ γὰρ κράτος ἐστὶ μέγιστον, 9.25 = 2.118). The king spoke of the power that has brought down the heights of many cities, and proposed flight; Diomedes answers that the greatest power is courage, and that the king lacks it. The king's proposal to flee, he adds, treats the whole army as cowards: “do you really think the sons of the Achaeans are so unwarlike and cowardly as you say?” (9.40–41). The army shouts its approval (9.50–51).

**Put on your courage.** Odysseus asks Achilles to save the ships, “unless you put on your courage” (εἰ μὴ σύ γε δύσεαι ἀλκήν, 9.231). Courage is a garment, as when the two Ajaxes are “clothed in furious courage” (7.164); Thetis will use the same verb when she sends Achilles back to the war, δύσεο δʼ ἀλκήν (19.36).

**Courage from food.** At the end of the night Diomedes gives the kings plain advice: sleep, eat and drink, “for in that is might and courage” (τὸ γὰρ μένος ἐστὶ καὶ ἀλκή, 9.706; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]). The man whose courage was doubted ends by saying where courage comes from. Odysseus gives the same advice in the same half-line when Achilles wants to lead the army out unfed (19.160–161).
