**Eudorus' father.** The second rank of the Myrmidons is led by “warlike Eudorus, / a maiden's son” (16.179–180; see [[eudorus]], [[myrmidons]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]): παρθένιος, the son of an unmarried girl. His mother was Polymele, “beautiful in the dance”, the daughter of Phylas, and “the mighty slayer of Argus / fell in love with her, when he saw her with his eyes among the girls singing and dancing / in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous” (16.180–183; see [[polymele]], [[phylas]], [[artemis]] and [[argeiphontes]]).

{{quote:79d9759a-e817-5ace-ab70-3439b1a91aaf}}

When Eileithyia had brought the child into the light, Echecles, son of Actor, married Polymele with a countless bride-price, and the old man Phylas reared the boy as his own son (16.187–192; see [[eileithyia]], [[echecles]] and [[bride-price]]). Eudorus is the only son of Hermes named in the Iliad. The god loved a Trojan too, Phorbas, “rich in flocks”, and gave him wealth (14.490–491).

**The pattern of the story.** A god sees a girl, goes up to her room and lies with her in secret: so Astyoche, “a modest maiden”, went up to the upper room to Ares, “and he lay with her in secret”, and bore Ascalaphus and Ialmenus (2.513–515; see [[the-birth-of-ascalaphus-and-ialmenus]]). παρελέξατο λάθρῃ, “lay with her in secret”, is the same in both. The dance of Artemis was a place where a god might see a girl. In the *Hymn to Aphrodite* the goddess, disguised as a Phrygian princess, tells Anchises that the slayer of Argus with the golden wand snatched her away “from the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous”, where many girls were dancing (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–121), in nearly the words of 16.183.

**His titles.** κρατὺς ἀργεϊφόντης, “the mighty slayer of Argus” (16.181), is the half-verse in which Hermes flies off with his wand to meet Priam (24.345) and to Calypso (*Odyssey* 5.49). Ἑρμείας ἀκάκητα, “Hermes the helper” (16.185), returns only once, when he leads the souls of the suitors down to the dead (*Odyssey* 24.10); the sense of ἀκάκητα is uncertain (see [[akaketa]] and the translation's note on 16.185).
