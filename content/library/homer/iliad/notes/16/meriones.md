summary: Meriones kills twice: Acamas as he mounts his chariot in the rout, and, in the fight over Sarpedon's body, Laogonus, son of Onetor, the priest of Idaean Zeus. Aeneas throws at him and misses, and taunts him as a dancer; Meriones answers that Aeneas too is mortal, and Patroclus rebukes him for wasting words: “the outcome of war lies in our hands” (16.342–344, 16.603–632).

**Acamas.** “And Meriones overtook Acamas with his swift feet and stabbed him / on the right shoulder as he was mounting his chariot” (16.342–343; see [[acamas]]). He is eighth in the list of leaders who each kill a man as the Trojans give way, between Peneleos and his own commander Idomeneus (16.306–351; see [[peneleos]], [[idomeneus]] and [[androktasia]]). The second verse is the one in which Idomeneus killed Phaestus in the first such list, in Book 5 (16.343 = 5.46). Which Acamas this is the poem does not say.

**Laogonus.** In the fight over Sarpedon's body, “Then in turn Meriones killed a helmeted man of the Trojans, / Laogonus, the bold son of Onetor, who was the priest of Zeus / of Ida, and was honored by the people like a god” (16.603–605; see [[laogonus]], [[onetor]] and [[helmeted]]). He strikes him under the jaw and the ear, “and quickly his spirit / went from his limbs, and hateful darkness seized him” (16.606–607).

**Aeneas' spear.** Aeneas throws at him, hoping to hit him as he steps forward under cover of his shield; Meriones, looking straight at the spear, bends forward and lets it fly over, and it sticks in the ground behind him and quivers (16.608–615; see [[aeneas]]). The verses are those of Aeneas' throw at Idomeneus in Book 13: “But he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear” (16.610 = 13.503), and “Aeneas' spear went quivering into the earth, / since it had sped in vain from his strong hand” (16.614–615 = 13.504–505). The quivering butt of the spear, “and then mighty Ares let its might go” (16.613), is the verse of the spear that the dying Alcathous' heart shook in the same battle (13.443–444; see [[alcathous]] and [[obrimos]]).

**The dancer.**

{{quote:ccfd5573-7a37-5f06-a66f-8a08313207f7}}

ὀρχηστήν περ ἐόντα, “dancer though you are” (16.617): the quick dodge has made Meriones a dancer, and the word is an insult. Priam will call his surviving sons “dancers” in his grief and anger (24.261; see [[dance]]). Meriones answers in kind: Aeneas cannot quench the might of every man who stands against him, “for you too are mortal”, and if a spear of his own found its mark, Aeneas would give him “triumph, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (16.620–625; see [[hades]] and [[of-the-famous-horses]]), the verse of the boasts of Sarpedon to Tlepolemus and Odysseus to Socus (5.654, 11.445; see [[euchomai]]).

**Rebuked.** Patroclus stops the exchange: “Meriones, why do you, brave as you are, say these things? / My friend, the Trojans will not, for abusive words, / give ground from the body; first the earth will hold many a man. / For the outcome of war lies in our hands, and the outcome of words in council; / so there is no need to heap up words, but to fight” (16.627–631; see [[patroclus]] and [[word-or-deed]]). “So saying, he led the way, and the other followed, a man equal to the gods” (16.632; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]): the verse in which Ajax followed Menelaus to rescue Odysseus and Melanippus followed Hector (11.472, 15.559).
