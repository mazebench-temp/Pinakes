summary: Book 16 carries out, step by step, the forecast that Zeus gave Hera in Book 15. The ship burns, the counterattack from the ships begins, Patroclus kills Sarpedon, and Hector kills Patroclus before Ilios; Zeus grants Achilles half his prayer, chooses over Sarpedon's body the order of what follows, and gives Hector the victory.

**The forecast fulfilled.** When Zeus woke on Ida in Book 15 he told Hera the course of the war: Apollo would drive the Achaeans to the ships of Achilles, “and he will rouse up his companion, / Patroclus; and shining Hector will kill him with the spear / before Ilios, after Patroclus has destroyed many young men, / others besides, and among them my son, brilliant Sarpedon” (15.64–67; see the note on Book 15). Book 16 tells each clause in its order. The Achaeans are at the ships of Achilles, Achilles sends out his companion (16.124–129), Patroclus kills many, Sarpedon among them (16.284–505), and Hector kills him beneath the walls of the city (16.698–867). The rest of the forecast lies beyond the book: “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68).

**The term.** Zeus had been waiting for one sight, “the blaze of a burning ship” (15.600), and from it he would bring about the counterattack from the ships (15.601–602). Book 16 begins at that moment. Ajax, hard pressed by “the mind of Zeus and the noble Trojans” (16.103), sees Hector lop off the head of his spear and knows “the work of the gods, how he was utterly cutting short the plans of his battle, / Zeus who thunders on high, and wished victory for the Trojans” (16.119–121; see [[ajax]]). He draws back, the fire falls on the ship (16.122–124), and Achilles sends Patroclus out. The turn comes at once: Patroclus kills the Paeonian leader, puts out the fire, and drives the Trojans from the ships (16.284–305; see [[patroclus]]). It is the παλίωξις, the “counterattack”, that Zeus foretold (15.69, 15.601).

**Half a prayer.** Achilles, who has not been told the plan, prays that Patroclus may win glory and drive the battle from the ships, and come back unharmed (16.241–248). Zeus grants the first and refuses the second (16.249–252; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[neuo]]). The narrator says so at once, and then shows Achilles going back into his hut and coming out to watch, still wishing in his heart to see the battle (16.253–256; see [[achilles]]). The audience knows the price of the plan, which Zeus named in Books 8 and 15 (8.473–477, 15.64–68); the man whose wish the plan fulfills does not.

**Zeus' own son.** The plan includes the death of Sarpedon, and Zeus, who announced it, thinks of breaking it. He asks Hera whether he should snatch his son out of the battle; she warns him that the other gods will not approve, and he lets his son die (16.431–461; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]], [[zeus]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). The author of the plan is held to it by the order of the gods.

**The order of events.** The end is fixed; the steps are chosen as they come. Over Sarpedon's body Zeus considers when Patroclus should die:

{{quote:d49246f4-69d0-594e-bd61-0376fee8d2b2}}

μερμηρίζων, “pondering” (16.647), is the verb of the night in Book 2 when Zeus pondered how to honor Achilles, and the plan of the lying Dream “seemed to him in his heart the best” (2.3–6; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). Here too a choice “seemed to him the better way” (16.652): Patroclus will drive the Trojans to the city and take many lives before he dies there. Zeus puts a craven heart into Hector, who knows “the sacred scales of Zeus” (16.656–658; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[hector]]).

**The mind of Zeus.** When Patroclus, blinded, pursues the Trojans against Achilles' word, the narrator states the rule of the plan: “But the mind of Zeus is always stronger than that of men” (16.688; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Zeus puts even a valiant man to flight and takes away his victory, and at other times urges him on, “and he it was who then too let loose the spirit in his breast” (16.689–691). Patroclus' folly is his own and Zeus' work at once (see [[ate]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**Victory for Hector.** The plan gives Hector the day. Apollo “granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (16.730; see [[apollo]] and [[kydos]]). Achilles' helmet had never been fouled with dust, “but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.799–800; see [[helmet]]): the gift that crowns Hector's victory looks forward to his death. Patroclus names the givers as he dies: “Boast loudly now, Hector; for to you they have given / victory, Zeus, son of Cronus, and Apollo” (16.844–845). In Book 8 Hector had boasted that Zeus “has readily nodded his promise to me of victory and great glory” (8.175–176). Now the promise is kept, and the next step of the forecast is Achilles' revenge.
