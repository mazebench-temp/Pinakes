summary: The book opens and turns on pity. Achilles pities the weeping Patroclus, who pities the Argives (16.5, 16.17); Zeus, seeing Sarpedon and Patroclus rush at each other, pities them, and Hera tells him that his heart grieves for his son (16.431, 16.450). Neither pity saves the man it is felt for.

**Achilles and Patroclus.** “And seeing him, brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him” (16.5), and then he mocks him gently, likening him to a girl who wants to be picked up (16.7–11; see [[like-a-girl]]). His question gives Patroclus' tears their cause: “Or are you grieving for the Argives, how they are perishing / beside the hollow ships because of their own transgression?” (16.17–18; see [[hybris]]). Patroclus' answer turns pity into reproach, “Pitiless one” (νηλεές, 16.33; see [[pitiless]]), the word the Myrmidons used of Achilles (16.204). Nestor had said that Achilles did not pity the Danaans (11.665); Achilles pities his companion's tears, and calls the army's ruin its own fault.

**Zeus.** “And seeing them the son of crooked-counseling Cronus pitied them” (16.431): the pity is for both men, and Zeus' words are for his son, “dearest of men to me” (16.433; see [[zeus]]). Hera answers that the gods will not approve, “But if he is dear to you, and your heart grieves for him” (16.450), let him die and send him home for burial (see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]] and [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]). The god's pity ends in a rain of blood and a funeral (16.459–461, 16.666–683; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).
