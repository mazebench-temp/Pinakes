summary: Automedon drives for Patroclus. He yokes the immortal horses Xanthus and Balius with the trace horse Pedasus, arms beside Patroclus in front of the Myrmidons, cuts the dead trace horse loose when Sarpedon's spear kills it, and at the end of the book, when Patroclus has fallen, the immortal horses carry him away from Hector (16.145–154, 16.218–220, 16.466–476, 16.684, 16.864–867).

**The charioteer.** When Patroclus has armed, “he bade Automedon yoke the horses quickly, / whom he honored most after Achilles, breaker of men, / and who was the most to be trusted to await his shout in battle” (16.145–147; see [[patroclus]], [[achilles]] and [[breaker-of-men]]). It is the first time the poem gives him horses to drive; in Book 9 he only held the meat while Achilles carved it (9.209). He leads under the yoke the immortal horses, “Xanthus and Balius, who flew as fast as the winds”, foaled by the Harpy Podarge to the West Wind, and puts the mortal Pedasus, taken at the sack of Eetion's city, in the trace horse's harness (16.148–154; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]], [[pedasus-the-horse]] and [[sack-of-thebe]]). The two men arm in front of all the Myrmidons, “Patroclus and Automedon, with one heart, / to fight in front of the Myrmidons” (16.218–220; see [[myrmidons]]), and when the Trojans see “himself and his attendant, gleaming in their armor”, they think that Achilles has given up his wrath (16.278–282).

**The trace horse.** Sarpedon misses Patroclus and kills Pedasus instead; the horse falls screaming in the dust, the two immortal horses start apart, the yoke creaks and the reins tangle (16.466–471; see [[sarpedon]]):

{{quote:e085a425-8697-5d73-812e-3132323e66bc}}

τέκμωρ, “a way out” (see [[tekmor]]): the charioteer's skill saves the team. Nestor did the same thing with his sword in Book 8, “cutting away the trace horse's harness” when Paris' arrow struck his horse, and nearly died of the delay (8.80–91; see [[nestor]]). δουρικλυτός, “famed for his spear”, is the title of Odysseus and Idomeneus, and in this book of Meriones (16.619; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]); it is given to Automedon only here.

**Left alive.** He drives Patroclus on after the Trojans and the Lycians (16.684), into the pursuit that Achilles had forbidden (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). When Patroclus is dead, Hector at once goes after Automedon, “the godlike attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus; / for he was eager to hit him; but his swift horses carried him away, / immortal, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.864–867; see [[hector]], [[antitheos]] and [[peleus]]). The two verses come from the passage in which Patroclus drove across the ditch after Hector, and Hector's horses carried him away (16.380–383): the book ends with the pursuit reversed. In the next book the horses will stand weeping for Patroclus, and Automedon will fight on alone in the chariot (17.426–465).
