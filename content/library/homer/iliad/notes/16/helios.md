**Midday and afternoon.** Over the body of Cebriones the battle hangs even:

{{quote:3e0085cc-2383-5057-b86a-eef1ac6f9897}}

The verses mark the day as the poem marked the morning of the second and third days of battle: “As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing, / so long the missiles of both sides struck home” (8.66–67, 11.84–85), and at midday the father held out his golden scales (8.68–69; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Here the morning has become the high sun, and the turn comes in the afternoon. βουλυτόνδε (printed βουλυτὸν δέ), “toward the time for unyoking oxen”, tells the hour by the plowman's day; the same verse tells how the Cicones turned on Odysseus' men in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 9.58; see the translation's note on 16.188). The Achaeans are stronger “beyond their portion” (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν, 16.780; see [[aisa]]), and Patroclus leaps on the Trojans to his death (16.781–867; see [[patroclus]]). The digitized text capitalizes Ἠέλιος, as at 8.68, and the translation keeps “the sun”. The day that began at 11.1 will not end until Hera sends the unwilling sun down into the streams of Oceanus (18.239–241; see [[hera]] and [[oceanus]]).

**The light of the sun.** When Eileithyia had brought the newborn Eudorus “out into the light, and he saw the rays of the sun” (16.188; see [[eileithyia]] and [[eudorus]]): to see the sun is to be alive.
