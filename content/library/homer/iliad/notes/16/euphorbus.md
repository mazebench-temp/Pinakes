---
summary: Euphorbus, son of Panthous, is the first man to wound Patroclus. When Apollo has struck off his helmet and loosened his corselet, he spears him in the back between the shoulders, pulls out his spear and runs back into the throng; the dying Patroclus names him among his killers, “and of men Euphorbus” (16.850).

**A Dardanian man.** He comes into the poem as Δάρδανος ἀνήρ, “a Dardanian man” (16.807), the words for the unnamed killer of Protesilaus, the first of the Achaeans to die at Troy (2.701; see [[dardanians]] and [[protesilaus]]). Here the next verse names him and his father, Πανθοΐδης Εὔφορβος (16.808; see [[panthous]]), and the word means no more than “Trojan”.

**A beginner.** As the blow falls, the narrator stops to say who he was: he “surpassed all of his age / with his spear and his horsemanship and his swift feet” (16.808–809), and he had brought down twenty men from their chariots “when he first came with his chariot, learning the ways of war” (16.811). The Greek does not say whether the twenty fell on this day, his first in battle, or earlier in the war (see the translation's note on 16.810).

**The blow.** Apollo, wrapped in mist, has struck Patroclus on the back with the flat of his hand and knocked the helmet from his head; the spear has broken in his hands, his shield has fallen, and the god has loosened his corselet. “And blind folly seized his mind”, and he “stood amazed” (16.788–806; see [[apollo]], [[ate]] and [[patroclus]]). Then Euphorbus strikes, “from behind, in the back, with a sharp spear, / between the shoulders, from close by” (16.806–807). He is the first to hit Patroclus, but not the one to kill him, and the narrator turns to Patroclus himself to say so: “he was the first to throw a missile at you, horseman Patroclus, / but did not bring you down” (16.812–813; see [[apostrophe]]). He snatches his ash spear out of the flesh and runs back into the throng, and does not stand his ground against Patroclus, “naked though he was, in the combat” (16.813–815). γυμνός, “naked”, is a man without his armor, as Lycaon is when Achilles finds him (21.50), and as Hector fears to be if he lays his armor aside before Achilles (22.124).

**The second slayer.** Patroclus draws back among his companions, and Hector, seeing him “giving ground, struck by the sharp bronze”, comes close and kills him (16.817–822; see [[hector]]). Dying, Patroclus ranks his killers: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus; and you are the third to slay me” (16.849–850). Euphorbus stands between the god and Hector. In Book 17 he claims Patroclus' body and spoils from Menelaus as the man who struck him first, and Menelaus kills him (17.9–60; see [[menelaus]]).
