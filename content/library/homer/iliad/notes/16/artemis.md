---
summary: Hermes falls in love with Polymele when he sees her among the girls singing and dancing “in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous”, and she bears him Eudorus.
passages:
  - 16.179-186 | The dance of Artemis
---

Polymele, “beautiful in the dance”, was among the girls singing and dancing “in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous” (16.180–183) when Hermes saw her and fell in love with her (see [[polymele]], [[hermes]], [[eudorus]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]). The dance, χορός, of Artemis was danced by girls: in the *Hymn to Aphrodite* the goddess pretends to have been snatched away by Hermes from the dance of Artemis, where many nymphs and maidens were playing and a crowd stood around (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–121). In the Odyssey Nausicaa among her maids is likened to Artemis among her nymphs in the mountains, taller than them all (*Odyssey* 6.102–109).

The two epithets come together again only once in the Iliad, when Artemis faces Hera in the battle of the gods (20.70). χρυσηλάκατος is “of the golden distaff” (see [[of-the-golden-distaff]]): in Homer the ἠλακάτη is always a distaff, and Helen, coming out of her chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff”, has a golden distaff brought to her (*Odyssey* 4.121–122, 4.131–135). At 21.511 κελαδεινή, “clamorous” (from κέλαδος, “din”), stands alone as her name (see [[clamorous]] and the translation's note on 16.183).
