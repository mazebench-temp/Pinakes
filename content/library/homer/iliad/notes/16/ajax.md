---
summary: The last man holding the ships can hold no longer. Worn out by the missiles, his helmet ringing and his shoulder tired, Ajax loses the head of his spear to Hector's sword, recognizes the work of Zeus and draws back, and the first ship burns (16.101–123). In the rout he keeps trying to reach Hector, and with the son of Oileus he is one of the two Ajaxes whom Patroclus rouses over Sarpedon's body (16.358–363, 16.555–562).
# Αἴας at 16.330 is the son of Oileus (Αἴας … Ὀϊλιάδης), whom ajax-son-of-oileus claims.
except: [16.330]
refs: [16.103-111, 16.115, 16.117-118, 16.120, 16.122, 16.557-562]
passages:
  - 16.101-123 | Ajax gives way, and the first ship burns

**Worn down.** “So they spoke such words to one another, / and Ajax no longer stood his ground; for he was hard pressed by missiles” (16.101–102). The second verse is the one in which, at the end of Book 15, he drew back to the seven-foot bench and fought on from there (15.727; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). The narrator now stays with him for ten verses:

{{quote:df1df716-74f8-56bc-b888-d9fbba6bf770}}

The god is named before the Trojans: “the mind of Zeus and the noble Trojans” (16.103; see [[noos]] and [[zeus]]). The flashing shield that tires his left shoulder is the shield of seven oxhides that Tychius made for him, “the flashing shield of seven oxhides” (16.107; 7.220–223; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[flashing]]). He has “no way at all / to draw breath” (ἀμπνεῦσαι, 16.110–111): the breathing space that Patroclus has just asked to win for the Achaeans, “and the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath” (ἀναπνεύσωσι, 16.42), is what Ajax has run out of (see [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]]).

**The spear and the fire.** The narrator calls on the Muses to tell “how fire first fell upon the ships of the Achaeans” (16.112–113; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). Hector comes close and strikes Ajax's ash spear with his great sword “behind the point, at the socket”, and lops it off, and Ajax brandishes the docked shaft “to no purpose” while the bronze point clangs to the ground far away (16.114–118; see [[hector]] and [[ash-spear]]). “And Ajax knew in his noble heart, and shuddered, / the work of the gods, how he was utterly cutting short the plans of his battle, / Zeus who thunders on high, and wished victory for the Trojans; / and he drew back out of range of the missiles” (16.119–122; see [[thunders-on-high]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The words are Teucer's when Zeus broke his bowstring: “Ah, truly the plans of our battle are being utterly cut short / by a god” (15.467–468; see [[teucer]]). Then Ajax had answered his brother that a god begrudged them, and told him to take up spear and shield (15.472–477); now he sees the same hand in his own spear, and yields. “And they threw tireless fire / on the swift ship, and at once an unquenchable flame poured over it” (16.122–123; see [[protesilaus]]). The fire that Hector has threatened since Book 8 is lit when Ajax gives way, and the sight of it sends Patroclus out (16.124–129; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]).

**Against Hector.** In the rout to the ditch, “great Ajax was ever eager to throw his spear at Hector armed in bronze”, but Hector, covering his broad shoulders with his shield of bull's hide, watches for the arrows and the spears, and though he knows that the victory has turned, stands his ground to save his companions (16.358–363; see [[armed-in-bronze]] and [[that-turns-the-battle]]). Αἴας δʼ ὃ μέγας, “great Ajax”, is said only here in Homer, a few verses after the son of Oileus has killed Cleobulus (16.330–334; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The narrator does not say that Ajax throws. Their duel in Book 7 was parted by the heralds at nightfall, and in Book 14 Ajax felled Hector with a stone (7.273–282, 14.409–420; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). In the next book, when Hector has stripped Patroclus, Ajax comes up with his shield like a tower and Hector gives ground (17.125–131).

**The two Ajaxes.** Over Sarpedon's body Patroclus speaks first to the pair, “who were already eager themselves”: “Ajaxes, now let it be your pleasure to defend yourselves, / such as you were before among men, or even better. / There lies the man who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans, / Sarpedon” (16.555–559; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[sarpedon]]). At that wall Ajax had killed Sarpedon's companion Epicles with a boulder and stabbed Sarpedon's own shield (12.378–386, 12.400–405; see [[epicles]]).
