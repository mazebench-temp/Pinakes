---
summary: Meriones tells Aeneas that if his spear struck him he would give his soul “to Hades of the famous horses”; and the soul of Patroclus, as he dies, flies from his limbs and goes to Hades, lamenting its fate.
---

**Hades of the famous horses.** Aeneas throws at Meriones and misses, and mocks him as a dancer; Meriones answers that Aeneas too is mortal, and if he struck him, “you would give me triumph, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (16.617–625; see [[meriones]], [[aeneas]] and [[of-the-famous-horses]]). It is the end of the boast that Sarpedon made over Tlepolemus and Odysseus over Socus (5.654, 11.445), here spoken in a conditional and cut short by Patroclus: “For the outcome of war lies in our hands, and the outcome of words in council” (16.630; see [[the-outcome-of-war]] and [[patroclus]]).

**The soul of Patroclus.** “As he spoke, the end of death covered him, / and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.855–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]], [[psyche]] and [[patroclus]]). Ἄϊδος δέ is Ἄϊδόσδε, “to Hades”, with the suffix of direction printed apart, as where the souls of the dead “have gone down to Hades” (7.330; see the translation's notes on 16.188 and 16.855–857). The same three verses tell the death of Hector (22.361–363). In the first lines of the poem the wrath of Achilles “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls / of heroes” (1.3–4); the soul of his dearest companion goes the same way. Plato's Socrates quoted the two verses among those to be struck out of the poems so that the young would not fear death (*Republic* 3.386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).
