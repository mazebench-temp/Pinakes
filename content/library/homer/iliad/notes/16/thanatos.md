summary: Death covers the dying of the book in every form, and divides the mortal from the immortal. Hera asks whether Zeus would set a mortal free from “grim-sounding death”, Meriones reminds Aeneas, “you too are mortal”, and the mortal horse Pedasus keeps pace with the immortal ones until he is killed. The gods call Patroclus “to your death”; Death himself, with Sleep, carries Sarpedon home.
grc: [θνητὸς, θνητὸν, τεθνηώτων, κατατεθνηῶτι, τεθνηῶτα]
en: [mortal, if they were dead, dead man, though he was dead]
# θάνατον (16.454) and θανάτῳ (16.672, 16.682) are Death the god, printed lowercase, the twin of Sleep (see death).
except: [16.454, 16.672, 16.682]
---

**Mortal.** Hera puts the case against saving Sarpedon in the word: “A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion, / do you wish to set free again from grim-sounding death?” (16.441–442; see [[aisa]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). Meriones answers Aeneas' taunt, “for you too are mortal” (16.622), and the horse Pedasus, taken at the sack of Eetion's city, “mortal though he was, kept pace with the immortal horses” (16.154), until Sarpedon's spear kills him (16.467–469; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]).

**The dying.** The book tells death in all its formulas: “purple death” (16.334), “the black cloud of death” (16.350), “death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (16.414, 16.580), and “the end of death” for Sarpedon and Patroclus (16.502, 16.855; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], [[purple-death]] and [[the-end-of-death]]). Death is also what a man asks for without knowing it: Patroclus begs for “his own evil death and doom” (16.47), and Achilles wishes that not one of the Trojans or the Argives would escape death, but only the two of them (16.98–100; see [[if-only-father-zeus]]). When the gods call Patroclus “to your death” (θάνατον δέ, 16.693), Patroclus in turn tells Hector that “death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (16.853; see [[prophecy]]).

**The dead.** Achilles speaks of the fathers they would grieve for “if they were dead” (16.16); Glaucus and the armies fight “around the corpse of the dead man” (16.526, 16.565); and Hector speaks to Patroclus “though he was dead” (16.858). The twins Sleep and Death, persons here, carry Sarpedon's body to Lycia (16.454, 16.671–673, 16.681–683; see [[death]] and [[sleep]]).
