---
summary: When Zeus thinks of snatching his son Sarpedon from death, Hera answers that the man is mortal and fated, that the other gods will not approve, and that others will want to save their own sons; let him die, and send Death and Sleep to carry him home for burial. Zeus does not disobey.
refs: [16.433-438, 16.440-458]
passages:
  - 16.431-461 | Hera answers Zeus over Sarpedon
---

**His sister and wife.** Zeus, pitying Sarpedon and Patroclus as they rush at each other, speaks to “Hera, his sister and wife” (16.432; see [[zeus]], [[sarpedon]] and [[kasignetos]]), as he will again in Book 18 (18.356). He tells her that his heart is divided: should he snatch his son out of the battle and set him down in Lycia, or let him fall at the hands of Patroclus (16.433–438)? Over Hector in Book 22 he will put the same question to all the gods (22.174–176); here he asks Hera alone.

**The answer.** “Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him: / ‘Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!’” (16.439–440; see [[ox-eyed]], [[queenly]] and [[most-dread]]): the verses of her answers in Book 1, when Zeus rebuffs her questions, and in Book 18 (1.551–552, 18.360–361), and the second is also her reply to his proposal of peace in Book 4 (4.25), to his threats in Book 8 (8.462) and to his desire on Ida (14.330). She reminds him of what he already knows, that Sarpedon is “a man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion” (16.441; see [[aisa]]), and ends her first point with the dare of Book 4: “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (16.443 = 4.29; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). In Book 22 Athena will answer Zeus over Hector in the same three verses (22.179–181; see [[athena]]).

**Other gods' sons.** Her second point is the precedent: if Zeus sends Sarpedon home alive, another god may wish to save his own son, “for many are fighting around the great city of Priam, / sons of immortals, and you will stir a dread grudge in them” (16.448–449; see [[immortals]]). One of them has died already: in Book 15 Hera herself told Ares that his son Ascalaphus had perished in battle, “the dearest of men to him”, and Ares armed to avenge him against Zeus' command (15.110–142; see [[ares]] and [[ascalaphus]]). Aphrodite carried her son Aeneas out of the war in Book 5 (5.311–318; see [[aphrodite]]).

**The prize of the dead.** Her last point is a consolation. If Sarpedon is dear to Zeus, let him die at Patroclus' hands, and when his soul and life have left him, “send Death and sweet Sleep to carry him / until they come to the land of wide Lycia, / where his brothers and his kinsmen will give him burial / with a tomb and a pillar; for that is the prize of the dead” (16.454–457; see [[sleep]], [[death]], [[tomb]], [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). What she offers is what a mortal can have: not life, but burial among his own people. “So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey” (16.458; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]]). Zeus follows her counsel, and his orders to Apollo end in her two verses (16.456–457 = 16.674–675; see [[apollo]]).

**Hera's husband.** Achilles names Zeus by her: if “the loud-thundering husband of Hera” grants Patroclus glory, he is still to come back (16.88; see [[loud-thundering]]). The title ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης is given to Zeus in the Iliad here and at 7.411, 10.329 and 13.154.
