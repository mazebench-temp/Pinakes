The verses of the fall are those of Book 13, unchanged (16.482–486 = 13.389–393; see the translation's note on 13.389–393). What comes before them differs. Asius came on foot in front of his horses to rescue a fallen comrade, and Idomeneus was quicker and struck him in the throat (13.384–388); Sarpedon has leapt down from his chariot to meet Patroclus, and has twice missed him (16.426–427, 16.466–479). Patroclus' spear “did not fly from his hand in vain, / but hit him where the midriff closes around the throbbing heart” (16.480–481). Both men end where the verse puts them, lying at full length “in front of his horses and chariot”, and both roar as they lie (βεβρυχώς, 16.486).

What follows differs too. Asius died without a word, and his charioteer, struck out of his wits, could not turn the horses (13.394–399). The poem gives Sarpedon a second simile, of a tawny bull that a lion kills among the herd and that dies groaning under its jaws, and then a speech: dying, he calls Glaucus by name and charges him to fight for his body and his armor (16.487–501; see [[lion-among-cattle]], [[sarpedon]] and [[glaucus]]). The same verses served in Book 13 for a Trojan ally who died rushing at the Achaeans; the second time the man who falls is a son of Zeus, whose father has just consented to his death (16.431–461; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Walter Leaf reports that the two similes side by side have made critics suspect the first as borrowed from Book 13; he answered that without it Sarpedon's fall is never told, and that the two describe different moments, the fall and then the struggle on the ground (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.487).
