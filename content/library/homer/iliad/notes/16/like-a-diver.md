{{quote:88726e36-8b31-5ed7-8262-ca83d05a0d47}}

Patroclus leaps down from his chariot with a jagged stone in his hand and throws it at Hector's charioteer, Cebriones, Priam's bastard son, as he holds the reins. It strikes him on the forehead and crushes both his brows, the bone does not hold, and his eyes fall to the ground in the dust before his feet (16.733–742; see [[cebriones]]). Then the half-verse of Book 12: ὃ δʼ ἄρʼ ἀρνευτῆρι ἐοικὼς / κάππεσʼ ἀπʼ εὐεργέος δίφρου, λίπε δʼ ὀστέα θυμός, “and he, like a diver, / fell from the well-made chariot, and his spirit left his bones” (16.742–743). Epicles fell “from the high tower” of the Achaean wall (12.386); Cebriones falls from the chariot, and the rest of the two verses is the same.

{{quote:7ace1ba8-6033-5ce6-9afe-83ce1c31b052}}

This time someone in the poem sees the likeness. τὸν δʼ ἐπικερτομέων προσέφης Πατρόκλεες ἱππεῦ, “And taunting him you spoke, horseman Patroclus” (16.744; see [[kertomia]] and [[apostrophe]]): “Ah, truly the man is nimble; how easily he tumbles!” (16.745). κυβιστάω, “tumble, turn head over heels”, is the word for the pair of tumblers who whirl among the dancers on the shield of Achilles (κυβιστητῆρε, 18.605) and for the fish leaping in the river when it burns (21.354); the dive itself is the narrator's ἀρνευτήρ (see the translation's notes on 12.385 and 16.745–750). If the man were ever on the fish-filled sea, he would feed many, “diving for oysters, / leaping from a ship, even if the sea were rough” (16.747–748). τήθεα, a shellfish of uncertain kind, are named only here in the Iliad, and δυσπέμφελος, “rough”, of the sea, only here in Homer. The jest ends with a verdict on the whole people: “So there are tumblers among the Trojans too” (16.750). The narrator said “like a diver”; Patroclus makes the dead man a diver for oysters and an acrobat, and the Trojans a nation of tumblers. Walter Leaf suggests that the metaphor may be from tumbling rather than diving: from Cebriones' skill in tumbling on land, Patroclus concludes that he would make a good diver at sea as well (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.745). He also reports an ancient note on τήθεα: against those who gave the Iliad and the Odyssey to different poets, because the heroes of the Iliad never eat fish, it answered that the heroes know such food, since Patroclus names it, and that the poet left it out as mean (on 16.747; see [[fish]]).

Patroclus mocks no other man he kills. After the jest he leaps at the body with the spring of a lion that is struck in the chest, and he and Hector fight over it like two lions (16.751–758; see [[wounded-lion]] and [[two-lions]]). The tumbler lies still at the end of the fight, “and he in a whirl of dust / lay, great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.775–776; see [[great-over-a-great-space]]).
