---
summary: In the simile of the fight over Cebriones the East Wind and the South Wind strive with each other in the glens of a mountain to shake a deep forest, as the Trojans and Achaeans leap at one another.
passages:
  - 16.765-771 | The East Wind and the South Wind in the forest
---

“And as the East Wind and the South Wind strive with each other / in the glens of a mountain to shake a deep forest” (16.765–766), the oak, the ash and the smooth-barked cornel dash their branches together with a wondrous din, “so the Trojans and Achaeans, leaping at one another, / cut each other down” (16.767–771; see [[the-winds-in-the-forest]] and [[notus]]). The two verses of the battle were used at the start of the day (11.70–71). In Book 2 the same two winds rush down together from the clouds of Zeus and raise the long waves of the Icarian sea (2.144–146; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]]); here they are rivals, and the dual ἐριδαίνετον, “the two strive”, matches the two armies. In the Odyssey the winds play with Odysseus' raft, the South Wind tossing it to the North Wind and the East Wind yielding it to the West Wind to chase (*Odyssey* 5.331–332).
