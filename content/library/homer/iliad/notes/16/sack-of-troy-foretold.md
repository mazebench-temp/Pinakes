summary: In Book 16 an Achaean reaches the wall of Troy. Achilles wishes that he and Patroclus alone might “loose the sacred veil of Troy” (16.97–100); Patroclus would have taken the city, had Apollo not stood on the tower and told him that it was fated to fall neither to his spear nor to Achilles' (16.698–711); and Hector, over the dying Patroclus, mocks the sack he meant to bring (16.830–836).

**The veil of Troy.** Achilles ends his charge to Patroclus with a wish:

{{quote:86b96f9a-3ea6-5b6a-94dc-f082e7bee45c}}

Τροίης ἱερὰ κρήδεμνα λύωμεν, “loose the sacred veil of Troy” (16.100): the city's crown of walls is a woman's head-veil, and to sack it is to loose the veil (see the translation's note on 16.97–100). In the Odyssey Odysseus asks Athena to stand by him as she did “when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (*Odyssey* 13.388); in the Iliad the veil that falls is Andromache's, the κρήδεμνον that she throws from her head when she sees Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot (22.468–470; see [[andromache]]). Achilles wishes every Trojan and every Argive dead and the sack for himself and Patroclus alone. Walter Leaf reports that the four verses were athetized in antiquity: a scholium says that they were inserted by someone who believed that Achilles loved Patroclus, and that Aristarchus approved Zenodotus' suspicion; Leaf himself found the wish “not too extravagant for Achilles' passion” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.97; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]).

**At the wall.** Achilles has told Patroclus not to lead the way toward Ilios, lest a god come against him (16.91–94). Patroclus pursues the Trojans to the city, and the narrator brings the sack within a verse of happening:

{{quote:0a964183-ad4c-5154-8008-3186a243fda1}}

Three times Patroclus sets foot on the angle of the wall and three times Apollo thrusts him back; at the fourth the god shouts at him:

{{quote:17d579e4-dfa5-5152-a5b1-b109489b2ac8}}

οὔ νύ τοι αἶσα, “It is not your portion” (16.707; see [[aisa]]): the sack has its appointed hand and its appointed day, and Apollo names two men who will not have them, Patroclus and “Achilles, who is far better than you” (16.709; see [[apollo]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The scene is built on Diomedes' attack on Apollo in Book 5, three charges and a warning at the fourth (5.436–444; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Diomedes drew back “a little way”, Patroclus “a long way” (5.443, 16.710). Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus read “a little way” here too, and that Aristarchus defended the difference: Diomedes had Athena's words urging him on, Patroclus has Achilles' words holding him back (on 16.710).

**Again in Book 21.** The verse with which the city nearly falls returns when Achilles drives the Trojans to the gates: “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy” (16.698 = 21.544), had not Apollo roused Agenor to stand against him (21.544–546; see [[would-have-had-not]]). Apollo has gone into Ilios, “for he was concerned for the wall of the well-built city, / lest the Danaans sack it beyond fate that day” (21.515–517), as Zeus feared that Achilles might sack the wall “against fate” (20.30). The fall of Troy is fated; the gods keep it from coming before its time.

**Hector's boast.** Over the dying Patroclus Hector names the sack that Patroclus had meant to bring: “Patroclus, I suppose you said that you would ravage our city, / and take away the day of freedom from the Trojan women, / and bring them in your ships to your dear native land” (16.830–832; see [[hector]] and [[captive-women]]). ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, “the day of freedom”, is what Hector feared that some Achaean would take from Andromache (6.455; see [[eleutheros]]), and he boasts that he wards off from the Trojans ἦμαρ ἀναγκαῖον, “the day of compulsion” (16.835–836), as in Book 6 he wished to be dead before he heard her cry as she was dragged away (6.464–465). The boast is made over the man whose death will bring Achilles back to the war (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The plan.** Zeus' forecast in Book 15 set the counterattack that Book 16 begins on a course that ends in the sack: from the ships the Achaeans would drive the Trojans “always and without a break, until the Achaeans / take steep Ilios through the counsels of Athena” (15.69–71; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Patroclus brings the counterattack to the wall in a day; the city falls only after the poem has ended (see [[the-fall-of-troy]]).
