summary: Two prayers are heard. Achilles, with the cup kept for Zeus alone, prays to Zeus of Dodona that Patroclus may drive the battle from the ships and come back unharmed, and Zeus grants the one and refuses the other. Glaucus, wounded, prays to Apollo to heal him so that he can fight over Sarpedon, and Apollo heals him at once.

**Achilles.** The prayer is prepared at length. Achilles takes from the chest his mother gave him a cup that no other man drank from and from which he poured to no god but Zeus, cleanses it with sulfur and water, washes his own hands, draws the wine, and prays standing in the middle of the enclosure, pouring the wine and looking up to the sky (16.220–232; see [[chest]], [[cup]], [[libation]] and [[hand-washing]]). He calls on Zeus as lord of Dodona, “Pelasgian”, where the Selli are his interpreters (16.233–235; see [[dodona]] and [[selli]]). The claim and the request are the words of Chryses' second prayer to Apollo (16.236–238 ~ 1.453–455; see [[fulfill-this-wish]]). The wish has two parts: give Patroclus glory and courage, so that Hector may learn whether he can fight alone, and when he has driven the battle from the ships let him come back unharmed, “with all his armor and his companions who fight hand to hand” (16.241–248). “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him. / And the father granted him one thing, and refused him the other” (16.249–250; see [[hear-me]] and [[neuo]]). Achilles puts the cup away and stands in front of his hut to watch the battle (16.253–256).

**Glaucus.** Glaucus prays with no preparation at all, gripping the arm that Teucer's arrow wounded at the wall (16.508–512; 12.387–391). His claim on Apollo is not a sacrifice but the god's power and his own need: the god can hear “from everywhere / a man in sorrow”, and the best of men, Sarpedon, has perished, while Zeus “does not defend even his own son” (16.514–522). He asks to be healed so that he can rouse the Lycians and fight over the body (16.523–526), and Apollo stops the pains, dries the blood and puts might into his heart (16.527–529; see [[healing]]). The prayer ends in the verse of Chryses' prayers (16.527 = 1.43, 1.457).

**Other senses.** εὔχομαι is also “boast” and εὖχος “triumph” (see [[euchomai]]): Meriones says that Aeneas would give him “triumph” if he hit him, Apollo promises Hector “triumph” over Patroclus, and the dying Patroclus tells Hector, “Boast loudly now” (16.625, 16.725, 16.844).
