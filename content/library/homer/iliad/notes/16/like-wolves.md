The leaders of the Myrmidons rush out around Patroclus like a pack of wolves that have torn a great antlered stag in the mountains and go, jaws red with blood, to lap the dark water of a spring (16.155–167); and the Danaans fall on the Trojans like wolves on lambs and kids that have strayed through a shepherd's thoughtlessness (16.352–357).

**The pack at the spring.** Achilles goes through the huts and arms the Myrmidons, “and they, like wolves / that eat raw flesh, in whose hearts there is boundless courage, / that have cut down a great antlered stag in the mountains / and tear at it, and the jaws of all of them are red with blood” (16.156–159; see [[eating-raw-flesh]] and [[antlered]]). The pack goes together (ἀγεληδόν, “in a pack”, only here) to a spring of dark water, λάψοντες γλώσσῃσιν ἀραιῇσιν μέλαν ὕδωρ / ἄκρον ἐρευγόμενοι φόνον αἵματος, “to lap with their thin tongues the dark water / at its surface, belching out the gore of blood” (16.160–162). ἐρεύγομαι, “belch”, is said elsewhere of the sea spewing at a river's mouth (17.265). Their hearts are fearless, ἄτρομος, and their bellies gorged full (16.162–163). The tenor gives the pack its leaders: “such were the leaders and rulers of the Myrmidons / who, around the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus, / rushed on; and among them stood warlike Achilles” (16.164–166; see [[myrmidons]]).

The wolves of the simile have already killed and eaten, and the Myrmidons have not yet fought. What the likeness gives is the look of them, red-jawed, fearless and together, and the pack's way of hunting, as a band around its leaders. Walter Leaf calls it the one simile that presents two distinct scenes, the tearing of the stag and the rush to the spring, and with Hentze he condemned the second (16.160–163): glutted wolves going off to drink are no likeness for men eager to fight, and a pack does not suit a simile confined to the leaders (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.156). In the wolf similes of Books 4 and 11 both armies were wolves and tore at each other (4.471–472, 11.72–73); here the wolves are one side, and they have a quarry. A spring of dark water (κρήνη μελάνυδρος) is what Patroclus was likened to at the start of the book, weeping before Achilles (16.3; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]). The stag is the beast that jackals tear in the mountains when a hunter's arrow has brought it down (11.474–481; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]] and [[deer]]).

{{quote:2ba0f6d4-2dc3-5bd2-a40b-2aff9b9281c6}}

**Wolves among the lambs.** When the leaders of the Danaans have each killed his man, the second simile follows: ὡς δὲ λύκοι ἄρνεσσιν ἐπέχραον ἢ ἐρίφοισι / σίνται, “And as wolves fall upon lambs or kids, / ravaging”, snatching them out of the flocks, when in the mountains “they have been scattered through the shepherd's thoughtlessness; and the wolves see them / and at once tear them apart, since their hearts have no courage” (16.352–355; see [[lambs]] and [[goats]]). σίντης, “ravager”, is the lion's word elsewhere (11.481, 20.165). The lambs and kids have ἀνάλκιδα θυμόν, “a heart without courage”; the same words tell what Zeus later puts into Hector, when he turns him to flight (16.656).

The tenor takes up the wolves' verb: ὣς Δαναοὶ Τρώεσσιν ἐπέχραον, “so the Danaans fell upon the Trojans; and they remembered / the ill-sounding rout, and forgot their furious courage” (16.356–357). λάθοντο δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, “forgot their furious courage”, is the half-verse that told the Achaeans' panic when Apollo shook the aegis in their faces (15.322); Patroclus had told the Myrmidons to “remember” it (μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 16.270). The point of likeness is the helplessness of the prey: the Trojan battalions, broken and scattered, are a flock without a guardian.

The Iliad's other wolves are the same pack hunters (see [[wolves]]). The wolf and the lamb return as the type of enemies who cannot be reconciled, when Achilles refuses Hector's offer of an agreement: there are no oaths between lions and men, “nor do wolves and lambs have a like-minded heart” (22.262–264).
