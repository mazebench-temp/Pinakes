summary: Book 16 tells the death that Zeus foretold in Books 8 and 15, and the narrator announces it at every stage, from Patroclus' request for the armor to Apollo's blow. Patroclus keeps the first part of Achilles' charge and breaks the second; Apollo strips him of Achilles' armor, Euphorbus wounds him and Hector kills him beneath the walls of Troy, and dying he foretells Hector's death (16.777–867).

**Foretold at every stage.** Zeus has told Hera twice that Patroclus will die, the second time naming the killer and the place: Hector will kill him “before Ilios” (8.473–477, 15.64–68; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). In Book 16 the narrator keeps the end in view at each turn of the story. When Patroclus asks for the armor, “he was / begging for his own evil death and doom” (16.46–47; see [[nepios]]). When Achilles prays for his safe return, Zeus “refused that he should come back safe from the battle” (16.252). Over Sarpedon's body Zeus ponders “the killing of Patroclus” (16.647). When Patroclus pursues the Trojans, the narrator says that if he had kept Achilles' word, “he would have escaped the evil doom of black death” (16.686–687), and asks him whom he killed “when the gods called you to your death” (16.693). At the last charge, “then, Patroclus, the end of your life appeared” (16.787). The narrator speaks to Patroclus in the second person eight times in this book, and the last time is as he dies (16.843; see [[apostrophe]] and [[patroclus]]).

**The charge broken.** Achilles sends him out with a charge in two parts:

{{quote:312cdc68-2577-5bf5-bd90-a260e731064c}}

Patroclus keeps the first part. He drives the Trojans from the ships, puts out the fire, and pens them between the ships, the river and the wall (16.284–418). Then he breaks the second, part by part. He pursues the Trojans and Lycians toward the city (16.684–697); a god comes against him, and it is Apollo, who “loves them dearly” (16.94), standing on the tower (16.698–711; see [[apollo]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The narrator gives two causes for the same act: Patroclus “was greatly blinded, / the fool” (μέγʼ ἀάσθη, 16.685–686; see [[ate]]), and “the mind of Zeus is always stronger than that of men”, for Zeus himself let loose the spirit in his breast (16.688–691; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]).

**Three times, and the fourth.** The death is told in a pattern of three and four. Three times Patroclus sets foot on the angle of the wall and three times Apollo thrusts him back; at the fourth the god warns him off (16.702–711). Three times he charges the Trojans and kills nine men each time; at the fourth Apollo meets him in the mist (16.784–789; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Φοῖβος … δεινός, “Phoebus … terrible”: Walter Leaf notes that the adjective, held back to the start of the next verse, has an effect almost unique in the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.789).

**Achilles' armor taken off.** Nestor's plan rested on the armor: let Patroclus go out in Achilles' armor, “in the hope that, taking you for him, they may hold off from the war” (11.798–799; 16.40–42). The death undoes it piece by piece. Apollo's hand strikes his back; the helmet rolls under the horses' feet, the spear breaks, the shield falls, and the god looses the corselet (16.791–804; see [[helmet]] and [[corselet]]). Euphorbus does not stand against him, though he is γυμνός, “naked” (16.815; see [[euphorbus]]), and Antilochus will tell Achilles that they are fighting over a naked body, for Hector has the armor (18.20–21). The helmet that had guarded the head of a divine man Zeus now gives to Hector, “and his destruction was near him” (16.797–800). Hector strips the armor from the body and puts it on in Book 17 (17.125, 17.192–197).

**Three killers.** The narrative gives the blows in order: the god from behind, Euphorbus' spear between the shoulders, Hector's spear in the flank (16.791–822; see [[hector]]). Patroclus repeats the order in his answer to Hector's boast: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus; and you are the third to slay me” (16.849–850; see [[moira]]). His last words turn to Hector's own death: “death and mighty fate stand close beside you, / to be brought down at the hands of Achilles, noble grandson of Aeacus” (16.853–854; see [[prophecy]]).

**One death in another.** The same verse closes the death of Sarpedon, whom Patroclus killed, and of Patroclus, whom Hector kills: “As he spoke, the end of death covered” (16.502, 16.855; see [[the-end-of-death]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). The three verses of Patroclus' death, with the dying man's prophecy before them and the victor's answer to the dead after them, will be repeated for Hector (16.855–857 = 22.361–363), and Hector's answer, “Who knows if Achilles … / may not first be struck by my spear and lose his life?” (16.860–861), is the question to which Book 22 gives the answer (see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Achilles has not yet heard: the book ends with Hector chasing Automedon and the immortal horses carrying him away (16.862–867), and Antilochus brings the news only in Book 18 (18.18–21; see [[automedon]] and [[antilochus]]).
