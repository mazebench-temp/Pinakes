**The keeper of the Chimaera.** When the Achaean leaders each kill a man, Antilochus stabs Atymnius in the flank, and when his brother Maris stands over the body to defend it, Thrasymedes shatters his arm before he can strike (16.317–325; see [[antilochus]], [[thrasymedes]], [[atymnius-son-of-amisodarus]] and [[maris]]). The narrator then names their father: Amisodarus, ὅς ῥα Χίμαιραν / θρέψεν ἀμαιμακέτην, “who reared the Chimaera, / the irresistible” (16.328–329; see [[amisodarus]] and [[chimaera]]). The epithet ἀμαιμάκετος is the one the Chimaera had in Glaucus' story, where the king of Lycia sent Bellerophon “to kill the irresistible Chimaera” (6.179); in the Iliad it is said of nothing else. Glaucus called her “of divine race, not of men” (6.180). Book 16 gives her a keeper in Lycia, a man who “reared” her (θρέψεν), and calls her πολέσιν κακὸν ἀνθρώποισιν, “an evil to many men” (16.329), a half-verse found only here.

**Beside Bellerophon's grandson.** The sons of the man who reared the monster are ἐσθλοὶ ἑταῖροι, “brave companions”, of Sarpedon (16.327; see [[sarpedon]]), and Sarpedon is Bellerophon's grandson, the son of his daughter Laodameia by Zeus (6.196–199; see [[laodameia]] and [[bellerophon]]). The story that Glaucus told Diomedes as a lineage reaches into the battle: the sons of the Chimaera's keeper and the grandson of her killer fight side by side against the Achaeans. The two brothers are brought down by two brothers, the sons of Nestor (δοιοῖσι κασιγνήτοισι δαμέντε, 16.326), and they “went down to Erebus” (16.327; see [[erebus]]), the only verse of the Iliad in which the dead are said to go to Erebus. Sarpedon himself dies later in the book (see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]).

**Unexpected lines.** Walter Leaf reports that the four verses were rejected by Payne Knight, and notes that Amisodarus is not named in Glaucus' account of the Chimaera, where she seems a wild creature rather than one nurtured by a man; he observes also that Amisodarus is a genuine non-Greek name, like Pixodarus of Caria (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.326–329).
