{{quote:255fa8de-b1c5-5401-8b68-16dd146ea25c}}

ὅσση δʼ αἰγανέης ῥιπὴ ταναοῖο τέτυκται, / ἥν ῥά τʼ ἀνὴρ ἀφέῃ πειρώμενος ἢ ἐν ἀέθλῳ / ἠὲ καὶ ἐν πολέμῳ δηΐων ὕπο θυμοραϊστέων, / τόσσον ἐχώρησαν Τρῶες, ὤσαντο δʼ Ἀχαιοί: “and as far as the rush of a long javelin carries, / which a man throws, trying his strength, either in a contest / or in war, under the attack of enemies who destroy the spirit, / so far the Trojans gave ground, and the Achaeans pushed them back” (16.589–592). The αἰγανέη is a light javelin, made for throwing: the Myrmidons amused themselves with discuses and javelins on the shore while Achilles kept from the war (2.773–775), and the suitors throw them in front of Odysseus' house (*Odyssey* 4.625–627). ῥιπή is the rush of a thing flung, as the flight of the stone with which Hector breaks the gate of the wall (12.462). ταναός, “long”, is the javelin's length, and πειρώμενος, “trying”, is the throw made to see how far it will go, as in the cast that measured Apollo's causeway, σθένεος πειρώμενος, “to test his strength” (15.359).

The simile gives the throw two settings, the contest and the battle, and the second is drawn out: a javelin thrown “under the attack of enemies who destroy the spirit”. Walter Leaf takes πειρώμενος, “trying his strength”, as a third: a throw made in practice, for a man's own satisfaction, as opposed to a contest (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.590). The Iliad's distances are measured in this way, in a spear's cast, a stone's throw, the run of a discus (3.12, 15.358–359, 23.431–432), and here the measure is of ground lost. The Trojans give way only a javelin's cast: Glaucus turns at once and kills Bathycles, and the fight over Sarpedon goes on (16.593–602; see [[glaucus]] and [[bathycles]]).

The retreat follows an exchange of stones over Sarpedon's body. Hector has killed the Myrmidon Epeigeus with a stone, and Patroclus, swooping like a hawk, kills Sthenelaus with another; “the champions and shining Hector gave ground” (16.577–588; see [[like-a-hawk]], [[epeigeus]] and [[sthenelaus]]).
