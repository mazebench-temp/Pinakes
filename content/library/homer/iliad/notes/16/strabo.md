---
summary: Strabo reads Book 16 as a geographer. Achilles' prayer to the Zeus of Dodona, “Pelasgian”, proves to him that Dodona was a Pelasgian foundation, and its interpreters “with unwashed feet, sleeping on the ground” that its people were barbarians, though whether Homer called them Selli or Helli the writing cannot settle. Menesthius, son of the Spercheius and of Achilles' sister, shows the river to be Achilles' own; the Pelian ash is a spear for throwing; and Amydon, Asius son of Dymas and Cebriones take him to the Axius, to Phrygia and to the Troad.
---

- **The Pelasgian Zeus.** Strabo cites Achilles' address twice as evidence that the Pelasgians once held Dodona. In the fifth book of the *Geography*, writing of the Pelasgians in Italy, he says that almost all agree they were an ancient people spread over all Greece, that Thessaly is called Pelasgian Argos, and that the poet himself names the Zeus of Dodona Pelasgian, “Lord Zeus, of Dodona, Pelasgian” (16.233; *Geography* 5.2.4). In his account of Epirus (*Geography* 7.7.10) he reports Ephorus' view that the oracle at Dodona was a foundation of the Pelasgians, the most ancient of the peoples who held power in Greece, and sets beside Homer's verse a line of Hesiod that calls Dodona and its oak “the seat of the Pelasgians”. He also reports that one Suidas, courting the Thessalians with tales, said that the sanctuary had been moved from the Pelasgian country about Scotussa in Thessaly, that most of the women came with it, and that Zeus was called Pelasgian for that reason (7.7.12). See [[dodona]], [[pelasgians]] and [[zeus]].
- **Selli or Helli.** In the same chapter (7.7.10) Strabo says that Homer shows from their way of life that the people round the sanctuary were barbarians, when he calls them “with unwashed feet, sleeping on the ground” (16.235; see [[with-unwashed-feet]]). Whether they should be called Helli, as Pindar calls them, or Selli, as people suppose the name stands in Homer, the writing is ambiguous and does not allow one to be sure. Philochorus says that the country about Dodona, like Euboea, was called Hellopia, for Hesiod speaks of it so: “There is a land Hellopia, rich in grain and in good meadows; and there at its edge is built Dodona.” Apollodorus says that the name was thought to come from the marshes (ἕλη) about the sanctuary, but holds that the poet called the people there Selli, not Helli, adding that the poet also names a river Selleis (2.659, 15.531; see [[selleis-ephyra]]). That river, the chapter goes on, is by the Ephyra in Elis, and there is none in Thesprotia or among the Molossians (see the Book 15 note). Elsewhere, praising Homer's order in naming peoples, Strabo counts among them the Selli round Dodona, as far as the Achelous (1.2.20). The two readings differ only in how the letters are divided: ἀμφὶ δὲ Σελλοί, “and around you the Selli” (16.234), and ἀμφὶ δέ σʼ Ἑλλοί, “and around you the Helli”, are written alike. See [[selli]].
- **The interpreters.** In the next chapter (*Geography* 7.7.11) Strabo says that Dodona lies under Mount Tomarus, and that from it, some say, the interpreters of Zeus whom the poet calls unwashed of feet and sleeping on the ground were called τόμουροι. Some even write the word into the Odyssey, where Amphinomus would have the suitors ask whether the τόμουροι of great Zeus approve of killing Telemachus. Strabo keeps the reading θέμιστες, taken loosely of the god's oracular commands, like the counsel of Zeus that Odysseus went to Dodona to hear from the oak (*Odyssey* 16.403, 14.327–328). At first, he adds, men gave the oracles, “and perhaps the poet shows this too, for he calls them ὑποφῆται”; later three old women were appointed, when Dione was made to share the temple with Zeus (7.7.12, on 16.235).
- **Menesthius and the Spercheius.** The Spercheius, Strabo argues, was a river of Achilles' own country, as Homer shows by Achilles' saying that he was growing his hair for it (23.141–151), and by calling Menesthius, one of his captains, the son of the Spercheius and of Achilles' sister (*Geography* 9.5.9, on 16.173–176). The Myrmidons, he adds, were probably all those under Achilles and Patroclus who had followed Peleus into exile from Aegina. See [[menesthius-son-of-spercheius]], [[spercheius]] and [[myrmidons]].
- **Rivers fallen from Zeus.** Homer calls the rivers διιπετέες, “fallen from Zeus”, Strabo explains, not the torrents only but all rivers alike, since all are filled by rain; and in the Nile the epithet is apt twice over (*Geography* 1.2.30). Book 16 gives it to the Spercheius (16.174; see [[fallen-from-zeus]]).
- **The Pelian ash.** The Euboeans, Strabo says, were good at close fighting with thrusting spears, as the Catalogue shows (2.543). Spears for throwing were perhaps of another kind, as the Pelian ash is likely to have been, which, as the poet says, “Achilles alone knew how to wield it” (16.142); and the man who says that he throws a spear as far as no other man can shoot an arrow means a throwing spear (*Geography* 10.1.13; *Odyssey* 8.229). In the later books Achilles does throw it, at Asteropaeus and at Hector (21.161–172, 22.273–277). See [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[spear]].
- **Amydon.** Pyraechmes, the first man Patroclus hits, had led the Paeonians “from Amydon, from the broad-flowing Axius” (16.287–288). In the excerpts that preserve the lost end of his seventh book, Strabo places Amydon on the Axius: a fortified place, called Abydon in his day and razed by the Argeadae, from which, Homer says, the Paeonians came to Troy as allies. He quotes the Catalogue's form of the verse (2.849) and discusses the reading of the next (*Geography* 7, fragments 20, 21 and 23). See [[amydon]], [[axius]], [[paeonians]] and [[pyraechmes]].
- **Asius, son of Dymas.** Listing the names that Thracians and Trojans share, Strabo notes that the poet has a second Asius beside the son of Hyrtacus, the one “who was the uncle of Hector, tamer of horses, / own brother of Hecuba, and son of Dymas, / who lived in Phrygia by the streams of the Sangarius” (16.717–719; *Geography* 13.1.21). Apollo takes his likeness to rouse Hector (16.715–725). See [[asius-son-of-dymas]], [[dymas]] and [[sangarius]].
- **Cebriones.** Demetrius of Scepsis, Strabo reports, thought that the land about Ilium ruled by Hector reached as far as Cebrenia, below Dardania, where the tombs of Alexander and Oenone were shown; and that the poet's “Cebriones, the bastard son of famous Priam” (16.738) was probably named after the district or, more likely, its city, Cebrene (*Geography* 13.1.33). See [[cebriones]].
