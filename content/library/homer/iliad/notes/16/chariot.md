**The team.** Xanthus and Balius go under the yoke, and Pedasus, taken by Achilles at the sack of Eetion's city, runs beside them in the trace harness, “and who, mortal though he was, kept pace with the immortal horses” (16.148–154; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[pedasus-the-horse]]). When Sarpedon's spear kills him, the yoke creaks and the reins are tangled, “since the trace horse lay in the dust”, until Automedon draws his sword “and darted in and cut the trace horse loose” (16.467–475; see [[automedon]] and [[tekmor]]). Nestor did the same in Book 8, when Paris' arrow killed his trace horse: he cut away its harness with his sword, “darting at it” (8.87–88).

**Chariots in the rout.** In the ditch many horses break their lords' chariots “at the end of the pole” and leave them, and men fall from their chariots under the axles while the chariots overturn (16.370–379; see the translation's note on 16.371). Thestor, crouching in his chariot with the reins slipped from his hands, is dragged over the rail on Patroclus' spear like a fish on a line (16.402–410; see [[the-fisherman]]). Sarpedon and Patroclus leap down from their chariots to fight on foot (16.426–427), and so do Patroclus and Hector over Cebriones (16.733, 16.755).

**The charioteer.** Cebriones holds the reins when the stone strikes him, and he falls “from the well-made chariot” like a diver; Patroclus mocks the fall, and the narrator closes over his body with his lost art, “forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.737–750, 16.776; see [[cebriones]] and [[great-over-a-great-space]]). Euphorbus, on the other side, excelled in horsemanship, and brought down twenty men from their chariots when he first came “with his chariot” (16.808–811).
