summary: Achilles prays to the Zeus of Dodona, and Zeus grants half of the prayer. He pities his son Sarpedon and thinks of saving him from his fate, yields to Hera and honors him with a rain of blood; he stretches night over the fight for the body, decides that Patroclus shall first drive the Trojans to the city, turns Hector to flight and sends Apollo to bring the body home; at the end he gives Hector the helmet of Achilles.

**The prayer.** Before the Myrmidons go out, Achilles takes from the chest that Thetis gave him a cup from which no other man drank, “nor did he pour libations from it to any god, except father Zeus” (16.225–227; see [[cup]], [[libation]] and [[thetis]]). He cleanses it with sulfur and water, washes his hands, pours the wine “looking up to the sky” and prays (16.228–232; see [[hand-washing]], [[prayer]] and [[sky]]), in the verses in which Priam will pray before he sets out for Achilles' hut (24.306–307). He calls on the god of the oracle in the northwest, far from Troy: “Lord Zeus, of Dodona, Pelasgian, who dwell far away, / ruling over wintry Dodona” (16.233–234; see [[dodona]], [[pelasgians]] and [[selli]]). Zeus has heard him once before, “and you honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans hard; / so now once more fulfill for me this wish” (16.237–238): the verses in which Chryses, his first prayer answered by the plague, prays to Apollo to end it (1.454–455). Achilles' first prayer was the one Thetis carried to Olympus (1.407–412, 1.503–510; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Now he asks glory for Patroclus from “far-seeing Zeus” (16.241; see [[far-seeing]]), and that he come back unharmed with his armor and his men (16.246–248).

{{quote:94f9322a-7ea1-5f54-9685-301dbba8cdca}}

The formula of the answered prayer, “and Zeus the counselor heard him” (see [[counselor]]), is the one in which Apollo hears Chryses (1.43, 1.457) and, later in this book, Glaucus (16.527; see [[apollo]]). The answer is not told to Achilles. He goes back into the hut, puts away the cup and comes out to watch the battle (16.253–256; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[achilles]]).

**Zeus in the speeches.** The men name Zeus as the power behind the battle. Patroclus asks whether Achilles is shunning an oracle, and whether “your lady mother has told you something from Zeus” (16.37); Achilles denies it in the same words (16.51). If Zeus, “the loud-thundering husband of Hera”, grants Patroclus glory, he must still come back (16.87–90; see [[loud-thundering]]), and Achilles' wish that only he and Patroclus might survive the war begins with a prayer, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (16.97; see [[if-only-father-zeus]]). Ajax, worn out at the ships, is overpowered by “the mind of Zeus and the noble Trojans” (16.103), and when Hector lops the head off his spear he knows “the work of the gods, how he was utterly cutting short the plans of his battle, / Zeus who thunders on high, and wished victory for the Trojans” (16.120–121; see [[ajax]] and [[thunders-on-high]]). Glaucus, praying to Apollo, says of Sarpedon that Zeus “does not defend even his own son” (16.522; see [[glaucus]]).

**Weather.** Three similes of the book show Zeus as the god of the sky. He moves a thick cloud from a mountain peak and the upper air breaks open, as the Danaans breathe again when the fire is put out (16.297–302; see [[the-cloud-lifted-from-the-peak]] and [[who-gathers-the-lightning]]); a cloud comes from Olympus “when Zeus spreads a storm” (16.364–365; see [[the-cloud-from-olympus]] and [[olympus]]); and in late summer he pours down rain, “when in resentment he grows angry with men / who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice, caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (16.386–388), until the rivers flood and the works of men are diminished (see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). Justice, δίκη, is named here for the first time in the Iliad (see the translation's note on 16.384–392). Hesiod speaks of the judges who take gifts in the same words: they give their rulings (κρίνωσι θέμιστας) with crooked judgments and drive Justice out (ἐξελάσωσι), and on the city of such men Zeus sends famine and plague (*Works and Days* 220–224, 238–247; see [[hesiod]] and [[themis]]).

**Sarpedon.** When Sarpedon and Patroclus rush at each other, Zeus pities them, and speaks to “Hera, his sister and wife” (16.431–432; see [[hera]] and [[pity]]):

{{quote:f1d7dbd3-4d36-5292-ad17-c111ab0fe5d3}}

He foretold the death himself in Book 15 (15.67), and twice he has kept it off: in Book 5 “his father still kept ruin away” (5.662), and at the wall “Zeus warded off the fates / from his son” (12.402–403; see [[sarpedon]]). To snatch a man from battle and set him down in safety is what gods do for those they love: Aphrodite snatches Paris from Menelaus “and set him down in his fragrant, perfumed chamber” (3.380–382), and Apollo shelters Aeneas (5.344–346). Hera answers that the man is mortal and long since ordained to his portion, and that if Zeus saves his son, other gods will want to save theirs; let him die, and then send Death and Sleep to carry him home (16.440–457; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]], [[aisa]], [[sleep]] and [[death]]). “So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey” (16.458; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]]). He sheds “bloody raindrops on the earth, / honoring his dear son” (16.459–460), as at the start of Book 11 he sent down dewdrops “wet with blood from the upper air” before a day of slaughter (11.53–54). In Book 22 the scene is repeated for Hector: Zeus grieves, asks whether to save him, and Athena answers in Hera's words (22.168–181). Plato's Socrates quoted Zeus' lament for Sarpedon among the verses in which the poets make the greatest of the gods mourn unworthily (*Republic* 3.388c–d; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]), and Roman readers took the scene as a lesson in fate (see [[jupiter-and-sarpedon]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]).

**Night over the body.** “And Zeus stretched deadly night over the fierce battle, / so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.567–568), as Ares shrouded the battle in night to help the Trojans in Book 5 (5.506–507; see [[ares]]). In Book 17 he will pour mist around the Achaeans who fight over Patroclus, for he did not hate the son of Menoetius (17.268–270).

**The pondering.** While they fight over the body, Zeus watches: “nor did Zeus ever / turn his shining eyes away from the fierce battle” (16.644–645). In Book 13 he had turned them away, to the Thracians and the Abii, and Poseidon came to help the Achaeans (13.3–16; see [[poseidon]]). Now he ponders whether Hector should kill Patroclus at once, over Sarpedon, or whether Patroclus should drive the Trojans further, and “this seemed to him the better way”: that he should drive them back to the city “and take the lives of many” (16.646–655). μερμηρίζω, “ponder”, is the verb of the night in Book 2, when Zeus pondered how to honor Achilles and sent the Dream (2.3). He puts a craven heart into Hector, who turns to flight, “for he knew the sacred scales of Zeus” (16.656–658; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[hector]]), and the Lycians flee from their dead king, on whom many had fallen “when the son of Cronus stretched tight the mighty strife” (16.659–662; see [[cronus]]).

**Apollo sent.** Then “Zeus the cloud-gatherer” calls Apollo “dear Phoebus” and gives him the burial in order: cleanse the dark blood from Sarpedon, wash him in the river, anoint him with ambrosia, clothe him in immortal clothing, and send him with “Sleep and Death, the twins” to Lycia, where his kin will bury him (16.666–675; see [[cloud-gatherer]], [[ambrosia]] and [[apollo]]). The god does each thing in the verses of the command (16.676–683).

**The mind of Zeus.** Patroclus, “the fool”, forgets Achilles' word and pursues the Trojans, and the narrator gives the reason: “But the mind of Zeus is always stronger than that of men” (16.686–688; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[noos]]). Zeus puts even a valiant man to flight and takes away his victory, and at another time urges him on to fight, “and he it was who then too let loose the spirit in his breast” (16.689–691; see the translation's note on 16.690). In the end it is the gods together who call Patroclus “to your death” (16.693), and Apollo who strikes him. The helmet of Achilles falls in the dust, “but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.799–800; see [[helmet]]). Patroclus himself names the givers of Hector's victory: “Zeus, son of Cronus, and Apollo, who brought me down / easily” (16.845–846).

**Zeus of Ida.** Laogonus, killed by Meriones, was the son of Onetor, “who was the priest of Zeus / of Ida, and was honored by the people like a god” (16.604–605; see [[laogonus]], [[onetor]] and [[priesthood]]). Both armies pray to “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida” (3.276, 3.320, 7.202), and Zeus has a precinct and a fragrant altar on Gargarus (8.47–48; see [[ida]] and [[gargarus]]). Hecuba bids Priam pray to the son of Cronus Ἰδαῖος, “of Ida”, who looks down on all Troy (24.290–291).
