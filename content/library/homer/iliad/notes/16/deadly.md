**Zeus.** “And Zeus stretched deadly night over the fierce battle, / so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.567–568; [[krateros]], [[war-and-night]], [[sarpedon]]): the god darkens the fight for his son's body, and the adjective comes twice in two verses.

**Apollo.** The sons of the Achaeans would have taken Troy “had not Phoebus Apollo stood on the well-built tower, / intending his destruction, and helping the Trojans” (16.700–701; [[phoebus]], [[well-built]]). ὀλοὰ φρονέων is the mind bent on harm that the entry names.

**The rout and the fate.** Over Cebriones, “neither side thought of deadly rout” (16.771; [[phobos]]). Patroclus' last speech ranks his killers: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus; and you are the third to slay me” (16.849–850; [[moira]], [[leto]], [[euphorbus]]). The forms ὀλοός, ὀλοή and the phrase ὀλοὰ φρονέων are new to the entry.
