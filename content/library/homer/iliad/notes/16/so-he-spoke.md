summary: Two speeches of Book 16 end with the speaker's death: Sarpedon and Patroclus are covered by “the end of death” in the closing verse itself (16.502, 16.855). The other closings turn at once to the effect, a prayer heard, an obedience, a rally, a retreat; the first carries the narrator's judgment of Patroclus, “great fool that he was”.

# ἦ ῥα καὶ ἐν Τρώεσσι κυβιστητῆρες ἔασιν (16.750): the particle "truly", inside Patroclus' taunt, not a closing.
except: [16.750]
---

**Death in the closing verse.** Sarpedon, dying, charges Glaucus to fight for his body, and the verse that closes his speech closes his life: “As he spoke, the end of death covered / his eyes and nostrils” (ὣς ἄρα μιν εἰπόντα τέλος θανάτοιο κάλυψεν, 16.502–503; see [[sarpedon]]). Patroclus, dying, foretells Hector's death at the hands of Achilles, and the same verse closes his speech: “As he spoke, the end of death covered him” (16.855; see [[patroclus]]). It returns once more, when Hector dies foretelling the death of Achilles (22.361). The formula that usually turns from a speech to its effect turns here to the speaker's end, and the next speech is made to a dead man: “And to him, though he was dead, shining Hector spoke” (16.858). See [[the-end-of-death]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]].

**The narrator's judgment.** The first closing of the book adds a verdict on the speaker. Patroclus has asked for Achilles' armor, “So he spoke, begging, great fool that he was; for truly he was / begging for his own evil death and doom” (16.46–47; see [[nepios]] and [[ker-doom]]).

**Speech and effect.** The other closings name the effect in the same verse. Two prayers are heard: “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him” (16.249) and “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (16.527; see [[prayer]] and [[hear-me]]). Zeus yields to Hera over Sarpedon in the verse with which he yielded to her in Book 4, “So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey” (16.458 = 4.68; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]), and Apollo obeys Zeus in the verse of Book 15, “So he spoke, and Apollo did not fail to heed his father” (16.676 = 15.236; see [[peitho]]). Achilles' and Patroclus' speeches to the Myrmidons end in the verse of the rally, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (16.210, 16.275; see [[exhortation]]). Glaucus' rebuke ends in grief, “So he spoke, and grief seized the Trojans from head to foot” (ὣς ἔφατο, 16.548; see [[penthos]]), and Apollo's warning in a retreat, “So he spoke, and Patroclus gave ground a long way back” (16.710; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). The talk of Achilles and Patroclus ends in the verse that closed the talk of the two Ajaxes, “So they spoke such words to one another” (16.101 = 13.81), and the story turns to Ajax at the ships.

**Not a closing.** Patroclus' taunt over Cebriones ends ἦ ῥα καὶ ἐν Τρώεσσι κυβιστητῆρες ἔασιν, “So there are tumblers among the Trojans too” (16.750), where ἦ ῥα is the particle “truly”, as at 14.49. The same three words close Sarpedon's rebuke of the Lycians: “He spoke, and leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor” (ἦ ῥα, καὶ ἐξ ὀχέων, 16.426).
