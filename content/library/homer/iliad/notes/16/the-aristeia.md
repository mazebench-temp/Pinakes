---
summary: Book 16 is the aristeia of Patroclus, from the arming in Achilles' armor to his death. It has every part of the scene: the shining start, the rout, the great opponent, a son of Zeus; the charges against a god, three times and a fourth; and an end in death, at the hands of a god and two men.

**The shining start.** Patroclus arms in Achilles' armor, all but the spear (16.130–144; see [[arming-scene]] and [[the-pelian-ash]]), and the Myrmidons pour out like wasps by the wayside (16.259–267). The light of the start is the armor itself: the Trojans see “the valiant son of Menoetius, / himself and his attendant, gleaming in their armor”, and think that Achilles has thrown away his wrath (16.278–283; see [[exchange-of-armor]]).

**The rout.** His first spear kills Pyraechmes, the leader of the Paeonians, beside the stern of Protesilaus' ship; the fire is put out and the half-burned ship left standing (16.284–296; see [[ships]]). The leaders of the Achaeans each kill a man (16.306–351), the Trojans break back over the ditch, and their chariots overturn in it (16.364–383). Then Patroclus cuts off the foremost battalions and hems them in “between / the ships and the river and the high wall”, and kills them there, Pronous, Thestor, whom he drags from his chariot on the spear like a fish on a line, Erylaus, and nine more in three verses (16.394–418; see [[androktasia]] and [[poine]]).

**The great opponent.** Sarpedon, son of Zeus, comes against him, and the gods take part: Zeus wonders whether to save his son, Hera warns him, and he sheds bloody raindrops in his honor (16.419–461; see [[sarpedon]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). Patroclus kills him, and the fight over the body fills the middle of the book (16.462–683; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The prayer and the god's help belong here to the other side: Glaucus, wounded, prays to Apollo and is healed (16.508–531; see [[healing]] and [[prayer]]).

**Against the gods.** Forgetting Achilles' word, Patroclus pursues the Trojans to the city; nine more names fall under the narrator's question (16.684–697; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]), and three times he climbs the wall of Troy before Apollo turns him back at the fourth (16.698–711; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). He kills Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, with a stone, and fights Hector over the body (16.733–782). Then three times he charges and three times kills nine men, and at the fourth “the end of your life appeared” (16.784–787).

**The end.** Apollo strikes him and strips the armor from him, Euphorbus wounds him, and Hector kills him (16.788–857; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The lion similes turn with the day: when he leaps at Cebriones he is a lion that ravages the farmsteads and is struck in the chest, “and its own courage destroys it” (16.752–753), and at his death he is the boar that a lion overpowers at a spring (16.823–828; see [[wounded-lion]] and [[lions-and-boars]]). The ancient title of the book is his name, Πατρόκλεια (see [[title-of-book-16]]).
