**The bearer.** Hera's counsel to Zeus is to let his son die and then “send Death and sweet Sleep to carry him / until they come to the land of wide Lycia, / where his brothers and his kinsmen will give him burial / with a tomb and a pillar; for that is the prize of the dead” (16.454–457; see [[hera]], [[sarpedon]], [[sleep]] and [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]). Zeus gives the order to Apollo, and Apollo hands the washed and anointed body to the swift escorts, “with Sleep and Death, the twins”, who set it down in Lycia (16.666–683; see [[zeus]], [[apollo]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). This is the only scene of the Iliad in which Death acts as a person, and he does not kill: Patroclus' spear has done that (16.480–505), and Death's errand is to bring the body home (see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[tomb]]).

**The thing itself.** Around this scene the book is full of death that is not a person. It seizes the eyes, “purple death and mighty fate” (16.334); “the black cloud of death” enfolds a man (16.350); “death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (16.414, 16.580); “the end of death covered” Sarpedon and then Patroclus (16.502, 16.855; see [[the-end-of-death]]). Patroclus begged for “his own evil death and doom” (16.47), the gods called him “to your death” (16.693), and he dies telling Hector that “death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (16.853; see [[thanatos]], [[moira]] and [[ker-doom]]). Only for Sarpedon, the son of Zeus, does Death come in person, and then as a bearer.
