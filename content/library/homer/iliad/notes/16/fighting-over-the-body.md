---
summary: Two great fights over a body fill the second half of the book. Over Sarpedon the Lycians and the Myrmidons fight until he cannot be recognized under missiles, blood and dust; the Achaeans take his armor, but Apollo carries the body away for burial. Over Cebriones, Hector holds the head and Patroclus the foot, and the Achaeans drag him away and strip him. The fight over Patroclus himself begins in the last verses.

**Sarpedon.** The dying man gives the order for his own defense. He charges Glaucus to rouse the Lycian leaders “to fight around Sarpedon”, and warns him of the shame if the Achaeans strip him of his armor (16.492–501; see [[exhortation]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). Glaucus rallies the Lycians and the Trojans, Patroclus the two Ajaxes, and the armies meet “around the corpse of the dead man”, while Zeus stretches deadly night over the battle “so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.532–568; see [[war-and-night]]). The price of the body is paid on both sides: Epeigeus, a Myrmidon, is killed by Hector's stone “as he took hold of the corpse” (16.577–580), and Bathycles and Laogonus fall after him (16.593–607). The din rises like that of woodcutters in the mountains; no one could recognize Sarpedon now, “since with missiles and blood and dust / he was wrapped from his head to the tips of his feet”; and the men throng around him like flies around the milk pails (16.633–644; see [[the-woodcutters]] and [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). Zeus decides the fight: he puts a craven heart in Hector, the Lycians flee, and the Achaeans take the armor from the body, and Patroclus gives it to his companions to carry to the ships (16.644–665). The body itself is taken by a god. Apollo lifts it out of the missiles, washes it in the river, anoints it with ambrosia, clothes it, and gives it to Sleep and Death to carry home to Lycia (16.666–683; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The enemy wins the armor; the body is saved.

**Cebriones.** The second fight is told with animals and winds. Patroclus leaps at the body with the spring of a lion, Hector leaps from his chariot, and the two fight over it “like lions, / which on the peaks of a mountain, over a slain deer, / both of them hungry, fight in high spirits” (16.752–758; see [[two-lions]]). “Hector, once he had taken hold of his head, would not let go; / and Patroclus on the other side held his foot” (16.762–763), and the armies clash around them like the East and South Winds in a forest (16.765–771). The dead charioteer lies “great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.776; see [[great-over-a-great-space]]). At the turn of the afternoon the Achaeans drag him out of the missiles and strip him (16.777–782).

**Patroclus.** Hector, when he has killed Patroclus, sets his heel on the body to draw out his spear and pushes him off it on his back, then goes after Automedon and the horses (16.862–867). Hector holding Cebriones by the head looks forward to Book 17, where he tries to drag Patroclus' body away to cut off its head (17.125–127).
