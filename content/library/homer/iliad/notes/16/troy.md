summary: Achilles wishes that he and Patroclus alone might “loose the sacred veil of Troy”, but warns Patroclus not to lead the way toward Ilios. Patroclus pursues the Trojans to the walls, and the Achaeans would have taken “high-gated Troy” had not Apollo stood on the tower and told him that it was not his portion to sack the city, nor Achilles'.

**The sacred veil.** Achilles ends his answer to Patroclus with a wish: that every Trojan and every Argive might die, “so that we alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy!” (16.97–100; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). Τροίης ἱερὰ κρήδεμνα, “the sacred veil of Troy”, is the city's crown of walls pictured as a woman's head-veil, and to loose it is to take the city (see the translation's note on 16.97–100 and [[veil]]). In the same speech he forbids Patroclus to “lead the way on toward Ilios”, lest a god come against him, “far-working Apollo loves them dearly” (16.91–94).

**Toward the city.** Patroclus first keeps the routed Trojans from the city, “nor would he let them / set foot in the city, as they longed to” (16.395–396). Zeus decides that he shall drive them “toward the city” before he dies (16.654–655; see [[zeus]]), and then he forgets the warning and pursues them (16.684–687):

Three times he sets foot on the angle of the wall and three times Apollo drives him back, and the fourth time the god tells him that the city of the proud Trojans is not to be sacked by his spear, “nor by Achilles, who is far better than you” (16.702–709; see [[apollo]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The poem does not tell the sack; Zeus has told Hera that the Achaeans will take “steep Ilios” through the counsels of Athena (15.70–71; see [[the-fall-of-troy]]).

**The city behind the battle.** The city appears in the speeches of both sides. Patroclus first hoped to drive the Trojans “back to the city” (16.45); Achilles speaks of “the whole city of the Trojans” come out against the ships (16.69); Hera warns Zeus that many sons of gods fight “around the great city of Priam” (16.448; see [[priam]]); and to the Trojans Sarpedon was “a prop of their city, / foreigner though he was” (16.549–550; see [[sarpedon]]). At the end Hector boasts over Patroclus, “I suppose you said that you would ravage our city” (16.830; see [[hector]]). Ilios is εὔπωλος, “of the fine horses”, when Peleus and Thetis send Epeigeus “to Ilios of the fine horses” (16.576; see [[of-the-fine-horses]] and [[epeigeus]]), and Troy is ἐριβῶλαξ, “deep-soiled”, where Sarpedon is to die “far from his native land” (16.461; see [[deep-soiled]]); at the wall it is ὑψίπυλος, “high-gated” (16.698; see [[high-gated]]).
