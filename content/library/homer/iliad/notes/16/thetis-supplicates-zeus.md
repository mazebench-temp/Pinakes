summary: When the first ship burns, the wish that Thetis carried to Zeus is fulfilled, and Book 16 looks back on it. Achilles reminds Zeus that he once heard his prayer, “and you honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans hard” (16.236–237), and Patroclus leads the Myrmidons out with the last verses of that wish (16.273–274 = 1.411–412); Achilles' new prayer is granted only in half.

**The prayer remembered.** Achilles prays to Zeus of Dodona from the cup that no one else drinks from (16.220–235; see [[zeus]], [[dodona]] and [[cup]]), and begins with the god's past favor:

{{quote:b2a04e89-82cd-5c3a-a09e-b0ba24dca437}}

The verses are Chryses' second prayer to Apollo (1.453–455; see [[fulfill-this-wish]]), and they speak of the first answered prayer as Achilles' own. In Book 1 he did not pray to Zeus. He prayed to his mother on the shore, and sent her to Olympus to clasp Zeus' knees and ask that the Achaeans be penned in among the sterns of their ships and slaughtered (1.351–356, 1.393–412); she supplicated Zeus, and Zeus nodded (1.500–530). Thetis herself will speak of it in the same way: “These things have been fulfilled for you / by Zeus, as you prayed before, lifting up your hands”, that the sons of the Achaeans should be penned in at the sterns of the ships for want of him (18.74–77). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus rejected 16.237 because Achilles had won his wish not by a prayer of his own but through Thetis, and answers that Thetis had in fact carried a prayer from Achilles to Zeus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.236).

**The wish fulfilled.** Book 16 is the moment that the supplication asked for. The Achaeans are pressed back against the sea at their ships, Ajax gives way, and the Trojans set fire to the ship of Protesilaus (16.101–124). Zeus had held out glory to Hector so that he might set the ships on fire “and fulfill the excessive prayer of Thetis / in full”, and he was waiting “to see with his eyes the blaze of a burning ship” (15.596–600; see the note on Book 15); from it he means to turn the battle. Patroclus, leading the Myrmidons out, ends his exhortation with the two verses of Achilles' wish in Book 1: “and so that the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, may learn / his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans” (16.273–274 = 1.411–412; see [[ate]] and [[patroclus]]). Achilles' charge to Patroclus asks for what Thetis asked of Zeus, τιμή, “honor”: Patroclus is to win him “great honor and glory / from all the Danaans” so that they send back the girl and give gifts (16.84–86; see [[time]]). Thetis had begged Zeus to give strength to the Trojans “until the Achaeans / pay honor to my son and exalt him with honor” (1.509–510).

**Half a prayer.** The second prayer has two parts, that Patroclus drive the battle from the ships, and that he come back unharmed with his armor and his companions (16.241–248). Zeus, who nodded his assent to Thetis (1.524–530), now grants the one and refuses the other with the opposite nod, the nod of refusal, ἀνένευσε (16.250, 16.252; see [[neuo]]):

{{quote:94f9322a-7ea1-5f54-9685-301dbba8cdca}}

The first prayer's fulfillment includes the refusal. When Zeus told Hera the course of the plan that began with Thetis' supplication, Hector was to kill Patroclus before Achilles killed Hector (15.64–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
