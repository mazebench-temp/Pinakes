---
summary: Achilles is “swift-footed” at the start of his answer to Patroclus and in the phrases that name his armor, his attendants and the Trojans' fear of him; the speed of feet belongs in this book above all to horses, his own immortal pair and the Trojans' teams in flight.
---

**Achilles.** The formula πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς comes only once, as he answers Patroclus' plea: “Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him” (16.48; [[deeply-troubled]]). Elsewhere his speed lives in the genitive ποδώκεος Αἰακίδαο, the phrase of what is his: the corselet that Patroclus puts on, “the inlaid corselet, starry, of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.134; [[inlaid]], [[starry]]), and his attendants, Patroclus himself, “the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.165), and Automedon, “the godlike attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.865; [[grandson-of-aeacus]], [[automedon]]). The Trojans, seeing Patroclus in the armor, think that “the swift-footed son of Peleus / had thrown away his wrath and chosen friendship” (16.281–282; [[son-of-peleus]]).

**Horses.** Automedon leads “the swift horses under the yoke, / Xanthus and Balius, who flew as fast as the winds” (16.148–149; [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). In the rout Hector's “swift-footed horses” carry him out of the battle (16.367–368), many “swift chariot-drawing horses” break their masters' chariots in the ditch (16.370–371; [[chariot-drawing]]), and Achilles' horses leap the ditch while Hector's carry him away (16.380–383). At the end Hector boasts that “the swift horses of Hector / stretch out their feet to fight” (16.833–834), and the book closes on Automedon: “his swift horses carried him away, / immortal, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.866–867; [[splendid]]).
