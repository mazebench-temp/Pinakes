---
summary: Achilles pities the weeping Patroclus and, still refusing to fight, gives him his armor, his horses and his Myrmidons, with a charge to drive the Trojans from the ships and come back. When the first ship burns he sends him out at once, arms the Myrmidons, and prays to Zeus from his own cup that Patroclus may win glory and come back unharmed; Zeus grants the one and refuses the other. He does not appear again in the book, but his name is in every turn of it: in the Trojans' fear, in Apollo's warning at the wall, on the helmet that falls from Patroclus' head, and in Patroclus' last words (16.5–100, 16.124–129, 16.155–256, 16.281–282, 16.707–709, 16.796–800, 16.853–854).
# Πηλέος υἱ- (16.203, the Myrmidons' address; 18.18, 20.2, 21.139, 22.8, 22.250) is always Achilles.

**Pity.** “And seeing him, brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him” (16.5; see [[pity]] and [[podarkes]]). ᾤκτιρε, “pitied”, is the word for Patroclus' pity for the wounded Eurypylus (11.814), and Nestor had said that Achilles “does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.665): he pities his friend. He mocks the tears as a child's:

{{quote:bf5c45b1-873d-56aa-bcb8-39a10078471a}}

νηπίη, “a little one” (16.8), is the word the narrator uses of Patroclus a few verses later, when he has made his request: μέγα νήπιος, “great fool that he was” (16.46; see [[like-a-girl]] and [[nepios]]). Achilles' last question is his mother's: “Speak out; do not hide it in your mind, so that we both may know” (16.19 = 1.363), the verse with which Thetis came up from the sea to her weeping son in Book 1 (see [[speak-out]] and [[thetis]]). He can think of one grief worth such tears, news from Phthia that Menoetius or Peleus has died (16.12–16; see [[phthia]], [[menoetius]] and [[peleus]]), and only then of the Argives, whom he blames for their own deaths: “Or are you grieving for the Argives, how they are perishing / beside the hollow ships because of their own transgression?” (16.17–18; see [[argives]]).

**The reproach.** Patroclus names the wounded and turns on him: “but there is no dealing with you, Achilles. / May such anger never take hold of me as this that you keep, / you whose prowess is a curse!” (16.29–31; see [[cholos]] and [[whose-prowess-is-a-curse]]). He denies him his parents:

{{quote:0bc02e2b-35b0-5c0b-ab9a-0ff57500fcf1}}

Phoenix had told him that he must not keep a pitiless heart (νηλεὲς ἦτορ, 9.496–497; see [[phoenix]]); Patroclus calls him νηλεές, “pitiless” (16.33), and the Myrmidons, as Achilles reports them, use the same word (16.204; see [[pitiless]] and [[gray-green-sea]]). Then Patroclus asks for the armor, in Nestor's words (16.36–45 ≈ 11.794–803; see [[nestor]] and [[patroclus]]).

**The answer.** “Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him” (16.48; see [[deeply-troubled]]). He heeds no oracle (16.50–51); what hurts him is the prize taken back by a man “greater in power”, “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (16.52–59; 16.59 = 9.648; see [[geras]], [[agamemnon]] and [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]). And then he lets it go: “But we will let all this be, as done and past; it was not possible after all / to be relentlessly angry in my heart” (16.60–61; see the translation's note on 16.60–61). ἀλλὰ τὰ μὲν προτετύχθαι ἐάσομεν, “we will let all this be, as done and past”, is the verse with which, after Patroclus' death, he will put away his wrath in earnest, to his mother and to the assembled Achaeans (18.112, 19.65; see [[menis]]). He had said, he goes on, that he would not end his wrath until the war reached his own ships (16.61–63), as he told Ajax in the embassy (9.650–655). μηνιθμός, “wrath”, is found in Homer only in this book, three times (16.62, 16.202, 16.282).

**The charge.** He gives Patroclus his armor and the Myrmidons, “for they do not see the front of my helmet / shining close by” (16.64–71; see [[helmet]]), and tells him to fall on the Trojans in force before they burn the ships and take away “our dear homecoming” (16.80–82; see [[nostos]]). The charge that follows is the hinge of the book:

{{quote:312cdc68-2577-5bf5-bd90-a260e731064c}}

The glory Patroclus wins is to be Achilles' (see [[time]] and [[kydos]]): the Danaans are to send back the girl and give gifts, as Agamemnon had offered in Book 9 and Achilles refused (9.121–157, 9.378–387). Fighting on without him will make him “more dishonored” (ἀτιμότερον, 16.90). The warning names the god who will kill Patroclus: “far-working Apollo loves them dearly” (16.94; see [[apollo]]). Patroclus keeps the first part and breaks the second, “and was greatly blinded, / the fool; if he had kept the word of the son of Peleus, / he would have escaped the evil doom of black death” (16.685–687; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The answer ends in a wish:

{{quote:86b96f9a-3ea6-5b6a-94dc-f082e7bee45c}}

He wishes death on Trojans and Argives alike, and the city for the two of them (see [[if-only-father-zeus]] and the translation's note on 16.97–100). Apollo will tell Patroclus at the wall that Troy is not fated to fall to his spear, “nor by Achilles, who is far better than you” (16.707–709; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**The fire.** “So the fire played about her stern; but Achilles / struck both his thighs and spoke to Patroclus” (16.124–125; see [[protesilaus]]). He makes the gesture with which Patroclus left Eurypylus' hut when he saw the Trojans storm the wall (15.397–398): “Up, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus, urger of horses! / I see the blast of consuming fire beside the ships; / they must not take the ships and leave us no more escape. / Put on the armor quickly, and I will gather the army” (16.126–129; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[urger-of-horses]]). Patroclus puts on the armor but leaves the spear, “the Pelian ash”, which no other Achaean could wield, “but Achilles alone knew how to wield it” (16.140–144; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).

**The Myrmidons.** He goes through the huts and arms them all, and stands among them, “urging on the horses and the shield-bearing men” (16.155–167; see [[myrmidons]], [[warlike]] and [[like-wolves]]). He brought fifty ships to Troy with fifty men in each, and “he himself, with great power, was lord over all” (16.168–172). Before they go he reminds them of what they said “all through my wrath, when each of you blamed me”: “Hard son of Peleus, your mother reared you on anger, / pitiless, who keep your companions by the ships against their will” (16.200–206; see [[stern-command]] and [[hard]]). Their words and Patroclus' agree: he is pitiless, and they look for the cause in his birth and his rearing (16.33–35). Now, he tells them, “the great work of the din of battle has appeared, which you loved before” (16.207–209).

**The cup and the prayer.** He takes from the chest that Thetis put on his ship a cup that no other man drank from, and from which he poured to no god but father Zeus; he cleanses it with sulfur and water, washes his hands, draws the wine, and prays “standing in the middle of the enclosure”, looking up to the sky (16.220–232; see [[cup]], [[purification]], [[hand-washing]], [[libation]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]). His father poured to Zeus from a golden cup in the enclosure of his courtyard on the day Nestor and Odysseus came to fetch his son (11.772–775; see [[peleus]]).

{{quote:f0930b1f-0ef9-56e9-9454-0db3bec8192b}}

The second half of the address is Chryses' second prayer to Apollo, nearly verse for verse (16.236–238 ≈ 1.453–455; see [[chryses]] and [[fulfill-this-wish]]). Like the priest, Achilles reminds the god that he once heard him and “struck the army of the Achaeans hard”: his first prayer, through Thetis, was for the Achaeans' ruin (1.407–412; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). What he asks now is glory for Patroclus, “so that Hector too / may know whether our attendant knows how to fight / even alone, or whether his hands rage irresistible / only when I go into the toil of Ares”, and his safe return with his armor and his companions (16.239–248; see [[irresistible-hands]]). The words are Diomedes' boast in Book 8, “so that Hector too / may know whether my spear also rages in my hands” (8.110–111; see [[diomedes]]). Zeus hears him:

{{quote:94f9322a-7ea1-5f54-9685-301dbba8cdca}}

He puts the cup away, and stands in front of his hut, “for he still wished in his heart / to watch the dread din of battle” (16.253–256; see [[watching-the-war]]), as he watched from the stern of his ship in Book 11 (11.599–601). The book does not return to him.

**His name in the battle.** When the Trojans see Patroclus in the armor, they think “that beside the ships the swift-footed son of Peleus / had thrown away his wrath and chosen friendship” (16.281–282; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). Patroclus sends the Myrmidons out to honor him, “who is by far the best / of the Argives beside the ships” (16.271–272; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). When Apollo strikes Patroclus, the helmet that rolls in the dust is his: “before that it had not been lawful / for the horsehair-crested helmet to be stained with dust, / but it guarded the head and the graceful brow of a divine man, / Achilles; but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.796–800; see [[themis]] and [[hector]]). Hector, boasting, imagines Achilles' parting words to Patroclus, “Do not come back to me … / to the hollow ships, before you have torn man-slaying Hector's / bloody tunic around his chest” (16.839–841), the reverse of the charge Achilles gave. Patroclus' last words foretell Hector's death “at the hands of Achilles, noble grandson of Aeacus” (16.854), and Hector answers the dead man with a question: “Who knows if Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, / may not first be struck by my spear and lose his life?” (16.860–861; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The immortal horses, the gods' gifts to his father, carry Automedon away (16.864–867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Achilles does not yet know. In the next book the narrator says that his mother had often told him the mind of Zeus, but had not told him this (17.401–411).
