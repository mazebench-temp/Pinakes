**Sarpedon's charge.** The dying Sarpedon calls him by name, “Glaucus, my friend, warrior among men, now you must indeed / be a spearman and a bold warrior” (16.492–493), and gives him two tasks: to rouse the Lycian leaders to fight around his body, and then to fight for him himself (16.495–497). If the Achaeans strip him, he will be “a shame and a reproach” to Glaucus all his days (16.498–500; see [[sarpedon]] and [[oneidos]]). At the wall Sarpedon spoke to him alone the speech on why the kings of Lycia must fight among the foremost (12.310–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]); these are his last words to him.

**The prayer.** “And dread grief came upon Glaucus when he heard the voice, / and his heart was stirred, that he could not help him” (16.508–509; see [[achos]]). He is still wounded in the arm by the arrow Teucer shot him at the wall (16.510–512; 12.387–391; see [[teucer]]), and he prays:

He calls on Apollo as a god who may be “in the rich land of Lycia / or in Troy” (16.514–515; see [[apollo]], [[lycia]] and [[lycian-born]]). His complaint, “and he does not defend even his own son” (16.522), turns the narrator's words at the wall, where Zeus “warded off the fates / from his son” (12.402–403; see [[zeus]]). **To the Trojans.** He does as Sarpedon bade him, in Sarpedon's words: “First he urged on the leaders of the Lycians, / ranging everywhere, to fight around Sarpedon” (16.532–533 ≈ 16.495–496; see [[lycians]]). Then he goes “with long strides” to the Trojan leaders, Polydamas, Agenor, Aeneas and Hector (16.534–536), the men who stood with him and Sarpedon over the fallen Hector in Book 14 (14.425–426; see [[polydamas]], [[agenor]] and [[aeneas]]), and he speaks to Hector:

{{quote:b0e90f12-8d01-5e1b-972a-9ca603875111}}

The allies waste away their lives “far from their friends and their native land” (16.539), the words with which Patroclus lamented the Achaean leaders who were to die at Troy (11.817; see [[trojan-allies]]). Sarpedon rebuked Hector for the allies in Book 5 (5.472–492), and Glaucus will rebuke him again in Book 17, more harshly, for leaving Sarpedon's body to the Argives (17.140–168). This time the words work: grief seizes the Trojans for Sarpedon, “a prop of their city”, and Hector leads them, “angered for Sarpedon” (16.548–553; see [[hector]]).

**Bathycles.** When Patroclus has driven back the champions and Hector, “Glaucus, leader of the shield-bearing Lycians, was the first / to turn” (16.593–594), and he kills Bathycles, son of Chalcon, who stood out among the Myrmidons for prosperity and wealth, turning suddenly as Bathycles overtakes him in pursuit (16.594–598; see [[bathycles]] and [[chalcon]]). He falls with a thud, and “thick grief seized the Achaeans / that a brave man had fallen; but the Trojans rejoiced greatly” (16.599–600). It is his second and last kill in the poem, after Iphinous in Book 7 (7.13–16).

He is Λυκίων ἀγὸς ἀσπιστάων, “leader of the shield-bearing Lycians” (16.593), the title of Sarpedon in this book (16.490, 16.541; see [[shield-bearing]]).
