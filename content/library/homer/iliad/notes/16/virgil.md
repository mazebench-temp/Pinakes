---
summary: The Aeneid remembers Sarpedon. Jupiter consoles Hercules over Pallas with the death of his own son under the walls of Troy, and Juno pleads for Turnus as Zeus had thought of saving Sarpedon; Diana promises to carry Camilla's body home in a cloud, as Sleep and Death carry Sarpedon's to Lycia; and Camilla and Turnus die with a line made from the soul's departure that closes the deaths of Patroclus and Hector. Pallas falls to Turnus as Patroclus falls to Hector, and Automedon, the driver of Achilles' horses, is at the sack of Troy with Achilles' son.
---

- Jupiter, Hercules and Sarpedon, and Juno's plea for Turnus (*Aeneid* 10.464–473, 10.606–632, on 16.431–461): [[jupiter-and-sarpedon]].
- **The life that flees to the shades.** Patroclus and Hector die with the same couplet, the soul flying from the limbs to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.856–857 = 22.362–363; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). Virgil also has one verse for two deaths, *vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras*, “and life with a groan flees, resentful, down to the shades”. It closes the death of Camilla (*Aeneid* 11.831) and the poem itself, when Aeneas kills Turnus (*Aeneid* 12.952). Homer's soul laments its fate; Virgil's life resents it.
- **Carried home in a cloud.** When Camilla's death is near, Diana sends her nymph Opis to avenge it and promises: “Afterwards I myself will carry the body of the pitiable girl and her arms, unspoiled, in a hollow cloud to the tomb, and lay her in her native land” (*Aeneid* 11.593–594). Sarpedon's body is carried home by Sleep and Death to Lycia, where his brothers and kinsmen will bury him “with a tomb and a pillar; for that is the prize of the dead” (16.666–683; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). But Sarpedon's arms are taken: the Achaeans strip them from his shoulders, and Patroclus sends them to the ships (16.663–665). Virgil's goddess promises to bring the arms home too.
- **Pallas.** Pallas, whom his father Evander has entrusted to Aeneas, dies as Patroclus dies, the young companion killed by the enemy's champion, who takes his spoils. Turnus stands over the body, sets his left foot on it and strips off the sword-belt, and the poet says that a time will come when Turnus will wish he had bought Pallas untouched at a great price, and will hate those spoils and that day (*Aeneid* 10.490–505); Hector sets his heel on Patroclus to draw out his spear, and Zeus has already given him Achilles' helmet, “and his destruction was near him” (16.799–800, 16.862–863). At the end of the poem Aeneas sees Pallas' belt on Turnus' shoulder and kills him (*Aeneid* 12.941–952), as Achilles kills Hector in the armor that Hector took from Patroclus (22.322–323). Turnus, unlike Hector, gives the body back for burial: “Arcadians, … I send back Pallas as he deserved”, with whatever honor a tomb may bring (*Aeneid* 10.491–494); Hector tells the dying Patroclus that the vultures will eat him (16.836).
- **Sarpedon's kin.** Virgil gives Sarpedon two brothers from high Lycia, Clarus and Thaemon, among the defenders of the Trojan camp in Italy (*Aeneid* 10.125–126), and a bastard son, Antiphates, born of a Theban mother, whom Turnus kills (*Aeneid* 9.696–697). Homer's Sarpedon has brothers and kinsmen only in Lycia, who will give him burial (16.456, 16.674). In the storm of the first book Aeneas wishes he had died at Troy, “where fierce Hector lies by the spear of the grandson of Aeacus, where huge Sarpedon lies” (*Aeneid* 1.99–100).
- **Automedon.** At the sack of Troy, Achilles' son Pyrrhus comes to the doors of Priam's palace with Automedon, “driver of Achilles' horses, his armor-bearer” (*Aeneid* 2.469–477). In Homer he takes up that part in this book, when he yokes Xanthus and Balius for Patroclus and drives them into the battle (16.145–154, 16.684; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
- The Myrmidons' ranks closed shield to shield (16.215, as 13.131) and Virgil's *haeret pede pes densusque viro vir*: see the Book 13 note.
