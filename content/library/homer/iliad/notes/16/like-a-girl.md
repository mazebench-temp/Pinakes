{{quote:bf5c45b1-873d-56aa-bcb8-39a10078471a}}

τίπτε δεδάκρυσαι Πατρόκλεες, ἠΰτε κούρη / νηπίη, ἥ θʼ ἅμα μητρὶ θέουσʼ ἀνελέσθαι ἀνώγει / εἱανοῦ ἁπτομένη, καί τʼ ἐσσυμένην κατερύκει, / δακρυόεσσα δέ μιν ποτιδέρκεται, ὄφρʼ ἀνέληται: “Why are you in tears, Patroclus, like a girl, / a little one, who runs beside her mother and bids her pick her up, / clutching at her robe, and holds her back as she hurries, / and looks up at her in tears, until she picks her up?” (16.7–10). νηπίη is “little, a child”, the word νήπιος that the narrator gives Patroclus forty verses later, “great fool that he was”, when he asks for the armor (16.46; see [[nepios]]). The girl runs beside her mother (θέουσα), the mother hurries (ἐσσυμένην), and the child holds her back by her robe (εἱανός, another spelling of ἑανός, the fine robe of Hera and of Artemis, 14.178, 21.507) and looks up at her, weeping. ἀνελέσθαι, “to pick up”, closes the second verse and ἀνέληται the fourth: what the child wants is said at the start and granted at the end.

The tenor is spoken to Patroclus by name: τῇ ἴκελος Πάτροκλε τέρεν κατὰ δάκρυον εἴβεις, “Like her, Patroclus, you let your tender tears fall” (16.11). The half-verse of the tender tears returns when Achilles, mourning Patroclus, thinks of his father, who perhaps in Phthia “is letting his tender tears fall” for want of such a son (19.321–324). Here Achilles guesses at the cause: news from Phthia, of Menoetius or of Peleus, or grief for the Argives, perishing by the ships for their own transgression (16.12–19).

The point of likeness is the tears and the appeal. A child who clings and cries is asking for something, and Patroclus has come to ask; he names his request only at the end of his speech (16.38–45). The simile ends with the child's wish granted, and so does the scene: Achilles gives him his armor and the Myrmidons (16.64–100). Achilles' simile answers the narrator's, the spring of dark water that brought Patroclus in weeping (16.2–4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]).

The Iliad's other similes of mothers and their young: Athena brushes the arrow from Menelaus as a mother keeps a fly from her sleeping child (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]), Teucer ducks behind Ajax's shield like a child behind its mother (8.271–272; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]), and Achilles compares his labors for the Achaeans to a bird bringing food to her unfledged chicks (9.323–324; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]).
