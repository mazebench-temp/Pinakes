---
summary: The rescue that does not happen. Zeus wonders whether to snatch Sarpedon alive out of the battle and set him down in Lycia, and Hera dissuades him; the god's carrying comes after death, when Apollo lifts the body out of the missiles and Sleep and Death set it down in Lycia, in the words of Zeus' wish (16.431–461, 16.666–683).
passages:
  - 16.431-438 | Zeus' wish to save Sarpedon
  - 16.666-683 | Apollo carries Sarpedon's body away
---

Zeus' question has the parts of the scene: the danger, Sarpedon “fated to be brought down by Patroclus”, and the rescue, “whether, while he is still alive, out of the tearful battle / I should snatch him up and set him down in the rich land of Lycia” (16.434–437; see [[decision-scene]]). Hera's answer is that the gods rescue only those whose time has not come: Sarpedon is a mortal “long since ordained to his portion”, and if Zeus saves him the other gods will save their sons (16.441–449; see [[aisa]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). Zeus yields.

The rescue is then carried out on the dead. Apollo lifts the body “out of range of the missiles”, washes and anoints it, and sends it with Sleep and Death, “who quickly / set him down in the rich land of wide Lycia” (16.678–683). θείω … Λυκίης ἐν πίονι δήμῳ, “set him down in the rich land of Lycia”, was Zeus' wish for the living man (16.437); κάτθεσαν ἐν Λυκίης εὐρείης πίονι δήμῳ is what is done for the dead one (see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). No god snatches Patroclus away. At the end of the book it is Automedon whom the immortal horses carry out of Hector's reach (16.864–867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
