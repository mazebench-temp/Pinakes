summary: The Trojans set the first ship on fire, then panic at the sight of Patroclus in Achilles' armor and are driven from the ships, over the ditch and back across the plain. They grieve for Sarpedon and fight for his body until Zeus turns Hector to flight; Patroclus pursues them to the wall of Troy, where Apollo stops him, and Hector, sent back by Apollo, kills him.

**The fire.** When Hector has lopped the head from Ajax's spear, “they threw tireless fire / on the swift ship” (16.122–123), the ship of Protesilaus (16.286; see [[hector]] and [[protesilaus]]). Ajax knows that Zeus “wished victory for the Trojans” (16.121).

**The sight of Patroclus.** The Myrmidons pour out like wasps and fall on the Trojans in a body (16.259–277; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]):

{{quote:e30b0a5e-24c5-5942-a0f5-2133d7e52fdf}}

They see the two men in their armor and think that Achilles has given up his wrath. It is what Patroclus hoped for when he asked for the armor, “in the hope that, taking me for you, they may hold off from the war, / the Trojans” (16.41–42; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]). The last verse is the one that told the Trojans' panic when Poseidon turned the battle in Book 14 (16.283 = 14.507; see [[aristotle-each-man-looked-around]]). Patroclus kills Pyraechmes, the Paeonians flee, and the fire is put out, but the Trojans do not yet run: “they still stood against them, and gave way from the ships under compulsion” (16.305; see [[pyraechmes]]).

**The rout.** As the Achaean leaders each kill a man, the Danaans fall on the Trojans like wolves on lambs and kids, “and they remembered / the ill-sounding rout, and forgot their furious courage” (16.356–357; see [[like-wolves]] and [[ill-sounding]]). They go back from the ships, and “they did not cross back in good order” (16.367); Hector's horses carry him away, and the army is caught at the ditch, where many horses break their lords' chariots (16.368–371; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The roads fill with them as a storm wind of dust rises under the clouds (16.373–376), and they run as the rivers run when Zeus pours down rain on men who give crooked judgments: “so the Trojan mares groaned loudly as they ran” (16.384–393; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). ἵπποι Τρῳαί, “the Trojan mares”, use the feminine adjective of the Trojan women (see the translation's note on 16.393 and [[trojan-women]]). Patroclus will not let them reach the city: between the ships, the river and the wall he kills them, “and exacted the penalty for many” (16.394–398; see [[poine]]).

**Sarpedon.** When Sarpedon dies, Glaucus' rebuke reaches them, and “grief seized the Trojans from head to foot, / uncontrollable and unyielding, since he was a prop of their city, / foreigner though he was” (16.548–550; see [[sarpedon]], [[glaucus]] and [[penthos]]). Hector leads them, and “the Trojans first pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans” (16.569), killing Epeigeus over the body. They give way the distance of a javelin's throw before Patroclus (16.589–592; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]), rejoice when Glaucus kills Bathycles (16.600), and hold their ground over the body, as Patroclus tells Meriones: “the Trojans will not, for abusive words, / give ground from the body” (16.628–629; see [[the-outcome-of-war]]).

**The flight to the city.** When Zeus puts a craven heart in Hector, he calls on the other Trojans to flee (16.656–658), and the Lycians run with them. Patroclus pursues “the Trojans and Lycians” (16.685), kills nine men, “and the others each turned their minds to flight” (16.694–697). At the wall Apollo stands on the tower, “helping the Trojans” (16.701), and denies Patroclus the city “of the proud Trojans” (16.708; see [[troy]], [[apollo]] and [[proud]]). Then Apollo turns the battle again and grants “glory to the Trojans and Hector” (16.730), and over Cebriones “the Trojans and Achaeans, leaping at one another, / cut each other down, and neither side thought of deadly rout” (16.770–771; see [[cebriones]]). Patroclus leaps on the Trojans “with evil intent” and three times kills nine men (16.783–785) before Apollo, Euphorbus and Hector kill him.

**Hector's Trojans.** Over Patroclus' body Hector speaks for them all: Patroclus meant to take away “the day of freedom from the Trojan women”, but Hector, standing out “among the war-loving Trojans”, wards off from them “the day of compulsion” (16.830–836; see [[trojan-women]], [[war-loving]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Achilles had wished that every Trojan might die, and every Argive, so that he and Patroclus alone might take Troy (16.97–100).
