---
summary: A note in the scholia, collected among the fragments of the Homeric Problems, reports that Aristotle counted “each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction” among Homer's most terrible phrases and found it fitting for barbarians. The Meteorology places the Selli, whom Achilles' prayer puts around the Zeus of Dodona, in the “ancient Hellas” of Deucalion's flood; and Achilles' complaint that Agamemnon treated him “as if I were some dishonored vagrant”, which Book 16 repeats from Book 9, is quoted twice, in the Rhetoric and in the Politics.
---

- The Trojans' panic at the sight of Patroclus in Achilles' armor (16.283; *Homeric Problems*, fr. 386 Gigon): [[aristotle-each-man-looked-around]].
- **The Selli.** In the *Meteorology* Aristotle explains floods as the great winters of long cycles, which do not always strike the same places. The flood of Deucalion's time, he says, came most of all upon the Greek country, and in it upon ancient Hellas, the country about Dodona and the Achelous, a river that has often changed its course (*Meteorology* 1.14, 352a28–b3): “for the Selli lived there, and those who were then called Graeci and now Hellenes”. Aristotle does not quote Homer, but his Selli live where Achilles' prayer puts them, around the Zeus of Dodona (16.233–235), and Strabo, too, finds in Homer the Selli round Dodona “as far as the Achelous” (see [[strabo]], [[selli]] and [[dodona]]).
- **The dishonored vagrant.** Achilles tells Patroclus that Agamemnon took his prize from him “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (16.59), the words with which he ended his answer to Ajax in Book 9 (9.648). Aristotle quotes the verse in the *Rhetoric*, among the words of men angered by a slight (2.2, 1378b32–35), and in the *Politics*, where the citizen is the man who has a share in the honors of the city, and the man who has none is like a resident alien (3.5, 1278a34–38): [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]] and [[aristotle-anger]].
