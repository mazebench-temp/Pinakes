{{quote:72dcdc2f-94a1-552f-8b1d-59e9e54830bc}}

οἳ δʼ ὥς τʼ αἰγυπιοὶ γαμψώνυχες ἀγκυλοχεῖλαι / πέτρῃ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ μεγάλα κλάζοντε μάχωνται, / ὣς οἳ κεκλήγοντες ἐπʼ ἀλλήλοισιν ὄρουσαν: “And as two vultures with crooked talons and curved beaks / fight with loud screams on a high rock, / so they rushed at one another, screaming” (16.428–430). The birds are named with a pair of epithets that the Odyssey gives again to the vultures that swoop from the mountains on smaller birds (*Odyssey* 22.302; see [[crooked-talons-and-curved-beaks]]). κλάζοντε is a dual: two vultures, one for each man (see the translation's note on 16.428–429). κλάζω is the scream of birds, the cry of the eagle that drops the serpent among the Trojans (κλάγξας, 12.207), and the men's cry is the birds': κεκλήγοντες, “screaming”, is said of the Trojans rushing at the wall (12.125). The high rock, πέτρῃ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ, is the perch of the bird of prey, as the high oak was for the two gods who sat “like vultures” in Book 7 (φηγῷ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ, 7.60).

The point of likeness is an equal fight: two birds of the same kind, with the same weapons, fighting each other and screaming. Neither is the prey. Sarpedon has seen his Lycians, “who wear their tunics without the band”, brought down at Patroclus' hands, rebuked them for fleeing, and leapt down from his chariot to meet the man; Patroclus leaps down on the other side (16.419–427; see [[sarpedon]] and [[patroclus]]). The simile shows two champions as they come together; the next verse turns to Zeus, who sees them and pities them, and speaks to Hera of his son's fate (16.431–438; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). When the fight is over, the likenesses are no longer equal: Sarpedon falls like a felled tree and dies like a bull killed by a lion (16.482–491; see [[the-felled-tree]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]).

The αἰγυπιός of the other similes swoops and kills. Meriones leaps in “like a vulture” to pull his spear from Deiphobus' arm (13.531), Automedon drives among the Trojans like a vulture after geese (17.460), and in the Odyssey Odysseus and his men fall on the suitors like vultures on smaller birds that cower in the plain (*Odyssey* 22.302–306). The γῦπες of Hector's boast over the dying Patroclus are named by another word, which Homer uses of the birds that feed on the dead: “but here the vultures will eat you” (16.836; see [[vultures]]).
