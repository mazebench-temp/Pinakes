**The eight addresses.** The narrator first speaks to Patroclus as he answers Achilles' question, “Groaning heavily, you said to him, horseman Patroclus” (16.20; see [[groaning-heavily]]). The second comes when grief for Epeigeus drives him through the champions like a hawk: “so straight at the Lycians, Patroclus, urger of horses, / you rushed, and at the Trojans, and your heart was angered for your companion” (16.584–585; see [[like-a-hawk]]). The third asks the question of the kill list in the second person: “Then whom first, whom last did you slay, / Patroclus, when the gods called you to your death?” (16.692–693; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]). From there the addresses come close together. He taunts the falling Cebriones, “And taunting him you spoke, horseman Patroclus” (16.744), and leaps at his body, “so at Cebriones, Patroclus, you leapt in your eagerness” (16.754). At his fourth charge, “then, Patroclus, the end of your life appeared; / for Phoebus met you in the fierce battle” (16.787–788). Euphorbus “was the first to throw a missile at you, horseman Patroclus” (16.812), and the last address introduces his dying answer to Hector (16.843).

**Telling him his death.** The last addresses tell Patroclus what is happening to him: the end of his life appears, a god meets him whom he does not see, a man's missile strikes him, and he answers his killer feebly. The vocative falls where the story is hardest. The addresses to Menelaus, the other man the narrator speaks to in this way, are spread over the poem (see the article above); those to Patroclus are gathered in the one book of his great day and his death (see [[the-aristeia]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
