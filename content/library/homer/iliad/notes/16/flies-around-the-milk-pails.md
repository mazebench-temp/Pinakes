{{quote:15d43c41-669f-5c81-8672-ac5e3476b2e5}}

οἳ δʼ αἰεὶ περὶ νεκρὸν ὁμίλεον, ὡς ὅτε μυῖαι / σταθμῷ ἔνι βρομέωσι περιγλαγέας κατὰ πέλλας / ὥρῃ ἐν εἰαρινῇ, ὅτε τε γλάγος ἄγγεα δεύει: “And they kept thronging around the body, as when flies / buzz in a steading around the buckets brimming with milk / in the springtime, when the milk wets the pails” (16.641–643). The last verse is Book 2's unchanged (16.643 = 2.471). The rest is new: βρομέω, “buzz, hum”, is found only here, and so are περιγλαγής, “brimming with milk” (γλάγος, “milk”), and πέλλα, “milk pail”. In Book 2 the flies swarmed about the shepherd's fold (κατὰ σταθμὸν ποιμνήϊον, 2.470); here they settle on the full pails in the steading (σταθμῷ ἔνι). The tenor repeats the frame: ὣς ἄρα τοὶ περὶ νεκρὸν ὁμίλεον, “so they thronged around the body” (16.644; see [[flies]]).

In Book 2 the simile counted an army, as many men as the flies of spring (2.469–473). Here it shows a crowd drawn to one place, and the place is a corpse. The verses before it say that no one, however sharp-eyed, could have known Sarpedon any longer, “since with missiles and blood and dust / he was wrapped from his head to the tips of his feet” (16.638–640; see [[sarpedon]]). Flies on the dead are a fear of the next books: Achilles is afraid that flies will settle in Patroclus' wounds and breed worms in them while he fights, and Thetis promises to keep them off (19.23–33).

The simile is the second of two pictures of country work set around the body, after the din of the woodcutters (16.633–637; see [[the-woodcutters]]). The verse of the tenor goes on to Zeus, who never turns his shining eyes from the fight and ponders the death of Patroclus (16.644–655; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). When the fight is over, Zeus has Apollo carry the body out of range, wash it in the river, anoint it with ambrosia and clothe it, and send it home to Lycia (16.666–683).
