summary: Sent by Hector against Patroclus, Cebriones is struck on the forehead by Patroclus' stone and falls from the chariot like a diver. Patroclus mocks him as a tumbler, and Patroclus and Hector fight over his body like two lions until, late in the day, the Achaeans drag it away and strip it. It is the last fight before Patroclus' own death.

**The last drive.** At the Scaean gates Apollo, in the likeness of Asius, sends Hector against Patroclus, “and shining Hector bade wise-hearted Cebriones / lash the horses into the war” (16.727–728; see [[hector]], [[asius-son-of-dymas]] and [[wise-hearted]]). As when he turned the horses toward Ajax in Book 11 (11.521–530), the charioteer takes Hector where the danger is, and Hector, letting the other Danaans be, drives straight at Patroclus (16.731–732).

**The stone.** Patroclus leaps from his chariot with his spear in his left hand and a jagged stone in the other, and throws (16.733–736; see [[stone-throwing]]):

{{quote:88726e36-8b31-5ed7-8262-ca83d05a0d47}}

“as he held the horses' reins” (16.739): the words in which Eniopeus, the first charioteer Hector lost, was struck by Diomedes (8.121; see [[eniopeus]]). His eyes fall “there before his feet” (16.742), and he falls ἀρνευτῆρι ἐοικώς, “like a diver”, as Epicles fell from the tower of the Achaean wall when Ajax's stone crushed his skull (16.742–743 ≈ 12.385–386; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[epicles]]). The half-verse λίπε δʼ ὀστέα θυμός, “and his spirit left his bones”, ends both falls.

**The jest.** “And taunting him you spoke, horseman Patroclus” (16.744; see [[kertomia]] and [[apostrophe]]):

{{quote:7ace1ba8-6033-5ce6-9afe-83ce1c31b052}}

κυβιστάω, “tumble”, is what the acrobats do among the dancers on Achilles' shield (18.605; see the translation's note on 16.745–750 and [[dance]]): the falling charioteer is an acrobat, and his dive would bring up oysters from a rough sea. Earlier in the fight Patroclus rebuked Meriones for trading taunts with Aeneas, “so there is no need to heap up words, but to fight” (16.631; see [[meriones]] and [[the-outcome-of-war]]), and Aeneas had mocked Meriones as a “dancer” (16.617).

**Over the body.** Patroclus goes at the body “with the spring of a lion, which as it ravages the farmsteads / is struck in the chest, and its own courage destroys it” (16.752–753; see [[wounded-lion]]), and Hector leaps down from his chariot on the other side (16.755). They fight over Cebriones like two lions over a slain deer, “both of them hungry” (16.756–758; see [[two-lions]]), “two masters of the war cry” (16.759): “Hector, once he had taken hold of his head, would not let go; / and Patroclus on the other side held his foot” (16.762–763; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Around them the armies clash like the East Wind and the South Wind in a mountain forest (16.765–771; see [[the-winds-in-the-forest]]), and spears, arrows and stones fall thick around the dead man (16.772–775):

{{quote:23b78cf1-55e6-505f-9538-fad785b1c33d}}

The half-verse μέγας μεγαλωστί, “great over a great space”, comes back for Achilles, lying in the dust when he hears of Patroclus' death (18.26–27), and the whole couplet for the dead Achilles in the Odyssey, where the shade of Agamemnon tells him how he lay “forgetful of his horsemanship” while they fought around him (*Odyssey* 24.39–40; see [[great-over-a-great-space]]). Cebriones, the charioteer, has forgotten the one art that was his.

**The end of the day.** “As long as the sun stood astride the middle of the sky, / so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell; / but when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen, / then indeed the Achaeans were stronger beyond their portion” (16.777–780). The battle is timed in the verses of Books 8 and 11, where the missiles struck home as long as the morning lasted (8.66–68, 11.84–85), and ὑπὲρ αἶσαν, “beyond their portion”, says that the Achaeans prevail beyond what is fated (see the translation's note on 6.487 and [[aisa]]). They drag “the hero Cebriones out of range of the missiles, / out of the cries of the Trojans, and took the armor from his shoulders” (16.781–782; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Patroclus charges three times and three times kills nine men, and the fourth time Apollo meets him (16.783–792; see [[patroclus]] and [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]).
