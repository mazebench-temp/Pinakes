summary: The cattle of Book 16 are the herd among which a lion kills a tawny bull, as Patroclus kills Sarpedon (16.487–489). Hera is “ox-eyed” (16.439), and the afternoon is told by the plowman's day, as the sun moves on “toward the time for unyoking oxen” (16.779).
grc: [βουλυτὸν]
en: [unyoking oxen]
---

**The herd.** ἠΰτε ταῦρον ἔπεφνε λέων ἀγέληφι μετελθὼν / αἴθωνα μεγάθυμον ἐν εἰλιπόδεσσι βόεσσι, “As a lion comes upon a herd and kills a bull, / tawny and great-hearted, among the shambling cattle” (16.487–488; see [[lion-among-cattle]], [[bulls]] and [[shambling]]). The bull is the leader of the herd, as Sarpedon is of the Lycians. The fighters' shields are oxhide, and in the din of the fight over Sarpedon's body the bronze and hide and “well-made shields of oxhide” (βοῶν τʼ εὐποιητάων) are struck with swords and spears (16.636; see [[the-woodcutters]] and [[shield]]).

**Ox-eyed.** βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “ox-eyed queen Hera”, answers Zeus when he thinks of saving Sarpedon (16.439; see [[ox-eyed]] and [[hera]]).

**The time for unyoking oxen.** ἦμος δʼ Ἠέλιος μετενίσετο βουλυτὸν δέ, “but when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen” (16.779). βουλυτόνδε, printed here in two words, is the hour of the afternoon when the plowman looses his oxen from the yoke (see the translation's note on 16.188). The verse marks the turn of the day's battle: until then the missiles of both sides struck home, and from then on the Achaeans were stronger beyond their portion (16.777–780; see [[helios]]). The same verse tells the hour when the Cicones routed Odysseus' men (*Odyssey* 9.58).
