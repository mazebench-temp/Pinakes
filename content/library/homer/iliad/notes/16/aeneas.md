summary: Aeneas is one of the Trojan leaders whom Glaucus rallies to fight for Sarpedon's body. He throws at Meriones and misses, and taunts him as a dancer; Meriones answers that Aeneas too is mortal, and Patroclus ends the exchange: “For the outcome of war lies in our hands, and the outcome of words in council” (16.630).

**The throw.** Glaucus goes among the Trojan leaders “and went after Aeneas and Hector armed in bronze” (16.536; see [[glaucus]]): the men who stood over the fallen Hector with him and Sarpedon in Book 14 (14.425–426). In the fight over Sarpedon's body Meriones kills Laogonus, son of Onetor, the priest of Zeus of Ida (16.603–607; see [[laogonus]] and [[meriones]]), and Aeneas throws at him:

{{quote:89c46452-b74c-5454-8654-1e8fc1d4da13}}

The verses come from Book 13. There Idomeneus' spear shook in the heart of Alcathous, Aeneas' brother-in-law, “and then mighty Ares let its might go” (16.613 = 13.444), and Aeneas, throwing at Idomeneus, missed as he misses Meriones here: Idomeneus too, “looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear” (16.610 = 13.503), and the spear “went quivering into the earth, / since it had sped in vain from his strong hand” (16.614–615 = 13.504–505; see [[idomeneus]] and [[alcathous]]).

**The taunt.** “Aeneas was angered in his heart” (16.616): “Meriones, dancer though you are, my spear / would soon have stopped you for good, if I had hit you” (16.617–618). The poem does not say why he calls Meriones a dancer, ὀρχηστής; Priam, in his grief, will call his surviving sons ψεῦσταί τʼ ὀρχησταί τε, “liars and dancers” (24.261; see [[dance]]). Meriones answers that it is hard for Aeneas to quench the might of every man who comes against him, “for you too are mortal” (16.620–622), and that if his own spear struck home, Aeneas would give him triumph, “and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (16.625; see [[hades]] and [[of-the-famous-horses]]). The son of Aphrodite is reminded that he is mortal.

**Words and deeds.** Patroclus rebukes Meriones, not Aeneas: “Meriones, why do you, brave as you are, say these things?” (16.627). The Trojans will not leave the body for insults, “For the outcome of war lies in our hands, and the outcome of words in council; / so there is no need to heap up words, but to fight” (16.628–631; see [[the-outcome-of-war]] and [[word-or-deed]]). In Book 20 Aeneas will make the same point to Achilles: they should not stand trading reproaches like children, for there are words enough on both sides; let them try each other with the spear (20.244–258; see [[achilles]]).
