summary: Book 16 is a book of chariots. Automedon yokes the immortal Xanthus and Balius with the mortal Pedasus for Patroclus; the Trojan teams break their chariots in the ditch while Patroclus' horses leap it; Sarpedon kills Pedasus; Patroclus kills Hector's charioteer Cebriones, who falls from the chariot like a diver; and at the end the immortal horses carry Automedon away from Hector (16.145–154, 16.367–393, 16.466–475, 16.733–776, 16.864–867).

# At 16.393 the Trojans' note claims the same phrase for "Trojan"; this note links "mares".

**Achilles' team.** Automedon yokes Xanthus and Balius, born of the Harpy Podarge to the West Wind, and in the traces beside them puts Pedasus, taken at the sack of Eetion's city, “who, mortal though he was, kept pace with the immortal horses” (16.148–154; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[pedasus-the-horse]]). Achilles stands among the Myrmidons “urging on the horses and the shield-bearing men” (16.167). Sarpedon's spear, meant for Patroclus, kills Pedasus; Automedon cuts the dead trace horse loose, and the pair are set straight and pull taut in the reins (16.466–475). The book ends with the immortal pair carrying Automedon out of Hector's reach (16.864–867).

**The ditch.** When the Trojans flee from the ships, Hector's swift-footed horses carry him out, but the ditch holds back the army; many swift chariot-drawing horses break their lords' chariots at the end of the pole and leave them; men fall headlong from their chariots under the axles, “and the chariots overturned with a clatter” (16.367–379). Patroclus' immortal horses leap the ditch, and Hector's carry him away (16.380–383). In Book 12 Hector's horses had stood neighing at the edge of the ditch and would not cross it (12.50–59), and in Book 15 Apollo made a causeway for them (15.355–360; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). Now the ditch traps them on their way back. The Trojan mares, ἵπποι Τρῳαί, groan loudly as they run for the city, like the torrents of a storm of late summer (16.384–393; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]] and the translation's note on 16.393).

**Charioteers.** Thestor, son of Enops, sits crouching in his chariot with the reins slipped from his hands when Patroclus drags him over the rail on his spear (16.402–410; see [[the-fisherman]]). Sarpedon leaps down from his chariot to meet Patroclus, and when he is dead the Myrmidons hold his snorting horses, “eager to flee in rout, since they had left their lords' chariot” (16.426, 16.506–507; see the translation's note on 16.371 and 16.507). At the Scaean gates Apollo tells Hector to drive his strong-hoofed horses at Patroclus, and Hector bids Cebriones lash the horses into the war (16.712–728). Cebriones, Hector's driver since the death of Archeptolemus (8.312–319), is killed holding the reins and falls from the chariot like a diver, and Patroclus mocks the fall as a tumbler's (16.737–750; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[cebriones]]); after the fight over his body he lies “great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship” (ἱπποσυνάων, 16.775–776). Euphorbus, who wounds Patroclus first, surpassed all of his age “with his spear and his horsemanship and his swift feet”, and brought down twenty men from their chariots when he first came with his chariot to learn the ways of war (16.808–811; see [[euphorbus]]). Hector's boast over Patroclus puts his own swift horses before the Trojan women (16.833–834).

The horse is also in the book's epithets. The narrator calls Patroclus “horseman” (ἱππεύς, 16.20, 16.744, 16.812, 16.843), and Achilles calls him ἱπποκέλευθος, “urger of horses” (16.126; see [[urger-of-horses]]); Peleus is ἱππότα, “the horseman” (16.33), Phoenix “horse-driving” (16.196), and Ilios is “of the fine horses” (16.576).
