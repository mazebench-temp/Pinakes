---
summary: The lions of Book 16 are Patroclus' until the last. He kills Sarpedon as a lion kills a bull among the herd (16.487–489), goes at the body of Cebriones with the spring of a lion that is struck in the chest (16.752–753), and fights over it with Hector like two hungry lions over a slain deer (16.756–758); then Hector kills him as a lion overpowers a boar at a spring (16.823–826).
---

**Patroclus the lion.** The first lion of the book kills: ἠΰτε ταῦρον ἔπεφνε λέων ἀγέληφι μετελθὼν, “As a lion comes upon a herd and kills a bull”, and the bull, tawny and great-hearted, dies groaning under the lion's jaws, as Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, rages as he dies under Patroclus (16.487–491; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Sarpedon had been the lion of Book 12, sent against the Argives by Zeus “like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293); at his death the beasts change places.

**The lion's end.** Then the lion's end is told before Patroclus' own. After killing Cebriones and mocking him, Patroclus goes at the body “with the spring of a lion, which as it ravages the farmsteads / is struck in the chest, and its own courage destroys it” (16.752–753; see [[wounded-lion]]), and he and Hector fight over it “like lions”, both hungry, over a slain deer on the mountain peaks (16.756–758; see [[two-lions]]).

**Hector the lion.** The last lion is Hector. Patroclus, struck by Apollo and wounded by Euphorbus, is stabbed by Hector in the flank, and the simile turns him into the beast that the lion kills: “As when a lion overpowers a tireless boar in battle, / when the two fight in high spirits on the peaks of a mountain / over a little spring” (16.823–825; see [[the-lion-and-the-boar]] and [[boar]]). In Book 15 Hector was the lion that turns back the hunters and the lion that falls on a herd in a marsh (15.271–280, 15.630–636); in Book 16 he becomes the lion again only to kill Patroclus. The lions of Book 17 are over Patroclus' body: Menelaus is driven from it like a lion from a farmyard, and Ajax stands over it like a lion over its cubs (17.108–113, 17.133–136).
