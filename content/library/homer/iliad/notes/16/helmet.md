summary: Achilles' helmet is the sign the Trojans fear, and its loss marks Patroclus' end. The Trojans press on because they do not see “the front of my helmet / shining close by” (16.70–71); Patroclus puts it on (16.137–138); and Apollo strikes it from his head, to roll in the dust where it had never been, until Zeus gives it to Hector, “and his destruction was near him” (16.793–800).

---

**Ajax's helmet.** At the start of the book Ajax's shining helmet rings terribly around his temples, struck again and again on its plates, until he gives way (16.104–106; see [[ajax]]). In the battle helmets fail: Lycon's sword shatters on the ridge of Peneleos' horsehair-crested helmet, at the socket that holds the crest (16.338–339; see the translation's note on 16.338), and the heads of Erylaus and Epeigeus are split in two “in the heavy helmet” (16.412–413, 16.578–579; see [[stone-throwing]]). The Myrmidons stand so close that their crests touch when they nod (16.214–217; see [[battle-order]]).

**Achilles' helmet.** Achilles names his helmet as what keeps the Trojans off (16.70–71), and Patroclus sets it on his head with the verses of Paris' arming, “a well-made helmet / with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it” (16.137–138; see [[arming-scene]]). It is the first piece the god takes. Apollo strikes it from Patroclus' head and it rolls under the horses' feet, and the plume is stained with blood and dust (16.793–796). The narrator stops for its history: “before that it had not been lawful / for the horsehair-crested helmet to be stained with dust, / but it guarded the head and the graceful brow of a divine man, / Achilles; but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.796–800; see [[themis]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). The helmet passes from Achilles to Patroclus to Hector, and the narrator joins Zeus' gift to Hector's death.
