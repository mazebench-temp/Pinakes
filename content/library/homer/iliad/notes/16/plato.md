---
summary: The Republic quotes two passages of Book 16 among the verses it would strike from poetry, the soul that flies to Hades lamenting its fate, which would teach the guardians to fear death, and Zeus' lament for Sarpedon, which shows the greatest of the gods grieving unlike himself. The Laws imagine Patroclus brought back to life without the arms that Hector held, to show that a man stripped of his arms by force has not thrown them away.
---

- The soul that flies from the limbs to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (*Republic* 3.386d, quoting 16.856–857): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]].
- Zeus' grief that Sarpedon, “dearest of men” to him, is fated to fall to Patroclus (*Republic* 3.388c–d, quoting 16.433–434): [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]].
- **The arms of Patroclus.** In the twelfth book of the *Laws* the Athenian stranger asks how the law should judge a soldier who loses his arms, since not every loss is a shameful throwing away. He takes a story for example. If Patroclus, carried back to his tent without his arms, had come to life again, as has happened to countless men, while Hector held the arms that the poet says the gods gave to Peleus as a dowry when he married Thetis, then all the cowards of that time could have reproached the son of Menoetius with the loss of his arms. A man robbed of them by reasonable force, or who loses them falling from cliffs, at sea or in a storm, should not be called a thrower-away of his shield; the suit for arms thrown away lies against the man who lets them go of his own will, choosing a shameful life over a fine death (*Laws* 12.944a–d). The case is Homer's. Patroclus does not let his arms go: Apollo strikes the helmet from his head, the spear is shattered in his hands, the shield falls from his shoulders and the god loosens his corselet, and Zeus gives the helmet to Hector (16.791–804; see [[patroclus]] and [[apollo]]). Patroclus says it himself in answer to Hector's boast, “for they themselves took the armor from my shoulders” (16.846). Hector takes the rest from the body in the next book (17.125), and the gift of the gods to Peleus on his wedding day is Achilles' word for the arms when he tells Thetis of their loss (18.82–85; see [[peleus]] and [[thetis]]).
