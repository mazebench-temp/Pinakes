summary: Three uses, three senses. In the simile of the autumn storm Zeus rages at men “who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice”, the first δίκη of the Iliad; Glaucus praises Sarpedon, “who guarded Lycia with his judgments and his strength”; and the narrator says that before Apollo struck it, “it had not been lawful” for Achilles' helmet to be stained with dust.
grc: [δίκην, δίκῃσι]
en: [justice, judgments]
passages:
  - 16.384-393 | The storm of Zeus
---

**Crooked ordinances.** The Trojan horses groan as they run like the torrents of a storm that Zeus pours down “when in resentment he grows angry with men / who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice, caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (16.386–388; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]], [[kotos]] and [[assembly]]). The θέμιστες are the ordinances a judge gives, and they can be crooked (σκολιάς); δίκη, “justice, judgment”, appears here for the first time in the Iliad (see the translation's note on 16.384–392). Zeus is the guardian of both, as Nestor told Agamemnon (9.98–99).

**A king's judgments.** Glaucus' praise of the dead Sarpedon gives the two things a king owes his people: he “guarded Lycia with his judgments and his strength” (δίκῃσί τε καὶ σθένεϊ ᾧ, 16.542; see [[sarpedon]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]).

**Not lawful.** θέμις is also what may properly happen. The helmet that Apollo strikes from Patroclus' head rolls in the dust, and the narrator explains why that is strange: “before that it had not been lawful / for the horsehair-crested helmet to be stained with dust, / but it guarded the head and the graceful brow of a divine man, / Achilles” (16.796–799; see [[helmet]]).
