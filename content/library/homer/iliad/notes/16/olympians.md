---
summary: The gods are a body whose approval Zeus must reckon with. Hera warns him that not all the other gods approve of saving Sarpedon, and that the sons of many immortals fight at Troy; at the end it is “the gods” who call Patroclus to his death.
refs: [16.443, 16.446-449, 16.693]
---

**Not all approve.** When Zeus thinks of saving Sarpedon, Hera answers: “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (16.443; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]], [[hera]] and [[zeus]]). If he sends his son home alive, another god may wish to save his own son, “for many are fighting around the great city of Priam, / sons of immortals, and you will stir a dread grudge in them” (16.446–449; see [[immortals]]). The gods are a body whose grudge even Zeus will not provoke, and he gives way, as he does when Athena answers him over Hector with the same verse (22.181).

**The gods together.** Achilles fears “one of the everlasting gods from Olympus” (16.93; see [[olympus]]); Ajax at the ships knows “the work of the gods” (16.120); the immortal horses are “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381, 16.867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]); and the narrator asks Patroclus whom he killed first and last “when the gods called you to your death” (16.692–693; see [[patroclus]]). The Muses of the invocation have their homes on Olympus (Ὀλύμπια δώματʼ ἔχουσαι, 16.112; see [[muses]] and [[homes-on-olympus]]).
