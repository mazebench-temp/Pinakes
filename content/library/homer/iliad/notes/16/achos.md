summary: “Such grief has overpowered the Achaeans”, Patroclus tells Achilles (16.22), and Achilles answers with his own “dread grief”, the loss of his prize (16.52–55). In the battle grief falls on the companions of the fallen: on Glaucus for Sarpedon, on Patroclus for Epeigeus, on the Achaeans for Bathycles and, at the end, for Patroclus himself.
grc: [ἀκαχοίμεθα, ἤκαχε]
en: [would grieve, grieved]
---

The two griefs at the start of the book are set side by side. Patroclus' is for the Achaeans, with their best men wounded among the ships (16.22–29; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]); Achilles', twice “dread grief” (αἰνὸν ἄχος), is for himself, robbed of his prize by a man greater in power (16.52–55; see [[geras]]). Achilles names a third grief that would be greater: if Menoetius and Peleus were dead, “we would grieve greatly for both of them” (16.16; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). In the battle the word follows the deaths: “dread grief came upon Glaucus” when he heard Sarpedon's dying voice (16.508), “grief came upon Patroclus for his fallen companion” (16.581), “thick grief seized the Achaeans” at the fall of Bathycles (16.599), and Patroclus' own fall “greatly grieved the army of the Achaeans” (16.822; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]).
