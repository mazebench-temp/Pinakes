Achilles answers Patroclus' plea by naming the grief that holds him: “this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit, / whenever a man wishes to rob his equal / and take back his prize, because he is greater in power” (16.52–54). The girl the sons of the Achaeans picked out for him as his prize, whom he won with his spear when he sacked a well-walled city, “powerful Agamemnon took her back out of my hands, / the son of Atreus, as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (16.56–59; see [[taking-of-briseis]] and [[geras]]). The verse is from his answer to Ajax in the embassy, where his heart swelled with anger whenever he remembered how Agamemnon had treated him (9.646–648).

What follows is new. In Book 9 the memory sent the envoys away with a refusal: he would not think of bloody war until Hector reached the huts and ships of the Myrmidons (9.649–655). Now: “But we will let all this be, as done and past; it was not possible after all / to be relentlessly angry in my heart” (16.60–61). He recalls his words of Book 9, that he would not end his wrath until the war cry and the war reached his own ships (16.61–63; see [[menis]]); when fire falls on the ship of Protesilaus, he does not wait for it to reach his own (16.122–129). The man who was treated like a vagrant still counts in honor. Patroclus is to win him “great honor and glory / from all the Danaans” (τιμὴν μεγάλην καὶ κῦδος), so that they send back the girl and give splendid gifts besides, and he must not fight on without him: “you will make me more dishonored” (ἀτιμότερον, 16.84–90; see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]).
