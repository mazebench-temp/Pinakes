summary: The deaths of Book 16 run from the first spear at the ships to the death of Patroclus, the turning point of the poem. Two great deaths are told in full, Sarpedon's and Patroclus', each with a god's part in it, a dying speech, a fight over the body and a care for it after death: Sarpedon is carried home by Sleep and Death, and Patroclus' soul goes to Hades lamenting.

  - 16.777-867 | The death of Patroclus

**The many.** The leaders' list at the ships, Patroclus' lists of nine, and his last charges, “three times he killed nine men”, give death in number (16.306–351, 16.415–418, 16.692–697, 16.784–785; see [[androktasia]]). The formulas of dying are used in all their forms (see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], [[loosened-his-limbs]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]]).

**Two deaths.** Sarpedon and Patroclus die alike in several things. Each death is decided in advance by Zeus (16.431–461, 16.644–655; see [[decision-scene]]). Each speaks as he dies and is covered by “the end of death” in the closing verse (16.502, 16.855; see [[the-end-of-death]]). Each body is fought over, Sarpedon's at length (16.532–683; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The difference is in what follows. Sarpedon's body is washed, anointed and carried home for burial, “the prize of the dead” (16.666–683; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]). Patroclus' death is followed to the soul: “and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.856–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]), and his body is left on the field for the next book.
