summary: At the height of Patroclus' charge the narrator brings the story to the edge of its greatest other outcome: “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy / at the hands of Patroclus; for he raged ahead with his spear, / had not Phoebus Apollo stood on the well-built tower” (16.698–700). Just before it he tells the other outcome of Patroclus' own story in the reverse order: had he kept Achilles' word, he would have escaped death.

**Troy.** The fall of Troy before its time is the largest thing the turn ever holds off. Apollo stands on the tower “intending his destruction, and helping the Trojans” (16.701), and three times thrusts Patroclus from the wall; at the fourth he tells him that it is not his portion to sack the city, nor even Achilles' (16.702–709; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The first verse returns once more, for Achilles, when Apollo again holds off the fall of the city by rousing Agenor against him (21.544–545).

**Patroclus.** A few verses earlier the narrator uses the same kind of sentence the other way round, the condition first: “if he had kept the word of the son of Peleus, / he would have escaped the evil doom of black death” (16.686–687; see [[nepios]] and [[ker-doom]]). Achilles' word was to come back once he had driven the Trojans from the ships (16.87–96). The narrator sees the other story and names the cause that kept it from happening: “But the mind of Zeus is always stronger than that of men” (16.688; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The dying Patroclus makes a counterfactual of his own: “But if twenty such as you had met me, / all would have perished here, brought down by my spear” (16.847–848).
