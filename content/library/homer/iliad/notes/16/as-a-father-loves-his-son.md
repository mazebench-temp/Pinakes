---
summary: Old Phylas rears his daughter's son Eudorus, whom Polymele bore to Hermes, “loving him as if he were his own son” (16.191–192).
grc: ["ὡς εἴ θʼ ἑὸν υἱὸν"]
en: [loving him as if he were his own son]
---

τὸν δʼ ὃ γέρων Φύλας εὖ ἔτρεφεν ἠδʼ ἀτίταλλεν / ἀμφαγαπαζόμενος ὡς εἴ θʼ ἑὸν υἱὸν ἐόντα: “and the old man Phylas reared the boy well and cherished him, / loving him as if he were his own son” (16.191–192). Eudorus, who leads the second rank of the Myrmidons, is “a maiden's son”: Hermes saw Polymele, Phylas' daughter, in the dance of Artemis and lay with her in secret, and when Eileithyia had brought the child into the light, Echecles, son of Actor, took her to his house as his wife, and the boy stayed with his grandfather (16.179–192; see [[eudorus]], [[phylas]], [[polymele]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]). ἀμφαγαπάζομαι is to embrace with love, to cherish; in the Odyssey Eumaeus uses it of the stranger he welcomed to his house (*Odyssey* 14.381).

The comparison is the one Phoenix made of Peleus, who received him when his own father had cursed him and loved him “as a father loves his son” (9.480–482), and the one the Odyssey gives to Eumaeus welcoming Telemachus (*Odyssey* 16.17–19). Each time the man who loves as a father is not the father. Phoenix himself leads the fourth rank of the Myrmidons four verses later (16.196; see [[phoenix]]).
