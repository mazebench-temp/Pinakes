summary: Patroclus arms in Achilles' armor in the verses of Paris' arming, piece by piece, but takes two spears of his own, for no other Achaean could wield the Pelian ash. Achilles arms the Myrmidons; and at the end of the book Apollo strips the same armor from Patroclus in the reverse order.
passages:
  - 16.130-144 | Patroclus arms in Achilles' armor
  - 16.793-804 | Apollo strips the armor
---

**Patroclus.** Achilles tells him to put on the armor quickly while he gathers the army (16.129), and the arming follows in the verses of Paris' arming in Book 3: “So he spoke, and Patroclus armed himself in gleaming bronze” (16.130), then greaves, corselet, sword, shield and helmet, a verse or two each (16.131–138 ~ 3.330–337; see [[greaves]], [[corselet]], [[sword]], [[shield]] and [[helmet]]). Two verses are changed. The corselet is “the inlaid corselet, starry, of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.134; see [[starry]]), where Paris put on “the corselet of his brother Lycaon” (3.333): both men go out in borrowed armor. And the spear verse: Patroclus “took two strong spears that fitted his grip” (16.139), the two spears of Agamemnon's arming (11.43), and leaves the one spear he cannot use. “Only the spear of the noble grandson of Aeacus he did not take, / heavy, huge, and massive; no other of the Achaeans / could wield it, but Achilles alone knew how to wield it” (16.140–142; see [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[spear]]). He can wear the armor; he cannot be Achilles (see [[exchange-of-armor]]).

**Horses and men.** The arming goes on past the man. Automedon yokes the immortal horses Xanthus and Balius, with the mortal Pedasus in the trace (16.145–154; see [[chariot]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and Achilles goes through the huts and arms the Myrmidons (θώρηξεν, 16.155–156; see [[myrmidons]]). In front of them all “two men were arming, / Patroclus and Automedon, with one heart” (16.218–219).

**Disarmed.** At the end of the book a god takes the same armor off, piece by piece, in the reverse order. Apollo strikes the helmet from Patroclus' head, and it rolls under the horses' feet (16.793–800; see [[helmet]]); the spear breaks in his hands, the shield falls from his shoulders with its strap, and Apollo loosens the corselet (16.801–804). In the book before, Athena disarmed Ares in the same way, helmet, shield and spear (15.125–127). The greaves and the sword are not mentioned. Euphorbus and Hector strike a man “naked though he was” (γυμνόν, 16.815; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]), and Hector puts on the armor in the next book (17.192–197).
