summary: Book 16 is Patroclus' book. He comes to Achilles weeping, reproaches him, and asks, in Nestor's words, to go out in his armor; the narrator calls him a great fool, begging for his own death. Armed as Achilles, he drives the Trojans from the ships, kills Sarpedon, and pursues them to the walls of Troy against Achilles' warning. Three times Apollo thrusts him from the wall; he kills Cebriones, and at his fourth charge Apollo strikes him from behind, Euphorbus wounds him, and Hector kills him. Dying, he foretells Hector's death (16.2–47, 16.130–144, 16.257–418, 16.419–505, 16.684–867).
# Μενοιτίου ἄλκιμον υἱόν (16.278, 16.827; also 18.455, 19.24) is Patroclus, as the nominative is in the Book 11 note.

**Tears.** “And Patroclus came and stood beside Achilles, shepherd of the people, / shedding hot tears like a spring of dark water / that pours its murky water down a sheer rock” (16.2–4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]] and [[hot-tears]]). He has run from Eurypylus' hut, where he saw the Trojans storm the wall (15.390–405). The simile is the one in which Agamemnon wept before the assembly when he proposed to give up the war (9.14–15), and δάκρυα θερμὰ χέων, “shedding hot tears”, will come back twice for him: Antilochus brings Achilles the news of his death “shedding hot tears” (18.17), and Achilles weeps hot tears when he sees him on the bier (18.235; see [[antilochus]]). Achilles pities him and mocks the tears as those of a little girl tugging at her mother's robe (16.5–11; see [[achilles]] and [[like-a-girl]]).

**The plea.** “Groaning heavily, you said to him, horseman Patroclus” (16.20; see [[groaning-heavily]] and [[hippeus]]). It is the first of eight times that the narrator turns to speak to him, all in this book and the last of them as he dies (16.20, 16.584, 16.693, 16.744, 16.754, 16.787, 16.812, 16.843; see [[apostrophe]]). He tells Achilles that all the best of the Achaeans lie wounded, in the verses of Nestor and of Eurypylus, and adds the healers, “busy over them, / healing their wounds” (16.23–29 ≈ 11.825–826, 11.660–662; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[healing]]); he has been one of them. Then he turns on his friend: “May such anger never take hold of me as this that you keep, / you whose prowess is a curse!”, and denies him his parents, Peleus and Thetis: “but the gray-green sea bore you, / and the lofty rocks, since your mind is harsh” (16.30–35; see [[whose-prowess-is-a-curse]] and [[gray-green-sea]]). The request is Nestor's plan in Nestor's verses, with the persons changed: if some oracle holds Achilles back, let him send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons, “in the hope that I may become a light to the Danaans”, and give him his armor, “in the hope that, taking me for you, they may hold off from the war” (16.36–45 ≈ 11.794–803; see [[nestor]], [[danaans]] and [[brief-is-the-breathing-space]]). The narrator answers before Achilles does:

{{quote:10b156bc-62b9-5eee-a2b2-f1d90d0fa071}}

λισσόμενος … λιτέσθαι, “begging … begging”: his plea to Achilles is a plea for his own death. νήπιος, “fool”, is the narrator's word for a man who does not see what is coming (see [[nepios]] and [[ker-doom]]). The narrator says it of him again when he pursues the Trojans to the city (16.686), and Hector says it over him as he dies (16.833). His ghost will use the word of himself, when it tells how as a boy he killed the son of Amphidamas, νήπιος οὐκ ἐθέλων, “a foolish child, not meaning to” (23.88).

**Armed.** Achilles gives him the armor and the Myrmidons, with the charge to drive the Trojans from the ships and come back (16.64–96), and when fire reaches the first ship he sends him out: “Up, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus, urger of horses!” (16.126; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[urger-of-horses]]). ἱπποκέλευθος, “urger of horses”, is said only of Patroclus, three times in this book (16.126, 16.584, 16.839). He arms in the verses in which Paris armed for his duel with Menelaus (16.131–139 ≈ 3.330–338; see [[arming-scene]]), and Achilles will arm in them in Book 19 (19.369–373). Paris put on the corselet of his brother Lycaon (3.333); Patroclus puts on “the inlaid corselet, starry, of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.134; see [[starry]] and [[corselet]]), and he takes two spears of his own where Paris took one. One piece he leaves:

{{quote:1e45a55e-35ad-52ce-b783-766c1a0c1c45}}

He goes out in everything of Achilles' but the spear that only Achilles can wield (see [[the-pelian-ash]]). Automedon yokes the immortal horses (16.145–154; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and the two men arm in front of the Myrmidons, “with one heart, / to fight in front of the Myrmidons” (16.218–220).

**The ships saved.** The Myrmidons pour out like wasps that boys have stirred up by the roadside (16.257–267; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]] and [[myrmidons]]), and Patroclus calls to them:

{{quote:d0b24351-affd-54bf-83bf-9478e318895c}}

The third verse is the army's exhortation, spoken before him four times by Hector and once by Ajax (16.270 = 6.112, 8.174, 11.287, 15.487, 15.734; see [[exhortation]]). The last two are Achilles' own words to his mother in Book 1, when he asked that the Achaeans be beaten back to the ships so that Agamemnon might learn his blind folly (16.273–274 = 1.411–412; see [[ate]] and [[agamemnon]]): the same lesson is now to be taught by saving them. The plan works as Nestor said it would. The Trojans see “the valiant son of Menoetius, / himself and his attendant, gleaming in their armor”, and think that Achilles has thrown away his wrath (16.278–283; see [[son-of-menoetius]]). His first spear kills Pyraechmes, the leader of the Paeonians, “beside the stern of the ship of great-hearted Protesilaus”, and he puts out the fire (16.284–294; see [[pyraechmes]] and [[protesilaus]]). When the leaders each kill a man, he is first, as Agamemnon was first in the list of that kind in Book 5 (16.306–311; 5.37–39; see [[areilycus-killed-by-patroclus]] and [[androktasia]]).

**Between the ships, the river and the wall.** He follows the rout to the ditch, and the immortal horses leap it; “his heart urged him on against Hector; / for he was eager to hit him; but his swift horses carried him away” (16.380–383; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[hector]]). Then he does what Achilles told him to do. He cuts off the foremost battalions, turns them back toward the ships, and will not let them reach the city: “but between / the ships and the river and the high wall / he rushed among them and killed them, and exacted the penalty for many” (16.394–398; see [[poine]]). He kills Pronous, drags Thestor from his chariot on his spear like a fisherman landing a fish, splits Erylaus' head with a stone, and brings down nine more, three to a verse (16.399–418; see [[pronous]], [[thestor-son-of-enops]], [[the-fisherman]] and [[erylaus]]). The last verse is the one that ended Teucer's run of victims in Book 8 and Leonteus' in Book 12 (16.418 = 8.277, 12.194).

**Sarpedon.** The Lycian king leaps down to meet him: “For I will meet this man, so that I may learn / whoever he is who is prevailing here, and has done much harm / to the Trojans” (16.423–425; see [[sarpedon]]). He does not know the man; Aeneas spoke the same verses of Diomedes, whom he did not know (5.175–176). They rush at each other like two vultures screaming on a high rock (16.428–430; see [[like-vultures]]), and Zeus, watching, says to Hera what is fated: “Ah me, that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me, / is fated to be brought down by Patroclus, son of Menoetius!” (16.433–434; see [[zeus]], [[hera]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Patroclus kills Sarpedon's attendant Thrasymelus; Sarpedon's spear kills the trace horse Pedasus and then misses again; and Patroclus' spear goes in “where the midriff closes around the throbbing heart” (16.462–481; see [[thrasymelus]] and [[pedasus-the-horse]]). Sarpedon falls like a felled tree, in the verses of Asius' death (16.482–486 = 13.389–393; see [[the-felled-tree]]), and dies roaring like a bull under a lion, with a last charge to Glaucus (16.487–501). Then: “and Patroclus, setting his heel on his chest, / drew the spear out of his flesh, and the midriff followed with it; / and he drew out his soul and the point of the spear together” (16.503–505; see [[psyche]]). Menelaus set his heel on Peisander's chest in the same words, λὰξ ἐν στήθεσι βαίνων (13.618). At the end of the book Hector will set his heel on Patroclus in the same way to draw his spear from him (16.862–863).

**Over the body.** “But the Achaeans / were roused by Patroclus of the shaggy heart, son of Menoetius” (16.553–554; see [[shaggy-chest]]). λάσιον κῆρ, “shaggy heart”, is said elsewhere in the Iliad only of Pylaemenes, the Paphlagonian leader (2.851; see [[pylaemenes]]). He asks the two Ajaxes to win the body: “if only we might seize him and defile him, / and take the armor from his shoulders” (16.559–560; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). ἀεικίζω, “defile”, is the word of Glaucus' fear for Sarpedon a few verses earlier (16.545), and of Achilles' fear for Patroclus' own body, that flies may breed in his wounds and defile it (19.24–26); Hector will try to drag Patroclus' body away and cut off its head (17.125–127). When Hector kills the Myrmidon Epeigeus with a stone, “grief came upon Patroclus for his fallen companion”, and he goes through the champions like a hawk (16.581–583; see [[epeigeus]] and [[like-a-hawk]]): “so straight at the Lycians, Patroclus, urger of horses, / you rushed, and at the Trojans, and your heart was angered for your companion” (16.584–585). His stone breaks the neck of Sthenelaus (16.586–587; see [[sthenelaus]]). When Meriones and Aeneas trade taunts, he rebukes Meriones:

{{quote:522e06f5-9720-549f-adee-30d25b5c7724}}

Hands are for war, and words for the council (see [[the-outcome-of-war]] and [[meriones]]). He will not keep to it: over Cebriones, a little later, he spends six verses jeering at a dead man (16.745–750). Zeus, watching the fight, ponders “the killing of Patroclus”, whether Hector should kill him there over Sarpedon's body or whether he should first drive the Trojans to the city and take the lives of many, and chooses the second (16.644–655; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Patroclus sends Sarpedon's armor to the ships (16.663–665).

**Blinded.** “And Patroclus, calling to his horses and to Automedon, / went after the Trojans and Lycians, and was greatly blinded, / the fool; if he had kept the word of the son of Peleus, / he would have escaped the evil doom of black death” (16.684–687; see [[black-death]]). μέγʼ ἀάσθη, “was greatly blinded”, is the verb of ἄτη, the blind folly with which his own exhortation charged Agamemnon (16.274; see [[ate]]). The narrator gives the reason in a general truth: “But the mind of Zeus is always stronger than that of men” (16.688; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]); it was Zeus who “let loose the spirit in his breast” (16.691). Then he asks Patroclus himself: “Then whom first, whom last did you slay, / Patroclus, when the gods called you to your death?” (16.692–693), and nine names answer (16.694–697; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]).

**At the wall.** “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy / at the hands of Patroclus” (16.698–699; see [[would-have-had-not]]), but Apollo stands on the tower. Three times Patroclus sets foot on the angle of the wall and three times the god thrusts him back, striking his shield with his immortal hands, and at the fourth he shouts:

{{quote:17d579e4-dfa5-5152-a5b1-b109489b2ac8}}

The scene is Diomedes' before Apollo in Book 5, three attacks and a warning at the fourth, and the last verse is the same (16.711 = 5.444); but Diomedes drew back “a little way”, and Patroclus “a long way” (5.443, 16.710; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]], [[like-a-god]] and [[apollo]]). Apollo uses Achilles' address, “Patroclus, sprung from Zeus”, and names Achilles, “who is far better than you” (16.707–709; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**Cebriones.** Apollo sends Hector against him (16.712–725). Patroclus leaps down, his spear in his left hand and a jagged stone in the other, and does not hang back from the man (16.733–736); the stone kills Hector's charioteer Cebriones, a bastard son of Priam (16.737–743; see [[cebriones]]). It is his third kill with a stone in the book, after Erylaus and Sthenelaus (16.411, 16.586–587; see [[stone-throwing]]). He jeers over the fallen charioteer:

{{quote:7ace1ba8-6033-5ce6-9afe-83ce1c31b052}}

The jest turns the dead man's fall into a diver's plunge and a tumbler's somersault (see [[like-a-diver]], [[kertomia]] and the translation's note on 16.745–750). Then “he went at the hero Cebriones / with the spring of a lion, which as it ravages the farmsteads / is struck in the chest, and its own courage destroys it” (16.751–753; see [[wounded-lion]]). The simile tells his end before it comes. He and Hector fight over the body like two lions over a slain deer, Hector holding the head and Patroclus a foot (16.756–763; see [[two-lions]]); in Book 18 Hector will three times seize Patroclus' own body by the feet (18.155–158).

**The fourth time.** As the sun moves toward evening the Achaeans prevail “beyond their portion”, strip Cebriones, and Patroclus charges (16.777–783; see [[achaeans]] and [[aisa]]): “Three times then he sprang at them, the equal of swift Ares, / shouting terribly, and three times he killed nine men” (16.784–785; see [[equal-of-ares]]). When he first came out of Achilles' hut in Book 11 he was “the equal of Ares”, and “this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]). The fourth charge is the end:

{{quote:882010a7-b31a-52af-b82c-cd0c9f26bd94}}

The god takes off the armor piece by piece. The helmet rolls under the horses' feet and its crest is fouled with blood and dust, “before that it had not been lawful” (16.793–800; see [[helmet]] and [[themis]]); the spear breaks in his hands, “heavy, huge, and massive” in the words that described the Pelian ash he left behind (16.801–802; 16.141; see [[heavy-spear]]); the shield falls with its strap; Apollo looses the corselet (16.803–804). “And blind folly seized his mind, and his shining limbs were loosened beneath him, / and he stood amazed” (16.805–806; see [[ate]]). Euphorbus, son of Panthous, spears him between the shoulders and runs, not daring to stand against him “naked though he was” (16.806–815; see [[euphorbus]]), and as Patroclus draws back among his companions, “avoiding doom”, Hector stabs him in the lowest part of the flank (16.816–822). He falls like a boar brought down by a lion at a little spring (16.823–828; see [[the-lion-and-the-boar]]).

**Last words.** Hector boasts that Patroclus meant to ravage Troy and carry off its women, calls him “fool”, and imagines Achilles sending him out to tear Hector's tunic (16.830–842; see [[hector]]). “Then you spoke to him feebly, horseman Patroclus” (16.843): ὀλιγοδρανέων, “feebly”, is said of Hector when Apollo found him after Ajax's stone, and will be said of him again as he dies (15.246, 22.337).

{{quote:c51afe3a-0c1e-5002-9c92-2d7f9f44d080}}

He ranks his killers: fate and Apollo first, then Euphorbus, and Hector only third (see the translation's note on 16.849–850 and [[moira]]). Then he prophesies: “you yourself will not live long, but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you, / to be brought down at the hands of Achilles, noble grandson of Aeacus” (16.852–854; see [[prophecy]] and [[mighty-fate]]). Hector, dying, will foretell Achilles' death in the same way (22.358–360).

**His death.** “As he spoke, the end of death covered him, / and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.855–857; see [[psyche]], [[hades]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]). The first of the three verses closed Sarpedon's last words too (16.502); all three are given to one other death in the poem, Hector's (22.361–363). Hector answers the dead man that Achilles may die first by his spear, sets his heel on him and pushes him off the spear, and runs after Automedon (16.858–867; see [[automedon]]). Plato's Socrates quotes the last two among the verses that he would strike out of the poets, so that men who are to be free will fear slavery more than death (*Republic* 3.386c–387b; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). The fight for his body fills the next book, and his death brings Achilles back to the war (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
