{{quote:f94ab366-866b-5c0e-9f0e-e2a80affcaa1}}

τὼ περὶ Κεβριόναο λέονθʼ ὣς δηρινθήτην, / ὥ τʼ ὄρεος κορυφῇσι περὶ κταμένης ἐλάφοιο / ἄμφω πεινάοντε μέγα φρονέοντε μάχεσθον: “The two fought over Cebriones like lions, / which on the peaks of a mountain, over a slain deer, / both of them hungry, fight in high spirits” (16.756–758). The simile is in the dual from start to finish, as the simile of the twin sons of Diocles was (5.554–560): δηρινθήτην, “the two fought” (found only here), πεινάοντε, “both hungry”, φρονέοντε, μάχεσθον. The lions are on the mountain peaks, ὄρεος κορυφῇσι, where the lions of Book 5 were reared (5.554). The quarry is already dead: the two are not hunting but fighting over a kill, “hungry” as the lion of Book 3 was hungry when it found a carcass, a stag or a wild goat (πεινάων, 3.23–25; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[deer]]).

The tenor names the pair: ὣς περὶ Κεβριόναο δύω μήστωρες ἀϋτῆς, / Πάτροκλός τε Μενοιτιάδης καὶ φαίδιμος Ἕκτωρ, / ἵεντʼ ἀλλήλων ταμέειν χρόα νηλέϊ χαλκῷ, “so over Cebriones two masters of the war cry, / Patroclus, son of Menoetius, and shining Hector, / were eager to cut each other's flesh with the pitiless bronze” (16.759–761). The deer is the body of Hector's charioteer, whom Patroclus has just killed with a stone (16.733–743; see [[cebriones]]). Then the two lions take hold of the carcass: “Hector, once he had taken hold of his head, would not let go; / and Patroclus on the other side held his foot” (16.762–763; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

Before the simile Patroclus went at the body with the spring of a lion that is struck in the chest (16.751–754; see [[wounded-lion]]); after it, the armies clash like the East and South Winds in a forest (16.765–771; see [[the-winds-in-the-forest]]). The two are equals here, two lions in the dual. When they meet for the last time the frame of the simile returns, ὥ τʼ ὄρεος κορυφῇσι μέγα φρονέοντε μάχεσθον, “when the two fight in high spirits on the peaks of a mountain”, but the pair are no longer alike: Hector is the lion and Patroclus the boar that it overpowers at a little spring (16.823–826; see [[the-lion-and-the-boar]]).
