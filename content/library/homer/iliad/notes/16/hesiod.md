---
summary: The Shield of Heracles, handed down under Hesiod's name, builds Heracles' fight with Cycnus out of Book 16. The fighters leap from their chariots, Zeus sends down bloody raindrops as a sign for his son, they meet like two lions over a slain deer and like vultures screaming on a high rock, Cycnus falls like an oak or a pine, and Athena tells Heracles to draw back from what is not fated for him. The Works and Days has the crooked judgments of the storm simile, and Hesiod's verses on Dodona stand beside Achilles' prayer in Strabo.
---

- **The Shield of Heracles.** The *Shield*, handed down under Hesiod's name, tells how Heracles and his charioteer Iolaus met Cycnus, son of Ares, and then Ares himself. Much of its fighting is made from Book 16, from the fight of Sarpedon and Patroclus and from the fight over Cebriones. Athena tells Heracles to kill Cycnus and leave him and his arms, to wound Ares where his shield leaves him bare, and then to draw back, for it is not fated for him to take the god's horses or his armor (*Shield* 325–337). Her words, ἂψ δʼ ἀναχάσσασθαι· ἐπεὶ οὔ νύ τοι αἴσιμόν ἐστιν, are close to Apollo's when he halts Patroclus at the wall of Troy, χάζεο … οὔ νύ τοι αἶσα: “Draw back, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus! It is not your portion” (16.707).
- **Bloody raindrops.** Heracles and Cycnus leap from their chariots to the ground (*Shield* 370–371), as Sarpedon and Patroclus do (16.426–427). Zeus thunders and casts down bloody raindrops from the sky, ψιάδας βάλεν αἱματοέσσας, setting a sign of war for his great-hearted son (*Shield* 383–385). In Book 16 he sheds bloody raindrops on the earth, αἱματοέσσας δὲ ψιάδας, “honoring his dear son”, whom Patroclus is about to kill (16.459–461; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]).
- **Lions and vultures.** The fighters rush at each other like two lions that fight over a slain deer (*Shield* 402–404), as Hector and Patroclus fight over the body of Cebriones like lions “over a slain deer” (16.756–758; see [[two-lions]]). Then they are like vultures with crooked talons and curved beaks that fight with loud screams on a high rock over a mountain goat or a deer that a hunter has shot (*Shield* 405–412); the two verses that begin the comparison are, almost word for word, Homer's for Sarpedon and Patroclus (16.428–429; see [[like-vultures]]).
- **The fall.** Cycnus falls as an oak falls, or a pine, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus (*Shield* 421–423); Sarpedon falls as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a tall pine cut down by builders (16.482–484; see [[the-felled-tree]]). In Book 16 the son of Zeus is killed. In the *Shield* he kills, and the god whose son falls is Ares (see [[sarpedon]] and [[heracles]]).
- **Crooked judgments.** In the storm simile Zeus pours down rain in anger at men who judge crooked ordinances and drive out justice, “caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (16.386–388). The *Works and Days* has the thought and the words (*Works and Days* 220–224, 238–251; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]).
- **Dodona.** Strabo sets a Hesiodic verse beside Achilles' prayer to the Zeus of Dodona, “Pelasgian” (16.233), in which someone goes to Dodona and its oak, the seat of the Pelasgians (fr. 14 Evelyn-White, of unknown place); and he reports that Philochorus quoted Hesiod's description of Hellopia, a land rich in grain and meadows, at whose edge Dodona is built (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 97 Evelyn-White; *Geography* 7.7.10). The longer fragment, from a scholium on Sophocles' *Women of Trachis*, adds that Zeus loved the place and made it his oracle. See [[strabo]], [[dodona]] and [[selli]].
