summary: Achilles rouses the Myrmidons with their own complaints, Patroclus with the honor of Achilles, Sarpedon the fleeing Lycians with shame, and the dying Sarpedon Glaucus with the reproach that will follow if his body is stripped. Glaucus and Apollo rebuke Hector back into the fight; once, when Zeus has turned the battle, Hector calls his men to flight.

**The Myrmidons.** Achilles turns his men's complaints against them. All through his wrath they blamed him for keeping them idle by the ships: “Hard son of Peleus, your mother reared you on anger” (16.203; see [[cholos]] and [[retelling]]). Now “the great work of the din of battle has appeared, which you loved before”, and each man who has a valiant heart should fight (16.207–209). Patroclus' speech has the usual call, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (16.270), the verse of Hector's rallies (6.112, 8.174, 11.287, 15.487) which Ajax used at the ships (15.734), and a reason of his own: “so that we may honor the son of Peleus”, and Agamemnon may learn his blind folly (16.271–274; see [[time]] and [[ate]]). Both speeches end in the verse of the rally, “So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man” (16.210, 16.275; see [[so-he-spoke]]).

**The Lycians.** Sarpedon calls to his fleeing companions, “Shame, Lycians! Where are you fleeing? Now be swift!” (16.422; see [[aidos]]). αἰδώς was the cry of Hera, Agamemnon, Poseidon and Ajax to the Argives (5.787, 8.228, 13.95, 15.502); this is the only time the cry is raised on the Trojan side. Dying, he charges Glaucus to rouse the Lycian leaders to fight around his body, and to fight for him himself, “For I will be a shame and a reproach to you hereafter, / all your days without end, if the Achaeans / strip me of my armor” (16.495–500; see [[oneidos]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). Glaucus does it in the same verses (16.532–533 = 16.495–496), and Patroclus answers with an exhortation to the two Ajaxes over the same body (16.556–561; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

**Hector.** Twice Hector is rebuked into the fight: by Glaucus, who accuses him of forgetting the allies who waste their lives for him (16.538–547; see [[trojan-allies]]), and by Apollo in the likeness of Asius, “Hector, why do you cease from battle? You have no need to” (16.721). Between the two, when Zeus has put a craven heart in him, Hector mounts his chariot and turns to flight, and calls on the other Trojans to flee (16.656–658; see [[retreat]]).
