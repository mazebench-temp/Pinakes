summary: When Patroclus routs the Trojans, Sarpedon shames the fleeing Lycians and goes to meet him himself. Zeus, grieving, thinks of snatching his son out of the battle, yields to Hera and sheds a rain of blood in his honor. Patroclus kills Sarpedon's attendant and then Sarpedon, who dies calling on Glaucus to defend his body; the Trojans grieve for him and fight over the body until the Lycians flee and the Achaeans strip his armor. Apollo carries him out of the battle, and Sleep and Death take him home to Lycia.

**His companions.** The first Lycians to die in the book are “brave companions of Sarpedon”, Atymnius and Maris, “the spearmen sons of Amisodarus, who reared the Chimaera” (16.327–328; see [[atymnius-son-of-amisodarus]], [[maris]] and [[amisodarus]]): the beast that Sarpedon's grandfather Bellerophon killed in Lycia (6.179–183; see [[chimaera]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). Then Patroclus pens the routed Trojans between the ships, the river and the wall and kills them one after another (16.394–418).

**To meet Patroclus.**

{{quote:aceb1add-b249-5489-8b4e-50314f03de77}}

ἀμιτροχίτωνας, “who wear their tunics without the band” (16.419), is said of the Lycians only here (see the translation's note on 16.419 and [[tunics-without-the-band]]). αἰδὼς ὦ Λύκιοι, “Shame, Lycians!”, is the Achaean rebuke αἰδὼς Ἀργεῖοι turned on his own men (5.787, 8.228, 13.95, 15.502; see [[aidos]]), and the verse that brings it in is the one with which he called the Lycians back at the Achaean wall (16.421 ≈ 12.408; see [[lycians]]). He does not know the man in Achilles' armor: “so that I may learn / whoever he is who is prevailing here, and has done much harm / to the Trojans” are, but for the first half-verse, Aeneas' words to Pandarus about the unknown Diomedes (16.423–425; 16.424–425 = 5.175–176; see [[aeneas]]). He leaps from his chariot, Patroclus leaps from his, and they rush at each other screaming, like two vultures on a high rock (16.426–430; see [[like-vultures]]).

**His father's grief.** Zeus sees them and pities them, and tells Hera that Sarpedon, “dearest of men to me”, is fated to die at Patroclus' hands (see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]):

{{quote:f1d7dbd3-4d36-5292-ad17-c111ab0fe5d3}}

Zeus foretold this death himself (15.67), and twice he has kept Sarpedon from death, when Tlepolemus wounded him and at the Achaean wall (5.662, 12.402–403; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). At the wall the narrator said where he was not to fall, “at the sterns of the ships” (12.403); he dies on the plain, between the ships and the city. Hera asks whether he means to set free from death a mortal man “long since ordained to his portion”, and warns that other gods would want to save their sons, “for many are fighting around the great city of Priam, / sons of immortals” (16.441–449; see [[hera]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). In Book 15 she told Ares that his son Ascalaphus was dead, “the dearest of men to him”, the words Zeus uses here of Sarpedon, and Athena had to take the spear from Ares' hand to keep him from avenging him (15.110–142; see [[ascalaphus]] and [[ares]]). She offers instead burial at home: Death and Sleep are to carry him to Lycia, “where his brothers and his kinsmen will give him burial / with a tomb and a pillar; for that is the prize of the dead” (16.456–457; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[tomb]]). Zeus agrees, “and he shed bloody raindrops on the earth, / honoring his dear son, whom Patroclus was to / kill in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (16.459–461; see [[zeus]]). Wounded in Book 5, Sarpedon asked Hector to let him die in Troy, “since I, it seems, was not to / return home to my dear native land / and gladden my dear wife and my infant son” (5.686–688). He dies far from home, but his body goes back.

**The duel.** Patroclus kills Sarpedon's attendant Thrasymelus, and Sarpedon, missing Patroclus, kills the trace horse Pedasus, whose fall tangles the team until Automedon cuts it loose (16.462–475; see [[thrasymelus]] and [[automedon]]). Sarpedon misses again, the point passing over Patroclus' left shoulder, and Patroclus does not:

{{quote:76f90686-44a9-5933-bdd6-ebfa9bbcea9d}}

He falls in the five verses that told the fall of Asius, son of Hyrtacus, at the ships, “roaring, clutching at the bloody dust” (16.482–486 = 13.389–393; see [[the-felled-tree]] and [[asius]]). At the wall Zeus roused him against the Argives “like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.293); now he dies like the bull under the lion's jaws, the leader of the Lycians raging as he is killed (16.487–491; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

**His last words.**

{{quote:eabe2e8c-75bb-54e4-8ae1-f8d9781a8718}}

Γλαῦκε πέπον, “Glaucus, my friend” (16.492): at the wall he called him ὦ πέπον, “My friend”, when he told him why the kings of Lycia must fight among the foremost and win glory or give it to another (12.322–328; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedons-speech]]). Now he gives Glaucus the charge of his body and armor, as he once asked Hector not to leave him “as prey for the Danaans” (5.684). If the Achaeans strip him, he will be κατηφείη καὶ ὄνειδος, “a shame and a reproach”, to Glaucus all his days (16.498–500; see [[oneidos]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

“As he spoke, the end of death covered / his eyes and nostrils” (16.502–503): the verse of Sarpedon's death returns for Patroclus and for Hector (16.855, 22.361), the three great deaths of the poem, each after a speech (see [[the-end-of-death]]). Patroclus sets his heel on his chest and draws out the spear, “and the midriff followed with it; / and he drew out his soul and the point of the spear together” (16.504–505; see [[psyche]]). The Myrmidons hold his snorting horses, which have lost their lords (16.506–507).

**The fight for the body.** Glaucus, healed by Apollo, tells Hector that “Sarpedon lies dead, the leader of the shield-bearing Lycians, / who guarded Lycia with his judgments and his strength” (16.541–542), and the Trojans are seized with grief, “since he was a prop of their city, / foreigner though he was” (16.549–550; see [[glaucus]], [[hector]] and [[trojan-allies]]). δίκῃσι, “judgments”, is the word's second use in the Iliad, after the storm simile of this book (16.388; see the translation's note on 16.384–392). On the other side Patroclus calls him “the man who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (16.558): the narrator said it of Hector, though it was Sarpedon who pulled down the battlement and “made a path for many” (12.438, 12.397–399; see the translation's note on 16.558). Patroclus wants to defile the body and strip it (16.559–561), and Zeus stretches deadly night over the battle, “so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.567–568; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Hector kills the Myrmidon Epeigeus as he takes hold of the body (16.577–580; see [[epeigeus]]), and the dead king vanishes under the fighting:

{{quote:a20737fe-9ace-55cb-a282-93cd094f5a3e}}

The men throng around the body like flies buzzing around the milk pails in spring (16.641–644; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]).

When Zeus turns Hector to flight, “not even the mighty Lycians stood their ground, but fled in rout, / all of them, when they saw their king harmed in the heart, / lying in the heap of the dead” (16.659–661; see [[basileus]]). The Achaeans strip “the armor from the shoulders of Sarpedon, / bronze, gleaming”, and Patroclus gives it to his companions to carry to the ships (16.663–665).

**Home to Lycia.** Zeus sends Apollo to carry the body out of range of the missiles, wash it in the river, anoint it with ambrosia, clothe it in immortal clothing and give it to Sleep and Death, the twins, to carry home (16.666–675; see [[apollo]], [[ambrosia]], [[sleep]] and [[death]]). The god does each thing in the words of the command:

{{quote:65f0f4c6-2fc0-5ecd-8d4a-be18ff2b00e6}}

Zeus' first wish, to snatch him up alive and set him down in Lycia (θείω, 16.437), is fulfilled after his death: the twins set him down (κάτθεσαν, 16.683) “in the rich land of wide Lycia” (see [[lycia]]). The burial with a tomb and a pillar is foretold twice (16.457, 16.675) and never told.

**Afterward.** In Book 17 Glaucus accuses Hector of leaving Sarpedon, his guest-friend and companion (ξεῖνον καὶ ἑταῖρον, 17.150), as prey and spoil for the Argives, and proposes to win back his armor by dragging Patroclus' body into Troy (17.150–163). At Patroclus' funeral games Achilles sets out the arms of Sarpedon that Patroclus had taken from him (τεύχεα Σαρπήδοντος, 23.800) as prizes for the duel in armor (23.798–800; see [[contests]]).

His epithets in the book are δῖος, “brilliant” (16.638, 16.678), and ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (16.649); he is Λυκίων ἀγὸς ἀσπιστάων, “the leader of the shield-bearing Lycians” (16.490, 16.541; see [[shield-bearing]]), ἄναξ, “lord” (16.464), Διὸς υἱός, “son of Zeus” (16.522; see [[son-of-zeus]]), and to the fleeing Lycians their βασιλεύς, “king” (16.660; see [[brilliant]], [[antitheos]], [[anax]] and [[basileus]]).
