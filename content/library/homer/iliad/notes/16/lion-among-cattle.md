{{quote:99dd8deb-eb9f-57a7-914d-a9c103992f64}}

ἠΰτε ταῦρον ἔπεφνε λέων ἀγέληφι μετελθὼν / αἴθωνα μεγάθυμον ἐν εἰλιπόδεσσι βόεσσι, / ὤλετό τε στενάχων ὑπὸ γαμφηλῇσι λέοντος: “As a lion comes upon a herd and kills a bull, / tawny and great-hearted, among the shambling cattle, / and it dies groaning under the lion's jaws” (16.487–489). The bull is αἴθων, “tawny”, the lion's own color in other similes (11.548, 18.161; see [[tawny]]), and μεγάθυμος, “great-hearted”, a word for heroes given here to a beast. The cattle are εἰλίποδες, “shambling” (see [[shambling]]). ἀγέληφι, “in the herd”, places the bull as Agamemnon was placed when he stood out among the heroes like a bull among the gathered cattle (2.480–483; see [[bull-above-the-herd]]). γαμφηλαί are jaws, the jaws in which two lions carry off a goat (13.200).

The tenor: ὣς ὑπὸ Πατρόκλῳ Λυκίων ἀγὸς ἀσπιστάων / κτεινόμενος μενέαινε, φίλον δʼ ὀνόμηνεν ἑταῖρον, “so under Patroclus the leader of the shield-bearing Lycians / raged as he was killed, and called his dear companion by name” (16.490–491). The bull is the leader of the herd, as Sarpedon is of the Lycians, and it does not die quietly: it groans, as Sarpedon roared when he fell (βεβρυχώς, 16.486), and the tenor's verb is μενέαινε, “raged”. Then he speaks, charging Glaucus to fight for his body and his armor (16.492–501; see [[sarpedon]] and [[glaucus]]). The simile is the second for his death, after the felled tree (16.482–486; see [[the-felled-tree]]): the tree gives the size of the fall, the bull the anger of the dying man.

The image turns Sarpedon's own lions round. Zeus roused his son against the Argives “like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293), and he went at the wall like a mountain lion long without meat, ready to seize a sheep or to be struck himself (12.299–306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). At his death he is the bull and Patroclus the lion. Patroclus keeps the lion's likeness until the end of the book, when Hector kills him as a lion overpowers a boar (16.823–826; see [[the-lion-and-the-boar]] and [[lion]]). In Book 17 Automedon, having killed Aretus, mounts his chariot “bloody, like a lion that has eaten a bull” (17.540–542), and on the shield of Achilles two lions drag a bellowing bull away from the herd while the herdsmen set on the dogs (18.579–586).
