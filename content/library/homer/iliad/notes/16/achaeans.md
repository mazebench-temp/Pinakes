---
summary: The book begins with the Achaeans beaten down at the ships, their best men wounded and the first ship on fire, and turns when the Myrmidons come out: the Trojans are driven back, and the Achaeans would have taken Troy at the hands of Patroclus if Apollo had not stood on the wall. At evening they are “stronger beyond their portion”, and Patroclus' fall grieves the whole army (16.21–29, 16.112–123, 16.257–305, 16.698–701, 16.777–780, 16.822).
---

**Beaten down.** Patroclus begins with their plight: “Achilles, son of Peleus, by far the strongest of the Achaeans, / do not be indignant; such grief has overpowered the Achaeans” (16.21–22; see [[patroclus]], [[achos]] and [[achilles]]). All their best men lie wounded among the ships (16.23–29; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), and Achilles hears Hector's voice “calling to the Trojans”, who “hold the whole plain, defeating the Achaeans in battle” (16.77–79; see [[hector]]). He asks the weeping Patroclus whether he grieves for the Argives perishing “because of their own transgression” (16.17–18; see [[argives]]). In his prayer to Zeus he remembers what his first prayer did: “you honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans hard” (16.237; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The narrator calls on the Muses to tell “how fire first fell upon the ships of the Achaeans” (16.113; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]] and [[protesilaus]]).

**The turn.** When the Myrmidons fall on the Trojans in a body, “around them the ships / echoed terribly with the shouting of the Achaeans” (16.276–277; see [[myrmidons]]). Patroclus puts out the fire, and the Danaans draw breath, but “there was no respite from the war; / for the Trojans were not yet fleeing before the Achaeans, dear to Ares” (16.302–303; see [[danaans]] and [[dear-to-ares]]). Over Sarpedon's body the Trojans push back “the bright-eyed Achaeans”, and grief seizes them when Bathycles falls, “nor did the Achaeans / forget their courage” (16.569, 16.599–602; see [[bright-eyed]] and [[bathycles]]). The armies are named in full as they close over the body: “Trojans and Lycians and Myrmidons and Achaeans” (16.564).

**Almost Troy.** “There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy / at the hands of Patroclus; for he raged ahead with his spear, / had not Phoebus Apollo stood on the well-built tower” (16.698–700; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[high-gated]], [[apollo]] and [[sons-of-the-achaeans]]). In Book 21 Apollo will fear the same, that the Danaans might sack the city that day beyond its fate (21.515–517; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**Beyond their portion.** As the sun moves on toward the time for unyoking oxen, “then indeed the Achaeans were stronger beyond their portion” (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν, 16.779–780; see [[aisa]]). Apollo has just told Patroclus at the wall that it is not his portion (αἶσα) to sack the city (16.707–708). The Achaeans drag Cebriones out of the missiles and strip him (16.781–782; see [[cebriones]]), and Patroclus makes his last charge. When Hector's spear goes through him, “he fell with a thud, and greatly grieved the army of the Achaeans” (16.822; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
