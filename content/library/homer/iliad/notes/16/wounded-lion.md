{{quote:f66816e3-9bcf-5d84-a671-b8e5dc581049}}

ὣς εἰπὼν ἐπὶ Κεβριόνῃ ἥρωϊ βεβήκει / οἶμα λέοντος ἔχων, ὅς τε σταθμοὺς κεραΐζων / ἔβλητο πρὸς στῆθος, ἑή τέ μιν ὤλεσεν ἀλκή· / ὣς ἐπὶ Κεβριόνῃ Πατρόκλεες ἆλσο μεμαώς: “So saying, he went at the hero Cebriones / with the spring of a lion, which as it ravages the farmsteads / is struck in the chest, and its own courage destroys it: / so at Cebriones, Patroclus, you leapt in your eagerness” (16.751–754). οἶμα is the spring or swoop of a beast of prey; Achilles leaps away from the river “with the swoop of an eagle” (αἰετοῦ οἴματʼ ἔχων, 21.252). The lion ravages the farmsteads, σταθμοὺς κεραΐζων, as the two young lions of Book 5 ravaged the farmsteads of men until they were killed (5.557–558; see [[two-lions]]), and it is struck, ἔβλητο, as Sarpedon's mountain lion was ready to be struck among the foremost (12.306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]).

The wounded lion of Book 5 was grazed by the shepherd and only grew fiercer (5.136–143). This one is struck in the chest, and what kills it is its own courage, ἑή … ἀλκή. The thought is the one that ends the simile of the boar or lion at bay, ἀγηνορίη δέ μιν ἔκτα, “and it is his pride that kills him” (12.46; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]), and Andromache's word to Hector, “your own might will destroy you” (φθίσει σε τὸ σὸν μένος, 6.407). The tenor speaks to Patroclus himself, ἆλσο μεμαώς, “you leapt in your eagerness” (see [[apostrophe]]). The simile gives him the lion's courage and the lion's end at once, at the height of his success. The narrator has already said that he went after the Trojans “and was greatly blinded, / the fool; if he had kept the word of the son of Peleus, / he would have escaped the evil doom of black death” (16.685–687), and some thirty verses after the simile Apollo meets him in the battle (16.787–792; see [[patroclus]]).

Hector leaps down on the other side, and the two fight over the body like two lions (16.755–758). Walter Leaf reports that Heyne and others objected to this simile, coming as it does just before a second lion simile, and to a likeness of Patroclus to a lion wounded as it ravages the folds rather than one attacking the hunters (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.752–754). See [[lion]].
