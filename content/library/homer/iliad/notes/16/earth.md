---
summary: The earth is the ground on which men fall and the spear of Aeneas sticks quivering, the native land the allies have left, and the earth that will hold the dead; in a simile the whole black earth is weighed down under Zeus' storm.
---

**The ground.** Men fall face down “on the ground” (ἐπὶ γαίῃ, 16.310, 16.413), Patroclus brings down a row of Trojans “to the bountiful earth” (16.418; see [[bountiful-earth]]), and Aeneas' spear, missing Meriones, goes “quivering into the earth” (16.614). The din of the woodcutters' simile rises “up from the wide-wayed earth” (16.635). When Zeus pours down the rain of late summer on the men who judge crooked ordinances, “the whole black earth is weighed down” (16.384; see [[black-earth]] and [[zeus]]).

**The holder of the dead.** Patroclus rebukes Meriones' taunt: the Trojans will not give ground from Sarpedon's body for abusive words; “first the earth will hold many a man” (16.627–629; see [[patroclus]] and [[meriones]]). The earth holds the dead as it held Helen's brothers in Book 3, “the life-giving earth already held them fast” (3.243; see [[life-giving-earth]]).

**The native land.** Glaucus reproaches Hector with the allies who are wasting away their lives for his sake, “far from their friends and their native land” (16.539; see [[glaucus]] and [[trojan-allies]]), and Hector taunts the dying Patroclus with his hope of taking the Trojan women “in your ships to your dear native land” (16.832; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[hector]]). Sarpedon is to die “in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (16.461; see [[sarpedon]]), and Sleep and Death will carry him home to be buried in the land of wide Lycia (16.454–457; see [[lycia]]).
