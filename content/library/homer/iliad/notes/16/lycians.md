summary: The Lycians lose Sarpedon's companions, the sons of Amisodarus, and flee from Patroclus until Sarpedon shames them and goes to meet him. When he falls, Glaucus rouses their leaders to fight for the body of their king, but when Zeus turns Hector to flight they too run, leaving Sarpedon to be stripped.

**Sarpedon's men.** The first Lycians to die are “brave companions of Sarpedon”, Atymnius and Maris, sons of Amisodarus, “who reared the Chimaera” that Bellerophon killed in Lycia (16.317–329; see [[chimaera]] and [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). When Patroclus has killed many more, Sarpedon sees his companions, ἀμιτροχίτωνες, “who wear their tunics without the band”, fall (see [[tunics-without-the-band]]), and shames the rest: “Shame, Lycians! Where are you fleeing? Now be swift!” (16.419–422; see [[sarpedon]] and [[aidos]]). νῦν θοοὶ ἔστε may also be a taunt, “now you are swift” (see the translation's note on 16.422). At the Achaean wall he called the “godlike Lycians” to their work in nearly the same verse (12.408 ≈ 16.421).

**Their leaders.** “The leader of the shield-bearing Lycians”, Λυκίων ἀγὸς ἀσπιστάων, is Sarpedon as he dies and when Glaucus tells Hector of his death (16.490, 16.541), and then Glaucus, who is the first to turn and kill a Myrmidon (16.593; see [[glaucus]] and [[shield-bearing]]). The dying king's first charge to Glaucus is to rouse “the leaders of the Lycians” to fight around him (16.495–496), and Glaucus does it in the same words (16.532–533). He prays to Apollo that he may call his Lycian companions to the fight (16.524–525; see [[apollo]]). Then “Trojans and Lycians and Myrmidons and Achaeans” close around the body (16.564).

**The flight.** Patroclus, angered for Epeigeus, rushes “straight at the Lycians” and at the Trojans (16.584–585). When Zeus turns Hector to flight, “not even the mighty Lycians stood their ground, but fled in rout, / all of them, when they saw their king harmed in the heart, / lying in the heap of the dead” (16.659–661), and the Achaeans strip Sarpedon's armor (16.663–665). Patroclus goes after “the Trojans and Lycians” toward the city (16.685). The Lycians at home, Sarpedon's “brothers and his kinsmen”, are to bury him in Lycia (16.456, 16.674; see [[lycia]]). In Book 17 Glaucus threatens to take the Lycians home, since Hector left Sarpedon's body to the Argives (17.142–155).
