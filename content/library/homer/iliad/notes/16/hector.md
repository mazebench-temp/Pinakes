summary: Hector lops the head from Ajax's spear, and the Trojans set the first ship on fire. When Patroclus comes out in Achilles' armor, Hector sees that the victory has turned and is carried from the field; rebuked by Glaucus, he leads the fight for Sarpedon's body until Zeus puts a craven heart in him. At the Scaean gates Apollo, in the likeness of his uncle Asius, sends him against Patroclus. Patroclus kills his charioteer Cebriones, and when Apollo and Euphorbus have struck, Hector kills Patroclus, boasts over him, and hears his own death foretold.

**The voice of Hector.** Achilles, explaining why the Achaeans are losing, says that Diomedes' spear no longer rages and that he hears no shout from Agamemnon, “but the voice of man-slaying Hector, / calling to the Trojans, breaks all around” (16.77–78; see [[achilles]] and [[man-slaying]]). In his prayer he names Hector as the man who must learn what Patroclus can do: “so that Hector too / may know whether our attendant knows how to fight / even alone” (16.242–244).

**The fire.** The narrator calls on the Muses to tell “how fire first fell upon the ships of the Achaeans” (16.113; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]), and the answer begins with Hector:

{{quote:508c0888-39e7-5200-88d8-cfcbc040242d}}

Ajax, who held the ship through the end of Book 15, sees in the docked shaft “the work of the gods”: Zeus “wished victory for the Trojans” (16.120–121; see [[ajax]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). So Hector keeps the vow he made in Book 8, “so that I may set fire to the ships and kill the men themselves” (8.182), and Zeus sees what he was waiting for, “the blaze of a burning ship” (15.600), before he turns the battle. The fire plays about the stern of Protesilaus' ship, which Hector seized at the end of Book 15 (16.124, 16.286; 15.704–706; see [[protesilaus]] and [[stern]]), and Achilles, seeing it, sends Patroclus out (16.124–129).

**The victory turns.** Patroclus kills Pyraechmes, puts out the fire and drives the Trojans from the ships (16.284–305). Hector does not run at once:

{{quote:30ed52a9-77ee-5fe0-9688-cce08c753d92}}

ἰδρείῃ πολέμοιο, “in his skill at war” (16.359), sheltering under his shield of bull's hide: in Book 7 he told Ajax that he knew how to swing the dry oxhide to the right and to the left (7.237–239; see [[shield]]). ἑτεραλκέα νίκην, “the victory that turns the battle” (16.362), is what Zeus' thunder promised the Trojans in Book 8 (8.171). Then his swift-footed horses carry him out of the battle with his armor, and he leaves behind the Trojan army, held back against its will by the ditch, where many horses break their lords' chariots at the end of the pole (16.367–371; see [[the-horses-of-hector]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). Patroclus, eager to hit him, cannot reach him: “for he was eager to hit him; but his swift horses carried him away” (16.383). At the end of the book the same verse is said of Hector chasing Automedon (16.866).

**Glaucus' rebuke.** When Sarpedon has fallen, Glaucus goes to the Trojan leaders, “and went after Aeneas and Hector armed in bronze” (16.536; see [[armed-in-bronze]]), and reproaches Hector: “Hector, now you have utterly forgotten your allies” (16.538; see [[glaucus]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Sarpedon made the same complaint in Book 5 (5.472–492), and Glaucus will make it again, more bitterly, in Book 17 (17.142–168). This time Hector answers with action: the Trojans go straight at the Danaans, “and leading them was / Hector, angered for Sarpedon” (16.552–553; see [[sarpedon]]). With a stone he splits the head of the Myrmidon Epeigeus as he takes hold of the body (16.577–580; see [[epeigeus]] and [[stone-throwing]]), and when Patroclus charges like a hawk, “The champions and shining Hector gave ground” (16.588).

**The craven heart.** Zeus, watching the fight over Sarpedon, ponders whether Hector should kill Patroclus there “and take the armor from his shoulders”, or Patroclus should first drive “the Trojans and Hector armed in bronze” back to the city (16.644–655; see [[zeus]]). He chooses the second:

{{quote:11444974-c1ac-545e-8240-afe1d1f5c8d1}}

ἀνάλκιδα θυμόν, “a craven heart”, is the heart of the lambs that the wolves tear apart earlier in the book (16.355; see [[like-wolves]]). The scales that Hector “knew” are the image of Zeus' decision, which he once held up at midday over the two armies (8.69–74), and in Book 22 they will weigh Hector's own death (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Once he has fled, “not even the mighty Lycians stood their ground” (16.659; see [[lycians]]). Zeus' first alternative is put off, not given up: Hector will take the armor from Patroclus in Book 17 (17.125).

**At the Scaean gates.** “And Hector held his single-hoofed horses at the Scaean gates; / for he was in doubt whether to drive back into the turmoil and fight, / or to call to the army to crowd within the wall” (16.712–714; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]). Achilles told the embassy that while he fought, Hector came no farther from the city than the Scaean gates and the oak (9.352–354). Apollo comes to him in the likeness of his uncle Asius, Hecuba's brother (16.715–720; see [[asius-son-of-dymas]] and [[apollo]]):

{{quote:6ce6a2fb-9b1d-53fa-aaef-1ed47e39410e}}

Hector obeys at once. He tells Cebriones to lash the horses into the war, and Apollo sends turmoil among the Argives “and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (16.730), the half-verse of the god's help with the aegis in Book 15 and of Zeus' help at the wall (15.327, 12.255). “And Hector let the other Danaans be, and did not slay them, / but he drove his strong-hoofed horses at Patroclus” (16.731–732).

**Cebriones.** Patroclus' stone strikes “Hector's charioteer, / Cebriones, the bastard son of famous Priam” (16.737–738), the third charioteer Hector loses, after Eniopeus and Archeptolemus in Book 8 (8.119–123, 8.312–315; see [[cebriones]], [[eniopeus]] and [[archeptolemus]]). Hector leaps down, and the two fight over the body like two hungry lions over a slain deer (16.755–758): “Hector, once he had taken hold of his head, would not let go; / and Patroclus on the other side held his foot” (16.762–763; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). They are δύω μήστωρες ἀϋτῆς, “two masters of the war cry” (16.759; see [[masters-of-the-war-cry]]). Late in the day the Achaeans drag the body away and strip it (16.777–782).

**The helmet.** When Apollo strikes the helmet of Achilles from Patroclus' head, the narrator looks ahead: “but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.799–800). He will put on Achilles' armor in Book 17, and Zeus, seeing him, will say that he will not come home from the battle (17.192–208).

**The killing.** Apollo has stripped Patroclus, and Euphorbus has wounded him in the back (16.788–815; see [[euphorbus]]). Then:

{{quote:112854c8-853c-537e-a604-0c0e726a8ba2}}

νείατον ἐς κενεῶνα, “in the lowest part of the flank” (16.821): there Diomedes' spear, driven by Athena, wounded Ares (5.857). The simile gives Hector the lion's part and Patroclus the boar's, two beasts fighting over a little spring (see [[the-lion-and-the-boar]]), “so, though the valiant son of Menoetius had killed many, / Hector, son of Priam, robbed him of his life with the spear at close range” (16.827–828; see [[son-of-priam]]). So Zeus' forecast is fulfilled: “shining Hector will kill him with the spear / before Ilios” (15.65–66; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The boast.**

{{quote:a6f498cb-2940-594c-832f-37f3372f3632}}

Hector imagines Patroclus' purpose in the words with which he foresaw Andromache's captivity, “taking away your day of freedom” (6.455; see [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]), and sets himself against it as the one who wards off from the Trojans ἦμαρ ἀναγκαῖον, “the day of compulsion” (16.836). νήπιε, “you fool!” (16.833), is the narrator's word for Patroclus when he begs to go out and when he forgets Achilles' warning (16.46, 16.686; see [[nepios]]). The command he puts in Achilles' mouth, to tear “man-slaying Hector's / bloody tunic around his chest” (16.840–841), is what Agamemnon prayed for at the start of the war, that he might tear “the tunic of Hector around his chest” (2.416); Achilles had told Patroclus the opposite, to drive the Trojans from the ships and come back (16.87–96). “here the vultures will eat you” (16.836; see [[vultures]]) is the threat of an unburied body, and in Book 17 Hector drags Patroclus' body away to cut off his head and give the corpse to the Trojan dogs (17.125–127; see [[dogs]]). ἆ δείλʼ, “Ah, wretch” (16.837): Zeus will begin with the same words when he sees Hector in Achilles' armor (17.201).

**The prophecy.** Patroclus answers that Zeus and Apollo gave Hector the victory, that fate and the son of Leto killed him, and of men Euphorbus, “and you are the third to slay me” (16.844–850). Then:

{{quote:c0b41005-42a5-5592-94c8-3d8a6498e676}}

The verse of death standing close, ἄγχι παρέστηκεν θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, Thetis will say to Achilles himself (16.853 = 24.132; see [[mighty-fate]]). The three verses of Patroclus' death (16.855–857; see [[the-end-of-death]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]) will close Hector's own, when he, dying, foretells that Paris and Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.358–363; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[prophecy]]).

**The answer to the dead.**

{{quote:34fe4ed6-e1c9-5e43-8fdf-290171ebf478}}

τὸν καὶ τεθνηῶτα προσηύδα, “And to him, though he was dead, … spoke” (16.858): the verse returns when Achilles answers the dead Hector (22.364). Hector's hope, τίς δʼ οἶδʼ, “Who knows”, is the phrase with which Nestor, and then Patroclus himself, hoped to stir Achilles' heart (11.792, 15.403). He draws the spear out with his heel on the body, λὰξ προσβάς (16.863), as Ajax did over Amphius (5.620), and goes after Automedon, but the immortal horses, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus”, carry Automedon away (16.864–867; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

His epithets in the book are ἀνδροφόνος, “man-slaying” (16.77, 16.840), χαλκοκορυστής, “armed in bronze” (16.358, 16.536, 16.654), φαίδιμος, “shining” (16.577, 16.588, 16.649, 16.727, 16.760, 16.858), and ἱππόδαμος, “tamer of horses” (16.717), and he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (16.828; see [[man-slaying]], [[armed-in-bronze]], [[phaidimos]], [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
