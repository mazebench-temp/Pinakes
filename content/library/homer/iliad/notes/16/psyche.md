---
summary: The ψυχή is named four times, each time leaving a man. Hera speaks of the moment when “his soul and his life have left” Sarpedon (16.453); Patroclus draws out Sarpedon's soul with his spear (16.505); Meriones tells Aeneas he would give his soul to Hades (16.625); and Patroclus' own soul flies from his limbs to Hades, lamenting its fate (16.856–857).
---

ψυχή τε καὶ αἰών, “his soul and his life” (16.453), is a pair found once more in Homer, when Odysseus wishes he could rob the Cyclops of both (*Odyssey* 9.523). αἰών is the span and force of life, the word of Achilles' “my life will be long” (9.415), and of Sarpedon's own plea when he was wounded in Book 5: let Hector defend his body, “and afterward let life leave me / in your city” (5.685–686), since he would not go home to Lycia. In Book 16 he dies by the Achaean ships, and his body goes home after all (see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]). The death of Sarpedon is told as a drawing out: Patroclus sets his heel on the chest and pulls the spear from the flesh, “and the midriff followed with it; / and he drew out his soul and the point of the spear together” (16.504–505; see [[phrenes]]). In Meriones' retort the soul is a thing given, with the triumph, to Hades “of the famous horses” (16.625; see [[hades]]). The fullest account is the last: “and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades, / lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.856–857; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]), two verses that Plato would strike from the poem (*Republic* 3.386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).
