summary: Aeacus is named through the patronymic of his son and his grandson. Achilles calls his father “Peleus, son of Aeacus” (16.15), and five times the narrator and Patroclus name Achilles “the grandson of Aeacus”: in the arming, over the Myrmidons, in Patroclus' dying words, and of Automedon at the end of the book (16.134, 16.140, 16.165, 16.854, 16.865).
# Αἰακίδης (nominative) names Peleus, Aeacus' son, at 16.15 and 21.189; the genitive Αἰακίδαο and the
# accusative Αἰακίδην (claimed in the Book 9 note) are always Achilles.
grc: [Αἰακίδης]
---

**The son.** ζώει δʼ Αἰακίδης Πηλεὺς μετὰ Μυρμιδόνεσσι, “and Peleus, son of Aeacus, lives among the Myrmidons” (16.15; see [[peleus]] and [[phthia]]). It is the first time the poem gives the patronymic to Peleus himself; until now Αἰακίδης has named only Achilles (2.860, 2.874, 9.184, 9.191, 10.402, 11.805; see [[grandson-of-aeacus]]). Achilles will give the whole line to the river god he fights: Zeus begot Aeacus, Aeacus begot Peleus, “Peleus, son of Aeacus” (Πηλεὺς Αἰακίδης), and Peleus begot him (21.187–189).

**The grandson.** ποδώκεος Αἰακίδαο, “of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus”, ends the verse three times in the book: the starry corselet that Patroclus puts on is his (16.134), Patroclus is “the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.165), and Automedon, whom Hector chases in the last verses, is “the godlike attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (16.865; see [[swift-footed]], [[patroclus]] and [[automedon]]). ἀμύμονος Αἰακίδαο, “of the noble grandson of Aeacus”, is used of the spear Patroclus leaves behind, the Pelian ash (16.140), and of the man who will kill Hector: “to be brought down at the hands of Achilles, noble grandson of Aeacus” (16.854; see [[achilles]], [[blameless]] and [[hector]]).
