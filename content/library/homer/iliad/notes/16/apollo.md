summary: Apollo heals the wounded Glaucus when he prays, and at Zeus' command carries Sarpedon's body out of the battle, washes and anoints it and gives it to Sleep and Death. He drives Patroclus three times from the wall of Troy, rouses Hector in the likeness of Asius, and at last, hidden in mist, strikes Patroclus and strips him of the armor of Achilles; the dying Patroclus names him first among his killers.

**The Trojans' god.** Achilles warns Patroclus not to lead the way on to Ilios, “lest one of the everlasting gods from Olympus / come in against you; far-working Apollo loves them dearly” (16.93–94; see [[far-worker]] and [[achilles]]). The warning comes true: when Patroclus goes on toward the city, it is Apollo who meets him, first on the wall and then in the battle.

**The healer.** Glaucus, still in pain from the arrow that Teucer shot him at the wall (12.387–391; 16.510–512), prays: “Hear me, lord, who are somewhere in the rich land of Lycia / or in Troy; and you can hear from everywhere / a man in sorrow” (16.514–516; see [[glaucus]], [[prayer]] and [[anax]]). Glaucus does not know whether the god is in Lycia or at Troy; Pandarus, too, prays to “Apollo, the Lycian-born” (4.101, 4.119; see [[lycian-born]] and [[lycia]]). Glaucus asks him to heal the wound, lull the pains and give him strength to fight over Sarpedon (16.523–526), and the prayer is answered in the formula of Chryses' prayers: “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (16.527 = 1.43, 1.457).

The god who sent the plague in Book 1 is here the healer (see [[healing]]); in Book 5 it was Leto and Artemis who healed Aeneas in his temple on Pergamus (5.445–448).

**Sarpedon's body.** When the Lycians have fled and Sarpedon has been stripped, Zeus calls him “dear Phoebus” and sends him to cleanse the body, wash it in the river, anoint it with ambrosia, clothe it in immortal clothing and give it to swift escorts, “Sleep and Death, the twins”, to carry home to Lycia (16.666–675; see [[zeus]], [[ambrosia]], [[sleep]] and [[death]]). “So he spoke, and Apollo did not fail to heed his father” (16.676), and he goes down from the mountains of Ida (16.677), the verses that sent him to Hector in Book 15 (15.236–237; see [[ida]]). He does each thing as he is bidden (16.678–683). The god of the Trojans' side gives Sarpedon the care that Hera promised: not life, but burial at home (16.453–457; see [[hera]] and [[sarpedon]]).

**At the wall.** Patroclus pursues the Trojans to the city, and the Achaeans would have taken Troy, “had not Phoebus Apollo stood on the well-built tower, / intending his destruction, and helping the Trojans” (16.698–701; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). Three times Patroclus sets foot on the angle of the wall, and three times Apollo drives him back, “thrusting at the shining shield with his immortal hands”; the fourth time, when he rushes on “like a god”, Apollo shouts at him (16.702–706; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]):

{{quote:17d579e4-dfa5-5152-a5b1-b109489b2ac8}}

The scene repeats Diomedes' attack on Aeneas in Book 5, where the god said “Take thought, son of Tydeus, and give way” and Diomedes drew back “avoiding the wrath of Apollo who shoots from afar” (5.436–444; see [[diomedes]], [[hekatebolos]] and the translation's note on 16.698–711). Diomedes drew back “a little way”; Patroclus goes back “a long way” (16.710). Apollo's words deny Achilles' wish that he and Patroclus might take Troy alone (16.97–100): the city will fall neither to Patroclus nor to Achilles (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**In the likeness of Asius.** At the Scaean gates Hector is in doubt whether to fight or to call the army inside the wall, and Apollo stands beside him “in the likeness of a man, young and strong, / Asius, who was the uncle of Hector, tamer of horses, / own brother of Hecuba, and son of Dymas” (16.712–718; see [[hector]], [[asius-son-of-dymas]] and [[scaean-gates]]). “In his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him” (16.720; see [[son-of-zeus]]): the verse with which he will speak to Aeneas as Lycaon (20.82), as Poseidon came to the Ajaxes as Calchas (13.45). He tells Hector to drive at Patroclus, “and Apollo give you triumph” (16.721–725), goes back into the toil of men, and “sent evil turmoil among the Argives, / and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (16.726–730), the end of the verse with which he routed the Achaeans with the aegis (15.327; see [[kydos]]).

**The blow.** The fourth time that Patroclus rushes on, “like a god” (16.786), “Phoebus met you in the fierce battle, / terrible; and he did not see him coming through the turmoil, / for he came to meet him wrapped in thick mist” (16.788–790). He stands behind him and strikes his back with the flat of his hand, and Patroclus' eyes whirl (16.791–792). The god takes the armor of Achilles off him piece by piece: “Phoebus Apollo struck the helmet from his head” (16.793), the spear breaks in his hands, the shield falls, “And lord Apollo, son of Zeus, loosened his corselet” (16.793–804; see [[helmet]] and [[corselet]]). Then blind folly seizes Patroclus, and Euphorbus and Hector kill him (16.805–828; see [[ate]], [[euphorbus]] and [[patroclus]]). Patroclus is “overpowered by the blow of the god and by the spear” (16.816).

**The son of Leto.** The dying man names his killers. Zeus and Apollo gave Hector the victory, “for they themselves took the armor from my shoulders” (16.845–846), and “deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus; and you are the third to slay me” (16.849–850; see [[leto]] and [[moira]]). Xanthus will tell Achilles the same: it was the best of the gods, the son of Leto, who killed Patroclus and gave Hector glory (19.413–414; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Achilles' own death will come from Apollo too, with Paris, at the Scaean gates (21.277–278, 22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
