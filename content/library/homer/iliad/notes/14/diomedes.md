summary: Wounded, Diomedes comes up from the ships with Agamemnon and Odysseus. When Agamemnon asks for a better plan, “young or old”, he answers as the youngest, claims a hearing by his birth, from Portheus and Oeneus to his father Tydeus, who settled in Argos and lies at Thebes, and gives the counsel the kings follow: go to the battle wounded as they are, keep out of range, and urge the others on (14.29, 14.109–134, 14.379–380).

**The youngest.** “And among them spoke also Diomedes, good at the war cry” (14.109), the verse with which he offered himself for the night raid in Book 10 (10.219; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]]). Agamemnon has just asked for “one who can speak of a better plan than this, / young or old” (14.107–108; see [[agamemnon]]), and Diomedes answers, “The man is near” (14.110). He asks the others not to resent it “because I am the youngest by birth among you” (14.112). Nestor said so in Book 9, when he praised his counsel: Diomedes might be his own son, “the youngest by birth; yet you speak wisely” (9.57–58; see [[nestor]]).

**His father.** He claims the right to be heard by his line: “but I too claim to be of the line of a brave father, / Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes” (14.113–114; see [[tydeus]], [[thebes]] and [[euchomai]]). The account runs down from Portheus, whose three sons lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon, through Oeneus, the best of them, to Tydeus, who settled in Argos after his wanderings, married a daughter of Adrastus, grew rich in wheat fields, orchards and flocks, “and he surpassed all the Achaeans / with the spear” (14.115–125; see [[portheus]], [[oeneus]], [[adrastus]], [[argos]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). “These things you must have heard, if they are true” (14.125): he gives his family's story as men tell it, as Aeneas will tell Achilles that each of them knows the other's lineage by hearsay, from what mortal men say (20.203–204).

The purpose is plain: “So you could not say that by birth I am a coward and a weakling / and dishonor the word I speak, if I speak it well” (14.126–127). The charge has been made before. Agamemnon said in Book 4 that Tydeus fathered a son “worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]), and Diomedes reminded him in Book 9 that he had called him “unwarlike and a coward” (9.34–35). In Book 8 Nestor told him that even if Hector called him “a coward and a weakling”, the Trojans would not believe it (8.153–156). Here, in council, he claims his father's name to be heard. In Book 6 he did not remember Tydeus, who left him as a child, “when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223); now he says where his father lies (see [[son-of-tydeus]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]).

**The counsel.**

{{quote:264a9dfc-7e6d-52d9-8877-d4343a9dedd8}}

The counsel answers Nestor, who said that a wounded man cannot fight (14.63), and Agamemnon, who feared that the army would not fight for him (14.49–51). The kings will go to the war without fighting. Out of range, “lest someone take wound upon wound” (14.130), they will send in the men who have been “doing their hearts a kindness” (θυμῷ ἦρα φέροντες, 14.132), indulging themselves as Hephaestus, “doing a kindness to his dear mother”, served Hera on Olympus (1.572). οὐτάμενοί περ, “stabbed though we are” (14.128), is said of all three, though Paris' arrow struck Diomedes in the foot (11.376–377; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (14.133), the verse that followed Nestor's counsel for the watch in Book 9 (9.79). Then too the young man had the last word of the night: when Achilles sent the envoys back, it was Diomedes who told the kings to rest and fight at dawn, with Agamemnon among the foremost (9.697–709).

**Marshaling the army.** He is “the son of Tydeus” first in the verse that names the three wounded kings as they come up from the ships and again as they marshal the army and share out the armor at Poseidon's command (14.29 = 14.380, 14.379–382; see [[kings]]).
