---
summary: Horses in Book 14 are the team that Hera does not bring and the team that carries Hector away. Zeus asks why she has come without horses or chariot, and she answers that they stand at the foot of Ida (14.298–299, 14.307–308); after Ajax's stone, Hector's companions carry him to his swift horses, which take him toward the city (14.428–435).

**Hera's horses.** Zeus' first words to Hera on Gargarus ask where she is going without horses or chariot: “Hera, where are you bound, that you have come here down from Olympus? And you have no horses here, and no chariot to mount” (14.298–299). The second verse is the one in which Pandarus regrets the horses he left at home in Lycaon's halls, with the person changed (5.192). The gods of the Iliad often travel by chariot: Zeus drove his bronze-footed team to Gargarus and unyoked it there (8.41–50), Hera drove with Athena down to the Simoeis (5.767–777), and Poseidon drove over the sea to the Achaean ships (13.23–38; see [[chariot-journey]]). Hera's answer is part of her tale. Her horses, she says, stand “at the foot of Ida of the many springs, standing ready to carry me over dry land and sea” (14.307–308), to take her to Oceanus and Tethys at the ends of the earth (14.301–311; see [[hera]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). The narrator has shown no horses. She darted down from Olympus and sped over Pieria, Emathia and the snowy mountains of the Thracians, her feet never touching the ground, and from Athos stepped out onto the sea (14.225–230); from Lemnos she came to Ida with Sleep, “clothed in mist” (14.281–285). When she goes back to Olympus at Zeus' bidding, she flies there as fast as a man's thought darts from place to place (15.78–83). πρυμνώρεια, “the foot of a mountain”, occurs only here; ἐπὶ τραφερήν τε καὶ ὑγρήν, “over dry land and sea”, returns once in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 20.98).

**Hector's horses.** When Ajax's stone has felled Hector, his companions lift him in their arms and carry him out of the toil, “until he came to his horses, his swift horses, which were standing behind the battle and the war with their charioteer and the inlaid chariot; and they carried him toward the city, groaning heavily” (14.429–432). These are the verses that told how the wounded Deiphobus was taken from the battle in Book 13 (14.430–432 = 13.536–538; see [[deiphobus]]). At the ford of the Xanthus his companions lift him down ἐξ ἵππων, “from the chariot”, and pour water over him (14.435; see [[hector]] and [[scamander]]); ἵπποι are the team and the car together, as often (see [[chariot]]).

The book's other horses are in its epithets: Thrasymedes “tamer of horses” (14.10; see [[tamer-of-horses]]), “the Danaans of the swift horses” (14.21; see [[of-the-swift-horses]]), “the Gerenian horseman Nestor” (14.52; see [[gerenian-horseman]]), “the horseman Oeneus” (14.117), “the horse-herding Thracians” (14.227; see [[horse-herding]]), and Antenor “tamer of horses” (14.473).
