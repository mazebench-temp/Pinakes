---
summary: Cattle in Book 14 are the herd that Enops was tending by the banks of the Satnioeis when a Naiad nymph bore him Satnius (14.443–445), and the epithet βοῶπις, “ox-eyed”, which Hera bears three times in the book of her deception.
---

**The herdsman.** The son of Oileus stabs Satnius, “the son of Enops, whom a noble Naiad nymph bore to Enops as he tended his cattle by the banks of the Satnioeis” (14.443–445; see [[satnius]], [[enops]], [[nymphs]] and [[satnioeis]]). βουκολέω, “tend cattle”, is the verb used of Anchises when Aphrodite bore him Aeneas (5.313; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]), and [[bucolion|Bucolion]], whose name is the herdsman's, was tending his sheep when the Naiad Abarbarea lay with him and bore him twin sons (6.21–26). Goddesses and nymphs come to herdsmen in the pastures of the Troad, and the poem tells such births as the sons are killed: Bucolion's twins, Aesepus and Pedasus, are stripped by the son of Mecisteus (6.21–28), and Satnius falls to the son of Oileus (see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**Ox-eyed.** βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “ox-eyed queen Hera”, stands at three turns of the deception: when she ponders how she might beguile the mind of Zeus (14.159–160), when she smiles and puts Aphrodite's strap in her bosom (14.222–223), and when she answers Sleep's refusal with the promise of a Grace (14.263–268; see [[ox-eyed]] and [[hera]]).
