---
summary: Achilles is absent but named three times. Agamemnon fears that the army is angry with him “just as Achilles is”; Poseidon, in an old man's likeness, tells Agamemnon that Achilles' deadly heart rejoices at the Achaeans' slaughter and wishes him maimed by a god; and he tells the army that there will be no great longing for Achilles if they defend one another (14.50, 14.139–142, 14.366–369).
refs: [14.141-142, 14.367-368]
---

**Agamemnon's fear.** “Ah, truly the other well-greaved Achaeans too / are laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is” (14.49–50; see [[agamemnon]] and [[cholos]]). The king measures his danger by the quarrel of Book 1 (see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]).

**The deadly heart.** Poseidon, in the likeness of an old man, takes Agamemnon by the hand: “Son of Atreus, now, I think, the deadly heart of Achilles / rejoices in his breast at the slaughter and the panic of the Achaeans / as he looks on, since there is no sense in him, not even a little. / But let him perish so, and may a god maim him” (14.139–142; see [[poseidon]] and [[phrenes]]). Achilles does look on: in Book 11 he was “standing on the stern of his ship, huge in the hull, / watching the sheer toil and the tearful onslaught” (11.600–601; see [[watching-the-war]]). ὀλοὸν κῆρ, “the deadly heart”, is said of no one else in Homer, and σιφλώσειε, “maim”, occurs only here (see the translation's note on 14.142). In Book 13 the same god, in Calchas' likeness, granted that Agamemnon might be wholly to blame, “because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (13.111–113); in an old man's likeness he now lays the blame on Achilles.

**No longing for him.** To the army Poseidon reports Hector's boast and answers it: “So he says and boasts, because Achilles / stays by the hollow ships, angered at heart; / but there will be no great longing for him, if we others / urge ourselves on to defend one another” (14.366–369; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). ποθή, “longing”, is the word of Achilles' oath on the scepter: “Someday a longing for Achilles will come upon the sons of the Achaeans” (1.240; see [[longing-for-achilles]] and [[pothe]]). The god denies the oath, and while Zeus sleeps he is right (see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]): the Achaeans drive the Trojans from the ships without Achilles (14.440–522). Zeus wakes at the beginning of the next book (15.4–8; see [[zeus]]).
