summary: Sheep in Book 14 are wealth. Tydeus, settled in Argos, had wheat fields, orchards and many flocks (14.121–124), and Ilioneus' father Phorbas was “rich in flocks”, the Trojan whom Hermes loved most and to whom he granted wealth (14.489–491).
grc: [πρόβατʼ]
en: [flocks]
refs: [14.490]
---

**Tydeus' flocks.** Diomedes, claiming the right to speak in council though he is the youngest, tells of his father's house. Tydeus settled in Argos after his wanderings and married one of the daughters of Adrastus, “and lived in a house wealthy in substance, and he had fields enough bearing wheat, and many rows of orchard trees all around, and he had many flocks; and he surpassed all the Achaeans with the spear” (14.121–125; see [[tydeus]], [[diomedes]] and [[adrastus]]). Land, trees and beasts are the measure of a man's wealth, and the spear the measure of the man. πρόβατα, “flocks”, livestock, occurs in the Iliad only here and when Antilochus reminds Achilles of the gold, the bronze and the πρόβατα in his hut (23.549–550).

**Rich in flocks.** Peneleos kills Ilioneus, “son of Phorbas, rich in flocks, whom most of the Trojans Hermes loved, and granted him wealth” (14.490–491; see [[rich-in-flocks]], [[ilioneus]] and [[hermes]]). Hermes is a god of herds and of the wealth they bring: in the Odyssey the swineherd sets aside a portion of his feast for the Nymphs and Hermes, son of Maia (*Odyssey* 14.434–436), and Hesiod says that Hecate, “with Hermes”, increases the stock in the steadings, the herds of cattle, the flocks of goats and of woolly sheep (*Theogony* 444–447; see [[hesiod]]). Phorbas' wealth was the god's gift, and Ilioneus was his only son, whose head Peneleos holds up to the Trojans (14.492, 14.499–502; see [[the-poppy]]).
