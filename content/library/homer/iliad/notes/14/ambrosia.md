---
summary: Hera begins her adornment by cleansing her body with ambrosia, and the oil, the tresses and the robe that follow are all ambrosial.
grc: [ἀμβροσίῃ]
passages:
  - 14.170-178 | Ambrosia and oil
---

“First, with ambrosia, she cleansed from her lovely body / all defilements” (ἀμβροσίῃ μὲν πρῶτον ἀπὸ χροὸς ἱμερόεντος / λύματα πάντα κάθηρεν, 14.170–171; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]] and [[purification]]). Ambrosia here is not food but a cleanser, as it is a preserver of the dead: Apollo washes Sarpedon's body in the river and anoints it with ambrosia (16.667–670, 16.679–680), and Thetis drips ambrosia and nectar into Patroclus' nostrils so that his flesh will not decay (19.38–39). What it touches belongs to the gods. The oil that follows is “ambrosial and sweet, which had been perfumed for her” (14.172), the tresses Hera plaits are “fair and ambrosial” (14.177), and the robe Athena made her is ambrosial (14.178; see [[ambrosial]] and [[robe]]). The verse of the oil is used once more, of Aphrodite's anointing at Paphos in the *Hymn to Aphrodite* (*Hymn* 5.63; see the translation's note on 14.172). Proclus took the ambrosia as a symbol of the goddess's undefiled power, beyond the reach of every impurity (*On the Republic* I 138 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
