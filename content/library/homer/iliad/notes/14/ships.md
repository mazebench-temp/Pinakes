---
summary: The narrator tells how the ships were drawn up, in rows filling the whole shore between the headlands, with the first of them hauled up toward the plain and the wall along their sterns. Agamemnon proposes to launch the row nearest the sea and the rest by night, and Odysseus answers that the army would not fight while the ships were dragged down (14.30–36, 14.75–79, 14.96–106).
passages:
  - 14.30-36 | The ships drawn up in rows
---

**Drawn up in rows.** The wounded kings come up “from beside the ships” to look at the battle (14.28), and the narrator stops to explain why they were so far from it:

{{quote:3d5c7190-26cf-583e-9850-9f4f00e2156c}}

τὰς … πρώτας, “the first of them” (14.31), are the ships that were beached first and hauled farthest up toward the plain, so that the wall built along their sterns faced the Trojans (see [[the-achaean-wall]], [[stern]] and the translation's note on 14.31–36). The rest lay behind them to the water's edge, row upon row, προκρόσσας, “in rows” (14.35), a word found only here in Homer. Herodotus uses it of the Persian fleet off Magnesia, which lay at anchor πρόκροσσαι, eight ships deep, because the beach was not large (*Histories* 7.188.1; see [[herodotus]]). The poem does not name the headlands that closed the shore (14.36). Strabo, going along the coast of the Troad, puts the Naval Station, the Harbor of the Achaeans and the Achaean Camp after Rhoeteum, by Sigeum and the mouths of the Scamander (*Geography* 13.1.31; see [[strabo]]). The ships of Ajax and of Achilles were drawn up at the two ends of the line (8.222–226 = 11.5–9; see [[ajax]] and [[achilles]]).

**Launching them.** Agamemnon would begin with the other end: “The ships that are drawn up first, nearest the sea, / let us haul down, and drag all of them into the shining sea, / and anchor them out on their mooring-stones, until there comes / the immortal night … and then we could drag down all the ships” (14.75–79; see [[agamemnon]], [[mooring-stones]] and [[war-and-night]]). The verb that drew the ships up the beach, εἰρύατο, “were drawn up” (14.30, 14.75), now drags them down. Odysseus turns the plan against itself: “you who bid us, while war and the war cry are joined, / to drag the well-benched ships to the sea” (14.96–97), when the Achaeans “will not hold out in the war while the ships are dragged to the sea” (14.99–100; see [[odysseus]] and [[well-benched]]). The phrase was Achilles' threat in Book 9, as Odysseus reported it: at dawn he would drag his well-benched ships to the sea and sail home (9.683; 9.357–363; see [[achilles]]). Agamemnon gives way in the same words: he is not ordering the sons of the Achaeans “to drag the well-benched ships to the sea” against their will (14.105–106). Plato quotes Odysseus' verses in the *Laws* as Homer's own lesson that ships standing by teach soldiers to flee (*Laws* 706d–707a; see [[plato-dragging-the-ships]]).

**What they are fought for.** Hector has threatened to burn the ships and kill the men beside them (14.44–47; see [[hector]]), and Poseidon asks the Argives whether they will yield Hector the victory “so that he may take the ships and win glory” (14.365; see [[poseidon]]). In the battle the sea surges up “toward the huts and ships / of the Argives” (14.392–393), and the stone with which Ajax fells Hector is one of the props of the swift ships that roll at the fighters' feet (14.410; see [[props]] and [[ajax]]). Peneleos, holding up Ilioneus' head, speaks of the day “we young men of the Achaeans come back from Troy with our ships” (14.505; see [[peneleos]] and [[nostos]]).
