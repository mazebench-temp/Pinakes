---
summary: All the senses of the verb meet in Book 14. Diomedes “claims” his descent from Tydeus; Poseidon says Hector “boasts” that he will take the ships; Polydamas and Acamas boast over the men they have killed and Peneleos speaks “in triumph”; and Acamas says why a man “prays” to leave a kinsman behind him.
passages:
  - 14.453-505 | Killings and boasts
---

**A claim.** Diomedes asks the kings to hear him though he is the youngest: “but I too claim to be of the line of a brave father, / Tydeus” (γένος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 14.113–114; see [[genee]] and [[diomedes]]). The claim of birth is the εὔχομαι of the genealogies (see the article above and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**Boasts.** Poseidon tells the Argives that Hector means to take the ships and win glory: “So he says and boasts, because Achilles / stays by the hollow ships, angered at heart” (φησὶ καὶ εὔχεται, 14.366–367; see [[hector]] and [[poseidon]]). In the fighting after Hector's fall the verb is the boast of the killer. Two verses introduce two of the boasts in the same words: “And Polydamas boasted terribly, shouting aloud”, “And Acamas boasted terribly over him, shouting aloud” (ἔκπαγλον ἐπεύξατο μακρὸν ἀΰσας, 14.453, 14.478), the verse that introduced the boasts of Deiphobus and Idomeneus in Book 13 (13.413, 13.445). Twice the effect follows in the same couplet: “grief came upon the Argives at his boasting” (εὐξαμένοιο, 14.458, 14.486; see [[achos]] and [[thymos]]). Peneleos lifts Ilioneus' head on his spear and “spoke in triumph” (εὐχόμενος ἔπος ηὔδα, 14.500; see [[peneleos]] and [[ilioneus]]). The boasts answer one another, and each but the last is answered with a death; after the last, the Trojans tremble and look for a way to escape (14.506–507; see [[poine]]).

**A prayer.** In the middle of the chain the verb turns to its other sense. Acamas ends his boast with a maxim: “and that is why a man prays / to leave a kinsman in his halls, to defend him against disaster” (τὼ καί κέ τις εὔχεται ἀνήρ, 14.484–485; see [[a-kinsman-in-his-halls]] and [[prayer]]). Acamas, who has just taken a life for his brother's, turns his boast into the prayer of any man who has kin.
