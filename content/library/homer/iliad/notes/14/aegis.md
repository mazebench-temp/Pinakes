---
summary: The aegis itself is not seen in Book 14. Zeus is “aegis-bearing” in the verse-end Διὸς νόον αἰγιόχοιο, the mind that Hera means to beguile and that Sleep once lulled; in the next book Zeus sends the aegis into battle with Apollo, and that mind rouses Hector.
---

“Then ox-eyed queen Hera pondered / how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (ὅππως ἐξαπάφοιτο Διὸς νόον αἰγιόχοιο, 14.159–160; see [[apate]] and [[noos]]), and Sleep, refusing her, recalls the first time: “I then lulled the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (ἤτοι ἐγὼ μὲν ἔλεξα Διὸς νόον αἰγιόχοιο, 14.252; see [[hypnos]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). The epithet that names the god's terror stands in the two verses where his mind is tricked or lulled, and the aegis itself has no part in the book.

When Zeus wakes, the aegis and the verse-end return together. He gives Apollo the tasseled aegis, to shake it and frighten the Achaean heroes (15.229–230; see [[apollo]]), and Hector, revived, sits up, since Διὸς νόος αἰγιόχοιο, the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus, had roused him (15.240–242). In the Odyssey the verse-end belongs to the rule that no other god can evade or frustrate the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus (*Odyssey* 5.103–104, 5.137–138).
