summary: Agamemnon proposes for the third time to flee from Troy, now by night, with the maxim that there is no blame in fleeing evil; Odysseus answers that the army would stop fighting the moment the ships were launched. Hector, struck down, draws back and is carried from the field, and the book ends with the Trojans in flight before the son of Oileus.

**Flight by night.** The wall is down, and Agamemnon proposes to drag the nearest ships into the sea and anchor them out, and when the immortal night comes, if the Trojans hold off from war, to launch the rest: “For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night. / Better the man who flees and escapes evil than the man who is caught” (14.74–81; see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]], [[agamemnon]] and [[war-and-night]]). He has proposed flight twice before, to test the army and in earnest (2.139–141, 9.26–28). This time the man who answers him is Odysseus, and his answer is the soldier's: if the ships are dragged down in the middle of the battle, the Achaeans “will not hold out in the war while the ships are dragged to the sea, / but will look away, and hang back from battle” (14.99–101; see [[odysseus]] and [[charme]]). Agamemnon gives way, and Diomedes proposes that the wounded kings go back to the battle and urge the others on (14.104–132; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**Giving ground.** In the battle the first man to draw back is Hector. When his spear has failed against Ajax, “he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom” (14.408; see [[hector]]). It is the verse in which Paris shrank back from Menelaus in Book 3, for which Hector rebuked him (3.32–57), and in which three Trojans drew back in Book 13 (13.566, 13.596, 13.648); Ajax's stone catches Hector as he goes. When the Argives see him carried away, they spring on the Trojans more fiercely (14.440–441).

**Rout.** After Peneleos' boast “trembling seized the limbs of them all, / and each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction” (14.506–507), and the book ends in pursuit: the son of Oileus killed the most, “for there was no one like him to pursue on foot / men who fled in fear, when Zeus rouses rout among them” (14.520–522; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[rout]]). The flight that Agamemnon proposed for the Achaeans falls on the Trojans.
