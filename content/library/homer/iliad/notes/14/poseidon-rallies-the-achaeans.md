---
summary: In Book 14 Poseidon's help turns from secret to open. He still comes in disguise, as an old man who heartens Agamemnon, but once Hera has put Zeus to sleep he leads the Achaeans himself with a sword like lightning, and the Trojans are routed.
passages:
  - 14.135-152 | Poseidon and Agamemnon
  - 14.354-401 | Poseidon leads the Achaeans
  - 14.508-522 | The Trojans routed
---

**Still in disguise.** The book takes up the story where Book 13 left it: Poseidon, who does not “keep a blind watch”, comes among the wounded kings “in the likeness of an old man” to hearten Agamemnon, then shouts like nine or ten thousand men and puts strength into every Achaean (14.135–152; see [[poseidon]] and [[agamemnon]]). The kings have just decided to go back to the battle, though wounded, and to urge the others on (14.128–134; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**Openly.** Hera, seeing Poseidon at work and Zeus watching from Ida, makes room for him: she has Zeus lulled to sleep on Gargarus (14.153–353; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Sleep tells Poseidon to help the Danaans “while he still sleeps” (14.357–360; see [[sleep]]). Poseidon then leaps out among the foremost, calls on the Argives, has the best men take the best armor, and leads them with a terrible sword “like lightning”, against Hector, who marshals the Trojans (14.361–391; see [[like-lightning]] and [[hector]]). In Book 13 he roused the army “secretly … in the likeness of a man” (13.357). Now he stands in the front, and the strife of war is stretched between the god and Hector (14.389–391).

**The rout.** Ajax strikes Hector down with a stone, the Trojans carry him from the field, and the Achaeans attack more fiercely (14.402–441). The poet calls on the Muses to tell who first won bloody spoils, “when the famous shaker of the earth had turned the battle” (14.508–510; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]), and the book ends with the Trojans in flight (14.511–522). The open help lasts only as long as Zeus' sleep (15.4–8).
