---
summary: Odysseus answers Agamemnon's plan of flight with the verse he used to him in Book 4, “Son of Atreus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!”; and Hera answers Zeus' invitation on Ida with her own verse of protest, “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!”, here in pretense.
---

**Odysseus to Agamemnon.** “Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said: / ‘Son of Atreus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!’” (14.82–83; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[odysseus]]). In the Iliad the verse is Odysseus' alone, and both times it is said to Agamemnon: in Book 4, when the king rebuked him for hanging back (4.350), and here, against the plan to launch the ships (see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]]). In the Odyssey it is most often a reproof spoken to a child or as to a child: by Zeus to Athena, by Athena to Telemachus, and by Eurycleia to Odysseus and to Penelope (*Odyssey* 1.64, 5.22, 3.230, 19.492, 23.70). Odysseus ends his rebuke in the same key: “But now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said” (οἷον ἔειπες, 14.95; see [[phrenes]]).

**Hera to Zeus.** “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” (αἰνότατε Κρονίδη ποῖον τὸν μῦθον ἔειπες, 14.330) is Hera's verse, and in the Iliad it is never anyone else's: she speaks it to Zeus six times, when he defends his secret counsels with Thetis (1.552), when he proposes peace between Trojans and Achaeans (4.25), when he mocks her and Athena for turning back from the battle in fear of him (8.447–462), when he thinks of saving Sarpedon (16.440), and when he taunts her with Achilles' return (18.361). Each time she protests what he has said. Here she protests his desire to lie with her on the open peak, and the protest is part of her plan (14.330–340; see [[hera]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).
