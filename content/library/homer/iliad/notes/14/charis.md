**Grace that shines.** Hera puts in her ears earrings “with three drops, clustered like mulberries; and much grace shone from them” (χάρις δʼ ἀπελάμπετο πολλή, 14.182–183; see [[earrings]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). χάρις here is the charm of a thing, a radiance that pleases the one who sees it. The half-verse returns with the earrings among the suitors' gifts to Penelope (*Odyssey* 18.297–298), and in the Odyssey Athena “poured” a wondrous grace over Telemachus as he went out (*Odyssey* 2.12, 17.63).

**Grace owed.** Hera's first offer to Sleep is a debt: “obey me; and I will be grateful to you all my days” (ἐγὼ δέ κέ τοι ἰδέω χάριν ἤματα πάντα, 14.235). χάριν εἰδέναι, “to know grace”, is to acknowledge a favor received and to owe it a return (see the article above and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). She adds a gift, a golden throne made by Hephaestus (14.238–241; see [[throne]] and [[doron]]), and when Sleep still refuses she offers one of the Χάριτες, the Graces themselves: “I will give you one of the younger Graces / to marry and to be called your wife” (14.267–268; see [[graces]], [[pasithea]] and [[marriage]]). This time he accepts, and the verb of his gladness belongs to the same family: “So she spoke, and Sleep rejoiced” (χήρατο, 14.270). The favor she asked for is paid with a Grace. A Grace is a god's wife again in Book 18, where the wife of Hephaestus is Charis (18.382–383).
