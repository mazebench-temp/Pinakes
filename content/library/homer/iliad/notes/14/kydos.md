grc: [κυδάνει]
passages:
  - 14.354-360 | Sleep's message
---

**Glory given to the Trojans.** Agamemnon, proposing flight, knows where glory comes from: Zeus once defended the Danaans, “and I know it now, when he makes those men like the blessed gods / in glory, and has bound our might and our hands” (τοὺς μὲν ὁμῶς μακάρεσσι θεοῖσι / κυδάνει, 14.72–73; see [[agamemnon]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Poseidon, rallying the Argives, gives Hector's purpose the same word: “are we once again to yield the victory to Hector, / son of Priam, so that he may take the ships and win glory?” (κῦδος ἄρηται, 14.364–365; see [[nike]] and [[hector]]). Glory is a gift of the gods, and the side that wins it is the side they favor (see the article above).

**Glory for a little while.** Sleep's message turns the gift around. “Now, Poseidon, help the Danaans with a ready heart, / and grant them glory, if only for a little while, while he still sleeps, / Zeus” (καί σφιν κῦδος ὄπαζε μίνυνθά περ, 14.357–359; see [[sleep]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). The narrator uses the same words when a god gives glory to the other side: Zeus at the wall (Τρωσὶν δὲ καὶ Ἕκτορι κῦδος ὄπαζε, 12.255), and Apollo in the same verse-end in the next books (15.327, 16.730). While Zeus sleeps, another god may give it to the Achaeans, and only for as long as the sleep lasts. In this book the epithet of the fighting is κυδιάνειρα, the battle “where men win glory” (14.155), the battle Poseidon is busy in when Hera sees him.
