---
summary: Hesiod's Theogony has the powers of Book 14 in its genealogy of the gods. Sleep and Death are the children of Night, and Deceit and Love are hers too; Eros overcomes the mind of all gods and men; Oceanus and Tethys are the parents of the rivers and of Styx, the great oath of the gods; the Titans lie beneath Tartarus; the youngest of the Graces is Hephaestus' wife; Pandora is adorned as Hera adorns herself; and the mind of Zeus, which Hera beguiles, is the one mind that Hesiod says no one can deceive.
---

- **The children of Night.** Hera meets “Sleep, the brother of Death” (14.231). In Hesiod, Night bore Doom, black Fate and Death, Sleep and the tribe of Dreams, lying with no one (*Theogony* 211–213), and she goes over the earth holding “Sleep in her arms, the brother of Death”, Ὕπνον μετὰ χερσί, κασίγνητον Θανάτοιο, Homer's κασιγνήτῳ Θανάτοιο (756). Sleep and Death have their dwelling there, by the house of Night, where the Sun never looks on them; Sleep goes quietly over the earth and the sea and is gentle to men, but Death has a heart of iron (758–766). Homer names no parents for Sleep. When Zeus hunted him, he fled to Night, “the tamer of gods and men”, and Zeus “shrank from doing what would displease swift Night” (14.259–261); in Hesiod she is his mother and older than Zeus, born of Chaos (123). Among her later children Hesiod names Deceit and Love, Ἀπάτη and Φιλότης (224). See [[sleep]], [[death]], [[night]], [[apate]] and [[philotes]].
- **Aphrodite and Eros.** Eros and fair Himeros attended Aphrodite from her birth, and her portion among men and gods is “maidens' whispers and smiles and deceits, sweet delight, love and gentleness” (παρθενίους τʼ ὀάρους μειδήματά τʼ ἐξαπάτας τε, *Theogony* 201–206). Homer's strap holds love, longing and intimate talk, “the persuasion that steals the mind even of men who think shrewdly” (14.216–217), and Hera smiles as she takes it (14.222–223; see [[aphrodites-band]], [[oaristys]] and [[himeros]]). Hesiod's Eros, born with the first powers, loosens the limbs and “overcomes the mind and the prudent counsel in the breast of all gods and all men” (πάντων δὲ θεῶν πάντων τʼ ἀνθρώπων δάμναται, 120–122). In Book 14 Hera asks Aphrodite for love and longing, “with which you overcome all the immortals and mortal men” (14.198–199), and calls Sleep “lord of all gods and of all men” (14.233); Zeus sleeps “overcome by sleep and love” (14.353; see [[eros]]).
- **The mind of Zeus.** At Mecone Prometheus divided the ox “seeking to deceive the mind of Zeus” (Διὸς νόον ἐξαπαφίσκων, *Theogony* 537) and spoke “with a crafty mind” (δολοφρονέων, 550); Zeus knew the trick and was not deceived (551), and the story ends, “so it is not possible to steal the mind of Zeus or to get past it” (ὣς οὐκ ἔστι Διὸς κλέψαι νόον οὐδὲ παρελθεῖν, 613). Hera ponders “how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (ὅππως ἐξαπάφοιτο Διὸς νόον, 14.160), speaks three times “with treachery in her heart” (δολοφρονέουσα, 14.197, 14.300, 14.329), and carries in her bosom the persuasion that steals the mind (ἔκλεψε νόον, 14.217). In Homer the theft succeeds. See [[apate]], [[noos]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]].
- **Oceanus and Tethys.** Hera calls Oceanus “the origin of the gods” (14.201), and Sleep “the origin of all” (14.246). Hesiod's first powers are Chaos, Earth, Tartarus and Eros (*Theogony* 116–122). Oceanus is the first named of the children of Earth and Heaven and Tethys the last of their daughters (133–136), and she bore him the rivers, the Scamander among them, and the daughters of Ocean, three thousand of them (337–370). Plato thought that Hesiod agreed with Homer (*Cratylus* 402b; see [[plato-oceanus-and-tethys]], [[oceanus]] and [[tethys]]).
- **The oath by the Styx.** Sleep makes Hera swear “by the inviolable water of Styx” (14.271). In Hesiod, Styx is the foremost of the daughters of Oceanus and the eldest (*Theogony* 361, 776–777). When Zeus called the gods to Olympus against the Titans she came first, with her children, and he made her the great oath of the gods (383–403); a god who swears falsely by her water lies breathless for a year, and for nine more is shut out from the councils and feasts of the gods (775–806). See [[styx]], [[oath]] and [[aristotle-oceanus-and-tethys]].
- **The Titans.** Sleep calls on “the gods below who are around Cronus” as witnesses, and Hera names “the gods / beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans” (14.274, 14.278–279). In Hesiod their father Heaven called them Titans in reproach (*Theogony* 207–210); after the war they were sent as far beneath the earth as heaven is above it, and are hidden in the murky gloom by the will of Zeus, behind bronze doors set by Poseidon (717–733), beyond dark Chaos (813–814). See [[titans]] and [[tartarus]].
- **The youngest Grace.** Hera promises Sleep “one of the younger Graces” (14.267, 14.275), and Sleep names Pasithea (14.276). Hesiod's Graces are three, Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia, daughters of Zeus and the Oceanid Eurynome (*Theogony* 907–909), and Hephaestus made Aglaea, “the youngest of the Graces” (ὁπλοτάτην Χαρίτων), his wife (945–946); Hesiod's Pasithea is a Nereid (246). In the Iliad Hephaestus' wife is Charis (18.382–383). Poseidon gives his daughter Cymopolea to Briareus “to marry” (ὀπυίειν, 817–819), the verb of Hera's promise (ὀπυιέμεναι, 14.268). See [[graces]] and [[pasithea]].
- **Pandora's adornment.** Athena girds and adorns Pandora (ζῶσε δὲ καὶ κόσμησε, *Theogony* 573), and on the golden crown that Hephaestus made for her “many intricate designs” were wrought, and “much grace shone from it” (δαίδαλα πολλά, 581; χάρις δʼ ἀπελάμπετο πολλή, 583). The half-verses are Homer's for Hera's robe, on which Athena “set many intricate designs” (14.179), and for her earrings: “and much grace shone from them” (14.183). Pandora, exulting in her adornment (κόσμῳ), is “a sheer snare” for men (δόλον αἰπύν, 587–589); in the *Works and Days* the Graces and Persuasion hang golden necklaces on her, and Hermes puts lies and wily words in her breast (*Works and Days* 72–80). Hera's adornment (κόσμον, 14.187) serves her deceit (see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]).
- **The wives of Zeus.** Hesiod tells Zeus' unions with goddesses in order, Metis, Themis, Eurynome, Demeter, Mnemosyne and Leto, and last Hera, whom he made his wife (*Theogony* 886–923); then Maia, and the mortal women Semele, who bore Dionysus, and Alcmene, who bore Heracles (938–944). Zeus' list in Book 14 has Demeter, Leto and Hera among the goddesses, and among the mortals Semele and Alcmene with Ixion's wife, Danae and the daughter of Phoenix (14.317–327; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The *Shield of Heracles*, handed down under Hesiod's name, opens with Zeus' night with Alcmene in Thebes (*Shield* 1–56; see [[alcmene]]), and the *Catalogue of Women* told of Europa, the daughter of Phoenix, who bore Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys (fr. 19 Evelyn-White; see [[europa]]).
- **Hera's childhood.** Hera says that Oceanus and Tethys took her from Rhea and reared her in their halls when Zeus put Cronus beneath the earth and the barren sea (14.202–204). In the *Theogony* Cronus swallowed his children as each was born, Hera among them, until Earth's counsel tricked him into bringing them up again (453–467, 494–497); of Hera's nursing by Oceanus and Tethys Hesiod says nothing. See [[hera]], [[rhea]] and [[cronus]].
