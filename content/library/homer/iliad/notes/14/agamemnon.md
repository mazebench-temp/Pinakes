summary: Wounded, Agamemnon comes up from the ships with Diomedes and Odysseus and despairs. Hector's threat to burn the ships is being fulfilled, the army is angry with him as Achilles is, and Zeus wills the Achaeans to perish, so for the third time he proposes flight, launching the ships by night. Odysseus rebukes him, he yields, and he leads the wounded kings back to the battle, where Poseidon takes him by the hand (14.27–152, 14.379–382).

**Hector's threat.** Nestor, pondering whether to join the fight or go “to the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people”, chooses the king (14.20–24; see [[nestor]] and [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). Agamemnon, Diomedes and Odysseus, all wounded earlier that day (11.248–283, 11.369–400, 11.434–488), have come up from beside the ships to look at the battle, and they meet him (14.27–40; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Agamemnon speaks first. He fears that “mighty Hector may make good his word to me”: he would not go back to Ilios “before he had set fire to the ships and killed the men themselves” (14.44–47; see [[hector]] and [[ships]]). Hector said so to the Trojans at nightfall in Book 8, when he had hoped “that I would destroy the ships and all the Achaeans / and go back home to windy Ilios” (8.498–499), and Odysseus told Achilles of his vow to burn the ships and slaughter the Achaeans beside them (9.241–243). “So he spoke then; and now it is all being fulfilled” (14.48) is the verse with which Odysseus, in Book 2, recalled Calchas' prophecy of victory in the tenth year (2.330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). The words that once recalled a promise of victory now recall the enemy's threat.

**Like Achilles.** “Ah, truly the other well-greaved Achaeans too / are laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is, / and are not willing to fight at the sterns of the ships” (14.49–51; see [[achilles]], [[cholos]] and [[stern]]). It is what Poseidon, in Calchas' likeness, told the army in Book 13: the Trojans are at the ships “through the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army, / who in their strife with him are not willing to defend / the swift-sailing ships” (13.108–110; see the note on Book 13 and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]).

**Flight by night.** Nestor answers that the wall is down and that the wounded cannot fight (14.53–63). Agamemnon draws the conclusion:

{{quote:6cd37c20-8992-551f-bf03-42c1b9c2817c}}

It is the third time he has proposed flight. He tested the army with it (2.139–141), and he proposed it in earnest at the night assembly of Book 9 (9.26–28), each time with the same verse, “Come then, let us all do as I say” (14.74 = 2.139, 9.26; see [[the-test-of-the-army]] and [[retreat]]). The reasons are the same too: “such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might” (14.69 = 2.116, 9.23; see [[supreme-in-might]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]), and the Achaeans are to perish “here, nameless, far from Argos” (14.70), the verse in which Polydamas wished it at the ditch and Idomeneus feared it (12.70, 13.227; see [[argos]]). But the plan is smaller. Before, he spoke of sailing home; now he would haul down the ships nearest the sea and anchor them out on their mooring-stones, and drag down the rest in the night, if the Trojans hold off (14.75–79; see [[mooring-stones]], [[war-and-night]] and the translation's note on 14.31–36). He ends with a maxim: there is no blame in fleeing from evil, even by night (14.80–81; see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]] and [[nemesis]]).

**He yields.** Odysseus calls the word one that no man who knew how to speak fitting words and bore a scepter would let pass his lips (14.82–102; see [[odysseus]]). In Book 4 the parts were reversed. Agamemnon rebuked Odysseus for hanging back, Odysseus answered in the same two verses, “Looking at him darkly…” and “Son of Atreus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (4.349–350 = 14.82–83; see [[what-a-word]]), and the king, “when he saw that he was angry”, took back his word (4.356–363). Now he takes back his plan:

{{quote:228b46c7-b635-5f38-8710-5c8f082661a0}}

“Young or old”: the youngest answers. Diomedes, who answered his proposal of flight in Book 9 (9.31–49), now gives the counsel the kings follow (14.110–132; see [[diomedes]]).

**He leads.** “They went, and Agamemnon, lord of men, led them” (14.134; see [[lord-of-men]]). Poseidon, in the likeness of an old man, goes with them and takes Agamemnon by the right hand, and answers his fears one by one (14.135–146; see [[poseidon]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). Achilles' “deadly heart” rejoices at the slaughter of the Achaeans, “since there is no sense in him, not even a little”; but “The blessed gods do not yet bear you a grudge altogether”, and Agamemnon “will see them / fleeing back to the city, away from the ships and huts” (14.139–146). Then the god shouts like nine or ten thousand men and puts strength into every Achaean (14.147–152; see [[ten-thousand-men]]).

**The armor.** When Poseidon has told the army to give the best shields and the longest spears to the best men, the wounded kings carry out the order: “And the kings themselves, stabbed though they were, marshaled them, / the son of Tydeus, and Odysseus, and the son of Atreus, Agamemnon” (14.379–380). The second verse repeats 14.29, which named the three when they came up from the ships. They go among the men, and “the brave man put on the good, and gave the worse to the worse man” (14.381–382; see [[exchange-of-armor]], [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[kings]]). The king who wanted to launch the ships now arms the army to fight at them.

The “son of Atreus” who kills Hyperenor in the rout at the end of the book is Menelaus (14.516; see [[menelaus]]).
