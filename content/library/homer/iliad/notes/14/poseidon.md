**The old man.** “Nor did the famous shaker of the earth keep a blind watch” (14.135; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]]): the verse that brought him to the battle in Book 13 (13.10) now brings him among the wounded kings as they go back to the fight. ἀλαοσκοπιή, “a blind watch”, is said in the Iliad only of Poseidon and of Apollo (10.515), and in the Odyssey of Ares watching for Hephaestus (*Odyssey* 8.285). He comes “in the likeness of an old man”, takes Agamemnon by the right hand and tells him that the deadly heart of Achilles rejoices at the Achaeans' slaughter, “since there is no sense in him, not even a little. / But let him perish so, and may a god maim him” (14.136–142; see [[divine-epiphany]], [[agamemnon]] and [[achilles]]). σιφλώσειε, “maim”, is found only here in Homer. The gods, he says, are not yet wholly angry with Agamemnon, and he will see the Trojans fleeing to the city (14.143–146).

{{quote:14232119-42df-5ae2-ae6b-6a010d2876f9}}

The shout is the bellow of Ares when Diomedes wounded him, in the same two verses (14.148–149 = 5.860–861; see [[ten-thousand-men]] and [[ares]]). The disguise is thin: no old man shouts like that.

**Seen by Hera.** From Olympus Hera sees him “busying himself in the battle where men win glory, / her own brother and her brother-in-law”, and rejoices (14.155–156; see [[hera]] and [[in-laws]]). His work is what she sets out to protect.

**Openly.** While Zeus sleeps, Sleep runs to the ships and speaks to him by name: “Now, Poseidon, help the Danaans with a ready heart, / and grant them glory, if only for a little while, while he still sleeps, / Zeus” (14.357–359; see [[sleep]]). He leaps out among the foremost and speaks to the Argives himself. Hector boasts because Achilles stays by the ships, “but there will be no great longing for him, if we others / urge ourselves on to defend one another”; let the best men take the biggest shields and the longest spears, “and I will lead” (14.364–377; see [[hector]] and [[longing-for-achilles]]). The wounded kings carry out his order and exchange the armor (14.378–383; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

{{quote:827bae67-54e0-573d-bcca-b3bd892be769}}

In Book 13 he struck the two Ajaxes with his staff and roused the army “secretly … in the likeness of a man” (13.59, 13.357); now he leads with a sword, and its flash is Zeus' own weapon (see [[like-lightning]] and [[sword]]). θέμις, “it is not lawful”, puts the god's weapon beyond what a mortal may face (see [[themis]]).

**The strife stretched tight.** “Then they stretched tight the most dread strife of war, / dark-haired Poseidon and shining Hector, / the one helping the Trojans, the other the Argives” (14.389–391; see [[dark-haired]]). In Book 13 it was the two sons of Cronus who stretched the rope of war over the armies (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]); now a god and a man pull against each other. The sea surges up to the huts and ships, and the armies meet with a shout louder than sea, fire or wind (14.392–401; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]).

**The battle turned.** Ajax strikes Hector down, and the poet calls on the Muses to tell who first won the bloody spoils, “when the famous shaker of the earth had turned the battle” (ἔκλινε μάχην, 14.510; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). In Book 15 Zeus wakes and sees “Poseidon the lord” among the Argives, and sends Iris to order him out of the war (15.8, 15.158–219; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).
