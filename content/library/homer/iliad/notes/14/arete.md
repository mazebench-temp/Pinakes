summary: Diomedes, the youngest of the kings, claims a hearing by his descent: his grandfather Oeneus “in prowess stood out” among his brothers, and his father Tydeus surpassed all the Achaeans with the spear.
---

“For to Portheus were born three noble sons, / and they lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon: / Agrius and Melas, and the third was the horseman Oeneus, / my father's father; and in prowess he stood out among them” (ἀρετῇ δʼ ἦν ἔξοχος αὐτῶν, 14.115–118; see [[oeneus]], [[portheus]] and [[genee]]). Diomedes says it so that the older kings will not hold his youth against him (14.110–112), nor “say that by birth I am a coward and a weakling / and dishonor the word I speak, if I speak it well” (14.126–127; see [[mythos]]). The prowess of the grandfather and the spear of the father, who “surpassed all the Achaeans / with the spear” (14.124–125; see [[tydeus]]), are offered as the warrant for a young man's counsel.

The word entered the poem in Hector's boast on the evening before, that Diomedes would learn his own prowess the next day (8.535; see the article above). On that day Diomedes, wounded, speaks of his grandfather's (see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[diomedes]]).
