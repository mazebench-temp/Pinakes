**The straps.** When the armies meet, Hector throws first, and at Ajax: the spear strikes “where the two straps were stretched across his chest, / the one of his shield, the other of his silver-studded sword; / and these two protected his tender flesh” (14.404–406; see [[hector]], [[shield]] and [[sword]]). The great shield hangs from its strap, and the sword from the other (see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). The sword is “silver-studded”, as was the sword Hector gave him when their duel was broken off, “with its sheath and its well-cut strap” (7.303–304; see [[silver-studded]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]); the poem does not say that this is the same sword. In that duel, too, Hector's spear stopped short of his body, in the seventh hide of the shield (7.245–248). Hector, enraged that the missile has flown in vain, draws back into the crowd “avoiding doom” (14.406–408).

**The stone.** “Then, as he went away, great Telamonian Ajax / with a stone—for many of them, the props of the swift ships, / rolled about at the feet of the fighters—he lifted one of them / and hit him in the chest over the shield rim, near the neck” (14.409–412; see [[props]], [[stone-throwing]] and [[telamonian]]). Hector spins like a top and falls as an oak falls under the thunderbolt of Zeus: “so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust” (14.413–418; see [[like-a-top]], [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]] and [[thunderbolt]]). So the duel of Book 7 ended, when Ajax, with “a far greater stone”, broke Hector's shield and “he was stretched out on his back”, until Apollo set him on his feet (7.268–272). This time no god lifts him at once. His companions carry him to his horses, which bear him “toward the city, groaning heavily” (14.428–432), as Ajax foretold when he challenged him at the end of Book 13: the time was near when Hector would pray, in flight, for horses swifter than hawks to carry him to the city (13.817–820). Zeus, waking, sees him lying on the plain vomiting blood, since it was no weakling of the Achaeans who had hit him (15.9–11), and Apollo revives him (15.239–262).

**Archelochus.** Polydamas kills the Boeotian Prothoenor and boasts, and “most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Ajax, / the son of Telamon; for the man fell very close to him” (14.459–460; see [[polydamas]], [[prothoenor]] and [[wise-hearted]]). The verses are those of Antilochus' grief when Deiphobus boasted over Hypsenor (13.417–418). Ajax throws at Polydamas as he goes away; Polydamas darts aside, and the spear takes Archelochus, son of Antenor, “for the gods had planned his destruction”, cutting through the tendons where head and neck join, so that his head and face strike the ground before his knees (14.461–468; see [[archelochus]] and [[antenor]]).

**The taunt.**

{{quote:bd30ad23-f005-5b25-9a7a-7049ec49c67d}}

ἦ ῥʼ εὖ γιγνώσκων, “He spoke, knowing well” (14.475): the narrator lets the listener know that Ajax knew whose son the man was, and that the guess is mockery. The taunt values a dead man by his birth, as Diomedes valued himself by his father in the council (14.113–127; see [[diomedes]]), and it prices him: Archelochus is killed ἀντί, “in return for”, Prothoenor. Acamas, Archelochus' brother, at once exacts the penalty for him (14.476–485; see [[acamas]], [[promachus]] and [[poine]]).

**The first in the rout.** When the poet asks the Muses “who was the first of the Achaeans to win the bloody spoils of men”, the answer is Ajax: “Telamonian Ajax was the first; he stabbed Hyrtius, / son of Gyrtius, leader of the stout-hearted Mysians” (14.508–512; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]], [[bloody-spoils]], [[hyrtius]] and [[mysians]]). The other Ajax closes the list, killing the most of all (14.520–522; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
