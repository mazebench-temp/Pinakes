---
summary: Hera ponders how she “might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus,” and Sleep recalls the day when he “lulled the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” before.
---

Both uses in the book are the same verse-end, Διὸς νόον αἰγιόχοιο, and both name the mind that is to be tricked. Hera, seeing Zeus on Ida, ponders “how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (14.159–160; [[hera]], [[apate]], [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Sleep, refusing her, recalls that he did it once: “I then lulled the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus, / pouring myself sweetly around him,” while Hera drove Heracles from Troy to Cos (14.252–255; [[sleep]], [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). The epithet of the god's power stands beside the mind that a goddess and a god can deceive. The Odyssey uses the same words to state the rule that this book breaks: no other god can get past or frustrate “the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (*Odyssey* 5.103–104, 5.137–138). See [[zeus]].
