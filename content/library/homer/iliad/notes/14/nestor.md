---
summary: Nestor opens the book. Still drinking with the wounded Machaon, he hears the cry grow louder at the ships, takes up his son's shield and goes out to look; he sees the wall thrown down, ponders, and goes to Agamemnon. To the wounded kings he says that what has happened cannot be undone, not even by Zeus, and that a wounded man cannot fight; then he falls silent (14.1–63).

**Over his wine.** The book begins where Book 11 left him, in his hut with Machaon. The narrative went away from the hut with Patroclus, who ran off with Nestor's plan (11.804–805), after the two men had drunk Hecamede's mixed drink “and were taking pleasure in talk” (11.641–643; see [[hecamede]] and [[kykeon]]). Three books later the drinking is still going on:

{{quote:6cae974b-942c-50bf-8b62-8c044305dc6e}}

The din of the battle at the ships has reached the hut (see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). φράζεο … ὅπως ἔσται τάδε ἔργα, “Consider … how these things are to be” (14.3): Nestor will say the half-verse again to the kings, “So let us consider how these things are to be” (14.61), in the verse with which Zeus opened the debate of the gods in Book 4 (4.14). Machaon is to go on drinking “the sparkling wine” until Hecamede has heated his bath (14.5–7; see [[machaon]], [[wine]] and [[bathing]]), and Nestor will go “to a lookout” (ἐς περιωπήν, 14.8). περιωπή, a place that looks out all around, occurs twice more in Homer: Idomeneus sits on one to watch the chariot race (23.451), and Odysseus climbs to one on Circe's island (Od. 10.146; see [[watching-the-war]]).

**His son's shield.** He takes “the wrought shield of his son / that lay in the hut of Thrasymedes, tamer of horses, / shining with bronze; and the son had his father's shield” (14.9–11; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[shield]]). Nestor's own shield was famous. In Book 8 Hector urged his horses on to take “Nestor's shield, whose fame now reaches the sky, / that it is all of gold, its rods and the shield itself” (8.192–193). Why father and son have exchanged shields the poem does not say. The next verse, “And he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze” (14.12), is the one with which he armed in the night of Book 10, when Agamemnon came to wake him (10.135; see [[strong-spear]] and [[arming-scene]]). Both times the old man arms to go out and see for himself.

**The wall thrown down.** Outside the hut “at once he saw a shameful thing”: the Achaeans in turmoil, the Trojans driving them from behind, “and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down” (14.13–15; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The wall had been his counsel. In Book 7 he proposed to build, close against the common tomb of the dead, “high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (7.338), and he names it to Agamemnon in the same words: “For the wall has been thrown down, which we trusted / would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (14.55–56).

**Two ways.** The old man's indecision is the great open sea that heaves with a soundless swell and rolls to neither side “until some settled wind comes down from Zeus” (14.16–19; see [[the-heaving-sea]]): “so the old man pondered, torn in his heart / two ways”, whether to join the throng of the Danaans or go to Agamemnon (14.20–22). “And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way: / to go to the son of Atreus” (14.23–24), in the verse with which Deiphobus chose to fetch Aeneas (13.458; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[decision-scene]]). The counselor goes to the council, not into the fight.

**The wounded kings.** Diomedes, Odysseus and Agamemnon, wounded earlier that day, come up from the ships, “leaning on their spears”, to look at the battle (14.27–38; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[kings]]). “And the old man met them, / Nestor, and made the heart cower in the breasts of the Achaeans” (14.39–40). πτῆξε, “made cower”, is the simple verb, found only here in Homer (compare καταπτήξας, 22.191). The poem does not say why the sight of him frightens them; Agamemnon's first question is why he has left “man-destroying war” and come there, and he greets him with the verse of Book 10 and Book 11, “Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans” (14.42–43 = 10.87, 11.511; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]], [[son-of-neleus]] and [[agamemnon]]). He answers as “the Gerenian horseman” (14.52; see [[gerenian-horseman]]):

{{quote:9168288e-8e98-510f-ac59-7cf089bf6dba}}

ἑτοῖμα τετεύχαται, “these things are here and done”: not even Zeus “who thunders on high” could make them otherwise (14.53–54; see [[thunders-on-high]]). Nestor puts the defeat in Zeus' hands, and Agamemnon says so outright in his reply: Zeus now gives the Trojans glory and “has bound our might and our hands” (14.73; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). He hopes that “the mind can do any good” (εἴ τι νόος ῥέξει, 14.62; see [[noos]]), but he offers no plan. He only forbids the wounded to fight, “for a man who has been hit cannot fight” (14.63). The proposals come from the others: Agamemnon's, to launch the ships, which Odysseus rejects, and that of Diomedes, the youngest of them, who finds a way round Nestor's objection: they will go to the battle, keep out of range and urge the others on (14.64–132; see [[diomedes]] and [[odysseus]]). Nestor does not speak again in the book. In Book 9 he had praised Diomedes for speaking wisely, though young enough to be his son, and then claimed the last word as the elder (9.53–62; see [[old-age-and-memory]]); here the young man has it.

**His sons.** Thrasymedes has his father's shield (14.11), and in the rout at the end of the book Antilochus “slew and stripped Phalces and Mermerus” (14.513; see [[antilochus]]).
