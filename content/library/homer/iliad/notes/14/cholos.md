summary: Achilles' anger still governs the war, and Agamemnon fears that the whole army shares it. Hera's false story is of an old couple parted by anger, and her pretext for coming to Zeus is fear of his; Sleep remembers how Zeus raged for Heracles; and Hector is enraged that his spear has failed.
grc: [χολώσεαι]
---

**Achilles' anger.** The wrath of Book 1 lies behind the book's first council. Agamemnon fears that “the other well-greaved Achaeans too / are laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is” (ἐν θυμῷ βάλλονται ἐμοὶ χόλον ὥς περ Ἀχιλλεύς, 14.49–50; see [[agamemnon]] and [[achilles]]), and Poseidon tells the Argives that Hector boasts of taking the ships “because Achilles / stays by the hollow ships, angered at heart” (κεχολωμένος ἦτορ, 14.366–367; see [[menis]]).

**Anger among the gods.** Hera's false story to Aphrodite and to Zeus is a tale of anger: Oceanus and Tethys have long kept away from each other's bed, “since anger has fallen upon their hearts” (ἐπεὶ χόλος ἔμπεσε θυμῷ, 14.207 = 14.306; see [[philotes]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). To Zeus she adds a pretext of her own: she has come down from Olympus “for fear you might be angry with me afterward, if in silence / I went off to the house of deep-streaming Oceanus” (μή πώς μοι μετέπειτα χολώσεαι, 14.310–311; see [[silence]]). The anger she claims to fear is real enough in the past. Sleep remembers Zeus waking to find Heracles driven to Cos, flinging the gods about his house, and stopping his pursuit only for Night, “angry though he was” (χωόμενός περ, 14.260), and Hera answers Sleep's fear by asking whether Zeus will help the Trojans as much “as he was enraged for Heracles, his own son” (περιχώσατο, 14.266; see [[heracles]] and [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). περιχώομαι, “be angry on account of”, is used elsewhere in the Iliad only of Phoenix's father, enraged over his concubine (9.449).

**Hector's anger.** In the battle the word is Hector's, when his spear is stopped by the straps on Ajax's chest: “And Hector was enraged / that his swift missile had flown from his hand in vain” (χώσατο, 14.406–407; see [[hector]] and [[ajax]]). He draws back, and Ajax's stone finds him.
