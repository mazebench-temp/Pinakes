---
summary: Agamemnon fears that Hector will make good his threat; Zeus tells Hera not to fear that any god or man will see them; and before Poseidon's sword “fear holds men back”.
---

“I fear that mighty Hector may make good his word to me” (δείδω, 14.44; see [[assembly]] and [[agamemnon]]): Agamemnon's fear opens the way to his plan of flight (14.65–81; see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]]). On Ida the word serves the deception. Hera pretends to dread being seen by one of the gods (14.330–336), and Zeus answers, “Hera, do not fear that any god or any man / will see it” (μήτε θεῶν τό γε δείδιθι μήτέ τινʼ ἀνδρῶν, 14.342–343; see [[the-golden-cloud]]), in the turn of phrase with which Athena told Diomedes not to fear Ares or any other of the immortals (5.827–828).

Poseidon leads the Achaeans with a sword “like lightning; it is not lawful to close with it / in wretched battle, but fear holds men back” (ἀλλὰ δέος ἰσχάνει ἄνδρας, 14.386–387; see [[like-lightning]] and [[themis]]). At the end of the book other words take over: trembling seizes the Trojans at Peneleos' boast (τρόμος, 14.506), and the son of Oileus is the best at pursuing men “when Zeus rouses rout among them” (φόβος, 14.522; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
