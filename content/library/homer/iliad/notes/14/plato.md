---
summary: Plato uses Book 14 in five dialogues. The Republic will not let the young hear of Zeus overcome by desire for Hera; the Theaetetus and the Cratylus take “Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys” for the doctrine that all things flow; the Cratylus asks why the bird is better called chalcis, as the gods call it, than cymindis, and derives Hera's name from love; the Timaeus makes Oceanus and Tethys the grandparents of Zeus and Hera; and the Laws quote Odysseus' rebuke of Agamemnon's plan to drag the ships to the sea.
---

- Zeus forgetting his plans for desire of Hera, refusing the chamber and lying with her on the ground, more possessed than when they first met “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (*Republic* 3.390b–c, on 14.292–353): [[plato-zeus-deceived]].
- Oceanus and Tethys, “the origin of the gods” and the streams from which all things flow (*Theaetetus* 152d–153a, 160d, 179d–180d; *Cratylus* 402a–d; *Timaeus* 40d–41a, on 14.201 and 14.246): [[plato-oceanus-and-tethys]] and [[plato-golden-rope]].
- The bird that the gods call chalcis and men cymindis, among the names that the gods give correctly (*Cratylus* 391d–392b, on 14.290–291): [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]], [[plato-astyanax]] and [[language-of-gods-and-men]].
- Odysseus against dragging the ships to the sea, as a lesson that fighting from ships makes cowards (*Laws* 4.706d–707a, quoting 14.96–102): [[plato-dragging-the-ships]].
- **Hera's name.** Explaining the names of the gods, Socrates derives Ἥρα from ἐρατή, “lovely”, “as indeed Zeus is said to have her because he loved her”, or else from ἀήρ, “air”, which the namegiver, speaking of things on high, disguised by putting the beginning of the word at its end; say “Hera” over and over, he adds, and you will hear it (*Cratylus* 404b–c). Homer's Zeus says it himself in Book 14: “as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.328; see [[hera]] and [[eros]]). Proclus cites the first etymology as Plato's own witness for his reading of the scene (*On the Republic* I 140 Kroll), and the allegorists whom Plutarch rejects made Hera's adornment a cleansing of the air (see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[plato-zeus-deceived]]).
