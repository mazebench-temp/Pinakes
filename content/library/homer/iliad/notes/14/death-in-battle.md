summary: The deaths of Book 14 come at its end, when Poseidon has turned the battle: Satnius, son of a Naiad; Prothoenor, Archelochus, Promachus and Ilioneus, killed one for another with boasts between; a list of seven more; and Hyperenor, whose soul goes out through his wound. Hector, struck by Ajax's stone, is told in the words of death and lives.

**A near death.** The first man to fall in the book does not die. Ajax's stone strikes Hector, and the narrator gives him the signs of a death: he falls like an oak struck by Zeus, his armor rings around him, and at the river he vomits dark blood and sinks back, “and his two eyes / black night covered” (14.409–439; see [[hector]], [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]]). Zeus will find him in the next book gasping for breath among his companions (15.9–11), and Apollo will raise him.

**Lives in a line.** The deaths that follow are given the details of a life. Satnius, the first killed, is “the son of Enops, whom a noble Naiad nymph bore / to Enops as he tended his cattle by the banks of the Satnioeis” (14.444–445; see [[satnius]] and [[nymphs]]); Ilioneus was the only child of Phorbas, “rich in flocks, whom most / of the Trojans Hermes loved” (14.489–492; see [[ilioneus]]). The list of the rout names Hyrtius, leader of the Mysians, and six others in five verses (14.511–515; see [[androktasia]]), and then Hyperenor, “shepherd of the people” (14.516).

**How they die.** The wounds are told exactly. Archelochus is struck “where the head and neck join, / on the last vertebra”, and his head and mouth and nostrils strike the ground before his shins and knees (14.465–468; see [[archelochus]]). Peneleos' spear drives out Ilioneus' eyeball and goes through the back of the head; Peneleos cuts off the head and holds it up on the spear (14.493–500; see [[eyes]] and [[the-poppy]]). Menelaus' bronze lets out Hyperenor's bowels, and his soul goes out through the wound (14.516–519; see [[psyche]]). The dead are made into speech: Polydamas jokes that Prothoenor will lean on the spear as a staff on his way to Hades (14.454–457), and Acamas calls the dead Promachus a sleeper (14.482–483; see [[hypnos]] and [[poine]]).
