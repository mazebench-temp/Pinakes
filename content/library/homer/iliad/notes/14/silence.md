---
summary: Odysseus tells Agamemnon to be silent, so that no other Achaean may hear his plan of flight; and Hera tells Zeus that she has come to him so that he will not be angry if she goes off to Oceanus in silence.
grc: [σίγα]
en: [Be silent]
---

**“Be silent.”** Odysseus breaks into his rebuke of Agamemnon's plan to launch the ships with a command: “Be silent, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears this / word, which no man would ever let pass through his mouth at all” (σίγα, μή τίς τʼ ἄλλος Ἀχαιῶν τοῦτον ἀκούσῃ, 14.90–91; see [[odysseus]] and [[no-blame-in-fleeing]]). The danger is in the hearing: an army that heard its king speak of flight would not fight (14.99–101). The imperative σίγα is Odysseus' word in the Odyssey too. In the tale he tells Eumaeus, the Odysseus of the story hushes a companion with nearly the same verse, “Be silent now, lest some other of the Achaeans hear you” (*Odyssey* 14.493), and he silences Eurycleia when she knows him by his scar, “lest anyone else in the halls learn of it” (*Odyssey* 19.486).

**In silence.** Hera's story to Zeus ends with a courtesy: she has come to Ida “for fear you might be angry with me afterward, if in silence / I went off to the house of deep-streaming Oceanus” (σιωπῇ, 14.310–311; see [[cholos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The wife who is deceiving her husband presents herself as one who would not leave him without a word.
