**Nestor.** From outside his hut Nestor sees the Achaeans in turmoil, the Trojans behind them, and the wall thrown down (14.13–15). The narrator gives his state of mind first in a simile, the great open sea heaving with a soundless swell and rolling neither way “until some settled wind comes down from Zeus” (14.16–19; see [[the-heaving-sea]]), and then in the formula: “so the old man pondered, torn in his heart / two ways: whether to go into the throng of the Danaans of the swift horses, / or to the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people” (ὅρμαινε δαϊζόμενος κατὰ θυμὸν / διχθάδιʼ, 14.20–22; see [[nestor]] and [[thymos]]). δαΐζω, “cleave, tear”, is the verb of men cut down by the bronze, and of the heart of the Achaeans “torn” by fear after two other similes of the sea, the winds that stir it up in Book 9 and the wave that falls on a ship in Book 15 (9.4–8, 15.624–629). Here too the sea comes first, and the tearing is in one old man's heart. The two courses are set out with ἢ … ἦε, and the resolution follows (14.23–24; see [[decision-scene]]).

**Hera.** “Then ox-eyed queen Hera pondered / how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (μερμήριξε δʼ ἔπειτα, 14.159–160; see [[hera]] and [[apate]]). Her pondering has no alternatives. It is a search for a means, and the best plan comes to her at once (14.161–165; see [[boule]]).
