**The son's account.** The Iliad has told of Tydeus before, but always to shame his son: Agamemnon's story of the embassy to Thebes and the fifty men in ambush (4.370–400), and Athena's, who calls him “small in build, but he was a fighter” (5.800–813; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Here Diomedes tells the story himself, and tells another part of it: not the deeds at Thebes but the settling, the marriage and the wealth that make his birth good (14.113–127; see [[diomedes]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

**The wanderer.** Oeneus “stayed there”, in Aetolia, but Tydeus settled in Argos πλαγχθείς, “after his wanderings” (14.119–120; see [[oeneus]] and [[argos]]). Homer gives no reason for the wandering beyond the will of Zeus and the other gods; πλάγχθη is the word of the Odyssey's opening for Odysseus' wanderings (Od. 1.2). The stranger's life follows a pattern the poem knows. Bellerophon, sent from Argos to Lycia, married the king's daughter and was given a domain “of orchard and plowland” (6.191–195; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]); Tydeus married one of the daughters of Adrastus and had “fields enough / bearing wheat, and many rows of orchard trees all around, / and he had many flocks” (14.121–124; see [[adrastus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). His son married a daughter of Adrastus too, Aegialeia (5.412–415; see [[aegialeia]]).

**The spearman.** κέκαστο δὲ πάντας Ἀχαιοὺς / ἐγχείῃ, “and he surpassed all the Achaeans / with the spear” (14.124–125). The Achaeans of his day are the army of the Seven, as Agamemnon called them when “the Achaeans sent Tydeus ahead on an embassy” (4.384; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). The Catalogue praised the son of Oileus with the same verb: “with the spear he surpassed” all the Panhellenes and Achaeans (2.530; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**The grave.** “Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes” (14.114; see [[thebes]] and [[tomb]]). χυτὴ … γαῖα, the earth heaped into a mound (see [[heaped-earth]]), is said once more in the Iliad, by Hector, who would rather be dead “and the heaped earth cover me” than hear Andromache dragged away (6.464). The Iliad never tells how Tydeus died. Sthenelus says only that the fathers “perished through their own recklessness” at Thebes (4.409), and in Book 6 Diomedes did not remember his father, who left him a child “when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223). Now the son knows where he lies.
