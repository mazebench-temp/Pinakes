summary: Book 14 is Hera's. Seeing Poseidon among the Achaeans and Zeus on Ida, she sets out to beguile her husband. She adorns herself, wins Aphrodite's strap with a false story, buys Sleep's help with the promise of a Grace and an oath by the Styx, and lies with Zeus in a golden cloud on Gargarus, so that Poseidon can lead the Achaeans while he sleeps.

**The two brothers.** The book turns from the battle to her:

{{quote:b388d70f-3590-5847-8f6b-3a11e886ffdf}}

Poseidon is αὐτοκασίγνητον καὶ δαέρα, “her own brother and her brother-in-law” (14.156; see [[in-laws]] and [[poseidon]]): the sons of Cronus are her brothers, and one of them is her husband. In Book 8 she begged Poseidon to resist Zeus and he refused (8.198–211). Now he is at work without her asking, and only Zeus, watching from Ida, stands in the way (see [[ida]] and [[zeus]]).

**The plan.** “Then ox-eyed queen Hera pondered / how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (14.159–160; see [[apate]] and [[ox-eyed]]). She will go to Ida adorned, “to see if he might long to lie in love beside / her body, and she might pour sleep, harmless and warm, / upon his eyelids and his shrewd mind” (14.163–165; see [[philotes]] and [[hypnos]]). In Book 8 she set out against his ban by chariot, with Athena, and was turned back by his threat (8.350–437). This time she goes alone, and the weapon is herself.

**The adornment.** She goes to her chamber, “which her dear son built for her, / Hephaestus”, with a secret bolt that no other god could open (14.166–169; see [[hephaestus]] and [[houses-of-the-gods]]). She cleanses her body with ambrosia, anoints it with oil whose fragrance reaches earth and heaven when it is stirred in the house of Zeus, braids her hair, puts on the robe that Athena worked for her and fastens it with golden pins, girds herself with a girdle of a hundred tassels, puts earrings in her ears, covers herself with a veil “white like the sun”, and binds on her sandals (14.170–186; see [[ambrosia]], [[athena]], [[robe]], [[girdle]], [[earrings]], [[veil]], [[sandals]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). From the earrings “much grace shone” (14.183; see [[charis]]).

**Aphrodite.** She calls Aphrodite apart from the other gods and asks whether she will refuse her, “because I help the Danaans and you the Trojans” (14.190–192; see [[aphrodite]]). Aphrodite answers with Hera's titles: “Hera, august goddess, daughter of great Cronus” (14.194; see [[august-goddess]] and [[cronus]]). Then, “with treachery in her heart” (δολοφρονέουσα, 14.197), Hera asks for “love and longing, with which you / overcome all the immortals and mortal men” (14.198–199; see [[himeros]]), on the pretext of a journey to the limits of the earth to end the quarrel of Oceanus and Tethys, who reared her in their halls when Zeus put Cronus below the earth (14.200–210; see [[oceanus]], [[tethys]] and [[rhea]]). Aphrodite cannot refuse the wife of Zeus, “for you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest of all” (14.212–213), and gives her the embroidered strap (14.214–221; see [[aphrodites-band]]).

“So she spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera smiled, / and smiling, she put it in her bosom” (14.222–223). The couplet is the one in which she smiled at Hephaestus and took the cup from his hand at the end of the quarrel in Book 1 (1.595–596; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]).

**Sleep.** She darts down from Olympus by Pieria, Emathia, the Thracian mountains and Athos to Lemnos (14.225–230; see [[pieria]], [[emathia]], [[athos]] and [[lemnos]]), and there she clings to Sleep's hand. She flatters him as “lord of all gods and of all men”, reminds him that he obeyed her once before, and offers a golden throne that Hephaestus, “my son”, will make, with a footstool (14.231–241; see [[sleep]], [[throne]] and [[footstool]]). Sleep refuses: her command once “taught me a lesson”, when she drove Heracles to Cos and Zeus would have thrown Sleep into the sea (14.242–262; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). She answers sharply: “Do you think that far-seeing Zeus will help the Trojans as much / as he was enraged for Heracles, his own son?” (14.265–266). Then she raises her price to a bride, one of the younger Graces (14.267–269; see [[pasithea]]). Sleep makes her swear by the Styx, with one hand on the earth and one on the sea, “and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey” (14.271–277; see [[white-armed]]). She names “all the gods / beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans” (14.278–279; see [[oath]], [[styx]] and [[titans]]).

**On Gargarus.** Leaving Sleep in a fir, “Hera quickly went up to the peak of Gargarus / on lofty Ida” (14.292–293; see [[gargarus]]). Zeus asks why she has come without horses or chariot, and she tells him, almost word for word, the story she told Aphrodite, adding that her horses wait at the foot of Ida and that she came to tell him lest he be angry if she went in silence (14.298–311). When he answers with the catalogue of his loves, ending “nor you yourself, / as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.327–328; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]), she begins as she does in their quarrels: “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” (14.330; 1.552, 4.25, 8.462; see [[most-dread]]). What if a god saw them on the open peaks and told the others? She could not go back to his house; “it would be a thing for blame” (14.331–336; see [[nemesis]]). Let them go to the chamber Hephaestus built, “your dear son”, she now says to Zeus (14.338–340). Zeus wraps them in a golden cloud instead, “and the son of Cronus took his wife in his arms” (14.342–351; see [[the-golden-cloud]]). He sleeps “and held his wife in his arms” (14.352–353), and Sleep can tell Poseidon that “Hera has beguiled him” (14.360; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

**Her titles.** The book gives Hera her full range of epithets: χρυσόθρονος, “of the golden throne” (14.153; see [[golden-throned]]), βοῶπις πότνια, “ox-eyed queen” (14.159, 14.222, 14.263; see [[queenly]]), πρέσβα θεά and θύγατερ μεγάλοιο Κρόνοιο, “august goddess, daughter of great Cronus”, in the mouths of Aphrodite and Sleep (14.194, 14.243), and θεὰ λευκώλενος, “the white-armed goddess” (14.277). δολοφρονέουσα, “with treachery in her heart”, is hers three times (14.197, 14.300, 14.329), and once more in Agamemnon's story of how she deceived Zeus at the birth of Heracles (19.106).

**After.** Zeus wakes beside her on the peaks of Ida, sees what she has done, and threatens her with the punishment of the past. She swears to him by Earth and Heaven and the water of Styx that Poseidon harms the Trojans of his own will and not through hers, and Zeus sends her back to Olympus with his orders (15.4–79; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).
