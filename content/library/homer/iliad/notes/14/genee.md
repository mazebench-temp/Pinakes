**Youngest by birth.** When Agamemnon asks for a better plan, “young or old” (14.107–108), Diomedes answers that the man is near, if they will not resent it “because I am the youngest by birth among you” (γενεῆφι νεώτατος, 14.112; see [[diomedes]]). γενεή here is age, a man's place by birth among his kin and peers: Nestor told him in Book 9 that he could be his son, “the youngest by birth”, and yet he spoke wisely (ὁπλότατος γενεῆφιν, 9.57–58), and Poseidon tells Apollo in the battle of the gods to begin, since he is “younger by birth” (21.439). Youth is a reason not to be heard, and Diomedes answers it with the other sense of the word: “but I too claim to be of the line of a brave father, / Tydeus” (πατρὸς δʼ ἐξ ἀγαθοῦ καὶ ἐγὼ γένος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 14.113–114; see [[euchomai]] and [[tydeus]]).

**The descent.** He gives it in three generations: Portheus had three sons who lived in Pleuron and Calydon, Agrius, Melas and Oeneus, who surpassed the others in prowess and was the father of his father; Tydeus wandered to Argos, married a daughter of Adrastus, and lived rich in fields, orchards and flocks, the best of the Achaeans with the spear (14.115–125; see [[portheus]], [[oeneus]], [[adrastus]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). The conclusion is drawn from it: “So you could not say that by birth I am a coward and a weakling / and dishonor the word I speak, if I speak it well” (γένος γε κακὸν καὶ ἀνάλκιδα, 14.126–127; see [[alke]]). A lineage is a warrant for counsel as well as for courage. Glaucus' genealogy before Diomedes ended in the same verb, “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (6.211).

**Lineage in a face.** Ajax, having killed Archelochus with the spear meant for Polydamas, calls out to Polydamas: the dead man “does not look to me like a coward, nor born of cowards, / but a brother of Antenor, tamer of horses, / or a son; for in his lineage he looked most like him” (αὐτῷ γὰρ γενεὴν ἄγχιστα ἐῴκει, 14.472–474; see [[archelochus]], [[antenor]] and [[ajax]]). The narrator has already said that Archelochus was Antenor's son (14.463–464), and adds that Ajax spoke “knowing well” (14.475). Ajax reads a family in a face, and offers the dead man's birth as proof that he is a worthy price for Prothoenor (see [[poine]]).
