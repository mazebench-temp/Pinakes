---
summary: Hera covers herself with a κρήδεμνον, “a fine one, new-made, and it was white like the sun”, the last garment of her adornment before her sandals.
grc: [κρηδέμνῳ]
en: [veil]
---

“And with a veil over all she covered herself, brilliant among goddesses, / a fine one, new-made, and it was white like the sun” (κρηδέμνῳ δʼ ἐφύπερθε καλύψατο, 14.184–185; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]] and [[helios]]). The κρήδεμνον goes on over everything else, ἐφύπερθε, “above”. Its brightness is the sun's, and it is “new-made” (νηγάτεος), the rare word of Agamemnon's tunic in Book 2 (2.43; see [[dressing-scene]]).

κρήδεμνον is a woman's veil or headdress, and the word appears in Homer at moments of weight. Andromache, seeing Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot, throws from her head her headdress and the veil that golden Aphrodite gave her on the day Hector led her from her father's house (22.468–472). Achilles wishes that he and Patroclus alone might loosen the sacred veil of Troy, its crown of walls (Τροίης ἱερὰ κρήδεμνα λύωμεν, 16.97–100). In the Odyssey Penelope stands before the suitors “holding her shining veil before her cheeks” (*Odyssey* 1.334), and Ino gives Odysseus her immortal veil to tie under his chest and save him from the sea (*Odyssey* 5.346–351).
