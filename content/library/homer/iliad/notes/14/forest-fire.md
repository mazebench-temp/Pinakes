---
summary: In Book 14 the forest fire is heard rather than seen: the roar of a fire rushing up the glens of a mountain to burn the forest is the second of three noises that the shout of Trojans and Achaeans outdoes (14.396–397).
grc: ["καιέμεν ὕλην"]
en: [to burn the forest]
---

οὔτε πυρὸς τόσσός γε ποτὶ βρόμος αἰθομένοιο / οὔρεος ἐν βήσσῃς, ὅτε τʼ ὤρετο καιέμεν ὕλην: “nor so loud is the roar of blazing fire in the glens of a mountain, when it rushes up to burn the forest” (14.396–397). The fire stands between the wave on the shore and the wind in the oaks, and none of the three is as loud as the two armies when Poseidon and Hector bring them together (14.394–401; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]] and [[blazing-fire]]). βρόμος, the roar, occurs only here in Homer.

The fires of the earlier similes were seen: the glare far off of a fire on the mountain peaks, which was the gleam of an army's bronze (2.455–458), and the thickets falling before a fire in uncut woodland, which were the Trojans falling before Agamemnon (11.155–159). This one is a sound. The fire to which Achilles is likened when his killing is at its height also runs through the deep glens of a mountain, and the whole forest burns while the wind drives the flame on every side (20.490–494).
