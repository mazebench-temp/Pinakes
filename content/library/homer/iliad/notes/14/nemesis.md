summary: Agamemnon excuses flight from the ships, “For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night”, and Odysseus shows him what blame is. On Ida Hera refuses an open bed in the words with which Helen refused Paris', “it would be a thing for blame”.
passages:
  - 14.74-81 | Agamemnon's proposal
  - 14.330-340 | Hera's objection
---

**No blame.** Agamemnon ends his proposal to launch the ships by night with a maxim: “For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night. / Better the man who flees and escapes evil than the man who is caught” (οὐ γάρ τις νέμεσις φυγέειν κακόν, 14.80–81; see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]] and [[agamemnon]]). The form is the elders' verdict on Helen, οὐ νέμεσις with an infinitive, “There is no blame that Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans / should suffer sorrows for so long for such a woman” (3.156–157; see [[no-blame]]). There the old men excuse a war; here the king excuses giving it up. Odysseus answers that no man would let such a word pass through his mouth who knew how to speak fitting words and bore a scepter, and that the army would look away from the battle as soon as the ships were dragged to the sea (14.83–102; see [[odysseus]] and [[retreat]]). What Agamemnon calls blameless, his best counselor calls shameful, and Agamemnon gives way (14.104–108).

**A thing for blame.** On the peak of Ida Hera answers Zeus' invitation with a show of modesty. If any of the gods saw them asleep there and told the others, “I would not go back to your house / when I rose from that bed; it would be a thing for blame” (νεμεσσητὸν δέ κεν εἴη, 14.335–336). The half-verse is Helen's, refusing to go to Paris' bed after the duel: “I will not go there—it would be a thing for blame— / to share his bed; the Trojan women would all hereafter / blame me” (3.410–412). Helen's fear is real, and she goes all the same; Hera's is pretended, and she proposes the chamber that Hephaestus built, where the doors close (14.337–340; see [[house]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The blame both women name is the talk of others: the Trojan women for Helen, the gods for Hera (see the article above, and [[aidos]]). The same verse-end is Hermes' reason for leaving Priam before Achilles' hut, that it would be a cause for blame for an immortal god to be welcomed openly by mortals (24.463–464).
