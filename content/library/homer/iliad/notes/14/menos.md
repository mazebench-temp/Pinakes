---
summary: Agamemnon complains that Zeus “has bound our might and our hands”; and when Ajax's stone strikes Hector, “the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust”.
passages:
  - 14.409-420 | Ajax's stone
---

**Might bound.** μένος καὶ χεῖρες, “might and hands”, is a pair in the Iliad, usually “irresistible” (ἀάπτους): Asius complained that the Achaeans would not, as he thought, hold back “our might and our irresistible hands” (12.166). Agamemnon, proposing flight, says that Zeus now gives the Trojans glory, “and has bound our might and our hands” (ἡμέτερον δὲ μένος καὶ χεῖρας ἔδησεν, 14.73; see [[agamemnon]] and [[kydos]]). Only here is the might bound, and by a god (see [[plan-of-zeus]]). By the end of the book Poseidon has unbound it.

**Hector's might.** The simile of the oak struck by Zeus' thunderbolt ends, “so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust” (ὣς ἔπεσʼ Ἕκτορος ὦκα χαμαὶ μένος ἐν κονίῃσι, 14.418; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]] and [[hector]]). The man is named by his might, as in “Let us rouse the strong might of Hector, tamer of horses” (7.38), and it is the might that falls. Hector does not die: his companions carry him from the field, and in the next book Apollo breathes great might into him again (μένος μέγα, 15.262; see [[ponos]]).
