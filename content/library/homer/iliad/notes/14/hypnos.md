**The plan.** Hera's plan ends in sleep. She will go to Ida adorned, to see whether Zeus might long to lie in love beside her body, “and she might pour sleep, harmless and warm, / upon his eyelids and his shrewd mind” (τῷ δʼ ὕπνον ἀπήμονά τε λιαρόν τε / χεύῃ ἐπὶ βλεφάροισιν, 14.163–165; see [[hera]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Sleep is a thing poured, as it was poured around Agamemnon when the Dream came to him (2.19), and the god Sleep says that he once lulled Zeus “pouring myself sweetly around him” (νήδυμος ἀμφιχυθείς, 14.253; see [[sleep]]).

**Lulling.** The bargain on Lemnos is made in the verbs of putting another to sleep. Hera asks, “Lull to sleep for me the shining eyes of Zeus beneath his brows” (κοίμησον, 14.236). Sleep answers that he could easily lull any other of the gods (κατευνήσαιμι, 14.245), but not Zeus, “nor lull him to sleep, unless he himself should bid me” (14.248), and he recalls the day when “I then lulled the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (ἔλεξα, 14.252). Elsewhere the Iliad uses them of those who lie down themselves: κοιμάομαι of Zeus going to bed in Book 1 and of the army at night (1.610, 7.482), and κατευνάω only of Paris and Helen, who “lay down together on the corded bed” (κατεύνασθεν, 3.448).

**Zeus asleep.** “So the father slept motionless on the peak of Gargarus, / overcome by sleep and love, and held his wife in his arms” (ὕπνῳ καὶ φιλότητι δαμείς, 14.352–353; see [[philotes]] and [[gargarus]]). Sleep and love stand together in one verse only here and in Menelaus' complaint that men have their fill of them (13.636; see the note on Book 13). Twice before, the poem has put Zeus to bed with Hera or kept him awake. Book 1 ended with Zeus going to the bed “where he always lay down when sweet sleep came upon him”, with Hera beside him (1.609–611); Book 2 began with the gods and men asleep, “but sweet sleep did not hold Zeus”, who was pondering how to honor Achilles (2.1–4; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Now sleep holds him, and his plan waits on it. Sleep runs to Poseidon: help the Danaans and give them glory, “if only for a little while, while he still sleeps, / Zeus; for I have wrapped a soft slumber around him” (μαλακὸν περὶ κῶμʼ ἐκάλυψα, 14.358–359; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). κῶμα, “slumber”, is found only here in the Iliad. In the Odyssey Penelope, waking from the sleep Athena has poured on her, says the same of herself, that a soft slumber wrapped her, and wishes that Artemis would give her so soft a death (*Odyssey* 18.201–202).

**Overcome.** δαμείς, “overcome”, is the verb of the book's powers. Love and longing are the gifts “with which you / overcome all the immortals and mortal men” (14.198–199); desire, Zeus says, has never before so flooded round the heart in his breast “and overcome it” (14.315–316); Night is “the tamer of gods and men” (δμήτειρα, 14.259); sleep and love overcome Zeus (14.353). In the battle the same verb is death's. The stone of Ajax “still overpowered” Hector's spirit as he lay in the dark (ἐδάμνα, 14.439), and Acamas boasts over the man he has killed: “Consider how your Promachus sleeps, overcome / by my spear” (δεδμημένος εὕδει, 14.482–483; see [[promachus]] and [[acamas]]). The dead man sleeps, as Iphidamas “slept the sleep of bronze” (11.241; see the note on Book 11 and [[sleep]]).
