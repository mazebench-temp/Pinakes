summary: Zeus sits on the peak of Ida while the Achaeans speak of his will, and Hera comes to beguile him. Desire overcomes him as never before; he names his past loves, wraps himself and Hera in a golden cloud on Gargarus and sleeps, “overcome by sleep and love”, while Poseidon turns the battle.

**In men's mouths.** Zeus is silent in the first part of the book, but the wounded kings speak of him again and again. Nestor says that not even “Zeus himself, who thunders on high”, could have fashioned things otherwise (14.53–54; see [[thunders-on-high]]). Agamemnon joins his own verse of Books 2 and 9 to the one that Polydamas and Idomeneus spoke: “such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might, / that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (14.69–70; 2.116, 9.23; 12.70, 13.227; see [[supreme-in-might]]): Zeus once defended the Danaans, and now he gives the Trojans glory “and has bound our might and our hands” (14.71–73). Odysseus answers that Zeus has given the Achaeans “from youth to old age, to wind out / grievous wars, until every one of us perishes” (14.85–87), and Diomedes says that his father wandered to Argos because “so, I think, Zeus willed it and the other gods” (14.120; see [[agamemnon]], [[odysseus]] and [[diomedes]]).

**On Ida.** Hera sees him “on the topmost peak of Ida of the many springs”, sitting, and he is hateful to her (14.157–158; see [[ida]] and [[hera]]). He has sat there since Book 11 (11.182–184), and since the beginning of Book 13 he has turned his eyes away from Troy (13.1–9). Her false tale recalls his victory over his own father: Oceanus and Tethys reared her “when far-seeing Zeus put Cronus / down beneath the earth and the barren sea” (14.203–204; see [[far-seeing]] and [[fall-of-cronus]]). Her plan is aimed at his mind, νόον, and his eyes: she will beguile “the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” and have Sleep lull “the shining eyes of Zeus beneath his brows” (14.160, 14.236; see [[aegis-bearing]] and [[apate]]).

**His anger, remembered.** Sleep will not come near him unless Zeus himself bids it, because of the last time. When Hera had Sleep lull him while she drove Heracles to Cos, Zeus woke in anger, “flinging the gods about the house”, and would have thrown Sleep from the upper air into the sea, but stopped for fear of displeasing Night (14.247–261; see [[sleep]] and [[night]]). Hera answers that he will not rage for the Trojans as he raged for Heracles, “his own son” (14.265–266; see [[heracles]]).

**Desire.** “And Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw her. / And as he saw her, so desire wrapped his shrewd mind, / as when they first lay together in love, / going to bed together without the knowledge of their dear parents” (14.293–296; see [[cloud-gatherer]] and [[eros]]). He tells Hera to put off her journey, and swears that never has desire for goddess or woman so overcome his heart, not for the wife of Ixion, Danae, the daughter of Phoenix, Semele, Alcmene, Demeter or Leto, “nor you yourself, / as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.313–328; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]] and [[himeros]]). The last verse is Paris' to Helen after the duel (3.446). Hera calls him “most dread son of Cronus” (14.330; see [[most-dread]]).

**The golden cloud.** To her fear of being seen he answers with a cloud.

{{quote:f932ba52-ccf0-5e73-946c-4939b6d327a5}}

Zeus, who gathers the clouds, uses one to hide himself; the god he names is the Sun, called on in oaths, “and you, Sun, who see all things and hear all things” (3.277; see [[helios]] and [[the-golden-cloud]]). He takes his wife in his arms, the earth sends up grass and flowers beneath them, and the father sleeps on Gargarus, “overcome by sleep and love” (14.346–353; see [[gargarus]], [[philotes]] and [[hypnos]]). Sleep tells Poseidon to give the Danaans glory “while he still sleeps, / Zeus” (14.358–359; see [[poseidon]]).

**His thunderbolt.** He still strikes in the similes. Hector falls under Ajax's stone “as when under the stroke of father Zeus an oak falls / uprooted”, with a dread smell of sulfur, “for hard to bear is the thunderbolt of great Zeus” (14.414–417; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]] and [[thunderbolt]]). Hector is carried to the ford of “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (14.433–434; see [[scamander]]), and the book ends with the son of Oileus pursuing the Trojans, “when Zeus rouses rout among them” (14.522).

**Waking.** At the start of Book 15 Zeus wakes on the peaks of Ida beside Hera, sees the Trojans in flight and Hector vomiting blood, and turns his anger on her (15.4–33; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).
