{{quote:827bae67-54e0-573d-bcca-b3bd892be769}}

ἦρχε δʼ ἄρά σφι Ποσειδάων ἐνοσίχθων / δεινὸν ἄορ τανύηκες ἔχων ἐν χειρὶ παχείῃ / εἴκελον ἀστεροπῇ· τῷ δʼ οὐ θέμις ἐστὶ μιγῆναι / ἐν δαῒ λευγαλέῃ, ἀλλὰ δέος ἰσχάνει ἄνδρας: “and Poseidon the earth-shaker led them, holding a dread long-edged sword in his strong hand, like lightning; it is not lawful to close with it in wretched battle, but fear holds men back” (14.384–387). ἄορ is a sword, τανυήκης “long-edged” (see [[sword]]). εἴκελον ἀστεροπῇ, “like lightning”, uses the fuller form of the word, ἀστεροπή, as at Idomeneus' going out in Book 13 (ἀστεροπῇ ἐναλίγκιος, 13.242).

The earlier lightnings of the poem are flashes of bronze seen from afar: the planted spears of Diomedes' companions (10.153–154), Hector in his armor among the foremost (11.65–66), the bronze about Idomeneus' chest as he runs (13.242–245). This one is also a danger. οὐ θέμις ἐστί, “it is not lawful” (see [[themis]]): no man may meet the sword, and fear holds them back. τῷ may be the sword, as rendered, or the god himself (see the translation's note on 14.386); either way, what Poseidon carries is a weapon in the likeness of the bolt of Zeus, while Zeus sleeps on Gargarus (14.352–353). The poem does not show him using it.

The sword leads the exchanged armor into battle. Sleep has told Poseidon that Zeus is asleep (14.354–360); Poseidon has bidden the Argives take the best and biggest shields, the all-gleaming helmets and the longest spears, and the wounded kings have seen to the exchange (14.361–382; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[poseidon]]). Hector marshals the Trojans, and the god and the man stretch tight “the most dread strife of war” (14.388–391; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). The sea surges up toward the ships, and the armies meet with a shout louder than sea, fire and wind (14.392–401; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]).
