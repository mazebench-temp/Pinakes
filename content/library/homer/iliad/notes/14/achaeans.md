---
summary: The army is seen first through its kings' fears, beaten among its ships with the wall of the Achaeans thrown down, and then, with Poseidon at its head and Zeus asleep, routing the Trojans; the book ends with the Muses asked which of the Achaeans first won the bloody spoils (14.15, 14.49–51, 14.99–101, 14.151–152, 14.509).
---

**Beaten.** Nestor comes out of his hut to see the Achaeans in turmoil, the Trojans driving them from behind, “and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down” (14.13–15; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). He cannot tell “on which side the Achaeans are driven in turmoil and confusion, / so mingled together are they being killed” (14.59–60; see [[nestor]]). Agamemnon sees them as already lost, fated by Zeus to “perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (14.70; see [[argos]]), and he suspects their loyalty: “Ah, truly the other well-greaved Achaeans too / are laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is” (14.49–50; see [[agamemnon]] and [[well-greaved]]). Odysseus judges them otherwise. Let no other man of the Achaeans hear the king's proposal of flight; if the ships are dragged down in the middle of the fighting, “the Achaeans / will not hold out in the war” but will look away and hang back (14.90–101; see [[odysseus]]). Agamemnon will not order “the sons of the Achaeans” to the sea against their will (14.105–106; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Rallied.** Poseidon tells Agamemnon that Achilles rejoices at “the slaughter and the panic of the Achaeans” (14.139–140; see [[poseidon]] and [[achilles]]), and then shouts like nine or ten thousand men, “and he put great strength into each of the Achaeans, / in his heart, to go to war without ceasing and to fight” (14.151–152). The two verses are those with which Strife roused the army at the start of the day, standing on Odysseus' ship (11.11–12; see [[eris]]). When Sleep brings word that Zeus is asleep, the god leads them openly, and the shout of the Trojans and the Achaeans as they rush together is louder than sea, fire or wind (14.354–401; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]).

**Winning.** “The sons of the Achaeans” run up with a great cry to drag away the fallen Hector, but the Trojan leaders cover him (14.421–432; see [[hector]]). In the slaughter that follows, Peneleos boasts of the day “we young men of the Achaeans come back from Troy with our ships” (14.505; see [[peneleos]] and [[nostos]]), and the poet calls on the Muses to tell “who was the first of the Achaeans to win the bloody spoils of men” (14.508–510; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]] and [[bloody-spoils]]). The book that began with Agamemnon's plan to escape by night ends with the Trojans in flight before the Achaeans (14.511–522; see [[rout]]).

Their name is given to an earlier army too. Tydeus “surpassed all the Achaeans / with the spear” (14.124–125), that is, the army of the Seven against Thebes, whom the poem also calls Achaeans (4.384; see [[tydeus]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]).
