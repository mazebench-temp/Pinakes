summary: Sleep will not lull Zeus until Hera swears by the water of Styx, one hand on the earth and one on the sea, with the gods below around Cronus as witnesses, that she will give him the Grace Pasithea; she swears and names the Titans.

{{quote:512fa886-cdaa-54fa-a0dc-d7a78c459d19}}

**The demand.** Sleep, who suffered once before for doing Hera a favor, accepts the offer of a Grace but asks for the gods' great oath. “Swear to me” (μοι ὄμοσσον) is said in the Iliad by those who need a stronger party bound: Calchas before he names the cause of the plague (1.76), Dolon before he spies for Hector (10.321), and Hera, in Agamemnon's story, when she makes Zeus swear on the day of Heracles' birth (19.108; see [[apate]]). The oath he asks for has the form of a solemn promise, ἦ μὲν … δώσειν, “that you will indeed give”, as Calchas asked Achilles to swear ἦ μέν to protect him (14.275; 1.76–77; see [[oath-formulas]]).

**Water, earth and sea.** Hera is to swear by “the inviolable water of Styx” (ἀάατον Στυγὸς ὕδωρ, 14.271; see [[styx]] and [[inviolable]]), and to take hold of the earth with one hand and the sea with the other (14.272–273; see [[earth]] and [[sea]]). The gods' oath by Styx is named again in the next book, when Hera swears to Zeus by Earth, the broad Sky and the down-flowing water of Styx, the greatest and most terrible oath for the blessed gods (15.36–38); in the Odyssey Calypso swears to Odysseus in the same verses (*Odyssey* 5.184–186). The Catalogue calls the Titaressus a branch of the water of Styx, “the dread oath” (2.755).

**Witnesses below.** The witnesses are not the gods of Olympus but “the gods below who are around Cronus” (μάρτυροι, 14.274), and Hera names them all, “the gods / beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans” (14.278–279; see [[titans]], [[tartarus]] and [[cronus]]). In the oath of the truce Agamemnon called on the powers beneath the earth who punish perjurers to be witnesses (μάρτυροι, 3.276–280); here the witnesses are the gods whom Zeus put below the earth (14.203–204). “But when she had sworn and completed the oath” (14.280), the two set off for Ida. The poem does not say whether Sleep received his bride (see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).
