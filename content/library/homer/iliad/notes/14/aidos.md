**Zeus' awe of Night.** Sleep tells Hera why he fears Zeus. When he woke and found Heracles driven to Cos, Zeus flung the gods about his house and looked for Sleep above all, and would have thrown him into the sea, had Night not saved him: “To her I came in flight, and he stopped, angry though he was; / for he shrank from doing what would displease swift Night” (ἅζετο γὰρ μὴ Νυκτὶ θοῇ ἀποθύμια ἕρδοι, 14.260–261; see [[night]], [[sleep]] and [[zeus]]). ἅζομαι is the awe that holds back a hand already raised (see the article above). Only here in the Iliad is the verb used of Zeus, and what he holds back from is Night. Elsewhere it is the regard men owe the gods: Chryses asks the Achaeans to free his daughter in awe of Apollo (1.20–21), and Hector shrinks from pouring wine to Zeus with unwashed hands (6.266–268).

**Dear and respected.** Hera ends her false story to Aphrodite with what she will gain by reconciling Oceanus and Tethys: “I would always be called dear and respected by them” (φίλη τε καὶ αἰδοίη καλεοίμην, 14.210; see [[philos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). φίλη and αἰδοίη are the words with which a welcome guest is greeted: Charis and then Hephaestus greet Thetis as αἰδοίη τε φίλη τε when she comes to their house (18.386, 18.425), and Calypso so greets Hermes (*Odyssey* 5.88). Hera invents a visit to an old couple in order to make a visit to her husband.
