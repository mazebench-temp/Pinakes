ἥν τʼ ἐν ὄρεσσι / χαλκίδα κικλήσκουσι θεοί, ἄνδρες δὲ κύμινδιν: “which in the mountains the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis” (14.290–291; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). This is the third double name in the Iliad. As with Briareus, whom the gods call by that name and all men Aegaeon (1.403–404), the gods' name comes first; at Batieia men's name came first and the gods' after it (2.813–814). The verb is the one used of Batieia, κικλήσκουσιν (2.813), not καλέουσι. Neither name here carries a story, as “the tomb of much-bounding Myrine” does: they are two words for one bird, and the human one, by Aristotle's account, was the name the bird had in Ionia (*History of Animals* 9.12).

The double name falls in a scene among gods. A god sits hidden like the bird, out of the sight of Zeus, while a goddess goes up to deceive him (14.286–293; see [[sleep]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]), and the narrator gives the bird's name in the gods' language first. Plato's Socrates cites this line, with the river that the gods call Xanthus and men Scamander and with Batieia, among the places where Homer distinguishes the names that gods and men give to the same things (*Cratylus* 391d–392a; see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]).
