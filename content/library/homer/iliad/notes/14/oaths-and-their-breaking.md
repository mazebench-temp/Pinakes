---
summary: The oaths of Book 14 are sworn among the gods. Sleep, who once suffered for trusting Hera, will not act without the gods' great oath by the Styx; and in the next book Hera swears to the waking Zeus by the same water an oath worded so as to deny her part in Poseidon's help without denying her plan.
passages:
  - 14.270-280 | Sleep makes Hera swear
---

**An oath demanded.** Sleep has reason to want a guarantee. Once before he did Hera's bidding and lulled Zeus, and Zeus, waking, would have thrown him from the sky into the sea had Night not saved him (14.249–261; see [[sleep]] and [[night]]). When she offers him a Grace, he asks for the gods' greatest oath: by the inviolable water of Styx, one hand on the earth and one on the sea, with the gods below around Cronus as witnesses (14.271–276), and Hera swears and names the Titans (14.277–280; see [[oath]]). Between men an oath calls on the gods and the powers below who punish perjury (3.276–280); between gods it calls on the Styx and on the old gods under the earth (see the translation's note on 14.271). Whether Hera kept it, the poem does not say.

**An oath worded with care.** When Zeus wakes and charges her with her evil trick, Hera swears again by the Styx, the greatest and most terrible oath for the blessed gods, and by his head and their marriage bed, that it is not through her will that Poseidon harms the Trojans and helps the Achaeans: his own heart urges him on, since he pitied them (15.36–44). Poseidon had indeed been helping of his own accord since Book 13, and Sleep, not Hera, told him to fight openly (13.10–16, 14.354–360). The oath says nothing of the sleep she brought on Zeus, and Zeus smiles at it (15.47; see [[truth-and-deception]]).
