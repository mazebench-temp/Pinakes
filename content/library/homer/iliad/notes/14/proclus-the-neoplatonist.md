---
summary: Proclus answers Plato's objection to the deception of Zeus with a theological reading of the whole scene, in which the union of Zeus and Hera is the union of the father and the mother of the world, Ida is the place of the Ideas, and Zeus' sleep is his life apart from the world. In his notes on the Cratylus he quotes two of the book's verses on Zeus and Hera, and explains why the gods' name for the bird is better than men's.
---

- **The union of Zeus and Hera.** In the sixth of his essays on the *Republic* Proclus takes up the charge of *Republic* 390b–c, that for love of Hera Zeus forgot all he had resolved, would not even go to the chamber and lay with her on the ground (see [[plato-zeus-deceived]]). He calls it a greater and harder problem than Plato's other objections, and answers it under a heading of its own: what the union of Zeus with Hera means, what Hera's adornment is, and the place of the union, and the love of Zeus, and the divine sleep. He draws on a whole treatise that “our guide”, his teacher Syrianus, had written on the myth (*On the Republic* I 132–133 Kroll). Zeus is the father and Hera the mother of all that Zeus fathers. Waking, Zeus provides for the world; sleeping, he lives his life apart from it, and does not forget the one in the other (I 133–136). Ida is the place of the Ideas, and the chamber that Hephaestus built, the craftsman of this universe, is the visible world, so that in lying with Hera on the peak rather than in the chamber Zeus chooses the union above the world (I 136–137). Her hair, her girdle with its tassels, her earrings and sandals, the ambrosia and the olive oil make Hera like Rhea, the mother of the gods; the strap that Aphrodite wears on her breast, Hera hides in her bosom, because she has its power within herself (I 137–139; see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). Their first union “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (14.296) and the greater love of the present are two kinds of union, one their own and one given from above (I 139–140). All that Homer says of the union, then, is said in the manner of theology, and Plato's Socrates bears witness, deriving Hera's name from love (*Cratylus* 404b–c); and if such things are not fit for the young on their surface, the poets might say that they wrote them not for the young but “with a raving mouth” (I 140).
- **Zeus and Hera in the Cratylus.** In his notes on the *Cratylus*, where Plato comes to Hera, Proclus says that Zeus is joined to Demeter, before him, by turning toward her, to Kore, after him, by providence, and to Hera, his equal, by love: “that is why Zeus is said to love Hera”, and he quotes “as I love you now” (14.328). This union is lawful, the other two seem unlawful. Hera is joined to Zeus or contained in him, as Aphrodite says to her, “for you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest of all” (14.213; *On the Cratylus* 169; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]).
- **The bird's two names.** The names that the gods give, Proclus says, are smoother, more euphonious and of fewer syllables than men's: Xanthus than Scamander, chalcis than cymindis, Myrine than Batieia. The chalcis is named for its clear voice, ringing like bronze, and the cymindis for its smallness (*On the Cratylus* 71): [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]].
