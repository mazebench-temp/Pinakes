summary: The sea is the way of escape Agamemnon proposes and Odysseus refuses, the road of the gods from Athos to Lemnos and from Lemnos to Lectum, and with the earth a witness of Hera's oath. When Poseidon leads the Achaeans, it surges up to their huts and ships.

**Flight by sea.** With the wall down, Agamemnon proposes to haul the nearest ships “into the shining sea” and anchor them out until night, then launch the rest and escape (14.74–81; see [[shining-sea]], [[agamemnon]] and [[ships]]). He opens with the verse that began his proposals to flee home in Book 2, to test the army, and in Book 9, in despair (14.74 = 2.139, 9.26). Odysseus answers that the men would lose heart the moment the ships were dragged to the sea (14.96–102; see [[odysseus]]). The ships were drawn up along the whole shore “of the gray sea”, in rows, because the beach could not hold them all (14.30–36; see [[gray-sea]]).

**The gods' road.** Hera's journey runs over sea as well as land: from Athos “she stepped out onto the billowing sea” and came to Lemnos (14.229–230), and with Sleep she crossed to Ida, “to Lectum, where they first left the sea” (14.284; see [[athos]], [[lemnos]] and [[lectum]]). To Zeus she claims that her horses wait to carry her “over dry land and sea” (14.308). The sea is also where Zeus would have thrown Sleep, “from the upper air into the sea, out of sight” (14.258; see [[sleep]]), and where Hera once raised storms against Heracles (14.253–254).

**The oath.** Sleep makes Hera swear with one hand on the earth “and with the other the gleaming sea” (ἅλα μαρμαρέην, 14.273; see [[earth]], [[styx]] and [[oath]]). She touches earth and sea, and the gods beneath them are named as witnesses (14.274).

**Poseidon's sea.** When Poseidon leads the Argives with his sword like lightning, “the sea surged up toward the huts and ships / of the Argives” (ἐκλύσθη δὲ θάλασσα, 14.392–393; see [[poseidon]]), and the shout of the armies is louder than the wave of the sea roaring against the dry land under the North Wind (14.394–395; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]). At the start of the book the sea was Nestor's simile for indecision, heaving with a soundless swell and rolling neither way until a wind comes from Zeus (14.16–19; see [[the-heaving-sea]]).
