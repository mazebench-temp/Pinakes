summary: Hera finds Sleep on Lemnos and asks him to lull Zeus. He refuses a golden throne, remembering how Zeus raged the last time, but agrees for the promise of Pasithea, sworn by the Styx. He goes with Hera to Ida, waits in a fir like a bird, and when Zeus sleeps he runs to tell Poseidon to help the Danaans.

**On Lemnos.** Hera comes down to Lemnos, “the city of divine Thoas”, and there she meets Sleep (14.230–231; see [[lemnos]] and [[thoas-of-lemnos]]). The poem does not say why he is there. She greets him as kin and friends greet each other, “and she clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (14.232; see [[called-him-by-name]]), and addresses him as “lord of all gods and of all men” (14.233). He once listened to her word; let him obey again, and she will be grateful all her days (14.234–235; see [[charis]]). She asks for one thing: “Lull to sleep for me the shining eyes of Zeus beneath his brows, / as soon as I have lain beside him in love” (14.236–237; see [[hypnos]]). Her gift is a golden throne that will never perish, made by Hephaestus, with a footstool, “on which you may rest your shining feet at the banquet” (14.238–241; see [[throne]], [[footstool]] and [[hephaestus]]).

**The refusal.** “Answering her, sweet Sleep said” (14.242; see [[sweet-sleep]]): he would lull any other god, even Oceanus, but not Zeus unless Zeus bade him, for her command once taught him a lesson. He tells the story of the day Heracles sailed from Ilios, when he lulled Zeus for her and only Night saved him from Zeus' anger (14.243–261; see [[night]] and [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). “And now again you bid me accomplish this other impossible thing” (14.262).

**The bride.** Hera dismisses his fear: Zeus will not rage for the Trojans as he raged for Heracles, his own son (14.264–266). Then she offers “one of the younger Graces / to marry and to be called your wife” (14.267–269; see [[graces]] and [[pasithea]]). “So she spoke, and Sleep rejoiced” (14.270). χήρατο, “rejoiced”, occurs only here in Homer. He makes her swear:

He asks for the oath that binds the gods, by the Styx, with the earth and the sea under her hands and the gods below as witnesses (see [[oath]], [[styx]], [[earth]], [[sea]] and [[titans]]), and he names the Grace he wants. For a throne he would not face Zeus; for Pasithea, whom he has longed for all his days, he will. Hera swears as he bids (14.277–280).

**To Ida.** “The two set off, leaving the city of Lemnos and Imbros, / clothed in mist, swiftly making their way”, and leave the sea at Lectum (14.281–284; see [[imbros]] and [[lectum]]). Sleep stays back, “before the eyes of Zeus could see him”, and climbs into the tallest fir on Ida, where he sits hidden among the branches “like a shrill-voiced bird, which in the mountains / the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis” (14.286–291; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]] and [[fir-trees]]). Gods sit in trees in the likeness of birds elsewhere too: Athena and Apollo, like vultures, perch on the oak of Zeus to watch the duel of Hector and Ajax (7.58–61; see [[like-vultures]]).

**The message.** When Zeus sleeps, “sweet Sleep went running to the ships of the Achaeans / to carry the message to the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth” (14.354–355; see [[poseidon]]). He tells Poseidon to give the Danaans glory “while he still sleeps, / Zeus; for I have wrapped a soft slumber around him, / and Hera has beguiled him into going to bed with her in love” (14.357–360). Then he goes “to the famous tribes of men” (14.361), and the poem does not mention him again until Book 16, where he and Death carry Sarpedon home (16.666–683; see [[death]]).
