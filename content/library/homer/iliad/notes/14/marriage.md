summary: Hera wins Sleep with the promise of a wife, one of the younger Graces, “to marry and to be called your wife”; Zeus and Hera, husband and wife, lie together on Gargarus; Tydeus, a wanderer, married a daughter of the king of Argos; and Peneleos foretells that the wife of Promachus will never see her husband come home.
grc: [ὀπυιέμεναι, ἔγημε, δάμαρ]
en: [He married]

**A wife for Sleep.** Hera's offer that works is a marriage: “But come, and I will give you one of the younger Graces / to marry and to be called your wife” (ὀπυιέμεναι καὶ σὴν κεκλῆσθαι ἄκοιτιν, 14.267–268; see [[sleep]], [[graces]] and [[pasithea]]). Sleep makes her swear to it by the Styx (14.271–280; see [[oath]]). ὀπυίω is the verb of the husband who takes a wife; it is used of Hephaestus and his wife Charis (18.382–383; see [[charis]]). Hera disposes of the Grace as a father gives a daughter (see [[bride-price]]).

**Husband and wife.** On Gargarus the poem calls Hera Zeus' wife twice: “the son of Cronus took his wife in his arms” (ἣν παράκοιτιν, 14.346), and he slept “and held his wife in his arms” (ἄκοιτιν, 14.353; see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[hera]]). Between their first secret union “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (14.296) and this one, Zeus lists the women and goddesses he has loved, beginning with “the wife of Ixion” (Ἰξιονίης ἀλόχοιο, 14.317; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]] and [[ixion]]).

**Marriages of men.** Diomedes tells how his father Tydeus came to Argos as a wanderer and “married one of the daughters of Adrastus”, and lived in a rich house (Ἀδρήστοιο δʼ ἔγημε θυγατρῶν, 14.119–122; see [[tydeus]] and [[adrastus]]), as Bellerophon, a stranger in Lycia, was given the king's daughter (6.191–193). And Peneleos, holding up Ilioneus' head, foretells a widow: “neither will the wife of Promachus, son of Alegenor, / be glad at her dear husband's coming home” (δάμαρ, 14.503–505; see [[promachus]] and [[children-and-widows]]).
