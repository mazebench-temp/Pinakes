---
summary: The θυμός of Book 14 is Hera's above all: she rejoices in it at Poseidon's work, finds Zeus hateful to it, and makes her plan there. Desire overcomes Zeus' heart; the boasts of the battle stir the hearts of the men nearest the dead; and Ajax's stone still overpowers Hector's spirit as he lies in the dark.
passages:
  - 14.153-165 | Hera's heart
  - 14.453-487 | Boasts that stir the heart
---

**Hera's heart.** The deception begins in Hera's θυμός. From Olympus she sees Poseidon busy in the battle, “and she rejoiced in her heart” (χαῖρε δὲ θυμῷ, 14.156), and Zeus sitting on Ida, “and he was hateful to her heart” (στυγερὸς δέ οἱ ἔπλετο θυμῷ, 14.158; see [[hera]]). The plan that follows “seemed to her in her heart the best” (κατὰ θυμόν, 14.161; see [[boule]]). Sleep reminds her that she once “devised evil against his son in your heart” (14.253), and on Ida she ends her objections with mock compliance: “But if you really wish it, and it has pleased your heart” (καί τοι φίλον ἔπλετο θυμῷ, 14.337). Zeus' own heart is the one overcome: never yet, he says, has desire for goddess or woman “so flooded round the heart in my breast and overcome it” (θυμὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσι περιπροχυθεὶς ἐδάμασσεν, 14.316; see [[eros]]). In Hera's false story the old couple are parted because “anger has fallen upon their hearts” (14.207 = 14.306; see [[cholos]]).

**The heart of the army.** Among the Achaeans the heart measures the danger. The wounded kings come up from the ships, “and grieved / was the heart in their breasts”, and Nestor's coming “made the heart cower in the breasts of the Achaeans” (14.38–40); Agamemnon fears that the others “are laying up anger against me in their hearts” (14.50); Odysseus' rebuke has “touched me deeply to the heart” (14.104); and Diomedes says that some have hung back from the battle, “doing their hearts a kindness” (θυμῷ ἦρα φέροντες, 14.132).

**Boasts.** In the killings after Hector's fall a boast is answered from the heart. Polydamas boasts over Prothoenor, and “grief came upon the Argives at his boasting, / and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Ajax” (14.458–459); Acamas boasts over Promachus, and the same two verses come again with Peneleos in place of Ajax (14.486–487; see [[achos]] and [[euchomai]]). Each time the man whose heart is stirred kills next. Ajax's own taunt brings grief on the other side: “grief seized the hearts of the Trojans” (14.475).

**Life.** θυμός is also the life. Hector, carried to the Xanthus, vomits dark blood and sinks back, “and the missile still overpowered his spirit” (βέλος δʼ ἔτι θυμὸν ἐδάμνα, 14.439; see [[hector]] and [[hypnos]]). The stone has not taken his life, but it holds it down.
