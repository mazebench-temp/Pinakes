summary: Overcome by desire on Gargarus, Zeus tells Hera that he never loved goddess or woman as he loves her now, and names seven of his loves and their sons to prove it (14.313–328). Aristarchus and Aristophanes of Byzantium rejected the list as out of place.

**The occasion.** Hera has come to the peak of Gargarus with Aphrodite's strap in her bosom, the strap “in which all her enchantments were wrought” (14.215; see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[hera]]). When Zeus sees her, “desire wrapped his shrewd mind, / as when they first lay together in love” (14.294–295; see [[eros]]). He hears her false story of a journey to Oceanus and Tethys, tells her that she can go later, and asks her to bed (14.300–314). Then he gives his reason, and the list is the proof of it: no desire for goddess or woman has ever overcome him as this one has (ἐδάμασσεν, 14.316). It is the power Hera asked of Aphrodite, the love and longing “with which you / overcome all the immortals and mortal men” (δαμνᾷ, 14.198–199).

**The order.** Each love is introduced by “nor when” (οὐδʼ ὅτε, οὐδʼ ὁπότε) and named in the genitive, governed by “I loved” (ἠρασάμην) in the first verse of the list (14.317). The first three mortal women have a verse each, and a verse follows for the son: ἣ τέκε, “who bore” (14.317–322). Semele and Alcmene share a verse, and their sons follow in reverse order, Heracles first, then Dionysus (14.323–325). Demeter has a verse and Leto half of one, and their children are not named (14.326–327). The other half belongs to Hera herself: “nor you yourself, / as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.327–328). ἔραμαι, “I love”, answers ἠρασάμην at the head of the list, and ἵμερος, “longing”, is one of the powers in Aphrodite's strap (14.216; see [[himeros]]).

**A speech of Paris.** Zeus' speech has the frame of the words with which Paris took Helen to bed after the duel: “let us go to bed and take our pleasure in love; / for never yet has desire so wrapped my mind, / not even when first”, and the same last verse (3.441–446, with 3.446 = 14.328; see [[helen-and-aphrodite]]). Paris remembers one love, the first night with Helen on the island of Cranae. Zeus fills the same place with seven, from the wife of Ixion to Leto, and ends with Hera.

**The sons.** The sons belong to the generations before Troy: Peirithous, “a counselor equal to the gods”, whose son leads the Lapiths (14.318, 2.740–744; see [[peirithous]] and [[counselor-equal-to-the-gods]]); Perseus, “preeminent among all men” (14.320; see [[perseus]]); Minos and “godlike Rhadamanthys”, the first of them the grandfather of Idomeneus (14.322, 13.450–452; see [[minos]] and [[rhadamanthus]]); and Heracles, “a fierce-hearted son”, born in Thebes (14.323–324; see [[heracles]] and [[fierce-hearted]]). Dionysus, the only god among them, is “a joy to mortals” (14.325; see [[dionysus]]). Three of the mortal mothers are named, Danae, Semele and Alcmene, and two are known by the men of their families, “the wife of Ixion” and “the daughter of far-famed Phoenix” (see [[danae]], [[semele]], [[alcmene]], [[ixion]], [[europa]] and [[phoenix-father-of-europa]]). Danae, Semele and the daughter of Phoenix are named nowhere else in Homer, and Perseus only in the patronymic of his son Sthenelus (19.116, 19.123).

**Hera's answer.** Hera does not answer the list. She meets it with her usual retort to Zeus, “Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!” (14.330 = 1.552, 4.25, 8.462; see [[what-a-word]]), and objects only that some god might see them on the open peak (14.331–336).

**The ancient critics.** Aristarchus and Aristophanes of Byzantium athetized 14.317–327, “because the enumeration of the names is ill-timed: it estranges Hera rather than winning her over; and a man in haste to lie with her because of the power of the strap talks at length” (the scholium as quoted by Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 14.317; see [[aristarchus]]). Without the eleven verses, Zeus' sentence runs from 14.316 straight to 14.328. Leaf added that the list recalls the Hesiodic catalogues of women, and that its stories are not Homeric, but he did not think this a convincing objection, since the whole deception of Zeus contains myths found nowhere else in Homer (see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Plato's Socrates, who would not have the young hear the scene, makes Zeus say that his desire was greater even than when he and Hera first met “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (14.296), and says nothing of the other loves (*Republic* 390b–c; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]]).
