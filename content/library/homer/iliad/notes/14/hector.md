summary: Agamemnon fears that Hector's threat to burn the ships is coming true, and Poseidon tells the Argives that he will not stand. Hector marshals the Trojans against Poseidon and throws first at Ajax, but the spear is stopped by Ajax's straps; Ajax's stone fells him like an oak struck by Zeus, the best of the Trojans cover him, and at the ford of the Xanthus he comes to, vomits dark blood and sinks back into darkness.

**The threat.** He is first named by Agamemnon, who fears that “mighty Hector may make good his word to me, / as he once threatened, speaking among the Trojans, / that he would not go back from the ships to Ilios / before he had set fire to the ships and killed the men themselves” (14.44–47; see [[agamemnon]] and [[obrimos]]). The last verse gives back Hector's own vow of Book 8, “so that I may set fire to the ships and kill the men themselves” (8.182), and at his assembly that night he said he had meant to destroy the ships and all the Achaeans and then go home to Ilios (8.498–499). “So he spoke then; and now it is all being fulfilled” (14.48).

**Against Poseidon.** When Zeus sleeps (see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]), Poseidon names Hector to the Argives: “are we once again to yield the victory to Hector, / son of Priam, so that he may take the ships and win glory? / So he says and boasts, because Achilles / stays by the hollow ships, angered at heart” (14.364–367; see [[poseidon]] and [[son-of-priam]]). With the best shields, helmets and spears, he says, “I do not think / that Hector, son of Priam, will stand his ground any longer, however eager he is” (14.374–375). Then, while the wounded kings marshal the Achaeans, “on the other side shining Hector marshaled the Trojans” (14.379, 14.388; see [[phaidimos]]), and “they stretched tight the most dread strife of war, / dark-haired Poseidon and shining Hector” (14.389–390). In Book 13 two gods, Zeus and Poseidon, stretched the rope of strife over both armies (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]); here a god and a man pull against each other.

**The spear and the straps.** The duel answers Ajax's challenge at the end of Book 13 (13.809–832; see [[ajax]]).

{{quote:c0184749-9a09-56a7-bdca-45de6ac0b607}}

In Book 13 Ajax's spear could find no skin on Hector, who was all covered in bronze (13.190–194); now Ajax's straps turn Hector's spear (see [[shield]] and [[sword]]). The words of his anger, χώσατο δʼ Ἕκτωρ, / ὅττί ῥά οἱ βέλος ὠκὺ ἐτώσιον ἔκφυγε χειρός, return once, when his spear rebounds from Achilles' shield in Book 22 (14.406–407 ≈ 22.291–292). The verse of his retreat is said of Paris shrinking from Menelaus (3.32) and of men whose blow has failed (13.566, 13.648), and in Book 13 Meriones struck two of them, Adamas and Harpalion, as they went away (13.566–567, 13.648–650; see [[adamas]] and [[harpalion]]). So Ajax strikes Hector.

**The stone.** “Then, as he went away, great Telamonian Ajax / with a stone” (14.409–410), one of the props of the ships rolling at the fighters' feet, hits him in the chest over the shield rim, near the neck, and sends him “spinning like a top” (14.410–413; see [[props]], [[stone-throwing]] and [[like-a-top]]). He falls as an oak falls under the stroke of father Zeus, with a dread smell of sulfur, “so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust” (14.414–418; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]] and [[thunderbolt]]). The simile gives Ajax's stone the force of Zeus' thunderbolt while Zeus himself sleeps on Gargarus (14.352–353). Spear, shield and helmet fall with him, “and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze” (14.419–420), the half-verse that ended the falls of Alcmaon and Imbrius (12.396, 13.181; see [[inlaid]] and [[imbrius]]). When Ajax's stone laid him on his back in their duel of Book 7, “Apollo at once set him on his feet” (7.272); now no god comes.

**Covered.** The sons of the Achaeans run up to drag him away, but “the shepherd of the people no one could / stab or hit” (14.423–424; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The best of the Trojans stand over him, “Polydamas and Aeneas and brilliant Agenor, / and Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, and noble Glaucus” (14.425–426; see [[polydamas]], [[aeneas]], [[agenor]], [[sarpedon]] and [[glaucus]]). They were among the leaders of four of the five companies that stormed the wall (12.88–104; see [[battle-order]]); the leaders of the third, Helenus, Deiphobus and Asius, have been wounded or killed (13.387–393, 13.527–539, 13.593–597). Sarpedon, wounded, once begged Hector not to leave him as a prey for the Danaans, and Hector rushed past him without a word (5.683–691). The others hold their shields before him, and his companions carry him out of the toil to his horses and chariot behind the battle, “and they carried him toward the city, groaning heavily” (14.427–432; see [[the-horses-of-hector]] and [[groaning-heavily]]). The last three verses carried the wounded Deiphobus from the field in Book 13 (14.430–432 = 13.536–538; see [[deiphobus]]). Ajax had told him that his horses would soon carry him to the city in flight (13.817–820).

**At the ford.**

{{quote:5d8b4733-339c-5a9d-92d1-1cdaa2af0431}}

The two verses of the ford come back twice: when the Trojans, fleeing from Achilles, reach the river and he splits them (21.1–3), and when Priam brings Hector's body home across it and Hermes leaves him (24.692–694; see [[scamander]] and [[eddying]]). Diomedes' spear on his helmet once brought him to his knees with night over his eyes, but he came to himself while Diomedes went after his spear and drove off in his chariot (11.354–360; see [[black-night]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). This time he vomits κελαινεφὲς αἷμα, “dark blood” (14.437; see [[dark-blood]]), and “the missile still overpowered his spirit” (14.439).

**Afterward.** “But when the Argives saw Hector going away, / they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (14.440–441), and the rout that ends the book begins (see [[trojans]]). When Zeus wakes, he sees Hector lying on the plain among his companions, gasping and vomiting blood, since it was no weakling of the Achaeans who had hit him (15.9–11). Apollo, sent by Zeus, finds him sitting up and gathering his spirit; Hector tells him that Ajax hit him in the chest with a stone, and that he thought he would see the house of Hades that day, and the god breathes great might into him (15.239–262; see [[apollo]]).

His epithets in the book are ὄβριμος, “mighty” (14.44), φαίδιμος, “shining” (14.388, 14.390, 14.402), and ποιμὴν λαῶν, “shepherd of the people” (14.423), and he is Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam”, in Poseidon's speech (14.365, 14.375; see [[obrimos]], [[phaidimos]], [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[son-of-priam]]).
