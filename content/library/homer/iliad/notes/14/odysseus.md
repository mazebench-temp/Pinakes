---
summary: Wounded, Odysseus comes up from the ships with Diomedes and Agamemnon. When Agamemnon proposes to launch the ships by night, Odysseus rebukes him, “Ruinous man”: Zeus has given the Achaeans grievous wars to wind out to the end, no king should utter such a word, and the army would lose heart the moment the ships were dragged down. Agamemnon yields, and Odysseus marshals the army with the other wounded kings (14.29, 14.82–108, 14.379–382).

**Wounded.** Socus' spear tore the skin from his ribs in Book 11 (11.434–438), and he is one of the three kings who come up from the ships to look at the battle, “leaning on their spears” (14.27–38; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). He says nothing until Agamemnon proposes flight (14.64–81; see [[agamemnon]]).

**The rebuke.**

{{quote:52ce21dc-deb9-5314-b084-4af434097c96}}

The first two verses are the ones with which he answered Agamemnon in Book 4, when the king rebuked him for hanging back (4.349–350; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[what-a-word]]). Then the insult was to Odysseus; now it is the king's counsel that is shameful. οὐλόμενε, “Ruinous man”, is a vocative found only here in the Iliad. οὐλόμενος is the word of the proem for the wrath of Achilles, “that ruinous wrath” (1.2); in the Odyssey one of the young suitors uses the vocative to Antinous when he has struck the beggar (Od. 17.482–484).

τολυπεύειν, “to wind out” (14.86), is to wind wool into a ball (τολύπη): Zeus has given the Achaeans to wind out grievous wars “from youth to old age … until every one of us perishes” (14.86–87). The Odyssey uses the verb for the war at Troy, wound out to its end (Od. 1.238, 24.95), and Penelope for her wiles (Od. 19.137); in the Iliad it returns when Achilles, sleepless, remembers all that he wound out with Patroclus (24.7). In Book 2 Odysseus told the commoners that Zeus has given the scepter to one king (2.204–206; see [[one-ruler]]); here Zeus has given all of them, the king with the rest, wars to wind out until they die.

**The scepter.** “Are you so eager, then, to leave the wide-streeted city of the Trojans / behind, for whose sake we have suffered so many evils?” (14.88–89; see [[wide-streeted]]). Then: “Be silent, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears this / word, which no man would ever let pass through his mouth at all / who knew in his mind how to speak fitting words, / and bore a scepter, and whom the people would obey” (14.90–93; see [[scepter-bearing]] and [[kings]]). In Book 2, carrying Agamemnon's scepter, Odysseus turned back the army that ran for the ships (2.185–206; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]); here he stops the king himself. σίγα, “Be silent”, is his word in the Odyssey too. In the tale of the ambush at Troy that he tells Eumaeus, Odysseus whispers to the shivering stranger, “Be silent now, lest some other of the Achaeans hear you” (Od. 14.493), and he silences Eurycleia with it when she knows him by his scar (Od. 19.486).

**The argument.** “But now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said” (14.95) is the verse with which Hector will turn on Glaucus (17.173). Odysseus' reason is practical. If the ships are dragged to the sea while the war is joined, the Achaeans “will look away, and hang back from battle”, and the plan would ruin them: “Then your counsel would do us harm, leader of the people” (14.96–102; see [[boule]]). Agamemnon yields at once: “Odysseus, you have touched me deeply to the heart with a grievous / rebuke” (14.104–105; see [[no-blame-in-fleeing]]). Plato's Athenian Stranger quotes the argument in the *Laws* to show that ships behind an army teach it to run (*Laws* 706d–707a; see [[plato-dragging-the-ships]]).

**With the kings.** He goes back to the battle with the others, and when Poseidon has ordered the best men into the best armor, he is one of the three wounded kings who marshal the army and see it done: “the son of Tydeus, and Odysseus, and the son of Atreus, Agamemnon” (14.379–382).
