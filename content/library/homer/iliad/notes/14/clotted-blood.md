---
summary: Nestor tells the wounded Machaon to drink until Hecamede heats a bath and “washes away the clotted blood,” the first time the formula is used of a living man.
---

“But you, for now, sit and drink the sparkling wine, / until Hecamede of the lovely tresses heats a warm bath / for you and washes away the clotted blood” (14.5–7; [[machaon]], [[hecamede]], [[nestor]], [[bathing]]). Machaon was struck in the shoulder by Paris' arrow and brought to Nestor's hut in Book 11 (11.505–520, 11.596–643). Before, the formula washed the dead on the day of the truce (7.425); later it washes the body of Patroclus (18.343–345), and the Achaean leaders try in vain to persuade Achilles to wash the gore from himself before Patroclus is burned (23.39–47). Here it is a care for the wounded, and the bath is promised, not described: Nestor goes out to look at the battle, and the poem leaves Machaon with his wine (14.8–15). See [[with-lovely-tresses]].
