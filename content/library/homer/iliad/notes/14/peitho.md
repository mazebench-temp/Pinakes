summary: The verse “Come then, let us all do as I say” introduces Agamemnon's plan of flight and Poseidon's plan of battle; the kings obey Diomedes and the army obeys Poseidon in the same verse. Among the gods Hera persuades, or tries to persuade, Aphrodite and Sleep, and in the end obeys Sleep.

**One verse, two plans.** Agamemnon introduces his proposal to launch the ships and escape by night: “Come then, let us all do as I say” (ἀλλʼ ἄγεθʼ ὡς ἂν ἐγὼν εἴπω πειθώμεθα πάντες, 14.74; see [[agamemnon]] and [[retreat]]). It is the verse with which he proposed flight twice before, to test the army and in earnest (2.139, 9.26). It is refused: Odysseus answers that the army would not hold out (14.83–102), and Agamemnon says that he will not order the Achaeans to drag the ships to the sea against their will (14.105–106). Diomedes asks only that they be willing “to obey” (πείθεσθαι, 14.111), and his counsel is taken: “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (14.133). When Poseidon leads the army, he opens his plan with Agamemnon's verse, “Come then, let us all do as I say” (14.370), and this time the plan is to fight: the best men are to take the best shields and the longest spears (14.371–377; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). The same verse of obedience follows (14.378 = 14.133). The words of persuasion are the same; what they serve is opposite.

**Among the gods.** Hera persuades with requests and gifts. To Aphrodite: “Will you listen to me now, dear child, in what I say” (πίθοιο, 14.190). In her false story she would “win over” Oceanus and Tethys with words (παραιπεπιθοῦσα, 14.208–209). To Sleep: “once before you listened to my word; so now once more / obey me” (πείθευ, 14.234–235), and when he still refuses, she offers more. At the end it is Hera who obeys: Sleep asks for an oath, “and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey” (οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε, 14.277; see [[oath]]). Between the requests stands the strap, whose last power is persuasion itself (πάρφασις, 14.217; see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[the-persuasion-that-steals-the-mind]]).

**Trust.** Nestor uses the pluperfect of trust, ἐπέπιθμεν, “we trusted”, for the wall that has failed: “the wall has been thrown down, which we trusted / would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (14.55–56; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).
