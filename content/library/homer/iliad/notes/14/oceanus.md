**The origin of the gods.** Hera tells the same story twice, to Aphrodite and to Zeus: she is going to see “the limits of the all-nourishing earth, / and Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys”, who reared her in their halls when Zeus put Cronus beneath the earth (14.200–204, again at 14.301–303; see [[all-nourishing]], [[tethys]], [[rhea]] and [[hera]]). Oceanus lives at the edge of the world, where the sun rises and sets (7.421–423, 8.485) and the gods went to feast with the Ethiopians (1.423–424), and his house is the one she names to Zeus, “the house of deep-streaming Oceanus” (14.311; see [[deep-streaming]]). The quarrel she means to end, that he and Tethys “have kept away from each other, / from the bed and from love” (14.206–207), is her pretext (see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).

θεῶν γένεσιν, “the origin of the gods”, is the only verse in which Homer makes Oceanus the source of the gods. Sleep goes further: he could lull to sleep any other of the gods, “even the streams of the river / Oceanus, who is the origin of all” (ὅς περ γένεσις πάντεσσι τέτυκται, 14.245–246; see [[sleep]]). In Book 21 Achilles says that all rivers, the whole sea and all springs and deep wells flow from Oceanus, and yet even Oceanus cannot vie with Zeus, and fears his thunderbolt (21.193–199). Homer tells no more of a creation; Hesiod's line of the gods begins elsewhere, with Chaos and Earth, and makes Oceanus a son of Earth and Heaven (*Theogony* 116–133; see [[hesiod]]).

**Later readers.** Plato's Socrates read the verse “Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys” as Homer's teaching that all things are the offspring of flow and motion (*Theaetetus* 152e; see [[plato-oceanus-and-tethys]]), and Aristotle reports that some thought the earliest theologians held the view of Thales, that water is the first principle, “for they made Oceanus and Tethys the parents of becoming”, and the gods' oath water, the Styx (*Metaphysics* 1.3, 983b27–33; see [[aristotle-oceanus-and-tethys]] and [[styx]]).
