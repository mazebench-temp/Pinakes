summary: Stirred most of all by Acamas' boast over the Boeotian Promachus, Peneleos rushes at Acamas, who does not wait for him, and kills Ilioneus instead. He drives the spear through the eye, cuts off the head, holds it up on the spear like a poppy head, and tells the Trojans to bid Ilioneus' parents wail for him (14.486–507).
# Πηνέλεῳ (14.487) and Πηνελέωο (14.489) are found only here; Πηνέλεως (14.496) is claimed by the entry.

**Stirred.** After Acamas' boast, “grief came upon the Argives at his boasting, / and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Peneleos” (14.486–487), in the verses used of Ajax after Polydamas' boast (14.458–459; see [[wise-hearted]] and [[achos]]). The man Acamas has killed is Promachus, “the Boeotian” (14.476), and Peneleos is the first-named of the five Boeotian leaders of the Catalogue (2.494; see [[boeotians]] and [[promachus]]); a second of them, Prothoenor, has just fallen to Polydamas (14.449–452; see [[prothoenor]]). He rushes at Acamas, but Acamas “did not await the force / of lord Peneleos” (14.488–489; see [[acamas]]).

**Ilioneus.** The spear goes to another man: Ilioneus, the only child of Phorbas, a man rich in flocks whom Hermes loved most of the Trojans (14.489–492; see [[ilioneus]], [[phorbas-father-of-ilioneus]] and [[hermes]]). Peneleos stabs him under the brow, drives out the eye, and the spear passes through the back of the head; Ilioneus sits down, stretching out his hands, and Peneleos strikes the neck with his sword and dashes the head to the ground, helmet and all, with the spear still in the eye (14.493–499). Heads have been cut off before in the poem. Agamemnon sent Hippolochus' trunk rolling through the throng “like a log” (11.145–147), and the son of Oileus sent Imbrius' head flying like a ball to Hector's feet (13.202–205; see [[like-a-ball]]). Peneleos holds his up to be seen: “and holding it up like a poppy head / he showed it to the Trojans, and spoke in triumph” (14.499–500; see [[the-poppy]]). φή, “like”, occurs once more in the Iliad (2.144; see the translation's note on 14.499). The poppy has been a dead man's head before, when Gorgythion's head “drooped to one side, weighed down by his helmet”, as a poppy in a garden droops with its seed and the showers of spring (8.306–308).

**The taunt.** “Trojans, tell noble Ilioneus' dear father for me / and his mother, to wail for him in their halls; / for neither will the wife of Promachus, son of Alegenor, / be glad at her dear husband's coming home” (14.501–504; see [[alegenor]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). Acamas boasted that the penalty for his brother had been paid (14.482–485; see [[poine]]). Peneleos answers grief with grief: a Trojan father and mother for an Achaean wife. “So he spoke, and trembling seized the limbs of them all, / and each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction” (14.506–507). The rout begins (see [[rout]]).
