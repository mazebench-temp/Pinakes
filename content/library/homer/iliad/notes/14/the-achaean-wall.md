summary: Nestor sees the wall of the Achaeans thrown down, and the narrator tells where it stood, along the sterns of the first ships, drawn up in rows on a beach too narrow for them. Nestor and Agamemnon name it as the wall they had trusted to be “an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (14.13–15, 14.30–36, 14.55–56, 14.65–68).
# τεῖχος ἐπὶ πρύμνῃσιν (14.32), τεῖχος μὲν γὰρ δὴ κατερήριπεν (14.55) and τεῖχος δʼ οὐκ ἔχραισμε (14.66)
# occur only here; ἄρρηκτον νηῶν τε καὶ αὐτῶν εἶλαρ is the wall at 14.56 and 14.68, and nowhere else.

en: [unbreakable safeguard]
---

**Thrown down.** Nestor comes out of his hut and sees the Trojans driving the Achaeans, “and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down” (ἐρέριπτο, 14.15; see [[nestor]]). He says it again to the kings: “For the wall has been thrown down” (κατερήριπεν, 14.55). The Trojans broke into the camp in Book 12, when Sarpedon tore away a battlement and “the wall was laid bare”, and Hector broke a gate, and some climbed over the wall while others poured in through the gates (12.397–399, 12.457–471; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]). Yet the wall still stands in the next book. The Trojans are driven out over the ditch at the end of this book's rout (15.1–2); when Hector comes back, the Achaeans are forced inside the wall again (15.343–345), and Apollo throws it down before the Trojans, ἔρειπε δὲ τεῖχος Ἀχαιῶν, as easily as a child scatters a heap of sand (15.355–366). The poem throws the wall down twice, here in Nestor's sight and in Book 15 by the god's hand (see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]).

**Where it stood.** The narrator stops to explain why the wounded kings had been far from the fighting. The ships were drawn up far from the battle; the beach was too narrow for all of them, so they were hauled up in rows, filling the whole shore between the headlands, and the first of them, hauled farthest toward the plain, had the wall built along their sterns (14.30–36; see [[ships]], [[stern]] and the translation's note on 14.31–36). Once the wall is passed, the fighting is at once “at the sterns of the ships” (14.51, 14.65).

**An unbreakable safeguard.** Nestor and Agamemnon both name the hope that the wall has failed, in the same verse: “For the wall has been thrown down, which we trusted / would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (14.55–56), and “the wall we built has been no help, nor the ditch at all, / for which the Danaans suffered much, and hoped in their hearts / that it would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for themselves” (14.66–68; see [[agamemnon]]). The words go back to Nestor's proposal in Book 7, to build “high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (7.338). ἄρρηκτον, “unbreakable”, is added now that it has broken. Poseidon complained in Book 7 that the Achaeans had built the wall without hecatombs to the gods, and that its fame would make men forget the wall of Troy that he and Apollo built (7.446–453); now he fights for the men it no longer protects (14.384–391; see [[poseidon]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).
