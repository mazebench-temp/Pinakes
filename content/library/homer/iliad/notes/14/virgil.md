---
summary: The Aeneid takes three scenes from Book 14's gods. Juno buys a storm from Aeolus with the promise of a nymph for his wife, as Hera buys Sleep with a Grace; Venus wins arms for her son from Vulcan in his golden chamber, and Vulcan, like Zeus, sinks into sleep in his wife's arms; and the god Sleep comes down in a man's likeness to take Palinurus, the life that Neptune asked as his price.
---

- Juno's promise of Deiopea to Aeolus for a storm against the Trojan fleet (*Aeneid* 1.50–86): [[virgil-juno-and-aeolus]].
- **Venus and Vulcan.** In the eighth book Venus, afraid for her son in the war in Latium, speaks to Vulcan in her husband's golden chamber and asks him for arms: Nereus' daughter and the wife of Tithonus, she reminds him, moved him with their tears (*Aeneid* 8.370–386). Thetis had asked Hephaestus for arms for her son in Book 18 (18.457–461). When he hesitates, the goddess wraps him in her snowy arms and warms him; the familiar flame runs through his bones, and “his wife, glad of her wiles and aware of her beauty, felt it” (*Aeneid* 8.387–393). He promises all that his art can do, gives her the embraces she wanted, and, sunk in his wife's lap, seeks calm sleep through his limbs (*Aeneid* 8.394–406). Hera, too, goes to her husband adorned, “with treachery in her heart” (14.300), and Zeus sleeps “overcome by sleep and love” and holding his wife in his arms (14.352–353; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). Virgil's Vulcan is not deceived against his will: he tells her that had she asked, he would have armed the Trojans even at Troy (*Aeneid* 8.395–399), and the guile serves a mother's request.
- **Sleep and Palinurus.** In the fifth book Neptune promises Venus a safe crossing for her son, but one life is to be given for many (*Aeneid* 5.799–815). At midnight Sleep glides down from the stars, sits on the high stern in the likeness of Phorbas, and urges the helmsman Palinurus to rest. When he refuses, the god shakes over his temples a branch dripping with the dew of Lethe and drowsy with the power of Styx, throws him overboard with part of the stern, and flies off on wings into the thin air (*Aeneid* 5.835–861). Homer's Sleep, too, works the plan of another god, sits hidden in a tree “like a shrill-voiced bird” (14.290), and goes running to Poseidon when his work is done (14.354–360; see [[sleep]] and [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). In Homer it is the god Sleep who is promised a bride and an oath by the Styx; in Virgil the Stygian power is in his branch.
