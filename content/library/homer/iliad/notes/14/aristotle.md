---
summary: Aristotle draws on Book 14 in three works. The Metaphysics reports that some found Thales' water in the poets' Oceanus and Tethys, “the parents of becoming”, and in the oath of the gods by the Styx; the Nicomachean Ethics quotes the verse of Aphrodite's strap to show that desire schemes; and the History of Animals describes the bird that men call cymindis and explains why Homer calls the Scamander Xanthus.
---

- Oceanus and Tethys, the parents of becoming, and the oath by the water of Styx (*Metaphysics* Α 3, 983b27–33; Ν 4, 1091b4–6, on 14.200–204, 14.246 and 14.271–279): [[aristotle-oceanus-and-tethys]].
- **The strap.** Desire, Aristotle argues, is more unjust than anger, because those who scheme are more unjust. The angry man does not scheme, nor does anger: it is open. But desire is crafty, “as they call Aphrodite guile-weaving”, and as Homer says of the embroidered strap, πάρφασις, ἥ τʼ ἔκλεψε νόον πύκα περ φρονέοντος, with the participle in the singular where our text has the plural φρονεόντων (14.217). So the lack of self-control in desire is more unjust and more shameful than in anger (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.6, 1149b13–18). Homer's verse describes what the strap holds; Aristotle makes it a description of desire itself (see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[the-persuasion-that-steals-the-mind]]).
- **The cymindis.** In the *History of Animals* the cymindis is a real bird: seldom seen, since it lives in the mountains, black, the size of the hawk called the dove-killer, long and slender in shape. The Ionians call it cymindis, and Homer mentions it in the Iliad, saying χαλκίδα κικλήσκουσι θεοί, ἄνδρες δὲ κύμινδιν (*History of Animals* 9.12, on 14.291). Homer's bird lives “in the mountains” too (14.290; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]] and [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]).
- **Xanthus.** The same work reports that the river Scamander is thought to make sheep yellow (ξανθά), and that this is why Homer is said to call it Xanthus instead of Scamander (*History of Animals* 3.12). In Book 14 the river where Hector's companions bring him is “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (14.433–434; see [[scamander]] and [[hector]]). Proclus repeats the explanation, citing Aristotle (see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]).
