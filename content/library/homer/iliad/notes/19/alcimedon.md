“And Automedon and Alcimus, busying themselves with the horses, / yoked them” (19.392–393; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The two put the breast straps on the horses, set the bits in their jaws and pull the reins back to the chariot, and Automedon mounts with the whip (19.393–397). The poem calls him Ἄλκιμος, Alcimus, here and twice in the last book, always beside Automedon (24.474, 24.574).

Walter Leaf takes the name for “the familiar short form of Ἀλκιμέδων” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.392). Ancient readers took it so too. Strabo reports that those who held Homer's Messe (2.582) to be a shortened Messene cited, among the poet's shortenings, “Automedon and Alcimus” (24.474), Ἄλκιμος “instead of Ἀλκιμέδων” (*Geography* 8.5.3; see [[strabo]]). The poem itself never says that they are one man. But the place is the same. In Book 17 Alcimedon took the whip and the reins of these horses from Automedon, who said that no Achaean was his equal at holding them in, now that Patroclus was dead (17.475–483); here Alcimus yokes them with Automedon; and in the last book Automedon and Alcimus are the two companions whom Achilles honors most after the dead Patroclus (24.574–575).
