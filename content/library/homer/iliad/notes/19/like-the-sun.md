summary: Armed in the armor that Hephaestus made, Achilles takes his stand in the chariot behind Automedon, “gleaming in his armor like shining Hyperion” (19.397–398), in the half-verse of Paris coming down to the war (6.513).
en: [gleaming in his armor]
---

τεύχεσι παμφαίνων ὥς τʼ ἠλέκτωρ Ὑπερίων, “gleaming in his armor like shining Hyperion” (19.398). The half-verse is the one that brought Paris down from Pergamus, “gleaming in his armor like the shining sun” (6.513), and the second half of the line gives the sun its own name, Hyperion, where Paris' line had a verb. ἠλέκτωρ is found in Homer only in these two verses. Walter Leaf compares the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*, where “black Earth and shining Hyperion” are to rot the body of the serpent that Apollo has killed (ἠλέκτωρ Ὑπερίων, 369; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 6.513). Ὑπερίων, a common name of the Sun in the Odyssey, occurs in the Iliad only here and in Book 8, where Iapetus and Cronus, in Tartarus, “have no joy in the rays of Hyperion the Sun” (8.479–481). Leaf notes that the name looks like a patronymic and that Homer knows nothing of the legend, in Hesiod, that makes Helios the son of Hyperion (*Theogony* 371–374), which he takes for a deduction from the patronymic Ὑπεριονίδης (*Odyssey* 12.176; Leaf on 8.480; see [[hyperion]], [[helios]] and the translation's notes on 6.513 and 8.480).

The comparison closes the lights of the arming. The army's bronze has made the earth laugh (19.362–363; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]), Achilles' eyes have shone like the blaze of fire, his shield like the moon and like a fire seen from the sea, and his helmet like a star (19.365–381; see [[eyes-like-fire]], [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]] and [[like-a-star]]); now the man himself shines like the sun. The line also joins Achilles to Paris, who, the dying Hector foretells, will kill him with Apollo at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). In the same book Achilles' bronze shines like the gleam of blazing fire or of the rising sun as he comes at Hector (22.134–135).
