**The seventh month.** Hera comes to Argos, where the wife of Sthenelus “was carrying her dear son, and the seventh month had come; / and she brought him out into the light, though he was short of his months” (19.117–118; see [[sthenelus-son-of-perseus]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). ἑστήκει, “had come”, is literally “was on foot”, of a month that has begun (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.117). ἠλιτόμηνος, “short of his months”, occurs nowhere else in Homer. Leaf notes that the Hesiodic *Shield* calls Eurystheus ἀλιτήμενος, “sinful” (*Shield* 91), where Bentley wished to read ἠλιτόμηνον, the word of this verse (on 19.118). Aristarchus took πρό in πρὸ φόως δέ as “before his time”, which Leaf rejects: the phrase is “out into the light”, as at the birth of Eudorus (16.188; see the translation's note on 19.115–123).

He is named twice, at his birth (19.123) and at the end of the story (19.133). Hera gives him the title that Zeus meant for his own son, in Zeus' own verb, ἀνάσσω, “be lord” (19.104, 19.109; 19.122, 19.124). He is σὸν γένος, “of your race” (19.124), which turns Zeus' boast of a man “of the lineage of those men who are of my blood” (19.105) against him. Eurystheus is of that blood through his grandfather Perseus, whom Danae bore to Zeus (14.319–320; see [[perseus]]). The Argives he will rule are the people of the Argolid: Leaf observes that his towns were Mycenae and Tiryns rather than the later town of Argos (on 19.115; see [[argos]] and [[mycenae]]).

**The labors.** The oath stands, and Heracles serves him: Zeus groaned whenever he saw his son “doing unseemly work under the labors that Eurystheus set him” (19.133; see [[heracles]]). ὑπʼ Εὐρυσθῆος ἀέθλων is the half-verse of Athena's complaint in Book 8, where she names one of the labors, the hound brought up from the house of Hades (8.362–369; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]); Book 15 names the herald who carried his orders to Heracles, Copreus (15.639–640; see [[copreus]]).
