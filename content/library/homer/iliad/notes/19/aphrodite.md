summary: When Briseis is brought back to Achilles' huts with Agamemnon's gifts and sees Patroclus dead, she is “like golden Aphrodite”.

{{quote:9cf1d6dc-c4a5-57b3-bb09-8f686bae9a74}}

ἰκέλη χρυσέῃ Ἀφροδίτῃ, “like golden Aphrodite”, is the poem's measure of a woman's beauty (see the note on Book 9 and [[golden]]). Achilles would not marry Agamemnon's daughter, “not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty” (9.389), and the same words as here are said of Cassandra, the first to see her father bringing Hector's body home (24.699); in the Odyssey Penelope comes down from her room like Artemis or golden Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 17.37 = 19.54). Here the beauty is set against the mourning. Briseis throws herself around the body and tears “her breasts and her tender neck and her beautiful face”, and two verses later she is “the woman, like the goddesses” (19.284–286; see [[briseis]], [[patroclus]], [[torn-in-mourning]], [[like-the-goddesses]] and [[lament]]). Achilles had wished in the assembly that Artemis had killed her on the day he took her (19.59–60; see [[artemis]]).
