summary: The quarrel is settled by amends made in due form. Agamemnon pleads blind folly and in the same breath offers to make amends, in the words of Book 9 (19.137–138); Odysseus sets out how it is to be done, the gifts shown to all, the oath, a gracious heart in Achilles, a feast, and adds that it is no cause for blame that a king should make amends to a man when he was the first to grow angry (19.172–183). The gifts and the oath are given that morning; the feast waits until Hector is dead.

**Blind folly and amends.** Agamemnon's apology turns on one sentence: “But since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits, / I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (19.137–138). The second verse is the one he spoke at his table in Book 9 (9.120), ἀρέσαι, “make amends”, with ἀπερείσια ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom” (see [[propitiation]] and [[apoina]]). He lays the act on the gods, and still pays for it; the plea and the payment stand side by side (see [[agamemnon]], [[ate]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The order of amends.** In Book 9 Nestor proposed to win Achilles over “with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.112–113), and the gifts went without the words. In Book 19 Odysseus sets out the whole procedure (19.172–180; see [[odysseus]]):

1. The gifts are to be brought “into the middle of the assembly, so that all the Achaeans / may see them with their eyes, and you may be warmed in your heart” (19.173–174; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).
2. Agamemnon is to stand up among the Argives and swear that he never lay with Briseis (19.175–177; see [[oath]]).
3. Achilles' heart is to be “gracious” (ἵλαος, 19.178), what Ajax asked of him at the embassy, “so make your heart gracious” (ἵλαον ἔνθεο θυμόν, 9.639), and the word of Hephaestus' counsel to his mother, “then at once the Olympian will be gracious to us” (1.583; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]).
4. Agamemnon is to make amends with a feast, ἀρεσάσθω, “so that you may have no lack of justice” (19.179–180; see [[feast]] and [[themis]]).

ἀπαρέσσασθαι, “make amends”, is found only here. Walter Leaf finds the sense unclear: the half-verse ὅτε τις πρότερος χαλεπήνῃ recurs with ἀπαμύνασθαι, “to ward off a man”, where the one who is first to grow angry is the other man (24.369; *Odyssey* 16.72, 21.133), but here the king himself gave the offense; Ameis and Hentze join βασιλῆα ἄνδρα, “a man of royal rank” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.182; see the translation's note on 19.182–183). The context requires that a king need not be ashamed to make amends for a quarrel he began, and Agamemnon had granted in Book 2 that he began this one: he and Achilles fought “for the sake of a girl / with hostile words, and I was the first to grow angry” (2.377–378; see [[no-blame-for-a-king-to-make-amends]], [[nemesis]] and [[kings]]). In Book 4 he took back a rebuke with ἀρεσσόμεθα, “we will make these things good later” (4.362), and Hector used the word to Paris (6.526).

**Accepted.** Agamemnon welcomes the counsel, “for you have gone through all of it rightly and told it in full” (19.186). The gifts are given and the oath is sworn before the army (19.238–268). Achilles accepts them without counting them, for the gifts are Agamemnon's “to give them if you wish, as is fitting, / or to keep them by you” (19.147–148). In Book 9 he would take nothing “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.387); the insult he now means to avenge is Hector's (19.208; see [[lobe]]). He had renounced his anger before any amends were offered (19.67–68), and the amends follow the reconciliation instead of buying it.

**The feast deferred.** The feast in Agamemnon's huts is not held that day, for Achilles will eat nothing until Patroclus is avenged (19.199–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]]). It comes after Hector's death, when the kings bring Achilles to Agamemnon's hut, and he bids them yield to “the hateful feast” (στυγερῇ … δαιτί, 23.35–56).
