---
summary: Argos is named twice, in two of its senses. “Achaean Argos” is the land where Hera brings Eurystheus to birth, to be lord of the Argives (19.115), and “horse-pasturing Argos” the homeland far from which Achilles expected to die at Troy (19.329).
---

**Achaean Argos.** In Agamemnon's story Hera leaves Olympus “and swiftly came to Achaean Argos, where she knew / the strong wife of Sthenelus, son of Perseus” (19.115–116; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]], [[sthenelus-son-of-perseus]] and [[hera]]). Ἄργος Ἀχαιϊκόν is the phrase of Agamemnon's offer of a daughter to Achilles (9.141, 9.283; see the note on Book 9). Walter Leaf takes it as the Argolid, set against “Pelasgian Argos” in Achilles' own country (2.681), and observes that the towns of Eurystheus were Mycenae and Tiryns rather than the later town of Argos (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.115; see [[pelasgian-argos]], [[mycenae]] and [[tiryns]]). The digitized text printed Ἀχαιικόν here, without the diaeresis it has everywhere else in the Iliad (see the translation's note on 19.115). Argos is the first of the three cities that Hera loves best (4.51–52). The child born there will be “lord of the Argives” (19.122, 19.124; see [[eurystheus]]): the Argives here are the people of Argos, not, as elsewhere in the poem, the army at Troy (see [[argives]]).

**Horse-pasturing Argos.** Achilles, lamenting Patroclus, remembers what he had hoped: “that I alone would perish far from horse-pasturing Argos, / here in Troy, and that you would go back to Phthia” (19.329–330; see [[achilles]], [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[horse-pasturing]]). His own home is Phthia (19.323, 19.330; see [[phthia]]), and Argos is the homeland of all the Achaeans, set against Troy. His words are close to Odysseus' fear in Book 9, that it may be their fate “to perish in Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos” (φθίσθαι ἐνὶ Τροίῃ ἑκὰς Ἄργεος ἱπποβότοιο, 9.246), and to the verse that Polydamas, Idomeneus and Agamemnon speak of the whole army, “that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (12.70, 13.227, 14.70; see the notes on Books 12 to 14). Achilles alone was to die so, and Patroclus was to go home.
