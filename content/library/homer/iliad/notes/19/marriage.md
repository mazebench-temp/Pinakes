summary: Briseis' lament tells of two marriages: to the husband her father and mother gave her, whom Achilles killed before her city, and to Achilles himself, whose wedded wife Patroclus promised to make her, with a marriage feast among the Myrmidons in Phthia (19.291–299). In Agamemnon's story Eurystheus is born to the strong wife of Sthenelus (19.116).

“The husband to whom my father and lady mother gave me / I saw before the city, torn by the sharp bronze” (19.291–292; see [[briseis]] and [[posis]]). Her parents gave her, as a bride is given; her husband is ἀνήρ, “man”, as often in Homer. The second marriage was a promise made by Patroclus to the captive:

{{quote:fe876828-b44c-5827-a11e-f054785c002a}}

κουριδίη ἄλοχος, the “wedded wife”, is said elsewhere in the Iliad of Clytemnestra (1.114), of Helen as Menelaus' wife (7.392, 13.626) and of the bride for whom Iphidamas had given much (11.243). Walter Leaf, after a scholium, says that it is always used elsewhere of a wife married as a maiden; he infers from the obeli in two manuscripts that Aristarchus athetized 19.295–300, no doubt because a marriage between Achilles and a captive is foreign to the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.298; see the translation's note on 19.295–300). In Book 9 Achilles called Briseis his ἄλοχος, “the wife dear to my heart”, though she was won by his spear (9.336–343), and said that at home Peleus would find him a wife among the daughters of the Achaean chiefs (9.393–400; see [[peleus]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). δαίσειν γάμον, “give the marriage feast” (19.299): in the Odyssey Menelaus is giving a wedding feast when Telemachus arrives, sending his daughter to the son of Achilles in the city of the Myrmidons (*Odyssey* 4.3–9; see [[neoptolemus]] and [[phthia]]).

In Agamemnon's story Hera hurries to Argos, where she knew “the strong wife of Sthenelus, son of Perseus” to be with child (19.115–117; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). ἰφθίμη ἄλοχος, “the strong wife”, was said of Diomedes' wife (5.415).
