summary: Book 19 begins and ends its mourning in tears. Thetis finds her son “weeping loudly” over Patroclus, with his companions weeping around him (19.4–6), and Achilles will not eat while his companions weep around the body (19.211–213). Briseis weeps over Patroclus, and the women weep with her (19.286–302); Achilles weeps in his lament, and the elders with him (19.338), and Zeus sees them weeping and pities them (19.340). Odysseus would allow the army a single day's weeping (19.229), and Achilles imagines Peleus in Phthia “letting his tender tears fall” (19.323).

Briseis' lament gives weeping a history. When Achilles killed her husband, Patroclus would not let her weep, “Nor would you let me … weep”, but promised her a marriage; “Therefore I weep for you without end, dead, who were always kind” (19.295–300; see [[evil-follows-evil]] and [[lament]]). The women weep with her “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.302), and the elders who groan with Achilles each remember what they left at home (19.338–339). Walter Leaf, after Heyne, takes πρόφασιν not of a pretense but of an occasion: a grief really felt for Patroclus wakes their own deeper sorrows (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.302; see the translation's note on 19.302 and [[captive-women]]). Odysseus' rule of one day's weeping is the army's answer to such grief (see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]).
