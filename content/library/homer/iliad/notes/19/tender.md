---
summary: Ate's feet are “tender,” Briseis tears her “tender neck” in grief, and Peleus in Phthia lets “his tender tears fall”; the book adds ἁπαλοί and ἁπαλήν.
grc: [ἁπαλοὶ, ἁπαλὴν]
---

**Ate.** “ruinous; her feet are tender, for she does not step / upon the ground, but walks over the heads of men” (19.92–93; [[ate]], [[daughter-of-zeus]]). Plato's Agathon quotes the verses as Homer's proof that Ate is a delicate god (*Symposium* 195d).

**Briseis.** “and with her hands she tore / her breasts and her tender neck and her beautiful face” (19.284–285; [[briseis]], [[torn-in-mourning]]). The form ἁπαλήν also catches Paris' “soft throat,” choked by the strap of his helmet in the duel (3.371).

**Peleus.** “who now, I think, in Phthia is letting his tender tears fall / for lack of such a son” (19.323–324; [[peleus]]). τέρεν κατὰ δάκρυον εἴβει is the half-verse of weeping that the entry takes its name from.
