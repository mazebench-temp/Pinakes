summary: Odysseus comes limping to the assembly with Diomedes, still wounded. When Achilles wants to lead the army out at once, he counsels food before battle and a settlement in due form, the gifts brought into the assembly, the oath, the feast; Agamemnon praises the counsel and gives him the gifts to fetch. When Achilles refuses to eat, he answers that the dead must be mourned for a day and the living must eat. He leads the young men who bring the gifts and weighs out the gold, and stays with Achilles after the assembly (19.47–50, 19.154–183, 19.215–248, 19.310).

**Limping.** “And the two attendants of Ares came limping, / the son of Tydeus, steadfast in battle, and brilliant Odysseus, / leaning on their spears; for they still had grievous wounds; / and they came and sat down in the front of the assembly” (19.47–50; see [[diomedes]], [[attendants-of-ares]] and [[assembly]]). The day before, Socus' spear went through his shield and corselet “and tore all the skin from his ribs” (11.437; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[socus]]). Walter Leaf observes that only Diomedes' limp is explained by his wound, an arrow through the foot (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.47; 11.377). The verb σκάζω, “limp”, is used elsewhere in the Iliad only of Eurypylus, shot in the thigh, “limping out of the war” (11.811; see [[eurypylus]]), and the three wounded kings came up from the ships in Book 14 “leaning on their spears, all together” (14.38).

**The gifts he promised.** Agamemnon offers “all the gifts that, coming to you / yesterday in your huts, brilliant Odysseus promised” (19.140–141). The second verse turns round the verse in which Odysseus began the list in Book 9: “all the gifts that Agamemnon promised you in his huts” (ὅσσά τοι ἐν κλισίῃσιν ὑπέσχετο δῶρʼ Ἀγαμέμνων, 9.263; χθιζὸς ἐνὶ κλισίῃσιν ὑπέσχετο δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, 19.141). The king promised; the envoy carried the promise; and the king now names the envoy (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).

**Food before battle.** Achilles tells Agamemnon to give the gifts or keep them, and to lead the army out at once (19.146–153; see [[achilles]]). “Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said: / ‘Not like this, godlike Achilles, brave as you are’” (19.154–155; see [[of-many-wiles]] and [[godlike]]). It is the verse with which Agamemnon began his answer to Achilles in the first quarrel, and Leaf gives it the same sense: do not expect of all men what you can do yourself (1.131; Leaf on 19.155). The men must not go out fasting, for the battle will be long, and a man who has not eaten cannot fight all day; “for in that is might and courage”, food and wine (19.155–170; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]] and [[fasting]]). Then the order of the settlement, each thing in its place: the army to its meal; the gifts brought “into the middle of the assembly, so that all the Achaeans / may see them with their eyes, and you may be warmed in your heart”; the oath, in the verses of Agamemnon's promise in Book 9; a rich feast in Agamemnon's huts, “so that you may have no lack of justice” (19.171–180; 19.176–177 = 9.275–276; see [[oath]] and [[feast]]). Last he turns to the king:

{{quote:eb80bfbc-66c3-5817-a15c-e23e5744a3f7}}

Agamemnon had said it of himself in Book 2: “I was the first to grow angry” (2.378; see [[no-blame-for-a-king-to-make-amends]], [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and [[kings]]). Leaf finds the couplet's sense unclear, since at 24.369 the same half-verse means the man who gave offense, and here the king gave it himself (on 19.182; see the translation's note on 19.182–183). The king answers, “I rejoice, son of Laertes, to hear your word; / for you have gone through all of it rightly and told it in full” (19.185–186; see [[son-of-laertes]]), and gives him the charge: “choose young men, the best of all the Achaeans, / and bring the gifts from my ship” (19.193–194; see [[kouretes]]).

**The living must eat.** Achilles will not eat while his companion lies dead in his hut, and answers both men at once: “and you two urge us to food!” (19.205; see [[patroclus]]). Odysseus answers him again, and claims the right of the elder:

{{quote:b331522a-f499-55d9-8942-188cd873fe76}}

“I was born first and know more” is said elsewhere by gods. Poseidon yields to Zeus because “Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355), and claims the same right over Apollo (21.440). Then the argument. Men quickly have their fill of battle, where the bronze reaps much straw and Zeus gives little harvest (19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]] and [[dispenser-of-war]]). “But the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly” (19.225): too many fall day after day, and the dead must be buried after a single day's weeping, and the living must remember food and drink (19.226–233; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]). Leaf thinks the close of the speech, with its summons to fight at once, out of place in Odysseus' mouth (on 19.230; see the translation's note on 19.230–237). ὀτρυντύς, “summons” (19.234–235), is found nowhere else in Homer.

**The gifts brought out.**

{{quote:1c4067cf-0c8e-52a0-b6f1-1106e66eb419}}

The men he takes are leaders: Meges commands forty ships, Thoas the Aetolians, Meriones the Cretans with Idomeneus, and Lycomedes was a captain of the watch on the night of the embassy, as was Thrasymedes, one of Nestor's sons (2.625–630, 2.638–644, 2.645–652, 9.81–84; see [[meges]], [[thoas]], [[meriones]], [[lycomedes]], [[nestor]] and [[melanippus-the-achaean]]). “No sooner was the word spoken than the work was done” (19.242). They bring the tripods, the cauldrons, the horses, the women and Briseis out of Agamemnon's hut, and “Odysseus weighed out ten talents of gold in all / and led the way, and with him the other young men of the Achaeans carried the gifts” (19.243–248; see [[gold-and-silver]]). στήσας is “weighing”, and Leaf takes the weighing as a sign that the talent was a known weight of gold (on 19.247). The same verse, without his name, tells how Priam weighs out ten talents of gold for the ransom of Hector (χρυσοῦ δὲ στήσας ἔφερεν δέκα πάντα τάλαντα, 24.232).

**With Achilles.** In Book 9 he was the envoy who spoke first; after the assembly of reconciliation he is one of the six who stay with Achilles when the other kings are sent away, “comforting him in his deep grief” (19.309–312; see [[elders]]).
