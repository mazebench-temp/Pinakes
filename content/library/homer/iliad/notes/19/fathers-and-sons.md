---
summary: Achilles' lament turns to the fathers and sons of his own house: Peleus in Phthia, weeping “for lack of such a son”, or dead, or waiting for the news of his death, and Neoptolemus in Scyros, whom Patroclus was to bring home (19.321–337). He goes out to battle with his father's spear and his father's horses (19.387–391, 19.399). In Agamemnon's story Zeus groans whenever he sees his son Heracles laboring for Eurystheus (19.132–133).
refs: [19.321-337, 19.387-391, 19.399]
---

Achilles sets the two losses side by side and finds the third worse: nothing could hurt him more than Patroclus' death, “not even if I should learn of the death of my father”, “or of my dear son” (19.321–327; see [[peleus]] and [[neoptolemus]]). It is the only place in the Iliad where Achilles' son is named, and Aristarchus rejected the verse (19.327; see the translation's note on 19.326–337). The spear is the one Chiron gave to Peleus, which no other Achaean could wield (19.387–391; see [[the-pelian-ash]]), and the horses were the gods' gift to Peleus (16.381; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
