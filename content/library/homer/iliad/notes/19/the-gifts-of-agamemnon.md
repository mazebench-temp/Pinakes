summary: The gifts of Book 9 are given. Agamemnon offers all that Odysseus promised, and Achilles tells him to give them or keep them; on Odysseus' counsel they are brought into the middle of the assembly, where all can see them. Young men carry the tripods, cauldrons, horses, women and Briseis out of Agamemnon's hut, Odysseus weighs out the gold, and after the oath the Myrmidons take everything to Achilles' ship. Of the gifts promised for after the sack of Troy and for the return home, nothing is said (19.140–148, 19.172–174, 19.190–195, 19.238–249, 19.277–281).

**Offered again.** Agamemnon ends his apology with the gifts: “I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (19.138 = 9.120; see [[apoina]]), and then:

{{quote:e2a0db20-c3df-54ca-a51e-153e6f2635f5}}

μενοεικέα, “will satisfy your heart” (19.144; see [[satisfying]]). He promises nothing new. The gifts are the ones named at his table on the night of the embassy and carried to Achilles by Odysseus, and the king now attributes the promise to his envoy (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Neither refused nor wanted.** “the gifts are yours, to give them if you wish, as is fitting, / or to keep them by you. But now let us remember the joy of battle” (19.147–148; see [[achilles]] and [[doron]]). In Book 9 Achilles answered the same offer with “His gifts are hateful to me” (9.378); now he neither refuses them nor asks for them. Phoenix had warned him that if he went into the war “without the gifts”, he would no longer be held in the same honor (9.604–605; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). He is ready to go without them, and it is Odysseus who insists that they be given in due form.

**In public.** Odysseus asks that Agamemnon bring the gifts “into the middle of the assembly, so that all the Achaeans / may see them with their eyes, and you may be warmed in your heart” (19.172–174; see [[assembly]]). In Book 9 they were named before the elders at night; now the whole army is to see them paid. Agamemnon keeps the army in its place “until the gifts / come from my hut and we swear trusty oaths”, and sends Odysseus with young men to “bring the gifts from my ship, all that we promised Achilles / yesterday that we would give, and bring the women” (19.190–195; see [[oath]]). Where Strabo quotes the verse, the gifts come “from the swift ship”, δῶρα θοῆς (*Geography* 10.3.8; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.194, who thinks δῶρα ἐμῆς a false archaism).

**What is given.** “No sooner was the word spoken than the work was done” (19.242). The list follows the first part of the offer of Book 9, the gifts that were to be “at hand at once” (9.135):

| Promised in Book 9 | Given in Book 19 |
|---|---|
| “seven tripods untouched by fire” (9.122) | “seven tripods they brought from the hut, which he had promised him” (19.243) |
| “ten talents of gold” (9.122) | “And Odysseus weighed out ten talents of gold in all” (19.247) |
| “and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses, / strong prize-winners” (9.123–124) | the same verse, without the prize-winners (19.244 = 9.123; see [[cauldron]]); the horses go to Achilles' herd (19.281) |
| “seven women skilled in flawless handiwork, / women of Lesbos” (9.128–129) | “women skilled in flawless handiwork, / seven” (19.245–246), not said to be from Lesbos |
| “the daughter of Briseus”, with a great oath that he never went up into her bed (9.131–134) | “the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis” (19.246), and the oath, sworn over a boar (19.258–265) |

The tripods are no longer called “untouched by fire”, the horses no longer prize-winners, and the women no longer Achilles' own spoil from Lesbos, chosen out by the king (9.129–130; see [[lesbos]] and [[captive-women]]). The verb of the promise returns three times: the gifts Odysseus “promised” (ὑπέσχετο, 19.141), “all that we promised” (ὑπέστημεν, 19.195), the tripods “which he had promised him” (ὑπέστη, 19.243). Odysseus weighs the gold himself: στήσας is “weighing”, and Leaf takes it to show that the talent was a known weight (on 19.247). The same verse, with Priam in Odysseus' place, weighs out the ten talents of Hector's ransom (24.232). The gifts are set “in the middle of the assembly” (19.249), and the oath is sworn over them (19.249–268; see [[agamemnon]]).

**What is still promised.** The rest of the offer depended on the future. If the gods granted the sack of Priam's city, Achilles was to load his ship with gold and bronze and choose twenty Trojan women; if they came home to Achaean Argos, he was to marry one of Agamemnon's three daughters, with a dowry, and rule seven cities by Pylos (9.135–156; see [[the-seven-cities]]). Phoenix described the offer in just these two parts: “now he gives you much at once, and has promised more hereafter” (9.519). Book 19 delivers the first part and is silent on the second, and the Iliad does not mention it again. The condition of Book 9, “if he ceased from his anger” (9.157), has been met (19.67–68), and the demand that followed it, that Achilles submit to the more kingly man (9.158–161), is not repeated (see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]).

**To Achilles' huts.** When the assembly breaks up, “the great-hearted Myrmidons busied themselves with the gifts, / and went carrying them to the ship of divine Achilles. / And they set them in the huts, and seated the women, / and the noble attendants drove the horses to the herd” (19.278–281; see [[myrmidons]] and [[horses]]). Briseis, coming into the hut, sees Patroclus and laments him, and the women weep with her, “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.282–302; see [[briseis]]).

Plato's Socrates would not praise Phoenix for advising Achilles to take the gifts and only then give up his wrath (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).
