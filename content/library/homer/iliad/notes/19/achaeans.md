summary: Achilles calls the Achaeans to assembly, and all of them come, even the helmsmen and the stewards; they rejoice when he renounces his wrath, and hear the quarrel ended, the gifts shown and the oath sworn. Young men of the Achaeans carry the gifts. Then they eat, arm, and pour out from the ships (19.34–75, 19.156–174, 19.193, 19.248, 19.351–356).

**The summons.** Thetis tells her son to call “the Achaean heroes to an assembly” (19.34), and he goes along the shore shouting and “roused the Achaean heroes” (19.41; see [[assembly]] and [[heros]]). Thetis' verse is the one in which Athena tells Telemachus to call the assembly of Ithaca, εἰς ἀγορὴν καλέσας ἥρωας Ἀχαιούς (19.34 ≈ *Odyssey* 1.272). All of them come: “even those who before had always stayed in the gathering of the ships, / who were helmsmen and held the steering oars of the ships, / and were stewards beside the ships, dealing out the food, / even they came to the assembly then, because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (19.42–46). Walter Leaf would take the helmsmen and stewards for a later gloss, if the book were not so concerned with feeding (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.43). Diomedes and Odysseus come limping, Agamemnon last and wounded, and “when all the Achaeans were gathered together” Achilles stands up to speak (19.47–55; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**The cost.** Achilles counts the quarrel's cost in Achaeans: “then not so many Achaeans would have bitten the boundless ground with their teeth / at the hands of their enemies, while I nursed my wrath apart. / For Hector and the Trojans that was the better thing; but the Achaeans, / I think, will long remember my strife and yours” (19.61–64; see [[androktasia]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). He asks Agamemnon to “urge the long-haired Achaeans on to war” (19.69; see [[long-haired]]). “So he spoke, and the well-greaved Achaeans rejoiced / that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (19.74–75; see [[well-greaved]] and [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). After the oath he gives the same reckoning to Zeus: Zeus “wanted death to come upon many of the Achaeans” (19.274).

**The army's part.** Agamemnon begins with the army's reproach: “Often the Achaeans have spoken this word to me / and kept rebuking me” (19.85–86; see [[agamemnon]]). Odysseus speaks for the army's needs. Achilles is not to urge “the sons of the Achaeans to Ilios fasting”; let him “bid the Achaeans eat by the swift ships” (19.156–161; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]). The gifts are to be brought “into the middle of the assembly, so that all the Achaeans / may see them with their eyes” (19.173–174), for the amends are made before the whole army (see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). “But the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly” (19.225): too many of them fall every day (see [[fasting]]). Achilles would have “the sons of the Achaeans fight / fasting and unfed” (19.206–207); Odysseus grants that he is “by far the strongest of the Achaeans”, but claims the better understanding for himself (19.216–219).

**Young men.** Agamemnon tells Odysseus to choose “young men, the best of all the Achaeans” (κούρητας ἀριστῆας Παναχαιῶν, 19.193), and “the other young men of the Achaeans carried the gifts” (κούρητες Ἀχαιῶν, 19.248). κούρητες, a form of κοῦροι, “young men”, is used only in these two verses, and is not the people the Curetes (see the translation's note on 19.193, 19.248, [[kouretes]] and [[curetes]]). Strabo quotes both verses to show that Homer called young soldiers so (*Geography* 10.3.8). Those named are leaders: the sons of Nestor, Meges, Thoas, Meriones, Lycomedes and Melanippus (19.238–240; see [[odysseus]] and [[melanippus-the-achaean]]). The verse-end ἀριστῆας Παναχαιῶν is that of the verse in which Agamemnon called the elders, “the best of all the Achaeans”, to his sacrifice in Book 2 (2.404; see [[elders]]). Παναχαιῶν, “of all the Achaeans”, counts for the people (see the note on Book 2).

**After the assembly.** The elders of the Achaeans beg Achilles to eat (19.303–304; see [[elders]]). In his lament he remembers how Patroclus served him a meal “whenever the Achaeans were in haste / to bring tearful Ares against the horse-taming Trojans” (19.317–318; see [[patroclus]]). While Athena drips nectar into his breast, “the Achaeans / were at once arming throughout the army”, and helmets, shields, corselets and spears pour from the ships as thick as snowflakes (19.351–361; see [[arming-scene]]). No other of the Achaeans could wield his father's spear (19.388–389; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).
