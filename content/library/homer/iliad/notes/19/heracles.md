**The birth.** Heracles is named once in the book, by his might, βίην Ἡρακληείην, “mighty Heracles” (19.98; see [[might-of-heracles]]), as in Hesiod's account of his birth (*Theogony* 943). The birth itself is never told. Zeus boasts that a man of his blood born that day will be lord over all who dwell around him (19.104–105); Hera binds him by an oath, brings Eurystheus out into the light in the seventh month and “stopped Alcmene's childbirth, and held back the Eileithyiai” (19.119; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]], [[alcmene]] and [[eileithyia]]). The rule that Zeus meant for his son goes to “Eurystheus, child of Sthenelus the son of Perseus” (19.123; see [[eurystheus]]). Heracles was born, like his rival, of the blood of Zeus, but he was Zeus' own son (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.105).

**The labors.** The story ends with Heracles grown and serving: Zeus “always groaned whenever he saw his dear son / doing unseemly work under the labors that Eurystheus set him” (19.132–133). ὑπʼ Εὐρυσθῆος ἀέθλων is the half-verse of Athena's complaint in Book 8, where she saved him “when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him” (8.363; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]] and [[contests]]). The labors are ἔργον ἀεικές, “unseemly work” (19.133), the work of a man under another's orders. Leaf reports Wilamowitz's view that the canonical number of twelve labors goes back to a poem, purely conjectural, by a Dorian of the Argolid of no later than the eighth century (on 19.133).

**Zeus' grief.** The groaning of Zeus is the grief the poem has already given him for this son: the ceaseless pain for “divine Heracles” that would not leave his heart (15.24–25), and Achilles' word that Heracles was “the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (18.118; see [[zeus]]). Hera's enmity begins on the day of his birth (see [[hera]] and [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]).

**So I too.** Heracles' story serves two speakers on two days. In Book 18 Achilles took his death as an example for his own: “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom … So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me, / shall lie still when I am dead” (18.117–121). In Book 19 Agamemnon takes his birth as an example of blind folly, and turns it on himself with the same words, ὣς καὶ ἐγών, “So I too” (19.134; see [[ate]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]).
