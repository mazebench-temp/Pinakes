summary: In Agamemnon's story Zeus boasts that Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, will that day bring into the light a man of his blood who will rule all who dwell around him; Hera brings Eurystheus to birth in the seventh month and holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene.
passages:
  - 19.103-105 | Eileithyia in Zeus' boast
  - 19.117-119 | Hera holds back the Eileithyiai
---

**Zeus' boast.** “Today Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, will bring into the light / a man who will be lord over all who dwell around him, / of the lineage of those men who are of my blood” (19.103–105; see [[zeus]], [[who-brings-hard-birth]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). μογοστόκος Εἰλείθυια ends the verse of the birth of Eudorus, whom she brought “out into the light” (16.187–188; see the note on Book 16). φόως δέ, “into the light”, is φόωσδε with the suffix of direction printed apart, as at 16.188 and at the birth of Eurystheus, “and she brought him out into the light” (19.118; see the translation's notes on 16.188 and 19.115–123).

**Held back.** Hera comes to Achaean Argos, brings the wife of Sthenelus to birth in the seventh month, “and she stopped Alcmene's childbirth, and held back the Eileithyiai” (σχέθε δʼ Εἰλειθυίας, 19.117–119; see [[hera]], [[alcmene]] and [[eurystheus]]). The goddess is one in Zeus' boast and many in Hera's trick, as she is many in the simile of Book 11, where the Eileithyiai are “the daughters of Hera” (11.270–271): Hera holds back her own daughters. Walter Leaf compares the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.119). There Leto is in labor on Delos for nine days and nights, because Hera in her jealousy keeps Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, on Olympus, unaware of it; when Iris has brought her and she sets foot on Delos, the labor takes Leto, and Apollo leaps out into the light, πρὸ φόωσδε (*Hymn to Apollo* 91–119; see [[leto]] and [[apollo]]).
