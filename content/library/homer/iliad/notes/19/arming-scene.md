summary: Achilles arms in the armor that Hephaestus made, the last of the four full arming scenes of the Iliad. Greaves, corselet, sword and shield come in the verses of Paris' and Patroclus' armings; then the shield shines like the moon and the helmet like a star, Achilles tries the armor, which lifts him like wings, and he takes up his father's spear (19.364–391).

**Before the arming.** Achilles says, “Now I will arm myself” (θωρήξομαι, 19.23), but fears for the body of Patroclus, and Thetis tells him to renounce his wrath and “arm yourself at once for the war” (θωρήσσεο, 19.36; see [[corselet]]). He arms only after the assembly, the oath and the gifts, and after Athena has dripped nectar into his breast, while the Achaeans arm throughout the army and pour out of the ships (19.352–363; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]). Then “in their midst brilliant Achilles armed himself” (19.364). Before the first piece come the gnashing of his teeth, his blazing eyes, the grief that enters his heart, and a verse that names the armor as the god's: “and raging against the Trojans / he put on the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus had toiled to make” (19.365–368; see [[eyes-like-fire]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

**Verse by verse.** Set beside the armings of Paris (3.330–338), Agamemnon (11.17–46) and Patroclus (16.131–144), the scene keeps the order and the formulas and changes what the moment needs:

1. **Greaves.** The same two verses in all four (19.369–370 = 3.330–331 = 11.17–18 = 16.131–132; see [[greaves]]).
2. **Corselet.** The same verse in all four (19.371 = 3.332 = 11.19 = 16.133). Paris' corselet is his brother Lycaon's (3.333), Agamemnon's a guest-gift from Cinyras with bands of κύανος, gold and tin (11.20–28), and Patroclus' is Achilles' own, “starry” (16.134); Achilles' new corselet has no verse of its own (see [[corselet]]). Walter Leaf notes that 369–373 repeat 3.330–332 and 334–335, and adds that “371 is later, as usual” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.369).
3. **Sword.** The verse and a half of Paris and Patroclus, the “silver-studded sword / of bronze” (19.372–373 = 3.334–335 = 16.135–136). Agamemnon's sword had golden studs and a silver sheath on golden straps (11.29–31; see [[sword]]).
4. **Shield.** “and then his shield, great and heavy” (19.373), the second half of 3.335 and 16.136. Agamemnon's was the shield with ten circles of bronze, twenty bosses of tin and the Gorgon (11.32–40). Here the half-verse runs on into a new line, “he took up, and from it, far off, came a blaze as of the moon”, and the simile of the fire seen by sailors (19.374–380; see [[shield]], [[moon]] and [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]).
5. **Helmet.** The others set on their heads a κυνέη “with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it” (3.336–337 = 16.137–138; Agamemnon's “with two ridges and four plates”, 11.41–42). Achilles lifts a τρυφάλεια, sets it on his head, heavy, and it shines like a star, with plumes of gold (19.380–383; see [[helmet]] and [[like-a-star]]). Leaf observes that the pieces always come in the order in which they would naturally be put on, except that one would expect the helmet to go on before the shield is taken on the arm (on 3.330).
6. **The trial.** Only here: Achilles tries himself in the armor, whether it fits him and his limbs move freely, “and it was like wings to him” (19.384–386; see [[like-wings]]).
7. **Spear.** Paris took “a strong spear that fitted his grip” (3.338), Agamemnon two spears whose bronze shone far off up into the sky (11.43–45), and Patroclus two spears, leaving the one he could not use: “Only the spear of the noble grandson of Aeacus he did not take, / heavy, huge, and massive; no other of the Achaeans / could wield it” (16.139–142). Achilles draws that spear from its case, his father's, in the same verses (19.387–391; 388–391 = 16.141–144; see [[spear]] and [[the-pelian-ash]]).

Of the six pieces Hephaestus made four, the shield, the corselet, the helmet and the greaves (18.609–613); the sword is not said to be his, and the spear is Peleus'.

**After the arming.** Patroclus' arming ran on into the yoking of Xanthus and Balius by Automedon (16.145–154), and so does Achilles': Automedon and Alcimus yoke the horses, and Achilles, κορυσσάμενος, “armed”, takes his stand behind his charioteer, gleaming like shining Hyperion (19.392–398; see [[chariot]] and [[like-the-sun]]). Agamemnon's arming ended with Athena and Hera thundering in his honor (11.45–46); Achilles' ends with Hera giving his horse a voice to foretell his death (19.404–417; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]).
