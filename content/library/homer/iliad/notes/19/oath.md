summary: Two oaths are sworn in Book 19. In Agamemnon's story Hera makes Zeus swear a mighty oath on the day of Heracles' birth, and the oath is his blinding (19.108–113, 19.127–129). In the assembly Agamemnon swears the oath he promised in Book 9, that he never touched Briseis, standing over a boar, with Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes as witnesses; Talthybius throws the victim into the sea (19.175–177, 19.185–197, 19.249–268).

**The oath of Zeus.** In Agamemnon's story Zeus boasts that a man of his blood will be born that day to rule all who dwell around, and Hera, with treachery in her heart, asks for an oath: “But come now, Olympian, swear me a mighty oath / that he truly will be lord over all who dwell around him, / whoever on this day falls between a woman's feet” (19.108–110; see [[zeus]], [[hera]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). μοι ὄμοσσον, “swear to me”, is said by those who need a stronger party bound, as Calchas said it to Achilles (1.76) and Sleep to Hera (14.271; see the note on Book 14). The oath has the solemn form ἦ μέν with a future infinitive, ἦ μὲν … ἀνάξειν, “that he truly will be lord” (19.109; see [[oath-formulas]]). “So she spoke; and Zeus did not perceive her treachery at all, / but swore a great oath, and in this was greatly blinded” (19.112–113; see [[ate]]). Some have found Hera's guile in a change of words, “of your stock” for Zeus' “of my blood”; Walter Leaf finds the blindness simply in the oath, sworn rashly, without condition and for a single day, by a god who forgot that births were in Hera's province (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.111; see the translation's note on 19.110–111). The oath holds: Hera brings Eurystheus to birth first, and Heracles labors under him (19.117–124, 19.133; see [[eurystheus]]). Zeus' answer is a second oath, “a mighty oath / that never to Olympus and the starry sky / would Blind Folly come again” (19.127–129).

**Proposed.** In the assembly Odysseus asks for the oath that Agamemnon promised on the night of the embassy: “And let him stand up among the Argives and swear you an oath / that he never went up into her bed or lay with her, / as is the custom, my lord, of men and of women” (19.175–177). The last two verses are those in which Odysseus passed the promise on to Achilles (9.275–276; see the note on Book 9 and [[themis]]). Leaf notes that τῆς, “her”, here refers to no one yet named, whereas in Book 9 Briseis is named in the verse before; the couplet is borrowed, and many manuscripts omit 177 (on 19.176; see the translation's note on 19.176–177). Agamemnon accepts: “These things I am willing to swear, and my heart bids me, / and I will not swear falsely before a god” (19.187–188). ἐπιορκήσω is the only form of the verb in Homer, and πρὸς δαίμονος is “before the face of god” (Leaf on 19.188). The oath is to be sworn in public and over a victim, “until the gifts / come from my hut and we swear trusty oaths” (19.190–191). ὅρκια πιστὰ τάμνειν, literally “to cut trusty oath-offerings”, is the phrase of the truce between the armies in Book 3 (3.73, 3.94, 3.252); here it binds two Achaeans, and the translation has “swear trusty oaths”, since there is no truce (see [[oath-formulas]]). The victim is a boar, “to sacrifice to Zeus and the Sun” (19.197; see [[boar]] and [[helios]]). In Book 3 there were three lambs, for Earth, the Sun and Zeus (3.103–104; see [[lambs]]).

**The rite.** The rite is the rite of Book 3, shortened:

1. The gifts are set in the middle of the assembly. Agamemnon stands up, and Talthybius stands beside him, holding the boar (19.249–251; see [[talthybius]]).
2. Agamemnon draws the knife that hangs beside the sheath of his sword and cuts the first hairs from the boar (19.252–254 ≈ 3.271–273; see [[knife]] and [[hair]]).
3. He lifts his hands to Zeus and prays, while the Argives sit in silence in their places; then, “looking up into the wide sky”, he speaks the oath (19.254–265; see [[prayer]] and [[silence]]).
4. He cuts the boar's throat “with the pitiless bronze” (19.266 ≈ 3.292; see [[pitiless-bronze]]).
5. Talthybius flings the boar into the sea (19.267–268).

There is no water for the hands, no mixing bowl, no wine poured out and no curse spoken by the army (compare 3.268–270, 3.295–301). The hairs are not shared out among the leaders, as they were in Book 3 (3.273–274). ἀπὸ τρίχας ἀρξάμενος, “cutting the first hairs”, uses the word of first-fruits: the hairs are the ἀπαρχή of the victim (Leaf on 19.254, referring to 3.273; see [[sacrifice]]).

**The words.**

{{quote:1b36d0ba-358d-5968-9bab-71a191bc0ccd}}

ἴστω, “let … be witness”: Zeus first, then Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes (see [[earth]] and [[erinyes]]). The witnesses are those of Book 3 without the rivers, and the powers below who punish perjurers, unnamed there, where the dual τίνυσθον implies two, are named here as the Erinyes (3.276–279; see the note on Book 3). Some ancient readers wrote οἵ θʼ for αἵ θʼ, making the punishers Hades and Persephone, as in Book 3; Leaf takes the words to mean punishment after death (on 19.258). The verse that opens the oath returns when Odysseus swears to Penelope that her husband will come home (*Odyssey* 19.303), and its first words opened Hector's oath to Dolon, which the narrator calls false (10.329–332; see the note on Book 10). The terms are sworn with μή and the indicative, μὴ μὲν ἐγὼ … ἐπένεικα, “that I never laid a hand” (19.261), as Hector swore μὴ μὲν … ἐποχήσεται (10.330; Leaf on 19.261). They go beyond the promise of Book 9: not only “that I never went up into her bed” (9.133), but no hand laid on her, “either wanting her for my bed or for anything else” (19.261–262; see [[briseis]]). ἀπροτίμαστος, “untouched” (19.263), occurs only here. Then he curses himself: may the gods give him sorrows, “all that they give to whoever sins against them in his oath” (19.264–265). In Book 3 each army called down a curse on whichever side should first break the oaths (3.298–301); here the man who swears calls it down on his own head.

**The victim.** “He spoke, and cut the boar's throat with the pitiless bronze” (19.266). Talthybius flings it “into the great gulf of the gray sea, / whirling it around, food for the fishes” (19.267–268; see [[sea]] and [[fish]]). The victim of an oath is neither burned nor eaten (Leaf on 19.267): in Book 3 Priam carried the lambs back to Troy in his chariot (3.310; see the note on Book 3). λαῖτμα, “gulf”, is found elsewhere only in the Odyssey, and βόσιν ἰχθύσιν, “food for the fishes”, only here.

**The quarrel's oaths.** The quarrel had its oath too. In the first assembly Achilles swore “a great oath” by the scepter that the Achaeans would long for him when they fell before Hector (1.233–244; see [[scepter]] and [[longing-for-achilles]]). It ends with Agamemnon's oath before the same army, and Achilles' answer is not to the oath but to Zeus, who gives men blind folly (19.270–275; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]] and [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]).

In Pausanias' day athletes still swore at Olympia over the pieces of a boar, and he quotes this passage for the old custom; a scholium collected among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems* asks why Hera wanted an oath from Zeus rather than his nod (see [[pausanias-the-oath-boar]] and [[aristotle-the-oath-of-zeus]]).
