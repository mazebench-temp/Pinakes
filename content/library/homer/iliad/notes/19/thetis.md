summary: At dawn Thetis brings the armor of Hephaestus to the ships, where Achilles lies weeping over Patroclus. She sets it before him, promises to keep the flies from the body, tells him to call the Achaeans to an assembly and renounce his wrath against Agamemnon, puts might into him, and drips ambrosia and red nectar into the dead man's nostrils, so that his flesh may stay sound.

**The promise kept.** “Now saffron-robed Dawn rose from the streams of Oceanus / to bring light to the immortals and to mortals; / and she came to the ships, bringing the gifts from the god” (19.1–3; see [[eos]], [[saffron-robed]] and [[hephaestus]]). She had told her son not to enter the battle until she came back, “for at dawn I will return with the rising sun, / bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.136–137), and he had kept her command (18.189–191, 18.216; see the note on Book 18). She finds him “lying with his arms around Patroclus, / weeping loudly”, with his companions weeping around him (19.4–6; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]); she stands among them, “brilliant among goddesses”, clings to his hand and calls him by name (19.6–7; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]] and [[called-him-by-name]]):

{{quote:dbaf83a1-a35e-5302-92c8-527b30e1909d}}

“This man we will let lie, for all our grief”: ἐάσομεν ἀχνύμενοί περ are Achilles' own words of Book 18, when he resolved to let the quarrel be (18.112), as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.8), and he will use them again before the assembly (19.65). πρῶτα, “once and for all”, marks a starting point not to be gone behind (Leaf on 19.9; see the translation's note on 19.9). θεῶν ἰότητι, “by the will of the gods”, is found only here in the Iliad and six times in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 7.214, 11.341, 12.190, 14.198, 16.232, 17.119). She sets the armor down before him, and “all its intricate designs rang” (19.12–13; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The Myrmidons tremble and cannot look at it (19.14–15; see [[myrmidons]]); Achilles' eyes shine out “like a blaze”, and he delights in “the splendid gifts of the god” (19.16–18; see [[eyes-like-fire]] and [[doron]]). He tells her that the armor is such as befits the works of immortals, and that he will arm; but he fears that flies will breed worms in the wounds of Patroclus and defile the body, and that all his flesh will rot (19.21–27; see [[flies]] and [[maggots]]).

**The flies.** She answers:

{{quote:c9e31504-6d4b-518b-928a-b6b40d834c6e}}

“Do not let these things trouble your mind” is what Hephaestus told her when she came to him in her grief (18.463, with θάρσει, “Take heart”); she now says it to her son. In Book 24 the gods keep Hector's body as she keeps Patroclus': Apollo holds all disfigurement away from his skin (24.18–21), and Hermes tells Priam on the twelfth day that his flesh is not rotting, nor do the worms eat him, the worms “which feed on men slain in battle” (24.414–415), in the half-verse that Thetis says of the flies (19.31). τελεσφόρον εἰς ἐνιαυτόν, “for a full year's round”, is elsewhere a phrase of the Odyssey (see the translation's note on 19.32). χρὼς ἔμπεδος, “his flesh … sound” (19.33, 19.39), is found nowhere else in Homer.

**The renunciation.** Then she gives him his course:

{{quote:cfa52be7-fcdb-55be-a67c-5c26e66dd141}}

μῆνιν ἀποειπών, “renouncing your wrath”, is her phrase, and Achilles does as she says: he goes along the shore shouting, calls the assembly, and puts a stop to his anger, and the Achaeans rejoice “that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (19.40–75; see [[menis]], [[assembly]] and [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). In Book 1 she carried his plea to Zeus, that the Achaeans be penned in among their ships and slaughtered, so that Agamemnon “may learn / his own blind folly” (1.407–412, 1.508–510; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). In this book Agamemnon speaks of the blind folly that Zeus, Fate and the Erinys cast into his mind (19.86–90; see [[ate]]).

**Ambrosia and nectar.** “So she spoke, and put in him a might full of daring, / and into Patroclus in turn ambrosia and red nectar / she dripped through his nostrils, so that his flesh might be sound” (19.37–39; see [[ambrosia]], [[nectar]] and [[red]]). μένος πολυθαρσές, “a might full of daring”, is what Glaucus wishes the Trojans had (17.156), and what Odysseus asks Athena to put into him (*Odyssey* 13.387). Zeus had Apollo wash the body of Sarpedon and anoint it with ambrosia before Sleep and Death carried it to Lycia (16.667–683; see [[sarpedon]]); here the gods' food and drink keep a dead man's body from decay. Leaf reports that a few manuscripts add a line after 19.39, which may survive from a time when the Making of the Arms ended here, at the end of Thetis' visit, instead of at the end of Book 18 (on 19.39; see the translation's note on 19.38–39).

**Father and mother.** At the end of the book, when his horse Xanthus has foretold his death, Achilles answers: “I know well myself that it is my fate to perish here, / far from my dear father and mother” (19.421–422; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). She had told him that his life would be short (1.416–418) and that his own fate was ready straight after Hector's (18.95–96).
