summary: When Achilles bids his horses not to leave him dead as they left Patroclus, Hera gives Xanthus a voice. He answers for the pair that they will save him this time, but that his day of destruction is near, and that the fault for Patroclus was not theirs but Apollo's, as his own death will be the work of a god and a man. The Erinyes check his voice, and Achilles answers that he knows his fate (19.404–423).

**The answer.** Achilles has told his horses to bring their charioteer back safe, “and do not leave him there dead, as you left Patroclus” (19.403; see [[achilles]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). One of them answers:

{{quote:07b1c74d-af8d-5cd7-8e3f-023fb33dc0a1}}

He speaks “from under the yoke”, still harnessed, and bows his head until his mane reaches the ground, in the verse that told how the pair stood mourning for Patroclus (19.406 = 17.440, as Walter Leaf notes, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.406). The voice is Hera's gift (see [[hera]]). Aristarchus athetized 19.407 as superfluous and as contradicting 19.418, since the god who gave the voice ought to have taken it away; Leaf answers that the objection tells equally against 418, and owns that it is not easy to see why Hera should have worked the miracle (on 19.407; see the translation's note on 19.407).

{{quote:b8fff83c-078d-5a12-a875-93bb56c46d8c}}

**Not to blame.** Xanthus speaks for the pair: “we will save you”, ἡμεῖς, “we”, ἡμετέρῃ βραδυτῆτι, “our slowness”, and in the dual νῶϊ, “we two” (19.408–415). His defense is the one Agamemnon made to the assembly that morning, “but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness” (19.86–87; see [[agamemnon]], [[ate]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]): οὐδέ τοι ἡμεῖς / αἴτιοι, ἀλλὰ θεός τε μέγας καὶ Μοῖρα κραταιή, “nor are we / to blame, but a great god and mighty Fate” (19.409–410; see [[aitios]], [[moira]] and [[mighty-fate]]). In both speeches Fate shares the blame (19.87, 19.410). ὀλέθριον ἦμαρ, “the day of your destruction” (19.409), occurs in Homer only here and in Briseis' lament, where her three brothers “all met their day of destruction” (19.294; see [[day-of-destruction]] and [[briseis]]).

**Patroclus.** Achilles' reproach is answered. It was not the horses' slowness or sloth that let the Trojans strip Patroclus, but “the best of the gods, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore”, who killed him and gave the glory to Hector (19.411–414; see [[apollo]], [[leto]], [[hector]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The dying Patroclus gave the same account: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus” (16.849–850; see [[euphorbus]]). The horses, who flew “as fast as the winds” in Book 16 (16.149), claim that they could run with the breath of the West Wind, “which they say is the swiftest of all” (19.415–416; see [[zephyrus]]). Aristarchus athetized 19.416–417 as needless, objecting among other things that φασί, “they say”, does not suit a horse; Leaf finds the reasons unconvincing, but observes that the poet here seems not to know that the West Wind is Xanthus' own father (on 19.416; compare 16.150; see the translation's note on 19.416–417).

**A god and a man.** “But for you yourself / it is fated to be brought down by force by a god and a man” (19.416–417; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The dying Hector will name the two, Paris and Phoebus Apollo, who will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[paris]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Leaf points out that the horse tells Achilles nothing that his mother has not told him, except this detail (on 19.407): Thetis had told him that his own fate was ready straight after Hector's (18.96; see [[thetis]]).

**The Erinyes.** “So he spoke, and the Erinyes checked his voice” (19.418; see [[erinyes]]). In this book they are the Erinys who cast blind folly into Agamemnon's mind (19.87) and the Erinyes below the earth who punish men who swear falsely, by whom Agamemnon swears (19.259–260). Here they stop a voice that nature does not give a horse. Leaf takes them as almost an abstraction of the law that orders the world, physical as well as moral, an idea he finds in Heraclitus but too refined for the early epic, and would sooner lose 19.418 than 19.407 (on 19.418; see the translation's note on 19.418).

**Achilles' reply.** “Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him” (19.419; see [[deeply-troubled]]): “Xanthus, why do you prophesy death for me? You have no need to. / I know well myself that it is my fate to perish here” (19.420–421; see [[divination]]). Hector asked the same question of the dying Patroclus, “Patroclus, why do you prophesy sheer destruction for me?” (16.859). Achilles does not dispute the prophecy. He drives the pair out with a cry among the foremost (19.424).
