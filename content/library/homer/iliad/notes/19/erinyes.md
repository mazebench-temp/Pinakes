**The Erinys who walks in darkness.** Agamemnon tells the assembly: “but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind” (19.86–88; see [[agamemnon]], [[ate]], [[moira]], [[who-walks-in-darkness]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). ἠεροφοῖτις Ἐρινύς is the Erinys who heard Meleager's mother calling on Hades and Persephone to give her son death (9.566–572; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). Walter Leaf reports an ancient variant, εἰαροπῶτις, explained as “blood-drinking”, and notes that in the Odyssey too the Erinys brings ἄτη (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.87): the seer Melampus suffered for the daughter of Neleus and for the grievous blind folly that the dread goddess Erinys laid on his mind (*Odyssey* 15.233–234; see the translation's note on 19.87).

In the oath of the truce in Book 3 the powers below are not named: “and you two who below the earth punish / dead men, whoever has sworn a false oath” (3.278–279). Leaf reports that some read οἵ θʼ for αἵ θʼ here, so as to give the punishment to Hades and Persephone, as in Book 3, and observes that the words naturally imply a punishment after death (on 19.258; see the translation's note on 19.258–260). Here the Erinyes stand in the list of witnesses beside Zeus, Earth and the Sun (see [[zeus]], [[earth]] and [[helios]]).

**The horse's voice.** Hera has given Achilles' horse Xanthus speech, and he has told Achilles that he is fated to be brought down by a god and a man (19.407–417; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[hera]]). “So he spoke, and the Erinyes checked his voice” (19.418). Leaf notes that their functions are elsewhere purely moral, and that here they seem to have become almost the law that orders the world, physical as well as moral, a conception he thinks too refined for early epic; he would sooner lose 418 than 407 (on 19.418; see the translation's note on 19.418). Aristarchus athetized 407, on the ground that the goddess who gave the voice ought to have taken it away (see the translation's note on 19.407). Leaf compares the seer Helenus in Virgil, who can tell Aeneas only part of what is to come, for “the Parcae forbid Helenus to know the rest, and Saturnian Juno forbids him to speak” (*Aeneid* 3.379–380).
