**The body.** The book opens on the mourners whom Book 18 left groaning over the body all night (18.354–355). At dawn Thetis finds Achilles lying over Patroclus, weeping, with his companions weeping around him (19.4–6; see [[thetis]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). She tells him to let the dead lie, “since he was brought down once and for all by the will of the gods” (19.9), and when Achilles fears that flies will breed worms in the wounds and the flesh will rot while he fights, she promises to keep it sound even if it lies a year, and drips ambrosia and nectar through the nostrils (19.23–39; see [[patroclus]], [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). In Book 18 Achilles vowed not to bury him until he brought back Hector's armor and head (18.333–335; see [[honoring-the-dead]]); the gods' care keeps the body for the funeral that the vow has put off.

**Vengeance before food.** Odysseus wants the army fed before it fights (19.155–172), and Achilles answers with the dead:

{{quote:5b57308c-7ec9-5fe0-8dbc-ab7c7bf99b32}}

He will not eat while his companion lies dead in his hut, “torn by the sharp bronze, / turned toward the doorway” (19.209–212), and his mind is on “slaughter and blood and the grievous groaning of men” (19.214). The insult to be avenged (λώβη, 19.208; see [[lobe]]) is the slaughter Hector made “when Zeus gave him glory” (19.204). Odysseus answers that the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly: the dead must be buried after a single day's weeping, and the living must eat (19.225–233; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[fasting]]). Achilles keeps his fast when the others have gone to their meal, and Zeus sends Athena to drip nectar and ambrosia into his breast (19.303–308, 19.340–354; see [[athena]]). The death was told in Book 18 as a homecoming denied; in Book 19 it is told at meals: Patroclus used to set Achilles' meal before him in the hut, and now Achilles' heart “goes without drink and food” for longing for him (19.315–321).

**The mourning.** Briseis, brought back with the gifts, finds him dead and leads the women's lament (19.282–302; see [[briseis]], [[captive-women]] and [[lament]]); the elders stay to comfort Achilles, and he laments in his turn (19.303–339; see [[elders]]). Both laments are spoken to the dead man, and both turn from him to the mourner's own griefs: Briseis' husband and brothers and her city (19.291–296), Achilles' father in Phthia and his son in Scyros (19.321–337; see [[peleus]] and [[neoptolemus]]). Before, Achilles had hoped that he alone would die at Troy and that Patroclus would go home (19.328–330); in Book 18 he said that both of them were fated to redden the same earth (18.329–330).

**Told again.** Achilles bids his horses not to leave him dead on the field, “as you left Patroclus” (19.403), and Xanthus, given a voice by Hera, answers for the horses (see [[xanthus-the-horse]]):

{{quote:9ce164c0-ee52-5764-8e5d-82502f000f67}}

The horse gives the death the cause that Patroclus gave it as he died, a god and fate: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus” (16.849–850; see [[apollo]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Verse 19.414 is Thetis' account to Hephaestus (18.456). Then Xanthus tells Achilles that he too is “fated to be brought down by force by a god and a man” (19.417), and Achilles answers that he knows it (19.420–423; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The dying Hector will name them: Paris and Apollo, at the Scaean gates (22.359–360).
