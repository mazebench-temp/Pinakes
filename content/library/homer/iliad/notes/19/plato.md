---
summary: Plato's Agathon quotes the tender feet of Ate as Homer's proof that a god can be tender, and the Republic will not have the young believe that Achilles was so fond of money as to take Agamemnon's gifts. The Republic also rejects a verse, not in our Homer, that makes Zeus the dispenser of good and of evil, and uses the noun of the book's title for Thrasymachus' giving up the argument.
---

- Blind Folly, whose feet are tender because she walks on the heads of men, as Homer's proof of a god's tenderness (*Symposium* 195c–e, on 19.92–93): [[plato-the-tender-feet-of-ate]].
- **The gifts.** Socrates will not praise Phoenix for advising Achilles to take the gifts and defend the Achaeans, but without gifts not to give up his wrath, nor will he believe that Achilles himself was so fond of money as to take gifts from Agamemnon (*Republic* 3.390e). In this book Achilles tells Agamemnon that the gifts are his “to give them if you wish, as is fitting, / or to keep them by you” (19.147–148), and the Myrmidons carry them to his huts (19.276–281): [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]].
- **The dispenser.** Among the things the poets may not say of the gods, Socrates names a verse that is not in our text, that Zeus is “the dispenser for us of good and of evil” (ὡς ταμίας ἡμῖν Ζεὺς ἀγαθῶν τε κακῶν τε τέτυκται, *Republic* 2.379e). The nearest Homeric line is the verse with which Odysseus ends his harvest of battle, “Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men” (19.224 = 4.84): [[plato-broken-oaths]], [[dispenser-of-war]] and [[satiety-of-battle]].
- **The word of the title.** Glaucon would not accept Thrasymachus' ἀπόρρησις, his giving up of the argument (*Republic* 2.357a). The same noun names this book, Μήνιδος ἀπόρρησις, “the Renunciation of Wrath”, and Plato's genitive of μῆνις is μήνιος, where the title has μήνιδος (*Republic* 3.390e): [[title-of-book-19]].
