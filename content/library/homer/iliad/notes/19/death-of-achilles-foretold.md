summary: Book 19 brings the foretelling to Achilles' chariot. In his lament he remembers that he had hoped to die alone at Troy (19.328–330); then his horse Xanthus, given a voice by Hera, tells him that the day of his destruction is near and that he is fated to be brought down “by a god and a man” (19.408–417), and Achilles answers that he knows it well, and will not stop fighting (19.420–423).

**Here in Troy.** Achilles' lament for Patroclus looks back on a plan that took his own death for granted: “For before this the heart in my breast hoped / that I alone would perish far from horse-pasturing Argos, / here in Troy, and that you would go back to Phthia” (19.328–330), to bring his son from Scyros and show him his house (19.331–333; see [[patroclus]], [[neoptolemus]] and [[argos]]). The day before, he had told the Myrmidons that both of them were fated to redden the same earth at Troy (18.329–332; see the note on Book 18).

**Xanthus.** When Achilles has armed in the god's armor and bids his horses not to leave him on the field as they left Patroclus (19.400–403; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), one of them answers:

{{quote:b8fff83c-078d-5a12-a875-93bb56c46d8c}}

ἐγγύθεν ἦμαρ ὀλέθριον, “the day of your destruction is near” (19.409), takes up Thetis' warning that his fate was ready “straight after Hector” (18.96), and the horses can promise to save him only “this time at least” (19.408). The new thing is the manner of his death. Walter Leaf points out that the horse tells him nothing his mother has not, except that he will fall “by a god and a man” (θεῷ τε καὶ ἀνέρι, 19.417; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.407). The god is the one who killed Patroclus, “the best of the gods, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore” (19.413; see [[apollo]]), and the dying Hector will name the man as well: Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy him at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[paris]]). Patroclus too was brought down by a god and men, by “deadly fate and the son of Leto … and of men Euphorbus”, and last by Hector (16.849–850; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The Erinyes stop the horse's voice (19.418; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[erinyes]]).

**I know well myself.**

{{quote:f0dd09fc-5311-56a6-b56d-e6befed14ac3}}

Achilles does not dispute the prophecy. μόρος, “fate” (19.421; see [[moira]]), is to die “here”, ἐνθάδε, “far from my dear father and mother” (19.422): there will be no homecoming, as he has told his mother (18.89–90) and the Myrmidons (18.330–332), and as he chose when Patroclus died (18.98–121; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). Hector asked the dying Patroclus the same question, “why do you prophesy … for me?” (16.859; see [[divination]]), and doubted him; Achilles believes, and drives out with a cry among the foremost (19.424). In the battle that follows he tells Lycaon that death and mighty fate are over him too, and that a dawn or an evening or a midday will come when someone takes his life in war, with a spear or an arrow (21.110–113; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]).
