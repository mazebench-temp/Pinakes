summary: Patroclus lies dead in Achilles' hut through the book. Thetis keeps his body sound with ambrosia and nectar (19.23–39); Achilles will not eat while his companion lies torn in the hut (19.203–214); Briseis, given back, throws herself on the body and laments the man who was always kind to her (19.282–302); Achilles laments the friend who used to set his meals before him and was to have outlived him (19.314–337); and the horse Xanthus tells Achilles that it was Apollo who killed him (19.403–414).

**The body kept.** At dawn Thetis finds her son “lying with his arms around Patroclus, / weeping loudly” (19.4–5), and tells him to let the dead man lie, “since he was brought down once and for all by the will of the gods” (19.9; see [[thetis]] and the translation's note on 19.9). περικείμενον with the dative is the embrace that Briseis will repeat over the same body, ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη (19.284; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.4). Achilles' fear is for the body while he fights:

{{quote:8f356c2e-123e-5177-b29f-a43059e44fb0}}

Μενοιτίου ἄλκιμον υἱόν, “the valiant son of Menoetius”, is the verse-end with which the narrator and Thetis named him in Books 16 and 18 (16.278, 16.827, 18.455; see [[son-of-menoetius]]). ἀεικίσσωσι δὲ νεκρόν, “and defile the body” (19.26), ends the verse in which Glaucus feared for the body of Sarpedon (16.545), and ἀεικίζω is the word for what Achilles will do to Hector's (22.404, 24.22). Thetis promises to keep off “the flies, which feed on men slain in battle”, even if he lies a full year (19.29–33; see [[flies]]), and she drips ambrosia and red nectar through his nostrils, “so that his flesh might be sound” (19.38–39; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). Leaf reports Heyne's suggestion of a memory of Egyptian embalming (on 19.38), in which the brain was drawn out through the nostrils and drugs poured in (Herodotus 2.86). The gods give the same care to Sarpedon, whom Apollo anoints with ambrosia (16.667–680; see [[sarpedon]]), and to Hector, whose body Aphrodite anoints with ambrosial oil of roses and Apollo covers with a cloud and with the golden aegis (23.184–191, 24.18–21; see [[hector]]).

**In the hut.** Achilles refuses food until he has his vengeance: “Before that, nothing would go down my throat, / neither drink nor food, while my companion is dead, / who lies in my hut, torn by the sharp bronze, / turned toward the doorway” (19.209–212; see [[fasting]] and [[hetairos]]). Leaf takes the feet turned to the door as an old custom of laying out the dead, a sign of departure (on 19.212; see the translation's note on 19.212). δεδαϊγμένος ὀξέϊ χαλκῷ, “torn by the sharp bronze”, is how Achilles saw him on the litter (18.236). The words come back when Briseis sees him (19.283) and when she remembers her husband (19.292), and Achilles says δεδαϊγμένος once more in his lament: “But now you lie torn” (19.319).

**Briseis' lament.** Briseis, brought back with Agamemnon's gifts, throws herself on the body: “Patroclus, dearest to my heart, wretched that I am, / I left you alive when I went from the hut” (19.287–288; see [[briseis]] and [[lament]]). When Achilles killed her husband and sacked her city, Patroclus would not let her weep, and promised that Achilles would make her his wedded wife (19.295–299); now she weeps for him “without end, dead, who were always kind” (19.300). μείλιχος, “kind”, is Menelaus' word for him (17.671; see [[meilichos]]). The women groan with her, “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302; see [[captive-women]]).

**Achilles' lament.** The elders beg Achilles to eat, and he refuses until sunset (19.303–308). Then, remembering, he speaks to the dead man:

{{quote:14aaaa0b-e2ef-5fdc-af80-9a260fd451bb}}

The memory is of a meal: in Book 9 Patroclus set out the bread for the envoys while Achilles served the meat (9.216–217; see [[feast]]). His grief, he says, is worse than the news of his father's death or his son's would be (19.321–327; see [[peleus]] and [[neoptolemus]]). He had hoped to die alone at Troy, and that Patroclus would go back to Phthia and bring his son from Scyros to see his house and his possessions (19.328–333; see [[phthia]] and [[scyros]]). Patroclus had grown up in that house, where Menoetius brought him as a boy (23.84–90; see [[menoetius]]). The elders groan with Achilles (19.338–339), and Zeus, pitying them, tells Athena that he sits by the ships “mourning his dear companion” (19.345; see [[zeus]] and [[athena]]).

**Not our slowness.** Mounting his chariot, Achilles bids his horses bring their charioteer back, “and do not leave him there dead, as you left Patroclus” (19.403; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Xanthus answers (see [[xanthus-the-horse]]):

Verse 19.414 is Thetis' account of the death to Hephaestus (18.456), and the god it names is the one Patroclus named as he died, “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me” (16.849; see [[apollo]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The horses had wept for their charioteer in Book 17 (17.426–440).
