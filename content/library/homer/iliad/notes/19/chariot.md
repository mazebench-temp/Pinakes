**The yoking.** ἵππους δʼ Αὐτομέδων τε καὶ Ἄλκιμος ἀμφιέποντες / ζεύγνυον· ἀμφὶ δὲ καλὰ λέπαδνʼ ἕσαν, ἐν δὲ χαλινοὺς / γαμφηλῇς ἔβαλον, κατὰ δʼ ἡνία τεῖναν ὀπίσσω / κολλητὸν ποτὶ δίφρον: “And Automedon and Alcimus, busying themselves with the horses, / yoked them; and they put the fine breast straps on them, and the bits / they set in their jaws, and pulled the reins back / to the well-joined chariot” (19.392–395; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]] and [[well-joined]]). The λέπαδνα, “breast straps”, were last named in the yoking of Hera's chariot, where they are golden (5.730–731); χαλινοί, “bits”, are named only here in Homer. The reins are drawn back from the front rail to the car, as Priam drew them back when he mounted his chariot in Book 3 (κατὰ δʼ ἡνία τεῖνεν ὀπίσσω, 3.261 = 3.311); Walter Leaf explains that they were fastened to the rail while no one was in the car (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 3.261). Then Automedon takes “the shining whip / that fitted his hand” and springs up behind the horses, “and behind him Achilles took his stand, armed” (19.395–397; see [[phaeinos]]): the driver in front, the fighter behind him.

**The charioteer.** Achilles calls on the horses to bring “your charioteer” back safe (ἡνιοχῆα, 19.401), meaning himself, the fighter in the chariot, as Patroclus had been (see the translation's note on 19.401 and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

**Under the yoke.** Xanthus answers “from under the yoke” (ὑπὸ ζυγόφι, 19.404), bowing his head, and his mane, “streaming from under the yoke-cushion down beside the yoke, reached the ground” (19.405–406). The verse is the one in which the two horses wept for Patroclus, their manes stained (17.439–440; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]). ζεύγλη is the cushion under the yoke.
