summary: Book 19 keeps the mourning for Patroclus going. Thetis finds Achilles lying over the body, weeping, with his companions weeping around him (19.4–6); Briseis throws herself on the body, wails and tears her breasts, neck and face, and laments him, and the women groan with her, each for her own sorrows (19.282–302); Achilles laments him before the elders, who groan with him, each remembering what he had left at home (19.314–339). Odysseus, urging the army to eat, says that the dead must be buried after a single day's weeping (19.225–229).

**Over the body.** The book opens on the mourning that Book 18 left over the body all night (18.354–355). Thetis finds her son lying over Patroclus, “weeping loudly; and many of his companions around him / were weeping” (19.5–6; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). When Achilles refuses food, the body lies in his hut “turned toward the doorway, and around him his companions / weep” (19.212–213); Walter Leaf takes the feet turned to the door as an old custom of laying out the dead (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.212). Odysseus answers him that “the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly”, and that “we must bury the man who dies, / having a pitiless heart, after weeping for him a single day” (19.225–229; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]); Leaf hears in γαστέρι, “with the belly”, a mockery of fasting as a way to mourn in wartime (on 19.225; see the translation's note on 19.225–229). Achilles keeps his fast all the same, and mourns while the others eat (19.303–313, 19.345; see [[fasting]]).

**Briseis.**

{{quote:9cf1d6dc-c4a5-57b3-bb09-8f686bae9a74}}

The gestures are a widow's. λίγʼ ἐκώκυε, “wailed shrilly”, is the cry of Thetis at the news (κώκυσεν, 18.37) and of Hecuba when she sees Hector dragged (22.407), and the half-line ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη λίγʼ ἐκώκυε returns, in the present tense, in the Odyssey's simile of the wife who throws herself on her husband fallen before his city, as the enemy drive her off into slavery (*Odyssey* 8.523–530; see [[captive-women]]). Leaf says that tearing the skin is not found elsewhere as a sign of grief, heroic mourners going no further than tearing their hair, and wonders whether a foreign custom is meant (on 19.282); but the widows of 2.700 and 11.393 have cheeks “torn in mourning” (see [[torn-in-mourning]]). Her lament speaks to the dead man and tells her own life: her husband and brothers killed, her city sacked, and Patroclus' kindness (19.287–300; see [[briseis]]). Leaf finds in it several expressions foreign to epic (on 19.282; see the translation's note on 19.282–300).

**The answer.**

{{quote:231e7d98-7b7e-5095-9165-60d08afb65f0}}

ἐπὶ δὲ στενάχοντο γυναῖκες, “and the women groaned with her”, closes the women's laments for Hector (22.515, 24.746) and follows the singers' dirge over his bier (24.722); with other mourners it closes the laments of Achilles and of Priam (19.338, 22.429). Leaf, with Heyne, takes πρόφασιν not as a pretense but as the occasion of a grief truly felt, which wakes the women's own (on 19.302; see the translation's note on 19.302).

**Achilles.** “And remembering, he heaved a heavy sigh and spoke” (19.314): like Briseis he speaks to the dead man, of the meals Patroclus used to set before him and of the son he was to have brought home (19.315–333). δυσάμμορε, “most ill-fated one” (19.315), returns in the laments for Hector (22.428, 22.485, 24.727). Like her, too, he turns from the dead to his own: his father in Phthia, his son in Scyros (19.321–337; see [[peleus]] and [[neoptolemus]]). “So he spoke, weeping, and the elders groaned with him, / each remembering what he had left in his halls” (19.338–339; see [[elders]]), as the women groaned for Patroclus and each for her own sorrows; Leaf sets the two passages side by side (on 19.302). Zeus sees them weeping and pities them (19.340; see [[zeus]] and [[pity]]).
