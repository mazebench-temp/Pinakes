---
summary: The food of the gods keeps the dead and the fasting. Thetis drips ambrosia and red nectar into the nostrils of Patroclus, so that his flesh may stay sound; Zeus sends Athena to drip nectar and lovely ambrosia into the breast of Achilles, who will not eat, so that hunger may not come upon him.
---

**For the dead.** When Thetis has brought the armor and promised to keep the flies from Patroclus, she puts might into her son, “and into Patroclus in turn ambrosia and red nectar / she dripped through his nostrils, so that his flesh might be sound” (19.38–39; see [[thetis]], [[patroclus]] and [[nectar]]). Apollo, at Zeus' bidding, washed the body of Sarpedon and anointed it with ambrosia (χρῖσόν τʼ ἀμβροσίῃ, 16.670, 16.680; see the note on Book 16), and in Book 23 Aphrodite will anoint Hector's body with “ambrosial” oil of roses, so that Achilles may not tear it as he drags it (23.185–187). Sarpedon's ambrosia and Hector's ambrosial oil are put on the skin; Patroclus' ambrosia goes in through the nostrils. Walter Leaf reports that Heyne saw in this a reminiscence of Egyptian embalming (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.38): Herodotus tells how the embalmers draw the brain out through the nostrils with a hooked iron and pour in drugs (Herodotus 2.86.3). In the Odyssey the sea goddess Eidothea puts ambrosia under the nose of each of Menelaus' men, to drive off the stench of the seals (*Odyssey* 4.445–446).

**For the living.** Achilles will not eat until he has avenged Patroclus, and Zeus tells Athena: “But go, drip nectar and lovely ambrosia / into his breast, so that hunger may not come upon him” (19.347–348; see [[zeus]], [[athena]] and [[fasting]]). She does so, “so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees” (19.352–354; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]]). ἀμβροσίη ἐρατεινή, “lovely ambrosia”, is found only in these two verses (19.347, 19.353; see [[lovely]]). Ambrosia is the gods' food: Calypso sets it before Hermes with red nectar, and her maids set it before her while Odysseus eats the food of mortal men (*Odyssey* 5.92–94, 5.196–199). No other living man in Homer is fed with it.
