summary: Briseis is given back. Achilles, renouncing his wrath, wishes that Artemis had killed her on the day he took her (19.56–62); she is led out of Agamemnon's hut as the eighth of the women among his gifts, and he swears over a boar that he never touched her (19.243–246, 19.258–265). In Achilles' hut she finds Patroclus dead, and in her only speech in the poem she laments him, the husband and brothers she lost and the city Achilles sacked, and the marriage Patroclus promised her (19.282–302).

**For the sake of a girl.** Achilles opens the assembly with a question to Agamemnon: was it better for them both “that we two, grieving at heart, / raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl?” (19.57–58; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]] and [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). εἵνεκα κούρης, “for the sake of a girl”, is how the quarrel has been named from the first: by Achilles, who would not fight “for the girl's sake” (1.298), by Agamemnon (2.377), and by Ajax, “for the sake of a girl, / one girl” (9.637–638). Then he wishes her dead:

{{quote:69f565ae-5c56-591c-b8c8-97bd92d55920}}

In Book 9 he said that he loved her “from my heart, though she was won by my spear” (9.343); now he sets her against the Achaeans who died while he kept apart (see [[achilles]] and [[menis]]). Artemis' arrows are the sudden death of women in the Iliad: she killed Bellerophon's daughter and Andromache's mother (6.205, 6.428), and Hera reminds her that Zeus made her a lion to women and let her kill whichever she will (21.483–484; see [[artemis]]). The day he took her is the day he sacked Lyrnessus (see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]).

**Among the gifts.** Agamemnon dates his blind folly from “the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (19.89; see [[ate]] and [[taking-of-briseis]]). Odysseus asks that he swear the oath promised in Book 9, “that he never went up into her bed or lay with her” (19.175–177; 9.132–134), and Odysseus and the young men bring the women out of Agamemnon's hut with the tripods, the cauldrons and the horses: “and quickly they led out women skilled in flawless handiwork, / seven, and the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis” (19.245–246; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[fair-cheeked]]). She is counted with the gifts, as in Book 9 she was to come with them (9.131–132). Over the boar Agamemnon swears more than he promised: “that I never laid a hand on the girl Briseis, / either wanting her for my bed or for anything else; / but she remained untouched in my huts” (19.261–263; see [[oath]]). In Book 9 Achilles had told the envoys that Agamemnon keeps “the wife dear to my heart; let him lie beside her / and take his pleasure” (9.336–337). Achilles' answer to the oath is a verdict on Zeus: the son of Atreus would never have stirred his heart, “nor would he have led the girl / away against my will”, had Zeus not wanted death for many of the Achaeans (19.270–274). The Myrmidons carry the gifts to his ship, “And they set them in the huts, and seated the women” (19.280; see [[captive-women]] and [[myrmidons]]).

**Over Patroclus.**

{{quote:9cf1d6dc-c4a5-57b3-bb09-8f686bae9a74}}

The narrator gives her two likenesses as she mourns. ἰκέλη χρυσέῃ Ἀφροδίτῃ, “like golden Aphrodite”, is said again of Cassandra, the first to see her father bringing Hector's body home (24.697–700), and in the Odyssey of Penelope, “like Artemis or golden Aphrodite” (*Odyssey* 17.37, 19.54; see [[aphrodite]] and [[golden]]). γυνὴ ἐϊκυῖα θεῇσι, “the woman, like the goddesses”, was said of Hecamede, Nestor's prize from Tenedos, “when Achilles sacked it” (11.624–627, 11.638; see [[like-the-goddesses]]). Her gestures are those of a widow over her husband. She throws herself around the body and wails shrilly, ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη λίγʼ ἐκώκυε, as in the Odyssey's simile a wife throws herself around the husband who has fallen before his city, ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη λίγα κωκύει, until the enemy drive her off with their spears into slavery (*Odyssey* 8.523–530). And she tears her breasts, her neck and her face (19.285; see [[lament]] and [[torn-in-mourning]]).

**Her lament.**

{{quote:f3c08a92-f504-51a1-816c-d4d834ad1e38}}

She left him alive “when I went from the hut” (19.288), on the day Patroclus led her out and gave her to the heralds (1.345–348), and she comes back to find him dead. Walter Leaf counts among the speech's non-epic features the hiatus of σε ἔλειπον (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.282 and 19.288; see the translation's note on 19.282–300). Then she tells her life, evil after evil (see [[evil-follows-evil]]):

{{quote:d8df0b2f-5179-5b23-ba8a-e24546a6d415}}

Her husband fell “before the city, torn by the sharp bronze” (19.292), the words in which she has just seen Patroclus (19.283). Her three brothers, “whom one mother bore with me”, are named in Helen's words for Castor and Polydeuces (3.238; see [[kasignetos]]), and their fate recalls that of Andromache's seven brothers, whom Achilles killed on a single day (6.421–423; see [[andromache]]). The poem does not name her husband. The city was “the city of divine Mynes” (19.296), and Mynes, killed by Achilles at Lyrnessus (2.692), is often taken for him (see [[mynes]]).

{{quote:fe876828-b44c-5827-a11e-f054785c002a}}

Patroclus would not let her weep, and promised her a marriage: to make her the κουριδίη ἄλοχος, the “wedded wife”, of the man who had killed her husband, and to give the marriage feast among the Myrmidons in Phthia (19.297–299; see [[marriage]] and [[phthia]]). Achilles had called her his ἄλοχος, “wife”, in Book 9 (9.336). Leaf infers from the obeli in two manuscripts that Aristarchus athetized 19.295–300, no doubt because a marriage between Achilles and a captive is foreign to the Iliad, and notes that κουριδίη ἄλοχος is used elsewhere of a wife married as a maiden; he keeps the lines all the same (on 19.298; see [[aristarchus]] and the translation's note on 19.295–300). Her last words are for his kindness: μείλιχον αἰεί, “who were always kind” (19.300), as Menelaus said of him, “for he knew how to be kind to all / while he lived” (17.671–672; see [[patroclus]] and [[meilichos]]).

The women groan with her, “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302; see [[captive-women]]). She does not speak again in the Iliad. In the last book she lies once more beside Achilles in his hut (24.675–676).
