**The gold of the gifts.** χρυσοῦ δὲ στήσας Ὀδυσεὺς δέκα πάντα τάλαντα / ἦρχʼ, “And Odysseus weighed out ten talents of gold in all / and led the way” (19.247–248). The ten talents are those Agamemnon promised in Book 9 (9.122; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]), and the verse returns, with ἔφερεν, “carried”, when Priam takes out ten talents of gold for Hector's ransom (24.232). Walter Leaf takes στήσας as “weighing”, which shows the talent to have been a known weight of gold; but he thinks that the talents were more probably bars that needed only to be counted, and that the stress on the weighing implies a large sum, as ten talents were not, perhaps from a later time when the talent had grown large (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.247).

**Golden Aphrodite.** When Briseis comes back to Achilles' huts and sees Patroclus dead, she is “like golden Aphrodite” (ἰκέλη χρυσέῃ Ἀφροδίτῃ, 19.282; see [[briseis]], [[aphrodite]] and [[golden]]).

**Silver and gold on the armor.** The greaves are fitted “with silver ankle-pieces” and the sword is “silver-studded”, in the verses of every arming scene (19.370, 19.372; see [[arming-scene]] and [[silver-studded]]). The plumes of the helmet are gold, “which Hephaestus had set thick about the crest” (19.382–383; see [[helmet]]), the golden crest he made in the night (18.612). Thetis, who brings the armor, is “silver-footed” (19.28; see [[silver-footed]]).
