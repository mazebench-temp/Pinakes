---
summary: The Myrmidons carry the gifts “to the ship of divine Achilles,” and Briseis, lamenting Patroclus, names “divine Mynes,” whose city Achilles sacked, and “divine Achilles,” whose wife Patroclus promised she would be.
---

**The gifts.** “and the great-hearted Myrmidons busied themselves with the gifts, / and went carrying them to the ship of divine Achilles” (19.278–279; [[myrmidons]], [[great-hearted]], [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The verse-end Ἀχιλλῆος θείοιο occurs only in this book, here and in Briseis' lament (19.297); it is the counterpart of the common Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο (2.335, 9.218, 11.806).

**Briseis' lament.** The epithet frames her two husbands, the one Achilles killed and the one Patroclus promised: “Nor would you let me, when swift Achilles killed my husband / and sacked the city of divine Mynes, / weep; but you said that of divine Achilles / you would make me the wedded wife” (19.295–298; [[mynes]], [[briseis]], [[marriage]]). Walter Leaf notes that later tradition made Mynes Briseis' husband, though nothing in Homer shows him more than her king (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.296).
