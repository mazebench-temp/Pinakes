---
summary: Odysseus tells Agamemnon that it is no cause for blame that a king should make amends to a man, when he was the first to grow angry (19.182–183). After the assembly the kings are the elders who beg Achilles to eat; he sends them away, and six stay to comfort him (19.303–312).
refs: [19.256, 19.303]
---

**A king's amends.** “For it is no cause for blame that a king / should make amends to a man, when he was the first to grow angry” (19.182–183; see [[no-blame-for-a-king-to-make-amends]], [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and [[nemesis]]). The king is Agamemnon. In Book 9 he demanded that Achilles submit to him, “inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.160); now he is told that a king may make amends without shame (see [[agamemnon]] and [[basileus]]). Walter Leaf discusses how the couplet is to be construed (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.182; see the translation's note on 19.182–183). At the oath Agamemnon is simply “the king”, to whom the Argives listen in silence (βασιλῆος, 19.256).

**Sent away.** The elders of the Achaeans gather round Achilles and beg him to eat, and he refuses: “So saying, he dismissed the other kings, / but the two sons of Atreus stayed, and brilliant Odysseus, / Nestor and Idomeneus and the old horse-driving Phoenix” (19.309–311; see [[elders]] and [[achilles]]). The elders of 19.303 are these kings, as in the councils of Books 2 and 9 (2.404–409, 9.89–90). Phoenix, who stays, is not a king of a contingent but a leader of the Myrmidons (16.196) and Achilles' foster father (see [[phoenix]]).
