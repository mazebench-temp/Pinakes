The last verses of the arming of Achilles are the charioteer's. Automedon yoked the same horses for Patroclus in Book 16, the man Patroclus “honored most after Achilles, breaker of men” (16.145–154; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). In Book 17 he gave the whip and the reins to Alcimedon so that he could fight on foot (17.479–483); now he and Alcimus yoke the pair together, and he takes the whip again (see [[alcimedon]] and [[chariot]]). The breast straps (λέπαδνα), the bits in the jaws and the reins pulled back to the chariot are the harness of a chariot team, and κατὰ δʼ ἡνία τεῖναν ὀπίσσω, “and pulled the reins back”, is the half-verse of Priam taking the reins in Book 3, κατὰ δʼ ἡνία τεῖνεν ὀπίσσω (3.261 = 3.311; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.394). The name is held back to the start of its verse: “And he, taking the shining whip / that fitted his hand, sprang up behind the horses, / Automedon” (19.395–397).

Behind him stands the fighter, “Achilles took his stand, armed, / gleaming in his armor like shining Hyperion” (19.397–398; see [[achilles]] and [[like-the-sun]]). When Achilles bids the horses bring “your charioteer” back safe (19.401), he means himself, the fighter in the chariot, as Patroclus had been, and not Automedon (see the translation's note on 19.401).
