summary: In Agamemnon's apology Zeus is both the power that blinded him, with Fate and the Erinys, and the first victim of Blind Folly, his own eldest daughter. On the day Heracles was to be born Hera tricked him into a rash oath, and he hurled Blind Folly from heaven and groaned ever after over his son's labors. The oath of reconciliation is sworn by Zeus over a boar, and Achilles answers that Zeus gives men great blind follies. When Achilles will not eat, Zeus pities him and sends Athena with nectar and ambrosia.

**Not I, but Zeus.** Agamemnon's apology to the assembly begins with the gods: “but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind / on the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (19.86–89; see [[agamemnon]], [[aitios]], [[moira]], [[erinyes]], [[ate]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). “But what could I do? It is a god who brings all things to fulfillment” (19.90; see [[a-god-brings-all-things-to-fulfillment]] and [[theos]]). The plea that Achilles sent to Zeus through Thetis in Book 1 was that Agamemnon might “learn / his own blind folly” (1.411–412; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). In Book 9 Achilles said of him, “Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (9.377), and now Agamemnon says it of himself: “But since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits” (19.137), the phrase the narrator used of Glaucus when he gave gold armor for bronze (6.234; see [[phrenes]]).

**The daughter of Zeus.** “Blind Folly is the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds all” (πρέσβα Διὸς θυγάτηρ Ἄτη, 19.91; see [[ate]] and [[daughter-of-zeus]]). In Phoenix's allegory the Prayers too are “the daughters of great Zeus”, and it is to Zeus that they pray that Blind Folly may follow the man who refuses them (9.502, 9.510–512; see [[litai]]).

**The best of men and gods.** Agamemnon's proof that no one is safe from her is Zeus himself: “For once even Zeus was blinded, who, they say, is the best / of men and gods; but even him / Hera, female though she was, deceived with her treachery / on the day when mighty Heracles / was to be born to Alcmene in well-crowned Thebes” (19.95–99; see [[hera]], [[heracles]], [[alcmene]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). Ζεὺς ἄσατο, “Zeus was blinded”, is Aristarchus' reading; the manuscripts' Ζῆνʼ would make Blind Folly blind Zeus (see the translation's note on 19.95–133). If Zeus could be blinded, so could a king: “So I too … could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded” (19.134–136; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Walter Leaf thinks the episode was worked in from an independent poem on Heracles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.95).

**The boast and the oath.** Zeus speaks to the gods in the verses with which he opened the assembly of Book 8 (19.101–102 ≈ 8.5–6; see [[hear-me]] and [[olympians]]):

{{quote:74a769a4-d9eb-5989-894b-8d5cf4881605}}

“And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed him” (19.106) is the verse with which she answers him in the Deception of Zeus (14.300, 14.329; see [[with-treachery-in-her-heart]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). “‘You will prove false, and hereafter will not put fulfillment to your word. / But come now, Olympian, swear me a mighty oath” (19.107–108; see [[olympian]]). When Zeus nodded to Thetis in Book 1, he called his nod the greatest sign among the immortals, “for no word of mine can be taken back, or prove false, / or go unfulfilled, once I nod my head to it” (1.526–527). Hera tells him that his word will prove false, and asks for an oath. “So she spoke; and Zeus did not perceive her treachery at all, / but swore a great oath, and in this was greatly blinded” (19.112–113; see [[oath]]). Nowhere else in Homer does Zeus swear an oath. Leaf finds Hera's guile not in a change of words but in Zeus' rashness in swearing an unconditional oath, limited to a single day (on 19.111; see the translation's note on 19.110–111).

**The trick.** Hera leaves Olympus in the verse with which she set out in Book 14 to deceive him (19.114 = 14.225), brings Sthenelus' son to birth in the seventh month and holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene (19.115–119; see [[eileithyia]]). Then she tells him: “now a brave man is born who will be lord of the Argives, / Eurystheus, child of Sthenelus the son of Perseus, / of your race” (19.122–124; see [[eurystheus]], [[sthenelus-son-of-perseus]] and [[perseus]]). The oath gives the rule to his great-grandson, for Perseus was his son by Danae (14.319–320; Leaf on 19.105). She calls him “Father Zeus of the gleaming thunderbolt” (Ζεῦ πάτερ ἀργικέραυνε, 19.121), a title that the Iliad gives him only here and twice later (20.16, 22.178).

**Blind Folly hurled from heaven.**

{{quote:634eb8bf-d3e9-5a97-bd4a-b046a355d797}}

He seizes her “by her head of shining tresses” (κεφαλῆς λιπαροπλοκάμοιο, only here), and binds himself with a second oath, that she will never come back to Olympus and the starry sky (19.127–129; see [[olympus]] and [[starry]]). ῥίπτω, “hurl”, is his verb: he seized Hephaestus by the foot and hurled him from the threshold of heaven (1.591; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]), he threatened to hurl any god who disobeyed him into misty Tartarus (8.13), and when he hung Hera from the sky in his anger over Heracles, he hurled from the threshold any god who tried to set her free, until he reached the earth with little strength left (15.18–24; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]); when he woke from the sleep that Hera brought on him, he went flinging the gods about the house, and only Night saved Sleep (14.256–261). Banished from heaven, Blind Folly “came to the works of men” (19.131), and walks over their heads (19.92–94).

**His son's labors.** “Over her he always groaned whenever he saw his dear son / doing unseemly work under the labors that Eurystheus set him” (19.132–133; see [[heracles]]). In Book 15 Zeus told Hera that “the ceaseless pain for divine Heracles” never let his heart go (15.24–25; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]); in Book 8 Athena complained that he did not remember how often she had saved his son “when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him” (8.362–363); and in Book 18 Achilles named Heracles as “the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus”, yet brought down by fate and the grievous anger of Hera (18.117–119).

**The witness of the oath.** Agamemnon bids Talthybius “make ready a boar for me, to sacrifice to Zeus and the Sun” (19.197; see [[talthybius]], [[boar]] and [[helios]]); for the truce of Book 3 the Achaeans brought a third lamb for Zeus (3.103–104). Over the boar he lifts his hands to Zeus and prays, “looking up into the wide sky” (19.254–257; see [[prayer]] and [[sacrifice]]), and begins: “Now let Zeus be witness first, highest and best of the gods” (19.258; see [[oath-formulas]]). The verse is Odysseus' when he swears to Penelope that her husband will come home (*Odyssey* 19.303), and Achilles swears by Zeus with the same title in Book 23 (23.43). Hector, in Book 10, called Zeus himself to witness, and swore an oath that would prove false (10.329–332; see [[dolon]]).

**Zeus in the counsels.** Achilles complains that the dead lie torn, whom Hector brought down “when Zeus gave him glory” (19.203–204; 19.204 = 8.216, 11.300; see [[kydos]]). Odysseus answers that men soon have their fill of a battle in which the harvest is smallest, “when he tips the scales, / Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men” (19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]], [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[dispenser-of-war]]). The last verse is the soldiers' in Book 4, wondering whether Zeus will bring war again or friendship (4.84). The scales are those he held out at midday on the second day of battle (8.69–72), and the figure of Hector's flight in Book 16, “for he knew the sacred scales of Zeus” (16.658).

**Achilles' answer.** When the boar has been thrown into the sea, Achilles takes up Agamemnon's account: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men” (19.270; see [[father-zeus]]). The son of Atreus would never have taken the girl against his will, “but Zeus, it seems, / wanted death to come upon many of the Achaeans” (19.273–274; see [[achilles]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). So the proem had put it: the wrath sent many souls to Hades, “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.3–5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Pity and nectar.** While the elders weep with Achilles, “the son of Cronus saw them weeping and pitied them” (19.340; see [[pity]] and [[son-of-cronus]]), in the verse in which he pitied the weeping horses of Achilles (17.441), as he pitied Hector (15.12) and Sarpedon and Patroclus (16.431). He turns to Athena:

{{quote:722e7bbe-f2eb-51d0-9a5b-7dfdd1963e86}}

ἀνδρὸς ἑῆος, “your brave man”, takes ἑῆος in Aristarchus' sense (see the translation's note on 19.342). “So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already” (19.349) is the verse with which he sent her down to break the truce in Book 4 (4.73), and will send her to Achilles' side against Hector (22.186); in the Odyssey it sends her to make peace in Ithaca (*Odyssey* 24.487; see [[athena]]). She drips the nectar and ambrosia into Achilles' breast, so that hunger may not come upon his knees, and goes back to “the well-built house of her mighty father” (19.352–355; see [[eristhenes]], [[nectar]], [[ambrosia]] and [[fasting]]).

**The snow of Zeus.** The Achaeans pour out from the ships as “snowflakes fly thick and fast from Zeus” (19.357; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]). Διός, “from Zeus”, keeps the old sense of the sky, as Leaf notes, comparing “the rays of Zeus” (on 19.357; 13.837). In the snow simile of Book 12 it is Zeus himself who sets out to snow (12.278–280; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]).
