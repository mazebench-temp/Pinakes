summary: In Agamemnon's story Hera deceives Zeus on the day Heracles is to be born. She makes him swear that the man of his blood who is born that day will rule all who dwell around him, brings Eurystheus to birth in the seventh month, holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene, and tells Zeus what he has sworn to. At the end of the book she gives Achilles' horse Xanthus a human voice.

**Female though she was.** “But even him / Hera, female though she was, deceived with her treachery / on the day when mighty Heracles / was to be born to Alcmene in well-crowned Thebes” (19.96–99; see [[zeus]], [[heracles]], [[alcmene]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). θῆλυς ἐοῦσα, “though a mere female” as Walter Leaf puts it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.97), returns only once in Homer, when Antilochus tells his horses not to let Aethe, Agamemnon's mare in Menelaus' team, “female though she is”, put them to shame (23.295, 23.408–409). δολοφροσύνη, “treachery”, is found only in this story (19.97, 19.112).

**The oath.** Zeus has boasted before all the gods that on this day a man of his blood will be born who will be lord over all who dwell around him (19.100–105). “And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed him” (19.106), in the verse with which she answers him on Ida in Book 14 (14.300, 14.329; see [[with-treachery-in-her-heart]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]):

{{quote:290a81d0-57bc-5592-8f65-0eb0dfd2b312}}

Zeus swears, “and in this was greatly blinded” (19.113; see [[ate]] and [[oath]]). Leaf finds her guile not in a change of words but in Zeus' rashness in swearing an unconditional oath, limited to a single day, forgetting that Hera's functions gave her some control in these matters (on 19.111). In Book 14 it was Sleep who made Hera swear, by the water of Styx and with her hands on the earth and the sea (14.270–280), and in Book 15 she swore to Zeus by Earth, Heaven and Styx that Poseidon did not fight at her bidding (15.36–46); here she binds Zeus himself.

**The trick.** “And Hera darted down and left the peak of Olympus” (19.114) is the verse with which she set out from Olympus in Book 14 to deceive him (14.225; see [[olympus]]). She comes “to Achaean Argos” (19.115; see [[argos]]), one of the three cities that she calls by far the dearest to her (4.51–52). There she brings the son of Sthenelus' wife “out into the light, though he was short of his months, / and she stopped Alcmene's childbirth, and held back the Eileithyiai” (19.118–119; see [[eurystheus]], [[sthenelus-son-of-perseus]] and [[eileithyia]]). The goddesses of childbirth are “the daughters of Hera” in a simile of Book 11 (11.270–271). Leaf compares the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (on 19.119), where Hera in her jealousy keeps Eileithyia on Olympus, under golden clouds, while Leto is in labor on Delos, until the goddesses send Iris to fetch her (*Hymn to Apollo* 91–115; see [[leto]]). Then Hera tells Zeus the news, “Father Zeus of the gleaming thunderbolt, I will put a word in your mind: / now a brave man is born who will be lord of the Argives” (19.121–122), and Zeus, in his grief, hurls Blind Folly from heaven (19.125–131).

**Her anger against Heracles.** The poem has told of her enmity before: Achilles said that fate and “the grievous anger of Hera” brought Heracles down (18.119), and Sleep and Zeus remembered the storm she raised against him on his voyage home from Troy, for which Zeus hung her from the sky (14.249–256, 15.18–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]] and [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). Agamemnon's story carries her anger back to the day of his birth.

**Xanthus' voice.** When Achilles bids his horses bring their charioteer back safe, “the white-armed goddess Hera gave him speech” (19.407; see [[xanthus-the-horse]], [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[white-armed]]). Aristarchus athetized the line as superfluous and as contradicting 418, where the Erinyes stop the voice (see the translation's note on 19.407 and [[erinyes]]). Leaf finds it hard to see why Hera should have worked the miracle, since the horse tells Achilles no more than his mother has told him, except that a god and a man will bring him down (on 19.407). Hera is the Achaeans' friend, who sent Iris to rouse Achilles in Book 18 (18.165–168; see [[iris]]); in Book 20 she will tell the gods that they have come down from Olympus so that Achilles may suffer nothing from the Trojans that day, though later he must suffer whatever his portion spun for him at his birth (20.125–128). The horse says the same: “Yes, we will save you still, this time at least, mighty Achilles; / but the day of your destruction is near” (19.408–409; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).
