summary: When the assembly breaks up, the elders of the Achaeans gather round Achilles and beg him to eat; he refuses, and keeps six of them with him. When he laments Patroclus, the elders groan with him, each remembering what he left at home (19.303–312, 19.338–339).
refs: [19.303, 19.338]
---

**Begging.** “And around him the elders of the Achaeans gathered, / begging him to take his meal; but he refused, groaning” (19.303–304; see [[achilles]] and [[fasting]]). λισσόμενοι, “begging”, is the word of the embassy: Thetis remembers it as “the elders begged him, / the elders of the Argives” (18.448–449), in the words that bring the Aetolian elders to Meleager in Phoenix's story (9.574; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Then, in Thetis' telling, they begged him to fight; now, with his wrath renounced, they beg him to eat, and he will not, until the sun goes down (19.305–308; see [[supplication]]). He dismisses “the other kings”, and the two sons of Atreus, Odysseus, Nestor, Idomeneus and “the old horse-driving Phoenix” stay (19.309–311; see [[kings]] and [[horse-driving]]). The elders and the kings are the same men, as at the sacrifice of Book 2, where Agamemnon called “the elders, the best of all the Achaeans” (2.404–407).

**Groaning.** Achilles laments Patroclus: no grief could be worse, not even the death of his father or of his son (19.315–337). “So he spoke, weeping, and the elders groaned with him, / each remembering what he had left in his halls” (19.338–339; see [[lament]] and [[peleus]]). ἐπὶ … στενάχοντο, “groaned with him”, is said in the same words of the women who groan with Briseis, “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302; see the translation's notes on 19.302 and 19.339, and [[captive-women]]). The old men grieve for what each of them has left at home. Zeus sees them weeping and pities them (19.340; see [[pity]] and [[zeus]]).
