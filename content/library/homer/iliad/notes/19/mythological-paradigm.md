---
summary: Agamemnon's apology rests on a paradigm: Blind Folly deceived even Zeus, on the day Heracles was to be born, when Hera tricked him into an oath that made Eurystheus lord of the Argives (19.95–133). The story is told not to move another man but to excuse the speaker: “So I too … could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded” (19.134–136).
passages:
  - 19.95-136 | Zeus blinded, and Agamemnon
---

The usual paradigm persuades. Phoenix told the wrath of Meleager to bring Achilles back to the war (9.524–605), and in the last book Achilles will tell Priam of Niobe to persuade him to eat (24.602–617). Agamemnon's turns the argument on himself, and Walter Leaf, who thinks the episode worked in from an independent poem on Heracles, remarks that “many speakers with a bad case take refuge in telling stories” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.95; see the translation's note on 19.95–133). The two books that end the quarrel both take their example from Heracles. Achilles, in Book 18, chose him as the proof that no favor of Zeus saves a man from death, “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom” (18.117); Agamemnon chooses his birth as the proof that no one is safe from blind folly (see [[heracles]], [[ate]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).
