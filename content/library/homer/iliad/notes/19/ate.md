summary: Agamemnon's apology makes blind folly a person. He is not to blame, he says, but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys, who cast savage ἄτη into his mind; Blind Folly, the eldest daughter of Zeus, walks over the heads of men, and once blinded Zeus himself, who hurled her from heaven to the works of men. When the oath has been sworn, Achilles accepts the account in his own words: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men” (19.270).

Book 19 uses the word and its verb more than any other book, and gives blind folly a body, a parent and a story.

**Not I to blame.** Achilles has renounced his wrath, and Agamemnon answers “from where he sat, and not standing up in their midst” (19.77; see [[agamemnon]]):

{{quote:36d7f3f6-4e1d-575b-905a-bed57d0c47e8}}

Three powers share the blame: Zeus, Μοῖρα, “Fate”, and the Erinys ἠεροφοῖτις, “who walks in darkness”, the Erinys who heard Althaea's curse in Phoenix's story (9.571; see [[zeus]], [[moira]] and [[erinyes]]). In the Odyssey too an Erinys sets ἄτη upon a man's mind, that of the seer Melampus (*Odyssey* 15.233–234), as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.87). The blind folly is ἄγριος, “savage”, and it was cast “into my mind” (φρεσίν, 19.88; see [[phrenes]]) at a time and place that every listener knows: “in the assembly … on the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (19.88–89; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]] and [[geras]]). γέρας αὐτὸς ἀπηύρων, “I myself took away the prize”, answers the charge that Achilles, Thetis and Thersites made against him in Books 1 and 2, γέρας αὐτὸς ἀπούρας, “having seized it himself” (1.356, 1.507, 2.240); Leaf takes αὐτός as “on my own authority”, as there (on 19.89). Agamemnon owns the deed and lays the blindness on the gods. “But what could I do? It is a god who brings all things to fulfillment” (19.90; see [[a-god-brings-all-things-to-fulfillment]]): θεός is divine power, not Ate herself (Leaf on 19.90).

**Book 9 and Book 19.** Before the elders in Book 9 he named no god: “I was blinded; I do not deny it myself” (9.116), and blinded “obeying my wretched mind” (9.119). Now the god takes the place of the mind. The verse comes back changed, “But since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits” (19.137), the charge that Achilles made against him in Book 9, “for Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (9.377), and the next verse is the same in both books: “I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (19.138 = 9.120; see [[apoina]] and [[boundless-ransom]]). Leaf finds much to suspect in 137–141, among it the repetition ἀάσθην … ἀασάμην, and thinks the lines may have been added to bring Book 19 into line with Book 9 (on 19.136; see the translation's note on 19.136–141). Either way the plea ends in payment.

**Blind Folly in person.**

{{quote:f96a7d91-2176-58f7-9e4e-0b5adcb6fbdc}}

πρέσβα Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “the eldest daughter of Zeus” (see [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[august-goddess]]), is the only parent Homer gives her; Hesiod makes her a daughter of Strife, born with Lawlessness (*Theogony* 230; see the article above). Phoenix's Blind Folly was “strong and sound of foot”, outrunning the lame Prayers over all the earth (9.505–507; see [[litai]]). Agamemnon's has tender feet and does not touch the ground. She walks over the heads of men, which expresses, to Leaf, “the mysterious and silent infliction from above” (on 19.92). Both are “doing harm to men”, the same half-verse, βλάπτουσʼ ἀνθρώπους (19.94, 9.507). “She fetters one or the other” (κατὰ … πέδησε) uses the verb of fate, as when “fate fettered Diores” (4.517); Leaf takes ἕτερον as “either party” to a quarrel, and keeps the verse, which the scholia report as athetized (on 19.94; see the translation's note on 19.91–94). He counts this personification, with Phoenix's, among the latest parts of the poems, and notes that Plato quotes 92–93 as Homer's with no sense that they were by another hand (on 19.91): Agathon, in the *Symposium*, takes her tender feet as proof that a god can be tender, since she walks on what is soft and not on what is hard (195d; see [[plato-the-tender-feet-of-ate]]).

**The verb.** ἀάω, “blind”, runs through the speech: Ate “blinds all” (ἀᾶται, 19.91, 19.129), Zeus “was blinded” (ἄσατο, 19.95) and “was greatly blinded” (πολλὸν ἀάσθη, 19.113), and Agamemnon “was first blinded” (ἀάσθην, 19.136) and “was blinded” (ἀασάμην, 19.137). ἀᾶται is the only form of the verb that cannot be referred to ἀϝάζω, and the only middle used transitively, except probably ἄσατο (Leaf on 19.91). At 95 the manuscripts read Ζῆνʼ, “she blinded Zeus”, Aristarchus Ζεύς, “Zeus was blinded”, which the text prints (Leaf on 19.95; see the translation's note on 19.95–133). Either way Zeus himself falls under his daughter's power.

**Even Zeus.** The story that follows is the proof: on the day Heracles was to be born, Hera made Zeus swear a rash oath, “and in this was greatly blinded” (19.113; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]] and [[oath]]). When Eurystheus was born first, Zeus turned on the power that had blinded him:

{{quote:634eb8bf-d3e9-5a97-bd4a-b046a355d797}}

So she came to live among men, and Zeus groaned over her (τὴν … στενάχεσχʼ) whenever he saw his son's labors for Eurystheus (19.132–133; see [[heracles]]). Agamemnon applies the story to himself: “So I too, when in turn great Hector of the gleaming helmet / was killing the Argives at the sterns of the ships, / could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded” (19.134–136; see [[mythological-paradigm]]).

**Achilles' answer.** After the oath, sworn by the Erinyes among others, who punish men beneath the earth for false oaths (19.258–265; see [[erinyes]]), Achilles speaks:

{{quote:cdcc5a5f-82bf-5300-9fb8-81094dd10c13}}

He accepts Agamemnon's account and adds the purpose behind it. ἄτας is plural, as in Agamemnon's admission, ἐμὰς ἄτας, “my blind folly” (9.115), and Dolon's excuse, ἄτῃσι, “with many blind follies” (10.391); they are the only plurals of the word in Homer. διδοῖσθα, “you give”, occurs only here (Leaf on 19.270). Achilles does not excuse Agamemnon altogether: “there was no dealing with him” (ἀμήχανος, 19.273). In Book 1 he asked that Agamemnon might learn “his own blind folly” through the deaths of the Achaeans (1.411–412); now he says that those deaths were what Zeus wanted (19.274; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[achilles]]).

**Blame and payment.** None of these accounts frees the king from paying. Ate, Zeus, Fate and the Erinys share the blame, and Agamemnon still gives the gifts and swears the oath (19.137–144, 19.238–266; see [[who-is-to-blame]], [[aitios]], [[divine-intervention]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).
