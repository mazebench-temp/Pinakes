ἣ δʼ ἀστὴρ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, “and like a star it shone” (19.381), is the half-verse of the robe that Hecuba chose for Athena, which “shone like a star” (ἀστὴρ δʼ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, 6.295), with its δέ moved forward. In Book 6 it was the finest work of women's hands, offered to the goddess with a prayer she did not grant; here it is the work of a god, and the man who puts it on will kill Hector (see [[helmet]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The helmet is the second light of the arming. Before it the shield shone far off like the moon and like a fire that sailors see from the sea (19.374–380; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]), and after it Achilles gleams in his armor “like shining Hyperion” (19.398; see [[like-the-sun]]).

The end of 382 and the whole of 383 return, nearly the same, when Achilles runs at Hector (22.315–316). Walter Leaf thinks 382 was made from that passage, where Achilles is running and περισσείοντο, “waved around”, has its full force, and that 383 was added in both places to recall the Making of the Arms (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.382; see the translation's note on 19.382–383). In Book 22 a star follows the helmet: the point of Achilles' spear gleams like the evening star, the most beautiful star in the sky (22.317–319).
