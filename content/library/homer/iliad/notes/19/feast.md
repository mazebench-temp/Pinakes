summary: Book 19 talks of meals more than any other, and its hero will not eat. Odysseus asks that the army take its meal and that Agamemnon make amends “with a rich feast in his huts” (19.171–172, 19.179); Achilles would hold “a great supper” only when Patroclus is avenged (19.208), sends the others “to your meal” (19.275), refuses the elders who beg him to eat (19.303–308), and remembers the meals Patroclus used to set before him (19.315–317). The army eats while he fasts (19.346).
grc: [δαιτὶ, δαίσειν, δειπνῆσαι]

**The meal before battle.** Odysseus' argument is that no man can fight all day unfed, while a man filled with wine and food fights with a bold heart (19.160–170; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]] and [[fasting]]). His terms for the reconciliation end in a feast: after the gifts and the oath, Agamemnon is to make amends to Achilles with a meal in his huts, “so that you may have no lack of justice” (19.179–180; see [[propitiation]] and [[no-blame-for-a-king-to-make-amends]]). Shared food is the seal of a settled quarrel, and Achilles refuses it.

**Supper after vengeance.** Achilles would rather send the army out unfed and “make ready a great supper” at sunset, when they have taken vengeance (19.207–208; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[lobe]]). When he dissolves the assembly he gives the order to eat in Agamemnon's own verse from Book 2: “But now go to your meal, so that we may join in the clash of Ares” (19.275 = 2.381). The army eats; Achilles does not. The elders gather round him “begging him to take his meal” (δειπνῆσαι, 19.304), and he begs them in turn not to bid him “with food or drink / to sate my dear heart” until sunset (19.306–308; see [[supplication]]).

**The meals of Patroclus.** In his lament Achilles remembers how Patroclus “would yourself set a pleasant meal before me in the hut, / swiftly and quickly”, whenever the Achaeans hurried out to war (19.316–317), and now his heart goes without drink and food, “though they are here within” (19.320). Walter Leaf takes ἔνδον ἐόντων of the store in the hut, “abundance though there be” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.320). The food is there; the companion who served it is not. When the envoys came to the hut in Book 9, Patroclus kindled the fire and set out the bread in baskets while Achilles carved and served the meat (9.205–217).

**Other feasts.** Briseis remembers Patroclus' promise to “give the marriage feast among the Myrmidons” (19.299; see [[marriage]]). Zeus' answer to the fast is food of another kind, nectar and ambrosia dripped into Achilles' breast (19.347–354; see [[nectar]] and [[ambrosia]]).
