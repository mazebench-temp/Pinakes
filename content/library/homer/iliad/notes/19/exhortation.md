summary: The calls to battle are argued against a call to eat. Achilles asks Agamemnon to “urge the long-haired Achaeans on to war” (19.69), and Agamemnon tells him to “rouse the rest of the army” (19.139); Odysseus answers “do not urge the sons of the Achaeans to Ilios fasting” (19.156), and Achilles mocks the two kings, “and you two urge us to food!” (19.205). Odysseus closes with a summons that leaves no man behind at the ships (19.233–237).
grc: ["ὄτρυνον πόλεμον", ὀτρυντὺν, ὀτρυντύς]
en: [summons]
---

ὀτρύνω, “urge on”, is the verb of exhortation, and in this book it is turned toward the meal. Odysseus will not have the army urged on unfed (19.156; see [[fasting]] and [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]), and Achilles throws the verb back in the dual, ὀτρύνετον, at Odysseus and Agamemnon (19.205; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.205). His own exhortation is the shortest: “So let each of you remember this and fight his man” (19.153).

The noun ὀτρυντύς, “summons”, occurs in Homer only in Odysseus' last lines: let no one of the army hold back, “waiting for some other summons; / for this summons will mean evil for whoever is left behind / at the ships of the Argives” (19.234–236), and let them “rouse sharp Ares against the horse-taming Trojans” (19.237), the verse with which Odysseus answered Agamemnon's rebuke at the review (4.352). Leaf would put a colon after ὀτρυντύς, “this is the summons”, which the printed text does not have, and finds the end of the speech, with its summons to battle, better suited to Achilles than to Odysseus (Leaf on 19.230, 19.235; see the translation's note on 19.230–237).
