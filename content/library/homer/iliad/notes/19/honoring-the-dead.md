---
summary: Patroclus' body is kept unburied and unspoiled. Achilles fears that flies will breed worms in its wounds and defile it while he fights (19.23–27); Thetis keeps them away and drips ambrosia and nectar into its nostrils, so that the flesh will stay sound even for a year (19.29–39). The body lies in Achilles' hut “turned toward the doorway”, mourned by his companions, by Briseis and the captive women, and by Achilles (19.211–213, 19.282–339). Odysseus states the rule of an army: bury the dead after a single day of weeping (19.228–229).
refs: [19.23-39, 19.211-213]
---

**Kept from corruption.** ἀεικίσσωσι δὲ νεκρόν, “and defile the body” (19.26), is the half-verse in which Glaucus feared that the Myrmidons would defile Sarpedon (16.545); the verb is used again when Zeus gives Hector's body to his enemies to defile (22.404). Thetis' remedy is the gods' food, as Apollo, at Zeus' bidding, anointed Sarpedon with ambrosia (16.670, 16.680; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). Walter Leaf reports Heyne's view that the dripping through the nostrils recalls Egyptian embalming, in which the brain is drawn out through the nostrils and drugs poured in (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.38; Herodotus 2.86; see the translation's note on 19.38–39).

**Turned toward the doorway.** ἀνὰ πρόθυρον τετραμμένος (19.212): Leaf takes it that the dead man's feet are turned to the door, a sign of departure and an ancient funeral custom, and reports a scholium that the dead, leaving human life, do not turn back to their homes (on 19.212). He compares two Latin writers. In Persius a glutton dies at the bath, and laid on a high bed and smeared with ointments “stretches out his stiff heels toward the door” (*in portam rigidas calces extendit*, *Satires* 3.103–105); and Pliny writes that it is nature's way for a man to be born head first, and custom to carry him out feet first (*ritus naturae capite hominem gigni, mos est pedibus efferri*, *Natural History* 7.46).

**A single day.** Odysseus' rule, “we must bury the man who dies, / having a pitiless heart, after weeping for him a single day” (19.228–229), is the rule Achilles will not keep; he has vowed no burial until Hector is dead (18.333–337; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]], [[fasting]] and [[lament]]).
