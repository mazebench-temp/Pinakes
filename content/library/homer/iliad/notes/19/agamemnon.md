summary: Agamemnon comes last to the assembly, still wounded, and answers Achilles from his seat. He is not to blame, he says, but Zeus, Fate and the Erinys, who cast blind folly into his mind on the day he took Achilles' prize; Blind Folly blinded even Zeus. He offers all the gifts of the embassy, accepts Odysseus' counsel, stands to swear over a boar that he never touched Briseis, and stays with Achilles when the other kings go (19.51–53, 19.76–144, 19.184–197, 19.249–266, 19.310).

**Last, and wounded.** Diomedes and Odysseus come limping to the assembly on their spears (19.47–50; see [[diomedes]] and [[odysseus]]). “And last of all came Agamemnon, lord of men, / with his wound; for him too in the fierce battle / Coon, son of Antenor, had stabbed with his bronze-fitted spear” (19.51–53; see [[coon]], [[lord-of-men]] and [[bronze-fitted]]). They are the three kings wounded on the third day of battle, who came up from the ships in Book 14 “leaning on their spears, all together” (14.29, 14.38; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Coon's spear went through his arm below the elbow, and when the wound dried the pains drove him to his chariot (11.251–253, 11.267–274). δεύτατος, “last”, is found nowhere else in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it is Menelaus, “the last of the bronze-armored Achaeans to come home” (*Odyssey* 1.286).

**From his seat.** Achilles renounces his anger, and the Achaeans rejoice (19.56–75; see [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). Agamemnon answers “from where he sat, and not standing up in their midst” (19.77). Zenodotus did not have the verse and made him stand; an explanation that the scholia ascribe to Aristarchus takes it of a king who, unable to stand, will still speak for himself rather than through another man's mouth; Walter Leaf thinks the verse an early and mistaken explanation of the next two, and notes that the wound is in the arm (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.76; see the translation's note on 19.76–84). The speech opens with the verse that opened his testing of the army, “Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares” (19.78 = 2.110; see [[danaans]] and [[attendants-of-ares]]). There the next verse said that Zeus had bound him fast in heavy blind folly (2.111); here the excuse waits. First he asks to be heard: “it is good to listen to a man when he stands up to speak, and it is not fitting / to interrupt; for that is hard even for one who is skilled. / But in the great din of men how could anyone listen / or speak? He is hindered, clear-voiced orator though he is” (19.79–82; see [[listen-to-the-man-who-stands]] and [[clear-voiced-orator]]). Leaf takes ὑββάλλειν as “to throw in a word”, the sense of ὑποβλήδην, “breaking in”, when Achilles answered him in the first quarrel (1.292; Leaf on 19.76). He reads the halting opening as the embarrassment of a man who follows a speaker the army has just applauded, “the peevish nervousness of a man who feels that he is in the wrong” (on 19.80, 19.85). He will open his mind to the son of Peleus, “but you others, / Argives, take heed” (19.83–84; see [[argives]]); ἐνδείξομαι, “I will open my mind”, is found only here.

**Not to blame.**

{{quote:36d7f3f6-4e1d-575b-905a-bed57d0c47e8}}

The last verse is a saying: the god brings all things to their end (see [[a-god-brings-all-things-to-fulfillment]] and [[theos]]). τοῦτον … μῦθον, “this word”, is the word Achilles has just spoken, that the strife profited only Hector and the Trojans (19.63; Leaf on 19.85). The Achaeans had said it often. Thersites said that the king had dishonored a far better man, “he has taken his prize and keeps it, having seized it himself” (2.239–240), and Nestor told him the same at his own table (9.106–111). Poseidon, rousing the young men at the ships, allowed that the son of Atreus might be “in very truth the one wholly to blame” (πάμπαν ἐτήτυμον αἴτιός ἐστιν, 13.111–113), and Agamemnon himself feared that the Achaeans were “laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is” (14.49–51). Now he answers the word: οὐκ αἴτιός εἰμι, “I am not to blame” (see [[aitios]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). He does not deny the act. “I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (αὐτὸς ἀπηύρων, 19.89) is Achilles' own charge, “he has taken my prize and keeps it, having seized it himself” (αὐτὸς ἀπούρας, 1.356; see [[geras]] and [[taking-of-briseis]]). What he denies is that the will was his. The blind folly was cast into his mind “in the assembly”, the assembly of Book 1, and he names those who cast it: Zeus, Μοῖρα, “Fate”, and the Erinys “who walks in darkness” (see [[zeus]], [[moira]], [[erinyes]] and [[who-walks-in-darkness]]). Achilles had asked his mother that the son of Atreus might learn “his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans” (1.411–412). In Book 9 Agamemnon owned it: “I was blinded, obeying my wretched mind” (9.119). Here he names the gods who did it (see [[ate]], [[divine-intervention]] and the translation's note on 9.115–120).

**Blind Folly.** He tells how Ate, “the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds all”, walks with tender feet over the heads of men, and how she blinded even Zeus. On the day Heracles was to be born, Hera made him swear that the child of his blood born that day would rule all who dwelt around; then she held back Alcmene's labor and brought Eurystheus into the light before his time. Zeus seized Ate by the hair, flung her from heaven to the works of men, and groaned whenever he saw his son toiling for Eurystheus (19.91–133; see [[ate]], [[the-birth-of-heracles]], [[heracles]] and [[eurystheus]]). The story is his precedent (see [[mythological-paradigm]]): “So I too, when in turn great Hector of the gleaming helmet / was killing the Argives at the sterns of the ships, / could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded” (19.134–136; see [[hector]]). If Zeus, who is “the best / of men and gods”, could be blinded (19.95–96), so could a king. Leaf, who thought the story worked in from an independent poem about Heracles, observes that “many speakers with a bad case take refuge in telling stories” (on 19.95). In Phoenix's allegory Ate was strong and sound of foot and outran the Prayers (9.505–507); in Agamemnon's her feet are tender, for she treads on men's heads (19.92–93; see [[litai]]). Both end with the same half-verse, “doing harm to men” (βλάπτουσʼ ἀνθρώπους, 9.507 = 19.94).

**The gifts.**

{{quote:80830781-ae46-5922-b4fd-de0255dfeccc}}

The first two verses are those of his admission in Book 9, with Zeus in place of “my wretched mind” (19.137–138; 9.119–120), which is what Achilles said of him that night, “Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits” (9.377). The excuse does not cancel the debt: since he was blinded, he will pay (see [[amends-and-reconciliation]], [[apoina]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). ἐγὼν ὅδε … παρασχέμεν, “here am I to provide”, makes the offer his reason for being there (Leaf on 19.140). The gifts are those Odysseus promised “yesterday” in Achilles' huts, though the embassy came the night before the third day of battle (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). One manuscript marks 136–140 with obeli; Leaf, referring the marks to 137–141 with Nicole, finds much in favor of rejecting those lines as added to join the book to Book 9: the gifts named twice, two verses copied from Book 9, “rouse yourself to war” beside “wait” (on 19.136; see the translation's note on 19.136–141).

**The counsel accepted.** Achilles tells him to give the gifts or keep them, as he pleases, and wants to fight at once (19.146–153; see [[achilles]]). Odysseus proposes the order of the day: food for the army, the gifts in the middle of the assembly, an oath, a feast in Agamemnon's huts; and he tells the king that he will be “more just hereafter”, for it is no cause for blame that a king should make amends “when he was the first to grow angry” (19.155–183; see [[odysseus]]). In Book 2 Agamemnon said as much himself: he and Achilles fought “for the sake of a girl / with hostile words, and I was the first to grow angry” (2.377–378). He welcomes the counsel:

{{quote:469b41f6-2616-53b9-8cb5-6fac53b2d52c}}

He gives Odysseus the charge of the gifts: “choose young men, the best of all the Achaeans” (19.193). The verse ends as the verse in which he called the elders, “the best of all the Achaeans”, to his sacrifice before the first battle (ἀριστῆας Παναχαιῶν, 2.404; see [[elders]] and [[achaeans]]). Talthybius is to make ready a boar “to sacrifice to Zeus and the Sun” (19.196–197; see [[talthybius]] and [[boar]]). Achilles answers both men with a dual: “and you two urge us to food!” (19.205; Leaf on 19.205).

**The oath.** When the gifts are in the middle of the assembly, “Agamemnon / stood up” (19.249–250). He spoke seated; he swears standing, as Odysseus asked, “let him stand up among the Argives and swear you an oath” (19.175). He draws the knife “that always hung beside the great sheath of his sword” (19.252–253 = 3.271–272), the verses of his oath over the lambs in Book 3, cuts the first hairs from the boar, lifts his hands to Zeus and prays, while the Argives sit in silence, “listening to the king, as was right” (19.254–257; see [[knife]], [[sheath]], [[prayer]] and [[silence]]). The oath is wider than the one he promised in Book 9, “that I never went up into her bed or lay with her” (9.133): “that I never laid a hand on the girl Briseis, / either wanting her for my bed or for anything else; / but she remained untouched in my huts” (19.261–263; see [[oath]] and [[briseis]]). “He spoke, and cut the boar's throat with the pitiless bronze” (19.266), as he cut the lambs' throats in Book 3 (3.292; see [[pitiless-bronze]]).

**Achilles' verdict.** Achilles answers with Agamemnon's own explanation: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men: / never would the heart in my breast have been stirred / through and through by the son of Atreus, nor would he have led the girl / away against my will—there was no dealing with him; but Zeus, it seems, / wanted death to come upon many of the Achaeans” (19.270–274; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). ἀμήχανος, “there was no dealing with him”, is the last word on the king's part in the quarrel. Achilles has used the formal address of the council twice, “Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men” (19.146, 19.199), where his first words to him in Book 1 were “Most glorious son of Atreus, most covetous of all men” (1.122; see [[most-glorious]]).

**With Achilles.** When the elders beg Achilles to eat and he sends the other kings away, “the two sons of Atreus stayed, and brilliant Odysseus, / Nestor and Idomeneus and the old horse-driving Phoenix, / comforting him in his deep grief” (19.310–312; see [[atreidae]] and [[kings]]). The feast that Odysseus proposed in Agamemnon's huts (19.179–180) comes only after Hector's death, when the kings bring Achilles to Agamemnon's hut and he bids them yield to “the hateful feast” (στυγερῇ … δαιτί, 23.35–48).
