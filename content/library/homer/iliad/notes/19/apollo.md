# Unnamed: "the best of the gods, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore", who killed Patroclus (19.413-414).
refs: [19.413-414]
---

**The best of the gods.** Xanthus answers Achilles' reproach that the horses left Patroclus dead on the field (19.403):

{{quote:c37b1045-4910-5967-baca-31e0f6e12226}}

The horse names Apollo by his mother, as the dying Patroclus did: “But deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me” (16.849; see [[leto]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). ὃν ἠΰκομος τέκε Λητώ, “whom Leto of the lovely hair bore”, is the half-verse with which the narrator names him as Chryses goes apart to pray to him in Book 1 (1.35–36; see [[lovely-haired]]), and the Odyssey uses it of him when he kills Otus and Ephialtes (*Odyssey* 11.318). θεῶν ὤριστος, “the best of the gods”, is Hector's title for Zeus in Book 13 (13.154), and in this book Zeus is “the best / of men and gods” and “highest and best of the gods” (19.95–96, 19.258; see [[zeus]]). The second verse is the verse with which Thetis ended her story to Hephaestus: “had not Apollo, / after the valiant son of Menoetius had done much harm, / killed him among the champions, and given glory to Hector” (19.414 = 18.456; 18.454–456). Both give the killing to Apollo alone, though Euphorbus wounded Patroclus from behind and Hector struck the last blow (16.806–822; see [[euphorbus]], [[hector]] and [[xanthus-the-horse]]).

**A god and a man.** The horse goes on: “but for you yourself / it is fated to be brought down by force by a god and a man” (19.416–417; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Aristarchus athetized the two verses (see the translation's note on 19.416–417), and Walter Leaf observes that the fate of 417 is the one thing the horse tells Achilles that his mother had not (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.407). The god and the man are named only when the dying Hector warns Achilles of the day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo will kill him at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[paris]] and [[scaean-gates]]), and in Book 21 Achilles remembers that his mother told him he would die under the wall of the Trojans by the swift arrows of Apollo (21.275–278; see [[thetis]]). The “great god” whom the horse blames with mighty Fate for the day of Achilles' destruction is not named (19.409–410; see [[mighty-fate]]).
