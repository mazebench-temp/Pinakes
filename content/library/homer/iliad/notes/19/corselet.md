---
summary: Achilles will arm himself, he tells his mother, and she bids him arm at once (19.23, 19.36); the Achaeans arm throughout the army, and their “corselets with strong plates” pour out of the ships (19.352, 19.361); and Achilles puts on his corselet second, in the verse of every arming scene (19.371).
grc: [θωρήσσεο]
---

**Arming.** The verb of the corselet is the verb of arming. Achilles says, “Now I will arm myself” (θωρήξομαι, 19.23), Thetis tells him to “arm yourself at once for the war” (θωρήσσεο, 19.36), and while Athena drips nectar into his breast the Achaeans “were at once arming throughout the army” (θωρήσσοντο, 19.352; see [[arming-scene]]).

**Strong plates.** The corselets that pour out of the ships with the helmets, shields and spears are κραταιγύαλοι, “with strong plates”, a word found only here (19.361; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]). The γύαλον is the curved plate of the corselet (θώρηκος γύαλον, 5.99, 13.507, 13.587, 17.314). Walter Leaf takes the corselet as made of a solid breastplate and backplate, and the verse, from its awkward position, as a late addition (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.361; see the translation's note on 19.361).

**Achilles' corselet.** “second, he put on the corselet around his chest” (19.371 = 3.332, 11.19, 16.133). Hephaestus made it “brighter than the light of fire” (18.610), but in the arming it has no verse of its own, where Paris', Agamemnon's and Patroclus' each have one (3.333, 11.20–28, 16.134). Leaf, noting that 369–373 repeat the arming of Paris, adds that “371 is later, as usual” (on 19.369); he reports Reichel's view that the corselet verse of the making of the arms is a later addition too (on 18.610; see the translation's note on 18.610).
