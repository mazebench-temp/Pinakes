---
summary: Achilles will not eat until Patroclus is avenged. Odysseus answers that no man can fight all day “going without food” and that the dead must be mourned for a day and buried; the army eats, the elders beg Achilles to take his meal, and he refuses until sunset. Zeus, seeing the man who “is unfed and has tasted nothing”, sends Athena to drip nectar and ambrosia into his breast (19.155–354).
---

**Odysseus' case.** Achilles wants the army to fight at once (19.146–153). Odysseus answers, “do not urge the sons of the Achaeans to Ilios fasting” (19.156): the battle will be long, and “no man all day long, until the sun goes down, / will be able to fight face to face, going without food”; thirst and hunger overtake him, and his knees are hindered, while the man who has eaten and drunk fights with a bold heart until the end (19.162–170; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]], [[gounata]] and [[etor]]).

**Achilles' answer.** He would send the army out “fasting and unfed” and make ready a great supper at sunset, when they have avenged the insult; before that “nothing would go down my throat, / neither drink nor food, while my companion is dead” (19.205–210). Food is not in his mind, “but slaughter and blood and the grievous groaning of men” (19.213–214; see [[feast]] and [[lobe]]).

**Odysseus again.** “But the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly”: men fall every day, the dead must be buried after a single day of weeping, and those who survive “must remember drink and food” (19.225–231; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[satiety-of-battle]]). Walter Leaf notes how the interest in feeding runs through the whole book, even to the stewards who deal out the food (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.43; 19.44).

**The refusal.** Achilles sends the army to its meal (19.275) and stays without one. The elders gather round him “begging him to take his meal”, and he begs them not to bid him sate his heart with food or drink: “until the sun goes down I will wait and endure all the same” (19.303–308; see [[supplication]] and [[sunset-lines]]). In his lament he remembers the meals Patroclus used to set before him, and says that his heart “goes without drink and food, though they are here within, / for longing for you” (19.315–321; see [[pothe]] and [[ker-heart]]).

**The gods' food.** Zeus sees the mourners and pities them, and tells Athena: “the others / have gone to their meal, while he is unfed and has tasted nothing. / But go, drip nectar and lovely ambrosia / into his breast, so that hunger may not come upon him” (19.345–348). She does it, “so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees” (19.354), the knees that Odysseus said hunger would weigh down (19.166). The man who will not eat with the army is fed by the gods, as Patroclus' body was kept sound by them (19.38–39; see [[nectar]], [[ambrosia]], [[pity]] and [[athena]]).
