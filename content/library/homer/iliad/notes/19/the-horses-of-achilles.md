**The yoking.** “And Automedon and Alcimus, busying themselves with the horses, / yoked them; and they put the fine breast straps on them, and the bits / they set in their jaws, and pulled the reins back / to the well-joined chariot” (19.392–395; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]] and [[chariot]]). Automedon takes the whip and springs up ἐφʼ ἵπποιιν, “behind the horses” (19.396), a dual, of the pair. The mortal trace horse that ran beside them in Book 16, Pedasus, was killed by Sarpedon's spear (16.152–154, 16.466–469; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]). Achilles takes his stand behind Automedon, armed (19.397).

**The command.** “And he called terribly to the horses of his father” (19.399): they are Peleus' horses, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381 = 16.867), and Achilles will say that Poseidon gave them to his father and his father to him (23.277–278; see [[peleus]] and [[poseidon]]).

{{quote:fc4d7b2c-1ec4-5fe7-a3bb-f4a0b2fc13cf}}

Hector called his own horses by name in Book 8, reminding them of their keep (8.185–190; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]); Achilles calls his by name and by their mother, τηλεκλυτὰ τέκνα Ποδάργης, “far-famed children of Podarge” (see [[podarge]]). The “charioteer” they are to bring back is Achilles himself, the fighter in the chariot, as Patroclus had been (see the translation's note on 19.401). The charioteer they lost they had wept for, standing apart from the battle until Zeus put might into them (17.426–458; see the note on Book 17).

**The answer.** “And from under the yoke the horse with flashing feet spoke to him, / Xanthus” (19.404–405; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]). He bows his head until his mane reaches the ground, in the verse of the pair's mourning for Patroclus (19.406 = 17.440), and Hera gives him a voice (19.407; see [[hera]]). He speaks for both. They will save Achilles this time, but his day of destruction is near; it was not their slowness that cost Patroclus his armor, for Apollo killed him; “we two could run even as fast as the breath of the West Wind” (19.415), the wind that sired them (16.150), though Walter Leaf thinks the poet here does not know it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.416; see [[zephyrus]]); and Achilles is fated to be brought down by a god and a man (19.408–417). The Erinyes check the voice (19.418; see [[erinyes]]), and Achilles answers Xanthus that he knows his fate (19.420–423; see [[achilles]]). The book ends as he “drove his single-hoofed horses among the foremost” (19.424; see [[single-hoofed]]).
