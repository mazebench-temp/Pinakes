summary: The quarrel ends in the assembly of Book 19. Achilles asks whether their strife for a girl did either of them any good, and puts an end to his anger; Agamemnon answers that he was not to blame, but Zeus, Fate and the Erinys, who blinded him on the day he took Achilles' prize; and after the oath Achilles grants it: Zeus gives men great blind follies, and must have wanted the death of many Achaeans (19.56–73, 19.85–89, 19.270–275).

**Achilles' question.**

{{quote:ff171d9f-a064-581d-b403-de858c921cae}}

The scene is set as the first quarrel was. In Book 1 too Achilles had called the assembly, and he rose to speak in the same verse and began with the same word, Ἀτρεΐδη, “son of Atreus” (19.55 = 1.58; 19.56, 1.59; 1.54). ἦ ἄρ τι makes it a question with an ironical color, “was this then better”; Walter Leaf thinks the other reading, a statement that reconciliation would have been the better course, much weaker (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.56; see the translation's note on 19.56–57). The strife is ἔρις, the word of the proem, which dates the poem “from the time when first the two of them stood apart in strife” (1.6; see [[eris]]), and it is θυμοβόρος, “heart-devouring”, the strife in which Hector and Ajax fought before they “parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302; see [[heart-devouring]] and [[parted-in-friendship]]). It was “for the sake of a girl” (εἵνεκα κούρης), as Agamemnon put it in Book 2, “Achilles and I fought for the sake of a girl” (2.377), and Ajax at the embassy, “for the sake of a girl, / one girl” (9.637–638). Achilles, who swore in Book 1 that he would not fight with his hands “for the girl's sake” (1.298), and in Book 9 called her “the wife dear to my heart” (9.336), now wishes that Artemis had killed her on the day he took her at Lyrnessus (19.59–60; see [[briseis]], [[artemis]] and [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). He counts the cost in the Achaeans who bit the ground while he “nursed my wrath apart” (ἐμεῦ ἀπομηνίσαντος, 19.62, the words of 9.426; see [[menis]] and [[androktasia]]), and the gain in Hector's and the Trojans' (19.63).

**Done and past.** “But we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief, / taming the dear heart in our breasts under compulsion” (19.65–66): the two verses with which he let the anger go before his mother in Book 18 (18.112–113; see the note on Book 18). Then: “Now I put a stop to my anger; there is no need for me / to rage stubbornly forever” (19.67–68; see [[cholos]]). He asks Agamemnon to rouse the army, and the Achaeans rejoice “that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (19.68–75; see [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]).

**Agamemnon's answer.** “Often the Achaeans have spoken this word to me / and kept rebuking me; but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind / on the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (19.85–89; see [[agamemnon]] and [[ate]]). He owns the act in Achilles' words of Book 1: “he has taken my prize and keeps it, having seized it himself” (αὐτὸς ἀπούρας, 1.356; αὐτὸς ἀπηύρων, 19.89; see [[geras]]). The assembly of 19.88 is the assembly of the quarrel. The cause he gives is blind folly, which Achilles had wished him to learn: “his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans” (1.411–412).

**Undone.** In Book 1 Agamemnon took the prize “so that you may know well / how much greater I am than you” (1.185–186), and in Book 9 his offer ended with the demand that Achilles “submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.160; see [[kingship-and-authority]]). Book 19 drops the demand. Odysseus tells the king that he will be “more just hereafter”, and that a king may make amends (19.181–183; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]); Agamemnon swears before the army that he never touched Briseis, and gives her back with the gifts (19.246, 19.258–265; see [[taking-of-briseis]], [[oath]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Achilles twice addresses him with the council's formal verse, “Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men” (19.146, 19.199; as 2.434, 9.96, 9.163). His first words to him in Book 1 were “Most glorious son of Atreus, most covetous of all men” (1.122).

**Achilles' verdict.**

{{quote:cdcc5a5f-82bf-5300-9fb8-81094dd10c13}}

Achilles accepts Agamemnon's account and goes beyond it: the blind folly was Zeus' gift, and so was the strife, because Zeus wanted many Achaeans dead. The proem had set the quarrel beside the plan of Zeus, which was being fulfilled as the wrath sent many mighty souls to Hades (1.2–7; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). ἀμήχανος, “there was no dealing with him” (19.273), is his last word on the king's part. In Book 9 he said that he would not be persuaded “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.387); the insult he now means to avenge is Hector's (19.208; see [[lobe]]).

**After.** The quarrel is over, but not its consequences. Achilles will not eat until Patroclus is avenged (19.199–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]]), and Briseis' lament over Patroclus follows the gifts into his hut (19.282–300). The feast that Odysseus proposed for the reconciliation, in Agamemnon's huts, is held only after Hector's death (19.179–180; 23.35–56). At the funeral games Achilles gives Agamemnon the prize for the javelin without a contest, since all know, he says, how far the son of Atreus surpasses everyone (23.884–897).
