---
summary: Achilles names Helen once, in his lament for Patroclus: he is fighting the Trojans in a foreign land “for the sake of Helen, at whom men shudder” (19.325).
---

Achilles weighs his grief against the news of his father's death, imagining Peleus weeping in Phthia “for lack of such a son; while I, in a foreign land, / for the sake of Helen, at whom men shudder, am fighting the Trojans” (19.324–325; see [[achilles]] and [[peleus]]). ῥιγεδανῆς, “at whom men shudder”, is found only here in Homer and comes from the root of ῥιγέω, “shudder” (see [[at-whom-men-shudder]]). Walter Leaf reports Hentze's remark that this is the only place in the Iliad, as *Odyssey* 14.68 is the only one in the Odyssey, where a Greek other than Helen herself speaks of her in anger, and compares her own word for herself, κρυοέσσης, “chilling” (6.344; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.325; see [[chilling]]). In the Odyssey it is Eumaeus who wishes that Helen's whole race had perished, since she loosed the knees of many men (*Odyssey* 14.68–69). In Book 9 Achilles named her as the cause of the war without blame, “Was it not for the sake of Helen of the lovely hair?” (9.339), to argue that he too had lost a wife (see [[briseis]]).
