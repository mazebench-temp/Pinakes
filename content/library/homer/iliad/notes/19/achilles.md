summary: Thetis brings him the armor of Hephaestus, and at her bidding he calls the Achaeans to an assembly and renounces his wrath. He leaves the gifts to Agamemnon and would lead the army out unfed; when the gifts are given and the oath sworn, he lays the quarrel to Zeus, but he will not eat himself, and he laments Patroclus with his father and his son in mind. Athena drips nectar into his breast; he arms in the god's armor, takes his father's spear and drives out, answering his horse Xanthus, who foretells his death, that he knows it (19.1–75, 19.145–153, 19.198–214, 19.268–276, 19.303–354, 19.364–424).

**The armor.** At dawn Thetis finds him “lying with his arms around Patroclus, / weeping loudly” (19.4–5; see [[thetis]] and [[patroclus]]), and gives him the armor of Hephaestus, “very fine, such as no man has ever yet worn on his shoulders” (19.11; see [[hephaestus]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

{{quote:911a3f46-35fc-573c-a4d0-34f0478e8c9b}}

The god's work that his men cannot look at drives the anger deeper into him, χόλος (19.16; see [[cholos]] and [[myrmidons]]), and his eyes shine out “like a blaze” (19.17), as they will again when he arms (19.365–366; see [[eyes-like-fire]]). When he has gazed his fill, he tells his mother that the armor is such as only immortals could make, and that he will arm; but he fears for the body while he fights, “but terribly / I fear that in the meantime into the valiant son of Menoetius / flies will make their way, through the wounds the bronze has struck, / and breed worms, and defile the body” (19.23–26; see [[flies]]). Thetis promises to keep the flesh sound, and tells him to call the Achaeans to an assembly, renounce his wrath against Agamemnon, and arm at once (19.29–36).

**The assembly.** “But brilliant Achilles went along the shore of the sea, / shouting terribly, and he roused the Achaean heroes” (19.40–41). Even the helmsmen and the stewards come, “because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (19.45–46). The narrator gave the same reason for the Trojans' fear in their assembly the night before (18.247–248), and Achilles had wished the Trojans to learn it, “and let them know that I have long ceased from the war” (18.125; see [[assembly]]). As in Book 1, it is Achilles who calls the Achaeans to an assembly (1.54). He stands up to speak (19.55); Agamemnon, wounded, answers from his seat (19.76–77; see [[agamemnon]]).

**The renunciation.** He begins with the quarrel: was it better for them both “that we two, grieving at heart, / raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl?” (19.57–58; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). He wishes that Artemis had killed Briseis on the day he took her (19.59–60; see [[briseis]], [[artemis]] and [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]), for the Achaeans died at the hands of their enemies “while I nursed my wrath apart” (19.62), and Hector and the Trojans had the profit of it (19.63). Then:

{{quote:c61a32e4-743c-5a93-b538-4832146f7aed}}

The first two verses are the ones he spoke to his mother the night before, when he chose to avenge Patroclus (19.65–66 = 18.112–113; see the note on Book 18). νῦν δʼ ἤτοι μὲν ἐγώ, “Now I”, begins both his resolutions in this book, to arm (19.23) and to put a stop to his anger (19.67). He calls it χόλος, “anger” (see [[cholos]]); his mother and the narrator call what he gives up μῆνις, with the same verb, μῆνιν ἀποειπών, “renouncing your wrath” (19.35), and μῆνιν ἀπειπόντος, “that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (19.75; see [[menis]] and [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]). The wrath that is the poem's first word is given up here.

**The gifts.** Agamemnon answers that not he is to blame but Zeus, Fate and the Erinys, who cast blind folly into his mind; he tells how Blind Folly blinded Zeus himself, and offers all the gifts that Odysseus promised (19.78–144; see [[ate]], [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Achilles leaves the gifts to him: “the gifts are yours, to give them if you wish, as is fitting, / or to keep them by you. But now let us remember the joy of battle / at once” (19.147–149). In the embassy he had said, “His gifts are hateful to me, and I honor him at a hair's worth” (9.378; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]); now they do not matter to him. “For we must not waste time in talk here / nor delay; for a great work is still undone” (19.149–150): κλοτοπεύειν, “waste time in talk”, is found nowhere else in Greek (see the translation's note on 19.149). He speaks of himself by name: “so that once again a man may see Achilles among the foremost / destroying the battalions of the Trojans with his bronze spear” (19.151–152).

**Not before vengeance.** Odysseus would have the army eat first, and the gifts brought into the assembly, and Agamemnon swear that he never lay with Briseis (19.155–183; see [[odysseus]], [[oath]] and [[fasting]]). Achilles answers that this can wait for a pause in the war: the men whom Hector killed lie torn, “and you two urge us to food!” (19.203–205). He would send the Achaeans to fight fasting and unfed, and make ready a great supper at sunset, “when we have avenged the insult” (19.207–208; see [[lobe]]). For himself:

{{quote:f4d64bae-42f7-5d7a-8b82-a80d7eaf1a61}}

The body lies “turned toward the doorway” (19.212), with its feet to the door, as for a departure (see the translation's note on 19.212). Odysseus grants that Achilles is the stronger with the spear, but claims the better understanding, being older, and argues that the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly (19.216–229).

**Zeus' blind follies.** When the gifts have been brought and Agamemnon has sworn over the boar that he never touched Briseis (19.238–266; see [[oath]]), Achilles accepts his account:

{{quote:cdcc5a5f-82bf-5300-9fb8-81094dd10c13}}

Agamemnon had said that Zeus, Fate and the Erinys cast ἄτη into his mind (19.86–88); Achilles answers with the same word, ἄτας, “blind follies”, and gives the deaths of the quarrel to the will of Zeus (see [[ate]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). ἀμήχανος, “there was no dealing with him” (19.273), is the word Patroclus used of Achilles himself: “but there is no dealing with you, Achilles” (16.29). He sends them to their meal, “so that we may join in the clash of Ares” (19.275), and dissolves the assembly (19.276), as he and Agamemnon dissolved the assembly of the quarrel in Book 1 (1.305).

**The fast.** The elders gather around him and beg him to eat. He refuses: “until the sun goes down I will wait and endure all the same” (19.303–308; see [[elders]] and [[supplication]]). He sends the other kings away; the sons of Atreus, Odysseus, Nestor, Idomeneus and Phoenix stay to comfort him (19.309–312; see [[phoenix]]), “but not at all in his heart / would he be comforted, before he entered the mouth of bloody war” (19.312–313).

**The lament.** He remembers Patroclus at the meal:

{{quote:e207f345-8e69-50ea-a0b6-1e032db14739}}

On the night of the embassy Patroclus set out the bread while Achilles served the meat (9.216–217; see [[feast]]). “But now you lie torn, and my heart / goes without drink and food, though they are here within, / for longing for you” (19.319–321). Nothing worse could happen to him, not the news of his father's death, “who now, I think, in Phthia is letting his tender tears fall / for lack of such a son”, while he fights at Troy “for the sake of Helen, at whom men shudder”, nor of his son's, “if indeed godlike Neoptolemus is still alive” (19.321–327; see [[peleus]], [[phthia]], [[helen]], [[neoptolemus]] and [[scyros]]). He had hoped that he alone would die at Troy and that Patroclus would bring the boy home from Scyros and show him his possessions; Peleus, he thinks, is dead, or barely alive in hateful old age, waiting for the news of his son's death (19.328–337). It is the only place where the Iliad names his son. Aristarchus and Aristophanes athetized 19.327, and Walter Leaf suspects the whole passage (see the translation's note on 19.326–337). The elders groan with him, “each remembering what he had left in his halls” (19.338–339), as the captive women wept with Briseis “for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302; see [[captive-women]] and [[lament]]).

**Athena's nectar.** Zeus sees them weeping and pities them, and speaks to Athena: “My child, you have utterly abandoned your brave man. / Is Achilles then no longer at all in your mind?” (19.342–343; see [[zeus]] and [[athena]]). The others have gone to their meal, “while he is unfed and has tasted nothing” (19.346). Athena leaps down from the sky “like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey” (19.350) and drips nectar and ambrosia into his breast, “so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees” (19.354; see [[nectar]] and [[ambrosia]]). In the morning Thetis dripped the same food of the gods into Patroclus' nostrils to keep his body sound (19.38–39); Leaf sets the two places side by side (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.344).

**The arming.** “And in their midst brilliant Achilles armed himself” (19.364). His teeth gnash, his eyes shine “like the blaze of fire”, unbearable grief enters his heart, “and raging against the Trojans / he put on the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus had toiled to make” (19.365–368). Aristarchus athetized the four verses, finding the gnashing of teeth grotesque (see the translation's note on 19.365–368). He puts on the greaves, the corselet, the sword, the shield and the helmet in the verses of Paris' arming (19.369–373 = 3.330–332, 334–335; see [[arming-scene]]); the shield shines like the moon and like a fire seen by sailors at sea, and the helmet like a star (19.373–383; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[like-a-star]]). Then he tries the armor:

{{quote:e55aa7af-3220-5739-bc8b-5635507da20b}}

εὖτε is “like”: Aristarchus is said to have read αὖτε, which would make them real wings (see the translation's note on 19.386 and [[like-wings]]). Last he draws his father's spear, the Pelian ash, which Patroclus left behind because no other Achaean could wield it (19.387–391, 16.140–144; see [[the-pelian-ash]]). Automedon and Alcimus yoke the horses, and Achilles takes his stand behind Automedon, “gleaming in his armor like shining Hyperion” (19.392–398; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]], [[hyperion]] and [[like-the-sun]]).

**Xanthus.** He calls “terribly to the horses of his father” and bids them bring him back safe, “and do not leave him there dead, as you left Patroclus” (19.399–403; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Xanthus, given a voice by Hera, answers that they will save him this time, but that his day of destruction is near, and that he is fated to be brought down “by a god and a man” (19.404–417; see [[xanthus-the-horse]]). The Erinyes check the voice (19.418), and Achilles answers:

{{quote:f0dd09fc-5311-56a6-b56d-e6befed14ac3}}

He has it from his mother, who told him that his life would be short and that his own fate was ready straight after Hector's (1.416–418, 18.95–96; see [[thetis]], [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). τί μοι … μαντεύεαι, “why do you prophesy … for me?”, is Hector's question to the dying Patroclus (16.859). Over Patroclus' body he said that neither Peleus nor Thetis would welcome him home (18.330–332), and now he will die “far from my dear father and mother” (19.422). ἅδην ἐλάσαι πολέμοιο, “given the Trojans their fill of war” (19.423), takes up his words to the horses, “when we have had our fill of war” (19.402); Leaf refers both to his note on 13.315 (on 19.402, 19.423). The book that began his return with a shout along the shore, σμερδαλέα ἰάχων (19.41), ends with a cry: “He spoke, and with a cry drove his single-hoofed horses among the foremost” (ἰάχων, 19.424).
