summary: The story ends at dawn. Thetis brings the god's gifts to the ships and sets them before Achilles; the arms ring, the Myrmidons cannot look at them, and Achilles delights in them, “the works of immortals” (19.1–23). Before the battle he puts on “the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus had toiled to make” (19.368).

**At dawn.** Thetis promised to come back “at dawn … with the rising sun, / bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.136–137), and she does:

{{quote:33138573-dfc6-5f72-88bc-e15913d3829a}}

The arms are θεοῦ πάρα δῶρα, “the gifts from the god” (19.3), as the old armor was the gods' gift to Peleus (18.84; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). She finds her son lying over Patroclus, weeping (19.4–6; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]), and gives him the armor:

{{quote:dbaf83a1-a35e-5302-92c8-527b30e1909d}}

**The arms ring out.**

{{quote:66a95f25-eb5a-5054-b836-94e33fd3929f}}

Hephaestus promised armor “such that many a man / of the many men will marvel at it, whoever sees it” (18.466–467). Now the Myrmidons cannot even look at it, and only Achilles looks, with rising anger and blazing eyes, and delights in it (19.14–19; see [[achilles]] and [[myrmidons]]). The δαίδαλα, “intricate designs”, are the work of the god's craft, on the shield above all (18.479–482; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Achilles' first words judge the maker: “the armor the god has given is such as is fitting / for the works of immortals, and no mortal man could make it” (19.21–22; see [[hephaestus]]). Then he says that he will arm (19.23).

**The end of the episode.** Thetis drips ambrosia and nectar into Patroclus' nostrils to keep his body sound (19.38–39). After 39 a few manuscripts add a line, which Walter Leaf thinks may survive from a time when the rhapsody of the Making of the Arms, the Ὁπλοποιία, ended here instead of at the end of Book 18 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.39; see the translation's note on 19.38–39 and [[title-of-book-18]]).

**The arms put on.** After the assembly and the fast, Achilles arms in the midst of the army: “he put on the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus had toiled to make” (19.368), greaves, corselet, sword, shield and helmet, with its plumes “of gold, which Hephaestus had set thick about the crest” (19.369–383; see [[arming-scene]]). Leaf thinks that 368 was altered, and that 383, here and in Book 22, was added, to bring in an allusion to the making of the arms (on 19.365, 19.382; see the translation's notes on 19.365–368 and 19.382–383). The shield shines like the moon and like a fire seen by sailors at sea, the helmet like a star, and the armor fits him so that it is like wings to him (19.373–386). The spear alone is not new. It is his father's, the Pelian ash that Chiron gave to Peleus, the one piece of Achilles' arms that Patroclus did not take (19.387–391, with 19.388–391 = 16.141–144; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).

**The god's limit.** Hephaestus wished that he could hide Achilles from death as surely as he would give him armor (18.464–467). When the armor is on and the horses are yoked, the horse Xanthus tells Achilles that the day of his destruction is near (19.409; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[xanthus-the-horse]]).
