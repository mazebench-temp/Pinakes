He had hoped that Patroclus would outlive him and bring the boy from Scyros to Phthia, to show him his father's house (19.328–333).

The son is named in a lament for another (see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]). He is a child still being reared, and it was Patroclus who was to fetch him and show him “everything, / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (19.332–333; see [[scyros]] and [[phthia]]). εἴ που ἔτι ζώει γε, “if indeed … is still alive”, leaves Achilles uncertain of his son as he is of his father, whom he imagines dead or barely alive (19.334–337; see [[peleus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). θεοειδής, “godlike”, is a common epithet of heroes (see [[godlike]]). The Iliad's only other mention of the son is unnamed, when Hermes bids Priam supplicate Achilles by his father, his mother and his child (24.466–467).

Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus and Aristophanes of Byzantium athetized 19.327, on the ground that Achilles could not doubt that his son was alive when Scyros was so near Troy, and that θεοειδής did not suit him, and that Didymus gives a further ground, a variant that named the son Πυρῆς, perhaps the later Pyrrhus. Leaf suspects the whole passage, 19.326–333 or 337, as a later addition meant to bring a famous hero of the Cycle into the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.327; see [[aristarchus]] and the translation's note on 19.326–337). He sets 19.332 beside the Odyssey, where Odysseus tells the shade of Achilles that he himself brought Neoptolemus from Scyros in his ship to the Achaeans (*Odyssey* 11.506–509), and notes that here the boy is plainly thought too young to travel alone, let alone to fight (on 19.332).
