summary: While Achilles fasts for Patroclus, Zeus rebukes Athena for abandoning her man and sends her with nectar and ambrosia. Eager already, she leaps down from the sky like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey, drips them into his breast so that hunger may not come upon his knees, and goes back to her father's house.

**Zeus' rebuke.** While Achilles mourns Patroclus and will not eat, Zeus pities him and the elders who weep with him, and turns to Athena (19.338–341): “My child, you have utterly abandoned your brave man. / Is Achilles then no longer at all in your mind? / There he sits before the straight-horned ships, / mourning his dear companion; and the others / have gone to their meal, while he is unfed and has tasted nothing” (19.342–346; see [[zeus]], [[achilles]] and [[fasting]]). τέκνον ἐμόν, “My child”, is how Zeus answers her in the Odyssey when she pleads for Odysseus (*Odyssey* 1.64, 5.22). ἀνδρὸς ἑῆος, “your brave man”, takes ἑῆος in Aristarchus' sense, “good, brave”; Zenodotus read ἑοῖο, “your own” (see the translation's note on 19.342). She has been Achilles' helper before: in Book 1 she seized him by the hair and held him back from killing Agamemnon (1.194–222), and in Book 18 she flung the aegis around his shoulders and set a golden cloud about his head (18.203–206; see [[aegis]]). Now Zeus tells her to “drip nectar and lovely ambrosia / into his breast, so that hunger may not come upon him” (19.347–348; see [[nectar]] and [[ambrosia]]).

**Like a bird of prey.**

{{quote:7892c043-89e9-58aa-b6f5-ebd557a99a41}}

“So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already” is the verse with which Zeus sends her down to break the truce in Book 4 (4.73), and will send her to Achilles' side against Hector (22.186); in the Odyssey it sends her to make peace in Ithaca (*Odyssey* 24.487). ἅρπη, “a bird of prey”, is named only here in Homer, and cannot be identified (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.350; see [[harpe]], [[like-a-bird-of-prey]] and the translation's note on 19.350). Gods come and go like birds in Homer: Athena and Apollo sit on the oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures” (7.58–59), Apollo goes down from Ida “like a hawk” (15.237), and Thetis leaps down from Olympus like a hawk with the armor (18.616; see [[like-a-hawk]]); in the Odyssey Athena departs in the likeness of a φήνη, another bird of prey (*Odyssey* 3.371–372). Whether the god takes the bird's shape or only moves like it is debated (see the translation's note on 7.59).

**Food for the fasting.** She drips nectar and ambrosia into Achilles' breast, “so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees” (19.354). Odysseus had warned that a man who fights unfed finds “thirst and hunger / overtake him, and his knees are hindered as he goes” (19.165–166); λιμός, “hunger”, occurs in the Iliad only in this book (19.166, 19.348, 19.354). No other living man in Homer is sustained with the gods' food and drink; Thetis dripped them into the nostrils of the dead Patroclus (19.38–39; see [[thetis]]). Then “she herself went to the well-built house of her mighty father” (19.355; see [[eristhenes]]), and the Achaeans poured out from the ships to arm (19.356–364).
