---
summary: Earth in person is a witness of Agamemnon's oath, with Zeus, the Sun and the Erinyes, who punish perjurers beneath the earth. In Odysseus' saying the bronze strews the ground with straw, and when the army arms for Achilles' return, all the earth around laughs under the flash of the bronze.
---

**A witness.** Over the boar Agamemnon swears: “Now let Zeus be witness first, highest and best of the gods, / and Earth and the Sun and the Erinyes, who beneath the earth / punish men, whoever has sworn a false oath” (19.258–260; see [[oath]], [[zeus]], [[helios]] and [[erinyes]]). Γῆ, the short form of the name, is Earth called on in person, as when Menelaus asked for two lambs “for Earth and the Sun” at the truce of Book 3 (Γῇ τε καὶ Ἠελίῳ, 3.103–104); the earth beneath which the Erinyes punish is γαῖα, in the same verse. Walter Leaf calls Γῆ for γαῖα late epic, and observes that Brandreth's easy correction here, γαῖα καὶ ἠέλιος, would leave the same phrase of Book 3 unexplained (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.259; see the translation's note on 19.258–260). In Book 15 Hera swore by Earth and the wide Heaven and the water of Styx (15.36–37; see the note on Book 15).

**The ground.** For Odysseus, battle is a harvest “in which the bronze strews the most straw on the ground, / but the harvest is smallest” (χθονί, 19.222–223; see [[satiety-of-battle]]). Other words for the ground stand in the book as well: Achilles wishes Briseis had died, so that fewer Achaeans would have “bitten the boundless ground with their teeth” (οὖδας, 19.59–61; see [[artemis]]), and Blind Folly's feet “are tender, for she does not step / upon the ground, but walks over the heads of men” (οὔδει, 19.92–93; see [[ate]]).

**The earth laughed.** When the Achaeans arm, “the gleam reached the sky, and all the earth around laughed / under the flash of the bronze” (γέλασσε δὲ πᾶσα περὶ χθών, 19.362–363; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]] and [[sky]]). Leaf finds that the ideas of laughing and shining pass so naturally into each other that γέλασσε cannot be confined to the sense of shining, though that is the original one (on 19.362). The earth laughs in the Homeric *Hymns* too: at the scent of the narcissus the whole wide sky above, all the earth and the salt swell of the sea laughed (*Hymn to Demeter* 13–14), and when Leto gave birth to Apollo on Delos, the earth beneath smiled (*Hymn to Apollo* 118).
