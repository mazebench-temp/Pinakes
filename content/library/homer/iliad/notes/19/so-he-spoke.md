summary: The book's speeches close on what they bring about. Thetis' words are followed by the armor set down before Achilles and the might she puts into him (19.12, 19.37); his renunciation by the army's joy (19.74); his last words to the army by the end of the assembly (19.276); Agamemnon's oath by the cutting of the boar's throat (19.266); the laments of Briseis and Achilles by the groaning of the women and of the elders (19.301, 19.338); and Xanthus' prophecy by the Erinyes, who check the horse's voice (19.418).
grc: ["ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντος"]
---

In Agamemnon's story the formulas pass into a speaker's mouth. Hera's two speeches to Zeus end “So she spoke” (19.112, 19.125), and Zeus' oath ends “So saying, he hurled her from the starry sky” (19.130). Walter Leaf notes that ὣς εἰπών elsewhere always follows the actual words of a speaker, not, as here, another man's summary of them (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.130; see [[ate]]). Zeus' charge to Athena ends in the verse with which he sent her to break the truce in Book 4, “So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already” (19.349 = 4.73).

The two laments end alike: “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” (19.301) and “So he spoke, weeping, and the elders groaned with him” (19.338). In each the mourners weep for their own as well, the women “each for her own sorrows”, the elders “each remembering what he had left in his halls” (19.302, 19.339; see [[lament]]). The half-verse ἐπὶ δὲ στενάχοντο, “groaned with her”, closes the laments for Hector in the last books (22.429, 22.515, 24.722, 24.746).

The last speech of the book is not closed by its speaker. ὣς ἄρα φωνήσαντος, “So he spoke”, is a genitive absolute, “when he had spoken so”, and what follows is not the horse's doing: “the Erinyes checked his voice” (19.418; see [[erinyes]] and [[xanthus-the-horse]]). Achilles' answer ends with the shortest form, “He spoke” (ἦ ῥα, 19.424), and the book with his cry as he drives out.
