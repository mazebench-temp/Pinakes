**Delivered.** Agamemnon tells Odysseus to bring the gifts from his ship “and bring the women” (19.195), and the young men lead them out of his hut: “and quickly they led out women skilled in flawless handiwork, / seven, and the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis” (19.245–246; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[briseis]]). The seven are the women of Lesbos whom Agamemnon chose out for himself when Achilles took Lesbos, offered in Book 9 in the same words (9.128–130, 9.270–272; see [[lesbos]] and [[achilles-raids]]): the gift gives back to Achilles what he won. They come between the horses and the gold, and are stored with them: “And they set them in the huts, and seated the women, / and the noble attendants drove the horses to the herd” (19.280–281; see [[myrmidons]]).

**A captive's story.** Briseis' lament is the only speech of a captive woman in the Iliad, and it tells the captive's side: her husband killed before the city, her three brothers dead, her city sacked by Achilles, and the consolation offered by his companion, a marriage to the man who did it (19.291–299; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]] and [[marriage]]). The Odyssey tells the same scene from the outside, in its simile of the wife who throws herself on her husband fallen before his city and is driven off into slavery with blows of the spear (*Odyssey* 8.523–530; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

**Each for her own sorrows.**

{{quote:231e7d98-7b7e-5095-9165-60d08afb65f0}}

Walter Leaf, following Heyne, takes πρόφασιν not as a pretense covering a grief the women dare not show, but as the occasion that wakes a grief of their own, as the elders who groan with Achilles each remember what they left at home (19.338–339), and as the women's cries over Patroclus in Book 18 are evidently meant to be sincere (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.302; see the translation's note on 19.302 and [[lament]]). In Book 18 the slave women whom Achilles and Patroclus had taken cried aloud at the news and beat their breasts, and Achilles promised that captive women would weep for Patroclus night and day (18.28–31, 18.339–342); here they weep for him.
