---
summary: Odysseus says Diomedes' verse again when the quarrel ends. Achilles wants the army to fight at once; Odysseus answers, “But bid the Achaeans eat by the swift ships / of food and wine; for in that is might and courage” (19.160–161), and argues it: no man can fight all day unfed, while a man who has eaten and drunk fights with a bold heart until the end (19.162–170).

The second verse is Diomedes' (19.161 = 9.706), and the speech around it argues what Diomedes only stated. Odysseus begins with the verse with which Agamemnon began his answer to Achilles in Book 1, “Not like this, godlike Achilles, brave as you are” (19.155 = 1.131); Walter Leaf notes that the thought is the same, do not expect all men to do what you can (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.155). The battle will be long, “once the battalions / of men first engage, and a god breathes might into both sides” (19.158–159; see [[menos]]): the might that a god gives must be kept up with food.

Achilles refuses to eat until Patroclus is avenged (19.199–214), and Odysseus answers again that the army cannot mourn with the belly (19.225–231; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[satiety-of-battle]]). The argument is settled in the gods' way. The hunger that, Odysseus said, makes a fasting man's knees heavy (19.166) is kept from Achilles' knees by Athena, who drips nectar and ambrosia into his breast (19.354; see [[gounata]], [[fasting]] and [[ambrosia]]). The man who will not eat is fed by the gods.
