**Two deaths.** Hector's spear strikes Eioneus in the neck below his helmet, “and his limbs were loosened” (λύντο δὲ γυῖα, 7.12); Glaucus strikes Iphinous in the shoulder as he leaps onto his chariot, “and he fell from the chariot to the ground, and his limbs were loosened” (7.16; see [[androktasia]], [[eioneus]] and [[iphinous]]). The half-verse turns λῦσε δὲ γυῖα, “he loosened his limbs”, into the passive: the limbs give way of themselves, and no killer is named in it. It returns once for a man who falls from a ship's stern (15.435), and as ὑπέλυντο δὲ γυῖα when Peneleos half severs Lycon's head (16.341).

**Weariness.** The same words describe the living. In the simile that brings Hector and Paris back into the battle, the Trojans long for them as rowers long for a fair wind, “when they have grown weary with polished oars of fir, rowing over the sea, and their limbs are loosened with weariness” (καμάτῳ δʼ ὑπὸ γυῖα λέλυνται, 7.5–6; see [[the-fair-wind]]). The phrase that ends men's lives on the field ends the rowers' strength at the oar, as Agamemnon spoke of the weariness that “takes” a man's limbs (γυῖα λάβῃ κάματος, 4.230). In the Odyssey Odysseus says the same of himself, worn out by the waves (Od. 8.230–233).

**Knees.** Ajax's stone does to Hector what a killing blow does, and he lives: “and made his knees give way; and he was stretched out on his back” (7.271), until Apollo sets him on his feet (see [[divine-rescue]] and [[gounata]]).
