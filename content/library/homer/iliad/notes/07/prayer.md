summary: The army prays for Ajax twice, at the lots and while he arms, and Ajax bids them pray in silence or aloud (7.177–205). Nestor imagines old Peleus lifting his hands to pray for death, and Hector imagines the Trojan women going to the gods praying for him (7.130–131, 7.298).

**Silent or aloud.** While he arms, Ajax asks the army to pray to Zeus “in silence among yourselves, so that the Trojans do not hear, or else openly, since in any case we fear no one” (7.194–196), and adds a boast of his strength and skill (7.197–199). The five verses are reported to have been athetized in antiquity, the change of mind and the boast being thought unfitting. Without them the prayer follows at once on his request (7.194, 7.200), and as it stands it is spoken aloud (7.201–205; see [[in-silence-or-openly]], [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[silence]]).

**Lifted hands.** “And the people prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods” (7.177). Nestor imagines the same gesture for old Peleus if he heard that the Achaeans all cower before Hector: “he would lift up his hands again and again to the immortals, that his spirit might go from his limbs down into the house of Hades” (7.130–131; see [[peleus]]). The old man's prayer would be for his own death, out of shame for the army of his son. Nestor's own wish to be young, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (7.132), has the invocation of a prayer and asks nothing of the gods (see [[if-only-father-zeus]]).

**The women.** Proposing to end the duel, Hector imagines his return to a city where the Trojan women “will enter the divine assembly, praying for me” (εὐχόμεναι, 7.298). θεῖος ἀγών is usually taken as the sanctuary where the images of the gods stand together, or as the gathering of worshippers there (see [[temple]]). In Book 6 the women's prayer for the city was refused (6.311); here Hector imagines them praying for him as he comes home alive.

**Triumph.** εὖχος, “triumph”, is formed from the root of εὔχομαι, which means “claim, boast” as well as “pray”. Hector hopes that Apollo will give him triumph (7.81), Nestor says that Athena gave it to him against Ereuthalion (7.154), and the army prays that Ajax may win “a splendid triumph” (7.203). The word is not counted here as a prayer; the verse of 7.203 is counted as part of the army's prayer (see [[euchomai]]). The night ends with a rite that needs no words, the libation poured to Zeus in fear of his thunder (7.480–481; see [[libation]]).
