In the first books λώβη was an outrage that one man did to another: Agamemnon's to Achilles (1.232), and in Thersites' mouth to the army (2.242). In Book 3 Hector called his brother “a disgrace like this” (3.42), a man who challenged the best of the Achaeans and then shrank from the one who came (3.15–37; see the note on Book 3). Now the Achaeans are in Paris' place. A challenge has been made before both armies, and if no one answers it the army itself will be the disgrace. λώβη here is predicate, with a future verb: “surely this will be a disgrace” (7.97). The outrage is to the army's standing, and it is done by its own silence (see [[aidos]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]).

αἰνόθεν αἰνῶς, “dreadful beyond dread”, found only here, doubles its word as οἰόθεν οἶος, “man to man, alone”, doubles “alone”, twice in this book and both times of the duel (7.39, 7.226). The rest of the speech makes the disgrace concrete: the men are “Achaean women” (see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]), they may as well turn to water and earth, and they sit “without glory” (see [[water-and-earth]] and [[kleos]]). Menelaus is the man who suffered the outrage that began the war, and he is the one who stands up to answer Hector. Agamemnon holds him back, since Hector is far the stronger (7.104–119; see [[menelaus]]).
