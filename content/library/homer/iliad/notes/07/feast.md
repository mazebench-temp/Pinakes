**The chine.** After the sacrifice the chiefs feast in Agamemnon's huts, in the verses of the feasts at Chryse and before the first battle: “And when they had ceased from the work and made the feast ready, they feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast” (7.319–320 = 1.467–468, 2.430–431; see [[equal-feast]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). Then the equal feast makes room for honor:

{{quote:de6f5be7-f8a0-5318-b1bd-d7e03afdc0fe}}

νῶτα are the meat along the backbone, and διηνεκής is “running right through”: the chine in its whole length is the portion of honor. The same verse, with Odysseus' name, tells how Eumaeus honors his guest (Od. 14.437), and in Sparta Menelaus hands his guests the chine of an ox that had been set before him as his own portion of honor (Od. 4.65–66). γεραίρω, “honor”, is the verb of the γέρας, the prize (see [[geras]] and [[ajax]]). The man who fought for the army is honored at the king's table, as Agamemnon honors his best men in Book 4 with the full cup and the first call to the feast (4.257–263, 4.343–346). Plato's Socrates took the chine of Ajax as Homer's model for honoring the brave (see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]).

**Counsel after the meal.** “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (7.323), Nestor begins to weave his plan for the truce and the wall (7.324–343). The four verses that bring him in return in Book 9, where the elders' feast leads to his proposal of the embassy to Achilles (7.323–326 = 9.92–95; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]] and [[with-good-intent]]).

**Supper in Troy.** Priam ends the stormy Trojan assembly with the evening meal: “Now take your supper throughout the city, as before” (7.370). δόρπον is the supper, and they take it “throughout the army, by companies” (7.380), with a watch set for the night (7.371; see [[assembly]]).

**All night.** On the evening of the wall the Achaeans slaughter oxen and take their supper among the huts (7.466), buy wine from the Lemnian ships, “and they set out a rich feast” (7.475; see [[rich-feast]], [[trade]] and [[wine]]). “All night long then the long-haired Achaeans feasted, and the Trojans and their allies throughout the city” (7.476–477). The two sides feast on the same night, as they burned their dead on the same day, and Zeus thunders over both feasts until they pour out their wine to him in fear (7.478–482; see [[teras]] and [[libation]]).
