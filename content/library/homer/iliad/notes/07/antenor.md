summary: In the Trojan assembly on the night after the duel, Antenor proposes to give Helen and her possessions back to the sons of Atreus, since the Trojans are fighting after breaking the oaths. Paris refuses, and Priam passes the proposal over.

**The assembly.** The Trojans meet at nightfall “on the height of the city of Ilios”, beside Priam's doors, in an assembly “dread and turbulent” (δεινὴ τετρηχυῖα, 7.345–346; see [[assembly]] and [[priams-palace]]); the Achaean assembly of Book 2 was “in uproar” in the same word (τετρήχει, 2.95). Antenor speaks first, and the narrator calls him “wise” (7.347; see [[wise]]), as he and Ucalegon were “both wise men” on the wall (3.148).

**Agamemnon's terms.** His first line is the one with which Agamemnon addressed the Trojan side after the duel of Book 3, “Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies” (3.456; see [[hear-me]] and [[dardanians]]), and his proposal is the substance of Agamemnon's demand: “so give back Argive Helen and the possessions with her” (3.458). The Trojan elder puts to his own people what the enemy king demanded of them, without the satisfaction (τιμή) that Agamemnon asked for as well (3.459; see [[argive-helen]] and [[possessions]]).

**The broken oaths.** His reason is given in one clause: νῦν δʼ ὅρκια πιστὰ ψευσάμενοι μαχόμεσθα, “for now, having played false with the trusty oaths, we are fighting” (7.351–352). ψεύδομαι, “lie, be false”, is the verb of Agamemnon's maxim that “father Zeus will be no helper to liars” (4.235; see [[no-helper-to-liars]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). That morning Hector had laid the failure of the oaths on Zeus, “the son of Cronus, throned on high, did not fulfill our oaths” (7.69; see [[hector]]), and said nothing of Pandarus' arrow. Antenor says plainly that the Trojans broke them, and draws the consequence: “I do not expect that anything better will come to pass for us, unless we do this” (7.352–353). ἵνα μὴ ῥέξομεν ὧδε is unusual Greek; ἵνα seems to mean “in which case” (see the translation's note on 7.353).

**Refused.** Paris answers that if Antenor means it, “then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits” (7.360), and he will give back the possessions but not the woman (7.357–364; see [[paris]]). Priam does not take up the proposal. He sends Paris' offer to the Achaeans and asks for a truce to burn the dead (7.366–378; see [[priam]]). Yet the herald tells the Achaeans that the Trojans “urge” Paris to give Helen up (7.393; see [[idaeus]]), so Antenor did not speak for himself alone. Diomedes answers that even a fool can see that “the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon the Trojans” (7.401–402; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]).

**Two counsels.** Antenor had received Menelaus and Odysseus as guests when they came to Troy to ask for Helen (3.205–207; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). Another Trojan, Antimachus, urged the assembly then to kill Menelaus on the spot (11.138–142), and later took gold from Paris and would not let Helen be given back (11.123–125). Antenor's counsel of Book 7 is the one the Trojans do not take.
