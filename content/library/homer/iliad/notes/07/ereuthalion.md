**Hector's forerunner.** Nestor tells the story to shame the Achaeans who sit silent before Hector's challenge, and he makes Ereuthalion Hector's forerunner (see [[nestor]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). He “stood forth as champion” (πρόμος ἵστατο, 7.136): πρόμος is the word of the book's duel, Hector's word for the man the Achaeans are to send out against him (7.75), and Agamemnon's for the one they will set up in Menelaus' place (7.116; see [[single-combat]]). “Wearing his armor he challenged all the best” (7.150), as Ajax will say of Hector, “it was he who challenged all the best to battle” (7.285); both verses end in πάντας ἀρίστους. The Pylians answered as the Achaeans do now: “they trembled greatly and were afraid, and no one dared” (7.151; see [[pylians]]). He is ἰσόθεος φώς, “a man equal to the gods” (7.136; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]), the praise of an enemy whose defeat makes the victor's name.

**The armor.** Nestor spends more verses on the armor than on the man. Ares gave it to Areithous the mace-man; Lycurgus killed Areithous by guile and wore it in battle; and when Lycurgus grew old he gave it to Ereuthalion, “his dear attendant”, to wear (7.137–149; see [[areithous]] and [[lycurgus-of-arcadia]]). Ereuthalion goes out in a god's gift that has already passed through two men's hands, and it did not save its first owner.

**The giant.** “He was the tallest and the strongest man I ever killed; for he lay sprawled out, huge, this way and that” (7.155–156). παρήορος, “sprawled out”, is the word for a trace horse, harnessed loose beside the yoked pair (16.474), and when the trace horse Pedasus is killed in Book 16, he too “lay sprawled” in the dust (κεῖτο παρήορος, 16.471). In Book 4 Nestor named this day in a single line, as the measure of the strength he has lost (4.318–319; see [[old-age-and-memory]]).
