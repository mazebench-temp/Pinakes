**Two rebukes.** Hector's challenge is met by silence, and two men rebuke the army. Menelaus stands up “reviling them with reproaches” (7.95): the Achaeans are “braggarts, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer”, their silence will be a disgrace, and he curses them to turn to water and earth where they sit (7.96–100; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]], [[lobe]] and [[water-and-earth]]). He arms himself, and no one follows. Nestor's rebuke is gentler in form and longer. He does not insult the men; he sets them before an imagined father, Peleus, who would pray to die if he heard of them, and before a story of his own youth, when he alone faced Ereuthalion (7.124–160; see [[penthos]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Only at the end does he turn on them: not even the best of them are eager to face Hector (7.159–160). “So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (7.161; see [[neikos]]). The rebuke that works is the one that shames by comparison, not by abuse (see [[aidos]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**Challenge and retort.** Before the duel the champions trade the words of challenge. Ajax tells Hector that he will now learn what kind of chiefs the Danaans have, even after Achilles (7.226–232), and Hector answers that he is not a feeble boy or a woman to be tested, and knows the works of war (7.234–243; see [[the-works-of-war]] and [[single-combat]]). Neither insults the other beyond that; each praises himself.

**Praise from an enemy.** At the end of the duel Hector praises Ajax: a god has given him “stature and strength and good sense, and with the spear you are the mightiest of the Achaeans” (7.288–289; see [[strength-is-a-gift]] and [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). He proposes gifts so that both armies may speak of them together (7.299–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). Among the Achaeans the praise is given in the language of honor, the chine at Agamemnon's feast (7.321; see [[geras]]), and in the army's welcome, leading Ajax “exulting in his victory” (7.312). The Trojans receive Hector with joy that he is alive (7.306–310). Praise and blame, in this book, are what move men to fight and what they are paid when they have fought.
