summary: Agamemnon seats the Achaeans for Hector's challenge, holds Menelaus back from answering it, stands first of the nine who volunteer, and has the lots cast in his own helmet. After the duel he sacrifices an ox and honors Ajax with the chine; the next morning he rejects Paris' offer but grants the Trojans a truce to burn their dead (7.57, 7.107–122, 7.162–180, 7.312–322, 7.405–412).

**The elder brother.** He seats the Achaeans as Hector seats the Trojans (7.55–57). When Menelaus arms to answer the challenge, Agamemnon takes him by the right hand and, in the narrator's words to Menelaus, “called you by name” (7.108; see [[called-him-by-name]]):

{{quote:d3d7b0a7-bf5c-566a-8f50-c67948c29fd2}}

The offer is ἀφροσύνη, “madness”, a word found only here in the Iliad, and made “out of strife” with a better man. Hector is one “whom others too dread”, a phrase later used of Zeus himself (7.112; 15.167, 15.183). The claim that even Achilles “shudders to meet this man” is usually taken as an exaggeration, meant to keep Menelaus out of the fight: Achilles says that while he fought, Hector came out no farther than the Scaean gates and the oak, and once barely escaped him there (9.352–355; see [[achilles]]). Agamemnon ends with a forecast about Hector, that he will be glad to rest “if he escapes from destructive war and grim combat” (7.118–119). The narrator approves: he persuaded his brother, “urging what was due” (7.121), the words that ended his rebuke of Menelaus over the suppliant Adrastus (6.62; see [[aisa]] and [[menelaus]]).

**First of the nine.** When Nestor's story brings the leaders to their feet, “first by far rose Agamemnon, lord of men” (7.162; see [[lord-of-men]]). The lots go into his helmet (7.176; see [[lots]] and [[helmet]]), and the army prays that the lot fall to Ajax, to Diomedes, “or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold” (7.179–180; see [[mycenae]]). It falls to Ajax.

**The host.** The Achaeans lead Ajax, “exulting in his victory”, to Agamemnon (7.312). He sacrifices “a male, five years old, to the son of Cronus, supreme in might” (7.314–315), as he sacrificed a five-year-old ox before the first battle (2.402–403; see [[sacrifice]] and [[feast]]), and he gives Ajax the portion of honor:

{{quote:de6f5be7-f8a0-5318-b1bd-d7e03afdc0fe}}

The verb, γέραιρεν, makes the chine a γέρας, a prize of honor (see [[geras]] and [[ajax]]). In Book 1 Agamemnon took the prize of the best of the Achaeans; here he gives one.

**The answer to Idaeus.** The next morning the Trojan herald finds the Achaeans in assembly by the stern of Agamemnon's ship and offers Paris' possessions, but not Helen (7.381–397; see [[idaeus]]). Diomedes speaks for the army, and Agamemnon makes Diomedes' word his own answer (see [[diomedes]]). The truce for the dead he grants on his own authority:

{{quote:0da0fd8b-f4c8-5a39-a114-50f7a5a3d910}}

He lifts his scepter to all the gods (7.412; see [[scepter]], [[oath]], [[the-comfort-of-fire]] and [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). The half-line ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, “the loud-thundering husband of Hera”, ends Hector's oath to Dolon, which he swears with the scepter in his hands (10.328–329). The book closes with a gift to him: Euneus of Lemnos sends the sons of Atreus a thousand measures of wine of their own (7.470–471; see [[euneus]]).
