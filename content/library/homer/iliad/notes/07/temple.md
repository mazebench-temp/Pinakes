**Armor on the temple.** If Hector wins, he will carry his opponent's armor “to sacred Ilios and hang it on the temple of Apollo the Far-Shooter” (7.82–83). Apollo's temple stands on Pergamus, the height of Troy, where the god laid Aeneas to be healed (5.445–448; see [[apollo]] and [[pergamus]]). Armor hung up in a god's house is a share of the victory given to the god who granted it, and Hector has named Apollo as the god who may give him the triumph (7.81; see [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**The divine assembly.** Proposing to end the duel, Hector imagines the Trojan women “who will enter the divine assembly, praying for me” (θεῖον δύσονται ἀγῶνα, 7.298). ἀγών is a gathering, or the place where things are gathered: the ships drawn up on the beach are νεῶν ἀγών (15.428, 16.500). The “divine assembly” is usually taken as a sanctuary where the images of the gods stand together, or as the gathering of worshippers there. At 18.376 the same phrase is the assembly of the gods on Olympus, into which Hephaestus' self-moving tripods are to roll (see [[prayer]] and [[divine]]). In Book 6 the women went to Athena's temple on the citadel, and their prayer was refused (6.297–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]).
