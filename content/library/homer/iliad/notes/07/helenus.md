**The gods' plan.** Athena has come down to help the Achaeans, and Apollo meets her by the oak. They agree to stop the battle for the day by rousing Hector to challenge one of the Danaans “to fight him face to face in grim combat” (7.17–42; see [[apollo]], [[athena]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The next line takes their plan across to the Trojans: Helenus “understood in his heart the plan that pleased the gods as they took counsel” (7.44–45; see [[boule]]). The narrator gives him understanding; Helenus himself says he heard: “so I have heard the voice of the everlasting gods” (7.53). Whether he overheard the two gods or speaks of a seer's insight as hearing is not said (see [[prophecy]] and [[divination]]).

**The message.** He passes on Apollo's words almost exactly. Apollo proposed that Hector challenge a Danaan ἀντίβιον μαχέσασθαι ἐν αἰνῇ δηϊοτῆτι (7.40), and Helenus gives Hector the same verse, “to fight you face to face in grim combat” (7.51). He adds an assurance that is not in the gods' conversation as the poem reports it: “for it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end” (7.52; see [[moira]]). He addresses Hector as “the equal of Zeus in counsel” (7.47; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]), the verse with which Iris will bring Hector a message from Zeus himself (11.200), and he claims a brother's hearing: “will you listen to me now? For I am your brother” (7.48; see [[kasignetos]]).

**Obeyed again.** In Book 6 Helenus sent Hector into the city, and Hector did not disobey him (6.102). Here “Hector rejoiced greatly when he heard his words”: he goes into the middle, holds back the Trojan battalions with his spear gripped by the middle, and they sit down (7.54–56; see [[hector]]). The seer is proved right. Hector's challenge brings out Ajax, who wounds him in the neck and knocks him flat, but Apollo sets him on his feet, and he comes back to the Trojans “alive and unhurt” (7.262, 7.271–272, 7.308; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). Helenus has no further part in the book.
