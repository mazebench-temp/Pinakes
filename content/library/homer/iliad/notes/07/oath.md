**Hector's account.** Hector begins his challenge with the fate of the truce of Book 3: “The son of Cronus, throned on high, did not fulfill our oaths, but, meaning evil, he ordains it for both sides” (7.69–70). The oaths were to end the war with the duel; Zeus did not bring them to that end, and the war goes on until one side or the other falls (7.71–72). Hector says nothing of Pandarus' arrow, which Agamemnon called the Trojans' breach of the oaths (4.155–168; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). For his own duel he offers no oaths and no victims, only a god to hear his word: “And this I declare, and let Zeus be our witness” (Ζεὺς δʼ ἄμμʼ ἐπιμάρτυρος ἔστω, 7.76; see [[single-combat]]). ἐπιμάρτυρος is found only here in the Iliad; the gods of Book 3 were μάρτυροι, “witnesses”, of the oaths (3.280).

**Antenor's account.** In the Trojan assembly Antenor names the breach from the Trojan side: “for now, having played false with the trusty oaths, we are fighting; and so I do not expect that anything better will come to pass for us, unless we do this” (7.351–353). ψευσάμενοι, “having played false”, is the verb of lies (see [[pseudos]]). What Agamemnon said over his wounded brother, a Trojan elder now says in Troy, and he proposes to give Helen back; Paris refuses (7.357–364; see [[antenor]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

**Agamemnon's oath.** Agamemnon grants the Trojans their truce for the dead with an oath: “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (ὅρκια δὲ Ζεὺς ἴστω, 7.411), “so saying, he lifted up his scepter to all the gods” (7.412; see [[scepter]] and [[truce]]). ἴστω, “let him know”, is the word with which oaths call the gods to witness, and Hector swears to Dolon in Book 10 in nearly the same verse, holding the scepter in his hands (10.321–331). There are no lambs, no wine and no curse, as there were in Book 3 (3.264–301). The oath is a word and a gesture, and this time it holds: both armies burn their dead in peace (7.421–432).
