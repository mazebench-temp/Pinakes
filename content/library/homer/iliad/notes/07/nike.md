**Victory put off.** Athena comes down from Olympus when she sees the Trojans killing Argives, and Apollo, who “wanted victory for the Trojans”, meets her at the oak (7.17–22). He asks whether she has come to give the Danaans “the victory that turns the battle” (ἑτεραλκέα νίκην, 7.26). The adjective is made of ἕτερος and ἀλκή, “strength for one of two sides”, and it is taken either as a decisive victory or as one that turns the fight toward the side that was losing (see the translation's note on 7.26). The two gods agree to stop the war “for today” and to rouse Hector to a single combat (7.29–42; see [[athena]] and [[apollo]]).

**Victory in the gods' hands.** Menelaus arms to face Hector knowing that “the bounds of victory are held among the immortal gods” (7.101–102; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]). When the lot leaps out of the helmet, Ajax rejoices, “since I think I will defeat brilliant Hector” (νικησέμεν, 7.192), and the army prays for him:

The prayer asks for two outcomes, and the second is the one granted. The duel ends with neither man killed and both honored (see [[kydos]] and [[euchomai]]).

**No victor.** The heralds part the two at nightfall, and Hector proposes to stop “for today; afterward we will fight again, until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.291–292; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[daimon]]). Priam uses the same words when he asks for a truce, and Idaeus repeats them to the Achaeans (7.377–378, 7.396–397); the formula is found only in this book. Each side then treats the day as a success. The Trojans rejoice to see Hector come back “alive and unhurt”, though they had not hoped to see him safe (7.306–310), and the Achaeans lead Ajax to Agamemnon “exulting in his victory” (κεχαρηότα νίκῃ, 7.311–312), where he is honored with the chine (7.321; see [[geras]]). The phrase is found only here. Ajax had the better of the fight: his spear grazed Hector's neck and his stone knocked him flat, until Apollo set him on his feet (7.260–272). But no one has yielded, and the question of victory is left where Hector left it, with a god.

In Book 3 the victory was claimed aloud: Agamemnon declared that it clearly belonged to Menelaus (3.457), and the gods set the claim aside (4.13–19; see the notes on Books 3 and 4). In Book 7 no one claims it in words. See [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]] and [[single-combat]].
