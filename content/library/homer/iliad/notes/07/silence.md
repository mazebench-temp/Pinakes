summary: The Achaeans meet Hector's challenge with a silence of shame and fear, and Idaeus' message with the same verse of silence (7.92–93, 7.398). Ajax asks the army to pray for him in silence, so that the Trojans do not hear, and at the burning of the dead Priam forbids the Trojans to wail (7.195, 7.427).

**Shame and fear.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence; they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (7.92–93). The verse is the one that followed Hector's announcement of the duel in Book 3 (3.95), and here the narrator explains it: the Achaeans are held between shame (αἴδεσθεν) and fear (δεῖσαν; see [[aidos]] and [[deos]]). Menelaus breaks the silence with an insult (7.94–102), and Nestor with a story of another silence: when Ereuthalion challenged all the best of the Pylians, “they trembled greatly and were afraid, and no one dared”, until the youngest of them went out to fight him (7.150–154; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]).

**A prayer in silence.** Ajax asks the army to pray to Zeus for him “in silence among yourselves, so that the Trojans do not hear, or else openly, since in any case we fear no one” (7.195–196). A silent prayer is a precaution against the enemy, and he takes it back in the same breath. The five verses are reported to have been athetized in antiquity, and the prayer that follows is spoken aloud (7.200–205; see [[in-silence-or-openly]] and [[prayer]]).

**Silence again.** Idaeus' message from Troy is met with the same verse as Hector's challenge, and this time Diomedes breaks it, “at last” (7.398–399; see [[assembly]]).

**The silent burning.** At the burning of the dead “great Priam did not allow them to wail; and in silence they heaped the dead on the pyre, grieving at heart” (7.427–428), and the Achaeans heap their own dead in the same verse (7.431; see [[lament]] and [[cremation]]).
