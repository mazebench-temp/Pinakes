---
summary: Hades is named twice in Book 7. Nestor imagines old Peleus praying to go down into his house if he heard of the Achaeans' cowardice, and after the duel he counts the dead of the day, whose souls have gone down to Hades.
---

**A wish for death.** Nestor shames the Achaeans, who sit silent before Hector's challenge, with an old man's grief. Peleus once questioned him about the lineage of all the Argives and rejoiced to hear it; if he heard that they all now cower before Hector, “he would lift up his hands again and again to the immortals, / that his spirit might go from his limbs down into the house of Hades” (7.125–131; see [[peleus]] and [[nestor]]). δόμον Ἄϊδος εἴσω, “into the house of Hades”, is the phrase of the armies' prayer at the lots in Book 3, that the man who began the war might go down there (3.322; see [[psyche]]). Here the one who would pray for death is an old man grieved by the cowardice of the young (see [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**The day's dead.** At the feast after the duel Nestor proposes the truce for the dead: many long-haired Achaeans are dead, “whose dark blood about the fair-flowing Scamander / sharp Ares has now scattered, and their souls have gone down to Hades” (7.328–330; see [[ares]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The body is left on the field and the soul goes below, as in the proem (1.3–5). The truce that follows is for the bodies: both armies gather, wash and burn them (7.421–432; see [[cremation]]). Ἄϊδος δέ, “to Hades”, is the genitive, “to the house of Hades”, with the suffix -δε of direction, which the Oxford text prints as a separate word, as in Οὔλυμπον δέ, “to Olympus” (8.12).
