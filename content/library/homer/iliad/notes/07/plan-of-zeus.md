summary: Zeus does not act in Book 7 until its last lines. Hector says that Zeus means evil for both sides, and the book ends with Zeus thundering all night, “devising evil” for the armies, on the eve of the day when he begins to give the Trojans the victory he promised Thetis (7.69–72, 7.476–482).
passages:
  - 7.476-482 | Zeus thunders through the night
---

Since the lying Dream of Book 2 the plan has been out of sight. Through Books 3 to 7 the Achaeans hold their own, and in Book 7 the gods who act are Athena, Apollo and Poseidon, while Zeus is only called on: in prayers, as a witness of oaths, and by the heralds, “messengers of Zeus and of men” (7.274), who part the champions (see [[zeus]]). Hector names the plan without knowing it. Zeus did not fulfill the oaths, he says, “but, meaning evil, he ordains it for both sides” (7.70; see the translation's note on 7.69–70).

The book ends with Zeus himself, in the night (see [[war-and-night]]):

{{quote:f233a103-34a1-5742-8956-6efeb72d8ce7}}

κακὰ μήδετο μητίετα Ζεύς, “Zeus the counselor devised evil” (7.478): the evil is for “them”, the two armies feasting in the camp and in the city. The thunder is a sign, and the men understand it well enough to fear it. They pour their wine on the ground, and no one drinks until he has poured a libation to Zeus (7.480–481; see [[libation]]).

The next morning Zeus forbids the gods to take part in the war, goes to Ida, and weighs the fates of the two armies; the Achaeans' day of doom sinks. He thunders from Ida and sends a flash among the Achaeans, “and pale fear took hold of them all” (8.77), much as it held them the night before (χλωρὸν δέος, 7.479; see [[pale-fear]]). Later that day he thunders three times from Ida, “giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (8.170–171). The plan resumes, and it brings what Book 2 foretold, sorrows and groaning “on Trojans and Danaans alike” (2.39–40).
