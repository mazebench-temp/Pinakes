---
summary: The Achaeans sit in shamed silence at Hector's challenge until Menelaus calls them “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (7.96). Nine leaders volunteer and the army prays for Ajax; after the duel they burn their dead, build the wall and ditch, buy wine from Lemnos, and feast through a night of Zeus' thunder.

**Shamed.** At Hector's challenge “they all fell hushed in silence; they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (7.92–93; see [[silence]] and [[aidos]]). Menelaus stands up and reviles them: ὤ μοι ἀπειλητῆρες Ἀχαιΐδες οὐκέτʼ Ἀχαιοί, “Ah me, you braggarts, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer!” (7.96; see [[menelaus]] and [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]). ἀπειλητῆρες, men of threats and boasts (ἀπειλαί), occurs only here in Homer; in Book 8 Agamemnon reminds the army of the boasts it made over its wine on Lemnos (8.229–234). The rest of the line is Thersites' insult to the army in Book 2 (2.235; see [[thersites]]). Ἀχαιΐδες is the feminine of the people's name, “Achaean women”. Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, shamed them in similar words, “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (5.787), and Agamemnon will repeat the line (8.228). Menelaus goes on to curse them: may they all turn to water and earth where they sit (7.99–100; see [[water-and-earth]]). Nestor adds shame of another kind: old Peleus would groan if he heard “that they all now cower before Hector” (7.125–131; see [[nestor]]). Then nine leaders stand up (7.161–169).

**The army's prayer.** When the nine have marked their lots, “the people prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods”, and each man said: “Father Zeus, let the lot fall to Ajax, or the son of Tydeus, or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold” (7.177–180; see [[lots]] and [[tis-speech]]). The lot “that they themselves wanted”, Ajax's, leaps from the helmet (7.182). While he arms they pray again, and now they allow for a draw: “but if you love Hector too and care for him, grant equal strength and glory to them both” (7.204–205; see [[prayer]] and [[ajax]]). The duel ends as the second prayer allows (7.273–305; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**The dead and the wall.** “Many long-haired Achaeans are dead,” Nestor tells the kings (7.328). At the truce they burn their dead, “grieving at heart” (7.430–432; see [[cremation]]), and before dawn “a chosen band of the Achaeans” heaps a single tomb over the pyre and builds the wall, with towers and gates and a staked ditch (7.433–441; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[tomb]]). The gods watch “the great work of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (7.444). Poseidon complains that they gave the gods no hecatombs (7.450), and Zeus promises that when they have gone home, the wall will be swept into the sea (7.459–463; see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]).

**Wine and slaves.** When the work was done they ate, and ships from Lemnos brought wine, which they bought “some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron, some with hides, and some with the cattle themselves, and some with slaves” (7.472–475; see [[lemnos]], [[euneus]] and [[trade]]). ἀνδράποδα, “slaves”, people taken in war and sold, are named only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 7.475). They feasted all night, and Zeus thundered; pale fear took hold of them, and no one dared drink before he had poured a libation (7.476–481; see [[libation]] and [[war-and-night]]).
