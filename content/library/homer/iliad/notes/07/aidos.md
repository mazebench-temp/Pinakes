**Shame against fear.** Hector has challenged the best of the Achaeans to come out “to be champion against brilliant Hector” (7.75), and promised the loser's body an honorable burial (7.76–91). The narrator gives the answer in two lines:

{{quote:4eeb9cc0-35e9-5074-aea9-960404c646d5}}

The second line is built as a balance, μέν … δέ. αἴδεσθεν, “they were ashamed”, is set against δεῖσαν, “they were afraid”, and each verb has its own infinitive, “to refuse” and “to accept”. Shame forbids one choice and fear the other, and the result is silence (see [[silence]] and [[deos]]). In the verse of the *Cypria* that Socrates quotes, αἰδώς goes with fear, “where there is fear, there is also αἰδώς” (Plato, *Euthyphro* 12a–c). Here the two part company: fear is felt before Hector, and αἰδώς before the challenger's eyes and the army's, since to refuse a challenge in public is a disgrace.

**Shame put into words.** The rebukes that follow name what the silent men already feel. Menelaus calls them “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer”, says that their refusal will be “a disgrace, dreadful beyond dread”, and curses them to sit “without glory” (7.96–100; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]], [[lobe]] and [[kleos]]). He shames them and cannot move them; only he stands up. Nestor shames them with a father's grief, that old Peleus would pray to die if he heard that they all cowered before Hector (7.124–131; see [[peleus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]), and with his own youth, when he alone dared to face Ereuthalion (7.150–154; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). “So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (7.161; see [[neikos]]). Later in the poem Ajax and Nestor rally the army with the words “be men, and put αἰδώς in your hearts” (αἰδῶ θέσθʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 15.561, 15.661), as Agamemnon told it in Book 5 that shame saves more men than it kills (5.529–532; see [[shame-saves-more]]).

**The challenger held.** The same pressure holds Hector. When Ajax comes out smiling behind his great shield, a dread trembling comes over the Trojans and Hector's heart pounds in his breast, “but he could no longer shrink back or slip away into the throng of the people, since he had made the challenge to battle” (7.215–218). The word αἰδώς is not used, but the constraint is the one he named to Andromache: he would feel shame before the Trojans if he shrank from the war like a coward (6.441–443), and he will say it again outside the Scaean gates, waiting for Achilles (22.105–107).
