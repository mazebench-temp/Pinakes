summary: Two scenes among the gods frame the book, and each is matched by a scene among men. Apollo and Athena, gods of opposite sides, agree to stop the fighting for a day, and at nightfall Hector and Ajax agree to stop in almost the same words; Hector wants a fame that will never perish, and Poseidon fears that his own will be forgotten.

**Two truces for a day.** Apollo, who wants victory for the Trojans, meets Athena, who has come to help the Danaans, and proposes: “let us now stop the war and the combat for today; afterward they will fight again, until they find the end of Ilios” (7.29–31). At nightfall Hector, who has fought the Achaeans' champion to a standstill, proposes to Ajax: “let us now cease from battle and combat for today; afterward we will fight again, until a god decides between us” (7.290–292). The gods' agreement and the men's are made in the same verse, νῦν μὲν παύσωμεν πόλεμον … σήμερον· ὕστερον αὖτε μαχήσοντʼ and νῦν μὲν παυσώμεσθα μάχης … σήμερον· ὕστερον αὖτε μαχησόμεθʼ. The gods speak of “they”, the men who will fight; Hector speaks of “we”. He does not know that he is carrying out the plan he echoes (see [[yield-to-night]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**Two fears for fame.** Hector's challenge imagines a mound on the Hellespont and a stranger reading it, and ends, “and my fame will never perish” (7.87–91). At the end of the book Poseidon looks at the Achaean wall and fears the opposite for himself: its fame will spread as far as the dawn, and men will forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon (7.451–453). The god, like the hero, lives in what men will say. Zeus answers him with the promise a hero makes to himself: “your fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads” (7.458). The difference is in the means. Hector can only hope that the mound will stand and be read; Poseidon can wash the Achaeans' wall away (7.459–463; see [[kleos]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
