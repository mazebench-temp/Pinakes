summary: Twice in Book 7 the narrator names a death that nearly happened at Hector's hands. Menelaus would have been killed had not the kings held him back (7.104–108), and Hector and Ajax would have fought on with swords had not the heralds come (7.273–276). Both times the rescuers are men.

**Menelaus.** “Then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have appeared at the hands of Hector, since he was far the mightier, had not the kings of the Achaeans sprung up and held you back, and the son of Atreus himself, wide-ruling Agamemnon, taken you by the right hand” (7.104–108). The narrator speaks to Menelaus at the moment he names the death that did not happen (see [[apostrophe]]), and he gives the reason plainly: ἐπεὶ πολὺ φέρτερος ἦεν, “since he was far the mightier”. Agamemnon gives the same reason in his speech, calling Hector “a man better than you” (7.111; see [[menelaus]] and [[agamemnon]]). Menelaus is one of the heroes whom the tradition brings home from Troy, and the Odyssey finds him in Sparta with Helen (Od. 4). The turn shows how near the story comes to a death it cannot tell.

**The swords.** “And now they would have been stabbing each other hand to hand with swords, had not the heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men, come” (7.273–275; see [[heralds]]). The duel breaks off where the duel of Book 3 broke off, as the swords come into play. There a goddess stopped it: Menelaus would have dragged Paris away, “had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (3.373–374; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[single-combat]]). Here men stop it, and their reason is the night (7.282). The same verse stops Hector and Automedon in Book 17, when the two Ajaxes part them (17.530–531; see [[sword]]).
