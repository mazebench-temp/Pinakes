{{quote:f3c34dc7-e6ed-5219-ba4b-a5e8a643656f}}

The last two lines are those of Book 5, where the best of the Argives stood around Diomedes (5.782–783), except for the words that begin them. In Book 5 the Argives were “crowded together” (εἰλόμενοι), a mass of allies around one man, standing still. Here the verb is σύν ῥʼ ἔπεσον, “they fell upon each other”: two enemies, in motion, closing on each other. The same beasts serve for a packed defense and for a charge.

The poem does not say which man is the lion and which the boar, and it may mean that both are like either. It gives both beasts to both men elsewhere. Ajax gives ground like a lion driven from the cattle yard (11.548–555) and goes through the champions like a boar in the mountains (17.281–285). Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion among dogs and hunters (12.41–48), and he kills Patroclus as a lion overcomes a tireless boar (16.823–826). See [[lion]] and [[boar]].

The simile opens the closest phase of the fight. Hector stabs the middle of Ajax's shield, and his point is bent back, in the words of Paris' cast against Menelaus (7.258–259; 3.348). Ajax leaps on him and drives his spear through Hector's shield, and it grazes his neck, “and the black blood welled up” (7.260–262). Then come the stones (7.263–272). The first half of the couplet returns once more, without the boars, for the Trojans storming the ships “like lions that eat raw flesh” (15.592). See [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].
