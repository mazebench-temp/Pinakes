---
summary: In Book 7 the vulture is a likeness for gods. Athena and Apollo sit on the high oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures”, to watch the duel they have arranged (7.58–61).
grc: [αἰγυπιοῖσι]
passages:
  - 7.58-61 | Athena and Apollo like vultures
---

The vulture of Book 7 is not the γύψ of the threats and curses, the eater of the unburied dead (4.237; 11.162; 16.836). It is the αἰγυπιός, the bird of the similes. The name has been explained as “goat-vulture” (αἴξ, “goat”, and γύψ), and the bird identified as the bearded vulture, or lammergeier; neither point is certain. In Homer's similes the αἰγυπιός is a bird of prey that fights and hunts. With crooked talons and curved beak it fights on a high rock (16.428–430), swoops on geese (17.460) and on smaller birds in the plain (*Odyssey* 22.302–306). The other vultures of the Iliad are fighters, and this is the only place where the likeness is given to gods.

{{quote:41c45a50-456a-56e4-a9c8-3fa86088b492}}

The two gods had met at the oak and talked (7.20–42). Now they sit on it “like birds, like vultures”, high above the armies, taking pleasure in the men. Whether the poet means that they took the birds' shape or only sat like them is debated; the index reads the line as a simile (see [[like-vultures]] and the translation's note on 7.59).

The vultures watch a book in which no one's body is left to the birds. Hector's challenge asks that the loser's body be given back to his own people (7.76–86), the duel ends without a death, and next morning both sides gather their dead from the plain and burn them under a truce (7.421–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[birds]]).
