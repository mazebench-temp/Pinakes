**Wanted by both sides.** The truce is planned in both camps on the same evening. At the feast in Agamemnon's huts Nestor tells the king to stop the fighting at dawn so that the Achaeans can bring in and burn their dead (7.331–335), and the kings approve (7.344). In Troy Priam sends Idaeus to the ships with Paris' offer and a second request, that the Achaeans cease from war “until we have burned the dead; afterward we will fight again, until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.375–378). The last verses are Hector's, from the end of the duel (7.291–292; see [[single-combat]]). Earlier that day the gods stopped the battle with a duel (7.29–32); now the men themselves agree on a day without fighting.

**Granted with an oath.** Idaeus repeats the request in the Achaean assembly (7.394–397; see [[heralds]] and [[message-repeated]]). The Achaeans reject Paris' offer with a shout, but the truce is Agamemnon's to give:

{{quote:0da0fd8b-f4c8-5a39-a114-50f7a5a3d910}}

The oath is a word and a gesture. There are no lambs and no wine, as there were for the truce of Book 3 (3.245–301): Zeus is called to witness, and the scepter is raised to all the gods (see [[oath]] and [[scepter]]). In later Greek warfare, to ask for one's dead was to admit defeat. Here the Trojans ask, but the Achaeans had resolved on the same truce, and neither side is said to have lost.

**The day of the dead.** At sunrise both armies go out, “some to bring the dead, and others to go for wood”, and they meet on the plain (7.417–423). Each side washes and burns its own dead and goes back, the Trojans in silence at Priam's word (7.424–432; see [[cremation]] and [[lament]]). Nothing passes between the two armies; the truce holds. The Achaeans use the next dawn to heap a mound over their pyre and build their wall against it (7.433–441; see [[tomb]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The story of the whole truce is told under [[the-truce-for-the-dead]].
