**Afraid to accept.** Hector's challenge is met by silence: “they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (δεῖσαν δʼ ὑποδέχθαι, 7.93). Shame and fear pull in opposite directions (see [[aidos]]). Nestor's story gives the silence a precedent. When Ereuthalion challenged the best of the Pylians, “they trembled greatly and were afraid (ἐδείδισαν), and no one dared”, until the youngest of them went out (7.151–153; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Agamemnon has already given Menelaus a reason to fear Hector: “even Achilles … shudders to meet this man” (ἔρριγʼ, 7.113–114). The claim is usually taken as Agamemnon's exaggeration, since Achilles himself says that while he fought, Hector would not lead the battle out beyond the Scaean gates and the oak, and once barely escaped him there (9.352–355; see the translation's note on 7.114 and [[achilles]]).

**Fear on both sides.** The lot falls to Ajax, and he tells the army to pray for him, silently or openly, “since in any case we fear no one” (οὔ τινα δείδιμεν, 7.196). When he comes out, it is the Trojans who are afraid: “a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan, and Hector's own heart pounded in his breast” (7.215–216; see [[thymos]]). Agamemnon had called Hector ἀδειής, “fearless” (7.117), and the narrator shows him afraid and unable to retreat, since he made the challenge (7.217–218).

**The fear of the gods.** When Poseidon complains of the Achaean wall, Zeus answers that another god might fear it, “one far feebler than you” (δείσειε, 7.456–457; see [[poseidon]]). The book ends with fear of Zeus himself. All night the two armies feast, and Zeus devises evil for them, “thundering terribly; and pale fear took hold of them” (χλωρὸν δέος, 7.479); they pour their wine on the ground, and no one dares to drink before he has poured a libation to Zeus (7.480–481; see [[libation]] and [[war-and-night]]). χλωρός is the pale green of fresh growth, and pale fear is the fear that drains the color from a face. The thunder foretells the next day. At noon Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies, thunders from Ida and sends his lightning among the Achaeans, “and pale fear took hold of them all” (8.69–77), and the battle turns against them. In the Odyssey Odysseus feels the same fear when the ghosts of the dead crowd round him with an unearthly cry (*Odyssey* 11.43).
