**Between the champions.** As Hector and Ajax are about to draw their swords, the heralds come between them, “one from the Trojans and one from the bronze-armored Achaeans, Talthybius and Idaeus, both of them wise” (7.275–276). The narrator calls them κήρυκες Διὸς ἄγγελοι ἠδὲ καὶ ἀνδρῶν, “messengers of Zeus and of men” (7.274), the words of Achilles' greeting to Talthybius and Eurybates in Book 1 (1.334; see [[heralds]] and [[talthybius]]). They hold out their scepters between the two men (7.277; see [[scepter]]), and Idaeus speaks, “the herald Idaeus, who knew wise counsels” (7.278). πεπνυμένα μήδεα εἰδώς is said also of the Ithacan herald Peisenor (Od. 2.38).

He calls them παῖδε φίλω, “dear sons”, in the dual, and speaks for both armies: Zeus loves both men, both are spearmen, and all know it. His last line, “it is good to yield to night too”, Hector repeats when he agrees (7.282 = 7.293; see [[yield-to-night]]). It is the Trojan herald who speaks, just after Ajax's stone has laid Hector on his back (7.268–272). Ajax answers the herald, not Hector: “Idaeus, bid Hector say these things; for it was he who challenged all the best to battle” (7.284–285; see [[ajax]]). The man who made the challenge must end it, and Hector does (7.287–302; see [[hector]] and [[single-combat]]).

**At the ships.** Priam sends him at dawn with Paris' offer and a request for a truce to burn the dead (7.372–378; see [[priam]]). He finds the Danaans in assembly by the stern of Agamemnon's ship and speaks among them, ἠπύτα κῆρυξ, “the loud-voiced herald” (7.384; see [[loud-voiced]]). His message follows Priam's words, but not exactly:

{{quote:98e7a647-0403-54a2-95bc-46f3e90eeb6c}}

A herald repeats the words he is given, and when Talthybius summoned Machaon he changed only what the delivery required (4.193–207; see [[message-repeated]] and [[messenger-scene]]). Idaeus does more. He speaks to the whole assembly, “Son of Atreus, and you others, the best of all the Achaeans”, in the words Nestor used the night before (7.385 = 7.327). He names as senders “Priam and the other noble Trojans” (7.386), and adds a courtesy, “if perhaps this should be welcome and pleasing to you” (7.387). Paris' “I will not give back the woman” (7.362) becomes “the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus he says he will not give” (7.392–393). κουριδίη ἄλοχος is the lawful wedded wife, and Menelaus uses the phrase of Helen himself when he names the wrongs the Trojans did him (13.626; see [[helen]] and [[menelaus]]). And he adds two things of his own: the wish that Paris “had perished before that” (7.390), and the report that the Trojans, “truly”, urge him to give her up (ἦ μὴν Τρῶές γε κέλονται, 7.393). Helen will make the same wish about herself in her lament for Hector: ὅς μʼ ἄγαγε Τροίηνδʼ· ὡς πρὶν ὤφελλον ὀλέσθαι, “who brought me to Troy; I wish I had died before that” (24.764).

**The answer.** The Achaeans fall silent, Diomedes speaks, and the army shouts its approval (7.398–404; see [[diomedes]] and [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). Agamemnon turns to the herald: “Idaeus, you yourself hear the word of the Achaeans, how they answer you” (7.406–407; see [[agamemnon]]). He grants the burning of the dead, calls Zeus to witness the oaths and lifts his scepter to all the gods (7.408–412; see [[the-comfort-of-fire]] and [[oath]]). Idaeus goes back to Ilios, where the Trojans and Dardanians sit in assembly waiting for him, and delivers the message “standing in their midst” (7.413–417; see [[dardanians]]). The poem does not repeat it.
