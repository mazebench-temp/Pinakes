summary: Book 7 begins with Athena and Apollo deciding that the fighting will stop for the day, and ends with Poseidon resenting a wall that mortals built without the gods and Zeus promising to wash it away. Between them the mortals know only what a seer perceives, pray for what the gods have already settled, and pour wine in fear of the thunder.

**The gods decide.** Athena comes down from Olympus to help the Achaeans, and Apollo meets her at the oak. The two gods of opposite sides agree to stop the war “for today”, until the gods' will for Troy is done, and to rouse Hector to a single combat (7.17–42; see [[athena]], [[apollo]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The day's fighting is settled among gods before any man knows of it. Helenus perceives the plan and passes it to Hector with the promise that it is not yet his fate to die (7.44–53; see [[helenus]] and [[moira]]), and the two gods sit down on the oak to watch the men they have set in motion (7.58–61; see [[watching-the-war]]).

**The mortals pray.** The Achaeans pray to Zeus as the lots are shaken and as Ajax arms, asking for Ajax to win or else for equal glory to both (7.177–180, 7.200–205; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[kydos]]). The duel goes as the gods planned it. Apollo sets Hector on his feet when Ajax's stone knocks him down (7.272), the heralds stop the fight at nightfall, and Idaeus tells the champions that Zeus loves them both (7.280). Hector's proposal to stop “for today” repeats Apollo's words without knowing it (7.290–291, 7.29–30; see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).

**The gods object.** At the end of the book the relation is reversed. The Achaeans build a great work of their own, and the gods sit beside Zeus and look at it (7.443–444). Poseidon's complaint is that mortals no longer tell the gods their plans or give them hecatombs, and that the wall's fame will make men forget the wall that he and Apollo built (7.446–453; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Zeus comforts him with the gods' advantage over men: when the Achaeans have gone home, he can sweep the wall into the sea (7.455–463). The gods' works last, and mortals' works last only as long as the gods allow (see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The thunder.** The last lines return to fear. All night Zeus devises evil for the armies, thundering, and pale fear takes hold of them; no one drinks before he has poured a libation to Zeus (7.478–481; see [[deos]] and [[libation]]). Then they sleep (7.482; see [[hypnos]]).
