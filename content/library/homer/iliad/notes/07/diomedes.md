**Second to rise.** When Nestor's story brings the leaders to their feet, “after him rose the son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes” (7.163; see [[son-of-tydeus]] and [[krateros]]), second only to Agamemnon. As the lots are shaken, the army prays that the lot fall “to Ajax, or the son of Tydeus” (7.179), or to Agamemnon: after the great day of Book 5 he is one of the three men the Achaeans want to see face Hector (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]] and [[lots]]). The lot falls to Ajax.

**The answer.** The next morning Idaeus brings Paris' offer to the Achaean assembly: all the possessions he brought to Troy, and more of his own, but not Helen, and a truce to burn the dead (7.385–397; see [[idaeus]] and [[paris]]). The Achaeans fall silent, and Diomedes speaks:

{{quote:850d27b0-200f-5c5e-a896-8fab9b67cf7b}}

He refuses both, “nor Helen” (7.401): the Achaeans are no longer fighting to get her back but for the city's fall (see [[helen]] and [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). ὀλέθρου πείρατʼ ἐφῆπται, “the bounds of destruction are already made fast”, joins two phrases of the last two books: Diomedes' own threat to Glaucus, “come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.143), and Menelaus' “bounds of victory”, held by the gods (7.102). The army shouts approval, “admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses” (7.404; see [[tamer-of-horses]]), and Agamemnon makes it his own answer (7.406–407; see [[agamemnon]]).

**At last.** The scene follows a pattern. A proposal is met by silence, “they all fell hushed in silence”, and ὀψὲ δὲ δή, “at last”, one man speaks (7.398–399). The first silence of the book was broken by Menelaus (7.92–94; see [[menelaus]] and [[silence]]); this one is broken by Diomedes. In Book 9 he breaks the silence twice more with the same verse, when Agamemnon proposes to give up the war and when Achilles has refused the embassy (9.29–31, 9.693–696), and each time he speaks for fighting on.
