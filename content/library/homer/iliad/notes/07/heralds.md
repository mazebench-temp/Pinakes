**The lot.** When the lot of Ajax leaps from the helmet, “the herald carried it through the throng everywhere and showed it from left to right to all the best of the Achaeans” (7.183–184). ἐνδέξια, “from left to right”, is the lucky direction, the way Hephaestus goes round the gods pouring their nectar (1.597–598). The herald does not know whose mark the lot bears, and he goes round until a man claims it (7.185–189; see [[lots]]).

**Between the champions.** The duel is ended by heralds, not by a god: “And now they would have been stabbing each other hand to hand with swords, had not the heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men, come, one from the Trojans and one from the bronze-armored Achaeans” (7.273–275; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[messengers-of-zeus]]). They are Talthybius and Idaeus, “both of them wise” (7.276; see [[talthybius]] and [[idaeus]]), the heralds who served the oath of Book 3, Talthybius fetching a lamb from the ships and Idaeus carrying the mixing bowl and summoning Priam (3.118–120, 3.245–258). “Messengers of Zeus and of men” is what Achilles called Agamemnon's heralds when they came for Briseis (1.334). Here they serve neither king. They hold out their scepters between the two men (σκῆπτρα, 7.277; see [[scepter]]), and Idaeus speaks for both sides:

He calls the two champions “dear sons”, and gives three reasons: Zeus loves them both, both are spearmen, and night is coming. The last is a maxim, and Hector repeats it word for word when he proposes to stop (7.282 = 7.293; see [[yield-to-night]]). Ajax answers the herald that Hector made the challenge and must be the one to end it (7.284–286), and the herald's word becomes Hector's proposal (see [[single-combat]]).

**The envoy.** Priam sends Idaeus at dawn to the ships with Paris' offer and a request for a truce to burn the dead (7.372–378). He finds the Danaans “in assembly beside the stern of Agamemnon's ship”, and “standing in their midst the loud-voiced herald spoke among them” (7.382–384). ἠπύτα, “loud-voiced”, is used only here in Homer (see [[loud-voiced]]). He delivers the message in Priam's words, with two additions of his own, a curse on Paris, “I wish he had perished before that!”, and a word for the Trojans, “though the Trojans, truly, urge him” (7.390, 7.393; see [[message-repeated]]). Agamemnon answers him by name, “Idaeus, you yourself hear the word of the Achaeans” (7.406), grants the truce, and swears to it with his scepter raised (7.408–412; see [[truce]]). Idaeus goes back to the Trojans, who are sitting in assembly waiting for him, and delivers the answer “standing in their midst” (7.413–417; see [[messenger-scene]]).

A herald could walk alone into the enemy's assembly and out again. The Iliad remembers what could happen to an envoy without that protection: in the Trojan assembly Antimachus once urged that Menelaus, who had come on an embassy with Odysseus, be killed on the spot (11.138–142; see [[embassy]]).
