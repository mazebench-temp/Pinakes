summary: Nestor opens his rebuke with the line he used to stop the quarrel in Book 1, “Ah, what great sorrow is coming upon the land of Achaea!”, and follows it with an imagined listener, as he did then. In Book 1 the listeners were the Trojans, who would rejoice; now it is old Peleus, who would pray to die.

{{quote:b9d3ffb7-ddf4-5eea-b575-5273c1a8392e}}

The first line is the one with which Nestor began his speech to Achilles and Agamemnon in Book 1 (1.254 = 7.124; see [[o-popoi]]), and the speech goes on in the same way. There he said that Priam and his sons and the other Trojans would rejoice “if they were to hear all this of you two quarreling” (1.255–258). Here he says that Peleus would groan aloud if he heard that the Achaeans all cower before Hector (7.125–131). Both times the sorrow of Achaea is measured by an absent hearer, and the old man uses the imagined report to shame the men in front of him (see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[kleos]]). The difference is in the listener. In Book 1 the enemy would take pleasure in the news; in Book 7 it is a friend, Achilles' father, the man who once questioned Nestor about the lineage of all the Argives, and he would want to die (see [[peleus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

The πένθος of Achaea is here the shame of an army that will not fight. In Book 4 Agamemnon feared the πένθος of his brother's death, the grief left to the Achaeans while the archer who shot him won fame (4.197; see [[fame-for-him-grief-for-us]] and the note on Book 4). The rebuke works where the one in Book 1 did not: nine men stand up (7.161; see [[neikos]]).
