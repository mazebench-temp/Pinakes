summary: Two Trojans look back on the broken truce. Hector says that Zeus “did not fulfill our oaths” and says nothing of Pandarus' arrow; Antenor admits that the Trojans are fighting “having played false with the trusty oaths” (7.69–72, 7.351–352). The next day a new truce is sworn, and kept.

**Hector.** His challenge begins with the broken truce:

{{quote:ddb17e1d-32bf-5f01-a125-f46fc3634e59}}

The oaths of Book 3 were to settle the war by the duel of Paris and Menelaus (3.245–301; see [[the-oaths-sworn]]). Hector lays their failure on Zeus. In a sense he is right, for the gods decided the breach: Zeus sent Athena to see that the Trojans broke the oaths first, and she persuaded Pandarus to shoot (4.64–104; see [[pandarus]] and [[athena]]). But Hector says nothing of the arrow, which Agamemnon had called the Trojans' crime (4.155–168; see the translation's note on 7.69–70 and [[hector]]).

**Antenor.** In the Trojan assembly Antenor says it plainly: “for now, having played false with the trusty oaths, we are fighting” (7.351–352). ψευσάμενοι, “having played false”, admits the Trojans' guilt, and Antenor expects no good to come of it unless Helen is given back (7.352–353; see [[antenor]]). Paris answers the proposal and ignores the charge (7.357–364; see [[paris]]).

**New oaths.** The next morning Agamemnon grants the Trojans a truce to burn their dead: “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness” (7.411). He lifts his scepter to all the gods, and this time both sides keep their word (7.412–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).
