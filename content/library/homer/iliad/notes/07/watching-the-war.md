summary: The duel of Book 7 is fought before seated spectators, both armies on the plain and Athena and Apollo on the oak of Zeus, “delighting in the men”. At the end of the book the gods sit beside Zeus and gaze at a different spectacle, the Achaeans building their wall.

**An audience for the duel.** When Hector steps between the armies, both sides sit down: Hector holds back the Trojan battalions with his spear, and Agamemnon seats the Achaeans (7.54–57). Then the two gods who planned the duel sit down to watch it:

{{quote:41c45a50-456a-56e4-a9c8-3fa86088b492}}

Whether Athena and Apollo take the form of vultures, or only perch on the oak like vultures to watch, is debated; the translation's “like birds, like vultures” leaves the question open (see its note on 7.59, [[like-vultures]], [[vultures]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). What the gods feel is plain. ἀνδράσι τερπόμενοι, “delighting in the men”, is the pleasure of spectators, the pleasure Zeus takes when he stays on Olympus to watch the battle of the gods in Book 20 (20.22–23). Below them the armies sit in their close-packed ranks, bristling with shields, helmets and spears, like the dark ripple the West Wind spreads over the sea (7.61–66; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]). The whole scene is still, set for a performance, as it was before the duel of Book 3: the same three lines seated the Trojans then (7.54–56 = 3.76–78), and the two armies watched Paris and Menelaus in amazement (3.342–343; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]).

**The spectators react.** When Ajax comes out, “the Argives rejoiced as they looked at him, but a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan” (7.214–215); each army watches its own man. At the end both sides receive their champions, the Trojans rejoicing to see Hector alive and unhurt, the Achaeans leading Ajax to Agamemnon exulting in his victory (7.306–312; see [[nike]]).

**The gods watch the wall.** After the burning of the dead the Achaeans build their wall, and “the gods, seated beside Zeus, lord of the lightning, gazed at the great work of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (7.443–444). θηέομαι is the verb of looking at a sight with wonder. Poseidon is the first to speak among them: men who build without sacrifices will make the gods' own works forgotten (7.446–453; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). The book that began with two gods on the oak, delighting in the men, ends with the gods seated around Zeus, and one of them resentful of what the men have made.
