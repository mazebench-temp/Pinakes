summary: Hector's terms for the duel give the victor the loser's armor but oblige him to give back the body, and Hector promises to hang the armor of the man he kills on Apollo's temple (7.77–86). In Nestor's story the armor of Areithous passes from the man who stripped it to the man who wore it against the Pylians (7.137–150).

**The armor, not the body.** Hector proposes that if he falls, the victor may “strip my armor and carry it to the hollow ships” (τεύχεα συλήσας, 7.78), but must give the body back for burial; if he wins, he will do the same, and “strip his armor and carry it to sacred Ilios and hang it on the temple of Apollo the Far-Shooter” (7.82–83; see [[single-combat]]). The victor's right to the armor is taken for granted, and the terms protect the body (see [[cremation]]). Hector will ask Achilles for the same terms before their last fight and be refused (22.254–267).

**An offering of spoils.** To hang an enemy's armor in a god's temple is to give the god a share of the victory. Odysseus holds up Dolon's spoils to Athena, promising them to her, and sets them on the stern of his ship until a sacrifice can be made ready (10.458–464, 10.570–571). Hector names Apollo, the god from whom he hopes for triumph (7.81), and Apollo's temple on the height of Troy (5.445–446; see [[temple]] and [[apollo]]).

**Armor with a history.** In Nestor's story Lycurgus killed Areithous, and “he stripped him of the armor that bronze Ares had given him” (7.146). He wore it himself in battle, and when he grew old gave it to his attendant Ereuthalion, who wore it when he challenged the Pylians (7.147–150; see [[areithous]], [[lycurgus-of-arcadia]] and [[ereuthalion]]). Stripped armor could be worn, and it carried its owners with it. So Hector will put on the armor he strips from Patroclus, the armor the gods gave Peleus and Peleus gave his son, and the narrator adds that the son did not grow old in his father's armor (17.192–197).
