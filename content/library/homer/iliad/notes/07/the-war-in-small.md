summary: Book 7 reaches back to the war's beginning and forward past its end. The Trojans debate giving Helen back, as they refused to do when the envoys came before the war, and Paris refuses again; the Achaeans build the fortification of a camp in the tenth year; and the gods foresee the day when the Greeks have sailed home and the wall is washed away.

**The beginning.** Before the war Menelaus and Odysseus came to Troy to ask for Helen, and Antenor received them in his house (3.205–224). The Trojans kept her, and in Book 11 the reason is given: Antimachus, “who took gold from Alexander, splendid gifts”, would not let them give her back, and even urged them to kill the envoys (11.123–125, 11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). In Book 7 the question is put again in the Trojan assembly. Antenor proposes to give back “Argive Helen, and the possessions with her”, since the Trojans are fighting with the oaths broken (7.350–353; see [[antenor]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]), and Paris refuses:

{{quote:940fba46-2889-589f-aad2-39ef84a8ed7c}}

He offers the goods and more, but not the woman, and the Achaeans refuse the goods without her (7.389–402; see [[paris]] and [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). The war's first cause is argued over in its tenth year, and the answer is the same. In Book 22, alone before the Scaean gates, Hector thinks of making Antenor's offer to Achilles, Helen and all the possessions “which was the beginning of the quarrel”, and puts the thought away (22.111–130; see [[neikos]]).

**A wall in the tenth year.** Nestor proposes, and the Achaeans build, a wall with towers and gates and a ditch around their ships (7.337–343, 7.436–441; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). An army might be expected to fortify its camp when it first lands, and the ancient historian Thucydides assumed that the Achaeans had done so: they could not have built the wall around their camp, he argues, unless they had first won a battle on landing (1.11.1; see [[thucydides]]). In the Iliad it is built only now, when Achilles has withdrawn and the Trojans are fighting in the plain. Why the poem places the building here has been much discussed (see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

**Past the end.** The book also looks beyond the fall of Troy. Diomedes tells the Achaean assembly that the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon the Trojans (7.401–402). Poseidon complains that the new wall will make men forget the wall he and Apollo built for Laomedon, Priam's father (7.452–453; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]), and Zeus promises him the day when the Achaeans have gone home and the wall can be swept into the sea (7.459–463). Book 12 tells that day as past: the city sacked in the tenth year, the Argives gone home, the rivers turned against the wall (12.13–33). See [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]].
