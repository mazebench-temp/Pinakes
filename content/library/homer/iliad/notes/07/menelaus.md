**The first to stand.** Hector's challenge meets silence: the Achaeans are “ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (7.92–93; see [[silence]] and [[aidos]]). The duel of Book 3 began the same way. When Hector announced Paris' challenge, “they all fell hushed in silence”, and Menelaus spoke first (3.95–96; 7.92, 7.94). There he took up a duel with the man who had wronged him (see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Here he offers himself for a fight that is not his own, against a stronger man.

**The rebuke.** He speaks νείκει ὀνειδίζων, “reviling them with reproaches” (7.95; see [[neikos]] and [[oneidos]]). His first words turn on the army Thersites' insult of Book 2, Ἀχαιΐδες οὐκέτʼ Ἀχαιοί, “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (7.96; 2.235; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]] and [[achaeans]]), and he calls them ἀπειλητῆρες, “braggarts”, men of threats, a word found only here in Homer. Their refusal will be a λώβη, “a disgrace, dreadful beyond dread” (7.97; see [[lobe]]). He curses them: may they “turn to water and earth”, sitting there “spiritless, without glory” (7.99–100; see [[water-and-earth]]). Then he arms, leaving the outcome to the gods: “above, the bounds of victory are held among the immortal gods” (7.101–102; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]] and [[nike]]).

**Held back.**

{{quote:fbe694dd-1f77-582a-ae82-081a6c87aa10}}

The narrator turns to speak to him, as he did twice in Book 4 when Pandarus' arrow struck him (4.127, 4.146; see [[apostrophe]]), and the sentence is one of the poem's “would have … had not” (see [[would-have-had-not]]): Hector “was far the mightier”, and Menelaus would have died. Agamemnon calls the offer madness (ἀφραίνεις, 7.109), made “out of strife” (7.111), and sends him to “sit down among the band of your companions” (7.115). “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind, urging what was due; and he obeyed” (7.120–121; see [[agamemnon]] and [[atreidae]]). His attendants “gladly” take the armor from his shoulders (7.122). When Nestor's story brings nine leaders to their feet, Menelaus is not among them (7.161–169).

**The brothers.** Agamemnon's care for him is a pattern of the poem. He shuddered over Menelaus' wound as if he were already dead (4.148–182), and in Book 10 he tells Nestor that his brother often holds back, not from sloth or thoughtlessness, but looking to Agamemnon and waiting for his lead (10.121–123). For the rest of the book Menelaus is named only in others' words. Priam sends his herald to “the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus” (7.373), the herald reports that Paris will not give back “the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus” (7.392), and Euneus of Lemnos sends the two brothers a thousand measures of wine (7.470–471; see [[euneus]]).
