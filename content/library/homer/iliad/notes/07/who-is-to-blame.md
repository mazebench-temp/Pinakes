summary: Book 7 puts the blame for the war in three mouths. Hector blames Zeus for the failed oaths; Antenor blames the Trojans, who broke them; and the herald Idaeus, carrying “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose”, wishes that Paris had perished before he brought his plunder to Troy.

**Zeus.** Hector's challenge begins with a charge against Zeus: “The son of Cronus, throned on high, did not fulfill our oaths, but, meaning evil, he ordains it for both sides” (7.69–70). He passes over Pandarus' arrow, which broke the truce, and puts the failure on the god who did not bring the oaths to their end (see the translation's note on 7.69–70, [[tekmor]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). It is the answer Priam gave Helen in Book 3, “you are not to blame for anything in my eyes; it is the gods who are to blame” (3.164), and the one Agamemnon will give for his own folly (19.86–89).

**The Trojans.** In the Trojan assembly Antenor gives the opposite answer: “now, having played false with the trusty oaths, we are fighting”, and nothing good will come to the Trojans unless they give Helen back (7.351–353; see [[antenor]]). Paris, whom the war is about, says that the gods have destroyed Antenor's wits, and refuses (7.357–364; see [[paris]] and [[phrenes]]).

**Paris.** Priam sends the herald Idaeus to the ships with “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose” (7.374), and Idaeus says the line again to the Achaeans. Reporting the offer of the goods, he adds a wish of his own: “All the possessions that Alexander in his hollow ships brought to Troy (I wish he had perished before that!), he is willing to give”; and of Helen, “he says he will not give; though the Trojans, truly, urge him” (7.389–393; see [[idaeus]] and [[neikos]]). The herald's aside names the guilty man in front of the enemy. In Book 3 Paris was hateful to all the Trojans “like black doom” (3.454; see [[like-black-doom]]), and in Book 6 Hector wished the earth would open for him (6.281–282); now a servant of the royal house says it in the Achaean assembly.

The gods' view is given at the end of the book, not about the war but about the wall: the Achaeans built it without giving the gods their hecatombs (7.450), and Zeus lets Poseidon destroy it. Blame among the gods is a matter of honor withheld (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).
