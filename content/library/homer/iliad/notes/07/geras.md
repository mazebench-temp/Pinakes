**The chine.** When the Achaeans have brought Ajax to Agamemnon's hut, the king sacrifices a five-year-old ox to Zeus, and the meat is jointed, spitted and roasted, and shared out (7.313–320; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]] and [[feast]]). No one's heart lacks an equal share of the feast (7.320; see [[equal-feast]]). Then:

{{quote:de6f5be7-f8a0-5318-b1bd-d7e03afdc0fe}}

γεραίρω is the verb of γέρας: to honor a man by giving him his prize. It occurs in the Iliad only here. The νῶτα are the meat along the backbone, and διηνεκής, “running right through”, means that the chine is cut whole, the length of the back. At a feast where every man has his equal share, the prize of honor is a portion beyond it, given by the host in front of the others. In Book 1 a γέρας was a woman chosen from the spoils; here it is a cut of meat, and it is no less a public statement of worth (see [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[division-of-spoils]]).

The Odyssey repeats the line when the swineherd Eumaeus honors the disguised Odysseus with the chine of a boar, and Odysseus answers him with a blessing, “since you honor me with good things” (γεραίρεις, *Odyssey* 14.437–441). Menelaus sets before Telemachus the fat chine of an ox that had been served to him as his own prize (γέρα, *Odyssey* 4.65–66). Among the Iliad's warriors honor at a feast is counted in portions. Hector taunts Diomedes that the Danaans used to honor him “with a seat of honor, with meat and with full cups”, and will honor him no longer (8.161–163), and Sarpedon asks Glaucus why the Lycians honor them with the same things, if not because they fight in the front (12.310–321).

**After the prize.** When the feast is done, Nestor, “whose counsel had shown itself best before”, proposes the truce and the wall (7.323–343; see [[boule]] and [[nestor]]). The old man's share is his counsel, as he said in Book 4: counsel and words are “the prize of the old” (γέρας γερόντων, 4.323; see [[prize-of-the-old]]).
