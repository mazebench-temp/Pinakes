summary: Zeus the cloud-gatherer is named twice in Book 7, in the herald's words that end the duel, “Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both,” and as he answers Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall.
---

**The duel.** Idaeus and Talthybius hold out their scepters between Hector and Ajax, and Idaeus speaks for both heralds:

{{quote:a6051940-4457-517d-b6c3-a037f449a54e}}

The herald speaks with the authority of the god whose messengers the heralds are (7.274; [[messengers-of-zeus]], [[heralds]]), and the first reason he gives for stopping the fight is Zeus' love for both men; the second is the night. The army had prayed that if Zeus loved Hector too he might grant both men equal strength and glory (7.204–205), and the duel ends as they asked. See [[idaeus]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].

**The wall.** “Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said to him” (7.454): the formula introduces the answer to Poseidon, in which Zeus gives him leave to destroy the Achaean wall once the army has sailed home (7.455–463; [[shaker-of-the-earth]], [[the-achaean-wall]]). The god of the storm promises the destruction that Book 12 carries out with rain and rivers: Apollo turns the rivers against the wall for nine days, and “Zeus rained without ceasing” (12.24–26).
