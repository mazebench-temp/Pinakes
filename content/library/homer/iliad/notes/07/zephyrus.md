When Hector and Agamemnon have made the two armies sit, and Athena and Apollo have settled on the oak, the ranks sit close together, “bristling with shields and helmets and spears” (7.61–62). Then comes the simile:

{{quote:685c9c73-21af-57fb-a5c4-081263896f79}}

The simile grows out of a word. The ranks are πεφρικυῖαι, “bristling” (7.62), and the West Wind's ripple is φρίξ (7.63), from the same verb φρίσσω, “bristle, ruffle”. The sea goes dark under the wind (μελάνει), as the plain goes dark with the seated men and their arms. Book 4 brought the wind and the bristling ranks together too. The storm cloud that a goatherd sees coming over the sea “before the blast of the West Wind” is like the battalions of the two Ajaxes, “dark, bristling with shields and spears” (πεφρικυῖαι, 4.275–282; see [[storm-cloud]]). There the wind drove a storm. Here it has “just risen” (ὀρνυμένοιο νέον, 7.64), and it only darkens the water. The armies are still, waiting to hear the challenge (see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]] and [[hector]]).

The Odyssey joins the same wind to the same dark ripple. At midday Proteus, the old man of the sea, comes up out of the water “under the breath of the West Wind, covered by the dark ripple” (πνοιῇ ὕπο Ζεφύροιο μελαίνῃ φρικὶ καλυφθείς, Od. 4.400–402). The North Wind has its ripple too: under it a fish leaps up on a weedy shore, in a simile for a boxer knocked off his feet at the funeral games (Βορέω ὑπὸ φρικός, 23.692–694).
