summary: Helenus tells Hector that “it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end”, and Hector goes out to challenge the best of the Achaeans with the gods' assurance that he will live. The prophecy holds, and the book's other near death, Menelaus', is turned aside by men.
passages:
  - 7.44-53 | Helenus hears the gods' plan
  - 7.104-108 | Agamemnon holds Menelaus back
---

**Not yet.** Helenus, who has perceived the gods' plan for a duel, tells Hector to challenge the best of the Achaeans and gives him a reason to dare it: “for it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end; so I have heard the voice of the everlasting gods” (οὐ γάρ πώ τοι μοῖρα θανεῖν καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, 7.52–53; see [[helenus]] and [[prophecy]]). μοῖρα here is the portion of life allotted to Hector, and οὔ πω, “not yet”, puts the end of it at some later day. The phrase πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, “to meet one's end”, is the language in which his end is spoken of elsewhere: Andromache speaks of the time “when you have met your fate” (ἐπεὶ ἂν σύ γε πότμον ἐπίσπῃς, 6.412), and Priam, from the wall, begs him to come inside “so that you may not soon meet your end” (22.39).

Hector acts on the prophecy at once (7.54–56), and it holds. Yet when Ajax comes toward him, his heart pounds in his breast (7.216; see [[thymos]]). Ajax's spear grazes his neck and Ajax's stone knocks him flat, and Apollo sets him on his feet (7.262–272). He comes back to Troy “alive and unhurt” (7.308; see [[hector]]). The gods' promise does not spare him the fear.

**A death turned aside.** The book's other near death is not averted by fate. When Menelaus arms to face Hector, the narrator turns to him: “Then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have appeared at the hands of Hector, since he was far the mightier, had not the kings of the Achaeans sprung up and held you back” (7.104–106; see [[apostrophe]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Agamemnon persuades him “urging what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών, 7.121; see [[aisa]]). Menelaus' life is saved by his brother's advice, and Hector's by a god's plan.

**Until a god decides.** At the end of the duel Hector leaves the outcome of the war where Helenus found his own safety, with the gods: “until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.291–292; see [[daimon]] and [[nike]]).
