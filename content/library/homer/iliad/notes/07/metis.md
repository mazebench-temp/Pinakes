**Weaving a plan.** When the feast in Agamemnon's hut is over, “the old man first of all began to weave a plan for them, Nestor, whose counsel had shown itself best before” (ὑφαίνειν ἤρχετο μῆτιν, 7.324–325). The image of weaving belongs to μῆτις: a plan is made as cloth is made, thread by thread. The two lines introduce Nestor again when he proposes the embassy to Achilles (9.93–94). This plan has two parts, the burning of the dead and a wall with towers and a ditch, and “all the kings approved” (7.327–344; see [[boule]], [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The Trojans take counsel the same evening, in a “dread and turbulent” assembly where Paris refuses Antenor's advice (7.345–364; see [[antenor]]).

**Plans kept from the gods.** Poseidon objects to the wall: “Father Zeus, is there any mortal on the boundless earth who will still tell his mind and his plans to the immortals?” (νόον καὶ μῆτιν ἐνίψει, 7.446–447). The Achaeans have built it without the hecatombs that would have told the gods their intent and asked for their favor (7.450; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[noos]]). Mortals who make plans without the gods are, to Poseidon, a threat to the gods' honor.

**Zeus the counselor.** Hector, in Helenus' address, is “the equal of Zeus in counsel” (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντε, 7.47; see [[equal-of-zeus-in-counsel]]). The last plan of the book is Zeus' own: “all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil for them, thundering terribly” (μητίετα Ζεύς, 7.478–479; see [[counselor]] and [[war-and-night]]). The next day shows what he devised (8.69–77).
