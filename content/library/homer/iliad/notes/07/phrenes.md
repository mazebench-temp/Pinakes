**Madness and a brother's mind.** Menelaus has armed to face Hector alone. Agamemnon takes him by the hand: “You are mad (ἀφραίνεις), Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, and you have no need of this madness (ἀφροσύνης)” (7.109–110). ἄφρων is the man “without φρένες”; Odysseus used the verb of Thersites' raving (2.258). The madness is a brave one, the wish to fight a better man out of rivalry (7.111; see [[eris]]), and Agamemnon answers it with a fact: even Achilles shudders to meet Hector (7.113–114). Then:

“So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind (ἀδελφειοῦ φρένας), urging what was due; and he obeyed” (7.120–121).

The verse is nearly the one of Book 6, where Agamemnon “turned his brother's mind” (ἔτρεψεν) and Menelaus pushed the suppliant Adrastus away to be killed (6.61–62). Twice in two books the elder brother changes the younger's mind with the same “due” advice, αἴσιμα παρειπών, first to kill a suppliant and then to spare himself (see [[aisa]], [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[menelaus]]). In Book 13 Hector's mind is changed in the same words by his brother Paris (13.788).

**The gods destroyed your wits.** In the Trojan assembly Antenor proposes to give back Helen and her possessions, and Paris answers him:

{{quote:8c8df549-a1cd-5a0c-aead-974df82ad82a}}

The four lines return in Book 12, spoken by Hector to Polydamas, who has read an omen as a warning to fall back from the Achaean ships (12.231–234). Both times the man with good advice is told that the gods have taken his wits away, and both times the poem proves him right: the Trojans who cheered Hector's answer in Book 18 were fools, “for Pallas Athena took away their wits” (18.310–311). In the Iliad a god can take a man's φρένες, as Zeus did Glaucus' (6.234) and Agamemnon's (19.137). Paris says it of the one Trojan counselor who spoke sense (see [[antenor]] and [[paris]]).

**Thinking and gladdening.** Zeus, Hector says, “meaning evil” (κακὰ φρονέων), ordains it for both sides (7.70; see [[tekmor]]); Athena came down “with this in mind” (7.34). And εὐφραίνω, “gladden”, is a verb of the φρένες too, to make them glad: Hector tells Ajax that they should stop fighting so that Ajax may gladden the Achaeans at the ships, and he the Trojans in the city (7.294–297).
