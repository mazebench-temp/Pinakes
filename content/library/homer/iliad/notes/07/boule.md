**The gods' plan.** Athena and Apollo meet at the oak and agree to stop the fighting for the day by rousing Hector to a single combat (7.17–43). No one tells the Trojans. Helenus perceives it: “Helenus, the dear son of Priam, understood in his heart the plan (βουλήν) that pleased the gods as they took counsel” (7.44–45). σύνθετο θυμῷ, “put together in his heart”, is the verb of a man who takes in and understands; the plan reaches him as a seer's insight, though he tells Hector that he “heard the voice of the everlasting gods” (7.53; see [[helenus]], [[prophecy]] and the translation's note on 7.53). Apollo's motive is given in the verb of the same family: he “wanted victory for the Trojans” (Τρώεσσι δὲ βούλετο νίκην, 7.21), the half-line used of Zeus when he gives the battle to the Trojans (13.347, 16.121; see [[nike]]). The mortals carry out the gods' plan without knowing whose it is (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).

The three lines introduce Nestor again in Book 9, when he proposes the embassy to Achilles (9.93–95), and the second is spoken of him at the funeral of Achilles, when he stops the Achaeans from running in terror from the sea (*Odyssey* 24.52). A plan is woven, ὑφαίνειν … μῆτιν (see [[metis]]). This one has two parts: a truce to burn the dead, and a wall with towers, gates and a ditch to protect the ships (7.327–343; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). “All the kings approved” (7.344). The half-line ἀρίστη φαίνετο βουλή, “counsel had shown itself best”, is otherwise used of the plan that a man or a god settles on after pondering (2.5, 10.17, 14.161).

**A counselor.** Nestor pictures Peleus as “the brave counselor and speaker of the Myrmidons” (βουληφόρος ἠδʼ ἀγορητής, 7.126; see [[peleus]]). The two words name the two skills of a leader in council and assembly, to give advice and to speak it well; ἀγορητής is the word the poem first used of Nestor himself, “the clear-voiced orator of the Pylians” (1.248). The old man praises in Peleus what he is.
