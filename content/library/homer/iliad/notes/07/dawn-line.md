summary: The two dawns of Book 7 are told without rosy-fingered Dawn. The sun strikes the fields as the armies go out for their dead (7.421–423), and on the next day the Achaeans are astir around the pyre “when it was not yet dawn, but still the half-light of night” (7.433–434).

**Sunrise.** The day of the truce begins with the sun, not the goddess: “The sun was just striking the fields, rising out of gently flowing, deep-streaming Oceanus up into the sky; and they met one another” (7.421–423; see [[helios]] and [[oceanus]]). The sun rises out of the river that circles the earth. The first two verses return in the Odyssey on the morning of the boar hunt on Parnassus, where the young Odysseus got his scar; there the whole-line dawn comes first (Od. 19.428–434).

**Before dawn.** The next day is marked by the frame of the dawn formula, ἦμος … τῆμος, “when … then”, but at an earlier hour: “When it was not yet dawn, but still the half-light of night, then around the pyre a chosen band of the Achaeans was astir” (7.433–434). ἀμφιλύκη νύξ occurs only here, the half-dark before dawn; the adjective is usually connected with a root for light (see [[half-light]]). The same frame, with the same verb, gathers the Trojans around Hector's pyre in the last book, but there it follows the whole-line dawn (24.788–789 ~ 7.433–434). At both pyres a crowd is astir at first light, and here the Achaeans are up before it, to build their mound and their wall (see [[pyres]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**At dawn.** Two dawns are named without a formula. Nestor wants the fighting stopped “at dawn” (ἅμʼ ἠοῖ, 7.331), and Priam orders Idaeus to go to the ships “at dawn” (ἠῶθεν), and at dawn he goes, in the same half-verse (7.372, 7.381). The book's three days turn at these dawns: the first battle day ends at nightfall, the day of the truce begins at 7.381, and the day of the wall at 7.433.
