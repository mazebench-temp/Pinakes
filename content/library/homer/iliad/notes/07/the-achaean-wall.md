summary: On Nestor's advice the Achaeans heap one mound over their dead and build against it, in a single day, a wall with towers and gates and a staked ditch in front. The gods watch the work, and Zeus grants Poseidon, jealous for his own wall at Troy, leave to destroy it when the Achaeans have gone home.

**Nestor's plan.** At the feast after the duel, the old man “began to weave a plan”, Nestor, “whose counsel had shown itself best before” (7.324–325; see [[nestor]]). Many Achaeans are dead, he says, and their souls have gone down to Hades (7.328–330). Let the army stop fighting at dawn, gather the dead and burn them, and heap one mound over the pyre:

{{quote:acfabf87-42f5-5487-8805-096a45d8e169}}

The kings approve (7.344). Nestor gives the reason, the many dead and the pressure of the Trojans. Achilles will give another. Agamemnon has built a wall “without me”, and it cannot hold Hector, who never came out beyond the Scaean gates while Achilles fought (9.348–355).

**A day's work.** The truce lasts one day, while both sides burn their dead (7.381–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). Before the next dawn, in “the half-light of night” (ἀμφιλύκη νύξ, 7.433; see [[half-light]]), a chosen band of the Achaeans builds the mound and the wall (7.433–441; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). The narrator tells the building in Nestor's words, almost line for line (7.336–341 ≈ 7.435–440), a plan carried out as it was made, and adds one thing Nestor did not ask for, the stakes in the ditch (7.441). The mound is τύμβος … ἄκριτον, “one for all alike”, with no man's grave set apart (7.336–337, 7.435–436; see [[tomb]] and the translation's note on those lines). “So the long-haired Achaeans toiled” (7.442), and at sunset “the work of the Achaeans was finished” (7.465).

**The gods look on.** The work is watched from above: “the gods, seated beside Zeus, lord of the lightning, / gazed at the great work of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (7.443–444). Poseidon complains that the Achaeans have built it without hecatombs to the gods, and that its fame will make men forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon (7.446–453; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The same line measures both fames: “the fame of it will reach as far as the dawn spreads”, Poseidon says of the wall, and Zeus answers, “your fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads” (7.451, 7.458; see [[kleos]]). Zeus gives him the wall to destroy, sweeping it into the sea and covering the beach with sand, “so that the great wall of the Achaeans may be brought to nothing” (7.459–463; see [[zeus]]). The wall is doomed on the day it is finished.

**Two mounds by the Hellespont.** The book began with a mound that did not exist. Hector imagined the tomb of the man he would kill, heaped up by the Hellespont, which a sailor would point out long afterward: “This is the mound of a man who died long ago” (7.86–91; see [[hector]]). It ends with a real mound on the same shore, and a wall built against it, which the gods decide no one shall see (see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).
