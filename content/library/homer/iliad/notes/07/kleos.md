summary: Hector's challenge ends in the Iliad's fullest picture of fame, a sailor reading the mound of the man Hector killed, “and my fame will never perish”. At the end of the book the gods argue over fame, when Poseidon fears that the Achaean wall will make men forget his own, and Zeus answers him with the same promise.
grc: [ἀκλεὲς, κλειτὰς]
en: [without glory]

**Fame from a death.** Hector's challenge sets out what is to be done with the loser's body, and then turns to what will be said:

{{quote:49b62789-a319-5614-b469-0ea22b08bbda}}

κλέος is what is heard, and Hector hears it in advance, in the voice of “someone” of the men born hereafter (see [[tis-speech]]). The sailor does not know the dead man's name. The only name in his speech is the killer's, and the fame that the mound keeps is Hector's. In Book 6 Hector fought to win fame “for my father and for myself” (6.446); here the fame is a victor's in single combat, and it is carried by the enemy's tomb (see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[single-combat]]). The last half-line, κλέος οὔ ποτʼ ὀλεῖται, is the one Calchas used of the portent at Aulis (2.325), and in the Odyssey the dead Agamemnon says it of Penelope's fame (*Odyssey* 24.196). No one accepts the challenge at first, and the fame Hector imagined is never won: the duel ends in a draw. When he falls to Achilles, his own body is refused the burial he promised his victim, until the last book (22.345–354; see [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**Without glory.** Menelaus answers the silence with a curse: may the Achaeans turn to water and earth, sitting there “spiritless, without glory” (ἀκλεές, 7.100; see [[water-and-earth]]). ἀκλεής is the negative of κλέος, and the Iliad's heroes fear it more than death. Sarpedon says that the kings of Lycia are “not without glory” because they fight in the front (12.318), and Hector, facing Achilles, asks only not to die “without glory” (ἀκλειῶς, 22.304). In the Odyssey it is the fate of a man with no tomb: the Harpies have snatched Odysseus away “without fame” (*Odyssey* 1.241).

**Fame from a reconciliation.** At nightfall Hector finds another thing for men to say. He proposes that he and Ajax exchange “glorious gifts” (δῶρα περικλυτά, from κλυτός, “famous”), so that Achaeans and Trojans alike may say, “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.299–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]] and [[famous]]). The draw too is to be told.

**The fame of the wall.** At the end of the book the Achaeans build their wall, and the gods sitting beside Zeus watch “the great work” (7.443–444; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Poseidon complains:

{{quote:d1ff4c64-f30c-58eb-b8b9-2a8e35dcc30e}}

The Achaeans have not given the gods “glorious hecatombs” (κλειτὰς ἑκατόμβας, 7.450, repeated at 12.6), and yet their wall will have κλέος. Its reach is measured by the dawn, “as far as the dawn spreads” (ὅσον τʼ ἐπικίδναται ἠώς), a phrase found only in this passage. The opposite of fame is forgetting: men “will forget” (ἐπιλήσονται) the wall that Poseidon and Apollo built for Laomedon (see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). A god's fame, like a man's, lives in what mortals remember. Zeus answers with Poseidon's own line, “but your fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads” (7.458), and lets him sweep the wall away once the Achaeans have gone (7.459–463). Book 12 tells how he did it (12.3–35). The Achaeans' wall has its fame after all, in the poem that tells of its destruction (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).
