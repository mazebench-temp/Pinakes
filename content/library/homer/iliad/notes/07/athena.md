summary: Athena comes down from Olympus to help the Argives, but Apollo meets her at the oak, and she agrees to his plan to stop the battle with a single combat; the two sit on the oak like vultures to watch it. Nestor remembers that she gave him his triumph over Ereuthalion.

**Down from Olympus.** Book 6 left her refusing the prayer of the Trojan women (6.311). Book 7 brings her down again: “when the gray-eyed goddess Athena saw them / killing the Argives in the fierce battle, / she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (7.17–19). It is the line of her descents to Odysseus in Book 2 and to Pandarus in Book 4 (7.19 ≈ 2.167, 4.74), and it will bring her down to Achilles in Book 22 (22.187; see [[olympus]]).

**Apollo's terms.** Apollo meets her beside the oak and speaks first. He knows why she has come: “Is it to give the Danaans the victory that turns the battle? / For you have no pity at all for the Trojans as they perish” (7.26–27; see [[that-turns-the-battle]]). The sack of Troy is what “you immortal goddesses” want, Athena and Hera (7.31–32; see [[hera]]). He proposes a halt for the day, and she agrees at once:

{{quote:84f9d3f8-77b7-5d5c-9967-a104554f3e98}}

She says she came with the same thing in mind. The narrator gave another reason: she saw the Argives dying (7.17–18). A halt helps the side that is losing, and Apollo's plan will put one Achaean champion against Hector (7.38–42; see [[apollo]] and [[single-combat]]). “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena did not disobey” (7.43). The verse that twice set her to work at Hera's word (2.166, 5.719) here records her obedience to Apollo.

**On the oak.** The two gods sit “on the high oak of aegis-bearing father Zeus”, “like birds, like vultures”, delighting in the men (7.58–61; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]] and [[vultures]]). Whether they take the shape of vultures or only sit in the tree as vultures sit is disputed (see [[like-vultures]] and the translation's note on 7.59). Athena does take the form of birds in the Odyssey. She leaves Nestor's feast in the likeness of a bird of prey, a φήνη, and all who see it are amazed (φήνῃ εἰδομένη, Od. 3.371–372), and she watches the fight in Odysseus' hall from the roof beam, “like a swallow to look at” (χελιδόνι εἰκέλη ἄντην, Od. 22.239–240). In the duel she does nothing. Apollo sets Hector on his feet when he falls (7.272), but Athena gives Ajax no help.

**Nestor's goddess.** Nestor's wish, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (7.132), is a formula of the Iliad (2.371, 4.288, 16.97; see [[if-only-father-zeus]]). But his story makes her his own helper: against Ereuthalion, “I fought him, and Athena gave me triumph” (7.154; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). In the longer tale of his youth she brings the Pylians word of the enemy by night, leads the fight, and turns the army back when it is won (11.714–761). Hector put his hope in almost the same words, with another god: “and Apollo gives me triumph” (7.81). Each speaker names the god who stands by his side.

**The next day.** In Book 8 Zeus forbids the gods to help either side. Athena answers that she will keep out of the fighting but give the Argives counsel, “so that they do not all perish” (8.35–37).
