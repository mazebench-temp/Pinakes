Ajax has come close behind his great shield and told Hector that he will now learn what kind of chiefs the Danaans have (7.226–232). Hector's answer takes up the division of labor that Zeus set out in Book 5, where the works of war belong to Ares and Athena and not to Aphrodite (5.428–430), and that Nestor used against the Achaeans in Book 2, calling them “foolish little ones, who care nothing for the works of war” (2.337–338). A boy and a woman are the two who do not know them (see [[women-and-war]] and [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]).

What follows is a list of what Hector does know, built on the repeated verb, “I know … I know … I know” (οἶδα, 7.237–241; see [[anaphora]]): battles and the killing of men, how to swing the dry oxhide shield to the right and to the left, how to charge in the chariot fight, and how “in close fighting, to dance for destructive Ares” (see the translation's notes on 7.238–239 and 7.241). War is a craft, and he has learned it, as he told Andromache that he had learned to be brave (6.444; see [[hector]]). Then he promises to strike openly, not by stealth (7.242–243). His spear cuts through six layers of Ajax's shield and stops in the seventh hide (7.244–248; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

The same phrase, with the same verb, marks the knowledge that a young man lacks. Nestor, telling of his first battle in Book 11, says that his father hid his horses from him, “for he said I knew nothing yet of the works of war” (11.718–719). In Book 20 Aeneas and Hector answer Achilles with Hector's defense against Ajax in a new form: do not think to frighten me with words, “like a child”, since I too know how to speak taunts (20.200–202, 20.431–433).
