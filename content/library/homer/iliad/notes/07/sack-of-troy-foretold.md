---
summary: In Book 7 the fall of Troy is taken for granted on both sides. Apollo tells Athena that the war will go on until the Achaeans find the end of Ilios, since the goddesses have willed the sack; Hector sets the fall of Troy against the Achaeans' defeat at their ships; Antenor expects nothing good for the Trojans; and Diomedes tells the Achaeans that the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon them (7.30–32, 7.71–72, 7.352–353, 7.401–402).
passages:
  - 7.24-32 | Apollo's proposal to Athena
  - 7.399-404 | Diomedes answers the Trojans
---

**The gods.** Apollo, who wants victory for the Trojans, proposes to stop the fighting for a day only: “afterward they will fight again, until they find the end of Ilios, since this is what has pleased the hearts of you immortal goddesses, to sack this city” (7.30–32). The god of the Trojans does not dispute the end, and asks only for time. τέκμωρ, “end”, can also be a way through; the phrase has been taken as “the appointed end of Ilios” or “the way to take Ilios” (see the translation's note on 7.30–31). The goddesses are Athena, to whom he speaks, and presumably Hera; in Book 4 the two sat side by side on Olympus, plotting evil for the Trojans (4.20–21; see [[athena]] and [[hera]]).

**Hector.** His challenge names two ends between which Zeus holds the war, “until either you take Troy of the fine towers or are yourselves beaten beside your seafaring ships” (7.71–72; see [[of-the-fine-towers]]). The next books bring the second, the Achaeans beaten back to their ships. The poem looks beyond its own end for the first.

**Antenor and Diomedes.** In the Trojan assembly Antenor says that the Trojans are fighting with the oaths broken, “and so I do not expect that anything better will come to pass for us” unless they give Helen back (7.351–353; see [[antenor]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). The next morning, when the herald brings Paris' offer of the possessions without Helen, Diomedes answers for the army (see [[diomedes]] and [[even-a-fool-can-see]]):

ὀλέθρου πείρατʼ ἐφῆπται, “the bounds of destruction are made fast”, is said again of the Achaeans at their wall (12.79) and, in the Odyssey, of the suitors when Odysseus has thrown off his rags (*Odyssey* 22.41). Earlier in the book Menelaus said that “the bounds of victory are held among the immortal gods” (7.102; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]).

**After the war.** Zeus gives Poseidon leave to destroy the Achaean wall “when the long-haired Achaeans again are gone with their ships to their dear native land” (7.459–460). In Book 12 the narrator says when that was: after Priam's city was sacked in the tenth year and the Argives sailed home (12.15–16; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). The gods make their plans on the far side of the fall of Troy.
