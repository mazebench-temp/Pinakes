---
summary: The narrator speaks to Menelaus as he names the death that Hector would have given him, “Then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have appeared” (7.104), the third of the seven addresses to Menelaus in the Iliad.
passages:
  - 7.104-108 | Menelaus held back
---

Menelaus has shamed the army, armed, and gone out to meet Hector alone, and the narrator turns to him: ἔνθά κέ τοι Μενέλαε φάνη βιότοιο τελευτή, “Then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have appeared at the hands of Hector, since he was far the mightier” (7.104–105). The address comes inside an unreal condition, and the next verses say who saved him: the kings, and his brother, who took him by the right hand (7.106–108; see [[would-have-had-not]]). In Book 4 the narrator turned to Menelaus twice as Pandarus' arrow wounded him (4.127, 4.146). Here he turns to him at a death that is only imagined. Each time the address falls where Menelaus is in danger, and each time something saves him, Athena then and his brother now (see [[menelaus]]).

The vocative Μενέλαε fits the verse where the nominative would not, and the formulas of address may have grown from that. The addresses have also been read as the narrator's sympathy for a gentle and vulnerable man, as those to Patroclus in Book 16 are read. The next voice to use the vocative is Agamemnon's, with a rebuke: “You are mad, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus” (7.109).
