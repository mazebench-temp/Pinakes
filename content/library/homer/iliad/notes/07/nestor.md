summary: Nestor shames the Achaeans into answering Hector's challenge with the story of how, the youngest of them all, he killed the Arcadian champion Ereuthalion; nine leaders stand up, and he has them cast lots. After the duel he proposes a truce to burn the dead, a single tomb, and towers, gates and a ditch to protect the ships (7.123–181, 7.323–344).

**The rebuke.** Menelaus has been held back, and no one else has stood up. Nestor rises and begins with the verse that began his first speech in the poem, when he tried to reconcile Achilles and Agamemnon: “Ah, what great sorrow is coming upon the land of Achaea!” (7.124 = 1.254; see [[o-popoi]]). Then he calls up a listener far away. Old Peleus, who once questioned him in his house about the lineage of all the Argives, would lift his hands to the gods and pray to die if he heard that they all cowered before Hector (7.125–131; see [[peleus]]). Book 11 tells of a visit that Nestor and Odysseus paid to Peleus' house, gathering the army for Troy (11.769–784).

**The story.** Then comes his youth (see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). He opens with a wish, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (7.132; see [[if-only-father-zeus]]), and tells how the Pylians and the Arcadians fought by the Celadon, beside the walls of Pheia (7.133–135; see [[pylians]], [[arcadians]], [[celadon]], [[pheia]] and [[iardanus]]). Ereuthalion, in armor that Ares once gave Areithous the mace-man, challenged all the best; “they trembled greatly and were afraid, and no one dared”; but Nestor, the youngest of them all, fought him, “and Athena gave me triumph” (7.136–154; see [[ereuthalion]] and [[areithous]]). The story is Hector's challenge told as a memory, with the answer the Achaeans have not given. It ends in the present:

The first of these verses begins his other tales of his youth (11.670 and, with τε for δέ, 23.629); in Book 4 Agamemnon wished him the same steadfast strength, and Nestor answered with the name of Ereuthalion (4.314–319; see [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**The lots.** “So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (7.161). Menelaus' reproaches roused no one; the old man's story raises nine (see [[neikos]]). Nestor tells them to cast lots, and promises that the man chosen will do the Achaeans good, and his own heart, “if he escapes from destructive war and grim combat” (7.171–174): the last words are Agamemnon's of Hector a few lines earlier (7.118–119). The lots go into Agamemnon's helmet, and “the Gerenian horseman Nestor shook the lots” (7.181; see [[gerenian-horseman]] and [[lots]]). Out leaps the lot of Ajax (7.182–183; see [[ajax]]).

**The counsel.** After the feast, “the old man first of all began to weave a plan for them, Nestor, whose counsel had shown itself best before” (7.324–325; see [[boule]]). The same three verses bring on his proposal of the embassy to Achilles in Book 9 (9.93–95). He asks Agamemnon to stop the fighting at dawn, so that the dead may be brought in with oxen and mules and burned in front of the ships, “so that each man may carry their bones home to their children, whenever we return again to our native land” (7.333–335). Some ancient critics rejected the lines, since the dead are then buried together under one mound at Troy (see [[cremation]] and [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). Around the pyre he proposes a single tomb, and against it “high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for ourselves”, with gates for chariots and a deep ditch outside (7.336–343; see [[tomb]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). “All the kings approved” (7.344). The wall is built the day after the burning (7.433–441), and it is the scene of the fighting from Book 8 to Book 15.
