The Achaeans pay for the wine in kind: “some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron, some with hides, and some with the cattle themselves, and some with slaves” (7.473–475; see [[trade]], [[bronze]], [[iron]] and [[cattle]]). The last item is ἀνδράποδα, people taken in war and sold. The word is common in later Greek prose and is found only here in Homer, whose words for slaves are otherwise δμῶες and δμῳαί; its dative is formed as if from a third-declension noun (-εσσι for -οισι), and both the word and its form have been taken as signs of a late line (see the translation's note on 7.475). In a list of goods, people are one more kind of payment, after hides and cattle.

The traffic ran both ways between the camp and Lemnos. The wine comes from Euneus, son of Jason, on Lemnos (7.467–469; see [[euneus]] and [[lemnos]]). In Book 21 the narrator recalls that Achilles once took Lycaon, a son of Priam, from his father's orchard at night and sold him to Lemnos, “and the son of Jason paid the price” (21.34–41). Lycaon himself says that he fetched Achilles “the worth of a hundred oxen” (21.79), and in Book 23 the price is named: a silver mixing bowl of Sidonian work, which Euneus gave to Patroclus and Achilles sets out as a prize (23.740–747). Hecuba remembers that Achilles sold her other sons “across the barren sea, to Samos and Imbros and to Lemnos” (24.751–753). The captives of the war were among the goods that paid for the army's wine.
