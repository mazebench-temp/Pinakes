summary: When Ajax's stone lays Hector on his back, “Apollo at once set him on his feet” (7.272), the briefest rescue in the poem. The god does not take Hector away, and the duel is ended by heralds, not by a god; Helenus has already told Hector that it is not yet his fate to die (7.52).
grc: ["ὤρθωσεν Ἀπόλλων"]
passages:

**Half a verse.** Ajax's stone breaks Hector's shield and knocks him flat, “and he was stretched out on his back, pressed against his shield; but Apollo at once set him on his feet” (7.271–272; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[apollo]]). The rescue has none of the usual parts: no “had not” before it, no mist, no snatching away, and no safe place (see [[would-have-had-not]]). The god puts the man back on his feet in the fight, as a victor in the boxing at Patroclus' games lifts up the man he has knocked down (ὤρθωσε, 23.695). In Book 3 Aphrodite carried Paris out of his duel to his bedroom (3.373–382), and in Book 5 Apollo carried Aeneas out of the battle to his temple (5.344–346, 5.445–448). Here Apollo leaves Hector where he is, and men end the duel: the heralds come before the swords are drawn (7.273–276; see [[heralds]] and [[single-combat]]).

**Not yet his fate.** The rescue was promised before the duel began. Helenus, who understood the gods' plan, told Hector to challenge the best of the Achaeans, “for it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end; so I have heard the voice of the everlasting gods” (7.52–53; see [[helenus]]). Apollo, who proposed the duel to stop the fighting for the day (7.37–42), keeps it from costing Hector his life. The Trojans still greet him as a man they had not hoped to see safe (7.307–310).
