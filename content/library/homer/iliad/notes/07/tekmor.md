**The end of Ilios.** Apollo, meeting Athena at the oak, proposes a pause: “let us now stop the war and the combat for today; afterward they will fight again, until they find the end of Ilios, since this is what has pleased the hearts of you immortal goddesses, to sack this city” (7.29–32). εἰς ὅ κε τέκμωρ Ἰλίου εὕρωσιν is either “until they reach the appointed end of Ilios” or “until they find the way to take Ilios”. τέκμωρ is a sign (Zeus' nod, 1.526), a goal (13.20) and a means or way out (16.472), and the translation, “until they find the end of Ilios”, leans to the first (see its note on 7.30–31). The phrase comes back in Book 9. When Agamemnon proposes to sail home, Diomedes answers that if all the others go, he and Sthenelus will fight on “until we find the end of Ilios” (9.46–49); Achilles tells the embassy that the Achaeans will never find the end of steep Ilios, and Odysseus reports his words to the kings (9.418–419 = 9.685–686). Apollo, who will defend Troy to the end, speaks of its fall as settled.

τεκμαίρεται is taken with κακά, “he ordains evils”, as when Helen says that “the gods ordained these evils” (θεοὶ κακὰ τεκμήραντο, 6.349); others supply an appointed end, “he sets a term for both sides until …”, echoing τέκμωρ in Apollo's speech (see the translation's note on 7.69–70). Either way, Hector sees the war as Apollo does: it will go on until one side is destroyed, Troy taken or the Achaeans beaten beside their ships. He says nothing of Pandarus' arrow, which broke the truce (4.104–140; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).
