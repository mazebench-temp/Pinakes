**Not drawn.** The duel goes through the thrown spear, the thrust spear and the stone, and stops at the sword: “And now they would have been stabbing each other hand to hand with swords” (ξιφέεσσʼ αὐτοσχεδὸν οὐτάζοντο), had not the heralds come (7.273–276; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[heralds]]). The same verse, with another verb, is broken off in Book 17, when Hector and Automedon are about to close with swords and the two Ajaxes part them (17.530–531). In Book 3 the sword was drawn and failed: Menelaus' shattered on the ridge of Paris' helmet (3.361–363). In Book 7 it is the one weapon the duel does not use (see [[single-combat]]).

**The gift.** Hector's gift is his sword: “So he spoke, and gave him a silver-studded sword, bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut strap” (7.303–304; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[well-cut]]). ξίφος ἀργυρόηλον, “silver-studded sword”, is the sword of the arming verse (2.45, 3.334; see [[silver-studded]]). The τελαμών is the strap from which it hung at a man's side, as the shield hung from its own (see [[shield]] and [[sheath]]). Ajax answers with a belt (7.305; see [[belt]]).

The second half of the gift verse returns once, at the funeral games of Patroclus. Ajax fights Diomedes in armor for a Thracian sword that Achilles took from Asteropaeus; the Achaeans stop the fight for fear for Ajax, and Achilles gives the sword to Diomedes “with its sheath and its well-cut strap” (23.807–808, 23.822–825; 23.825 = 7.304). And in Book 14, when Hector's spear strikes Ajax, two straps cross his chest, the strap of his shield and the strap of his silver-studded sword, and they save him (14.402–406). The poem does not say whether this is the sword Hector gave him. In Sophocles' Ajax it is the sword on which Ajax falls (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]).
