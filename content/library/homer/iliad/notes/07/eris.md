**Rivalry.** When Menelaus arms to face Hector, Agamemnon takes him by the right hand: “do not wish, out of strife, to fight a man better than you” (ἐξ ἔριδος, 7.111). ἔρις here is rivalry, the will to contend with another man, even one who is stronger. Menelaus has shamed the silent army and stands alone against Hector, and Agamemnon calls it madness (ἀφραίνεις, 7.109; see [[phrenes]] and [[menelaus]]).

The last line of the simile recalls the proem's question, “Which of the gods, then, brought the two together to fight in strife?” (ἔριδι ξυνέηκε μάχεσθαι, 1.8). There a god set two Achaean leaders at odds; here it is the son of Cronus who brings men together “in the fury of heart-devouring strife” (θυμοβόρου ἔριδος μένεϊ ξυνέηκε μάχεσθαι, 7.210), and the god of war goes among them (see [[like-huge-ares]], [[menos]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Heart-devouring strife.** θυμοβόρος, “eating the heart”, is the standing epithet of ἔρις (see [[heart-devouring]]). The strife that eats a man's heart is the passion of the fight, the quarrel in which both men waste themselves. Achilles uses the phrase of his quarrel with Agamemnon, “when we raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl” (19.58), and Aeneas of women who scold each other in the street (20.251–254); Sarpedon and Patroclus close with each other again “in heart-devouring strife” (16.476). In Book 7 Hector uses it of the duel he has just fought, in the words he wants men to say of it: “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). Strife ends in φιλότης, and the two words answer each other across the pair of lines (see [[philotes]]). Later Greek thinkers made the pair into cosmic powers: in Empedocles, Strife and Love (Νεῖκος and Φιλότης) by turns separate and unite the elements (see [[neikos]]).
