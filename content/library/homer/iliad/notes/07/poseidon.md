**His first speech.** Before this book Poseidon has been named only three times: among the gods who plotted to bind Zeus (1.400), as the god whose chest Agamemnon has (2.479), and as the lord of the grove at Onchestus (2.506). Here, as the gods sit beside Zeus watching the Achaeans build their wall, he is the one who “began to speak” (7.445):

{{quote:d1ff4c64-f30c-58eb-b8b9-2a8e35dcc30e}}

His grievance is honor. Mortals no longer tell their “mind” and “plans” to the gods (7.447), and the Achaeans have built without giving the gods hecatombs (7.450; see [[hecatomb]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Worse, the new wall threatens his own fame. Its κλέος will reach as far as the dawn spreads, and men will forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon (7.451–453; see [[kleos]], [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[laomedon]]). πολίσσαμεν, “we built (a city's wall)”, is found only here. ἀθλήσαντε, “having toiled”, a dual, makes the work Apollo's as well as his, where in Book 21 Poseidon built the wall alone while Apollo herded Laomedon's cattle (21.446–449; see [[apollo]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).

**Zeus' answer.** Zeus answers “deeply troubled” (7.454; see [[deeply-troubled]]): “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!” Some feebler god might fear this device, but Poseidon's fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads. When the Achaeans have sailed home, let him break down the wall, sweep it into the sea and cover the great beach again with sand (7.455–463; see [[zeus]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The narrator tells how it was done at the start of Book 12. Poseidon and Apollo turn the rivers of Ida against the wall, Zeus rains for nine days, and Poseidon leads the way with his trident and sweeps the foundations into the waves (12.17–33).

**Earth-shaker.** The scene gives him his first epithets in the poem: ἐνοσίχθων, “the earth-shaker” (7.445), and ἐννοσίγαιος, “shaker of the earth”, with εὐρυσθενής, “wide in strength” (7.455; see [[earth-shaker]], [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[wide-in-strength]]). Both names make him the god of earthquakes as well as of the sea, and both mean the same thing, from ἔνοσις, “shaking”, and χθών or γαῖα, “earth”.

**A twin in the Odyssey.** When the Phaeacians have carried Odysseus home, Poseidon complains to Zeus that he will no longer be honored among the gods, since mortals do not honor him; Zeus answers with the same verse, “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said”, and lets him do as he likes. Poseidon turns the Phaeacian ship to stone, and the Phaeacians sacrifice twelve bulls to him, in fear that he will hide their city under a mountain (Od. 13.125–187; 13.140 = 7.455). Each time Poseidon resents a great work of men, and each time Zeus, with the same words, lets him undo it.

**The Achaeans' friend.** The god who begrudges the Achaeans their wall is their strongest helper among the gods. The next day Hera reminds him of the gifts they bring him at Helice and Aegae, and he refuses to fight Zeus for them (8.198–211). In Books 13–15 he goes among them against Zeus' will and fights beside them at the ships, behind the broken wall (13.351–360, 14.384–387). His quarrel is with Troy, whose king cheated him (21.441–460).
