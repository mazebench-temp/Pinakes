**Thunder in the night.** The book ends with a sign from Zeus: “and all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil for them, thundering terribly; and pale fear took hold of them” (7.478–479). The thunder is not called a τέρας, and no seer reads it, but the feasters understand it at once and answer it with libations (7.480–481; see [[pale-fear]], [[libation]] and [[zeus]]). Thunder is Zeus' sign elsewhere too. In the Odyssey Odysseus prays for “another portent of Zeus” to appear outside the house, and at once Zeus thunders from Olympus (Διὸς τέρας, Od. 20.97–104); when Odysseus has strung the bow, Zeus thunders loud, “showing signs” (σήματα φαίνων, Od. 21.413–415).

**Read the next day.** Book 8 shows what the thunder meant. At dawn Zeus forbids the gods to help either side (8.1–27). At midday he weighs the fates of the two armies, and the Achaeans' day of doom sinks (8.68–74); then he thunders from Ida and sends a blazing flash among the Achaeans, and pale fear takes hold of them all, in the words of 7.479 (8.75–77). Three times more he thunders from Ida, “giving a sign to the Trojans” (σῆμα τιθείς, 8.170–171). The evil devised in the night is the Trojan victory that Zeus promised Thetis in Book 1 (1.508–510; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**A mark.** σῆμα is also the mark each of the nine scratched on his lot, which only its owner could recognize: Ajax “knew the mark on the lot when he saw it” (7.189; see [[lots]]). It is a sign made by a man for himself, as the signs Proetus scratched on a folded tablet were made for one reader (6.168–178; see [[writing]]).

At 7.86 and 7.89 σῆμα is the mound over a grave, and those lines are not counted here (see [[tomb]]).
