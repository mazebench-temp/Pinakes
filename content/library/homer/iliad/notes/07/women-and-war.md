summary: After Book 6, where every scene in Troy is played among women, Book 7 is a book of men: no mortal woman speaks in it. Women are present as the measure the fighters must not fall to, “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer”, and at the edges of the war, as mothers, mourners and worshippers, and as Helen, whom Paris will not give back.

**The measure.** Twice in the book a man is shamed by being called a woman. Menelaus rebukes the silent Achaeans as “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (7.96; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]), and Hector warns Ajax not to test him “as if I were some feeble boy or a woman, who does not know the works of war” (7.235–236; see [[the-works-of-war]]). The fighters define what they are by what women are not.

**At the edges.** The women of the book are named in the men's lives and deaths. Menesthius, the first man killed, is the son of “ox-eyed Phylomedusa” (7.10; see [[phylomedusa]]). Areithous was called the mace-man by “both men and lovely-girdled women” (7.138–139; see [[areithous]]): his fame was spoken by women too. Hector's terms for the duel send his body home so that “the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (7.79–80), and when he lives he promises to gladden “the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes, who will enter the divine assembly, praying for me” (7.297–298; see [[trojan-women]] and [[euchomai]]). Euneus, who sends the wine from Lemnos, is named with his mother Hypsipyle (7.469; see [[hypsipyle]]). And the slaves with whom the Achaeans pay for the wine (7.475) are captives of the raids, among whom the Iliad's women are many (see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[captive-women]]).

**Helen.** The one woman at the center of the book does not appear in it. Antenor proposes to give back “Argive Helen, and the possessions with her” (7.350), Paris answers, “I will not give back the woman” (7.362), the Achaeans hear that he will not give “the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus”, though the Trojans urge him (7.392–393), and Diomedes tells them not to accept the goods, “nor Helen” (7.400–401; see [[helen]] and [[the-war-in-small]]).
