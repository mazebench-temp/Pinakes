**Back to the war.** Book 6 ended with the brothers leaving the city together (6.515–529), and Book 7 opens as they come out through the gates, “and in their hearts both were eager to make war and to fight” (7.2–3; see [[hector]]). The Trojans, who have longed for them, welcome them as weary rowers welcome the fair wind a god sends (7.4–7; see [[the-fair-wind]]). Then each of the two brothers kills a man. Paris takes “the son of lord Areithous, Menesthius, who lived in Arne” (7.8–9; see [[menesthius]], [[areithous]] and [[arne]]). It is the first man he kills in the Iliad, and the narrator does not say how. In later books he fights with the bow: he wounds Diomedes in the foot, Machaon in the shoulder and Eurypylus in the thigh (11.369–379, 11.505–507, 11.581–584), and kills Euchenor with an arrow (13.660–672).

**The assembly.** That night the Trojans meet on the height of the city beside Priam's doors, and Antenor proposes to give Helen back with the possessions, since they are fighting with the oaths broken (7.345–353; see [[antenor]] and [[priams-palace]]). Paris rises, “brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair” (7.355, as at 3.329; see [[husband-of-helen]]), and answers him:

The first four lines return in Book 12, where Hector silences Polydamas, who has read a portent as a warning not to attack the ships (12.231–234). In both scenes a man who counsels caution is told that the gods have destroyed his wits (φρένας ὤλεσαν, 7.360; see [[phrenes]]). Then Paris speaks out “among the horse-taming Trojans” (7.361). He refuses outright (ἀντικρύ) to give back the woman, but offers the possessions he brought from Argos and more from his own house (7.362–364; see [[possessions]]). The terms of Book 3 joined the two: whichever man won the duel was to take “all the possessions and the woman” home (3.72, 3.93), and after it Agamemnon demanded “Argive Helen and the possessions with her” (3.458), the words Antenor has just used (7.350). Paris now separates them.

**His word at the ships.** Priam does not answer him. He sends Idaeus to the ships with “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose” (7.374; see [[priam]] and [[idaeus]]), the line with which Hector announced his brother's challenge in Book 3 (3.87). The herald delivers it the next morning and adds a curse of his own on the possessions “that Alexander in his hollow ships brought to Troy (I wish he had perished before that!)” (7.389–390). He adds too that the Trojans “truly” urge Paris to give Helen back (7.393). Diomedes answers for the army: “Let no one now accept the possessions of Alexander, nor Helen” (7.400–401; see [[diomedes]]).

**Gold in the assembly.** Book 11 names a Trojan who kept Helen in Troy for him. Antimachus “had taken gold from Alexander, splendid gifts”, and more than anyone would not let Helen be given back to Menelaus (11.123–125); he had once urged the Trojan assembly to kill Menelaus when he came as an envoy with Odysseus (11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). The poem does not say whether he spoke in the assembly of Book 7.
