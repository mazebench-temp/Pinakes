summary: The heart commands the speeches of Book 7, sends Athena down from Olympus and the young Nestor against Ereuthalion, and pounds in Hector's breast when Ajax comes near. In Nestor's picture of old Peleus it is the θυμός, not the soul, that goes down to the house of Hades.
passages:
  - 7.123-131 | Nestor imagines Peleus' grief
  - 7.214-218 | Hector's heart pounds
---

**What the heart commands.** Hector begins his challenge with a formula of the assembly: “Hear me, Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans, so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me” (7.67–68). The second line comes three times in this book, for Hector, for Antenor and for Priam (7.68, 7.349, 7.369; see [[hear-me]]); elsewhere Zeus uses it on Olympus (8.6), and it is common in the Odyssey. A speech that matters starts in the θυμός. Hector goes on to ask for the man “whose heart now bids him fight with me” (7.74).

**The heart that sends.** Apollo asks Athena why she has come down from Olympus: “Has your great heart sent you?” (μέγας δέ σε θυμὸς ἀνῆκεν, 7.25). Ares will ask her the same question in the battle of the gods (21.395). Nestor, telling how no one dared to face Ereuthalion, says, “but my much-enduring heart sent me to fight him in my daring” (7.152; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[tharsos]]), and Hector will say it to Achilles when he stops running: “now my heart has sent me to stand against you” (22.252). The θυμός is what moves a god or a man to act when others hold back.

**The heart that fears.** When Ajax comes out like Ares, “a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan, and Hector's own heart pounded in his breast” (7.215–216). The verb πατάσσω is used of the coward in an ambush, whose heart pounds against his ribs (13.282), and of the charioteers waiting for the race to begin (23.370). Hector cannot retreat, “since he had made the challenge to battle” (7.218; see [[aidos]] and [[deos]]). Ajax, drawing the lot, rejoices in his heart (7.189, 7.192), and Menelaus groans deeply at heart as he rebukes the Achaeans (7.95).

**The heart that goes to Hades.** Nestor imagines old Peleus, who once questioned him about the lineage of all the Argives, hearing that they cower before Hector: he would lift up his hands again and again to the immortals, “that his spirit might go from his limbs down into the house of Hades” (7.130–131; see [[peleus]]). Elsewhere it is the ψυχή that goes down to Hades. In this same book the souls of the Achaean dead “have gone down to Hades” (ψυχαὶ δʼ Ἄϊδος δὲ κατῆλθον, 7.330), and so do the souls of Patroclus and Hector (16.856, 22.362), while the θυμός “went from his limbs” (13.672, 16.607). In Nestor's line the θυμός itself goes down into the house of Hades. See [[psyche]] and [[old-age-and-memory]].

**Heart-devouring, lion-hearted.** Two compounds of the word are epithets in the book: θυμοβόρος, “heart-devouring”, of strife (7.210, 7.301; see [[heart-devouring]] and [[eris]]), and θυμολέων, “lion-hearted”, of Achilles (7.228; see [[lion-hearted]]).
