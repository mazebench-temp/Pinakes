---
summary: Book 7 calls Zeus “father” in two prayers of the army, in Nestor's wish for his youth, in Poseidon's complaint, and in the name of the oak on which the gods sit to watch.
---

**The army's prayers.** When the lots are shaken in Agamemnon's helmet, the people lift their hands to the gods and pray: “Father Zeus, let the lot fall to Ajax, or the son of Tydeus, or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold” (7.179–180; [[rich-in-gold]], [[lots]]). When Ajax arms, they pray again, in the full address of the prayers at Troy: “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest” (7.202; [[who-rules-from-ida]], [[most-glorious]]). Both prayers are introduced by the same verse, “this is what a man would say, looking up at the wide sky” (7.178, 7.201; [[wide-sky]], [[tis-speech]]), and both are answered: the lot falls to Ajax, and the duel ends as the second prayer allowed if Zeus loved Hector too, with neither man the victor (7.204–205, 7.273–282).

**Nestor's wish.** “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, I were young” (7.132–133): the old man's wish for his lost strength opens with the three gods, the formula with which Agamemnon and Achilles also wish for what they cannot have (2.371–372, 4.288–289, 16.97–100; [[if-only-father-zeus]]).

**Poseidon's address.** Poseidon, Zeus' brother, begins his complaint about the wall with “Father Zeus” (7.446). The title is one of respect, as it was in Thetis' supplication (1.503), and it acknowledges the rank of the god who will decide (7.455–463; [[earth-shaker]]). The oak on which Athena and Apollo sit is “the high oak of aegis-bearing father Zeus” (7.60; [[aegis-bearing]]). See [[zeus]].
