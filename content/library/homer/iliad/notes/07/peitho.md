summary: Apollo persuades Athena and Helenus persuades Hector with the same words, “if you would listen to me”; Agamemnon persuades Menelaus not to fight; and the heralds tell the champions that it is good “to yield to night”. Only Paris will not be persuaded.

**Listen to me.** Apollo's proposal to Athena begins, “But if you would listen to me (εἴ μοί τι πίθοιο), it would be far better” (7.28), and Helenus' advice to Hector begins with the same verb, “will you listen to me now? For I am your brother” (ἦ ῥά νύ μοί τι πίθοιο, 7.48). The gods' plan passes to the Trojans by persuasion at both steps. Athena “did not disobey” (οὐδʼ ἀπίθησε, 7.43), and Hector rejoices at Helenus' words and acts on them at once (7.54–56; see [[helenus]] and [[boule]]).

**A brother persuaded.** Agamemnon talks Menelaus out of fighting Hector: “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind, urging what was due; and he obeyed” (παρέπεισεν … ὃ δʼ ἐπείθετο, 7.120–121). In Book 6 nearly the same words ended the scene in which Agamemnon turned his brother against the suppliant Adrastus (ἔτρεψεν, 6.61–62; see the note on Book 6 and [[phrenes]]). Menelaus obeys both times.

**Yielding to night.** When the heralds part the champions, Idaeus gives his reason: “Night is already coming on; it is good to yield to night too” (πιθέσθαι, 7.282; see [[yield-to-night]]). Ajax answers that Hector, who made the challenge, must speak first, “and I will obey, just as he does” (πείσομαι ᾗ περ ἂν οὗτος, 7.286), and Hector repeats the herald's line (7.293). The duel ends because both men agree to obey the same thing.

**One who will not.** In the Trojan assembly Priam gives his orders and the Trojans “listened closely to him and obeyed” (ἐπίθοντο, 7.379). Paris is the exception. Antenor's advice he calls a sign that the gods have destroyed his wits (7.357–360), and Idaeus has to tell the Achaeans that Paris will not give Helen back, “though the Trojans, truly, urge him” (7.393; see [[paris]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). See [[obedience-and-persuasion]].
