**Even Achilles.** Holding his brother back from Hector, Agamemnon says: “Even Achilles, in the battle where men win glory, shudders to meet this man, and he is far better than you” (7.113–114; see [[agamemnon]], [[menelaus]] and [[where-men-win-glory]]). The claim is usually taken as Agamemnon's exaggeration, meant to keep Menelaus out of the fight. Achilles' own account is the opposite: while he fought, Hector would not lead the battle away from the wall, but came out only as far as the Scaean gates and the oak, and once waited for him there alone and barely escaped his charge (9.352–355; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). In Book 5 Hera said much the same (5.788–791), and in Book 6 Helenus measured Diomedes' fury against Achilles (6.99–101). Achilles is the standard by which both armies judge the men who fight.

**After Achilles.** Ajax, facing Hector, names him too:

{{quote:e5e698a5-f8cb-58d8-be86-2e911e62618d}}

καὶ μετʼ Ἀχιλλῆα, “even after Achilles”, can mean next after Achilles in rank, or even without Achilles, now that he has withdrawn; the translation keeps the ambiguity (see [[ajax]]). The epithets are ῥηξήνωρ, “breaker of men”, and θυμολέων, “lion-hearted” (see [[breaker-of-men]] and [[lion-hearted]]). The last two verses are the Catalogue's. There the narrator names Telamonian Ajax the best of the men “while Achilles kept up his wrath”, and then says where Achilles lay (2.768–772; see [[myrmidons]]); here Ajax speaks the lines himself. ἀπομηνίσας, “nursing his wrath”, belongs to the family of μῆνις, the poem's first word (see [[menis]]).

His father is in the book as well. Nestor shames the Achaeans with the grief of “old horse-driving Peleus”, if he heard that they cowered before Hector (7.124–131; see [[peleus]]).
