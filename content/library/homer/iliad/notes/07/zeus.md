summary: Zeus does not act in the duel of Hector and Ajax, but everyone calls on him: Hector blames him for the broken oaths and makes him the witness of his terms, the Achaeans pray to him for Ajax, and Agamemnon swears the truce for the dead by him. At the end he grants Poseidon the destruction of the Achaean wall and thunders all night, devising evil.

Zeus does not come down in Book 7, but the book happens under his eye. Athena and Apollo watch the armies from his tree, “the high oak of aegis-bearing father Zeus” (7.60; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]), and the men speak of him at every turn.

**Blamed and called to witness.** Hector opens his challenge by laying the failure of the truce on Zeus: “The son of Cronus, throned on high, did not fulfill our oaths, / but, meaning evil, he ordains it for both sides” (7.69–70; see [[high-throned]]). He says nothing of Pandarus' arrow, which Agamemnon called the Trojans' breach and which Antenor will call so in this book (4.155–168, 7.351–352; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and the translation's note on 7.69–70). Then he names Zeus as the witness of his terms: “let Zeus be our witness” (7.76). ἐπιμάρτυρος, “witness”, is found only here in Homer. Hector will offer Achilles the same bargain, with the gods as “the best witnesses and guardians of agreements”, and Achilles will refuse it (22.254–267; see [[hector]]).

**Prayers.** The Achaeans pray to him twice. When the nine champions have marked their lots, they pray that the lot fall to Ajax, Diomedes or Agamemnon (7.179–180), and out leaps “the lot that they themselves wanted” (7.182; see [[lots]]). While Ajax arms, they pray again, beginning with the verse of Agamemnon's oath and of the armies' prayer at the lots in Book 3 (7.202 = 3.276, 3.320; see [[who-rules-from-ida]] and [[prayer]]):

{{quote:0ce2ea4e-b83e-5c42-a26b-d3d31c0c40ed}}

The duel ends as the second half of the prayer asked, with neither man beaten. When the heralds, “messengers of Zeus and of men” (7.274 = 1.334), part the fighters, Idaeus states as a fact what the prayer supposed: “Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both” (7.280; φιλέεις 7.204, φιλεῖ 7.280; see [[heralds]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**Sacrifice and oath.** Agamemnon sacrifices a five-year-old bull “to the son of Cronus, supreme in might” (7.314–315), in nearly the words of his sacrifice before the first battle (2.402–403; see [[sacrifice]] and [[supreme-in-might]]). The next morning he grants the Trojans their truce and swears it by Zeus: “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (7.411; see [[oath]] and [[loud-thundering]]). ἴστω Ζεύς, “let Zeus know”, is the form of an oath. Hector swears in the same words when he promises Dolon the horses of Achilles (10.329), and Agamemnon begins so when he swears that he never touched Briseis (19.258).

**The wall.** The gods watch the Achaeans build “seated beside Zeus, lord of the lightning” (7.443; see [[lord-of-the-lightning]]). To Poseidon's complaint he answers “deeply troubled” (μέγʼ ὀχθήσας), as he answered Thetis and Hera (7.454; 1.517, 4.30; see [[deeply-troubled]]):

{{quote:83653725-580e-5895-a91a-4f274572ffb7}}

He soothes his brother's jealousy and gives him what he wants. When the time comes he rains for nine days to help (12.25–26; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). In the Odyssey he settles Poseidon's complaint against the Phaeacians in the same way, with the same first verse (Od. 13.140).

**Thunder in the night.** The book ends with him:

{{quote:f233a103-34a1-5742-8956-6efeb72d8ce7}}

κακὰ μήδετο, “devised evil”, looks forward to the next day, when Zeus forbids the gods to take part in the war, weighs the fates of the two armies, and thunders from Ida against the Achaeans, so that “pale fear took hold of them all” (8.5–27, 8.68–77), as it takes hold of the feasting men here (χλωρὸν δέος, 7.479; see [[pale-fear]]). The men take the thunder as a sign, and no one drinks before he has poured wine for Zeus (see [[libation]], [[teras]] and [[war-and-night]]). In his challenge Hector said that Zeus, “meaning evil”, ordains it for both sides (κακὰ φρονέων, 7.70). The book ends with Zeus devising evil.
