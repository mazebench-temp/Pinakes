summary: At the end of the duel Hector turns Agamemnon's taunt of Book 1 into praise: “Ajax, since a god has given you stature and strength and good sense”, it is no shame to stop. Throughout the book strength, triumph and victory are things the gods give.
grc: ["δῶκε θεὸς μέγεθός"]
en: [a god has given you stature and strength]
passages:
  - 7.288-293 | Hector proposes to stop
---

**Praise.** The heralds have parted the fighters, and Ajax has said that Hector, who made the challenge, must be the one to end it (7.284–286). Hector begins his answer with praise: “Ajax, since a god has given you stature and strength and good sense, and with the spear you are the mightiest of the Achaeans, let us now cease from battle and combat for today” (7.288–291). The thought is Agamemnon's in Book 1, “if you are very strong, it was a god, I think, who gave you that” (1.178), and the verb is the same, δίδωμι. Agamemnon used it to take the credit from Achilles; Hector uses it to give Ajax all the credit he can. A man whom a god has made so strong may be yielded to without shame, and Hector's proposal to stop follows from the compliment (see [[yield-to-night]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). He adds πινυτή, “good sense”, to the gifts of body, as the gods in the Odyssey give looks, sense and eloquence to different men (*Odyssey* 8.167–177; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]).

**Gifts in the book.** The same verb runs through Book 7. Hector hopes that Apollo will “give” him triumph (δώῃ, 7.81), and Nestor remembers that Athena “gave” it to him (δῶκεν, 7.154; see [[euchomai]]). The armor of Areithous was the gift of Ares (τά οἱ πόρε χάλκεος Ἄρης, 7.146; see [[areithous]]). The army prays that Zeus may give Ajax victory, or else grant “equal strength and glory to them both” (βίην καὶ κῦδος, 7.203–205; see [[kydos]]), and the duel ends, in Hector's words, until a god “gives victory to one side or the other” (7.292; see [[daimon]]). What a man has, in this book, he has from a god, and he does not doubt it.
