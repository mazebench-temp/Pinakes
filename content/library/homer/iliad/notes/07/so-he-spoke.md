---
summary: Book 7 opens with the close of a speech from Book 6, “So saying”, and its closings mark the turns of the day: silence after Hector's challenge and after Idaeus' message, a brother persuaded, the kings approving, the army's shout for Diomedes, and a verse that turns from the gods' talk to the sunset.
grc: ["ὣς νείκεσσʼ"]
en: [So the old man rebuked them]
---

The closings of Book 7: 7.1, 43, 54, 92, 103, 120, 161, 175, 181, 200, 206, 244, 303, 344, 354, 365, 379, 398, 403, 412, 464.

**Across the division.** The book begins with the close of a speech: “So saying, shining Hector rushed out through the gates” (7.1). The speech is Hector's last word to Paris at the end of Book 6, “let us go” (6.521–529), and the division between the books falls in the middle of a scene (see [[paris]]).

**Silence.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence” (7.92) follows Hector's challenge, as it followed his announcement of the duel in Book 3 (3.95), and in both books it is Menelaus who breaks it (3.96–97, 7.94). The verse returns after Idaeus' message, and this time Diomedes breaks it (7.398–399; see [[silence]]).

**Persuaded and obeyed.** “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind, urging what was due; and he obeyed” (7.120–121). Agamemnon's advice to Menelaus is closed with the verses that closed his advice about Adrastus, with “persuaded” (παρέπεισεν) for “turned” (παρέτρεψεν; 6.61–62). Athena “did not disobey” Apollo (7.43; see [[litotes]]), and the Trojans “listened closely to him and obeyed” Priam (7.379), a verse the Iliad uses seven times.

**Rebuke, approval, applause.** Nestor's speech is closed with a verb of its own: “So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (ὣς νείκεσσʼ ὃ γέρων, 7.161; see [[neikos]]). His plan for the wall is closed with the kings' approval (ἐπῄνησαν, 7.344), and Diomedes' refusal of Paris' offer with the army's shout, in a verse that returns when Diomedes answers Agamemnon in Book 9 (7.403–404 = 9.50–51).

**The army's voice.** The two prayers of the army, at the lots and before the duel, are closed in the plural, “So they spoke” (ὣς ἄρʼ ἔφαν, 7.181, 7.206; see [[tis-speech]]).

**Into the deed.** “He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly” (7.244) is the verse of Menelaus' throw in Book 3 (3.355). “So he spoke, and gave him a silver-studded sword” (7.303) turns Hector's proposal into the gift, and “So saying, he lifted up his scepter to all the gods” (7.412) turns Agamemnon's word into an oath (see [[guest-gifts]] and [[scepter]]).

**Changing the scene.** “So they spoke such words to one another” (7.464) closes the talk of Poseidon and Zeus, and the next verse brings the sun down on the finished wall (7.465; see [[sunset-lines]]).
