summary: Book 7 gives the Iliad's fullest account of the burning of the war dead. Hector asks that his body be given its “portion of fire”, Nestor proposes a day to burn the dead and carry the bones home, Agamemnon grants the Trojans the same, and both armies gather, wash and burn their dead on the same day (7.79–80, 7.327–335, 7.408–410, 7.421–432).

**A portion of fire.** Hector's terms for the duel begin with the body. If he falls, the victor may take his armor, “but give my body back to be taken home, so that when I am dead the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (7.79–80; see [[single-combat]]). πυρὸς λαγχάνω, literally “give a share of fire”, makes the burning the dead man's due. Hector asks it again in the same verses as he dies (22.342–343), and the ghost of Patroclus uses the phrase of his own burning (πυρὸς λελάχητε, 23.76).

**Bones for the children.** After the feast Nestor proposes that the Achaeans stop fighting at dawn, bring in their dead with oxen and mules, and burn them “a little way off, in front of the ships, so that each man may carry their bones home to their children, whenever we return again to our native land” (7.332–335). Some ancient critics rejected the last two lines (see [[bones-for-the-children]]), since the dead are then buried under a single mound at Troy (7.336, 7.435), and elsewhere in the Iliad the bones of the dead stay at Troy (4.174–175; 23.83–92, 23.243–248). The practice recalls the Athenian custom of bringing home the bones of the war dead (Thucydides 2.34).

**The comfort of fire.** The Trojans ask for a pause in the war “until we have burned the dead” (7.376–377, 7.395–396), and Agamemnon grants it: “But as for burning the dead, I do not begrudge it; for one should not grudge the corpses of the dead, once they have died, the quick comfort of fire” (7.408–410; see [[the-comfort-of-fire]] and [[truce]]). μειλίσσω is “soothe, appease”. The fire is a kindness owed to the dead even by their enemies, and it should come quickly (ὦκα).

The dead are not named, and the living can hardly tell them apart. Washing the body is the first service to the dead. Achilles' companions wash the clotted blood from Patroclus in the same words (ἄπο βρότον αἱματόεντα, 18.345 ~ 7.425), and his handmaids wash Hector's body before it is given back to Priam (24.582–587). The Trojans heap their dead on the pyre in silence at Priam's word, and the Achaeans do the same in the same verses (7.427–432; see [[lament]], [[silence]], [[pyres]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Before the next dawn the Achaeans heap a single mound over their pyre and build their wall against it (7.433–437; see [[tomb]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).
