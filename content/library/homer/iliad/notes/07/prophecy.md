summary: Helenus tells Hector that it is not yet his fate to die, and the duel proves him right; Zeus promises Poseidon that the Achaean wall will be swept away when the Achaeans have gone home, and Book 12 tells it as done. The night ends with Zeus' thunder, a sign of evil to come.

**The seer.** Helenus, “by far the best of the bird-seers” in Book 6 (6.76), perceives the gods' plan without any sign that the narrator describes: he “understood in his heart the plan that pleased the gods” (7.44–45), and he tells Hector that he has heard their voice (7.53; see [[helenus]] and the translation's note on 7.53). His prophecy is the only one in the book spoken by a seer: “it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end” (7.52; see [[moira]]). It is fulfilled the same day, when Hector returns from the duel “alive and unhurt” (7.308). οὔ πω, “not yet”, leaves the rest of Hector's fate where the poem keeps it, in the future (see [[hector]]).

**The god.** Zeus' promise to Poseidon is a prophecy of another kind, a god's decision about the future: “when the long-haired Achaeans again are gone with their ships to their dear native land, break down the wall and sweep it all into the sea” (7.459–461; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). The narrator confirms it in Book 12, where he tells the destruction of the wall as something that happened after the fall of Troy, and so tells the fall of Troy as certain (12.10–33; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Diomedes, in the Achaean assembly, reads the Trojans' offer as a sign that “the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon the Trojans” (7.402; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]).

**The sign.** The last lines of the book give a sign without a seer to read it. All night Zeus thunders terribly, devising evil, and the armies pour libations in fear (7.478–481). No one interprets the thunder; the next book fulfills it, when Zeus thunders again from Ida and the Achaeans are driven back (8.75–77; see [[teras]] and [[war-and-night]]).
