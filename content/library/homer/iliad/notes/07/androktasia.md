summary: The book opens with three killings in nine verses: Menesthius by Paris, Eioneus by Hector and Iphinous by Glaucus, all of them Achaeans (7.8–16). Hector later boasts that he knows “battles and the killing of men”, the word from which the scene takes its name (7.237).

**Three killings.** Hector and Paris come out through the gates, and at once each of them takes a man (ἔνθʼ ἑλέτην, a dual, 7.8). Paris kills Menesthius, who lived in Arne, the son of Areithous the mace-man and Phylomedusa (7.8–10). Hector strikes Eioneus with his spear “in the neck below the rim of his bronze helmet” (7.11–12). Glaucus spears Iphinous, son of Dexius, in the shoulder as he leaps up behind his horses, and he falls from the chariot (7.13–16; see [[menesthius]], [[eioneus]], [[iphinous]] and [[dexius]]). The parts of the killing scene are here in small: the killer, the victim's father, mother and home, the blow and where it struck, the fall, and twice the formula of death, “and his limbs were loosened” (7.12, 7.16; see [[loosened-his-limbs]]).

**The other side.** In Book 6 every man killed on the field was a Trojan or an ally, and no Achaean died (6.5–65). Book 7 begins the other way round: three Achaeans die, and no Trojan. The Trojans had longed for the two brothers as weary rowers long for a fair wind (7.4–7), and the three killings are what they bring. Athena sees the Trojans “killing the Argives in the fierce battle” and comes down from Olympus, and the gods agree to end the general fighting for the day (7.17–43; see [[the-war-in-small]]).

**The word.** ἀνδροκτασίη, “the slaying of men”, is Homer's own word for what these scenes tell. Hector uses it of himself when he answers Ajax: “But I know battles well, and the killing of men” (μάχας τʼ ἀνδροκτασίας τε, 7.237). The Iliad has it five times, three of them in the plural (5.909, 7.237, 24.548) and two in the singular (11.164, 23.86).

**In Nestor's story.** The book's other killings are told by Nestor: Lycurgus caught Areithous in a narrow road and ran him through with his spear, and the young Nestor killed Ereuthalion, “the tallest and the strongest man I ever killed”, who lay sprawled out, huge, this way and that (7.142–156; see [[mace]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]).
