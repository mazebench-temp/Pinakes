**The lot.** Nestor's rebuke brings nine leaders to their feet, “the two Ajaxes” among them (7.161–164; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Each marks a lot and throws it into Agamemnon's helmet, and the army prays: “Father Zeus, let the lot fall to Ajax, or the son of Tydeus, or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold” (7.179–180). Ajax is named first.

{{quote:320c95e4-0f83-5653-bb62-3484f546aeb7}}

No one owns the lot until the herald reaches the man “who had scratched his mark on it”. He knows the mark (σῆμα), a sign that means something only to him, and rejoices: “Friends, the lot is mine indeed, and I myself rejoice in my heart, since I think I will defeat brilliant Hector” (7.183–192; see [[lots]] and [[writing]]).

**The prayer.** He asks the army to pray while he arms, “in silence among yourselves, so that the Trojans do not hear, or else openly, since in any case we fear no one”, and boasts that no one will drive him back by force or by skill, for he was not born and bred in Salamis so unskilled as that (7.193–199; see [[salamis]]). The five verses 7.195–199 are reported to have been athetized in antiquity as unfitting (see [[in-silence-or-openly]]). The army prays aloud, for victory for Ajax, “but if you love Hector too and care for him, grant equal strength and glory to them both” (7.202–205; see [[prayer-type-scene]]). The herald's verdict at nightfall answers the second wish: “Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both” (φιλεῖ, 7.280; φιλέεις, 7.204).

**Going out.** His arming takes one verse (7.206–207; see [[arming-scene]]). He hurries forward “as huge Ares goes forth” (7.208–210; see [[like-huge-ares]]), with the titles Helen gave him on the wall (3.229; see [[huge]] and [[bulwark-of-the-achaeans]]):

{{quote:208c2d3f-5e6d-5f51-b9a2-67b6eace53e3}}

μειδιόων βλοσυροῖσι προσώπασι, “smiling with grim face”. No other warrior in the Iliad smiles as he goes into battle. βλοσυρός, “grim, fierce”, is the look of the Gorgon on Agamemnon's shield (βλοσυρῶπις, 11.36) and of Hector's brows when he rages at the ships (15.608). The Argives rejoice, and Hector's heart pounds (7.214–216; see [[hector]]).

**The shield.** He comes near “carrying his shield like a tower”, seven oxhides with an eighth layer of bronze, the work of Tychius of Hyle (7.219–223; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[tychius]]).

**The challenge.** “Hector, now you will know clearly, man to man, what kind of chiefs are among the Danaans too, even after Achilles, breaker of men, lion-hearted” (7.226–228; see [[breaker-of-men]] and [[lion-hearted]]). οἰόθεν οἶος, “man to man, alone”, is Apollo's phrase for the duel he planned (7.39), and Homer has it nowhere else. Ajax measures himself by the absent Achilles, as the Catalogue measured him (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and speaks of him in the narrator's verses from the Catalogue: “he lies among his curved, seafaring ships, nursing his wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people” (7.229–230 = 2.771–772).

**The fight.** Ajax has the better of every exchange (see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). Hector's spear stops in the seventh hide of his shield; his own goes through Hector's shield and corselet, told in the verses of Menelaus' cast at Paris (7.250–254 ≈ 3.356–360; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). His thrust draws blood from Hector's neck, and his stone, “like a millstone”, lays Hector on his back, until Apollo sets him on his feet (7.260–272; see [[stone-throwing]]).

**The parting.** When the heralds part them at nightfall (see [[yield-to-night]]), Ajax answers as the man who was challenged: “Idaeus, bid Hector say these things; for it was he who challenged all the best to battle. Let him begin” (7.284–286; see [[heralds]] and [[idaeus]]). Hector praises him: a god has given him “stature and strength and good sense”, and with the spear he is the mightiest of the Achaeans (7.288–289). πινυτή, “good sense”, occurs nowhere else in the Iliad, and it is his enemy who credits him with it. Ajax gives Hector “a belt bright with purple”, in the words of Oeneus' gift to Bellerophon (7.305; 6.219), and receives a silver-studded sword (7.303–304; see [[belt]], [[sword]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[parted-in-friendship]]).

**The chine.** The Achaeans lead him to Agamemnon “exulting in his victory” (7.312; see [[nike]]), although the heralds named no victor.

{{quote:de6f5be7-f8a0-5318-b1bd-d7e03afdc0fe}}

The chine was a portion of honor (see [[geras]] and [[feast]]). The first verse returns when Eumaeus honors his disguised master (Od. 14.437), and at Sparta the chines of all victims sacrificed on campaign belonged to the kings (Herodotus 6.56). Plato's Socrates takes the chine as his model for honoring the brave (*Republic* 5.468c–e; see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]).

**Afterward.** In Book 11 Hector “avoided battle with Ajax son of Telamon” (11.542), and a verse that Aristotle quotes after it, missing from the manuscripts, gives the reason: “for Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (*Rhetoric* 2.9.11). Ajax fells him with a stone again (14.409–420). In Sophocles' *Ajax* the gifts turn deadly: Ajax falls on the sword Hector gave him, “enemies' gifts are no gifts” (665), and Teucer says that Hector was dragged to his death by Ajax's belt (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]).
