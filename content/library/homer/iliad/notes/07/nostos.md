---
summary: In Book 7 the homecomings are of the dead: Hector asks that his body go home to be burned, and Nestor proposes that the bones of the Achaean dead be carried home to their children. The gods look past the war to the Achaeans' return, the day when their wall will be swept away.
passages:

  - 7.459-463 | When the Achaeans have gone home
---

**The body sent home.** Hector's terms for the duel send the loser's body home: if he is killed, the victor is to “give my body back to be taken home” (σῶμα δὲ οἴκαδʼ ἐμὸν δόμεναι πάλιν, 7.79), so that the Trojans and their wives may burn it (see [[honoring-the-dead]]). He will ask Achilles for the same thing in the same words, and be refused (22.342–354).

**Bones home to their children.** Nestor's proposal for a truce ends with a homecoming for the dead: they will burn them near the ships, “so that each man may carry their bones home to their children, whenever we return again to our native land” (7.334–335; see [[dear-native-land]]). The lines were rejected by some ancient critics. The dead are buried under a single mound at Troy (7.336, 7.435), and elsewhere in the Iliad the bones of the dead stay there: Agamemnon imagines Menelaus' bones rotting in Trojan earth (4.174–175), and Patroclus asks that his bones lie with Achilles' in one urn (23.83–92). The custom the lines describe is known from classical Athens, which brought home the bones of its war dead for a public funeral (Thucydides 2.34; see the translation's note on 7.334–335 and [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]).

**When the Achaeans have gone.** Zeus answers Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall by looking forward to their homecoming: “when the long-haired Achaeans again are gone with their ships to their dear native land, break down the wall and sweep it all into the sea” (7.459–461). In Book 12 the narrator tells it as done, after the city had fallen and “the Argives had gone in their ships to their dear native land” (12.15–16; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The gods speak of the Achaeans' return as certain; Achilles, who was told that he must choose between his homecoming and his fame, will not share it (9.410–416).
