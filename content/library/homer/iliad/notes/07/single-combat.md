It is fought for the loser's armor and body, not for Helen, and on no oaths; the lot chooses Ajax, who has the better of the fight, and the heralds part the two at nightfall with nothing decided.

**Planned by the gods.** In Book 3 the duel was a man's proposal: Paris, shamed by his brother, offered to fight Menelaus for Helen (3.67–75). The second duel is a device of the gods. Athena and Apollo agree to “stop the war and the combat for today” (7.29–30) by rousing Hector to “challenge one of the Danaans, man to man, alone, to fight him face to face in grim combat” (7.39–40). Helenus passes the plan on with an assurance, “it is not yet your fate to die” (7.52; see [[helenus]]), and the armies sit down in the verses of Book 3 (7.55–56 = 3.77–78).

**The terms.** There are no oaths: the oaths of Book 3, Hector says, Zeus did not fulfill, and he calls on Zeus only as a witness to his word (7.69–76; see [[oath]]). The stake is not Helen or the war but the loser's armor and his body:

{{quote:f740682a-56fe-523b-a7ea-091f95c88a1c}}

If Hector wins, he will hang the armor on Apollo's temple and give the body back for a mound by the Hellespont (7.81–86; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[tomb]]). The duel will decide nothing but the fame of the two men (7.87–91; see [[kleos]]). When Hector at last faces Achilles he asks for the same agreement and is refused, and dying he asks again for his body in the words of this challenge (22.254–267; 22.342–343 = 7.79–80).

**The champion.** No one answers: “they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (7.93). Menelaus is held back, Nestor shames the rest with a story of his youth, and nine men rise (7.94–169; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). In Book 3 Menelaus accepted at once, and the lot decided only who would throw first; here the lot chooses the champion (3.97–110, 3.314–325; 7.170–191; see [[lots]]). The challenge binds the man who made it: when Ajax comes on, Hector's heart pounds, but he cannot slip back into the crowd, “since he had made the challenge to battle” (7.216–218).

**The fight.** The two strike in turn, a thrown spear each, then both spears at once at close quarters, then a stone each (7.244–272), and much of it is told in the verses of Book 3. Ajax's spear goes through Hector's shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, and Hector swerves, as Paris did from Menelaus' spear (7.250–254 = 3.356–360); Hector's point bends back on Ajax's shield, as Paris' did on Menelaus' (7.259 = 3.348). Then Book 7 goes further. Ajax's thrust grazes Hector's neck, “and the black blood welled up” (7.262), and his stone lays Hector on his back until Apollo sets him on his feet (7.268–272; see [[spear]], [[shield]], [[stone-throwing]] and [[divine-rescue]]).

**The end.** Both duels break off at the sword. In Book 3 Menelaus' sword shattered on Paris' helmet (3.361–363); here “they would have been stabbing each other hand to hand with swords, had not the heralds” come at nightfall (7.273–282; see [[heralds]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). No god takes a man away, and no one wins. Ajax leaves it to Hector, who made the challenge, to end it, and Hector proposes that they part “for today; afterward we will fight again, until a god decides between us” (7.284–292). The sword that was not used becomes his gift (7.303–305; see [[guest-gifts]]). The Trojans rejoice to see Hector “alive and unhurt” (7.308), and the Achaeans lead Ajax “exulting in his victory” to Agamemnon (7.312).

**Two duels.** Readers have long set the two duels side by side, and some critics took the second for an imitation of the first. Book 3 staked the war on its duel and was undone by a goddess and then by a broken oath (see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Book 7 stakes only the men's fame, and ends in gifts and in the words Hector wants men to say of them: “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).
