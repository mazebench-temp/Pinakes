summary: Hector goes back to the battle with Paris and, at the gods' prompting through Helenus, challenges the best of the Achaeans, imagining the mound of the man he will kill. Ajax has the better of him until the heralds part them; they exchange gifts, and the Trojans welcome him back alive and unhurt.

**Out through the gates.** The book opens where Book 6 ended: “So saying, shining Hector rushed out through the gates, and with him went Alexander, his brother” (7.1–2; see [[paris]]). Hector kills Eioneus with a spear in the neck below his helmet's rim (7.11–12; see [[eioneus]]).

**The gods' plan.** Apollo and Athena agree to end the day's battle by rousing “the strong might of Hector, tamer of horses” to challenge one of the Danaans (7.38–42). Helenus perceives their plan and tells his brother that it is not yet his fate to die (7.44–53; see [[helenus]]). Hector rejoices and holds back the Trojan battalions, “gripping his spear by the middle”, in nearly the verses with which he proclaimed Paris' duel in Book 3 (7.54–56; 3.76–78).

**The challenge.** He lays the broken oaths of Book 3 on Zeus and says nothing of Pandarus' arrow (7.69–72; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). His terms are for the dead: the victor may strip the loser's armor but must give back the body, Hector's to be burned in Troy, the other man's to be buried beside the Hellespont (7.76–86; see [[cremation]]). Then he imagines that man's mound:

{{quote:49b62789-a319-5614-b469-0ea22b08bbda}}

The words spoken at the mound are about “a man who died long ago”, and they do not name him. The name they keep is Hector's, and so is the fame: “my fame will never perish” (see [[kleos]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[hellespont]]). In Book 6 he imagined a stranger pointing out Andromache as the wife of Hector, “who was always the best in the fighting” (6.459–462; see [[tis-speech]]). His terms come back at his death. Facing Achilles, he proposes the same pact and is refused: there are no trusty oaths between lions and men (22.254–267; see [[single-combat]]). Dying, he asks for his body in the words of this challenge (22.342–343 = 7.79–80).

**Fear.** The Achaeans are ashamed to refuse him and afraid to accept (7.93). But when the lot falls to Ajax and he comes on:

{{quote:140aacc6-32d2-5649-9b0e-f8ab17d9088e}}

In Book 3 Hector rebuked Paris for slipping back among his companions at the sight of Menelaus (3.30–57); now he cannot, “since he had made the challenge”. In Book 17 he does draw back into the throng when Ajax comes on with his tower shield (17.128–129), and Glaucus reproaches him for it (17.166–168).

**The fight.** He answers Ajax as a man who knows the works of war: how to swing the dry oxhide to right and to left, how to charge in the chariot fray, how “to dance for destructive Ares” in the standing fight (7.235–241; see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]] and [[with-the-oxhide-shield]]). In the duel he has Paris' part from Book 3. His spear point bends on the shield (7.259 = 3.348), and he escapes Ajax's spear by swerving, “and avoided black doom” (7.254 = 3.360; see [[black-doom]]). Wounded in the neck and felled by a stone, he is set on his feet by Apollo (7.262–272; see [[apollo]]). When Ajax's stone fells him again, Apollo revives him, saying he has protected him before (14.409–420; 15.254–257).

**Friendship.** When the heralds part them at nightfall, Hector, who made the challenge, ends it (7.273–286; see [[yield-to-night]]). He proposes to stop “for today”, “until a god decides between us” (7.291–292), and to exchange gifts, so that men will say: “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.300–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]] and [[philotes]]). His second imagined saying answers the first: friendship in place of a mound. He gives Ajax a silver-studded sword with its sheath and strap (7.303–304; see [[sword]]).

{{quote:178ffd45-9722-53c6-ad57-8c4fa217eab8}}

The same words greeted Aeneas, sent back to the fight by Apollo (5.515).
