summary: Book 7 is a book of words that bind. Hector declares the terms of his duel with Zeus as witness, the Trojans send “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose”, and the Achaeans answer with the word of Diomedes, which Agamemnon makes his own.

en: [declare]

**Words that start a duel.** When Helenus has told Hector to challenge the best of the Achaeans, “Hector rejoiced greatly when he heard his words” (μῦθον ἀκούσας, 7.54). The line is the one that began the duel of Book 3, when Paris offered to fight Menelaus (3.76; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Both duels are started by a brother's word. Hector then states his terms: “And this I declare (μυθέομαι), and let Zeus be our witness” (7.76). μυθέομαι here is a formal declaration, made before both armies with a god called to witness, like an oath (see [[oath]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). The duel is also ended by words. Idaeus speaks his μῦθος between the fighters (7.277), and Ajax tells him that the man who made the challenge should be the one to say it: “Idaeus, bid Hector say these things (μυθήσασθαι)” (7.284).

**The word of Alexander.** In the Trojan assembly Paris tells Antenor that he knows how to think of “another word better than this” (ἄλλον μῦθον, 7.358), and refuses to give Helen back. Priam sends Idaeus to the Achaeans with “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose” (μῦθον Ἀλεξάνδροιο, τοῦ εἵνεκα νεῖκος ὄρωρε, 7.374), and Idaeus repeats the line in the Achaean assembly (7.388). In Book 3 Hector announced Paris' offer of the duel in the same words (3.87; see [[neikos]]). Then Alexander's word was an offer to settle the quarrel by fighting; now it is a refusal to settle it at all (see [[paris]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The word of the Achaeans.** Diomedes answers for the army: no one should accept the possessions of Alexander, or Helen (7.400–402; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). The Achaeans shout aloud, “admiring the word of Diomedes” (μῦθον ἀγασσάμενοι, 7.404), and Agamemnon tells Idaeus, “you yourself hear the word of the Achaeans, how they answer you; and my pleasure is the same” (7.406–407). The king adopts the army's word as his own before he speaks for himself about the dead (see [[the-comfort-of-fire]] and [[agamemnon]]). The last speeches of the book are the gods': among the gods watching the wall, Poseidon “began to speak” (μύθων ἦρχε, 7.445; see [[poseidon]]).
