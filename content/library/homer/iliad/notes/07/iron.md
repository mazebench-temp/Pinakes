summary: Iron appears twice in Book 7: as the iron mace of Areithous, which could not save him in a narrow road (7.141, 7.144), and as the “gleaming iron” with which some of the Achaeans buy Lemnian wine (7.473).

**The iron mace.** Areithous was called the mace-man because he “broke the battalions with an iron mace” (σιδηρείῃ κορύνῃ, 7.141), and in the narrow road where Lycurgus caught him “his mace could not keep destruction from him, the iron mace” (7.143–144; see [[mace]] and [[areithous]]). The heroes of the poems fight with bronze, and an iron weapon is rare; the only other is Pandarus' iron arrowhead (4.123; see [[bronze]]). The iron mace belongs to a story that Nestor tells of the time before his own youth. σιδήρεος, “of iron”, is used elsewhere in the Iliad for the gates of Tartarus (8.15), the din of battle (17.424) and a heart that will not yield (24.205, 24.521).

**Iron as payment.** The Achaeans buy the wine of Lemnos “some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron” (αἴθωνι σιδήρῳ, 7.473; see [[trade]] and [[wine]]). Iron is a store of value in the poems, kept in houses among the treasures and offered as ransom (6.48; see [[possessions]]). αἴθων, “gleaming”, is its epithet in the simile of the poplar (4.485), and in the Odyssey Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, says that she is sailing to Temesa “for bronze”, carrying the same gleaming iron (Od. 1.184).
