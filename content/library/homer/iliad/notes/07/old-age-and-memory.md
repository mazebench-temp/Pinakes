**Two memories.** When no one but Menelaus will face Hector, Nestor rises. He begins with a memory of Peleus, “the brave counselor and speaker of the Myrmidons”, who once questioned him in his house and rejoiced to learn the lineage of all the Argives; if Peleus heard that they all cower before Hector, he would pray to die (7.124–131; see [[peleus]], [[penthos]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Then he wishes himself young, and tells the story he named in Book 4 (4.318–319):

{{quote:6b4ee1ef-0542-5537-acd9-5c854a070e5f}}

Ereuthalion came forward in the armor of Areithous the mace-man, whom Lycurgus had killed by guile; the armor passed from Lycurgus to Ereuthalion, and the champion challenged all the best, and no one dared to face him but Nestor, though “by birth I was the youngest of them all”; and Athena gave him triumph (7.136–156; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]], [[areithous]] and [[lycurgus-of-arcadia]]). The story is a mythological paradigm: a challenge refused by all and taken up by one, told to men who are refusing a challenge (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Nestor ends where he began, with the wish: “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast! Then Hector of the gleaming helmet would soon find his fight” (7.157–158).

**A memory that works.** In Book 1 Nestor told Achilles and Agamemnon of the better men he had known, and neither listened (1.259–284; see the note on Book 1). This time “the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (7.161; see [[neikos]] and [[aidos]]). Nestor manages what follows too: he proposes the lots and shakes the helmet (7.170–181; see [[lots]]). The old man cannot fight, but he can make the young men do it, as he said in Book 4 when he claimed counsel and words as “the prize of the old” (4.322–323; see [[prize-of-the-old]]).

**Counsel.** After the duel and the feast, “the old man first of all began to weave a plan for them, Nestor, whose counsel had shown itself best before” (7.324–325; see [[boule]] and [[metis]]): the burning of the dead, the single mound, the wall and the ditch (7.327–343). All the kings approve (7.344). The memory of the old serves the present in two ways in this book, as a story that shames and as experience that plans. See [[nestor]] and [[geron]].
