summary: Book 7 shows the exchange with the gods at its start and its end. Hector vows the armor of the man he kills to Apollo's temple, and Agamemnon sacrifices an ox to Zeus after the duel; but the Achaeans build their wall without hecatombs, Poseidon complains that the gods have been left out, and the book ends with the armies pouring wine to Zeus in fear of his thunder.

**Spoils for a god.** Hector's challenge includes a vow. If Apollo gives him triumph, he will strip his victim's armor, carry it to sacred Ilios, “and hang it on the temple of Apollo the Far-Shooter” (7.81–83). The god who grants the victory receives its spoils. In Book 10 Odysseus holds up Dolon's cap, wolfskin, bow and spear to Athena, promises her the first share among the gods, and later sets the bloody spoils on the stern of his ship for an offering to her (10.458–464, 10.570–571; see [[temple]], [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[euchomai]]). The duel of Book 7 ends without a victor, and Hector's vow is never paid.

**Sacrifice and feast.** When the Achaeans bring Ajax to Agamemnon, the king sacrifices “a male, five years old, to the son of Cronus, supreme in might” (7.314–315), and the victim becomes the feast at which Ajax is honored (7.316–322; see [[sacrifice]], [[feast]] and [[geras]]). The army had prayed to Zeus for Ajax (7.200–205), and Zeus receives the first offering after the duel.

**A work without offerings.** The wall is the Achaeans' own work, and Poseidon objects that it was built without the gods' share: “Do you not see how the long-haired Achaeans now have built a wall to protect their ships, and driven a ditch around it, and have not given glorious hecatombs to the gods?” (7.448–450). Worse, no mortal “will still tell his mind and his plans to the immortals” (7.447; see [[metis]]). A hecatomb before a great work would have asked the gods' favor and told them of it; without it the gods are left out of the exchange, and the fame the wall will win is fame taken from them (7.451–453; see [[kleos]] and [[hecatomb]]). Zeus allows Poseidon to destroy the wall when the Achaeans have gone (7.455–463), and in Book 12 the narrator says that it did not stand long, because it had been built “against the will of the gods, the immortals” (12.8–9; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Libations in fear.** The night after the wall is finished, the armies feast, and Zeus thunders terribly, devising evil for them. “And they poured the wine from their cups on the ground, and no one dared drink before he had poured a libation to the son of Cronus, supreme in might” (7.480–481; see [[libation]] and [[war-and-night]]). The offering is made in fear, to turn aside what the thunder threatens. The next morning Zeus forbids the other gods to help either side, and at noon he weighs the fates of the two armies, and the Achaeans' sinks (8.1–27, 8.69–74).
