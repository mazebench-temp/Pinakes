---
summary: Hector boasts that he knows how, “in close fighting, to dance for destructive Ares” (7.241): the standing fight on foot is a dance performed in honor of the war god.
grc: ["μέλπεσθαι Ἄρηϊ"]
en: [dance]
passages:
  - 7.234-243 | Hector answers Ajax
---

Answering Ajax's threat, Hector lists what he knows of war: battles and the killing of men, how to swing the oxhide shield to the right and to the left, how to charge into the fray of swift horses, and last, “I know how, in close fighting, to dance for destructive Ares” (οἶδα δʼ ἐνὶ σταδίῃ δηΐῳ μέλπεσθαι Ἄρηϊ, 7.241; see [[hector]] and [[ares]]). μέλπω is to sing and dance in a god's honor. The young Achaeans at Chryse sang the paean to Apollo all day, “hymning the Far-Worker” (μέλποντες, 1.472–474; see [[paean]]), and Hermes fell in love with a girl he saw among the dancers in the chorus of Artemis (16.182–183). ἐνὶ σταδίῃ is the standing fight on foot (σταδίῃ ὑσμίνῃ, 13.314, 13.713), set against the chariot fight of the verse before (7.240). The man who holds his ground in the front rank is a dancer in the chorus of Ares.

The dancer is elsewhere set against the fighter. Aphrodite tells Helen that Paris, back from the duel of Book 3, looks as if he were going to a dance, not coming from a fight (3.392–394), and in Book 16 Aeneas mocks Meriones, who has ducked his spear, as a dancer (ὀρχηστήν, 16.617). Hector claims both skills in one. The same verb, turned grim, gives the μέλπηθρα that a dead man becomes for the dogs, “a plaything for dogs” (13.233, 17.255, 18.179).
