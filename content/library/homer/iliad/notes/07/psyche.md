Nestor's proposal for a truce begins with the dead: “many long-haired Achaeans are dead, whose dark blood about the fair-flowing Scamander sharp Ares has now scattered, and their souls have gone down to Hades” (ψυχαὶ δʼ Ἄϊδος δὲ κατῆλθον, 7.328–330). The two halves of a dead man part company in the line. The blood stays on the plain, and the ψυχή goes below; what is left for the living to do is to care for the body, which Nestor proposes to gather, burn and bury (7.331–337; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). The proem made the same division, souls sent to Hades and the men themselves left to dogs and birds (1.3–5).

The ψυχή is the usual subject of the journey to Hades (16.856, 22.362). In Nestor's earlier speech it was the θυμός: old Peleus, hearing that the Achaeans cowered before Hector, would pray “that his spirit might go from his limbs down into the house of Hades” (7.131; see [[thymos]] and [[hades]]).
