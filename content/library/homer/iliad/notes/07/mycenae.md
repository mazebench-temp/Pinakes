πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης, “of Mycenae rich in gold” (7.180), is the first time the poem gives the city this epithet (see [[agamemnon]] and [[lots]]). It returns in Book 11, when Athena and Hera thunder “honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold” as Agamemnon arms (βασιλῆα πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης, 11.46), and in the Odyssey, where Aegisthus rules it for seven years after the murder (Od. 3.304–305). The army names Agamemnon by his city and its wealth. Hector says that men once called Troy itself “rich in gold, rich in bronze”, before the war spent its treasures (18.288–292). Archaeology has borne the epithet out: the shaft graves that Schliemann opened at Mycenae in 1876 held gold in abundance, though they are centuries older than any date proposed for a Trojan War.
