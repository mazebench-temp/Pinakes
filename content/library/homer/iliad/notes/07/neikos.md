**Rebukes.** When no one answers Hector's challenge, Menelaus stands up “reviling them with reproaches” (νείκει ὀνειδίζων, 7.95; see [[oneidos]]), and calls them Achaean women and braggarts (7.96–102; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]). His rebuke brings only himself to his feet. Nestor's is longer. He imagines old Peleus groaning to hear that the Achaeans cower before Hector, tells the story of his own fight with Ereuthalion, and ends with a taunt: not even the best of them are eager to face Hector (7.124–160; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). The narrator closes it: “So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up” (ὣς νείκεσσʼ ὃ γέρων, 7.161). The verb νεικέω is the one used when Hector rebukes Paris (3.38, 6.325) and Agamemnon the leaders he finds holding back (4.336, 4.368); a rebuke from one's own side is meant to shame a man into fighting, and this one works (see [[praise-and-blame]] and [[aidos]]).

**The quarrel.** In the Trojan assembly Priam sends Idaeus to the ships to tell the sons of Atreus “the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose” (τοῦ εἵνεκα νεῖκος ὄρωρε, 7.374), and Idaeus repeats the line to the Achaeans (7.388). The same line announced Paris' offer of the duel in Book 3 (3.87; see [[mythos]]). The νεῖκος is the whole war, named by its cause, and the word of the man who caused it is now a refusal to end it (see [[paris]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Idaeus, reporting the refusal, adds his own wish that Paris had perished before he brought the goods to Troy (7.390). In Book 22, waiting for Achilles, Hector will think of offering Helen and all the possessions that Alexander brought to Troy in his ships, “which was the beginning of the quarrel” (νείκεος ἀρχή, 22.114–117), the offer Antenor made in this book (7.350–353; see [[antenor]]).
