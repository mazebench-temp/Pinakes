summary: The gods begin the duel by rousing “the strong might of Hector”, and the Trojans end it rejoicing that he has come back “escaping the might and the irresistible hands of Ajax”, the words in which his household had mourned him in Book 6. At the end of the book Zeus measures Poseidon by his hands and might.

**Might roused.** Athena asks Apollo how he means to stop the war, and he answers, “Let us rouse the strong might of Hector, tamer of horses” (Ἕκτορος ὄρσωμεν κρατερὸν μένος, 7.38), so that he may challenge one of the Danaans to fight him alone (7.38–42; see [[apollo]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). μένος is the power a god can put into a man, as Athena put it into Diomedes in Book 5 (5.2, 5.125–126; see the note on Book 5). Here it is roused for a single combat that will end the day's fighting, not prolong it. When Ajax goes out like Ares, the simile gives him the god's own drive, among men whom Zeus has brought together “in the fury of heart-devouring strife” (ἔριδος μένεϊ, 7.210; see [[eris]] and [[like-huge-ares]]).

**Might escaped.** The Trojans receive Hector back from the duel with joy:

{{quote:178ffd45-9722-53c6-ad57-8c4fa217eab8}}

The line answers the end of Book 6. There the women of Hector's house mourned him while he was still alive, “for they thought he would never come back again from the war escaping the might and the hands of the Achaeans” (6.500–502). Now he has come back, and the Trojans had not hoped to see him safe (7.310). The phrase μένος καὶ χεῖρας ἀάπτους, “the might and the irresistible hands”, is used of the strength that a man must escape or withstand (12.166, 13.318, 17.638; see [[hector]] and [[ajax]]).

**The might of gods.** Zeus answers Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall that another god might fear it, “one far feebler than you in hands and might” (χεῖράς τε μένος τε, 7.457). The measure of a god is the measure of a hero: strength of hand and the force behind it (see [[poseidon]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Eagerness.** The verb μέμονα, “be eager, strive”, is formed from the same root, and Book 7 uses it throughout. Hector and Paris are both “eager to make war” as they come out of the gates (μέμασαν, 7.3); Apollo asks Athena why she has come “so eagerly” (μεμαυῖα, 7.24) and she asks how he means (μέμονας) to stop the war (7.36); Nestor tells the Achaean leaders that not even the best of them “are eager” to face Hector (μέμαθʼ, 7.160); and Ajax's spear drives Hector back “as he pressed on” (μεμαῶτα, 7.261).
