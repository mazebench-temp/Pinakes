**The proposal.** When nine men have risen to answer Hector, Nestor proposes the lot: “Now cast lots, all of you in turn, to see who is chosen” (κλήρῳ νῦν πεπάλασθε διαμπερὲς ὅς κε λάχῃσιν, 7.171). πεπάλασθε is a perfect middle imperative of πάλλω, “shake”, the verb of the lots in Book 3 (πάλλον, 3.316; πάλλεν, 3.324) and of Nestor's shaking here (πάλλεν, 7.181), and Odysseus' men cast lots in the same form in the Cyclops' cave (κλήρῳ πεπαλάσθαι, Od. 9.331). λαγχάνω is “get by lot”. In Book 3 the two fighters were known already, and the lot decided only who would throw first (3.314–325). Here it chooses the champion himself.

**Marked and shaken.** “Each man marked his own lot” (κλῆρον ἐσημήναντο ἕκαστος), and they threw the lots into the helmet of Agamemnon (7.175–176; see [[helmet]]). The army prays with lifted hands and eyes on the sky, in one voice for all:

{{quote:acc204b4-9c69-52f1-a980-000ce4a77f7b}}

Nestor shakes the helmet, “and out of the helmet leapt the lot that they themselves wanted, the lot of Ajax” (7.181–183). The lot is shaken until one jumps out, as Paris' did in Book 3 (3.325). In Book 3 the armies prayed that the man who caused the war might die, and no answer was told (3.318–323). Here they name three men they would welcome, and the lot answers them; the narrator's “that they themselves wanted” leaves it open whether chance or Zeus has done it (see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[tis-speech]]).

**The mark.** No one can read the lot but its owner. A herald carries it round from left to right (ἐνδέξια), the way wine goes round at a feast (see [[cupbearing]]), and each man disowns it, until it comes to “the man who had scratched his mark on it and thrown it into the helmet, shining Ajax”, and he “knew the mark on the lot when he saw it, and rejoiced in his heart” (7.183–189; see [[heralds]]). ἐπιγράψας, “scratching”, is the verb of an arrow grazing the skin (ἐπέγραψε, 4.139), and the σῆμα, the “mark”, is a sign that means something to one man only, not writing that others could read. The passage is often set beside the “grim signs” that Proetus scratched on a folded tablet, the only thing in Homer that looks like writing (6.168–169; see [[writing]]). Ajax throws the lot down at his feet and claims it: “Friends, the lot is mine indeed” (7.190–191; see [[ajax]]). In Sophocles' Ajax, Teucer reminds Agamemnon of the lot that leapt first from the helmet (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]).
