# Homer, Iliad, Book 16 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So they fought around the well-benched ship;
2	and Patroclus came and stood beside Achilles, shepherd of the people,
3	shedding hot tears like a spring of dark water
4	that pours its murky water down a sheer rock.
5	And seeing him, brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him,
6	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
7	“Why are you in tears, Patroclus, like a girl,
8	a little one, who runs beside her mother and bids her pick her up,
9	clutching at her robe, and holds her back as she hurries,
10	and looks up at her in tears, until she picks her up?
11	Like her, Patroclus, you let your tender tears fall.
12	Have you something to tell the Myrmidons, or me myself,
13	or have you alone heard some news from Phthia?
14	Yet they say that Menoetius, son of Actor, still lives,
15	and Peleus, son of Aeacus, lives among the Myrmidons;
16	we would grieve greatly for both of them, if they were dead.
17	Or are you grieving for the Argives, how they are perishing
18	beside the hollow ships because of their own transgression?
19	Speak out; do not hide it in your mind, so that we both may know.”
20	Groaning heavily, you said to him, horseman Patroclus:
21	“Achilles, son of Peleus, by far the strongest of the Achaeans,
22	do not be indignant; such grief has overpowered the Achaeans.
23	For all those who were the best before
24	lie among the ships, shot or stabbed.
25	The son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes, has been shot,
26	and Odysseus famed for his spear has been stabbed, and Agamemnon,
27	and Eurypylus too has been shot in the thigh with an arrow.
28	The healers, with their many drugs, are busy over them,
29	healing their wounds; but there is no dealing with you, Achilles.
30	May such anger never take hold of me as this that you keep,
31	you whose prowess is a curse! What good will anyone have of you, even one born hereafter,
32	if you do not ward off shameful ruin from the Argives?
33	Pitiless one, the horseman Peleus was not your father after all,
34	nor Thetis your mother; but the gray-green sea bore you,
35	and the lofty rocks, since your mind is harsh.
36	But if in your mind you are shunning some oracle,
37	and your lady mother has told you something from Zeus,
38	then send me out quickly at least, and grant me the rest of the army
39	of the Myrmidons, in the hope that I may become a light to the Danaans.
40	And give me your armor to put on my shoulders,
41	in the hope that, taking me for you, they may hold off from the war,
42	the Trojans, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath,
43	worn out as they are; and brief is the breathing space in war.
44	And easily would we, who are fresh, drive men worn out by the battle
45	back to the city, away from the ships and huts.”
46	So he spoke, begging, great fool that he was; for truly he was
47	begging for his own evil death and doom.
48	Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him:
49	“Ah, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus, what a thing you have said!
50	I do not heed any oracle that I know of,
51	nor has my lady mother told me anything from Zeus;
52	but this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit,
53	whenever a man wishes to rob his equal
54	and take back his prize, because he is greater in power.
55	This is my dread grief, since I have suffered sorrows in my heart.
56	The girl whom the sons of the Achaeans picked out for me as my prize,
57	and I won her with my spear, when I sacked a well-walled city,
58	powerful Agamemnon took her back out of my hands,
59	the son of Atreus, as if I were some dishonored vagrant.
60	But we will let all this be, as done and past; it was not possible after all
61	to be relentlessly angry in my heart; though I did say
62	that I would not end my wrath until
63	the war cry and the war reached my own ships.
64	So put my glorious armor on your shoulders,
65	and lead the war-loving Myrmidons into battle,
66	if indeed a dark cloud of Trojans has settled about
67	the ships in force, and the others are pressed back
68	against the shore of the sea, holding only a little portion of land still,
69	the Argives, and the whole city of the Trojans has come out against them,
70	emboldened; for they do not see the front of my helmet
71	shining close by; soon they would flee and fill the gullies
72	with their dead, if powerful Agamemnon
73	were kind to me; but now they are fighting around the camp.
74	For not in the hands of Diomedes, son of Tydeus,
75	does the spear rage to ward off ruin from the Danaans;
76	nor have I yet heard the voice of the son of Atreus shouting
77	from his hated head; but the voice of man-slaying Hector,
78	calling to the Trojans, breaks all around, and they with a shout
79	hold the whole plain, defeating the Achaeans in battle.
80	But even so, Patroclus, to ward off ruin from the ships,
81	fall on them in force, so that with blazing fire
82	they do not burn the ships and take away our dear homecoming.
83	But obey, as I put the end of my word in your mind,
84	so that you may win me great honor and glory
85	from all the Danaans, and they send back to me the beautiful girl
86	and give splendid gifts besides.
87	When you have driven them from the ships, come back; and if
88	the loud-thundering husband of Hera grants you to win glory,
89	do not long to fight without me
90	against the war-loving Trojans; you will make me more dishonored.
91	And do not, exulting in war and combat,
92	and killing the Trojans, lead the way on toward Ilios,
93	lest one of the everlasting gods from Olympus
94	come in against you; far-working Apollo loves them dearly.
95	But turn back, once you have brought light to the ships,
96	and let the others go on fighting over the plain.
97	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
98	not one of the Trojans would escape death, of all there are,
99	nor one of the Argives, but we two might escape destruction,
100	so that we alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy!”
101	So they spoke such words to one another,
102	and Ajax no longer stood his ground; for he was hard pressed by missiles;
103	the mind of Zeus and the noble Trojans were overpowering him
104	as they struck him; and around his temples his shining helmet,
105	as it was struck, rang terribly, and it was struck again and again
106	on its well-made plates; and his left shoulder grew weary
107	from always holding his flashing shield steady; nor could they
108	shake him where he stood, pressing on him with their missiles.
109	And he was held constantly by a grievous gasping, and the sweat
110	poured down in streams from all his limbs, and he had no way at all
111	to draw breath; and everywhere evil was piled on evil.
112	Tell me now, Muses, you who have your homes on Olympus,
113	how fire first fell upon the ships of the Achaeans.
114	Hector came up close and struck Ajax's ash spear
115	with his great sword, behind the point, at the socket,
116	and lopped it clean off; and Telamonian Ajax
117	brandished the docked spear in his hand to no purpose, and far away from him
118	the bronze point fell to the ground with a clang.
119	And Ajax knew in his noble heart, and shuddered,
120	the work of the gods, how he was utterly cutting short the plans of his battle,
121	Zeus who thunders on high, and wished victory for the Trojans;
122	and he drew back out of range of the missiles. And they threw tireless fire
123	on the swift ship, and at once an unquenchable flame poured over it.
124	So the fire played about her stern; but Achilles
125	struck both his thighs and spoke to Patroclus:
126	“Up, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus, urger of horses!
127	I see the blast of consuming fire beside the ships;
128	they must not take the ships and leave us no more escape.
129	Put on the armor quickly, and I will gather the army.”
130	So he spoke, and Patroclus armed himself in gleaming bronze.
131	First he put the greaves around his shins,
132	fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
133	second, he put on the corselet around his chest,
134	the inlaid corselet, starry, of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus.
135	And around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword
136	of bronze, and then his shield, great and heavy;
137	and on his mighty head he set a well-made helmet
138	with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it.
139	And he took two strong spears that fitted his grip.
140	Only the spear of the noble grandson of Aeacus he did not take,
141	heavy, huge, and massive; no other of the Achaeans
142	could wield it, but Achilles alone knew how to wield it,
143	the Pelian ash, which Chiron gave to his dear father
144	from the peak of Pelion, to be death for heroes.
145	And he bade Automedon yoke the horses quickly,
146	whom he honored most after Achilles, breaker of men,
147	and who was the most to be trusted to await his shout in battle.
148	And for him Automedon led the swift horses under the yoke,
149	Xanthus and Balius, who flew as fast as the winds,
150	whom the Harpy Podarge bore to the West Wind
151	as she grazed in a meadow beside the stream of Oceanus.
152	And in the trace horse's harness he put the noble Pedasus,
153	whom Achilles once brought back when he took the city of Eetion,
154	and who, mortal though he was, kept pace with the immortal horses.
155	And Achilles went among the Myrmidons and armed them,
156	all of them, throughout the huts, with their armor; and they, like wolves
157	that eat raw flesh, in whose hearts there is boundless courage,
158	that have cut down a great antlered stag in the mountains
159	and tear at it, and the jaws of all of them are red with blood;
160	and they go in a pack to a spring of dark water
161	to lap with their thin tongues the dark water
162	at its surface, belching out the gore of blood; and the heart in their breasts
163	is fearless, and their bellies are gorged full:
164	such were the leaders and rulers of the Myrmidons
165	who, around the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus,
166	rushed on; and among them stood warlike Achilles,
167	urging on the horses and the shield-bearing men.
168	Fifty were the swift ships in which Achilles,
169	dear to Zeus, led his men to Troy; and in each
170	were fifty men, companions, at the rowing benches;
171	and he had made five leaders, whom he trusted
172	to give the orders; and he himself, with great power, was lord over all.
173	One rank was led by Menesthius of the flashing corselet,
174	son of Spercheius, the river fallen from Zeus;
175	the daughter of Peleus, fair Polydora, bore him
176	to tireless Spercheius, a woman who lay with a god,
177	but by name to Borus, son of Perieres,
178	who married her openly, after giving a boundless bride-price.
179	The second was led by warlike Eudorus,
180	a maiden's son; Polymele, beautiful in the dance, bore him,
181	the daughter of Phylas; the mighty slayer of Argus
182	fell in love with her, when he saw her with his eyes among the girls singing and dancing
183	in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous.
184	At once he went up to her upper room and lay with her in secret,
185	Hermes the helper, and he gave her a splendid son,
186	Eudorus, surpassingly swift at running, and a fighter.
187	But when Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, had brought him
188	out into the light, and he saw the rays of the sun,
189	the strong might of Echecles, son of Actor, took her
190	to his house, after giving a countless bride-price,
191	and the old man Phylas reared the boy well and cherished him,
192	loving him as if he were his own son.
193	The third was led by warlike Peisander,
194	son of Maemalus, who stood out among all the Myrmidons
195	at fighting with the spear, after the companion of the son of Peleus.
196	The fourth was led by the old horse-driving Phoenix,
197	and the fifth by Alcimedon, the noble son of Laerces.
198	But when Achilles had set them all in order with their leaders,
199	sorting them well, he laid a stern command on them:
200	“Myrmidons, let none of you forget the threats
201	with which you threatened the Trojans beside the swift ships
202	all through my wrath, when each of you blamed me:
203	‘Hard son of Peleus, your mother reared you on anger,
204	pitiless, who keep your companions by the ships against their will;
205	let us at least go back home again with our seafaring ships,
206	since such evil anger has fallen on your heart.’
207	This is what you often said to me as you gathered; and now
208	the great work of the din of battle has appeared, which you loved before.
209	There let each man who has a valiant heart fight the Trojans.”
210	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man,
211	and the ranks closed up more tightly, when they heard their king.
212	And as when a man fits the wall together with close-set stones
213	of a high house, keeping out the force of the winds,
214	so closely were the helmets and bossed shields fitted together.
215	Shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man;
216	and the helmets with horsehair crests touched with their bright ridges
217	when the men nodded, so close did they stand to one another.
218	And in front of them all two men were arming,
219	Patroclus and Automedon, with one heart,
220	to fight in front of the Myrmidons. But Achilles
221	went into his hut, and opened the lid of a chest,
222	fine and intricately worked, which silver-footed Thetis
223	had put on his ship for him to take, filling it well with tunics
224	and cloaks to keep off the wind and woolly rugs.
225	And there he kept a well-made cup, and no other
226	of men drank the sparkling wine from it,
227	nor did he pour libations from it to any god, except father Zeus.
228	This he took from the chest then and cleansed it with sulfur
229	first, and then washed it in fair streams of water,
230	and washed his own hands, and drew the sparkling wine.
231	Then he prayed, standing in the middle of the enclosure, and poured out the wine,
232	looking up to the sky; and he did not escape the notice of Zeus who delights in thunder:
233	“Lord Zeus, of Dodona, Pelasgian, who dwell far away,
234	ruling over wintry Dodona, and around you the Selli
235	dwell, your interpreters, with unwashed feet, sleeping on the ground:
236	once before you listened to my word when I prayed,
237	and you honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans hard;
238	so now once more fulfill for me this wish.
239	For I myself will stay in the gathering of the ships,
240	but I am sending my companion with many Myrmidons
241	to fight; send glory with him, far-seeing Zeus,
242	and embolden the heart in his breast, so that Hector too
243	may know whether our attendant knows how to fight
244	even alone, or whether his hands rage irresistible
245	only when I go into the toil of Ares.
246	But when he has driven the battle and the cries from the ships,
247	then let him come back to me unharmed to the swift ships,
248	with all his armor and his companions who fight hand to hand.”
249	So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him.
250	And the father granted him one thing, and refused him the other:
251	he granted that he should drive the war and the battle from the ships,
252	but refused that he should come back safe from the battle.
253	Now when he had poured the libation and prayed to father Zeus,
254	he went back into the hut, and put the cup away in the chest,
255	and came and stood in front of the hut; for he still wished in his heart
256	to watch the dread din of battle between the Trojans and the Achaeans.
257	And those who had armed with great-hearted Patroclus
258	marched on, until in high spirits they leapt upon the Trojans.
259	At once they poured out like wasps
260	by the wayside, which boys make a habit of provoking,
261	always teasing them, where they have their homes beside the road,
262	the foolish little ones; and they make a common evil for many.
263	And if some wayfarer passing by
264	stirs them up unwittingly, they, with valiant hearts,
265	all fly out and each defends its young.
266	With the heart and spirit of these the Myrmidons then
267	poured out from the ships; and an unquenchable cry went up.
268	And Patroclus called out to his companions, shouting aloud:
269	“Myrmidons, companions of Achilles, son of Peleus,
270	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage,
271	so that we may honor the son of Peleus, who is by far the best
272	of the Argives beside the ships, he and his attendants who fight hand to hand,
273	and so that the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, may learn
274	his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans.”
275	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man,
276	and they fell upon the Trojans in a body; and around them the ships
277	echoed terribly with the shouting of the Achaeans.
278	And when the Trojans saw the valiant son of Menoetius,
279	himself and his attendant, gleaming in their armor,
280	the hearts of all were stirred, and the battalions wavered,
281	thinking that beside the ships the swift-footed son of Peleus
282	had thrown away his wrath and chosen friendship;
283	and each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction.
284	And Patroclus was the first to throw his shining spear,
285	straight into the middle, where the most men were thrown into confusion,
286	beside the stern of the ship of great-hearted Protesilaus,
287	and he hit Pyraechmes, who had led the Paeonians who fight from chariots
288	from Amydon, from the broad-flowing Axius;
289	he hit him in the right shoulder; and he fell on his back in the dust
290	with a groan, and his companions around him fled in rout,
291	the Paeonians; for Patroclus sent rout among them all
292	when he killed their leader, who was always the best in the fighting.
293	He drove them from the ships, and put out the blazing fire.
294	And the half-burned ship was left there; and they fled in rout,
295	the Trojans, with a wondrous din; and the Danaans poured after them
296	among the hollow ships, and a relentless din arose.
297	And as when from the high peak of a great mountain
298	Zeus who gathers the lightning moves away a thick cloud,
299	and all the lookout places and the high headlands stand out,
300	and the glens; and from the sky the boundless upper air breaks open,
301	so the Danaans, when they had thrust the consuming fire from the ships,
302	drew breath a little, but there was no respite from the war;
303	for the Trojans were not yet fleeing before the Achaeans, dear to Ares,
304	in headlong rout away from the black ships,
305	but they still stood against them, and gave way from the ships under compulsion.
306	Then man killed man, as the battle scattered,
307	among the leaders. First the valiant son of Menoetius,
308	as Areilycus turned, at once struck him on the thigh
309	with his sharp spear, and drove the bronze straight through;
310	and the spear broke the bone, and he fell face down on the ground.
311	And warlike Menelaus stabbed Thoas
312	in the chest, where it was bared beside the shield, and loosened his limbs.
313	And the son of Phyleus, watching Amphiclus as he rushed at him,
314	was first to reach out and hit him at the top of the leg, where a man's
315	muscle is thickest; and around the point of the spear
316	the sinews were torn apart; and darkness covered his eyes.
317	And of the sons of Nestor, one stabbed Atymnius with his sharp spear,
318	Antilochus, and drove the bronze spear through his flank,
319	and he fell forward. And Maris, at close quarters with his spear,
320	sprang at Antilochus, angered for his brother,
321	standing in front of the corpse; but godlike Thrasymedes
322	was first to reach out, before he could stab, and did not miss,
323	in the shoulder at once; and the point of the spear tore away the upper arm
324	from the muscles, and broke the bone clean through.
325	He fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes.
326	So these two, brought down by two brothers,
327	went down to Erebus, brave companions of Sarpedon,
328	the spearmen sons of Amisodarus, who reared the Chimaera,
329	the irresistible, an evil to many men.
330	And Ajax, son of Oileus, sprang at Cleobulus
331	and took him alive, caught in the turmoil; but there and then
332	he loosed his strength, striking him on the neck with his hilted sword,
333	and the whole sword grew warm with blood; and his eyes
334	were seized by purple death and mighty fate.
335	And Peneleos and Lycon ran together; for with their spears
336	they had missed each other, and both had thrown in vain;
337	and the two ran together again with swords. There Lycon
338	struck the ridge of his horsehair-crested helmet, and at the socket
339	the sword was shattered; but he struck him on the neck below the ear,
340	Peneleos, and the whole sword sank in, and only the skin
341	held, and the head hung to one side, and his limbs were loosened.
342	And Meriones overtook Acamas with his swift feet and stabbed him
343	on the right shoulder as he was mounting his chariot;
344	and he fell from the chariot, and a mist was poured over his eyes.
345	And Idomeneus stabbed Erymas in the mouth with the pitiless bronze;
346	and the bronze spear passed straight through
347	below the brain, and split the white bones,
348	and his teeth were shaken out, and both his eyes
349	were filled with blood; and he spurted it up through his mouth and nostrils
350	as he gaped; and the black cloud of death enfolded him.
351	These leaders of the Danaans each killed his man.
352	And as wolves fall upon lambs or kids,
353	ravaging, snatching them out of the flocks, when in the mountains
354	they have been scattered through the shepherd's thoughtlessness; and the wolves see them
355	and at once tear them apart, since their hearts have no courage:
356	so the Danaans fell upon the Trojans; and they remembered
357	the ill-sounding rout, and forgot their furious courage.
358	And great Ajax was ever eager to throw his spear at Hector armed in bronze;
359	but he, in his skill at war,
360	covering his broad shoulders with his shield of bull's hide,
361	watched for the whizzing of arrows and the thudding of spears.
362	Truly he knew the victory that turns the battle;
363	but even so he stood his ground, and saved his trusty companions.
364	And as when a cloud comes from Olympus into the sky
365	out of the shining upper air, when Zeus spreads a storm,
366	so from the ships came their shouting and rout,
367	and they did not cross back in good order. And Hector's swift-footed horses
368	carried him out with his armor, and he left the army
369	of the Trojans, whom the ditch that had been dug held back against their will.
370	And many swift chariot-drawing horses in the ditch
371	broke their lords' chariots at the end of the pole and left them,
372	and Patroclus followed, fiercely calling on the Danaans,
373	with evil intent against the Trojans; and they, with shouting and rout,
374	filled all the roads, once they were split apart; and high up a storm wind of dust
375	spread out under the clouds, and the single-hoofed horses strained
376	back toward the city, away from the ships and huts.
377	And Patroclus, wherever he saw the army most in turmoil,
378	there he drove, shouting; and under the axles men fell
379	headlong from their chariots, and the chariots overturned with a clatter.
380	And straight over the ditch leapt the swift horses,
381	immortal, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus,
382	pressing onward, and his heart urged him on against Hector;
383	for he was eager to hit him; but his swift horses carried him away.
384	And as under a storm the whole black earth is weighed down
385	on a day of late summer, when he pours down the most violent rain,
386	Zeus, when in resentment he grows angry with men
387	who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances
388	and drive out justice, caring nothing for the regard of the gods;
389	and all their rivers run in flood,
390	and the torrents then cut away many hillsides,
391	and they groan loudly as they run into the purple sea
392	headlong from the mountains, and the works of men are diminished:
393	so the Trojan mares groaned loudly as they ran.
394	And Patroclus, when he had cut off the foremost battalions,
395	hemmed them back again toward the ships, nor would he let them
396	set foot in the city, as they longed to, but between
397	the ships and the river and the high wall
398	he rushed among them and killed them, and exacted the penalty for many.
399	There first he struck Pronous with his shining spear
400	in the chest, where it was bared beside the shield, and loosened his limbs;
401	he fell with a thud; and he rushed at Thestor, son of Enops,
402	the second; he sat crouching in the well-polished chariot;
403	for he was struck out of his wits, and out of his hands
404	the reins had slipped; and he came up and stabbed him with his spear
405	on the right jaw, and drove it through his teeth,
406	and he took hold of the spear and dragged him over the rail, as when a man
407	sitting on a jutting rock drags a sacred fish
408	out of the sea onto the shore with line and gleaming bronze:
409	so he dragged him gaping from the chariot on his shining spear,
410	and threw him down on his face; and as he fell his spirit left him.
411	Then he struck Erylaus with a stone as he rushed on,
412	full on the head; and it was all split in two
413	in the heavy helmet; and he fell face down on the ground,
414	and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him.
415	Then Erymas and Amphoterus and Epaltes,
416	Tlepolemus, son of Damastor, and Echius and Pyris,
417	Ipheus and Euippus and Polymelus, son of Argeas,
418	all of them, one after another, he brought down to the bountiful earth.
419	And when Sarpedon saw his companions, who wear their tunics without the band,
420	brought down at the hands of Patroclus, son of Menoetius,
421	he called out, approaching the godlike Lycians with words:
422	“Shame, Lycians! Where are you fleeing? Now be swift!
423	For I will meet this man, so that I may learn
424	whoever he is who is prevailing here, and has done much harm
425	to the Trojans, since he has loosened the knees of many brave men.”
426	He spoke, and leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor;
427	and Patroclus on the other side, when he saw him, leapt down from his chariot.
428	And as two vultures with crooked talons and curved beaks
429	fight with loud screams on a high rock,
430	so they rushed at one another, screaming.
431	And seeing them the son of crooked-counseling Cronus pitied them,
432	and he spoke to Hera, his sister and wife:
433	“Ah me, that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me,
434	is fated to be brought down by Patroclus, son of Menoetius!
435	And my heart is divided as I ponder in my mind
436	whether, while he is still alive, out of the tearful battle
437	I should snatch him up and set him down in the rich land of Lycia,
438	or bring him down now at the hands of the son of Menoetius.”
439	Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him:
440	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
441	A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion,
442	do you wish to set free again from grim-sounding death?
443	Do it; but not all of us other gods approve.
