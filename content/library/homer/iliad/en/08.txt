# Homer, Iliad, Book 8 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now saffron-robed Dawn was spreading over all the earth,
2	and Zeus who delights in thunder held an assembly of the gods
3	on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus.
4	He himself spoke to them, and all the gods listened:
5	“Hear me, all you gods and all you goddesses,
6	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
7	Let no female god, then, and no male either,
8	try to frustrate my word; but all of you together
9	approve it, so that I may accomplish these things as soon as I can.
10	Whomever I see going off apart from the gods, intending
11	to help either the Trojans or the Danaans,
12	struck in no seemly way, he will come back to Olympus;
13	or I will take him and hurl him into misty Tartarus,
14	very far off, where the pit beneath the earth is deepest,
15	where the gates are of iron and the threshold of bronze,
16	as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth.
17	Then he will know how far I am the mightiest of all the gods.
18	Come, gods, try it, so that you may all know:
19	hang a golden rope from the sky
20	and lay hold of it, all you gods and all you goddesses;
21	still you could not drag down from the sky to the plain
22	Zeus, the highest counselor, not even if you toiled very hard.
23	But should I in my turn be minded to pull in earnest,
24	I would pull you up, earth itself and sea itself with you;
25	and then I would bind the rope around a peak of Olympus,
26	and all of it would hang in midair.
27	So far am I above the gods, and above men.”
28	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence,
29	amazed at his word, for he had spoken very forcefully.
30	At last the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke among them:
31	“Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords,
32	we too know well that your strength is unyielding;
33	but still we grieve for the Danaan spearmen,
34	who will fill up the measure of an evil fate and perish.
35	We will keep out of the war, as you command;
36	but we will put counsel in the Argives' minds that will help them,
37	so that they do not all perish because of your anger.”
38	Smiling at her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
39	“Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak
40	in full earnest, and I want to be kind to you.”
41	So saying, he yoked to his chariot his two bronze-footed horses,
42	swift-flying, with flowing manes of gold,
43	and he himself put on gold about his body, and took the whip,
44	golden and well made, and mounted his chariot,
45	and whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling
46	between earth and the starry sky.
47	He came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts,
48	to Gargarus, where he has a precinct and a fragrant altar.
49	There the father of men and gods halted his horses,
50	unyoked them from the chariot, and poured a thick mist around them;
51	and he himself sat on the peaks, exulting in his glory,
52	looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans.
53	And the long-haired Achaeans took their meal
54	quickly among the huts, and straight after it they armed themselves.
55	On the other side the Trojans armed themselves throughout the city,
56	fewer as they were; but even so they were eager to fight in the battle,
57	under hard necessity, for their children and for their wives.
58	All the gates were opened, and the army poured out,
59	foot soldiers and horsemen, and a great din arose.
60	And when they had come together into one place,
61	they dashed together their shields of hide, and their spears, and the fury of men
62	in bronze corselets; and the bossed shields
63	pressed against one another, and a great din arose.
64	Then there were cries of pain and cries of triumph together from men
65	killing and being killed, and the earth ran with blood.
66	As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing,
67	so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell.
68	But when the sun stood astride the middle of the sky,
69	then the father held out his golden scales,
70	and in them he set two fates of death that lays men low,
71	of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans,
72	and he took the scales by the middle and raised them; and the fated day of the Achaeans sank.
73	The fates of the Achaeans settled down on the bountiful earth,
74	and those of the Trojans were lifted toward the wide sky.
75	And he himself thundered loudly from Ida, and sent a blazing
76	flash among the army of the Achaeans; and when they saw it
77	they were amazed, and pale fear took hold of them all.
78	Then neither Idomeneus dared to stay, nor Agamemnon,
79	nor did the two Ajaxes stay, attendants of Ares;
80	only Nestor stayed, the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans,
81	not of his own will: his horse was in pain; with an arrow
82	brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair,
83	had struck it on the top of the crown, where the foremost hairs of horses
84	grow on the skull, and a blow is deadliest.
85	In agony it reared, and the arrow sank into its brain,
86	and it threw the horses into confusion, writhing around the bronze.
87	While the old man was cutting away the trace horse's harness,
88	darting at it with his sword, Hector's swift horses
89	came on through the onslaught, carrying a bold charioteer,
90	Hector; and now the old man would have lost his life there,
91	had not Diomedes, good at the war cry, been quick to see it;
92	and he shouted terribly, urging on Odysseus:
93	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
94	where are you fleeing, turning your back like a coward in the throng?
95	Do not let someone plant a spear in your back as you flee;
96	stay, so that we may thrust this savage man away from the old man.”
97	So he spoke, but much-enduring brilliant Odysseus did not hear,
98	and darted past toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans.
99	But the son of Tydeus, alone as he was, mingled with the champions,
100	and stood in front of the horses of the old son of Neleus,
101	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
102	“Old man, the young fighters are truly pressing you hard;
103	your strength is loosed, and grievous old age is upon you;
104	your attendant is a weakling, and your horses are slow.
105	But come, mount my chariot, so that you may see
106	what the horses of Tros are like, how they know the plain,
107	how to pursue and to flee very swiftly this way and that;
108	I took them once from Aeneas, those masters of rout.
109	Let our attendants look after your horses, and let the two of us drive
110	mine straight at the horse-taming Trojans, so that Hector too
111	may know whether my spear also rages in my hands.”
112	So he spoke, and the Gerenian horseman Nestor did not disobey.
113	Then the two attendants took charge of Nestor's horses,
114	mighty Sthenelus and manly Eurymedon,
115	and the two of them mounted Diomedes' chariot.
116	Nestor took the glossy reins in his hands
117	and whipped the horses, and soon they were close to Hector.
118	As he came straight on, the son of Tydeus threw his spear at him;
119	he missed him, but the charioteer, his attendant,
120	Eniopeus, son of high-hearted Thebaeus,
121	he struck in the chest beside the nipple as he held the horses' reins.
122	He fell from the chariot, and the horses shied back,
123	the swift-footed horses; and there his soul and his strength were loosed.
124	Dread grief for his charioteer clouded Hector's mind;
125	yet he left him, grieving though he was for his comrade,
126	to lie there, and went after a bold charioteer; and not for long
127	were the two horses without a driver, for he soon found
128	Archeptolemus, the bold son of Iphitus, and set him behind
129	the swift-footed horses, and put the reins in his hands.
130	Then there would have been ruin, and deeds past mending would have been done,
131	and they would have been penned up in Ilios like lambs,
132	had not the father of men and gods been quick to see it.
133	Thundering terribly, he let fly a gleaming thunderbolt
134	and sent it down to the ground in front of Diomedes' horses;
135	a terrible flame of burning sulfur rose up,
136	and the two horses in terror cowered beneath the chariot.
137	The glossy reins slipped from Nestor's hands,
138	and he was afraid in his heart, and said to Diomedes:
139	“Son of Tydeus, come, turn the single-hoofed horses to flight.
140	Do you not see that no help from Zeus goes with you?
141	For now Zeus, son of Cronus, grants glory to this man
142	today; afterward, if he wishes, to us as well
143	he will give it. No man can hold back the mind of Zeus,
144	however mighty he is, since he is far the stronger.”
145	Then Diomedes, good at the war cry, answered him:
146	“Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right.
147	But this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit:
148	Hector will say one day, speaking among the Trojans,
149	‘The son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships.’
150	So he will boast one day; then may the wide earth gape for me.”
151	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
152	“Ah, son of wise-hearted Tydeus, what a thing you have said!
153	Even if Hector calls you a coward and a weakling,
154	the Trojans and the Dardanians will not believe him,
155	nor the wives of the great-hearted shield-bearing Trojans,
156	whose husbands in their prime you have flung in the dust.”
157	So he spoke, and turned the single-hoofed horses to flight
158	back through the onslaught; and the Trojans and Hector
159	with a wondrous din poured their groaning missiles on them.
160	And over him great Hector of the gleaming helmet shouted aloud:
161	“Son of Tydeus, the Danaans of the swift horses honored you above others
162	with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups;
163	but now they will dishonor you: you are no better than a woman after all.
164	Off with you, wretched doll! It will not be through my giving way
165	that you climb our towers, or carry off our women
166	in your ships; before that I will deal you your doom.”
167	So he spoke, and the son of Tydeus was torn two ways in thought,
168	whether to turn his horses and fight him face to face.
169	Three times he pondered in his mind and in his heart,
170	and three times Zeus the counselor thundered from the mountains of Ida,
171	giving the Trojans a sign of the victory that turns the battle.
172	And Hector called out to the Trojans, shouting aloud:
173	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
174	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage.
175	I know that the son of Cronus has readily nodded his promise to me
176	of victory and great glory, and of a bane to the Danaans.
177	Fools, who contrived these walls,
178	feeble things, worth nothing! They will not hold back my might,
179	and the horses will easily leap over the ditch they have dug.
180	But when I come among the hollow ships,
181	then let there be some remembrance of consuming fire,
182	so that I may set fire to the ships and kill the men themselves,
183	the Argives, beside the ships, dazed by the smoke.”
184	So saying, he called to his horses and spoke:
185	“Xanthus, and you, Podargus, and Aethon and brilliant Lampus,
186	now repay me for your keep, all the care that
187	Andromache, the daughter of great-hearted Eetion,
188	gave you first, setting honey-sweet wheat before you
189	and mixing wine for you to drink whenever your heart bade you,
190	before me, who boast that I am her husband in my prime.
191	But follow hard and hurry, so that we may take
192	Nestor's shield, whose fame now reaches the sky,
193	that it is all of gold, its rods and the shield itself,
194	and from the shoulders of Diomedes, tamer of horses,
195	the intricately worked corselet that Hephaestus toiled to make.
196	If we could take these two, I would hope to make the Achaeans
197	board their swift ships this very night.”
198	So he spoke, boasting, and queen Hera was indignant;
199	she shook on her throne, and made high Olympus quake,
200	and she spoke to Poseidon, the great god, face to face:
201	“Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, not even your
202	heart grieves in your breast for the Danaans as they perish.
203	Yet they bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae,
204	many and pleasing; so you should will them victory.
205	For if we were willing, all of us who help the Danaans,
206	to drive back the Trojans and hold off far-seeing Zeus,
207	he would sit there alone on Ida and fret.”
208	Deeply troubled, the powerful earth-shaker said to her:
209	“Hera, reckless in speech, what a thing you have said!
210	I would not wish that we should fight against Zeus, son of Cronus,
211	we others, since he is far the stronger.”
212	So they spoke such words to one another;
213	and all the space that the ditch enclosed, out from the ships beyond the wall,
214	was filled alike with horses and shield-bearing men
215	hemmed in; and the one who hemmed them in, the equal of swift Ares,
216	was Hector, son of Priam, now that Zeus had given him glory.
217	And now he would have burned the balanced ships with blazing fire,
218	had not queen Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind
219	to bestir himself and quickly urge on the Achaeans.
220	He went along the huts and ships of the Achaeans,
221	holding a great purple cloak in his strong hand,
222	and he stood by Odysseus' black ship, huge in the hull,
223	which lay in the middle, so that a shout could carry to both ends,
224	both to the huts of Ajax, son of Telamon,
225	and to those of Achilles, who had hauled up their balanced ships
226	at the farthest ends, trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands.
227	And he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Danaans:
228	“Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!
229	Where have our boasts gone, when we said that we were the best,
230	the empty boasts you uttered on Lemnos,
231	eating much meat of straight-horned cattle
232	and drinking mixing bowls brim-full of wine,
233	that each of you would stand against a hundred, two hundred Trojans
234	in battle? But now we are not worth even one man,
235	Hector, who will soon burn the ships with blazing fire.
236	Father Zeus, have you ever before struck any of the mighty kings
237	with such blind folly, and robbed him of great glory?
238	Yet I say that I never passed by your beautiful altar
239	in my many-benched ship on my ill-starred way here,
240	but on every one I burned the fat and the thighs of oxen,
241	eager to sack well-walled Troy.
242	But, Zeus, fulfill for me at least this wish:
243	let us at least escape and get away,
244	and do not let the Achaeans be so beaten down by the Trojans.”
245	So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears,
246	and nodded to him that his people would be saved and not perish.
247	At once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds,
248	holding in its talons a fawn, the young of a swift deer;
249	and beside the beautiful altar of Zeus it dropped the fawn,
250	where the Achaeans used to sacrifice to Zeus, lord of all omens.
251	When they saw that the bird had come from Zeus,
252	they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle.
253	Then none of the Danaans, many as they were,
254	could boast that he had held his swift horses ahead of the son of Tydeus,
255	driven them out over the ditch, and fought face to face;
256	but far the first, he killed a helmeted man of the Trojans,
257	Agelaus, son of Phradmon. He had turned his horses to flight,
258	and as the man turned, he planted his spear in his back
259	between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest;
260	and he fell from the chariot, and his armor clattered upon him.
261	After him came the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
262	and after them the two Ajaxes, clothed in furious courage,
263	and after them Idomeneus and Idomeneus' squire
264	Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men,
265	and after them Eurypylus, the splendid son of Euaemon;
266	and Teucer came ninth, stretching his back-bent bow,
267	and he took his stand under the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon.
268	Then Ajax would move his shield aside, and the hero
269	would look about him, and when he had shot someone in the throng
270	and hit him, that man would fall there and lose his life,
271	and he would go back again, like a child under its mother, and slip
272	to Ajax, who would hide him with his shining shield.
273	Whom then did noble Teucer kill first of the Trojans?
274	Orsilochus first, and Ormenus and Ophelestes,
275	Daetor and Chromius and godlike Lycophontes,
276	and Amopaon, son of Polyaemon, and Melanippus:
277	all of them, one after another, he brought down to the bountiful earth.
278	Agamemnon, lord of men, rejoiced when he saw him
279	destroying the battalions of the Trojans with his strong bow;
280	and he came and stood beside him and spoke to him:
281	“Teucer, dear heart, son of Telamon, lord of the people,
282	shoot on like this, and you may become a light to the Danaans
283	and to your father Telamon, who reared you when you were small
284	and cared for you in his own house, bastard though you were;
285	bring him to glory, far away though he is.
286	And I will tell you this, just as it will surely come to pass:
287	if aegis-bearing Zeus and Athena grant me
288	to sack the well-built citadel of Ilios,
289	I will put into your hand, first after me, a gift of honor,
290	a tripod, or two horses with their chariot,
291	or a woman, who would go up into your bed with you.”
292	Answering him, noble Teucer said:
293	“Most glorious son of Atreus, why do you urge me on
294	when I am eager myself? I do not stop, as far as I have strength;
295	ever since we drove them back toward Ilios,
296	from that time I have been lying in wait with my bow and killing men.
297	Eight long-barbed arrows I have let fly,
298	and all of them were fixed in the flesh of young men swift in war;
299	but this mad dog I cannot hit.”
300	He spoke, and let fly another arrow from the string
301	straight at Hector, and his heart was eager to hit him;
302	and he missed him, but noble Gorgythion,
303	the brave son of Priam, he struck in the chest with the arrow,
304	whom his mother, a bride brought from Aesyme, had borne,
305	lovely Castianeira, like the goddesses in form.
306	As a poppy droops its head to one side, a poppy in a garden
307	heavy with its seed and with the showers of spring,
308	so his head drooped to one side, weighed down by his helmet.
309	Teucer let fly another arrow from the string
310	straight at Hector, and his heart was eager to hit him.
311	But he missed again, for Apollo made it swerve;
312	but Archeptolemus, Hector's bold charioteer,
313	he struck in the chest beside the nipple as the man rushed into the fight.
314	He fell from the chariot, and the horses shied back,
315	the swift-footed horses; and there his soul and his strength were loosed.
316	Dread grief for his charioteer clouded Hector's mind;
317	yet he left him, grieving though he was for his comrade,
318	and told Cebriones, his brother, who was close by,
319	to take the horses' reins; and he heard and did not disobey.
320	And he himself leapt to the ground from the all-gleaming chariot,
321	shouting terribly; and he took up a stone in his hand,
322	and went straight at Teucer, and his heart bade him hit him.
323	Teucer had taken a bitter arrow from his quiver
324	and set it on the string; but Hector of the gleaming helmet,
325	as the archer drew it back by his shoulder, where the collarbone parts
326	the neck and the chest, and a blow is deadliest,
327	there, as he took aim at him, struck him with the jagged stone,
328	and broke his bowstring; and his hand went numb at the wrist,
329	and he sank to his knees and stayed there, and the bow fell from his hand.
330	But Ajax did not neglect his brother when he fell,
331	but ran and stood over him, and covered him with his shield.
332	Then two trusty companions stooped under him,
333	Mecisteus, son of Echius, and brilliant Alastor,
334	and carried him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.
335	And again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans,
336	and they drove the Achaeans straight back to the deep ditch;
337	and Hector went among the foremost, exulting in his strength.
338	As when a hound goes after a wild boar or a lion
339	with swift feet, and catches at it from behind,
340	at hips and buttocks, and watches for it as it wheels,
341	so Hector pressed hard on the long-haired Achaeans,
342	always killing the hindmost; and they fled.
343	But when they had crossed the stakes and the ditch
344	in their flight, and many had been brought down at the hands of the Trojans,
345	they halted beside the ships and stood their ground,
346	calling to one another, and to all the gods
347	they lifted up their hands, and each man prayed aloud.
348	And Hector wheeled his fine-maned horses this way and that,
349	with the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares, ruin of mortals.
350	Seeing the men, the white-armed goddess Hera pitied them,
351	and at once she addressed Athena with winged words:
352	“Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, shall we two no longer
353	care for the Danaans as they perish, even at the last,
354	who will fill up the measure of an evil fate and perish
355	under the rush of one man? And he rages past all bearing,
356	Hector, son of Priam, and has done much harm.”
357	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to her in turn:
358	“Yes, and how I wish that man would lose his strength and his life,
359	killed at the hands of the Argives in his native land;
360	but my father rages with a mind that is not good,
361	hard as he is, forever perverse, the thwarter of my purposes;
362	nor does he remember at all how very often I saved his son
363	when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him.
364	He would weep to the sky, and Zeus
365	would send me down from the sky to help him.
366	If I had known all this in my shrewd mind
367	when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades the gatekeeper
368	to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades,
369	he would never have escaped the sheer streams of the water of Styx.
370	But now he hates me, and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis,
371	who kissed his knees and took his chin in her hand,
372	begging him to honor Achilles, sacker of cities.
373	Yet the day will come when he calls me his dear gray-eyed one again.
374	But now harness the single-hoofed horses for the two of us,
375	while I go into the house of aegis-bearing Zeus
376	and arm myself in armor for war, so that I may see
377	whether the son of Priam, Hector of the gleaming helmet,
378	will be glad when we two appear along the bridges of war,
379	or whether some Trojan too will glut the dogs and the birds
380	with his fat and his flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans.”
381	So she spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey.
382	She went about harnessing the horses with golden headbands,
383	Hera, the august goddess, daughter of great Cronus;
384	but Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
385	let her soft robe fall on her father's floor,
386	richly worked, which she herself had made and labored at with her hands;
387	and putting on the tunic of Zeus the cloud-gatherer
388	she armed herself in armor for tearful war.
389	And she stepped onto the flaming chariot, and took up her spear,
390	heavy, huge, and massive, with which she overpowers the ranks of men,
391	of heroes, against whom she, the daughter of the mighty father, bears resentment.
392	And Hera quickly touched the horses with the whip;
393	and of themselves the gates of heaven groaned open, which the Hours kept,
394	to whom great heaven and Olympus are entrusted,
395	both to throw open the thick cloud and to close it.
396	Through them they drove the horses, spurred on by the goad.
397	But when father Zeus saw them from Ida, he was terribly angry,
398	and he urged on golden-winged Iris to carry a message:
399	“Go, swift Iris, turn them back, and do not let them come
400	face to face with me; it will not be a fair thing if we meet in war.
401	For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass:
402	I will lame their swift horses under the chariot,
403	and hurl the two of them from the chariot, and smash it;
404	and not in ten circling years
405	will they heal of the wounds the thunderbolt deals them,
406	so that the gray-eyed one may know what it is to fight her father.
407	With Hera I am not so indignant or so angry,
408	for she is always used to thwarting whatever I say.”
409	So he spoke, and storm-footed Iris rose to carry the message,
410	and she went from the mountains of Ida to high Olympus.
411	At the outermost gates of many-folded Olympus
412	she met them and held them back, and gave them the message of Zeus:
413	“Where are you rushing? What madness is in your hearts?
414	The son of Cronus will not let you help the Argives.
415	For this is the threat of the son of Cronus, and he will make it good:
416	that he will lame your swift horses under the chariot,
417	and hurl you yourselves from the chariot, and smash it;
418	and not in ten circling years
419	will you heal of the wounds the thunderbolt deals you,
420	so that you may know, gray-eyed one, what it is to fight your father.
421	With Hera he is not so indignant or so angry,
422	for she is always used to thwarting whatever he says.
423	But you are most dread, you shameless dog, if you really
424	dare to lift your huge spear against Zeus.”
425	So saying, swift-footed Iris went away,
426	and Hera spoke to Athena:
427	“Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, I will no longer
428	let the two of us fight against Zeus for the sake of mortals.
429	Let one of them perish and another live,
430	as chance may fall; and let him, thinking his own thoughts in his heart,
431	judge between Trojans and Danaans, as is fitting.”
432	So she spoke, and turned back the single-hoofed horses.
433	The Hours unyoked their fine-maned horses for them,
434	and tied them at their ambrosial mangers,
435	and leaned the chariot against the gleaming walls;
436	and the goddesses themselves sat down on golden chairs
437	among the other gods, grieving at heart.
438	But father Zeus drove his well-wheeled chariot and horses from Ida
439	to Olympus, and came to the seats of the gods.
440	For him the famous shaker of the earth unyoked the horses,
441	and set the chariot on its stand and spread a cloth over it;
442	and far-seeing Zeus himself sat down on his golden throne,
443	and great Olympus shook beneath his feet.
444	Athena and Hera alone sat apart from Zeus,
445	and said nothing to him, and asked him nothing;
446	but he understood in his heart, and spoke:
447	“Why are you so grieved, Athena and Hera?
448	Surely you have not tired yourselves in the battle where men win glory
449	destroying the Trojans, against whom you have conceived a dread grudge.
450	In any case, such is my might and such are my irresistible hands,
451	that all the gods there are on Olympus could not turn me back;
452	but trembling seized your shining limbs before
453	you ever saw the war and the grim deeds of war.
454	For I will tell you this, and it would have come to pass:
455	struck by the thunderbolt, you would not have come back on your chariot
456	to Olympus, where the seat of the immortals is.”
457	So he spoke, and Athena and Hera muttered against it;
458	they sat side by side, and were devising evils for the Trojans.
459	Athena kept silent and said nothing,
460	sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her;
461	but Hera's breast could not contain her anger, and she spoke to him:
462	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
463	We too know well that your strength is no weakling's;
464	but still we grieve for the Danaan spearmen,
465	who will fill up the measure of an evil fate and perish.
466	We will keep out of the war, if you command;
467	but we will put counsel in the Argives' minds that will help them,
468	so that they do not all perish because of your anger.”
469	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
470	“At dawn you will see the son of Cronus, supreme in might,
471	more than ever, if you wish, ox-eyed queen Hera,
472	destroying the great army of the Argive spearmen.
473	For mighty Hector will not cease from war
474	until the swift-footed son of Peleus is roused beside the ships
475	on the day when they fight at the sterns of the ships
476	in the direst strait, over Patroclus dead.
477	For so it is decreed. And I do not care about you
478	in your anger, not even if you go to the lowest limits
479	of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronus
480	sit and have no joy in the rays of Hyperion the Sun
481	or in the winds, and deep Tartarus is all around them;
482	not even if you go wandering there do I care
483	about your sulking, since there is nothing more shameless than you.”
484	So he spoke, and white-armed Hera said nothing to him.
485	And the bright light of the sun fell into Oceanus,
486	drawing black night over the grain-giving earth.
487	For the Trojans the light went down against their will, but to the Achaeans
488	welcome, thrice prayed for, came the murky night.
489	Then shining Hector held an assembly of the Trojans,
490	leading them away from the ships, beside the eddying river,
491	in a clear space, where the ground showed free of corpses.
492	They stepped down from their chariots to the ground and listened to the word
493	that Hector, dear to Zeus, spoke; and in his hand
494	he held a spear eleven cubits long; and before him shone
495	the bronze point of the spear, and a golden ring ran around it.
496	Leaning on it, he spoke his words among the Trojans:
497	“Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies:
498	I thought just now that I would destroy the ships and all the Achaeans
499	and go back home to windy Ilios;
500	but darkness came first, and that, above all, has now saved
501	the Argives and their ships on the shore of the sea.
502	But now let us yield to black night
503	and make ready our supper; unyoke the fine-maned horses
504	from the chariots, and set fodder beside them;
505	and bring from the city cattle and fat sheep,
506	quickly, and provide yourselves with honey-sweet wine
507	and bread from the halls, and gather much wood besides,
508	so that all night long until early-born Dawn
509	we may burn many fires, and their glare may reach the sky,
510	in case even by night the long-haired Achaeans
511	set out to flee over the broad back of the sea.
512	Let them not board their ships at their ease, without a struggle;
513	let many a one of them nurse a wound even at home,
514	struck by an arrow or a sharp spear
515	as he leaps aboard his ship, so that others too may shrink
516	from bringing tearful Ares against the horse-taming Trojans.
517	And let the heralds, dear to Zeus, proclaim through the city
518	that boys in their first youth and gray-templed old men
519	are to spend the night around the city on the god-built towers;
520	and let the womenfolk, each in her hall,
521	burn a great fire; and let there be a steadfast watch,
522	so that no ambush may enter the city while the army is away.
523	Let it be as I say, great-hearted Trojans.
524	The word I have spoken now, let it be a sound word;
525	and the other I will speak at dawn among the horse-taming Trojans.
526	I hope, as I pray to Zeus and the other gods,
527	to drive away from here these dogs borne along by the fates,
528	whom the fates bring here on the black ships.
529	But for the night we will guard ourselves,
530	and early, at daybreak, armed in our armor,
531	we will rouse sharp Ares beside the hollow ships.
532	I will know whether the son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes,
533	will push me back from the ships to the wall, or whether I
534	will kill him with the bronze and carry off his bloody spoils.
535	Tomorrow he will learn his own prowess, if he stands up to my spear
536	as it comes at him; but among the foremost, I think,
537	he will lie wounded, and many of his companions around him,
538	when the sun rises tomorrow. If only I were
539	immortal and ageless all my days,
540	and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored,
541	as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives.”
542	So Hector spoke, and the Trojans shouted in applause.
543	They unyoked their sweating horses from under the yoke,
544	and tied them with straps beside their chariots, each man by his own;
545	and they brought from the city cattle and fat sheep,
546	quickly, and provided themselves with honey-sweet wine
547	and bread from the halls, and gathered much wood besides.
548	[And they offered perfect hecatombs to the immortals,]
549	and the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven,
550	[sweet savor; but the blessed gods took no share of it,]
551	[nor did they wish to; for sacred Ilios was hateful to them,]
552	[and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.]
553	And they, in high spirits, along the bridges of war
554	sat all night long, and many fires burned among them.
555	As when in the sky the stars around the shining moon
556	shine out clear, when the upper air is windless,
557	and all the lookout places and the high headlands stand out,
558	and the glens; and from the sky the boundless upper air breaks open,
559	and all the stars are seen, and the shepherd is glad at heart;
560	so many, between the ships and the streams of the Xanthus,
561	shone the fires the Trojans were burning before Ilios.
562	A thousand fires were burning on the plain, and beside each
563	sat fifty men in the glare of the blazing fire.
564	And the horses, munching white barley and spelt,
565	stood beside the chariots and waited for Dawn of the fair throne.
