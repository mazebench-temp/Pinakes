# Homer, Iliad, Book 6 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now the dread din of battle between Trojans and Achaeans was left to itself,
2	and again and again the battle surged this way and that across the plain
3	as they aimed their bronze-fitted spears at one another
4	between the streams of the Simoeis and the Xanthus.
5	Telamonian Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans, was the first
6	to break a battalion of the Trojans, and he brought light to his companions
7	by striking the man who was the best among the Thracians,
8	the son of Eussorus, Acamas, tall and brave.
9	He was first to strike him on the ridge of his horsehair-crested helmet,
10	and fixed it in his forehead, and it pierced into the bone,
11	the bronze point; and darkness covered his eyes.
12	And Diomedes, good at the war cry, killed Axylus,
13	son of Teuthras, who lived in well-built Arisbe,
14	wealthy in substance, and he was a friend to all men;
15	for he lived in a house beside the road and gave welcome to all.
16	But not one of them was there then to keep grim destruction from him
17	by standing in front of him to meet the enemy; he took the lives of both,
18	himself and his attendant Calesius, who at that time
19	drove his horses as his charioteer; and the two of them went down beneath the earth.
20	And Euryalus slew and stripped Dresus and Opheltius,
21	and went after Aesepus and Pedasus, whom once the nymph
22	Abarbarea, a Naiad, bore to noble Bucolion.
23	Bucolion was the son of noble Laomedon,
24	the eldest by birth, but his mother bore him in secret;
25	and while he was tending his sheep he lay with her in love and bed,
26	and she conceived and bore twin sons.
27	And of these too he loosened the strength and the shining limbs,
28	the son of Mecisteus, and stripped the armor from their shoulders.
29	And Polypoetes, steadfast in battle, killed Astyalus;
30	and Odysseus slew and stripped Pidytes of Percote
31	with his bronze spear, and Teucer slew brilliant Aretaon.
32	And Antilochus slew Ablerus with his shining spear,
33	the son of Nestor, and Agamemnon, lord of men, slew Elatus;
34	he lived beside the banks of the fair-flowing Satnioeis,
35	in steep Pedasus. And the hero Leitus killed Phylacus
36	as he fled; and Eurypylus slew and stripped Melanthius.
37	Then Menelaus, good at the war cry, took Adrastus
38	alive; for his horses, bolting in terror across the plain,
39	caught in a tamarisk branch and broke the curved chariot
40	at the end of the pole, and went on by themselves
41	toward the city, where the others too were fleeing in terror,
42	and he himself tumbled out of the chariot beside the wheel,
43	face down in the dust, on his mouth; and beside him stood
44	Menelaus, son of Atreus, holding his long-shadowed spear.
45	Then Adrastus clasped his knees and begged him:
46	“Take me alive, son of Atreus, and accept a worthy ransom;
47	many treasures lie stored in the house of my wealthy father,
48	bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil;
49	from these my father would gladly give you a boundless ransom,
50	if he heard that I was alive at the ships of the Achaeans.”
51	So he spoke, and he began to win over the heart in his breast;
52	and now he was on the point of sending him to the swift ships of the Achaeans,
53	giving him to his attendant to lead down; but Agamemnon
54	came running to meet him, and rebuked him and said:
55	“My dear Menelaus, why are you so concerned
56	for these men? Were you so very well treated in your house
57	by the Trojans? Let not one of them escape sheer destruction
58	and our hands, not even the boy whom his mother
59	carries in her womb, not even he escape, but let them all together
60	perish from Ilios, unmourned and without a trace.”
61	So saying, the hero turned his brother's mind,
62	urging what was due; and his brother pushed away from him with his hand
63	the hero Adrastus; and powerful Agamemnon
64	stabbed him in the flank, and he fell back; and the son of Atreus
65	set his heel on his chest and pulled out the ash spear.
66	And Nestor called out to the Argives, shouting aloud:
67	“Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares,
68	let no one now fling himself on the spoils and stay behind
69	so as to come back to the ships carrying the most;
70	but let us kill men; and afterward, at your ease,
71	you can strip the dead bodies all over the plain.”
72	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
73	Then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares,
74	would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice,
75	had there not stood beside Aeneas and Hector, and spoken to them,
76	Helenus, son of Priam, by far the best of the bird-seers:
77	“Aeneas and Hector, since on you most of all rests the toil
78	of the Trojans and the Lycians, because you are the best
79	in every undertaking, both in fighting and in counsel,
80	stand fast here, and hold the people back before the gates,
81	ranging everywhere, before they fall into the arms of their women
82	in flight, and become a joy to their enemies.
83	And when you two have urged on all the battalions,
84	we will stay here and fight the Danaans,
85	hard pressed though we are; for necessity compels us.
86	But you, Hector, go to the city, and then tell
87	your mother and mine; let her gather the old women
88	to the temple of gray-eyed Athena on the height of the city,
89	and open with the key the doors of the sacred house,
90	and the robe that seems to her the loveliest and largest
91	in her hall, and by far the dearest to her own heart,
92	let her lay on the knees of Athena of the lovely hair,
93	and promise to sacrifice twelve cows to her in her temple,
94	yearlings, never touched by the goad, if she will pity
95	the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children,
96	so that she may hold the son of Tydeus back from sacred Ilios,
97	that savage spearman, mighty master of rout,
98	who, I say, has become the strongest of the Achaeans.
99	We never feared like this even Achilles, leader of men,
100	who they say was born of a goddess; but this man
101	rages beyond measure, and no one can match his might.”
102	So he spoke, and Hector did not disobey his brother;
103	and at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor,
104	and brandishing his sharp spears he went everywhere through the army,
105	urging them to fight, and he awoke the dread din of battle.
106	And they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans;
107	and the Argives gave ground and ceased from slaughter,
108	and thought that one of the immortals had come down from the starry sky
109	to bring help to the Trojans, so sharply did they rally.
110	And Hector called out to the Trojans, shouting aloud:
111	“High-hearted Trojans and far-famed allies,
112	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage,
113	while I go up to Ilios and tell the elders
114	who sit in council, and our wives,
115	to pray to the gods and promise them hecatombs.”
116	So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet went away;
117	and the black hide knocked against his ankles and his neck,
118	the rim that ran around the outer edge of his bossed shield.
119	And Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, and the son of Tydeus
120	came together between the two armies, eager to fight.
121	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
122	Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke to him first:
123	“Who are you, good sir, among mortal men?
124	For I have never seen you in the battle where men win glory
125	before now; but now you have come out far in front of all
126	in your daring, since you have stood up to my long-shadowed spear.
127	It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might.
128	But if you are one of the immortals come down from heaven,
129	I would not fight with the gods of heaven.
130	For not even the son of Dryas, mighty Lycurgus,
131	lived long, who strove with the gods of heaven;
132	who once drove the nurses of raving Dionysus
133	down the sacred Nysean mount; and all of them together
134	let their sacred wands fall to the ground as man-slaying Lycurgus
135	struck them with his ox-goad; and Dionysus, put to flight,
136	plunged beneath the wave of the sea, and Thetis took him to her bosom
137	in his fear; for a strong trembling had hold of him at the man's shouting.
138	Then the gods who live at ease were angry with him,
139	and the son of Cronus made him blind; and he did not live
140	long after, since he was hated by all the immortal gods.
141	Nor would I wish to fight with the blessed gods.
142	But if you are one of the mortals who eat the fruit of the field,
143	come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction.”
144	Then the shining son of Hippolochus spoke to him in turn:
145	“Great-hearted son of Tydeus, why do you ask about my lineage?
146	As is the generation of leaves, so is that of men.
147	The wind scatters the leaves on the ground, but the forest
148	puts forth others as it flourishes, and the season of spring comes on;
149	so it is with the generations of men: one grows, another passes away.
150	But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well
151	our lineage—and many men know it—
152	there is a city, Ephyra, in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos,
153	and there lived Sisyphus, who was the craftiest of men,
154	Sisyphus, son of Aeolus; and he fathered a son, Glaucus,
155	and Glaucus fathered noble Bellerophon.
156	To him the gods gave beauty and lovely manhood;
157	but Proetus devised evil against him in his heart,
158	and drove him from the land, since he was far the mightier
159	among the Argives; for Zeus had made them subject to his scepter.
160	The wife of Proetus, brilliant Anteia, was mad with longing
161	to lie with him in secret love; but she could not
162	persuade wise-hearted Bellerophon, for his thoughts were good.
163	So she lied, and said to Proetus the king:
164	‘Either die, Proetus, or kill Bellerophon,
165	who wanted to lie with me in love against my will.’
166	So she spoke, and anger seized the lord at what he heard;
167	he shrank from killing him, for his heart held back in awe from that,
168	but he sent him to Lycia, and gave him grim signs,
169	scratching on a folded tablet many things to destroy a man's life,
170	and told him to show them to his own father-in-law, so that he would perish.
171	And he went to Lycia under the blameless escort of the gods.
172	But when he came to Lycia and the flowing Xanthus,
173	the lord of wide Lycia honored him with a ready heart;
174	nine days he entertained him and sacrificed nine oxen.
175	But when the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared,
176	then he questioned him and asked to see the sign
177	that he was bringing from his son-in-law Proetus.
178	And when he had received the evil sign of his son-in-law,
179	first he told him to kill the irresistible Chimaera;
180	and she was of divine race, not of men,
181	a lion in front, a serpent behind, and in the middle a she-goat,
182	breathing out the terrible might of blazing fire.
183	And he killed her, obeying the portents of the gods.
184	Second, he fought with the glorious Solymi;
185	that, he said, was the fiercest battle of men he ever entered.
186	Third, he killed the Amazons, the equals of men.
187	And as he was coming back, the king wove another cunning trap for him;
188	he chose the best men out of wide Lycia
189	and set an ambush; but they never came home again,
190	for noble Bellerophon killed them all.
191	But when the king knew that he was the noble offspring of a god,
192	he kept him there, and gave him his daughter,
193	and gave him half of all his royal honor;
194	and the Lycians marked out for him a domain finer than all others,
195	a fair one, of orchard and plowland, for him to hold.
196	And she bore three children to wise-hearted Bellerophon,
197	Isander and Hippolochus and Laodameia.
198	With Laodameia Zeus the counselor lay,
199	and she bore godlike Sarpedon, armed in bronze.
200	But when he too was hated by all the gods,
201	he wandered alone over the Aleian plain,
202	eating his heart out, avoiding the paths of men;
203	and Ares, insatiable of war, killed his son Isander
204	as he fought the glorious Solymi;
205	and Artemis of the golden reins killed his daughter in her anger.
206	But Hippolochus begot me, and from him I claim my birth;
207	and he sent me to Troy, and gave me many instructions,
208	always to be the best and to excel above the others,
209	and not to shame the race of my fathers, who were by far the best
210	in Ephyra and in wide Lycia.
211	This is the lineage and the blood I claim.”
212	So he spoke, and Diomedes, good at the war cry, was glad;
213	he fixed his spear in the bountiful earth,
214	and spoke to the shepherd of the people with soothing words:
215	“Then truly you are an old guest-friend of mine from our fathers' time;
216	for brilliant Oeneus once entertained noble Bellerophon
217	in his halls, and kept him there twenty days;
218	and they gave each other fine guest-gifts:
219	Oeneus gave a belt bright with purple,
220	and Bellerophon a two-handled cup of gold,
221	which I left in my house when I came here.
222	Tydeus I do not remember, since I was still little
223	when he left me, when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes.
224	So now I am your dear guest-friend in the heart of Argos,
225	and you are mine in Lycia, whenever I come to that land.
226	Let us avoid each other's spears, even in the throng;
227	for there are many Trojans and famous allies for me
228	to kill, whomever a god gives me and my feet overtake,
229	and many Achaeans in turn for you to slay, whomever you can.
230	And let us exchange armor with each other, so that these men too
231	may know that we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time.”
232	So the two spoke, and leapt down from their chariots,
233	and took each other's hands and pledged their faith.
234	But then Zeus, son of Cronus, took away Glaucus' wits,
235	for he exchanged armor with Diomedes, son of Tydeus,
236	gold for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine.
237	And when Hector came to the Scaean gates and the oak,
238	the wives and daughters of the Trojans came running around him,
239	asking after their sons and brothers and kinsmen
240	and husbands; and he told them to pray to the gods,
241	all of them in turn; and sorrows were in store for many.
242	But when he came to the beautiful house of Priam,
243	built with polished colonnades—and in it
244	were fifty chambers of polished stone,
245	built close to one another, and there the sons
246	of Priam slept beside their wedded wives;
247	and for his daughters, on the other side, facing them within the courtyard,
248	were twelve roofed chambers of polished stone,
249	built close to one another, and there the sons-in-law
250	of Priam slept beside their modest wives—
251	there his generous mother came to meet him,
252	leading in Laodice, the most beautiful in form of her daughters;
253	and she clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
254	“My child, why have you left the bold war and come here?
255	Surely the sons of the Achaeans, of evil name, are wearing us down
256	as they fight around the city, and your heart has sent you here
257	to come and lift up your hands to Zeus from the height of the city.
258	But wait while I bring you honey-sweet wine,
259	so that you may pour a libation to father Zeus and the other immortals
260	first, and then you yourself will be the better for it, if you drink.
261	For in a weary man wine greatly increases might,
262	as you are weary from defending your kinsmen.”
263	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered her:
264	“Do not bring me wine sweet to the heart, lady mother,
265	for fear you unnerve me and rob me of my might, and I forget my courage;
266	and with unwashed hands I shrink from pouring sparkling wine to Zeus;
267	nor may a man in any way offer the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds,
268	a prayer when he is spattered with blood and gore.
269	But you go to the temple of Athena, driver of spoil,
270	with burnt offerings, gathering the old women;
271	and the robe that is the loveliest and largest
272	in your hall, and by far the dearest to your own heart,
273	lay it on the knees of Athena of the lovely hair,
274	and promise to sacrifice twelve cows to her in her temple,
275	yearlings, never touched by the goad, if she will pity
276	the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children,
277	if she will hold the son of Tydeus back from sacred Ilios,
278	that savage spearman, mighty master of rout.
279	But you go to the temple of Athena, driver of spoil,
280	and I will go after Paris, to call him,
281	if he is willing to listen to what I say. There and then
282	may the earth gape for him! For the Olympian has raised him as a great bane
283	to the Trojans and to great-hearted Priam and his sons.
284	If I could see him going down into the house of Hades,
285	I would think my heart had forgotten its joyless misery.”
286	So he spoke, and she went to the hall and called to her handmaids,
287	and they gathered the old women throughout the city.
288	But she herself went down into the fragrant chamber,
289	where her robes were, richly embroidered, the work of women
290	of the Sidonians, whom godlike Alexander himself
291	had brought from Sidon, sailing over the wide sea
292	on that voyage on which he brought back Helen, daughter of a noble father;
293	of these Hecuba took one and carried it as a gift to Athena,
294	the one that was most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest,
295	and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others.
296	And she went her way, and many old women hurried after her.
297	And when they came to the temple of Athena on the height of the city,
298	fair-cheeked Theano opened the doors for them,
299	daughter of Cisseus, the wife of Antenor, tamer of horses;
300	for the Trojans had made her priestess of Athena.
301	And with a loud cry they all lifted up their hands to Athena;
302	and fair-cheeked Theano took the robe
303	and laid it on the knees of Athena of the lovely hair,
304	and made her prayer to the daughter of great Zeus:
305	“Lady Athena, protector of the city, brilliant among goddesses,
306	break the spear of Diomedes, and grant that he himself
307	may fall headlong before the Scaean gates,
308	so that we may now at once sacrifice twelve cows to you in your temple,
309	yearlings, never touched by the goad, if you will pity
310	the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children.”
311	So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head.
312	So they prayed to the daughter of great Zeus,
313	and Hector went to the house of Alexander,
314	a beautiful house, which he himself had built with the men who were then the best
315	builders in deep-soiled Troy,
316	who made him a bedchamber and a hall and a courtyard
317	near those of Priam and Hector on the height of the city.
318	There Hector, dear to Zeus, went in, and in his hand
319	he held a spear eleven cubits long; and before him shone
320	the bronze point of the spear, and a golden ring ran around it.
321	He found him in his chamber busy with his beautiful armor,
322	his shield and his corselet, and handling his curved bow;
323	and Argive Helen sat among her slave women
324	and was directing her handmaids in their glorious work.
325	Seeing him, Hector rebuked him with shaming words:
326	“Strange one, you do wrong to keep this anger in your heart.
327	The people are dying around the city and the steep wall
328	in the fighting; and it is for your sake that the war cry and the war
329	blaze around this city; and you yourself would quarrel with any other man
330	whom you saw holding back from hateful war.
331	Up, then, for fear the city will soon burn with consuming fire.”
332	Then godlike Alexander spoke to him in turn:
333	“Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair,
334	I will tell you this; and you take heed and listen to me.
335	It was not so much because I was angry or indignant with the Trojans
336	that I sat in my chamber; I wanted to give myself up to grief.
337	But just now my wife, persuading me with gentle words,
338	spurred me to the war; and it seems to me, too,
339	that it will be better so; victory shifts from man to man.
340	But come now, wait while I put on my armor of war;
341	or go, and I will follow; and I think I will overtake you.”
342	So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet said nothing to him,
343	and Helen spoke to him with soothing words:
344	“Brother-in-law to me, dog that I am, evil-scheming and chilling,
345	how I wish that on the day when my mother first bore me
346	an evil storm of wind had carried me away
347	to a mountain or into the wave of the loud-roaring sea,
348	where the wave would have swept me away before these things came to pass.
349	But since the gods ordained these evils so,
350	then I wish I had been the wife of a better man,
351	one who felt the blame and the many reproaches of men.
352	But this man's mind is not steadfast now, nor will it ever be
353	hereafter; and so I think he will reap the fruit of it.
354	But come now, come in and sit on this chair,
355	brother-in-law, since the toil has beset your mind most of all
356	for my sake, dog that I am, and for Alexander's blind folly,
357	on whom Zeus has set an evil fate, so that hereafter
358	we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be.”
359	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet answered her:
360	“Do not ask me to sit, Helen, though you love me; you will not persuade me.
361	For already my heart is eager to go and help
362	the Trojans, who long for me greatly when I am away.
363	But you rouse this man, and let him hurry himself,
364	so that he may catch up with me while I am still inside the city.
365	For I too will go home, to see
366	my household and my dear wife and my infant son.
367	For I do not know if I will ever come back to them again,
368	or if the gods will now bring me down at the hands of the Achaeans.”
369	So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet went away;
370	and quickly he came to his well-appointed house,
371	but he did not find white-armed Andromache in the halls;
372	she had gone with her child and a well-robed handmaid
373	and was standing on the tower, lamenting and weeping.
374	And when Hector did not find his noble wife within,
375	he went and stood on the threshold, and spoke among the slave women:
376	“Come, slave women, tell me truly:
377	where has white-armed Andromache gone from the hall?
378	Has she gone to my sisters, or to my brothers' well-robed wives,
379	or to the temple of Athena, where the other
380	Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess?”
381	Then the ready housekeeper spoke to him in turn:
382	“Hector, since you bid me tell you the truth,
383	she has not gone to your sisters or to your brothers' well-robed wives,
384	nor to the temple of Athena, where the other
385	Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess;
386	she has gone to the great tower of Ilios, because she heard
387	that the Trojans were hard pressed, and the Achaeans had great strength.
388	She has gone hurrying to the wall
389	like a woman out of her mind; and the nurse is carrying the child with her.”
390	The housekeeper spoke, and Hector hurried from the house
391	back the same way through the well-built streets.
392	When he had crossed the great city and came to the gates,
393	the Scaean gates, by which he meant to go out to the plain,
394	there his richly dowered wife came running to meet him,
395	Andromache, the daughter of great-hearted Eetion,
396	Eetion who lived beneath wooded Placus,
397	in Thebe under Placus, lord over the Cilician men;
398	it was his daughter who was the wife of Hector armed in bronze.
399	She met him then, and with her came a handmaid
400	holding the child at her breast, an infant of tender mind, quite small,
401	the beloved son of Hector, like a lovely star,
402	whom Hector called Scamandrius, but the others
403	Astyanax; for Hector alone was guarding Ilios.
404	And he smiled as he looked at his son in silence;
405	but Andromache stood close beside him, shedding tears,
406	and she clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
407	“Strange one, your own might will destroy you, and you have no pity
408	for your infant son or for me, ill-fated, who soon will be
409	your widow; for soon the Achaeans will kill you,
410	all rushing upon you together; and for me it would be better,
411	if I lose you, to go down beneath the earth; for there will be no other
412	comfort for me, when you have met your fate,
413	only grief; and I have no father and no lady mother.
414	For brilliant Achilles killed my father,
415	and utterly sacked the well-peopled city of the Cilicians,
416	high-gated Thebe; he killed Eetion,
417	but he did not strip him, for his heart held back in awe from that,
418	but he burned him with his intricate armor
419	and heaped a mound over him; and elms were planted around it
420	by the mountain nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus.
421	And the seven brothers I had in our halls
422	all went down on a single day into the house of Hades;
423	for brilliant swift-footed Achilles killed them all
424	among their shambling cattle and white sheep.
425	And my mother, who was queen beneath wooded Placus,
426	he brought here with the rest of the possessions,
427	and then he set her free, taking a boundless ransom,
428	and in her father's halls Artemis of the showering arrows struck her down.
429	Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother,
430	and brother, and you are my husband in his prime;
431	but come now, have pity, and stay here on the tower,
432	do not make your child an orphan and your wife a widow;
433	and station the army by the wild fig tree, where most of all
434	the city can be scaled and the wall is open to assault.
435	For three times the best of them have come there and made trial of it,
436	with the two Ajaxes and far-famed Idomeneus
437	and with the sons of Atreus and the valiant son of Tydeus;
438	perhaps someone well skilled in oracles told them of it,
439	or perhaps their own heart urges them on and bids them.”
440	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to her in turn:
441	“All these things are on my mind too, lady; but most terribly
442	I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes,
443	if like a coward I shrink away from the war;
444	nor does my heart bid me, since I have learned to be brave
445	always, and to fight among the foremost of the Trojans,
446	winning great fame for my father and for myself.
447	For I know this well in my mind and in my heart:
448	a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish,
449	and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.
450	But it is not so much the sorrow of the Trojans hereafter that troubles me,
451	nor of Hecuba herself, nor of lord Priam,
452	nor of my brothers, who, many and brave,
453	may fall in the dust at the hands of hostile men,
454	as yours, when one of the bronze-armored Achaeans
455	leads you away in tears, taking away your day of freedom;
456	and in Argos you may weave at the loom at another woman's bidding,
457	and carry water from Messeis or Hypereia,
458	much against your will, and harsh necessity will lie upon you;
459	and someday a man will say as he sees you shedding tears:
460	‘This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting
461	of the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought around Ilios.’
462	So someone will say one day; and it will be fresh sorrow for you
463	to lack such a man to ward off the day of slavery.
464	But may I be dead, and the heaped earth cover me,
465	before I hear your cries as they drag you away.”
466	So saying, shining Hector reached out for his son;
467	but the child shrank back crying into the bosom of his fair-belted nurse,
468	frightened at the sight of his dear father,
469	afraid of the bronze and the plume of horsehair,
470	as he saw it nodding terribly from the top of the helmet.
471	And his dear father and his lady mother laughed aloud;
472	and at once shining Hector took the helmet from his head
473	and laid it, all gleaming, on the ground;
474	and when he had kissed his dear son and dandled him in his arms,
475	he spoke in prayer to Zeus and the other gods:
476	“Zeus and you other gods, grant that this child of mine
477	may become, as I am, preeminent among the Trojans,
478	as good in strength, and rule Ilios with might;
479	and may someone say one day, ‘He is far better than his father,’
480	as he comes back from war; and may he bring back bloody spoils
481	when he has killed his enemy, and may his mother's heart rejoice.”
482	So saying, he placed in the arms of his dear wife
483	his son; and she took him to her fragrant bosom,
484	laughing through her tears; and her husband saw it and pitied her,
485	and stroked her with his hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
486	“Strange one, do not grieve too much in your heart for me;
487	for no man will hurl me down to Hades beyond my portion;
488	and no man, I say, has ever escaped his fate,
489	no coward and no brave man, once he has been born.
490	But go into the house and attend to your own work,
491	the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids
492	to go about their work; and war will be the concern of men,
493	of all the men who were born in Ilios, and mine most of all.”
494	So he spoke, and shining Hector took up his helmet
495	with its horsehair crest; and his dear wife went home,
496	turning back again and again, shedding big tears.
497	And quickly she came to the well-appointed house
498	of man-slaying Hector, and found within many
499	handmaids, and she roused lamentation among them all.
500	So they lamented Hector in his own house while he was still alive;
501	for they thought he would never come back again from the war
502	escaping the might and the hands of the Achaeans.
503	Nor did Paris linger long in his lofty house,
504	but when he had put on his glorious armor, inlaid with bronze,
505	he hurried through the city, trusting in his swift feet.
506	As when a stalled horse, fed full of barley at the manger,
507	breaks his halter and gallops across the plain, pounding the ground,
508	used to bathing in the fair-flowing river,
509	exulting; he holds his head high, and his mane
510	streams about his shoulders; and trusting in his splendor
511	his knees carry him swiftly to the haunts and pasture of the horses:
512	so Paris, son of Priam, came down from the height of Pergamus,
513	gleaming in his armor like the shining sun,
514	laughing aloud, and his swift feet carried him; and quickly then
515	he came upon brilliant Hector, his brother, just as he was about
516	to turn from the place where he had talked intimately with his wife.
517	Godlike Alexander spoke to him first:
518	“Dear brother, I am surely holding you back in your haste
519	by lingering, and did not come in due time, as you told me?”
520	Answering him, Hector of the gleaming helmet said:
521	“Strange one, no man who was fair-minded
522	would dishonor your work in battle, since you are valiant;
523	but you hold back of your own will and do not want to fight; and my heart
524	grieves in my spirit when I hear shameful things said of you
525	by the Trojans, who have much toil for your sake.
526	But let us go; we will make these things right later, if ever Zeus
527	grants us, for the everlasting gods of heaven,
528	to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls,
529	when we have driven the well-greaved Achaeans out of Troy.”
