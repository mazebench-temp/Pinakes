# Homer, Iliad, Book 5 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Then in turn to Diomedes, son of Tydeus, Pallas Athena
2	gave might and daring, so that he would stand out among all
3	the Argives and win noble fame.
4	She kindled a tireless fire from his helmet and his shield,
5	like the star of late summer, which above all others
6	shines out bright when it has bathed in Oceanus;
7	such was the fire she kindled from his head and shoulders,
8	and she roused him into the middle, where the most men were thrown into confusion.
9	There was among the Trojans one Dares, wealthy and noble,
10	a priest of Hephaestus; and he had two sons,
11	Phegeus and Idaeus, well skilled in every kind of fighting.
12	These two separated from the rest and charged straight at him,
13	they from their chariot, while he rushed at them on foot from the ground.
14	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
15	Phegeus was the first to let fly his long-shadowed spear;
16	and over the left shoulder of the son of Tydeus went the point
17	of the spear, and did not hit him; and after him he rushed on with the bronze,
18	the son of Tydeus; and the shaft did not fly from his hand in vain,
19	but struck him on the chest between the nipples, and thrust him from the chariot.
20	And Idaeus sprang down, leaving the beautiful chariot,
21	and did not dare to stand over his slain brother;
22	for he himself would not have escaped black doom either,
23	but Hephaestus protected him, and saved him, covering him in night,
24	so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief.
25	And the son of great-hearted Tydeus drove off the horses
26	and gave them to his companions to take down to the hollow ships.
27	And when the great-hearted Trojans saw the two sons of Dares,
28	the one escaping, the other killed beside the chariot,
29	the hearts of all were shaken; but gray-eyed Athena
30	took furious Ares by the hand and addressed him:
31	“Ares, Ares, ruin of mortals, blood-stained stormer of walls,
32	should we not leave the Trojans and the Achaeans
33	to fight, and see to which side father Zeus will hold out glory,
34	while we two withdraw, and avoid the wrath of Zeus?”
35	So saying, she led furious Ares out of the battle,
36	and then she sat him down by the high banks of the Scamander;
37	and the Danaans turned the Trojans back; and each of the leaders
38	took his man. First Agamemnon, lord of men,
39	hurled great Odius, leader of the Halizones, from his chariot;
40	for as the man was the first to turn, he planted his spear in his back
41	between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest;
42	and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
43	And Idomeneus slew Phaestus, son of the Maeonian
44	Borus, who had come from deep-soiled Tarne.
45	Idomeneus, famed for his spear, stabbed him with his long spear
46	on the right shoulder as he was mounting his chariot;
47	and he fell from the chariot, and hateful darkness seized him.
48	The attendants of Idomeneus stripped him of his armor.
49	And the son of Strophius, Scamandrius, skilled in the hunt,
50	Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed with his sharp spear,
51	a good hunter; for Artemis herself had taught him
52	to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains.
53	But Artemis of the showering arrows was no help to him then,
54	nor his far-shooting, in which he had excelled before;
55	but the son of Atreus, Menelaus famed for his spear,
56	stabbed him with his spear in the back as he fled before him,
57	between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest;
58	and he fell face down, and his armor clattered upon him.
59	And Meriones slew Phereclus, son of the builder,
60	the son of Harmon, who knew how to make with his hands every kind
61	of intricate work; for Pallas Athena loved him above all others.
62	It was he who built for Alexander the balanced ships
63	that began the evil, which became an evil for all the Trojans
64	and for himself, since he knew nothing of the oracles of the gods.
65	Meriones, when he caught up with him in pursuit,
66	struck him in the right buttock; and straight through,
67	under the bone and into the bladder, went the point;
68	and he fell to his knees with a groan, and death enfolded him.
69	And Meges killed Pedaeus, son of Antenor,
70	who was a bastard, but brilliant Theano had reared him with care
71	like her own dear children, to please her husband.
72	The son of Phyleus, famed for his spear, came close
73	and struck him on the back of the head with his sharp spear;
74	and straight on through the teeth, under the tongue, the bronze cut its way;
75	and he fell in the dust, and bit the cold bronze with his teeth.
76	And Eurypylus, son of Euaemon—brilliant Hypsenor,
77	son of high-hearted Dolopion, who had been made priest of the Scamander
78	and was honored by the people like a god—
79	him Eurypylus, the splendid son of Euaemon,
80	ran after him as he fled before him and struck him on the shoulder,
81	darting at him with his sword, and sheared off his heavy arm;
82	and the bloody arm fell to the plain; and his eyes
83	were seized by purple death and mighty fate.
84	So they toiled on through the fierce battle;
85	but as for the son of Tydeus, you could not have told which side he was on,
86	whether he was mingling with the Trojans or with the Achaeans.
87	For he rushed across the plain like a river in flood,
88	a winter torrent, which, running fast, scatters the dikes;
89	and the close-built dikes do not hold it back,
90	nor do the walls of the flourishing orchards stop it
91	when it comes on suddenly, when the rain of Zeus falls heavy;
92	and many fair works of young men fall in ruin before it:
93	so before the son of Tydeus the close-packed battalions
94	of the Trojans were thrown into confusion, and did not stand against him, many though they were.
95	And when the splendid son of Lycaon caught sight of him
96	rushing across the plain, driving the battalions in confusion before him,
97	at once he drew his curved bow at the son of Tydeus,
98	and hit him as he rushed on, striking him on the right shoulder,
99	on the plate of his corselet; and the bitter arrow flew through it,
100	and held straight on, and the corselet was spattered with blood.
101	And over him the splendid son of Lycaon shouted aloud:
102	“Rise up, great-hearted Trojans, goaders of horses;
103	the best of the Achaeans is hit, and I do not think
104	he will hold out long against the mighty shaft, if truly
105	the lord, the son of Zeus, roused me when I set out from Lycia.”
106	So he spoke, boasting; but the swift shaft had not overcome him;
107	he drew back, and stood in front of his horses and chariot,
108	and spoke to Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus:
109	“Up, dear son of Capaneus, get down from the chariot,
110	so that you may draw the bitter arrow out of my shoulder.”
111	So he spoke, and Sthenelus leapt from the chariot to the ground,
112	and standing beside him drew the swift shaft clean through and out of his shoulder;
113	and the blood spurted up through the plaited tunic.
114	Then Diomedes, good at the war cry, prayed:
115	“Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone:
116	if ever in friendship you stood by my father and me
117	in destructive war, now be my friend in turn, Athena;
118	grant that I may kill this man, and that he come within the cast of my spear,
119	who struck me first, and boasts over me, and says that I
120	shall not look much longer on the bright light of the sun.”
121	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him,
122	and she made his limbs light, his feet and his hands above.
123	And standing near him she addressed him with winged words:
124	“Take heart now, Diomedes, and fight against the Trojans;
125	for in your breast I have put your father's might,
126	undaunted, such as the horseman Tydeus, wielder of the shield, used to have;
127	and I have taken from your eyes the mist that was on them before,
128	so that you may clearly tell god from man.
129	So now, if any god comes here to test you,
130	do not fight face to face with the immortal gods,
131	with any of the others; but if Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus,
132	comes into the war, her you may wound with the sharp bronze.”
133	So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away,
134	and the son of Tydeus went back and mingled with the champions;
135	even before, his heart had been eager to fight the Trojans,
136	but now three times as great a fury seized him, like a lion
137	that a shepherd in the fields, watching over his woolly sheep,
138	has grazed as it leapt over the wall of the fold, but has not killed;
139	he has roused its strength, and then he does not defend them,
140	but slips into the farm buildings, and the sheep, left undefended, flee in panic;
141	they lie heaped close one upon another,
142	but the lion in his fury leaps back out of the high-walled fold:
143	so furiously did mighty Diomedes close with the Trojans.
144	Then he killed Astynous and Hypeiron, shepherd of the people,
145	the one struck above the nipple with his bronze-fitted spear,
146	the other with his great sword on the collarbone beside the shoulder
147	he struck, and cut the shoulder away from the neck and from the back.
148	These he left, and went after Abas and Polyidus,
149	sons of Eurydamas, the old interpreter of dreams;
150	but the old man read no dreams for them when they set out,
151	and mighty Diomedes slew them and stripped them.
152	He went after Xanthus and Thoon, the two sons of Phaenops,
153	both his darlings; and he was worn down by grievous old age,
154	and had begotten no other son to leave as heir to his possessions.
155	There he slew them, and took away the dear life
156	of both; and to their father he left lamentation and grievous sorrows,
157	since he did not receive them back alive, returned from battle;
158	and distant kinsmen divided his wealth among them.
159	Then he caught two sons of Priam, descendant of Dardanus,
160	who were in one chariot, Echemmon and Chromius.
161	And as a lion leaps among cattle and breaks the neck
162	of a heifer or a cow as they graze in a thicket,
163	so the son of Tydeus brought them both down from their chariot,
164	roughly, against their will, and then stripped off their armor;
165	and the horses he gave to his companions to drive to the ships.
166	Aeneas saw him wreaking havoc on the ranks of men,
167	and went his way through the battle and through the turmoil of spears,
168	seeking godlike Pandarus, if he might find him anywhere.
169	He found the son of Lycaon, noble and strong,
170	and stood before him and spoke to him face to face:
171	“Pandarus, where are your bow and your feathered arrows
172	and your fame? In this no man here can rival you,
173	and no one in Lycia claims to be better than you.
174	But come, lift up your hands to Zeus and let fly a shaft at this man,
175	whoever he is who is prevailing here, and has done much harm
176	to the Trojans, since he has loosened the knees of many brave men;
177	unless he is some god who bears resentment against the Trojans,
178	in wrath over sacrifices; and the wrath of a god is hard to bear.”
179	Then the splendid son of Lycaon spoke to him in turn:
180	“Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-armored Trojans,
181	I liken him in everything to the wise-hearted son of Tydeus,
182	knowing him by his shield and his helmet with its socket for the plume,
183	and by the look of his horses; but I do not know for sure whether he is a god.
184	But if he is the man I say, the wise-hearted son of Tydeus,
185	he does not rage like this without a god, but someone
186	of the immortals stands near him, his shoulders wrapped in cloud,
187	who turned my swift shaft aside as it reached him.
188	For I have already shot a shaft at him, and hit him on the shoulder,
189	the right one, straight through the plate of his corselet;
190	and I thought I would hurl him down to Aidoneus,
191	and yet I did not overcome him; surely some god is resentful.
192	And I have no horses here, and no chariot to mount;
193	but in the halls of Lycaon there are eleven chariots,
194	beautiful, newly built, just finished; and over them cloths
195	are spread; and beside each one a yoke of horses
196	stands, munching white barley and spelt.
197	Truly the old spearman Lycaon gave me many instructions
198	as I was setting out, in his well-built house;
199	he told me to mount my horses and chariot
200	and lead the Trojans through the fierce battles;
201	but I did not listen—it would have been far better—
202	sparing the horses, for fear they would go short of fodder
203	with men penned in, when they were used to eating their fill.
204	So I left them, and have come to Ilios on foot,
205	trusting in my bow; but it was not going to help me after all.
206	For I have already shot at two of the chieftains,
207	the son of Tydeus and the son of Atreus, and from both of them
208	I drew real blood with my shots, but only roused them more.
209	So it was in an evil hour that I took my curved bow from its peg
210	on that day when to lovely Ilios
211	I led the Trojans, to do a favor to brilliant Hector.
212	But if I come home and see with my eyes
213	my native land and my wife and my great high-roofed house,
214	then may a stranger cut my head from my shoulders on the spot
215	if I do not put this bow in the blazing fire,
216	broken in pieces with my hands; for it goes with me as empty wind.”
217	Then Aeneas, leader of the Trojans, answered him face to face:
218	“Do not talk this way; nothing will change
219	until we two go against this man with horses and chariot,
220	face to face, and make trial of him in arms.
221	But come, mount my chariot, so that you may see
222	what the horses of Tros are like, how they know the plain,
223	how to pursue and to flee very swiftly this way and that;
224	they will bring the two of us safely to the city, even if once again
225	Zeus holds out glory to Diomedes, son of Tydeus.
226	But come now, take the whip and the glossy reins,
227	and I will get down from the chariot to fight;
228	or you face this man, and the horses will be my care.”
229	Then the splendid son of Lycaon spoke to him in turn:
230	“Aeneas, you yourself keep the reins and your own horses;
231	they will draw the curved chariot better under the charioteer they know,
232	if we should have to flee from the son of Tydeus;
233	I fear the two of them may take fright and balk, and refuse
234	to carry us out of the fighting, missing your voice,
235	and the son of great-hearted Tydeus may rush at us
236	and kill us both and drive off the single-hoofed horses.
237	So you yourself drive your chariot and your horses,
238	and I will meet him with my sharp spear as he comes on.”
239	So they spoke, and mounted the inlaid chariot
240	and eagerly drove the swift horses at the son of Tydeus.
241	Sthenelus, the splendid son of Capaneus, saw them,
242	and at once addressed the son of Tydeus with winged words:
243	“Diomedes, son of Tydeus, dear to my heart,
244	I see two strong men eager to fight against you,
245	men of measureless strength; one is well skilled with the bow,
246	Pandarus, and he claims to be the son of Lycaon;
247	and Aeneas claims that he was born the son of noble Anchises,
248	and his mother is Aphrodite.
249	But come, let us draw back in the chariot, and do not, I beg you,
250	rush like this among the champions, or you may lose your dear life.”
251	Looking at him darkly, mighty Diomedes said:
252	“Do not talk to me of flight, for I do not think you will persuade me.
253	It is not in my blood to fight shrinking back
254	or to cower; my might is still steadfast.
255	I am loath to mount the chariot; just as I am
256	I will go to meet them; Pallas Athena does not let me flee.
257	Their swift horses will not carry these two back again,
258	both of them, away from us, even if one of them escapes.
259	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
260	if Athena of many counsels holds out to me the glory
261	of killing them both, then you hold these swift horses of ours
262	here, and tie the reins fast to the chariot rail,
263	and remember to rush at the horses of Aeneas
264	and drive them away from the Trojans to the well-greaved Achaeans.
265	For they are of that breed which far-seeing Zeus gave to Tros
266	in recompense for his son Ganymede, because they are the best
267	of all horses under the dawn and the sun;
268	and from that breed Anchises, lord of men, stole a strain
269	without Laomedon's knowledge, putting his mares to them;
270	and from them six foals were born to him in his halls.
271	Four of them he kept himself and reared at the manger,
272	and these two he gave to Aeneas, masters of rout.
273	If we could take these two, we would win noble fame.”
274	So they spoke such words to one another,
275	and quickly the other two came near, driving their swift horses.
276	The splendid son of Lycaon spoke to him first:
277	“Stout-hearted and wise-hearted son of noble Tydeus,
278	so my swift shaft did not overcome you after all, the bitter arrow;
279	now I will try with the spear, to see if I can hit you.”
280	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
281	and struck the son of Tydeus on his shield; and straight through it
282	the bronze point flew on and reached his corselet.
283	And over him the splendid son of Lycaon shouted aloud:
284	“You are hit clean through the flank, and I do not think
285	you will hold out much longer; and you have given me a great triumph.”
286	Unafraid, mighty Diomedes answered him:
287	“You missed; you did not hit me. But I do not think you two
288	will stop before one or the other of you has fallen
289	and glutted Ares with blood, the warrior with the oxhide shield.”
290	So saying, he let fly; and Athena guided the shaft
291	to the nose beside the eye, and it passed through the white teeth.
292	The unyielding bronze cut off his tongue at the root,
293	and the point came out at the base of the chin;
294	and he fell from the chariot, and his armor clattered upon him,
295	flashing and gleaming all over; and his horses shied aside,
296	the swift-footed horses; and there his soul and his strength were loosed.
297	And Aeneas sprang down with his shield and his long spear,
298	fearing that the Achaeans might somehow drag the body away from him.
299	He bestrode it like a lion trusting in his courage,
300	and held in front of it his spear and his shield, balanced on every side,
301	eager to kill whoever came against him,
302	shouting terribly. But he took up a stone in his hand,
303	the son of Tydeus, a huge thing, which two men could not carry,
304	such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone.
305	With it he struck Aeneas on the hip, where the thigh
306	turns in the hip joint, and men call it the cup;
307	and he crushed the cup, and broke both tendons besides;
308	and the rough stone tore away the skin. And the hero
309	sank to his knees and stayed there, and leaned with his strong hand
310	on the earth; and black night covered his eyes.
311	And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have perished there,
312	had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it,
313	his mother, who bore him to Anchises as he tended his cattle;
314	and she flung her white arms around her dear son,
315	and in front of him she spread a fold of her shining robe
316	as a shelter from missiles, for fear one of the Danaans of the swift horses
317	might strike the bronze into his chest and take away his life.
318	So she was carrying her dear son out of the war;
319	nor did the son of Capaneus forget the instructions
320	that Diomedes, good at the war cry, had given him,
321	but he held his own single-hoofed horses
322	away from the din, and tied the reins fast to the chariot rail,
323	and rushing at the fine-maned horses of Aeneas
324	drove them away from the Trojans to the well-greaved Achaeans,
325	and gave them to Deipylus, his dear companion, whom above all
326	the men of his age he honored, because his mind was in tune with his own,
327	to drive to the hollow ships. Then the hero
328	mounted his own chariot and took the glossy reins,
329	and at once drove the strong-hoofed horses after the son of Tydeus,
330	eagerly. But he was going after Cypris with the pitiless bronze,
331	knowing that she was a feeble god, and not one of those goddesses
332	who rule over the war of men,
333	neither Athena nor Enyo, sacker of cities.
334	But when he had caught up with her, pursuing her through the great throng,
335	then the son of great-hearted Tydeus, reaching out,
336	leapt at her and wounded her hand with his sharp spear,
337	her delicate hand; and at once the spear pierced the skin
338	through the ambrosial robe that the Graces themselves had made for her,
339	above the base of the palm; and the immortal blood of the goddess flowed,
340	ichor, such as flows in the blessed gods;
341	for they do not eat bread, they do not drink sparkling wine,
342	and so they are bloodless, and are called immortals.
343	And she, with a great cry, let her son fall from her;
344	and Phoebus Apollo took him up in his arms and protected him
345	in a dark cloud, for fear one of the Danaans of the swift horses
346	might strike the bronze into his chest and take away his life.
347	And over her Diomedes, good at the war cry, shouted aloud:
348	“Keep away, daughter of Zeus, from war and combat;
349	is it not enough that you deceive feeble women?
350	If you keep coming into war, then I think
351	you will shudder at war even if you hear of it far away.”
352	So he spoke, and she went away distraught, in terrible pain;
353	and wind-footed Iris took her and led her out of the throng,
354	weighed down with pain, and her beautiful skin was darkening.
355	Then she found furious Ares, on the left of the battle,
356	sitting; and his spear and his swift horses were resting in a mist.
357	And she fell on her knees and begged her dear brother,
358	with many entreaties, for the horses with golden headbands:
359	“Dear brother, take care of me, and give me your horses,
360	so that I may reach Olympus, where the seat of the immortals is.
361	I am in great pain from a wound a mortal man gave me,
362	the son of Tydeus, who now would fight even with father Zeus.”
363	So she spoke, and Ares gave her the horses with golden headbands;
364	and she mounted the chariot, grieved at heart,
365	and Iris mounted beside her and took the reins in her hands,
366	and whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling.
367	And quickly they came to the seat of the gods, steep Olympus;
368	there swift Iris, wind-footed, halted the horses,
369	unyoked them from the chariot, and set ambrosial fodder before them;
370	and brilliant Aphrodite fell on the knees of Dione,
371	her mother; and she took her daughter in her arms,
372	and stroked her with her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
373	“Who of the heavenly gods has done such things to you, dear child,
374	for no reason, as if you were caught doing some wrong openly?”
375	Then laughter-loving Aphrodite answered her:
376	“The son of Tydeus wounded me, high-hearted Diomedes,
377	because I was carrying my dear son out of the war,
378	Aeneas, who is dearest to me by far of all.
379	For it is no longer the dread din of battle between Trojans and Achaeans;
380	now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals.”
381	Then Dione, brilliant among goddesses, answered her:
382	“Be patient, my child, and endure it, grieved though you are;
383	for many of us who have our homes on Olympus have endured
384	cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another.
385	Ares endured it, when Otus and mighty Ephialtes,
386	the sons of Aloeus, bound him in strong bonds;
387	and he lay bound in a bronze jar for thirteen months;
388	and now Ares, insatiable of war, would have perished there,
389	had not their stepmother, the beautiful Eeriboea,
390	brought word to Hermes; and he stole Ares away,
391	already worn out, for the cruel bonds were overpowering him.
392	Hera endured it, when the mighty son of Amphitryon
393	struck her in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow;
394	and then incurable pain seized even her.
395	And among them huge Hades endured a swift arrow,
396	when the same man, the son of aegis-bearing Zeus,
397	shot him in Pylos among the dead, and gave him over to pains;
398	and he went to the house of Zeus and high Olympus,
399	grieving at heart, pierced through with pains; for the arrow
400	had been driven into his massive shoulder, and it troubled his spirit.
401	And Paeëon spread pain-killing drugs on it
402	and healed him; for he was not made to be mortal.
403	Hard man, doer of violent deeds, who cared nothing for the evil he did,
404	who with his bow brought sorrow on the gods who hold Olympus!
405	And against you the gray-eyed goddess Athena set this man on;
406	fool, the son of Tydeus does not know this in his heart,
407	that the man who fights with the immortals does not live long,
408	and his children do not call him papa at his knees
409	when he comes home from war and grim combat.
410	So now let the son of Tydeus, strong as he is,
411	take care that no one better than you fights him,
412	or Aegialeia, the wise daughter of Adrastus,
413	will long be waking her household from sleep with her wailing,
414	longing for her wedded husband, the best of the Achaeans,
415	the strong wife of Diomedes, tamer of horses.”
416	She spoke, and with both hands wiped the ichor from her daughter's hand;
417	the hand was healed, and the heavy pains were eased.
418	But Athena and Hera, looking on,
419	provoked Zeus, son of Cronus, with cutting words.
420	And among them the gray-eyed goddess Athena began to speak:
421	“Father Zeus, will you be angry with me for what I say?
422	Surely Cypris has been urging one of the Achaean women
423	to follow along with the Trojans, whom she now loves beyond measure,
424	and caressing one of these well-robed Achaean women
425	she has scratched her slender hand on a golden brooch.”
426	So she spoke, and the father of men and gods smiled,
427	and he called golden Aphrodite and said to her:
428	“Not to you, my child, are given the works of war;
429	you attend to the lovely works of marriage,
430	and all these things will be the business of swift Ares and Athena.”
431	So they spoke such words to one another,
432	and Diomedes, good at the war cry, sprang at Aeneas,
433	though he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him;
434	but he had no awe even of the great god, and was ever eager
435	to kill Aeneas and strip off his glorious armor.
436	Three times then he sprang at him, eager to kill him,
437	and three times Apollo battered back his shining shield;
438	but when for the fourth time he rushed on like a god,
439	then far-working Apollo shouted at him terribly and said:
440	“Take thought, son of Tydeus, and give way, and do not wish

445	And Apollo set Aeneas apart from the throng
446	in sacred Pergamus, where his temple had been built.

508	ranging everywhere; and he was fulfilling the commands

517	for the other toil did not allow it, the toil that the god of the silver bow had awakened,
518	and Ares, ruin of mortals, and Strife, raging without end.

528	And the son of Atreus ranged through the throng, giving many orders:
529	“Friends, be men, and take a valiant heart,
530	and feel shame before one another in the fierce battles;
531	when men feel shame, more are saved than are killed;
532	but when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage.”
533	He spoke, and quickly threw his spear, and struck a man in the front rank,
534	Deicoon, companion of great-hearted Aeneas,
535	son of Pergasus, whom the Trojans, as they did the sons of Priam,

537	Powerful Agamemnon struck him on the shield with his spear;

542	the sons of Diocles, Crethon and Orsilochus,
543	whose father lived in well-built Pherae,
544	wealthy in substance, and his lineage was from the river

549	Crethon and Orsilochus, well skilled in every kind of fighting.

551	followed with the Argives to Ilios of the fine horses,

554	Like two lions on the mountain peaks

608	Then Hector killed two men who knew the joy of battle,
609	who were in one chariot, Menesthes and Anchialus.
610	And great Telamonian Ajax pitied them as they fell;
611	and came up close and stood and threw his shining spear,

613	lived, rich in possessions, rich in grainland; but his fate

616	and the long-shadowed spear stuck in his lower belly,

627	So they toiled on through the fierce battle;
628	and Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, tall and brave,

635	They lie when they say you are the offspring of aegis-bearing Zeus,
636	since you fall far short of those men
637	who were born of Zeus among men of former times;
638	but what a man, they say, was mighty Heracles,
639	my father, bold in endurance, lion-hearted;
640	who once came here for the sake of the horses of Laomedon
641	with only six ships and fewer men,
642	and sacked the city of Ilios, and widowed its streets;
643	but yours is a coward's heart, and your people are dwindling away.

645	by coming from Lycia, not even if you are very strong,
646	but brought down by me you will pass the gates of Hades.”
647	Then Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, answered him face to face:
648	“Tlepolemus, that man did indeed destroy sacred Ilios
649	through the folly of a man, noble Laomedon,
650	who rebuked him with evil words though he had done him good,
651	and did not give him the horses for whose sake he had come from far away.
652	But for you, I say, slaughter and black doom
653	will be made here at my hands, and brought down by my spear

656	Tlepolemus; and the long spears of both at the same moment
657	sped from their hands; one struck him full on the neck,
658	Sarpedon, and the painful point went clean through;
659	and murky night covered his eyes.
660	And Tlepolemus with his long spear had struck him on the left thigh,
661	and the point sped through in its fury,
662	grazing the bone; but his father still kept ruin away.
663	His brilliant companions carried godlike Sarpedon
664	out of the war; and the long spear weighed him down
665	as it dragged; no one noticed it or thought
666	to draw the ash spear from his thigh, so that he could walk,
667	in their haste; such was the toil they had in tending him.
668	And on the other side the well-greaved Achaeans carried Tlepolemus
669	out of the war; and brilliant Odysseus saw it,
670	he of the enduring spirit, and his heart raged within him;

721	Hera, the august goddess, daughter of great Cronus;

888	Looking at him darkly, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
889	“Do not sit beside me and whine, you turncoat.

891	strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles.
892	You have your mother's fury, uncontrollable and unyielding,
893	Hera's; I can hardly keep her down with my words;
894	so I think it is by her promptings that you suffer this.
895	But I will not endure it any longer that you should be in pain;
896	for you are of my stock, and it was to me your mother bore you;
897	but if you had been born of any other god, destructive as you are,
898	you would long ago have been lower down than the sons of Uranus.”
899	So he spoke, and told Paeëon to heal him.
900	And Paeëon spread pain-killing drugs on it
901	and healed him; for he was not made to be mortal.
902	And as fig juice, stirred in, quickly curdles white milk,
903	liquid as it is, and very quickly it thickens as a man stirs it,
904	so quickly he healed furious Ares.
905	And Hebe bathed him, and dressed him in lovely clothes;
906	and he sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronus, exulting in his glory.
907	And back to the house of great Zeus went
908	Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae,
909	having stopped Ares, ruin of mortals, from his slaughter of men.
