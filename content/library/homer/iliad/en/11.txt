# Homer, Iliad, Book 11 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus,
2	to bring light to the immortals and to mortals;
3	and Zeus sent Strife forth to the swift ships of the Achaeans,
4	grievous, holding in her hands a portent of war.
5	And she stood by Odysseus' black ship, huge in the hull,
6	which lay in the middle, so that a shout could carry to both ends,
7	both to the huts of Ajax, son of Telamon,
8	and to those of Achilles, who had hauled up their balanced ships
9	at the farthest ends, trusting in their manhood and the strength of their hands.
10	There the goddess stood and shouted, loud and terrible,
11	shrill, and she put great strength into each of the Achaeans,
12	in his heart, to go to war without ceasing and to fight.
13	And at once war became sweeter to them than to go home
14	in the hollow ships to their dear native land.
15	And the son of Atreus shouted and bade them gird themselves,
16	the Argives; and he himself put on the gleaming bronze.
17	First he put the greaves around his shins,
18	fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
19	second, he put on the corselet around his chest,
20	which Cinyras had once given him to be a guest-gift.
21	For he had heard in Cyprus the great report that the Achaeans
22	were about to sail up to Troy in their ships;
23	and so he gave it to him, to do the king a favor.
24	It had ten bands of dark blue enamel,
25	and twelve of gold, and twenty of tin;
26	and dark blue serpents stretched up toward the neck,
27	three on each side, like the rainbows that the son of Cronus
28	sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men.
29	And around his shoulders he slung his sword; and on it studs
30	of gold shone bright, and the sheath around it was
31	of silver, fitted with golden straps.
32	And he took up the furious shield, man-covering, intricately worked,
33	a fine one, with ten circles of bronze around it,
34	and on it were twenty bosses of tin,
35	white, and in their midst was one of dark blue enamel.
36	And on it the grim-faced Gorgon was set as a crown,
37	glaring terribly, and around her were Terror and Rout.
38	From it hung a strap of silver; and on the strap
39	a dark blue serpent writhed, and it had heads,
40	three of them, turned every way, growing from a single neck.
41	And on his head he set a helmet with two ridges and four plates,
42	with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it;
43	and he took two strong spears tipped with bronze,
44	sharp; and far off the bronze shone from them up into the sky;
45	and Athena and Hera thundered over him,
46	honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold.
47	Then each man gave orders to his own charioteer
48	to hold the horses in good order there at the ditch,
49	and they themselves, on foot, armed in their armor,
50	rushed on; and an unquenchable cry went up in the early dawn.
51	They were drawn up at the ditch far ahead of the horsemen,
52	and the horsemen followed a little behind; and among them an evil
53	tumult the son of Cronus roused, and from on high he sent down dewdrops
54	wet with blood from the upper air, because he meant
55	to hurl down to Hades many mighty heads.
56	And the Trojans on the other side, on the rise of the plain, were drawn up
57	around great Hector and noble Polydamas,
58	and Aeneas, who was honored by the Trojan people like a god,
59	and the three sons of Antenor, Polybus and brilliant Agenor,
60	and young Acamas, like the immortals.
61	And Hector among the foremost carried his shield, balanced on every side;
62	and as a baleful star shows itself from out of the clouds,
63	shining bright, and then again sinks into the shadowy clouds,
64	so Hector now would appear among the foremost,
65	and now among the hindmost, giving orders; and all of him in bronze
66	shone like the lightning of aegis-bearing father Zeus.
67	And as reapers facing one another
68	drive their swaths through a rich man's field
69	of wheat or barley, and the handfuls fall thick,
70	so the Trojans and Achaeans, leaping at one another,
71	cut each other down, and neither side thought of deadly rout.
72	The battle held their heads level, and they like wolves
73	rushed on; and Strife of the many groans rejoiced to look on;
74	for she alone of the gods was there among the fighters,
75	and the other gods were not with them, but sat at ease
76	in their own halls, where for each of them
77	a fine house had been built in the folds of Olympus.
78	And they all blamed the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds,
79	because he meant to hold out glory to the Trojans.
80	But the father paid them no heed; withdrawing apart,
81	away from the others he sat, exulting in his glory,
82	looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans,
83	and on the flash of bronze, and the slayers and the slain.
84	As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing,
85	so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell;
86	but at the hour when a woodcutter makes ready his meal
87	in the glens of a mountain, when his arms have had their fill
88	of cutting tall trees, and weariness has come into his heart,
89	and longing for sweet food takes hold of his mind,
90	then the Danaans by their prowess broke the battalions,
91	calling to their companions along the ranks; and Agamemnon
92	sprang in first, and killed a man, Bianor, shepherd of the people,
93	him first, and then his companion, Oileus, driver of horses.
94	He had leapt down from the chariot and stood to face him;
95	and as the man came straight on, eager, he stabbed him in the forehead with his sharp spear,
96	and the helmet, heavy with bronze, did not hold the spear,
97	but it went through it and through the bone, and the brain
98	inside was all spattered; and he beat him down for all his eagerness.
99	And Agamemnon, lord of men, left them there
100	with their chests shining bare, for he had stripped off their tunics;
101	and he went on to slay and strip Isus and Antiphus,
102	two sons of Priam, one a bastard and one born in wedlock, both
103	in one chariot; the bastard was driving,
104	and renowned Antiphus stood beside him. These two Achilles once
105	had bound with pliant willow shoots on the slopes of Ida,
106	catching them as they herded sheep, and had released them for a ransom.
107	This time the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
108	struck the one in the chest above the nipple with his spear,
109	and Antiphus he struck beside the ear with his sword, and threw him from the chariot.
110	And in haste he stripped the two of their fine armor,
111	recognizing them; for he had seen them before by the swift ships,
112	when swift-footed Achilles brought them from Ida.
113	And as a lion seizes the helpless young of a swift hind
114	in his strong teeth and easily crushes them,
115	coming into their lair, and takes away their tender life;
116	and the mother, even if she happens to be very near, cannot
117	help them, for a dreadful trembling comes over her herself;
118	and swiftly she darts away through the thick brush and the woods,
119	hurrying and sweating, before the onrush of the mighty beast;
120	so none could ward off destruction from those two,
121	none of the Trojans, but they themselves were fleeing before the Argives.
122	Then he went after Peisander and Hippolochus, steadfast in battle,
123	the sons of wise-hearted Antimachus, who above all others,
124	having taken Alexander's gold, splendid gifts,
125	would not let Helen be given to fair-haired Menelaus,
126	his two sons powerful Agamemnon caught
127	in one chariot, as they were driving the swift horses together;
128	for the glossy reins had slipped from their hands,
129	and the two were thrown into confusion; and he rushed against them like a lion,
130	the son of Atreus; and the two begged him from the chariot:
131	“Take us alive, son of Atreus, and accept a worthy ransom;
132	there are many treasures lying in the house of Antimachus,
133	bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil;
134	from these our father would gladly give you a boundless ransom,
135	if he heard that we two were alive at the ships of the Achaeans.”
136	So the two, weeping, addressed the king
137	with soothing words; but the voice they heard would not be soothed:
138	“If you two are indeed the sons of wise-hearted Antimachus,
139	who once in the Trojans' assembly urged that Menelaus,
140	when he came on an embassy with godlike Odysseus,
141	be killed on the spot, and not be let go back to the Achaeans,
142	now you will pay for your father's shameful insult.”
143	He spoke, and thrust Peisander from the chariot to the ground,
144	striking him in the chest with his spear, and he was flung on his back on the ground.
145	Hippolochus leapt down, and him he slew and stripped on the ground,
146	cutting off his arms with the sword and hewing through his neck,
147	and he sent him rolling like a log through the throng.
148	These he left; and where the most battalions were thrown into confusion,
149	there he leapt in, and with him the other well-greaved Achaeans.
150	Foot soldiers killed foot soldiers fleeing under compulsion,
151	and horsemen horsemen; and under them the dust rose
152	from the plain, raised by the thundering hooves of horses,
153	as they cut each other down with the bronze; and powerful Agamemnon
154	followed, always killing, calling on the Argives.
155	And as when destroying fire falls on thick, uncut woodland,
156	and the whirling wind carries it everywhere, and the thickets
157	fall uprooted, pressed by the onrush of the fire,
158	so under Agamemnon, son of Atreus, fell the heads
159	of the fleeing Trojans, and many horses with high-arched necks
160	rattled empty chariots along the bridges of war,
161	missing their noble charioteers; but they lay on the ground,
162	far dearer to the vultures than to their wives.
163	But Zeus drew Hector out from the missiles and from the dust,
164	from the slaughter of men, from the blood and from the tumult;
165	and the son of Atreus followed, fiercely calling on the Danaans.
166	And they rushed past the mound of ancient Ilus, descendant of Dardanus,
167	through the middle of the plain, past the wild fig tree,
168	making for the city; and he kept following, shouting,
169	the son of Atreus, and his irresistible hands were spattered with gore.
170	But when they came to the Scaean gates and the oak,
171	there they halted and waited for one another.
172	But others were still fleeing in rout through the middle of the plain, like cattle
173	that a lion has put to flight, coming on them in the dead of night,
174	all of them, but to one sheer destruction appears:
175	he seizes her in his strong teeth and breaks her neck
176	first, and then gulps down her blood and all her entrails;
177	so powerful Agamemnon, son of Atreus, pursued them,
178	always killing the hindmost; and they fled.
179	And many fell headlong or on their backs from their chariots
180	at the hands of the son of Atreus; for he raged ahead with his spear.
181	But when he was just about to come beneath the city and its steep wall,
182	then the father of men and gods
183	sat down on the peaks of Ida rich in springs,
184	coming down from heaven, with the lightning in his hands,
185	and he urged on golden-winged Iris to carry a message:
186	“Go, swift Iris, and tell this word to Hector:
187	as long as he sees Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
188	rushing among the champions, cutting down the ranks of men,
189	so long let him draw back, and order the rest of the army
190	to fight the enemy in the fierce battle.
191	But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,
192	he leaps into his chariot, then I will put power in his hands
193	to kill, until he comes to the well-benched ships,
194	and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on.”
195	So he spoke, and swift Iris, wind-footed, did not disobey,
196	and she went down from the mountains of Ida to sacred Ilios.
197	She found the son of wise-hearted Priam, brilliant Hector,
198	standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot;
199	and standing near, swift-footed Iris spoke to him:
200	“Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
201	father Zeus has sent me to tell you this:
202	as long as you see Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
203	rushing among the champions, cutting down the ranks of men,
204	so long withdraw from the fighting, and order the rest of the army
205	to fight the enemy in the fierce battle.
206	But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,
207	he leaps into his chariot, then he will put power in your hands
208	to kill, until you come to the well-benched ships,
209	and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on.”
210	So saying, swift-footed Iris went away,
211	and Hector leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor,
212	and brandishing his sharp spears he went everywhere through the army,
213	urging them to fight, and he awoke the dread din of battle.
214	And they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans;
215	and the Argives on the other side strengthened their battalions.
216	And the battle was set in order, and they stood facing each other; and Agamemnon
217	sprang in first, for he wished to fight far in front of them all.
218	Tell me now, Muses, you who have your homes on Olympus,
219	who was the first to come against Agamemnon,
220	whether of the Trojans themselves or of their famous allies.
221	Iphidamas, son of Antenor, tall and brave,
222	who was reared in deep-soiled Thrace, mother of flocks;
223	Cisseus reared him in his house when he was small,
224	his mother's father, who fathered fair-cheeked Theano;
225	and when he reached the measure of glorious youth,
226	he kept him there, and gave him his daughter;
227	but newly married he came from the bridal chamber at the report of the Achaeans,
228	with twelve curved ships that followed him.
229	These balanced ships he then left at Percote,
230	but he himself had come on foot to Ilios;
231	he it was who now came against Agamemnon, son of Atreus.
232	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
233	the son of Atreus missed, and his spear turned aside,
234	but Iphidamas stabbed him at the waist, below the corselet,
235	and leaned his own weight on it, trusting in his heavy hand;
236	but he did not pierce the gleaming belt; long before,
237	meeting the silver, the point was bent like lead.
238	And wide-ruling Agamemnon caught the spear in his hand
239	and dragged it toward him, eager as a lion, and out of his hand
240	he wrenched it, and struck him on the neck with his sword, and loosened his limbs.
241	So he fell there and slept the sleep of bronze,
242	pitiable, far from his wife, whom he had wooed, fighting for his townsmen,
243	his wedded wife, from whom he had seen no joy, though he had given much:
244	first he gave a hundred cattle, and then he promised a thousand,
245	goats and sheep together, which were pastured for him in untold numbers.
246	Then Agamemnon, son of Atreus, stripped him,
247	and went carrying his fine armor into the throng of the Achaeans.
248	But when Coon, preeminent among men, caught sight of him,
249	the eldest-born son of Antenor, strong grief
250	veiled his eyes for his fallen brother.
251	He stood off to one side with his spear, unseen by brilliant Agamemnon,
252	and stabbed him in the middle of the arm, below the elbow,
253	and the point of the shining spear went straight through.
254	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, shuddered;
255	but not even so did he cease from battle and war,
256	but sprang at Coon, holding his wind-nurtured spear.
257	He was dragging Iphidamas, his brother by the same father,
258	by the foot, eagerly, and calling on all the best;
259	and as he dragged him into the throng, beneath the bossed shield
260	he stabbed him with his bronze-fitted spear, and loosened his limbs;
261	and standing beside him he cut off his head over Iphidamas.
262	There the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the king, the son of Atreus,
263	fulfilled their fate and went down into the house of Hades.
264	But he ranged through the ranks of the other men
265	with spear and sword and with great stones,
266	while the blood still welled up warm from his wound.
267	But when the wound dried and the blood stopped,
268	sharp pains came over the might of the son of Atreus.
269	As when the sharp dart seizes a woman in labor,
270	the piercing dart that the Eileithyiai send, who bring hard birth,
271	the daughters of Hera, who have the bitter pangs in their keeping,
272	so sharp pains came over the might of the son of Atreus.
273	He sprang up into his chariot and ordered his charioteer
274	to drive to the hollow ships; for his heart was distressed.
275	And he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Danaans:
276	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
277	it is for you now to ward off from the seafaring ships
278	the grievous din of battle, since Zeus the counselor did not
279	allow me to fight the Trojans all day long.”
280	So he spoke, and the charioteer lashed the fine-maned horses
281	toward the hollow ships, and the pair flew on not unwilling;
282	their chests were flecked with foam, and they were sprinkled with dust beneath,
283	as they carried the worn-out king away from the battle.
284	But when Hector saw Agamemnon going away,
285	he called out to the Trojans and Lycians, shouting aloud:
286	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
287	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage.
288	Their best man is gone, and a great triumph has been given me
289	by Zeus, son of Cronus; come, drive your single-hoofed horses straight
290	at the mighty Danaans, so that you may win a higher triumph.”
291	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
292	And as a hunter sets his white-toothed hounds
293	on a wild boar or a lion,
294	so against the Achaeans he set on the great-hearted Trojans,
295	Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals.
296	And he himself strode among the foremost in high spirits,
297	and fell on the battle like a blustering storm wind
298	that swoops down and stirs up the violet sea.
299	Then whom first, whom last did he slay,
300	Hector, son of Priam, when Zeus gave him glory?
301	Asaeus first, and Autonous and Opites,
302	and Dolops, son of Clytius, and Opheltius and Agelaus,
303	and Aesymnus and Orus and Hipponous, steadfast in battle.
304	These were the leaders of the Danaans he killed, and after them
305	the multitude; as when the West Wind batters the clouds
306	of the brightening South Wind, striking with a deep squall,
307	and many a swollen wave rolls on, and the spray is scattered
308	high under the blast of the wandering wind,
309	so thick were the heads of the people beaten down by Hector.
310	Then there would have been ruin, and deeds past mending would have been done,
311	and the Achaeans would have fled and fallen among their ships,
312	had not Odysseus called to Diomedes, son of Tydeus:
313	“Son of Tydeus, what has happened to us, that we have forgotten our furious courage?
314	Come here, my friend, and stand by me; for it will be a disgrace
315	if Hector of the gleaming helmet takes the ships.”
316	Answering him, mighty Diomedes said:
317	“I will stay, and I will endure; but short
318	will be our profit of it, since Zeus the cloud-gatherer
319	wishes to give power to the Trojans rather than to us.”
320	He spoke, and thrust Thymbraeus from the chariot to the ground,
321	striking him on the left nipple with his spear; and Odysseus
322	struck down Molion, the godlike attendant of that lord.
323	These they left, since they had stopped them from fighting;
324	and the two went through the throng spreading tumult, as when two boars
325	fall in high spirits on the hunting dogs;
326	so they turned again and killed the Trojans; and the Achaeans
327	were glad to take breath as they fled from brilliant Hector.
328	Then they took a chariot and two men, the best of their people,
329	the two sons of Merops of Percote, who above all men
330	knew the art of prophecy, and would not let his sons
331	go into man-destroying war; but they would not
332	obey him at all, for the fates of black death were leading them on.
333	These two Diomedes, son of Tydeus, famed for his spear,
334	robbed of spirit and life, and took their glorious armor;
335	and Odysseus slew and stripped Hippodamus and Hypeirochus.
336	Then the son of Cronus stretched the battle evenly for them,
337	looking down from Ida; and they kept killing one another.
338	The son of Tydeus wounded Agastrophus with his spear,
339	the hero, son of Paeon, on the hip; and his horses
340	were not near for him to escape; he was greatly blinded in his heart.
341	For his attendant held them some way off, and he on foot
342	rushed through the champions, until he lost his dear life.
343	But Hector was quick to see them across the ranks, and rushed at them
344	shouting; and with him followed the battalions of the Trojans.
345	Seeing him, Diomedes, good at the war cry, shuddered,
346	and at once he spoke to Odysseus, who was near:
347	“Here is this bane rolling down on us, mighty Hector;
348	but come, let us stand firm and hold our ground and beat him off.”
349	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
350	and hit him, and did not miss, aiming at his head,
351	on the top of the helmet; but bronze glanced off bronze,
352	and did not reach the fair skin, for the helmet held it off,
353	three-layered, with its socket for the plume, which Phoebus Apollo had given him.
354	And Hector quickly ran back a measureless way and mingled with the throng,
355	and he sank to his knees and stayed there, and leaned with his strong hand
356	on the earth; and black night covered his eyes.
357	But while the son of Tydeus went after the cast of his spear,
358	far through the champions, where it had sunk into the ground,
359	Hector meanwhile came to himself, and leaping back into his chariot
360	he drove out into the multitude, and avoided black doom.
361	And darting at him with his spear, mighty Diomedes said:
362	“Once again you have escaped death now, you dog; truly, evil came close
363	to you; and now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you,
364	to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins.
365	I will surely finish you off when I meet you later,
366	if there is any god who is a helper to me as well.
367	Now I will go after the others, whomever I can catch.”
368	He spoke, and began to strip the son of Paeon, famed for his spear.
369	But Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair,
370	was bending his bow at the son of Tydeus, shepherd of the people,
371	leaning against a pillar on the man-made tomb
372	of Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, an elder of the people long ago.
373	The one was taking the gleaming corselet of mighty Agastrophus
374	from his chest, and the shield from his shoulders,
375	and his heavy helmet; and the other drew back his bow by the grip
376	and shot, and the shaft did not fly from his hand in vain,
377	but struck the flat of his right foot; and the arrow went right through
378	and stuck in the ground; and he laughed very heartily,
379	and leapt out from his ambush, and spoke in triumph:
380	“You are hit, and the shaft did not fly in vain. I wish I had
381	struck you in the lowest part of the flank and taken your life.
382	Then the Trojans too would have had a breathing space from misery,
383	who shudder at you as bleating goats shudder at a lion.”
384	Unafraid, mighty Diomedes answered him:
385	“Archer, insulter, glorying in your bow, ogler of girls,
386	if you should make trial of me face to face in armor,
387	your bow would not help you, nor your thick-flying arrows;
388	but now you have scratched the flat of my foot, and you boast for nothing.
389	I care no more than if a woman had hit me, or a witless child;
390	for blunt is the shaft of a weakling, a nobody.
391	It is otherwise with mine: even if it only grazes,
392	the shaft is sharp, and quickly lays a man lifeless;
393	and the cheeks of his wife are torn in mourning,
394	and his children are orphans; and he, reddening the earth with his blood,
395	rots, and there are more birds around him than women.”
396	So he spoke, and Odysseus, famed for his spear, came near
397	and stood in front of him; and he sat down behind him and pulled the swift shaft
398	out of his foot, and a grievous pain went through his flesh.
399	He sprang up into his chariot and ordered his charioteer
400	to drive to the hollow ships; for his heart was distressed.
401	And Odysseus, famed for his spear, was left alone, and no one
402	of the Argives stayed beside him, since rout had seized them all;
403	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
404	“Ah me, what will become of me? It is a great evil if I flee,
405	fearing the multitude; but it is worse if I am caught
406	alone; and the son of Cronus has put the other Danaans to rout.
407	But why does my heart debate these things with me?
408	For I know that it is cowards who leave the fighting,
409	but whoever would be best in battle must by all means
410	stand his ground strongly, whether he is struck or strikes another.”
411	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
412	the ranks of the shield-bearing Trojans came on,
413	and hemmed him in among them, setting their own bane in their midst.
414	And as when around a boar the dogs and young men in their prime
415	rush, and he comes out of a deep thicket,
416	whetting the white tusk in his curving jaws,
417	and they rush at him all around, and a gnashing of tusks
418	rises, and they stand their ground at once, terrible though he is,
419	so then around Odysseus, dear to Zeus, rushed
420	the Trojans; and he first wounded noble Deiopites
421	on the shoulder from above, leaping at him with his sharp spear,
422	and then he slew and stripped Thoon and Ennomus.
423	And then, as Chersidamas leapt down from his chariot,
424	he stabbed him with his spear in the navel, beneath the bossed shield;
425	and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm.
426	These he left, and wounded Charops, son of Hippasus, with his spear,
427	the own brother of wealthy Socus.
428	And Socus, a man equal to the gods, came to defend him,
429	and he came up close and stood and spoke to him:
430	“Much-praised Odysseus, insatiable of tricks and toil,
431	today either you will boast over both sons of Hippasus,
432	having killed two such men and taken their armor,
433	or struck by my spear you will lose your life.”
434	So saying, he stabbed at his shield, balanced on every side.
435	And through the shining shield went the heavy spear,
436	and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet,
437	and tore all the skin from his ribs; but it was not allowed
438	by Pallas Athena to mingle with the man's entrails.
439	And Odysseus knew that the stroke had not come to a deadly place,
440	and drawing back he spoke a word to Socus:
441	“Ah, wretch, sheer destruction is surely overtaking you.
442	You have stopped me, it is true, from fighting the Trojans;
443	but for you, I say, slaughter and black doom
444	will come here this very day, and brought down by my spear
445	you will give me triumph, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses.”
446	He spoke, and the other had turned again to flee,
447	and as the man turned, he planted his spear in his back
448	between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest;
449	and he fell with a thud; and brilliant Odysseus boasted over him:
450	“Socus, son of wise-hearted Hippasus, tamer of horses,
451	the end of death was too quick for you and caught you, and you did not escape.
452	Ah, wretch, your father and your lady mother
453	will not close your eyes in death, but the birds
454	that eat raw flesh will tear at you, beating their thick wings around you.
455	But if I die, the brilliant Achaeans will give me burial rites.”

462	Three times then he shouted, as loud as a man's head could hold,

465	“Telamonian Ajax, sprung from Zeus, lord of the people,

599	And brilliant swift-footed Achilles saw him and took note;

602	And at once he spoke to his companion Patroclus,
603	calling out from the ship; and he heard it in the hut

606	“Why do you call me, Achilles? What need do you have of me?”
607	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
608	“Brilliant son of Menoetius, dear to my heart,
609	now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees

611	But go now, Patroclus, dear to Zeus, and ask Nestor
612	who this man is that he brings wounded from the war.
613	From behind, it is true, he looks in every way like Machaon,
614	the son of Asclepius, but I did not see the man's eyes;
615	for the mares darted past me, straining forward.”
616	So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion,
617	and he went running along the huts and ships of the Achaeans.
618	And when they reached the hut of the son of Neleus,
619	they themselves stepped down onto the bountiful earth,
620	and Eurymedon, the old man's attendant, unyoked the horses
621	from the chariot; and the two let the sweat dry from their tunics,
622	standing in the breeze beside the shore of the sea; and then
623	they went into the hut and sat down on chairs.
624	And Hecamede of the lovely tresses made them a mixed drink,
625	whom the old man had won from Tenedos, when Achilles sacked it,
626	the daughter of great-hearted Arsinous, whom the Achaeans
627	picked out for him, because he was the best of all in counsel.
628	She first pushed up before the two of them a table,
629	a fine one, well polished, with feet of dark blue enamel, and on it
630	a bronze basket, and in it an onion, a relish for the drink,
631	and pale honey, and beside it the meal of holy barley,
632	and beside them a beautiful cup, which the old man had brought from home,
633	studded with golden nails; and its handles
634	were four, and around each two doves
635	of gold were feeding, and below were two bases.
636	Another man could move it from the table only with toil
637	when it was full, but Nestor, the old man, lifted it without toil.

645	And seeing him the old man rose from his shining chair,
646	and took him by the hand and led him in, and bade him sit down.
647	But Patroclus on his side refused, and spoke a word:
648	“There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, and you will not persuade me.
649	Revered and quick to blame is the one who sent me to find out
650	who this man is that you bring wounded; but I myself
651	know him, for I see Machaon, shepherd of the people.
652	And now I go back as a messenger, to tell the word to Achilles.
653	You know well, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, what kind of man

656	“Why does Achilles grieve like this for the sons of the Achaeans,

659	lie among the ships, shot or stabbed.
660	The son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes, has been shot,
661	and Odysseus, famed for his spear, has been stabbed, and Agamemnon;
662	and Eurypylus too has been shot in the thigh with an arrow;

664	shot with an arrow from the string. But Achilles,
665	brave as he is, does not care for the Danaans or pity them.

667	burn with consuming fire in spite of the Argives,

673	the brave son of Hypeirochus, who lived in Elis,

681	all mares, and many had foals beneath them.

691	in the years before, and all our best were killed;

693	of these I alone was left, and all the others perished.

695	treated us with outrage and devised reckless deeds.
696	And the old man took for himself a herd of cattle and a great flock of sheep,
