# Homer, Iliad, Book 2 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	Now the other gods and the men who fight from chariots
2	slept all night long, but sweet sleep did not hold Zeus;
3	he was pondering in his mind how Achilles
4	he might honor, and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans.
5	And this plan seemed to him in his heart the best:
6	to send a baneful Dream to Agamemnon, son of Atreus.
7	And he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
8	“Go, baneful Dream, to the swift ships of the Achaeans;
9	go into the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
10	and tell him everything exactly as I command.
11	Bid him arm the long-haired Achaeans
12	with all speed; for now he may take the wide-streeted city
13	of the Trojans. For the immortals who have their homes on Olympus
14	are no longer of two minds; she has bent them all,
15	Hera, with her pleading, and sorrows hang over the Trojans.”
16	So he spoke, and the Dream went, when he had heard the command,
17	and quickly he reached the swift ships of the Achaeans,
18	and went to Agamemnon, son of Atreus; and he found him
19	sleeping in his hut, and ambrosial sleep was poured around him.
20	He stood above his head in the likeness of the son of Neleus,
21	Nestor, whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders;
22	in his likeness the divine Dream spoke to him:
23	“You are asleep, son of wise-hearted Atreus, tamer of horses.
24	A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,
25	a man to whom the people are entrusted, and who has so much to care for.
26	Now listen to me quickly: I am a messenger to you from Zeus,
27	who, though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you.
28	He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans
29	with all speed; for now you may take the wide-streeted city
30	of the Trojans. For the immortals who have their homes on Olympus
31	are no longer of two minds; she has bent them all,
32	Hera, with her pleading, and sorrows hang over the Trojans
33	from Zeus. But keep this in your mind, and do not let forgetfulness
34	take hold of you when honey-sweet sleep lets you go.”
35	So he spoke and went away, and left him there
36	pondering in his heart things that were not to be fulfilled.
37	For he thought that he would take the city of Priam that very day—
38	the fool, who did not know what deeds Zeus was planning;
39	for he was yet to lay more sorrows and groaning
40	on Trojans and Danaans alike in the course of fierce battles.
41	He woke from his sleep, and the divine voice was poured around him.
42	He sat up and put on his soft tunic,
43	fine and new-made, and threw his great cloak around him,
44	and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals,
45	and around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword;
46	and he took up the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever,
47	and with it went along the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans.
48	Now the goddess Dawn went up to high Olympus
49	to announce the light to Zeus and the other immortals,
50	and he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
51	to summon the long-haired Achaeans to the assembly.
52	They made the summons, and the men gathered very quickly.
53	But first he seated a council of the great-hearted elders
54	beside the ship of Nestor, the king born in Pylos.
55	When he had called them together, he laid out a shrewd plan:
56	“Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep
57	through the ambrosial night, and he was most like brilliant Nestor,
58	very close to him in looks and stature and build.
59	He stood above my head and spoke to me:
60	‘You are asleep, son of wise-hearted Atreus, tamer of horses.
61	A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,
62	a man to whom the people are entrusted, and who has so much to care for.
63	Now listen to me quickly: I am a messenger to you from Zeus,
64	who, though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you.
65	He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans
66	with all speed; for now you may take the wide-streeted city
67	of the Trojans. For the immortals who have their homes on Olympus
68	are no longer of two minds; she has bent them all,
69	Hera, with her pleading, and sorrows hang over the Trojans
70	from Zeus. But keep this in your mind.’ So he spoke,
71	and flew away and was gone, and sweet sleep let me go.
72	Come then, let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans.
73	But first I will test them with words, as is the custom,
74	and I will order them to flee with their many-benched ships;
75	and you, each from his own place, hold them back with words.”
76	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
77	Nestor, who was king of sandy Pylos.
78	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
79	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
80	if any other of the Achaeans had told us of this dream,
81	we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more;
82	but now the man who has seen it claims to be by far the best of the Achaeans.
83	Come then, let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans.”
84	So he spoke, and led the way out of the council,
85	and they rose up after him and obeyed the shepherd of the people,
86	the scepter-bearing kings; and the people came streaming on.
87	As the tribes of bees go out in their swarms,
88	pouring from a hollow rock, always coming on anew,
89	and fly in clusters over the flowers of spring,
90	and in their crowds some are on the wing here, and some there,
91	so the many tribes of men, from the ships and huts,
92	marched in companies along the front of the deep shore
93	to the assembly, crowd after crowd; and Rumor blazed among them,
94	the messenger of Zeus, urging them on; and they gathered.
95	The place of assembly was in uproar, and the earth groaned
96	as the people sat down, and there was a din. Nine heralds
97	were shouting to restrain them, if they would ever hold back from their clamor
98	and listen to the kings whom Zeus has nurtured.
99	With difficulty the people were seated and held in their places,
100	and ceased from their clamor; and powerful Agamemnon
101	stood up, holding the scepter that Hephaestus had toiled to make.
102	Hephaestus gave it to lord Zeus, son of Cronus,
103	and Zeus gave it to the guide, the slayer of Argus,
104	and lord Hermes gave it to Pelops, driver of horses,
105	and Pelops in turn gave it to Atreus, shepherd of the people,
106	and Atreus when he died left it to Thyestes, rich in flocks,
107	and Thyestes in turn left it to Agamemnon to carry,
108	to be lord of many islands and of all Argos.
109	Leaning on it, he spoke his words among the Argives:
110	“Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares,
111	Zeus, son of Cronus, has bound me fast in heavy blind folly—
112	hard god, who once promised me and nodded his assent
113	that I would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home;
114	but now he has devised an evil deceit, and bids me
115	go back to Argos in disgrace, when I have lost so many men.
116	Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might,
117	who has brought down the heights of many cities
118	and will bring down more; for his power is the greatest.
119	For this is a shameful thing, even for men to come to hear of,
120	that so fine and so great an army of the Achaeans should, all in vain,
121	wage a war that comes to nothing, and fight
122	against fewer men, and no end has yet come in sight.
123	For if we were willing, Achaeans and Trojans,
124	to swear a trusty truce, and both be counted—
125	the Trojans gathering all who have hearths in the city,
126	and we Achaeans ranged in companies of ten,
127	and each company chose a Trojan man to pour its wine—
128	many a ten would go without a wine-pourer.
129	So far, I say, do the sons of the Achaeans outnumber
130	the Trojans who live in the city. But there are allies
131	from many cities, spear-brandishing men,
132	who drive me far off course and will not let me, much as I want to,
133	sack the well-peopled citadel of Ilios.
134	Nine years of great Zeus have gone by,
135	and the timbers of our ships have rotted, and the ropes have come loose;
136	and our wives, I suppose, and our little children
137	sit in our halls and wait for us; and still the work
138	for which we came here lies unfinished, just as it was.
139	Come then, let us all do as I say:
140	let us flee with our ships to our dear native land;
141	for we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy.”
142	So he spoke, and stirred the heart in the breast
143	of every man in the crowd who had not heard the council.
144	The assembly was stirred like the long waves of the sea
145	on the Icarian deep, which the East Wind and the South Wind
146	raise up, rushing down from the clouds of father Zeus.
147	And as when the West Wind comes and stirs a deep field of grain,
148	blowing hard and wild, and the field bows down with its ears,
149	so their whole assembly was stirred; and with a shout
150	they rushed to the ships, and from under their feet the dust
151	rose and hung in the air; and they called to one another
152	to lay hold of the ships and drag them down to the shining sea,
153	and they cleared out the launching-trenches; their shout reached the sky
154	as they longed for home; and they began to pull the props from under the ships.
155	Then the Argives would have had their homecoming against fate,
156	had not Hera spoken a word to Athena:
157	“Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone,
158	is it so, then, that home to their dear native land
159	the Argives will flee, over the broad back of the sea,
160	and leave to Priam and the Trojans a thing to boast of,
161	Argive Helen, for whose sake many of the Achaeans
162	have perished in Troy, far from their dear native land?
163	Go now through the army of the bronze-armored Achaeans;
164	with your gentle words hold back every man,
165	and do not let them drag their curving ships to the sea.”
166	So she spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena did not disobey,
167	but went darting down from the peaks of Olympus,
168	and quickly she reached the swift ships of the Achaeans.
169	There she found Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
170	standing still. He had not laid a hand on his black, well-benched ship,
171	for grief had come upon his heart and spirit.
172	Standing near him, gray-eyed Athena spoke to him:
173	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
174	is it so, then, that home to your dear native land
175	you will flee, tumbling into your many-benched ships,
176	and leave to Priam and the Trojans a thing to boast of,
177	Argive Helen, for whose sake many of the Achaeans
178	have perished in Troy, far from their dear native land?
179	Go now through the army of the Achaeans, and hang back no longer;
180	with your gentle words hold back every man,
181	and do not let them drag their curving ships to the sea.”
182	So she spoke, and he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke,
183	and he set off running, and threw off his cloak; it was taken up
184	by the herald Eurybates of Ithaca, who attended him.
185	He himself went straight to Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
186	and received from him the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever,
187	and with it went along the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans.
188	Whenever he came upon a king or a man of note,
189	he would stand beside him and hold him back with gentle words:
190	“Strange one, it is not right for you to panic like a coward.
191	Sit down yourself, and make the rest of the people sit.
192	For you do not yet clearly know the mind of the son of Atreus;
193	now he is testing them, but soon he will strike the sons of the Achaeans.
194	Not all of us heard what he said in the council.
195	Beware that in his anger he does not harm the sons of the Achaeans.
196	Great is the spirit of kings whom Zeus has nurtured;
197	a king's honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves him.”
198	But whenever he saw a man of the people and found him shouting,
199	he would strike him with the scepter and rebuke him with a word:
200	“Strange one, sit still, and listen to the word of others
201	who are better than you. You are unwarlike and weak,
202	never counted for anything in war or in council.
203	We Achaeans shall not all be kings here, not by any means.
204	The rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler,
205	one king, to whom the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given
206	the scepter and the ordinances, so that he may take counsel for them.”
207	So, like a master, he went through the army; and back to the assembly
208	they rushed again from the ships and huts
209	with a roar, as when a wave of the loud-roaring sea
210	crashes on the long beach, and the sea resounds.
211	The others sat down and were held in their places;
212	only Thersites still kept up his brawling, a man of unmeasured words,
213	whose mind was full of words, many and disorderly,
214	for quarreling with the kings, idly and against all order,
215	saying whatever he thought would make the Argives
216	laugh. He was the ugliest man who came to Ilios.
217	He was bandy-legged and lame in one foot, and his two shoulders
218	were rounded and hunched together over his chest; and above them
219	his head was pointed, and a scant stubble sprouted on it.
220	He was most hateful of all to Achilles and to Odysseus,
221	for those two he was always abusing; but now at brilliant Agamemnon
222	he screamed out his shrill insults; and the Achaeans
223	resented him terribly, and were indignant in their hearts.
224	But he, shouting loudly, abused Agamemnon in these words:
225	“Son of Atreus, what are you complaining of now, what do you lack?
226	Your huts are full of bronze, and there are many women
227	in your huts, the choicest, whom we Achaeans
228	give to you first of all, whenever we take a citadel.
229	Or do you want still more gold, which someone will bring
230	out of Ilios, one of the horse-taming Trojans, as ransom for his son,
231	whom I or another of the Achaeans may bind and bring in?
232	Or a young woman, to lie with in love,
233	and keep apart for yourself alone? It is not right
234	for one who is their leader to bring the sons of the Achaeans to grief.
235	Weaklings, wretched disgraces, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer,
236	let us go home with our ships, and leave this man
237	here in Troy to stew over his prizes, so that he may see
238	whether we too are any help to him or not—
239	for even now, Achilles, a far better man than himself,
240	he has dishonored: he has taken his prize and keeps it, having seized it himself.
241	But there is no anger at all in Achilles' heart; he lets it pass.
242	Otherwise, son of Atreus, this insult would be your last.”
243	So he spoke, abusing Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
244	Thersites; but brilliant Odysseus quickly came and stood beside him,
245	and looking at him darkly rebuked him with harsh words:
246	“Thersites, reckless in speech, clear-voiced orator though you are,
247	hold your tongue, and do not try all alone to quarrel with kings.
248	For I say there is no mortal worse than you
249	of all who came with the sons of Atreus to Ilios.
250	So you should not have the kings in your mouth as you harangue us,
251	casting reproaches at them, and watching for your homecoming.
252	We do not yet clearly know how these things will turn out,
253	whether we sons of the Achaeans shall return well or badly.
254	And now at Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people,
255	you sit hurling reproaches, because they give him so very much,
256	the Danaan heroes; and you speak only to taunt.
257	But I will speak out to you, and it will surely come to pass:
258	if I find you playing the fool again, as you are now,
259	then may the head of Odysseus no longer sit on his shoulders,
260	and may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus,
261	if I do not take you and strip off your own clothes,
262	your cloak and your tunic, which cover your nakedness,
263	and send you howling back to the swift ships,
264	beaten out of the assembly with shameful blows.”
265	So he spoke, and with the scepter he struck him on the back and shoulders;
266	and he doubled over, and a big tear fell from his eye,
267	and a bloody welt rose up on his back
268	under the golden scepter; and he sat down, terrified,
269	and in pain, with a helpless look, he wiped away the tear.
270	And troubled though they were, they laughed heartily at him;
271	and this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
272	“Ah, truly Odysseus has done countless good deeds,
273	leading the way in good counsel and marshaling the battle;
274	but now this is by far the best thing he has done among the Argives,
275	to stop this word-slinging slanderer from haranguing us.
276	Surely his proud spirit will never drive him again
277	to rail at the kings with abusive words.”
278	So said the crowd; and up rose Odysseus, sacker of cities,
279	holding the scepter; and beside him gray-eyed Athena,
280	in the likeness of a herald, ordered the people to be silent,
281	so that the nearest and the farthest of the sons of the Achaeans alike
282	might hear his words and take thought for his counsel.
283	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
284	“Son of Atreus, now, my lord, the Achaeans want
285	to make you the most despised of all mortal men,
286	and they will not fulfill the promise that they made you
287	while they were still on their way here from horse-pasturing Argos,
288	that you would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home.
289	For like young children or widowed women
290	they wail to one another about going home.
291	Truly, the toil here is enough to make a man go home in weariness.
292	For a man who stays even a single month away from his wife
293	chafes beside his many-benched ship, when the winter
294	storms and the surging sea keep him penned in;
295	but for us this is the ninth year come round
296	that we have been waiting here. So I do not blame the Achaeans
297	for chafing beside the curved ships; but all the same,
298	it is shameful to stay so long and go home empty-handed.
299	Endure, friends, and stay a little while, so that we may learn
300	whether Calchas' prophecy is true or not.
301	For this we know well in our hearts, and you are all
302	witnesses, all whom the fates of death have not carried away:
303	it was yesterday, it seems, or the day before, when at Aulis the ships of the Achaeans
304	were gathering, bringing evils to Priam and the Trojans,
305	and we, around a spring, at the holy altars,
306	were offering perfect hecatombs to the immortals
307	beneath a fine plane tree, where bright water flowed.
308	There a great sign appeared: a serpent, blood-red on its back,
309	a terrible thing, which the Olympian himself had sent into the light,
310	darted out from beneath the altar and made for the plane tree.
311	There were the nestlings of a sparrow, helpless young,
312	on the topmost branch, cowering beneath the leaves,
313	eight of them, and the mother who bore them was the ninth.
314	There the serpent devoured them as they cheeped pitifully,
315	while the mother fluttered around them, lamenting her dear young;
316	and coiling himself he caught her by the wing as she shrieked around him.
317	But when he had devoured the sparrow's young and the mother too,
318	the god who had brought him to light made him a sign for all to see;
319	for the son of crooked-counseling Cronus turned him to stone,
320	and we stood there and marveled at what had come to pass.
321	So when the dread portents of the gods broke in upon the hecatombs,
322	Calchas at once spoke out, prophesying:
323	‘Why are you silent, long-haired Achaeans?
324	To us Zeus the counselor has shown this great sign,
325	late in coming, late in fulfillment, whose fame shall never perish.
326	As this serpent devoured the sparrow's young and the mother too,
327	eight of them, and the mother who bore them was the ninth,
328	so we shall wage war there for as many years,
329	and in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city.’
330	So he spoke then; and now it is all being fulfilled.
331	Come, stay, all you well-greaved Achaeans,
332	right here, until we take the great city of Priam.”
333	So he spoke, and the Argives shouted aloud, and all around the ships
334	echoed terribly with the shouting of the Achaeans
335	as they praised the words of divine Odysseus.
336	And among them spoke also the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
337	“Ah, truly you talk in the assembly like children,
338	foolish little ones, who care nothing for the works of war.
339	What will become of our compacts and our oaths?
340	Into the fire, then, with the counsels and plans of men,
341	the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted!
342	For we go on quarreling like this with words, and can find
343	no remedy, though we have been here so long.
344	Son of Atreus, you, as before, holding to your steadfast purpose,
345	lead the Argives through the fierce battles,
346	and let these few waste away, the one or two of the Achaeans
347	who make plans apart from the rest—though nothing will come of them—
348	to go to Argos before they know whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus
349	is a lie or not.
350	For I say that the son of Cronus, supreme in might, nodded his assent
351	on that day when they went aboard their swift-sailing ships,
352	the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans:
353	he flashed his lightning on our right, showing signs of good omen.
354	So let no one be in a hurry to go home
355	before he has lain beside the wife of some Trojan
356	and paid them back for the struggles and groans for Helen's sake.
357	And if any man is desperate to go home,
358	let him lay hands on his black, well-benched ship,
359	so that before the others he may meet his death and his fate.
360	But you, my lord, take good counsel yourself, and listen to another;
361	the word I speak will not be one to cast aside.
362	Separate the men by tribes and by clans, Agamemnon,
363	so that clan may help clan, and tribe tribe.
364	If you do this, and the Achaeans obey you,
365	then you will know which of the leaders is a coward, and which of the people,
366	and which is brave; for they will fight each among their own.
367	And you will know whether it is by the will of heaven that you do not sack the city,
368	or by the cowardice of men and their ignorance of war.”
369	Answering him, powerful Agamemnon said:
370	“Once again, old man, you surpass the sons of the Achaeans in the assembly.
371	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
372	I had ten such counselors among the Achaeans!
373	Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head,
374	taken and sacked beneath our hands.
375	But aegis-bearing Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me sorrows,
376	who throws me into fruitless strife and quarrels.
377	For Achilles and I fought for the sake of a girl
378	with hostile words, and I was the first to grow angry.
379	But if ever we are of one mind in counsel, then no longer
380	will the Trojans have any respite from evil, not even a little.
381	But now go to your meal, so that we may join in the clash of Ares.
382	Let each man sharpen his spear well and make his shield ready,
383	and let each give his swift-footed horses their feed,
384	and look carefully over his chariot and take thought for war,
385	so that all day long we may try our strength in the hateful work of Ares.
386	For there will be no pause, not even a little,
387	unless night comes and parts the fury of men.
388	Many a man will sweat through the strap across his chest
389	of his man-covering shield, and his hand will grow weary on his spear;
390	and many a horse will sweat as it strains at the polished chariot.
391	But any man I see wishing to hang back from the fighting
392	and linger beside the curved ships, for him there will be
393	no hope then of escaping the dogs and the birds.”
394	So he spoke, and the Argives shouted aloud, like a wave
395	against a high shore, when the South Wind comes and stirs it,
396	against a jutting crag, which the waves never leave alone
397	from winds of every kind, whenever they rise from this side or that.
398	They stood up and hurried off, scattering among the ships,
399	and made fires in the huts and took their meal.
400	Each man sacrificed to one or another of the everlasting gods,
401	praying to escape death and the toil of Ares.
402	But Agamemnon, lord of men, sacrificed an ox,
403	a fat one, five years old, to the son of Cronus, supreme in might,
404	and he called the elders, the best of all the Achaeans:
405	Nestor first of all, and lord Idomeneus,
406	and then the two Ajaxes, and the son of Tydeus,
407	and sixth, Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel.
408	And Menelaus, good at the war cry, came to him unbidden,
409	for he knew in his heart how his brother was laboring.
410	They stood around the ox and took up the barley grains;
411	and praying among them, powerful Agamemnon said:
412	“Zeus, most glorious, greatest, lord of the dark clouds, who dwell in the upper air,
413	let the sun not go down and darkness not come on
414	before I have thrown down headlong the hall of Priam,
415	blackened with smoke, and burned its doors with consuming fire,
416	and torn the tunic of Hector around his chest,
417	rent with the bronze; and may many of his companions around him
418	fall headlong in the dust and bite the earth.”
419	So he spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill his prayer;
420	he accepted the offerings, but made the toil greater, a toil none would envy.
421	And when they had prayed and scattered the barley grains,
422	first they drew back the victim's head, and cut its throat, and flayed it,
423	and cut out the thigh-bones and wrapped them in fat,
424	making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them.
425	These they burned on split logs stripped of their leaves,
426	and they spitted the entrails and held them over the flame of Hephaestus.
427	But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails,
428	they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
429	and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off.
430	And when they had ceased from the work and made the feast ready,
431	they feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast.
432	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
433	the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them:
434	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
435	let us not sit here talking any longer, and let us no longer
436	put off the work which the god is putting into our hands.
437	Come, let the heralds of the bronze-armored Achaeans
438	make proclamation and gather the people along the ships,
439	and let us go together, as we are, through the wide army of the Achaeans,
440	so that we may the sooner rouse sharp Ares.”
441	So he spoke, and Agamemnon, lord of men, did not disobey.
442	At once he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
443	to summon the long-haired Achaeans to war.
