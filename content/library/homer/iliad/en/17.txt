# Homer, Iliad, Book 17 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares,
2	that Patroclus had been brought down by the Trojans in the combat.
3	He went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze,
4	and bestrode him, as a mother stands over her calf,
5	one that has borne her first, lowing plaintively, and never knew birth before;
6	so fair-haired Menelaus bestrode Patroclus,
7	and held in front of him his spear and his shield, balanced on every side,
8	eager to kill whoever came against him.
9	Nor did the son of Panthous, of the good ash spear, neglect
10	noble Patroclus when he fell; and close beside him
11	he stood, and spoke to Menelaus, dear to Ares:
12	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
13	draw back, and leave the body, and let the bloody spoils be;
14	for none of the Trojans or their famous allies before me
15	hit Patroclus with the spear in the fierce battle;
16	so let me win noble fame among the Trojans,
17	lest I hit you and take away your honey-sweet life.”
18	Deeply troubled, fair-haired Menelaus said to him:
19	“Father Zeus, it is no fine thing to boast so overweeningly.
20	Neither is the leopard's might so great, nor the lion's,
21	nor the deadly wild boar's, whose spirit is greatest of all
22	in his breast, exulting surpassingly in his strength,
23	as the pride of the sons of Panthous, of the good ash spear.
24	Nor indeed did mighty Hyperenor, tamer of horses,
25	have joy of his youth, when he scorned me and stood up to me
26	and said that I was the most despised warrior among the Danaans;
27	and I say that he did not go home on his own feet
28	to gladden his dear wife and his devoted parents.
29	So surely I will loose your strength too, if you come
30	and stand against me; no, I bid you draw back
31	and go into the crowd, and do not stand against me,
32	before you suffer some harm; a fool learns a thing once it is done.”
33	So he spoke, but he did not persuade him; and answering he said:
34	“Now, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, now you will surely pay
35	for my brother, whom you killed, and you boast of it as you speak;
36	and you widowed his wife in the inmost part of her new bridal chamber,
37	and brought accursed lamentation and grief upon his parents.
38	Surely I would put an end to the lamentation of those wretched ones
39	if I brought back your head and your armor
40	and laid them in the hands of Panthous and of brilliant Phrontis.
41	But the toil will not be untried much longer,
42	nor left unfought, whether it end in courage or in rout.”
43	So saying, he stabbed at his shield, balanced on every side;
44	but the bronze did not break through, and its point was bent back
45	in the strong shield. And then he rushed second with the bronze,
46	Menelaus, son of Atreus, praying to father Zeus;
47	and as the other gave ground he stabbed him at the base of the throat,
48	and leaned his own weight on it, trusting in his heavy hand;
49	and straight through the soft neck went the point,
50	and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
51	His hair was drenched with blood, hair like the Graces',
52	and his braids, which were bound tight with gold and silver.
53	And as a man rears a flourishing sapling of olive
54	in a lonely place, where water has drenched it in plenty,
55	a fine, thriving tree; and the breezes of all the winds
56	shake it, and it teems with white blossom;
57	but a wind comes suddenly with a great storm
58	and wrenches it out of its trench and stretches it on the ground;
59	such was the son of Panthous, Euphorbus of the good ash spear,
60	whom Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed, and began to strip of his armor.
61	And as when a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage,
62	snatches from a grazing herd the cow that is the best;
63	he seizes her in his strong teeth and breaks her neck
64	first, and then gulps down her blood and all her entrails,
65	mauling her; and around him the dogs and the herdsmen
66	raise a great clamor from far off, but they are not willing
67	to come against him; for pale fear takes hold of them;
68	so no one of them had the heart in his breast to dare
69	to come against glorious Menelaus.
70	Then he would easily have carried off the glorious armor of the son of Panthous,
71	he, the son of Atreus, had not Phoebus Apollo begrudged him that,
72	who roused Hector against him, the equal of swift Ares,
73	in the likeness of a man, Mentes, the leader of the Cicones;
74	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
75	“Hector, now you are running like this, chasing what cannot be caught,
76	the horses of the wise-hearted grandson of Aeacus; but they are hard
77	for mortal men to master and to drive,
78	for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore.
79	And meanwhile warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus,
80	standing over Patroclus, has killed the best of the Trojans,
81	Euphorbus, son of Panthous, and stopped him from his furious courage.”
82	So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men;
83	and dread grief clouded Hector's heart, dark on every side.
84	Then he glanced along the ranks, and at once he knew
85	the one taking away the glorious armor, and the other on the ground,
86	lying; and the blood was flowing from the stab wound.
87	He went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze,
88	shouting shrilly, like the flame of Hephaestus,
89	unquenchable; nor did his shrill shout escape the son of Atreus;
90	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
91	“Ah me, if I leave behind the fine armor
92	and Patroclus, who lies here to win satisfaction for me,
93	I fear that any of the Danaans who sees it will be indignant with me.
94	But if I fight Hector and the Trojans alone
95	out of shame, I fear they may surround me, many against one;
96	for Hector of the gleaming helmet is leading all the Trojans here.
97	But why does my heart debate these things with me?
98	When a man is willing, against a god's will, to fight with a man
99	whom a god honors, soon a great bane rolls down on him.
100	So no one of the Danaans who sees it will be indignant with me
101	if I give way before Hector, since he fights with help from the gods.
102	But if somewhere I could hear of Ajax, good at the war cry,
103	the two of us would go back again and remember the joy of battle,
104	even against a god's will, if somehow we might drag away the body
105	for Achilles, son of Peleus; of evils that would be the best.”
106	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
107	the ranks of the Trojans came on; and Hector led them.
108	And he gave ground backward, and left the body,
109	turning back again and again, like a bearded lion
110	that dogs and men drive from the steading
111	with spears and shouting; and the valiant heart within him
112	grows chill, and he goes unwillingly from the farmyard;
113	so fair-haired Menelaus went from Patroclus.
114	He turned and stood, when he reached the crowd of his companions,
115	looking about for great Ajax, the son of Telamon.
116	And very quickly he saw him on the left of all the battle,
117	encouraging his companions and urging them on to fight;
118	for Phoebus Apollo had cast a heaven-sent rout among them.
119	He set off running, and at once, standing beside him, he spoke a word:
120	“Ajax, come here, my friend; for the sake of dead Patroclus
121	let us make haste, if we may bring at least his corpse to Achilles,
122	naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor.”
123	So he spoke, and he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Ajax,
124	and he went through the champions, and fair-haired Menelaus with him.
125	Hector, when he had taken the glorious armor from Patroclus,
126	was dragging him, to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze,
127	and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs.
128	And Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower;
129	and Hector gave ground, going back into the throng of his companions,
130	and sprang up into his chariot; and he gave the fine armor
131	to the Trojans to carry to the city, to be great fame for him.
132	And Ajax covered the son of Menoetius with his broad shield
133	and stood like a lion over his young,
134	when men meet him in the forest as he leads his little ones,
135	huntsmen; and he exults in his strength,
136	and draws down all the skin of his brow, covering his eyes;
137	so Ajax stood over the hero Patroclus.
138	And on the other side the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares,
139	stood, nursing great grief in his breast.
140	And Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, leader of the Lycian men,
141	looking at Hector darkly, rebuked him with harsh words:
142	“Hector, best in looks, you fall far short in battle, it seems.
143	Truly it is for nothing that noble fame is yours, a runaway as you are.
144	Consider now how you will save your city and your town
145	alone, with the people who were born in Ilios;
146	for none of the Lycians at least will go out to fight the Danaans
147	for the sake of the city, since there was, it seems, no thanks
148	for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever.
149	How would you save a lesser man in the throng,
150	hard man, when you left Sarpedon, at once your guest-friend and your companion,
151	to become the prey and spoil of the Argives,
152	who was of great use to you, to the city and to yourself,
153	while he lived? And now you did not dare to keep the dogs from him.
154	So now, if any of the Lycian men will listen to me,
155	we will go home, and sheer destruction will appear for Troy.
156	For if there were now in the Trojans a might full of daring,
157	undaunted, such as comes into men who for their country
158	have set toil and contest against hostile men,
159	we would quickly drag Patroclus into Ilios.
160	And if this man came to the great city of lord Priam
161	dead, and we dragged him out of the battle,
162	the Argives would quickly release the fine armor of Sarpedon,
163	and we could bring his body into Ilios;
164	for the attendant of such a man has been slain, who is by far the best
165	of the Argives beside the ships, he and his attendants who fight hand to hand.
166	But you could not bring yourself, before great-hearted Ajax,
167	to stand against him, looking him in the eye amid the war cry of the enemy,
168	or to fight him face to face, since he is stronger than you.”
169	Looking at him darkly, Hector of the gleaming helmet said:
170	“Glaucus, why have you, such a man as you are, spoken arrogantly?
171	Ah, truly I thought that you surpassed in wisdom all others
172	of those who live in deep-soiled Lycia;
173	but now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said,
174	you who say that I did not stand up to huge Ajax.
175	I tell you, I do not shudder at battle or at the din of horses;
176	but the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger;
177	he puts even a valiant man to flight and takes away victory
178	easily, and at another time he himself urges him on to fight.
179	But come here, my friend, and stand by me, and watch my work,
180	whether I shall be a coward all day long, as you say,
181	or whether I shall stop some one of the Danaans, however eager with courage,
182	from defending dead Patroclus.”
183	So saying, he called out to the Trojans, shouting aloud:
184	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
185	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage,
186	while I put on the armor of noble Achilles,
187	the fine armor I stripped from mighty Patroclus when I killed him.”
188	So he spoke, and Hector of the gleaming helmet went away
189	out of the destructive war; and running he overtook his companions
190	very quickly, not yet far off, following them on swift feet,
191	who were carrying to the city the glorious armor of the son of Peleus.
192	And standing apart from the tearful battle he changed his armor;
193	his own he gave to carry to sacred Ilios
194	to the war-loving Trojans, and he put on the immortal armor
195	of Achilles, son of Peleus, which the heavenly gods
196	gave to his dear father; and he granted it to his son
197	when he grew old; but the son did not grow old in his father's armor.
198	And when Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw him from afar
199	arming himself in the armor of the divine son of Peleus,
200	he shook his head and spoke to his own heart:
201	“Ah, wretch, death is not on your mind at all,
202	which is now close to you; and you put on the immortal armor
203	of the best of men, before whom others too tremble;
204	and you have killed his companion, gentle and strong,
205	and the armor, against all order, from his head and shoulders
206	you took; but now at least I will put great power in your hands,
207	in recompense, since you will not come home from the battle
208	for Andromache to receive from you the glorious armor of the son of Peleus.”
209	He spoke, and the son of Cronus nodded with his dark brows,
210	and he fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered into him,
211	dread Enyalius, and his limbs within were filled
212	with courage and strength; and he went among the famous allies,
213	with a great cry; and he appeared to them all
214	flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus.
215	And he went to each and urged him on with words,
216	Mesthles and Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus
217	and Asteropaeus and Deisenor and Hippothous
218	and Phorcys and Chromius and Ennomus the augur;
219	and urging them on he addressed them with winged words:
220	“Hear me, countless tribes of allies who dwell around us;
221	it was not in search of numbers, nor because I lacked them,
222	that I gathered each of you here from your cities,
223	but so that the wives of the Trojans and their little children
224	you might protect with a ready heart from the war-loving Achaeans.
225	With this in mind I wear down with gifts and with food
226	my people, and build up the spirit of each of you.
227	So now let each man turn straight at the enemy, and either perish
228	or be saved; for that is the intimate talk of war.
229	And whoever drags Patroclus, dead though he is,
230	among the horse-taming Trojans, and Ajax gives way to him,
231	to him I will give half of the spoils, and half I myself
232	will keep; and his fame will be as great as mine.”
233	So he spoke, and they went straight at the Danaans with all their weight,
234	holding up their spears; and their hearts had high hopes
235	of dragging the body away from under Ajax, son of Telamon,
236	the fools; truly over it he took the lives of many.
237	And then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war cry:
238	“My dear Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, no longer do I expect
239	that we two by ourselves will come back from the war.
240	I do not fear so much for the corpse of Patroclus,
241	which will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans,
242	as I fear for my own head, that it may suffer something,
243	and for yours, since he covers everything around, a cloud of war,
244	Hector, and for us in turn sheer destruction appears.
245	But come, call the best of the Danaans, if any may hear.”
246	So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war cry, did not disobey,
247	and he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Danaans:
248	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
249	you who beside the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
250	drink at the public cost, and each of you gives orders
251	to his people; and from Zeus come honor and glory.
252	It is hard for me to spy out each one
253	of the leaders, so fiercely has the strife of war blazed up;
254	but let each come of himself, and be indignant in his heart
255	that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs.”
256	So he spoke, and swift Ajax, son of Oileus, heard him clearly;
257	and he was the first to come running through the combat to meet him,
258	and after him Idomeneus and Idomeneus' squire,
259	Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men.
260	But of the others, who could tell the names from his own mind,
261	of all the Achaeans who came after and roused the battle?
262	And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them.
263	And as at the mouth of a river fallen from Zeus
264	a great wave roars against the stream, and all around the headlands
265	of the shore resound as the salt sea belches out,
266	with so great a shout the Trojans came on. But the Achaeans
267	stood around the son of Menoetius with one heart,
268	fenced with their bronze-fitted shields; and around them,
269	about their shining helmets, the son of Cronus poured a thick mist,
270	since he had not hated the son of Menoetius before,
271	while he was alive and was the attendant of the grandson of Aeacus;
272	and he hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies,
273	the Trojan dogs; so he roused his companions to defend him.
274	And the Trojans first pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans;
275	they left the body and shrank back; but not one of them
276	did the high-hearted Trojans kill with their spears, eager though they were,
277	but they dragged at the corpse; yet only for a short while were the Achaeans
278	to be away from it; for very quickly he rallied them,
279	Ajax, who in looks and in deeds stood out above the rest
280	of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus.
281	He made straight through the champions, like a boar in courage,
282	a wild boar, which in the mountains the dogs and the young men in their prime
283	easily scatters, wheeling about through the glens;
284	so the son of noble Telamon, shining Ajax,
285	easily going in among them, scattered the battalions of the Trojans
286	who stood around Patroclus and had it most in mind
287	to drag him to their own city and win glory.
288	Now the shining son of Lethus the Pelasgian,
289	Hippothous, was dragging him by the foot through the fierce battle,
290	having tied a strap around the tendons by the ankle,
291	to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans; but quickly upon him
292	came the evil that no one could keep from him, eager though they were.
293	The son of Telamon, rushing at him through the throng,
294	struck him at close quarters through the bronze-cheeked helmet;
295	and the horsehair-crested helmet split around the point of the spear,
296	struck by the great spear and the strong hand,
297	and the brain spurted from the wound along the spear's socket,
298	bloody; and there his strength was loosed, and from his hands
299	he let the foot of great-hearted Patroclus fall to the ground
300	to lie there; and he fell face down beside him on the body,
301	far from deep-soiled Larisa; and he did not repay
302	his dear parents for his rearing, and short was his life,
303	brought down by the spear of great-hearted Ajax.
304	And Hector in turn threw his shining spear at Ajax;
305	but he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear
306	narrowly; but Schedius, son of great-hearted Iphitus,
307	by far the best of the Phocians, who in famous Panopeus
308	had his home and ruled over many men—
309	him he hit under the middle of the collarbone, and clean through it the tip
310	of the bronze point came out at the base of the shoulder;
311	and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
312	And Ajax in turn, as Phorcys, the wise-hearted son of Phaenops,
313	stood over Hippothous, struck him in the middle of the belly,
314	and broke the plate of his corselet, and the bronze let out
315	his bowels; and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm.
316	The champions and shining Hector gave ground;
317	and the Argives shouted aloud, and dragged away the dead,
318	Phorcys and Hippothous, and loosed the armor from their shoulders.
319	Then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares,
320	would have gone up into Ilios, beaten by their own cowardice,
321	and the Argives would have won glory even beyond the portion of Zeus
322	by their own power and strength; but Apollo himself
323	urged on Aeneas, in form like Periphas,
324	the herald, son of Epytus, who beside Aeneas' old father
325	had grown old as a herald, a man whose mind was kindly toward him;
326	in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him:
327	“Aeneas, how could you protect, even in defiance of a god,
328	steep Ilios? As I have seen other men do,
329	trusting in their power and their strength and their manhood
330	and in their numbers, and having a people above fear;
331	but Zeus wills far rather for us than for the Danaans
332	victory; but you yourselves tremble beyond telling and do not fight.”
333	So he spoke, and Aeneas knew Apollo who shoots from afar
334	as he looked him in the face, and he shouted aloud to Hector:
335	“Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and the allies,
336	this now is a shame, before the Achaeans dear to Ares
337	to go up into Ilios, beaten by our own cowardice.
338	But one of the gods has come and stood close beside me, and says
339	that Zeus, the highest counselor, is still our helper in the battle;
340	so let us go straight at the Danaans, and let them not at their ease
341	bring dead Patroclus to the ships.”
342	So he spoke, and leaping out far in front of the champions he stood;
343	and they rallied, and stood facing the Achaeans.
344	Then Aeneas stabbed Leiocritus with his spear,
345	the son of Arisbas, the brave companion of Lycomedes.
346	And Lycomedes, dear to Ares, pitied him as he fell,
347	and came up close and stood and threw his shining spear,
348	and hit Apisaon, son of Hippasus, shepherd of the people,
349	in the liver beneath the midriff, and at once loosened his knees,
350	who had come from deep-soiled Paeonia,
351	and after Asteropaeus he was the best in the fighting.
352	And warlike Asteropaeus pitied him as he fell,
353	and he too made straight for them, eager to fight the Danaans;
354	but he could do nothing more; for they were fenced all around with their shields,
355	standing around Patroclus, and held their spears before them.
356	For Ajax went among them all with many commands;
357	he would let no one draw back from the body
358	nor any of the Achaeans fight out in front of the others,
359	but bade them stand firm around it and fight at close quarters.
360	So huge Ajax commanded; and the earth was wet
361	with purple blood, and they fell heaped close together,
362	the dead of the Trojans and their mighty allies
363	and of the Danaans; for they too did not fight without bloodshed,
364	but far fewer of them perished; for they always remembered
365	to ward off sheer slaughter from one another in the throng.
366	So they fought on like blazing fire; and you would not have said
367	that the sun was still safe, or the moon;
368	for they were covered in mist, in all that part of the battle where the best men
369	stood around the dead son of Menoetius.
370	But the other Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans
371	fought at their ease under the upper air, and the sharp light
372	of the sun was spread over them, and no cloud was seen over all
373	the earth or the mountains; and they fought with pauses between,
374	avoiding one another's groaning missiles,
375	standing far apart. But those in the middle suffered pains
376	from the mist and the war, and were worn down by the pitiless bronze,
377	all who were the best; but two men had not yet learned,
378	two glorious men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus,
379	that noble Patroclus was dead, but they still thought
380	that he was alive and fighting the Trojans in the front of the throng.
381	And the two, watchful against the death and the panic of their companions,
382	were fighting apart, since so Nestor had commanded
383	urging them on to war from the black ships.
384	But for the others all day long the great quarrel of strife went on,
385	grievous; and with labor and sweat, without rest, forever,
386	the knees and the shins and the feet beneath of each man
387	and the hands and the eyes were spattered, as the two sides fought
388	around the brave attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus.
389	And as when a man gives the hide of a great bull
390	to his people to stretch, drenched with fat;
391	and they take it and stand apart and stretch it
392	in a circle, and at once the moisture goes out and the fat sinks in
393	as many pull, and it is stretched all the way through;
394	so they kept pulling the corpse this way and that in a little space,
395	both sides; and their hearts had high hopes,
396	the Trojans' of dragging it to Ilios, and the Achaeans'
397	to the hollow ships; and around it the toil arose,
398	savage; not Ares, who rouses armies, nor Athena,
399	would have made light of it when they saw it, not even if great anger came upon them;
400	such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus
401	stretched tight that day, an evil toil. Nor yet at all
402	did brilliant Achilles know that Patroclus was dead;
403	for they were fighting far away from the swift ships,
404	under the wall of the Trojans; so he never expected in his heart
405	that he was dead, but that alive, after pressing close to the gates,
406	he would come back again, since he did not expect at all
407	that he would sack the citadel without him, nor with him either;
408	for often he had learned this from his mother, hearing it in secret,
409	who would report to him the purpose of great Zeus.
410	But this time she did not tell him of so great an evil as had come about,
411	his mother, that by far the dearest of his companions had perished.
412	And they around the body, holding their sharpened spears,
413	kept pressing on without rest and killing one another;
414	and this is what a man would say, of the bronze-armored Achaeans:
415	“Friends, it is no glorious thing for us to go back
416	to the hollow ships; rather let the black earth here
417	gape for us all; that would at once be far better for us
418	than to yield this man to the horse-taming Trojans
419	to drag to their own city and win glory.”
420	And so in turn a man of the great-hearted Trojans would speak:
421	“Friends, even if it is our fate to be brought down beside this man,
422	all of us alike, let no one yet hang back from the war.”
423	So a man would say, and would rouse the might of each.
424	So they fought on, and the iron din
425	went up through the barren upper air to the bronze sky.
426	But the horses of the grandson of Aeacus, standing apart from the battle,
427	were weeping, ever since they first learned that their charioteer
428	had fallen in the dust at the hands of man-slaying Hector.
429	Truly Automedon, the valiant son of Diores,
430	often touched them, striking them with the swift whip,
431	and often spoke to them with soothing words, and often with threats;
432	but the two would neither go back to the ships by the broad Hellespont
433	nor into the battle among the Achaeans,
434	but as a pillar stays fixed that stands on the tomb
435	of a man who has died, or of a woman,
436	so they stood unmoving, holding the beautiful chariot,
437	bowing their heads to the ground; and hot tears
438	flowed from their eyelids to the ground as they wept
439	in longing for their charioteer; and their rich manes were stained,
440	streaming from under the yoke-cushion down beside the yoke on both sides.
441	And the son of Cronus saw them weeping and pitied them,
442	and he shook his head and spoke to his own heart:
443	“Ah, wretched pair, why did we give you to lord Peleus,
444	a mortal, while you are ageless and immortal?
445	Was it so that you should have sorrows among unhappy men?
446	For there is nothing, I think, more miserable than man
447	of all the things that breathe and creep upon the earth.
448	But not behind you, nor in your intricately worked chariot,
449	will Hector, son of Priam, ride; I will not allow it.
450	Is it not enough that he has the armor too and boasts of it for nothing?
451	But I will put might into your knees and into your hearts,
452	so that you may bring Automedon too safe out of the war
453	to the hollow ships; for I will still hold out glory to them
454	to kill, until they come to the well-benched ships,
455	and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on.”
456	So saying, he breathed noble might into the horses.
457	And the two shook the dust from their manes to the ground,
458	and lightly bore the swift chariot among the Trojans and the Achaeans.
459	And behind them Automedon fought, grieving though he was for his companion,
460	swooping with his horses like a vulture after geese;
461	for easily he would escape out of the din of the Trojans,
462	and easily he would charge, pursuing through the great throng.
463	But he killed no men whenever he rushed in pursuit;
464	for there was no way, alone as he was in the sacred chariot,
465	to attack with the spear and hold in the swift horses.
466	But at last a companion saw him with his eyes,
467	Alcimedon, son of Laerces, son of Haemon;
468	and he stood behind the chariot and spoke to Automedon:
469	“Automedon, which of the gods has put a profitless plan
470	in your breast, and taken away your good wits,
471	that you fight against the Trojans in the front of the throng
472	alone? Your companion has been killed, and Hector
473	wears the armor of the grandson of Aeacus on his own shoulders and glories in it.”
474	Then Automedon, son of Diores, spoke to him in turn:
475	“Alcimedon, which other of the Achaeans is your equal
476	at keeping in hand the taming and the might of immortal horses,
477	except Patroclus, a counselor equal to the gods,
478	while he lived? But now death and fate have overtaken him.
479	But you, take the whip and the glossy reins,
480	and I will get down from the chariot to fight.”
481	So he spoke, and Alcimedon, leaping onto the chariot swift to the rescue,
482	quickly took the whip and the reins in his hands,
483	and Automedon leapt down; and shining Hector saw it,
484	and at once he spoke to Aeneas, who was near:
485	“Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-armored Trojans,
486	I see the two horses of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus here,
487	come out into the battle with poor charioteers;
488	so I would hope to take them, if you in your heart
489	are willing, since if we two rushed at them
490	they would not dare to stand and fight against us face to face in the work of Ares.”
491	So he spoke, and the brave son of Anchises did not disobey.
492	And the two went straight on, their shoulders wrapped in oxhides,
493	dry and tough; and much bronze was beaten over them.
494	And with them went Chromius and godlike Aretus,
495	both of them; and their hearts had high hopes
496	of killing the two men and driving off the horses with high-arched necks,
497	the fools; they were not to come back without bloodshed
498	from Automedon. And he prayed to father Zeus,
499	and his heart, dark on every side, was filled with courage and strength;
500	and at once he spoke to Alcimedon, his trusted companion:
501	“Alcimedon, do not keep the horses far from me,
502	but right behind me, breathing on my back; for I do not think
503	that Hector, son of Priam, will hold back from his fury
504	before he mounts behind the fine-maned horses of Achilles
505	when he has killed us both, and puts to rout the ranks of men
506	of the Argives, or else is himself taken among the foremost.”
507	So saying, he called to the two Ajaxes and Menelaus:
508	“Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Menelaus,
509	leave the body now to the best men,
510	to stand around it and keep off the ranks of men,
511	and from us two, who are alive, keep off the pitiless day;
512	for here they are bearing down through the tearful war,
513	Hector and Aeneas, who are the best of the Trojans.
514	But these things lie on the knees of the gods;
515	for I too will throw, and all this will be the care of Zeus.”
516	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
517	and struck Aretus on his shield, balanced on every side;
518	and it did not stop the spear, but the bronze went on through,
519	and he drove it through the belt into the lower belly.
520	And as when a man in his prime, holding a sharp axe,
521	strikes behind the horns of an ox of the fields
522	and cuts clean through the sinew, and it leaps forward and falls,
523	so he leapt forward and fell on his back; and the spear,
524	very sharp, quivering in his bowels, loosened his limbs.
525	And Hector threw his shining spear at Automedon;
526	but he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear;
527	for he bent forward, and the long spear behind him
528	stuck in the ground, and the butt quivered
529	of the spear; and then mighty Ares let its might go.
530	And now they would have rushed at each other hand to hand with swords,
531	had not the two Ajaxes parted them, eager as they were,
532	who came through the throng at their companion's call;
533	in fear of them they gave ground and went back again,
534	Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius,
535	and they left Aretus there with his heart torn,
536	lying; and Automedon, the equal of swift Ares,
537	stripped off his armor, and spoke in triumph:
538	“Truly, a little at least, for the dead son of Menoetius
539	I have eased my heart of grief, though it is a lesser man I have killed.”
540	So saying, he took the bloody spoils and put them in the chariot,
541	and mounted it himself, his feet and his hands above
542	bloody, like a lion that has devoured a bull.
543	And again over Patroclus the fierce battle was stretched tight,
544	grievous and tearful, and Athena roused the quarrel,
545	coming down from heaven; for far-seeing Zeus had sent her
546	to rouse the Danaans; for his mind had now turned.
547	As when a purple rainbow is stretched out for mortals
548	by Zeus from heaven, to be a portent either of war
549	or of chilling winter, which from their work
550	makes men cease upon the earth, and troubles the flocks,
551	so she, wrapping herself in a purple cloud,
552	entered the crowd of the Achaeans, and roused each man.
553	And first she spoke, urging on the son of Atreus,
554	mighty Menelaus; for he was close to her,
555	in the likeness of Phoenix in form and in tireless voice:
556	“To you, Menelaus, it will be a shame and a reproach
557	if the trusted companion of noble Achilles
558	is torn by swift dogs under the wall of the Trojans.
559	But hold firm, and urge on the whole army.”
560	Then Menelaus, good at the war cry, spoke to her in turn:
561	“Phoenix, old father, aged one, if only Athena
562	would give me strength, and keep off the force of the missiles!
563	Then I would be willing to stand by him and defend
564	Patroclus; for his death has touched my heart deeply.
565	But Hector has the dread might of fire, and he does not cease
566	cutting men down with the bronze; for Zeus grants him glory.”
567	So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena was glad
568	that he had prayed to her first of all the gods.
569	And she put strength into his shoulders and his knees,
570	and put in his breast the daring of a fly,
571	which, however much it is driven from a man's skin,
572	keeps on trying to bite, and human blood is sweet to it;
573	with such daring she filled his heart, dark on every side,
574	and he went to stand over Patroclus, and threw his shining spear.
575	There was among the Trojans Podes, son of Eetion,
576	rich and brave; and Hector honored him most
577	of all the people, since he was his dear companion at the feast;
578	him fair-haired Menelaus struck on the belt
579	as he darted into flight, and drove the bronze straight through;
580	and he fell with a thud; and Menelaus, son of Atreus,
581	dragged the body out from among the Trojans into the crowd of his companions.
582	And Apollo stood close beside Hector and urged him on,
583	like Phaenops, son of Asius, who of all
584	his guest-friends was dearest to him, and had his home at Abydos;
585	in his likeness far-working Apollo spoke to him:
586	“Hector, which other of the Achaeans would still fear you,
587	now that you have shrunk back before Menelaus, who before now at least
588	was a soft spearman? But now he has gone off, lifting alone
589	a body from among the Trojans, and he has killed your trusted companion,
590	brave among the champions, Podes, son of Eetion.”
591	So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him,
592	and he went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze.
593	And then the son of Cronus took up the tasseled aegis,
594	gleaming, and covered Ida in clouds,
595	and flashing his lightning he thundered loudly, and shook it,
596	and gave victory to the Trojans, and put the Achaeans to flight.
597	Peneleos the Boeotian was the first to begin the rout.
598	For he was hit on the shoulder with a spear, always facing forward,
599	a grazing blow on the top; and right to the bone it scored him,
600	the point of Polydamas; for he had come close and hit him.
601	And Hector, coming close, stabbed Leitus on the hand at the wrist,
602	the son of great-hearted Alectryon, and stopped him from battle;
603	and he fell back in fear, glancing around, since he no longer hoped in his heart
604	to hold a spear in his hand and fight with the Trojans.
605	And as Hector rushed after Leitus, Idomeneus
606	hit him on the corselet, on the chest beside the nipple;
607	but the long spear snapped at the socket, and they shouted,
608	the Trojans; and he threw at Idomeneus, son of Deucalion,
609	as he stood in his chariot; and he missed him by a little;
610	but Meriones' squire and charioteer,
611	Coeranus, who had followed him from well-built Lyctus—
612	for at first Idomeneus had left the curving ships and come
613	on foot, and he would have put great power in the Trojans' hands,
614	had not Coeranus quickly driven up the swift-footed horses;
615	and he came as a light to him and kept off the pitiless day,
616	but he himself lost his life at the hands of man-slaying Hector—
617	him he struck under the jaw and the ear, and his teeth
618	the spear pushed out by the roots, and cut through the middle of his tongue.
619	And he fell from the chariot, and let the reins fall to the ground.
620	And Meriones took them up in his own hands,
621	stooping down to the plain, and spoke to Idomeneus:
622	“Whip them on now, until you reach the swift ships;
623	you know yourself that the power is no longer with the Achaeans.”
624	So he spoke, and Idomeneus lashed the fine-maned horses
625	toward the hollow ships; for fear had fallen on his heart.
626	Nor did it escape great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus
627	that Zeus was now giving the Trojans the victory that turns the battle.
628	And among them great Telamonian Ajax began to speak:
629	“Ah, by now even one who is an utter fool
630	would know that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans.
631	For the missiles of all of them strike home, whoever throws,
632	coward or brave man; Zeus guides them all the same;
633	but ours, all of them, fall idly to the ground in vain.
634	But come, let us ourselves consider the best plan,
635	both how we may drag away the body, and how we ourselves
636	may come back and be a joy to our dear companions,
637	who are grieving, I think, as they look this way, and say that no longer
638	will the might and the irresistible hands of man-slaying Hector
639	be held back, but will fall upon the black ships.
640	Would that there were some companion to take the news quickly
641	to the son of Peleus, since I do not think he has even heard
642	the grievous news, that his dear companion has perished.
643	But I can see no such man anywhere among the Achaeans;
644	for they are covered in mist, the men themselves and the horses alike.
645	Father Zeus, rescue the sons of the Achaeans from under the mist,
646	and make clear sky, and grant us to see with our eyes;
647	and in the light destroy us, since that is now your pleasure.”
648	So he spoke, and the father pitied him as he shed tears,
649	and at once he scattered the mist and thrust away the fog,
650	and the sun shone out, and all the battle was seen;
651	and then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war cry:
652	“Look now, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, if you can see
653	Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor, still alive,
654	and urge him to go quickly to wise-hearted Achilles
655	and tell him that by far the dearest of his companions has perished.”
656	So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war cry, did not disobey,
657	and he went, like a lion from a farmyard,
658	when he grows weary of provoking the dogs and the men
659	who do not let him take the fattest of the cattle,
660	keeping watch all night long; and he, craving meat,
661	charges straight on, but gains nothing; for thick the javelins
662	dart against him from bold hands,
663	and blazing torches, which he dreads, eager though he is;
664	and at dawn he goes off far away, with grieving heart;
665	so Menelaus, good at the war cry, went from Patroclus
666	very unwillingly; for he feared greatly that the Achaeans
667	in grievous rout would leave him as a prey to the enemy.
668	And he gave many commands to Meriones and the Ajaxes:
669	“Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Meriones,
670	now let each remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus;
671	for he knew how to be kind to all
672	while he lived; but now death and fate have overtaken him.”
673	So he spoke, and fair-haired Menelaus went away,
674	looking about everywhere like an eagle, which, they say,
675	has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven,
676	and even when he is high up, the hare, swift of foot, does not escape him,
677	crouching under a leafy bush, but he swoops
678	down on it, and quickly seizes it and takes its life.
679	So then, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, your shining eyes
680	roved everywhere through the crowd of your many companions,
681	to see if somewhere they might find the son of Nestor still alive.
682	And very quickly he saw him on the left of all the battle,
683	encouraging his companions and urging them on to fight,
684	and standing near him fair-haired Menelaus spoke:
685	“Antilochus, come here, nurtured by Zeus, so that you may hear
686	grievous news, which I wish had never been.
687	Already, I think, you yourself see
688	and know that a god is rolling a bane upon the Danaans,
689	and victory is the Trojans'; and the best of the Achaeans has been slain,
690	Patroclus, and great longing for him has come upon the Danaans.
691	But run quickly to the ships of the Achaeans, to Achilles,
692	and tell him, in the hope that he may quickly bring the corpse safe to his ship,
693	naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor.”
694	So he spoke, and Antilochus shuddered when he heard the word;
695	and for a long time speechlessness took him, and his two eyes
696	filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked.
697	But even so he did not neglect the command of Menelaus,
698	but set off running, and gave his armor to his noble companion
699	Laodocus, who was wheeling his single-hoofed horses close by.
700	And his feet carried him out of the war, shedding tears,
701	to bring the evil news to Achilles, son of Peleus.
702	Nor did your heart, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, wish
703	to help the hard-pressed companions from whom he had gone,
704	Antilochus, and great longing for him came upon the Pylians;
705	but he sent brilliant Thrasymedes to them,
706	and he himself went back to stand over the hero Patroclus,
707	and came running and stood beside the Ajaxes, and at once he said:
708	“That man I have sent to the swift ships
709	to go to Achilles, swift of foot; but I do not think
710	that he will come now, however angry he is with brilliant Hector;
711	for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans.
712	But let us ourselves consider the best plan,
713	both how we may drag away the body, and how we ourselves
714	may escape death and doom out of the cries of the Trojans.”
715	Then great Telamonian Ajax answered him:
716	“All this you have said fairly, famous Menelaus;
717	but you and Meriones, getting under it very quickly,
718	lift the body and carry it out of the toil; and behind you
719	we two will fight the Trojans and brilliant Hector,
720	with equal spirit, of one name, we who even before now
721	have withstood sharp Ares, standing side by side.”
722	So he spoke, and they took the body up from the ground in their arms
723	and lifted it very high, with a great effort; and the army shouted behind,
724	the Trojan army, when they saw the Achaeans lifting the corpse.
725	And they charged like hounds that at a wild boar
726	that has been hit dart ahead of the young hunters;
727	for a while they run, eager to tear it apart,
728	but when it wheels around among them, trusting in its courage,
729	they draw back and scatter in fear, this way and that;
730	so the Trojans for a while kept following in a mass,
731	stabbing at them with swords and double-pointed spears;
732	but whenever the two Ajaxes turned round
733	and stood against them, their color changed, and no one dared
734	to dart forward and fight for the body.
735	So the two eagerly carried the corpse out of the war
736	toward the hollow ships; and the war was stretched tight over them,
737	savage as fire, which, rushing on a city of men,
738	springs up suddenly and blazes, and the houses dwindle
739	in the great blaze; and the force of the wind roars over it.
740	So the din of horses and of spearmen,
741	ceaseless, came upon them as they went;
742	and they, as mules that have put their strong might into it
743	drag down from the mountain along a rocky path
744	a beam or a great ship's timber; and their hearts
745	are worn down by labor and sweat together as they strain;
746	so the two eagerly carried the corpse. And behind them
747	the two Ajaxes held the enemy back, as a ridge holds back water,
748	a wooded ridge that stretches right across the plain,
749	which holds back even the harsh streams of mighty rivers,
750	and at once sets the course of all of them onto the plain,
751	turning them aside; nor can they break it with their strength as they flow;
752	so the two Ajaxes kept holding back the battle
753	of the Trojans; but they kept following, and two among them most of all,
754	Aeneas, son of Anchises, and shining Hector.
755	And as a cloud of starlings or of jackdaws goes
756	screaming a cry of death, when they see coming
757	a falcon, which brings slaughter to small birds,
758	so before Aeneas and Hector the young men of the Achaeans
759	went screaming a cry of death, and forgot the joy of battle.
760	And much fine armor fell around and about the ditch
761	as the Danaans fled; and there was no respite from the war.
