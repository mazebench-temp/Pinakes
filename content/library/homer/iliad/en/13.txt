# Homer, Iliad, Book 13 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now when Zeus had brought the Trojans and Hector close to the ships,
2	he left them beside the ships to have toil and misery
3	without ceasing, and he himself turned his shining eyes away,
4	looking out far off on the land of the horse-herding Thracians
5	and of the Mysians, who fight hand to hand, and of the proud mare-milkers
6	who live on milk, and of the Abii, the most just of men.
7	And to Troy he no longer turned his shining eyes at all;
8	for he did not expect in his heart that any of the immortals
9	would come to help either the Trojans or the Danaans.
10	But the powerful earth-shaker kept no blind watch;
11	for he too sat marveling at the war and the battle,
12	high up on the topmost peak of wooded Samos,
13	the Thracian; for from there all of Ida could be seen,
14	and the city of Priam could be seen, and the ships of the Achaeans.
15	There he had come up out of the sea and sat, and he pitied the Achaeans
16	as they were beaten down by the Trojans, and he was fiercely indignant with Zeus.
17	At once he came down from the rocky mountain,
18	striding swiftly on his feet; and the high mountains and the woods trembled
19	under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went.
20	Three strides he took, and with the fourth he reached his goal,
21	Aegae, where his glorious house is built in the depths of the sea,
22	golden, gleaming, imperishable forever.
23	There he came and yoked to his chariot his two bronze-footed horses,
24	swift-flying, with flowing manes of gold,
25	and he himself put on gold about his body, and took the whip,
26	golden and well made, and mounted his chariot,
27	and set out to drive over the waves; and the sea beasts frolicked beneath him
28	on every side, out of their lairs, and did not fail to know their lord;
29	and the sea parted for joy; and the horses flew
30	very swiftly, and the bronze axle was not wetted beneath;
31	and the bounding horses carried him to the ships of the Achaeans.
32	There is a wide cave in the depths of the deep sea,
33	between Tenedos and rocky Imbros;
34	there Poseidon the earth-shaker halted his horses,
35	unyoked them from the chariot, and set ambrosial fodder before them
36	to eat; and around their feet he cast golden hobbles,
37	unbreakable, not to be loosed, so that they might stay there steadfast
38	until their lord returned; and he went to the army of the Achaeans.
39	And the Trojans, like a flame or a storm wind, in a body,
40	raging without end, followed Hector, son of Priam,
41	roaring and crying aloud; and they hoped to take the ships of the Achaeans
42	and kill all the best men there beside them.
43	But Poseidon, the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
44	urged on the Argives, coming up out of the deep sea
45	in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice;
46	first he spoke to the two Ajaxes, who were already eager themselves:
47	“Ajaxes, you two will save the army of the Achaeans
48	if you remember your courage, and not chilling rout.
49	For elsewhere I do not fear the irresistible hands
50	of the Trojans, who have climbed over the great wall in a throng;
51	the well-greaved Achaeans will hold them all;
52	but here I am most dreadfully afraid that we may suffer some harm,
53	where that frenzied man leads them, like a flame,
54	Hector, who claims to be the son of mighty Zeus.
55	But for you two, may some god put it in your minds
56	to stand firm yourselves and to urge the others;
57	then you might hold him back from the swift-sailing ships, for all his haste,
58	even if the Olympian himself is rousing him.”
59	He spoke, and with his staff the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
60	struck them both and filled them with strong might,
61	and made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above.
62	And he himself sped off as a swift-winged hawk flies,
63	that rises from a sheer and towering rock,
64	and darts across the plain to chase some other bird;
65	so from them darted Poseidon the earth-shaker.
66	And of the two, swift Ajax, the son of Oileus, was the first to know him,
67	and at once he spoke to Ajax, the son of Telamon:
68	“Ajax, since one of the gods who hold Olympus,
69	in the likeness of the seer, bids us fight beside the ships,
70	this is not Calchas, the prophet and augur;
71	for I easily knew the movements of his feet and legs from behind
72	as he went away; and gods are easy to know.
73	And the heart in my own breast
74	is more eager than before to make war and to fight,
75	and my feet below and my hands above are quivering.”
76	Answering him, Telamonian Ajax said:
77	“So too now my irresistible hands quiver around my spear,
78	and my might has risen, and beneath me both my feet
79	are rushing on; and I long even alone
80	to fight Hector, son of Priam, raging without end.”
81	So they spoke such words to one another,
82	rejoicing in the joy of battle that the god had cast into their hearts;
83	and meanwhile the earth-holder roused the Achaeans in the rear,
84	who were refreshing their hearts beside the swift ships.
85	Their limbs were loosened by grievous weariness,
86	and grief came into their hearts as they watched
87	the Trojans, who had climbed over the great wall in a throng.
88	As they looked at them they let tears fall from beneath their brows;
89	for they thought they would not escape from the evil. But the earth-shaker
90	came easily among them and urged on the strong battalions.
91	First he came to Teucer and Leitus, giving orders,
92	and to the hero Peneleos, and Thoas and Deipyrus,
93	and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war cry;
94	and urging them on he addressed them with winged words:
95	“Shame, Argives, young men! I for my part am confident
96	that if you fight you will save our ships;
97	but if you hold back from wretched war,
98	now indeed the day is in sight when we will be beaten down by the Trojans.
99	Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes,
100	a terrible one, which I used to say would never come to pass:
101	the Trojans advancing against our ships, who before now
102	were like deer quick to flee, which through the forest
103	are the prey of jackals and leopards and wolves,
104	straying aimlessly, without courage, with no joy of battle in them;
105	so before this the Trojans would not await the might and the hands of the Achaeans
106	face to face, not even a little;
107	but now they are fighting far from the city, by the hollow ships,
108	through the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army,
109	who in their strife with him are not willing to defend
110	the swift-sailing ships, but are being killed among them.
111	But even if in very truth the one wholly to blame is
112	the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
113	because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus,
114	for us at least there is no way to hold back from war.
115	Let us rather heal it quickly: the hearts of good men can be healed.
116	But you no longer do well to let go of your furious courage,
117	all of you, the best men in the army. I would not
118	quarrel with a man who held back from war
119	if he were a wretch; but with you I am indignant with all my heart.
120	Weaklings, soon you will make some evil greater
121	by this slackness. But let each of you set in his mind
122	shame and blame; for a great quarrel has arisen.
123	Hector, good at the war cry, is fighting beside the ships
124	in his strength, and he has broken the gates and the long bar.”
125	So with his orders the earth-holder roused the Achaeans.
126	And around the two Ajaxes the battalions took their stand,
127	strong ones, which not even Ares could have made light of if he came among them,
128	nor Athena who rouses armies; for the best men,
129	chosen out, awaited the Trojans and brilliant Hector,
130	fencing spear with spear, shield with overlapping shield;
131	shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man;
132	and the helmets with horsehair crests touched with their bright ridges
133	when the men nodded, so close did they stand to one another;
134	and the spears from their bold hands overlapped
135	as they shook; and the men set their minds straight ahead, and were eager to fight.
136	And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them,
137	rushing straight on, like a rolling boulder from a rock,
138	which a river in winter flood thrusts down from a crest,
139	breaking with its untold rain the props of the shameless rock;
140	and it leaps up high and flies, and the forest crashes
141	beneath it; and it runs on surely and unchecked, until it reaches
142	level ground, and then it rolls no more, for all its haste;
143	so Hector for a while threatened that he would go through to the sea
144	easily, through the huts and ships of the Achaeans,
145	killing; but when he came up against the close-packed battalions,
146	he stood still, pressed hard against them; and the sons of the Achaeans, facing him,
147	stabbing at him with swords and double-pointed spears,
148	pushed him away from them; and he gave ground, reeling.
149	And he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Trojans:
150	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
151	stand by me! The Achaeans will not hold me back for long,
152	though they have arrayed themselves in close order like a tower;
153	no, I think they will give ground before my spear, if truly
154	the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera.”
155	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
156	And among them Deiphobus strode in high spirits,
157	the son of Priam; and he held in front of him his shield, balanced on every side,
158	stepping lightly forward and advancing under cover of his shield.
159	And Meriones took aim at him with his shining spear,
160	and hit him, and did not miss, on his shield, balanced on every side,
161	made of bull's hide; but he did not drive through it, and long before
162	the long spear snapped at the socket. And Deiphobus
163	held the shield of bull's hide away from him, and feared in his heart
164	the spear of wise-hearted Meriones. But the hero
165	drew back into the crowd of his companions, and was terribly enraged
166	on both counts, for the victory and for the spear that he had shattered.
167	He went along the huts and ships of the Achaeans
168	to bring a long spear, which he had left behind in his hut.
169	And the others fought on, and an unquenchable cry went up.
170	And Teucer, son of Telamon, was the first to kill his man,
171	Imbrius the spearman, son of Mentor, rich in horses;
172	he had lived in Pedaeum before the sons of the Achaeans came,
173	and had as his wife a bastard daughter of Priam, Medesicaste;
174	but when the curving ships of the Danaans came,
175	he came back to Ilios, and stood out among the Trojans,
176	and lived beside Priam; and Priam honored him as he honored his children.
177	Him the son of Telamon, below the ear, with his long spear
178	stabbed, and drew the spear out; and he fell like an ash tree
179	that on the peak of a mountain seen from afar on every side
180	is cut by the bronze and brings its tender leaves to the ground;
181	so he fell, and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze.
182	And Teucer rushed forward, eager to strip off his armor;
183	but as he rushed, Hector threw his shining spear at him.
184	But he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear
185	narrowly; and Hector struck Amphimachus, son of Cteatus, of Actor's line,
186	as he was going into the war, in the chest with his spear;
187	and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
188	And Hector rushed to snatch the helmet, fitted close to the temples,
189	from the head of great-hearted Amphimachus;
190	but as he rushed, Ajax reached out with his shining spear
191	at Hector; but none of his skin showed, and all of him was covered
192	in terrible bronze; but Ajax stabbed the boss of his shield
193	and pushed him with great strength; and he gave ground backward
194	from both the dead, and the Achaeans dragged them away.
195	Amphimachus, then, Stichius and brilliant Menestheus,
196	the leaders of the Athenians, carried back to the army of the Achaeans;
197	and Imbrius the two Ajaxes, eager with furious courage, carried off.
198	As two lions snatch a goat away from sharp-toothed dogs
199	and carry it through the thick brushwood,
200	high above the ground, holding it in their jaws,
201	so the two Ajaxes, helmeted men, held him high
202	and stripped his armor; and his head from his tender neck
203	the son of Oileus cut off, in his anger for Amphimachus,
204	and with a swing he sent it flying like a ball through the throng;
205	and it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet.
206	And then indeed Poseidon was angry with all his heart
207	for his grandson, fallen in grim combat,
208	and he went along the huts and ships of the Achaeans
209	to urge on the Danaans, and he was making sorrows for the Trojans.
210	And Idomeneus, famed for his spear, met him
211	as he came from a companion who had just come to him from the war,
212	struck behind the knee by the sharp bronze.
213	His companions had carried him off, and Idomeneus, having given orders to the healers,
214	was going to his hut; for he was still eager to meet
215	the war. And the powerful earth-shaker spoke to him,
216	making his voice like that of Thoas, son of Andraemon,
217	who in all Pleuron and steep Calydon
218	was lord of the Aetolians, and was honored by the people like a god:
219	“Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have your threats gone,
220	the threats the sons of the Achaeans used to make against the Trojans?”
221	Then Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him face to face:
222	“Thoas, no man now is to blame, as far as I
223	know; for all of us know how to make war.
224	No heartless fear has hold of anyone, nor does anyone, giving way
225	to shrinking, slip out of evil war; but so, I think,
226	it must be the pleasure of the son of Cronus, supreme in might,
227	that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos.
228	But, Thoas, since even before this you were staunch against the enemy,
229	and you urge on another man too, wherever you see him holding back,
230	so now do not give up, and call on every man.”
231	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered him:
232	“Idomeneus, may that man never return home
233	from Troy, but stay here and become a plaything for dogs,
234	whoever on this day of his own will holds back from fighting.
235	But come, take your armor and come with me; we must hurry at this together,
236	if we may be of some use, though we are only two.
237	Even the prowess of very wretched men counts when it is joined,
238	and we two would know how to fight even against brave men.”
239	So saying, the god went back again into the toil of men;
240	and when Idomeneus came to his well-made hut,
241	he put on his fine armor about his body, and took two spears,
242	and went on his way like the lightning that the son of Cronus
243	takes in his hand and brandishes from gleaming Olympus,
244	showing a sign to mortals; and its flashes are plain for all to see;
245	so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran.
246	And Meriones, his good attendant, met him
247	still near the hut; for he was going after a bronze spear
248	to bring it back; and mighty Idomeneus spoke to him:
249	“Meriones, son of Molus, swift of foot, dearest of my companions,
250	why have you come here, leaving the war and the combat?
251	Have you been hit, and is the point of a missile wearing you down,
252	or have you come to me with some message? I myself have no desire
253	to sit in the huts, but to fight.”
254	And wise Meriones answered him face to face:
255	“Idomeneus, counselor of the bronze-armored Cretans,
256	I am going to see if a spear is left for you in the huts,
257	to bring it; for the one I had before I have shattered
258	striking the shield of overweening Deiphobus.”
259	Then Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him face to face:
260	“Spears, if you want them, you will find, one or twenty,
261	standing in my hut against the gleaming walls,
262	Trojan spears, which I take from the men I kill; for I do not mean
263	to fight standing far off from hostile men.
264	So I have spears and bossed shields
265	and helmets and corselets shining brightly.”
266	And wise Meriones answered him face to face:
267	“I too, beside my hut and my black ship,
268	have many spoils of the Trojans; but they are not near enough for me to take one.
269	For I tell you that I too have not forgotten my courage,
270	but among the foremost, in the battle where men win glory,
271	I take my stand, whenever the quarrel of war arises.
272	Some other of the bronze-armored Achaeans, perhaps,
273	may not notice me fighting, but you yourself, I think, know it.”
274	Then Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him face to face:
275	“I know what you are in prowess; why do you need to tell of it?
276	For if now all the best of us were being chosen beside the ships
277	for an ambush, where most of all the prowess of men is seen clearly,
278	where the coward and the valiant man are shown for what they are—
279	for the coward's color changes this way and that,
280	and the heart in his breast cannot be held to sitting still,
281	but he keeps shifting his crouch and sits back on both his feet,
282	and his heart pounds loudly against his chest
283	as he foresees the fates, and his teeth chatter;
284	but the brave man's color does not change, nor is he much
285	afraid, once he first takes his place in the ambush of men,
286	but he prays to close with them quickly in grim battle—
287	even there no one could make light of your might and your hands.
288	For if you were hit as you toiled, or struck,
289	the missile would not fall on the back of your neck, nor on your back,
290	but it would meet your chest or your belly
291	as you pressed forward to the intimate talk of the champions.
292	But come, let us no longer talk like this, like children,
293	standing here, lest someone be overbearingly indignant;
294	but go to the hut and take a heavy spear.”
295	So he spoke, and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,
296	quickly took up a bronze spear from the hut
297	and he went after Idomeneus, much intent on war.
298	And as Ares, ruin of mortals, goes into war,
299	and Rout, his dear son, strong and fearless, follows with him,
300	who routs even a warrior of enduring heart;
301	these two arm themselves to go out from Thrace to the Ephyri
302	or to the great-hearted Phlegyes; and they do not
303	listen to both sides, but give glory to one or the other;
304	like them, Meriones and Idomeneus, leaders of men,
305	went into the war, armored in gleaming bronze.
306	And Meriones was the first to speak to him:
307	“Son of Deucalion, where are you eager to enter the throng?
308	On the right of the whole army, or through the middle,
309	or on the left? For nowhere else, I think,
310	are the long-haired Achaeans so lacking in war.”
311	Then Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him face to face:
312	“In the middle of the ships there are others to defend them,
313	the two Ajaxes and Teucer, who is the best of the Achaeans
314	at archery, and good also in close battle;
315	they will give him his fill of it, for all his eagerness for war,
316	Hector, son of Priam, even if he is very strong.
317	It will be a steep climb for him, for all his eagerness to fight,
318	to overcome their might and their irresistible hands
319	and burn the ships, unless the son of Cronus himself
320	casts a blazing firebrand on the swift ships.
321	But to no man would great Telamonian Ajax give way,
322	a man who was mortal and ate the grain of Demeter
323	and could be broken by bronze and by great stones.
324	He would not give ground even to Achilles, breaker of men,
325	in the standing fight at least; but in speed no one can contend with him.
326	But steer us this way, to the left of the army, so that as quickly as may be
327	we may see whether we will hold out triumph to another, or another to us.”
328	So he spoke, and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,
329	led the way, until they came to the part of the army where Idomeneus bade him.
330	And when the Trojans saw Idomeneus, like a flame in his courage,
331	himself and his attendant, in their intricate armor,
332	calling to one another through the throng they all came at him.
333	And the quarrel stood even among them at the sterns of the ships.
334	As when storms rush on before shrill winds
335	on a day when the dust lies thickest on the roads,
336	and they raise together a great mist of dust,
337	so their battle came together, and they were eager in their hearts
338	to slay one another through the throng with the sharp bronze.
339	And the battle, destroyer of mortals, bristled with the spears,
340	long and flesh-cutting, that they held; and eyes were blinded
341	by the gleam of bronze from the shining helmets
342	and the newly burnished corselets and the bright shields
343	as they came on together; he would be very bold of heart
344	who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved.
345	And the two mighty sons of Cronus, with their minds set on different sides,
346	were making grievous sorrows for the heroes.
347	Zeus wished for victory for the Trojans and for Hector,
348	to give glory to Achilles, swift of foot; but he did not at all
349	wish the Achaean army to perish before Ilios,
350	but was giving glory to Thetis and her stout-hearted son.
351	But Poseidon went among the Argives and spurred them on,
352	having risen secretly out of the gray sea; for he was vexed
353	as they were beaten down by the Trojans, and he was fiercely indignant with Zeus.
354	Both indeed were of one race and of one father's line,
355	but Zeus was born first and knew more.
356	Therefore he shrank from giving help openly,
357	but secretly he kept rousing the army, in the likeness of a man.
358	And the two of them took the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all,
359	and knotting it crosswise they stretched it tight over both sides,
360	unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many.
361	Then, though he was half gray, calling on the Danaans,
362	Idomeneus leapt among the Trojans and roused rout among them.
363	For he killed Othryoneus, from Cabesus, who was within the city,
364	who had come not long before at the report of the war,
365	and he asked for the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam,
366	Cassandra, without bride-price, and promised a great deed:
367	to drive the sons of the Achaeans from Troy against their will.
368	And the old man Priam promised him and nodded his assent
369	to give her; and he fought, trusting in the promises.
370	And Idomeneus took aim at him with his shining spear,
371	and struck him squarely as he came striding high; and the bronze corselet
372	that he wore did not protect him, and he fixed it in the middle of his belly.
373	And he fell with a thud; and Idomeneus boasted over him and spoke:
374	“Othryoneus, I praise you above all mortals,
375	if you will truly accomplish everything you promised
376	to Priam, descendant of Dardanus; and he promised you his daughter.
377	And we too would promise you this and fulfill it:
378	we would give you the most beautiful in form of the daughters of the son of Atreus,
379	bringing her from Argos, to marry her, if with us
380	you would sack the well-peopled citadel of Ilios.
381	But follow me, so that at the seafaring ships we may come to terms
382	about the marriage; for we are not stingy with bride-gifts.”
383	So saying, he dragged him by the foot through the fierce battle,
384	the hero Idomeneus; but Asius came to his defense,
385	on foot in front of his horses; and they, breathing on his shoulders,
386	were held always close by the charioteer, his attendant; and he was eager in his heart
387	to hit Idomeneus; but Idomeneus was quicker and struck him with his spear
388	in the throat beneath the chin, and drove the bronze straight through.
389	And he fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar,
390	or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders
391	cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber;
392	so he lay stretched out in front of his horses and chariot,
393	roaring, clutching at the bloody dust.
394	And his charioteer was struck out of the wits he had before,
395	and he did not dare to escape from the hands of the enemy
396	by turning the horses back; and Antilochus, steadfast in battle,
397	pierced him squarely through the middle with his spear; and the bronze corselet
398	that he wore did not protect him, and he fixed it in the middle of his belly.
399	And gasping he fell from the well-made chariot,
400	and as for the horses, Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor,
401	drove them away from the Trojans to the well-greaved Achaeans.
402	And Deiphobus came very close to Idomeneus,
403	grieving for Asius, and threw his shining spear.
404	But he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear,
405	Idomeneus; for he was hidden beneath his shield, balanced on every side,
406	which he carried, made of oxhides and gleaming bronze,
407	rounded, fitted with two rods;
408	under it he crouched entirely, and the bronze spear flew over,
409	and the shield gave a dry ring as it was grazed
410	by the spear. Yet Deiphobus did not let it go in vain from his heavy hand,
411	but struck Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, shepherd of the people,
412	in the liver beneath the midriff, and at once loosened his knees.
413	And Deiphobus boasted terribly, shouting aloud:
414	“Asius does not lie unavenged after all; no, I tell you,
415	even as he goes to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper,
416	he will be glad in his heart, since I have given him an escort.”
417	So he spoke, and grief came upon the Argives at his boasting,
418	and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Antilochus;
419	but even in his grief he did not neglect his companion,
420	but ran and stood over him, and covered him with his shield.
421	Then two trusty companions stooped under him,
422	Mecisteus, son of Echius, and brilliant Alastor,
423	and carried him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.
424	And Idomeneus did not let his great might slacken, but was eager always
425	either to cover some one of the Trojans in murky night
426	or himself to fall with a thud, warding off ruin from the Achaeans.
427	Then the dear son of Aesyetes, whom Zeus had nurtured,
428	the hero Alcathous—he was the son-in-law of Anchises,
429	and had married the eldest of his daughters, Hippodameia,
430	whom her father and lady mother loved with all their hearts
431	in their hall; for she surpassed all the girls of her age
432	in beauty and handiwork and wits; and so
433	the best man in wide Troy had married her—
434	him then Poseidon beat down at the hands of Idomeneus,
435	bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs;
436	for he could neither escape backward nor swerve aside,
437	but like a pillar or a tree with high foliage,
438	as he stood motionless, he stabbed him in the middle of the chest with his spear,
439	the hero Idomeneus, and broke the tunic around him,
440	of bronze, which before had kept destruction from his body;
441	and then it rang dry as it was torn about the spear.
442	And he fell with a thud, and the spear was fixed in his heart,
443	and the heart, still quivering, shook even the butt
444	of the spear; and then mighty Ares let its might go.
445	And Idomeneus boasted terribly, shouting aloud:
446	“Deiphobus, shall we then think it a fair exchange,
447	three killed for one, since you boast like this?
448	Strange one, come and stand against me yourself,
449	so that you may see what kind of offspring of Zeus I am, who have come here.
450	Zeus first fathered Minos, a guardian over Crete;
451	and Minos in turn fathered a son, noble Deucalion,
452	and Deucalion fathered me, a lord over many men
453	in wide Crete; and now the ships have brought me here
454	to be an evil to you and to your father and to the other Trojans.”
455	So he spoke, and Deiphobus was torn two ways in thought,
456	whether to take as his companion one of the great-hearted Trojans,
457	drawing back, or to try it even alone.
458	And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way:
459	to go for Aeneas; and he found him at the very back of the throng,
460	standing there; for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam,
461	because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all.
462	And standing near him he addressed him with winged words:
463	“Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans, now you must indeed
464	defend your brother-in-law, if any grief for your kin touches you.
465	But follow me; let us defend Alcathous, who in former days,
466	being your brother-in-law, reared you in his house when you were small;
467	and Idomeneus, famed for his spear, has slain him and stripped him of his armor.”
468	So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast;
469	and he went after Idomeneus, much intent on war.
470	But flight did not seize Idomeneus as if he were a darling child,
471	but he stood his ground like a boar in the mountains, trusting in its courage,
472	which awaits a great din of men coming on
473	in a lonely place, and bristles up along its back;
474	and its two eyes blaze with fire; and its tusks
475	it whets, eager to ward off dogs and men;
476	so Idomeneus, famed for his spear, stood his ground, and did not give way
477	as Aeneas came on to help; and he called to his companions,
478	looking to Ascalaphus and Aphareus and Deipyrus
479	and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war cry;
480	and urging them on he addressed them with winged words:
481	“Come here, friends, and defend me; I am alone, and terribly afraid
482	of Aeneas, swift of foot, coming on, who is attacking me,
483	who is very strong at slaying men in battle;
484	and he has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power.
485	For if we were of the same age, with the spirit we have now,
486	soon either he would carry off the great mastery, or I would.”
487	So he spoke, and they all, with one spirit in their hearts,
488	stood close beside him, leaning their shields against their shoulders.
489	And Aeneas on the other side called to his companions,
490	looking to Deiphobus and Paris and brilliant Agenor,
491	who were leaders of the Trojans with him; and then
492	the army followed, as sheep follow the ram
493	from the pasture to drink, and the shepherd is glad at heart;
494	so the heart of Aeneas rejoiced in his breast
495	when he saw the crowd of the army following him.
496	And around Alcathous they rushed at one another hand to hand
497	with their long spears; and around their chests the bronze
498	rang terribly as they took aim at one another through the throng;
499	and two warlike men beyond the others,
500	Aeneas and Idomeneus, the equals of Ares,
501	were eager to cut each other's flesh with the pitiless bronze.
502	Aeneas was the first to throw at Idomeneus;
503	but he, looking straight at it, avoided the bronze spear,
504	and Aeneas' spear went quivering into the earth,
505	since it had sped in vain from his strong hand.
506	But Idomeneus struck Oenomaus in the middle of the belly,
507	and broke the plate of his corselet, and the bronze let out
508	his bowels; and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm.
509	As for Idomeneus, his long-shadowed spear from the corpse
510	he drew out; but he could not take the rest of the beautiful armor
511	from his shoulders; for he was hard pressed by missiles.
512	For his legs were no longer steady when he rushed,
513	to dart after his own spear or to avoid a missile.
514	So in close fighting he kept off the pitiless day,
515	but his feet no longer carried him nimbly to flee out of the war.
516	And as he went step by step away, he threw his shining spear at him,
517	Deiphobus; for he had always kept a steady grudge against him.
518	But he missed again, and struck Ascalaphus with his spear,
519	the son of Enyalius; and the heavy spear went through his shoulder;
520	and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm.
521	And loud-voiced, mighty Ares had not yet heard at all
522	that his son had fallen in the fierce battle,
523	but he was sitting on the peak of Olympus beneath golden clouds,
524	held back by the counsels of Zeus, where the other
525	immortal gods were also, shut out from the war.
526	And around Ascalaphus they rushed at one another hand to hand;
527	Deiphobus snatched the shining helmet from Ascalaphus,
528	but Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,
529	leapt on him and struck his arm with his spear, and from his hand
530	the helmet with its socket for the plume fell to the ground with a clang.
531	And Meriones, leaping in again like a vulture,
532	pulled the heavy spear out from the top of his arm,
533	and drew back into the crowd of his companions. And Polites,
534	his own brother, put his arms around his waist
535	and led him out of grim-sounding war, until he came to his horses,
536	his swift horses, which were standing behind the battle and the war
537	with their charioteer and the inlaid chariot;
538	and they carried him toward the city, groaning heavily
539	in his distress; and the blood ran down from his freshly wounded arm.
540	And the others fought on, and an unquenchable cry went up.
541	Then Aeneas sprang at Aphareus, son of Caletor,
542	and struck him in the throat as he turned toward him, with his sharp spear;
543	and his head sank to one side, and his shield fell with it,
544	and his helmet, and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him.
545	And Antilochus, watching for Thoon as he turned around,
546	stabbed him with a rush, and cut through the whole vein
547	that runs right up the back and reaches the neck;
548	he cut it through entirely; and he fell on his back in the dust,
549	stretching out both his hands to his dear companions.
550	And Antilochus sprang on him and began to strip the armor from his shoulders,
551	looking about him; and the Trojans, standing around, one from here, one from there,
552	stabbed at his broad, gleaming shield, but they could not
553	get through to graze the tender flesh with the pitiless bronze
554	of Antilochus; for Poseidon the earth-shaker
555	guarded the son of Nestor all around, even among many missiles.
556	For he was never away from the enemy, but among them
557	he kept turning; nor did his spear keep still, but always
558	it was shaken and whirled about; and he took aim in his mind
559	either to throw at someone, or to rush at him close at hand.
560	But as he aimed through the throng he did not escape the notice of Adamas,
561	son of Asius, who stabbed the middle of his shield with the sharp bronze,
562	rushing at him from close by; but his spear-point was made powerless
563	by dark-haired Poseidon, who begrudged him the other's life.
564	And part of it stayed there like a fire-hardened stake
565	in the shield of Antilochus, and the other half lay on the ground;
566	and he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom.
567	But Meriones followed him as he went away and struck him with his spear
568	between the genitals and the navel, where most of all
569	Ares is painful for miserable mortals.
570	There he fixed the spear; and he, following it, bent around the spear
571	and writhed, as an ox writhes that herdsmen in the mountains
572	have bound by force with twisted ropes and lead along against its will;
573	so, struck, he writhed for a little while, not long at all,
574	until he came close and pulled the spear out of his body,
575	the hero Meriones; and darkness covered his eyes.
576	And Helenus struck Deipyrus close at hand on the temple with a sword,
577	a great Thracian sword, and struck off his helmet.
578	It glanced away and fell to the ground, and one of the Achaeans
579	picked it up as it rolled among the feet of the fighters;
580	and murky night covered his eyes.
581	And grief seized the son of Atreus, Menelaus, good at the war cry;
582	and he went at Helenus, the hero and lord, with threats,
583	brandishing his sharp spear; and the other drew back his bow by the grip.
584	And at the same moment the one was eager to throw his sharp spear,
585	and the other to shoot an arrow from the string.
586	Then the son of Priam struck him in the chest with an arrow,
587	on the plate of his corselet, and the bitter arrow flew off.
588	As from a broad winnowing shovel across a great threshing floor
589	dark-skinned beans leap, or chickpeas,
590	under the shrill wind and the swing of the winnower,
591	so from the corselet of glorious Menelaus
592	the bitter arrow glanced far away and flew off.
593	And the son of Atreus, Menelaus, good at the war cry, struck
594	the hand that held the polished bow; and into the bow
595	the bronze spear was driven right through the hand.
596	And he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom,
597	with his hand hanging at his side; and the ash spear trailed along with it.
598	And great-hearted Agenor drew it out of his hand,
599	and bound the hand up with well-twisted sheep's wool,
600	a sling, which an attendant carried for him, the shepherd of the people.
601	And Peisander went straight for glorious Menelaus;
602	and an evil fate was leading him toward the end of death,
603	to be beaten down by you, Menelaus, in grim combat.
604	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
605	the son of Atreus missed, and his spear turned aside,
606	and Peisander stabbed the shield of glorious Menelaus,
607	but could not drive the bronze right through;
608	for the broad shield held it, and the spear broke at the socket;
609	and he rejoiced in his heart and hoped for victory.
610	Then the son of Atreus drew his silver-studded sword
611	and leapt at Peisander; but he, from under his shield, took a fine
612	axe of good bronze, on a handle of olive wood,
613	long and well polished; and they reached each other at the same moment.
614	Peisander struck the ridge of his horsehair-crested helmet,
615	up under the crest itself; and Menelaus struck him on the forehead as he came on,
616	above the base of the nose; and the bones cracked, and his two eyes
617	fell bloody at his feet on the ground in the dust,
618	and he doubled over as he fell; and Menelaus set his heel on his chest
619	and stripped off his armor, and spoke in triumph:
620	“So, I think, you will leave the ships of the Danaans of the swift horses,
621	you overbearing Trojans, insatiable of the dread war cry,
622	and you are not lacking in the other insult and shame
623	with which you insulted me, you evil dogs, and in your hearts you did not at all
624	fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud,
625	god of guests, who will one day destroy your steep city;
626	you who carried off my wedded wife and many possessions
627	wantonly, after you had been welcomed by her;
628	and now again you are eager, among the seafaring ships,
629	to throw deadly fire, and to kill the Achaean heroes.
630	But somewhere you will be held back, eager though you are for Ares.
631	Father Zeus, they say that you surpass in wisdom all others,
632	men and gods; and all these things come from you.
633	How you favor men who are arrogant,
634	the Trojans, whose might is always reckless, and who cannot
635	have their fill of the din of battle, of war common to all.
636	Of all things there is satiety, of sleep and of love,
637	of sweet song and of flawless dancing,
638	of which a man would rather have his fill
639	than of war; but the Trojans are insatiable of battle.”
640	So saying, he stripped the bloody armor from his body,
641	noble Menelaus, and gave it to his companions,
642	and he himself went back again and mingled with the champions.
643	There the son of king Pylaemenes leapt at him,
644	Harpalion, who had followed his dear father to make war
645	at Troy, and did not come back again to his native land;
646	he then stabbed the middle of the shield of the son of Atreus with his spear
647	from close by, but could not drive the bronze right through;
648	and he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom,
649	glancing all around, lest someone graze his flesh with the bronze.
650	But as he went away, Meriones let fly a bronze-fitted arrow,
651	and struck him in the right buttock; and the arrow
652	went straight through, under the bone and into the bladder.
653	And sitting down there in the arms of his dear companions,
654	breathing out his life, like a worm on the ground
655	he lay stretched out; and the dark blood flowed and soaked the earth.
656	The great-hearted Paphlagonians tended him,
657	and lifting him into a chariot they took him to sacred Ilios,
658	grieving; and his father went with them, letting tears fall,
659	and no penalty was paid for his dead son.
660	And Paris was very angry at heart over his killing;
661	for he had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians;
662	and in his anger for him he let fly a bronze-fitted arrow.
663	There was one Euchenor, son of Polyidus the seer,
664	rich and brave, who lived in his house at Corinth,
665	who had gone on board his ship knowing well his deadly fate;
666	for often the good old man Polyidus had told him
667	that he would perish of a grievous sickness in his own halls,
668	or be beaten down by the Trojans among the ships of the Achaeans;
669	so he avoided at once the grievous fine of the Achaeans
670	and the hateful sickness, so that he would not suffer sorrows in his heart.
671	Him Paris struck under the jaw and the ear; and quickly his spirit
672	went from his limbs, and hateful darkness seized him.
673	So they fought on like blazing fire;
674	but Hector, dear to Zeus, had not heard, and did not know at all

678	urge on the Argives, and himself defended them with his strength;
679	but Hector held on where he had first leapt in at the gates and the wall,

681	where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were,

686	the Locrians and the Phthians and the shining Epeians,

694	One was the bastard son of divine Oileus,

710	men, his companions, who would take over his shield

726	“Hector, there is no dealing with you, to make you heed persuasion.
