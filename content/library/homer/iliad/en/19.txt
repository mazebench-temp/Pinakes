# Homer, Iliad, Book 19 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now saffron-robed Dawn rose from the streams of Oceanus
2	to bring light to the immortals and to mortals;
3	and she came to the ships, bringing the gifts from the god.
4	She found her dear son lying with his arms around Patroclus,
5	weeping loudly; and many of his companions around him
6	were weeping; and she stood among them, brilliant among goddesses,
7	and she clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
8	“My child, this man we will let lie, for all our grief,
9	since he was brought down once and for all by the will of the gods;
10	but you, receive this glorious armor from Hephaestus,
11	very fine, such as no man has ever yet worn on his shoulders.”
12	So she spoke, and the goddess set the armor down
13	before Achilles; and all its intricate designs rang.
14	And trembling seized all the Myrmidons, and no one dared
15	look at it face to face, but they fled in fear. But Achilles,
16	as he saw it, anger entered him the more, and his eyes
17	shone out terribly beneath his lids, like a blaze;
18	and he delighted in holding in his hands the splendid gifts of the god.
19	But when he had taken his fill of delight in his heart, gazing on the intricate designs,
20	at once he addressed his mother with winged words:
21	“My mother, the armor the god has given is such as is fitting
22	for the works of immortals, and no mortal man could make it.
23	Now I will arm myself; but terribly
24	I fear that in the meantime into the valiant son of Menoetius
25	flies will make their way, through the wounds the bronze has struck,
26	and breed worms, and defile the body—
27	the life is slain out of it—and all his flesh will rot.”
28	Then the goddess silver-footed Thetis answered him:
29	“My child, do not let these things trouble your mind.
30	From him I will try to ward off the savage tribes,
31	the flies, which feed on men slain in battle;
32	for even if he lies here for a full year's round,
33	his flesh will always be sound, or even better.
34	But you, call the Achaean heroes to an assembly,
35	renouncing your wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
36	and arm yourself at once for the war, and put on your courage.”
37	So she spoke, and put in him a might full of daring,
38	and into Patroclus in turn ambrosia and red nectar
39	she dripped through his nostrils, so that his flesh might be sound.
40	But brilliant Achilles went along the shore of the sea,
41	shouting terribly, and he roused the Achaean heroes.
42	And even those who before had always stayed in the gathering of the ships,
43	who were helmsmen and held the steering oars of the ships,
44	and were stewards beside the ships, dealing out the food,
45	even they came to the assembly then, because Achilles
46	had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle.
47	And the two attendants of Ares came limping,
48	the son of Tydeus, steadfast in battle, and brilliant Odysseus,
49	leaning on their spears; for they still had grievous wounds;
50	and they came and sat down in the front of the assembly.
51	And last of all came Agamemnon, lord of men,
52	with his wound; for him too in the fierce battle
53	Coon, son of Antenor, had stabbed with his bronze-fitted spear.
54	And when all the Achaeans were gathered together,
55	swift-footed Achilles stood up among them and spoke:
56	“Son of Atreus, was this then better for us both,
57	for you and for me, that we two, grieving at heart,
58	raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl?
59	I wish Artemis had killed her with an arrow among the ships
60	on the day when I took her, when I destroyed Lyrnessus;
61	then not so many Achaeans would have bitten the boundless ground with their teeth
62	at the hands of their enemies, while I nursed my wrath apart.
63	For Hector and the Trojans that was the better thing; but the Achaeans,
64	I think, will long remember my strife and yours.
65	But we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief,
66	taming the dear heart in our breasts under compulsion.
67	Now I put a stop to my anger; there is no need for me
68	to rage stubbornly forever; but come, quickly
69	urge the long-haired Achaeans on to war,
70	so that I may go against the Trojans once more and make trial of them,
71	whether they will want to spend the night by the ships; but I think
72	many a man among them will be glad to bend his knees and rest, who escapes
73	from the destructive war, from under our spear.”
74	So he spoke, and the well-greaved Achaeans rejoiced
75	that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath.
76	And among them spoke also Agamemnon, lord of men,
77	from where he sat, and not standing up in their midst:
78	“Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares,
79	it is good to listen to a man when he stands up to speak, and it is not fitting
80	to interrupt; for that is hard even for one who is skilled.
81	But in the great din of men how could anyone listen
82	or speak? He is hindered, clear-voiced orator though he is.
83	To the son of Peleus I will open my mind; but you others,
84	Argives, take heed, and each of you mark my word well.
85	Often the Achaeans have spoken this word to me
86	and kept rebuking me; but I am not to blame,
87	but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness,
88	who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind
89	on the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles.
90	But what could I do? It is a god who brings all things to fulfillment.
91	Blind Folly is the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds all,
92	ruinous; her feet are tender, for she does not step
93	upon the ground, but walks over the heads of men,
94	doing harm to men; and she fetters one or the other.
95	For once even Zeus was blinded, who, they say, is the best
96	of men and gods; but even him
97	Hera, female though she was, deceived with her treachery
98	on the day when mighty Heracles
99	was to be born to Alcmene in well-crowned Thebes.
100	He spoke out, boasting among all the gods:
101	‘Hear me, all you gods and all you goddesses,
102	so that I may say what the heart in my breast bids me.
103	Today Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, will bring into the light
104	a man who will be lord over all who dwell around him,
105	of the lineage of those men who are of my blood.’
106	And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed him:
107	‘You will prove false, and hereafter will not put fulfillment to your word.
108	But come now, Olympian, swear me a mighty oath
109	that he truly will be lord over all who dwell around him,
110	whoever on this day falls between a woman's feet,
111	of the men who are sprung from the blood of your stock.’
112	So she spoke; and Zeus did not perceive her treachery at all,
113	but swore a great oath, and in this was greatly blinded.
114	And Hera darted down and left the peak of Olympus,
115	and swiftly came to Achaean Argos, where she knew
116	the strong wife of Sthenelus, son of Perseus.
117	She was carrying her dear son, and the seventh month had come;
118	and she brought him out into the light, though he was short of his months,
119	and she stopped Alcmene's childbirth, and held back the Eileithyiai.
120	And she herself, bringing the news, addressed Zeus, son of Cronus:
121	‘Father Zeus of the gleaming thunderbolt, I will put a word in your mind:
122	now a brave man is born who will be lord of the Argives,
123	Eurystheus, child of Sthenelus the son of Perseus,
124	of your race; it is not unseemly for him to be lord of the Argives.’
125	So she spoke, and sharp grief struck him deep in his heart;
126	and at once he seized Blind Folly by her head of shining tresses,
127	angered in his heart, and swore a mighty oath
128	that never to Olympus and the starry sky
129	would Blind Folly come again, who blinds all.
130	So saying, he hurled her from the starry sky,
131	whirling her round in his hand; and soon she came to the works of men.
132	Over her he always groaned whenever he saw his dear son
133	doing unseemly work under the labors that Eurystheus set him.
134	So I too, when in turn great Hector of the gleaming helmet
135	was killing the Argives at the sterns of the ships,
136	could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded.
137	But since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits,
138	I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom.
139	But rouse yourself to war, and rouse the rest of the army.
140	And here am I to provide all the gifts that, coming to you
141	yesterday in your huts, brilliant Odysseus promised.
142	But if you wish, wait, eager though you are for Ares,
143	and attendants will take the gifts from my ship
144	and bring them, so that you may see that what I give will satisfy your heart.”
145	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
146	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
147	the gifts are yours, to give them if you wish, as is fitting,
148	or to keep them by you. But now let us remember the joy of battle
149	at once; for we must not waste time in talk here
150	nor delay; for a great work is still undone;
151	so that once again a man may see Achilles among the foremost
152	destroying the battalions of the Trojans with his bronze spear.
153	So let each of you remember this and fight his man.”
154	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
155	“Not like this, godlike Achilles, brave as you are:
156	do not urge the sons of the Achaeans to Ilios fasting
157	to fight with the Trojans, since it will be no short time
158	that the din of battle lasts, once the battalions
159	of men first engage, and a god breathes might into both sides.
160	But bid the Achaeans eat by the swift ships
161	of food and wine; for in that is might and courage.
162	For no man all day long, until the sun goes down,
163	will be able to fight face to face, going without food;
164	for even if in his heart he is eager to make war,
165	yet his limbs grow heavy unawares, and thirst and hunger
166	overtake him, and his knees are hindered as he goes.
167	But the man who has had his fill of wine and food
168	and fights all day long against hostile men—
169	the heart in his breast is bold, and his limbs
170	do not grow weary until all have held back from the war.
171	But come, dismiss the army, and bid them make ready
172	their meal; and let Agamemnon, lord of men, bring the gifts
173	into the middle of the assembly, so that all the Achaeans
174	may see them with their eyes, and you may be warmed in your heart.
175	And let him stand up among the Argives and swear you an oath
176	that he never went up into her bed or lay with her,
177	as is the custom, my lord, of men and of women;
178	and let the heart in your breast be gracious too.
179	And then let him make amends to you with a rich feast in his huts,
180	so that you may have no lack of justice.
181	And you, son of Atreus, will be more just hereafter
182	toward another too. For it is no cause for blame that a king
183	should make amends to a man, when he was the first to grow angry.”
184	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to him in turn:
185	“I rejoice, son of Laertes, to hear your word;
186	for you have gone through all of it rightly and told it in full.
187	These things I am willing to swear, and my heart bids me,
188	and I will not swear falsely before a god. But let Achilles
189	stay here meanwhile, eager though he is for Ares,
190	and all you others stay here in a body, until the gifts
191	come from my hut and we swear trusty oaths.
192	And to you yourself I give this charge and command:
193	choose young men, the best of all the Achaeans,
194	and bring the gifts from my ship, all that we promised Achilles
195	yesterday that we would give, and bring the women.
196	And let Talthybius quickly, through the wide army of the Achaeans,
197	make ready a boar for me, to sacrifice to Zeus and the Sun.”
198	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
199	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
200	at some other time you ought rather to busy yourselves with these things,
201	when there comes some pause in the war
202	and there is not so much might in my breast.
203	But now they lie torn, those whom he brought down,
204	Hector, son of Priam, when Zeus gave him glory,
205	and you two urge us to food! For my part, I
206	would now bid the sons of the Achaeans fight
207	fasting and unfed, and as the sun goes down
208	make ready a great supper, when we have avenged the insult.
209	Before that, nothing would go down my throat,
210	neither drink nor food, while my companion is dead,
211	who lies in my hut, torn by the sharp bronze,
212	turned toward the doorway, and around him his companions
213	weep; so these things are not in my mind at all,
214	but slaughter and blood and the grievous groaning of men.”
215	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
216	“Achilles, son of Peleus, by far the strongest of the Achaeans,
217	you are stronger than I, and better by no small amount
218	with the spear, but I would surpass you in understanding
219	by far, since I was born first and know more.
220	So let your heart be patient with my words.
221	Quickly men come to satiety of the din of battle,
222	in which the bronze strews the most straw on the ground,
223	but the harvest is smallest, when he tips the scales,
224	Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men.
225	But the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly;
226	for too many, and in rows, day after day,
227	are falling: when could anyone draw breath from toil?
228	But we must bury the man who dies,
229	having a pitiless heart, after weeping for him a single day;
230	and all who survive the hateful war
231	must remember drink and food, so that still more
232	we may fight against hostile men without rest, forever,
233	our bodies clothed in unyielding bronze. And let none
234	of the army hold back, waiting for some other summons;
235	for this summons will mean evil for whoever is left behind
236	at the ships of the Argives; but rushing on all together
237	let us rouse sharp Ares against the horse-taming Trojans.”
238	He spoke, and took with him the sons of glorious Nestor,
239	and Meges, son of Phyleus, and Thoas and Meriones,
240	and Lycomedes, son of Creon, and Melanippus;
241	and they went to the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus.
242	No sooner was the word spoken than the work was done:
243	seven tripods they brought from the hut, which he had promised him,
244	and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses;
245	and quickly they led out women skilled in flawless handiwork,
246	seven, and the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis.
247	And Odysseus weighed out ten talents of gold in all
248	and led the way, and with him the other young men of the Achaeans carried the gifts.
249	And they set them in the middle of the assembly, and Agamemnon
250	stood up; and Talthybius, like a god in his voice,
251	holding the boar in his hands, stood beside the shepherd of the people.
252	And the son of Atreus drew with his hand the knife
253	that always hung beside the great sheath of his sword,
254	and cutting the first hairs from the boar, he lifted up his hands to Zeus
255	and prayed; and they all sat there in silence in their places,
256	the Argives, listening to the king, as was right.
257	And praying he spoke, looking up into the wide sky:
258	“Now let Zeus be witness first, highest and best of the gods,
259	and Earth and the Sun and the Erinyes, who beneath the earth
260	punish men, whoever has sworn a false oath,
261	that I never laid a hand on the girl Briseis,
262	either wanting her for my bed or for anything else;
263	but she remained untouched in my huts.
264	And if any of this is falsely sworn, may the gods give me sorrows,
265	very many, all that they give to whoever sins against them in his oath.”
266	He spoke, and cut the boar's throat with the pitiless bronze.
267	And Talthybius flung it into the great gulf of the gray sea,
268	whirling it around, food for the fishes; but Achilles
269	stood up and spoke among the war-loving Argives:
270	“Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men:
271	never would the heart in my breast have been stirred
272	through and through by the son of Atreus, nor would he have led the girl
273	away against my will—there was no dealing with him; but Zeus, it seems,
274	wanted death to come upon many of the Achaeans.
275	But now go to your meal, so that we may join in the clash of Ares.”
276	So he spoke, and quickly dissolved the assembly.
277	And they scattered, each to his own ship,
278	and the great-hearted Myrmidons busied themselves with the gifts,
279	and went carrying them to the ship of divine Achilles.
280	And they set them in the huts, and seated the women,
281	and the noble attendants drove the horses to the herd.
282	And then Briseis, like golden Aphrodite,
283	when she saw Patroclus torn by the sharp bronze,
284	threw herself around him and wailed shrilly, and with her hands she tore
285	her breasts and her tender neck and her beautiful face.
286	And weeping, the woman, like the goddesses, spoke:
287	“Patroclus, dearest to my heart, wretched that I am,
288	I left you alive when I went from the hut,
289	and now I find you dead, leader of the people,
290	as I come back; so evil follows evil for me always.
291	The husband to whom my father and lady mother gave me
292	I saw before the city, torn by the sharp bronze,
293	and my three brothers, whom one mother bore with me,
294	dearly loved, who all met their day of destruction.
295	Nor would you let me, when swift Achilles killed my husband
296	and sacked the city of divine Mynes,
297	weep; but you said that of divine Achilles
298	you would make me the wedded wife, and take me in the ships
299	to Phthia, and give the marriage feast among the Myrmidons.
300	Therefore I weep for you without end, dead, who were always kind.”
301	So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her,
302	for Patroclus outwardly, but each for her own sorrows.
303	And around him the elders of the Achaeans gathered,
304	begging him to take his meal; but he refused, groaning:
305	“I beg you, if any of my dear companions will obey me,
306	do not bid me, before that, with food or drink
307	to sate my dear heart, since dread grief has come upon me:
308	until the sun goes down I will wait and endure all the same.”
309	So saying, he dismissed the other kings,
310	but the two sons of Atreus stayed, and brilliant Odysseus,
311	Nestor and Idomeneus and the old horse-driving Phoenix,
312	comforting him in his deep grief; but not at all in his heart
313	would he be comforted, before he entered the mouth of bloody war.
314	And remembering, he heaved a heavy sigh and spoke:
315	“Truly once, you too, most ill-fated one, dearest of my companions,
316	would yourself set a pleasant meal before me in the hut,
317	swiftly and quickly, whenever the Achaeans were in haste
318	to bring tearful Ares against the horse-taming Trojans.
319	But now you lie torn, and my heart
320	goes without drink and food, though they are here within,
321	for longing for you; for I could suffer nothing worse,
322	not even if I should learn of the death of my father,
323	who now, I think, in Phthia is letting his tender tears fall
324	for lack of such a son; while I, in a foreign land,
325	for the sake of Helen, at whom men shudder, am fighting the Trojans;
326	or of my dear son, who is being reared for me in Scyros,
327	if indeed godlike Neoptolemus is still alive.
328	For before this the heart in my breast hoped
329	that I alone would perish far from horse-pasturing Argos,
330	here in Troy, and that you would go back to Phthia,
331	so that you might take my son in a swift black ship
332	from Scyros, and show him everything,
333	my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house.
334	For by now I think Peleus is either altogether
335	dead, or still alive just barely, and grieving
336	in hateful old age, and always waiting for
337	the grievous news of me, when he hears that I have perished.”
338	So he spoke, weeping, and the elders groaned with him,
339	each remembering what he had left in his halls;
340	and the son of Cronus saw them weeping and pitied them,
341	and at once he addressed Athena with winged words:
342	“My child, you have utterly abandoned your brave man.
343	Is Achilles then no longer at all in your mind?
344	There he sits before the straight-horned ships,
345	mourning his dear companion; and the others
346	have gone to their meal, while he is unfed and has tasted nothing.
347	But go, drip nectar and lovely ambrosia
348	into his breast, so that hunger may not come upon him.”
349	So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already;
350	and she, like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey,
351	leapt down from the sky through the upper air. But the Achaeans
352	were at once arming throughout the army; and into Achilles'
353	breast, nectar and lovely ambrosia
354	she dripped, so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees;
355	and she herself went to the well-built house of her mighty father,
356	and they poured out away from the swift ships.
357	And as when snowflakes fly thick and fast from Zeus,
358	cold, under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky,
359	so then, thick and fast, the helmets, shining brightly,
360	were carried out from the ships, and bossed shields
361	and corselets with strong plates and ash spears.
362	And the gleam reached the sky, and all the earth around laughed
363	under the flash of the bronze; and beneath rose a din from the feet
364	of men; and in their midst brilliant Achilles armed himself.
365	And there was a gnashing of his teeth, and his two eyes
366	shone like the blaze of fire, and into his heart
367	entered unbearable grief; and raging against the Trojans
368	he put on the gifts of the god, which Hephaestus had toiled to make.
369	First he put the greaves around his shins,
370	fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
371	second, he put on the corselet around his chest.
372	And around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword
373	of bronze, and then his shield, great and heavy,
374	he took up, and from it, far off, came a blaze as of the moon.
375	And as when to sailors out at sea there appears the blaze
376	of a burning fire, which burns high up in the mountains
377	in a lonely farmstead; and storm winds bear them, unwilling,
378	over the fish-filled sea, far from their friends;
379	so from the shield of Achilles the blaze reached the upper air,
380	the fine shield, intricately worked; and lifting the helmet,
381	he set it, heavy, on his head; and like a star it shone,
382	the helmet with its horsehair crest, and around it waved the plumes
383	of gold, which Hephaestus had set thick about the crest.
384	And brilliant Achilles tried himself in his armor,
385	whether it fitted him and his splendid limbs moved freely in it;
386	and it was like wings to him, and it lifted the shepherd of the people.
387	And from its case he drew his father's spear,
388	heavy, huge, and massive; no other of the Achaeans
389	could wield it, but Achilles alone knew how to wield it,
390	the Pelian ash, which Chiron gave to his dear father
391	from the peak of Pelion, to be death for heroes.
392	And Automedon and Alcimus, busying themselves with the horses,
393	yoked them; and they put the fine breast straps on them, and the bits
394	they set in their jaws, and pulled the reins back
395	to the well-joined chariot. And he, taking the shining whip
396	that fitted his hand, sprang up behind the horses,
397	Automedon; and behind him Achilles took his stand, armed,
398	gleaming in his armor like shining Hyperion,
399	and he called terribly to the horses of his father:
400	“Xanthus and Balius, far-famed children of Podarge,
401	take thought in another way to bring your charioteer back
402	safe to the throng of the Danaans, when we have had our fill of war,
403	and do not leave him there dead, as you left Patroclus.”
404	And from under the yoke the horse with flashing feet spoke to him,
405	Xanthus, and at once he bowed his head; and all his mane,
406	streaming from under the yoke-cushion down beside the yoke, reached the ground;
407	and the white-armed goddess Hera gave him speech:
408	“Yes, we will save you still, this time at least, mighty Achilles;
409	but the day of your destruction is near; nor are we
410	to blame, but a great god and mighty Fate.
411	For it was not through our slowness or sloth
412	that the Trojans took the armor from the shoulders of Patroclus;
413	but the best of the gods, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore,
414	killed him among the champions, and gave glory to Hector.
415	But we two could run even as fast as the breath of the West Wind,
416	which they say is the swiftest of all; but for you yourself
417	it is fated to be brought down by force by a god and a man.”
418	So he spoke, and the Erinyes checked his voice.
419	Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him:
420	“Xanthus, why do you prophesy death for me? You have no need to.
421	I know well myself that it is my fate to perish here,
422	far from my dear father and mother; but all the same
423	I will not stop until I have given the Trojans their fill of war.”
424	He spoke, and with a cry drove his single-hoofed horses among the foremost.
