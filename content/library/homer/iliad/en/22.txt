# Homer, Iliad, Book 22 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So they throughout the city, in panic like fawns,
2	let the sweat dry from them, and drank, and quenched their thirst,
3	leaning on the fair battlements; but the Achaeans
4	came nearer the wall, leaning their shields against their shoulders.
5	And deadly fate fettered Hector to stay where he was,
6	in front of Ilios and the Scaean gates.
7	But Phoebus Apollo addressed the son of Peleus:
8	“Why, son of Peleus, do you pursue me with your swift feet,
9	you who are mortal, me, an immortal god? Not even yet
10	have you recognized that I am a god, and you rage so relentlessly.
11	Truly you care nothing now for your toil against the Trojans, whom you put to flight,
12	who have crowded into the city, while you have turned aside here.
13	You will not kill me, since I am not fated to fall to you.”
14	Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to him:
15	“You have foiled me, Far-Worker, most baneful of all the gods,
16	turning me now away from the wall to this place; truly many more
17	would have bitten the earth with their teeth before they reached Ilios.
18	But now you have taken great glory from me, and saved them
19	easily, since you have no fear at all of vengeance hereafter.
20	Truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength.”
21	So saying, he strode toward the city in high spirits,
22	rushing like a prize-winning horse with its chariot,
23	that runs easily at full stretch over the plain;
24	so Achilles plied his nimble feet and knees.
25	And the old man Priam was the first to see him with his eyes,
26	gleaming like the star, as he sped over the plain,
27	the star that comes in late summer, and its rays are plain for all to see,
28	shining among the many stars in the dead of night,
29	the star that men call by the name of the dog of Orion.
30	It is the brightest, but it is made a sign of evil,
31	and brings much fever to wretched mortals;
32	so the bronze shone about his chest as he ran.
33	And the old man groaned aloud, and beat his head with his hands,
34	lifting them up high, and with a great groan he called out,
35	begging his dear son; but he stood in front of the gates,
36	raging without end to fight with Achilles;
37	and the old man addressed him pitifully, stretching out his hands:
38	“Hector, dear child, for my sake do not wait for this man
39	alone, apart from the others, lest you soon meet your fate,
40	brought down by the son of Peleus, since he is far the stronger,
41	the hard man! Would that he were as dear to the gods
42	as he is to me: soon the dogs and vultures would eat him
43	as he lay; truly then dread grief would go from my heart;
44	he who has made me bereft of many brave sons,
45	killing them, and selling them off to islands far away.
46	For even now there are two sons, Lycaon and Polydorus,
47	whom I cannot see among the Trojans who have crowded into the city,
48	whom Laothoe bore me, a queen among women.
49	But if they are alive in the camp, then indeed
50	we will ransom them with bronze and gold, for we have it at home;
51	for the old man of famous name, Altes, granted much to his daughter.
52	But if they are already dead and in the house of Hades,
53	it is sorrow to my heart and to their mother, we who brought them into the world;
54	but to the rest of the people the sorrow will be shorter-lived,
55	unless you die too, brought down by Achilles.
56	But come inside the wall, my child, so that you may save
57	the Trojans and the Trojan women, and not hold out great glory
58	to the son of Peleus, and be robbed yourself of your dear life.
59	And pity me too, unhappy as I am, while I am still alive and aware,
60	ill-fated, whom the father, the son of Cronus, on the threshold of old age
61	will destroy in a grievous fate, when I have looked on many evils,
62	my sons perishing and my daughters dragged off,
63	and the chambers ravaged, and little children
64	dashed to the ground in grim combat,
65	and my sons' wives dragged off by the deadly hands of the Achaeans.
66	And myself, last of all, at the outer doors the dogs
67	that eat raw flesh will tear at, when someone with the sharp bronze
68	has struck or hit me and taken the life from my limbs,
69	the dogs I reared in my halls to share my table and guard my doors,
70	who will drink my blood and, frenzied at heart,
71	will lie in the doorway. For a young man it is all fitting,
72	killed in battle, torn by the sharp bronze,
73	to lie there; all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is;
74	but when dogs disfigure the gray head and the gray beard
75	and the nakedness of an old man who is slain,
76	this is the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals.”
77	The old man spoke, and with his hands he pulled at the gray hairs,
78	tearing them from his head; but he did not persuade Hector's heart.
79	And his mother on the other side mourned, shedding tears,
80	loosening the fold of her robe, and with her other hand she held up her breast;
81	and shedding tears she addressed him with winged words:
82	“Hector, my child, respect this, and pity me
83	myself, if ever I gave you the breast that makes care forgotten;
84	remember these things, dear child, and ward off the enemy
85	from inside the wall, and do not stand forth as champion against this man,
86	the hard man! For if he kills you, I shall never
87	weep for you on a bier, dear scion, whom I bore myself,
88	nor will your richly dowered wife; but far away from us both,
89	beside the ships of the Argives, the swift dogs will devour you.”
90	So the two, weeping, addressed their dear son
91	begging him earnestly; but they did not persuade Hector's heart,
92	but he waited for huge Achilles as he came nearer.
93	As a serpent of the mountains waits for a man at its hole,
94	having fed on evil drugs, and dread anger has come into it,
95	and it glares terribly as it coils about its hole;
96	so Hector, with unquenchable might, did not give ground,
97	leaning his shining shield against a jutting tower;
98	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
99	“Ah me! If I go within the gates and the walls,
100	Polydamas will be the first to heap disgrace on me,
101	who bade me lead the Trojans to the city
102	during this deadly night, when brilliant Achilles rose up.
103	But I did not listen—it would have been far better.
104	But now, since I have destroyed the army by my own recklessness,
105	I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes,
106	lest some day another man, baser than I, should say:
107	‘Hector trusted in his own strength and destroyed the army.’
108	So they will say; and then it would be far better for me
109	either to face Achilles, kill him, and come home,
110	or to perish gloriously at his hands before the city.
111	Or what if I set down my bossed shield
112	and my heavy helmet, and lean my spear against the wall,
113	and go myself to meet noble Achilles,
114	and promise him Helen, and the possessions with her,
115	all, as many as Alexander in his hollow ships
116	brought to Troy, which was the beginning of the quarrel,
117	to give to the sons of Atreus to take away, and besides among the Achaeans
118	to share out a part of all else that this city holds hidden;
119	and afterwards take from the Trojans an oath of the elders
120	to hide nothing, but to divide in two all
121	the wealth that the lovely citadel holds within it?
122	But why does my heart debate these things with me?
123	I fear that I may go up to him, and he will not pity me,
124	nor respect me at all, but will kill me naked,
125	just like a woman, once I have taken off my armor.
126	There is no way now, from an oak or from a rock,
127	to talk intimately with him, as a maiden and a young man,
128	a maiden and a young man talk intimately with each other.
129	Better to drive together in strife as quickly as may be;
130	let us see to which of us the Olympian will hold out triumph.”
131	So he pondered as he waited; and Achilles came near him,
132	the equal of Enyalius, the warrior of the shaking helmet,
133	brandishing over his right shoulder the Pelian ash,
134	the terrible spear; and the bronze shone around him like the light
135	of a blazing fire or of the rising sun.
136	And when Hector saw him, trembling seized him; and he could no longer bear
137	to stay there, but left the gates behind him, and went off in flight;
138	and the son of Peleus sprang at him, trusting in his swift feet.
139	As a falcon in the mountains, the nimblest of winged birds,
140	swoops easily after a timid dove,
141	and she flees out from under him, but he, close behind, with shrill cries,
142	darts at her again and again, and his heart bids him seize her;
143	so he flew straight on in his fury, and Hector fled
144	beneath the wall of the Trojans, and plied his nimble knees.
145	And they ran past the lookout and the windy wild fig tree,
146	always out from under the wall, along the wagon road,
147	and came to the two fair-flowing springs, where the two sources
148	of the eddying Scamander leap up.
149	For the one flows with warm water, and smoke
150	rises from it all around as if from a blazing fire;
151	and the other in summer flows forth like hail,
152	or cold snow, or ice formed from water.
153	And there beside them are the washing troughs, wide,
154	fair, and of stone, where the glossy clothes
155	the wives of the Trojans and their fair daughters used to wash
156	in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came.
157	There they ran past, one fleeing, the other pursuing behind;
158	in front a brave man fled, but a far better one pursued him
159	swiftly, for it was no beast for sacrifice or ox-hide
160	they strove to win, such as are prizes for the feet of men,
161	but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses.
162	And as when prize-winning single-hoofed horses round the turning posts
163	run very swiftly, and a great prize is set out,
164	a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died;
165	so the two whirled three times around the city of Priam
166	on swift feet; and all the gods were watching;
167	and among them the father of men and gods began to speak:
168	“Ah, truly a man dear to me, pursued around the wall,
169	I see with my eyes; and my heart grieves
170	for Hector, who has burned many thighs of oxen for me
171	on the peaks of many-folded Ida, and at other times
172	on the height of the city; and now brilliant Achilles
173	is pursuing him with swift feet around the city of Priam.
174	But come, consider, you gods, and take counsel,
175	whether we shall save him from death, or now at last
176	bring him down by Achilles, son of Peleus, brave as he is.”
177	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
178	“Father, lord of the gleaming thunderbolt and of the dark clouds, what a thing you have said!
179	A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion,
180	do you wish to set free again from grim-sounding death?
181	Do it; but not all of us other gods approve.”
182	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
183	“Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child; I do not speak
184	in full earnest, and I want to be kind to you;
185	do as your mind is set, and hang back no longer.”
186	So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already,
187	and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus.
188	And swift Achilles pressed on after Hector, relentlessly driving him on.
189	As when in the mountains a dog chases the fawn of a deer,
190	having roused it from its lair, through the valleys and through the glens;
191	and even if it escapes his notice, cowering under a bush,
192	yet he runs on, tracking it steadily, until he finds it;
193	so Hector did not escape the notice of the swift-footed son of Peleus.
194	As often as he made a rush for the Dardanian gates,
195	to dart straight in beneath the well-built towers,
196	in the hope that from above they might defend him with their missiles,
197	so often would the other get in front of him and turn him back
198	toward the plain; and he himself flew always on the side of the city.
199	As in a dream a man cannot pursue one who flees;
200	the one cannot escape, nor the other pursue;
201	so the one could not catch him with his feet, nor the other escape.
202	And how could Hector have escaped the fates of death,
203	had not Apollo come to him for the very last time,
204	close by, who roused his might and his nimble knees?
205	And brilliant Achilles shook his head at his men,
206	and would not let them hurl their bitter missiles at Hector,
207	lest someone win the glory by hitting him, and he himself come second.
208	But when for the fourth time they came to the springs,
209	then the father held out his golden scales,
210	and in them he set two fates of death that lays men low,
211	one of Achilles, and one of Hector, tamer of horses,
212	and he took the scales by the middle and raised them; and the fated day of Hector sank,
213	and went off to the house of Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him.
214	And the gray-eyed goddess Athena came to the son of Peleus,
215	and standing near him she addressed him with winged words:
216	“Now at last I hope that we two, shining Achilles, dear to Zeus,
217	will carry off great glory for the Achaeans to the ships,
218	having cut down Hector, insatiable of battle though he is.
219	Now there is no way for him to get clear of us any longer,
220	not even if far-working Apollo should go to great lengths,
221	groveling before aegis-bearing father Zeus.
222	But you stand still now and catch your breath, and I
223	will go to him and persuade him to fight you face to face.”
224	So spoke Athena, and he obeyed, and rejoiced in his heart,
225	and he stood, leaning on his ash spear with its point of bronze.
226	And she left him, and came upon brilliant Hector
227	in the likeness of Deiphobus in form and in tireless voice;
228	and standing near him she addressed him with winged words:
229	“Dear brother, truly swift Achilles is pressing you hard,
230	pursuing you with swift feet around the city of Priam;
231	but come, let us stand firm and hold our ground and beat him off.”
232	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to her in turn:
233	“Deiphobus, truly before now you were by far the dearest to me
234	of my brothers, the sons whom Hecuba and Priam bore;
235	but now I mean to honor you even more in my heart,
236	you who dared for my sake, when you saw me with your eyes,
237	to come outside the wall, while the others stay inside.”
238	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
239	“Dear brother, truly our father and our lady mother
240	begged me hard, imploring me one after the other, and our companions around me,
241	to stay there; so much do they all tremble before him;
242	but my heart within me was worn down by grim grief.
243	But now let us charge straight on and fight, and let there be no sparing
244	of spears, so that we may see whether Achilles
245	will kill us both and carry off our bloody spoils
246	to the hollow ships, or whether he will be brought down by your spear.”
247	So saying, Athena led the way by craft;
248	and when they had come close, advancing against each other,
249	great Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him first:
250	“I will no longer flee from you, son of Peleus, as before
251	I fled three times around the great city of Priam, and never dared
252	to wait for you as you came on; but now my heart has sent me
253	to stand against you face to face; I may kill you, or be killed.
254	But come, let us call upon the gods; for they will be the best
255	witnesses and overseers of agreements;
256	for I will not defile you terribly, if Zeus grants me
257	to outlast you, and I take your life;
258	but when I have stripped you of your glorious armor, Achilles,
259	I will give your body back to the Achaeans; and you do the same.”
260	Looking at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles said:
261	“Hector, accursed one, do not talk to me of agreements;
262	as there are no trusty oaths between lions and men,
263	nor do wolves and lambs have hearts at one,
264	but they mean evil to one another through and through,
265	so it is not possible for you and me to be friends, nor will there be
266	oaths between us two, before one or the other has fallen
267	and glutted Ares with blood, the warrior with the oxhide shield.
268	Remember all your prowess; now you must indeed
269	be a spearman and a bold warrior;
270	there is no escape for you any longer, but at once Pallas Athena
271	will bring you down by my spear; and now you will pay back all at once
272	the sorrows of my companions, whom you killed raging with your spear.”
273	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
274	but shining Hector, looking straight at it, avoided it;
275	for he sank down, seeing it in time, and the bronze spear flew over,
276	and stuck fast in the ground; but Pallas Athena snatched it up,
277	and gave it back to Achilles, unseen by Hector, shepherd of the people.
278	And Hector spoke to the noble son of Peleus:
279	“You missed! So, Achilles, like the gods, you did not yet at all
280	know my fate from Zeus, though truly you said you did;
281	but you were a glib talker and a cheat with words,
282	so that in fear of you I might forget my might and my courage.
283	You will not plant your spear in my back as I flee,
284	but drive it through my chest as I come straight on,
285	if a god has granted it to you; now in your turn avoid my spear,
286	my bronze one; would that you might take it whole into your flesh!
287	And the war would be lighter for the Trojans
288	once you were dead; for you are their greatest bane.”
289	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
290	and struck the middle of the shield of the son of Peleus, and did not miss;
291	but the spear glanced far off from the shield; and Hector was angry
292	that his swift missile had flown from his hand in vain,
293	and he stood downcast, for he had no other ash spear.
294	And he called to Deiphobus of the white shield, shouting aloud,
295	and asked him for a long spear; but he was nowhere near him.
296	And Hector understood in his heart, and spoke:
297	“Ah, truly the gods have called me to my death;
298	for I thought that the hero Deiphobus was beside me;
299	but he is within the wall, and Athena has deceived me.
300	And now evil death is near me, and no longer far off,
301	nor any way to avoid it; for truly this long ago was dearer
302	to Zeus and to the far-shooting son of Zeus, who before now
303	protected me with ready hearts; but now my fate has overtaken me.
304	Let me not die without a struggle and without glory,
305	but having done some great thing, even for men to come to hear of.”
306	So he spoke, and drew the sharp sword
307	that hung beneath his flank, great and heavy,
308	and crouching, he swooped like an eagle flying high
309	that goes down to the plain through the murky clouds
310	to snatch a tender lamb or a cowering hare;
311	so Hector swooped, brandishing his sharp sword.
312	And Achilles rushed at him, and filled his heart with savage
313	fury, and covered his chest in front with his shield,
314	fine and intricately worked, and nodded with his shining helmet
315	with its four ridges; and around it waved the fair plumes
316	of gold, which Hephaestus had set thick about the crest.
317	And as a star goes among the stars in the dead of night,
318	the evening star, which stands in the sky the fairest star of all,
319	so light shone from the well-sharpened point which Achilles
320	brandished in his right hand, with evil intent against brilliant Hector,
321	eyeing his fair skin, where it would best give way.
322	All the rest of his flesh the bronze armor covered,
323	the fine armor he had stripped from mighty Patroclus when he killed him;
324	but his flesh showed where the collarbones hold the neck away from the shoulders,
325	at the throat, where the destruction of life is swiftest;
326	there, as he came on, brilliant Achilles drove at him with his spear,
327	and straight through the soft neck went the point;
328	but the ash spear heavy with bronze did not cut the windpipe,
329	so that he might still say something to him in answer.
330	And he fell in the dust; and brilliant Achilles boasted over him:
331	“Hector, you thought, I suppose, when you were stripping Patroclus,
332	that you would be safe, and you had no regard for me, since I was far away,
333	fool! But far from him a helper, a much better man,
334	was left behind at the hollow ships, I myself,
335	who have loosened your knees; and you the dogs and the birds
336	will tear shamefully, but him the Achaeans will give burial rites.”
337	Then Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him feebly:
338	“I beg you by your life and your knees and your parents,
339	do not let the dogs devour me beside the ships of the Achaeans,
340	but take bronze and gold in plenty,
341	the gifts that my father and my lady mother will give you,
342	but give my body back to be taken home, so that when I am dead
343	the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire.”
344	Looking at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles said:
345	“Do not implore me, you dog, by my knees or my parents;
346	I wish that my fury and my heart would somehow send me
347	to carve your flesh and eat it raw, for what you have done to me,
348	as surely as there is no one who will keep the dogs from your head,
349	not even if ten and twenty times the ransom
350	they bring here and weigh out, and promise still more besides,
351	not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold,
352	Priam, descendant of Dardanus; not even so shall your lady mother
353	lay you on a bier and lament for you, whom she bore herself,
354	but the dogs and the birds will divide you all among them.”
355	Then, dying, Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him:
356	“Truly I know you well as I look at you, and I was not going
357	to persuade you; for truly the heart in your breast is iron.
358	Take care now, lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you,
359	on that day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo
360	destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean gates.”
361	As he spoke, the end of death covered him,
362	and his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades,
363	lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth.
364	And to him, though he was dead, brilliant Achilles spoke:
365	“Lie dead; and I will accept my doom then, whenever
366	Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods.”
367	He spoke, and drew the bronze spear out of the body,
368	and laid it aside, and began to strip the armor from his shoulders,
369	bloody; and the other sons of the Achaeans ran up around him,
370	and gazed at the stature and admirable looks
371	of Hector; and not one stood beside him without stabbing him.
372	And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
373	“Ah, truly much softer to handle now
374	is Hector than when he burned the ships with blazing fire.”
375	So a man would say, and would stand beside him and stab him.
376	And when brilliant swift-footed Achilles had stripped him,
377	he stood among the Achaeans and spoke winged words:
378	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
379	since the gods have granted us to bring down this man,
380	who has done more harm than all the others together,
381	come, let us make trial around the city in our armor,
382	so that we may know what mind the Trojans have,
383	whether they will leave the height of the city now that this man has fallen,
384	or are eager to stay, even though Hector is no more.
385	But why does my heart debate these things with me?
386	There lies beside the ships a corpse unwept, unburied,
387	Patroclus; and him I will not forget, as long as I
388	am among the living and my knees have strength to move;
389	and even if in the house of Hades men forget the dead,
390	yet even there I will remember my dear companion.
391	But now come, young men of the Achaeans, singing the paean,
392	let us go back to the hollow ships, and take this man with us.
393	We have won great glory; we have killed brilliant Hector,
394	to whom the Trojans throughout the city prayed as to a god.”
395	He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector.
396	Behind both his feet he pierced the tendons
397	from heel to ankle, and fastened straps of oxhide to them,
398	and tied them to the chariot, and let the head drag;
399	and mounting the chariot, and lifting the glorious armor in,
400	he whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling.
401	And as he was dragged a cloud of dust rose, and around him his dark hair
402	spread out, and all his head lay in the dust,
403	that once was handsome; but then Zeus gave him to his enemies
404	to defile in his own native land.
405	So his head was all grimed with dust; and his mother
406	tore her hair, and flung her shining head covering
407	far from her, and wailed very loudly when she saw her son;
408	and his dear father groaned pitifully, and around them the people
409	were seized by wailing and groaning throughout the city.
410	And it was most like as if all
411	beetling Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom.
412	The people could hardly hold back the old man as he chafed,
413	eager to go out through the Dardanian gates.
414	He entreated them all, rolling in the dung,
415	calling each man by his name:
416	“Hold back, my friends, and for all your care let me go alone
417	out of the city and come to the ships of the Achaeans.
418	Let me beg this reckless man, this doer of violent deeds,
419	in the hope that he may respect my years and pity
420	my old age; for he too has a father like me,
421	Peleus, who begot him and reared him to be a bane
422	to the Trojans; and on me above all others he has laid sorrows.
423	For so many sons of mine he has killed in their bloom;
424	for all of them I do not mourn so much, grieved though I am,
425	as for one, for whom sharp grief will bring me down to the house of Hades,
426	for Hector; would that he had died in my arms;
427	then we would have had our fill of weeping and shedding tears,
428	his mother, most ill-fated, who bore him, and I myself.”
429	So he spoke, weeping, and the citizens groaned with him;
430	and among the Trojan women Hecuba led the unceasing lamentation:
431	“My child, wretched that I am! Why should I live, when I have suffered dreadful things,
432	now that you are dead? You who night and day were
433	my boast throughout the city, and a blessing to all,
434	to the Trojans and the Trojan women throughout the city, who greeted you
435	as a god; for truly you were their very great glory
436	while you lived; but now death and fate have overtaken you.”
437	So she spoke, weeping; but the wife had not yet learned anything
438	of Hector; for no true messenger had come and told her
439	that her husband was staying outside the gates,
440	but she was weaving a web in the inner part of the high house,
441	a double-folded purple cloth, and was working flowers of many colors into it.
442	And she called to her handmaids with lovely tresses through the house
443	to set a great tripod over the fire, so that there might be
444	a hot bath for Hector when he came home from the battle,
445	the fool, nor did she know that far from all baths
446	gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles.
447	And she heard the wailing and the groaning from the tower;
448	and her limbs shook, and the shuttle fell from her to the ground;
449	and she spoke again among her slave women with lovely tresses:
450	“Come, two of you follow me, let me see what has been done.
451	I heard the voice of my revered mother-in-law, and in my own
452	breast my heart leaps up to my mouth, and beneath me my knees
453	are frozen; surely some evil is near for the children of Priam.
454	May the word be far from my hearing! But I am terribly afraid
455	that brilliant Achilles has cut off my bold Hector
456	alone from the city and is chasing him toward the plain,
457	and has now put an end to the grievous pride
458	that held him, since he never stayed in the crowd of men,
459	but ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might.”
460	So saying, she rushed through the hall like a maenad,
461	with her heart leaping; and her handmaids went with her.
462	But when she came to the tower and the throng of men,
463	she stood on the wall, looking about, and saw him
464	being dragged in front of the city; and the swift horses
465	were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans.
466	And murky night covered her eyes,
467	and she fell backward, and breathed out her soul.
468	And far from her head she flung the glossy bindings,
469	the headband, and the cap, and the plaited band,
470	and the veil that golden Aphrodite had given her
471	on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet took her
472	from the house of Eetion, after giving a countless bride-price.
473	And around her stood her husband's sisters and his brothers' wives in plenty,
474	and they held her up among them, dazed to the point of death.
475	But when she came to herself and her spirit was gathered back into her heart,
476	lamenting, with deep sobs, she spoke among the Trojan women:
477	“Hector, unhappy that I am! We were both born, it seems, to one fate,
478	both of us, you in Troy in the house of Priam,
479	and I in Thebe under wooded Placus,
480	in the house of Eetion, who reared me when I was little,
481	he ill-fated, and I of dread fate; would that he had never begotten me!
482	But now you go to the house of Hades beneath the depths of the earth,
483	and leave me in hateful grief,
484	a widow in the halls; and the boy is still a mere infant,
485	whom you and I begot, both most ill-fated; neither will you be to him
486	a blessing, Hector, since you are dead, nor he to you.
487	For even if he escapes the tearful war of the Achaeans,
488	always there will be toil and sorrows for him
489	hereafter; for others will take away his lands.
490	The day of orphanhood leaves a child without friends of his own age;
491	in all things he hangs his head, and his cheeks are wet with tears,
492	and in his need the boy goes up to his father's companions,
493	plucking one by the cloak and another by the tunic;
494	and of those who pity him one holds out a cup for a moment;
495	he wets his lips, but does not wet his palate.
496	And the boy whose parents are both alive drives him from the feast,
497	striking him with his hands and rebuking him with abusive words:
498	‘Off with you! Your father does not feast among us.’
499	And in tears the boy goes up to his widowed mother,
500	Astyanax, who once on his father's knees
501	ate only marrow and the rich fat of sheep;
502	and when sleep came over him, and he stopped his childish play,
503	he would sleep in a bed, in the arms of his nurse,
504	in a soft bed, his heart filled with good things;
505	but now he will suffer much, having lost his dear father,
506	he whom the Trojans call by the name of Astyanax;
507	for you alone guarded their gates and long walls.
508	But now beside the curved ships, far from your parents,
509	writhing worms will eat you, when the dogs have had their fill
510	of your naked body; yet there are clothes laid up for you in the halls,
511	fine and lovely, made by the hands of women.
512	But all of these I will burn up with blazing fire,
513	of no use to you, since you will not lie in them,
514	but to be your fame before the Trojans and the Trojan women.”
515	So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her.
