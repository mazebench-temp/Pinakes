# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	But when they had crossed the stakes and the ditch
2	in their flight, and many had been brought down at the hands of the Danaans,
3	they halted beside their chariots and stood their ground,
4	pale with fear, in their rout; and Zeus awoke
5	on the peaks of Ida, beside Hera of the golden throne,
6	and he sprang up and stood, and saw the Trojans and the Achaeans,
7	the one side in turmoil, and the others driving them in confusion from behind,
8	the Argives, and among them lord Poseidon;
9	and he saw Hector lying on the plain, and around him his companions
10	were sitting, and he was held by a grievous gasping, senseless at heart,
11	vomiting blood, for it was not the feeblest of the Achaeans who had hit him.
12	And seeing him, the father of men and gods pitied him,
13	and looking at Hera darkly and terribly he spoke a word to her:
14	“Truly, Hera, there is no dealing with you: your evil-working trick
15	has stopped brilliant Hector from battle and put the army to rout.
16	I do not know whether, for your grievous mischief, you will not again
17	be the first to reap the fruit, and I lash you with blows.
18	Do you not remember when you hung from on high, and from your feet
19	I let down two anvils, and around your hands I cast a bond
20	of gold, unbreakable? And you hung in the upper air and the clouds,
21	and the gods chafed throughout high Olympus,
22	but they could not stand beside you and set you free; and whomever I caught
23	I would seize and hurl from the threshold, until he reached
24	the earth with little strength left. Yet not even so did it let my heart go,
25	the ceaseless pain for divine Heracles,
26	whom you, winning over the storms with the help of the North Wind,
27	sent over the barren sea, devising evil,
28	and then you carried him off to well-peopled Cos.
29	From there I rescued him and brought him back again
30	to horse-pasturing Argos, though he had suffered many labors.
31	Of these things I will remind you again, so that you may leave off your deceits,
32	so that you may see whether love and the bed will help you,
33	in which you lay with me, coming away from the gods, and deceived me.”
34	So he spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera shuddered,
35	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
36	“Let Earth now be witness to this, and the wide Heaven above,
37	and the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest
38	and most dread oath for the blessed gods,
39	and your own sacred head, and the wedded bed of us two,
40	by which I would never swear idly:
41	it is not through my will that Poseidon the earth-shaker
42	harms the Trojans and Hector and helps the others,
43	but perhaps it is his own heart that urges him on and bids him,
44	and he saw the Achaeans hard pressed at the ships and pitied them.
45	But I would advise even him
46	to go wherever you lead, lord of the dark clouds.”
47	So she spoke, and the father of men and gods smiled,
48	and answering her he addressed her with winged words:
49	“If from now on, ox-eyed queen Hera,
50	you were to sit among the immortals thinking as I do,
51	then Poseidon, however much he wishes otherwise,
52	would quickly turn his mind to follow your heart and mine.
53	But if you are speaking truly and exactly,
54	go now among the tribes of the gods, and call here
55	Iris to come, and Apollo famed for his bow,
56	so that she may go among the army of the bronze-armored Achaeans
57	and tell lord Poseidon
58	to cease from war and go to his own house,
59	and so that Phoebus Apollo may urge Hector on into battle,
60	and breathe might into him again, and make him forget the pains
61	that now wear him down in his mind; and the Achaeans
62	he may turn back again, rousing a craven panic among them,
63	so that in their flight they tumble among the many-benched ships
64	of Achilles, son of Peleus; and he will rouse up his companion,
65	Patroclus; and shining Hector will kill him with the spear
66	before Ilios, after Patroclus has destroyed many young men,
67	others besides, and among them my son, brilliant Sarpedon.
68	And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector.
69	From then on I would bring about a counterattack from the ships,
70	always and without a break, until the Achaeans
71	take steep Ilios through the counsels of Athena.
72	But until then I do not cease from my anger, nor will I let any other
73	of the immortals defend the Danaans here,
74	until the wish of the son of Peleus is fulfilled,
75	as I promised him at first, and nodded my head to it,
76	on the day when the goddess Thetis clasped my knees,
77	begging me to honor Achilles, sacker of cities.”
78	So he spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey,
79	and she went from the mountains of Ida to high Olympus.
80	As when the mind of a man darts, who has traveled
81	over much of the earth, and in his shrewd mind he thinks,
82	“If only I were there, or there,” and longs for many things,
83	so swiftly queen Hera flew in her eagerness;
84	and she came to steep Olympus, and came upon them gathered together,
85	the immortal gods, in the house of Zeus; and seeing her
86	they all sprang up and greeted her with their cups.
87	She let the others be, but from fair-cheeked Themis
88	she took the cup; for she came running first to meet her,
89	and she spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
90	“Hera, why have you come? You look like one in terror;
91	surely the son of Cronus, your husband, has put you to flight.”
92	Then the white-armed goddess Hera answered her:
93	“Do not ask me about these things, goddess Themis; you know yourself
94	what kind of heart he has, overbearing and harsh.
95	But you begin the gods' equal share of the feast in the halls;
96	and you will hear, with all the immortals,
97	what evil deeds Zeus is making known; and I do not think
98	that the hearts of all alike will rejoice, either among mortals
99	or among gods, if anyone still feasts in good cheer now.”
100	So saying, queen Hera sat down,
101	and throughout the house of Zeus the gods were troubled; and she laughed
102	with her lips, but her forehead above her dark brows
103	was not warmed; and in her indignation she spoke among them all:
104	“Fools, we who rage against Zeus in our folly!
105	Are we still eager to stop him by going near him,
106	by word or by force? But he sits apart and does not care
107	or mind us; for he says that among the immortal gods
108	in power and in strength he is far the best.
109	So accept whatever evil he sends to each of you.
110	For already now, I think, a bane has been made for Ares;
111	for his son has perished in battle, the dearest of men to him,
112	Ascalaphus, whom mighty Ares says is his own.”
113	So she spoke, and Ares struck both his sturdy thighs
114	with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word:
115	“Do not be indignant with me now, you who have your homes on Olympus,
116	if I go to the ships of the Achaeans to avenge the killing of my son,
117	even if it is my fate to be struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus
118	and lie with the dead among the blood and the dust.”
119	So he spoke, and he told Terror and Rout to yoke
120	his horses, and he himself put on his gleaming armor.
121	Then another, still greater and more grievous,
122	anger and wrath would have come from Zeus upon the immortals,
123	had not Athena, terribly afraid for all the gods,
124	rushed out through the doorway and left the throne where she sat,
125	and taken the helmet from his head and the shield from his shoulders,
126	and she took the spear from his strong hand and stood it aside,
127	the bronze spear; and she approached furious Ares with words:
128	“Madman, crazed in your mind, you are lost! Truly it is for nothing
129	that you have ears to hear; your mind is gone, and your sense of shame.
130	Do you not hear what the white-armed goddess Hera says,
131	who has just now come from Olympian Zeus?
132	Do you want, when you yourself have filled up the measure of many evils,
133	to come back to Olympus, grieving though you are, under compulsion,
134	and to plant a great evil for all the others?
135	For at once the high-hearted Trojans and the Achaeans
136	he will leave, and come to Olympus to throw us into tumult,
137	and he will seize us one after another, the one to blame and the one who is not.
138	So now I bid you let go your anger for your own son;
139	for already many a man better than he in strength and hands
140	has been killed, or will be killed hereafter; and it is hard
141	to save the lineage and birth of all men.”
142	So saying, she sat furious Ares down on his throne.
143	And Hera called Apollo out of the house,
144	and Iris, who is the messenger among the immortal gods,
145	and she spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
146	“Zeus bids you two come to Ida as quickly as you can;
147	and when you have come and looked upon the face of Zeus,
148	do whatever he urges and bids you.”
149	So saying, queen Hera went back,
150	and sat on her throne; and the two darted off and flew.
151	They came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts,
152	and they found the far-seeing son of Cronus on the peak of Gargarus,
153	sitting, and a fragrant cloud was wreathed around him.
154	And the two came before Zeus the cloud-gatherer
155	and stood; and when he saw them he was not angry in his heart,
156	since they had quickly obeyed the words of his dear wife.
157	And he addressed Iris first with winged words:
158	“Go, swift Iris, to lord Poseidon,
159	take him all this message, and do not be a false messenger.
160	Bid him cease from battle and war
161	and go among the tribes of the gods, or into the shining sea.
162	But if he will not obey my words, but disregards them,
163	then let him consider in his mind and in his heart
164	lest, strong though he is, he may not dare, when I come at him,
165	to await me; for I say I am far stronger than he in strength,
166	and the elder by birth; yet his heart does not shrink
167	from speaking to me as an equal, whom others too dread.”
168	So he spoke, and swift Iris, wind-footed, did not disobey,
169	and she went down from the mountains of Ida to sacred Ilios.
170	As when snow or hail flies from the clouds,
171	cold, under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky,
172	so swiftly swift Iris flew in her eagerness,
173	and standing near she spoke to the famous shaker of the earth:
174	“A message for you, dark-haired earth-holder,
175	I have come here bringing, from aegis-bearing Zeus.
176	He bade you cease from battle and war
177	and go among the tribes of the gods, or into the shining sea.
178	But if you will not obey his words, but disregard them,
179	he threatened that he himself would come here to fight against you
180	face to face; and he bids you keep out of reach of
181	his hands, since he says he is far stronger than you in strength,
182	and the elder by birth; yet your heart does not shrink
183	from speaking to him as an equal, whom others too dread.”
184	Deeply troubled, the famous shaker of the earth said to her:
185	“Ah, truly, brave as he is, he has spoken arrogantly,
186	if he will hold me back by force against my will, when I am his equal in honor.
187	For we are three brothers, born of Cronus, whom Rhea bore,
188	Zeus and I, and the third is Hades, lord of those below.
189	All things have been divided in three, and each has his share of honor:
190	I got by lot the gray sea, to dwell in forever,
191	when the lots were shaken, and Hades got the misty darkness,
192	and Zeus got the wide sky, in the upper air and the clouds;
193	but the earth is still common to all, and high Olympus.
194	So I will not live by the mind of Zeus; but at his ease,
195	strong though he is, let him stay in his third share.
196	And let him not try at all to frighten me with his hands, as if I were a coward;
197	for it would be better for him to rebuke his daughters and his sons
198	with terrible words, the children he fathered himself,
199	who will listen to him when he urges them, even under compulsion.”
200	Then swift Iris, wind-footed, answered him:
201	“Am I really to carry it in just this way, dark-haired earth-holder,
202	this word to Zeus, so harsh and stern,
203	or will you turn it somewhat? The hearts of good men can be turned.
204	You know how the Erinyes always follow the elder.”
205	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker spoke to her in turn:
206	“Goddess Iris, this word you have spoken is fair and right;
207	it is a good thing, too, when a messenger knows what is due.
208	But this dread grief comes upon my heart and spirit,
209	whenever one who has an equal lot, ordained to the same portion as himself,
210	he wishes to rebuke with angry words.
211	But now, for all my indignation, I will give way;
212	and I will tell you something else, and this I threaten from my heart:
213	if, against my will and that of Athena, driver of spoil,
214	of Hera and Hermes and lord Hephaestus,
215	he spares steep Ilios, and will not consent
216	to sack it, and to give great power to the Argives,
217	let him know this, that between us two there will be incurable anger.”
218	So saying, the shaker of the earth left the Achaean army,
219	and went and plunged into the sea; and the Achaean heroes missed him.
220	And then Zeus the cloud-gatherer spoke to Apollo:
221	“Go now, dear Phoebus, to Hector armed in bronze;
222	for already the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
223	has gone into the shining sea, avoiding the sheer anger
224	of mine; for surely others too would have heard of our fighting,
225	even the gods below, who are around Cronus.
226	But this was far better both for me and for himself,
227	that before it came to that, for all his indignation, he gave way
228	before my hands, since it would not have been settled without sweat.
229	But take the tasseled aegis in your hands,
230	and shake it hard over them, and put the Achaean heroes to flight;
231	and let shining Hector be your own care, you who shoot from afar;
232	rouse great might in him for so long, until the Achaeans
233	in their flight reach the ships and the Hellespont.
234	From then on I myself will devise both deed and word,
235	so that the Achaeans may draw breath again from their toil.”
236	So he spoke, and Apollo did not fail to heed his father,
237	and he went down from the mountains of Ida like a hawk,
238	a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds.
239	He found the son of wise-hearted Priam, brilliant Hector,
240	sitting up, no longer lying down, and he was just gathering his spirit again,
241	and knew his companions around him; and the gasping and the sweat
242	had stopped, since the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus had roused him.
243	And standing near him far-working Apollo spoke to him:
244	“Hector, son of Priam, why is it that you, apart from the others,
245	sit here with little strength left? Has some sorrow come upon you?”
246	Then Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him feebly:
247	“Who are you, good sir, among the gods, who question me face to face?
248	Have you not heard that at the sterns of the ships of the Achaeans,
249	as I was killing his companions, Ajax, good at the war cry, struck me
250	on the chest with a stone, and stopped me from my furious courage?
251	And truly I thought that I would come to the dead and the house of Hades
252	on this very day, since I was gasping out my heart.”
253	Then far-working lord Apollo spoke to him in turn:
254	“Take heart now: such a helper has the son of Cronus
255	sent forth from Ida to stand by you and defend you,
256	Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who before now too
257	have protected you, both you yourself and your steep citadel.
258	But come now, urge on your many horsemen
259	to drive their swift horses against the hollow ships;
260	and I will go on ahead, and the road for the horses
261	I will make smooth all the way, and turn back the Achaean heroes.”
262	So saying, he breathed great might into the shepherd of the people.
263	As when a stalled horse, fed full of barley at the manger,
264	breaks his halter and gallops across the plain, pounding the ground,
265	used to bathing in the fair-flowing river,
266	exulting; he holds his head high, and his mane
267	streams about his shoulders; and trusting in his splendor
268	his knees carry him swiftly to the haunts and pasture of the horses:
269	so Hector plied his nimble feet and knees,
270	urging on the horsemen, once he had heard the voice of the god.
271	And as when an antlered stag or a wild goat
272	is chased by dogs and country men,
273	but a lofty rock or a shady forest
274	shelters it, and it is not their lot to catch it;
275	and at their shouting a bearded lion appears
276	in their path, and at once turns them all back, for all their eagerness:
277	so the Danaans for a while kept following in a mass,
278	stabbing at them with swords and double-pointed spears;
279	but when they saw Hector going along the ranks of men,
280	they were afraid, and the hearts of all sank to their feet.
281	Then Thoas, son of Andraemon, spoke among them,
282	by far the best of the Aetolians, skilled with the javelin
283	and good in close fighting; and in the assembly few of the Achaeans
284	could beat him, when the young men contended in speeches;
285	with good intent he addressed them and spoke:
286	“Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes,
287	how he has risen up once more, escaping the fates,
288	Hector! Truly the heart of each of us hoped
289	that he had died at the hands of Ajax, son of Telamon.
290	But once again one of the gods has rescued and saved
291	Hector, who has loosened the knees of many of the Danaans,
292	as I think he will now too; for not without
293	loud-thundering Zeus does he stand forth as champion, so eager.
294	Come then, let us all do as I say.
295	Let us order the multitude to go back to the ships;
296	but let us ourselves, all of us who claim to be the best in the army,
297	stand firm, in the hope that we may first hold him back, meeting him
298	with our spears held up; and I think that, eager though he is,
299	he will be afraid in his heart to plunge into the throng of the Danaans.”
300	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed.
301	Those around Ajax and lord Idomeneus,
302	Teucer and Meriones and Meges, the equal of Ares,
303	called the best men and set the battle in order
304	against Hector and the Trojans; but behind them
305	the multitude went back to the ships of the Achaeans.
306	And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them,
307	with long strides; and before him went Phoebus Apollo,
308	his shoulders wrapped in cloud, and he held the furious aegis,
309	dreadful, shaggy all around, conspicuous, which the smith
310	Hephaestus gave to Zeus to carry, to put men to rout;
311	holding this in his hands he led the army.
312	And the Argives held their ground in a body, and the war cry rose
313	shrill on both sides, and arrows leapt from the bowstrings;
314	and many spears from bold hands
315	were fixed, some of them, in the flesh of young men swift in war,
316	and many, too, in between, before they could reach the white flesh,
317	stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh.
318	As long as Phoebus Apollo held the aegis motionless in his hands,
319	so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell.
320	But when he looked the Danaans of the swift horses in the face
321	and shook it, and himself shouted very loudly besides, then their hearts
322	in their breasts he bewitched, and they forgot their furious courage.
323	And as a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep
324	is thrown into confusion by two wild beasts in the dead of black night,
325	coming on them suddenly when the herdsman is not there,
326	so the Achaeans fled in rout, their courage gone; for Apollo
327	sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector.
328	Then man killed man, as the battle scattered.
329	Hector killed Arcesilaus and Stichius,
330	the one a leader of the bronze-armored Boeotians,
331	the other a trusted companion of great-hearted Menestheus;
332	and Aeneas slew and stripped Medon and Iasus.
333	One was the bastard son of divine Oileus,
334	Medon, the brother of Ajax; but he lived
335	in Phylace, away from his native land, since he had killed a man,
336	a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, whom Oileus had as wife;
337	and Iasus had been made a leader of the Athenians,
338	and was called the son of Sphelus, son of Bucolus.
339	And Polydamas killed Mecisteus, and Polites Echius,
340	in the front line of battle, and brilliant Agenor killed Clonius.
341	And Paris struck Deiochus at the base of the shoulder from behind,
342	as he fled among the champions, and drove the bronze straight through.
343	While these were stripping them of their armor, the Achaeans meanwhile,
344	plunging into the ditch they had dug and the stakes,
345	fled this way and that, and went inside the wall under compulsion.
346	And Hector called out to the Trojans, shouting aloud:
347	“Rush on the ships, and leave the bloody spoils!
348	Whomever I see away from the ships, on the other side,
349	there and then I will devise his death, and his kinsmen
350	and kinswomen shall not give him his portion of fire when he is dead,
351	but dogs shall tear him in front of our city.”
352	So saying, he drove on the horses with the whip from the shoulder,
353	calling to the Trojans along the ranks; and with him they
354	all shouted and drove the chariot-drawing horses
355	with a wondrous din; and before them Phoebus Apollo
356	easily kicked down the banks of the deep trench with his feet
357	and threw them into the middle, and bridged a path
358	long and wide, as far as the cast of a spear
359	goes, when a man throws it to test his strength.
360	There they poured forward in battalions, and before them Apollo
361	holding the aegis, highly prized; and he threw down the wall of the Achaeans
362	very easily, as when a boy beside the sea throws down the sand,
363	who, when he has made playthings in his childishness,
364	scatters them again with his feet and hands in play:
365	so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery
366	of the Argives, and roused panic among them.
367	So they halted beside the ships and stood their ground,
368	calling to one another, and to all the gods
369	they lifted up their hands, and each man prayed aloud;
370	and above all Nestor, the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans,
371	prayed, stretching out his hands to the starry sky:
372	“Father Zeus, if ever anyone in Argos rich in wheat,
373	burning to you the fat thigh pieces of ox or sheep,
374	prayed that he might return, and you promised and nodded your assent,
375	remember these things, Olympian, and ward off the pitiless day,
376	and do not let the Achaeans be so beaten down by the Trojans.”
377	So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor thundered loudly,
378	hearing the prayers of the old son of Neleus.
379	And the Trojans, when they heard the thunder of aegis-bearing Zeus,
380	sprang on the Argives more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle.
381	And as a great wave of the wide-wayed sea
382	comes down over the sides of a ship, when it is driven on
383	by the force of the wind; for that above all swells the waves:
384	so the Trojans with a great shout went down over the wall,
385	and drove their horses in and fought at the sterns
386	hand to hand with double-pointed spears, the one side from their chariots,
387	the others from the black ships, having climbed up high,
388	with the long spears that lay ready for them on the ships,
389	for fighting at sea, jointed, clad in bronze at the tip.
390	And Patroclus, as long as the Achaeans and Trojans
391	were fighting around the wall, outside the swift ships,
392	so long sat in the hut of manly Eurypylus
393	and cheered him with talk, and on the grievous wound
394	he spread healing drugs against the black pains.
395	But when he saw them rushing at the wall,
396	the Trojans, and among the Danaans there rose shouting and rout,
397	he groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs
398	with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word:
399	“Eurypylus, I can no longer, much as you need me,
400	stay here beside you; for a great quarrel has arisen.
401	But let your attendant cheer you, and I
402	will hurry to Achilles, to urge him to fight.
403	Who knows if, with a god's help, I might stir his heart
404	by persuading him? Good is the persuasion of a companion.”
405	So he spoke, and his feet carried him off; and the Achaeans
406	awaited the Trojans steadfastly as they came on, but could not
407	push them, fewer as they were, away from the ships;
408	nor could the Trojans ever break the battalions of the Danaans
409	and force their way among the huts and the ships.
410	But as a chalk line makes a ship's timber straight
411	in the hands of a skilled carpenter, who knows
412	well all his craft by the promptings of Athena,
413	so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight;
414	and others were fighting their battle around other ships,
415	and Hector went straight at glorious Ajax.
416	The two were toiling over one ship, and could not,
417	the one drive the other off and burn the ship with fire,
418	nor the other thrust him back, since a god had brought him there.
419	Then shining Ajax struck Caletor, son of Clytius,
420	as he brought fire to the ship, in the chest with his spear.
421	He fell with a thud, and the brand dropped from his hand.
422	And when Hector saw with his eyes his cousin
423	fallen in the dust in front of the black ship,
424	he called out to the Trojans and Lycians, shouting aloud:
425	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
426	do not draw back from the battle yet, in this narrow place,
427	but save the son of Clytius, so that the Achaeans do not
428	strip him of his armor, fallen in the gathering of the ships.”
429	So saying, he threw his shining spear at Ajax.
430	He missed him, but then Lycophron, son of Mastor,
431	the attendant of Ajax, from Cythera, who lived with him,
432	since he had killed a man in holy Cythera—
433	him he struck on the head above the ear with the sharp bronze
434	as he stood near Ajax; and he fell on his back in the dust
435	from the stern of the ship to the ground, and his limbs were loosened.
436	And Ajax shuddered, and spoke to his brother:
437	“Teucer, my friend, our trusted companion has been killed,
438	the son of Mastor, who came from Cythera and lived in our house,
439	and whom we honored in our halls like our own dear parents;
440	and great-hearted Hector has killed him. Where now are your arrows
441	of swift death, and the bow that Phoebus Apollo gave you?”
442	So he spoke, and the other understood, and ran and stood beside him,
443	holding in his hand the back-bent bow and the quiver
444	that holds the arrows; and very swiftly he let fly his missiles at the Trojans.
445	And he struck Cleitus, the splendid son of Peisenor,
446	the companion of Polydamas, the noble son of Panthous,
447	as he held the reins in his hands; he was busy with the horses,
448	for he drove them where by far the most battalions were thrown into confusion,
449	to do a favor to Hector and the Trojans; but quickly upon him
450	came the evil that no one could keep from him, eager though they were.
451	For the arrow of many groans fell on his neck from behind;
452	he fell from the chariot, and the horses shied back,
453	rattling the empty chariot. And the lord was quickest to notice,
454	Polydamas, and was the first to come to meet the horses.
455	He gave them to Astynous, son of Protiaon,
456	and urged him strongly to keep them close, with his eyes on him,
457	the horses; and he himself went back and mingled with the champions.
458	And Teucer took another arrow for Hector armed in bronze,
459	and he would have stopped him from battle at the ships of the Achaeans,
460	if he had hit him while he was the best in the fighting, and taken away his life.
461	But he did not escape the shrewd mind of Zeus, who was guarding
462	Hector, and he took the triumph away from Teucer, son of Telamon,
463	for he broke the well-twisted string on his flawless bow
464	as he was drawing it against him; and it went astray elsewhere,
465	the arrow heavy with bronze, and the bow fell from his hand.
466	And Teucer shuddered, and spoke to his brother:
467	“Ah, truly the plans of our battle are being utterly cut short
468	by a god, who has knocked the bow from my hand,
469	and broken the newly twisted string that I bound on
470	this morning, so that it might bear the arrows springing thick and fast.”
471	Then great Telamonian Ajax answered him:
472	“My friend, let your bow and your thick-flying arrows
473	lie, since a god has confounded them, begrudging the Danaans;
474	but take a long spear in your hands, and a shield on your shoulder,
475	and fight the Trojans, and rouse the rest of the army.
476	Let them not, even though they have overpowered us, take without a struggle
477	the well-benched ships; let us remember the joy of battle.”
478	So he spoke, and the other put the bow away in the huts,
479	and around his shoulders he put a shield of four layers,
480	and on his mighty head he set a well-made helmet
481	with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it;
482	and he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
483	and went his way, and running very swiftly stood beside Ajax.
484	And when Hector saw that Teucer's missiles had been foiled,
485	he called out to the Trojans and Lycians, shouting aloud:
486	“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight hand to hand,
487	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage
488	along the hollow ships; for I have seen with my eyes
489	the missiles of their best man foiled by Zeus.
490	Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus,
491	both in those into whose hands he puts the higher glory
492	and in those whom he diminishes and will not defend,
493	as now he diminishes the might of the Argives, and helps us.
494	So fight at the ships in a body; and whoever of you
495	is hit or struck and meets his death and his fate,
496	let him die; it is not unseemly for him, fighting in defense of his native land,
497	to die; but his wife is safe and his children after him,
498	and his house and his allotment unharmed, if the Achaeans
499	are gone with their ships to their dear native land.”
500	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
501	And Ajax on the other side called to his companions:
502	“Shame, Argives! Now it is sure: either we perish
503	or we are saved and thrust the evil away from the ships.
504	Do you expect, if Hector of the gleaming helmet takes the ships,
505	that you will each come on foot to your native land?
506	Do you not hear him urging on his whole army,
507	Hector, who is eager to set fire to the ships?
508	Truly it is not to a dance that he bids them come, but to fight.
509	For us there is no better thought or counsel than this,
510	to mingle our hands and our might with theirs hand to hand.
511	Better to perish once and for all, or to live,
512	than to be slowly drained away in grim combat
513	like this, beside the ships, by worse men than we are.”
514	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
515	Then Hector killed Schedius, son of Perimedes,
516	leader of the Phocians, and Ajax killed Laodamas,
517	leader of the foot soldiers, the splendid son of Antenor;
518	and Polydamas slew and stripped Otus of Cyllene,
519	companion of the son of Phyleus, and a leader of the great-hearted Epeians.
520	Meges saw it and sprang at him; but he ducked under and slipped aside,
521	Polydamas; and he missed him; for Apollo
522	would not let the son of Panthous be overpowered among the champions;
523	but he stabbed Croesmus in the middle of the chest with his spear,
524	and he fell with a thud; and the other was stripping the armor from his shoulders.
525	Meanwhile Dolops sprang at him, well skilled with the spear,
526	the son of Lampus, the strongest son that Lampus,
527	son of Laomedon, fathered, well skilled in furious courage;
528	he then stabbed the middle of the shield of the son of Phyleus with his spear,
529	rushing at him from close by; but the close-fitted corselet kept it off,
530	which he wore, fitted with plates; Phyleus had once
531	brought it from Ephyra, from the river Selleis.
532	For his guest-friend, Euphetes, lord of men, had given it to him
533	to wear into war, a defense against enemy men;
534	and then too it kept destruction from his son's body.
535	And Meges, on his bronze-fitted, horsehair-crested helmet,
536	stabbed the topmost peak with his sharp spear,
537	and broke off the horsehair plume from it; and it all fell
538	to the ground in the dust, bright with fresh purple.
539	While he stood his ground and fought him, and still hoped for victory,
540	warlike Menelaus came to his defense,
541	and stood off to one side with his spear, unseen, and hit him in the shoulder from behind;
542	and the point sped through his chest in its fury,
543	pressing forward; and he sank face down.
544	The two hurried to strip the bronze-fitted armor from his shoulders;
545	but Hector called on his kinsmen,
546	all of them, and first he rebuked the son of Hicetaon,
547	mighty Melanippus. For a time he grazed shambling cattle
548	in Percote, while the enemy were still far away;
549	but when the curving ships of the Danaans came,
550	he came back to Ilios, and stood out among the Trojans,
551	and lived beside Priam; and Priam honored him as he honored his children.
552	Hector rebuked him, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
553	“Are we to hold back like this, Melanippus? Is your dear heart
554	not moved at all, now that your cousin is killed?
555	Do you not see how they are busy about the armor of Dolops?
556	Come with me; for we can no longer fight the Argives
557	from a distance, until either we kill them, or from top to bottom
558	they take steep Ilios and kill its citizens.”
559	So saying, he led the way, and the other followed, a man equal to the gods.
560	And great Telamonian Ajax urged on the Argives:
561	“Friends, be men, and set shame in your hearts,
562	and feel shame before one another in the fierce battles;
563	when men feel shame, more are saved than are killed;
564	but when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage.”
565	So he spoke, and they too were eager to defend themselves,
566	and they laid his word to heart, and fenced the ships
567	with a bulwark of bronze; but Zeus roused the Trojans against them.
568	And Menelaus, good at the war cry, urged on Antilochus:
569	“Antilochus, no other of the Achaeans is younger than you,
570	nor swifter of foot, nor as valiant as you in fighting;
571	if only you would leap out and strike some man of the Trojans.”
572	So saying, he hurried back again, and spurred him on;
573	and he leapt out from the champions and threw his shining spear,
574	glancing around him; and the Trojans shrank back
575	from the man as he threw; and he did not throw the shaft in vain,
576	but struck the son of Hicetaon, high-hearted Melanippus,
577	as he was going into the war, in the chest beside the nipple.
578	He fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes.
579	And Antilochus sprang at him like a dog that darts
580	at a wounded fawn, which, as it leapt from its lair,
581	a hunter has hit with his shot, and loosened its limbs;
582	so at you, Melanippus, Antilochus, steadfast in battle, leapt
583	to strip your armor; but he did not escape brilliant Hector's notice,
584	who came running through the combat to meet him.
585	And Antilochus did not stand his ground, swift warrior though he was,
586	but fled in fear, like a wild beast that has done some harm,
587	that has killed a dog or a herdsman beside his cattle,
588	and flees before a throng of men can gather;
589	so the son of Nestor fled in fear, and the Trojans and Hector
590	with a wondrous din poured their groaning missiles on him;
591	and he turned and stood, when he reached the crowd of his companions.
592	And the Trojans, like lions that eat raw flesh,
593	rushed upon the ships, and fulfilled the commands of Zeus,
594	who kept rousing great might in them, and bewitched the heart
595	of the Argives, and took away their glory, and spurred the others on.
596	For his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector,
597	son of Priam, so that on the curved ships he might cast wondrous fire,
598	tireless fire, and fulfill the excessive prayer of Thetis
599	in full; for Zeus the counselor was waiting for this,
600	to see with his eyes the blaze of a burning ship.
601	For from then on he was going to bring about a counterattack
602	against the Trojans from the ships, and to hold out glory to the Danaans.
603	With this in mind he roused at the hollow ships
604	Hector, son of Priam, though he was very eager himself.
605	He raged as when spear-brandishing Ares, or deadly fire,
606	rages in the mountains, in the thickets of the deep forest;
607	and foam formed around his mouth, and his two eyes
608	blazed under his grim brows, and his helmet
609	shook terribly around his temples as he fought,
610	Hector; for from the upper air he himself was his defender,
611	Zeus, who, though Hector was alone among many men,
612	honored him and gave him glory. Short-lived he was to be;
613	for already she was rousing his fated day against him,
614	Pallas Athena, under the strength of the son of Peleus.
615	And he wanted to break the ranks of men, testing them,
616	where he saw the greatest throng and the best armor;
617	but even so he could not break them, though he raged greatly;
618	for they held, fitted together in close order like a tower, like a rock,
619	lofty and great, close to the gray sea,
620	which withstands the swift paths of the shrill winds
621	and the swollen waves that belch against it:
622	so the Danaans awaited the Trojans steadfastly and did not flee.
623	But he, blazing all over with fire, leapt into the throng,
624	and fell on them as when a wave falls on a swift ship,
625	violent under the clouds, wind-nurtured; and the ship is all
626	hidden under the foam, and the dread blast of the wind
627	roars in the sail, and the sailors tremble at heart
628	in fear; for they are carried out from under death by only a little:
629	so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn.
630	But he, like a deadly lion coming upon cattle
631	that graze in the low wet ground of a great marsh,
632	countless, and among them a herdsman who does not yet well know
633	how to fight a wild beast over the slaughter of a cow with curving horns;
634	he walks always with the first and the last of the cattle,
635	keeping in step with them, but the lion leaps into the middle
636	and devours a cow, and they all flee in fear: so then the Achaeans
637	fled in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus,
638	all of them; but he killed only Periphetes of Mycenae,
639	the dear son of Copreus, who used to go for lord Eurystheus
640	with messages to mighty Heracles.
641	From him, a far worse father, was born a better son
642	in every kind of prowess, both in running and in fighting,
643	and in mind he was among the first of the Mycenaeans;
644	he it was who then put the higher glory into Hector's hands.
645	For as he turned back, he tripped on the rim of his shield,
646	which he carried himself, reaching to his feet, a barrier against javelins;
647	caught in it, he fell on his back, and his helmet
648	rang terribly around his temples as he fell.
649	But Hector was quick to see it, and ran and stood beside him,
650	and fixed his spear in his chest, and close to his dear companions
651	he killed him; and they could not, though grieving for their companion,
652	help him; for they themselves feared brilliant Hector greatly.
653	And they came face to face with the ships, and the outermost ships,
654	all those drawn up first, hemmed them in; and the others poured in.
655	And the Argives gave way from the ships, under compulsion,
656	from the first ones, but there beside the huts they stood their ground
657	all together, and did not scatter through the camp; for shame held them
658	and fear; for they called to one another ceaselessly.
659	And above all Gerenian Nestor, the guardian of the Achaeans,
660	begged each man by his parents, imploring him:
661	“Friends, be men, and set shame in your hearts
662	before other men; and let each of you remember
663	your children and your wives and your wealth and your parents,
664	both he whose parents are living and he whose parents are dead;
665	for their sake, though they are not here, I implore you
666	to stand firm, and not to turn to rout.”
667	So saying, he urged on the might and spirit of every man.
668	And from their eyes Athena thrust away the cloud of mist,
669	wondrous; and a great light came to them on both sides,
670	both toward the ships and toward the war, common to all.
671	And they saw Hector, good at the war cry, and his companions,
672	both those who stood back behind and were not fighting,
673	and those who were fighting their battle beside the swift ships.
674	Nor did it please the heart of great-hearted Ajax any longer
675	to stand where the other sons of the Achaeans had stood back;
676	but he went up and down the decks of the ships with long strides,
677	and wielded in his hands a great spear for fighting at sea,
678	joined with bands, twenty-two cubits long.
679	And as a man well skilled in riding horses,
680	who, when he has harnessed together four horses out of many,
681	drives them from the plain and speeds toward a great city
682	along the public road; and many gaze at him,
683	men and women; and he, always steadily and safely,
684	leaps from one horse to another as they fly on:
685	so Ajax went over many decks of the swift ships,
686	ranging with long strides, and his voice reached the upper air,
687	and always shouting terribly he ordered the Danaans
688	to defend the ships and the huts. Nor did Hector
689	stay in the throng of the close-armored Trojans;
690	but as a tawny eagle swoops on a tribe of winged birds
691	as they feed beside a river,
692	geese or cranes or long-necked swans,
693	so Hector made straight for a dark-prowed ship,
694	darting at it face to face; and Zeus pushed him on from behind
695	with his very great hand, and urged on the army with him.
696	And again a bitter battle was fought beside the ships;
697	you would have said that unwearied and unyielding they met one another
698	in war, so furiously did they fight.
699	And as they fought, this was their mind: the Achaeans
700	thought they would not escape from the evil, but would perish,
701	and the heart in the breast of each of the Trojans hoped
702	to set fire to the ships and kill the Achaean heroes.
703	With these thoughts they stood against one another;
704	and Hector took hold of the stern of a seafaring ship,
705	a fine, swift-sailing ship, which had brought Protesilaus
706	to Troy, and did not take him back again to his native land.
707	It was around his ship that the Achaeans and Trojans
708	were cutting one another down hand to hand; nor did they
709	stand apart and await the rush of arrows any longer, nor of javelins,
710	but standing close by, with one heart,
711	they fought with sharp axes and battle-axes
712	and with great swords and double-pointed spears.
713	And many fine swords, with black-bound hilts,
714	fell to the ground, some from the hands, some from the shoulders
715	of fighting men; and the black earth ran with blood.
716	And Hector, once he had taken hold of the stern, would not let go,
717	holding the stern ornament in his hands, and he called to the Trojans:
718	“Bring fire, and at the same time raise the war cry yourselves, in a body!
719	Now Zeus has given us a day worth all the rest,
720	to take the ships, which came here against the will of the gods
721	and brought many banes upon us, through the cowardice of the elders,
722	who, when I wanted to fight at the sterns of the ships,
723	held me back, and restrained the army;
724	but if far-seeing Zeus was harming our minds then,
725	now he himself is urging us on and commanding us.”
726	So he spoke, and they sprang more fiercely on the Argives.
727	And Ajax no longer stood his ground; for he was hard pressed by missiles;
728	but he drew back a little way, thinking he would die,
729	to the seven-foot bench, and left the deck of the balanced ship.
730	There he stood on the watch, and with his spear he always
731	kept the Trojans from the ships, whoever brought tireless fire;
732	and always shouting terribly he ordered the Danaans:
733	“Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares,
734	be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage.
735	Do we think there are helpers behind us,
736	or some stronger wall, that could ward off ruin from men?
737	There is no city near, fitted with towers,
738	where we could defend ourselves, with a people to turn the battle;
739	but here in the plain of the close-armored Trojans
740	we sit with our backs to the sea, far from our native land;
741	so in our hands lies the light, not in softness in war.”
742	He spoke, and in his fury pressed on with his sharp spear.
743	And whoever of the Trojans rushed at the hollow ships
744	with blazing fire, to do a favor to Hector who urged them on,
745	Ajax would wait for him and stab him with his long spear;
746	and twelve he stabbed hand to hand in front of the ships.
