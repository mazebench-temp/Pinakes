# Homer, Iliad, Book 23 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So they groaned throughout the city; but the Achaeans,
2	when they had reached the ships and the Hellespont,
3	they scattered, each to his own ship;
4	but Achilles would not let the Myrmidons scatter,
5	and he spoke among his war-loving companions:
6	“Myrmidons of the swift horses, my trusty companions,
7	let us not yet loose the single-hoofed horses from under the chariots,
8	but with the horses themselves and the chariots let us go near
9	and weep for Patroclus; for that is the prize of the dead.
10	And when we have taken our fill of deadly lament,
11	we will loose the horses and all take our supper here.”
12	So he spoke, and they groaned aloud in a body, and Achilles led them.
13	And three times they drove their well-maned horses around the body,
14	weeping; and Thetis roused among them the longing for lament.
15	The sands were wet, and wet was the armor of the men
16	with tears; such a master of rout did they long for.
17	And among them the son of Peleus led the unceasing lamentation,
18	laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion:
19	“Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the house of Hades;
20	for now I am fulfilling for you all that I promised before,
21	to drag Hector here and give him raw to the dogs to divide among them,
22	and before your pyre to cut the throats of twelve
23	splendid children of the Trojans, in my anger over your killing.”
24	He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector,
25	stretching him face down beside the bier of the son of Menoetius
26	in the dust. And they each took off their armor,
27	bronze, gleaming, and loosed their high-neighing horses,
28	and sat down beside the ship of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus,
29	countless men; and he gave them a funeral feast to satisfy the heart.
30	Many sleek oxen bellowed around the iron
31	as their throats were cut, and many sheep and bleating goats;
32	and many white-tusked hogs, rich with lard,
33	were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus;
34	and everywhere around the corpse the blood ran, enough to be drawn in cups.
35	But the lord, the swift-footed son of Peleus,
36	the kings of the Achaeans led to brilliant Agamemnon,
37	having won him over with difficulty, for he was angry at heart for his companion.
38	And when they reached the hut of Agamemnon,
39	at once they ordered the clear-voiced heralds
40	to set a great tripod over the fire, in the hope that they might persuade
41	the son of Peleus to wash off the clotted blood.
42	But he refused stubbornly, and swore an oath besides:
43	“No, by Zeus, who is highest and best of the gods,
44	it is not lawful that water for washing should come near my head
45	until I have laid Patroclus on the fire and heaped up a mound
46	and cut my hair, since never again will a second grief like this
47	come to my heart while I am among the living.
48	But now let us yield to the hateful feast;
49	and at dawn urge them on, Agamemnon, lord of men,
50	to bring wood, and to provide all that is fitting
51	for a dead man to have when he goes beneath the misty darkness,
52	so that the tireless fire may burn him away
53	the sooner from our eyes, and the army may turn to its tasks.”
54	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed.
55	And quickly they made ready their supper, each of them,
56	and feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast.
57	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
58	they went to lie down, each to his own hut,
59	but the son of Peleus, on the shore of the loud-roaring sea,
60	lay groaning heavily among the many Myrmidons
61	in a clear space, where the waves washed up on the beach;
62	and when sleep seized him, loosening the cares of his heart,
63	sweet sleep, poured around him (for his shining limbs were very weary
64	from chasing Hector toward windy Ilios),
65	there came to him the soul of poor Patroclus,
66	like him in all things, in stature and in the fair eyes
67	and the voice, and it wore such clothes about its body as he had worn;
68	and it stood above his head and spoke to him:
69	“You are asleep, and you have forgotten me, Achilles.
70	You were not careless of me while I lived, but you are now that I am dead.
71	Bury me as quickly as may be, so that I may pass the gates of Hades.
72	The souls keep me far away, the phantoms of the dead,
73	and they do not yet let me mingle with them beyond the river,
74	but I wander aimlessly through the wide-gated house of Hades.
75	And give me your hand, in my grief; for never again
76	shall I come back from Hades, once you have given me my portion of fire.
77	For no longer, alive, apart from our dear companions,
78	shall we sit and take counsel together; but hateful doom
79	has gaped around me, the doom allotted me even at my birth;
80	and it is your fate too, Achilles, like the gods,
81	to die beneath the wall of the wealthy Trojans.
82	And I will tell you something else, and charge you with it, if you will obey me:
83	do not lay my bones apart from yours, Achilles,
84	but together, just as we were reared in your house,
85	when Menoetius brought me, still a little boy, from Opus
86	to your house, because of a grievous killing,
87	on the day when I killed the son of Amphidamas,
88	a foolish child, not meaning to, in anger over the knucklebones;
89	then the horseman Peleus received me into his halls
90	and reared me with care and named me your attendant;
91	so too let one urn enclose the bones of us both,
92	the golden two-handled jar that your lady mother gave you.”
93	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
94	“Why, dear heart, have you come here to me,
95	and charge me with all these things one by one? I
96	will accomplish them all and obey as you bid me.
97	But stand nearer to me; let us put our arms around each other, if only for a little while,
98	and take our fill of deadly lament.”
99	So he spoke, and reached out with his own hands,
100	but did not take hold of it; and the soul went beneath the earth like smoke,
101	gibbering; and Achilles sprang up in amazement,
102	and struck his hands together, and spoke a piteous word:
103	“Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some
104	soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all;
105	for all night long the soul of poor Patroclus
106	stood over me, lamenting and weeping,
107	and charged me with each thing, and it was wondrously like him.”
108	So he spoke, and in them all he roused the longing for lament;
109	and rosy-fingered Dawn appeared to them as they wept
110	around the pitiable corpse. But powerful Agamemnon
111	urged on mules and men to bring wood
112	from all the huts; and a brave man watched over them,
113	Meriones, the attendant of manly Idomeneus.
114	And they went holding woodcutting axes in their hands
115	and well-plaited ropes; and the mules went before them.
116	Many a time they went uphill and downhill, sideways and aslant;
117	but when they reached the slopes of Ida of the many springs,
118	at once with the long-edged bronze they cut the high-leafed oaks
119	in haste; and these fell with a great crashing;
120	and then the Achaeans split them
121	and bound them behind the mules; and the mules tore up the ground with their feet,
122	longing for the plain, through the thick brushwood.
123	And all the woodcutters carried logs; for so he bade them,
124	Meriones, the attendant of manly Idomeneus.
125	And they threw them down on the shore one after another, where Achilles
126	had planned a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself.
127	But when they had thrown down the vast load of wood on every side,
128	they sat there waiting in a body. But Achilles
129	at once ordered the war-loving Myrmidons
130	to gird on their bronze, and each to yoke beneath the chariot
131	his horses; and they rose and put on their armor,
132	and mounted their chariots, fighters and charioteers,
133	the horsemen in front, and behind them followed a cloud of foot soldiers,
134	countless; and in the middle his companions carried Patroclus.
135	And they covered the whole corpse with their hair, which they cast on it
136	as they cut it off; and behind, brilliant Achilles held his head,
137	grieving; for he was sending his noble companion to Hades.
138	And when they came to the place that Achilles had pointed out to them,
139	they set him down, and quickly heaped up ample wood for him.
140	Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles thought of another thing:
141	standing apart from the pyre he cut off his fair hair,
142	which he had grown thick and long for the river Spercheius;
143	and deeply troubled he spoke, looking out over the wine-dark sea:
144	“Spercheius, it was in vain that my father Peleus prayed to you
145	that when I came home there to my dear native land
146	I would cut my hair for you and offer a holy hecatomb,
147	and would sacrifice fifty uncut rams right there
148	into your waters, where you have a precinct and a fragrant altar.
149	So the old man prayed, but you did not fulfill what he had in mind.
150	But now, since I am not going home to my dear native land,
151	I would grant my hair to the hero Patroclus to take with him.”
152	So saying he placed the hair in the hands of his dear companion,
153	and in them all he roused the longing for lament.
154	And now the light of the sun would have set on them as they mourned,
155	had not Achilles quickly stood beside Agamemnon and said:
156	“Son of Atreus, for it is your words above all that the army of the Achaeans
157	will obey; they may have their fill of lament, it is true;
158	but now dismiss them from the pyre, and bid them make ready
159	their meal; we will see to these things, we to whom most of all
160	the corpse is dear; and let the leaders stay here with us.”
161	But when Agamemnon, lord of men, heard this,
162	at once he dismissed the army to the balanced ships,
163	but those who cared for the dead stayed there and heaped up the wood,
164	and made a pyre a hundred feet this way and that,
165	and on the top of the pyre they laid the body, grieving at heart.
166	And many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns
167	they flayed before the pyre and were busy over them; and from them all
168	great-hearted Achilles took the fat and covered the corpse with it
169	from head to foot, and heaped the flayed carcasses around it.
170	And he set in it two-handled jars of honey and of ointment,
171	leaning them against the bier; and four horses with high-arched necks
172	he threw on the pyre in haste, groaning loudly.
173	The lord had nine dogs that fed at his table,
174	and he cut the throats of two of them and threw them on the pyre,
175	and twelve brave sons of the great-hearted Trojans,
176	cutting them down with the bronze; and he devised evil deeds in his mind;
177	and he let loose in it the iron might of the fire, to feed on them.
178	He groaned aloud then, and called his dear companion by name:
179	“Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the house of Hades;
180	for now I am fulfilling for you all that I promised before:
181	twelve brave sons of the great-hearted Trojans,
182	all these the fire is eating along with you; but Hector,
183	the son of Priam, I will not give to the fire to devour, but to the dogs.”
184	So he spoke, threatening; but no dogs busied themselves about him,
185	for Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, kept the dogs from him
186	by day and by night, and anointed him with rose-sweet oil,
187	ambrosial, so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him.
188	And Phoebus Apollo brought a dark cloud over him
189	from the sky down to the plain, and covered all the place
190	that the corpse lay on, lest the might of the sun should first
191	parch the skin around his sinews and limbs.
192	But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not burn;
193	then brilliant swift-footed Achilles thought of another thing:
194	standing apart from the pyre he prayed to two winds,
195	the North Wind and the West Wind, and promised them fine sacrifices;
196	and pouring many libations from a golden cup he entreated them
197	to come, so that the dead might blaze with fire as quickly as may be,
198	and the wood be quick to burn. And swiftly Iris,
199	hearing his prayers, went as messenger to the winds.
200	They were all together in the house of the stormy West Wind,
201	feasting at a banquet; and Iris came running and stood
202	on the stone threshold; and when they saw her with their eyes,
203	they all sprang up, and each called her to sit by him;
204	but she refused to sit, and spoke a word:
205	“There is no time to sit; for I am going back to the streams of Oceanus,
206	to the land of the Ethiopians, where they are offering hecatombs
207	to the immortals, so that I too may feast among them on the sacrifices.
208	But Achilles prays the North Wind and the clamorous West Wind
209	to come, and promises fine sacrifices,
210	so that you may rouse the pyre to burn, on which lies
211	Patroclus, over whom all the Achaeans groan.”
212	So saying she went away, and they rose
213	with a wondrous din, driving the clouds in confusion before them.
214	And swiftly they came to the sea to blow, and a wave rose
215	under the shrill wind; and they came to deep-soiled Troy,
216	and fell on the pyre, and the wondrous fire roared loud.
217	And all night long, together, they tossed the flame of the pyre,
218	blowing shrilly; and all night long swift Achilles,
219	taking a two-handled cup, drew wine from a golden mixing bowl
220	and poured it on the ground, and soaked the earth,
221	calling on the soul of poor Patroclus.
222	And as a father mourns as he burns the bones of his son,
223	newly married, whose death has grieved his wretched parents,
224	so Achilles mourned as he burned the bones of his companion,
225	dragging himself beside the pyre, groaning without ceasing.
226	When the morning star goes forth to herald the light over the earth,
227	the star after which saffron-robed Dawn spreads over the sea,
228	then the pyre was dying down, and the flame ceased.
229	And the winds went back again to go home
230	over the Thracian sea; and it groaned, raging with its swell.
231	And the son of Peleus, turning away from the pyre to one side,
232	lay down worn out, and sweet sleep leapt upon him;
233	and those around the son of Atreus gathered in a body;
234	and the din and the thudding of them as they came woke him,
235	and he sat up and spoke to them:
236	“Son of Atreus, and you others, the best of all the Achaeans,
237	first put out the pyre with sparkling wine,
238	all of it, as far as the might of the fire has reached; and then
239	let us gather the bones of Patroclus, son of Menoetius,
240	telling them carefully apart; and they are easy to know;
241	for he lay in the middle of the pyre, and the others apart
242	were burned at its edge, horses and men all mingled together.
243	And let us put them in a golden bowl and a double fold of fat,
244	until I myself am hidden in Hades.
245	And I do not bid you labor over a very great tomb,
246	but one just such as is fitting; and afterwards, you Achaeans,
247	make it wide and high, you who after me
248	are left behind among the many-benched ships.”
249	So he spoke, and they obeyed the swift-footed son of Peleus.
250	First they put out the pyre with sparkling wine,
251	as far as the flame had come, and the ash had fallen deep;
252	and weeping, the white bones of their gentle companion
253	they gathered into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat,
254	and laid them in the huts and covered them with a soft linen cloth;
255	and they marked out the circle of the mound and laid its foundations
256	around the pyre; and at once they heaped up the loose earth,
257	and when they had heaped up the mound they were going back. But Achilles
258	kept the army there and made them sit in a wide gathering,
259	and brought prizes out of the ships, cauldrons and tripods
260	and horses and mules and the strong heads of oxen,
261	and fair-belted women, and gray iron.
262	For the swift horsemen first he set out splendid prizes:
263	a woman to lead away, skilled in flawless handiwork,
264	and a tripod with handles, holding twenty-two measures,
265	for the first; and for the second he set a mare,
266	six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal in her womb;
267	and for the third he set out a cauldron untouched by fire,
268	a fine one, holding four measures, still white as it was;
269	and for the fourth he set two talents of gold,
270	and for the fifth he set a two-handled bowl untouched by fire.
271	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
272	“Son of Atreus, and you other well-greaved Achaeans,
273	these prizes lie in the gathering, awaiting the horsemen.
274	If now we Achaeans were holding games in honor of another,
275	truly I would take the first prize and carry it to my hut.
276	For you know how far my two horses surpass the rest in prowess;
277	for they are immortal, and Poseidon gave them
278	to my father Peleus, and he in turn put them into my hands.
279	But I for my part will stay here, and my single-hoofed horses with me;
280	for they have lost the noble fame of such a charioteer,
281	a kind one, who very often poured liquid olive oil
282	on their manes, after washing them with clear water.
283	Him the two stand and mourn, and their manes
284	rest on the ground, and they stand grieving at heart.
285	But you others make ready throughout the army, whoever of the Achaeans
286	trusts in his horses and his well-joined chariot.”
287	So spoke the son of Peleus, and the swift horsemen gathered.
288	First by far rose Eumelus, lord of men,
289	the dear son of Admetus, who excelled in horsemanship;
290	and after him rose the son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes,
291	and he led under the yoke the horses of Tros, of which he had once robbed
292	Aeneas, though Apollo saved the man himself.
293	And after him rose the son of Atreus, fair-haired Menelaus,
294	sprung from Zeus, and he led under the yoke the swift horses,
295	Aethe, Agamemnon's mare, and his own Podargus;
296	her Echepolus, son of Anchises, had given to Agamemnon
297	as a gift, so that he might not have to follow him to windy Ilios,
298	but might stay at home and take his pleasure; for great wealth
299	had Zeus given him, and he lived in Sicyon with its wide dancing floors;
300	her he led under the yoke, straining hard for the race.
301	And Antilochus, fourth, made ready his well-maned horses,
302	the splendid son of Nestor, the high-hearted lord,
303	the son of Neleus; and horses born in Pylos,
304	swift-footed, drew his chariot; and his father, standing close beside him,
305	spoke to him for his good, a wise man to one who understood it himself:
306	“Antilochus, truly, young as you are, they have loved you,
307	Zeus and Poseidon, and have taught you horsemanship
308	of every kind; so there is no great need to teach you;
309	for you know well how to wheel around the turning posts; but your horses
310	are the slowest at running; so I think there will be ruin.
311	The others' horses are swifter, but they themselves
312	do not know how to devise more than you yourself.
313	But come, my friend, put cunning into your heart,
314	cunning of every kind, so that the prizes do not slip past you.
315	By cunning, far more than by strength, is a woodcutter better;
316	by cunning again a helmsman on the wine-dark sea
317	steers a swift ship buffeted by the winds;
318	by cunning one charioteer gets the better of another.
319	But he who, trusting in his horses and his chariot,
320	wheels heedlessly wide, this way and that,
321	his horses wander over the course, and he does not hold them in;
322	but he who knows crafty ways, though he drives worse horses,
323	always watches the turning post and turns close, and does not forget
324	how at first to hold them at full stretch with the oxhide straps,
325	but keeps them steady and watches the man in front.
326	And I will tell you a sign, very easy to see, and it will not escape you.
327	There stands a dry stump, about a fathom above the ground,
328	of oak or of pine; it does not rot in the rain,
329	and two white stones lean against it on either side
330	at the narrowing of the road, and the course is smooth all around;
331	it is either the mound of some mortal who died long ago,
332	or it was set as a post in the time of earlier men,
333	and now brilliant swift-footed Achilles has made it the turning post.
334	Pressing close to it, drive your chariot and horses near,
335	and lean yourself in the well-woven chariot
336	a little to the left of them; but the right-hand horse
337	goad on with a shout, and let the reins go with your hands.
338	And at the post let your left-hand horse press close,
339	so that the hub may seem to graze its edge,
340	the hub of the well-made wheel; but avoid touching the stone,
341	lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot;
342	that would be a joy to the others, and a disgrace to yourself;
343	but, my friend, be wise and be on your guard.
344	For if at the post you drive past them in pursuit,
345	there is no one who could spring after you and catch you, or pass you,
346	not even if he drove brilliant Arion behind you,
347	the swift horse of Adrastus, who was of the stock of the gods,
348	or the horses of Laomedon, the good ones that were reared here.”
349	So saying, Nestor, son of Neleus, sat down again in his place,
350	since he had told his son the sum of each thing.
351	And Meriones, fifth, made ready his well-maned horses.
352	And they mounted their chariots and cast in their lots;
353	Achilles shook them, and out leapt the lot of the son of Nestor,
354	Antilochus; after him powerful Eumelus drew his place;
355	and after him the son of Atreus, Menelaus famed for his spear,
356	and after him Meriones drew the lot to drive; and last
357	the son of Tydeus, by far the best, drew the lot to drive his horses.
358	And they stood in a row, and Achilles pointed out the turning post
359	far off on the smooth plain; and beside it he set as a lookout
360	godlike Phoenix, his father's squire,
361	so that he might mark the running and report the truth.
362	And all together they raised their whips over their horses,
363	and struck them with the straps, and shouted at them with words,
364	eagerly; and quickly they crossed the plain,
365	swiftly, away from the ships; and beneath their chests the dust
366	rose and stood like a cloud or a storm,
367	and their manes streamed in the blasts of the wind.
368	And the chariots at one moment skimmed the bountiful earth,
369	and at another bounded high in the air; and the drivers
370	stood in the chariots, and the heart of each was pounding
371	as they strove for victory; and each called to his own
372	horses, and they flew on, raising the dust of the plain.
373	But when the swift horses were finishing the last stretch
374	back toward the gray sea, then the prowess of each
375	showed itself, and at once the horses' running was strained to the full; and quickly then
376	the swift-footed mares of the grandson of Pheres took the lead.
377	And after them the stallions of Diomedes drew out ahead,
378	the horses of Tros, and they were not far behind, but very close;
379	for they always seemed about to mount the chariot,
380	and with their breath Eumelus' back and broad shoulders
381	grew warm; for they flew with their heads laid on him.
382	And now he would have passed him or left the race in doubt,
383	had not Phoebus Apollo borne a grudge against the son of Tydeus,
384	and struck the shining whip from his hands.
385	And tears poured from his eyes in his anger,
386	because he saw the mares going on still faster,
387	while his own were hindered, running without the goad.
388	Nor did Apollo escape Athena's notice as he cheated
389	the son of Tydeus, and very quickly she sped after the shepherd of the people,
390	and gave him the whip, and put might into his horses;
391	and she went after the son of Admetus in resentment,
392	and the goddess broke his horses' yoke; and his mares
393	ran apart, off the road, and the pole was dashed to the ground.
394	And he himself tumbled out of the chariot beside the wheel,
395	and the skin was torn from his elbows and mouth and nose,
396	and his forehead above the brows was bruised; and his two eyes
397	filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked.
398	And the son of Tydeus turned aside and drove his single-hoofed horses past,
399	leaping far out ahead of the others; for Athena
400	put might into his horses and set glory on him.
401	And after him drove the son of Atreus, fair-haired Menelaus.
402	And Antilochus called to the horses of his father:
403	“You two as well, step out! Go at full stretch, as fast as you can.
404	Those over there I do not bid you strive against at all,
405	the horses of the wise-hearted son of Tydeus, to which Athena
406	has now held out speed, and has set glory on him;
407	but catch the horses of the son of Atreus, and do not be left behind,
408	and quickly, so that she does not pour disgrace on you two,
409	Aethe, female though she is. Why are you lagging, good sirs?
410	For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass:
411	there will be no keep for you two with Nestor, shepherd of the people,
412	but he will kill you at once with the sharp bronze,
413	if you grow slack and we carry off a worse prize.
414	But follow hard and hurry as fast as you can;
415	and this I myself will contrive and devise,
416	to slip past him in the narrow road, and it will not escape me.”
417	So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke,
418	ran harder for a little while; and quickly then
419	Antilochus, steadfast in battle, saw a narrow place in the hollow road.
420	There was a rift in the ground, where winter water, pent up,
421	had broken away part of the road and deepened the whole place;
422	there Menelaus was driving, shunning a clash of wheels.
423	But Antilochus turned aside and drove his single-hoofed horses
424	off the road, and swerving a little he pressed on after him.
425	And the son of Atreus was afraid and called out to Antilochus:
426	“Antilochus, you drive heedlessly; hold in your horses!
427	For the road is narrow; soon it is wider for driving past;
428	or you may run into my chariot and bring us both to harm.”
429	So he spoke, but Antilochus drove on still harder, much harder,
430	urging them with the goad, as if he had not heard.
431	As far as the range of a discus swung from the shoulder carries,
432	which a man in his prime lets fly, testing his young strength,
433	so far they ran ahead; and they hung back behind,
434	the mares of the son of Atreus; for he himself of his own will let up on the driving,
435	lest the single-hoofed horses collide in the road
436	and overturn the well-woven chariots, and they themselves
437	fall in the dust in their eagerness for victory.
438	And fair-haired Menelaus spoke to him, rebuking him:
439	“Antilochus, no other mortal is more baneful than you.
440	Off with you! We Achaeans were wrong to say that you were wise.
441	But not even so will you carry off the prize without an oath.”
442	So saying, he called to his horses and spoke:
443	“Do not hold back, do not stand still, grieving at heart.
444	Their feet and knees will grow weary sooner
445	than yours; for both of them are lacking in youth.”
446	So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke,
447	ran harder, and soon they came close to the others.
448	And the Argives, sitting in the gathering, were watching
449	the horses; and they flew on, raising the dust of the plain.
450	And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, was the first to make out the horses;
451	for he sat outside the gathering, highest of all, at a lookout.
452	And hearing the man who shouted at them, far off as he was,
453	he knew him; and he made out a horse, conspicuous, in the lead,
454	which was bay in all the rest, but on its forehead
455	there was a white mark, round like the moon.
456	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
457	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
458	do I alone discern the horses, or do you as well?
459	Other horses seem to me to be in front,
460	and another charioteer appears; and the mares must have come to grief
461	somewhere there on the plain, that were the better going out.
462	For I saw them first swinging around the turning post,
463	but now I cannot see them anywhere; and everywhere my eyes
464	search across the Trojan plain as I look.
465	Or did the reins escape the charioteer, and he could not
466	hold them well around the turning post, and failed to wheel?
467	There, I think, he was thrown out and broke his chariot,
468	and the mares swerved off, when fury seized their hearts.
469	But stand up and look, you too; for I
470	cannot tell them apart well; but the man seems to me to be
471	an Aetolian by lineage, and he is lord among the Argives,
472	the son of Tydeus, tamer of horses, mighty Diomedes.”
473	And swift Ajax, son of Oileus, rebuked him shamefully:
474	“Idomeneus, why do you bluster as you always have? The mares are far off,
475	the high-stepping mares, speeding over the wide plain.
476	You are not by so much the youngest among the Argives,
477	nor are the eyes that look out of your head the sharpest;
478	but you are always blustering with words; and you have no need
479	to be a blusterer; for there are others here, better men.
480	The mares in front are the same that were in front before,
481	those of Eumelus, and he stands in the chariot holding the reins.”
482	Then in anger the leader of the Cretans answered him face to face:
483	“Ajax, best in quarreling, ill-counseling, in all else
484	you fall short of the Argives, since your mind is harsh.
485	Come here now, let us wager a tripod or a cauldron,
486	and let us both make Agamemnon, son of Atreus, the arbiter
487	of whose horses are in front, so that you may learn by paying.”
488	So he spoke, and at once swift Ajax, son of Oileus, rose
489	in anger, to answer him with harsh words;
490	and now the strife between the two would have gone further still,
491	had not Achilles himself stood up and spoken:
492	“No longer now answer each other with harsh words,
493	Ajax and Idomeneus, with evil words, since it is not fitting.
494	You would be indignant with another man who did such things.
495	But sit in the gathering and watch
496	for the horses; they themselves will soon, in their eagerness for victory,
497	come here; and then you will each know
498	the horses of the Argives, which are second and which in front.”
499	So he spoke, and the son of Tydeus came on very close, driving hard,
500	and he kept driving them with the whip from the shoulder; and his horses
501	lifted themselves high as they swiftly made their way.
502	And the drops of dust kept striking the charioteer,
503	and the chariot, overlaid with gold and tin,
504	ran close behind the swift-footed horses; and there was no deep
505	track of the tires left behind
506	in the fine dust; and the two flew on in their haste.
507	And he stopped in the middle of the gathering, and much sweat poured
508	from the horses, from their necks and from their chests to the ground.
509	And he himself leapt to the ground from the all-gleaming chariot,
510	and leaned the whip against the yoke; nor did he dally,
511	mighty Sthenelus, but quickly took the prize,
512	and gave the woman to his high-hearted companions to lead away
513	and the tripod with handles to carry; and he unyoked the horses.
514	And after him Antilochus, of the line of Neleus, drove in his horses,
515	by crafty ways, not by speed, having got in ahead of Menelaus;
516	but even so Menelaus kept his swift horses close.
517	As far as a horse is from the wheel, a horse that draws its master
518	over the plain at full stretch with the chariot,
519	and the tips of its hairs touch the tire,
520	the hairs of its tail; and the wheel runs very close, and there is no great
521	space between, as it runs over the wide plain;
522	so far was Menelaus behind noble Antilochus.
523	At first, it is true, he was left behind as far as the range of a discus,
524	but he quickly caught up with him; for the noble might was growing
525	of Agamemnon's mare, fine-maned Aethe;
526	and if the race had gone on still further for the two,
527	he would have passed him and not left it in doubt.
528	But Meriones, the good attendant of Idomeneus,
529	was behind famous Menelaus by the cast of a spear;
530	for his fine-maned horses were the slowest,
531	and he himself was the least skilled at driving a chariot in a contest.
532	And the son of Admetus came in last of all the others,
533	dragging his fine chariot, driving his horses in front of him.
534	And seeing him, brilliant swift-footed Achilles pitied him,
535	and standing among the Argives he spoke winged words:
536	“The best man is driving in his single-hoofed horses last.
537	But come, let us give him a prize, as is fitting,
538	the second; and let the son of Tydeus carry off the first.”
539	So he spoke, and they all approved what he bade.
540	And now he would have given him the mare, for the Achaeans approved,
541	had not Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor,
542	stood up and answered Achilles, son of Peleus, claiming his right:
543	“Achilles, I will be very angry with you if you accomplish
544	this word; for you mean to take away my prize,
545	thinking that his chariot and swift horses came to grief,
546	and he himself, good as he is; but he should have prayed
547	to the immortals; then he would not have come in last of all, driving.
548	But if you pity him and he is dear to your heart,
549	there is much gold in your hut, and there is bronze
550	and flocks, and there are slave women and single-hoofed horses;
551	take from these and give him afterward an even greater prize,
552	or even at once, now, so that the Achaeans may praise you.
553	But her I will not give up; for her let any man make trial
554	who is willing to fight with me hand to hand.”
555	So he spoke, and brilliant swift-footed Achilles smiled,
556	rejoicing in Antilochus, since he was his dear companion;
557	and answering him he addressed him with winged words:
558	“Antilochus, if you bid me bring something else from my store
559	and give it to Eumelus besides, I will do that too.
560	I will give him the corselet that I took from Asteropaeus,
561	of bronze, around which a casting of shining tin
562	runs in a circle; it will be worth much to him.”
563	He spoke, and bade Automedon, his dear companion,
564	bring it from the hut; and he went and brought it for him,
565	and put it in Eumelus' hands; and he received it with joy.
566	And among them Menelaus too stood up, grieving in his heart,
567	angry without end at Antilochus; and a herald
568	put the scepter in his hand, and bade the Argives
569	be silent; and then the man equal to the gods spoke among them:
570	“Antilochus, who were wise before, what a thing you have done!
571	You have shamed my prowess, and hindered my horses
572	by throwing your own in front, which were far worse than mine.
573	But come, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
574	judge between the two of us impartially, and not to help one,
575	lest someday one of the bronze-clad Achaeans say:
576	‘Menelaus overpowered Antilochus with lies,
577	and has gone off leading the mare, because his horses were far worse,
578	but he himself was the greater in prowess and strength.’
579	But come, I myself will judge, and I say that no other
580	of the Danaans will rebuke me; for the judgment will be straight.
581	Antilochus, come here, nurtured by Zeus, as is the custom;
582	stand in front of your horses and chariot, and hold the whip
583	in your hands, the slender one with which you drove before,
584	and touching the horses, by the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
585	swear that you did not willingly hamper my chariot by a trick.”
586	Then wise Antilochus answered him face to face:
587	“Bear with me now; for I am much younger
588	than you, lord Menelaus, and you are the elder and the better.
589	You know what the transgressions of a young man are like;
590	for his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin.
591	So let your heart be patient; and the mare I myself
592	will give you, the one I won. And if you should ask for something else from my store,
593	something greater, I would rather give it to you at once
594	than, nurtured by Zeus, for all my days
595	fall from your heart and be an offender against the gods.”
596	He spoke, and leading the mare the son of great-hearted Nestor
597	put her in the hands of Menelaus; and his heart
598	was warmed, as when there is dew around the ears
599	of a growing crop, when the fields are bristling;
600	so, Menelaus, was the spirit in your heart warmed.
601	And he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
602	“Antilochus, now I myself will give way to you,
603	for all my anger, since you were never flighty or light of mind
604	before; but this time youth has overcome your mind.
605	Another time, avoid deceiving your betters.
606	For no other man of the Achaeans would have won me over so soon.
607	But you have suffered much and toiled much,
608	you and your brave father and your brother, for my sake;
609	so I will yield to you, since you beg me, and the mare as well
610	I will give you, though she is mine, so that these men too may know
611	that my heart is never overbearing and harsh.”
612	He spoke, and to Noemon, the companion of Antilochus, he gave
613	the mare to lead away; and then he took the all-gleaming cauldron.
614	And Meriones took up the two talents of gold,
615	fourth, as he had driven in. And the fifth prize was left,
616	the two-handled bowl; Achilles gave it to Nestor,
617	carrying it through the gathering of the Argives, and stood beside him and said:
618	“Here now, old man, let this be a treasure for you too,
619	to be a memorial of the funeral of Patroclus; for you will not see him
620	among the Argives any more. And I give you this prize
621	just as it is; for you will not fight with fists, nor wrestle,
622	nor enter the javelin throw any more, nor run
623	with your feet; for already grievous old age presses upon you.”
624	So saying, he put it in his hands; and he received it with joy,
625	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
626	“Yes, my child, all that you have said is fair and right.
627	For my limbs are no longer steady, my friend, nor my feet, nor do my arms
628	dart out lightly from my shoulders on either side.
629	If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast,
630	as when the Epeians were burying powerful Amarynceus
631	at Buprasium, and his sons set out prizes in honor of the king!
632	There no man was a match for me, neither of the Epeians
633	nor of the Pylians themselves nor of the great-hearted Aetolians.
634	In boxing I defeated Clytomedes, son of Enops,
635	and in wrestling Ancaeus of Pleuron, who stood up against me;
636	Iphiclus I outran in the footrace, good as he was,
637	and with the spear I outthrew Phyleus and Polydorus.
638	Only with horses did the two sons of Actor drive past me,
639	getting in front by their numbers, grudging me the victory,
640	because the greatest prizes were left there.
641	They were twins: the one held the reins steadily,
642	held the reins steadily, and the other urged them on with the whip.
643	So I was once; but now let younger men take on
644	such deeds; and I must yield to grievous old age,
645	but then I stood out among heroes.
646	But go, and likewise honor your companion with funeral games.
647	This I accept gladly, and my heart rejoices
648	that you always remember me, a gentle friend, and I am not forgotten by you,
649	nor the honor with which it is fitting that I be honored among the Achaeans.
650	And for this may the gods give you thanks in return, to satisfy your heart.”
651	So he spoke, and the son of Peleus went through the great throng of the Achaeans,
652	when he had listened to all the praise of the son of Neleus.
653	Then he set out prizes for the painful boxing:
654	he led in a hard-working mule and tethered it in the gathering,
655	six years old, unbroken, the hardest to tame;
656	and for the loser he set out a two-handled cup.
657	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
658	“Son of Atreus, and you other well-greaved Achaeans,
659	for these we bid two men, the very best,
660	to put up their fists and strike hard; and the one whom Apollo
661	grants to outlast the other, and all the Achaeans know it,
662	let him lead the hard-working mule and go back to his hut;
663	and the loser shall carry off the two-handled cup.”
664	So he spoke, and at once a man rose, tall and brave,
665	skilled in boxing, the son of Panopeus, Epeius;
666	and he laid hold of the hard-working mule and spoke:
667	“Let him come near, whoever will carry off the two-handled cup;
668	but the mule, I say, no other of the Achaeans will lead away
669	by winning at boxing, since I claim to be the best.
670	Is it not enough that I fall short in battle? It is not possible, after all,
671	for a man to be skilled in every kind of work.
672	For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass:
673	I will tear his flesh right through and crush his bones.
674	Let those who care for him stay here in a body,
675	to carry him out when he is brought down beneath my hands.”
676	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence.
677	Euryalus alone stood up against him, a man equal to the gods,
678	son of lord Mecisteus, son of Talaus,
679	who once came to Thebes, when Oedipus had fallen,
680	for his funeral; and there he defeated all the Cadmeians.
681	And the son of Tydeus, famed for his spear, busied himself about him,
682	encouraging him with words, and he greatly wished him victory.
683	First he put the loincloth about him, and then
684	he gave him the well-cut straps of an ox of the fields.
685	And the two, having girded themselves, went into the middle of the gathering,
686	and raising their strong hands against each other, both at once,
687	they fell to, and their heavy hands were mingled together.
688	And there was a terrible grinding of jaws, and sweat flowed
689	from all their limbs; and brilliant Epeius rushed on,
690	and struck him on the cheek as he peered about; and he did not
691	stand long; for there his shining limbs gave way beneath him.
692	And as when under the ripple of the North Wind a fish leaps up
693	on a shore full of seaweed, and a dark wave hides it,
694	so he leapt up when he was struck; but great-hearted Epeius
695	took him in his hands and set him upright; and his dear companions stood around him,
696	who led him through the gathering with trailing feet,
697	spitting thick blood, his head lolling to one side;
698	and they led him and set him down among them, his wits wandering,
699	and they themselves went and fetched the two-handled cup.
700	And at once the son of Peleus set out other prizes, a third set,
701	showing them to the Danaans, for the painful wrestling:
702	for the winner a great tripod to stand on the fire,
703	which the Achaeans valued among themselves at twelve oxen;
704	and for the man who lost he set a woman in their midst,
705	and she was skilled in much handiwork, and they valued her at four oxen.
706	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
707	“Rise up, you two who will make trial of this prize too.”
708	So he spoke, and then great Telamonian Ajax rose,
709	and up stood Odysseus of many wiles, who knew crafty ways.
710	And the two, having girded themselves, went into the middle of the gathering,
711	and took hold of each other in their arms with strong hands,
712	like rafters that a famous craftsman has fitted together
713	of a high house, keeping out the force of the winds.
714	And their backs creaked under bold hands,
715	tugged hard; and the wet sweat ran down,
716	and thick welts along their ribs and shoulders
717	rose up, crimson with blood; and they kept on, always
718	striving for victory, for the well-made tripod;
719	neither could Odysseus trip him and bring him to the ground,
720	nor could Ajax, but the strong force of Odysseus held.
721	But when they began to vex the well-greaved Achaeans,
722	then great Telamonian Ajax said to him:
723	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
724	either lift me, or I you; and all the rest will be the business of Zeus.”
725	So saying, he lifted him; but Odysseus did not forget his trickery:
726	he struck the hollow of his knee from behind, and loosened his limbs,
727	and threw him down backward; and on his chest Odysseus
728	fell; and the people gazed and were amazed.
729	Then second in his turn much-enduring brilliant Odysseus tried to lift him,
730	and moved him a little from the ground, but could not lift him,
731	but hooked his knee in; and both fell on the ground
732	close to each other, and were stained with dust.
733	And now they would have sprung up again and wrestled a third time,
734	had not Achilles himself stood up and held them back:
735	“Strain no longer, and do not wear yourselves out with hurt;
736	victory is for both; take equal prizes
737	and go, so that the other Achaeans too may compete.”
738	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed,
739	and wiping off the dust they put on their tunics.
740	And at once the son of Peleus set out other prizes, for speed:
741	a mixing bowl of silver, well wrought; it held six measures,
742	and in beauty it surpassed all others on the whole earth
743	by far, since Sidonians, skilled in intricate work, had worked it finely,
744	and Phoenician men brought it over the misty sea
745	and set it out in harbors, and gave it as a gift to Thoas;
746	and as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam, it was given
747	to the hero Patroclus by Euneus, son of Jason.
748	This too Achilles set as a prize in honor of his companion,
749	for whoever should prove the nimblest with his swift feet;
750	for the second in turn he set an ox, great and rich with fat,
751	and he set half a talent of gold for the last.
752	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
753	“Rise up, you who will make trial of this prize too.”
754	So he spoke, and at once swift Ajax, son of Oileus, rose,
755	and Odysseus of many wiles, and then the son of Nestor,
756	Antilochus; for he, for his part, beat all the young men in running.
757	And they stood in a row; and Achilles pointed out the turning post.
758	For them the running was strained to the full from the post; and quickly then
759	the son of Oileus took the lead; and brilliant Odysseus rushed on,
760	very close, as close as to a fair-belted woman's
761	breast is the rod, when she pulls it very skillfully with her hands,
762	drawing the spool out past the warp, and holds it near
763	her breast; so close Odysseus ran, and behind
764	he trod in his footprints before the dust could settle around them;
765	and brilliant Odysseus poured his breath on his head,
766	running swiftly all the while; and all the Achaeans shouted
767	for him as he strove for victory, and cheered him on as he sped.
768	But when they were finishing the last stretch, at once Odysseus
769	prayed in his heart to gray-eyed Athena:
770	“Hear me, goddess, come as a good helper to my feet.”
771	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him,
772	and she made his limbs light, his feet and his hands above.
773	But when they were just about to dash in upon the prize,
774	there Ajax slipped as he ran, for Athena hindered him,
775	where the dung was spread from the loud-bellowing oxen that were slain,
776	which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus;
777	and his mouth and nostrils were filled with the dung of oxen;
778	and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus took up the mixing bowl,
779	since he came in first; and shining Ajax took the ox.
780	And he stood holding in his hands the horn of the ox of the fields,
781	spitting out the dung, and spoke among the Argives:
782	“Ah, truly the goddess hindered my feet, she who always before
783	stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him.”
784	So he spoke, and they all laughed heartily at him.
785	And Antilochus carried off the last prize,
786	smiling, and spoke among the Argives:
787	“You all know it, friends, but I will tell you: that still, even now,
788	the immortals honor older men.
789	For Ajax is only a little older than I,
790	but this man is of an earlier generation and of earlier men;
791	they say he is in green old age; and it is hard
792	for the Achaeans to contend with him in running, except for Achilles.”
793	So he spoke, and gave glory to the swift-footed son of Peleus.
794	And answering him with these words, Achilles said:
795	“Antilochus, your praise shall not be spoken in vain,
796	but I will add half a talent of gold for you.”
797	So saying, he put it in his hands, and he received it with joy.
798	Then the son of Peleus brought a long-shadowed spear
799	and set it down in the gathering, and a shield and a helmet,
800	the armor of Sarpedon, which Patroclus had taken from him.
801	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
802	“For these we bid two men, the very best,
803	to put on their armor and take the flesh-cutting bronze
804	and make trial of each other in front of the throng.
805	Whichever of them is first to reach the other's fair flesh
806	and touch his inward parts through the armor and the black blood,
807	to him I will give this silver-studded sword,
808	a fine Thracian one, which I took from Asteropaeus;
809	and let both of them carry off this armor in common;
810	and we will set a good feast before them in the huts.”
811	So he spoke, and then great Telamonian Ajax rose,
812	and up rose the son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes.
813	And when they had armed on either side of the throng,
814	the two came together into the middle, eager to fight,
815	glaring terribly; and amazement held all the Achaeans.
816	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
817	three times they sprang at each other, and three times they rushed in close.
818	Then Ajax stabbed at his shield, balanced on every side,
819	but did not reach the flesh; for the corselet within protected him;
820	and the son of Tydeus then, over the great shield,
821	kept reaching his neck with the point of his shining spear.
822	And then the Achaeans, terribly afraid for Ajax,
823	bade them stop and take equal prizes.
824	But the hero gave the great sword to the son of Tydeus,
825	bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut strap.
826	Then the son of Peleus set out a lump of iron, rough as it was cast,
827	which once the great strength of Eetion used to hurl;
828	but him brilliant swift-footed Achilles killed,
829	and brought the lump away in his ships with the rest of the possessions.
830	And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives:
831	“Rise up, you who will make trial of this prize too.
832	Even if the winner's rich fields lie very far away,
833	he will have it to use for five circling years;
834	for not for lack of iron will his shepherd
835	or his plowman go to the city, but this will supply it.”
836	So he spoke, and then Polypoetes, steadfast in battle, rose,
837	and up rose the strong might of godlike Leonteus,
838	and up rose Ajax, son of Telamon, and brilliant Epeius.
839	And they stood in order, and brilliant Epeius took the lump,
840	and whirled it and threw; and at this all the Achaeans laughed.
841	Then second in his turn Leonteus, offshoot of Ares, let it fly;
842	and third great Telamonian Ajax hurled it
843	from his strong hand, and overshot the marks of all.
844	But when Polypoetes, steadfast in battle, took the lump,
845	as far as a herdsman hurls his throwing stick,
846	and it flies spinning through the cattle of the herd,
847	so far did he overshoot the whole gathering; and they shouted.
848	And the companions of mighty Polypoetes stood up
849	and carried the king's prize to the hollow ships.
850	Then for the archers he set out dark iron:
851	he set down ten double axes and ten single axes,
852	and he set up the mast of a dark-prowed ship
853	far off on the sand, and to it he tied a timid dove
854	by the foot with a thin cord, and he bade them
855	shoot at it: “Whoever hits the timid dove,
856	let him take up all the double axes and carry them home;
857	and whoever hits the cord, missing the bird,
858	since he is the lesser, he shall carry off the single axes.”
859	So he spoke, and then mighty lord Teucer rose,
860	and up rose Meriones, the good attendant of Idomeneus.
861	And they took the lots and shook them in a bronze-fitted helmet,
862	and Teucer drew the first lot; and at once he shot an arrow
863	with force, but did not promise the lord
864	that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs.
865	He missed the bird; for Apollo begrudged him that;
866	but he hit the cord beside its foot, where the bird was tied;
867	and the bitter arrow cut the cord clean through.
868	Then the bird darted up toward the sky, and the cord
869	hung loose toward the ground; and the Achaeans shouted in applause.
870	Then Meriones in haste snatched the bow from his hand;
871	but he had long been holding an arrow, while the other aimed.
872	And at once he promised far-shooting Apollo
873	that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs.
874	And high up under the clouds he saw the timid dove;
875	and there, as she circled, he struck her under the wing, in the middle,
876	and the shaft went straight through; and it fell back to the ground
877	and stuck in front of the foot of Meriones; and the bird,
878	settling on the mast of the dark-prowed ship,
879	let her neck hang down, and her thick feathers drooped.
880	And swiftly the spirit flew from her limbs, and far from him
881	she fell; and the people gazed and were amazed.
882	And Meriones took up all ten double axes,
883	and Teucer carried the single axes to the hollow ships.
884	Then the son of Peleus brought a long-shadowed spear
885	and a cauldron untouched by fire, worth an ox, flowered,

889	And among them spoke also brilliant swift-footed Achilles:
890	“Son of Atreus, for we know how far you are ahead of all,
891	and how far you are the best in strength and in throwing,
892	take this prize and go to the hollow ships,
893	and let us give the spear to the hero Meriones,
894	if you would be willing in your heart; for so I bid.”
895	So he spoke, and Agamemnon, lord of men, did not disobey;
896	and he gave Meriones the bronze spear; but the hero
897	gave the beautiful prize to the herald Talthybius.
