# Homer, Iliad, Book 14 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	The shouting did not escape Nestor, though he was drinking,
2	and he addressed the son of Asclepius with winged words:
3	“Consider, brilliant Machaon, how these things are to be;
4	the cry of the young men in their prime is louder now beside the ships.
5	But you, for now, sit and drink the sparkling wine,
6	until Hecamede of the lovely tresses heats a warm bath
7	for you and washes away the clotted blood;
8	and I will go to a lookout and quickly find out.”
9	So saying, he took the wrought shield of his son
10	that lay in the hut of Thrasymedes, tamer of horses,
11	shining with bronze; and the son had his father's shield.
12	And he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
13	and stood outside the hut, and at once he saw a shameful thing:
14	the one side in turmoil, and the others driving them in confusion from behind,
15	the high-hearted Trojans; and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down.
16	As when the great open sea heaves with a soundless swell,
17	sensing the swift paths of the shrill winds,
18	aimlessly, and does not roll forward to either side
19	until some settled wind comes down from Zeus,
20	so the old man pondered, torn in his heart
21	two ways: whether to go into the throng of the Danaans of the swift horses,
22	or to the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people.
23	And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way:
24	to go to the son of Atreus. And they kept killing one another,
25	fighting; and the unyielding bronze rang about their bodies
26	as they stabbed at one another with swords and double-pointed spears.
27	And the kings whom Zeus has nurtured met Nestor
28	as they came up from beside the ships, all who had been hit by the bronze:
29	the son of Tydeus, and Odysseus, and the son of Atreus, Agamemnon.
30	For the ships were drawn up far away from the battle,
31	on the shore of the gray sea; for the first of them they had hauled up toward the plain,
32	and built the wall along their sterns.
33	For wide as it was, there was not room for all
34	the ships on the beach, and the army was crowded together;
35	so they hauled them up in rows, and filled the long mouth of the whole
36	shore, as much as the headlands shut in between them.
37	So they came on, eager to look on the war cry and the war,
38	leaning on their spears, all together; and grieved
39	was the heart in their breasts. And the old man met them,
40	Nestor, and made the heart cower in the breasts of the Achaeans.
41	And powerful Agamemnon spoke and said to him:
42	“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
43	why have you left man-destroying war and come here?
44	I fear that mighty Hector may make good his word to me,
45	as he once threatened, speaking among the Trojans,
46	that he would not go back from the ships to Ilios
47	before he had set fire to the ships and killed the men themselves.
48	So he spoke then; and now it is all being fulfilled.
49	Ah, truly the other well-greaved Achaeans too
50	are laying up anger against me in their hearts, just as Achilles is,
51	and are not willing to fight at the sterns of the ships.”
52	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
53	“Truly, these things are here and done, and not otherwise
54	could Zeus himself, who thunders on high, have fashioned them.
55	For the wall has been thrown down, which we trusted
56	would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves;
57	and they keep up a relentless battle at the swift ships
58	without rest; nor could you tell any longer, however closely you looked,
59	on which side the Achaeans are driven in turmoil and confusion,
60	so mingled together are they being killed; and the shout reaches the sky.
61	So let us consider how these things are to be,
62	if the mind can do any good; but I do not bid us enter
63	the war; for a man who has been hit cannot fight.”
64	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to him in turn:
65	“Nestor, since they are fighting at the sterns of the ships,
66	and the wall we built has been no help, nor the ditch at all,
67	for which the Danaans suffered much, and hoped in their hearts
68	that it would be an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for themselves,
69	such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might,
70	that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos.
71	For I knew it when he defended the Danaans with a ready heart,
72	and I know it now, when he makes those men like the blessed gods
73	in glory, and has bound our might and our hands.
74	Come then, let us all do as I say.
75	The ships that are drawn up first, nearest the sea,
76	let us haul down, and drag all of them into the shining sea,
77	and anchor them out on their mooring-stones, until there comes
78	the immortal night, if even in it they will hold off from war,
79	the Trojans; and then we could drag down all the ships.
80	For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night.
81	Better the man who flees and escapes evil than the man who is caught.”
82	Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said:
83	“Son of Atreus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
84	Ruinous man, I wish you were giving orders to some other, unworthy army,
85	and were not lord over us, to whom Zeus
86	has given it, from youth to old age, to wind out
87	grievous wars, until every one of us perishes.
88	Are you so eager, then, to leave the wide-streeted city of the Trojans
89	behind, for whose sake we have suffered so many evils?
90	Be silent, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears this
91	word, which no man would ever let pass through his mouth at all
92	who knew in his mind how to speak fitting words,
93	and bore a scepter, and whom the people would obey,
94	as many as those you are lord over among the Argives.
95	But now I utterly scorn your mind, from what you have said,
96	you who bid us, while war and the war cry are joined,
97	to drag the well-benched ships to the sea, so that still more
98	the Trojans may have what they pray for, winning as they are already,
99	and sheer destruction may weigh down on us. For the Achaeans
100	will not hold out in the war while the ships are dragged to the sea,
101	but will look away, and hang back from battle.
102	Then your counsel would do us harm, leader of the people.”
103	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, answered him:
104	“Odysseus, you have touched me deeply to the heart with a grievous
105	rebuke; but I am not ordering them against their will,
106	the sons of the Achaeans, to drag the well-benched ships to the sea.
107	Now let there be one who can speak of a better plan than this,
108	young or old; and I would be glad of it.”
109	And among them spoke also Diomedes, good at the war cry:
110	“The man is near; we shall not search long, if you are willing
111	to obey, and do not each of you resent it with a grudge
112	because I am the youngest by birth among you;
113	but I too claim to be of the line of a brave father,
114	Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes.
115	For to Portheus were born three noble sons,
116	and they lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon:
117	Agrius and Melas, and the third was the horseman Oeneus,
118	my father's father; and in prowess he stood out among them.
119	He stayed there; but my father settled in Argos
120	after his wanderings; for so, I think, Zeus willed it and the other gods.
121	He married one of the daughters of Adrastus, and lived in a house
122	wealthy in substance, and he had fields enough
123	bearing wheat, and many rows of orchard trees all around,
124	and he had many flocks; and he surpassed all the Achaeans
125	with the spear. These things you must have heard, if they are true.
126	So you could not say that by birth I am a coward and a weakling
127	and dishonor the word I speak, if I speak it well.
128	Come, let us go to the war, stabbed though we are; we must.
129	And there let us ourselves keep away from combat,
130	out of range of the missiles, lest someone take wound upon wound;
131	but we will urge on the others and send them in, who even before now
132	have been doing their hearts a kindness, standing apart and not fighting.”
133	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed.
134	They went, and Agamemnon, lord of men, led them.
135	Nor did the famous shaker of the earth keep a blind watch,
136	but he went along with them in the likeness of an old man,
137	and took the right hand of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
138	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
139	“Son of Atreus, now, I think, the deadly heart of Achilles
140	rejoices in his breast at the slaughter and the panic of the Achaeans
141	as he looks on, since there is no sense in him, not even a little.
142	But let him perish so, and may a god maim him.
143	The blessed gods do not yet bear you a grudge altogether;
144	but surely the leaders and rulers of the Trojans will yet
145	fill the wide plain with dust, and you yourself will see them
146	fleeing back to the city, away from the ships and huts.”
147	So saying, he gave a great shout as he sped over the plain.
148	As loud as nine thousand or ten thousand men shout
149	in war as they join in the strife of Ares,
150	so loud was the voice that the powerful earth-shaker
151	sent out of his breast; and he put great strength into each of the Achaeans,
152	in his heart, to go to war without ceasing and to fight.
153	And Hera of the golden throne looked out with her eyes,
154	standing on a peak of Olympus; and at once she knew
155	the one busying himself in the battle where men win glory,
156	her own brother and her brother-in-law, and she rejoiced in her heart;
157	but Zeus, on the topmost peak of Ida of the many springs,
158	she saw sitting, and he was hateful to her heart.
159	Then ox-eyed queen Hera pondered
160	how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus.
161	And this plan seemed to her in her heart the best:
162	to go to Ida, when she had adorned herself well,
163	to see if he might long to lie in love beside
164	her body, and she might pour sleep, harmless and warm,
165	upon his eyelids and his shrewd mind.
166	She went to her chamber, which her dear son built for her,
167	Hephaestus, and he fitted close doors to the doorposts
168	with a secret bolt, which no other god could open.
169	There she went in and closed the shining doors.
170	First, with ambrosia, she cleansed from her lovely body
171	all defilements, and anointed herself richly with olive oil,
172	ambrosial and sweet, which had been perfumed for her;
173	and when it was stirred in the bronze-floored house of Zeus,
174	even so, its fragrance reached to earth and heaven.
175	With this she anointed her fair skin, and her locks
176	she combed, and with her hands she plaited the shining tresses,
177	fair and ambrosial, from her immortal head.
178	And she put on an ambrosial robe, which Athena
179	had smoothed and worked for her, and set many intricate designs in it;
180	and she fastened it at her breast with golden pins.
181	And she girded herself with a girdle fitted with a hundred tassels,
182	and in her well-pierced ears she put earrings
183	with three drops, clustered like mulberries; and much grace shone from them.
184	And with a veil over all she covered herself, brilliant among goddesses,
185	a fine one, new-made, and it was white like the sun;
186	and under her shining feet she bound her fine sandals.
187	But when she had put all her adornment about her body,
188	she went out of her chamber, and calling Aphrodite
189	apart from the other gods she spoke a word to her:
190	“Will you listen to me now, dear child, in what I say,
191	or would you refuse me, holding a grudge in your heart
192	because I help the Danaans and you the Trojans?”
193	Then Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, answered her:
194	“Hera, august goddess, daughter of great Cronus,
195	say what is in your mind; my heart bids me accomplish it,
196	if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished.”
197	And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed her:
198	“Give me now love and longing, with which you
199	overcome all the immortals and mortal men.
200	For I am going to see the limits of the all-nourishing earth,
201	and Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys,
202	who nursed me well in their halls and cherished me,
203	taking me from Rhea, when far-seeing Zeus put Cronus
204	down beneath the earth and the barren sea.
205	I am going to see them, and I will end their endless quarrels;
206	for a long time now they have kept away from each other,
207	from the bed and from love, since anger has fallen upon their hearts.
208	If with words I could win over the dear hearts of those two
209	and bring them back to their bed to be joined in love,
210	I would always be called dear and respected by them.”
211	Then laughter-loving Aphrodite spoke to her in turn:
212	“It is not possible, nor would it be fitting, to refuse your word;
213	for you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest of all.”
214	She spoke, and loosed from her breast the embroidered strap,
215	richly worked, in which all her enchantments were wrought:
216	in it was love, in it longing, in it intimate talk,
217	the persuasion that steals the mind even of men who think shrewdly.
218	This she put into her hands, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
219	“Here, now, put this strap in your bosom,
220	richly worked, in which all things are wrought; and I do not think
221	you will come back with nothing done, whatever you have in your mind.”
222	So she spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera smiled,
223	and smiling, she put it in her bosom.
224	Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, went to her house,
225	and Hera darted down and left the peak of Olympus,
226	and stepping on Pieria and lovely Emathia
227	she sped over the snowy mountains of the horse-herding Thracians,
228	over their topmost peaks, and her feet did not touch the ground;
229	and from Athos she stepped out onto the billowing sea,
230	and came to Lemnos, the city of divine Thoas.
231	There she met Sleep, the brother of Death,
232	and she clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
233	“Sleep, lord of all gods and of all men,
234	once before you listened to my word; so now once more
235	obey me; and I will be grateful to you all my days.
236	Lull to sleep for me the shining eyes of Zeus beneath his brows,
237	as soon as I have lain beside him in love.
238	And I will give you gifts: a fine throne, imperishable forever,
239	golden; and Hephaestus, my son, the god lame in both legs,
240	will make it with skill, and set a footstool beneath it for your feet,
241	on which you may rest your shining feet at the banquet.”
242	Answering her, sweet Sleep said:
243	“Hera, august goddess, daughter of great Cronus,
244	any other of the everlasting gods
245	I would easily lull to sleep, even the streams of the river
246	Oceanus, who is the origin of all;
247	but I would not come near Zeus, the son of Cronus,
248	nor lull him to sleep, unless he himself should bid me.
249	For once before now a command of yours taught me a lesson,
250	on the day when that high-hearted son of Zeus
251	was sailing from Ilios, when he had sacked the city of the Trojans.
252	I then lulled the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus,
253	pouring myself sweetly around him; and you devised evil against his son in your heart,
254	rousing blasts of grievous winds over the sea,
255	and then you carried him off to well-peopled Cos,
256	far from all his friends. And he, waking, grew angry,
257	flinging the gods about the house, and me above all
258	he sought; and he would have thrown me from the upper air into the sea, out of sight,
259	had not Night, the tamer of gods and men, saved me.
260	To her I came in flight, and he stopped, angry though he was;
261	for he shrank from doing what would displease swift Night.
262	And now again you bid me accomplish this other impossible thing.”
263	Then ox-eyed queen Hera spoke to him in turn:
264	“Sleep, why do you have these things in your mind?
265	Do you think that far-seeing Zeus will help the Trojans as much
266	as he was enraged for Heracles, his own son?
267	But come, and I will give you one of the younger Graces
268	to marry and to be called your wife,
269	[Pasithea, for whom you are always longing, all your days.]”
270	So she spoke, and Sleep rejoiced, and answering he said:
271	“Come now, swear to me by the inviolable water of Styx,
272	and with one hand take hold of the bountiful earth,
273	and with the other the gleaming sea, so that all of them may be
274	witnesses between us, the gods below who are around Cronus,
275	that you will indeed give me one of the younger Graces,
276	Pasithea, whom I myself have longed for all my days.”
277	So he spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey,
278	and she swore as he bade, and named all the gods
279	beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans.
280	But when she had sworn and completed the oath,
281	the two set off, leaving the city of Lemnos and Imbros,
282	clothed in mist, swiftly making their way.
283	And they came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts,
284	to Lectum, where they first left the sea; and over the dry land
285	they went, and the treetops of the forest shook beneath their feet.
286	There Sleep stayed back, before the eyes of Zeus could see him,
287	climbing into a towering fir, which then on Ida
288	grew the tallest, and reached up through the mist into the upper air.
289	There he sat, hidden among the branches of the fir,
290	like a shrill-voiced bird, which in the mountains
291	the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis.
292	And Hera quickly went up to the peak of Gargarus
293	on lofty Ida; and Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw her.
294	And as he saw her, so desire wrapped his shrewd mind,
295	as when they first lay together in love,
296	going to bed together without the knowledge of their dear parents.
297	He stood before her, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
298	“Hera, where are you bound, that you have come here down from Olympus?
299	And you have no horses here, and no chariot to mount.”
300	And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed him:
301	“I am going to see the limits of the all-nourishing earth,
302	and Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys,
303	who nursed me well in their halls and cherished me.
304	I am going to see them, and I will end their endless quarrels;
305	for a long time now they have kept away from each other,
306	from the bed and from love, since anger has fallen upon their hearts.
307	And my horses are at the foot of Ida of the many springs,
308	standing ready to carry me over dry land and sea.
309	But now I have come here down from Olympus for your sake,
310	for fear you might be angry with me afterward, if in silence
311	I went off to the house of deep-streaming Oceanus.”
312	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
313	“Hera, you can set out for that place later;
314	but come, let the two of us go to bed and take our pleasure in love.
315	For never yet has desire for goddess or for woman
316	so flooded round the heart in my breast and overcome it,
317	not when I loved the wife of Ixion,
318	who bore Peirithous, a counselor equal to the gods;
319	nor when I loved Danae of the lovely ankles, daughter of Acrisius,
320	who bore Perseus, preeminent among all men;
321	nor when I loved the daughter of far-famed Phoenix,
322	who bore me Minos and godlike Rhadamanthys;
323	nor when I loved Semele, nor Alcmene in Thebes,
324	who bore Heracles, a fierce-hearted son;
325	and Semele bore Dionysus, a joy to mortals;
326	nor when I loved Demeter of the lovely tresses, the queen,
327	nor when I loved glorious Leto, nor you yourself,
328	as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me.”
329	And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed him:
330	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
331	If you are eager now to go to bed in love
332	on the peaks of Ida, where everything is in plain view,
333	how would it be if one of the everlasting gods
334	saw the two of us sleeping, and went among all the gods
335	and told them? I would not go back to your house
336	when I rose from that bed; it would be a thing for blame.
337	But if you really wish it, and it has pleased your heart,
338	you have a chamber, which your dear son built for you,
339	Hephaestus, and he fitted close doors to the doorposts;
340	let us go there and lie down, since bed is what pleases you.”
341	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
342	“Hera, do not fear that any god or any man
343	will see it; such a cloud will I wrap around you,
344	golden; not even the Sun could see through it to us,
345	whose light is the sharpest of all for seeing.”
346	He spoke, and the son of Cronus took his wife in his arms;
347	and under them the shining earth sent up fresh-sprung grass,
348	and dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth,
349	thick and soft, which held them up off the ground.
350	On it they lay, and they drew over them a cloud,
351	fine and golden; and glistening drops of dew fell from it.
352	So the father slept motionless on the peak of Gargarus,
353	overcome by sleep and love, and held his wife in his arms;
354	and sweet Sleep went running to the ships of the Achaeans
355	to carry the message to the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth;
356	and standing near him he addressed him with winged words:
357	“Now, Poseidon, help the Danaans with a ready heart,
358	and grant them glory, if only for a little while, while he still sleeps,
359	Zeus; for I have wrapped a soft slumber around him,
360	and Hera has beguiled him into going to bed with her in love.”
361	So saying, he went away to the famous tribes of men,
362	and set him on still more to defend the Danaans.
363	At once he leapt far out in front among the foremost and gave his orders:
364	“Argives, are we once again to yield the victory to Hector,
365	son of Priam, so that he may take the ships and win glory?
366	So he says and boasts, because Achilles
367	stays by the hollow ships, angered at heart;
368	but there will be no great longing for him, if we others
369	urge ourselves on to defend one another.
370	Come then, let us all do as I say:
371	the shields that are best in the army and biggest,
372	let us put them on, and cover our heads in all-gleaming helmets,
373	and take in our hands the longest spears,
374	and go; and I will lead, and I do not think
375	that Hector, son of Priam, will stand his ground any longer, however eager he is.
376	And whoever is a man steadfast in battle, but has a small shield on his shoulder,
377	let him give it to a worse man, and himself put on a bigger shield.”
378	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed.
379	And the kings themselves, stabbed though they were, marshaled them,
380	the son of Tydeus, and Odysseus, and the son of Atreus, Agamemnon;
381	going among them all, they exchanged the armor of war:
382	the brave man put on the good, and gave the worse to the worse man.
383	But when they had put the gleaming bronze about their bodies,
384	they went; and Poseidon the earth-shaker led them,
385	holding a dread long-edged sword in his strong hand,
386	like lightning; it is not lawful to close with it
387	in wretched battle, but fear holds men back.
388	And on the other side shining Hector marshaled the Trojans.
389	Then they stretched tight the most dread strife of war,
390	dark-haired Poseidon and shining Hector,
391	the one helping the Trojans, the other the Argives.
392	And the sea surged up toward the huts and ships
393	of the Argives; and they came together with a great shout.
394	Not so loud does the wave of the sea roar against the dry land,
395	roused from the deep by the grievous blast of the North Wind;
396	nor so loud is the roar of blazing fire
397	in the glens of a mountain, when it rushes up to burn the forest;
398	nor does the wind cry so loud around the high-leafed oaks,
399	the wind that roars the loudest in its rage,
400	as loud as was the voice of the Trojans and the Achaeans
401	as they shouted terribly, when they rushed upon one another.
402	At Ajax first shining Hector threw
403	his spear, since he was turned straight toward him, and he did not miss,
404	where the two straps were stretched across his chest,
405	the one of his shield, the other of his silver-studded sword;
406	and these two protected his tender flesh. And Hector was enraged
407	that his swift missile had flown from his hand in vain,
408	and he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom.
409	Then, as he went away, great Telamonian Ajax
410	with a stone—for many of them, the props of the swift ships,
411	rolled about at the feet of the fighters—he lifted one of them
412	and hit him in the chest over the shield rim, near the neck,
413	and with the blow he sent him spinning like a top, and he whirled all around.
414	As when under the stroke of father Zeus an oak falls
415	uprooted, and a dread smell of sulfur rises
416	from it, and daring fails the man who sees it
417	close by, for hard to bear is the thunderbolt of great Zeus,
418	so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust;
419	and the spear dropped from his hand, and his shield fell upon him,
420	and his helmet, and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze.
421	And with a great cry the sons of the Achaeans ran up,
422	hoping to drag him away, and they hurled thick and fast
423	spears; but the shepherd of the people no one could
424	stab or hit; for before that the best stood over him,
425	Polydamas and Aeneas and brilliant Agenor,
426	and Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, and noble Glaucus.
427	And of the others not one neglected him, but before him
428	they held their well-rounded shields. And his companions
429	lifted him in their arms and carried him out of the toil, until he came to his horses,
430	his swift horses, which were standing behind the battle and the war
431	with their charioteer and the inlaid chariot;
432	and they carried him toward the city, groaning heavily.
433	But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river,
434	the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,
435	there they lifted him down from the chariot to the ground, and poured water
436	over him; and he got his breath back and looked up with his eyes,
437	and kneeling he vomited dark blood;
438	then he sank back again to the ground, and his two eyes
439	black night covered; and the missile still overpowered his spirit.
440	But when the Argives saw Hector going away,
441	they sprang on the Trojans more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle.
442	Then by far the first, swift Ajax, the son of Oileus,
443	leapt upon Satnius and stabbed him with his sharp spear,
444	the son of Enops, whom a noble Naiad nymph bore
445	to Enops as he tended his cattle by the banks of the Satnioeis.
446	Him the son of Oileus, famed for his spear, coming close,
447	stabbed in the flank, and he fell back; and around him
448	Trojans and Danaans joined in fierce battle.
449	And to his defense came spear-brandishing Polydamas,
450	the son of Panthous, and he hit Prothoenor on the right shoulder,
451	the son of Areilycus, and the heavy spear went through his shoulder;
452	and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth in his palm.
453	And Polydamas boasted terribly, shouting aloud:
454	“Truly, I do not think that from the great-hearted son of Panthous,
455	from his strong hand, the javelin has leapt in vain this time;
456	one of the Argives has caught it in his body, and I think
457	he will lean on it as a staff as he goes down into the house of Hades.”
458	So he spoke, and grief came upon the Argives at his boasting,
459	and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Ajax,
460	the son of Telamon; for the man fell very close to him.
461	And quickly he threw his shining spear at him as he went away.
462	Polydamas himself avoided black doom,
463	darting aside, but the son of Antenor caught it,
464	Archelochus; for the gods had planned his destruction.
465	He hit him where the head and neck join,
466	on the last vertebra, and sheared through both the tendons;
467	and his head and mouth and nostrils struck the ground
468	far sooner than his shins and knees as he fell.
469	And Ajax in turn called out to noble Polydamas:
470	“Consider, Polydamas, and tell me truly:
471	is not this man, killed in return for Prothoenor,
472	worthy of him? He does not look to me like a coward, nor born of cowards,
473	but a brother of Antenor, tamer of horses,
474	or a son; for in his lineage he looked most like him.”
475	He spoke, knowing well, and grief seized the hearts of the Trojans.
476	Then Acamas stabbed Promachus the Boeotian with his spear
477	as he stood over his brother, while the other dragged at the body by the feet.
478	And Acamas boasted terribly over him, shouting aloud:
479	“Argives, you arrow-fighters, insatiable of threats,
480	surely the toil and the misery will not be ours
481	alone; but some day you too will be killed like this.
482	Consider how your Promachus sleeps, overcome
483	by my spear, so that the penalty for my brother
484	may not long go unpaid; and that is why a man prays
485	to leave a kinsman in his halls, to defend him against disaster.”
486	So he spoke, and grief came upon the Argives at his boasting,
487	and most of all he stirred the heart of wise-hearted Peneleos.
488	He rushed at Acamas; but the other did not await the force
489	of lord Peneleos, who stabbed Ilioneus,
490	son of Phorbas, rich in flocks, whom most
491	of the Trojans Hermes loved, and granted him wealth;
492	and to him the mother bore Ilioneus, an only child.
493	Him he stabbed then under the brow, at the roots of the eye,
494	and drove out the eyeball; and the spear went straight through the eye
495	and through the back of the head, and he sat down, stretching out both
496	his hands; and Peneleos, drawing his sharp sword,
497	struck him full in the middle of the neck, and dashed to the ground
498	the head, helmet and all; and the heavy spear was still
499	in the eye; and holding it up like a poppy head
500	he showed it to the Trojans, and spoke in triumph:
501	“Trojans, tell noble Ilioneus' dear father for me
502	and his mother, to wail for him in their halls;
503	for neither will the wife of Promachus, son of Alegenor,
504	be glad at her dear husband's coming home, when
505	we young men of the Achaeans come back from Troy with our ships.”
506	So he spoke, and trembling seized the limbs of them all,
507	and each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction.
508	Tell me now, Muses, you who have your homes on Olympus,
509	who was the first of the Achaeans to win the bloody spoils of men,
510	when the famous shaker of the earth had turned the battle.
511	Telamonian Ajax was the first; he stabbed Hyrtius,
512	son of Gyrtius, leader of the stout-hearted Mysians;
513	and Antilochus slew and stripped Phalces and Mermerus;
514	and Meriones killed Morys and Hippotion;
515	and Teucer slew Prothoon and Periphetes;
516	and then the son of Atreus stabbed Hyperenor, shepherd of the people,
517	in the flank, and the bronze let out his bowels
518	as it tore through; and his soul through the stab wound
519	rushed out in haste, and darkness covered his eyes.
520	But most were killed by Ajax, the swift son of Oileus;
521	for there was no one like him to pursue on foot
522	men who fled in fear, when Zeus rouses rout among them.
