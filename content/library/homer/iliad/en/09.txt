# Homer, Iliad, Book 9 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So the Trojans kept their watch; but the Achaeans
2	were held by heaven-sent Panic, companion of chilling Rout,
3	and all the best of them were stricken with unbearable grief.
4	As two winds stir up the fish-filled sea,
5	the North Wind and the West Wind, which blow from Thrace,
6	coming on suddenly, and at once the dark wave
7	rears up in a crest and casts much seaweed along the shore;
8	so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn.
9	And the son of Atreus, stricken at heart with great grief,
10	went to and fro, ordering the clear-voiced heralds
11	to summon every man by name to the assembly,
12	but not to shout; and he himself toiled among the foremost.
13	They sat in the assembly, grieving; and Agamemnon
14	stood up, shedding tears like a spring of dark water
15	that pours its murky water down a sheer rock;
16	so, groaning heavily, he spoke his words among the Argives:
17	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
18	Zeus, son of Cronus, has bound me fast in heavy blind folly—
19	hard god, who once promised me and nodded his assent
20	that I would sack well-walled Ilios and then sail home;
21	but now he has devised an evil deceit, and bids me
22	go back to Argos in disgrace, when I have lost so many men.
23	Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might,
24	who has brought down the heights of many cities
25	and will bring down more; for his power is the greatest.
26	Come then, let us all do as I say:
27	let us flee with our ships to our dear native land;
28	for we shall never now take wide-streeted Troy.”
29	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence.
30	For a long time the sons of the Achaeans were silent in their grief;
31	at last Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them:
32	“Son of Atreus, I will fight with you first, for your folly,
33	as is the custom, my lord, in the assembly; and do not be angry at all.
34	It was my courage you first reproached among the Danaans,
35	saying I was unwarlike and a coward; and all this
36	the Argives know, both young and old.
37	But the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given you only half:
38	he granted you to be honored above all men for your scepter,
39	but courage he did not give you, and that is the greatest power.
40	Strange one, do you really think the sons of the Achaeans
41	are so unwarlike and cowardly as you say?
42	If your own heart is so eager to go home,
43	go: the road is open to you, and your ships stand
44	by the sea, the many ships that followed you from Mycenae.
45	But the other long-haired Achaeans will stay
46	until we sack Troy. And if they too wish,
47	let them flee with their ships to their dear native land;
48	but the two of us, I and Sthenelus, will fight until we find the end
49	of Ilios; for we came here with a god.”
50	So he spoke, and all the sons of the Achaeans shouted aloud,
51	admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses.
52	And the horseman Nestor stood up among them and spoke:
53	“Son of Tydeus, you are mighty in war above all others,
54	and in counsel you are the best among all men of your age.
55	No one of all the Achaeans will find fault with your word
56	or speak against it; but you have not reached the end of the matter.
57	You are young, it is true; you might even be my son,
58	the youngest by birth; yet you speak wisely
59	to the kings of the Argives, for what you have said is fair and right.
60	But come, let me, who claim to be older than you,
61	speak out and go through it all; and no one
62	will dishonor my word, not even powerful Agamemnon.
63	Without clan, without law, without hearth is the man
64	who loves war among his own people, that chilling thing.
65	But now let us yield to black night
66	and make ready our supper; and let the sentries, each of them,
67	spend the night beside the ditch they have dug, outside the wall.
68	This I lay on the young men; and after that,
69	son of Atreus, you take the lead, for you are the most kingly.
70	Give a feast for the elders; it is fitting for you, and not unseemly.
71	Your huts are full of wine, which the ships of the Achaeans
72	bring you day by day from Thrace over the wide sea;
73	you have all the means of entertaining, and you rule over many.
74	When many are gathered, you will follow the man who devises
75	the best counsel; and all the Achaeans have great need
76	of good and shrewd counsel, since the enemy close by the ships
77	are burning many fires; who could rejoice at this?
78	This night will either shatter the army or save it.”
79	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed.
80	And the sentries hurried out with their armor,
81	around Thrasymedes, son of Nestor, shepherd of the people,
82	and around Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, sons of Ares,
83	and around Meriones and Aphareus and Deipyrus,
84	and around the son of Creon, brilliant Lycomedes.
85	Seven were the leaders of the sentries, and with each a hundred
86	young men marched, holding long spears in their hands;
87	and they went and sat down midway between the ditch and the wall;
88	there they kindled fires, and each man made ready his supper.
89	But the son of Atreus led the elders of the Achaeans all together
90	to his hut, and set before them a satisfying feast.
91	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
92	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
93	the old man first of all began to weave a plan for them,
94	Nestor, whose counsel had shown itself best before;
95	with good intent he addressed them and spoke:
96	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
97	with you I shall end, and with you I shall begin, since of many
98	people you are lord, and Zeus has put into your hands
99	the scepter and the ordinances, so that you may take counsel for them.
100	So you above all must speak your word, and listen,
101	and fulfill another's word too, whenever anyone's heart bids him
102	speak for the good; whatever he begins will rest with you.
103	But I will speak as it seems to me best.
104	For no one else will think a better thought than this,
105	the thought I have held, both long ago and still now,
106	ever since the time when you, sprung from Zeus, took the girl Briseis
107	and went from the hut of Achilles in his anger,
108	not at all as we would have it; for I, for my part,
109	urged you hard against it; but you, giving way to your proud heart,
110	the best of men, whom the immortals themselves honored,
111	you dishonored; for you took his prize and keep it. But even now
112	let us consider how we may make amends to him and win him over
113	with kindly gifts and with soothing words.”
114	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to him in turn:
115	“Old man, it is no lie, your account of my blind folly.
116	I was blinded; I do not deny it myself. Worth many
117	people is the man whom Zeus loves in his heart,
118	as now he has honored this man and beaten down the army of the Achaeans.
119	But since I was blinded, obeying my wretched mind,
120	I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom.
121	And before you all I will name the glorious gifts:
122	seven tripods untouched by fire, and ten talents of gold,
123	and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses,
124	strong prize-winners, which have won prizes with their feet.
125	No man would be landless who had as much,
126	nor lacking in precious gold,
127	as the prizes these single-hoofed horses have carried off for me.
128	And I will give seven women skilled in flawless handiwork,
129	women of Lesbos, whom, when he himself took well-built Lesbos,
130	I chose out for myself, who surpassed the tribes of women in beauty.
131	These I will give him, and with them will be the one I took from him then,
132	the daughter of Briseus; and I will swear a great oath besides
133	that I never went up into her bed or lay with her,
134	as is the custom of mankind, of men and women.
135	All these things shall be at hand at once; and if hereafter
136	the gods grant us to sack the great city of Priam,
137	let him load his ship full with gold and bronze,
138	going in when we Achaeans divide the spoil,
139	and let him choose for himself twenty Trojan women,
140	the most beautiful after Argive Helen.
141	And if we come to Achaean Argos, the udder of the plowland,
142	he would be my son-in-law; and I would honor him equally with Orestes,
143	who is reared as my darling son in great abundance.
144	I have three daughters in my well-built hall,
145	Chrysothemis and Laodice and Iphianassa;
146	of these let him take whichever he will as his own, without bride-price,
147	to the house of Peleus; and I will give a dowry besides,
148	very great, such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter.
149	And I will give him seven well-peopled citadels,
150	Cardamyle and Enope and grassy Hire,
151	and holy Pherae and Antheia of the deep meadows,
152	and fair Aepeia and vine-rich Pedasus.
153	All lie near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos;
154	and in them live men rich in flocks and rich in cattle,
155	who will honor him with gifts like a god
156	and under his scepter will bring his ordinances to rich fulfillment.
157	This I would accomplish for him, if he ceased from his anger.
158	Let him be tamed: Hades, you know, is implacable and untamable,
159	and for that he is the most hateful to mortals of all the gods.
160	And let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly,
161	and inasmuch as I claim to be the elder by birth.”
162	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
163	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
164	the gifts you offer lord Achilles are no longer to be scorned.
165	Come, let us urge on chosen men, who may go with all speed
166	to the hut of Achilles, son of Peleus.
167	Come, those whom I shall pick out, let them obey.
168	First of all let Phoenix, dear to Zeus, lead the way,
169	and after him great Ajax and brilliant Odysseus;
170	and of the heralds let Odius and Eurybates go with them.
171	Bring water for our hands, and bid them keep holy silence,
172	so that we may pray to Zeus, son of Cronus, if he will have pity.”
173	So he spoke, and the word he spoke was pleasing to them all.
174	At once the heralds poured water over their hands,
175	and the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink,
176	and served it out to all, first pouring drops for the libation into the cups.
177	But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired,
178	they set out from the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus.
179	And the Gerenian horseman Nestor gave them many instructions,
180	glancing at each of them, and at Odysseus most of all,
181	to try how they might win over the noble son of Peleus.
182	So the two went along the shore of the loud-roaring sea,
183	praying earnestly to the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
184	that they might easily win over the great mind of the grandson of Aeacus.
185	And they came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons,
186	and found him delighting his heart with a clear-toned lyre,
187	a beautiful one, finely wrought, and on it was a crossbar of silver;
188	he had taken it from the spoils when he destroyed the city of Eetion.
189	With it he was delighting his heart, and he sang the famous deeds of men.
190	And Patroclus alone sat facing him in silence,
191	waiting for the grandson of Aeacus to leave off his singing.
192	And the two came forward, and brilliant Odysseus led the way,
193	and they stood before him; and Achilles sprang up in amazement
194	with the lyre still in his hand, leaving the seat where he sat.
195	And Patroclus likewise, when he saw the men, stood up.
196	And greeting the two, swift-footed Achilles said:
197	“Welcome, both of you! Surely you come as friends; surely the need is great,
198	you who are to me, angry as I am, the dearest of the Achaeans.”
199	So he spoke, and brilliant Achilles led them farther in,
200	and seated them on chairs with purple rugs.
201	And at once he called to Patroclus, who was near:
202	“Set out a larger mixing bowl, son of Menoetius,
203	and mix the wine stronger, and make ready a cup for each;
204	for these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof.”
205	So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion.
206	And he set down a great chopping block in the light of the fire,
207	and laid on it the back of a sheep and of a fat goat,
208	and the chine of a fat hog, rich with lard.
209	Automedon held them for him, and brilliant Achilles carved.
210	And he cut them up well and threaded them on spits,
211	and the son of Menoetius, a man equal to the gods, made the fire blaze high.
212	But when the fire had burned down and the flame had died away,
213	he spread out the embers and stretched the spits above them,
214	and sprinkled them with divine salt, lifting them onto the firedogs.
215	But when he had roasted it and heaped it on the platters,
216	Patroclus took bread and set it out on the table
217	in beautiful baskets, while Achilles served the meat.
218	And he himself sat facing divine Odysseus,
219	by the other wall, and bade his companion sacrifice to the gods,
220	Patroclus; and he threw the offerings into the fire.
221	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
222	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
223	Ajax nodded to Phoenix; and brilliant Odysseus saw it,
224	and filling a cup with wine he pledged Achilles:
225	“Your health, Achilles! We do not lack an equal share of the feast,
226	either in the hut of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
227	or here now; for there is much to satisfy the heart
228	to feast on. But the business of the lovely feast is not our concern;
229	no, we look on a calamity all too great, you who are nurtured by Zeus,
230	and we are afraid; it is in doubt whether we save or lose
231	the well-benched ships, unless you put on your courage.
232	For near the ships and the wall they have pitched their camp,
233	the high-hearted Trojans and their far-famed allies,
234	and kindled many fires throughout the army, and they say they will no longer
235	be held back, but will fall upon the black ships.
236	And Zeus, son of Cronus, shows them signs on the right
237	with his lightning; and Hector, exulting greatly in his strength,
238	rages terribly, trusting in Zeus, and pays no honor at all
239	to men or gods; a mighty frenzy has entered into him.
240	He prays for brilliant Dawn to appear with all speed;
241	for he vows to hack the high sternposts from the ships,
242	and to burn the ships themselves with ravening fire, and the Achaeans
243	to slaughter beside them, bewildered by the smoke.
244	I am terribly afraid at heart of this, that the gods may fulfill
245	his threats for him, and that it may be our fate
246	to perish in Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos.
247	Up, then, if you are eager, late though it is, to save the sons of the Achaeans
248	who are worn down under the din of the Trojans.
249	To you yourself there will be grief hereafter, and there is no means
250	to find a cure for an evil once it is done; but long before that
251	consider how you will ward off the evil day from the Danaans.
252	My friend, surely your father Peleus charged you
253	on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon:
254	‘My child, Athena and Hera will give you strength,
255	if they will; but you, hold back your proud heart
256	in your breast; for kindliness is better;
257	and cease from strife that devises evil, so that all the more
258	the Argives may honor you, both young and old.’
259	So the old man charged you, but you forget. But even now
260	stop, and let go your heart-grieving anger; Agamemnon
261	offers you worthy gifts, if you cease from your anger.
262	Come, listen to me, and I will recount to you
263	all the gifts that Agamemnon promised you in his huts:
264	seven tripods untouched by fire, and ten talents of gold,
265	and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses,
266	strong prize-winners, which have won prizes with their feet.
267	No man would be landless who had as much,
268	nor lacking in precious gold,
269	as the prizes Agamemnon's horses have won with their feet.
270	And he will give seven women skilled in flawless handiwork,
271	women of Lesbos, whom, when you yourself took well-built Lesbos,
272	he chose out for himself, who then surpassed the tribes of women in beauty.
273	These he will give you, and with them will be the one he took from you then,
274	the daughter of Briseus; and he will swear a great oath besides
275	that he never went up into her bed or lay with her,
276	as is the custom, my lord, of men and of women.
277	All these things shall be at hand at once; and if hereafter
278	the gods grant us to sack the great city of Priam,
279	you may load your ship full with gold and bronze,
280	going in when we Achaeans divide the spoil,
281	and choose for yourself twenty Trojan women,
282	the most beautiful after Argive Helen.
283	And if we come to Achaean Argos, the udder of the plowland,
284	you would be his son-in-law; and he would honor you equally with Orestes,
285	who is reared as his darling son in great abundance.
286	He has three daughters in his well-built hall,
287	Chrysothemis and Laodice and Iphianassa;
288	of these you may take whichever you will as your own, without bride-price,
289	to the house of Peleus; and he in turn will give a dowry besides,
290	very great, such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter.
291	And he will give you seven well-peopled citadels,
292	Cardamyle and Enope and grassy Hire,
293	and holy Pherae and Antheia of the deep meadows,
294	and fair Aepeia and vine-rich Pedasus.
295	All lie near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos;
296	and in them live men rich in flocks and rich in cattle,
297	who will honor you with gifts like a god
298	and under your scepter will bring your ordinances to rich fulfillment.
299	This he would accomplish for you, if you ceased from your anger.
300	But if the son of Atreus is too hateful to your heart,
301	himself and his gifts, yet the rest, all the Achaeans,
302	worn down throughout the army, pity them; like a god
303	they will honor you; for you would win very great glory in their eyes.
304	For now you might kill Hector, since he would come very close to you
305	in his deadly frenzy, since he says there is no one equal
306	to him among the Danaans whom the ships brought here.”
307	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
308	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
309	I must speak out my word without regard for anyone,
310	as I mean it and as it will be accomplished,
311	so that you do not sit beside me and murmur, one from this side, one from that.
312	For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man
313	who hides one thing in his mind and says another.
314	But I will speak as it seems to me best:
315	neither, I think, will Agamemnon, son of Atreus, persuade me,
316	nor the other Danaans, since there was, it seems, no thanks
317	for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever.
318	The same share for the man who stays behind and for one who fights his hardest;
319	the coward and the brave are held in a single honor;
320	the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much.
321	Nothing is laid up for me, after I suffered sorrows in my heart,
322	always staking my life in battle.
323	As a bird brings to her unfledged chicks
324	each morsel she takes, while it goes hard with her herself,
325	so I too spent many sleepless nights,
326	and passed through bloody days in battle,
327	fighting with men for the sake of their wives.
328	Twelve cities of men I have sacked with my ships,
329	and eleven, I say, on foot throughout deep-soiled Troy;
330	from all of these I took out many fine treasures,
331	and all of them I would bring and give to Agamemnon,
332	son of Atreus; and he, staying behind beside the swift ships,
333	would take them, and share out a few, and keep many.
334	Others he gave as prizes to the chiefs and the kings;
335	they keep theirs safe, but from me alone of the Achaeans
336	he has taken mine, and he keeps the wife dear to my heart; let him lie beside her
337	and take his pleasure. But why must the Trojans be fought
338	by the Argives? Why did he gather the army and lead it here,
339	the son of Atreus? Was it not for the sake of Helen of the lovely hair?
340	Are they the only ones of mortal men who love their wives,
341	the sons of Atreus? For any man who is good and sensible
342	loves his own and cares for her, as I too
343	loved her from my heart, though she was won by my spear.
344	But now, since he has taken my prize from my hands and deceived me,
345	let him not try me; I know him well; he will not persuade me.
346	No, Odysseus, with you and the other kings
347	let him consider how to ward off the consuming fire from the ships.
348	Truly he has toiled at a great deal without me:
349	he has built a wall, and driven a ditch along it,
350	wide and great, and in it he planted stakes;
351	but not even so can he hold the strength of man-slaying Hector
352	in check. As long as I was fighting among the Achaeans,
353	Hector had no wish to rouse the battle far from the wall,
354	but came only as far as the Scaean gates and the oak;
355	there once he waited for me alone, and barely escaped my charge.
356	But now, since I have no wish to fight with brilliant Hector,
357	tomorrow, when I have made sacrifice to Zeus and all the gods,
358	and loaded my ships well, when I have drawn them down to the sea,
359	you will see, if you wish and if you care about it,
360	very early, sailing on the fish-filled Hellespont,
361	my ships, and in them men eager to row;
362	and if the famous shaker of the earth grants me fair sailing,
363	on the third day I would reach deep-soiled Phthia.
364	There I have a great many things, which I left on my ill-starred way here;
365	and from here I will bring other gold, and red bronze,
366	and fair-belted women, and gray iron,
367	all that I won by lot; but my prize, the very man who gave it
368	has taken back again in his arrogance, powerful Agamemnon,
369	son of Atreus. Tell him everything, just as I charge you,
370	openly, so that the other Achaeans too may be indignant,
371	if he still hopes to deceive some other of the Danaans,
372	clothed forever in shamelessness; but he would not,
373	dog though he is, dare to look me in the face.
374	I will join him in no counsel, nor in any deed;
375	for he deceived me and did me wrong; never again
376	will he beguile me with words; once is enough for him. Let him go
377	to his ruin at his ease; for Zeus the counselor has taken away his wits.
378	His gifts are hateful to me, and I honor him at a hair's worth.
379	Not if he gave me ten times and twenty times as much
380	as all he has now, and more were to come to him from elsewhere,
381	not all that comes in to Orchomenus, nor all that comes to Thebes
382	in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses,
383	which has a hundred gates, and through each two hundred
384	men march out with horses and chariots;
385	not if he gave me as much as the sand and the dust,
386	not even so would Agamemnon any longer persuade my heart,
387	until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult.
388	And I will not marry the daughter of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
389	not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty
390	and equaled gray-eyed Athena in handiwork;
391	not even so will I marry her. Let him choose another of the Achaeans,
392	one who befits him and who is more kingly.
393	For if the gods keep me safe and I come home,
394	Peleus himself will surely seek out a wife for me then.
395	There are many Achaean women throughout Hellas and Phthia,
396	daughters of chieftains who guard the citadels;
397	of these whichever I wish I will make my dear wife.
398	And there my proud spirit was very eager
399	to marry a wedded wife, a fitting mate,
400	and enjoy the possessions that the old man Peleus acquired.
401	For nothing to me is worth my life, not all that they say
402	Ilios possessed, that well-peopled citadel,
403	in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came,
404	nor all that is shut within the stone threshold of the Archer,
405	Phoebus Apollo, in rocky Pytho.
406	For cattle and fat sheep can be had by raiding,
407	and tripods can be won, and the tawny heads of horses,
408	but a man's soul cannot come back again; it cannot be taken by raiding
409	or caught, once it has passed the barrier of his teeth.
410	For my mother, the goddess silver-footed Thetis, tells me
411	that two different fates are carrying me toward the end of death.
412	If I stay here and fight around the city of the Trojans,
413	my homecoming is lost, but my fame will be imperishable;
414	but if I come home to my dear native land,
415	my noble fame is lost, but my life will be long,
416	and the end of death will not come on me quickly.
417	And I would advise the rest of you, too,
418	to sail away home, since you will no longer find the end
419	of steep Ilios; for far-seeing Zeus has held
420	his hand over it, and its people have taken heart.
421	But you go to the chiefs of the Achaeans
422	and declare my message—for that is the prize of the old—
423	so that they may think of some other, better plan in their minds,
424	which may save their ships and the army of the Achaeans
425	beside the hollow ships, since this one is not ready to their hand,
426	the one they devised now, while I nurse my wrath apart.
427	But let Phoenix stay here with us and lie down to sleep,
428	so that he may follow me in the ships to our dear native land
429	tomorrow, if he wishes; but I will not take him by force.”
430	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence,
431	amazed at his word, for he had refused very forcefully.
432	At last the old horse-driving Phoenix spoke among them,
433	bursting into tears, for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans:
434	“If it is homecoming, shining Achilles, that you are turning over
435	in your mind, and you have no wish at all to ward off from the swift ships
436	the destroying fire, since anger has fallen upon your heart,
437	how then could I be left here apart from you, dear child,
438	alone? The old horse-driving Peleus sent me with you
439	on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon,
440	a child, who knew nothing yet of war, common to all,
441	nor of assemblies, where men become distinguished.
442	For this reason he sent me, to teach you all these things:
443	to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds.
444	So, dear child, I would not wish to be parted from you
445	and left behind, not even if a god himself should promise me
446	to scrape away my old age and make me young and in my prime,
447	as I was when I first left Hellas of the lovely women,
448	fleeing the quarrels of my father Amyntor, son of Ormenus,
449	who was enraged at me over his fair-haired concubine,
450	whom he himself loved, and he dishonored his wife,
451	my mother; and she kept begging me by my knees
452	to lie with the concubine first, so that she would hate the old man.
453	I obeyed her and did it; and my father, suspecting it at once,
454	cursed me many times, and called on the hateful Erinyes
455	that he would never set upon his knees a dear son
456	born of me; and the gods fulfilled his curses,
457	Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone.
458	[Then I planned to kill him with the sharp bronze;]
459	[but one of the immortals checked my anger, who put in my heart]
460	[the talk of the people and the many reproaches of men,]
461	[so that I might not be called a father-killer among the Achaeans.]
462	Then the heart in my breast could no longer at all be held
463	to staying in the halls while my father was angry.
464	Truly my kinsmen and cousins, who were all around me,
465	begging me hard, tried to keep me there in the halls,
466	and many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns
467	they slaughtered, and many hogs, rich with lard,
468	were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus,
469	and much wine was drunk from the old man's jars.
470	For nine nights they slept around me, close at my side;
471	they kept watch by turns, and the fire never
472	went out, one under the colonnade of the well-fenced court,
473	and another in the porch, before the doors of my chamber.
474	But when the tenth murky night came upon me,
475	then I broke the closely fitted doors of the chamber
476	and went out, and leapt over the fence of the court
477	easily, unseen by the watchmen and the slave women.
478	Then I fled far away through Hellas with its wide dancing floors,
479	and came to deep-soiled Phthia, mother of flocks,
480	to lord Peleus; and he received me gladly,
481	and loved me as a father loves his son,
482	his only son, his darling, heir to great possessions;
483	and he made me wealthy, and gave me many people,
484	and I lived at the far edge of Phthia, ruling over the Dolopians.
485	And I made you what you are, Achilles, like the gods,
486	loving you from my heart; for you would not go with anyone else,
487	either to a feast, or to eat in the halls,
488	until I set you on my knees,
489	and fed you your fill of meat, cutting it up first, and held the wine to your lips.
490	Many times you wet the tunic on my chest,
491	sputtering out the wine in your troublesome infancy.
492	So I have suffered much for you and toiled much,
493	with this in mind, that the gods would never bring to birth a child
494	of my own; but it was you, Achilles, like the gods, that I made
495	my son, so that one day you might ward off shameful ruin from me.
496	So, Achilles, tame your great spirit; you have no need
497	to have a pitiless heart; even the gods themselves can be turned,
498	and theirs is the greater prowess and honor and strength.
499	And with burnt offerings and gentle vows,
500	with libation and the savor of the burnt fat, men turn them aside,
501	entreating them, whenever someone oversteps and does wrong.
502	For the Prayers too are the daughters of great Zeus,
503	lame and wrinkled and with eyes that look askance,
504	and they take care to follow behind Blind Folly.
505	But Blind Folly is strong and sound of foot, and so she far
506	outruns them all, and comes first over all the earth,
507	doing harm to men; and they come after and heal the harm.
508	Whoever respects the daughters of Zeus when they draw near,
509	him they greatly help, and they hear him when he prays;
510	but when a man refuses them and stubbornly denies them,
511	then they go to Zeus, son of Cronus, and pray
512	that Blind Folly may follow him, so that he may be harmed and pay the price.
513	But, Achilles, you too grant that the daughters of Zeus be attended
514	by the honor that bends the minds of other men, brave as they are.
515	For if he were not bringing gifts, and naming more to come,
516	the son of Atreus, but were always furiously angry,
517	I would not bid you cast aside your wrath
518	and defend the Argives, however great their need;
519	but now he gives you much at once, and has promised more hereafter,
520	and he has sent the best men to entreat you,
521	choosing them throughout the Achaean army, the ones who to you yourself
522	are dearest of the Argives; you must not put their words to shame,
523	or their coming; before now there was no blame in your anger.
524	So too we have heard the famous deeds of the men of old,
525	the heroes, whenever furious anger came upon one of them:
526	they could be won with gifts and swayed by words.
527	I remember this deed from long ago, not recent at all,
528	how it was; and I will tell it among you, who are all my friends.
529	The Curetes and the Aetolians, steadfast in battle, were fighting
530	around the city of Calydon, and slaying one another,
531	the Aetolians defending lovely Calydon,
532	and the Curetes eager to sack it in the work of Ares.
533	For Artemis of the golden throne had roused an evil against them,
534	angry because no harvest offering on the slope of his orchard
535	was made to her by Oeneus; the other gods feasted on hecatombs,
536	but to her alone, the daughter of great Zeus, he made no sacrifice.
537	Either he forgot, or did not think of it; and he was greatly blinded in his heart.
538	And in her anger the divine child, she of the showering arrows,
539	roused against him a wild boar, a lurker in the grass, with white tusks,
540	which did much harm, as was its way, to the orchard of Oeneus;
541	and many tall trees it threw to the ground, uprooted,
542	with their very roots and with their fruit blossoms.
543	But Meleager, son of Oeneus, killed it,
544	gathering huntsmen from many cities
545	and dogs; for it could not have been subdued by a few mortals,
546	so huge it was, and it set many men on the grievous pyre.
547	But she raised around it a great din and shouting
548	over the boar's head and its shaggy hide,
549	between the Curetes and the great-hearted Aetolians.
550	Now as long as Meleager, dear to Ares, was fighting,
551	so long it went badly for the Curetes, and they could not
552	hold their ground outside the wall, many as they were;
553	but when anger entered into Meleager, the anger that swells
554	the mind in the breasts of others too, even of men who think shrewdly,
555	then, angry at heart with his dear mother Althaea,
556	he lay beside his wedded wife, the beautiful Cleopatra,
557	daughter of Marpessa of the lovely ankles, daughter of Evenus,
558	and of Idas, who was the strongest of men on earth
559	in those days; and he even took up his bow against the lord
560	Phoebus Apollo, for the sake of the bride of the lovely ankles.
561	In those days, in the halls, her father and her lady mother
562	called her by the name of Alcyone, because her
563	mother, having the lot of the halcyon bird of many sorrows,
564	wept that the far-working Phoebus Apollo had snatched her away.
565	Beside her he lay, brooding on his heart-grieving anger,
566	angered by his mother's curses; for to the gods she
567	prayed long in her grief over the killing of her brother,
568	and many times she beat the all-nourishing earth with her hands,
569	calling on Hades and dread Persephone,
570	kneeling down, and her bosom was wet with tears,
571	to give death to her son; and the Erinys who walks in darkness
572	heard her out of Erebus, she of the implacable heart.
573	And soon the din and the thudding rose about their gates,
574	as the towers were battered; and the elders begged him,
575	the elders of the Aetolians, and sent the best priests of the gods,
576	to come out and defend them, promising a great gift:
577	where the plain of lovely Calydon was richest,
578	there they bade him choose a beautiful domain
579	of fifty acres, half of it vineyard,
580	and half bare plowland, to be cut from the plain.
581	And the old horse-driving Oeneus entreated him again and again,
582	standing on the threshold of the high-roofed chamber,
583	shaking the jointed doors, imploring his son;
584	and many times his sisters and his lady mother
585	begged him; but he refused all the more; and many times his companions,
586	who were the most loyal and dearest to him of all;
587	but not even so did they persuade the heart in his breast,
588	until his chamber was being hit hard, and onto the towers
589	the Curetes were climbing and setting fire to the great city.
590	And then at last his fair-belted wife begged Meleager,
591	weeping, and recounted to him all
592	the sorrows that come to men whose city is taken:
593	they kill the men, and fire levels the city,
594	and strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women.
595	And his heart was stirred as he heard of these evil deeds,
596	and he went, and put on his gleaming armor.
597	So he warded off the evil day from the Aetolians,
598	yielding to his own heart; but they no longer paid him the gifts,
599	many and pleasing, and he warded off the evil all the same.
600	But do not have such thoughts in your mind, and let no god
601	turn you that way, my friend; it would be worse
602	to defend the ships when they are already burning. While the gifts are offered,
603	come; for the Achaeans will honor you as equal to a god.
604	But if you enter man-destroying war without the gifts,
605	you will no longer be held in the same honor, though you turn back the war.”
606	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
607	“Phoenix, old father, nurtured by Zeus, I have no need
608	of this honor; I think I am honored by the portion of Zeus,
609	which will keep me beside the curved ships, as long as breath
610	stays in my breast and my knees have strength to move.
611	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
612	do not confuse my heart with weeping and grieving
613	to do a favor to the hero son of Atreus; you have no need
614	to love him, or you will make yourself hateful to me, who love you.
615	It is good for you to vex with me the one who vexes me.
616	Be king equally with me, and take half my honor.
617	These men will carry the message; but you stay here and lie down
618	in a soft bed; and when dawn appears
619	we will consider whether to go home to our own or to stay.”
620	He spoke, and nodded to Patroclus with his brows in silence
621	to spread a thick bed for Phoenix, so that as soon as possible
622	the others might think of going home from the hut. And Ajax,
623	the godlike son of Telamon, spoke among them:
624	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
625	let us go; for I do not think the end of our errand
626	will be accomplished on this journey; and we must report
627	the word to the Danaans at once, though it is not good,
628	who are sitting now, I suppose, waiting for it. But Achilles
629	has made savage the proud heart in his breast,
630	hard man, and does not regard the friendship of his companions,
631	with which we honored him beside the ships above all others,
632	pitiless one. And yet a man accepts from the killer of his brother
633	the penalty, or for his own son who is dead;
634	and the killer stays there among his people, having paid much,
635	and the other's heart and proud spirit are held in check
636	once he has accepted the penalty; but in you the gods have put
637	a heart that is unrelenting and evil, for the sake of a girl,
638	one girl; and now we offer you seven, by far the best,
639	and many other things besides; so make your heart gracious,
640	and respect your own roof; we are here beneath your roof,
641	from the multitude of the Danaans, and we are eager above all others
642	to be the nearest and dearest to you, of all the Achaeans.”
643	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
644	“Telamonian Ajax, sprung from Zeus, lord of the people,
645	all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart;
646	but my heart swells with anger whenever I remember
647	how he treated me with contempt among the Argives,
648	the son of Atreus, as if I were some dishonored vagrant.
649	But you go and declare my message;
650	for I will not think of bloody war
651	until the son of wise-hearted Priam, brilliant Hector,
652	comes to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons,
653	killing the Argives, and burns the ships with fire.
654	But around my hut and my black ship,
655	I think, Hector will be held back, eager for battle though he is.”
656	So he spoke, and each man took a two-handled cup
657	and poured libation, and they went back along the ships; and Odysseus led the way.
658	And Patroclus bade his companions and the slave women
659	spread a thick bed for Phoenix as quickly as possible.
660	And they obeyed and spread the bed as he bade,
661	fleeces and a rug and the fine nap of linen.
662	There the old man lay down and waited for brilliant Dawn.
663	But Achilles slept in the inner part of the well-built hut,
664	and beside him lay a woman, whom he had brought from Lesbos,
665	the daughter of Phorbas, fair-cheeked Diomede.
666	And Patroclus lay down on the other side; and beside him too
667	lay fair-belted Iphis, whom brilliant Achilles gave him
668	when he took steep Scyros, the citadel of Enyeus.
669	And when they had come to the huts of the son of Atreus,
670	the sons of the Achaeans pledged them with golden cups,
671	standing up, one here, one there, and questioned them;
672	and first Agamemnon, lord of men, asked:
673	“Come, tell me, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans:
674	is he willing to ward off the consuming fire from the ships,
675	or has he refused, and does anger still hold his proud heart?”
676	Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus spoke to him in turn:
677	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
678	that man will not quench his anger, but is filled still more
679	with fury, and rejects you and your gifts.
680	He bids you yourself take counsel among the Argives
681	how you may save the ships and the army of the Achaeans;
682	and he himself threatened that when dawn appears
683	he will drag his well-benched, curving ships to the sea.
684	And he said he would advise the rest of you, too,
685	to sail away home, since you will no longer find the end
686	of steep Ilios; for far-seeing Zeus has held
687	his hand over it, and its people have taken heart.
688	So he spoke; and these men too who came with me are here to tell it,
689	Ajax and the two heralds, both wise men.
690	But the old man Phoenix lay down there, for so he bade him,
691	so that he may follow him in the ships to their dear native land
692	tomorrow, if he wishes; but he will not take him by force.”
693	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence,
694	amazed at his word, for he had spoken very forcefully.
695	For a long time the sons of the Achaeans were silent in their grief;
696	at last Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them:
697	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
698	I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus,
699	offering countless gifts; he is proud enough as it is;
700	now you have driven him much further into his pride.
701	But let us leave him alone, whether he goes
702	or stays; he will fight again when
703	the heart in his breast bids him and a god rouses him.
704	Come then, let us all do as I say:
705	now go to sleep, when you have satisfied your hearts
706	with food and wine; for in that is might and courage;
707	but when fair rosy-fingered Dawn appears,
708	quickly draw up the army and the horses before the ships,
709	urging them on, and fight yourself among the foremost.”
710	So he spoke, and all the kings approved,
711	admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses.
712	And then they poured libations and went each to his hut,
713	and there they lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep.
