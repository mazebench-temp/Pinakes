# Homer, Iliad, Book 12 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	So in the hut the valiant son of Menoetius
2	was tending wounded Eurypylus; and they fought on,
3	the Argives and Trojans, in a mass. And no longer
4	would the ditch of the Danaans hold them back, nor the wall above it,
5	the broad wall they had built to protect their ships, and had driven a ditch
6	around it; and they did not give glorious hecatombs to the gods,
7	so that it might keep safe the swift ships and the abundant plunder
8	that it held within. It was built against the will of the gods,
9	the immortals; and so it did not stand firm for long.
10	As long as Hector was alive and Achilles kept up his wrath,
11	and the city of lord Priam was unsacked,
12	so long the great wall of the Achaeans also stood firm.
13	But when the best of the Trojans had died,
14	and of the Argives many were beaten down and some were left,
15	and the city of Priam was sacked in the tenth year,
16	and the Argives had gone in their ships to their dear native land,
17	then Poseidon and Apollo took counsel
18	to bring the wall to nothing, leading against it the might of the rivers,
19	all that flow from the mountains of Ida to the sea,
20	the Rhesus and Heptaporus, the Caresus and Rhodius,
21	the Granicus and Aesepus, and brilliant Scamander,
22	and the Simoeis, where many oxhide shields and helmets
23	had fallen in the dust, and the race of men half-divine.
24	Phoebus Apollo turned the mouths of them all together,
25	and for nine days he sent their stream against the wall; and Zeus rained
26	without ceasing, so that he might the sooner make the walls float out to sea.
27	And the shaker of the earth himself, holding his trident in his hands,
28	led the way, and sent out on the waves all the foundations
29	of logs and stones that the Achaeans had laid with toil,
30	and made all smooth beside the strong-flowing Hellespont,
31	and covered the great beach over again with sand,
32	when he had brought the wall to nothing; and he turned the rivers back to flow
33	down their channels, where before they had poured their fair-flowing water.
34	So Poseidon and Apollo were to do in time to come;
35	but now battle and cries blazed around
36	the well-built wall, and the timbers of the towers rang
37	as they were struck; and the Argives, beaten down by the lash of Zeus,
38	were penned in beside the hollow ships, and held back there
39	in fear of Hector, the mighty master of rout;
40	and he fought as before, like a storm wind.
41	And as when among hounds and hunting men
42	a boar or a lion turns this way and that, exulting in his strength,
43	and they, arraying themselves in close order like a tower,
44	stand against him and hurl thick and fast
45	their spears from their hands; but his glorious heart
46	never fears or flees, and it is his pride that kills him;
47	and again and again he turns, testing the ranks of men,
48	and wherever he charges, there the ranks of men give way;
49	so Hector went through the throng and begged his companions,
50	urging them to cross the ditch; but his horses
51	did not dare, swift-footed though they were, and neighed loudly, standing at the very
52	edge; for the ditch frightened them off,
53	a wide one, not to be leapt across from close at hand, nor crossed
54	with ease; for overhanging banks stood all along it
55	on both sides, and on top it was fitted with stakes,
56	sharp ones, which the sons of the Achaeans had planted,
57	close-set and great, a defense against enemy men.
58	There a horse drawing a well-wheeled chariot could not easily
59	go in; but the men on foot were eager to see if they could accomplish it.
60	Then Polydamas came and stood beside bold Hector and said:
61	“Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and the allies,
62	it is foolish of us to drive our swift horses through the ditch;
63	it is very hard to cross; for there are stakes,
64	sharp ones, standing in it, and the wall of the Achaeans close against them,
65	where there is no way to get down and fight
66	for horsemen; for it is narrow, and there I think they will be wounded.
67	For if, meaning them evil, he is destroying them utterly,
68	Zeus who thunders on high, and wishes to help the Trojans,
69	I for my part would wish it to happen even now,
70	that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos;
71	but if they turn back, and a counterattack comes
72	from the ships, and we plunge into the ditch they have dug,
73	then I think not even a messenger will get back
74	to the city again, once we have been turned back by the Achaeans.
75	Come then, let us all do as I say:
76	let the attendants hold the horses at the ditch,
77	and we ourselves, on foot, armed in our armor,
78	all follow Hector in a body; and the Achaeans
79	will not stand, if the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon them.”
80	So spoke Polydamas, and his safe counsel pleased Hector,
81	and at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor.
82	Nor did the other Trojans gather on their chariots,
83	but all sprang down, when they saw brilliant Hector.
84	Then each man gave orders to his own charioteer
85	to hold the horses in good order there at the ditch;
86	and they, parting and arraying themselves,
87	marshaled in five companies, followed with their leaders.
88	Some went with Hector and noble Polydamas,
89	who were the most and the best, and were most eager
90	to break through the wall and fight at the hollow ships.
91	And Cebriones went with them as a third; for beside the chariot
92	Hector had left another man, weaker than Cebriones.
93	The second were led by Paris and Alcathous and Agenor,
94	the third by Helenus and godlike Deiphobus,
95	two sons of Priam; and a third with them was Asius, the hero,
96	Asius, son of Hyrtacus, whom his horses carried from Arisbe,
97	great and tawny horses, from the river Selleis.
98	The fourth were led by the brave son of Anchises,
99	Aeneas, and with him the two sons of Antenor,
100	Archelochus and Acamas, well skilled in every kind of fighting.
101	And Sarpedon led the famous allies,
102	and took Glaucus with him and warlike Asteropaeus;
103	for they seemed to him to be the best by far
104	of the rest, after himself; but he stood out even among them all.
105	And when they had locked themselves together with their well-made oxhides,
106	they went straight at the Danaans in eager haste, and said the enemy would no longer
107	hold out, but would fall among the black ships.
108	Then the other Trojans and their far-famed allies
109	obeyed the counsel of blameless Polydamas;
110	but the son of Hyrtacus, Asius, leader of men, would not
111	leave his horses there, and his charioteer and attendant,
112	but drove close to the swift ships with them,
113	the fool! He was not going to escape the evil fates
114	and, glorying in his horses and chariot, from the ships
115	go back home to windy Ilios;
116	for before that, fate of evil name enfolded him
117	through the spear of Idomeneus, noble son of Deucalion.
118	For he went toward the left of the ships, where the Achaeans
119	were coming back from the plain with their horses and chariots;
120	there he drove his horses and chariot through; nor did he find at the gates
121	the doors shut and the long bar in place,
122	but men held them wide open, in case some companion
123	fleeing from the war they might bring safe to the ships.
124	Straight there, with his mind set on it, he drove his horses, and they followed with him
125	with shrill cries; for they said the Achaeans would no longer
126	hold out, but would fall among the black ships,
127	the fools! At the gates they found two men, the best,
128	high-hearted sons of Lapith spearmen,
129	the one the son of Peirithous, mighty Polypoetes,
130	the other Leonteus, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals.
131	These two, in front of the high gates,
132	stood like oaks in the mountains, with lofty crowns,
133	that withstand the wind and the rain all their days,
134	made fast by great roots that reach far down;
135	so these two, trusting in their hands and their strength,
136	awaited great Asius as he came on, and did not flee.
137	And they came straight for the well-built wall, holding their dry oxhides
138	up high, with a great shout,
139	around lord Asius and Iamenus and Orestes
140	and Adamas, son of Asius, and Thoon and Oenomaus.
141	For a while the two, being inside, kept rousing the well-greaved Achaeans
142	to fight in defense of the ships;
143	but when they saw them rushing at the wall,
144	the Trojans, and among the Danaans there rose shouting and rout,
145	the two darted out and fought in front of the gates,
146	like two wild boars that in the mountains
147	await the din of men and dogs coming on,
148	and darting sideways they break the wood around them,
149	cutting it off at the root, and a gnashing of tusks
150	goes on, until someone hits them and takes their life;
151	so rang the shining bronze on the chests of these two
152	as they were hit face on; for they fought very fiercely,
153	trusting in the men above them and in their own strength.
154	And these, with stones, from the well-built towers
155	kept shooting, defending themselves and the huts
156	and the swift-sailing ships; and the stones fell to the ground like snowflakes,
157	that a blustering wind, driving the shadowy clouds,
158	pours down thick upon the bountiful earth;
159	so the missiles poured from their hands, from the Achaeans
160	and from the Trojans too; and the helmets rang dry all around,
161	and the bossed shields, as men were struck with stones like millstones.
162	Then he groaned aloud and struck both his thighs,
163	Asius, son of Hyrtacus, and in his indignation he spoke:
164	“Father Zeus, so you too, it seems, have become a lover of lies
165	through and through; for I did not think the Achaean heroes
166	would hold back our might and our irresistible hands.
167	But they, like wasps with nimble waists, or bees,
168	that have made their homes beside a rocky road,
169	and do not leave their hollow house, but stand their ground
170	and fight off the hunting men for the sake of their young,
171	so these men, though they are only two, will not give way from the gates
172	until they have either killed or been taken.”
173	So he spoke, but he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this;
174	for his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector.
175	And others were fighting their battle around other gates;
176	and it is hard for me to tell all these things like a god;
177	for everywhere a wondrous fire had risen around the wall
178	of stone; and the Argives, troubled though they were, of necessity
179	defended the ships; and the gods were grieved at heart,
180	all who were the helpers of the Danaans in battle.
181	And the Lapiths joined in the war and the combat.
182	Then the son of Peirithous, mighty Polypoetes,
183	struck Damasus with his spear through the bronze-cheeked helmet;
184	nor did the bronze helmet hold it, but right through
185	the bronze point broke the bone, and the brain
186	inside was all spattered; and he beat him down for all his eagerness.
187	And then he slew and stripped Pylon and Ormenus.
188	And Leonteus, offshoot of Ares, struck the son of Antimachus,
189	Hippomachus, with his spear, hitting him at the belt.
190	And then, drawing his sharp sword from its sheath,
191	first he darted through the throng at Antiphates
192	and struck him hand to hand; and he was flung on his back on the ground;
193	and then Menon and Iamenus and Orestes,
194	all of them, one after another, he brought down to the bountiful earth.
195	While these were stripping them of their gleaming armor,
196	the young men who followed Polydamas and Hector,
197	who were the most and the best, and were most eager
198	to break through the wall and set the ships on fire,
199	still stood hesitating beside the ditch.
200	For a bird had come to them as they were eager to cross,
201	an eagle flying high, on the left, skirting the army,
202	carrying in its talons a blood-red serpent, monstrous,
203	alive and still struggling; and it had not yet forgotten the joy of battle,
204	for it struck the bird that held it, on the breast beside the neck,
205	bending back; and the eagle flung it away to the ground
206	in the pain of its wounds, and dropped it in the middle of the throng,
207	and with a scream it flew off on the blasts of the wind.
208	And the Trojans shuddered when they saw the writhing snake
209	lying in their midst, the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus.
210	Then Polydamas came and stood beside bold Hector and said:
211	“Hector, you always somehow rebuke me in the assemblies
212	when I give good counsel, since it is not fitting at all
213	for a man of the people to speak against you, neither in council
214	nor ever in war, but always to increase your power;
215	but now again I will speak as it seems to me best.
216	Let us not go on to fight the Danaans at the ships.
217	For this is how I think it will end, if truly
218	this bird came to the Trojans as they were eager to cross,
219	an eagle flying high, on the left, skirting the army,
220	carrying in its talons a blood-red serpent, monstrous,
221	alive; but it let it go at once, before it reached its own home,
222	and did not finish carrying it to give to its young.
223	So we, even if we break through the gates and the wall of the Achaeans
224	with great strength, and the Achaeans give way,
225	shall not come back in good order from the ships by the same paths;
226	for we shall leave many of the Trojans behind, whom the Achaeans
227	will cut down with the bronze, fighting in defense of the ships.
228	So would a prophet interpret it, one who clearly in his heart
229	knew of portents, and whom the people would obey.”
230	Looking at him darkly, Hector of the gleaming helmet said:
231	“Polydamas, what you say is no longer pleasing to me;
232	you know how to think of another word better than this.
233	But if you really say this in earnest,
234	then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits,
235	you who tell me to forget loud-thundering Zeus'
236	counsels, which he himself promised me and nodded his assent to;
237	and you tell me to obey long-winged birds,
238	which I do not heed at all, nor do I care
239	whether they go to the right, toward the dawn and the sun,
240	or to the left, toward the misty darkness.
241	But let us obey the counsel of great Zeus,
242	who is lord over all mortals and immortals.
243	One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land.
244	Why are you afraid of the war and the combat?
245	For even if all the rest of us are killed
246	at the ships of the Argives, there is no fear that you will perish;
247	for your heart is not staunch against the enemy, nor eager for battle.
248	But if you hold back from the combat, or turn any other man
249	away from the war, beguiling him with your words,
250	at once, struck by my spear, you will lose your life.”
251	So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him
252	with a wondrous din; and Zeus who delights in thunder
253	roused from the mountains of Ida a storm of wind
254	that carried the dust straight at the ships; and the Achaeans'
255	minds he bewitched, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector.
256	Trusting in his portents and in their own strength,
257	they tried to break through the great wall of the Achaeans.
258	They tore at the copings of the towers, and threw down the battlements,
259	and pried at the jutting buttresses, which the Achaeans
260	had set first in the ground to be the props of the towers.
261	These they dragged out, and they hoped to break the Achaeans' wall
262	through; but not even yet did the Danaans draw back from their path,
263	but they fenced the battlements with oxhides
264	and shot from them at the enemy as they came up under the wall.
265	And the two Ajaxes, calling out orders, ranged along the towers
266	in every direction, urging on the might of the Achaeans.
267	One with soothing words, another with harsh words
268	they rebuked, whomever they saw holding back from the battle altogether:
269	“Friends, whoever among the Argives is outstanding, or middling,
270	or worse, since men are by no means all alike
271	in war, now there is work for all;
272	and you yourselves know this, I think. Let no one turn back
273	toward the ships at the sound of the man who shouts at us,
274	but press forward, and urge one another on,
275	in the hope that Zeus may grant it, the Olympian lord of the lightning,
276	that we thrust back the quarrel and drive the enemy to the city.”
277	So the two, shouting out in front, urged on the battle of the Achaeans.
278	And as the flakes of snow fall thick
279	on a winter's day, when Zeus the counselor has set out
280	to snow, showing men these arrows of his;
281	and he lulls the winds and pours it down steadily, until he has covered
282	the peaks of the high mountains and the high headlands
283	and the lotus plains and the rich tilled fields of men,
284	and it is poured over the harbors and shores of the gray sea,
285	but the wave that washes up against it holds it off; and all else
286	is wrapped over from above, when the rain of Zeus falls heavy;
287	so from both sides the stones flew thick,
288	some at the Trojans, and some from the Trojans at the Achaeans,
289	as they pelted one another; and over the whole wall a thudding rose.
290	Not even then would the Trojans and shining Hector
291	have broken the gates of the wall and the long bar,
292	had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon,
293	against the Argives, like a lion against cattle with curving horns.
294	At once he held before him his shield, balanced on every side,
295	beautiful, of bronze, beaten out, which the smith
296	had beaten, and inside it he stitched oxhides close together
297	with golden wires running all around the circle.
298	Holding it before him, shaking two spears,
299	he went on like a mountain-bred lion, which has long
300	gone without meat, and his proud spirit bids him
301	make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold;
302	for even if he finds herdsmen there beside it
303	with dogs and spears keeping watch over the sheep,
304	he has no mind to be driven from the steading without a try,
305	but either he springs on one and seizes it, or he himself
306	is hit among the foremost by a javelin from a swift hand;
307	so then his spirit sent godlike Sarpedon
308	to rush at the wall and break through the battlements.
309	And at once he spoke to Glaucus, son of Hippolochus:
310	“Glaucus, why is it that we two are honored most
311	with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups
312	in Lycia, and all look on us as gods,
313	and we hold a great domain by the banks of the Xanthus,
314	a fair one, of orchard and wheat-bearing plowland?
315	So now we must take our stand among the foremost Lycians
316	and meet the blazing battle,
317	so that one of the close-armored Lycians may say this:
318	‘Truly not without glory are they who rule over Lycia,
319	these kings of ours, and they eat fat sheep
320	and choice wine, honey-sweet; but there is strength in them too,
321	noble strength, since they fight among the foremost Lycians.’
322	My friend, if, once we had escaped this war,
323	we two were to be ageless and immortal forever,
324	then neither would I myself fight among the foremost,
325	nor would I send you into the battle where men win glory;
326	but now, since in any case the fates of death stand over us,
327	countless, which no mortal can flee or escape,
328	let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us.”
329	So he spoke, and Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey;
330	and the two went straight on, leading the great host of the Lycians.
331	And seeing them, Menestheus, son of Peteos, shuddered;
332	for it was to his tower that they came, bringing misery.
333	And he peered along the rampart of the Achaeans, to see whether he could catch sight of
334	one of the leaders who might ward off disaster from his companions;
335	and he caught sight of the two Ajaxes, insatiable of war,
336	standing there, and Teucer, who had just come from his hut,
337	close by; but there was no way for him to make them hear by shouting,
338	so great was the din, and the shout reached the sky,
339	as shields were struck, and helmets with horsehair crests,
340	and the gates; for all of them had been shut, and the enemy, standing at them,
341	were trying to break them by force and come in.
342	Quickly he sent the herald Thootes to Ajax:
343	“Go, brilliant Thootes, run and call Ajax,
344	both of them, rather; for that would be by far the best of all,
345	since sheer destruction will soon be wrought here.
346	For the leaders of the Lycians have pressed on so hard, who even before now
347	have been violent in the fierce battles.
348	But if over there too toil and quarrel have arisen,
349	then at least let valiant Telamonian Ajax come alone,
350	and let Teucer follow with him, well skilled with the bow.”
351	So he spoke, and the herald heard him and did not disobey,
352	and he went running along the wall of the bronze-armored Achaeans,
353	and came and stood beside the Ajaxes, and at once he said:
354	“Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives,
355	the dear son of Peteos, whom Zeus has nurtured, bids you
356	go there, so that you may face the toil, even for a little while,
357	both of you, rather; for that would be by far the best of all,
358	since sheer destruction will soon be wrought there.
359	For the leaders of the Lycians have pressed on so hard, who even before now
360	have been violent in the fierce battles.
361	But if here too war and quarrel have arisen,
362	then at least let valiant Telamonian Ajax go alone,
363	and let Teucer follow with him, well skilled with the bow.”
364	So he spoke, and great Telamonian Ajax did not disobey.
365	At once he addressed the son of Oileus with winged words:
366	“Ajax, you two stay here, you and mighty Lycomedes,
367	and stand and urge the Danaans to fight with all their might;
368	but I will go over there and face the war,
369	and I will come back again quickly, when I have helped them well.”
370	So he spoke, and Telamonian Ajax went away,
371	and with him went Teucer, his brother by the same father,
372	and with them Pandion carried Teucer's curved bow.
373	When they came to the tower of great-hearted Menestheus,
374	going along inside the wall, they came to men hard pressed;
375	and onto the battlements, like a dark storm, were climbing
376	the mighty leaders and rulers of the Lycians;
377	and they clashed, fighting face to face, and the war cry rose.
378	And Telamonian Ajax was the first to kill his man,
379	Sarpedon's companion, great-hearted Epicles,
380	hitting him with a jagged boulder, which inside the wall
381	lay, a big one, uppermost beside the battlement; nor could
382	a man easily hold it in both hands, not even one in his prime,
383	such as mortals are now; but he lifted it and hurled it from above,
384	and crushed the four-ridged helmet, and smashed the bones
385	of the head all together; and he, like a diver,
386	fell from the high tower, and his spirit left his bones.
387	And Teucer hit Glaucus, the mighty son of Hippolochus,
388	with an arrow as he rushed at the high wall,
389	where he saw his arm laid bare, and stopped him from battle.
390	And he leapt back from the wall unseen, so that none of the Achaeans
391	should see him hit and boast over him with words.
392	And grief came upon Sarpedon as Glaucus went away,
393	as soon as he noticed; but even so he did not forget the joy of battle,
394	but Alcmaon, son of Thestor, he hit with his spear
395	and stabbed, and drew the spear out; and following the spear he fell
396	headlong, and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze.
397	And Sarpedon seized the battlement in his strong hands
398	and pulled, and it all came away, through and through; and above,
399	the wall was laid bare, and he made a path for many.
400	And Ajax and Teucer came at him together; and the one with an arrow
401	hit the shining strap around his chest
402	of his man-covering shield; but Zeus warded off the fates
403	from his son, so that he should not be beaten down at the sterns of the ships;
404	and Ajax leapt on him and stabbed his shield; but not right through
405	went the spear, and it drove him back as he pressed on.
406	And he gave ground a little from the battlement; but he did not altogether
407	draw back, since his heart hoped to win glory.
408	And he called out to the godlike Lycians, wheeling around:
409	“Lycians, why do you let go of your furious courage like this?
410	It is hard for me, mighty though I am,
411	to break through alone and make a path to the ships;
412	but come on with me; the work of more men is better.”
413	So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke,
414	bore down the harder around their lord, the counselor.
415	And the Argives on the other side strengthened their battalions
416	inside the wall, and a great task appeared before them;
417	for neither could the mighty Lycians break the Danaans'
418	wall and make a path to the ships,
419	nor could the Danaan spearmen ever thrust the Lycians
420	back from the wall, once they had first come close to it.
421	But as two men contend over boundary stones,
422	holding measuring rods in their hands, in a common field,
423	and in a narrow space they strive for an equal share,
424	so the battlements kept these apart; and over them
425	they hacked at the oxhide shields around one another's chests,
426	the well-rounded shields and the fluttering bucklers of hide.
427	And many were stabbed in the flesh with the pitiless bronze,
428	both whoever turned and laid his back bare
429	as they fought, and many right through the shield itself.
430	Everywhere the towers and battlements were spattered with the blood of men
431	on both sides, from Trojans and Achaeans.
432	But even so they could not put the Achaeans to rout,
433	but they held, as an honest working woman holds the scales,
434	who, holding the weight and the wool, lifts them up on either side
435	until they are equal, so that she may win a meager wage for her children;
436	so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight,
437	until Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector,
438	son of Priam, who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans.
439	And he shouted piercingly, making himself heard to the Trojans:
440	“Rise up, horse-taming Trojans, break through the wall
441	of the Argives, and throw the wondrous fire on the ships.”
442	So he spoke, urging them on, and they all heard him with their ears,
443	and rushed straight at the wall in a body; and then
444	they climbed onto the copings, holding their sharpened spears,
445	and Hector snatched up and carried a stone that in front of the gates
446	stood, thick at the base, but at the top
447	it was sharp; not even two men, the best of the people,
448	could easily heave it from the ground onto a wagon,
449	such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone.
450	The son of crooked-counseling Cronus made it light for him.
451	And as a shepherd easily carries the fleece of a ram,
452	taking it in one hand, and the weight presses on him little,
453	so Hector lifted the stone and carried it straight at the doors
