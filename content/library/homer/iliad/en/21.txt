# Homer, Iliad, Book 21 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river,
2	the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,
3	there he split them, and some he drove across the plain
4	toward the city, where the Achaeans had been fleeing in terror
5	the day before, when shining Hector raged;
6	there they poured forward in panic, and Hera spread
7	a deep mist before them to hold them back; and half of them
8	were penned into the deep-streaming, silver-eddying river,
9	and they fell in with a great crash, and the sheer streams roared,
10	and the banks all around resounded loudly; and with a shout
11	they swam this way and that, whirled around in the eddies.
12	As when, under the rush of fire, locusts flutter up into the air
13	to flee to a river, and the tireless fire burns,
14	springing up suddenly, and they cower down into the water,
15	so before Achilles deep-eddying Xanthus'
16	sounding stream was filled with horses and men, all mingled together.
17	But he, sprung from Zeus, left his spear there on the bank,
18	leaning against the tamarisks, and leapt in like a god,
19	holding only his sword, and he devised evil deeds in his mind,
20	and he struck, turning this way and that; and an ugly groaning rose
21	from the men struck by the sword, and the water grew red with blood.
22	As before a huge-bellied dolphin the other fishes
23	flee and fill the recesses of a harbor with good anchorage,
24	in fear; for he devours greedily whatever he catches;
25	so the Trojans, down the streams of the dread river,
26	cowered under the banks. And when his hands grew weary with killing,
27	he chose twelve young men out of the river alive,
28	as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died.
29	These he led out, dazed, like fawns,
30	and bound their hands behind them with well-cut straps,
31	which they themselves used to wear on their plaited tunics,
32	and gave them to his companions to take down to the hollow ships.
33	But he sprang at them again, eager to cut men down.
34	There he met a son of Priam, descendant of Dardanus,
35	Lycaon, fleeing out of the river, whom he himself had once
36	taken and led away against his will from his father's orchard,
37	coming out by night; and he was cutting from a wild fig tree with the sharp bronze
38	young shoots, to be the rails of a chariot;
39	and upon him, an evil he never foresaw, came brilliant Achilles.
40	And that time he sold him into well-built Lemnos,
41	taking him there in his ships, and the son of Jason paid the price;
42	and from there a guest-friend ransomed him, and paid much,
43	Eetion of Imbros, and sent him to shining Arisbe;
44	and from there he escaped and came to his father's house.
45	For eleven days he delighted his heart with his friends,
46	after he came from Lemnos; but on the twelfth, again,
47	a god threw him into the hands of Achilles, who was to
48	send him on his way to the house of Hades against his will.
49	And when brilliant swift-footed Achilles caught sight of him,
50	naked, without helmet or shield, and he had no spear either,
51	for he had flung all these from him to the ground, since sweat wore him down
52	as he fled out of the river, and weariness was overpowering his knees beneath him;
53	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
54	“Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes:
55	surely the great-hearted Trojans whom I have killed
56	will rise up again from under the misty darkness,
57	now that this man too has come back, escaping the pitiless day,
58	though he was sold into sacred Lemnos; nor did it hold him,
59	the deep of the gray sea, which holds back many against their will.
60	But come, the point of my spear too
61	he shall taste, so that I may see and learn in my mind
62	whether he will come back from there just the same, or whether
63	the life-giving earth will hold him back, which holds back even the strong.”
64	So he pondered as he waited; and the other came near him, dazed,
65	eager to clasp his knees, and above all he wished in his heart
66	to escape evil death and black doom.
67	Now brilliant Achilles lifted up his long spear,
68	eager to stab him, but he ran in under it and clasped his knees,
69	stooping; and the spear, passing over his back, in the ground
70	stood fixed, straining to glut itself on the flesh of a man.
71	And with one hand he took hold of his knees and begged him,
72	and with the other he held the sharpened spear and would not let it go;
73	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
74	“I implore you, Achilles; respect me and pity me;
75	I am as good as a revered suppliant to you, you who are nurtured by Zeus;
76	for with you first I tasted the grain of Demeter
77	on the day when you took me in the well-built orchard,
78	and you sold me, taking me far away from my father and my friends,
79	into sacred Lemnos, and I fetched you the worth of a hundred oxen.
80	And now I have been ransomed, paying three times as much; and this dawn
81	is the twelfth since I came to Ilios,
82	after much suffering; and now again into your hands
83	deadly fate has put me; I must be hateful to father Zeus,
84	who has given me to you again; and to be short-lived my mother
85	bore me, Laothoe, daughter of the old man Altes,
86	Altes, who rules over the war-loving Leleges,
87	holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis.
88	His daughter Priam had for a wife, and many others too;
89	and two of us were born of her, and you will cut the throats of both.
90	Him you brought down among the foremost foot soldiers,
91	godlike Polydorus, when you struck him with your sharp spear;
92	and now here evil will come to me; for I do not think
93	I shall escape your hands, since a god has brought me here.
94	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
95	do not kill me, since I am not of the same womb as Hector,
96	who killed your companion, gentle and strong.”
97	So the shining son of Priam spoke to him,
98	begging with words; but the voice he heard would not be soothed:
99	“Fool, do not offer me ransom, nor make speeches;
100	for before Patroclus met his fated day,
101	until then it was somehow dearer to my heart to spare
102	Trojans, and many I took alive and sold;
103	but now there is no one who shall escape death, whomever a god
104	throws into my hands before Ilios,
105	no one of all the Trojans, and above all of the sons of Priam.
106	No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this?
107	Patroclus died too, who was far better than you.
108	Do you not see what a man I am too, how handsome and how tall?
109	I am the son of a brave father, and a goddess was the mother who bore me;
110	yet over me too hang death and mighty fate;
111	there will come a morning or an evening or a midday
112	when someone will take the life from me too in the work of Ares,
113	striking me either with a spear or with an arrow from the string.”
114	So he spoke, and the other's knees and dear heart were loosened where he was;
115	he let go of the spear, and sat down, stretching out both
116	his hands; and Achilles, drawing his sharp sword,
117	struck him on the collarbone beside the neck, and all the way in
118	the two-edged sword went; and face down on the ground
119	he lay stretched out; and the dark blood flowed and soaked the earth.
120	And Achilles took him by the foot and flung him into the river to be swept away,
121	and boasting over him he spoke winged words:
122	“Lie there now among the fishes, who will lick the blood
123	from your wound and care nothing for you; nor will your mother
124	lay you on a bier and lament for you, but Scamander
125	will carry you, eddying, into the broad bosom of the sea.
126	Leaping along the wave, one will dart up under the dark ripple,
127	some fish, to eat the white fat of Lycaon.
128	Perish, until we reach the city of sacred Ilios,
129	you fleeing, and I ravaging behind you.
130	Not even the river, fair-flowing and silver-eddying though it is,
131	will protect you, to whom you have long sacrificed many bulls,
132	and thrown single-hoofed horses alive into its eddies.
133	But even so you will perish by an evil fate, until all of you
134	pay for the slaughter of Patroclus and the ruin of the Achaeans,
135	whom you killed beside the swift ships while I was away.”
136	So he spoke, and the river grew all the more angry in his heart,
137	and he pondered in his heart how he might stop from his toil
138	brilliant Achilles, and ward off ruin from the Trojans.
139	Meanwhile the son of Peleus, holding his long-shadowed spear,
140	leapt at Asteropaeus, eager to kill him,
141	the son of Pelegon; and him the Axius of the broad streams
142	begot, and Periboea, of the daughters of Acessamenus
143	the eldest; for with her the deep-eddying river lay.
144	At him Achilles sprang, and he, coming out of the river, against him
145	stood, holding two spears; and might was put in his heart
146	by Xanthus, since he was angry for the young men slain in battle,
147	whom Achilles was cutting down along the stream, and did not pity them.
148	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
149	brilliant swift-footed Achilles spoke to him first:
150	“Who are you among men, and from where, that you have dared to come against me?
151	It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might.”
152	Then the shining son of Pelegon spoke to him in turn:
153	“Great-hearted son of Peleus, why do you ask about my lineage?
154	I come from deep-soiled Paeonia, which lies far away,
155	leading the Paeonian men with their long spears; and this is now
156	the eleventh dawn since I came to Ilios.
157	But my lineage is from the broad-flowing Axius,
158	the Axius, who sends the fairest water over the earth,
159	who begot Pelegon, famed for his spear; and he, they say,
160	was my father. Now let us fight, shining Achilles.”
161	So he spoke, threatening, and brilliant Achilles raised up
162	the Pelian ash; but with two spears at once, one from each hand,
163	the hero Asteropaeus threw, for he could use either hand.
164	With the one spear he hit the shield, but it did not go through
165	and break the shield; for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god;
166	and with the other he grazed the forearm of his
167	right arm, and the dark blood spurted; and the spear, passing over him,
168	was fixed in the ground, longing to glut itself on flesh.
169	Then second in his turn Achilles let fly his straight-flying ash
170	at Asteropaeus, eager to kill him,
171	and he missed him, but hit the high bank,
172	and drove the ash spear halfway into the bank.
173	Then the son of Peleus drew the sharp sword from beside his thigh
174	and leapt at him eagerly; and he could not draw Achilles' ash spear
175	out of the bank with his strong hand.
176	Three times he shook it, eager to draw it out,
177	and three times he held back from the effort; and the fourth time he wished in his heart
178	to bend and break the ash spear of the grandson of Aeacus,
179	but before that Achilles took his life with his sword at close quarters.
180	For he struck him in the belly beside the navel, and all
181	his bowels poured out on the ground, and darkness covered his eyes
182	as he gasped; and Achilles, springing upon his chest,
183	stripped off his armor, and spoke in triumph:
184	“Lie there as you are. It is hard for you to strive with the children of the mighty son of Cronus,
185	even though you are sprung from a river.
186	You say that you are of the lineage of a broad-flowing river,
187	but I claim to be of the lineage of great Zeus.
188	The man who begot me is lord over many Myrmidons,
189	Peleus, son of Aeacus; and Aeacus was the son of Zeus.
190	So, as Zeus is mightier than rivers that murmur to the sea,
191	so the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's.
192	For here beside you is a great river, if he can do anything
193	to help; but there is no fighting with Zeus, son of Cronus,
194	with whom not even lord Achelous can match himself,
195	nor the great strength of deep-flowing Oceanus,
196	from whom all rivers and all the sea
197	and all springs and deep wells flow;
198	yet even he fears the thunderbolt of great Zeus
199	and his dread thunder, when it resounds from the sky.”
200	He spoke, and drew the bronze spear out of the bank,
201	and left him there, since he had robbed him of his dear life,
202	lying in the sand, and the dark water lapped over him.
203	And the eels and the fishes busied themselves about him,
204	tearing at the fat about his kidneys and nibbling it away.
205	But he went on his way after the Paeonians who fight from chariots,
206	who were still fleeing in rout along the eddying river,
207	when they saw their best man in the fierce battle
208	brought down by force at the hands of the son of Peleus and by his sword.
209	There he killed Thersilochus and Mydon and Astypylus
210	and Mnesus and Thrasius and Aenius and Ophelestes;
211	and swift Achilles would have killed still more of the Paeonians,
212	had not the deep-eddying river spoken to him in anger,
213	in the likeness of a man, and called out from a deep eddy:
214	“Achilles, beyond all men you are strong, and beyond men you do evil;
215	for the gods themselves are always there to defend you.
216	If the son of Cronus has given you all the Trojans to destroy,
217	at least drive them out of me, and do your grim deeds across the plain;
218	for my lovely streams are full of corpses,
219	and I can find no way to pour my stream into the shining sea,
220	choked with corpses, and you go on killing, destroying them.
221	But come, leave off; I am filled with amazement, leader of the people.”
222	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
223	“So it shall be, Scamander, nurtured by Zeus, as you bid.
224	But I will not stop slaying the overbearing Trojans
225	until I have penned them in the city and made trial of Hector
226	face to face, whether he will bring me down or I him.”
227	So saying, he rushed on the Trojans like a god;
228	and then the deep-eddying river spoke to Apollo:
229	“Ah, god of the silver bow, child of Zeus, you have not kept
230	the plans of the son of Cronus, who charged you many times over
231	to stand by the Trojans and defend them, until there comes
232	the evening, late in its setting, and darkens the deep-soiled earth.”
233	He spoke; and Achilles, famed for his spear, leapt into the midst of him,
234	springing down from the bank; and the river rushed on him, raging with his swell,
235	and stirred up all his streams, churning, and thrust out the bodies,
236	the many that lay thick in him, whom Achilles had killed;
237	these he cast out, bellowing like a bull,
238	onto the dry land; and the living he saved in his fair streams,
239	hiding them in his deep, great eddies.
240	And terribly around Achilles the churning wave rose up,
241	and the stream, falling on his shield, pushed him back; nor with his feet
242	could he keep his footing; and he caught hold of an elm with his hands,
243	well-grown and great; and it fell, torn out by the roots,
244	and tore away the whole bank, and held back the fair streams
245	with its thick branches, and bridged the river itself,
246	falling full length into it; and he, leaping up out of the eddy,
247	darted away to fly over the plain on his swift feet,
248	in fear; but the great god did not stop, and rose against him,
249	darkening at the crest, so that he might stop from his toil
250	brilliant Achilles, and ward off ruin from the Trojans.
251	And the son of Peleus sprang away as far as the cast of a spear,
252	with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter,
253	which is at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds;
254	like him he darted away, and on his chest the bronze
255	rang terribly; and swerving out from under the river
256	he fled, and it came flowing behind him with a great din.
257	As when a man leading a channel from a spring of dark water
258	guides the course of the water among his plants and gardens,
259	a mattock in his hands, clearing the obstructions out of the channel;
260	and as it flows on, all the pebbles beneath
261	are swept along; and it runs down quickly, murmuring,
262	on a sloping place, and outruns even the man who leads it;
263	so the wave of the stream kept overtaking Achilles,
264	nimble though he was; for the gods are stronger than men.
265	As often as brilliant swift-footed Achilles set out
266	to stand against it and learn whether all
267	the immortals were putting him to flight, who hold the wide sky,
268	so often the great wave of the river fallen from Zeus
269	beat upon his shoulders from above; and he leapt upward with his feet,
270	vexed in his heart; and the river was overpowering his knees beneath him,
271	rushing violently under him, and ate the ground away from under his feet.
272	And the son of Peleus groaned aloud, looking up into the wide sky:
273	“Father Zeus, how no one of the gods has undertaken to save me in my misery
274	from the river! After that I could suffer anything.
275	No other of the heavenly gods is so much to blame for this
276	as my dear mother, who bewitched me with lies;
277	she told me that under the wall of the armored Trojans
278	I would perish by the swift arrows of Apollo.
279	Would that Hector had killed me, the best man bred here;
280	then a brave man would have been the killer, and he would have slain a brave man.
281	But now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death,
282	trapped in a great river, like a swineherd boy
283	whom a torrent sweeps away as he crosses it in winter.”
284	So he spoke, and very swiftly Poseidon and Athena
285	came and stood near him, in form like men,
286	and taking his hand in their hands they pledged their faith with words.
287	And among them Poseidon the earth-shaker began to speak:
288	“Son of Peleus, do not tremble too much, nor be afraid;
289	for such helpers from among the gods are we two to you,
290	with the consent of Zeus, I and Pallas Athena;
291	so it is not your fate to be brought down by a river,
292	but he will soon subside, and you yourself will see it.
293	But we will give you shrewd counsel, if you will obey us:
294	do not stop your hands from war, common to all,
295	until you have penned the army within the glorious walls of Ilios,
296	the Trojan army, whoever escapes; and when you have taken Hector's life,
297	go back to the ships; we grant that you win a triumph.”
298	So saying, the two went away among the immortals;
299	and he went, for the command of the gods had greatly urged him on,
300	toward the plain; and all of it was full of the water that had poured out,
301	and much fine armor of young men slain in battle
302	was floating there, and corpses; and his knees leapt high
303	as he rushed straight on against the stream, nor did the broad-flowing river
304	hold him back; for Athena had put great strength in him.
305	Nor did Scamander abate his fury, but all the more
306	he was enraged at the son of Peleus, and made the wave of his stream rear its head,
307	lifting himself on high, and he called to Simoeis with a shout:
308	“Dear brother, let us both together check the strength of this man,
309	since soon he will sack the great city of lord Priam,
310	and the Trojans will not hold their ground in the fray.
311	But come to my aid quickly, and fill your streams
312	with water from your springs, and spur on all your torrents,
313	and raise a great wave, and stir up a great din
314	of logs and stones, so that we may stop this savage man,
315	who now prevails, and is bent on being the equal of the gods.
316	For I say that neither his strength will help him, nor his looks,
317	nor that fine armor, which, I think, deep at the bottom of the lake
318	will lie covered under the slime; and himself
319	I will wrap in the sands, pouring heaps of gravel,
320	countless, around him, nor will the Achaeans know how
321	to gather his bones, so much silt will I cover him with from above.
322	Right there his mound shall be made, and he will have no need
323	of a heaped-up tomb, when the Achaeans hold his funeral.”
324	He spoke, and rushed on Achilles, churning, raging on high,
325	seething with foam and blood and corpses.
326	And the surging wave of the river fallen from Zeus
327	stood, lifted high, and was bringing the son of Peleus down;
328	and Hera gave a great shout, terribly afraid for Achilles,
329	lest the great deep-eddying river sweep him away,
330	and at once she spoke to Hephaestus, her dear son:
331	“Rise, crook-footed one, my child; for against you
332	we judged eddying Xanthus to be matched in battle;
333	but come to our aid quickly, and let your great flame shine forth.
334	And I will go and rouse the West Wind and the brightening South Wind
335	to a hard storm out of the sea,
336	to burn away the heads and the armor of the Trojans,
337	carrying the evil blaze; and you, along the banks of Xanthus,
338	burn the trees, and set the river himself on fire; and do not let him at all
339	turn you back with soothing words and threats;
340	and do not stop your fury before then, but when I
341	call out with a cry, then hold back the tireless fire.”
342	So she spoke, and Hephaestus made ready a wondrous fire.
343	First the fire blazed up on the plain, and burned the bodies,
344	the many that lay thick on it, whom Achilles had killed;
345	and the whole plain was dried, and the bright water was checked.
346	As when the North Wind of late summer a newly watered orchard
347	quickly dries, and whoever tends it rejoices;
348	so the whole plain was dried, and he burned up
349	the bodies; and then he turned on the river the flame, gleaming all over.
350	The elms and the willows and the tamarisks were burning,
351	and the lotus was burning, and the rushes and the galingale,
352	which grew in plenty about the fair streams of the river;
353	and the eels and the fishes in the eddies were in distress,
354	which tumbled this way and that in the fair streams,
355	distressed by the breath of Hephaestus of many wiles.
356	And the force of the river was burning, and he spoke to him, and called him by name:
357	“Hephaestus, no one of the gods can match himself against you,
358	nor would I fight with you, blazing with fire as you are.
359	Cease from strife; and as for the Trojans, let brilliant Achilles at once
360	drive them out of their city; what have I to do with strife and help?”
361	He spoke, burning with fire, and his fair streams bubbled up.
362	As a cauldron boils within, pressed by a great fire,
363	as it melts the fat of a tender-fed hog,
364	bubbling up on every side, and dry sticks lie beneath it,
365	so his fair streams were blazing with fire, and the water boiled;
366	and it would not flow on, but was held back; and the blast of the strength
367	of Hephaestus, of much wisdom, wore him down. And he addressed Hera,
368	begging her earnestly, with winged words:
369	“Hera, why has your son attacked my stream to trouble it
370	above all others? I am surely not so much to blame
371	as all the others who help the Trojans.
372	But I will cease, if you bid me,
373	and let him cease too; and I will swear this oath besides,
374	never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans,
375	not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire,
376	burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans are burning it.”
377	But when the white-armed goddess Hera heard this,
378	at once she spoke to Hephaestus, her dear son:
379	“Hephaestus, hold back, my famous child; for it is not fitting
380	to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals.”
381	So she spoke, and Hephaestus quenched the wondrous fire,
382	and the wave rushed back down along its fair streams.
383	But when the might of Xanthus had been overpowered, then the two of them
384	ceased; for Hera held them back, angry though she was.
385	But among the other gods strife fell, heavy
386	and grievous, and the heart within them was blown two ways;
387	and they fell upon each other with a great crash, and the wide earth roared,
388	and the great sky trumpeted around. And Zeus heard it
389	as he sat on Olympus; and his heart within him laughed
390	for joy, as he saw the gods coming together in strife.
391	Then they stood apart no longer; for Ares began it,
392	the piercer of shields, and he first sprang at Athena,
393	holding his bronze spear, and spoke abusive words:
394	“Why again, you dog-fly, do you drive the gods together in strife,
395	with your gale of daring, and has your great heart sent you?
396	Do you not remember when you set Diomedes, son of Tydeus, on
397	to stab me, and you yourself took the spear seen by all
398	and drove it straight at me, and tore through my fair skin?
399	So now I think you will pay for all that you have done.”
400	So saying, he stabbed at the tasseled aegis,
401	the terrible one, which not even the thunderbolt of Zeus overpowers;
402	on it blood-stained Ares stabbed her with his long spear.
403	But she, giving ground, took up in her strong hand a stone
404	that lay on the plain, black and jagged and great,
405	which men of former times had set there to be the boundary stone of a field;
406	with it she struck furious Ares on the neck, and loosened his limbs.
407	He covered seven plethra as he fell, and grimed his hair with dust,
408	and his armor clattered about him; and Pallas Athena laughed,
409	and boasting over him she addressed him with winged words:
410	“Little fool, not even yet have you realized how much stronger
411	I claim to be than you, since you match your might with mine.
412	So you may pay in full for the Erinyes of your mother,
413	who in her anger is devising evil against you, because the Achaeans
414	you deserted, and give help to the overbearing Trojans.”
415	So she spoke, and turned her shining eyes away;
416	and Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took him by the hand and led him away,
417	groaning thick and fast; and hardly did he gather his spirit again.
418	And when the white-armed goddess Hera saw her,
419	at once she addressed Athena with winged words:
420	“Ah, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone,
421	there again that dog-fly is leading Ares, ruin of mortals,
422	out of destructive war, through the turmoil; go after her!”
423	So she spoke, and Athena rushed after her, and rejoiced in her heart,
424	and rushing at her, on the breast with her strong hand
425	she struck her; and her knees and dear heart were loosened where she was.
426	So the two of them lay on the bountiful earth,
427	and she, boasting over them, spoke winged words:
428	“Would that all who help the Trojans were now such as these
429	whenever they fight with the armored Argives,
430	so bold and hardy, as Aphrodite was
431	when she came as an ally to Ares, to meet my might;
432	then long ago we would have ceased from war,
433	having sacked the well-built citadel of Ilios.”
434	So she spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera smiled.
435	And the powerful earth-shaker spoke to Apollo:
436	“Phoebus, why is it that we two stand apart? It is not fitting,
437	when the others have begun; it would be the more shameful if without a fight
438	we went back to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus.
439	You begin, for you are younger by birth; for me it would not
440	be good, since I was born first and know more.
441	Little fool, how senseless is the heart you have! You do not even
442	remember all the evils we suffered around Ilios,
443	we two alone of the gods, when to proud Laomedon
444	we came from Zeus and served for hire for a year
445	for a fixed wage; and he was our master and gave us our orders.
446	I built a wall around the city for the Trojans,
447	wide and very beautiful, so that the city might be unbreakable;
448	and you, Phoebus, herded the shambling cattle with curving horns
449	on the slopes of wooded, many-folded Ida.
450	But when the joyful seasons were bringing on the term of our wage,
451	then he robbed us by force of our whole wage,
452	Laomedon, that terrible man, and sent us away with threats.
453	He threatened to bind together our feet, and our hands above,
454	and to sell us off to islands far away;
455	and he vowed that he would lop off the ears of both of us with the bronze.
456	And we two went back with resentful hearts,
457	angry over the wage, which he had promised and did not pay.
458	It is to his people that you now do a favor, and you do not try with us
459	to bring it about that the overbearing Trojans perish
460	utterly and wretchedly, with their children and their modest wives.”
461	Then far-working lord Apollo spoke to him in turn:
462	“Shaker of the earth, you would not call me sound of mind
463	if I should fight with you for the sake of mortals,
464	wretched creatures, who, like leaves, at one time
465	blaze with life, eating the fruit of the field,
466	and at another waste away lifeless. No, let us
467	cease from battle at once; and let them fight it out themselves.”
468	So he spoke, and turned back; for he was ashamed
469	to come to blows with his father's brother.
470	But his sister rebuked him harshly, the mistress of wild beasts,
471	Artemis of the wild, and spoke abusive words:
472	“So you flee, Far-Worker, and to Poseidon you have yielded
473	the whole victory, and given him the triumph for nothing!
474	Little fool, why do you carry a bow as empty as the wind?
475	Let me never again hear you in our father's halls
476	boasting, as before among the immortal gods,
477	that you would fight face to face against Poseidon.”
478	So she spoke, and far-working Apollo said nothing to her,
479	but the revered wife of Zeus, in anger,
480	rebuked her of the showering arrows with abusive words:
481	“How do you now mean, you shameless dog, to stand
482	against me? I am hard for you to match in might,
483	bow-bearer though you are, since a lion to women
484	Zeus has made you, and has given you leave to kill whichever of them you wish.
485	Truly it is better to slay wild beasts on the mountains
486	and wild deer than to fight in force with those stronger than you.
487	But if you wish to learn of war, so that you may know well
488	how much stronger I am, since you match your might with mine…”
489	She spoke, and caught both her hands at the wrist
490	with her left hand, and with her right took the bow from her shoulders,
491	and with the bow itself she beat her about the ears, smiling,
492	as she twisted this way and that; and the swift arrows fell out.
493	And the goddess fled from under her in tears, like a dove
494	that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock,
495	a cleft; nor was it her fate to be caught;
496	so she fled in tears, and left her bow there.
497	And the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to Leto:
498	“Leto, I will not fight with you at all; it is a hard thing
499	to trade blows with the wives of Zeus the cloud-gatherer;
500	no, with a ready heart, among the immortal gods,
501	boast that you overcame me by your mighty strength.”
502	So he spoke, and Leto gathered up the curved bow
503	and the arrows, fallen here and there in the whirl of dust.
504	When she had taken the bow she went back after her daughter;
505	and the daughter came to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus,
506	and the maiden sat in tears on her father's knees,
507	and the ambrosial robe trembled around her; and her father,
508	the son of Cronus, drew her to him, and laughing heartily asked her:
509	“Who of the heavenly gods has done such things to you, dear child,
510	for no reason, as if you were caught doing some wrong openly?”
511	Then the well-crowned, the clamorous goddess spoke to him in turn:
512	“It was your wife who battered me, father, white-armed Hera,
513	from whom strife and quarrel have fastened upon the immortals.”
514	So they spoke such words to one another,
515	but Phoebus Apollo went into sacred Ilios;
516	for the wall of the well-built city was on his mind,
517	lest the Danaans sack it against fate that day.
518	And the other gods who live forever went to Olympus,
519	some of them angry, and some exulting greatly,
520	and they sat down beside the father, lord of the dark clouds. But Achilles
521	was killing the Trojans themselves and their single-hoofed horses alike.
522	As when smoke goes up and reaches the wide sky
523	from a burning city, and the wrath of the gods has sent it up,
524	and it brings toil on all, and sends sorrows on many,
525	so Achilles brought toil and sorrows on the Trojans.
526	And the old man Priam stood on the divine tower,
527	and caught sight of huge Achilles; and before him
528	the Trojans were at once driven in confusion, in panic, and no courage
529	arose in them; and with a groan he came down from the tower to the ground,
530	urging on the famous gatekeepers along the wall:
531	“Hold the gates wide open in your hands until the people
532	come to the city in panic; for Achilles is here,
533	close by, driving them in confusion; now I think there will be ruin.
534	But when they have crowded inside the wall and draw breath,
535	put the closely fitted doors back in their place again;
536	for I fear that this baneful man may leap inside the wall.”
537	So he spoke, and they opened the gates and pushed back the bars;
538	and the gates, thrown open, made a light for them; and Apollo
539	leapt out in front, to ward off ruin from the Trojans.
540	And they, straight for the city and the high wall,
541	parched with thirst and grimed with dust, from the plain
542	were fleeing; and he pressed on fiercely with his spear, and a mighty frenzy
543	held his heart unceasingly, and he was eager to win glory.
544	There the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy,
545	had not Phoebus Apollo set brilliant Agenor on,
546	a man, the son of Antenor, noble and strong.
547	He cast daring into his heart, and himself stood
548	beside him, to ward off the heavy hands of Death,
549	leaning against the oak; and he was hidden in thick mist.
550	But when he caught sight of Achilles, sacker of cities,
551	he stood, and his heart heaved with many thoughts as he waited;
552	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
553	“Ah me! If I flee before mighty Achilles,
554	where the others too are being driven in confusion, in terror,
555	he will catch me even so, and cut my throat, a coward.
556	But what if I let these men be driven in confusion
557	by Achilles, son of Peleus, and on my feet, away from the wall,
558	flee elsewhere toward the Ilean plain, until I reach
559	the slopes of Ida and slip down into the brushwood;
560	and then in the evening, when I have bathed in the river,
561	and let the sweat dry from me, I might go back to Ilios.
562	But why does my heart debate these things with me?
563	I fear he may notice me as I go away from the city toward the plain,
564	and dart after me and catch me with his swift feet.
565	Then there will be no more escaping death and the fates;
566	for he is far too strong, beyond all men.
567	But what if I go out to meet him in front of the city?
568	For surely his flesh too can be pierced by the sharp bronze,
569	and there is one life in him, and men say that he
570	is mortal; but Zeus, son of Cronus, grants him glory.”
571	So saying, he crouched and waited for Achilles, and his valiant heart
572	within him was eager to make war and to fight.
573	As a leopard comes out of a deep thicket
574	to face a hunting man, and in her heart
575	neither fears nor flees, when she hears the baying of the hounds;
576	for even if he is first and stabs her or shoots her,
577	yet even pierced through with the spear she does not give up
578	her courage, until she either closes with him or is brought down;
579	so the son of noble Antenor, brilliant Agenor,
580	would not flee before he had made trial of Achilles.
581	But he held before him his shield, balanced on every side,
582	and took aim at him with his spear, and gave a great shout:
583	“Surely you hoped in your mind, shining Achilles,
584	that on this day you would sack the city of the proud Trojans,
585	you little fool; truly many sorrows will yet be wrought over it.
586	For there are many of us within it, valiant men,
587	who in front of our dear parents and wives and sons
588	guard Ilios; but you will meet your fate here,
589	terrible and bold warrior though you are.”
590	He spoke, and hurled the sharp javelin from his heavy hand,
591	and hit him on the shin below the knee, and did not miss.
592	And around him the greave of newly wrought tin
593	rang terribly; and the bronze sprang back from the man it struck,
594	and did not pierce it, for the gifts of the god held it back.
595	Then the son of Peleus rushed at godlike Agenor
596	in his turn; but Apollo no longer allowed him to win glory,
597	but snatched him away, and hid him in thick mist,
598	and sent him away to go out of the war in peace.
599	And then by a trick he kept the son of Peleus away from the people;
600	for the Far-Worker, in the likeness of Agenor himself in every way,
601	stood before his feet, and he rushed after him to chase him on foot.
602	While he was chasing him over the wheat-bearing plain,
603	turning him along the deep-eddying river Scamander,
604	as he ran a little way ahead—for by a trick Apollo was bewitching him,
605	so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet—
606	meanwhile the other Trojans in their rout came in a throng
607	gladly to the city, and the city was filled with them as they crowded in.
608	Nor did they dare any longer to wait for one another outside the city
609	and the wall, and learn who had escaped
610	and who had died in the war; but eagerly they poured
611	into the city, all whose feet and knees had saved them.
