# Homer, Iliad, Book 7 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So saying, shining Hector rushed out through the gates,
2	and with him went Alexander, his brother; and in their hearts
3	both were eager to make war and to fight.
4	And as a god gives to sailors who long for it
5	a fair wind, when they have grown weary with polished oars of fir,
6	rowing over the sea, and their limbs are loosened with weariness,
7	so these two appeared to the Trojans who longed for them.
8	Then each of them killed a man: the one, the son of lord Areithous,
9	Menesthius, who lived in Arne, whom the mace-man
10	Areithous fathered, and ox-eyed Phylomedusa bore;
11	and Hector struck Eioneus with his sharp spear
12	in the neck below the rim of his bronze helmet, and his limbs were loosened.
13	And Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, leader of the Lycian men,
14	struck Iphinous with his spear in the fierce battle,
15	the son of Dexius, as he was leaping up behind his swift horses,
16	in the shoulder; and he fell from the chariot to the ground, and his limbs were loosened.
17	And when the gray-eyed goddess Athena saw them
18	killing the Argives in the fierce battle,
19	she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus
20	to sacred Ilios; and Apollo rose up to meet her,
21	looking down from Pergamus, for he wanted victory for the Trojans;
22	and the two of them met each other beside the oak.
23	Lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to her first:
24	“Why have you come again so eagerly, daughter of great Zeus,
25	from Olympus? Has your great heart sent you?
26	Is it to give the Danaans the victory that turns the battle?
27	For you have no pity at all for the Trojans as they perish.
28	But if you would listen to me, it would be far better:
29	let us now stop the war and the combat
30	for today; afterward they will fight again, until they find the end
31	of Ilios, since this is what has pleased the hearts
32	of you immortal goddesses, to sack this city.”
33	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
34	“So be it, Far-Worker; for with this in mind I too
35	came from Olympus among the Trojans and the Achaeans.
36	But come, how do you mean to stop the war of men?”
37	Then lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to her in turn:
38	“Let us rouse the strong might of Hector, tamer of horses,
39	in the hope that he may challenge one of the Danaans, man to man, alone,
40	to fight him face to face in grim combat,
41	and that the bronze-greaved Achaeans, indignant,
42	may rouse up one man alone to fight with brilliant Hector.”
43	So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena did not disobey.
44	And Helenus, the dear son of Priam, understood in his heart
45	the plan that pleased the gods as they took counsel;
46	and he came and stood beside Hector and spoke to him:
47	“Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
48	will you listen to me now? For I am your brother.
49	Make the other Trojans and all the Achaeans sit down,
50	and you yourself challenge whoever is best of the Achaeans
51	to fight you face to face in grim combat;
52	for it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end;
53	so I have heard the voice of the everlasting gods.”
54	So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly when he heard his words,
55	and he went into the middle and held back the battalions of the Trojans,
56	gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down.
57	And Agamemnon made the well-greaved Achaeans sit down;
58	and Athena and Apollo, god of the silver bow,
59	sat down, like birds, like vultures,
60	on the high oak of aegis-bearing father Zeus,
61	delighting in the men; and their ranks sat close-packed,
62	bristling with shields and helmets and spears.
63	As the ripple of the West Wind spreads over the sea
64	when it has just risen, and the sea grows dark beneath it,
65	so sat the ranks of the Achaeans and the Trojans
66	on the plain; and Hector spoke between the two armies:
67	“Hear me, Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans,
68	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
69	The son of Cronus, throned on high, did not fulfill our oaths,
70	but, meaning evil, he ordains it for both sides,
71	until either you take Troy of the fine towers
72	or are yourselves beaten beside your seafaring ships.
73	Among you are the best of all the Achaeans;
74	of these let the one whose heart now bids him fight with me
75	come here out of them all to be champion against brilliant Hector.
76	And this I declare, and let Zeus be our witness:
77	if that man takes my life with the long-edged bronze,
78	let him strip my armor and carry it to the hollow ships,
79	but give my body back to be taken home, so that when I am dead
80	the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire.
81	But if I take his life, and Apollo gives me triumph,
82	I will strip his armor and carry it to sacred Ilios
83	and hang it on the temple of Apollo the Far-Shooter,
84	but his corpse I will give back to the well-benched ships,
85	so that the long-haired Achaeans may give him burial
86	and heap up a mound for him beside the broad Hellespont.
87	And someday one of the men born hereafter will say,
88	sailing in his many-benched ship over the wine-dark sea:
89	‘This is the mound of a man who died long ago,
90	whom once, when he was the best in the fighting, shining Hector killed.’
91	So someone will say one day; and my fame will never perish.”
92	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence;
93	they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept.
94	At last Menelaus stood up and spoke among them,
95	reviling them with reproaches, and he groaned deeply at heart:
96	“Ah me, you braggarts, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer!
97	Surely this will be a disgrace, dreadful beyond dread,
98	if none of the Danaans now goes to face Hector.
99	May you all turn to water and earth,
100	sitting here each of you, spiritless, without glory, just as you are!
101	Against this man I will arm myself; but above,
102	the bounds of victory are held among the immortal gods.”
103	So he spoke, and put on his fine armor.
104	Then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have appeared
105	at the hands of Hector, since he was far the mightier,
106	had not the kings of the Achaeans sprung up and held you back,
107	and the son of Atreus himself, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
108	taken you by the right hand, and spoken to you, and called you by name:
109	“You are mad, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, and you have no need
110	of this madness; hold back, though you are grieved,
111	and do not wish, out of strife, to fight a man better than you,
112	Hector, son of Priam, whom others too dread.
113	Even Achilles, in the battle where men win glory,
114	shudders to meet this man, and he is far better than you.
115	So now go and sit down among the band of your companions,
116	and against this man the Achaeans will set up another champion.
117	Fearless though he is, and insatiable of the fray,
118	I say he will be glad to bend his knees and rest, if he escapes
119	from destructive war and grim combat.”
120	So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind,
121	urging what was due; and he obeyed; and then
122	his attendants gladly took the armor from his shoulders.
123	And Nestor stood up among the Argives and spoke:
124	“Ah, what great sorrow is coming upon the land of Achaea!
125	Surely the old horse-driving Peleus would groan aloud,
126	the brave counselor and speaker of the Myrmidons,
127	who once, questioning me in his house, rejoiced greatly
128	to learn the lineage and birth of all the Argives.
129	If he heard that they all now cower before Hector,
130	he would lift up his hands again and again to the immortals,
131	that his spirit might go from his limbs down into the house of Hades.
132	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
133	I were young, as when by the swift-flowing Celadon there fought,
134	gathered together, the Pylians and the Arcadians, fighters with the spear,
135	beside the walls of Pheia, about the streams of the Iardanus.
136	Among them Ereuthalion stood forth as champion, a man equal to the gods,
137	wearing on his shoulders the armor of lord Areithous,
138	brilliant Areithous, whom they called by the name of the mace-man,
139	both men and lovely-girdled women,
140	because he did not fight with bows or the long spear,
141	but broke the battalions with an iron mace.
142	Lycurgus killed him by guile, not by strength at all,
143	in a narrow road, where his mace could not keep destruction
144	from him, the iron mace; for Lycurgus, striking first,
145	pierced him through the middle with his spear, and he was flung on his back on the ground;
146	and he stripped him of the armor that bronze Ares had given him.
147	And he himself wore it afterward into the toil of Ares;
148	but when Lycurgus grew old in his halls,
149	he gave it to Ereuthalion, his dear attendant, to wear;
150	and wearing his armor he challenged all the best.
151	But they trembled greatly and were afraid, and no one dared;
152	but my much-enduring heart sent me to fight him
153	in my daring; and by birth I was the youngest of them all.
154	And I fought him, and Athena gave me triumph.
155	He was the tallest and the strongest man I ever killed;
156	for he lay sprawled out, huge, this way and that.
157	If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast!
158	Then Hector of the gleaming helmet would soon find his fight.
159	But of you, who are the best of all the Achaeans,
160	not even you are eager with a ready heart to go and face Hector.”
161	So the old man rebuked them, and nine in all stood up.
162	First by far rose Agamemnon, lord of men,
163	and after him rose the son of Tydeus, mighty Diomedes,
164	and after them the two Ajaxes, clothed in furious courage,
165	and after them Idomeneus and Idomeneus' squire
166	Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men,
167	and after them Eurypylus, the splendid son of Euaemon,
168	and up rose Thoas, son of Andraemon, and brilliant Odysseus;
169	all of these were willing to fight with brilliant Hector.
170	And again the Gerenian horseman Nestor spoke among them:
171	“Now cast lots, all of you in turn, to see who is chosen;
172	for he will do the well-greaved Achaeans good,
173	and he will do his own heart good as well, if he escapes
174	from destructive war and grim combat.”
175	So he spoke, and each man marked his own lot,
176	and they threw them into the helmet of Agamemnon, son of Atreus.
177	And the people prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods,
178	and this is what a man would say, looking up at the wide sky:
179	“Father Zeus, let the lot fall to Ajax, or the son of Tydeus,
180	or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold.”
181	So they spoke, and the Gerenian horseman Nestor shook the lots,
182	and out of the helmet leapt the lot that they themselves wanted,
183	the lot of Ajax; and the herald carried it through the throng everywhere
184	and showed it from left to right to all the best of the Achaeans.
185	But they did not know it, and each man disowned it.
186	But when, carrying it through the throng everywhere, he came to the man
187	who had scratched his mark on it and thrown it into the helmet, shining Ajax,
188	he held out his hand, and the herald stood beside him and dropped it in,
189	and he knew the mark on the lot when he saw it, and rejoiced in his heart.
190	He threw it down on the ground beside his foot and spoke:
191	“Friends, the lot is mine indeed, and I myself rejoice
192	in my heart, since I think I will defeat brilliant Hector.
193	But come, while I put on my armor for war,
194	you meanwhile pray to lord Zeus, son of Cronus,
195	in silence among yourselves, so that the Trojans do not hear,
196	or else openly, since in any case we fear no one;
197	for no one shall drive me back by force, at his will and against mine,
198	nor by skill; for I do not think that I was born
199	and bred in Salamis so unskilled as that.”
200	So he spoke, and they prayed to lord Zeus, son of Cronus;
201	and this is what a man would say, looking up at the wide sky:
202	“Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest,
203	grant victory to Ajax, and that he win a splendid triumph;
204	but if you love Hector too and care for him,
205	grant equal strength and glory to them both.”
206	So they spoke, and Ajax armed himself in gleaming bronze.
207	And when he had put all his armor about his body,
208	then he hurried forward, as huge Ares goes forth
209	when he goes to war among men, whom the son of Cronus
210	has brought together to fight in the fury of heart-devouring strife.
211	Such was huge Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans, as he rose up,
212	smiling with grim face; and beneath, with his feet,
213	he went with long strides, brandishing his long-shadowed spear.
214	And the Argives rejoiced as they looked at him,
215	but a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan,
216	and Hector's own heart pounded in his breast;
217	but he could no longer shrink back or slip away
218	into the throng of the people, since he had made the challenge to battle.
219	And Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower,
220	bronze, of seven oxhides, which Tychius had toiled to make for him,
221	by far the best of leather-workers, who lived in Hyle,
222	who made him the flashing shield of seven oxhides
223	of well-fed bulls, and hammered an eighth layer of bronze upon it.
224	Holding this before his chest, Telamonian Ajax
225	stood very near Hector, and spoke to him threateningly:
226	“Hector, now you will know clearly, man to man,
227	what kind of chiefs are among the Danaans too,
228	even after Achilles, breaker of men, lion-hearted.
229	But he lies among his curved, seafaring ships,
230	nursing his wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people;
231	and we are such as can face you,
232	and many of us. So begin the fight and the battle.”
233	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him in turn:
234	“Telamonian Ajax, sprung from Zeus, lord of the people,
235	do not test me as if I were some feeble boy
236	or a woman, who does not know the works of war.
237	But I know battles well, and the killing of men;
238	I know how to swing to the right, I know how to swing to the left, the dry oxhide,
239	and that, to me, is fighting as a warrior with the oxhide shield;
240	I know how to charge into the fray of swift horses;
241	I know how, in close fighting, to dance for destructive Ares.
242	But I do not want to strike you, such a man as you are,
243	by spying for a chance in secret, but openly, if I can hit you.”
244	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
245	and struck Ajax's dread shield of seven oxhides
246	on the outermost bronze, which was the eighth layer upon it.
247	Through six layers went the unyielding bronze, cutting its way,
248	but in the seventh hide it stopped. Then second in his turn
249	Ajax, sprung from Zeus, let fly his long-shadowed spear,
250	and struck the son of Priam on his shield, balanced on every side.
251	And through the shining shield went the heavy spear,
252	and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet,
253	and straight on beside the flank it cut through the tunic,
254	the spear; but he swerved aside and avoided black doom.
255	Then the two, drawing out the long spears with their hands at the same moment,
256	fell upon each other like lions that eat raw flesh
257	or wild boars, whose strength is no weakling's.
258	The son of Priam then stabbed the middle of the shield with his spear,
259	but the bronze did not break through, and its point was bent back;
260	and Ajax leapt on him and stabbed his shield; and right through
261	went the spear, and it drove him back as he pressed on,
262	and it grazed his neck with a cut, and the black blood welled up.
263	But not even so did Hector of the gleaming helmet cease from battle,
264	but giving ground he took up in his strong hand a stone
265	that lay on the plain, black and jagged and great;
266	with this he struck Ajax's dread shield of seven oxhides
267	in the middle, on the boss; and the bronze rang all around.
268	Then second in his turn Ajax lifted a far greater stone
269	and hurled it, whirling it round, and leaned his measureless strength into it,
270	and broke the shield inward, hitting it with a stone like a millstone,
271	and made his knees give way; and he was stretched out on his back,
272	pressed against his shield; but Apollo at once set him on his feet.
273	And now they would have been stabbing each other hand to hand with swords,
274	had not the heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men,
275	come, one from the Trojans and one from the bronze-armored Achaeans,
276	Talthybius and Idaeus, both of them wise.
277	Between the two of them they held out their scepters, and a word was spoken
278	by the herald Idaeus, who knew wise counsels:
279	“Fight no longer, dear sons, and do not do battle;
280	for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both,
281	and both of you are spearmen; this indeed we all know.
282	Night is already coming on; it is good to yield to night too.”
283	Answering him, Telamonian Ajax said:
284	“Idaeus, bid Hector say these things;
285	for it was he who challenged all the best to battle.
286	Let him begin; and I will obey, just as he does.”
287	Then great Hector of the gleaming helmet spoke to him in turn:
288	“Ajax, since a god has given you stature and strength
289	and good sense, and with the spear you are the mightiest of the Achaeans,
290	let us now cease from battle and combat
291	for today; afterward we will fight again, until a god
292	decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other.
293	Night is already coming on; it is good to yield to night too,
294	so that you may gladden all the Achaeans beside the ships,
295	and most of all your kinsmen and companions, who are yours;
296	and I, throughout the great city of lord Priam,
297	will gladden the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes,
298	who will enter the divine assembly, praying for me.
299	But come, let us both give each other glorious gifts,
300	so that this is what a man will say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike:
301	‘They fought in heart-devouring strife,
302	and then parted in friendship, reconciled.’”
303	So he spoke, and gave him a silver-studded sword,
304	bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut strap;
305	and Ajax gave a belt bright with purple.
306	And they parted, and one went to the army of the Achaeans,
307	and the other to the throng of the Trojans; and they rejoiced
308	to see him coming to join them alive and unhurt,
309	escaping the might and the irresistible hands of Ajax;
310	and they brought him to the city, though they had not hoped to see him safe.
311	And on the other side the well-greaved Achaeans led Ajax,
312	exulting in his victory, to brilliant Agamemnon.
313	And when they had come to the huts of the son of Atreus,
314	Agamemnon, lord of men, sacrificed an ox for them,
315	a male, five years old, to the son of Cronus, supreme in might.
316	They flayed it and dressed it, and jointed it all,
317	and cut it up skillfully and threaded it on spits,
318	and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off.
319	And when they had ceased from the work and made the feast ready,
320	they feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast.
321	And Ajax was honored with the long chine
322	by the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon.
323	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
324	the old man first of all began to weave a plan for them,
325	Nestor, whose counsel had shown itself best before;
326	with good intent he addressed them and spoke:
327	“Son of Atreus, and you others, the best of all the Achaeans,
328	many long-haired Achaeans are dead,
329	whose dark blood about the fair-flowing Scamander
330	sharp Ares has now scattered, and their souls have gone down to Hades;
331	therefore you must stop the fighting of the Achaeans at dawn,
332	and we ourselves will gather and wheel the dead here
333	with oxen and mules; and then we will burn them
334	a little way off, in front of the ships, so that each man may carry their bones
335	home to their children, whenever we return again to our native land.
336	And around the pyre let us heap up a single tomb, bringing earth
337	from the plain, one for all alike; and close against it let us quickly build
338	high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for ourselves.
339	And in them let us make gates that fit well,
340	so that through them there may be a way for driving horses;
341	and outside let us dig a deep ditch close by,
342	which, running all around, may hold back horses and men,
343	lest the war of the proud Trojans ever press hard upon us.”
344	So he spoke, and all the kings approved.
345	And the Trojans held an assembly on the height of the city of Ilios,
346	a dread and turbulent one, beside Priam's doors;
347	and among them wise Antenor began to speak:
348	“Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,
349	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
350	Come now, let us give Argive Helen, and the possessions with her,
351	to the sons of Atreus to take away; for now, having played false with the trusty oaths,
352	we are fighting; and so I do not expect that anything better
353	will come to pass for us, unless we do this.”
354	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
355	brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair,
356	who answered him and addressed him with winged words:
357	“Antenor, what you say is no longer pleasing to me;
358	you know how to think of another word better than this.
359	But if you really say this in earnest,
360	then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits.
361	But I will speak out among the horse-taming Trojans:
362	I refuse outright; I will not give back the woman;
363	but the possessions that I brought from Argos to our house
364	I am willing to give, all of them, and to add more from my own.”
365	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
366	Priam, descendant of Dardanus, a counselor equal to the gods;
367	with good intent he addressed them and spoke:
368	“Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,
369	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
370	Now take your supper throughout the city, as before,
371	and remember to keep watch, and let each man stay awake;
372	and at dawn let Idaeus go to the hollow ships
373	to tell the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
374	the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose;
375	and let him also say this shrewd word, if they are willing
376	to cease from grim-sounding war until we have burned
377	the dead; afterward we will fight again, until a god
378	decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other.”
379	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed;
380	then they took their supper throughout the army, by companies;
381	and at dawn Idaeus went to the hollow ships.
382	He found the Danaans, attendants of Ares, in assembly
383	beside the stern of Agamemnon's ship; and standing
384	in their midst the loud-voiced herald spoke among them:
385	“Son of Atreus, and you others, the best of all the Achaeans,
386	Priam and the other noble Trojans bade me
387	tell you, if perhaps this should be welcome and pleasing to you,
388	the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose.
389	All the possessions that Alexander in his hollow ships
390	brought to Troy (I wish he had perished before that!),
391	he is willing to give, all of them, and to add more from his own;
392	but the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus
393	he says he will not give; though the Trojans, truly, urge him.
394	And they also bade me say this word, if you are willing
395	to cease from grim-sounding war until we have burned
396	the dead; afterward we will fight again, until a god
397	decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other.”
398	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence;
399	at last Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them:
400	“Let no one now accept the possessions of Alexander,
401	nor Helen; it is plain even to one who is an utter fool
402	that the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon the Trojans.”
403	So he spoke, and all the sons of the Achaeans shouted aloud,
404	admiring the word of Diomedes, tamer of horses.
405	And then powerful Agamemnon spoke to Idaeus:
406	“Idaeus, you yourself hear the word of the Achaeans,
407	how they answer you; and my pleasure is the same.
408	But as for burning the dead, I do not begrudge it;
409	for one should not grudge the corpses of the dead,
410	once they have died, the quick comfort of fire.
411	As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera.”
412	So saying, he lifted up his scepter to all the gods,
413	and Idaeus went back to sacred Ilios.
414	And the Trojans and the Dardanians sat in assembly,
415	all gathered together, waiting for the moment when
416	Idaeus would come; and he came and delivered his message,
417	standing in their midst; and they made ready very quickly
418	for both tasks, some to bring the dead, and others to go for wood;
419	and the Argives on the other side, from their well-benched ships,
420	hurried, some to bring the dead, and others to go for wood.
421	The sun was just striking the fields,
422	rising out of gently flowing, deep-streaming Oceanus
423	up into the sky; and they met one another.
424	There it was hard to tell each man apart;
425	but they washed the clotted blood from them with water,
426	and shedding hot tears lifted them onto the wagons.
427	But great Priam did not allow them to wail; and in silence
428	they heaped the dead on the pyre, grieving at heart,
429	and when they had burned them in the fire they went back to sacred Ilios.
430	And in the same way on the other side the well-greaved Achaeans
431	heaped the dead on the pyre, grieving at heart,
432	and when they had burned them in the fire they went back to the hollow ships.
433	When it was not yet dawn, but still the half-light of night,
434	then around the pyre a chosen band of the Achaeans was astir,
435	and around it they made a single tomb, bringing earth
436	from the plain, one for all alike; and close against it they built a wall
437	and high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for themselves;
438	and in them they made gates that fit well,
439	so that through them there might be a way for driving horses;
440	and outside, alongside it, they dug a deep ditch,
441	wide and great, and in it they planted stakes.
442	So the long-haired Achaeans toiled;
443	and the gods, seated beside Zeus, lord of the lightning,
444	gazed at the great work of the bronze-armored Achaeans.
445	And among them Poseidon the earth-shaker began to speak:
446	“Father Zeus, is there any mortal on the boundless earth
447	who will still tell his mind and his plans to the immortals?
448	Do you not see how the long-haired Achaeans now
449	have built a wall to protect their ships, and driven a ditch around it,
450	and have not given glorious hecatombs to the gods?
451	The fame of it will reach as far as the dawn spreads;
452	and men will forget the wall that I and Phoebus Apollo
453	toiled to build for the hero Laomedon.”
454	Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said to him:
455	“Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!
456	Some other god might fear this device,
457	one far feebler than you in hands and might;
458	but your fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads.
459	Come then, when the long-haired Achaeans again
460	are gone with their ships to their dear native land,
461	break down the wall and sweep it all into the sea,
462	and cover the great beach over again with sand,
463	so that the great wall of the Achaeans may be brought to nothing.”
464	So they spoke such words to one another,
465	and the sun set, and the work of the Achaeans was finished;
466	and they slaughtered oxen among the huts and took their supper.
467	And ships from Lemnos stood by, bringing wine,
468	many of them, which Euneus, son of Jason, had sent,
469	whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people.
470	And to the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus, apart,
471	the son of Jason had given a thousand measures of wine to take away.
472	From these the long-haired Achaeans bought their wine,
473	some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron,
474	some with hides, and some with the cattle themselves,
475	and some with slaves; and they set out a rich feast.
476	All night long then the long-haired Achaeans
477	feasted, and the Trojans and their allies throughout the city;
478	and all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil for them,
479	thundering terribly; and pale fear took hold of them;
480	and they poured the wine from their cups on the ground, and no one dared
481	drink before he had poured a libation to the son of Cronus, supreme in might.
482	Then they lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep.
