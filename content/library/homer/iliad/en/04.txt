# Homer, Iliad, Book 4 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now the gods, seated beside Zeus, were holding assembly
2	on the golden floor, and among them lady Hebe
3	poured out the nectar; and they with golden cups
4	pledged one another, looking out upon the city of the Trojans.
5	At once the son of Cronus set about provoking Hera
6	with cutting words, speaking with sly intent:
7	“Two of the goddesses are helpers of Menelaus,
8	Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae.
9	But they sit apart and look on
10	and take their pleasure; while laughter-loving Aphrodite
11	is always at his side and wards off doom from him,
12	and now she has saved him, when he thought that he would die.
13	But the victory belongs to Menelaus, dear to Ares.
14	So let us consider how these things are to be:
15	whether we shall rouse evil war again and the dread din of battle,
16	or put friendship between the two sides.
17	And if this should somehow be welcome and pleasing to all,
18	then the city of lord Priam would still be inhabited,
19	and Menelaus would take Argive Helen back home.”
20	So he spoke, and Athena and Hera muttered against it;
21	they sat side by side, and were devising evils for the Trojans.
22	Athena kept silent and said nothing,
23	sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her;
24	but Hera's breast could not contain her anger, and she spoke to him:
25	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
26	How can you wish to make my labor vain and unfulfilled,
27	and the sweat I sweated in my toil? My horses were worn out
28	as I gathered the army, to bring evils on Priam and his sons.
29	Do it; but not all of us other gods approve.”
30	Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said to her:
31	“Strange one, what great evils have Priam and the sons of Priam
32	done to you, that you rage so furiously
33	to sack the well-built citadel of Ilios?
34	If you could go in through the gates and the long walls
35	and eat Priam raw, and the sons of Priam,
36	and the other Trojans, then you might heal your anger.
37	Do as you wish; let this quarrel not become hereafter
38	a great cause of strife between you and me.
39	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
40	whenever I too am eager to sack a city,
41	one I choose, where men dear to you were born,
42	do not try to hinder my anger, but let me be;
43	for I have granted you this willingly, though with an unwilling heart.
44	For of all the cities under the sun and the starry sky
45	where men on earth have their dwelling,
46	sacred Ilios was the most honored in my heart,
47	and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.
48	For my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast,
49	libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us.”
50	Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him:
51	“Truly, three cities are by far the dearest to me,
52	Argos and Sparta and wide-streeted Mycenae;
53	sack them whenever they become hateful to your heart.
54	I do not stand up for them against you, nor begrudge you them.
55	For even if I grudge them and will not let you sack them,
56	I accomplish nothing by grudging, since you are far the stronger.
57	But my labor too must not be left unfulfilled;
58	for I too am a god, and my birth is from the same stock as yours,
59	and crooked-counseling Cronus fathered me, the most senior,
60	on both counts, by birth and because I am called
61	your wife, and you are lord among all the immortals.
62	But let us give way to each other in this,
63	I to you and you to me; and the other gods will follow,
64	the immortals. And you, bid Athena quickly
65	go into the dread din of battle between the Trojans and the Achaeans,
66	and contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans,
67	be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths.”
68	So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey;
69	at once he addressed Athena with winged words:
70	“Go quickly to the army, among the Trojans and the Achaeans,
71	and contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans,
72	be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths.”
73	So saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already,
74	and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus.
75	As the son of crooked-counseling Cronus sends a star
76	as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men,
77	a bright one, and many sparks fly from it,
78	in its likeness Pallas Athena darted down to the earth,
79	and leapt into their midst; and amazement held those who watched,
80	the horse-taming Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans;
81	and this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
82	“Surely evil war and the dread din of battle
83	will come again, or else friendship is set between the two sides
84	by Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men.”
85	So a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike.
86	And she slipped into the throng of the Trojans in the likeness of a man,
87	Laodocus, son of Antenor, a strong spearman,
88	looking for godlike Pandarus, if she might find him anywhere.
89	She found the son of Lycaon, noble and strong,
90	standing, and around him were the strong ranks of shield-bearing
91	men, who had followed him from the streams of the Aesepus.
92	And standing near him she addressed him with winged words:
93	“Will you listen to me now, wise-hearted son of Lycaon?
94	Then you would dare to let fly a swift arrow at Menelaus,
95	and you would win thanks and glory from all the Trojans,
96	and most of all from king Alexander.
97	From him before all others you would carry off splendid gifts,
98	if he should see warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus,
99	brought down by your arrow and laid on the grievous pyre.
100	Come, shoot your arrow at glorious Menelaus,
101	and pray to Apollo, the Lycian-born, famed for his bow,
102	that you will offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs
103	when you come home to the city of sacred Zeleia.”
104	So spoke Athena, and she persuaded the mind of the fool.
105	At once he uncovered his polished bow, made from a leaping wild goat
106	which he himself had once hit under the breast
107	as it came out from a rock, lying in wait for it in ambush;
108	he struck it in the chest, and it fell backward on the rock.
109	Its horns grew from its head sixteen palms long;
110	and a craftsman who worked in horn dressed them and fitted them together,
111	and smoothed it all well, and set on it a golden tip.
112	He strung the bow against the ground and laid it carefully down,
113	leaning it there; and his brave companions held their shields in front of him,
114	so that the warlike sons of the Achaeans would not spring up
115	before warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus, was struck.
116	Then he removed the lid of his quiver, and took out an arrow,
117	feathered, never shot before, a source of black pains;
118	and quickly he fitted the bitter arrow to the string,
119	and prayed to Apollo, the Lycian-born, famed for his bow,
120	that he would offer a glorious hecatomb of firstborn lambs
121	when he came home to the city of sacred Zeleia.
122	He drew, gripping the notches and the ox-sinew string together;
123	the string he brought to his breast, and the iron to the bow.
124	And when he had drawn the great bow into a circle,
125	the bow twanged, the string sang loud, and the arrow leapt,
126	sharp-pointed, eager to fly into the throng.
127	But you, Menelaus, the blessed gods did not forget,
128	the immortals, and first of all the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil,
129	who stood in front of you and warded off the piercing shaft.
130	She brushed it away from his flesh, as a mother
131	brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep,
132	and she herself guided it to where the golden clasps of the belt
133	held it together, and the corselet met it in a double layer.
134	The bitter arrow fell upon the close-fitted belt;
135	through the elaborate belt it was driven,
136	and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet,
137	and the band he wore to guard his flesh, a barrier against javelins,
138	which protected him most; but it went on through that too.
139	The arrow grazed the outermost flesh of the man,
140	and at once the dark blood flowed from the wound.
141	As when a woman stains ivory with purple,
142	a Maeonian or a Carian, to be a cheekpiece for horses;
143	it lies in a chamber, and many have prayed for it,
144	horsemen who long to carry it, but it lies there as a delight for a king,
145	both an ornament for the horse and a glory to the driver:
146	so, Menelaus, your thighs were stained with blood,
147	your shapely thighs, and your shins, and your fine ankles beneath.
148	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, shuddered
149	when he saw the black blood flowing down from the wound;
150	and Menelaus himself, dear to Ares, shuddered too.
151	But when he saw that the binding and the barbs were outside,
152	his spirit was gathered back into his breast.
153	Groaning heavily, powerful Agamemnon spoke among them,
154	holding Menelaus by the hand, and his companions groaned with him:
155	“Dear brother, it was your death, then, that I swore in the oaths,
156	setting you out alone before the Achaeans to fight the Trojans,
157	since the Trojans have shot you and trampled on the trusty oaths.
158	Yet the oath is in no way vain, and the blood of lambs,
159	and the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted.
160	For even if the Olympian has not fulfilled it at once,
161	he fulfills it in the end, even late, and they pay a great price,
162	with their own heads and their wives and their children.
163	For I know this well in my mind and in my heart:
164	a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish,
165	and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear,
166	and Zeus, son of Cronus, throned on high, who dwells in the upper air,
167	will himself shake his dark aegis over them all,
168	in resentment at this deceit. These things will not go unfulfilled.
169	But I will have dread grief for you, Menelaus,
170	if you die and fill up the measure of your life.
171	And I would come in utter disgrace to thirsty Argos;
172	for at once the Achaeans will remember their native land,
173	and we would leave to Priam and the Trojans a thing to boast of,
174	Argive Helen; and the earth will rot your bones
175	as you lie in Troy, with your work unfinished.
176	And one of the overweening Trojans will say,
177	leaping on the tomb of glorious Menelaus:
178	‘May Agamemnon fulfill his anger like this against all,
179	as now he has led an army of the Achaeans here in vain,
180	and has gone home to his dear native land
181	with empty ships, leaving brave Menelaus behind.’
182	So someone will say one day; then may the wide earth gape for me.”
183	Encouraging him, fair-haired Menelaus said:
184	“Take heart, and do not frighten the army of the Achaeans at all.
185	The sharp shaft did not stick in a deadly place; in front
186	the gleaming belt stopped it, and underneath
187	the kilt and the band that the bronze-smiths made.”
188	Answering him, powerful Agamemnon said:
189	“May it be so, dear Menelaus!
190	But a healer will examine the wound and lay on it
191	drugs that will stop the black pains.”
192	He spoke, and addressed Talthybius, the divine herald:
193	“Talthybius, call Machaon here as fast as you can,
194	the man who is the son of Asclepius, the noble healer,
195	so that he may see warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus,
196	whom someone well skilled with the bow has hit with an arrow,
197	one of the Trojans or Lycians: fame for him, and grief for us.”
198	So he spoke, and the herald heard him and did not disobey,
199	and went his way through the army of the bronze-armored Achaeans,
200	looking for the hero Machaon; and he saw him
201	standing, and around him were the strong ranks of shield-bearing
202	men, who had followed him from horse-pasturing Tricca.
203	And standing near him he addressed him with winged words:
204	“Rise, son of Asclepius; powerful Agamemnon calls you,
205	so that you may see warlike Menelaus, leader of the Achaeans,
206	whom someone well skilled with the bow has hit with an arrow,
207	one of the Trojans or Lycians: fame for him, and grief for us.”
208	So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast;
209	and they went on their way through the throng, through the wide army of the Achaeans.
210	But when they came to where fair-haired Menelaus
211	had been hit, and around him were gathered all the best men
212	in a circle, and he came and stood among them, the man equal to the gods,
213	at once he drew the arrow out of the close-fitted belt;
214	and as it was drawn out, the sharp barbs were broken back.
215	He loosened the gleaming belt and, underneath,
216	the kilt and the band that the bronze-smiths made.
217	And when he saw the wound where the bitter arrow had struck,
218	he sucked out the blood and skillfully spread on it soothing drugs,
219	which Chiron once gave to his father in friendship.
220	While they were busy about Menelaus, good at the war cry,
221	the ranks of the shield-bearing Trojans came on;
222	and the Achaeans put on their armor again, and remembered the joy of battle.
223	Then you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing,
224	nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight,
225	but very eager for the battle where men win glory.
226	For he left his horses and his chariot, inlaid with bronze;
227	and his attendant kept the snorting horses apart,
228	Eurymedon, son of Ptolemaeus, son of Peiraeus;
229	and he told him again and again to keep them close at hand, for when
230	weariness should take his limbs, as he went commanding so many;
231	but he himself, on foot, ranged through the ranks of men.
232	And those of the Danaans of the swift horses whom he saw eager,
233	these he would encourage, standing beside them, with words:
234	“Argives, do not yet let go of your furious courage;
235	for father Zeus will be no helper to liars,
236	but those who first did harm in defiance of the oaths,
237	vultures will eat their tender flesh,
238	and we will lead away their dear wives and little children
239	in our ships, when we take the citadel.”
240	And those whom he saw holding back from hateful war,
241	these he would rebuke with angry words:
242	“Argives, you arrow-fighters, you disgraces, have you no shame?
243	Why do you stand like this, dazed, like fawns
244	that, when they are worn out from running over a wide plain,
245	stand still, and there is no courage in their hearts:
246	so you stand dazed and do not fight.
247	Are you waiting for the Trojans to come close to where the ships
248	are drawn up with their fine sterns on the shore of the gray sea,
249	to see if the son of Cronus will hold his hand over you?”
250	So, like a master, he ranged through the ranks of men;
251	and he came to the Cretans, going through the crowd of men.
252	They were arming around wise-hearted Idomeneus;
253	Idomeneus among the champions, like a boar in courage,
254	while Meriones was urging on the rearmost battalions for him.
255	Seeing them, Agamemnon, lord of men, rejoiced,
256	and at once addressed Idomeneus with soothing words:
257	“Idomeneus, I honor you above the Danaans of the swift horses,
258	both in war and in any other work,
259	and at the feast, when the sparkling wine of the elders
260	is mixed in the mixing bowl by the best of the Argives.
261	For even if the other long-haired Achaeans
262	drink a measured portion, your cup always
263	stands full, as mine does, to drink whenever your heart bids you.
264	But rouse yourself for war, and be the man you have always boasted you are.”
265	Then Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him face to face:
266	“Son of Atreus, I will indeed be your trusty companion,
267	as I promised at first and nodded my assent;
268	but urge on the other long-haired Achaeans,
269	so that we may fight as soon as we can, since the oaths have been confounded
270	by the Trojans; for them there will be death and sorrows hereafter,
271	since they were the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths.”
272	So he spoke, and the son of Atreus passed on, glad at heart;
273	and he came to the two Ajaxes, going through the crowd of men.
274	They were arming themselves, and with them followed a cloud of foot soldiers.
275	As when a goatherd from a lookout sees a cloud
276	coming over the sea before the blast of the West Wind;
277	and to him, being far away, blacker than pitch
278	it seems as it goes over the sea, and it brings a great storm,
279	and he shudders at the sight and drives his flocks under a cave:
280	so with the two Ajaxes the battalions of young men nurtured by Zeus
281	moved thick into destructive war,
282	dark, bristling with shields and spears.
283	And seeing them, powerful Agamemnon rejoiced,
284	and he spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
285	“Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives,
286	to you two I give no orders, since it is not fitting to urge you on;
287	for you yourselves bid the people fight with all their might.
288	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
289	there were such a spirit in the breasts of all!
290	Then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head,
291	taken and sacked beneath our hands.”
292	So saying he left them there and went on to others.
293	There he found Nestor, the clear-voiced orator of the Pylians,
294	marshaling his companions and urging them to fight
295	around great Pelagon and Alastor and Chromius
296	and powerful Haemon and Bias, shepherd of the people.
297	The horsemen he set in front with their horses and chariots,
298	and the foot soldiers behind, many and brave,
299	to be a bulwark against war; and he drove the cowards into the middle,
300	so that even an unwilling man would have to fight.
301	He gave his orders first to the horsemen; he told them
302	to keep their horses in hand and not be thrown into confusion in the throng:
303	“Let no one, trusting in his horsemanship and his manhood,
304	be eager to fight the Trojans alone, ahead of the others,
305	nor let him fall back; for you will be the weaker.
306	But whoever from his own chariot reaches an enemy's chariot,
307	let him thrust with his spear, since that is far better.
308	This is how the men of old sacked cities and walls,
309	keeping this purpose and this spirit in their breasts.”
310	So the old man urged them on, long skilled in wars.
311	And seeing him, powerful Agamemnon rejoiced,
312	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
313	“Old man, I wish that, as the spirit is in your breast,
314	so your knees might follow, and your strength be steadfast.
315	But old age, the common lot, wears you down; I wish that some
316	other man had it, and you were among the younger.”
317	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
318	“Son of Atreus, I too would very much wish myself
319	to be as I was when I killed brilliant Ereuthalion.
320	But the gods never give men all things at once.
321	If I was a young man then, now old age is upon me.
322	But even so I will be among the horsemen and give them orders
323	with counsel and with words; for that is the prize of the old.
324	The spears will be wielded by the younger men, who are
325	born later than I, and trust in their strength.”
326	So he spoke, and the son of Atreus passed on, glad at heart.
327	He found the son of Peteos, Menestheus, driver of horses,
328	standing, and around him the Athenians, masters of the war cry;
329	and near him stood Odysseus of many wiles,
330	and beside him, all around, the ranks of the Cephallenians, no weaklings,
331	were standing; for their people had not yet heard the war cry,
332	but the battalions were only now stirring and moving,
333	of the horse-taming Trojans and the Achaeans; and these stood waiting
334	until another column of Achaeans should come on
335	and charge the Trojans, and begin the fighting.
336	Seeing them, Agamemnon, lord of men, rebuked them,
337	and he spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
338	“Son of Peteos, the king whom Zeus has nurtured,
339	and you, skilled in evil tricks, with your mind set on gain,
340	why do you stand apart, cowering, and wait for others?
341	It would be fitting for you two to stand among the foremost
342	and meet the blazing battle;
343	for you two are the first to hear my call to the feast
344	whenever we Achaeans prepare a feast for the elders.
345	There you are glad to eat roast meat and to drink cups
346	of honey-sweet wine for as long as you like;
347	but now you would gladly watch, even if ten columns of Achaeans
348	were fighting in front of you with the pitiless bronze.”
349	Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said:
350	“Son of Atreus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
351	How can you say that we hold back from war, whenever we Achaeans
352	rouse sharp Ares against the horse-taming Trojans?
353	You will see, if you wish and if you care about it,
354	the dear father of Telemachus mingling with the champions
355	of the horse-taming Trojans; what you say is empty wind.”
356	Smiling at him, powerful Agamemnon said,
357	when he saw that he was angry, and he took back his word:
358	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
359	I do not rebuke you beyond measure, nor give you orders;
360	for I know how the heart in your breast
361	knows kindly thoughts; for you think as I do.
362	But come, we will make these things good later, if anything bad
363	has been said now; and may the gods make it all blow away.”
364	So saying he left them there and went on to others.
365	He found the son of Tydeus, high-hearted Diomedes,
366	standing among his horses and his well-joined chariot;
367	and beside him stood Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus.
368	And seeing him, powerful Agamemnon rebuked him,
369	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
370	“Ah, son of wise-hearted Tydeus, tamer of horses,
371	why do you cower, why do you stare at the bridges of war?
372	Tydeus was not one to cower like this,
373	but to fight the enemy far ahead of his dear companions,
374	so said those who saw him at his work; for I myself
375	never met him or saw him; but they say he was above all others.
376	Once he came to Mycenae, not in war,
377	a guest, with godlike Polynices, gathering an army;
378	for they were then making war against the sacred walls of Thebes,
379	and they begged hard to be given famous allies;
380	and the men were willing to give them, and approved what they asked,
381	but Zeus turned them back, showing ill-omened signs.
382	So when they had gone and were well on their way,
383	and had come to the Asopus, deep in rushes, with grassy meadows,
384	there the Achaeans sent Tydeus ahead on an embassy.
385	He went, and found the many Cadmeians
386	feasting in the house of mighty Eteocles.
387	There, though he was a stranger, horse-driving Tydeus
388	was not afraid, alone among the many Cadmeians,
389	but challenged them to contests, and won them all
390	easily; such a helper was Athena to him.
391	But the Cadmeians, goaders of horses, were angry,
392	and as he went back they set a close ambush for him, leading
393	fifty young men; and there were two leaders,
394	Maeon son of Haemon, like the immortals,
395	and the son of Autophonus, Polyphontes, steadfast in battle.
396	Tydeus brought a shameful doom on these too:
397	he killed them all, and let only one go home:
398	he sent away Maeon, obeying the portents of the gods.
399	Such was Tydeus the Aetolian; but the son
400	he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly.”
401	So he spoke, and mighty Diomedes said nothing to him,
402	in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered.
403	But the son of glorious Capaneus answered him:
404	“Son of Atreus, do not lie, when you know how to speak plainly.
405	We boast that we are far better than our fathers;
406	we even took the seat of seven-gated Thebes,
407	leading a smaller army under a stronger wall,
408	trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus;
409	but they perished through their own recklessness.
410	So never put our fathers in equal honor with us.”
411	Looking at him darkly, mighty Diomedes said:
412	“Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word.
413	I do not blame Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
414	for urging the well-greaved Achaeans to fight;
415	for glory will go with him if the Achaeans
416	cut down the Trojans and take sacred Ilios,
417	and great grief will be his if the Achaeans are cut down.
418	But come, let us two also think of our furious courage.”
419	He spoke, and leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor;
420	and the bronze on the breast of the lord rang terribly
421	as he moved; fear would have seized even a stout-hearted man.
422	As when on the loud-echoing shore the swell of the sea
423	rises wave upon wave, stirred by the West Wind;
424	it rears its head first on the open sea, but then
425	it breaks upon the land and roars loud, and around the headlands
426	it curls and crests, and spits out the foam of the sea:
427	so then the battalions of the Danaans moved, rank upon rank,
428	unceasingly to war; and to his own men each one
429	of the leaders gave orders; and the rest went in silence, and you would not have said
430	that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts,
431	silent, in fear of their commanders; and on all of them
432	gleamed the inlaid armor that they wore as they marched in their ranks.
433	But the Trojans, like sheep in the fold of a man of many possessions,
434	that stand in their thousands while they are milked of their white milk,
435	bleating ceaselessly as they hear the voices of their lambs,
436	so the shouting of the Trojans rose through the wide army;
437	for not all of them had the same speech or one language,
438	but their tongues were mingled, and the men were called from many lands.
439	Ares roused these, and gray-eyed Athena those,
440	and Terror and Rout, and Strife, raging without end,
441	the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares,
442	who is small when first she rears her head, but afterwards
443	she plants her head against the sky and walks upon the earth;
444	she then too cast the quarrel, common to all, into their midst,
445	as she went through the throng, increasing the groaning of men.
446	And when they had come together into one place,
447	they dashed together their shields of hide, and their spears, and the fury of men
448	in bronze corselets; and the bossed shields
449	pressed against one another, and a great din arose.
450	Then there were cries of pain and cries of triumph together from men
451	killing and being killed, and the earth ran with blood.
452	As when winter torrents, flowing down the mountains,
453	throw their heavy water together where two valleys meet,
454	from great springs, inside a hollow ravine,
455	and far off in the mountains a shepherd hears their roar:
456	so from their mingling came the shouting and the toil.
457	Antilochus was the first to kill a helmeted man of the Trojans,
458	brave among the champions, Echepolus, son of Thalysias;
459	he was first to strike him on the ridge of his horsehair-crested helmet,
460	and fixed it in his forehead, and it pierced into the bone,
461	the bronze point; and darkness covered his eyes,
462	and he fell like a tower in the fierce battle.
463	When he had fallen, powerful Elephenor took him by the feet,
464	son of Chalcodon, leader of the great-hearted Abantes,
465	and dragged him out from under the missiles, in eager haste
466	to strip off his armor; but his effort lasted only a little while.
467	For great-hearted Agenor saw him dragging the corpse,
468	and in his side, which was exposed beside his shield as he stooped,
469	he stabbed him with his bronze-fitted spear, and loosened his limbs.
470	So his spirit left him, and over him there was work,
471	grievous work, of Trojans and Achaeans; and they like wolves
472	leapt upon one another, and man hurled man about.
473	Then Telamonian Ajax struck the son of Anthemion,
474	the young man in his prime, Simoeisius, whom once his mother
475	bore beside the banks of the Simoeis, coming down from Ida,
476	when she had gone with her parents to watch over the flocks;
477	therefore they called him Simoeisius; but he did not repay
478	his dear parents for his rearing, and short was his life,
479	brought down by the spear of great-hearted Ajax.
480	For as he came on in front, he struck him in the chest beside the nipple,
481	the right one; and straight through the shoulder the bronze spear
482	went; and he fell to the ground in the dust like a poplar
483	that has grown in the low wet ground of a great marsh,
484	smooth, but with branches growing at the very top;
485	a chariot-maker has cut it down with the gleaming iron,
486	to bend a wheel rim for a beautiful chariot,
487	and it lies drying by the banks of the river:
488	so was Simoeisius, son of Anthemion, stripped of his armor
489	by Ajax, sprung from Zeus. And at him Antiphus of the flashing corselet,
490	son of Priam, threw his sharp spear through the throng.
491	He missed him, but hit Leucus, the brave companion of Odysseus,
492	in the groin, as he was dragging the corpse the other way;
493	he fell over it, and the body dropped from his hand.
494	Odysseus was very angry at heart over his killing,
495	and went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze,
496	and came up close and stood and threw his shining spear,
497	glancing around him; and the Trojans shrank back
498	from the man as he threw; and he did not throw the shaft in vain,
499	but struck a bastard son of Priam, Democoon,
500	who had come to him from Abydos, from his swift mares.
501	Odysseus, in his anger for his companion, struck him with his spear
502	on the temple, and it passed through the other temple,
503	the bronze point; and darkness covered his eyes,
504	and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
505	The champions and shining Hector gave ground;
506	and the Argives shouted aloud, and dragged away the dead,
507	and drove on much farther forward. But Apollo was indignant,
508	looking down from Pergamus, and shouted a command to the Trojans:
509	“Rise up, horse-taming Trojans, and do not yield the joy of battle
510	to the Argives, since their flesh is not stone or iron
511	to withstand the flesh-cutting bronze when they are struck;
512	and Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, is not
513	fighting, but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger.”
514	So spoke the dread god from the city; but the Achaeans
515	were roused by the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia,
516	as she went through the throng, wherever she saw them holding back.
517	Then fate fettered Diores, son of Amarynceus;
518	for he was hit by a jagged stone beside the ankle,
519	on the right shin; and the leader of the Thracian men threw it,
520	Peirous son of Imbrasus, who had come from Aenus.
521	The shameless stone crushed both the tendons and the bones
522	utterly; and he fell on his back in the dust,
523	stretching out both his hands to his dear companions,
524	breathing out his life; and the man who had hit him ran up,
525	Peirous, and stabbed him with his spear beside the navel; and all
526	his bowels poured out on the ground, and darkness covered his eyes.
527	As Peirous sprang back, Thoas the Aetolian struck him with his spear
528	in the chest above the nipple, and the bronze stuck in his lung;
529	and Thoas came up close to him, and pulled the heavy spear
530	out of his chest, and drew his sharp sword,
531	and struck him full in the belly, and took away his life.
532	But he did not strip his armor; for his companions stood around him,
533	the Thracians with the topknots, holding long spears in their hands,
534	who, big and mighty and noble as he was,
535	pushed him away from them; and he gave ground, reeling.
536	So the two lay stretched in the dust beside each other,
537	one of the Thracians, the other of the bronze-armored Epeians,
538	both of them leaders; and many others were killed around them.
539	Then no man who came into the work could make light of it any more,
540	any man who, still unhit and unwounded by the sharp bronze,
541	might whirl about in the middle, and Pallas Athena should lead him
542	by the hand, and keep off the force of the missiles;
543	for many of the Trojans and the Achaeans on that day
544	lay stretched face down in the dust beside one another.
