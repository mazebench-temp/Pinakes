# Homer, Iliad, Book 24 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	The gathering broke up, and the people scattered, each of them,
2	to go to the swift ships. And the others thought of supper
3	and of taking their fill of sweet sleep; but Achilles
4	wept, remembering his dear companion, and sleep
5	that subdues all did not take him, but he tossed this way and that,
6	longing for Patroclus, for his manhood and his noble might,
7	and all that he had wound out with him, and the sorrows he had suffered,
8	passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves;
9	remembering these, he let big tears fall,
10	now lying on his side, and now again
11	on his back, and now face down; and then he would stand up
12	and wander distraught along the shore of the sea; nor would the dawn
13	escape him as it appeared over the sea and the shores.
14	But whenever he had yoked his swift horses under the chariot,
15	he would tie Hector behind the chariot to be dragged,
16	and when he had dragged him three times around the mound of the dead son of Menoetius,
17	he would rest again in his hut, and leave him
18	stretched out face down in the dust; but Apollo
19	kept all defilement from his flesh, pitying the man,
20	though he was dead; and he covered him all over with the aegis,
21	golden, so that he should not tear his skin as he dragged him.
22	So in his rage he defiled brilliant Hector;
23	and the blessed gods, looking on, kept pitying him,
24	and kept urging the keen-sighted slayer of Argus to steal him away.
25	This pleased all the others, but never Hera,
26	nor Poseidon, nor the gray-eyed maiden;
27	but they held to it, as when sacred Ilios first became hateful to them,
28	and Priam, and the people, for Alexander's blind folly,
29	who insulted the goddesses when they came to his farmyard,
30	and praised her who gave him his grievous lust.
31	But when the twelfth dawn after that had come,
32	then Phoebus Apollo spoke among the immortals:
33	“You are hard, you gods, and destructive. Was it not for you
34	that Hector burned thighs of oxen and unblemished goats?
35	And now you have not dared to save him, corpse though he is,
36	for his wife to see, and his mother and his child,
37	and his father Priam and the people, who would quickly
38	burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites.
39	But it is deadly Achilles, you gods, that you choose to help,
40	who has no fitting mind, nor any thought
41	in his breast that can be bent; he knows savage ways, like a lion
42	that gives way to his great strength and proud spirit
43	and goes among the flocks of men to take his feast;
44	so Achilles has lost all pity, and he has no shame,
45	which greatly harms men and helps them.
46	A man may well have lost another, dearer still,
47	a brother of the same womb, or even a son;
48	yet when he has wept and mourned, he leaves off;
49	for the Fates have put an enduring heart in men.
50	But this man, when he has robbed brilliant Hector of his dear life,
51	ties him to his horses and drags him around the mound of his dear companion;
52	and that is not more honorable for him, nor better.
53	Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him;
54	for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth.”
55	Then in anger white-armed Hera said to him:
56	“Even that word of yours might stand, god of the silver bow,
57	if you gods are to give Achilles and Hector equal honor.
58	Hector is a mortal and was suckled at a woman's breast;
59	but Achilles is the offspring of a goddess, whom I myself
60	nursed and cherished, and gave as wife to a man,
61	to Peleus, who was dear above all to the hearts of the immortals.
62	And all you gods came to the wedding; and you among them
63	feasted with your lyre, friend of the wicked, faithless always.”
64	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
65	“Hera, do not be wholly angry with the gods;
66	their honor will not be one and the same; but Hector too
67	was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios;
68	so he was to me, for he never failed in the gifts I love.
69	For my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast,
70	libation and the savor of the burnt fat; for that is the prize allotted to us.
71	But stealing him we will let be, and there is no way to do it,
72	bold Hector, unknown to Achilles; for his mother
73	is always beside him, night and day alike.
74	But let one of the gods call Thetis to come near me,
75	so that I may tell her a shrewd word, how Achilles
76	may receive gifts from Priam and release Hector.”
77	So he spoke, and storm-footed Iris rose to carry the message,
78	and between Samos and rocky Imbros
79	she leapt into the dark sea; and the water groaned over her.
80	And she sped down to the depths like a lead weight,
81	which, mounted on the horn of an ox of the fields,
82	goes down bringing doom to the fishes that eat raw flesh.
83	And she found Thetis in a hollow cave, and around her the other
84	goddesses of the sea sat assembled; and she in their midst
85	was weeping for the fate of her noble son, who was to
86	perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land.
87	And standing near, swift-footed Iris spoke to her:
88	“Rise, Thetis; Zeus calls you, whose counsels are imperishable.”
89	Then the goddess silver-footed Thetis answered her:
90	“Why does he summon me, the great god? I feel shame
91	to mingle with the immortals, and I have griefs past measure in my heart.
92	Yet I will go, and whatever he says, his word will not be in vain.”
93	So she spoke, and the brilliant goddess took a veil,
94	dark blue, and no garment was ever blacker than this.
95	And she went her way, and before her swift Iris, wind-footed,
96	led the way; and around them the wave of the sea parted.
97	And stepping out on the shore, they shot up to the sky,
98	and found the far-seeing son of Cronus, and around him all the others,
99	the blessed gods who live forever, sat assembled.
100	And she sat down beside father Zeus, and Athena made way for her.
101	And Hera put a fine golden cup in her hand
102	and gladdened her with words; and Thetis drank and handed it back.
103	And among them the father of men and gods began to speak:
104	“You have come to Olympus, goddess Thetis, grieved though you are,
105	with grief not to be forgotten in your heart; I know it myself;
106	but even so I will tell you why I called you here.
107	For nine days a quarrel has arisen among the immortals
108	about Hector's corpse and Achilles, sacker of cities;
109	and they urge the keen-sighted slayer of Argus to steal him away;
110	but I bestow this glory on Achilles,
111	keeping your respect and your friendship for the time to come.
112	Go quickly to the army and give your son this charge:
113	tell him the gods are angry with him, and that I above all
114	the immortals am angry, because in his raging heart
115	he keeps Hector beside the curved ships and has not released him,
116	in the hope that he may fear me and release Hector.
117	And I will send Iris to great-hearted Priam,
118	to ransom his dear son, going to the ships of the Achaeans,
119	and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart.”
120	So he spoke, and the goddess silver-footed Thetis did not disobey,
121	and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus.
122	And she came to the hut of her son; and there she
123	found him groaning without ceasing; and around him his dear companions
124	were busy in haste and making ready their morning meal;
125	and a great shaggy ram was being sacrificed for them in the hut.
126	And she sat down very close to him, his lady mother,
127	and stroked him with her hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
128	“My child, how long, mourning and grieving,
129	will you eat out your heart, remembering neither food
130	nor bed? It is good even to lie with a woman
131	in love; for you will not live long for me, but already
132	death and mighty fate stand close beside you.
133	But listen to me quickly: I am a messenger to you from Zeus.
134	He says the gods are angry with you, and that he above all
135	the immortals is angry, because in your raging heart
136	you keep Hector beside the curved ships and have not released him.
137	But come, release him, and accept a ransom for the body.”
138	Answering her, swift-footed Achilles said:
139	“So be it: let him who brings the ransom carry off the body,
140	if the Olympian himself so bids with a ready heart.”
141	So, among the gathered ships, mother and son
142	spoke many winged words to one another;
143	and the son of Cronus urged Iris on to sacred Ilios:
144	“Go, swift Iris, leave the seat of Olympus,
145	and take a message to great-hearted Priam within Ilios,
146	to ransom his dear son, going to the ships of the Achaeans,
147	and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart,
148	alone; let no other man of the Trojans go with him.
149	Let a herald go with him, an older man, to guide
150	the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back
151	the body to the city, the man whom brilliant Achilles killed.
152	Let him have no thought of death in his heart, and no fear;
153	for such an escort will we grant him, the slayer of Argus,
154	who will lead him until he brings him to Achilles.
155	And when he has led him into the hut of Achilles,
156	he himself will not kill him, and he will keep all the others off;
157	for he is not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender,
158	but with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant.”
159	So he spoke, and storm-footed Iris rose to carry the message.
160	And she came to the house of Priam, and found there cries and lament.
161	His sons sat around their father within the courtyard
162	and soaked their clothes with tears; and in their midst the old man
163	sat wrapped close in his cloak; and around him much
164	dung lay on the head and neck of the old man,
165	which, rolling in it, he had heaped on himself with his own hands.
166	And his daughters and his sons' wives were mourning through the house,
167	remembering those who, many and brave,
168	lay dead at the hands of the Argives, having lost their lives.
169	And the messenger of Zeus stood beside Priam, and spoke to him
170	softly; and trembling seized his limbs:
171	“Take heart in your mind, Priam, descendant of Dardanus, and have no fear;
172	for I do not come here boding evil for you,
173	but with good intent; I am a messenger to you from Zeus,
174	who, though he is far away, cares greatly for you and pities you.
175	The Olympian bids you ransom brilliant Hector,
176	and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart,
177	alone; let no other man of the Trojans go with you.
178	Let a herald go with you, an older man, to guide
179	the mules and the well-wheeled wagon, and bring back
180	the body to the city, the man whom brilliant Achilles killed.
181	Have no thought of death in your heart, and no fear;
182	for such an escort will go with you, the slayer of Argus,
183	who will lead you until he brings you to Achilles.
184	And when he has led you into the hut of Achilles,
185	he himself will not kill you, and he will keep all the others off;
186	for he is not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender,
187	but with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant.”
188	So saying, swift-footed Iris went away;
189	and he bade his sons make ready the well-wheeled mule wagon
190	and bind the wicker body upon it.
191	And he himself went down into the fragrant chamber,
192	high-roofed, of cedar, which held many bright trinkets;
193	and he called in Hecuba his wife and said:
194	“Strange one, an Olympian messenger has come to me from Zeus,
195	to ransom my dear son, going to the ships of the Achaeans,
196	and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart.
197	But come, tell me this: how does it seem to you in your mind?
198	For terribly my own might and heart bid me
199	to go there to the ships, into the wide army of the Achaeans.”
200	So he spoke, and the woman wailed aloud and answered him:
201	“Ah me, where has your mind gone, for which before
202	you were famous among strangers and among those you rule?
203	How can you wish to go alone to the ships of the Achaeans,
204	before the eyes of the man who has slain many and brave
205	sons of yours? Your heart is iron.
206	For if he takes you and sets his eyes on you,
207	that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man, he will not pity you
208	or respect you at all. No, let us weep for him now, apart,
209	sitting in the hall; this is how mighty Fate
210	spun for him with her thread at his birth, when I myself bore him,
211	that he should glut the swift dogs, far from his parents,
212	at the side of a mighty man; I wish I could fix my teeth
213	in the middle of his liver and eat it; then there would be requital
214	for my son, since he did not kill him as he played the coward,
215	but as he stood before the Trojans and the deep-bosomed Trojan women,
216	with no thought of flight or of shelter.”
217	Then the old man, godlike Priam, spoke to her in turn:
218	“Do not hold me back when I wish to go, and do not yourself
219	be a bird of evil omen in the halls; you will not persuade me.
220	For if any other of the men on earth had bidden me,
221	seers who read the smoke of the sacrifice, or priests,
222	we would call it a lie and turn away from it all the more;
223	but now, since I myself heard the goddess and looked on her face to face,
224	I will go, and the word will not be in vain. And if it is my fate
225	to die beside the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans,
226	I would have it so; let Achilles kill me at once,
227	when I have taken my son in my arms and put away my desire for lament.”
228	He spoke, and opened the fine lids of the chests;
229	and from them he took twelve beautiful robes,
230	and twelve single cloaks, and as many rugs,
231	and as many white mantles, and as many tunics besides.
232	And he weighed out and brought ten talents of gold in all,
233	and brought out two gleaming tripods and four cauldrons,
234	and brought out a beautiful cup, which Thracian men had given him
235	when he went to them on an embassy, a great possession; not even this
236	did the old man spare in his halls, for above all he wished in his heart
237	to ransom his dear son. And all the Trojans
238	he kept away from the colonnade, rebuking them with shaming words:
239	“Off with you, you wretches, you disgraces! Have you not
240	lament enough at home, that you come to trouble me?
241	Or do you make light of it that Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me sorrows,
242	to lose my best son? But you will learn it too;
243	for you will be all the easier now for the Achaeans
244	to slay, now that he is dead. But as for me,
245	before I see the city sacked and ravaged
246	with my eyes, may I go down into the house of Hades.”
247	He spoke, and drove the men off with his staff; and they went out
248	before the old man in his haste. And he shouted at his sons,
249	rebuking Helenus and Paris and brilliant Agathon
250	and Pammon and Antiphonus and Polites, good at the war cry,
251	and Deiphobus and Hippothous and brilliant Agauus;
252	to these nine the old man shouted his commands:
253	“Hurry, you wicked children, you shames of mine! I wish that all of you
254	had been killed beside the swift ships in Hector's place.
255	Ah me, utterly ill-starred that I am, since I fathered the best sons
256	in wide Troy, and of them, I say, not one is left,
257	godlike Mestor and Troilus, who fought from his chariot,
258	and Hector, who was a god among men, and did not seem
259	the son of a mortal man, but of a god.
260	Ares has destroyed them, and these disgraces are all that is left,
261	liars and dancers, best at beating the dancing floor,
262	robbers of lambs and kids from their own people.
263	Will you not make ready the wagon for me at once,
264	and put all these things on it, so that we may make our way?”
265	So he spoke, and they, fearing their father's rebuke,
266	brought out the well-wheeled mule wagon,
267	fine and newly built, and bound the wicker body upon it,
268	and took down from its peg the mule yoke,
269	of boxwood, with its knob, well fitted with guide rings;
270	and they brought out the yoke band, nine cubits long, with the yoke.
271	And they laid the yoke carefully on the polished pole,
272	at the front end, and put the ring over the pin,
273	and bound it three times on each side around the knob, and then
274	tied it fast all along, and tucked the end under the hook.
275	And they brought from the chamber and heaped on the polished wagon
276	the boundless ransom for Hector's head,
277	and yoked the strong-hoofed mules that work in harness,
278	which the Mysians had once given to Priam, splendid gifts.
279	And for Priam they led under the yoke the horses that the old man
280	kept for himself and reared at the polished manger.
281	So the two had the horses yoked in the high palace,
282	the herald and Priam, with shrewd counsels in their minds;
283	and Hecuba came near them with grieving heart,
284	holding wine sweet to the heart in her right hand
285	in a golden cup, so that they might pour a libation before they went;
286	and she stood before the horses, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
287	“Here, pour a libation to father Zeus, and pray that you come home
288	again from hostile men, since your heart
289	urges you to the ships, though I do not wish it.
290	But pray then to the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds,
291	the god of Ida, who looks down on all of Troy,
292	and ask for a bird of omen, his swift messenger, the one that to him
293	is dearest of birds, and whose strength is greatest,
294	on the right, so that you may see it yourself with your eyes
295	and, trusting in it, go to the ships of the Danaans of the swift horses.
296	But if far-seeing Zeus will not give you his own messenger,
297	then I would not urge you on and bid you
298	go to the ships of the Argives, however eager you are.”
299	Answering her, godlike Priam said:
300	“Lady, I will not disobey you in this that you bid me;
301	for it is good to lift up one's hands to Zeus, in the hope that he may have pity.”
302	He spoke, and the old man urged the housekeeper, a handmaid,
303	to pour pure water over his hands; and she stood beside him,
304	the handmaid, holding a basin and a pitcher in her hands.
305	And when he had washed, he took the cup from his wife;
306	then he prayed, standing in the middle of the enclosure, and poured out the wine,
307	looking up to the sky, and he spoke and said:
308	“Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest,
309	grant that I come to Achilles as one welcomed and pitied,
310	and send a bird of omen, your swift messenger, the one that to you yourself
311	is dearest of birds, and whose strength is greatest,
312	on the right, so that I may see it myself with my eyes
313	and, trusting in it, go to the ships of the Danaans of the swift horses.”
314	So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him,
315	and at once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds,
316	the dark hunter, which men also call the dusky eagle.
317	As wide as the door of the high-roofed chamber
318	of a wealthy man is made, well fitted with bolts,
319	so wide were its wings on either side; and it appeared to them
320	darting on the right across the city; and when they saw it
321	they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed.
322	And the old man in his haste mounted his chariot,
323	and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade.
324	In front the mules drew the four-wheeled wagon,
325	and wise-hearted Idaeus drove them; and behind
326	came the horses, which the old man drove, urging them on with the whip,
327	swiftly through the city; and all his dear ones followed with him,
328	lamenting much, as if he were going to his death.
329	And when they had come down from the city and reached the plain,
330	the others went back toward Ilios,
331	his sons and his sons-in-law; but the two did not escape the notice of far-seeing Zeus
332	as they came out on the plain; and seeing the old man he pitied him,
333	and at once he spoke to Hermes, his dear son, face to face:
334	“Hermes, since to you above all it is dearest
335	to keep company with a man, and you listen to whom you will,
336	go, and lead Priam to the hollow ships of the Achaeans
337	in such a way that no one sees him or notices him
338	of the other Danaans, until he comes to the son of Peleus.”
339	So he spoke, and the guide, the slayer of Argus, did not disobey.
340	At once he bound under his feet his fine sandals,
341	ambrosial, golden, which carried him over the waters
342	and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind;
343	and he took the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men,
344	of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping;
345	holding it in his hands the mighty slayer of Argus flew.
346	And quickly he came to Troy and the Hellespont,
347	and went on his way in the likeness of a young prince,
348	with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful.
349	And when they had driven past the great mound of Ilus,
350	they halted the mules and the horses to drink
351	at the river; for darkness had now come over the earth.
352	And the herald, seeing him close by, made out
353	Hermes, and he spoke to Priam and said:
354	“Take thought, descendant of Dardanus; this is work for a prudent mind.
355	I see a man, and I think he will soon tear us to pieces.
356	But come, let us flee on the horses, or else
357	clasp his knees and entreat him, in the hope that he may have pity.”
358	So he spoke, and the old man's mind was confounded, and he was terribly afraid,
359	and the hair stood up on his supple limbs,
360	and he stood amazed; but the great helper came near of himself,
361	and took the old man's hand, and questioned him and said:
362	“Where, father, are you guiding your horses and mules like this
363	through the ambrosial night, when other mortals are asleep?
364	Do you not fear the Achaeans breathing fury,
365	who are hostile to you and merciless, and near at hand?
366	If one of them should see you through the swift black night
367	bringing so many good things, what would you have in mind then?
368	You are not young yourself, and this man who goes with you is old,
369	to ward off a man, when someone is the first to grow angry.
370	But I will do you no harm, and I would even keep another
371	away from you; for you look to me like my dear father.”
372	Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
373	“It is much as you say, dear child.
374	But still some god has held his hand over me too,
375	who sent such a wayfarer to meet me,
376	one of good omen, so admirable are you in build and looks,
377	and you are wise in mind, and come of blessed parents.”
378	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to him in turn:
379	“Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right.
380	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
381	are you sending away many fine treasures
382	to foreign men, so that they may stay safe for you,
383	or are you all now leaving sacred Ilios
384	in fear? For such a man, the best, has perished,
385	your son; for he never held back from battle with the Achaeans.”
386	Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
387	“Who are you, good sir, and of what parents are you,
388	that you speak so well of the fate of my ill-starred son?”
389	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to him in turn:
390	“You are testing me, old sir, in asking about brilliant Hector.
391	Him I have seen many times in the battle where men win glory
392	with my own eyes, and when, having driven them to the ships,
393	he kept killing the Argives, cutting them down with the sharp bronze;
394	and we stood there and marveled; for Achilles
395	would not let us fight, being angry with the son of Atreus.
396	For I am his attendant; one well-made ship brought us;
397	I am one of the Myrmidons, and my father is Polyctor.
398	He is wealthy, and an old man, just as you are,
399	and he has six sons, and I am the seventh;
400	and casting lots with them I drew the lot to follow him here.
401	And now I have come to the plain from the ships; for at dawn
402	the bright-eyed Achaeans will set battle in array around the city.
403	For these men chafe as they sit idle, nor can
404	the kings of the Achaeans hold them back in their eagerness for war.”
405	Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
406	“If you are indeed the attendant of Achilles, son of Peleus,
407	come, tell me the whole truth:
408	is my son still beside the ships, or by now
409	has Achilles cut him limb from limb and set him before his dogs?”
410	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to him in turn:
411	“Old man, the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds,
412	but he still lies beside the ship of Achilles,
413	just as he was, in the huts; and this is the twelfth dawn
414	that he has lain there, and his flesh does not rot at all, nor do the worms
415	eat him, which feed on men slain in battle.
416	Truly he drags him around the mound of his dear companion
417	ruthlessly, whenever brilliant Dawn appears,
418	yet he does not disfigure him; you yourself would gaze, if you came,
419	at how fresh as dew he lies, and the blood is washed from him,
420	and he is nowhere foul; and all the wounds are closed
421	where he was struck; for many drove their bronze into him.
422	So do the blessed gods care for your own son,
423	corpse though he is, since he was dear above all to their hearts.”
424	So he spoke, and the old man rejoiced, and answered him:
425	“My child, truly it is good to give the gifts that are their due
426	to the immortals; for my son, if that was ever so,
427	never forgot in his halls the gods who hold Olympus;
428	so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death.
429	But come, accept from me this fine goblet,
430	and protect me myself, and with the gods' help escort me,
431	until I come to the hut of the son of Peleus.”
432	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to him in turn:
433	“You are testing me, old sir, a younger man, but you will not persuade me,
434	bidding me take gifts from you behind Achilles' back.
435	I fear him and feel shame above all in my heart
436	to rob him, lest some harm come to me afterward.
437	But as your escort I would go even to famous Argos,
438	with great care, in a swift ship or going with you on foot;
439	and no one would fight you in scorn of your escort.”
440	He spoke, and the great helper, springing onto the chariot behind the horses,
441	quickly took the whip and the reins in his hands,
442	and breathed noble might into the horses and the mules.
443	But when they came to the towers of the ships and the ditch,
444	the sentries had just begun to busy themselves about their supper;
445	and the guide, the slayer of Argus, poured sleep over them
446	all, and at once opened the gates and pushed back the bars,

491	rejoices in his heart, and hopes every day

495	I had fifty when the sons of the Achaeans came;
496	nineteen were born to me of one womb,

499	and he who was my only one, who guarded the city and its people,

502	to ransom him from you, and I bring a boundless ransom.
503	But respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me,
504	remembering your own father; I am more pitiable still,
505	for I have endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured,
506	to put to my lips the hand of the man who killed my sons.”
507	So he spoke, and in him he roused the longing for lament for his father;
508	and taking the old man's hand he gently pushed him away.
509	And the two remembered, the one man-slaying Hector,
510	and wept unceasingly, crouched before the feet of Achilles,
511	and Achilles wept for his own father, and now again
512	for Patroclus; and their groaning rose through the house.
513	But when brilliant Achilles had taken his fill of lament,
514	and the longing for it had gone from his heart and from his limbs,
515	at once he rose from his chair and raised the old man by the hand,
516	pitying his gray head and his gray beard,
517	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
518	“Ah, wretch, truly you have endured many evils in your heart.
519	How did you dare to come alone to the ships of the Achaeans,
520	before the eyes of the man who has slain many and brave
521	sons of yours? Your heart is iron.
522	But come, sit down on a chair, and let us leave our sorrows
523	to lie quiet in our hearts, for all our grief;
524	for there is no profit in chilling lament;
525	for so the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals,
526	to live in grief; but they themselves are free from care.
527	For two jars stand on the floor of Zeus
528	of the gifts that he gives, one of evils, and the other of blessings;
529	the man to whom Zeus who delights in thunder gives a mixture
530	meets now with evil, and now with good;
531	but the man to whom he gives only from the grievous, he makes a thing of scorn,
532	and evil, ravening hunger drives him over the shining earth,
533	and he wanders honored neither by gods nor by mortals.
534	So to Peleus too the gods gave splendid gifts
535	from his birth; for he excelled all men
536	in prosperity and wealth, and he ruled over the Myrmidons,
537	and to him, a mortal, they gave a goddess as his wife.
538	But on him too the god laid evil, that no
539	offspring of princely sons was born to him in his halls,
540	but he fathered one son, doomed to an untimely death; nor do I
541	care for him as he grows old, since very far from my native land
542	I sit here in Troy, bringing sorrow to you and your children.
543	And you too, old man, we hear, were prosperous once;
544	all that Lesbos, the seat of Macar, shuts in, out to sea,
545	and Phrygia inland, and the boundless Hellespont,
546	over all these, old man, they say you excelled in wealth and sons.
547	But since the heavenly gods brought this bane upon you,
548	there are always battles around your city, and the killing of men.
549	Bear up, and do not mourn relentlessly in your heart;
550	for you will achieve nothing by grieving for your own son,
551	nor will you raise him up; before that you will suffer some other evil.”
552	Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
553	“Do not yet bid me sit on a chair, nurtured by Zeus, while Hector
554	lies uncared for in the huts, but quickly
555	release him, so that I may see him with my eyes; and accept the ransom,
556	the many things that we bring you; may you have joy of them, and may you come
557	to your native land, since you have first let me
558	live myself and see the light of the sun.”
559	Looking at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles said:
560	“Do not provoke me any longer, old man; I have it in mind myself
561	to release Hector to you; for a messenger came to me from Zeus,
562	my mother, who bore me, the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea.
563	And I know you too, Priam, in my heart, and you do not escape me:
564	one of the gods led you to the swift ships of the Achaeans.
565	For no mortal would dare to come, not even one in his prime,
566	into the camp; for he would not escape the sentries, nor would he
567	easily push back the bar of our doors.
568	So now do not stir my heart any more among its sorrows,
569	lest I do not spare even you, old man, in my huts,
570	suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus.”
571	So he spoke, and the old man was afraid, and obeyed his word.
572	And the son of Peleus sprang out of the door of the house like a lion,
573	not alone: with him went two attendants,
574	the hero Automedon and Alcimus, whom above all
575	of his companions Achilles honored, after Patroclus who was dead;
576	who then loosed the horses and the mules from under the yoke,
577	and led in the herald, the old man's crier,
578	and sat him on a chair; and from the polished wagon
579	they took the boundless ransom for Hector's head.
580	But they left two mantles and a well-spun tunic,
581	so that he could wrap the corpse and give it to be carried home.
582	And he called out the slave women and bade them wash and anoint him,
583	taking him aside, so that Priam should not see his son,
584	lest in the grief of his heart he should not hold back his anger
585	when he saw his son, and Achilles' own heart be stirred,
586	and he kill him, and sin against the commands of Zeus.
587	And when the slave women had washed him and anointed him with oil,
588	and put a fine mantle and a tunic around him,
589	Achilles himself lifted him and laid him on a bier,
590	and his companions lifted it with him onto the polished wagon.
591	He groaned aloud then, and called his dear companion by name:
592	“Do not be angry with me, Patroclus, if you learn,
593	even in the house of Hades, that I have released brilliant Hector
594	to his dear father, since the ransom he gave me is not unseemly.
595	And I in turn will give you your share of this too, as much as is fitting.”
596	He spoke, and brilliant Achilles went back into the hut,
597	and sat down on the intricately worked chair from which he had risen,
598	by the other wall, and spoke a word to Priam:
599	“Your son is released to you, old man, as you asked,
600	and he lies on a bier; and when dawn appears
601	you will see him yourself as you take him away; but now let us remember supper.
602	For even Niobe of the lovely hair remembered food,
603	she whose twelve children perished in her halls,
604	six daughters and six sons in their prime.
605	The sons Apollo killed with arrows from his silver bow,
606	in his anger with Niobe, and the daughters Artemis of the showering arrows,

608	she said that the goddess had borne two, while she herself had borne many;
609	and so those two, though they were only two, destroyed them all.
610	For nine days they lay in their blood, and there was no one
611	to bury them, for the son of Cronus had turned the people to stones;
612	and on the tenth day the heavenly gods buried them.
613	And she remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears.
614	And now somewhere among the rocks, in the lonely mountains,
615	on Sipylus, where they say the goddesses have their beds,
616	the nymphs who dance around the Achelous,
617	there, though she is stone, she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her.
618	But come, let us two also think, brilliant old sir,
619	of food; and then you may weep again for your dear son
620	when you have brought him into Ilios; he will be much wept for.”
621	He spoke, and springing up swift Achilles slaughtered a silver-white sheep,
622	and his companions flayed it and were busy over it in good order,

624	and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off.
625	And Automedon took bread and set it out on the table
626	in beautiful baskets, while Achilles served the meat.
627	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
628	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
629	then Priam, descendant of Dardanus, marveled at Achilles,
630	how tall he was and how fair; for he looked like the gods face to face;
631	and Achilles marveled at Priam, descendant of Dardanus,
632	seeing his noble looks and hearing his words.
633	But when they had taken their fill of looking at one another,
634	the old man, godlike Priam, spoke to him first:
635	“Put me to bed now quickly, nurtured by Zeus, so that at last
636	we may lie down and take our fill of sweet sleep;
637	for my eyes have not yet closed beneath my lids
638	since my son lost his life at your hands,

640	rolling in the dung in the enclosure of the courtyard.
641	Now at last I have tasted food and let sparkling wine
642	go down my throat; before now I had tasted nothing.”

645	blankets on them, and spread rugs over them,

647	And the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands,
648	and quickly, in haste, they spread two beds.
649	Taunting him, swift-footed Achilles said:
650	“Lie outside, dear old man, lest one of the Achaeans
651	come in here, a counselor, one of those who always
652	sit beside me and take counsel with me, as is the custom;
653	if one of them should see you through the swift black night,
654	he would at once tell Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
655	and there might be delay in the ransoming of the body.
656	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
657	for how many days are you minded to give brilliant Hector his funeral rites,
658	so that for so long I may stay here myself and hold back the army?”
659	Then the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
660	“If you are willing that I should hold a funeral for brilliant Hector,

662	For you know how we are penned within the city, and the wood is far
663	to bring from the mountain, and the Trojans are greatly afraid.
664	Nine days we would lament him in our halls,
665	and on the tenth we would bury him, and the people would feast,

667	and on the twelfth we will fight, if we must.”
668	Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles spoke to him in turn:
669	“This too shall be as you ask, old Priam;

673	So they slept there in the porch of the house,

683	“Old man, you have no thought of any evil, so soundly do you still sleep

685	And now you have ransomed your dear son, and given much for him;

688	son of Atreus, learns of you, and all the Achaeans know it.”

693	the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,

751	For my other sons swift-footed Achilles
