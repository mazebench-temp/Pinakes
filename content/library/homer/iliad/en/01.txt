# Homer, Iliad, Book 1 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles, son of Peleus—
2	that ruinous wrath, which laid countless sorrows on the Achaeans,
3	hurled down to Hades many mighty souls
4	of heroes, and made the men themselves the spoil of dogs
5	and of every bird; and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled—
6	from the time when first the two of them stood apart in strife,
7	the son of Atreus, lord of men, and brilliant Achilles.
8	Which of the gods, then, brought the two together to fight in strife?
9	The son of Leto and of Zeus. For he, in anger at the king,
10	roused an evil sickness through the army, and the people kept dying,
11	because the priest Chryses had been dishonored
12	by the son of Atreus. For Chryses had come to the swift ships of the Achaeans
13	to win his daughter's freedom, bringing a boundless ransom,
14	holding in his hands the fillets of far-shooting Apollo
15	upon a golden scepter, and he begged all the Achaeans,
16	but most of all the two sons of Atreus, marshals of the people:
17	“Sons of Atreus, and you other well-greaved Achaeans,
18	may the gods who have their homes on Olympus grant you
19	to sack the city of Priam, and to come safely home;
20	but set my dear child free, and accept this ransom,
21	in reverence for the son of Zeus, far-shooting Apollo.”
22	Then all the other Achaeans shouted their assent:
23	to respect the priest and accept the splendid ransom.
24	But this did not please the heart of Agamemnon, son of Atreus;
25	he sent the man away harshly, and laid a stern command on him:
26	“Old man, let me not find you beside the hollow ships,
27	either lingering now or coming back again later,
28	or the scepter and the fillet of the god may not protect you.
29	Her I will not release. Sooner will old age come upon her
30	in our house, in Argos, far from her native land,
31	as she walks to and fro at the loom and comes to share my bed.
32	Go now; do not provoke me, so that you may return the safer.”
33	So he spoke, and the old man was afraid, and obeyed his word.
34	He went in silence along the shore of the loud-roaring sea;
35	and then, when he had gone far off, the old man prayed long and hard
36	to lord Apollo, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore:
37	“Hear me, god of the silver bow, who stand guard over Chryse
38	and holy Cilla, and rule Tenedos with might,
39	Smintheus: if ever I roofed a temple to your pleasure,
40	or if ever I burned for you the fat thigh-pieces
41	of bulls and goats, fulfill for me this wish:
42	let the Danaans pay for my tears with your arrows.”
43	So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
44	Down from the peaks of Olympus he strode, angry at heart,
45	with his bow on his shoulders and his close-covered quiver.
46	The arrows clattered on the shoulders of the angry god
47	as he moved; and he came on like the night.
48	Then he sat down apart from the ships and let fly an arrow,
49	and terrible was the twang of the silver bow.
50	First he went after the mules and the swift dogs,
51	but then, letting loose his piercing shaft against the men themselves,
52	he struck; and the pyres of the dead burned on, thick and constant.
53	For nine days the arrows of the god ranged through the army;
54	on the tenth, Achilles called the people to assembly,
55	for the white-armed goddess Hera had put it in his mind:
56	she cared for the Danaans, because she saw them dying.
57	And when they had gathered and were all assembled,
58	swift-footed Achilles stood up among them and spoke:
59	“Son of Atreus, now I think we shall be beaten back
60	and sail for home again—if we can even escape death—
61	if war and plague together are to crush the Achaeans.
62	But come, let us ask some seer, or a priest,
63	or even an interpreter of dreams—for a dream, too, comes from Zeus—
64	who might tell us why Phoebus Apollo is so enraged:
65	whether he finds fault with us for some vow or hecatomb,
66	in the hope that, receiving the savor of lambs and unblemished goats,
67	he may be willing to keep this ruin away from us.”
68	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
69	Calchas, son of Thestor, by far the best of the bird-seers,
70	who knew the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before,
71	and who had guided the ships of the Achaeans to Ilios
72	by his gift of prophecy, which Phoebus Apollo had granted him.
73	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
74	“Achilles, dear to Zeus, you bid me explain
75	the wrath of Apollo, the lord who shoots from afar.
76	Well then, I will speak; but you take heed, and swear to me
77	that you will readily defend me with words and with your hands.
78	For I think I will anger a man who rules with great power over all
79	the Argives, and whom the Achaeans obey.
80	A king is the stronger when he is angry with a lesser man;
81	for even if he swallows down his anger for the day,
82	still he keeps a grudge thereafter, until he has fulfilled it,
83	deep in his chest. So consider whether you will keep me safe.”
84	Answering him, swift-footed Achilles said:
85	“Take heart, and speak out whatever oracle you know.
86	For by Apollo, dear to Zeus, to whom you, Calchas,
87	pray when you reveal the oracles to the Danaans,
88	no one, while I live and look upon the earth,
89	shall lay heavy hands on you beside the hollow ships,
90	not one of all the Danaans—not even if you mean Agamemnon,
91	who now claims to be far the best of the Achaeans.”
92	Then at last the noble seer took courage and spoke:
93	“It is not for a vow that he finds fault, nor for a hecatomb,
94	but because of his priest, whom Agamemnon dishonored:
95	he did not release his daughter or accept the ransom.
96	For this the Far-Shooter has given sorrows, and will give still more;
97	nor will he thrust this shameful ruin away from the Danaans
98	until the bright-eyed girl is given back to her dear father,
99	unbought, unransomed, and a holy hecatomb is led
100	to Chryse. Then we might appease him and win him over.”
101	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
102	the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
103	in distress; and his heart, dark on every side, was filled
104	with mighty fury, and his eyes were like blazing fire.
105	First of all he addressed Calchas, with menace in his look:
106	“Prophet of evils, never yet have you told me anything good.
107	It is always dear to your heart to prophesy evil;
108	not one good word have you ever spoken or brought to pass.
109	And now among the Danaans you prophesy and declare
110	that this is why the Far-Shooter is bringing sorrows on them:
111	because the splendid ransom for the girl Chryseis
112	I was not willing to accept, since I much prefer to keep her
113	at home. Indeed, I prefer her to Clytemnestra,
114	my wedded wife, since she is in no way inferior to her,
115	not in form or stature, not in mind or in handiwork.
116	Yet even so I am willing to give her back, if that is better:
117	I would rather the people be safe than perish.
118	But make ready a prize for me at once, so that I am not the only one
119	of the Argives without a prize, since that would not be fitting.
120	For you all see this: my prize is going elsewhere.”
121	Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles answered him:
122	“Most glorious son of Atreus, most covetous of all men,
123	how will the great-hearted Achaeans give you a prize?
124	We know of no great common store laid up anywhere;
125	what we plundered from the cities has all been divided,
126	and it is not fitting for the people to gather it back again.
127	No—give her up now to the god; and we Achaeans
128	will repay you three and four times over, if ever Zeus
129	grants us to sack the well-walled city of Troy.”
130	Answering him, powerful Agamemnon said:
131	“Not like this, godlike Achilles, brave as you are:
132	do not try to trick me. You will not outwit me or win me over.
133	Do you want to keep a prize for yourself, while I
134	sit here empty-handed? Is that why you order me to give her back?
135	No: if the great-hearted Achaeans will give me a prize,
136	suiting it to my heart, so that it will be of equal worth—well and good.
137	But if they will not give it, then I myself will go and take
138	your prize, or Ajax's, or that of Odysseus—
139	I will seize it and carry it off; and the man I come to will be angry.
140	But these things we will consider again later.
141	Now come, let us drag a black ship down to the shining sea,
142	gather oarsmen for it, enough for the task, and put a hecatomb
143	aboard, and set Chryseis herself, the fair-cheeked,
144	on board; and let one man of counsel be in command,
145	either Ajax or Idomeneus or brilliant Odysseus,
146	or you, son of Peleus, most terrible of all men,
147	to offer the sacrifices and appease the Far-Worker for us.”
148	Looking at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles said:
149	“Ah, you clothed in shamelessness, with your mind set on gain,
150	how can any of the Achaeans readily obey your orders,
151	either to go on a raid or to fight men in force?
152	It was not because of the Trojan spearmen that I came
153	here to fight, since they have done me no wrong.
154	They never drove off my cattle or my horses,
155	nor ever in deep-soiled Phthia, nurse of men,
156	did they ravage my harvest, since there is a great deal between us:
157	shadowy mountains and the echoing sea.
158	No, it was you we followed, you utterly shameless one, to please you,
159	to win satisfaction for Menelaus—and for you, dog-face—
160	from the Trojans. This you neither heed nor care about;
161	and now you threaten to take away my prize yourself,
162	for which I toiled hard, and which the sons of the Achaeans gave me.
163	Never do I receive a prize equal to yours whenever the Achaeans
164	sack some well-peopled citadel of the Trojans.
165	The greater part of the furious fighting
166	my hands carry through; but whenever the division comes,
167	the far greater prize is yours, and I, with something small but dear,
168	go back to my ships, when I am worn out with fighting.
169	Now I will go to Phthia, since it is far better
170	to go home with my curved ships; and I do not intend,
171	while I stay here dishonored, to pile up riches and wealth for you.”
172	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, answered him:
173	“Run away, then, if your heart so urges you; I will not
174	beg you to stay for my sake. I have others at my side
175	who will honor me, and above all Zeus the counselor.
176	Of all the kings whom Zeus has nurtured, you are the most hateful to me;
177	strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles.
178	If you are very strong, it was a god, I think, who gave you that.
179	Go home with your ships and your companions
180	and lord it over the Myrmidons. I do not care about you,
181	nor do I mind your resentment. But this is my threat to you:
182	since Phoebus Apollo is taking Chryseis from me,
183	her I will send home in my own ship with my companions;
184	but I will lead away fair-cheeked Briseis,
185	going myself to your hut to take your prize, so that you may know well
186	how much greater I am than you, and another man may shrink
187	from speaking to me as an equal and matching himself with me to my face.”
188	So he spoke. Grief came upon the son of Peleus, and the heart
189	within his shaggy chest was torn two ways in thought:
190	whether to draw the sharp sword from beside his thigh,
191	break up the assembly, and kill the son of Atreus,
192	or to check his anger and restrain his spirit.
193	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
194	and was drawing his great sword from its sheath, Athena came
195	down from the sky; for the white-armed goddess Hera had sent her,
196	loving both men alike in her heart and caring for them both.
197	She stood behind him and caught the son of Peleus by his fair hair,
198	appearing to him alone; none of the others saw her.
199	Achilles was astonished, and turned around, and at once he knew
200	Pallas Athena; and her eyes blazed terribly.
201	And he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
202	“Why have you come now, child of aegis-bearing Zeus?
203	Is it to see the arrogance of Agamemnon, son of Atreus?
204	But I will speak out to you, and I think it will be fulfilled:
205	by his own overbearing pride he may soon lose his life.”
206	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
207	“I came to stop your fury, if you will obey me,
208	down from the sky; the white-armed goddess Hera sent me,
209	loving you both alike in her heart and caring for you both.
210	Come now, cease from strife, and do not draw your sword with your hand.
211	Rather, reproach him with words, telling him how it will be.
212	For I will tell you this, and it will surely come to pass:
213	someday three times as many splendid gifts will be laid before you
214	because of this outrage. But hold back, and obey us.”
215	Answering her, swift-footed Achilles said:
216	“Goddess, a man must heed the word of you two,
217	however angry he may be in his heart; for that is better.
218	Whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen.”
219	He spoke, and held his heavy hand on the silver hilt,
220	and thrust the great sword back into its sheath, and did not disobey
221	the word of Athena. And she had gone back to Olympus,
222	to the house of aegis-bearing Zeus, among the other gods.
223	But the son of Peleus once again, with harsh words,
224	addressed the son of Atreus, and did not yet cease from his anger:
225	“You wine-sodden drunk, with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer,
226	never yet have you dared to arm for battle with the people
227	or to go out on ambush with the best of the Achaeans—
228	you haven't the heart; to you that looks like death.
229	Far better, surely, throughout the wide army of the Achaeans,
230	to strip the gifts from any man who speaks against you!
231	People-devouring king—since you rule over nobodies;
232	otherwise, son of Atreus, this insult would be your last.
233	But I will speak out to you, and swear a great oath upon it:
234	yes, by this scepter, which never again will leaves and branches
235	put forth, since first it left its stump in the mountains,
236	nor will it bloom again, for the bronze has stripped it all around
237	of leaves and bark; and now the sons of the Achaeans
238	carry it in their hands when they give judgment, those who guard
239	the ordinances that come from Zeus: this shall be a great oath for you.
240	Someday a longing for Achilles will come upon the sons of the Achaeans,
241	on all of them together; and then, for all your grief, you will be powerless
242	to help, when many at the hands of man-slaying Hector
243	fall dying; and you will tear the heart within you,
244	in anger that you paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans.”
245	So spoke the son of Peleus, and flung the scepter to the ground,
246	studded with golden nails, and sat down himself,
247	while on the other side the son of Atreus nursed his wrath. Then Nestor
248	sprang up among them, sweet of speech, the clear-voiced orator of the Pylians,
249	from whose tongue the voice flowed sweeter than honey.
250	In his lifetime two generations of mortal men
251	had already passed away, who had been born and raised with him long before
252	in sacred Pylos, and now he was king among the third.
253	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
254	“Ah, what great sorrow is coming upon the land of Achaea!
255	Surely Priam would rejoice, and the sons of Priam,
256	and the other Trojans would be very glad at heart,
257	if they were to hear all this of you two quarreling,
258	you who are first among the Danaans in counsel, and first in battle.
259	But listen to me; you are both younger than I.
260	For in the past I have kept company with men even better
261	than you, and never once did they make light of me.
262	Never yet have I seen such men, nor shall I see them again,
263	as Peirithous and Dryas, shepherd of the people,
264	Caeneus and Exadius and godlike Polyphemus,
265	and Theseus, son of Aegeus, like the immortals.
266	They were the mightiest of all men ever raised on earth;
267	mightiest they were, and they fought against the mightiest,
268	the beasts who lived in the mountains, and destroyed them terribly.
269	And I kept company with them, when I came from Pylos,
270	far away, from a distant land; for they themselves had summoned me.
271	And I fought on my own account; but against those men no one
272	of the mortals now living on earth could fight.
273	And yet they heeded my counsels and obeyed my word.
274	So you too obey, since it is better to obey.
275	You, great as you are, do not take the girl away from him,
276	but let her be, since the sons of the Achaeans first gave her to him as his prize;
277	nor you, son of Peleus, seek to strive with a king
278	in open conflict, since no ordinary share of honor falls
279	to a scepter-bearing king, to whom Zeus has given glory.
280	Even if you are strong, and a goddess was the mother who bore you,
281	yet he is the greater, since he rules over more.
282	Son of Atreus, check your fury; and I myself
283	implore you to let go your anger against Achilles, who for all
284	the Achaeans is a mighty bulwark against evil war.”
285	Answering him, powerful Agamemnon said:
286	“Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right.
287	But this man wants to be above all others;
288	he wants to have power over all, to be lord over all,
289	and to give orders to all—but there is one, I think, who will not obey him.
290	If the gods who live forever made him a spearman,
291	do his insults therefore rush forth to be spoken?”
292	Breaking in on him, brilliant Achilles answered:
293	“Yes, I would be called a coward and a nobody
294	if I gave way to you in every matter, whatever you say.
295	Give these orders to others, but do not give them
296	to me; for I, I think, will obey you no longer.
297	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
298	I will not fight with my hands for the girl's sake,
299	not with you nor with any other, since you who gave her are taking her away;
300	but of the other things that I have beside my swift black ship,
301	of those you could not seize and carry off a single thing against my will.
302	Come, try it, so that these men too may know:
303	at once your dark blood will gush out around my spear.”
304	So the two of them, having fought it out with hostile words,
305	stood up, and dissolved the assembly beside the ships of the Achaeans.
306	The son of Peleus went back to his huts and his balanced ships
307	with the son of Menoetius and his companions;
308	but the son of Atreus drew a swift ship down to the sea,
309	chose twenty oarsmen for it, and put aboard a hecatomb
310	for the god, and brought fair-cheeked Chryseis
311	and seated her on board; and Odysseus of many wiles went as commander.
312	So they embarked and sailed over the watery ways;
313	and the son of Atreus ordered the people to purify themselves.
314	They cleansed themselves, and threw the defilements into the sea,
315	and offered to Apollo perfect hecatombs
316	of bulls and goats along the shore of the barren sea;
317	and the savor went up to heaven, curling in the smoke.
318	So they were busy with these things throughout the army. But Agamemnon
319	did not cease from the strife in which he had first threatened Achilles;
320	instead he spoke to Talthybius and Eurybates,
321	who were his heralds and his ready attendants:
322	“Go to the hut of Achilles, son of Peleus;
323	take fair-cheeked Briseis by the hand and bring her here.
324	And if he will not give her, then I myself will take her,
325	coming with more men; and that will be the worse for him.”
326	So saying he sent them off, and laid a stern command on them.
327	Unwilling, the two went along the shore of the barren sea,
328	and came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons.
329	They found him beside his hut and his black ship,
330	sitting there; and Achilles was not glad to see them.
331	The two, in fear and awe of the king,
332	stood still, and said nothing to him, and asked him nothing;
333	but he understood in his heart, and spoke:
334	“Welcome, heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men;
335	come closer. You are not to blame in my eyes, but Agamemnon,
336	who sent you both here for the girl Briseis.
337	Come, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus, bring out the girl
338	and give her to them to lead away. But let these two themselves be witnesses
339	before the blessed gods and before mortal men
340	and before that harsh king, if ever again
341	there comes a need of me to ward off shameful ruin
342	from the others. For truly he rages with a ruinous mind,
343	and does not know how to look both before and after,
344	so that his Achaeans might fight in safety beside the ships.”
345	So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion.
346	He led fair-cheeked Briseis out of the hut
347	and gave her to them to lead away; and they went back along the ships of the Achaeans,
348	and the woman went with them, unwilling. But Achilles
349	burst into tears, and at once withdrew from his companions, and sat apart
350	on the shore of the gray sea, looking out over the boundless deep.
351	And stretching out his hands, he prayed long and hard to his dear mother:
352	“Mother, since you bore me to be short-lived,
353	surely the Olympian ought to have granted me honor at least,
354	Zeus who thunders on high; but now he has not honored me even a little.
355	For the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
356	has dishonored me: he has taken my prize and keeps it, having seized it himself.”
357	So he spoke, shedding tears, and his lady mother heard him
358	as she sat in the depths of the sea beside her aged father.
359	Swiftly she rose from the gray sea like a mist,
360	and sat down before him as he shed tears,
361	and stroked him with her hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
362	“My child, why are you weeping? What grief has come upon your heart?
363	Speak out; do not hide it in your mind, so that we both may know.”
364	Groaning heavily, swift-footed Achilles said to her:
365	“You know. Why should I tell all this to you, who know it already?
366	We went to Thebe, the sacred city of Eetion,
367	and sacked it, and brought everything here.
368	The sons of the Achaeans divided the spoils fairly among themselves,
369	and for the son of Atreus they chose out fair-cheeked Chryseis.
370	Then Chryses, priest of Apollo who shoots from afar,
371	came to the swift ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans
372	to win his daughter's freedom, bringing a boundless ransom,
373	holding in his hands the fillets of far-shooting Apollo
374	upon a golden scepter, and he begged all the Achaeans,
375	but most of all the two sons of Atreus, marshals of the people.
376	Then all the other Achaeans shouted their assent:
377	to respect the priest and accept the splendid ransom.
378	But this did not please the heart of Agamemnon, son of Atreus;
379	he sent the man away harshly, and laid a stern command on him.
380	The old man went back in anger; and Apollo
381	heard his prayer, for he was very dear to him,
382	and he let fly an evil arrow at the Argives. And the people
383	died one after another, as the arrows of the god ranged
384	everywhere through the wide army of the Achaeans. Then a seer
385	with sure knowledge declared to us the oracles of the Far-Shooter.
386	At once I was the first to urge that we appease the god;
387	but then anger seized the son of Atreus, and he stood up at once
388	and uttered a threat, which has now been carried out.
389	For the one, the bright-eyed Achaeans are sending in a swift ship
390	to Chryse, and they are bringing gifts to the lord;
391	but the other, just now, the heralds came and led away from my hut—
392	the daughter of Briseus, whom the sons of the Achaeans gave me.
393	But you, if you have the power, protect your own son.
394	Go to Olympus and entreat Zeus, if ever in the past
395	you have gladdened the heart of Zeus by word or deed.
396	For often I have heard you, in my father's halls,
397	boasting, how you said that from the son of Cronus, lord of the dark clouds,
398	you alone among the immortals warded off shameful ruin,
399	at the time when the other Olympians wished to put him in bonds—
400	Hera and Poseidon and Pallas Athena.
401	But you came, goddess, and freed him from his bonds,
402	quickly summoning the hundred-handed one to high Olympus,
403	whom the gods call Briareus, but all men
404	call Aegaeon—for he is mightier even than his father.
405	He sat down beside the son of Cronus, exulting in his glory,
406	and the blessed gods shrank in fear of him, and bound him no more.
407	Remind him of this now; sit beside him and clasp his knees,
408	in the hope that he may be willing to help the Trojans
409	and pen the Achaeans in among their ships' sterns and along the sea
410	as they are slaughtered, so that they may all have joy of their king,
411	and so that the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon, may learn
412	his own blind folly, in that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans.”
413	Then Thetis answered him, letting her tears fall:
414	“Ah me, my child, why did I raise you, when I bore you to such sorrow?
415	Would that beside the ships, free of tears and free of harm,
416	you might sit, since your portion of life is brief, not long at all;
417	but as it is, you are at once swift-fated and wretched beyond all men
418	—so to an evil fate I bore you in our halls.
419	Yet to tell this word of yours to Zeus who delights in thunder
420	I will go myself to snowy Olympus, in the hope that he will listen.
421	But you, for now, sit beside your swift-sailing ships
422	and keep up your wrath against the Achaeans, and hold back from war entirely.
423	For Zeus went to Oceanus, to the blameless Ethiopians,
424	yesterday, to a feast, and all the gods went with him;
425	but on the twelfth day he will come back again to Olympus,
426	and then I will go for you to the bronze-floored house of Zeus,
427	and I will clasp his knees, and I think I will persuade him.”
428	So she spoke and went away, and left him there
429	angry at heart for the sake of the fair-belted woman
430	whom they had taken by force against his will. Meanwhile Odysseus
431	came to Chryse, bringing the holy hecatomb.
432	When they had come within the deep harbor,
433	they furled the sails and stowed them in the black ship;
434	they lowered the mast by the forestays and brought it to the mast-crutch
435	quickly, and rowed her forward to the anchorage with oars.
436	They cast out the mooring-stones and made fast the stern-cables,
437	and they themselves stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore,
438	and they brought out the hecatomb for far-shooting Apollo,
439	and Chryseis stepped out of the seafaring ship.
440	Then Odysseus of many wiles led her to the altar
441	and placed her in the arms of her dear father, and said to him:
442	“Chryses, Agamemnon, lord of men, sent me here
443	to bring your child to you, and to offer a holy hecatomb to Phoebus
444	on behalf of the Danaans, so that we may appease the lord
445	who has now sent grievous sorrows upon the Argives.”
446	So saying he placed her in his arms, and he received with joy
447	his dear child. And quickly they set the holy hecatomb for the god
448	in order around the well-built altar;
449	then they washed their hands and took up the barley grains.
450	And Chryses prayed aloud for them, lifting up his hands:
451	“Hear me, god of the silver bow, who stand guard over Chryse
452	and holy Cilla, and rule Tenedos with might:
453	once before you heard me when I prayed,
454	and you honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans hard;
455	so now once more fulfill for me this wish:
456	now at last ward off this shameful ruin from the Danaans.”
457	So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
458	And when they had prayed and scattered the barley grains,
459	first they drew back the victims' heads, and cut their throats, and flayed them,
460	and cut out the thigh-bones and wrapped them in fat,
461	making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them.
462	The old man burned them on split wood, and over them poured
463	sparkling wine; and young men beside him held five-pronged forks in their hands.
464	But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails,
465	they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
466	and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off.
467	And when they had ceased from the work and made the feast ready,
468	they feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast.
469	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
470	the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink,
471	and served it out to all, first pouring drops for the libation into the cups.
472	And all day long they sought the god's favor with song and dance,
473	the young men of the Achaeans, singing a beautiful paean,
474	hymning the Far-Worker; and his heart was glad as he listened.
475	When the sun went down and darkness came on,
476	they lay down to sleep beside the stern-cables of the ship;
477	and when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
478	then they put out to sea, back to the wide army of the Achaeans,
479	and far-working Apollo sent them a following wind.
480	They set up the mast and spread the white sails,
481	and the wind blew full into the sail, and around the cutwater
482	the surging wave roared loud as the ship went on,
483	and she ran over the waves, accomplishing her way.
484	And when they came to the wide army of the Achaeans,
485	they drew the black ship up on the land,
486	high on the sands, and set long props beneath it;
487	and they themselves scattered among their huts and ships.
488	But he kept up his wrath, sitting beside his swift-sailing ships,
489	the son of Peleus, sprung from Zeus, swift-footed Achilles;
490	never going to the assembly, where men win glory,
491	nor ever to war; but he wasted away his own heart,
492	staying where he was, and he longed for the war cry and for battle.
493	But when the twelfth dawn after that had come,
494	then the gods who live forever went back to Olympus
495	all together, and Zeus led the way. And Thetis did not forget
496	the requests of her son, but rose up from the wave of the sea,
497	and early in the morning she went up to great heaven and Olympus.
498	She found the far-seeing son of Cronus sitting apart from the others
499	on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus.
500	She sat down before him, and clasped his knees
501	with her left hand, and with her right she took him beneath the chin,
502	and in supplication she spoke to lord Zeus, son of Cronus:
503	“Father Zeus, if ever among the immortals I helped you
504	by word or deed, fulfill for me this wish:
505	honor my son, who is swifter to his fate than any other
506	man; and yet now Agamemnon, lord of men,
507	has dishonored him: he has taken his prize and keeps it, having seized it himself.
508	But you, at least, honor him, Olympian Zeus the counselor;
509	give strength to the Trojans until the Achaeans
510	pay honor to my son and exalt him with honor.”
511	So she spoke; but Zeus the cloud-gatherer said nothing to her,
512	but sat a long time in silence. And Thetis, as she had clasped his knees,
513	so she held on, clinging close, and asked him a second time:
514	“Promise me truly, and nod your assent,
515	or refuse—since you have nothing to fear—so that I may know well
516	how far I am the most dishonored goddess of all.”
517	Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said to her:
518	“This is a sorry business, since you will set me at odds
519	with Hera, when she provokes me with abusive words.
520	Even as it is, among the immortal gods she is forever
521	quarreling with me, and says that I help the Trojans in battle.
522	But you go back again now, so that nothing may be noticed
523	by Hera; I will take care of these things, to bring them to fulfillment.
524	Come, I will nod my head to you, so that you may be certain;
525	for this, from me, among the immortals is the greatest
526	sign; for no word of mine can be taken back, or prove false,
527	or go unfulfilled, once I nod my head to it.”
528	He spoke, and the son of Cronus nodded with his dark brows,
529	and the ambrosial locks of the lord flowed down
530	from his immortal head; and he made great Olympus shake.
531	When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then
532	leapt down into the deep sea from gleaming Olympus,
533	and Zeus went to his own house. All the gods together rose
534	from their seats to meet their father; no one dared
535	to wait for him to approach, but all stood up to face him.
536	So he sat down there on his throne; but Hera,
537	when she saw him, was not unaware that plans had been laid with him
538	by silver-footed Thetis, daughter of the Old Man of the Sea.
539	At once she addressed Zeus, son of Cronus, with cutting words:
540	“Which of the gods, you schemer, has been laying plans with you this time?
541	It is always dear to you, when you are away from me,
542	to ponder secret things and decide them; never yet
543	have you willingly brought yourself to tell me a word of what you intend.”
544	Then the father of men and gods answered her:
545	“Hera, do not hope that all my counsels
546	will be known to you: they would be hard for you, though you are my wife.
547	Whatever it is fitting for you to hear, no one
548	of gods or men shall know it before you;
549	but whatever I wish to devise apart from the gods,
550	do not question me about each of these things, or pry.”
551	Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him:
552	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
553	Truly, in the past I have neither questioned you nor pried,
554	but quite undisturbed you devise whatever you please.
555	But now I am terribly afraid at heart that she has talked you round,
556	silver-footed Thetis, daughter of the Old Man of the Sea;
557	for early this morning she sat beside you and clasped your knees.
558	To her, I think, you truly nodded your assent that Achilles
559	you would honor, and destroy many beside the ships of the Achaeans.”
560	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
561	“Strange one, you are always suspecting, and I never escape you;
562	but you can achieve nothing even so, except to grow
563	more distant from my heart; and that will be the worse for you.
564	If this is so, then it must be my pleasure.
565	Sit down in silence, and obey my word,
566	or all the gods there are on Olympus will be no help to you
567	as I come closer, when I lay my irresistible hands on you.”
568	So he spoke, and ox-eyed queen Hera was afraid,
569	and she sat in silence, bending her heart to obedience;
570	and throughout the house of Zeus the heavenly gods were troubled.
571	Then Hephaestus, famed for his craft, began to speak among them,
572	doing a kindness to his dear mother, white-armed Hera:
573	“This will be a sorry business, and not to be endured any longer,
574	if you two quarrel like this for the sake of mortals
575	and set the gods brawling; there will be no pleasure
576	in the good feast, since worse things prevail.
577	I advise my mother, though she understands this herself,
578	to do a kindness to our dear father Zeus, so that he may not
579	scold her again, our father, and throw our feast into confusion.
580	For if the Olympian lord of the lightning should wish
581	to blast us from our seats—for he is by far the strongest.
582	No, approach him with gentle words;
583	then at once the Olympian will be gracious to us.”
584	So he spoke, and springing up he placed a two-handled cup
585	in his dear mother's hand, and said to her:
586	“Be patient, my mother, and endure it, grieved though you are,
587	or dear as you are, I may see you with my own eyes
588	struck down, and then, for all my grief, I will be powerless
589	to help you; for the Olympian is hard to stand against.
590	Once before, when I was eager to defend you,
591	he seized me by the foot and hurled me from the divine threshold,
592	and all day long I was carried down, and as the sun was setting
593	I fell on Lemnos, and there was little life left in me;
594	there the Sintian men quickly took care of me where I had fallen.”
595	So he spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera smiled,
596	and smiling she took the cup from her son's hand.
597	And he, going from left to right, poured for all the other gods,
598	drawing sweet nectar from the mixing bowl.
599	And unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods
600	as they saw Hephaestus bustling through the halls.
601	So all day long, until the sun went down,
602	they feasted, and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast,
603	nor of the beautiful lyre that Apollo held,
604	nor of the Muses, who sang, answering one another with lovely voices.
605	But when the bright light of the sun had set,
606	they went to lie down, each to his own house,
607	where for each of them the renowned god, lame in both legs,
608	Hephaestus, had built a house with his cunning skill;
609	and Zeus, the Olympian lord of the lightning, went to his bed,
610	where he always lay down when sweet sleep came upon him;
611	there he went up and slept, and beside him Hera of the golden throne.
