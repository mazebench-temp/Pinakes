# Homer, Iliad, Book 3 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now when they had been marshaled, each company with its leaders,
2	the Trojans came on with clamor and with cries, like birds,
3	as the clamor of cranes rises under the face of heaven
4	when they have fled the winter and its endless rain,
5	and they fly with clamor toward the streams of Oceanus,
6	bringing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men,
7	and at dawn they bring on their evil strife.
8	But the Achaeans came on in silence, breathing fury,
9	eager in their hearts to stand by one another.
10	As when the South Wind pours mist over the peaks of a mountain,
11	no friend to shepherds, but better than night for a thief,
12	and a man can see only as far as he can throw a stone,
13	so a whirling cloud of dust rose from under their feet
14	as they came; and very quickly they crossed the plain.
15	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
16	godlike Alexander stepped out as a champion for the Trojans,
17	with a leopard's skin on his shoulders and his curved bow
18	and a sword; and brandishing two spears tipped with bronze
19	he challenged all the best of the Argives
20	to fight him face to face in grim combat.
21	And when Menelaus, dear to Ares, caught sight of him
22	coming out in front of the throng with long strides,
23	as a lion rejoices when he comes upon a great carcass,
24	finding an antlered stag or a wild goat,
25	when he is hungry; for he devours it greedily, even though
26	the swift dogs and the young men in their prime rush at him;
27	so Menelaus rejoiced when he saw godlike Alexander
28	with his eyes; for he thought he would take vengeance on the wrongdoer.
29	And at once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor.
30	But when godlike Alexander caught sight of him
31	appearing among the champions, his heart was struck with fear,
32	and he drew back into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom.
33	As when a man sees a serpent and starts back
34	in the glades of a mountain, and trembling seizes his limbs,
35	and he backs away, and pallor takes his cheeks,
36	so back into the crowd of the proud Trojans he slipped,
37	godlike Alexander, in fear of the son of Atreus.
38	Seeing him, Hector rebuked him with shaming words:
39	“Evil Paris, best in looks, mad for women, seducer,
40	I wish you had never been born, or had died unmarried.
41	That is what I would want, and it would be far better
42	than for you to be a disgrace like this, and scorned by others.
43	Surely the long-haired Achaeans are cackling with laughter,
44	who thought our champion a prince because he has
45	handsome looks; but there is no strength in his heart, and no courage.
46	Was it as such a man that in your seafaring ships
47	you sailed over the sea, gathering your trusty companions,
48	and, mingling with foreigners, brought back a lovely woman
49	from a distant land, the bride of a people of spearmen,
50	a great grief to your father and your city and all the people,
51	a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself?
52	Would you not stand and face Menelaus, dear to Ares?
53	You would learn what kind of man he is whose flourishing wife you have.
54	Your lyre would be no help to you then, nor the gifts of Aphrodite,
55	your hair and your looks, when you lay mingled with the dust.
56	But the Trojans are great cowards; or else by now
57	you would have put on a tunic of stone for all the evil you have done.”
58	Then godlike Alexander spoke to him in turn:
59	“Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair—
60	your heart is always unyielding, like an axe
61	that goes through a beam in the hand of a man who with skill
62	shapes a ship's timber, and it adds to the man's force;
63	so fearless is the mind in your breast—
64	do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite.
65	The glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside,
66	whatever they themselves may give; no man could take them by his own will.
67	But now, if you want me to go to war and fight,
68	make the other Trojans and all the Achaeans sit down,
69	and set me in the middle with Menelaus, dear to Ares,
70	to fight for Helen and all her possessions;
71	and whichever of us wins and proves the stronger,
72	let him take all the possessions and the woman, and lead them home;
73	and let the rest of you, making friendship and swearing a trusty truce,
74	live in deep-soiled Troy, and let them go back
75	to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the lovely women.”
76	So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly when he heard his words,
77	and he went into the middle and held back the battalions of the Trojans,
78	gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down.
79	But the long-haired Achaeans kept shooting at him,
80	taking aim with arrows and pelting him with stones,
81	but Agamemnon, lord of men, shouted aloud:
82	“Hold, Argives! Do not shoot, young men of the Achaeans!
83	Hector of the gleaming helmet means to say something.”
84	So he spoke, and they held back from battle and fell silent
85	at once; and Hector spoke between the two armies:
86	“Hear me, Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans,
87	hear the word of Alexander, for whose sake the quarrel arose.
88	He bids the other Trojans and all the Achaeans
89	lay aside their fine armor on the bountiful earth,
90	and himself, in the middle, and Menelaus, dear to Ares,
91	to fight alone for Helen and all her possessions.
92	And whichever of them wins and proves the stronger,
93	let him take all the possessions and the woman, and lead them home;
94	and let the rest of us make friendship and swear a trusty truce.”
95	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence;
96	and among them spoke also Menelaus, good at the war cry:
97	“Hear me now as well; for sorrow comes most of all
98	to my heart, and I think that they should now be parted,
99	the Argives and the Trojans, since you have suffered many evils
100	for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it.
101	And whichever of us two death and fate are prepared for,
102	let him die; and let the rest of you be parted as soon as may be.
103	Bring two lambs, a white ram and a black ewe,
104	for Earth and the Sun; and we will bring another for Zeus.
105	And bring the mighty Priam, so that he may swear the oaths
106	himself, for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted,
107	so that no one may violate the oaths of Zeus by transgression.
108	The minds of younger men are always flighty;
109	but in whatever an old man takes part, he looks both before and after,
110	so that things turn out far the best for both sides.”
111	So he spoke, and the Achaeans and the Trojans rejoiced,
112	hoping for an end to miserable war.
113	They held their horses in the ranks, and got down themselves,
114	and stripped off their armor and laid it on the ground
115	close to one another, and there was little ground between them.
116	And Hector sent two heralds to the city
117	quickly, to bring the lambs and to summon Priam;
118	and powerful Agamemnon sent Talthybius
119	to go to the hollow ships, and told him to bring
120	a lamb; and he did not disobey brilliant Agamemnon.
121	And Iris came as a messenger to white-armed Helen
122	in the likeness of her sister-in-law, the wife of Antenor's son,
123	whom the son of Antenor, powerful Helicaon, had as his wife,
124	Laodice, most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam.
125	She found her in the hall. She was weaving a great web,
126	a double-folded purple cloth, and working into it the many trials
127	of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans
128	that they were suffering for her sake at the hands of Ares.
129	And standing near, swift-footed Iris spoke to her:
130	“Come here, dear bride, and see the wondrous doings
131	of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans.
132	Before, they were bringing tearful Ares on one another
133	on the plain, longing for deadly war;
134	now they are sitting in silence, and the war has stopped,
135	leaning on their shields, and their long spears are fixed beside them.
136	But Alexander and Menelaus, dear to Ares,
137	will fight with their long spears for you,
138	and you will be called the dear wife of the one who wins.”
139	So saying, the goddess put sweet longing in her heart
140	for her former husband, and her city, and her parents;
141	and at once she veiled herself in white linen
142	and hurried from her chamber, letting her tender tears fall,
143	not alone; two handmaids went along with her,
144	Aethra, daughter of Pittheus, and ox-eyed Clymene;
145	and quickly they came to where the Scaean gates were.
146	And those around Priam, Panthous and Thymoetes,
147	Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, offshoot of Ares,
148	Ucalegon and Antenor, both wise men,
149	were sitting, elders of the people, at the Scaean gates,
150	done with war now because of their age, but excellent
151	speakers, like the cicadas that in the woods
152	sit on a tree and send out their lily-soft voice;
153	so the leaders of the Trojans were sitting on the tower.
154	And when they saw Helen coming to the tower,
155	softly they spoke winged words to one another:
156	“There is no blame that Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans
157	should suffer sorrows for so long for such a woman;
158	she is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on.
159	But even so, beautiful as she is, let her go home in the ships,
160	and not be left here to be a grief to us and to our children after us.”
161	So they spoke, and Priam called Helen to him:
162	“Come here, dear child, and sit in front of me,
163	so that you may see your former husband, and your kin by marriage and your friends—
164	you are not to blame for anything in my eyes; it is the gods who are to blame,
165	who roused against me the tearful war of the Achaeans—
166	and so that you may name this huge man for me,
167	who this Achaean man is, so tall and brave.
168	There are others, it is true, taller than he by a head,
169	but so handsome a man I have never yet seen with my eyes,
170	nor one so stately; for he looks like a king.”
171	And Helen, brilliant among women, answered him:
172	“You are one I respect, dear father-in-law, and one I fear.
173	I wish that evil death had pleased me when I came here
174	with your son, leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen,
175	and my darling daughter, and the lovely companions of my youth.
176	But that did not happen; and so I waste away in tears.
177	But I will tell you what you ask and question me about:
178	this is the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
179	both a good king and a strong spearman.
180	And he was my brother-in-law, dog-faced as I am, if that was ever so.”
181	So she spoke, and the old man marveled at him and said:
182	“O blessed son of Atreus, child of fortune, favored by heaven,
183	truly many young men of the Achaeans have been made subject to you.
184	Once before I went to vine-rich Phrygia,
185	and there I saw the Phrygian men in great numbers, with their quick horses,
186	the people of Otreus and godlike Mygdon,
187	who were then encamped along the banks of the Sangarius;
188	for I too, as an ally, was counted among them
189	on the day when the Amazons came, the equals of men;
190	but even they were not as many as the bright-eyed Achaeans.”
191	Second, the old man saw Odysseus, and asked:
192	“Come, tell me about this one too, dear child, who he is:
193	he is shorter by a head than Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
194	but broader in the shoulders and chest to look at.
195	His armor lies on the bountiful earth,
196	but he himself ranges like a ram through the ranks of men;
197	I liken him to a thick-fleeced ram
198	that goes through a great flock of white sheep.”
199	Then Helen, child of Zeus, answered him:
200	“This is the son of Laertes, Odysseus of many wiles,
201	who was raised in the land of Ithaca, craggy though it is,
202	and knows all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels.”
203	And wise Antenor answered her face to face:
204	“Lady, truly this word you have spoken is exact.
205	For once before brilliant Odysseus came here
206	on an embassy about you, with Menelaus, dear to Ares;
207	and I received them as guests and welcomed them in my halls,
208	and came to know the build of both of them and their shrewd counsels.
209	But when they mingled with the Trojans gathered in assembly,
210	while they stood, Menelaus rose above him by his broad shoulders,
211	but when both were seated, Odysseus was the more stately.
212	And when they began to weave their words and counsels before all,
213	Menelaus spoke fluently,
214	few words but very clear, since he was not a man of many words
215	nor one who missed the point; and indeed he was the younger.
216	But when Odysseus of many wiles sprang up,
217	he would stand and look down, fixing his eyes on the ground,
218	and he would not move the scepter backward or forward,
219	but held it stiff, like a man who knows nothing;
220	you would have said he was some surly fellow, and a fool as well.
221	But when he let the great voice go from his chest,
222	and words like the snowflakes of winter,
223	then no other mortal could rival Odysseus;
224	then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks.”
225	Third, the old man saw Ajax, and asked:
226	“Who is this other Achaean man, so tall and brave,
227	towering above the Argives by his head and broad shoulders?”
228	And Helen of the long robes, brilliant among women, answered him:
229	“This is huge Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans.
230	And Idomeneus on the other side stands among the Cretans
231	like a god, and around him the leaders of the Cretans are gathered.
232	Often Menelaus, dear to Ares, entertained him
233	in our house, whenever he came from Crete.
234	And now I see all the other bright-eyed Achaeans,
235	whom I would know well and could tell you their names;
236	but two marshals of the people I cannot see,
237	Castor, tamer of horses, and Polydeuces, good with his fists,
238	my own brothers, whom one mother bore with me.
239	Either they did not come with the rest from lovely Lacedaemon,
240	or they came here in their seafaring ships
241	but now are unwilling to enter the battle of men
242	for fear of the shame and the many reproaches that are mine.”
243	So she spoke; but the life-giving earth already held them fast
244	there in Lacedaemon, in their own dear native land.
245	Meanwhile the heralds were carrying through the city the offerings for the trusty oaths of the gods,
246	two lambs, and wine that cheers the heart, the fruit of the earth,
247	in a goatskin; and a shining mixing bowl was carried
248	by the herald Idaeus, and golden cups;
249	and standing beside the old man he urged him with these words:
250	“Rise, son of Laomedon; the best men call you,
251	of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans,
252	to come down to the plain, so that you may swear a trusty truce.
253	But Alexander and Menelaus, dear to Ares,
254	will fight with their long spears for the woman,
255	and to the one who wins, the woman and the possessions would go;
256	and the rest of us, making friendship and swearing a trusty truce,
257	would live in deep-soiled Troy, and they would go back
258	to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the lovely women.”
259	So he spoke, and the old man shuddered, and he told his companions
260	to yoke the horses; and they quickly obeyed.
261	Priam mounted and pulled back the reins,
262	and beside him Antenor stepped into the beautiful chariot;
263	and through the Scaean gates they drove the swift horses to the plain.
264	But when they came among the Trojans and the Achaeans,
265	they stepped down from the chariot onto the bountiful earth
266	and walked into the middle between the Trojans and the Achaeans.
267	At once Agamemnon, lord of men, rose,
268	and Odysseus of many wiles; and the noble heralds
269	brought together the offerings for the trusty oaths of the gods, and mixed wine
270	in the mixing bowl, and poured water over the hands of the kings.
271	And the son of Atreus drew with his hand the knife
272	that always hung beside the great sheath of his sword,
273	and cut hair from the heads of the lambs; and then
274	the heralds gave it out to the best of the Trojans and Achaeans.
275	And the son of Atreus prayed aloud among them, lifting up his hands:
276	“Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest,
277	and you, Sun, who see all things and hear all things,
278	and rivers and earth, and you two who below the earth punish
279	dead men, whoever has sworn a false oath,
280	be witnesses, and keep watch over the trusty oaths.
281	If Alexander kills Menelaus,
282	then let him keep Helen and all the possessions,
283	and let us go home in our seafaring ships;
284	but if fair-haired Menelaus kills Alexander,
285	then let the Trojans give back Helen and all the possessions,
286	and pay the Argives such satisfaction as is fitting,
287	one that will be remembered among men who are yet to come.
288	But if Priam and the sons of Priam refuse to pay me satisfaction
289	when Alexander has fallen,
290	then I will fight on for the sake of the penalty,
291	staying here, until I reach the end of the war.”
292	He spoke, and cut the lambs' throats with the pitiless bronze,
293	and laid them on the ground, gasping,
294	their life failing, for the bronze had taken away their strength.
295	And drawing wine from the mixing bowl in cups
296	they poured it out, and prayed to the everlasting gods;
297	and this is what a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike:
298	“Zeus, most glorious, greatest, and you other immortal gods,
299	whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths,
300	may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine,
301	theirs and their children's, and may their wives be subdued by others.”
302	So they spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer.
303	And Priam, descendant of Dardanus, spoke among them:
304	“Hear me, Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans:
305	I will go back to windy Ilios,
306	since I could never bear to see with my own eyes
307	my dear son fighting with Menelaus, dear to Ares.
308	Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods,
309	for which of the two the end of death is fated.”
310	He spoke, and the man equal to the gods put the lambs in the chariot,
311	and mounted himself, and pulled back the reins;
312	and beside him Antenor stepped into the beautiful chariot.
313	And the two of them went back to Ilios.
314	But Hector, son of Priam, and brilliant Odysseus
315	first measured out the ground, and then
316	took the lots and shook them in a bronze-fitted helmet,
317	to see which of the two should first let fly his bronze spear.
318	And the people prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods,
319	and this is what a man would say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike:
320	“Father Zeus, who rule from Ida, most glorious, greatest,
321	whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides,
322	grant that he may die and go down into the house of Hades,
323	and that friendship and trusty oaths may be ours.”
324	So they spoke, and great Hector of the gleaming helmet shook the lots,
325	looking away; and the lot of Paris leapt out at once.
326	Then they sat down in their ranks, where each man's
327	high-stepping horses stood and inlaid armor lay;
328	and he put on his fine armor around his shoulders,
329	brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair.
330	First he put the greaves around his shins,
331	fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
332	second, he put on the corselet around his chest,
333	the corselet of his brother Lycaon, and fitted it to himself.
334	And around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword
335	of bronze, and then his shield, great and heavy;
336	and on his mighty head he set a well-made helmet
337	with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it;
338	and he took up a strong spear that fitted his grip.
339	And in the same way warlike Menelaus put on his armor.
340	And when they had armed on either side of the throng,
341	they walked into the middle between the Trojans and the Achaeans,
342	glaring terribly; and amazement held those who watched,
343	the horse-taming Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans.
344	They stood close to one another on the measured ground,
345	brandishing their spears, in bitter resentment of each other.
346	Alexander was first to let fly his long-shadowed spear,
347	and struck the son of Atreus on his shield, balanced on every side,
348	but the bronze did not break through, and its point was bent back
349	in the strong shield. And then he rushed second with the bronze,
350	Menelaus, son of Atreus, praying to father Zeus:
351	“Lord Zeus, grant me vengeance on the man who first wronged me,
352	brilliant Alexander, and subdue him beneath my hands,
353	so that any man of those born hereafter may shudder
354	to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship.”
355	He spoke, and poising his long-shadowed spear, he let it fly,
356	and struck the son of Priam on his shield, balanced on every side;
357	and through the shining shield went the heavy spear,
358	and it forced its way through the intricately worked corselet,
359	and straight on beside the flank it cut through the tunic,
360	the spear; but he swerved aside and avoided black doom.
361	Then the son of Atreus drew his silver-studded sword,
362	raised it and struck the ridge of his helmet; but on it
363	the sword shattered in three and four pieces and fell from his hand.
364	And the son of Atreus groaned aloud, looking up into the wide sky:
365	“Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you.
366	Truly I thought I would pay back Alexander for his wickedness;
367	and now my sword has broken in my hands, and my spear
368	flew from my grip in vain, and I did not hit him.”
369	He spoke, and leaping on him, he seized him by the horsehair-crested helmet,
370	and turning, dragged him toward the well-greaved Achaeans;
371	and the richly worked strap choked him under his soft throat,
372	the strap drawn tight under his chin to hold the helmet.
373	And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory,
374	had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it,
375	who broke for him the strap, made from the hide of an ox slain by force;
376	and the empty helmet came away in his strong hand.
377	This the hero then flung among the well-greaved Achaeans,
378	whirling it around, and his trusty companions picked it up;
379	but he sprang at him again, eager to kill him
380	with his bronze spear. But Aphrodite snatched him away
381	very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist,
382	and set him down in his fragrant, perfumed chamber.
383	And she herself went to call Helen; and she found her
384	on the high tower, and around her were Trojan women in plenty.
385	She took hold of her fragrant robe and plucked at it with her hand,
386	and spoke to her in the likeness of an aged woman,
387	a wool-comber, who, when she lived in Lacedaemon,
388	used to work fine wool for her, and loved her most of all;
389	in her likeness brilliant Aphrodite spoke to her:
390	“Come here: Alexander calls you to come home.
391	There he is in his chamber, on the inlaid bed,
392	shining with beauty and fine clothes; you would not say
393	that he had come from fighting a man, but that he was going to the dance,
394	or had just stopped dancing and sat down.”
395	So she spoke, and stirred the heart in her breast;
396	and when she noticed the goddess's beautiful neck
397	and her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes,
398	she was amazed, and she spoke to her and called her by name:
399	“Strange one, why do you long to deceive me like this?
400	Will you lead me still farther on, to one of the well-peopled cities
401	of Phrygia or of lovely Maeonia,
402	if there too there is some mortal man who is dear to you?
403	Is it because Menelaus has now beaten brilliant Alexander
404	and wants to take me, hateful as I am, home with him,
405	that you have come and stood here now, with treachery in your heart?
406	Go and sit beside him yourself, and give up the paths of the gods,
407	and never again turn your feet back to Olympus,
408	but always suffer for him and watch over him,
409	until he makes you his wife, or his slave.
410	I will not go there—it would be a thing for blame—
411	to share his bed; the Trojan women would all hereafter
412	blame me; and I have griefs past measure in my heart.”
413	And brilliant Aphrodite spoke to her in anger:
414	“Do not provoke me, wretched woman, or in my anger I may let you go,
415	and hate you as much as now I love you beyond measure,
416	and devise grim hatreds in the midst of both sides,
417	Trojans and Danaans, and you would perish by an evil fate.”
418	So she spoke, and Helen, child of Zeus, was afraid,
419	and she went, wrapping herself in her shining white robe,
420	in silence, and escaped the notice of all the Trojan women; and the divinity led the way.
421	And when they came to the beautiful house of Alexander,
422	the handmaids turned quickly to their tasks,
423	and she, brilliant among women, went into the high-roofed chamber.
424	And laughter-loving Aphrodite took a chair for her,
425	and the goddess carried it and set it down facing Alexander;
426	there sat Helen, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
427	turning her eyes away, and she rebuked her husband:
428	“You have come back from the war. I wish you had died there,
429	beaten by a stronger man, who was my former husband.
430	Truly, before now you boasted that you were better than Menelaus, dear to Ares,
431	in your strength and your hands and your spear.
432	Go now, and challenge Menelaus, dear to Ares,
433	to fight again face to face. But I, for my part,
434	tell you to stop, and not against fair-haired Menelaus
435	to make war face to face and fight
436	foolishly, or you may soon be brought down by his spear.”
437	And answering her with these words, Paris said:
438	“Lady, do not rebuke my heart with harsh reproaches.
439	This time Menelaus has won, with Athena's help;
440	another time I will beat him; for we have gods on our side too.
441	But come, let us go to bed and take our pleasure in love;
442	for never yet has desire so wrapped my mind,
443	not even when first, from lovely Lacedaemon,
444	I snatched you away and sailed off in the seafaring ships,
445	and on the island of Cranae lay with you in love and bed,
446	as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me.”
447	He spoke, and led the way to the bed, and his wife followed with him.
448	So the two of them lay down together on the corded bed;
449	but the son of Atreus ranged through the throng like a wild beast,
450	to see if he could catch sight of godlike Alexander.
451	But none of the Trojans or their famous allies could
452	show Alexander then to Menelaus, dear to Ares;
453	for they would not have hidden him out of love, if anyone had seen him;
