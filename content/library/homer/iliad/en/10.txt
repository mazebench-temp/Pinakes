# Homer, Iliad, Book 10 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now beside the ships the others, the best of all the Achaeans,
2	slept all night long, overcome by soft sleep;
3	but the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
4	was not held by sweet sleep, as he pondered many things in his mind.
5	As when the husband of Hera of the lovely hair flashes his lightning,
6	making ready a heavy, endless rain, or hail,
7	or snow, when the snowflakes sprinkle the fields,
8	or somewhere the great mouth of bitter war,
9	so thick and fast did Agamemnon groan in his breast,
10	from the depths of his heart, and his mind trembled within him.
11	Whenever he looked out over the Trojan plain,
12	he marveled at the many fires that burned before Ilios,
13	at the sound of flutes and pipes and the din of men;
14	but whenever he looked at the ships and the army of the Achaeans,
15	he tore out many hairs from his head by the roots
16	to Zeus on high, and his glorious heart groaned greatly.
17	And this plan seemed to him in his heart the best:
18	to go first of all men to Nestor, son of Neleus,
19	in the hope that he might devise with him some flawless plan
20	that would ward off evil from all the Danaans.
21	He stood up and put on his tunic about his chest,
22	and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals,
23	and then he put around him the blood-red skin of a lion,
24	a great tawny lion, reaching to his feet, and he took up his spear.
25	And trembling held Menelaus likewise; for neither on his eyelids
26	would sleep settle, for fear that they might suffer harm,
27	the Argives, who for his sake had come over the wide waters
28	to Troy, bent on bold war.
29	First he covered his broad back with a leopard's skin,
30	dappled, and lifting a helmet of bronze
31	he set it on his head, and took up a spear in his strong hand.
32	He went to rouse his brother, who ruled mightily over all
33	the Argives, and was honored by the people like a god.
34	He found him putting his fine armor about his shoulders
35	beside the stern of his ship; and his coming was welcome to him.
36	Menelaus, good at the war cry, spoke to him first:
37	“Why are you arming like this, dear brother? Will you urge one of the companions
38	to go and spy on the Trojans? But I am terribly afraid
39	that no one will undertake this task for you,
40	to go out alone and spy on the enemy
41	through the ambrosial night; he would have to be very bold of heart.”
42	Answering him, powerful Agamemnon said:
43	“You and I have need, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, of counsel,
44	shrewd counsel, that will protect and save
45	the Argives and the ships, since the mind of Zeus has turned.
46	He has set his heart more on the sacrifices of Hector.
47	For I never yet saw, nor heard anyone tell,
48	that one man devised so many grim deeds in a single day
49	as Hector, dear to Zeus, has done to the sons of the Achaeans,
50	just as he is, the dear son of no goddess and of no god.
51	He has done deeds that I say will weigh on the Argives
52	long and for many a day, such evils has he devised for the Achaeans.
53	But go now and call Ajax and Idomeneus,
54	running quickly along the ships; and I will go to brilliant Nestor
55	and urge him to get up, if he is willing
56	to go to the sacred company of the sentries and give them orders.
57	They would heed him above all, for his son
58	commands the sentries, and so does Idomeneus' squire
59	Meriones; to them above all we entrusted it.”
60	Then Menelaus, good at the war cry, answered him:
61	“How then do you charge me with your word, and what is your command?
62	Shall I stay there with them, waiting until you come,
63	or run back after you, when I have given them their orders well?”
64	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to him in turn:
65	“Stay there, so that we do not somehow miss one another
66	as we go; for there are many paths through the camp.
67	Call out wherever you go, and bid them stay awake,
68	naming each man by his father and his lineage,
69	and giving glory to them all; and do not be proud in your heart,
70	but let us toil ourselves as well; so, it seems,
71	at our birth Zeus laid heavy misery on us.”
72	So saying, he sent his brother off, having given him good instructions;
73	and he himself went on his way to Nestor, shepherd of the people;
74	and he found him beside his hut and his black ship,
75	in a soft bed; and beside him lay his inlaid armor,
76	a shield and two spears and a shining helmet.
77	And beside them lay the gleaming belt with which the old man
78	girded himself whenever he armed for man-destroying war,
79	leading his people, for he did not give way to grievous old age.
80	He raised himself on his elbow, lifting his head,
81	and spoke to the son of Atreus, and questioned him:
82	“Who is this, coming alone along the ships through the camp
83	through the dark night, when other mortals are asleep?
84	Are you looking for one of your mules, or one of your companions?
85	Speak, and do not come at me in silence. What need brings you?”
86	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, answered him:
87	“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
88	you will know Agamemnon, son of Atreus, whom above all men
89	Zeus has plunged into toils without end, as long as breath
90	stays in my breast and my knees have strength to move.
91	I wander like this because sweet sleep does not settle on my eyes,
92	but war and the sorrows of the Achaeans weigh on me.
93	For I am terribly afraid for the Danaans, and my heart
94	is not steady, but I am distraught, and my heart leaps out
95	of my breast, and my shining limbs tremble beneath me.
96	But if you would do something, since sleep does not come to you either,
97	come, let us go down to the sentries, so that we may see
98	whether, worn out by weariness and by sleep,
99	they have lain down to rest and have quite forgotten their watch.
100	Hostile men are camped close by, and we do not know at all
101	whether they may be eager to fight even by night.”
102	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
103	“Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
104	surely Zeus the counselor will not fulfill for Hector all the plans
105	that he now hopes for, I suppose; but I think he will labor
106	under sorrows still greater, if Achilles
107	turns his dear heart from its grievous anger.
108	I will gladly follow you; and let us wake the others too,
109	the son of Tydeus, famed for his spear, and Odysseus,
110	and swift Ajax, and the valiant son of Phyleus.
111	And I wish someone would go and call these too,
112	godlike Ajax and lord Idomeneus;
113	for their ships are farthest off, and not close at all.
114	But Menelaus, dear and respected though he is,
115	I will rebuke, even if you blame me for it, and I will not hide it,
116	because he is sleeping, and has left the toil to you alone.
117	Now he should be toiling among all the chiefs,
118	beseeching them; for a need has come that is no longer bearable.”
119	Then Agamemnon, lord of men, spoke to him in turn:
120	“Old man, at other times I would even bid you blame him;
121	for often he hangs back and is unwilling to toil,
122	not giving way to sloth or to thoughtlessness of mind,
123	but looking to me and waiting for me to lead.
124	But now he woke far earlier than I and came to me,
125	and I sent him on ahead to call the men you ask for.
126	But let us go; we shall find them before the gates
127	among the sentries, for there I told them to gather.”
128	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
129	“In that case no one will blame him or disobey him
130	among the Argives, whenever he urges anyone on and gives him an order.”
131	So saying, he put on his tunic about his chest,
132	and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals,
133	and fastened around him with a brooch a crimson cloak,
134	double and wide, with a thick woolly nap upon it.
135	And he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
136	and went his way along the ships of the bronze-armored Achaeans.
137	Then first Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
138	the Gerenian horseman Nestor roused from sleep,
139	calling out; and at once the cry came around his mind,
140	and he came out of his hut and spoke a word to them:
141	“Why do you wander like this alone along the ships through the camp
142	through the ambrosial night? What need so great has come?”
143	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
144	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
145	do not be indignant; such grief has overpowered the Achaeans.
146	But follow, so that we may wake another too, one whom it befits
147	to take counsel, whether to flee or to fight.”
148	So he spoke, and Odysseus of many wiles went into his hut,
149	put his inlaid shield about his shoulders, and went after them.
150	They went to Diomedes, son of Tydeus, and found him
151	outside his hut with his armor; and around him his companions
152	were sleeping, with their shields under their heads; and their spears
153	were driven upright on their butt-spikes, and far off the bronze
154	shone like the lightning of father Zeus; but the hero himself
155	slept, and under him was spread the hide of an ox of the fields,
156	and under his head a shining rug was stretched.
157	The Gerenian horseman Nestor stood beside him and woke him,
158	stirring him with his heel, and urged him on and rebuked him to his face:
159	“Wake up, son of Tydeus! Why do you sleep the whole night through?
160	Do you not hear how the Trojans are camped on the rise of the plain
161	near the ships, and only a little space still holds them off?”
162	So he spoke, and he sprang up out of sleep very quickly,
163	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
164	“You are a hard man, old sir; you never cease from toil.
165	Are there not other, younger sons of the Achaeans
166	who could then wake each one of the kings,
167	going around everywhere? But there is no dealing with you, old sir.”
168	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor spoke to him in turn:
169	“Yes, my friend, all that you have said is fair and right.
170	I have noble sons, and I have people too,
171	and many of them, one of whom might go round and call them;
172	but a very great need has overpowered the Achaeans.
173	For now it stands for all of us on the edge of a razor,
174	whether grim destruction comes on the Achaeans, or life.
175	But go now, and rouse swift Ajax and the son of Phyleus;
176	for you are younger, if you have pity on me.”
177	So he spoke, and he put around his shoulders the skin of a lion,
178	a great tawny lion, reaching to his feet, and he took up his spear.
179	The hero went his way, and roused them from there and brought them.
180	And when they came among the sentries where they were gathered,
181	they did not find the leaders of the sentries asleep,
182	but they were all sitting awake with their arms.
183	As dogs keep a troubled watch around the sheep in a fold
184	when they hear a fierce-hearted beast that comes through the woods
185	among the mountains, and a great din rises against it
186	of men and dogs, and sleep is lost to them;
187	so sweet sleep was lost from their eyelids
188	as they kept watch through the evil night; for they were always turned
189	toward the plain, to hear if the Trojans were coming on.
190	And the old man was glad when he saw them, and cheered them with his words,
191	and he spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
192	“Keep watch like this now, dear children, and let no one be taken
193	by sleep, so that we do not become a joy to our enemies.”
194	So saying, he hurried across the ditch, and with him followed
195	the kings of the Argives, all who had been called to the council.
196	And with them went Meriones and the splendid son of Nestor,
197	for the kings themselves had called them to take counsel with them.
198	And crossing over the ditch they had dug, they sat down
199	in a clear space, where the ground showed free of corpses
200	of the fallen; from there mighty Hector had turned back again
201	from killing the Argives, when night covered all around.
202	There they sat down and spoke their words to one another;
203	and among them the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak:
204	“Friends, is there no man who would trust his own
205	daring heart, and go among the great-hearted Trojans,
206	in the hope that he might catch some straggler of the enemy,
207	or perhaps hear some talk among the Trojans,
208	what they are planning among themselves, whether they mean
209	to stay there by the ships, far from home, or to the city
210	go back again, now that they have beaten the Achaeans?
211	All this he might learn, and come back to us
212	unharmed; great would be his fame under heaven
213	among all men, and he will have a fine gift:
214	for of all the best men who hold command over the ships,
215	each one will give him a black ewe
216	with a lamb at her udder; no possession is like it,
217	and he will always have a place at feasts and banquets.”
218	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence.
219	And among them spoke also Diomedes, good at the war cry:
220	“Nestor, my heart and my proud spirit urge me
221	to enter the camp of the hostile men close by,
222	the Trojans; but if some other man would go with me,
223	there would be more comfort, and more boldness.
224	When two go together, one sees before the other
225	how the advantage lies; but one man alone, even if he sees it,
226	his mind is slower, and his counsel thin.”
227	So he spoke, and many were willing to follow Diomedes.
228	The two Ajaxes were willing, attendants of Ares,
229	Meriones was willing, and very willing the son of Nestor,
230	and the son of Atreus was willing, Menelaus famed for his spear,
231	and enduring Odysseus was willing to slip into the throng
232	of the Trojans; for the heart in his breast was always daring.
233	And among them spoke also Agamemnon, lord of men:
234	“Diomedes, son of Tydeus, dear to my heart,
235	you shall choose the companion you wish,
236	the best of those who offer, since many are eager.
237	And do not, out of respect in your heart, leave the better man
238	behind and take a worse one with you, giving way to respect,
239	looking to his lineage, not even if he is more kingly.”
240	So he spoke, and he feared for fair-haired Menelaus.
241	And again Diomedes, good at the war cry, spoke among them:
242	“If you bid me choose a companion myself,
243	how then could I forget divine Odysseus,
244	whose heart and proud spirit are eager above all others
245	in every toil, and Pallas Athena loves him?
246	If he goes with me, even out of blazing fire
247	we would both come home, since he is wise beyond all others.”
248	Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus spoke to him in turn:
249	“Son of Tydeus, do not praise me too much, nor rebuke me at all;
250	for you are saying this among Argives who know it.
251	But let us go; for the night is far spent, and dawn is near;
252	the stars have moved on, and the greater part of the night has gone,
253	two portions of it, and a third portion still remains.”
254	So saying, the two put on their dread armor.
255	Thrasymedes, steadfast in battle, gave the son of Tydeus
256	a two-edged sword (his own had been left behind at his ship)
257	and a shield; and he put a helmet on his head,
258	of bull's hide, without ridge or crest, the kind called a skullcap,
259	and it guards the heads of young men in their prime.
260	And Meriones gave Odysseus a bow and a quiver
261	and a sword, and he put a helmet on his head,
262	made of leather; inside it was stretched taut with many thongs,
263	firmly, and on the outside the white teeth
264	of a white-tusked boar were set close, this way and that,
265	well and skillfully, and in the middle a lining of felt was fitted.
266	It was taken once from Eleon, from Amyntor, son of Ormenus,
267	by Autolycus, who broke into his well-built house,
268	and at Scandeia he gave it to Amphidamas of Cythera;
269	and Amphidamas gave it to Molus to be a guest-gift,
270	and Molus gave it to his son Meriones to wear;
271	and now it covered the head of Odysseus, set close around it.
272	And when the two had put on their dread armor,
273	they set out, and left all the best there behind them.
274	And a heron on the right, close to their path, was sent them
275	by Pallas Athena; they did not see it with their eyes
276	through the dark night, but they heard it cry.
277	And Odysseus rejoiced at the bird, and prayed to Athena:
278	“Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, you who always
279	stand by me in all my toils, and whatever I do
280	you see it; now again love me above all, Athena,
281	and grant that we come back to the ships with glory,
282	having done a great deed that the Trojans will rue.”
283	Then Diomedes, good at the war cry, prayed second:
284	“Now hear me too, child of Zeus, Atrytone:
285	go with me, as when you went with my father, brilliant Tydeus,
286	to Thebes, when he went ahead of the Achaeans as a messenger.
287	He left the bronze-armored Achaeans by the Asopus,
288	and himself carried a soothing word to the Cadmeians
289	there; but on his way back he devised very grim deeds
290	with you, brilliant goddess, when you stood by him with a ready heart.
291	So now be willing to stand by me, and guard me.
292	And I in turn will sacrifice to you a yearling heifer, broad of brow,
293	unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke;
294	I will sacrifice her to you, with gold poured around her horns.”
295	So they spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard them.
296	And when they had prayed to the daughter of great Zeus,
297	they went like two lions through the black night,
298	through the slaughter, through the corpses, through the armor and the black blood.
299	Nor did Hector let the proud Trojans
300	sleep, but he called together all the best,
301	all who were leaders and rulers of the Trojans;
302	when he had called them together, he laid out a shrewd plan:
303	“Who would undertake this task for me and accomplish it
304	for a great gift? His reward will be sure.
305	For I will give him a chariot and two horses with high-arched necks,
306	the best there are at the swift ships of the Achaeans,
307	to whoever dares, and wins glory for himself,
308	to go close to the swift-sailing ships and find out
309	whether the swift ships are guarded as before,
310	or whether, beaten down already by our hands,
311	they are planning flight among themselves, and are unwilling
312	to keep watch through the night, worn out by dreadful weariness.”
313	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence.
314	Now there was among the Trojans one Dolon, son of Eumedes
315	the divine herald, a man rich in gold, rich in bronze;
316	he was ill-favored to look at, but swift-footed,
317	and he was the only son among five sisters.
318	He then spoke a word to the Trojans and to Hector:
319	“Hector, my heart and my proud spirit urge me
320	to go close to the swift-sailing ships and find out.
321	But come, hold up your scepter for me, and swear to me
322	that you will give me the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze
323	that carry the noble son of Peleus,
324	and I will not be a spy for you in vain, nor disappoint your hopes.
325	For I will go right through the camp until I come
326	to Agamemnon's ship, where I suppose the chiefs
327	are taking counsel, whether to flee or to fight.”
328	So he spoke, and the other took the scepter in his hands and swore to him:
329	“Now let Zeus himself be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera,
330	that no other man shall ride behind those horses,
331	no other Trojan, but I say that you shall glory in them forever.”
332	So he spoke, and swore an oath that would prove false, and spurred him on.
333	At once he put his curved bow about his shoulders,
334	and put on over it the skin of a gray wolf,
335	and on his head a cap of ferret skin, and he took a sharp javelin,
336	and went from the army toward the ships; but he was not to
337	come back from the ships and bring word to Hector.
338	But when he had left behind the throng of horses and men,
339	he went eagerly along the road; and as he came on, he was noticed
340	by Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, who said to Diomedes:
341	“Diomedes, here is some man coming from the army;
342	I do not know whether he comes to spy on our ships,
343	or to strip one of the corpses of the dead.
344	But first let us allow him to get past us across the plain
345	a little way; then we could rush on him and catch him
346	quickly. And if he outruns us on his feet,
347	keep pressing him always toward the ships, away from the army,
348	darting at him with your spear, so that he does not get away toward the city.”
349	So the two spoke, and off the road, among the dead,
350	they lay down; and he ran quickly past them in his thoughtlessness.
351	But when he was as far off as the furrow-length
352	of mules (for they are better than oxen
353	at dragging the jointed plow through the deep fallow),
354	the two ran after him; and he stood still when he heard the sound,
355	for he hoped in his heart that companions were coming to turn him back,
356	on their way from the Trojans at Hector's urging.
357	But when they were a spear's throw away, or even less,
358	he knew them for enemy men, and plied his nimble knees
359	to flee; and they at once sprang after him in pursuit.
360	And as when two sharp-toothed hounds, skilled in the hunt,
361	press hard on a fawn or a hare, without letting up,
362	through wooded country, and it runs on ahead, crying out,
363	so the son of Tydeus and Odysseus, sacker of cities,
364	cut him off from the army and chased him without letting up.
365	But when he was just about to fall in with the sentries,
366	fleeing toward the ships, then Athena put might
367	into the son of Tydeus, so that none of the bronze-armored Achaeans
368	might boast that he struck him first, and he himself come second.
369	And darting at him with his spear, mighty Diomedes said:
370	“Stand, or I will catch you with my spear; and I tell you
371	you will not long escape sheer destruction at my hand.”
372	He spoke, and let fly his spear, and missed the man on purpose;
373	over his right shoulder the point of the polished spear
374	stuck fast in the ground; and he stood still, terrified,
375	stammering, and there was a chattering of teeth in his mouth,
376	pale with fear; and the two came up, panting,
377	and seized his hands; and he burst into tears and said:
378	“Take me alive, and I will ransom myself; for there is at home
379	bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil;
380	from these my father would gladly give you a boundless ransom,
381	if he heard that I was alive at the ships of the Achaeans.”
382	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
383	“Take heart, and do not let death be on your mind at all.
384	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
385	why are you coming like this toward the ships from the army, alone,
386	through the dark night, when other mortals are asleep?
387	Is it to strip one of the corpses of the dead?
388	Or did Hector send you out to spy out everything
389	at the hollow ships? Or did your own heart send you?”
390	Then Dolon answered him, and his limbs trembled beneath him:
391	“With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense,
392	for the single-hoofed horses of the noble son of Peleus
393	he nodded his assent to give me, and his chariot inlaid with bronze;
394	and he ordered me to go through the swift black night
395	and come close to the enemy men, and find out
396	whether the swift ships are guarded as before,
397	or whether, beaten down already by our hands,
398	they are planning flight among themselves, and are unwilling
399	to keep watch through the night, worn out by dreadful weariness.”
400	Smiling at him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
401	“Truly your heart was reaching for great gifts,
402	the horses of the wise-hearted grandson of Aeacus; but they are hard
403	for mortal men to master and to drive,
404	for any other than Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore.
405	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
406	where did you leave Hector, shepherd of the people, when you came here?
407	Where does his armor of war lie, and where are his horses?
408	And how are the watches and the sleeping places of the other Trojans,
409	what they are planning among themselves, whether they mean
410	to stay there by the ships, far from home, or to the city
411	go back again, now that they have beaten the Achaeans?”
412	Then Dolon, son of Eumedes, spoke to him in turn:
413	“Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly.
414	Hector, with all those who are counselors,
415	is holding council by the mound of divine Ilus,
416	away from the din; but the watches you ask about, hero,
417	there is no picked guard that protects the army or keeps watch.
418	At all the hearth-fires of the Trojans, those who must
419	are awake, and they keep calling on each other
420	to watch; but the allies, called from many lands,
421	are asleep; for they leave it to the Trojans to keep watch,
422	since their children are not near them, nor their wives.”
423	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
424	“How is it, then: are they mingled with the horse-taming Trojans
425	as they sleep, or apart? Tell me plainly, so that I may know.”
426	Then Dolon, son of Eumedes, answered him:
427	“Well then, I will tell you this too, very exactly.
428	Toward the sea are the Carians and the Paeonians with their curved bows,
429	and the Leleges and the Caucones and the brilliant Pelasgians;
430	and toward Thymbre the lot fell to the Lycians and the proud Mysians
431	and the Phrygians who fight from horses and the Maeonians who fight from chariots.
432	But why do you question me about all these things, one by one?
433	If you two are eager to slip into the throng of the Trojans,
434	here are the Thracians, apart, newly come, the farthest out of all;
435	and among them is Rhesus, their king, the son of Eioneus.
436	His are the finest horses I have seen, and the biggest:
437	whiter than snow, and like the winds in running;
438	and his chariot is finely worked with gold and silver;
439	and armor of gold, huge, a wonder to see,
440	he brought with him; it is not fit at all for mortal
441	men to wear, but for the immortal gods.
442	But as for me, take me now to the swift-sailing ships,
443	or bind me with a pitiless bond and leave me here,
444	until you two have gone and put me to the test,
445	whether I have spoken to you fairly, or not.”
446	Looking at him darkly, mighty Diomedes said:
447	“Do not put any thought of escape in your heart, Dolon,
448	good though your news is, now that you have come into our hands.
449	For if we take ransom and release you now, or let you go,
450	surely later too you will come to the swift ships of the Achaeans,
451	either to spy, or to fight face to face;
452	but if you are beaten down beneath my hands and lose your life,
453	then never again will you be a bane to the Argives.”
454	He spoke; and the other was about to take him by the chin with his strong hand
455	and beg him, but he struck him full in the middle of the neck,
456	darting at him with his sword, and sheared through both the tendons;
457	and while he was still speaking his head was mingled with the dust.
458	Then they took from his head the cap of ferret skin,
459	and the wolfskin, and the back-bent bow, and the long spear;
460	and these brilliant Odysseus held up to Athena, goddess of the spoil,
461	high in his hand, and he prayed and said:
462	“Rejoice in these, goddess; for you, first on Olympus
463	of all the immortals, we will call upon; but once again
464	guide us to the horses and the beds of the Thracian men.”
465	So he spoke, and lifting them high, away from himself,
466	he set them on a tamarisk bush; and he put a clear mark by them,
467	gathering together reeds and the luxuriant branches of the tamarisk,
468	so that they would not miss it when they came back through the swift black night.
469	And the two went on, through the armor and the black blood,
470	and soon they came to the company of the Thracian men.
471	They were sleeping, worn out by weariness, and their armor,
472	fine armor, lay beside them on the ground in good order,
473	in three rows; and beside each one a yoke of horses.
474	Rhesus was sleeping in the middle, and beside him his swift horses
475	were tied by straps to the end of the chariot rail.
476	Odysseus saw him first and pointed him out to Diomedes:
477	“Here is the man, Diomedes, and here are the horses
478	that Dolon, whom we killed, told us about.
479	But come now, bring out your strong might; you have no need
480	to stand idle with your weapons. Untie the horses;
481	or else you kill the men, and the horses will be my care.”
482	So he spoke, and gray-eyed Athena breathed might into him,
483	and he killed, turning this way and that; and an ugly groaning rose
484	from the men struck by the sword, and the earth grew red with blood.
485	And as a lion, coming on flocks that have no herdsman,
486	on goats or sheep, leaps on them with evil intent,
487	so the son of Tydeus went at the Thracian men,
488	until he had killed twelve; and Odysseus of many wiles,
489	whenever the son of Tydeus stood over a man and struck him with his sword,
490	Odysseus would take him by the foot from behind and drag him aside,
491	with this thought in his heart: that the fine-maned horses
492	might pass through easily, and not tremble in their hearts
493	at treading on the dead; for they were not yet used to them.
494	But when the son of Tydeus came to the king,
495	him, the thirteenth, he robbed of his honey-sweet life,
496	as he lay gasping; for an evil dream stood over his head
497	that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena.
498	Meanwhile enduring Odysseus untied the single-hoofed horses,
499	and tied them together with the straps, and drove them out of the throng,
500	striking them with his bow, since he had not thought of the shining whip,
501	to take it in his hands from the inlaid chariot;
502	and he whistled as a signal to brilliant Diomedes.
503	But he stayed and pondered what was the most shameless thing he could do:
504	whether to take the chariot, where the inlaid armor lay,
505	and drag it out by the pole, or lift it up high and carry it out,
506	or take the lives of still more of the Thracians.
507	While he was pondering this in his mind, just then Athena
508	came and stood near and spoke to brilliant Diomedes:
509	“Think now of your return, son of great-hearted Tydeus,
510	to the hollow ships, or you may get there in flight,
511	if some other god should wake the Trojans too.”
512	So she spoke, and he knew the voice of the goddess as she spoke,
513	and quickly he mounted the horses; and Odysseus struck them
514	with his bow; and they flew toward the swift ships of the Achaeans.
515	Nor did Apollo, god of the silver bow, keep a blind watch,
516	when he saw Athena attending the son of Tydeus;
517	in resentment at her he went down into the great throng of the Trojans,
518	and roused Hippocoon, a counselor of the Thracians,
519	the brave cousin of Rhesus; and he started up from sleep,
520	and when he saw the place empty where the swift horses had stood,
521	and the men gasping in the grievous slaughter,
522	he groaned aloud then, and called his dear companion by name.
523	And a clamor and an untold tumult rose among the Trojans
524	as they came rushing together; and they gazed at the grim deeds
525	the men had done before they went off to the hollow ships.
526	But when the two came to where they had killed Hector's spy,
527	there Odysseus, dear to Zeus, held in the swift horses,
528	and the son of Tydeus leapt to the ground and put the bloody spoils
529	into Odysseus' hands, and mounted the horses again;
530	and he whipped the horses; and the pair flew on not unwilling
531	toward the hollow ships; for there their hearts were glad to go.
532	Nestor was the first to hear the sound, and he said:
533	“Friends, leaders and rulers of the Argives,
534	shall I be wrong, or speak the truth? But my heart bids me speak.
535	The sound of swift-footed horses is beating about my ears.
536	If only it were Odysseus and mighty Diomedes
537	driving single-hoofed horses from the Trojans, so soon!
538	But I am terribly afraid at heart that the best men
539	of the Argives have come to some harm under the din of the Trojans.”
540	Not yet had his whole word been spoken when they came themselves.
541	And they got down onto the ground, and the others rejoiced
542	and welcomed them with handclasps and with soothing words;
543	and first the Gerenian horseman Nestor asked:
544	“Come, tell me, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans:
545	how did you two take these horses? By slipping into the throng
546	of the Trojans, or did some god meet you and give them to you?
547	They are terribly like the rays of the sun.
548	I am always closing with the Trojans, and I tell you I do not
549	hang back by the ships, old warrior though I am;
550	but never yet have I seen or noticed horses like these.
551	No, I think some god met you and gave them to you;
552	for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both,
553	and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena.”
554	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
555	“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
556	a god, if he wished, could easily give horses even better
557	than these, since the gods are far stronger than we are.
558	But these horses you ask about, old sir, are newly come;
559	they are Thracian; and their lord was killed by brave Diomedes,
560	and beside him twelve companions, all of the best.
561	A thirteenth, a spy, we took close to the ships,
562	one sent out to be a scout upon our army
563	by Hector and the other noble Trojans.”
564	So saying, he drove the single-hoofed horses across the ditch,
565	laughing aloud; and the other Achaeans went with him, rejoicing.
566	And when they came to the well-made hut of the son of Tydeus,
567	they tied the horses with well-cut straps
568	at the horses' manger, where the horses of Diomedes
569	stood, swift-footed, eating honey-sweet wheat;
570	and in the stern of the ship the bloody spoils of Dolon
571	Odysseus set, until they could make ready an offering to Athena.
572	And they themselves washed off the heavy sweat in the sea,
573	wading in, from their shins and necks and thighs.
574	And when the wave of the sea had washed the heavy sweat
575	from their skin, and their hearts were refreshed,
576	they stepped into polished bathtubs and bathed.
577	And when the two had bathed and anointed themselves richly with olive oil,
578	they sat down to their meal; and to Athena, from the full mixing bowl,
579	they drew honey-sweet wine and poured it out.
