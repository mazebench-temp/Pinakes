# Homer, Iliad, Book 18 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So they fought on like blazing fire;
2	and Antilochus, swift of foot, came to Achilles as a messenger.
3	He found him in front of the straight-horned ships,
4	pondering in his heart things that had now been fulfilled;
5	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
6	“Ah me, why then again are the long-haired Achaeans
7	driven in confusion toward the ships, in terror across the plain?
8	May the gods not have fulfilled evil sorrows for my heart,
9	as my mother once made plain to me and told me,
10	that the best of the Myrmidons, while I was still alive,
11	at the hands of the Trojans would leave the light of the sun.
12	Surely now the valiant son of Menoetius is dead,
13	hard man! And yet I bade him, once he had thrust off the consuming fire,
14	to come back to the ships, and not to fight Hector in force.”
15	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
16	the son of noble Nestor came near him,
17	shedding hot tears, and told his grievous news:
18	“Ah me, son of wise-hearted Peleus, truly you will hear
19	grievous news, which I wish had never been.
20	Patroclus lies dead, and they are fighting around his corpse,
21	naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor.”
22	So he spoke, and a black cloud of grief covered him;
23	and taking the sooty dust in both his hands
24	he poured it down over his head, and fouled his handsome face,
25	and the black ash settled on his fragrant tunic.
26	And he himself, great over a great space, stretched out in the dust,
27	lay there, and with his own hands he tore his hair and fouled it.
28	And the slave women whom Achilles and Patroclus had taken as spoil,
29	grieving at heart, cried aloud, and ran out of doors
30	around wise-hearted Achilles, and with their hands all of them
31	beat their breasts, and the limbs of each were loosened beneath her.
32	And Antilochus on the other side mourned, letting tears fall,
33	holding the hands of Achilles; and he groaned in his glorious heart;
34	for he feared that he would cut his throat with the iron.
35	And he groaned aloud terribly; and his lady mother heard him
36	as she sat in the depths of the sea beside her aged father,
37	and then she wailed aloud; and the goddesses gathered around her,
38	all the Nereids that were in the depths of the sea.
39	There were Glauce and Thaleia and Cymodoce,
40	Nesaea and Speio and Thoe and ox-eyed Halia,
41	and Cymothoe and Actaea and Limnoreia,
42	and Melite and Iaera and Amphithoe and Agave,
43	Doto and Proto and Pherusa and Dynamene,
44	and Dexamene and Amphinome and Callianeira,
45	Doris and Panope and famous Galateia,
46	and Nemertes and Apseudes and Callianassa;
47	and there were Clymene and Ianeira and Ianassa,
48	Maera and Oreithyia and Amatheia of the lovely tresses,
49	and the other Nereids that were in the depths of the sea.
50	The silver-white cave was filled with them; and all of them together
51	they beat their breasts, and Thetis led the lamentation:
52	“Hear me, Nereids, my sisters, so that you may all
53	know well, when you have heard, how many sorrows are in my heart.
54	Ah me, wretched that I am, ah me, unhappy mother of the best of men,
55	for I bore a son, noble and strong,
56	preeminent among heroes; and he shot up like a sapling;
57	I reared him like a plant on the slope of an orchard,
58	and sent him out with the curved ships to Ilios
59	to fight with the Trojans; but I shall not welcome him again
60	returning home to the house of Peleus.
61	And while he lives and looks on the light of the sun
62	he grieves, and I can do nothing to help him by going to him.
63	But I will go, so that I may see my dear child, and hear
64	what grief has come upon him while he stays away from the war.”
65	So she spoke and left the cave; and they went with her
66	in tears, and around them the wave of the sea
67	broke; and when they came to deep-soiled Troy
68	they went up onto the shore one after another, where thick
69	the ships of the Myrmidons were drawn up around swift Achilles.
70	And as he groaned heavily his lady mother stood beside him,
71	and wailing shrilly she took her son's head in her hands,
72	and lamenting addressed him with winged words:
73	“My child, why are you weeping? What grief has come upon your heart?
74	Speak out; do not hide it. These things have been fulfilled for you
75	by Zeus, as you prayed before, lifting up your hands,
76	that all the sons of the Achaeans should be penned in among the sterns
77	for want of you, and suffer unseemly deeds.”
78	Groaning heavily, swift-footed Achilles said to her:
79	“My mother, these things the Olympian has indeed fulfilled for me;
80	but what pleasure have I in them, since my dear companion has perished,
81	Patroclus, whom I honored above all my companions,
82	as my own life? I have lost him; and Hector, when he had cut him down,
83	stripped off the armor, huge, a wonder to see,
84	and fine, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus
85	on the day when they put you into the bed of a mortal man.
86	If only you had stayed there among the immortal goddesses of the sea,
87	and Peleus had taken a mortal wife!
88	But as it is, it was so that you too might have countless grief in your heart
89	for your son who has perished, whom you will not welcome again
90	returning home; for my heart does not bid me
91	live, or remain among men, unless Hector
92	is first struck by my spear and loses his life,
93	and pays for making Patroclus, son of Menoetius, his prey.”
94	Then Thetis spoke to him in turn, letting her tears fall:
95	“Then you will be swift-fated, my child, from what you say;
96	for straight after Hector your own fate is ready.”
97	Deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles said to her:
98	“May I die at once, since I was not, it seems, to defend my companion
99	when he was killed; and he, very far from his native land,
100	perished, and lacked me to be his defender against disaster.
101	But now, since I am not going home to my dear native land,
102	and I was no light at all to Patroclus, nor to my companions,
103	the others, the many who were brought down by brilliant Hector,
104	but I sit idly beside the ships, a burden on the earth,
105	I, who am such as none of the bronze-armored Achaeans
106	in war, though in the assembly others too are better—
107	may strife perish from among gods and from among men,
108	and anger, which sets even a man of much wisdom to rage,
109	and which, far sweeter than dripping honey,
110	swells in the breasts of men like smoke;
111	so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now.
112	But we will let all this be, as done and past, for all our grief,
113	taming the dear heart in our breasts under compulsion.
114	Now I will go, so that I may find the destroyer of that dear life,
115	Hector; and I will accept my doom then, whenever
116	Zeus is willing to bring it to pass, and the other immortal gods.
117	For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom,
118	who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus;
119	but fate brought him down, and the grievous anger of Hera.
120	So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me,
121	shall lie still when I am dead; but now may I win noble fame,
122	and make some woman among the Trojan and the deep-bosomed Dardanian women,
123	with both her hands, from her soft cheeks,
124	wiping away the tears, groan without ceasing,
125	and let them know that I have long ceased from the war.
126	Do not keep me from the battle, though you love me; you will not persuade me.”
127	Then the goddess silver-footed Thetis answered him:
128	“Yes, my child, truly this is no bad thing,
129	to ward off sheer destruction from your hard-pressed companions.
130	But your fine armor is held among the Trojans,
131	bronze, gleaming; Hector of the gleaming helmet
132	wears it on his own shoulders and glories in it; but I say
133	he will not exult in it long, since his death is near him.
134	But do not yet enter the toil of Ares
135	until you see me come back here with your own eyes;
136	for at dawn I will return with the rising sun,
137	bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus.”
138	So she spoke, and turned away from her son,
139	and, turning, spoke among her sisters of the sea:
140	“Now go down into the broad bosom of the sea,
141	to see the Old Man of the Sea and our father's house,
142	and tell him everything; and I will go to high Olympus,
143	to Hephaestus, famed for his craft, in the hope that he may be willing
144	to give my son glorious armor, gleaming all over.”
145	So she spoke, and at once they plunged beneath the wave of the sea;
146	and she, the goddess silver-footed Thetis, went to Olympus
147	to bring glorious armor to her dear son.
148	So her feet carried her to Olympus; but the Achaeans,
149	with a wondrous shout, before man-slaying Hector,
150	in their flight reached the ships and the Hellespont.
151	Nor would the well-greaved Achaeans have drawn Patroclus
152	out of range of the missiles, the corpse of the attendant of Achilles;
153	for once more the army and the horses caught up with him,
154	and Hector, son of Priam, like a flame in his courage.
155	Three times shining Hector seized him from behind by the feet,
156	eager to drag him off, and he called loudly to the Trojans;
157	and three times the two Ajaxes, clothed in furious courage,
158	drove him back from the body; but he, steadfast, trusting in his courage,
159	now would charge through the fray, and now again
160	would stand fast with a great cry; and he did not draw back at all.
161	And as shepherds of the fields cannot drive a tawny lion
162	away from a carcass at all, when he is very hungry,
163	so the two Ajaxes, helmeted men, could not
164	frighten Hector, son of Priam, away from the body.
165	And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory,
166	had not swift Iris, wind-footed, to the son of Peleus
167	come running from Olympus as a messenger, to arm,
168	in secret from Zeus and the other gods; for Hera had sent her.
169	And standing near him she addressed him with winged words:
170	“Rise, son of Peleus, most terrible of all men;
171	defend Patroclus, for whose sake the dread din of battle
172	stands before the ships; and they are killing one another,
173	the one side defending the corpse of the dead man,
174	and the other side striving to drag it off to windy Ilios,
175	the Trojans; and above all shining Hector
176	is bent on dragging it; and his heart bids him fix the head
177	on the stakes, when he has cut it from the tender neck.
178	Up, then, and lie here no longer; let shame come into your heart
179	that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs;
180	yours will be the disgrace, if the corpse comes back disfigured at all.”
181	Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles answered her:
182	“Goddess Iris, who of the gods sent you to me as a messenger?”
183	Then swift Iris, wind-footed, spoke to him in turn:
184	“Hera sent me, the glorious wife of Zeus;
185	and the son of Cronus, throned on high, does not know, nor any other
186	of the immortals who dwell around snowy Olympus.”
187	Answering her, swift-footed Achilles said:
188	“How then shall I go into the toil? Those men have my armor;
189	and my dear mother does not allow me to arm
190	until I see her come back with my own eyes;
191	for she promised to bring fine armor from Hephaestus.
192	And I know of no other man whose glorious armor I could put on,
193	unless it were the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon.
194	But he himself, I think, is in the throng among the foremost,
195	cutting men down with his spear over Patroclus dead.”
196	Then swift Iris, wind-footed, spoke to him in turn:
197	“We too know well that your glorious armor is held by them;
198	but go just as you are to the ditch and show yourself to the Trojans,
199	in the hope that in fear of you they may hold off from the war,
200	the Trojans, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath,
201	worn out as they are; and brief is the breathing space in war.”
202	So saying, swift-footed Iris went away;
203	and Achilles, dear to Zeus, rose up; and Athena around
204	his mighty shoulders flung the tasseled aegis,
205	and around his head she set a cloud as a crown, brilliant among goddesses,
206	a golden one, and from him she kindled a flame, gleaming all over.
207	And as when smoke goes up from a city and reaches the upper air
208	far off, from an island that enemies are fighting around,
209	and all day long the men try their strength in the hateful work of Ares
210	from their own city; and as the sun goes down
211	the beacon fires blaze in rows, and the glare goes darting
212	up high, for those who live around to see,
213	in the hope that they may come with their ships to defend them against disaster;
214	so from the head of Achilles the blaze reached the upper air.
215	And he went from the wall and stood at the ditch, but with the Achaeans
216	he did not mingle; for he heeded his mother's shrewd command.
217	There he stood and shouted, and apart from him Pallas Athena
218	cried out; and among the Trojans he roused untold tumult.
219	And as a voice rings out clear, when a trumpet sounds
220	because enemies who destroy the spirit surround a city,
221	so clear then rang out the voice of the grandson of Aeacus.
222	And when they heard the bronze voice of the grandson of Aeacus,
223	the hearts of all were shaken; and the fine-maned horses
224	turned the chariots back; for they foresaw sorrows in their hearts.
225	And the charioteers were struck with terror when they saw the tireless fire
226	blazing terribly above the head of the great-hearted son of Peleus;
227	the gray-eyed goddess Athena kindled it.
228	Three times across the ditch brilliant Achilles shouted aloud,
229	and three times the Trojans and their famous allies were thrown into confusion.
230	And there and then twelve of their best men perished
231	among their own chariots and spears. But the Achaeans
232	gladly drew Patroclus out from under the missiles
233	and laid him on a bier; and his dear companions stood around him
234	weeping; and swift-footed Achilles followed among them
235	shedding hot tears, when he saw his trusted companion
236	lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze,
237	whom he had sent out with horses and chariot
238	into the war, and did not welcome him home again.
239	Then ox-eyed queen Hera sent the tireless Sun,
240	unwilling, to go back to the streams of Oceanus;
241	and the sun set, and the brilliant Achaeans ceased
242	from the fierce din of battle and from war, common to all.
243	And the Trojans on the other side, away from the fierce battle,
244	withdrew and unyoked their swift horses from under the chariots,
245	and gathered in assembly before they thought of supper.
246	The assembly was held with all standing, and no one dared
247	to sit down; for trembling held them all, because Achilles
248	had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle.
249	Among them wise Polydamas began to speak,
250	the son of Panthous; for he alone saw before and after;
251	he was Hector's companion, and they were born in the same night,
252	but the one excelled far in words, the other with the spear.
253	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
254	“Consider this well on every side, friends; for I bid you
255	go now to the city, and not wait for brilliant Dawn
256	on the plain beside the ships; we are far from the wall.
257	As long as this man nursed his wrath against brilliant Agamemnon,
258	so long the Achaeans were easier to fight;
259	for I myself used to rejoice, spending the night beside the swift ships,
260	hoping that we would take the curving ships.
261	But now I am terribly afraid of the swift-footed son of Peleus;
262	such is his overweening spirit, he will not be willing
263	to stay on the plain, where the Trojans and the Achaeans,
264	both, in the middle ground, share between them the fury of Ares;
265	but he will fight for our city and our women.
266	So let us go to the city; do as I say; for this is how it will be:
267	now night has stopped the swift-footed son of Peleus,
268	the ambrosial night; but if he finds us still here
269	tomorrow, as he rushes out in his armor, then one will know him
270	well indeed; for glad will he be to reach sacred Ilios,
271	whoever escapes, and dogs and vultures will eat many
272	of the Trojans; may that be far from my hearing!
273	But if we do as I say, troubled though we are,
274	through the night we will keep our strength in the place of assembly, and the towers
275	and the high gates and the doors fitted on them,
276	long, well polished, bolted fast, will guard the city;
277	and early, at daybreak, armed in our armor,
278	we will take our stand on the towers; and the worse for him, if he wants
279	to come from the ships and fight us around the wall.
280	He will go back again to the ships, when he has given his horses with high-arched necks
281	their fill of running every way as he roams beneath the city;
282	but his heart will not let him rush in,
283	nor will he ever sack it; before that the swift dogs will eat him.”
284	Looking at him darkly, Hector of the gleaming helmet said:
285	“Polydamas, what you say is no longer pleasing to me,
286	you who bid us go back and be penned in the city.
287	Have you not had your fill yet of being penned within the towers?
288	Once all mortal men used to speak of the city of Priam
289	as rich in gold, rich in bronze;
290	but now the fine treasures of our houses are gone,
291	and many have gone to Phrygia and lovely Maeonia,
292	possessions sold away, since great Zeus grew angry.
293	But now, when the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has granted me
294	to win glory at the ships, and to pen the Achaeans against the sea,
295	fool, no longer show these thoughts among the people;
296	for none of the Trojans will obey you; I will not allow it.
297	Come then, let us all do as I say.
298	Now take your supper throughout the army, by companies,
299	and remember to keep watch, and let each man stay awake;
300	and whoever of the Trojans is overly troubled about his possessions,
301	let him gather them and give them to the people to consume in common;
302	it is better that someone among the people should have the good of them than the Achaeans.
303	And early, at daybreak, armed in our armor,
304	we will rouse sharp Ares beside the hollow ships.
305	And if it is true that brilliant Achilles has risen up beside the ships,
306	the worse it will be for him, if he wants it so; I at least
307	will not flee from him out of grim-sounding war, but face to face
308	I will stand, and either he will carry off the great mastery, or I might.
309	Enyalius is impartial, and he kills the man who would kill.”
310	So Hector spoke, and the Trojans shouted in applause,
311	fools; for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits.
312	For they approved of Hector, who devised evil,
313	but no one approved of Polydamas, who had devised good counsel.
314	Then they took their supper throughout the army; but the Achaeans
315	all night long groaned over Patroclus, lamenting.
316	And among them the son of Peleus led the unceasing lamentation,
317	laying his man-slaying hands on the breast of his companion,
318	groaning thick and fast, like a bearded lion
319	whose cubs a deer hunter has snatched away
320	out of the thick wood; and he grieves when he comes back too late,
321	and goes through many valleys, searching out the man's tracks,
322	in the hope that he may find him somewhere; for very bitter anger seizes him;
323	so, groaning heavily, he spoke among the Myrmidons:
324	“Ah, truly I let fall a vain word on that day
325	when I was encouraging the hero Menoetius in his halls;
326	I told him that I would bring his renowned son back to Opus
327	when he had sacked Ilios and won his share of the spoil.
328	But Zeus does not fulfill for men all their plans;
329	for it is fated that both of us redden the same earth
330	here in Troy, since I too shall not come home
331	to be welcomed in his halls by the old horse-driving Peleus,
332	nor by my mother Thetis, but the earth will hold me here.
333	But now, Patroclus, since I shall go under the earth after you,
334	I will not give you burial rites until I bring here Hector's
335	armor and head, your great-hearted slayer's;
336	and before your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve
337	splendid children of the Trojans, in my anger over your killing.
338	Until then you shall lie just as you are beside the curved ships,
339	and around you the Trojan and the deep-bosomed Dardanian women
340	shall weep night and day, shedding tears,
341	whom we ourselves won by toil with our strength and our long spear,
342	as we two sacked the rich cities of mortal men.”
343	So saying, brilliant Achilles called to his companions
344	to set a great tripod over the fire, so that with all speed
345	they might wash the clotted blood from Patroclus.
346	And they set the tripod for the bath on the blazing fire,
347	and poured water into it, and took wood and kindled it beneath.
348	The fire played around the belly of the tripod, and the water grew warm;
349	and when the water had boiled in the gleaming bronze,
350	then they washed him and anointed him richly with olive oil,
351	and filled his wounds with ointment nine years old;
352	and laying him on a bier they covered him with a soft linen cloth
353	from head to foot, and over it with a white cloak.
354	Then all night long, around Achilles, swift of foot,
355	the Myrmidons groaned over Patroclus, lamenting.
356	And Zeus spoke to Hera, his sister and wife:
357	“You have had your way after all, ox-eyed queen Hera,
358	rousing Achilles, swift of foot; truly then, from your own self
359	the long-haired Achaeans must have been born.”
360	Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him:
361	“Most dread son of Cronus, what a thing you have said!
362	Surely even a mortal will accomplish his purpose against another man,
363	though he is mortal and does not know so many counsels;
364	how then should I, who say I am the best of goddesses
365	on both counts, by birth and because I am called
366	your wife, and you are lord among all the immortals,
367	not stitch together evil for the Trojans, in my resentment against them?”
368	So they spoke such words to one another,
369	And silver-footed Thetis came to the house of Hephaestus,
370	imperishable, starry, standing out among the immortals,
371	of bronze, which the crook-footed god had built for himself.
372	She found him sweating as he turned about among his bellows,
373	in haste; for he was making tripods, twenty in all,
374	to stand around the wall of his well-founded hall,
375	and he had set golden wheels under the base of each,
376	so that of themselves they might enter the divine assembly
377	and go back again to his house, a wonder to see.
378	They were finished so far, but the handles were not yet
379	fixed on, intricately worked; these he was fitting, and forging the rivets.
380	While he was toiling at these things with his cunning skill,
381	the goddess silver-footed Thetis came near him.
382	And Charis of the shining veil came forward and saw her,
383	the lovely one, whom the renowned god, lame in both legs, had married;
384	and she clung to her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
385	“Why, Thetis of the long robes, have you come to our house,
386	respected and dear? Before now you have not come often.
387	But come farther in, so that I may set good cheer before you.”
388	So she spoke, and led her in, brilliant among goddesses.
389	Then she sat her down on a silver-studded throne,
390	fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet;
391	and she called to Hephaestus, famed for his craft, and spoke a word:
392	“Hephaestus, come out here; Thetis has need of you.”
393	Then the renowned god, lame in both legs, answered her:
394	“Truly then a goddess I fear and respect is in our house,
395	who saved me when pain came on me after I had fallen far
396	by the will of my dog-faced mother, who wished
397	to hide me away because I was lame; then I would have suffered sorrows at heart,
398	had not Eurynome and Thetis taken me to their bosom,
399	Eurynome, daughter of backward-flowing Oceanus.
400	With them for nine years I forged many intricate designs,
401	brooches and curved spirals, rosettes and necklaces,
402	in a hollow cave; and around it the stream of Oceanus
403	flowed boundless, seething with foam; and no one else
404	knew of it, neither of the gods nor of mortal men,
405	but Thetis and Eurynome knew, who had saved me.
406	And now she has come to our house; so I must indeed
407	pay Thetis of the lovely tresses the full price for my life.
408	But you, for now, set fine guest-gifts before her,
409	while I put away my bellows and all my tools.”
410	He spoke, and rose from the anvil block, a huge, panting god,
411	limping; and his slender shins moved nimbly beneath him.
412	He set his bellows away from the fire, and all the tools
413	he toiled with he gathered into a silver chest;
414	and with a sponge he wiped his face and both his hands
415	and his massive neck and his shaggy chest,
416	and put on his tunic, and took a thick staff, and went out of doors,
417	limping; and handmaids moved nimbly to support their lord,
418	golden ones, like living young women.
419	There is a mind in their hearts, and voice in them,
420	and strength, and they are skilled in handiwork from the immortal gods.
421	They bustled about, supporting their lord; and he, hobbling
422	near to where Thetis was, sat down on a shining throne,
423	and he clung to her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
424	“Why, Thetis of the long robes, have you come to our house,
425	respected and dear? Before now you have not come often.
426	Say what is in your mind; my heart bids me accomplish it,
427	if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished.”
428	Then Thetis answered him, letting her tears fall:
429	“Hephaestus, is there any, of all the goddesses on Olympus,
430	who has endured in her heart so many grievous sorrows
431	as the sorrows Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me beyond all of them?
432	Out of all the other goddesses of the sea he subdued me to a man,
433	Peleus, son of Aeacus, and I endured the bed of a man,
434	much against my will. And he now, by grievous old age
435	lies worn out in his halls; but now I have other sorrows:
436	since he gave me a son to bear and to rear,
437	preeminent among heroes; and he shot up like a sapling;
438	I reared him like a plant on the slope of an orchard,
439	and sent him out with the curved ships to Ilios
440	to fight with the Trojans; but I shall not welcome him again
441	returning home to the house of Peleus.
442	And while he lives and looks on the light of the sun
443	he grieves, and I can do nothing to help him by going to him.
444	The girl whom the sons of the Achaeans picked out for him as his prize,
445	powerful Agamemnon took her back out of his hands.
446	Grieving for her, he was wasting his heart away; but the Achaeans
447	the Trojans were penning in among the sterns, and would not let them
448	come out of doors; and the elders begged him,
449	the elders of the Argives, and named many glorious gifts.
450	Then he himself refused to ward off ruin,
451	but he put his own armor on Patroclus,
452	and sent him into the war, and granted a great army to go with him.
453	All day long they fought around the Scaean gates;
454	and on that very day they would have sacked the city, had not Apollo,
455	after the valiant son of Menoetius had done much harm,
456	killed him among the champions, and given glory to Hector.
457	That is why I come now to your knees, in the hope that you may be willing
458	to give my swift-fated son a shield and a helmet,
459	and fine greaves fitted with ankle-pieces,
460	and a corselet; for what he had, his trusted companion lost
461	when he was brought down by the Trojans; and he lies on the ground, grieving in his heart.”
462	Then the renowned god, lame in both legs, answered her:
463	“Take heart; do not let these things trouble your mind.
464	If only I could hide him as surely from grim-sounding death,
465	away and apart, when dread fate comes upon him,
466	as he will have fine armor, such that many a man
467	of the many men will marvel at it, whoever sees it.”
468	So saying he left her there, and went to his bellows;
469	and he turned them toward the fire and bade them work.
470	And the bellows, twenty in all, blew on the crucibles,
471	sending out every kind of blast that fans the flame,
472	now to be at hand for him as he hurried, and now otherwise,
473	as Hephaestus wished and as the work went on.
474	And he cast into the fire unyielding bronze and tin
475	and precious gold and silver; and then
476	he set a great anvil on the anvil block, and took in one hand
477	a mighty hammer, and in the other he took the tongs.
478	And first he made a shield, great and heavy,
479	working it intricately all over, and he set around it a shining rim,
480	threefold, gleaming, and from it a silver strap.
481	There were five layers to the shield itself; and on it
482	he made many intricate designs with his cunning skill.
483	On it he made the earth, on it the sky, on it the sea,
484	and the tireless sun, and the moon at the full,
485	and on it all the constellations with which the sky is crowned,
486	the Pleiades and the Hyades and the strength of Orion,
487	and the Bear, which they also call by the name of the Wagon,
488	which turns in its place and watches Orion,
489	and alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus.
490	On it he made two cities of mortal men,
491	fine ones. In the one there were weddings and banquets,
492	and they were leading the brides from their chambers under blazing torches
493	through the city, and the bridal song rose loud;
494	and young men were whirling in the dance, and among them
495	flutes and lyres kept up their sound; and the women
496	stood each at her doorway and marveled.
497	And the people were all together in the place of assembly; and there a quarrel
498	had arisen, and two men were quarreling over the penalty
499	for a man who had perished; the one vowed to pay it all,
500	declaring it to the people, and the other refused to take anything;
501	and both were eager to win a decision at the hands of an arbiter.
502	And the people were cheering both, supporting either side;
503	and heralds were holding the people back; and the elders
504	sat on polished stones in the sacred circle,
505	and held in their hands the scepters of the far-voiced heralds;
506	with these they then sprang up, and gave their judgments in turn.
507	And in the middle lay two talents of gold,
508	to give to the one among them who spoke the straightest judgment.
509	And around the other city sat two armies of men,
510	flashing in their armor; and they were divided in their counsel,
511	whether to sack it or to divide in two all
512	the wealth that the lovely citadel held within it;
513	but the others would not yet yield, and were secretly arming for an ambush.
514	The wall was guarded by their dear wives and little children
515	standing upon it, and with them the men whom old age held;
516	and the others went; and Ares led them and Pallas Athena,
517	both of gold, and clad in golden clothing,
518	fine and tall in their armor, as gods should be,
519	plain for all to see from every side; and the people were smaller.
520	And when they came to the place where it seemed good to lie in ambush,
521	in a river, where there was a watering place for all the herds,
522	there they sat down, wrapped in gleaming bronze.
523	And for them two lookouts sat apart from the army,
524	waiting for when they should see the sheep and the cattle with curving horns.
525	And these soon came into view, and two herdsmen went along with them,
526	taking their delight in their pipes; and they did not foresee the trick at all.
527	The others saw them first and ran at them, and quickly then
528	cut off the herds of cattle and the fine flocks
529	of white sheep, and killed the shepherds as well.
530	And they, when they heard the great din among the cattle
531	as they sat in front of their places of assembly, at once mounted
532	behind their high-stepping horses and went after them, and quickly reached them.
533	And they set their battle in array and fought beside the river's banks,
534	and they struck at one another with bronze-fitted spears.
535	And among them Strife and Tumult were in the throng, and deadly Doom,
536	holding one man alive and freshly wounded, another unwounded,
537	and another, dead, she was dragging through the fray by the feet;
538	and the cloak on her shoulders was blood-red with the blood of men.
539	And they were in the throng and fought like living mortals,
540	and they dragged away the bodies of one another's dead.
541	And on it he set a soft fallow field, rich plowland,
542	broad, thrice plowed; and many plowmen on it
543	were wheeling their teams and driving them this way and that.
544	And whenever they turned and reached the end of the field,
545	then a cup of honey-sweet wine would be put into their hands
546	by a man who came up to them; and they would turn back along the furrows,
547	eager to reach the end of the deep fallow.
548	And the earth grew dark behind them, and looked like plowed land,
549	though it was made of gold; that was a wonder beyond all.
550	And on it he set a king's domain; there hired laborers
551	were reaping, with sharp sickles in their hands.
552	Some of the handfuls were falling to the ground in rows along the swath,
553	and others the sheaf-binders were tying with bands of straw.
554	Three sheaf-binders stood over the work; and behind them
555	boys were gathering the handfuls and carrying them in their arms,
556	and kept supplying them relentlessly; and among them the king in silence,
557	holding his scepter, stood by the swath, glad at heart.
558	And apart, under an oak, heralds were making ready a feast,
559	and had sacrificed a great ox and were busy over it; and the women
560	were sprinkling much white barley meal for the laborers' dinner.
561	And on it he set a vineyard heavily laden with clusters,
562	fine and golden; and the grapes on it were black,
563	and it was propped all through on silver poles.
564	And around it he drew a trench of dark blue, and around that a fence
565	of tin; and there was a single path to it,
566	by which the carriers went when they gathered the vintage.
567	And girls and young men, with light hearts,
568	were carrying the honey-sweet fruit in woven baskets.
569	And in the middle of them a boy on a clear-toned lyre
570	played lovely music, and to it he sang the beautiful Linus song
571	in a delicate voice; and the others, stamping all together,
572	with song and with shouting, followed, skipping with their feet.
573	And on it he made a herd of straight-horned cattle;
574	the cows were made of gold and of tin,
575	and lowing they hurried out of the dung-yard to the pasture,
576	beside a sounding river, beside a bed of swaying reeds.
577	And golden herdsmen walked in line with the cattle,
578	four of them, and nine dogs, fleet of foot, followed them.
579	But two terrible lions among the foremost cows
580	had seized a bellowing bull; and he, lowing loudly,
581	was being dragged off; and the dogs and the young men went after him.
582	The two had torn open the hide of the great bull
583	and were gulping down the entrails and the black blood; and the herdsmen
584	were setting on their swift dogs and urging them, for nothing;
585	for the dogs shrank back from biting the lions,
586	but standing very close they kept barking, and kept out of the way.
587	And on it the renowned god, lame in both legs, made a pasture
588	in a lovely glen, a great one, for white sheep,
589	and farmsteads and roofed huts and pens.
590	And on it the renowned god, lame in both legs, inlaid a dancing floor,
591	like the one that once in wide Cnossus
592	Daedalus fashioned for Ariadne of the lovely tresses.
593	There young men and maidens who would bring many oxen
594	were dancing, holding one another's hands by the wrist.
595	The girls had on fine linen, and the young men wore tunics
596	well-spun, shining softly with oil;
597	and the girls had fine garlands, and the young men had knives
598	of gold, hanging from silver straps.
599	And now they would run on skillful feet
600	very easily, as when a wheel that fits his hands
601	is tried by a potter sitting at it, to see whether it will run;
602	and now again they would run in ranks toward one another.
603	And a great throng stood around the lovely dance,
604	delighting in it; and among them a divine singer sang
605	to the lyre; and two tumblers among them,
606	leading the song and dance, whirled in the midst.
607	And on it he set the great strength of the river Oceanus
608	along the outermost rim of the strongly made shield.
609	And when he had made the shield, great and heavy,
610	he made him a corselet brighter than the light of fire,
611	and he made him a heavy helmet fitted to his temples,
612	fine and intricately worked, and set a golden crest on it,
613	and he made him greaves of soft tin.
614	And when the famous god, lame in both legs, had made all the armor,
615	he lifted it and set it before the mother of Achilles.
616	And she like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus,
617	carrying the gleaming armor from Hephaestus.
