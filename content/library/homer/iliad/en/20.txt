# Homer, Iliad, Book 20 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Monro–Allen Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	So they were arming themselves beside the curved ships,
2	around you, son of Peleus, insatiable of battle, the Achaeans;
3	and the Trojans on the other side, on the rise of the plain.
4	And Zeus bade Themis call the gods to assembly
5	from the summit of many-folded Olympus; and she went everywhere,
6	going to and fro, and bade them come to the house of Zeus.
7	And not one of the rivers was absent, except Oceanus,
8	nor any of the nymphs who dwell in the lovely groves
9	and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows.
10	And when they came to the house of Zeus the cloud-gatherer,
11	they sat down in the polished colonnades, which for father Zeus
12	Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill.
13	So they were gathered within the house of Zeus; nor did the earth-shaker
14	fail to heed the goddess, but came up out of the sea to join them,
15	and sat down in their midst, and asked Zeus his plan:
16	“Why, lord of the gleaming thunderbolt, have you called the gods to assembly again?
17	Are you pondering something about the Trojans and the Achaeans?
18	For now their battle and war have blazed up at very close quarters.”
19	Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
20	“You know, shaker of the earth, the plan in my breast,
21	for which I have gathered you: I care for them, even as they perish.
22	But I for my part will stay here in a fold of Olympus,
23	seated, where I shall look on and delight my heart; but you others
24	go, until you come among the Trojans and the Achaeans,
25	and help either side, whichever way each of you has a mind.
26	For if Achilles fights alone against the Trojans,
27	they will not hold the swift-footed son of Peleus even for a little while.
28	Even before now they used to tremble when they saw him;
29	and now that he is terribly enraged at heart over his companion,
30	I fear he may even sack the wall against fate.”
31	So spoke the son of Cronus, and roused relentless war.
32	And the gods set out to go to war, their hearts divided:
33	Hera went to the gathering of the ships, and Pallas Athena,
34	and Poseidon the earth-holder, and the great helper
35	Hermes, who excels in his shrewd mind;
36	and Hephaestus went with them, exulting in his strength,
37	limping; and his slender shins moved nimbly beneath him.
38	And to the Trojans went Ares of the gleaming helmet, and with him
39	Phoebus of the unshorn hair, and Artemis of the showering arrows,
40	and Leto and Xanthus and laughter-loving Aphrodite.
41	Now as long as the gods were apart from mortal men,
42	so long the Achaeans exulted greatly, because Achilles
43	had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle;
44	but a dread trembling came over the limbs of every Trojan,
45	in their fear, when they saw the swift-footed son of Peleus
46	flashing in his armor, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals.
47	But when the Olympians had come into the throng of men,
48	mighty Strife rose up, who rouses armies, and Athena shouted,
49	standing now beside the ditch they had dug, outside the wall,
50	and now on the loud-resounding shores she shouted aloud.
51	And Ares on the other side shouted, like a dark storm,
52	shrilly calling on the Trojans from the topmost height of the city,
53	and now running beside the Simoeis on Callicolone.
54	So the blessed gods urged both sides on
55	and brought them together, and made heavy strife break out among them;
56	and terribly the father of men and gods thundered
57	from on high; and below, Poseidon shook
58	the boundless earth and the steep peaks of the mountains.
59	And all the feet of Ida of the many springs were shaken,
60	and its peaks, and the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans.
61	And beneath, Aidoneus, lord of those below, was afraid,
62	and in fear he leapt from his throne and cried out, lest above him
63	Poseidon the earth-shaker should break the earth open,
64	and his dwellings be laid bare to mortals and immortals,
65	terrible and dank, which even the gods hate:
66	so great was the din that rose as the gods came together in strife.
67	For against lord Poseidon
68	stood Phoebus Apollo with his feathered arrows,
69	and against Enyalius the gray-eyed goddess Athena;
70	against Hera stood she of the golden distaff, the clamorous
71	Artemis of the showering arrows, sister of the Far-Shooter;
72	against Leto stood strong Hermes, the great helper,
73	and against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river,
74	whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander.
75	So gods went to meet gods; but Achilles
76	longed above all to plunge into the throng to meet Hector,
77	son of Priam; for it was with his blood above all that his heart bade him
78	glut Ares, the warrior with the oxhide shield.
79	But Apollo, who rouses armies, roused Aeneas straight
80	to face the son of Peleus, and put noble might into him;
81	and he made his voice like that of Priam's son Lycaon;
82	in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him:
83	“Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans, where have your threats gone,
84	which you promised the kings of the Trojans as you drank your wine,
85	that you would fight face to face with Achilles, son of Peleus?”
86	Then Aeneas, answering, spoke to him in turn:
87	“Son of Priam, why do you bid me do this, though I am unwilling,
88	to fight face to face with the high-hearted son of Peleus?
89	For not now for the first time against swift-footed Achilles
90	shall I stand; but already once before he put me to flight with his spear
91	from Ida, when he came upon our cattle
92	and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus; but Zeus
93	rescued me, who roused my might and my nimble knees.
94	Otherwise I would have been brought down at the hands of Achilles and of Athena,
95	who went before him and brought him light, and bade him
96	slay the Leleges and the Trojans with his bronze spear.
97	So no man can fight face to face with Achilles;
98	for always at his side is one of the gods, who keeps ruin away.
99	And even without that, his weapon flies straight, and does not cease
100	until it has passed through a man's skin. But if a god
101	were to stretch the outcome of war evenly, he would not very easily
102	win, not even if he boasts that he is all of bronze.”
103	Then lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him in turn:
104	“Come, hero, you too pray to the everlasting gods;
105	for they say that you too were born of Zeus' daughter Aphrodite,
106	and he is from a lesser goddess;
107	for one is the daughter of Zeus, and the other of the Old Man of the Sea.
108	But carry the unyielding bronze straight at him, and do not let him at all
109	turn you back with wretched words and threats.”
110	So saying, he breathed great might into the shepherd of the people,
111	and he went through the champions, armored in gleaming bronze.
112	Nor did the son of Anchises escape the notice of white-armed Hera
113	as he went to face the son of Peleus through the crowd of men;
114	and she gathered the gods together and spoke among them:
115	“Consider now, you two, Poseidon and Athena,
116	in your minds, how these things are to be.
117	Here is Aeneas, gone forth armored in gleaming bronze
118	to face the son of Peleus, and Phoebus Apollo set him on.
119	But come, let us ourselves turn him back
120	from where he is; or else let one of us stand by Achilles
121	and give him great power, and let him lack nothing in his heart,
122	so that he may know that the best of the immortals love him,
123	and that those are empty wind who before now
124	have warded off the war and the combat from the Trojans.
125	We have all come down from Olympus to take our part
126	in this battle, so that he may suffer nothing among the Trojans
127	today; hereafter he shall suffer whatever his fate
128	spun for him with her thread at his birth, when his mother bore him.
129	But if Achilles does not learn this from the voice of the gods,
130	he will be afraid then, when some god comes against him face to face
131	in battle; for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight.”
132	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered her:
133	“Hera, do not rage beyond good sense; you have no need to.
134	I would not wish that we should drive the gods together in strife,
135	we others, since we are far the stronger.
136	But let us go now and sit down
137	off the path, at a lookout, and war will be the concern of men.
138	But if Ares or Phoebus Apollo begins the battle,
139	or they hold Achilles back and do not let him fight,
140	then at once there will rise for us too, right there, the quarrel
141	of the din of battle; and very soon, I think, they will be parted and
142	go back to Olympus, to the gathering of the other gods,
143	brought down at our hands under compulsion.”
144	So he spoke, and the dark-haired god led the way
145	to the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles,
146	the high wall, which the Trojans and Pallas Athena
147	had made for him, so that he might slip away and escape the sea monster
148	whenever it chased him from the shore toward the plain.
149	There Poseidon sat down, and the other gods,
150	and they wrapped an unbreakable cloud about their shoulders;
151	and the others sat down on the other side, on the brows of Callicolone,
152	around you, archer Phoebus, and Ares, sacker of cities.
153	So they sat on either side, devising
154	their plans; but to begin harsh war
155	both sides were loath, though Zeus, sitting on high, bade them.
156	And the whole plain was filled with them, and it gleamed with bronze,
157	with men and horses; and the earth rumbled beneath their feet
158	as they rushed on together. And two men, by far the best,
159	came together between the two armies, eager to fight,
160	Aeneas, son of Anchises, and brilliant Achilles.
161	Aeneas strode out first, threatening,
162	tossing his heavy helmet; and he held his furious shield
163	before his chest, and shook his bronze spear.
164	And the son of Peleus rushed against him from the other side like a lion,
165	a ravager, whom men are eager to kill,
166	gathered together, a whole people; and at first he goes his way
167	heedless of them; but when one of the young men swift in war
168	hits him with a spear, he crouches with gaping jaws, and foam gathers
169	about his teeth, and his valiant heart groans within him,
170	and with his tail he lashes his ribs and his hips on both sides,
171	and he urges himself on to fight,
172	and glaring he rushes straight on in his fury, to see if he will kill
173	one of the men, or perish himself in the front of the throng;
174	so his might and his proud spirit urged Achilles on
175	to go to meet great-hearted Aeneas.
176	And when they had come close, advancing against each other,
177	brilliant swift-footed Achilles spoke to him first:
178	“Aeneas, why have you come so far out from the throng
179	to stand here? Does your heart bid you fight with me
180	in the hope that you will rule over the horse-taming Trojans
181	with the honor of Priam? But even if you slay me,
182	Priam will not put the prize in your hand for that;
183	for he has sons, and he is steady, and not light of mind.
184	Or have the Trojans marked out for you a domain finer than all others,
185	a fair one, of orchard and plowland, for you to hold,
186	if you kill me? But I think you will find it hard to do.
187	Already, I say, once before I put you to flight with my spear.
188	Do you not remember when you were alone, and I drove you
189	away from the cattle, down the mountains of Ida on swift feet,
190	swiftly? Then you never turned around as you fled.
191	From there you escaped to Lyrnessus; but I
192	sacked it, going after you with Athena and father Zeus,
193	and took away the day of freedom from the women, and led them off
194	as captives; but Zeus and the other gods rescued you.
195	But they will not rescue you now, I think, as you
196	imagine in your heart; no, I bid you draw back
197	and go into the crowd, and do not stand against me,
198	before you suffer some harm; a fool learns a thing once it is done.”
199	Then Aeneas answered him in turn and said:
200	“Son of Peleus, do not think that with words, as if I were a child,
201	you will frighten me, since I too know well myself
202	how to speak both taunts and evil words.
203	We know each other's lineage, and we know each other's parents,
204	hearing the tales mortal men have told of old;
205	but by sight you have never yet seen mine, nor I yours.
206	They say that you are the offspring of noble Peleus,
207	and that your mother is Thetis of the lovely tresses, daughter of the sea;
208	but I claim that I was born the son of great-hearted Anchises,
209	and my mother is Aphrodite.
210	Of these, one pair or the other will weep for their dear son
211	today; for I do not think that with childish words
212	we two shall part like this and come back from the battle.
213	But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well
214	our lineage—and many men know it—
215	first Zeus the cloud-gatherer fathered Dardanus,
216	and he founded Dardania, since sacred Ilios was not yet
217	built in the plain, a city of mortal men,
218	but they still dwelt on the slopes of Ida of the many springs.
219	And Dardanus in turn fathered a son, king Erichthonius,
220	who was the richest of mortal men;
221	three thousand horses of his were pastured along the marshland,
222	mares, glorying in their tender foals.
223	And the North Wind fell in love with them as they grazed,
224	and in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion he lay with them;
225	and they conceived and bore twelve foals.
226	These, when they bounded over the grain-giving earth,
227	would run on the very tips of the ripe ears and not break them;
228	but when they bounded over the broad back of the sea,
229	they would run on the very crests of the breakers of the gray sea.
230	And Erichthonius fathered Tros, lord of the Trojans;
231	and to Tros in turn were born three noble sons,
232	Ilus and Assaracus and godlike Ganymede,
233	who was the most beautiful of mortal men;
234	and the gods snatched him up to pour wine for Zeus,
235	for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals.
236	And Ilus in turn fathered a son, noble Laomedon,
237	and Laomedon fathered Tithonus and Priam,
238	Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, offshoot of Ares;
239	and Assaracus fathered Capys, and he fathered Anchises, his son;
240	and Anchises fathered me, and Priam brilliant Hector.
241	This is the lineage and the blood I claim.
242	But it is Zeus who increases and diminishes the prowess of men,
243	as he wishes; for he is the mightiest of all.
244	But come, let us no longer talk like this, like children,
245	standing in the middle of the battle of combat.
246	For there are insults enough for both of us to speak,
247	a great many, so that a ship of a hundred benches could not carry the load.
248	The tongue of mortals is a twisting thing, and there are many words in it
249	of every kind, and wide is the pasture of words, this way and that.
250	Whatever kind of word you speak, such will you hear back.
251	But what need is there for strife and quarrels between us two,
252	to quarrel with each other face to face, like women
253	who in their anger over heart-devouring strife
254	go out into the middle of the street and quarrel with each other,
255	saying many things, true and not true; for anger bids them say these too.
256	You will not turn me from courage with words, eager as I am,
257	until we have fought face to face with the bronze; but come, quickly,
258	let us taste of each other with our bronze-fitted spears.”
259	He spoke, and drove his heavy spear into the dread shield,
260	the terrible shield; and the shield groaned loudly around the point of the spear.
261	And the son of Peleus held the shield away from him with his strong hand
262	in fear; for he thought that the long-shadowed spear
263	of great-hearted Aeneas would easily pass through it,
264	the fool, nor did he consider in his mind and in his heart
265	that the glorious gifts of the gods are not easy
266	for mortal men to master, nor do they give way.
267	Nor did the heavy spear of wise-hearted Aeneas then
268	break the shield; for the gold held it back, the gifts of the god;
269	but it drove through two layers, and there were still three,
270	since the crook-footed god had beaten out five layers,
271	two of bronze, and two of tin inside,
272	and one of gold, and in that the ash spear was stopped.
273	Then second in his turn Achilles let fly his long-shadowed spear,
274	and struck Aeneas on his shield, balanced on every side,
275	under the outermost rim, where the bronze ran thinnest,
276	and the oxhide on it was thinnest; and right through
277	sped the Pelian ash, and the shield cracked beneath it.
278	And Aeneas crouched, and held the shield up away from him
279	in fear; and the spear, passing over his back, stood fixed in the ground,
280	still straining, and it had parted both circles
281	of the man-covering shield; and he, having avoided the long spear,
282	stood still, and countless grief was poured over his eyes,
283	in fear that the shaft had stuck so near him. But Achilles
284	sprang at him eagerly, drawing his sharp sword,
285	shouting terribly. But he took up a stone in his hand,
286	Aeneas, a huge thing, which two men could not carry,
287	such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone.
288	Then Aeneas would have hit him with the stone as he rushed on,
289	on helmet or on the shield that had kept grim destruction from him,
290	and the son of Peleus would have taken his life with his sword at close quarters,
291	had not Poseidon the earth-shaker been quick to see it;
292	and at once he spoke among the immortal gods:
293	“Ah, truly I have grief for great-hearted Aeneas,
294	who will soon go down to Hades, brought down by the son of Peleus,
295	because he obeyed the words of Apollo the Far-Shooter,
296	the fool, and the god will do nothing to ward off grim destruction from him.
297	But why does this man, who is not to blame, suffer sorrows now
298	for nothing, for the sake of others' griefs, when he always gives pleasing
299	gifts to the gods who hold the wide sky?
300	But come, let us ourselves lead him out from death,
301	lest the son of Cronus too be angry, if Achilles
302	kills this man; for it is fated for him to escape,
303	so that the lineage may not perish without seed and without a trace,
304	the lineage of Dardanus, whom the son of Cronus loved above all the children
305	who were born to him from mortal women.
306	For already the son of Cronus has come to hate the lineage of Priam;
307	and now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans,
308	and his children's children, who will be born hereafter.”
309	Then ox-eyed queen Hera answered him:
310	“Shaker of the earth, consider for yourself in your own mind
311	whether you will rescue Aeneas or let him,
312	brave as he is, be brought down by Achilles, son of Peleus.
313	For we two have sworn many oaths
314	among all the immortals, I and Pallas Athena,
315	never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans,
316	not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire,
317	burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans are burning it.”
318	But when Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this,
319	he went his way through the battle and through the turmoil of spears,
320	and came to where Aeneas and famous Achilles were.
321	At once he poured a mist over the eyes
322	of Achilles, son of Peleus; and he drew the ash spear with its good bronze
323	out of the shield of great-hearted Aeneas,
324	and set it down before the feet of Achilles,
325	and lifting Aeneas high he sent him flying from the ground.
326	And over many ranks of heroes, and many ranks of horses,
327	Aeneas leapt, springing from the hand of the god,
328	and came to the far edge of the furious war,
329	where the Caucones were arming themselves for war.
330	And Poseidon the earth-shaker came very near him,
331	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
332	“Aeneas, which of the gods bids you so recklessly
333	fight face to face with the high-hearted son of Peleus,
334	who is at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals?
335	No, draw back whenever you meet him,
336	lest you come to the house of Hades even beyond your portion.
337	But when Achilles has met his death and his fate,
338	then take heart, and fight among the foremost;
339	for no other of the Achaeans will slay you.”
340	So saying, he left him there, when he had made all this plain to him.
341	And at once he scattered the mist from the eyes of Achilles,
342	the heaven-sent mist; and he stared hard with his eyes,
343	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
344	“Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes:
345	here is my spear lying on the ground, and I do not see the man at all
346	at whom I hurled it, eager to kill him.
347	Truly Aeneas too was dear to the immortal gods;
348	but I thought he boasted idly, all for nothing.
349	Let him go to his ruin! He will have no heart to try me again,
350	he who even now was glad to escape from death.
351	But come, calling on the war-loving Danaans,
352	I will go against the other Trojans and make trial of them.”
353	He spoke, and leapt along the ranks, and called on every man:
354	“Stand no longer far from the Trojans now, brilliant Achaeans,
355	but come, let man go against man, and let him be eager to fight.
356	It is hard for me, mighty though I am,
357	to chase so many men and fight with them all;
358	not even Ares, though he is an immortal god, nor Athena
359	could deal with the mouth of so great a battle and toil through it;
360	but as far as I can, with hands and feet
361	and strength, I say that I will hold back no longer, not even a little,
362	but I will go right through their ranks, and I think no man
363	of the Trojans will rejoice, whoever comes near my spear.”
364	So he spoke, urging them on; and shining Hector called to the Trojans
365	with a shout, and said that he would go against Achilles:
366	“High-hearted Trojans, do not fear the son of Peleus.
367	I too could fight even with the immortals in words,
368	but with the spear it is hard, since they are far stronger than we are.
369	Nor will Achilles put fulfillment to all his words,
370	but one he will fulfill, and another he will cut short halfway.
371	I will go against him, even if his hands are like fire,
372	if his hands are like fire, and his might like gleaming iron.”
373	So he spoke, urging them on, and they raised their spears to face them,
374	the Trojans; and their might mingled together, and the war cry rose.
375	And then Phoebus Apollo stood beside Hector and said:
376	“Hector, no longer step out at all as a champion against Achilles,
377	but wait for him in the crowd and out of the din,
378	lest he hit you with a spear, or strike you with his sword at close quarters.”
379	So he spoke, and Hector went back again into the crowd of men,
380	afraid, when he heard the voice of the god as he spoke.
381	And Achilles leapt among the Trojans, clothed in courage in his heart,
382	shouting terribly, and first he killed Iphition,
383	the brave son of Otrynteus, leader of many people,
384	whom a Naiad nymph bore to Otrynteus, sacker of cities,
385	beneath snowy Tmolus, in the rich land of Hyde;
386	and as he came straight on, eager, brilliant Achilles hit him with his spear
387	full on the head; and it was all split in two,
388	and he fell with a thud; and brilliant Achilles boasted over him:
389	“You lie there, son of Otrynteus, most terrible of all men;
390	here is your death, but your birthplace is by the lake,
391	the Gygaean, where your father's domain is,
392	by the fish-filled Hyllus and the eddying Hermus.”
393	So he spoke, boasting, and darkness covered his eyes.
394	And the horses of the Achaeans tore him apart with the tires of their wheels
395	in the front line of battle; and after him Demoleon,
396	a brave defender against battle, the son of Antenor,
397	he stabbed in the temple, through the bronze-cheeked helmet.
398	Nor did the bronze helmet hold it, but right through it
399	the point, pressing on, broke the bone, and the brain
400	inside was all spattered; and he beat him down for all his eagerness.
401	And then, as Hippodamas leapt down from his chariot,
402	he stabbed him with his spear in the back as he fled before him.
403	And he gasped out his life and bellowed, as when a bull
404	bellows as he is dragged around the Heliconian lord
405	by young men dragging him; and the earth-shaker is glad of them;
406	so as he bellowed, his proud spirit left his bones.
407	But he went with his spear after godlike Polydorus,
408	son of Priam. His father would not let him fight at all,
409	because he was the youngest born among his children,
410	and the dearest to him, and he surpassed them all in speed of foot;
411	and then in his childishness, showing off the prowess of his feet,
412	he rushed through the champions, until he lost his dear life.
413	Brilliant swift-footed Achilles hit him in the middle with a javelin
414	in the back as he darted past, where the golden clasps of the belt
415	held it together, and the corselet met it in a double layer;
416	and the point of the spear went straight through, beside the navel,
417	and he fell to his knees with a groan, and a cloud enfolded him,
418	a dark cloud, and sinking down he clutched his bowels to him with his hands.
419	But when Hector saw his brother Polydorus
420	holding his bowels in his hands, sinking to the ground,
421	a mist was poured over his eyes, and he could no longer bear
422	to range far off for long, but he came against Achilles,
423	brandishing his sharp spear, like a flame; but Achilles,
424	as soon as he saw him, leapt up, and spoke in triumph:
425	“Here near me is the man who has touched my heart most deeply,
426	who killed my honored companion; no longer now
427	will we shrink from each other along the bridges of war.”
428	He spoke, and looking at him darkly he addressed brilliant Hector:
429	“Come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction.”
430	Unafraid, Hector of the gleaming helmet answered him:
431	“Son of Peleus, do not think that with words, as if I were a child,
432	you will frighten me, since I too know well myself
433	how to speak both taunts and evil words.
434	And I know that you are brave, and I am far worse than you.
435	But these things lie on the knees of the gods,
436	whether, worse though I am, I shall take your life
437	hitting you with my spear, since my weapon too is sharp at the point.”
438	He spoke, and poising his spear he let it fly; and Athena
439	turned it back from glorious Achilles with a breath,
440	blowing very gently; and it came back to brilliant Hector,
441	and fell right there before his feet. But Achilles
442	sprang at him in his fury, eager to kill him,
443	shouting terribly; but Apollo snatched him away
444	very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist.
445	Three times then brilliant swift-footed Achilles sprang at him
446	with his bronze spear, and three times he struck the deep mist.
447	But when for the fourth time he rushed on like a god,
448	he shouted at him terribly and addressed him with winged words:
449	“Once again you have escaped death now, you dog; truly, evil came close
450	to you; and now again Phoebus Apollo has saved you,
451	to whom you must pray when you go into the thud of javelins.
452	I will surely finish you off when I meet you later,
453	if there is any god who is a helper to me as well.
454	Now I will go after the others, whomever I can catch.”
455	So saying, he stabbed Dryops in the middle of the neck with a javelin,
456	and he fell before his feet. And he left him there,
457	and Demuchus, son of Philetor, tall and brave,
458	he hit on the knee with his spear and stopped him; and then
459	stabbing him with his great sword he took away his life.
460	And then Laogonus and Dardanus, the two sons of Bias,
461	he rushed upon, and thrust both from their chariot to the ground,
462	hitting the one with his spear, and striking the other with his sword at close quarters.
463	And Tros, son of Alastor—he came to meet him, to clasp his knees,
464	in the hope that he would take him and spare him and let him go alive,
465	and not kill him, pitying a man of his own age,
466	the fool, and he did not know that the other would not be persuaded;
467	for he was no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted,
468	but in a great fury; and he was trying to clasp his knees with his hands,
469	eager to beg him, but the other stabbed him with his sword in the liver;
470	and the liver slipped out of him, and the dark blood from it
471	filled his bosom; and darkness covered his eyes
472	as his life failed; and he went up to Mulius and stabbed him
473	with his spear in the ear; and at once through the other ear went
474	the bronze point. And Echeclus, son of Agenor,
475	he struck full on the head with his hilted sword,
476	and the whole sword grew warm with blood; and his eyes
477	were seized by purple death and mighty fate.
478	And then Deucalion, where the sinews of the elbow
479	hold together, there he pierced through the arm
480	with the bronze point; and he awaited him, his arm weighed down,
481	seeing death before him; and he struck his neck with his sword
482	and sent his head flying far, helmet and all; and the marrow
483	spurted from his vertebrae, and he lay stretched out on the ground.
484	And he went on his way after the noble son of Peirous,
485	Rhigmus, who had come from deep-soiled Thrace;
486	he hit him in the middle with a javelin, and the bronze stuck in his belly,
487	and he fell from the chariot; and Areithous, his attendant,
488	as he turned the horses back, he stabbed in the back with his sharp spear,
489	and thrust him from the chariot; and his horses were thrown into confusion.
490	As a wondrous fire rages through the deep valleys
491	of a dry mountain, and the deep forest burns,
492	and the wind, driving it everywhere, whirls the flame,
493	so he rushed everywhere with his spear, like a god,
494	chasing the men he was killing; and the black earth ran with blood.
495	As when a man yokes broad-browed bulls
496	to tread out white barley on a well-built threshing floor,
497	and quickly it is husked beneath the feet of the loud-bellowing bulls,
498	so beneath great-hearted Achilles his single-hoofed horses
499	trampled corpses and shields alike; and with blood the axle
500	was all spattered beneath, and the rails that ran around the chariot,
501	struck by the drops thrown up from the horses' hooves
502	and from the tires. And he was eager to win glory,
503	the son of Peleus, and his irresistible hands were spattered with gore.
