# Odyssey — translation conventions

The English is a new line-for-line translation by Claude (Anthropic), made from A. T. Murray's Greek
text (Loeb Classical Library, 1919, as digitized by the Perseus Digital Library) in `grc/`. Every
English row carries the number of the Greek verse it renders, so quotations, the index, and the
interlinear reader can address any range of lines in either language. English syntax occasionally
runs across a line break; when that happens the words stay within the same one or two verses as the
Greek.

The Greek is printed as Perseus digitized Murray's text: slips are left in the text and recorded in
the last section. The changes are the punctuation of 3.303, which the numbered order of 3.304–305
requires (see the note on 3.303–305), and the quotation marks of the speeches Menelaus reports at
4.492–555 (see the note there).

American spelling is used throughout (“gray-eyed Athena”, “the wine-dark sea”). In this file a bare
reference (1.183) is a line of the Odyssey; Iliad lines are named (*Iliad* 6.215).

## Principles

1. **Line fidelity.** Each verse is rendered on its own row. Names and key terms are kept on the
   same line as in the Greek wherever possible, because the index links words line by line.
2. **The Iliad's English is the standard.** The Odyssey shares hundreds of verses and formulas with
   the Iliad, and they keep the Iliad's English (see the Iliad's `TRANSLATION.md`): "Then the
   gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him" (1.44 = *Iliad* 5.825), "Our father, son of Cronus,
   highest of lords" (1.45 = *Iliad* 8.31), "what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!"
   (1.64, as *Iliad* 4.350), "But these things lie on the knees of the gods" (1.267 = *Iliad*
   17.514), "they went to lie down, each to his own house" (1.424, as *Iliad* 1.606). Its key words
   keep the Iliad's English too: νόστος "homecoming", ἄλγεα "sorrows", ὀδύρομαι "mourn", ἀμύμων of
   persons "noble", πεπνυμένος "wise", φάρμακον "drug", χαλκήρης "bronze-fitted", κτήματα
   "possessions", ὄρνυμι "rouse" and ὀρίνω "stir".
3. **One Greek formula, one English formula.** The Odyssey's own repeated verses and noun–epithet
   phrases are translated the same way every time; the tables below record them as they first occur.

## Places where the Greek is disputed

Merry is cited from W. W. Merry, J. Riddell and D. B. Monro, *Commentary on the Odyssey* (1886), as
digitized by Perseus (Books 1–3) and as scanned from the Clarendon Press volume at the Internet
Archive (Book 4): "(Merry on 1.241)" is the note on that line.

- **1.1** — πολύτροπον "of many turns": Merry (on 1.1) glosses "of many devices" (*versutus*), a word found again only at 10.330, with many equivalents (πολύμητις, πολύφρων, πολυμήχανος, ποικιλόμητις); Nitzsch took it as πολύπλαγκτος, with ὃς μάλα πολλὰ πλάγχθη as its explanation. The English keeps both the turns of the mind and the wanderings. Livius Andronicus rendered the line "Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum" (Merry on 1.1).
- **1.10** — ἁμόθεν "beginning where you will": the Muse chooses where the singer takes up the story (Merry on 1.10, comparing 8.500); Aristarchus thought the καί of καὶ ἡμῖν superfluous (Merry on 1.10).
- **1.11** — ἔνθα "Now": the point of time at which the Odyssey begins, the end of the seventh year on Calypso's island (Merry on 1.11), though the opening ἔνθʼ ἄλλοι μὲν πάντες is "Then all the other Achaeans" at *Iliad* 1.22. Merry (on 1.16) reckons the ten years from the fall of Troy.
- **1.18–19** — οὐδʼ ἔνθα … καὶ μετὰ οἷσι φίλοισι: for Merry (on 1.19) ἔνθα answers ὅτε δὴ ἔτος ἦλθε (16), and καί after the negative is "or": "Not even then was he safe out of danger or among his friends", as the English takes it. Nitzsch made the words a parenthesis, with the main clause at θεοὶ δʼ ἐλέαιρον; Köchly, after Bekker, rejected 18–19, and critics of the same school struck out 23–26 (Merry on 1.19).
- **1.29** — ἀμύμονος Αἰγίσθοιο "noble Aegisthus": the epithet marks birth or beauty, with no necessary moral sense, "almost titular, like our epithet ‘gallant’" (Merry on 1.29).
- **1.34** — ὑπὲρ μόρον "against fate" (as *Iliad* 21.517): Merry (on 1.34) calls it a popular solution of the problem of divine power and human freedom, a fated share that misconduct may aggravate.
- **1.40** — τίσις Ἀτρεΐδαο, vengeance for the son of Atreus, Agamemnon; Merry (on 1.40) rejects taking Ὀρέσταο Ἀτρεΐδαο together, and notes the change from reported to direct speech, as at 1.276 and 1.374.
- **1.47** — Scipio Africanus quoted the line on the death of Tiberius Gracchus (Merry on 1.47).
- **1.48** — δαΐφρων "wise-hearted", from δαῆναι "learn", not "warlike" from δαΐς (Merry on 1.48, against Buttmann).
- **1.52–54** — Atlas: ὀλοόφρων "baleful", the word of those whose knowledge is feared, as Aeetes and Minos (Merry on 1.52); ἀμφὶς ἔχουσι, either "support at either side" or "keep asunder", earth below and heaven above, which Merry (on 1.53) finds simpler and the English follows. Merry quotes Hesiod (*Theogony* 517, 746) for Atlas holding up the wide sky.
- **1.62** — ὠδύσαο "Why are you so angry with him": a play on the name of Odysseus, "a man of wrath" (Merry on 1.62, comparing 19.275 and 19.407 ff.).
- **1.64** — ἕρκος ὀδόντων "the barrier of your teeth": the teeth themselves, which look like a fence when the lips part, rather than the lips (Merry on 1.64).
- **1.68** — γαιήοχος "the earth-holder": "earth-surrounding" (the older commentators), "earth-supporting", or "lord of earth" (Autenrieth), as Merry (on 1.68) reports.
- **1.70** — ὅου, which Curtius would write ὅο; Bekker's ἔσκε for ἐστί rests on slight authority (Merry on 1.70).
- **1.91** — ἀπειπέμεν "speak out", not "renounce" (Merry on 1.91, against Nitzsch), as μῦθον ἀπηλεγέως ἀποείπω "speak out my word … without regard for anyone" (1.373), and ἀποειπεῖν at *Iliad* 9.309.
- **1.92** — μῆλʼ ἁδινά "thronging sheep" (Merry's "thick-thronging"), where the Iliad's ἁδινός is "swarming" of bees and flies (2.87, 2.469); εἰλίποδας "shambling", of the rolling gait of oxen, against Buttmann's "heavy-treading"; ἕλικας "with curving horns", one of the two senses the ancient lexicon gives, which the Hymn to Hermes (192) supports (Merry on 1.92). The phrase keeps the Iliad's "shambling cattle with curving horns" (*Iliad* 9.466).
- **1.105** — Mentes, leader of the Taphians, island pirates off the Leucadian coast (Merry on 1.105). The phrase Ταφίων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ answers Κικόνων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, the leader of the Cicones whose likeness Apollo takes (*Iliad* 17.73).
- **1.107** — πεσσοί "game pieces": Merry (on 1.107) quotes Athenaeus on the game as the suitors played it, each casting a piece at one set in the middle and called Penelope.
- **1.130** — ὑπὸ λῖτα πετάσσας "spreading a cloth beneath her" is best taken as a parenthesis, so that καλὸν δαιδάλεον goes with θρόνον; λῖτα is an accusative singular, from λίς, not Wolf's and Nitzsch's neuter plural (Merry on 1.130). The next line keeps the Iliad's "fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet" (*Iliad* 18.390).
- **1.132** — κλισμός and θρόνος, both "chair": the θρόνος stood higher, a chair of state, and the κλισμός had a sloping back (Merry on 1.132).
- **1.140** — The line serves elsewhere in the Odyssey for what can be made ready quickly for an unexpected guest, and it was objected to here, where the banquet is about to be served; Merry (on 1.140) answers that Telemachus' separate provision for his guest marks his distance from the suitors.
- **1.152** — μολπή "song": Aristarchus held that in Homer the word means play, above all dancing, not song; Merry (on 1.152), after Lehrs, finds that this passage, with Phemius' lyre, leans to the wider sense. ἀναθήματα "accompaniments", not the later sense of ornaments or offerings to the gods (Merry on 1.152).
- **1.170** — τίς πόθεν: two questions blended into one (Merry on 1.170).
- **1.176** — ἴσαν "used to come", from εἶμι, against Nitzsch's "knew" (Merry on 1.176).
- **1.182** — ὧδε "as you see": Aristarchus held that ὧδε in Homer always means "so", never "here"; Buttmann disputed it (Merry on 1.182).
- **1.184** — Temese: Strabo places it in Bruttium (later Tempsa); Merry (on 1.184) prefers Tamassus in Cyprus, famous for its copper. χαλκός is "bronze", as everywhere.
- **1.185** — ἐπʼ ἀγροῦ "by the fields": whether the ship is drawn up on land or afloat (Merry on 1.185).
- **1.193** — γουνὸν ἀλωῆς οἰνοπέδοιο "the slope of his vineyard": γουνός from γόνυ, a knoll or rising ground (Merry on 1.193).
- **1.216** — The scholia set beside the line sayings of Euripides and Menander on a man's not knowing his father (Merry on 1.216).
- **1.226** — εἰλαπίνη ἠὲ γάμος: by Herodian's rule the second member of a double question takes the circumflex, ἦε (Merry on 1.175, and on 1.226); the text prints ἠὲ, as the digitized Murray does. εἰλαπίνη "banquet", given by one host; ἔρανος "shared feast", held at each house in turn, as the suitors feast at 1.375 (Merry on 1.226).
- **1.227** — ὥς τε: comparative, "like men committing outrage", as Merry (on 1.227) prefers; others take it as final or exclamatory.
- **1.241** — ἀκλειῶς "without report": Merry (on 1.241) holds that κλέος in Homer means "report", "tidings", so that no news of Odysseus has come (ἄιστος ἄπυστος, 1.242); the Iliad's ἀκλειῶς is "without glory" (22.304). ἅρπυιαι, "the snatchers", the storm-winds personified (Merry on 1.241); compare the Harpy Podarge (*Iliad* 16.150).
- **1.259** — Ephyra, the Thesprotian or the Elean (Merry on 1.259); Ilus son of Mermerus is not the Ilus of Troy.
- **1.263** — νεμεσίζετο takes an accusative only here, "feared the indignation of"; poisoned arrows appear nowhere in the Iliad, and the Odyssey alludes to them as impious (Merry on 1.263).
- **1.267** — θεῶν ἐν γούνασι κεῖται: Merry (on 1.267) rejects the suppliant's and the votive explanations and suggests the gods as umpires, with the prizes on their knees.
- **1.275–278** — The anacoluthon of μητέρα δʼ … ἂψ ἴτω (Merry on 1.275). Whether οἱ δέ (277) are the bride's family (Nitzsch, after Eustathius) or, as Merry finds simpler, the suitors; ἔεδνα "bride-price", gifts to the bride's family, which are not a dowry before Pindar (Merry on 1.277).
- **1.276** — Icarius: Aristotle (*Poetics* 25, 1461b4–9) reports the Cephallenians' story that Odysseus married among them and that the man was Icadius, not Icarius (Merry on 1.276).
- **1.293–302** — Nauck marks the lines spurious, and so Hentze–Ameis, as inconsistent with the advice of 1.274–278 (Merry on 1.293).
- **1.318** — ἑλών "taking out", from the store-room, not "choosing" (Merry on 1.318).
- **1.320** — ἀνόπαια: a bird's name, "unseen", "through the smoke-vent", or "upward", which Merry (on 1.320) prefers, after Eustathius and Empedocles.
- **1.327** — The grim homecoming: the anger of Pallas for the outrage of Ajax, son of Oileus, to Cassandra in her temple; at 4.499 his death is laid to his insolence against Poseidon (Merry on 1.326).
- **1.344** — καθʼ Ἑλλάδα καὶ μέσον Ἄργος "through Hellas and the heart of Argos": a phrase for the whole of Greece, north and south (Merry on 1.344).
- **1.349** — ἀλφησταί "toiling": "enterprising, gain-getting" men, or "bread-eating" ones, from ἄλφιτον (Merry on 1.347).
- **1.351–352** — Plato (*Republic* 424b) quotes the lines with ἐπιφρονέουσιν and ἀειδόντεσσι (Merry on 1.351).
- **1.356–359** — Aristarchus rejected the lines; Merry (on 1.360) doubts the rejection, since something in Telemachus' words must account for Penelope's amazement. They are Hector's words to Andromache (*Iliad* 6.490–493), with μῦθος "speech" for πόλεμος "war" and a new close to 359.
- **1.374–375** — Kirchhoff took the lines for an awkward imitation of 2.139 (Merry on 1.374).
- **1.377, 1.380** — νήποινον "without payment"; νήποινοι "unavenged": once warned, the suitors would die in his house with no compensation owed for them (Merry on 1.377).
- **1.391** — ἦ φῂς: Nitzsch preferred ἢ "or" (Merry on 1.391).
- **1.402** — δώμασιν οἷσιν: whether the possessive ὅς may refer to the second person, or σοῖσιν should be read (Merry on 1.402).
- **1.431** — ἐεικοσάβοια "the worth of twenty oxen", a high price for a slave, where a skilled woman is valued at four oxen at *Iliad* 23.705 (Merry on 1.431).
- **1.440** — τρητοῖσι λέχεσσι "the corded bed", as *Iliad* 3.448; Merry (on 1.440) takes τρητός as "morticed", of the frame, citing Plato (*Statesman* 279e), against the holes for cords.
- **1.441–442** — How the door is fastened: a bolt drawn home by a strap through a slit in the door, and a handle (κορώνη) on the outside (Merry on 1.441).
- **2.1** — ῥοδοδάκτυλος "rosy-fingered": Merry (on 2.1) sees perhaps the spreading rays of rosy light before sunrise, like the fingers of an open hand, and quotes Eustathius' allegory of the sun's rays as Dawn's fingers.
- **2.3** — εἵματα ἑσσάμενος: it was the custom to sit up and put on at least part of one's clothing before leaving the bed (Merry on 2.3, comparing *Iliad* 2.42, 10.21).
- **2.5** — ἄντην "to look upon": "in presence", literally "if looked at in the face" (Merry on 2.5, comparing 4.310).
- **2.7, 2.26** — ἀγορή and θῶκος: the council of kings and chiefs was the βουλή or θῶκος, and the ἀγορή the gathering of the people, who assented or dissented (Merry on 2.7, comparing *Iliad* 2.53 and 2.93, and reporting Aristotle's account of the Cretan constitution in the *Politics*). θόωκος is "session", the council of elders (Merry on 2.26, comparing 3.127), kept apart in the English from βουλή "council".
- **2.9** — The line is no tautology: the first clause is the people gathering, the second the assembly fully formed (Merry on 2.9).
- **2.11** — δύω κύνες ἀργοί "two swift dogs": Merry (on 2.11) defends the reading, notes the variant κύνες πόδας ἀργοί, and takes ἀργός of swiftness, not of color, against the older "white" (Eustathius' λευκόποδες).
- **2.13** — θηεῦντο, from a present θηέομαι beside the commoner θεάομαι (Merry on 2.13).
- **2.14** — The elders sit on seats of stone in the place of assembly, and Telemachus, as the king's son, takes his father's seat while they make way (Merry on 2.14, comparing 17.61–63, 20.144–146).
- **2.17** — καὶ γάρ more probably gives the reason why Aegyptius speaks first, his longing for his lost son, than an explanation of his age (Merry on 2.17).
- **2.20** — πύματον: Antiphus was the last of the six companions of Odysseus whom the Cyclops ate (Merry on 2.20, comparing 9.369, and rejecting Eustathius' "last of all men"); the stress may fall on ὡπλίσσατο, since once blinded the Cyclops could no longer wait on himself. ὡπλίσσατο δόρπον is the Cyclops' own formula (9.291, 9.344).
- **2.22** — ἔργα "fields", the farms (Merry on 2.22: *opera rustica*).
- **2.30, 2.42** — στρατοῦ "of the army": the scholia give two senses, an enemy's army or the army that went to Ilios; Ameis takes the second, since Aegyptius longs for his son, but Merry (on 2.30) thinks an invasion or a raid of pirates the likelier cause for calling an assembly in haste. The English keeps the ambiguity. The optative εἴποι (2.31) becomes the subjunctive εἴπω when Telemachus repeats the words (2.43), marking that he would tell such news if he had it (Merry on 2.31).
- **2.33** — ὀνήμενος "may he prosper!": the older commentators supplied εἴη ("may he be blessed"), an ellipse Merry (on 2.33) calls unparalleled in Homer; he prefers Classen's explanation, which takes the participle from a formula of blessing, as οὐλόμενος comes from the curse ὄλοιο.
- **2.34** — The digitized text prints ὅτι; the sense is the relative ὅ τι "whatever", as this text prints it at 5.89 and 8.577 and the Iliad at 14.221, and the English takes it so.
- **2.35** — φήμη "word of omen": a chance utterance whose bearing the speaker does not know; Aegyptius blesses Telemachus unawares, before he has said that he called the assembly (Merry on 2.35, comparing κλεηδών, 18.117, 20.120).
- **2.37** — The scepter was the badge of public office, carried by kings, priests, prophets, heralds and judges; the herald who puts it in a speaker's hand invests him with that office for the time (Merry on 2.37).
- **2.39** — καθαπτόμενος "approaching … with words": earnest or animated address, not necessarily rough, as of an urgent but submissive appeal (10.70) or of an angry retort (18.415; Merry on 2.39, comparing 2.240).
- **2.40** — οὗτος ἀνήρ "that man": not a periphrasis for "I", but "this man (about whom you ask) is not far off", until he adds that it is he (Merry on 2.40).
- **2.45** — With Aristophanes' κακά, printed here, ὅ is "in that": "two evils have fallen upon my house"; with Aristarchus' κακόν, δοιά is "in two ways" or answers the "first" and "far greater" evils of 2.46–48 (Merry on 2.45). The English follows the text printed.
- **2.46, 2.48** — τοίσδεσσιν "here", with a gesture of the hand (Merry on 2.46); νῦν δʼ αὖ καὶ πολὺ μεῖζον understands κακὸν ἔμπεσε, where the balance wants τὸ δέ after τὸ μέν (Merry on 2.48).
- **2.51–52** — The scholia ask why only the suitors of Ithaca, "the men who are the best here", are blamed; and they take εἰς οἶκον to mean that Icarius lived in Ithaca (Merry on 2.51, 2.52, who also cites the legend that made Icarius king of Cephallenia, *Poetics* 25: see the note on 1.276).
- **2.53** — ἐεδνώσαιτο "settle the bride-price": not "to dower" his daughter, a later usage, but to accept gifts of wooing for her, or "to make terms about the marriage" (Merry on 2.53, after Butcher and Lang and Cobet; see the note on 1.275–278).
- **2.60** — ἦ καὶ ἔπειτα "truly, even then": ἔπειτα, as distinguished from ὀπίσσω "hereafter", points to the immediate future: if we do (καί) try, we shall prove weaklings (Merry on 2.60, comparing 1.65 and 2.273, 2.280, where the English has "then" as well).
- **2.107** — The same verse at 19.152 and 24.142 is followed by μηνῶν φθινόντων, περὶ δʼ ἤματα πόλλʼ ἐτελέσθη, which this passage lacks.
- **2.141** — ὑμῖν here, ὕμιν in the identical verse at 1.376, as the digitized text prints them.
- **2.167** — ἐυδείελος "clear-seen" (provisional): the word recurs of Ithaca and islands (9.21, 13.212, 13.325, 19.132); δείελος "evening" (*Iliad* 21.232) bears on the alternative sense "lying toward the west".
- **2.172** — εἰσανέβαινον "were embarking", as ἀναβήμεναι "embarked" (1.210); the Iliad's Ἴλιον εἰσανέβησαν is "gone up into Ilios" (6.74).
- **2.227** — γέροντι: either Mentor, whom the household is to obey, or Laertes; neither infinitive has a subject in the Greek, and the English makes the house obey the old man and Mentor keep all things safe.
- **2.245** — ἀνδράσι καὶ πλεόνεσσι goes with μαχήσασθαι, of the suitors who outnumber the people (as 2.251), or with ἀργαλέον, "hard even for men who are more numerous".
- **2.262** — θεός is a predicate: Athena came in the likeness of Mentes, and Telemachus knew her for a god (1.323, 1.420).
- **2.270, 2.278** — οὐδʼ ὄπιθεν … οὐδʼ ἀνοήμων: "no coward and no fool" hereafter, or "not hereafter either", as he has not been until now.
- **2.344** — ἔπεσαν is from ἔπειμι, "were on it", not from πίπτω.
- **2.377** — γρῆυς, as the digitized text prints it here and at 7.8; γρηῢς in the same opening at 18.185, 19.361, 19.386, 19.503, 22.433. ἀπώμνυ is "swore not to".
- **2.385** — ἀγέρεσθαι is accented as a present infinitive, though Homer's middle forms of the verb are aorists (ἀγέροντο, *Iliad* 2.94, 18.245).
- **2.412** — δμωαί, without the iota subscript, as the digitized text prints it here and at 6.307, 7.103 (δμῳαί elsewhere).
- **2.434** — ἠῶ "through the dawn": time over which, or "and at dawn".
- **3.1** — λίμνην "water": the sea, here the farther margin of the Ocean stream, "water which washes a shore" (Merry on 3.1, who reports that Nitzsch refuted Voss's view that the Caspian is meant).
- **3.4** — Pylos: Strabo argued for the Triphylian Pylos; the scholia, Eustathius and Pindar (*Pythian* 6.35, "the Messenian old man") for the Messenian, which Merry prefers (on 3.4).
- **3.7** — The nine companies of five hundred answer to the nine cities of Nestor, and 4,500 men to his ninety ships at fifty a ship (Merry on 3.7, after the scholia and *Iliad* 2.719).
- **3.8** — προύχοντο, as the digitized text prints it, for προὔχοντο (Merry): "held before them", the bulls set out ready for the sacrifice (Merry on 3.8, comparing *Iliad* 17.355).
- **3.11** — The sail is furled by brailing it up to the yard, not lowered and stowed (Merry on 3.11, who reports the reading σεῖσαν ascribed to Zenodotus).
- **3.95** — πλαζομένου goes with μῦθον and refers to Odysseus, as the following γάρ shows: "word of him … as he wandered" (Merry on 3.95).
- **3.124–125** — ἐοικότες … ἐοικότα "like his", "so like him", not "fitting" (Nitzsch): Merry (on 3.124), from ὧδε and from 4.141–143 and 19.380–381.
- **3.146** — The verse is *Iliad* 20.466, where the one who would not be persuaded is Achilles; here it is Athena ("she").
- **3.171** — ἐπὶ Ψυρίης "by the island of Psyria", keeping Psyria itself on the left, the straight course from Lesbos to Geraestus (Merry on 3.169); the scholia, he reports, were unsure whether αὐτήν is Psyria or Chios.
- **3.199** — καὶ σὺ φίλος, without the comma after σύ that the same verse has at 1.301 (and the same opening at 3.313), as the digitized text prints it.
- **3.204** — καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι is Wolf's reading from Eustathius; the manuscripts have καὶ ἐσσομένοισιν ἀοιδήν, "a song even for men to come" (Merry on 3.204).
- **3.244–245** — περὶ … οἶδε … ἄλλων "knows better than others", with δίκας "rules of right" (Merry on 3.244). ἀνάξασθαι is the only middle of ἀνάσσω in Homer: Merry (on 3.245) suspects the line, reports Nauck's conjecture ἀνασσέμεναι, and, if it is genuine, takes γένεʼ as duration, "has been king for three generations of men", the tradition of *Iliad* 1.250–252.
- **3.246** — ὥς τε "like", comparative (Merry on 3.246, referring to his note on 1.227), not the later consecutive ὥστε.
- **3.269** — μιν: Clytemnestra, "fettered" to yield, as the English takes it with Eustathius' preferred view; Merry (on 3.269) thinks Aegisthus, "fate-bound to his ruin", the simplest, and Nitzsch took it of the singer.
- **3.290** — τροφέοντο "swelled", as the digitized text prints it; Merry's text reads τροφόεντα "big", which would make the waves a second object of "poured" (Merry on 3.290).
- **3.303–305** — Murray prints 305 (ἑπτάετες δʼ ἤνασσε …) before 304 (κτείνας Ἀτρεΐδην …), and so does Merry's text, after Bergk and La Roche and the scholiast on Sophocles' *Electra* 267, who quotes "Aegisthus ruled over Mycenae rich in gold, having killed the son of Atreus" (Merry on 3.304). The rows of this text must run in the order of their numbers, so they keep the manuscripts' order, in which ταῦτα "throws its meaning forward" and κτείνας explains it (Merry): the full stop after λυγρά (303) becomes a comma, the one change made to the digitized text, and the English reads "devised these grim things at home, / killing the son of Atreus, …".
- **3.307** — ἀπʼ Ἀθηνάων "back from Athens": the tragic legend brings Orestes from Phocis, and Aeschylus sends him to Athens only after the deed (Merry on 3.307).
- **3.309–310** — The scholia say these lines were missing in some editions, and report Aristarchus' view that they hint that Clytemnestra died with Aegisthus, though not plainly by Orestes' hand (Merry on 3.309).
- **3.335** — The raised dot after ἔοικεν, as the digitized text prints it; the infinitives of 3.336 depend on ἔοικεν.
- **3.348** — ἦ, as the digitized text prints it; Merry (on 3.348) construes the line with ἢ, as 19.109 prints ὥς τέ τευ ἢ βασιλῆος.
- **3.372** — φήνη "sea eagle": named with vultures at 16.217, "commonly taken to be the sea-eagle", or the lammergeier (Merry on 3.372); after Seiler, he takes εἰδομένη of bodily likeness, so that the goddess is seen to change.
- **3.384, 3.437** — περιχεύας, περίχευεν "poured around": gold leaf spread round the horns, not molten metal, since the smith brings anvil, hammer and tongs (Merry on 3.384, 3.437).
- **3.400** — πὰρʼ δʼ, as the digitized text prints it, for πὰρ δʼ (3.267, 3.416, 3.482).
- **3.442** — No stop after κανέῳ in the digitized text, though a new clause begins with πέλεκυν δέ.
- **3.453** — ἀνελόντες has no object: they lift the victim's head to bare the throat (Merry on 3.453, comparing *Iliad* 1.459).
- **4.1** — κητώεσσαν "with its many ravines" (*Iliad* 2.581): Zenodotus read καιετάεσσαν (Merry's critical note, from the scholia and Eustathius). Merry (on 4.1) derives κητώεις from κῆτος, "gulf", hence "cavernous", and with Tozer takes it of the many rifts and fissures of the valley, citing Strabo on the καιετοί, the rifts left by earthquakes in Laconia.
- **4.3** — ἔται "kinsmen", as in the Iliad (6.239, 9.464, 16.456). The scholia explain the word as πολῖται "citizens", Apollonius as ἑταῖροι, and Nitzsch as near relations or dependents; Merry (on 4.3) argues from the Iliad's places that it names neither kinsmen nor ἑταῖροι but a wider bond that women shared, short of the whole people: "perhaps *aequales*, persons of the same generation".
- **4.3–19** — Athenaeus (5.180, as Merry reports him on 4.3–19 and 4.15–19) says that Diodorus the Aristophanean struck out the wedding, and that the singer and the tumblers (4.15–19) were brought in from the Shield (*Iliad* 18.604–606) by Aristarchus' school; a scholium calls the five lines Aristarchus', not Homer's (Merry's critical note). Merry answers that without 4.3–19 the passage from 4.2 to 4.20 is very awkward.
- **4.10** — Σπάρτηθεν "of Sparta" goes with Ἀλέκτορος, not with ἤγετο, for the wedding was at Sparta (Merry on 4.10).
- **4.12** — δούλης "a slave woman": some read Δούλης as a name (scholia, in Merry's critical note); she was not a slave born, Merry says, but a captive (on 4.12).
- **4.19** — μολπῆς ἐξάρχοντος, singular: the singer leads the song and dance (Merry on 4.19), where the Shield has the plural, of the tumblers (ἐξάρχοντες, *Iliad* 18.606).
- **4.57–58, 4.62–64** — Most editors since Wolf reject 4.57–58, which the Harleian and several other manuscripts lack; 4.62–64 were athetized, as already by Zenodotus and Aristophanes, for the monosyllable σφῷν, a contracted dual found only here (Merry's critical note, and on 4.62).
- **4.73** — ἤλεκτρον "amber": Pliny took it for electrum, gold alloyed with silver, and cited this verse; Merry (on 4.73) finds that tempting between gold and silver, but the necklaces strung with ἤλεκτρα (15.460, 18.296) require amber.
- **4.74** — αὐλή "court": Athenaeus (188F) quotes Seleucus' reading τοιαῦτα δόμοις …, without the court (Merry's critical note); if the word keeps its proper sense, Telemachus turns back from the splendours of the hall to the court (Merry on 4.74).
- **4.84** — Ἐρεμβούς is Aristarchus' reading; Crates read Ἐρεμνούς (Merry's critical note).
- **4.85** — ἄφαρ κεραοὶ τελέθουσι "are horned at once": Herodotus (4.29) quotes the verse, with ὅθι for ἵνα, as rightly said, since horns grow quickly in hot lands; Merry (on 4.85) takes τελέθω here as "be", πέλω.
- **4.89** — ἐπηετανόν "unfailing": the old explanation "lasting out the year" (ἐπί, ἔτος); Curtius, after Döderlein, derives it from ἐπί and αἰεί, "perpetual" (Merry on 4.89).
- **4.113** — ὑφʼ ἵμερον ὦρσε γόοιο "roused the longing for lament": Merry (on 4.113) says that Aristotle quotes "this line" (*Rhetoric* 1.11.12) for the pleasure there is in mourning, but the verse the *Rhetoric* quotes, τοῖσι δὲ πᾶσιν ὑφʼ ἵμερον ὦρσε γόοιο (1370b29), is 4.183, the same as *Iliad* 23.108.
- **4.119** — ἕκαστά τε πειρήσαιτο "put every point to the test": ἕκαστα is the object, "should test all he said" (Merry on 4.119), not "in every particular" with αὐτοῦ understood (Ameis).
- **4.122** — χρυσηλάκατος "of the golden distaff", as in the Iliad (16.183, 20.70), and here beside Helen's own golden distaff (4.131); Merry (on 4.122) thinks the Iliad's company of epithets requires "with golden arrows".
- **4.140** — ψεύσομαι, ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω; "Shall I be wrong, or speak the truth?", as *Iliad* 10.534: Merry (on 4.140) argues from the Iliad's verse that the choice is between speaking the truth and saying nothing, "Shall I withhold the truth?", against the common "Shall I be mistaken?" (Ameis).
- **4.151–152** — ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆι goes with μυθεόμην, "I was speaking about Odysseus", while μεμνημένος "remembering" stands by itself (Merry on 4.151).
- **4.158–167** — Rhianus' text lacked 4.158–160, and the scholia report the athetesis of five lines as superfluous and unfit for a young man to speak; others rejected 4.163 (Merry's critical note, who reports Ameis' view that the athetesis concerns 4.163–167).
- **4.192** — Aristarchus rejected the verse (scholia, in Merry's critical note), and Wolf and the modern editors except Fäsi follow him; Merry (on 4.192) keeps it, taking οἷσιν ἐνὶ μεγάροισι with Nestor's saying, "in his own halls".
- **4.194** — μεταδόρπιος "after supper": Telemachus came at sunset (3.497), so the meal is the evening δόρπον; Peisistratus does not like to end the day in weeping, and Menelaus takes up the meal again (4.213) (Merry on 4.194, with Lehrs, for whom the word can mean nothing else). Nitzsch and most commentators take it as "during supper".
- **4.220–232** — The drug: Merry (on 4.220) reports Dioscorides (4.28) on bugloss cast into wine to cheer, the view of Sprengel and of Mure that it was opium, of Sir H. Halford that it was henbane, and the allegory of Plutarch (*Table Talk* 1.1.4) and Macrobius (*Saturnalia* 7.1), who took it for Helen's eloquence.
- **4.248** — δέκτῃ "a beggar", as Aristarchus took it (ἐπαίτῃ); the cyclic poet read Δέκτης as a name (scholia, in Merry's critical note).
- **4.285–289** — Hardly any edition had the five lines, and Aristarchus rejected them, since the Iliad does not mention Anticlus (Merry's critical note); Merry (on 4.285) adds that they agree ill with 4.282–284 and 4.275, and reports a scholium that Anticlus came from the Cycle.
- **4.325** — περὶ, as the digitized text prints it, where the same verse at 3.95 has πέρι; Merry's text also has περὶ here.
- **4.339** — ἀμφοτέροισι "on both of them": Aristophanes took it of the hind and her young; Merry (on 4.339) prefers Eustathius' remark that the hind has twins, so that the many suitors are not likened to one fawn.
- **4.353** — Zenodotus rejected the line, asking what commands there had been (scholia, in Merry's critical note); for Merry it is a saying added by a later rhapsode.
- **4.366** — Εἰδοθέη: Zenodotus read Εὐρυνόμη, and the scholia add that Aeschylus in his *Proteus* named her Εἰδοθέα (Merry's critical note).
- **4.400** — ἀμφιβεβήκῃ, the subjunctive, is Bekker's reading from a Vienna manuscript, for ἀμφιβεβήκει (Merry's critical note).
- **4.404** — νέποδες "brood": "web-footed" (Apollonius, the *Etymologicum Magnum*) or "footless" (other grammarians); Merry (on 4.404) refers the word to the root of Latin nepos.
- **4.412** — πεμπάσσεται "will count them off by fives", properly on the fingers (Merry on 4.412).
- **4.441** — ἔνθα κεν "There … would have been": the scholia's lemma is κεῖθι δὴ αἰνότατος, adding that most copies have ἔνθα κεν, which Bekker restored (Merry's critical note).
- **4.456** — ἠυγένειος "bearded" (*Iliad* 15.275); Merry (on 4.456) suspects a lengthened form of εὐγενής, "noble".
- **4.465** — παρατροπέων "trying to mislead me", transitive, as παρατρωπάω at *Iliad* 9.500 (Merry on 4.465); Nitzsch took it intransitively, "shirking the truth". Aristarchus read ἐρεείνεις, the text's word, not ἀγορεύεις (Merry's critical note).
- **4.477, 4.581** — διιπετέος ποταμοῖο "the river fallen from Zeus" (*Iliad* 16.174): Eustathius explains it as "rain-fed", since the Nile is filled by the heavy summer rains in Ethiopia, "as Aristotle and Eudoxus say" (Merry on 4.477).
- **4.489** — ἀδευκέι "bitter": the older rendering, from δεῦκος, "sweet" in Aetolian (scholia); Apollonius explains it as "unseemly", Hesychius as "unexpected" (Merry on 4.489).
- **4.492–555** — The quotation marks of the speeches Menelaus reports inside his tale (4.333–592) are single throughout: the digitized text gave nine of the thirteen single marks, and four double or mixed (no opening mark at 492, ” at 537, “ ” at 543–547 and 551–553, ‘“ at 555), which the rows correct.
- **4.500** — Γυρῇσιν "the Gyrae", rocks named for their rounded shape (Γυραίῃ, 4.507): Eustathius and Hesychius place them near Myconos, Quintus of Smyrna (14.560) off Caphereus in Euboea, which Merry thinks more correct (on 4.499).
- **4.511** — The scholia say that the line was in no edition and call it very cheap; Nitzsch rejects it on much the same ground (Merry on 4.510–511 and his critical note).
- **4.514–520** — What brought Agamemnon near Malea? Merry (on 4.514) weighs the course by the islands, reports that Nitzsch held 4.514–516 an interpolation and that Bothe would move 4.519–520, and notes that the difficulty disappears if, with the geographer Andron, Thyestes and Aegisthus after him had their home on Cythera.
- **4.536** — None of Agamemnon's companions survived, nor one of Aegisthus' men; the story here differs from the later form at 11.405 and following and at 24.97, where Clytemnestra plays so great a part (Merry on 4.536).
- **4.553** — Generally rejected (Merry's critical note); Eustathius defends it as the doubt of a man confused by grief (Merry on 4.553).
- **4.563** — Ἠλύσιον: Apion derived the name from the mud (ἰλύς) of the Nile, so that he may have read Ἰλύσιον (Merry's critical note, from Eustathius); Merry (on 4.563) suggests ἠλυθ-, "where men go".
- **4.567** — The *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle quote the verse as ἀλλʼ αἰεὶ ζεφύροιο διαπνείουσιν ἀῆται, "but always the breezes of the West Wind blow through", and ask why the west wind is the fairest and sweetest of the winds (26.31). The breeze shows that the Elysian plain belongs to the land of the living (Merry on 4.567).
- **4.569** — σφιν "in their eyes": the gods count Menelaus the son-in-law of Zeus, an ethical dative (Merry on 4.569).
- **4.572, 4.574** — ἤια, and νύξ, with commas, as the digitized text prints them, where the same verses at 4.427 and 4.429 end in raised dots.
- **4.590** — τρεῖς ἵππους "three horses": a pair and a trace-horse (scholia, in Merry on 4.590; compare *Iliad* 16.149–154).
- **4.594** — Telemachus asks to leave at once, yet the reckoning of the later books keeps him at Sparta for a month (Merry on 4.594).
- **4.603** — λωτός "lotus": a kind of clover, not the lotus of Book 9 (Merry on 4.603).
- **4.617** — Φαίδιμος, printed as a name: the scholia say it is uncertain whether φαίδιμος "glorious" is a proper name (Merry's critical note).
- **4.620–624** — Most editors since Wolf reject the lines as a patch, since a shared feast (ἔρανος) is another thing from the wedding feast of 4.3; Merry (on 4.621–624) rejects Eustathius' view that the lines describe what was going on in Ithaca.
- **4.641** — Ἐυπείθεος, as the digitized text prints it here only (Εὐπείθεος at 4.660 and 1.383), and without the comma after προσέφη that the same verse has at 1.383.
- **4.655–656** — Noemon saw Mentor "yesterday at daybreak" (χθιζὸν ὑπηοῖον), the morning after the ship sailed: the scenes in Ithaca (4.625–847) fall on the fourth day of the poem's action, while Telemachus drives from Pylos to Pherae, though the poem tells them after the day in Sparta (see structure/04.json).
- **4.668** — πρὶν ἥβης μέτρον ἱκέσθαι is Aristarchus' reading; the common text had πρὶν ἡμῖν πῆμα γενέσθαι, or φυτεῦσαι (Merry's critical note and on 4.668).
- **4.678** — αὐλῆς ἐκτὸς ἐών "being outside the courtyard": Merry (on 4.678) takes αὐλῆς as a genitive of place, "in the yard, outside" the hall.
- **4.726** — The scholia call the verse superfluous (cp. 1.344; Merry's critical note). The digitized text ends it with a full stop, though the sentence runs on from μέν (4.724) to αὖ (4.727), and the same verse at 4.816 ends in a raised dot.
- **4.774–775** — πάντας ὁμῶς goes with μύθους, "all of it alike"; Nitzsch needlessly reads πάντες (Merry on 4.774).
- **4.784** — τεύχεα "arms": the weapons the suitors take against Telemachus, not the ship's tackle (Merry on 4.784).
- **4.785** — ἐν νοτίῳ "out in the water": Aristophanes read εἰνοδίῳ, "ready for the voyage" (Merry's critical note); the ship lies afloat, moored to the shore at the stern and to the anchor-stones at the bow, ready to sail (Merry on 4.785).
- **4.797** — Ἰφθίμη: Aristarchus doubted whether ἰφθίμη is an epithet or a name (Merry's critical note).
- **4.846** — Asteris: Merry rejects the modern identification with the rock of Daskalio (on 4.846, referring to his Appendix on Ithaca).
