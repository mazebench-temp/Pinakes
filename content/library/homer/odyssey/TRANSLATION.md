# Odyssey — translation conventions

The English is a new line-for-line translation by Claude (Anthropic), made from A. T. Murray's Greek
text (Loeb Classical Library, 1919, as digitized by the Perseus Digital Library) in `grc/`. Every
English row carries the number of the Greek verse it renders, so quotations, the index, and the
interlinear reader can address any range of lines in either language. English syntax occasionally
runs across a line break; when that happens the words stay within the same one or two verses as the
Greek.

The Greek is printed as Perseus digitized Murray's text: slips are left in the text and recorded in
the last section. The changes are the punctuation of 3.303, which the numbered order of 3.304–305
requires (see the note on 3.303–305), and the quotation marks of the speeches Menelaus reports at
4.492–555 (see the note there).

American spelling is used throughout (“gray-eyed Athena”, “the wine-dark sea”). In this file a bare
reference (1.183) is a line of the Odyssey; Iliad lines are named (*Iliad* 6.215).

## Principles

1. **Line fidelity.** Each verse is rendered on its own row. Names and key terms are kept on the
   same line as in the Greek wherever possible, because the index links words line by line.
2. **The Iliad's English is the standard.** The Odyssey shares hundreds of verses and formulas with
   the Iliad, and they keep the Iliad's English (see the Iliad's `TRANSLATION.md`): "Then the
   gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him" (1.44 = *Iliad* 5.825), "Our father, son of Cronus,
   highest of lords" (1.45 = *Iliad* 8.31), "what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!"
   (1.64, as *Iliad* 4.350), "But these things lie on the knees of the gods" (1.267 = *Iliad*
   17.514), "they went to lie down, each to his own house" (1.424, as *Iliad* 1.606). Its key words
   keep the Iliad's English too: νόστος "homecoming", ἄλγεα "sorrows", ὀδύρομαι "mourn", ἀμύμων of
   persons "noble", πεπνυμένος "wise", φάρμακον "drug", χαλκήρης "bronze-fitted", κτήματα
   "possessions", ὄρνυμι "rouse" and ὀρίνω "stir".
3. **One Greek formula, one English formula.** The Odyssey's own repeated verses and noun–epithet
   phrases are translated the same way every time; the tables below record them as they first occur.

## Places where the Greek is disputed

Merry is cited from W. W. Merry, J. Riddell and D. B. Monro, *Commentary on the Odyssey* (1886), as
digitized by Perseus (1.1–2.66 and Book 3) and as scanned from the Clarendon Press volume at the
Internet Archive (2.67–434, Books 4 and 5, and his Appendix I, "The Homeric Ship"): "(Merry on 1.241)"
is the note on that line, "(Merry, Appendix I §6)" a section of the appendix.

- **1.1** — πολύτροπον "of many turns": Merry (on 1.1) glosses "of many devices" (*versutus*), a word found again only at 10.330, with many equivalents (πολύμητις, πολύφρων, πολυμήχανος, ποικιλόμητις); Nitzsch took it as πολύπλαγκτος, with ὃς μάλα πολλὰ πλάγχθη as its explanation. The English keeps both the turns of the mind and the wanderings. Livius Andronicus rendered the line "Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum" (Merry on 1.1).
- **1.10** — ἁμόθεν "beginning where you will": the Muse chooses where the singer takes up the story (Merry on 1.10, comparing 8.500); Aristarchus thought the καί of καὶ ἡμῖν superfluous (Merry on 1.10).
- **1.11** — ἔνθα "Now": the point of time at which the Odyssey begins, the end of the seventh year on Calypso's island (Merry on 1.11), though the opening ἔνθʼ ἄλλοι μὲν πάντες is "Then all the other Achaeans" at *Iliad* 1.22. Merry (on 1.16) reckons the ten years from the fall of Troy.
- **1.18–19** — οὐδʼ ἔνθα … καὶ μετὰ οἷσι φίλοισι: for Merry (on 1.19) ἔνθα answers ὅτε δὴ ἔτος ἦλθε (16), and καί after the negative is "or": "Not even then was he safe out of danger or among his friends", as the English takes it. Nitzsch made the words a parenthesis, with the main clause at θεοὶ δʼ ἐλέαιρον; Köchly, after Bekker, rejected 18–19, and critics of the same school struck out 23–26 (Merry on 1.19).
- **1.29** — ἀμύμονος Αἰγίσθοιο "noble Aegisthus": the epithet marks birth or beauty, with no necessary moral sense, "almost titular, like our epithet ‘gallant’" (Merry on 1.29).
- **1.34** — ὑπὲρ μόρον "against fate" (as *Iliad* 21.517): Merry (on 1.34) calls it a popular solution of the problem of divine power and human freedom, a fated share that misconduct may aggravate.
- **1.40** — τίσις Ἀτρεΐδαο, vengeance for the son of Atreus, Agamemnon; Merry (on 1.40) rejects taking Ὀρέσταο Ἀτρεΐδαο together, and notes the change from reported to direct speech, as at 1.276 and 1.374.
- **1.47** — Scipio Africanus quoted the line on the death of Tiberius Gracchus (Merry on 1.47).
- **1.48** — δαΐφρων "wise-hearted", from δαῆναι "learn", not "warlike" from δαΐς (Merry on 1.48, against Buttmann).
- **1.52–54** — Atlas: ὀλοόφρων "baleful", the word of those whose knowledge is feared, as Aeetes and Minos (Merry on 1.52); ἀμφὶς ἔχουσι, either "support at either side" or "keep asunder", earth below and heaven above, which Merry (on 1.53) finds simpler and the English follows. Merry quotes Hesiod (*Theogony* 517, 746) for Atlas holding up the wide sky.
- **1.62** — ὠδύσαο "Why are you so angry with him": a play on the name of Odysseus, "a man of wrath" (Merry on 1.62, comparing 19.275 and 19.407 ff.).
- **1.64** — ἕρκος ὀδόντων "the barrier of your teeth": the teeth themselves, which look like a fence when the lips part, rather than the lips (Merry on 1.64).
- **1.68** — γαιήοχος "the earth-holder": "earth-surrounding" (the older commentators), "earth-supporting", or "lord of earth" (Autenrieth), as Merry (on 1.68) reports.
- **1.70** — ὅου, which Curtius would write ὅο; Bekker's ἔσκε for ἐστί rests on slight authority (Merry on 1.70).
- **1.91** — ἀπειπέμεν "speak out", not "renounce" (Merry on 1.91, against Nitzsch), as μῦθον ἀπηλεγέως ἀποείπω "speak out my word … without regard for anyone" (1.373), and ἀποειπεῖν at *Iliad* 9.309.
- **1.92** — μῆλʼ ἁδινά "thronging sheep" (Merry's "thick-thronging"), where the Iliad's ἁδινός is "swarming" of bees and flies (2.87, 2.469); εἰλίποδας "shambling", of the rolling gait of oxen, against Buttmann's "heavy-treading"; ἕλικας "with curving horns", one of the two senses the ancient lexicon gives, which the Hymn to Hermes (192) supports (Merry on 1.92). The phrase keeps the Iliad's "shambling cattle with curving horns" (*Iliad* 9.466).
- **1.105** — Mentes, leader of the Taphians, island pirates off the Leucadian coast (Merry on 1.105). The phrase Ταφίων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ answers Κικόνων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, the leader of the Cicones whose likeness Apollo takes (*Iliad* 17.73).
- **1.107** — πεσσοί "game pieces": Merry (on 1.107) quotes Athenaeus on the game as the suitors played it, each casting a piece at one set in the middle and called Penelope.
- **1.130** — ὑπὸ λῖτα πετάσσας "spreading a cloth beneath her" is best taken as a parenthesis, so that καλὸν δαιδάλεον goes with θρόνον; λῖτα is an accusative singular, from λίς, not Wolf's and Nitzsch's neuter plural (Merry on 1.130). The next line keeps the Iliad's "fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet" (*Iliad* 18.390).
- **1.132** — κλισμός and θρόνος, both "chair": the θρόνος stood higher, a chair of state, and the κλισμός had a sloping back (Merry on 1.132).
- **1.140** — The line serves elsewhere in the Odyssey for what can be made ready quickly for an unexpected guest, and it was objected to here, where the banquet is about to be served; Merry (on 1.140) answers that Telemachus' separate provision for his guest marks his distance from the suitors.
- **1.152** — μολπή "song": Aristarchus held that in Homer the word means play, above all dancing, not song; Merry (on 1.152), after Lehrs, finds that this passage, with Phemius' lyre, leans to the wider sense. ἀναθήματα "accompaniments", not the later sense of ornaments or offerings to the gods (Merry on 1.152).
- **1.170** — τίς πόθεν: two questions blended into one (Merry on 1.170).
- **1.176** — ἴσαν "used to come", from εἶμι, against Nitzsch's "knew" (Merry on 1.176).
- **1.182** — ὧδε "as you see": Aristarchus held that ὧδε in Homer always means "so", never "here"; Buttmann disputed it (Merry on 1.182).
- **1.184** — Temese: Strabo places it in Bruttium (later Tempsa); Merry (on 1.184) prefers Tamassus in Cyprus, famous for its copper. χαλκός is "bronze", as everywhere.
- **1.185** — ἐπʼ ἀγροῦ "by the fields": whether the ship is drawn up on land or afloat (Merry on 1.185).
- **1.193** — γουνὸν ἀλωῆς οἰνοπέδοιο "the slope of his vineyard": γουνός from γόνυ, a knoll or rising ground (Merry on 1.193).
- **1.216** — The scholia set beside the line sayings of Euripides and Menander on a man's not knowing his father (Merry on 1.216).
- **1.226** — εἰλαπίνη ἠὲ γάμος: by Herodian's rule the second member of a double question takes the circumflex, ἦε (Merry on 1.175, and on 1.226); the text prints ἠὲ, as the digitized Murray does. εἰλαπίνη "banquet", given by one host; ἔρανος "shared feast", held at each house in turn, as the suitors feast at 1.375 (Merry on 1.226).
- **1.227** — ὥς τε: comparative, "like men committing outrage", as Merry (on 1.227) prefers; others take it as final or exclamatory.
- **1.241** — ἀκλειῶς "without report": Merry (on 1.241) holds that κλέος in Homer means "report", "tidings", so that no news of Odysseus has come (ἄιστος ἄπυστος, 1.242); the Iliad's ἀκλειῶς is "without glory" (22.304). ἅρπυιαι, "the snatchers", the storm-winds personified (Merry on 1.241); compare the Harpy Podarge (*Iliad* 16.150).
- **1.259** — Ephyra, the Thesprotian or the Elean (Merry on 1.259); Ilus son of Mermerus is not the Ilus of Troy.
- **1.263** — νεμεσίζετο takes an accusative only here, "feared the indignation of"; poisoned arrows appear nowhere in the Iliad, and the Odyssey alludes to them as impious (Merry on 1.263).
- **1.267** — θεῶν ἐν γούνασι κεῖται: Merry (on 1.267) rejects the suppliant's and the votive explanations and suggests the gods as umpires, with the prizes on their knees.
- **1.275–278** — The anacoluthon of μητέρα δʼ … ἂψ ἴτω (Merry on 1.275). Whether οἱ δέ (277) are the bride's family (Nitzsch, after Eustathius) or, as Merry finds simpler, the suitors; ἔεδνα "bride-price", gifts to the bride's family, which are not a dowry before Pindar (Merry on 1.277).
- **1.276** — Icarius: Aristotle (*Poetics* 25, 1461b4–9) reports the Cephallenians' story that Odysseus married among them and that the man was Icadius, not Icarius (Merry on 1.276).
- **1.293–302** — Nauck marks the lines spurious, and so Hentze–Ameis, as inconsistent with the advice of 1.274–278 (Merry on 1.293).
- **1.318** — ἑλών "taking out", from the store-room, not "choosing" (Merry on 1.318).
- **1.320** — ἀνόπαια: a bird's name, "unseen", "through the smoke-vent", or "upward", which Merry (on 1.320) prefers, after Eustathius and Empedocles.
- **1.327** — The grim homecoming: the anger of Pallas for the outrage of Ajax, son of Oileus, to Cassandra in her temple; at 4.499 his death is laid to his insolence against Poseidon (Merry on 1.326).
- **1.344** — καθʼ Ἑλλάδα καὶ μέσον Ἄργος "through Hellas and the heart of Argos": a phrase for the whole of Greece, north and south (Merry on 1.344).
- **1.349** — ἀλφησταί "toiling": "enterprising, gain-getting" men, or "bread-eating" ones, from ἄλφιτον (Merry on 1.347).
- **1.351–352** — Plato (*Republic* 424b) quotes the lines with ἐπιφρονέουσιν and ἀειδόντεσσι (Merry on 1.351).
- **1.356–359** — Aristarchus rejected the lines; Merry (on 1.360) doubts the rejection, since something in Telemachus' words must account for Penelope's amazement. They are Hector's words to Andromache (*Iliad* 6.490–493), with μῦθος "speech" for πόλεμος "war" and a new close to 359.
- **1.374–375** — Kirchhoff took the lines for an awkward imitation of 2.139 (Merry on 1.374).
- **1.377, 1.380** — νήποινον "without payment"; νήποινοι "unavenged": once warned, the suitors would die in his house with no compensation owed for them (Merry on 1.377).
- **1.391** — ἦ φῂς: Nitzsch preferred ἢ "or" (Merry on 1.391).
- **1.402** — δώμασιν οἷσιν: whether the possessive ὅς may refer to the second person, or σοῖσιν should be read (Merry on 1.402).
- **1.431** — ἐεικοσάβοια "the worth of twenty oxen", a high price for a slave, where a skilled woman is valued at four oxen at *Iliad* 23.705 (Merry on 1.431).
- **1.440** — τρητοῖσι λέχεσσι "the corded bed", as *Iliad* 3.448; Merry (on 1.440) takes τρητός as "morticed", of the frame, citing Plato (*Statesman* 279e), against the holes for cords.
- **1.441–442** — How the door is fastened: a bolt drawn home by a strap through a slit in the door, and a handle (κορώνη) on the outside (Merry on 1.441).
- **2.1** — ῥοδοδάκτυλος "rosy-fingered": Merry (on 2.1) sees perhaps the spreading rays of rosy light before sunrise, like the fingers of an open hand, and quotes Eustathius' allegory of the sun's rays as Dawn's fingers.
- **2.3** — εἵματα ἑσσάμενος: it was the custom to sit up and put on at least part of one's clothing before leaving the bed (Merry on 2.3, comparing *Iliad* 2.42, 10.21).
- **2.5** — ἄντην "to look upon": "in presence", literally "if looked at in the face" (Merry on 2.5, comparing 4.310).
- **2.7, 2.26** — ἀγορή and θῶκος: the council of kings and chiefs was the βουλή or θῶκος, and the ἀγορή the gathering of the people, who assented or dissented (Merry on 2.7, comparing *Iliad* 2.53 and 2.93, and reporting Aristotle's account of the Cretan constitution in the *Politics*). θόωκος is "session", the council of elders (Merry on 2.26, comparing 3.127), kept apart in the English from βουλή "council".
- **2.9** — The line is no tautology: the first clause is the people gathering, the second the assembly fully formed (Merry on 2.9).
- **2.11** — δύω κύνες ἀργοί "two swift dogs": Merry (on 2.11) defends the reading, notes the variant κύνες πόδας ἀργοί, and takes ἀργός of swiftness, not of color, against the older "white" (Eustathius' λευκόποδες).
- **2.13** — θηεῦντο, from a present θηέομαι beside the commoner θεάομαι (Merry on 2.13).
- **2.14** — The elders sit on seats of stone in the place of assembly, and Telemachus, as the king's son, takes his father's seat while they make way (Merry on 2.14, comparing 17.61–63, 20.144–146).
- **2.17** — καὶ γάρ more probably gives the reason why Aegyptius speaks first, his longing for his lost son, than an explanation of his age (Merry on 2.17).
- **2.20** — πύματον: Antiphus was the last of the six companions of Odysseus whom the Cyclops ate (Merry on 2.20, comparing 9.369, and rejecting Eustathius' "last of all men"); the stress may fall on ὡπλίσσατο, since once blinded the Cyclops could no longer wait on himself. ὡπλίσσατο δόρπον is the Cyclops' own formula (9.291, 9.344).
- **2.22** — ἔργα "fields", the farms (Merry on 2.22: *opera rustica*).
- **2.30, 2.42** — στρατοῦ "of the army": the scholia give two senses, an enemy's army or the army that went to Ilios; Ameis takes the second, since Aegyptius longs for his son, but Merry (on 2.30) thinks an invasion or a raid of pirates the likelier cause for calling an assembly in haste. The English keeps the ambiguity. The optative εἴποι (2.31) becomes the subjunctive εἴπω when Telemachus repeats the words (2.43), marking that he would tell such news if he had it (Merry on 2.31).
- **2.33** — ὀνήμενος "may he prosper!": the older commentators supplied εἴη ("may he be blessed"), an ellipse Merry (on 2.33) calls unparalleled in Homer; he prefers Classen's explanation, which takes the participle from a formula of blessing, as οὐλόμενος comes from the curse ὄλοιο.
- **2.34** — The digitized text prints ὅτι; the sense is the relative ὅ τι "whatever", as this text prints it at 5.89 and 8.577 and the Iliad at 14.221, and the English takes it so.
- **2.35** — φήμη "word of omen": a chance utterance whose bearing the speaker does not know; Aegyptius blesses Telemachus unawares, before he has said that he called the assembly (Merry on 2.35, comparing κλεηδών, 18.117, 20.120).
- **2.37** — The scepter was the badge of public office, carried by kings, priests, prophets, heralds and judges; the herald who puts it in a speaker's hand invests him with that office for the time (Merry on 2.37).
- **2.39** — καθαπτόμενος "approaching … with words": earnest or animated address, not necessarily rough, as of an urgent but submissive appeal (10.70) or of an angry retort (18.415; Merry on 2.39, comparing 2.240).
- **2.40** — οὗτος ἀνήρ "that man": not a periphrasis for "I", but "this man (about whom you ask) is not far off", until he adds that it is he (Merry on 2.40).
- **2.45** — With Aristophanes' κακά, printed here, ὅ is "in that": "two evils have fallen upon my house"; with Aristarchus' κακόν, δοιά is "in two ways" or answers the "first" and "far greater" evils of 2.46–48 (Merry on 2.45). The English follows the text printed.
- **2.46, 2.48** — τοίσδεσσιν "here", with a gesture of the hand (Merry on 2.46); νῦν δʼ αὖ καὶ πολὺ μεῖζον understands κακὸν ἔμπεσε, where the balance wants τὸ δέ after τὸ μέν (Merry on 2.48).
- **2.51–52** — The scholia ask why only the suitors of Ithaca, "the men who are the best here", are blamed; and they take εἰς οἶκον to mean that Icarius lived in Ithaca (Merry on 2.51, 2.52, who also cites the legend that made Icarius king of Cephallenia, *Poetics* 25: see the note on 1.276).
- **2.53** — ἐεδνώσαιτο "settle the bride-price": not "to dower" his daughter, a later usage, but to accept gifts of wooing for her, or "to make terms about the marriage" (Merry on 2.53, after Butcher and Lang and Cobet; see the note on 1.275–278).
- **2.60** — ἦ καὶ ἔπειτα "truly, even then": ἔπειτα, as distinguished from ὀπίσσω "hereafter", points to the immediate future: if we do (καί) try, we shall prove weaklings (Merry on 2.60, comparing 1.65 and 2.273, 2.280, where the English has "then" as well).
- **2.67** — μεταστρέψωσιν "turn upon you": "lest they make some change, in wrath at evil deeds", the gods turning from indifference to interference, as μεταστρέψεις at *Iliad* 15.203 (Merry on 2.67); Fäsi joins μεταστρέψωσιν κακὰ ἔργα, "bring the evil deeds down on the guilty", comparing μετάτροπα ἔργα in Hesiod (*Theogony* 89, as Merry reports him).
- **2.70** — σχέσθε, φίλοι "hold back, my friends", "let be": with φίλοι Telemachus addresses not the suitors but the Ithacans (Merry on 2.70).
- **2.86** — ἐθέλοις δέ κε: many manuscripts have ἐθέλεις, and Bekker reads ἐθέλεις δʼ ἐκ μῶμον ἀνάψαι (Merry's critical note and on 2.86).
- **2.89** — τάχα δʼ εἶσι τέταρτον "and soon the fourth will pass": 2.106–107 and 13.377 decide the sense (Merry on 2.89). ἐστίν is "is completed", as at *Iliad* 2.295, so that three years are past and the fourth is "fast passing", as the scholium explains εἶσι (διελεύσεται); Nitzsch took it as "is coming".
- **2.92** — ἀγγελίας "messages", as ἀγγελιάων (2.255); Ameis takes it as "messengers", from a supposed ἀγγελίης (Merry on 2.92).
- **2.98** — μεταμώνια "wasted": a variant μεταμώλια, which Eustathius calls Attic; Apion derived the word from going "with the winds", which Merry calls very doubtful (Merry's critical note and on 2.98).
- **2.100** — τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο "of death that lays men low": commonly "the outstretcher", of the body laid out for burial, though from the root of λέγω, "lay", the form would be τανηλεχής (Düntzer, in Merry on 2.100).
- **2.102** — κεῖται, the manuscripts' reading, which Buttmann defends on the ground that the verb has no distinct subjunctive form; Merry's text prints Wolf's conjecture κῆται (Merry's critical note).
- **2.105** — ἐπεὶ … παραθεῖτο, as Bekker reads with Eustathius; nearly all manuscripts have ἐπήν, which Merry keeps, an optative of recurring action, comparing *Iliad* 24.227 (Merry's critical note and on 2.105).
- **2.107** — The same verse at 19.152 and 24.142 is followed by μηνῶν φθινόντων, περὶ δʼ ἤματα πόλλʼ ἐτελέσθη, which this passage lacks.
- **2.116** — ὅ οἱ is the Harleian manuscript's reading, ὅ with the force of "that", as at *Iliad* 9.493; with the plural ἅ she ponders the gifts that Athena has given her (Merry on 2.116).
- **2.126** — ποθήν: two manuscripts have ποθή, with ἐστί understood; ποιεῖτʼ is ποιεῖται, "she is making fame for herself; but for thee the loss of much substance" (Merry on 2.126).
- **2.131–132** — ζώει ὅ γʼ ἦ τέθνηκε "alive or dead": "as to my father, he is away elsewhere in the world, whether he be alive or dead", a doubt put as an indirect question, with the circumflex on the second member (Merry on 2.131).
- **2.133** — ἑκών "of my own will": the manuscripts are divided between ἑκών and ἐγών, with the greater number for ἑκών; Bekker adopts ἐγών (Merry's critical note).
- **2.141** — ὑμῖν here, ὕμιν in the identical verse at 1.376, as the digitized text prints them.
- **2.151** — πυκνά "thick" is La Roche's reading, with eleven manuscripts, for the common πολλά (Merry's critical note).
- **2.154** — αὐτῶν "of the people": Aristophanes read αὖτις or αὔτως (Merry's critical note).
- **2.167** — ἐυδείελος "clear-seen": the word recurs of Ithaca and islands (9.21, 13.212, 13.325, 19.132). The scholia give "lying well toward the evening", from δείελος "evening" (*Iliad* 21.232), the view of Buttmann and of Düntzer, "well-defined" and "conspicuous"; Merry (on 2.167) takes the last as right, if with Curtius εὐδείελος is another form of εὐδέελος, that is εὔδηλος, "clear to see".
- **2.172** — εἰσανέβαινον "were embarking", as ἀναβήμεναι "embarked" (1.210); the Iliad's Ἴλιον εἰσανέβησαν is "gone up into Ilios" (6.74).
- **2.190** — ἀνιηρέστερον: Ameis, with Bekker, reads ἀνιηρώτερον from a Vienna manuscript, needlessly; the Alexandrines called the form Attic (Merry's critical note).
- **2.191** — The verse is wanting in two Venetian manuscripts, among others, and Wolf rejected it as made up from *Iliad* 1.562 with a varying second half, some reading εἵνεκα τῶνδε and others οἷος ἀπʼ ἄλλων; neither Eustathius nor the scholia notice it (Merry's critical note). Merry (on 2.191) calls it spurious: εἵνεκα τῶνδε may mean "because of [the resistance of] these suitors", but τῶνδε is an odd way to say "us", and the verse spoils the contrast of αὐτῷ μέν οἱ (2.190) and σοὶ δέ (2.192). The English renders it "because of these men".
- **2.198** — παύσεσθαι is Wolf's conjecture for παύσασθαι or παύεσθαι (Merry's critical note).
- **2.209** — ἀγαυοί "noble": the scholia record a variant ἀγανοί (Merry's critical note).
- **2.222** — χεύω "heap up": Ptolemy son of Oroandes wrote χείω, and so did Aristarchus; Herodian read χεύω, a present for the future (the scholia, in Merry's critical note).
- **2.227** — γέροντι: Eustathius and Merry take the old man as Laertes, "Odysseus entrusted all his house to Mentor, to obey his aged father, and keep all secure"; Nitzsch and others take him as Mentor, with the house the subject of πείθεσθαι and Mentor of φυλάσσειν (Merry on 2.227, citing Monro), which the English follows: the house is to obey the old man, and Mentor to keep all things safe.
- **2.236** — κακορραφίῃσι "in the mischief": the scholia record κακοφραδίῃσι (Merry's critical note).
- **2.245** — ἀνδράσι καὶ πλεόνεσσι goes with μαχήσασθαι, of the suitors who outnumber the people (as 2.251), or with ἀργαλέον, "hard even for men who are more numerous". - **5.39** — Τροίης "from Troy": a variant Τρωίης, which the scholia take as an adjective with ληίδος (5.40), "from the Trojan spoil" (Merry's critical note and on 5.39).
- **5.43–49** — = *Iliad* 24.339–345 (and 5.45–46 = 1.97–98). One scholium says the verses were carried from here, without need, to Athena's departure in Book 1 (1.96) and to Hermes' descent to Troy (*Iliad* 24.339); another reports the objection that the wand is of no use here, as it is in the Iliad, where it puts the guards to sleep, and answers that such things are the gods' own attributes, as Poseidon carries his trident to the Ethiopians (Merry's critical note). Merry (on 5.43–49) finds the wand more fitting in the Iliad.
- **5.54** — τῷ ἴκελος "like it": the scholia and Eustathius say the verse was added without need (Merry's critical note). Merry (on 5.54) defends the repetition after ἐοικώς (5.51), comparing *Iliad* 5.87, 5.93: Hermes is like the bird in his swoop, not in his form.
- **5.59** — ἐσχαρόφιν "on the hearth": a form of the o-declension for the metre; the ἐσχάρα here is perhaps a portable brazier (Merry on 5.59).
- **5.60** — κέδρου τʼ εὐκεάτοιο θύου τε "of cedar that splits easily and of thyine wood": Homer's κέδρος is not the cedar of Lebanon but a juniper, probably *Juniperus oxycedrus*; εὐκέατος is "easily split" or "ready split" into billets; θύον is a tree with resinous wood, perhaps the arbor vitae, not incense (Merry on 5.60, citing Athenaeus and Pliny). The English keeps "cedar" and "thyine wood".
- **5.62** — κερκίς "shuttle", rather than the rod that beats the threads close (Merry on 5.62).
- **5.66** — σκῶπες "owls": some wrote κῶπες (Eustathius, in Merry's critical note); Merry (on 5.66) reports Pliny's doubt what bird the scopes were.
- **5.69** — ἡμερίς "garden vine": a vine brought into cultivation from the wild (Merry on 5.69).
- **5.72** — ἴου "of violets": a variant σίου, "of marsh parsley", is said to be the correction of King Ptolemy Euergetes, who held the violet out of place there (Athenaeus, as Merry reports; Eustathius says many of the ancients liked it: Merry's critical note).
- **5.77–78** — ἄντην goes with ἰδοῦσα, "when she looked him in the face" (Merry on 5.77).
- **5.80** — οὐδʼ εἴ τις: Aristarchus read οὐδʼ ἥ τις, and ναίει, not ναίοι (the scholia, in Merry's critical note).
- **5.83–84** — 5.83–84 return at 5.157–158; the scholia call 5.84 superfluous here and say that 5.158 was carried up from its place (Merry's critical note), and Merry's text brackets 5.84.
- **5.90** — εἰ τετελεσμένον ἐστίν "if it is a thing that can be accomplished": Merry (on 5.90) renders "if it is a thing that ever hath been done", which would imply that it can be done again, and reports Nitzsch's comparison with Aristotle's "what has happened is evidently possible" (*Poetics* 1451b17–18).
- **5.91** — "But come farther in …" = *Iliad* 18.387 is wanting in the best manuscripts, and does not fit Hermes already seated (5.86); Merry thinks it interpolated from the Iliad, unless the guest is bidden from one room to another (Merry's critical note).
- **5.92** — ὥς ἄρα: the digitized text prints the acute, where every other instance in Homer has ὣς ἄρα (5.148, 5.351 and often): a slip, left in the text.
- **5.100** — τίς δʼ ἄν: Bekker and Nauck would read τίς κε, since ἑκών once began with a digamma (Merry's critical note).
- **5.104** — ἁλιῶσαι "made vain": Merry (on 5.104) renders it "to thwart by open resistance", and παρεξελθεῖν "to overreach".
- **5.105–111** — Hermes sums up the returns: the Achaeans sinned against Athena, who roused an evil wind against them (as 3.132–135). Merry (on 5.108) holds that, whether or not the whole passage 5.105–111 is rejected, 5.110–111 cannot stand here (his text brackets them), since they would make Athena's storm the one that wrecked Odysseus, while the wreck was the work of Zeus, as Calypso says in the same verses (5.131–134) and Odysseus later (12.403–425).
- **5.112** — ἠνώγειν "bade" stands for ἠνώγεεν; Aristarchus seems to have written the pluperfect with the movable ν (Merry's critical note). The tense looks back to the moment when Zeus sent Hermes (Merry on 5.112).
- **5.118** — ζηλήμονες "jealous": Eustathius and the scholia record δηλήμονες, the word of *Iliad* 24.33; Merry (on 5.118) keeps ζηλήμονες, explained by the next verse.
- **5.120** — Merry puts the comma after ἀμφαδίην, so that "if one makes a man her dear husband" explains lying with men "openly"; ἤν τις has a variant ἥτις (Merry's critical note and on 5.120).
- **5.121–124** — Orion's death by Artemis in Ortygia: Merry (on 5.121, 5.123) reports Apollodorus' account of Artemis killing him in Delos and later forms of the story, and takes Ortygia as Delos, as against the Syracusan Ortygia; the Ortygia of 15.404 lies near Syrie. Some athetized 5.123–124, since in Homer Artemis never kills men (the scholia, in Merry's critical note); Merry's text reads ἕως μιν for ἧος.
- **5.124** — ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν "gentle arrows": of a painless death, here an arrow shot in anger (Merry on 5.124).
- **5.125–128** — Iasion: Hesiod has Demeter bear Plutus to the hero Iasion, "in a thrice-plowed fallow, in the rich land of Crete" (νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ, *Theogony* 969–971; Merry on 5.125 cites the verse with the form Ἰάσιος); τρίπολος is a field plowed three times (Merry on 5.127); the scholia quote, with disapproval, a reading τριπύλῳ (Merry's critical note).
- **5.132** — ἔλσας "struck", from εἴλω, of a crushing blow, or "driving down" (Merry on 5.132); Zenodotus read ἐλάσας (Merry's critical note).
- **5.136** — ἀγήραον: Aristarchus read ἀγήρων (Merry's critical note). Merry (on 5.136) reports Hayman's remark that Calypso now feeds Odysseus with mortal food (5.196–199).
- **5.146** — ἐποπίζεο "have regard for": from ὄπις, the regard due to the gods, or their watch over men (Merry on 5.146).
- **5.156** — ἂμ πέτρῃσι is Aristarchus' reading for ἐν πέτρῃσι (Merry's critical note).
- **5.164** — ὑψοῦ "high up": the ἴκρια are the highest part of the hull; Brieger puts a comma after αὐτῆς and takes ὑψοῦ with φέρῃσιν (Merry on 5.164).
- **5.168** — ἵκηαι: Aristophanes read ἵκοιο, and two manuscripts ἣν πατρίδα (Merry's critical note).
- **5.179** — ἄλλο: Aristophanes wrote ἄλλοις, which Nauck thinks a slip for ἄλλως (Merry's critical note).
- **5.185** — ὕδωρ: a variant ὕδατος (Merry's critical note); Merry (on 5.185) reports Hesiod's Styx, made the great oath of the gods (*Theogony* 389, 775 and following).
- **5.202** — τοῖς ἄρα μύθων ἦρχε: the plural of the formula stands though Odysseus alone is there, Hermes having gone (Merry on 5.202); Aristonicus marked the line, since one speaks to one (the scholia, in Merry's critical note).
- **5.208** — σὺν ἐμοί: most manuscripts; Eustathius and most editors παρʼ ἐμοί (Merry's critical note).
- **5.216–217** — Calypso spoke of Penelope's looks; Odysseus calls her περίφρων, "prudent", and speaks of his home (Merry on 5.216). ἀκιδνοτέρη is uncertain, "weaker" or "meaner" in the scholia (Merry on 5.217); εἰσάντα: Aristarchus εἰς ἄντα, the commoner texts εἰς σῶμα (Merry's critical note).
- **5.229** — "a cloak and a tunic": the cloak is named first, as the more conspicuous garment (Merry on 5.229).
- **5.232** — ἐφύπερθε is the reading of Aristarchus' copies, where the commoner ones had ἐπέθηκε, the text of 10.545 (Merry's critical note); κεφαλῇ goes with περιβάλετο (Merry on 5.232).
- **5.234** — δῶκέν οἱ is Bekker's reading and most modern editors', for the manuscripts' δῶκε μέν οἱ, because οἱ once began with a digamma; La Roche would have taken it if a single manuscript had it (Merry's critical note).
- **5.235** — ἀμφοτέρωθεν ἀκαχμένον "sharpened on both sides": a double blade (Merry on 5.235), as Göbel takes the axes of the contest of the bow (19.574).
- **5.240** — περίκηλα "well seasoned" is Aristarchus' reading; Chrysippus divided περὶ κῆλα (Merry's critical note): trees long dead but standing (Merry on 5.240).
- **5.243** — ἤνυτο: Cobet would read ἤνετο, which Nauck adopts (Merry on 5.243).
- **5.247–248** — Aristophanes held that the two verses say the same thing; ἄρασσεν has a variant ἄρηρεν (Merry's critical note). Merry (on 5.247) takes them as boring the pieces through to match and driving home the pegs.
- **5.249–251** — "As wide as a man … marks out the hull of a broad freight ship, … so wide did Odysseus make his broad raft" (Merry on 5.249); some read τορνώσατʼ for ποιήσατʼ in 5.251 (Merry's critical note). τορνώσεται points to curved sides, against Brieger's flat box (Appendix I §6).
- **5.252–253** — ἴκρια … ἐπηγκενίδεσσι: Merry translates "And he worked away, setting up the decks by fitting them to the close-set ribs, and he finished off with the long side-planking" (on 5.252): σταμῖνες the ribs, ἴκρια the decks fore and aft, ἐπηγκενίδες the long planks of the sides (Appendix I §§4–6). Brieger and Hentze make the ἴκρια the ribs and the σταμῖνες their stays. Rhianus read ἐπητανίδεσσι (Merry's critical note). The English follows Merry, with "deck" for the ἴκρια, as *Iliad* 15.729.
- **5.256–257** — φράξε … ῥίπεσσι … οἰσυΐνῃσι "fenced it … with osier withes": a wattled bulwark round the edge, "a safeguard against the waves" (Appendix I §7, after Eustathius); Autenrieth took it of calking the seams with withes. ὕλην: the scholia take it as ballast, which Merry finds open to objection (ἐπιχέω is "pour over", not "pour in"); the English keeps "wood" (Appendix I §7).
- **5.260** — ὑπέρας τε κάλους τε πόδας τε "the braces and halyards and sheets": the κάλοι raise the yard, the ὑπέραι at the yard-arms trim it, the πόδες at the lower corners set the sail (Appendix I §16).
- **5.261** — μοχλοῖσιν "with levers": a lever lifts rather than drags, and Merry thinks the word may mean "rollers" (Merry on 5.261).
- **5.262** — τέτρατον ἦμαρ ἔην, καὶ τῷ τετέλεστο ἅπαντα: "It was the end of the fourth day when all was now accomplished", καί joining the clauses (Merry on 5.262).
- **5.272** — ἐσορῶντι: Aristarchus' copies had both ἐσορῶντι and ἐσορῶντα (Merry's critical note).
- **5.273–275** — = *Iliad* 18.487–489, but the text prints Ἄρκτον θʼ … ἄμαξαν, where the Iliad has Ἄρκτόν θʼ … Ἄμαξαν, the Wagon as a name; Merry's text too has ἄμαξαν. Aristotle cites οἴη δʼ ἄμμορος as a metaphor, "the best known" put for the only one (*Poetics* 1461a20–21), since the Bear is not the only constellation that never sets; Merry (on 5.275) reports the attempts of Strabo and others to save the statement.
- **5.277** — ἐπʼ ἀριστερὰ χειρός "on his left hand": a variant νηός, which Nauck adopts (Merry's critical note); the phrase is found only here in Homer with χειρός, perhaps of the steersman's hand (Merry on 5.277).
- **5.281** — εἴσατο δʼ ὡς ὅτε ῥινόν "it looked like a shield": ῥινός is the oxhide, the shield, the low coast its rim and the mountains its boss; Aristarchus read ἐρινόν, "a fig", and some scholia explain ῥινόν as "mist" (Merry's critical note and on 5.281). Merry would read ὥς τε ῥινόν; Fäsi conjectured ὡς ὅτε τε ῥίον (Merry's critical note).
- **5.283** — the mountains of the Solymi: Strabo puts the Solymi on the heights of Taurus in Lycia and Pisidia (Merry on 5.283).
- **5.289** — μέγα πεῖραρ ὀιζύος "the great bounds of the misery": ἥ agrees with ὀιζύος, the misery that has come upon him. Merry (on 5.289) refers to his note on 12.51, where he rejects "snares of misery" (Autenrieth's *laquei miseriae*) and takes πεῖραρ ὀιζύος as the end, the consummation of misery, as τέλος θανάτοιο.
- **5.290** — ἅδην ἐλάαν κακότητος "give him his fill of misery": "drive him to his heart's content along the road of misery" (Merry on 5.290), as *Iliad* 19.423; others take ἅδην as a noun, "to satiety"; Cobet proposed a form of ἄω, "satiate" (Merry on 5.290).
- **5.296** — αἰθρηγενέτης "born of the clear sky", passive, of the North Wind coming down from the heights; the scholia explain it as bringing fair weather, or cold (Merry on 5.296); Rhianus and Aristophanes read αἰθρηγενέης (Merry's critical note).
- **5.302** — ἀναπλήσειν: also written ἀναπλῆσαι (Merry's critical note).
- **5.304** — Odysseus, not knowing that the storm is Poseidon's, blames Zeus, as men commonly do (the scholia, in Merry on 5.304).
- **5.309–311** — the fight over the body of Achilles, where Ajax carried off the body and Odysseus held off the Trojans, is told at 24.36–42 (Merry on 5.309).
- **5.311** — κλέος ἦγον "would have spread my fame", or "brought back the report of me" (Merry on 5.311).
- **5.312** — ἁλῶναι: Demetrius Ixion read ἀλέσθαι, for which Buttmann reads ὀλέσθαι (Merry's critical note).
- **5.314** — ἐπεσσύμενον: Aristarchus wrote ἐπισσύμενον, taking ἐπεσσύμενον for a perfect passive (Merry's critical note).
- **5.315** — πέσε: Rhianus read αὐτὸν βάλε, "it threw him", which the scholia call better (Merry's critical note).
- **5.318** — σπεῖρον "sail", the word of the winding-sheet at 2.102 (again 6.269).
- **5.328–329** — ὀπωρινὸς Βορέης "the North Wind of late summer": ὀπώρα runs from the rising of the Dog Star to the setting of the Pleiads (Merry on 5.328); the thistles are the dry heads blown over the plain (Merry on 5.329).
- **5.334** — βροτὸς αὐδήεσσα "a mortal with human voice": the ancients doubted the word; Merry (on 5.334) prefers Aristophanes' "speaking with human voice" to "famous" or the variant οὐδήεσσα, "earthly", which the scholia give to Aristotle, who is said to have written οὐδήεσσα of Ino and αὐλήεσσα of Circe and Calypso, living alone in their halls (Merry's critical note).
- **5.337** — ἀνεδύσετο: the verse was missing in most copies, and Aristarchus doubted its athetesis and wrote ὑπεδύσατο (the scholia, in Merry's critical note); Merry's text brackets it, as made up from 5.353.
- **5.344–345** — νόστου γαίης Φαιήκων "strive to come / to the land of the Phaeacians": νόστος here of coming to a place, not of returning (Merry on 5.345).
- **5.346–349** — στέρνοιο: Aristarchus' two editions had στέρνοισι; the scholia accent τανύσσαι as an infinitive, to go with βαλέειν at 5.349, where Merry prints the imperative τάνυσσαι; 5.347 δέος has a variant κακόν, and at 5.349 Aristophanes wrote αἶψʼ for ἄψ (Merry's critical notes).
- **5.350** — ἀπονόσφι τραπέσθαι "turn yourself away": those who may not watch a power above men at work turn away, as Circe bids at 10.528 (Merry on 5.350).
- **5.357** — ὅ τε "since": the text prints ὅ τε, "in that"; the common reading, which Merry keeps, is ὅτε, "when", and Bekker writes ὅ τε in several such places (Merry's critical note and on 5.357).
- **5.368–369** — ἠΐων θημῶνα … καρφαλέων "a heap of husks, dry ones": the refuse heaps of the winnowing floor (Merry on 5.368, comparing *Iliad* 5.502).
- **5.371** — κέληθʼ ὡς ἵππον ἐλαύνων "as though riding a horse": the art of riding is alluded to elsewhere in Homer only in the simile of *Iliad* 15.679–684, Diomedes' ride on Rhesus' horses being a case of necessity (*Iliad* 10.513; Merry on 5.371).
- **5.378** — διοτρεφέεσσι "whom Zeus has nurtured", here of a whole people; a variant gives Φαιήκεσσι (Merry's critical note and on 5.378).
- **5.379** — ὀνόσσεσθαι κακότητος "make light of your misery": a sarcastic threat, as if nothing short of the extremity of suffering would satisfy him (Merry on 5.379).
- **5.385–386** — πρὸ δὲ κύματʼ ἔαξεν "broke the waves before him" (Merry on 5.385); for ἧος some wrote ὅππως (the scholia, in Merry's critical note).
- **5.389** — πλάζετο "he wandered": Aristarchus read it with a long α, perhaps taking it from πλήσσω, "was beaten", not from πλάζομαι (Merry's critical note).
- **5.391** — ἠδὲ γαλήνη: Aristarchus read ἡ δὲ γαλήνη, the article (Merry's critical note); Merry (on 5.391) prefers it, "and there came a breathless calm".
- **5.393** — ὑπό: Aristophanes and Rhianus read ἐπὶ κύματος (Merry's critical note).
- **5.395** — κεῖται: Merry's text prints κῆται, after Hermann; the manuscripts give κεῖται (Merry's critical note).
- **5.400** — ὅσσον τε γέγωνε βοήσας "as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout" (Merry on 5.400).
- **5.402** — ῥόχθει: Demosthenes is said to have cured his lisp by repeating this verse (Merry on 5.402, reporting Zosimus' life of Demosthenes).
- **5.404–405** — ἐπιωγαί are roadsteads under the lee of the land, not harbors; ἀκταί headlands, σπιλάδες jagged rocks (Merry on 5.404–405).
- **5.409** — ἐπέρησα: Merry's text reads ἐτέλεσσα, with a variant ἐπέρασσα, which La Roche adopts (Merry's critical note).
- **5.422** — ἐξ ἁλός: Aristarchus wrote εἰν ἁλί (the scholia, in Merry's critical note); with ἐξ ἁλός, ἅλς is the water by the shore (Merry on 5.422).
- **5.431** — ἐπεσσύμενον is the wave rushing on him (Merry on 5.431); Demetrius Ixion read ἀπεσσύμενον (Merry's critical note).
- **5.432–435** — the octopus: as pebbles stick to the suckers of an octopus dragged from its hole, so the skin of his hands stuck to the rock; the likeness is the clinging grip, and the simile is not to be pressed (Merry on 5.432–433).
- **5.437** — ἐπιφροσύνην δῶκε "given him presence of mind": a variant εἰ μὴ ἐπὶ φρεσὶ θῆκε, as 5.427 (Merry's critical note).
- **5.445** — πολύλλιστον "longed for with many prayers", "greatly longed for", as τρίλλιστος (*Iliad* 8.488); the scholia also take it as an adverb, or as owning the river's divinity (Merry on 5.445 and his critical note).
- **5.453–454** — γούνατʼ ἔκαμψε "let both his knees sink", with "his strong hands": the picture is of a man exhausted, arms dropping and knees giving way, not of the rest that γόνυ κάμψειν means at *Iliad* 7.118 (Merry on 5.453).
- **5.458** — ἄμπνυτο "got his breath back" is the common reading (printed again at 24.349); Aristarchus read ἔμπνυτο, as at *Iliad* 22.475, whose second half the verse shares (Merry's critical note).
- **5.459** — ἀπὸ ἕο: Aristarchus' reading, as La Roche shows; the scholia give it to Zenodotus, with ἀπὸ ἕθεν as the common text (Merry's critical note).
- **5.466–469** — φυλάσσω is Aristarchus' reading, with φυλάξω (Merry's critical note); "I fear lest the cruel frost and fresh dew quench my life" (Merry's translation of 5.467–468); with κεκαφηότα Merry follows Eustathius, "having gasped out the spirit" (on 5.468). αὔρη δʼ has a variant αὔρη γάρ (Merry's critical note).
- **5.471** — μεθείη: the majority of manuscripts; others μεθείῃ, and La Roche gives Aristarchus μεθήῃ (Merry's critical note). The optative marks a further hope, beyond falling asleep (Merry on 5.471).
- **5.476–477** — ἐν περιφαινομένῳ "in a place open to view", a clearing; ἐξ ὁμόθεν "from the same spot", or "from the same root" (Merry on 5.476–477); the scholia report γεγαῶτας in the commentaries for πεφυῶτας, which Merry takes for a correction by Aristarchus (his critical note).
- **5.488–491** — the brand in the ashes: a man so lonely that he has no neighbor to give him a light keeps a brand buried in the ashes, as Odysseus keeps his spark of life under the leaves (Merry on 5.488).
- **5.492–493** — ὕπνος is the subject of παύσειε, as the masculine ἀμφικαλύψας shows (Merry on 5.492).
