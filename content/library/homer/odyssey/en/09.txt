# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
2	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
3	truly it is a fine thing to listen to a singer
4	such as this one is, like the gods in his voice.
5	For I, for my part, say there is no fulfillment more gracious
6	than when gladness holds the whole people,
7	and the banqueters throughout the house listen to a singer,
8	sitting in order, and beside them the tables are laden
9	with bread and meat, and drawing wine from the mixing bowl
10	the wine-pourer carries it round and pours it into the cups;
11	this seems to me in my mind to be something like the finest thing.
12	But your heart is set on my groaning sorrows,
13	to ask about them, so that I may groan and mourn still more.
14	What then shall I recount to you first, and what last?
15	For the heavenly gods have given me many sorrows.
16	Now first I will tell you my name, so that you too
17	may know it, and I hereafter, when I have escaped the pitiless day,
18	may be your guest-friend, though I live in a house far away.
19	I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who for tricks of every kind
20	am in the thoughts of men, and my fame reaches the sky.
21	I live in clear-seen Ithaca; and on it is a mountain,
22	Neritum with its quivering leaves, conspicuous; and around it many islands
23	lie, very near to one another,
24	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus.
25	But my island itself lies low in the sea, farthest out of all
26	toward the darkness, while the others lie apart toward the dawn and the sun,
27	rugged, but a good nurse of young men; and I, for my part,
28	can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land.
29	Truly Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, held me back there
30	in her hollow caves, longing for me to be her husband;
31	and in the same way Circe kept me in her halls,
32	the wily one of Aeaea, longing for me to be her husband;
33	but they never persuaded the heart in my breast.
34	So true is it that nothing is sweeter than one's native land and parents,
35	even if a man lives far off in a rich house
36	in a foreign land, apart from his parents.
37	But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows,
38	which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy.
39	From Ilios the wind bore me and brought me near the Cicones,
40	to Ismarus. There I sacked the city and destroyed the men themselves;
41	and from the city we took their wives and many possessions
42	and divided them, so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share.
43	Then indeed I bade us flee with nimble foot,
44	but they, the great fools, would not obey.
45	But there much wine was drunk, and many sheep
46	they slaughtered beside the shore, and shambling cattle with curving horns.
47	Meanwhile the Cicones went off and called to other Cicones,
48	who were their neighbors, more numerous and braver too,
49	living inland, who knew how to fight with men from chariots,
50	and on foot too, where there was need.
51	Then they came, as many as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season,
52	early in the morning; and then an evil fate from Zeus stood beside us,
53	men of dread fate, so that we might suffer many sorrows.
54	They set their battle in array and fought beside the swift ships,
55	and they struck at one another with bronze-fitted spears.
56	As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing,
57	so long we held our ground and fought them off, though they were more.
58	But when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen,
59	then at last the Cicones overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back.
60	And from each ship six of my well-greaved companions
61	perished; but the rest of us escaped death and fate.
62	From there we sailed on, grieving at heart,
63	glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions.
64	Nor did my curving ships go on farther
65	until we had called three times on each of our wretched companions
66	who had died on the plain, cut down by the Cicones.
67	But against the ships Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused the North Wind
68	with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered
69	land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky.
70	Then the ships were driven on slantwise, and their sails
71	the force of the wind tore in three and four pieces.
72	And we lowered them into the ships, fearing destruction,
73	and rowed the ships themselves with all speed toward the land.
74	There for two nights and two days without ceasing
75	we lay, eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together.
76	But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day,
77	we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails
78	and sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships.
79	And now I would have reached my native land unharmed;
80	but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current
81	and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera.
82	From there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds
83	over the fish-filled sea; but on the tenth we set foot
84	on the land of the Lotus-eaters, who eat a flowery food.
85	There we went up onto the land and drew water,
86	and at once my companions took their meal beside the swift ships.
87	But when we had tasted food and drink,
88	then I sent some of my companions to go and find out
89	what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth,
90	choosing two men, and sending a herald with them as the third.
91	They went at once and mingled with the Lotus-eaters;
92	and the Lotus-eaters devised no destruction for our companions,
93	but gave them some of the lotus to taste.
94	And whoever of them ate the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus
95	no longer wished to bring back word or to return,
96	but they wished to remain there among the Lotus-eaters,
97	munching the lotus, and to forget their homecoming.
98	These I brought back to the ships by force, weeping,
99	and in the hollow ships I dragged them under the thwarts and bound them.
100	But the rest of my trusty companions I ordered
101	to make haste and go on board the swift ships,
102	for fear that one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming.
103	And they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches,
104	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
105	From there we sailed on, grieving at heart;
106	and we came to the land of the Cyclopes, overbearing and lawless,
107	who, trusting in the immortal gods,
108	neither plant anything with their hands nor plow,
109	but all these things grow unsown and unplowed,
110	wheat and barley and vines, which bear
111	wine of great clusters, and the rain of Zeus makes it grow for them.
112	They have no assemblies that bring counsel, and no ordinances,
113	but they live on the peaks of high mountains
114	in hollow caves, and each one gives the law
115	to his children and his wives, and they care nothing for one another.
116	Now there is an island of good soil that stretches out beyond the harbor,
117	neither near the land of the Cyclopes nor far off,
118	a wooded one; and on it live countless goats,
119	wild ones; for no path of men keeps them away,
120	nor do hunters go into it, who in the woods
121	suffer pains as they range over the peaks of the mountains.
122	It is not taken up by flocks nor by plowing,
123	but unsown and unplowed all its days
124	it is empty of men, and feeds bleating goats.
125	For the Cyclopes have no ships with red-painted prows,
126	nor are there shipwrights among them, who might build
127	well-benched ships, which might accomplish each errand,
128	going to the cities of men, as men often
129	cross the sea in ships to visit one another;
130	such men would also have made the island well settled for them.
131	For it is not a poor place at all, and would bear all things in their season;
132	for in it are meadows beside the banks of the gray sea,
133	well-watered and soft; the vines there would be imperishable.
134	In it is smooth plowland; they would reap a deep field of grain always
135	in its season, since the soil beneath is very rich.
136	In it is a harbor with good anchorage, where there is no need of a cable,
137	neither to cast out mooring-stones nor to make fast stern-cables,
138	but men may run their ships ashore and wait a while, until the sailors'
139	hearts urge them on, and the breezes blow fair.
140	And at the head of the harbor flows bright water,
141	a spring from under a cave; and around it poplars grow.
142	There we sailed in, and some god guided us
143	through the dark night, and nothing could be seen;
144	for a thick mist was around the ships, and the moon
145	did not shine from the sky, but was held fast in clouds.
146	Then no one saw the island with his eyes,
147	nor did we see the long waves rolling toward the shore,
148	before we ran our well-benched ships ashore.
149	And when the ships were beached we lowered all the sails,
150	and we ourselves too stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore;
151	There we fell asleep and waited for brilliant Dawn.
152	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
153	we roamed over the island itself, marveling at it.
154	And the nymphs, the daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, roused
155	the goats that lived in the mountains, so that my companions might have their meal.
156	At once we took our curved bows and long-socketed javelins
157	from the ships, and, arranged in three bands,
158	we began to shoot; and at once the god gave us game to satisfy the heart.
159	Twelve ships followed me, and to each
160	nine goats fell by lot; but for me alone they chose out ten.
161	So all day long, until the sun went down,
162	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine;
163	for the red wine was not yet spent from the ships,
164	but there was still some in them; for we had each drawn off much in two-handled jars
165	when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones.
166	And we looked across to the land of the Cyclopes, who were near,
167	and saw their smoke, and heard their voices, and those of the sheep and the goats.
168	When the sun went down and darkness came on,
169	and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore.
170	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
171	then I called an assembly and spoke among them all:
172	‘Stay here now, the rest of you, my trusty companions;
173	but I, in my own ship with my companions,
174	will go and make trial of these men, who they are,
175	whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust,
176	or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods.’
177	So saying, I went on board the ship, and ordered my companions
178	to go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables.
179	And they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches,
180	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
181	But when we had come to the place, which was near,
182	there at the far edge of the land, close to the sea, we saw a cave,
183	high, and overhung with laurels. And there many
184	flocks, sheep and goats, used to sleep; and around it a high fold
185	had been built with stones set deep in the earth
186	and with tall pines and high-leafed oaks.
187	There a huge man used to sleep, who herded his flocks
188	alone, far off; and he did not go among the others,
189	but lived apart, and knew lawless ways.
190	For he was made a huge wonder, and was not like
191	a man who eats bread, but like a wooded peak
192	of high mountains, which stands out alone, apart from the others.
193	Then I ordered the rest of my trusty companions
194	to stay there beside the ship and guard the ship,
195	but I chose twelve of my companions, the best,
196	and went; and I had with me a goatskin of dark wine,
197	sweet wine, which Maron, son of Euanthes, had given me,
198	the priest of Apollo, who used to stand guard over Ismarus,
199	because we had protected him with his child and his wife
200	in reverence; for he lived in the grove full of trees
201	of Phoebus Apollo. And he gave me splendid gifts:
202	he gave me seven talents of well-made gold,
203	and he gave me a mixing bowl all of silver, and then
204	he drew off wine in two-handled jars, twelve in all,
205	sweet unmixed wine, a divine drink; and no one
206	of the slaves or the handmaids in the house knew of it,
207	but only he himself and his dear wife and one housekeeper.
208	And whenever they drank that honey-sweet red wine,
209	he would fill one cup and pour it into twenty measures of water,
210	and a sweet fragrance rose from the mixing bowl,
211	a wondrous one; then it would not have been your pleasure to hold back.
212	With this I filled a great skin and carried it, and provisions too
213	in a leather sack; for at once my proud spirit foreboded
214	that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage,
215	a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances.
216	Quickly we came to the cave, but we did not find him
217	inside; he was herding his fat flocks in the pasture.
218	We went into the cave and gazed at each thing.
219	The racks were laden with cheeses, and the pens were crowded
220	with lambs and kids; each kind was penned
221	apart, the firstlings by themselves, the later ones by themselves,
222	and the newborn by themselves. And all the vessels were swimming with whey,
223	the pails and the bowls, well made, into which he milked.
224	Then my companions begged me at first with words
225	to take some of the cheeses and go back, and then
226	quickly to drive the kids and lambs to the swift ship
227	out of the pens, and to sail over the salt water;
228	but I did not listen—it would have been far better—
229	so that I might see him himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts.
230	But when he appeared, he was not to be a lovely sight for my companions.
231	There we kindled a fire and made an offering, and we ourselves too
232	took some of the cheeses and ate, and waited for him inside,
233	sitting there, until he came, herding his flocks. He carried a heavy load
234	of dry wood, to have light for his supper,
235	and threw it down inside the cave with a din;
236	and we in terror shrank back into the inner part of the cave.
237	But he drove his fat flocks into the wide cave,
238	all those that he milked, and left the males outside the door,
239	the rams and the he-goats, outside in the high-walled fold.
240	Then he lifted high and set in place a great door-stone,
241	a heavy one; two and twenty wagons,
242	good four-wheeled ones, could not have heaved it from the ground;
243	so lofty a rock he set against the doorway.
244	Then he sat down and milked the sheep and the bleating goats,
245	all in good order, and put her young one under each.
246	And at once he curdled half of the white milk,
247	gathered it, and laid it in woven baskets,
248	and half again he set in vessels, so that he might have it
249	to take and drink, and have it for his supper.
250	But when he had busily finished his tasks,
251	then he kindled the fire, and saw us, and asked us:
252	‘Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the watery ways?
253	Is it on some business, or do you wander at random
254	over the sea, like pirates, who wander
255	staking their lives and bringing evil to foreigners?’
256	So he spoke, and our dear heart was broken,
257	in terror of his heavy voice and of the monster himself.
258	But even so I answered him with words and said:
259	‘We, you must know, are Achaeans, driven off course from Troy
260	by winds of every kind over the great gulf of the sea,
261	eager to go home, by another way, by other paths
262	we have come; so, I suppose, Zeus wished to devise it.
263	And we claim to be the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
264	whose fame is now the greatest under heaven;
265	so great a city did he sack, and destroyed many
266	people. But we in our turn have reached you and come to your knees,
267	to see if you will give us a guest-gift, or otherwise
268	give us some present, as is the custom with strangers.
269	But respect the gods, good sir; we are your suppliants,
270	and Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers,
271	the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers.’
272	So I spoke, and at once he answered me with pitiless heart:
273	‘You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,
274	you who bid me either fear or avoid the gods;
275	for the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus
276	nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger.
277	Nor would I, to avoid the hatred of Zeus, spare
278	either you or your companions, unless my own heart bade me.
279	But tell me where you moored your well-made ship when you came,
280	whether somewhere at the far edge of the land, or near, so that I may know.’
281	So he spoke, testing me, but he did not escape my notice, for I knew much,
282	but I answered him back with crafty words:
283	‘My ship Poseidon the earth-shaker shattered,
284	dashing it against the rocks at the borders of your land,
285	driving it on a headland; and a wind from the sea had borne it there;
286	but I with these escaped sheer destruction.’
287	So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, with pitiless heart,
288	but sprang up and put out his hands to my companions,
289	and seizing two together he dashed them to the ground like puppies,
290	and their brains flowed out on the ground and soaked the earth.
291	Then he cut them limb from limb and made ready his supper;
292	and he ate like a mountain-bred lion, and left nothing,
293	entrails and flesh and marrowy bones.
294	And we, weeping, lifted up our hands to Zeus,
295	seeing the cruel deeds, and helplessness held our hearts.
296	But when the Cyclops had filled his great belly,
297	eating human flesh and drinking unmixed milk on top of it,
298	he lay inside the cave, stretched out among his flocks.
299	Then I planned in my proud heart
300	to go up close, drawing the sharp sword from beside my thigh,
301	and stab him in the chest, where the midriff holds the liver,
302	feeling for the place with my hand; but a second thought held me back.
303	For there we too would have perished by sheer destruction;
304	for we could not have pushed away from the high doorway
305	with our hands the heavy stone that he had set against it.
306	So then, groaning, we waited for brilliant Dawn.
307	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
308	then he kindled the fire and milked his fine flocks,
309	all in good order, and put her young one under each.
310	But when he had busily finished his tasks,
311	again he seized two men together and made ready his meal.
312	And when he had dined he drove his fat flocks out of the cave,
313	easily taking away the great door-stone; and then
314	he set it back again, as one sets the lid on a quiver.
315	And with much whistling he turned his fat flocks toward the mountain,
316	the Cyclops; but I was left there, brooding evil deep within,
317	to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph.
318	And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best:
319	there lay beside the pen a great club of the Cyclops,
320	of green olive wood; he had cut it to carry with him
321	when it was dry. As we looked at it, we likened it
322	to the mast of a black ship of twenty oars,
323	a broad freight ship, which crosses the great gulf;
324	so great was it in length, so great in thickness to look upon.
325	From it I stood by and cut off about a fathom's length,
326	and set it beside my companions, and bade them taper it;
327	and they made it smooth; and I stood by and sharpened
328	the point, and at once took it and hardened it in the blazing fire.
329	And I laid it away carefully, hiding it under the dung,
330	which lay spread throughout the cave in great abundance.
331	Then I ordered the others to cast lots
332	for who would dare to lift the stake with me
333	and grind it into his eye, when sweet sleep came upon him.
334	And the lot fell on those whom I myself would have wished to choose,
335	four of them, and I counted myself in with them as the fifth.
336	In the evening he came, herding his fine-fleeced flocks.
337	At once he drove his fat flocks into the wide cave,
338	all of them, and left none outside in the high-walled fold,
339	either because he suspected something, or because a god so bade him.
340	Then he lifted high and set in place a great door-stone,
341	and sat down and milked the sheep and the bleating goats,
342	all in good order, and put her young one under each.
343	But when he had busily finished his tasks,
344	again he seized two men together and made ready his supper.
345	Then I stood close by the Cyclops and spoke to him,
346	holding in my hands an ivy-wood bowl of dark wine:
347	‘Cyclops, here, drink wine, since you have eaten human flesh,
348	so that you may know what kind of drink this was that our ship
349	held hidden. I was bringing it to you as a libation, in the hope that you would pity me
350	and send me home; but you rage past all bearing.
351	Hard man, how should anyone else come to you hereafter
352	of the many men there are, since you have not acted fair and right?’
353	So I spoke, and he took it and drank it off; and he was terribly pleased
354	drinking the sweet drink, and asked me for it a second time:
355	‘Give me more, and gladly, and tell me your name
356	right now, so that I may give you a guest-gift, in which you may rejoice;
357	for the grain-giving earth bears for the Cyclopes too
358	wine of great clusters, and the rain of Zeus makes it grow for them;
359	but this is a branch of ambrosia and nectar.’
360	So he spoke, and again I gave him the sparkling wine.
361	Three times I brought it and gave it to him, and three times he drank it off in his folly.
362	But when the wine had come around the Cyclops' wits,
363	then I spoke to him with soothing words:
364	‘Cyclops, you ask me my famous name, and I will tell it
365	to you; and you give me a guest-gift, as you promised.
366	Nobody is my name; Nobody they call me,
367	my mother and my father and all my other companions.’
368	So I spoke, and at once he answered me with pitiless heart:
369	‘Nobody I will eat last among his companions,
370	and the others before him; that shall be your guest-gift.’
371	He spoke, and leaning back he fell on his back, and then
372	he lay with his thick neck bent aside, and sleep
373	that subdues all took him; and from his throat rushed wine
374	and gobbets of human flesh; and he belched, sodden with wine.
375	Then I drove the stake under the deep ashes
376	until it grew hot, and with words I encouraged all my companions,
377	so that no one might draw back from me in fear.
378	But when the stake of olive wood was just about to catch fire,
379	green though it was, and glowed terribly,
380	then I brought it near from the fire, and around me my companions
381	stood; and a god breathed great daring into us.
382	They took the stake of olive wood, sharp at the point,
383	and thrust it into his eye; and I, throwing my weight on it from above,
384	twirled it, as when a man bores a ship's timber
385	with a drill, and those below keep it spinning with a strap,
386	holding it at either end, and it runs round without letting up.
387	So we took the fire-pointed stake and twirled it in his eye,
388	and the blood flowed round the stake, hot as it was.
389	And the blast singed all his eyelids round about, and his brows,
390	as the eyeball burned, and its roots crackled in the fire.
391	And as when a smith dips a great axe or an adze
392	in cold water, and it hisses loudly,
393	to temper it; for this in turn is the strength of iron;
394	so his eye hissed around the stake of olive wood.
395	And he groaned aloud terribly, and the rock rang round about,
396	and we in terror shrank back; but he wrenched the stake
397	out of his eye, drenched with much blood.
398	Then he flung it from him with his hands, distraught,
399	and called loudly to the Cyclopes, who lived around him
400	in caves among the windy heights.
401	And they heard his shout and came, one from one place and one from another,
402	and standing around the cave they asked what ailed him:
403	‘What has hurt you so, Polyphemus, that you have cried out like this
404	through the ambrosial night, and make us sleepless?
405	Surely no one among mortals is driving off your flocks against your will?
406	Surely no one is killing you yourself by trickery or by force?’
407	Then from the cave mighty Polyphemus spoke to them in turn:
408	‘Friends, Nobody is killing me by trickery, and not by force.’
409	And they answered him and spoke winged words:
410	‘If then nobody is doing you violence, alone as you are,
411	there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus;
412	but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon.’
413	So they spoke as they went away, and my dear heart laughed within me,
414	how my name and my flawless plan had deceived him.
415	But the Cyclops, groaning and writhing in agonies of pain,
416	groping with his hands, took the stone away from the doorway,
417	and sat down himself in the doorway, stretching out both his hands,
418	to see if he could catch anyone going out of the door among the sheep;
419	for so foolish, I suppose, he hoped in his mind that I was.
420	But I planned how all might turn out best by far,
421	if I might find some release from death for my companions and for myself;
422	and I wove every kind of trick and plan,
423	as one does where life is at stake; for great evil was near.
424	And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best:
425	there were rams, well fed and thick of fleece,
426	fine and large, with violet-dark wool;
427	these I bound together in silence with well-twisted willow shoots,
428	on which the Cyclops slept, the monster, who knew lawless ways,
429	taking them three together; the one in the middle carried a man,
430	and the other two went on either side, saving my companions.
431	Three sheep carried each man; but as for me—
432	for there was a ram, by far the best of all the flock—
433	I grasped him by the back, and curled up under his shaggy belly
434	I lay; and to the wondrous fleece, with my hands,
435	turning myself over, I held on unceasingly with enduring heart.
436	So then, groaning, we waited for brilliant Dawn.
437	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
438	then the males of the flock rushed out to the pasture,
439	and the females bleated unmilked around the pens,
440	for their udders were bursting. And their master, worn down
441	by evil pains, felt along the backs of all the sheep
442	as they stood upright; but the fool did not notice
443	how the men were bound under the chests of the woolly sheep.
444	Last of the flock the ram went out of the door,
445	weighed down by his wool and by me, thinking shrewd thoughts.
446	And mighty Polyphemus felt him over and spoke to him:
447	‘Dear ram, why do you hurry out through the cave like this, the last
448	of the flock? Before you never used to come behind the sheep,
449	but you were far the first to graze on the tender flowers of the grass,
450	with long strides, and the first to reach the streams of the rivers,
451	and the first to long to go back to the farmstead
452	in the evening; but now you are the very last. Surely you miss your master's
453	eye, which a bad man blinded
454	with his wretched companions, when he had overpowered my wits with wine,
455	Nobody, who I say has not yet escaped destruction.
456	If only you could be of one mind with me and could find a voice
457	to tell where he is skulking from my might!
458	Then his brains would be dashed against the ground, this way and that through the cave,
459	as he was struck down, and my heart would find relief
460	from the evils that Nobody, a nobody, gave me.’
461	So saying, he sent the ram away from him out of the door.
462	When we had gone a little way from the cave and the fold,
463	I first loosed myself from under the ram, and set my companions free.
464	And quickly the long-legged flocks, rich with fat,
465	we drove off, often turning them about, until we came
466	to the ship. And we were a welcome sight to our dear companions,
467	we who had escaped death; but for the others they groaned and lamented.
468	But I would not let them—I signed no with my brows to each—
469	weep, but bade them quickly throw the many fine-fleeced sheep
470	on board the ship and sail over the salt water.
471	And they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches,
472	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
473	But when I was as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout,
474	then I spoke to the Cyclops with cutting words:
475	‘Cyclops, it was not a weakling's companions after all that you were
476	to eat in your hollow cave with your mighty strength.
477	Yes, truly your evil deeds were to catch up with you,
478	hard man, since you did not shrink from eating strangers
479	in your own house; therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you.’
480	So I spoke, and he grew all the more angry in his heart,
481	and he broke off the peak of a great mountain and hurled it,
482	and threw it down in front of the dark-prowed ship,
483	by a little, and it failed to reach the tip of the steering oar.
484	And the sea surged up under the falling rock;
485	and the backwash at once carried the ship toward the land,
486	a swell from the sea, and drove it to reach the shore.
487	But I took a very long pole in my hands
488	and pushed the ship off and away, and urged on my companions and bade them
489	lay to their oars, so that we might escape from evil,
490	nodding with my head; and they bent forward and rowed.
491	But when we had gone over the sea twice as far,
492	then I spoke to the Cyclops; and around me my companions
493	tried to hold me back with soothing words, one from one side and one from another:
494	‘Hard man, why do you want to provoke a savage man?
495	Just now he threw his missile into the sea and brought the ship
496	back to the land, and we thought we would perish there.
497	And if he had heard any of us utter a sound or speak,
498	he would have crushed our heads and the timbers of our ship
499	with a jagged boulder flung at us; so far does he throw.’
500	So they spoke, but they did not persuade my proud heart,
501	but I answered him back with resentful heart:
502	‘Cyclops, if any mortal man
503	asks you about the unsightly blinding of your eye,
504	say that Odysseus, sacker of cities, blinded you,
505	the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca.’
506	So I spoke, and he groaned and answered me:
507	‘Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me.
508	There was once a seer here, a man tall and brave,
509	Telemus, son of Eurymus, who excelled in prophecy
510	and grew old among the Cyclopes as their seer;
511	he told me that all these things would come to pass hereafter,
512	that I would lose my sight at the hands of Odysseus.
513	But I always expected some tall and handsome man
514	to come here, clothed in great courage;
515	but now one who is small and a nobody and feeble
516	has blinded my eye, when he had overpowered me with wine.
517	But come here, Odysseus, so that I may set good cheer before you
518	and speed your escort, that the famous shaker of the earth may grant it;
519	for I am his son, and he claims to be my father.
520	And he himself, if he wishes, will heal me, and no one else,
521	neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men.’
522	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
523	‘If only I could make you bereft of soul and life
524	and send you into the house of Hades,
525	as surely as not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye.’
526	So I spoke, and then to lord Poseidon he
527	prayed, stretching out his hands to the starry sky:
528	‘Hear me, Poseidon, dark-haired earth-holder,
529	if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father,
530	grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may not reach home,
531	the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca.
532	But if it is his fate to see his friends and reach
533	his well-built house and his native land,
534	let him come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions,
535	on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house.’
536	So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him.
537	But he lifted again a far greater stone,
538	and hurled it, whirling it round, and leaned his measureless strength into it,
539	and threw it down behind the dark-prowed ship,
540	by a little, and it failed to reach the tip of the steering oar.
541	And the sea surged up under the falling rock;
542	and the wave carried the ship forward, and drove it to reach the shore.
543	But when we came to the island, where the other
544	well-benched ships waited all together, and around them our companions
545	sat mourning, always waiting for us,
546	there we came and ran the ship ashore on the sand,
547	and we ourselves too stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore.
548	And we took the Cyclops' flocks out of the hollow ship
549	and divided them, so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share.
550	But the ram my well-greaved companions gave to me alone,
551	as a special gift when the flocks were divided; and on the shore
552	to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all,
553	I sacrificed him and burned the thigh-pieces; but he took no heed of the offering,
554	but was pondering how they might all perish,
555	my well-benched ships and my trusty companions.
556	So all day long, until the sun went down,
557	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine.
558	When the sun went down and darkness came on,
559	and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore.
560	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
561	then I urged on my companions and bade them
562	go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables;
563	and they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches,
564	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
565	From there we sailed on, grieving at heart,
566	glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions.”
