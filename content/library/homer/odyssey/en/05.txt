# Homer, Odyssey, Book 5 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus,
2	to bring light to the immortals and to mortals;
3	and the gods sat down to their session, and among them
4	Zeus who thunders on high, whose power is the greatest.
5	And Athena told them of the many sorrows of Odysseus,
6	remembering them; for he was her concern, there in the house of the nymph:
7	“Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever,
8	Never again let any man with a ready heart be gentle and kind,
9	a scepter-bearing king, nor know in his mind what is due,
10	but let him always be harsh and do evil;
11	since no one remembers divine Odysseus
12	among the people he was lord over, and he was kind, like a father.
13	But he lies on an island, suffering bitter sorrows,
14	in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who by force
15	holds him there; and he cannot reach his native land;
16	for he has no ships with oars and no companions
17	who might take him over the broad back of the sea.
18	And now they are eager to kill his beloved son
19	as he comes home; for he has gone in search of news of his father
20	to sacred Pylos and to shining Lacedaemon.”
21	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
22	“My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
23	Was it not you yourself who devised this plan,
24	that Odysseus should come and take vengeance on those men?
25	And as for Telemachus, escort him skillfully, for you are able,
26	so that he may reach his native land quite unharmed,
27	and the suitors in their ship may go back again.”
28	He spoke, and said to Hermes, his dear son, face to face:
29	“Hermes, since you for your part are our messenger in all else,
30	tell the nymph of the lovely tresses our unerring counsel,
31	the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus, that he may return
32	with the escort neither of gods nor of mortal men;
33	but on a raft of many bindings, suffering woes,
34	on the twentieth day he may reach deep-soiled Scheria,
35	the land of the Phaeacians, who are near kin to the gods,
36	who will honor him with all their hearts like a god,
37	and will send him in a ship to his dear native land,
38	giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing,
39	a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy,
40	if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil.
41	For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach
42	his high-roofed house and his native land.”
43	So he spoke, and the guide, the slayer of Argus, did not disobey.
44	At once he bound under his feet his fine sandals,
45	ambrosial, golden, which carried him over the waters
46	and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind;
47	and he took the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men,
48	of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping;
49	holding it in his hands the mighty slayer of Argus flew.
50	Stepping on Pieria, he fell from the upper air upon the sea;
51	then he hurried over the wave like a bird, a gull,
52	which through the dread bosom of the barren sea,
53	hunting fish, wets its thick plumage in the brine;
54	like it, Hermes rode over the many waves.
55	But when he came to the island, which lay far away,
56	then stepping out of the violet sea onto the land
57	he went on, until he came to the great cave, in which the nymph
58	of the lovely tresses lived; and he found her within.
59	A great fire was burning on the hearth, and far off the scent
60	of cedar that splits easily and of thyine wood spread over the island
61	as they burned; and she within, singing with a lovely voice,
62	walked to and fro at the loom and wove with a golden shuttle.
63	And around the cave a wood had grown, flourishing,
64	alder and poplar and fragrant cypress.
65	And there long-winged birds would roost,
66	owls and hawks and long-tongued crows
67	of the sea, whose concern is the works of the sea.
68	And there, stretched about the hollow cave,
69	was a garden vine in its prime, and it flourished with clusters of grapes.
70	And four springs in a row were flowing with clear water,
71	near one another, turned this way and that.
72	And all around, soft meadows of violets and parsley
73	were in bloom. There even an immortal, if he came,
74	would gaze at the sight and delight his mind.
75	There the guide, the slayer of Argus, stood and gazed.
76	But when he had gazed at everything in his heart,
77	at once he went into the wide cave; and when she looked him in the face
78	Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, did not fail to know him;
79	for the gods are not unknown to one another,
80	the immortals, not even if one lives in a house far away.
81	But great-hearted Odysseus he did not find within;
82	he sat weeping on the shore, as he had before,
83	racking his heart with tears and groans and sorrows.
84	He would look out over the barren sea, letting tears fall.
85	And Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, questioned Hermes,
86	when she had seated him on a shining, glossy throne:
87	“Why, Hermes of the golden wand, have you come to me,
88	respected and dear? Before now you have not come often.
89	Say what is in your mind; my heart bids me accomplish it,
90	if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished.
91	But come farther in, so that I may set good cheer before you.”
92	So she spoke, and the goddess set a table beside him,
93	filling it with ambrosia, and mixed red nectar.
94	And the guide, the slayer of Argus, drank and ate.
95	But when he had dined and satisfied his heart with food,
96	then at last he answered her with words and said:
97	“You, a goddess, ask me, a god, why I have come; and I will tell you
98	my story truly; for you bid me.
99	It was Zeus who bade me come here, against my will;
100	who of his own will would speed across so much salt water,
101	boundless? Nor is there any city of mortals near, men who to the gods
102	offer sacrifices and choice hecatombs.
103	But there is no way that the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus
104	can be evaded by any other god, or made vain.
105	He says that there is with you a man more wretched than all the others,
106	the men who fought around the city of Priam
107	for nine years, and in the tenth sacked the city and went
108	home; but on their homecoming they sinned against Athena,
109	who roused against them an evil wind and long waves.
110	There all his other noble companions perished,
111	but the wind and the wave bore him and brought him here.
112	This man he bade you now send away with all speed;
113	for it is not his lot to perish here, far from his friends,
114	but it is still his fate to see his friends and to reach
115	his high-roofed house and his native land.”
116	So he spoke, and Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, shuddered,
117	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
118	“You are hard, you gods, and jealous beyond all others,
119	you who begrudge goddesses that they should lie with men
120	openly, if one makes a man her dear husband.
121	So it was when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself:
122	you gods who live at ease begrudged her so long,
123	until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne
124	came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him.
125	So too when Demeter of the lovely tresses, with Iasion,
126	yielding to her own heart, lay with him in love and bed
127	in a thrice-plowed fallow field; and not long without hearing of it
128	was Zeus, who killed him, striking him with a gleaming thunderbolt.
129	And so now again you begrudge me, you gods, that a mortal man should be with me.
130	Him I saved when he was astride the keel,
131	alone, when his swift ship, with a gleaming thunderbolt,
132	Zeus struck and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea.
133	There all his other noble companions perished,
134	but the wind and the wave bore him and brought him here.
135	Him I loved and cared for, and I said
136	that I would make him immortal and ageless all his days.
137	But since there is no way that the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus
138	can be evaded by any other god, or made vain,
139	let him go to his ruin, if that god urges him on and commands it,
140	over the barren sea; but in no way shall I send him myself;
141	for I have no ships with oars and no companions
142	who might take him over the broad back of the sea.
143	But with a ready heart I will give him counsel, and not hide it,
144	so that he may reach his native land quite unharmed.”
145	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, spoke to her in turn:
146	“Send him away now, then, and have regard for the wrath of Zeus,
147	lest in time to come he grow angry with you in resentment.”
148	So he spoke, and the mighty slayer of Argus went away;
149	and the lady nymph went to great-hearted Odysseus,
150	since she had heard the messages of Zeus.
151	She found him sitting on the shore; and his eyes were never
152	dry of tears, and his sweet life was ebbing away
153	as he mourned for his homecoming, since the nymph no longer pleased him.
154	By night, it is true, he would sleep beside her, under compulsion,
155	in the hollow caves, unwilling beside the willing;
156	but by day he would sit on the rocks and the beaches,
157	racking his heart with tears and groans and sorrows,
158	and would look out over the barren sea, letting tears fall.
159	And standing near him, she spoke to him, brilliant among goddesses:
160	“Ill-fated man, do not mourn here any longer, I beg you, nor let your life
161	waste away; for now with a ready heart I will send you away.
162	But come, cut long beams with the bronze and fit together
163	a broad raft; and fix a deck upon it
164	high up, so that it may carry you over the misty sea.
165	And I will put in it bread and water and red wine
166	to satisfy your heart, that may keep hunger from you,
167	and I will clothe you in garments; and I will send a fair wind behind you,
168	so that you may reach your native land quite unharmed,
169	if the gods are willing, who hold the wide sky,
170	who are stronger than I am both to devise and to fulfill.”
171	So she spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus shuddered,
172	and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
173	“Something else, goddess, is what you are planning here, and not my escort,
174	when you bid me cross on a raft the great gulf of the sea,
175	dread and grievous; over it not even balanced ships
176	cross, swift-sailing, glorying in the wind of Zeus.
177	But I would not set foot on a raft against your will,
178	unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath
179	that you will not plan some other evil bane against me myself.”
180	So he spoke, and Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, smiled,
181	and stroked him with her hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
182	“Truly you are a rogue, and no fool,
183	what a speech you have thought of making!
184	Let Earth now be witness to this, and the wide Heaven above,
185	and the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest
186	and most dread oath for the blessed gods,
187	that I will not plan some other evil bane against you yourself.
188	No, I am thinking of and will devise just what I would
189	plan for myself, if so great a need should come upon me;
190	for my mind too is right, and my own heart
191	in my breast is not of iron, but full of pity.”
192	So she spoke, and led the way, brilliant among goddesses,
193	swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess.
194	And they came to the hollow cave, the goddess and the man,
195	and he sat down there on the throne from which had risen
196	Hermes, and the nymph set before him all kinds of food,
197	to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat;
198	and she herself sat facing divine Odysseus,
199	and the slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her.
200	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
201	But when they had taken their fill of food and drink,
202	Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, began to speak among them:
203	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
204	is it so, then, that home to your dear native land
205	you wish to go at once, now? Then farewell to you, even so.
206	Yet if you knew in your mind what a measure of
207	sorrows it is your lot to fill up before you reach your native land,
208	you would stay here on the spot and keep this house with me,
209	and be immortal, for all your longing to see
210	your wife, for whom you yearn always, all your days.
211	Surely I claim to be in no way inferior to her,
212	not in form or stature, since it is in no way fitting
213	for mortal women to vie with immortals in form and looks.”
214	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
215	“Lady goddess, do not be angry with me for this; I know it myself,
216	all of it, that prudent Penelope
217	is less than you in looks and stature to behold;
218	for she is mortal, and you are immortal and ageless.
219	But even so I wish and yearn all my days
220	to come home and see the day of my homecoming.
221	And if again some god wrecks me on the wine-dark sea,
222	I will endure it, with a heart in my breast that bears up under sorrow;
223	for already I have suffered very much and toiled much
224	on the waves and in war; let this too be added to them.”
225	So he spoke, and the sun went down and darkness came on;
226	and the two of them went in, and in the inner part of the hollow cave
227	took their pleasure in love, staying by each other.
228	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
229	at once Odysseus put on a cloak and a tunic,
230	and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle,
231	fine and lovely, and around her waist she cast a girdle,
232	fine and golden, and over her head a head covering.
233	And then she planned the escort for great-hearted Odysseus.
234	She gave him a great axe that fitted his hands,
235	of bronze, sharpened on both sides; and in it
236	a beautiful handle of olive wood, well fitted;
237	and then she gave him a polished adze; and she led the way
238	to the far edge of the island, where tall trees grew,
239	alder and poplar, and fir that reached to the sky,
240	dry long since, well seasoned, that would float lightly for him.
241	But when she had shown him where tall trees grew,
242	Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, went to her house,
243	and he set about cutting beams; and his work went quickly.
244	Twenty in all he felled, and hewed them with the bronze,
245	and smoothed them skillfully and made them straight to the chalk line.
246	Meanwhile Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, brought him augers;
247	and he bored them all and fitted them to one another,
248	and hammered it together with pegs and fastenings.
249	As wide as the floor of a ship that a man marks out,
250	a broad freight ship, a man well skilled in carpentry,
251	so wide did Odysseus make his broad raft.
252	And setting up the deck, fitting it to the close-set ribs,
253	he worked on; and he finished with long side planks.
254	And he made a mast in it, and a yard fitted to it;
255	and he made a steering oar besides, to steer with.
256	And he fenced it from end to end with osier withes
257	to be a safeguard against the waves; and he heaped much wood upon it.
258	Meanwhile Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, brought cloth
259	to make sails; and he fashioned these well too.
260	And he made fast in it the braces and halyards and sheets,
261	and with levers he dragged it down into the shining sea.
262	It was the fourth day, and all his work was done;
263	and on the fifth brilliant Calypso sent him from the island,
264	when she had clothed him in fragrant garments and bathed him.
265	And the goddess put on board for him a skin of dark wine,
266	one skin, and another, a great one, of water, and provisions too
267	in a leather sack; and she put in many meats to satisfy his heart;
268	and she sent forth a fair wind, harmless and warm.
269	Glad of the wind, brilliant Odysseus spread his sails.
270	And he steered with skill with the steering oar,
271	sitting there, and sleep did not fall on his eyelids
272	as he watched the Pleiades and Boötes, who sets late,
273	and the Bear, which they also call by the name of the Wagon,
274	which turns in its place and watches Orion,
275	and alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus.
276	For this was the one Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, had bidden him
277	keep on his left hand as he crossed the sea.
278	For seventeen days he sailed, crossing the sea,
279	and on the eighteenth there appeared the shadowy mountains
280	of the land of the Phaeacians, where it lay nearest to him;
281	and it looked like a shield on the misty sea.
282	But the powerful earth-shaker, returning from the Ethiopians,
283	saw him from far away, from the mountains of the Solymi; for he came into his sight
284	sailing over the sea. And he grew all the more angry in his heart,
285	and he shook his head and spoke to his own heart:
286	“Ah, truly the gods have changed their purpose
287	about Odysseus, while I was among the Ethiopians,
288	and now he is near the land of the Phaeacians, where it is his lot
289	to escape the great bounds of the misery that has come upon him.
290	But I say I will yet give him his fill of misery.”
291	So saying, he gathered the clouds and threw the sea into confusion,
292	taking his trident in his hands; and he spurred on all the storms
293	of winds of every kind, and with clouds he covered
294	land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky.
295	The East Wind and the South Wind clashed together, and the stormy West Wind
296	and the North Wind, born of the clear sky, rolling up a great wave.
297	And then Odysseus' knees and dear heart were loosened,
298	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
299	“Ah me, wretched that I am, what will befall me in the end?
300	I fear that everything the goddess said was true,
301	when she said that on the sea, before I reached my native land,
302	I would fill up the measure of sorrows; and now it is all being fulfilled.
303	With what clouds he is wreathing the wide sky,
304	Zeus, and he has thrown the sea into confusion, and the storms rush on
305	of winds of every kind. Now sheer destruction is sure for me.
306	Three times blessed are the Danaans, and four times, who perished then
307	in wide Troy, doing a favor to the sons of Atreus.
308	Would that I too had died and met my fate
309	on that day when, in their greatest numbers, bronze-fitted spears
310	the Trojans hurled at me around the dead son of Peleus.
311	Then I would have had my funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame;
312	but now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death.”
313	As he spoke, a great wave struck him from above,
314	rushing on him terribly, and it whirled the raft around.
315	He himself fell far from the raft, and the steering oar
316	he let fly from his hands; and his mast was broken in the middle
317	by a dread storm of winds clashing together that came upon it,
318	and far off the sail and the yard fell into the sea.
319	And it held him under water a long time, and he could not
320	quickly rise from under the rush of the great wave;
321	for the garments weighed him down that brilliant Calypso had given him.
322	At last he came up, and spat out of his mouth the brine,
323	the bitter brine, that ran streaming in plenty from his head.
324	But even so he did not forget the raft, worn out though he was,
325	but darted after it in the waves and caught hold of it,
326	and sat down in the middle of it, avoiding the end of death.
327	And the great wave carried it with the current this way and that.
328	As when the North Wind of late summer carries thistles
329	over the plain, and they cling thick together,
330	so the winds carried it over the open sea this way and that;
331	now the South Wind would toss it to the North Wind to carry,
332	and now again the East Wind would yield it to the West Wind to chase.
333	But the daughter of Cadmus saw him, Ino of the lovely ankles,
334	Leucothea, who was once a mortal with human voice,
335	but now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods.
336	She pitied Odysseus as he wandered, suffering sorrows,
337	and like a diving bird on the wing she rose up from the sea,
338	and sat on the raft of many bindings and spoke a word:
339	“Ill-fated man, why is Poseidon the earth-shaker
340	so terribly angry with you, that he plants so many evils for you?
341	Yet he will not destroy you, though he rages greatly.
342	But do just this, for you do not seem to me to be senseless:
343	take off these garments, and leave the raft for the winds to carry,
344	leave it, and swimming with your hands strive to come
345	to the land of the Phaeacians, where it is your fate to escape.
346	Here, take this veil and stretch it under your chest,
347	immortal as it is; and there is no fear that you will suffer anything or perish.
348	But when you have laid hold of the land with your hands,
349	untie it and throw it back into the wine-dark sea
350	far from the land, and turn yourself away.”
351	So she spoke, and the goddess gave him the veil,
352	and she herself plunged back into the billowing sea
353	like a diving bird; and a dark wave hid her.
354	But much-enduring brilliant Odysseus pondered,
355	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
356	“Ah me, may it not be that someone is weaving a trap for me again,
357	one of the immortals, since she bids me leave the raft.
358	But I will certainly not obey yet, since far off, with my eyes,
359	I saw the land where she said there was a refuge for me.
360	But I will do just this, and it seems to me best:
361	as long as the beams hold together in their fastenings,
362	so long I will stay here and endure, suffering sorrows;
363	but when the wave shakes the raft apart for me,
364	I will swim, since there is nothing better to think of beforehand.”
365	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
366	Poseidon the earth-shaker roused a great wave against him,
367	dread and grievous, arching over him, and it struck him.
368	And as a blustering wind shakes a heap of husks,
369	dry ones, and scatters them this way and that,
370	so it scattered the long beams of the raft. But Odysseus
371	bestrode a single beam, as though riding a horse,
372	and stripped off the garments that brilliant Calypso had given him.
373	And at once he stretched the veil under his chest,
374	and plunged headlong into the sea, stretching out both his hands,
375	eager to swim. And the powerful earth-shaker saw him,
376	and he shook his head and spoke to his own heart:
377	“So now, after suffering many evils, wander over the sea,
378	until you come among men whom Zeus has nurtured.
379	But even so I do not expect you to make light of your misery.”
380	So he spoke, and lashed the fine-maned horses,
381	and came to Aegae, where his glorious house is.
382	But Athena, daughter of Zeus, thought of another thing.
383	She bound up the paths of the other winds,
384	and bade them all cease and lie down to rest;
385	and she roused the swift North Wind, and broke the waves ahead,
386	until he should come among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar,
387	Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, escaping death and the fates.
388	There for two nights and two days on the swollen wave
389	he wandered, and many times his heart foresaw destruction.
390	But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day,
391	then the wind ceased, and there was a calm,
392	a windless calm; and he saw the land near at hand,
393	with a sharp look ahead, as he was lifted by a great wave.
394	And as when the life of a father appears welcome to his children,
395	a father who lies in sickness, suffering bitter pains,
396	long wasting away, and a hateful god has attacked him,
397	and welcome it is, when the gods free him from his misery,
398	so welcome did the land and the woods appear to Odysseus,
399	and he swam on, eager to set his feet on the land.
400	But when he was as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout,
401	he heard the roar of the sea on the reefs;
402	for the great wave was roaring against the dry land,
403	belching terribly, and everything was wrapped in the foam of the sea;
404	for there were no harbors to hold ships, nor roadsteads,
405	but there were jutting headlands and reefs and crags;
406	and then Odysseus' knees and dear heart were loosened,
407	and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
408	“Ah me, now that he has granted me to see land beyond all hope,
409	Zeus, and I have cut through this gulf and crossed it,
410	nowhere does a way out of the gray sea appear;
411	for outside are sharp crags, and around them the wave
412	roars and surges, and the rock runs up sheer,
413	and the sea is deep close in, and there is no way to stand
414	on both feet and escape misery;
415	lest somehow, as I come out, it should dash me against the jagged rock,
416	a great wave snatching me up; and my effort will be in vain.
417	But if I swim on farther along the shore, in case I may find
418	beaches where the waves strike slanting, and harbors of the sea,
419	I fear that the storm may snatch me up again
420	and carry me, groaning heavily, over the fish-filled sea,
421	or a god may even send some great sea monster against me
422	out of the sea, such as famous Amphitrite breeds in great numbers;
423	for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me.”
424	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
425	a great wave carried him onto the rugged shore.
426	There his skin would have been stripped off and his bones crushed together,
427	had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena put it in his mind:
428	he rushed at the rock and seized it with both hands,
429	and clung to it, groaning, until the great wave went past.
430	And so he escaped that wave, but its backwash struck him again
431	as it rushed on, and flung him far out into the sea.
432	And as when an octopus is dragged out of its den,
433	many pebbles cling thick to its suckers,
434	so from his bold hands, against the rocks,
435	the skin was stripped away; and the great wave covered him.
436	Then unhappy Odysseus would have perished against fate,
437	had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind.
438	He came up out of the waves that belch against the land,
439	and swam along outside it, looking toward the land, in case he might find
440	beaches where the waves strike slanting, and harbors of the sea.
441	But when he came to the mouth of a fair-flowing river,
442	swimming, where the place seemed to him the best,
443	smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind,
444	he knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart:
445	“Hear me, lord, whoever you are; I come to you, much prayed for,
446	fleeing out of the sea from the rebukes of Poseidon.
447	Even to the immortal gods he is to be respected,
448	whoever of men comes as a wanderer, as I now
449	come to your stream and to your knees, after toiling much.
450	But pity me, lord; I claim to be your suppliant.”
451	So he spoke, and at once the god stopped his stream, and held back the wave,
452	and made a calm before him, and brought him safe
453	into the river's mouth. And he let both his knees sink,
454	and his strong hands; for his dear heart was overcome by the sea.
455	All his flesh was swollen, and much sea water gushed
456	up through his mouth and nostrils; and breathless and speechless
457	he lay with little strength left, and dreadful weariness came upon him.
458	But when he got his breath back and his spirit was gathered back into his heart,
459	then he untied the veil of the goddess from himself.
460	And he let it go into the river that murmurs to the sea,
461	and a great wave carried it back down the stream, and at once Ino
462	took it in her own hands; and he, withdrawing from the river,
463	sank down among the rushes, and kissed the grain-giving earth.
464	And deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart:
465	“Ah me, what will become of me? What will befall me in the end?
466	If I keep watch through the comfortless night by the river,
467	I fear that the cruel frost and the fresh dew together
468	may overcome my spirit, gasped away in my weakness;
469	and the breeze from a river blows cold in the early dawn.
470	But if I climb up the slope to the shady forest
471	and fall asleep in the thick bushes, in the hope that
472	the chill and weariness may leave me, and if sweet sleep comes over me,
473	I fear that I may become the prey and spoil of wild beasts.”
474	And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way:
475	he went to the forest; and he found it near the water
476	in a place open to view; and he crept under two bushes
477	that grew from the same spot, one of wild olive, one of olive.
478	Through these neither the wet might of the blowing winds could pass,
479	nor did the shining sun ever strike them with its rays,
480	nor could the rain pass right through them; so thick
481	they grew, intertwined with one another. Under these Odysseus
482	crept. And at once with his own hands he heaped up a bed,
483	a broad one; for there was a litter of leaves in great abundance,
484	enough to protect two men or three
485	in the winter season, however hard it might rage.
486	Much-enduring brilliant Odysseus saw it and rejoiced,
487	and lay down in the middle of it, and heaped the litter of leaves over himself.
488	As when a man hides a brand in the black ashes
489	at the far edge of the fields, where he has no other neighbors,
490	keeping alive the seed of fire, so that he need not kindle it from elsewhere,
491	so Odysseus covered himself with the leaves; and Athena
492	poured sleep on his eyes, so that it might free him as quickly as possible
493	from his grievous weariness, enfolding his dear eyelids.
