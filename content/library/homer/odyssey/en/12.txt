# Homer, Odyssey, Book 12 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	“And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus
2	and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea,
3	and to the island of Aeaea, where are the house of early-born Dawn
4	and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun,
5	there we came and ran the ship ashore on the sand,
6	and we ourselves too stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore.
7	There we fell asleep and waited for brilliant Dawn.
8	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
9	then I sent some of my companions to the house of Circe
10	to bring the body of Elpenor, who had died.
11	And quickly we cut logs, and where the headland jutted out farthest
12	we buried him, grieving and shedding big tears.
13	But when the body was burned, and the armor of the body,
14	we heaped up a tomb, and dragged a pillar up onto it,
15	and on the top of the tomb we fixed the well-fitted oar.
16	We were arranging all these things; nor did Circe
17	fail to notice that we had come from Hades, but very quickly
18	she came, having made herself ready; and her handmaids brought with her
19	bread and meat in plenty and sparkling red wine.
20	And standing in our midst she spoke, brilliant among goddesses:
21	‘Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades,
22	twice dying, when other men die once.
23	But come, eat food and drink wine,
24	here all day long; and when dawn appears
25	you shall sail; and I will show you the way and point out
26	each thing, so that you may not, through some grievous mischief,
27	either on sea or on land, suffer pain and meet with bane.’
28	So she spoke, and our proud spirit was persuaded.
29	So all day long, until the sun went down,
30	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine.
31	When the sun went down and darkness came on,
32	the others lay down to sleep beside the stern-cables of the ship,
33	but she took me by the hand, away from my dear companions,
34	and seated me, and reclined near me, and questioned me about each thing;
35	and I told her all of it fair and right.
36	Then lady Circe spoke to me with words:
37	‘All these things have been brought to an end; but you, listen
38	to what I shall tell you, and a god himself will remind you.
39	First you will come to the Sirens, who bewitch
40	all men, whoever comes to them.
41	Whoever draws near in his ignorance and hears the voice
42	of the Sirens, for him his wife and little children
43	do not stand beside him when he comes home, nor are they glad;
44	but the Sirens bewitch him with their clear song,
45	sitting in a meadow, and about them is a great heap of bones
46	of rotting men, and around the bones the skins dwindle.
47	But row on past, and anoint the ears of your companions,
48	kneading honey-sweet wax, so that none of the others
49	may hear; but if you yourself are willing to listen,
50	let them bind you in the swift ship hand and foot,
51	upright in the mast-step, and let the rope-ends be fastened to the mast itself,
52	so that you may hear with delight the voice of the two Sirens.
53	And if you beg your companions and bid them set you free,
54	then let them bind you with still more bonds.
55	But when your companions have rowed past them,
56	then I will no longer tell you from beginning to end
57	which of the two ways shall be yours, but you yourself
58	must plan it in your heart; but I will tell you of both.
59	On the one side are overhanging rocks, and against them
60	roars the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite;
61	the Wanderers, as the blessed gods call them.
62	By that way not even winged things pass, not even the timid doves
63	that carry ambrosia to father Zeus,
64	but even of these the sheer rock always takes one away,
65	and the father sends in another to make up the number.
66	And by that way no ship of men that comes there has ever yet escaped,
67	but the planks of ships and the bodies of men together
68	the waves of the sea and the storms of deadly fire sweep away.
69	Only one seafaring ship has sailed past there,
70	the Argo, in the thoughts of all, sailing from Aeetes;
71	and even her the waves would quickly have dashed there against the great rocks,
72	but Hera sent her past, since Jason was dear to her.
73	And on the other side are two crags: the one reaches the broad sky
74	with its sharp peak, and a cloud has settled about it,
75	a dark cloud; it never departs, and clear sky never
76	holds its peak, neither in summer nor in late summer.
77	No mortal man could climb it or set foot on its top,
78	not even if he had twenty hands and feet;
79	for the rock is sheer, like rock that is polished.
80	And in the middle of the crag is a misty cave,
81	turned toward the darkness, toward Erebus, by the way that you
82	will steer your hollow ship past, shining Odysseus.
83	Not even a man in his prime, from the hollow ship,
84	shooting an arrow with his bow, could reach the hollow cave.
85	There within dwells Scylla, yelping terribly.
86	Her voice indeed is only as loud as a newborn puppy's,
87	but she herself is an evil monster; nor would anyone
88	be glad to see her, not even if a god should meet her.
89	She has twelve feet, all dangling,
90	and six necks, very long, and on each one
91	a terrible head, and in it teeth in three rows,
92	thick and close-set, full of black death.
93	Up to her middle she is sunk in the hollow cave,
94	but she holds her heads out of the dread pit,
95	and there she fishes, questing round the crag,
96	for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater
97	sea monster, of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers.
98	By her no sailors yet can boast that they have fled
99	unharmed with their ship; for with each head she carries off
100	a man, snatching him from the dark-prowed ship.
101	The other crag, lower, you will see, Odysseus.
102	They are near one another; you could even shoot an arrow across.
103	On it is a great wild fig tree, in full leaf;
104	and beneath it brilliant Charybdis sucks down the black water.
105	For three times a day she sends it up, and three times she sucks it down,
106	terribly. May you not be there when she sucks it down;
107	for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil.
108	But be sure to draw close to Scylla's crag, and quickly
109	row your ship past, since it is far better
110	to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together.’
111	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
112	‘Come now, goddess, tell me this truly,
113	whether I might somehow escape from deadly Charybdis
114	and ward off the other, when she would harm my companions.’
115	So I spoke, and at once she answered, brilliant among goddesses:
116	‘Hard man, once again your mind is on the works of war
117	and toil; will you not give way even to the immortal gods?
118	She is not mortal, but an immortal evil,
119	dread and grievous and savage, not to be fought;
120	there is no help; the strongest course is to flee from her.
121	For if you linger, arming yourself beside the rock,
122	I fear that she will rush at you again and reach you
123	with as many heads, and seize as many men.
124	But row past with all your force, and call on Crataeis,
125	the mother of Scylla, who bore her to be a bane to mortals;
126	and she will stop her from rushing out again.
127	And you will come to the island of Thrinacia; and there in great numbers
128	graze the cattle of the Sun and his fat sheep,
129	seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep,
130	fifty in each. And no young are born of them,
131	nor do they ever die. And goddesses are their herders,
132	nymphs of the lovely tresses, Phaethusa and Lampetie,
133	whom brilliant Neaera bore to Hyperion the Sun.
134	When their lady mother had borne them and reared them,
135	she settled them on the island of Thrinacia, to dwell far off
136	and guard their father's sheep and his cattle with curving horns.
137	If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming,
138	you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils;
139	but if you harm them, then I foretell destruction for you,
140	for your ship and your companions. And even if you yourself escape,
141	you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions.’
142	So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came.
143	She then went away up the island, brilliant among goddesses;
144	but I went to the ship and urged on my companions
145	to go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables;
146	and they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches,
147	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
148	And behind our dark-prowed ship
149	she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion,
150	Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice.
151	At once, when we had busied ourselves with all the tackle throughout the ship,
152	we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her.
153	Then I spoke among my companions, grieving at heart:
154	‘Friends, it is not right that only one or two should know
155	the oracles that Circe told me, brilliant among goddesses;
156	but I will tell them, so that knowing them we may either die
157	or else avoid death and doom and escape.
158	First, of the wondrous Sirens, she bids us avoid
159	the voice and the flowering meadow.
160	She bade me alone to hear their voice; but you must bind me
161	in a grievous bond, so that I may stay there steadfast,
162	upright in the mast-step, and let the rope-ends be fastened to the mast itself.
163	And if I beg you and bid you set me free,
164	then you must press me with still more bonds.’
165	So I was telling each thing and making it known to my companions;
166	and meanwhile the well-made ship quickly came
167	to the island of the two Sirens; for a harmless wind drove it on.
168	Then at once the wind ceased, and there was a calm,
169	a windless calm, and a god lulled the waves to sleep.
170	And my companions stood up and furled the ship's sails
171	and stowed them in the hollow ship, and sitting at the oars
172	they made the water white with their polished oars of fir.
173	But I with the sharp bronze cut a great round of wax
174	into small pieces, and pressed them with my strong hands;
175	and at once the wax grew warm, since the great strength bade it
176	and the ray of the lord Sun, son of Hyperion;
177	and I anointed the ears of all my companions in turn.
178	And they bound me in the ship hand and foot together,
179	upright in the mast-step, and fastened the rope-ends to the mast itself;
180	and they themselves, sitting down, struck the gray sea with their oars.
181	But when we were as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout,
182	driving swiftly on, the swift-sailing ship did not escape their notice
183	as it drew near, and they raised their clear song:
184	‘Come here, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans,
185	stop your ship, so that you may hear the voice of us two.
186	For no one has ever yet rowed past here in his black ship
187	before he has heard the honeyed voice from our lips;
188	but he goes home delighted, and knowing more.
189	For we know all the things that in wide Troy
190	the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods,
191	and we know all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth.’
192	So they spoke, sending out their beautiful voice; and my heart
193	wished to listen, and I bade my companions set me free,
194	nodding with my brows; but they bent forward and rowed.
195	And at once Perimedes and Eurylochus stood up
196	and bound me with more bonds and pressed me tighter.
197	But when they had rowed past them, and we could no longer
198	hear the voice of the Sirens or their song,
199	at once my trusty companions took away the wax
200	with which I had anointed their ears, and freed me from my bonds.
201	But when we were leaving the island, then at once
202	I saw smoke and a great wave, and heard a roar.
203	And in their terror the oars flew from their hands,
204	and all the oars splashed down in the current; and the ship stopped there,
205	since they no longer plied the tapering oars with their hands.
206	But I went through the ship and urged on my companions
207	with soothing words, standing beside each man:
208	‘Friends, we are not without knowledge of evils at all;
209	surely this evil that besets us is no greater than when the Cyclops
210	penned us in his hollow cave with his mighty strength;
211	yet even from there, by my prowess and my counsel and my mind,
212	we escaped, and some day, I think, we will remember these things too.
213	But now come, let us all do as I say.
214	You keep striking the deep breakers of the sea with your oars,
215	sitting at the rowing benches, if ever Zeus
216	will grant us to escape this destruction and get away;
217	and to you, helmsman, I give this charge; lay it
218	to heart, since you handle the steering oar of the hollow ship.
219	Keep the ship away from this smoke and wave,
220	and make for the crag, lest without your noticing
221	she rush off there and you throw us into evil.’
222	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words.
223	But of Scylla I said nothing more, a vexation past remedy,
224	lest my companions in fear should stop
225	rowing, and huddle themselves together inside the ship.
226	And then I let the grievous command of Circe
227	slip from my mind, since she had told me not to arm myself;
228	but I put on my glorious armor, and taking two long spears
229	in my hands I went onto the deck of the ship
230	at the prow; for from there I expected that she would first appear,
231	Scylla of the rock, who brought a bane on my companions.
232	But I could not catch sight of her anywhere, and my eyes grew weary
233	as I glanced all around toward the misty rock.
234	We sailed up the narrow strait, lamenting;
235	on one side was Scylla, and on the other brilliant Charybdis
236	terribly sucked down the salt water of the sea.
237	Truly, whenever she vomited it up, like a cauldron on a great fire
238	she would seethe up, all of her, churning, and high overhead the foam
239	fell on the tops of both crags;
240	but whenever she swallowed up the salt water of the sea,
241	all within her appeared, churning, and around her the rock
242	roared terribly, and beneath, the earth appeared,
243	dark with sand; and pale fear took hold of them.
244	We looked toward her, fearing destruction;
245	and meanwhile Scylla seized my companions from the hollow ship,
246	six of them, who were the strongest with their hands and in strength.
247	And turning to look at the swift ship and at my companions,
248	I saw already their feet and their hands above
249	as they were lifted on high; and they lifted up their voices and called
250	on me by name, then for the last time, grieving at heart.
251	As when on a jutting rock a fisherman with a very long rod
252	casts down bait as a snare for the little fishes,
253	and lets the horn of an ox of the fields down into the sea,
254	and then catches one and flings it out, gasping,
255	so they were lifted, gasping, toward the rocks;
256	and there at her doors she devoured them, screaming,
257	stretching out their hands to me in grim combat.
258	That was the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes
259	of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea.
260	But when we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis
261	and Scylla, then at once we came to the flawless island of the god;
262	and there were the fine broad-browed cattle,
263	and many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun.
264	Then while I was still out at sea in the black ship
265	I heard the lowing of the cattle being stalled for the night
266	and the bleating of the sheep; and there fell upon my heart the word
267	of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias,
268	and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again
269	to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals.
270	Then I spoke among my companions, grieving at heart:
271	‘Hear my words, my companions, though you are suffering evils,
272	so that I may tell you the prophecies of Teiresias
273	and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again
274	to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals;
275	for there, she said, was the most dread evil for us.
276	But drive the black ship on past the island.’
277	So I spoke, and their dear heart was broken.
278	And at once Eurylochus answered me with a hateful word:
279	‘You are a hard man, Odysseus; your might is beyond measure, and your limbs
280	never grow weary; truly you are made all of iron,
281	you who do not let your companions, worn out by weariness and by sleep,
282	set foot on land, where once more
283	on the sea-washed island we might make ready a pleasant supper,
284	but you bid us wander on just as we are through the swift night,
285	driven off course from the island on the misty sea.
286	Out of the nights come harsh winds, the ruin of ships;
287	how could anyone escape sheer destruction,
288	if suddenly a storm of wind should come,
289	of the South Wind or the stormy West Wind, which most of all
290	shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods?
291	But now let us yield to black night
292	and make ready our supper, staying beside the swift ship,
293	and at dawn we will go on board and put out into the wide sea.’
294	So spoke Eurylochus, and the other companions applauded.
295	And then indeed I knew that a god was devising evil,
296	and I spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
297	‘Eurylochus, truly you are pressing me hard, one man alone.
298	But come now, all of you, swear me a mighty oath:
299	if we find a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep,
300	that no one, in evil recklessness,
301	will kill a cow or a sheep; but at your ease
302	eat the food that immortal Circe gave.’
303	So I spoke, and at once they swore that they would not, as I bade them.
304	But when they had sworn and completed the oath,
305	we moored the well-made ship in the hollow harbor
306	near sweet water, and my companions went out
307	of the ship, and then skillfully made ready their supper.
308	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
309	then they remembered and wept for their dear companions
310	whom Scylla had seized from the hollow ship and devoured;
311	and as they wept, sweet sleep came upon them.
312	But when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over,
313	Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused a blustering wind against us
314	with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered
315	land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky.
316	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
317	we anchored the ship, dragging it into a hollow cave.
318	There were the fair dancing floors and seats of the nymphs.
319	And then I called an assembly and spoke among them:
320	‘Friends, there is food and drink in the swift ship;
321	so let us keep away from the cattle, lest we suffer harm;
322	for these are the cattle and fat sheep of a dread god,
323	of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things.’
324	So I spoke, and their proud spirit was persuaded.
325	And for a whole month the South Wind blew without ceasing, nor did any other
326	of the winds arise then, except the East Wind and the South Wind.
327	And as long as they had bread and red wine,
328	they kept away from the cattle, longing for life.
329	But when all the provisions in the ship were spent,
330	and they were roaming about in quest of game, as they had to,
331	fish and birds, whatever came to their hands,
332	with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies;
333	then I went away up the island, so that I might pray
334	to the gods, in case one of them might show me a way to go home.
335	But when, going through the island, I had got away from my companions,
336	I washed my hands, where there was shelter from the wind,
337	and prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus;
338	and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids.
339	And Eurylochus led the way in evil counsel among my companions:
340	‘Hear my words, my companions, though you are suffering evils.
341	All deaths are hateful to wretched mortals,
342	but to die of hunger and meet one's fate is the most pitiable.
343	But come, let us drive off the best of the cattle of the Sun
344	and sacrifice to the immortals, who hold the wide sky.
345	And if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land,
346	we will at once build a rich temple to Hyperion the Sun
347	and put in it many fine offerings.
348	But if, angry over his straight-horned cattle,
349	he wishes to destroy our ship, and the other gods follow his lead,
350	I would rather lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave
351	than be slowly drained away on a desolate island.’
352	So spoke Eurylochus, and the other companions applauded.
353	And at once they drove off the best of the cattle of the Sun
354	from near at hand, for not far from the dark-prowed ship
355	the fine broad-browed cattle with curving horns were grazing;
356	and they stood around them and prayed to the gods,
357	having stripped tender leaves from a high-leafed oak;
358	for they had no white barley on board the well-benched ship.
359	And when they had prayed and slaughtered and flayed them,
360	and cut out the thigh-bones and wrapped them in fat,
361	making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them.
362	And they had no wine to pour over the burning offerings,
363	but poured libations with water as they roasted all the entrails.
364	But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails,
365	they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits.
366	And then sweet sleep rushed from my eyelids,
367	and I went to the swift ship and the shore of the sea.
368	But when I was close to the curving ship as I went,
369	then the sweet fragrance of the fat came round me.
370	And groaning I called out loudly to the immortal gods:
371	‘Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever,
372	truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep,
373	and my companions, staying behind, have devised a great deed.’
374	And swiftly to Hyperion the Sun came a messenger,
375	Lampetie of the long robes, to tell him that we had killed his cattle.
376	And at once he spoke among the immortals, angry at heart:
377	‘Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever,
378	punish the companions of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
379	who overweeningly have killed my cattle, in which I
380	always took delight as I went up into the starry sky
381	and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth.
382	And if they do not pay me fitting recompense for the cattle,
383	I will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead.’
384	Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
385	‘Sun, do you keep giving light among the immortals
386	and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth;
387	and as for them, I will soon strike their swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt
388	and shatter it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea.’
389	This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair;
390	and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide.
391	And when I had come down to the ship and the sea,
392	I rebuked them, going up to each in turn, but we could find
393	no remedy, for the cattle were already dead.
394	And at once the gods showed signs to them:
395	the hides crawled, and the meat on the spits lowed,
396	both roast and raw, and there was a sound as of cattle.
397	For six days then my trusty companions
398	feasted, having driven off the best of the cattle of the Sun;
399	but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day,
400	then the wind ceased raging with its storm,
401	and we quickly went on board and put out into the wide sea,
402	setting up the mast and hoisting the white sails.
403	But when we were leaving the island, and no other
404	land appeared, but only sky and sea,
405	then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud
406	above the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it.
407	And she ran on for no long time; for at once there came
408	the screaming West Wind, raging with a great storm,
409	and the storm of wind broke the forestays of the mast,
410	both of them; and the mast fell backward, and all the tackle
411	was spilled into the bilge. And on the stern of the ship
412	it struck the helmsman's head, and smashed the bones
413	of the head all together; and he, like a diver,
414	fell from the deck, and his proud spirit left his bones.
415	And at the same moment Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt at the ship;
416	and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus,
417	and was filled with sulfur, and my companions fell out of the ship.
418	And like sea crows around the black ship
419	they were carried on the waves, and the god took away their homecoming.
420	But I kept pacing through the ship, until her sides
421	were loosed from the keel by the surge, and the wave carried it along bare,
422	and snapped the mast off at the keel; but over it
423	the backstay had been flung, made of oxhide;
424	with it I lashed the two together, the keel and the mast,
425	and sitting on them I was borne by deadly winds.
426	Then the West Wind ceased raging with its storm,
427	and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart,
428	that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis.
429	All night long I was borne along, and with the rising sun
430	I came to Scylla's crag and to dread Charybdis.
431	She sucked down the salt water of the sea;
432	but I, springing up on high toward the tall wild fig tree,
433	clung to it and held on like a bat. And I could not anywhere
434	either plant my feet firmly or climb up it;
435	for its roots were far below, and its branches hung high out of reach,
436	long and great, and they overshadowed Charybdis.
437	And I held on unceasingly, until she should vomit up again
438	the mast and keel; and as I yearned for them they came,
439	late; at the hour when a man rises from the assembly for his supper,
440	one who judges the many quarrels of young men pleading their cases,
441	at that hour the timbers came to light out of Charybdis.
442	And I let myself drop from above, feet and hands,
443	and fell with a splash in the middle, beyond the very long timbers,
444	and sitting on them I rowed with my hands.
445	And the father of men and gods no longer let Scylla
446	see me; for then I would not have escaped sheer destruction.
447	From there for nine days I was borne along, and on the tenth night
448	the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso
449	lives, she of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice,
450	who loved me and looked after me. But why do I tell you this tale?
451	For I already told it to you yesterday in your house,
452	to you and to your strong wife; and it is hateful to me
453	to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told.”
