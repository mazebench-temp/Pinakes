# Homer, Odyssey, Book 7 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So he prayed there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus;
2	and the might of the two mules carried the girl to the city.
3	And when she came to the famous house of her father,
4	she halted them in the doorway, and her brothers stood
5	around her, like the immortals, and they from under the wagon
6	unyoked the mules and carried the clothing inside.
7	And she herself went to her own chamber; and a fire was kindled for her
8	by an old woman from Apeire, her chamber-woman Eurymedusa,
9	whom once the curving ships had brought from Apeire;
10	and they had picked her out as a prize for Alcinous, because over all
11	the Phaeacians he was lord, and the people listened to him as to a god.
12	She had reared white-armed Nausicaa in the halls.
13	She lit the fire for her, and made ready her supper within.
14	And then Odysseus rose to go to the city; and Athena
15	poured a thick mist around him, in friendship for Odysseus,
16	lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him,
17	should taunt him with words and ask him who he was.
18	But when he was about to enter the lovely city,
19	there the gray-eyed goddess Athena met him,
20	like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar.
21	She stood before him, and brilliant Odysseus questioned her:
22	“My child, will you not lead me to the house of the man
23	Alcinous, who is lord among these people?
24	For I have come here, a much-tried stranger,
25	far away, from a distant land; so I know no one
26	of the people who hold this city and land.”
27	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
28	“Well then, stranger, father, I will show you the house that you bid me
29	show you, since he lives near my noble father.
30	But go on in silence, just so, and I will lead the way,
31	and do not look at any man or question anyone.
32	For these men do not much put up with strangers,
33	nor do they greet with open arms and make welcome whoever comes from elsewhere.
34	Trusting in the speed of their swift ships,
35	they cross the great gulf, since the earth-shaker granted it to them;
36	their ships are swift as a wing or a thought.”
37	So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way
38	swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess.
39	But the Phaeacians famed for ships did not notice him
40	as he went through the city among them; for Athena
41	of the lovely tresses, the dread goddess, would not allow it, who poured a mist
42	over him, a heaven-sent mist, in friendship in her heart.
43	And Odysseus marveled at the harbors and the balanced ships,
44	and at the places of assembly of the heroes themselves, and the long walls,
45	high, fitted with stakes, a wonder to see.
46	But when they came to the famous house of the king,
47	then among them the gray-eyed goddess Athena began to speak:
48	“This, stranger, father, is the house that you bid me
49	show you; and you will find the kings whom Zeus has nurtured
50	feasting at their feast; but go inside, and do not in your heart
51	be afraid at all; for a bold man is better in all
52	deeds, even if he comes from some other place.
53	First you will find the lady in the halls;
54	Arete is her name, a name with a meaning, and she comes of the same
55	parents who bore King Alcinous.
56	Nausithous first was born to Poseidon the earth-shaker
57	and Periboea, most beautiful in form of women,
58	the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon,
59	who once was king over the high-hearted Giants.
60	But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself.
61	And Poseidon lay with her and begot a son,
62	great-hearted Nausithous, who was lord among the Phaeacians;
63	and Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous.
64	Him, when he had no son, Apollo, god of the silver bow, struck down,
65	newly married, in his hall, leaving one only child, a daughter,
66	Arete; and Alcinous made her his wife,
67	and honored her as no other woman on earth is honored,
68	of all the women who now keep house under their husbands.
69	So she has been honored with all their hearts, and still is,
70	by her dear children and by Alcinous himself
71	and by the people, who look on her as a god
72	and greet her with words when she walks through the city.
73	For she herself is in no way lacking in good sense:
74	for the women she favors, and for men as well, she settles quarrels.
75	If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart,
76	then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach
77	your high-roofed house and your native land.”
78	So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away
79	over the barren sea, and left lovely Scheria,
80	and came to Marathon and to wide-streeted Athens,
81	and entered the well-built house of Erechtheus. But Odysseus
82	went to the glorious house of Alcinous; and his heart pondered much
83	as he stood there, before he reached the bronze threshold.
84	For there was a gleam as of the sun or of the moon
85	throughout the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous.
86	For walls of bronze were drawn out this way and that,
87	from the threshold to the inner part, and around them a coping of dark blue enamel;
88	and golden doors shut in the well-built house;
89	silver doorposts stood in the bronze threshold,
90	and silver was the lintel above them, and golden the handle.
91	And on either side were golden and silver dogs,
92	which Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill
93	to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous,
94	being immortal and ageless all their days.
95	And within, chairs were set firm along the wall this way and that,
96	from the threshold right through to the inner part, and on them cloths,
97	fine and well-spun, were thrown, the work of women.
98	There the leaders of the Phaeacians used to sit,
99	drinking and eating; for they had unfailing store.
100	And golden young men on well-built pedestals
101	stood, holding blazing torches in their hands,
102	giving light by night throughout the house to the banqueters.
103	And he has fifty slave women in the house:
104	some grind the yellow grain at the mills,
105	and others weave at the looms and turn the yarn
106	as they sit, like the leaves of a tall poplar;
107	and from the close-woven linen the liquid olive oil drips off.
108	As much as the Phaeacians are skilled beyond all men
109	in driving a swift ship on the sea, so are the women
110	skilled at the loom; for Athena has given them above all others
111	to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits.
112	And outside the court, near the doors, is a great orchard
113	of four acres; and a fence is drawn around it on both sides.
114	There tall trees grow, flourishing,
115	pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit,
116	and sweet figs and flourishing olives.
117	Of these the fruit never perishes or fails,
118	in winter or in summer, lasting all the year; but always
119	the West Wind, blowing, brings some to growth and ripens others.
120	Pear upon pear grows old, apple upon apple,
121	and cluster upon cluster, fig upon fig.
122	And there his fruitful vineyard is rooted,
123	one part of which, a warm spot on level ground,
124	is dried by the sun, while other grapes they are gathering,
125	and others they are treading; and in front are unripe grapes
126	shedding their bloom, and others are faintly turning dark.
127	And there, by the last row, are trim garden beds
128	of every kind, growing, shining unfailingly;
129	and within are two springs: one scatters its water through all the garden,
130	and the other, on the other side, runs under the threshold of the court
131	toward the high house; from this spring the citizens drew water.
132	Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous.
133	There much-enduring brilliant Odysseus stood and gazed.
134	But when he had gazed at everything in his heart,
135	he quickly stepped over the threshold into the house.
136	And he found the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians
137	pouring libations from their cups to the keen-sighted slayer of Argus,
138	to whom they would pour the last libation, whenever they thought of bed.
139	But much-enduring brilliant Odysseus went through the house,
140	wrapped in the thick mist that Athena had poured around him,
141	until he came to Arete and King Alcinous.
142	And Odysseus threw his arms around the knees of Arete,
143	and then the god-sent mist flowed back from the man himself.
144	And they fell silent when they saw the man in the house,
145	and marveled as they looked; and Odysseus entreated her:
146	“Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor,
147	to your husband and to your knees I have come, after toiling much,
148	and to these banqueters; may the gods grant them prosperity
149	in their lives, and may each of them hand on to his children
150	the possessions in his halls and the prize the people gave him.
151	But for me, speed my escort, so that I may reach my native land
152	the sooner, since long now I suffer woes far from my friends.”
153	He spoke thus and sat down at the hearth in the ashes
154	beside the fire; and they all fell hushed in silence.
155	At last the old hero Echeneus spoke among them,
156	who was one of the older Phaeacian men,
157	and excelled in speaking, knowing many things of old.
158	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
159	“Alcinous, this is not more honorable, nor is it fitting,
160	that a stranger should sit on the ground at the hearth in the ashes;
161	and these here, waiting for your word, are holding themselves back.
162	But come, seat the stranger on a silver-studded chair,
163	raising him up, and bid the heralds
164	mix the wine, so that to Zeus who delights in thunder
165	we may pour libations too, who attends on revered suppliants;
166	and let the housekeeper give the stranger supper from the stores within.”
167	But when the sacred might of Alcinous heard this,
168	he took wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles by the hand
169	and roused him from the hearth, and seated him on a shining chair,
170	making his son, manly Laodamas, stand up,
171	who sat beside him, and whom he loved most of all.
172	And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it,
173	a fine golden one, over a silver basin,
174	for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them.
175	And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
176	adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had.
177	And much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate.
178	And then the might of Alcinous spoke to the herald:
179	“Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve out wine
180	to all throughout the hall, so that to Zeus who delights in thunder
181	we may pour libations too, who attends on revered suppliants.”
182	So he spoke, and Pontonous mixed the wine sweet to the heart,
183	and served it out to all, first pouring drops for the libation into the cups.
184	But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired,
185	then Alcinous addressed them and spoke:
186	“Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians,
187	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
188	Now that you have feasted, go home and lie down;
189	and at dawn we will call more of the elders
190	and entertain the stranger in the halls, and to the gods
191	offer fine sacrifices, and then we will remember his escort too,
192	so that the stranger, without toil and vexation,
193	may reach his native land under our escort,
194	rejoicing, and quickly, even if he is from very far away,
195	and suffer no evil and no bane in between,
196	before he sets foot on his own land; and there, after that,
197	he shall suffer whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners
198	spun for him with their thread at his birth, when his mother bore him.
199	But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven,
200	then this is something else that the gods are devising.
201	For always, before now, the gods have appeared in plain sight
202	to us, whenever we offer famous hecatombs,
203	and they feast beside us, sitting where we sit.
204	And if even a lone wayfarer meets them on his way,
205	they do not hide themselves at all, since we are near to them,
206	as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants.”
207	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
208	“Alcinous, let something else be on your mind; for I, for my part,
209	am not like the immortals, who hold the wide sky,
210	in form or stature, but like mortal men.
211	Whoever you know who bear the most misery
212	among men, to them I might liken myself in sorrows.
213	And I could tell of still more evils,
214	all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods.
215	But let me eat my supper, grieved though I am;
216	for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly,
217	which bids a man remember it under compulsion,
218	even one sorely worn down and with grief in his heart,
219	as I too have grief in my heart, but always it
220	bids me eat and drink, and makes me forget
221	all that I have suffered, and bids me take my fill.
222	But you, make haste when dawn appears,
223	so that you may set me, unhappy man, on my native land,
224	even after my many sufferings; let life leave me, once I have seen
225	my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house.”
226	So he spoke, and they all approved and bade
227	send the stranger on his way, since he had spoken fair and right.
228	But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired,
229	they went to lie down, each to his own house,
230	but brilliant Odysseus was left behind in the hall,
231	and beside him Arete and godlike Alcinous
232	were sitting; and the handmaids cleared away the gear of the feast.
233	And among them white-armed Arete began to speak;
234	for she knew the mantle and the tunic when she saw the clothes,
235	fine ones, which she herself had made with her handmaids;
236	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
237	“Stranger, I myself will be the first to ask you this:
238	Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?
239	Did you not say that you came here wandering over the sea?”
240	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
241	“It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end
242	my sorrows, since the heavenly gods have given me many;
243	but I will tell you what you ask and question me about:
244	There is an island, Ogygia, lying far off in the sea;
245	there the daughter of Atlas, wily Calypso,
246	lives, she of the lovely tresses, the dread goddess; and no one mingles with her,
247	neither of the gods nor of mortal men.
248	But me, unhappy man, a god brought to her hearth,
249	alone, when my swift ship, with a gleaming thunderbolt,
250	Zeus struck and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea.
251	There all my other noble companions perished,
252	but I took the keel of the curving ship in my arms
253	and was borne along for nine days; and on the tenth black night
254	the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso
255	lives, she of the lovely tresses, the dread goddess, who took me in
256	and loved me with care and cared for me, and said
257	that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days;
258	but she never persuaded the heart in my breast.
259	There I stayed for seven years on end, and always the garments,
260	immortal, that Calypso gave me, I kept wet with my tears;
261	but when the eighth year came round in its course,
262	then she urged me and bade me return,
263	at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned.
264	And she sent me on a raft of many bindings, and gave me much,
265	bread and sweet wine, and put immortal garments on me,
266	and she sent forth a fair wind, harmless and warm.
267	For seventeen days I sailed, crossing the sea,
268	and on the eighteenth there appeared the shadowy mountains
269	of your land, and my dear heart rejoiced,
270	ill-fated as I was; for I was yet to keep company with misery
271	much misery, which Poseidon the earth-shaker roused against me,
272	who roused the winds against me and bound up my path,
273	and stirred up the endless sea, nor would the wave
274	let me be borne on the raft as I groaned without ceasing.
275	Then the storm scattered it; but I
276	swam and cut through this gulf, until to your land
277	the wind and the water carried and drove me.
278	There, as I came out onto the land, the wave would have overpowered me,
279	dashing me against great rocks, a joyless place;
280	but I drew back and swam out again, until I came
281	to the river, where the place seemed to me the best,
282	smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind.
283	And I fell out of the water, gathering my spirit, and the ambrosial night
284	came on. And I, apart from the river fallen from Zeus,
285	came out and slept among the bushes, and drew the leaves
286	about me; and a god poured boundless sleep over me.
287	There among the leaves, grieving at heart,
288	I slept all night long, and into the dawn and to midday.
289	And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go.
290	And I noticed your daughter's handmaids on the shore
291	playing, and she herself was among them, like the goddesses;
292	I came to her as a suppliant; and she did not fall short of good sense,
293	as you would not expect one younger, on meeting you,
294	to do; for the young are always thoughtless.
295	She gave me bread in plenty and sparkling wine,
296	and bathed me in the river, and gave me these clothes.
297	This is the truth I have told you, grieved though I am.”
298	Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said:
299	“Stranger, truly this, at least, she did not devise rightly,
300	my child, in that she did not bring you with her handmaids
301	to our house; and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant.”
302	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
303	“Hero, do not rebuke the noble girl for that;
304	for she bade me follow with her handmaids,
305	but I would not, out of fear and shame,
306	lest your heart too should be indignant when you saw it;
307	for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger.”
308	Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said:
309	“Stranger, the dear heart in my breast is not such
310	as to be angry for no reason; what is due is better in all things.
311	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
312	being such a man as you are, and thinking as I do,
313	you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law,
314	staying here; and I would give you a house and possessions,
315	if you stayed willingly; but against your will no one will keep you
316	of the Phaeacians: may that never be the pleasure of father Zeus.
317	And I ordain your escort for a set day, so that you may know it well:
318	for tomorrow; and then you, overcome by sleep,
319	will lie down, and they will row over the calm, until you come
320	to your native land and your house, or wherever it is your pleasure,
321	even if it is very much farther away than Euboea,
322	which those who saw it say is the farthest away,
323	of our people, when they carried fair-haired Rhadamanthys
324	to visit Tityus, the son of Earth.
325	And they went there, and accomplished it without weariness
326	on the same day, and made their way back home.
327	And you yourself will know in your mind how far the best
328	are my ships, and my young men at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade.”
329	So he spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad,
330	and he spoke in prayer, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
331	“Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said,
332	Alcinous! Then his fame over the grain-giving earth
333	would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land.”
334	So they spoke such words to one another;
335	and white-armed Arete called to her handmaids
336	to set beds beneath the colonnade, and throw fine purple
337	blankets on them, and spread rugs over them,
338	and put woolly cloaks on top for them to wear.
339	And the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands;
340	and when they had spread the thick bed in haste,
341	they stood beside Odysseus and urged him with words:
342	“Rise and go to bed, stranger; your bed has been made.”
343	So they spoke, and it seemed welcome to him to lie down to sleep.
344	So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus,
345	on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade,
346	and Alcinous lay down in the inner part of the high house,
347	and beside him the lady his wife shared his bed and couch.
