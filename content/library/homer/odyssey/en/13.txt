# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence,
2	and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls.
3	Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said:
4	“Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house,
5	high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back
6	and sail for home again, even though you have suffered very much.
7	And to each man of you I say this, and charge you with it,
8	all who in my halls drink the sparkling wine of the elders
9	always, and listen to the singer.
10	Clothes for the stranger lie in the polished chest,
11	and intricately worked gold and all the other
12	gifts that the counselors of the Phaeacians brought here;
13	but come, let us give him a great tripod and a cauldron,
14	man by man; and we in turn will gather it among the people
15	and repay ourselves; for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift.”
16	So spoke Alcinous, and his word pleased them.
17	They went to lie down, each to his own house,
18	and when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
19	they hurried to the ship, and brought the bronze that makes men strong.
20	And the sacred might of Alcinous stowed it well,
21	under the thwarts, going himself through the ship, so that it might not
22	hinder any of the companions as they rowed, whenever they were in haste with their oars.
23	And they went to the house of Alcinous and made ready a feast.
24	And for them the sacred might of Alcinous sacrificed an ox
25	to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all,
26	and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast,
27	delighting in it; and among them a divine singer sang
28	Demodocus, honored by the people. But Odysseus
29	kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun,
30	in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home.
31	And as when a man longs for his supper, for whom all day long
32	two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field,
33	and gladly for him the light of the sun sets,
34	so that he may go about his supper, and his knees are hindered as he goes,
35	so welcome to Odysseus did the light of the sun set.
36	And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar,
37	and making it known to Alcinous most of all he said:
38	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
39	send me on my way unharmed, when you have poured libations, and farewell to you;
40	for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired,
41	an escort and gifts of friendship; and may the heavenly gods
42	make them prosper for me; and may I find my noble wife at home
43	when I return, and my dear ones unhurt.
44	And may you, staying here, gladden your wives,
45	your wedded wives, and your children; and may the gods grant you good
46	of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people.”
47	So he spoke, and they all approved and bade
48	send the stranger on his way, since he had spoken fair and right.
49	And then the might of Alcinous spoke to the herald:
50	“Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve out wine
51	to all throughout the hall, so that when we have prayed to father Zeus
52	we may send the stranger on his way to his native land.”
53	So he spoke, and Pontonous mixed the wine sweet to the heart,
54	and served it out to all, going up to each in turn; and they poured libations
55	to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky,
56	from where they sat. But brilliant Odysseus stood up,
57	and placed the two-handled cup in the hand of Arete,
58	and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
59	“Farewell to you, queen, continually, until old age
60	and death come, which are the lot of men.
61	But I am going home; and you, take your delight in this house
62	with your children and your people and King Alcinous.”
63	So saying, brilliant Odysseus stepped over the threshold,
64	and with him the might of Alcinous sent a herald
65	to lead him to the swift ship and the shore of the sea;
66	and Arete sent slave women with him,
67	one carrying a freshly washed mantle and a tunic,
68	and another she granted him, to carry the close-fitted chest along;
69	and a third brought bread and red wine.
70	And when they had come down to the ship and the sea,
71	at once in the hollow ship the noble escorts
72	took these and stowed them, all the drink and food;
73	and for Odysseus they spread a rug and a linen sheet
74	on the deck of the hollow ship, so that he might sleep unwaking,
75	at the stern; and he himself went on board and lay down
76	in silence; and they sat down at the rowing benches, each man
77	in good order, and loosed the cable from the pierced stone.
78	When they leaned back and tossed up the sea with the oar-blade,
79	then sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids,
80	unwaking, sweetest, most like death.
81	And she, as four stallions yoked together on the plain,
82	all rushing on together under the blows of the whip,
83	lift themselves high as they swiftly make their way,
84	so her stern lifted, and behind her
85	the great surging wave of the loud-roaring sea seethed.
86	And she ran on very surely and unchecked; not even a hawk,
87	a falcon, the nimblest of winged birds, could have kept pace with her.
88	So swiftly she ran, cutting the waves of the sea,
89	bearing a man whose counsels were like the gods',
90	who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart,
91	passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves;
92	but now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered.
93	When that brightest star rose up which most of all
94	comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn,
95	then the seafaring ship drew near to the island.
96	There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea,
97	in the land of Ithaca; and in it two jutting
98	headlands, sheer, crouch down toward the harbor
99	and keep off the great wave of the stormy winds
100	outside; but within, without a mooring rope, there stay
101	the well-benched ships, once they have reached the measure of anchorage.
102	And at the head of the harbor is a long-leaved olive,
103	and near it a lovely cave, misty,
104	sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads.
105	In it are mixing bowls and two-handled jars
106	of stone; and there the bees store up honey.
107	And in it are towering looms of stone, where the nymphs
108	weave sea-purple webs, a wonder to see;
109	and in it are ever-flowing waters. And it has two doors,
110	one toward the North Wind, by which men go down,
111	and the other, toward the South Wind, is for gods; by that one
112	men do not enter, but it is the way of the immortals.
113	There they rowed in, knowing the place before; and the ship
114	ran up on the land as much as half her length,
115	in her haste; so hard was she driven by the hands of the rowers.
116	And they stepped out of the well-thwarted ship onto the land,
117	and first they lifted Odysseus out of the hollow ship,
118	linen sheet and glossy rug and all,
119	and laid him down on the sand, overcome by sleep;
120	and they lifted out the possessions which the noble Phaeacians
121	had given him as he went home, through great-hearted Athena.
122	These they set all together by the trunk of the olive,
123	away from the road, lest some wayfarer
124	should come upon them and do them harm before Odysseus woke;
125	and they themselves went back home again. But the earth-shaker
126	did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus
127	at first, and he asked Zeus his plan:
128	“Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods
129	be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all,
130	the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock.
131	For even now I said that Odysseus, after suffering many evils,
132	would come home; his homecoming I never took from him
133	altogether, since you first promised it and nodded your assent.
134	But they carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea
135	and set him down in Ithaca, and gave him untold gifts,
136	bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing,
137	a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy,
138	if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil.”
139	Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
140	“Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!
141	The gods do not dishonor you at all; it would be hard
142	to assail the eldest and the best with acts of dishonor.
143	But if any man, yielding to his own force and strength,
144	does not honor you at all, vengeance is always yours, even hereafter.
145	Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart.”
146	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered him:
147	“I would do it at once, lord of the dark clouds, as you say;
148	but I always have regard for your spirit, and avoid it.
149	But now I wish the beautiful ship of the Phaeacians,
150	as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea,
151	to shatter, so that at last they may hold back and cease from escorting
152	men, and to cover their city round about with a great mountain.”
153	Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
154	“My friend, this is what seems best to my heart:
155	when all catch sight of her as she is driven on,
156	the people from the city, to turn her to stone near the land,
157	like a swift ship, so that all men may marvel,
158	and to cover their city round about with a great mountain.”
159	But when Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this,
160	he went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live.
161	There he waited; and the seafaring ship came very near,
162	coursing swiftly along; and the earth-shaker came near her,
163	and turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath
164	with a blow of the flat of his hand; and then he was gone.
165	And they spoke winged words to one another,
166	the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships.
167	And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
168	“Ah me, who has fettered the swift ship on the sea
169	as she was driven home? And she was already in plain sight.”
170	So a man would say; but they did not know how things had been brought about.
171	Then Alcinous addressed them and spoke:
172	“Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me,
173	the oracles of my father, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged
174	us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm.
175	He said that one day a beautiful ship of the Phaeacian men,
176	as it came back from an escort on the misty sea,
177	he would shatter, and would cover our city round about with a great mountain.
178	So the old man used to say; and now it is all being fulfilled.
179	Come then, let us all do as I say:
180	stop escorting mortals, whenever anyone comes
181	to our city; and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls
182	let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity
183	and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain.”
184	So he spoke, and they were afraid, and made the bulls ready.
185	So they prayed to lord Poseidon,
186	the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians,
187	standing around the altar. And brilliant Odysseus woke
188	where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it—
189	after being away so long; for the goddess had poured a mist around him,
190	Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, so that she might make him
191	unknown, and tell him each thing,
192	lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends,
193	before the suitors had paid for all their transgression.
194	Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord,
195	the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring,
196	and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees.
197	And he sprang up and stood, and looked at his native land;
198	he groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs
199	with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word:
200	“Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?
201	Are they arrogant and savage and unjust,
202	or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?
203	Where am I taking all these goods? And where am I myself
204	wandering? Would that they had stayed there among the Phaeacians
205	where they were; and I would have come to some other of the mighty kings,
206	who would have welcomed me and sent me on my way home.
207	But now I do not know where to put these things, nor yet here
208	will I leave them, lest somehow they become a prey for others.
209	Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just,
210	the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians,
211	who have brought me away to another land; truly they told me
212	they would bring me to clear-seen Ithaca, and they have not done it.
213	May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over
214	other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong.
215	But come, let me count my goods and look at them,
216	to see whether they have gone off with anything in the hollow ship.”
217	So saying, he counted the beautiful tripods and cauldrons
218	and the gold and the fine woven clothes.
219	Of these he missed nothing; but he mourned for his native land,
220	dragging himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea,
221	lamenting much. And Athena came near to him,
222	in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep,
223	very delicate, such as are the sons of lords,
224	with a well-made cape folded double around her shoulders;
225	and under her shining feet she had sandals, and in her hands a javelin.
226	And Odysseus was glad to see her, and came to meet her,
227	and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
228	“My friend, since you are the first I find in this place,
229	greetings to you, and may you not meet me with evil intent,
230	but save these things, and save me; for to you
231	I pray as to a god, and I come to your dear knees.
232	And tell me this truly, so that I may know it well,
233	what land is this, what people, what men are born here?
234	Is it one of the clear-seen islands, or some shore
235	of the deep-soiled mainland that lies upon the sea?”
236	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
237	“You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,
238	if indeed you ask about this land. It is not so very
239	nameless; very many know it,
240	both those who live toward the dawn and the sun
241	and those behind, toward the misty darkness.
242	Truly it is rugged and not good for driving horses,
243	but it is not very poor, though it is not broad.
244	For in it grows endless grain, and wine
245	grows too; and there is always rain and the rich dew;
246	it is good goat-pasturing and cattle-pasturing land; there is forest
247	of every kind, and in it are unfailing watering places.
248	Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy,
249	which they say is far from the Achaean land.”
250	So she spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad,
251	rejoicing in his own land, the land of his fathers, as she told him,
252	Pallas Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus;
253	and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
254	yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word,
255	always turning a very crafty mind in his breast:
256	“I heard of Ithaca, yes, even in wide Crete,
257	far over the sea; and now I have come here myself
258	with these goods; and leaving as much again to my children,
259	I am an exile, since I killed the dear son of Idomeneus,
260	swift-footed Orsilochus, who in wide Crete
261	outran toiling men with his swift feet,
262	because he wished to rob me of all my spoil
263	from Troy, for which I suffered sorrows in my heart,
264	passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves,
265	because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant
266	in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own.
267	Him I struck with my bronze-fitted spear as he came down
268	from the country, lying in ambush near the road with one companion.
269	A very dark night covered the sky, and no one
270	of men noticed us, and I took his life unseen.
271	But when I had killed him with the sharp bronze,
272	at once I went to a ship, and noble Phoenicians
273	I begged, and gave them spoil to satisfy their hearts;
274	I bade them take me on board and set me down at Pylos,
275	or at shining Elis, where the Epeians rule.
276	But the force of the wind drove them away from there,
277	much against their will; they did not wish to deceive me.
278	Driven off course from there, we came here by night.
279	With difficulty we rowed forward into the harbor, and none of us
280	had any thought of supper, though we sorely needed to take it,
281	but just as we were we all went ashore from the ship and lay down.
282	Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was,
283	and they took my goods out of the hollow ship
284	and set them down where I myself lay on the sands.
285	And they went aboard for well-peopled Sidon
286	and were gone; but I was left behind, grieving at heart.”
287	So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled,
288	and stroked him with her hand; and she was like a woman in form,
289	beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork;
290	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
291	“Crafty would he be, and a cheat, who should get past you
292	in tricks of every kind, even if it were a god who met you.
293	Hard man, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks, you were not, then,
294	even in your own land, to leave off your deceits
295	and thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up.
296	But come, let us no longer talk like this, since we both know
297	crafty ways; for you are by far the best of all mortals
298	in counsel and in words, and I among all the gods
299	am famed for cunning and crafty ways; yet you did not know
300	Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, who always
301	stand by you in all your toils and guard you,
302	and made you dear to all the Phaeacians.
303	And now I have come here again, to weave a plan with you
304	and to hide the goods, all that the noble Phaeacians
305	gave you as you went home, by my counsel and my mind,
306	and to tell you all the sorrows it is your lot in your well-built house
307	to endure; and you must be patient, even under compulsion,
308	and not speak out to anyone, man or woman,
309	of them all, that you have come back from your wanderings, but in silence
310	suffer many sorrows, submitting to the violence of men.”
311	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
312	“It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you,
313	even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything.
314	But this I know well, that before now you were kind to me,
315	while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy.
316	But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam
317	and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans,
318	after that I did not see you, daughter of Zeus, nor did I notice you
319	boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me.
320	But always, bearing a torn heart in my breast,
321	I wandered, until the gods freed me from misery;
322	until the time when, in the rich land of the Phaeacian men,
323	you encouraged me with words and led me into the city yourself.
324	But now I implore you by your father—for I do not think
325	that I have come to clear-seen Ithaca, but I am roaming some other
326	land; and I think that you are taunting me
327	in saying these things, to deceive my mind—
328	tell me if it is true that I have come to my dear native land.”
329	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
330	“Always such is the thought in your breast;
331	therefore I cannot abandon you, unhappy as you are,
332	because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible.
333	For gladly would another man, coming home from his wanderings,
334	have been eager to see his children and his wife in his halls;
335	but it is not yet your pleasure to learn or to hear,
336	before you have made trial of your wife yourself, who sits, just as she was,
337	in your halls; and always in misery for her
338	the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears.
339	But I never doubted it, but in my heart
340	I knew that you would come home, having lost all your companions;
341	but I did not wish to fight with Poseidon,
342	my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart,
343	angry because you blinded his dear son.
344	But come, I will show you the seat of Ithaca, so that you may be certain.
345	This is the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea,
346	and here at the head of the harbor is the long-leaved olive;
347	and near it a lovely cave, misty,
348	sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads;
349	and this is the vaulted cave where you used to offer
350	many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs;
351	and this is Mount Neritum, clothed in forest.”
352	So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view;
353	and then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad,
354	rejoicing in his own land, and kissed the grain-giving earth.
355	And at once he prayed to the nymphs, lifting up his hands:
356	“Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus, never did I
357	think to see you again; but now with my gentle vows
358	rejoice; and we will give you gifts as well, as before,
359	if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants
360	that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow.”
361	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
362	“Take heart; do not let these things trouble your mind.
363	But let us put the goods in the inner part of the wondrous cave
364	at once, now, where they may stay safe for you;
365	and let us ourselves consider how all may turn out best by far.”
366	So saying, the goddess went into the misty cave,
367	searching through the cave for its hiding places; and Odysseus
368	brought everything near, the gold and the unyielding bronze
369	and the well-made clothes, which the Phaeacians had given him.
370	And she laid them away well, and set a stone against the doors,
371	Pallas Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus.
372	Then the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive
373	and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors.
374	And among them the gray-eyed goddess Athena began to speak:
375	“Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
376	consider how you will lay hands on the shameless suitors,
377	who for three years now have been lording it in your hall,
378	wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price;
379	and she, always mourning in her heart for your homecoming,
380	gives hope to all, and makes promises to each man,
381	sending out messages, but her mind has other things in view.”
382	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
383	“Ah, truly, like Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
384	I was about to perish by an evil fate in my halls,
385	had you not told me each thing, goddess, fair and right.
386	But come, weave a plan, how I may take vengeance on them;
387	and stand beside me yourself, putting in me a might full of daring,
388	as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy.
389	If you would stand beside me, gray-eyed one, as eager as that,
390	I would fight even with three hundred men
391	with you, lady goddess, if you would help me with a ready heart.”
392	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
393	“Yes, truly I will be beside you, and you will not escape my notice,
394	whenever we are busy with these things; and I think that one or another
395	will spatter the boundless floor with blood and brains
396	among the suitors, who are devouring your substance.
397	But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals;
398	I will wither the fair skin on your supple limbs,
399	and destroy the fair hair from your head, and around you a rag
400	I will put, which anyone who sees a man wearing it would hate,
401	and I will dim your eyes, that were so beautiful before,
402	so that you may seem unsightly to all the suitors
403	and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls.
404	But you yourself go first of all to the swineherd,
405	who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike,
406	and loves your son and sensible Penelope.
407	You will find him sitting beside the swine; and they are feeding
408	by the Rock of Corax and at the spring Arethusa,
409	eating acorns to satisfy their hearts and the dark water
410	drinking, which nourish the rich lard of swine.
411	Stay there, and sitting beside him ask him about everything,
412	until I go to Sparta of the lovely women
413	to call Telemachus, your dear son, Odysseus,
414	who went to Lacedaemon with its wide dancing floors, to Menelaus,
415	to learn the report of you, whether you were still alive anywhere.”
416	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
417	“Why then did you not tell him, you who know all things in your mind?
418	Was it so that he too, wandering, might suffer sorrows
419	over the barren sea, while others devour his substance?”
420	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
421	“Do not let him be too much on your mind.
422	I myself escorted him, so that he might win noble fame
423	by going there; and he has no toil, but sits at his ease
424	in the house of the son of Atreus, and untold plenty lies beside him.
425	Truly young men in a black ship lie in ambush for him,
426	eager to kill him before he reaches his native land;
427	but I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some
428	of the suitors, who are devouring your substance.”
429	So saying, Athena touched him with her wand.
430	She withered the fair skin on his supple limbs,
431	and destroyed the fair hair from his head, and the skin
432	of an aged old man she put around all his limbs,
433	and dimmed his eyes, that were so beautiful before;
434	and around him she put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic,
435	rent, dirty, stained with foul smoke;
436	and around him she put the great skin of a swift deer,
437	worn bare; and she gave him a staff and a shabby pouch,
438	rent in many places; and on it was a twisted cord to sling it by.
439	When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then
440	went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus.
