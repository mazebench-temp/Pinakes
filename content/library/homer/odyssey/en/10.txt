# Homer, Odyssey, Book 10 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	“And we came to the island of Aeolia; and there lived
2	Aeolus, son of Hippotes, dear to the immortal gods,
3	on a floating island; and all around it is a wall
4	of bronze, unbreakable, and the rock runs up sheer.
5	And he has twelve children too in his halls,
6	six daughters and six sons in their prime.
7	And he gave his daughters to his sons to be their wives.
8	And they, always beside their dear father and devoted mother,
9	feast, and countless good things lie before them,
10	and the house, full of the savor of meat, resounds round about in the court
11	by day; and at night again, beside their modest wives,
12	they sleep on rugs and on corded beds.
13	And so we came to their city and their fine house.
14	And for a whole month he made me welcome and questioned me about each thing,
15	Ilios and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans;
16	and I told him all of it fair and right.
17	But when I in my turn asked to be on my way and bade him
18	send me on, he did not refuse at all, but made ready my escort.
19	He gave me a skin of an ox nine years old that he had flayed,
20	and in it he bound up the paths of the blustering winds;
21	for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds,
22	both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes.
23	And in the hollow ship he bound it fast with a shining cord
24	of silver, so that not even a little breath might slip past;
25	but for me he sent forth the breath of the West Wind to blow,
26	to carry the ships and ourselves; but he was not to
27	bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly.
28	For nine days we sailed on, night and day alike,
29	and on the tenth our native fields were already coming into sight,
30	and already we could see men tending fires, we were so near.
31	Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was,
32	for I was always handling the sheet of the ship, and would not give it
33	to any other of my companions, so that we might reach our native land the sooner;
34	and my companions spoke with words to one another,
35	and said that I was bringing home gold and silver,
36	gifts from Aeolus, great-hearted son of Hippotes.
37	And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
38	‘Ah, how dear and honored this man is
39	by all men, to whatever people's city and land he comes!
40	Many fine treasures he is bringing from Troy
41	out of the spoil, while we, who have gone the same road to its end,
42	are going home, all of us, with empty hands;
43	and now these things have been given him, a favor done in friendship,
44	by Aeolus. But come, quickly, let us see what these are,
45	how much gold and silver is in the skin.’
46	So they spoke, and the evil counsel of my companions prevailed;
47	they loosed the skin, and all the winds rushed out.
48	And at once the storm wind seized them and carried them out to sea,
49	weeping, away from their native land. But I
50	woke, and pondered in my noble heart
51	whether I should fall from the ship and perish in the sea,
52	or endure in silence and remain still among the living.
53	But I endured and stayed, and covering my head I lay
54	in the ship. And the ships were borne by the evil storm of wind
55	back to the Aeolian island, and my companions groaned.
56	There we went up onto the land and drew water,
57	and at once my companions took their meal beside the swift ships.
58	But when we had tasted food and drink,
59	then I took a herald with me and one companion
60	and went to the glorious house of Aeolus; and I found him
61	feasting beside his wife and his children.
62	And coming into the house, beside the doorposts, on the threshold
63	we sat down; and they were amazed in their hearts and questioned us:
64	‘How have you come, Odysseus? What evil god has attacked you?
65	Surely we sent you off with care, so that you might reach
66	your native land and your house, or wherever it is your pleasure.’
67	So they spoke, and I spoke among them, grieving at heart:
68	‘My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep,
69	cruel sleep. But make it good, my friends; for you have the strength.’
70	So I spoke, approaching them with gentle words;
71	and they fell silent; but the father answered me:
72	‘Off with you from the island, quickly, most despised of the living;
73	for it is not lawful for me to care for or to send on his way
74	a man who is hated by the blessed gods;
75	off with you, since it is as one hated by the gods that you come here.’
76	So saying, he sent me from the house, groaning heavily.
77	From there we sailed on, grieving at heart.
78	And the men's spirit was worn down by the painful rowing
79	through our own folly, since no escort showed itself any longer.
80	For six days we sailed on, night and day alike,
81	and on the seventh we came to the steep citadel of Lamus,
82	Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, where herdsman hails herdsman
83	as he drives his flock in, and the other answers as he drives his herd out.
84	There a man who took no sleep could have earned two wages,
85	one by herding cattle, the other by pasturing silver-white sheep;
86	for the paths of night and of day are close together.
87	When we had come into the famous harbor, around which a rock
88	stands lofty and unbroken on both sides,
89	and jutting headlands facing one another
90	stand out at the mouth, and the entrance is narrow,
91	there they all steered their curving ships inside.
92	They were moored within the hollow harbor,
93	close together; for no wave ever swelled in it,
94	great or small, but there was a white calm all around;
95	but I alone kept my black ship outside,
96	there at the far edge, making the cables fast to a rock;
97	and I climbed to a rugged lookout and stood there.
98	There no works of oxen or of men were to be seen;
99	only smoke we saw rising from the land.
100	Then I sent some of my companions to go and find out
101	what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth,
102	choosing two men, and sending a herald with them as the third.
103	They went ashore and took a smooth road, by which the wagons
104	brought wood down to the city from the high mountains;
105	and before the city they met a girl drawing water,
106	the strong daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian.
107	She had come down to the fair-flowing spring
108	Artacia; for from there they used to carry water to the city;
109	and they stood beside her and spoke to her, and questioned her
110	who was king of these people and over whom he ruled;
111	and she at once pointed out the high-roofed house of her father.
112	And when they had entered the glorious house, they found his wife
113	as huge as a mountain peak, and they shuddered at her.
114	And at once she called from the place of assembly famous Antiphates,
115	her husband, who devised a grim destruction for them.
116	At once he seized one of my companions and made ready his meal;
117	but the other two darted off in flight and came to the ships.
118	And he raised a cry throughout the city; and hearing it
119	the mighty Laestrygonians came, one from one place and one from another,
120	countless, not like men, but like the Giants.
121	From the cliffs they pelted us with stones, each a load for a man;
122	and at once an evil din rose up throughout the ships,
123	of men being killed and of ships being shattered together;
124	and spearing them like fish they carried them off to a joyless meal.
125	While they were killing them within the deep harbor,
126	I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
127	and with it cut the cables of my dark-prowed ship.
128	And at once I urged on my companions and bade them
129	lay to their oars, so that we might escape from evil,
130	and they all tossed up the sea with their oars, fearing destruction.
131	Gladly out to the open sea, away from the overhanging rocks, fled
132	my ship; but the others perished there in a body.
133	From there we sailed on, grieving at heart,
134	glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions.
135	And we came to the island of Aeaea; and there lived
136	Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice,
137	own sister of baleful Aeetes;
138	both were born of the Sun, who brings light to mortals,
139	and of their mother Perse, whom Oceanus begot as his child.
140	There we brought our ship in to the shore in silence,
141	into a harbor where ships can lie, and some god guided us.
142	Then we went ashore there, and for two days and two nights
143	we lay, eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together.
144	But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day,
145	then I took my spear and my sharp sword
146	and went up quickly from beside the ship to a lookout,
147	in the hope that I might see the works of mortals and hear their voices.
148	And I climbed to a rugged lookout and stood there,
149	and smoke appeared to me rising from the wide-wayed earth,
150	in the halls of Circe, through the thick brush and the woods.
151	And then I pondered in my mind and in my heart
152	whether to go and find out, since I had seen the fiery smoke.
153	And as I pondered, this seemed to me the better way:
154	first to go to the swift ship and the shore of the sea,
155	and give my companions their meal, and send some of them to find out.
156	But when I was close to the curving ship as I went,
157	then one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was,
158	and sent a great high-horned stag into my very path.
159	It was coming down to the river from its pasture in the woods
160	to drink; for the might of the sun had hold of it.
161	And as it came out, I struck it on the spine, in the middle of the back,
162	and the bronze spear passed straight through,
163	and it fell down in the dust with a cry, and its spirit flew away.
164	And setting my foot on it I drew the bronze spear out of the wound,
165	and laid it down there on the ground
166	and left it; then I plucked brushwood and willow shoots,
167	and plaiting a cable, about a fathom's length, well twisted from both ends,
168	I bound together the feet of the dread monster,
169	and went, carrying it on my neck, to the black ship,
170	leaning on my spear, since there was no way to carry it on the shoulder
171	with one hand; for it was a very great beast.
172	And I threw it down in front of the ship, and roused my companions
173	with soothing words, standing beside each man:
174	‘Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief,
175	into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us;
176	but come, as long as there is food and drink in the swift ship,
177	let us think of eating, and not be worn down by hunger.’
178	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words,
179	and uncovering their heads, along the shore of the barren sea
180	they gazed at the stag; for it was a very great beast.
181	But when they had taken their fill of looking with their eyes,
182	they washed their hands and made ready a glorious feast.
183	So all day long, until the sun went down,
184	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine;
185	when the sun went down and darkness came on,
186	and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore.
187	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
188	then I called an assembly and spoke among them all:
189	‘Hear my words, my companions, though you are suffering evils:
190	friends, we do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn,
191	nor where the sun, who brings light to mortals, goes beneath the earth,
192	nor where he rises; but let us consider quickly
193	whether there will still be any plan. I for my part do not think there is.
194	For I climbed to a rugged lookout and saw
195	an island, all around which the boundless sea is set as a crown;
196	and the island itself lies low; and in the middle of it I saw
197	smoke with my eyes, through the thick brush and the woods.’
198	So I spoke, and their dear heart was broken
199	as they remembered the deeds of Antiphates the Laestrygonian
200	and of the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men.
201	And they wept loudly, shedding big tears;
202	but there was no profit for them in their weeping.
203	But I counted off all my well-greaved companions
204	into two bands, and gave each band a leader;
205	one I led myself, and godlike Eurylochus the other.
206	And quickly we shook the lots in a bronze-fitted helmet,
207	and out leapt the lot of great-hearted Eurylochus.
208	And he set out, and with him went two and twenty companions,
209	weeping; and they left us behind them lamenting.
210	In the glens they found the house of Circe, built
211	of polished stones, in a place with a view all round;
212	and around her were mountain wolves and lions,
213	whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs.
214	Nor did these rush upon the men, but they
215	stood up on their hind legs, fawning on them with their long tails.
216	And as when dogs fawn around their master as he comes from a feast,
217	for he always brings them tidbits to please their hearts,
218	so the strong-clawed wolves and lions fawned
219	around these men; and they were afraid when they saw the dread monsters.
220	And they stood in the doorway of the goddess of the lovely tresses,
221	and they heard Circe within singing with a lovely voice
222	as she walked to and fro at a great immortal loom, such as are
223	the fine and lovely and splendid works of goddesses.
224	Then Polites, leader of men, began to speak among them,
225	who was the nearest and dearest to me of my companions:
226	‘Friends, someone within, walking to and fro at a great loom,
227	is singing beautifully, and the whole floor rings all round with it,
228	either a goddess or a woman; come, let us call to her quickly.’
229	So he spoke, and they lifted up their voices and called to her.
230	And at once she came out and opened the shining doors
231	and called them in; and they all went with her in their ignorance;
232	but Eurylochus stayed behind, for he suspected a trick.
233	She led them in and seated them on chairs and seats,
234	and for them she made a mixture of cheese and barley meal and pale honey
235	with Pramnian wine; and she mixed into the food
236	grim drugs, so that they might utterly forget their native land.
237	But when she had given it and they had drunk it off, then at once
238	she struck them with her wand and penned them in the sties.
239	And they had the heads of swine, and the voice, and the bristles,
240	and the form, but their minds were steadfast, as before.
241	So they were penned there weeping, and before them Circe
242	threw ilex acorns and oak acorns and the fruit of the cornel
243	to eat, such as swine that sleep on the ground always eat.
244	But Eurylochus came at once to the swift black ship
245	to bring the news of his companions and of their bitter fate.
246	And he could not speak out a word, though he longed to,
247	stricken at heart with great grief; and his eyes
248	were filled with tears, and his heart was set on lamenting.
249	But when we had all questioned him in amazement,
250	then he told us of the destruction of the rest of our companions:
251	‘We went, as you bade us, through the brush, shining Odysseus;
252	in the glens we found a fine house, built
253	of polished stones, in a place with a view all round.
254	There someone, walking to and fro at a great loom, was singing in a clear voice,
255	either a goddess or a woman; and they lifted up their voices and called to her.
256	And at once she came out and opened the shining doors
257	and called them in; and they all went with her in their ignorance;
258	but I stayed behind, for I suspected a trick.
259	And all at once they vanished in a body, and not one of them
260	came to light again; and I sat a long while and kept watch.’
261	So he spoke, and I slung about my shoulders my silver-studded sword,
262	great and made of bronze, and my bow about them;
263	and bade him lead me back by the same road.
264	But he took hold of my knees with both hands and begged me,
265	and lamenting addressed me with winged words:
266	‘Do not lead me there against my will, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me here.
267	For I know that you will neither come back yourself nor bring back
268	any other of your companions. No, let us flee quickly with these here;
269	for we may still escape the evil day.’
270	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
271	‘Eurylochus, you may stay here in this place,
272	eating and drinking beside the hollow black ship;
273	but I will go, for harsh necessity has come upon me.’
274	So saying, I went up from the ship and the sea.
275	But when, as I went through the sacred glens, I was about
276	to come to the great house of Circe, skilled in many drugs,
277	there Hermes of the golden wand met me
278	as I was going toward the house, in the likeness of a young man
279	with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful.
280	And he clung to my hand, and spoke to me, and called me by name:
281	‘Where are you going now again, unhappy man, alone across the heights,
282	not knowing the place? And your companions there in the house of Circe
283	are penned like swine, and keep to close-packed sties.
284	Is it to free them that you come here? I tell you that you
285	will not come home yourself, but will stay where the others are.
286	But come, I will free you from evils and save you.
287	Here, take this good drug and go into the house of Circe,
288	and it will ward off the evil day from your head.
289	And I will tell you all the baneful thoughts of Circe.
290	She will make you a mixed drink, and put drugs in the food;
291	but even so she will not be able to bewitch you; for it will not let her,
292	the good drug that I will give you; and I will tell you each thing.
293	When Circe strikes you with her very long wand,
294	then draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh
295	and spring at Circe as though eager to kill her.
296	And in fear of you she will bid you lie with her;
297	then do not refuse the bed of the goddess any longer,
298	so that she may free your companions and care for you yourself;
299	but bid her swear a great oath by the blessed gods
300	that she will not plan some other evil bane against you yourself,
301	lest, when you are stripped, she make you a coward and unmanned.’
302	So he spoke, and the slayer of Argus gave me the drug,
303	pulling it from the ground, and showed me its nature.
304	At the root it was black, but its flower was like milk;
305	moly the gods call it; and it is hard to dig up
306	for mortal men; but the gods can do all things.
307	Then Hermes went away to high Olympus
308	across the wooded island, and I to the house of Circe
309	went my way; and my heart heaved with many thoughts as I went.
310	And I stood at the doors of the goddess of the lovely tresses;
311	there I stood and shouted, and the goddess heard my voice.
312	And at once she came out and opened the shining doors
313	and called me in; and I followed, grieving at heart.
314	She led me in and seated me on a silver-studded chair,
315	fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under my feet;
316	and she made me the mixed drink in a golden cup, for me to drink,
317	and put the drug in it, meaning evil in her heart.
318	But when she had given it and I had drunk it off, and it did not bewitch me,
319	she struck me with her wand, and spoke to me, and called me by name:
320	‘Go now to the sty, and lie down with the rest of your companions.’
321	So she spoke, and I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
322	and sprang at Circe as though eager to kill her.
323	And she, with a great cry, ran in under it and clasped my knees,
324	and lamenting addressed me with winged words:
325	‘Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?
326	Wonder holds me, how you drank these drugs and were not bewitched at all;
327	for no other man either has withstood these drugs,
328	whoever drinks them, once they have passed the barrier of his teeth.
329	But there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched.
330	Surely you are Odysseus of many turns; he always told me
331	you would come, the slayer of Argus of the golden wand,
332	returning from Troy in your swift black ship.
333	But come, put your sword in its sheath, and then let us two
334	go up into our bed, so that, having lain together
335	in bed and love, we may trust one another.’
336	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
337	‘Circe, how can you bid me be kind to you,
338	you who turned my companions into swine in your halls,
339	and keeping me myself here, with treachery in your heart, you bid me
340	go into your chamber and go up into your bed,
341	so that when I am stripped you may make me a coward and unmanned?
342	No, I would not be willing to go up into your bed,
343	unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath
344	that you will not plan some other evil bane against me myself.’
345	So I spoke, and at once she swore that she would not, as I bade her.
346	But when she had sworn and completed the oath,
347	then I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe.
348	Meanwhile the handmaids were busy in the halls,
349	four of them, who are her serving women in the house;
350	and they are born of the springs and of the groves
351	and of the sacred rivers that flow on to the sea.
352	One of them threw fine blankets over the chairs,
353	purple ones on top, and spread linen cloths beneath them;
354	another drew up tables before the chairs,
355	silver ones, and set golden baskets on them;
356	the third mixed wine sweet to the heart in a mixing bowl,
357	sweet wine in a silver bowl, and served out golden cups;
358	and the fourth brought water and kindled a great fire
359	under a great tripod; and the water grew warm.
360	But when the water had boiled in the gleaming bronze,
361	she set me in a bathtub and bathed me from the great tripod,
362	mixing it to suit my heart, pouring it over my head and shoulders,
363	until she had taken from my limbs the weariness that destroys life.
364	And when she had bathed me and anointed me richly with olive oil,
365	and put a fine cloak and a tunic around me,
366	she led me in and seated me on a silver-studded chair,
367	fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under my feet.
368	And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it,
369	a fine golden one, over a silver basin,
370	for me to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside me.
371	And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before me,
372	adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had.
373	And she bade me eat; but it did not please my heart,
374	but I sat with my thoughts elsewhere, and my heart foreboded evil.
375	And when Circe saw me sitting there, and not putting out
376	my hands to the food, but held by strong grief,
377	standing close by me, she addressed me with winged words:
378	‘Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man without speech,
379	eating your heart, and do not touch food or drink?
380	Do you suspect some other trick? You have no need
381	to fear; for I have already sworn you a mighty oath.’
382	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
383	‘Circe, what man who was fair-minded
384	would bring himself to taste food and drink
385	before he had set his companions free and seen them with his eyes?
386	But if with a ready heart you bid me drink and eat,
387	release them, so that I may see my trusty companions with my eyes.’
388	So I spoke, and Circe went out through the hall
389	with her wand in her hand, and opened the doors of the sty,
390	and drove them out, looking like fat hogs nine years old.
391	Then they stood facing her, and she went among them
392	and anointed each of them with another drug.
393	And from their limbs fell the bristles that had been made to grow before
394	by the ruinous drug that lady Circe had given them;
395	and they became men again, younger than they had been before,
396	and much handsomer and taller to look upon.
397	And they knew me, and each of them clung to my hands.
398	And on them all came the longing for lament, and all round the house
399	rang terribly; and the goddess herself was moved to pity.
400	And she came and stood near me and spoke, brilliant among goddesses:
401	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
402	go now to the swift ship and the shore of the sea.
403	First of all drag the ship up onto the land,
404	and put your possessions and all the tackle away in caves,
405	and come back yourself and bring your trusty companions.’
406	So she spoke, and my proud spirit was persuaded,
407	and I went to the swift ship and the shore of the sea.
408	Then I found my trusty companions at the swift ship
409	lamenting piteously, shedding big tears.
410	And as when the calves of the fields, around the cows of the herd
411	as they come back to the dung-yard when they have had their fill of pasture,
412	all skip together to meet them, and the pens no longer
413	hold them, but lowing without ceasing they run around
414	their mothers: so those men, when they saw me with their eyes,
415	poured around me in tears; and it seemed to their hearts
416	as if they had come to their native land and to the very city
417	of rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised.
418	And lamenting they addressed me with winged words:
419	‘At your return, you who are nurtured by Zeus, we are as glad
420	as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land;
421	but come, tell us of the destruction of the rest of our companions.’
422	So they spoke, and I spoke to them with gentle words:
423	‘First of all let us drag the ship up onto the land,
424	and put our possessions and all the tackle away in caves;
425	and make haste, all of you, to follow me,
426	so that you may see your companions in the sacred house of Circe
427	drinking and eating; for they have unfailing plenty.’
428	So I spoke, and they quickly obeyed my words.
429	Only Eurylochus tried to hold back all my companions,
430	and he spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
431	‘Ah, wretched men, where are we going? Why do you long for these evils,
432	to go down to the hall of Circe, who will make all of us
433	either swine or wolves or lions,
434	to guard her great house for her even under compulsion,
435	as the Cyclops penned them in, when to his farmyard came
436	our companions, and with them went this bold Odysseus;
437	for it was through his recklessness that they too perished.’
438	So he spoke, and I pondered in my mind
439	whether to draw the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh,
440	and strike off his head with it and bring it to the ground,
441	kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near; but my companions
442	tried to hold me back with soothing words, one from one side and one from another:
443	‘Sprung from Zeus, we will leave this man, if you bid it,
444	to stay there beside the ship and guard the ship,
445	but lead us to the sacred house of Circe.’
446	So saying, they went up from the ship and the sea.
447	Nor was Eurylochus left behind beside the hollow ship,
448	but he followed; for he was afraid of my terrible rebuke.
449	Meanwhile in her house Circe had bathed the rest of my companions
450	with care and anointed them richly with olive oil,
451	and put woolly cloaks and tunics around them;
452	and we found them all feasting well in the halls.
453	And when they saw one another and knew each other face to face,
454	they wept and mourned, and the house groaned round about.
455	And she came and stood near me and spoke, brilliant among goddesses:
457	‘Rouse this swelling lament no longer; I myself know too
458	both all the sorrows you suffered on the fish-filled sea,
459	and all the harm that merciless men did you on land.
460	But come, eat food and drink wine,
461	until you take heart again in your breasts,
462	such as you had when you first left your native land
463	of rugged Ithaca. But now you are withered and spiritless,
464	always remembering your hard wandering, and never is your heart
465	in gladness, since you have suffered very much.’
466	So she spoke, and our proud spirit was persuaded.
467	There every day, for a full year's round,
468	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine.
469	But when a year had passed, and the seasons returned in their course
470	as the months waned, and the long days had come full circle,
471	then my trusty companions called me out and said:
472	‘Strange one, think now at last of your native land,
473	if it is decreed for you to be saved and to reach
474	your high-roofed house and your native land.’
475	So they spoke, and my proud spirit was persuaded.
476	So all day long, until the sun went down,
477	we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine.
478	When the sun went down and darkness came on,
479	they lay down to sleep throughout the shadowy halls.
480	But I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe
481	and entreated her by her knees, and the goddess heard my voice;
482	and I spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
483	‘Circe, fulfill for me the promise you made,
484	to send me home; my heart is eager now,
485	and the hearts of my other companions, who waste my dear heart away
486	mourning around me, whenever you are away.’
487	So I spoke, and at once she answered, brilliant among goddesses:
488	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
489	stay no longer in my house against your will.
490	But first you must accomplish another journey and come
491	to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone,
492	to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias,
493	the blind seer, whose wits are steadfast;
494	to him even in death Persephone has given a mind,
495	that he alone should be wise; but the others flit as shadows.’
496	So she spoke, and my dear heart was broken,
497	and I wept as I sat on the bed, and my heart
498	no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun.
499	But when I had had my fill of weeping and rolling about,
500	then at last I answered her with words and said:
501	‘Circe, who will guide us on this journey?
502	No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship.’
503	So I spoke, and at once she answered, brilliant among goddesses:
504	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
505	let no longing for a guide trouble you beside your ship,
506	but set up your mast and spread the white sails,
507	and sit; and the breath of the North Wind will carry her.
508	But when in your ship you have crossed Oceanus,
509	where there is a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone,
510	tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit,
511	beach your ship there by deep-eddying Oceanus,
512	and go yourself into the dank house of Hades.
513	There into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon
514	and Cocytus, which is a branch of the water of Styx,
515	and there is a rock, and the meeting of the two loud-resounding rivers;
516	there then, hero, draw close, as I bid you,
517	and dig a pit about a cubit's length this way and that,
518	and around it pour a drink offering to all the dead,
519	first with honey and milk, then with sweet wine,
520	and the third time with water; and sprinkle white barley meal over it.
521	And earnestly implore the strengthless heads of the dead,
522	vowing that, when you come to Ithaca, a barren heifer, the best you have,
523	you will sacrifice in your halls, and heap the pyre with good things,
524	and that to Teiresias apart you will sacrifice a sheep, to him alone,
525	all black, that stands out among your flocks.
526	But when you have begged the glorious tribes of the dead with prayers,
527	then sacrifice a ram and a black ewe,
528	turning them toward Erebus, and turn yourself away,
529	making toward the streams of the river; and there many
530	souls of the dead who have died will come.
531	Then urge on your companions and bid them
532	flay the sheep that lie there slaughtered with the pitiless bronze,
533	and burn them, and pray to the gods,
534	to mighty Hades and dread Persephone;
535	and you yourself draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh
536	and sit there, and do not let the strengthless heads of the dead
537	come near the blood until you have questioned Teiresias.
538	Then the seer will come to you at once, leader of the people,
539	he will tell you the way and the measures of your path
540	and your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea.’
541	So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came.
542	And she put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing;
543	and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle,
544	fine and lovely, and around her waist she cast a girdle,
545	fine and golden, and on her head she set a head covering.
546	But I went through the house and urged on my companions
547	with soothing words, standing beside each man:
548	‘No longer sleep now, drowsing in sweet sleep,
549	but let us go; for lady Circe has shown me the way.’
550	So I spoke, and their proud spirit was persuaded.
551	But not even from there did I lead my companions away unharmed.
552	There was one Elpenor, the youngest, not very
553	valiant in war nor sound in his wits;
554	he, apart from my companions, in the sacred house of Circe,
555	longing for the cool air, had lain down to sleep, sodden with wine.
556	And hearing the din and the thudding of the companions as they moved about,
557	he sprang up suddenly, and forgot in his mind
558	to come back down by going to the long ladder,
559	but fell straight down from the roof; and his neck
560	was broken from the vertebrae, and his soul went down to Hades.
561	And as they were going, I spoke among them:
562	‘You think, no doubt, that you are going home to your dear native land;
563	but Circe has ordained another journey for us,
564	to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone,
565	to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias.’
566	So I spoke, and their dear heart was broken,
567	and they sat down there and lamented, and tore their hair;
568	but there was no profit for them in their weeping.
569	But when we were going to the swift ship and the shore of the sea,
570	grieving, shedding big tears,
571	meanwhile Circe had gone and tied up beside the black ship
572	a ram and a black ewe,
573	slipping past us easily; who could see a god against his will
574	with his eyes, going either this way or that?”
