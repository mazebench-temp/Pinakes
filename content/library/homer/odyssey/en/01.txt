# Homer, Odyssey, Book 1 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who was driven
2	far astray, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy;
3	and he saw the cities of many men and learned their mind,
4	and on the sea he suffered many sorrows in his heart,
5	seeking to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions.
6	But even so he did not save his companions, eager though he was;
7	for they perished through their own recklessness,
8	fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun;
9	and he took away from them the day of their homecoming.
10	Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, beginning where you will, tell us too.
11	Now all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction,
12	were at home, having escaped both the war and the sea;
13	but him alone, wanting his homecoming and his wife,
14	the lady nymph Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, held back
15	in her hollow caves, longing for him to be her husband.
16	But when, as the years circled, the year had come
17	in which the gods had spun the thread for him to return home
18	to Ithaca, not even then was he free of his trials,
19	or among his friends. And all the gods pitied him
20	except Poseidon; and he raged relentlessly
21	against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land.
22	But he had gone to the Ethiopians, who are far away,
23	the Ethiopians, who are divided in two, the farthest of men,
24	some where Hyperion sets and some where he rises,
25	to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams.
26	There he sat at the feast and took his delight; but the others
27	were all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus.
28	And among them the father of men and gods began to speak;
29	for in his heart he remembered noble Aegisthus,
30	whom the son of Agamemnon, far-famed Orestes, had killed;
31	remembering him, he spoke his words among the immortals:
32	“Ah, how mortals now blame the gods!
33	For they say that evils come from us; but they themselves
34	through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate,
35	as now Aegisthus, against fate, married the wife of the son of Atreus,
36	his wedded wife, and killed him on his return,
37	knowing it was sheer destruction, since we ourselves had told him beforehand,
38	sending Hermes, the keen-sighted slayer of Argus,
39	neither to kill the man himself nor to woo his wife;
40	for from Orestes vengeance will come for the son of Atreus,
41	when he comes of age and longs for his own land.
42	So Hermes spoke, but the mind of Aegisthus
43	he did not persuade, for all his good intent; and now he has paid back all at once.”
44	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
45	“Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords,
46	yes, truly that man lies fallen in a fitting destruction;
47	so may any other perish too who did such things.
48	But my heart is torn for wise-hearted Odysseus,
49	ill-fated man, who long now suffers woes far from his friends
50	on a sea-washed island, where the navel of the sea is.
51	It is an island full of trees, and a goddess has her dwelling there,
52	the daughter of baleful Atlas, who knows the depths
53	of the whole sea, and himself holds the tall pillars
54	that hold earth and heaven apart.
55	It is his daughter who holds the unhappy man back, mourning,
56	and always with gentle and wheedling talk
57	she bewitches him, so that he may forget Ithaca; but Odysseus,
58	eager to see even the smoke leaping up
59	from his own land, longs to die. Yet not even your
60	dear heart is moved, Olympian. Did not Odysseus
61	please you beside the ships of the Argives, offering sacrifices
62	in wide Troy? Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?”
63	Answering her, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said:
64	“My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
65	How then could I forget divine Odysseus,
66	who is first among mortals in mind, and first in sacrifices
67	given to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky?
68	But Poseidon the earth-holder is stubbornly forever
69	angry because of the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye,
70	godlike Polyphemus, whose power is the greatest
71	among all the Cyclopes; and the nymph Thoosa bore him,
72	the daughter of Phorcys, who rules over the barren sea,
73	having lain with Poseidon in the hollow caves.
74	From that time Poseidon the earth-shaker, as for Odysseus,
75	does not kill him, but drives him wandering from his native land.
76	But come, let all of us here take thought for
77	his homecoming, how he may come; and Poseidon will let go
78	his anger; for he will not be able, against all
79	the immortals, against the will of the gods, to strive alone.”
80	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
81	“Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords,
82	if this is now truly dear to the blessed gods,
83	that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house,
84	then let us urge on Hermes, the guide, the slayer of Argus,
85	to the island of Ogygia, so that with all speed
86	he may tell the nymph of the lovely tresses our unerring counsel,
87	the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus, that he may return;
88	while I will go to Ithaca, so that I may urge his son on
89	the more, and put might in his heart,
90	to call the long-haired Achaeans to an assembly
91	and speak out to all the suitors, who are forever
92	slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns.
93	And I will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
94	to learn of his dear father's homecoming, if he may hear of it somewhere,
95	and so that noble fame may be his among men.”
96	So saying, she bound under her feet her fine sandals,
97	ambrosial, golden, which carried her over the waters
98	and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind;
99	and she took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
100	heavy, huge, and massive, with which she overpowers the ranks of men,
101	of heroes, against whom she, the daughter of the mighty father, bears resentment.
102	And she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus,
103	and stood in the land of Ithaca at the doorway of Odysseus,
104	on the threshold of the courtyard; and in her hand she held the bronze spear,
105	in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes.
106	And she found the proud suitors. They at that time
107	were delighting their hearts with game pieces in front of the doors,
108	sitting on the hides of oxen that they themselves had killed;
109	and heralds and ready attendants were busy for them,
110	some mixing wine and water in the mixing bowls,
111	and others again with sponges full of holes were washing the tables
112	and setting them out, and others were dividing much meat.
113	And godlike Telemachus was by far the first to see her;
114	for he was sitting among the suitors, grieving at heart,
115	seeing in his mind his brave father, if he might come from somewhere
116	and make a scattering of the suitors here through the house,
117	and himself have his honor and rule over his own house.
118	With this in mind, as he sat among the suitors, he caught sight of Athena.
119	He went straight to the doorway, and he was indignant in his heart
120	that a stranger should stand long at the doors; and standing close by
121	he took her right hand and received the bronze spear,
122	and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
123	“Welcome, stranger! Here with us you will be treated as a friend; and then,
124	when you have tasted the meal, you will tell us what you need.”
125	So saying, he led the way, and Pallas Athena followed.
126	And when they were inside the high house,
127	he carried the spear and stood it against a tall pillar
128	inside a polished spear-rack, where many other
129	spears of stout-hearted Odysseus stood,
130	and he led her and seated her on a chair—spreading a cloth beneath her—
131	fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet.
132	And beside her he set an inlaid chair for himself, apart from the others,
133	the suitors, so that the stranger, troubled by the din,
134	should not loathe his meal, coming among overbearing men,
135	and so that he might ask him about his absent father.
136	And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it,
137	a fine golden one, over a silver basin,
138	for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them.
139	And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
140	adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had;
141	and a carver lifted up platters of meat and set them before them,
142	of every kind, and set golden cups beside them;
143	and a herald went to and fro, pouring wine for them.
144	And the proud suitors came in. Then they
145	sat down in order on the chairs and the seats,
146	and the heralds poured water over their hands,
147	and slave women heaped bread beside them in baskets,
148	and the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink.
149	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
150	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
151	the suitors had other things on their minds,
152	song and dancing; for these are the accompaniments of a feast.
153	And a herald put a beautiful lyre in the hands
154	of Phemius, who sang among the suitors under compulsion.
155	And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song.
156	But Telemachus spoke to gray-eyed Athena,
157	putting his head close, so that the others might not hear:
158	“Dear stranger, will you be indignant with me for what I say?
159	These men care for these things, the lyre and song,
160	easily, since they eat another man's substance without payment,
161	a man whose white bones are rotting somewhere in the rain,
162	lying on the mainland, or the wave rolls them in the sea.
163	If they were to see him come home to Ithaca,
164	they would all pray to be nimbler of foot
165	rather than richer in gold and clothing.
166	But now he has perished so, by an evil fate, and there is no
167	comfort for us, even if some one of the men on earth
168	says that he will come; but the day of his homecoming is lost.
169	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
170	Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?
171	And on what sort of ship did you come? And how did sailors
172	bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
173	For I do not think that you came here on foot.
174	And tell me this truly, so that I may know it well,
175	whether you are newly come, or are a guest-friend
176	from our fathers' time; for many other men used to come
177	to our house, since he too was one who went about among men.”
178	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
179	“Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly.
180	I claim to be Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus,
181	and I am lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar.
182	And now I have come down here, as you see, with my ship and companions,
183	sailing over the wine-dark sea to men of another tongue,
184	to Temese, for bronze, and I carry gleaming iron.
185	My ship stands over there, by the fields, away from the city,
186	in the harbor of Rheithron, under wooded Neion.
187	We claim to be each other's guest-friends from our fathers' time,
188	from of old, if you will only go and ask the old man,
189	the hero Laertes, who, they say, no longer comes to the city,
190	but far away in the country suffers woes
191	with an old handmaid, who sets before him food and drink
192	whenever weariness takes hold of his limbs
193	as he creeps along the slope of his vineyard.
194	And now I have come; for they did say that he was at home,
195	your father; but the gods are hindering him from his journey.
196	For brilliant Odysseus has not yet died upon the earth,
197	but somewhere, still alive, he is held back on the wide sea,
198	on a sea-washed island, and harsh men hold him,
199	savage men, who keep him back against his will.
200	But now I will prophesy to you, as the immortals
201	put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled,
202	though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds.
203	He will not be away much longer from his dear native land,
204	not even if bonds of iron hold him;
205	he will devise how to return, since he is a man of many devices.
206	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account,
207	whether you, so tall, are truly the son of Odysseus himself.
208	Terribly like him are you, in your head and your fine eyes,
209	since we used to meet each other so very often,
210	before he embarked for Troy, where the others too,
211	the best of the Argives, went in their hollow ships;
212	and since then I have not seen Odysseus, nor he me.”
213	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
214	“Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
215	My mother says that I am his; but I for my part
216	do not know; for no one yet has known his own parentage himself.
217	I wish that I had been the son of some blessed man
218	whom old age overtook among his own possessions.
219	But as it is, of him who was the most ill-starred of mortal men
220	they say that I was born, since you ask me this.”
221	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
222	“The gods have not made your lineage nameless for the time to come,
223	since Penelope bore such a son as you.
224	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
225	What feast, what throng is this? What need do you have of it?
226	A banquet, or a wedding? For this is surely no shared feast;
227	so overbearingly, like men committing outrage, they seem to me
228	to feast through the house. A man would be indignant
229	seeing so much that is shameful, any man of good sense who came among them.”
230	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
231	“Stranger, since you ask me this and question me,
232	this house was once likely to be rich and flawless,
233	while that man was still at home;
234	but now the gods have willed otherwise, devising evil,
235	who have put him out of sight beyond all other
236	men; for I would not grieve so for him if he were dead,
237	if he had been brought down among his companions in the land of the Trojans,
238	or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war.
239	Then all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him,
240	and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come.
241	But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report;
242	he is gone, out of sight, unheard of, and pains and lamentations
243	he has left to me. Nor is it for him alone that I groan and mourn,
244	since the gods have brought other evil sorrows on me.
245	For all the best men who hold command over the islands,
246	Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
247	and all who rule over craggy Ithaca,
248	so many woo my mother and wear down my house.
249	And she neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can she
250	make an end; and with their eating they are wasting away
251	my house; soon they will tear me apart as well.”
252	And chafing at this, Pallas Athena spoke to him:
253	“Ah, truly you are in great need of absent Odysseus,
254	who would lay his hands on the shameless suitors.
255	If only he would come now and stand at the outer doors of the house,
256	with helmet and shield and two spears,
257	such as he was when I first saw him
258	in our house, drinking and taking his delight,
259	on his way back from Ephyra, from Ilus, son of Mermerus—
260	for Odysseus had gone there too on his swift ship,
261	seeking a man-slaying drug, so that he might have it
262	to smear his bronze-fitted arrows; but he would not
263	give it to him, since he feared the indignation of the gods who live forever,
264	but my father gave it to him; for he loved him terribly—
265	if only Odysseus, such as that, would engage the suitors:
266	they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be.
267	But these things lie on the knees of the gods,
268	whether he will return and take vengeance, or not,
269	in his own halls; but I bid you consider
270	how you may drive the suitors out of the hall.
271	Come now, listen, and heed my words.
272	Tomorrow call the Achaean heroes to an assembly
273	and declare your word to all, and let the gods be witnesses.
274	Bid the suitors scatter to their own homes,
275	and your mother, if her heart is eager to marry,
276	let her go back to the hall of her father, a man of great power;
277	and they will arrange a wedding and make ready the bride-price,
278	a very great one, as much as is fitting to go with a dear daughter.
279	And to you yourself I will give shrewd counsel, if you will obey me:
280	fit out with twenty oarsmen the best ship there is,
281	and go to learn of your father, who has long been gone,
282	in the hope that some mortal may tell you, or you may hear a rumor
283	from Zeus, which most of all brings report to men.
284	First go to Pylos and ask brilliant Nestor,
285	and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus;
286	for he was the last of the bronze-armored Achaeans to come home.
287	If you hear of your father's life and homecoming,
288	then, worn down though you are, you could endure for another year;
289	but if you hear that he is dead and no longer living,
290	then come home to your dear native land,
291	and heap up a mound for him, and give him funeral rites,
292	very many, as many as are fitting, and give your mother to a husband.
293	But when you have done all this and brought it to an end,
294	then consider in your mind and in your heart
295	how you may kill the suitors in your halls,
296	whether by guile or openly; you have no need
297	to keep up childish ways, since you are no longer of that age.
298	Or have you not heard what fame brilliant Orestes won
299	among all men, when he killed his father's slayer,
300	scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father?
301	You too, my friend—for I see that you are very handsome and tall—
302	be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you.
303	But now I will go down to my swift ship
304	and my companions, who must be chafing greatly as they wait for me;
305	let all this be your own care, and heed my words.”
306	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
307	“Stranger, truly you say these things in friendship,
308	as a father to his son, and I will never forget them.
309	But come now, wait, eager though you are for your journey,
310	so that when you have bathed and satisfied your dear heart,
311	you may go to your ship with a gift, rejoicing in your heart,
312	a precious one, very beautiful, which will be a treasure for you
313	from me, such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends.”
314	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena answered him:
315	“Do not hold me back now any longer, longing as I am for my journey.
316	And the gift that your dear heart bids you give me,
317	give it to me on my way back, to carry home,
318	taking out a very beautiful one; and it will bring you its worth in return.”
319	So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away,
320	and like a bird she flew upward and away; and in his heart
321	she put might and daring, and made him think of his father
322	even more than before. And he, perceiving it in his mind,
323	was amazed in his heart; for he thought that it was a god.
324	And at once he went among the suitors, a man equal to the gods.
325	The renowned singer was singing to them, and they in silence
326	sat listening; and he sang of the homecoming of the Achaeans,
327	the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy.
328	And from her upper room she understood in her mind the wondrous song,
329	the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope;
330	and she came down the high stairway of her house,
331	not alone; two handmaids went along with her.
332	And when she, brilliant among women, came to the suitors,
333	she stood by the doorpost of the strongly made hall,
334	holding her shining veil before her cheeks;
335	and a devoted handmaid stood on either side of her.
336	Then, bursting into tears, she spoke to the divine singer:
337	“Phemius, you know many other enchantments for mortals,
338	deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous;
339	sing them one of these as you sit among them, and let them in silence
340	drink their wine; but cease from this song,
341	this grim song, which always wears down my dear heart in my breast,
342	since upon me above all has come grief not to be forgotten.
343	Such a head do I long for, remembering always
344	the man whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos.”
345	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
346	“My mother, why do you begrudge the trusty singer
347	giving delight in whatever way his mind is roused? It is not the singers
348	who are to blame, but Zeus, I think, is to blame, who gives
349	to toiling men, to each, as he wills.
350	There is no blame on this man for singing the evil fate of the Danaans;
351	for men praise that song the more
352	which comes newest to those who listen.
353	Let your heart and spirit bear to listen;
354	for Odysseus was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming
355	in Troy, but many other men perished too.
356	But go into the house and attend to your own work,
357	the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids
358	to go about their work; and speech will be the concern of men,
359	of all of them, and mine most of all; for mine is the power in this house.”
360	And she, amazed, went back into the house;
361	for she laid her son's wise word to heart.
362	And going up to her upper room with her handmaids,
363	she wept then for Odysseus, her dear husband, until sweet
364	sleep was cast upon her eyelids by gray-eyed Athena.
365	And the suitors raised a din throughout the shadowy halls,
366	and they all prayed to lie beside her in bed.
367	And among them wise Telemachus began to speak:
368	“Suitors of my mother, overweening in your arrogance,
369	now let us feast and take our delight, and let there be no
370	shouting, since it is a fine thing to listen to a singer
371	such as this one is, like the gods in his voice.
372	And at dawn let us all go and sit in the assembly,
373	so that I may speak out my word to you without regard for anyone,
374	that you leave my halls; and provide other feasts,
375	eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house.
376	But if this seems to you better and more profitable,
377	that one man's substance should be destroyed without payment,
378	then eat it up; but I will call upon the gods who live forever,
379	if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done;
380	then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house.”
381	So he spoke, and they all, biting their lips with their teeth,
382	marveled at Telemachus, that he spoke so boldly.
383	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him in turn:
384	“Telemachus, surely the gods themselves are teaching you
385	to be a man of lofty speech and to speak boldly.
386	May the son of Cronus never make you king in sea-girt Ithaca,
387	which is yours by birth from your father.”
388	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
389	“Antinous, will you be indignant with me for what I say?
390	This too I would be willing to win, if Zeus should grant it.
391	Do you think this is the worst thing among men?
392	For it is no bad thing to be a king; at once his house
393	grows rich, and he himself is more honored.
394	But there are other kings of the Achaeans,
395	many in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old;
396	let one of them have this, since brilliant Odysseus is dead;
397	but I will be lord of our own house
398	and of the slaves whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil for me.”
399	Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face:
400	“Telemachus, truly these things lie on the knees of the gods,
401	who will be king of the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca;
402	but may you keep your possessions yourself and rule over your own house.
403	May that man never come who by force, against your will,
404	will rob you of your possessions, while Ithaca is still inhabited.
405	But I want to ask you, good sir, about the stranger,
406	where this man comes from, from what land he claims to be,
407	and where are his birthplace and his native fields.
408	Does he bring some news of your father's coming,
409	or has he come in want of some business of his own?
410	How he sprang up and was gone at once, and did not wait
411	to be known; for he was not like a base man to look on.”
412	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
413	“Eurymachus, truly my father's homecoming is lost;
414	so I no longer trust in news, if any should come from somewhere,
415	nor do I heed any oracle that my mother
416	may ask of a prophet when she has called him to the hall.
417	This man is my guest-friend from our fathers' time, from Taphos,
418	and he claims to be Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus,
419	and he is lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar.”
420	So Telemachus spoke, but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess.
421	And they turned to dancing and to lovely song
422	and took their delight, and waited for evening to come;
423	and as they took their delight black evening came on.
424	Then they went to lie down, each to his own house.
425	But Telemachus, where his chamber in the beautiful courtyard
426	had been built high, in a place with a view all round,
427	went there to his bed, pondering many things in his mind.
428	And with him, carrying blazing torches, went true-hearted
429	Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor,
430	whom Laertes once bought with his own possessions
431	when she was still in her first youth, and gave the worth of twenty oxen for her,
432	and he honored her in his halls as he honored his devoted wife,
433	but he never lay with her in bed, for he shunned his wife's anger;
434	she carried the blazing torches with him, and she loved him most
435	of the slave women, and had reared him when he was small.
436	He opened the doors of the strongly made chamber,
437	sat down on the bed, and took off his soft tunic,
438	and put it in the hands of the shrewd old woman.
439	She folded and smoothed the tunic,
440	hung it on a peg beside the corded bed,
441	and went out of the chamber, and pulled the door to by the handle
442	of silver, and drew home the bolt with its strap.
443	There all night long, covered with sheep's wool,
444	he planned in his mind the journey that Athena had shown him.
