# Homer, Odyssey, Book 15 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	But Pallas Athena went to Lacedaemon with its wide dancing floors,
2	to the shining son of great-hearted Odysseus,
3	to remind him of his homecoming and to urge him to return.
4	She found Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor
5	sleeping in the porch of glorious Menelaus,
6	the son of Nestor overcome by soft sleep;
7	but sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus, but in his heart
8	through the ambrosial night cares for his father kept him awake.
9	Standing near him, gray-eyed Athena spoke to him:
10	“Telemachus, it is no longer good for you to wander far from your home,
11	leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house
12	so overbearing, lest they devour everything you have,
13	dividing your possessions, and you have gone on a useless journey.
14	But urge Menelaus, good at the war cry, as quickly as you can
15	to send you on, so that you may still find your noble mother at home.
16	For already her father and her brothers are bidding her
17	marry Eurymachus; for he surpasses all
18	the suitors in his gifts, and has increased his bride-price greatly;
19	beware lest against your will she carry some possession from the house.
20	For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast:
21	she wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her,
22	and her former children and her dear wedded husband
23	she no longer remembers, once he is dead, nor asks about them.
24	But you should go yourself and entrust each thing
25	to whichever of the slave women seems to you the best,
26	until the gods show you a glorious wife.
27	And I will tell you something else, and you take heed of it in your heart.
28	The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose
29	in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos,
30	eager to kill you before you reach your native land.
31	But I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some
32	of the suitors, who are devouring your substance.
33	But keep your well-made ship far from the islands,
34	and sail by night as well; and he will send you a fair wind behind you,
35	whoever of the immortals guards and protects you.
36	But when you reach the first shore of Ithaca,
37	send the ship and all your companions on to the city,
38	but you yourself go first of all to the swineherd,
39	who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike.
40	There spend the night; and send him into the city
41	to bring the news to prudent Penelope
42	that you are safe and have come back from Pylos.”
43	So saying, she went away to high Olympus;
44	but he woke the son of Nestor from sweet sleep,
45	stirring him with his heel, and spoke to him:
46	“Wake up, Peisistratus, son of Nestor; bring the single-hoofed horses
47	and yoke them under the chariot, so that we may make our way.”
48	And Peisistratus, son of Nestor, answered him face to face:
49	“Telemachus, there is no way, eager though we are for our journey,
50	to drive through the dark night; and soon it will be dawn.
51	But wait until he brings gifts and sets them in the chariot,
52	the hero, the son of Atreus, Menelaus famed for his spear,
53	and sends us off, speaking comfortingly with gentle words.
54	For a guest remembers all his days
55	the host who has shown him friendship.”
56	So he spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came.
57	And Menelaus, good at the war cry, came near them,
58	having risen from his bed, from beside Helen of the lovely hair.
59	And when the dear son of Odysseus caught sight of him,
60	in haste he put his glossy tunic about his body,
61	and threw a great mantle over his massive shoulders,
62	the hero, and went out of doors, and standing beside him spoke,
63	Telemachus, the dear son of divine Odysseus:
64	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
65	send me away now to my dear native land;
66	for already my heart yearns to come home.”
67	Then Menelaus, good at the war cry, answered him:
68	“Telemachus, I will not keep you here for long
69	when you are eager for your homecoming; I am indignant with another
70	man, a host, who loves beyond measure
71	and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things.
72	It is an equal wrong, both when a man urges on a guest who does not wish
73	to go home, and when he holds back one who is eager to go.
74	One should make welcome a guest who is present, and send him off when he wishes.
75	But wait until I bring gifts and set them in the chariot,
76	fine ones, and you see them with your eyes; and I will tell the women
77	to make ready a meal in the halls from the plenty within.
78	It brings both, glory and splendor and a blessing too,
79	to dine before going over the wide, boundless earth.
80	And if you wish to turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos,
81	so that I myself may go with you, and I will yoke the horses for you
82	and lead you to the cities of men; and no one will send us
83	away just as we came, but each will give us at least one thing to take with us,
84	either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron,
85	or two mules or a golden goblet.”
86	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
87	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
88	I wish to go back now to my own; for when I left I did not leave behind
89	anyone to watch over my possessions;
90	lest while I seek my godlike father I perish myself,
91	or some fine treasure be lost to me from my halls.”
92	But when Menelaus, good at the war cry, heard this,
93	at once he bade his wife and the slave women
94	make ready a meal in the halls from the plenty within.
95	And Eteoneus, son of Boethous, came near him,
96	having risen from his bed, since he lived not far from him;
97	Menelaus, good at the war cry, bade him kindle a fire
98	and roast meat; and he heard and did not disobey.
99	And he himself went down into the fragrant chamber,
100	not alone: with him went Helen and Megapenthes.
101	But when they came to where the treasures were stored,
102	the son of Atreus took a two-handled cup,
103	and bade his son Megapenthes carry a mixing bowl
104	of silver; and Helen stood beside the chests,
105	where her robes were, richly embroidered, which she had made herself.
106	Of these Helen, brilliant among women, took one and carried it,
107	the one that was most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest,
108	and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others.
109	And they went on through the house, until they came
110	to Telemachus; and fair-haired Menelaus spoke to him:
111	“Telemachus, as for your homecoming, as you have it in mind,
112	may Zeus bring it to pass for you, the loud-thundering husband of Hera.
113	Of the gifts that lie stored as treasures in my house,
114	I will give you the one that is finest and most precious.
115	I will give you a well-wrought mixing bowl; of silver
116	it is all, and its rims are finished with gold,
117	the work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it to me,
118	the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me
119	as I came there on my way home; and I wish to grant it to you.”
120	So saying, he put the two-handled cup in his hand,
121	the hero, the son of Atreus; and the shining mixing bowl
122	strong Megapenthes brought and set before him,
123	the silver one; and fair-cheeked Helen stood beside him,
124	holding the robe in her hands, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
125	“I too give you this gift, dear child,
126	a remembrance of Helen's hands, for the time of your lovely marriage,
127	for your wife to wear; but until then let it lie with your dear mother
128	in the hall. And may you reach, rejoicing,
129	your well-built house and your native land.”
130	So saying, she put it in his hands, and he received it with joy.
131	And the hero Peisistratus laid them in the wicker body of the chariot
132	when he had received them, and gazed at them all in his heart;
133	and fair-haired Menelaus led them to the house.
134	And they sat down on the chairs and the seats.
135	And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it,
136	a fine golden one, over a silver basin,
137	for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them.
138	And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
139	adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had;
140	and beside them the son of Boethous divided the meat and served out the portions;
141	and the son of glorious Menelaus poured the wine.
142	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
143	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
144	then Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor
145	yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot,
146	and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade.
147	And fair-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus, went after them,
148	holding wine sweet to the heart in his right hand
149	in a golden cup, so that they might pour a libation before they went.
150	He stood before the horses, and pledging them he spoke:
151	“Farewell, young men, and give my greeting to Nestor, shepherd of the people;
152	for truly he was kind to me like a father,
153	while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy.”
154	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
155	“Yes, truly, nurtured by Zeus, we will tell him, as you say,
156	all these things when we come; would that,
157	having come home to Ithaca and finding Odysseus in the house,
158	I might tell him so, as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness,
159	and bring many fine treasures.”
160	As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right,
161	an eagle, carrying in its talons a huge white goose,
162	a tame one from the court; and they followed, shouting,
163	men and women; and coming near them it darted
164	to the right, in front of the horses; and when they saw it
165	they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed.
166	And among them Peisistratus, son of Nestor, began to speak:
167	“Consider now, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
168	whether the god has shown this sign to us two, or to you yourself.”
169	So he spoke, and Menelaus, dear to Ares, pondered
170	how he might read it to him fair and right, with understanding.
171	But Helen of the long robes was before him, and said:
172	“Hear me, and I will prophesy, as the immortals
173	put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled.
174	As this eagle snatched the goose that was being reared in the house,
175	coming from the mountain, where he was born and bred,
176	so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering,
177	will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already
178	at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors.”
179	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
180	“So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera;
181	then even there I would pray to you as to a god.”
182	He spoke, and laid the whip on the horses; and very quickly
183	they sped through the city toward the plain, eager.
184	And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side.
185	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
186	and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles,
187	son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus begot as his son.
188	There they spent the night, and he set good cheer before them.
189	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
190	they yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot,
191	and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade.
192	And he whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling.
193	And quickly then they came to the steep citadel of Pylos;
194	and then Telemachus spoke to the son of Nestor:
195	“Son of Nestor, how might you promise and fulfill
196	a word of mine? We claim to be guest-friends without end
197	from our fathers' friendship, and we are of the same age as well;
198	and this journey will bring us still further into oneness of mind.
199	Do not take me past the ship, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me there,
200	lest the old man keep me in his house against my will
201	in his eagerness to make me welcome; but I must get home quickly.”
202	So he spoke, and the son of Nestor took counsel with his heart
203	how he might promise and fulfill it fair and right.
204	And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way:
205	he turned the horses to the swift ship and the shore of the sea,
206	and took out the beautiful gifts and put them in the stern of the ship,
207	the clothing and the gold, which Menelaus had given him;
208	and urging him on he addressed him with winged words:
209	“Go aboard now in haste, and bid all your companions do the same,
210	before I reach home and bring word to the old man.
211	For I know this well in my mind and in my heart:
212	such is his overweening spirit, he will not let you go,
213	but he will come here himself to call you, and I say that he
214	will not go back empty-handed; for he will be very angry, for certain.”
215	So he spoke, and drove the fine-maned horses
216	back to the city of the Pylians, and quickly reached the house.
217	And Telemachus urged on his companions and gave them orders:
218	“Put the arms in order, companions, in the black ship,
219	and let us go aboard ourselves, so that we may make our way.”
220	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed,
221	and they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches.
222	So he was busy with these things, and was praying and sacrificing to Athena
223	beside the stern of the ship; and a man came up close to him,
224	from a far land, fleeing from Argos, having killed a man,
225	a seer; and by birth he was a descendant of Melampus,
226	who once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks,
227	a rich man among the Pylians, living in a house far above the rest;
228	but then he came to the land of other men, fleeing his native land
229	and great-hearted Neleus, the most noble of living men,
230	who for a full year's round held his many goods
231	by force. And all that time, in the halls of Phylacus,
232	he lay bound in a grievous bond, suffering bitter pains
233	for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy blind folly
234	which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind.
235	But he escaped his doom and drove the loud-bellowing cattle
236	to Pylos from Phylace, and took vengeance for the shameful deed
237	on godlike Neleus, and for his brother he brought the woman
238	home. And he himself came to the land of other men,
239	to horse-pasturing Argos; for there it was his lot
240	to dwell, ruling over many Argives;
241	and there he married a wife and built a high-roofed house,
242	and begot Antiphates and Mantius, two mighty sons.
243	Antiphates begot great-hearted Oicles,
244	and Oicles Amphiaraus, who rouses armies,
245	whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts
246	with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age,
247	but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman.
248	His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus.
249	And Mantius begot Polypheides and Cleitus;
250	but Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne
251	for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals.
252	But Apollo made high-hearted Polypheides a seer,
253	by far the best of mortals, after Amphiaraus died;
254	he moved away to Hyperesia, angry with his father,
255	and living there he prophesied for all mortals.
256	His son it was who came up, and Theoclymenus was his name,
257	who now stood near Telemachus; and he found him
258	pouring libations and praying beside the swift black ship,
259	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
260	“My friend, since I find you making sacrifice in this place,
261	I beg you by the burnt offerings and by the god, and then
262	by your own head and those of the companions who follow you,
263	tell me truly, as I ask, and do not hide it:
264	Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?”
265	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
266	“Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
267	My line is from Ithaca, and my father is Odysseus,
268	if that was ever so; but now he has perished by a grim destruction.
269	Therefore I have taken companions and a black ship
270	and come to learn of my father, who has long been gone.”
271	Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him in turn:
272	“So I too am far from my native land, having killed a man
273	of my own tribe; and he has many brothers and kinsmen
274	in horse-pasturing Argos, and they rule with great power over the Achaeans.
275	Avoiding death and black doom at their hands,
276	I am an exile, since it is my lot to wander among men.
277	But take me on board the ship, since I have come to you as a suppliant in my flight,
278	lest they kill me; for I think they are pursuing me.”
279	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
280	“I will not drive you from the balanced ship when you wish to come;
281	but follow me; and there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have.”
282	So he spoke, and took the bronze spear from him,
283	and laid it on the deck of the curving ship;
284	and he himself went on board the seafaring ship.
285	Then he sat down in the stern, and beside him
286	he seated Theoclymenus; and they loosed the stern-cables.
287	And Telemachus urged on his companions and bade them
288	lay hold of the tackle; and they eagerly obeyed.
289	The mast of fir, within the hollow mast-box,
290	they raised and set up, and made it fast with forestays,
291	and hoisted the white sails with well-twisted ox-hide ropes.
292	And gray-eyed Athena sent them a following wind,
293	blowing hard and wild through the upper air, so that as quickly as possible
294	the ship, running, might cross the salt water of the sea.
295	And they went past Cruni and fair-flowing Chalcis.
296	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
297	and the ship made for Pheae, driven on by the wind of Zeus,
298	and past shining Elis, where the Epeians rule.
299	And from there he sent her on toward the pointed islands,
300	pondering whether he would escape death or be caught.
301	And the two of them in the hut, Odysseus and the brilliant swineherd,
302	were having supper; and the other men were having supper beside them.
303	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
304	Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd,
305	whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay
306	there at the farmstead, or would urge him on to the city:
307	“Hear me now, Eumaeus, and all you other companions;
308	at dawn I long to go off to the city
309	to beg, so that I may not wear you and your companions out.
310	But give me good counsel, and grant me a good guide to go with me
311	to take me there; and through the city by myself, as I must,
312	I will wander, in case someone may hold out to me a cup and a loaf.
313	And I might go to the house of divine Odysseus
314	and tell my news to prudent Penelope,
315	and mingle with the overbearing suitors,
316	in case they might give me a meal, having countless good things.
317	Among them I would readily serve well, in whatever they wished.
318	For I will speak out to you, and you take heed and listen to me:
319	by the favor of Hermes the guide, who grants
320	grace and glory to the works of all men,
321	in service no other mortal could rival me,
322	in heaping up a fire well and splitting dry wood,
323	in carving and roasting meat and pouring wine,
324	such services as the lesser do for their betters.”
325	Then, deeply troubled, you said to him, swineherd Eumaeus:
326	“Ah me, stranger, why has this thought come
327	into your mind? Surely you long to perish there altogether,
328	if you wish to slip into the throng of the suitors,
329	whose arrogance and violence reach the iron sky.
330	Not such as you are their servants,
331	but young men, well clad in cloaks and tunics,
332	their heads and their handsome faces always shining with oil,
333	who serve them; and the well-polished tables
334	are laden with bread and meat and wine.
335	But stay; for no one is vexed by your presence,
336	neither I nor any other of the companions who are with me.
337	But when the dear son of Odysseus comes,
338	he will clothe you in a cloak and a tunic as clothing,
339	and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you.”
340	Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus answered him:
341	“Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus
342	as you are to me, since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery.
343	There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming;
344	but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows,
345	the men whom wandering and bane and sorrow come upon.
346	But now, since you hold me back and bid me wait for him,
347	come, tell me of the mother of divine Odysseus,
348	and of his father, whom he left on the threshold of old age when he went,
349	whether they still live somewhere under the rays of the sun,
350	or are already dead and in the house of Hades.”
351	Then the swineherd, leader of men, spoke to him in turn:
352	“Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
353	Laertes still lives, but he prays to Zeus always
354	that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls;
355	for he mourns terribly for his son who is gone,
356	and for his wise-hearted wedded wife, whose death grieved him
357	most of all, and brought him to old age before its time.
358	And she died of grief for her glorious son,
359	by a wretched death—may no one die so who
360	lives here as my friend and does me kindness.
361	So long as she lived, grieving though she was,
362	so long it was a pleasure to me to question and to ask,
363	because she herself reared me together with Ctimene of the long robes,
364	her strong daughter, whom she bore the youngest of her children;
365	with her I was reared, and she honored me little less.
366	But when we both came to the lovely prime of youth,
367	they gave her in marriage to Same, and got countless gifts,
368	but me she clothed in a cloak and a tunic, clothes
369	very fine, and gave me sandals for my feet,
370	and sent me to the farm; and she loved me dearly in her heart.
371	But now I lack these things; yet for me myself
372	the blessed gods make the work grow at which I stay;
373	from it I have eaten and drunk and given to those I respect.
374	But from my mistress it is not possible to hear anything kindly,
375	neither word nor deed, since evil has fallen on the house,
376	overbearing men; and the slaves greatly long
377	to speak face to face with their mistress and learn of everything,
378	and to eat and drink, and then to take something with them
379	to the farm, such things as always warm the hearts of slaves.”
380	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
381	“Ah, so you were still small, swineherd Eumaeus,
382	when you were driven so far from your native land and your parents.
383	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
384	was the wide-streeted city of men sacked,
385	in which your father and lady mother lived,
386	or were you alone with the sheep or the cattle
387	when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you
388	to this man's house, and he paid a worthy price for you?”
389	Then the swineherd, leader of men, spoke to him in turn:
390	“Stranger, since you ask me this and question me,
391	listen now in silence and take your delight, and drink your wine
392	as you sit. These nights are endless; there is time to sleep,
393	and time for those who take delight in it to listen; and you have no need
394	to go to bed before it is time; much sleep too is a vexation.
395	And of the rest, whoever's heart and spirit bids him,
396	let him go out and sleep; and when dawn appears
397	let him take his meal and follow the master's swine.
398	But let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut,
399	take delight in each other's wretched sorrows
400	as we remember them; for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows,
401	whoever has suffered very much and wandered much.
402	And I will tell you what you ask and question me about.
403	There is an island called Syrie, if perhaps you have heard of it,
404	above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are,
405	not so very populous, but a good one,
406	good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat.
407	Famine never comes upon its people, nor does any
408	hateful sickness besides fall upon wretched mortals;
409	but when the tribes of men grow old throughout the city,
410	Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis
411	and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them.
412	There are two cities, and everything is divided between them;
413	and over both of them my father was king,
414	Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals.
415	And there came Phoenicians, men famed for ships,
416	greedy rogues, bringing countless baubles in a black ship.
417	And there was in my father's house a Phoenician woman,
418	beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork;
419	and the artful Phoenicians deceived her.
420	First, as she was washing clothes, one of them lay with her by the hollow ship
421	in bed and love, which deceive the minds
422	of the womenfolk, even of one who does good.
423	Then he asked her who she was and where she came from;
424	and she at once pointed out the high-roofed house of my father:
425	‘I claim to be from Sidon, rich in bronze,
426	and I am the daughter of Arybas, whose wealth came in a flood;
427	but Taphian men, pirates, seized me
428	as I was coming from the fields, and brought me here and sold me
429	to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me.’
430	Then the man who had lain with her in secret spoke to her in turn:
431	‘Would you then come home again with us,
432	so that you may see the high-roofed house of your father and mother
433	and themselves? For truly they are still alive and are reputed rich.’
434	And the woman spoke to him in turn and answered:
435	‘That might well be, if you sailors would be willing
436	to pledge me with an oath to bring me home unharmed.’
437	So she spoke, and they all swore as she bade them.
438	But when they had sworn and completed the oath,
439	the woman spoke among them again and answered:
440	‘Silence now, and let none of your companions speak to me,
441	meeting me either in the street
442	or perhaps at the spring; lest someone go to the house and tell
443	the old man, and he suspect it and bind me
444	in a grievous bond, and devise destruction for you.
445	But keep the word in your minds, and hasten the buying of your cargo.
446	And when the ship is full of substance,
447	then let a message come quickly to me at the house;
448	for I will bring gold too, whatever comes to my hand;
449	and I would gladly give something else besides, for my passage.
450	For I am rearing in the halls the child of a good man,
451	a crafty one indeed, who runs along with me out of doors;
452	him I would bring on board, and he would fetch you a price beyond counting,
453	wherever you sell him among men of another tongue.’
454	So saying, she went away to the fine house,
455	and they stayed there among us for a whole year,
456	and got much substance by trading for their hollow ship.
457	But when the hollow ship was laden for their going,
458	then they sent a messenger to bring the woman word.
459	There came a man of great knowledge to my father's house
460	with a golden necklace, strung here and there with amber.
461	In the hall the slave women and my lady mother
462	were handling it and gazing at it with their eyes,
463	offering a price; and he nodded to her in silence.
464	And when he had nodded, he went off to the hollow ship,
465	and she took me by the hand and led me out of the house.
466	And in the porch she found the cups and the tables
467	of the banqueters, who attended on my father;
468	they had gone out to the session and the talk of the people,
469	and she quickly hid three goblets in her bosom
470	and carried them off; and I followed in my childish thoughtlessness.
471	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
472	and we went quickly and came to the famous harbor,
473	where the swift-sailing ship of the Phoenician men was.
474	Then they embarked and sailed over the watery ways,
475	having taken us two on board; and Zeus sent a fair wind.
476	For six days we sailed on, night and day alike,
477	but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day,
478	then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman,
479	and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird.
480	And her, to become a spoil for seals and fish,
481	they threw overboard; but I was left behind, grieving at heart;
482	and the wind and the water carried them and drove them to Ithaca,
483	where Laertes bought me with his own possessions.
484	So it was that I came to see this land with my eyes.”
485	And Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, answered him:
486	“Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me
487	by telling all these things, all the sorrows you have suffered in your heart.
488	But truly for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil,
489	since, after toiling much, you came to the house of a man
490	who is kind, who gives you food and drink
491	with care, and you live a good life; but I
492	have come here after wandering through many cities of mortals.”
493	So they spoke such words to one another,
494	and they slept, not for long, but a little while;
495	for soon came Dawn of the fair throne. And by the shore
496	the companions of Telemachus loosed the sails, and took down the mast
497	quickly, and brought her forward to the anchorage with oars;
498	they cast out the mooring-stones and made fast the stern-cables,
499	and they themselves stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore,
500	and made ready their meal and mixed the sparkling wine.
501	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
502	among them wise Telemachus began to speak:
503	“You now row the black ship on to the city,
504	but I will go to the fields and the herdsmen;
505	and in the evening, when I have looked at my tilled fields, I will come down to the city.
506	And at dawn I would set before you a reward for the voyage,
507	a good feast of meat and of wine sweet to drink.”
508	Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him in turn:
509	“And where shall I go, dear child? To whose house shall I come
510	of the men who rule over craggy Ithaca?
511	Or shall I go straight to your mother and your house?”
512	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
513	“Were things otherwise, I would bid you go even to our house;
514	for there is no lack of good cheer for guests; but it would be worse
515	for you yourself, since I shall be away, and my mother
516	will not see you; for she does not often show herself to the suitors in the house,
517	but away from them she weaves at her loom in the upper room.
518	But I will name to you another man to whom you may go,
519	Eurymachus, the splendid son of wise-hearted Polybus,
520	whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god;
521	for he is by far the best man, and most of all he is eager
522	to marry my mother and to hold the prize of honor of Odysseus.
523	But Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air, knows this,
524	whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them.”
525	As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right,
526	a falcon, the swift messenger of Apollo; and in its feet
527	it held a dove and was plucking it, and it poured the feathers down on the ground
528	between the ship and Telemachus himself.
529	And Theoclymenus called him apart from his companions,
530	and he clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
531	“Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right;
532	for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen.
533	There is no other race more kingly than yours
534	in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful.”
535	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
536	“Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled;
537	then you would quickly know friendship and many gifts
538	from me, so that anyone who met you would call you blessed.”
539	He spoke, and called to Peiraeus, his trusted companion:
540	“Peiraeus, son of Clytius, you in other things most of all
541	obey me, of my companions who followed me to Pylos;
542	now too take this stranger for me to your house,
543	and welcome him with care and honor him, until I come.”
544	And Peiraeus, famed for his spear, answered him face to face:
545	“Telemachus, why, if you were to stay here a long time,
546	I will take care of this man, and he shall not lack good cheer.”
547	So saying, he went on board, and bade his companions
548	to go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables.
549	And they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches.
550	And Telemachus bound under his feet his fine sandals,
551	and he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
552	from the deck of the ship; and they loosed the stern-cables.
553	They pushed off and sailed to the city, as he had bidden them,
554	Telemachus, the dear son of divine Odysseus;
555	and his feet carried him quickly on as he strode, until he came to the fold,
556	where his countless swine were, among which the swineherd,
557	being good, used to sleep, kind to his masters.
