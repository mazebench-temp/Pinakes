# Homer, Odyssey, Book 6 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus,
2	worn out by sleep and weariness; but Athena
3	went to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men,
4	who once lived in Hypereia with its wide dancing floors,
5	near the Cyclopes, overweening men,
6	who used to plunder them, and were the stronger in strength.
7	From there godlike Nausithous had moved them and led them away,
8	and settled them in Scheria, far from toiling men;
9	and he drove a wall around the city, and built houses,
10	and made temples of the gods, and divided the fields.
11	But he had already been overcome by his doom and gone to the house of Hades,
12	and Alcinous was ruling then, who knew counsels from the gods.
13	To his house went the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
14	devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus.
15	She went to the intricately worked chamber, in which a girl
16	was sleeping, like the immortals in stature and in looks,
17	Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
18	and beside her two handmaids, who had their beauty from the Graces,
19	one on either side, by the doorposts; and the shining doors were closed.
20	And she rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed,
21	and stood above her head and spoke to her,
22	in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships,
23	who was of an age with her, and dear to her heart.
24	In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her:
25	“Nausicaa, how did your mother bear you so slack?
26	Your glossy clothes lie uncared for,
27	and your marriage is near, when you must wear fine clothes yourself
28	and provide others for those who will lead you home.
29	For it is from these things that talk goes up among men,
30	good talk, and your father and lady mother rejoice.
31	But let us go to wash when dawn appears;
32	and I will go with you to help, so that you may make ready
33	as quickly as can be, since you will not be a maiden much longer;
34	for already the best men in the land are wooing you,
35	of all the Phaeacians, where you yourself have your birth.
36	But come, urge your famous father in the early dawn
37	to make ready the mules and the wagon, which may carry
38	the sashes and the robes and the glossy blankets.
39	And for you yourself it is much finer so than to go
40	on foot; for the washing troughs are far from the city.”
41	So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away,
42	to Olympus, where, they say, the seat of the gods stands
43	firm forever. It is not shaken by winds, nor ever
44	wet with rain, nor does snow come near it, but the clear sky
45	is spread wholly cloudless, and a white gleam runs over it;
46	in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days.
47	There the gray-eyed one went away, when she had told the girl all.
48	And at once Dawn of the fair throne came, and she woke her,
49	well-robed Nausicaa; and at once she marveled at the dream,
50	and went through the house to bring the news to her parents,
51	her dear father and her mother; and she found them within.
52	Her mother was sitting at the hearth with her handmaids,
53	turning the sea-purple yarn; and him, as he was going out of doors,
54	she met on his way to join the famous kings
55	at the council, to which the noble Phaeacians had called him.
56	And she stood very close and spoke to her dear father:
57	“Papa dear, will you not make ready the wagon for me,
58	high, with good wheels, so that I may take the glorious clothes
59	to the river to wash them, for they lie soiled?
60	And for you yourself it is fitting, when you are among the foremost,
61	to take counsel with clean clothes on your body.
62	And five dear sons are born to you in the halls,
63	two married, and three young unmarried men in their bloom;
64	and they always want to have freshly washed clothes
65	when they go to the dance; and all these things are a care to my mind.”
66	So she spoke; for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage
67	to her dear father. But he understood everything, and answered her:
68	“I do not grudge you the mules, my child, nor anything else.
69	Go; the slaves will make ready the wagon for you,
70	high, with good wheels, fitted with a frame above.”
71	So saying, he called to the slaves, and they obeyed.
72	Outside they made ready the well-wheeled mule wagon,
73	and led the mules under and yoked them beneath the wagon;
74	and the girl brought the shining clothing out of her chamber,
75	and laid it on the polished wagon,
76	and her mother put in a box food to satisfy the heart,
77	of every kind, and put in meats, and poured wine
78	in a goatskin; and the girl mounted the wagon.
79	And she gave her liquid olive oil in a golden flask,
80	so that she might bathe and anoint herself, with her handmaids.
81	And she took the whip and the glossy reins,
82	and whipped them to a run; and there was a clatter of the mules,
83	and they strained on without end, and carried the clothing and the girl herself,
84	not alone; the other handmaids too went along with her.
85	And when they came to the beautiful stream of the river,
86	where the washing troughs were, unfailing, and much fair water
87	welled up and flowed through, to clean even what is very dirty,
88	there they unyoked the mules and set them free from the wagon,
89	and drove them along the eddying river
90	to graze on the honey-sweet grass; and they took from the wagon
91	the clothes in their hands and carried them into the dark water,
92	and trampled them in the pits quickly, vying with one another in strife.
93	But when they had washed and cleansed away all the dirt,
94	they spread them out in a row along the shore of the sea, just where
95	the sea, beating on the land, used to wash the pebbles clean.
96	And when they had bathed and anointed themselves richly with olive oil,
97	they took their meal then beside the banks of the river,
98	and waited for the clothes to dry in the rays of the sun.
99	But when they had taken their fill of food, the slave women and she herself,
100	they played with a ball, throwing off their veils;
101	and among them white-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance.
102	And as Artemis of the showering arrows goes over the mountains,
103	along towering Taygetus or Erymanthus,
104	delighting in boars and swift deer,
105	and with her the nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus,
106	who haunt the fields, play, and Leto is glad at heart;
107	above them all she holds her head and brow,
108	and she is easy to know, though all are beautiful;
109	so she stood out among her handmaids, a maiden unwed.
110	But when she was about to go back home,
111	yoking the mules and folding the fine clothes,
112	then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing,
113	that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl,
114	who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men.
115	So then the princess flung the ball to one of her handmaids;
116	she missed the handmaid, and threw it into a deep eddy;
117	and they shouted aloud at it; and brilliant Odysseus woke,
118	and sitting up he pondered in his mind and in his heart:
119	“Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?
120	Are they arrogant and savage and unjust,
121	or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?
122	It is as though a female cry of girls came round me,
123	of nymphs, who hold the steep peaks of the mountains
124	and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows.
125	Am I perhaps somewhere near men with human voice?
126	But come, I will make trial myself and see.”
127	So saying, brilliant Odysseus came out from under the bushes,
128	and from the thick forest with his strong hand he broke off a branch
129	of leaves, to cover the man's nakedness about his body.
130	And he went on like a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage,
131	that goes on, rained upon and blown by the wind, and his eyes
132	blaze; and he goes after the cattle or the sheep
133	or after the wild deer; and his belly bids him
134	make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold;
135	so Odysseus was about to mingle with the girls of the lovely tresses,
136	naked though he was; for need had come upon him.
137	And terrible he appeared to them, afflicted by the brine,
138	and they fled in fear this way and that over the jutting beaches;
139	only the daughter of Alcinous stood her ground; for Athena
140	put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs.
141	She checked herself and stood facing him; and Odysseus pondered
142	whether he should clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and beg her,
143	or, as he was, with soothing words, standing apart,
144	beg her, if she would show him the city and give him clothes.
145	And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way,
146	to beg her with soothing words, standing apart,
147	lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees.
148	At once he spoke a soothing and shrewd word:
149	“I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?
150	If you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky,
151	to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus,
152	I liken you most nearly, in looks and stature and build;
153	but if you are one of the mortals who live upon the earth,
154	three times blessed are your father and your lady mother,
155	and three times blessed your brothers; surely their hearts
156	are always warmed with gladness because of you,
157	as they watch such a scion going into the dance.
158	But he in turn is the most blessed in his heart, beyond all others,
159	who prevails with his bride-price and leads you to his home.
160	For never yet have I seen with my eyes such a mortal,
161	neither man nor woman; awe holds me as I look.
162	Once in Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, such a thing
163	I saw, a young sapling of a palm tree springing up;
164	for I went there too, and many people followed me,
165	on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows.
166	And in the same way, when I saw that, I was astounded in my heart
167	for a long time, since never yet had such a tree come up from the earth,
168	as I marvel at you, lady, and am astounded, and I am terribly afraid
169	to clasp your knees; and hard grief has come upon me.
170	Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped from the wine-dark sea;
171	and all that time the wave and the swift storm winds were carrying me
172	from the island of Ogygia. And now some god has cast me ashore here,
173	so that, I suppose, I may yet suffer evil here too; for I do not think
174	it will cease, but the gods will yet accomplish much before that.
175	But pity me, queen; for it is to you, after toiling through many evils,
176	that I have come first, and of the others I know no one
177	of the people who hold this city and land.
178	Show me the city, and give me a rag to throw around me,
179	if you had perhaps some wrapping for the clothes when you came here.
180	And may the gods give you all that you have in your mind,
181	a husband and a home, and may they grant you oneness of mind,
182	a noble one; for there is nothing stronger or better than this,
183	than when, of one mind in their thoughts, they keep house together,
184	a man and a woman: many sorrows to their enemies,
185	and joy to their well-wishers; and they themselves know it best.”
186	And white-armed Nausicaa answered him face to face:
187	“Stranger, since you seem like neither a base man nor a witless one—
188	and it is Zeus himself, the Olympian, who deals out prosperity to men,
189	to the good and to the bad, to each as he wishes;
190	and he has given you these things, I suppose, and you must endure them all the same—
191	now, since you have come to our city and our land,
192	you shall not lack clothing, nor anything else
193	that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way.
194	I will show you the city, and tell you the name of the people.
195	The Phaeacians hold this city and land,
196	and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
197	on whom the strength and the force of the Phaeacians depend.”
198	She spoke, and gave orders to her handmaids of the lovely tresses:
199	“Stand still, my handmaids: where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?
200	Surely you do not think that he is one of the hostile men?
201	That man does not exist as a living mortal, nor will he ever be born,
202	who shall come to the land of the Phaeacian men
203	bringing combat; for we are very dear to the immortals.
204	And we live far apart in the sea of many waves,
205	the farthest of men, and no other mortal mingles with us.
206	But this is some unhappy wanderer who has come here,
207	whom we must look after now; for they are all from Zeus,
208	strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear.
209	But, handmaids, give the stranger food and drink,
210	and bathe him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind.”
211	So she spoke, and they stood still and called to one another,
212	and they set Odysseus down in the shelter, as she had ordered,
213	Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous;
214	and beside him they laid a mantle and a tunic for clothing,
215	and gave him liquid olive oil in a golden flask,
216	and bade him bathe in the streams of the river.
217	Then brilliant Odysseus spoke among the handmaids:
218	“Handmaids, stand off there like that, so that I myself
219	may wash the brine from my shoulders, and with olive oil
220	anoint myself all over; for truly it is long since oil was on my skin.
221	But I will not bathe in front of you; for I am ashamed
222	to strip naked when I have come among girls of the lovely tresses.”
223	So he spoke, and they went apart and told the girl.
224	But brilliant Odysseus washed from his skin with the river water
225	the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders,
226	and from his head he wiped the scurf of the barren sea.
227	But when he had bathed all over and anointed himself richly,
228	and had put on the clothes that the maiden unwed had given him,
229	Athena, child of Zeus, made him
230	taller to look upon and stouter, and down from his head
231	she made the thick hair fall, like the hyacinth flower.
232	And as when a man pours gold around silver,
233	a skilled man whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught
234	every kind of craft, and he accomplishes lovely works,
235	so she poured grace upon his head and shoulders.
236	Then he went apart along the shore of the sea and sat down,
237	shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him.
238	Then she spoke among her handmaids of the lovely tresses:
239	“Hear me, white-armed handmaids, so that I may tell you something.
240	Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus
241	does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians;
242	for before he seemed to me unsightly,
243	but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky.
244	Would that such a man might be called my husband,
245	living here, and that it might please him to stay here.
246	But, handmaids, give the stranger food and drink.”
247	So she spoke, and they listened closely to her and obeyed,
248	and set food and drink beside Odysseus.
249	Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate
250	greedily; for he had long tasted no food.
251	But white-armed Nausicaa thought of another thing.
252	She folded the clothes and put them on the fine wagon,
253	and yoked the strong-hoofed mules, and mounted herself,
254	and urged Odysseus on, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
255	“Rise now, stranger, to go to the city, so that I may send you
256	to the house of my wise-hearted father, where, I tell you,
257	you will come to know all the Phaeacians who are the best.
258	But do just this, for you do not seem to me to be senseless:
259	while we are going through the fields and the works of men,
260	so long go with the handmaids behind the mules and the wagon
261	quickly; and I will lead the way.
262	But when we set foot in the city, around which runs a rampart,
263	high, and there is a fine harbor on either side of the city,
264	and the way in is narrow; and the curving ships are drawn up along the road;
265	for every man has a slip of his own.
266	And there is their place of assembly, around the fine precinct of Poseidon,
267	fitted with hauled stones set deep in the earth.
268	There they take care of the tackle of the black ships,
269	cables and sails, and they taper the oars.
270	For the Phaeacians care nothing for bow or quiver,
271	but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships,
272	in which they glory as they cross the gray sea.
273	It is their bitter talk I shun, lest someone hereafter
274	should blame me; for there are very overbearing men in the land;
275	and this is what some baser man might say, if he met us:
276	‘Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall,
277	a stranger? Where did she find him? He will be her own husband, surely.
278	Perhaps she has taken care of some wanderer from his ship,
279	a man from a far-off people, since none live near us;
280	or some god has come to her at her prayer, much prayed for,
281	down from the sky, and he will have her all his days.
282	It is better so, if she has gone about and found herself a husband
283	from elsewhere; for truly she dishonors these here in the land,
284	the Phaeacians, many and noble, who woo her.’
285	So they will say, and this would be a reproach to me.
286	And I would be indignant with another girl who did such things,
287	who against the will of her friends, her father and mother, still living,
288	mingled with men before she came openly to her marriage.
289	Stranger, listen to my word quickly, so that as soon as may be
290	you may win escort and a homecoming from my father.
291	You will find a splendid grove of Athena near the road,
292	of poplars; and in it a spring flows, and around it is a meadow;
293	and there is my father's domain and his flourishing orchard,
294	as far from the city as a man can make himself heard with a shout.
295	Sit down there and wait a while, until we
296	come to the city and reach my father's house.
297	But when you think that we have reached the house,
298	then go to the city of the Phaeacians and ask
299	for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous.
300	It is easy to know, and even a child could lead you,
301	a little one; for not at all like it are they built,
302	the houses of the Phaeacians, like the house of Alcinous
303	the hero. But when the house and the court have hidden you,
304	go very quickly through the hall, until you come
305	to my mother; and she sits at the hearth in the light of the fire,
306	turning the sea-purple yarn, a wonder to see,
307	leaning against a pillar; and her slave women sit behind her.
308	And there my father's chair leans against hers,
309	on which he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal.
310	Pass him by, and throw your arms around the knees
311	of my mother, so that you may see the day of your homecoming
312	rejoicing, and quickly, even if you are from very far away.
313	If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart,
314	then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach
315	your well-built house and your native land.”
316	So she spoke, and with the shining whip she lashed
317	the mules; and they quickly left the streams of the river.
318	And they ran well, and well they stepped out with their feet;
319	and she drove just so that they could follow on foot,
320	the handmaids and Odysseus, and she laid on the whip with judgment.
321	And the sun set, and they came to the famous grove,
322	sacred to Athena, where brilliant Odysseus sat down.
323	At once then he prayed to the daughter of great Zeus:
324	“Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone:
325	listen to me now at least, since before you never listened
326	to me when I was being wrecked, when the famous shaker of the earth wrecked me.
327	Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied.”
328	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
329	But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected
330	her father's brother; and he raged furiously
331	against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land.
