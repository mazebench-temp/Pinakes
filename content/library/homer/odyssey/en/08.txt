# Homer, Odyssey, Book 8 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
2	the sacred might of Alcinous rose from his bed,
3	and up rose Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities.
4	And the sacred might of Alcinous led the way for them
5	to the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, which was built for them beside the ships.
6	And they came and sat down on the polished stones
7	near together. And Pallas Athena went up and down through the city
8	in the likeness of the herald of wise-hearted Alcinous,
9	devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus,
10	and standing beside each man she spoke her word:
11	“Come now, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians,
12	go to the place of assembly, so that you may learn about the stranger
13	who has newly come to the house of wise-hearted Alcinous,
14	after wandering over the sea, like the immortals in form.”
15	So saying, she urged on the might and spirit of every man.
16	And quickly the places of assembly and the seats were filled with mortals
17	as they gathered; and many gazed when they saw
18	the wise-hearted son of Laertes; for upon him Athena
19	poured a wondrous grace, upon his head and shoulders,
20	and made him taller and stouter to look upon,
21	so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians,
22	and feared and respected, and might accomplish the many contests
23	in which the Phaeacians made trial of Odysseus.
24	And when they had gathered and were all assembled,
25	then Alcinous addressed them and spoke:
26	“Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians,
27	so that I may say what the heart in my breast commands me.
28	This stranger, I do not know who he is, has come to my house in his wanderings,
29	whether from the men of the east or from the men of the west;
30	and he urges an escort, and begs that it be made sure.
31	So let us, as has been our custom, urge on an escort.
32	For no other man either, who comes to my house,
33	stays here long, mourning, for want of an escort.
34	But come, let us drag a black ship down to the shining sea
35	on her first voyage, and let two and fifty young men
36	be chosen throughout the people, those who are the best, as always.
37	And when you have all made the oars fast at the rowing benches,
38	step out again; and then make ready a quick feast,
39	coming to our house; and I will provide well for all.
40	This I lay on the young men; but the rest of you,
41	the scepter-bearing kings, come to my beautiful house,
42	so that we may make the stranger welcome in the halls;
43	and let no one refuse. And call the divine singer,
44	Demodocus; for to him above all the god has given song,
45	to give delight in whatever way his heart urges him to sing.”
46	So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him,
47	the scepter-bearing kings; and a herald went to fetch the divine singer.
48	And the two and fifty young men who had been chosen
49	went, as he had bidden, to the shore of the barren sea.
50	And when they had come down to the ship and the sea,
51	they dragged the black ship down into deep water,
52	and set the mast and the sails in the black ship,
53	and fitted the oars in their leather straps,
54	all in good order, and spread the white sails;
55	and they anchored her out in the water; and then
56	they went to the great house of wise-hearted Alcinous.
57	And the colonnades and the courts and the rooms were filled with men
58	as they gathered; and there were many, young and old.
59	And for them Alcinous sacrificed twelve sheep,
60	and eight white-tusked hogs, and two shambling cattle;
61	these they flayed and dressed, and made ready a lovely feast.
62	And the herald came near, leading the trusty singer,
63	whom the Muse loved above all, and she gave him both good and evil:
64	she robbed him of his eyes, but gave him sweet song.
65	And Pontonous set for him a silver-studded chair
66	in the midst of the banqueters, leaning it against a tall pillar;
67	and he hung the clear-toned lyre from its peg
68	right above his head, and showed him where to take it in his hands,
69	the herald; and beside him he set a basket and a beautiful table,
70	and beside it a cup of wine, to drink whenever his heart bade him.
71	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
72	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
73	the Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men,
74	from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky,
75	the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus,
76	how once they contended at a rich feast of the gods
77	with terrible words; yet Agamemnon, lord of men,
78	rejoiced in his mind that the best of the Achaeans were contending.
79	For so Phoebus Apollo had told him in prophecy
80	in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold
81	to consult the oracle; for then the beginning of bane was rolling upon
82	Trojans and Danaans through the counsels of great Zeus.
83	This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus
84	took his great purple mantle in his strong hands
85	and drew it down over his head, and covered his handsome face;
86	for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians, letting tears fall from beneath his brows.
87	Each time the divine singer stopped singing,
88	he would wipe away his tears and take the mantle from his head,
89	and take the two-handled cup and pour libations to the gods;
90	but whenever he began again, and they urged him to sing,
91	the best of the Phaeacians, since they delighted in his words,
92	Odysseus would cover his head again and lament.
93	Then he escaped the notice of all the others as he let tears fall,
94	but Alcinous alone noticed him and observed him,
95	sitting near him, and heard him groaning heavily.
96	And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar:
97	“Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians.
98	Already we have had our hearts' fill of our equal share of the feast
99	and of the lyre, which is the companion of the rich feast;
100	but now let us go out and make trial of the contests,
101	all of them, so that the stranger may tell his friends,
102	when he has returned home, how far we surpass others
103	in boxing and in wrestling and in jumping and in running.”
104	So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him.
105	And he hung the clear-toned lyre from its peg,
106	and took Demodocus by the hand and led him out of the hall,
107	the herald; and he led him on the same road by which the others went,
108	the best of the Phaeacians, to marvel at the games.
109	They went to the place of assembly, and a great throng followed with them,
110	countless; and young men stood up, many and noble.
111	There rose up Acroneus and Ocyalus and Elatreus,
112	Nauteus and Prymneus and Anchialus and Eretmeus,
113	Ponteus and Proreus, Thoon and Anabesineus,
114	and Amphialus, son of Polyneus, the son of Tecton;
115	and up rose Euryalus too, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals,
116	the son of Naubolus, who was the best in looks and form
117	of all the Phaeacians after noble Laodamas.
118	And up stood the three sons of noble Alcinous,
119	Laodamas and Halius and godlike Clytoneus.
120	And first of all they made trial of one another in running.
121	For them the running was strained to the full from the post; and all together
122	they flew swiftly on, raising the dust of the plain.
123	And of them noble Clytoneus was by far the best at running:
124	as far as the furrow-length of a pair of mules in the fallow,
125	so far did he run out ahead and reach the people, and the others were left behind.
126	Then they made trial of painful wrestling,
127	and in this, in turn, Euryalus surpassed all the best.
128	In jumping Amphialus was the foremost of all,
129	and with the discus, again, Elatreus was by far the strongest of all,
130	and in boxing, again, Laodamas, the good son of Alcinous.
131	But when they had all delighted their hearts with the contests,
132	then Laodamas, the son of Alcinous, spoke among them:
133	“Come, friends, let us ask the stranger whether there is some contest
134	he knows and has learned. In build at least he is no mean man,
135	in his thighs and shins, and both his hands above,
136	and his massive neck and his great strength; nor does he lack
137	anything of youth, but he has been broken by many evils;
138	for I, for my part, say there is nothing else worse than the sea
139	to unstring a man, however strong he may be.”
140	Then Euryalus answered him in turn and said:
141	“Laodamas, this word you have spoken is fair and right.
142	Now go yourself and challenge him, and make your word known.”
143	But when the good son of Alcinous heard this,
144	he went and stood in the middle and spoke to Odysseus:
145	“Come here, you too, stranger, father, and make trial of the contests,
146	if you have perhaps learned any; and it is fitting that you know contests;
147	for there is no greater fame for a man, as long as he lives,
148	than what he achieves with his feet and with his hands.
149	But come, make trial, and scatter the sorrows from your heart.
150	For your journey will not be far off much longer; but already
151	the ship has been drawn down, and the companions are ready.”
152	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
153	“Laodamas, why do you bid me do these things, taunting me?
154	Sorrows are on my mind far more than contests,
155	who before have suffered very much and toiled much,
156	and now in the midst of your assembly, in need of my homecoming,
157	I sit, begging the king and all the people.”
158	Then Euryalus answered him in turn and rebuked him to his face:
159	“No, stranger, I do not liken you to a man skilled
160	in contests, such as there are many among men,
161	but to one who goes to and fro with a many-benched ship,
162	a leader of sailors who are traders,
163	and who is mindful of his freight, or watches over his homeward cargo
164	and his greedily snatched gains; you are not like an athlete.”
165	Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said:
166	“Stranger, you have not spoken well; you are like a reckless man.
167	So true is it that the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all
168	men alike, neither stature nor mind nor eloquence.
169	For one man is less in looks,
170	but a god crowns his words with beauty, and men look on him
171	with delight; and he speaks surely,
172	with soothing modesty, and stands out among those gathered,
173	and as he goes through the city they look on him as a god.
174	Another again in looks is like the immortals,
175	but no grace is set as a crown around his words;
176	so with you too, your looks are outstanding, and not even a god
177	would fashion them otherwise, but in mind you are worthless.
178	You have stirred the heart in my breast
179	by speaking in no seemly way. I am not unskilled in contests,
180	as you say, but among the foremost, I think,
181	I used to be, as long as I trusted in my youth and my hands.
182	But now I am held fast by misery and sorrows; for I have endured much,
183	passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves.
184	But even so, though I have suffered many evils, I will make trial of the contests;
185	for your word bites the heart, and you have urged me on by your speech.”
186	He spoke, and sprang up with his mantle still on, and seized a discus
187	bigger and thicker, and heavier by no little,
188	than those with which the Phaeacians threw among themselves.
189	This he whirled round and let fly from his strong hand,
190	and the stone hummed; and down to the earth they cowered,
191	the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships,
192	under the rush of the stone; and it flew beyond the marks of all,
193	speeding swiftly from his hand. And Athena set the mark,
194	in form like a man, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
195	“Even a blind man, stranger, could sort out your mark
196	by feeling for it, since it is not mingled with the throng,
197	but far the first. Take heart about this contest at least:
198	none of the Phaeacians will reach this or throw beyond it.”
199	So she spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad,
200	rejoicing that he saw a gentle companion in the gathering.
201	And then he spoke more lightly among the Phaeacians:
202	“Reach that now, young men. And presently I will send another after it,
203	as far, I think, or even farther still.
204	And of the rest, whoever's heart and spirit bids him,
205	let him come here and make trial, since you have angered me greatly,
206	in boxing or wrestling or even in running; I do not begrudge it
207	to any of all the Phaeacians, except Laodamas himself.
208	For he is my host; who would fight with one who makes him welcome?
209	Witless indeed and a nobody is that man
210	who brings on strife in contests with his host
211	in a foreign land; he cuts short all that is his own.
212	But of the others I refuse no one, nor do I make light of any,
213	but I am willing to know him and to make trial face to face.
214	For I am no mean man at all the contests there are among men:
215	I know well how to handle the polished bow;
216	I would be the first to shoot and strike my man in a throng
217	of hostile men, even if very many companions
218	stood near beside me and were shooting at the men.
219	Philoctetes alone surpassed me with the bow
220	in the land of the Trojans, when we Achaeans shot with the bow.
221	But of the others I say that I am far better,
222	of all the mortals who are now on the earth and eat bread.
223	But with men of former days I will not wish to vie,
224	neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia,
225	who vied even with the immortals in archery.
226	That is why great Eurytus died early, and did not reach old age
227	in his halls; for Apollo in anger
228	killed him, because he challenged him to shoot with the bow.
229	And I throw the spear as far as no other man shoots an arrow.
230	Only in running I fear that someone may outstrip me,
231	one of the Phaeacians; for I have been too shamefully overcome
232	among many waves, since on board the ship my keep was not
233	unfailing; and so my limbs are loosened.”
234	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence.
235	But Alcinous alone, answering him, said:
236	“Stranger, since what you say among us is not ungracious,
237	but you wish to show the excellence that attends you,
238	angry because this man stood beside you in the gathering
239	and rebuked you, in such a way as no mortal would make light of your excellence
240	who knew in his mind how to speak fitting words,
241	but come now, listen to my word, so that you may tell it
242	to another of the heroes too, when in your own halls
243	you feast beside your wife and your children,
244	remembering our excellence, the kind of deeds that on us too
245	Zeus lays, continually, from the days of our fathers.
246	For we are not flawless boxers, nor wrestlers,
247	but we run swiftly with our feet, and are the best with ships;
248	and always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances
249	and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds.
250	But come, you Phaeacian dancers, all who are the best,
251	make your sport, so that the stranger may tell his friends,
252	when he has returned home, how far we surpass others
253	in seafaring and in running and in dancing and in song.
254	And let someone go at once and bring Demodocus the clear-toned lyre
255	that lies somewhere in our house.”
256	So spoke godlike Alcinous, and the herald rose
257	to bring the hollow lyre from the king's house.
258	And the chosen stewards, nine in all, stood up,
259	officers of the people, who used to manage everything well at the gatherings,
260	and they leveled a dancing floor, and widened a fine gathering.
261	And the herald came near, bringing the clear-toned lyre
262	for Demodocus; and he then went into the middle; and around him young men
263	in their first youth took their stand, skilled in dancing,
264	and they struck the divine dancing floor with their feet. And Odysseus
265	gazed at the flashing of their feet, and marveled in his heart.
266	And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song
267	about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite,
268	how they first lay together in the house of Hephaestus
269	in secret; and he gave her many gifts, and shamed the bed and couch
270	of lord Hephaestus. But at once a messenger came to him,
271	the Sun, who had seen them lying together in love.
272	And when Hephaestus heard the heart-grieving tale,
273	he went to his smithy, brooding evil deep in his mind,
274	and set the great anvil on the anvil block, and forged bonds
275	unbreakable, not to be loosed, so that they might stay there steadfast.
276	But when he had made the snare, in his anger at Ares,
277	he went to the chamber where his bed lay,
278	and all around the bedposts he poured the bonds in a circle on every side,
279	and many too were poured down from above, from the roof beam,
280	fine as spider's webs, which no one could even see,
281	not even one of the blessed gods; for they were made exceedingly cunning.
282	But when he had poured the whole snare around the bed,
283	he made as if to go to Lemnos, the well-built citadel,
284	which is by far the dearest to him of all lands.
285	Nor did Ares of the golden reins keep a blind watch,
286	when he saw Hephaestus, famed for his craft, going away;
287	and he went to the house of renowned Hephaestus,
288	eager for the love of well-crowned Cytherea.
289	She had just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus,
290	and had sat down; and he came into the house,
291	and he clung to her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
292	“Come here, my dear, let us go to bed and take our pleasure, lying together;
293	for Hephaestus is no longer at home, but has gone already, I think,
294	to Lemnos, to the Sintians of savage speech.”
295	So he spoke, and it seemed welcome to her to lie down to sleep.
296	And the two went to the bed and slept; and around them the bonds
297	of Hephaestus of much wisdom poured, skillfully made,
298	and they could not move or lift any of their limbs.
299	And then indeed they knew that there was no more escape.
300	And the renowned god, lame in both legs, came near them,
301	having turned back before he reached the land of Lemnos;
302	for the Sun had kept watch for him and told him the tale.
303	He went to the house, grieving at heart,
304	and stood in the doorway, and a savage anger gripped him;
305	and he shouted terribly, and called out to all the gods:
306	“Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever,
307	come here, so that you may see deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear:
308	how Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus, because I am lame,
309	always dishonors me, and loves destructive Ares,
310	because he is handsome and sound of foot, while I
311	was born a weakling. Yet no one else is to blame for this,
312	but my two parents, who I wish had never begotten me.
313	But you will see where the two of them sleep in love,
314	having gone into my bed, and I am grieved to see it.
315	Yet I do not expect that they will lie like this even a little longer,
316	however much in love they are; soon they will both lose their wish
317	to sleep; but the snare and the bonds will hold them
318	until her father gives me back the whole bride-price,
319	all that I put into his hands for the sake of the dog-faced girl,
320	because his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control.”
321	So he spoke, and the gods gathered to the bronze-floored house;
322	there came Poseidon, the earth-holder; there came the great helper
323	Hermes; and far-working lord Apollo came.
324	But the goddesses, being female, stayed each at home for shame.
325	And the gods, the givers of blessings, stood in the doorway;
326	and unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods
327	as they saw the craft of Hephaestus of much wisdom.
328	And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor:
329	“Evil deeds do not prosper; the slow overtakes the swift,
330	as now Hephaestus, slow as he is, has caught Ares,
331	though he is the swiftest of the gods who hold Olympus,
332	lame as he is, by his craft; and so he owes the adulterer's fine.”
333	So they spoke such words to one another;
334	and lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to Hermes:
335	“Hermes, son of Zeus, guide, giver of blessings,
336	would you be willing, held fast in strong bonds,
337	to sleep in bed beside golden Aphrodite?”
338	Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered him:
339	“If only this might come to pass, lord Apollo who shoots from afar!
340	Let three times as many boundless bonds hold me fast all around,
341	and you gods look on, and all the goddesses too,
342	but let me sleep beside golden Aphrodite.”
343	So he spoke, and laughter rose among the immortal gods.
344	But laughter did not hold Poseidon, and he kept begging
345	Hephaestus, famed for his work, to set Ares free;
346	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
347	“Set him free; and I promise you that he himself, as you bid,
348	will pay all that is due among the immortal gods.”
349	Then the renowned god, lame in both legs, spoke to him in turn:
350	“Do not bid me do this, Poseidon, earth-holder;
351	pledges for the worthless are worthless things even to accept.
352	How could I bind you among the immortal gods,
353	if Ares should go off, escaping both the debt and the bonds?”
354	Then Poseidon the earth-shaker spoke to him in turn:
355	“Hephaestus, even if Ares should escape the debt
356	and go off in flight, I myself will pay you this.”
357	Then the renowned god, lame in both legs, answered him:
358	“It is not possible, nor would it be fitting, to refuse your word.”
359	So saying, the might of Hephaestus loosed the bonds.
360	And the two, when they were loosed from the bonds, strong as they were,
361	sprang up at once, and he went his way to Thrace,
362	and she, laughter-loving Aphrodite, came to Cyprus,
363	to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar.
364	There the Graces washed her and anointed her with oil
365	immortal, such as is upon the gods who live forever,
366	and they clothed her in lovely garments, a wonder to see.
367	This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus
368	was delighted in his heart as he listened, and so were the others,
369	the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships.
370	And Alcinous bade Halius and Laodamas
371	dance alone, since no one vied with them.
372	And when they had taken in their hands the beautiful ball,
373	a purple one, which wise-hearted Polybus had made for them,
374	one of them would fling it toward the shadowy clouds,
375	bending back, and the other, leaping high from the ground,
376	would catch it easily before his feet touched the ground.
377	But when they had made trial with the ball thrown straight up,
378	then they danced on the bountiful earth,
379	changing places again and again; and the other young men beat time,
380	standing about the gathering, and a great din arose.
381	Then brilliant Odysseus spoke to Alcinous:
382	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
383	you boasted that your dancers were the best,
384	and indeed it has proved true; awe holds me as I look.”
385	So he spoke, and the sacred might of Alcinous rejoiced,
386	and at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar:
387	“Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians.
388	The stranger seems to me to be a very wise man.
389	But come, let us give him a guest-gift, as is fitting.
390	For twelve distinguished kings throughout the land
391	rule as leaders, and I myself am the thirteenth;
392	of these let each of you bring him a freshly washed mantle and a tunic
393	and a talent of precious gold.
394	And let us quickly bring them all together, so that with them in his hands
395	the stranger may go to supper rejoicing in his heart.
396	And let Euryalus make amends to the man himself with words
397	and with a gift, since the word he spoke was by no means fair and right.”
398	So he spoke, and they all approved and bade it be done,
399	and each sent forth a herald to bring the gifts.
400	Then Euryalus answered him in turn and said:
401	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
402	well then, I will make amends to the stranger, as you bid.
403	I will give him this sword, all of bronze, which has a hilt
404	of silver, and a sheath of newly sawn ivory
405	is wound around it; and it will be worth much to him.”
406	So saying, he put the silver-studded sword in his hands,
407	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
408	“Farewell, stranger, father; and if any word has been spoken
409	that was terrible, may the storm winds at once snatch it up and carry it away.
410	And may the gods grant you to see your wife and to reach your native land,
411	since long now you suffer woes far from your friends.”
412	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
413	“And you too, my friend, fare very well, and may the gods grant you prosperity.
414	And may no longing for this sword ever come upon you hereafter,
415	which you have given me, making amends with your words.”
416	He spoke, and around his shoulders he slung the silver-studded sword.
417	And the sun set, and the glorious gifts were there for him.
418	And the noble heralds carried them to the house of Alcinous;
419	and the sons of noble Alcinous received them
420	and set the beautiful gifts beside their revered mother.
421	And the sacred might of Alcinous led the way for them,
422	and they came and sat down on the high chairs.
423	Then the might of Alcinous spoke to Arete:
424	“Come here, lady, bring a splendid chest, the best there is,
425	and put in it yourself a freshly washed mantle and a tunic.
426	And warm a cauldron on the fire for him, and heat water,
427	so that when he has bathed and seen all the gifts well laid away,
428	which the noble Phaeacians have brought here for him,
429	he may take delight in the feast and in hearing the strains of the song.
430	And I will grant him this beautiful goblet of mine,
431	of gold, so that remembering me all his days
432	he may pour libations in his hall to Zeus and the other gods.”
433	So he spoke, and Arete told her slave women
434	to set a great tripod over the fire with all speed.
435	And they set the tripod for the bath on the blazing fire,
436	and poured water into it, and took wood and kindled it beneath.
437	The fire played around the belly of the tripod, and the water grew warm;
438	meanwhile Arete brought out for the stranger a beautiful chest
439	from the chamber, and put in it the beautiful gifts,
440	the clothing and the gold, which the Phaeacians had given him;
441	and in it she herself put a mantle and a fine tunic,
442	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
443	“See to the lid yourself now, and quickly tie a knot on it,
444	so that no one may do you harm on the way, when hereafter
445	you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship.”
446	But when much-enduring brilliant Odysseus heard this,
447	at once he fitted on the lid, and quickly tied a knot on it,
448	a complicated one, which lady Circe had once taught him.
449	And straightway the housekeeper bade him bathe,
450	stepping into the bathtub; and he was glad in his heart to see
451	the warm bath, since he had not often been cared for so
452	since he left the house of Calypso of the lovely hair;
453	but all that time his keep had been steady, as for a god.
454	And when the slave women had washed him and anointed him with oil,
455	and put a fine cloak and a tunic around him,
456	he stepped out of the bathtub and went among the men
457	drinking wine; and Nausicaa, who had her beauty from the gods,
458	stood by the doorpost of the strongly made hall,
459	and marveled at Odysseus as she looked at him with her eyes,
460	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
461	“Farewell, stranger, so that someday, when you are in your native land,
462	you may remember me, since to me first you owe the price for your life.”
463	Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said:
464	“Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
465	so may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera,
466	that I may come home and see the day of my homecoming;
467	then even there I would pray to you as to a god
468	always, all my days; for you gave me my life, maiden.”
469	He spoke, and sat down on a chair beside King Alcinous;
470	and they were already serving out portions and mixing the wine.
471	And the herald came near, leading the trusty singer,
472	Demodocus, honored by the people; and he seated him
473	in the midst of the banqueters, leaning him against a tall pillar.
474	Then Odysseus of many wiles spoke to the herald,
475	having cut off a portion of the chine (and yet more was left over)
476	of a white-tusked boar, and there was rich lard on either side:
477	“Herald, here, give this meat, so that he may eat,
478	to Demodocus; and I will greet him, for all my grief.
479	For among all men upon the earth singers
480	have their share of honor and respect, because
481	the Muse has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers.”
482	So he spoke, and the herald took it and put it in the hands
483	of the hero Demodocus; and he received it and rejoiced in his heart.
484	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
485	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
486	then Odysseus of many wiles spoke to Demodocus:
487	“Demodocus, I praise you far above all mortals.
488	Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo;
489	for all too well, in due order, you sing the fate of the Achaeans,
490	all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through,
491	as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another.
492	But come now, change your theme, and sing of the fashioning of the horse
493	of wood, which Epeius made with Athena's help,
494	which once brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a snare,
495	having filled it with the men who sacked Ilios.
496	If you tell me all this fair and right,
497	at once I will tell all men
498	how the god has readily granted you wondrous song.”
499	So he spoke, and he, stirred by the god, began, and showed forth his song,
500	taking it up from where they had gone on board their well-benched ships
501	and were sailing away, having thrown fire on their huts,
502	the Argives, while the others, around famous Odysseus, were already
503	sitting in the place of assembly of the Trojans, hidden in the horse;
504	for the Trojans themselves had dragged it into the citadel.
505	So it stood there, and they talked on and on, confusedly,
506	sitting around it; and they were divided three ways in their counsel:
507	either to split the hollow timber with the pitiless bronze,
508	or to drag it to the top of the height and throw it down the rocks,
509	or to let it stand as a great offering to appease the gods;
510	and that is how it was to be accomplished in the end;
511	for it was fated that the city should perish once it had enfolded
512	the great wooden horse, where they were sitting, all the best
513	of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans.
514	And he sang how the sons of the Achaeans sacked the city,
515	pouring out from the horse, leaving the hollow ambush.
516	He sang how each in his own place ravaged the steep city,
517	but how Odysseus went to the house of Deiphobus
518	like Ares, together with godlike Menelaus.
519	There, he said, he dared the most dread fight,
520	and conquered in the end, through great-hearted Athena.
521	This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus
522	was melting, and tears wet his cheeks beneath his eyelids.
523	And as a woman weeps, flinging herself upon her dear husband,
524	who has fallen before his city and his people,
525	keeping off the pitiless day from his town and his children;
526	she, seeing him dying and gasping,
527	throws herself around him and wails shrilly; but they, behind her,
528	beating her back and shoulders with their spears,
529	lead her away into slavery, to have toil and misery,
530	and her cheeks waste away with most pitiful grief;
531	so Odysseus let a pitiful tear fall from beneath his brows.
532	Then he escaped the notice of all the others as he let tears fall,
533	but Alcinous alone noticed him and observed him,
534	sitting near him, and heard him groaning heavily.
535	And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar:
536	“Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians,
537	and let Demodocus now stop his clear-toned lyre;
538	for it is not to all of us that he gives delight by singing this.
539	Ever since we have been at supper and the divine singer was roused to sing,
540	from then on he has never ceased from miserable lamentation,
541	the stranger; surely grief has beset his mind.
542	But come, let him stop, so that we may all take delight alike,
543	hosts and guest, since it is much better so;
544	for it is for the sake of the revered stranger that all this has been made ready,
545	the escort and the gifts of friendship, which we give him, making him welcome.
546	As good as a brother are a stranger and a suppliant
547	to a man who has even a little hold on wisdom.
548	So now you too must not hide with crafty thoughts
549	whatever I ask you; it is better for you to speak.
550	Tell me the name by which they called you there, your mother and your father,
551	and the others who live in the city and round about it.
552	For no one at all among men is nameless,
553	no base man and no noble one, once he has been born,
554	but parents give names to all, when they have borne them.
555	And tell me your land and your people and your city,
556	so that our ships may carry you there, aiming with their minds;
557	for the Phaeacians have no helmsmen,
558	nor any steering oars, such as other ships have;
559	but the ships themselves know the thoughts and minds of men,
560	and they know the cities and rich fields of all
561	men, and most swiftly they cross the gulf of the sea,
562	wrapped in mist and cloud; and never do they
563	have any fear of coming to harm or of perishing.
564	But this I once heard told by my father,
565	Nausithous, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged
566	us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm.
567	He said that one day a well-made ship of the Phaeacian men,
568	as it came back from an escort on the misty sea,
569	he would shatter, and would cover our city round about with a great mountain.
570	So the old man used to say; and these things the god may bring to pass,
571	or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart.
572	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
573	where you were driven in your wanderings, and to what lands
574	of men you came, and of the men themselves and their well-peopled cities,
575	both those who are harsh and savage and unjust,
576	and those who are kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods.
577	And tell me why you weep and mourn within your heart
578	as you hear the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios.
579	This the gods wrought, and they spun destruction
580	for men, so that there might be song even for those yet to be.
581	Did some kinsman of yours by marriage perish before Ilios,
582	a good man, a son-in-law or a father-in-law, who are
583	the dearest after one's own blood and kin?
584	Or was it perhaps some companion, a man of kindly heart,
585	a good man? Since in no way inferior to a brother
586	is a companion who has wise understanding.”
