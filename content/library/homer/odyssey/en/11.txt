# Homer, Odyssey, Book 11 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	“And when we had come down to the ship and the sea,
2	first of all we dragged the ship down into the shining sea,
3	and we set the mast and the sails in the black ship,
4	and we took the sheep and put them on board, and went on board ourselves,
5	grieving, shedding big tears.
6	And behind our dark-prowed ship
7	she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion,
8	Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice.
9	And when we had busied ourselves with all the tackle throughout the ship,
10	we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her.
11	And all day long her sails were stretched as she crossed the sea.
12	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
13	and she came to the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus.
14	There are the land and the city of the Cimmerian men,
15	wrapped in mist and cloud; and never does
16	the shining sun look down on them with his rays,
17	neither when he climbs toward the starry sky,
18	nor when he turns back again from the sky toward the earth,
19	but deadly night is spread over those wretched mortals.
20	There we came and ran the ship ashore, and took the sheep
21	out of it; and we ourselves went along beside the stream of Oceanus
22	until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out.
23	There Perimedes and Eurylochus held
24	the victims; and I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
25	and dug a pit about a cubit's length this way and that,
26	and around it poured a drink offering to all the dead,
27	first with honey and milk, then with sweet wine,
28	and the third time with water; and I sprinkled white barley meal over it.
29	And I earnestly implored the strengthless heads of the dead,
30	vowing that, when I came to Ithaca, a barren heifer, the best I had,
31	I would sacrifice in my halls, and heap the pyre with good things,
32	and that to Teiresias apart I would sacrifice a sheep, to him alone,
33	all black, that stands out among our flocks.
34	And when with vows and prayers the tribes of the dead
35	I had begged, I took the sheep and cut their throats
36	over the pit, and the dark blood flowed; and there gathered
37	out of Erebus the souls of the dead who have died:
38	brides and young unmarried men and old men who had borne much,
39	and tender maidens with hearts new to sorrow,
40	and many stabbed by bronze-fitted spears,
41	men slain in battle, wearing their armor clotted with blood;
42	who kept coming in great numbers about the pit, one from one place and one from another,
43	with a wondrous cry; and pale fear took hold of me.
44	Then I urged on my companions and bade them
45	flay the sheep that lay there slaughtered with the pitiless bronze,
46	and burn them, and pray to the gods,
47	to mighty Hades and dread Persephone;
48	and I myself drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh
49	and sat there, and did not let the strengthless heads of the dead
50	come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias.
51	And first came the soul of my companion Elpenor;
52	for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth;
53	for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe,
54	unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us.
55	When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart,
56	and I spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
57	‘Elpenor, how did you come beneath the misty darkness?
58	You have come faster on foot than I in my black ship.’
59	So I spoke, and he groaned and answered me:
60	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
61	an evil fate from a god led me into blind folly, and endless wine.
62	Lying down in the hall of Circe I did not think
63	to come back down by going to the long ladder,
64	but fell straight down from the roof; and my neck
65	was broken from the vertebrae, and my soul went down to Hades.
66	Now I implore you by those you left behind, though they are not here,
67	by your wife and your father, who reared you when you were small,
68	and by Telemachus, whom you left an only son in your halls;
69	for I know that going from here, out of the house of Hades,
70	you will put in with your well-made ship at the island of Aeaea;
71	there, lord, I bid you remember me.
72	Do not go away and leave me behind unwept, unburied,
73	turning away, lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you;
74	but burn me with my armor, whatever I have,
75	and heap up a mound for me on the shore of the gray sea,
76	the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of.
77	Do this for me, and fix on the tomb the oar
78	with which I rowed when I was alive, among my companions.’
79	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
80	‘All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you.’
81	So we two sat exchanging hateful words,
82	I on one side, holding my sword over the blood,
83	and on the other the phantom of my companion, saying many things.
84	And there came the soul of my dead mother,
85	Anticleia, daughter of great-hearted Autolycus,
86	whom I had left alive when I went to sacred Ilios.
87	When I saw her I wept, and pitied her in my heart;
88	but even so, though deeply grieved, I did not let her first
89	come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias.
90	And there came the soul of Theban Teiresias,
91	holding a golden scepter, and he knew me and spoke to me:
92	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
93	why now, unhappy man, have you left the light of the sun
94	and come, to see the dead and this joyless place?
95	But draw back from the pit, and hold away your sharp sword,
96	so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly.’
97	So he spoke, and I drew back and thrust my silver-studded sword
98	into its sheath. And when he had drunk the dark blood,
99	then the noble seer spoke to me with words:
100	‘You seek a honey-sweet homecoming, shining Odysseus;
101	but a god will make it grievous for you; for I do not think
102	you will escape the shaker of the earth, since he has laid up a grudge against you in his heart,
103	angry because you blinded his dear son.
104	But even so you may still come home, though suffering evils,
105	if you will restrain your own heart and your companions',
106	when first you bring your well-made ship
107	to the island of Thrinacia, escaping the violet sea,
108	and find grazing there the cattle and fat sheep
109	of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things.
110	If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming,
111	you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils;
112	but if you harm them, then I foretell destruction for you,
113	for your ship and your companions. And even if you yourself escape,
114	you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions,
115	on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house,
116	overbearing men, who are devouring your substance,
117	wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price.
118	Yet you will surely take vengeance for their violence when you come.
119	But when you have killed the suitors in your halls,
120	whether by guile or openly with the sharp bronze,
121	then take a well-fitted oar and go,
122	until you come to men who do not know the sea
123	and do not eat food mixed with salt;
124	nor do they know ships with crimson prows,
125	or well-fitted oars, which are wings to ships.
126	And I will tell you a sign, very easy to see, and it will not escape you.
127	When another wayfarer meets you
128	and says that you have a winnowing fan on your shining shoulder,
129	then fix your well-fitted oar in the ground,
130	and make fine sacrifices to lord Poseidon,
131	a ram and a bull and a boar that mounts the sows,
132	and go home and offer holy hecatombs
133	to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky;
134	to all of them in order. And death will come to you yourself away from the sea,
135	a death so very gentle, that will kill you
136	worn out by an old age of ease; and around you your people
137	will be prosperous. This I tell you truly.’
138	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
139	‘Teiresias, this, I suppose, the gods themselves have spun.
140	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
141	I see here the soul of my dead mother;
142	she sits in silence near the blood, and has not brought herself
143	to look on her own son face to face or to speak to him.
144	Tell me, lord: how may she know me for who I am?’
145	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
146	‘An easy word I will tell you and put in your mind.
147	Whichever of the dead who have died you let
148	come near the blood will tell you truly;
149	but whoever you begrudge it to will go back again.’
150	So saying, the soul went into the house of Hades,
151	the soul of lord Teiresias, when he had told the oracles;
152	but I stayed there steadfastly, until my mother
153	came and drank the dark blood; and at once she knew me,
154	and lamenting addressed me with winged words:
155	‘My child, how did you come beneath the misty darkness
156	while you are alive? It is hard for the living to see these things.
157	For between are great rivers and dread streams,
158	Oceanus first of all, which no one can cross
159	on foot, unless he has a well-made ship.
160	Have you come here now from Troy, after wandering
161	a long time with your ship and your companions? And have you not yet come
162	to Ithaca, nor seen your wife in your halls?’
163	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
164	‘My mother, need brought me down to the house of Hades
165	to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias;
166	for I have not yet come near the Achaean land, nor yet set foot
167	on our own land, but I have been wandering always, enduring misery,
168	ever since I first followed brilliant Agamemnon
169	to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans.
170	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
171	What fate of death that lays men low brought you down?
172	Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis of the showering arrows
173	come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?
174	And tell me of my father and of my son, whom I left behind,
175	whether my prize of honor is still with them, or some other man
176	already holds it, and they say that I will come home no more.
177	And tell me of my wedded wife, her plan and her thought,
178	whether she stays beside her son and keeps all things safe,
179	or whether whoever is best of the Achaeans has already married her.’
180	So I spoke, and at once my lady mother answered:
181	‘Yes, truly she stays with enduring heart
182	in your halls; and always in misery for her
183	the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears.
184	No one yet holds your fine prize of honor, but undisturbed
185	Telemachus holds the domains, and feasts at equal feasts,
186	such as it is fitting for a man who gives judgment to attend;
187	for all men invite him. But your father stays there
188	in the country, and does not come down to the city. Nor has he, to sleep in,
189	beds and cloaks and glossy blankets,
190	but in the winter he sleeps where the slaves sleep in the house,
191	in the dust near the fire, and wears poor clothes on his body;
192	but when summer comes and the flourishing late summer,
193	everywhere on the slope of his vineyard
194	beds of fallen leaves are strewn on the ground for him.
195	There he lies grieving, and nurses a great sorrow in his heart,
196	longing for your homecoming, and hard old age comes upon him besides.
197	For so it was that I too perished and met my fate;
198	it was not the keen-sighted goddess of the showering arrows who in my halls
199	came upon me with her gentle arrows and killed me,
200	nor did any sickness come upon me, such as most often
201	takes the spirit from the limbs with hateful wasting;
202	but longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus,
203	and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life.’
204	So she spoke, and I pondered in my mind, and wished
205	to clasp the soul of my dead mother.
206	Three times I sprang toward her, and my heart bade me clasp her,
207	and three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream,
208	and sharp grief rose in my heart all the more,
209	and I spoke to her, addressing her with winged words:
210	‘My mother, why do you not stay for me when I am eager to clasp you,
211	so that even in the house of Hades, throwing our arms around each other,
212	we two might take our fill of chilling lament?
213	Or is this some phantom that noble Persephone
214	has urged on toward me, so that I may groan and mourn still more?’
215	So I spoke, and at once my lady mother answered:
216	‘Ah me, my child, ill-fated beyond all men,
217	it is not that Persephone, the daughter of Zeus, deceives you,
218	but this is the way of mortals, when one of them dies:
219	the sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones together,
220	but the strong might of the blazing fire
221	overcomes them, as soon as the spirit has left the white bones,
222	and the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers.
223	But long for the light as quickly as you can; and all these things
224	know, so that you may tell them to your wife hereafter.’
225	So we two were exchanging words, and the women
226	came, for noble Persephone urged them on,
227	all who had been the wives and daughters of chieftains.
228	And they gathered in a body about the dark blood,
229	but I planned how I might question each one.
230	And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best:
231	drawing the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh,
232	I would not let them all drink the dark blood at once.
233	So they came up one after another, and each
234	declared her own parentage; and I questioned them all.
235	There the first I saw was Tyro, daughter of a noble father,
236	who said that she was the offspring of noble Salmoneus,
237	and said she was the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus.
238	She fell in love with a river, the divine Enipeus,
239	which is by far the fairest of the rivers that run upon the earth,
240	and she used to go often to the fair streams of Enipeus.
241	And in his likeness the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth,
242	lay with her at the mouth of the eddying river;
243	and the surging wave stood around them, high as a mountain,
244	arched over, and hid the god and the mortal woman.
245	And he loosed her maiden girdle, and shed sleep upon her.
246	But when the god had finished the works of love,
247	and he clung to her hand, and spoke to her, and called her by name:
248	‘Rejoice in this love, lady; and as the year circles round
249	you will bear splendid children, for not in vain are the embraces
250	of the immortals; and you must look after them and rear them.
251	But now go to your house, and keep silent, and do not name me;
252	but I, you must know, am Poseidon the earth-shaker.’
253	So saying, he plunged beneath the billowing sea.
254	And she conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus,
255	who became mighty attendants of great Zeus,
256	both of them; Pelias lived in Iolcus with its wide dancing floors,
257	rich in flocks, and the other in sandy Pylos.
258	And her other sons she, the queen among women, bore to Cretheus,
259	Aeson and Pheres and Amythaon, who fought from his chariot.
260	And after her I saw Antiope, the daughter of Asopus,
261	who claimed that she had slept even in the arms of Zeus,
262	and she bore two sons, Amphion and Zethus,
263	who first founded the seat of seven-gated Thebes
264	and fenced it with towers, since without towers they could not
265	dwell in Thebes with its wide dancing floors, mighty though they were.
266	And after her I saw Alcmene, the wife of Amphitryon,
267	who bore Heracles, bold in endurance, lion-hearted,
268	having lain in love in the arms of great Zeus;
269	and Megara, the daughter of high-hearted Creon,
270	whom the son of Amphitryon, ever unyielding in his might, had as wife.
271	And I saw the mother of Oedipus, fair Epicaste,
272	who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind
273	in marrying her own son; and he, when he had slain his own father,
274	married her; and at once the gods made it known among men.
275	But he, suffering sorrows in lovely Thebes
276	through the deadly plans of the gods, ruled the Cadmeans;
277	but she went to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper,
278	when she had fastened a noose hanging sheer from the high roof-beam,
279	held fast by her own grief; but to him she left sorrows behind,
280	very many, all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass.
281	And I saw beautiful Chloris, whom Neleus once
282	married for her beauty, after giving a countless bride-price,
283	the youngest daughter of Amphion, son of Iasus,
284	who once ruled with might in Minyan Orchomenus;
285	and she was queen in Pylos, and bore him splendid children,
286	Nestor and Chromius and proud Periclymenus.
287	And besides these she bore strong Pero, a marvel to mortals,
288	whom all who dwelt around were wooing; but Neleus
289	would give her to no one who did not drive the broad-browed cattle with curving horns
290	out of Phylace, the cattle of mighty Iphiclus,
291	hard to drive; and these the noble seer alone promised
292	to drive off; but a hard fate of a god fettered him,
293	and grievous bonds, and the country herdsmen.
294	But when the months and the days were being fulfilled
295	as the year circled round again, and the seasons came round,
296	then at last mighty Iphiclus released him,
297	when he had told all the oracles; and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled.
298	And I saw Leda, the wife of Tyndareus,
299	who bore to Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons,
300	Castor, tamer of horses, and Polydeuces, good with his fists,
301	both of whom the life-giving earth holds fast, alive though they are;
302	and even beneath the earth, having honor from Zeus,
303	now they live on alternate days, and now again
304	they are dead; and they have been allotted honor like the gods.
305	And after her I saw Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus,
306	who declared that she had lain with Poseidon,
307	and she bore two sons, but they were short-lived,
308	godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes,
309	who were the tallest men the grain-giving earth ever reared,
310	and by far the handsomest, after famous Orion;
311	for at nine years old they were nine cubits
312	in breadth, and in height they had grown nine fathoms.
313	They even threatened the immortals on Olympus
314	that they would raise the din of furious war.
315	They were eager to set Ossa on Olympus, and on Ossa
316	Pelion with its quivering leaves, so that heaven might be scaled.
317	And they would have accomplished it, if they had reached the measure of youth;
318	but the son of Zeus, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore, destroyed
319	them both, before the whiskers under their temples
320	had bloomed and covered their jaws with a fresh growth of down.
321	And I saw Phaedra and Procris and beautiful Ariadne,
322	the daughter of baleful Minos, whom Theseus once
323	was bringing from Crete to the hill of sacred Athens,
324	but he had no joy of her; for before that Artemis slew her
325	in sea-washed Dia, on the testimony of Dionysus.
326	And I saw Maera and Clymene and hateful Eriphyle,
327	who took precious gold as the price of her own husband.
328	But all of them I could not tell or name,
329	all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw;
330	for before that even the immortal night would wane. But it is time
331	to sleep, either when I have gone to the swift ship and my companions,
332	or here; and my escort will be the care of the gods and of you.”
333	So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence,
334	and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls.
335	And among them white-armed Arete began to speak;
336	“Phaeacians, how does this man seem to you to be
337	in looks and stature and the well-balanced mind within?
338	And he is my guest, though each of you has his share of honor.
339	So do not hurry to send him away, and the gifts
340	for one in such need do not cut short; for many
341	possessions lie in your halls by the will of the gods.”
342	And among them spoke also the old hero Echeneus,
343	who was one of the older Phaeacian men:
344	“Friends, not wide of the mark, nor against our expectation,
345	is our prudent queen speaking to us; so obey her.
346	But on Alcinous here depend both deed and word.”
347	Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said:
348	“This word shall indeed be so, as surely as I
349	live and am lord over the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar.
350	But let our guest, much as he is in need of his homecoming, bring himself
351	to stay on nonetheless until tomorrow, until I complete
352	the whole present; and his escort will be the concern of men,
353	of all of them, and mine most of all; for mine is the power among the people.”
354	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
355	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
356	even if you should bid me stay here for a year,
357	and should speed my escort and give splendid gifts,
358	that is what I would want, and it would be far better
359	to come to my dear native land with a fuller hand;
360	and I would be more respected and dearer to men,
361	by all who should see me come home to Ithaca.”
362	Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said:
363	“Odysseus, this we do not judge you to be when we look at you,
364	a deceiver and a cheat, such as the many
365	whom the black earth feeds, men scattered far and wide,
366	fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see;
367	but you have beauty of words, and within you are good wits,
368	and you have told your tale skillfully, as a singer does,
369	the grievous sorrows of all the Argives and of yourself.
370	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
371	whether you saw any of your godlike companions, who with you
372	followed together to Ilios and there met their fate.
373	This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time
374	to sleep in the hall; so go on telling me of wondrous doings.
375	I could hold out even till brilliant Dawn, if you
376	would bring yourself to tell in the hall of your sorrows.”
377	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
378	“Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people,
379	there is a time for many words, and a time also for sleep;
380	but if you still long to listen, I would not
381	begrudge telling you of other things more pitiable even than these,
382	the sorrows of my companions, who perished afterward,
383	who escaped the groaning war cry of the Trojans,
384	but in their homecoming perished by the will of an evil woman.
385	But when the souls had been scattered this way and that
386	by holy Persephone, the souls of the womenfolk,
387	there came the soul of Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
388	grieving; and around him were gathered the others, all who with him
389	had died in the house of Aegisthus and met their fate.
390	He knew me at once, when he had drunk the dark blood,
391	and he wept loudly, letting big tears fall,
392	stretching out his hands to me, eager to reach me;
393	but no longer was there steadfast strength in him, or any vigor,
394	such as there was before in his supple limbs.
395	When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart,
396	and I spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
397	‘Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men,
398	what fate of death that lays men low brought you down?
399	Was it among the ships that Poseidon brought you down,
400	rousing a blast of grievous winds that none would envy?
401	Or did merciless men do you harm on land
402	as you were cutting off their cattle and fine flocks of sheep,
403	or fighting for a city and its women?’
404	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
405	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
406	it was not among the ships that Poseidon brought me down,
407	rousing a blast of grievous winds that none would envy,
408	nor did merciless men do me harm on land,
409	but Aegisthus, contriving death and fate for me,
410	killed me, with my ruinous wife, when he had called me to his house
411	and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger.
412	So I died a most pitiable death; and around me my other companions
413	were killed unceasingly, like white-tusked hogs
414	that are slaughtered in the house of a rich man of great power
415	for a wedding, or a shared feast, or a rich banquet.
416	You have been present already at the slaughter of many men,
417	killed singly and in the fierce battle;
418	but you would have grieved most in your heart, had you seen that sight,
419	how around the mixing bowl and the laden tables
420	we lay in the hall, and the whole floor seethed with blood.
421	And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter,
422	Cassandra, whom scheming Clytemnestra killed
423	beside me; and I, raising my hands, let them fall to the ground
424	as I lay dying with the sword through me; but she, the dog-faced one,
425	turned away, and did not bring herself, though I was going to the house of Hades,
426	to close my eyes with her hands or to shut my mouth.
427	So there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman
428	who puts such deeds as these into her heart;
429	such as the shameful deed that she too devised,
430	contriving slaughter for her wedded husband. Truly I thought
431	that I would come home welcome to my children and my slaves;
432	but she, with her mind set on grim things beyond all others,
433	has poured shame on herself and on those to come hereafter,
434	the womenfolk, even on one who does good.’
435	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
436	‘Ah, truly far-seeing Zeus has hated the offspring of Atreus
437	terribly, through the plans of women,
438	from of old: for Helen's sake many of us perished,
439	and against you Clytemnestra was preparing a snare while you were far away.’
440	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
441	‘So now you too must never be kind even to your wife;
442	do not declare to her every word that you know well,
443	but tell her one part, and let another part be hidden.
444	But not to you, Odysseus, will death come from your wife;
445	for she has great good sense, and knows sound counsels in her heart,
446	the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope.
447	Truly we left her a young bride
448	when we went to the war; and she had a child at her breast,
449	an infant, who now, I think, sits among the number of men,
450	happy one! For his dear father will see him when he comes,
451	and he will greet his father, as is the custom.
452	But my wife did not even let me have my fill of my son
453	with my eyes; before that she killed me myself.
454	And I will tell you something else, and you lay it to heart:
455	secretly, and not openly, to your dear native land
456	bring your ship to shore; for there is no trusting women any more.
457	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
458	whether you have heard of my son as still living anywhere,
459	perhaps in Orchomenus, or in sandy Pylos,
460	or perhaps with Menelaus in broad Sparta;
461	for brilliant Orestes has not yet died upon the earth.’
462	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
463	‘Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this? I do not know at all
464	whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind.’
465	So we two stood exchanging hateful words,
466	grieving, shedding big tears;
467	and there came the soul of Achilles, son of Peleus,
468	and of Patroclus, and of noble Antilochus,
469	and of Ajax, who was the best in looks and form
470	of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus.
471	And the soul of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus knew me,
472	and lamenting addressed me with winged words:
473	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
474	hard man, what still greater deed will you devise in your mind?
475	How did you dare to come down to Hades, where the dead
476	dwell without sense, the phantoms of dead mortals?’
477	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
478	‘Achilles, son of Peleus, by far the strongest of the Achaeans,
479	I came to consult Teiresias, in case he might tell me some plan
480	how I might come to rocky Ithaca;
481	for I have not yet come near the Achaean land, nor yet set foot
482	on our own land, but always I have troubles. As for you, Achilles,
483	no man before was more blessed than you, nor will be hereafter.
484	For before, while you lived, we honored you equally with the gods,
485	we Argives, and now again you are mighty among the dead,
486	being here; so do not grieve at all that you are dead, Achilles.’
487	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
488	‘Do not speak comfortingly to me of death, shining Odysseus.
489	I would rather be bound to the soil and serve another for hire,
490	a man who had no land of his own and no great livelihood,
491	than be lord over all the dead who have perished.
492	But come, tell me word of my noble son,
493	whether he followed to the war to be a champion, or not.
494	And tell me of noble Peleus, if you have heard anything,
495	whether he still has honor among the many Myrmidons,
496	or whether they dishonor him throughout Hellas and Phthia,
497	because old age holds him fast in his hands and feet.
498	For I am not there to help him under the rays of the sun,
499	such as I was once in wide Troy,
500	when I killed the best of the army, defending the Argives.
501	If I could come like that, even for a little while, to my father's house,
502	then I would make my might and my irresistible hands hateful to any
503	of those who do him violence and keep him from his honor.’
504	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
505	‘Truly, of noble Peleus I have heard nothing,
506	but of your dear son Neoptolemus
507	I will tell you the whole truth, as you bid me;
508	for it was I myself who brought him in the hollow, balanced ship
509	from Scyros to join the well-greaved Achaeans.
510	Truly, whenever we took counsel about the city of Troy,
511	he always spoke first, and did not miss the right words;
512	godlike Nestor and I alone surpassed him.
513	But whenever we fought with the bronze on the plain of the Trojans,
514	he never stayed in the crowd of men or in the throng,
515	but ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might,
516	and many men he killed in grim combat.
517	But all of them I could not tell or name,
518	how great an army he killed, defending the Argives,
519	but what a man he slew with the bronze, the son of Telephus,
520	the hero Eurypylus, and many of his companions around him,
521	the Ceteians, were killed, for the sake of gifts to a woman.
522	He was the handsomest man I saw, after brilliant Memnon.
523	And when we went down into the horse that Epeius made,
524	we, the best of the Argives, and to me had been given the whole command,
525	both to throw open the close ambush and to close it,
526	then the other leaders and rulers of the Danaans
527	were wiping away their tears, and the limbs of each trembled beneath him;
528	but him I never saw at all with my eyes
529	either turning pale in his fair skin or wiping
530	a tear from his cheeks; but he kept supplicating me again and again
531	to let him out of the horse, and he kept feeling for the hilt of his sword
532	and his spear heavy with bronze, and he had evil in mind for the Trojans.
533	But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam,
534	with his share and a noble prize he went on board his ship
535	unharmed, neither struck by the sharp bronze
536	nor stabbed hand to hand, as often
537	happens in war; for Ares rages without distinction.’
538	So I spoke, and the soul of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus
539	went ranging with long strides across the meadow of asphodel,
540	glad because I said that his son was preeminent.
541	And the other souls of the dead who have died
542	stood grieving, and each one asked about its own sorrows.
543	Alone the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon,
544	stood apart, still angry because of the victory
545	that I won over him when I pleaded my case beside the ships
546	for the armor of Achilles; his lady mother had set it as a prize,
547	and the sons of the Trojans were the judges, and Pallas Athena.
548	Would that I had never been the victor for such a prize!
549	For because of it the earth has held fast such a head,
550	Ajax, who in looks and in deeds stood out above the rest
551	of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus.
552	To him I spoke with soothing words:
553	‘Ajax, son of noble Telamon, were you not then
554	even in death to forget your anger against me over the armor,
555	the ruinous armor? The gods made it a bane to the Argives;
556	for in you they lost so great a tower; and for you we Achaeans
557	grieve as much as for the head of Achilles, son of Peleus,
558	now that you have perished, without end; and no one else
559	is to blame, but Zeus, who hated the army of the Danaan spearmen
560	terribly, and set your fate upon you.
561	But come here, my lord, so that you may hear my word
562	and speech; and tame your might and your proud spirit.’
563	So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, but went after the other
564	souls of the dead who have died, into Erebus.
565	There, angry as he was, he would still have spoken to me, or I to him;
566	but the heart in my own breast wished
567	to see the souls of the others who have died.
568	There I saw Minos, the splendid son of Zeus,
569	holding a golden scepter, giving the law to the dead,
570	seated, and around him they asked the lord for judgment,
571	sitting and standing, throughout the wide-gated house of Hades.
572	And after him I caught sight of huge Orion
573	herding together wild beasts across the meadow of asphodel,
574	beasts that he himself had killed on the lonely mountains,
575	holding in his hands a club all of bronze, forever unbroken.
576	And I saw Tityus, the son of glorious Earth,
577	lying on the ground; and he lay stretched over nine plethra,
578	and two vultures, sitting one on either side, were tearing his liver,
579	plunging within the caul, and he could not ward them off with his hands;
580	for he had dragged off Leto, the glorious wife of Zeus,
581	as she went to Pytho through Panopeus with its lovely dancing floors.
582	And I saw Tantalus too, suffering bitter pains,
583	standing in a lake; and the water came up to his chin,
584	and he strained in his thirst, but could not get anything to drink;
585	for every time the old man stooped, eager to drink,
586	so often the water was swallowed up and vanished, and about his feet
587	the black earth appeared, and a god dried it up.
588	And trees with high foliage let their fruit hang down over his head,
589	pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit,
590	and sweet figs and flourishing olives;
591	and whenever the old man made straight for them, to grasp them with his hands,
592	the wind would fling them toward the shadowy clouds.
593	And I saw Sisyphus too, suffering bitter pains,
594	heaving a huge stone with both hands.
595	Truly, bracing himself with hands and feet,
596	he would push the stone up toward the crest; but whenever he was about
597	to heave it over the top, then its mighty weight would turn it back;
598	back then to the plain rolled the shameless stone.
599	But he would push it up again, straining, and the sweat
600	ran from his limbs, and dust rose up from his head.
601	And after him I caught sight of mighty Heracles,
602	his phantom; but he himself among the immortal gods
603	takes his delight in their feasts, and has Hebe of the lovely ankles,
604	the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals.
605	And around him rose a clamor of the dead, as of birds
606	scattering in terror on every side; and he was like dark night,
607	holding his naked bow, with an arrow on the string,
608	glancing terribly around, like one ever about to shoot.
609	And terrible was the baldric around his chest,
610	a golden strap, on which wondrous works were wrought,
611	bears and wild boars and lions with flashing eyes,
612	combats and battles and slaughters and killings of men.
613	May he who fashioned it never fashion another work,
614	he who laid up that strap in his craft.
615	And he in turn knew me, when he saw me with his eyes,
616	and lamenting addressed me with winged words:
617	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
618	ah, wretch, truly you too drag out some evil fate,
619	such as I bore under the rays of the sun.
620	I was the son of Zeus, son of Cronus, yet I had misery
621	boundless; for I was made subject to a man far worse than I,
622	and he laid hard labors upon me.
623	And once he sent me here to bring the hound; for he could think
624	of no other labor that would be harder for me than this;
625	and I brought it up and led it out of Hades,
626	and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena.’
627	So saying, he went back again into the house of Hades,
628	but I stayed there steadfastly, in case any more should come
629	of the heroes who had perished in former days.
630	And I would have seen still more men of earlier times, whom I wished to see,
631	Theseus and Peirithous, glorious children of the gods;
632	but before that the countless tribes of the dead gathered
633	with a wondrous din; and pale fear took hold of me
634	lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster,
635	noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades.
636	At once then I went to the ship and ordered my companions
637	to go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables.
638	And they went on board at once and sat down at the rowing benches.
639	And the wave of the stream carried the ship down the river Oceanus,
640	first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind.”
