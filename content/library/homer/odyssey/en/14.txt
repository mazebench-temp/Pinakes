1	But he went up from the harbor by the rugged path
2	through the wooded country, among the heights, to where Athena
3	had shown him the brilliant swineherd, who cared most for his substance
4	of all the servants that brilliant Odysseus had acquired.
5	He found him sitting in the porch, where his fold
6	had been built high, in a place with a view all round,
7	beautiful and great, with a clear space all around; the swineherd
8	had built it himself for the swine, while his master was away,
9	without his mistress and old Laertes,
10	with hauled stones, and had topped it with a coping of wild pear;
11	and outside he had driven stakes from end to end, this way and that,
12	thick and close-set, splitting off the dark heart of the oak;
13	and within the fold he had made twelve sties
14	close to one another, beds for the swine; and in each
15	fifty swine that sleep on the ground were penned,
16	breeding sows; but the boars lay outside,
17	far fewer; for these kept dwindling, eaten
18	by the godlike suitors, since the swineherd sent them
19	always the best of all the well-fed fat hogs;
20	and they numbered three hundred and sixty.
21	And beside them always lay the dogs, like wild beasts,
22	four of them, which the swineherd, leader of men, had reared.
23	And he himself was fitting sandals about his feet,
24	cutting a good-colored oxhide; and the others
25	had gone off this way and that with the herded swine,
26	three of them; and the fourth he had sent to the city
27	to take a boar to the overbearing suitors, under compulsion,
28	so that they might sacrifice it and have their hearts' fill of meat.
29	Suddenly the barking dogs caught sight of Odysseus.
30	And they ran at him, baying; but Odysseus
31	sat down, by craft, and the staff fell from his hand.
32	There by his own farmstead he would have suffered shameful pain;
33	but the swineherd, following quickly on swift feet,
34	rushed out through the gateway, and the leather fell from his hand.
35	He shouted at the dogs and drove them this way and that,
36	with showers of stones; and he spoke to his master:
37	“Old man, truly the dogs came near to tearing you to pieces
38	all of a sudden, and you would have poured disgrace on me.
39	And the gods have given me other sorrows and groaning;
40	for, mourning and grieving for my godlike master,
41	I sit here, and rear fat hogs for others
42	to eat; while he, perhaps yearning for food,
43	wanders over the land and the city of men of another tongue,
44	if he still lives somewhere and looks on the light of the sun.
45	But follow me, let us go to the hut, old man, so that you too,
46	when you have had your heart's fill of bread and wine,
47	may tell me where you are from and how many sorrows you have endured.”
48	So saying, the brilliant swineherd led the way to the hut,
49	and brought him in and seated him, and strewed thick brushwood beneath him,
50	and spread on it the hide of a shaggy wild goat,
51	his own bedding, great and thick. And Odysseus rejoiced
52	that he received him so, and he spoke to him, and called him by name:
53	“May Zeus grant you, stranger, and the other immortal gods,
54	what you most desire, since you have received me gladly.”
55	Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus:
56	“Stranger, it is not lawful for me, not even if one more wretched than you should come,
57	to dishonor a stranger; for they are all from Zeus,
58	strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear,
59	when it comes from us; for that is the way of slaves,
60	who are always afraid, when masters hold command over them,
61	the young ones. For truly the gods have bound up the homecoming of that man
62	who would have loved me with care and granted me wealth,
63	such as a kind-hearted master gives to his servant,
64	a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife,
65	when he has toiled much for him, and a god makes his work grow,
66	as this work of mine grows, at which I stay.
67	So my master would have helped me greatly, if he had grown old here;
68	but he has perished—would that the race of Helen had perished utterly,
69	brought to its knees, since she loosed the knees of many men;
70	for he too went, to win satisfaction for Agamemnon,
71	to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans.”
72	So saying, he quickly drew his tunic tight with his belt,
73	and went to the sties, where the herds of porkers were penned.
74	From there he chose two and brought them, and sacrificed them both,
75	and singed them and cut them up and threaded them on spits.
76	And when he had roasted it all, he brought it and set it before Odysseus,
77	hot, on the spits as they were; and he sprinkled white barley meal on it;
78	and in an ivy-wood bowl he mixed honey-sweet wine,
79	and he himself sat facing him, and urged him on and said:
80	“Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here,
81	porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat,
82	with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard, nor any pity.
83	The blessed gods do not love cruel deeds,
84	but honor justice and the righteous deeds of men.
85	Even men hostile and merciless, who set foot on another's
86	land, and Zeus gives them spoil,
87	and they fill their ships and set out to go home,
88	even on their minds falls a mighty fear of the gods' regard.
89	But these men know something—they have heard some voice of a god—
90	of that man's grim destruction, since they will not woo
91	justly, nor go back to their own, but at their ease
92	they devour his possessions overweeningly, and there is no sparing.
93	For as many nights and days as come from Zeus,
94	they never sacrifice one victim only, nor two;
95	and they waste the wine, drawing it off overweeningly.
96	For truly his livelihood was untold; no one of the heroes
97	had so much, neither on the dark mainland
98	nor in Ithaca itself; not even twenty men together
99	have so much wealth; and I will recount it to you.
100	Twelve herds of cattle on the mainland; as many flocks of sheep,
101	as many droves of pigs, as many wide-ranging herds of goats,
102	which strangers and his own herdsmen graze.
103	And here, in all, eleven wide-ranging herds of goats
104	graze at the far edge, and good men keep watch over them.
105	Each one of these always brings them a beast for the day,
106	whichever of the well-fed goats seems the best.
107	But I guard these swine here and protect them,
108	and I pick out the best of the swine with care and send it to them.”
109	So he spoke; and the other heartily ate the meat and drank the wine,
110	greedily, in silence, and he was planting evils for the suitors.
111	But when he had dined and satisfied his heart with food,
112	then he filled the bowl from which he himself drank and gave it to him,
113	full of wine; and he received it, and rejoiced in his heart,
114	and he spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
115	“My friend, who was it that bought you with his own possessions,
116	so very rich and mighty as you say?
117	And you say that he perished to win satisfaction for Agamemnon.
118	Tell me, in case I may know him, being such a man.
119	Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods,
120	whether I might bring news of having seen him; for I have wandered far.”
121	Then the swineherd, leader of men, answered him:
122	“Old man, no man who comes here from his wanderings
123	bringing news of him could persuade his wife and his dear son;
124	but vagrants wanting keep tell lies at random,
125	and do not wish to tell the truth.
126	Whoever comes wandering to the land of Ithaca
127	goes to my mistress and speaks deceits;
128	and she receives him well and makes him welcome and questions him about each thing,
129	and as she mourns, the tears fall from her eyelids,
130	as is the custom of a woman, when her husband has perished far away.
131	Quickly you too, old sir, would fashion a tale,
132	if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing.
133	Already the dogs and the swift birds must have
134	torn the skin from his bones, and his soul has left him;
135	or else the fish have eaten him in the sea, and his bones
136	lie on the mainland, wrapped in deep sand.
137	So he has perished there, and sorrows hereafter are made ready
138	for all his friends, and for me most of all; for never again another
139	master so kind shall I find, wherever I go,
140	not even if I should come again to my father's and mother's
141	house, where I was first born, and they themselves reared me.
142	Nor do I mourn so much for them any longer, eager though I am
143	to see them with my eyes, being in my native land;
144	but longing for Odysseus, who is gone, takes hold of me.
145	Him, stranger, even though he is not here, I feel shame
146	to name; for he loved me greatly and cared for me in his heart;
147	but I call him dear master, even though he is far away.”
148	Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus spoke to him in turn:
149	“My friend, since you deny it utterly, and say no longer
150	that he will come, and your heart is always unbelieving,
151	yet I will not speak idly, but with an oath,
152	that Odysseus is coming home; and let me have a reward for good news
153	at once, when he comes and reaches his own house:
154	clothe me in a cloak and a tunic, fine clothes;
155	but before that, though I am in great want, I would accept nothing.
156	For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man
157	who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits.
158	Now let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the table of hospitality,
159	and the hearth of noble Odysseus, to which I have come:
160	truly all these things will be fulfilled as I say.
161	In this very year Odysseus will come here;
162	as the old month wanes and the new begins,
163	he will come home, and will take vengeance on whoever
164	here dishonors his wife and his shining son.”
165	Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus:
166	“Old man, I shall never pay you that reward for good news,
167	nor will Odysseus ever come home again; but at your ease
168	drink, and let us think of other things, and do not remind me
169	of these; for truly the heart in my breast
170	grieves whenever anyone reminds me of my devoted master.
171	But let us leave the oath alone, and may Odysseus
172	come, as I wish it, and as Penelope does,
173	and the old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus.
174	But now again I mourn, past forgetting, for the son whom Odysseus begot,
175	Telemachus; when the gods had reared him like a sapling,
176	and I thought that he would be among men in no way inferior
177	to his dear father, admirable in form and looks,
178	then one of the immortals harmed the well-balanced mind within him,
179	or one of men; and he has gone in search of news of his father
180	to sacred Pylos; and the noble suitors
181	lie in ambush for him on his way home, so that the race
182	of godlike Arceisius may perish utterly from Ithaca, nameless.
183	But let us leave him alone, whether he is caught
184	or escapes, and the son of Cronus holds his hand over him.
185	But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows,
186	and tell me this truly, so that I may know it well:
187	Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?
188	And on what sort of ship did you come? And how did sailors
189	bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
190	For I do not think that you came here on foot.”
191	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
192	“Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly.
193	May there now be, for us two, for a long time, both food
194	and sweet wine, as we stay inside the hut,
195	to feast on in silence, while others go about the work;
196	then easily, even for a whole year,
197	I could not come to the end of telling the sorrows of my heart,
198	all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods.
199	I claim that my line is from wide Crete,
200	the son of a rich man; and many other
201	sons too were born and reared in his halls,
202	lawful sons of his wife; but me a bought mother bore,
203	a concubine; yet he honored me equally with his true-born sons,
204	Castor, son of Hylax, of whose line I claim to be,
205	who was honored then among the Cretans by the people like a god
206	for his prosperity and wealth and his glorious sons.
207	But the fates of death came and carried him away
208	to the house of Hades; and his livelihood was divided
209	by his high-hearted sons, who cast lots for it,
210	but to me they gave very little, and allotted me a house.
211	And I took a wife from people of many allotments
212	for my excellence, since I was no worthless man
213	nor one who fled from war; but now all that has left me;
214	but even so, looking at the stubble, I think you can
215	tell what it was; for truly great misery holds me, beyond measure.
216	Truly Ares and Athena gave me daring
217	and the strength that breaks men; whenever I picked for an ambush
218	the best men, planting evils for the enemy,
219	never did my proud spirit foresee death,
220	but leaping out far the first I would kill with my spear
221	whatever man of the enemy gave way before me with his feet.
222	Such was I in war; but work was never dear to me,
223	nor the care of a household, which rears splendid children,
224	but ships with oars were always dear to me,
225	and wars, and well-polished javelins, and arrows,
226	grim things, which to others are things to shudder at.
227	But to me those things were dear which, I suppose, a god put in my mind;
228	for different men take delight in different works.
229	For before the sons of the Achaeans set foot on Troy,
230	nine times I led men and swift-sailing ships
231	against foreign men, and very much fell to my lot.
232	From this I picked out what satisfied my heart, and much afterward
233	I received by lot; and quickly my house grew, and then
234	I was feared and respected among the Cretans.
235	But when far-seeing Zeus devised that hateful journey,
236	which loosed the knees of many men,
237	then they bade me and far-famed Idomeneus
238	lead the ships to Ilios; and there was no means
239	to refuse, for the harsh talk of the people held us.
240	There we sons of the Achaeans fought for nine years,
241	and in the tenth we sacked the city of Priam and set out
242	for home with the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans.
243	But for me, wretched man, Zeus the counselor devised evils.
244	For only one month I stayed, taking delight in my children
245	and my wedded wife and my possessions; but then
246	my heart bade me sail to Egypt,
247	when I had fitted out ships well, with my godlike companions.
248	Nine ships I fitted out, and the men quickly gathered.
249	For six days then my trusty companions
250	feasted; and I provided many victims
251	to sacrifice to the gods and to prepare a feast for themselves.
252	And on the seventh day we went aboard and from wide Crete
253	we sailed with a fresh and fair North Wind,
254	easily, as if downstream; and not one of my
255	ships came to harm, but unharmed and free of sickness
256	we sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships.
257	On the fifth day we came to the fair-flowing Aegyptus,
258	and in the river Aegyptus I brought my curving ships to a halt.
259	Then indeed I bade my trusty companions
260	stay there beside the ships and guard the ships,
261	and I urged on scouts to go to the lookouts;
262	but they, giving way to arrogance, following their own might,
263	at once the beautiful fields of the Egyptian men
264	they laid waste, and carried off the women and the little children,
265	and killed the men; and quickly the cry came to the city.
266	And they heard the shout, and when dawn appeared
267	they came; and the whole plain was filled with foot soldiers and horses
268	and the flash of bronze; and Zeus who delights in thunder
269	cast evil panic among my companions, and no one dared
270	to stand and face them; for evils stood about us on every side.
271	There they killed many of us with the sharp bronze,
272	and others they led up inland alive, to work for them under compulsion.
273	But Zeus himself put this thought in my mind
274	—would that I had died and met my fate
275	there in Egypt; for a bane was still waiting for me—
276	at once I took the well-made helmet from my head
277	and the shield from my shoulders, and threw the spear from my hand;
278	and I went to meet the horses of the king
279	and clasped his knees and kissed them; and he protected me and pitied me,
280	and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears.
281	Truly very many rushed at me with their ash spears,
282	eager to kill me—for they were very angry—
283	but he kept them off, and had regard for the wrath of Zeus,
284	the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds.
285	There I stayed for seven years, and I gathered many
286	goods among the Egyptian men; for they all gave to me.
287	But when the eighth year came round in its course,
288	then came a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits,
289	a greedy rogue, who had already done many evils to men;
290	he took me away, winning me over by his cunning, until we came
291	to Phoenicia, where his house and his possessions lay.
292	There I stayed with him for a full year's round.
293	But when the months and the days were being fulfilled
294	as the year circled round again, and the seasons came round,
295	he put me on board a seafaring ship bound for Libya,
296	having planned lies, so that I might take a cargo there with him,
297	but there, in truth, so that he might sell me and get an untold price.
298	I followed him on board, suspecting it, but under compulsion.
299	And she ran before a fresh and fair North Wind,
300	on the mid-sea course past Crete; and Zeus devised destruction for them.
301	But when we were leaving Crete, and no other
302	land appeared, but only sky and sea,
303	then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud
304	above the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it.
305	And at the same moment Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt at the ship;
306	and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus,
307	and was filled with sulfur; and all fell out of the ship.
308	And like sea crows around the black ship
309	they were carried on the waves, and the god took away their homecoming.
310	But for me, Zeus himself, though I had sorrows in my heart,
311	put the mast of vast length of the dark-prowed ship
312	into my hands, so that I might still escape bane.
313	Clinging to it I was borne along by the deadly winds.
314	For nine days I was borne along; and on the tenth black night
315	a great rolling wave brought me to the land of the Thesprotians.
316	There the king of the Thesprotians, Pheidon, took care of me,
317	the hero, without price; for his dear son came upon me
318	overcome by cold and weariness, and led me home,
319	raising me by the hand, until he came to his father's house;
320	and he put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing.
321	There I heard of Odysseus; for the king said
322	that he had entertained him and made him welcome on his way to his native land,
323	and he showed me the possessions that Odysseus had gathered,
324	bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil.
325	It would feed another man to the tenth generation,
326	so many treasures of his lay stored in the halls of the lord.
327	And he said that he had gone to Dodona, so that from the god's
328	high-leafed oak he might hear the will of Zeus,
329	how he should return to the rich land of Ithaca,
330	after being away so long, openly or in secret.
331	And he swore to me myself, pouring libations in his house,
332	that the ship had been drawn down and the companions were ready
333	who would take him to his dear native land.
334	But he sent me off first; for a ship happened to be going
335	of Thesprotian men to Dulichium, rich in wheat.
336	There he bade them take me to King Acastus
337	with care; but an evil counsel pleased their minds
338	concerning me, so that I might yet come utterly to a bane of misery.
339	But when the seafaring ship had sailed far from the land,
340	at once they contrived the day of slavery for me.
341	They stripped me of my cloak and my tunic, my clothing,
342	and around me they put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic,
343	rent, which you see with your own eyes;
344	and in the evening they came to the tilled fields of clear-seen Ithaca.
345	There they bound me fast in the well-benched ship
346	with a well-twisted rope, firmly, and they themselves went ashore
347	and hastily took their supper beside the shore of the sea.
348	But the gods themselves undid my bonds
349	easily; and covering my head with the rag,
350	I slid down the polished lading plank and brought to the sea
351	my chest, and then I rowed through it with both my hands,
352	swimming, and very quickly I was out of the water, away from them.
353	There I went up to where there was a thicket in the flowering forest,
354	and lay crouching. And they, groaning loudly,
355	went to and fro; but since it seemed to them no better
356	to search further, they went back again
357	on board the hollow ship; and the gods themselves hid me
358	easily, and they brought me and set me at the farmstead
359	of a man of understanding; for it is still my lot to live.”
360	Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus:
361	“Ah, wretched stranger, truly you have stirred my heart greatly
362	by telling all these things, how much you have suffered and how far you have wandered.
363	But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me
364	with what you say of Odysseus. Why should a man like you
365	lie for no reason? I know well myself
366	of my master's homecoming, that he was hated by all the gods
367	utterly, since they did not bring him down among the Trojans
368	or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war.
369	Then all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him,
370	and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come.
371	But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report.
372	But I live apart, among the swine; nor do I go to the city,
373	unless perhaps prudent Penelope
374	urges me to come, when news comes from somewhere.
375	Then they sit beside the man and question him about each thing,
376	both those who grieve for their master, who has long been gone,
377	and those who rejoice as they eat his substance without payment;
378	but it is no pleasure to me to question and to ask,
379	ever since an Aetolian man beguiled me with a tale,
380	who had killed a man, and after wandering far over the earth
381	came to my house; and I welcomed him warmly.
382	He said he had seen him among the Cretans, with Idomeneus,
383	mending his ships, which the storm winds had shattered;
384	and he said he would come either by summer or by the late summer,
385	bringing many goods, with his godlike companions.
386	And you too, old man of many sorrows, since a god has brought you to me,
387	do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them;
388	for it is not for that reason that I shall respect you or make you welcome,
389	but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself.”
390	Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said:
391	“Truly the heart in your breast is very unbelieving,
392	seeing that not even with an oath have I won you over, nor do I persuade you.
393	But come now, let us make a compact; and hereafter
394	let the gods who hold Olympus be witnesses for us both.
395	If your master comes home to this house,
396	clothe me in a cloak and a tunic as clothing, and send me
397	to go to Dulichium, where my heart was glad to go;
398	but if your master does not come as I say,
399	set your slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock,
400	so that another beggar too may avoid deceiving.”
401	Answering him, the brilliant swineherd said:
402	“Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity
403	among men, both now and hereafter,
404	if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer,
405	I should then kill you and take away your dear life;
406	readily, after that, would I pray to Zeus, son of Cronus!
407	But now it is time for supper; may my companions be inside
408	very soon, so that we may make ready a pleasant supper in the hut.”
409	So they spoke such words to one another,
410	and the swine and the men, the swineherds, came near.
411	They penned the sows in their haunts to sleep,
412	and an untold clamor arose from the swine as they were stalled for the night;
413	but the brilliant swineherd called to his companions:
414	“Bring the best of the swine, so that I may sacrifice it for the stranger
415	from a far land; and we ourselves shall have profit of it too, who have endured misery
416	for long, suffering because of the white-tusked hogs,
417	while others eat up our labor without payment.”
418	So he spoke, and split wood with the pitiless bronze,
419	and they brought in a very fat boar, five years old.
420	They set it by the hearth; nor did the swineherd
421	forget the immortals; for his mind was good;
422	but he made the first offering, throwing into the fire hairs from the head
423	of the white-tusked boar, and he prayed to all the gods
424	that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house.
425	And raising his arm he struck it with a billet of oak, which he had kept back when splitting,
426	and its soul left it. And they cut its throat and singed it,
427	and quickly they jointed it; and the swineherd laid raw flesh,
428	taking the first offerings from all the limbs, in the rich fat,
429	and these he threw into the fire, sprinkling them with the meal of barley;
430	they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
431	and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off,
432	and they threw it on the platters all together; and the swineherd
433	stood up to carve; for in his mind he knew well what was due.
434	And cutting it, he divided it all into seven portions;
435	one he set aside for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia,
436	with a prayer, and the others he gave to each;
437	and he honored Odysseus with the long chine
438	of the white-tusked boar, and gladdened the heart of his master;
439	and Odysseus of many wiles spoke to him and said:
440	“Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus
441	as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am.”
442	Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus:
443	“Eat, strange guest, and take your delight in these things,
444	such as they are; and the god will give one thing and let another be,
445	whatever he wishes in his heart; for he can do all things.”
446	He spoke, and made an offering of the firstling pieces to the everlasting gods,
447	and when he had poured a libation of sparkling wine, to Odysseus, sacker of cities,
448	he put it into his hands; and he sat down by his own portion.
449	And Mesaulius served them bread, whom the swineherd
450	had acquired by himself alone, while his master was away,
451	without his mistress and old Laertes;
452	and he had bought him from the Taphians with his own possessions.
453	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
454	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
455	Mesaulius took away their bread, and they hastened to bed,
456	full of bread and meat.
457	And an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained
458	all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet.
459	And Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd,
460	whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him, or urge one of his companions
461	to do so, since he cared for him greatly:
462	“Hear me now, Eumaeus, and all you other companions;
463	I will speak a word with a wish; for the wine bids me,
464	the mad wine, which sets even a man of much wisdom to sing
465	and to laugh softly, and stirs him up to dance,
466	and brings out a word that is better left unsaid.
467	But since I have once spoken out, I will not hide it.
468	If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast,
469	as when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it.
470	Odysseus and Menelaus, son of Atreus, were the leaders,
471	and with them I was third in command; for they themselves bade me.
472	But when we came to the city and the steep wall,
473	As for us, around the city in the thick brushwood,
474	among the reeds and the marsh, crouching under our armor,
475	we lay. And an evil night came on, when the North Wind fell,
476	freezing; and from above came snow like hoarfrost,
477	cold, and ice grew thick on our shields.
478	Then all the others had cloaks and tunics,
479	and slept at ease, their shoulders wrapped in their shields;
480	but I had left my cloak with my companions when I went,
481	in my folly, since I did not think I would be cold even so,
482	but I followed with only my shield and my shining kilt.
483	But when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over,
484	then I spoke to Odysseus, who was near me,
485	nudging him with my elbow; and he was quick to listen:
486	‘Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
487	I shall no longer be among the living, but the cold
488	is bringing me down; for I have no cloak; a god beguiled me
489	into wearing only a tunic; and now there is no more escape.’
490	So I spoke, and then he had this thought in his heart,
491	such a man was he at planning and at fighting;
492	and speaking in a low voice he said to me:
493	‘Be silent now, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears you.’
494	He spoke, and propped his head on his elbow and said:
495	‘Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep.
496	For we have come very far from the ships; but would that someone
497	would go and tell Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people,
498	whether he might urge more men to come from the ships.’
499	So he spoke, and then Thoas, son of Andraemon, rose
500	quickly, and laid aside his crimson cloak,
501	and set off running to the ships; and in his clothing
502	I lay gladly, and Dawn of the golden throne shone forth.
503	If only I were young like that now, and my strength were steadfast;
504	then someone in the farmstead of the swineherds would give me a cloak,
505	for both reasons, out of friendship and respect for a good man;
506	but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body.”
507	Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus:
508	“Old man, the tale you have told is flawless,
509	and you have not yet spoken any word amiss or profitless;
510	so you shall not lack clothing, nor anything else
511	that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way,
512	for now; but at dawn you will knock about in your own rags.
513	For we have not many cloaks here, or tunics to change into
514	to put on, but only one for each man.
515	But when the dear son of Odysseus comes,
516	he himself will give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing,
517	and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you.”
518	So saying, he sprang up, and set for him near the fire
519	a bed, and threw on it the skins of sheep and goats.
520	There Odysseus lay down; and he threw over him a cloak,
521	thick and large, which he kept by him to change into,
522	to wear whenever some terrible storm arose.
523	So Odysseus slept there, and beside him
524	the young men slept; but the swineherd
525	did not like to sleep there, away from the boars,
526	but he went outside and made himself ready; and Odysseus rejoiced
527	that he cared so much for his substance while he was far away.
528	First he slung his sharp sword about his massive shoulders,
529	and put on a cloak that kept off the wind, very thick,
530	and took up the fleece of a large, well-fed goat,
531	and took a sharp javelin, a defense against dogs and men.
532	And he went to lie down where the white-tusked hogs
533	slept beneath a hollow rock, sheltered from the North Wind.
