# Homer, Odyssey, Book 2 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
2	the dear son of Odysseus rose from his bed,
3	put on his clothes, and about his shoulder set his sharp sword,
4	and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals,
5	and went out of his chamber, like a god to look upon.
6	At once he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
7	to summon the long-haired Achaeans to the assembly.
8	They made the summons, and the men gathered very quickly.
9	And when they had gathered and were all assembled,
10	he went to the assembly, and in his hand he held the bronze spear,
11	not alone: with him went two swift dogs.
12	And Athena poured a wondrous grace upon him,
13	and all the people gazed at him as he came;
14	and he sat in his father's seat, and the elders made way.
15	Among them then the hero Aegyptius began to speak,
16	who was bent with age and knew countless things.
17	For his dear son too had gone with godlike Odysseus
18	to Ilios of the fine horses in the hollow ships,
19	Antiphus the spearman; but the savage Cyclops killed him
20	in his hollow cave, and made ready of him the last of his suppers.
21	He had three others: one kept company with the suitors,
22	Eurynomus, and two always kept to their father's fields.
23	But even so he did not forget that one, mourning and grieving.
24	Shedding tears for him, he addressed them and spoke:
25	“Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say.
26	Never has there been an assembly of ours or a session
27	since brilliant Odysseus went away in the hollow ships.
28	Who has gathered us now like this? On whom has such need come,
29	on one of the young men, or of those who are older?
30	Has he heard some news of the army's coming,
31	which he might tell us plainly, since he learned it first?
32	Or is it some other public matter that he declares and speaks of?
33	He seems to me a good man, may he prosper! For him himself
34	may Zeus bring to pass some good, whatever he has in his mind.”
35	So he spoke, and the dear son of Odysseus rejoiced at the word of omen,
36	and he did not sit long, but was eager to speak,
37	and stood in the middle of the assembly; and the scepter was put in his hand
38	by the herald Peisenor, who knew wise counsels.
39	Then first, approaching the old man with words, he spoke to him:
40	“Old man, that man is not far off, and you will soon know it yourself,
41	who gathered the people: it was I; and sorrow comes most of all to me.
42	I have not heard any news of the army's coming,
43	which I might tell you plainly, since I learned it first,
44	nor is it some other public matter that I declare or speak of,
45	but a business of my own, since evil has fallen upon my house,
46	twofold: first, I have lost my brave father, who once among you
47	here was king, and was kind, like a father;
48	and now an evil far greater still, which will soon shatter my whole house
49	utterly, and destroy my substance altogether.
50	Suitors have fallen upon my mother against her will,
51	the dear sons of the men who are the best here,
52	who shudder at going to the house of her father,
53	Icarius, so that he himself might settle the bride-price for his daughter
54	and give her to whom he wished, the man who came pleasing to him;
55	but they haunt our house every day,
56	sacrificing oxen and sheep and fat goats,
57	and hold banquets and drink the sparkling wine,
58	wantonly; and much of it is wasted. For there is no man here
59	such as Odysseus was, to ward off disaster from the house.
60	And we are in no way such as to ward it off; truly, even then
61	we shall be wretched, and unskilled in courage.
62	Truly I would ward it off myself, if I had the strength.
63	For deeds no longer to be borne have been done, and no longer fairly
64	has my house been destroyed. Be indignant yourselves,
65	and feel shame before others, the men who dwell around,
66	who live about us; and fear the wrath of the gods,
67	lest, indignant at your evil deeds, they turn upon you.
68	I beg you by Olympian Zeus and by Themis,
69	who dissolves the assemblies of men and seats them:
70	hold back, my friends, and leave me alone with grim grief
71	to be worn down, unless perhaps my brave father Odysseus
72	in ill will did harm to the well-greaved Achaeans,
73	taking vengeance on me for these, you do me harm in ill will,
74	urging these men on. For me it would be better
75	if you yourselves ate up my treasures and my herds.
76	If you were the ones to eat them, there would soon perhaps be recompense;
77	for so long we would press you with words throughout the city,
78	asking back our goods, until all of it was given back;
79	but now you cast pains past remedy into my heart.”
80	So he spoke in anger, and flung the scepter to the ground,
81	bursting into tears; and pity seized all the people.
82	Then all the others were silent, and no one dared
83	to answer Telemachus with harsh words;
84	but Antinous alone, answering him, said:
85	“Telemachus, lofty in speech, uncontrollable in your fury, what a thing you have said
86	to shame us! You would like to fasten blame on us.
87	But it is not the Achaean suitors who are to blame toward you:
88	it is your dear mother, who knows crafty ways beyond all others.
89	For it is now the third year, and soon the fourth will pass,
90	since she has been cheating the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans.
91	She gives hope to all, and makes promises to each man,
92	sending out messages, but her mind has other things in view.
93	And she pondered in her mind this further trick:
94	she set up a great loom in the halls and began to weave,
95	fine and very wide; and at once she said to us:
96	‘Young men, my suitors, since brilliant Odysseus is dead,
97	wait, eager though you are for my marriage, until I have this mantle
98	finished, so that my threads may not be wasted and perish,
99	a shroud for the hero Laertes, against the time when
100	the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down,
101	lest one of the Achaean women in the land be indignant with me,
102	if he were to lie without a winding-sheet, who won great possessions.’
103	So she spoke, and our proud spirit was persuaded.
104	Then by day she would weave the great web,
105	but by night she would unravel it, when she had set torches beside her.
106	So for three years she escaped notice by her trick and persuaded the Achaeans;
107	but when the fourth year came and the seasons came round,
108	then indeed one of her women told us, who knew it clearly,
109	and we caught her unraveling the splendid web.
110	So she finished it, against her will, under compulsion.
111	But to you the suitors give this answer, so that you may know it
112	in your own heart, and all the Achaeans may know it:
113	send your mother away, and order her to marry
114	whichever man her father bids, and who pleases her.
115	But if she goes on vexing the sons of the Achaeans for a long time,
116	pondering in her heart what Athena has given her beyond all others,
117	to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits,
118	and crafty ways, such as we have never heard of in any woman, not even of old,
119	of those who were before, the Achaean women with lovely tresses,
120	Tyro and Alcmene and well-crowned Mycene:
121	none of them knew devices like Penelope's;
122	and yet this one, at least, she did not devise rightly.
123	For so long will they eat up your substance and your possessions,
124	as long as she keeps to this purpose, which now
125	the gods put in her breast. Great fame she makes
126	for herself, but for you a longing for much substance.
127	And we will not go to our fields or anywhere else
128	until she marries whichever of the Achaeans she wishes.”
129	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
130	“Antinous, there is no way for me to thrust out of the house against her will
131	the woman who bore me, who raised me; and my father is elsewhere on earth,
132	alive or dead. And it would be hard for me to pay back much
133	to Icarius, if of my own will I send my mother away.
134	For I shall suffer evil from her father, and a god will give
135	me other evils, since my mother will call down the hateful Erinyes
136	as she leaves the house; and blame will come upon me from men.
137	So I will never speak that word.
138	And if your own hearts are indignant at this,
139	leave my halls, and provide other feasts,
140	eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house.
141	But if this seems to you better and more profitable,
142	that one man's substance should be destroyed without payment,
143	then eat it up; but I will call upon the gods who live forever,
144	if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done;
145	then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house.”
146	So Telemachus spoke, and for him far-seeing Zeus sent two eagles
147	flying from on high, from the peak of a mountain.
148	For a while they flew along in the blasts of the wind,
149	close beside one another, with wings at full stretch;
150	but when they came over the middle of the assembly of many voices,
151	there they wheeled about and shook their thick wings,
152	and looked down at the heads of all, and foresaw destruction;
153	and tearing each other's cheeks and necks all round with their talons,
154	they darted on the right across the houses and the city of the people.
155	And they were amazed at the birds, when they saw them with their eyes,
156	and pondered in their hearts what was to be fulfilled.
157	And among them spoke also the old hero Halitherses,
158	son of Mastor; for he alone surpassed those of his age
159	in knowing birds and in speaking words of fate.
160	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
161	“Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say;
162	and it is above all to the suitors that I declare these things.
163	For a great bane is rolling upon them: Odysseus will not
164	be long away from his dear ones, but is already somewhere
165	near, and plants slaughter and doom for these men,
166	for all of them; and for many others too there will be evil,
167	of us who live in clear-seen Ithaca. But long before that
168	let us consider how we may stop them; and let them stop
169	of themselves; for this is at once the better thing for them.
170	For I do not prophesy untried, but with sure knowledge;
171	for to him too, I say, all has been fulfilled
172	as I told him, when they were embarking for Ilios,
173	the Argives, and among them went Odysseus of many wiles.
174	I said that after suffering many evils, and losing all his companions,
175	unknown to all, in the twentieth year,
176	he would come home; and now it is all being fulfilled.”
177	Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face:
178	“Old man, come now, prophesy to your own children,
179	going home, lest they suffer some evil hereafter;
180	but in these things I am far better than you at prophesying.
181	Many are the birds that go to and fro under the rays of the sun,
182	and not all of them are birds of fate. But Odysseus
183	has perished far away, as you too ought to have perished
184	along with him. Then you would not prophesy and declare so much,
185	nor would you set Telemachus on like this in his anger,
186	waiting for a gift for your own house, in case he may give one.
187	But I will speak out to you, and it will surely come to pass:
188	if you, who know many things of old, urge a younger man,
189	beguiling him with your words, to grow angry,
190	first it will be the more vexing for him himself,
191	and he will be able to achieve nothing even so, because of these men;
192	and on you, old man, we will lay a fine, which you will
193	chafe at heart to pay, and grievous will be your sorrow.
194	And to Telemachus I myself will give counsel before them all:
195	let him bid his mother go back to her father's house;
196	and they will arrange a wedding and make ready the bride-price,
197	a very great one, as much as is fitting to go with a dear daughter.
198	For before that, I think, the sons of the Achaeans will not cease
199	from their grievous wooing, since in any case we fear no one,
200	not even Telemachus, for all his many words,
201	nor do we heed the oracle that you, old sir,
202	pronounce, never to be fulfilled; and you make yourself hateful all the more.
203	And his goods will be wretchedly eaten up, and there will never
204	be amends, as long as she keeps the Achaeans waiting
205	for her marriage; and we, waiting every day,
206	strive for the sake of her excellence, and do not go after other women,
207	whom it is fitting for each of us to marry.”
208	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
209	“Eurymachus, and you others, all the noble suitors,
210	I will no longer beg you or speak to you about these things;
211	for the gods know them already, and all the Achaeans.
212	But come, give me a swift ship and twenty companions,
213	who will accomplish the way for me there and back.
214	For I will go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
215	to learn of the homecoming of my father, who has long been gone,
216	in the hope that some mortal may tell me, or I may hear a rumor
217	from Zeus, which most of all brings report to men.
218	If I hear of my father's life and homecoming,
219	then, worn down though I am, I could endure for another year;
220	but if I hear that he is dead and no longer living,
221	then I will come home to my dear native land,
222	and heap up a mound for him, and give him funeral rites,
223	very many, as many as are fitting, and give my mother to a husband.”
224	He spoke thus and sat down, and among them stood up
225	Mentor, who was the companion of noble Odysseus,
226	and to whom, when he went in the ships, he had entrusted all his house,
227	that it should obey the old man, and that he should keep all things safe.
228	With good intent he addressed them and spoke:
229	“Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say.
230	Never again let any man with a ready heart be gentle and kind,
231	a scepter-bearing king, nor know in his mind what is due,
232	but let him always be harsh and do evil;
233	since no one remembers divine Odysseus
234	among the people he was lord over, and he was kind, like a father.
235	As for the proud suitors, I do not begrudge them at all
236	their deeds of violence in the mischief of their minds;
237	for they stake their own heads when they violently devour
238	the house of Odysseus, and say that he will come home no more.
239	But it is with the rest of the people that I am indignant, that you all
240	sit silent, and do not approach them with words
241	and stop the suitors, few as they are, though you are many.”
242	Then Leiocritus, son of Euenor, answered him face to face:
243	“Harsh Mentor, crazed in your mind, what a thing you have said,
244	urging them to stop us! It is a hard thing
245	to fight over a feast with men who are more numerous, at that.
246	For even if Odysseus of Ithaca himself were to come
247	and find the noble suitors feasting in his house,
248	and be eager in his heart to drive them out of the hall,
249	his wife, much though she longs for him, would have no joy
250	of his coming, but he would meet a shameful doom right here,
251	if he fought with men who outnumbered him; what you have said is not fair and right.
252	But come, people, scatter, each to his work,
253	and Mentor and Halitherses will speed this one on his way,
254	who are his father's companions from of old.
255	But I think he will sit here a long time, and hear
256	news in Ithaca, and never accomplish this journey.”
257	So he spoke, and quickly dissolved the assembly.
258	And they scattered, each to his own house,
259	and the suitors went to the house of divine Odysseus.
260	But Telemachus went apart along the shore of the sea,
261	and washed his hands in the gray sea, and prayed to Athena:
262	“Hear me, you god who came yesterday to our house
263	and bade me go in a ship over the misty sea
264	to learn of the homecoming of my father, who has long been gone:
265	all this the Achaeans are delaying,
266	and most of all the suitors, wickedly overweening.”
267	So he spoke in prayer, and Athena came near to him
268	in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice,
269	and she spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
270	“Telemachus, hereafter you will be no coward and no fool,
271	if indeed your father's noble might has been instilled in you,
272	such a man as he was to accomplish both deed and word:
273	then your journey will not be in vain or unfulfilled.
274	But if you are not his offspring and Penelope's,
275	then I do not expect you to accomplish what you have in mind.
276	Few sons are like their father;
277	most are worse, and few are better than their father.
278	But since hereafter you will be no coward and no fool,
279	and the counsel of Odysseus has not wholly failed you,
280	then there is hope that you will accomplish these things.
281	So now let be the plan and the thought of the suitors,
282	senseless men, since they are in no way discerning or just;
283	nor do they know anything of death and black doom,
284	which is near them now, that they should all perish in a single day.
285	But the journey you have in mind will not be far off much longer;
286	such a companion of your father's am I to you
287	that I will fit out a swift ship for you and go with you myself.
288	But you go to the house and keep company with the suitors,
289	and make ready provisions and fit them all into vessels,
290	wine in two-handled jars, and barley meal, the marrow of men,
291	in thick skins; and I will go through the land and quickly
292	gather companions who will go willingly. There are many ships
293	in sea-girt Ithaca, new and old;
294	of these I will pick out for you the best there is,
295	and we will quickly make her ready and launch her on the wide sea.”
296	So spoke Athena, daughter of Zeus; nor did
297	Telemachus stay there long, once he had heard the voice of the goddess.
298	He went to the house, grieving at heart,
299	and found the proud suitors in the halls,
300	flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard.
301	And Antinous, with a laugh, came straight up to Telemachus,
302	and he clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
303	“Telemachus, lofty in speech, uncontrollable in your fury, let no other
304	evil be a care in your breast, either deed or word,
305	but eat and drink with me, just as before.
306	And all these things the Achaeans will surely bring about for you,
307	a ship and choice oarsmen, so that you may come the sooner
308	to sacred Pylos, in search of news of your noble father.”
309	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
310	“Antinous, there is no way for me among you overbearing men
311	to feast in silence and make merry at my ease.
312	Is it not enough that in the past you ate up many fine
313	possessions of mine, you suitors, while I was still a child?
314	But now that I am grown, and hearing the words of others
315	I learn, and the spirit within me grows,
316	I will try to let loose the evil fates upon you,
317	whether I go to Pylos or stay here in this land.
318	I will go, and the journey I speak of will not be in vain,
319	a passenger; for I have no ship of my own, nor oarsmen:
320	that, I suppose, is what seemed to you the better way.”
321	He spoke, and drew his hand from the hand of Antinous
322	easily; and the suitors were making ready a feast throughout the house.
323	And they insulted him and taunted him with words,
324	and this is what one of the overweening young men would say:
325	“Surely Telemachus is pondering slaughter for us.
326	Either he will bring some helpers from sandy Pylos,
327	or even from Sparta, since he is so terribly eager;
328	or he means to go to Ephyra too, that rich plowland,
329	to bring back from there drugs that destroy life,
330	and put them in the mixing bowl, and destroy us all.”
331	And another of the overweening young men would say in turn:
332	“Who knows if he himself too, going on the hollow ship,
333	may perish far from his friends, wandering like Odysseus?
334	So he would increase our toil even more;
335	for we would divide all his possessions, and the house
336	we would give to his mother to keep, and to whoever married her.”
337	So they spoke; but he went down into the high-roofed chamber of his father,
338	a wide one, where gold and bronze lay heaped,
339	and clothing in chests, and fragrant oil in plenty;
340	and in it jars of old wine, sweet to drink,
341	stood holding within them the unmixed, divine drink,
342	fitted in order against the wall, in case Odysseus ever
343	came home, even after toiling through many sorrows.
344	And closely fitted doors were set on it that could be locked,
345	double doors; and a woman was in it as housekeeper night and day,
346	who guarded everything with the great knowledge of her mind,
347	Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor.
348	Telemachus called her to the chamber then and said to her:
349	“Nurse, come, draw me off wine in two-handled jars,
350	sweet wine, the sweetest after the one you are keeping,
351	thinking of that ill-fated man, if from somewhere he might come,
352	Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, escaping death and the fates.
353	Fill twelve, and fit them all with lids;
354	and pour me barley meal into well-sewn leather bags:
355	let there be twenty measures of the meal of mill-ground barley.
356	You alone must know of it; and let it all be got ready together.
357	For I will take it in the evening, when
358	my mother has gone up to her upper room and is thinking of bed.
359	For I will go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
360	to learn of my dear father's homecoming, if I may hear of it somewhere.”
361	So he spoke, and the dear nurse Eurycleia wailed aloud,
362	and lamenting addressed him with winged words:
363	“Why, dear child, has this thought come
364	into your mind? Where do you mean to go, far over the earth,
365	you, an only and beloved son? He has perished far from his native land,
366	Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, among a strange people.
367	And these men, as soon as you are gone, will devise evil for you hereafter,
368	so that you may perish by guile, and they will divide all this among themselves.
369	No, stay here, sitting over what is yours; you have no need
370	to suffer evils on the barren sea or to wander.”
371	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
372	“Take heart, nurse, since this plan is not without a god.
373	But swear to tell none of this to my dear mother
374	until the eleventh or the twelfth day has come,
375	or she herself misses me and hears that I have gone,
376	so that she may not mar her fair skin with weeping.”
377	So he spoke, and the old woman swore a great oath by the gods not to tell.
378	But when she had sworn and completed the oath,
379	then at once she drew off wine for him in two-handled jars,
380	and poured barley meal for him into well-sewn leather bags.
381	And Telemachus went into the house and kept company with the suitors.
382	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing.
383	In the likeness of Telemachus she went everywhere through the city,
384	and standing beside each man she spoke her word,
385	and bade them gather in the evening at the swift ship.
386	And she asked Noemon, the shining son of Phronius,
387	for a swift ship; and he gladly promised it to her.
388	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
389	and then she dragged the swift ship down to the sea, and put in her
390	all the tackle that well-benched ships carry.
391	She moored her at the far edge of the harbor, and around her the brave companions
392	gathered all together; and the goddess urged on each man.
393	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing.
394	She went to the house of divine Odysseus,
395	and there she poured sweet sleep upon the suitors,
396	and confused them as they drank, and made the cups fall from their hands.
397	And they rose to go to sleep through the city, and did not
398	sit there long, since sleep was falling on their eyelids.
399	But gray-eyed Athena spoke to Telemachus,
400	calling him out of the well-appointed halls,
401	in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice:
402	“Telemachus, already your well-greaved companions
403	sit at the oars, waiting for you to set out;
404	so let us go, and not delay the journey long.”
405	So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way
406	swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess.
407	And when they had come down to the ship and the sea,
408	they found on the shore their long-haired companions.
409	And among them spoke also the sacred strength of Telemachus:
410	“Come here, friends, let us bring the provisions; for they are all
411	together in the hall now. My mother has learned nothing of it,
412	nor have the other slave women; only one has heard the word.”
413	So he spoke, and led the way, and they followed with him.
414	And they brought everything to the well-benched ship
415	and stowed it, as the dear son of Odysseus bade them.
416	And Telemachus went on board the ship, and Athena went before him
417	and sat down in the stern of the ship; and close beside her
418	sat Telemachus. And they loosed the stern-cables,
419	and went on board themselves, and sat down at the rowing benches.
420	And gray-eyed Athena sent them a following wind,
421	a fresh West Wind, sounding over the wine-dark sea.
422	And Telemachus urged on his companions and bade them
423	lay hold of the tackle; and they heeded his urging.
424	The mast of fir, within the hollow mast-box,
425	they raised and set up, and made it fast with forestays,
426	and hoisted the white sails with well-twisted ox-hide ropes.
427	And the wind blew full into the sail, and around the cutwater
428	the surging wave roared loud as the ship went on,
429	and she ran over the waves, accomplishing her way.
430	And when they had made fast the tackle throughout the swift black ship,
431	they set up mixing bowls brim-full of wine,
432	and poured libations to the immortal, everlasting gods,
433	and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus.
434	All night long and through the dawn the ship cut her way.
