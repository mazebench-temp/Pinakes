# Homer, Odyssey, Book 3 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	And the sun sprang up, leaving the beautiful water,
2	into the brazen sky, to give light to the immortals
3	and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth;
4	and they came to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus.
5	There on the shore of the sea the people were offering sacrifice,
6	bulls all black, to the dark-haired earth-shaker.
7	There were nine companies, and in each five hundred men
8	were seated, and in each company they held nine bulls before them.
9	When they had tasted the entrails and were burning the thigh-pieces on the altar for the god,
10	the others put straight in to land, and the sails of the balanced ship
11	they hauled up and furled, and anchored her, and stepped out themselves.
12	And Telemachus stepped out of the ship, and Athena went before him.
13	The gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him first:
14	“Telemachus, you have no more need of shame, not even a little;
15	for this is why you have sailed over the sea, to learn
16	of your father, where the earth hid him and what fate he has met.
17	But come now, go straight to Nestor, tamer of horses;
18	let us learn what counsel he keeps hidden in his breast.
19	And you yourself must beg him to tell you truly;
20	he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise.”
21	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
22	“Mentor, how then shall I go? How shall I greet him?
23	I am not yet practiced at all in shrewd words,
24	and there is shame for a young man in questioning an older one.”
25	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
26	“Telemachus, some things you will think of yourself in your own mind,
27	and in others a god too will give you counsel; for I do not think
28	that you were born and bred against the will of the gods.”
29	So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way
30	swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess.
31	And they came to the gathering and the seats of the Pylian men,
32	where Nestor sat with his sons, and around them his companions,
33	making ready the feast, were roasting meat and threading more on spits.
34	And when they saw the strangers, they all came together,
35	and welcomed them with their hands, and bade them sit down.
36	First Peisistratus, son of Nestor, came close
37	and took them both by the hand and seated them at the feast
38	on soft fleeces on the sands of the sea,
39	beside his brother Thrasymedes and his father;
40	and he gave them portions of the entrails, and poured wine
41	into a golden cup, and pledging her he spoke
42	to Pallas Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus:
43	“Pray now, stranger, to lord Poseidon;
44	for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here.
45	And when you have poured libation and prayed, as is the custom,
46	then give this man too the cup of honey-sweet wine
47	to pour libation, since I think that to the immortals he as well
48	prays; and all men have need of the gods.
49	But he is younger, of an age with me myself;
50	so I will give the golden goblet to you first.”
51	So saying, he put the cup of sweet wine in her hand;
52	and Athena rejoiced in the wise and just man,
53	because he gave the golden goblet to her first;
54	and at once she prayed earnestly to lord Poseidon:
55	“Hear me, Poseidon, earth-holder, and do not begrudge
56	us, as we pray, the accomplishing of these things.
57	To Nestor first of all and to his sons grant glory,
58	and then to the others give a gracious return,
59	to all the Pylians, for this famous hecatomb.
60	And grant also that Telemachus and I may return when we have done the business
61	for which we came here in our swift black ship.”
62	So she prayed, and she herself was bringing it all to pass.
63	And she gave Telemachus the fine two-handled cup,
64	and in the same way the dear son of Odysseus prayed.
65	And when they had roasted the outer flesh and drawn it off,
66	they divided the portions and feasted on a glorious feast.
67	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
68	the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them:
69	“Now it is more fitting to question and to ask
70	the strangers who they are, now that they have taken their fill of food.
71	Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the watery ways?
72	Is it on some business, or do you wander at random
73	over the sea, like pirates, who wander
74	staking their lives and bringing evil to foreigners?”
75	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face,
76	taking heart; for Athena herself had put daring in his mind,
77	so that he might ask him about his absent father,
78	and so that noble fame may be his among men:
79	“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
80	you ask where we are from; and I will recount it to you.
81	We have come from Ithaca under Neion;
82	and this business I speak of is private, not public.
83	I am in quest of the wide report of my father, if I may hear of it somewhere,
84	of brilliant Odysseus the stout-hearted, who once, they say,
85	fighting at your side sacked the city of the Trojans.
86	For of all the others, as many as fought against the Trojans,
87	we have heard where each one perished by a grim destruction;
88	but of that man the son of Cronus has made even the destruction unknown.
89	For no one can tell plainly where he has perished,
90	whether he was brought down on the mainland by hostile men
91	or on the open sea among the waves of Amphitrite.
92	That is why I come now to your knees, in the hope that you may be willing
93	to tell me of his grim destruction, if perhaps you saw it
94	with your own eyes, or heard word of him from someone else
95	as he wandered; for beyond all men his mother bore him to misery.
96	And do not soften anything out of respect for me, or out of pity,
97	but tell me in full how you came upon the sight of him.
98	I beg you, if ever my brave father Odysseus
99	promised you any word or deed and fulfilled it
100	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered woes,
101	remember these things now for me, and tell me truly.”
102	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
103	“My friend, since you have reminded me of the misery that we endured
104	in that land, we sons of the Achaeans, uncontrollable in our might,
105	both all that we bore with our ships over the misty sea,
106	roaming after plunder wherever Achilles led,
107	and all the battles around the great city of lord Priam
108	that we fought; and there all our best were killed.
109	There lies warlike Ajax, there Achilles,
110	there Patroclus, a counselor equal to the gods,
111	and there my own dear son, at once strong and noble,
112	Antilochus, surpassingly swift at running, and a fighter;
113	and many other evils we suffered besides; who could ever
114	tell them all, of mortal men?
115	Not even if you stayed five years, or six,
116	and asked about all the evils the brilliant Achaeans suffered there:
117	you would grow weary first and go home to your native land.
118	For nine years we stitched evils together for them, busy all around them
119	with tricks of every kind, and the son of Cronus barely brought it to an end.
120	There no man ever wished to match himself with him in counsel to his face,
121	since brilliant Odysseus far surpassed them
122	in tricks of every kind, your father, if truly
123	you are his offspring; awe holds me as I look at you.
124	For truly your words are like his, and you would not say
125	that a younger man would speak so like him.
126	There, all that time, I and brilliant Odysseus
127	never spoke at odds, in the assembly or in the council,
128	but with one heart, with mind and thoughtful counsel,
129	we considered for the Argives how all might turn out best by far.
130	But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam
131	and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans,
132	then indeed Zeus devised in his mind a grim homecoming
133	for the Argives, since in no way discerning or just
134	were they all; so many of them met an evil fate
135	through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father,
136	who set strife between the two sons of Atreus.
137	And the two of them called all the Achaeans to assembly,
138	idly and against all order, at the setting of the sun,
139	and the sons of the Achaeans came heavy with wine,
140	and the two told them the word for which they had gathered the army.
141	Then Menelaus bade all the Achaeans
142	to think of their homecoming over the broad back of the sea,
143	but it did not please Agamemnon at all; for he wished
144	to hold back the army and offer holy hecatombs,
145	so that he might heal the terrible anger of Athena,
146	the fool, and he did not know that she would not be persuaded;
147	for the mind of the gods who live forever is not quickly turned.
148	So the two of them, answering each other with harsh words,
149	stood there; and the well-greaved Achaeans sprang up
150	with a wondrous din, and they were divided in their counsel.
151	We spent the night pondering harsh things in our minds
152	against one another; for Zeus was preparing a bane of evil for us;
153	and at dawn some of us dragged our ships down into the shining sea
154	and put our possessions on board, and the deep-girdled women.
155	But half of the army halted and stayed
156	there beside Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people;
157	and half of us embarked and rowed away; and very swiftly
158	the ships sailed, and a god made smooth the vast-gulfed sea.
159	And when we came to Tenedos we offered sacrifice to the gods,
160	eager to go home; but Zeus was not yet devising our homecoming,
161	the hard god, who roused evil strife against us once more, a second time.
162	Some turned back and went off in their curving ships
163	with lord Odysseus, wise-hearted, of subtle wiles,
164	to do a kindness once more to Agamemnon, son of Atreus;
165	but I, with all the ships that followed me in a body,
166	fled, since I knew that a god was devising evil.
167	And the warlike son of Tydeus fled, and roused his companions.
168	And late, after us two, came fair-haired Menelaus,
169	and he overtook us in Lesbos as we pondered the long voyage,
170	whether we should go home above rocky Chios,
171	by the island of Psyria, keeping it on our left,
172	or below Chios, past windy Mimas.
173	And we asked the god to show us a sign; and he
174	showed us one, and bade us cut across the middle of the open sea to Euboea,
175	so that we might escape from evil as quickly as we could.
176	And a shrill fair wind rose to blow; and very swiftly
177	the ships ran over the fish-filled ways, and at Geraestus
178	they put in by night; and for Poseidon, of bulls,
179	we laid many thigh-pieces on the altar, having measured the great open sea.
180	It was the fourth day when in Argos the balanced ships
181	of the companions of Diomedes, son of Tydeus, tamer of horses,
182	were moored; but I held on for Pylos, and the fair wind never
183	died down, once the god had first sent it forth to blow.
184	So I came, dear child, without tidings, and I know nothing
185	of those, which of the Achaeans were saved and which perished.
186	But all that I have heard as I sit in our halls
187	you shall learn, as is the custom, and I will not hide it from you.
188	They say that the Myrmidons, fighters with the spear, came home safely,
189	whom the shining son of great-hearted Achilles led,
190	and safely Philoctetes, the splendid son of Poias.
191	And Idomeneus brought all his companions back to Crete,
192	all who had escaped from the war, and the sea took none of them from him.
193	Of the son of Atreus you yourselves have heard, far off though you are,
194	how he came, and how Aegisthus devised a grim destruction for him.
195	But truly that man paid for it miserably;
196	how good a thing it is for a son to be left behind by a man
197	who has perished, since that son too took vengeance on his father's slayer,
198	scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father.
199	You too, my friend—for I see that you are very handsome and tall—
200	be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you.”
201	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
202	“Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Achaeans,
203	yes, truly that son took vengeance, and the Achaeans
204	will carry his fame wide, even for men to come to hear of.
205	If only the gods would clothe me with such strength,
206	to take vengeance on the suitors for their grievous transgression,
207	who treat me with outrage and devise reckless deeds.
208	But the gods have spun no such prosperity for me,
209	for my father and for me; and now I must endure it all the same.”
210	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
211	“My friend, since you have reminded me of this and spoken of it,
212	they say that many suitors, for your mother's sake,
213	devise evils in your halls against your will;
214	tell me, do you submit willingly, or do the people
215	throughout the land hate you, following the voice of a god?
216	Who knows if he will come someday and take vengeance on them for their violence,
217	either he alone or all the Achaeans together?
218	If only gray-eyed Athena were willing to love you
219	as she cared then for glorious Odysseus
220	in the land of the Trojans, where we Achaeans suffered sorrows—
221	for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly
222	as Pallas Athena openly stood beside him—
223	if she were willing to love you so and care for you in her heart,
224	then one or another of those men would forget all about marriage.”
225	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
226	“Old man, I do not think this word will ever be fulfilled;
227	for you have said too great a thing; amazement holds me. Not for me,
228	for all my hoping, would these things happen, not even if the gods so willed.”
229	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
230	“Telemachus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
231	A god, if he wished, could easily save a man even from far away.
232	And I for my part would rather, even after toiling through many sorrows,
233	come home and see the day of my homecoming,
234	than come home and perish at my own hearth, as Agamemnon
235	perished by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife.
236	But death, which is common to all, not even the gods
237	can ward off from a man they love, whenever
238	the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down.”
239	And wise Telemachus answered her face to face:
240	“Mentor, let us no longer talk like this, grieved though we are;
241	for him there is no longer any true homecoming, but already
242	the immortals have devised for him death and black doom.
243	But now I wish to question and to ask another thing
244	of Nestor, since beyond all others he knows judgments and wisdom;
245	for they say he has been king for three generations of men,
246	and he appears to me like an immortal to look upon.
247	Nestor, son of Neleus, tell me the truth:
248	how did the son of Atreus die, wide-ruling Agamemnon?
249	Where was Menelaus? What destruction did he devise for him,
250	scheming Aegisthus, since he killed a far better man?
251	Was he not in Achaean Argos, but somewhere else
252	wandering among men, so that the other took heart and killed him?”
253	Then the Gerenian horseman Nestor answered him:
254	“Well then, my child, I will tell you all the truth.
255	You yourself can surely guess how it would have turned out,
256	if he had found Aegisthus alive in the halls,
257	the son of Atreus, coming from Troy, fair-haired Menelaus:
258	then they would not have heaped up the loose earth over him even in death,
259	but the dogs and the birds would have devoured him
260	as he lay on the plain far from the city, and no one
261	of the Achaean women would have wept for him; for very great was the deed he devised.
262	For we were there, toiling through many trials,
263	and sat there; but he, undisturbed, in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos,
264	kept bewitching the wife of Agamemnon with many words.
265	And at first she refused the shameful deed,
266	brilliant Clytemnestra; for her mind was good;
267	and there was also a singer beside her, and many were the charges
268	the son of Atreus gave him, as he left for Troy, to guard his wife.
269	But when the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome,
270	then he took the singer away to a desert island
271	and left him there to become the prey and spoil of birds,
272	and, willing, he led her, willing, to his own house.
273	And he burned many thigh-pieces on the holy altars of the gods,
274	and hung up many offerings, woven things and gold,
275	having accomplished a great deed, which he had never expected in his heart.
276	Now we were sailing together on our way from Troy,
277	the son of Atreus and I, with friendly hearts toward one another;
278	but when we came to holy Sunium, the cape of Athens,
279	there Phoebus Apollo came upon Menelaus' helmsman
280	with his gentle arrows and killed him,
281	as he held the steering oar of the speeding ship in his hands,
282	Phrontis, son of Onetor, who surpassed the tribes of men
283	in steering a ship whenever storms rushed on.
284	So he was held back there, eager though he was for his journey,
285	to bury his companion and give him funeral rites.
286	But when he too, going over the wine-dark sea
287	in the hollow ships, reached in his running the steep mountain of Malea,
288	then far-seeing Zeus devised a hateful journey for him
289	and poured on him the breath of shrill winds,
290	and the waves swelled huge, like mountains.
291	There he split the fleet, and some he drove to Crete,
292	where the Cydonians lived about the streams of the Iardanus.
293	There is a smooth rock, steep to the sea,
294	at the far edge of Gortyn, in the misty sea;
295	there the South Wind drives the great wave against the left headland,
296	toward Phaestus, and a small rock holds back the great wave.
297	These came there, and with difficulty escaped destruction,
298	the men did, but the ships were smashed against the reefs
299	by the waves; but the five dark-prowed ships
300	the wind and the water carried and drove to Egypt.
301	So he, gathering much substance and gold there,
302	wandered with his ships among men of another tongue;
303	and meanwhile Aegisthus devised these grim things at home,
304	killing the son of Atreus, and the people were made subject to him;
305	and for seven years he ruled over Mycenae rich in gold;
306	but in the eighth brilliant Orestes came, an evil for him,
307	back from Athens, and killed his father's slayer,
308	scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father.
309	And when he had killed him he gave the Argives a funeral feast
310	for his hateful mother and for the coward Aegisthus;
311	and on the same day Menelaus, good at the war cry, came to him,
312	bringing many possessions, as much as his ships could carry as their load.
313	And you too, my friend, do not wander long far from your home,
314	leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house
315	so overbearing, lest they devour everything you have,
316	dividing your possessions, and you have gone on a useless journey.
317	But to Menelaus I urge and bid you
318	to go; for he has lately come home from abroad,
319	from men from whom no one would expect in his heart
320	to come home, once the storms have driven him astray
321	into a sea so great, from which not even the birds
322	come back within the year, since it is so great and terrible.
323	But go now with your ship and your companions;
324	or if you wish to go by land, here are a chariot and horses for you,
325	and here are my sons, who will be your escorts
326	to shining Lacedaemon, where fair-haired Menelaus is.
327	And you yourself must beg him to tell you truly;
328	he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise.”
329	So he spoke, and the sun went down and darkness came on.
330	And among them spoke also the gray-eyed goddess Athena:
331	“Old man, truly you have told all this fair and right;
332	but come, cut out the tongues and mix the wine,
333	so that to Poseidon and to the other immortals
334	we may pour libations and then think of bed; for it is the hour.
335	For already the light has gone down under the darkness, and it is not fitting
336	to sit long at the feast of the gods, but to go home.”
337	So spoke the daughter of Zeus, and they listened to her words.
338	And the heralds poured water over their hands,
339	and the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink,
340	and served it out to all, first pouring drops for the libation into the cups.
341	And they threw the tongues into the fire, and standing up poured libations over them.
342	But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired,
343	then Athena and godlike Telemachus
344	were both eager to go back to the hollow ship.
345	But Nestor held them back, and approached them with words:
346	“May Zeus ward this off, and the other immortal gods,
347	that you should go from my house to your swift ship
348	as from a man altogether without clothing, and poor,
349	who has no cloaks and blankets in plenty in his house
350	for himself or his guests to sleep softly in.
351	But I have cloaks and fine blankets.
352	Surely the dear son of this man Odysseus will not
353	lie down on the deck of a ship while I
354	am alive, and after me my sons are left in the halls
355	to entertain the guests, whoever comes to my house.”
356	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn:
357	“You have spoken well in this, dear old man; and it is fitting
358	that Telemachus should obey you, since it is much better so.
359	So he shall go with you now, to sleep
360	in your halls; but I will go to the black ship
361	to encourage the companions and tell them each thing.
362	For I claim to be the only older man among them;
363	the others are younger men who follow out of friendship,
364	all of an age with great-hearted Telemachus.
365	There I will lie down by the hollow black ship
366	tonight; but at dawn I will go to the great-hearted Caucones,
367	where a debt is owed me, not a recent one at all
368	nor a small one. And this man, since he has come to your house,
369	send on with a chariot and your son; and give him horses,
370	the nimblest of yours at running and the best in strength.”
371	So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away
372	in the likeness of a sea eagle; and amazement seized all who saw it.
373	And the old man marveled, when he saw it with his eyes;
374	and he took Telemachus by the hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
375	“My friend, I do not think you will be a coward and a weakling,
376	if, young as you are, the gods go with you as your escorts.
377	For this was no other of those who have their homes on Olympus,
378	but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia,
379	who also honored your brave father among the Argives.
380	But, queen, be gracious, and grant me noble fame,
381	to me and my children and my revered wife;
382	and I in turn will sacrifice to you a yearling heifer, broad of brow,
383	unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke;
384	I will sacrifice her to you, with gold poured around her horns.”
385	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
386	And the Gerenian horseman Nestor led the way for them,
387	his sons and his sons-in-law, to his beautiful house.
388	And when they came to the famous house of that lord,
389	they sat down in order on the chairs and the seats,
390	and for them, when they had come, the old man mixed a bowl
391	of wine sweet to drink, which in its eleventh year
392	the housekeeper opened, and loosed its cover;
393	of this the old man mixed a bowl, and earnestly to Athena
394	he prayed as he poured the libation, to the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus.
395	But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired,
396	they went to lie down, each to his own house,
397	but the Gerenian horseman Nestor had him sleep there,
398	Telemachus, the dear son of divine Odysseus,
399	on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade,
400	and beside him Peisistratus of the good ash spear, leader of men,
401	who of his sons was still a young unmarried man in his halls;
402	and he himself slept in the inner part of the high house,
403	and the lady his wife shared his bed and couch.
404	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
405	the Gerenian horseman Nestor rose from his bed,
406	and went out and sat down on the polished stones
407	which stood before his high doors,
408	white, shining with oil; on them in former days
409	Neleus used to sit, a counselor equal to the gods;
410	but he had already been overcome by his doom and gone to the house of Hades,
411	and now Nestor in his turn sat on them, the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans,
412	holding the scepter. And around him his sons gathered in a body,
413	coming from their chambers, Echephron and Stratius
414	and Perseus and Aretus and godlike Thrasymedes,
415	and then the hero Peisistratus came to them as the sixth,
416	and they brought godlike Telemachus and seated him beside them.
417	And among them the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak:
418	“Quickly, dear children, fulfill my wish,
419	so that first of all the gods I may propitiate Athena,
420	who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god.
421	But come, let one go to the plain for a heifer, so that she may come
422	as soon as may be, and the herdsman of the cattle drive her here;
423	and let one go to the black ship of great-hearted Telemachus
424	and bring all his companions, and leave two only;
425	and let one in turn call the goldsmith Laerces to come
426	here, so that he may pour gold around the heifer's horns.
427	And you others stay here together, and tell the slave women
428	inside to prepare a feast throughout the famous house,
429	and to bring seats and wood all about, and bright water.”
430	So he spoke, and they all bustled about. The heifer came
431	from the plain, and from the swift balanced ship came
432	the companions of great-hearted Telemachus, and the smith came
433	holding in his hands his smith's tools, the instruments of his craft,
434	anvil and hammer and well-made tongs,
435	with which he worked the gold; and Athena came
436	to share in the sacrifice. And the old horse-driving Nestor
437	gave the gold; and the smith then poured it around the heifer's horns,
438	working it with skill, so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering.
439	And Stratius and brilliant Echephron led the heifer by the horns.
440	And Aretus brought them water for the hands in a flowered basin,
441	coming from the chamber, and in his other hand he held the barley grains
442	in a basket; and Thrasymedes, steadfast in battle,
443	stood by, holding a sharp axe in his hand, to strike the heifer down.
444	And Perseus held the bowl for the blood; and the old horse-driving Nestor
445	began the rite with the water for the hands and the barley grains, and earnestly to Athena
446	he prayed as he made the first offering, throwing hairs from the victim's head into the fire.
447	And when they had prayed and scattered the barley grains,
448	at once the son of Nestor, high-hearted Thrasymedes,
449	standing close, struck; and the axe cut through the sinews
450	of the neck, and loosed the strength of the heifer. And the women raised the cry,
451	his daughters and his sons' wives and the revered wife

459	The old man burned them on split wood, and over them poured
460	sparkling wine; and young men beside him held five-pronged forks in their hands.
461	But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails,
462	they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
463	and roasted it, holding the sharp-pointed spits in their hands.
464	Meanwhile lovely Polycaste bathed Telemachus,
465	the youngest daughter of Nestor, son of Neleus.
466	And when she had bathed him and anointed him richly with olive oil,
467	and put a fine mantle and a tunic around him,
468	he stepped out of the bathtub, like the immortals in form;
469	and he went and sat down beside Nestor, shepherd of the people.
470	And when they had roasted the outer flesh and drawn it off,
471	they sat and feasted; and good men stood by to serve,
472	pouring wine into golden cups.
473	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
474	among them the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak:
475	“My sons, come, bring fine-maned horses for Telemachus
476	and yoke them under the chariot, so that he may make his way.”
477	So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed,
478	and quickly they yoked the swift horses under the chariot.
479	And a woman, the housekeeper, put bread and wine in it,
480	and meats, such as kings whom Zeus has nurtured eat.
481	And Telemachus mounted the beautiful chariot;
482	and beside him Peisistratus, son of Nestor, leader of men,
483	mounted the chariot and took the reins in his hands,
484	and whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling
485	to the plain, and left the steep citadel of Pylos behind.
486	And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side.
487	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark;
488	and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles,
489	son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus begot as his son.
490	There they spent the night, and he set good cheer before them.
491	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
492	they yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot,
493	and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade.
494	And he whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling.
495	And they came to the wheat-bearing plain, and there then
496	they were finishing their journey; so well did the swift horses carry them on.
497	And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark.
