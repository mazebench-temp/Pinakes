# Homer, Odyssey, Book 4 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek; each row carries the Greek verse number.

1	And they came to hollow Lacedaemon with its many ravines,
2	and drove on to the house of glorious Menelaus.
3	They found him giving a wedding feast to his many kinsmen
4	for his son and his noble daughter in his house.
5	The daughter he was sending to the son of Achilles, breaker of men;
6	for in Troy he had first promised and nodded his assent
7	that he would give her, and now the gods were bringing their wedding to pass.
8	So he was sending her there with horses and chariots to go
9	to the renowned city of the Myrmidons, over whom he ruled.
10	And for his son he was bringing the daughter of Alector of Sparta,
11	strong Megapenthes, who was born to him as his darling
12	from a slave woman; for to Helen the gods brought no more offspring to light,
13	once she had first borne a lovely child,
14	Hermione, who had the form of golden Aphrodite.
15	So they were feasting throughout the great high-roofed house,
16	the neighbors and kinsmen of glorious Menelaus,
17	delighting in it; and among them a divine singer sang
18	to the lyre; and two tumblers among them,
19	as he led the song and dance, whirled in the midst.
20	And the two of them, themselves and their horses, in the doorway of the house,
21	the hero Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor,
22	stood; and powerful Eteoneus came forward and saw them,
23	the ready attendant of glorious Menelaus,
24	and he went through the house to bring the news to the shepherd of the people,
25	and standing near him he addressed him with winged words:
26	“There are two strangers here, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus,
27	two men, and they are like the lineage of great Zeus.
28	But tell me: shall we unyoke their swift horses for them,
29	or send them on to come to some other man, who will make them welcome?”
30	Deeply troubled, fair-haired Menelaus said to him:
31	“You were no fool, Eteoneus, son of Boethous,
32	before now; but now you babble folly like a child.
33	Surely the two of us ate much good cheer
34	of other men before we came here, if ever Zeus
35	might give us rest from misery, hereafter at least. But unyoke the horses
36	of the strangers, and bring them on into the house to feast.”
37	So he spoke, and the other hurried through the hall, and called the others,
38	the ready attendants, to follow along with him.
39	They unyoked the sweating horses from under the yoke,
40	and tied them at the horses' mangers,
41	and threw spelt before them, and mixed white barley with it,
42	and leaned the chariot against the gleaming walls;
43	and they led the men themselves into the divine house. And when they saw it,
44	they marveled throughout the house of the king whom Zeus had nurtured;
45	for there was a gleam as of the sun or of the moon
46	throughout the high-roofed house of glorious Menelaus.
47	But when they had taken their fill of looking with their eyes,
48	they stepped into polished bathtubs and bathed.
49	And when the slave women had washed them and anointed them with oil,
50	and put woolly cloaks and tunics around them,
51	they sat down on chairs beside Menelaus, son of Atreus.
52	And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it,
53	a fine golden one, over a silver basin,
54	for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them.
55	And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
56	adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had;
57	and a carver lifted up platters of meat and set them before them,
58	of every kind, and set golden cups beside them.
59	And greeting the two, fair-haired Menelaus said:
60	“Take some of the food, and welcome, both of you! And then,
61	when you have tasted the meal, we will ask who you are
62	among men; for in you two the lineage of your parents is not lost,
63	but you are of the lineage of men, of kings whom Zeus has nurtured,
64	scepter-bearing kings, since base men would not beget such sons as you.”
65	So he spoke, and he set before them the fat chine of an ox,
66	lifting the roast meat in his hands, which they had set before himself as his portion of honor.
67	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
68	But when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
69	then Telemachus spoke to the son of Nestor,
70	putting his head close, so that the others might not hear:
71	“Take note, son of Nestor, dear to my heart,
72	of the flash of bronze throughout the echoing halls,
73	and of gold and amber and silver and ivory.
74	Surely such is the court of Olympian Zeus within,
75	so many are these things, untold; awe holds me as I look at them.”
76	And fair-haired Menelaus heard him as he spoke,
77	and he spoke to them, addressing them with winged words:
78	“Dear children, truly no mortal could rival Zeus;
79	for his house and his possessions are immortal;
80	but among men there may be one who will rival me, or there may be none,
81	in possessions. For after much suffering and much wandering
82	I brought them home in my ships, and came back in the eighth year,
83	after wandering to Cyprus and Phoenicia and the Egyptians,
84	and I came to the Ethiopians and the Sidonians and the Erembi
85	and to Libya, where the lambs are horned at once.
86	For the flocks bear young three times in the full year's round.
87	There neither master nor shepherd has any lack
88	of cheese or meat or sweet milk,
89	but the flocks always yield unfailing milk to drink.
90	While I, gathering much substance in those lands,
91	was wandering, meanwhile another man killed my brother
92	in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife;
93	so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions.
94	And you must have heard of this from your fathers, whoever they are,
95	for I suffered very much, and lost a house
96	that was very well appointed and held many good things.
97	I wish that I lived in my halls with even a third part of this wealth,
98	and that the men were safe who perished then
99	in wide Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos.
100	But still, though I mourn and grieve for them all,
101	often, as I sit in our halls—
102	at one moment I comfort my heart with lament, and at another
103	I stop; for satiety of chilling lament comes quickly—
104	for all of them I do not mourn so much, grieved though I am,
105	as for one, who makes sleep and food hateful to me
106	when I remember him, since no one of the Achaeans toiled as much
107	as Odysseus toiled and endured. But for him it was to be
108	that he himself should have sorrows, and for me, always, grief not to be forgotten
109	for him, that he is gone so long, and we know nothing,
110	whether he is alive or dead. And surely they mourn for him,
111	the old man Laertes and sensible Penelope
112	and Telemachus, whom he left newly born in his house.”
113	So he spoke, and in him he roused the longing for lament for his father;
114	and he let a tear fall from his eyelids to the ground when he heard of his father,
115	holding up his purple cloak before his eyes
116	with both his hands. And Menelaus noticed him,
117	and then he pondered in his mind and in his heart
118	whether he should let him make mention of his father himself
119	or first question him and put every point to the test.
120	While he was pondering this in his mind and in his heart,
121	Helen came out of her fragrant high-roofed chamber,
122	like Artemis of the golden distaff.
123	And with her came Adreste and set for her a well-made chair,
124	and Alcippe brought a rug of soft wool,
125	and Phylo brought a silver basket, which had been given her
126	by Alcandre, the wife of Polybus, who lived in Thebes
127	in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses.
128	He gave Menelaus two silver bathtubs
129	and two tripods, and ten talents of gold;
130	and apart from these his wife gave Helen beautiful gifts:
131	she granted her a golden distaff and a basket on wheels
132	of silver, and its rims were finished with gold.
133	This the handmaid Phylo brought and set beside her,
134	filled with finely spun yarn; and across it
135	lay the distaff, laden with violet-dark wool.
136	She sat down in the chair, and there was a footstool under her feet,
137	and at once she questioned her husband with words on every point:
138	“Do we know, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, who these men
139	claim to be who have come to our house?
140	Shall I be wrong, or speak the truth? But my heart bids me speak.
141	For I say I have never yet seen anyone so like another,
142	neither man nor woman; awe holds me as I look,
143	as this man is like the son of great-hearted Odysseus,
144	Telemachus, whom he left newly born in his house,
145	that man, when for my sake, dog-faced as I am, you Achaeans
146	came to Troy, bent on bold war.”
147	Answering her, fair-haired Menelaus said:
148	“I too see it so now, lady, as you liken them;
149	for such were his feet, and such his hands,
150	and the glances of his eyes, and his head and the hair above.
151	And just now, remembering, I was speaking about Odysseus,
152	of all that he toiled in misery
153	for my sake, and he let a bitter tear fall from beneath his brows,
154	holding up his purple cloak before his eyes.”
155	And Peisistratus, son of Nestor, answered him face to face:
156	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
157	this is truly his son, as you say;
158	but he is modest, and he is indignant in his heart
159	at making a show of forward talk, having come here for the first time,
160	before you, whose voice the two of us delight in as a god's.
161	And the Gerenian horseman Nestor sent me forth
162	to go with him as his escort; for he longed to see you,
163	so that you might give him counsel in some word or some deed.
164	For a son whose father is gone has many sorrows
165	in his halls, when there are no others to be his helpers,
166	as now with Telemachus: his father is gone, and there are no others
167	among the people who might ward off misery from him.”
168	Answering him, fair-haired Menelaus said:
169	“Ah, truly the son of a man very dear to me has come to my house,
170	who for my sake toiled through many trials;
171	and I thought that when he came I would make him welcome above all other
172	Argives, if he had granted the two of us a homecoming over the sea
173	with our swift ships, far-seeing Olympian Zeus.
174	And I would have given him a city in Argos to dwell in, and built him a house,
175	bringing him from Ithaca with his possessions and his son
176	and all his people, emptying one of the cities
177	that lie round about and are ruled by me myself.
178	And living here we would often have met together; and nothing
179	would have parted us as we loved and delighted in each other,
180	until the black cloud of death had enfolded us.
181	But the god himself, it seems, was to begrudge us this,
182	who has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming.”
183	So he spoke, and in them all he roused the longing for lament;
184	Argive Helen wept, child of Zeus,
185	and Telemachus wept, and Menelaus, son of Atreus,
186	nor did the son of Nestor keep his eyes free of tears;
187	for in his heart he remembered noble Antilochus,
188	whom the splendid son of shining Dawn had killed;
189	remembering him, he spoke winged words:
190	“Son of Atreus, that you were wise beyond all mortals
191	old Nestor used to say, whenever we made mention of you
192	in his halls and asked one another about you.
193	And now, if it is at all possible, be persuaded by me; for I, for my part,
194	take no delight in mourning after supper; there will be early-born
195	Dawn as well. Yet I am not at all indignant
196	at weeping for any mortal who dies and meets his fate.
197	This is the only prize for miserable mortals,
198	to cut one's hair and let a tear fall from one's cheeks.
199	For my brother too is dead, by no means the worst
200	of the Argives; you may have known him; for I, for my part,
201	never met him or saw him; but they say he was above all others,
202	Antilochus, surpassingly swift at running, and a fighter.”
203	Answering him, fair-haired Menelaus said:
204	“My friend, since you have said all that a wise man
205	would say and do, even one who was older;
206	for your father too is such a man, and so you speak wisely.
207	Easily known is the offspring of a man for whom the son of Cronus
208	spins prosperity at his marriage and at his birth,
209	as now he has granted Nestor all his days, continually,
210	himself to grow old in ease in his halls,
211	and his sons in turn to be men of good sense and the best with the spear.
212	So let us leave off the weeping that came before,
213	and let us remember supper again, and let them pour water
214	over our hands. And there will be talk at dawn too
215	for Telemachus and me to have with one another.”
216	So he spoke, and Asphalion poured water over their hands,
217	the ready attendant of glorious Menelaus.
218	And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
219	Then Helen, child of Zeus, thought of another thing:
220	at once she cast a drug into the wine from which they were drinking,
221	a drug against grief and anger, that brought forgetfulness of every ill.
222	Whoever swallowed it down, once it was mixed in the mixing bowl,
223	would not let a tear fall down his cheeks for that day,
224	not even if his mother and his father lay dead,
225	not even if before his face they cut down his brother or his dear son
226	with the bronze, and he saw it with his own eyes.
227	Such cunning drugs the daughter of Zeus had,
228	good ones, which Polydamna had given her, the wife of Thon,
229	a woman of Egypt, where the grain-giving earth bears the most
230	drugs, many good and many grim, growing intermixed;
231	and every man there is a healer, skilled beyond all
232	other men; for they are of the stock of Paeëon.
233	And when she had cast it in and bidden them pour the wine,
234	she spoke again, answering them with these words:
235	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, and you here,
236	sons of brave men—though the god, now to one and now to another,
237	Zeus, gives good and evil; for he can do all things—
238	now feast, sitting in the halls,
239	and take delight in stories; for I will tell you what is fitting.
240	All of them I could not tell or name,
241	all the trials of stout-hearted Odysseus;
242	but what a thing this was that the strong man did and endured
243	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered woes:
244	marring himself with shameful blows,
245	throwing wretched rags about his shoulders, like a servant,
246	he slipped into the wide-streeted city of hostile men;
247	and hiding himself he made himself like another man,
248	a beggar, he who was nothing of the kind by the ships of the Achaeans.
249	In his likeness he slipped into the city of the Trojans, and they suspected nothing,
250	all of them; but I alone knew him for what he was,
251	and I questioned him; but by craft he evaded me.
252	But when I was bathing him and anointing him with oil,
253	and had put clothes on him, and sworn a mighty oath
254	not to reveal Odysseus among the Trojans
255	before he had reached the swift ships and the huts,
256	then at last he told me the whole plan of the Achaeans.
257	And after killing many Trojans with the long-edged bronze
258	he went back among the Argives, and brought back much intelligence.
259	Then the other Trojan women wailed shrilly; but my heart
260	rejoiced, since already my heart was turned to going
261	back home, and I groaned over the blind folly that Aphrodite
262	gave me, when she led me there away from my dear native land,
263	leaving behind my child and my bridal chamber and my husband,
264	who lacked nothing, in mind or in form.”
265	Answering her, fair-haired Menelaus said:
266	“Yes, lady, all that you have said is fair and right.
267	Before now I have come to know the plan and the thought of many
268	heroes, and I have gone over much of the earth;
269	but never yet have I seen with my eyes such a one
270	as was the dear heart of stout-hearted Odysseus.
271	What a thing this too was that the strong man did and endured
272	in the polished horse, where we were sitting, all the best
273	of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans!
274	Then you came there; some god must have bidden you,
275	a god who meant to hold out glory to the Trojans;
276	and godlike Deiphobus followed you as you went.
277	Three times you walked around the hollow ambush, feeling it all over,
278	and you called out by name the best of the Danaans,
279	making your voice like the voices of the wives of all the Argives.
280	But I and the son of Tydeus and brilliant Odysseus
281	were sitting in the midst and heard how you shouted.
282	The two of us were both eager to rise up
283	and either go out or answer at once from inside;
284	but Odysseus held us back and checked us, eager though we were.
285	Then all the other sons of the Achaeans were silent,
286	and Anticlus alone wanted to answer you
287	with words; but Odysseus pressed upon his mouth with his hands,
288	unceasingly, with his strong hands, and saved all the Achaeans,
289	and held him so until Pallas Athena led you away.”
290	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
291	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
292	it is all the harder; for none of this kept grim destruction from him,
293	not even if the heart within him had been of iron.
294	But come, send us to bed, so that now
295	we may lie down and take our fill of sweet sleep.”
296	So he spoke, and Argive Helen bade the slave women
297	set beds beneath the colonnade, and throw fine purple
298	blankets on them, and spread rugs over them,
299	and put woolly cloaks on top for them to wear.
300	And the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands,
301	and spread the beds; and a herald led the strangers out.
302	So they slept there in the porch of the house,
303	the hero Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor;
304	but the son of Atreus slept in the inner part of the high house,
305	and beside him lay Helen of the long robes, brilliant among women.
306	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
307	Menelaus, good at the war cry, rose from his bed,
308	put on his clothes, and about his shoulder set his sharp sword,
309	and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals,
310	and went out of his chamber, like a god to look upon.
311	He sat down beside Telemachus, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
312	“What need has brought you here, hero Telemachus,
313	to shining Lacedaemon, over the broad back of the sea?
314	Is it a public matter or your own? Tell me this truly.”
315	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
316	“Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people,
317	I have come in the hope that you might tell me some report of my father.
318	My house is being eaten up, and my rich tilled fields are ruined,
319	and the house is full of hostile men, who are always
320	slaughtering my thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns,
321	the suitors of my mother, overweening in their arrogance.
322	That is why I come now to your knees, in the hope that you may be willing
323	to tell me of his grim destruction, if perhaps you saw it
324	with your own eyes, or heard word of him from someone else
325	as he wandered; for beyond all men his mother bore him to misery.
326	And do not soften anything out of respect for me, or out of pity,
327	but tell me in full how you came upon the sight of him.
328	I beg you, if ever my brave father Odysseus
329	promised you any word or deed and fulfilled it
330	in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered woes,
331	remember these things now for me, and tell me truly.”
332	Deeply troubled, fair-haired Menelaus said to him:
333	“Ah, truly it was in the bed of a fierce-hearted man
334	that they wished to lie, weaklings as they are.
335	As when in the thicket of a strong lion a hind
336	has lulled her newborn suckling fawns to sleep,
337	and goes out to search the mountain slopes and the grassy valleys
338	for pasture, and then he comes back into his lair
339	and brings a shameful doom on both of them,
340	so Odysseus will bring a shameful doom on those men.
341	If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo,
342	such as he was once in well-built Lesbos,
343	when he stood up and wrestled Philomeleides out of strife,
344	and threw him down mightily, and all the Achaeans rejoiced,
345	if only Odysseus, such as that, would engage the suitors:
346	they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be.
347	But as for what you ask and beg of me, I would not
348	swerve aside to speak of other things, nor deceive you;
349	but what the unerring Old Man of the Sea told me,
350	of that I will not conceal a word from you, or hide it.
351	In Egypt, eager as I was to come home, the gods
352	still held me back, since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs;
353	and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered.
354	Now there is an island in the sea of many waves
355	in front of Egypt, and they call it Pharos,
356	as far off as a hollow ship covers in a whole day
357	when a shrill fair wind blows behind it;
358	and in it there is a harbor with good anchorage, from which men put their balanced ships
359	out into the sea, when they have drawn dark water.
360	There the gods kept me twenty days, and no fair winds
361	ever appeared, blowing over the sea, the winds that are the escorts
362	of ships over the broad back of the sea.
363	And now all our provisions would have been spent, and the strength of the men,
364	had not one of the gods pitied me and saved me,
365	the daughter of mighty Proteus, the Old Man of the Sea,
366	Eidothea; for it was her heart above all that I stirred.
367	She met me as I went my ill-starred way alone, apart from my companions;
368	for they were always wandering about the island, fishing
369	with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies.
370	She came and stood near me, and spoke and said:
371	‘Are you so utterly a fool, stranger, and feeble-minded,
372	or do you give up of your own will, and take delight in suffering sorrows,
373	seeing that you are held back so long on the island, and can find
374	no end to it, and the heart of your companions dwindles?’
375	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
376	‘I will speak out to you, whoever you are of the goddesses,
377	that I am not held back here of my own will; but it must be that I
378	have sinned against the immortals, who hold the wide sky.
379	But you at least tell me—for the gods know all things—
380	who of the immortals fetters me and has bound me from my path,
381	and tell me of my homecoming, how I shall go over the fish-filled sea.’
382	So I spoke, and at once she answered, brilliant among goddesses:
383	‘Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
384	There often comes here an unerring Old Man of the Sea,
385	immortal Proteus of Egypt, who knows the depths
386	of the whole sea, the servant of Poseidon;
387	and they say that he is my father, who begot me.
388	If somehow you could lie in ambush and catch him,
389	he will tell you the way and the measures of your path
390	and your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea.
391	And he will tell you, nurtured by Zeus, if you are willing,
392	whatever evil and good has been done in your halls
393	while you have been gone on your long and grievous way.’
394	So she spoke, and I answered her and said:
395	‘Devise now yourself an ambush for the divine old man,
396	lest somehow he see me first, or know beforehand, and avoid me;
397	for a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower.’
398	So I spoke, and at once she answered, brilliant among goddesses:
399	‘Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
400	When the sun stands astride the middle of the sky,
401	then the unerring Old Man of the Sea comes out of the sea
402	at the breath of the West Wind, hidden in the dark ripple,
403	and when he has come out he lies down to sleep in the hollow caves;
404	and around him the seals, the brood of the lovely daughter of the sea,
405	sleep all together, having come up out of the gray sea,
406	breathing out the bitter smell of the deep sea.
407	There I will lead you, when dawn appears,
408	and lay you down in order; and you choose well your companions,
409	three, who are the best you have beside your well-benched ships.
410	And I will tell you all the wiles of that old man.
411	First he will count the seals and go his rounds among them;
412	and when he has counted them all off by fives and looked them over,
413	he will lie down in their midst, like a herdsman among his flocks of sheep.
414	As soon as you see him laid to sleep,
415	then let your strength and your force be your concern,
416	and hold him there, however he strives and struggles to escape.
417	He will make his attempt by becoming all the things that upon the earth
418	creep, and water, and wondrous fire;
419	but you hold him steadfastly and press him all the more.
420	But when he himself questions you in words,
421	being such as you saw him laid to sleep,
422	then stop your force and set the old man free,
423	hero, and ask him which of the gods is oppressing you,
424	and about your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea.’
425	So saying, she plunged beneath the billowing sea.
426	And I, to the ships where they stood on the sand,
427	went my way; and my heart heaved with many thoughts as I went.
428	And when I had come down to the ship and the sea,
429	we made ready our supper, and the ambrosial night came on;
430	and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore.
431	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
432	then along the shore of the wide-wayed sea
433	I went, imploring the gods earnestly; and companions,
434	three of them I brought, whom I trusted most in every undertaking.
435	Meanwhile she, having plunged into the broad bosom of the sea,
436	brought from the sea the skins of four seals;
437	all were freshly flayed; for she was devising a trick against her father.
438	And having scooped out beds in the sands of the sea
439	she sat waiting; and we came very close to her;
440	and she laid us down in order, and threw a skin over each.
441	There our ambush would have been most dread; for terribly it wore us down,
442	the most baneful smell of the sea-nurtured seals;
443	for who would lie down to sleep beside a beast of the sea?
444	But she herself saved us, and devised a great blessing:
445	she brought ambrosia and set it under each man's nose,
446	very sweet in its fragrance, and it killed the smell of the beast.
447	All the morning we waited with enduring heart;
448	and the seals came out of the sea in a body. Then they
449	lay down in order along the shore of the sea;
450	and at midday the old man came out of the sea, and found the seals,
451	well-fed ones, and went among them all, and counted their number;
452	and among the beasts he counted us first, and had no thought
453	in his heart that there was a trick; and then he lay down himself.
454	And we rushed on him with a shout, and threw our arms
455	around him; nor did the old man forget his crafty art,
456	but first of all he became a bearded lion,
457	and then a serpent, and a leopard, and a great boar;
458	and he became flowing water, and a tree with high foliage;
459	but we held him steadfastly with enduring heart.
460	But when the old man, skilled in wiles, grew weary,
461	then he questioned me with words and said:
462	‘Which of the gods, son of Atreus, helped you to devise this plan,
463	so that you might lie in ambush and take me against my will? What do you need?’
464	So he spoke, and I answered him and said:
465	‘You know, old man—why do you ask me this, trying to mislead me?—
466	how I am held back so long on the island, and can find
467	no end to it, and my heart dwindles within me.
468	But you at least tell me—for the gods know all things—
469	who of the immortals fetters me and has bound me from my path,
470	and tell me of my homecoming, how I shall go over the fish-filled sea.’
471	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
472	‘Why, surely to Zeus and the other gods you ought
473	to have offered fine sacrifices before you embarked, so that very quickly
474	you might come to your native land, sailing over the wine-dark sea.
475	For it is not your fate to see your friends and to reach
476	your well-built house and your native land
477	until to the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus,
478	you have gone back again, and offered holy hecatombs
479	to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky;
480	and then the gods will grant you the journey you have in mind.’
481	So he spoke, and my dear heart was broken,
482	because he bade me go back again over the misty sea
483	to Egypt, a long and grievous way.
484	But even so I answered him with these words and said:
485	‘This I will accomplish, old man, just as you bid me.
486	But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account:
487	did all the Achaeans come home unharmed with their ships,
488	whom Nestor and I left behind when we went from Troy,
489	or did any perish by a bitter destruction on board his ship
490	or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war?’
491	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
492	‘Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this? You have no need
493	to know it, or to learn my mind; for I do not think
494	you will be long without tears, when you have heard it all.
495	For many of them were brought down, and many were left;
496	but of the leaders of the bronze-armored Achaeans only two
497	perished in their homecoming; and at the battle you were there yourself.
498	And one, still alive somewhere, is held back on the wide sea.
499	Ajax was brought down among his long-oared ships.
500	First Poseidon drove him onto the Gyrae,
501	the great rocks, and saved him from the sea;
502	and he would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena,
503	had he not let fall an overbearing word and been greatly blinded:
504	he said that against the will of the gods he had escaped the great gulf of the sea.
505	And Poseidon heard him speaking big words;
506	at once he took his trident in his strong hands
507	and struck the Gyraean rock, and split it apart;
508	and the one part stayed where it was, but the broken piece fell into the sea,
509	the piece on which Ajax had first sat when he was greatly blinded;
510	and it carried him down into the boundless billowing sea.
511	So he perished there, when he had drunk the salt water.
512	But your brother escaped the dooms and got away from them
513	in his hollow ships; for queen Hera saved him.
514	But when he was just about to reach the steep mountain of Malea,
515	then a storm wind caught him up
516	and carried him over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily,
517	to the far edge of the land, where Thyestes had his dwelling
518	before, but at that time Aegisthus, son of Thyestes, dwelt there.
519	But when from there too a safe homecoming appeared,
520	and the gods turned the wind back, and they reached home,
521	then he set foot on his native land rejoicing,
522	and he touched his native land and kissed it; and many
523	hot tears poured from him, since he was glad to see his land.
524	But a lookout saw him from his lookout, whom scheming Aegisthus
525	had brought and posted there, and promised him as wages
526	two talents of gold; and he had kept watch for a year,
527	lest he should pass by unseen, and remember his furious courage.
528	And he went to the house to bring the news to the shepherd of the people.
529	And at once Aegisthus devised a crafty scheme:
530	he chose twenty of the best men in the land
531	and set an ambush, and on the other side bade them prepare a feast.
532	Then he went to invite Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
533	with horses and chariot, pondering shameful things;
534	and he led him up, unaware of his destruction, and killed him
535	when he had feasted him, as one kills an ox at the manger.
536	And not one of the companions of the son of Atreus who followed him was left,
537	nor any of Aegisthus' men, but they were killed in the halls.’
538	So he spoke, and my dear heart was broken,
539	and I wept as I sat on the sand, and my heart
540	no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun.
541	But when I had had my fill of weeping and rolling on the ground,
542	then the unerring Old Man of the Sea said to me:
543	‘No longer, son of Atreus, so long and so stubbornly
544	weep, since we shall find that nothing comes of it; but as quickly as you can
545	try how you may reach your native land.
546	For either you will find him alive, or Orestes
547	will have killed him before you, and you may come in time for the funeral feast.’
548	So he spoke, and my heart and proud spirit
549	were warmed again in my breast, grieved though I was,
550	and I spoke to him, addressing him with winged words:
551	‘These I know of; but name the third man to me,
552	who is still alive and held back on the wide sea,
553	or dead; I want to hear, grieved though I am.’
554	So I spoke, and at once he answered me and said:
555	‘It is the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca;
556	him I saw on an island shedding big tears
557	in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who by force
558	holds him there; and he cannot reach his native land;
559	for he has no ships with oars and no companions
560	who might take him over the broad back of the sea.
561	But for you, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, it is not decreed
562	to die and meet your fate in horse-pasturing Argos,
563	but to the Elysian plain and the limits of the earth
564	the immortals will send you, where fair-haired Rhadamanthys is,
565	where life is easiest for men;
566	there is no snow, nor severe winter, nor ever rain,
567	but always the breezes of the West Wind, blowing shrill,
568	Oceanus sends up to refresh men;
569	because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus.’
570	So saying, he plunged beneath the billowing sea.
571	And I, to the ships with my godlike companions,
572	went my way; and my heart heaved with many thoughts as I went.
573	And when we had come down to the ship and the sea,
574	we made ready our supper, and the ambrosial night came on;
575	and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore.
576	And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered,
577	first of all we dragged the ships down into the shining sea,
578	and set the masts and the sails in the balanced ships,
579	and they went on board themselves, and sat down at the rowing benches;
580	and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars.
581	And back to the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus,
582	I brought my ships to a halt, and offered perfect hecatombs.
583	And when I had ended the anger of the gods who live forever,
584	I heaped up a tomb for Agamemnon, so that his fame might be unquenchable.
585	When I had done this I went home, and they gave me a fair wind,
586	the immortals, and quickly sent me to my dear native land.
587	But come now, wait in my halls
588	until the eleventh or the twelfth day has come;
589	and then I will send you on your way well, and give you splendid gifts,
590	three horses and a well-polished chariot; and then
591	I will give you a fine goblet, so that you may pour libations to the gods,
592	the immortals, remembering me all your days.”
593	And wise Telemachus answered him face to face:
594	“Son of Atreus, do not keep me here a long time.
595	For I would bear to stay beside you even for a year,
596	sitting here, and no longing for home or parents would seize me;
597	for listening to your stories and your words I am terribly
598	delighted. But already my companions are growing weary
599	in sacred Pylos; and you keep me here a long time.
600	And whatever gift you would give me, let it be a treasure;
601	but horses I will not take to Ithaca; for you yourself
602	I will leave them here as a delight; for you are lord of a plain
603	that is wide, in which there is much lotus, and galingale,
604	and wheat and spelt and broad-eared white barley.
605	But in Ithaca there are no wide courses for running, nor any meadow;
606	it is a goat-pasturing land, and lovelier than a horse-pasturing one.
607	For none of the islands is good for driving horses or rich in meadows,
608	those that lie upon the sea; and Ithaca above them all.”
609	So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war cry, smiled,
610	and stroked him with his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
611	“You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak;
612	Well then, I will change these gifts for you; for I can.
613	Of the gifts that lie stored as treasures in my house,
614	I will give you the one that is finest and most precious;
615	I will give you a well-wrought mixing bowl; of silver
616	it is all, and its rims are finished with gold,
617	the work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it to me,
618	the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me
619	as I came there on my way home; and I wish to grant it to you.”
620	So they spoke such words to one another,
621	and the banqueters went to the house of the divine king.
622	They drove sheep, and brought wine that makes men strong,
623	and their wives with lovely veils sent them bread.
624	So they were busy about the meal in the halls.
625	But the suitors, in front of the hall of Odysseus,
626	amused themselves throwing the discus and the javelin
627	on the leveled ground, where they used to, in their arrogance.
628	And Antinous was sitting there, and godlike Eurymachus,
629	leaders of the suitors, and in excellence they were by far the best.
630	And Noemon, son of Phronius, came near them
631	and questioned Antinous with these words and said:
632	“Antinous, do we know at all in our minds, or not,
633	when Telemachus will come back from sandy Pylos?
634	He went off with a ship of mine; and I have need of it
635	to cross over to Elis with its wide dancing floors, where I have horses,
636	twelve mares, and at their teats hard-working mules,
637	unbroken; one of these I would drive off and break in.”
638	So he spoke, and they were amazed in their hearts; for they did not think
639	that he had gone to Neleian Pylos, but that he was somewhere there
640	on the farm, with the flocks or with the swineherd.
641	Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him in turn:
642	“Tell me truly: when did he go, and what young men
643	followed him? Chosen men of Ithaca, or his own
644	hired men and slaves? He could do even that.
645	And tell me this truly, so that I may know it well,
646	whether he took the black ship from you by force, against your will,
647	or you gave it to him willingly, when he greeted you with words.”
648	And Noemon, son of Phronius, answered him face to face:
649	“I gave it to him myself, willingly. What would anyone else do,
650	when such a man, with cares in his heart,
651	asks him? It would be hard to refuse the gift.
652	And the young men who are the best in the land after us
653	are the ones who followed him; and as their leader I saw going on board
654	Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way.
655	But at this I marvel: I saw brilliant Mentor here
656	yesterday at daybreak, though at that time he went on board the ship for Pylos.”
657	So he spoke, and went away to his father's house,
658	and the proud spirit in both of them was indignant.
659	And they made the suitors sit down together, and stopped their games.
660	And Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them
661	in distress; and his heart, dark on every side, was filled
662	with mighty fury, and his eyes were like blazing fire.
663	“Ah, truly a great deed has been overbearingly accomplished
664	by Telemachus, this journey; and we said it would never come to pass.
665	Against the will of so many of us the young boy is gone, just like that,
666	launching a ship and choosing the best men in the land.
667	He will begin to be a mischief further on; but may Zeus
668	destroy his strength before he reaches the measure of youth.
669	But come, give me a swift ship and twenty companions,
670	so that I may lie in ambush for him on his way and watch for him
671	in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos,
672	so that he may sail miserably in search of his father.”
673	So he spoke, and they all approved and bade him do it.
674	Then at once they rose and went into the house of Odysseus.
675	Nor was Penelope long without hearing
676	of the plans that the suitors were brooding deep in their minds;
677	for the herald Medon told her, who heard their counsels
678	as he stood outside the courtyard, while they inside were weaving their plan;
679	and he went through the house to bring the news to Penelope.
680	And as he stepped over the threshold Penelope spoke to him:
681	“Herald, why have the noble suitors sent you?
682	Was it to tell the slave women of divine Odysseus
683	to cease from their work and prepare a feast for them?
684	May they, no longer wooing, nor gathering anywhere else,
685	take their meal here now for the very last time—
686	you who gather here constantly and waste much substance,
687	the wealth of wise-hearted Telemachus; nor did you hear at all
688	from your fathers before, when you were children,
689	what kind of man Odysseus was among your parents,
690	who never did anything excessive to anyone, nor said anything excessive
691	among the people, though that is the way of divine kings:
692	one man among mortals they hate, another they love.
693	But he never did anything at all reckless to a man.
694	But your spirit and your shameful deeds
695	are plain to see, and there is no thanks afterward for good deeds done.”
696	Then Medon, who had wise understanding, spoke to her in turn:
697	“If only, queen, this were the greatest evil.
698	But another, far greater and more grievous,
699	the suitors are plotting, which may the son of Cronus not bring to pass:
700	they are eager to kill Telemachus with the sharp bronze
701	as he comes home; for he has gone in search of news of his father
702	to sacred Pylos and to shining Lacedaemon.”
703	So he spoke, and her knees and dear heart were loosened where she was,
704	and for a long time speechlessness took her, and her two eyes
705	filled with tears, and her strong voice was checked.
706	But at last she answered him with words and said:
707	“Herald, why has my child gone? He had no need
708	to go on board swift-sailing ships, which are horses of the sea
709	for men, and cross over the wide waters.
710	Was it so that not even his name should be left among men?”
711	Then Medon, who had wise understanding, answered her:
712	“I do not know whether some god roused him, or whether his own
713	heart was eager to go to Pylos, to learn
714	of his father's homecoming, or what fate he has met.”
715	So he spoke, and went away through the house of Odysseus.
716	And grief that destroys the heart was poured about her, and she could no longer bear
717	to sit on a chair, though there were many chairs in the house,
718	but she sat on the threshold of her chamber, worked with much toil,
719	lamenting piteously; and around her the slave women whimpered,
720	all who were in the house, young and old.
721	And Penelope, lamenting without ceasing, spoke among them:
722	“Hear me, friends; for the Olympian has given me sorrows beyond
723	all the women who were born and raised with me:
724	I who first lost my brave, lion-hearted husband,
725	who excelled among the Danaans in every kind of prowess,
726	a brave man, whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos;
727	and now the storm winds have snatched away my beloved son
728	from the halls without report, and I did not hear of his setting out.
729	Hard-hearted women, not even you, any of you, thought in your minds
730	to rouse me from my bed, though you knew it well in your hearts,
731	when he went on board the black hollow ship.
732	For if I had learned that he was pondering this journey,
733	then surely he would have stayed, eager though he was for the journey,
734	or he would have left me dead in the halls.
735	But let someone readily call old Dolius,
736	my slave, whom my father gave me when I came here,
737	and who keeps my garden of many trees, so that as quickly as he can
738	he may sit beside Laertes and tell him all this,
739	in the hope that he may perhaps weave some plan in his mind
740	and come out and mourn before the people, who are eager
741	to destroy his offspring, the offspring of godlike Odysseus.”
742	Then the dear nurse Eurycleia spoke to her in turn:
743	“Dear bride, kill me with the pitiless bronze
744	or let me be in the hall; but I will not hide my word from you.
745	I knew all this, and I gave him all that he bade me,
746	bread and sweet wine; and he took a great oath from me
747	not to tell you before the twelfth day had come,
748	or you yourself missed him and heard that he had gone,
749	so that you might not mar your fair skin with weeping.
750	But bathe, and put clean clothes on your body,
751	and go up to your upper room with your handmaids
752	and pray to Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus;
753	for then she might save him even from death.
754	And do not afflict an afflicted old man; for I do not think
755	that to the blessed gods the offspring of the son of Arceisius
756	is altogether hateful; someone will still be left, I think, to hold
757	the high-roofed house and the rich fields far away.”
758	So she spoke, and lulled her lament, and checked her eyes from lament.
759	And she bathed, and put clean clothes on her body,
760	and went up to her upper room with her handmaids,
761	and put barley grains in a basket, and prayed to Athena:
762	“Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone:
763	if ever Odysseus of many wiles in his halls
764	burned to you the fat thigh-pieces of ox or sheep,
765	remember these things now for me, and save my dear son,
766	and ward off the suitors, wickedly overweening.”
767	So saying, she raised the cry, and the goddess heard her prayer.
768	And the suitors raised a din throughout the shadowy halls,
769	and this is what one of the overweening young men would say:
770	“Surely a marriage for us the much-wooed queen
771	is preparing, and does not know that slaughter has been made ready for her son.”
772	So a man would say; but they did not know how things had been brought about.
773	And Antinous addressed them and spoke:
774	“Strange men, avoid overbearing talk,
775	all of it alike, lest someone report it even inside.
776	But come, let us rise in silence and accomplish
777	the plan that has pleased all of us in our minds.”
778	So saying, he chose twenty of the best men,
779	and they went to the swift ship and the shore of the sea.
780	First of all they dragged the ship down into deep water,
781	and set the mast and the sails in the black ship,
782	and fitted the oars in their leather straps,
783	all in good order, and spread the white sails;
784	and their high-hearted attendants brought them their arms.
785	They anchored her out in the water, and stepped out themselves;
786	and there they took their supper, and waited for evening to come.
787	But up in her upper room prudent Penelope
788	lay without food, tasting neither food nor drink,
789	pondering whether her noble son would escape death,
790	or be brought down by the overbearing suitors.
791	And as much as a lion ponders in a throng of men
792	in fear, when they draw their crafty circle around him,
793	so much was she pondering when sweet sleep came upon her;
794	and she slept, leaning back, and all her limbs were loosened.
795	Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing.
796	She made a phantom, and it was like a woman in form,
797	Iphthime, daughter of great-hearted Icarius,
798	whom Eumelus had married, who had his home in Pherae.
799	And she sent it to the house of divine Odysseus,
800	to make Penelope, mourning and lamenting,
801	cease from her weeping and her tearful lament.
802	It went into the chamber past the strap of the bolt,
803	and it stood above her head and spoke to her:
804	“Are you asleep, Penelope, grieving at heart?
805	The gods who live at ease do not let you
806	weep or grieve, since he will yet come home,
807	your son; for he is no offender against the gods.”
808	Then prudent Penelope answered her,
809	slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams:
810	“Why have you come here, sister? You have not been
811	a visitor before, since you live very far away;
812	and you bid me cease from the misery and the pains,
813	the many pains that vex me in my mind and in my heart,
814	I who first lost my brave, lion-hearted husband,
815	who excelled among the Danaans in every kind of prowess,
816	a brave man, whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos;
817	and now my beloved son has gone off in a hollow ship,
818	a child, knowing nothing well either of toils or of assemblies.
819	For him I mourn even more than for that man;
820	for him I tremble and fear lest something happen to him,
821	either in the land of the people where he has gone, or on the sea;
822	for many enemies are plotting against him,
823	eager to kill him before he reaches his native land.”
824	Answering her, the dim phantom said:
825	“Take heart, and do not be at all too much afraid in your mind;
826	for such an escort goes with him, one whom other men too
827	have prayed to stand beside them, for she has the power,
828	Pallas Athena; and she pities you in your mourning;
829	and she has sent me now to tell you this.”
830	Then prudent Penelope spoke to her in turn:
831	“If you are a god and have heard a god's voice,
832	come, tell me also of that wretched man,
833	whether he still lives somewhere and looks on the light of the sun,
834	or is already dead and in the house of Hades.”
835	Answering her, the dim phantom said:
836	“Of him I will not tell you from beginning to end,
837	whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind.”
838	So saying, it slipped away past the bolt of the doorpost
839	into the blasts of the winds. And she started up out of sleep,
840	the daughter of Icarius; and her dear heart was warmed,
841	so clear was the dream that had rushed upon her in the dead of night.
842	But the suitors embarked and sailed over the watery ways,
843	pondering in their minds sheer slaughter for Telemachus.
844	There is a rocky island in the middle of the sea,
845	between Ithaca and rocky Samos,
846	Asteris, not large; and there are harbors in it where ships can lie,
847	with an entrance on either side; there the Achaeans waited for him, lying in ambush.
