**The second telling.** The eleventh book told the story in Odysseus' account of Chloris and did not name the seer (11.287–297; see the Book 11 note). This book names him in passing, as the ancestor of the seer who comes to Telemachus' ship at Pylos, “and by birth he was a descendant of Melampus” (15.225; see [[melampus]] and [[theoclymenus]]): ἔκγονος is “great grandson”, and Monro refers the reader to Merry's table of the family (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.225; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.281). The relative clause that follows, ὅς, “who”, of Melampus (Monro on 15.226, comparing 11.291), tells the whole story in seventeen verses, from Pylos to Argos.

**Pylos and the flight.** Melampus “once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks, / a rich man among the Pylians, living in a house far above the rest” (15.226–227; see [[pylos]], [[mother-of-flocks]] and [[pylians]]): Πυλίοισι is a locatival dative with μέγʼ ἔξοχα δώματα ναίων, as in ἀγέληφι μέγʼ ἔξοχος of the bull that stands out in the herd (Monro on 15.227, citing *Iliad* 2.480). “But then he came to the land of other men, fleeing his native land / and great-hearted Neleus, the most noble of living men” (15.228–229; see [[neleus]]): ἄλλων δῆμον is Argos, as we learn from 15.238, where the words are taken up again (Monro on 15.228).

**The year.** Neleus “for a full year's round held his many goods / by force” (15.230–231), with the phrase of a full year in the Iliad and in Odysseus' tales (*Iliad* 19.32; 10.467, 14.292). “And all that time, in the halls of Phylacus, / he lay bound in a grievous bond, suffering bitter pains” (15.231–232; see [[phylacus]]). τῆος, “all that time”, joins the two tellings: the year in bonds of the eleventh book, which ended when “the months and the days were being fulfilled / as the year circled round again” (11.294–295), is the year in which Neleus held his goods. The grievous bond, δεσμῷ ἐν ἀργαλέῳ, is the eleventh book's δεσμοί τʼ ἀργαλέοι (11.293), and the phrase comes once more in this book, in the Phoenician nurse's fear of the bond in which her master would bind her (15.443–444; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).

**The blind folly.** He suffered “for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (15.233–234; see [[pero]], [[ate]] and [[erinyes]]). δασπλῆτις is “smiter of houses”, as τειχεσιπλήτης is a stormer of walls, the first part of the compound being probably from the root of δεσ- in δεσπότης, “house-master” (Monro on 15.234; see the translation's key-word row for δασπλῆτις Ἐρινύς). In the eleventh book it was “a hard fate of a god” that fettered him (11.292); here an Erinys puts ἄτη in his mind, as Agamemnon says that Zeus and Fate and the Erinys “cast savage blind folly into my mind” in the assembly (*Iliad* 19.87–88). The poem does not say what the folly was.

**The vengeance.** “But he escaped his doom and drove the loud-bellowing cattle / to Pylos from Phylace, and took vengeance for the shameful deed / on godlike Neleus, and for his brother he brought the woman / home” (15.235–238; see [[cattle]], [[loud-bellowing]], [[phylace]] and [[poine]]). ἐριμύκους, “loud-bellowing”, is found elsewhere in Homer only twice, of oxen in the Iliad (*Iliad* 20.497, 23.775). The cattle are those of Iphiclus, who is not named here (11.289–290; see [[iphiclus]]); the brother is named in neither book, and Merry names him Bias (on 11.281; see [[bias]]).

**Argos.** “And he himself came to the land of other men, / to horse-pasturing Argos; for there it was his lot / to dwell, ruling over many Argives” (15.238–240; see [[argos]], [[horse-pasturing]] and [[argives]]): ἀνάσσοντʼ is the accusative ἀνάσσοντα, agreeing with the subject of ναιέμεναι (Monro on 15.240). There he married and built a high-roofed house and begot two mighty sons, Antiphates and Mantius (15.241–242; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]] and [[mantius]]), and the genealogy goes on through both of them to Amphiaraus and to [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] (15.243–256; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]], [[polypheides]] and [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]]). How Melampus came to rule in Argos, by curing its women of their madness for a share of the kingship, is a later story, which Herodotus tells: the Argives sent to Pylos for him, and he asked half the kingship for himself and a third for his brother Bias (Herodotus 9.34.1–2; see the general article of [[melampus]]).
