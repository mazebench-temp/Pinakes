---
summary: The center of Athena's first reason for Telemachus' return (15.10–26): his mother is pressed to marry Eurymachus, and a woman who remarries forgets the house and the children of her first husband. Aristophanes rejected the verse before it, and Monro defends it; some ancient critics rejected the three after it.
# The maxim's verse is the entry's form (15.20); 15.21-23 unfold it, 15.16-19 give the occasion.
refs: [15.16-19, 15.21-23]
---

**The occasion.** Athena's speech begins in the words of Nestor's warning at Pylos, that it is no longer good to wander far from home while overbearing men devour his possessions (15.10–13; 3.313–316; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Then she names the danger at home: “For already her father and her brothers are bidding her / marry Eurymachus; for he surpasses all / the suitors in his gifts, and has increased his bride-price greatly” (15.16–18; see [[eurymachus]], [[icarius]] and [[bride-price]]). Only here does the poem give Penelope brothers, and at the end of the book Telemachus himself calls Eurymachus the best man, and “most of all he is eager / to marry my mother and to hold the prize of honor of Odysseus” (15.521–522; see [[marriage]] and [[geras]]). The maxim turns the pressure into a threat to the house: “beware lest against your will she carry some possession from the house” (15.19; see [[possessions]]). φέρηται is “carry with her”, “carry off” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.19).

**The critics.** Aristophanes is said to have rejected 15.19 ἐπὶ σμικρολογίᾳ, for the meanness to which it appeals; Monro answers that the verse is necessary to the next sentence, οἶσθα γάρ, which gives its reason, and that “the ancient critics were apt to judge Homeric sentiment from their own standard” (Monro on 15.19). Verses 24–26, the advice to entrust everything to the best of the slave women “until the gods show you a glorious wife”, were rejected by some ancient critics because they lead to nothing in the story (Monro on 15.24–26); ἐπιτρέψειας is a polite imperative, “I would have you entrust” (see the translation's note on 15.19, 15.24–26, 15.91). The text prints τεθνηκότος at 15.23, where Monro has τεθνηότος (see the translation's note on 15.23).

**What follows.** Telemachus does not answer the goddess. He wakes Peisistratus “stirring him with his heel” and asks for the horses at once, in the night (15.44–47; see [[peisistratus]]). The next time he speaks of his mother's marriage, it is to send a stranger away from her house to the house of Eurymachus (15.513–522; see [[theoclymenus]]).
