**Awake at Sparta.** Athena goes to Lacedaemon “to the shining son of great-hearted Odysseus, / to remind him of his homecoming and to urge him to return” (15.2–3; see [[athena]] and [[phaidimos]]). She finds both young men asleep in the porch, and then the poet corrects himself: “but sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus, but in his heart / through the ambrosial night cares for his father kept him awake” (15.7–8; see [[sweet-sleep]] and [[ambrosial]]). The apparent contradiction is the manner of epic narration, as with Zeus asleep and not asleep at the turn of *Iliad* 1 and 2, and μελεδήματα πατρός are “his anxious thoughts about his father” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.4–7, 15.8; see the translation's note on 15.1–7). She stands beside him, awake, and speaks:

{{quote:5a4a14c6-c614-53a2-9e28-1de8f2c9d2e6}}

τῆλε, “far”, is perhaps said with allusion to his name (Monro on 15.10; see the entry), and the verses that follow are Nestor's warning at Pylos almost word for word (15.11–13 ≈ 3.314–316). Penelope's father and brothers, she says, urge her to marry Eurymachus, who outdoes the suitors in gifts, and a woman forgets her first husband and her children (15.16–23; see [[eurymachus]], [[icarius]], [[penelope]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). Let him trust his house to the best of the slave women until the gods show him a wife (15.24–26). The best of the suitors lie in ambush for him in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos, eager to kill him, “But I do not think it will be so” (15.28–32; see [[ambush]] and [[samos]]): the verses in which she told Odysseus of the same ambush on the night he came home (15.30–32 = 13.426–428). He is to keep his ship far from the islands, sail by night as well, and land at the first shore of Ithaca and go first to the swineherd, again in the words she spoke to his father, “who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike” (15.33–39, the last two verses ≈ 13.404–405; Monro on 15.33, 15.34; see the translation's note on 15.33–34, and [[eumaeus]]).

**The leave-taking.** He wakes Peisistratus, “stirring him with his heel”, and is told to wait for dawn and for the gifts of the host, “For a guest remembers all his days / the host who has shown him friendship” (15.44–55; Monro on 15.45; see [[peisistratus]] and [[a-guest-remembers-his-host]]). At dawn he dresses and goes out to Menelaus, “Menelaus, son of Atreus, nurtured by Zeus, leader of the people, / send me away now to my dear native land” (15.59–66; see [[dressing-scene]], [[menelaus]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]). Menelaus will not keep him against his will, since a host who loves or hates beyond measure is as wrong as one who holds back a guest or hurries him away (15.68–74; see [[what-is-due-is-better]] and [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]]), but he offers a meal and a tour “through Hellas and the heart of Argos”, from which no host would send them away without a gift (15.75–85; see [[hellas]] and the translation's note on 15.78–85). Telemachus declines: he left no one to watch over his possessions, and fears to lose either his life or some treasure while he looks for his father (15.87–91). οὖρον is “watcher”, like the singer whom Agamemnon left in charge of his wife (Monro on 15.89; 3.267; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]).

**The gifts.** Menelaus gives him a two-handled cup and the silver mixing bowl with rims of gold, “the work of Hephaestus”, the gift of Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, which he promised at their first parting (15.111–122; 15.113–119 = 4.613–619; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[cup]] and [[phaedimus]]). Helen gives him the robe she made herself, “a remembrance of Helen's hands, for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear; but until then let it lie with your dear mother / in the hall” (15.123–129; see [[helen]], [[robe]] and [[guest-gifts]]). “So saying, she put it in his hands, and he received it with joy” (15.130).

**The omen.** At the parting cup he answers Menelaus' greeting to Nestor: would that he might find Odysseus at home and tell him so, “as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness” (15.155–159; Monro on 15.156–158; see the translation's note on 15.156–158). As he speaks, an eagle flies by on the right with a white goose from the court in its talons, and Helen reads it before Menelaus can: Odysseus will come home and take vengeance (15.160–178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]], [[eagle]] and [[geese]]). His answer is the verse with which his father took leave of Nausicaa: “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.180–181 ≈ 8.465, 8.467; see [[nausicaa]]).

**Not to Nestor's house.** They drive to Pherae and spend the night with Diocles, and reach Pylos the next day, in the verses of the outward journey (15.182–193; 15.184–192 ≈ 3.486–494, Monro on 15.184–192; see [[diocles]] and [[chariot-journey]]). There he asks a favor:

{{quote:00e6c7ca-4c9d-5dae-b044-ab4e2cf6b84d}}

ὁμοφροσύνῃσιν ἐνήσει, “will bring us still further into oneness of mind” (15.198; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]), and παρέξ, “past”, shows, as Strabo points out, that the city of Pylos lay some way inland (Monro on 15.199; see [[pylos]] and [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]). Peisistratus agrees, takes out the gifts at the stern of the ship, and bids him embark before he himself reaches home: the old man's “overweening spirit” would not let him go (15.202–214; see [[nestor]]).

**The seer.** He bids his companions put the arms in order in the ship, τὰ τεύχεα being the arms and not the rigging (15.217–219; Monro on 15.218; see the translation's note on 15.218), and he is praying and sacrificing to Athena at the stern when the fugitive seer Theoclymenus comes to him (15.222–264; see [[theoclymenus]] and [[sacrifice]]). Asked who he is, he answers with his father's name, “if that was ever so; but now he has perished by a grim destruction” (15.267–268): εἴ ποτʼ ἔην is an assurance, “as surely as there was a Ulysses”, not a doubt (Monro on 15.268; see the translation's note on 15.268). He takes the suppliant aboard, “and there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have”, lays his spear on the deck and seats him beside himself in the stern (15.280–286; see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]).

**Past the ambush.** He bids the crew lay hold of the tackle, they raise the mast and hoist the sails, and Athena sends a following wind, in the verses of the outward launch (15.287–294; 15.287–292 ≈ 2.420–426; see [[sailing-type-scene]]). They pass Cruni and Chalcis, Pheae and Elis at sunset (15.295–298; see [[cruni]], [[chalcis-in-elis]], [[pheia]] and [[elis]]), “And from there he sent her on toward the pointed islands, / pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.299–300; see [[the-pointed-islands]]). Strabo took θοαί for “sharp”, the islands for some of the Echinades, and the course for a turn to the east that brought the ship round Ithaca to land on the far side of the island, not in the strait the suitors guarded (*Geography* 8.3.26; see [[strabo]]); Monro finds no ground for the Echinades, leaves θοῇσιν unexplained, and takes “the islands” for the group of Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus (Monro on 15.299–300; see the translation's note on 15.295–300). The suitors' ship comes home empty in the next book (16.342–370).

**In the hut.** While he sails, his father and Eumaeus talk through the night, and he is in their talk: “when the dear son of Odysseus comes, / he will clothe you in a cloak and a tunic as clothing, / and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (15.337–339 = 14.515–517), and the stranger agrees to wait for him (15.346; see [[eumaeus]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The landing.** At dawn the companions land on Ithaca and eat (15.495–501; see [[mooring-stones]]). He sends them on to the city with the ship, and will go himself to the fields and the herdsmen and come down to the city in the evening, and give them a feast at dawn as a reward for the voyage (15.503–507); he does not in fact return till the next day (Monro on 15.505; see the translation's note on 15.505). When the seer asks where he is to go, he has no house to offer, since he will be away and his mother does not show herself to the suitors, and names Eurymachus, until the falcon flies on the right and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] reads it for his house; then he gives the seer to Peiraeus (15.508–546; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]], [[eurymachus]] and [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]).

**To the fold.** “And Telemachus bound under his feet his fine sandals, / and he took a strong spear, tipped with sharp bronze, / from the deck of the ship” (15.550–552; see [[sandals]] and [[spear]]). The second verse is Athena's when she arms for Ithaca (1.99), and the Iliad's when Nestor arms, twice, and when Teucer puts away his bow for a spear (*Iliad* 10.135, 14.12, 15.482). The spear is not the seer's, which he laid on the deck at Pylos (15.282–283), as Ameis supposed, but his own, which he carries elsewhere too (2.10; Monro on 15.551); W. W. Merry had taken it for the same spear, laid down on the deck and taken up again (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §5). The ship pushes off, “and his feet carried him quickly on as he strode, until he came to the fold, / where his countless swine were, among which the swineherd, / being good, used to sleep, kind to his masters” (15.555–557; see [[fold]] and the translation's note on 15.555). There the next book begins.
