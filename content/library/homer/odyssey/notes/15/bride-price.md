**Eurymachus' gifts.** “For already her father and her brothers are bidding her / marry Eurymachus; for he surpasses all / the suitors in his gifts, and has increased his bride-price greatly” (15.16–18; see [[eurymachus]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). περιβάλλει … δώροισι, “surpasses … in his gifts”, and ἐξώφελλεν ἔεδνα, “has increased his bride-price”, are words of a contest of offers (see the translation's table of key words), the contest Antinous will propose, that each suitor woo her with gifts from his own house and she marry the man who gives the most (16.390–392, a plain rendering). The gifts are the suitor's, given to win the bride; but it is her father and brothers who press her to take the highest bidder, the family who in the first book were to arrange the ἔεδνα if she went back to her father's house (1.275–278; 2.52–54; see [[icarius]]). The two kinds of gift that the entry sets apart meet here in one house.

**Ctimene.** Eumaeus, reared with Laertes' youngest daughter, tells how her marriage was made: “But when we both came to the lovely prime of youth, / they gave her in marriage to Same, and got countless gifts” (15.366–367; see [[ctimene]] and [[marriage]]). μυρία, “countless”, is the word of the Iliad's formula for a man winning a bride, ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα, “after giving a countless bride-price” (*Iliad* 16.190, 22.472; also 11.282), seen here from the side of the parents who receive it. The same day the swineherd was given a cloak, a tunic and sandals and sent out to the farm (15.368–370; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).
