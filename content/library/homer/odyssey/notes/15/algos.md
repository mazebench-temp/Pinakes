summary: Melampus, bound in Phylacus' halls, “suffering bitter pains” (15.232); the wanderer's “evil sorrows” for the sake of the belly (15.344–345); the two men in the hut who “take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them; for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows” (15.399–400); and Odysseus' answer to Eumaeus' story, “all the sorrows you have suffered in your heart” (15.487).
# κήδεσιν (15.399; again 19.378), first met here among the Odyssey's notes, beside κήδεα and κήδεʼ of the Book 5 note.
grc: [κήδεσιν]
en: [sorrow]

**Bitter pains.** The story of Melampus is told in a few verses: while Neleus held his goods, “in the halls of Phylacus, / he lay bound in a grievous bond, suffering bitter pains / for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (κρατέρʼ ἄλγεα πάσχων, 15.231–234; see [[melampus]], [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]], [[ate]] and [[erinyes]]). The ancestor of the seer who boards Telemachus' ship was himself an exile from his native land (15.228; see [[exile-and-refuge]]), and suffered for a woman's sake (15.233).

**The wanderer's sorrows.** Odysseus thanks his host for keeping him: “There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming; / but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows, / the men whom wandering and bane and sorrow come upon” (κακὰ κήδεʼ … ἄλη καὶ πῆμα καὶ ἄλγος, 15.343–345; see [[gaster]], [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]] and [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]]).

**Sorrows remembered.** Eumaeus turns the night into an exchange of sorrows: “But let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut, / take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them; for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows, / whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (κήδεσιν ἀλλήλων τερπώμεθα λευγαλέοισι, 15.398–401; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]], [[we-will-remember-these-things-too]] and [[a-time-for-many-words]]). μνωομένω, “as we remember them”, is from μνάομαι, “call to mind” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.400). The verses are the ones Aristotle quotes, in a form that differs from our text, for the pleasure of remembering what one has suffered (*Rhetoric* 1370b5–6). At the end of the story Odysseus answers in the same word: “Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me / by telling all these things, all the sorrows you have suffered in your heart” (15.486–487; see [[thymos]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), where ἄλγεα, though it stands in the relative clause, properly belongs to the antecedent, “all these things … all the sorrows you have suffered” (Monro on 15.487). Then he sets the good beside the sorrow: Zeus brought the stolen child to a kind master (15.488–491; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]).
