# The entry's Ἐρινυ* claims Ἐρινύς (15.234).
---

**The house-smiting Erinys.** The seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] is brought in with his descent, and the poet tells again the story of his ancestor Melampus, who left Pylos, where Neleus held his goods by force: “And all that time, in the halls of Phylacus, / he lay bound in a grievous bond, suffering bitter pains / for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (15.231–234; see [[melampus]], [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]], [[pero]], [[phylacus]] and [[ate]]). The last verse is the verse in which a goddess puts a thought into a hero's mind: “for the white-armed goddess Hera had put it in his mind” to call the assembly of *Iliad* 1, and Athena so guides Odysseus in the sea and Penelope in the palace (*Iliad* 1.55; 5.427, 18.158, 21.1). Here the goddess is the Erinys, and what she puts there is ἄτη, “blind folly”, as Agamemnon blamed “Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind” (*Iliad* 19.87–88; see [[who-walks-in-darkness]] and the general article). W. W. Merry cites the two passages together for the blindness of mind that “may be the direct work of some god” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.261). For Merry the Erinys “does not seem originally to be much more than the solemnly uttered evil wish of a deeply injured person”, as in Telemachus' fear of his mother's curse (2.135), and he sets this verse against his own view, “though we have θεὰ δασπλῆτις” in it, the Erinys called a goddess (Merry on 1.241; see the Book 2 note). In Book 11 the same captivity was the work of “a hard fate of a god”, and when Iphiclus let him go “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (11.291–297; see the Book 11 note on [[melampus]]). The poem does not say why the Erinys sent him folly.

**δασπλῆτις.** The epithet is found only here. D. B. Monro renders it “smiter of houses”, comparing τειχεσιπλήτης, “stormer of walls”, the title of Ares (*Iliad* 5.31), and takes the first part of the compound to be probably from the root of δεσ- in δεσπότης, “house-master” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.234; see the translation's key-word row). The Suda glosses the accusative δασπλῆτα as “she who draws near for harm”, and adds, “for the Erinys is δασπλῆτις” (Suda δ 85).
