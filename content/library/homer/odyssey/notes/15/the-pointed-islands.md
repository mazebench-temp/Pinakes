---
summary: After Elis the ship is sent on “toward the pointed islands”, its last landmark before the dawn landing on Ithaca, while Telemachus ponders “whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.299–300); Strabo makes the islands the Oxeiai among the Echinades and the course a detour round the suitors' ambush, and D. B. Monro takes them for Ithaca's own neighbors.
---

**Far from the islands.** Athena's warning at Sparta sets the course: the suitors lie in ambush “in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos”, “But keep your well-made ship far from the islands, / and sail by night as well” (28–34; see [[athena]], [[asteris]] and [[ambush]]). ἑκὰς νήσων means keeping close to the mainland: the islands meant are above all Cephallenia, the poem's Samos, since the suitors waited between it and Ithaca, but the phrase regularly denotes the islands named with Ithaca, Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus, which the poem places “toward horse-pasturing Elis” (21.347, a plain rendering) and which had to be passed (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.33); ὁμῶς is “as well”, by night as by day (Monro on 15.34; see the translation's note on 15.33–34).

**Toward the pointed islands.** “And from there he sent her on toward the pointed islands, / pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (299–300; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[cruni]]). The second verse is close to the one in which Penelope, the night the suitors set out, lay without food “pondering whether her noble son would escape death” (4.789; see [[penelope]]). Monro prints the optative ἁλοίη, from one manuscript, for the vulgate's ἁλώῃ (his critical note on 15.300). Then the poem leaves the ship at sea until the dawn of the next day, when the companions take down the mast “by the shore” and row her in to the anchorage (495–498; see [[harbor]] and [[ithaca]]).

**Strabo's detour.** Strabo makes θοαί “sharp” and the islands the Oxeiai among the Echinades: after Elis the ship turned east toward them, away from the strait the suitors watched, passed Ithaca so that it lay behind, and came back between Acarnania and Ithaca to land on the far side of the island (*Geography* 8.3.26, 10.2.19; see [[echinae]] and [[strabo]]). Monro answers: “Plausible as this theory is, it fails to explain the Homeric narrative.” It would leave out the last stage of the journey, from the Echinades home, and the words “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” “evidently apply best to the critical moment when Telemachus was approaching the shores of Ithaca”; and there is no independent ground for Νῆσοι Θοαί as the name of any of the Echinades, “nothing to show that it is more than the ancient geographer's hypothesis” (Monro on 15.299–300).

**Ithaca's islands.** For Monro “the islands” are Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus, as in Telemachus' list of the islands from which the suitors come (1.245–246; see [[dulichium]], [[samos]] and [[zacynthus]]). Athena's command would be kept by giving them “a moderately wide berth: for he trusts rather to darkness … and to landing on Ithaca at a distance from the town”; and Odysseus' own description of Ithaca, lying “farthest out of all / toward the darkness” (9.25–26), “falls in with the view now taken” (Monro on 15.299–300). The ambush failed: in the next book Antinous tells how the suitors' lookouts sat on the heights by day and their ship waited at sea through the nights, “but meanwhile some god has brought him home” (16.364–370, a plain rendering; see [[antinous]]).
