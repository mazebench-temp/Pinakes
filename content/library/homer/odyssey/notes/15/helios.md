summary: The sun sets three times in the verse of nightfall, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark”: at the end of the day's drive to Pherae, on the voyage home past Elis, and in Eumaeus' story, as the nurse leads him to the Phoenicians' ship (15.185, 15.296, 15.471). The disguised Odysseus asks whether his parents still live “under the rays of the sun” (15.349), and Eumaeus' island of Syrie lies “where the turnings of the sun are” (15.404).
# The entry's ἠέλι* claims ἠέλιος (15.185, 15.296, 15.471) and ἠελίοιο (15.349, 15.404).
---

**Sunset.** “And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side. / And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles” (15.184–186 = 3.486–488; see [[sunset-lines]], [[chariot-journey]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]). D. B. Monro calls the verse “obviously archaic and conventional”, used whenever the poet wishes to say what point a journey had reached at sunset, and an instance of “primitive parataxis”: “they trotted along until, at sunset, they reached Pherae” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.184–192). The verse comes again after Athena's wind, on the night voyage past Elis (15.296; see the Book 15 note on [[athena]]), and Monro uses its habit, a journey before it and the stage reached after it, to settle the order of the next verses (Monro on 15.295–298; see [[cruni]]). The third time it is the sunset of Eumaeus' childhood: the nurse has hidden three goblets in her bosom and led the boy out of the house, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and we went quickly and came to the famous harbor” (15.469–472; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]).

**Under the rays of the sun.** The stranger asks Eumaeus about “the mother of divine Odysseus, / and of his father”, “whether they still live somewhere under the rays of the sun, / or are already dead and in the house of Hades” (15.347–350; see [[anticleia]], [[laertes]] and the Book 15 note on [[hades]]). ὑπʼ αὐγὰς ἠελίοιο is the Odyssey's phrase for the world of the living (2.181, 11.498, 11.619; see the Book 11 note), where Penelope's question to the phantom had “looks on the light of the sun” (4.833; see the Book 14 note).

**The turnings of the sun.** Syrie lies “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (15.404; see [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]], [[syrie]] and [[ortygia]]). Monro gives the two islands “a mythical character, as the ‘turning points’ of the sun”, comparing Circe's island, where are “the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4; Monro on 15.403; see [[aeaea]] and [[eos]]).
