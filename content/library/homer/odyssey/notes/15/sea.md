---
summary: The sea is the road home: Athena's wind blows so that the ship “might cross the salt water of the sea” as quickly as possible (15.294); in Eumaeus' story the sea takes the body of his nurse, thrown overboard “to become a spoil for seals and fish” (15.480).
---

**The salt water of the sea.** “And gray-eyed Athena sent them a following wind, / blowing hard and wild through the upper air, so that as quickly as possible / the ship, running, might cross the salt water of the sea” (292–294; see [[athena]], [[the-winds]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). θαλάσσης ἁλμυρὸν ὕδωρ, “the salt water of the sea”, is the verse-end of Charybdis, who “terribly sucked down the salt water of the sea” (12.236, 12.431; see [[charybdis]]); here it is the stretch of water to be got over quickly. The *Hymn to Apollo* has the verse of the Cretan ship (*Hymn to Apollo* 435; see [[cruni]]). ὕδωρ is the object of ἀνύσειε, “might cross”, or perhaps rather of the phrase ἀνύσειε θέουσα, which is equivalent to διαπρήξειε (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.294). The voyage that follows is told by the coast, not the sea: the ship goes past Cruni and Chalcis, makes for Pheae and runs past Elis by night (295–300; see [[cruni]], [[chalcis-in-elis]] and [[the-pointed-islands]]).

**A spoil for seals and fish.** On the seventh day of the Phoenicians' voyage Artemis strikes the nurse, “and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird. / And her, to become a spoil for seals and fish, / they threw overboard” (479–481; see [[like-a-sea-bird]], [[seals]], [[fish]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), and the wind and the water bring the ship to Ithaca (482; see [[ithaca]]).
