---
summary: On the Ithacan shore Theoclymenus reads the falcon in the words of the saying: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.531–532).
---

**The seer's word.** Telemachus has just named Eurymachus, and left it to Zeus whether an evil day will come on the suitors before the marriage, when “a bird flew by on the right, / a falcon, the swift messenger of Apollo”, plucking a dove and pouring its feathers down between the ship and Telemachus (15.523–528; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]] and [[falcon]]). The first verse is that of the eagle at Sparta and of the eagle in the Iliad (15.160; *Iliad* 13.821; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and δεξιός is the lucky direction, from left to right (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.160, 15.525). Theoclymenus takes Telemachus apart and answers with the saying: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.531–532; see [[theoclymenus]] and [[divination]]). Some manuscripts have ἤλυθε, “came”, for ἔπτατο, “flew” (Monro's critical note on 15.531).

**Which god.** In Telemachus' mouth the saying named an unnamed god behind a plan (2.372). Here the god is in the verse before: the bird is the messenger of Apollo, the god who made the seer's father the best of seers (15.252–253; see [[apollo]] and [[polypheides]]). To know the bird for a sign is the seer's first business, as W. W. Merry notes, comparing these verses with Eurymachus' scorn, “not all of them are birds of fate” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.182; see [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]]); and Eurymachus is the man the bird has just answered.
