---
summary: Telemachus throws a great mantle over his shoulders to meet Menelaus at dawn (15.61). In the hut the guest's dress of Book 14 comes back: the suitors' servants are young men well clad in cloaks and tunics (15.331), Telemachus, Eumaeus says, will clothe the beggar in a cloak and a tunic (15.338), and Eumaeus remembers how Anticleia clothed him in a cloak and a tunic and sent him to the farm (15.368–370).
---

**The mantle.** When Telemachus sees Menelaus coming, “in haste he put his glossy tunic about his body, / and threw a great mantle over his massive shoulders” (15.60–61; see [[tunic]], [[glossy]], [[dressing-scene]] and [[telemachus]]). Agamemnon dresses in the same order on the morning of the dream, a soft tunic and then a great φᾶρος, which the Iliad renders “cloak” (*Iliad* 2.42–43); μέγα φᾶρος is also the purple mantle that Odysseus draws over his head to hide his tears among the Phaeacians (8.84; see the Book 8 note).

**The guest's dress.** The cloak and the tunic, the guest's dress that the beggar asked for in Book 14 (see the Book 14 note), are named three times in the hut. Eumaeus warns him that the suitors' servants are “young men, well clad in cloaks and tunics, / their heads and their handsome faces always shining with oil” (15.331–332; see [[the-suitors]]); he should stay, and when Telemachus comes, “he will clothe you in a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (15.337–338 = 14.515–516; see [[clothing]] and [[xenia]]). In Book 14 several manuscripts omit these verses, with a reference to this place (see the translation's note on 14.515–517).

**Anticleia's gift.** Eumaeus was reared with Ctimene, the youngest daughter of the house, and when she was given in marriage to Same, “me she clothed in a cloak and a tunic, clothes / very fine, and gave me sandals for my feet, / and sent me to the farm” (15.368–370; see [[anticleia]], [[ctimene]], [[sandals]] and [[eumaeus]]): the guest's dress, given to the slave boy whom she had brought up with her own child.
