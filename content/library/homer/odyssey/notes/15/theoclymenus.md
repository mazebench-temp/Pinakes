**The suppliant.** At Pylos, while Telemachus prays and sacrifices to Athena beside the stern of his ship (15.222–223; see [[athena]] and [[sacrifice]]), “a man came up close to him, / from a far land, fleeing from Argos, having killed a man, / a seer” (15.223–225; see [[argos]]). The poet stops to give his line, the house of Melampus, before he names him (15.225–255; see [[melampus]]), and comes back to him with “His son it was who came up, and Theoclymenus was his name” (15.256), the son of Polypheides (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.256; see [[polypheides]]). He finds Telemachus “pouring libations and praying beside the swift black ship” (15.258; see [[libation]] and [[prayer]]), and begs him “by the burnt offerings and by the god, and then / by your own head and those of the companions who follow you” to answer the host's questions, “Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?” (15.261–264, the last verse = 1.170; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). Telemachus names his father, “if that was ever so” (15.267–268), an assurance and not a doubt (Monro on 15.268; see [[telemachus]]). Then the stranger tells what he is:

ἔμφυλος, “of my own tribe”, is found only here (see the translation's key words), and τῶν goes with the ὑπ- of ὑπαλευάμενος, “under stress of” them (Monro on 15.275; see the translation's note on 15.275). He is a homicide in flight, like Medon, who lived at Phylace “away from his native land, since he had killed a man” (*Iliad* 13.694–697), and like the Cretan of Odysseus' first false tale, “I am an exile, since I killed the dear son of Idomeneus” (13.259; see [[the-killer-in-exile]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]); the verb is the same, φεύγω, “I am an exile” (15.276). He has come “as a suppliant in my flight” (15.277; see [[supplication]]). Telemachus will not drive him from the ship: “but follow me; and there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have” (15.280–281; see [[xenia]]). He takes the seer's bronze spear and lays it on the deck, goes on board, and seats him beside himself in the stern (15.282–286; see [[deck]] and [[spear]]).

**Where is he to go?** At the landing in Ithaca the seer asks, “And where shall I go, dear child? To whose house shall I come / of the men who rule over craggy Ithaca? / Or shall I go straight to your mother and your house?” (15.509–511; see [[craggy]]). Telemachus would bid him to his own house were things otherwise, but he himself will be away and his mother does not show herself to the suitors (15.513–517; see [[penelope]]), and he names Eurymachus, “whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, the man most eager to marry his mother (15.518–522; see [[eurymachus]]). The name, Monro observes, leads to nothing in the sequel and is “merely intended to give occasion for the prophecy of Theoclymenus” (Monro on 15.519).

**The falcon.** As Telemachus speaks, a falcon, “the swift messenger of Apollo”, flies by on the right, plucking a dove in its feet (15.525–528; see [[falcon]], [[doves]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Theoclymenus calls him apart, clings to his hand and gives the reading: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen. / There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (15.531–534; see [[not-without-a-god]] and [[birds]]). ἔγνων is the aorist of what happens in the moment of speaking, and καρτεροί, “powerful”, is said of rule rather than of strength (Monro on 15.532, 15.534). W. W. Merry sets his first words beside the first business of the seer, to tell the birds that are significant from those that are not (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.182; see [[divination]]). After it, Monro notes, “the resort to Eurymachus is tacitly given up” (Monro on 15.519). Telemachus answers with the wish that Penelope will make to the seer, and later to the stranger, in the same verses: “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled” (15.536–538 = 17.163–165 = 19.309–311; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). He gives him to Peiraeus, “his trusted companion”, to welcome with care and honor until he comes, and Peiraeus answers, “I will take care of this man, and he shall not lack good cheer” (15.539–546; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]] and [[trusted-companion]]). The reading he gives here is guarded; the plain one, that Odysseus is already in Ithaca, he gives Penelope in the palace (17.151–161).
