---
summary: Hesiod's “turnings of the sun” are the solstices of the farmer's year, and Eumaeus' island lies “where the turnings of the sun are”; his rule “neither a man of many guests nor a man of none” is the measure of Menelaus' farewell; his golden race and his just city know no famine, like Syrie; his drones “wear out” the toil of the bees, as the beggar fears to wear out his host; and his farmer ends his days “judging the birds”. The Hesiodic poems also tell of the house of Melampus, whose descendants this book traces.
---

- **The turnings of the sun.** In the Works and Days τροπαὶ ἠελίοιο are the solstices: the farmer who plows “at the turnings of the sun” will reap little; sixty wintry days “after the turnings of the sun” Arcturus rises at dusk; fifty days “after the turnings of the sun”, at the end of the toilsome summer, is the season for sailing (*Works and Days* 479–480, 564–567, 663–665, plain renderings). The phrase is found in Homer only in this book, of Eumaeus' island, “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (ὅθι τροπαὶ ἠελίοιο, 15.404). Monro took Syrie and Ortygia for mythical islands, “the ‘turning points’ of the sun”, as Circe's island has the house of Dawn and the risings of the sun (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.403; see the translation's note on 15.403–404): [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]], [[syrie]] and [[ortygia]].
- **Neither many guests nor none.** “Do not be called a man of many guests, nor a man of none, / nor a companion of the bad, nor a reviler of the good” (μηδὲ πολύξεινον μηδʼ ἄξεινον καλέεσθαι, *Works and Days* 715–716, a plain rendering), the verse that Aristotle quotes as aptly said about hospitality (*Nicomachean Ethics* 1170b20–23; see [[aristotle]]). Menelaus' rule for the host is the same measure: “I am indignant with another / man, a host, who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things” (15.69–71): [[what-is-due-is-better]], [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]] and [[xenia]].
- **No famine.** The golden race lived like gods, without toil or grief, and “wretched old age was not upon them”; “they died as if overcome by sleep”, and the grain-giving earth bore fruit of itself; among men who give straight judgments “famine never attends”, the flocks are heavy with wool, and they do not go on ships, since the earth bears fruit for them (*Works and Days* 112–119, 225–237, plain renderings). Eumaeus' Syrie is “good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat. / Famine never comes upon its people, nor does any / hateful sickness besides fall upon wretched mortals”, and its people, when they grow old, die by the gentle arrows of Apollo and Artemis (15.406–411); Monro speaks of “the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants” (Monro on 15.403). Hesiod's people of the just city do not sail; on Syrie the sailors who come are Phoenicians, and they carry off the king's son (15.415–484): [[syrie]], [[gentle-arrows]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]].
- **The drones.** Gods and men are indignant with the man who lives idle, “like the stingless drones in temper, / who wear out the toil of the bees, eating it without working” (οἵ τε μελισσάων κάματον τρύχουσιν ἀεργοὶ / ἔσθοντες, *Works and Days* 303–306, a plain rendering). Monro sets the verse beside the beggar's offer to leave the hut and beg in the city, “so that I may not wear you and your companions out” (ἵνα μή σε κατατρύχω καὶ ἑταίρους, 15.309; Monro on 15.309). The beggar then offers his work, to split wood, carve and pour wine for the suitors (15.317–324): [[bees]], [[peira]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]].
- **Judging the birds.** The Works and Days ends with the man who knows all these things and “works without blame before the immortals, / judging the birds and avoiding transgressions” (ὄρνιθας κρίνων, *Works and Days* 826–828, a plain rendering). Some, Proclus reports, appended to these verses a *Divination by Birds*, which Apollonius of Rhodes rejected (*Divination by Birds* fr. 1 Evelyn-White, a plain rendering). This book has two such judgments: Helen's of the eagle that carries off a goose, and the seer's of the falcon that plucks a dove (15.160–178, 15.525–534): [[divination]], [[birds]], [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]].
- **The house of Melampus.** In the *Great Eoiae* Melampus was “especially dear to Apollo”, and the young snakes he reared licked his ears and breathed prophecy into him (fr. 12 Evelyn-White, a plain rendering); in this book Zeus and Apollo loved his great-grandson Amphiaraus, and Apollo made Polypheides a seer (15.244–246, 15.252–253). The *Catalogue* names among the suitors of Helen “the two sons of lord Amphiaraus, son of Oicles”, who courted her from Argos (fr. 68 Evelyn-White, a plain rendering), the brothers whom this book names, “His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus” (15.248). Strabo, before he quotes the *Melampodia* on the contest of Calchas and Mopsus at Clarus, says that Calchas came there with Amphilochus, son of Amphiaraus, on the return from Troy, and he reports that Hesiod made Apollo kill Amphilochus at Soli (*Geography* 14.1.27, 14.5.17; *Melampodia* frr. 1 and 8 Evelyn-White): [[melampus]], [[amphiaraus]], [[polypheides]], [[oicles]], [[amphilochus]] and [[alcmaeon]].
- **Dawn's lovers.** Dawn bore Memnon and Emathion to Tithonus, and to Cephalus a son, Phaethon, whom Aphrodite snatched up while he was young and made a keeper of her temple by night (*Theogony* 984–991, plain renderings). In this book “Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (15.250–251): [[dawn-and-orion]], [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]] and [[eos]].
