---
summary: Athena bids Telemachus entrust his goods to the best of his slave women until he has a wife (15.24–26); at Sparta the slave women make ready the parting meal, a handmaid brings water for the hands and the housekeeper the bread (15.93–94, 15.135–139); and in Eumaeus' story the slave women of his father's house handle the trader's necklace (15.461–463).
---

**Until the gods show you a wife.** Athena's warning about Penelope's new marriage ends with advice for the house: “But you should go yourself and entrust each thing / to whichever of the slave women seems to you the best, / until the gods show you a glorious wife” (24–26; see [[telemachus]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). ἐπιτρέψειας is an optative used as a polite imperative, “I would have you entrust”, and some ancient critics rejected the three verses because they lead to nothing in the story (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.24–26; see the translation's note on 15.19, 15.24–26, 15.91).

**At Menelaus' house.** Menelaus bids “his wife and the slave women / make ready a meal in the halls from the plenty within” (93–94; see [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]), and the meal is served in the verses of the first book: “And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it, / a fine golden one, over a silver basin, / for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them. / And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them, / adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had” (135–139 = 1.136–140; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[hand-washing]]). Verse 139, like 63 and 74, is omitted in many manuscripts and bracketed by Monro (see the translation's note on 15.63, 15.74, 15.139).

**In the king's house on Syrie.** When the Phoenician trader comes to Eumaeus' father's house with a golden necklace, “In the hall the slave women and my lady mother / were handling it and gazing at it with their eyes, / offering a price” (461–463; see [[syrie]] and [[necklace]]), while he nods to the nurse, herself a woman bought from pirates (428–429; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
