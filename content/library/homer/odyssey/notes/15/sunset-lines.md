---
summary: The Odyssey's sunset verse three times: at Pherae on the drive back, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and they came to Pherae” (15.185–186); at sea, on the night of the voyage past the ambush (15.296); and in Eumaeus' story, as the nurse leads him to the ship (15.471).
---

D. B. Monro finds the verse “obviously archaic and conventional”: it is always used to say what point in a journey, or in some process, had been reached at sunset, and it sets three statements side by side, “virtually forming a single sentence”: “the horses rattled the yoke—the sun set—and they reached Pherae,” an instance of “primitive parataxis” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.184–192). The same usage decides for him the order of the disputed verses of the voyage. The sunset verse is preceded by an account of the journey, here the wind that Athena sends, and followed by the stage it has reached, so that “Such a line as βὰν δὲ παρὰ Κρουνοὺς κτλ. would come very well after δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος, but not before it,” and he prints 15.295 after 15.297 (Monro on 15.295–298; see the translation's note on 15.295–300, and [[cruni]] and [[chalcis-in-elis]]). Merry notes that with sunset darkness begins at once, with little or no twilight, and that “The simultaneous effect is brought out by the double τε” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.388). In the nurse's story the verse brings the child and his captor to the harbor at nightfall (15.471–473; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]).
