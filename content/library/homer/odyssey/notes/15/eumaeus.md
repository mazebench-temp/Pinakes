**The swineherd in the book.** At Sparta Athena tells Telemachus to go first to the swineherd, “who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike”, and to send him to the city with news for Penelope (15.38–42 ≈ 13.404–405; see [[telemachus]] and [[penelope]]). The scene in the hut that fills the night of the voyage, D. B. Monro observes, “carries on the idyl of Ulysses in the swine-herd's hut, and gives it artistic continuity”, and “fills up the otherwise blank space of the night voyage of Telemachus” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.301–495; see the translation's note on 15.301–495). The book ends where it began for him, at the fold “where his countless swine were, among which the swineherd, / being good, used to sleep, kind to his masters” (15.556–557; see [[fold]]), ἤπια εἰδώς as in Athena's words (15.39).

**The test.** After supper the stranger says that at dawn he will go to the city to beg and to serve the suitors, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay / there at the farmstead, or would urge him on to the city” (15.304–306; see [[peira]] and [[odysseus]]). The poet turns to him, “Then, deeply troubled, you said to him, swineherd Eumaeus” (15.325; see [[apostrophe]] and [[deeply-troubled]]), and he answers that the stranger must long to perish, if he would go among men “whose arrogance and violence reach the iron sky” (15.326–329; see [[the-suitors]]); their servants are not such as he, “but young men, well clad in cloaks and tunics, / their heads and their handsome faces always shining with oil” (15.330–332; Monro on 15.330). “But stay; for no one is vexed by your presence, / neither I nor any other of the companions who are with me” (15.335–336), and Telemachus will clothe him when he comes (15.337–339 = 14.515–517). The stranger blesses him for the rest from wandering, in the verse of the night before (15.341 = 14.440; see [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]]).

**The house of Laertes.** Asked about Odysseus' mother and father, he answers of Laertes, who prays to die, and of Anticleia, who died of grief for her son and who reared him with her daughter Ctimene, clothed him and sent him to the farm, and loved him dearly (15.351–370; see [[laertes]], [[anticleia]] and [[ctimene]]). “But now I lack these things; yet for me myself / the blessed gods make the work grow at which I stay; / from it I have eaten and drunk and given to those I respect” (15.371–373; see [[blessed-gods]]), αἰδοῖοι being those who have a claim on one's regard, family, guests and suppliants (Monro on 15.373; see [[aidos]]). From his mistress there is no kind word now, and the slaves long to speak with her and to take something back to the fields, “such things as always warm the hearts of slaves” (15.374–379; see [[penelope]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

**His own story.** “Ah, so you were still small, swineherd Eumaeus, / when you were driven so far from your native land and your parents” (15.381–382), the stranger says, and asks the two ways by which men became slaves: was his father's city sacked, or did enemies take him alone with the sheep or the cattle and sell him to “this man's house” (15.383–388; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Eumaeus asks for a long hearing, since the nights are endless and much sleep is a vexation, and the two will take delight in each other's sorrows as they remember them (15.390–402; see [[a-time-for-many-words]], [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). Then he tells it: the island Syrie, where no famine or sickness comes; his father Ctesius, king of both its cities; the Phoenician traders and the Phoenician woman in his father's house, who bargained with them for her passage home and paid it with the king's son; how she led him out by the hand, “and I followed in my childish thoughtlessness”; her death at sea; and the ship that the wind and the water carried to Ithaca, “where Laertes bought me with his own possessions. / So it was that I came to see this land with my eyes” (15.403–484; see [[syrie]], [[ctesius]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[phoenicians]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The last verse but one answers the stranger's question of the night before, “My friend, who was it that bought you with his own possessions” (14.115), with the name of the stranger's father.

**The answer.** Odysseus answers that the tale has stirred his heart, “But truly for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil, / since, after toiling much, you came to the house of a man / who is kind, who gives you food and drink / with care, and you live a good life” (15.486–491; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), καί going with ἐσθλόν and κακῷ set between to bring the contrast together (Monro on 15.488; see the translation's note on 15.488). The kind man is the speaker's own father. Then they sleep, “not for long, but a little while; / for soon came Dawn of the fair throne” (15.494–495).
