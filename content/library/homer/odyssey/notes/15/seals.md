---
summary: The Phoenician sailors throw the body of the woman who stole Eumaeus, struck dead by Artemis on the seventh day at sea, overboard “to become a spoil for seals and fish” (15.480–481).
---

**A spoil for seals and fish.** καὶ τὴν μὲν φώκῃσι καὶ ἰχθύσι κύρμα γενέσθαι / ἔκβαλον: “And her, to become a spoil for seals and fish, / they threw overboard” (15.480–481; see [[fish]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The woman has fallen into the bilge “like a sea bird”, struck by Artemis (15.478–479; see [[like-a-sea-bird]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]), and the Phoenicians, who had sworn to take her home unharmed (15.435–438), give her body to the sea. κύρμα γενέσθαι, “to become a spoil”, is the end of the verse of the unburied dead. Aegisthus left the singer of Clytemnestra on a desert island “to become the prey and spoil of birds” (3.271; see [[birds]]), Odysseus, cast ashore on Scheria, feared that he would become the prey and spoil of wild beasts (5.473), and in the Iliad Zeus “hated that he should become a spoil for the dogs of his enemies, / the Trojan dogs”, when the body of Patroclus lay on the field (*Iliad* 17.272–273). There the eaters are the dogs and birds of the land; here they are the beasts of the sea.

**Proteus' flock.** The seals of the Odyssey's fourth book are the flock of the Old Man of the Sea, which comes up out of the gray sea at midday and sleeps on the shore of Pharos (4.400–453; see the Book 4 note and [[proteus]]). This is the only other place in Homer that names them, and the only one where they are eaters of the dead. The fish are the eaters of a body lost at sea in Eumaeus' own fears for his master, “or else the fish have eaten him in the sea” (14.135), and again in Laertes' (24.291); in the swineherd's story it is the woman who stole him who goes to them, while the child is carried on to Ithaca (15.481–483; see [[eumaeus]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]).
