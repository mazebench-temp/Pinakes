---
summary: “Heart and spirit” as a free man's will: Telemachus will send the stranger “wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (15.339), and Eumaeus' men may go to bed, “whoever's heart and spirit bids him” (15.395).
---

The heart appears in the book only in the pair κραδίη θυμός τε. Eumaeus promises the beggar that Telemachus will clothe him “and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (πέμψει δʼ ὅππη σε κραδίη θυμός τε κελεύει, 15.339 = 14.517; see [[pompe]] and [[thymos]]), and at the start of his story lets his men go: “And of the rest, whoever's heart and spirit bids him, / let him go out and sleep” (ὅτινα κραδίη καὶ θυμὸς ἀνώγει, 15.395–396; see [[hypnos]]). The pair is Monro's first example of a hendiadys of nearly synonymous words, in which “The two meanings are fused, as it were, into a single more complete conception” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.175; see [[genee]]).
