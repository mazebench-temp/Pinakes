---
summary: Menelaus keeps the promise he made at the end of Book 4. Megapenthes carries the silver mixing bowl from the treasure chamber, Menelaus tells its story in the verses of the promise, the work of Hephaestus and the gift of Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, and Megapenthes sets it before Telemachus (15.103–104, 15.113–123).
---

**The bowl brought out.** In the chamber where the treasures lie, Menelaus takes a two-handled cup “and bade his son Megapenthes carry a mixing bowl / of silver” (15.103–104; see [[cup]], [[megapenthes]] and [[possessions]]). When they come to Telemachus, he gives the bowl its worth and its history in the verses of his promise in Book 4, “Of the gifts that lie stored as treasures in my house, / I will give you the one that is finest and most precious. / I will give you a well-wrought mixing bowl; of silver / it is all, and its rims are finished with gold, / the work of Hephaestus” (15.113–117 = 4.613–617; see the Book 4 note, [[phaedimus]], [[sidonians]] and [[guest-gifts]]). χρυσῷ δʼ ἐπὶ χείλεα κεκράανται Monro renders “The lips are wrought above”, that is, finished, “with gold” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.116), the half-verse of the silver work basket that Alcandre gave Helen in Egypt (4.131–132; see [[basket]]). Strabo takes “the work of Hephaestus” for praise in excess (*Geography* 1.2.33; see the entry and [[hephaestus]]). The seven verses of the promise are omitted in the manuscripts P and H, and Monro brackets them; Murray prints them (Monro's critical note on 15.113–119; see the translation's note on 15.113–119).

**Set before him.** “So saying, he put the two-handled cup in his hand, / the hero, the son of Atreus; and the shining mixing bowl / strong Megapenthes brought and set before him, / the silver one” (15.120–123; see [[son-of-atreus]], [[phaeinos]] and [[krateros]]). Megapenthes is κρατερός only here and at his wedding in Book 4 (4.11; see the translation's row κρατερὸς Μεγαπένθης). Peisistratus lays the gifts in the wicker body of the chariot and gazes at them all (15.131–132; see [[wagon]] and [[peisistratus]]), and at Pylos he puts “the beautiful gifts” in the stern of Telemachus' ship, “the clothing and the gold” (15.206–207; see [[stern]]).
