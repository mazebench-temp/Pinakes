**Eurymachus.** Telemachus would have taken the stranger to his own house, ἄλλως, “were things otherwise” (Monro: “were it otherwise”), for “there is no lack of good cheer for guests” (15.513–514; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.513; see [[xenia]]). But he will be away, and his mother seldom shows herself to the suitors (15.515–517; see [[penelope]]); so he names Eurymachus, “whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, the best man and the most eager “to marry my mother and to hold the prize of honor of Odysseus” (15.519–522; see [[eurymachus]], [[honored-like-a-god]] and [[geras]]). The introduction of the name “seems at first sight to have no sufficient motive, and to lead to nothing in the sequel”; it gives occasion for the prophecy (Monro on 15.519; see the general article). Telemachus ends with Zeus, who knows “whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (15.523–524): εἴ κε … τελευτήσει is the only instance in Homer of εἴ κε with the future in an object clause, and Monro would read τελευτήσῃ (on 15.524; see the translation's note on 15.524, and [[evil-day]]).

**The sign.** “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right” (15.525 = 15.160): for the verse and for δεξιός, “on the right”, Monro refers back to his note on the eagle at Sparta (on 15.525; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). This bird is κίρκος, “a falcon”, as in the Iliad's chase of the dove and in the verse of the Phaeacian ship that not even a hawk, a falcon, could keep pace with (*Iliad* 22.139; 13.86–87; see the translation's key-word row for κίρκος, and [[falcon]]). It holds the dove ἐν δὲ πόδεσσι, “in its feet”, where the eagle at Sparta carried the goose in its talons, ὀνύχεσσι (15.526; 15.161). It plucks her, τίλλε, and pours the feathers down on the ground “between the ship and Telemachus himself” (15.527–528; see [[doves]]).

**The seer.** [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] calls Telemachus “apart from his companions”, clings to his hand and calls him by name (15.529–530; see [[called-him-by-name]]). “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right” (15.531; see [[not-without-a-god]]): for ἔπτατο, “flew”, three manuscripts and others read ἤλυθε, “came” (Monro's critical note on 15.531). “For I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.532): ἔγνων is the aorist “of what happens in the moment of speaking”, and οἰωνός is formed from the stem of Latin *avis*, “a bird”, with a rare suffix, as in υἱωνός, “grandson”, which “apparently has an ampliative meaning” (Monro on 15.532; see [[birds]] and the translation's key-word row for οἰωνός). The reading is two verses: “There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (15.533–534). For γένος the vulgate has the genitive γένευς, “than your race”, and two manuscripts γένεος, though the contraction of the genitive in -εος “is extremely rare in Homer” (Monro's critical note on 15.533). καρτεροί, “powerful”, is generally used of strength of body, but compare the common use of κρατέω, “bear rule” (Monro on 15.534; see the translation's key-word row for βασιλεύτερος; καρτερός, and [[krateros]]).

**The answer.** “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled” (15.536; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]): for ἔπος τετελεσμένον εἴη, one manuscript has τελέσειε Κρονίων, “may the son of Cronus fulfill”, as at 4.699 and 20.236 (Monro's critical note on 15.536). φιλότητά τε πολλά τε δῶρα, “friendship and many gifts”, is perhaps a hendiadys, “hospitality as shown in many gifts” (Monro on 15.537; see [[guest-gifts]]). The three verses come twice again in Penelope's mouth: to the seer, when he gives her the plain reading of this omen, and to the beggar, when he swears that Odysseus will come (17.163–165, 19.309–311; see [[penelope]]). The resort to Eurymachus is given up without a word, and the seer is entrusted to Peiraeus (15.539–546; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]).

**The plain reading.** In the seventeenth book [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] tells Penelope that Odysseus is already in Ithaca and, in the half-verse of Helen's reading at Sparta, “is planting evil for all the suitors” (17.157–159; 15.178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and that he made out such a bird of omen as he sat on the ship and declared it aloud to Telemachus (17.160–161): ἐγεγώνευν is “declared aloud, made known”, a use common in later poets, especially Aeschylus (Monro on 17.161). One scholium says that the whole passage 17.150–165 was rejected; others, that the better copies rejected only 17.160–161 and the more careless ones the sixteen verses, which Monro takes to mean that the common texts favored rejecting the whole passage but that Aristarchus, led by his own better sources, confined the athetesis to the two verses (Monro's critical note on 17.150–165). In this book the seer declared only the kingship of the house (15.533–534).
