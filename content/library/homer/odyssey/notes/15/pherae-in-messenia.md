---
summary: Driving home from Sparta, Telemachus and Peisistratus come at sunset “to Pherae, to the house of Diocles”, and spend the night there, in the verses of the outward journey (15.182–192; 3.484–494).
---

The return is told in the verses of the outward drive in Book 3 (184–192 = 3.486–494): “And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side. / And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, / son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus begot as his son. / There they spent the night, and he set good cheer before them” (184–188; see [[diocles]], [[ortilochus]], [[alpheus]] and [[chariot-journey]]). Pherae is again the halfway house between Sparta and Pylos, and the next morning, when they have driven out from Diocles' doorway, “quickly then they came to the steep citadel of Pylos” (189–193; see [[pylos]] and [[lacedaemon]]). Diocles' ξείνια, “good cheer”, is the entertainment owed to guests (see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**Until at sunset.** The verse of sunset, δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος σκιόωντό τε πᾶσαι ἀγυιαί, “is obviously archaic and conventional”, D. B. Monro writes, always used to say what point of a journey had been reached at sunset, and the three verses are “an instance of primitive parataxis”: “the horses rattled the yoke—the sun set—and they reached Pherae”, that is, “they trotted along until, at sunset, they reached Pherae” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.184–192; see [[sunset-lines]]). The morning after is the 37th day of the poem (Monro on 15.189). The same verse of sunset comes twice more in the book, at sea and on Eumaeus' island (296, 471; see [[cruni]] and [[syrie]]).
