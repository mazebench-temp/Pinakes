---
summary: The houses of the book: Menelaus' at Sparta, with the porch where his guests sleep and the fragrant chamber of his treasures (15.5, 15.99–113); the house that Penelope may increase for a new husband (15.19–23); the houses of Melampus' line (15.227–241); the high-roofed house of Eumaeus' father on Syrie, from which the nurse leads the child away (15.417–466); and the houses of Ithaca, the house of Odysseus, where the suitors feast, and the houses to which Theoclymenus may go (15.313–375, 15.509–542).
---

**Menelaus' house.** Athena finds the two young men “sleeping in the porch of glorious Menelaus” (15.5; see [[telemachus]] and [[peisistratus]]), the front of the house where Helen had beds made for them on their first night (4.296–303; see the Book 4 note). For the gifts Menelaus goes down “into the fragrant chamber” (15.99; see [[fragrant]]), as Priam did to choose the ransom for Hector (*Iliad* 24.191), and gives from “the gifts that lie stored as treasures in my house” (15.113; see [[possessions]] and [[mixing-bowl]]); then the guests drive out through the gateway and the colonnade of the court (15.146; see [[door]]). Telemachus will not stop at Nestor's, “lest the old man keep me in his house against my will / in his eagerness to make me welcome” (15.200–201; see [[nestor]] and [[xenia]]).

**A wife and her house.** Athena warns Telemachus that his mother may carry some possession “from the house” to a new husband: “she wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her” (15.19–21; see [[penelope]] and [[a-womans-heart]]), and he should entrust everything to the best of the slave women until the gods show him a wife (15.24–26; see [[handmaids]]).

**The houses of Melampus' line.** Melampus once lived at Pylos, “a rich man among the Pylians, living in a house far above the rest”, lay bound “in the halls of Phylacus”, and in Argos “married a wife and built a high-roofed house” (15.227–241; see [[melampus]], [[phylacus]] and [[high-roofed]]).

**The house of Ctesius.** In Eumaeus' story the nurse, asked who she is, points out “the high-roofed house of my father” (15.424; see [[ctesius]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]), the verse in which the daughter of the Laestrygonian king pointed out her father's house (10.111), and the trader answers with the same words, “so that you may see the high-roofed house of your father and mother” (15.432). Monro thinks the repetition intentional, as if the Phoenician said, “You have shown us the ‘great house’ where you are kept in slavery: will you return to the ‘great house’ of your father and mother?” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.424). The messenger with the necklace comes “to my father's house”, and the nurse leads the child “out of the house” by the porch, where the tables and cups of the feast had been taken (15.459–466; Monro on 15.466; see [[necklace]] and [[table]]).

**Houses on Ithaca.** The beggar proposes to go “to the house of divine Odysseus” and serve the suitors (15.313; see [[the-suitors]]), and Eumaeus, speaking of his mistress, says that no kind word or deed comes from her “since evil has fallen on the house, / overbearing men” (15.374–376; see [[penelope]] and [[overbearing]]). When the swineherd has told his story, Odysseus answers that Zeus set good beside the evil for him, since “you came to the house of a man / who is kind” (15.488–490; see [[laertes]], [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). On the shore Theoclymenus asks, “To whose house shall I come / of the men who rule over craggy Ithaca? / Or shall I go straight to your mother and your house?” (15.509–511; see [[theoclymenus]]), and Telemachus, who cannot take him home, gives him to Peiraeus: “now too take this stranger for me to your house, / and welcome him with care and honor him, until I come” (15.542–543; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]).
