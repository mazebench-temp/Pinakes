---
summary: The beggar's belly makes its first appearance in Odysseus' thanks to his host: “There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming; / but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows” (15.343–344).
---

Eumaeus has kept his guest from going to beg in the city, and Odysseus thanks him: “since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery. / There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming; / but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows, / the men whom wandering and bane and sorrow come upon” (ἀλλʼ ἕνεκʼ οὐλομένης γαστρὸς κακὰ κήδεʼ ἔχουσιν, 15.342–345; see [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]], [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]], [[algos]] and [[peira]]). The wanderer of Book 7, who asked to be let eat because the hateful belly “bids me eat and drink” (7.215–221; see the Book 7 note), is now a beggar, and the belly is what sends a beggar on the road. Its epithet is the one the proem gives the wrath, οὐλομένην, “ruinous” (*Iliad* 1.2): οὐλόμενος always means “miserable, accursed”, and in this context it has “a slightly mock-heroic effect” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.344; see the translation's note on 15.344 and [[deadly]]). The beggar will say it again of the belly that cannot be hidden and that got him Antinous' blow, “the ruinous belly, which gives many evils to men” (17.287, 17.474, a plain rendering). Three manuscripts omit 15.345 (Monro's critical note on 15.345).
