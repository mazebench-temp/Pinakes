---
summary: The beggar: “There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming; / but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows” (15.343–344).
grc: [οὐλομένης]
---

οὐλομένης γαστρός is the belly of the beggar's later speeches too (17.287, 17.474). Monro, after Schulze, takes οὐλόμενος as the second aorist participle ὀλόμενος, not transitive: it always means “miserable,” “accursed,” and stands to the curse ὄλοιο as ὀνήμενος, “happy,” to the blessing ὄναιο; the epithet, he says, has here a slightly mock-heroic effect (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.344). The English keeps “ruinous,” as of the wrath in the Iliad's second verse (*Iliad* 1.2; see the translation's note on 15.344 and [[gaster]]). The form, new to the entry, takes in also Agamemnon's “ruinous wife” (4.92).
