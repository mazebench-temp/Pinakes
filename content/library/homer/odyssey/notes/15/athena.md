summary: Athena opens the book. She goes to Lacedaemon to remind Telemachus of his homecoming, finds him lying awake in the porch of Menelaus, and, “Standing near him”, bids him hurry home before his mother is given in marriage, warns him of the suitors' ambush in the strait, tells him to sail by night and to go first to the swineherd, and goes back to Olympus. At Pylos he is praying and sacrificing to her at the stern when the seer Theoclymenus comes up, and on the voyage she sends the ship a following wind (15.292).
# The entry claims Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη (15.1), Ἀθήνη (15.9, 15.292) and Ἀθήνῃ (15.222). Unnamed: her speech (15.10-42);

**To Lacedaemon.** “But Pallas Athena went to Lacedaemon with its wide dancing floors, / to the shining son of great-hearted Odysseus, / to remind him of his homecoming and to urge him to return” (15.1–3; see [[pallas]], [[lacedaemon]], [[with-wide-dancing-floors]] and [[telemachus]]). The verses take up the sentence that ended Book 13, when she left Odysseus on his way to the swineherd: “She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (13.439–440; see the Book 13 note). Since that book ended at nightfall, D. B. Monro counts this one from the next day, the 36th of the poem, and finds that “The chronology is not quite clear” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.1; see the translation's note on 15.1–7). At the first council she meant to go to Ithaca “so that I may urge his son on / the more” and to send him to Sparta and Pylos (1.88–95), and she told Odysseus that she had escorted the boy herself, “so that he might win noble fame / by going there” (13.422–423; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Now she comes to urge him home, ὀτρυνέουσα νέεσθαι (15.3; see [[nostos]]).

**Standing near him.** “She found Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor / sleeping in the porch of glorious Menelaus”, and yet “sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus, but in his heart / through the ambrosial night cares for his father kept him awake” (15.4–8; see [[peisistratus]], [[sweet-sleep]] and [[ambrosial]]). The contradiction is only apparent, Monro says, like the famous one about Zeus, asleep at the end of *Iliad* 1 and awake at the beginning of *Iliad* 2: “In both places the difficulty lies in the epic style of narration” (Monro on 15.4–7; *Iliad* 1.611, 2.2). The voyage began with a night like it, when Telemachus lay awake and “planned in his mind the journey that Athena had shown him” (1.443–444). “Standing near him, gray-eyed Athena spoke to him” (15.9; see [[gray-eyed]] and [[divine-epiphany]]) is the verse with which she came to Odysseus by the ships in the Iliad, when he stood grieving after Agamemnon's test of the army (*Iliad* 2.169–172). There is no dream in the book. To the sleeping Penelope she sent a phantom in the likeness of her sister, and she came to the sleeping Nausicaa in the likeness of a friend (4.795–841, 6.20–40; see [[dream]] and [[eidolon]]); here the poem gives her no likeness, and Telemachus is awake. He does not answer her, and the poem does not say whether he knew her: he wakes Peisistratus at once, “stirring him with his heel” (15.44–47). She comes to a wakeful man again in Book 20, to Odysseus lying awake in his own house on the night before the slaughter, and there the poem gives her a form: “and she was like a woman in form; / and she stood above his head and spoke to him” (20.31–32, a plain rendering).

**The warning.** Her speech begins with Nestor's advice at Pylos: “Telemachus, it is no longer good for you to wander far from your home, / leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house / so overbearing” (15.10–12; 15.10–13 ~ 3.313–316; see [[nestor]] and [[overbearing]]). Then the reasons. Penelope's father and brothers are pressing her to marry Eurymachus, who surpasses the other suitors in his gifts (15.16–18; see [[eurymachus]], [[icarius]] and [[bride-price]]), and she may carry some possession from the house, “For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast” (15.19–23; see [[a-womans-heart]] and [[penelope]]). Aristophanes is said to have rejected the first of these verses “on account of the meanness to which it appeals”; Monro keeps it, since the next sentence needs it, and adds that “The ancient critics were apt to judge Homeric sentiment from their own standard” (Monro on 15.19). The suitors lie in ambush for him “in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos” (15.28–30; see [[ambush]] and [[the-suitors]]), the place Antinous chose in Book 4 (4.669–672), and he must keep his ship far from the islands and sail by night, “and he will send you a fair wind behind you, / whoever of the immortals guards and protects you” (15.33–35; see [[the-winds]] and [[immortals]]). The goddess speaks of the god who guards him as of another, as at Pylos, in the likeness of Mentor, she prayed to Poseidon for a safe return for Telemachus and herself, “and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.60–62; see the Book 3 note).

**The same words to father and son.** She sends the son where she has sent the father. “but you yourself go first of all to the swineherd, / who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike” (15.38–39) are the verses with which she sent Odysseus to Eumaeus at the harbor (13.404–405; see [[eumaeus]]), and the ambush and her confidence about it were told to him in the verses she uses now: “But I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (15.31–32 = 13.427–428). The swineherd is to take the news of his safe return to Penelope (15.40–42; see [[periphron]]). So the book's two threads, the voyage home and the night in the hut, run toward the meeting she has arranged, when Telemachus comes to the swineherd's door at the beginning of Book 16 (16.4–12; see [[odysseus]]). Then “So saying, she went away to high Olympus” (15.43; see [[olympus]] and [[high-olympus]]), with the verse-end that takes Hermes back to Olympus from Priam and from Odysseus (*Iliad* 24.468, 24.694; 10.307; see [[hermes]]).

**The sacrifice at the stern.** At Pylos, while the companions go on board, Telemachus “was busy with these things, and was praying and sacrificing to Athena / beside the stern of the ship” (15.222–223; see [[sacrifice]] and [[prayer]]), and there the seer Theoclymenus finds him “pouring libations and praying beside the swift black ship” and begs him “by the burnt offerings and by the god” (15.258–261; see [[theoclymenus]], [[libation]] and [[supplication]]). When the voyage began, the crew poured libations to the gods, “and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.432–433; see the Book 2 note). The words of this prayer are not told.

**The wind.** When the mast is up and the sails are hoisted, “gray-eyed Athena sent them a following wind, / blowing hard and wild through the upper air, so that as quickly as possible / the ship, running, might cross the salt water of the sea” (15.292–294; see [[sailing-type-scene]], [[upper-air]] and [[the-winds]]). The first verse is the wind of the outward voyage, where “a fresh West Wind” followed (2.420–421; see [[zephyrus]] and the Book 2 note), and of Apollo's wind for the ship from Chryse (*Iliad* 1.479). For the launching Monro sends the reader to the notes on Book 2 (Monro on 15.287–292), where W. W. Merry takes ἴκμενος, a word used only of the fair wind, for an “accompanying” or “following” wind (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.420; see the translation's key-word row). λάβρον ἐπαιγίζοντα, “blowing hard and wild”, is said of the West Wind that comes upon a deep field of grain in the Iliad (*Iliad* 2.147–148); Aristophanes read ἐπαΐσσοντα, “rushing” (Monro's critical note on 15.293). The second and third verses return, in the nominative and with ἐξ αἰθέρος, in the *Hymn to Apollo*, of the great West Wind that comes to the Cretans' ship “by the decree of Zeus” (ἐκ Διὸς αἴσης, *Hymn to Apollo* 433–435), and a few verses on Telemachus' ship runs “driven on by the wind of Zeus” (15.297; see [[zeus]]). So she keeps her own promise of a fair wind from “whoever of the immortals guards and protects you” (15.34–35), and the ship passes the ambush. In the next book the ambushers come home, and Antinous says that “some god has brought him home” (16.370, a plain rendering).
