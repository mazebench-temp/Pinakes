summary: As Telemachus takes leave of Menelaus at Sparta, an eagle flies by on the right, “carrying in its talons a huge white goose, / a tame one from the court”, and darts across in front of the horses; Helen reads the sign: as the eagle, “coming from the mountain, where he was born and bred”, snatched the goose reared in the house, so Odysseus will come home and take vengeance (15.160–178).
# The eagle without its name: “coming near them it darted / to the right” (15.163–164), ὅδε, “this eagle” (15.174), and
# the bird “coming from the mountain” (15.175).
refs: [15.163-164, 15.174-175]
---

**The prey in its talons.** αἰετὸς ἀργὴν χῆνα φέρων ὀνύχεσσι πέλωρον, / ἥμερον ἐξ αὐλῆς: “an eagle, carrying in its talons a huge white goose, / a tame one from the court” (15.161–162; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]], [[geese]] and the translation's row ἀργὴ χήν; πέλωρος; ἥμερος). φέρων ὀνύχεσσι πέλωρον is the half-verse of the eagle of the Iliad's portent at the ditch, “carrying in its talons a blood-red serpent, monstrous” (*Iliad* 12.202; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]): there πέλωρον is the serpent, here the goose. Two eagles of the Iliad's omens carry wild prey, the serpent and a fawn, “the young of a swift deer” (*Iliad* 8.248), and the two eagles of Ithaca in Book 2 carry nothing and turn their talons on each other (2.153; see the Book 2 note). This one has taken a farmyard bird: ἥμερος, “tame”, is found only here in Homer, and the goose comes ἐξ αὐλῆς, “from the court”. The household runs after it: “and they followed, shouting, / men and women” (15.162–163).

**On the right.** The bird comes near the company and “darted / to the right, in front of the horses” (15.163–164; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The verse that brings it, “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right”, is the verse of the eagle that answered Ajax in the Iliad (15.160 = *Iliad* 13.821), and the gladness that follows is the gladness of Troy when Zeus sent Priam his eagle: “and when they saw it / they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (15.164–165 ~ *Iliad* 24.320–321; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]). D. B. Monro takes δεξιός as “from left to right”, so that the bird crossed the path of the chariot in the lucky direction (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.160; see [[birds]] and [[divination]]). Priam asked for his sign, “a bird of omen, your swift messenger, the one that to you yourself / is dearest of birds” (*Iliad* 24.310–311); this one comes unasked, at a parting, and Peisistratus asks Menelaus whether the god has shown it to the two young men or to him (15.167–168; see [[peisistratus]] and [[teras]]).

**Helen's reading.** While Menelaus ponders, Helen reads the sign: “As this eagle snatched the goose that was being reared in the house, / coming from the mountain, where he was born and bred, / so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (15.174–178; see [[helen]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The eagle is Odysseus; the goose she leaves unnamed, and her last words are of the suitors. γενεή τε τόκος τε, “where he was born and bred”, is a hendiadys of two nearly synonymous words fused into one conception (Monro on 15.175; see the translation's note on 15.175). The eagle's home is the mountain. Zeus sent the two eagles of the Ithacan assembly “from the peak of a mountain” (2.147), and in Penelope's dream an eagle with a curved beak comes from the mountain and kills her twenty geese, and then tells her, “The geese are the suitors, and I was the eagle” (19.535–550, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]] and [[geese]]).

**The eagle and the falcon.** Near the end of the book the same verse brings a second bird, a falcon with a dove in its feet, which flies by as Telemachus lands on Ithaca and which the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] reads (15.525–534; see [[falcon]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). The eagle, the bird of Zeus, comes as Telemachus leaves Sparta (see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and Apollo's falcon as he reaches Ithaca. The last eagle of omen that the poem tells flies on the left of the suitors as they plot his death, “an eagle flying high, and it held a timid dove” (20.242–243, a plain rendering; see [[doves]]).
