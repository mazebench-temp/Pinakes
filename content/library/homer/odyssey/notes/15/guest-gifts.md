summary: Menelaus offers a tour of the cities of Hellas and Argos, each of which would give a tripod, a cauldron, two mules or a golden goblet (15.80–85), and gives the finest thing in his house, the silver mixing bowl that Phaedimus of Sidon gave him, with a two-handled cup; Helen gives a robe of her own making (15.99–130). The gifts are stowed in the ship's stern at Pylos (15.206–207); Diocles sets good cheer before the travelers at Pherae (15.188); and Telemachus promises Theoclymenus friendship and many gifts (15.536–538).

**Gifts promised.** Menelaus' first answer to the request to go home is a gift: “But wait until I bring gifts and set them in the chariot, / fine ones, and you see them with your eyes” (15.75–76; see [[pompe]]). Then he offers more. If Telemachus will turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos, Menelaus will go with him, and no one will send them away “just as we came”, without any present (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.83), but each will give at least one thing, “either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron, / or two mules or a golden goblet” (15.83–85; see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]], [[mules]] and [[cup]]). These are the gifts Menelaus himself brought home from his wanderings, as Polybus of Egyptian Thebes gave him silver bathtubs, two tripods and ten talents of gold (4.125–129; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Aristarchus obelized 15.78–85 (Monro's critical note), and Telemachus declines the tour (15.86–91; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]).

**Gifts given.** From the fragrant chamber where the treasures lie, Menelaus takes a two-handled cup and bids Megapenthes carry a silver mixing bowl, and Helen takes the largest and most beautiful of the robes she had made, which “shone like a star” (15.99–108; see [[megapenthes]], [[robe]] and [[like-a-star]]). Menelaus gives “the one that is finest and most precious”, the bowl that Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, gave him “when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (15.113–119 = 4.613–619; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[phaedimus]] and [[sidonians]]): a gift with a history, passed on to the son of a guest-friend. The seven verses are omitted in two manuscripts and bracketed by Monro (see the translation's note on 15.113–119). Helen's gift is of another kind, her own work: “I too give you this gift, dear child, / a remembrance of Helen's hands, for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear”, and until then it is to lie with his mother (15.125–128; see [[helen]] and [[a-guest-remembers-his-host]]). Peisistratus lays them in the chariot “and gazed at them all in his heart” (15.131–132), that is, in Monro's words, “with all his thoughts centred upon them” (Monro on 15.132).

**On the road.** At Pherae Diocles “set good cheer before them” (15.188 = 3.490; see [[diocles]]): ξείνια here is the meal of a guest. At Pylos Peisistratus takes out the gifts and puts them in the stern of Telemachus' ship, “the clothing and the gold, which Menelaus had given him” (15.206–207); ἐξαίνυτο is used pregnantly, “took out (and placed)” (Monro on 15.206). The gifts go ashore on Ithaca to the house of [[clytius-father-of-peiraeus|Clytius]], and Peiraeus will offer to send them on (16.326–327; 17.74–76; plain renderings; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]).

**In Ithaca.** Telemachus' house has “no lack of good cheer for guests” (15.514), and [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius|Peiraeus]] promises the same, “he shall not lack good cheer” (15.546). When the falcon has flown, Telemachus answers the seer's reading with the promise of “friendship and many gifts / from me, so that anyone who met you would call you blessed” (15.537–538; see [[theoclymenus]], [[xenia]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).
