summary: Telemachus tells the fugitive seer at Pylos that his father is Odysseus, “if that was ever so; but now he has perished by a grim destruction” (15.267–268). Monro takes the formula as an assurance, not a doubt.
# "εἴ ποτʼ ἔην" (15.268), without the γε of the entry's phrase, only here.
grc: ["εἴ ποτʼ ἔην"]
en: [if that was ever so]
---

**Telemachus' answer.** The stranger at the ship has asked the host's questions, who he is and where he is from (15.263–264; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). Telemachus answers in the verse with which he answered Athena in the first book, “Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly” (15.266 = 1.214), and then names his father with the formula: “My line is from Ithaca, and my father is Odysseus, / if that was ever so; but now he has perished by a grim destruction” (15.267–268; see [[telemachus]] and [[odysseus]]). The man he takes on board will tell his mother that Odysseus is already in his native land (17.157, a plain rendering; see [[theoclymenus]]).

**Doubt or assurance.** The formula is generally understood as a pathetic expression of doubt whether a former happiness ever really existed. Monro takes it rather as an assurance, “Ulysses was my father if he lived (as of course he did)”, that is, “as surely as there was a Ulysses”, and reads Helen's words of Agamemnon and Nestor's of himself in the same way (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.268, citing *Iliad* 3.180 and 11.762). The translation keeps “if that was ever so” (see its note on 15.268). Here the second half of the verse gives the sense of loss to the whole: “but now he has perished”. Murray prints λυγρῷ ὀλέθρῳ with the manuscripts, Aristarchus read λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον (see the translation's note on 15.268).
