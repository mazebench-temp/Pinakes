---
summary: The second of the passages from which Strabo proves that Nestor's city lay inland is in this book: on the way back from Sparta Telemachus asks Peisistratus not to take him past the ship to the city, as though the road to the city and the road to the anchorage were not the same. Strabo also finds his Triphylian Pylos in the voyage north past Cruni, Chalcis and Pheae, which he says no ship leaving the Elean or the Messenian Pylos could have made.
refs: [15.193-216, 15.295-298]
---

**Inland.** Strabo's Pylos stood more than thirty stades above the sea, “as is clear from the verses”: a messenger is sent to Telemachus' companions at the ship, “and Telemachus, on the return from Sparta, does not let Peisistratus drive to the city, but bids him turn aside and hurry to the ship, as though the road to the city and to the anchorage were not the same” (*Geography* 8.3.26, plain renderings; see the Book 3 note). The verses are Telemachus' request at the end of the drive, “Do not take me past the ship, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me there, / lest the old man keep me in his house against my will / in his eagerness to make me welcome” (15.199–201). Peisistratus “turned the horses to the swift ship and the shore of the sea”, and afterward “drove the fine-maned horses / back to the city of the Pylians, and quickly reached the house” (15.205, 15.215–216; see [[peisistratus]] and [[pylos]]). Monro accepts the inference: “This shows, as Strabo points out, that the city of Pylos was some way inland” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.199; see the translation's note on 15.199). The city they reach is “the steep citadel of Pylos” (15.193), the phrase with which the two left it in Book 3 (3.485).

**The departure.** “And the departure of Telemachus would be told fittingly” only from the Triphylian Pylos, Strabo goes on, and he quotes the voyage: “And they went past Cruni and fair-flowing Chalcis. / And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and the ship made for Pheae, driven on by the wind of Zeus, / and past shining Elis, where the Epeians rule” (15.295–298; *Geography* 8.3.26, where the ship is ἀγαλλομένη, “rejoicing”, in the wind, for our ἐπειγομένη; see [[cruni]], [[chalcis-in-elis]], [[pheia]] and [[elis]]). So far the course is northward; from there it turns east, toward the “pointed” islands, which he takes for the Oxeiai among the Echinades, and the ship comes round Ithaca and lands on its far side, not in the strait that the suitors were watching (*Geography* 8.3.26, 10.2.19; see [[the-pointed-islands]]). A ship that left the Elean Pylos for Ithaca, he argues, would have left Pheae, Chalcis and Cruni behind it, since they lie south of Elis, and would have passed Elis before sunset, not after; and a ship from the Messenian Pylos would have passed the Neda, the Acidon and the Alpheus first, before the places the poet names, “names of obscure rivers, or rather of channels” (*Geography* 8.3.27, plain renderings; see [[alpheus]]). Monro, who accepts the inference from the ship, does not accept Strabo's islands: the course to the Echinades would leave out the last stage of the voyage, and the islands are the three so often named with Ithaca, Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus (Monro on 15.299–300; see the translation's note on 15.295–300).
