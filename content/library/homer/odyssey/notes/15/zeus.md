summary: Zeus is named in wishes and blessings, in the seer's genealogy and in the swineherd's story. Menelaus and Telemachus pray that Zeus, “the loud-thundering husband of Hera”, may bring the homecoming and the omen to pass (15.112, 15.180); Amphiaraus was loved by “aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo” and did not reach old age (15.245); Telemachus' ship runs before “the wind of Zeus” (15.297). In the hut the stranger wishes Eumaeus as dear to father Zeus as to himself (15.341), Laertes “prays to Zeus always” for death (15.353), Zeus sends the Phoenicians a fair wind and adds the seventh day of their voyage (15.475, 15.477), and the stranger tells Eumaeus that “for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil” (15.488–489). On the shore Telemachus leaves the suitors' end to “Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air” (15.523).

**The husband of Hera.** Menelaus begins his farewell with a wish: “Telemachus, as for your homecoming, as you have it in mind, / may Zeus bring it to pass for you, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (15.111–112; see [[husband-of-hera]], [[loud-thundering]], [[hera]] and [[nostos]]). When Helen has read the eagle that carried off the goose, Telemachus answers her in the verses with which Odysseus answered Nausicaa, “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.180–181 = 8.465, 8.467; see the Book 8 note and [[helen]]). The omen itself is given to no god by name. Peisistratus asks Menelaus “whether the god has shown this sign to us two, or to you yourself” (15.168; see [[teras]]), and Helen prophesies “as the immortals / put it in my heart” (15.172–173, the words of Athena as Mentes, 1.200–201; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[olympians]]). In the omen of Book 2 it was “far-seeing Zeus” who sent the two eagles (2.146; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]), and in the Iliad Priam asks Zeus for “a bird of omen, your swift messenger, the one that to you yourself / is dearest of birds”, and Zeus sends an eagle (*Iliad* 24.310–315; see [[eagle]]).

**Loved by Zeus and Apollo.** In the line of Melampus the seer, Amphiaraus was the man “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.245–247; see [[amphiaraus]], [[aegis-bearing]], [[apollo]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). περὶ κῆρι is the love of the immortals for Peleus, “who was dear above all to the hearts of the immortals”, and of Hippodameia's father and mother for her (*Iliad* 24.61, 13.430), and Agamemnon owns that “Worth many / people is the man whom Zeus loves in his heart” (*Iliad* 9.116–117). Some of the ancients, Plutarch among them, saw in the verse a proof of the saying that those whom the gods love die young; D. B. Monro answers that “no such ‘pathetic fallacy’ was in the poet's mind”, and that the threshold of old age is old age itself, in which Amphiaraus never fairly set foot (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.246, citing Plutarch, *Consolation to Apollonius* 17; see the translation's note on 15.225–255).

**The wind of Zeus.** Athena sends Telemachus the wind, and his ship makes for Pheae “driven on by the wind of Zeus” (15.292–297; see [[athena]], [[the-winds]] and [[pheia]]). Διὸς οὖρος is any fair wind at sea: Odysseus tells Calypso that not even balanced ships cross the gulf to her island, “glorying in the wind of Zeus” (5.175–176), and in an Iliad simile the open sea heaves without rolling either way “until some settled wind comes down from Zeus” (*Iliad* 14.16–19). Strabo, quoting the verse, and the *Hymn to Apollo* in the same voyage have ἀγαλλομένη, “glorying”, the word of Book 5, for ἐπειγομένη (Strabo 8.3.26; *Hymn to Apollo* 427; Monro's critical note on 15.297).

**In the hut.** When Eumaeus bids the stranger stay, he blesses him: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery” (15.341–342; see [[father-zeus]], [[eumaeus]] and [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]]), the verse of his blessing at the sacrifice in Book 14, with a new reason (14.440–441; see the Book 14 note). He asks after the parents of Odysseus, and Eumaeus answers that “Laertes still lives, but he prays to Zeus always / that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls”, grieving for his son and for his dead wife (15.353–357; see [[laertes]], [[anticleia]] and [[prayer]]).

**The swineherd's story.** In Eumaeus' account of how he was stolen as a child Zeus favors the thieves: the Phoenicians put to sea with the nurse and the boy on board, “and Zeus sent a fair wind” (15.475; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[phoenicians]]), and the end of the voyage comes in his verse, “but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day, / then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman” (15.477–478, the first verse = 12.399; see [[son-of-cronus]], [[cronus]], [[artemis]] and the Book 12 note). The stranger answers that the same god gave the boy good as well: “But truly for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil, / since, after toiling much, you came to the house of a man / who is kind” (15.488–490; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). The voyage that Zeus had sped ended at that house, when “the wind and the water carried them and drove them to Ithaca, / where Laertes bought me with his own possessions” (15.482–483; see the Book 15 note on [[the-winds]]). Eumaeus had said of the god in Book 14 that he gives one thing and lets another be, “for he can do all things” (14.444–445; see the Book 14 note).

**Zeus the Olympian.** On the shore of Ithaca Telemachus, having named Eurymachus, “whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, as the host for his guest, leaves the suitors' end to Zeus: “But Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air, knows this, / whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (15.520–524; see [[olympian]], [[dwelling-in-the-upper-air]], [[evil-day]], [[eurymachus]] and [[the-suitors]]). αἰθέρι ναίων is the Iliad's title for Zeus in Agamemnon's prayer before the first battle and in his prophecy of the fall of Ilios, when Zeus “will himself shake his dark aegis over them all” (*Iliad* 2.412, 4.166–167). As he speaks, a falcon flies by on the right, “the swift messenger of Apollo” (15.525–526; see [[apollo]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]), and Theoclymenus reads it for the house of Odysseus (15.531–534; see [[theoclymenus]]).
