---
summary: Book 15 tells how a king's son became a slave. Eumaeus, son of the king of Syrie, was led out of his father's house by his nurse, herself a captive sold by pirates, carried off by Phoenician traders and bought by Laertes (15.403–484); reared beside Laertes' daughter and sent to the farm when she married (15.363–370), he speaks for the slaves of the house, who long for a word from their mistress (15.374–379). The stranger asks how he was enslaved (15.381–388) and calls his lot good beside the evil (15.486–492).
# Unnamed: the slaves' longing (15.376-379) and the purchase (15.483).

**Reared in the house.** The swineherd's first story is of his childhood among his masters. Anticleia “reared me together with Ctimene of the long robes, / her strong daughter, whom she bore the youngest of her children; / with her I was reared, and she honored me little less” (15.363–365; see [[anticleia]], [[ctimene]] and [[childhood]]). When the daughter was married to Same, the slave was dressed in a cloak, a tunic and sandals and sent out to the farm, “and she loved me dearly in her heart” (15.366–370); κηρόθι μᾶλλον is “right well”, not “more than before” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.370). The work prospers, and from it he eats and drinks and gives to αἰδοῖοι, those who have a claim on his regard, as family, guests and suppliants have (15.372–373; Monro on 15.373; see [[aidos]]).

**What slaves long for.** Since the suitors came, no kind word or deed comes from the mistress, “and the slaves greatly long / to speak face to face with their mistress and learn of everything, / and to eat and drink, and then to take something with them / to the farm, such things as always warm the hearts of slaves” (15.374–379; see [[penelope]] and [[doulios]]). οἷά τε refers to all the clauses before it (Monro on 15.379): the word, the news, the meal and the gift to carry home are together what warms a slave's heart. The comforts are small, and the suitors have taken them away.

**The day of slavery.** The stranger pities him, “so you were still small … when you were driven so far from your native land and your parents”, and asks how it happened: a sacked city, or men in ships who took him alone with the flocks and sold him (15.381–388; see [[piracy]] and [[trade]]). The answer is a third way. He was a king's son, “Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals” was his father (15.413–414; see [[ctesius]] and [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]]), and he was taken by his own nurse, a captive herself (15.425–429; see [[captive-women]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]), to the traders' ship and sold in Ithaca, “where Laertes bought me with his own possessions” (15.483; see [[laertes]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The verse-end is that of Eurycleia's purchase and of the swineherd's own purchase of Mesaulius (1.430; 14.452). In Book 17 Eumaeus will say that far-seeing Zeus takes away half the good of a man “when the day of slavery takes him” (17.322–323, a plain rendering; see the entry).

**Good beside the evil.** Odysseus' answer gives the slave's life its measure: he came, after much toil, to the house of a kind man, and lives a good life, while the stranger wanders (15.486–492; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). The book's last words of him are of the slave's virtue: the fold where “the swineherd, / being good, used to sleep, kind to his masters” (15.556–557; see [[eumaeus]]).
