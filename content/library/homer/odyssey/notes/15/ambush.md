---
summary: Athena warns Telemachus at Sparta that the best of the suitors lie in ambush for him in the strait between Ithaca and Samos, but that some of them will die first, and tells him to keep his ship far from the islands and to sail by night (15.27–35). He sails home past the islands “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.299–300), and comes to land at dawn near the swineherd's farm (15.495–500).
# λοχόωσιν (15.28), only here; the Book 13 note has λοχόωσι (13.425), and the Book 14 note the English "lie in ambush".
grc: [λοχόωσιν]
refs: [15.29-30, 15.33-34, 15.300]
---

**The warning.** Athena's second reason for haste is the ambush: “The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos, / eager to kill you before you reach your native land. / But I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (15.28–32; see [[a-womans-heart]] and [[the-suitors]]). ἐπιτηδές is “of set purpose” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.28). The plan is Antinous', laid in Book 4 in the same words, to lie in wait in the strait (4.669–672), and the suitors lay waiting at Asteris (4.842–847; see [[asteris]]); the last three verses are those in which the goddess told Odysseus of it on Ithaca (15.30–32 = 13.426–428; see [[prophecy]]).

**The way round.** “But keep your well-made ship far from the islands, / and sail by night as well” (15.33–34). ἑκὰς νήσων means keeping close to the mainland; the suitors lay in wait between Ithaca and Cephallenia, but the phrase seems regularly to denote the islands named with Ithaca, Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus, which lie toward Elis and had therefore to be passed (Monro on 15.33; see the translation's note on 15.33–34). ὁμῶς is “as well”, by night as by day (Monro on 15.34). On landing he is to send the ship on to the city and go first to the swineherd (15.36–39).

**The voyage.** The ship runs north along the coast of Elis by night, and from there Telemachus “sent her on toward the pointed islands, / pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.299–300; see [[the-pointed-islands]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). How he passed the ambush the poem does not say. At dawn the crew take down the mast and row to the anchorage (15.495–500), and the ambushers come home in the next book, having watched from the heights by day and from the sea by night, “lying in ambush for Telemachus, so that we might seize him and destroy / him; but meanwhile some god has brought him home” (16.364–370, a plain rendering).
