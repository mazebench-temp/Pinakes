summary: The φρένες as the seat of purpose and of what is put there: the homecoming Telemachus has “in mind” (15.111), the folly the Erinys “had put in his mind” (15.234), the thought of begging that Eumaeus cannot understand (15.326), the minds that love deceives (15.421) and the plan the nurse bids the sailors keep “in your minds” (15.445); and the deliberation of Peisistratus, “as he pondered” (15.204).
# φρονέοντι (15.204; again 5.474, 6.145, 10.153, 18.93, 22.338, 24.239, the verse of decision, and 9.445), first met here
# among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [φρονέοντι]
en: [as he pondered, as I pondered]
---

**Purpose.** Menelaus gives the bowl with a prayer for Telemachus' return, “as for your homecoming, as you have it in mind, / may Zeus bring it to pass for you” (ὅπως φρεσὶ σῇσι μενοινᾷς, 15.111–112; see [[nostos]]). At Pylos Peisistratus weighs the request to leave his friend at the ship: “And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way” (ὧδε δέ οἱ φρονέοντι δοάσσατο κέρδιον εἶναι, 15.204; see [[decision-scene]] and [[kerdos]]), the verse with which Odysseus decides on the shore of Scheria and before Nausicaa (5.474, 6.145), and he answers, “For I know this well in my mind and in my heart” (κατὰ φρένα καὶ κατὰ θυμόν, 15.211; see [[thymos]]).

**What is put in the mind.** In the story of Melampus the blindness is a god's: “the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (τήν οἱ ἐπὶ φρεσὶ θῆκε, 15.233–234; see [[ate]] and [[erinyes]]). In the hut Eumaeus cannot account for the beggar's plan to serve the suitors: “Ah me, stranger, why has this thought come / into your mind? Surely you long to perish there altogether” (τίη τοι ἐνὶ φρεσὶ τοῦτο νόημα / ἔπλετο, 15.326–327; see [[noos]] and [[peira]]).

**Deceived and keeping counsel.** In Eumaeus' story the Phoenician seduces the nurse with love, “which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk” (φρένας ἠπεροπεύει, 15.421–422; see [[eperopeuo]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]), and the deceived woman binds the sailors to her plot: “But keep the word in your minds” (ἀλλʼ ἔχετʼ ἐν φρεσὶ μῦθον, 15.445; see [[mythos]]). When the story is done Odysseus answers, “truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me” (ἐνὶ φρεσὶ θυμὸν ὄρινας, 15.486; see [[algos]]). After the omen of the eagle “the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (15.165; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]).
