---
summary: θυμός in the book: Athena's warning, “For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast” (15.20); hearts warmed by an omen and by a gift from the house (15.165, 15.379); Peisistratus, who takes counsel with his heart and knows Nestor's “overweening spirit” (15.202, 15.211–212); Laertes, who prays that “his spirit may perish from his limbs” (15.354); the “heart and spirit” that bid a man go where he will (15.339, 15.395); and Odysseus' heart, stirred by Eumaeus' story (15.486).
---

**A woman's heart.** Athena warns Telemachus that Penelope's father and brothers are urging her to marry Eurymachus: “For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast: / she wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her” (οἶσθα γὰρ οἷος θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσι γυναικός, 15.20–21; see [[a-womans-heart]] and [[penelope]]), and she bids him “take heed of it in your heart” when she tells him of the ambush (15.27; see [[ambush]]).

**Warmed hearts.** When the eagle flies by on the right with the goose, “they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (πᾶσιν ἐνὶ φρεσὶ θυμὸς ἰάνθη, 15.165; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[phrenes]]), and Helen prophesies “as the immortals / put it in my heart” (15.172–173; see [[prophecy]]). The same verb warms a slave's heart in Eumaeus' account of the house of Laertes: the farm hands long to speak with their mistress, eat and drink, and take something back to the farm, “such things as always warm the hearts of slaves” (θυμὸν … ἰαίνει, 15.376–379; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and the translation's table of key words, θυμὸν ἰαίνω). At Sparta Peisistratus, laying the gifts in the chariot, “gazed at them all in his heart” (15.132), “with all his thoughts centred upon them” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.132; see [[doron]]).

**Counsel and temper.** At Pylos “the son of Nestor took counsel with his heart / how he might promise and fulfill it fair and right” (15.202–203; see [[decision-scene]] and [[moira]]), and sends Telemachus aboard with a warning about his father: “For I know this well in my mind and in my heart: / such is his overweening spirit, he will not let you go” (θυμὸς ὑπέρβιος, 15.211–212; see [[nestor]] and [[hyperbios]]). The first verse is the one with which Agamemnon and Hector foretell the fall of Troy (*Iliad* 4.163, 6.447), the second is said of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.262), and here the words are only half-serious (Monro on 15.212; see the translation's note on 15.212).

**The spirit that leaves.** Laertes “prays to Zeus always / that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls” (θυμὸν ἀπὸ μελέων φθίσθαι, 15.353–354; see [[laertes]], [[euchomai]] and [[thanatos]]): θυμός is the life that goes at death.

**Heart and spirit.** The pair κραδίη θυμός τε, a hendiadys of nearly synonymous words (Monro on 15.175), is the will of a free man: Telemachus will send the stranger “wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (15.339 = 14.517), and Eumaeus' men may go to bed, “whoever's heart and spirit bids him” (15.395; see [[kradie]] and [[pompe]]). At the end of the night of stories the guest answers his host as his host had answered him the night before (14.361): “Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me / by telling all these things, all the sorrows you have suffered in your heart” (15.486–487; see [[algos]]).
