summary: Two birds fly on the right in the book, and each is read. At Sparta Menelaus ponders how to read the eagle with the goose, and Helen prophesies “as the immortals put it in my heart” (15.166–178); on Ithaca the seer Theoclymenus knows the falcon at sight for a bird of omen (15.529–534). Theoclymenus is of a house of seers: Melampus, Amphiaraus, and his father Polypheides, whom Apollo made the best of seers and who prophesied at Hyperesia (15.225, 15.244–255).

**The eagle at Sparta.** As Telemachus takes leave, an eagle carrying a tame white goose flies by on the right and darts across in front of the horses (15.160–165; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[eagle]]). δεξιός is “to the right”, ἐπιδέξια, from left to right: the bird crosses the path in the lucky direction (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.160; see the translation's note on 15.160, 15.164). Peisistratus asks whose sign it is, “whether the god has shown this sign to us two, or to you yourself” (15.167–168; see [[teras]]). Menelaus “pondered / how he might read it to him fair and right, with understanding” (15.169–170): ὑποκρίνομαι is to expound, to read the sign, as Penelope will ask the beggar to read her dream of the geese (Monro on 15.170; 19.535, 19.555; see the translation's table of key words). Helen is before him, and her reading is not a seer's craft but prophecy: “Hear me, and I will prophesy, as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled” (15.172–173; see [[helen]] and [[prophecy]]). The verses are those of Athena as Mentes, who added that she was no seer and had no clear knowledge of birds (1.200–202). αὐτάρ marks the slight contrast between the listeners, κλῦτέ μευ, and the speaker (Monro on 15.172).

**A house of seers.** The stranger who comes to Telemachus at Pylos is “a seer; and by birth he was a descendant of Melampus”, ἔκγονος, a great-grandson (15.225; Monro on 15.225; see [[theoclymenus]] and [[melampus]]). Melampus was the noble seer who alone promised to drive off the cattle of Iphiclus (11.291; see [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]). His descent gives the seers of the line: Amphiaraus, whom Zeus and Apollo loved (15.244–247; see [[amphiaraus]]), and Polypheides, son of [[mantius|Mantius]], whom “Apollo made … a seer, / by far the best of mortals, after Amphiaraus died” (15.252–253; see [[polypheides]] and [[apollo]]), in the words of Calchas, “by far the best of the bird-seers” (*Iliad* 1.69; see [[best-of-bird-seers]]). Angry with his father, he moved to Hyperesia, “and living there he prophesied for all mortals” (15.254–255; see [[hyperesia]]). Plato calls Theoclymenus the seer of the Melampodidae (*Ion* 538e–539a; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]).

**The falcon on Ithaca.** At the landing a falcon, “the swift messenger of Apollo”, flies by on the right plucking a dove (15.525–528; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]] and [[falcon]]). [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] calls Telemachus apart: “not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.531–532; see [[not-without-a-god]] and [[birds]]). ἔγνων is the aorist of what happens at the moment of speaking (Monro on 15.532). To tell the significant birds from the rest is the seer's first business, W. W. Merry notes, comparing these verses (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.182), and it is what Eurymachus denied when he scorned Halitherses' eagles, “not all of them are birds of fate” (2.181–182; see [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]]). Telemachus has just named Eurymachus as the man most eager to marry his mother (15.518–524). The seer's reading is oblique, no race in Ithaca more kingly than yours (15.533–534); the plain one he will give Penelope: Odysseus is already in his native land, “Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (17.157–161, a plain rendering).
