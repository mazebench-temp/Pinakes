---
summary: The gods who give signs: Athena bids Telemachus wait “until the gods show you a glorious wife” (15.26); Peisistratus asks for whom “the god has shown this sign” (15.168), and Theoclymenus reads the falcon, “not without a god did the bird fly on the right” (15.531). Two mortals are honored as gods: Helen by Telemachus (15.181) and Eurymachus by the men of Ithaca (15.520). Erinys is “the goddess” who blinded Melampus (15.234), and for the swineherd “the blessed gods make the work grow” (15.372).
---

**Signs from a god.** The book's two omens are given by a god the speakers do not name. When the eagle flies by on the right with the goose, Peisistratus asks Menelaus “whether the god has shown this sign to us two, or to you yourself” (ἢ νῶϊν τόδʼ ἔφηνε θεὸς τέρας, 15.168; see [[teras]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); when the falcon flies by with the dove, [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] tells Telemachus, “not without a god did the bird fly on the right” (οὔ τοι ἄνευ θεοῦ, 15.531; see [[not-without-a-god]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Athena uses the same verb of the gods' gift of a wife: Telemachus is to entrust the house to the best of the slave women “until the gods show you a glorious wife” (εἰς ὅ κέ τοι φήνωσι θεοὶ κυδρὴν παράκοιτιν, 15.25–26; see [[marriage]] and [[kydros]]).

**As a god.** Two mortals are honored as gods. Telemachus answers Helen's prophecy, “then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (θεῷ ὣς εὐχετοῴμην, 15.181; see [[euchomai]], [[helen]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); and he names to the seer “Eurymachus, the splendid son of wise-hearted Polybus, / whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god” (τὸν νῦν ἶσα θεῷ Ἰθακήσιοι εἰσορόωσι, 15.519–520; see [[eurymachus]] and [[honored-like-a-god]]), the man who seems likely to be the next lord of Ithaca until the falcon appears (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.519).

**The goddess.** In the story of Melampus, Erinys is “the goddess” who blinded him: “the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (θεὰ δασπλῆτις Ἐρινύς, 15.233–234; see [[erinyes]] and [[ate]]).

**The swineherd's gods.** Eumaeus has lost the kindness of the house since Anticleia died, “yet for me myself / the blessed gods make the work grow at which I stay” (ἔργον ἀέξουσιν μάκαρες θεοὶ ᾧ ἐπιμίμνω, 15.371–372; see [[blessed-gods]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]), as on the first night he had said that a god makes a faithful servant's work grow (14.65–66; see the Book 14 note). The gods give the slave his living; Hermes gives the beggar his skill in service (15.319–321; see [[hermes]] and [[charis]]); and Zeus, in Odysseus' answer to the swineherd's story, has set good beside the evil (15.488–489; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
