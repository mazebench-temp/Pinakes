---
summary: Strabo reads this book as geography. Telemachus' request at Pylos proves that Nestor's city lay inland, and his voyage home past Cruni, Chalcis and Pheae that it was the Triphylian Pylos; the strait of the ambush and Ctimene's marriage “to Same” bear on the names of Cephallenia; Eumaeus' Syrie is Syros; “Hellas and the heart of Argos” answers Thucydides; and the bowl of Phaedimus shows the skill of the Sidonians, “as the poet too shows”. Athena's warning that Penelope's father and brothers urge her to marry puts Icarius, for Strabo, in Acarnania.
---

- **Pylos inland.** Nestor's city lay more than thirty stades from the sea, “as is clear from the verses”: on the return from Sparta Telemachus does not let Peisistratus drive him to the city but has him turn aside to the ship, “as though the road to the city and to the anchorage were not the same” (*Geography* 8.3.26, plain renderings). The verses are “Do not take me past the ship, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me there, / lest the old man keep me in his house against my will” (15.199–200), and Monro accepts the inference (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.199): [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]], [[pylos]] and [[peisistratus]].
- **The voyage home.** Strabo quotes the departure, “And they went past Cruni and fair-flowing Chalcis …” (15.295–298), as fitting only a ship that sailed north from the Triphylian Pylos, since Cruni, Chalcis and Pheae lie south of Elis, in that order from south to north; after Elis the ship turns east toward the islands that the poet calls θοαί, “pointed”, which he takes for the Oxeiai among the Echinades, and lands on the far side of Ithaca, away from the strait that the suitors were watching (*Geography* 8.3.26–27, 10.2.19). On the coast of Triphylia he places “another river, Chalcis, and a spring, Cruni, and a settlement, Chalcis”, and after them Samicum (*Geography* 8.3.13, a plain rendering); listing the places called Chalcis, he quotes the line again with “rocky” for “fair-flowing”, βὰν δὲ παρὰ Κρουνοὺς καὶ Χαλκίδα πετρήεσσαν, “Telemachus and his men going home from Nestor” (*Geography* 10.1.9, a plain rendering); and he cites “and past shining Elis, where the Epeians rule” (15.298) for the old division of the country between the Epeians and Nestor (*Geography* 8.3.1). The line of Cruni and Chalcis is in no manuscript of the Odyssey and comes from Strabo and the *Hymn to Apollo* (see the translation's note on 15.295–300): [[cruni]], [[chalcis-in-elis]], [[pheia]], [[elis]], [[epeians]] and [[the-pointed-islands]].
- **Samos and Same.** By Samos Homer means the island now called Cephallenia, “as also when he says, ‘in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos’”, the epithet marking the island and not the city of the same name (*Geography* 10.2.10, a plain rendering; 15.29). Apollodorus argued that the city was called Same, from the suitors' count, “from Same there are twenty-four men” (16.249, a plain rendering), and from the story of Ctimene, “they gave her in marriage to Same” (15.367); Strabo grants that this is reasonable, “for the poet does not give a clear account” of Cephallenia, Ithaca and the places near them (*Geography* 10.2.10, a plain rendering): [[samos]], [[ctimene]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]].
- **Icarius in Acarnania.** The poet presents Penelope's father and her brothers as living, Strabo says, quoting the suitors' fear of going to the house of Icarius (2.52–53) and, for the brothers, Athena's words at Sparta, “For already her father and her brothers are bidding her / marry Eurymachus” (15.16–17); they cannot have lived in Lacedaemon, “for Telemachus would not have lodged with Menelaus when he came there”, and so he places them in Acarnania (*Geography* 10.2.24, plain renderings): [[the-icarius-problem]] and [[icarius]].
- **Syros.** Among the Cyclades is Syros, “from which came Pherecydes son of Babys”, and “the poet seems to mention this island, calling it Syrie: ‘There is an island called Syrie … above Ortygia’” (*Geography* 10.5.8, plain renderings; 15.403–404). Monro took Syrie and Ortygia for mythical islands, and saw no need to identify them with Syra and Delos (Monro on 15.403): [[syrie]] and [[ortygia]].
- **The Sidonians' craft.** Homer names the Sidonians beside the Phoenicians to show Menelaus' long stay among them, Strabo argues, and shows it by praising their craftsmanship: the robes that Alexander brought from Sidon are “the work of women / of the Sidonians” (*Iliad* 6.289–292), and Menelaus says to Telemachus, “I will give you a well-wrought mixing bowl; of silver / it is all, and its rims are finished with gold, / the work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it to me, / the king of the Sidonians” (15.115–118 = 4.615–618). “The work of Hephaestus” must be taken as hyperbole, “as beautiful things are called the works of Athena, the Graces and the Muses” (*Geography* 1.2.33, plain renderings). Elsewhere he says that the Sidonians are reported to be skilled in many arts and in fine arts, “as the poet too shows”, and astronomers and arithmeticians as well, having begun with reckoning and with sailing by night, “for each is the business of merchants and shipmasters” (*Geography* 16.2.24, plain renderings). In this book the Phoenician nurse who carries off the swineherd is “skilled in splendid handiwork” and from “Sidon, rich in bronze”, and of the traders' voyage the swineherd says, “For six days we sailed on, night and day alike” (15.418, 15.425, 15.476): [[sidonians]], [[sidon]], [[phaedimus]], [[mixing-bowl]], [[phoenicians]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]].
- **Hellas and Argos.** Against Thucydides, who said that Homer never speaks of barbarians because the Hellenes were not yet set apart by one name, Strabo quotes the poet himself, “the man whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos”, and again, “if you wish to turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (*Geography* 14.2.28; 1.344, 15.80); others had made the same reply (*Geography* 8.6.6). Some, he reports, took the verse for a poetic figure that names the part together with the whole (*Geography* 8.3.8). Monro calls the phrase “a piece of Epic commonplace” (Monro on 15.80–85): [[hellas]] and [[argos]].
- **Pherae.** Pherae, the house of Ortilochus, is in Messenia, as the verse of both journeys shows: “and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, / son of Ortilochus” (15.186–187 = 3.488–489; *Geography* 8.5.8; see the Book 3 note): [[pherae-in-messenia]], [[diocles]] and [[ortilochus]].
- **The house of Melampus.** The Anigrus in Triphylia, some say, took its smell from the cleansings that Melampus washed in it when he purified the daughters of Proetus (*Geography* 8.3.19). Near Oropus stood the oracle of Amphiaraus, once honored, “where, as Sophocles says, the Theban dust burst open and received Amphiaraus in his flight, with his arms and his four-horse chariot”, an oracle brought there from Cnopia near Thebes, and Harma was named from his chariot (*Geography* 9.1.22, 9.2.10–11, plain renderings). His sons are men of later stories: Calchas came on foot to Clarus “with Amphilochus, son of Amphiaraus”, on the return from Troy (*Geography* 14.1.27), Mopsus and Amphilochus founded Mallus (*Geography* 14.5.16), and Amphilochian Argos was founded, as Ephorus says, by Alcmaeon and his sons, or, as Thucydides says, by Amphilochus himself after Troy (*Geography* 7.7.7, 10.2.26). Homer names the brothers only in this book, “His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus”, and says nothing of Troy (15.248): [[melampus]], [[amphiaraus]], [[amphilochus]], [[alcmaeon]] and [[harma]].
