---
summary: In Eumaeus' story a Phoenician lay with the nurse “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (15.421–422).
---

The second verse ended the curse of Agamemnon's shade on Clytemnestra, who “has poured shame on herself and on those to come hereafter, / the womenfolk, even on one who does good” (11.433–434), and returns in his praise of Penelope in the last book (24.202). Here it is a general truth in the swineherd's mouth, of the love that deceives (see [[eperopeuo]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Like Merry, D. B. Monro takes θηλύτερος as “female” in contrast to male, not “more female,” comparing θεώτεραι, the door of the cave that is “for gods” (13.111), and ἀγρότερος “of the country” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.422; see the general article).
