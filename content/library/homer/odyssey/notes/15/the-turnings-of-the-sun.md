---
summary: Eumaeus places the island of his birth “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (15.404), at the beginning of his story.
---

Asked by the stranger whether his city was sacked or whether he was carried off alone with the flocks (15.381–388), Eumaeus begins with the place: “There is an island called Syrie, if perhaps you have heard of it, / above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are, / not so very populous, but a good one, / good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat” (ὅθι τροπαὶ ἠελίοιο, 15.403–406; see [[syrie]], [[ortygia]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The island is fixed by two marks, a neighboring island and a place of the sun, and the stranger, who has been everywhere, may never have heard of it. What follows belongs to the same far-off world: no famine and no sickness, and death by the gentle arrows of Apollo and Artemis only in old age (15.407–411; see [[nousos]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). It was for this blessedness, as well as for the turning points of the sun, that Monro took Syrie and Ortygia for mythical islands (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.403; see the translation's note on 15.403–404). The king's son of that island is the swineherd telling the story (15.412–414; see [[ctesius]] and [[eumaeus]]).
