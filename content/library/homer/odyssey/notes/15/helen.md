summary: At Telemachus' departure Helen chooses from her chests the largest and most beautiful of the robes she has woven, “a remembrance of Helen's hands”, for his bride to wear; and when the eagle flies by with the goose, she reads the omen before Menelaus can: Odysseus will come home and take vengeance (15.58, 15.93–130, 15.171–181).

**The robe.** When Telemachus asks to go, Menelaus bids “his wife and the slave women” make ready a meal, and goes down to the fragrant treasure chamber with Helen and their son (15.93–100; see [[menelaus]] and [[megapenthes]]). “And Helen stood beside the chests, / where her robes were, richly embroidered, which she had made herself. / Of these Helen, brilliant among women, took one and carried it, / the one that was most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest, / and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others” (15.104–108; see [[robe]], [[weaving]], [[brilliant]] and [[like-a-star]]). The verses are those in which Hecuba chooses a robe for Athena at Troy, from robes that were “the work of women / of the Sidonians”, whom Paris brought home on the voyage that brought Helen (*Iliad* 6.288–295): here the robes are her own work, οὓς κάμεν αὐτή.

**The gift.** “And fair-cheeked Helen stood beside him, / holding the robe in her hands, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (15.123–124; see [[fair-cheeked]] and [[called-him-by-name]]):

{{quote:45afdb12-ca6e-55e2-81b6-73e7a3a9c313}}

μνῆμα, “a remembrance”, is a keepsake of the giver (see the translation's key words), and the robe is not for Telemachus to wear but for his bride. Murray prints κείσθω, “let it lie”; Aristarchus read κεῖσθαι, which Monro prints, an infinitive carrying on the construction of φορέειν (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.128; see the translation's note on 15.128). The last verse is her blessing, “your well-built house and your native land” (15.129 = 4.476, 6.315; see [[well-built]]). She had woven at Troy too, the great purple web of the battles of the Trojans and the Achaeans (*Iliad* 3.125–128; see [[helens-web]]); her robe now waits for the wife of Odysseus' son in the house of Penelope, who weaves at her loom in the upper room (15.517; see [[penelope]]).

**The omen.** As Telemachus says his farewell, an eagle flies by on the right with a tame goose from the court, and Peisistratus asks Menelaus whom the sign is for (15.160–168; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). “But Helen of the long robes was before him, and said” (15.171; see [[long-robed]]): she will prophesy “as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled” (15.172–173 ≈ 1.200–201, the words of Athena in the likeness of Mentes; see [[divination]] and [[athena]]): “As this eagle snatched the goose that was being reared in the house, / coming from the mountain, where he was born and bred, / so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (15.174–178; see [[eagle]] and [[geese]]). ὑποφθαμένη, “was before him”, gives her the quickness that W. W. Merry, after Nitzsch, sets against Menelaus' slowness of resolve, pointing to this scene (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.117; see [[menelaus]]). Telemachus answers her with the words Odysseus spoke to Nausicaa at parting: “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.180–181 ≈ 8.465, 8.467; see [[telemachus]] and [[nausicaa]]). The seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] will repeat her last half-verse to Penelope, after the falcon's omen (17.159; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).

**Her names.** Ἑλένης … καλλικόμοιο, “Helen of the lovely hair” (15.58), only here in the Odyssey (see the translation's key words and [[lovely-haired]]); δῖα γυναικῶν, “brilliant among women” (15.106); καλλιπάρῃος, “fair-cheeked” (15.123); τανύπεπλος, “of the long robes” (15.171).
