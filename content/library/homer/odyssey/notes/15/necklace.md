“then they sent a messenger to bring the woman word. / There came a man of great knowledge to my father's house / with a golden necklace, strung here and there with amber. / In the hall the slave women and my lady mother / were handling it and gazing at it with their eyes, / offering a price; and he nodded to her in silence” (15.458–463; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[phoenicians]], [[trade]] and [[ctesius]]). The ὅρμος was a chain that passed round the neck and hung down on the breast, and so of some length: Monro cites the necklaces about the neck and breast of Aphrodite in the Hymns, and the necklace nine cubits long that the goddesses promised Eileithyia (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.460; *Hymn to Aphrodite* 88–90; *Hymn* 6.10–11; *Hymn to Apollo* 103–104; see [[aphrodite]] and [[eileithyia]]). That necklace is “strung with golden threads”, χρυσείοισι λίνοισιν ἐερμένον, with the verb of this one, ἔερτο. μετά is “at intervals”, and ἠλέκτροισιν “with pieces of amber” (Monro on 15.460; see [[amber]] and the translation's note on 15.460). ὑπισχόμεναι is “tendering”, making offers for it, chaffering (Monro on 15.463). The other necklace of the Odyssey is a suitor's gift: Eurymachus gives Penelope a golden necklace strung with amber, like the sun (18.295–296, a plain rendering; see [[eurymachus]]).
