summary: The journey ends. Athena comes to Sparta while Telemachus lies awake, sends him home and warns him of the ambush (15.1–42); he takes leave of Menelaus and Helen with gifts and a sign (15.43–183), drives back by Pherae to Pylos and leaves Peisistratus at the ship, so as not to be kept by Nestor (15.184–221), takes on board the fugitive seer Theoclymenus (15.222–286), and sails by night past the ambush (15.287–300); at dawn he lands on Ithaca, sends the ship on to the city, and goes on foot to the swineherd's fold (15.495–557).

**Athena at Sparta.** “But Pallas Athena went to Lacedaemon with its wide dancing floors, / to the shining son of great-hearted Odysseus, / to remind him of his homecoming and to urge him to return” (15.1–3), where the thirteenth book left her (13.439–440; see [[athena]] and [[lacedaemon]]). As that book ended at nightfall, Monro counts the story from the next day, the 36th, and adds, “The chronology is not quite clear” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.1, referring to 17.515; see the translation's note on 15.1–7). She finds the two young men asleep in the porch, but “sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus, but in his heart / through the ambrosial night cares for his father kept him awake” (15.7–8): the apparent contradiction, both asleep and one awake, recalls “the famous contradiction about Zeus” between *Iliad* 1.611 and 2.2, and in both places “the difficulty lies in the epic style of narration” (Monro on 15.4–7; see [[sweet-sleep]] and [[ambrosial]]). The journey ends as it began, with Telemachus awake at night over his father (1.443–444). The goddess comes to a waking man, “Standing near him” (15.9). Merry speaks of her “urging him in a dream to return home at once” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.594), but there is no dream in the book (see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[visit-scene]]).

**The counsel.** Her warning begins in Nestor's words: Telemachus is not to wander long from home, leaving his possessions to overbearing men, “lest they devour everything you have, / dividing your possessions, and you have gone on a useless journey” (15.10–13; 15.11–13 = 3.314–316). τῆλε, “far”, is perhaps said with allusion to the name Τηλέμαχος (Monro on 15.10; see [[telemachus]]). Those who would cut out the return of Telemachus as a later addition urged that the journey could not be called τηϋσίη, “useless”, after what Menelaus had told him; Monro answers that “Menelaus has only given him hopes of the return of Ulysses” (Monro, Appendix I §12, note). The new matter is the marriage: Penelope's father and brothers are bidding her marry Eurymachus, who surpasses the suitors in his gifts (15.16–23; see [[penelope]], [[eurymachus]], [[icarius]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). Then the ambush: “The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos, / eager to kill you before you reach your native land” (15.28–30), the last verse and the two after it being those in which she told Odysseus of it (15.30–32 = 13.426–428; see [[ambush]] and [[asteris]]). He is to keep his ship ἑκὰς νήσων, “far from the islands”, that is, close to the mainland: the reference must be especially to Cephallenia, Monro says, where the suitors lay in wait, but the phrase seems regularly to mean the islands named with Ithaca, Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus, which lie toward Elis (21.347) and had to be passed (Monro on 15.33; see the translation's note on 15.33–34). He is to sail by night “as well”, and when he reaches Ithaca he is to send the ship on to the city and go first to the swineherd (15.33–42), in the verses in which she sent Odysseus there (15.38–39 = 13.404–405; see [[eumaeus]]).

**The leave-taking.** He wakes Peisistratus, “stirring him with his heel” (15.44–45): Aristarchus and modern critics object to λὰξ ποδί as absurd when the man to be woken was in bed, “But we do not know the form of the bed” (Monro on 15.45, comparing *Iliad* 10.158; see [[peisistratus]]). Peisistratus will not drive through the dark night, and bids him wait for the gifts and the farewell of Menelaus, “For a guest remembers all his days / the host who has shown him friendship” (15.49–55; see [[a-guest-remembers-his-host]]). At dawn Telemachus asks Menelaus to send him home (15.64–66), and Menelaus will not keep him: “I am indignant with another / man, a host, who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things” (15.69–71; see [[what-is-due-is-better]] and [[xenia]]). His offer of a tour through Hellas and the heart of Argos, with a gift at every city (15.80–85), Telemachus declines, since he left no one to watch over his possessions (15.86–91; see [[hellas]] and [[argos]]). The gifts are given (15.92–130; see [[guest-gifts]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[robe]]), and the farewell ends with a sign (15.147–181; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). Merry reports critics who held that in the original poem the scenes of this book followed at once on the fourth book's gift of the mixing bowl, which ends at 4.619 and is given again here in the same verses (15.113–119 = 4.613–619; Merry on 4.594; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**How long at Sparta.** Telemachus asked leave to go in the fourth book, since his companions were waiting at Pylos (4.594–599); yet by the reckoning of the following books he is still at Sparta when Athena comes, thirty days later by Merry's count (on 4.594) and twenty by Monro's, the time his father took to come from Ogygia to Ithaca (5.278, 6.48, 8.1, 13.18). The visit “is always represented as a hurried one”, Monro says, and answers: “the epic poet does not aim at accuracy of this kind. If an error is one that can only be detected by a calculation which his hearer is not able to make, or which nothing in the story leads him to make, he takes no pains to avoid it” (Monro, Appendix I §10; see the translation's note on 4.594).

**Pherae and Pylos.** The drive back is told in the verses of the drive out (15.184–191 = 3.486–493; see [[chariot-journey]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]). The sunset verse, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (15.185), is “obviously archaic and conventional”, used whenever the poet says what point in a journey had been reached at sunset, and its clauses virtually form one sentence: “they trotted along until, at sunset, they reached Pherae” (Monro on 15.184–192; see [[sunset-lines]]). On the morning of the 37th day (Monro on 15.189) they reach Pylos, and Telemachus asks his friend to leave him at the ship: they are guest-friends from their fathers' friendship and of the same age, and the journey will bring them “still further into oneness of mind”; “Do not take me past the ship”, lest the old man keep him in his eagerness to make him welcome (15.193–201; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]). The request shows, as Strabo points out, “that the city of Pylos was some way inland” (Monro on 15.199; see [[pylos]] and [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]). Peisistratus puts the gifts in the stern and bids him hurry on board before Nestor hears: “such is his overweening spirit, he will not let you go” (15.202–214), words repeated from the Iliad, where they are said of Achilles, and “here are only half-serious” (Monro on 15.212, citing *Iliad* 18.262; see [[nestor]] and [[hyperbios]]); εἴσεται is “will make his way” (Monro on 15.213). Telemachus bids his companions put τὰ τεύχεα in the ship, “the arms”, not the rigging, which is taken in hand later (15.217–221; Monro on 15.218; see the translation's note on 15.218).

**The seer comes on board.** While Telemachus prays and sacrifices to Athena at the stern, a man comes up “from a far land, fleeing from Argos, having killed a man, / a seer” (15.222–225; see [[theoclymenus]], [[sacrifice]] and [[the-killer-in-exile]]). Before he speaks the poem gives his descent, from Melampus through Mantius and Polypheides, and with it the stories of Melampus and the cattle, of Amphiaraus, who perished at Thebes, and of Cleitus, whom Dawn carried off (15.225–256; see [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]], [[the-seven-against-thebes]], [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]], [[mantius]], [[polypheides]] and [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]]). Telemachus, who came to Pylos while its people were sacrificing to Poseidon (3.4–12; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]), is found at a sacrifice in his turn by a stranger, who asks him the question that he himself put to Athena in the first book, “Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?” (15.264 = 1.170; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). He answers that he is of Ithaca and the son of Odysseus, “if that was ever so” (15.267–268; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]]), takes the suppliant on board (15.272–281; see [[supplication]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]), takes his spear from him and lays it on the deck, and seats him beside himself in the stern (15.282–286): on the outward voyage Athena sat in the stern and Telemachus close beside her (2.416–418).

**The voyage.** The launching is told in the verses of the outward voyage, in another order: the companions lay hold of the tackle, raise the mast in its box and hoist the sails, and Athena sends a following wind (15.287–292; 2.420–426; see [[sailing-type-scene]], [[mast-box]] and [[forestays]]). She had promised that “whoever of the immortals guards and protects you” would send it (15.34–35), and she sends it herself. The course runs north past Cruni and Chalcis, Pheae and shining Elis, and on toward the pointed islands (15.295–299): for the places and the doubts about their verses see [[cruni]], [[chalcis-in-elis]], [[pheia]], [[elis]] and [[the-pointed-islands]], and the translation's note on 15.295–300. The last verse leaves him “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.300), and the poem turns to the hut, where the night passes in talk (15.301–494; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The ambush fails. The suitors watched from the heights by day and from their ship by night, and when Telemachus is home Antinous can say only that some god has brought him there, while Amphinomus guesses that a god told the ambushers, or that they saw the ship go by and could not overtake it (16.355–357, 16.364–370; see [[the-suitors]] and [[antinous]]).

**The landing.** “For soon came Dawn of the fair throne” (15.495): the dawn of the 38th day, and a change of scene “to the landing of Telemachus on the neighbouring coast” (Monro on 15.495). ἐπὶ χέρσου means that they had reached land, not that they had landed, for the mast is taken down before the ship is run aground; they bring her in with oars, cast out the mooring-stones and make fast the stern-cables, and step out ἐπὶ ῥηγμῖνι, into the “broken water” or surf, not onto the beach (15.495–499; Monro on 15.495, 15.499; see [[mast]], [[mooring-stones]] and [[stern-cables]]). The last two of these verses are those of the landing at Chryse (15.498–499 = *Iliad* 1.436–437). Telemachus sends the ship on to the city, as Athena bade him, and goes to his fields and herdsmen, promising the crew a feast in the morning as a reward for the voyage (15.503–507; see [[feast]]); he says he will come down to the city in the evening, but “does not in fact return till next day” (Monro on 15.505). The seer asks where he should go, and the answer is the falcon (15.508–538; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Peiraeus takes the stranger home and takes command of the ship, “and proceeds to carry out the directions of Telemachus” (15.539–549; Monro on 15.547; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]); that the crew go on board before they loose the cables is a hysteron proteron, the main action put first (15.548; Monro on 15.548; see [[hysteron-proteron]]). Telemachus binds on his sandals and takes his own strong spear from the deck, not [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]]', as Ameis supposed (15.550–552; Monro on 15.551; see [[sandals]] and [[spear]]), and his feet carry him to the fold, where the swineherd sleeps among the swine (15.555–557; see [[fold]] and [[eumaeus]]). He has landed, Monro observes, “at a point not far from the homestead of Eumaeus” (on 15.545); the sixteenth book opens with his arrival at the hut (16.1–16).

**The return and the poem.** Monro answers those who take the journey for a later addition by the order of the narrative, which in Homer is “always approximately consecutive” and continuous. The fourteenth book ends at nightfall in the hut; this book follows Telemachus home and comes back to the hut at supper-time, so that between 15.1 and the supper of 15.301 “there is a gap of one or more days in the story of Ulysses, which is filled up by the story of Telemachus”: without it “there is a sensible hiatus in the course of events”. The landing at dawn fills no such blank, but “it is so managed as not to interrupt the main action” (Monro, Appendix I §12).
