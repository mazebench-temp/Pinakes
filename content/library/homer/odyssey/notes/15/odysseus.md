**The father in absence.** At Sparta he is the subject of his son's sleepless night, “cares for his father kept him awake” (15.8), and of his fear of perishing “while I seek my godlike father” (15.90; see [[telemachus]] and [[antitheos]]). Telemachus wishes he might find him at home and tell him of Menelaus' kindness (15.155–159), and Helen reads the eagle's omen: “so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (15.176–178; see [[helen]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The second reading is the truth, and the book turns at once to the hut where he sits. At Pylos his son names him to the seer, “if that was ever so; but now he has perished by a grim destruction” (15.267–268), an assurance that Odysseus was his father, not a doubt (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.268).

**The test.** In the hut, after supper, “Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd, / whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay / there at the farmstead, or would urge him on to the city” (15.304–306; the first verse = 14.459; see [[peira]] and [[eumaeus]]). He says that at dawn he will go to the city and beg, tell his news to Penelope and serve the suitors:

The infinitives depend on ἐρίσσειε and explain δρηστοσύνῃ, δανά is “dry”, and τοῖς ἀγαθοῖσι takes the article as with comparatives (Monro on 15.322, 15.324; see the translation's notes on 15.317, 15.324 and 15.322, and [[hermes]], [[guide]] and [[cupbearing]]). Eumaeus begs him to stay until Telemachus comes (15.325–339).

**The question about his parents.** He thanks him in the verse of the night before, “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me” (15.341 = 14.440), and adds, “There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming; / but for the sake of the ruinous belly they have evil sorrows, / the men whom wandering and bane and sorrow come upon” (15.343–345; see [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]] and [[gaster]]). οὐλομένης, “ruinous”, has here “a slightly mock-heroic effect” (Monro on 15.344; see the translation's note on 15.344). Then, since he must wait, he asks about “the mother of divine Odysseus, / and of his father”, whether they still live (15.346–350), and hears from his slave, who does not know him, that his father prays to die and that his mother died of grief for “her glorious son” (15.353–360; see [[laertes]] and [[anticleia]]), and that he has a sister, married into Same (15.363–367; see [[ctimene]]). The poem gives no word of what he feels.

**The swineherd's story.** “Ah, so you were still small, swineherd Eumaeus, / when you were driven so far from your native land and your parents” (15.381–382; see [[o-popoi]]): in his mouth the address with which the poet speaks to the swineherd (see [[apostrophe]]). He asks whether a city was sacked or a boy taken alone with the flocks (15.383–388), and the answer is the long tale of [[syrie|Syrie]] and the Phoenicians (15.389–484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). His reply: “Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me / by telling all these things, all the sorrows you have suffered in your heart. / But truly for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil, / since, after toiling much, you came to the house of a man / who is kind” (15.486–490; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]), the kind man being Laertes, his own father; “but I / have come here after wandering through many cities of mortals” (15.491–492). The disguise holds to the end of the night.

**His names in this book.** “divine Odysseus”, Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο (15.63, 15.313, 15.347, 15.554; see [[divine]]); “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (15.340; see [[much-enduring]] and [[brilliant]]); “Odysseus of many wiles” (15.380; see [[of-many-wiles]]); “Odysseus, sprung from Zeus” (15.485; see [[sprung-from-zeus]]); and to his son's companions and hosts, “the dear son of Odysseus” is Telemachus (15.59, 15.337; see [[dear-son-of-odysseus]]).
