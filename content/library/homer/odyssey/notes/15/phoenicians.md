summary: In Eumaeus' story the Phoenicians are traders who come to his island with “countless baubles”, stay a year trading, win over his father's Phoenician slave woman and sail away with her and the child, whom they sell to Laertes on Ithaca (15.415–484).

**Men famed for ships.** “And there came Phoenicians, men famed for ships, / greedy rogues, bringing countless baubles in a black ship” (415–416; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[syrie]]). ναυσίκλυτοι, “famed for ships”, is otherwise the Phaeacians' epithet (7.39, 8.191, 8.369, 13.166, 16.227; see [[famed-for-ships]] and the translation's key-word row), one of the likenesses that led Mure to take the Phaeacians for a satire on a Phoenician colony (see [[phaeacians]]). τρῶκται, “greedy rogues”, is the word of the Phoenician who took the Cretan of the false tale to Phoenicia (14.289; see the Book 14 note), and D. B. Monro refers to his note there, where the noun is a “gnawer” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.416, 14.289). ἀθύρματα, “baubles”, are the traders' wares, the word of the playthings that a boy builds in the sand in the Iliad (*Iliad* 15.363; see [[the-boy-and-the-sand]] and the translation's key-word row).

**The artful Phoenicians.** “And there was in my father's house a Phoenician woman, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork; / and the artful Phoenicians deceived her” (417–419; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). Φοίνισσα, the feminine of the people's name, and πολυπαίπαλοι, “artful”, are found only here in Homer. One of them lay with her by the hollow ship as she was washing clothes, and when she had told him that she came from Sidon and had been sold to the king's house by Taphian pirates, he offered to take her home (420–433; see [[sidon]] and [[taphians]]). The traders swear the oath she asks (436–438; see [[oath]]), and she promises them the child, “he would fetch you a price beyond counting, / wherever you sell him among men of another tongue” (452–453).

**Traders' talk.** Monro hears the trade in their words. καλέονται, in “they are still alive and are reputed rich” (433), “looks like a trader's way of speaking”; ὦνον ὁδαίων, “the buying of your cargo” (445), is “the buying (lit. the price) of your freight”, ὁδαῖα being “things belonging to the voyage”, hence the cargo taken in on the way; and ἐπίβαθρον (449) is “passage money” (Monro on 15.433, 15.445, 15.449; see the translation's note on 15.433, 15.445). The traders “stayed there among us for a whole year, / and got much substance by trading for their hollow ship” (455–456; see [[trade]]): the Phoenician of the false tale had kept his Cretan for a full year's round before he shipped him off to sell (14.292–297). Monro counts “the signs of an active Phoenician commerce” among the marks of a time of peace and prosperity that the Odyssey shows more plainly than the Iliad, with “the splendour shown in the palaces of Alcinous and Menelaus, and even (in its way) in the homestead of Eumaeus” (Monro, Appendix II §6).

**The Phoenician ship.** “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark; / and we went quickly and came to the famous harbor, / where the swift-sailing ship of the Phoenician men was” (471–473; see [[harbor]] and [[swift-sailing-ships]]). They sail six days with a fair wind from Zeus; on the seventh Artemis strikes the woman, and they throw her overboard “to become a spoil for seals and fish”; the wind and the water bring them to Ithaca, “where Laertes bought me with his own possessions” (474–483; see [[laertes]], [[seals]], [[like-a-sea-bird]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). The Phoenicians whom Odysseus' false tales made honest sailors and a dishonest dealer in men (13.271–277, 14.287–297; see the Book 13 and Book 14 notes) are here, in a story the poem tells as true, both traders and kidnappers. Plato's judgment of the Phoenicians' love of money is given in the Book 14 note.
