summary: δῆμος as land and as people: Melampus, an exile, “came to the land of other men”, Argos (15.228, 15.238); on Syrie “Famine never comes upon its people” (15.407); the counsellors of Eumaeus' father had gone out “to the session and the talk of the people” (15.468); and Theoclymenus foretells that no race is more kingly “in the land of Ithaca” (15.534).
# δήμοιο (15.468; again 16.75, 19.527, in the same phrase), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [δήμοιο]
---

**The land of other men.** In the seer's genealogy Melampus “came to the land of other men, fleeing his native land / and great-hearted Neleus” (ἄλλων δῆμον ἀφίκετο, 15.228–229), and, when he had won the cattle and the bride, “he himself came to the land of other men, / to horse-pasturing Argos” (15.238–239; see [[melampus]], [[argos]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The land of other men is Argos, as we learn when the words are taken up again (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.228); his great-grandson comes to Telemachus “fleeing from Argos” (15.224; see [[theoclymenus]]).

**The people of Syrie.** Eumaeus' island is blessed: “Famine never comes upon its people, nor does any / hateful sickness besides fall upon wretched mortals” (15.407–408; see [[syrie]] and [[nousos]]).

**The talk of the people.** The cups the nurse stole were left in the porch by the banqueters of the king's house: “they had gone out to the session and the talk of the people” (δήμοιό τε φῆμιν, 15.468; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]], [[assembly]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). θῶκος is the sitting-place or tribunal, and φῆμις, which elsewhere means the talk of the people, is here the place of talking, like ἀγορή (Monro on 15.466–468; see the translation's note on 15.466–468). Elsewhere the phrase is the talk Penelope respects, with her husband's bed (16.75, 19.527).

**The land of Ithaca.** Reading the falcon, [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] tells Telemachus, “There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (ἐν δήμῳ Ἰθάκης, 15.533–534; see [[basileus]], [[genee]], [[ithaca]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).
