---
summary: At dawn Menelaus rises from his bed beside Helen and comes to his departing guests, and Eteoneus rises from his to kindle the fire (15.58, 15.96); in Eumaeus' story one of the Phoenicians lies with the nurse “in bed and love” (15.420–421).
---

**Rising at dawn.** “And Menelaus, good at the war cry, came near them, / having risen from his bed, from beside Helen of the lovely hair” (15.57–58; see [[menelaus]], [[helen]] and [[lovely-haired]]), and Eteoneus came near him, “having risen from his bed, since he lived not far from him” (15.95–96; see [[eteoneus]] and [[fire]]): the half-verse ἀνστὰς ἐξ εὐνῆς, found only in these two places, rouses the king and his squire for the leave-taking. Telemachus had lain awake through the night with cares for his father, while Peisistratus slept beside him in the porch (15.4–8; see [[house]] and [[sweet-sleep]]).

**In bed and love.** The Phoenician trader “lay with her by the hollow ship / in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (15.420–422; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[eperopeuo]] and [[womenfolk]]). εὐνῇ καὶ φιλότητι reverses the usual φιλότητι καὶ εὐνῇ, “in love and bed”, said of Paris and Helen on the island of Cranae and of Demeter and Iasion (*Iliad* 3.445; 5.126; see [[philotes]]), and keeps the order of Circe's invitation, “having lain together / in bed and love, we may trust one another” (10.334–335). The next verse ends as the shade of Agamemnon ended his words on Clytemnestra's shame, which falls on “the womenfolk, even on one who does good” (11.434).
