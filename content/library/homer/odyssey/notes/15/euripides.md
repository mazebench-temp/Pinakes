---
summary: Euripides' Amphiaraus, “the son of Oicles”, is the seer of the Seven, who goes to Thebes against his judgment and whom the gods take alive into the earth with his chariot, and his Alcmaeon kills his mother at his father's charge for the necklace brought to Argos; this book names both in the line of Melampus. Euripides also has the verse on the sweetness of remembered toils that Aristotle sets beside the swineherd's, a Polynices for whom exile is the greatest of evils, a Dawn who snatched up a husband from Troy as Dawn snatched up Cleitus, and a king of Egypt named Theoclymenus.
---

- **The seer of the Seven.** From the palace roof the old servant shows Antigone “the seer Amphiaraus”, driving a white chariot, with the victims for sacrifice beside him; in the battle he goes to the Proetid gates with victims on his chariot, “not bearing insolent emblems, but arms prudently unmarked” (*Phoenician Women* 172–174, 1109–1112, plain renderings; see [[euripides-teichoskopia]]). In the *Suppliant Women* Adrastus confesses to Theseus that he went to war “against the will of Amphiaraus”; the Theban herald asks whether a chasm did not swallow “the bird-watching seer” (οἰωνοσκόπον) with his four-horse chariot; and Theseus says that the gods “snatched up alive into the depths of the earth / the noble son of Oicles, with his four horses, and praise him openly” (*Suppliant Women* 158, 500–501, 925–927, plain renderings). The poem says only that Zeus and Apollo loved him, and that “he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.245–247): [[amphiaraus]], [[oicles]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]].
- **The necklace.** In the *Alcmeon* the son says, “Above all my father urged me on with his command, / when he mounted his chariot to go to Thebes”, and the play spoke of “a golden necklace” brought “to the city of Argos” (frr. 69–70 Nauck, plain renderings). The poem names the gifts and the sons, “for the sake of gifts to a woman. / His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus” (15.247–248), and tells neither the charge nor the matricide: [[alcmaeon]], [[amphilochus]], [[eriphyle]], [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]] and [[necklace]].
- **Sweet to remember.** “But it is sweet, once safe, to remember toils” (*Andromeda* fr. 133 Nauck, a plain rendering) is the verse that Aristotle quotes beside the swineherd's “for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows” (*Rhetoric* 1370b4–6; 15.400–401), and Cicero renders it in Latin as a verse of Euripides that everyone knows (*On Ends* 2.105; see [[cicero]]): [[sweet-to-remember-toils]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]].
- **The worst of evils.** Jocasta asks her exiled son, “What is it to be deprived of one's native land? Is it a great evil?”, and Polynices answers, “The greatest; and in fact greater than in word”; the hardest thing, he says, is that the exile has no freedom of speech, and Jocasta answers, “That is a slave's lot, not to say what one thinks” (*Phoenician Women* 388–392, plain renderings). In this book the disguised Odysseus says, “There is nothing worse for mortals than roaming” (15.343); the exile [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] must “wander among men” (15.276); and the swineherd, a slave since childhood, says that the slaves greatly long “to speak face to face with their mistress and learn of everything” (15.376–377): [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]], [[exile-and-refuge]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]].
- **Snatched by Dawn.** The chorus of Trojan women sing that the light of white-winged Day saw the ruin of Pergamum, though she had in her chamber a husband from that land, the father of her children, “whom the golden four-horse chariot of the stars / snatched up and carried off”; the gods' love for Troy is gone (*Trojan Women* 847–858, plain renderings). In this book “Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (15.250–251): [[dawn-and-orion]], [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]] and [[eos]].
- **Another Theoclymenus.** In the *Helen* the son of Proteus, king of Egypt, who wants Helen for his wife, is called Theoclymenus, and a phrase that editors bracket explains the name, “because he passed his life revering the gods”; his sister, the prophetess Theonoe, “knew all things divine, the things that are and the things to come” (*Helen* 8–15, plain renderings). The seer of this book bears the same name (15.256): [[euripides-helen]] and [[theoclymenus]].
