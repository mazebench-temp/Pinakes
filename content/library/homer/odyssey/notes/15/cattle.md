summary: The cattle of Book 15 are the loud-bellowing herd that Melampus drove from Phylace to Pylos (15.235–236), the herd beside which a boy might be seized by raiders, as Odysseus asks Eumaeus (15.386–388), and the wealth of the island Syrie, “good for cattle, good for sheep” (15.406).

**Melampus' cattle.** Tracing the line of the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], the poet goes back to his ancestor Melampus, who lay bound for a year in the halls of Phylacus: “But he escaped his doom and drove the loud-bellowing cattle / to Pylos from Phylace, and took vengeance for the shameful deed / on godlike Neleus, and for his brother he brought the woman / home” (15.235–238; see [[melampus]], [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]] and [[phylace]]). Book 15 does not say why he wanted them. Book 11 does: Neleus would give his daughter Pero to no one “who did not drive the broad-browed cattle with curving horns / out of Phylace, the cattle of mighty Iphiclus” (11.289–290; see [[pero]], [[iphiclus]] and the Book 11 note). The cattle are a bride-price, won for a brother (see [[bride-price]] and [[bias]]). ἐριμύκους, “loud-bellowing”, is said of oxen only here in the Odyssey; the Iliad has it of the oxen that tread out the barley and of the oxen slain for Patroclus' funeral, whose dung lies on the course (*Iliad* 20.495–497, 23.775–776; see [[loud-bellowing]]).

**Alone with the herds.** Hearing that Eumaeus was reared in Laertes' house, Odysseus understands that he was still small when he was driven from home, and asks how: “was the wide-streeted city of men sacked, / in which your father and lady mother lived, / or were you alone with the sheep or the cattle / when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house, and he paid a worthy price for you?” (15.384–388; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Odysseus imagines the two ways in which a king's son is made a slave (see [[slavery-and-freedom]]), and the Iliad knows both: Achilles killed Andromache's seven brothers “among their shambling cattle and white sheep” on the day he sacked her father's city, and Aeneas tells how Achilles came upon “our cattle” on Ida and drove him off before sacking Lyrnessus and Pedasus (*Iliad* 6.421–424, 20.89–92; see the Book 15 note on [[sheep]]). βουσίν, “the cattle”, is the dative of both Iliad passages.

**Good for cattle.** Syrie, in Eumaeus' account, is “not so very populous, but a good one, / good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat” (15.405–406; see [[syrie]] and [[sheep]]). εὔβοτος is found only here in Homer (see the translation's row περιπληθής; εὔβοτος, εὔμηλος, οἰνοπληθής, πολύπυρος). It belongs with the words of pasture made from βόσκω, “feed”: Ithaca is αἰγίβοτος for Telemachus, “a goat-pasturing land, and lovelier than a horse-pasturing one” (4.606; see [[horse-pasturing]]), and for Athena “it is good goat-pasturing and cattle-pasturing land” (αἰγίβοτος … καὶ βούβοτος, 13.246; see the Book 13 note). εὔβοτος is “of good pasture”, and the translation renders it “good for cattle”. The herdsmen whom Telemachus goes to see on landing are βοτῆρες, from the same root (15.504).
