---
summary: The beggar speaks of himself as of another man: “And I might go to the house of divine Odysseus / and tell my news to prudent Penelope” (15.313–314), and “come, tell me of the mother of divine Odysseus, / and of his father” (15.347–348); and Telemachus is “the dear son of divine Odysseus” (15.63, 15.554).
---

Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο closes the verse four times in the book. πρὸς δώματʼ Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο is the verse-end of going to his house (2.394, 4.799); in the beggar's mouth it is a stranger's name for the master, as he tests the swineherd with his plan to beg in the city and serve the suitors (15.304–324). His question about his own mother and father brings Eumaeus' account of Laertes and of Anticleia's death (15.346–379; see [[odysseus]], [[laertes]] and [[anticleia]]). For Telemachus' verse see [[dear-son-of-odysseus]].
