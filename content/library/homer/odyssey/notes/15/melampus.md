**The ancestor of the seer.** The man who comes to Telemachus' ship at Pylos is “a seer; and by birth he was a descendant of Melampus” (15.225; see [[theoclymenus]]), ἔκγονος being “great grandson” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.225). Before he names the man, the poet tells his ancestor's story, the second time the Odyssey tells it: the catalogue of heroines told it without his name, of the “noble seer” who undertook to drive the cattle of Iphiclus for Pero's hand (11.287–297; see [[pero]] and [[iphiclus]]). ὅς, “who”, is Melampus, as at 11.291 (Monro on 15.226).

**Pylos and Phylace.** He “once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks, / a rich man among the Pylians, living in a house far above the rest” (15.226–227; see [[pylos]], [[mother-of-flocks]] and [[pylians]]), Πυλίοισι being a locatival dative with μέγʼ ἔξοχα δώματα ναίων (Monro on 15.227). “But then he came to the land of other men, fleeing his native land / and great-hearted Neleus, the most noble of living men, / who for a full year's round held his many goods / by force” (15.228–231; see [[neleus]]): the land of other men is Argos, as the words taken up at 238 show (Monro on 15.228). Meanwhile, “in the halls of Phylacus, / he lay bound in a grievous bond, suffering bitter pains / for the sake of the daughter of Neleus and the heavy blind folly / which the goddess, the house-smiting Erinys, had put in his mind” (15.231–234; see [[phylacus]], [[ate]] and [[erinyes]]). δασπλῆτις is “smiter of houses”, the first part of the compound being probably from the root of δεσπότης, “house-master” (Monro on 15.234; see the translation's key words). Book 11 laid his bonds on “a hard fate of a god” (11.292); here the cause is an ἄτη set in his mind by an Erinys, as Agamemnon lays his on “Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind” (*Iliad* 19.87–88).

**The cattle and Argos.** “But he escaped his doom and drove the loud-bellowing cattle / to Pylos from Phylace, and took vengeance for the shameful deed / on godlike Neleus, and for his brother he brought the woman / home” (15.235–238; see [[cattle]], [[loud-bellowing]], [[phylace]], [[bias]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]). Then he went himself to “horse-pasturing Argos; for there it was his lot / to dwell, ruling over many Argives” (15.239–240; see [[argos]], [[horse-pasturing]] and [[argives]]), ἀνάσσοντʼ being an accusative with the unexpressed subject of ναιέμεναι (Monro on 15.240), “and there he married a wife and built a high-roofed house, / and begot Antiphates and Mantius, two mighty sons” (15.241–242; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]] and [[mantius]]). From the two sons the pedigree runs to Amphiaraus in one line and to [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] in the other (15.243–256; see [[amphiaraus]] and [[polypheides]]). Why he ruled in Argos the poem does not say; Herodotus' story of his fee for curing the Argive women is told in the entry.
