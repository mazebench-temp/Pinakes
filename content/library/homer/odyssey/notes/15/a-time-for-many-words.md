summary: Eumaeus gives the thought of Odysseus' saying among the Phaeacians in his own words (15.392–394): these nights are endless, “there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen”, and much sleep too is a vexation. Now the king's son who was sold is the teller, and Odysseus the listener.
# The swineherd's version of the saying (15.392-393); its last half-verse is much-sleep-is-a-vexation.
refs: [15.392-393]
---

**The saying turned.** Among the Phaeacians it was Odysseus who broke off his tale because it was time to sleep, and Alcinous who answered that the night was endless and not yet the hour; Odysseus granted that there is a time for many words and a time also for sleep (11.330–332, 11.373–379; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). In the hut the roles are reversed. The host is the one who will tell, and he gives both halves of the saying at once, with ἔστι for ὥρη: “there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen” (15.392–393). The infinitive ἀκούειν is Aristarchus' reading, where the manuscripts have the epic ἀκουέμεν (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.393). The night is ἀθέσφατος as Alcinous' was (15.392; 11.373; see [[endless]]), and the half-verse that ends the sentence, “much sleep too is a vexation”, takes the swineherd's side of the dispute (15.394; see [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]]).

**Who listens.** Odysseus has told his own false tale in the hut the night before (14.192–359; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Tonight the man who was a king's son tells a true one, and the master learns how his swineherd came to his father's house (15.403–484; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
