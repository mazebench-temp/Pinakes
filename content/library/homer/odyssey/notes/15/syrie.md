---
summary: Eumaeus begins the story of his birth with his island, Syrie, “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are”, a good land where no famine and no sickness come, but Apollo and Artemis kill the old with their gentle arrows; it has two cities, and his father Ctesius was king over both (15.403–414).
---

{{quote:d951e1cd-2bdc-590b-b8e5-2ac69f0a2255}}

**An island called Syrie.** The stranger has asked whether Eumaeus' city was sacked, or whether hostile men took him in their ships when he was alone with the sheep or the cattle (381–388; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), and Eumaeus answers with an island. νῆσός τις … κικλήσκεται, “There is an island called”, opens the story as a place is brought into a story elsewhere: Menelaus begins his account of Pharos, “Now there is an island in the sea of many waves” (4.354), and the poet brings in the suitors' island, “There is a rocky island in the middle of the sea” (4.844; see [[pharos]] and [[asteris]]). εἴ που ἀκούεις, “if perhaps you have heard of it”, allows that the stranger may not know the place. Syrie is not named again in Homer.

**A good island.** It is “not so very populous, but a good one, / good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat” (405–406). In λίην τόσον, τόσον insists on the word before it “as not too much”, as in Eidothea's “Are you so utterly a fool” (λίην τόσον, 4.371; see [[eidothea]]): the island, D. B. Monro paraphrases, “is large, but not quite to be called very large” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.405). Three of the four epithets of the next verse are found only here in Homer, εὔβοτος, εὔμηλος (elsewhere only the name Eumelus; see [[eumelus]]) and οἰνοπληθής; πολύπυρος, “rich in wheat”, is said of Dulichium (14.335, 16.396, 19.292) and in the Iliad of Buprasium and of Argos (*Iliad* 11.756, 15.372; see [[rich-in-wheat]]). “Famine never comes upon its people, nor does any / hateful sickness besides fall upon wretched mortals” (407–408): ἄλλη νοῦσος is “disease as well”, by a familiar idiom, for famine is not counted a disease (Monro on 15.407; see [[nousos]] and the translation's note on 15.407). πείνη, “famine”, is found only here in Homer.

**The gentle arrows.** No one there dies of sickness: “but when the tribes of men grow old throughout the city, / Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis / and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them” (409–411; see [[gentle-arrows]], [[apollo]] and [[artemis]]). The last verse is the one in which Artemis killed Orion in Ortygia, with the god in place of the goddess (5.124; see [[ortygia]]).

**Two cities and a king.** “There are two cities, and everything is divided between them; / and over both of them my father was king, / Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals” (412–414; see [[ctesius]] and [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]]). The rest of the story shows the king's city as Eumaeus remembered it: his father's “high-roofed house” (424, 432), the session and “the talk of the people” to which the banqueters went out (468; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]), the streets that grew dark at sunset and “the famous harbor” where the Phoenician ship lay (471–473; see [[harbor]] and [[phoenicians]]).

**Where the sun turns.** The island lies “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (404; see [[ortygia]] and [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]]). Strabo and W. W. Merry took it for Syros in the Cyclades (see the general article). For Monro both islands have “a mythical character, as the ‘turning points’ of the sun”, as Circe's island has “the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4; see [[aeaea]]), “and also in respect of the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants” (Monro on 15.403). The translation's note on 15.403–404 follows him, and gives both explanations of the turnings, the solstices or the west where the sun turns.
