---
summary: Virgil's guest-gifts carry the marks of this book's. Andromache gives the boy Ascanius garments as “remembrances of my hands”, in Helen's words to Telemachus; Aeneas sends Dido the finery of Helen herself; and the Aeneid's mixing bowls, one given to Anchises by Cisseus and one by “Sidonian Dido”, have histories like the Sidonian bowl of Menelaus. Virgil's hawk plucking a dove in the clouds, his “perhaps one day it will be a pleasure to remember even these things”, and his Melampus, a master healer, have their counterparts here too.
---

- **Remembrances of the hands.** At Buthrotum Andromache, sad at the last parting, brings Ascanius garments embroidered with gold thread and a Phrygian cloak and loads him with woven gifts: “Take these too, boy, to be remembrances of my hands, and to bear witness to the long love of Andromache, Hector's wife” (*accipe et haec, manuum tibi quae monimenta mearum / sint, puer, et longum Andromachae testentur amorem*, *Aeneid* 3.482–488, plain renderings). Helen's words to Telemachus are “I too give you this gift, dear child, / a remembrance of Helen's hands” (15.125–126): [[robe]], [[helens-web]] and [[guest-gifts]].
- **Helen's finery.** Among the gifts saved from the ruins of Ilium that Aeneas sends to Dido are a mantle stiff with figures and gold and a veil bordered with yellow acanthus, “the adornments of Argive Helen, which she had carried from Mycenae when she sought Pergamum and her forbidden marriage, the wondrous gift of her mother Leda” (*ornatus Argivae Helenae*, *Aeneid* 1.647–652, plain renderings). In this book Helen gives a robe of her own making for another marriage, “for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear” (15.126–127): [[robe]] and [[helen]].
- **Bowls with a history.** After the omen of the burning arrow Aeneas gives Acestes “a mixing bowl embossed with figures, which Thracian Cisseus once gave my father Anchises to carry, a great gift, as a memorial and pledge of his love” (*Aeneid* 5.533–538, plain renderings); and Ascanius promises Nisus, if he brings back his father, two silver cups rough with figures, twin tripods, two talents of gold, and “an ancient mixing bowl, which Sidonian Dido gives” (*cratera antiquum quem dat Sidonia Dido*, *Aeneid* 9.263–266, plain renderings). Menelaus gives Telemachus a two-handled cup and a silver mixing bowl with a history of its own, “The hero Phaedimus gave it to me, / the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (15.102–103, 15.113–122), and offers him tripods and cauldrons and a golden goblet on a tour of Hellas (15.80–85): [[mixing-bowl]], [[phaedimus]], [[sidonians]], [[cup]] and [[tripod]].
- **The hawk and the dove.** Camilla catches and kills her enemy as easily as “the hawk, the sacred bird, from a high rock overtakes on its wings a dove high in a cloud, and seizes and holds it and tears it with its hooked feet; then blood and plucked feathers fall from the sky” (*accipiter saxo sacer ales ab alto*, *Aeneid* 11.721–724, plain renderings). The falcon of this book is “the swift messenger of Apollo; and in its feet / it held a dove and was plucking it, and it poured the feathers down on the ground” (15.526–527), and the seer reads it as a sign for the house of Odysseus (15.531–534): [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]], [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]] and [[falcon]].
- **Perhaps one day.** Aeneas ends his speech to his shipwrecked men on the Libyan shore with *forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit*, “perhaps one day it will be a pleasure to remember even these things” (*Aeneid* 1.203, a plain rendering). The swineherd invites his guest to that pleasure now: “for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows, / whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (15.400–401): [[we-will-remember-these-things-too]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]].
- **Melampus the healer.** In the plague that once fell on the herds of the Noric Alps, Virgil says, the remedies sought out did harm, and “the masters gave up, Chiron son of Philyra and Melampus son of Amythaon” (*cessere magistri, / Phillyrides Chiron Amythaoniusque Melampus*, *Georgics* 3.474–481, 3.549–550, plain renderings). The Melampus of this book drove off cattle, “the loud-bellowing cattle / to Pylos from Phylace”, and was a seer, the ancestor of seers (15.225–256): [[melampus]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]].
