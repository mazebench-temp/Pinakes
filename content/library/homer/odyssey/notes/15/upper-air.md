# αἰθέρι (15.523), in αἰθέρι ναίων, as the Iliad's forms take it (Il. 2.412, 4.166); the only αἰθέρι of the Odyssey.
grc: [αἰθέρι]
---

**The wind through the upper air.** “And gray-eyed Athena sent them a following wind, / blowing hard and wild through the upper air, so that as quickly as possible / the ship, running, might cross the salt water of the sea” (292–294; see [[athena]], [[the-winds]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The first verse sent the ship out from Ithaca in the second book, where the wind is the West Wind (2.420–421; see [[zephyrus]]). λάβρος ἐπαιγίζων, “blowing hard and wild”, is said in the Iliad of the West Wind that bows a field of grain in a simile (*Iliad* 2.147–148; see the translation's key-word row), and the *Hymn to Apollo* has the second and third of these verses, with ἐξ αἰθέρος, “from the upper air”, for διʼ αἰθέρος, of the West Wind that brings the Cretan ship to Crisa (*Hymn to Apollo* 433–435; see [[cruni]]). For ἐπαιγίζοντα Aristophanes read ἐπαΐσσοντα, “rushing” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.293).

**Who dwells in the upper air.** On Ithaca Telemachus leaves the suitors' fate to Zeus: “But Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air, knows this, / whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (523–524; see [[zeus]], [[dwelling-in-the-upper-air]] and [[the-suitors]]). W. W. Merry recalled the title when he explained the αἰθήρ of Hermes' flight in Book 5 as the clear air of the mountain heights (see the Book 5 note).
