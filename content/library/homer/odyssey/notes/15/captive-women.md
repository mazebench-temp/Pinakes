---
summary: Eumaeus' nurse was a captive: a rich man's daughter of Sidon, seized by Taphian pirates as she came from the fields and sold to the king of Syrie, in whose house she nursed the king's son (15.417–429, 15.450–451). She bargains her way home with the Phoenician traders, paying with the child, and dies at sea (15.430–481).
# Unnamed: the nurse's account of her capture (15.425-429).
refs: [15.425-429]
---

**Taken and sold.** Asked by the trader who has lain with her who she is, the Phoenician woman points out the king's house and tells her story: “I claim to be from Sidon, rich in bronze, / and I am the daughter of Arybas, whose wealth came in a flood; / but Taphian men, pirates, seized me / as I was coming from the fields, and brought me here and sold me / to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me” (15.425–429; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[arybas]], [[taphians]] and [[piracy]]). Her capture is the one the stranger had imagined for the swineherd, a child taken alone in the country by men with ships (15.386–388); the Iliad's captives are taken when their cities fall, though Achilles once caught two sons of Priam on Ida as they herded sheep, and released them for a ransom (*Iliad* 11.104–106; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[trade]]). In the king's house she has the work and the trust of a captive woman: she is “skilled in splendid handiwork” and she rears the king's child (15.418, 15.450–451; see [[weaving]]).

**The way home.** The trader offers what a captive longs for, to see “the high-roofed house of your father and mother” again (15.431–433). She asks only for an oath that they will bring her home unharmed (15.435–436; see [[oath]]), and pays her passage with gold and with the child she nursed, to be sold “among men of another tongue” (15.448–453). A captive who escapes by making another captive, she does not reach home: on the seventh day of the voyage Artemis strikes her, and she is thrown overboard (15.477–481; see [[artemis]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
