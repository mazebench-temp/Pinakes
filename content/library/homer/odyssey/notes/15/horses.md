summary: The horses of Book 15 are Nestor's pair, which Telemachus wants yoked in the night (15.46–47) and the two young men yoke after the farewell meal at Sparta (15.145); Menelaus stands before them with the parting cup, the eagle of the omen darts across in front of them, and they carry Telemachus back to Pylos in the verses of the journey out (15.150, 15.164, 15.182–193), until Peisistratus drives them home alone (15.215–216).

**Before dawn.** Athena has told Telemachus to go home, and he wakes Peisistratus with his heel: “Wake up, Peisistratus, son of Nestor; bring the single-hoofed horses / and yoke them under the chariot, so that we may make our way” (15.46–47; see [[peisistratus]] and [[single-hoofed]]). The son of the horseman Nestor knows better: “there is no way, eager though we are for our journey, / to drive through the dark night; and soon it will be dawn” (15.49–50), and they must wait for Menelaus' gifts (15.51–55). Menelaus then offers to yoke the horses himself and guide Telemachus through Hellas and the heart of Argos, where every host would give him a gift (15.80–85; see [[hellas]] and the translation's note on 15.78–85, verses that Aristarchus obelized). Telemachus has refused his horses once already, since Ithaca is no land for driving them (4.601–608; see the Book 4 note), and now he refuses the tour.

**The verses of the road.** After the meal the two young men “yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot, / and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade” (15.145–146), the verses with which they had left Pherae on the way out (3.492–493; again 15.190–191; see [[chariot]] and [[inlaid]]). The colonnade across the gateway was loud-resounding, ἐρίδουπος, D. B. Monro explains, because it echoed to the tramp of the horses (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.146; see [[loud-resounding]]). Menelaus follows them with wine in a golden cup, “He stood before the horses, and pledging them he spoke” (15.150; see [[kind-as-a-father]] and [[libation]]), as Hecuba stood before Priam's horses with the cup when he set out for Achilles' hut (*Iliad* 24.286). Then the eagle comes, and “darted / to the right, in front of the horses” (15.163–164; see [[eagle]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]): it crosses the chariot's path in the lucky direction (Monro on 15.160).

**Back to Pylos.** “He spoke, and laid the whip on the horses; and very quickly / they sped through the city toward the plain, eager” (15.182–183). ἐφʼ ἵπποιϊν is a dual, as the Iliad's charioteers at the start of the race “raised their whips over their horses” (ἐφʼ ἵπποιιν μάστιγας ἄειραν, *Iliad* 23.362), and μάστιν is a rare form of the whip, found once more in Homer, in the dative, when Diomedes drives his horses on in the same race (μάστι, *Iliad* 23.500). The rest is the journey out, told again: “all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side” (15.184 = 3.486), the night at Pherae with Diocles (15.185–188 = 3.487–490; see [[pherae-in-messenia]] and [[diocles]]), the yoking at dawn (15.189–191), and “the pair flew on not unwilling” (15.192; see [[chariot-journey]]), where the text prints the contracted οὐκ ἄκοντε for the ἀέκοντε of the formula (see the translation's note on 15.192). On the way out the swift horses carried them across the wheat-bearing plain to Lacedaemon (3.495–497); now “quickly then they came to the steep citadel of Pylos” (15.193; see [[pylos]]).

**Home without him.** Telemachus asks not to be taken past the ship, and Peisistratus “turned the horses to the swift ship and the shore of the sea” (15.205), unloads Menelaus' gifts into the stern, and sends him aboard before Nestor can keep him (15.206–214; see [[nestor]]). “So he spoke, and drove the fine-maned horses / back to the city of the Pylians, and quickly reached the house” (15.215–216; see [[fine-maned]] and [[pylians]]). The horses were Nestor's, and they go back to Pylos; the rest of the way is by ship, to an island that, as Telemachus told Menelaus, is no land for horses (4.605–608).
