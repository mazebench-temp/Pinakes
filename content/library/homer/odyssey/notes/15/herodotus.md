---
summary: Herodotus begins his Histories with Phoenician traders who set out their cargo at Argos and carry off the king's daughter, a story the Phoenicians told as the seduction and willing flight of Io, as the swineherd's nurse goes off with the traders of this book. He quotes the verses of the Sidonian robes, which this book turns to Helen's own robes; he tells of Croesus' gifts to Amphiaraus, of the price that Melampus asked for curing the women of Argos, and of the cities of Amphilochus; and his Syria, the land of the Phoenicians, has the name of Eumaeus' island.
---

- **Phoenicians at Argos.** The learned men of Persia, Herodotus says, blame the Phoenicians for the quarrel: they came to Argos with Egyptian and Assyrian wares and set out their cargo, and on the fifth or sixth day, when nearly everything had been sold, the women came down to the shore, among them the king's daughter Io; the Phoenicians rushed on them and carried her off to Egypt. The Phoenicians tell it otherwise: at Argos Io “lay with the master of the ship”, and when she found that she was with child, she sailed away with them of her own will, for shame before her parents (*Histories* 1.1.1–4, 1.5.2, plain renderings). The traders of this book come to Syrie with countless baubles and stay a whole year; one of them lies with the king's Phoenician nurse by the ship, and she bargains for her passage home and goes with them, taking the king's son (15.415–475). Herodotus does not name Homer; the likeness is one of kind: [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[syrie]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[phoenicians]] and [[trade]].
- **The robes from Sidon.** Homer, Herodotus says, knew that Alexander was driven off course with Helen and came to Sidon, and showed it in the Aristeia of Diomedes, in the verses of Hecuba's robes, “where her robes were, richly embroidered, the work of women / of the Sidonians, whom godlike Alexander himself / had brought from Sidon, sailing over the wide sea / on that voyage on which he brought back Helen” (*Histories* 2.116.2–3, quoting *Iliad* 6.289–292). This book repeats those verses for Helen's robes at Sparta, “where her robes were, richly embroidered, which she had made herself”, and the Sidonians it names are the givers of Menelaus' bowl (15.105, 15.117–118). For Herodotus Sidon belongs to the Phoenicians, “and the Phoenicians, to whom Sidon belongs, live in Syria” (ἐν τῇ Συρίῃ, *Histories* 2.116.6, a plain rendering), the name, in the same form, of Eumaeus' island, Syrie (15.403): [[robe]], [[helens-web]], [[sidonians]], [[mixing-bowl]], [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[syrie]].
- **Amphiaraus' oracle.** Croesus tested the oracles of Greece and Libya, among them that of Amphiaraus, and judged it a true one; to Amphiaraus, “having learned of his valor and his suffering”, he dedicated a shield all of gold and a spear of solid gold, which were still at Thebes in Herodotus' day (*Histories* 1.46.2, 1.49, 1.52, plain renderings). No Theban might sleep in his shrine, because Amphiaraus had once bidden the Thebans choose him either as their seer or as their ally, and they chose him as their ally (*Histories* 8.134.2). In this book Zeus and Apollo loved him, “but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.245–247): [[amphiaraus]] and [[oicles]].
- **Melampus' price.** When the women of Argos went mad and the Argives sent to Pylos for Melampus, he asked half the kingdom as his fee; they refused, more women went mad, and when they came back to accept, he asked a third for his brother Bias as well (*Histories* 9.34, plain renderings). This book tells how Melampus left Pylos for “horse-pasturing Argos; for there it was his lot / to dwell, ruling over many Argives” (15.239–240), and how earlier he had won a wife for his brother with Neleus' cattle (15.231–238): [[melampus]], [[bias]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]].
- **Amphilochus.** Herodotus' fifth province of the Persian empire begins at Posideion, “which Amphilochus son of Amphiaraus founded on the borders of the Cilicians and the Syrians”, and the Pamphylians are descended from those who were scattered from Troy with Amphilochus and Calchas (*Histories* 3.91.1, 7.91, plain renderings). Homer names Amphilochus only in this book, as a son of Amphiaraus (15.248), and nowhere at Troy: [[amphilochus]].
- **Amber.** Tin and amber, Herodotus says, come from the ends of the earth, though he will not believe in a river Eridanus from which amber is said to come (*Histories* 3.115). The Phoenician trader of this book brings “a golden necklace, strung here and there with amber” (15.460): [[amber]] and [[necklace]].
