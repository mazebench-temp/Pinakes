---
summary: Three mothers: Penelope, whom Telemachus must hurry home to find unmarried (15.15), who will keep Helen's robe for his bride (15.127), and who will not see the stranger (15.515); Anticleia, the mother of Odysseus, of whom the beggar asks (15.347); and Eumaeus' “lady mother”, who handled the Phoenician's necklace while the nurse plotted (15.461).
---

**Penelope.** Athena sends Telemachus home “so that you may still find your noble mother at home” (15.15), before her father and brothers marry her to Eurymachus (15.16–18; see [[penelope]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). Helen gives him a robe for his bride, “but until then let it lie with your dear mother / in the hall” (15.127–128; see [[robe]] and [[helen]]). On Ithaca the seer asks, “Or shall I go straight to your mother and your house?” (15.511), and Telemachus explains why not: “my mother / will not see you; for she does not often show herself to the suitors in the house, / but away from them she weaves at her loom in the upper room” (15.515–517; see [[weaving]] and [[upper-room]]); the man he names instead is the one most eager “to marry my mother” (15.522; see [[eurymachus]]).

**Anticleia.** In the hut the beggar asks, “come, tell me of the mother of divine Odysseus, / and of his father” (15.347–348), and hears of her death from grief for her son (15.358–360; see [[anticleia]] and [[achos]]), as he had heard it from her shade (11.202–203).

**Eumaeus' mother.** Odysseus asks whether the city “in which your father and lady mother lived” was sacked (πατὴρ καὶ πότνια μήτηρ, 15.385; see [[queenly]]). In the swineherd's story his mother is busy with the trader's wares when her son is stolen: “In the hall the slave women and my lady mother / were handling it and gazing at it with their eyes, / offering a price; and he nodded to her in silence” (15.461–463; see [[necklace]], [[neuo]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), and while she bargained for the necklace the nurse led her son out of the house (15.464–465; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). The sailor had offered the nurse the sight of her own mother: “so that you may see the high-roofed house of your father and mother” (15.432). Pylos, in the seer's genealogy, is “mother of flocks” (15.226; see [[mother-of-flocks]]).
