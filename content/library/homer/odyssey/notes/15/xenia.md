summary: A book of leave-takings and of strangers received. Peisistratus' maxim, “For a guest remembers all his days / the host who has shown him friendship” (15.54–55), and Menelaus' rule of measure, that a host should make a guest welcome while he stays and send him off when he wishes (15.68–74); the young men's guest-friendship “from our fathers' friendship”, and Telemachus' fear of being kept by Nestor (15.195–201); the suppliant seer received on board and handed to Peiraeus, “for there is no lack of good cheer for guests” (15.277–281, 15.509–546); and in the hut Odysseus' test of his host, “whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay” (15.304–305).

# ξεινίων (Monro's critical notes on 15.514 and 15.546). Only here in Homer.

**A guest remembers.** Peisistratus will not drive through the night, but bids Telemachus wait for Menelaus' gifts and farewell: “But wait until he brings gifts and sets them in the chariot, / the hero, the son of Atreus, Menelaus famed for his spear, / and sends us off, speaking comfortingly with gentle words. / For a guest remembers all his days / the host who has shown him friendship” (15.51–55; see [[a-guest-remembers-his-host]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[gentle-words]]). The bond is made at the parting as much as at the welcome: the gifts are what the guest keeps, and the host's gentle words are his last service, which, Monro thinks, “ancient manners required … from the host” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.80–85).

**Measure.** Menelaus states the host's rule: “I am indignant with another / man, a host, who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things. / It is an equal wrong, both when a man urges on a guest who does not wish / to go home, and when he holds back one who is eager to go. / One should make welcome a guest who is present, and send him off when he wishes” (15.69–74; see [[what-is-due-is-better]], [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]], [[nemesis]] and [[pompe]]). “Too much kindness, shown in keeping a guest against his will, is as bad as sending him away before he wishes to go” (Monro on 15.70). The host who had pressed his guest to stay “until the eleventh or the twelfth day” (4.588) now keeps the rule, “I will not keep you here for long / when you are eager for your homecoming” (15.68–69), and holds him only for the meal and the gifts. The meal is part of the send-off, “glory and splendor and a blessing too” (15.78), a feast being both an honor and a benefit (Monro on 15.78; see [[kydos]]); and on the tour he offers, no one would send them “away just as we came” (15.83), that is, without a present (Monro on 15.83).

**From our fathers.** At Pylos Telemachus asks a favor of Peisistratus on the ground of an inherited bond: “We claim to be guest-friends without end / from our fathers' friendship, and we are of the same age as well; / and this journey will bring us still further into oneness of mind” (15.196–198; see [[euchomai]], [[philotes]] and [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]). Athena, as Mentes, had claimed the same bond, “We claim to be each other's guest-friends from our fathers' time” (1.187), and Glaucus and Diomedes found it on the battlefield (*Iliad* 6.215). The favor is to be spared a host's excess: “Do not take me past the ship …, / lest the old man keep me in his house against my will / in his eagerness to make me welcome” (15.199–201; see [[nestor]]), as Nestor had held them back from their ship on the first night (3.345–355). Peisistratus agrees, and knows that Nestor would come to the ship himself and not go back “empty-handed” (15.212–214; see [[cholos]]).

**The suppliant stranger.** At the ship Theoclymenus puts the host's question to the host, “Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?” (15.264; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]), and asks to come aboard as a suppliant (15.277; see [[supplication]] and [[theoclymenus]]). Telemachus receives him: “I will not drive you from the balanced ship when you wish to come; / but follow me; and there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have” (15.280–281; see [[philos]]). On Ithaca he cannot take him home: “Were things otherwise, I would bid you go even to our house; / for there is no lack of good cheer for guests; but it would be worse / for you yourself, since I shall be away” (οὐ γάρ τι ξενίων ποθή, 15.513–515; see [[pothe]]), and he names Eurymachus (15.518–522; see [[eurymachus]]). The name, Monro thinks, is there to give occasion for the omen and Theoclymenus' reading of it, after which the resort to Eurymachus is given up and Telemachus promises ample hospitality (Monro on 15.519): “then you would quickly know friendship and many gifts / from me” (15.537–538), perhaps a hendiadys, hospitality as shown in many gifts (Monro on 15.537; see [[doron]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Until then the stranger goes to Peiraeus: “now too take this stranger for me to your house, / and welcome him with care and honor him, until I come”, and Peiraeus answers, “he shall not lack good cheer” (15.542–546; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]] and [[time]]).

**The host tested.** In the hut the guest tries his host again. “Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd, / whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay / there at the farmstead, or would urge him on to the city” (15.304–306; see [[peira]]); he offers to go and serve the suitors, and Eumaeus keeps him: “But stay; for no one is vexed by your presence” (15.335). Odysseus thanks him with the blessing of the night before, “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery” (15.341–342; the first verse is 14.440). The story Eumaeus then tells is of hospitality betrayed: the Phoenician traders stay a whole year in his father's land, and the nurse carries off the master's son and the goblets from the tables of the banqueters (15.455–470; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). Odysseus' answer sets the slave's fortune against his own: Zeus brought him “to the house of a man / who is kind, who gives you food and drink / with care” (15.489–491; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[laertes]]), while the guest has come “after wandering through many cities of mortals” (15.492).
