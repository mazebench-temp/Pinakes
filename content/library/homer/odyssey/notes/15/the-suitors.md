summary: Athena warns Telemachus that the suitors are devouring his house, that Eurymachus surpasses them all in gifts, and that the best of them lie in ambush for him (15.10–32); Helen reads the eagle's omen as Odysseus “planting evil for all the suitors” (15.178); in the hut Eumaeus warns the stranger against “the throng of the suitors, / whose arrogance and violence reach the iron sky” (15.328–334); and Telemachus, back on Ithaca, names Eurymachus as the man the island now honors, before the falcon's omen (15.513–524).

**In your house.** Athena's first words to Telemachus at Sparta are about the suitors: “it is no longer good for you to wander far from your home, / leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house / so overbearing, lest they devour everything you have, / dividing your possessions, and you have gone on a useless journey” (10–13; see [[overbearing]] and [[possessions]]). Penelope's father and brothers, she says, are urging her to marry Eurymachus, “for he surpasses all / the suitors in his gifts, and has increased his bride-price greatly” (16–18; see [[eurymachus]], [[icarius]] and [[bride-price]]). And she warns of the ambush: “The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos, / eager to kill you before you reach your native land. / But I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (28–32, the last three verses as 13.426–428; see [[ambush]], [[samos]] and [[asteris]]). ἐπιτηδές is “of set purpose” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.28).

**Evil for the suitors.** Helen reads the eagle that carried off the goose from the court: “so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (176–178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[helen]]). The words recall the poet's description of the stranger in Eumaeus' hut, “and he was planting evils for the suitors” (14.110; see the Book 14 note), and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] repeats Helen's half-verse to Penelope (17.159).

**The throng of the suitors.** In the hut the stranger offers to go to the city and serve the suitors, “and mingle with the overbearing suitors, / in case they might give me a meal” (315–317; see [[peira]]). Eumaeus will not hear of it: “Surely you long to perish there altogether, / if you wish to slip into the throng of the suitors, / whose arrogance and violence reach the iron sky. / Not such as you are their servants, / but young men, well clad in cloaks and tunics, / their heads and their handsome faces always shining with oil” (327–332; see [[hybris]] and [[sky]]). In Book 17 the stranger himself says the verse of the iron sky, when he asks Eumaeus to tell Penelope why he will not yet come to her: “But I fear the throng of the harsh suitors, / whose arrogance and violence reach the iron sky” (17.564–565, a plain rendering). The suitors are the evil that has fallen on the house, and the reason why the slaves no longer hear a kind word from their mistress: “since evil has fallen on the house, / overbearing men” (374–376; see [[penelope]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

**Eurymachus.** On Ithaca, Telemachus cannot take the seer home, since his mother “does not often show herself to the suitors in the house, / but away from them she weaves at her loom in the upper room” (516–517; see [[weaving]] and [[upper-room]]), and he names Eurymachus, the best of them and the most eager to marry her, adding: “But Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air, knows this, / whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (518–524; see [[ithacans]] and [[evil-day]]). At once the falcon flies on the right (525; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).
