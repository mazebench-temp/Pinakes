# Unnamed: Cleitus taken up (15.250-251).
refs: [15.250-251]
---

**Cleitus.** The line of Melampus has one man taken up from among mortals: “but Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (15.250–251; see [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]], [[eos]] and [[dawn-and-orion]]). The second verse is the Iliad's for Ganymede, whom the gods snatched up to pour wine for Zeus (*Iliad* 20.235; see [[ganymede]]), and Aristarchus obelized it here as borrowed from there (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.251; see the translation's note on 15.225–255). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* has the two halves of the couplet in two stories, nearly word for word: Zeus snatched up Ganymede “for his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals”, and “so too Dawn of the golden throne snatched up Tithonus” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–203, 218, plain renderings; see the entry and [[tithonus]]). The poem says nothing of what became of Cleitus among the immortals, nor whether he was made ageless.

**Amphiaraus.** The same genealogy tells of the opposite favor. Amphiaraus was the man “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.245–247; see [[amphiaraus]]). The threshold is not the beginning of old age: he never fairly set foot in it (Monro on 15.246). Some of the ancients, Plutarch among them, saw in the verse a proof of the saying that those whom the gods love die young; “but no such ‘pathetic fallacy’ was in the poet's mind” (Monro on 15.246, reporting Plutarch's *Consolation to Apollonius* 17). The gods' love does not make a mortal immortal: one kinsman is taken up to dwell among them, the other dies before his time.
