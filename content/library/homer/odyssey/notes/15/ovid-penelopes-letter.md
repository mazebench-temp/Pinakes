---
summary: Four things in Penelope's letter are told in this book from the other side. The father who chides her delays is the father who, with her brothers, presses her to marry Eurymachus, in the warning that sends Telemachus home; Sparta, which in the letter does not know the truth, gives Telemachus Helen's reading of the eagle; the ambush she speaks of as past is escaped in this book; and the old Laertes of the letter, who holds off his last day for his son, prays here for death.
refs: [15.16-23, 15.28-35, 15.295-300, 15.353-357]
---

**The father.** “My father Icarius compels me to leave my widowed bed, and keeps chiding my endless delays. Let him chide: yours I am, yours I must be called; Penelope will always be the wife of Ulysses” (*Me pater Icarius viduo discedere lecto / cogit et immensas increpat usque moras*, *Heroides* 1.81–84, plain renderings). The pressure is the one that Athena reports at Sparta: “For already her father and her brothers are bidding her / marry Eurymachus; for he surpasses all / the suitors in his gifts” (15.16–18; see [[icarius]] and [[eurymachus]]). The goddess draws from it a warning that Ovid's Penelope answers in advance: “For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast: / she wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her, / and her former children and her dear wedded husband / she no longer remembers, once he is dead, nor asks about them” (15.20–23; see [[a-womans-heart]]).

**Sparta.** “We sent to Pylos, the Nelean fields of old Nestor; uncertain is the report sent back from Pylos. We sent to Sparta too: Sparta too does not know the truth” (*misimus et Sparten; Sparte quoque nescia veri*, *Heroides* 1.63–65, plain renderings). In this book Sparta gives Telemachus a sign and its reading. As he leaves, an eagle carries off a goose from the court, and Helen prophesies: “so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (15.176–178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[helen]]). The second half of her reading is already true.

**The ambush.** “He was almost taken from me lately by an ambush, while he made ready to go to Pylos against the will of all” (*Heroides* 1.99–100, a plain rendering). Ovid's Penelope sets the danger at his going; in the poem it waits for his return, and this book tells how he escapes it. Athena warns him at Sparta that “The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos”, and tells him to keep his ship far from the islands and to sail by night (15.28–34; see [[the-suitors]]). He sails home past Elis, “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.300), and comes to land at dawn on the coast near the swineherd's farm (15.495–500; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**Laertes.** Penelope counts the household that is left: “a wife without strength, old Laertes and Telemachus, a boy” (*Heroides* 1.97–98, a plain rendering), and bids her husband look to his father, “that you may close his eyes, he holds off the last day of fate” (*respice Laerten; ut tu sua lumina condas, / extremum fati sustinet ille diem*, *Heroides* 1.113–114, plain renderings). The swineherd tells the disguised son of the same old man: “Laertes still lives, but he prays to Zeus always / that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls; / for he mourns terribly for his son who is gone” (15.353–355; see [[laertes]]). In the letter the old man waits for his son; in the poem he prays to die. The third of Penelope's allies, “the faithful keeper of the filthy sty” (*Heroides* 1.104, a plain rendering), is the teller here, and he says that since the suitors came he can hear no kind word from his mistress (15.374–379; see [[eumaeus]]).
