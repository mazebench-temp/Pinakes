summary: “And they went past Cruni and fair-flowing Chalcis” (15.295): Cruni is the first landmark of the voyage home from Pylos, passed before sunset as the ship runs north up the coast with the wind Athena sent, in a verse that no manuscript of the Odyssey has.

**The course.** The voyage from Pylos to Ithaca is told by its landmarks. Athena sends a following wind, “blowing hard and wild through the upper air” (292–294; see [[athena]] and [[the-winds]]); “they went past Cruni and fair-flowing Chalcis” (295; see [[chalcis-in-elis]]); the sun sets (296); the ship makes for Pheae and goes past shining Elis, “where the Epeians rule” (297–298; see [[pheia]], [[elis]] and [[epeians]]); and from there Telemachus sends her on “toward the pointed islands, / pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (299–300; see [[the-pointed-islands]] and [[ambush]]). Then the poem turns to the swineherd's hut, and the night there fills “the otherwise blank space of the night voyage of Telemachus” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.301–495), until it takes him up again at dawn on the coast of Ithaca (495–500). Strabo reads the verses as the log of a ship sailing north from the Triphylian Pylos: Cruni and Chalcis by day, Pheae and Elis after sunset; then, to keep out of the strait between Ithaca and Samos where the suitors lay in wait (29), it turns east toward the Echinades, passes Ithaca and comes back to land on the far side of the island (*Geography* 8.3.26–27; see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]], [[asteris]] and the general article). For Monro the last stage of that course is no more than “the ancient geographer's hypothesis” (Monro on 15.299–300; see [[the-pointed-islands]]).

**A verse in no manuscript.** Verse 295 is not in any manuscript of the Odyssey. Strabo quotes it first of the four verses 295–298 in his discussion of the voyage, and the *Hymn to Apollo* has 295, 298 and 297, in that order and with other variations, in its own voyage up the same coast (*Hymn to Apollo* 425–427). Monro holds the verse genuine and argues for its place. The Hymn, he says, is right against Strabo in putting it just before παρʼ Ἤλιδα δῖαν, “past shining Elis”: in Strabo's order, as in the text of the Odyssey, those words must be construed with ἐπέβαλλεν, “made for”, “a ‘zeugma’ of more than ordinary harshness”, while the Hymn's “she went past Cruni … and past shining Elis” runs smoothly, as the souls of the suitors in Book 24 “went past the streams of Oceanus and the White Rock, / and past the gates of the Sun” (24.11–12, a plain rendering), a close parallel, Monro thinks, and perhaps an imitation. Besides, the conventional verse of sunset is always preceded by a journey and followed by the stage that has been reached, so that a verse naming the places passed “would come very well after δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος, but not before it”. He therefore prints the verses in the order 296, 297, 295, 298, with 295 in brackets, and since the Hymn's καὶ παρὰ Δύμην, “and past Dyme”, names a town of Achaea that is not on Telemachus' course, he concludes that the whole passage belongs originally to the Odyssey and was borrowed by the Hymn for its voyage from Crete to Delphi (Monro on 15.295–298, and his critical note on 15.295). Murray keeps Strabo's order, which the translation follows (see the translation's note on 15.295–300).

**Before sunset.** Read in Strabo's order, Cruni and [[chalcis-in-elis|Chalcis]] are passed by daylight and Pheae is reached in the dark; that is the order Strabo needs for his Pylos (*Geography* 8.3.27). In Monro's order the sun sets first, and the ship makes for Pheae, passes Cruni and Chalcis, and runs along Elis by night; Pheae, “being to the south of Elis, naturally comes before it in this narrative” (Monro on 15.295–298). Strabo himself, describing the coast, puts the spring Cruni and the river Chalcis south of the Alpheus, and Pheia north of it (*Geography* 8.3.12–13; see [[alpheus]]).
