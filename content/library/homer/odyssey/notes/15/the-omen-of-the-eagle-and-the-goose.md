**The wish.** Menelaus has pledged the two young men and sent his greeting to Nestor (15.147–153; see [[kind-as-a-father]]), and Telemachus answers with a wish: “would that, / having come home to Ithaca and finding Odysseus in the house, / I might tell him so, as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness” (15.156–158). αἲ γὰρ ἐγὼν ὣς … ὡς are the correlatives of a wish, “would that I may tell it … even as I go on my way after receiving all hospitality from you”, that is, “my debt to you for hospitality is as great and sure as my desire to see my father again in Ithaca”: the only interpretation, Monro holds, consistent with the idiom (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.156–158; see the translation's note on 15.156–158); φιλότητος ἁπάσης is, “as we say, ‘nothing but kindness’” (Monro on 15.158; see [[philotes]]).

**The bird.** “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right” (15.160): ἐπέπτατο is “flew by”, with ἐπί as in ἐπαληθείς, “wandering”, sixteen verses later; δεξιός, “to the right”, is equivalent to ἐπιδέξια, “from left to right”, so that δεξιὸς ἤϊξε πρόσθʼ ἵππων (164) means that the eagle “sped to the right in front of the chariot, so as to cross its path in the lucky direction” (Monro on 15.160; see the translation's note on 15.160, 15.164). Monro notes the order of the narrative: first the main fact, in the words ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις, then the incident in detail, so that the main fact is told over again at 164, as the story of Bellerophon in the Iliad begins with his banishment and then goes back to its cause (on 15.160, citing *Iliad* 6.158–168). The verse is the one in which an eagle flies by on the right as Ajax ends his challenge to Hector (*Iliad* 13.821), and the bird, as there, is named in the next verse. La Roche noticed, as Merry reports, that where the kind of bird is added to ὄρνις, as here and with the falcon at 15.525–526, “there is a reason for such an addition, as the reference is to augury” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.320; see [[birds]], [[eagle]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).

**The goose.** The eagle carries “a huge white goose, / a tame one from the court” (15.161–162): ἀργός is “white” here, where the Iliad's dogs are “swift” and its oxen “sleek”, and ἥμερος, “tame”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row for ἀργὴ χήν, and [[geese]]). The men and women of the house follow it shouting, ἰΰζοντες (15.162): the verb is found elsewhere in Homer only in the simile of the mountain-bred lion that snatches the best cow from a grazing herd, while the dogs and herdsmen raise a clamor from far off but will not come against him (*Iliad* 17.61–67; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). “And when they saw it / they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (15.164–165) is the end of the omen of the eagle that Zeus sends to Priam, which appears “darting on the right across the city” (*Iliad* 24.320–321; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]).

**The question.** Peisistratus asks whether “the god has shown this sign to us two, or to you yourself” (15.168): φαίνω τέρας, “show a sign”, as in the Iliad and at Lesbos (*Iliad* 2.324; 3.173; see the translation's key-word row for φαίνω τέρας, and [[teras]]). Menelaus ponders “how he might read it to him fair and right, with understanding” (15.170): ὑποκρίναιτο is “should expound”, “read the sign”, the verb with which Penelope asks the beggar to read her dream and with which he answers (Monro on 15.170, citing 19.535, 19.555; see [[divination]]).

**The prophecy.** “Hear me, and I will prophesy, as the immortals / put it in my heart” (15.172–173): the verses are Athena's in the first book, but for κλῦτέ μευ, “Hear me”, where she said νῦν τοι, “now … to you” (1.200–201). αὐτάρ marks the slight contrast between the listeners and the speaker, “do you hear, and I &c.” (Monro on 15.172; see [[hear-me]] and [[prophecy]]). The eagle came “from the mountain, where he was born and bred”: γενεή τε τόκος τε is a hendiadys of nearly synonymous words, which are “fused, as it were, into a single more complete conception” (Monro on 15.175; see the translation's note on 15.175). “So Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering” (15.176) takes up Menelaus' words of his own eight years of wandering, πολλὰ παθὼν καὶ πόλλʼ ἐπαληθείς (4.81; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]), and Eumaeus uses them that night of the man who takes delight in his sorrows afterward, “whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (15.401; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). The reading has two branches, “or else he is already / at home” (15.177–178), and the second is true: Odysseus is already in the swineherd's hut (14.1–533; see [[odysseus]] and [[eumaeus]]).

**The answer.** “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.180–181 = 8.465, 8.467, Odysseus to Nausicaa; see [[loud-thundering]] and [[husband-of-hera]]): τῷ κέν τοι is “then to thee I &c.” (Monro on 15.181). Telemachus lays the whip on the horses, and they drive out through the city to the plain (15.182–183; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
