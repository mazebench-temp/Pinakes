---
summary: The verb in its two senses. “Claim”: Telemachus' “We claim to be guest-friends without end” (15.196) and the nurse's “I claim to be from Sidon, rich in bronze” (15.425). “Pray”: Telemachus praying and sacrificing to Athena at the stern when the seer finds him (15.222, 15.258), Laertes, who “prays to Zeus always” for death (15.353), and Telemachus' promise to Helen, “then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.181).
---

**Claims.** At Pylos Telemachus asks his favor of Peisistratus on the strength of a claimed bond: “We claim to be guest-friends without end / from our fathers' friendship” (ξεῖνοι δὲ διαμπερὲς εὐχόμεθʼ εἶναι, 15.196–197; see [[xenia]] and [[philotes]]), the half-verse with which Athena, as Mentes, had claimed an inherited bond with his father's house, “We claim to be each other's guest-friends from our fathers' time” (1.187; see the Book 1 note). διαμπερές is “without end” of time, as at 11.558 (see the translation's table of key words). In Eumaeus' story the Phoenician woman, asked who she is, makes the claim of birth: “I claim to be from Sidon, rich in bronze, / and I am the daughter of Arybas, whose wealth came in a flood” (ἐκ μὲν Σιδῶνος πολυχάλκου εὔχομαι εἶναι, 15.425–426; see [[sidon]], [[arybas]], [[genee]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). The slave claims a free and rich father, and the claim wins her the offer of a passage home (15.430–436; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).

**Prayers.** Telemachus prays before he sails. “So he was busy with these things, and was praying and sacrificing to Athena / beside the stern of the ship” (εὔχετο, θῦε δʼ Ἀθήνῃ, 15.222–223; see [[prayer]], [[sacrifice]] and [[athena]]), and the seer comes up and finds him “pouring libations and praying beside the swift black ship” (σπένδοντʼ εὐχόμενόν τε, 15.258; see [[libation]] and [[theoclymenus]]): the suppliant addresses him by the offerings he has seen (15.260–261; see [[supplication]]). In the hut a prayer for death: “Laertes still lives, but he prays to Zeus always / that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls” (Διὶ δʼ εὔχεται αἰεί, 15.353–354; see [[laertes]] and [[thymos]]).

**As to a god.** When Helen has read the omen of the eagle, Telemachus answers, “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (θεῷ ὣς εὐχετοῴμην, 15.180–181; see [[helen]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The verses are Odysseus' farewell to Nausicaa, who had saved his life (8.465, 8.467; see the Book 8 note); the son gives the same honor to the woman who has foretold his father's return.
