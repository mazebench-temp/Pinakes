summary: Athena warns Telemachus that the suitors may divide and devour his possessions while he is away, and that Penelope, if she marries, may carry some possession from the house (15.10–19); he gives Menelaus the same reason for going home (15.88–91) and takes many fine treasures with him (15.101–159). In the book's stories Neleus holds Melampus' goods for a year (15.230), and Laertes buys the child Eumaeus with his own possessions (15.483).

**Athena's warning.** “Telemachus, it is no longer good for you to wander far from your home, / leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house / so overbearing, lest they devour everything you have, / dividing your possessions, and you have gone on a useless journey” (15.10–13; see [[athena]], [[the-suitors]] and [[overbearing]]): nearly the words of Nestor's warning at Pylos (3.313–316; see [[nestor]]). For κτήματα at 11 some manuscripts have χρήματα, “goods”, the variation that runs through the manuscripts of the Odyssey (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.11; see the entry). The goddess adds a fear for the house itself: Penelope's father and brothers bid her marry Eurymachus, “beware lest against your will she carry some possession from the house”, for a woman wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her (15.16–23; see [[penelope]], [[eurymachus]] and [[a-womans-heart]]). Aristophanes is said to have rejected the verse of the possession for the meanness to which it appeals, but it is needed for the next sentence, and Monro defends it (on 15.19; see the translation's note on 15.19, 15.24–26, 15.91).

**At Sparta.** Telemachus gives Menelaus the same reason: “for when I left I did not leave behind / anyone to watch over my possessions; / lest while I seek my godlike father I perish myself, / or some fine treasure be lost to me from my halls” (15.88–91; see [[menelaus]] and [[telemachus]]). οὖρος is a “watcher”, like the singer whom Agamemnon left in charge of his wife (Monro on 15.89; 3.267–268; see the translation's row οὖρος, a guardian), and the last verse was anciently rejected, like 19 (Monro on 15.91). Menelaus goes down “to where the treasures were stored” and gives from “the gifts that lie stored as treasures in my house” the finest and most precious (15.101, 15.113–114; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[guest-gifts]]), so that Telemachus can say he comes from him “having met with nothing but kindness, / and bring many fine treasures” (15.158–159; see [[philotes]]), as Achilles took “many fine treasures” from the cities he sacked (*Iliad* 9.330).

**Goods held and spent.** In the story of Melampus, Neleus “for a full year's round held his many goods / by force” while Melampus lay bound in the halls of Phylacus (15.230–231; see [[neleus]], [[melampus]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]). The end of Eumaeus' story is a purchase: the wind and the water brought the Phoenicians to Ithaca, “where Laertes bought me with his own possessions” (15.482–483; see [[laertes]], [[eumaeus]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), the verse-end in which Laertes bought Eurycleia and Eumaeus his slave Mesaulius (1.430, 14.452; see [[mesaulius]] and the Book 14 note).
