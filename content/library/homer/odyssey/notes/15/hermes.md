**By the favor of Hermes.** Testing the swineherd, the stranger says that at dawn he will go to the city to beg, and to the house of Odysseus, where he might serve the suitors (15.304–317; see [[peira]] and [[odysseus]]):

{{quote:bf3e71cb-0d9d-58b5-a989-5ee0bc215498}}

ἕκητι with the name of a god, “by the favor of”, is found three times in Homer, all in the Odyssey: here; of Telemachus, grown to be like his father “by the favor of Apollo” (19.86, a plain rendering); and in Odysseus' words to Athena on the night before the slaughter, “even if I should kill them by the favor of Zeus and of you” (20.42, a plain rendering). διάκτορος, “the guide”, is his old title (see [[guide]]), and the relative clause says what he gives: χάριν καὶ κῦδος, “grace and glory”, a pair found elsewhere in Homer only in Athena's promise to Pandarus, in another sense, that he would win “thanks and glory from all the Trojans” (*Iliad* 4.95; see [[charis]] and [[kydos]]). In Demodocus' song Apollo greets him as “giver of blessings” (8.335; see the Book 8 note).

**The servant's work.** The tasks the beggar claims are the ones Eumaeus performed at the evening sacrifice of Book 14: he split the wood, the boar was roasted, and he stood up to carve, setting aside one portion “for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia” (14.418–436; see the Book 14 note and [[sacrifice]]); κεάσσαι, ὀπτῆσαι and δαιτρεῦσαι (15.322–323) are the verbs of that scene (14.418, 14.431, 14.433), and pouring wine is the cupbearer's office (see [[cupbearing]]). The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* makes the newborn god the first to do these things for himself: he gathered much wood and sought out the craft of fire, “For Hermes it was who first gave fire-sticks and fire”; then he roasted the meat of two of Apollo's cattle on spits and divided it into twelve portions (*Hymn to Hermes* 108–129; see the general article). Hesiod gives him the herdsman's increase too: Hecate is good “in the steadings, with Hermes, at increasing the stock” (*Theogony* 444), with the verb that Eumaeus uses later this night of his own herds, “the blessed gods make the work grow at which I stay” (15.372; see the Book 15 note on [[olympians]]). Eumaeus hears the offer with dismay. The suitors' servants are not such as the stranger, “but young men, well clad in cloaks and tunics, / their heads and their handsome faces always shining with oil” (15.330–332; see [[the-suitors]]), and the king in disguise is told to stay in the hut (15.335–339).
