---
summary: Cicero knows the pleasure of remembered pain that the swineherd offers his guest, as a proverb, as a verse of Euripides, and in his own words, and he distinguishes it from the pleasure that others take in hearing of it. His seers of old were kings as well as augurs, Amphilochus among them, and Amphiaraus, whom this book counts in the line of Melampus, was honored as a god; his Eriphyle, who sold her husband's life for gold, is the woman of this book's “gifts to a woman”, and the argument from her to all women is his example of a proof that proves too little.
---

- **The pleasure of past pain.** Against the Epicurean rule that the wise man should not remember evils, Cicero asks, “What if the memory of past evils is even pleasant?”, and cites the common saying, “Past labors are pleasant”, and Euripides' verse, which he puts into Latin, *Suavis laborum est praeteritorum memoria*, “Sweet is the memory of past labors” (*On Ends* 2.105, plain renderings). Writing to Lucceius, who was to write the history of his consulship and exile, he says that the changes of his fortune, which were not to be wished for in the experience, will be pleasant in the reading, “for the untroubled recollection of past pain has delight”, while those who have suffered nothing take pleasure even in pity, as they look on the misfortunes of others (*habet enim praeteriti doloris secura recordatio delectationem*, *Letters to Friends* 5.12.4–5, plain renderings). The swineherd joins the two pleasures: “But let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut, / take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them” (15.398–400), and the listener answers, “Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me” (15.486): [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]], [[sweet-to-remember-toils]] and [[euripides]].
- **Seers and kings.** Homer, Quintus says, makes Calchas by far the best of augurs; “Amphilochus and Mopsus were kings of the Argives, but augurs as well, and they founded Greek cities on the coast of Cilicia; and before them Amphiaraus and Tiresias”, famous men “who foretold the future, warned by birds and signs”; and Greek fame so honored Amphiaraus “that he was held a god, and oracles were sought from the ground in which he was buried”; for among the ancients the rulers also held the auguries, since they thought divination kingly, like wisdom (*On Divination* 1.87–89, plain renderings). The names of Polyidus, Melampus, Mopsus, Amphiaraus, Calchas and Helenus would not have been so great, Cicero says in his own person, unless their predictions had come true (*On the Laws* 2.33). In this book the seer Melampus came to Argos to rule “over many Argives”, Amphiaraus and Amphilochus are his descendants, and Apollo made Polypheides a seer “by far the best of mortals, after Amphiaraus died” (15.239–255): [[melampus]], [[amphiaraus]], [[amphilochus]], [[polypheides]] and [[divination]].
- **Amphiaraus.** Cicero, explaining why he followed Pompey into a war he did not approve, says that like Amphiaraus in the stories he went “knowing and foreseeing, to the destruction set before my eyes” (*ut in fabulis Amphiaraus, sic ego 'prudens et sciens ad pestem ante oculos positam' sum profectus*, *Letters to Friends* 6.6.6, a plain rendering). Cotta, against the Stoics, asks, “Will Amphiaraus be a god, and Trophonius?”, since the tax-farmers in Boeotia denied that any who had once been men were immortal (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.49, a plain rendering). The poem says that Zeus and Apollo loved him and that he perished at Thebes (15.245–247): [[amphiaraus]].
- **Eriphyle's gold.** “We have heard in the stories that Eriphyle had such greed that when she saw a necklace, of gold and gems, I suppose, she was inflamed by its beauty and betrayed her husband's life” (*Against Verres* 2.4.39, a plain rendering). Among the faults of argument Cicero counts proving a part when one has promised the whole: “The race of women is greedy; for Eriphyle sold her husband's life for gold” (*Mulierum genus avarum est; nam Eriphyla auro viri vitam vendidit*, *On Invention* 1.94, a plain rendering). In this book Amphiaraus “perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.247), and Athena argues from one heart to all women's: “For you know what kind of heart is in a woman's breast” (15.20): [[eriphyle]], [[a-womans-heart]] and [[womenfolk]].
