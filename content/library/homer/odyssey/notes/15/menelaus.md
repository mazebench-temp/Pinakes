**The host's rule.** Peisistratus has promised that Menelaus will send them off “speaking comfortingly with gentle words” (15.53; see [[peisistratus]] and [[gentle-words]]). At dawn he comes from his bed, “from beside Helen of the lovely hair” (15.58; see [[helen]] and [[lovely-haired]]), and Telemachus, dressed in haste, asks to be sent home (15.59–66; see [[telemachus]]). He answers with a host's rule: “Telemachus, I will not keep you here for long / when you are eager for your homecoming; I am indignant with another / man, a host, who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things” (15.68–71; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]), and to keep a guest who wishes to go is as bad as to hurry away one who wishes to stay, “One should make welcome a guest who is present, and send him off when he wishes” (15.72–74; see [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]] and [[xenia]]). Too much kindness is as bad as too little, but the last, gnomic verse, which many copies lacked, Monro takes for an addition (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.70, and his critical note on 15.74; see the translation's notes on 15.63, 15.74, 15.139 and 15.70–74).

**The offer of a tour.** First the guests are to see their gifts and eat, since a meal before a journey is both an honor and a benefit (15.75–79; Monro on 15.78); and if Telemachus wishes to turn aside “through Hellas and the heart of Argos”, Menelaus will yoke the horses and guide him through the cities of men, and no one will send them away without a gift, a tripod, a cauldron, two mules or a golden goblet (15.80–85; see [[hellas]], [[argos]], [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]). Aristarchus rejected 78–85. J. B. Bury found the offer strange from a host who has just spoken of [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on|speeding the parting guest]], and took Hellas here for the later Achaia, so that Menelaus would propose only a detour through his own country; Monro answers that the phrase is an epic commonplace, wide and conventional, and that “it may be that ancient manners required some such speech from the host”, the gentle words that Peisistratus promised (Monro on 15.80–85; see the translation's note on 15.78–85). Telemachus declines, and Menelaus has a meal prepared (15.87–98; see [[eteoneus]]).

**The gifts.** He goes down to the fragrant treasure chamber with Helen and Megapenthes, takes a two-handled cup himself and bids his son carry a silver mixing bowl, while Helen chooses a robe (15.99–108; see [[megapenthes]], [[cup]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The lips of the bowl are “wrought above, and finished, with gold” (Monro on 15.116), and the verses 113–119, which repeat 4.613–619, are missing in two manuscripts and bracketed by Monro (see the translation's note on 15.113–119). The giver is the Sidonian king (see [[phaedimus]], [[sidonians]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Strabo cites the verses to show the craft of the Sidonians, and reads Menelaus' long stay among them into the poet's praise of their work (*Geography* 1.2.33; see [[strabo]]). Menelaus puts the cup in Telemachus' hand, “the hero, the son of Atreus” (15.120–121; see [[son-of-atreus]]).

**The parting cup.** When the young men have yoked the horses, “fair-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus, went after them, / holding wine sweet to the heart in his right hand / in a golden cup, so that they might pour a libation before they went” (15.147–149; see [[fair-haired]] and [[libation]]), and he pledges them with a greeting for Nestor, who was kind to him like a father at Troy (15.150–153; see [[nestor]] and [[kind-as-a-father]]). When the eagle flies by with the goose and Peisistratus asks him whether the sign is for them or for himself, “Menelaus, dear to Ares, pondered / how he might read it to him fair and right, with understanding” (15.169–170; see [[dear-to-ares]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and Helen speaks first (15.171; see [[helen]]). W. W. Merry, after Nitzsch, sees Menelaus' “characteristic slowness of resolve” in the same verb when he cannot decide how to question Telemachus at their first meeting, and points to this scene as a like case of his indecision and of Helen's quickness (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.117). The last that the book says of him is that the gifts Peisistratus puts in the stern are those “which Menelaus had given him” (15.207).
