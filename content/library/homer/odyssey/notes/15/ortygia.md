---
summary: Eumaeus places his island, Syrie, “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (15.404), and its people die, as Orion died in Ortygia, by the gentle arrows (15.409–411).
---

“There is an island called Syrie, if perhaps you have heard of it, / above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (403–404; see [[syrie]], [[eumaeus]] and [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]]). Ortygia is named for the second and last time in Homer, as a landmark by which to find an island the stranger may not know, and nothing is said of the place itself. καθύπερθεν, “above”, places one land by another, as in Nestor's “whether we should go home above rocky Chios” (3.170; see [[chios]] and [[psyria]]), and in the Iliad Phrygia lies καθύπερθε, “inland”, of Priam's coast (*Iliad* 24.545; see [[phrygia]]); which way Syrie lies from Ortygia the word does not say.

**The gentle arrows again.** In Book 5 Ortygia is where Artemis “came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him”, Orion, Dawn's lover (5.123–124; see [[orion]] and the Book 5 note); on [[syrie|Syrie]], when men grow old, “Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis / and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them” (410–411; see [[apollo]], [[artemis]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). The second verse is the verse of Orion's death, with ἐποιχόμενος for ἐποιχομένη, the god in place of the goddess. W. W. Merry, who took Ortygia for Delos or its neighbor Rhenaea, found in the two gods together a confirmation that Syrie was Syros, near Delos (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.123); D. B. Monro, taking both islands for mythical places, “as the ‘turning points’ of the sun”, would not look for them on the map (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.403; see the general article and [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]]).
