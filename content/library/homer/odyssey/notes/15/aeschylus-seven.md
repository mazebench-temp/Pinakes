summary: The seer whom Tydeus reviles at the first gate, and who stands at the sixth himself, is Amphiaraus, “the son of Oicles”, whose descent from Melampus this book traces. The poem says only that Zeus and Apollo loved him and that he perished at Thebes, “for the sake of gifts to a woman”; Aeschylus' seer goes to his death knowing it, and the play says nothing of gifts.

---

**The son of Oicles.** At the first gate Tydeus reviles “the wise seer, the son of Oicles” (μάντιν Οἰκλείδην σοφόν), who will not let the army cross the Ismenus because the sacrifices are not favorable (*Seven against Thebes* 377–383, a plain rendering; see the entry above). The father's name is the one that this book gives in the line of Melampus: “Antiphates begot great-hearted Oicles, / and Oicles Amphiaraus, who rouses armies” (15.243–244; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]], [[oicles]], [[amphiaraus]] and [[who-rouses-armies]]). At the sixth gate the messenger names him as “the most prudent man, / the seer best in valor, mighty Amphiaraus”, and his shield of solid bronze bears no device, “for he wishes not to seem the best, but to be it” (*Seven* 568–569, 590–592, plain renderings).

**Foreknowledge.** The Homeric account is brief: Zeus and Apollo “loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.245–247; see [[eriphyle]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). Aeschylus' seer knows his end: “For my part, I shall enrich this soil, / a prophet hidden beneath an enemy's earth”, and Eteocles expects him not even to attack the gates, “for he knows that he must die in the battle, / if the oracles of Loxias are to bear fruit” (*Seven* 587–588, 617–618, plain renderings). The god who loved him in this book is in Aeschylus the god of the oracles that foretell his death. The gifts to a woman, Eriphyle's necklace, are not in the play. Eteocles explains the seer's fate by his company instead: a just man mixed with impious men is destroyed with them, as a pious man who goes on board a ship with hot-headed sailors perishes with that god-hated crew (*Seven* 597–614). In this book another seer of the house goes on board, at Pylos, and the man who takes him on board is praying and sacrificing at the stern (15.222–286; see [[theoclymenus]]).
