summary: The seer whose vision Socrates assigns to the seer's art comes into the poem in this book, with the genealogy that makes him one of Plato's “Melampodidae”, and gives his first reading here, of a falcon that flies on the right. In this book he does what the second of Socrates' passages shows: he reads a bird.
refs: [15.223-225, 15.249-256, 15.531-534]
---

**The seer of the Melampodidae.** Socrates introduces his first passage as what “the seer of the Melampodidae” says to the suitors, Theoclymenus (ἃ ὁ τῶν Μελαμποδιδῶν λέγει μάντις πρὸς τοὺς μνηστῆρας, Θεοκλύμενος, *Ion* 538e). The family name is Plato's; the descent is told in this book. When the seer comes up to Telemachus at Pylos, “by birth he was a descendant of Melampus” (15.225), and the poet traces the house before he names him: Melampus' son Mantius begot Polypheides, whom Apollo made “a seer, / by far the best of mortals, after Amphiaraus died”, and “His son it was who came up, and Theoclymenus was his name” (15.249–256; see [[melampus]], [[mantius]], [[polypheides]] and [[theoclymenus]]). The other branch of the house ends in Amphiaraus, also a seer (15.243–247; see [[amphiaraus]]). ἔκγονος, “descendant”, is a great-grandson, and τοῦ, “his”, is Polypheides (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.225, 15.256).

**A bird read.** Socrates' first passage is the seer's vision of the suitors' doom in Book 20; his second is the eagle and the serpent of *Iliad* 12, a sign that Polydamas reads and Hector rejects (see the entry above). The seer's first reading in the poem joins the two: at the landing on Ithaca a falcon flies by on the right with a dove in its feet, and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] takes Telemachus apart: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen. / There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (15.525–534; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]], [[not-without-a-god]] and [[falcon]]). His word for it, οἰωνός, “a bird of omen” (15.532; see [[birds]]), names the matter of the art that Socrates gives to the seer to examine and to judge (*Ion* 539d; see [[divination]]). Earlier in the book a bird is read by one who is not a seer, when Helen, before Menelaus can answer, interprets the eagle that snatches a goose (15.160–178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]).
