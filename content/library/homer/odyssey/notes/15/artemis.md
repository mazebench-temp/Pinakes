---
summary: Artemis comes twice into Eumaeus' story. On his island of Syrie no sickness falls upon men, but when they grow old “Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis” and kills them with his gentle arrows (15.409–411); and on the seventh day of the Phoenicians' voyage “Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman” who had stolen him (15.478).
# The entry's ^Ἄρτεμ* claims Ἀρτέμιδι (15.410) and Ἄρτεμις (15.478).
---

**On Syrie.** “Famine never comes upon its people, nor does any / hateful sickness besides fall upon wretched mortals; / but when the tribes of men grow old throughout the city, / Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis / and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them” (15.407–411; see [[syrie]], [[apollo]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). Odysseus had offered his mother the two common ends of a woman's life, a long sickness or the gentle arrows of Artemis (11.171–173; see the Book 11 note); on Syrie there is no sickness, and the arrows come only in old age. The island lies “above Ortygia” (15.404), the name of the island where Artemis killed Orion with her gentle arrows (5.121–124; see [[ortygia]] and [[dawn-and-orion]]). W. W. Merry sets the passage beside the other deaths that the two archers bring, the god generally to men and the goddess to women (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.280).

**The nurse.** The Phoenicians have sailed for six days with the woman and the boy, “but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day, / then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman, / and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird” (15.477–479; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[of-the-showering-arrows]], [[like-a-sea-bird]] and the Book 15 note on [[zeus]]). Merry cites the verse, with the death of Andromache's mother, for the rule that Artemis kills women, as Apollo kills men; he numbers it 15.477 (Merry on 3.280; *Iliad* 6.428). Her arrows here are not called gentle, and there is no burial: the sailors throw the body overboard, “to become a spoil for seals and fish” (15.480–481; see [[seals]] and [[fish]]). The poem gives no reason for her death, any more than for the death of Menelaus' helmsman at Apollo's hands (3.279–283; see the Book 3 note on [[apollo]]). The mother of Odysseus, as Eumaeus has just told, died otherwise, “of grief for her glorious son, / by a wretched death” (15.358–359; see [[anticleia]]), as she had told her son herself: “it was not the keen-sighted goddess of the showering arrows who in my halls / came upon me with her gentle arrows and killed me” (11.198–199).
