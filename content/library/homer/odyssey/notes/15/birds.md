summary: The birds of Book 15 are the two birds of omen that fly by on the right in the same verse, an eagle with a goose as Telemachus leaves Sparta and a falcon with a dove as he comes to land on Ithaca (15.160–161, 15.525–526), the second of which Theoclymenus knows at sight for “a bird of omen” (15.531–532); and the sea bird to which Eumaeus likens the Phoenician woman as she falls dead (15.479).

**A bird on the right.** The book has two omens, one at each end of Telemachus' voyage home, and the same verse brings each: ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις, “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right” (15.160 = 15.525). It is the verse of the eagle that answers Ajax's challenge to Hector in the Iliad, where the army shouts, “emboldened by the omen” (*Iliad* 13.821–823). The first time the bird comes as Telemachus says farewell to Menelaus at Sparta, the second as he comes ashore on Ithaca, and each time the next verse names the kind: αἰετός, an eagle with a goose in its talons (15.161; see [[eagle]], [[geese]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and κίρκος, a falcon with a dove in its feet (15.526–527; see [[falcon]], [[doves]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). W. W. Merry reports La Roche's observation that where Homer adds the kind to ὄρνις, the bird is an omen, citing these two verses (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.320; see the Book 1 note). D. B. Monro takes δεξιός as ἐπιδέξια, “from left to right”, so that the eagle, which “darted / to the right, in front of the horses” (15.163–164), crossed the chariot's path in the lucky direction; he notes the order of the telling, the bare fact in the first verse and the incident in detail after it, so that the main fact is told over again at 15.164 (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.160; on 15.525 he refers to that note; see the translation's note on 15.160, 15.164 and [[divination]]).

**Readers of birds.** Neither omen is read by the man to whom it comes. Peisistratus asks Menelaus whether the god has shown the sign to the two of them or to him; while Menelaus ponders “how he might read it to him fair and right”, Helen is before him and reads it, prophesying in the words that Athena spoke as Mentes (15.167–178; 15.172–173 ~ 1.200–201; see [[helen]], [[teras]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The falcon is read by a seer, Theoclymenus, the son of [[polypheides|Polypheides]] of the house of Melampus, whom Telemachus has taken aboard at Pylos: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.531–532; see [[theoclymenus]], [[not-without-a-god]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Some manuscripts have ἤλυθε, “came”, for ἔπτατο, “flew” (Monro's critical note on 15.531).

**A bird of omen.** οἰωνός, the large bird whose flight is watched (see the entry), is here the bird as a sign, “a bird of omen”, as [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] will call it again when he tells Penelope what it meant: “Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (17.160–161, a plain rendering; see the translation's row οἰωνός, and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). In his note on 15.532 Monro derives the word from the root of Latin *avis*, “a bird”; the suffix, he says, is rare, as in υἱωνός, “grandson”, and seems to enlarge the sense. In the Iliad Priam asks Zeus for “a bird of omen, your swift messenger”, the eagle (*Iliad* 24.310); here the falcon is “the swift messenger of Apollo” (15.526). Eurymachus had answered Halitherses that “not all of them are birds of fate” (2.182; see the Book 2 note and [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]]); the seer knows this one at sight for a bird of omen, and his reading names the house of Odysseus as the most kingly in Ithaca, just after Telemachus has named Eurymachus as the man most eager to marry his mother (15.518–534; see [[eurymachus]]).

**A sea bird.** The book's third bird is a likeness. On the seventh day of the voyage that carried Eumaeus away from [[syrie|Syrie]], Artemis struck the Phoenician woman who had stolen him, “and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird” (15.478–479; see [[like-a-sea-bird]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). κήξ is named only here in Homer, and εἰνάλιος, “of the sea”, is the word of the crows of the sea that roost in Calypso's wood (5.66–67; see the Book 5 note).
