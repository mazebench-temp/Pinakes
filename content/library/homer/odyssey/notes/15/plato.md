---
summary: Plato names the seer of this book by his house, “the seer of the Melampodidae”, and sets the Phoenicians, whose traders steal the swineherd here, beside the Egyptians as peoples marked by the love of money. His two examples of a ruinous price, a child sold into slavery for gold and Eriphyle's necklace, both have their likeness in this book; the Laws, which call much sleep unfit for body and soul and send the homicide out of his country for a year, bear on the swineherd's long night and on the exile who comes on board at Pylos; and the Phaedrus sets the reading of birds below the prophecy that comes from a god.
---

- **The seer of the Melampodidae.** Socrates gives the seer's art “what the seer of the Melampodidae, Theoclymenus, says to the suitors” (*Ion* 538e, a plain rendering). The descent behind Plato's family name is told in this book, where the seer is brought in as “a descendant of Melampus” and his line is traced through Mantius and Polypheides before he is named (15.225–256): [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]], [[theoclymenus]], [[melampus]], [[mantius]] and [[polypheides]].
- **Lovers of money.** The spirited part of the soul, Socrates says, is attributed to the Thracians and Scythians, the love of learning to his own country, “and the love of money, which one might say belongs not least to the Phoenicians and those in Egypt” (*Republic* 435e–436a, a plain rendering). In the Laws the Athenian warns that the study of numbers, without laws that take illiberality and the love of money out of the soul, will produce “what is called knavery instead of wisdom, as one may now see the Egyptians and the Phoenicians” (*Laws* 747b–c, a plain rendering). The Phoenicians of this book come to Syrie as traders, “men famed for ships, / greedy rogues, bringing countless baubles in a black ship”, stay a year “and got much substance by trading”, and by deceit carry off a king's son to sell him (15.415–416, 15.455–456, 15.419–475): [[phoenicians]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[trade]] and [[syrie]].
- **A ruinous price.** Is a man not wretched, Socrates asks, who enslaves the most divine part of himself to the most godless? “If he took gold and enslaved his son or daughter, and that to savage and evil men”, it would not profit him, however much he took; and the man who enslaves his soul “takes gold as a bribe for a more terrible ruin than Eriphyle, who received the necklace for her husband's life” (*Republic* 589e–590a, plain renderings). Both of Plato's examples have their likeness in this book. The swineherd's nurse promises the Phoenicians gold for her passage, and the child in her care, who “would fetch you a price beyond counting, / wherever you sell him among men of another tongue” (15.448–453), and Amphiaraus, Eriphyle's husband, “perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.247): [[eriphyle]], [[amphiaraus]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]].
- **Much sleep.** It must be held shameful, the Athenian says, for any citizen to sleep the whole night through and not be seen by all his servants to be the first awake, and shameful for a mistress to be woken by her maids; “for much sleep is by nature fitting neither for our bodies nor for our souls, nor for the actions concerned with all these”, and “a man asleep is worth nothing, no more than one who is not alive” (*Laws* 807e–808c, plain renderings). The swineherd keeps his guest awake with a story, since “there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen; and you have no need / to go to bed before it is time; much sleep too is a vexation”, and sends to bed those of his men who wish to go, to rise at dawn and follow the swine (15.392–397); when the talk is over, the two sleep “not for long, but a little while” (15.494): [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]] and [[a-time-for-many-words]].
- **The homicide's exile.** A man who kills a free man unintentionally must be purified and must go away for all the seasons of a year, out of all the places familiar to the dead man in all the country, for an old story says that the dead man, freshly slain, is angry with his killer when he sees him going about in his own haunts (*Laws* 865d–866a, plain renderings). The seer of this book has fled from Argos “having killed a man / of my own tribe”, pursued by the dead man's many brothers and kinsmen (15.223–224, 15.272–278); the poem says nothing of a term to his exile: [[exile-and-refuge]], [[the-killer-in-exile]] and [[theoclymenus]].
- **Birds and madness.** Prophecy, μαντική, was once called μανική, Socrates argues, because the ancients thought madness that comes from a god a fine thing; but the search into the future made by men in their senses, through birds and other signs, they called οἰονοϊστική, now οἰωνιστική, and “by as much as prophecy is more perfect and more honored than augury”, so much is god-sent madness finer than human sense (*Phaedrus* 244b–d, plain renderings). In this book Helen reads the eagle as one inspired, “as the immortals / put it in my heart” (15.172–173), while the seer knows the falcon at a glance for a bird of augury, “for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen”, οἰωνόν, and says that it flew “not without a god” (15.531–532): [[divination]], [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]].
