**Three dawns.** Telemachus would leave Sparta in the night, but Peisistratus will not drive “through the dark night; and soon it will be dawn” (15.49–50; see [[peisistratus]] and [[dnopheros]]). ἔσσεται ἠώς are the words with which he put off his grief till the morning in Book 4, “there will be early-born / Dawn as well” (4.194–195; see the Book 4 note). “So he spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (15.56; see [[golden-throned]]): the verse of the dawns that ended Circe's two speeches of instruction, and of the dawn of the day on which the suitors die (10.541, 12.142; 20.91; see the Book 10 note). D. B. Monro counts the book from this day, the 36th of the poem (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.1). After the night at Pherae, “when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / they yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot” (15.189–190 = 3.491–492; see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]], [[rosy-fingered]] and [[chariot-journey]]), on the morning of the 37th day (Monro on 15.189). The third dawn joins the book's two threads. Odysseus and Eumaeus talk through the night, “and they slept, not for long, but a little while; / for soon came Dawn of the fair throne. And by the shore / the companions of Telemachus loosed the sails” (15.494–496; see [[of-the-fair-throne]] and [[eumaeus]]): “Dawn of the 38th day. Change of scene, to the landing of Telemachus on the neighbouring coast” (Monro on 15.495). ἐΰθρονος, “of the fair throne”, is hers only here and at the dawn that wakes Nausicaa (6.48; see the Book 6 note).

**Cleitus.** In the line of Melampus, “Mantius begot Polypheides and Cleitus; / but Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (15.249–251; see [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]], [[mantius]], [[polypheides]] and [[immortality]]). The last verse is the Iliad's verse of Ganymede: “and the gods snatched him up to pour wine for Zeus, / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (*Iliad* 20.234–235; see [[ganymede]]); Aristarchus obelized it here (Monro's critical note on 15.251; see the translation's note on 15.225–255). Cleitus is the third of the mortals whom Dawn loves in the Odyssey, with Tithonus, from whose bed she rises, and Orion, whom the gods begrudged her (5.1, 5.121–124; see [[tithonus]], [[orion]], [[dawn-and-orion]] and the Book 5 note); the poem does not say that any harm came to him.
