summary: The heading of the book before fits this book's night in the hut as well as its own. The beggar again begins by “testing the swineherd”, in the same verse, and this time it is Eumaeus who tells his sorrows, how a Phoenician nurse stole him from his father's house on Syrie; the talk ends in the formula that ended the talk of Book 14.

**The second evening.** The scene in the hut that fills the middle of this book is the second of the three evenings that the poem tells there, “one in each of the three books 14–16” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 17.515; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-15]]). It begins as the talk of the first evening ended: after supper “Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd” (15.304), the verse with which he began the story of the cloak (14.459; see [[peira]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). The test this time is whether his host will keep him at the farmstead or send him to beg in the city, and the swineherd keeps him (15.305–339). Then the stranger asks after his master's parents and hears that Laertes prays for death and that his wife died of grief for her son (15.346–360; see [[laertes]] and [[anticleia]]).

**The swineherd's sorrows.** On the first evening Eumaeus asked for the stranger's sorrows, “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows” (14.185); now he tells his own. He bids the stranger sit and drink and listen, since “These nights are endless; there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen” (15.392–393; see [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]]), and gives the reason for such an evening: “But let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut, / take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them” (15.398–400), with the word of his first request, κήδεα, “sorrows” (14.185, 15.399). These are the verses that Aristotle quotes, in a form that differs from our text, for the pleasure of remembered toil (*Rhetoric* 1370b5–6; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]] and [[aristotle]]). His story is how his nurse, a Phoenician woman in his father's house on Syrie, led him out to the ship of Phoenician traders, and how they sold him to Laertes in Ithaca (15.403–484; see [[syrie]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). The stranger answers that his own heart is stirred, and that the swineherd at least came to a kind master, while he himself has “come here after wandering through many cities of mortals” (15.486–492). “So they spoke such words to one another” (15.493) is the verse that closed the talk of the first evening before supper (14.409), and they sleep only a little while before dawn (15.494–495).
