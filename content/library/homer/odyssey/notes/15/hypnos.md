summary: Two sleepless nights. At Sparta Peisistratus lies “overcome by soft sleep”, “but sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus” (15.6–7); in the hut Eumaeus keeps his guest from bed, “These nights are endless; there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen”, for “much sleep too is a vexation” (15.392–394), and the two sleep “not for long, but a little while” (15.494). The book ends at the fold where the swineherd “used to sleep” among the swine (15.557).

**Awake at Sparta.** Athena finds the two young men “sleeping in the porch of glorious Menelaus, / the son of Nestor overcome by soft sleep; / but sweet sleep did not hold Telemachus, but in his heart / through the ambrosial night cares for his father kept him awake” (15.5–8; see [[sweet-sleep]], [[soft]] and [[ambrosial]]). The verses are the opening of *Iliad* 10, where the chiefs “slept all night long, overcome by soft sleep; / but the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, … / was not held by sweet sleep, as he pondered many things in his mind” (*Iliad* 10.2–4): the leader who cannot sleep for his cares has become the son who cannot sleep for his father. The narrator first says that she found both sleeping and then that Telemachus was not; the “contradiction”, like that of Zeus asleep and awake at the end of *Iliad* 1 and the beginning of *Iliad* 2, lies in the epic style of narration (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.4–7; see the translation's note on 15.1–7). The goddess speaks to him awake, standing near him (15.9), and when she is gone he wakes his friend “from sweet sleep” with his heel (15.44–45; see [[peisistratus]]).

**The endless night.** In the hut, after the second supper, Eumaeus begins his story by keeping his guest awake: “These nights are endless; there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen; and you have no need / to go to bed before it is time; much sleep too is a vexation. / And of the rest, whoever's heart and spirit bids him, / let him go out and sleep” (15.392–396; see [[a-time-for-many-words]], [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). πρὶν ὥρη, “before it is time” (15.394), is short for πρὶν ὥρη ἔῃ (Monro on 15.394). Alcinous had kept Odysseus at his story with the same thought: “This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time / to sleep in the hall” (11.373–374). The night of stories is the night of Telemachus' voyage (Monro on 15.301–495), and the two in the hut talk until near dawn: “and they slept, not for long, but a little while; / for soon came Dawn of the fair throne” (15.494–495; see [[dawn-line]]).

**The swineherd's bed.** The book ends where Telemachus is going: to the fold, “where his countless swine were, among which the swineherd, / being good, used to sleep, kind to his masters” (15.556–557; see [[eumaeus]] and [[fold]]), as on the first night he would not sleep in the hut “away from the boars” (14.524–525).
