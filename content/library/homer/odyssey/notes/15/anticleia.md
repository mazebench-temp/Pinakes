**Her death.** The disguised Odysseus asks the swineherd first about “the mother of divine Odysseus” (15.347; see [[odysseus]]), and hears of her last: Laertes mourns “his wise-hearted wedded wife, whose death grieved him / most of all, and brought him to old age before its time. / And she died of grief for her glorious son, / by a wretched death—may no one die so who / lives here as my friend and does me kindness” (15.356–360; see [[laertes]], [[wise-hearted]], [[wedded]] and [[achos]]). It is the death that her shade told him of in the land of the dead: “but longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus, / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (11.202–203). He hears it now from a slave who does not know whom he is telling.

**The swineherd's mistress.** “So long as she lived, grieving though she was, / so long it was a pleasure to me to question and to ask” (15.361–362), the verse with which he had told the stranger that it was no pleasure to him now (14.378): she reared him with her own daughter.

κηρόθι μᾶλλον is a fixed phrase, “right well”, by a litotes, and does not mean “more than before”, as Ameis took it (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.370; see the translation's note on 15.370 and [[ker-heart]]). The bought boy was brought up “little less” honored than the youngest daughter of the house, and given his clothes and sandals when she married (see [[ctimene]], [[eumaeus]], [[cloak]] and [[sandals]]). Since her death, he says, there is no kindness from his mistress, “since evil has fallen on the house” (15.374–375; see [[penelope]]).
