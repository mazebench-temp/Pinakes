summary: The friendship of host and guest: the host who “has shown him friendship” (15.55), the kindness Telemachus met at Sparta (15.158), the guest-friendship “from our fathers' friendship” (15.197) and the “friendship and many gifts” he promises the seer (15.537); the love of the gods for Amphiaraus (15.246); and the love by which the Phoenician seduced Eumaeus' nurse, “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk” (15.420–422).
en: [kindness]

**Friendship given.** φιλότης is what a host gives and a guest remembers. Peisistratus: “For a guest remembers all his days / the host who has shown him friendship” (ὅς κεν φιλότητα παράσχῃ, 15.54–55; see [[xenia]] and [[a-guest-remembers-his-host]]), the half-verse of Menelaus' prayer against Paris in the Iliad (*Iliad* 3.354). Telemachus answers Menelaus' farewell with a wish that he might tell Odysseus of it at home, “as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness” (τυχὼν φιλότητος ἁπάσης, 15.158; see [[menelaus]]), ἁπάσης being, “as we say, ‘nothing but kindness’” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.158). To Peisistratus he claims a friendship inherited: “We claim to be guest-friends without end / from our fathers' friendship” (ἐκ πατέρων φιλότητος, 15.196–197; see [[euchomai]] and [[peisistratus]]). To [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], if the omen comes true, he promises “friendship and many gifts / from me” (15.537–538), perhaps a hendiadys, “hospitality as shown in many gifts” (Monro on 15.537; see [[doron]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]); Penelope will answer Theoclymenus' prophecy and the beggar's oath with the same three verses (17.163–165, 19.309–311).

**The love of the gods.** Amphiaraus, “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love” (παντοίην φιλότητʼ, 15.245–246; see [[amphiaraus]] and [[philos]]), was killed at Thebes before old age (15.246–247; see the translation's note on 15.225–255).

**Love that deceives.** In Eumaeus' story the Phoenician sailor lies with the nurse beside the ship, “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (εὐνῇ καὶ φιλότητι, 15.420–422; the last verse is 11.434; see [[eperopeuo]], [[womenfolk]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]): the pair of words with which Homer names the union of lovers, here as the bait of a theft.
