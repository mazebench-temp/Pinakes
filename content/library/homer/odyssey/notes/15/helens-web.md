summary: Helen's weaving appears in this book as a gift. From the chests where her own robes lie she takes the finest and largest, in the verses of Hecuba's robe for Athena, and gives it to Telemachus as “a remembrance of Helen's hands”, naming herself as its maker.
refs: [15.104-108, 15.123-130]
---

**The robe of her making.** In *Iliad* 3 Helen weaves into a great web the struggles that Trojans and Achaeans suffer for her sake (see the entry above); in this book she gives away a robe of her own making. At Sparta she stands beside the chests, “where her robes were, richly embroidered, which she had made herself”, and takes the one that is most beautiful and largest, “and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others” (15.104–108; see [[robe]] and [[like-a-star]]). The verses are those of the robe that Hecuba carries to Athena, with Helen's name for Hecuba's and one passage changed (*Iliad* 6.289–295). Hecuba's robes were “the work of women / of the Sidonians, whom godlike Alexander himself / had brought from Sidon, sailing over the wide sea / on that voyage on which he brought back Helen” (*Iliad* 6.289–292; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]); here, in place of the women and the voyage that brought Helen to Troy, stand the words οὓς κάμεν αὐτή, “which she had made herself” (15.105). The maker of the web in Troy is the maker of the robe in Sparta.

**A remembrance.** She gives the robe with her own name: “I too give you this gift, dear child, / a remembrance of Helen's hands, for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear” (μνῆμʼ Ἑλένης χειρῶν, 15.125–127). In the Iliad she says that Zeus has set an evil fate on her and Paris “so that hereafter / we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (*Iliad* 6.357–358), and the web shows the war of which she is the cause. The robe is to be kept with Penelope until Telemachus marries (15.127–128), and to keep the memory of its maker in another house. Virgil gives Andromache Helen's words at her parting gift of woven garments to the boy Ascanius, *manuum tibi quae monimenta mearum / sint*, “to be remembrances of my hands” (*Aeneid* 3.486–487; see [[virgil]]).
