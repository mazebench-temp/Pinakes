summary: Eumaeus' story is a traders' story. Phoenicians “famed for ships, / greedy rogues” bring countless baubles to Syrie, stay a whole year trading for a cargo, take the king's son aboard with his nurse, and sell him in Ithaca, where Laertes buys him “with his own possessions” (15.415–483). Their talk has the traders' idiom (15.426–453). The nurse herself was sold by Taphian pirates to the king for a worthy price (15.427–429), and the stranger asks whether the swineherd was sold in the same way (15.386–388).

**The traders.** “And there came Phoenicians, men famed for ships, / greedy rogues, bringing countless baubles in a black ship” (15.415–416; see [[phoenicians]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). τρῶκται, “greedy rogues”, is the word for the Phoenician cheat of the false tale, “a greedy rogue” (14.289), a barytone noun of contemptuous sense, “gnawers” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.416, referring to his note on 14.289); ἀθύρματα, “baubles”, are traders' wares, the word that is a child's “playthings” in the Iliad (*Iliad* 15.363; see the translation's table of key words, and [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). They stay a whole year on the island “and got much substance by trading for their hollow ship” (15.455–456), until “the hollow ship was laden for their going” (15.457).

**The traders' idiom.** Monro hears the market in their words. The nurse's father Arybas, “whose wealth came in a flood” (ῥυδὸν ἀφνειός), has a name that seems Phoenician, perhaps Hasdrubal, and ῥυδόν “sounds like a piece of mercantile argot” (Monro on 15.426; see [[arybas]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]); καλέονται, “are reputed” rich, “looks like a trader's way of speaking” (on 15.433). ὦνον ὁδαίων is “the buying (lit. the price) of your freight”, ὁδαῖα being the cargo taken in on the way, and the nurse's “hasten the buying of your cargo” is a slightly colloquial way of saying “make haste with your bargaining” (on 15.445; 8.163); ἐπίβαθρον is “passage money” (on 15.449). When the cargo is laden, a man of great knowledge brings a golden necklace to the king's house, and the women handle it, “offering a price” (15.459–463; see [[necklace]]): ὑπισχόμεναι, “tendering” (on 15.463). The nurse pays her own way with the child: “he would fetch you a price beyond counting, / wherever you sell him among men of another tongue” (15.452–453). Monro prints περάσαιτε, calling the subjunctive post-Homeric (see the translation's note on 15.453).

**Bought and sold.** The stranger's question names the two ways a free child becomes a slave: a sacked city, or “were you alone with the sheep or the cattle / when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house, and he paid a worthy price for you?” (15.384–388; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). ἐπέρασσαν πρὸς δώματα is pregnant, “brought to the house and there sold” (Monro on 15.387). The nurse's own story was the second way: “Taphian men, pirates, seized me / as I was coming from the fields, and brought me here and sold me / to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me” (15.427–429, the last verse = 15.388; see [[piracy]], [[captive-women]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). The swineherd's is a third: carried off by traders who took him on board as part of their cargo, and sold where the wind brought them, “where Laertes bought me with his own possessions” (15.482–483; 1.430, 14.452; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
