---
summary: Two ponderings at the leave-taking: Menelaus ponders “how he might read it to him fair and right, with understanding,” but Helen is before him (15.169–171); and Peisistratus takes counsel “how he might promise and fulfill it fair and right. / And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way”: to turn the horses to the ship (15.202–205).
refs: [15.169-171, 15.202-205]
---

The two are built alike: the speech closed by ὣς φάτο, the man named, and the question ὅππως οἱ κατὰ μοῖραν. Menelaus' pondering is cut short by Helen, who reads the omen herself (15.171–178; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); Peisistratus' ends with the verse of the choice, as Deiphobus' does in the Iliad (15.204; *Iliad* 13.455–458; see the note on Book 5, and [[kerdos]]). Between his father's wish to keep a guest and his friend's to be gone, he chooses the friend's, and warns him to sail before Nestor comes himself (15.209–214; see [[nestor]] and [[xenia]]). At sea the night ends in a question with no choice: Telemachus sails on, “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (15.300; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).
