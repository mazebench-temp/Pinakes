---
summary: Aristotle quotes the swineherd's verses on the pleasure of remembered sorrows, in a form that makes memory part of the line, beside a verse of Euripides. The Ethics takes from Hesiod a rule for hospitality, “neither a man of many guests nor a man of none”, which is the measure of Menelaus' farewell; the house of Amphiaraus, whose sons this book names, gives the Poetics Alcmaeon and Eriphyle and the Amphiaraus of Carcinus; and the Constitution of the Ithacans, against Hesiod, gave Telemachus Nausicaa for the wife for whom Helen's robe was made.
---

- **Remembered sorrows.** “Things remembered are pleasant, then, not only those that were pleasant at the time, when they were present, but some that were not, if what came after was noble and good; hence this saying: ‘It is sweet to remember toils when one is safe,’ and ‘For a man takes delight even in his sorrows afterward, remembering, one who has suffered much and done much’” (*Rhetoric* 1370b1–6). The first verse is Euripides'; the second is the swineherd's, as he begins the story of his childhood: “for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows, / whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (15.400–401). Aristotle's text has μνημένος, “remembering”, in the verse itself, and “done much” for “wandered much”; in the poem the remembering is in the line before, “take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them” (15.399–400): [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]], [[sweet-to-remember-toils]], [[we-will-remember-these-things-too]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]].
- **The measure of hospitality.** Should one make as many friends as possible, Aristotle asks, “or, just as in the matter of hospitality it seems to have been aptly said, ‘neither a man of many guests nor a man of none’”, so in friendship too one should be neither friendless nor a man of many friends to excess (*Nicomachean Ethics* 9.10, 1170b20–23, a plain rendering, quoting Hesiod, *Works and Days* 715). Menelaus states the same rule of measure for the host: “I am indignant with another / man, a host, who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure; what is due is better in all things”, and keeping a guest who wishes to go is as wrong as sending away one who wishes to stay (15.68–74): [[what-is-due-is-better]], [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]], [[xenia]] and [[hesiod]].
- **The house of Amphiaraus.** Of the line of Melampus that this book traces, Aristotle knows the branch of Amphiaraus. The finest tragedies, he says, are composed about a few houses, “as about Alcmaeon and Oedipus and Orestes and Meleager”, and the stories handed down cannot be undone, “such as the death of Clytemnestra at the hands of Orestes, and of Eriphyle at the hands of Alcmaeon” (*Poetics* 1453a19–21, 1453b22–24). In the Ethics the things that compelled Euripides' Alcmaeon to kill his mother “appear ridiculous” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.1, 1110a27–29, a plain rendering). Carcinus' *Amphiaraus* failed on the stage because the hero came back from the temple in a way the poet had not pictured (*Poetics* 1455a26–29), and the young are least fond of money, “as Pittacus' saying about Amphiaraus has it”, a saying now lost (*Rhetoric* 1389a14–16). In this book Amphiaraus “perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman”, and “His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus” (15.247–248): [[amphiaraus]], [[alcmaeon]], [[amphilochus]], [[eriphyle]], [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]], [[carcinus]] and [[pittacus]].
- **Telemachus' wife.** Helen gives Telemachus her robe “for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear”, and Athena bids him leave his house in the care of the best of the slave women “until the gods show you a glorious wife” (15.125–127, 15.24–26). Eustathius, on *Odyssey* 16.118, reports that Hesiod gave Telemachus Polycaste, the youngest daughter of Nestor, and their son Perseptolis, but that Aristotle, in the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, and Hellanicus said that Telemachus married Nausicaa, the daughter of Alcinous (*Constitution of the Ithacans* fr. 512 Gigon, paraphrased): [[telemachus]], [[polycaste]] and [[nausicaa]].
- **The swineherd's descendants.** Plutarch, in a passage printed among the fragments of the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, tells that Telemachus freed the people around Eumaeus and settled them among the citizens, and that the family of the Koliadai descends from Eumaeus (fr. 511 Gigon, paraphrased). In this book the swineherd tells how he was born a king's son on Syrie and sold as a slave to Laertes (15.403–484): [[eumaeus]], [[slavery-and-freedom]], [[syrie]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]].
