---
summary: Menelaus offers to take Telemachus on a tour “through Hellas and the heart of Argos”, collecting gifts from the cities on the way (15.80–85).
---

“And if you wish to turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos, / so that I myself may go with you, and I will yoke the horses for you / and lead you to the cities of men; and no one will send us / away just as we came, but each will give us at least one thing to take with us, / either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron, / or two mules or a golden goblet” (80–85; see [[menelaus]], [[argos]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The phrase is the one in which Penelope spoke of her husband's fame, “through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.344, 4.726, 4.816), the whole of Greece, north and south (see the Book 1 and Book 4 notes).

**Which Hellas.** J. B. Bury asked why Menelaus, who has just spoken of [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on|speeding the parting guest]], should offer Telemachus, who wants to go home, a tour of all Greece, and answered that Ἑλλάς had come to mean the later Achaea, the north coast of the Peloponnese, so that the offer is only a detour through Argolis and Achaea, lands of the house of Atreus, as D. B. Monro reports his argument. Monro thinks the difficulty not insuperable: ancient manners may have required such a speech from a host, and the phrase “is (or became) a piece of Epic commonplace”, whose meaning “must always have been wide and conventional”. “It is hard to believe that Menelaus would use them to describe a route which he particularly wished to represent as a definite and limited one” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.80–85; see the translation's note on 15.78–85). In the Iliad Hellas is part of Achilles' kingdom in Thessaly (*Iliad* 9.395, 9.447), and the Odyssey keeps that sense when the shade of Achilles asks after Peleus “throughout Hellas and Phthia” (11.496; see [[phthia]]). Aristarchus rejected the whole offer, 78–85 (Monro's critical note on 15.78–85).
