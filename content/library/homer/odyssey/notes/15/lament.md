summary: Laertes, Eumaeus tells the stranger, prays for death, “for he mourns terribly for his son who is gone, / and for his wise-hearted wedded wife”, who died of grief for her son (15.353–359).
# ὀδύρεται (15.355) is caught by the Book 4 note's ὀδυρ*; the Book 14 note has the English "mourns".
refs: [15.356-358]
---

**Laertes' mourning.** The stranger asks whether Odysseus' mother and father still live (15.347–350), and the swineherd answers with a household in mourning: “Laertes still lives, but he prays to Zeus always / that his spirit may perish from his limbs in his own halls; / for he mourns terribly for his son who is gone, / and for his wise-hearted wedded wife, whose death grieved him / most of all, and brought him to old age before its time” (15.353–357; see [[laertes]] and [[prayer]]). ἐν ὠμῷ γήραϊ is “old age before its time”: ὠμός is “unripe”, “premature” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.357; see the translation's note on 15.357). The mourning is the one Anticleia's shade described in the underworld, where Laertes lies grieving on his vineyard slope, “longing for your homecoming, and hard old age comes upon him besides” (11.187–196).

**Anticleia's death.** “And she died of grief for her glorious son, / by a wretched death—may no one die so who / lives here as my friend and does me kindness” (15.358–360; see [[anticleia]]). The swineherd's account agrees with the shade's own: no sickness took her, “but longing for you … / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (11.202–203). Her son, who heard it from her in the underworld, hears it again from a slave who was reared beside her daughter (15.363–365; see [[ctimene]]), and must not show that he knew it.
