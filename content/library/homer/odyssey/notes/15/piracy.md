**Taphian pirates.** The Phoenician woman tells her lover how she came to the king's house on [[syrie|Syrie]]: “but Taphian men, pirates, seized me / as I was coming from the fields, and brought me here and sold me / to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me” (15.427–429; see [[taphians]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[captive-women]]). ληΐστωρ is another form of ληϊστήρ, the pirate of the host's question to strangers (3.73; see the entry). The Taphians are the poem's raiders by sea and its dealers in slaves: Eumaeus bought his own slave Mesaulius from them (14.452; see [[mesaulius]] and [[trade]]), and Athena in the likeness of their lord Mentes sailed with a cargo of iron for bronze (1.180–184; see [[mentes-the-taphian]]).

**How men are taken.** The stranger's question to the swineherd puts the two ways in which a free child becomes a slave: “was the wide-streeted city of men sacked, / in which your father and lady mother lived, / or were you alone with the sheep or the cattle / when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house” (15.384–388; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[hostile-men]]). The raid on the lonely herdsman is the pirate's way. Eumaeus' answer is neither: the men who took him were traders, who stayed a whole year on the island, bought and sold, and took the child by deceit with the help of his nurse (15.415–484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[phoenicians]] and [[eperopeuo]]). The deceit is the Phoenicians' in the poem, as the Cretan's Phoenician “skilled in deceits” meant to sell him in Libya (14.288–297).
