---
summary: Peisistratus' maxim holds back the departure from Sparta until dawn (15.49–55), and the book bears it out. Menelaus gives his gifts and his blessing, Telemachus answers that he goes “having met with nothing but kindness” (15.158), and at the end Telemachus, Peiraeus and the stranger they take in are joined in the same bond (15.537–546).
# 15.53 is the promise of farewell words that the maxim explains.
refs: [15.53]
---

**At Sparta.** Peisistratus asks for patience in the dark and gets it: “So he spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (15.56). What he promised follows in order. Menelaus gives the cup and the silver bowl, Helen her robe “for the time of your lovely marriage” (15.113–129; see [[guest-gifts]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[robe]]); there is a meal before the road, which brings “both, glory and splendor and a blessing too” (15.75–79; see [[feast]]); and at the horses Menelaus pledges them with a golden cup and sends his greeting to Nestor (15.147–153; see [[libation]] and [[kind-as-a-father]]). Telemachus' answer is the guest's half of the bargain: he will tell it all to his father, “as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness” (15.155–159; see [[philotes]]). ἁπάσης is “nothing but kindness”, as we say (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.158).

**At Pylos.** Here the host's welcome is a danger to be avoided. Telemachus asks Peisistratus to leave him at the ship, “lest the old man keep me in his house against my will / in his eagerness to make me welcome” (15.199–201; see [[nestor]] and [[what-is-due-is-better]]). The friendship of the two young men is inherited and made firmer by the road: “We claim to be guest-friends without end / from our fathers' friendship” (15.196–198; see [[xenia]] and [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]).

**The new guest.** The book's last guest is taken in on the same terms. At Pylos Telemachus promises the seer Theoclymenus that “there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have” (15.281), and when the falcon has flown on the right he answers the reading with a host's promise, “then you would quickly know friendship and many gifts / from me” (15.536–538), perhaps a hendiadys, “hospitality as shown in many gifts” (Monro on 15.537; see [[theoclymenus]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). Peiraeus, asked to take the guest home, answers that “he shall not lack good cheer” (15.546; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]).
