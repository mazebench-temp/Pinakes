---
summary: As Telemachus comes to land on Ithaca, “a bird flew by on the right, / a falcon, the swift messenger of Apollo”, holding a dove in its feet and plucking it, and poured the feathers on the ground between the ship and Telemachus; the seer Theoclymenus knows it for a bird of omen and reads it as a sign of the kingly house of Odysseus (15.525–534).
# The falcon without its name: “it held a dove and was plucking it, and it poured the feathers down” (15.527),
# and “the bird” of Theoclymenus' reading (15.531–532).
refs: [15.527, 15.531-532]
---

**Apollo's messenger.** ὣς ἄρα οἱ εἰπόντι ἐπέπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις, / κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος: “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right, / a falcon, the swift messenger of Apollo” (15.525–526; see the translation's row κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος, and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). The first verse brought the eagle of Telemachus' departure from Sparta (15.160; see [[eagle]], [[birds]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and as there the kind is named after the general word. ταχὺς ἄγγελος is the eagle's title in the Iliad, where Priam asks Zeus for “a bird of omen, your swift messenger, the one that to you yourself / is dearest of birds” (*Iliad* 24.310–311; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]). The eagle is the messenger of Zeus, and the falcon is Apollo's. In the Iliad the god himself goes down from the mountains of Ida “like a hawk, / a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (*Iliad* 15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]] and [[apollo]]); in the Odyssey his messenger is the falcon, “the nimblest of winged birds”, the measure of the Phaeacian ship's speed (13.86–87; see the Book 13 note).

**The dove in its feet.** ἐν δὲ πόδεσσι / τίλλε πέλειαν ἔχων, κατὰ δὲ πτερὰ χεῦεν ἔραζε / μεσσηγὺς νηός τε καὶ αὐτοῦ Τηλεμάχοιο: “and in its feet / it held a dove and was plucking it, and it poured the feathers down on the ground / between the ship and Telemachus himself” (15.526–528; see [[doves]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). The translation keeps “feet” for πόδεσσι, as the eagle's “talons” are ὄνυχες (15.161; see the translation's row). In the Iliad's simile the falcon swoops again and again on a timid dove that flees out from under it (*Iliad* 22.139–142; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]); this one has its prey and is plucking it.

**The seer's reading.** Telemachus has just named Eurymachus as the man to whom the stranger may go, “whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, the most eager of the suitors to marry his mother (15.518–524; see [[eurymachus]]). Theoclymenus calls him apart from his companions and clings to his hand: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen. / There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (15.531–534; see [[theoclymenus]], [[not-without-a-god]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). καρτερός is generally said of strength of body, but for καρτεροί, “powerful”, D. B. Monro (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.534) compares the common use of κρατέω in the sense of “bear rule”: the power is the power to rule. The reading does not say what the dove is, and Telemachus answers only with a wish, “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled” (15.536; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). The plain reading comes when the seer tells Penelope that Odysseus is already in his native land, “and he is planting evil for all the suitors. / Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (17.157–161, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]]).
