summary: In Eumaeus' story the Phoenician sailors throw the body of the woman who stole him overboard, “to become a spoil for seals and fish” (15.480–481).
---

**Eaters of the dead.** καὶ τὴν μὲν φώκῃσι καὶ ἰχθύσι κύρμα γενέσθαι / ἔκβαλον: “And her, to become a spoil for seals and fish, / they threw overboard” (15.480–481; see [[seals]] and [[like-a-sea-bird]]). The fish are the sea's eaters of the dead in both poems. Achilles throws Lycaon into the Scamander and tells him to lie “among the fishes, who will lick the blood / from your wound and care nothing for you; nor will your mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you”, and the eels and fishes tear at the fat of Asteropaeus where he lies in the sand (*Iliad* 21.122–124, 21.201–204; see the entry). In the Odyssey it is the fate that the swineherd fears for his master, “or else the fish have eaten him in the sea” (14.135; see the Book 14 note), and that Laertes will fear for his son (24.291). The woman who stole the child dies on the voyage that was to take her home, and goes to the fish without burial; the child she brought aboard as the price of her passage is carried on by the wind and the water to Ithaca and bought by Laertes (15.481–483; see [[eumaeus]], [[laertes]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). ἰχθύσι is the word of the little fishes of the angler's simile in Book 12 (12.252; see the Book 12 note).
