---
summary: Eumaeus' Phoenician nurse, a Sidonian, tells her lover that “Taphian men, pirates” seized her as she was coming from the fields and sold her to the house of Eumaeus' father (15.427–429).
---

“I claim to be from Sidon, rich in bronze, / and I am the daughter of Arybas, whose wealth came in a flood; / but Taphian men, pirates, seized me / as I was coming from the fields, and brought me here and sold me / to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me” (425–429; see [[sidon]], [[arybas]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). Τάφιοι ληΐστορες ἄνδρες names them by their trade: ληΐστωρ, “pirate”, is found only here in Homer, beside the usual ληϊστήρ, the word of Nestor's question to his guests, whether they wander over the sea “like pirates” (3.73), and of the Taphian pirates whom Antinous' father joined against the Thesprotians (16.426; see [[piracy]], [[eupeithes]] and the translation's key-word row).

**Seized and sold.** The nurse's story is the one Odysseus had imagined for Eumaeus himself, a child taken alone in the fields: “or were you alone with the sheep or the cattle / when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house, and he paid a worthy price for you?” (386–388; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). Her last verse is his, ὁ δʼ ἄξιον ὦνον ἔδωκε, “and he paid a worthy price” (388 = 429; see [[trade]]). The pirates who seized her sold her across the sea to a king, as the Phoenicians, in their turn, sell the king's son to Laertes (483; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). In the book before, Eumaeus himself had bought his servant Mesaulius “from the Taphians” (14.452; see [[mesaulius]] and the Book 14 note), and in the first book Athena, in the likeness of their lord Mentes, says that she is sailing with iron to trade for bronze (1.180–184; see [[mentes-the-taphian]]): the same people buy, sell and raid.
