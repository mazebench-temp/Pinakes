**The slave woman.** “And there was in my father's house a Phoenician woman, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork; / and the artful Phoenicians deceived her” (15.417–419; see [[phoenicians]] and [[ctesius]]). πατρός goes with οἴκῳ, “in my father's house”, not with γυνή, as Ameis took it (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.417). The traders' ship had come with countless baubles (15.415–416). As she was washing clothes by the ship, one of them lay with her, “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (15.420–422, the last verse = 11.434 and 24.202; see [[womenfolk]] and [[eperopeuo]]). Asked who she is, she points out her master's house and names her own: she is from Sidon, the daughter of rich Arybas, seized by Taphian pirates and sold here (15.424–429; see [[arybas]], [[sidon]], [[taphians]] and [[piracy]]).

**The bargain.** The trader offers to take her home to her father and mother, “for truly they are still alive and are reputed rich” (15.431–433), and she asks the sailors to swear to bring her home unharmed; the oath is sworn and completed (15.435–438; see [[oath]]). Then she gives her orders: silence, lest someone tell the old man and he bind her and devise destruction for them; let them hasten the buying of their cargo, and send her word when the ship is full; she will bring gold, whatever comes to her hand, “and I would gladly give something else besides, for my passage”:

{{quote:49656936-9758-5019-8e17-bccfdbe8556e}}

κερδαλέον δὴ τοῖον is “a crafty one indeed”, τοῖον after an adjective meaning not “so very” but “quite”, “just” (Monro on 15.451; see the translation's note on 15.451). For περάσητε, the reading of the manuscripts, Monro reads περάσαιτε, since the subjunctive “is post-Homeric both in form and in syntax” (Monro's critical note on 15.453; see the translation's note on 15.453). The traders stay a year, filling their ship (15.454–457; see [[trade]]).

**The theft.** When the ship is laden, a man of great knowledge comes to the house with a golden necklace strung with amber; while the mistress and the slave women handle it and offer a price, he nods to her in silence and goes back to the ship (15.458–464; see [[necklace]] and [[amber]]). “And she took me by the hand and led me out of the house” (15.465): in the porch she finds the cups and tables of the banqueters, who have gone out to the session, hides three goblets in her bosom and carries them off, “and I followed in my childish thoughtlessness” (15.466–470; Monro on 15.470; see [[cup]]). At sunset they reach the harbor and the ship sails, “having taken us two on board” (15.471–475).

**Her death.** “For six days we sailed on, night and day alike, / but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day, / then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman, / and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird. / And her, to become a spoil for seals and fish, / they threw overboard” (15.476–481; see [[artemis]], [[of-the-showering-arrows]], [[like-a-sea-bird]] and [[seals]]). ἐνδούπησε πεσοῦσα is an adaptation, perhaps a parody, of the Iliad's δούπησεν δὲ πεσών, the fall of a warrior (Monro on 15.479; see the translation's note on 15.479). Artemis is the goddess of sudden death for women, and W. W. Merry counts “the daughter of Arybas” among the women she kills, with Ariadne and Andromache's mother (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.280, where the line is numbered 15.477; see [[gentle-arrows]] and [[arybas]]). The child she stole is left “grieving at heart”, and the wind and the water carry the ship to Ithaca, where Laertes buys him (15.481–483; see [[eumaeus]], [[laertes]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).
