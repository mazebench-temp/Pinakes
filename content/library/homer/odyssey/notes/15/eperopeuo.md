summary: In Eumaeus' story “the artful Phoenicians deceived” his nurse, and one of them seduced her “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (15.419–422).
en: [deceived]
---

The traders who came to [[syrie|Syrie]] were “greedy rogues” (15.416), and the woman in the king's house was their prey: “and the artful Phoenicians deceived her. / First, as she was washing clothes, one of them lay with her by the hollow ship / in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (ἠπερόπευον … τά τε φρένας ἠπεροπεύει, 15.419–422; see [[phoenicians]], [[philotes]], [[womenfolk]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). The verb is used twice in three verses, of the men and of love itself. πολυπαίπαλοι, “artful”, is found only here (see the translation's table of key words), and θηλύτερος is not “more female” but female as against male (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.422). The last verse is the one in which Agamemnon's shade says that Clytemnestra “has poured shame on herself and on those to come hereafter, / the womenfolk, even on one who does good” (11.433–434; see [[clytemnestra]]): there one woman's crime shames all women, even a good one; here love deceives them all, even a good one. The deceived woman becomes a deceiver in her turn, and leads her master's son out of the house (15.449–453, 15.465; see [[phrenes]]).
