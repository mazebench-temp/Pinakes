The elder line of Melampus runs through [[antiphates-son-of-melampus|Antiphates]] and Oicles to the great man of the house: “and Oicles Amphiaraus, who rouses armies, / whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman. / His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus” (15.244–248; see [[oicles]], [[who-rouses-armies]], [[aegis-bearing]], [[zeus]], [[apollo]], [[alcmaeon]] and [[amphilochus]]). Zenodotus read Ἀμφιάρηον for Ἀμφιάραον (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.244). Homer names him only here and at 15.253, and never in the Iliad (Monro, Appendix III §10).

**The threshold of old age.** The “threshold” is not the entrance or beginning: “The meaning is that he never fairly set foot in old age”, as of Priam, whom Zeus will destroy “on the threshold of old age” (Monro on 15.246; *Iliad* 22.60; see the translation's note on 15.225–255). Some of the ancients, Plutarch among them (*Consolation to Apollonius* 17, as Monro reports it), saw in the verse a proof of the saying that those whom the gods love die young; “But no such ‘pathetic fallacy’ was in the poet's mind” (Monro on 15.246). The same phrase in the same book marks the other end of life, Laertes, whom his son left “on the threshold of old age” (15.348; see [[laertes]]).

**Gifts to a woman.** γυναίων εἵνεκα δώρων is said of the Ceteians who died with Eurypylus, when Odysseus tells Achilles of his son in the underworld (11.521; Monro on 15.247; see [[eurypylus-son-of-telephus]]), and the gifts are those of his wife Eriphyle, who “took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (11.326–327; see [[eriphyle]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). Monro reads the verse as meaning that Amphiaraus was forced to take part in the war, in which he fell, “because of the necklace given to his wife Eriphyle” (Monro, Appendix III §6).

**The seer.** The poem does not call him a seer in this pedigree, but says that Apollo made his father's cousin Polypheides “by far the best of mortals, after Amphiaraus died” (15.252–253; see [[polypheides]] and [[divination]]), so that the gift of the house passes from the elder line to the younger, and to Theoclymenus (15.256; see [[theoclymenus]]).
