---
summary: The chariot that brought Telemachus and Peisistratus to Sparta takes them home. Menelaus sets his gifts in it and Peisistratus stows them in its wicker body (15.51, 15.75, 15.131); the two drive out after the parting cup, lodge again at Pherae and come to Pylos the next morning, in the verses of their journey out (15.144–192); and at the ship Peisistratus drives back to the city alone (15.215–216).
# Forms first met here; they apply in the Odyssey only: ἐπιδίφρια, "in the chariot" (15.51, 15.75, only here in
# Homer), and μάστιν, "the whip" (15.182, only here in Homer; the Iliad's μάστι, 23.500, is in its Book 23 note).

---

**Waiting for the gifts.** Woken by Telemachus in the night, “bring the single-hoofed horses / and yoke them under the chariot, so that we may make our way” (15.46–47), Peisistratus will not drive through the dark, but bids him wait “until he brings gifts and sets them in the chariot” (15.49–51; see [[peisistratus]], [[single-hoofed]] and [[horses]]), and Menelaus says the same: “But wait until I bring gifts and set them in the chariot, / fine ones, and you see them with your eyes” (15.75–76; see [[guest-gifts]]). ἐπιδίφριος, “in the chariot”, is found only in these two verses (see the translation's row ἐπιδίφριος); the Suda explains ἐπιδίφρια as the things put on the seat of a chariot, and the word in the masculine as one who sits on a seat (ε 2302). For a journey through Hellas and Argos Menelaus offers to yoke the horses himself (15.80–81; see [[tripod]]). When the gifts are given, Peisistratus lays them “in the wicker body of the chariot” (15.131; see [[wagon]]).

**The drive.** After the meal “Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor / yoked the horses and mounted the inlaid chariot, / and drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade” (15.144–146, the last two verses as 3.492–493; see [[inlaid]], [[door]] and [[loud-resounding]]). Helen reads the omen of the eagle, and when Telemachus has answered her, “He spoke, and laid the whip on the horses; and very quickly / they sped through the city toward the plain, eager” (15.182–183; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). μάστιν, “whip”, is a rare accusative, found only here in Homer, beside the Iliad's dative μάστι (*Iliad* 23.500; see the Iliad's Book 23 note). The rest is the journey out told again: they shake the yoke all day, come to Pherae at sunset and lodge with Diocles, and set out again at dawn, “And he whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling” (15.184–192 = 3.486–494; see [[chariot-journey]], [[sunset-lines]], [[pherae-in-messenia]] and [[diocles]]). The text prints οὐκ ἄκοντε, where the same verse has ἀέκοντε elsewhere (see the translation's note on 15.192).

**Back to Pylos alone.** At Pylos Telemachus asks not to be taken past his ship, for fear that the old man would keep him, and Peisistratus “turned the horses to the swift ship and the shore of the sea” and took out the gifts; then he “drove the fine-maned horses / back to the city of the Pylians, and quickly reached the house” (15.199–216; see [[nestor]], [[fine-maned]] and [[pylos]]). The city lay some way inland, as Strabo points out (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.199; see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]).
