summary: Apollo is the god of the seers' house. He and Zeus loved Amphiaraus “with all their hearts” (15.245), and when Amphiaraus died he made Polypheides a seer, “by far the best of mortals” (15.252–253); on Eumaeus' island of Syrie he comes with Artemis to the old and kills them with his gentle arrows (15.409–411); and the falcon that flies by on the right as Telemachus lands is “the swift messenger of Apollo” (15.526), which Polypheides' son Theoclymenus reads.

**The god of the seers.** The seer Theoclymenus is brought in with his descent from Melampus, and two gods stand in it. Amphiaraus was the man “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts” (15.245; see [[amphiaraus]] and the Book 15 note on [[zeus]]), and after his death “Apollo made high-hearted Polypheides a seer, / by far the best of mortals” (15.252–253; see [[polypheides]], [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]). βροτῶν ὄχʼ ἄριστον is the praise of Calchas, “by far the best of the bird-seers”, who guided the Achaean ships to Ilios “by his gift of prophecy, which Phoebus Apollo had granted him” (*Iliad* 1.69–72; see [[calchas]] and [[best-of-bird-seers]]). Polypheides moved to Hyperesia, “angry with his father”, and “living there he prophesied for all mortals” (15.254–255; see [[hyperesia]]); “His son it was who came up, and Theoclymenus was his name” (15.256; see [[theoclymenus]]), τοῦ being Polypheides (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.256). In the Odyssey Apollo also gives oracles at Pytho, where Agamemnon once consulted him (8.79–82; see the Book 8 note).

**The gentle arrows.** On Syrie no sickness comes: “but when the tribes of men grow old throughout the city, / Apollo, god of the silver bow, comes with Artemis / and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them” (15.409–411; see [[syrie]], [[god-of-the-silver-bow]], [[gentle-arrows]] and [[artemis]]). The last verse is said elsewhere of one archer alone: of Apollo, when he killed Menelaus' helmsman at Sunium (3.280; see the Book 3 note), and of Artemis (5.124, 11.173, 11.199); here the god comes with the goddess. In the rest of the poem the deaths that Apollo sends come before their time: the helmsman at his steering oar, Rhexenor newly married, and Eurytus, who challenged him with the bow and “did not reach old age / in his halls” (3.279–283, 7.64–66, 8.226–228; see the Book 7 note), as Amphiaraus, whom Apollo loved, “did not reach the threshold of old age” (15.246). On Syrie his arrows come only when the people have grown old, and the island is one of the two that Monro calls mythical, “in respect of the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants” (Monro on 15.403). W. W. Merry, who takes the gentle arrows for a sudden death without suffering, notes that here such a death is set against every form of sickness (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.280). Monro prints ἀγανοῖσι βέλεσσιν, the reading of two manuscripts (Monro's critical note on 15.411), which is the form of the verse in Hecuba's lament for Hector (*Iliad* 24.759); Murray prints the vulgate ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν.

**The swift messenger.** As Telemachus leaves the suitors' end to Zeus, “a bird flew by on the right, / a falcon, the swift messenger of Apollo; and in its feet / it held a dove and was plucking it” (15.525–527; see [[falcon]], [[doves]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). ταχὺς ἄγγελος is the Iliad's phrase for the bird of Zeus, which Priam asks for before he goes to Achilles, “a bird of omen, your swift messenger, the one that to you yourself / is dearest of birds, and whose strength is greatest, / on the right”, and Zeus sends an eagle (*Iliad* 24.310–315; see [[eagle]]). Here the messenger is Apollo's, and the god himself once went down from Ida “like a hawk, / a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (*Iliad* 15.237–238). The reader of the sign is the seer of Apollo's house, the son of [[polypheides|Polypheides]]: “Telemachus, not without a god did the bird fly on the right; / for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (15.531–532; see [[not-without-a-god]] and [[birds]]).
