**The question.** Eumaeus has told how the mother of Odysseus reared him with her daughter Ctimene (15.361–370; see [[anticleia]] and [[ctimene]]), and the beggar takes it up: “Ah, so you were still small, swineherd Eumaeus, / when you were driven so far from your native land and your parents” (15.381–382). Was his city sacked, or was he alone with the sheep or the cattle “when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house” (15.383–388)? οἴεσιν stands for ὄεσιν for the sake of the meter, and ἐπέρασσαν … πρὸς δώματα is a pregnant use, “brought to the house and there sold”, as Ctimene was given in marriage “to Same” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.386, 15.387, comparing 15.367; see the translation's note on 15.367). The verse and its sequel are those of Lycaon's sale in the Iliad: Achilles “sold him into well-built Lemnos, / taking him there in his ships, and the son of Jason paid the price”, ἐπέρασσε … νηυσὶν ἄγων … ὦνον ἔδωκε (*Iliad* 21.40–41; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[trade]]).

**The preface.** “These nights are endless; there is time to sleep, / and time for those who take delight in it to listen” (15.392–393; see [[a-time-for-many-words]] and [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]]). The others may go to sleep and follow the master's swine at dawn, but the two of them, drinking and feasting in the hut, will “take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them” (15.398–400): μνωομένω is from μνάομαι, “call to mind”, and μετά is “in turn”, afterward (Monro on 15.400; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]).

**The island and the king.** The tale begins with the island, “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (15.403–404), whose people know no famine or sickness and die only when Apollo and Artemis come upon them in old age with their gentle arrows (15.405–411). For Monro the island has “a mythical character”, both as the turning point of the sun and in “the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants” (on 15.403; see [[syrie]], [[ortygia]], [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). It has two cities, “and everything is divided between them”, and the swineherd's father ruled them both, “Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals” (15.412–414; see [[ctesius]] and [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]]).

**The traders.** The Phoenicians are ναυσίκλυτοι, “famed for ships”, the epithet otherwise of the Phaeacians (15.415; 7.39; see [[famed-for-ships]]); τρῶκται, “greedy rogues”, which Monro explains at the Phoenician of the Cretan tale, “‘Gnawer’ may suggest thieving vermin” (15.416; Monro on 15.416, referring to his note on 14.289); they bring ἀθύρματα, “baubles”, the word of the child's playthings in the Iliad (15.416; *Iliad* 15.363; see the translation's key-word row for τρῶκται; ἀθύρματα, and [[phoenicians]]); and they are πολυπαίπαλοι, “artful”, a word found only here (15.419). The woman is “in my father's house”: πατρός goes with οἴκῳ, not with γυνή, as Ameis took it (15.417; Monro on 15.417; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). She is one of themselves, a Phoenician, and she is seduced first: “in bed and love, which deceive the minds / of the womenfolk, even of one who does good” (15.421–422; 15.422 = 11.434, of Clytemnestra; see [[womenfolk]] and [[eperopeuo]]). θηλύτερος is not “more female”, but female as against male, as θεώτερος is “divine” (Monro on 15.422).

**The bargain.** Asked who she is, “she at once pointed out the high-roofed house of my father” (15.424), the verse in which the Laestrygonian girl pointed out the house “of her father” (10.111): here πατρός “must surely mean ‘my father’”, Monro holds with Pierron and Hayman, and the house she points to is the one where she is a slave, so that the man's answer, “so that you may see the high-roofed house of your father and mother” (15.432), repeats the words “with a different reference”, probably on purpose: “You have shown us the ‘great house’ where you are kept in slavery: will you return to the ‘great house’ of your father and mother?” (on 15.424; see the translation's note on 15.417, 15.424). Her story of herself, Sidon and Arybas and the Taphian pirates, ends in the verse of Odysseus' guess about Eumaeus, “to this man's house; and he paid a worthy price for me” (15.429 = 15.388; see [[arybas]], [[taphians]] and [[captive-women]]). The talk on both sides is traders' talk: ῥυδὸν ἀφνειοῖο, “whose wealth came in a flood”, “sounds like a piece of mercantile argot”, and καλέονται, “are reputed”, “looks like a trader's way of speaking” (Monro on 15.426, 15.433). They swear, in the verse of the companions' oath not to kill the cattle of the Sun (15.438 = 12.304; also 18.59; see [[oath]]), and she gives her orders: if the old man heard of it he would bind her “in a grievous bond” (15.443–444), the bond of Melampus in the halls of Phylacus (15.232); ὦνον ὁδαίων is “the buying (lit. the price) of your freight”, ἐπίβαθρον is “passage money”, and κερδαλέον δὴ τοῖον is “a crafty one indeed”, τοῖον after an adjective meaning “quite”, “just” (Monro on 15.445, 15.449, 15.451; see the translation's notes on 15.433, 15.445 and 15.451). For περάσητε (15.453) Monro prints περάσαιτε, the subjunctive being “post-Homeric both in form and in syntax” (his critical note on 15.453; see the translation's note on 15.453).

**The theft.** The traders stay “for a whole year” and get “much substance by trading” (15.455–456; see [[trade]]). Their messenger is ἀνὴρ πολύϊδρις, “a man of great knowledge”, and he brings a golden necklace, μετὰ δʼ ἠλέκτροισιν ἔερτο, “strung here and there with amber” (15.459–460): the ὅρμος “passed round the neck and hung down on the breast”, μετά is “at intervals”, and the plural ἠλέκτροισιν suits pieces of amber, “always found in lumps”, not the alloy of gold and silver (Monro on 15.460; see [[necklace]] and [[amber]]). The mistress and her women handle it, ὦνον ὑπισχόμεναι, “offering a price”, “tendering, i.e. making offers for it, chaffering”, and the man nods to the nurse in silence (15.461–463; Monro on 15.463). In the porch she finds the cups and tables of the banqueters, who are the king's counsellors, like the twelve elders of the Phaeacians, and are gone to the θῶκος, the “sitting-place” or tribunal, and the φῆμις, here the place of the people's talk (15.466–468; Monro on 15.466, 15.467, 15.468; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]); she hides three goblets in her bosom, and the child follows, ἀεσιφροσύνῃσι, “in my childish thoughtlessness” (15.469–470; Monro on 15.470; see [[cup]]).

**The voyage.** “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (15.471; see [[sunset-lines]]): they reach the harbor and sail, and Zeus sends a fair wind (15.472–475). Six days they sail, and on the seventh Artemis strikes the woman (15.476–478; 15.476 = 10.80, 15.477 = 12.399; see [[artemis]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). ἐνδούπησε πεσοῦσα, “fell … with a splash”, is “an adaptation, perhaps a parody, of the conventional δούπησεν δὲ πεσών of the Iliad”, the fall of a warrior in his armor (15.479; Monro on 15.479; see [[like-a-sea-bird]]). They throw her overboard “to become a spoil for seals and fish”, the wind and the water carry the ship to Ithaca, and there Laertes buys the boy (15.480–483; see [[seals]], [[fish]] and [[laertes]]). “So it was that I came to see this land with my eyes” (15.484): Monro prints Bothe's τήνδε γε for the manuscripts' τήνδε τε (his critical note on 15.484).

**Odysseus' answer.** “Eumaeus, truly you have greatly stirred the heart within me” (15.486): ἄλγεα, “sorrows”, properly belongs to the antecedent, as if the verse said ἄλγεα ὅσα δὴ πάθες, “the sorrows that you suffered” (Monro on 15.487). “But truly for you Zeus has set good too beside the evil” (15.488): καί goes with ἐσθλόν, and κακῷ is set between them to bring the contrasted words together (Monro on 15.488; see the translation's note on 15.488, and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). The boy came to the house of a man who is ἤπιος, “kind” (15.489–490), the word of Eumaeus' praise of his absent master on the first evening, “never again another / master so kind shall I find” (14.138–139); the master who says it ends with himself, a wanderer through many cities of mortals (15.491–492; see [[odysseus]]). They sleep only a little, “for soon came Dawn of the fair throne” (15.493–495), and the poem goes back to Telemachus' ship (see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
