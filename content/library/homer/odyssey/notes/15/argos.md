---
summary: Menelaus offers Telemachus a tour “through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (15.80); the seer Theoclymenus comes “fleeing from Argos, having killed a man”, where his ancestor Melampus had settled to rule “over many Argives”, and where the dead man's kin “rule with great power over the Achaeans” (15.224, 15.238–242, 15.272–274).
---

**The heart of Argos.** When Telemachus asks to go home, Menelaus offers instead to take him round the country, gathering gifts: “And if you wish to turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos, / so that I myself may go with you” (80–85; see [[hellas]], [[menelaus]] and [[guest-gifts]]). ἀνʼ Ἑλλάδα καὶ μέσον Ἄργος is the verse-end of Penelope's praise of her husband, whose fame is wide “through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.344, 4.726, 4.816; see the Book 1 note), with ἀνά for κατά. J. B. Bury, as D. B. Monro reports him, took Hellas here for the later Achaea, the north coast of the Peloponnese, so that Menelaus would be offering only a detour through Argolis and Achaea; Monro answers that the phrase “is (or became) a piece of Epic commonplace”, a variation of the Iliad's “to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the lovely women” (*Iliad* 3.75, 3.258), whose meaning “must always have been wide and conventional”, and that “μέσον Ἄργος is not to be pressed”, any more than Diomedes' Ἄργεϊ μέσσῳ, “in the heart of Argos” (*Iliad* 6.224): nothing ties it to the Argive plain as against the wider Argos (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.80–85; see the translation's note on 15.78–85). Aristarchus rejected 78–85 (Monro's critical note on 15.78–85).

**Horse-pasturing Argos.** The seer who comes up to Telemachus at Pylos is “from a far land, fleeing from Argos, having killed a man” (224; see [[theoclymenus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]), and the poem gives his descent from Melampus, who left Pylos for Argos: “And he himself came to the land of other men, / to horse-pasturing Argos; for there it was his lot / to dwell, ruling over many Argives; / and there he married a wife and built a high-roofed house, / and begot Antiphates and Mantius, two mighty sons” (238–242; see [[melampus]], [[argives]], [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]] and [[mantius]]). ἄλλων δῆμον, “the land of other men” (228), is Argos, as the taking up of the words ten verses later shows, and ἀνάσσοντʼ is an accusative agreeing with the understood subject of ναιέμεναι, “to dwell … ruling” (Monro on 15.228, 15.240). Herodotus knew how Melampus came to rule there: when the women of Argos went mad, the Argives sent for him to Pylos to cure them, and he asked half the kingship as his price, and then a third for his brother Bias (*Histories* 9.34; see [[herodotus]] and [[bias]]). Ἄργος ἐς ἱππόβοτον, “to horse-pasturing Argos”, is the Iliad's verse-beginning for the Achaeans' homeland and for the Argos to which Zeus brought Heracles back (*Iliad* 3.75, 15.30; see [[horse-pasturing]]).

**The kin of the dead man.** [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] asks to come on board: “So I too am far from my native land, having killed a man / of my own tribe; and he has many brothers and kinsmen / in horse-pasturing Argos, and they rule with great power over the Achaeans” (272–274; see [[the-killer-in-exile]] and [[supplication]]). Ἀχαιῶν here is the people of Argos itself, among whom the dead man's family is powerful; the same verb, κρατέουσιν, “rule”, is said of the Epeians in Elis a few verses later (298; see [[epeians]]).
