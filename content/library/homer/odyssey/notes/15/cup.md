summary: Menelaus gives Telemachus a two-handled cup with the silver bowl (15.102, 15.120) and brings the parting wine in a golden cup, in the verses of Hecuba's cup for Priam (15.147–150). The beggar asks only for “a cup and a loaf” (15.312), and Eumaeus' nurse hides three goblets in her bosom as she flees (15.466–470).
# Forms first met here; they apply in the Odyssey only. κοτύλην, the beggar's cup (15.312; later 17.12), since the
# Iliad's κοτύλην is also the socket of the hip (Il. 5.306–307); δέπα, the cups of a feast (15.466; later 19.62,
# 20.153); and ἄλεισα, the goblets the nurse steals (15.469, the only plural in Homer). ἄλεισον (15.85), δέπας (15.102,
# 15.120) and δέπαϊ (15.149) are claimed by the Book 3 note.

**The gifts.** In Book 4 Menelaus had offered three horses, a chariot and “a fine goblet, so that you may pour libations to the gods”, and when Telemachus asked for a treasure instead of horses, he promised the silver bowl (4.589–619; see the Book 4 note). Now, in the chamber where the treasures lie, “the son of Atreus took a two-handled cup, / and bade his son Megapenthes carry a mixing bowl / of silver” (15.102–104; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[megapenthes]] and [[possessions]]), and when he has told the bowl's story, “he put the two-handled cup in his hand” (15.120; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[telemachus]]), nearly the words with which Odysseus, taking his leave of the Phaeacians, put the two-handled cup in Arete's hand (13.57). A golden goblet is also among the gifts that Menelaus promises from the hosts of a journey through Hellas and Argos: no one will send them away empty-handed, “but each will give us at least one thing to take with us, / either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron, / or two mules or a golden goblet” (15.83–85; see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[mules]]).

**The parting cup.** “And fair-haired Menelaus, son of Atreus, went after them, / holding wine sweet to the heart in his right hand / in a golden cup, so that they might pour a libation before they went” (15.147–149; see [[libation]] and [[wine]]): Hecuba came to Priam with the same verses before he drove out to Achilles (*Iliad* 24.283–285), where the cup is χρυσέῳ ἐν δέπαϊ, and some manuscripts give that order here too; Monro suggests χρυσείῳ δέπαϊ, which Aristarchus read at 3.41 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 15.149; see the translation's formula row for 15.148–150). He stands before the horses and pledges them (15.150), as Nestor's son pledged Athena with a golden cup at Pylos (3.41; see [[peisistratus]]).

**The beggar's cup.** Testing the swineherd, Odysseus says that he will go to the city and wander through it, “in case someone may hold out to me a cup and a loaf” (15.312; see [[peira]] and [[eumaeus]]). κοτύλη is a small cup, the beggar's, as in Andromache's picture of her orphaned son among his father's companions, of whom “one holds out a cup for a moment” (*Iliad* 22.494; see the translation's row κοτύλη; πύρνον; ὀρέγω). Telemachus will send him to the city in the same words, to be given “a loaf and a cup” by whoever will (17.12, a plain rendering).

**The stolen goblets.** When the nurse leads the child out of the house, “in the porch she found the cups and the tables / of the banqueters, who attended on my father; / they had gone out to the session and the talk of the people, / and she quickly hid three goblets in her bosom / and carried them off” (15.466–470; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[table]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). After a feast the slave women carried the tables and the cups away (19.61–62), and from this place Monro infers that they were taken into the πρόδομος, the entrance hall of the μέγαρον (on 15.466; see [[house]]). She had promised the traders gold, “whatever comes to my hand”, and the child for her passage (15.448–453; see [[trade]]). For ὑπὸ κόλπῳ, “in her bosom”, Aristophanes read κόλπου (Monro's critical note on 15.469).
