---
summary: Eumaeus' reason for staying up with his guest (15.398–401): the two of them will take delight in each other's sorrows as they remember them, “for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows, / whoever has suffered very much and wandered much”. Aristotle quotes the verses in another form (*Rhetoric* 1370b5–6).
# "ἄλγεσι τέρπεται" (15.400), only here in Homer; the Rhetoric note has the same phrase for Aristotle's text.
grc: ["ἄλγεσι τέρπεται"]
en: [for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows]
refs: [15.398-399, 15.401]
---

{{quote:29935b95-827e-5bbf-8b00-5032f087be60}}

**Each other's sorrows.** The swineherd has sent the others out to sleep and kept the stranger with him (15.390–397; see [[much-sleep-is-a-vexation]]). The night is to be an exchange: κήδεσιν ἀλλήλων τερπώμεθα, “take delight in each other's wretched sorrows” (15.399). The stranger told his own the night before, a false tale of Crete, Egypt and Thesprotia (14.192–359; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]); now Eumaeus tells his, the true story of a king's son stolen and sold (15.403–484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). μνωομένω is from μνάομαι, “call to mind”, and μετά is “in turn” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.400): the delight comes after the suffering, in its turn.

**Who has wandered much.** The man the maxim speaks of is “whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (ὅς τις δὴ μάλα πολλὰ πάθῃ καὶ πόλλʼ ἐπαληθῇ, 15.401), in the words with which Helen foretold Odysseus' return that morning, “after much suffering and much wandering” (κακὰ πολλὰ παθὼν καὶ πόλλʼ ἐπαληθείς, 15.176; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]), and the poem's first verses tell the same man by the same two things (1.1–4; see [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]]). Eumaeus offers the pleasure as a host to his guest; it fits the guest better than he knows. Aristotle's text has “done much” for “wandered much” (*Rhetoric* 1370b5–6; see the entry and [[sweet-to-remember-toils]]).
