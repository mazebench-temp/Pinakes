**Mother of flocks.** Melampus, the ancestor of [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], “once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks, / a rich man among the Pylians” (15.226–227; see [[melampus]] and [[pylos]]). μήτηρ μήλων is the epithet of Iton in the Iliad's Catalogue, “Iton, mother of flocks” (*Iliad* 2.696; see [[mother-of-flocks]] and the translation's row μήτηρ μήλων); here it is given to Pylos.

**Alone with the sheep.** Eumaeus has said that Odysseus' mother reared him with her daughter Ctimene (15.363; see [[anticleia]] and [[ctimene]]), and Odysseus understands that he was still small when he was driven from home, and asks how: “was the wide-streeted city of men sacked, / in which your father and lady mother lived, / or were you alone with the sheep or the cattle / when hostile men took you in their ships and sold you / to this man's house, and he paid a worthy price for you?” (15.384–388; see [[eumaeus]], [[cattle]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). οἴεσιν is found only here; it stands for ὄεσιν, lengthened for the meter (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.386). The usual datives are ὄεσσι and ὀΐεσσι, and the Iliad has them in the same story of herdsmen taken among their flocks: Achilles bound Priam's sons Isus and Antiphus on the slopes of Ida, “catching them as they herded sheep” (ποιμαίνοντʼ ἐπʼ ὄεσσι λαβών), and released them for a ransom (*Iliad* 11.104–106; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]), and he killed Andromache's seven brothers “among their shambling cattle and white sheep” (*Iliad* 6.423–424). In these stories princes herd the flocks, and the flocks are where the raiders find them. The ships and the price are in the Iliad too: Achilles took Lycaon from his father's orchard and sold him into Lemnos, “taking him there in his ships, and the son of Jason paid the price” (*Iliad* 21.35–41; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Eumaeus answers that it was neither: the woman who nursed him led him out of his father's house to the ship of Phoenician traders, who sold him in Ithaca (15.403–484; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]]).

**Good for sheep.** Syrie, his father's island, is “not so very populous, but a good one, / good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat” (15.405–406; see [[syrie]] and the translation's row περιπληθής; εὔβοτος, εὔμηλος, οἰνοπληθής, πολύπυρος). εὔμηλος, “good for sheep”, is found as a common word only here in Homer; as a name it is the son of Admetus who led the men of Pherae to Troy (*Iliad* 2.711–714), who in the Odyssey is the husband of Penelope's sister Iphthime (4.797–798; see [[eumelus]] and [[iphthime]]). πολύμηλος, “rich in flocks”, is said in the Iliad of a town and of men (*Iliad* 2.605, 2.705, 14.490; see [[rich-in-flocks]]). The king's son of that island now keeps another man's swine (see [[boar]]).
