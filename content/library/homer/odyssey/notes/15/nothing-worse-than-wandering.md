summary: The beggar's thanks to the swineherd who has kept him from the city (15.341–345), at the center of a book of wanderers: Telemachus is told not to wander far from home, Theoclymenus must wander among men, Eumaeus was driven far from his home as a child, and Odysseus, in Helen's reading of the eagle, comes home after much suffering and much wandering.
# The reason (15.342) and the second half of the maxim (15.344-345); 15.343 is the entry's form.
refs: [15.342, 15.344-345]
---

**In the hut.** The saying is the hinge of the evening. The test is over: the stranger has offered to go to the city at dawn and beg, and the swineherd has bidden him stay (15.307–339; see [[peira]] and [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]]). Odysseus thanks him with a wish and a maxim, and then, “since you hold me back and bid me wait for him”, asks about his own mother and father, whether they still live (15.346–350; see [[laertes]] and [[anticleia]]). The answer leads to [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus|the swineherd's story]], and the night of talk ends with the beggar's own wandering again: “but I / have come here after wandering through many cities of mortals” (ἀλώμενος, 15.491–492).

**A book of wanderers.** ἀλάομαι and πλάζομαι run through the book. Athena tells Telemachus that it is no longer good for him to wander far from his home (ἀλάλησαι, 15.10; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]); Helen reads the eagle as Odysseus coming home “after much suffering and much wandering” (ἐπαληθείς, 15.176; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], fleeing the kin of the man he killed, says “it is my lot to wander among men” (ἀλάλησθαι, 15.276; see [[exile-and-refuge]]); Eumaeus was “driven so far” from his native land as a child (ἀπεπλάγχθης, 15.382), and he gives the night's pleasure to “whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (ἐπαληθῇ, 15.401; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). Only the wanderer who speaks the saying is at rest for the night, in the hut of his own swineherd.
