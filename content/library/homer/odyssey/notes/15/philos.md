summary: φιλέω of hosts and of love: the host who “loves beyond measure” and the rule that one should “make welcome a guest who is present” (15.70, 15.74); Nestor's “eagerness to make me welcome” (15.201), the stranger who “shall be welcomed” on Telemachus' ship (15.281) and in Peiraeus' house (15.543), and Odysseus' test whether Eumaeus “would still welcome him with care” (15.305); Zeus and Apollo, who “loved” Amphiaraus (15.245), and Anticleia, who “loved me dearly in her heart” (15.370); and φίλος of the dear ones, the dear son, the dear land, a dear husband and a friend (15.22, 15.59, 15.65, 15.360).
# φιλέῃσιν (15.70), φιλεῖν (15.74), φιλέοι (15.305), first met here among the Odyssey's notes, and φίλει (15.245, 15.370;
# again 10.14, Aeolus' welcome).
grc: [φιλέῃσιν, φιλεῖν, φιλέοι, φίλει]
en: [welcomed, kindness]
---

**To make welcome.** φιλέω is the host's verb, and the book uses it for every kind of host. Menelaus blames the host “who loves beyond measure / and hates beyond measure” (15.70–71), where the verb keeps “love” for the antithesis, and gives the rule, “One should make welcome a guest who is present, and send him off when he wishes” (φιλεῖν, 15.74; see the translation's table of key words, ἔξοχα φιλέω, and [[xenia]], [[what-is-due-is-better]] and [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]]). Telemachus fears the opposite fault in Nestor, who would keep him “in his eagerness to make me welcome” (ἱέμενος φιλέειν, 15.201; see [[nestor]]). He promises the suppliant seer, “there you shall be welcomed with such things as we have” (15.281), and asks Peiraeus to “welcome him with care and honor him, until I come” (ἐνδυκέως φιλέειν καὶ τιέμεν, 15.543; again 17.56; see [[time]] and [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]). In the hut Odysseus makes trial of the same verb: he speaks of leaving, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would still welcome him with care and bid him stay” (ἐνδυκέως φιλέοι, 15.304–305; see [[peira]]). ἐνδυκέως, “with care”, belongs to the Odyssey and the last two books of the Iliad, and must mean “kindly”, “in gentle fashion”, or else “zealously” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.62); Odysseus uses it again of Laertes, who gives Eumaeus food and drink “with care” (15.490–491).

**Loved.** Of the gods: Amphiaraus, “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age” (περὶ κῆρι φίλει, 15.245–246; see [[amphiaraus]], [[ker-heart]] and [[philotes]]). Some of the ancients, Plutarch among them, saw in the verse the saying that those whom the gods love die young, “But no such ‘pathetic fallacy’ was in the poet's mind” (Monro on 15.246). Of a mistress: Anticleia reared the slave child with her daughter, clothed him and sent him to the farm, “and she loved me dearly in her heart” (φίλει δέ με κηρόθι μᾶλλον, 15.370; see [[anticleia]] and [[ctimene]]).

**Dear ones.** φίλος keeps its formulas: “the dear son of Odysseus” (15.59, 15.63, 15.337, 15.554; see [[dear-son-of-odysseus]]), “my dear native land” (15.65; see [[dear-native-land]]), Helen's and Theoclymenus' “dear child” (15.125, 15.509) and “your dear mother” (15.127). Athena's warning turns on the word: a woman who remarries forgets “her dear wedded husband” once he is dead (15.22; see [[a-womans-heart]]). The seer greets Telemachus at the ship with “My friend” (ὦ φίλʼ, 15.260; see [[theoclymenus]]). In the hut Eumaeus wishes no such death as Anticleia's on anyone “who / lives here as my friend and does me kindness” (φίλος εἴη καὶ φίλα ἔρδοι, 15.359–360), and says that while she lived “it was a pleasure to me to question and to ask” (φίλον, 15.362); Odysseus' blessing returns the word, “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me” (15.341–342; see [[zeus]]).
