summary: Offering to take Telemachus on a tour through Hellas and the heart of Argos, Menelaus promises that no host will send them away empty, “but each will give us at least one thing to take with us, / either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron, / or two mules or a golden goblet” (15.83–85).
---

**A gift of mules.** “And if you wish to turn aside through Hellas and the heart of Argos, / so that I myself may go with you, and I will yoke the horses for you / and lead you to the cities of men; and no one will send us / away just as we came, but each will give us at least one thing to take with us, / either a tripod of good bronze or a cauldron, / or two mules or a golden goblet” (15.80–85; see [[menelaus]], [[hellas]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The mules stand among the treasures of bronze and gold that a host gives a guest (see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[cup]]). So the Mysians once gave Priam the strong-hoofed mules that work in harness, “splendid gifts”, which draw his wagon to Achilles' hut (*Iliad* 24.277–278), and mules are prizes at the games for Patroclus, among them a hard-working mule, six years old and unbroken, for the winner of the boxing (*Iliad* 23.260, 23.653–655; see [[hard-working]] and [[unbroken]]). δύʼ ἡμιόνους, “two mules”, are a team: the mules that draw Nausicaa's wagon are named in the dual, ἡμιόνοιιν (6.82; see the Book 6 note). In Book 4 Menelaus had offered Telemachus three horses and a chariot, which he refused because Ithaca is no land for horses (4.589–608; see the Book 4 note on [[horses]]); Noemon, who lent him his ship, keeps his mares and their mule foals in Elis (4.634–637; see the Book 4 note). Here too Telemachus says no: he left no one to watch over his possessions, and must go home (15.86–91; see [[possessions]]). The verses of the offer were obelized by Aristarchus (15.78–85; see the translation's note on 15.78–85).
