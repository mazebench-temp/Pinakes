---
summary: Four prophecies and readings in the book: Athena's, that some of the suitors will die before they can kill Telemachus (15.31–32); Helen's reading of the eagle, “as I think it will be fulfilled”, that Odysseus will come home and take vengeance, or is already at home (15.172–178); Polypheides', who prophesied for all mortals at Hyperesia (15.252–255); and Theoclymenus' reading of the falcon, oblique here and plain in Book 17 (15.531–534). Telemachus answers the last with “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled” (15.536).
# Unnamed: Athena's prophecy (15.31-32); Helen's (15.172-178); Polypheides' prophesying (15.252-255);
# Theoclymenus' reading (15.531-534).
refs: [15.31-32, 15.172-178, 15.252-255, 15.531-534]
---

**Athena's word.** “But I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (15.31–32 = 13.427–428; see [[ambush]]). The goddess told Odysseus the same on Ithaca, and the poem will fulfill it in Book 22.

**Helen's reading.** When Menelaus hesitates over the eagle and the goose, Helen speaks first, in the verses of Athena's prophecy as Mentes: “Hear me, and I will prophesy, as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled” (15.172–173 ≈ 1.200–201; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]], [[helen]] and [[divination]]). Her reading gives two alternatives: “so Odysseus, after much suffering and much wandering, / will come home and take vengeance; or else he is already / at home, and is planting evil for all the suitors” (15.176–178). The second is true as she speaks: Odysseus is already on Ithaca, in the swineherd's hut. Her last half-verse is Halitherses' thought in the assembly, that Odysseus is already near and plants slaughter for the suitors (2.163–166; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]), and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] will repeat it to Penelope (17.159). Telemachus answers with a prayer that Zeus may grant it (15.180–181; see [[prayer]]).

**The seers.** Melampus' descendant Polypheides, whom Apollo made the best of seers after Amphiaraus died, “moved away to Hyperesia, angry with his father, / and living there he prophesied for all mortals” (15.254–255; see [[polypheides]] and [[hyperesia]]). His son is the seer who reads the falcon on Ithaca (see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]).

**The falcon.** Telemachus has just named Eurymachus as the man most eager to marry his mother and left it to Zeus “whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (15.519–524); the falcon flies on the right as he speaks (15.525). Theoclymenus' reading answers the question indirectly: “There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (15.533–534; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]] and [[theoclymenus]]). καρτεροί is “powerful”, generally of physical strength, but compare κρατέω, “bear rule” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.534). Telemachus takes it as a prophecy and answers with the formula of fulfillment, “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled” (ἔπος τετελεσμένον εἴη, 15.536; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). The plain reading comes when the seer stands before Penelope: Odysseus is already in his native land, “and he is planting evil for all the suitors. / Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (17.157–161, a plain rendering).
