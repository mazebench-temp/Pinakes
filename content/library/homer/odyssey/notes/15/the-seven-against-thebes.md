# Unnamed: the death of Amphiaraus at Thebes (15.246-247). Ἀμφιάραον (244) and Θήβῃσι (247) are their own entries'
# lines.
refs: [15.246-247]
---

**The seer at Thebes.** The line of Melampus runs through [[antiphates-son-of-melampus|Antiphates]] and Oicles to “Amphiaraus, who rouses armies, / whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love; but he did not reach the threshold of old age, / but perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (15.244–247; see [[amphiaraus]], [[oicles]] and [[thebes]]). λαοσσόος, “who rouses armies”, is an epithet that Homer otherwise gives only to gods (see [[who-rouses-armies]]). The war of the Seven is in these verses, though the poem does not name it, and the Iliad, whose heroes are the sons of the men who fought it, never names Amphiaraus: he “occurs in the Odyssey (15.244, 253), but not in the Iliad” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix III §10; see the general article). His death is told again as a date: Apollo made Polypheides the best of seers “after Amphiaraus died” (15.252–253; see [[polypheides]]).

**The threshold of old age.** The “threshold” of old age is not here its entrance or beginning: “the meaning is that he never fairly set foot in old age”, as of Priam on the threshold of old age in the Iliad (Monro on 15.246, citing *Iliad* 22.60). “Some of the ancients (as Plutarch, Consol. ad Apoll. c. 17) saw in this verse a proof of the saying that those whom the gods love die young. But no such ‘pathetic fallacy’ was in the poet's mind” (Monro on 15.246; see the translation's note on 15.225–255). The same book speaks of Laertes, whom Odysseus left “on the threshold of old age” when he went to Troy (15.348; see [[laertes]]).

**The gifts to a woman.** γυναίων εἵνεκα δώρων, “for the sake of gifts to a woman”, is said only once more in Homer, of the Ceteians who died around Eurypylus (Monro on 15.247, citing 11.521; see [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]]). Monro uses this verse to explain that one: the scholia on the eleventh book, on the authority of Acusilaus, say that Priam sent a golden vine to Eurypylus' mother Astyoche and so persuaded her to send her son to the Trojans' aid, and “This explanation is borne out by Od. 15. 247, where the same thing is said of Amphiaraus”: “he was forced to take part in the war of Thebes, in which he fell, because of the necklace given to his wife Eriphyle” (Monro, Appendix III §6). The woman is not named here; the eleventh book names her as the one “who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (11.326–327; see [[eriphyle]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).

**The Thebaid.** Monro sets the Odyssey's Amphiaraus beside the lost epic of the war, the *Thebaid*, “also known as the ‘expedition of Amphiaraus’”, in which he was the leading figure: he “stood to the Argive king Adrastus somewhat as Achilles to Agamemnon”, and like Achilles he fought in a cause not his own, “and with full consciousness of impending fate”; but he was a hero of a new type, “one in which valour was united with prophetic wisdom”, and his death “is connected with the foundation of an oracle”, a kind of local oracle unknown in the Iliad and rare in the Odyssey (Monro, Appendix III §10; see [[adrastus]]).
