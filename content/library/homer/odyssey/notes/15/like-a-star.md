summary: At Sparta Helen takes from her chests the robe that she made herself, the most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest, “and it shone like a star; and it lay beneath all the others” (15.106–108), and gives it to Telemachus for his bride (15.123–130).

**Hecuba's verses.** ἀστὴρ δʼ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, “and it shone like a star”, is the half-verse of the robe that Hecuba chose for Athena, and the verse it begins and the one before it are the Iliad's unchanged (15.107–108 = *Iliad* 6.294–295). The verses before them are made over for Helen. In the Iliad the queen goes down into the fragrant chamber “where her robes were, richly embroidered, the work of women / of the Sidonians”, whom Alexander brought from Sidon on the voyage on which he brought back Helen, and of these she takes one “as a gift to Athena” (*Iliad* 6.288–293; see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[sidonians]]). At Sparta it is Menelaus who goes down into the fragrant chamber (15.99, as Priam at *Iliad* 24.191), and Helen who stands beside the chests “where her robes were, richly embroidered, which she had made herself” (οὓς κάμεν αὐτή for ἔργα γυναικῶν, 15.104–105); she takes one and carries it, “Helen, brilliant among women” (15.106, for Ἑκάβη φέρε δῶρον Ἀθήνῃ; see [[helen]], [[robe]] and [[helens-web]]). The robe of the Iliad came from Sidon in the ship that carried Helen to Troy; the robe of the Odyssey is her own work.

**A gift received.** The finest robe of each store “lay beneath all the others”, and each is given away. Hecuba's is laid on the knees of Athena by the priestess Theano, with a prayer that the goddess refuses: “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (*Iliad* 6.302–311). Helen's goes to a guest: “I too give you this gift, dear child, / a remembrance of Helen's hands, for the time of your lovely marriage, / for your wife to wear; but until then let it lie with your dear mother / in the hall” (15.125–128), “and he received it with joy” (15.130; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[telemachus]]). The star of the comparison is the robe's beauty, as it was in the Iliad; what changes is the giver and the end of the gift. The half-verse has a third use, with its δέ moved forward, for the helmet that Hephaestus made for Achilles, “and like a star it shone” (ἣ δʼ ἀστὴρ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, *Iliad* 19.381; see [[helmet]]).
