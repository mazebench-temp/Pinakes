# "κακῷ ἐσθλὸν ἔθηκε" (15.488), only here; Ζεύς begins 15.489.

{{quote:2a7105c4-c7ab-5e90-aac6-55e58e3fe665}}

**The answer.** Eumaeus has ended his story with his arrival on Ithaca, “So it was that I came to see this land with my eyes” (15.484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Odysseus answers first with the swineherd's own words of the night before, when the stranger's tale had moved him: “truly you have stirred my heart greatly / by telling all these things” (14.361–362; 15.486–487). Then he names the mixture in the swineherd's life. καί goes with ἐσθλόν, and κακῷ is placed between them to bring the contrasted words together, κακῷ ἐσθλόν, “good too beside the evil” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.488; see the translation's note on 15.488); in 15.487 ἄλγεα belongs properly to the clause before, “the sorrows you have suffered” (Monro on 15.487). The good is the house he came to, bought by Laertes: the house “of a man / who is kind, who gives you food and drink / with care, and you live a good life” (15.489–491; see [[laertes]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[feast]]).

**The two lots.** The thought is that of the two jars on the floor of Zeus, as Achilles tells it to Priam: the man to whom Zeus gives a mixture meets now with evil and now with good, while the man to whom he gives only from the grievous is made a thing of scorn, “and evil, ravening hunger drives him over the shining earth, / and he wanders honored neither by gods nor by mortals” (*Iliad* 24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). Odysseus gives the swineherd the first lot and takes the second for himself: “but I / have come here after wandering through many cities of mortals” (15.491–492; see [[nothing-worse-than-wandering]]). The man who claims the lot of the wanderer is the master of the house he praises.
