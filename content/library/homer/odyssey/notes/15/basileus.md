summary: Kings of the book: Phaedimus, “the king of the Sidonians”, who gave Menelaus the bowl (15.118); Ctesius, Eumaeus' father, who “was king” over both cities of Syrie (15.413); and the house of Odysseus, than which, the seer says, there is no “other race more kingly” in Ithaca (15.533).
# ἐμβασίλευε (15.413) and βασιλεύτερον (15.533), each only here in the Odyssey.
grc: [ἐμβασίλευε, βασιλεύτερον]
en: [more kingly]
---

**Kings abroad.** Menelaus' bowl was a king's gift: “The hero Phaedimus gave it to me, / the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (Σιδονίων βασιλεύς, 15.117–119 = 4.617–619; see [[phaedimus]], [[sidonians]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The swineherd too is a king's son: on Syrie “There are two cities, and everything is divided between them; / and over both of them my father was king, / Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals” (ἐμβασίλευε, 15.412–414; see [[ctesius]], [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]], [[syrie]] and [[like-the-immortals]]). The slave who keeps Odysseus' swine was born to the rank of the man he serves.

**The kingly race.** Telemachus has named Eurymachus to the seer as the man whom “the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, the best of the suitors and the most eager “to marry my mother and to hold the prize of honor of Odysseus” (15.519–522; see [[eurymachus]] and [[geras]]). The falcon answers him, and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] reads it: “There is no other race more kingly than yours / in the land of Ithaca, but you are always powerful” (γένος βασιλεύτερον … ὑμεῖς καρτεροὶ αἰεί, 15.533–534; see [[genee]] and [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). καρτεροί, “powerful”, is generally used of physical strength, but compare the common use of κρατέω in the sense “bear rule” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.534). Telemachus had spoken as though Eurymachus were to be the next lord of Ithaca; the seer declares that the line of Odysseus will remain the most kingly, and the resort to Eurymachus is given up (Monro on 15.519). The comparative is the Iliad's word for the rank that claims obedience: Agamemnon would have Achilles submit, “inasmuch as I am more kingly” (*Iliad* 9.160), and Achilles bids him find “one who befits him and who is more kingly” (*Iliad* 9.392).
