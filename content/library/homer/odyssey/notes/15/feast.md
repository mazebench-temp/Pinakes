summary: Menelaus will not let his guest go without a meal, for to dine before the road is an honor and a benefit (15.75–79); the meal at Sparta is served in the Odyssey's verses, with Eteoneus dividing the meat and Megapenthes pouring the wine (15.93–143). In the hut the two men have supper and then sit up drinking and feasting through the night (15.301–303, 15.390–398); Ctesius' banqueters leave their cups in the porch (15.466–467); and Telemachus promises his crew a feast as the reward of the voyage (15.500–507).

**A meal before the road.** Menelaus grants Telemachus' departure and keeps him for a meal: “I will tell the women / to make ready a meal in the halls from the plenty within. / It brings both, glory and splendor and a blessing too, / to dine before going over the wide, boundless earth” (15.76–79; see [[menelaus]] and [[what-is-due-is-better]]). ἀμφότερον is adverbial: a feast is both an honor, κῦδος καὶ ἀγλαΐη, and a benefit, ὄνειαρ (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 15.78); Aristarchus obelized 15.78–85 (Monro's critical note). The order is given at once (15.93–94), and Eteoneus rises from his bed to kindle a fire and roast the meat (15.95–98; see [[eteoneus]]).

**At Sparta.** The meal is served in the verses with which guests are fed in the first book: water for the hands from a golden pitcher over a silver basin, a polished table, bread and many dainties from the housekeeper (15.135–139 = 1.136–140; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). Where the first book has a carver with platters, here the household serves: “and beside them the son of Boethous divided the meat and served out the portions; / and the son of glorious Menelaus poured the wine” (15.140–141; see [[cupbearing]] and [[megapenthes]]). δαίομαι, “divide”, is the verb from which the feast has its name (see the entry). The meal ends in the verses of the Odyssey's meals, “And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them. / But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (15.142–143; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).

**In the hut.** “And the two of them in the hut, Odysseus and the brilliant swineherd, / were having supper; and the other men were having supper beside them” (15.301–302), and the meal ends in the same formula (15.303). The stranger offers to serve the suitors, since no mortal could rival him at carving and roasting meat and pouring wine, such services as the lesser do for their betters (15.321–324; see [[hermes]]); Eumaeus answers that the suitors' servants are young men and their tables “are laden with bread and meat and wine” (15.330–334; see [[the-suitors]]). Then he keeps his guest up with wine and talk: “But let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut, / take delight in each other's wretched sorrows” (15.398–399; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). At the end Odysseus counts the swineherd fortunate in a master who gives him food and drink with care (15.489–491; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]).

**On Syrie and on the shore.** In Eumaeus' story the nurse finds the cups and the tables of the banqueters in the porch, where the serving-maids had carried them after the feast, while the banqueters, the king's counselors, have gone out to the session (15.466–468; Monro on 15.466, 15.467; see [[assembly]], [[syrie]], [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]). At dawn Telemachus' crew land, “made ready their meal and mixed the sparkling wine” (15.500), and he promises them for the next morning “a good feast of meat and of wine sweet to drink” as their reward for the voyage (ὁδοιπόριον, 15.506–507; see the translation's table of key words).
