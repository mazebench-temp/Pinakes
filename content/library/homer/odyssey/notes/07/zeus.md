summary: Zeus does not act in the book; he is named in rite, in story and in prayer. Echeneus bids Alcinous have wine mixed for a libation “to Zeus who delights in thunder ... who attends on revered suppliants”, and Alcinous gives the order in the same words. In his story to Arete Odysseus tells how Zeus shattered his ship with a thunderbolt, and how Calypso let him go “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned”. Alcinous begins his offer with “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo”, and the evening ends with Odysseus' prayer to father Zeus that Alcinous may accomplish all that he has said.

**The god of suppliants.** When Odysseus sits in the ashes of the hearth and no one speaks, the old Echeneus tells Alcinous to seat the stranger on a chair, “and bid the heralds / mix the wine, so that to Zeus who delights in thunder / we may pour libations too, who attends on revered suppliants” (7.163–165; see [[echeneus]], [[supplication]] and [[delighting-in-thunder]]). Alcinous raises Odysseus and seats him, and gives the herald the order in the same words, “Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve out wine / to all throughout the hall, so that to Zeus who delights in thunder / we may pour libations too, who attends on revered suppliants” (7.179–181; see [[pontonous]] and [[libation]]). ὀπηδεῖ, “attends on”, makes the god the suppliant's companion, who goes where he goes (see the translation's key-word row on ἱκέτης αἰδοῖος). In his tale Odysseus warns the Cyclops in the same terms: Zeus avenges suppliants and strangers, the god of strangers, ὃς ξείνοισιν ἅμʼ αἰδοίοισιν ὀπηδεῖ, who attends on revered strangers (9.270–271). Bekker put that second verse at the foot of the page, and Köchly thought it weak after the first and taken from this one (Merry's critical note on 9.271). On Ithaca, thinking that the Phaeacians have left him in a strange land, Odysseus prays that Zeus of suppliants, ἱκετήσιος, may punish them (13.209–214; see [[xenia]] and [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). Nausicaa had told her handmaids that strangers and beggars are all from Zeus, and Merry, on her words, compares this verse (on 6.207; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).

**The thunderbolt.** In his story to Arete Odysseus tells how he came to Calypso: “a god brought to her hearth, / alone, when my swift ship, with a gleaming thunderbolt, / Zeus struck and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (7.248–250; see [[thunderbolt]], [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[wine-dark]]). Calypso had told it to Hermes in the same verses (5.130–132), and they are the words of Zeus' promise to Helios, to strike the ship of the companions who had eaten his cattle (12.385–388), which he carries out with thunder and the bolt (12.415–419; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). To Arete Odysseus gives no reason for the god's stroke. A variant ἐλάσας is reported for ἔλσας, perhaps Zenodotus' reading, and two readings for the verb, ἐκέασσε and ἐκέδασσε (Merry's critical note on 7.250; see the translation's note on 7.250).

**A message from Zeus.** He does not know why Calypso let him go in the eighth year: “then she urged me and bade me return, / at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (7.262–263; see [[calypso]]). The poem has shown the message: Zeus sent Hermes to her (5.28–42, 5.97–115; see [[hermes]]), and she told Odysseus nothing of it, only that she would send him away with a ready heart (5.160–161). ἀγγελίη is a “message” here, as when Idaeus delivers the Trojans' message in the Iliad (*Iliad* 7.416; see the translation's key-word row).

**Father Zeus.** Alcinous begins his offer of his daughter with the verse of wishes, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (7.311; see [[if-only-father-zeus]], [[athena]] and [[apollo]]), and ends it with a prayer that no Phaeacian shall keep the stranger against his will: “may that never be the pleasure of father Zeus” (7.316; see [[father-zeus]]). Merry: “I pray this may not be the will of Zeus”, that any of the Phaeacians should detain him (on 7.316). φίλον, “pleasure”, is a god's will, as in the Iliad's “Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of Zeus, supreme in might” (*Iliad* 2.116; see the translation's key-word row).
