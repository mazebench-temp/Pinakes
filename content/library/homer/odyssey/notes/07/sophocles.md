---
summary: Merry and the scholia he quotes bring Sophocles to four places of this book: the swift ships of the Ajax, after the two epithets of the Phaeacian ships; the leaves of a tall poplar stirred by a breeze, in the Aegeus, for the women at the looms; Oedipus at the seats of the Eumenides, for the stranger who belongs to the one he first approached; and the vine of Euboea in the Thyestes that goes through every stage of the vintage in a day, for the garden where every stage goes on at once.
---

- **Swift ships.** Of the two epithets in “Trusting in the speed of their swift ships” (7.34), θοός is the conventional one and ὠκύς the special, and Merry finds that Sophocles imitates the pairing in θοῶν ὠκυάλων νεῶν (*Ajax* 710, as Merry cites it; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.34; see the translation's note on 7.34): [[the-travelers-thought]] and [[swift-ships]].
- **The poplar leaves.** The scholia explain the women at the looms, “like the leaves of a tall poplar” (7.106), either by their number or by the quickness of their hands as they turn the weft, as poplar leaves turn against one another and move at any chance breeze, “as Sophocles says in the *Aegeus*”: “for as among the leaves of a tall poplar, if nothing else, some breeze stirs its crown and lifts its plume” (*Aegeus* fr. 24 Dindorf, quoted in the scholia, in Merry on 7.106; a plain rendering). Merry takes the movement for the point of the likeness: [[like-the-leaves-of-a-poplar]].
- **The first to be approached.** A stranger, Merry says, was held to be above all the suppliant of the one with whom he first had dealings; he cites Oedipus' words to the Eumenides, at whose seats he first bent his knee in their land (*Oedipus at Colonus* 85, as Merry quotes it), with Alcinous' complaint that it was to his daughter first that the stranger came as a suppliant (7.301), and Arete's “he is my guest” in Book 11 (11.338, a plain rendering; Merry on 7.53): [[supplication]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]].
- **The vine of a day.** In the garden and the vineyard of Alcinous, Merry says, the marvel is that every stage of growth and ripeness goes on at once (7.117–126), and he quotes a fragment of the *Thyestes* on a vine of Euboea that passes through all of them in one day: at dawn the ground is covered with its shoots in fair blossom, at midday the unripe grape swells and the cluster bends and darkens, and in the evening the whole crop is cut and the wine mixed (fr. 239 Dindorf, as Merry quotes it; a plain rendering; Merry on 7.126): [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[euboea]].
