# αἰδοίοισιν (7.165, 7.181; again 9.271, of strangers, and 15.373, of guests). αἰδοίη (7.175) is in the Book 1 note.
grc: [αἰδοίοισιν]
---

αἰδοῖος is the one to whom αἰδώς is owed. The suppliant is such a one, in Odysseus' plea to the river god, “Even to the immortal gods he is to be respected” (5.447; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]), and in Lycaon's plea to Achilles, “I am as good as a revered suppliant to you” (ἱκέταο … αἰδοίοιο, *Iliad* 21.74–75). In the Phaeacian hall the rule becomes a libation, poured to Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.163–165, 7.179–181; see [[supplication]] and [[zeus]]), and Alcinous will say at the farewell feast that all of it is done “for the sake of the revered stranger” (ξείνοιο … αἰδοίοιο, 8.544, a plain rendering; see [[xenia]]). The housekeeper of the house is αἰδοίη too, in the formula of the meal that she sets before the guest (7.175 = 1.139; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[handmaids]]). The adjective can also look the other way: when the beggar will not come to her at once, Penelope says κακὸς δʼ αἰδοῖος ἀλήτης, “it will never do for a mendicant to be shy”, in Merry's rendering (17.578; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.310; see [[a-bold-man-is-better]]). Odysseus' own “shame” in this book is another word: he would not follow Nausicaa into the town δείσας αἰσχυνόμενός τε, “out of fear and shame” (7.305), the verb of αἶσχος (see [[aischros]] and [[quick-to-anger]]).
