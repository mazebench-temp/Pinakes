---
summary: Plutarch, as Nitzsch quotes him, took the oil that drips from the close-woven linen of Alcinous' house for a sign of the fineness of the weaving; and his Themistocles, as Merry cites him, throws himself down at a king's hearth as a suppliant.
---

- **The close-woven linen.** Homer, Plutarch writes, showed the exactness and fineness of the weave when he called the linen close-woven, since the oil does not stay on it but runs off and slips away, the closeness not letting it through (*On the Oracles of the Pythia* 4, as Nitzsch quotes him; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.107). The verse is “and from the close-woven linen the liquid olive oil drips off” (7.107; see the translation's note on 7.107): [[weaving]] and [[athenaeus]].
- **At the king's hearth.** Themistocles in exile throws himself down at the hearth of Admetus (Plutarch, *Life of Themistocles*, as Merry cites it), one of the passages from which Merry concludes that the ἑστία of later writers implies far more than Homer's ἐσχάρη, where Odysseus sits in the ashes (7.153; Merry on 7.153): [[hearth]], [[supplication]] and [[apollonius]].
