summary: The queen at the center of the book. Athena tells Odysseus to find her first in the hall, and tells her name, her lineage and the honor in which her husband, her children and her people hold her (7.48–77); wrapped in the mist, he goes through the hall and clasps her knees (7.139–152). After the feast she knows the mantle and the tunic she made and asks who he is and who gave him the clothes, and he tells her his story (7.233–297); she has a bed made for him in the colonnade, and lies down beside the king (7.335–347).

**Find the queen first.** At the door of the palace Athena bids Odysseus go in without fear, and adds: “First you will find the lady in the halls; / Arete is her name, a name with a meaning” (7.53–54; see [[athena]]). κιχήσεαι is a simple future, “you will find”, not “approach” nor “make suit to”, and πρῶτα goes with δέσποιναν, as πρῶτα goes with σε when Odysseus greets Athena, in the likeness of a young shepherd, on Ithaca (13.228): of all who are in the hall, his eye will rest first on the queen (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.53). So read, Athena's words agree with Nausicaa's, who told him to pass her father by and clasp her mother's knees (6.303–315), with the kings he will find at their feast (7.49), and with what he does: he finds the leaders at their libation, and goes through the hall until he comes to Arete and the king (7.136–141). A stranger was the suppliant, with all the rights the word implies, of the person with whom he first established relations, and so Arete can say of him later, “he is my guest” (11.338, a plain rendering; Merry on 7.53). δέσποινα, “the lady”, is not found in the Iliad, since the relation it names is a domestic one (Merry on 7.53).

**Her name and her descent.** In Ἀρήτη δʼ ὄνομʼ ἐστὶν ἐπώνυμον, ὄνομα is the subject, “Arete is her name”, as Οὖτις ἐμοί γʼ ὄνομα, “Nobody is my name” (9.366, a plain rendering; Merry on 7.54); on ἐπώνυμον, “a name with a meaning”, see the entry and the translation's note on 7.54–55. The genealogy that follows explains why she “comes of the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55): Poseidon begot Nausithous on Periboea, Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous, and when Apollo struck down Rhexenor, newly married, “leaving one only child, a daughter, / Arete”, Alcinous made her his wife (7.56–66; see [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[nausithous]], [[rhexenor]] and [[alcinous]]).

**Her honor.**

{{quote:63b01e03-01b2-5c76-a580-b5691db2b83c}}

περὶ κῆρι, “with all their hearts”, is said as at 5.36 (Merry on 7.69). Nauck called τετίμηταί τε καὶ ἔστιν corrupt, though the manuscripts give no variant (see the translation's note on 7.69); Merry suggests that περί is to be understood again with ἔστιν, “is honoured above all, as she is [in worth] above all”, as Zeus says of Odysseus that he “is first among mortals in mind, and first in sacrifices” (1.66), and that the construction of τετίμηται is then resumed in ἔκ τε φίλων παίδων (on 7.69). The people “look on her as a god” (7.71), as they listened to her husband “as to a god” (7.11), and δειδέχαται, a perfect, is “welcome with loyal speeches” (Merry on 7.72; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). “For she herself is in no way lacking in good sense” (7.73): καὶ αὐτή, “of herself”, that is, she does not shine only with light reflected from the king (Merry on 7.73; see [[noos]]). In the next verse the text has the feminine ᾗσι, “for the women she favors, and for men as well, she settles quarrels” (7.74), the reading Eustathius calls the more graceful; Merry prints οἷσιν with the scholia, “for those to whom she shows favour, be they even men, she settles disputes”, rejects the rendering “gives wise counsel”, and takes τε as explanatory, as in 7.129 (on 7.74; see the translation's note on 7.74). Athena ends with the promise that Nausicaa had made: “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77), the verses that stand doubtfully in Book 6 (6.313–315), where modern editors reject them and place them here (see the translation's note on 6.313–315 and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**At her knees.** Odysseus goes through the hall in the mist that Athena poured around him, “until he came to Arete and King Alcinous. / And Odysseus threw his arms around the knees of Arete, / and then the god-sent mist flowed back from the man himself” (7.141–143; see [[supplication]], [[gounata]] and [[thesphaton]]). αὐτοῖο is “the hero himself”, now visible (Merry on 7.143), and the hall falls silent and marvels, naturally, a scholium says, since they had not seen him come (7.144–145; Merry on 7.144). He names her by her father, and her husband with her knees: “Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor, / to your husband and to your knees I have come, after toiling much, / and to these banqueters” (7.146–148; see [[posis]]). He prays that the gods may grant them prosperity and that each may hand on to his children his possessions and the prize the people gave him, the royal domain (7.148–150; Merry on 7.150; see [[geras]]); “But for me, speed my escort, so that I may reach my native land / the sooner” (7.151–152; see [[pompe]]), θᾶσσον, “the sooner the better” (Merry on 7.152). Then he sits down in the ashes of the hearth (7.153–154; see [[hearth]]). The queen does not answer him. The old Echeneus breaks the silence, and the king raises him (7.155–171; see [[echeneus]] and [[alcinous]]).

**Her question.** When the guests have gone home, Odysseus is left in the hall, “and beside him Arete and godlike Alcinous / were sitting; and the handmaids cleared away the gear of the feast” (7.231–232; see [[handmaids]]). Then she speaks:

{{quote:0fd7683e-3f82-550f-af38-5e3993093fe8}}

The clothes were her own work, given to him at the river from the washing (6.214, 6.228; see [[clothing]], [[cloak]], [[tunic]] and [[weaving]]), and her question goes straight to them. αὐτή gives her words their force: not “this will be the first thing I shall ask thee”, but “I will begin (the conversation) by asking thee this”, the hostess taking the lead in the conversation; Penelope speaks the same verse when she is left alone with the disguised Odysseus (19.104; Merry on 7.237). φῆς is “did you not say”, looking back to his plea for an escort home (7.152; Merry on 7.239). On the second line, see [[who-are-you-among-men]].

**His answer to her.** He answers the queen: “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows, since the heavenly gods have given me many” (7.241–242; see [[basileus]]), διηνεκέως being “at full length” (Merry on 7.241). He tells her of Ogygia and Calypso, of the raft and the storm and the swim, and last of her daughter: “And I noticed your daughter's handmaids on the shore / playing, and she herself was among them, like the goddesses” (7.290–291), who bathed him in the river “and gave me these clothes” (7.296; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]], [[calypso]] and [[nausicaa]]). So he answers her second question last. He ends, “This is the truth I have told you, grieved though I am” (7.297), and it is the king who answers him (7.298–301).

**The bed in the colonnade.** When the talk is over, “white-armed Arete called to her handmaids / to set beds beneath the colonnade, and throw fine purple / blankets on them, and spread rugs over them, / and put woolly cloaks on top for them to wear” (7.335–338). These are the verses with which Helen has beds made for Telemachus and Peisistratus at Sparta and Achilles for Priam (7.336–339 = 4.297–300, *Iliad* 24.644–647; see [[beds-beneath-the-colonnade]], [[bed]] and [[blankets]]). The women go out with torches, make the bed, and bid him rise and go to it (7.339–342; see [[handmaids]]).

**Beside the king.** “And Alcinous lay down in the inner part of the high house, / and beside him the lady his wife shared his bed and couch” (7.346–347; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]), after the verse of Nestor's wife at Pylos (3.403). πορσύνω is used always of the wife who shares the bed: no one but the wife had free access to her husband's chamber, so that she actually prepared his bed for the night, and from this the word passed into the sense of sharing it, as here (Merry on 3.403; on Aristarchus' πόρσαινε, see the translation's note on 7.347). δέσποινα, the word with which Athena first named her (7.53), is the book's last word for her.

**Her epithets.** λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (7.233, 7.335; see [[white-armed]]); βασίλεια, “queen”, in Odysseus' address (7.241); δέσποινα, “the lady” (7.53, 7.347). See the entry.
