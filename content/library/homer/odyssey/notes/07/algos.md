summary: Odysseus tells Alcinous that he would liken himself “in sorrows” (ἐν ἄλγεσιν) to the men who bear the most misery (7.211–214), and begins his story to Arete, “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows, since the heavenly gods have given me many” (κήδεʼ, 7.241–242).
# ἄλγεσιν (7.212). κήδεʼ (7.242) is in the Book 5 note.
grc: [ἄλγεσιν]
---

**The most misery.** The king has wondered whether the stranger is a god, and the stranger's answer is his sorrows: “Whoever you know who bear the most misery / among men, to them I might liken myself in sorrows. / And I could tell of still more evils, / all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods” (7.211–214; see [[immortality]]). ὀχέοντας ὀιζύν is “bearing misery”, and Merry gives the sense in Latin, whomever of men you know to bear hardship beyond the rest, to them I could liken myself; ξύμπαντα is “from first to last” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.211, 7.214). Then he leaves the sorrows for the belly (7.215–221; see [[gaster]] and [[penthos]]).

**The story begun.** To the queen he begins in the same key, ἀργαλέον, βασίλεια, διηνεκέως ἀγορεῦσαι / κήδεʼ, “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows” (7.241–242; see [[basileus]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). διηνεκέως is “at full length”, from the stem of ἤνεγκα, of what is carried right through (Merry on 7.241; see the translation's table of key words). Merry compares the opening of Aeneas' story to Dido, *Infandum, regina, iubes renovare dolorem*, “Unspeakable, queen, is the grief you bid me renew” (Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.3; Merry on 7.241). The sorrows are the gods' gift (δόσαν θεοὶ Οὐρανίωνες, 7.242; see [[heavenly-gods]]), and the story keeps to the words of misery: he was to keep company “with misery / much misery, which Poseidon the earth-shaker roused against me” (ὀιζυῖ / πολλῇ, 7.270–271; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). In the plea at the queen's knees the same sufferings are πήματα, “woes” (7.152), and the king's escort will keep him from any “evil and … bane” (κακὸν καὶ πῆμα) on the way (7.195; see [[pompe]]).
