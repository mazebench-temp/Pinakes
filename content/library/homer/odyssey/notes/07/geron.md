**The old man.** The hall waits in silence for the king, and it is an old man who speaks: γέρων ἥρως Ἐχένηος, ὃς δὴ Φαιήκων ἀνδρῶν προγενέστερος ἦεν (7.155–156; see [[echeneus]] and [[heros]]). προγενέστερος is not a comparison with the others but a qualifying comparative, “like our use of elderly”, with Φαιήκων a partitive genitive after ὅς, “one of the older Phaeacian men”; Bekker reads προγενέστατος, “the oldest” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.156, and his critical note; see the translation's note on 7.156). His authority is the old man's knowledge, παλαιά τε πολλά τε εἰδώς, “knowing many things of old” (7.157), and he uses it to remind the king of the custom of the house (7.159–166; see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]). Old men speak with this authority in Ithaca too: the hero Aegyptius, bent with age, opens the assembly, and the old hero Halitherses, who surpassed the men of his age in knowing birds, reads the omen (2.15–16, 2.157–159).

**The elders.** The next day's entertainment will need more of them: ἠῶθεν δὲ γέροντας ἐπὶ πλέονας καλέσαντες, “and at dawn we will call more of the elders” (7.189), ἐπί going with the participle, “bidding them gather to the meeting” (Merry on 7.189; see [[elders]] and the translation's note on 7.189).

**Old age in the garden.** In the orchard of Alcinous the fruit never fails, and the verb of old age is said of the trees' fruit, ὄγχνη ἐπʼ ὄγχνῃ γηράσκει, “Pear upon pear grows old” (7.120; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]).
