summary: The book shows the Phaeacians at home. Athena warns that they do not welcome strangers but trust in their ships, swift as a wing or a thought (7.32–36); their leaders feast and pour libations in the house of Alcinous (7.98–99, 7.136–138), their women are as skilled at the loom as the men at sailing (7.108–111), the gods come among them openly (7.199–206), and their young men will row Odysseus home while he sleeps (7.317–328).

**Strangers among them.** Athena hides Odysseus in a mist as he sets out for the city, “lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him, / should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (7.16–17; see [[athena]], [[achlys]] and [[great-hearted]]). Then, in the likeness of a girl carrying a water jar, she tells him to walk in silence and question no one:

{{quote:e35e9853-2b5b-500d-93ef-03f8d4f8a8b4}}

οὐ … μάλα … ἀνέχονται is a strong understatement, “they are not over fond of”, that is, not at all, and ἀνέχονται says more than that they do not entertain strangers: they cannot put up with them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.32; see [[xenia]]). Nitzsch found the warning at odds with Nausicaa's welcome (6.206–210), with Echeneus' rebuke (7.159–166) and with Alcinous' speech (7.186–206), and took it for the thoughts of the wary, much-traveled hero as he enters a strange city; Merry thinks this too subtle, and supposes that Athena speaks of the ordinary Phaeacians whom a stranger would meet in the street, who by Nausicaa's own showing could make themselves very unpleasant (on 7.32; 6.273–288; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). The reason she gives is their seafaring. Of the two epithets of the ships, θοός, “swift”, is the conventional one and ὠκύς the special one, “trusting in the speed of their swift ships” (Merry on 7.34; see the translation's note on 7.34 and [[ships]]); they cross “the great gulf” because the earth-shaker granted it to them (7.35; see [[poseidon]]), and in the eighth book Alcinous says it in the same words, that their ships cross the gulf of the sea most swiftly, wrapped in mist and cloud (λαῖτμα … ἐκπερόωσιν, 8.561–562, Greek only). For “swift as a wing or a thought”, see [[like-wings]] and [[the-travelers-thought]]; on the voyage home their ship runs so fast that not even the falcon, the swiftest of birds, could keep pace (13.86–87, Greek only).

**Famed for ships.** So Odysseus passes through their city unseen: “But the Phaeacians famed for ships did not notice him / as he went through the city among them” (7.39–40; see [[famed-for-ships]] and [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). ναυσικλυτοί is their epithet again in the eighth, thirteenth and sixteenth books (8.191, 8.369, 13.166, 16.227), and once the Phoenicians' (15.415). What he marvels at are the works of a seafaring people, the harbors and the balanced ships first of all (7.43–45; see [[harbor]] and [[balanced-ships]]). The women have their own craft to match: “As much as the Phaeacians are skilled beyond all men / in driving a swift ship on the sea, so are the women / skilled at the loom”, for Athena has given them skill in beautiful handiwork, and good wits (7.108–111; see [[weaving]] and [[athena]]).

**The leaders at the feast.** Athena tells him that he will find “the kings whom Zeus has nurtured / feasting at their feast” (7.49–50), and bids him not be afraid; Merry compares Tydeus, who found the many Cadmeians feasting in the house of Eteocles and was not afraid, though a stranger (*Iliad* 4.385–390; Merry on 7.49; see [[zeus-nurtured]] and [[kings]]). In the hall the chairs stand along the walls: “There the leaders of the Phaeacians used to sit, / drinking and eating; for they had unfailing store” (7.98–99). Odysseus finds “the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians / pouring libations from their cups to the keen-sighted slayer of Argus, / to whom they would pour the last libation, whenever they thought of bed” (7.136–138; see [[hermes]] and [[libation]]). When the mist falls from him at Arete's knees they fall silent and marvel (7.144–145), and they keep silent while he sits in the ashes, until “one of the older Phaeacian men”, Echeneus, speaks for him (7.154–166; see [[echeneus]] and [[supplication]]). Alcinous addresses them with the verse that opens his speeches to them in the next book as well, “Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (7.186; again 8.26, 8.97, 8.387, 8.536; see [[hegemon]]), sends them home to bed, and will call more of the elders in the morning (7.188–191; see [[elders]]); “they all approved” the stranger's answer, and went home, each to his own house (7.226–229).

**Near to the gods.** Alcinous wonders whether the stranger may be a god, and explains why the Phaeacians would know one: “For always, before now, the gods have appeared in plain sight / to us, whenever we offer famous hecatombs, / and they feast beside us, sitting where we sit” (7.201–203), and they do not hide themselves even from a lone wayfarer, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (7.205–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]], [[cyclopes]] and [[giants]]). ἐγγύθεν is generally taken as “near-related”, like ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (5.35, 19.279; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). Welcker took it of place, as though the Phaeacians lived in a land close to the gods, like the Hyperboreans, and Merry finds this in keeping with Nitzsch's remark that the peoples at the edges of the world are the gods' favorites: Poseidon visits the Ethiopians, “the farthest of men” (1.23; see [[ethiopians]]), Herodotus says that the extremities of the inhabited world somehow received the finest things (*Histories* 3.106.1; see [[herodotus]]), and Plato's ancients were better than we and dwelt nearer to the gods (*Philebus* 16c). Against this he sets Hesiod's making of the Giants and the Cyclopes children of Earth, and the same descent given to the Phaeacians by Acusilaus and Alcaeus, as he reports them (Merry on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205). The poem has already given them both kinds of nearness: their first king was Poseidon's son (7.56–62), and they live “far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men” (6.204–205; see [[nausithous]] and [[scheria]]).

**The rowers.** Their escort is the one gift that matters to the stranger (7.151–152, 7.191–196; see [[nostos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Alcinous tells him that against his will no one “of the Phaeacians” will keep him (7.315–316), and promises the voyage for the next day: “you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm, until you come / to your native land” (7.318–320). His measure of their speed is the farthest voyage they know, to Euboea and back on the same day, when they carried Rhadamanthys to visit Tityus (7.321–326; see [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]], [[euboea]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[tityus]]), and he ends with a boast: “you yourself will know in your mind how far the best / are my ships, and my young men at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.327–328). πηδῷ, “with the oar-blade”, may be connected with πηδᾶν, “leap”, because the blade seems to spring from the water at the end of the stroke (Merry on 7.328; see [[oars]]). Both promises are kept in the thirteenth book: the young men lean back and toss up the sea with the oar-blade, ἀνερρίπτουν ἅλα πηδῷ, in the words of the boast, and sleep falls on Odysseus' eyelids, unwaking, sweetest, most like to death (13.78–80, Greek only).
