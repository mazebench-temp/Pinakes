---
summary: Alcinous names the Cyclopes with the Giants as peoples to whom the gods are near, as they are to the Phaeacians (7.205–206).
---

Wondering whether the stranger may be a god, Alcinous says that the gods come among the Phaeacians openly, at their hecatombs and to a lone wayfarer, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (7.205–206; see [[phaeacians]], [[giants]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The Cyclopes were the Phaeacians' old neighbors in Hypereia, who plundered them until Nausithous led his people away (6.4–8; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]), and Polyphemus, the mightiest of them, is a son of Poseidon, like Nausithous, the first king of the Phaeacians (1.70–73, 7.56–62; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]], [[poseidon]] and [[nausithous]]). ἐγγύθεν, “near”, is generally taken of kinship, like ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (5.35); against Welcker's view that it means neighbors living near the gods, Merry sets Hesiod, who makes the Giants and the Cyclopes children of Earth (*Theogony* 139–146, 183–186; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.205; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 7.205). Yet the Cyclops whom Odysseus will describe to the Phaeacians in the ninth book says that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus or the blessed gods, since they are far stronger (9.275–276, Greek only).
