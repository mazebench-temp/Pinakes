---
summary: A fragment of Aristotle's Homeric Problems explains why Odysseus tells the Phaeacians that he refused Calypso's offer of immortality, which he does in this book, in his story to Arete. The Poetics and the Rhetoric quote nothing from the book, but they name the tales that follow it with the genitive of its later title, “the tale to Alcinous”.
---

- **Calypso's offer.** Why did Odysseus not accept immortality when Calypso offered it? Aristotle answers that Odysseus says these things to the Phaeacians to appear more dignified and to make them more eager to send him on, since it was to his advantage to be sent home quickly; and that he seems to say he refused the gift not because he was unpersuaded but because he did not believe her when she said it (*Homeric Problems* fr. 401 Gigon, from the Vienna scholia on 23.337). The words he explains are those of this book, where Odysseus tells Arete that Calypso “said / that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days; / but she never persuaded the heart in my breast” (7.256–258). The poet's summary of the tale to Penelope repeats them in the third person (23.335–337), and Calypso herself tells Hermes that she said it (5.135–136): [[immortality]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[calypso]].
- **The tale to Alcinous.** Aristotle names the long story of the books that follow with the contracted genitive of this book's heading, Ἀλκίνου: recognition through memory, as “in the tale told to Alcinous”, when Odysseus hears the lyre player and weeps (*Poetics* 1455a2–3), and “the tale to Alcinous, which is retold to Penelope in sixty lines” (*Rhetoric* 1417a13–15): [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]].
