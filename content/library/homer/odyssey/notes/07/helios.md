---
summary: The sun is the measure of the house of Alcinous, which gleams “as of the sun or of the moon” (7.84), and in his vineyard it dries the grapes on a warm spot of level ground (7.123–124). In Odysseus' tale to Arete it ends his sleep in the leaves: “And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go” (7.289).
---

**The gleam of the house.** Before the bronze threshold Odysseus stands and ponders, “For there was a gleam as of the sun or of the moon / throughout the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous” (7.84–85; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[moon]] and [[high-roofed]]). The first verse is the verse of the house of Menelaus at Sparta (4.45; see the Book 4 note), and W. W. Merry reports Nitzsch's remark that the palace of Menelaus rather surpasses that of Alcinous in magnificence (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.84).

**The sun in the vineyard.** In the vineyard of Alcinous one part, “a warm spot on level ground, / is dried by the sun, while other grapes they are gathering” (7.123–124; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[vine]]). Merry takes the grapes there to be left on the vines to dry in the sun for raisin wine, rather than cut and hung up (on 7.123).

**The sun declining.** Odysseus tells Arete that he slept among the leaves “all night long, and into the dawn and to midday. / And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go” (7.288–289; see [[hypnos]] and [[sweet-sleep]]). δείλετο, “was declining”, is Aristarchus' reading, as Eustathius reports; others read δύσετο, “set”, which Merry prints (see the translation's note on 7.289). The reading touches the day. If the sun set as he woke, sunset would come before the meeting with Nausicaa, the bath, the meal and the slow journey toward the town, at the end of which, in Book 6, the sun sets (6.321; see the Book 6 note). Merry reports Buttmann's defense of δείλετο, but finds δείλη as much tied to sunset in Homer as δύσετο, and grants only that the imperfect δείλετο allows a little more latitude than the aorist. He suggests that each of the three parts of the day, “a morning or an evening or a midday” (*Iliad* 21.111), was named from its characteristic moment and could be named so at any time within it, so that the whole of the afternoon may be called sunset; or that epic formulas are used with a certain carelessness once they have become formulas (on 7.289). The three parts of the day stand in Odysseus' two verses: the dawn, the midday and the declining sun (see [[eos]]).
