---
summary: Herodotus describes the temple of Erechtheus on the Acropolis, which Merry takes this book's house of Erechtheus to be; his Croesus asks a stranger at his hearth who he is and where he comes from; and his saying that the ends of the earth received the finest things is Merry's evidence for reading the Phaeacians' nearness to the gods as nearness in place.
---

- **The house of Erechtheus.** Athena leaves Scheria and enters “the well-built house of Erechtheus” (7.81). Herodotus describes on the Acropolis “a temple of Erechtheus, who is said to be earth-born”, with the olive and the sea water that, the Athenians say, Poseidon and Athena set there as witnesses when they disputed the land; the Persians burned the olive with the rest of the sanctuary, and on the next day a shoot a cubit long had sprung from its stump (*Histories* 8.55.1, a plain rendering). Merry takes the house of this verse for that temple, where Athena and Erechtheus were worshiped together, and thinks that Herodotus refers to the passage without naming it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.81): [[erechtheus]], [[athena-and-erechtheus]] and [[athens]].
- **A stranger at the hearth.** The Phrygian Adrastus, who has killed his brother unwillingly, comes to Croesus at Sardis; Croesus purifies him, and only then asks, “who are you, and from what part of Phrygia have you come to my hearth?”, ἐπίστιός μοι ἐγένεο (*Histories* 1.35.1–3, a plain rendering). Merry cites the word as Herodotus' equivalent of ἱκέτης, “suppliant”, in his note on Odysseus' seat at the hearth (on 7.153). Homer's form, ἐφέστιος, is in Odysseus' story to Arete: “But me, unhappy man, a god brought to her hearth” (7.248), Calypso's: [[hearth]] and [[supplication]].
- **The ends of the earth.** “Somehow the farthest parts of the inhabited world received the finest things” (αἱ δʼ ἐσχατιαί κως τῆς οἰκεομένης τὰ κάλλιστα ἔλαχον, *Histories* 3.106.1, a plain rendering). Merry quotes the sentence for the reading of ἐγγύθεν, “near” (7.205), that takes it of place and not of kin: Welcker seems to place the Phaeacians in a sort of heavenly country, like the Hyperboreans, which agrees with Nitzsch's remark that the distant nations at the edges of the world are the gods' favorites, as Poseidon visits the Ethiopians, “the farthest of men” (1.22–23; Merry on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205). Nausicaa calls her own people by the same words (6.205): [[phaeacians]] and [[ethiopians]].
