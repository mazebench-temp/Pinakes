---
summary: Athena tells Odysseus that Apollo of the silver bow struck down Rhexenor, Alcinous' brother, in his hall when he was newly married and had no son, so that his only daughter, Arete, became his brother's wife (7.63–66). Alcinous names Apollo with Zeus and Athena in his wish for a son-in-law like the stranger (7.311).
---

**Rhexenor.** In her account of the queen's descent Athena tells how the line passed to Arete: Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous, and “Him, when he had no son, Apollo, god of the silver bow, struck down, / newly married, in his hall, leaving one only child, a daughter, / Arete” (7.64–66; see [[rhexenor]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[alcinous]]). ἄκουρον is “without male issue”, and νυμφίον “before his bridal days were over”, as μίαν οἴην παῖδα λιπόντα, “leaving one only child”, explains; Nauck's ἄωρον, “untimely”, is needless, and Rhexenor must have been married long enough to have a daughter, unless Arete was born after his death (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.64; see the translation's note on 7.64–65). The death shapes the royal house: Rhexenor left no son, his only daughter became the wife of her father's brother, and Alcinous is “uncle as well as husband” (Merry on 7.54; see the translation's note on 7.54–55).

**The archer's death.** The verse gives the god his bow, ἀργυρότοξος, and the archer's verb, βάλε, “struck down”, and no reason. So Melanthius wishes that Apollo would strike Telemachus, αἲ γὰρ Τηλέμαχον βάλοι ἀργυρότοξος Ἀπόλλων (17.251), and Penelope wishes the same on Antinous (17.494). Apollo's arrows are the poems' ordinary account of a man's sudden death (see the general article): he kills Menelaus' helmsman at Sunium, and with Artemis the people of the island Syria when they grow old (3.279–281, 15.409–411). In anger he killed Eurytus, who challenged him with the bow, so that he did not come to old age in his halls (8.226–228); Rhexenor too dies “in his hall”.

**In Alcinous' wish.** When Alcinous offers the stranger his daughter, he begins, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, / being such a man as you are, and thinking as I do, / you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law” (7.311–313; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[if-only-father-zeus]]). The first verse opens wishes in both poems, among them Menelaus' at Sparta (4.341; *Iliad* 2.371, 4.288, 7.132, 16.97). Aristarchus doubted whether the six verses of the offer were Homer's (Merry's critical note on 7.311–316; see the translation's note on 7.311–316).
