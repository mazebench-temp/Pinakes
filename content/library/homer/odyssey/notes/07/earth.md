summary: The earth of Book 7 is first a land: the distant land Odysseus comes from, the city and land of the Phaeacians, and the native land that Athena gives him hope of and Alcinous promises to bring him to (7.25–26, 7.75–77, 7.193–196, 7.269, 7.276). It is also the earth on which mortals live, where no woman is honored as Arete is and the tribes of men are quick to anger (7.67, 7.307), and over which the fame of Alcinous would spread (7.332); and Earth is the mother of Tityus (7.324).
# Γαιήιον "of Earth" (7.324, only here in Homer), the adjective of a parent's name, as Ποιάντιον ἀγλαὸν υἱόν "the
# splendid son of Poias" (3.190); tityus claims it for the son, and it counts for the mother as a patronymic
# counts for the father.

**The land.** Odysseus, meeting Athena in the likeness of a girl, asks the way as a stranger: “For I have come here, a much-tried stranger, / far away, from a distant land; so I know no one / of the people who hold this city and land” (7.24–26; see [[distant-land]] and [[much-tried]]). The last verse is the one he spoke to Nausicaa (6.177), and the pair πόλις καὶ γαῖα, “city and land”, is the town and the country around it (see the Book 6 note). Athena gives him the hope that Nausicaa gave him, in the same words: if the queen thinks of him in friendship, “then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77, with “high-roofed” where 6.315 has “well-built”; see [[dear-native-land]], [[high-roofed]] and the translation's note on 6.313–315). Alcinous promises it: the stranger is to reach his native land under the Phaeacians' escort and suffer no harm “before he sets foot on his own land” (7.193–196; see [[pompe]] and [[nostos]]). In his tale the land of Scheria is what he saw from the raft, “the shadowy mountains / of your land” (7.268–269; see [[shadowy-mountains]]), and what the wind and the water brought him to (7.276–277).

**Mortals on the earth.** Alcinous made Arete his wife and honored her “as no other woman on earth is honored, / of all the women who now keep house under their husbands” (7.67–68; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Odysseus, taking on himself the blame for not coming to the house with Nausicaa, ends with a saying: “for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (7.307; see [[quick-to-anger]] and [[phylon]]). ἐπὶ χθονί, “on the earth”, is the place of mortals against the gods, as Odysseus tells Alcinous that he is not like the immortals “who hold the wide sky”, but like mortal men (7.209–210; see [[sky]]).

**The grain-giving earth.** At the end of the evening Odysseus prays that Alcinous may fulfill his promise: “Then his fame over the grain-giving earth / would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land” (7.332–333; see [[grain-giving]] and [[kleos]]). ἐπὶ … ἄρουραν is “would be spread throughout the world”, by the praise that Odysseus in his gratitude would give him (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.332).

**The son of Earth.** Alcinous tells how the Phaeacians once carried fair-haired Rhadamanthys “to visit Tityus, the son of Earth” (7.323–324; see [[tityus]] and [[rhadamanthus]]). Γαιήιον υἱόν makes the mother's name an adjective, as Ποιάντιον ἀγλαὸν υἱόν, “the splendid son of Poias”, names Philoctetes by his father (3.190; Merry on 7.324). When Odysseus sees Tityus punished in the underworld, he is Γαίης ἐρικυδέος υἱόν, the son of glorious Earth (11.576, a plain rendering).
