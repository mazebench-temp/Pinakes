summary: The night of the book is a night of sleep. Odysseus tells Arete how he slept among the leaves after his landing, “and a god poured boundless sleep over me”, until “sweet sleep let me go” (7.285–289); Alcinous sends the leaders home to lie down (7.188, 7.229) and promises that the stranger will be rowed home “overcome by sleep” (7.318); and the book ends with him asleep in the colonnade (7.342–345).

**The sleep on the shore, retold.** In his story to Arete Odysseus tells his landing again in his own words: “I … came out and slept among the bushes, and drew the leaves / about me; and a god poured boundless sleep over me. / There among the leaves, grieving at heart, / I slept all night long, and into the dawn and to midday. / And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go” (7.284–289; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]], [[trees]], [[helios]] and [[sweet-sleep]]). Book 5 told it as Athena's gift, the sleep she poured on his eyes to end his weariness (5.491–493), and Book 6 began with him asleep (6.1–2); here the giver is “a god”, and the sleep ἀπείρων, “boundless” (see [[boundless-sea]] and [[daimon]]). ἠφυσάμην, “drew”, is a word proper to liquids, said of leaves (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.286). Whether the sun was “declining” or “setting” when he woke depends on the reading, δείλετο, Aristarchus', or δύσετο, which Merry prints and discusses at length (see the translation's note on 7.289).

**Sleep in the escort.** The king promises a voyage in which the passenger will not keep watch: “then you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm” (7.318–319; see [[pompe]] and [[sea]]). In Book 13 Odysseus lies down on the deck and a sleep falls on him “most like to death”, and he does not wake until the ship has set him ashore on Ithaca (13.73–80, 13.116–119, plain renderings).

**Bedtime.** The Phaeacian evening has a fixed end: the leaders pour the last libation to Hermes “whenever they thought of bed” (7.137–138; see [[hermes]] and [[libation]]). On this night the stranger's coming delays it, and they go home to lie down only when the escort has been promised (7.188, 7.228–229), and the stranger is left with the king and queen until the handmaids make his bed in the colonnade and bid him, “Rise and go to bed, stranger; your bed has been made” (7.342; see [[bed]] and [[handmaids]]). “So they spoke, and it seemed welcome to him to lie down to sleep” (7.343), and the book ends with the verse that opened Book 6, “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (7.344 = 6.1; see [[much-enduring]]), while Alcinous lies down beside the queen in the inner part of the house (7.346–347; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).
