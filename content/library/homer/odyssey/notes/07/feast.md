summary: The book is an evening and a feast. Nausicaa's supper is made ready in her chamber (7.13); Odysseus finds the kings feasting in the house of Alcinous, at the hour of the last libation (7.49–50, 7.98–99, 7.136–138); the suppliant raised from the hearth is given his supper (7.166, 7.172–177); Alcinous sends the guests home and promises a feast for the stranger in the morning (7.188–191), and the handmaids clear away the gear of the feast (7.232).
# Forms first met here (they apply in the Odyssey only): δαίτην and δαινυμένους (7.50; δαινυμένους also 2.247, 10.452),
# δαιτυμόνεσσι (7.102; later 22.12), δαιτυμόνας (7.148) and δαισάμενοι (7.188; later 18.408).

**Supper.** The book passes in the evening and the night after the day at the river. In her chamber the old woman who reared Nausicaa lights her fire “and made ready her supper within” (7.13; see [[nausicaa]]). δόρπον is the evening meal (see the translation's note on 4.194); a scholium took the fire of 7.7 for a sign of winter, but W. W. Merry thinks the evening a reason enough for it, for light or warmth (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.7; see [[fire]] and the translation's note on 7.7). Echeneus bids that “the housekeeper give the stranger supper from the stores within” (7.166).

**The kings at their feast.** Athena tells Odysseus that he “will find the kings whom Zeus has nurtured / feasting at their feast” (7.49–50; see [[kings]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]); δαίτην δαινυμένους is a cognate accusative, like βουλὰς βουλεύειν (Merry on 7.50, 6.61). The leaders of the Phaeacians sit on the chairs along the walls “drinking and eating; for they had unfailing store”, and golden youths give light “by night throughout the house to the banqueters” (7.98–102; see [[throne]], [[torches]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). Odysseus finds them pouring their last libation before bed (7.136–138; see [[libation]] and [[hermes]]), and his first prayer at the queen's knees is for “these banqueters”, that the gods may grant them prosperity in their lives (7.148–150; see [[possessions]]).

**The guest's meal.** When Alcinous has raised him from the hearth, the stranger is served as every guest is: water for the hands, a polished table, bread and many dainties, “And much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate” (7.172–177; see [[basin-and-pitcher]], [[table]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). The wine is then mixed for the libation to Zeus (7.179–184; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[wine]]). When Alcinous wonders whether he is a god, Odysseus asks leave to eat his supper, “grieved though I am”, for nothing is more shameless than the belly (7.215–221; see [[gaster]]); the gods, Alcinous had said, feast beside the Phaeacians when they appear to them, “sitting where we sit” (7.201–203; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[hecatomb]]).

**Tomorrow's feast.** “Now that you have feasted, go home and lie down”, Alcinous tells the leaders; at dawn they will call more of the elders, entertain the stranger in the halls and offer sacrifices to the gods (7.188–191; see [[xenia]]). The feast takes place in Book 8. The leaders pour the libation and go home, and when Odysseus is left with the king and queen, “the handmaids cleared away the gear of the feast” (7.228–232; see [[handmaids]] and [[table]]).
