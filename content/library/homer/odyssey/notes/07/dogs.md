# κύντερον (7.216; later 11.427, 20.18), the comparative formed from κύων, first met here in the Odyssey, as the Iliad's
# κύντατον is claimed in the Iliad's Book 10 note. κύνες (7.91) is in the Book 2 note.
grc: [κύντερον]
en: [more shameless]

**Dogs of gold and silver.** “And on either side were golden and silver dogs, / which Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill / to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous” (7.91–93; see [[the-gold-and-silver-dogs]], [[hephaestus]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). They have the watchdog's task, to guard the house, and a god's life, “being immortal and ageless all their days” (7.94). The dogs of flesh in the poem so far are Telemachus' two swift dogs, which go with him to the assembly (2.11; see the Book 2 note).

**More doglike.** “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly” (7.216; see [[gaster]]): κύντερον, literally “more doglike”, is the comparative of κύων, the dog that stands for shamelessness (see the entry and [[shameless-dog]]). Zeus says it of Hera, “there is nothing more shameless than you” (*Iliad* 8.483); in later books the shade of Agamemnon says it of a wife who plots her husband's death, and Odysseus of what his heart endured on the day the Cyclops ate his companions (11.427, 20.18–20, plain renderings).
