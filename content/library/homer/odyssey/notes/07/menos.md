summary: μένος with a genitive names a being by its might: “the might of the two mules carried the girl to the city” (7.2), “the sacred might of Alcinous” heard the old man's advice (7.167), and “the might of Alcinous” spoke to the herald (7.178).

The book opens and goes on with a periphrasis. φέρεν μένος ἡμιόνοιιν, “the might of the two mules carried the girl” (7.2; see [[mules]] and [[nausicaa]]), puts the animals' strength in the place of the animals, as the Iliad says that sharp pains “came over the might of the son of Atreus” (*Iliad* 11.268, 11.272) and that Apollo covered the place where Hector's body lay with a dark cloud, lest “the might of the sun” should parch the skin (*Iliad* 23.188–191). Of Alcinous the phrase is a formula of the Phaeacian books. ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the sacred might of Alcinous” (7.167), comes again six times in Books 8 and 13, and μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the might of Alcinous”, without the adjective, three times more (7.178; 8.423, 13.49, 13.64; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]], the translation's table of formulas and [[alcinous]]). It is made like ἱερὴ ἲς Τηλεμάχοιο, “the sacred strength of Telemachus” (2.409; see [[sacred-strength-of-telemachus]] and [[hieros]]). Here it is the king's might that acts on the old man's word, raising the suppliant from the hearth (7.167–171; see [[supplication]]).
