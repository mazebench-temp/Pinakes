---
summary: Alcinous says of the stranger what Hector said of every man: once the Phaeacians have brought him home, “he shall suffer whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (7.196–198).
refs: [7.196-198]
---

Hector told Andromache that no man, coward or brave, has ever escaped his fate “once he has been born” (*Iliad* 6.488–489; see the entry), and Homer's speakers picture the portion fixed at birth as a thread spun for the newborn child (*Iliad* 20.127–128, 24.209–210). Alcinous uses the rule to mark the limit of what his people can do. They will bring the stranger home “without toil and vexation” and keep him from evil “in between”, but what waits for him on his own land is his fate's and the Spinners' (7.192–198; see [[pompe]], [[aisa]] and [[fates]]). Merry reports Eusebius' reading of the verses: there are some things a man cannot escape, and these will befall him when his hosts have done their part (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.197). The stranger answers the speech by calling himself a mortal whose sorrows are the most of all men's (7.208–214; see [[algos]]).
