summary: The book's supplication, made as Nausicaa told him. Wrapped in Athena's mist, Odysseus throws his arms around Arete's knees and entreats her, “to your husband and to your knees I have come, after toiling much” (7.139–152), then sits down in the ashes of the hearth until the old Echeneus bids the king raise him; Alcinous takes him by the hand and seats him in his son's chair, and the Phaeacians pour wine to Zeus, “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.153–183). At the end of the evening the king blames his daughter, for “it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.292, 7.301).

**The gesture made.** In Book 6 Odysseus begged Nausicaa “with soothing words, standing apart”, and did not clasp her knees (6.141–147); she told him that the knees he must clasp were her mother's (6.310–311; see the note there). Here he makes the gesture in full. He goes through the hall still wrapped in the mist, “until he came to Arete and King Alcinous. / And Odysseus threw his arms around the knees of Arete” (7.141–142; see [[gounata]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), and only then does the mist flow back and the hall see him (7.143–145; see [[thesphaton]] and [[silence]]). The verb of the plea is λιτανεύω, “entreat”, the word of Phoenix's story, in which the old Oeneus entreated Meleager again and again, “standing on the threshold of the high-roofed chamber” (*Iliad* 9.581–583); λιτάνευεν is Aristarchus' reading here, where the vulgate has ἐλλιτάνευεν (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 7.145; see the translation's note on 7.144–145).

**The words.**

The plea has the parts of the scene (see [[supplication-type-scene]]). He names the one he begs by her father, “daughter of godlike Rhexenor” (see [[rhexenor]]); he states the gesture in words, in the verse he spoke to the river god with “your husband” for “your stream”, “to your husband and to your knees I have come, after toiling much” (7.147 = 5.449; see the Book 5 note and [[posis]]), and adds the banqueters, so that the whole hall is drawn into the claim; he offers what a suppliant can give, a blessing, that the gods grant them prosperity and that each may hand on his possessions and his prize to his children (7.148–150; see [[olbos]] and [[geras]]); and he asks for his escort home (7.151–152; see [[pompe]]). He does not say who he is, and no one asks him until the evening is over (7.237–238; see [[xenia]]).

**In the ashes.** “He spoke thus and sat down at the hearth in the ashes / beside the fire” (7.153–154). He sits in the posture of a suppliant, though, as Merry says after Ameis, there is no distinct notion of sanctity or sanctuary connected with the hearth in Homer. Merry gathers the later evidence: Apollonius has Medea and Jason take refuge at Circe's hearth (*Argonautica* 4.693), Plutarch has Themistocles throw himself down at the hearth of Admetus, and in Herodotus ἐπίστιος, “at one's hearth”, stands for ἱκέτης, “suppliant”; the ἑστία of these passages implies far more than Homer's ἐσχάρη (Merry on 7.153; see [[hearth]]). The Herodotean passage is Croesus' question to the Phrygian Adrastus, who has come to him with blood on his hands: “from what part of Phrygia have you come to my hearth?” (ἐπίστιός μοι ἐγένεο, Herodotus 1.35.3). In his story the word for his coming to Ogygia is of the same family: a god brought him ἐφέστιον, “to her hearth” (7.248), “to be her guest”, in Merry's rendering (Merry on 7.248; see [[calypso]]). The old Echeneus measures the place against the custom of the house: it is not more honorable, nor fitting, that a stranger should sit on the ground at the hearth in the ashes (7.159–160; see [[echeneus]]).

**Raised by the hand.** The answer is given by the king, who “took wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles by the hand / and roused him from the hearth, and seated him on a shining chair, / making his son, manly Laodamas, stand up” (7.168–170; see [[alcinous]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]), as Achilles “rose from his chair and raised the old man by the hand” when Priam had clasped his knees (*Iliad* 24.515; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). The suppliant is now a guest: water for his hands, a table, bread and dainties (7.172–177; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[xenia]]), and a libation to the god of suppliants (7.179–183; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[zeus]]).

**The first to be supplicated.** Telling his story, Odysseus says of Nausicaa, τὴν ἱκέτευσʼ, “I came to her as a suppliant” (7.292), and Alcinous finds fault with her because of it: she did not bring him home with her handmaids, “and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.299–301). The clause gives the reason why she was wrong. A stranger was regarded as the suppliant, with all the rights implied in the word, of the person with whom he first established relations, Merry explains, comparing Oedipus' words to the Eumenides in Sophocles, at whose seats he has first bent his knee in that land (*Oedipus at Colonus* 85), and Arete's word on the stranger in Book 11, “he is my guest” (11.338, a plain rendering; Merry on 7.53, 7.301; see the translation's note on 7.301). Odysseus takes the blame on himself (7.302–307; see [[quick-to-anger]]).
