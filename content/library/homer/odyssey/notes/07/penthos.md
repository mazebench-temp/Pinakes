summary: Odysseus asks leave to eat, though he grieves: the belly “bids a man remember it under compulsion, / even one sorely worn down and with grief in his heart, / as I too have grief in my heart” (7.217–219).
# πένθος (7.218, 7.219) is in the entry.
---

The grief is named twice in two verses, first of any man and then of himself, ἐνὶ φρεσὶ πένθος ἔχοντα … πένθος μὲν ἔχω φρεσίν (7.218–219), and both times the belly overrules it (see [[gaster]]). It is the reverse of what he told Achilles at Troy, that the Achaeans cannot mourn (πενθῆσαι) a corpse with the belly (*Iliad* 19.225; see [[fasting]]): there fasting was the mourner's way, and here eating is the sufferer's need. The book has other words for the same state. He asks to eat κηδόμενόν περ, “grieved though I am” (7.215), and ends his story to Arete ἀχνύμενός περ, “grieved though I am” (7.297; see [[achos]]); on the night of his landing he slept among the leaves φίλον τετιημένος ἦτορ, “grieving at heart” (7.287; see [[algos]]).
