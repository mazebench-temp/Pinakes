**The orchard.** “There tall trees grow, flourishing, / pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, / and sweet figs and flourishing olives” (7.114–116; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[olive]]). The first verse is nearly the verse of the tall trees on Calypso's island, ὅθι δένδρεα μακρὰ πεφύκει (5.238, 5.241; see the Book 5 note). Those were alder, poplar and fir, long dead and dry, felled for a raft; these are fruit trees, and their fruit “never perishes or fails, / in winter or in summer” (7.117–118). Of the five only the olive is named in the Iliad, whose fig is the wild fig (see [[the-fig-tree]]). ῥοιή, the pomegranate, is found only in these verses and in their return in the underworld, where the same trees hang their fruit over Tantalus and the wind tosses it away whenever he reaches for it (11.588–592, a plain rendering); the pear, the apple and the fig come back in Laertes' orchard on Ithaca, where Odysseus names the thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees and forty fig trees his father gave him as a boy (24.340–341, a plain rendering). In the orchard of Alcinous the fruit ripens on the tree while more comes on: “Pear upon pear grows old, apple upon apple, / and cluster upon cluster, fig upon fig” (7.120–121).

**The poplar.** The poplar of Book 7 is in a simile: the slave women at the looms sit “like the leaves of a tall poplar” (7.106; see [[like-the-leaves-of-a-poplar]]). W. W. Merry, who takes Homer's αἴγειρος for the aspen, finds the point of the likeness in the trembling of its leaves (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.106; on 5.64).

**The bushes.** Telling Arete how he came ashore, Odysseus says that he went up from the river “and slept among the bushes, and drew the leaves / about me”, and slept “There among the leaves” all night and into the next day (7.285–288). The bushes, θάμνοι, are the two of Book 5, of wild olive and olive, grown from one spot (5.476–477; see [[olive]]); ἐκβάς is “came out” of the ravine in which the river ran (Merry on 7.285, comparing 5.462). ἠφυσάμην, “drew”, is a word for drawing liquids, and Merry sets it beside the χύσις, the “heap” of leaves that Odysseus poured over himself in Book 5 (5.487): in both places words proper to liquids are used of leaves (on 7.286; see the translation's table of key words).
