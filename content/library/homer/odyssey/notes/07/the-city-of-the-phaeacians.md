**To the city.** The book begins with the two journeys that Nausicaa had planned: the mules carry her “to the city” (7.2), and Odysseus sets out after her once she has had time to reach home (7.14; 6.295–296; see [[nausicaa]] and [[the-grove-of-athena]]). Athena pours a thick mist around him, so that no Phaeacian meeting him may taunt him or ask who he is, and when he is about to enter “the lovely city” she meets him in the likeness of a young girl carrying a water jar (7.14–20; see [[athena]] and [[achlys]]). ἐραννός, “lovely”, is the epithet of Calydon in the Iliad (*Iliad* 9.531, 9.577); it stands to ἐράω, “love”, as the Latin *amoenus* to *amare*, and the double ν comes from ἐρασ-νός (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.18). He asks her the way to the house of Alcinous, since he knows no one “of the people who hold this city and land” (7.22–26 = 6.177; see [[alcinous]]), and she leads him through it, telling him to look at no one and ask nothing (7.28–38; see [[phaeacians]]).

**What he saw.** The Phaeacians do not see him “as he went through the city among them”, διὰ σφέας, through the midst of them on his way to the palace (7.40; Merry on 7.40), but he sees their city:

The imperfect θαύμαζεν is the wonder that went on, at each new marvel as he came to it (Merry on 7.43; see [[thambos]]). He sees what Nausicaa had described to him: the harbors and the ships drawn up along the road (6.263–265; see [[harbor]] and [[balanced-ships]]), the place of assembly (6.266–267; see [[assembly]]) and the high rampart around the city (6.262). αὐτῶν θʼ ἡρώων, “of the heroes themselves”, turns from the city of stone to the men who live in it (Merry on 7.44, comparing πόλιν αὐτῶν, 2.154; see [[heros]]). The walls are “long”, τείχεα μακρά, as the walls of Troy are (*Iliad* 4.34; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]), and “fitted with stakes”, as the Achaeans' ditch before their wall was fitted on top with sharp stakes (σκολόπεσσιν … ἠρήρει, *Iliad* 12.55–56; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). σκόλοπες is found only here in the Odyssey; here they are a palisade along the coping of the long walls, as Eumaeus coped the stone wall of his yard with wild pear (14.10; Merry on 7.45). Then they come to the famous house of the king (7.46), whose wonders the poem describes when Athena has gone (7.81–132; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**The queen in the city.** The people honor Arete, Athena says, and “look on her as a god / and greet her with words when she walks through the city” (7.71–72; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The citizens' spring.** Of the two springs in the king's orchard, one runs “under the threshold of the court / toward the high house; from this spring the citizens drew water” (7.130–131; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). πολῖται, “citizens”, are the townsfolk. Merry supposes that this spring was carried from outside under the entrance of the court and came up, perhaps in a basin, in the middle of the court; but ὅθεν refers to the spring itself, so that the townsfolk drew their water at the fountain-head, or at least at some point of the stream before it reached the threshold of the court (on 7.130).
