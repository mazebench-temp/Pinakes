summary: Three of Odysseus' answers open with “Odysseus of many wiles” (πολύμητις, 7.207, 7.240, 7.302), and when the king raises him from the hearth he is “wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” (ποικιλομήτην, 7.168).
# πολύμητις (7.207, 7.240, 7.302) is in the entry.
---

The epithet introduces the three answers in which the stranger has to choose what to say: to the king's wonder whether he is a god, which he turns aside (7.207–225; see [[immortality]] and [[gaster]]); to the queen's question who he is and who gave him the clothes, which he answers with Ogygia, the raft and the river, and not with his name (7.240–297; see [[xenia]]); and to the king's blame of his daughter, which he takes on himself with a story that Eustathius, as Merry reports, calls an open lie, such as the wise man tells at the right moment (7.302–307; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.305; see [[quick-to-anger]]). ποικιλομήτης, “of subtle wiles”, joined with δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted”, is his formula in the Iliad when the Trojans close round him like jackals round a wounded stag (*Iliad* 11.482), in Nestor's story of the return (3.163), and in the fight in the hall (22.115, 22.202, 22.281); Athena calls him ποικιλομῆτα, δόλων ἆτʼ, “of subtle wiles, insatiable in tricks”, to his face on Ithaca (13.293, a plain rendering; see [[of-subtle-wiles]] and [[wise-hearted]]).
