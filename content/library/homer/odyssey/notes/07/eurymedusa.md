**The fire and the supper.** Nausicaa leaves the mules and the clothes to her brothers: “And she herself went to her own chamber; and a fire was kindled for her / by an old woman from Apeire, her chamber-woman Eurymedusa” (7.7–8; see [[nausicaa]] and [[apeire]]). The narrator stops to tell who the old woman is, a captive picked out long ago as a prize for Alcinous (7.9–11; see [[alcinous]] and [[geras]]), and then: “She had reared white-armed Nausicaa in the halls. / She lit the fire for her, and made ready her supper within” (7.12–13). ἥ … ἥ are both demonstratives (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.12–13), and ἐκόσμει, “made ready”, is the opposite of ἀπεκόσμεον, “cleared away”, when the handmaids clear away the gear of the feast in the hall (7.232; Merry on 7.12).

**Why a fire.** A scholium took the fire for a sign that it was winter, and the older interpreters generally put this part of the wanderings late in the year; Merry thinks the evening reason enough, since a fire served for light or warmth or both, as when the slave women of Odysseus' house pile wood on the braziers, φόως ἔμεν ἠδὲ θέρεσθαι, for light and for warmth (19.63–64; Merry on 7.7; see the translation's note on 7.7 and [[fire]]). Zenodotus athetized 7.13, since 7.7 has already told of the fire (see the translation's note on 7.13).

**An old nurse.** The verses that bring her in follow the pattern of those that bring in Eurycleia, who lights Telemachus to his chamber: “whom Laertes once bought with his own possessions” (1.430) and “whom once the curving ships had brought from Apeire” (7.9); Eurycleia “had reared him when he was small” (1.435), and Eurymedusa “had reared white-armed Nausicaa in the halls” (7.12; see [[eurycleia]] and [[handmaids]]). It is the last we see of the princess in this book: while her father receives the stranger in the hall, she sups in her own chamber, served by her old nurse.
