---
summary: The Giants are named twice in the book: as the high-hearted people of Eurymedon, the ancestor of the Phaeacian kings, whom he destroyed with himself (7.58–60), and with the Cyclopes as peoples to whom the gods are near, as they are to the Phaeacians (7.205–206).
# "his reckless people" (7.60) is the Giants.
refs: [7.60]
---

**Eurymedon's people.** Athena names them in the descent of Arete and Alcinous: Periboea, the mother of Nausithous, was the youngest daughter of Eurymedon, “who once was king over the high-hearted Giants. / But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.59–60; see [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]] and [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]]). The Phaeacian kings, Poseidon's descendants, are the Giants' descendants too. How the people came to ruin is not told; Merry weighs a foolhardy war against another tribe, a rising against their king, or the king's own guilt, and prefers the first (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59). On the same verse he reports a derivation of their name from γῆ, “earth”, and quotes Pausanias, who took these verses to show that Homer's Giants were mortal men (see the general article).

**Near to the gods.** Alcinous, wondering whether the stranger may be a god, says that the gods have always appeared to the Phaeacians in plain sight, at their hecatombs and to a lone wayfarer, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (7.205–206; see [[phaeacians]], [[cyclopes]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). ἐγγύθεν, “near”, is generally taken as near in kin, like ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (5.35), though Welcker took it of neighbors living in a land close to the gods; the translation's “near to them” keeps both (Merry on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205 and [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). Against Welcker, Merry sets Hesiod, who makes both the Giants and the Cyclopes children of Earth (*Theogony* 139–146, 183–186; see [[hesiod]]), and Acusilaus and Alcaeus, who, he reports, gave the Phaeacians the same descent. So Alcinous names as the Phaeacians' peers the people from whom his own line descends through Periboea, and the Cyclopes who once plundered them in Hypereia (6.4–6; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]]). φῦλα, “tribes”, is the word Odysseus uses of all mankind a hundred verses later: “for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (7.307).
