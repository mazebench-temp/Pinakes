**The silence.** Odysseus has thrown his arms around Arete's knees, made his plea for an escort, and sat down “at the hearth in the ashes / beside the fire; and they all fell hushed in silence” (7.153–154; see [[supplication]], [[hearth]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The hall waits for the king's word (7.161), until an old man speaks: “At last the old hero Echeneus spoke among them, / who was one of the older Phaeacian men, / and excelled in speaking, knowing many things of old” (7.155–157). ὀψὲ δὲ δὴ μετέειπε is the formula that breaks a silence after a hard word in the Iliad, as when Achilles has refused the embassy and “At last the old horse-driving Phoenix spoke among them” (*Iliad* 9.430–432). On προγενέστερος, “one of the older”, see the entry; on the scholium that names Halitherses in his place, see the translation's note on 7.155.

**His advice.**

{{quote:dfe67a99-2000-571b-aca6-4d07e8c3ea34}}

He speaks to the king alone, by name. κάλλιον, “more honorable”, measures this strange treatment of a stranger against the Phaeacians' ordinary custom of making him welcome (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.159; see the translation's note on 7.159 and [[xenia]]): Nestor's house shows the custom, where strangers are fed before they are questioned (3.69–70). The infinitive ἧσθαι explains τόδε, and the clauses answer each other, ξεῖνον μέν (7.160) by οἵδε δέ (7.161), and οὐ μέν τοι (7.159) by ἀλλʼ ἄγε (7.162; Merry on 7.159; on the text's ἄλλʼ ἄγε, see the translation's note on 7.162). οἵδε are “thy people here, waiting for a bidding from thee”, who “are putting constraint on themselves”: ἰσχανόωνται is a lengthened form of ἴσχω, with frequentative force (Merry on 7.161). ἐπικρῆσαι is to mix the wine with the water (Merry on 7.164; see [[mixing-bowl]]).

**What he asks.** He asks for three things: a chair for the stranger, a libation, and a supper. The libation is for the god of suppliants, Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.165; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]], [[zeus]] and [[libation]]), and the supper is to come from the housekeeper's stores (7.166; see [[handmaids]]). “But when the sacred might of Alcinous heard this” (7.167), the king did it all: he raised Odysseus by the hand from the hearth and seated him in the chair of his son Laodamas, the meal was set before him, and the herald Pontonous was bidden to mix the wine for Zeus in Echeneus' own words (7.167–183, with 7.180–181 as 7.164–165; see [[alcinous]], [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]] and [[pontonous]]).
