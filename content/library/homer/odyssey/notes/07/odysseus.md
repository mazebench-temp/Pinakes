summary: The book of his arrival. Wrapped in Athena's mist, he walks into the city, asks a girl with a water jar the way and follows her to the palace; he stands and wonders before the house of Alcinous, passes the feasting leaders unseen and clasps the queen's knees, then sits in the ashes of the hearth until the king raises him (7.14–171). Fed and promised an escort, he denies that he is a god and pleads the shameless belly (7.172–232); asked by Arete who he is and who gave him his clothes, he tells of Calypso, the raft and the storm, and of Nausicaa, without giving his name (7.233–297), takes the blame for the girl (7.302–307), prays for Alcinous and sleeps in the colonnade (7.329–347).

**Into the city.** For the whole book, see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]. It opens where the last left him, praying in Athena's grove: “So he prayed there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (7.1), after the verse that opened Book 6 (6.1; see the translation's note on 7.1). When Nausicaa is home, “Odysseus rose to go to the city; and Athena / poured a thick mist around him, in friendship for Odysseus, / lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him, / should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (7.14–17; see [[athena]] and [[achlys]]). In the Iliad, Nitzsch remarked, the gods use such a mist only to hide themselves, while in the Odyssey they use it for their favorites too (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.15; on the reading ἀμφί, see the translation's note on 7.14). At the edge of the city the goddess meets him “like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar” (7.20), and he asks her the way:

{{quote:03e108b8-dc6d-5811-a91e-f6226e758c8d}}

The name explains “the man”, as “from a distant land” explains “far away” (Merry on 7.25; see [[distant-land]] and [[much-tried]]); the last verse is the one he spoke to Nausicaa on the shore (6.177). The girl calls him ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father” (7.28, 7.48), the respectful address of the young to an older man (see the translation's key words and [[xenia]]), tells him to go in silence and question no one, for the Phaeacians do not much put up with strangers, and leads him; “and he followed in the footprints of the goddess” (7.28–38; see [[followed-in-the-footprints]] and [[phaeacians]]). Unseen in the mist, he marvels at the harbors and the balanced ships, the places of assembly and the long walls (7.39–45; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[thambos]]): θαύμαζεν, the imperfect, is the constant wonder at each new marvel (Merry on 7.43).

**At the door.** At the palace Athena bids him go in and not be afraid at all, “for a bold man is better in all / deeds, even if he comes from some other place” (7.50–52; see [[a-bold-man-is-better]], [[tharsos]] and the translation's note on 7.52), and tells him to find the queen first: if Arete thinks of him in friendship, there is hope that he will reach his home (7.53–77; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Then she leaves him for Marathon and Athens (7.78–81). “But Odysseus / went to the glorious house of Alcinous; and his heart pondered much / as he stood there, before he reached the bronze threshold” (7.81–83): ἱσταμένῳ, as he caught glimpses of the glories of the palace from without, his pace betrayed the wonder of his mind, and he stopped again and again (Merry on 7.83). After the house and the garden (7.84–132; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]), “There much-enduring brilliant Odysseus stood and gazed. / But when he had gazed at everything in his heart, / he quickly stepped over the threshold into the house” (7.133–135), the verses with which Hermes stood and gazed at Calypso's cave (5.75–76; see [[threshold]]).

**At the queen's knees.** He finds the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians pouring the last libation of the evening to Hermes (7.136–138; see [[hermes]] and [[libation]]), goes through the hall in the mist, throws his arms around Arete's knees, and the mist flows back from him (7.139–143; see [[thesphaton]]). His plea names her father and her husband, blesses the banqueters, and asks for one thing, an escort home, “since long now I suffer woes far from my friends” (7.146–152; see [[supplication]], [[gounata]] and [[pompe]]). “He spoke thus and sat down at the hearth in the ashes / beside the fire; and they all fell hushed in silence” (7.153–154; see [[hearth]]). ἐπʼ ἐσχάρῃ is “at the hearth”: he sits in the posture of a suppliant, though, as Ameis observes, the hearth in Homer has no distinct notion of sanctity or sanctuary (Merry on 7.153). The old Echeneus speaks for him, and the king takes him by the hand, raises him from the hearth and seats him on a shining chair, his son's (7.155–171; see [[echeneus]], [[alcinous]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). He washes and eats, “And much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate” (7.172–177), and wine is poured for Zeus, who attends on revered suppliants (7.178–183; see [[pontonous]]).

**No god.** When the king, wondering whether the stranger is one of the immortals come down from heaven, has promised him an escort (7.186–206), Odysseus answers:

{{quote:da7aaefc-0880-59ce-b1ab-c2f6482cb643}}

“Let something else be on your mind” means that there is no ground for such anxiety as the king surmised (Merry on 7.208). ξύμπαντα is “from first to last” (Merry on 7.214; see [[algos]] and [[mortal-men]]). Then he asks leave to eat, grieved though he is, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly, / which bids a man remember it under compulsion, / even one sorely worn down and with grief in his heart” (7.215–218; see [[gaster]] and [[penthos]]). ἐπὶ γαστέρι is a formula of comparison, “more shameless than”, literally “beyond”, the belly; ἔπλετο and ἐκέλευσεν are aorists of custom; and ἐκ … ληθάνει, “makes me forget”, is causative (Merry on 7.216, 7.217, 7.220). His last words ask the Phaeacians to make haste at dawn and set him on his native land, “even after my many sufferings; let life leave me, once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (7.222–225; see [[nostos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). καί περ stand side by side only here in Homer, and καὶ λίποι, as Sarpedon's “and afterward let life leave me / in your city” (*Iliad* 5.685–686), makes the loss of life the worst that could happen (Merry on 7.224; on ὀτρύνεσθαι, the infinitive for the imperative, see the translation's note on 7.222). “So he spoke, and they all approved and bade / send the stranger on his way, since he had spoken fair and right” (7.226–227; see [[moira]]).

**The story.** Left in the hall with the king and queen, he is asked by Arete who he is, and who gave him the clothes she knows for her own work (7.233–239; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[who-are-you-among-men]]). He answers the second question and not the first: his name waits until his tale begins (9.19). It is hard, he says, to tell his sorrows at full length, but he will tell what she asks (7.241–243; Merry on 7.241). There is an island, Ogygia, where “the daughter of Atlas, wily Calypso” lives, with whom no one mingles, god or man (7.244–247; see [[ogygia]], [[calypso]] and [[wily]]): δολόεσσα gives his own experience of her rather than her common character, and means not “treacherous” but “sly” (Merry on 7.245; see the translation's key words). Zeus shattered his ship with a thunderbolt, in the words that Calypso used of him to Hermes (7.249–251, as 5.131–133); he drifted nine days on the keel to her island, and she “took me in / and loved me with care and cared for me, and said / that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days; / but she never persuaded the heart in my breast” (7.255–258; see [[immortality]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]). λαβοῦσα probably implies that she made him stay (Merry on 7.255). Seven years he kept wet with his tears the immortal garments she gave him; in the eighth she sent him off on a raft, “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (7.259–266; see [[tears]] and [[raft]]); and after seventeen days at sea Poseidon raised the storm, and he swam ashore by the river and slept among the leaves until the sun was declining (7.267–289; see [[poseidon]], [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[sleep]]). He tells it in the verses of Book 5, and not quite as Book 5 told it: see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and the translation's notes on 7.250, 7.251–258, 7.272, 7.273 and 7.289. Last comes Nausicaa, who did not fall short of good sense, gave him bread and wine and a bath in the river, “and gave me these clothes” (7.290–296; see [[nausicaa]]). “This is the truth I have told you, grieved though I am” (7.297), ἀληθείην being predicative to ταῦτα, “as the truth” (Merry on 7.297).

**His tact.** When the king blames his daughter for not bringing him home with her handmaids, he takes the blame on himself: she bade him follow with them, but he would not, “out of fear and shame, / lest your heart too should be indignant when you saw it; / for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (7.302–307; see [[aidos]], [[deos]] and [[quick-to-anger]]). It was she who had told him to wait in the grove (6.295–299); Eustathius remarks that here Odysseus plainly lies, as a wise man would do at the right moment (Merry on 7.305; see [[nausicaa]]). The king answers with his wish to have him for a son-in-law and his promise of an escort for the next day (7.308–328; see [[alcinous]] and [[nausicaas-wish]]).

**The prayer and the bed.** “So he spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / and he spoke in prayer, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (7.329–330; see [[called-him-by-name]] and the translation's note on 7.330): “Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said, / Alcinous! Then his fame over the grain-giving earth / would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land” (7.331–333; see [[prayer]], [[zeus]] and [[kleos]]). Arete has a bed made for him, and the handmaids bid him rise and go to it: “‘Rise and go to bed, stranger; your bed has been made.’ / So they spoke, and it seemed welcome to him to lie down to sleep” (7.342–343; see [[handmaids]] and [[beds-beneath-the-colonnade]]). The book ends as it began, with his name in the verse that left him asleep under the olives (6.1), and the corded bed on which Telemachus slept at Pylos (3.399): “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus, / on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade” (7.344–345; see [[corded]] and [[loud-resounding]]).

**His epithets and names.** πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (7.1, 7.133, 7.139, 7.177, 7.329, 7.344; see [[much-enduring]]); δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (7.21, 7.230; see [[brilliant]]); πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles” (7.207, 7.240, 7.302; see [[of-many-wiles]]); Ὀδυσῆα δαΐφρονα ποικιλομήτην, “wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” (7.168; see [[wise-hearted]] and [[of-subtle-wiles]]). To the Phaeacians he is only ξεῖνος, “the stranger” (7.160, 7.190, 7.227, 7.342), a ξεῖνος ταλαπείριος, “a much-tried stranger”, in his own words (7.24), and δύστηνος, “unhappy man” (7.223, 7.248).
