summary: Alcinous will send the stranger home without harm, and “there, after that, / he shall suffer whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (7.196–198). The king blames his daughter for one thing she “did not devise rightly” (ἐναίσιμον, 7.299), and answers the stranger's fear of his anger with a maxim, “what is due is better in all things” (αἴσιμα, 7.310).
# αἶσα (7.197) is in the entry; ἐναίσιμον (7.299) and αἴσιμα (7.310) are in the Book 2 note.
---

**The portion spun at birth.** The escort answers for the voyage, and for no more: the Phaeacians will bring the stranger home “and suffer no evil and no bane in between, / before he sets foot on his own land”; after that, “he shall suffer whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth, when his mother bore him” (7.195–198; see [[pompe]]). The verses are Hera's of Achilles, whom the gods will guard for the day, “hereafter he shall suffer whatever his fate / spun for him with her thread at his birth” (*Iliad* 20.127–128; see the translation's note on 7.198), and Hecuba's of Hector, with mighty Fate as the spinner (*Iliad* 24.209–210; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). Merry, supposing the derivation of αἶσα from ἴσος to be correct, finds the usage bearing it out: αἶσα is generally a due portion, as of spoil (*Iliad* 18.327) or of hope (19.84), and so the measure of a life; it may take the genitive of its author, as “the evil fate of a god” (11.61, a plain rendering), or stand nearly for a power or a person, as here and in *Iliad* 20.127, with verbs such as “stood beside” (9.52, a plain rendering). κατά goes adverbially with νήσαντο, “span off for him with their thread at his birth” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.197).

**Not fatalism.** Merry reports Eusebius' remark (*Preparation for the Gospel* 6.8) that the Homeric poems do not countenance fatalism, and that no more is meant here than when we say there are some things we cannot escape: the king will do his part for the stranger, and then whatever his own efforts cannot avert will befall him; the relative ἅσσα, “whatever”, leaves the outcome open (Merry on 7.197). The κλῶθες, “the Spinners”, found only here in Homer, are the half-personified agency of αἶσα; the three Moirai are post-Homeric, first named in Hesiod (*Theogony* 217–219, 904–906), and the epithet βαρεῖαι, “heavy”, “stern”, is no more than could have been said of αἶσα itself (Merry on 7.197; see [[fates]] and the translation's note on 7.197).

**What is fitting.** The adjectives of the word keep its sense of a fair measure. The king finds one fault in his daughter: “truly this, at least, she did not devise rightly” (τοῦτό γʼ ἐναίσιμον οὐκ ἐνόησε, 7.299), the half-verse with which Antinous, in the assembly of Ithaca, found the one fault in Penelope's devices, that while she keeps to her purpose the suitors will eat up her son's substance (2.121–124; see [[nausicaa]] and [[noos]]). Odysseus takes the fault on himself (7.302–307; see [[quick-to-anger]]), and the king answers that he is not angry for no reason: ἀμείνω δʼ αἴσιμα πάντα, “what is due is better in all things” (7.310; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]).
