summary: Pausanias, as Merry quotes him, found in this book his proof that Homer's Giants were mortal men and not a race of gods, in Alcinous' verse on the peoples near to the gods and, more clearly, in the verse on Eurymedon, who destroyed his reckless people and perished himself.
---

- **Mortal Giants.** Homer, Pausanias says, made no mention of the Giants in the Iliad; but in the Odyssey he wrote that the Laestrygonians came against the ships of Odysseus likened not to men but to Giants (10.119–120, Greek only), and he made the king of the Phaeacians say that the Phaeacians are near to the gods, as are the Cyclopes and the race of the Giants (7.205–206). In these verses Homer shows that the Giants are mortal and not a divine race, and more clearly still in the verses on Eurymedon, “who once was king over the high-hearted Giants. / But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.59–60), for λαός, “people”, means in his verses the mass of men (Pausanias 8.29.2, as Merry quotes him; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59). Merry concludes that Homer knows nothing of the battle of the gods and the Giants of later legend, and finds three ways to read Eurymedon's fall: a foolhardy war against another people, a rising of the people against their king that destroyed both, or the king's own guilt bringing ruin on himself and his people; he thinks the first the likeliest, from the epithets “high-hearted” and “reckless” (Merry on 7.59): [[giants]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[cyclopes]] and [[phaeacians]].
