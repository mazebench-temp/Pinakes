summary: The book passes in the house of Alcinous, and its words for the parts of a house are all in it, from the bronze walls and coping of the court to the hall where the leaders feast, Nausicaa's chamber, the colonnade where the guest sleeps and the inner part where the king lies down (7.3–347). Odysseus asks only to see his own high-roofed house before he dies (7.224–225), and Alcinous offers him a house if he will stay (7.314).

**One house.** Nausicaa comes “to the famous house of her father” (7.3), and the stranger asks the girl with the water jar to lead him “to the house of the man / Alcinous” (7.22–23); Athena shows him “the house that you bid me / show you” (7.28–29, 7.48–49), and from its threshold the poem describes it, from the walls of the court to the hall within (7.84–132). The rest of the book passes inside it (see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). Another house appears for a moment when Athena leaves him and enters “the well-built house of Erechtheus” (7.81), which W. W. Merry takes for the temple on the Acropolis where Athena and Erechtheus were worshipped together (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.81; see [[erechtheus]] and [[athena-and-erechtheus]]).

**The parts of the house.** Walls of bronze “were drawn out this way and that, / from the threshold to the inner part, and around them a coping of dark blue enamel” (7.86–87; see [[bronze]] and [[kyanos]]). ἐληλέδατο, “were drawn out”, uses ἐλαύνω of the continuous line of a wall, as one “drives” a swath in the Iliad (Merry on 7.86, citing *Iliad* 11.68; see the translation's note on 7.86 and its row ἐληλέδατο, ἐρηρέδατο). The walls are those of the court, Merry thinks, since θριγκός, the coping, is the finish of a wall in the open air: Odysseus' own court is fitted with a wall and copings (17.266–267, a plain rendering), and Euripides' Menelaus comes up to a house in Egypt “ringed round with copings” (Merry on 7.86; Euripides, *Helen* 430). The Suda explains θριγκός as the fence of a house, a crown, a little wall, an enclosure, or the topmost parts of houses (θ 497). The μυχός to which the walls run is then the inner end of the court, though the word is used elsewhere of the inner part of a hall, a chamber, a hut or a cave (Merry on 7.86). Within, ἐν δέ, the chairs are set firm “along the wall this way and that, / from the threshold right through to the inner part” (7.95–96; see [[throne]]): the words are now of the interior of the men's hall, and ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα means both lengthwise and breadthwise (Merry on 7.95, 7.96). Outside the court, “near the doors”, lies the orchard (7.112), and a spring runs “under the threshold of the court / toward the high house” (7.130–131; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]], [[door]] and [[threshold]]).

**Hall, chamber, colonnade.** The μέγαρον, the hall, is where the leaders drink, where wine is served “to all throughout the hall” and where Odysseus is “left behind in the hall” with the king and queen when the others have gone (7.180, 7.230; see [[feast]]). Nausicaa goes “to her own chamber”, θάλαμος, where the old woman who reared her lights her fire (7.7; see [[nausicaa]]). At the end of the evening the women bring torches “out from the hall” and make the guest's bed “beneath the colonnade” (7.336–339), the αἴθουσα, the roofed porch along the court where guests sleep at Pylos and at Sparta (3.399, 4.297; see the Book 3 note); the Suda glosses the word with στοά, “portico” (αι 139). “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus, / on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade, / and Alcinous lay down in the inner part of the high house” (7.344–346), as Nestor and Menelaus do (3.402, 4.304; see [[bed]], [[corded]] and [[loud-resounding]]).

**Home.** οἶκος is the home a man returns to. Athena tells Odysseus that if the queen thinks of him in friendship, “then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.76–77), the verses that Nausicaa had spoken with “well-built” (6.314–315; see [[high-roofed]] and [[nostos]]). His own wish is to see “my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” and then die (7.224–225; see [[possessions]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Alcinous answers with the offer of another: “and I would give you a house and possessions, / if you stayed willingly” (7.314–315). Arete is honored above “all the women who now keep house under their husbands” (7.68; see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), and the leaders of the Phaeacians go to bed “each to his own house” (7.229).
