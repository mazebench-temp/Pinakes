**Cluster upon cluster.** The grapes are first named among the fruit trees of the orchard: “Pear upon pear grows old, apple upon apple, / and cluster upon cluster, fig upon fig” (7.120–121; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[trees]]). W. W. Merry finds the cluster unexpected there, since the vines belong to the vineyard that is described next (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.122). On Calypso's island the garden vine over the cave “flourished with clusters of grapes” (5.69; see the Book 5 note).

**The vineyard.** “And there his fruitful vineyard is rooted, / one part of which, a warm spot on level ground, / is dried by the sun, while other grapes they are gathering, / and others they are treading; and in front are unripe grapes / shedding their bloom, and others are faintly turning dark” (7.122–126; see [[helios]]). Merry reads three scenes in it: at the back the ripe grapes, some left on the vines to dry for raisin wine and others gathered; in the middle the treading at the wine-press; in front the young grapes, ὄμφακες, which have just shed their blossom or are beginning to color (on 7.123, 7.125, 7.126). τρυγόωσιν, “they are gathering”, is the verb of the vintage on the Shield of Achilles, where girls and young men carry the grapes in baskets by the single path (τρυγόῳεν, *Iliad* 18.566; see [[the-vintage]]). On the Shield it is the moment of the gathering; in the garden of Alcinous the year's whole course is there at once, “the various stages of growth and maturity are all going on together” (Merry on 7.126).

**Laertes' vines.** At the end of the poem Odysseus proves himself to his father by naming the trees of his orchard, among them the fifty rows of vines that Laertes promised him as a boy, each to be gathered in its turn, with clusters of every kind on them (24.341–344, a plain rendering; see [[laertes]]).
