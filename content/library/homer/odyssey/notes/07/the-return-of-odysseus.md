summary: The return is promised. Athena gives Odysseus Nausicaa's hope in the words of Zeus' decree (7.75–77); he asks the queen and then the leaders for an escort home (7.151–152, 7.222–225); Alcinous promises it for the next day, in a ship that will carry him home asleep however far his land may be, and leaves the rest to his fate (7.189–198, 7.317–328); and Odysseus prays that the king may accomplish it (7.331–333).

**Hope.** Nausicaa told Odysseus that if her mother thought of him in friendship, there was hope that he would reach his home (6.313–315). Athena, in the likeness of a girl, tells him the same at the door of the palace, with one change: “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). “High-roofed house” is the house of Zeus' decree, that it was his fate “to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42; again 5.114–115, in Hermes' message to Calypso; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]): the goddess who heard the decree speaks it as a hope (see [[athena]]). In the sixth book the three verses are wanting in several manuscripts, and modern editors generally reject them there, as adding nothing new; their place is here (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 6.313–315; see the translation's note on 6.313–315).

**The escort.** At the queen's knees Odysseus asks for nothing but the escort: “But for me, speed my escort, so that I may reach my native land / the sooner, since long now I suffer woes far from my friends” (7.151–152; see [[pompe]] and [[supplication]]). Alcinous answers when the libation has been poured. At dawn the elders will entertain the stranger and sacrifice to the gods, and then they will remember his escort, “so that the stranger, without toil and vexation, / may reach his native land under our escort, / rejoicing, and quickly, even if he is from very far away” (7.191–194). The last verse is his daughter's, when she told Odysseus how to see the day of his homecoming (6.311–312), and Merry, comparing it, takes τηλόθεν ἐστί of the stranger, not of his land (on 7.194). The escort's care ends at the shore: “and there, after that, / he shall suffer whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth” (7.196–198; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]], [[aisa]] and [[fates]]).

**Home, then death.** Odysseus asks the leaders to make haste at dawn, “so that you may set me, unhappy man, on my native land, / even after my many sufferings; let life leave me, once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (7.223–225; see [[nostos]] and [[possessions]]). In the first book Athena told the gods that he longed to see even the smoke leaping up from his own land, and to die (1.57–59).

**Tomorrow, asleep.** After Arete's question and his story the king fixes the day: “And I ordain your escort for a set day, so that you may know it well: / for tomorrow; and then you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm, until you come / to your native land and your house” (7.317–320; see [[alcinous]]), however far it may be, though it be farther than Euboea (7.321–326; see [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]]). He would keep the stranger as his son-in-law, but no Phaeacian will keep him against his will (7.311–316), where Calypso held him by force (4.557–558; see [[calypso]]). The promise is kept in its own words: they spread his bed on the deck, he lies down and sleeps as they row, and they set him on the shore of Ithaca still overcome by sleep, δεδμημένον ὕπνῳ (13.73–80, 13.116–119, after 7.318; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**The prayer.** Odysseus prays that the king may keep his word: “Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said, / Alcinous! Then his fame over the grain-giving earth / would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land” (7.331–333; see [[prayer]], [[kleos]] and [[zeus]]). The king's fame would spread over the world, Merry explains, through the praise that Odysseus would give him in his gratitude (on 7.332).
