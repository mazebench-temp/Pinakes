summary: The Phaeacians “did not notice” the stranger in Athena's mist (7.39). Athena says of Arete that she “is in no way lacking in good sense” (νόου … ἐσθλοῦ, 7.73), and Odysseus says of her daughter that “she did not fall short of good sense” (νοήματος … ἐσθλοῦ, 7.292); he tells how Calypso let him go, “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (7.263).

**Noticing.** νοέω is first of all to perceive. The Phaeacians famed for ships “did not notice him / as he went through the city among them”, for Athena's mist was around him (7.39–42; see [[achlys]]); later he tells how he “noticed” Nausicaa's handmaids playing on the shore (ἐνόησα, 7.290). The ships of the Phaeacians are “swift as a wing or a thought” (νόημα, 7.36), a simile that the Iliad sets out at length in Hera's flight, quick as the thought of a much-traveled man (*Iliad* 15.80–83; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.36; see [[the-travelers-thought]]).

**Good sense, in mother and daughter.** The queen and the princess are praised in the same terms. Athena tells the stranger that Arete “is in no way lacking in good sense” (οὐ … νόου γε … δεύεται ἐσθλοῦ, 7.73), of herself and not only by reflection from the king (Merry on 7.73; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]); Odysseus tells her parents that Nausicaa “did not fall short of good sense” (οὔ τι νοήματος ἤμβροτεν ἐσθλοῦ, 7.292), as one would not expect of the young (see [[the-young-are-thoughtless]] and [[nausicaa]]). Her father finds one thing she “did not devise rightly” (ἐναίσιμον οὐκ ἐνόησε, 7.299; see [[aisa]]).

**A mind that turned.** Telling Arete of Ogygia, Odysseus gives two reasons why he was let go and does not choose between them: Calypso urged him to return, “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (ἢ καὶ νόος ἐτράπετʼ αὐτῆς, 7.262–263; see [[calypso]]). The poem has told both. Hermes brought Zeus' command (5.43–148), and Calypso told Odysseus only that she would send him with a ready heart, “for my mind too is right, and my own heart / in my breast is not of iron, but full of pity” (5.160–161, 5.190–191; see the Book 5 note). The message is ἀγγελίη, the singular as at *Iliad* 7.416 (see the translation's table of key words).
