summary: Athena tells the stranger that Alcinous “honored her as no other woman on earth is honored, / of all the women who now keep house under their husbands. / So she has been honored with all their hearts, and still is” (7.67–69), by her children, her husband and the people, who look on her as a god (7.70–72).
# ἔτισʼ, τίεται (7.67), τετίμηταί (7.69).
grc: ["ἔτισʼ", τίεται, τετίμηταί]
---

The book's τιμή belongs to a woman. In three verses Athena uses the verbs of honor, τίω and τιμάω, three times of Arete (7.67–69; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The measure is set against all wives, ὅσσαι νῦν γε γυναῖκες ὑπʼ ἀνδράσιν οἶκον ἔχουσιν, “of all the women who now keep house under their husbands” (7.68; see [[husbands-and-wives]]), and the honor is given by everyone around her: her dear children, Alcinous himself, and the people, “who look on her as a god / and greet her with words when she walks through the city” (7.70–72). The words are those in which Sarpedon describes the honor of the Lycian kings, “and all look on us as gods” (θεοὺς ὣς εἰσορόωσι, *Iliad* 12.312; see [[sarpedons-speech]]), and her husband has the same honor, for “the people listened to him as to a god” (7.11; see [[honored-like-a-god]] and [[alcinous]]). On Nauck's suspicion of τετίμηταί τε καὶ ἔστιν, see the translation's note on 7.69 and the queen's note. The honor is the ground of Athena's advice: if this woman thinks of him in friendship, he may hope to see his home (7.75–77; see [[nostos]]). In Book 11 Arete will tell the Phaeacian lords that the stranger is her guest, and that each of them has a share in the honor of it (11.338, a plain rendering).
