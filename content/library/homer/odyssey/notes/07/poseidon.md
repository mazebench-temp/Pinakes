summary: Poseidon is the ancestor of the house Odysseus enters and the god of his last storm. On the road Athena tells Odysseus that the earth-shaker granted the Phaeacians their crossing of the sea, and at the door that he begot Nausithous, the father of Rhexenor and Alcinous, on Periboea, the daughter of Eurymedon; in his story to Arete, the god's great-granddaughter, Odysseus names Poseidon as the god who roused the storm against him.
# Unnamed: "who roused the winds against me and bound up my path, / and stirred up the endless sea" (7.272-273).

  - 7.56-66 | The descent of the Phaeacian kings from Poseidon

**The god's gift.** On the road Athena tells Odysseus what the Phaeacians trust in: “Trusting in the speed of their swift ships, / they cross the great gulf, since the earth-shaker granted it to them; / their ships are swift as a wing or a thought” (7.34–36; see [[phaeacians]], [[earth-shaker]] and [[like-wings]]). δῶκε has no object expressed: the gift is what the verse describes, the crossing of the great gulf. The skill of their women at the loom is Athena's gift, told in the same words, πέρι γάρ σφισι δῶκεν Ἀθήνη after ἐπεί σφισι δῶκʼ ἐνοσίχθων (7.110; see [[athena]] and [[weaving]]). The god will resent the use his descendants make of his gift. At the end of Book 8 Alcinous recalls his father's saying that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians their safe escort of all men (8.564–569), and in Book 13 Poseidon complains to Zeus that the Phaeacians, ἐμῆς ἔξ … γενέθλης, of his own lineage, have carried Odysseus home asleep, and turns their ship to stone as it comes back close to their city (13.128–138, 13.159–164).

**The ancestor.** At the palace door Athena gives the queen's descent: “Nausithous first was born to Poseidon the earth-shaker / and Periboea, most beautiful in form of women, / the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon” (7.56–58), and when Eurymedon had destroyed his reckless people and himself, “Poseidon lay with her and begot a son, / great-hearted Nausithous, who was lord among the Phaeacians” (7.61–62; see [[nausithous]], [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]] and [[giants]]). Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous, and Rhexenor's only daughter, Arete, is Alcinous' wife (7.63–66), so that the king is the god's grandson and the queen his great-granddaughter; Merry draws the family tree (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.54; see [[alcinous]], [[rhexenor]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The Cyclops whom Odysseus has blinded is the god's son too, by the nymph Thoosa (1.68–73; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), and later in the book Alcinous sets his people beside the Cyclopes and the Giants as near to the gods (7.205–206; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]] and [[cyclopes]]).

**The storm, told to his kin.** In his story to Arete Odysseus tells how the mountains of Scheria appeared on the eighteenth day of his voyage, “and my dear heart rejoiced, / ill-fated as I was; for I was yet to keep company with misery, / much misery, which Poseidon the earth-shaker roused against me, / who roused the winds against me and bound up my path, / and stirred up the endless sea” (7.269–273; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[endless]]). The verses tell in brief the storm of Book 5, where the god gathered the clouds, took his trident and stirred the sea, and spurred on the storms of every wind (5.291–296). In κατέδησε κέλευθον, “bound up my path”, the god does to Odysseus what Athena did to the other winds in that storm, when she “bound up the paths of the other winds” (5.383). Merry, after Ellendt, reads κέλευθα, the word for a path through air or sea that has no fixed track, since Odysseus means only his progress, his way home being in his own conception uncertain and trackless; Ameis and La Roche read κέλευθον, as the text does (Merry on 7.272 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 7.272). Odysseus has learned from Athena at the door that the house descends from Poseidon (7.56–63). He names the god before his great-granddaughter, and does not say why the god is against him. The cause waits for the tale of Book 9, where the blinded Cyclops prays to his father that Odysseus may never reach home, or, if it is his fate to see his friends and reach his well-built house and his native land, that he may come late and in evil plight (9.526–535; see [[cholos]]). The Cyclops' verses are, nearly word for word, those of Zeus' decree at the second council of the gods (5.41–42) and of the hope that Nausicaa and Athena held out to Odysseus (6.314–315, 7.76–77).
