**The founder's family.** In the sixth book the poet told how Nausithous led the Phaeacians away from the Cyclopes and settled them in Scheria (6.4–12); in the seventh Athena, in the likeness of a young girl, tells Odysseus who the founder was, to explain who the queen is. Arete “comes of the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]):

{{quote:4a0e6190-5176-5f7f-9732-9c642bca8643}}

τοκῆες are her ancestors here: Alcinous is her uncle as well as her husband (see the translation's note on 7.54–55). The founder's father is the god of the sea, and his mother the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon, who “destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.60; see [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]], [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]] and [[giants]]). So the king who led his people away from the Cyclopes, who “used to plunder them, and were the stronger in strength” (6.6), was a son of Poseidon, as the Cyclops Polyphemus is (1.68–73; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), and a grandson of a king who brought his own people to ruin. Of his two sons, Rhexenor was struck down by Apollo in his hall, newly married and “when he had no son”, and left one daughter; Alcinous, who ruled after their father (6.12), made her his wife, and the line of Nausithous is joined again in the royal pair of the city he founded (7.63–66; see [[rhexenor]], [[apollo]] and [[alcinous]]).

**Near to the gods.** In his speech to the leaders Alcinous names together the two peoples of the founder's story. The gods appear openly to the Phaeacians, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (7.205–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]], [[cyclopes]] and [[giants]]): the Cyclopes were the neighbors Nausithous led his people away from (6.4–8), and the Giants the people of his mother's father (7.58–59). Merry reports that ἐγγύθεν, “near”, is generally taken here as “near-related”, like ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (5.35), while Welcker took it in its usual sense, of a people living next to the gods in a sort of celestial country; this agrees, Merry adds, with Nitzsch's remark that the distant nations on the borders of the world are shown as special favorites of the gods, as Poseidon visits the Ethiopians, “the farthest of men” (1.22–25). Against it he sets Hesiod, who makes the Giants and the Cyclopes, named here beside the Phaeacians, children of Earth, and Acusilaus and Alcaeus, who, he says, give the Phaeacians the same descent (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.205; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]] and [[ethiopians]]). The English “near to them” keeps both senses (see the translation's note on 7.205).
