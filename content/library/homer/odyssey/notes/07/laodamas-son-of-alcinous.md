**His chair.** Echeneus had bidden the king raise the stranger and seat him on a silver-studded chair (7.162–163; see [[echeneus]]), and Alcinous does so with the chair nearest his own: he “took wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles by the hand / and roused him from the hearth, and seated him on a shining chair, / making his son, manly Laodamas, stand up, / who sat beside him, and whom he loved most of all” (7.168–171; see [[alcinous]], [[throne]] and [[supplication]]). The same participle, ἀναστήσας, “raising up”, serves for the stranger in Echeneus' words and for the son in the king's act (7.163, 7.170): the son is made to stand so that the suppliant may sit. μάλιστα δέ μιν φιλέεσκε is a clause joined by the demonstrative instead of the relative, as of the old wool-comber who “used to work fine wool for her, and loved her most of all”, Helen (*Iliad* 3.387–388), and the subject of φιλέεσκε is his father (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.171).

On his epithet ἀγαπήνωρ, “manly”, given in the Odyssey to him alone, see [[manly]]. He is not named again in this book; at the games of the next day he is the best boxer and the first to challenge the stranger (8.117–157, Greek only; see the entry).
