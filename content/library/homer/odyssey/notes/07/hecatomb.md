---
summary: Alcinous tells the stranger that the gods have always appeared in plain sight to the Phaeacians “whenever we offer famous hecatombs”, and feast beside them (7.201–203).
---

“For always, before now, the gods have appeared in plain sight / to us, whenever we offer famous hecatombs, / and they feast beside us, sitting where we sit” (7.201–203; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[phaeacians]]). The hecatomb is the occasion of the gods' coming, as Poseidon went to the far-off Ethiopians “to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams” and took his delight sitting at the feast (1.22–26), and as Iris will not sit down in the house of the winds because she is going back to the Ethiopians, “where they are offering hecatombs / to the immortals, so that I too may feast among them on the sacrifices” (*Iliad* 23.205–207; see [[ethiopians]]). With the Phaeacians, a people who live apart at the edge of the world (6.204–205), the gods do what they do among the Ethiopians: they come to the feast and sit at it in person (see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). ἀγακλειτάς, “famous”, is the epithet of the Pylians' hecatomb to Poseidon in Athena's prayer, “for this famous hecatomb” (3.59; see [[famous]]). Alcinous has just promised sacrifices for the next day: at dawn the Phaeacians will entertain the stranger “and to the gods / offer fine sacrifices” (7.190–191; see [[sacrifice]]), and in Book 8 he sacrifices twelve sheep, eight boars and two oxen for the feast (8.59–61).
