**The first mother of the line.** To tell Odysseus who the queen is, Athena goes back to the beginning of the royal house: “Nausithous first was born to Poseidon the earth-shaker / and Periboea, most beautiful in form of women, / the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon, / who once was king over the high-hearted Giants” (7.56–59; see [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]] and [[giants]]). The king's fate breaks in before the mother's story is finished: “But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself. / And Poseidon lay with her and begot a son, / great-hearted Nausithous, who was lord among the Phaeacians” (7.60–62). Of the Giants' end the poem says no more. The words allow a foolhardy expedition against another tribe, a rising of the people against their king, or the king's own guilt; W. W. Merry thinks the first perhaps the likeliest, on the strength of the epithets ὑπερθύμοισι and ἀτάσθαλον (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59). τῇ δέ, “with her”, picks up Periboea after the parenthesis on her father (7.61).

The line that begins with her is the line of the queen as well as the king: Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous, and Arete is Rhexenor's daughter (7.63–66; see [[rhexenor]], [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). So Athena can say that Arete comes “of the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55; see the translation's note on 7.54–55).
