**“There is an island.”** Arete has asked who he is, where he comes from, and who gave him his clothes (7.237–239; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[who-are-you-among-men]]). He does not tell her his name; he answers with the story of his coming, which ends with the clothes (7.296), and begins far away: “There is an island, Ogygia, lying far off in the sea; / there the daughter of Atlas, wily Calypso, / lives, she of the lovely tresses, the dread goddess” (7.244–246; see [[calypso]] and [[atlas]]). Ὠγυγίη τις νῆσος, “an island” with its name, opens the tale as Menelaus opens his with Pharos: “Now there is an island in the sea of many waves” (4.354). That “no one mingles with her, / neither of the gods nor of mortal men” (7.246–247) is only a way of describing her lonely home far off in the sea, Merry explains, and he compares Nausicaa's words of the Phaeacians, who live “far apart in the sea of many waves”, so that no other mortal mingles with them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.247; 6.204–205; see [[phaeacians]]).

**The shipwreck and the stay.** After Zeus shattered his ship, Odysseus was borne nine days on the keel, “and on the tenth black night / the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso / lives” (7.253–255; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The scholia report that eight verses were athetized because the story is told later (12.447–453); Buttmann referred the remark to 7.251–258, and Merry finds 7.254–255 the most suspicious, since they repeat what Odysseus has just said (Merry's critical note on 7.251–258; see the translation's note on 7.251–258). On the island he stayed seven years, wetting with his tears the immortal garments Calypso gave him, until in the eighth year she sent him off on a raft (7.259–266; see [[raft]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). From Ogygia he sailed seventeen days before the mountains of the Phaeacians' land appeared (7.267–268), the reckoning of the fifth book (5.278–280; see [[scheria]]).
