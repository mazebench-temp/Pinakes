---
summary: The first book of the Aeneid follows the first half of this one. Venus wraps Aeneas in a cloud as Athena wraps Odysseus in mist, and goes home to her temple as Athena goes to hers; Aeneas walks unseen through a new city and marvels at it, and the cloud parts before the queen. Dido's house has fifty serving women within, as the house of Alcinous has fifty slave women, and Aeneas begins his tale to the queen as Odysseus begins his story to Arete. In the Georgics the woods of Alcinous are a kind of fruit tree.
---

- **The mist.** As Aeneas and Achates set out for Carthage, “Venus hedged them as they walked with dark air, and the goddess poured around them a thick mantle of cloud, so that no one could see them or touch them, or make delay, or ask why they had come” (*Aeneid* 1.411–414, a plain rendering), Virgil's imitation, Merry says, of the mist that Athena pours around Odysseus “lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him, / should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (7.14–17; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.15). Merry reports Nitzsch's remark that in the Iliad the gods use such a mist to hide themselves and in the Odyssey to hide their favorites, and notes the exception, Apollo, who protects Aeneas “in a dark cloud” (*Iliad* 5.344–345); Aphrodite too had hidden Paris “in thick mist” (*Iliad* 3.380–381). Then Venus goes off to Paphos, to her temple and its hundred altars (*Aeneid* 1.415–417), as Athena goes to Athens and enters the house of Erechtheus (7.78–81). Inside the cloud Aeneas marvels at the gates, the din and the paved streets of Carthage, and at the Tyrians building walls and digging harbors, and he goes through their midst “and is seen by no one” (*Aeneid* 1.419–440), as Odysseus, unnoticed by the Phaeacians, marvels at their harbors, ships, places of assembly and long walls (7.39–45). The cloud parts only when he stands before the queen (*Aeneid* 1.586–587), as “the god-sent mist flowed back” when Odysseus throws his arms around Arete's knees (7.142–143); but Aeneas names himself at once (*Aeneid* 1.595–596), and Odysseus does not: [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]], [[achlys]], [[thesphaton]] and [[virgils-dido-as-diana]].
- **Fifty serving women.** In Dido's house there are “fifty maidservants within, whose task is to lay out the long store in order and to honor the household gods with fire” (*Aeneid* 1.703–704, a plain rendering), which Merry sets beside the fifty slave women of Alcinous (7.103; Merry on 7.103): [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[handmaids]].
- **The queen's question.** At the end of the feast Dido asks Aeneas to tell “from the first beginning” the treachery of the Greeks, the fortunes of his people and his own wanderings (*Aeneid* 1.753–756), and his tale begins, *Infandum, regina, iubes renovare dolorem*, “Unspeakable, queen, is the grief you bid me renew” (*Aeneid* 2.3), which Merry compares with Odysseus' answer to Arete, “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows” (7.241–242; Merry on 7.241): [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[who-are-you-among-men]].
- **The gear of the feast.** For ἔντεα δαιτός, “the gear of the feast” that the handmaids clear away (7.232), Merry compares Virgil's name for the tools of milling and baking, *Cerealia arma*, “the arms of Ceres” (*Aeneid* 1.177; Merry on 7.232): [[feast]].
- **The woods of Alcinous.** *pomaque et Alcinoi silvae*, “apples and the woods of Alcinous”, among the kinds of trees that do not all grow alike (*Georgics* 2.83–88; on 7.112–132): [[the-garden-of-alcinous-in-later-writers]] and [[the-garden-of-alcinous]].
