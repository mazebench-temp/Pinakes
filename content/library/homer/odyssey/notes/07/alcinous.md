summary: The king receives the suppliant. His house is the goal of Odysseus' walk and the wonder of the book; at Echeneus' word he raises the stranger from the hearth and seats him in his favorite son's chair, has wine poured for Zeus, sends the leaders home and promises an escort for the next day, wondering whether the stranger is a god (7.167–206). After the stranger's story he blames his daughter, wishes such a man might be his son-in-law, and promises that his young men will row him home while he sleeps, as far as he likes (7.298–328); then he lies down beside the queen (7.346–347).

**The king of the book.** For the whole book, see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]. The first we hear of him is a captive woman picked out as his prize, “because over all / the Phaeacians he was lord, and the people listened to him as to a god” (7.10–11; see [[eurymedusa]]): θεοῦ δʼ ὣς, “the people hearkened to him as to a god” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.11). It is his house that Odysseus asks the girl with the water jar to show him, “the house of the man / Alcinous, who is lord among these people” (7.22–23), where the name explains “the man” (Merry on 7.25), and his house that the goddess brings him to, “the famous house of the king” (7.46; see [[athena]]). She tells him of the king's descent from Nausithous and of his marriage to his brother's daughter, whom he honors “as no other woman on earth is honored, / of all the women who now keep house under their husbands” (7.56–68; see [[nausithous]], [[rhexenor]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Then the house itself, with its bronze walls and golden doors, “the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous”, guarded by the gold and silver dogs of Hephaestus, its fifty slave women, its orchard and vineyard and two springs: “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (7.81–132; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[great-hearted]]).

**He raises the suppliant.** When Odysseus has clasped the queen's knees, naming “your husband” with them (7.147), and sat down in the ashes, the king does not move, and the hall is silent until the old Echeneus speaks to him by name: it is not more honorable than their custom to leave a stranger on the ground at the hearth; let him raise him and seat him on a chair, and have wine mixed for Zeus, who attends on revered suppliants (7.153–166; see [[echeneus]] and [[supplication]]). Then:

{{quote:6cce2284-a105-5a06-8233-db1df6ba7534}}

ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the sacred might of Alcinous” (7.167), names the king by his might, a periphrasis that recurs in Books 8 and 13, after ἱερὴ ἲς Τηλεμάχοιο (2.409; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]], [[hieros]] and [[sacred-strength-of-telemachus]], and the translation's formulas); μένος Ἀλκινόοιο alone, “the might of Alcinous”, introduces his order to the herald (7.178). On the chair of his favorite son, see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]. He has the stranger fed, and bids Pontonous mix a bowl so that they may pour a libation to Zeus, in the words that Echeneus used (7.172–183; see [[pontonous]]).

**The promise of an escort.** When they have poured and drunk, he speaks to the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians:

{{quote:8b1f3928-5909-55ed-b080-6f35de75f2f5}}

κατακείετε, “go home and lie down”, is taken by some as an aorist imperative, by others as a desiderative form (Merry on 7.188). ἐπί goes with καλέσαντες, “bidding them gather to the meeting” (Merry on 7.189; see the translation's note on 7.189 and [[elders]]). The subject of τηλόθεν ἐστί is the stranger, not the land, as the nearer parallel 6.312 suggests (Merry on 7.194), and μεσσηγύς is of space, “in between”, as a scholium explains it, between them and his city (Merry on 7.195; see the translation's note on 7.195). On the Spinners, see [[fates]] and the translation's note on 7.197; on the escort, [[pompe]]. Then the king wonders whether the stranger is a god:

“But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven, / then this is something else that the gods are devising” (7.199–200). The first verse, with the person changed, is the one with which Diomedes asks Glaucus whether he is a god (*Iliad* 6.128; Merry on 7.200). ἔπειτα draws the inference, “then this is some strange thing which the gods are intending towards us” (Merry on 7.199); ἄλλο τι is said euphemistically for some withdrawal of their favor, and it was the stranger's majestic look and his sudden appearance among them that put the thought into the king's mind (Merry on 7.200, citing 6.229–235). For the gods have always appeared to the Phaeacians in plain sight when they offer hecatombs, and feast beside them, and do not hide themselves even from a lone wayfarer, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (7.201–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]], [[cyclopes]] and [[giants]]); they appear, Merry explains, not only in public at the sacrifices but privately as well (on 7.204), and ἐγγύθεν, “near”, may be of kinship or of place (on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205). Odysseus answers that he may set his mind at rest: he is no god but a man of many sorrows (7.208–225; see [[odysseus]]). “Alcinous, let something else be on your mind” means that there is no ground for such anxiety as the king surmised (Merry on 7.208).

**His blame of his daughter.** After the stranger has told Arete his story, it is the king who answers: “Stranger, truly this, at least, she did not devise rightly, / my child, in that she did not bring you with her handmaids / to our house; and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.299–301; see [[nausicaa]]). The last clause gives the reason why she was wrong, “for it was to her first thou didst make thy supplication” (Merry on 7.301): a stranger was the suppliant of the person with whom he first established relations (Merry on 7.53; see the translation's note on 7.301). Odysseus takes the blame on himself, and the king answers: “Stranger, the dear heart in my breast is not such / as to be angry for no reason; what is due is better in all things” (7.309–310). The infinitive explains τοιοῦτον (Merry on 7.309), and ἀμείνω δʼ αἴσιμα πάντα is “measure in all things is best” (Merry on 7.310; see [[what-is-due-is-better]] and [[aisa]]).

**The offer.** Then the wish that answers his daughter's (6.244–245): “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, / being such a man as you are, and thinking as I do, / you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, / staying here; and I would give you a house and possessions, / if you stayed willingly; but against your will no one will keep you / of the Phaeacians: may that never be the pleasure of father Zeus” (7.311–316; see [[nausicaas-wish]], [[if-only-father-zeus]] and [[in-laws]]). “Thinking as I do” is “coming to an agreement with me”, agreeing to stay as the king would wish (Merry on 7.312); on δέ κʼ, Hermann's correction, see the translation's note on 7.314; and “may that never be the pleasure of father Zeus” is a prayer that no Phaeacian may detain him (Merry on 7.316). Aristarchus doubted whether the six verses were Homer's, and said that even if they were, they were rightly removed: how could the king, not knowing the man, betroth his daughter to him, and urge it on him so earnestly? (the scholia, in Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.311–316).

**The escort.**

{{quote:2fd3616a-a60b-51c5-ac1d-74470516b503}}

ἐς τόδʼ, “for a set day”, looks forward to αὔριον ἐς, “for tomorrow” (Merry on 7.317; on the division of the verse, see the translation's note on 7.317–318). The promise of sleep on the voyage comes true when the Phaeacians row him home (13.78–80, Greek only; see [[phaeacians]] and [[pompe]]). On Euboea, the farthest land his sailors know, and the voyage with Rhadamanthys to Tityus, see [[euboea]], [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[tityus]]. Odysseus answers with a prayer for him: “Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said, / Alcinous! Then his fame over the grain-giving earth / would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land” (7.331–333), his fame being spread through the world by the praise that Odysseus in his gratitude would give him (Merry on 7.332; see [[kleos]]).

**Rest.** When the stranger has gone to his bed in the colonnade, “Alcinous lay down in the inner part of the high house, / and beside him the lady his wife shared his bed and couch” (7.346–347), as Nestor slept at Pylos, “in the inner part of the high house” (3.402–403; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[house]]).

**His epithets in this book.** ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο and μένος Ἀλκινόοιο (7.167, 7.178; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (7.85, 7.93; see [[great-hearted]]); θεοειδής, “godlike” (7.231; see [[godlike]]); Ἀλκίνοος βασιλεύς, “King Alcinous” (7.55, 7.141; see [[kings]]); ἥρως, “hero”, in Odysseus' address (7.303; see [[heros]]).
