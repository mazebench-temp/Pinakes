summary: Chairs covered with fine cloths stand along the walls of Alcinous' hall, where the leaders of the Phaeacians sit and feast (7.95–99). Echeneus bids the king raise the suppliant from the hearth to a silver-studded chair, and Alcinous seats him on a shining chair beside his own, making his son Laodamas give it up (7.162–171).
# θρόνοι (7.95), the plural, first met here in the Odyssey; θρόνου (7.162, 7.169) is in the entry.
grc: [θρόνοι]
---

**The chairs of the hall.** “And within, chairs were set firm along the wall this way and that, / from the threshold right through to the inner part, and on them cloths, / fine and well-spun, were thrown, the work of women. / There the leaders of the Phaeacians used to sit, / drinking and eating; for they had unfailing store” (7.95–99; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[weaving]] and [[well-spun]]). ἐρηρέδατο, “were set firm”, is from ἐρείδω, “prop, fix” (see the translation's row ἐληλέδατο, ἐρηρέδατο); W. W. Merry notes that a scholium reads ἐληλέδατο here too, as in 7.86, but explains it as “were fixed in” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 7.95). The cloths laid on the chairs are like the rugs laid on a chair for Helen at Sparta (4.124; see [[blankets]]). In the Odyssey's houses of men θρόνος is “chair” (see the translation's row κλισμός, θρόνος).

**From the ashes to a chair.** The suppliant sits on the ground at the hearth, until the old Echeneus tells the king: “this is not more honorable, nor is it fitting, / that a stranger should sit on the ground at the hearth in the ashes”, and bids him “seat the stranger on a silver-studded chair, / raising him up” (7.159–163; see [[echeneus]], [[hearth]], [[silver-studded]] and [[supplication]]). Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand “and roused him from the hearth, and seated him on a shining chair, / making his son, manly Laodamas, stand up, / who sat beside him, and whom he loved most of all” (7.169–171; see [[alcinous]], [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]] and [[phaeinos]]). The stranger is raised from the ground to the seat beside the king, the seat of the son he loves most. When the others have gone, he is left in the hall with Arete and Alcinous sitting beside him (7.230–232).
