---
summary: Hesiod's poems touch the descent of the Phaeacian kings and the peoples Alcinous names as their neighbors among the gods. The Catalogue of Women made Arete the sister of Alcinous; the Theogony names the Cyclopes with the words of Arete's “name with a meaning”, makes the Cyclopes and the Giants children of Earth, which Merry weighs in reading the Phaeacians' nearness to the gods, and counts the Spinner among the three Fates who give mortals good and evil at their birth. The Works and Days dries the grapes in the sun, as some are dried in the vineyard of Alcinous.
---

- **Arete.** Athena says that Arete “comes of the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55), and then that she is the only child of his brother Rhexenor, whom Alcinous made his wife (7.63–66). The scholium on the first of these verses records another genealogy: “Hesiod took Arete to be the sister of Alcinous” (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 49 Evelyn-White, from the scholia on 7.54). Merry takes τοκῆες here for ancestors, since Alcinous is her uncle as well as her husband (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.54; see the translation's note on 7.54–55): [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[rhexenor]] and [[alcinous]].
- **A name with a meaning.** Athena's Ἀρήτη δʼ ὄνομʼ ἐστὶν ἐπώνυμον, “Arete is her name, a name with a meaning” (7.54), has its words in the Theogony, where the three Cyclopes are named for their single round eye: Κύκλωπες δʼ ὄνομʼ ἦσαν ἐπώνυμον, “Cyclopes was their name, a name with a meaning, because one round eye was set in their foreheads” (*Theogony* 144–145, a plain rendering). The Odyssey uses the phrase again when Autolycus names his grandson Odysseus (19.409, Greek only; Merry on 7.54): [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[cyclopes]].
- **Children of Earth.** Alcinous sets the Phaeacians beside the Cyclopes and “the savage tribes of the Giants” as peoples to whom the gods are near (7.205–206). In the Theogony both are children of Earth: she bore the Cyclopes, who gave Zeus the thunder and made the thunderbolt, “like the gods in all else”, but with one eye in the middle of the forehead (*Theogony* 139–143), and from the drops of blood of the castrated Uranus she bore the strong Erinyes and the great Giants, “gleaming in armor, holding long spears in their hands” (*Theogony* 183–186; plain renderings). Merry, weighing whether ἐγγύθεν means near in place or near in kin, sets this descent against the first view: the two peoples named as a parallel to the Phaeacians are Earth's children in Hesiod, and Acusilaus and Alcaeus, he reports, gave the Phaeacians the same descent (Merry on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205). Of Eurymedon's Giants (7.59) he says that Homer does not see them as Hesiod did, as serpent-footed monsters who tried to storm Olympus and were hurled back by the thunderbolts of Zeus, and cites Apollodorus 1.6.1 (Merry on 7.59); the Giants of the Theogony have no serpent feet and make no assault on heaven, and the story he describes is the one he cites from Apollodorus: [[giants]], [[cyclopes]] and [[phaeacians]].
- **The Spinners.** Once at home, Alcinous says, the stranger will suffer “whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth” (7.197–198). Hesiod's Fates are three, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, “who give mortals at their birth to have good and evil”: once among the children of Night, and again among the children of Zeus and Themis (*Theogony* 217–219, 904–906, a plain rendering). Merry notes that the three Moirai are post-Homeric and appear first in Hesiod, and that the κλῶθες of this verse are no more than “the half-personified agency of αἶσα” (Merry on 7.197; see the translation's note on 7.197): [[fates]] and [[aisa]].
- **The vintage.** In the vineyard of Alcinous one part, “a warm spot on level ground, / is dried by the sun, while other grapes they are gathering, / and others they are treading” (7.123–125). Hesiod's farmer, when Orion and Sirius have come into mid-heaven and Dawn sees Arcturus, picks all his grapes and brings them home, shows them to the sun for ten days and ten nights, shades them for five, and on the sixth draws off into jars the gifts of Dionysus (*Works and Days* 609–614, a plain rendering). Merry takes the grapes of Alcinous to be left on the vine to dry for raisin wine, rather than cut and hung up (Merry on 7.123; see the translation's note on 7.123–126): [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[the-vintage]].
- **Another Nausithous.** The Theogony ends its list of goddesses who lay with mortal men with Calypso, who “bore to Odysseus Nausithous and Nausinous” (*Theogony* 1017–1018, a plain rendering). The Nausithous of this book is another man, the son of Poseidon and Periboea, father of Rhexenor and Alcinous (7.56–63): [[nausithous]] and [[calypso]].
