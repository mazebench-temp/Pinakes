---
summary: Athena warns that the Phaeacians “do not much put up with strangers, / nor do they greet with open arms and make welcome whoever comes from elsewhere” (7.32–33), yet the stranger at the hearth is seated, washed, fed and given wine before anyone asks his name, and Alcinous promises to entertain him the next day and give him escort (7.162–196). Only when the guests have gone does Arete ask, “Who are you among men, and from where?” (7.238). He is ξεῖνος, “stranger”, from his first word in the book to the last word spoken to him (7.24, 7.342).
# ἀγαπαζόμενοι φιλέουσʼ (7.33), the welcome Athena says the Phaeacians do not give.
grc: ["ἀγαπαζόμενοι φιλέουσʼ"]
en: [greet with open arms and make welcome]
passages:
  - 7.30-36 | Athena's warning
  - 7.186-196 | Alcinous promises entertainment and escort
---

**A people that does not welcome strangers.** Athena, in the likeness of a girl, tells him to go in silence and to look at no one and question no one, “For these men do not much put up with strangers, / nor do they greet with open arms and make welcome whoever comes from elsewhere” (7.30–33; see [[athena]] and [[phaeacians]]). μάλα goes with οὐ and the verb: they are “not over and above fond of” strangers, which means not fond of them at all, and οὐκ ἀνέχονται, “they cannot put up with”, is stronger than “they do not entertain” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.32). ἀγαπάζομαι is “greet with open arms”, and φιλέω of a guest is to make him welcome, as when Menelaus' attendant Eteoneus asks whether the strangers at the gate should be sent on to some other man, “who will make them welcome” (4.28–29; see the translation's table of key words). Nitzsch found the warning at odds with what Nausicaa says of her people (6.207–208), with the old Echeneus' words and with the king's (7.159–166, 7.186–196), and explained it as the thought of a wary traveler entering a strange city; Merry thought it simpler that Athena speaks of the ordinary Phaeacians a stranger would meet in the street, who, by Nausicaa's showing in Book 6, could make themselves very unpleasant (Merry on 7.32; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). The mist keeps him from them, lest any Phaeacian “should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (7.16–17; see [[achlys]]).

**The stranger.** ξεῖνος is his name in the book from beginning to end. He calls himself “a much-tried stranger … from a distant land” (7.24–25; see [[much-tried]] and [[distant-land]]), and the goddess in the likeness of a young girl answers him ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father”, the respectful address of the young to an older man (7.28, 7.48; see the translation's table of key words and [[pater]]). Echeneus speaks of “a stranger” on the ground at the hearth (7.160, 7.162), the housekeeper is to give “the stranger” supper (7.166), and the Phaeacians bid “send the stranger on his way” (7.227). Arete, Alcinous and the handmaids address him as ξεῖνε (7.237, 7.299, 7.309, 7.342). He is a stranger and not yet a guest-friend: the guest-gift that makes the bond is proposed by Alcinous in Book 8, “as is fitting” (8.389, a plain rendering; see [[guest-gifts]]).

**Fed before he is asked.** The order of Nestor's house holds here: the strangers are fed first and asked afterwards who they are (3.69–71). The king raises the suppliant and seats him; water for his hands, a table, bread and dainties are set before him, wine is mixed and the libation poured (7.167–184; see [[supplication]] and [[water-for-the-hands]]). He has asked for nothing but his escort (7.151–152), and when he speaks again it is to ask leave to eat (7.215–221; see [[gaster]]). The question of his name waits until the guests have gone home: “Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (7.238; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). He answers it in part, with the story from Ogygia to the shore of Scheria, and gives his name only in Book 9 (9.19).

**Entertainment and escort.** Alcinous sends the leaders home with the plan for the next day: “and at dawn we will call more of the elders / and entertain the stranger in the halls, and to the gods / offer fine sacrifices, and then we will remember his escort too” (7.189–191; see [[elders]] and [[pompe]]). ξεινίζω, “entertain”, is the host's verb. The escort will take him home “without toil and vexation … rejoicing, and quickly, even if he is from very far away” (7.192–194). When the king has heard the stranger's story he offers him more, a house and possessions if he will stay as his son-in-law, and adds the host's limit: “but against your will no one will keep you / of the Phaeacians” (7.313–316; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[what-is-due-is-better]]). The night ends with the guest's bed in the colonnade, made by the queen's handmaids in the verses with which Helen has beds made for Telemachus and Peisistratus (7.335–342; 7.336–339 = 4.297–300; see [[bed]]).
