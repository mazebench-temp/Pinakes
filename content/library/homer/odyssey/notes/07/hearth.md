summary: When he has clasped Arete's knees, Odysseus sits down at the hearth in the ashes, beside the fire, and all are silent, until the old Echeneus tells the king that it is not fitting for a stranger to sit on the ground at the hearth, and Alcinous raises him from it to a chair (7.153–171). Telling his story, he says that a god brought him to Calypso's hearth (7.248).

**In the ashes.** When he has made his prayer at the queen's knees, the suppliant goes to the hearth: “He spoke thus and sat down at the hearth in the ashes / beside the fire; and they all fell hushed in silence” (7.153–154; see [[supplication]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[fire]]). It is the hearth at which Nausicaa had told him her mother would be sitting, “in the light of the fire” (6.305; see the Book 6 note). He sits there in the posture of a suppliant, W. W. Merry explains, though, as Ameis reminds us, Homer connects no distinct notion of sanctity or sanctuary with the hearth (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.153). In later writers the suppliant takes refuge at the ἑστία: Medea and Jason sit at the hearth of Circe in Apollonius' *Argonautica* (4.693, as Merry quotes it), and Themistocles throws himself down at the hearth of Admetus in Plutarch's life (as Merry cites it); in Herodotus Croesus asks the Phrygian Adrastus, who has come to him to be purified, from where he has come “to be a suppliant at my hearth”, ἐπίστιος (Herodotus 1.35.3). These passages, Merry says, show that ἑστία implies far more than ἐσχάρα (on 7.153; see the entry).

**Raised from the hearth.** The old Echeneus breaks the silence: “Alcinous, this is not more honorable, nor is it fitting, / that a stranger should sit on the ground at the hearth in the ashes” (7.159–160; see [[echeneus]]). κάλλιον, “more honorable”, means that this unusual way of treating a stranger is no better than their ordinary custom of welcoming him (Merry on 7.159; see the translation's note on 7.159). Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand, “and roused him from the hearth, and seated him on a shining chair” (7.169; see [[alcinous]] and [[throne]]); ἐσχαρόφιν is the form of the o-declension made for the meter, as at Calypso's hearth (5.59; Merry on 5.59).

**To her hearth.** In his story to Arete, Odysseus says of Calypso: “But me, unhappy man, a god brought to her hearth, / alone” (7.248–249; see [[calypso]] and [[daimon]]). ἐφέστιον goes with ἤγαγε, “brought me to be her guest”, as a scholium explains it (Merry on 7.248), and the adjective is the one of Athena's words at Pylos, in the likeness of Mentor, that she would rather come home after many sorrows than “come home and perish at my own hearth, as Agamemnon / perished” (3.234–235; see the translation's row ἐφέστιος). Eurycleia will tell Penelope that Odysseus has come home alive to his hearth (23.55, a plain rendering).
