Of the fifty slave women in the house of Alcinous, some grind at the mills, “and others weave at the looms and turn the yarn / as they sit” (7.105–106; see [[mill]], [[distaff]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). Then the poet sets the women's craft beside the men's:

{{quote:a4585156-9a05-50ef-96a9-8ea09aea604e}}

ἱστῶν τεχνῆσσαι, “skilled at the loom”, answers ἴδριες, “skilled”, as ἱστῶν answers νῆα … ἐλαυνέμεν, “in driving a swift ship” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.110). The genitive plural is the reading that La Roche took from two Vienna manuscripts; the vulgate has ἱστὸν … τεχνῆσαι, an infinitive that would make the antithesis to ἐλαυνέμεν, but Homer elsewhere has only the middle τεχνάομαι (Merry's critical note and on 7.110; see the translation's note on 7.110). The word that names the loom also names the mast (see [[mast]]), and here the two crafts stand side by side: the Phaeacians care for “masts and the oars of ships” (6.271; see [[phaeacians]]), and their women for the loom.
