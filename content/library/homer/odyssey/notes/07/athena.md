summary: Athena brings Odysseus to the house of Alcinous unseen. She pours a thick mist around him, meets him at the city in the likeness of a young girl carrying a water jar, leads him and warns him that the Phaeacians do not welcome strangers, and at the door tells him to find the queen first and gives him Arete's descent; then she goes away over the sea to Marathon and Athens. Her mist lasts until he clasps Arete's knees. In the account of the house she is the giver of the Phaeacian women's skill at the loom.

**The mist.** The book's first act after Nausicaa's homecoming is the goddess's (see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]):

{{quote:281f22d9-15d1-5a46-96a8-0eec4a5ecc21}}

The text and the English follow ἀμφὶ δʼ Ἀθήνη, the reading of two manuscripts and of Macrobius, where Merry prints αὐτὰρ Ἀθήνη (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.14). πολλὴν ἠέρα, “a thick mist”, is the mist in which the gods of the Iliad hide their favorites to take them out of battle: Aphrodite snatches Paris away “and hid him in thick mist”, ἐκάλυψε δʼ ἄρʼ ἠέρι πολλῇ, and Apollo does the same for Hector and for Agenor (*Iliad* 3.380–381, 20.443–444, 21.596–597; see [[divine-rescue]]). Here it lets a stranger walk through a city of men without being stopped: “the Phaeacians famed for ships did not notice him / as he went through the city among them; for Athena / of the lovely tresses, the dread goddess, would not allow it” (7.39–41; see [[phaeacians]], [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). ἀχλὺν θεσπεσίην, “a heaven-sent mist” (7.41–42), is the mist that Poseidon scatters from the eyes of Achilles (*Iliad* 20.341–342; see [[achlys]] and the translation's key-word row). Zenodotus read ἥ σφισιν, as if the Phaeacians were covered, and the scholia reject it, since only Odysseus is wrapped in it (Merry's critical note on 7.41; see the translation's note on 7.41). On Ithaca she pours a mist around again, so that Odysseus does not know his own land and she can make him unknown (13.187–196). Merry cites Virgil's imitation (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.15): Venus wraps Aeneas and Achates in a dark mist as they walk to Carthage, so that no one may see them or touch them or ask why they have come (*Aeneid* 1.411–414), and the cloud parts when Aeneas stands before the queen (*Aeneid* 1.586–589).

ἐυπλόκαμος, “of the lovely tresses”, is said of Athena only here; in the Iliad she is ἠύκομος, “of the lovely hair” (Merry on 7.41; *Iliad* 6.92, 6.273, 6.303). The verse in which the Trojan women appease her has both words, the first of the women: Τρῳαὶ ἐϋπλόκαμοι δεινὴν θεὸν ἱλάσκονται, “Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess” (*Iliad* 6.380, 6.385). Later in the book the same pair, ἐυπλόκαμος, δεινὴ θεός, belongs to Calypso (7.246, 7.255), and in later books to Circe (10.136, 11.8, 12.150; see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[dread-goddess]]). φίλα φρονέουσα, “in friendship” (7.15, 7.42), is her motive twice, and at the palace door she tells him that his homecoming hangs on the same thing in the queen (7.75; see the translation's key-word row).

**The girl with the water jar.** At the edge of the city “there the gray-eyed goddess Athena met him, / like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar” (7.19–20; see [[gray-eyed]]). παρθενικῇ may be an adjective with νεήνιδι or a noun in apposition to it (Merry on 7.20); κάλπις, “water jar”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row). She keeps up the part. He calls her “My child” (7.22); she calls him ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father”, the respectful address of the young to an older man (7.28, 7.48; see the translation's key-word row), and gives herself a noble father, a neighbor of the king (7.29). In his tale Odysseus' men meet such a girl before the city of the Laestrygonians, the king's daughter drawing water at a spring, and she shows them her father's high-roofed house (10.105–111).

**On the road.** She bids him go in silence and question no one, “For these men do not much put up with strangers, / nor do they greet with open arms and make welcome whoever comes from elsewhere” (7.32–33; see [[xenia]]); their trust is in their ships, “since the earth-shaker granted it to them” (7.35; see [[poseidon]] and [[like-wings]]). Then “Pallas Athena led the way / swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess” (7.37–38), the verses in which Telemachus follows her, in the likeness of Mentor, down to the ship and to the gathering of the Pylians (2.405–406, 3.29–30; see [[followed-in-the-footprints]] and [[pallas]]).

**At the door.** “This, stranger, father, is the house that you bid me / show you” (7.48–49). She tells him to go in without fear, “for a bold man is better in all / deeds, even if he comes from some other place” (7.51–52; see [[tharsos]]), and to find the queen first, “First you will find the lady in the halls” (7.53). Then she gives Arete's descent from Poseidon through Nausithous and his son Rhexenor, whom Apollo struck down, and the honor in which Alcinous, her children and the people hold her (7.54–74; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[nausithous]], [[rhexenor]] and [[alcinous]]). Nausicaa had given him the same counsel, to pass her father by and throw his arms around her mother's knees (6.303–312), and Athena ends with the girl's last three verses, “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77), with “high-roofed” where Nausicaa said “well-built” (6.313–315). Several manuscripts lack the verses in Book 6, and modern editors generally reject them there; their place is here (see the translation's note on 6.313–315 and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). With that word her last verse is, but for the pronoun, the one in which Zeus declared Odysseus' fate at the second council of the gods, “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42).

**To Athens.** “So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away / over the barren sea, and left lovely Scheria, / and came to Marathon and to wide-streeted Athens, / and entered the well-built house of Erechtheus” (7.78–81; see [[scheria]], [[marathon]], [[athens]] and [[erechtheus]]). The first verse is the one with which she left Nestor in the likeness of a sea eagle, when all who saw it were amazed and Nestor named her (3.371–379; see [[divine-epiphany]]); in Book 6 she went to Olympus (6.41–42). Ἀθήνην at 7.80 is the city. In the Catalogue Athena raised Erechtheus and set him down “in Athens, in her own rich temple” (*Iliad* 2.549); here she enters his house, which Merry takes for the temple on the Acropolis where the two were worshiped together (on 7.81). The scholia report that the passage was suspected, and Merry suggests that a later rhapsode may have added it to gratify Athenian feeling (Merry's critical note on 7.79–80; see the translation's note on 7.79–80 and [[athena-and-erechtheus]]).

**The mist lifted.** She does not go into the palace with him; her mist does. He goes through the house “wrapped in the thick mist that Athena had poured around him, / until he came to Arete and King Alcinous”, throws his arms around the queen's knees, “and then the god-sent mist flowed back from the man himself” (7.140–143; see [[thesphaton]] and [[supplication]]), and the company “fell silent when they saw the man in the house, / and marveled as they looked” (7.144–145; see [[thambos]]). The mist that kept him from being questioned in the street (7.17) has brought him unhindered to the place of the suppliant, and leaves him there.

**The women's skill.** In the account of the house, the Phaeacian women are as skilled at the loom as the men at sailing, “for Athena has given them above all others / to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits” (7.110–111; see [[weaving]] and [[loom]]). πέρι γάρ σφισι δῶκεν Ἀθήνη answers the words she spoke herself of the men's ships, ἐπεί σφισι δῶκʼ ἐνοσίχθων, “since the earth-shaker granted it to them” (7.35): the seafaring of the Phaeacians is Poseidon's gift, their women's work hers. The second verse names the gift she gave Penelope (2.116–117; see [[penelope]]), and she taught the daughters of Pandareus to make glorious works (20.72; see the general article).

**Not in his story.** When Odysseus tells Arete how he came to Scheria, Athena has no part in it. The presence of mind she gave him on the rocks (5.436–437) is in his telling his own act, “but I drew back and swam out again” (7.280), and the sleep she poured on his eyes (5.491–493) is the work of “a god” (7.286; see [[hypnos]] and [[daimon]]). Alcinous names her in his wish, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (7.311; see [[if-only-father-zeus]]). On Ithaca, when she has made herself known, she tells him that she made him dear to all the Phaeacians (13.302), and he answers that he never saw her after Troy until in the land of the Phaeacians she encouraged him with words and led him into the city herself (13.316–323).

**Her names.** θεά, γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “the gray-eyed goddess Athena” (7.19, 7.27, 7.47), and γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη (7.78); Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη (7.37); Ἀθήνη alone (7.14, 7.40, 7.110, 7.140); ἐυπλόκαμος, δεινὴ θεός (7.41); Ἀθηναίη (7.311). See [[gray-eyed]], [[pallas]] and [[dread-goddess]].
