**A god or a mortal?** The stranger has appeared out of nowhere at the queen's knees, and Alcinous ends his promise of an escort with a doubt: “But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven, / then this is something else that the gods are devising” (7.199–200). ἄλλο τι is a euphemism for some withdrawal of the gods' favor; what suggested the thought was the stranger's majestic looks and his sudden appearance among them, the beauty Athena had poured on him at the river (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.200; 6.229–235). The Phaeacians have reason to ask, for the gods feast among them openly and do not hide themselves from a lone wayfarer (7.201–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[phaeacians]]). Odysseus denies it in the terms of the difference: he is not like the immortals, “who hold the wide sky”, in form or stature, but like mortal men, and among men like those who bear the most misery (7.208–212; see [[immortals]], [[mortal-men]] and [[eidos]]). Then he asks leave to eat, as no god needs to (7.215–221; see [[gaster]]).

**The offer told.** To Arete he tells of Calypso, “who took me in / and loved me with care and cared for me, and said / that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days; / but she never persuaded the heart in my breast” (7.255–258; see [[calypso]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The verses are those of Calypso's protest to Hermes, now told by the man she spoke of (7.256–257, after 5.135–136), with λαβοῦσα, “took me in”, an unusual word for a hostess, which probably implies that she kept him (Merry on 7.255). In Book 5 the refusal was spoken to her face, with the grant that she was immortal and ageless and his wife was not (5.215–220; see the Book 5 note); here he tells it to the queen, in the verse that comes again in his tale to Penelope (23.335–337), and the goddess's gift, the garments that are ἄμβροτα, “immortal”, are the garments that, he says, “I kept wet with my tears” for seven years (7.259–260; see [[tears]] and the translation's table of key words). The scholia report that the eight verses 7.251–258 were athetized, since Odysseus tells the story again in Book 12 and says there that he has told it already (12.447–453); Merry finds 7.254–255 the most suspicious (see the translation's note on 7.251–258).
