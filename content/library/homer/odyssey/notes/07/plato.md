---
summary: Two passages of Plato stand in Merry's notes on this book. Socrates' “the ancients, who were better than we and dwelt nearer to the gods” is set beside the Phaeacians, to whom the gods are near; and Agathon's Love, who “is always with the young, and is young”, beside the construction of Arete's honor.
---

- **Nearer to the gods.** Socrates tells of a gift of the gods flung down to men by some Prometheus with a fire most bright, and of the report handed down by “the ancients, who were better than we and dwelt nearer to the gods”, οἱ μὲν παλαιοί, κρείττονες ἡμῶν καὶ ἐγγυτέρω θεῶν οἰκοῦντες, that the things which are said to be are made of one and many (*Philebus* 16c, a plain rendering). Merry sets the words beside Alcinous' “since we are near to them”, ἐπεί σφισιν ἐγγύθεν εἰμέν (7.205), with Herodotus' saying on the ends of the earth, for the view that the nearness is one of place and not of kin (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205): [[phaeacians]], [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[herodotus]].
- **Honored, and still is.** Athena says of Arete, ὣς κείνη περὶ κῆρι τετίμηταί τε καὶ ἔστιν, “So she has been honored with all their hearts, and still is” (7.69). Merry finds the same turn in Agathon's praise of Love, who flees old age, μετὰ δὲ νέων ἀεὶ σύνεστί τε καὶ ἔστιν, “but with the young he is always together, and is young” (*Symposium* 195b, a plain rendering), where the second verb takes its complement from what goes before; he adds Nitzsch's parallel from Propertius and his own suggestion that περί is to be understood again, “is honored above all, as she is above all” (Merry on 7.69). Nauck called the words corrupt, though the manuscripts give no variant (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.69): [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[propertius]].
