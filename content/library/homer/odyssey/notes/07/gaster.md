summary: Odysseus answers Alcinous' wonder whether he is a god: he is a mortal, whose sorrows are the most of all men's, and he asks leave to eat, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly, / which bids a man remember it under compulsion, / even one sorely worn down and with grief in his heart” (7.208–221).
# γαστέρι (7.216) is in the entry.
passages:

**Not a god.** Alcinous has wondered aloud whether the stranger is one of the immortals come down from heaven, since the gods appear openly to the Phaeacians (7.199–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Odysseus turns the thought aside, ἄλλο τί τοι μελέτω φρεσίν, “let something else be on your mind” (7.208), that is, there is no such ground for anxiety as the king supposes (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.208): he is not like the immortals “in form or stature, but like mortal men”, and like the men who bear the most misery (7.208–214; see [[immortality]], [[eidos]] and [[algos]]). Then he gives the proof that ends his answer. The gods do not eat bread (*Iliad* 5.341–342); he must eat, grieved though he is.

{{quote:5a3110b9-b503-5237-85d2-e82a04a8d54d}}

**The belly.** ἐπὶ γαστέρι is “beyond the belly”, a formula of comparison: nothing is more shameless than it (Merry on 7.216, comparing Herodotus 4.118.3 and Thucydides 3.45). κύντερον, “more shameless”, is literally “more doglike”, the comparative that Zeus uses of Hera (*Iliad* 8.483) and the shade of Agamemnon of a murderous wife (11.427; see the entry and [[dogs]]). The belly is spoken of as a master. ἔπλετο and ἐκέλευσεν are aorists of custom, what it has always done (Merry on 7.217), and the verbs that follow are its commands: it bids him remember it “under compulsion” (ἀνάγκῃ), bids him eat and drink, “makes me forget / all that I have suffered”, ἐκ … ληθάνει, a causative form of the verb (Merry on 7.220), and bids him take his fill (7.217–221; on ἐνιπλησθῆναι, Aristarchus' reading, see the translation's note on 7.221). The grief is real, πένθος in his heart, and he says it twice (7.218–219; see [[penthos]]); the belly overrules it. It is the argument he gave Achilles at Troy, that the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly, and must remember food and drink so as to fight (*Iliad* 19.225–233; see [[fasting]]). There it was a rule for an army; here it is his own need, as the hungry lion of Book 6 went where “his belly bids him” (6.133; see [[the-hungry-lion]]).

**The rest of the request.** He has asked to eat, and he asks for one thing more: “But you, make haste when dawn appears, / so that you may set me, unhappy man, on my native land, / even after my many sufferings; let life leave me, once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (7.222–225; see [[pompe]] and [[nostos]]). The last verse is the one with which Achilles, mourning Patroclus, names what his friend was to have shown his son in Phthia (7.225 = *Iliad* 19.333). The Phaeacians approve, “since he had spoken fair and right” (7.226–227; see [[moira]]), and the belly that he blamed will be the beggar's master in the second half of the poem (see the entry).
