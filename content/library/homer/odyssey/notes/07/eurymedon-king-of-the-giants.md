**The ancestor.** Athena tells Odysseus who the queen is by going back four generations: Nausithous was born to Poseidon and Periboea, “the youngest daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon” (7.56–58; see [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[nausithous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The next two verses belong to Eurymedon himself: he “once was king over the high-hearted Giants. / But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.59–60; see [[giants]]). Then the genealogy takes up Periboea again, “And Poseidon lay with her and begot a son” (7.61; see [[poseidon]]).

**What he did.** The words allow three readings, Merry thinks: the Giants and their king perished in some foolhardy expedition against another tribe; or a rising of the people against their king ended in ruin for both; or Eurymedon's own guilt brought destruction on himself and his people. He thinks the first the likeliest, because of the epithets ὑπερθύμοισι, “high-hearted”, and ἀτάσθαλον, “reckless” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59; see [[high-hearted]]). ἀτάσθαλος is the word of the poem's opening, where Odysseus' companions “perished through their own recklessness” (1.7). μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, may be a standing epithet, but Merry suggests that it can also mean “arrogant” or “haughty”, and cites Eurymedon when the word is given to the man-eating Cyclops (10.200; Merry on 10.200; see [[great-hearted]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Pausanias, as Merry quotes him, took the two verses as the clearest proof that the Giants were mortal (see the general article).
