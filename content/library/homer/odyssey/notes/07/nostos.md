summary: The book's speeches keep returning to the homecoming. Athena repeats the hope that if Arete is kind he will reach “your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77); the suppliant asks for his escort, “so that I may reach my native land / the sooner” (7.151–152); Alcinous promises that he will reach it unharmed (7.192–196); and Odysseus would die content, “once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (7.224–225). He tells how Calypso bade him return (7.262), and ends the evening with a prayer to reach his native land (7.333).
# οἴκαδʼ at 7.188 is the leaders going to their houses to sleep, not a homecoming. At 7.326 it is the Phaeacian crew
# coming home from Euboea the same day.

**The hope repeated.** Athena ends her account of Arete with Nausicaa's promise: “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (7.75–77), with “high-roofed” where Book 6 had “well-built” (see the translation's note on 6.313–315 and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). The book is spent in the house of the people who can grant it.

**Asked and promised.** At the queen's knees he asks for nothing but the voyage, πομπὴν ὀτρύνετε πατρίδʼ ἱκέσθαι / θᾶσσον, “speed my escort, so that I may reach my native land / the sooner, since long now I suffer woes far from my friends” (7.151–152; see [[pompe]] and [[supplication]]). The king promises that he “may reach his native land under our escort, / rejoicing, and quickly … before he sets foot on his own land” (7.193–196), and after the stranger's story fixes the day: they will row him “until you come / to your native land and your house, or wherever it is your pleasure” (7.319–320; see [[dear-native-land]]).

**Home and then death.** Odysseus asks only that it be soon: “But you, make haste when dawn appears, / so that you may set me, unhappy man, on my native land, / even after my many sufferings; let life leave me, once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (7.222–225). καὶ λίποι, “let life leave me”, makes life's leaving the worst thing that could happen, and still a price he would pay, as the wounded Sarpedon asks Hector to defend him and afterward let life leave him in the city of Troy, since he was not to come home (*Iliad* 5.684–688; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.224). The last verse is the one with which Achilles names what Patroclus was to have shown his son in Phthia (7.225 = *Iliad* 19.333; see [[gaster]]).

**The goddess who let him go, and the king who would keep him.** He tells Arete that in the eighth year Calypso “urged me and bade me return” (νέεσθαι, 7.262), “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (7.263; see [[calypso]] and [[noos]]). Alcinous, having heard the story, wishes that such a man might stay as his son-in-law, and in the same breath gives up the wish, since no Phaeacian will keep him against his will (7.311–316; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[xenia]]). The Phaeacians' own voyages end at home on the same day, as when they came back from Euboea (οἴκαδʼ, 7.326; see [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]]). Odysseus prays that Zeus may let the king accomplish all he has said: then the king's fame would be unquenchable, “and I would reach my native land” (7.331–333; see [[kleos]] and [[prayer]]).
