summary: Athena bids the stranger go through the city “in silence” (σιγῇ, 7.30); when the mist lifts at the queen's knees, the Phaeacians “fell silent when they saw the man in the house” (ἄνεῳ, 7.144), and after his plea “they all fell hushed in silence” (7.154), until the old Echeneus spoke “at last” (7.155).
# σιγῇ (7.30), ἄνεῳ (7.144) and ἀκήν (7.154) are in the entry and its Odyssey notes.
---

**The stranger's silence.** Athena's first instruction is to keep quiet: “But go on in silence, just so, and I will lead the way, / and do not look at any man or question anyone” (7.30–31; see [[athena]] and [[xenia]]). The mist serves the same purpose, so that no Phaeacian may meet him and “ask him who he was” (7.16–17; see [[achlys]]).

**The hall's silence.** Two silences fall in the hall. The first is surprise: when the mist flows back, “they fell silent when they saw the man in the house, / and marveled as they looked” (7.144–145; see [[thambos]]). ἄνεῳ is the word of men struck dumb, as when Calchas asks the Achaeans at Aulis why they have fallen silent at the portent (*Iliad* 2.323), or the Achaeans sit silent and grieving when Agamemnon proposes flight (*Iliad* 9.29–30); Aristarchus took it as an adverb (see the translation's note on 7.144–145). The second is the silence of a hall that waits for its king. The stranger has made his plea and sat down in the ashes, “and they all fell hushed in silence” (7.154), the verse with which the Iliad marks a hard word that no one will answer, as when Hector proposes the duel or Achilles refuses the embassy (*Iliad* 7.92, 9.430). “These here, waiting for your word, are holding themselves back”, the old man tells the king when he breaks it (7.161; see [[echeneus]] and [[kings]]). In Book 11 the same verse follows the stranger's tale of the dead, and the silence is broken by Arete and then by Echeneus (11.333–346).
