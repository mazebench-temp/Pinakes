---
summary: In the Argonautica Medea and Jason, come to Circe as suppliants, refuse the shining chairs she offers and sit at her hearth in silence, “as is the way for wretched suppliants”; Merry sets the scene beside Odysseus in the ashes of Alcinous' hearth.
---

- **Suppliants at the hearth.** Circe “bade them sit on shining chairs”, but the two, “silent and speechless, darted to the hearth and sat down, as is the way for wretched suppliants” (τὼ δʼ ἄνεῳ καὶ ἄναυδοι ἐφʼ ἑστίῃ ἀΐξαντες / ἵζανον, ἥ τε δίκη λυγροῖς ἱκέτῃσι τέτυκται, *Argonautica* 4.693, as Merry quotes it; plain renderings). Homer's scene goes the other way. Odysseus sits down in the ashes unbidden; it is the company who fall silent, ἄνεῳ, when the mist flows back from him at the queen's knees (7.144), and are hushed again when he sits by the fire (7.154); and the shining chair comes after the hearth, when the king raises him from it (7.169). Merry notes, after Ameis, that Homer connects no distinct notion of sanctity or sanctuary with the hearth, and that the ἑστία of Apollonius and of later writers implies far more than Homer's ἐσχάρη (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.153): [[hearth]] and [[supplication]].
