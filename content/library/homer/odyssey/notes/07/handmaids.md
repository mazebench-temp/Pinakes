**The house's women.** The first of them in the book is Nausicaa's old chamber-woman, Eurymedusa, a captive brought by ship from Apeire and picked out as a prize for the king, who reared the princess and lights her fire (7.7–13; see [[eurymedusa]]). Among the wonders of the house the narrator counts “fifty slave women in the house: / some grind the yellow grain at the mills, / and others weave at the looms and turn the yarn / as they sit, like the leaves of a tall poplar” (7.103–106; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[mill]], [[loom]], [[distaff]] and [[like-the-leaves-of-a-poplar]]). The house of Odysseus has the same number (22.421; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.103). The Phaeacian women are as skilled at the loom as their men at driving a ship on the sea, by the gift of Athena (7.108–111; see [[phaeacians]] and [[weaving]]).

**The meal.** Echeneus bids the king let “the housekeeper give the stranger supper from the stores within” (7.166), and when the stranger is seated, the house's women serve him in the verses with which guests are served in the halls of the Odyssey, at Ithaca, at Sparta and later on Circe's island: “And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it, / a fine golden one, over a silver basin, / for them to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside them. / And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them, / adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had” (7.172–176, as 1.136–140, 4.52–56, 10.368–372; see [[water-for-the-hands]], [[basin-and-pitcher]] and [[table]]). After the guests have gone, “the handmaids cleared away the gear of the feast” (7.232; see [[feast]]): ἔντεα are here the implements of the meal (Merry on 7.232), and a scholium transmitted at 7.174, which objects that Homer does not have the tables taken away while the banqueters are present, Merry would assign to this verse (see the translation's note on 7.174).

**The queen's women.** The mantle and the tunic that Arete knows on the stranger's back are those “which she herself had made with her handmaids” (7.235; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[clothing]]), as in Book 6 she sat at the hearth among her women turning the sea-purple yarn (6.52–53).

**The bed.** At the end of the evening “white-armed Arete called to her handmaids / to set beds beneath the colonnade” (7.335–336), the verses with which Helen's slave women make the guests' beds at Sparta and Achilles' companions and slave women make Priam's (7.336–339 = 4.297–300, *Iliad* 24.644–647; see [[beds-beneath-the-colonnade]] and [[bed]]). “And the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands; / and when they had spread the thick bed in haste, / they stood beside Odysseus and urged him with words: / ‘Rise and go to bed, stranger; your bed has been made’” (7.339–342; see [[torches]]). στόρεσαν takes up the work described in the verses before, and λέχος is the bedstead, firmly framed together; ἐγκονέουσαι, “in haste”, is found only in the feminine participle, here, at *Iliad* 24.648, and at 23.291, when Eurynome and the nurse make the bed of Odysseus and Penelope (Merry on 7.340); on ὄρσο κέων, “Rise and go to bed”, see the translation's key words. Their one speech in the book is a single verse, and “it seemed welcome to him to lie down to sleep” (7.343).

**Nausicaa's handmaids.** The handmaids of Book 6 come back in the stranger's story: “And I noticed your daughter's handmaids on the shore / playing, and she herself was among them, like the goddesses” (7.290–291; see [[nausicaa]]). Her father blames her for not bringing him home “with her handmaids” (7.300), and Odysseus answers that she bade him follow with them, but he would not (7.304), though in fact she had bidden him go with them only through the fields (6.259–261, 6.295–296).
