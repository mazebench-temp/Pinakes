summary: The sky is the gods' home: Alcinous wonders whether the stranger is “one of the immortals come down from heaven” (7.199), and Odysseus answers that he is not like the immortals “who hold the wide sky”, but like mortal men (7.208–210).
# οὐρανοῦ (7.199), first met in the Odyssey here; it also takes 20.113 and 20.357, the starry sky of Zeus' thunder and
# the sky from which the sun has perished. The Iliad's οὐρανοῦ is claimed in its Book 19 note.

**Come down from heaven.** When he has promised the stranger an escort, Alcinous adds a doubt: “But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven, / then this is something else that the gods are devising” (7.199–200; see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The first verse is Diomedes' question to Glaucus on the battlefield, turned from the second person to the third: “But if you are one of the immortals come down from heaven” (*Iliad* 6.128; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]); W. W. Merry notes that it stands there in a different connection (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.200). Aristarchus' copies read κατʼ οὐρανόν (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.199). ἄλλο τι, “something else”, Merry takes as a euphemism for some withdrawal of the gods' favor; what put the thought in Alcinous' mind was the stranger's majestic look, which Athena had given him by the river, and his sudden appearance in their midst (on 7.200; 6.229–235, 7.143). The gods, Alcinous goes on, have always appeared openly to the Phaeacians and feast beside them (7.201–206; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]).

**The wide sky.** Odysseus answers: “Alcinous, let something else be on your mind; for I, for my part, / am not like the immortals, who hold the wide sky, / in form or stature, but like mortal men” (7.208–210; see [[wide-sky]] and [[mortal-men]]). ἄλλο τί τοι μελέτω, “turn your thoughts to something else”, means that there is no ground for the anxiety Alcinous has surmised (Merry on 7.208). That afternoon Nausicaa had said the opposite to her handmaids, “but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.243; see the Book 6 note). When he tells Arete his story, he begins with the sorrows “the heavenly gods have given me” (7.241–242; see [[heavenly-gods]]).
