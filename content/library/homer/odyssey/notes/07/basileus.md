# βασίλευεν (7.59). βασιλῆος (7.46) and βασιλῆα (7.55, 7.141) are in the entry; βασίλεια (7.241) is in the Book 4 note.
grc: [βασίλευεν]
---

**The king of the Phaeacians.** The book calls Alcinous king where it speaks of his house and his line: “the famous house of the king” (7.46), “the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55), “until he came to Arete and King Alcinous” (7.141; see [[alcinous]]). The verb for his rule is ἀνάσσω, “be lord”: over all the Phaeacians he was lord, and the people listened to him as to a god (7.10–11), as his father Nausithous was lord before him (7.62; see [[anax]] and [[nausithous]]). He is the first of a class, the kings whom Zeus has nurtured, who sit at his feast (7.49; see [[kings]]).

**A king who destroyed his people.** βασιλεύω is said once in the book, of the oldest name in the queen's line: Periboea was the daughter of great-hearted Eurymedon, “who once was king over the high-hearted Giants. / But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.58–60; see [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[giants]] and [[atasthalie]]). Merry found the words open to three readings, a foolhardy expedition against another people, a revolt of the people against their king, or the king's own guilt bringing ruin on both, and thought the first the most likely, on the strength of the epithets ὑπερθύμοισι and ἀτάσθαλον (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59).

**The queen.** “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows” (7.241–242): βασίλεια, the word the poem uses of Penelope (4.697, 4.770), is Odysseus' title for Arete in his answer (see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[algos]]). In Book 6 the same word was Nausicaa's, “the princess” (6.115; see the Book 6 note).
