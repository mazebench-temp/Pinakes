**The last libation.** Odysseus steps over the threshold at the end of the evening:

{{quote:406a704f-852b-5b78-8900-f1e4ea4c9ac5}}

ἐύσκοπος ἀργεϊφόντης, “the keen-sighted slayer of Argus”, is the title by which Zeus names him in the first council, when he tells how the gods sent him to warn Aegisthus (1.37–38), and by which the Iliad names him when the gods urge him to steal Hector's body (*Iliad* 24.24, 24.109); ἐύσκοπος is said once more, of Artemis the archer (11.198; see [[keen-sighted]] and [[argeiphontes]]). σπένδεσκον and μνησαίατο make it a custom: whenever they thought of bed, the last libation was his (see [[libation]]). His name is not used; the titles are enough.

**Why Hermes.** A scholium explains the custom by Hermes as the escort of dreams or the giver of sleep, with the wand “with which he bewitches the eyes of men” (5.47; *Iliad* 24.343; see [[wand]]). Nitzsch objected that the dream-god belongs to the later idea of Hermes as escort of souls, Zeus being in Homer the real sender of dreams; that the wand is the sign not of natural sleep but of some extraordinary trance; and that a good night's rest was too much a matter of course to be the subject of a special libation. He compared the libation of later times to Zeus the Savior or Zeus Teleios, and thought it was poured to Hermes as guardian and protector, leaving the whole household in his keeping for the night (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.138; see [[dream]]). At Pylos the last libation of the evening was poured to Poseidon and the other immortals, and the tongues of the victims were thrown into the fire (3.332–341; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]); some took the tongues for an offering to Hermes as god of speech, after the custom of the Phaeacians, but Merry finds no trace of Hermes there, and with Nitzsch gives them to Poseidon, in whose honor the day had been kept (on 3.332).

**A new libation.** The evening does not end as the custom would have it. The suppliant has come, and on Echeneus' advice the herald mixes wine afresh, so that they may pour a libation to Zeus, “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.163–165, 7.179–183; see [[zeus]], [[echeneus]] and [[pontonous]]); only then do the Phaeacians go home to bed (7.228–229).
