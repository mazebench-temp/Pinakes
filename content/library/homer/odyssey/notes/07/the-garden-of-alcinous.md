---
summary: The garden is the last of the wonders that Odysseus, unseen in Athena's mist, stands and gazes at before he crosses the threshold of Alcinous' house (7.82–83, 7.133–135): an orchard that bears in every season, a vineyard at every stage of the vintage, garden beds, and two springs, from one of which the townspeople draw their water (7.112–132).
---

**Seen by a stranger.** Athena leaves Odysseus at the house of Alcinous, and the poem shows it to him before he goes in: “and his heart pondered much / as he stood there, before he reached the bronze threshold” (7.82–83; see [[odysseus]] and [[threshold]]). The description runs from the gleam of the house to the garden (7.84–132; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]), and returns to him in the verses said of Hermes at the cave of Calypso, with Odysseus in the god's place: “There much-enduring brilliant Odysseus stood and gazed. / But when he had gazed at everything in his heart, / he quickly stepped over the threshold into the house” (7.133–135), as “There the guide, the slayer of Argus, stood and gazed. / But when he had gazed at everything in his heart, / at once he went into the wide cave” (5.75–77; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[hermes]]). Both places are shown through the eyes of a newcomer who stops before going in, and both have their vine and their springs: at the cave a garden vine and four springs of clear water, a sight at which even an immortal would gaze and delight his mind (5.68–74), at the house of Alcinous the vineyard and the two springs (7.122–131).

**Unseen.** He can stand and look because no one sees him. Athena has poured a heaven-sent mist over him, so that the Phaeacians did not notice him as he went through the city, and it flows back from him only when he has thrown his arms around Arete's knees (7.39–42, 7.139–143; see [[achlys]] and [[athena]]). On the way he had marveled at the harbors, the ships and the long walls (7.43–45; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]); at the house he stands and gazes.

**In the present tense.** From the fifty slave women on, house and garden are told in the present: the women grind and weave, the fruit never fails, the vineyard is rooted, the grapes are dried, gathered and trodden, the springs run (7.103–130). The last two verses go back to the past of the story, “from this spring the citizens drew water”, “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (7.131–132).

**The king's garden and the town's water.** The garden lies outside the court, near the doors, and its second spring runs under the threshold of the court toward the house, the spring at which the townspeople drew water (7.112, 7.129–131; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.130; see [[alcinous]]). Nausicaa had named another of her father's lands, his domain and flourishing orchard beside the grove of Athena, as far from the city as a man can make himself heard with a shout (6.291–294; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]).

**The gifts of the gods.** The poem calls house and garden together the gifts of the gods (7.132). Later in the evening Alcinous tells his leaders that the gods have always appeared to the Phaeacians in plain sight, and feast beside them, “since we are near to them” (7.201–205; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]] and [[phaeacians]]).
