summary: She comes home ahead of the stranger: her brothers unyoke the mules and carry in the clothes, and the old Eurymedusa lights her fire and makes ready her supper (7.1–13). She does not appear again in the book, but the talk in the hall turns to her: Odysseus tells her parents how he found her on the shore and how she did not fall short of good sense (7.290–297), takes on himself the blame her father lays on her (7.298–307), and the king wishes that such a man might have her (7.311–316).

**Home.** While Odysseus prays in Athena's grove, “the might of the two mules carried the girl to the city. / And when she came to the famous house of her father, / she halted them in the doorway, and her brothers stood / around her, like the immortals, and they from under the wagon / unyoked the mules and carried the clothing inside” (7.2–6; see [[mules]], [[wagon]] and [[clothing]]). στῆσεν, “she halted”, has the mules for its object, as Hera halted her horses (*Iliad* 5.755) and Menelaus his ships (4.582; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.4). The brothers are the five sons of the house, who wanted freshly washed clothes for the dance (6.62–65; see [[kasignetos]] and [[like-the-immortals]]). She goes to her own chamber, where the old chamber-woman who reared her, Eurymedusa, lights her fire and makes ready her supper (7.7–13; see [[eurymedusa]]). It is the last we see of her in this book: she does not come into the hall, and the stranger, as she told him, waits in the grove until she is home (6.295–299; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]).

**Odysseus' account of her.** She comes back into the book in the last part of the story he tells her mother, which answers the queen's question about the clothes:

{{quote:de8c5444-cfc0-595d-b4c1-5a95d3a00e09}}

“And she herself was among them, like the goddesses” (7.291; see [[like-the-goddesses]]), as the narrator had likened her among her handmaids to Artemis among the nymphs (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). ἤμβροτεν, “fell short”, is a sort of metathesis of ἥμαρτεν, as ἔδρακον is of δέρκομαι, with the β inserted as in μεσημβρία (Merry on 7.292; see [[noos]]). In the next verse ἄν is long because ἔλποιο began with a digamma, and for ἀντιάσαντα Merry compares 6.193 (on 7.293). “For the young are always thoughtless” (7.294; see [[the-young-are-thoughtless]]) seems to come in awkwardly, and ἐρξέμεν is probably an aorist (Merry on 7.294; see the translation's note on 7.294).

**Her father's blame.** When the story is done, Alcinous finds fault with her: “Stranger, truly this, at least, she did not devise rightly, / my child, in that she did not bring you with her handmaids / to our house; and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.299–301; see [[alcinous]]). Odysseus defends her:

{{quote:060743ed-f7cd-5d23-9851-12ee4aeece51}}

He bends the truth. She had bidden him follow the wagon with her handmaids, but only through the fields, and then wait in Athena's grove until she was home, for fear of the town's talk if a stranger were seen walking into the city with her (6.259–299; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]): it was she, not he, who would not have him come in with her. Eustathius remarks, with what Merry calls charming simplicity, that here Odysseus plainly lies, as a wise man would do at the right moment (Merry on 7.305). ἰδόντι takes up ἕπεσθαι, “should you catch sight of me following her” (Merry on 7.306), and δύσζηλοι is “quick to anger”, “touchy” (Merry on 7.307; see the translation's key words, [[quick-to-anger]] and [[odysseus]]). He calls her ἀμύμονα κούρην, “the noble girl” (7.303; see [[blameless]]).

**The wish.** The king, who will not be angry “for no reason” (7.309–310), wishes that such a man as the stranger might stay, “you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law” (7.313; see [[alcinous]]). The wish answers the one she spoke to her handmaids on the shore, “Would that such a man might be called my husband, / living here” (6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[marriage]]).

**Her epithets.** λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (7.12; see [[white-armed]]); ἐικυῖα θεῇσι, “like the goddesses” (7.291); ἀμύμων, “noble” (7.303). Her father calls her παῖς ἐμή, “my child” (7.300), and Odysseus τεῆς … θυγατρός, “your daughter” (7.290).
