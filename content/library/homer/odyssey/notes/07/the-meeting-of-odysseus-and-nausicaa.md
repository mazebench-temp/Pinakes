summary: The sequel of the meeting. Nausicaa reaches home first, and Odysseus follows her directions to her mother's knees; the queen knows the clothes her daughter gave him, and in his account he tells only what is to the girl's credit, taking on himself the fault her father finds in her (7.290–307). Alcinous wishes that such a man might have his daughter (7.311–316).

**Home before him.** As she told him she would, Nausicaa reaches home before the stranger: “the might of the two mules carried the girl to the city”, and her brothers, like the immortals, unyoke the mules at the doorway and carry in the clothes whose washing was the pretext of her journey (7.2–6; see [[nausicaa]] and [[mules]]). In her chamber the old Eurymedusa, who reared her, lights her fire and makes ready her supper (7.7–13; see [[eurymedusa]]). The poem does not say that she told her parents of the stranger. Odysseus does what she bade him: he waits in the grove, asks the way to her father's house, goes through the hall, passes the king and throws his arms around her mother's knees (7.14–26, 7.139–142, after 6.295–315; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**The clothes.** The queen learns of the meeting from the clothes. She knows the mantle and the tunic the handmaids laid beside him at the river (6.214), “fine ones, which she herself had made with her handmaids” (7.235), and asks “Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (7.238; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[clothing]]). Odysseus ends his account with the answer to the second question: the girl “gave me these clothes” (7.296).

**His account of her.**

{{quote:de8c5444-cfc0-595d-b4c1-5a95d3a00e09}}

He tells only what is to her credit: the girl among her handmaids at play, “like the goddesses”, his supplication, and her good sense in feeding, bathing and clothing him (see [[like-the-goddesses]] and [[supplication]]). Of his waking among the girls, the leafy branch and the flight of the handmaids he says nothing. The verse on the young (see [[the-young-are-thoughtless]]) comes in awkwardly, Merry thinks: ἐρξέμεν may have been added to keep out an elliptical construction, and a commonplace then completes the line (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.294; see the translation's note on 7.294).

**The fault and the excuse.** Alcinous finds one fault in her: “Stranger, truly this, at least, she did not devise rightly, / my child, in that she did not bring you with her handmaids / to our house; and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.299–301; see [[alcinous]]). The last clause gives the reason why she was wrong, “for it was to her first thou didst make thy supplication” (Merry on 7.301): the suppliant was hers to bring home. Odysseus answers with a story of his own. She bade him follow with the handmaids, he says, “but I would not, out of fear and shame, / lest your heart too should be indignant when you saw it; / for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (7.305–307; see [[quick-to-anger]], [[aidos]] and [[deos]]). She did bid him follow the wagon with the handmaids through the fields (6.259–261); but it was she who bade him stay behind at the grove, for fear of the people's talk (6.262–296; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). Eustathius remarks, with what Merry calls charming simplicity, that here Odysseus lies openly, as a wise man would do at the right moment (Merry on 7.305; see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**The wish.** “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, / being such a man as you are, and thinking as I do, / you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, / staying here” (7.311–314). The father's wish answers the daughter's to her handmaids, that such a man might be called her husband, living there, and that it might please him to stay (6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[marriage]]), and each is spoken in the other's absence. Alcinous does not press it: against his will no Phaeacian will keep the stranger (7.315–316). Aristarchus doubted whether the six verses were Homer's, and held that even if they were, they were rightly removed: how could the king, not knowing the man, woo him for his daughter, and press her on him unasked? (the scholia, in Merry's critical note on 7.311–316; see the translation's note on 7.311–316). Köchly rejected the whole passage as the work of an interpolator, perhaps the one who added 6.245, who meant to confirm with the father's authority what Nausicaa had lightly let fall of Odysseus as a husband (6.244, 6.277 and following); Merry calls this “most arbitrary criticism” (on 7.314).

**The day of the meeting.** In his account Odysseus slept among the leaves “all night long, and into the dawn and to midday. / And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go” (7.288–289), and then he saw the girls at play on the shore. δείλετο, “was declining”, is Aristarchus' reading; others read δύσετο, “set”, which Merry prints, though it seems to make the sun set as Odysseus woke, when much of the day of the sixth book was still to come (6.321). Merry finds the two words equally tied to sunset in Homer, the imperfect easing the difficulty a little, and thinks it wiser to remember that epic poetry uses its formulas with a certain carelessness (Merry on 7.289 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 7.289).
