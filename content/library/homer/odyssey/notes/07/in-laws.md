summary: Alcinous, having heard the stranger's story, wishes that such a man might “have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, / staying here” (γαμβρός, 7.311–315).
# γαμβρός (7.313) is in the Book 4 note.
---

The king's wish answers his daughter's, who said to her handmaids in Book 6, “Would that such a man might be called my husband” (6.244; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[posis]]). He would give the son-in-law a house and possessions, if he stayed willingly, but no Phaeacian will keep him against his will (7.313–316; see [[xenia]] and [[what-is-due-is-better]]). καλέεσθαι, “be called”, is the same periphrasis of a title as κεκλημένος εἴη in the girl's wish (see the Book 6 note on [[posis]]). Aristarchus doubted whether the six verses 7.311–316 were Homer's, asking how the king could seek a husband for his daughter in a man he did not know, and not as one asked but as one entreating (the scholia, in W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 7.311–316; see the translation's note on 7.311–316). Menelaus is the poem's other γαμβρός, the son-in-law of Zeus, who for that reason will not die in Argos (4.569; see [[elysium]]).
