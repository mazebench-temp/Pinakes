---
summary: Ovid calls on the kindly field of Alcinous to give its fruit and makes fruit for Alcinous a proverb for a needless gift; and from exile he names the Phaeacid of his friend Tuticanus, a Latin poem on the Phaeacians “worthy of Maeonian pages”.
---

- **Fruit for Alcinous.** “Let the kindly field of Alcinous furnish its fruit!” (*Amores* 1.10.55–56), and “Who would give honey to Aristaeus, … fruit to Alcinous?” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.2.9–10; on 7.112–132): [[the-garden-of-alcinous-in-later-writers]] and [[the-garden-of-alcinous]].
- **The Phaeacid.** Ovid remembers correcting the verses of his friend Tuticanus “when the Pierian goddesses taught you to compose a Phaeacid worthy of Maeonian pages” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.12.27–28), and counts among the poets of his day “the one who turned the Maeonian Phaeacid” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.16.27; plain renderings): a name, formed like *Aeneis*, for Homer's story of the Phaeacians, which Aelian's list of the parts of the Odyssey leaves without one: [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]] and [[phaeacians]].
