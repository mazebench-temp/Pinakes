---
summary: Unseen in his mist, Odysseus “marveled at the harbors and the balanced ships, / and at the places of assembly of the heroes themselves, and the long walls, / high, fitted with stakes, a wonder to see” (7.43–45); then he stands and gazes at the house and the garden (7.133–134). When the mist is lifted at the queen's knees, it is the Phaeacians who marvel: they “fell silent when they saw the man in the house, / and marveled as they looked” (7.144–145).
# θαύμαζεν (7.43) and θαύμαζον (7.145) are in the Book 3 and Book 1 notes.
---

The book's wonder runs both ways. The stranger marvels first. θαύμαζεν, an imperfect, expresses the constant process of wonder at each new marvel as he passes it (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.43): the harbors and the ships, the places of assembly and the walls, “a wonder to see” (θαῦμα ἰδέσθαι, 7.45; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[balanced-ships]]). At the bronze threshold his heart ponders much (7.82–83), and when he has seen the house and the garden, “There much-enduring brilliant Odysseus stood and gazed. / But when he had gazed at everything in his heart, / he quickly stepped over the threshold into the house” (7.133–135; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]); the second verse is the one with which Hermes gazed at Calypso's island before going into her cave (7.134 = 5.76; see [[hermes]]). Then the Phaeacians marvel at him. The mist flows back, and the two participles of 7.144–145 mark two moments: a hush fell on them the moment they caught sight of him (ἰδόντες), and they kept wondering as they gazed (ὁρόωντες), since they had not seen him come (Merry on 7.144, after a scholium; see [[silence]] and [[thesphaton]]). Their wonder is the ground of the king's question later in the evening, whether the stranger is one of the immortals (7.199–206; see [[immortality]]).
