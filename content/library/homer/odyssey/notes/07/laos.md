summary: Three peoples: Eurymedon, king of the Giants, “destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.60); Arete is honored by her husband, her children and “the people, who look on her as a god” (7.71); and the Phaeacian sailors who saw Euboea are “of our people” (7.323).
# λαόν (7.60) and λαῶν (7.71, 7.323) are in the entry.
---

ἀλλʼ ὁ μὲν ὤλεσε λαὸν ἀτάσθαλον, ὤλετο δʼ αὐτός, “But he destroyed his reckless people, and perished himself” (7.60; see [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[giants]] and [[atasthalie]]). Pausanias, as Merry quotes him, took the verse for evidence that Homer's Giants were mortal men and not a race of gods, since in the epic λαός means the people, the many (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.59). The Phaeacian λαοί are the people of a king: they honor their queen and greet her “as a god” when she walks through the city (7.70–72; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[time]]), and they are the sailors who carried Rhadamanthys to Euboea and say that it is the farthest of lands (7.322–323; see [[euboea]] and [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]]).
