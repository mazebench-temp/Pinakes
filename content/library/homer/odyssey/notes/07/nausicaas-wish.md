---
summary: Alcinous answers his daughter's wish with his own, that a man such as the stranger might have her and be called his son-in-law, staying in Scheria; Aristarchus doubted the father's six verses as he had doubted the daughter's two.
refs: [7.311-316]
---

**The father's wish.** When Odysseus has taken on himself the blame for not coming home with Nausicaa's handmaids, Alcinous answers that he is not one to be angry for no reason, and then, calling on father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, wishes that a man such as the stranger, “thinking as I do”, might “have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, / staying here” (7.309–314; see [[alcinous]] and [[if-only-father-zeus]]). He would give him a house and possessions if he stayed willingly; but no Phaeacian will keep him against his will, and the escort is fixed for the next day (7.314–318; see [[pompe]]). Nausicaa's words were “Would that such a man might be called my husband, / living here” (6.244–245). Her father's are the same wish from the other side, the son-in-law where she had the husband. Hers was spoken to her handmaids while the stranger sat apart (6.236–239); his is spoken to the stranger, while she is in her chamber (7.7–13).

**Aristarchus.** The scholia report that Aristarchus doubted whether the six verses were Homer's, and said that even if they were Homeric they had rightly been removed: πῶς γὰρ ἀγνοῶν τὸν ἄνδρα μνηστεύεται αὐτῷ τὴν θυγατέρα, καὶ οὐ προστρεπόμενος ἀλλὰ λιπαρῶν; “for how, not knowing the man, does he offer him his daughter, and that not as one who approaches but as one who entreats?” (the scholia, in Merry's critical note on 7.311–316; see the translation's note on 7.311–316 and [[aristarchus]]). Lehrs, as Merry reports, took “even if they are Homeric” to mean even if nothing in them is foreign to the language and the ways of Homer's age. As with the daughter's verses, which Plutarch blamed for their boldness (see the entry above), the objection is to what is fitting for the speaker. Merry explains the construction instead: a wish that turns into a prayer “under the influence of vehement emotion”, as in Laertes' “If only, father Zeus” (24.376; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.311), and he takes “thinking as I do” for agreement with the king's own wish, that the stranger should stay instead of wanting to go home (Merry on 7.312).
