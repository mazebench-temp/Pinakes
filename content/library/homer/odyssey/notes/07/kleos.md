---
summary: When Alcinous has promised the escort, Odysseus prays: “Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said, / Alcinous! Then his fame over the grain-giving earth / would be unquenchable, and I would reach my native land” (7.331–333).
# κλέος (7.333) is in the entry.
---

The fame in the prayer is the host's. ἐπὶ ζείδωρον ἄρουραν, “over the grain-giving earth”, means that it would be spread throughout the world, by the praise that the stranger would give the king in his gratitude (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.332; see [[grain-giving]]). It is the fame that Penelope will promise to the blameless host, whose report strangers carry far and wide among all men (19.332–334, a plain rendering; see [[xenia]]). ἄσβεστον κλέος, “unquenchable fame”, is said once more in the poem, of the mound Menelaus heaped for Agamemnon by the river Aegyptus, “so that his fame might be unquenchable” (4.584; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]); in the Iliad ἄσβεστος is the word of the gods' laughter and of the battle cry (*Iliad* 1.599, 11.50; see [[unquenchable-laughter]] and [[unquenchable-cry]]). The prayer joins the two ends of the voyage, the king's fame and the stranger's homecoming, in one verse (7.333; see [[nostos]], [[pompe]] and [[prayer]]). The king's people will pay for the escort: Poseidon turns their ship to stone as it comes home (13.159–164).
