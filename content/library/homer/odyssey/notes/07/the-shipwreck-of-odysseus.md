**In Calypso's verses.** Asked who he is and who gave him his clothes, Odysseus begins with Ogygia and wily Calypso, the daughter of Atlas, with whom no god or mortal mingles, and tells how he came to her: a god brought him to her hearth (7.244–248; see [[ogygia]], [[calypso]] and [[daimon]]). The wreck is told in the verses in which Calypso told it to Hermes, with “my swift ship” for “his swift ship” (7.249–250 = 5.131–132, with μοι for οἱ; see [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]] and [[thunderbolt]]), and the verse of the companions is the same in all three places, “There all my other noble companions perished” (7.251 = 5.110 = 5.133). ἔνθα takes up the moment of the shipwreck (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.251). Then he adds what Calypso did not say: he took the keel in his arms and was borne along for nine days, and on the tenth night the gods brought him to the island (7.252–254). In the tale of his wanderings he ends with the same verses, and breaks off because he told the rest to the king and his wife the day before (12.447–453, Greek only; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Of the cattle of the Sun, the cause of the wreck, he says nothing to the queen (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**The text.** ἔλσας, “struck”, has a variant ἐλάσας, perhaps Zenodotus' reading, and the scholia know two readings, ἐκέασσε and ἐκέδασσε (Merry's critical note on 7.250; see the translation's note on 7.250). The scholia report that eight verses were athetized, since the story is told later (12.447–453); Buttmann refers the remark to 7.251–258, and Merry finds 7.254–255, a mere repetition of what Odysseus has just said, the most suspicious (Merry's critical note on 7.251–258; see the translation's note on 7.251–258). In the second wreck the text reads κέλευθον, “path”, with Ameis and La Roche, where Nauck and Merry read the plural κέλευθα, after Ellendt's distinction between the two (Merry's critical note and on 7.272; see the translation's notes on 7.272 and 7.273).

**The second wreck.** His account goes on to a second wreck, the one the fifth book told. Calypso said nothing to him of Hermes' errand, only that he would reach home if the gods were willing (5.160–170), and he gives two reasons for his release: “at a message from Zeus, or perhaps her own mind had turned” (7.263; see [[zeus]] and [[noos]]). She sent him off on a raft of many bindings, and for seventeen days he sailed, until the shadowy mountains of the Phaeacians' land appeared (7.264–268, after 5.263–279; see [[raft]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). Then the earth-shaker “roused the winds against me and bound up my path, / and stirred up the endless sea, nor would the wave / let me be borne on the raft as I groaned without ceasing. / Then the storm scattered it” (7.272–275; see [[poseidon]]). He swam through “this gulf”, pointing, Merry thinks, toward the sea (on 7.276), was nearly dashed against the rocks, and swam out again until he came to the river, “smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind” (7.276–282, where 7.282 = 5.443; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). The two wrecks stand side by side in the account, the first by Zeus' thunderbolt and the second by Poseidon's storm. Of Ino and her veil, of Athena's help in the storm and of his prayer to the river he says nothing (5.333–353, 5.382–387, 5.445–450; see [[leucothea]] and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).
