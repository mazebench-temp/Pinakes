summary: The book turns on who is seen. Athena hides Odysseus in a mist so that no Phaeacian sees him, and meets him herself in the likeness of a girl whom he does not know; the mist lifts only at Arete's knees, and the company marvel at a man who has appeared among them unseen. Alcinous wonders whether the stranger is a god, since the gods have always appeared in plain sight to the Phaeacians, at their hecatombs and to a lone wayfarer on the road; Odysseus answers that he is no immortal but a mortal of many sorrows.

**Unseen.** The book opens with the theme reversed: a god makes a man invisible to men. Athena pours a thick mist around Odysseus, “lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him, / should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (7.16–17), and “the Phaeacians famed for ships did not notice him / as he went through the city among them” (7.39–40; see [[athena]] and [[achlys]]). Merry compares the helmet of Hades, which Athena puts on in the Iliad so that Ares may not see her (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.15; *Iliad* 5.845; see [[helmet-of-hades]]).

**The goddess unknown.** At the edge of the city she meets him “like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar” (7.20), and he takes her for what she seems: “My child, will you not lead me to the house of the man / Alcinous, who is lord among these people?” (7.22–23). Nothing in the book shows that he knew her. On Ithaca, when she has made herself known, he tells her that it is hard for a mortal who meets her to know her, however skilled he is, since she takes every likeness, and that he had not seen her since Troy until, in the land of the Phaeacians, she encouraged him and led him into the city herself (13.312–323; see [[divine-epiphany]]).

**Seen at the queen's knees.** In the hall the mist covers him until he clasps Arete's knees, “and then the god-sent mist flowed back from the man himself. / And they fell silent when they saw the man in the house, / and marveled as they looked” (7.143–145; see [[thesphaton]] and [[thambos]]). They marveled, the scholium says, as was natural, because they had not seen him coming (Merry on 7.144). A man has appeared among them out of the air, as a god might.

**The gods in plain sight.** That sudden appearance and the stranger's majestic looks, Merry thinks, suggest to Alcinous that he may be a god (on 7.200; 6.229–235):

{{quote:5c747d25-9d8e-5d3d-9003-7183fd4bb5b5}}

The first verse is Diomedes' question to Glaucus, “But if you are one of the immortals come down from heaven” (*Iliad* 6.128; Merry on 7.200); Aristarchus' copies read κατʼ οὐρανόν for κατʼ οὐρανοῦ (see the translation's note on 7.199). ἄλλο τι, “something else”, is said euphemistically, Merry suggests, for some withdrawal of the gods' favor (on 7.200). ἐναργεῖς, “in plain sight”, is the word of Hera's warning, “gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (*Iliad* 20.131; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]), and of Nestor's goddess, “who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (3.420); in Eumaeus' hut Odysseus and the dogs see Athena and Telemachus does not, for the gods do not appear in plain sight to all (16.159–163). The Phaeacians see the gods at their hecatombs, feasting beside them (7.202–203; see [[hecatomb]]), and on the road too, privately as well as in public (Merry on 7.204): “they do not hide themselves at all”, that is, they do not disguise themselves, as Odysseus disguised himself as a beggar to go into Troy (κατακρύπτων, 4.247; Merry on 7.205). ἐγγύθεν, “near”, is generally taken of kinship, as the Phaeacians are “near kin to the gods” (5.35); Welcker seems to take it of place, as though they lived in a sort of heavenly country, like the Hyperboreans, and Nitzsch remarks that the distant peoples at the edges of the world are the gods' especial favorites; Merry gives both views, and the English keeps both (on 7.205; see the translation's note on 7.205, [[near-kin-to-the-gods]], [[cyclopes]] and [[giants]]). The lone wayfarer of Alcinous' words is what Odysseus has just been, and the goddess who met him on his way came disguised as a girl.

For the thought Merry compares the end of Catullus' poem on the wedding of Peleus and Thetis: the gods used to visit the chaste houses of heroes and show themselves to gatherings of mortals, while piety was not yet spurned; since the earth was stained with crime they no longer deign to visit such gatherings or let themselves be touched by the clear light of day (on 7.201; Catullus 64.384–386, 397–408).

**No god.** Odysseus answers, “Alcinous, let something else be on your mind; for I, for my part, / am not like the immortals, who hold the wide sky, / in form or stature, but like mortal men” (7.208–210; see [[wide-sky]] and [[mortal-men]]). In Book 6 it was he who asked whether Nausicaa was a goddess, and she who saw him, bathed and anointed, “like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.149–152, 6.243). On Ithaca Telemachus takes him for a god when Athena has changed his looks, and he answers that he is no god but his father (16.181–188).
