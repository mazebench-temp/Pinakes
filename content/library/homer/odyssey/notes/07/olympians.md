**The heavenly gods.** Odysseus begins his story to Arete, “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows, since the heavenly gods have given me many” (7.241–242; see [[heavenly-gods]] and [[algos]]). The second verse is the one with which he begins his long tale to Alcinous (9.15). Οὐρανίωνες are “the heavenly ones”: Curtius takes the ending as merely amplifying, οὐρανίωνες beside οὐράνιοι as αὐλών beside αὐλός. In the Iliad the word seems once to be a true patronymic, the Titans as “sons of Uranus” (*Iliad* 5.898), though Nägelsbach takes even that verse of the Olympians; Aristarchus set the Οὐρανίωνες apart from the Olympians, noting that Homer gives that name, with “the gods below” and the Titans, to the gods around Cronus (on *Iliad* 15.225); here the reference is unmistakably to the gods of heaven (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.242).

**In men's words.** The rest of the book speaks of the gods from below. The orchard, the vineyard and the springs of Alcinous are “the splendid gifts of the gods” (7.132; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). The suppliant prays for the banqueters, “may the gods grant them prosperity / in their lives” (7.148–149; see [[olbos]] and [[supplication]]), and Alcinous plans for the next day to entertain the stranger “and to the gods / offer fine sacrifices” (7.190–191; see [[sacrifice]]), and tells how the gods appear to his people in plain sight at their hecatombs (7.199–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Odysseus denies that he is like “the immortals, who hold the wide sky” (7.209; see [[wide-sky]]), and could tell of still more evils, “all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods” (7.214). In his story no one, “neither of the gods nor of mortal men”, comes near Calypso (7.247), and “the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia” (7.254; see [[calypso]] and [[daimon]]).
