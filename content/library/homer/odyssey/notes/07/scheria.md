---
summary: Athena leaves “lovely Scheria” for Marathon and Athens (7.79); in his story to the queen, Odysseus calls it “your land”, whose shadowy mountains appeared on the eighteenth day and to which the wind and the water carried him (7.268–277).
# The land without its name: "this city and land" (7.26), "the shadowy mountains / of your land" (7.268-269), "until
# to your land the wind and the water carried and drove me" (7.276-277).
refs: [7.26, 7.269, 7.276-277]
---

**Lovely Scheria.** The name is spoken once in the book, as Athena leaves the island: “So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away / over the barren sea, and left lovely Scheria, / and came to Marathon and to wide-streeted Athens” (7.78–80; see [[athena]], [[lovely]], [[barren-sea]], [[marathon]] and [[athens]]). The scholia report that Chaeris suspected the passage, and Merry thinks it was suspected as an addition by a later rhapsode to please Athenian feeling (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 7.79–80; see the translation's note on 7.79–80).

**Your land.** When he asks the way to the king's house, Odysseus knows no one “of the people who hold this city and land” (7.26 = 6.177; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). A scholium and Eustathius record another ending for the verse, καὶ ἔργα νέμονται, “and tend the fields” (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.26). Telling Arete his story, he speaks of “your land”: on the eighteenth day “there appeared the shadowy mountains / of your land” (7.268–269), the verses of the voyage in the fifth book, where it was “the land of the Phaeacians” (5.279–280; see [[shadowy-mountains]] and [[phaeacians]]); beside ὑμετέρης, “your”, Merry's critical note records the reading Φαιήκων, “of the Phaeacians” (on 7.269). After the storm he swam, “until to your land / the wind and the water carried and drove me” (7.276–277; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). τόδε λαῖτμα, “this gulf” (7.276), is “yonder gulf”: he points toward the sea (Merry on 7.276). The coast he describes is the coast of the fifth book, great rocks and “a joyless place” where the wave would have overpowered him, until he came to the river (7.278–282; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]).

**How far.** Alcinous measures the distance of other lands from his own by Euboea, “which those who saw it say is the farthest away” (7.321–322). Merry, who can find no clue to the legend of that voyage, thinks it just possible that Scheria was imagined as not far from Elysium, where Rhadamanthys lived, so that Phaeacian sailors were at hand to carry him (on 7.323; see [[elysium]] and [[rhadamanthus]]).
