summary: The formula for the condition of the gods is said twice in the book: of the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard the house of Alcinous, “being immortal and ageless all their days” (7.94), and of Calypso's offer, which Odysseus tells to Arete: she said “that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days” (7.257).
# ἀθανάτους ὄντας καὶ ἀγήρως (7.94), the formula with a participle between its words.
grc: ["ἀθανάτους ὄντας καὶ ἀγήρως"]
---

**The dogs of Hephaestus.** The first use is a marvel of craft. On either side of the doors stand gold and silver dogs, “which Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill / to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous, / being immortal and ageless all their days” (7.91–94; see [[the-gold-and-silver-dogs]] and [[hephaestus]]). The words that the Iliad gives to the aegis of Athena, “ageless and immortal” (*Iliad* 2.447), and to the immortal horses of Achilles (*Iliad* 17.444), are here given to works of metal. Merry takes the dogs, like the golden youths with torches, for images described in the hyperbole Homer uses as a tribute to the craftsman's skill, as of Hephaestus' self-moving tripods and golden handmaids (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.91; *Iliad* 18.373–377, 18.417–420; see [[the-golden-handmaids]]).

**The offer refused.** The second is the verse of Calypso's offer, as she spoke it to Hermes (5.136), now in the mouth of the man she offered it to: she loved him and cared for him, “and said / that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days; / but she never persuaded the heart in my breast” (7.256–258; see [[immortality]] and [[calypso]]). He will say it once more, to Penelope (23.336).
