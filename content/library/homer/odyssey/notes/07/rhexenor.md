**Athena's account.** Athena explains to Odysseus who the queen is by going back to the founder: Poseidon's son Nausithous ruled the Phaeacians, “and Nausithous begot Rhexenor and Alcinous. / Him, when he had no son, Apollo, god of the silver bow, struck down, / newly married, in his hall, leaving one only child, a daughter, / Arete; and Alcinous made her his wife” (7.63–66; see [[nausithous]], [[apollo]], [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). ἄκουρον … νυμφίον is “without male issue … before his bridal days were over”: μίαν οἴην παῖδα λιπόντα explains the first word, so that Nauck's ἄωρον, “taken by an untimely death”, from a gloss of Aristophanes, is needless; and he must have been married long enough to have a daughter born to him, unless Arete was born after his death (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.64; see the translation's note on 7.64–65). From this verse Walter Leaf concluded that a man was called νυμφίος after his marriage until he had a son, as the Iliad translation's note on 23.223 reports, where a father burns the bones of his son, νυμφίου, “newly married” (*Iliad* 23.222–223; see [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]). The genealogy explains Athena's words that Arete “comes of the same / parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55): τοκῆες are here her forebears, since Alcinous is her uncle as well as her husband (Merry on 7.54; see the translation's note on 7.54–55).

**The suppliant's address.** Wrapped in the god-sent mist, Odysseus goes through the hall to the queen, throws his arms around her knees, and names her by her father: “Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor” (7.146; see [[supplication]] and [[antitheos]]). He has learned her lineage from Athena on the doorstep (7.54–66), and he uses it at once.
