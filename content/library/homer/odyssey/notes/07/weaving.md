**The work of women.** The chairs along the walls of the hall are covered with cloths, “fine and well-spun, … the work of women” (7.96–97; see [[throne]] and [[well-spun]]), as Hecuba's robes in her fragrant chamber are “richly embroidered, the work of women” (*Iliad* 6.289; see [[robe]]).

**At the looms.** Of the fifty slave women in the house, “others weave at the looms and turn the yarn / as they sit, like the leaves of a tall poplar; / and from the close-woven linen the liquid olive oil drips off” (7.105–107; see [[loom]], [[distaff]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). καιρουσσέων, “close-woven”, is contracted from καιροεσσέων, from καῖρος, the loop that holds each upright thread of the warp in the loom, as a scholium explains it; a web in which the καῖροι stand close together is of close texture (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.107). The Suda quotes the verse with the spelling καιροσέων, which the scholia give as Aristarchus' reading (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.107), and explains it in the same way, by the καίρωμα woven into the warp beside the thread so that the warp threads are not confused (κ 1193). ὀθόνη is any fine woven stuff (Suda ο 80); the girls who dance on the shield of Achilles wear it (*Iliad* 18.595; see [[veil]]).

**The oil.** What the dripping oil means was disputed. Nitzsch took the words for a sign of the closeness of the web, so close that the oil cannot soak in and runs off it, and quoted Plutarch, who said that Homer wrote καιροσέων to show the fineness of the weaving, since the oil does not stay on it but slips off; a passage of Athenaeus, in which a fuller will not give back a dress until he is brought oil, suggests rather that oil was used in dressing the cloth (both as Merry reports them). Most commentators understood the oil here, the tunics of the dancers “shining softly with oil” (*Iliad* 18.596) and the polished stones before Nestor's doors, “white, shining with oil” (3.408), as a glossy look only; Merry grants that this is conceivable in the Iliad's verse, but finds that so strong a phrase as ἀπολείβεται ἔλαιον goes far to support real oil, comparing the Hymn to Hestia, “ever from your locks the liquid oil drips” (Merry on 7.107; *Homeric Hymn* 24.3, in the same words; see [[bathing]] and [[olive]]).

**Athena's gift.** The Phaeacian women are as skilled at the loom as the men are at driving a swift ship, “for Athena has given them above all others / to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits” (7.108–111; see [[athena]] and [[phaeacians]]), the verse of Penelope's skill (2.117; see the Book 2 note).
