---
summary: Hephaestus made the gold and silver dogs that stand on either side of the door of Alcinous' house to guard it, “being immortal and ageless all their days” (7.91–94).
passages:
  - 7.91-94 | The gold and silver dogs of Hephaestus
---

**The dogs.** At the golden doors of the house, with their silver doorposts on a bronze threshold, “on either side were golden and silver dogs, / which Hephaestus had made with his cunning skill / to guard the house of great-hearted Alcinous, / being immortal and ageless all their days” (7.91–94; see [[the-gold-and-silver-dogs]], [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[alcinous]]). ἰδυίῃσι πραπίδεσσι, “with his cunning skill”, is said in Homer only of his work: the houses he built for the gods on Olympus, the self-moving tripods, the shield of Achilles and the polished colonnades of the house of Zeus (*Iliad* 1.608, 18.380, 18.482, 20.12; see [[with-cunning-skill]]). Of all the works of the house the dogs are the only ones the poem gives to a named god's hand; the orchard and the springs outside are, as a whole, “the splendid gifts of the gods” (7.132; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). Merry doubts that the poet meant more than images, of dogs here as of boys in the golden youths who hold the torches (on 7.91; 7.100–102), and compares the hyperbole with which Homer praises the god's other works, the tripods that move of themselves and the golden handmaids “like living young women” (*Iliad* 18.376, 18.418; see [[tripod]] and [[the-golden-handmaids]]).

**Immortal and ageless.** ἀθανάτους ὄντας καὶ ἀγήρως ἤματα πάντα gives the dogs the gods' condition, in the words of Hector's wish, “If only I were / immortal and ageless all my days” (*Iliad* 8.538–539), and of Calypso's offer, which Odysseus tells Arete later in the same book: she said that she would make him “immortal and ageless all my days”, and never persuaded him (7.257–258; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[calypso]]). The guards at the door have by a god's work what Odysseus refused from a goddess.

**Two gods in one house.** In the same house Athena has given the women their skill at the loom (7.110–111; see [[athena]] and [[weaving]]). In Book 6 the two gods together are the teachers of the skilled man who pours gold around silver (6.232–234; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]); in Alcinous' house each has left a gift, the smith in metal, the goddess in the women's work.
