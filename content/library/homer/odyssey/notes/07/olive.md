summary: Olives are the last of the five trees of Alcinous' orchard, “sweet figs and flourishing olives” (7.116), whose fruit never fails, winter or summer; and in his tale to Arete Odysseus sleeps again under the olive bushes of Book 5 (7.285–287).
# ἐλαῖαι (7.116), which also takes the olives that hang over Tantalus in the same verse (11.590).
grc: [ἐλαῖαι]
en: [olives]
---

**In the orchard.** The orchard outside the court of Alcinous has five kinds of tree, “pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, / and sweet figs and flourishing olives” (7.115–116; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]] and [[trees]]), and of these “the fruit never perishes or fails, / in winter or in summer, lasting all the year” (7.117–118). The olive is the only one of the five that the Iliad names, in the simile of the flourishing sapling a man rears in a lonely, well-watered place (*Iliad* 17.53–58; see [[the-olive-sapling]]); there the olive is uprooted by a sudden storm, here the West Wind only brings the fruit on and ripens it (7.119; see [[zephyrus]]). The two verses of the trees return in the underworld, over Tantalus, who can never reach their fruit (11.589–590).

**Under the olives.** When Odysseus tells Arete how he came ashore, he slept “among the bushes” and drew the leaves about him (7.285–286; see [[trees]]): the bushes are the two of Book 5, one of wild olive and one of olive, grown from one spot, so thick that neither wind nor sun nor rain came through them (5.476–480; see the Book 5 note).

**The oil.** The olive oil of the book is in the house: from the close-woven linen that the slave women weave “the liquid olive oil drips off” (7.107; see [[weaving]] and the translation's note on 7.107).
