summary: No dawn rises in Book 7, which passes in a single evening and night. Dawn is the time the Phaeacians plan for, when Odysseus asks them to make haste “when dawn appears” (7.222), and the time he slept through in the leaves, “all night long, and into the dawn and to midday” (7.288).
# Unnamed: ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφιν "when dawn appears" (7.222), as in the Book 4 and Book 6 notes (4.407, 6.31), and ἐπʼ
# ἠῶ "into the dawn" (7.288), as ἠῶ (2.434). Not counted: ἠῶθεν "at dawn" (7.189), as in the earlier books' notes.
refs: [7.222, 7.288]

**The dawn to come.** The book is the evening and night after the long day of Book 6, from Odysseus' prayer in the grove at sunset to his sleep in the colonnade (6.321–328, 7.1–347; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). Its dawn is the next one, which all of them wait for. Alcinous will call more of the elders “at dawn” to entertain the stranger and to plan his escort (7.189–191; see [[elders]] and [[pompe]]); Odysseus asks the leaders, “But you, make haste when dawn appears, / so that you may set me, unhappy man, on my native land” (7.222–223; see [[dawn-line]]); and Alcinous fixes the escort “for tomorrow” (7.318). That dawn opens the next book, in the verse of the Odyssey's other mornings, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (8.1 = 2.1; see [[early-born]] and [[rosy-fingered]]).

**The dawn of the tale.** Telling Arete how he slept in the leaves on the night he came ashore, Odysseus counts the hours: “I slept all night long, and into the dawn and to midday. / And the sun was declining, and sweet sleep let me go” (7.288–289; see [[hypnos]] and [[helios]]). The dawn he slept through is the dawn of Book 6, when Dawn of the fair throne woke Nausicaa in the city (6.48–49; see the Book 6 note).
