# Unnamed: the master and the labors (11.620-622); the hound brought up from Hades (11.623-626).

**The other teller.** In the Iliad the story is Athena's, told in anger to Hera: she saved Zeus' son whenever Eurystheus' labors wore him down, and if she had known what she knows now, when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades the gatekeeper “to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades”, he would never have escaped the waters of Styx (*Iliad* 8.362–369). Here the hero tells it, among the dead, to a living man who has come there as he did: “ah, wretch, truly you too drag out some evil fate, / such as I bore under the rays of the sun” (11.618–619; see [[heracles]] and [[wretch]]). The worse man who laid labors on him is Eurystheus, bound to him by the spite of Hera (11.620–622; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.621; *Iliad* 19.95–133; see [[eurystheus]]). Of all the labors he names one, the one that brought him to the place where Odysseus sees him: “And once he sent me here to bring the hound; for he could think / of no other labor that would be harder for me than this; / and I brought it up and led it out of Hades, / and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena” (11.623–626; see [[contests]]). The text prints τοῦδέ γε, with La Roche and good manuscripts; others read τοῦδέ τι or τοῦδʼ ἔτι (Merry's critical note on 11.624; see the translation's note on 11.614, 11.622, 11.624).

**The guides.** Athena's part agrees with her own account in the Iliad; Hermes is added, the god who guides, as he leads the souls of the suitors down in the last book (24.1–14, Greek only; see [[hermes]] and [[athena]]). ἔπεμψεν is “conducted me”, the verb with which Nausicaa offers to see the stranger to her father's house (Merry on 11.626, citing 6.255).

**The hound unnamed.** Homer does not list the labors of Heracles, nor does he name the dog, which first appears as Cerberus in Hesiod (*Theogony* 311). Merry reports from Pausanias that Hecataeus spoke of a dread serpent at Taenarum called the hound of Hades, and takes the Iliad's verse to show that the story was known to Homer in a simple form, the name and the other details being a later growth (on 11.623). The phantom that tells it is itself a puzzle of the passage, for “he himself” is among the gods (11.602–604); for the doubts on those verses, see [[heracles]] and the translation's note on 11.602–604.
