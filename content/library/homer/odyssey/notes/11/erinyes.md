---
summary: The Erinyes are named once in Book 11, at the end of the story of Oedipus' mother: when she had hanged herself, “to him she left sorrows behind, / very many, all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass” (11.279–280).
---

**The Erinyes of a mother.** Epicaste married her own son, and when the gods made it known, she “went to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper, / when she had fastened a noose hanging sheer from the high roof-beam, / held fast by her own grief; but to him she left sorrows behind, / very many, all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass” (11.277–280; see [[jocasta]], [[oedipus]], [[oedipus-kills-laius]] and [[hades]]). μητρὸς Ἐρινύες are the powers who carry out a mother's curse, as Athena tells Ares in the Iliad that he is paying for his mother's Erinyes (*Iliad* 21.412; see the translation's key-word row and [[curses]]). The poem does not say that Epicaste cursed her son; her death leaves him the sorrows that such a curse would bring. Merry, on this verse, sends the reader to his note on Telemachus' fear of “the hateful Erinyes” (2.135), where he describes them as the agents of the gods below, the avengers of perjury and the executors of the parental curse, so that the Erinys may even stand for the curse itself, as at *Iliad* 21.412; in a word, their office is the punishment of crimes against the family and against society (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.280 and 2.135; see the Book 2 note).

**The story of Oedipus.** The Erinyes close what Merry calls the simplest and probably earliest form of the story, in which the gods make the marriage known at once, and Oedipus goes on ruling the Cadmeans in Thebes, “suffering sorrows … / through the deadly plans of the gods” (11.274–276; Merry on 11.271; see the translation's notes on 11.273–274 and 11.275–276). His sorrows are not told, only that they were all that a mother's Erinyes bring to pass.
