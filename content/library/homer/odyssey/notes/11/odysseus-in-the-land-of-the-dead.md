summary: The adventure fills the book, in thirteen scenes told in one night: the voyage to the limits of Oceanus and the rite at the pit (11.1–50); Elpenor, Teiresias and Anticleia (11.51–224); the women sent by Persephone (11.225–332); the pause in the hall of Alcinous (11.333–384); Agamemnon, Achilles and Ajax (11.385–567); Minos, Orion, Tityus, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Heracles (11.568–627); and the din of the dead and the flight to the ship (11.628–640). Odysseus tells it all from his place by the pit, and the dead come to him.

**The voyage.** κατήλθομεν, “we had come down” to the ship and the sea, is the regular word for coming from inland to the coast (11.1; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.1). The sheep are those that Circe had tethered by the ship (11.4; 10.571–572; Merry on 11.4), and the men grieve for the dreadful journey ahead (11.5; Merry on 11.5). The wind is the one Circe sends again when they leave Aeaea for the last time, in the same three verses (11.6–8 = 12.148–150, Greek only; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]). The voyage takes a single day, πανημερίη, and Merry hears in the dactyls of 11.11 the ship dancing over the waves (on 11.11). At sunset, in the verse of the evenings of the Telemachy (11.12 = 2.388; see [[sunset-lines]]), the ship comes to πείρατα Ὠκεανοῖο, “the limits of Oceanus”, which Merry takes as the boundary formed by Ocean to the land of the living, not Ocean's farthest shore (11.13; Merry on 11.13; see [[oceanus]]). For the Cimmerians and their sunless land, see the translation's note on 11.14. Circe had told Odysseus to cross Oceanus and beach his ship by the shore where the groves of Persephone grow, and had named the rivers of the dead and the rock where two of them meet (10.508–515); here nothing more is heard of the shore, the groves or the rivers, and the notice of the Cimmerians comes instead. The men go along the stream “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22), φράζω referring, by the rule of Aristarchus, not to her words but to the signs by which the spot could be known (Merry on 11.22).

**The rite.** Perimedes and Eurylochus, who hold the victims, are the two most important of the companions (11.23; Merry on 11.23, comparing 12.195 and 10.205; see [[eurylochus]]). The rite is the one Circe prescribed, carried out in her verses turned to the first person (11.25–50; 10.516–537; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]], [[the-land-of-the-dead]] and the translation's note on 10.516–537). Six verses of the souls' gathering, the brides, the young men, the old men, the girls and the men in armor clotted with blood (11.38–43), were athetized by Zenodotus and Aristophanes, as the scholia report, because the souls do not come up together in the sequel, but one at a time, and because wounds are not seen on phantoms. Those who defended them called the scene an anticipatory summary, and answered that the shades keep the look they had in life: Orion still hunts with his club, Agamemnon comes with the men who fell around him, and Ajax keeps his haughty bearing. Merry finds the objections “hardly conclusive”; Bergk would move the verses to follow 11.632, where the tribes of the dead gather at the end (Merry on 11.38–43; see the translation's note on 11.38–43). At 11.43 Odysseus' fear has no cause but the cries of the shades, while at 11.633 there is good ground for terror (Merry on 11.43; see [[pale-fear]]).

**The blood.** Odysseus sits by the pit with his sword held over the blood, and lets none of the dead come near it before Teiresias (11.48–50, 11.82, 11.88–89). The dead differ in their need of it. Elpenor speaks without drinking (11.82): not yet buried, Merry explains, he had not passed into the full condition of “the strengthless heads of the dead” (11.29, 11.49; Merry on 11.82; see [[the-strengthless-heads]]). Teiresias knows Odysseus and speaks to him before he drinks, for his mind is steadfast among the dead (10.493–495), and he asks for the blood only so that he may “tell you truly” (11.91–96); the blood, Merry concludes, was for him a gratification, the libation proper to the dead, as the sacrifice on the raised altar is to the gods of heaven, and the scholia say that the souls come to it like flies to honey (Merry on 11.96). The verse in which he addresses Odysseus by name (11.92) is found in Eustathius and a few manuscripts only, and bears on the same question (see the translation's note on 11.60, 11.92). His mother sits by the blood in silence and does not know her son until she drinks (11.141–144, 11.153), as Teiresias' rule explains: whichever of the dead he lets come to the blood will speak truly, and the others will go back (11.146–149; see [[teiresias]]). Agamemnon knows him “when he had drunk the dark blood” (11.390; see [[dark-blood]]); no drinking is told of Achilles, who knows him (11.471), or of Heracles, who knows him “when he saw me with his eyes” (11.615), and Ajax will not speak (11.563; see [[psyche]], [[eidolon]] and [[meeting-with-the-dead]]).

**One at a time.** Elpenor comes first, because, being unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades (11.51; Merry on 11.51; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). The mother who follows is kept back for Teiresias (11.84–89). The women come up one after another, προμνηστῖναι, and each tells her own parentage (11.233–234; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). After the pause the narrative is resumed with αὐτὰρ ἐπεί, “But when”, where it broke off (11.385; Merry on 11.385; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]): Persephone, who sent the women up, scatters them, and the heroes of the war come in their place (11.226, 11.385–387). Achilles comes with Patroclus, Antilochus and Ajax (11.467–470). When he has gone, the other souls stand grieving, each asking about its own sorrows (11.541–542), and Ajax alone stands apart (11.543–567; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).

**The sights.** From 11.565 Odysseus no longer questions the dead who come to the pit. He sees Minos judging, Orion hunting, and Tityus, Tantalus and Sisyphus at their punishments, and Heracles comes and goes back into the house of Hades (11.568–627). Merry, following the scholium that marks 11.568–627 as spurious, thinks that the addition begins at 11.565, where ὅμως, “still”, is found only here in the poem (see the translation's note on 11.565–567). Odysseus did not, like Aeneas, go down into the world below, but sat at the edge of the trench and questioned the shades as they came to taste the blood; in these verses the story is told by one who is an eyewitness of what is going on in the depths of Erebus, and the view of the punishment of the dead seems later than Homer. Plato already knew the passage, so that the addition is early: Socrates says that Homer has made kings and rulers the ones punished forever in Hades, Tantalus and Sisyphus and Tityus, and quotes the verse of Minos and his golden scepter (*Gorgias* 525d–e, 526c–d; 11.569; see [[plato]]). La Roche looked to an Attic source, and a scholium says that a verse of the Heracles passage was put in by Onomacritus (on 11.604), one of the men named as helpers of Peisistratus in his recension of the poems (Merry on 11.565). Herodotus tells how Onomacritus, the arranger of the oracles of Musaeus, was caught by Lasus of Hermione inserting an oracle into them and was banished by Hipparchus (*Histories* 7.6.3; see [[herodotus]]). For the doubts on 11.602–604 and 11.631, see the translation's notes, and [[heracles]] and [[theseus-line]].

**The din of the dead.** Odysseus stays steadfastly in the hope of seeing more of the heroes of old (11.628–631), in the half-verse with which he waited for his mother (11.152, 11.628). The tribes of the dead gather “with a wondrous din” (11.633), and Odysseus fears that Persephone will send against him “the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster” (11.634): Merry takes the phrase of any horrible specter, not of the Gorgon's head itself (on 11.634; see [[gorgon]]). The companions go aboard, loose the stern-cables and sit at the benches in the verses of the departures of the ninth book (11.637–638 = 9.178–179, 9.562–563; see [[stern-cables]] and [[rowing-benches]]). The current carries the ship down the river, “first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind” (11.640): there was no wind, Merry suggests, because they were in an unearthly place beyond the ordinary air, and the fair wind came when they were back in the world of men (on 11.640 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.640).
