{{quote:daf99ea1-125a-5b89-936e-6bc0c735d0b9}}

σῆμα … ἀριφραδές, “a sign, very easy to see”, is Teiresias' word for it (11.126; see [[teras]]): men who know nothing of the sea, ships or oars (11.122–125) will see in the oar only the tool they know. W. W. Merry takes it as the proof that such a country had been reached, a mistake that would not be absurd in men who had never seen the sea, since both the oar and the winnowing shovel are blades (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.128; see [[oars]]). The ancients, he reports, took the people of the oracle for the Epirots or Thesprotians, who answer badly to the description; it probably means only that Odysseus was to go as far inland as he could on the mainland nearest Ithaca (on 11.122). The fixing of the oar in the ground, where the sea-god's sacrifice is then made, plants the token of the sea in a land that has never known it (see [[poseidon]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]).
