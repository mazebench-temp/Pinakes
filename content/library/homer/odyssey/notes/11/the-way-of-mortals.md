---
summary: Anticleia's answer ends her meeting with her son (11.216–224): after it she sends him back toward the light, and the women come. The rest of the book bears it out, in Agamemnon's hands that cannot reach him (11.392–394), and makes its exceptions of the sons of Leda and of Heracles (11.301–304, 11.601–604).
---

**The end of the meeting.** The saying closes the longest conversation of the book. Anticleia has answered her son's questions about his wife, his son, his father and her own death (11.180–203; see [[anticleia]]); three times he tries to embrace her, and asks whether she is a phantom sent to grieve him (11.204–214); her answer turns from the rule to a farewell: “But long for the light as quickly as you can; and all these things / know, so that you may tell them to your wife hereafter” (11.223–224). She bids him press back to the light of the upper world and bear in mind all he has learned, and Eustathius took the last words for nothing more than a kindly feeling toward Penelope, “to show her friendship” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.223; see [[penelope]]). Then Persephone sends the women (11.225–227; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]).

**The rule in the book.** What she says of the sinews and the fire holds for every soul that follows. Agamemnon stretches out his hands to Odysseus, “but no longer was there steadfast strength in him, or any vigor, / such as there was before in his supple limbs” (11.392–394; see [[agamemnon]]); κῖκυς, “vigor”, is found only here in Homer (Merry on 11.393; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.393). Two passages make exceptions: the sons of Leda, held alive beneath the earth (11.301–304), and Heracles, whose phantom is among the dead while he himself feasts with the gods (11.601–604; see [[immortality]] and [[eidolon]]).
