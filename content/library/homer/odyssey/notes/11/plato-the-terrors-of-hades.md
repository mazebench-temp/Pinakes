summary: Plato's list of the verses that make Hades fearful begins with this book, with Achilles' answer to Odysseus: he would rather be bound to the soil and serve another for hire than be lord over all the dead. Proclus took the verses for a true account of a soul of action that longs for the life it has lost, and saw in the jest that opens the myth of Er Plato's refusal of Homer's Nekyia.

**The first verse struck out.** The poets, Socrates says, must be asked not to revile the things in Hades but rather to praise them, since what they say is neither true nor useful for men who are to be fighters; “We shall strike out, then, beginning from this verse, all such things”, and he quotes Achilles' three verses (*Republic* 3.386b–c, plain renderings throughout; see [[plato]] and [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]). His text agrees with ours word for word, ἐπάρουρος included, where some copies read πάρουρος (see the translation's note on 11.489). In the poem the verses answer Odysseus' consolation, that Achilles was honored like a god among the living and is mighty now among the dead, so that he should not grieve at being dead (11.482–486; see [[achilles]] and [[three-times-blessed]]). Plato quotes neither the consolation nor what follows, the questions about Peleus and Neoptolemus and the soul's long strides across the meadow of asphodel, glad at the news of its son (11.492–540). The other verses on the list come from the Iliad, from Circe's words on Teiresias in the book before, “but the others flit as shadows” (10.495), and from the last book of the Odyssey (24.6–9); from this book Plato takes only Achilles' answer.

**Again in the cave.** Plato uses the verses once more, this time in his own voice. The prisoner who has been led out of the cave into the light and remembers his fellow prisoners would not envy those who are honored and powerful among them, but would rather have felt what Homer says, and very much wish “to be bound to the soil and serve another for hire, a man who had no land of his own”, and suffer anything rather than hold their opinions and live as they do (*Republic* 7.516c–d). The verses that the guardians must not hear say there what the philosopher feels.

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus sets Achilles' words first in his list of Homer's sayings about Hades, “the service in this life preferred to all the goods in Hades” (*On the Republic* I 118 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). He answers that the souls that leave the body differ. Those that lived a life of action and have not yet given it up cling to the instrument that served their actions, and in Hades they long for its company, “like the soul of Achilles, because it prefers the life here to the separate life, since it cannot act in that one but was first in the life of action”. What wonder, then, that Achilles, a man of practical virtue, should long for the life with the body, which could serve his deeds? Heracles, purified by the rites, was restored entirely to the gods, and Proclus quotes the verses of his phantom, “but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts” (11.602–603); Achilles still loves success in action, and pursues this life and longs for its instrument, as Plato himself says that souls mostly choose their second lives according to their habits (I 119–120 Kroll; see [[heracles]] and [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]). In another essay Proclus explains the opening of the myth of Er, “not a tale to Alcinous”, as a sign that Plato did not accept Homer's Nekyia, which likens the souls in Hades to shadows and brings on “the bravest of men on earth” longing for the life here, but told his own story as befits men who are to be brave and to face death without fear (II 112 Kroll; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]).
