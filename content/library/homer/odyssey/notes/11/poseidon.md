summary: Poseidon is the god of Teiresias' prophecy and of the first heroine's tale. Teiresias tells Odysseus that he will not escape “the shaker of the earth”, who is “angry because you blinded his dear son” (11.102–103), and that when the suitors are dead he must carry an oar inland until he comes to men who do not know the sea, and there “make fine sacrifices to lord Poseidon” (11.130). In the likeness of the river Enipeus the god lay with Tyro and told her at last, “but I, you must know, am Poseidon the earth-shaker” (11.252); Iphimedeia “declared that she had lain with Poseidon” (11.306); and Odysseus asks the soul of Agamemnon whether it was “among the ships that Poseidon brought you down” (11.399).

**The shaker of the earth.** Teiresias' first words, when he has drunk the blood, are of Poseidon: “You seek a honey-sweet homecoming, shining Odysseus; / but a god will make it grievous for you; for I do not think / you will escape the shaker of the earth, since he has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (11.100–103; see [[teiresias]], [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). The seer says “a god” before he names him, and then names him only by his title, ἐννοσίγαιος. ὅ is the conjunction, “in that he hath stored up wrath in his heart against thee”, and Merry compares the Achaeans who “are laying up anger” against Agamemnon in the Iliad (*Iliad* 14.50; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.102; see the translation's note on 11.102). The cause is the one that Zeus gave at the first council, “angry because of the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye” (1.69; see the Book 1 note), but the seer does not name the son (see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). On Ithaca Athena will say the same verses to Odysseus, with the relative ὅς: “my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.342–343, a plain rendering; see [[athena]]).

**The Cyclops' curse.** The prophecy grants what the Cyclops asked of his father. If the companions harm the cattle of the Sun, Odysseus “will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house” (11.114–115): the last verses of the Cyclops' prayer, put into the second person (9.534–535; see [[curses]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and the Book 9 note). Between the god's anger and the curse the seer sets a condition that the curse did not have: “But even so you may still come home, though suffering evils, / if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'” (11.104–105).

**Lord Poseidon.** The end of the prophecy is a reconciliation. When he has killed the suitors, Odysseus is to take a well-fitted oar and go “until you come to men who do not know the sea” (11.122), and when a wayfarer says that he carries a winnowing fan on his shoulder, he is to fix the oar in the ground, “and make fine sacrifices to lord Poseidon, / a ram and a bull and a boar that mounts the sows”, and then go home and offer hecatombs to all the gods in order (11.121–134; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]], [[oars]], [[winnowing-fan]], [[sacrifice]] and [[hecatomb]]). Merry reads the commands as serious: as the wrath of Poseidon was the prime cause of Odysseus' dangers and trials, so a final reconciliation with him must be the prelude to the peaceful close of his life. Among a people to whom the sea, and still more the name of Poseidon, is unknown, “there, in that most unlikely place, he shall publish the name of the ruler of the ocean”, and leave the oar planted as a memorial (Merry on 11.119). He accepts Eustathius' account of the purpose of the journey, that Poseidon should be honored as a god of the mainland in places where his name is not spoken (Merry on 11.122). The three victims make a sacrifice of a special kind. Eustathius, as Merry reports, says that the ancients called a sacrifice of three animals a τριττύς, which might be made up of sheep, oxen, goats and swine in different ways, the number three giving the sense of completeness, and Nitzsch compares the Roman *suovetaurilia*; the usual offering to the god of the sea was a black bull, as at Pylos, where the people sacrifice “bulls all black, to the dark-haired earth-shaker” (3.6; Merry on 11.131; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). Odysseus tells the command to Penelope in almost the same verses (23.276–278).

**Poseidon and Tyro.** The first of the heroines “fell in love with a river, the divine Enipeus” (11.238; see [[tyro]], [[tyro-and-her-sons]] and [[enipeus]]). “And in his likeness the earth-holder, the shaker of the earth, / lay with her at the mouth of the eddying river; / and the surging wave stood around them, high as a mountain, / arched over, and hid the god and the mortal woman” (11.241–244; see [[earth-holder]]). τῷ δʼ ἄρα εἰσάμενος is “having made himself like unto him”, the river-god, and Merry sets the story beside the Iliad's unions of mortal women with rivers, Periboea with the Axius and Polydora with the Spercheius (*Iliad* 21.141–143, 16.174–176; Merry on 11.241). ἐν προχοῇς is the mouth of the river, used loosely, since the Enipeus does not run into the sea where the sea-god could come to it, but into the Peneus (Merry on 11.242). The god sheds sleep on her (11.245), a verse rejected in antiquity, since there was no need of sleep for a woman who loved him and was willing (the scholium in Merry's critical note). Hayman suggested that the sleep gave the god time to take his own form again, comparing Aphrodite, who sheds sweet sleep on Anchises before she stands by his couch in her immortal beauty (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 170–176); Merry thinks the clause more likely means no more than that he shed sleep upon her (Merry on 11.245). When the works of love are done, he takes her hand and names himself: “Rejoice in this love, lady; and as the year circles round / you will bear splendid children, for not in vain are the embraces / of the immortals; and you must look after them and rear them. / But now go to your house, and keep silent, and do not name me; / but I, you must know, am Poseidon the earth-shaker” (11.248–252). χαῖρε is a blessing, “Be happy in this love” (Merry on 11.248), and ἴσχεο is “refrain thyself” from speaking (Merry on 11.251). The god keeps his name for the end and gives it with his title ἐνοσίχθων, “the earth-shaker”, where the narrator had called him γαιήοχος ἐννοσίγαιος (11.241; see [[earth-shaker]] and the translation's key-word row). Then “he plunged beneath the billowing sea” (11.253), the verse of Eidothea and of Proteus (4.425, 4.570). Tyro's sons by him are Pelias and Neleus (11.254–257; see [[pelias]] and [[neleus]]), and so the god is the grandfather of Nestor (11.286; see [[nestor]]).

**Iphimedeia.** “And after her I saw Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus, / who declared that she had lain with Poseidon, / and she bore two sons, but they were short-lived” (11.305–307; see [[iphimedeia]]): Otus and Ephialtes, “the tallest men the grain-giving earth ever reared”, whom the son of Leto killed before they could pile the mountains on Olympus (11.308–320; see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]], [[aloeus]], [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[apollo]]). φάσκε, “declared”, leaves the claim to her, as φάτο gave Tyro's descent (11.236) and εὔχετο Antiope's boast (11.261); Merry calls it the common form of legend, as in the stories of Tyro, Antiope, Alcmene and Leda, and reports that Aloeus himself was called a son of Poseidon, by Canace the daughter of Aeolus (Merry on 11.305).

**Not among the ships.** Odysseus' first guess at Agamemnon's death is a storm at sea: “Was it among the ships that Poseidon brought you down, / rousing a blast of grievous winds that none would envy?” (11.399–400; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-winds]]). It is the death that the god gave the lesser Ajax on the way home from Troy, when he split the Gyraean rock with his trident (4.499–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Agamemnon denies it in the same words (11.406–407). Aristophanes rejected the question, 11.399–403, as carried over from the answer, and 11.407 is missing from most manuscripts (Merry's critical notes; see the translation's notes on 11.399–403 and 11.407).
