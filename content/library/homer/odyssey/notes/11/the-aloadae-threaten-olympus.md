summary: The story is told within the catalogue of women, as the deed of the sons of Iphimedeia (11.305–320): their size, their threat of war against the gods, the mountains they meant to pile up to heaven, and their death at the hands of Apollo before they grew to manhood.
# Unnamed: the sons (11.307-312); the threat and the mountains (11.313-317); their death (11.318-320).

**Told of the mother.** The brothers come into the tale through their mother, who “declared that she had lain with Poseidon” (11.306), as Tyro was loved by Poseidon and Antiope claimed Zeus (11.241–252, 11.261; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). Merry notes the verb, φάσκε, “declared”, as Tyro “said” (φάτο, 11.236) and Antiope “claimed” (εὔχετο, 11.261), and the pattern: the common form of the legend, which makes a god the father, comes back in the stories of Tyro, Antiope, Alcmene and Leda (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.305). μινυνθαδίω, “short-lived”, is explained by the end of the story (11.307, 11.318–320; Merry on 11.307). For the brothers' names, their size and the verses of the threat, see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[iphimedeia]].

**Would have.** “And they would have accomplished it, if they had reached the measure of youth” (11.317): the verse of what almost happened, turned by the god who killed them (see [[would-have-had-not]]). Those who defended the doubted verses of the mountains (11.315–316) laid the stress on μέμασαν, “they were eager”: the brothers had the will to do it, but not the power (Merry on 11.315–316, reporting Eustathius; see the translation's note on 11.315–316 and [[ossa-mountain]]).

**The killer.** Apollo is named only as “the son of Zeus, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore” (11.318; see [[apollo]] and [[leto]]); Pindar, as Merry reports, gives the killing to Artemis, on Naxos (on 11.318). They die young, before the down had covered their jaws (11.319–320).
