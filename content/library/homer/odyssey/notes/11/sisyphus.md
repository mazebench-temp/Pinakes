summary: The last of the three whom Odysseus sees punished. Sisyphus pushes a huge stone with hands and feet toward the crest of a hill; whenever he is about to heave it over the top, its weight turns it back and “the shameless stone” rolls down to the plain, and he pushes it up again, sweating, with the dust rising from his head (11.593–600).

**The labor.** The verse that brings Sisyphus on is the one that brought on Tantalus, “suffering bitter pains” (11.582, 11.593; see [[tantalus]] and [[algos]]), and like Tantalus he suffers without a crime named. He is seen “heaving a huge stone with both hands” (11.594; see [[huge]]), and the verbs that follow are iteratives, ὤθεσκε, ἀποστρέψασκε, ὤσασκε: the same thing happens every time. In ἀλλʼ ὅτε μέλλοι / ἄκρον ὑπερβαλέειν, Sisyphus, not the stone, is the subject: “but ever as he was just going to topple it over the hill's crest” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.596). What turns the stone back is κραταιίς, “its mighty weight”: either a power in the stone itself, “the force of gravity”, as a scholiast's gloss, “the mighty power, that is, the weight”, suggests, or a Power outside it, like the δαίμων that dries up Tantalus' water (11.587), and like Crataeis, whom Circe calls “the mother of Scylla” (12.124–125); Merry sets aside Aristarchus, who took the word for an adverb, and Ptolemy of Ascalon, who divided it into two (Merry on 11.597; see the translation's note on 11.596–597).

**The sound of the verses.** Merry quotes Eustathius on 11.596: the vowels crowded together in λᾶαν ἄνω ὤθεσκε fill the mouth and keep the verse from running, so that it goes slowly, like the labor of pushing uphill, while the next verse but one, without a hiatus, runs, and shows how easily the stone falls; and he adds Quintilian, that when vowels meet, the speech gapes and halts and as it were labors (*Institutio oratoria* 9.4, as Merry quotes it; Merry on 11.596). The falling verse, αὖτις ἔπειτα πέδονδε κυλίνδετο λᾶας ἀναιδής, “back then to the plain rolled the shameless stone” (11.598), is all dactyls to its last foot, and Merry gives other dactylic lines that express rapid motion, in Homer and in Virgil (on 11.597–598). He takes ἀναιδής, “shameless”, for the original of Virgil's *improbus* (on 11.597–598; see [[shameless-stone]] and [[anaideia]]).

**Aristotle's line.** Aristotle quotes 11.598 in the *Rhetoric* as Homer's way of making lifeless things alive by metaphor, with ἐπὶ δάπεδόνδε, “down to the ground”, where our text has ἔπειτα πέδονδε (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1411b34–35; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]] and the translation's note on 11.598).

**The sweat and the dust.** “But he would push it up again, straining, and the sweat / ran from his limbs, and dust rose up from his head” (11.599–600). As he thrusts his hands forward against the stone, Merry explains, his head is brought down near the ground, so that the dust raised by his feet and by the moving stone seems to rise from it (Merry on 11.600). The poem does not say whether the stone ever goes over.

**Later Latin.** Merry cites an imitation of the line that Cicero quotes, and that he gives to Lucilius: *Sisyphus versat / saxum sudans nitendo neque proficit hilum*, “Sisyphus rolls his stone, sweating with straining, and gets not a whit forward” (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 1.10, where the verse is not named; Merry on 11.596; see [[cicero]]). Lucretius made the stone the image of the man who seeks office from the people and always comes away beaten: to strive for power, which is empty and never given, is “to push uphill, straining, a stone which from the very summit rolls back again and makes headlong for the levels of the plain” (*On the Nature of Things* 3.995–1002, a plain rendering; see [[lucretius-the-punishments-of-acheron]]). Virgil's Sibyl tells of sinners in Tartarus who “roll a huge stone” (*Aeneid* 6.616; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]).
