summary: In the pause of the tale she is the first to speak: she asks the Phaeacians what they think of the stranger, in looks and stature and mind, claims him as her own guest, though each of them shares in the honor, and bids them not to hurry him away nor cut short their gifts, for their halls are full of possessions. Echeneus approves her words, and Alcinous makes them good (11.335–353).

**The first to speak.** When Odysseus breaks off among the heroines, the Phaeacians sit hushed, “held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (11.333–334; see [[thelgo]] and [[silence]]), and it is the queen who speaks first, as it was at her knees that the stranger had made his first appeal (7.142; see [[supplication]]):

**Her words.** πῶς … φαίνεται εἶναι is used like the later πῶς ἔχειν, “how seemeth he to stand in your eyes?” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.336); φρένες ἐῖσαι is “the well-balanced mind”, if ἐΐσας is taken as ἶσος, “equal” (Merry on 11.337; see the translation's key words and [[phrenes]]). The words δʼ αὖτε mark a new thought: she seems to find her answer in the approving looks of the Phaeacians, and goes on, “Now, he is my guest”, hers especially because he made his first appeal to her, “but each one of you has a share in the honor” of entertaining him (Merry on 11.338; see [[white-armed]], [[xenia]] and [[time]]). ἕκαστος δʼ ἔμμορε τιμῆς is the verse-end of Poseidon's account of the threefold division of the world among the sons of Cronus (*Iliad* 15.189; Merry on 11.338). The gifts are those already given at the end of the games (8.438–445); Arete knows that more will come if his departure is a little delayed, as Alcinous proposes (13.13, Greek only), and κολούετε, “cut short”, means limiting the number of the gifts, not taking back any that were promised (Merry on 11.339, 11.340; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[possessions]]).

**Echeneus and the king.** The old Echeneus answers that “our prudent queen” speaks not wide of the mark nor against their expectation, but that on Alcinous depend both deed and word (11.342–346; see [[echeneus]], [[periphron]] and [[basileus]]), and the king answers that “This word shall indeed be so, as surely as I / live and am lord over the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar”: their guest must stay until the morrow, until the whole present is complete (11.347–353; see [[alcinous]]). τοῦτο is the queen's suggestion, to add to the gifts and keep the guest a little longer, and the emphasis is on ζωός, “as sure as I am alive and reigning” (Merry on 11.348). When the tale is over, the gifts are brought to the ship (13.10–22, Greek only).
