summary: Twice in the scene of Anticleia the soul of the dead is likened to a dream. Three times she flew from Odysseus' hands “like a shadow or a dream” (11.207), and she tells him that when the fire has burned the body, “the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers” (11.222).
# ὀνείρῳ (11.207) is claimed here, as a form of the Odyssey, where it is always the dream (later 19.541, 19.581, 21.79);
# in the Iliad its one occurrence is the simile of 22.199 (as-in-a-dream). ὄνειρος (11.222) is claimed by the entry.
grc: [ὀνείρῳ]
---

**Like a shadow or a dream.** When his mother has told him of her death, Odysseus tries to embrace her: “Three times I sprang toward her, and my heart bade me clasp her, / and three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream” (11.206–207; see [[like-a-shadow-or-a-dream]], [[anticleia]], [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]). σκιή and ὄνειρος are “shadow” and “dream” (see the translation's key-word row), the two things that can be seen but not held; Merry takes εἴκελον adverbially, as ἶσον at 11.557 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.207). Achilles' arms closed on nothing in the same way when he reached for the soul of Patroclus, which “went beneath the earth like smoke” (*Iliad* 23.99–101; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). Virgil, as Merry notes, gave the three embraces to Aeneas and the shade of Creusa, and kept the dream: *ter conatus ibi collo dare bracchia circum; / ter frustra comprensa manus effugit imago, / par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno*, “three times he tried to throw his arms around her neck; three times the image, grasped in vain, escaped his hands, like the light winds and most like a winged dream” (*Aeneid* 2.792–794; Merry on 11.206; see [[virgil]]).

**The soul like a dream.** Anticleia answers that this is “the way of mortals, when one of them dies”: the sinews no longer hold the flesh and bones, the fire overcomes them, “and the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers” (11.218–222; see [[the-way-of-mortals]]). πεπότηται is a continuous state, “hovers to and fro” (Merry on 11.222). ἀποπταμένη, “flitting away”, is the word of the Dream that Zeus sent to Agamemnon in the Iliad, which spoke and “flew away and was gone” (*Iliad* 2.71); there the dream was a messenger, here it is the likeness of what the dead have become (see the general article and [[as-in-a-dream]]).
