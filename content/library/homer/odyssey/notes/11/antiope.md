**Her claim.** εὔχετο here is “claimed”, not “prayed” (see [[euchomai]]): Antiope boasted, W. W. Merry explains, that her lover was higher even than Poseidon, Tyro's lover, for it was Zeus himself, and καὶ Διός, “even … of Zeus”, makes the point (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.261; see [[zeus]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The words are those of Aphrodite to Hera in the Iliad, “for you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the best of gods” (*Iliad* 14.213). Her father is the Boeotian river Asopus, which the Iliad calls “deep in rushes, with grassy meadows” (*Iliad* 4.383; see [[asopus]]), and Merry counts her among the Boeotian legends of the catalogue (on 11.260).

**The walls of Thebes.** Her sons are the founders of the city: Θήβης ἕδος, “the seat of seven-gated Thebes”, are the words in which Sthenelus boasts that the sons took Thebes (*Iliad* 4.406), and Ithaca has its ἕδος too (13.344). A fenced city was needed, Merry suggests, against the wild tribes around Thebes, and the singular Θήβη is the older form of Θῆβαι, as Μυκήνη of Μυκῆναι (on 11.263; see [[thebes]], [[seven-gated]] and [[amphion-of-thebes]]).
