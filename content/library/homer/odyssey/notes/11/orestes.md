summary: His dead father complains that his wife killed him before he could have his fill of the sight of his son, and asks Odysseus for news of him, “perhaps in Orchomenus, or in sandy Pylos, / or perhaps with Menelaus in broad Sparta; / for brilliant Orestes has not yet died upon the earth”. Odysseus does not know whether he is alive or dead (11.452–464).

**The son not seen.** “But my wife did not even let me have my fill of my son / with my eyes; before that she killed me myself” (11.452–453; see [[agamemnon]] and [[clytemnestra]]): she gave him no loving welcome, and did not let him have even the joy of meeting his son (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.452). Then the question:

{{quote:3d809169-1235-514f-877d-ed0fccf6716d}}

ἀκούετε is said to Odysseus and his companions, who might have heard of the lost Orestes on their travels. It is implied, Merry thinks, that Orestes was away from Mycenae when his father came home, so that the murder took place in his absence; the places are named only as refuges far from Mycenae, there is no clue which Orchomenus is meant, and it is surprising that Phocis and Athens are not named, since in the Odyssey's own account Orestes came back from Athens (3.307); Agamemnon takes it for granted that his son is not dead, or they would have met among the shades (Merry on 11.458; see [[orchomenus]], [[pylos]], [[sparta]] and [[menelaus]]). The last verse was athetized “for its silliness”: if he was sure that his son had not died, why ask whether anyone had heard that he was alive? Aristarchus read οὐ γάρ που, “for surely he has not died” (Merry's critical note on 11.461; see the translation's note on 11.461 and [[brilliant]]).

**What Odysseus does not know.** “Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this? I do not know at all / whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (11.463–464; see [[odysseus]] and [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]). The poem's listeners know more than either of them: Orestes came back in the eighth year and killed Aegisthus, as Zeus, Athena and Nestor have told (1.29–30, 1.298–300, 3.306–310; see [[aegisthus]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]).
