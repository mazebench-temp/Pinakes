---
summary: The ἄλγεα of the book are Oedipus' “sorrows” after his mother's death (11.275–280) and the “bitter pains” of Tantalus and Sisyphus (11.582, 11.593); the κήδεα are the matter of Odysseus' tale, the sorrows of the Argives and of his companions (11.369, 11.376, 11.382), and the dead ask about their own (11.542).
---

**Sorrows left behind.** Oedipus, “suffering sorrows in lovely Thebes / through the deadly plans of the gods, ruled the Cadmeans”, and his mother when she hanged herself left him “sorrows behind, / very many, all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass” (11.275–280; the plans of the gods go with “suffering sorrows”: W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.275; see [[oedipus-kills-laius]] and [[curses]]).

**The sorrows of the tale.** Alcinous praises Odysseus for telling “the grievous sorrows of all the Argives and of yourself” like a singer, and would hold out till dawn to hear “your sorrows” (11.368–376; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[singers]]). Odysseus offers “other things more pitiable even than these, / the sorrows of my companions, who perished afterward” (11.381–382; see [[a-time-for-many-words]]). The dead, too, ask about their κήδεα: after Achilles has gone, the souls stand grieving, “and each one asked about its own sorrows”, that is, about those dear to them among the living; the scholium takes it of telling their sorrows, but εἴρομαι implies a question (11.542; Merry on 11.542).

**Pains without end.** Tantalus and Sisyphus are introduced in the same verse, “suffering bitter pains” (κρατέρʼ ἄλγεʼ ἔχοντα, 11.582, 11.593; see [[tantalus]] and [[sisyphus]]).
