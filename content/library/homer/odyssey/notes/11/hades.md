---
summary: Hades is named in Book 11 as a god only twice, when the companions pray over the victims “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (11.47) and when Epicaste goes down to the house of Hades, “the strong gatekeeper” (11.277). Everywhere else his name is the house of the dead. The souls go back “into the house of Hades”, Minos gives judgment “throughout the wide-gated house of Hades” (11.571), Heracles tells how he led the hound “out of Hades” (11.625), and Odysseus flees in fear that Persephone will send the Gorgon's head against him “out of Hades” (11.635). The god himself never appears.
# The entry claims every form in the book: Ἀΐδῃ (11.47), Ἄϊδόσδε (11.65, 11.475), Ἀίδαο (11.69, 11.164, 11.211, 11.277,
# 11.425, 11.625), Ἄϊδος (11.150, 11.571, 11.627) and Ἀίδεω (11.635).
---

**Mighty Hades.** When the souls have gathered at the pit, Odysseus bids his companions flay and burn the slaughtered sheep, “and pray to the gods, / to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (11.46–47; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]], [[mighty]] and [[persephone]]). The verses are Circe's instruction carried out word for word (10.533–534; see the Book 10 note). It is the only worship that the book gives him. Afterward the dead are left to his queen: Persephone sends the women up and scatters them again, and at the end Odysseus fears what she may send (see the Book 11 note on [[persephone]]).

**The strong gatekeeper.** Epicaste, when she had fastened the noose, “went to the house of Hades, the strong gatekeeper” (11.277; see [[jocasta]] and [[gatekeeper]]). The Iliad has the same words in Deiphobus' boast over the dead Asius (*Iliad* 13.415), and the title once more when Athena recalls how Eurystheus sent Heracles “to the house of Hades the gatekeeper / to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades” (*Iliad* 8.367–368). Merry renders πυλάρτης “the warder”. Apion explained it as one fastened to the gates; Merry takes it rather as the god who keeps the gates close-fitted, comparing the gates “fitted close and strong” of *Iliad* 12.454 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.277).

**The house of Hades.** Elsewhere in the book the name stands for the place. The genitive is the god's house, with δόμον understood, as in Book 10 (see the translation's key-word row and its note on 10.502): δόμου ἐξ Ἀίδαο, “out of the house of Hades” (11.69); εἰς Ἀίδαο, “to the house of Hades” (11.164, 11.277, 11.425); εἰν Ἀίδαο, “even in the house of Hades” (11.211); δόμον Ἄϊδος εἴσω, “into the house of Hades” (11.150, 11.627); and the adverb Ἄϊδόσδε, “to Hades”, of Elpenor's soul (11.65) and of Odysseus' coming (11.475). The house is where the souls belong. When he has told the oracles, “the soul went into the house of Hades, / the soul of lord Teiresias” (11.150–151; see [[teiresias]]), and Heracles' phantom goes “back again into the house of Hades” (11.627); Ajax goes “after the other / souls of the dead who have died, into Erebus” (11.563–564; see [[erebus]]). Odysseus himself stays at the pit (11.628). Yet the living and the dead speak of his coming as a descent: “My mother, need brought me down to the house of Hades” (11.164), and Achilles asks, “How did you dare to come down to Hades” (11.475). His mother asks how he came “beneath the misty darkness” while alive (11.155), ὑπὸ ζόφον ἠερόεντα, which is the share of the world that fell to Hades by lot (*Iliad* 15.191; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). Merry finds in the voyage no descent into the depths, only the farther shore of Oceanus (on 10.512; see the Book 10 note and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).

**The wide-gated house.** Minos sits with the golden scepter, and the dead sit and stand around him asking for judgment, “throughout the wide-gated house of Hades” (11.571; see [[minos]] and [[wide-gated]]). The verse-end is the Iliad's, in the plea of the unburied Patroclus: “Bury me as quickly as may be, so that I may pass the gates of Hades”, for the souls keep him away, “but I wander aimlessly through the wide-gated house of Hades” (*Iliad* 23.71–74; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). Merry explains in the same way why Elpenor comes first in Book 11: being unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades and take his place among the rest of the dead (Merry on 11.51; see [[elpenor]]).

**Lord of the dead.** Achilles' answer to Odysseus' comfort takes up the word of Hades' own rule. He would rather serve another for hire, a man with no land of his own, “than be lord over all the dead who have perished” (11.491; see [[achilles]] and [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]). In the Iliad Hades is ἐνέροισιν ἀνάσσων, “lord of those below” (*Iliad* 15.188), and Aidoneus ἄναξ ἐνέρων, “lord of those below”, who leaps from his throne in fear when Poseidon shakes the earth (*Iliad* 20.61–65).

**Out of Hades.** The phantom of Heracles tells of the labor that brought him there alive: “and I brought it up and led it out of Hades, / and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena” (11.625–626; see [[heracles]], [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[hermes]] and [[athena]]). In the Iliad the same son of Zeus once shot Hades himself “in Pylos among the dead” (*Iliad* 5.395–397; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). The book's last fear comes from the same house. As the countless tribes of the dead gather with a wondrous din, Odysseus fears “lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades” (11.634–635; see [[gorgon]]), and he goes at once to the ship. Ἀίδεω, the genitive in -εω, was the form of “the dank house of Hades” in Circe's instructions (10.512), and it returns when Circe sees that the men have come back “from Hades” (12.17, a plain rendering).
