---
summary: Circe sends a following wind behind the ship to carry it to Oceanus, and “the wind and the helmsman steered her” (11.6–10); a fair wind takes it home again (11.640). Among the dead, Odysseus asks Agamemnon whether Poseidon killed him “rousing a blast of grievous winds that none would envy” (11.400, 11.407), and the wind snatches Tantalus' fruit “toward the shadowy clouds” (11.592).
# οὖρον, the following wind that Circe sends (11.7), and οὖρος, the fair wind of the return (11.640), are not forms
# of ἄνεμος and are counted here.
refs: [11.7, 11.640]
---

**Circe's wind.** “And behind our dark-prowed ship / she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion, / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (11.6–8; see [[circe]] and [[dark-prowed]]). ἴκμενος οὖρος is a wind that goes with the ship, from astern, and πλησίστιος one “that filled the sail” (see the translation's key-word row); the half-verse is the one in which Apollo sends the ship home from Chryse and Athena sends Telemachus to sea (*Iliad* 1.479; 2.420). The men have only to sit: “we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her. / And all day long her sails were stretched as she crossed the sea” (11.10–11; see [[sails]]). Book 12 repeats the verses when the ship leaves Aeaea for the last time (12.148–152). Circe had promised it: “the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.507; see [[boreas]] and the Book 10 note).

**Not the winds of Poseidon.** Odysseus' first guess at the death of Agamemnon is a storm: “Was it among the ships that Poseidon brought you down, / rousing a blast of grievous winds that none would envy?” (11.399–400; see [[poseidon]] and [[agamemnon]]), and Agamemnon denies it in the same words (11.406–407). ἀμέγαρτος, “that none would envy”, Merry refers to Buttmann's account of μεγαίρω, “begrudge” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.400). Aristophanes read λευγαλέων for ἀργαλέων, “grievous” (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 11.399–403).

**The wind and Tantalus.** In the last sights the wind is part of a punishment. Whenever Tantalus reaches for the fruit above his head, “the wind would fling them toward the shadowy clouds” (11.591–592; see [[tantalus]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]).

**The fair wind home.** The ship goes back down the river Oceanus “first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind” (11.640; see [[oceanus]]). Merry thinks there was no wind at first because they were in an unearthly place beyond the ordinary air, and that the wind came as they got into the ordinary world again (Merry on 11.640).
