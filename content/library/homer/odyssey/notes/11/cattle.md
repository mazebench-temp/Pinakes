summary: The cattle of Book 11 are the barren heifer that Odysseus vows to the dead (11.30); the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia, which the companions must leave unharmed (11.104–115); the broad-browed cattle with curving horns of Iphiclus, which Neleus demanded for his daughter's hand (11.288–297); the cattle that raiders cut off from the herd (11.402); and the ox killed at the manger, to which Agamemnon likens his own death (11.411).

**The barren heifer.** Odysseus vows to the strengthless heads of the dead that “when I came to Ithaca, a barren heifer, the best I had, / I would sacrifice in my halls, and heap the pyre with good things” (11.30–31), the words of Circe's instruction (10.522–523; see the Book 10 note, [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and the translation's row στεῖρα βοῦς; ἔθνεα νεκρῶν).

**The cattle of the Sun.** Teiresias' warning turns on cattle: “when first you bring your well-made ship / to the island of Thrinacia, escaping the violet sea, / and find grazing there the cattle and fat sheep / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.106–109; see [[thrinacia]], [[helios]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[fat-sheep]]). W. W. Merry notes that εὕρητε is still governed by ὁππότε κε: they may see their homes again if they restrain themselves when they land on Thrinacia and when they find the grazing herds (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.108). “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming, / you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils; / but if you harm them, then I foretell destruction for you, / for your ship and your companions” (11.110–113; see the translation's rows ἀσινής; σίνομαι and τεκμαίρομαι). The companions will kill them in the next book (12.339–365).

**The cattle of Iphiclus.** Neleus would give his daughter Pero “to no one who did not drive the broad-browed cattle with curving horns / out of Phylace, the cattle of mighty Iphiclus, / hard to drive” (11.288–291; see [[pero]], [[iphiclus]], [[phylace]], [[broad-browed]] and [[with-curving-horns]]). Merry takes ἐλάσειε as a condition in implied indirect speech, “unless he had driven them off”, and supplies ἐλάσαι with ἀργαλέας: the cattle were hard, or dangerous, to drive away, because they were so well guarded; the seer who promised to drive them off was held by “grievous bonds, and the country herdsmen”, βουκόλοι ἀγροιῶται, who describe and define the “hard fate of a god” that fettered him (on 11.290, 11.291, 11.293; see [[raiding]] and the translation's note on 11.289–290). The seer, unnamed here, is Melampus, whose story Book 15 tells again (15.225–242; see [[melampus]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]).

**Cattle cut off.** Odysseus asks Agamemnon whether he died on land “as you were cutting off their cattle and fine flocks of sheep” (11.402; see the Book 11 note on [[sheep]]), the raid of a band of marauders who surround a number of oxen and cut them off from the herd (Merry on 11.402).

**The ox at the manger.** Agamemnon tells how Aegisthus killed him “when he had called me to his house / and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger” (11.410–411; see [[the-ox-at-the-manger]] and [[agamemnon]]), Proteus' verse of Book 4 (4.535; see the Book 4 note).
