---
summary: The dead are addressed as lords: Elpenor calls Odysseus ἄναξ (11.71), Odysseus calls Teiresias and Ajax so (11.144, 11.561), Teiresias is “lord Teiresias” (11.151), and Minos is “the lord” whom the dead ask for judgment (11.570); Achilles would rather serve for hire on earth than “be lord over all the dead” (11.491).
---

The title survives death. The dead use it of the living and the living of the dead: Elpenor, “there, lord, I bid you remember me” (11.71; see [[elpenor]]); Odysseus to Teiresias, “Tell me, lord: how may she know me for who I am?” (11.144), and the narrator's “the soul of lord Teiresias” (11.151; see [[teiresias]]); Odysseus to the silent Ajax, “But come here, my lord, so that you may hear my word” (11.561; see [[ajax]]); and the dead around Minos “asked the lord for judgment” (11.570; see [[minos]]). Achilles' refusal turns on the verb: he would rather serve another for hire “than be lord over all the dead who have perished” (ἀνάσσειν, 11.491; see [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]). Among the living, Agamemnon is “lord of men” (11.397; see [[lord-of-men]]), Poseidon “lord Poseidon” (11.130), Oedipus “ruled the Cadmeans” and Amphion ruled in Orchomenus (11.276, 11.284), and Alcinous swears by his own life and lordship over the Phaeacians (11.348–349; see [[alcinous]]).
