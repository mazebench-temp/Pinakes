---
summary: Anticleia tells her son that between the living and the dead “are great rivers and dread streams, / Oceanus first of all” (11.157–158); Tyro loves the Enipeus, “by far the fairest of the rivers that run upon the earth” (11.238–242); and at the end the ship goes back “down the river Oceanus” (11.639).
---

**Great rivers and dread streams.** Anticleia marvels that her son has come alive beneath the misty darkness: “It is hard for the living to see these things. / For between are great rivers and dread streams, / Oceanus first of all, which no one can cross / on foot, unless he has a well-made ship” (11.156–159; see [[anticleia]] and [[oceanus]]). The three verses were rejected by the Alexandrian critics. The difficulty, W. W. Merry explains, is that Anticleia marvels to see her living son in the kingdom of death, so that πῶς ἦλθες, “how did you come”, is almost an exclamation, while these verses treat it as a question about the route and its dangers; the naive πεζὸν ἐόντα, “on foot”, is not un-Homeric in itself, but Odysseus had said the contrary to Circe, that no one has ever yet come to Hades in a black ship (10.502), and μὲν πρῶτα, “first of all”, leads one to expect a description of the other rivers that never comes (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.157–159; see the translation's note on 11.157–159). Circe's directions had named rivers of the dead, Acheron and the two that flow into it, Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus (10.513–515; see [[acheron]] and the Book 10 note), but Anticleia names only Oceanus.

**The river Tyro loved.** Among the heroines, Tyro “fell in love with a river, the divine Enipeus, / which is by far the fairest of the rivers that run upon the earth”, and Poseidon lay with her “at the mouth of the eddying river” (11.238–242; see [[enipeus]], [[tyro]] and [[eddying]]). The river is a god and a lover, like the rivers of the Iliad who father children on mortal women (Merry on 11.241; *Iliad* 16.173–176, 21.141–143).

**The river Oceanus.** Oceanus is a river in this book too: the ship comes to “the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus” (11.13), the men go “beside the stream of Oceanus” to the place Circe showed (11.21), and at the end “the wave of the stream carried the ship down the river Oceanus” (11.639; see [[oceanus]] and [[deep-streaming]]).
