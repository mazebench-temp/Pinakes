**What a man.** Of all the men Neoptolemus killed at Troy, Odysseus names one: “but what a man he slew with the bronze, the son of Telephus, / the hero Eurypylus, and many of his companions around him, / the Ceteians, were killed, for the sake of gifts to a woman. / He was the handsomest man I saw, after brilliant Memnon” (11.519–522; see [[neoptolemus]], [[telephus]], [[heros]] and [[memnon]]). οἷον is exclamatory, as at 4.242 (see the translation's note on 11.519). He is named only here in Homer, and his father only in the patronymic Τηλεφίδης.

**The Ceteians and the gifts.** The words γυναίων εἵνεκα δώρων, “for the sake of gifts to a woman”, recur of Eriphyle, who sent her husband Amphiaraus to his death for a bribe (15.247, Greek only; see [[eriphyle]] and [[amphiaraus]]). The gift here, in the later story, was the golden vine with which Priam persuaded Eurypylus' mother to send him to Troy (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.520). Κήτειοι, the Ceteians, Merry takes for a stock of the Mysians living in Teuthrania, whose name is said to come from the river Ceteius, a tributary of the Caicus, though there was great uncertainty about it, and some read κήδειοι, “kinsmen”, or χήτειοι, “bereft of women” (Merry on 11.521 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.521 and [[ceteians]]). Strabo confessed that he knew neither who the Ceteians were nor what the gifts meant (*Geography* 13.1.69; see the entry).

**The handsomest.** κεῖνον δὴ κάλλιστον ἴδον μετὰ Μέμνονα δῖον, “He was the handsomest man I saw, after brilliant Memnon” (11.522), praises him as the Catalogue praised Nireus, “the most handsome man who came to Ilios / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (*Iliad* 2.673–674), and as this book praises Ajax, the best in looks after Achilles (11.469–470; see [[best-in-looks]]). Neither Eurypylus nor Memnon is named in the Iliad: their coming to Troy belongs to the later epics of the Cycle (see [[memnon]] and [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]]).
