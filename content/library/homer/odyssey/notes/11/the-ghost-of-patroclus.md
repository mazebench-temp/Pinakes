---
summary: The Iliad's one meeting of a living man with a soul has its doubles here. Odysseus tries to embrace his mother's soul as Achilles reached for Patroclus', asking in Achilles' words that they take their fill of lament, and three times she flies from his hands (11.204–214); her explanation answers Achilles' discovery that the dead have no mind (11.216–222); and the unburied Elpenor asks, as Patroclus asked, for the fire and the mound (11.51–83).
# Unnamed: the embrace attempted (11.204-212); the way of mortals (11.218-222).
refs: [11.204-212, 11.218-222]
---

**The embrace.** Achilles, promising the soul of Patroclus everything it asks, says: “But stand nearer to me; let us put our arms around each other, if only for a little while, / and take our fill of deadly lament”, and reaches out with his hands and takes nothing, for the soul goes beneath the earth like smoke (*Iliad* 23.97–101). Odysseus, three times eluded by his mother, asks why she will not stay for him, “so that even in the house of Hades, throwing our arms around each other, / we two might take our fill of chilling lament” (11.210–212; see [[anticleia]] and [[lament]]). The verse-end is the same, τεταρπώμεσθα γόοιο, and Merry notes that the Iliad's passage is the only one where the object of the embrace is left out, as it is here, so that ἀμφοτέρω stands where the Iliad has ἀλλήλους (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.211). Achilles reaches once; Odysseus springs three times, “and three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream” (11.206–207), and each failure brings its own grief (Merry on 11.208). Merry compares the three vain embraces of Aeneas, of Creusa's ghost at Troy (on 11.206, citing *Aeneid* 2.792; see [[meeting-with-the-dead]]).

**The lesson.** Achilles draws his lesson from the empty air: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.103–104). Odysseus asks whether this is some phantom that Persephone has sent to grieve him (11.213–214), and his mother answers with the reason: “this is the way of mortals, when one of them dies”, for the sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones together, and the fire overcomes them once the spirit has left the white bones, “and the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers” (11.218–222; see [[psyche]], [[eidolon]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]). The fire that leaves the soul without a body is the portion of fire that the soul of Patroclus asked for, so that it might pass the gates of Hades (*Iliad* 23.71–76; see [[cremation]]).

**The unburied.** Merry explains Elpenor's coming first by the plea of Patroclus' soul: being unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades (on 11.51; *Iliad* 23.71–74). Elpenor too asks for burning and a mound (11.72–78; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]), though he is among the shades, where the other souls kept Patroclus away; for the difference, see the translation's note on *Iliad* 23.71–74.
