summary: Elpenor, left unburied in Circe's hall, is the first soul at the pit and asks for his due: to be burned with his armor and given a mound on the shore with his oar on it, under threat of the gods' wrath (11.51–80). Odysseus vows the dead a barren heifer and a pyre heaped with good things (11.30–31); Agamemnon's wife would not close his eyes as he died (11.424–426).

---

**The unburied.** Elpenor's soul comes first, “for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth; / for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.51–54; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). Not being buried, Merry explains, he could not pass within the gates of Hades and take his place among the rest, as the soul of Patroclus begs to be buried so that it may pass the gates (*Iliad* 23.71–74); Plutarch, as Merry reports him, says that of the souls Homer names in his νέκυια only Elpenor's was not yet mingled with those in Hades, because his body had not been buried, and wandered as on a border (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51; see [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).

**What he asks.** He begs by those Odysseus loves (11.66–68; see [[supplication]]) not to be left “unwept, unburied”, “lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you” (11.72–73; see [[menis]]), and asks to be burned with his armor and given a mound on the shore of the gray sea, with the oar he rowed with fixed on it (11.74–78; see [[cremation]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]], [[tomb]] and [[oars]]). The threat, Merry holds, is a post-Homeric refinement: the gods are not shown taking offense at the sight of an unburied body, and Hector's words to Achilles in the same verse (*Iliad* 22.358) speak of the outrages done to a corpse, not of its burial (Merry on 11.51). Odysseus promises in a line, and in Book 12 the companions fetch the body from Circe's house, burn it with its armor, heap a tomb, drag a pillar onto it and fix the oar on top (11.80; 12.8–15, a plain rendering).

**Other dues.** At the pit Odysseus vows the dead a barren heifer in his halls and a pyre heaped with good things (11.30–31; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). Agamemnon's wife turned away as he died, and would not “close my eyes with her hands or to shut my mouth” (11.424–426): κατὰ … ἑλέειν is to close the eyes, as Laertes grieves that his son's wife never closed his eyes on his bier, “for that is the prize of the dead” (24.294–296, a plain rendering), and as Odysseus tells Socus that his father and mother will not close his eyes in death (*Iliad* 11.452–453; Merry on 11.426; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]).
