---
summary: Teiresias puts an easy word “in your mind” (11.146); Arete asks the Phaeacians how the stranger seems “in looks and stature and the well-balanced mind within” (11.337), and Alcinous says that within him “are good wits” (11.367); Penelope “knows sound counsels in her heart” (11.445), and Clytemnestra put the deed into hers (11.428); and the dead “dwell without sense” (11.475–476).
---

**The mind of the living.** The φρένες are where words and plans are put and kept. Teiresias tells Odysseus “an easy word I will tell you and put in your mind”, the rule of the blood (ἐπὶ φρεσὶ θήσω, 11.146; ἐπὶ φρεσί is La Roche's reading against ἐνὶ φρεσί: the Odyssey translation's note on 11.146, 11.149; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]); Agamemnon bids him “lay it to heart” (ἐνὶ φρεσὶ βάλλεο, 11.454; see [[lay-it-to-heart]]); Odysseus ponders “in my mind” (11.204), and Achilles asks him what greater deed he will devise in his mind (11.474). Laertes nurses a great sorrow there (11.195; see [[penthos]] and [[laertes]]).

**Good and bad.** Arete presents the stranger to the Phaeacians by his looks, his stature and his φρένες ἔνδον ἐῗσαι, “the well-balanced mind within” (11.337): ἐΐσας may be taken as “seemly”, or, if it is ἴσος, “equal”, as “fair” or well-balanced, like the ships that ride on an even keel; Merry doubts the scholium's “in proportion to the body” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.337; see the translation's table of key words; [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[eidos]]). Alcinous answers that Odysseus has “beauty of words, and within you are good wits” (11.367; see [[mythos]]). Agamemnon gives the two kinds of wife their minds: Clytemnestra put such deeds into her heart (μετὰ φρεσί, 11.428), and Penelope “knows sound counsels in her heart” (εὖ φρεσὶ μήδεα οἶδε, 11.445; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

**The dead.** The dead have no φρένες to put anything in. Achilles names them “the dead / who dwell without sense”, ἀφραδέες, an adjective that goes with the verb ναίουσι (11.475–476; Merry on 11.476, referring to 10.495), and only Teiresias kept his mind steadfast in death, which is why he knows Odysseus before he drinks the blood (Merry on 11.96; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).
