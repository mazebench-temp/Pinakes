summary: The Odyssey's own Theseus line. At the end of his tale Odysseus would have stayed to see “Theseus and Peirithous, glorious children of the gods” (11.631), a verse that Hereas of Megara, as Plutarch reports him, said Peisistratus had put into Homer's Nekyia to please the Athenians.

**The verse.** “And I would have seen still more men of earlier times, whom I wished to see, / Theseus and Peirithous, glorious children of the gods; / but before that the countless tribes of the dead gathered / with a wondrous din” (11.630–633; see [[theseus]] and [[peirithous]]). Without the middle verse the sentence still stands: the men of earlier times go unnamed, and the dead gather before Odysseus can see them.

**The charge.** W. W. Merry quotes Plutarch in his critical note: Hereas of Megara says that Peisistratus inserted into Homer's Nekyia the verse “Theseus and Peirithous, conspicuous children of the gods”, to gratify the Athenians (Plutarch, *Theseus* 20, as Merry quotes it; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 11.631). Plutarch's verse has ἀριδείκετα, “conspicuous”, where ours has ἐρικυδέα, “glorious” (see [[glorious]]). Merry brackets the verse in his text (see the translation's note on 11.631 and [[pisistratus|Peisistratus]]). The accuser is a Megarian, and the charge is of the same kind as the one brought against the Salamis line of the Catalogue (see [[pisistratus]]).

**The Athenian verses.** Merry counts this verse, the verses on Theseus and Ariadne earlier in the book (11.321–325), and *Iliad* 1.265 among the traces of Athenian interpolation in Homer, and he takes the whole passage of the last sights for a later and probably Attic addition (Merry on 11.321, 11.565; see the translation's notes on 11.321–325 and 11.565–567). The likeness to the Iliad's line is close. There too Theseus is named beside Peirithous as a hero of an older generation, in a list of the mightiest men Nestor ever saw, and there too the verse can be taken out without leaving a gap (*Iliad* 1.262–265; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]).

**The name of the book.** Hereas' phrase, “Homer's Nekyia”, is one of the early witnesses to the name that the book still bears (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]).
