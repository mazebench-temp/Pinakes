summary: Castor, “tamer of horses”, is named with Polydeuces in Leda's verses, in the line of *Iliad* 3.237; where the Iliad buried the brothers in Lacedaemon, the Odyssey says that the life-giving earth holds them fast though they are alive, and that they live and are dead on alternate days, with honor like the gods (11.298–304).

**The Iliad's verse.** Leda bore to Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons, “Castor, tamer of horses, and Polydeuces, good with his fists” (11.299–300; see [[leda]], [[tyndareus]], [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[good-with-his-fists]]). The second verse is Helen's on the wall of Troy, word for word (*Iliad* 3.237), and the next answers the Iliad's next. There, “the life-giving earth already held them fast / there in Lacedaemon, in their own dear native land” (*Iliad* 3.243–244); here, τοὺς ἄμφω ζωοὺς κατέχει φυσίζοος αἶα, “both of whom the life-giving earth holds fast, alive though they are” (11.301; see [[life-giving-earth]]). W. W. Merry notes that γαῖα κατέχει, “the earth holds”, has its regular meaning of death and burial in the other places where it is used (*Iliad* 16.629; *Odyssey* 13.427 = 15.31), and that ζωούς can be reconciled with it only by a sort of oxymoron: dead, buried and beneath the earth, they are yet “alive” to the extent that the next verses allow. Nitzsch proposed to read ἀμφιζωούς, which those verses would then explain; but only by straining the interpretation, Merry concludes, can the Iliad's form of the story be harmonized with the Odyssey's (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.301; see the translation's note on 11.298–304).

**On alternate days.** οἳ καὶ νέρθεν γῆς τιμὴν πρὸς Ζηνὸς ἔχοντες / ἄλλοτε μὲν ζώουσʼ ἑτερήμεροι, ἄλλοτε δʼ αὖτε / τεθνᾶσιν, “and even beneath the earth, having honor from Zeus, / now they live on alternate days, and now again / they are dead” (11.302–304). ἑτερήμεροι, “on alternate days”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's row ἑτερήμεροι). Their honor is a gift of Zeus, and “they have been allotted honor like the gods”, τιμὴν δὲ λελόγχασιν ἶσα θεοῖσι (11.304; see [[time]] and [[immortality]]); λελόγχασιν is the reading of all the manuscripts in spite of its unusual short vowel, which Eustathius accepted while suggesting λελόγχασʼ, the form Wolf printed (Merry's critical note on 11.304). The verses do not say why the brothers died or why Zeus honored them so; the later story that one brother shared his immortality with the other is not told here (see [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]] and [[polydeuces]]).

**Light and darkness.** Merry, who doubts the whole passage (on 11.298), sets out the later forms of the myth: the sons of Leda were given different fathers, Castor mortal and Polydeuces immortal, and when Castor fell in the fight with the sons of Aphareus, Polydeuces gave up half his immortality to share it with him on alternate days; he quotes Pindar for their alternate days with Zeus and under the earth at Therapne, where their grave was shown (*Nemean* 10.55–56; *Pythian* 11.63–64, as Merry quotes them). The alternation suggested to some a myth of light and darkness: Welcker made the brothers the morning and evening star, and their epithets in Pindar and Euripides, white-horsed and flashing on their horses, recall Sophocles' “white-horsed day”. But if the alternate days were so necessary to such a myth, Merry objects, it is remarkable that the Iliad has no place for them, where Helen's brothers are dead and buried before the war began; he leans to Müller's view, that the worship of old Peloponnesian gods was blended with the heroic honors of the human sons of Tyndareus (on 11.298). Euripides' phrase is Clytemnestra's, who reminds Agamemnon that her brothers, the two sons of Zeus, “flashing on their horses”, made war on him (*Iphigenia at Aulis* 1153–1154; see [[iphigenia-at-aulis]]).
