summary: His soul comes with those of Patroclus, Antilochus and Ajax, knows Odysseus, and asks how he dared to come down to Hades. To Odysseus' praise, that he was honored like a god in life and is mighty among the dead, he answers that he would rather serve another for hire on earth than be lord over all the dead, and asks after his son and his old father; told of Neoptolemus' glory, he goes off with long strides across the meadow of asphodel, glad (11.467–540). Odysseus names him again to Ajax, over his arms (11.546, 11.557).

**The four souls.** After Agamemnon, “there came the soul of Achilles, son of Peleus, / and of Patroclus, and of noble Antilochus, / and of Ajax, who was the best in looks and form / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (11.467–470; see [[patroclus]], [[antilochus]] and [[ajax]]). The friendship of these heroes, W. W. Merry notes, is resumed in the underworld: Patroclus was the dearest comrade of Achilles, and Achilles smiled at Antilochus at the games, “rejoicing in Antilochus, since he was his dear companion” (*Iliad* 23.555–556); and the last book will lay the bones of Achilles and Patroclus mixed in one golden urn, with those of Antilochus apart (24.76–79, Greek only; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.467). Nothing is said, in this second scene, of the souls' drinking the blood (Merry on 11.467).

**How did you dare?** “And the soul of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus knew me, / and lamenting addressed me with winged words” (11.471–472; see [[swift-footed]], [[grandson-of-aeacus]] and [[aeacus]]): “hard man, what still greater deed will you devise in your mind? / How did you dare to come down to Hades, where the dead / dwell without sense, the phantoms of dead mortals?” (11.474–476; see [[hades]] and [[eidolon]]). ἀφραδέες, “without sense”, goes with ναίουσι as a predicate, and καμόντες, “the weary”, is a euphemism for the dead, the aorist thinking of the moment of death rather than of the state of the dead (Merry on 11.476, after Classen; see the translation's key words and its note on 11.476, 11.478, 11.483).

**The consolation refused.** Odysseus tells him that he came to consult Teiresias, and that no man before or after was more blessed: honored like the gods in life, he is mighty among the dead (11.478–486; see [[odysseus]] and [[three-times-blessed]]). κρατέεις νεκύεσσιν, with the dative, is found only here and once more in the Odyssey, perhaps a local dative, “among the dead”, or even “in the eyes of the dead” (Merry on 11.485). Plato would gladly strike this passage and its like out of Homer, as causing groundless fears and giving a false picture of the world below (*Republic* 386c; Merry on 11.490; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). καταφθιμένοισιν, “who have perished”, is again the aorist of the moment of death (Merry on 11.491).

**His son and his father.** “But come, tell me word of my noble son, / whether he followed to the war to be a champion, or not”, and of noble Peleus, whether the Myrmidons still honor him, or dishonor him throughout Hellas and Phthia, “because old age holds him fast in his hands and feet” (11.492–497; see [[neoptolemus]], [[peleus]], [[myrmidons]], [[hellas]] and [[phthia]]). τοῦ παιδὸς ἀγαυοῦ is “that noble son of mine” (Merry on 11.492), and Phthia must include the neighborhood as well as the town (Merry on 11.496). He cannot help his father: “For I am not there to help him under the rays of the sun, / such as I was once in wide Troy, / when I killed the best of the army, defending the Argives” (11.498–500), the rays of the sun set against the darkness below (Merry on 11.498; see [[while-i-live]] and [[troy]]). If he could come so, even for a little while, to his father's house, “then I would make my might and my irresistible hands hateful to any / of those who do him violence and keep him from his honor” (11.501–503; see [[irresistible-hands]] and [[time]]): στύξαιμι is causative, “in that case I would make bitter my might and my invincible hands to many an one of those who” (Merry on 11.502). Zenodotus read εἰ γὰρ ἐγών at 11.498, and Aristarchus τέων at 11.502 (see the translation's note on 11.489, 11.498, 11.502).

**Glad.** Odysseus has heard nothing of Peleus, but tells him of Neoptolemus, his counsel and his courage, his killing of Eurypylus and his steadiness in the horse, and how he came home unhurt with his prize (11.505–537; see [[neoptolemus]]):

{{quote:d4c2acf8-8819-51f6-97d6-3ceaca702675}}

γηθοσύνη ὅ is “rejoicing that”; Aristophanes and Herodian wrote γηθοσύνῃ (Merry on 11.540 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.531, 11.539, 11.540, and [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]]). The long strides are those of the living hero, πόδας ὠκύς (see [[swift-footed]]).

**Named to Ajax.** Odysseus speaks of him twice more, to the silent Ajax: the victory “for the armor of Achilles; his lady mother had set it as a prize” (11.546), and the grief of the Achaeans for Ajax, “as much as for the head of Achilles, son of Peleus” (11.557; see [[ajax]], [[thetis]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).

**His names and epithets in this book.** Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος, “Achilles, son of Peleus” (11.467), and Ἀχιλλῆος κεφαλῇ Πηληϊάδαο, “the head of Achilles, son of Peleus” (11.557; see [[peleus]]); ποδώκεος Αἰακίδαο, “the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” (11.471, 11.538); μετʼ ἀμύμονα Πηλεΐωνα, “after the noble son of Peleus” (11.470, 11.551; see [[blameless]]); ὦ Ἀχιλεῦ Πηλῆος υἱέ, μέγα φέρτατʼ Ἀχαιῶν, “Achilles, son of Peleus, by far the strongest of the Achaeans” (11.478), the verse with which Patroclus speaks to him in the Iliad (*Iliad* 16.21; again 19.216, of Odysseus to Achilles).
