summary: She is in every account the dead give of Odysseus' house. Teiresias foretells the suitors wooing her; Odysseus asks his mother whether she still keeps his house or has married the best of the Achaeans, and hears that she waits in his halls, weeping night and day. Agamemnon, who warns Odysseus against trusting any wife, praises her good sense: “the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope” (11.117, 11.177–183, 11.444–448).

**What the dead tell.** Elpenor begs Odysseus by his wife (11.67; see [[elpenor]]), and Teiresias foretells the overbearing men in his house, “wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (11.116–117; see [[the-suitors]], [[godlike]] and [[bride-price]]). His mother asks whether he has not yet come home “nor seen your wife in your halls” (11.162), and he puts his last question about her: “And tell me of my wedded wife, her plan and her thought, / whether she stays beside her son and keeps all things safe, / or whether whoever is best of the Achaeans has already married her” (11.177–179; see [[wedded]], [[boule]] and [[noos]]). A few manuscripts add a verse after φυλάσσει, naming his possessions, his slaves and his great high-roofed house (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 11.178; see the translation's note on 11.174, 11.178). Anticleia answers that question first: “Yes, truly she stays with enduring heart / in your halls; and always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (11.181–183; see [[anticleia]] and [[tears]]), καὶ λίην being “in very truth” (Merry on 11.181). Her last words bid her son learn all these things “so that you may tell them to your wife hereafter” (11.224), a kindly thought for Penelope, as Eustathius takes it (Merry on 11.223).

The young bride and the child at the breast are the household of the day when Agamemnon and Menelaus came to Ithaca to urge Odysseus to follow them to Ilios (24.115–117, Greek only; Merry on 11.447; see [[agamemnon]] and [[telemachus]]). περίφρων, “prudent”, is her standing epithet, and the whole verse is the Odyssey's verse of her name (1.329; see [[periphron]] and [[icarius]]). Then Agamemnon goes back to his warning: Odysseus is to bring his ship to shore in secret, “for there is no trusting women any more” (11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). A scholium noted, in the Harleian manuscript, that the lines were wanting in most copies as contradicting what precedes, and Merry, who takes the note to belong to 11.454–456, thinks it the expression of a feeling that after such praise of Penelope Agamemnon cannot consistently bid Odysseus be suspicious of her (Merry on 11.452 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.454–456).
