summary: The first of the heroes of Troy to come after the pause. His soul comes grieving, with the souls of those who died with him in the house of Aegisthus; he knows Odysseus when he has drunk, and weeps and reaches for him, but his strength is gone. He tells how Aegisthus and his ruinous wife killed him at a feast, “as one kills an ox at the manger”, his companions slaughtered around him, how he heard Cassandra's dying cry, and how his wife would not close his eyes; he warns Odysseus against women, makes an exception of Penelope, bids him come home in secret, and asks for news of Orestes (11.385–466).

**The leader he followed.** Odysseus first names him to his mother, telling her how long he has wandered, “ever since I first followed brilliant Agamemnon / to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (11.168–169; see [[brilliant]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The soul.** When Persephone has scattered the souls of the women, “there came the soul of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / grieving; and around him were gathered the others, all who with him / had died in the house of Aegisthus and met their fate” (11.385–389; see [[persephone]], [[aegisthus]] and [[psyche]]). ὅσσοι, “all who”, is printed for the manuscripts' ὅσσαι, and αὐτὰρ ἐπεί takes up the narrative broken off at 11.329 (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.385 and critical note on 11.388; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

μενεαίνων gives the reason for his stretching out his hands, “because he desired to reach me”; after it a thought is left out, “but he could not take hold of me, for…” (Merry on 11.392, 11.393). γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσι, “supple limbs”, contrasts the limbs of the living with the stiffness and starkness of the dead (Merry on 11.394; see [[supple]]), and κῖκυς, “vigor”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key words and its note on 11.393). Odysseus weeps and asks him how he died, whether Poseidon wrecked him among his ships or enemies killed him on land as he raided their cattle or fought for a city (11.395–403; see [[lord-of-men]], [[most-glorious]] and [[son-of-atreus]]); Aristophanes athetized the questions as carried over from the answer (see the translation's note on 11.399–403).

**His death.** He answers that neither happened: “but Aegisthus, contriving death and fate for me, / killed me, with my ruinous wife, when he had called me to his house / and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger” (11.409–411; see [[aegisthus]] and [[clytemnestra]]). The Homeric version, Merry notes, makes Aegisthus the murderer and a banquet the scene, where Aeschylus and Euripides have Clytemnestra kill him in the bath; but even here she is the accomplice, as σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ, “with my ruinous wife”, shows, and the asyndeton of δειπνίσσας is intentional, befitting the outburst of indignation and sorrow (Merry on 11.409, 11.411; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]]). His companions were killed around him “like white-tusked hogs” slaughtered for a rich man's feast, and Odysseus, who has seen many men killed in battle, would have grieved most at the sight of them lying “around the mixing bowl and the laden tables”, while “the whole floor seethed with blood” (11.412–420; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[table]] and the translation's note on 11.420).

**Cassandra and the wife.** “And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter, / Cassandra, whom scheming Clytemnestra killed / beside me”, and he, “raising my hands, let them fall to the ground / as I lay dying with the sword through me”, while she, “the dog-faced one, / turned away”, and would not close his eyes or shut his mouth as he went to the house of Hades (11.421–426; see [[cassandra]], [[schemer]] and [[dog-face]]). οἰκτροτάτην is predicative, “but saddest of all that I heard was the voice of Priam's daughter” (Merry on 11.421). The translation follows Merry's reading of the gesture, hands raised in supplication that fall to the earth, rather than the usual one, hands thrown about the sword (Merry on 11.423; see the translation's note on 11.423–424). κατὰ … ἑλέειν is to close the eyes of the dead, the last service of a wife, as Laertes will grieve that neither the mother nor the wife of Odysseus closed his (24.292–296, Greek only; Merry on 11.426).

**The lesson.** From his own death he draws the rule: there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman who puts such deeds into her heart, and Clytemnestra “has poured shame on herself and on those to come hereafter, / the womenfolk, even on one who does good” (11.427–434; see [[womenfolk]] and [[shame]]). Merry compares Hesiod, for whom nothing is more chilling than a bad wife (*Works and Days* 702–703), Hippolytus' “woman is a great evil” (Euripides, *Hippolytus* 627), and Medea, who calls women most helpless for good and most skilled in every evil (*Medea* 407–409; Merry on 11.427; see [[hesiod]] and [[euripides]]). κουρίδιος πόσις, “wedded husband” (11.430), is the honorable marriage of free equals, as against the concubine's place (Merry on 11.430; see [[wedded]]). To Odysseus' answer, that Zeus has hated the offspring of Atreus from of old through the plans of women, Helen and Clytemnestra (11.436–439; see [[atreus]] and [[helen]]), he replies with counsel: never be kind even to your wife, never tell her everything, but tell her one part and hide another (11.441–443); not that Penelope will kill him (11.444–451; see [[penelope]] and [[telemachus]]), but “my wife did not even let me have my fill of my son / with my eyes; before that she killed me myself” (11.452–453), where ἡ δʼ ἐμή is set apart from ἄκοιτις for a painful emphasis (Merry on 11.452). He ends: bring your ship to shore secretly, “for there is no trusting women any more” (11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

**Orestes.** Then he asks for news of his son, “perhaps in Orchomenus, or in sandy Pylos, / or perhaps with Menelaus in broad Sparta; / for brilliant Orestes has not yet died upon the earth” (11.457–461; see [[orestes]] and [[menelaus]]). It is implied, Merry thinks, that Orestes was away from Mycenae when his father came home; the places named are only refuges far from Mycenae, and there is no clue which Orchomenus is meant; Agamemnon takes for granted that his son is alive, or they would have met among the shades (Merry on 11.458; see [[orchomenus]] and [[pylos]]). The last verse was athetized as silly, since if he was sure that his son was alive he had no need to ask (see the translation's note on 11.461). Odysseus knows nothing, and “So we two stood exchanging hateful words, / grieving, shedding big tears” (11.463–466).

**His names and epithets in this book.** Ἀτρεΐδη κύδιστε, ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν Ἀγάμεμνον, “Most glorious son of Atreus, Agamemnon, lord of men” (11.397), the verse of the Iliad's address (*Iliad* 2.434 and often; see [[lord-of-men]] and [[most-glorious]]); Ἀγαμέμνονι δίῳ, “brilliant Agamemnon” (11.168); Ἀτρεΐδη, “Son of Atreus” (11.463; see [[son-of-atreus]]).
