summary: Odysseus performs the rite in the verses of Circe's instructions (11.20–50). Perimedes and Eurylochus hold the victims; he digs the pit, pours the drink offering of honey and milk, wine and water, sprinkles the barley, vows to the dead, and cuts the victims' throats over the pit; the souls gather out of Erebus, and he sits with drawn sword over the blood. The blood is what the dead must drink to know him and tell him the truth.
# βόθρου, the pit (11.95), is found in the Odyssey only here; at Il. 17.58 it is the olive's trench.

**The deed.** The ship comes to the limits of Oceanus, and the men walk along its stream “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.13–22; see [[oceanus]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). Of the shore and the groves, the confluence of the rivers and the rock of which Circe spoke, nothing more is said: the land of the Cimmerians takes their place, and φράσε, “pointed out”, refers by Aristarchus' rule not to her words but to the marks by which the spot could be known (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.22; see [[cimmerians]]). Perimedes and Eurylochus “held the victims”, ready for the sacrifice (11.23–24; Merry on 11.24; see [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]), and the rite follows in the first person and in the words of the instructions (10.516–537): the pit “about a cubit's length this way and that”, the drink offering to all the dead, “first with honey and milk, then with sweet wine, / and the third time with water”, the white barley meal (11.25–28; see [[honey]], [[milk]], [[wine]] and [[barley-meal]]), the entreaty of “the strengthless heads of the dead”, and the vows of a barren heifer to be sacrificed in his halls and a black sheep for Teiresias (11.29–33; see [[the-strengthless-heads]], [[supplication]] and [[prayer]]).

**The slaughter.** ἀπεδειροτόμησα ἐς βόθρον is “I cut their throats over the pit”: the preposition includes the heads bent down toward the pit and the blood flowing into it from the throats, as the victims were to be turned toward Erebus (11.35–36; Merry on 11.35; 10.527–528; see [[sacrifice]] and the Odyssey translation's note on 11.34–36). At once the souls gather, brides, young men, old men, maidens, and men slain in battle in their bloody armor (11.36–41; see [[psyche]], [[the-dead-who-have-died]] and [[erebus]]). Zenodotus and Aristophanes athetized these verses as at odds with what follows, since the souls come afterward one kind at a time and not mingled together, and wounds are not seen on phantoms (Merry's critical note on 11.38–43; see the translation's note on 11.38–43). The cry of the gathering souls makes Odysseus pale with fear, and the companions flay and burn the victims “that lay there slaughtered” and pray to Hades and Persephone (11.42–47; see [[pale-fear]], [[hades]] and [[persephone]]), while he sits with his sharp sword and lets none of the dead near the blood until he has questioned Teiresias (11.48–50).

**The blood.** The rest of the book turns on the blood. Odysseus keeps his sword over it while Elpenor speaks (11.82; see [[elpenor]]) and keeps back even his mother (11.88–89; see [[anticleia]]). Teiresias knows him and speaks before he drinks, but asks him to draw back from the pit “so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (11.95–98; see [[teiresias]]). Merry concludes that the blood did not give the seer his recognition or his speech, whose mind was still steadfast; he craved it as a gratification, a drink offering proper to the dead, and pouring it into a pit was the exact opposite of offering a bloody sacrifice on a raised altar to the gods of heaven. A scholium on 11.37 says that the souls came to the blood like flies to honey, and Eustathius puts it that Teiresias knows Odysseus before he drinks but does not prophesy, and gives the prophecy as a payment for the drink (Merry on 11.96; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.60, 11.92). For the others the blood is the rule that Teiresias gives: whichever of the dead Odysseus lets come near it “will tell you truly; / but whoever you begrudge it to will go back again” (11.146–149; Merry on 11.146 calls it a simple rule for holding or refusing communication with the dead). His mother drinks and knows him at once (11.152–153); the women come up one after another, since he will not let them all drink at once (11.228–234); and Agamemnon knows him “when he had drunk the dark blood” (11.390; see [[dark-blood]]). For Achilles, Ajax and Heracles no drinking is mentioned (11.467–471, 11.615; Merry on 11.467; see [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).
