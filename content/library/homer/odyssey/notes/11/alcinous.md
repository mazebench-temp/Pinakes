summary: The listener of the tale, in the pause. On him, Echeneus says, depend deed and word; he makes good the queen's proposal, as surely as he lives and reigns, and bids the guest stay until the morrow for the rest of the gifts. Then he praises him as no deceiver, a man who has told his tale with a singer's skill, and asks whether he saw any of his companions who died at Troy: the night is long, and he could hold out till dawn (11.346–376).

**Deed and word.** Echeneus ends his approval of the queen with the king: “But on Alcinous here depend both deed and word” (11.346; see [[echeneus]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). He answers: “This word shall indeed be so, as surely as I / live and am lord over the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (11.348–349; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]] and [[phaeacians]]). τοῦτο is Arete's suggestion, to add to the presents and keep the guest a little longer, and the emphasis falls on ζωός, “as sure as I am alive and reigning” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.348). The guest is to bring himself, “much as he is in need of his homecoming”, to stay until tomorrow, until the whole present is complete; “and his escort will be the concern of men, / of all of them, and mine most of all; for mine is the power among the people” (11.350–353; see [[pompe]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). Odysseus answers him with the address of the Phaeacian books, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (11.355, again 11.378; see [[powerful]] and [[preeminent]]), and with the wish to come home “with a fuller hand” (11.355–361; see [[odysseus]]).

**As a singer does.**

{{quote:190c528a-e0c3-5452-b352-149c4497f6be}}

τὸ μέν anticipates the words that follow, and Merry renders: “This in truth we do not judge thee when we look upon thee, viz. to be a deceiver and cheat; even as dark earth breeds many men broadcast framing lies out of things which one can never bring to proof of sight” (Merry on 11.363; see [[black-earth]] and the translation's key words). The scholia take the last words of lies so perfect that no one can detect them; but Odysseus has just been telling marvels of the land of Hades, where no mortal could go to test his story, and Merry compares the man in Herodotus who, telling of Ocean, “carried his tale into the unseen” and so cannot be refuted (*Histories* 2.23): not that Alcinous means to throw discredit on his adventures, but he seems gently to remind him of the doubtful truth of some travelers' tales (Merry on 11.366; see [[pseudos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). He praises the μορφὴ ἐπέων, the “beauty of words”, and the tale told ἐπισταμένως, “skillfully”, as by a singer (11.367–368; see [[singers]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Odysseus did not in fact tell the fate of “all the Argives” (11.369); Eustathius treats it as an inaccuracy, and Merry suggests that Alcinous may mean the men Odysseus brought with him from Troy (Merry on 11.369).

**The long night.** He asks whether Odysseus saw any of his godlike companions “who with you / followed together to Ilios and there met their fate” (11.371–372): the first ἅμα is mere accompaniment, the second time, “who set out at the same time with you yourself” (Merry on 11.371). “This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time / to sleep in the hall; so go on telling me of wondrous doings. / I could hold out even till brilliant Dawn, if you / would bring yourself to tell in the hall of your sorrows” (11.373–376; see [[endless]] and [[dawn-line]]). He seems to parody what Odysseus said of the immortal night that would wane before he could name all the women (11.330–331), and ἀνασχοίμην is used absolutely, like the vernacular “I could hold on” (Merry on 11.373, 11.375; see [[a-time-for-many-words]]). Odysseus goes on with the heroes of Troy, and the book, with the tale, ends without a word from the king; his answer comes when the tale is done (13.1–15).
