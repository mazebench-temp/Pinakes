summary: Her soul comes to the pit after Elpenor's, but her son, weeping, keeps her from the blood until Teiresias has spoken. When she has drunk she knows him and marvels that he has come alive into the darkness; she tells him that Penelope waits in tears, that Telemachus holds his domains, that Laertes lives on his farm in rags and grief, and that she herself died of longing for him. Three times he tries to embrace her, and she tells him that this is the way of mortals, and sends him back to the light (11.84–89, 11.141–144, 11.152–224).

**Kept from the blood.** “And there came the soul of my dead mother, / Anticleia, daughter of great-hearted Autolycus, / whom I had left alive when I went to sacred Ilios” (11.84–86; see [[autolycus]] and [[odysseus]]). The verse that brings her, ἦλθε δʼ ἐπὶ ψυχή, brings in turn Teiresias, Agamemnon and Achilles (11.90, 11.387, 11.467; see the translation's formulas). Her son weeps and pities her, “but even so, though deeply grieved, I did not let her first / come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias” (11.88–89), in which W. W. Merry sees his characteristic prudence in sacrificing feeling to interest (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.88). Merry thinks that the episode of Elpenor, which comes before her, was shaped to tally in form with the beginning and the end of her scene (Merry on 11.51; see [[elpenor]]).

**Silent near the blood.** Before the seer leaves him, Odysseus asks about her: “I see here the soul of my dead mother; / she sits in silence near the blood, and has not brought herself / to look on her own son face to face or to speak to him. / Tell me, lord: how may she know me for who I am?” (11.141–144). τήνδε is “yonder” (Merry on 11.141), and πῶς brings in a direct question, “how may she recognise me, that I am he?” (Merry on 11.144; see the translation's note on 11.144); the ghost seems to linger about the place, Merry adds, as though she had a dim consciousness of some familiar presence. Teiresias gives the rule: any of the dead he lets near the blood will speak truly to him (11.146–149; see [[teiresias]]). The other shades, unlike Teiresias, seem to need the blood to take up again their powers of mind and memory, as she does (Merry on 11.96).

**She knows him.** “but I stayed there steadfastly, until my mother / came and drank the dark blood; and at once she knew me, / and lamenting addressed me with winged words” (11.152–154; see [[dark-blood]] and [[winged-words]]): “My child, how did you come beneath the misty darkness / while you are alive? It is hard for the living to see these things” (11.155–156), the question he had put to Elpenor (11.57). The verses that follow, on the great rivers and the dread streams between, and Oceanus, which no one can cross on foot without a ship (11.157–159; see [[oceanus]] and [[rivers]]), were rejected by the Alexandrian critics. The difficulty, Merry thinks, is that she marvels to see her living son in the kingdom of death, so that her πῶς is almost an exclamation, while the three verses make it a question about the way he came; and Circe had said the contrary, that no one had yet come to Hades in a black ship (10.502; Merry on 11.157–159; see the translation's note on 11.157–159). She asks whether he has come from Troy after long wandering, and has not yet been to Ithaca nor seen his wife (11.160–162), two verses that Aristophanes athetized (Merry's critical note on 11.161–162).

**His questions.** He tells her why he came, and asks how she died: “What fate of death that lays men low brought you down? / Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis of the showering arrows / come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?” (11.171–173; see [[ker-doom]], [[that-lays-men-low]], [[artemis]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). κὴρ … θανάτοιο is the special form of death that comes to each one (Merry on 11.171). Then he asks about his father and his son, whether his prize of honor is still theirs, and about his wife, her plan and her thought (11.174–179).

**Her news.** She answers him in turn: his wife stays in his halls with enduring heart, weeping night and day; no one yet holds his fine prize of honor, and Telemachus holds the domains and feasts at the equal feasts; his father stays in the country, sleeping in winter in the dust by the fire with the slaves, and in summer on beds of fallen leaves on the slope of his vineyard, grieving and longing for his son's homecoming (11.181–196; see [[penelope]], [[telemachus]] and [[laertes]]). Last she tells her own death:

It was not the gentle arrows of Artemis nor a sickness, but “longing for thee, and [for] thy counsels and [for] thy tenderness robbed me of sweet life”, as Merry renders it, and ἐξείλετο, “takes”, is a gnomic aorist, of what commonly happens (Merry on 11.201, 11.202; see the translation's note on 11.202–203, where one manuscript reads κήδεα for μήδεα, and [[honey-sweet]] and [[pothe]]). The swineherd Eumaeus tells the same: “and she died of grief for her glorious son, / a wretched death” (15.358–359, a plain rendering; Merry on 11.85).

**Three times.** Odysseus ponders and wishes to clasp her soul, and three times she flies from his hands “like a shadow or a dream” (11.204–208; see [[odysseus]] and the translation's key words): Merry compares Virgil's Aeneas, who three times tried to put his arms around the neck of Creusa's ghost (*Aeneid* 2.792; Merry on 11.206). He asks why she will not stay, so that they might throw their arms around each other and take their fill of chilling lament, or whether this is “some phantom that noble Persephone / has urged on toward me, so that I may groan and mourn still more” (11.210–214; see [[persephone]], [[eidolon]] and [[lament]]). The pleasure of lament, Seiler noted in Merry's report, is Ovid's *est quaedam flere voluptas*, “there is a certain pleasure in weeping” (*Tristia* 4.3.37; Merry on 11.211).

**The way of mortals.** “Ah me, my child, ill-fated beyond all men, / it is not that Persephone, the daughter of Zeus, deceives you, / but this is the way of mortals, when one of them dies” (11.216–218): the sinews no longer hold flesh and bones together, the fire overcomes them, and the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers (11.219–222; see [[the-way-of-mortals]], [[psyche]] and [[thymos]]). “But long for the light as quickly as you can; and all these things / know, so that you may tell them to your wife hereafter” (11.223–224): the last words, Merry thinks, show nothing more than a kindly feeling toward Penelope, as Eustathius takes them (Merry on 11.223; see [[penelope]]). “So we two were exchanging words” (11.225), and the women sent by Persephone come.
