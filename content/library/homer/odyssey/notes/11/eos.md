summary: No dawn rises in Book 11, which passes within the one night of Odysseus' tale. Dawn is named once, in the pause in the hall, when Alcinous begs Odysseus to go on: “I could hold out even till brilliant Dawn, if you / would bring yourself to tell in the hall of your sorrows” (11.375–376).
# ἠῶ δῖαν (11.375), lowercase in the text, is claimed here as the phrase, as the Iliad's Book 9 note claims it (Il. 9.662).
grc: ["ἠῶ δῖαν"]

**Till brilliant Dawn.** The tale of Books 9 to 12 is told in one night in the hall of Alcinous, and in the pause both speakers measure the night (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Odysseus breaks off the catalogue of heroines, “for before that even the immortal night would wane. But it is time / to sleep” (11.330–331), and Alcinous answers that “This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time / to sleep in the hall” (11.373–374; see [[alcinous]]): “I could hold out even till brilliant Dawn, if you / would bring yourself to tell in the hall of your sorrows” (11.375–376; see [[brilliant]] and [[dawn-line]]). Merry renders “even till morn” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.375). Ἠῶ δῖαν, “brilliant Dawn”, is the end of the verse in which the men “waited for brilliant Dawn” on the island of goats and in the Cyclops' cave (9.151, 9.306, 9.436; see the Book 9 note); here the king will wait for her with a story. The dawn he speaks of comes in Book 13, when the Phaeacians have gone home to sleep, “and when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (13.18), they bring the gifts to the ship.
