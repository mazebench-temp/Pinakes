---
summary: Persephone rules the dead in Book 11. The companions pray over the victims “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (11.47); Odysseus asks whether the phantom of his mother is one that “noble Persephone” has urged on toward him (11.213–214), and his mother answers that “Persephone, the daughter of Zeus” does not deceive him (11.217); the goddess sends up the wives and daughters of chieftains (11.225–227) and scatters them again, “holy Persephone” (11.385–386); and at the end Odysseus flees in fear that she will send the Gorgon's head against him out of Hades (11.633–635).
relations:
  - father: zeus (11.217)
---

**Dread Persephone.** The prayer after the sacrifice, “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (11.47), carries out Circe's instruction in her own words (10.534; see [[hades]], [[dread]] and the Book 10 note). It is the last time Homer calls her ἐπαινή. In the rest of the book she is ἀγαυή, “noble” (11.213, 11.226, 11.635; see [[noble]]), a title that she has only in this book, and once ἁγνή, “holy” (11.386; see [[hagnos]]). Hades is not seen in the book, and of the gods she alone acts among the dead.

**A phantom from the goddess?** Three times Odysseus springs toward his mother's soul, and three times she flies from his hands “like a shadow or a dream” (11.206–207; see [[like-a-shadow-or-a-dream]] and [[psyche]]). He asks: “Or is this some phantom that noble Persephone / has urged on toward me, so that I may groan and mourn still more?” (11.213–214; see [[eidolon]]). Merry takes εἴδωλον for something unreal and illusive, a visible but unsubstantial copy of the person it represents, like the phantom of Aeneas that Apollo made to deceive the Greeks (*Iliad* 5.449) and the phantom of Iphthime that Athena sent to Penelope (4.796) (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.213; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]] and [[iphthime]]). Odysseus suspects a trick of the queen of the dead, a likeness sent to make him grieve. His mother answers:

{{quote:98e0fb00-f7c1-5abc-b938-9e1e932b8690}}

The goddess does not deceive him; the dead are like that (see [[the-way-of-mortals]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]). Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “the daughter of Zeus”, is the only parentage that Homer gives her (see the general article and [[zeus]]).

**She sends the women.** “So we two were exchanging words, and the women / came, for noble Persephone urged them on, / all who had been the wives and daughters of chieftains” (11.225–227). ὤτρυνεν, “urged them on”, is the verb of Odysseus' suspicion (ὤτρυνʼ, 11.214): what he feared she had done with a phantom, she does with the souls of the heroines, and they come up to the blood one after another (11.228–234). Merry observes that, if 11.321–325 are left out, all the women of the catalogue are taken from the legends of the Minyans and the Thebans, and he accepts Lauer's arguments for a Boeotian origin of the passage. Lauer gave the whole book a Boeotian author, in a country “singularly devoted to the worship of Hades and Persephone”, with many oracles of the dead: of Amphiaraus in Thebes, of Teiresias at the springs of Tilphosa, of Trophonius at Lebadea, and at Coronea an opening into the nether world; and the Boeotians were fond of lists of noble women, as the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* shows (Merry on 11.225; see [[hesiod]]).

**Holy Persephone.** The tale goes on after the pause in the hall “when the souls had been scattered this way and that / by holy Persephone, the souls of the womenfolk” (11.385–386), and the soul of Agamemnon comes (see [[agamemnon]] and [[womenfolk]]). ἁγνή is the Odyssey's word for Artemis, “chaste Artemis of the golden throne” (5.123; again 18.202, 20.71; see [[artemis]]), and for the feast of the god on which Antinous would put the bow away (21.258–259); the Iliad never uses it. Merry thinks it possible that here it is a later alteration, from a time when Persephone was regarded from a fresh point of view, and Eustathius knew copies that read αἰνὴ Περσεφόνη, “terrible Persephone” (Merry on 11.386 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.385–386).

**The Gorgon's head.** At the last the countless tribes of the dead gather “with a wondrous din; and pale fear took hold of me / lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades” (11.633–635; see [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]], [[pale-fear]] and [[gorgon]]). The queen who sent the souls up could send a horror with them. Merry takes the Gorgon's head here for any horrible spectre, not the Gorgon's head of the aegis (Merry on 11.632–634). Odysseus does not wait: he goes at once to the ship and orders his companions to loose the stern-cables (11.636–637).
