summary: Elpenor begs by him, “whom you left an only son in your halls”, and Odysseus asks his mother whether his son still holds his prize of honor; she answers that Telemachus holds the domains undisturbed and is invited to the feasts of a man who gives judgment. Agamemnon remembers him as the child at Penelope's breast, a man now, who will greet his father when he comes home (11.68, 11.174–176, 11.184–187, 11.448–451).

**The only son.** Elpenor begs Odysseus by his wife, his father, “and by Telemachus, whom you left an only son in your halls” (11.68; see [[elpenor]]): μοῦνος, the word with which Eurycleia had called him “an only and beloved son” (2.365; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.68). Odysseus asks his mother “of my father and of my son, whom I left behind, / whether my prize of honor is still with them, or some other man / already holds it” (11.174–176; see [[geras]] and [[anticleia]]).

**The domains and the feasts.** She answers: “No one yet holds your fine prize of honor, but undisturbed / Telemachus holds the domains, and feasts at equal feasts, / such as it is fitting for a man who gives judgment to attend; / for all men invite him” (11.184–187; see the translation's key words). Merry takes it that Telemachus is represented as acting king in his father's absence; or, if we must press the fact that at this time he could not have been more than fourteen years old, that he is already admitted to some of the royal honors, the feasts which it is a king's privilege, as dispenser of justice, to enjoy (Merry on 11.184). δικασπόλος is the word of the Iliad's elders who carry the scepter when they give judgment (*Iliad* 1.238), and the invitation to public banquets was a regular part of a king's prerogatives, as Sarpedon tells Glaucus, naming the seat of honor, the meat, the full cups and the domain (*Iliad* 12.310–314; Merry on 11.186; see [[sarpedons-speech]] and [[feast]]). τεμένεα is the reading of Aristarchus, which most editors since Bekker print for τεμένη, and for καλέουσι, “invite him”, one Vienna manuscript has κοτέουσι, perhaps for ποθέουσι (Merry's critical notes on 11.185, 11.187; see the translation's note on 11.184–187).

**The chronology.** The picture does not fit the time. Odysseus visits the dead at the end of the third year after the fall of Troy, when the suitors' misconduct had not yet begun (2.106–110), and Telemachus is spoken of as a grown man here and in Agamemnon's words, as he may have been at his father's return, but not seven years earlier: a scholium on 2.313 records that Aristophanes noticed it, and read κατέδοιεν at 11.116 (Merry on 11.115; see the translation's notes on 11.115–116 and 11.184–187).

**Agamemnon's envy.** “and she had a child at her breast, / an infant, who now, I think, sits among the number of men, / happy one! For his dear father will see him when he comes, / and he will greet his father, as is the custom” (11.448–451; see [[agamemnon]], [[penelope]] and [[themis]]). ὄλβιος, “happy one”, is set against Agamemnon's own lot: his wife did not even let him have his fill of the sight of his son before she killed him (11.452–453; Merry on 11.452; see [[orestes]]).
