**The measure of the giants.** Iphimedeia's sons, Otus and Ephialtes, “were the tallest men the grain-giving earth ever reared, / and by far the handsomest, after famous Orion” (11.309–310; see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[grain-giving]]). W. W. Merry takes the words from καὶ πολύ to Ὠρίωνα for a parenthesis, so that the γάρ of the next verse explains μηκίστους, “the tallest”: at nine years old they were nine cubits broad and nine fathoms tall (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.311). Book 5 told of the hunter whom Dawn took for herself and Artemis killed (5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]]); here his beauty is a standard, as it is among the dead his size.

**The hunter among the dead.**

{{quote:a68514a8-ac91-5b22-b86b-31d37c6a5f5d}}

“Like Minos, Orion continues his customary pursuits in Hades,” Merry writes: a phantom hunter, he drives before him phantom beasts, the shadows of those that he himself (αὐτός) once hunted on the lonely hills (Merry on 11.572; see [[minos]] and [[lonely]]). For the distinction between the man himself and his phantom, which the doubtful verse on Heracles draws more sharply (11.602), Merry adds a remark that Gladstone quoted from Bunsen's *Egypt*: in the Book of the Dead there is a picture of the corpse of a dead man walking, with his soul behind it offering prayers to the Sun-god (Merry on 11.572; see [[heracles]] and [[eidolon]]). εἰλεῦντα is “massing them together”, “cooping them up”, the word used when the great North Wind penned men in (19.200, a plain rendering; Merry on 11.573). Orion is πελώριος, “huge” (11.572; see [[huge]]), as the stone of Sisyphus is “huge” (11.594).

**The club.** His weapon is ῥόπαλον παγχάλκεον, αἰὲν ἀαγές, “a club all of bronze, forever unbroken” (11.575; see [[mace]] and [[bronze]]). The dead in Homer keep their arms and their marks, Merry argues in defending the passage on Heracles: Teiresias holds his golden scepter and Heracles his bow, and “Heracles is not Heracles without it; nor Orion, Orion, without his club” (Merry on 11.601; see [[teiresias]] and [[scepter]]).

**The meadow.** He hunts “across the meadow of asphodel” (κατʼ ἀσφοδελὸν λειμῶνα, 11.573), the meadow over which the soul of Achilles strides away, glad at the news of his son (11.539; see [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]] and [[achilles]]).
