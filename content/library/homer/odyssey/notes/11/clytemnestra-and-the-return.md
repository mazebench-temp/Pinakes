summary: The homecoming that the Iliad's Agamemnon looked forward to is told here by his soul. He thought he would come home welcome to his children and his slaves, and his wife helped to kill him at the feast, and killed beside him the captive he had brought home, Priam's daughter Cassandra (11.405–434).
# Unnamed: the homecoming expected (11.430-431); the captive killed (11.421-423).
refs: [11.421-423, 11.430-431]
---

**The captive.** In the Iliad Agamemnon would not give back Chryseis, but meant to take her home to Argos, where she would grow old in his house, and said that he preferred her to Clytemnestra, his wedded wife (*Iliad* 1.29–31, 1.113–115; see [[chryseis]]). The captive he took home was another, and his soul tells her death: “And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter, / Cassandra, whom scheming Clytemnestra killed / beside me” (11.421–423; see [[cassandra]] and [[clytemnestra]]). Merry imagines her sitting at his side at the feast, her hated presence adding a sting of jealousy to his wife's bitterness (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.423).

**The welcome.** “Truly I thought / that I would come home welcome to my children and my slaves” (11.430–431): he names his children and his slaves, and not his wife, as Merry observes (on 11.430; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). She killed him “contriving slaughter for her wedded husband” (11.430), and the word, κουρίδιος, is the one with which he named her when he set her below his captive, κουριδίης ἀλόχου, “my wedded wife” (*Iliad* 1.114): it implies, Merry explains, the honorable bond of husband and wife of the same station, as against a concubine (on 11.430; see [[wedded]]).
