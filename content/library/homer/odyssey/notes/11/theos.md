---
summary: The gods of a book of the dead: “a god will make it grievous for you” (11.101) and the gods themselves have spun Odysseus' fate (11.139); the gods made Oedipus' marriage known and their deadly plans made him suffer (11.274–276); a god's wrath is threatened for an unburied man (11.73); the Dioscuri have “honor like the gods” (11.304), and Achilles was honored “equally with the gods” (11.484); and Heracles himself is “among the immortal gods” while his phantom is below (11.602).
---

**What the gods do.** The book's gods act from far off. Teiresias tells Odysseus that “a god will make it grievous for you”, meaning Poseidon (11.101; see [[poseidon]]), and Odysseus accepts the prophecy as what “the gods themselves have spun” (11.139; see [[moira]]). The gods brought the truth of Oedipus' marriage to light “at once”, and his sorrows came “through the deadly plans of the gods” (11.274–276; see [[oedipus-kills-laius]]); a hard fate “of a god” fettered the seer of Pylos (11.292; see [[melampus]]); the gods made the arms of Achilles “a bane to the Argives” (11.555; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]). In the pause of the tale Odysseus leaves his escort to “the care of the gods and of you” (11.332), and Arete speaks of the Phaeacians' wealth as held “by the will of the gods” (11.341; see [[pompe]]). Elpenor warns that his unburied body may become “a cause of the gods' wrath” (11.73; see [[menis]]).

**Gods and the dead.** At the pit the companions pray to the gods below (11.46–47; see [[prayer]]), and Teiresias bids Odysseus sacrifice at last “to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky; / to all of them in order” (11.133–134; see [[hecatomb]]). The measure of honor among the dead is the gods': the Dioscuri “have been allotted honor like the gods” (11.304), Achilles was honored “equally with the gods” in life (11.484; see [[time]]), and Theseus and Peirithous are “glorious children of the gods” (11.631). Heracles alone is divided between the two worlds, his phantom among the dead and “he himself among the immortal gods” (11.602; see [[immortality]] and [[eidolon]]). The goddesses of the book are Circe, “a dread goddess with human voice” (11.8; see [[dread-goddess]]), and Persephone, who sends the women and may send the Gorgon's head (11.226, 11.635; see [[persephone]]).
