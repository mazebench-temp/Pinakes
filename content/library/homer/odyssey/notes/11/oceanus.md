---
summary: Oceanus frames Book 11. The ship comes “to the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus” (11.13), the men go “beside the stream of Oceanus” to the place that Circe had shown (11.21–22), and at the end “the wave of the stream carried the ship down the river Oceanus” (11.639). Between, Anticleia names it as the first of the rivers that part the living from the dead, “which no one can cross / on foot, unless he has a well-made ship” (11.158–159).
---

**The limits of Oceanus.** Circe's wind carries the ship all day, and at sunset “she came to the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus” (11.13; see [[deep-streaming]] and [[the-winds]]). Merry renders πείρατα Ὠκεανοῖο “Ocean's bounding-line”. The genitive is almost a material genitive, like the fence of tin on the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.564–565), so that the phrase does not mean Ocean's farthest edge but the boundary that Ocean makes to the land of the living; he compares the “lowest limits / of earth and sea” where Iapetus and Cronus sit (*Iliad* 8.478–479) and Hera's journey “to see the limits of the all-nourishing earth, / and Oceanus, the origin of the gods” (*Iliad* 14.200–201; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.13; see the translation's key-word row and [[cronus]]). There live the Cimmerians, on whom the sun never shines (11.14–19; see [[cimmerians]] and [[helios]]).

**Beside the stream.** “There we came and ran the ship ashore, and took the sheep / out of it; and we ourselves went along beside the stream of Oceanus / until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.20–22; see [[circe]]). Merry notes that the book says nothing more of the shore and the groves of Persephone, or of the meeting of the rivers and the rock that Circe had described; the men only go along the side of the Ocean-stream to the spot, and φράσε, by Aristarchus' rule, means the signs and marks that she gave rather than her words (Merry on 11.22; see [[the-groves-of-persephone]], [[acheron]] and the Book 10 note).

**The first of the rivers.** Anticleia, seeing her son alive among the dead, asks how he came, “For between are great rivers and dread streams, / Oceanus first of all, which no one can cross / on foot, unless he has a well-made ship” (11.157–159; see [[anticleia]], [[rivers]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]). The Alexandrian critics rejected the three verses, and a mutilated scholium gives their reason, that it is absurd to speak of crossing on foot. Merry finds the difficulty elsewhere: Anticleia's πῶς ἦλθες, “how did you come”, is almost a cry of wonder that her son is alive in the kingdom of death, and the verses turn it into a question about the route; and in Book 10 Odysseus had said the contrary, “No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship” (10.502). The words μὲν πρῶτα lead one to expect a description of the other rivers and streams, but nothing more is said of them (Merry on 11.157–159 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.157–159).

**Down the river.** At the end of the book the companions go on board at once, “And the wave of the stream carried the ship down the river Oceanus, / first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind” (11.639–640). κῦμα ῥόοιο is the current down the Ocean-stream, carrying them again toward the sea (Merry on 11.639), the Iliad's phrase for the river that pursues Achilles (*Iliad* 21.263, 21.306; see the translation's key-word row). Merry thinks there was no wind at first because they were in an unearthly place, beyond the ordinary air, and that the fair wind came as they got into the ordinary world again (Merry on 11.640). Book 12 begins where this ends: “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea” (12.1–2).
