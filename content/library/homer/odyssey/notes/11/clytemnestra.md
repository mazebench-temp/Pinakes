summary: In her husband's own account she is the accomplice of Aegisthus, “my ruinous wife”; “scheming Clytemnestra” kills Cassandra beside him, and “the dog-faced one” turns away and will not close his eyes. Odysseus names her with Helen as the women through whom Zeus has hated the house of Atreus, and Agamemnon makes her the reason why no woman can be trusted (11.384, 11.409–410, 11.421–439, 11.452–453).

**The evil woman.** Odysseus announces the tale of the companions who escaped the war “but in their homecoming perished by the will of an evil woman” (11.384): κακῆς γυναικός can only refer to Clytemnestra, W. W. Merry thinks, considering the story that follows at once, though Eustathius and the scholia thought that Helen, the beginner of the evil, might be meant, or even Cassandra (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.384; see [[odysseus]]).

**The accomplice.** Aegisthus killed Agamemnon “with my ruinous wife” (σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ, 11.410; see [[aegisthus]] and [[agamemnon]]): in the Homeric version he is the murderer, and the scene a banquet, where Aeschylus and Euripides make her the killer in the bath, but even here she is the accomplice (Merry on 11.409; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]]). With her own hand she kills Cassandra: “And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter, / Cassandra, whom scheming Clytemnestra killed / beside me” (11.421–423; see [[cassandra]] and [[schemer]]). Merry imagines that Cassandra came in with Agamemnon and sat at his side, her hated presence adding a sting of jealousy to her bitterness (Merry on 11.423). And when the dying king raised his hands to her, “she, the dog-faced one, / turned away, and did not bring herself, though I was going to the house of Hades, / to close my eyes with her hands or to shut my mouth” (11.424–426; see [[dog-face]]): νοσφίσατο, “turned away”, is the word of 23.98, and she would not listen to his prayer (Merry on 11.423).

**The example.** From her deed Agamemnon draws his rule, that there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman who puts such deeds into her heart, “such as the shameful deed that she too devised, / contriving slaughter for her wedded husband” (11.427–430; see [[wedded]]): οἷον δή illustrates the general sentiment by the special instance (Merry on 11.429). She has poured shame on herself and on all women to come, “even on one who does good” (11.432–434). Odysseus answers that Zeus has hated the offspring of Atreus “through the plans of women, / from of old: for Helen's sake many of us perished, / and against you Clytemnestra was preparing a snare while you were far away” (11.437–439; see [[helen]] and [[dolos]]), and Agamemnon ends with her last wrong, that she killed him before he could have his fill of the sight of his son (11.452–453; see [[orestes]]) and so “there is no trusting women any more” (11.456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). Penelope is set against her (11.444–446; see [[penelope]] and [[clytemnestra-and-the-return]]).
