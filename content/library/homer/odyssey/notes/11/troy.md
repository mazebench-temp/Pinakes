summary: Troy is the past that the dead and the living share: Anticleia was alive when her son “went to sacred Ilios” (11.86); Alcinous asks after the companions who “followed together to Ilios” (11.372); and Odysseus tells Achilles of his son's deeds at Troy, in the councils, on the plain and in the horse, until “we had sacked the steep city of Priam” (11.499–537).
# Troy without the name: "the steep city of Priam" (11.533), Πριάμοιο πόλιν.
refs: [11.533]
---

**Sacred Ilios.** The war divides the dead from the living. Odysseus left his mother alive “when I went to sacred Ilios” (11.86; see [[anticleia]] and [[hieros]]); she asks whether he has come “from Troy, after wandering / a long time” (11.160–161), and he answers that he has wandered ever since he followed Agamemnon “to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (11.168–169; see [[agamemnon]] and [[trojans]]). In the pause, Alcinous asks whether he saw any of the companions “who with you / followed together to Ilios and there met their fate” (11.371–372; see [[alcinous]]); in ἅμʼ αὐτῷ … ἅμʼ ἕποντο, W. W. Merry notes, the first ἅμα marks company and the second time, “who set out together with you yourself” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.371).

**Wide Troy.** Achilles wishes he were under the rays of the sun “such as I was once in wide Troy, / when I killed the best of the army, defending the Argives” (11.499–500; see [[achilles]] and [[argives]]), and Odysseus answers with the war his son fought: “whenever we took counsel about the city of Troy” Neoptolemus spoke first (11.510–512); on the plain of the Trojans he ran far out in front (11.513–516); in the wooden horse he alone did not weep (11.523–532); and “when we had sacked the steep city of Priam” he went aboard with his share and a noble prize, unharmed (11.533–537; see [[neoptolemus]], [[the-wooden-horse]] and [[steep]]). πόλιν Τροίην, “the city of Troy”, is said as πόλιν Καλυδῶνα, “the city of Calydon”, and there is no need, Merry says, to make Τροίην an adjective, as some ancient readers did with Τροΐην, “the Trojan city” (on 11.510 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.510–513). The prize, γέρας, was in the *Little Iliad*, as Merry reports a fragment of it, Andromache, Hector's wife, but the word need mean no more than a special piece of the spoils (on 11.534).
