**The giants.** Iphimedeia “bore two sons, but they were short-lived, / godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes” (11.307–308; see [[iphimedeia]], [[ephialtes]] and [[antitheos]]). The Iliad calls the brothers “the sons of Aloeus” (*Iliad* 5.386); their mother here says that their father was Poseidon (11.306; see [[aloeus]] and [[poseidon]]). They were “the tallest men the grain-giving earth ever reared, / and by far the handsomest, after famous Orion” (11.309–310; see [[orion]] and [[grain-giving]]), “for at nine years old they were nine cubits / in breadth, and in height they had grown nine fathoms” (11.311–312; see the translation's row ἐννέωρος, ἐννεάπηχυς, ἐννεόργυιος). W. W. Merry takes ἐννέωροι here as “at nine years of age”, which ἐννεαπήχεες seems to require and which suits their short life; γάρ goes back to μηκίστους, “tallest”, and the words about Orion are a parenthesis (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.311; see the Book 10 note on [[cattle]], where the same word is said of Aeolus' ox). εὖρος is “breadth” here, not the East Wind. The size of these children of the soil, Merry adds, recalls the earth-born Tityus, who lies over nine plethra (on 11.307; 11.577; see [[tityus]]).

**The threat.** “They even threatened the immortals on Olympus / that they would raise the din of furious war” (11.313–314; see [[olympus]]). φύλοπις, “the din of war”, is common in the Iliad but in the Odyssey found only here and twice more (16.268, 24.475); Merry gives the common derivation from φῦλον and ὄψ, “the battle cry of the hosts”, and compares ἔριν στήσαντες, “raising strife”, for the verb (on 11.314; 16.292 = 19.11). Then: “They were eager to set Ossa on Olympus, and on Ossa / Pelion with its quivering leaves, so that heaven might be scaled” (11.315–316; see [[the-aloadae-threaten-olympus]], [[ossa-mountain]], [[pelion]] and [[with-quivering-leaves]]). The ancient critics rejected the two verses “as impossible” (Aristarchus, according to the scholion), and Eustathius records that those who defended them laid the weight on μέμασαν, “they were eager”: the brothers had the will but not the power. The difficulty Aristarchus felt, Merry suggests, was that in one verse Olympus is the scene of the fight and in the next it is only the first step of a stairway to a heaven above it, whereas Aristarchus held that Ὄλυμπος in Homer always means the mountain; Merry thinks the verses very likely an interpolation from some poem of the battle of gods and giants, a story that Homer does not tell and the Hesiodic epic does, and compares the tower on the plain of Shinar, “whose top should reach unto heaven” (on 11.315–316 and his critical note; Genesis 11:4; see the translation's note on 11.315–316).

**Before their beards grew.** “And they would have accomplished it, if they had reached the measure of youth” (11.317). Their killer is named by his parents, “the son of Zeus, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore” (11.318; see [[apollo]], [[leto]] and [[lovely-haired]]), and he killed them “before the whiskers under their temples / had bloomed and covered their jaws with a fresh growth of down” (11.319–320; see the translation's row ἴουλοι; ἐυανθὴς λάχνη; γένυς). ἴουλος is found only here in Homer. Merry would keep ἰούλους as the subject of both verbs, “and covered their cheeks with sprouting down”, where others make γένυς the subject and the verb intransitive (on 11.320). For their death he cites Pindar, in whom they are slain by Artemis in Naxos (on 11.318, citing *Pythian* 4.88; see the general article).

**The Iliad's story.** The Odyssey says nothing of the binding of Ares (*Iliad* 5.385–391; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]]). Merry quotes the scholion on that passage, that the Aloadae put an end to war and to the preparations for it, and made men live in peace (on 11.307).
