summary: Theseus is named twice in Odysseus' tale of the dead: as the man who was bringing Ariadne from Crete to Athens when Artemis killed her in Dia (11.321–325), and, with Peirithous, among the heroes of former days whom Odysseus would have stayed to see, “glorious children of the gods”, had the dead not gathered around him (11.628–632). Both passages were suspected of being Athenian additions.

**Ariadne.** “And I saw Phaedra and Procris and beautiful Ariadne, / the daughter of baleful Minos, whom Theseus once / was bringing from Crete to the hill of sacred Athens, / but he had no joy of her; for before that Artemis slew her / in sea-washed Dia, on the testimony of Dionysus” (11.321–325; see [[ariadne]], [[minos]], [[athens]], [[artemis]], [[dia]] and [[dionysus]]). W. W. Merry notes that this form of the story differs from the common account, in which Theseus deserts Ariadne in Dia, that is Naxos, and Dionysus takes her to wife: Homer's Dia is the islet off Heracleion on the north coast of Crete, which Theseus would pass on the way to Athens. Of the “testimony” of Dionysus he reports two explanations, that Dionysus told Artemis that Theseus had lain with Ariadne in her sacred grove on Dia, or that Ariadne had been promised to Dionysus and gave herself to a mortal lover (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.322; see the translation's note on 11.321–325). οὐδʼ ἀπόνητο, “he had no joy of her”, stands without the genitive that the verb takes elsewhere, the enjoyment of one's excellence, honor or youth (Merry on 11.324). Aristophanes read ἔσχε for ἔκτα, which Merry thinks probably original: Artemis then “stopped her”, let her go no further with Theseus (Merry on 11.324 and his critical note). Hesiod knows the other story, that golden-haired Dionysus made Ariadne, the daughter of Minos, his wife, and Zeus made her immortal and ageless (*Theogony* 947–949; see [[hesiod]]).

**Athenian verses.** The three heroines of 11.321 belong to Attic legend and break the run of Thessalian and Boeotian women, and Merry takes 11.321–325 for a later interpolation, perhaps of the time of the Peisistratean recension (Merry on 11.321). He counts these verses and 11.631, with *Iliad* 1.265, among the traces of Athenian interpolation in Homer (Merry on 11.565; see [[theseus-line]] and [[pisistratus|Peisistratus]]).

**The heroes he did not see.** At the end of the tale Odysseus waits on, “in case any more should come / of the heroes who had perished in former days. / And I would have seen still more men of earlier times, whom I wished to see, / Theseus and Peirithous, glorious children of the gods; / but before that the countless tribes of the dead gathered / with a wondrous din” (11.628–633; see [[peirithous]], [[glorious]], [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). The pair are named as men of an older generation, like the heroes whom Nestor names as the mightiest he ever knew, Peirithous among them and, in the suspected line, Theseus (*Iliad* 1.260–265; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). Odysseus does not say why they should be in Hades. Hereas of Megara, as Plutarch reports him, said that Peisistratus inserted the verse into Homer's Nekyia to please the Athenians, quoting it with ἀριδείκετα, “conspicuous”, where our text has ἐρικυδέα (Plutarch, *Theseus* 20, as Merry quotes it in his critical note on 11.631); Merry brackets the verse (see the translation's note on 11.631 and [[theseus-line]]).

**Later underworlds.** Later poets put Theseus and Peirithous in Hades for their own descent. Virgil's Charon says that he was not glad to take on his lake either Alcides or “Theseus and Pirithous, though they were born of gods”, for Heracles came to put the warder of Tartarus in chains, and “these set out to carry off the queen from the chamber of Dis” (*Aeneid* 6.392–397, plain renderings), and in Tartarus “unhappy Theseus sits, and will sit forever” (*Aeneid* 6.617–618; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]). In Euripides' *Heracles* the hero says that he was delayed under the earth by bringing Theseus back from Hades (*Heracles* 619; see [[euripides]]).
