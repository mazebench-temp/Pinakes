**The first example.** “And as Homer often does, making lifeless things alive through metaphor. In all of them it is by making activity that he is well liked, as in these: ‘Back down to the ground rolled the shameless stone’”, and four more verses follow, all from the Iliad, with a simile after them (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1411b31–1412a9; see [[before-the-eyes]] and [[metaphora]]). The verse is 11.598, and Aristotle's text of it differs from ours by two words: αὖτις ἐπὶ δάπεδόνδε κυλίνδετο λᾶας ἀναιδής, with ἐπὶ δάπεδόνδε, “down to the ground”, where ours has ἔπειτα πέδονδε, “then to the plain” (*Rhetoric* 1411b34–35; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 11.598; see the translation's note on 11.598). δάπεδον is the floor or ground, the word on which Tityus lies a few lines before (ἐν δαπέδῳ, 11.577; see [[tityus]]).

**The analogy.** The verses please, Aristotle says, because the things seem active, being alive: “being shameless and raging and the rest are activity. He attached these through metaphor by analogy: as the stone is to Sisyphus, so the shameless man is to the one he treats shamelessly” (*Rhetoric* 1412a3–7; see [[sisyphus]]). The stone that rolls back whenever its pusher is about to heave it over the top behaves toward him as a shameless man behaves toward one he wrongs, without regard. Merry, too, cites the passage as Aristotle's famous instance of making lifeless things alive by metaphor (on 11.597–598).

**Homer's shameless stone.** λᾶας ἀναιδής is said in the Iliad of a stone thrown in battle, which “crushed both the tendons and the bones” of Diores (*Iliad* 4.521), and the rock from which a winter torrent breaks the boulder of Hector's charge is “the shameless rock” (*Iliad* 13.139); in the Odyssey the phrase is used only here (see [[shameless-stone]] and [[anaideia]]). Only here does the stone act of its own accord, with no thrower behind it, and Merry takes the word for the original of Virgil's *improbus* (on 11.597–598). The verse in which it falls is all dactyls to its last foot, and Eustathius, as Merry quotes him, heard the labor of the climb in the crowded vowels of 11.596 and the easy fall of the stone in this verse, which has no hiatus and runs (Merry on 11.596).
