summary: Hera is named once in Book 11, as the mother of Hebe, the wife of Heracles among the gods: “the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (11.604). Heracles' phantom speaks of the man “far worse than I” to whom he was made subject (11.621–622), the servitude that her trick at his birth imposed on him in the Iliad.
relations:
  - child: hebe (11.604)

**Hera of the golden sandals.** Of Heracles Odysseus sees only “his phantom; but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts, and has Hebe of the lovely ankles, / the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (11.602–604; see [[heracles]], [[hebe]] and [[immortality]]). χρυσοπέδιλος, “of the golden sandals”, is found only here in Homer (see [[of-the-golden-sandals]] and the translation's key-word row), where the Iliad calls her χρυσόθρονος, “of the golden throne” (*Iliad* 1.611, 14.153, 15.5; see [[golden-throned]]). The whole verse is in Hesiod's account of the same marriage: Heracles, his labors done, made Hebe, “the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals”, his wife on snowy Olympus (*Theogony* 950–955), and Hesiod gives Hera the epithet again among the children of Rhea (*Theogony* 454; see [[hesiod]]). The verses were rejected in antiquity and ascribed to Onomacritus, and Merry notes that 11.604 is copied from Hesiod (Merry's critical note on 11.602–603; see the translation's note on 11.602–604).

**Heracles and Hera.** In the Iliad Heracles is the son whom Hera persecuted: on the day of his birth she tricked Zeus into an oath and hastened the birth of Eurystheus, so that Heracles was made subject to him (*Iliad* 19.95–133), and Achilles says that fate brought Heracles down “and the grievous anger of Hera” (*Iliad* 18.119). The phantom's own words recall that servitude: “for I was made subject to a man far worse than I, / and he laid hard labors upon me” (11.621–622; see [[eurystheus]] and [[heracles-and-cerberus]]). Merry names the man as Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus, to whose service Heracles was bound through the spiteful intrigues of Hera (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.621, citing *Iliad* 19.97). The verses that make him Hera's son-in-law among the gods have no part in the Iliad's story, and Merry, with the critics, would cut them (Merry on 11.601).
