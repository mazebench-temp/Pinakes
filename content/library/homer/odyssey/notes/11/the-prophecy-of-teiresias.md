**The anger of Poseidon.** “You seek a honey-sweet homecoming” (11.100; see [[nostos]] and [[honey-sweet]]): δίζηαι is the second person of δίζημαι without its σ (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.100). ὅ in the next verses is the conjunction, “in that he hath stored up wrath in his heart against thee”, and the subject of λήσειν is “you”: “I do not think / you will escape” the earth-shaker's notice (11.101–103; Merry on 11.102; see the translation's note on 11.102 and [[poseidon]]). The two verses of the god's grudge return when Athena tells Odysseus on Ithaca that she did not wish to fight with Poseidon, her father's brother, “who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son”, with the relative ὅς for ὅ (13.341–343, a plain rendering).

**The cattle.** ἀλλʼ ἔτι μέν κε καὶ ὣς: “but still for all that ye may yet come home, if only thou dost choose to restrain thy desire and that of thy comrades” (11.104–105; Merry on 11.104), the restraint being needed when they reach Thrinacia and find the herds grazing (11.106–108; Merry on 11.108). The Sun “sees all things and hears all things” (11.109), and the scholia draw the lesson that no wrongdoer escapes his notice (Merry on 11.109; see [[helios]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]). τεκμαίρομαι, which elsewhere in the poem is “ordain”, is in the seer's mouth “foretell”: “then I foretell destruction for you” (11.112; see the translation's note on 11.112). Five of these verses, from “If you leave them unharmed” to “having lost all your companions”, are the ones Circe speaks on Aeaea (11.110–114 = 12.137–141, Greek only; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**The curse.** From “you will come home late and in evil plight” the seer speaks the Cyclops' curse, in its two verses (11.114–115 = 9.534–535 in the second person; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). In δήεις δʼ ἐν πήματα οἴκῳ the preposition stands apart from its noun, a licence that Merry discusses at 6.167 (Merry on 11.115; see the translation's note on 6.167). The suitors “are devouring your substance” in the present tense, though the visit to the dead falls at the end of the third year after Troy, and the second book shows that their outrages began no more than four years before Odysseus came home (Merry on 11.115, citing 2.106 and following); elsewhere in the book Telemachus, too, is spoken of as a grown man (11.184–187, 11.449). Merry weighs the explanations, a prophetic present or the future force of δήεις carried over, and reports that Aristophanes read κατέδοιεν, “who may be devouring”, while Aristarchus kept κατέδουσι (11.115–117; Merry on 11.115–116 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.115–116 and [[the-suitors]]). “whether by guile or openly” (11.120) leaves open the manner of the vengeance (see [[dolos]]).

**The oar.** The commands of the last part are serious ones: Odysseus explains to Penelope that a necessity is laid upon him (Merry on 11.119; 23.248–253). The men “who do not know the sea / and do not eat food mixed with salt” (11.122–123) are a people as far inland as possible; if not to know the sea means not to use salt, salt was made from sea water, as the plural ἅλες, “salt grains”, may also suggest (Merry on 11.122; see the translation's key-word row for ἅλεσσι μεμιγμένον εἶδαρ). They know neither “ships with crimson prows” nor oars, “which are wings to ships” (11.124–125): the oars speed the ship, and their rise and fall on either side is like the movement of a bird's wings; Merry compares Virgil's *remigium alarum*, “the oarage of wings” (on 11.129; see the translation's key-word rows for φοινικοπάρῃος and πτερὰ νηυσί, and [[oars]]). The sign is a wayfarer who takes the oar on his shoulder for a winnowing fan, ἀθηρηλοιγός, a mistake that would not be absurd, since both could be called πλάται, “blades” (11.126–128; Merry on 11.128; see [[winnowing-fan]]). The verse of the sign is the verse in which Nestor gives his son the sign of the turning post (11.126 = *Iliad* 23.326), and the oar to be planted in the ground recalls Elpenor's oar on his mound (11.77, 11.129; Merry on 11.129; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).

**The sacrifices.** The ram, the bull and the boar make a triple offering, which Nitzsch compares with the Roman *suovetaurilia*; it is a special sacrifice, for the usual victim of the sea god is a black bull (11.131; Merry on 11.131, citing 3.5–6; see [[sacrifice]] and [[ram]]). Then the hecatombs at home, “to all of them in order” (11.132–134; see [[hecatomb]]). The text prints ἱερᾶς for ἱερὰς at 11.132 (see the translation's note on 11.132).

**The death.** θάνατος … ἐξ ἁλός, “death … away from the sea” (11.134). Interpreters, Eustathius among them, take ἐξ ἁλός so, with ἐκ in the sense of “outside”, “far from”; Merry argues that with ἐλεύσεται anyone would take the words to mean “will come upon you out of the sea”, as the earth-shaker came ἐξ ἁλός (*Iliad* 20.14), and that the verses allude to the later story of Odysseus' death by the spear of Telegonus (on 11.134; see the translation's note on 11.134–137 and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]). He finds other signs of a later hand: ἀβληχρός, “gentle”, means in Homer rather “weak”, “exhausted” (*Iliad* 5.337, 8.178); a death that is only the decay of natural strength is strangely said to “kill” (πέφνῃ); the personified θάνατος is suspicious; and ἀρημένος, “worn out”, is used with a plain dative, not with ὑπό. The last words, he grants, are meant to give the picture of a king dying of old age among loyal and prosperous subjects (Merry on 11.134; see [[thanatos]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). μάλα τοῖος, “so very”, may have been spoken with a gesture, a slow dropping of the hand (Merry on 11.135).

**What Circe promised.** Teiresias does not do what Circe foretold: he was to tell the way and the measures of the path and the homecoming (10.539–540), and he may be said to have described the measures, but not the way (Merry on 11.136; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]).

**The answer.** “Teiresias, this, I suppose, the gods themselves have spun” (11.139): που gives the sentence a tone of resignation, and the stress on αὐτοί at the end of the verse means “of their own will”, “without consulting me” (Merry on 11.139; see [[fates]]). Then Odysseus turns at once to his mother, who sits by the blood and does not know him, and asks “how may she know me for who I am?”, a direct question (11.141–144; Merry on 11.144; see the translation's note on 11.144). The seer's answer gives the rule of the blood (11.146–149), and his soul goes back into the house of Hades “when he had told the oracles” (11.150–151; see [[teiresias]] and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]).
