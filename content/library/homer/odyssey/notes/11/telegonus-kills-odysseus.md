# Unnamed: the death foretold (11.134-137).
refs: [11.134-137]
passages:

**Away from the sea or out of it.** “And death will come to you yourself away from the sea, / a death so very gentle, that will kill you / worn out by an old age of ease; and around you your people / will be prosperous” (11.134–137; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[thanatos]]). The translation takes ἐξ ἁλός as interpreters generally have, Eustathius among them, of a death far from the sea, at the end of a journey to men who do not know it. Merry argues that with ἐλεύσεται, “will come”, the natural sense is “will come upon you out of the sea”, and that the words fall in so completely with the later legends of Odysseus' death that the passage must be a post-Homeric addition: Lauer thought it written when the story of Telegonus had been formed and rhapsodes sought to join it to the wanderings (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.134; see the translation's note on 11.134–137). The scholia already explain the words by the spine of the stingray that Telegonus used for a spear-point, and Ptolemy of Ascalon read ἔξαλος, “of the mainland”, to keep the death away from the sea (Merry's critical note on 11.134).

**The Telegony.** Merry tells the plot as he knew it: the *Telegonia*, ascribed to Eugammon of Cyrene and said to be taken from an older *Thesprotis*, brought Odysseus to Thesprotia, where he married its queen Callidice; after her death he went back to Ithaca, where Telegonus, his son by Circe, landed in search of his father and ravaged the coast, and Odysseus, coming out against him, was killed by his son. Later forms of the story made sure of the death “out of the sea” with a spear tipped with the bone of the sea fish τρυγών, the stingray, the subject of Sophocles' *Odysseus Struck by the Spine*; and a fragment of Aeschylus' *Soul-Summoners* gives a heron's dung and the spine of a creature of the sea (on 11.134; fr. 275 Nauck; see [[telegonus]], [[odysseus-wounded]] and [[aeschylus]]). Odysseus tells Penelope the same death in the same verses, turned to the first person, on the night of his return (23.281–284, Greek only).
