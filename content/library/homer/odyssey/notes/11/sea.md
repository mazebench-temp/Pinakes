---
summary: The sea opens and closes the book's voyage (11.1–2, 11.639–640), and in Teiresias' prophecy it marks the measure of Odysseus' life: an oar carried inland “until you come to men who do not know the sea”, and a gentle death “away from the sea” (11.121–137).
---

**Men who do not know the sea.** Teiresias sends Odysseus, after the killing of the suitors, on a last journey: to carry a well-fitted oar inland “until you come to men who do not know the sea / and do not eat food mixed with salt” (11.121–123; see [[teiresias]] and [[oars]]). The ancients, W. W. Merry reports, took them for the Epirots or Thesprotians, who answer very badly to the description; he thinks the tradition only means that Odysseus went as far inland as he could (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.122). The sign that he has arrived is that a wayfarer will take his oar for a winnowing fan (11.127–128; see [[winnowing-fan]]).

**Away from the sea.** The death foretold for him is θάνατος … ἐξ ἁλός, “death … away from the sea”, as the tradition takes it, from Eustathius on: “And death will come to you yourself away from the sea, / a death so very gentle” (11.134–135; see [[thanatos]]). Merry argues that anyone would naturally render the words “out of the sea”, as the Earthshaker came ἐξ ἁλός in the Iliad (*Iliad* 20.14), and that the prophecy fits the later legend of Odysseus' death from the sea in the *Telegony*; Ptolemy of Ascalon read ἔξαλος, “away from the sea”, to remove the difficulty (on 11.134; see the translation's note on 11.134–137).

**The sea of the voyage.** The book opens as the ship is dragged “down into the shining sea” (11.2; see [[shining-sea]]) and ends as “the wave of the stream carried the ship down the river Oceanus” (11.639; see [[oceanus]]), out of which it comes to “the wave of the wide-wayed sea” in the next book (12.1–2). Elpenor asks for a mound “on the shore of the gray sea” (11.75; see [[gray-sea]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]); Thrinacia lies beyond “the violet sea” (11.107; see [[violet-sea]] and [[thrinacia]]); and Poseidon, after lying with Tyro, “plunged beneath the billowing sea” (11.253; see [[poseidon]]).
