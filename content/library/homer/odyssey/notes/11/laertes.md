**Son of Laertes.** διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices”, is the verse with which Elpenor, Teiresias, Agamemnon, Achilles and Heracles address Odysseus (11.60, 11.92, 11.405, 11.473, 11.617; see [[son-of-laertes]] and [[odysseus]]). At 11.60 it is missing from most manuscripts, and at 11.92 it is found only in a few (see the translation's note on 11.60, 11.92).

**The father at home.** Elpenor begs Odysseus “by your wife and your father, who reared you when you were small, / and by Telemachus” (11.67–68; see [[elpenor]] and [[supplication]]), and Odysseus asks his mother to tell him “of my father and of my son, whom I left behind” (11.174; see [[anticleia]]). Her answer:

{{quote:24f5744d-613b-5ed3-a9c9-57aea436e04b}}

αὐτόθι, “there”, is explained by ἀγρῷ, “in the country” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.187), and εὐναί is predicative: “nor has he for bedding couches and cloaks and bright-coloured rugs, but in winter time he sleeps where the servants sleep in the house, in the dust near the fire, and sorry clothes has he got on his back” (Merry on 11.188; see the translation's note on 11.188–189 and [[bed]], [[cloak]], [[blankets]] and [[clothing]]). The low bed of leaves strewn on the ground in the summer is contrasted with the beds, δέμνια, that he lacks (Merry on 11.194), and ὀπώρη is the latter part of the summer (Merry on 11.192; see the translation's key words). πένθος ἀέξει is to nurse a sorrow, as ἀέξειν is said of fostering grief at 17.489, and ἐπί is “besides”: old age comes on him to add to his trouble (Merry on 11.195, 11.196; see [[penthos]]). The reading σὸν νόστον ποθέων, “longing for your homecoming”, is that of the better copies according to a scholium, against σὸν πότμον γοόων, “lamenting your fate”, in most manuscripts (Merry's critical note on 11.196; see the translation's note on 11.196).

This is the picture of Laertes that the first book drew: he no longer comes to the city, but suffers woes far away in the country with an old handmaid who gives him food and drink “as he creeps along the slope of his vineyard” (1.189–193), the slope of 11.193. The last book will show it again, when Odysseus finds him alone in his orchard, digging around a plant in a patched and dirty tunic, πένθος ἀέξων, “nursing his sorrow”, in the words of 11.195 (24.226–231, a plain rendering). His wife's death, Anticleia says, came from the same longing (11.197–203); the swineherd tells Odysseus that her death grieved Laertes most of all (15.355–357).
