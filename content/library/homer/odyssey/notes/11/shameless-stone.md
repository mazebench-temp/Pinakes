---
summary: Sisyphus pushes the stone up toward the crest, “but whenever he was about / to heave it over the top, then its mighty weight would turn it back; / back then to the plain rolled the shameless stone” (11.596–598).
---

λᾶας ἀναιδής, the stone of battle in the Iliad (see the general article), is here the stone that will not stay. Merry calls the epithet the original of Virgil's *improbus*, and notes that Aristotle quotes the verse as a famous instance of making lifeless things alive by metaphor (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.598; *Rhetoric* 1411b34–35; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]] and [[sisyphus]]). κραταιίς, “its mighty weight,” may also be a power outside the stone (see the translation's note on 11.596–597).
