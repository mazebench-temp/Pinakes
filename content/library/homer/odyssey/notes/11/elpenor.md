**The first of the dead.** “And first came the soul of my companion Elpenor; / for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth; / for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.51–54; see [[psyche]] and [[wide-wayed-earth]]). His soul comes first, W. W. Merry explains, because, being unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades and take his place among the rest of the departed, as the soul of Patroclus begs Achilles to bury him quickly so that he may pass the gates, since the other souls keep him off (*Iliad* 23.71–74; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). σῶμα is always the dead body in Homer (Merry on 11.53). ἄκλαυτον καὶ ἄθαπτον, “unwept and unburied”, is said in the Iliad of the corpse of Patroclus lying beside the ships, νέκυς ἄκλαυτος ἄθαπτος (*Iliad* 22.386; see [[unwept-and-unburied]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Callistratus athetized these three verses, because Odysseus, having just told of the death, goes on to ask how he came there; unless, the scholium adds, he means that they had not noticed the death for being busy with other things (Merry's critical note on 11.52–54; see the translation's note on 11.51–83).

**How did you come?** Odysseus weeps and greets him: “Elpenor, how did you come beneath the misty darkness? / You have come faster on foot than I in my black ship” (11.57–58; see [[odysseus]]). ἰών, “coming”, is printed against ἐών, the reading of all the manuscripts but two (Merry's critical note on 11.58; see the translation's note on 11.58). The scholiast warns that the words are not banter but astonishment that there was a quicker way to the realm of Hades than the one he had come by, with a strong following wind. The scholia ask, too, why none of the men killed by the Laestrygonians or eaten by the Cyclops come to meet their captain, and answer that they were not, like Elpenor, hanging about the gates of Hades: they were buried, “even if lawlessly”. Merry does not think the answer absurd, since the chief evil of lying unburied was to be left exposed to sight in the upper world, and to be eaten by a monster at least hid a man away (Merry on 11.58; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[laestrygonians]]).

**Wine and the roof.** He answers in the verse of address that Murray prints and most manuscripts leave out (11.60; see the translation's note on 11.60, 11.92): “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices, / an evil fate from a god led me into blind folly, and endless wine” (11.60–61; see [[ate]], [[wine]] and the translation's key words). δαίμονος is quite vague, “some evil power”, with no allusion to Circe (Merry on 11.61; see [[daimon]] and [[aisa]]). Then he tells his fall in the words of the narrative of Book 10, “Lying down in the hall of Circe I did not think / to come back down by going to the long ladder, / but fell straight down from the roof; and my neck / was broken from the vertebrae, and my soul went down to Hades” (11.62–65, the last three verses as 10.558–560 in the first person; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[house]]).

**The plea.**

{{quote:ec1f74b3-7b63-50b7-b63f-aaae98d98285}}

τῶν ὄπιθεν are those left behind at home, as the scholiast explains, and οὐ παρεόντων, “though they are not here”, is the appeal of Nestor to the Achaeans by their children and wives and parents, τῶν ὕπερ ἐνθάδʼ ἐγὼ γουνάζομαι οὐ παρεόντων (*Iliad* 15.662–666; Merry on 11.66; see [[supplication]]). νοσφισθείς, “turning away”, carries the idea of desertion (Merry on 11.73), and θεῶν μήνιμα, “a cause of the gods' wrath” (see the translation's key words and [[menis]]), is the word of the dying Hector to Achilles (*Iliad* 22.358). Merry calls the claim a post-Homeric refinement: the gods are not shown taking offense at the sight of an unburied corpse, and Hector's warning concerns the outrage done to his body, not the denial of burial (Merry on 11.51). He wants to be burned with his armor, as Achilles burned Eetion “with his intricate armor” (*Iliad* 6.418; Merry on 11.74; see [[cremation]]), and a mound on the shore, “the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of” (11.76; see [[tomb]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]), where the genitive ἀνδρὸς δυστήνοιο depends on σῆμα as if no μοι had gone before (Merry on 11.76). The oar on the mound would mark it for all who passed by the shore, few indeed off the solitary coasts of Aeaea (Merry on 11.74; see [[oars]] and [[aeaea]]): Nitzsch compared the epitaph of the fisherman Pelagon, ascribed to Sappho, on whose tomb his father set a net and an oar (*Palatine Anthology* 7.505, as Merry reports it), and Aristotle records that the Iberians, a warlike people, fix around a man's tomb as many spits as he killed enemies (*Politics* 1324b19–21; Merry on 11.74).

**How did he know?** Elpenor knows that Odysseus will put in again at Aeaea (11.69–70), though no one has told him. Merry finds the knowledge unaccountable: Eustathius has him speak as a prophet, and Nitzsch would weaken οἶδα, “I know”, to an expression of strong belief (Merry on 11.51, 11.69). Odysseus answers in one verse, “All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you” (11.80), which Lauer found short and abrupt, as if the poet were hurrying on to the heart of the book (Merry on 11.80).

**The two sitting apart.** “So we two sat exchanging hateful words, / I on one side, holding my sword over the blood, / and on the other the phantom of my companion, saying many things” (11.81–83; see [[sword]], [[eidolon]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). στυγεροῖσιν is “lamentable” for Merry, “hateful” in the translation as everywhere, and ἥμεθα may mean only “we bided there” (see the translation's note on 11.81–82). Elpenor asks for no blood and shows no need of it (Merry on 11.96).

**The doubts.** Merry thought the whole episode hard to accept as genuine. With Ad. Hermann he finds it strange that so large a part goes to one humble companion when none of the others Odysseus lost is mentioned, and with Lauer that the meeting with Teiresias, the purpose of the journey, is put off for a man described as the youngest and not sound in his wits (10.552–553). Odysseus knew of the death and yet asks how he came; Elpenor knows what no one has told him; and his claim to burial is a later refinement. Eustathius remarks, not amiss in Merry's view, that Elpenor is not sound in his wits even in Hades, since he asks simply for cheap things, and for a mound with an oar set up on it that can serve no purpose. Merry takes the episode for an addition from a time when the book of the dead was recited as a separate rhapsody, and the interpolator for having shaped its beginning and end to match the scene of Anticleia (11.51 with 11.84, and 11.48–50 with 11.81–82; Merry on 11.51; see the translation's note on 11.51–83 and [[anticleia]]).

**The burial.** At dawn after their return to Aeaea, Odysseus sends companions to Circe's house to bring the body; they cut logs where the headland juts out farthest and bury him, grieving, and when the body and the armor are burned they heap up a tomb, drag a pillar up onto it, and fix the well-fitted oar on the top of the tomb (12.8–15): the promise of 11.74–78 kept to the letter (see [[cremation]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).
