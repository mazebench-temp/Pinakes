---
summary: Athena is named twice in Book 11, both times in the stories of heroes: “the sons of the Trojans were the judges, and Pallas Athena” of the arms of Achilles, when Odysseus won them and Ajax lost (11.547), and Heracles says that when he brought the hound out of Hades, “Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena” (11.626).
---

**The judgment of the arms.** The soul of Ajax stands apart from the others, still angry over the victory that Odysseus won “for the armor of Achilles; his lady mother had set it as a prize, / and the sons of the Trojans were the judges, and Pallas Athena” (11.546–547; see [[ajax]], [[the-judgment-of-arms]], [[thetis]] and [[pallas]]). Aristarchus rejected the verse. His reasons are not given, but Merry reports Nitzsch's: the Trojan captives would be the most unlikely umpires in a quarrel between the Achaean chieftains; Homer does not say παῖδες Τρώων, “sons of the Trojans”; the verse is superfluous; and Odysseus would scarcely have represented Athena as the author of so disastrous a decision (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.547). A scholium adds that the story comes from the Cyclic poets (Merry's critical note on 11.547). In the *Little Iliad*, as Merry reports it from the scholia on Aristophanes' *Knights*, spies sent to the walls of Troy heard two girls dispute which hero was the braver, and the one “whose mind Athena had influenced” gave the prize to Odysseus (Merry on 11.547; see the translation's note on 11.547). Odysseus himself, here, lays the blame on Zeus (11.559–560; see [[zeus]]).

**With Heracles.** At the end of his story the phantom of Heracles says that he brought the hound up out of Hades, “and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena” (11.626; see [[heracles]], [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[hermes]] and [[gray-eyed]]). Athena tells the same in the Iliad, angry that Zeus has forgotten it: “nor does he remember at all how very often I saved his son / when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him”, and if she had known what she knows now, when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades to bring the hound, “he would never have escaped the sheer streams of the water of Styx” (*Iliad* 8.362–369; see [[eurystheus]] and [[styx]]).
