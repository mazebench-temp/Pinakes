summary: Tyro is the first of the heroines whom Persephone sends to the blood: she tells Odysseus her parentage, her love for the river Enipeus, Poseidon's embrace in the river's likeness and his words to her, and her sons, Pelias and Neleus by the god, Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon by Cretheus (11.235–259).
passages:
  - 11.235-259 | Tyro
---

**The first of the heroines.** When Anticleia has gone, “the women came, for noble Persephone urged them on, / all who had been the wives and daughters of chieftains” (11.226–227; see [[persephone]]), and Odysseus, with his sword drawn, lets them drink one at a time, “and each / declared her own parentage” (11.233–234; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). “There the first I saw was Tyro, daughter of a noble father, / who said that she was the offspring of noble Salmoneus, / and said she was the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus” (11.235–237; see [[salmoneus]], [[cretheus]] and [[daughter-of-a-noble-father]]). W. W. Merry notes that the catalogue that follows draws on the legends of the Minyans and the Thebans, and accepts Lauer's argument for its Boeotian origin, in a country fond of lists of noble women like Hesiod's *Catalogue* (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.225; see [[hesiod]]). Antinous had named her first of the women of old in Book 2 (2.120).

**The river and the god.** “She fell in love with a river, the divine Enipeus” (11.238; see [[enipeus]]), and Poseidon, in the river's likeness, lay with her at its mouth while the wave, “high as a mountain, / arched over”, hid them both (11.241–244; see [[poseidon]]). “And he loosed her maiden girdle, and shed sleep upon her” (11.245; see [[girdle]]), a verse that the ancient critics rejected (see the translation's note on 11.245).

{{quote:c2c40615-95f1-563a-bcf8-97d8c6cd1e80}}

χαῖρε is meant as a blessing, “Be happy in this love”, Merry explains, and ἴσχεο is “refrain thyself” from speaking (on 11.248, 11.251); for “not in vain are the embraces” see the translation's note on 11.249. The god names himself only as he leaves, and he “plunged beneath the billowing sea” (11.253).

**Her sons.** “And she conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus, / who became mighty attendants of great Zeus, / both of them”: θεράποντε Διός are “kings”, as the θεράποντες of Ares are warriors and those of the Muses singers (Merry on 11.255). Pelias ruled in Iolcus and Neleus in sandy Pylos (11.254–257; see [[pelias]], [[neleus]], [[iolcus]] and [[pylos]]), and to Cretheus “the queen among women” bore Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon (11.258–259; see [[aeson]], [[pheres]] and [[amythaon]]). The book says nothing of the twins' exposure, which the tragedians told (see [[tyro-and-her-sons]]).
