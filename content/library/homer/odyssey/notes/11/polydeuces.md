---
summary: Polydeuces, “good with his fists”, is the second of Leda's two sons, named in the line of *Iliad* 3.237; with Castor he is held fast by the life-giving earth though alive, and the two live and are dead on alternate days, with honor like the gods (11.298–304).
passages:
  - 11.298-304 | Leda and her sons
---

“Who bore to Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons, / Castor, tamer of horses, and Polydeuces, good with his fists, / both of whom the life-giving earth holds fast, alive though they are” (11.299–301; see [[leda]], [[tyndareus]], [[castor]] and [[good-with-his-fists]]). The verse of the names is Helen's in the Iliad (*Iliad* 3.237), and the verse after it turns the Iliad's burial into a strange life beneath the earth: “now they live on alternate days, and now again / they are dead; and they have been allotted honor like the gods” (11.303–304; see [[immortality]]). The Book 11 note on [[castor]] gives W. W. Merry's notes on the passage and on the later myth. Homer makes Leda bear both sons to Tyndareus (11.299), and says nothing of the later story in which Polydeuces, the son of Zeus, chose to share his immortality with his mortal brother (see [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]] and the general article). Merry takes his name to mean “very comely” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.300, referring to his note on ἀδευκής at 4.489).
