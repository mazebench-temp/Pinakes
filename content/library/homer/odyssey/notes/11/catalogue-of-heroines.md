summary: Fourteen women in a hundred and eight verses, from Persephone's sending to Odysseus' breaking off (11.225–332). Odysseus keeps them from the blood with his sword and lets them drink one by one; he tells their stories in his own voice, and only Poseidon, in Tyro's story, speaks in his own words.

**The gathering.** “So we two were exchanging words, and the women / came, for noble Persephone urged them on, / all who had been the wives and daughters of chieftains” (11.225–227; see [[persephone]] and [[noble]]). They crowd about the blood, and Odysseus plans “how I might question each one” (11.228–229): ὅπως points not to the form of the questions but to the means of putting them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.229). His plan is the sword, held drawn so that they cannot all drink at once; they come up προμνηστῖναι, “one after another”, a word found again only when Odysseus tells the two herdsmen to come into the hall one after another, not all together (21.230, Greek only), and each declares her own descent (11.230–234; Merry on 11.233; see the translation's key-word row for προμνηστῖνοι and [[sword]]).

**Told in his voice.** Odysseus says that he questioned them all (11.234), but he gives no questions and almost no answers: the women's words show only in the verbs, what Tyro “said”, what Antiope “claimed”, what Iphimedeia “declared” (11.236–237, 11.261, 11.306). The one speech in the catalogue is the god's, Poseidon's words to Tyro, “Rejoice in this love, lady”, ending with his name (11.248–252; see [[tyro-and-her-sons]]). Four of the women bore their sons to gods, Tyro and Iphimedeia to Poseidon, Antiope and Alcmene to Zeus (11.241–254, 11.260–268, 11.305–307; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]] and [[poseidon]]); the others are named for a husband or a father, and two for a death that a god sent, Ariadne killed by Artemis and the sons of Iphimedeia by Apollo (11.318–325; see [[artemis]] and [[apollo]]).

**The doubts.** Merry, who thought the list Boeotian in origin (on 11.225), doubted three parts of it: the passage of Leda, which breaks the run of Boeotian and Thessalian legends and uses τήν before the husband's name as an article (on 11.298, 11.304); the verses of Ossa and Pelion (on 11.315–316); and the Attic women, Phaedra, Procris and Ariadne (on 11.321; see the translation's notes on 11.298–304, 11.315–316 and 11.321–325, and [[leda]], [[the-aloadae-threaten-olympus]] and [[ariadne]]).

**Breaking off.** “But all of them I could not tell or name, / all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw; / for before that even the immortal night would wane” (11.328–330). This is the only place where Odysseus breaks off his tale, and he does it in the middle of the dead; the Phaeacians sit spellbound, and when Alcinous asks him to go on he asks not for more women but for the men who fell at Troy (11.333–334, 11.370–372; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). The women are not seen again: when the tale resumes, Persephone scatters “the souls of the womenfolk” and the soul of Agamemnon comes (11.385–387; see [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]).
