**Baleful Minos.** Among the heroines Odysseus sees Ariadne, “the daughter of baleful Minos, whom Theseus once / was bringing from Crete to the hill of sacred Athens” (11.321–323; see [[ariadne]], [[theseus]] and [[crete]]). ὀλοόφρων, “baleful”, is the word for Atlas and Aeetes (1.52, 10.137; see [[oloophron]]). W. W. Merry suggests that it remembers the cruel tribute Minos laid on Athens, and he takes the five verses, which belong to Attic legend and break the run of Thessalian and Boeotian heroines, for a later interpolation, perhaps of the time of the Peisistratean recension (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.321–322; see the translation's note on 11.321–325).

**The judge.** After Ajax has turned away into Erebus, Odysseus waits to see the souls of the others:

{{quote:c7517a82-7231-5f03-aa20-010af3fcffb8}}

He is Διὸς ἀγλαὸς υἱός, “the splendid son of Zeus” (11.568; see [[zeus]] and [[splendid]]), and his golden scepter is the seer's (11.91; see [[scepter]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). θεμιστεύοντα takes the dative here, “giving the law”, *ius dicentem*; of the Cyclopes, where the sense is “being master of”, it takes the genitive, “each one gives the law / to his children and his wives” (9.114–115; Merry on 11.569; see [[themis]]). Merry pictures the scene: Minos is on his seat, and the ghosts sit waiting their turn or stand as each states his case or asks for the judge's decision; ἄνακτα is in apposition to μιν, “they all around him, the king, were questioning him of their rights”, for εἴροντο means “asked” and not “stated their cases” (Merry on 11.569–570; see [[anax]]). The judgments are given “throughout the wide-gated house of Hades” (11.571; see [[wide-gated]] and [[hades]]).

**Not the judge of the dead.** Merry warns against reading the later legend into the verses. Minos is not shown trying and sentencing the dead, as he does in Virgil (*Aeneid* 6.431–433); he “only presents a shadowy counterpart of himself as he was when alive. Lawgiver, king, and judge in the flesh, he is also lawgiver, king, and judge in the shades”, and only in a later mythology was his brother Rhadamanthys, whom the Odyssey places in Elysium (4.564), given him as an assessor, with Aeacus or Sarpedon (Merry on 11.568; see the translation's note on 11.568, [[rhadamanthus]] and [[elysium]]). So Orion goes on hunting among the dead (Merry on 11.572; see [[orion]]).

**Where the suspected lines begin.** With Minos the tale changes its manner. Odysseus has been sitting by the pit and questioning the souls as they come to the blood, but now he tells what is going on in the depths of Erebus, as an eyewitness would. A scholiast marks the lines from here to 627 as spurious; Merry thinks the insertion begins at 565, where ὅμως, “notwithstanding”, is suspect, and finds in the scene a view of the punishment of the dead later than Homer. With La Roche he looks for an Attic source, and recalls the scholiast's report that 604 was the work of Onomacritus, one of the helpers of Peisistratus in his recension of Homer (Merry on 11.565; see the translation's note on 11.565–567 and [[pisistratus|Peisistratus]]). Herodotus tells how Onomacritus, an Athenian collector and arranger of the oracles of Musaeus, was caught by Lasus of Hermione inserting an oracle of his own into them, and was banished by Hipparchus, the son of Peisistratus (Herodotus 7.6.3; see [[herodotus]]). A scholiast found the same difficulty in Tityus: if he lies there, how was he seen? (Merry on 11.581; see [[tityus]]).

**How Odysseus knew him.** Proclus asks how the souls in Hades recognize one another, and answers from this book: Odysseus knew the souls of Achilles and Ajax, whom he had seen alive, and Minos, whom he knew by report; perhaps those in Hades told him about Minos and about Tantalus, Tityus and Sisyphus, unless their stories were already common talk, revealed by the gods in oracles, so that when he saw them he recognized at once what report had handed down (*On the Republic* II 166 Kroll, a plain rendering; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**Plato and after.** In the myth of the *Gorgias* Zeus makes three of his sons judges of the dead, Minos and Rhadamanthys from Asia and Aeacus from Europe, to judge in the meadow at the crossroads from which the roads go to the Isles of the Blessed and to Tartarus, and gives Minos the final decision when the other two are in doubt (*Gorgias* 523e–524a). Rhadamanthys and Aeacus judge holding a staff, “but Minos sits overseeing, alone holding a golden scepter, as Homer's Odysseus says he saw him: ‘holding a golden scepter, giving the law to the dead’” (*Gorgias* 526c–d, plain renderings; see [[plato]], [[aeacus]] and [[tartarus]]). In the *Apology* Socrates looks forward to finding in Hades “the true judges, who are said to judge there, Minos and Rhadamanthys and Aeacus and Triptolemus” (*Apology* 41a; see [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]). The *Minos*, transmitted with Plato's dialogues, counts the verse as Homer's highest praise of him: “in the Nekyia of the Odyssey he has made Minos judging, holding a golden scepter, not Rhadamanthys” (*Minos* 319d; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]). Proclus repeats the sentence, and holds that Plato took from Homer's Nekyia the figure of Minos judging with a golden scepter and overseeing the judgment of the others (*On the Republic* I 156, 168 Kroll). Strabo, who set the Elysian plain in the far west, says that the mention of Rhadamanthys points to a place near Minos, and quotes 11.568–569 (*Geography* 3.2.13; see [[strabo]] and [[elysium-and-the-isles-of-the-blessed]]). Virgil's Minos is the judge of later legend, who shakes the urn, calls the silent to council and learns their lives and their crimes (*Aeneid* 6.432–433, a plain rendering; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]).
