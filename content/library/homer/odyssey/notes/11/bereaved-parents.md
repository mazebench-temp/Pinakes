---
summary: The bereaved parents of the book are Odysseus' own. His mother died of longing for him (11.197–203), his father lies grieving on the slope of his vineyard (11.187–196), and Achilles, among the dead, fears for his father Peleus, old and without the son who could defend him (11.494–503).
refs: [11.187-203, 11.494-503]
---

**Laertes and Anticleia.** Here the parents lose a son who is alive. Laertes no longer comes down to the city; in winter he sleeps among the slaves in the dust by the fire, in summer on beds of leaves on the slope of his vineyard, “and nurses a great sorrow in his heart, / longing for your homecoming, and hard old age comes upon him besides” (11.187–196; see [[laertes]], [[penthos]] and [[geron]]). Anticleia did not die of sickness or of Artemis' arrows: “longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus, / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (11.197–203; see [[anticleia]] and [[pothe]]).

**Peleus.** The Iliad's pattern is turned around: the dead son grieves for the father. Achilles asks whether Peleus still has honor among the Myrmidons, or whether they dishonor him because old age holds him fast, “for I am not there to help him under the rays of the sun”, and wishes he could come back even for a little while (11.494–503; see [[peleus]], [[achilles]] and [[time]]); Odysseus has no news of him (11.505).
