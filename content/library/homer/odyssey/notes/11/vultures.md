# γῦπε (11.578), the dual "two vultures", found only here in Homer.

**Two vultures.** γῦπε δέ μιν ἑκάτερθε παρημένω ἧπαρ ἔκειρον, / δέρτρον ἔσω δύνοντες, “and two vultures, sitting one on either side, were tearing his liver, / plunging within the caul” (11.578–579; see [[tityus]]). The noun and its participle are duals, a pair; the imperfect ἔκειρον is a tearing that goes on. δέρτρον, from δέρω, “flay”, is properly any skin or membrane, and so the caul, the membrane that wraps the bowels, later called ἐπίπλοον; W. W. Merry reports another explanation in Apollonius' lexicon, “the mouth of the vulture”, which would require δύνοντες to be rendered “plunging” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.579; see the translation's row πέλεθρα; δέρτρον). Tityus “could not ward them off with his hands” (11.579), and the reason is given in the next verse: “for he had dragged off Leto, the glorious wife of Zeus, / as she went to Pytho through Panopeus with its lovely dancing floors” (11.580–581; see [[leto]], [[pytho]] and [[panopeus]]).

**Eaters of the dead.** In the Iliad the vulture is a threat spoken over the living: vultures will eat the tender flesh of the oath-breakers, Hector tells Patroclus that the vultures will eat him, and Priam pictures Achilles dead and eaten by dogs and vultures (*Iliad* 4.237, 16.836, 22.42; see the entry and [[birds]]); the dead of the battle lie on the plain “far dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (*Iliad* 11.162). The Odyssey's suitors threaten the stranger with them (22.30). Here the bird that eats the unburied dead has become the punishment of one of the dead, and feeds on his liver. Merry compares Ovid's Tityus, who offered his entrails to be torn, stretched over nine acres (*Metamorphoses* 4.457–458), and Virgil's (on 11.579), whose single huge vulture with its hooked beak crops the undying liver and the entrails that are fruitful for punishment, searching them for its meal and dwelling deep in his breast, and no rest is given to the fibers, born again (*Aeneid* 6.595–600; see [[virgil]]).
