---
summary: The dead are phantoms in this book: Elpenor is “the phantom of my companion” (11.83); Odysseus asks whether his mother is “some phantom that noble Persephone / has urged on toward me” (11.213–214); Achilles calls the dead “the phantoms of dead mortals” (11.476); and of Heracles Odysseus sees only “his phantom”, while he himself feasts among the gods (11.601–604).
---

**Phantom and soul.** εἴδωλον is best rendered “phantom”. Merry explains it as used almost in the same way as ψυχή, of the immaterial ghost that remains when the body is dead and the θυμός put out, but with more in it: the idea of something unreal and illusive, and of a visible though insubstantial copy of the person it represents (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.213; see [[psyche]] and the Odyssey translation's table of key words, εἴδωλον). So Elpenor's soul, speaking at the pit, is “the phantom of my companion” (11.83; see [[elpenor]]), and the dead, in Achilles' words, “dwell without sense, the phantoms of dead mortals” (βροτῶν εἴδωλα καμόντων, 11.475–476; see [[achilles]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).

**A trick of Persephone?** When his mother slips from his arms Odysseus suspects a phantom of the Iliad's kind, made by a god: “Or is this some phantom that noble Persephone / has urged on toward me, so that I may groan and mourn still more?” (11.213–214; see [[persephone]]). His mother answers that it is no deceit, but the way of mortals when they die (11.216–222; see [[the-way-of-mortals]] and [[anticleia]]).

**Heracles.** For Merry the strongest passage for the word's sense of an unreal image is the last one, where εἴδωλον is set against αὐτός: Odysseus caught sight of mighty Heracles, “his phantom; but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts” (11.601–603; Merry on 11.213; see [[heracles]] and [[immortality]]). The three verses that make the division were suspected in antiquity and attributed to Onomacritus, and Aristonicus objected to the division of the man into εἴδωλον, body and soul; Merry would cut only those verses and keep the rest of the scene (Merry on 11.601; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.602–604).
