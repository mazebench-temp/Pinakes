summary: The dead tell the truth when they have drunk the blood (11.96, 11.146–149); Anticleia assures her son that Persephone is not deceiving him (11.217); Agamemnon counsels him to tell his wife one part and hide another (11.441–443); and Alcinous declares that he is no deceiver, “fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see” (11.363–368).
refs: [11.96, 11.137, 11.148, 11.217, 11.363-368, 11.441-443, 11.507]
---

**The truth of the dead.** At the pit truth is a matter of the blood. Teiresias drinks “so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (νημερτέα εἴπω, 11.96), and ends, “This I tell you truly” (11.137); whichever of the dead Odysseus lets come near it “will tell you truly” (11.148; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). Odysseus fears a deception from the queen of the dead, a phantom sent to grieve him, and his mother answers that “it is not that Persephone, the daughter of Zeus, deceives you” (ἀπαφίσκει, 11.213–218; see [[eidolon]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]). He in turn will tell Achilles “the whole truth” of his son (11.507).

**Counsel to hide.** Agamemnon's soul, deceived by his wife, counsels deceit: “do not declare to her every word that you know well, / but tell her one part, and let another part be hidden”, and bring the ship to shore secretly (11.441–443, 11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

**The truth of the tale.** Alcinous gives his verdict on the teller: not “a deceiver and a cheat”, like those who fashion lies about what no one can see, but a man with “beauty of words” and good wits, who has told his tale skillfully, as a singer does (11.363–368; see [[eperopeuo]], [[pseudos]] and [[singers]]). Merry hears in it a gentle reminder of the doubtful truth of travelers' tales, told of a land of the dead where no hearer could test them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.366).
