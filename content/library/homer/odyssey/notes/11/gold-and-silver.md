summary: The gold of the book belongs to the dead and the gods: the golden scepters of Teiresias and Minos (11.91, 11.569), Hera of the golden sandals (11.604), and the golden baldric of Heracles' phantom (11.610); Odysseus' sword is silver-studded (11.97), and Eriphyle took precious gold as the price of her husband (11.327).
# χρύσεος (11.610), the golden strap, first met in the Odyssey here (the Iliad's χρύσεος is in its notes); it applies in
# the Odyssey only.
grc: [χρύσεος]
---

**Scepters and sandals.** Teiresias comes to the blood “holding a golden scepter” (11.91), and so does Minos as he gives the law to the dead (11.569; see [[scepter]], [[teiresias]] and [[minos]]). Hera is χρυσοπέδιλος, “of the golden sandals” (11.604; see [[of-the-golden-sandals]] and [[hera]]), and the phantom of Heracles wears a terrible baldric, χρύσεος … τελαμών, “a golden strap, on which wondrous works were wrought” (11.609–610; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]]).

**Silver and gold among men.** Odysseus' sword is ἀργυρόηλον, “silver-studded” (11.97; see [[silver-studded]] and [[sword]]). The one gift of gold among the heroines is a bribe: Eriphyle, “who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (11.327; see [[eriphyle]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).
