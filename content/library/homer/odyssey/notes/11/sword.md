summary: Odysseus draws his sword to dig the pit, and sits with it drawn to keep the dead from the blood until Teiresias has drunk; he draws it again to let the heroines drink one at a time (11.24–25, 11.48–50, 11.82, 11.95–98, 11.231–232). Agamemnon dies “with the sword through me” (11.424), and Neoptolemus in the horse keeps feeling for the hilt of his (11.531).

**The rite of the sword.** Circe had bidden him sit at the pit with his sword drawn (10.535–537), and he does all she said. First the sword is a tool: “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and dug a pit about a cubit's length this way and that” (11.24–25; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). Then it is a guard: “and I myself drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and sat there, and did not let the strengthless heads of the dead / come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias” (11.48–50; see [[the-strengthless-heads]]), and he and Elpenor talk across it, “I on one side, holding my sword over the blood” (11.82; see [[elpenor]]). Teiresias, who has come with his golden scepter, asks him to put it away: “But draw back from the pit, and hold away your sharp sword, / so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly”, and Odysseus “thrust my silver-studded sword / into its sheath” (11.95–98; see [[teiresias]], [[silver-studded]] and [[sheath]]). With the heroines it is drawn again, now to keep order: “drawing the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh, / I would not let them all drink the dark blood at once” (11.231–232; see [[long-edged]]). The verse of the drawn sword, Achilles' verse against Agamemnon (*Iliad* 1.190), comes twice at the pit (11.24, 11.48), as four times in Book 10, and its variant with the long-edged sword a third time (11.231, as 10.439 and *Iliad* 16.473).

**Agamemnon's death.** In his account of the feast in Aegisthus' house, Agamemnon tells how, as Cassandra was killed beside him, “I, raising my hands, let them fall to the ground / as I lay dying with the sword through me” (11.423–424; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The English follows the reading that W. W. Merry quotes from Königinhoff through Seiler: he raised his hands to protect Cassandra, or to plead for her, but run through by the sword and spent, he let them fall; the common rendering, “I tried to throw my hands about the sword”, gives χεῖρας βάλλον a sense that the plural hardly bears, and Merry suggests that the φάσγανον may be Aegisthus' sword, clutched at in the death agony (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.423; see the translation's note on 11.423–424).

**Neoptolemus in the horse.** While the other leaders in the wooden horse wept and trembled, Neoptolemus “kept supplicating me again and again / to let him out of the horse, and he kept feeling for the hilt of his sword / and his spear heavy with bronze” (11.530–532; see [[neoptolemus]], [[the-wooden-horse]] and [[spear]]).
