# "like dark night" (11.606), the English of ἐρεμνῇ νυκτὶ ἐοικώς; the entry's "like the night" is the Iliad's.

**Dark night.** ὁ δʼ ἐρεμνῇ νυκτὶ ἐοικώς, “and he was like dark night” (11.606; see [[heracles]]). The words are the Iliad's first simile with an epithet added: Apollo strode down from Olympus with his bow and his close-covered quiver on his shoulders, the arrows clattering, ἔκλαγξαν, as he moved, “and he came on like the night”, ὃ δʼ ἤϊε νυκτὶ ἐοικώς; “and terrible was the twang of the silver bow” (*Iliad* 1.44–49; see [[apollo]] and the entry). Both are archers, and the Odyssey's archer is also in the middle of a clamor: around him rose “a clamor of the dead, as of birds” (11.605; see [[a-clamor-as-of-birds]]), as the κλαγγή of Apollo's bow rang in the Iliad. ἐρεμνός, “dark”, is said in the Iliad of the aegis and of the dark storm to which the Lycian leaders, climbing the battlements, and Ares, shouting, are likened (*Iliad* 4.167, 12.375–376, 20.51), and in the Odyssey of the earth beneath which the suitors went down (24.106). W. W. Merry takes νυκτὶ ἐοικώς as a stern, lowering look, “not unlike to our vernacular ‘looking as black as thunder’”, and recalls Hector, νυκτὶ θοῇ ἀτάλαντος ὑπώπια, “his face like swift night” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.606; *Iliad* 12.463).

**The figure.** The night opens a picture closed by another likeness: “holding his naked bow, with an arrow on the string, / glancing terribly around, like one ever about to shoot” (11.607–608; see [[bow]] and [[like-one-about-to-shoot]]). The four participles have no finite verb, and the English supplies “was” in the first verse; Merry suspects a deliberate art in the abrupt clauses, which give the passage itself the attitude of uneasy expectation (on 11.608; see the translation's note on 11.606–608). Below the dark figure shines the golden baldric (11.609–614; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]]).
