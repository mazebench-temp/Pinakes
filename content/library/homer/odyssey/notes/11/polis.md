summary: The Cimmerians have “the land and the city” at the limits of Oceanus (11.14); Laertes “does not come down to the city” (11.188); Odysseus asks whether Agamemnon died “fighting for a city and its women” (11.403); and Neoptolemus counseled “about the city of Troy” and came away unharmed when “we had sacked the steep city of Priam” (11.510, 11.533).
# πτόλιος (11.403; again 6.294).
grc: [πτόλιος]
en: [city]
---

The city of the Cimmerians is a phrase with little behind it: “the land and the city of the Cimmerian men, / wrapped in mist and cloud” (δῆμός τε πόλις τε, 11.14–15). The words give a sort of reality to the account, Merry observes, though it is hard to imagine social life going on in a country of perpetual darkness (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.15; see [[cimmerians]] and the Odyssey translation's note on 11.14). The other cities of the book are those of the living and of the war: Laertes no longer comes down from his vineyard to the city (11.187–188; see [[laertes]]); Odysseus asks whether Agamemnon was killed “fighting for a city and its women”, where Merry prefers “fighting to take a city” (11.403; Merry on 11.403; see [[raiding]]); and Troy is “the city of Troy”, πόλιν Τροίην, as the Iliad says πόλιν Καλυδῶνα (11.510; Merry on 11.510), and “the steep city of Priam” that the Achaeans sacked (11.533; see [[troy]]).
