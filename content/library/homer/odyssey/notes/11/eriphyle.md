---
summary: “Hateful Eriphyle, / who took precious gold as the price of her own husband”, is the last heroine whom Odysseus names, and the only one in the catalogue who betrayed her husband (11.326–327).
passages:
  - 11.326-327 | Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle
---

{{quote:6b683ee6-7fab-5c14-a652-aef91866ba79}}

**Hateful.** Maera and Clymene have only their names; Eriphyle has an epithet and a crime (see [[maera-the-heroine]] and [[clymene-the-heroine]]). στυγερή, “hateful” (see [[hateful]]), is said of a mortal woman again in the Odyssey only of Clytemnestra, “his hateful mother”, for whom Orestes gave the Argives a funeral feast when he had killed Aegisthus (3.309–310; see [[clytemnestra]]); in the Iliad Helen says it of herself, when she asks Aphrodite whether Menelaus “wants to take me, hateful as I am, home with him” (*Iliad* 3.404). Of all the heroines Eriphyle alone is named for a betrayal: she sold her husband.

**The price of her husband.** ἣ χρυσὸν φίλου ἀνδρὸς ἐδέξατο τιμήεντα, “who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (11.327; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[amphiaraus]]). φίλου ἀνδρός is a genitive of price, as W. W. Merry notes, comparing the exchange of Glaucus' armor, “gold for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine” (*Iliad* 6.236), Achilles' release of the sons of Priam “for a ransom” (*Iliad* 11.106) and Priam's “we will ransom them with bronze and gold” (*Iliad* 22.50) (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.327). The poem does not name the gold a necklace, nor the man who gave it, nor say what was bought: Merry supplies the later story, in which Polynices bribed her with a golden necklace to persuade Amphiaraus to march against Thebes, where he knew he would die, and Zeus, when he was about to be killed, split the earth with his thunderbolt and swallowed him with his chariot and horses (on 11.326; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]] and [[polynices]]). The Odyssey says in a later book that Amphiaraus perished at Thebes “for the sake of gifts to a woman”, the words this book uses of the Ceteians killed with Eurypylus (15.247; 11.521; see [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).

**The end of the catalogue.** With Eriphyle Odysseus breaks off the catalogue (see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]): “But all of them I could not tell or name, / all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw; / for before that even the immortal night would wane” (11.328–330). Merry hears in Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle a return to Boeotian legend, Eriphyle's story being bound up with the war against Thebes (on 11.326). The next women of the book are those of Agamemnon's story, his wife and Cassandra, and his warning against wives (11.405–456; see [[clytemnestra]]).
