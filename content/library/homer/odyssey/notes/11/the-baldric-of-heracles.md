**The baldric.** It is worn by the phantom, while Heracles himself feasts with the gods (11.601–604; see [[heracles]] and [[eidolon]]), and it is the last thing Odysseus describes before Heracles speaks. W. W. Merry translates: “And his terrible baldric round about his breast was a strap of gold, whereon were wrought marvellous devices, bears and wild boars and bright-eyed lions, and battles and fights, and bloodshed and slayings of men”; ἀμφὶ περί is “round about” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.609). χαροποί, “with flashing eyes”, Merry would rather refer to a root meaning “glow” than to χαρά, “joy” (on 11.611; see the translation's row ἀορτήρ, τελαμών; χαροπός). θέσκελα ἔργα, “wondrous works”, are the works of a craftsman here, as in Alcinous' request for “wondrous doings” in the tale they are the deeds of heroes (11.374).

**The wish.** “May he who fashioned it never fashion another work, / he who laid up that strap in his craft” (11.613–614). Merry gives two readings: praise of a masterpiece that a second attempt could only spoil, or, as he prefers, a naive expression of terror at the ghastliness of the images, “Would that he had never made it, but now that he has made it may he never make anything else like it!”, in keeping with the participle after μή that stands for a separate wish (on 11.613; see the translation's note on 11.611, 11.613).
