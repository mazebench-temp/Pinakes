summary: The Phaeacians are the audience of the tale. When Odysseus breaks off, they sit hushed, “held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (11.333–334); Arete asks them how the stranger seems to them and bids them not cut short their gifts (11.336–341), the old Echeneus approves (11.342–346), and Alcinous, “lord over the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar”, promises the gifts and the escort (11.347–353).

**The spell.** Odysseus ends the first half of his tale with the women he saw among the dead and leaves his escort home to “the care of the gods and of you” (11.330–332; see [[pompe]]). The Phaeacians fall silent, κηληθμῷ δʼ ἔσχοντο, “held by a spell” (11.333–334; see [[thelgo]] and [[silence]]): κηληθμός is the glamour of his story, W. W. Merry explains: the scholium calls it the stillness that comes with pleasure and delight, and Quintilian seems to mean the same when he renders it *intentionis silentium*, the silence of rapt attention (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.334). The halls are σκιόεντα, “shadowy”, for it is night (see [[shadowy-mountains]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The queen's appeal.** Arete speaks first: “Phaeacians, how does this man seem to you to be / in looks and stature and the well-balanced mind within?” (11.336–337; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). She seems to find her answer in their approving looks, Merry suggests, and goes on: the stranger is her guest, since he made his first appeal to her, but each of them has a share in the honor of entertaining him, and they must not hurry to send him away or cut short their gifts to one in such need (on 11.338–340; see [[xenia]] and [[doron]]). The gifts are those already given in the eighth book, and κολούετε, “cut short”, does not mean taking back what was promised, but giving fewer than they would if he stayed a little longer (Merry on 11.339–340). ἕκαστος δʼ ἔμμορε τιμῆς, “each of you has his share of honor”, is the phrase in which Poseidon describes the division of the world among the three sons of Cronus (*Iliad* 15.189; Merry on 11.338).

**The old man and the king.** Echeneus, “one of the older Phaeacian men”, tells them that the queen speaks not wide of the mark, but that word and deed depend on Alcinous (11.342–346; see [[echeneus]]); the verse that introduces him is wanting in some of the best manuscripts (Merry's critical note on 11.343). Alcinous answers that it shall be so, “as surely as I / live and am lord over the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (11.348–349; see [[alcinous]] and [[lovers-of-the-oar]]), and that the escort “will be the concern of men, / of all of them, and mine most of all” (11.352–353). Then he praises the tale, told “skillfully, as a singer does”, and asks for more (11.362–376).
