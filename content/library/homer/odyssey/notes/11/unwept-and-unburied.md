---
summary: Odysseus tells that Elpenor's body was left in the hall of Circe “unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.53–54), and the soul itself begs him: “Do not go away and leave me behind unwept, unburied” (11.72).
---

The narrator's words come first, as the reason why the soul of Elpenor is the first to come to the pit: “for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth” (11.52; [[wide-wayed-earth]]). The dead man then uses them of himself, in the plea that asks for fire with his armor, a mound “on the shore of the gray sea,” and his oar fixed on the tomb (11.72–78; [[elpenor]], [[gray-sea]], [[tomb]]). If he is left so, he warns, he may become “a cause of the gods' wrath” against Odysseus: θεῶν μήνιμα, the words of the dying Hector when Achilles refuses to give his body back (*Iliad* 22.358). Odysseus promises, “All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you” (11.80), and keeps his word on his return to Aeaea: the companions bring the body from Circe's house, burn it with its armor, heap a tomb, drag a pillar onto it and fix the well-fitted oar on its top (12.9–15, a plain rendering; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).

The verses that state the death so plainly (11.52–54) were rejected by Callistratus, since Odysseus goes on to ask the soul how it came there; the scholia, as Merry reports them, offer the answer that the companions had not noticed the death, being busy with other things (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 11.52–54; see the translation's note on 11.51–83 and [[misty]]).
