**The thirst.** He stands “in a lake; and the water came up to his chin” (11.583), and “he strained in his thirst” (στεῦτο δὲ διψάων, 11.584). W. W. Merry rejects the old gloss of στεῦτο as “stood”, since στεῦμαι is never used in Homer of standing, and follows Aristarchus, for whom the word marks an effort of the mind: “eagerly essayed in his thirst” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.584; see the translation's note on 11.584). The water “was swallowed up and vanished” (ἀναβροχέν, “swallowed up”: Merry on 11.586), and about his feet “the black earth appeared, and a god dried it up” (11.587; see [[black-earth]] and [[daimon]]).

**The fruit.** The trees let their fruit hang “over his head” (κατὰ κρῆθεν, the reading of Aristarchus, which the scholiast rightly explains by κατὰ κεφαλῆς, “over the head”; Bekker wrote κατʼ ἄκρηθεν: Merry on 11.588 and his critical note; see [[with-high-foliage]] and the translation's note on 11.586–588). They are the trees of the garden of Alcinous, in the same two verses: “pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, / and sweet figs and flourishing olives” (11.589–590 = 7.115–116; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). In Scheria the West Wind, blowing, “brings some to growth and ripens others”, and the fruit never fails, winter or summer (7.117–119); here “the wind would fling them toward the shadowy clouds” whenever the old man reaches for them (11.591–592; see [[the-winds]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]). On ἐπὶ χερσὶ μάσασθαι, “to grasp them with his hands”, see the translation's note on 11.591. The verbs of the scene are iteratives, ἀπολέσκετο, φάνεσκε, καταζήνασκε, ῥίπτασκε (on their formation, Merry on 11.587): it happens every time he tries.

**No crime.** Tityus' offense is told (11.580–581; see [[tityus]]); of Tantalus the poem says only that he is “the old man” (11.585, 11.591), and the verse that brings him on, “And I saw Tantalus too, suffering bitter pains” (11.582), brings on Sisyphus too (11.593; see [[sisyphus]]). His crime, his family and the rock over his head come from later poets (see the entry).

**Suspected verses.** The scholiast on Pindar's first Olympian ode, quoting 11.583–584 and the lines that follow, adds “unless, according to Aristarchus, these verses are spurious” (Merry's critical note on 11.582, citing Buttmann). The whole passage of the judge and the punished falls within the lines that a scholiast marks as spurious, from 568 to 627 (Merry on 11.565; see the translation's note on 11.565–567 and [[minos]]).

**Plato's sophists.** In the *Protagoras* Socrates describes the sophists gathered in the house of Callias in the words of this book. Protagoras walks in the colonnade with a chorus of listeners, whom he charms with his voice like Orpheus; then “‘And after him I caught sight of’, as Homer said, Hippias of Elis”, seated on a chair in the colonnade opposite and giving his rulings on the questions put to him about nature and the heavens; “and Tantalus too I saw, for Prodicus of Ceos was in town”, lying in a room that had been a storeroom, wrapped in many fleeces and blankets (*Protagoras* 314e–315d, plain renderings; see [[protagoras]], [[hippias-of-elis]], [[prodicus]] and [[callias-son-of-hipponicus]]). The first words are those that bring on Heracles (11.601; see [[heracles]]); the second are this verse, recast, καὶ μὲν δὴ καὶ Τάνταλόν γε εἰσεῖδον for καὶ μὴν Τάνταλον εἰσεῖδον. Merry cites the passage, with the *Gorgias*, as evidence that the lines were in the poem early (on 11.565).

**Kings punished forever.** In the *Gorgias* Socrates calls Homer to witness that the incurable among the dead, hung up in Hades as examples to the others, are mostly tyrants, kings and potentates: Homer “has made kings and potentates those who are punished in Hades for all time, Tantalus and Sisyphus and Tityus”, and no poet has shown Thersites so punished (*Gorgias* 525c–e, plain renderings; see [[plato-thersites]] and [[plato]]). Proclus, who holds that Plato took his judgments of the dead from Homer's Nekyia, repeats the point in his own words: “for Tityuses and Sisyphuses and Tantaluses, he says, are punished in Homer too” (*On the Republic* I 168–169 Kroll, a plain rendering; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]).
