---
summary: The mother of the book is Anticleia, “my dead mother”, whose soul comes to the pit, drinks the blood, knows her son, answers his questions and slips three times from his arms (11.84–224). Epicaste is “the mother of Oedipus” (11.271), Oedipus suffers what “the Erinyes of a mother” bring to pass (11.280), and Thetis, Achilles' “lady mother”, set his arms as a prize (11.546).
---

**Anticleia.** The soul “of my dead mother” is the second to come, and Odysseus, weeping, keeps her from the blood until Teiresias has spoken (11.84–89, 11.141–144; see [[anticleia]] and [[pity]]). When she has drunk, she knows him and speaks first: “My child, how did you come beneath the misty darkness / while you are alive?” (11.155–156). He calls her “My mother” in his answer and in his cry when she slips from his arms (μῆτερ ἐμή, 11.164, 11.210), and the narrator calls her “my lady mother” (πότνια μήτηρ, 11.180, 11.215). Her answers are the book's account of his house: Penelope's tears, Telemachus' place, Laertes' grief, and her own death of longing (11.181–203; see [[pothe]]), and she sends him back to the light with all that he has learned, “so that you may tell them to your wife hereafter” (11.223–224; see [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]).

**Other mothers.** Epicaste is introduced as “the mother of Oedipus” (11.271), and the sorrows she leaves him are “all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass” (11.280; see [[jocasta]] and [[curses]]). Thetis is Achilles' “lady mother”, who set his arms as the prize between Odysseus and Ajax (11.546; see [[thetis]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).
