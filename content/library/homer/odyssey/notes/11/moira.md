summary: The dead “met their fate” (11.372, 11.389), and Anticleia “met my fate” (11.197); Odysseus accepts the prophecy as what “the gods themselves have spun” (11.139); a “hard fate of a god” fettered the seer of Pylos (11.292); Zeus “set your fate upon you”, Odysseus tells Ajax (11.560); and Heracles knows that Odysseus too drags out “some evil fate” (11.618). μοῖρα is also a share: of honor (11.338) and of the spoils of Troy (11.534).
# "ἐπέκλωσαν θεοὶ αὐτοί" (11.139, only here).
grc: ["ἐπέκλωσαν θεοὶ αὐτοί"]
en: [the gods themselves have spun]
---

**Spun by the gods.** Odysseus receives the prophecy of Teiresias with resignation: “this, I suppose, the gods themselves have spun” (11.139). πού gives the sentence its tone of resignation, and αὐτοί at the end of the verse its emphasis, as if to say “of their own will, without consulting me” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.139; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[fates]]).

**Met their fate.** The formula πότμον ἐπέσπον closes three lives: Anticleia's, “For so it was that I too perished and met my fate” (11.197), those of the companions who followed Odysseus to Ilios “and there met their fate”, of whom Alcinous asks (11.371–372), and those of the men killed with Agamemnon in the house of Aegisthus (11.388–389). Aegisthus contrived “death and fate” for Agamemnon (θάνατόν τε μόρον τε, 11.409; see [[thanatos]]); Zeus, Odysseus tells Ajax, hated the army of the Danaans “and set your fate upon you” (11.559–560), as Helen says that Zeus set an evil fate on her and Paris (*Iliad* 6.357; Merry on 11.559; see [[ajax]]); and Heracles, the son of Zeus who had boundless misery on earth, tells Odysseus that he too must “drag out some evil fate, / such as I bore under the rays of the sun” (κακὸν μόρον ἡγηλάζεις, 11.618–619; see [[heracles]]). Anticleia calls her son κάμμορε, “ill-fated beyond all men” (11.216). The seer of Pylos was held by “a hard fate of a god”, and the bonds and the herdsmen of the next verse say what it was (11.292–293; Merry on 11.293; see [[melampus]]).

**A share.** The older sense is a portion. Each of the Phaeacians “has his share of honor” (ἔμμορε τιμῆς, 11.338; see [[time]]), and Neoptolemus went on board at Troy “with his share and a noble prize”, μοῖρα being his share of the spoil, as in Achilles' complaint, “The same share for the man who stays behind and for one who fights his hardest” (*Iliad* 9.318); the noble prize, Merry notes, was in the *Little Iliad* Andromache, though γέρας need not refer to her (11.534; Merry on 11.534; see [[geras]] and [[neoptolemus]]).
