summary: Horace's fifth satire of the second book goes on from the consultation of this book. Teiresias has foretold that Odysseus will come home late and in evil plight to find the suitors devouring his substance (11.114–117), and Horace's Ulysses, taking him at his word, asks how he may recover his lost fortune; the seer teaches him to hunt for old men's wills, and goes back at the call of Proserpina.

**What Teiresias foretold.** If the companions harm the cattle of the Sun, Teiresias tells Odysseus, “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house, / overbearing men, who are devouring your substance, / wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price. / Yet you will surely take vengeance for their violence when you come” (11.114–118; see [[teiresias]], [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]], [[the-suitors]] and [[bride-price]]).

**Horace's sequel.** The satire opens in the middle of the same conversation, as if Teiresias' answer had just ended: “Answer me this too, Tiresias, besides what you have told me: by what arts and means may I recover my lost fortune? Why do you laugh?” The seer asks whether it is not enough for the man of guile to be carried back to Ithaca and see his ancestral gods, and Ulysses answers: “O you who have never lied to anyone, you see how I come home naked and in want, as you prophesy, and there neither cellar nor flock is untouched by the suitors; and yet birth and worth, without substance, are cheaper than seaweed” (*nudus inopsque domum redeam te vate, neque illic / aut apotheca procis intacta est aut pecus*, Horace, *Satires* 2.5.1–8, plain renderings throughout; see [[horace]]). Ulysses' words are this book's prophecy, the homecoming in evil plight and the substance devoured, taken as a question of money.

**The seer's manner.** Horace keeps the oracle's tone for the hunt for legacies. “I said it and I say it again: hunt cunningly everywhere for old men's wills” (*Satires* 2.5.23–24). When Ulysses asks whether he is mad, or is mocking him with dark prophecy, the seer answers in the manner of an oracle: “O son of Laertes, whatever I say will either come to pass or not, for great Apollo grants me the gift of divining” (*o Laertiade, quidquid dicam, aut erit aut non*, *Satires* 2.5.58–60). Homer's Teiresias ends his prophecy, “This I tell you truly” (11.137), and greets Odysseus by the same patronymic (11.92; see [[son-of-laertes]]). Penelope, whom Teiresias named only as the godlike wife the suitors woo, becomes a stake in the game (*Satires* 2.5.75–83; see [[penelope]]), and the satire ends as the soul's visit in Homer does, with a return to the dead: “But imperious Proserpina drags me back: live and farewell” (*sed me / imperiosa trahit Proserpina: vive valeque*, *Satires* 2.5.109–110). In Homer the soul of Teiresias goes back into the house of Hades when it has told its oracles (11.150–151; see [[persephone]] and [[hades]]).
