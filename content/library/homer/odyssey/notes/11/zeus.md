---
summary: Zeus is named in Book 11 as lover, father and judge of the heroic age. Antiope claimed to have slept in his arms and Alcmene bore him Heracles (11.261, 11.268); Pelias and Neleus became his “mighty attendants” (11.255); the sons of Leda have honor from him beneath the earth (11.302); Persephone, Apollo, Minos, Hebe and Heracles are his children (11.217, 11.318, 11.568, 11.604, 11.620); and at the end of the seer's story “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (11.297). Twice Odysseus lays the misfortunes of the dead on his hatred, of the offspring of Atreus “through the plans of women” (11.436–437), and of the army of the Danaans, when Ajax lost the arms (11.559–560).
---

**The loves of Zeus.** Two of the heroines were loved by Zeus. Antiope “claimed that she had slept even in the arms of Zeus, / and she bore two sons, Amphion and Zethus” (11.261–262; see [[antiope]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). εὔχετο is “claimed”, and Merry reads her boast as a claim to a lover higher than Poseidon himself, comparing Aphrodite's words to Hera, “for you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest of all” (*Iliad* 14.213; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.261). Alcmene bore Heracles “having lain in love in the arms of great Zeus” (11.268; see [[alcmene]] and [[heracles]]), and in the Iliad Zeus himself counts her among his loves, “Alcmene in Thebes, / who bore Heracles, a fierce-hearted son” (*Iliad* 14.323–324). Leto is “the glorious wife of Zeus”, whom Tityus dragged off on her way to Pytho (11.580; see [[leto]] and [[tityus]]).

**His children.** Persephone is “the daughter of Zeus” (11.217; see [[persephone]]), and Hebe is “the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (11.604; see [[hebe]] and [[hera]]). Among the dead Odysseus sees “Minos, the splendid son of Zeus, / holding a golden scepter, giving the law to the dead” (11.568–569; see [[minos]]), whom Zeus names in the Iliad as the son of the daughter of Phoenix (*Iliad* 14.321–322). The phantom of Heracles says, “I was the son of Zeus, son of Cronus, yet I had misery / boundless” (11.620–621). Κρονίονος, “son of Cronus”, is found only here and in Sleep's refusal in the Iliad, “but I would not come near Zeus, the son of Cronus” (*Iliad* 14.247; see [[son-of-cronus]] and [[cronus]]). Achilles said that “not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom, / who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (*Iliad* 18.117–118), and here the phantom of the son of Zeus tells of the labors of his life, while he himself feasts among the immortal gods (11.601–604, 11.617–626; see [[immortality]]). Apollo, unnamed, is “the son of Zeus, whom Leto of the lovely hair bore”, who killed the sons of Aloeus (11.318–320; see [[apollo]]).

**Attendants of Zeus.** Tyro's sons by Poseidon, Pelias and Neleus, “became mighty attendants of great Zeus” (11.255; see [[pelias]] and [[neleus]]). θεράποντε Διός are kings, Merry explains, as the θεράποντες Ἄρηος are warriors and the θεράποντες Μουσάων singers (Merry on 11.255; see [[attendants-of-ares]]): Hesiod calls the singer Μουσάων θεράπων, “a servant of the Muses” (*Theogony* 100). The sons of Leda are held by the earth though alive, and even beneath the earth they have “honor from Zeus”: “they have been allotted honor like the gods” (11.301–304; see [[leda]], [[castor]], [[polydeuces]] and [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]]).

**The plan of Zeus.** The story of the seer who drove off the cattle of Iphiclus ends, “then at last mighty Iphiclus released him, / when he had told all the oracles; and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (11.296–297; see [[melampus]], [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]], [[iphiclus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Διὸς δʼ ἐτελείετο βουλή is the half-verse with which the proem of the Iliad ends (*Iliad* 1.5). Here it closes a year of bonds that began when “a hard fate of a god fettered him” (11.292).

**Far-seeing Zeus has hated.** When Agamemnon has told of his murder, Odysseus answers: “Ah, truly far-seeing Zeus has hated the offspring of Atreus / terribly, through the plans of women, / from of old: for Helen's sake many of us perished, / and against you Clytemnestra was preparing a snare while you were far away” (11.436–439; see [[far-seeing]], [[helen]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[o-popoi]]). Merry explains διὰ βουλάς of the means: Zeus made the intrigues of Helen and of Clytemnestra the means of working out his enmity (Merry on 11.437). Aristophanes rejected 11.435–440 (see the translation's note on 11.435–440). To the angry soul of Ajax Odysseus says the same of the arms of Achilles: “and no one else / is to blame, but Zeus, who hated the army of the Danaan spearmen / terribly, and set your fate upon you” (11.558–560; see [[ajax]], [[the-judgment-of-arms]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Merry writes out the thought, that Zeus was really guilty of it, because in his hatred of the Danaans he laid doom on Ajax, and compares Helen's words, “on whom Zeus has set an evil fate” (*Iliad* 6.357; Merry on 11.559). Agamemnon's excuse in the Iliad has the same shape, “but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness” (*Iliad* 19.86–87).
