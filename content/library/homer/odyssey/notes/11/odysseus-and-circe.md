summary: Circe's part in the book is the journey she prescribed. Her wind carries the ship to the limits of Oceanus (11.6–8), the men go to “the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22), and the rite at the pit is hers, carried out in her verses (11.25–50); Elpenor's body lies in her hall (11.53, 11.62), and his soul knows that the ship will put in at Aeaea again (11.69–70).
# Unnamed: her wind (11.6-8); the place she pointed out (11.22); the rite as she ordained it (11.25-50); the return to
# Aeaea foreseen (11.69-70).
refs: [11.6-8, 11.22, 11.25-50, 11.69-70]
---

**Her wind.** The ship leaves Aeaea with a following wind that Circe sends, “a good companion”, and she is named with her epithets of the tenth book, “Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (11.6–8; 10.136; see [[circe]], [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]). The same three verses send the ship away from Aeaea for the last time (12.148–150, Greek only), and the text here prints εὐπλόκαμος where the other two places read ἐυπλόκαμος (see the translation's note on 11.8).

**Her directions.** The men go along the stream of Oceanus “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22). She had named the shore, the groves of Persephone, the rivers of the dead and the rock where two of them meet (10.508–515); of these the book says nothing more, and gives the Cimmerians instead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.22; see [[cimmerians]] and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). The pit, the libations, the vows, the slaughter and the sword held over the blood are done as she ordained, in her own verses turned to the first person (11.25–50; 10.516–537; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and the translation's note on 10.516–537). She had promised that the seer would tell the way and the measures of the path (10.539–540), and Teiresias tells only part of it (Merry on 11.136; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]); the rest she tells herself when the ship comes back to her island (12.37–141, Greek only).

**Her hall.** Elpenor's body lies in her hall, unwept and unburied (11.53), and his soul tells how he lay down there and forgot the ladder (11.62–64; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). He knows that Odysseus, going back from the house of Hades, “will put in with your well-made ship at the island of Aeaea” (11.69–70; see [[aeaea]]), a knowledge that Merry found unaccountable, since Circe had told it to no one but Odysseus (on 11.51). The ship does return: the companions bury Elpenor, and Circe comes down with bread, meat and wine and greets them as men “twice dying, when other men die once” (12.1–27, a plain rendering).
