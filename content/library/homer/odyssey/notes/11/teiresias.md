summary: The seer for whose sake the journey is made. Odysseus vows him a black sheep and keeps the dead from the blood until he comes; he comes holding a golden scepter, knows Odysseus, and bids him put up his sword so that he may drink and speak truly. He foretells Poseidon's anger, the cattle of the Sun, a late and evil homecoming in another's ship, the suitors and the vengeance, the journey inland with an oar, the sacrifice to Poseidon and a gentle death away from the sea; then he tells how the dead may speak, and goes back into the house of Hades (11.32–33, 11.90–151). Odysseus names him to his mother and to Achilles as the reason he came (11.165, 11.479).

**The vow.** At the pit Odysseus vows, as Circe told him, that “to Teiresias apart I would sacrifice a sheep, to him alone, / all black, that stands out among our flocks” (11.32–33, after 10.524–525; see [[sheep]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]), and he sits with his sword drawn and does not let the dead near the blood, not even his mother, “until I had questioned Teiresias” (11.50, 11.89; see [[odysseus]] and [[anticleia]]).

**The scepter.** “And there came the soul of Theban Teiresias, / holding a golden scepter, and he knew me and spoke to me” (11.90–91; see [[thebes]] and [[scepter]]). ψυχὴ … ἔχων is a construction according to the sense, the soul being the man (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.90). Such a gold-studded staff was the badge of office, carried by kings, by priests and prophets, by heralds and by judges (Merry on 11.90; see *Iliad* 1.15, 18.505); Minos holds one among the dead (11.569; see [[minos]]).

**His greeting.** He speaks first, with the verse of address that only Eustathius, two Venice manuscripts and the Roman edition give (11.92): its presence or absence bears, Merry notes, on the question whether Teiresias could know Odysseus without tasting the blood (Merry's critical note on 11.92; see the translation's note on 11.60, 11.92). “why now, unhappy man, have you left the light of the sun / and come, to see the dead and this joyless place?” (11.93–94; see [[while-i-live]]): ἀτερπὴς χῶρος is what Odysseus called the rocky shore of Scheria where the wave would have dashed him (7.279), and here it is the joyless realm of the shades, like Ovid's *inamoena regna* (*Metamorphoses* 10.15; Merry on 11.94; see the translation's key words). Zenodotus read τίπτʼ αὔτως (Merry's critical note on 11.93).

**The blood.** “But draw back from the pit, and hold away your sharp sword, / so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (11.95–96; see [[sword]] and [[dark-blood]]). Merry draws the lesson of the scene. Teiresias alone of the dead has kept his mind (10.494–495); he knows Odysseus and speaks to him before he drinks, so that it is not the blood that gives him recognition or speech, as it seems to give them to the other shades, to Anticleia among them (11.153). He craves it as a gratification, the drink offering proper to the dead, poured into a pit as the antithesis of the bloody sacrifice on a raised altar to the gods of heaven, as Neoptolemus bids his father come and drink the dark, pure blood of the maiden Polyxena (Euripides, *Hecuba* 534–537). The scholiast on 11.37 says that the souls come to the blood like flies; Eustathius puts it well, that Teiresias knows him before he drinks but does not prophesy, and pays the prophecy as the price of the drink (Merry on 11.96). Odysseus thrusts his sword into its sheath, and “when he had drunk the dark blood, / then the noble seer spoke to me with words” (11.97–99; see [[blameless]] and [[prophecy]]).

**The prophecy.**

{{quote:9118b837-8cdc-5363-8f42-7e709f46b440}}

ὅ is the conjunction, “in that he hath stored up wrath in his heart against thee” (Merry on 11.102; see the translation's note on 11.102 and [[poseidon]], [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[honey-sweet]]). They may still come home, “if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'”, when they come to the island of Thrinacia and find the cattle and fat sheep “of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.104–109; see [[thrinacia]], [[helios]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]): the scholiast takes the epithet as a hint that no one who wronged such a god could escape his notice (Merry on 11.109). If they harm them, he foretells destruction for the ship and the companions, and for Odysseus a homecoming late and in evil plight, “on someone else's ship”, with overbearing men in his house wooing his wife (11.110–117), the curse of the Cyclops come true (9.534–535; see the translation's formulas and [[the-suitors]]). Merry notes that the suitors' outrages had not begun when Teiresias spoke, at the end of the third year after the fall of Troy, and that Aristophanes read κατέδοιεν, “would devour”, for κατέδουσι, though Aristarchus read the present (Merry on 11.115 and his critical note on 11.116; see the translation's note on 11.115–116).

**The oar and the gentle death.** After the vengeance he must take a well-fitted oar and go inland “until you come to men who do not know the sea / and do not eat food mixed with salt”; when a wayfarer says he has a winnowing fan on his shoulder, he is to fix the oar in the ground, sacrifice a ram, a bull and a boar to Poseidon, and go home and offer hecatombs to all the gods; “And death will come to you yourself away from the sea, / a death so very gentle, that will kill you / worn out by an old age of ease; and around you your people / will be prosperous. This I tell you truly” (11.119–137; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]], [[oars]], [[winnowing-fan]], [[sacrifice]] and [[hecatomb]]). Eustathius says that the speech imitates the obliquity of an oracle, being obscure and hard to explain (Merry on 11.119). The ancients took the men who do not know the sea for the Epirots or Thesprotians, who answer the description very badly; Merry thinks the tradition means only that Odysseus went as far inland as possible on the mainland nearest Ithaca, and accepts Eustathius' account of the purpose of the journey, that Poseidon should be honored as a god of the mainland where his name was not known (Merry on 11.122). The threefold victim was called τριττύα, which Nitzsch compared with the Roman *suovetaurilia*, a special offering, since the usual victim of the sea god was a black bull (3.5–6; Merry on 11.131). Whether ἐξ ἁλός means “away from the sea” or “out of the sea”, the death that later legend brought on Odysseus from the sea, is disputed (see the translation's note on 11.134–137 and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]). And Teiresias, Merry observes, does not quite do what Circe said he would: she promised that he would tell “the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming” (10.539–540), and he gives perhaps the measures, but not the way (Merry on 11.136).

**The rule of the blood.** Odysseus accepts what the gods have spun and asks how his mother may know him (11.139–144; see [[anticleia]]). The answer is ῥηίδιον … ἔπος, “An easy word”, a simple rule for holding or refusing communication with the dead (Merry on 11.146): “Whichever of the dead who have died you let / come near the blood will tell you truly; / but whoever you begrudge it to will go back again” (11.147–149; see [[the-dead-who-have-died]]). The text prints the subjunctive ἐπιφθονέῃς, against the vulgate's optative (Merry's critical note on 11.149; see the translation's note on 11.146, 11.149). “So saying, the soul went into the house of Hades, / the soul of lord Teiresias, when he had told the oracles” (11.150–151; see [[anax]], [[thesphaton]] and [[hades]]).

**Remembered.** Odysseus gives him as the reason for his coming, to his mother, “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (11.165), and to Achilles, “I came to consult Teiresias, in case he might tell me some plan / how I might come to rocky Ithaca” (11.479–480; see [[achilles]]): κατὰ χρέος is often rendered “on business with”, but because of the frequent phrase ψυχῇ χρησόμενος Merry would translate “for consulting with” (Merry on 11.479; see the translation's key words).
