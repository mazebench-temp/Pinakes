summary: The Gorgon ends the book. As the countless tribes of the dead gather with a wondrous din, Odysseus is seized by pale fear “lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades” (11.634–635), and he goes at once to the ship.
# Γοργείην (11.634) is claimed by the entry.
---

**The Gorgon's head.** Odysseus would have waited to see more of the heroes of earlier times, Theseus and Peirithous, “but before that the countless tribes of the dead gathered / with a wondrous din; and pale fear took hold of me / lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades” (11.630–635; see [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]], [[pale-fear]], [[persephone]] and [[hades]]). Γοργείην κεφαλὴν δεινοῖο πελώρου is the Iliad's verse for the head on Athena's aegis, “the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / dreadful and terrible, the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (*Iliad* 5.741–742; see [[aegis]] and the general article). Merry takes it here for any horrible spectre, not the Gorgon's head of the Iliad's aegis and Agamemnon's shield, and renders “a Gorgon-head of some terrible monster”; δεινοῖο πελώρου stands in apposition to the Gorgon implied in the adjective Γοργείην. Hesiod, as Merry notes, makes three Gorgons, daughters of Phorcys and Ceto, Sthenno, Euryale and Medusa (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.632–634; *Theogony* 270–276; see [[hesiod]] and the translation's note on 11.632–634), and they live “beyond famous Oceanus, at the edge toward Night” (*Theogony* 274–275; see [[oceanus]]). The book does not say that the head comes; the fear is enough, and the ship goes down the stream of Oceanus (11.636–640).

**The dread monster.** δεινοῖο πελώρου is said in Book 10 of the great stag that Odysseus kills on Aeaea, “I bound together the feet of the dread monster” (10.168), where it is only a huge beast (see [[deer]]).
