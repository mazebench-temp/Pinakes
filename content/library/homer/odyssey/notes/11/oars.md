summary: Elpenor asks that the oar he rowed with be fixed on his mound (11.77–78), and Teiresias tells Odysseus to carry a well-fitted oar inland until he meets men who know nothing of the sea or of oars, “which are wings to ships”, and to fix it in the ground when a wayfarer calls it a winnowing fan (11.121–129). The ship leaves Oceanus “first with rowing” (11.640).

**Elpenor's oar.** The unburied companion asks for a mound on the shore of the gray sea, “Do this for me, and fix on the tomb the oar / with which I rowed when I was alive, among my companions” (11.77–78; see [[elpenor]], [[tomb]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). The oar marks the grave as a rower's, and on the shore it would be seen by all who passed, W. W. Merry notes, few as they must have been off Circe's lonely island; Nitzsch, as Merry reports, compared an epitaph ascribed to Sappho for Pelagon the fisherman, on whose grave his father set a fish-trap and an oar (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.74, citing *Palatine Anthology* 7.505). The companions do as he asked when they come back to Aeaea, and “on the top of the tomb we fixed the well-fitted oar” (12.15).

**Teiresias' oar.** After the suitors are dead Odysseus is to “take a well-fitted oar and go, / until you come to men who do not know the sea / and do not eat food mixed with salt; / nor do they know ships with crimson prows, / or well-fitted oars, which are wings to ships” (11.121–125; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). ἐυήρης, “well-fitted”, is not from ἐρέσσω, “row”, Merry explains, but from ἀραρίσκω, “fit”, like Latin *habilis*; oars are wings because they speed a ship along and because their rows, rising and falling on either side, are like the beat of a bird's wings, and he compares the “winged” oar-stroke of Euripides and Virgil's *remigium alarum*, “the oarage of wings” (on 11.129; Euripides, *Iphigenia in Tauris* 1346; see the translation's rows ἐυήρης and πτερὰ νηυσί; ἀθηρηλοιγός). When a wayfarer takes it for a winnowing fan, he is to fix it in the ground and sacrifice to Poseidon (11.126–131; see [[winnowing-fan]] and [[poseidon]]). The planted oar as a memorial, Merry adds, recalls Elpenor's request (on 11.129).

**Rowing.** At the end of the book the stream of Oceanus carries the ship, “first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind” (11.640; see [[oceanus]]), where Merry prefers the nominative εἰρεσίη, “first there was rowing”, to the text's dative (his critical note on 11.640; see the translation's note on 11.640).
