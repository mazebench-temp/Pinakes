summary: Agamemnon's own account in this book is the Odyssey's telling nearest to the tragedians'. His wife has a hand in the killing, she contrives it, she kills Cassandra beside him and will not close his eyes, and he is struck down “as one kills an ox at the manger”; but the place is Aegisthus' house and the time a feast, not the bath of Aeschylus' Agamemnon.

**The wife's part.** In Nestor's account Aegisthus wins Clytemnestra with words after she has long refused, and he alone devises the murder (3.262–275, 3.303–304; see the entry and [[aegisthus]]). The shade of Agamemnon tells it otherwise. Aegisthus killed him “with my ruinous wife” (11.410); Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him, and as he lay dying with the sword through him she turned away, and “did not bring herself, though I was going to the house of Hades, / to close my eyes with her hands or to shut my mouth” (11.421–426; see [[cassandra]] and [[clytemnestra]]); hers was “the shameful deed that she too devised, / contriving slaughter for her wedded husband” (11.429–430), and at the end he says that she did not let him have his fill of the sight of his son: “before that she killed me myself” (11.452–453). Odysseus answers that Clytemnestra “was preparing a snare” for him while he was far away (δόλον ἤρτυε, 11.439; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**Aeschylus.** These are the points the *Agamemnon* makes its own. Cassandra foresees the bath and the net and the blow: “Keep the bull from the cow! In the robes she has caught him with the black-horned contrivance; she strikes; he falls in the bath of water” (*Agamemnon* 1125–1128, plain renderings throughout; see [[aeschylus]]), the image of Homer's “as one kills an ox at the manger” (11.411 = 4.535; see [[the-ox-at-the-manger]]). She knows that the queen will kill her too (*Agamemnon* 1260–1263). Clytemnestra, standing over the body, tells how she threw around him a boundless net, as for fish, and struck him twice, and a third time when he had fallen (1382–1387), and shows the two bodies: “This is Agamemnon, my husband, a corpse, the work of this right hand” (1404–1406), and Cassandra, “his concubine”, who “lies there, having sung her last, her dying lament, like a swan” (1438–1446). The burial, she says, will be hers, “not with laments from the house” (1551–1554), as Homer's queen would not close the dead man's eyes. Two things the tragedy changes. Homer's king is killed at a feast in Aegisthus' house, with his companions slaughtered around him “like white-tusked hogs” (11.409–420; see [[like-white-tusked-hogs]]); and in Homer Aegisthus is named first as the killer, with the wife beside him (11.409–410), while in Aeschylus the queen strikes alone and Aegisthus comes after.
