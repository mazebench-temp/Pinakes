**Over his head.** δένδρεα δʼ ὑψιπέτηλα κατὰ κρῆθεν χέε καρπόν, “And trees with high foliage let their fruit hang down over his head” (11.588; see [[tantalus]] and [[with-high-foliage]]). κατὰ κρῆθεν is Aristarchus' reading, “down over the head”, κρῆθεν being, W. W. Merry thinks, a syncopated form from the stem of κάρη, “head”, as in κρήδεμνον; the Hesiodic *Shield* has ἀπὸ κρῆθεν, “from her head” (*Shield* 7), and Bekker read κατʼ ἄκρηθεν, comparing κατʼ ἄκρης, “from the top” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.588 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.586–588).

**The orchard's trees.** The next two verses are the verses of Alcinous' orchard: “pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, / and sweet figs and flourishing olives” (11.589–590 = 7.115–116; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]], [[olive]] and the Book 7 note). On Scheria the fruit “never perishes or fails, / in winter or in summer” (7.117–118); here it hangs over a man who can never eat it: “and whenever the old man made straight for them, to grasp them with his hands, / the wind would fling them toward the shadowy clouds” (11.591–592; see [[the-winds]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]). The trees of the orchard that bears fruit all the year stand over a man who can never eat, as the water of the lake vanishes whenever he stoops to drink (11.583–587). ῥίπτασκε ποτὶ νέφεα σκιόεντα, “would fling … toward the shadowy clouds”, is the half-verse of the Phaeacian ball game, in which one of the young men “would fling it toward the shadowy clouds” and the other caught it (8.374; see [[ball]]); Merry refers to his note there on the iterative's anomalous α (on 11.592; 8.374).
