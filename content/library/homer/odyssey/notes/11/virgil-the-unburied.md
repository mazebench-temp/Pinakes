---
summary: Elpenor, the unburied companion who is the first soul to meet Odysseus, is the Odyssey's counterpart of Virgil's Palinurus. Both men met their death by a fall, both are spoken to first by their leader, both beg him by his father and his son, and both are promised a tomb on the shore that will keep their memory; but Elpenor's soul comes to the pit, while Palinurus is held back from the river.

**Elpenor.** “And first came the soul of my companion Elpenor; / for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth” (11.51–52; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). Odysseus speaks first: “Elpenor, how did you come beneath the misty darkness? / You have come faster on foot than I in my black ship” (11.57–58). The soul blames “an evil fate from a god … and endless wine” for his fall from Circe's roof (11.61–65), begs by Odysseus' wife, his father and Telemachus not to be left “unwept, unburied”, and asks to be burned with his armor and given a mound on the shore of the gray sea, “the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of”, with the oar he rowed with fixed on it (11.66–78; see [[unwept-and-unburied]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[oars]]). The companions do it when they come back to Aeaea, burning the body and the armor, heaping a tomb, dragging a pillar onto it and fixing the oar on top (12.8–15, a plain rendering). Merry explains why Elpenor comes first: unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades and take his place among the rest, as the soul of Patroclus says to Achilles (*Iliad* 23.71–74; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51).

**Palinurus.** Virgil's helmsman fell from the stern on the voyage from Africa and was killed on the Italian shore; among the unburied by the river Aeneas knows him, and speaks first, as Odysseus does: “Which of the gods, Palinurus, snatched you from us and drowned you in the middle of the sea? Tell me” (*Aeneid* 6.337–343, plain renderings throughout; see [[virgil]]). Palinurus answers that no god drowned him: he fell overboard dragging the torn-off rudder with him, swam for three nights and was killed on the shore by a savage people, and now the waves hold him (*Aeneid* 6.347–362). Elpenor blamed a god and wine; Palinurus clears both the oracle and the gods, for the rudder was torn away by chance. Then the plea: “And so I beg you, by the pleasant light of heaven and the air, by your father, by the hopes of rising Iulus, snatch me from these evils, unconquered one: either throw earth on me, … or, if there is any way, … give your right hand to a wretched man and take me with you across the waves” (*per genitorem oro, per spes surgentis Iuli*, *Aeneid* 6.363–371). Elpenor begs by Odysseus' father and by Telemachus, “whom you left an only son in your halls” (11.67–68); Palinurus by Aeneas' father and by Iulus. The Sibyl refuses him the crossing, but promises that the neighboring peoples, driven by signs from heaven, will lay his bones to rest, raise a tomb and send it solemn offerings, “and the place will have the name of Palinurus forever” (*aeternumque locus Palinuri nomen habebit*, *Aeneid* 6.372–381). Elpenor's mound is to be “for men to come to hear of” (11.76).

**The difference.** Elpenor, though unburied, comes to the pit before Teiresias and speaks with Odysseus; Palinurus may not cross the river until his bones are at rest, as the ghost of Patroclus is kept from the other souls beyond the river (*Iliad* 23.71–74). The entry tells the Iliad's rule and Virgil's.
