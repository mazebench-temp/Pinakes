summary: Achilles asks whether his son followed him to the war to be a champion, and Odysseus tells him the whole truth: he brought the boy from Scyros himself; in council only Nestor and Odysseus surpassed him, and in battle he ran far ahead of the rest and killed many, above all Eurypylus son of Telephus; in the wooden horse he alone did not weep or tremble, but begged to be let out; and after the sack he went on board with his share and a noble prize, unwounded. His father strides away glad (11.492–540).

**The question.** “But come, tell me word of my noble son, / whether he followed to the war to be a champion, or not” (11.492–493; see [[achilles]]). τοῦ παιδὸς ἀγαυοῦ is “that noble son of mine” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.492). Odysseus has heard nothing of Peleus, “but of your dear son Neoptolemus / I will tell you the whole truth, as you bid me; / for it was I myself who brought him in the hollow, balanced ship / from Scyros to join the well-greaved Achaeans” (11.506–509; see [[scyros]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[well-greaved]]). Merry notes that he had been brought up in Scyros by his grandfather Lycomedes, as the Iliad implies (*Iliad* 19.326–327), and that Odysseus fetched him in accordance with the oracle of Helenus, that Troy could never be taken without him, comparing Sophocles, *Philoctetes* 114–115 (Merry on 11.508; see [[neoptolemus-comes-to-troy]]).

**In council and in battle.** Whenever they took counsel about the city of Troy, “he always spoke first, and did not miss the right words; / godlike Nestor and I alone surpassed him” (11.511–512; see [[nestor]] and [[odysseus]]): οὐχ ἡμάρτανε μύθων is “he did not miss the right words to say”, the opposite of the Iliad's ἁμαρτοεπής, “blundering in speech” (*Iliad* 13.824; Merry on 11.511). On the plain he never stayed in the crowd, “but ran far out in front, yielding to no one in his might” (11.513–515): ὅμιλος is the actual battle-broil, and τὸ ὃν μένος “in that spirit of his” (Merry on 11.514, 11.515; see the translation's note on 11.510–513 for the reading at 11.513). He killed more men than Odysseus could name, in the words with which Odysseus broke off his list of the heroines (11.517 = 11.328), “but what a man he slew with the bronze, the son of Telephus, / the hero Eurypylus” (11.519–520; see [[eurypylus-son-of-telephus]], [[ceteians]] and [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]]).

The verse of the ambush (11.525), which Aristarchus did not know, is probably borrowed from the Hours who keep the gates of heaven, “both to throw open the thick cloud and to close it” (*Iliad* 5.749–751), and words fitting a cloud are very forced when said of an ambush (Merry on 11.525; see the translation's note on 11.525). ἐξέμεναι, “to let him out”, from ἐξίημι, suits ἱκέτευεν better than the variant ἐξίμεναι, “to go out” (Merry on 11.531). See [[the-wooden-horse]], [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[epeius]].

**Unharmed.** “But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam, / with his share and a noble prize he went on board his ship / unharmed, neither struck by the sharp bronze / nor stabbed hand to hand, as often / happens in war; for Ares rages without distinction” (11.533–537; see [[priam]], [[geras]] and [[ares]]). μοῖρα is his share of the spoil, and the special prize, in the *Little Iliad*, was Andromache: in the fragment that Nitzsch quotes from Tzetzes, the son of Achilles leads Hector's wife to the ships and throws her child from the tower; but γέρας here need be no more than any special piece of value out of the spoils (Merry on 11.534; see [[andromache]] and [[sack-of-troy]]). ἐπιμίξ, “without distinction”, is the war god raging indiscriminately, as in the Iliad “Enyalius is impartial” (*Iliad* 18.309; Merry on 11.537; see the translation's key words). Then the soul of Achilles goes off with long strides across the meadow of asphodel, “glad because I said that his son was preeminent” (11.538–540; see [[preeminent]] and [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]]).
