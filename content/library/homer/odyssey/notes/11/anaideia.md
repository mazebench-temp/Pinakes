# ἀναιδής (11.598).
grc: [ἀναιδής]
en: [shameless]
refs: [11.427]
---

The adjective is given to a thing that will not yield. Sisyphus heaves his stone toward the crest, “then its mighty weight would turn it back; / back then to the plain rolled the shameless stone” (λᾶας ἀναιδής, 11.597–598; see [[sisyphus]], [[shameless-stone]] and the Odyssey translation's note on 11.598). The Iliad gives the same epithet to the stone that crushed the tendons and bones of Diores, and to the rock of a simile (*Iliad* 4.521, 13.139), and Merry calls ἀναιδής the original of Virgil's *improbus* (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.598). Aristotle quotes the verse as a famous instance of making lifeless things alive (*Rhetoric* 1411b34–35; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]]). Of persons the book's word is κύντερον, “more doglike”: Agamemnon's soul says that “there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman / who puts such deeds as these into her heart” (11.427–428; see [[dogs]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).
