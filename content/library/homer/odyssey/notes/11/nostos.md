---
summary: Teiresias answers the question of the homecoming: “You seek a honey-sweet homecoming, shining Odysseus; / but a god will make it grievous for you”, and he will come home “late and in evil plight” (11.100–114). Laertes lies in the country longing for it (11.196); Alcinous keeps his guest one more day “much as he is in need of his homecoming” (11.350); and Odysseus tells of the companions who escaped the war and “in their homecoming perished” (11.382–384).
# νεῖαι (11.114; again 12.141), "you will come home".
grc: [νεῖαι]
en: [you will come home]
---

**The seer's answer.** Teiresias begins with the word the poem is named for: “You seek a honey-sweet homecoming” (νόστον … μελιηδέα, 11.100; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, νόστος μελιηδής, and [[honey-sweet]]), but Poseidon's grudge will make it grievous (11.101–103; see [[poseidon]] and [[kotos]]). It still depends on Odysseus, “if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'” and “think of your homecoming” on Thrinacia (11.104–111; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]); if the cattle are harmed, he will come home “late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship”, νεῖαι being the second person of νέομαι with the force of a future (11.112–115; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]), the words of the Cyclops' curse (9.534–535; see [[curses]]). Even then the homecoming is not the end: he must set out again with an oar before he can “go home” and sacrifice (11.121–132).

**Waiting at home.** The people of Ithaca say that he “will come home no more” (11.176). His father lies on the slope of his vineyard, “longing for your homecoming” (σὸν νόστον ποθέων, 11.196), where most manuscripts read σὸν πότμον γοόων, “lamenting your fate” (Merry's critical note on 11.196; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.196 and [[laertes]]).

**Among the Phaeacians.** In the pause of the tale Alcinous asks his guest, “much as he is in need of his homecoming”, to stay one more day for the gifts (11.350–352; see [[pompe]] and [[alcinous]]), and Odysseus answers that he would stay a year to come home with a fuller hand, more respected “by all who should see me come home to Ithaca” (11.356–361; see [[shameful-to-stay-long]]). He then promises the sorrows of the companions “who escaped the groaning war cry of the Trojans, / but in their homecoming perished by the will of an evil woman” (11.383–384), and Agamemnon's soul tells his: “Truly I thought / that I would come home welcome to my children and my slaves” (11.430–432; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).
