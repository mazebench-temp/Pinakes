**Circe's roof.** Elpenor's soul tells his own death in the words of Book 10: “Lying down in the hall of Circe I did not think / to come back down by going to the long ladder, / but fell straight down from the roof; and my neck / was broken from the vertebrae” (11.62–65 = 10.558–560; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).

**Laertes' house.** Anticleia tells her son that his father “stays there / in the country, and does not come down to the city”, and “in the winter he sleeps where the slaves sleep in the house, / in the dust near the fire” (11.187–191; see [[laertes]], [[fire]] and [[bed]]). Telemachus, meanwhile, holds the domains undisturbed, and Penelope stays “in your halls” (11.181–186; see [[telemachus]] and [[penelope]]).

**The roof-beam.** Epicaste, the mother of Oedipus, hanged herself, “when she had fastened a noose hanging sheer from the high roof-beam” (11.278; see [[jocasta]]). The μέλαθρον is the long rafter that ran from wall to wall and carried the roof, W. W. Merry explains; it is the beam on which Athena perches as a swallow among the suitors, and from it Hephaestus let down his nets over Ares and Aphrodite (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.278; 8.279, 22.239–240).

**Aegisthus' hall.** Agamemnon died “in the house of Aegisthus” (11.389), who killed him “when he had called me to his house / and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger”, and his companions were slaughtered like hogs “in the house of a rich man of great power / for a wedding, or a shared feast, or a rich banquet” (11.410–415; see [[agamemnon]], [[aegisthus]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]); “we lay in the hall, and the whole floor seethed with blood” (11.420; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[table]]). The hall of a feast, the place of guest-friendship, is the scene of the crime (see [[xenia]]).

**In the hall of Alcinous.** The tale itself is told in a hall: at the pause the Phaeacians “were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (11.334; see [[thelgo]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]), and Alcinous would listen “in the hall” until dawn (11.374–376; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).
