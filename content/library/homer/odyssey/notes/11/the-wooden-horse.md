summary: Odysseus tells Achilles that when the best of the Argives went down into the horse that Epeius made, he had the whole command, to open the ambush and to close it, and that Neoptolemus alone neither paled nor wept inside it, but begged to be let out, feeling for his sword and his spear (11.523–532).

# applies in the Odyssey only.

The horse is the third and last of the Odyssey's accounts, seen from inside (see the general article). Odysseus, who in Menelaus' tale held back the men when Helen called them (4.271–289), here claims the whole command, ἠμὲν ἀνακλῖναι πυκινὸν λόχον ἠδʼ ἐπιθεῖναι, “both to throw open the close ambush and to close it” (11.525; see [[ambush]] and [[odysseus]]). Aristarchus did not know that line, which is in all the manuscripts; W. W. Merry thinks it borrowed from the Iliad's verse of the Hours, who keep the gates of heaven, “both to throw open the thick cloud and to close it” (*Iliad* 5.751), words that are forced when said of an ambush (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.525; see the translation's note on 11.525). The other leaders “were wiping away their tears, and the limbs of each trembled beneath him”, but Neoptolemus never paled or wept, and “kept supplicating me again and again / to let him out of the horse” (11.526–531; see [[neoptolemus]], [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]); ἐξέμεναι, from ἐξίημι, “let out”, suits ἱκέτευεν better than the variant ἐξίμεναι, “go out” (Merry on 11.531). The ambush is the Iliad's test of courage too: there the coward's color changes and he cannot sit still, but the brave man's color does not change, and “he prays to close with them quickly in grim battle” (*Iliad* 13.276–286), and the horse was Epeius' work (11.523; see [[epeius]]).
