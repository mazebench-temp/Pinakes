# πύργος ἀπώλεο (11.556), the tower said of a man, found only here; the Iliad's notes claim σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον.

**So great a tower.** τὰ δὲ πῆμα θεοὶ θέσαν Ἀργείοισι, / τοῖος γάρ σφιν πύργος ἀπώλεο, “The gods made it a bane to the Argives; / for in you they lost so great a tower” (11.555–556; see [[ajax]] and the translation's row δικάζομαι; πύργος (of a man)). W. W. Merry puts the emphasis on θεοί: Ajax need not visit his anger on Odysseus, for it was the gods who set the arms as a prize, and that was the beginning of sorrow for the Greeks, “for so mighty a stronghold was lost to them in thee”; he compares the same use of πύργος for a person in Psalm 144:2 (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.555). Apollonius the Sophist, he notes, has ἀπώλετο for ἀπώλεο (his critical note on 11.556). The tower is a defense of a city's wall, as Amphion and Zethus “fenced” Thebes “with towers” in this book (11.264; see [[thebes]]).

**From the shield to the man.** In the Iliad Ajax comes on “carrying his shield like a tower”, σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον (*Iliad* 7.219, 11.485, 17.128; see the entry and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]), and Helen points him out to Priam from the wall as “huge Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans”, ἕρκος Ἀχαιῶν (*Iliad* 3.229; again 6.5, 7.211). Odysseus' word turns the likeness of the shield into the man: the Achaeans lost their tower when he died, and they grieve for him “as much as for the head of Achilles, son of Peleus” (11.557). The man who was the bulwark of the army stands apart among the dead, and answers nothing (11.543–544, 11.563; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).
