---
summary: Anticleia tells her son that Laertes has no beds, cloaks or glossy blankets to sleep in, but sleeps in winter in the dust by the fire and in summer on beds of fallen leaves on the slope of his vineyard (11.188–194). Poseidon tells Tyro that the embraces of the immortals are not in vain (11.249).
# The English 'embraces', εὐναί of the gods' love at 11.249, applies in the Odyssey only.
en: [embraces]
passages:
  - 11.187-196 | Laertes in the country
---

**Laertes' beds.** “Nor has he, to sleep in, / beds and cloaks and glossy blankets, / but in the winter he sleeps where the slaves sleep in the house, / in the dust near the fire, and wears poor clothes on his body; / but when summer comes and the flourishing late summer, / everywhere on the slope of his vineyard / beds of fallen leaves are strewn on the ground for him” (11.188–194; see [[laertes]], [[blankets]], [[cloak]], [[clothing]] and [[fire]]). εὐναί is predicative, “for bedding”, W. W. Merry explains, and the low bed of leaves strewn on the ground (χθαμαλαὶ εὐναί) is set against the bedsteads, δέμνια, that he has given up (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.188, 11.194; see the translation's note on 11.188–189). The bedding that a host gives his guests, bedsteads, cloaks and blankets (4.297–299, 7.336–338), is what the old king refuses himself in his grief, “longing for your homecoming” (11.196; see [[nostos]]).

**The embraces of the gods.** Poseidon's word to Tyro, ἐπεὶ οὐκ ἀποφώλιοι εὐναὶ / ἀθανάτων, “for not in vain are the embraces / of the immortals” (11.249–250; see [[tyro]] and [[poseidon]]), uses the word of the bed for the act of love, as Circe's “in bed and love” (10.335; see [[philotes]]). Merry takes εὐναί as “embraces”, and reports Autenrieth's rendering of ἀποφώλιοι as “without offspring” (on 11.249; see the translation's note on 11.248–249).
