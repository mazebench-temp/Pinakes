summary: Thebes is the city of Teiresias, the seer whom Odysseus has come to consult (11.90, 11.165), and of the heroines who follow him: Antiope, whose sons Amphion and Zethus founded seven-gated Thebes and fenced it with towers (11.260–265), and Epicaste, whose son Oedipus ruled the Cadmeans in lovely Thebes (11.271–280).

**Theban Teiresias.** The seer whose soul Odysseus has crossed Oceanus to consult is “Theban Teiresias” when his soul comes to the pit, holding a golden scepter (11.90), and when Odysseus tells his mother why he has come (11.165; see [[teiresias]] and the Book 10 note). He is the first of a line of Thebans among the dead: among the women whom Persephone sends, Antiope, Alcmene, Megara and Epicaste belong to Theban legend.

Here again, W. W. Merry notes, we have Boeotian legend, the Asopus flowing between the lands of Thebes and Plataea; and the account that makes Amphion and Zethus the first builders and fortifiers of Thebes is earlier than the common story of its foundation by Cadmus. The scholia tried to reconcile the two, making Eurymachus, king of the Phlegyae, sack the newly settled town after the death of Amphion and Zethus, so that Cadmus had to found it anew; Apollodorus, following the older writers, puts Cadmus first and has Amphion and Zethus build the lower city at the foot of the Cadmea, and so does Pausanias, as Merry reports them (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.260; see [[antiope]], [[amphion-of-thebes]], [[zethus]] and [[asopus]]). Θήβης ἕδος, “the seat of Thebes”, is said as Ἰθάκης ἕδος, “the seat of Ithaca”, and a fenced city, Merry thinks, was needed against the wild tribes around (on 11.263; see [[seven-gated]] and [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]). The two brothers, “mighty though they were”, could not dwell in it without towers (11.264–265).

**Lovely Thebes.** Epicaste married her own son unknowing; “But he, suffering sorrows in lovely Thebes / through the deadly plans of the gods, ruled the Cadmeans”, while she hanged herself and left him the sorrows that a mother's Erinyes bring to pass (11.271–280; see [[oedipus]], [[jocasta]] and [[cadmeians]]). Merry suspects a contrast between the loveliness of the city, πολυηράτῳ, and the misery of the king who reigned there (on 11.275), and θεῶν ὀλοὰς διὰ βουλάς goes with ἄλγεα πάσχων: he suffered through the plans of the gods (on 11.275; see the translation's note on 11.275–276). Alcmene, the mother of Heracles, and Megara, daughter of “high-hearted Creon” and wife of Heracles, belong to Theban legend too, though the city is not named in their verses (11.266–270): Heracles was born at Thebes (*Iliad* 19.98–99), and Creon, Merry notes, was its king (on 11.269; see [[alcmene]], [[megara-wife-of-heracles]] and [[creon-of-thebes]]).
