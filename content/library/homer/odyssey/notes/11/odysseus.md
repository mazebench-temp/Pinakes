summary: The narrator goes on, from Circe's island to the edge of the world of the dead and back to the ship. He digs the pit, pours the offerings and keeps the dead from the blood with his drawn sword until Teiresias has drunk; he weeps for Elpenor and promises him burial, hears the seer's prophecy, tries three times to embrace his mother, and questions the heroines one by one. In the pause he answers Arete and Alcinous; then he meets Agamemnon, consoles Achilles and is rebuffed, speaks in vain to the silent Ajax, sees the last sights of the dead, and flees to the ship for fear of the Gorgon's head (11.1–640).

**The narrator goes on.** The book is the third of Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians, going on from Book 10 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). Its first verse, “And when we had come down to the ship and the sea” (11.1), is the verse in which Menelaus tells how he went down to his ships after the prophecy of Proteus (4.573); κατήλθομεν is the regular word for coming from inland to the coast (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.1). The tale is broken only once, when he stops among the heroines and the hall sits silent (11.333–384); the words of Elpenor, Teiresias, his mother, Agamemnon, Achilles and Heracles are speeches within his speech.

**To the edge of Oceanus.** They launch the ship and put the sheep on board, “grieving, shedding big tears” (11.5), their sorrow caused by the dreadful journey in prospect (Merry on 11.5); Circe sends a following wind, and at sunset the ship comes to the limits of Oceanus and the land of the Cimmerians (11.6–19; see [[circe]], [[oceanus]] and [[cimmerians]]). They go along the stream “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22). Circe had spoken of the groves of Persephone, the shore and the rock where the rivers meet (10.508–515); here nothing more is said of them, and the Cimmerians and their gloomy land are brought in instead (Merry on 11.22; see [[the-groves-of-persephone]]).

**The rite.** While Perimedes and Eurylochus hold the victims, “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and dug a pit about a cubit's length this way and that” (11.23–25; see [[perimedes-the-companion]], [[eurylochus]] and [[sword]]); he pours the drink offering, sprinkles the barley, makes his vows and cuts the throats of the sheep over the pit, doing in the first person what Circe told him to do (11.26–36, 10.516–537; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and the translation's note on 10.516–537). The souls gather “with a wondrous cry; and pale fear took hold of me” (11.43; see [[pale-fear]]). There seems no reason for his panic here, Merry thinks, unless the weird sound of their cries, whereas at the end of the book there is good ground for terror (Merry on 11.43). Then he bids the companions flay and burn the sheep and pray to Hades and Persephone, “and I myself drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and sat there, and did not let the strengthless heads of the dead / come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias” (11.44–50; see [[the-strengthless-heads]]).

**Elpenor.** The first to come is the soul of his companion, left unburied in Circe's house, and he weeps: “When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart” (11.55; see [[pity]] and [[tears]]). His greeting, “Elpenor, how did you come beneath the misty darkness? / You have come faster on foot than I in my black ship” (11.57–58), has a singular naivety, like Telemachus' to his guest, “For I do not think that you came here on foot” (1.173); the scholiast is careful to say that it is not banter but astonishment (Merry on 11.58). To Elpenor's plea he answers in a single verse, “All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you” (11.80), an answer that Lauer found brief and abrupt, as if the poet hastened on to the heart of the book (Merry on 11.80). “So we two sat exchanging hateful words, / I on one side, holding my sword over the blood, / and on the other the phantom of my companion, saying many things” (11.81–83; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).

**His mother kept back.** Then comes the soul of his mother, “whom I had left alive when I went to sacred Ilios” (11.86): he learns of her death by seeing her soul, and will ask her what fate brought her down (11.171). “When I saw her I wept, and pitied her in my heart; / but even so, though deeply grieved, I did not let her first / come near the blood until I had questioned Teiresias” (11.87–89; see [[anticleia]]). Merry sees here the characteristic prudence of Odysseus in sacrificing feeling to interest, on which Eustathius moralizes (Merry on 11.88).

**Teiresias.** The seer bids him draw back from the pit and put up his sword: “So he spoke, and I drew back and thrust my silver-studded sword / into its sheath” (11.97–98; see [[silver-studded]] and [[sheath]]). The prophecy is spoken to him: a god will make his homecoming grievous for the blinding of Poseidon's son; he may still come home if he spares the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia (see [[thrinacia]]); if not, “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house” (11.114–115); he will kill the suitors, carry an oar inland to men who do not know the sea, sacrifice to Poseidon, and die “away from the sea” in an old age of ease (11.100–137; see [[teiresias]], [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]], [[the-suitors]], [[winnowing-fan]] and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]). He answers with resignation, “Teiresias, this, I suppose, the gods themselves have spun” (11.139): που gives the tone of resignation, and the stress on αὐτοί, at the end of the verse, means “of their own will, without consulting me” (Merry on 11.139; see [[moira]]). Then, at once, he asks how his mother may know him (11.140–144). Merry reads the last part of the prophecy as a reconciliation: as the wrath of Poseidon was the first cause of his trials, so he must publish the sea god's name among a people who have never heard it, and then, far from the sight and sound of the sea, spend a happy and honored old age and pass away by a calm and painless death; Penelope, hearing the prophecy, seems to understand it so (Merry on 11.119; 23.286–287, Greek only; see the translation's note on 11.134–137).

**My mother.** When she has drunk and knows him, he tells her why he has come: “My mother, need brought me down to the house of Hades / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (11.164–165), the verses in which Circe sent him (10.491–492), now in the first person; he has not yet come near the Achaean land, but has been wandering always “ever since I first followed brilliant Agamemnon / to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (11.166–169; see [[agamemnon]]). He asks how she died, of a long sickness or by the gentle arrows of Artemis, and about his father, his son and his prize of honor, and whether his wife has married another (11.170–179; see [[gentle-arrows]], [[laertes]], [[telemachus]] and [[penelope]]). When she has answered, he tries to embrace her:

{{quote:0f262f31-da89-52d4-975d-6f5eca7b87f4}}

The vain embrace is that of Achilles at the ghost of Patroclus, who reached out with his hands and could not take hold of it, and Achilles' wish, that throwing their arms around each other they might take their fill of lament, is the wish Odysseus speaks here (*Iliad* 23.97–101; Merry on 11.211; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[lament]]). Her answer is the way of mortals: the soul flies away like a dream (11.216–224; see [[the-way-of-mortals]], [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]).

**The heroines.** Persephone sends the women, and he questions them one at a time: “And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best: / drawing the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh, / I would not let them all drink the dark blood at once” (11.230–232; see [[long-edged]] and [[dark-blood]]). The middle verse is the one in which he thought of drawing his sword on Eurylochus (10.439). He breaks off: “But all of them I could not tell or name”, for the night would wane first, “But it is time / to sleep, either when I have gone to the swift ship and my companions, / or here; and my escort will be the care of the gods and of you” (11.328–332; see [[pompe]]). The companions are the sailors whom Alcinous had sent down to the harbor ready to take the ship on the morrow (Merry on 11.331).

**In the pause.** The Phaeacians sit spellbound; Arete praises him and bids them not to cut short their gifts, and Alcinous promises the gifts and the escort for the next day (11.333–353; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[echeneus]] and [[alcinous]]). He answers:

{{quote:0b967595-ee60-5202-99c3-3b0913d8041a}}

The apodosis is brought in by καί, “that is what I would want”, and the second καί adds a second advantage (Merry on 11.358, 11.360): it was a bad thing to come home with empty hands, as the companions complained before they opened the skin of the winds (10.42; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[possessions]]). Alcinous answers that they do not judge him a deceiver and a cheat, such as the black earth feeds, “but you have beauty of words, and within you are good wits, / and you have told your tale skillfully, as a singer does” (11.363–368; see [[singers]]), and asks whether he saw any of his companions who died at Troy. Odysseus grants that “there is a time for many words, and a time also for sleep” (11.379; see [[a-time-for-many-words]]) and promises the sorrows of companions who escaped the war “but in their homecoming perished by the will of an evil woman” (11.380–384): κακῆς γυναικός can only be Clytemnestra, given the story that follows at once, though Eustathius and the scholia thought of Helen or even Cassandra (Merry on 11.384; see [[clytemnestra]]).

**Agamemnon.** He weeps at the sight of him in the verse he used for Elpenor (11.395 = 11.55) and asks how he died, at sea by Poseidon or on land at the hands of enemies (11.397–403; see [[agamemnon]]). When he has heard the story of the murder, he answers: “Ah, truly far-seeing Zeus has hated the offspring of Atreus / terribly, through the plans of women, / from of old: for Helen's sake many of us perished, / and against you Clytemnestra was preparing a snare while you were far away” (11.436–439; see [[o-popoi]], [[helen]] and [[clytemnestra]]): Zeus made the intrigues of Helen and of Clytemnestra the means of working out his enmity (Merry on 11.437). Agamemnon warns him to come home in secret and trust no woman, though Penelope is not like his own wife (11.441–456; see [[penelope]] and [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]), and asks for news of Orestes; Odysseus has none: “Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this? I do not know at all / whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (11.463–464; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]] and [[orestes]]). He has no certain knowledge, Merry explains, and will not waste time in talk (Merry on 11.464). “So we two stood exchanging hateful words, / grieving, shedding big tears” (11.465–466).

**Achilles.** The soul of Achilles knows him and asks, “hard man, what still greater deed will you devise in your mind? / How did you dare to come down to Hades” (11.474–475; see [[hard]]): “Surely this must be the crowning enterprise of all!”, σχέτλιε being here “daring”, almost “foolhardy” (Merry on 11.474). He answers that he came to consult Teiresias, and turns his own troubles into praise of the dead man:

{{quote:57286b65-a6bb-536c-9e4e-7b957c83cc68}}

Achilles refuses the comfort: he would rather be bound to the soil and serve another for hire than be lord over all the dead (11.488–491; see [[rather-a-hired-laborer]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[achilles]]). Of Peleus Odysseus has heard nothing, but of Neoptolemus he can tell the whole truth, “for it was I myself who brought him in the hollow, balanced ship / from Scyros to join the well-greaved Achaeans” (11.505–509); in council “godlike Nestor and I alone surpassed him” (11.512), and when the best of the Argives went down into the horse, “to me had been given the whole command” (11.524), and the young man alone did not weep or tremble (11.505–537; see [[neoptolemus]], [[nestor]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

**Ajax.** Only the soul of Ajax stands apart, angry over “the victory / that I won over him when I pleaded my case beside the ships / for the armor of Achilles”, and Odysseus wishes he had never won it: “Would that I had never been the victor for such a prize!” (11.543–548; see [[ajax]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]). He speaks to him “with soothing words” (11.552; see [[soothing-words]]), and puts the blame on the gods and on Zeus: you need not visit your anger on me, Merry paraphrases, it was the gods who offered these arms as a prize (11.553–562; Merry on 11.555). “So I spoke, and he answered me nothing” (11.563). The verses that follow, “There, angry as he was, he would still have spoken to me, or I to him; / but the heart in my own breast wished / to see the souls of the others who have died” (11.565–567), have been suspected (see the translation's note on 11.565–567): Odysseus does not go down into the world of the dead, as Aeneas does, but sits at the edge of the trench and questions the shades as they come; in what follows he is an eyewitness of what goes on in the depths of Erebus (Merry on 11.565).

**The last sights.** He sees Minos giving judgment, Orion hunting across the meadow of asphodel, Tityus, Tantalus and Sisyphus punished, and the phantom of Heracles, who knows him and pities him: “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices, / ah, wretch, truly you too drag out some evil fate, / such as I bore under the rays of the sun” (11.617–619; see [[minos]], [[orion]], [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]], [[tityus]], [[tantalus]], [[sisyphus]], [[heracles]] and [[wretch]]). The words are a statement, not a question (see the translation's note on 11.618–619).

**The flight.** “but I stayed there steadfastly, in case any more should come / of the heroes who had perished in former days”, and he would have seen Theseus and Peirithous, “but before that the countless tribes of the dead gathered / with a wondrous din; and pale fear took hold of me / lest the Gorgon's head, the head of the dread monster, / noble Persephone should send against me out of Hades” (11.628–635; see [[theseus]], [[peirithous]], [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]] and [[gorgon]]). He uses the phrase, Merry thinks, of any horrible specter (Merry on 11.632; see the translation's note on 11.632–634). “At once then I went to the ship and ordered my companions / to go on board themselves and loose the stern-cables” (11.636–637; see [[stern-cables]]), and the wave of the stream carries the ship down the river Oceanus, “first with rowing, and afterward a fair wind” (11.639–640): there was no wind, Merry suggests, in that unearthly place, until they came back into the ordinary world (Merry on 11.640).

**His names and epithets in this book.** διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices”, the address of Elpenor, Teiresias, Agamemnon, Achilles and Heracles (11.60, 11.92, 11.405, 11.473, 11.617; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]); φαίδιμʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “shining Odysseus”, in the mouths of Teiresias, his mother and Achilles (11.100, 11.202, 11.488; see [[phaidimos]]); πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles”, in the verses that bring in his answers in the pause (11.354, 11.377; see [[of-many-wiles]]); Ὀδυσεῦ in the address of Alcinous and of Agamemnon (11.363, 11.444). Unnamed, he is ἄναξ, “lord”, to Elpenor (11.71; see [[anax]]); ὦ δύστηνε, “unhappy man”, to Teiresias (11.93), as Elpenor is to him (11.80); τέκνον ἐμόν, “My child”, and περὶ πάντων κάμμορε φωτῶν, “ill-fated beyond all men”, to his mother (11.155, 11.216); ἀνὴρ ὅδε, “this man”, and ξεῖνος … ἐμός, “my guest”, to Arete (11.336, 11.338; see [[xenia]]); σχέτλιε, “hard man”, to Achilles (11.474); and ἆ δείλʼ, “ah, wretch”, to Heracles (11.618).
