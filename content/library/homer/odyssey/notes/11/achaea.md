---
summary: Twice in the land of the dead, to his mother and to Achilles, Odysseus says the same verse of his wanderings: “for I have not yet come near the Achaean land, nor yet set foot / on our own land” (11.166–167, 11.481–482).
---

**Not even near Achaea.** Anticleia asks her son whether he has come from Troy after long wandering and has not yet reached Ithaca (11.160–162), and he answers that need has brought him down to consult Teiresias, “for I have not yet come near the Achaean land, nor yet set foot / on our own land, but I have been wandering always, enduring misery, / ever since I first followed brilliant Agamemnon / to Ilios” (11.164–169; see [[anticleia]] and [[troy]]). He gives Achilles the same reason in the same verse, with a shorter close, “but always I have troubles” (11.479–482; see [[achilles]]). Ἀχαιΐδος stands alone, with γῆς understood, as Τροίης stands for the land of Troy (10.332): not only has he not been home, W. W. Merry explains, but he has not even been near Achaea (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.166). The Achaean land is the whole Greek homeland, and ἁμὴ γῆ, “our own land”, is Ithaca within it (see [[ithaca]]). The phrase returns when the island is in sight: Athena, disguised, tells Odysseus on the shore of Ithaca that its name has reached even Troy, “which they say is far from the Achaean land” (13.248–249).
