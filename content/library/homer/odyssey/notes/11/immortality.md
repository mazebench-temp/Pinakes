---
summary: Two exceptions to death are seen among the dead: the sons of Leda, held alive beneath the earth, who live and die on alternate days with honor like the gods (11.300–304), and Heracles, of whom Odysseus sees only “his phantom”, while he himself feasts among the immortal gods with Hebe as his wife (11.601–604).
refs: [11.301-304, 11.602-604]
---

**The sons of Leda.** Castor and Polydeuces are both held by the earth “alive though they are; / and even beneath the earth, having honor from Zeus, / now they live on alternate days, and now again / they are dead; and they have been allotted honor like the gods” (11.301–304; see [[castor]], [[polydeuces]] and [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]]). The phrase γαῖα κατέχει regularly means to be dead and buried, and the Iliad says that the earth already holds them in Lacedaemon (*Iliad* 3.243–244); Merry finds it hard to reconcile ζωούς with that, unless by a kind of oxymoron, notes Nitzsch's conjecture ἀμφιζωούς, and doubts the whole passage on Leda (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.301; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.298–304 and [[time]]).

**Heracles.** Odysseus sees “mighty Heracles, / his phantom; but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts, and has Hebe of the lovely ankles, / the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (11.601–604; see [[heracles]], [[hebe]] and [[eidolon]]). The verses that divide him were suspected in antiquity and attributed to Onomacritus; Merry thinks the legend of the apotheosis, though post-Homeric, very early, and the insertion the work of a rhapsodist who feared to offend his audience by leaving their deified hero in the underworld (Merry on 11.601; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.602–604). Achilles, honored “equally with the gods” in life, has no such exception (11.484–491; see [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]).
