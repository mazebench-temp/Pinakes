summary: The sun sets as the ship comes to the limits of Oceanus (11.12), and never shines on the land of the Cimmerians beyond (11.15–19). Teiresias warns Odysseus of the cattle and fat sheep “of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.108–109), and the dead speak of life as the light of the sun: Teiresias asks why Odysseus has left “the light of the sun” (11.93), and Achilles and Heracles look back to their days “under the rays of the sun” (11.498, 11.619).
# Unnamed: the sun "climbs toward the starry sky" and "turns back again from the sky toward the earth" (11.17-18).
refs: [11.17-18]
---

**The sun that never shines.** The ship runs before Circe's wind all day, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (11.12, the verse of 2.388; see [[sunset-lines]]), and she comes to the limits of Oceanus, where the Cimmerians live “wrapped in mist and cloud; and never does / the shining sun look down on them with his rays, / neither when he climbs toward the starry sky, / nor when he turns back again from the sky toward the earth, / but deadly night is spread over those wretched mortals” (11.15–19; see [[cimmerians]], [[oceanus]] and [[deadly]]). Hesiod uses the same words of the house of Night's children, Sleep and Death, whom “the shining Sun never looks upon with his rays”, Ἠέλιος φαέθων ἐπιδέρκεται ἀκτίνεσσιν, neither going up into the sky nor coming down from it (*Theogony* 758–761), and his Night is Νὺξ ὀλοή, “deadly Night”, νεφέλῃ κεκαλυμμένη, “wrapped in cloud” (*Theogony* 757); the Odyssey's reading καταδέρκεται is that of Aristophanes and Aristarchus, where the manuscripts have ἐπιδέρκεται, as in Hesiod (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 11.16). The two verses of the sun's rising and setting return in the Sun's own complaint in Book 12, over the cattle in which he took delight “as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.380–381), and there he threatens to “go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.383).

**The light of the sun.** For the dead, life is the sunlight they have left. Teiresias asks, “why now, unhappy man, have you left the light of the sun / and come, to see the dead and this joyless place?” (11.93–94; see [[teiresias]]), as Odysseus in Book 10 no longer wished “to live and see the light of the sun” (10.498; see the Book 10 note). Achilles is “not there to help him under the rays of the sun” (11.498; see [[achilles]] and [[peleus]]), and Heracles drags out an evil fate “such as I bore under the rays of the sun” (11.619; see [[heracles]]). ὑπʼ αὐγὰς ἠελίοιο is the Odyssey's phrase for the living world, of the birds that “go to and fro under the rays of the sun” (2.181–182), and of Odysseus' mother and father in the beggar's question to Eumaeus, whether they still live under the rays of the sun or are already dead and in the house of Hades (15.347–350).

**The cattle of the Sun.** Teiresias' warning names the god: “when first you bring your well-made ship / to the island of Thrinacia, escaping the violet sea, / and find grazing there the cattle and fat sheep / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.106–109; see [[thrinacia]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]). The relative clause is the Iliad's prayer to the Sun as a witness of oaths, “and you, Sun, who see all things and hear all things” (*Iliad* 3.277; see [[oath]]), and Odysseus will repeat it to his companions on the island (12.323). Merry quotes the scholium on the words: they hint that no one who wronged such a god could escape his notice (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.109). Yet in Book 12 the Sun learns of the slaughter from a messenger, the nymph Lampetie (12.374–375).
