summary: The judgment of the arms is told only here in Homer, by the winner, when the soul of Ajax stands apart from the others, still angry (11.541–567). Thetis set the armor of Achilles as a prize, the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena judged, and Odysseus won; now he wishes he had never won it, lays the blame on Zeus, and begs Ajax to speak to him, but Ajax answers nothing and goes off into Erebus.

**The only telling.** The Iliad ends before the death of Achilles, and the Odyssey tells his funeral and games only in its last book, where Thetis sets out splendid prizes for the best of the Achaeans (24.85–92, Greek only); at this point, Merry notes, the story is taken up by the Cyclic epics (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.543). The judgment itself is told here only, in the words of the man who won: “Alone the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon, / stood apart, still angry because of the victory / that I won over him when I pleaded my case beside the ships / for the armor of Achilles; his lady mother had set it as a prize, / and the sons of the Trojans were the judges, and Pallas Athena” (11.543–547; see [[ajax]] and [[thetis]]). δικαζόμενος is “pleading my case”, as in a dispute at law (see the translation's key-word row for δικάζομαι), and ἔθηκε is the verb of setting out prizes, as Achilles sets them out for the chariot race (Merry on 11.546; *Iliad* 23.262–263).

**The judges.** Aristarchus athetized the verse of the judges (11.547), without a reason that has come down. Nitzsch, as Merry reports, found four: the Trojan captives would be the most unlikely umpires in a dispute between two Achaean chiefs; Homer would not say παῖδες Τρώων, “the sons of the Trojans”, but κοῦροι or υἷες; the verse is superfluous; and Odysseus would hardly make Athena the author of so disastrous a decision (Merry on 11.547; see the translation's note on 11.547). Merry then sets out the later versions. In the *Aethiopis* of Arctinus, Ajax carried the body of Achilles from the fight while Odysseus held off the Trojans; Thetis added her son's arms to the prizes of the games, for the bravest of the Greeks; Agamemnon and the chiefs could not decide between the two, and on Nestor's advice the question was put to the Trojan captives, which of the heroes had done the Trojans most harm; they answered Odysseus, and Ajax fell on his sword. In the *Little Iliad* of Lesches, spies sent to listen under the walls of Troy overheard two girls: one gave the prize to Ajax, who carried the body off when Odysseus would not, and the other, prompted by Athena, answered that even a woman could carry a burden once a man had put it on her, but she could not fight (from the scholia on Aristophanes' *Knights* 1056). Neither poem made the sons of Atreus the judges, but Pindar implies treachery in the decision, and Sophocles' Ajax says so plainly (Merry on 11.543, citing Pindar's seventh and eighth *Nemean* odes and his *Isthmian* odes, and Sophocles, *Ajax* 445; see [[the-judgment-of-arms-play]] and [[ajax-plays]]).

**The victor's regret.** “Would that I had never been the victor for such a prize! / For because of it the earth has held fast such a head” (11.548–549): ἀέθλῳ is the prize, as ἕνεκʼ αὐτῶν, “because of it”, shows, and κατέσχεν is “covered, buried” (Merry on 11.548, 11.549; see the translation's note on 11.548). Ajax was the best of the Danaans in looks and in deeds after Achilles, in the verse that brought him up with Achilles' soul (11.550–551; 11.469–470; see [[best-in-looks]]). Odysseus calls the armor “ruinous”: “The gods made it a bane to the Argives; / for in you they lost so great a tower” (11.555–556; see [[like-a-tower]]). The stress lies on θεοί: it was the gods who set these arms as a prize, a beginning of sorrow for the Greeks, and for πύργος said of a person Merry compares the Psalms (on 11.555, citing Psalm 144.2). “And no one else / is to blame, but Zeus, who hated the army of the Danaan spearmen / terribly, and set your fate upon you” (11.558–560): “no one else is to blame, but Zeus is to blame”, in Merry's paraphrase, as Helen says that Zeus has set an evil fate on herself and Paris (on 11.559; *Iliad* 6.357; see [[zeus]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The silence.** Odysseus begs him to come near and hear, and to tame his anger (11.561–562). “So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, but went after the other / souls of the dead who have died, into Erebus” (11.563–564; see [[erebus]] and [[silence]]); for the critics who admired the silence, see [[longinus-ajaxs-prayer]]. The next verses say that Ajax, angry as he was, would still have spoken, but that Odysseus wished to see the other dead (11.565–567); Merry suspects them, and thinks the later addition to the book begins there (on 11.565; see the translation's note on 11.565–567 and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]).
