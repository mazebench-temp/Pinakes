summary: The second part of the story (11.51–83). The soul of Elpenor, the first of the dead to come to the pit, tells its death in the verses in which Odysseus told it in the tenth book, and begs not to be left unwept and unburied, but to be burned with its armor and given a mound on the shore with its oar fixed on it; Odysseus promises. The burial follows in the twelfth book.

**Left behind.** The tenth book left the dead man where he fell, and the eleventh gives the reason in Odysseus' words: “for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.53–54; see [[unwept-and-unburied]]). The toil was the journey that this book tells (see [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). The soul comes first of all the dead, because the body is unburied, and it needs no blood to speak (11.51, 11.82; for Merry's notes on both, see [[elpenor]]).

**Told twice.** Odysseus asks how Elpenor came beneath the misty darkness faster on foot than the ship (11.57–58), and the soul answers with the death that the hearers have already heard. It gives first the cause, “an evil fate from a god led me into blind folly, and endless wine” (11.61), where the narrator had said only that he lay apart, longing for the cool air, sodden with wine (10.554–555); then the fall, in a verse of its own and three of the narrator's turned to the first person, from the ladder it forgot to the neck broken from the vertebrae (11.62–65; 11.63–65 = 10.558–560; see [[ate]], [[wine]] and the translation's key-word row for καταντικρύ). The soul knows its own death as the living saw it.

**The plea and the oar.** The soul begs by those whom Odysseus left at home, his wife, his father and Telemachus, and asks for what the living owe the dead: to be wept, burned with its armor, and given a mound “on the shore of the gray sea, / the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of”, with the oar it rowed with fixed on the tomb (11.66–78; see [[supplication]], [[cremation]], [[tomb]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Odysseus promises in a single verse (11.80). Teiresias, in the next scene, will bid Odysseus himself fix a well-fitted oar in the ground, far inland, with the same verb, γαίῃ πήξας ἐυῆρες ἐρετμόν (11.129; πῆξαι, 11.77; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[oars]]). The promise is kept to the letter: back on Aeaea the companions burn the body and its armor, heap up a tomb, drag a pillar onto it and fix the well-fitted oar on the top (12.8–15, a plain rendering).

**Doubted.** Merry held the episode a later addition, made when the book of the dead was recited as a separate rhapsody, its beginning and end shaped to match the scene of Odysseus' mother (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51; see the translation's note on 11.51–83). His reasons, and Eustathius' remark that Elpenor had no sense even in Hades, are set out under [[elpenor]].
