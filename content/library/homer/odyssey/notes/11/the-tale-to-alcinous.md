heading: The third book of the tale
summary: The tale goes on from the tenth book with the journey to the dead, and is broken here for the only time. In the middle of the women's catalogue Odysseus offers to stop, since it is time to sleep (11.328–332); the Phaeacians sit spellbound, Arete asks for more gifts, and Alcinous, judging that he has told his tale “skillfully, as a singer does”, asks for the heroes of Troy, for “This night is very long, endless” (11.333–376). Odysseus goes on to the end of the book (11.377–640). Merry heads it Νέκυια.

**The heading.** Merry heads the book Νέκυια, “the Nekyia” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 11; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]). It has no beginning of its own: the tenth book ends at the ship, where Circe has tethered the ram and the black ewe (10.569–574), and the eleventh goes on, “And when we had come down to the ship and the sea” (11.1, as 4.573; see [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). In the summary that Odysseus gives Penelope the journey to the dead comes between Circe and the Sirens, in the same place (23.321–325, Greek only).

**Breaking off.** The tale stops in the middle of the women: “But all of them I could not tell or name, / all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw” (11.328–329; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). The verse is Helen's when she tells of Odysseus in Troy, “All of them I could not tell or name” (4.240), and the poet's before the Catalogue of Ships, “The multitude I could not tell or name” (*Iliad* 2.488; Merry on 11.328; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). Night, he says, would pass first, “even the immortal night”, the only place where ἄμβροτος is an epithet of night, and Merry compares the words of Aeneas to his disguised mother, that if he told his toils from the beginning the evening star would close Olympus and lay the day to rest first (11.330; Merry on 11.330, citing *Aeneid* 1.374; see the translation's key-word row for νὺξ ἄμβροτος). He will sleep at the ship or here, and his escort will be the care of the gods and of his hosts (11.330–332). The companions at the ship are the Phaeacian sailors whom Alcinous had already sent down to make the ship ready for the morrow (Merry on 11.331, citing 7.317 and 8.50; see [[pompe]]).

**The spell.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (11.333–334). κηληθμός is the glamour of his story: the scholia explain it as a stillness full of pleasure and delight, Eustathius as the height of enjoyment, and Quintilian, as Merry reports, renders it “the silence of attention” (Merry on 11.334; see [[thelgo]] and [[silence]]). The same two verses open the thirteenth book, when the tale is over (13.1–2 = 11.333–334), and Eumaeus, later, will say that the beggar charmed him, sitting in his hall, as men gaze at a singer who sings to mortals the lovely verses he learned from the gods (17.518–521, Greek only; see [[singers]]).

**Arete and the gifts.** Arete speaks first, asking how the guest seems in looks and stature and the mind within, calling him her guest, and asking the Phaeacians not to hurry his escort or cut short his gifts (11.335–341; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The gifts are those of the day before (8.385–448), and Arete knows that if his departure is put off there will be more, as Alcinous proposes in the thirteenth book (Merry on 11.339; 13.13–15). Echeneus agrees, and leaves the decision to the king (11.342–346; see [[echeneus]]). Alcinous promises the gifts and the escort for the next day (11.348–353), and Odysseus answers that he would stay a year to go home “with a fuller hand” (11.355–361), for it was a bad thing to come home empty-handed, as the companions complained before they opened the skin of the winds (Merry on 11.358; 10.42; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]).

**The judgment.** Alcinous does not take Odysseus for a deceiver and a cheat, like the many men whom the black earth feeds, scattered far and wide, “fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see” (11.363–366). Odysseus had just been telling of marvels in the land of Hades, where no mortal could go to test his tale: the king does not mean to doubt it, Merry thinks, but he gently reminds his guest of the doubtful truth of travelers' tales (Merry on 11.366; see the translation's key-word row for ἠπεροπεύς). Merry compares Herodotus on the man who told of a river Ocean: he carried his story into the unseen, and it cannot be refuted; Herodotus knew of no such river, and thought that Homer or some earlier poet had found the name and brought it into poetry (*Histories* 2.23.1; see [[oceanus]] and [[herodotus]]). “But you have beauty of words, and within you are good wits, / and you have told your tale skillfully, as a singer does, / the grievous sorrows of all the Argives and of yourself” (11.367–369; see [[demodocus]]). Odysseus has not told the sorrows of all the Argives: Eustathius saw the difficulty, and Merry suggests that the king means the men Odysseus brought from Troy (on 11.369).

**The night.** Alcinous asks whether Odysseus saw any of his godlike companions who went with him to Ilios and died there (11.370–372; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]). “This night is very long, endless” (11.373): to Odysseus, who wished to go to bed, the night seemed far spent; Alcinous, eager to hear more, will have it that it is still early, and seems to parody his guest's words about the immortal night (Merry on 11.373). He could hold out till dawn, he says, if Odysseus would tell his sorrows in the hall (11.374–376).

**The answer.** “There is a time for many words, and a time also for sleep” (11.379; see [[a-time-for-many-words]]): the stress, Merry says, is on the second half, for Odysseus thinks it time to sleep, but is willing to give it up (on 11.379). He offers instead “other things more pitiable even than these”, the sorrows of the companions who escaped the war cry of the Trojans and perished in their homecoming “by the will of an evil woman” (11.380–384). The woman can only be Clytemnestra, given the story that follows, though Eustathius thought also of Helen or even Cassandra (Merry on 11.384; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The tale is taken up again where it broke off, with the women scattered by Persephone (11.385–386; Merry on 11.385), and it runs on to the end of the book and of the next.
