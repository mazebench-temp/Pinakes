summary: The first warning of the cattle comes from the dead. Teiresias names the island of Thrinacia and the cattle and fat sheep of the Sun, and sets the condition of the homecoming on them: left unharmed, they let the companions reach Ithaca; harmed, they bring destruction on the ship and the companions, and a late homecoming for Odysseus alone (11.104–115).
# Unnamed: the condition (11.104-105); the herds (11.108-109); the two outcomes (11.110-115).
refs: [11.104-105, 11.108-109, 11.110-115]
---

**The condition.** Teiresias does not forbid the cattle outright: “But even so you may still come home, though suffering evils, / if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'” (11.104–105), the restraint to be shown when they come to the island and find the herds grazing (11.106–108; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.108; see [[thrinacia]]). The herds are “the cattle and fat sheep / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.108–109; see [[helios]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]), the god from whom, the scholia observe, no wrongdoer can hide (Merry on 11.109). Then the two outcomes: “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming, / you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils; / but if you harm them, then I foretell destruction for you, / for your ship and your companions” (11.110–113; see [[nostos]]).

**Heard twice.** Circe gives the same warning in five of the seer's verses, and adds what the seer did not say: the number of the herds, that they neither breed nor die, and the nymphs who tend them (12.127–141, Greek only). On Thrinacia Odysseus remembers both warnings and tells them to his companions, who will not row past (12.266–276, Greek only). The proem has already told the end, the companions who “perished through their own recklessness, / fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun” (1.7–8; see [[atasthalie]]). For the prophecy as a whole, see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]].
