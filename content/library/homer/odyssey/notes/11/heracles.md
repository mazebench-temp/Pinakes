summary: Heracles is named among the heroines as Alcmene's son by Zeus and Megara's husband (11.266–270), and he is the last of the great dead whom Odysseus sees: his phantom, bow drawn, the dead scattering around him like birds, while he himself feasts among the gods with Hebe as his wife. He knows Odysseus, calls him a fellow sufferer, and tells of the hound he brought up from Hades (11.601–627).

**Alcmene's son.** In the catalogue of heroines Odysseus sees “Alcmene, the wife of Amphitryon, / who bore Heracles, bold in endurance, lion-hearted, / having lain in love in the arms of great Zeus; / and Megara, the daughter of high-hearted Creon, / whom the son of Amphitryon, ever unyielding in his might, had as wife” (11.266–270; see [[alcmene]], [[amphitryon]], [[megara-wife-of-heracles]], [[bold-in-endurance]], [[lion-hearted]] and [[unyielding]]). θρασυμέμνονα θυμολέοντα is the pair of epithets that his son Tlepolemus gives him in the Iliad (*Iliad* 5.639), and “the son of Amphitryon” is the Iliad's name for him in Dione's story of the wounding of Hera (*Iliad* 5.392).

**The phantom.** After Sisyphus:

{{quote:a4da15bf-0340-5c55-ab79-11232ae5cb25}}

βίην Ἡρακληείην, “mighty Heracles”, is the periphrasis of the Iliad (see [[might-of-heracles]]). What Odysseus sees is his εἴδωλον, “his phantom” (11.602; see [[eidolon]]); “he himself” (αὐτός) is among the gods, with Hebe as his wife (see [[hebe]], [[of-the-lovely-ankles]], [[hera]] and [[of-the-golden-sandals]]). The last verse is Hesiod's (*Theogony* 952), in the passage where the son of fair-ankled Alcmene, having finished his groaning labors, makes Hebe his wife on snowy Olympus and lives “unharmed and ageless for all days” (*Theogony* 950–955, plain renderings; see [[hesiod]]); and the Homeric Hymn to Heracles ends with the same picture, in the words of 11.603: “now he dwells happily in the fair seat of snowy Olympus, and has Hebe of the lovely ankles” (*Homeric Hymn* 15.7–8).

**The verses suspected.** The three verses were athetized and said to be the work of Onomacritus, though one scholiast seems to say this of 604 alone, the verse copied from Hesiod; Aristonicus rejected them because they divide the man into three, phantom, body and soul, a division the poet does not know (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 11.602–603; see the translation's note on 11.602–604 and [[psyche]]). A scholiast on 11.385 sums up the objections to the whole passage: how can Heracles stay here, being a god? how can the same man be in Hades and in heaven? in Homer Hebe is a maiden, who pours the gods' wine; it is not likely that he should have his gear; and how does he speak without drinking the blood? (Merry on 11.601). Merry thinks that cutting 602–604 disposes of almost all of these. Homer knew nothing of the apotheosis, for Achilles says, “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom” (*Iliad* 18.117), though the legend is very early: Merry finds it in the *Capture of Oechalia* ascribed to Creophylus, after Welcker, and reports Diodorus' statement that the Athenians were the first to honor Heracles as a god (Diodorus 4.39, as Merry quotes it). A rhapsode who feared to offend an audience that worshipped him, Merry suggests, would be tempted to add that only his ghost dwelt below, while the hero himself enjoyed divine honors among the gods; the scholiasts' Onomacritus may at least point to an Attic reviser (Merry on 11.601; see [[pisistratus|Peisistratus]] and [[minos]]). The rest he keeps: the dead in Homer keep their stature, voice and features, their armor and their wounds, Teiresias his scepter, and “Heracles is not Heracles without it; nor Orion, Orion, without his club” (Merry on 11.601; see [[orion]]). And he finds a fitness in bringing Heracles into the company, since he and Odysseus are alike, both under the particular protection of Athena (Merry on 11.601; see [[athena]]).

**The archer.** “And around him rose a clamor of the dead, as of birds / scattering in terror on every side; and he was like dark night, / holding his naked bow, with an arrow on the string, / glancing terribly around, like one ever about to shoot” (11.605–608; see [[a-clamor-as-of-birds]], [[like-night]] and [[bow]]). γυμνόν is “uncased”, out of its bow-case (Merry on 11.607); νυκτὶ ἐοικώς is a stern, lowering look, as Apollo “came on like the night” in his anger (*Iliad* 1.47; Merry on 11.606); and for αἰεὶ βαλέοντι ἐοικώς Merry sets beside it Virgil's rock that hangs over the sinners, “ever about to slip and like one falling” (*Aeneid* 6.602–603; Merry on 11.608). The four participles without a finite verb, he thinks, are an art of their own, abrupt clauses that have the very attitude of uneasy expectation (Merry on 11.608; see the translation's note on 11.606–608). The Odyssey's Heracles is an archer, as in Book 8, where Odysseus will not vie with him at the bow (8.223–225).

**The baldric.** “And terrible was the baldric around his chest, / a golden strap, on which wondrous works were wrought, / bears and wild boars and lions with flashing eyes, / combats and battles and slaughters and killings of men”, and Odysseus wishes that the craftsman who made it may never make another (11.609–614; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]] and the translation's note on 11.611, 11.613). Merry hears in the wish not praise of the work but a naive terror at the ghastly reality of the images (Merry on 11.613).

**The fellow sufferer.** “And he in turn knew me, when he saw me with his eyes” (11.615), and he greets Odysseus with the verse of Circe and of the dead, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (11.617; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]): “ah, wretch, truly you too drag out some evil fate, / such as I bore under the rays of the sun” (11.618–619; see [[wretch]]). It is a statement, not a question (Merry on 11.618; see the translation's note on 11.618–619). “I was the son of Zeus, son of Cronus, yet I had misery / boundless; for I was made subject to a man far worse than I, / and he laid hard labors upon me” (11.620–622): the worse man is Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus, to whom Heracles was bound through the spite of Hera (*Iliad* 19.97–133; Merry on 11.621; see [[eurystheus]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). One manuscript adds two mutilated lines after 622, which Cobet restored to name Eurystheus (Merry's critical note on 11.622). The labor he names is the hound: he was sent “here” to bring it, and brought it up out of Hades with Hermes and gray-eyed Athena to guide him (11.623–626; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[hermes]] and [[contests]]). Homer does not list the labors or name the dog, which is Cerberus first in Hesiod (*Theogony* 311; Merry on 11.623). “So saying, he went back again into the house of Hades” (11.627), as Achilles and Ajax had gone (Merry on 11.601).

**The phantom and the self.** Proclus finds in these verses the source of Plato's teaching about man. Homer, he says, was the first to distinguish each of us from the instruments attached to us, and to separate the images from the primary realities: “the most wise Odysseus shows it in the Nekyia, saying that he saw Heracles holding his naked bow, and adding ‘phantom’, ‘but he himself among the immortal gods’”, as if to say that the true being of Heracles lies in his soul, and that the image attached to it is its instrument and likeness, not the man. Plato's phrase “the self itself”, Proclus asks, where else does it come from than from “he himself among the immortal gods”, and his calling the body an image of the true being, from here (*On the Republic* I 172 Kroll, plain renderings; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[plato]]). Elsewhere Proclus sets Heracles against Achilles, who would rather serve for hire on earth than rule the dead: Heracles, purified by the rites, was restored entirely to the gods, as these verses say, while Achilles, a man of practical virtue, still longs for the body that served his deeds (I 119–120 Kroll; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). Plato himself borrows the words that bring Heracles on for Hippias the sophist, “And after him I caught sight of” (*Protagoras* 315b; see [[tantalus]]).
