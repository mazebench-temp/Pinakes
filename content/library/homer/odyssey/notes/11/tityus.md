**The son of Earth.** In Book 7 he is Γαιήιον υἱόν (7.324); here he is Γαίης ἐρικυδέος υἱόν, “the son of glorious Earth” (11.576; see [[earth]] and [[glorious]]). There he was in Euboea, where the Phaeacians carried Rhadamanthys to visit him; here he is among the dead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.576; see [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]]).

**Nine plethra.** He lies “on the ground” (ἐν δαπέδῳ), stretched over nine πέλεθρα (11.577). Merry explains the measure: as a length the plethron was later reckoned at a hundred Greek feet, and as an area at ten thousand square feet, but that is a later limitation; Hultsch, as Curtius reports him, took it for a certain length of plowing furrow, like the Latin *vorsus*, and “roods” would do for a translation (Merry on 11.577). The same measure is given for a god: struck by Athena's stone, Ares “covered seven plethra as he fell” (*Iliad* 21.407; see [[ares]]).

**The vultures.** “and two vultures, sitting one on either side, were tearing his liver, / plunging within the caul, and he could not ward them off with his hands” (11.578–579; see [[vultures]]). δέρτρον is properly any skin or membrane, here the caul that envelops the bowels, later called ἐπίπλοον; Apollonius' lexicon records another view, that it is the vulture's mouth, which would require δύνοντες to be taken as “plunging” (Merry on 11.579; see the translation's note on 11.579–580). The dual, γῦπε … παρημένω, makes the vultures a pair.

**The crime.** “for he had dragged off Leto, the glorious wife of Zeus, / as she went to Pytho through Panopeus with its lovely dancing floors” (11.580–581; see [[leto]], [[pytho]] and [[panopeus]]). ἕλκησε is “laid violent hands on”: the manuscripts give several forms, and La Roche prints ἕλκησε as probably Aristarchus' reading (Merry on 11.580). Merry finds κυδρὴν παράκοιτιν, “glorious wife”, somewhat out of place for Leto, since it is the distinctive title of Hera (*Iliad* 18.184; Merry on 11.581). Leto is the mother of the son of Zeus who killed the Aloadae a little earlier in the tale (11.318; see [[apollo]]). Of the three whom Odysseus sees punished, Tityus is the only one whose offense the poem tells (see [[tantalus]] and [[sisyphus]]).

**How was he seen?** A scholiast found a difficulty in the scene: “How did the one lying there come out? And if he did not come out, how was he seen?” (Merry on 11.581). Odysseus sits by the pit and questions the souls that come to the blood, but from Minos on he describes what is happening in the depths of Erebus, as an eyewitness would; this is one of Merry's reasons for thinking the passage a later addition (Merry on 11.565; see [[minos]] and the translation's note on 11.565–567).

**Later tellings.** Virgil's Sibyl tells Aeneas of Tityos, “the nursling of Earth, the mother of all”, whose body is stretched over nine whole *iugera*; a single monstrous vulture with a hooked beak crops his immortal liver and searches his entrails for its feasts, and the fibers that grow again are given no rest (*Aeneid* 6.595–600, plain renderings; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]). Lucretius keeps the nine *iugera*, and makes the birds that tear him the anguish of love and the cares of other desires (*On the Nature of Things* 3.984–994; see [[lucretius-the-punishments-of-acheron]]). Plato's Socrates names him, with Tantalus and Sisyphus, among the kings and potentates whom Homer shows punished forever (*Gorgias* 525e; see [[tantalus]]).
