---
summary: The θυμός feels, wishes and leaves at death. Odysseus pities the dead “in my heart” (11.55, 11.87, 11.395), and his heart bids him clasp his mother (11.206); the dead girls have hearts “new to sorrow” (11.39); Poseidon has laid up a grudge in his heart (11.102); sickness takes the spirit from the limbs, and longing robbed Anticleia of her honey-sweet life (11.200–203); and at death the spirit leaves the white bones before the fire consumes them (11.221).
---

**Feeling and will.** Most of the book's θυμός is the seat of feeling. Odysseus weeps and pities the dead “in my heart” (ἐλέησά τε θυμῷ, 11.55, 11.87, 11.395; see [[pity]]), and his heart bids him clasp his mother's soul (θυμὸς ἀνώγει, 11.206). Poseidon “has laid up a grudge against you in his heart” (κότον ἔνθετο θυμῷ, 11.102; see [[kotos]] and [[poseidon]]), and Teiresias tells Odysseus that he may still come home “if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'” (11.105; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Penelope waits “with enduring heart” (τετληότι θυμῷ, 11.181; see [[penelope]]), the girls among the dead have hearts “new to sorrow” (νεοπενθέα θυμόν, 11.39; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, νεοπενθής), and Odysseus tells Ajax to “tame your might and your proud spirit” (11.562; see [[ajax]] and [[menos]]).

**The life.** θυμός is also the life. Anticleia did not die of the sickness “such as most often / takes the spirit from the limbs with hateful wasting”, ἐξείλετο being a gnomic aorist of what commonly happens (11.200–201; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.201; see [[nousos]]); longing for her son “robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (μελιηδέα θυμὸν ἀπηύρα, 11.202–203; see [[pothe]]). And at death the fire consumes flesh and bones “as soon as the spirit has left the white bones” (λίπῃ λεύκʼ ὀστέα θυμός, 11.221), while the ψυχή flies off like a dream (11.222; see [[psyche]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]). Merry connects θυμός with the root of “smoke” (Sanskrit *dhūmas*, Latin *fumus*), notes Plato's derivation of the word “from the raging and boiling of the soul” (ἀπὸ τῆς θύσεως καὶ ζέσεως τῆς ψυχῆς, *Cratylus* 419e), and gives its general meaning as feeling, temper, affection or desire, seated in the breast or the φρένες, and often the vital principle itself, as here (Merry on 11.221; see [[phrenes]] and [[stethos]]).
