summary: His soul comes with those of Achilles, Patroclus and Antilochus, “the best in looks and form / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus”. **The best after Achilles.** His soul comes with the others of the Iliad, “and of Ajax, who was the best in looks and form / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (11.469–470; see [[achilles]], [[best-in-looks]] and [[danaans]]), and Odysseus praises him again in the verses that the Iliad gave him over the body of Patroclus, “Ajax, who in looks and in deeds stood out above the rest / of all the Danaans, after the noble son of Peleus” (11.550–551, as *Iliad* 17.279–280): περὶ μέν, “superior in form and in achievements to the other Greeks” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.550).

**Apart.** The other souls stand grieving, each asking about its own sorrows (11.541–542):

{{quote:e9ac9cf0-de45-56eb-bd11-bd4c30253d6c}}

Merry renders, “Alone of them all, the soul of Ajax son of Telamon stood aloof, enraged because of the victory which I won over him, when defending my right at the ships about the arms of Achilles. His lady-mother put them up for a prize, and the sons of the Trojans gave the sentence, and Pallas Athena” (Merry on 11.543; see [[thetis]], [[athena]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]). ἔθηκε is “set up as a prize”, as Achilles sets prizes for the games (*Iliad* 23.262–265; Merry on 11.546), and ἀέθλῳ is the prize itself, “for such a prize”, as ἕνεκʼ αὐτῶν, “because of them”, shows, referring back to the arms (Merry on 11.548; see the translation's note on 11.548 and [[contests]]). κατέσχεν is “covered, buried” (Merry on 11.549): the Odyssey does not say how he died. Aristarchus rejected 11.547, the verse of the judges, and Merry gives Nitzsch's reasons: the Trojan captives would be the most unlikely umpires in a dispute between two Achaean chieftains; Homer would say κοῦροι or υἷες, not παῖδες Τρώων; the verse is superfluous; and Odysseus would scarcely have made Athena the author of so disastrous a decision (Merry on 11.547; see the translation's note on 11.547). In the *Aethiopis*, as Merry tells the Cyclic story, the Trojan captives decide the prize, and in the *Little Iliad* spies hear two Trojan girls by the walls, one of whom, under Athena's influence, gives it to Odysseus; Pindar and Sophocles hint at treachery in the decision (Merry on 11.543; see the translation's note on 11.547).

**The appeal.**

{{quote:def344c2-844f-536b-9deb-690546cfa0a6}}

μειλιχίοισιν, “with soothing words”, is the manner of the whole speech (11.552; see [[soothing-words]]). The emphasis lies on θεοί: you need not visit your anger on me, Merry paraphrases, it was the gods who offered these arms as a prize, a beginning of sorrow for the Greeks, “for so mighty a stronghold was lost to them in thee”; πύργος, a tower, is said of a man as in the Psalm (Merry on 11.555; see [[like-a-tower]] and the translation's key words). σεῖο … ἀχνύμεθα is “we evermore sorrow for thee, fallen in death, as sorely as for Achilles” (Merry on 11.556). In οὐδέ τις ἄλλος αἴτιος, ἀλλὰ Ζεύς, the logic would be “no one else is to blame, but Zeus is to blame, who hated”: Zeus was really guilty, because in his hatred of the Danaans he laid doom on Ajax (Merry on 11.559; see [[zeus]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). He ends by asking him to “tame your might and your proud spirit” (11.562; see [[menos]] and [[proud]]).

**The silence.** “So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, but went after the other / souls of the dead who have died, into Erebus” (11.563–564; see [[erebus]] and [[the-dead-who-have-died]]). Odysseus adds that even so, angry as he was, Ajax would have spoken to him, or he to Ajax, if his heart had not wished to see the souls of the others (11.565–567); Merry suspects these verses, in which ὅμως, “still”, is found nowhere else in the Odyssey, and thinks that the later passage that follows begins with them (Merry on 11.565; see the translation's note on 11.565–567). For the silence as later readers took it, see the entry.

**His names in this book.** Αἴαντος ψυχὴ Τελαμωνιάδαο, “the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon” (11.543), and Αἶαν, παῖ Τελαμῶνος ἀμύμονος, “Ajax, son of noble Telamon” (11.553; see [[telamon]] and [[telamonian]]); ἄναξ, “my lord” (11.561; see [[anax]]).
