summary: The book begins and ends its scenes in tears: the companions go aboard “grieving, shedding big tears” (11.5); Odysseus weeps at the sight of Elpenor, his mother and Agamemnon (11.55, 11.87, 11.395); Elpenor lies “unwept” (11.54, 11.72); Penelope's nights and days waste away in tears (11.183); Agamemnon weeps aloud (11.391); and in the wooden horse every leader wept but Neoptolemus (11.526–530).
# δάκρυσα (11.55, 11.87, 11.395), in the verse "When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart".
grc: [δάκρυσα]
en: [wept]
---

**Setting out.** The companions go aboard for the land of the dead “grieving, shedding big tears” (11.5), their sorrow caused by the dreadful journey ahead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.5; see [[achos]]); Book 10 ended with the same verse, as they went down to the ship (10.570).

**At the pit.** Three times a verse joins weeping and pity: “When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart”, of Elpenor, of his mother and of Agamemnon (11.55, 11.87, 11.395; see [[pity]]). The dead weep too. Agamemnon “wept loudly, letting big tears fall”, stretching out hands that could not reach him (11.390–394), and the two of them stand “grieving, shedding big tears” as they talk (11.465–466; see [[agamemnon]]). Of Elpenor, left “unwept and unburied” (ἄκλαυτον, 11.54, 11.72), the tears are owed and not yet paid (see [[lament]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Anticleia tells of the tears at home: Penelope waits in the halls, and “always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (11.181–183; see [[penelope]]), verses that Athena will repeat to Odysseus in Ithaca (13.337–338).

**The man who did not weep.** In the wooden horse the other leaders of the Danaans “were wiping away their tears, and the limbs of each trembled beneath him”, but Odysseus never saw Neoptolemus turn pale or wipe a tear from his cheeks (11.526–530; see [[neoptolemus]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). For the warriors of the Iliad tears are no shame; here they are set beside the trembling of fear, and the young man who has neither is praised to his father.
