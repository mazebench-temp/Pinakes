---
summary: Artemis is the death that Odysseus supposes for his mother: “Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis of the showering arrows / come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?” (11.172–173). Anticleia answers that it was neither the goddess nor a sickness, but longing for her son (11.197–203). In the catalogue of heroines Artemis killed Ariadne in Dia, “on the testimony of Dionysus” (11.324–325).
# Unnamed: "the keen-sighted goddess of the showering arrows" (11.198-199).
refs: [11.198-199]
---

**The death of women.** Odysseus asks his mother how she died: “What fate of death that lays men low brought you down? / Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis of the showering arrows / come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?” (11.171–173; see [[anticleia]], [[that-lays-men-low]], [[of-the-showering-arrows]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). He offers the two common ends of a woman's life, a slow sickness or a sudden death from the goddess's arrows, as Apollo's are for men. The arrows are “gentle” because the death they bring is painless: Merry, arguing that the death foretold for Odysseus himself is too slow to be said to “kill” him, observes that a painless sudden death is called not ἀβληχρός but ἀγανός, in the familiar phrase of the gentle arrows (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.134; see the Book 5 note). The Odyssey sets the two deaths side by side again on the island of [[syrie|Syrie]], where no hateful sickness comes upon men, but when they grow old Apollo comes with Artemis and kills them with his gentle arrows (15.407–411; see [[apollo]]).

**Not the archer goddess.** Anticleia answers with the same verse: “it was not the keen-sighted goddess of the showering arrows who in my halls / came upon me with her gentle arrows and killed me, / nor did any sickness come upon me, such as most often / takes the spirit from the limbs with hateful wasting; / but longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus, / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (11.198–203; see [[nousos]] and [[pothe]]). The goddess is not named in the answer. ἐύσκοπος, “keen-sighted”, is elsewhere a title of Hermes beside the slayer of Argus (*Iliad* 24.24, 24.109; 1.38, 7.137), and only here of Artemis (see [[keen-sighted]]).

**Ariadne.** Among the heroines Odysseus sees “beautiful Ariadne, / the daughter of baleful Minos, whom Theseus once / was bringing from Crete to the hill of sacred Athens, / but he had no joy of her; for before that Artemis slew her / in sea-washed Dia, on the testimony of Dionysus” (11.321–325; see [[ariadne]], [[theseus]], [[dia]] and [[dionysus]]). Merry gives the explanations of the testimony: some suppose that Dionysus told Artemis that Theseus had lain with Ariadne in her sacred grove in Dia, or that Ariadne, promised to Dionysus, had given herself to a mortal lover (Merry on 11.322). Aristophanes read ἔσχε, “stopped her”, for ἔκτα, “slew her”, which Merry thinks probably the original reading: the goddess would then only have kept her from going on with Theseus (Merry on 11.324 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 11.321–325). The killing is Artemis' second in the Odyssey's tales of the old days, after Orion, whom she killed with her gentle arrows in Ortygia (5.121–124; see [[orion]]). Merry thinks the verses 11.321–325, with their Attic legend, a later interpolation (Merry on 11.321).
