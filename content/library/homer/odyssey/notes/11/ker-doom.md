---
summary: Odysseus asks his mother and then Agamemnon the same question, “What fate of death that lays men low brought you down?” (11.171, 11.398), and offers each the deaths that might have come: a long sickness or the arrows of Artemis; Poseidon's storm, or enemies on land.
---

The question is the same verse both times, τίς νύ σε κὴρ ἐδάμασσε τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο; (11.171, 11.398; see [[that-lays-men-low]]). κὴρ θανάτοιο is the particular form of death that comes to each man, as against the μοῖρα that is common to all; Merry compares Sarpedon's fates of death that “stand over us, / countless” (*Iliad* 12.326–327) and Eurylochus' “All deaths are hateful to wretched mortals” in Book 12 (12.341, a plain rendering; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.171; see [[moira]] and [[thanatos]]). So the question lists the possible deaths. For his mother: “Was it a long sickness, or did Artemis of the showering arrows / come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?” (11.172–173; see [[nousos]], [[artemis]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). For Agamemnon: Poseidon at sea, “rousing a blast of grievous winds”, or merciless men on land as he was raiding their cattle or fighting for a city (11.399–403; see [[raiding]]). Neither guess is right. Anticleia died of longing for her son (11.197–203), and Agamemnon was killed at a feast by Aegisthus and his own wife (11.405–411; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).
