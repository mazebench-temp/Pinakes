**Three Attic names.** Phaedra, Procris and Ariadne come together in one verse (11.321; see [[phaedra]] and [[procris]]). W. W. Merry notes that the three belong to Attic legend and break the run of Thessalian and Boeotian stories, and judges 11.321–325 “probably a later interpolation”, which, like other passages alluding to Athenian legend, may belong to the time of the Peisistratean recension (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.321; see the translation's note on 11.321–325). With Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle, he says, the catalogue returns to Boeotia (on 11.326).

**The daughter of Minos.** She is καλή, “beautiful”, and κούρη Μίνωος ὀλοόφρονος, “the daughter of baleful Minos” (11.321–322; see [[minos]] and [[oloophron]]). Merry renders ὀλοόφρων “evil-hearted” and thinks it may remember the cruel tribute that Minos laid on Athens (on 11.322); in this book's last sights the same Minos is the splendid son of Zeus, giving the law to the dead (11.568–571). Theseus “was bringing” her ἐκ Κρήτης ἐς γουνὸν Ἀθηνάων ἱεράων, “from Crete to the hill of sacred Athens” (11.323; see [[theseus]], [[crete]] and [[athens]]); γουνός, elsewhere the slope of a vineyard (1.193, 11.193), is Merry's “soil” (see the translation's row γουνός (of a city)).

**He had no joy of her.** ἦγε μέν, οὐδʼ ἀπόνητο, “but he had no joy of her” (11.324), is said in the same shape of Argos, the dog that Odysseus reared but had no joy of, because before that he went to sacred Ilios: θρέψε μέν, οὐδʼ ἀπόνητο, πάρος δʼ εἰς Ἴλιον ἱρήν (17.293, a plain rendering), and Telemachus says it of his father, who left him an only son in his halls and had no joy of him (16.120); Merry compares both (on 11.324). For ἀπόνητο some copies, a scholion says, wrote ἐτέλεσσεν (Merry's critical note on 11.324).

**Artemis in Dia.** πάρος δέ μιν Ἄρτεμις ἔκτα / Δίῃ ἐν ἀμφιρύτῃ Διονύσου μαρτυρίῃσιν, “for before that Artemis slew her / in sea-washed Dia, on the testimony of Dionysus” (11.324–325; see [[artemis]], [[dia]], [[sea-washed]] and [[dionysus]]). Artemis kills women in Homer: of Laodameia, the mother of Sarpedon, the Iliad says that “Artemis of the golden reins killed his daughter in her anger” (*Iliad* 6.205), and Odysseus asks his mother whether Artemis came upon her with her gentle arrows (11.172–173; see [[gentle-arrows]]). Aristophanes read ἔσχε, “held her back”, for ἔκτα, “slew”; Eustathius gives both readings, and Merry thinks ἔσχε probably the original, rendering it “stopped her”, that is, let her go no further with Theseus, rather than, with Eustathius, held her fast in death (his note and critical note on 11.324; see the translation's note on 11.321–325). Dia, Merry says, is the islet off Heracleion on the north coast of Crete, which Theseus would pass on the way to Athens, not the Dia of the common account, Naxos, where Theseus deserted her and Dionysus took her to wife (on 11.322). What Dionysus testified is not said. Some, Merry reports, supposed that he told Artemis that Theseus had lain with Ariadne in her sacred grove in Dia, others that Ariadne had been promised to Dionysus and gave herself to a mortal lover (on 11.322). The form Διονύσου is found only here; elsewhere Homer has Διώνυσος (Merry on 11.325; *Iliad* 6.132).

**Plutarch's verse.** The book's other Athenian verse, “Theseus and Peirithous, glorious children of the gods” (11.631), was said by Hereas of Megara, as Plutarch reports, to have been added by Peisistratus to please the Athenians (see [[theseus-line]] and the general article).
