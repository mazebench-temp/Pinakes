summary: “So we two sat exchanging hateful words” (11.81), and the same verse with “stood” (11.465); Anticleia did not die of a sickness, “such as most often / takes the spirit from the limbs with hateful wasting” (11.200–201); and Odysseus saw “hateful Eriphyle, / who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (11.326–327).
grc: [στυγεροῖσιν]
---

ἐπέεσσιν … στυγεροῖσιν closes Odysseus' talk with Elpenor and with Agamemnon (11.81, 11.465; see [[meeting-with-the-dead]]). Merry takes the word here for “lamentable,” as of Eurylochus' answer “with a hateful word” in the next book (12.278, a plain rendering; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.81); the translation keeps “hateful,” its word for στυγερός everywhere (see the translation's note on 11.81–82). The wasting sickness is set beside the gentle arrows of Artemis as the two common deaths of a woman (11.172–173, 11.198–201; see [[gentle-arrows]]), and Eriphyle's epithet is the only word the tale spends on her besides her crime (see [[eriphyle]]).
