---
summary: The dog of Book 11 is in Agamemnon's words for his wife: she is κυνῶπις, “the dog-faced one”, and “there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless”, κύντερον, “than a woman / who puts such deeds as these into her heart” (11.424–428); the hound that Heracles brought up from Hades is the other dog of the book (11.623–625).
---

**More doglike.** Agamemnon tells how Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him, and how “she, the dog-faced one, / turned away, and did not bring herself, though I was going to the house of Hades, / to close my eyes with her hands or to shut my mouth” (11.424–426; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[dog-face]]); then he generalizes: ὣς οὐκ αἰνότερον καὶ κύντερον ἄλλο γυναικός, / ἥ τις δὴ τοιαῦτα μετὰ φρεσὶν ἔργα βάληται, “So there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman / who puts such deeds as these into her heart” (11.427–428; see [[anaideia]] and [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). κύντερος, “more doglike”, is a comparative formed from κύων, “dog”, and means more shameless: Zeus says it to Hera, “since there is nothing more shameless than you” (*Iliad* 8.483), Odysseus says it of his hunger, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly” (7.216), and he says it to his own heart in a later book, bidding it endure, for it once endured something more shameless still (20.18; see the entry). W. W. Merry sets the sentiment beside Hesiod's saying that nothing is more chilling than a bad wife, Hippolytus' that a woman is a great evil, and Medea's that women are most helpless for good and cleverest contrivers of every evil (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.427; Hesiod, *Works and Days* 702–703; Euripides, *Hippolytus* 627, *Medea* 407–409).

**The hound of Hades.** Heracles tells how the man far worse than he, whom he served, once sent him down to the land of the dead “to bring the hound”, κύνʼ ἄξοντʼ, the hardest of his labors, and how he led it up out of Hades with the help of Hermes and Athena (11.623–626; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]] and [[heracles]]). Homer does not name the dog. Hesiod does: Cerberus, “the raw-eating, bronze-voiced hound of Hades, / fifty-headed, shameless and strong” (*Theogony* 311–312; see the story).
