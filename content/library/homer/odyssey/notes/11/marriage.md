**Penelope.** Odysseus asks his mother about his “wedded wife, her plan and her thought, / whether she stays beside her son and keeps all things safe, / or whether whoever is best of the Achaeans has already married her” (11.177–179; see [[penelope]]). Teiresias has already told him of the suitors “wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (11.117; see [[bride-price]] and [[the-suitors]]). Agamemnon remembers her as “a young bride” when they left for the war (νύμφην, 11.447).

**The wives of the catalogue.** The women whom Persephone sends are “the wives and daughters of chieftains” (ἀριστήων ἄλοχοι ἠδὲ θύγατρες, 11.227), and each is named by her marriage as by her birth: Tyro “the wife of Cretheus”, Alcmene “the wife of Amphitryon”, Megara whom Heracles “had as wife”, Chloris whom Neleus married “for her beauty, after giving a countless bride-price”, Leda “the wife of Tyndareus”, Iphimedeia “the wife of Aloeus” (11.237, 11.266, 11.270, 11.281–282, 11.298, 11.305; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). Their sons are often a god's: Tyro bore Pelias and Neleus to Poseidon, and Alcmene Heracles to Zeus (see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The worst marriage of the list is Epicaste's, who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind, “in marrying her own son” (γημαμένη ᾧ υἷι, 11.273–274; see [[jocasta]] and [[oedipus-kills-laius]]). Leto too is named as “the glorious wife of Zeus” (Διὸς κυδρὴν παράκοιτιν, 11.580), words Merry finds somewhat inappropriate to her, since they are Hera's distinctive title (*Iliad* 18.184; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.580; see [[leto]] and [[hera]]).

**Agamemnon's wife.** Agamemnon was killed by Aegisthus “with my ruinous wife” (11.410), and Clytemnestra contrived “slaughter for her wedded husband” (κουριδίῳ … πόσει, 11.430). κουρίδιος belongs to κοῦρος in the sense of “free-born, gentle”, not “young”: κουρίδιος πόσις and κουριδίη ἄλοχος mark the honorable bond of a husband and wife of the same high station, as Briseis recalls Patroclus' promise to make her Achilles' “wedded wife” and give the marriage feast in Phthia, against the lot of a concubine (*Iliad* 19.297–299; Merry on 11.430; see [[posis]] and [[clytemnestra]]). His wife did not even let him see his son (ἄκοιτις, 11.452), and from her he draws the counsel against all wives (see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). His companions were slaughtered like hogs killed in a rich man's house “for a wedding, or a shared feast, or a rich banquet” (11.413–415; see [[feast]]).
