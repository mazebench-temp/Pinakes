# ἑταίρου (11.51, 11.83).
grc: [ἑταίρου]
en: [companion]
---

**At the pit.** The companions do the work of the rite: they take the sheep aboard, Perimedes and Eurylochus hold the victims, and the others flay and burn them while Odysseus keeps the dead from the blood (11.4, 11.23–24, 11.44–47; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]], [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]); at the end they go aboard and loose the stern-cables (11.636–638). Even Circe's wind is “a good companion” (ἐσθλὸν ἑταῖρον, 11.7; see [[the-winds]]).

**The lost companion.** “And first came the soul of my companion Elpenor”, and Odysseus calls him so again as they talk across the blood, “the phantom of my companion” (11.51, 11.83; see [[elpenor]]); he asks for the oar “with which I rowed when I was alive, among my companions” (11.78).

**The companions to be lost.** Teiresias makes their survival depend on the cattle of the Sun and on Odysseus restraining “your own heart and your companions'”, and foretells that he will come home “late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (11.105–114; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). Alcinous asks whether he saw any of his godlike companions who died at Troy (11.371–372; Merry takes these as the chieftains, W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.369), and Odysseus promises “the sorrows of my companions, who perished afterward” (11.382). Agamemnon's companions were killed around him at the feast (11.412), and Eurypylus' around him at Troy (11.520).
