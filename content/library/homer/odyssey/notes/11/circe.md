---
summary: Circe sends the ship on its way to the dead with a following wind, “Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (11.6–8), and Odysseus carries out her instructions at “the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22). The soul of Elpenor, whose body lies unburied “in the hall of Circe” (11.53, 11.62), knows that the ship will put in again at her island, and asks to be remembered there (11.69–71).
---

**The following wind.** The book opens with Circe's gift: “And behind our dark-prowed ship / she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion, / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (11.6–8; see [[the-winds]], [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]). The verse of her titles is the one with which Odysseus named her when the ship first came to Aeaea (10.136) and with which he will name her when she sends the ship off again (12.150); the text here prints εὐπλόκαμος where the other two have ἐυπλόκαμος (see the translation's note on 11.8).

**Her instructions.** The men go along the stream of Oceanus “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (11.22; see [[oceanus]]). φράσε, by the rule of Aristarchus, refers not to her actual words but to the signs and marks by which the spot might be known; the book says nothing more of the shore and the groves of Persephone, or of the meeting of the rivers (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.22). The rite at the pit follows her instructions verse for verse, now in the first person (11.25–50 after 10.516–537; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and the translation's note on 10.516–537). One thing she foretold does not happen as she said. She told Odysseus that the seer would tell him “the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming” (10.539–540; see the Book 10 note), and Merry observes that Teiresias may perhaps be said to have described the measures of the path, but not the way (Merry on 11.136; see [[teiresias]]). Circe herself will tell him the way, past the Sirens and Scylla, when he comes back to her island (12.37–141).

**Elpenor in her hall.** The first soul to come is Elpenor's, “for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.53–54; see [[elpenor]] and [[unwept-and-unburied]]). He tells how he fell from her roof: “Lying down in the hall of Circe I did not think / to come back down by going to the long ladder” (11.62–63), and he knows what lies ahead: “for I know that going from here, out of the house of Hades, / you will put in with your well-made ship at the island of Aeaea” (11.69–70; see [[aeaea]]). There Odysseus buries him, and Circe comes to the shore to greet the men who have come back from Hades (12.8–22).
