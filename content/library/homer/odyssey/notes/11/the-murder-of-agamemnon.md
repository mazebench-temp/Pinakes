summary: The fourth telling, and the first by the dead king himself (11.385–466). Aegisthus killed him at the feast “with my ruinous wife”, like an ox at the manger, his companions were slaughtered around him, and Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him and would not close his eyes. From his death he draws a judgment on women and a warning to Odysseus, and he asks for news of his son.

**The fourth telling.** Zeus told the murder to the gods (1.35–43), Nestor told the seduction and the vengeance (3.254–312), and Menelaus told what Proteus told him of the homecoming and the feast (4.512–537). Here the dead man tells it, and he is the first to give his wife a hand in it: Aegisthus killed him “with my ruinous wife”, σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ (11.409–410; see [[aegisthus]] and [[clytemnestra]]). The Homeric version, Merry notes, makes Aegisthus the murderer and a banquet the scene, where Aeschylus and Euripides have Clytemnestra kill him in the bath; but even here she is the accomplice (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.409). In Menelaus' telling no one survived on either side, and the story there differs from the form here and in the last book, where Clytemnestra plays so great a part (Merry on 4.536; see the translation's note on 4.536). Odysseus has already named her, before the tale resumed, as the evil woman by whose will the companions perished in their homecoming (11.382–384; Merry on 11.384; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**Not by the sea.** Odysseus asks whether Poseidon wrecked him among his ships, or men killed him on land as he drove off their cattle or fought for their city (11.397–403; see [[agamemnon]]); Aristophanes athetized 11.399–403 as carried over from the answer (see the translation's note on 11.399–403). The answer turns both away in the same verses (11.406–408): “but Aegisthus, contriving death and fate for me, / killed me, with my ruinous wife, when he had called me to his house / and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger” (11.409–411; 11.411 = 4.535; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]). The asyndeton of δειπνίσσας, “feasted me”, is meant: it suits the outburst of indignation and sorrow (Merry on 11.411).

**The hall.** The companions were killed around him like white-tusked hogs slaughtered for a wedding or a feast in a rich man's house (11.412–415). Odysseus has seen many men killed in battle, but he would have grieved most at that sight, the dead lying among the mixing bowl and the laden tables, “and the whole floor seethed with blood” (11.416–420; θῦεν, “swam, bubbled”, Merry on 11.420; see the translation's key-word row for θύω and [[mixing-bowl]]).

**Cassandra.** “And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter, / Cassandra, whom scheming Clytemnestra killed / beside me” (11.421–423; see [[cassandra]] and [[schemer]]). ἀμφʼ ἐμοί is “close by me”, or “as she clung about me”: there is no difficulty, Merry thinks, in supposing that she came in with Agamemnon and sat at his side, her hated presence adding jealousy to his wife's bitterness (on 11.423). The English of the next verses follows the reading Merry takes from Königinhoff through Seiler: Agamemnon raised his hands, as if to protect the captive or to beg for her, and let them fall to the ground as he lay dying with the sword through him, and “she, the dog-faced one, / turned away”, not even closing his eyes and mouth as he went to the house of Hades (11.423–426; see the translation's note on 11.423–424 and [[dog-face]]).

**The judgment on women.** From his death Agamemnon draws a judgment: there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman who puts such deeds into her heart (11.427–430), which Merry compares with Hesiod's saying that a man wins nothing better than a good wife, and nothing more chilling than a bad one (on 11.427; *Works and Days* 702–703; see [[hesiod]]). “Truly I thought / that I would come home welcome to my children and my slaves” (11.430–431): he studiously avoids naming his wife among those who would welcome him (Merry on 11.430). She has poured shame on herself and on the women to come, “even on one who does good” (11.432–434), the verse with which his soul, in the last book, will end its praise of Penelope (24.202, Greek only; see [[penelope]]). Odysseus answers that Zeus has long hated the house of Atreus “through the plans of women”, Helen's and Clytemnestra's (11.436–439; Merry on 11.437; see [[helen]]); Aristophanes athetized these verses (see the translation's note on 11.435–440).

**The warning.** Agamemnon bids Odysseus never to be kind even to his wife, nor tell her all he knows (11.441–443), and then excepts Penelope, “for she has great good sense” (11.444–446). He remembers the infant Telemachus, whom he will never see grown as he never saw his own son: “But my wife did not even let me have my fill of my son / with my eyes; before that she killed me myself” (11.447–453; see [[telemachus]]). His last counsel is to put in to his own land in secret, “for there is no trusting women any more” (11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]), verses that the scholia say were missing in most copies, as conflicting with the praise of Penelope (see the translation's note on 11.454–456). Odysseus does come home in secret, as a beggar.

**The question.** Last he asks for news of Orestes, at Orchomenus, at Pylos or with Menelaus in Sparta (11.457–461; see [[orestes]]). Odysseus knows nothing, “and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (11.463–464; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]). The poem's audience already knows the answer from Nestor: in the eighth year Orestes came back from Athens and killed Aegisthus (3.306–310; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). The two stand weeping until the soul of Achilles comes (11.465–467). In the second journey to the dead the soul of Agamemnon tells its death once more, to the soul of Achilles: in his homecoming Zeus devised a grim destruction for him at the hands of Aegisthus and his ruinous wife (24.96–97, a plain rendering).
