summary: Hebe is named once in Book 11, as the wife of Heracles among the gods. Of Heracles Odysseus sees only “his phantom; but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts, and has Hebe of the lovely ankles, / the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (11.602–604).

**The wife of Heracles.** The verses are the only place in Homer where Hebe is married, or given parents: in the Iliad she is the gods' servant on Olympus, who pours their nectar (*Iliad* 4.2–3; see the general article and [[cupbearing]]). Here she is καλλίσφυρος, “of the lovely ankles” (see [[of-the-lovely-ankles]]), and her mother is Hera “of the golden sandals” (see [[hera]], [[of-the-golden-sandals]] and [[zeus]]). A scholium objected, among other things, that Heracles cannot be a god and stay among the dead, or be at once in Hades and in heaven, and that in Homer Hebe is a maiden, which is why she pours the wine; Aristonicus rejected the verses because they divide the man into phantom, body and soul (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.601 and his critical note on 11.602–603). Merry would cut 11.602–604 alone. Homer, he says, knew nothing of the legend of the apotheosis, for in the Iliad “not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom” (*Iliad* 18.117), and the cutting disposes of the later story of the apotheosis and of the marriage of Hebe (Merry on 11.601; see [[heracles]] and [[immortality]]).

**In Hesiod.** The marriage is Hesiod's: Hera bore Hebe, Ares and Eileithyia to Zeus (*Theogony* 921–923), and Heracles, when he had finished his groaning labors, made Hebe, “the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals”, his wife on snowy Olympus, and lives free from harm and ageless for all his days (*Theogony* 950–955; see [[hesiod]]). The verse of her parentage is the same in both poems (11.604 = *Theogony* 952).
