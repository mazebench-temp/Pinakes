---
summary: Tyro is the first of the women whom Odysseus questions at the pit, and her story the longest (11.235–259): the daughter of Salmoneus and wife of Cretheus loved the river Enipeus; Poseidon took its likeness and lay with her at its mouth, hidden by an arching wave, bade her rejoice and keep silent, and named himself; she bore him Pelias and Neleus, and to Cretheus Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon. Homer tells nothing of the exposure of the twins or of their recognition.

**The first of the women.** “There the first I saw was Tyro, daughter of a noble father” (11.235; see [[tyro]] and [[daughter-of-a-noble-father]]). Each woman declares her own descent (11.233–234), and Tyro “said” she was the offspring of noble Salmoneus and the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus (11.236–237; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]); for ἀμύμονος, “noble”, some read ἀτασθάλου, “reckless” (Merry's critical note on 11.236; see the translation's note on 11.233, 11.236), the Salmoneus whom Virgil puts among the punished for mimicking the thunder of Jupiter (*Aeneid* 6.585–594). Cretheus and Salmoneus were brothers, both sons of Aeolus, so that Tyro, like Arete, married her uncle, and her name, from τυρός, means “curd-white”, “fair-faced” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.235; see [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The river.** She fell in love with the river Enipeus, “which is by far the fairest of the rivers that run upon the earth”, and went often to its streams (11.238–240; see [[enipeus]]). The Enipeus that Merry knows rises in Mount Othrys in Thessaly and falls into the Peneus; since Salmoneus had homes in Thessaly and in Elis, the river of his Elean city Salmone bore the same name (on 11.235). The earth-shaker took the river's likeness (11.241): for the love of a river god and a mortal woman Merry compares the Axius and Periboea and the Spercheus and Polydora (on 11.241; *Iliad* 21.141–143, 16.174–176). ἐν προχοῇς is the mouth of the river, used loosely, since the Enipeus falls into the Peneus and not into the sea (Merry on 11.242), though the god leaves by plunging beneath the sea (11.253).

**The wave.** “And the surging wave stood around them, high as a mountain, / arched over, and hid the god and the mortal woman” (11.243–244; see [[poseidon]]). Merry compares the river that curved like a mountain around Aristaeus and took him down into its wide fold (*Georgics* 4.360–362), with Conington's explanation of a wave swelling to the height of a mountain and leaving a hollow beneath in which a man might hide, and Eustathius' description of the wave as a chamber's roof (on 11.244). The next verse, “And he loosed her maiden girdle, and shed sleep upon her” (11.245), was athetized: why should the god pour sleep on a willing woman? It is in all the manuscripts, and every editor but La Roche brackets it (see the translation's note on 11.245). The scholia object too that a maiden's girdle does not suit the wife of Cretheus. Hayman suggested that the sleep gave the god time to resume his own form, as Aphrodite pours sleep on Anchises before she shows herself in her divine beauty (Merry on 11.245; *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 170–175; see [[girdle]]).

**The god's words.** “Rejoice in this love, lady; and as the year circles round / you will bear splendid children, for not in vain are the embraces / of the immortals” (11.248–250): χαῖρε is a blessing, and Merry reports Favorinus' explanation, in Gellius, of the year “circling round” as a year near its end, since the child is born before the year is out (on 11.248; see the translation's note on 11.248–249). εὐναί are “embraces”, and ἀποφώλιοι, which Autenrieth took as “without offspring”, is “in vain” (Merry on 11.249). The god bids her go home and keep silent, and gives his name: “but I, you must know, am Poseidon the earth-shaker” (11.251–252). Then he plunges into the sea (11.253, the verse of Eidothea and of Proteus in the fourth book).

**The sons.** “And she conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus, / who became mighty attendants of great Zeus”, θεράποντε Διός, that is, kings (11.254–255; Merry on 11.255; see [[pelias]] and [[neleus]]). Pelias ruled in Iolcus, Neleus in sandy Pylos (11.256–257; see [[iolcus]] and [[pylos]]), and to Cretheus she bore Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon (11.258–259). Merry tells the sequel from the later legend: after Cretheus' death Pelias refused his half-brother Aeson a share in the kingdom, drove out Neleus, and sent Aeson's son Jason after the golden fleece; Pheres founded Pherae; and Amythaon was the father of Bias and Melampus (on 11.259; see [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]). The house of Neleus returns with Chloris and her children (11.281–297).
