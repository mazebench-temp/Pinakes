---
summary: The μένος of fire consumes the bodies of the dead (11.220); Heracles was “ever unyielding in his might” (11.270); Achilles wishes he could come back with his might and irresistible hands to defend his father (11.502); Neoptolemus ran out in front, “yielding to no one in his might” (11.515); and Odysseus asks Ajax to tame his might and his proud spirit (11.562).
---

The μένος of the book is the force of the living and of fire. Anticleia tells how “the strong might of the blazing fire” overcomes flesh and bones once the spirit has gone (11.220; see [[cremation]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]). Heracles, the son of Amphitryon, is “ever unyielding in his might” (μένος αἰὲν ἀτειρής, 11.270), ἀτειρής meaning “sturdy”, literally “unwearying”, the word Paris uses of Hector's heart, “always unyielding, like an axe” (*Iliad* 3.60; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.270; see [[unyielding]] and [[heracles]]). The dead have lost it: Achilles, among them, wishes he could come to his father's house even for a little while, such as he was at Troy, and “make my might and my irresistible hands hateful” to those who wrong him (11.498–503; see [[achilles]], [[peleus]] and [[irresistible-hands]]); Agamemnon has no steadfast strength left in his supple limbs (11.393–394). Neoptolemus at Troy ran far out in front, “yielding to no one in his might”, τὸ ὃν μένος, “in that spirit of his” (11.515; Merry on 11.515; see [[neoptolemus]]), and Odysseus begs Ajax to “tame your might and your proud spirit” and come and hear him (11.561–562; see [[ajax]] and [[thymos]]).
