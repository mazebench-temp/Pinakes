**The oldest form.** “And I saw the mother of Oedipus, fair Epicaste, / who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind / in marrying her own son; and he, when he had slain his own father, / married her; and at once the gods made it known among men” (11.271–274; see [[jocasta]] and [[oedipus]]). Merry calls it the story in its simplest and probably earliest form: the incest, the parricide and Epicaste's death are the only events, and the marriage may imply the Sphinx and her riddle (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.271). The killing of Laius is a clause, ὁ δʼ ὃν πατέρʼ ἐξεναρίξας, “when he had slain his own father”, with ἐξεναρίζω, the verb of killing a man in battle and stripping his armor; the father is not named (11.273; see [[laius]]). μέγα ἔργον is “a great deed”, used even of crime, as of Aegisthus' (3.261; see the translation's note on 11.272), and ἀιδρείῃσι νόοιο, “in the ignorance of her mind”, gives her innocence with the word used of the companions who went into Circe's house “in their ignorance” (10.231; see the translation's key-word row for ἀιδρείῃσιν).

**At once.** Everything turns on ἄφαρ, “at once” (11.274). Merry takes it, with the ancient paraphrasts, of immediate sequence: the union had only just taken place when its nature was revealed, and Epicaste, instead of living many years with Oedipus and bearing him children, put an end to her life at once. The scholia felt the difficulty, asking how the couple then had children, and the tragedians defer the discovery for years and give Oedipus four children by Jocasta; but the old epic called the *Oedipodia*, as Merry reports from Grote, gave the children to a second wife, Euryganeia (Merry on 11.271 and 11.274; see the translation's note on 11.273–274). ἀνάπυστα, “known”, suggests that the gods made no special revelation, but stirred men's curiosity and set them on the right track (Merry on 11.274).

**Oedipus in Thebes.** “But he, suffering sorrows in lovely Thebes / through the deadly plans of the gods, ruled the Cadmeans” (11.275–276; see [[thebes]] and [[cadmeians]]). θεῶν ὀλοὰς διὰ βουλάς goes with ἄλγεα πάσχων, and “lovely” Thebes may be set against the misery of its king (Merry on 11.275; see the translation's note on 11.275–276). There is no blinding here and no wandering to Athens: Homer has Oedipus die at Thebes, where Mecisteus came to his funeral games (*Iliad* 23.679–680; Merry on 11.271), and the Hesiodic *Catalogue* told that Argeia, daughter of Adrastus, came with others to his funeral (fr. 24 Evelyn-White; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]).

**Epicaste's death.** She went to the house of Hades, “the strong gatekeeper, / when she had fastened a noose hanging sheer from the high roof beam, / held fast by her own grief” (11.277–279; see [[hades]] and [[gatekeeper]]). The μέλαθρον is the long rafter that ran from wall to wall and carried the roof, and ᾧ ἄχεϊ σχομένη, “held fast by her own grief”, is said as the Phaeacians are “held by a spell” (Merry on 11.278, 11.279; 11.334; see the translation's key-word row for βρόχος αἰπύς). To her son she left “sorrows behind, / very many, all that the Erinyes of a mother bring to pass” (11.279–280), the avenging powers of a mother's curse, as in the Iliad (*Iliad* 21.412; see [[erinyes]] and [[curses]]).
