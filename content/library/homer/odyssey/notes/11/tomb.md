summary: Elpenor begs Odysseus to burn him with his armor and heap up a mound for him on the shore of the gray sea, “the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of”, with his oar fixed on it (11.74–78).
# The English 'mound', the translation of σῆμα at 11.75, applies in the Odyssey only.
en: [mound]
passages:
  - 11.72-78 | Elpenor's mound
---

“But burn me with my armor, whatever I have, / and heap up a mound for me on the shore of the gray sea, / the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of. / Do this for me, and fix on the tomb the oar / with which I rowed when I was alive, among my companions” (11.74–78; see [[elpenor]], [[cremation]], [[oars]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Both words of the grave are here: σῆμα, the mound as a marker (11.75), and τύμβος, the mound itself (11.77). ἀνδρὸς δυστήνοιο, “of an unhappy man”, stands in the genitive as if it hung on σῆμα, though by the grammar it should follow μοι, as W. W. Merry notes (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.76). The mound is to keep his name for men to come, as Hector imagined the mound of the man he would kill by the Hellespont, pointed out to men in years to come (*Iliad* 7.84–91), and a mound on the shore is seen from the sea. The companions keep the promise in Book 12: “we heaped up a tomb, and dragged a pillar up onto it, / and on the top of the tomb we fixed the well-fitted oar” (12.14–15; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). σῆμα is also the word of Teiresias' sign, the oar taken for a winnowing fan (11.126), which is excepted here (see [[teras]]).
