summary: The sheep of Book 11 are the victims of the rite of the dead, which the companions take aboard and ashore (11.4, 11.20), whose throats Odysseus cuts over the pit and whose bodies are flayed and burned (11.35–36, 11.44–47), with the black sheep vowed to Teiresias (11.32–33); then the fat sheep of the Sun on Thrinacia (11.108) and the fine flocks that raiders cut off from the herd (11.402).

**Aboard and ashore.** “And we took the sheep and put them on board, and went on board ourselves” (11.4; see [[ships]]); at the edge of Oceanus “we came and ran the ship ashore, and took the sheep / out of it” (11.20–21). τὰ μῆλα, “those sheep”, are the ram and the black ewe that Circe tied up beside the ship before they sailed (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.4; 10.571–572; see [[ram]] and the Book 10 note).

**The vows and the victims.** As Circe bade him, Odysseus vows a barren heifer for the dead when he comes to Ithaca, “and that to Teiresias apart I would sacrifice a sheep, to him alone, / all black, that stands out among our flocks” (11.30–33, Circe's words of 10.522–525 turned into his own; see [[teiresias]], [[cattle]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). Then: “I took the sheep and cut their throats / over the pit, and the dark blood flowed” (11.35–36; see [[dark-blood]]). Merry begins the second clause at τὰ δὲ μῆλα, comparing Circe's instruction, and explains ἀπεδειροτόμησα ἐς βόθρον, “I cut their throats over the pit”, by εἰς Ἔρεβος στρέψας, “turning them toward Erebus” (10.528): the preposition includes the heads bent down toward the pit and the blood flowing into it, as Peleus vowed to sacrifice fifty rams to the river Spercheius ἐς πηγάς, “into your waters” (on 11.35; *Iliad* 23.147–148; see the translation's note on 11.34–36). While the souls crowd about the pit, the companions “flay the sheep that lay there slaughtered with the pitiless bronze, / and burn them, and pray to the gods, / to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (11.44–47; see [[hades]], [[persephone]] and [[pitiless-bronze]]); the verses are Circe's again, and Merry notes that κατέκειτʼ, “lay”, is right in tense here, and that the manuscripts which read it in Circe's verse, where κατάκειτʼ, “lie”, is needed, took it from this place (on 10.532; see the Book 10 note). The blood of the sheep is what the dead must drink before they know Odysseus and speak to him truly (11.95–98, 11.146–149, 11.153; see [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).

**The flocks of the Sun.** Teiresias warns of the island of Thrinacia, where they will find grazing “the cattle and fat sheep / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (11.108–109; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]], [[helios]], [[thrinacia]] and [[fat-sheep]]). βόας καὶ ἴφια μῆλα, “the cattle and fat sheep”, is a formula of the Odyssey, which returns in later books (18.278, 20.51, 23.304).

**Flocks cut off.** Among the deaths that Odysseus imagines for Agamemnon is a raid: “Or did merciless men do you harm on land / as you were cutting off their cattle and fine flocks of sheep” (11.401–402; see [[agamemnon]] and [[raiding]]). Merry explains περιταμνόμενον, with the scholion, as “surrounding and driving off”: the full picture is of a band of marauders surrounding a number of oxen and cutting them off from the main herd, as the raiders on the shield of Achilles “cut off the herds of cattle and the fine flocks / of white sheep” (on 11.402; *Iliad* 18.527–529). In the last book Agamemnon's soul asks the same question of a suitor's (24.111–112).
