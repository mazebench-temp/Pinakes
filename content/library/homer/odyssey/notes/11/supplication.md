summary: Odysseus implores the strengthless heads of the dead with vows (11.29, 11.35); the soul of Elpenor implores him, “by those you left behind, though they are not here”, by his wife, his father and Telemachus, to burn and bury him (11.66–78); and in the wooden horse Neoptolemus “kept supplicating me again and again” to let him out to fight (11.530–531).
# γουνούμην (11.29), ἐλλισάμην (11.35; again 13.273), γουνάζομαι (11.66; again 13.324), ἱκέτευεν (11.530).
grc: [γουνούμην, ἐλλισάμην, γουνάζομαι, ἱκέτευεν]
en: [implored, begged, implore, supplicating]
---

**The living to the dead.** Odysseus “earnestly implored the strengthless heads of the dead” (πολλὰ δὲ γουνούμην, 11.29), the verb of clasping the knees used of the vow, and only when he has begged them does he cut the victims' throats (ἐλλισάμην, 11.35; see [[the-strengthless-heads]], [[prayer]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). The Book 10 note gives the form of the entreaty and the words of the vow.

**The dead to the living.** The first soul to come is a suppliant. Elpenor implores Odysseus “by those you left behind, though they are not here, / by your wife and your father, who reared you when you were small, / and by Telemachus” (11.66–68), and asks for his burial (11.71–78; see [[elpenor]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The verse is Nestor's at the ships, where he begs the Achaeans to stand firm, “for their sake, though they are not here, I implore you”, by their children, wives, wealth and parents (*Iliad* 15.661–666); Lauer counted it among the borrowed verses of the episode (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51). The suppliant names those the other loves and threatens what the gods will do: “lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you” (11.73; see [[menis]]). Odysseus grants it in a verse: “All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you” (11.80).

**The eager.** In the wooden horse the other leaders wept and trembled, but Neoptolemus “kept supplicating me again and again / to let him out of the horse” (ἱκέτευεν, 11.530–531), the word of entreaty turned to a young man's eagerness for battle (see [[neoptolemus]], [[the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]).
