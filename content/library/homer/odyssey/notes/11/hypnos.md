summary: Poseidon sheds sleep on Tyro after their union (11.245); Laertes sleeps in winter where the slaves sleep, by the fire (11.190); Odysseus breaks off his tale because “it is time / to sleep” (11.330–331), Alcinous answers that “it is not yet time / to sleep” (11.373–374), and Odysseus grants that “there is a time for many words, and a time also for sleep” (11.379).
# εὕδει (11.190).
grc: [εὕδει]
en: [sleeps]
---

**The god's sleep.** When Poseidon has lain with Tyro under the arching wave, “he loosed her maiden girdle, and shed sleep upon her” (11.245; see [[tyro]], [[poseidon]] and [[girdle]]). The verse was athetized, on the ground that sleep was needless for a woman who loved the god and wished to lie with him; it stands in all the manuscripts, and every editor but La Roche brackets it (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 11.245; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.245). Hayman suggested that the sleep gave the god time to resume his own form, and compared Aphrodite, who pours sweet sleep on Anchises before she puts on her immortal beauty and wakes him (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 170–177); Merry thinks the clause only a loose equivalent of “shed sleep upon her” (Merry on 11.245). In the Hymn, as here, the god then speaks and calls the mortal by name (ἔπος τʼ ἔφατʼ ἔκ τʼ ὀνόμαζεν, *Hymn to Aphrodite* 176; 11.247; see [[called-him-by-name]]).

**Laertes.** The old man has no bed in winter: “he sleeps where the slaves sleep in the house, / in the dust near the fire” (11.190–191; see [[laertes]] and [[bed]]).

**Time for sleep.** The pause in the tale is a dispute about the hour of sleep (11.330–331, 11.373–374, 11.379; see [[a-time-for-many-words]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).
