**The harbor.** As the morning star rises, the Phaeacian ship draws near the island (13.93–95), and the poet describes the place before the landing: “There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / in the land of Ithaca; and in it two jutting / headlands, sheer, crouch down toward the harbor / and keep off the great wave of the stormy winds / outside; but within, without a mooring rope, there stay / the well-benched ships, once they have reached the measure of anchorage” (13.96–101; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[harbor]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). ποτιπεπτηυῖαι is “crouching towards”, sinking down in front of the harbor and so closing it in, and ὅρμου μέτρον “the measure of anchorage”, the distance at which ships are fastened by a cable to the shore if fastening is needed (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.98 and 13.101; see the translation's note on 13.98–99). At its head stand the long-leaved olive and the cave of the Naiads (13.102–112; see [[olive]], [[nymphs]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The poem does not say why the harbor bears his name, or that he lives there.

**The Old Man of the Sea.** ἅλιος γέρων, “the Old Man of the Sea”, is the title that the Iliad gives to the father of Thetis and the Nereids, and the Odyssey to Proteus (*Iliad* 1.538; 4.349, 4.365; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]] and [[proteus]]), and the English renders it alike in both poems (see the translation's key-word row on ἅλιος γέρων). Here, in the genitive ἁλίοιο γέροντος, it belongs to Phorcys, whom Book 1 called “who rules over the barren sea” and made the father of the nymph Thoosa, the Cyclops' mother (1.71–72; see the Book 1 note and [[thoosa]]). So Odysseus comes home at a harbor named for the grandfather of the Cyclops whose blinding kept him so long from home, while Poseidon, the Cyclops' father, turns the Phaeacian ship to stone (13.159–164; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[poseidon]]).

**Athena's proof.** On waking Odysseus does not know the harbor, for Athena's mist has changed the look of everything, “the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring” (13.195; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[athena]]). When he doubts her word, she shows him the land, and the harbor comes first: “But come, I will show you the seat of Ithaca, so that you may be certain. / This is the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / and here at the head of the harbor is the long-leaved olive” (13.344–346). The second verse repeats the poet's 13.96 with a pointing word, ὅδʼ ἐστὶ λιμήν, “This is the harbor” (13.345). Then she scatters the mist (13.352).

**Later.** The Suda knows him only as a name and from this harbor: Φόρκυν· ὄνομα. λιμὴν τοῦ Φόρκυνος, “Phorcys: a name. The harbor of Phorcys” (Suda φ 599; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]).
