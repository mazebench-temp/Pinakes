summary: The sunset of the departure, told in a simile: Odysseus keeps turning his head toward the sun, “in haste for it to set,” as a plowman longs for his supper, “and gladly for him the light of the sun sets,” and “so welcome to Odysseus did the light of the sun set” (13.29–35).
grc: ["κατέδυ φάος ἠελίοιο", "ἔδυ φάος ἠελίοιο"]
en: [the light of the sun sets, the light of the sun set]
---

The sunset is the time for which Odysseus waits, since the voyage will be made by night (13.70–95). Its formula is given twice, once in the simile and once in the story it describes: κατέδυ φάος ἠελίοιο, the verse-end of the gods' feast at the end of *Iliad* 1 without its λαμπρόν, “bright” (*Iliad* 1.605), and ἔδυ φάος ἠελίοιο, as at the lament for Patroclus (*Iliad* 23.154). D. B. Monro renders δῦναι ἐπειγόμενος “in haste, eager for (his) setting” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.30; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]], [[wine-dark]] and [[dawn-line]]).
