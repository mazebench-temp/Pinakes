summary: The book of his homecoming. When his tale is over he waits for the sunset like a plowman for his supper, takes leave of the Phaeacians and of Arete, sleeps through the night voyage a sleep most like death, and is set ashore asleep with his gifts at the harbor of Phorcys (13.1–124). He wakes in a land he does not know, laments, counts his treasure, and tells Athena, whom he takes for a young herdsman, the false tale of a Cretan exile; she smiles, reveals herself and scatters the mist, and he kisses the earth and prays to the Naiads. They hide the gifts in the cave, plan the suitors' death under the sacred olive, and she changes him into an old beggar and sends him to the swineherd (13.187–440).

**The homecoming.** After four books of his own tale the poet speaks again, and the book that ends the Phaeacian episode brings Odysseus home. D. B. Monro marks the change: “From the thirteenth book onwards the character of the narrative perceptibly changes”, and “Like Ulysses after his landing in Ithaca, when Athene removed the mist from his eyes, we find ourselves in a familiar world—the world of the Trojan story” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §3). What the prophets said comes true in it. Teiresias had told him that he would come home “late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship”, and find in his house overbearing men wooing his wife (11.114–117), the words of the Cyclops' curse (9.534–535; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]); Halitherses had foretold his return “unknown to all, in the twentieth year” (2.174–176; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]). He comes home in a Phaeacian ship, alone, and Athena makes him unknown to all (13.397).

**The end of the tale.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2), as at the pause among the heroines (11.333–334; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]], [[thelgo]] and [[silence]]). Alcinous answers that since he has come to his house he will not be beaten back on his way home (13.4–6): the words are Achilles' at the first assembly of the Iliad, but here the meaning is simply that Ulysses will not return driven back from the house of Alcinous, but will have his desire (Monro on 13.5–6; *Iliad* 1.59–60; see the translation's note on 13.5–6). The elders are to add a great tripod and a cauldron each to the gifts already in the chest (13.10–15; see [[alcinous]], [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[possessions]]).

**Waiting for the sunset.** At dawn, the morning of the thirty-fourth day of the action (Monro on 13.18), the gifts are stowed in the ship, and at the king's house an ox is sacrificed to Zeus and Demodocus sings (13.18–28; see [[demodocus]]). “But Odysseus / kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun, / in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (13.28–30; see [[nostos]]), δῦναι ἐπειγόμενος being “in haste, eager for (his) setting” (Monro on 13.30). Then the poem's simile of the plowman, “as when a man longs for his supper”, for whom the sun sets gladly after a day behind the oxen (13.31–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]]). Its second verse, the two wine-dark oxen and the jointed plow, has the words of the Iliad's simile of the two Ajaxes (*Iliad* 13.703; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]), and its last, “and his knees are hindered as he goes”, is Odysseus' own in the Iliad, when he tells Achilles that no man can fight all day until the sun goes down without food (*Iliad* 19.162–166).

**The farewell.** “And at once he spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (13.36): it is the verse that three times brings in the king's speeches in Book 8 (8.96, 8.386, 8.535), given now to the guest. He asks for the escort, “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship” (13.40–41; see [[pompe]] and [[doron]]), prays to find his noble wife at home and his dear ones unhurt (13.42–43; see [[penelope]]), and wishes the Phaeacians joy of their wives and children, “and may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (13.45–46), ἀρετή being “good”, not merit or excellence (Monro on 13.45; see the translation's note on 13.45 and [[arete]]). They approve in the verses with which they granted his first request for an escort on the night he came (13.47–48 = 7.226–227). After the libation he puts the cup in the queen's hand and bids her farewell “until old age / and death come, which are the lot of men” (13.59–60; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[pontonous]] and [[libation]]), and steps over the threshold (13.63). His prayer for them is answered otherwise: the ship that carries him is turned to stone in sight of their city on its way home, and they pray that the city may not be covered with a mountain (13.159–187).

**Asleep on the ship.** At the ship the escorts spread “a rug and a linen sheet / on the deck of the hollow ship, so that he might sleep unwaking, / at the stern” (13.73–75; see [[blankets]], [[deck]] and [[stern]]), and he lies down “in silence” (13.76). As soon as the oars strike the sea, “sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids, / unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (13.79–80; see [[sweet-sleep]], [[hypnos]] and [[sleep]]). Monro thinks ἥδιστος, quite apart from the digamma of ἡδύς, weak after νήδυμος, and suggests reading ἥκιστος, “most gentle” (Monro's critical note on 13.80). The ship runs like four stallions under the whip, faster than the falcon (13.81–87; see [[the-four-stallions]]), and the poet looks at the sleeper:

{{quote:87f9dbaa-8f95-50db-a37c-19058baded4e}}

θεοῖς ἐναλίγκια μήδεʼ ἔχοντα is a brachylogy, “like the (counsels of the) gods” (Monro on 13.89; see [[metis]]). The second verse recalls the proem, “and on the sea he suffered many sorrows in his heart” (1.4); in the third, πτολέμους and κύματα both depend on πάθʼ ἄλγεα, and πείρων belongs only to the waves, so that it is not properly a zeugma (Monro on 13.91; see the translation's note on 13.91). It is the verse with which he told Euryalus that he was “held fast by misery and sorrows” (8.182–183), and in the Iliad the verse of the sufferings that Achilles, sleepless, remembers having shared with Patroclus (*Iliad* 24.3–9). Here the man who suffered them sleeps “motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered”. At the rising of the morning star, the dawn of the thirty-fifth day, the ship comes to Ithaca (13.93–95; Monro on 13.93; see [[eos]]).

**Set ashore asleep.** In the harbor of Phorcys the ship runs up on the beach half her length (13.96–115; see [[phorcys-the-sea-god]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]), “and first they lifted Odysseus out of the hollow ship, / linen sheet and glossy rug and all, / and laid him down on the sand, overcome by sleep” (13.117–119), αὐτῷ σύν τε λίνῳ being “with the linen cloth as it was” (Monro on 13.118). They set his possessions by the trunk of the olive, away from the road, “lest some wayfarer / should come upon them and do them harm before Odysseus woke” (13.120–124; see [[olive]] and [[possessions]]); διά in “through great-hearted Athena” is “by the agency of” (Monro on 13.121), and for μή πως Aristarchus read μή πω, a use of πω that can hardly be defended (Monro on 13.123). So he comes home as Agamemnon's soul had advised, “secretly, and not openly” (11.455; see [[agamemnon]]). Aristotle names the putting ashore as one of the irrational things in the Odyssey that a bad poet would have made intolerable (*Poetics* 1460a35–b2; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**Poseidon's grudge.** “But the earth-shaker / did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first” (13.125–127; see [[poseidon]]), πρῶτον being “once” (Monro on 13.127). The proem had said that Poseidon raged against godlike Odysseus “until he reached his own land” (1.20–21). Now that he has reached it, the god complains that the Phaeacians carried him asleep over the sea and gave him more than he would ever have carried off from Troy (13.131–138), in the words of Zeus' own promise in Book 5 (13.137–138 = 5.39–40; see [[zeus]]), and his anger turns from Odysseus to his escorts (13.146–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**The waking.** “And brilliant Odysseus woke / where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it— / after being away so long” (13.187–189), as he woke on Scheria (6.117). ἤδη δὴν ἀπεών goes with εὕδων ἐν γαίῃ πατρωΐῃ, while περὶ γάρ … gives the reason why he did not know it (Monro on 13.189). Athena had poured a mist around him “so that she might make him / unknown, and tell him each thing, / lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends, / before the suitors had paid for all their transgression” (13.190–193; see [[athena]] and [[the-suitors]]). The mist was not to make him invisible: Athena meant to prepare him for the work before him by consultation and by changing his appearance, as she does at 13.429–438, and without these measures he would have gone straight to his palace, “and all would have been lost”; the talk comes before the change, so that the order is a prothysteron (Monro on 13.191; see the translation's note on 13.189–191). “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord” (13.194; see [[anax]]). The manuscripts are divided between φαίνετο and φαινέσκετο, and Monro prints ἀλλοειδέʼ ἐφαίνετο (Monro on 13.194; see the translation's note on 13.194); in his additional notes he allows that the strangeness “may be only the exaggeration of a natural effect”, and quotes Wordsworth's *The Brothers*, in which a man come home after long absence doubts the fields he once knew well (Monro, Additional Notes and Corrections, p. 286). He springs up, groans and strikes his thighs in the verses of Patroclus when he sees the Trojans storming the wall (13.198–199 = *Iliad* 15.397–398).

**The lament.** “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time? / Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (13.200–202): the words with which he woke on Scheria (6.119–121) and went to make trial of the Cyclops (9.175–176; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]), a double question, “are they savage or hospitable?” (Monro on 13.201–202). His next care is the treasure: “Where am I taking all these goods? And where am I myself / wandering? Would that they had stayed there among the Phaeacians” (13.203–204), the goods being the subject of ὄφελον μεῖναι (Monro on 13.204). The Phaeacians, he thinks, have cheated him: “Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just” (13.209), that is, “they are not, as I thought they were” (Monro on 13.209), and he calls on Zeus as the god of suppliants, to whom the Phaeacians had poured a libation on the night he came to them (7.164–165, 7.180–181): “May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over / other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong” (13.213–214; see [[zeus]] and [[supplication]]). ἱκετήσιος is found only here in Homer; Zenodotus read the imperative τισάσθω, which may have seemed improper of a god, Aristarchus τίσαιτο (Monro's critical note on 13.213). He counts the tripods, the cauldrons, the gold and the clothes: “Of these he missed nothing; but he mourned for his native land, / dragging himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, / lamenting much” (13.219–221; see [[dear-native-land]]). He mourns for his native land while he walks on it.

**The young herdsman.** Athena comes “in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords” (13.222–223; see [[shape-changing]] and [[divine-epiphany]]), and he goes to meet her gladly. He greets her as a suppliant, “save these things, and save me; for to you / I pray as to a god, and I come to your dear knees” (13.230–231; see [[supplication]]), and asks: “And tell me this truly, so that I may know it well, / what land is this, what people, what men are born here?” (13.232–233; see [[tell-me-this-truly]]). She answers with the Cyclops' first words to him, “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away” (13.237 = 9.273; see [[nepios]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), describes a rugged island good for goats and cattle, and names it only in her last verses: “Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy” (13.248; see [[ithaca]]).

**The Cretan tale.** He is glad, “rejoicing in his own land, the land of his fathers” (13.251), “yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word, / always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (13.254–255; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[kerdos]], [[noos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). “Took back his speech” is to leave unsaid what he would have said if he had spoken the truth (Monro on 13.254), where the same words in the Iliad are said of Agamemnon withdrawing his rebuke of Odysseus (*Iliad* 4.357); πολυκερδέα is “very cunning” (Monro on 13.255). He has heard of Ithaca “even in wide Crete” (13.256; Κρήτῃ, for which one manuscript has Τροίῃ, Monro's critical note); he is an exile who killed Orsilochus, the swift-footed son of Idomeneus, from ambush by night, because Orsilochus tried to rob him of his spoil from Troy (13.258–270; see [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]], [[idomeneus]], [[crete]] and [[ambush]]); noble Phoenicians, begged to set him down at Pylos or Elis, were driven here against their will by the wind (13.271–278; see [[phoenicians]], [[pylos]], [[elis]] and [[epeians]]); and while he slept on the sand they set his goods beside him and sailed for Sidon (13.279–286; see [[sidon]]). The lie is built from what the herdsman could see: a stranger alone at a harbor with his treasure, after a night voyage. Its sleep is the sleep that took him within sight of Ithaca in Book 10, when the companions opened the bag of winds, “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (13.282 = 10.31), and the Cretan's sufferings at Troy and on the sea are told in the verse the poet had used of Odysseus asleep on the ship (13.264 = 13.91). αὔτως is “as we were”, without attempting more (Monro on 13.281). It is the first of the false stories, and like the others it turns on the Trojan war (Monro, Appendix I §3, citing 13.256 ff., 14.235 ff., 14.468 ff. and 19.172 ff.).

**Athena revealed.** “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled, / and stroked him with her hand; and she was like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.287–289; see [[athena]]), the last verse being omitted in two manuscripts (Monro's critical note on 13.289). “Crafty would he be, and a cheat, who should get past you / in tricks of every kind, even if it were a god who met you. / Hard man, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks, you were not, then, / even in your own land, to leave off your deceits / and thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up” (13.291–295; see [[crafty-would-he-be]], [[dolos]], [[hard]] and [[of-subtle-wiles]]). κερδαλέος is “crafty”, καὶ εἰ θεὸς ἀντιάσειε “even were it a god”, and σχέτλιε “hard”, said in a friendly and admiring tone (Monro on 13.291–293, who compares *Iliad* 22.41, 22.86); πεδόθεν is “from the ground up”, like *funditus*, but probably with the notion of constancy, as in ἔμπεδος, and the vulgate's παιδόθεν is itacism (Monro on 13.295; see the translation's note on 13.295). ἐν πάντεσσι δόλοισι, “in tricks of every kind”, is his own boast when he named himself to the Phaeacians, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who for tricks of every kind / am in the thoughts of men” (9.19–20), and ἐπίκλοπος, “a cheat”, is what Alcinous said the Phaeacians did not judge him to be (11.363–364). δόλων ἆτʼ, “insatiable of tricks”, is the Iliad's taunt of Socus to him (*Iliad* 11.430). Then: “let us no longer talk like this, since we both know / crafty ways; for you are by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words, and I among all the gods / am famed for cunning and crafty ways” (13.296–299; see [[metis]] and [[boule]]). She has come to weave a plan with him, to hide his goods, and to tell him what sorrows he must bear in his house: “and you must be patient, even under compulsion, / and not speak out to anyone, man or woman, / of them all, that you have come back from your wanderings, but in silence / suffer many sorrows, submitting to the violence of men” (13.307–310; see [[much-enduring]] and [[silence]]), οὕνεκα being “that”, a meaning confined in Homer to the Odyssey (Monro on 13.309).

**It is hard to know you.** “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313): the mortal's side of the saying that the gods know one another at sight (5.79–80; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]] and [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]). She was kind to him at Troy, but after the sack of the city “I did not see you, daughter of Zeus” (13.314–319). He tells it in the verses with which Nestor began the story of the returns, where many met an evil fate “through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father” (13.316–317 = 3.130–131; 3.135; see [[priam]]). The next four verses, which end his wanderings with her help among the Phaeacians (13.320–323), are generally condemned: the first two certainly, since πρίν γʼ ὅτε goes with οὐ σέ γʼ ἔπειτα ἴδον; against the last two the ancients argued that he could not know of her presence in Phaeacia, where she did not reveal herself, an objection Monro thinks too exact for a poem, though he allows that they have the air of an insertion meant to reconcile the speech with the Phaeacian books (Monro on 13.320–323; see the translation's note on 13.320–323). And he still doubts. He does not think that he has come to clear-seen Ithaca, and thinks that she is “taunting me” (κερτομέουσαν, “seeking to vex”, Monro on 13.326): “tell me if it is true that I have come to my dear native land” (13.324–328; see [[eperopeuo]]).

**Why she cannot abandon him.** Athena answers:

{{quote:34a69e69-86b2-5cc1-9617-3b8506316d72}}

ἐπητής is “charming”, “polite”, as ἐπητύς is “civility” (Monro on 13.332, comparing 21.306; see [[sensible]]). Another man would have hurried home to his wife and children; he will learn nothing “before you have made trial of your wife yourself” (13.333–336), πειρήσεαι being “observe for yourself”, not trusting to report (Monro on 13.336; see [[peira]] and [[penelope]]). Aristarchus rejected 13.333–338 (Monro's critical note). She never doubted that he would come home “having lost all your companions” (13.340), Teiresias' word (11.114), but she would not fight with Poseidon, “my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.342–343, after 11.102–103; see [[kotos]] and [[poseidon]]), the reason the poet gave when she would not appear to him openly on Scheria (6.328–331).

**The land shown.** She points out the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave where “you used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs”, and Mount Neritum (13.344–351; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[nymphs]], [[hecatomb]] and [[neritum]]). “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view; / and then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / rejoicing in his own land, and kissed the grain-giving earth” (13.352–354; see [[grain-giving]] and [[earth]]). He kissed the earth when he came out of the sea on Scheria (5.463), and Agamemnon “touched his native land and kissed it” when he came home to his death (4.521–523). The joy is told in the verses of his first gladness (13.353–354 ~ 13.250–251); then he answered with a lie, now with a prayer: “Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus, never did I / think to see you again; but now with my gentle vows / rejoice; and we will give you gifts as well, as before, / if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants / that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow” (13.356–360; see [[prayer]], [[driver-of-spoil]] and [[telemachus]]). χαίρετε is hardly more than a greeting, but it takes the dative εὐχωλῇς as though it meant “be gladdened”, and in διδώσομεν Odysseus associates Telemachus with himself (Monro on 13.357, 13.358).

**The gifts hidden.** At her word they put the goods in the inner part of the cave: “and Odysseus / brought everything near, the gold and the unyielding bronze / and the well-made clothes, which the Phaeacians had given him”, and she laid them away and set a stone against the doors (13.361–371; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[possessions]] and [[door]]). He will tell his son that they “lie in caves by the will of the gods” (16.232, a plain rendering).

**The plan.** “Then the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive / and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[olive]], [[overbearing]] and [[the-suitors]]). Athena tells him of the suitors “who for three years now have been lording it in your hall” (13.377), κοιρανέουσι being ironical (Monro on 13.377), and of his wife's promises (13.379–381; see [[penelope]]). He sees the fate of Agamemnon in his own, “had you not told me each thing, goddess, fair and right” (13.383–385; see [[agamemnon]]), and asks for her plan and her help: “But come, weave a plan, how I may take vengeance on them; / and stand beside me yourself, putting in me a might full of daring, / as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.386–388). αὐτούς is perhaps emphatic, “Now I shall take vengeance on the men in their turn (who sought to kill me)” (Monro, Additional Notes and Corrections, p. 286), and the shining veil is “the diadem of towers”, from Achilles' wish that he and Patroclus alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy (Monro on 13.388; *Iliad* 16.100; see [[veil]], [[the-fall-of-troy]] and the translation's note on 13.388). With her beside him he would fight three hundred men (13.389–391); she promises to be there, and that the floor will be spattered with the suitors' blood and brains (13.393–396).

**Made unknown.** “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals” (13.397), ἄγνωστον, as the mist was to make him unknown (13.191) and as Halitherses had foretold (2.175). She will wither his skin, destroy his hair, put a rag around him and dim his eyes, “so that you may seem unsightly to all the suitors / and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls” (13.398–403; see [[shape-changing]]). In 13.400 Monro adopts ἄνθρωπον, a variant mentioned by Eustathius, since the manuscripts' ἄνθρωπος could not stand in Homer for “any man”; it is the only place in the Odyssey where the word occurs in the singular (Monro on 13.400). He is to go first to the swineherd (13.404–411; see [[eumaeus]]), while she calls Telemachus home from Sparta; to his question why she let his son wander she answers that she sent him to win noble fame, and that the suitors' ambush will fail (13.412–428; see [[telemachus]] and [[ambush]]).

**The beggar.** “So saying, Athena touched him with her wand” (13.429; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[wand]]). She does what she had said, in the same verses: she withers the fair skin on his supple limbs, destroys the fair hair, puts on all his limbs “the skin / of an aged old man”, and dims his eyes (13.430–433; see [[supple]]); she dresses him in “a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, dirty, stained with foul smoke”, “the great skin of a swift deer, / worn bare”, and gives him “a staff and a shabby pouch, / rent in many places; and on it was a twisted cord to sling it by” (13.434–438; see [[clothing]], [[tunic]], [[deer]] and [[scepter]]). ἄλλο is “other” (than his own), and ψιλόν “bare”, the wool worn off (Monro on 13.434, 13.437); the last two verses come again when he slings the pouch on his shoulders (17.197–198, 18.108–109). He is here supposed to be fair-haired, ξανθός, but when Athena restores him his beard grows dark: the poet forgets that Ulysses had “yellow” hair before (Monro on 13.431 and on 16.176; see 6.231 and [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). She will restore him with her golden wand for his son and make him old again (16.172–176, 16.454–457). The change, as Kirchhoff insisted, is a magical one, “not a mere illusion”; Kirchhoff thought it inserted to join two poems, since some later recognitions imply only the rags, and Monro answers that the poet “has made a somewhat excessive use of the marvellous, and afterwards returns unconsciously to a more natural point of view” (Monro, Appendix I §6 and its note).

**The parting.** “When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (13.439–440). The first verse is the Iliad's for Zeus and Thetis parting after the nod (*Iliad* 1.531; see [[zeus]] and [[thetis]]). The book ends in the middle of a sentence, ἡ μὲν … αὐτὰρ ὁ, “she went to Sparta, while he &c.” (Monro on 13.440): the next begins with him going up from the harbor by the rugged path to the swineherd (14.1–4, a plain rendering; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]).

**His names and epithets in this book.** Ὀδυσεῦ in Alcinous' address (13.4) and Ὀδυσσεῦ in Athena's (13.375, 13.413); δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (13.56, 13.63, 13.187; see [[brilliant]]); ἀντιθέῳ Ὀδυσῆϊ, “godlike Odysseus”, of Poseidon's threats, as in the proem (13.126; 1.21; see [[antitheos]]); πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (13.250, 13.353; see [[much-enduring]]); πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles” (13.311, 13.382, 13.416; see [[of-many-wiles]]); and διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (13.375; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]], [[of-many-devices]] and [[laertes]]). Unnamed, he is “the stranger” to the Phaeacians and to Athena (13.10, 13.48, 13.237, 13.248), “a man whose counsels were like the gods'” (13.89), the island's lord (13.194), and to Athena σχέτλιε, ποικιλομῆτα, δόλων ἆτʼ, “Hard man, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks” (13.293), and ἐπητής … καὶ ἀγχίνοος καὶ ἐχέφρων, “courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.332).

**Outside Homer.** Aristotle asked why Odysseus did not reveal himself to Penelope, who was mature and loved him, but did to Telemachus, though he was young, and to the swineherd and the cowherd. He answered that Odysseus had to tell those who were to share the danger, and told each privately and at the proper time, so that none could shift the blame to another; and that he feared Penelope's joy would betray him, since Telemachus was used to controlling his feelings, while she was used only to weeping, and if she stopped, suspicion would arise (*Homeric Problems* fr. 399 Gigon, from the scholia on 13.1; see [[aristotle]] and [[penelope]]). In Aeschylus' *Penelope* a speaker, perhaps Odysseus in disguise, claims to be a Cretan by birth: ἐγὼ γένος μέν εἰμι Κρὴς ἀρχέστατον (fr. 187 Nauck, from the *Etymologicum Magnum*; see [[aeschylus]]). Plato's Hippias holds that Homer made Achilles true and simple and Odysseus πολύτροπός τε καὶ ψευδής, “versatile and false” (*Hippias Minor* 365b; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]). Cicero, showing that what suits one man's nature does not suit another's, says that Ulysses at home bore even the insults of slaves and maidservants, so that he might at last come to what he desired, where Ajax would rather have died a thousand times than bear them (*On Duties* 1.113; see [[cicero]]).
