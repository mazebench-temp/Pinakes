summary: The book of the homecoming accomplished. Alcinous promises that Odysseus “will not be beaten back” (13.5–6); Odysseus waits all day, “eager to go home” (13.30), and leaves Arete with “But I am going home” (13.61); Poseidon admits that he never took the homecoming from him altogether (13.132–133); and on Ithaca Athena says she knew he would “come home, having lost all your companions” (13.340), while Penelope mourns for his homecoming (13.379).
# νέομαι (13.61) and νοστήσεις (13.340), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [νέομαι, νοστήσεις]
en: [going home, come home]
---

**Not beaten back.** Alcinous' first words after the tale are a promise: “Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back / and sail for home again, even though you have suffered very much” (ἂψ ἀπονοστήσειν, 13.4–6; see [[alcinous]]). The words come from Achilles' speech in the first assembly of the Iliad, “now I think we shall be beaten back / and sail for home again” (*Iliad* 1.59–60), with a change of application that has made them obscure: in the Iliad the return is a disappointment, the army driven back to Greece; here the meaning is simply that Odysseus will not leave the house of Alcinous driven back, but will have his desire (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.5–6; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.5–6).

**Eager to go home.** At the farewell feast Odysseus “kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun, / in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (μενέαινε νέεσθαι, 13.29–30; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]]). His farewell prays “may I find my noble wife at home / when I return, and my dear ones unhurt” (νοστήσας, 13.42–43), and his last words to Arete are “But I am going home” (αὐτὰρ ἐγὼ νέομαι, 13.61; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The god's admission.** Poseidon, complaining to Zeus, concedes the homecoming itself: “For even now I said that Odysseus, after suffering many evils, / would come home; his homecoming I never took from him / altogether, since you first promised it and nodded your assent” (νόστον, 13.131–133; see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]). The ship that brought him home is what he punishes (13.149–164; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**Home unknown.** Odysseus wakes in the land of his fathers and does not know it, and wishes he had reached another king “who would have welcomed me and sent me on my way home” (13.206; see [[basileus]]). Athena tells him that she never doubted, “but in my heart / I knew that you would come home, having lost all your companions” (ὃ νοστήσεις ὀλέσας ἄπο πάντας ἑταίρους, 13.339–340; see [[hetairos]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). At home his wife is “always mourning in her heart for your homecoming” (σὸν αἰεὶ νόστον ὀδυρομένη, 13.379; see [[penelope]]), that is, crying for it, as the Achaeans at Troy “wail to one another about going home” (Monro on 13.379; *Iliad* 2.290).
