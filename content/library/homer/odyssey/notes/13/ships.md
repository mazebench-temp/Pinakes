---
summary: The book's ship is the Phaeacians' last: it carries Odysseus asleep to Ithaca, swifter than a falcon (13.70–95), runs half its length up the shore of the harbor of Phorcys (13.113–125), and on its way home is turned to stone by Poseidon in sight of the city (13.149–169). In Odysseus' false tale a Phoenician ship sets him down on Ithaca (13.272–286), and a black ship of the suitors lies in wait for Telemachus (13.425).

**Loading.** At dawn the lords bring the bronze to the ship, and Alcinous stows it under the thwarts (13.18–22; see [[tripod]] and [[rowing-benches]]). In the evening a herald leads Odysseus “to the swift ship and the shore of the sea”, and the noble escorts stow the food and drink “in the hollow ship” and spread his bed on the deck (13.64–75; see [[swift-ships]], [[hollow-ships]], [[provisions]] and [[deck]]).

**The voyage.** The ship runs like four stallions under the whip, and “not even a hawk, / a falcon, the nimblest of winged birds, could have kept pace with her. / So swiftly she ran, cutting the waves of the sea, / bearing a man whose counsels were like the gods'” (13.81–89; see [[the-four-stallions]], [[stern]], [[falcon]] and [[hawk]]). It is the ship of which Alcinous had boasted, that needs no helmsman and no steering oar, since the Phaeacian ships “themselves know the thoughts and minds of men” and cross the sea most swiftly, wrapped in mist and cloud (8.556–563; see [[phaeacians]] and [[steering-oar]]). “When that brightest star rose up which most of all / comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn, / then the seafaring ship drew near to the island” (13.93–95; see [[seafaring-ship]] and [[the-morning-star]]).

**The landing.** “There they rowed in, knowing the place before; and the ship / ran up on the land as much as half her length” (13.113–114; see [[oars]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). The crew step out of “the well-thwarted ship”, lift Odysseus and the gifts out of “the hollow ship”, and go home (13.116–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). In the harbor itself ships may lie without a mooring rope (13.100–101; see [[stern-cables]] and [[well-benched]]).

**Turned to stone.** Poseidon asks Zeus to let him shatter “the beautiful ship of the Phaeacians, / as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea” (13.149–150), the ship of Nausithous' prophecy (8.567–569; 13.175–177; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[nausithous]]); for περικαλλέα at 13.175 some manuscripts have ἐυεργέα, as at 8.567 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.175). Zeus answers that it seems best “to turn her to stone near the land, / like a swift ship, so that all men may marvel” (13.156–157; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[zeus]] and [[poseidon]]). The ship comes on “coursing swiftly along”, διωκομένη, a word of driving, as ἅρμα διώκειν is the phrase for driving a chariot (Monro on 13.162), “and the earth-shaker came near her, / and turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath / with a blow of the flat of his hand” (13.162–164; see the translation's row λᾶαν τίθημι; ῥιζόω; χειρὶ καταπρηνεῖ). The Phaeacians on the shore ask one another, “who has fettered the swift ship on the sea / as she was driven home? And she was already in plain sight” (13.168–169; see the translation's row πεδάω, of the ship). Monro reports that a small island near the entrance of the old harbor of Corfu was pointed out as the Phaeacian ship and is perhaps enough “like a swift ship” to have given rise to the story, citing Pliny (*Natural History* 4.53), though it is not the only rock to claim the honor (Monro on 13.154–158).

**Other ships.** In the tale he tells Athena, Odysseus went “to a ship, and noble Phoenicians / I begged” to set him down at Pylos or at Elis; the wind drove them to Ithaca, where they took his goods “out of the hollow ship”, set them beside him as he slept on the sand, and sailed for Sidon (13.272–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[phoenicians]] and [[sidon]]): the Phaeacians' landing told again as a lie. He tells her that after Troy he never saw her “boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.317–319), and she tells him that “young men in a black ship lie in ambush” for Telemachus (13.425; see [[ambush]], [[black-ships]] and [[the-suitors]]).
