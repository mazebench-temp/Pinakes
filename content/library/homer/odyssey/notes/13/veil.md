summary: Odysseus asks Athena to stand by him against the suitors as she did “when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.388): the city's crown of walls is a woman's veil, as in Achilles' wish in the Iliad.
# The phrase "λύομεν λιπαρὰ κρήδεμνα" (13.388), first met here; it applies in the Odyssey only. A bare κρήδεμνα would
# also catch Penelope's veil, which the Book 1 note claims in its own phrase (1.334).
grc: ["λύομεν λιπαρὰ κρήδεμνα"]

“and stand beside me yourself, putting in me a might full of daring, / as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.387–388; see [[athena]], [[troy]], [[sack-of-troy]] and [[full-of-daring]]). κρήδεμνα are “the diadem of towers”, from Achilles' wish that he and Patroclus alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy (*Iliad* 16.100; see the *Iliad* 16 note): a picturesque refinement on the more ordinary metaphor by which the battlements of a town are called its head, κάρηνα (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.388, comparing *Iliad* 2.117; see the translation's note on 13.388 and [[battlements]]). The Iliad's epithet is ἱερά, “sacred”; the Odyssey gives the veil of Troy the epithet of Penelope's veil, λιπαρά, “shining”, which she holds before her cheeks when she comes down among the suitors (1.334; see the Book 1 note). With the goddess beside him as at the sack, Odysseus would fight three hundred men (13.389–391).
