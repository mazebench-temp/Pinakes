**The swineherd's swine.** Before she changes Odysseus into an old beggar, Athena tells him where to go: “But you yourself go first of all to the swineherd, / who is the guardian over your swine, and is kind to you alike, / and loves your son and sensible Penelope” (13.404–406; see [[eumaeus]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). The swineherd, συβώτης, is not yet named. ὑῶν ἐπίουρος, “the guardian over your swine”, makes him the keeper of a part of Odysseus' wealth, and Athena says the verse again when she sends Telemachus to him (15.38–39). How ὁμῶς, “alike”, is to be taken is disputed. Ameis, as D. B. Monro reports him, refers it to ὑῶν ἐπίουρος, as if Eumaeus were as much devoted to Odysseus himself as to his possessions, the swine; Monro will not separate ὁμῶς from τοι, and finds in the word the idea of agreement blended with that of loyalty, so that Eumaeus was “at one with Ulysses in the loyalty of his heart” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.405; see the translation's note on 13.405).

**Acorns and dark water.** The swine feed by the Rock of Corax and the spring Arethusa (13.408; see [[the-rock-of-corax]] and [[arethusa]]). The herd is of sows, for αἱ, ἔσθουσαι and πίνουσαι are feminine; in the next book the sows are penned in twelve sties, fifty to each, while the boars sleep outside, “far fewer; for these kept dwindling, eaten / by the godlike suitors” (14.13–20, a plain rendering). βάλανος, the acorn, is the food that Circe threw to the companions she had turned into swine, “such as swine that sleep on the ground always eat” (10.241–243; see [[trees]] and the Book 10 note), and the dark water is the water of the spring, as springs are μελάνυδρος, “of dark water” (see [[of-dark-water]]). τεθαλυῖαν ἀλοιφήν, “the rich lard”, is the fat of well-fed swine, as at the Iliad's feasts the chine of a fat hog is τεθαλυῖαν ἀλοιφῇ, “rich with lard”, and the hogs are θαλέθοντες ἀλοιφῇ, “rich with lard” (*Iliad* 9.208, 9.467, 23.32), and as the chine of the white-tusked boar from which Odysseus cut a portion for Demodocus had “rich lard on either side” (8.474–476; see the Book 8 note). The participle is the same in Athena's account of Ithaca, “the rich dew” (τεθαλυῖά τʼ ἐέρση, 13.245; see the translation's row βάλανος; τεθαλυῖα ἀλοιφή).

**Ithaca's swine.** Athena calls Ithaca good land for goats and cattle (13.246; see the Book 13 notes on [[goats]] and [[cattle]]). W. W. Merry, who finds her description in harmony with Telemachus' words that the island is no place for horses but best suited for goats, observes that Eumaeus is nonetheless represented as having charge of twelve sties of fifty breeding sows, besides three hundred and sixty boars, some of which were brought in every day for the suitors' table (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, pp. 555–556; 4.605–608; 14.13–20). The swine are what the suitors eat (see the Book 2 note), and the swineherd who guards them is the first of Odysseus' household to take in the beggar (14.1–80).
