summary: The nymphs of Book 13 are the Naiads of Ithaca. Their cave, “sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads”, is at the head of the harbor of Phorcys, and there they weave sea-purple webs on looms of stone (13.102–112). Athena reminds Odysseus that he used to offer them many perfect hecatombs there (13.347–350), and when she has scattered the mist he prays to them, “Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus”, and promises them gifts again (13.355–360).

**Who are called Naiads.** At the harbor of Phorcys, “at the head of the harbor is a long-leaved olive, / and near it a lovely cave, misty, / sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads” (13.102–104; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[olive]]). The poet names them as men name them, αἳ νηϊάδες καλέονται, “who are called Naiads”. νηϊάς is found in Homer only in this verse, in its repetition and in Odysseus' prayer (13.104, 13.348, 13.356); the Iliad has νηΐς for the Naiads who bore Trojan warriors to mortal men (*Iliad* 6.22, 14.444, 20.384; see [[abarbarea]] and the general article). The cave is furnished with their things: “In it are mixing bowls and two-handled jars / of stone; and there the bees store up honey. / And in it are towering looms of stone, where the nymphs / weave sea-purple webs, a wonder to see; / and in it are ever-flowing waters” (13.105–109; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[two-handled-jar]], [[bees]], [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). φάρεα are “webs”, as in Penelope's account of the web she wove by day and unraveled by night (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.108, comparing 19.138). The nymphs weave as the goddesses of the poem weave, Calypso singing at her loom and Circe at hers (5.61–62, 10.221–223; see [[calypso]] and [[circe]]), and ἁλιπόρφυρα, “sea-purple”, is said elsewhere only of the yarn that Arete spins by her hearth (6.53, 6.306; see [[purple]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Of the two doors one is the gods': “and the other, toward the South Wind, is for gods; by that one / men do not enter, but it is the way of the immortals” (13.111–112; see [[notus]] and [[olympians]]). θεώτεραι is not “more divine” but “divine”, in contrast to the human door (Monro on 13.111; see the translation's note on 13.111).

**His hecatombs.** When Athena shows Odysseus his island she repeats the first two verses and adds the proof that he knows the place: “and this is the vaulted cave where you used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs” (13.347–350; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[hecatomb]] and [[athena]]). Some manuscripts omit 13.347–348, and Monro takes them for a wrong repetition of 13.103–104, since the cave is first mentioned at 13.349, with the deictic τοῦτο (Monro on 13.347–348 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 13.347–348). The worship is remembered on the island. At his own table Eumaeus sets aside one portion of the meat for the nymphs and Hermes (14.435–436), and at the spring of the town, where every wayfarer made offerings at the altar of the nymphs, he prays, “Nymphs of the spring, daughters of Zeus, if ever Odysseus / burned thigh-pieces to you, wrapping them in rich fat, / of lambs and kids, fulfill for me this wish” (17.204–211, 17.240–242, a plain rendering; see [[eumaeus]]).

**The prayer.** When the mist is scattered, Odysseus “kissed the grain-giving earth. / And at once he prayed to the nymphs, lifting up his hands” (13.354–355; see [[grain-giving]] and [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]]):

He greets them as κοῦραι Διός, “daughters of Zeus”, the words of Eumaeus' prayer (17.240), as the nymphs of the Iliad's mountains and of the island of goats are “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (*Iliad* 6.420; 9.154; see [[zeus]]). For διδώσομεν, “we will give”, Aristophanes read παρέξομεν (Monro's critical note on 13.358); Monro's notes on χαίρετε, on the plural and on the broken construction of the last verse are given in the Book 13 note on [[prayer]], with the sacrifice to the nymphs that the *Telegony* gave Odysseus after the burial of the suitors. The condition of the vow belongs to Athena, “the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil” (see [[driver-of-spoil]]): the nymphs are to have their gifts if she lets him live and makes his son grow.

**A cave not found.** Strabo, listing places that have changed since Homer's day, adds that there is no such cave on Ithaca, nor any shrine of the nymphs, as Homer describes, and that it is better to blame a change in the place than the poet's ignorance or a fabulous misrepresentation; but the matter, he says, is unclear (*Geography* 1.3.18; see [[strabo]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).
