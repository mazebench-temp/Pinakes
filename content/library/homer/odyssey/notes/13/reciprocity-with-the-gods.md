summary: Poseidon complains that mortals no longer honor him, and Zeus grants that vengeance is always his (13.128–145); the Phaeacians offer him twelve bulls in the hope of pity (13.181–183); and Odysseus prays to the Naiads with the hecatombs of the past and the promise of more gifts, if Athena grants him life and his son's growth (13.349–360). Athena's own reason for standing by him is not his gifts but his mind (13.330–332).

**The god's due.** Poseidon's complaint is a reckoning of honor: “Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods / be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all, / the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130; see [[poseidon]], [[time]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Zeus answers that the gods do not dishonor him, and that if any man fails to honor him, “vengeance is always yours, even hereafter” (13.141–144), with a play of words between τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of vengeance (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.144). What men do not pay in honor the god takes in punishment, and the Phaeacians, when the ship has been struck, try to pay in bulls: “and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity” (13.181–182; see [[propitiation]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**The prayer to the Naiads.** On Ithaca the proof of the land is a place of exchange, the cave “where you used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs” (13.349–350; see [[hecatomb]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]), and Odysseus' prayer there has the shape of Chryses' in the Iliad, a claim from past gifts and a request (*Iliad* 1.37–42; see the entry): “but now with my gentle vows / rejoice; and we will give you gifts as well, as before, / if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants / that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow” (13.357–360; see [[prayer]]). The promise is conditional, and the condition is laid on another god, the one who stands beside him (see [[driver-of-spoil]]).

**Mind, not gifts.** In the first council of the poem Zeus remembered Odysseus on two counts, “who is first among mortals in mind, and first in sacrifices / given to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky” (1.66–67; see [[zeus]]). Athena, on Ithaca, names only the first. She cannot abandon him, “because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.331–332; see [[pity]] and [[crafty-would-he-be]]): the goddess of cunning stands by the man of cunning for what he is.
