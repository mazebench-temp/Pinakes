summary: The Phaeacians' clothes go with Odysseus to Ithaca: they lie in the chest (13.10), Poseidon names them among the gifts (13.136), Odysseus counts them on the shore and carries them into the cave (13.218, 13.369). Then Athena dresses him in a beggar's rag (13.399–400, 13.434–435).

**The gifts of clothing.** “Clothes for the stranger lie in the polished chest” (13.10; see [[chest]]): the mantles and tunics that the kings brought and the queen laid in the chest (8.392, 8.438–441; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]). Poseidon names them in his complaint to Zeus: the Phaeacians “gave him untold gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.135–137; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]), in the words in which Zeus had foretold the gifts (5.38–39). On the shore Odysseus counts “the gold and the fine woven clothes” (13.218), and at Athena's bidding he carries into the cave “the gold and the unyielding bronze / and the well-made clothes, which the Phaeacians had given him” (13.368–369; see [[possessions]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).

**The beggar's rag.** Athena tells him what she will do: “around you a rag / I will put, which anyone who sees a man wearing it would hate” (13.399–400; see [[rags]], [[athena]], [[shape-changing]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). λαῖφος, “rag”, is found in Homer only here and in the cowherd's words of the beggar's dress, “wearing such rags as these” (20.206, a plain rendering; see the translation's row λαῖφος; ῥάκος). The manuscripts read ἄνθρωπος, “any man who should see”, but Homer would not use ἄνθρωπος for “any man”; the participle ἰδών can stand for “anyone that sees”, and Monro adopts ἄνθρωπον from a variant reported by Eustathius, noting that the singular is found nowhere else in the Odyssey (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.400). Then she does it: “around him she put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, dirty, stained with foul smoke” (13.434–435; see [[tunic]]). The ῥάκος is what Odysseus begged of Nausicaa to throw around him on Scheria (6.178; see the Book 6 note); it is also the dress he gives himself in the tale he tells the swineherd, where the [[thesprotians|Thesprotians]] strip him of cloak and tunic, “and around me they put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, which you see with your own eyes” (14.341–343; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). He wears the rags until he strips them off to begin the killing of the suitors (22.1).
