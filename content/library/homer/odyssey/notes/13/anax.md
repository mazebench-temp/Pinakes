---
summary: The lords of the book: Zeus “who rules over all” (13.25), “lord Poseidon” (13.185), Odysseus, to whom his own land looks strange, “their lord” (13.194), and the “sons of lords” whose likeness Athena takes (13.223).
---

**Gods.** Alcinous sacrifices an ox for the farewell feast “to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all” (ὃς πᾶσιν ἀνάσσει, 13.25; see [[zeus]] and [[lord-of-dark-clouds]]), the verse in which Odysseus told of sacrificing the Cyclops' ram after his escape, an offering Zeus did not heed (9.551–555). When the ship that carried him comes home, the Phaeacians stand around the altar and pray “to lord Poseidon” (Ποσειδάωνι ἄνακτι, 13.185; see [[poseidon]] and [[prayer]]), the god who has just turned it to stone.

**The lord of the land.** When Odysseus wakes, Athena's mist makes everything strange: “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (ἄνακτι, 13.194–196; see [[eidos]]). The paths, harbors, rocks and trees are his, and their lord does not know them.

**Sons of lords.** Athena meets him “in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords” (οἷοί τε ἀνάκτων παῖδες ἔασι, 13.222–223; see [[athena]] and [[shape-changing]]). Princes herd in Homer: Achilles once caught two sons of Priam on Ida, “catching them as they herded sheep” (*Iliad* 11.101–106), and killed Andromache's brothers “among their shambling cattle and white sheep” (*Iliad* 6.423–424). Odysseus greets the young noble with a suppliant's words (13.228–235; see [[supplication]]).
