---
summary: Virgil builds the Libyan harbor where Aeneas lands after the storm from the harbor of Phorcys, with its cave of the nymphs and its ships held by no cable. His Venus meets her son on an unknown shore as Athena meets Odysseus, in disguise and with a weapon in her hand, tells him the land, and shows herself only as she goes, and Aeneas reproaches her disguises as Odysseus does Athena's. The Trojans sail past the rocks of Ithaca, cursing the land of Ulysses, and lose sight of the heights of the Phaeacians; Deiphobus' last sleep is “most like a peaceful death”, and the ships of the boat race outrun chariots.
---

- **The Libyan harbor.** The harbor where the storm leaves Aeneas is sheltered by an island and by twin cliffs, and under the cliff is a cave with sweet waters and seats of living rock, “the home of the nymphs”, where no cable holds the weary ships and no anchor (*Aeneid* 1.159–169, plain renderings), as the harbor of Phorcys is sheltered by two headlands and ships stay in it “without a mooring rope”, beside the cave of the Naiads with its ever-flowing waters (13.96–112): [[virgils-storm]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]].
- **The goddess on the shore.** At dawn Aeneas goes out to learn what shores the wind has brought him to, and who holds them, “men or beasts” (*Aeneid* 1.305–309), as Odysseus, waking, asks whether the people of the land are “arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers” (13.200–202). His mother meets him in the wood “wearing a maiden's face and dress, and a maiden's arms” (*virginis os habitumque gerens, et virginis arma*, *Aeneid* 1.315), a huntress with her bow; Athena comes to Odysseus “in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep”, with a javelin in her hands (13.221–225). Aeneas begs her to tell him “under what sky, on what shores of the world” they are tossed, for “not knowing men or places we wander” (*Aeneid* 1.331–333), as Odysseus asks “what land is this, what people, what men are born here?” (13.233), and Venus tells him whose land it is, as Athena tells Odysseus (*Aeneid* 1.338–368; 13.237–249). As she turns away she shines out, “and by her step she was revealed a true goddess”, and Aeneas calls after her: “Why do you, cruel too, mock your son so often with false shapes? Why may I not join hand to hand, and hear and answer true words?” (*quid natum totiens, crudelis tu quoque, falsis / ludis imaginibus?*, *Aeneid* 1.402–409). Odysseus says to the goddess revealed, “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313). Aeneas' first words to her, “surely a goddess”, are those that Merry sets beside Odysseus' first words to Nausicaa (*Aeneid* 1.327–329; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.149): [[virgils-dido-as-diana]], [[athena]] and [[shape-changing]].
- **Past Ithaca.** Sailing on from the Strophades, the Trojans see Zacynthos, Dulichium, Same and “Neritos, steep with rocks” rise out of the sea: “We flee the crags of Ithaca, the realm of Laertes, and curse the land that nursed cruel Ulysses” (*effugimus scopulos Ithacae, Laertia regna, / et terram altricem saevi exsecramur Ulixi*, *Aeneid* 3.270–273); farther on, “at once we lose sight of the airy heights of the Phaeacians” (*protinus aerias Phaeacum abscondimus arces*, *Aeneid* 3.291). In this book Athena calls Ithaca rugged and Neritum a mountain “clothed in forest” (13.242, 13.351): [[ithaca]], [[neritum]] and [[phaeacians]].
- **A sleep like death.** On the last night of Troy, Deiphobus tells Aeneas, he lay in his chamber “worn out with cares and heavy with sleep, and a rest lay on me, sweet and deep and most like a peaceful death” (*dulcis et alta quies placidaeque simillima morti*, *Aeneid* 6.520–522), while Helen let Menelaus in. The sleep that falls on Odysseus in the Phaeacian ship is “unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (13.80): [[sweet-sleep]] and [[hypnos]].
- **Ships and chariots.** At the start of the boat race in Sicily, “not so headlong do chariots in the two-horse contest seize the plain and rush pouring from the barrier, nor so do the charioteers shake the waving reins over the loosed teams and hang forward over the lash” (*Aeneid* 5.144–147). The Phaeacian ship runs as four stallions lift themselves high “under the blows of the whip” (13.81–83): [[the-four-stallions]].
