summary: The book is the main telling. At nightfall Odysseus lies down on the deck of the Phaeacian ship and sleeps as it runs through the night (13.70–92); at the rising of the morning star the crew bring it into the harbor of Phorcys, lay him on the sand asleep and set his gifts by the olive (13.93–125). He wakes in a mist and does not know his land (13.187–221); Athena comes to him as a young herdsman, hears his false tale, makes herself known, shows him Ithaca and helps him hide the gifts in the cave of the nymphs (13.221–371).

**The bed on the deck.** Arete sends three slave women down to the ship with Odysseus, carrying a freshly washed mantle and a tunic, the close-fitted chest that she filled for him, and bread and red wine (13.66–69; 8.438–448; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[chest]]). The noble escorts, πομπῆες ἀγαυοί (13.71; see [[pompe]]), stow them and spread a rug and a linen sheet on the deck at the stern, ἵνα νήγρετον εὕδοι, “so that he might sleep unwaking” (13.73–75; see [[blankets]], [[deck]] and [[stern]]). Odysseus goes on board and lies down σιγῇ, “in silence”; the rowers sit at the benches “in good order” and loose the cable from the pierced stone (13.75–77; see [[silence]], [[rowing-benches]] and [[stern-cables]]). “When they leaned back and tossed up the sea with the oar-blade” (13.78): ἀνακλινθέντες is an aorist describing the movement (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.78). It is the stroke of which Alcinous had boasted, “and you yourself will know in your mind how far the best / are my ships, and my young men at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.327–328; see [[alcinous]]).

**Most like death.** “Then sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids, / unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (13.79–80; see [[sweet-sleep]], [[hypnos]] and [[sleep]]). νήδυμος, “sweet”, is in Monro's view an early corruption of ϝήδυμος (on 13.79). νήγρετος, “unwaking”, is found in Homer only here and six verses before, of the bed spread for this sleep (13.74), and θανάτῳ ἄγχιστα ἐοικώς, “most like death”, only here (see the translation's key-word row and [[thanatos]]). Monro found ἥδιστος, “sweetest”, weak after νήδυμος, and suggested ἥκιστος, “most gentle”, “feeblest”, the word in which the Iliad makes Meriones “the least skilled at driving a chariot” (Monro's critical note on 13.80; *Iliad* 23.531). The sleep is the one Alcinous promised, “and then you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm” (7.318–319), and the one for which Arete bade him tie a knot on his chest, “when hereafter / you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship” (8.444–445).

**The run through the night.** The ship's speed is told in two images, the four stallions that leap together under the whip and the falcon that could not keep pace with her (13.81–87; see [[the-four-stallions]], [[falcon]] and [[hawk]]). ἡ δʼ, “and she”, is left without a verb and taken up again at ὣς ἄρα τῆς, “so her stern”, a harshness softened by the nominative ἵπποι of the simile (Monro on 13.81). Some read πρῴρη, “prow”, for πρύμνη, “stern”, as a truer picture, but Monro asks how πρύμνη could then have crept into the text (on 13.84; see the translation's note on 13.81–84). ἴρηξ κίρκος, “a hawk, / a falcon”: in such pairs the second term is usually the specific one, as βοῦς ταῦρος, σῦς κάπρος (Monro on 13.86); ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, “the nimblest of winged birds”, is the falcon of Achilles' pursuit of Hector (*Iliad* 22.139). For ἔταμνεν, “cutting”, some manuscripts have ἔτεμνεν and ἔτετμεν (Monro's critical note on 13.88). Then the poet turns from the ship to the man it carries:

{{quote:87f9dbaa-8f95-50db-a37c-19058baded4e}}

θεοῖς ἐναλίγκια μήδεʼ, “counsels like the gods'”, is a brachylogy, “like the (counsels of the) gods”, as κόμαι Χαρίτεσσιν ὁμοῖαι, “hair like the Graces'” (Monro on 13.89; *Iliad* 17.51; see the translation's note on 13.89). πτολέμους and κύματα carry on the construction of πολλὰ πάθʼ ἄλγεα, while πείρων, “passing through”, belongs only to the waves, so that it is not properly a zeugma (Monro on 13.91). The verse was Achilles' before it was Odysseus': in the Iliad he lies awake remembering the sorrows he had suffered with Patroclus, “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (*Iliad* 24.6–8); Odysseus used it of himself in his answer to Euryalus at the Phaeacian games (8.182–183), and he uses it again in his lie to Athena (13.264; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). ἀτρέμας εὗδε, “he slept motionless” (13.92), closes the voyage with the stillness of the sleeper against the speed of the ship.

**The harbor.** “When that brightest star rose up which most of all / comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn” (13.93–94; see [[the-morning-star]], [[eos]] and [[early-born]]): ὑπερέσχε is intransitive, “rises”, and it is the dawn of the thirty-fifth day of the action (Monro on 13.93; see the translation's note on 13.93). The ship draws near to the island (13.95), and the poet describes the harbor before he brings her into it: the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, with two jutting headlands that crouch down toward it and keep out the waves raised by the winds, so that ships stay within without a mooring rope, the long-leaved olive at its head, and the misty cave of the Naiads beside it, with its stone bowls and jars, its bees, its looms of stone and its two doors, one for men and one for the gods (13.96–112; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[phorcys-the-sea-god]], [[olive]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). Monro explains ποτιπεπτηυῖαι as “crouching towards”, sinking down in front of the harbor and so closing it in, and ὅρμου μέτρον, “the measure of anchorage”, as the distance at which ships are fastened by a cable to the shore, if fastening is needed (on 13.98, 13.101; see the translation's note on 13.98–99).

**Set ashore.** “There they rowed in, knowing the place before” (13.113). The ship runs up on the land ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ ἥμισυ πάσης, “to half its length” (Monro on 13.114, comparing *Iliad* 10.351), so hard is she driven by the rowers (13.115). They lift Odysseus out first, αὐτῷ σύν τε λίνῳ καὶ ῥήγεϊ σιγαλόεντι, “linen sheet and glossy rug and all”, “with the linen cloth as it was” (13.118; Monro on 13.118), and lay him on the sand δεδμημένον ὕπνῳ, “overcome by sleep” (13.119), the words of Alcinous' promise (7.318). Then they lift out the possessions which the Phaeacians had given him “through great-hearted Athena”, διά meaning “by the agency of” (13.120–121; Monro on 13.121; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]], [[possessions]] and [[athena]]), and set them together by the trunk of the olive, away from the road, μή πώς τις ὁδιτάων ἀνθρώπων, “lest some wayfarer” should come and harm them before Odysseus woke (13.122–124). Aristarchus read μή πω, “that no one yet”, a use of πω that Monro thinks can hardly be defended (on 13.123). Manuscripts vary between κτήματα and χρήματα here, as often (Monro's critical note on 13.120). Then the crew go home (13.125), and the poem follows them and Poseidon to Scheria before it comes back to the sleeper (13.125–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**The waking.** “And brilliant Odysseus woke / where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it— / after being away so long” (13.187–189). ἤδη δὴν ἀπεών, “after being away so long”, belongs with “where he slept in the land of his fathers”, and περὶ γάρ … gives the reason why he did not know it (Monro on 13.189; see the translation's note on 13.189–191). The goddess had poured a mist around him, ὄφρα μιν αὐτὸν / ἄγνωστον τεύξειεν, “so that she might make him / unknown”: αὐτόν is “himself”, his person, as in Odysseus' complaint that she makes herself, σὲ … αὐτήν, like anything (13.190–191, 13.313; Monro on 13.190; Aristophanes read αὐτῷ, Monro's critical note). Monro denies that the mist was meant to make Odysseus invisible. Athena meant to prepare him by consultation and by changing his appearance, as she does at the end of the book, and ἄγνωστον τεύξειεν refers to the same process as ἄγνωστον τεύξω, “I will make you unknown”, in 13.397; the talk comes before the change in time, so that there is a prothysteron, putting the more definite act first (on 13.191; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (13.194–196). The manuscripts are divided between φαίνετο and φαινέσκετο; Monro prints ἀλλοειδέʼ ἐφαίνετο, since the frequentative is out of place here and the slight change is called for by the caesura (on 13.194; see the translation's note on 13.194). After 13.197 some witnesses add the verse with which Odysseus meets Athena (13.226; Monro's critical note).

**The lament.** “He groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs / with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word” (13.198–199), the verses of Patroclus in the hut of Eurypylus when he sees the Trojans storming the wall (*Iliad* 15.397–398). His first words are the question he asked when he woke on the shore of Scheria, and before the Cyclops' island: “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time? / Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (13.200–202 = 6.119–121; 9.175–176; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). It is a double question, “are they savage or hospitable?”, as Odysseus will ask the herdsman whether the land is an island or a shore of the mainland (Monro on 13.201–202, comparing 13.234). Then he thinks of his goods, πῇ δὴ χρήματα πολλὰ φέρω τάδε; “Where am I taking all these goods?” (13.203), which Monro renders “where am I with all this wealth in my hands?” (on 13.203); he wishes that they had stayed among the Phaeacians and he had gone to some other king (13.204–206). The Phaeacians, he decides, “were not altogether discerning or just”, in Nestor's words for the Argives after the sack (3.133), οὐκ ἄρα … ἦσαν, “they are not, as I thought they were” (13.209–212; Monro on 13.209; see [[phaeacians]]), and he prays that Zeus the god of suppliants may punish them (13.213–214; see [[zeus]] and [[supplication]]). τίσαιτο is Aristarchus' reading; Zenodotus read the imperative τισάσθω, which, Monro suggests, may have seemed improper applied to a deity (Monro's critical note on 13.213). He counts the tripods, the cauldrons, the gold and the woven clothes, μή τί μοι οἴχωνται, “to see whether they have gone off with anything” (13.215–218; Monro on 13.216; see [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]), misses nothing, and drags himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, mourning for his native land (13.219–221; see [[dear-native-land]]).

**The herdsman.** “And Athena came near to him, / in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords” (13.221–223; see [[athena]] and [[shape-changing]]). She wears a cape folded double, has sandals under her shining feet and a javelin in her hands (13.224–225; see [[cloak]] and [[spear]]). Odysseus greets the first man he has met as he would greet a god: “save these things, and save me; for to you / I pray as to a god, and I come to your dear knees” (13.230–231; see [[supplication]]); σάω, unless it is a mistake for σάου, points to a contracted non-thematic form (Monro on 13.230). He asks the stranger to tell him truly “what land is this, what people, what men are born here?”, whether one of the clear-seen islands or a shore of the mainland (13.232–235; see [[tell-me-this-truly]] and [[clear-seen]]). εὐδείελος is for Monro “shining”, like Horace's *nitentes Cycladas*, “the gleaming Cyclades” (on 13.234), and ἀκτὴ is to be joined with ἠπείροιο, “a shore of the mainland” (Monro on 13.235).

**The name of Ithaca.** The answer begins as the Cyclops' answer to Odysseus' talk of the gods, “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away” (13.237 = 9.273; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The text prints the vulgate τήνδε τε, which Monro calls impossible; one of the best manuscripts has τήνδε γε, “if you ask about *this* land” (Monro on 13.238). The land is not nameless: very many know it, those toward the dawn and those “behind, toward the misty darkness”, μετόπισθε being westwards, the west being the end, as the east is the beginning, of the day (13.238–241; Monro on 13.241; see the translation's note on 13.241). The account of the island that follows, rugged and narrow but rich in grain and wine, rain and dew, pasture and water, ends with its fame: “Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy, / which they say is far from the Achaean land” (13.242–249; see [[ithaca]]). Odysseus' joy is told in the verses that will greet the land when it is revealed (13.250–251; 13.353–354), but he answers with a lie, “always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (13.254–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**The goddess revealed.** “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled, / and stroked him with her hand; and she was like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.287–289; see [[smiling-at-him]]). In this likeness she will come to Odysseus again at the swineherd's hut (16.157–158; see [[shape-changing]]). The third verse is omitted in two manuscripts (Monro's critical note on 13.289). Her speech begins in mockery and admiration (13.291–302; see the general article and [[crafty-would-he-be]]). κερδαλέος, “crafty”, goes with πολυκερδής, “very crafty”, of his mind (13.255), and with κέρδεα, “crafty ways”, of both of them (13.297, 13.299; Monro on 13.291; see [[kerdos]]); “even if it were a god who met you” looks forward to his answer, that it is hard for a mortal who meets her to know her (Monro on 13.292, comparing 13.312). σχέτλιε, “Hard man”, is said “in a friendly and admiring tone” (Monro on 13.293, citing *Iliad* 22.41 and 22.86, where Priam and Hecuba call Achilles σχέτλιος; see [[hard]]); ποικιλομῆτα, “of subtle wiles”, is the only vocative of the epithet in Homer, and δόλων ἆτʼ, “insatiable of tricks”, is Socus' taunt to Odysseus in the Iliad (*Iliad* 11.430; see [[of-subtle-wiles]]); Monro would write ἆατʼ (critical note on 13.293). The tricks are dear to him πεδόθεν, “from the ground up”, like *funditus*, but probably with the notion of constancy, as in ἔμπεδος; the vulgate παιδόθεν, “from childhood”, is itacism (13.295; Monro on 13.295; see the translation's note on 13.295). Then she names herself, “Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus”, who always stands by him and made him dear to the Phaeacians, and tells him why she has come: to weave a plan with him, to hide his goods, and to tell him the sorrows he must endure at home, revealing himself to no one, man or woman, οὕνεκα being “that”, a sense confined in Homer to the Odyssey (13.299–310; Monro on 13.309; see [[metis]]).

**“You make yourself like anything.”** Odysseus answers that it is hard for a mortal to know her, “even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]). She was kind to him at Troy, he says; but after the sack, “when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans”, in Nestor's verses, he never saw her on his ship to ward off sorrow (13.314–319; 3.130–131; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The next four verses, on his wandering until she encouraged him in the land of the Phaeacians and led him into the city, are generally condemned as spurious: the first two make an un-Homeric parenthesis, since πρίν γʼ ὅτε goes with “after that I did not see you” (13.318), and the ancients argued that Odysseus could not know of her presence in Phaeacia, since she did not reveal herself to him there, an objection that supposes more accuracy than a poem allows, though Monro grants that 13.322–323 have something of the air of an insertion meant to reconcile this speech with the Phaeacian episode, especially 7.12–81 (Monro on 13.320–323; see the translation's note on 13.320–323 and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). He implores her by her father to tell him whether this is truly his native land, for he thinks she is κερτομέουσα, “seeking to vex” him (13.324–328; Monro on 13.326).

**The land shown.** “Always such is the thought in your breast; / therefore I cannot abandon you, unhappy as you are, / because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.330–332). ἐπητής is “charming”, “polite”, and ἐπητύς is “civility” (Monro on 13.332, citing 21.306; see [[sensible]]). Another man coming home would have hurried to see his wife and children, but he will first make trial of his wife, πειρήσεαι, “observe for yourself”, not trusting to report, while any other man would have made for his home without even inquiring (13.333–338; Monro on 13.336); Aristarchus rejected these six verses (Monro's critical note on 13.333–338; see [[penelope]]). She never doubted that he would come home, having lost all his companions, but she would not fight with Poseidon, her father's brother, angry because he blinded his son (13.339–343; see [[poseidon]], [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]). Then she shows him “the seat of Ithaca”: the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave where he used to offer the nymphs perfect hecatombs, and Mount Neritum, clothed in forest (13.344–351; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[neritum]] and [[hecatomb]]). Some manuscripts omit 13.347–348, which repeat the description of the cave from 13.103–104, and Monro brackets them: the cave, he holds, is first pointed out at 13.349, with the deictic τοῦτο (Monro on 13.347–348; see the translation's note on 13.347–348). “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view” (13.352). Odysseus rejoices and kisses the grain-giving earth, as he kissed the earth of Scheria when he came out of the river, and as Agamemnon kissed his native land (13.353–354; 5.463; 4.521–522; see [[grain-giving]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), and prays to the Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus, with the promise of gifts “as before”, in which Monro sees Odysseus associating Telemachus with himself (13.355–360; Monro on 13.358; see [[nymphs]] and [[prayer]]).

**The gifts hidden.** “Take heart” (13.362, the verse in which Hephaestus answers Thetis, *Iliad* 18.463): they are to put the goods at once in the inner part of the wondrous cave, ἵνα, “where”, they may stay safe (13.362–365; Monro on 13.364; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The text's θείμεν is no form; the short-vowel subjunctive θείομεν, which Monro prints, is meant (see the translation's note on 13.364). The goddess goes into the misty cave searching for its hiding places, and Odysseus brings near the gold, the unyielding bronze and the well-made clothes that the Phaeacians had given him; three manuscripts add here the verses on the gifts given “through great-hearted Athena” from the landing (13.366–369; 13.120–121; Monro's critical note on 13.369). “And she laid them away well, and set a stone against the doors, / Pallas Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (13.370–371; see [[door]] and [[possessions]]). The gifts stay in the cave: Odysseus tells his son that they lie in caves by the will of the gods (16.232, a plain rendering). The two then sit by the trunk of the sacred olive to plan the suitors' death (13.372–373; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).
