---
summary: The book crosses the sea once more, by night. The Phaeacians toss up the sea with their oars, and the ship runs on with the great wave of the loud-roaring sea seething behind her stern, “cutting the waves of the sea” (13.78–88), carrying a man who had passed through “the grievous waves” (13.91). Poseidon would shatter the ship “on the misty sea” as she comes home (13.150); on Ithaca Odysseus walks along the loud-roaring sea (13.220), and fears that his son too is wandering “over the barren sea” (13.418–419).
---

**The night crossing.** “When they leaned back and tossed up the sea with the oar-blade, / then sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids” (13.78–79): ἀνερρίπτουν ἅλα πηδῷ is the stroke of which Alcinous had boasted, his young men “at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.328; see the translation's key-word row and [[oars]]). The ship runs “so her stern lifted, and behind her / the great surging wave of the loud-roaring sea seethed” (13.84–85; see [[loud-roaring-sea]] and [[stern]]): πορφύρεον, “surging”, is said of a wave as at the meeting of Poseidon and Tyro, where “the surging wave stood around them, high as a mountain” (11.243; see the translation's formula row for 13.85). “So swiftly she ran, cutting the waves of the sea” (13.88), where the manuscripts are divided between ἔταμνεν, ἔτεμνεν and ἔτετμεν (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.88). She bears a man “who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.90–91; see [[ships]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). πτολέμους and κύματα both depend on πάθʼ ἄλγεα, while πείρων, “passing through”, belongs only to the waves, so that the verse is not properly a zeugma (Monro on 13.91). It is the verse of Achilles' sleepless night in the Iliad and of Odysseus' answer at the Phaeacian games, and Odysseus will use it again of himself in his lie (*Iliad* 24.8; 8.183; 13.264).

**Poseidon's sea.** Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians “carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca” (13.134–135; see [[poseidon]]), and he means to shatter their ship “as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea” (13.150), the words of Nausithous' prophecy, which Alcinous repeats (13.176; see [[misty]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). The Phaeacians who watch the ship turned to stone ask, “who has fettered the swift ship on the sea”, with ἐπέδησε, the verb of a fetter (13.168; see the translation's key-word row).

**The sea round Ithaca.** On Ithaca the sea is the boundary of the land he does not know: he walks “along the shore of the loud-roaring sea” (13.220; see [[shore]]), and asks whether this is an island, or a shore of the mainland “that lies upon the sea”, ἁλὶ κεκλιμένη, as Telemachus said of the islands, “those that lie upon the sea” (13.234–235; 4.608; Monro on 13.235). The Cretan of his tale heard of Ithaca “far over the sea” (13.257; see [[crete]]). When Athena tells him that his son has gone to Sparta, he asks whether it was “so that he too, wandering, might suffer sorrows / over the barren sea” (13.418–419; see [[barren-sea]] and [[telemachus]]).
