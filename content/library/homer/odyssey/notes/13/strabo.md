---
summary: Strabo names this book's event the sailing in of Odysseus to Ithaca, κατάπλους, and sets it with the killing of the suitors among things that no sensible reader insists happened in just the poet's way. He counts the two doors of the cave of the Naiads, one toward the north and one toward the south, among the proofs of Homer's learning in geography, but finds no such cave on Ithaca in his own day, and he explains Odysseus' lies by the poet's way of mixing truth with fiction.
---

- **The sailing in.** Those who say that Homer set the wanderings around Sicily and Italy may mean it in a better or a worse way, Strabo says: better, if they mean that the poet, persuaded that the wandering took place there, took that as true and elaborated it as a poet; worse, if they take the elaboration itself for history, Ocean and Hades and the cattle of the Sun, entertainment by goddesses and transformations, the sizes of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians and the shape of Scylla, which the poet plainly tells as marvels. Such a man so plainly falsifies the poet that he is not worth arguing with, any more than if he said that “the sailing in of Odysseus to Ithaca” (τὸν εἰς τὴν Ἰθάκην κατάπλουν τοῦ Ὀδυσσέως), the killing of the suitors and the battle that the Ithacans fought against him in the country happened in just that way (*Geography* 1.2.11, plain renderings). The landing of this book is the first of the three: [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]], [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[aristotle-the-putting-ashore]].
- **The two doors.** Homer shows his wide learning in geography, Strabo argues against Eratosthenes, in the latitudes and the winds that he names when he describes a place, and among his examples are the verses that set Ithaca “toward the darkness” and the other islands “toward the dawn and the sun” (*Odyssey* 9.25–26) and those that give the cave two doors, “one toward the north, the other toward the south”, a free quotation of 13.109–111 (*Geography* 1.2.20, plain renderings): [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[boreas]] and [[notus]].
- **No such cave.** Among the changes that the land has suffered, islands joined to the mainland and peninsulas cut into islands, Strabo notes that Asteris, whose harbors Homer gives “an entrance on either side” (*Odyssey* 4.847), now has not even a good anchorage, and that “in Ithaca there is no such cave, nor any shrine of the nymphs, as Homer describes”; it is better, he says, to put this down to change than to ignorance or to a false account of the places for the sake of the myth, and since the matter is unclear he leaves it to common inquiry (*Geography* 1.3.18, plain renderings): [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[ithaca]] and [[asteris]].
- **Lies like the truth.** Homer, Strabo holds, built his myths on facts, adding fiction to true events as a man pours gold around silver; for one lies more persuasively by mixing in some of the truth, as Polybius says of the wanderings of Odysseus, and so the poet says of Odysseus' tale to Penelope, “He made the many lies he told like the truth” (*Odyssey* 19.203, a plain rendering): “not all, but many, for otherwise they would not have been like the truth” (*Geography* 1.2.9, a plain rendering). The first of Odysseus' lying tales is told in this book, to the disguised Athena (13.256–286): [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[polybius]].
