---
summary: The maxim opens Odysseus' answer to Athena (13.311–328): he knew her kindness at Troy, but after the city fell he never saw her come aboard his ship to help him; now he begs her by her father to tell him whether this is truly his native land. She answers that she would not fight her father's brother (13.341–343).
---

**The reproach.** After the maxim Odysseus turns what he cannot know into a complaint: “But this I know well, that before now you were kind to me, / while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy. / But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans, / after that I did not see you, daughter of Zeus, nor did I notice you / boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.314–319; see [[athena]] and [[raiding]]). The next four verses, of his wandering until she encouraged him in the land of the Phaeacians and led him into the city, are generally condemned as spurious (13.320–323; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.320–323; see the translation's note on 13.320–323). There can be little doubt about the first two, since πρίν γʼ ὅτε goes with “I did not see you” (13.318). Against the second two the ancients argued that Odysseus could not know that Athena had been with him in Phaeacia, since she did not reveal herself to him there; Monro finds the objection too exact for a poem, but grants that the verses have something of the air of an insertion meant to reconcile the speech with the Phaeacian episode (7.12–81), and Nitzsch rejected all four.

**The goddess's answer.** Then he begs her “by your father” to tell him whether he has come to his own land, for he thinks she is taunting him (13.324–328; see [[supplication]]). She answers that he is always the same, and that is why she cannot leave him (13.330–332), and she gives the reason for her absence: “but I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343; see [[poseidon]]). It is the reason the poem itself gave on Scheria, where she “did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). What he could not know was hidden from him on purpose.
