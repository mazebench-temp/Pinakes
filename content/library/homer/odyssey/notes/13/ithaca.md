summary: Odysseus comes home asleep and wakes in a land he does not know. The Phaeacians set him down at the harbor of Phorcys “in the land of Ithaca” (13.96–97); waking in a mist, he complains that they have not brought him “to clear-seen Ithaca” (13.212); Athena, in the likeness of a young herdsman, tells him the name of the island, which “has reached even to Troy” (13.237–249), and at last shows him “the seat of Ithaca” and scatters the mist (13.344–354).

**Home asleep.** The island enters the book as the end of a night's voyage: when the morning star rises, “then the seafaring ship drew near to the island” (13.93–95; see [[the-morning-star]]), and the poet places the harbor of Phorcys ἐν δήμῳ Ἰθάκης, “in the land of Ithaca” (13.96–97; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). Poseidon, complaining to Zeus, names the island as the end of the escort: “But they carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca, and gave him untold gifts” (13.134–135; see [[poseidon]] and [[phaeacians]]). Odysseus' homecoming is accomplished while he sleeps (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[nostos]]).

**A land he does not know.** “And brilliant Odysseus woke / where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it— / after being away so long; for the goddess had poured a mist around him” (13.187–189; see [[athena]]). Everything looks strange: “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (13.194–196). The strangeness of Ithaca to a man after his long absence, D. B. Monro suggests, “may be only the exaggeration of a natural effect”, and he quotes Wordsworth's *The Brothers*, where a man walking through fields once well known to him imagines “that the rocks / And everlasting hills themselves were changed” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, additional note on 13.194, p. 545). Odysseus complains that the Phaeacians “told me / they would bring me to clear-seen Ithaca, and they have not done it” (13.211–212; see [[clear-seen]]); εὐδείελος is for Monro “shining”, like Horace's *nitentes Cycladas* (on 13.212, 13.234). When he has counted his gifts, “he mourned for his native land” (13.219; see [[dear-native-land]]).

**Athena's account.** The young herdsman to whom he appeals answers his question, “what land is this” (13.233), with the name of the island, held back to the end of the speech:

{{quote:831e7d6c-1f00-5dde-b37d-e00fc309dbf4}}

τήνδε γε, “if you ask about *this* land”, is the reading of one of the best manuscripts; the vulgate τήνδε τε is impossible (Monro on 13.238). Those who live “behind, toward the misty darkness” are those to the west, μετόπισθε, “the west being the end, as the east is the beginning, of the day” (Monro on 13.241; see the translation's note on 13.241): Athena divides the world by the words with which Odysseus placed his island among its neighbors, “farthest out of all / toward the darkness, while the others lie apart toward the dawn and the sun” (9.25–26; see the Book 9 note, where Strabo's interpreters took ζόφος for the north). For οὐδʼ at 13.243, which Aristarchus read, the vulgate has οὐκ (Monro's critical note on 13.243). The account agrees with Telemachus' refusal of Menelaus' horses, that Ithaca has no broad courses and no meadow and is “a goat-pasturing land” (4.601–608; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 555; see [[goats]] and [[rugged]]): the translation keeps λυπρός “poor”, βούβοτος “cattle-pasturing” and ἀρδμοί “watering places” beside the αἰγίβοτος “goat-pasturing” and ἱππήλατος “good for driving horses” of Book 4 (see its key-word row). The island's name has reached Troy as Odysseus' fame reaches the sky (9.20; see [[kleos]] and [[troy]]); ὄνομα, “name”, has here the sense of renown.

**The lie.** Odysseus, glad, does not tell the truth (13.250–255). His first words take up the herdsman's: “I heard of Ithaca, yes, even in wide Crete, / far over the sea” (13.256–257; see [[crete]] and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). The Suda quotes the half-verse πυνθανόμην Ἰθάκης γε to show πυνθάνεσθαι used, in Homer and the orators, of hearing and learning rather than asking (Suda π 3173). When the goddess has made herself known, he still will not believe her: “for I do not think / that I have come to clear-seen Ithaca, but I am roaming some other / land; and I think that you are taunting me / in saying these things, to deceive my mind— / tell me if it is true that I have come to my dear native land” (13.324–328; see [[eperopeuo]]). In the last book the question comes back in his own mouth, as part of another feigned tale, when he asks his father “whether it is truly Ithaca that we have come to” (24.259, a plain rendering; see [[laertes]]).

**The seat of Ithaca.** Athena answers with the places themselves:

{{quote:fd50addf-150e-5d0a-82e4-28b9d38a29d5}}

ἕδος, “seat”, is the word of the gods' seat on Olympus and of the seat of seven-gated Thebes (6.42; 11.263; *Iliad* 4.406). She names the harbor, the olive and the cave in the order and in the words of the description of 13.96–112, and adds the mountain (see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[olive]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[neritum]]). “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view; / and then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / rejoicing in his own land, and kissed the grain-giving earth” (13.352–354; see [[earth]] and [[grain-giving]]). The second and third verses echo 13.250–251, where his joy came before the lie; the kiss is the one he gave the earth of Scheria when he came out of the river (5.463), and Agamemnon “touched his native land and kissed it” when he came home to his death (4.522; see [[agamemnon]]). Monro took the lifting of the mist for a figure of the poem itself: from Book 13 on, the folklore of the wanderings is still present but “held in solution, so to speak, in the mass of heroic mythology”, and “Like Ulysses after his landing in Ithaca, when Athene removed the mist from his eyes, we find ourselves in a familiar world—the world of the Trojan story” (Monro, Appendix I §3).
