summary: The blinding has its last consequences. Poseidon remembers his threats against Odysseus and punishes the Phaeacians who brought him home (13.125–164), and on Ithaca Athena gives the blinding as the reason why she would not fight her father's brother: he has laid up a grudge against Odysseus, “angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343). Three verses of the Cyclops' story come back in the book.

**The grudge.** Odysseus has reached home, and Athena tells him why she left him alone on the sea: “I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343; see [[athena]], [[poseidon]] and [[kotos]]). The last two verses are the words in which Teiresias foretold that Odysseus would not escape the earth-shaker (11.102–103; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]), with ὅς, “who”, for the seer's conjunction ὅ, “since” (see the translation's note on 11.102). Some manuscripts read χόλον, “anger”, for κότον, “grudge” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.342; see [[cholos]]). The god's son is not named; nor does Poseidon name him when he complains to Zeus of the Phaeacians, remembering only “the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first” (13.126–127; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). The Phaeacians, “sprung from my own stock” (13.130), whose founder was Poseidon's son, pay for escorting the man who blinded another of his sons (see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**Verses of the cave.** Three verses of the story come back in the book. When the ship is turned to stone, Alcinous recognizes his father's prophecy in the words of the blinded Cyclops recognizing the prophecy of Telemus, “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me” (13.172 = 9.507; see [[thesphaton]]). When Odysseus wakes in Ithaca, he asks whether its people are “arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (13.201–202), the question with which he went to learn what men the Cyclopes were (9.175–176; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). And when he asks the disguised Athena what land this is, she begins her answer with the Cyclops' scorn of the man who bade him fear the gods, “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away” (13.237 = 9.273; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).
