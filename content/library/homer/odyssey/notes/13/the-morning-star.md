**The star.** εὖτʼ ἀστὴρ ὑπερέσχε φαάντατος, ὅς τε μάλιστα / ἔρχεται ἀγγέλλων φάος Ἠοῦς ἠριγενείης (13.93–94; see [[eos]] and [[early-born]]). ὑπερέσχε is intransitive, “rises” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.93; see the translation's note on 13.93). φαάντατος, “brightest”, the superlative of φαεινός, “shining”, is found only here in Homer. The star is not named. It is known by its office, the star “which most of all comes to announce” the light (see the translation's row ἀστὴρ φαάντατος), as the Iliad's morning star goes forth “to herald the light over the earth” (*Iliad* 23.226; see the general article). Later readers gave it the Iliad's name: the Suda, glossing the evening star of the Iliad, “the fairest”, notes that elsewhere Homer calls Eosphorus φαάντατος (Suda ε 3188).

**The hour.** It is the dawn of the thirty-fifth day of the action (Monro on 13.93). The ship left Scheria at sunset, the sunset that was as welcome to Odysseus as to a plowman longing for his supper (13.33–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]]), and ran all night, swifter than a falcon (13.81–92; see [[the-four-stallions]]). The arrival is told as the poems tell an hour, “When … then”, εὖτʼ … τῆμος δή (13.93, 13.95), and what the hour brings is the landfall: τῆμος δὴ νήσῳ προσεπίλνατο ποντοπόρος νηῦς, “then the seafaring ship drew near to the island” (13.95; see [[seafaring-ship]]). The island is named only in the next verses, which describe the harbor of Phorcys “in the land of Ithaca” before the ship enters it (13.96–113; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[ithaca]] and [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]).

**Asleep at daybreak.** The light comes, but Odysseus does not see it. The Phaeacians lift him out of the ship with his linen sheet and rug and lay him on the sand, “overcome by sleep” (13.116–119), and the poem follows their ship home to Scheria, where Poseidon turns it to stone, before it comes back to him waking (13.125–187; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). In the Iliad the morning star ends a night of mourning at a pyre (*Iliad* 23.217–228); here it ends a night of sleep “most like death” (13.80; see [[sweet-sleep]]) and finds Odysseus in his own land.
