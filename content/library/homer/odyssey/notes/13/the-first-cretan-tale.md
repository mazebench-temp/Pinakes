Told to Athena, who knows the truth, in the likeness of a young herdsman, it runs from the exile's flight from Crete to his waking alone on this shore (13.256–286), between the poet's warning that he “did not speak the truth” (13.254–255) and the goddess' smile (13.287).

**The frame.** Odysseus is glad, rejoicing in the land of his fathers, and he answers the herdsman, “yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word, / always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (13.250–255). πάλιν … λάζετο μῦθον is “took back his speech”, that is, left unsaid what he would have said if he had spoken the truth (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.254); in the Iliad the same words are Agamemnon's, when he sees that Odysseus is angry and takes back his rebuke (*Iliad* 4.357). νωμῶν is “turning about”, “revolving”, and πολυκερδέα “very cunning” (Monro on 13.255; see [[kerdos]]), an adjective found in Homer only here, beside πολυκερδείῃσιν, “in his great craftiness”, of the same man in the last book (24.167, a plain rendering).

**Crete.** “I heard of Ithaca, yes, even in wide Crete, / far over the sea” (13.256–257): the goddess has just said that the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy (13.248), and he caps her with Crete (see [[crete]]). One manuscript reads Τροίῃ for Κρήτῃ (Monro's critical note on 13.256). He has come with these goods, leaving as much again, ἔτι τοσαῦτα, to his children (13.258; Monro on 13.258), and he is an exile, φεύγω, for he killed “the dear son of Idomeneus, / swift-footed Orsilochus”, the fastest runner in Crete (13.259–261; see [[idomeneus]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]).

**The quarrel.** Orsilochus wished to rob him of all his spoil from Troy, τῆς ληΐδος … / Τρωϊάδος, the article perhaps with a possessive sense, “my spoil” (13.262–263; Monro on 13.262). It was the spoil “for which I suffered sorrows in my heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.263–264), the verse in which the poet has just described the sleeping Odysseus (13.91; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). The cause was a service refused: “because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (13.265–266). θεράπευον is “served as θεράπων”, and the negative applies also to χαριζόμενος, “I did not court his favour by serving” (Monro on 13.265; see the translation's note on 13.265).

**The ambush.** “Him I struck with my bronze-fitted spear as he came down / from the country, lying in ambush near the road with one companion” (13.267–268; see [[ambush]] and [[bronze-fitted]]). ἀγρόθεν goes with κατιόντα, “coming down from the country”, as at 15.428 (Monro on 13.268). The night was very dark, no one saw them, and he took the young man's life unseen (13.269–270; see [[dnopheros]]).

**The Phoenicians.** At once he went to a ship of noble Phoenicians, begged them, and gave them spoil to satisfy their hearts; for ληΐδα, “spoil”, Aristophanes read ἤϊα, “provisions” (13.272–273; Monro's critical note on 13.273; see [[phoenicians]]). He bade them “take me on board and set me down at Pylos, / or at shining Elis, where the Epeians rule” (13.274–275; see [[pylos]], [[elis]] and [[epeians]]). Πύλονδε καταστῆσαι is a pregnant construction, “to bring to Pylos and set down there”, and ἐφέσσαι “to put me on board”: the main action is put first, a common prothysteron (Monro on 13.274). The force of the wind drove them off course, “much against their will; they did not wish to deceive me” (13.276–277), and they came to the harbor by night (13.278; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). προερέσσαμεν, “we rowed forward”, is Aristarchus' reading, which Didymus reports from all the editions Aristarchus used; other manuscripts have προερύσσαμεν (13.279; Monro's critical note). Hungry as they were, they had no thought of supper, but went ashore αὔτως, “as we were”, without attempting more, and lay down (13.280–281; Monro on 13.281).

**Left on the sand.** “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (13.282) is the verse of the sleep that fell on Odysseus when his native land was in sight and the companions opened the skin of the winds (10.31; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]] and [[sweet-sleep]]); the vulgate reads ἐπέλλαβε, “seized”, for ἐπήλυθε, “came upon” (Monro's critical note on 13.282). The sailors took his goods out of the hollow ship and set them where he lay on the sands, and went aboard for well-peopled Sidon (13.283–286; see [[sidon]]). The scene is the one the Phaeacians have just left on this shore, the sleeper on the sand and the goods set down beside him (13.116–124). So the Cretan was left behind, “grieving at heart” (13.286).

**The answer.** “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled” (13.287; see [[smiling-at-him]]): for her answer, and the truce between the two tricksters, see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]].
