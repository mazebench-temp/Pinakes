---
heading: After the tale
summary: The book opens as the tale ends. The Phaeacians sit spellbound in the two verses that met its pause (13.1–2 = 11.333–334), and Alcinous answers that the stranger, who has suffered so much, will not be beaten back from his house, and asks the leaders for more gifts (13.3–15). From here on the poet tells the story himself.
# Unnamed: the end of the tale (13.1-2); Alcinous' answer (13.3-6).

**The spell.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2; see [[silence]] and [[thelgo]]). The two verses met the pause in the middle of the tale (11.333–334), where Merry explains κηληθμός as the glamour of the story (see the Book 11 note); here they close the whole of it, which has run from the feast of the evening through the night. The first verse is also the silence that follows a hard proposal in the Iliad's night council and Hector's offer to the Trojans (*Iliad* 10.218, 10.313), and the Phaeacians' silence after Odysseus' challenge at the games (8.234).

**Alcinous' answer.** “Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back / and sail for home again, even though you have suffered very much” (13.4–6; see [[alcinous]] and [[bronze-floored]]). The words come from Achilles' speech in the first assembly of the Iliad, νῦν ἄμμε παλιμπλαγχθέντας ὀΐω / ἂψ ἀπονοστήσειν, “now I think we shall be beaten back / and sail for home again” (*Iliad* 1.59–60), “but with a change of application which has made them somewhat obscure”: in the Iliad to go home is to return disappointed to Greece, here it is the stranger's desire. Monro, after Naegelsbach, takes the sense to be simply that Odysseus will not go back from the house of Alcinous driven from his course, but will have his desire (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.5–6; see the translation's note on 13.5–6). τῶ is the adverb “so”, distinct from the dative τῷ (Monro on 13.5). The king's last clause, εἰ καὶ μάλα πολλὰ πέπονθας, “even though you have suffered very much”, is his judgment of all that he has heard, and he answers it at once with the gifts that he promised during the pause, a tripod and a cauldron from each of the leaders (13.7–15; 11.348–353; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**The poet's voice again.** The tale filled four books, and the poem now goes on in the poet's own voice, with the leaders going to bed (13.16–17) and the farewell of the next day. When the poet describes his hero asleep on the Phaeacian ship, he uses a verse that Odysseus had spoken of himself in Scheria, “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.91 = 8.183; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). Before the book ends, Odysseus is telling another tale, this time a false one (13.256–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).
