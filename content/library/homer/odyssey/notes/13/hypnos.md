summary: Odysseus comes home asleep. The Phaeacians spread his bed on the deck “so that he might sleep unwaking” (13.74), and sleep falls on him “unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (13.80); they set him on the sand “overcome by sleep” (13.119), and he wakes in his own land without knowing it (13.187–188). In his false tale he gives the same sleep to the Cretan exile, in the verse of his sleep within sight of Ithaca in Book 10 (13.282).

**The sleep of the voyage.** After the farewell feast the Phaeacian kings “went to lie down, each to his own house” (κακκείοντες, 13.17 = 7.229), and the next evening the crew spread a rug and a linen sheet for Odysseus “on the deck of the hollow ship, so that he might sleep unwaking, / at the stern” (ἵνα νήγρετον εὕδοι, 13.74–75; see [[blankets]] and [[deck]]). As soon as they row, “then sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids, / unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (13.79–80; see [[sweet-sleep]], [[thanatos]] and [[sleep]]). νήγρετος, “from which one does not wake”, is used in Homer only of this sleep (see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, ἴκρια; νήγρετος), and Monro, finding ἥδιστος weak after νήδυμος, proposed ἥκιστος, “most gentle” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.80; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). The narrator sets the sleep against all that the man has suffered: “but now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered” (ἀτρέμας εὗδε, λελασμένος, 13.92; see [[algos]]).

**Set ashore asleep.** At the harbor of Phorcys the crew lift him out “linen sheet and glossy rug and all, / and laid him down on the sand, overcome by sleep” (δεδμημένον ὕπνῳ, 13.118–119; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]), and set his gifts by the olive, away from the road, lest someone harm them “before Odysseus woke” (13.122–124). Poseidon's complaint names the sleep with the gifts: “But they carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca” (εὕδοντʼ, 13.134–135; see [[poseidon]]), and Odysseus will tell his son his coming in the same words (16.229). He wakes “where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it” (εὕδων ἐν γαίῃ πατρωΐῃ, 13.187–188; see [[eidos]]).

**Sleep in the lie.** In the tale he tells the disguised Athena, the Phoenician ship lands at night and the Cretan sleeps: “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was, / and they took my goods out of the hollow ship / and set them down where I myself lay on the sands” (13.282–284; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). The first of these verses is the one in which he told the Phaeacians how he fell asleep within sight of Ithaca, when the companions opened the bag of the winds (10.31; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]] and [[skin-of-the-winds]]). That sleep lost him his homecoming; this one, told as a stranger's, is the sleep in which he came home.
