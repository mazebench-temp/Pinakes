**An ox for Zeus.** On the day of his departure the Phaeacians feast in the house of Alcinous: “And for them the sacred might of Alcinous sacrificed an ox / to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all, / and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast” (13.24–26; see [[zeus]], [[sacrifice]], [[feast]] and [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]). The first verse plays on ἱέρευσʼ, “sacrificed”, and ἱερόν, “sacred”, and D. B. Monro sets it beside other plays of language in these books, among them, in Zeus' answer to Poseidon a little later, τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of “vengeance” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.24 and 13.144). The ox for Zeus begins the Phaeacians' last day with Odysseus. The next day, when Poseidon has turned their ship to stone, they make twelve bulls ready for him (13.180–187; see the Book 13 note on [[bulls]]).

**The plowman's oxen.** In the simile of the plowman who longs for his supper, “two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field” all day long (13.31–32; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]], [[wine-dark]] and [[jointed]]). βόε and its verb ἕλκητον are duals: the team is a yoke of two. οἶνοψ, the color of the sea, is given to oxen only here and in the Iliad's simile of the two Ajaxes, which has the same second half-verse (*Iliad* 13.703; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]). The working ox tells the hours of the farm's day: the Odyssey's afternoon is βουλυτόνδε, “toward the time for unyoking oxen” (9.58; see the Book 9 note), and the plowman's supper comes at sunset.

**Cattle-pasturing land.** Athena's account of Ithaca: “it is good goat-pasturing and cattle-pasturing land; there is forest / of every kind, and in it are unfailing watering places” (13.246–247; see [[ithaca]] and the Book 13 note on [[goats]]). βούβοτος, “cattle-pasturing”, from βοῦς and βόσκω, “feed”, is found only here in Homer, formed like αἰγίβοτος, “goat-pasturing”, beside it, and like ἱππόβοτος, the “horse-pasturing” land of Argos (see [[horse-pasturing]] and the translation's row λυπρός; βούβοτος; ἀρδμοί). Telemachus had told Menelaus that Ithaca has no meadow and is a land for goats (4.605–608), and W. W. Merry finds Athena's description in harmony with his: “There were standing pools for the cattle, that never were dry, and good pasturage for goats and kine” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 555). The great herds of Odysseus, however, are kept across the water: twelve herds of cattle on the mainland, Eumaeus tells the beggar (14.100), and the herdsman who brings a heifer to the suitors has it brought over by ferrymen (20.185–188).
