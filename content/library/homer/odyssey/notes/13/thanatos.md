# θανάτῳ (13.80; again 5.312, 11.412, 15.359, 22.462, 24.34), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [θανάτῳ]
---

**The lot of men.** Odysseus' farewell to the queen of the Phaeacians is a mortal's wish: “Farewell to you, queen, continually, until old age / and death come, which are the lot of men” (γῆρας / ἔλθῃ καὶ θάνατος, τά τʼ ἐπʼ ἀνθρώποισι πέλονται, 13.59–60; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[geron]] and [[basileus]]). The Phaeacians are “near kin to the gods” (5.35), but old age and death are their lot too.

**Most like death.** As the Phaeacians row, “sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids, / unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (θανάτῳ ἄγχιστα ἐοικώς, 13.79–80; see [[hypnos]], [[sweet-sleep]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). The comparison is made only here in Homer; in the Iliad Sleep is the brother of Death, and the twins carry Sarpedon's body home to Lycia (*Iliad* 14.231, 16.671–683; see [[sleep]] and [[death]]). He wakes from it in the land of his fathers, without knowing it (13.187–188).
