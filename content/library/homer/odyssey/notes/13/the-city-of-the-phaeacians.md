---
summary: The city is what Poseidon threatens. He would shatter the returning ship and “cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.149–152), and Zeus, approving, advises him to turn the ship to stone when “the people from the city” catch sight of it (13.154–158). Alcinous bids the Phaeacians escort no more strangers who come “to our city” and sacrifice to Poseidon, so that he may not cover it with a towering mountain (13.179–183); the book does not say whether he did.
# Unnamed: the city of the threat, of the onlookers and of the prayer (13.152, 13.156, 13.158, 13.177, 13.181,
# 13.183).
refs: [13.152, 13.156, 13.158, 13.177, 13.181, 13.183]
---

**Poseidon's design.** Poseidon tells Zeus what he means to do: “But now I wish the beautiful ship of the Phaeacians, / as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea, / to shatter, so that at last they may hold back and cease from escorting / men, and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.149–152; see [[poseidon]] and [[pompe]]). ἀμφικαλύψαι stands rather far from the verb that governs it, ἐθέλω, “I wish”, and the scholia seem to show an ancient variant, probably the future indicative ἀμφικαλύψω, “and I will cover” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.152); some manuscripts read πόλιν for πόλει (Monro's critical note on 13.152). The mountain is the one of Nausithous' prophecy, which W. W. Merry took not for a mountain hurled on the city to crush it but for a mountain-wall to pen the Phaeacians in, cutting off their access to the water and ending their seafaring life (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.569; see the Book 8 note).

**Zeus' answer.** “My friend, this is what seems best to my heart: / when all catch sight of her as she is driven on, / the people from the city, to turn her to stone near the land, / like a swift ship, so that all men may marvel, / and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.154–158; see [[zeus]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). The infinitives are an epexegesis of δοκεῖ εἶναι ἄριστα, “as seems best to me, viz. to turn their ship into stone, &c. (so I say)”, and the sentence has no grammatical apodosis (Monro on 13.154–158). For μέγα δέ, “and … a great”, in the last verse, Aristophanes read μηδέ, “and not”, so that Zeus would forbid the mountain, “no doubt in view of the prayers of the Phaeacian elders (l. 183). But these need not affect what Zeus says now” (Monro on 13.158; see the translation's note on 13.158). The ship is turned to stone in sight of the city, as Zeus advised (13.161–164).

**The city's prayer.** Alcinous recalls that his father used to say that the god would shatter a ship “and would cover our city round about with a great mountain” (13.177; see [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). He bids his people stop escorting mortals, “whenever anyone comes / to our city”, and sacrifice twelve picked bulls to Poseidon “in the hope that he may have pity / and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain” (13.180–183; see [[alcinous]] and [[hecatomb]]). ἄστυ, “city”, here stands beside πόλις, “city”, of the same place (13.181, 13.183). The book leaves the leaders of the people standing around the altar (13.185–187; see [[phaeacians]]), and the city is not seen again: whether the mountain closed round it, the poem does not say.
