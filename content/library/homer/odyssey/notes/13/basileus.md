---
summary: Odysseus takes leave of Arete as “queen” (13.59) and leaves her with her children, her people “and King Alcinous” (13.62); waking in Ithaca, he wishes he had come instead to “some other of the mighty kings” (13.205).
---

The farewell is made to the queen. Odysseus puts the cup in Arete's hand and speaks to her: “Farewell to you, queen, continually, until old age / and death come, which are the lot of men. / But I am going home; and you, take your delight in this house / with your children and your people and King Alcinous” (χαῖρέ μοι, ὦ βασίλεια, 13.59–62; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[alcinous]] and [[geron]]). It was at her knees that he had first made his supplication in the palace, as Nausicaa and Athena had told him to do (6.310–315, 7.53–77, 7.142–152; see [[supplication]]). παισί in 13.62 is instrumental, with a partly comitative sense (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.62).

On Ithaca, believing himself left in a strange land, he wishes that the Phaeacians had kept their goods, “and I would have come to some other of the mighty kings, / who would have welcomed me and sent me on my way home” (ὑπερμενέων βασιλήων, 13.205–206; see [[xenia]] and [[pompe]]). The kings of the poem are its hosts, and a wanderer's hope is to reach one.
