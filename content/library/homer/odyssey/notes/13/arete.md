---
summary: Odysseus' farewell prayer for the Phaeacians: “may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (13.45–46), where ἀρετή is good fortune.
---

Standing to take his leave, Odysseus prays for his hosts: “And may you, staying here, gladden your wives, / your wedded wives, and your children; and may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (θεοὶ δʼ ἀρετὴν ὀπάσειαν / παντοίην, 13.44–46; see [[phaeacians]] and [[prayer]]). ἀρετή is “good” here, not restricted, as in later Greek, to merit or excellence: Monro compares the swineherd's ἐϋκλείη τʼ ἀρετή τε, “good fame and prosperity” (14.402), and the verb ἀρετάω, “prosper”, of a people under a good king (19.114) (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.45; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.45). The prayer is followed by its opposite. When the ship that carried him comes home, Poseidon turns it to stone in sight of the city, and the Phaeacians stand around his altar praying that he may not cover their city with a mountain (13.159–187; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).
