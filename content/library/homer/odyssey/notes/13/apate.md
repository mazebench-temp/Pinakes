summary: In his false tale Odysseus vouches for the Phoenicians who left him on the shore, “they did not wish to deceive me” (13.277), and Athena tells him that he was not to leave off his “deceits” even in his own land (13.294).
# ἐξαπατῆσαι (13.277) and ἀπατάων (13.294), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [ἐξαπατῆσαι, ἀπατάων]
en: [deceits, deceive me]
---

**Honest Phoenicians.** The liar's invented sailors are honest men. The Cretan exile begged a passage of Phoenicians and paid them to set him down at Pylos or Elis, “But the force of the wind drove them away from there, / much against their will; they did not wish to deceive me” (οὐδʼ ἤθελον ἐξαπατῆσαι, 13.276–277; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]), and they left him and his goods on the shore and sailed for Sidon. In the false tale he tells Eumaeus the Phoenician is the deceiver, “a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits” (14.288, a plain rendering).

**Leave off your deceits.** Athena answers the tale: “Hard man, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks, you were not, then, / even in your own land, to leave off your deceits / and thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up” (λήξειν ἀπατάων, 13.293–295; see [[dolos]], [[mythos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The words are Zeus' to Hera when he wakes from the sleep she contrived on Ida, “Of these things I will remind you again, so that you may leave off your deceits” (ἵνʼ ἀπολλήξῃς ἀπατάων, *Iliad* 15.31; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]): there a god threatens the goddess who deceived him, here a goddess smiles at the man who has tried to deceive her.
