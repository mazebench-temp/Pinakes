---
summary: The book opens in the silence after the tale, “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2); Odysseus goes on board and lies down “in silence” (13.75–76); and on Ithaca Athena bids him tell no one who he is, “but in silence / suffer many sorrows, submitting to the violence of men” (13.307–310).
---

**The end of the tale.** The two verses that halted the tale in the middle of the night (11.333–334) now end it: “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]). In the Iliad the first is the silence that meets a hard word, a challenge that no one takes up or a refusal (*Iliad* 7.92, 9.430, 10.218; see the Book 8 note); here it is the silence of listeners who have been held by a story, κηληθμῷ, “by a spell” (see [[thelgo]]). Alcinous breaks it, and turns it into gifts (13.3–15; see [[alcinous]]).

**On board.** At sunset Odysseus is taken down to the ship, the Phaeacians spread a rug and a linen sheet on the deck at the stern, “and he himself went on board and lay down / in silence” (13.75–76; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). The man who talked far into the night goes home without a word, and sleep, “unwaking, sweetest, most like death”, falls on him as the oars strike the water (13.78–80; see [[hypnos]]).

**The silence of the return.** On Ithaca Athena lays down the rule for what is to come. He must bear up under everything, “and not speak out to anyone, man or woman, / of them all, that you have come back from your wanderings, but in silence / suffer many sorrows, submitting to the violence of men” (13.308–310; see [[athena]] and [[the-suitors]]). οὕνεκα is “that”, a meaning confined in Homer to the Odyssey (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.309). The command is Agamemnon's counsel made the plan of the poem, to bring the ship home secretly and tell one part and hide the rest (11.441–443, 11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]), and Athena sets the same silence over his coming in the mist she pours around him, “lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends” (13.192; see [[shape-changing]]).
