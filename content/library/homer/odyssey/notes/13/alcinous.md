summary: When the tale ends, Alcinous promises Odysseus that he will not be beaten back from his house, and bids each of the elders add a great tripod and a cauldron to the gifts, to be repaid by a levy on the people; at dawn he stows the bronze in the ship himself, sacrifices an ox to Zeus, and at sunset has Pontonous mix the wine for the parting libation. When the ship is turned to stone in sight of the city, he recognizes his father's prophecy and bids the Phaeacians give up their escorts and sacrifice twelve bulls to Poseidon (13.3–64, 13.171–187).

**The answer.** The tale is done, and the hall is silent (13.1–2; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[silence]]). “Then Alcinous answered him in turn and said” (13.3), the verse of his answers in the pause of the tale and on the night the stranger came (11.347, 11.362; 7.298, 7.308): “Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back / and sail for home again, even though you have suffered very much” (13.4–6; see [[odysseus]], [[bronze-floored]], [[high-roofed]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). The words are Achilles' in the first assembly of the Iliad (*Iliad* 1.59–60), “but with a change of application which has made them somewhat obscure”: there ἂψ ἀπονοστήσειν is a return to Greece in disappointment; here the meaning is simply that Ulysses will not return driven back from the house of Alcinous, but will have his desire (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.5–6, after Naegelsbach; see the translation's note on 13.5–6). τῶ is the adverb “so”, not the dative (Monro on 13.5).

**The last gifts.** He turns to the elders who “in my halls drink the sparkling wine of the elders / always, and listen to the singer” (13.8–9; see [[geron]] and [[wine]]), ἀκουάζεσθε being “please yourselves with listening”, a verb of the ampliative and often unfavorable kind (Monro on 13.9; see the translation's note on 13.9). The clothes, the gold and the other gifts of the counselors already lie in the polished chest (13.10–12; see [[chest]], [[possessions]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]):

{{quote:cf3b1272-57b6-5ef4-b818-9da65107f71b}}

τισόμεθα is “will repay ourselves”, and προικός “as a free gift”, without such repayment, a genitive used as an adverb, like the Attic προῖκα (Monro on 13.15; see [[hard-for-one-man-to-give]], [[tripod]], [[cauldron]], [[demos]] and the translation's key words); in his additional notes he adds that the adverbial genitive is found in Attic inscriptions (Monro, Additional Notes and Corrections, p. 286). The tripods and cauldrons are added to the gifts of Book 8, when the twelve kings and Alcinous, “the thirteenth”, each gave a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold (8.389–393), and in the pause of the tale the king had promised to complete the whole present the next day (11.348–352; see [[guest-gifts]]). “So spoke Alcinous, and his word pleased them” (13.16).

**The stowing and the sacrifice.** At dawn the Phaeacians bring “the bronze that makes men strong”, and “the sacred might of Alcinous stowed it well, / under the thwarts, going himself through the ship, so that it might not / hinder any of the companions as they rowed” (13.19–22; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]] and [[rowing-benches]]); ὑπὸ ζυγά goes with κατέθηκε (Monro on 13.21). Then he sacrifices an ox “to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all” (13.24–25; see [[sacrifice]] and [[zeus]]), with a play of language in ἱέρευσʼ ἱερὸν μένος (Monro on 13.24). The second verse is the one with which Odysseus told of the ram he sacrificed to Zeus on the Cyclops' shore, an offering Zeus did not heed (9.552–555).

**The parting.** At sunset Odysseus addresses him above all, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (13.37–38; see [[powerful]] and [[preeminent]]), asks for the escort and prays for the Phaeacians, and the king gives the order: “And then the might of Alcinous spoke to the herald: / ‘Pontonous, mix a bowl and serve out wine / to all throughout the hall, so that when we have prayed to father Zeus / we may send the stranger on his way to his native land’” (13.49–52; see [[pontonous]] and [[libation]]), the verses of the first libation of the stranger's stay (7.178–179). Odysseus bids the queen farewell and bids her take delight in the house “with your children and your people and King Alcinous” (13.62; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), and the might of Alcinous sends a herald to lead him to the ship (13.64; see [[heralds]]).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** When Poseidon has turned the ship to stone as it comes home, in sight of all (13.159–170; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]] and [[poseidon]]), “Then Alcinous addressed them and spoke” (13.171): “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me, / the oracles of my father”, who used to say that Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship coming home from an escort and cover their city with a great mountain, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (13.172–178; see [[nausithous]] and [[thesphaton]]). He had told the prophecy to his guest at the end of the day of the games, and ended it then with “and these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.564–571). Now he gives his people two commands: “stop escorting mortals, whenever anyone comes / to our city; and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity / and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain” (13.180–183; see [[pompe]], [[bulls]] and [[hecatomb]]). “So he spoke, and they were afraid, and made the bulls ready. / So they prayed to lord Poseidon, / the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians, / standing around the altar” (13.184–187; see [[prayer]], [[altar]] and [[phaeacians]]). The poem leaves them there, with half a verse, and turns to Odysseus waking on Ithaca. Whether the mountain fell it does not say. Zeus advised Poseidon to turn the ship to stone “and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.158), where Aristophanes read μηδέ, “and not”, no doubt because of the Phaeacians' prayers; “But these need not affect what Zeus says now” (Monro on 13.158; see the translation's note on 13.158 and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**His titles in this book.** ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the sacred might of Alcinous” (13.20, 13.24), and μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the might of Alcinous” (13.49, 13.64; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]); Ἀλκίνοε κρεῖον, πάντων ἀριδείκετε λαῶν, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (13.38); and Ἀλκινόῳ βασιλῆϊ, “King Alcinous”, in Odysseus' farewell to the queen (13.62; see [[basileus]]).
