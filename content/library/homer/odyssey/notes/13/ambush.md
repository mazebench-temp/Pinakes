summary: In his false tale the Cretan kills the son of Idomeneus from ambush, by night, “lying in ambush near the road with one companion” (13.267–270); and Athena tells Odysseus that young men in a black ship lie in ambush for Telemachus, but that some of the suitors will die first (13.425–428).
# λοχόωσι (13.425), only here; 15.28 has λοχόωσιν.
grc: [λοχόωσι]

**The Cretan's ambush.** The killing that makes the Cretan of the false tale an exile is done from ambush: “Him I struck with my bronze-fitted spear as he came down / from the country, lying in ambush near the road with one companion. / A very dark night covered the sky, and no one / of men noticed us, and I took his life unseen” (13.267–270; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[idomeneus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The tale has all the parts of an ambush: a place chosen beside the road, a companion, the night, and a man coming home from the country who does not know that he is awaited (ἀγρόθεν, “from the country”, with κατιόντα, D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.268). In the Iliad it was Idomeneus who called the ambush the place “where most of all the prowess of men is seen clearly” (*Iliad* 13.276–277); in the lie his son dies in one where no one sees anything at all.

**The ambush for Telemachus.** The plot of Book 4 is still waiting at sea. Antinous asked for a swift ship and twenty companions, “so that I may lie in ambush for him on his way and watch for him / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos” (4.669–671), and the suitors lay in wait at Asteris (4.842–847; see [[asteris]], [[the-suitors]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). On Ithaca Athena tells Odysseus of it and dismisses it in the same breath: “Truly young men in a black ship lie in ambush for him, / eager to kill him before he reaches his native land; / but I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (13.425–428). The second verse is Penelope's fear in Book 4 (4.823), and in Sparta Athena gives Telemachus himself the same warning and the same assurance, “The best of the suitors lie in ambush for you of set purpose” (15.28–32, a plain rendering; see [[telemachus]]).
