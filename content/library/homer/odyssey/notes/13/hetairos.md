---
summary: The Phaeacian oarsmen who carry Odysseus home are “the companions” (13.21); the Cretan of his false tale “led other companions of my own” and killed Orsilochus “with one companion” (13.266, 13.268); and Athena knew that he would come home “having lost all your companions” (13.340).
---

**The Phaeacian crew.** The young Phaeacians who row him to Ithaca are ἑταῖροι too: Alcinous himself goes through the ship and stows the gifts under the thwarts, “so that it might not / hinder any of the companions as they rowed, whenever they were in haste with their oars” (13.21–22; see [[alcinous]] and [[rowing-benches]]). ὑπὸ ζυγά, “under the thwarts”, goes with κατέθηκε (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.21).

**The Cretan's companions.** In the false tale the Cretan would not serve Idomeneus at Troy, “but led other companions of my own” (13.266; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[charis]]), and killed his son on the road at night, “lying in ambush near the road with one companion” (13.268; see [[ambush]]).

**All lost.** Athena explains her absence: “But I never doubted it, but in my heart / I knew that you would come home, having lost all your companions” (ὀλέσας ἄπο πάντας ἑταίρους, 13.339–340; see [[nostos]]). The half-verse is the Cyclops' curse, “let him come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship” (9.534–535; see [[curses]]), which Teiresias and Circe repeated as a prophecy (11.114, 12.141; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). Athena speaks of it as done, and the curse is fulfilled to the letter: he has come home alone, on someone else's ship.
