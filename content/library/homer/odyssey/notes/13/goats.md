**A land for goats.** “It is good goat-pasturing and cattle-pasturing land; there is forest / of every kind, and in it are unfailing watering places” (13.246–247; see [[ithaca]] and the Book 13 note on [[cattle]]). αἰγίβοτος, “goat-pasturing”, from αἴξ, “goat”, and βόσκω, “feed”, is the word in which Telemachus had refused Menelaus' gift of horses: Ithaca has no wide courses for running and no meadow, “it is a goat-pasturing land, and lovelier than a horse-pasturing one” (4.605–606; see the Book 4 note and [[horse-pasturing]]). Athena, disguised as a young herdsman of sheep, praises the island in the same word to a stranger who does not know where he has landed (13.221–223, 13.237–249; see the Book 13 note on [[sheep]]). W. W. Merry sets the two accounts side by side and finds them in harmony: a rugged land, unfit for driving horses, with standing pools that never ran dry and “good pasturage for goats and kine” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, pp. 555–556).

**Odysseus' goats.** The goats graze on the island itself. Eumaeus tells the beggar of his master's wealth, twelve herds of cattle and as many flocks of sheep and droves of pigs and herds of goats on the mainland, “And here, in all, eleven wide-ranging herds of goats / graze at the far edge, and good men keep watch over them” (14.100–104), and each day the herdsmen bring the suitors the best of the well-fed goats (14.105–106).
