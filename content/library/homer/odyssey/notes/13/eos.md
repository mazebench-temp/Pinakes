summary: Dawn brings two mornings. “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered”, the Phaeacians bring the bronze to the ship (13.18), on the last day Odysseus spends among them; and at the end of the night's voyage the star rises that “comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn”, as the ship draws near to Ithaca (13.93–95). On Ithaca Athena says that the island is known to men “toward the dawn and the sun” and toward the darkness (13.240–241).
# The entry claims Ἠώς (13.18) and Ἠοῦς (13.94); the Book 9 note claims "ἠῶ τʼ ἠέλιόν", the east (13.240).

  - 13.93-95 | The morning star

**The last day in Scheria.** After the night of the tale, the Phaeacians go home to sleep, “and when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / they hurried to the ship, and brought the bronze that makes men strong” (13.18–19; see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]] and [[rosy-fingered]]). The first verse is the Odyssey's verse of morning (2.1; 12.8, 12.316; see the Book 12 note). D. B. Monro reckons it the morning of the thirty-fourth day of the action of the poem (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.18). The day is spent in sacrifice and feasting, and Odysseus spends it watching the sun, eager for it to set (13.23–35; see [[helios]]).

**The star that announces Dawn.** The ship runs all night with Odysseus asleep: “When that brightest star rose up which most of all / comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn, / then the seafaring ship drew near to the island” (13.93–95; see [[the-morning-star]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). ὑπερέσχε is intransitive, “rises”, and this is the dawn of the thirty-fifth day (Monro on 13.93; see the translation's note on 13.93). The Iliad names the star: the funeral pyre of Patroclus dies down “When the morning star goes forth to herald the light over the earth, / the star after which saffron-robed Dawn spreads over the sea” (*Iliad* 23.226–227; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]); and Hesiod makes it Dawn's own child, for after the winds “the Early-born bore the star Eosphorus” and the shining stars with which the sky is crowned (*Theogony* 381–382; see [[hesiod]]). The poem marks the landing by the star and not by Dawn herself: the Phaeacians row into the harbor at its rising, set Odysseus ashore and are gone before he wakes (13.113–125; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]).

**Toward the dawn.** When Odysseus asks what land this is, the young herdsman answers that it is not so nameless: “very many know it, / both those who live toward the dawn and the sun / and those behind, toward the misty darkness” (13.239–241; see [[athena]] and [[ithaca]]). The phrase “toward the dawn and the sun” is the east, as in Odysseus' description of Ithaca to the Phaeacians, which lies “toward the darkness, while the others lie apart toward the dawn and the sun” (9.25–26; see the Book 9 note), and in Hector's scorn of the birds, whether they go to the right, “toward the dawn and the sun” (*Iliad* 12.239–240). μετόπισθε is “westwards: the west being the end, as the east is the beginning, of the day” (Monro on 13.241; see the translation's note on 13.241). Strabo cites such verses, with the two doors of the cave of the Naiads, as proof that Homer knew the quarters of the sky and the winds (*Geography* 1.2.20; see [[strabo]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[boreas]] and [[notus]]).
