---
summary: In the prologue of Euripides' Philoctetes, as Monro reports, Odysseus told how Athena had promised to change him so that Philoctetes would not know him, the device by which in this book she makes him an old beggar whom no one in Ithaca will recognize.
---

- **The Philoctetes.** Defending the change of Odysseus into an old beggar against Kirchhoff, who held it a magical transformation inserted to join two poems with two different heroes, Monro argues that some means of concealment was a poetical necessity once Odysseus was in danger of being recognized in Ithaca, and adds: “Similarly in the *Philoctetes* of Euripides the opening speech of Ulysses related how Athene had promised to change him so that he might not be recognized by Philoctetes” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §6, note). The play is lost; Monro does not name his source, and the fragments of the play read here do not include the promise. In this book Athena promises, “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals”, and with a touch of her wand withers his skin, takes away his hair and dims his eyes (13.397–403, 13.429–438): [[shape-changing]], [[athena]] and [[philoctetes]].
