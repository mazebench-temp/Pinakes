---
summary: “Clothes for the stranger lie in the polished chest”, Alcinous reminds the lords (13.10–12), and when Odysseus leaves the house Arete sends a slave woman to carry “the close-fitted chest” to the ship with him (13.66–68).
---

**In the house.** “Clothes for the stranger lie in the polished chest, / and intricately worked gold and all the other / gifts that the counselors of the Phaeacians brought here” (13.10–12; see [[alcinous]], [[clothing]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]): the chest that Arete filled with the kings' mantles, tunics and gold and her own mantle and tunic, and whose lid Odysseus tied with the knot that Circe taught him (8.438–448; see the Book 8 note). The tripods and cauldrons that Alcinous now asks for go to the ship apart: the lords bring them at dawn, and he stows them himself under the thwarts (13.13–22; see [[tripod]]).

**To the ship.** When Odysseus steps over the threshold, “Arete sent slave women with him, / one carrying a freshly washed mantle and a tunic, / and another she granted him, to carry the close-fitted chest along; / and a third brought bread and red wine” (13.66–69; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[handmaids]], [[cloak]] and [[provisions]]). πυκινός, “close-fitted”, is the word of the doors that Hephaestus fitted close to the doorposts of Hera's chamber (*Iliad* 14.167; see the translation's row χηλὸς πυκινή). For ὄπασσε, “granted”, some manuscripts and Eustathius have ἔπεμπε, “sent” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.68). The chest is not named again: the escorts stow what the women bring in the hollow ship (13.71–72), and on Ithaca the sailors lift out the possessions and set them by the olive (13.120–122; see [[possessions]] and [[olive]]).
