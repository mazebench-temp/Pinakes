summary: The book leaves one house and turns to another. In the bronze-floored, high-roofed house of Alcinous the Phaeacians sit spellbound by the tale, and Odysseus bids the queen take her delight in it (13.1–5, 13.61); in his own halls on Ithaca, Athena tells him, the suitors have been lording it for three years while his wife weeps, and he would have perished there like Agamemnon (13.306, 13.334–338, 13.377, 13.383–384). Telemachus sits at ease in the house of Menelaus (13.424).
# The phrases "δόμοις ἔνι ποιητοῖσι" (13.306; δόμοις ἔνι again 19.584, Odysseus' house) and "Ἀτρεΐδαο δόμοις" (13.424),
# first met here; they apply in the Odyssey only. Bare δόμοις is also the house of Hades (εἰν Ἀΐδαο δόμοις, 24.204).

**The house of Alcinous.** When the tale ends, the Phaeacians are “held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.2; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), and Alcinous begins: “Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back / and sail for home again” (13.4–6; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[bronze-floored]] and [[high-roofed]]). The house is the ground of his assurance: Monro takes the words to mean simply that Odysseus will not be driven back from the house of Alcinous without what he came for, but will have his desire (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.5–6; see the translation's note on 13.5–6). The lords go to lie down, “each to his own house” (13.17), and after the feast and the libation Odysseus bids the queen “take your delight in this house / with your children and your people and King Alcinous” (13.61–62; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), where some manuscripts read χώρῳ, “land”, for οἴκῳ (Monro's critical note on 13.61). Then he steps over the threshold (13.63; see [[threshold]]).

**The house of Odysseus.** On Ithaca, Athena has come to tell him “all the sorrows it is your lot in your well-built house / to endure” (13.306–307; see [[aisa]]). ποιητός, “well-built”, describes the workmanship, as πηκτόν does the plow (13.32): the point is that the thing is a work of mechanical skill (Monro on 13.32). Another man would have hurried to see his children and his wife “in his halls”, while Penelope “sits, just as she was, / in your halls; and always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (13.333–338; see [[penelope]]); the suitors “for three years now have been lording it in your hall” (13.377; see [[the-suitors]] and [[koiranos]]), and Odysseus answers that, like Agamemnon, he “was about to perish by an evil fate” in his halls (13.383–385; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Athena will make him unsightly to his wife and to the son “whom you left in your halls” (13.402–403).

**The house of Menelaus.** Telemachus has gone to Sparta, but “he has no toil, but sits at his ease / in the house of the son of Atreus, and untold plenty lies beside him” (13.423–424; see [[telemachus]], [[menelaus]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
