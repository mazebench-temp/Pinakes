---
summary: The last escort of the Phaeacians. Odysseus thanks them for “an escort and gifts of friendship” (13.41), and “the noble escorts” carry him asleep to Ithaca (13.71); Poseidon would shatter their ship “as it comes back from an escort” (13.150), and Alcinous, recognizing his father's prophecy, bids his people “stop escorting mortals” (13.180). On Ithaca Athena says that she herself escorted Telemachus (13.422).
---

**The escort given.** The escort promised in Books 7 and 8 is made in this one. Odysseus asks Alcinous, “send me on my way unharmed, when you have poured libations”, and names what he has received, “an escort and gifts of friendship” (πομπὴ καὶ φίλα δῶρα, 13.39–41; see [[doron]] and [[libation]]), the pair that Alcinous had named as the host's due (8.545); the Phaeacians bid “send the stranger on his way, since he had spoken fair and right” (13.48 = 7.227; see [[moira]]). Then “the noble escorts” (πομπῆες ἀγαυοί, 13.71) stow the food and drink, spread his bed on the deck, and row him through the night to the harbor of Phorcys (13.70–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]), as Alcinous had promised: “and then you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm” (7.318–319). πομπῆες, “escorts”, stands beside πομποί (8.566 = 13.174; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, πομπῆες).

**The escort punished.** The god who begrudged the Phaeacians their escorts punishes this one. Poseidon asks to shatter “the beautiful ship of the Phaeacians, / as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea, / … so that at last they may hold back and cease from escorting / men” (ἐκ πομπῆς ἀνιοῦσαν … ἀπολλήξωσι δὲ πομπῆς, 13.149–152; see [[poseidon]]). When the ship has been turned to stone, Alcinous recognizes the prophecy of his father, that Poseidon begrudged them “because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (πομποὶ ἀπήμονες, 13.173–174, as 8.565–566; see [[nausithous]] and [[thesphaton]]), and decrees the end of the custom: “stop escorting mortals, whenever anyone comes / to our city” (πομπῆς μὲν παύσασθε βροτῶν, 13.180–181; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). Some manuscripts read the present παύεσθε (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.180).

**The goddess as escort.** On Ithaca Athena answers Odysseus' question about his son: “I myself escorted him, so that he might win noble fame / by going there” (αὐτή μιν πόμπευον, 13.422–423; see [[telemachus]] and [[kleos]]). The verb πομπεύω occurs only here in Homer. In the likeness of Mentor she had sailed with Telemachus to Pylos, sitting beside him in the stern (2.399–401, 2.416–418; see [[mentor]]), and the escort that the Phaeacians give up is taken over by a god, as in the Iliad, where escorts are gods' work (see the entry).
