---
summary: Four speeches open with the cry: Zeus' answer to Poseidon (13.140); Alcinous', “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me” (13.172); Odysseus' on waking, “Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just, / the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (13.209–210); and his answer to Athena, “Ah, truly, like Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / I was about to perish by an evil fate in my halls” (13.383–384).
---

Zeus' cry is told under [[shaker-of-the-earth]]. Alcinous' verse is the Cyclops' when he remembers the prophecy of his blinding (9.507): each prophecy is recognized as it comes true, and each is fulfilled through Odysseus (13.172–178; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[thesphaton]]). In Odysseus' cry on waking, οὐκ ἄρα … ἦσαν is “they are not, as I thought they were” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.209), with the words that Nestor used of the Achaeans whom Zeus punished on their way home, νοήμονες οὐδὲ δίκαιοι, “discerning or just” (3.133); he is wrong, for the land is Ithaca (see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). The last cry recognizes a fate escaped, and the example is the one he heard among the dead (11.405–434; see [[agamemnon]] and [[son-of-atreus]]).
