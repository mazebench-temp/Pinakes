---
summary: Scheria is named once in the book: given leave by Zeus, Poseidon “went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live”, waited for the returning ship, and turned it to stone near the land, in sight of the city (13.159–164). In later times a rock off Corcyra was pointed out as that ship.
---

**The god at Scheria.** Zeus advises Poseidon to wait until the people of the city see the ship coming home, and then “to turn her to stone near the land, / like a swift ship, so that all men may marvel” (13.155–157; see [[zeus]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). “But when Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this, / he went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live. / There he waited; and the seafaring ship came very near, / coursing swiftly along” (13.159–162; see [[poseidon]] and [[phaeacians]]). ὅθι Φαίηκες γεγάασιν, “where the Phaeacians live”, has the perfect of γίγνομαι, “be born”, the verb of the question Odysseus will ask on Ithaca, “what men are born here?” (ἐγγεγάασιν, 13.233). διωκομένη is “coursing along”, as in the phrase ἅρμα διώκειν, “drive a chariot” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.162). The god “turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath / with a blow of the flat of his hand; and then he was gone” (13.163–164), and the stone ship stays by the shore of Scheria, a wonder for all men, as Zeus had said. It is the island's last appearance in the poem's narrative. When Odysseus tells his tale to Penelope, he ends it with the Phaeacians, “who honored him with all their hearts like a god, / and sent him in a ship to his dear native land” (23.339–340, a plain rendering).

**The rock off Corcyra.** Those who took Scheria for Corcyra could point to the ship. Pliny, describing the islands off Corcyra, mentions off Phalacrum, a promontory of the island, “a rock into which, the story goes, the ship of Ulysses was changed, from its likeness” (*scopulus in quem mutatam Ulixis navem a simili specie fabula est*, *Natural History* 4.53). Monro writes that a small island near the entrance of the old harbor of Corfu is pointed out as the Phaeacian ship, “and perhaps is sufficiently ‘like a swift ship’ to have given rise to the story”, and prints a view of the old harbor of Corfu after his notes on the book; “It is not, however, the only claimant”: Andrew Lang, in *Longman's Magazine* for January 1898, quoting Samuel Butler's *The Authoress of the Odyssey*, reported a rock outside the harbor of Trapani in Sicily “said to have been a Turkish war vessel, turned into stone by the Madonna” (Monro on 13.154–158). For the identification of Scheria with Corcyra, see the general article.
