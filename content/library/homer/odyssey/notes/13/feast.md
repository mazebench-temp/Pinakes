summary: The last feast in the house of Alcinous fills the day of departure, with an ox for Zeus and Demodocus' song (13.23–28), while Odysseus watches the sun like a plowman who longs for his supper (13.29–35); in his false tale the Cretan's crew, landing by night, has no thought of supper (13.279–281).
# δόρποιο (13.31; again 14.407, 19.402), first met here in the Odyssey.
grc: [δόρποιο]
---

**The last feast.** When the gifts have been stowed in the ship, the Phaeacians “went to the house of Alcinous and made ready a feast” (13.23). The king sacrifices an ox “to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all” (13.24–25), the verse of the sacrifice of the Cyclops' ram, whose offering Zeus did not heed (9.551–553; see [[zeus]] and [[sacrifice]]); “and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast, / delighting in it; and among them a divine singer sang, / Demodocus, honored by the people” (13.26–28; see [[demodocus]]). The verse of the singer is that of the wedding feast in Menelaus' house and of the dance on the Shield of Achilles (4.17, *Iliad* 18.604). The song is not told, and Demodocus is not named again in the poem; “But Odysseus / kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun” (13.28–29; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**Longing for supper.** He was “in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (13.30), and the poem compares him to a plowman at the end of the day: “And as when a man longs for his supper, for whom all day long / two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field, / and gladly for him the light of the sun sets, / so that he may go about his supper, and his knees are hindered as he goes, / so welcome to Odysseus did the light of the sun set” (13.31–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]], [[the-plowing]] and [[oxen-at-the-plow]]). δόρπον ἐποίχεσθαι is “in that he can now go about his supper” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.34). The guest at the feast is likened to a laborer waiting for his meal, and what the sunset brings him is not supper but the voyage home. The plowman's half-verse, βλάβεται δέ τε γούνατʼ ἰόντι, ends Odysseus' own picture in the Iliad of the man who goes into battle without food: “yet his limbs grow heavy unawares, and thirst and hunger / overtake him, and his knees are hindered as he goes” (*Iliad* 19.165–166; see [[gounata]]).

**No supper.** In the tale he tells Athena, the Phoenicians' ship comes into the harbor by night, “and none of us / had any thought of supper, though we sorely needed to take it, / but just as we were we all went ashore from the ship and lay down” (13.279–281; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]); αὔτως is “as we were”, without attempting more (Monro on 13.281). The next verse, “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was”, is the one in which Odysseus told of falling asleep before the bag of the winds was opened (13.282 = 10.31; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]).
