**Beneath the olive.** “Then the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive / and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[olive]], [[the-suitors]] and [[overbearing]]). Athena addresses him with his full titles, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (13.375; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]): he is to consider how he will lay hands on the shameless suitors, who for three years now have been lording it in his hall, κοιρανέουσι, said ironically (13.376–377; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.377; see [[koiranos]]). For 13.376 several manuscripts read a verse modeled on 13.373, φράζεο νῦν μνηστῆρσιν ὑπερφιάλοισιν ὄλεθρον (Monro's critical note). The three years are the years of the suitors' wooing that Antinous complained of in the assembly, when Penelope's web had kept them waiting (2.89–110). Penelope, “always mourning in her heart for your homecoming”, ὀδυρομένη being “crying for it” (Monro on 13.379), “gives hope to all, and makes promises to each man, / sending out messages, but her mind has other things in view” (13.379–381), the verses of Antinous' complaint in the assembly, now in the goddess' mouth (2.91–92; see [[penelope]]); ἀγγελίας are “messages” (Monro on 13.381).

**“Like Agamemnon.”** “Ah, truly, like Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / I was about to perish by an evil fate in my halls, / had you not told me each thing, goddess, fair and right” (13.383–385; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Then he asks her to weave a plan, ὅπως ἀποτίσομαι αὐτούς, “how I may take vengeance on them”, where Monro takes the pronoun to be perhaps emphatic (13.386; Monro, Additional Notes, on 13.386; see [[metis]]); she had come “to weave a plan with you” (13.303). He asks her to stand beside him, “putting in me a might full of daring, / as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.387–388; see [[full-of-daring]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]), and with her he would fight three hundred men (13.389–391). Her answer promises blood and brains on the boundless floor (13.393–396); παλαξέμεν, “spatter”, is found in Homer only here.

**The change foretold.** “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals” (13.397), the ἄγνωστον of the mist, which Monro takes to refer to the same process (on 13.191; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). She will wither his skin, destroy his fair hair, and put about him a [[rags|rag]] ὅ κε στυγέῃσιν ἰδὼν ἄνθρωπον ἔχοντα, “which anyone who sees a man wearing it would hate” (13.398–400). The manuscripts have ἄνθρωπος, “which any man who should see thee wearing would loathe”, but Homer does not use ἄνθρωπος in that indefinite sense, as Nitzsch pointed out; Monro adopts ἄνθρωπον from a variant reported by Eustathius, takes ἰδών in the indefinite sense “any one who sees”, and notes that this is the only place in the Odyssey where the singular ἄνθρωπος occurs at all (on 13.400). She will dim his eyes, κνυζώσω, a verb found in Homer only here and in its fulfillment (13.401, 13.433), so that he may seem unsightly to the suitors and to his wife and his son (13.402–403; see [[eyes]]). Then she sends him to the swineherd, who is ὑῶν ἐπίουρος, “guardian over your swine”, and is “at one with Ulysses in the loyalty of his heart” (13.404–406; Monro on 13.405; see [[eumaeus]]), to find him sitting by the swine where they feed on acorns at the Rock of Corax and drink at the spring Arethusa (13.407–410; see [[the-rock-of-corax]] and [[arethusa]]); παρήμενον is “abiding with” (Monro on 13.407). For her errand to Sparta and the ambush of Telemachus, see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]].

**The change.** “So saying, Athena touched him with her wand” (13.429; see [[wand]]). The verses that follow carry out her promise word for word, the withered skin, the lost hair and the dimmed eyes (13.430–433, after 13.398–401), and then give more than she promised: the skin of an aged old man over all his limbs, a vile rag and a tunic, “rent, dirty, stained with foul smoke”, the great skin of a swift deer worn bare, a staff, and “a shabby pouch, / rent in many places”, with a twisted cord to sling it by (13.431–438; see [[rags]], [[clothing]], [[tunic]], [[deer]], [[scepter]] and [[pouch]]). At 13.430 the manuscripts read κάρψε μέν οἱ; the verse was originally, Monro thinks, κάρψεν μέν, as Bekker reads and the text prints, or κάρψεν οἱ, as Hermann proposed (Monro's critical note on 13.430; see the translation's note on 13.430), and for ῥυπόωντα one manuscript and Eustathius have ῥυπόεντα (Monro's critical note on 13.435). For the hair, the rag and the deerskin, and for Kirchhoff's view of the change, see [[shape-changing]] and the general article.

**The parting.** “When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (13.439–440; see [[lacedaemon]]). The book ends in the middle of a sentence, ἡ μὲν … αὐτὰρ ὁ …, “she went to Sparta, while he …”, and the next book goes on with Odysseus (Monro on 13.440).
