“But I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (ὅς τοι κότον ἔνθετο θυμῷ, 13.341–343; see [[cholos]], [[poseidon]] and [[kasignetos]]). The half-verse is Teiresias' prophecy (11.102), now spoken by the goddess as the reason for her absence; some manuscripts read χόλον for κότον (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.342). The book has already shown the grudge at work. As soon as the Phaeacians have set Odysseus ashore, “the earth-shaker / did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first” (13.125–127). He had never meant to take away Odysseus' homecoming altogether, since Zeus had promised it (13.131–133), and he turns his anger on those who carried him home (13.128–164; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). κότος is anger kept until it is fulfilled (see the entry); the god's is fulfilled on the Phaeacians.
