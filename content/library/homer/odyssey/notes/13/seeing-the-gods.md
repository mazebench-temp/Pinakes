**The mist.** On Scheria Athena's mist hid Odysseus from the Phaeacians (7.14–17, 7.39–42; see the Book 7 note); on Ithaca it hides his land from him. Odysseus woke where he slept in his own land and did not know it, “for the goddess had poured a mist around him, / Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, so that she might make him / unknown”, and “all things appeared in another shape to their lord” (13.189–196; see [[shape-changing]] and [[achlys]]). The mist is lifted only when she has told him what he must know: “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view” (13.352). In the Iliad Athena took the mist from Diomedes' eyes so that he might tell god from man (*Iliad* 5.127–128; see the entry); here the god has already declared herself, and the mist goes from the land he did not know.

**The goddess unknown.** “And Athena came near to him, / in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords” (13.221–223; see [[athena]]). He greets the stranger as a suppliant and prays to him “as to a god” (13.229–231; see [[supplication]] and [[prayer]]), not knowing that he is one. He does not know her through the whole of his lie, and when she has changed into a tall, beautiful woman she has to tell him: “yet you did not know / Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, who always / stand by you in all your toils and guard you” (13.299–301). No sign reveals her, as Aphrodite's neck and eyes revealed her to Helen (*Iliad* 3.396–398); the goddess makes herself known by her word, as she never did on Scheria, where he took her for a girl carrying a water jar (7.19–23; see the Book 7 note).

**Hard to know.** His answer gives the rule of the theme in his own case: “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]). And he turns it into a reproach: after the fall of Troy “I did not see you, daughter of Zeus, nor did I notice you / boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.318–319). Athena gives her reason, that she would not fight her father's brother Poseidon (13.341–343), and the poem had given the same reason on Scheria, where “she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; see [[poseidon]]).
