---
summary: The Phaeacians make twelve bulls ready for Poseidon “in the hope that he may have pity” (13.182); Odysseus, waking in a land he does not know, groans, strikes his thighs and laments (13.197–199, 13.219–221); and Athena says that she cannot abandon him, “unhappy as you are”, because of what he is (13.331–332).
---

**In the hope of pity.** Alcinous' proposal asks pity of the god who has just struck them: “and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity / and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain” (13.181–183; see [[poseidon]] and [[hecatomb]]). ἱερεύσομεν, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ are the words of the Trojan women's vow of twelve cows to Athena, “if she will pity / the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (*Iliad* 6.93–95, 6.308–310), which Athena refused (*Iliad* 6.311). The poem leaves the Phaeacians praying at the altar and never says whether Poseidon pitied them (13.185–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**Lamenting.** Odysseus wakes, looks at his own land and does not know it, “he groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs / with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word” (13.198–199). The two verses are those of Patroclus in the Iliad when he sees the Trojans rushing at the wall and the Danaans in rout (*Iliad* 15.397–398), and the second is that of Ares when he hears that his son is dead (*Iliad* 15.113–114): the gesture of a man struck by sudden disaster. His lament begins with the verse of his waking on Scheria, “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?” (13.200 = 6.119; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]), and when he has counted his gifts he drags himself along the shore, “lamenting much” (13.219–221; see [[lament]]). While he is lamenting, Athena comes to him in the likeness of a young shepherd (13.221–225; see [[shape-changing]]).

**Not pity.** When Athena has heard his lie and seen through it, she tells him why she stays by him: “therefore I cannot abandon you, unhappy as you are, / because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.331–332; see [[athena]]). She calls him δύστηνος, “unhappy”, but her reason is not his misery. It is his mind: “Always such is the thought in your breast” (13.330; see [[noos]]).
