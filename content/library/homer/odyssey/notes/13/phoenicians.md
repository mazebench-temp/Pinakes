**Noble Phoenicians.** After killing Orsilochus, the Cretan of the tale goes at once to a ship: “and noble Phoenicians / I begged, and gave them spoil to satisfy their hearts; / I bade them take me on board and set me down at Pylos, / or at shining Elis, where the Epeians rule” (13.272–275; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[pylos]] and [[elis]]). ἀγαυούς, “noble”, is the epithet that the poem has just given the Phaeacians who set Odysseus ashore (13.120), a likeness that Mure noted (see the general article and [[phaeacians]]). For ληΐδα, “spoil”, Aristophanes read ἤϊα, “provisions” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.273). Πύλονδε καταστῆσαι is a pregnant construction, “to bring to Pylos and set down there”, and the Greek order, “set me down at Pylos and put me on board”, puts the main action first, a common prothysteron that the English turns round (Monro on 13.274).

**Honest sailors.** “But the force of the wind drove them away from there, / much against their will; they did not wish to deceive me” (13.276–277; see [[the-winds]]). In the tale he tells Eumaeus later the same day, the Phoenician is “a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits”, who takes him home and then ships him to Libya meaning to sell him (14.288–297), and the Phoenician traders of Eumaeus' own story carry off the child and sell him (15.415–484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]); these keep faith.

**The landing retold.** The rest of the tale is the night Odysseus has just slept through, told of other sailors: they came to Ithaca by night, rowed into the harbor and lay down on the shore without supper; sleep came upon him, and they took his goods out of the hollow ship “and set them down where I myself lay on the sands”, and then went aboard “for well-peopled Sidon / and were gone” (13.278–286; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[sidon]]). So the Phaeacians had laid him asleep on the sand and set his gifts beside him before they rowed away (13.116–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).
