**At the head of the harbor.** The poet describes the harbor of Phorcys and the cave beside it as the ship draws near the island, before he tells how she came in (see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]):

{{quote:20f4966f-b577-5ea7-ba3a-d458a2aa1729}}

The olive stands at the head of the harbor and the cave “near it”, ἀγχόθι δʼ αὐτῆς (see [[olive]]). φάρεα are “webs”, as in Penelope's tale of her web (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.108, comparing 19.138; see [[weaving]] and [[loom]]); θεώτεραι, “for gods”, is not “more divine” but “divine”, in contrast with the door for men (Monro on 13.111; see the translation's note on 13.111 and [[immortals]]). The two doors are named by the winds they face, πρὸς Βορέαο, “toward the North Wind”, and πρὸς Νότου, “toward the South Wind” (see [[boreas]] and [[notus]]): the genitive is one of direction or aspect, as with ποτι- in the headlands that “crouch down toward the harbor”, λιμένος ποτιπεπτηυῖαι (Monro on 13.98). The Phaeacians lay Odysseus on the sand and set his gifts by the trunk of the olive, ἐκτὸς ὁδοῦ, “away from the road” (13.119–124; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]), not in the cave.

**This is the vaulted cave.** When Odysseus will not believe that he is home, Athena shows him the places one by one, the harbor, the olive, the cave and the mountain, each with a demonstrative, ὅδε, ἥδε, τοῦτο:

{{quote:fd50addf-150e-5d0a-82e4-28b9d38a29d5}}

The first two verses on the cave repeat 13.103–104, and some manuscripts omit them (Monro's critical note on 13.347–348). Monro takes them as wrongly repeated in some copies from the earlier description: the cave is first pointed out at 13.349, “and with the deictic τοῦτο: ‘and there &c.’” (on 13.347–348; see the translation's note on 13.347–348). For ἐστί at 13.349 some manuscripts read εὐρύ, “wide”, the word of Calypso's cave and the Cyclops' (Monro's critical note on 13.349; 5.77, 9.237; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]]). The cave is Odysseus' own place of worship: there he “used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs” (13.349–350; see [[hecatomb]] and [[nymphs]]), and when the mist is scattered he prays to the Naiads at once and promises them gifts “as before” (13.355–360; see [[prayer]]).

**The gifts hidden.** Athena bids him put the goods “in the inner part of the wondrous cave” (13.363; see [[wondrous]]), the half-verse of the bats that hang in the inner part of a cave in the simile of the suitors' souls (24.6, a plain rendering). “So saying, the goddess went into the misty cave, / searching through the cave for its hiding places” (13.366–367): κευθμών is the word of the “close-packed sties” in which the companions are penned like swine in the house of Circe (10.283; see the translation's key-word row and [[sties]]). Odysseus brings near the gold, the bronze and the clothes, and Athena lays them away and sets a stone against the doors (13.368–371; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]], [[possessions]] and [[door]]), as the Cyclops set his great stone against the doorway of his cave (9.240–243; see [[door-stone]]). The gifts are still there in Book 16, when Odysseus tells Telemachus that they “lie in caves by the will of the gods” (16.232, a plain rendering; see [[telemachus]]).
