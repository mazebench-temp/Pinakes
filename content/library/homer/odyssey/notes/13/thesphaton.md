---
summary: When the ship is turned to stone, Alcinous recognizes his father's prophecy: “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me” (13.172), the verse of the Cyclops when Odysseus told him his name.
---

The Phaeacians see their ship stop dead in sight of the city and do not know how it has come about; Alcinous does: “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me, / the oracles of my father, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (παλαίφατα θέσφαθʼ, 13.172–174; see [[alcinous]], [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). He had told Odysseus of the prophecy in Book 8, ending, “and these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.564–571); now he ends it, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (13.178). The first verse is the Cyclops' when he learned that Nobody was Odysseus, whose coming the seer Telemus had foretold to him (9.507–512; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[prophecy]]): in both books an old prophecy is fulfilled by the coming of Odysseus. Monro distinguishes ἔφασκε, “alleged”, the cause the old man gave, from φῆ, “said”, where the prophecy itself begins (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.173), and takes ἀγάσασθαι as “had been surprised”, that is, offended, made jealous (on 13.173; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.173 and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]).
