---
summary: In his first false tale Odysseus makes himself a Cretan. He heard of Ithaca “even in wide Crete”, and has fled from it as an exile, having killed Orsilochus, the son of Idomeneus, “who in wide Crete / outran toiling men with his swift feet” (13.256–261).
---

**Wide Crete.** Told by the young herdsman that the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy, Odysseus answers: “I heard of Ithaca, yes, even in wide Crete, / far over the sea” (13.256–257; see [[ithaca]] and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). Κρήτῃ εὐρείῃ, “wide Crete”, comes twice in five verses (13.256, 13.260); in the Iliad Idomeneus calls himself the son of Deucalion, “a lord over many men / in wide Crete” (*Iliad* 13.452–453; see [[idomeneus]]), and the later tales keep the phrase, “I claim that my line is from wide Crete” (14.199, a plain rendering). For Κρήτῃ at 13.256 one manuscript has Τροίῃ, “Troy” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.256).

**A Cretan's quarrel.** The tale is made of the Iliad's Crete. Its king is Idomeneus, who led the Cretans to Troy (*Iliad* 2.645–652); his son Orsilochus was a runner, who “outran toiling men with his swift feet” (13.260–261; see [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]] and [[toiling-men]]); and the quarrel was over the spoil the speaker brought from Troy, because he “would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (13.262–266; see [[troy]] and [[raiding]]). θεράπευον is “served as θεράπων”, and the negative covers χαριζόμενος too (Monro on 13.265; see the translation's note on 13.265). The Suda explains the proverb κρητίζειν, “to play the Cretan”, that is, to lie, by a story of Idomeneus himself: entrusted with dividing the bronze from the spoils, he took the best for himself; and it cites another proverb, “to play the Cretan with Cretans”, since they are liars and cheats (Suda κ 2407).

**Cretans off their course.** The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* tells of another Cretan ship. Cretans from Minoan Cnossus, sailing in a black ship “for trade and goods” to sandy Pylos, are carried by the god past Pylos and “shining Elis, where the Epeians rule”, in sight of “the steep mountain of Ithaca”; when the god meets them in the likeness of a young man, their leader asks him in Odysseus' words, “And tell me this truly, so that I may know it well”, what people, what land, what mortals are born there, and says that they were sailing “to Pylos from Crete” and came there “not willingly”, brought by one of the immortals (*Hymn to Apollo* 393–399, 424–429, 463–473; see [[pylos]] and [[elis]]). Odysseus' Cretan too was bound for Pylos or Elis and was driven off course against his sailors' will (13.274–278; see [[phoenicians]]). He will be a Cretan again in the tales he tells Eumaeus and Penelope (14.199–359, 19.165–202).
