summary: On the day of the escort Alcinous sacrifices an ox to Zeus and the Phaeacians burn the thigh-pieces and feast (13.24–26); when their ship has been turned to stone they make twelve picked bulls ready for Poseidon and pray around the altar (13.180–187); and Athena reminds Odysseus of the hecatombs he used to offer the nymphs in their cave (13.349–350).
# "βοῦν ἱέρευσʼ" (13.24), "μῆρα δὲ κήαντες" (13.26) and ἱερεύσομεν (13.182) are each found only here in the Odyssey.
grc: ["βοῦν ἱέρευσʼ", "μῆρα δὲ κήαντες", ἱερεύσομεν]
en: [sacrificed an ox, burned the thigh-pieces, let us sacrifice]
---

**An ox for Zeus.** The day of departure is spent at a sacrifice and its feast: “And for them the sacred might of Alcinous sacrificed an ox / to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all, / and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast” (13.24–26; see [[zeus]] and [[feast]]). The first verse is shaped on the Iliad's for the ox that Agamemnon sacrificed to Zeus for the chiefs, before the battle and after Ajax's duel (*Iliad* 2.402, 7.314), and the king sacrifices without a priest, as kings do in Homer (see [[priesthood]]). ἱέρευσʼ ἱερὸν μένος, “the sacred might … sacrificed”, is one of the plays of language that D. B. Monro collects (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.24, comparing 13.144). The rite is told in a verse, the burning of the thigh-pieces and the feast, where the Iliad can give it in full (see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]).

**Bulls for Poseidon.** When their ship has been turned to stone, Alcinous bids the Phaeacians give up their escorts, “and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity” (13.181–182; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]] and [[propitiation]]). ἱερεύσομεν, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ is the Trojan women's vow to Athena (*Iliad* 6.309; see [[hecatomb]] and [[pity]]). “So he spoke, and they were afraid, and made the bulls ready” (13.184), and the leaders stand around the altar and pray (13.185–187; see [[altar]] and [[prayer]]). The poem leaves the sacrifice at its beginning and does not tell how the god received it.

**For the nymphs.** On Ithaca the cave of the Naiads is the place “where you used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs” (13.349–350; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[nymphs]]), and Odysseus' prayer promises them gifts “as before” (13.358). In Book 17 the swineherd will pray to the nymphs of the spring, “if ever Odysseus / burned thigh-pieces to you” (17.240–241, a plain rendering; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
