summary: The Phaeacians' last day in the poem. Their elders add a great tripod and a cauldron each to Odysseus' gifts (13.7–15); they feast and pour libations as he takes his leave (13.23–69); their ship carries him home asleep in a single night, and the crew set him and his gifts down on the shore of Ithaca (13.70–125). For this Poseidon, whose stock they are, turns the ship to stone in sight of their city, and the poem leaves them praying round his altar, resolved to escort no more strangers (13.125–187).

**The last gifts.** When the tale is over, Alcinous bids each of the elders, “the counselors of the Phaeacians” who brought the gifts already in the chest, add a great tripod and a cauldron, “and we in turn will gather it among the people / and repay ourselves; for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift” (13.7–15; see [[alcinous]], [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]], [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[hard-for-one-man-to-give]]). τισόμεθα is “will repay ourselves” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.15), and the adverbial genitive προικός, “as a free gift”, is found in Attic inscriptions (Monro's additional note on 13.15, p. 545). At dawn the gifts are stowed under the thwarts by Alcinous himself, and the day passes in sacrifice and feasting while Odysseus watches the sun (13.18–35; see [[possessions]] and [[feast]]).

**The farewell.** Odysseus speaks “among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (13.36; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]]) and asks to be sent home, praying for them in turn: “And may you, staying here, gladden your wives, / your wedded wives, and your children; and may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (13.44–46). ἀρετή is “good”, not yet restricted, as in later Greek, to “merit” or “excellence” (Monro on 13.45; see [[arete]]). The wish is not granted: the ship that carries him is turned to stone in sight of the city as she comes home, and the whole people fears worse (13.125–187).

**The escorts.** The “noble escorts”, πομπῆες ἀγαυοί (13.71; see [[pompe]]), make his bed on the deck, row through the night and bring the ship at dawn into the harbor of Phorcys, “knowing the place before” (13.113; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). They lift him out asleep, set out the possessions that “the noble Phaeacians” had given him, Φαίηκες ἀγαυοί, and lay them by the olive, away from the road, lest a passer-by harm them before he woke; then they go home (13.116–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). Mure, as W. W. Merry reports him, set the epithet beside the same ἀγαυοί of the Phoenicians of Odysseus' false tale (13.272), one of the likenesses from which he made the Phaeacians Homer's playful satire on some Phoenician colony in the western Mediterranean; Merry finds the idea fanciful (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.34; see the general article and [[phoenicians]]).

**Poseidon's stock.** Poseidon complains to Zeus that mortals no longer honor him, “the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130), τοι, “after all”, marking the wrong done him by his own (Monro on 13.130; see [[poseidon]] and [[nausithous]]). Zeus leaves the vengeance to him and advises him to turn the ship to stone near the land, “so that all men may marvel” (13.139–158; see [[zeus]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). The punishment is watched from the shore: “And they spoke winged words to one another, / the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships”, and a man would say to his neighbor, “Ah me, who has fettered the swift ship on the sea / as she was driven home? And she was already in plain sight” (13.165–169; see [[long-oared]], [[famed-for-ships]] and [[tis-speech]]). “But they did not know how things had been brought about” (13.170).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** Alcinous knows. “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me, / the oracles of my father, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.172–174), in the words in which he had told Odysseus of the prophecy before the tale began (8.564–569; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[thesphaton]]). ἔφασκε, “alleged”, is set apart from φῆ, “said”, with which the prophecy itself begins (Monro on 13.173). He bids them give up the escort of mortals and sacrifice twelve picked bulls to Poseidon, in the hope that he will pity them and not cover their city with a mountain (13.179–183; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[hecatomb]]). “So he spoke, and they were afraid”, and the book leaves them at prayer: “So they prayed to lord Poseidon, / the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians, / standing around the altar” (13.184–187; see [[prayer]] and [[altar]]). The poem does not tell whether the god relented.

**Odysseus' reproach.** At that moment Odysseus wakes and, not knowing his land, blames them: “Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just, / the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians, / who have brought me away to another land” (13.209–211), οὐκ ἄρα … ἦσαν meaning “they are not, as I thought they were” (Monro on 13.209). νοήμονες οὐδὲ δίκαιοι, “discerning or just”, are the words in which Nestor explained why Zeus devised a grim homecoming for the Argives after Troy, “since in no way discerning or just / were they all” (3.133–134). Odysseus prays that Zeus, the god of suppliants, may punish them (13.213–214; see [[god-of-suppliants]] and [[supplication]]), and counts his goods to see whether they have gone off with anything; he misses nothing (13.215–219). Athena puts the account right: she made him dear to all the Phaeacians, and their gifts were given “by my counsel and my mind” (13.302–305; see [[athena]]). Odysseus' answer, that she encouraged him “in the rich land of the Phaeacian men” and led him into the city herself (13.322–323), stands in four verses generally condemned as spurious. The case against these two is less clear than against the two before them, but Monro grants that they have something of the air of an insertion meant to reconcile the speech with the Phaeacian episode, above all with Athena's guidance of Odysseus to the palace (Monro on 13.320–323, citing 7.12–81; see the translation's note on 13.320–323).

**What became of them.** Proclus, arguing that Plato's Atlantis, which the sea covered, is no fiction, denies that the author here removed the means of refuting his story “as Homer did with the Phaeacians and with the wall which the Greeks made” (Proclus, *Commentary on the Timaeus* I 190 Diehl, a plain rendering; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]): the view he rejects took the Phaeacians, like the Achaean wall, for a fiction that its poet made to vanish.
