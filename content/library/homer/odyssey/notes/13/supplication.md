summary: Waking in a land he does not know, Odysseus calls on “Zeus, the god of suppliants” to punish the Phaeacians (13.213–214); he greets the young shepherd as a suppliant, “I come to your dear knees” (13.228–235), and implores Athena “by your father” to tell him whether he is home (13.324–328); in his false tale the Cretan begged the Phoenicians for passage (13.272–273).
# ἱκετήσιος (13.213), only here in Homer, is Zeus' title: the line is a ref, not a form.
refs: [13.213]
en: [your dear knees]
---

**The god of suppliants.** On waking he thinks that the Phaeacians have broken their word: “Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just, / the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians, / who have brought me away to another land” (13.209–211), and he calls their own god down on them, “May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over / other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong” (13.213–214; see [[zeus]]). ἱκετήσιος, “of suppliants”, is found only here in Homer. It names the god to whom the Phaeacians poured libations the night he came to them as a suppliant, Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.165, 7.181), as Odysseus told the Cyclops that “Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers” (9.270; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[xenia]]). Aristarchus read the optative τίσαιτο, Zenodotus the imperative τισάσθω: “The imper. may have seemed improper (ἀπρεπές) applied to a deity” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.213). The curse is mistaken. The Phaeacians have kept their word, and the god who is punishing them at that moment is Poseidon, for the escort itself (13.125–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**At the shepherd's knees.** Then a young man comes, a shepherd, and Odysseus greets him as a suppliant greets the first comer: “My friend, since you are the first I find in this place, / greetings to you, and may you not meet me with evil intent, / but save these things, and save me; for to you / I pray as to a god, and I come to your dear knees” (13.228–231; see [[gounata]] and [[prayer]]). The knees are named, not clasped, as when he came to the river of Scheria “to your stream and to your knees” (5.449) and told Nausicaa that she was the first he had come to in that land (6.175–177; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). The suppliant asks to be told where he is, “what land is this, what people, what men are born here?” (13.233; see [[tell-me-this-truly]]). In Book 15 the fugitive [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] will supplicate Telemachus in nearly the same first verse, “My friend, since I find you making sacrifice in this place” (15.260; see [[exile-and-refuge]]).

**By your father.** When the shepherd has become a goddess and Odysseus still doubts that he is home, he entreats her again: “But now I implore you by your father—for I do not think / that I have come to clear-seen Ithaca” (13.324–325; see [[athena]]). γουνάζομαι is the suppliant's verb, and he names what the goddess holds dearest, as Elpenor implored him “by your wife and your father” (11.66–67) and Hector begged Achilles “by your life and your knees and your parents” (*Iliad* 22.338). Her father is Zeus. She answers by showing him the land (13.344–352).

**The Cretan's plea.** In the false tale the Cretan, a killer in flight, “begged” noble Phoenicians to take him on board (ἐλλισάμην, 13.272–274; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[phoenicians]] and [[the-killer-in-exile]]), and paid them with spoil. What the Cretan claims to have done, [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] does in fact: a man who has killed a kinsman, he comes to Telemachus' ship as a suppliant in his flight (15.272–278).
