---
summary: The winds are named twice. The two headlands of the harbor of Phorcys “keep off the great wave of the stormy winds / outside” (13.99–100), and in Odysseus' false tale “the force of the wind” drove the Phoenicians from Elis to Ithaca, “much against their will” (13.276–277). The two doors of the cave of the Naiads face the North Wind and the South Wind (13.110–111).
# The entry claims ἀνέμων (13.99) and ἀνέμοιο (13.276).
---

**The sheltered harbor.** In the harbor of Phorcys “two jutting / headlands, sheer, crouch down toward the harbor / and keep off the great wave of the stormy winds / outside; but within, without a mooring rope, there stay / the well-benched ships” (13.97–101; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). ἀνέμων κῦμα is “the waves raised by the winds” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.99, comparing *Iliad* 2.396 and 11.305), as in the Iliad's simile of the wave that never leaves a jutting crag alone, “from winds of every kind” (*Iliad* 2.394–397). δυσαήων, “stormy”, is a metrical license, since δυσαέων would be impossible in the hexameter (Monro on 13.99; see the translation's key-word row on δυσαής, the epithet of the West Wind at 5.295). The harbor of the island off the land of the Cyclopes was as safe, with no need of a cable or of mooring-stones (9.136–137; see [[mooring-stones]]).

**The force of the wind.** In his lie to the disguised Athena, Odysseus blames the wind for bringing him to Ithaca: “But the force of the wind drove them away from there, / much against their will; they did not wish to deceive me. / Driven off course from there, we came here by night” (13.276–278; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[phoenicians]]). ἲς ἀνέμοιο, “the force of the wind”, is the wind that tore the sails of his ships after Ismarus (9.71), and in his false tale to Penelope it will carry Odysseus himself to Crete, “driving him off course past Malea” (19.186–187, a plain rendering). The excuse is the one his own wanderings gave him: they began when, as he rounded Malea, “the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera” (9.80–81; see [[malea]]).

**The quarters.** The cave of the Naiads has a door “toward the North Wind” and another “toward the South Wind” (13.110–111; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]): the winds name the quarters of the sky (see the Book 13 notes on [[boreas]] and [[notus]]).
