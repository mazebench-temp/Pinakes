summary: Athena takes over the book's second half. The Phaeacians' gifts were given “through great-hearted Athena” (13.121); on Ithaca she pours a mist around the waking Odysseus so that he does not know his own land, comes to him as a young herdsman and tells him where he is, hears his lie and smiles, and takes the form of a tall and beautiful woman. She makes herself known and speaks of the cunning they share, answers his complaint that he never saw her after Troy, shows him the island and scatters the mist, hides his goods in the cave of the Naiads, plans the suitors' death with him under the sacred olive, makes him an old beggar with her wand, and goes to Lacedaemon to bring Telemachus home.

**Through great-hearted Athena.** When the Phaeacians have laid Odysseus asleep on the sand, they lift out “the possessions which the noble Phaeacians / had given him as he went home, through great-hearted Athena” (13.120–121; see [[possessions]] and [[great-hearted]]). διά is “by the agency of” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.121): the gifts were her work. μεγάθυμος is said of her only here and in Demodocus' song, where Odysseus conquered at the house of Deiphobus “through great-hearted Athena” (8.520; see the Book 8 note). She claims the gifts herself when she makes herself known: she has come “to hide the goods, all that the noble Phaeacians / gave you as you went home, by my counsel and my mind” (13.304–305), with the verse-end of 13.121, and she made him “dear to all the Phaeacians” (13.302), as she had poured grace on him “so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians” (8.21) and as he had asked of her in her grove, “Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied” (6.327; see the Book 6 note). In three manuscripts, when Odysseus brings the goods to the cave, the clothes at 13.369 are once more those that the noble Phaeacians gave him through great-hearted Athena, in the words of 13.120–121 (Monro's critical note on 13.369; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).

**The mist.** Odysseus wakes in his own land and does not know it, “for the goddess had poured a mist around him, / Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, so that she might make him / unknown, and tell him each thing, / lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends, / before the suitors had paid for all their transgression” (13.189–193; see [[pallas]], [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). The mist is not meant to make him invisible, Monro explains: Athena wishes to prepare him for the work before him by consultation and by changing his appearance, as she does at 13.429–438, and ἄγνωστον τεύξειεν, “that she might make him unknown”, is the same act as her ἄγνωστον τεύξω, “I will make you unknown”, before the change (13.397); without these measures Odysseus “would have gone straight to his palace, and all would have been lost” (Monro on 13.191; see the translation's note on 13.189–191). αὐτόν is “himself”, his person, as Odysseus later says that she makes herself (αὐτήν) like anything (13.313; Monro on 13.190); Aristophanes read αὐτῷ (Monro's critical note on 13.190). In Scheria her mist hid Odysseus from the Phaeacians on his way to the palace (7.14–17, 7.140–143; see the Book 7 note); here it hides his own island from him, “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord” (13.194). She takes it away herself when she has named the harbor, the olive, the cave and the mountain: “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view” (13.352; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). In the Iliad she took the mist from the eyes of Diomedes, “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (*Iliad* 5.127–128; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The young herdsman.** “And Athena came near to him, / in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords, / with a well-made cape folded double around her shoulders; / and under her shining feet she had sandals, and in her hands a javelin” (13.221–225; see [[shape-changing]]). The half-verse that brings her is the one with which she came to Telemachus “in the likeness of Mentor” (2.267–268; see [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]]), and ἀνδρὶ δέμας εἰκυῖα, “in form like a man”, is her likeness at the Phaeacian games (8.194; see the Book 8 note). ἐπιβώτωρ, “herdsman”, παναπάλος, “very delicate”, and λώπη, “cape”, are found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row). In the poem she has been Mentes, Mentor, a girl with a water jar and a herald (1.105, 2.268, 7.19–20, 8.8); this time the man she deceives is himself a master of disguise. He greets the herdsman as a suppliant and prays to him “as to a god” (13.231; see [[supplication]] and [[prayer]]), and she tells him the land in a speech that keeps its name, Ithaca, for the last verses (13.237–249; see [[ithaca]]).

**The smile.** When he has told her that he is a Cretan in exile (13.256–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]), “the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled, / and stroked him with her hand; and she was like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.287–289; see [[gray-eyed]] and [[smiling-at-him]]). The smile and the touch of the hand are Calypso's when Odysseus suspects her of a plot (5.180–181; see [[calypso]]) and Menelaus' when Telemachus refuses his horses (4.609–610). The woman's form is the one in which she will appear at the hut of Eumaeus, seen by Odysseus and the dogs but not by Telemachus, “for the gods by no means appear in plain sight to all” (16.157–161, a plain rendering), and in which she comes down to him on the night before the slaughter (20.30–31). Two manuscripts omit 13.289 (Monro's critical note on 13.289). The skill in handiwork is her own gift to the women she favors (7.110–111, 2.116–117; see [[weaving]]).

**The cunning they share.** Then she speaks:

She praises in him what she claims for herself: he is the best of mortals “in counsel and in words”, she is famed “among all the gods” for μῆτις, “cunning”, and κέρδεα, “crafty ways” (13.297–299; see [[crafty-would-he-be]], [[metis]], [[kerdos]], [[dolos]] and [[of-subtle-wiles]]). In Homer πολύμητις, “of many wiles”, is the epithet of Odysseus (once of Hephaestus, *Iliad* 21.355); the *Homeric Hymn to Athena* gives it to her (see the general article). Her “who always / stand by you in all your toils” turns into her own words the prayer he made to her on the night raid at Troy, “you who always / stand by me in all my toils” (*Iliad* 10.278–279; see [[the-doloneia]]), and the complaint of the lesser Ajax that the goddess “always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (*Iliad* 23.782–783); Nestor had told Telemachus that he never saw the gods show love so openly “as Pallas Athena openly stood beside him” (3.221–222; see the Book 3 note). She has come “to weave a plan with you” (13.303), and to tell him the sorrows he must bear in his house in silence, “submitting to the violence of men” (13.306–310; see [[silence]] and [[much-enduring]]).

**Not seen since Troy.** Odysseus answers that a mortal cannot know her, “for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]] and [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]), and reproaches her: “But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans, / after that I did not see you, daughter of Zeus, nor did I notice you / boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.316–319). The first two verses are Nestor's, who goes on to tell how Zeus devised a grim homecoming for the Argives “through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father” (3.130–135; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and the Book 3 note). The verses that follow, that he wandered until in the land of the Phaeacians she encouraged him and led him into the city herself (13.320–323), are generally condemned: the first two certainly, since the clause πρίν γʼ ὅτε belongs with 13.318 and the parenthesis is unhomeric; against the last two the ancients argued that Odysseus could not know of her presence in Phaeacia, where she never revealed herself to him. Monro thinks that objection asks for more precision than a poem can be expected to keep, but allows that the two verses look like an insertion meant to reconcile the speech with Book 7, and reports that Nitzsch rejected all four (Monro on 13.320–323; see the translation's note on 13.320–323). Then he implores her “by your father” to tell him whether he has come home (13.324–328; see [[zeus]]).

**Her father's brother.** “Always such is the thought in your breast; / therefore I cannot abandon you, unhappy as you are, / because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.330–332; see [[sensible]]). Another man would have hurried home to his wife; he will make trial of her first (13.333–338), six verses that Aristarchus rejected (Monro's critical note on 13.333–338; see [[penelope]] and [[peira]]). She never doubted that he would come home, “but I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343; see [[poseidon]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The narrator had given the reason in Scheria, where she heard his prayer “But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; see the Book 6 note), and W. W. Merry notes that she throws off this reserve as soon as Odysseus is on Ithaca and beyond Poseidon's power (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.330). Poseidon's last act in the poem has already been told: the punishment of the Phaeacians and their ship (13.125–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**The land, the treasure and the plan.** “But come, I will show you the seat of Ithaca, so that you may be certain” (13.344): she names the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave where he used to sacrifice to the nymphs, and Mount Neritum, and scatters the mist (13.345–352; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[neritum]]). He kisses the earth and prays to the Naiads, and the condition of his vow is hers to grant, “if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants / that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow” (13.359–360; see [[driver-of-spoil]], [[nymphs]] and [[prayer]]). Her answer begins with Hephaestus' words to Thetis, “Take heart; do not let these things trouble your mind” (13.362 = *Iliad* 18.463). The goddess herself goes “into the misty cave, / searching through the cave for its hiding places”, lays the goods away and sets a stone against the doors (13.366–371; see [[possessions]]). Then “the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive / and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[olive]] and [[the-suitors]]). She tells him of the suitors who have been lording it in his hall for three years and of Penelope's messages (13.375–381); he asks her to “weave a plan” and to stand beside him “as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.386–388; see [[veil]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]), and would fight three hundred men “with you, lady goddess, if you would help me with a ready heart” (13.390–391). γλαυκῶπι, “gray-eyed one” (13.389), is a vocative that Homer uses elsewhere only in the threat that Zeus sends her by Iris (*Iliad* 8.420), and πότνα θεά, “lady goddess”, is his address to Calypso (5.215). “Yes, truly I will be beside you”, she answers (13.393), and foretells the floor spattered with the suitors' blood and brains (13.394–396).

**The wand and the errand.** “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals” (13.397): she will wither his skin, take away his hair, dim his eyes and dress him in [[rags|rags]], so that he will seem unsightly to the suitors, to his wife and to his son (13.398–403); and she sends him to the swineherd while she goes to Sparta for Telemachus (13.404–415). To his question why she did not tell his son, she answers, “I myself escorted him, so that he might win noble fame / by going there” (13.422–423; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[kleos]]), the purpose she named at the first council, “and so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). “So saying, Athena touched him with her wand” (13.429; see [[wand]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). The wand is Hermes' and Circe's elsewhere in Homer; Athena uses one only on Odysseus, here and twice in Book 16, a golden wand to restore him for his son and the wand again to make him old (16.172, 16.456). Monro counts this change among the incidents “of the fairy-tale order”: unlike the beauty she gives him in Scheria, it is “a magical one,—not a mere illusion”, and if later scenes imply only a beggar's rags, “The poet has made a somewhat excessive use of the marvellous, and afterwards returns unconsciously to a more natural point of view” (Monro, Appendix I §6, answering Kirchhoff). The book ends with her going: “When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (13.439–440; see [[lacedaemon]]). The first verse is the parting of Zeus and Thetis in the Iliad, after she has won his promise for Achilles (*Iliad* 1.531). The book ends in the middle of a sentence, ἡ μὲν … αὐτὰρ ὁ, “she went to Sparta, while he” (Monro on 13.440): Book 14 goes on with Odysseus on his way to the swineherd, and her arrival at Lacedaemon, “to remind him of his homecoming and to urge him to return”, waits for the beginning of Book 15 (15.1–3, a plain rendering).

**Her names.** Παλλὰς Ἀθηναίη, κούρη Διός (13.190, 13.300) and Παλλὰς Ἀθηναίη, κούρη Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο (13.252, 13.371); θεὰ γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, seven times in the verses that introduce her speeches and her smile (13.236, 13.287, 13.329, 13.361, 13.374, 13.392, 13.420); Ἀθήνη alone (13.221, 13.429); μεγάθυμον Ἀθήνην (13.121); θεός and θεά, “the goddess” (13.189, 13.352, 13.366). Odysseus calls her θεά, κούρη Διός, γλαυκῶπι and πότνα θεά (13.312, 13.318, 13.385, 13.389, 13.391), and in his prayer Διὸς θυγάτηρ ἀγελείη (13.359). See [[pallas]], [[gray-eyed]], [[daughter-of-zeus]], [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]] and [[driver-of-spoil]].

**Later readers.** Proclus, explaining why Plato calls Athena a lover of wisdom, says that she is “called Metis among the gods”, and gives her words here as his proof: she says of herself that she is “famed for cunning and crafty ways” (Proclus, *On the Timaeus* I 168 Diehl, quoting 13.299; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Plato's Socrates would allow no poet to show a god who changes his form or deceives: the god is simple and true in deed and word, “and neither changes himself nor deceives others” (*Republic* 382e; see [[shape-changing]] and [[plato]]). In this book Athena does both, and boasts of it.
