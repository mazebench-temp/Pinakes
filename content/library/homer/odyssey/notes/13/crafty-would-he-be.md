---
summary: Athena's answer to the Cretan tale (13.287–310): she smiles, strokes him with her hand and takes a woman's shape, then speaks the maxim, sets her own cunning beside his, and tells him why she has come.
---

**The smile.** Odysseus ends his lie, “but I was left behind, grieving at heart” (13.286), and “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled, / and stroked him with her hand” (13.287–288; see [[smiling-at-him]]). It is the gesture of Calypso when he would not trust her raft without an oath, “Truly you are a rogue, and no fool” (5.180–182), and of Menelaus when Telemachus had refused his horses, “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak” (4.609–611): the smile of one who has seen through a speech and is pleased by it. As she speaks she puts off the shepherd and is “like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.288–289; see [[shape-changing]]).

**The speech.** The maxim opens the speech (13.291–292), the admiring reproach follows (13.293–295), and then the pairing of the two, “since we both know / crafty ways” (13.296–299; see [[kerdos]] and [[metis]]). The rest of the speech gives her purpose: she has stood by him in all his toils, “and made you dear to all the Phaeacians”, and now she has come “to weave a plan with you / and to hide the goods” and to tell him what he must endure at home, in silence (13.300–310; see [[silence]] and [[possessions]]). His answer begins with the same thought turned round, that a mortal cannot know her (13.312–313; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]), and her reply ends the contest of wits with praise: “therefore I cannot abandon you, unhappy as you are, / because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.331–332; see [[pity]]).
