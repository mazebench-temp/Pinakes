---
summary: The sky is named twice: the Phaeacians pour their parting libations “to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky” (13.54–55), and in Odysseus' false tale “a very dark night covered the sky” when the Cretan killed Orsilochus from ambush (13.269).
---

**The gods of the wide sky.** At the farewell the Phaeacians “poured libations / to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky, / from where they sat” (13.54–56; see [[libation]], [[blessed-gods]] and [[wide-sky]]). The verse-end τοὶ οὐρανὸν εὐρὺν ἔχουσιν is the one Teiresias used for the gods to whom Odysseus must sacrifice at home, and Eurylochus for the gods to whom he would offer the Sun's cattle (11.133, 12.344; see the Book 12 note).

**A very dark night.** In the tale he tells Athena, the Cretan lay in ambush by the road with one companion and struck down Orsilochus as he came down from the country: “A very dark night covered the sky, and no one / of men noticed us, and I took his life unseen” (13.267–270; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]] and [[ambush]]). δνοφερή, “dark”, is said of night only here and in Book 15 (15.50, a plain rendering); in the Iliad it is the murky water of the spring that pours down a sheer rock (*Iliad* 9.15, 16.4; see [[dnopheros]] and [[night]]).
