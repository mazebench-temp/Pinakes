summary: On the day of the departure Alcinous sacrifices an ox to Zeus, and the Phaeacians burn the thigh-pieces and feast (13.24–27), in the verse with which Odysseus once sacrificed to Zeus a ram that the god did not heed.
# μῆρα (13.26, the only place in Homer where the word is printed unelided), first met here; it applies in the Odyssey
# only. Elsewhere the Odyssey has μῆρʼ and μηρία, which the entry and the earlier notes claim in their phrases.
grc: [μῆρα]

“And for them the sacred might of Alcinous sacrificed an ox / to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all, / and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast, / delighting in it” (13.24–27; see [[alcinous]], [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]], [[zeus]], [[sacrifice]] and [[feast]]). The second verse is the one with which Odysseus sacrificed to Zeus the ram his companions gave him when they escaped from the Cyclops, “and burned the thigh-pieces; but he took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.552–555; see [[lord-of-dark-clouds]] and the Book 9 note). The poem does not say whether Zeus heeded this sacrifice; when Poseidon complains of the Phaeacians, Zeus bids him do as he wishes (13.139–145; see [[poseidon]]). ἱέρευσʼ ἱερὸν μένος, “the sacred might … sacrificed”, is a play of language of the kind Monro collects (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.24; see [[hieros]]).
