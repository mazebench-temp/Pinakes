---
summary: The harbor in Libya where the storm leaves Aeneas is built from the harbor of Phorcys, where the Phaeacians set Odysseus ashore. It too is sheltered by a barrier on which the waves break and by twin cliffs; under the cliff is a cave with fresh water and seats of living rock, the home of the nymphs; and no cable or anchor holds the ships.

**The harbor.** When the storm has passed, the weary Trojans make for the nearest shore and turn toward Libya. “There is a place in a deep inlet: an island makes a harbor with the barrier of its sides, on which every wave from the deep is broken and parts into the curving bays” (*Est in secessu longo locus: insula portum / efficit obiectu laterum, quibus omnis ab alto / frangitur inque sinus scindit sese unda reductos*, *Aeneid* 1.159–161; plain renderings of the Latin throughout). The harbor of Phorcys is introduced in the same way, “There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / in the land of Ithaca”, and its shelter is the same: two headlands “keep off the great wave of the stormy winds / outside” (13.96–100; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). Virgil's island takes the place of the headlands, and the headlands come back as “vast rocks and twin cliffs” that tower on either side, “beneath whose summit the waters lie safe and silent far and wide” (*vastae rupes geminique … scopuli*, *Aeneid* 1.162–164). Above them hangs a dark wood (*Aeneid* 1.164–165), where Homer has a single long-leaved olive at the head of the harbor (13.102; see [[olive]]).

**The cave of the nymphs.** “Facing them, under hanging rocks, is a cave; within it are sweet waters and seats of living rock, the home of the nymphs” (*Fronte sub adversa scopulis pendentibus antrum, / intus aquae dulces vivoque sedilia saxo, / nympharum domus*, *Aeneid* 1.166–168). Homer's cave is “sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads”, and in it are “ever-flowing waters”, with bowls, jars and towering looms, all of stone (13.103–109; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[nymphs]]). Virgil keeps the water and the stone, turns the stone into seats, and leaves out the bees, the looms and the two doors, one for men and one for the gods (13.106–112).

**No cable, no anchor.** “Here no cables hold the weary ships, no anchor binds them with its hooked bite” (*hic fessas non vincula navis / ulla tenent, unco non alligat ancora morsu*, *Aeneid* 1.168–169). In Homer's harbor “within, without a mooring rope, there stay / the well-benched ships, once they have reached the measure of anchorage” (ἄνευ δεσμοῖο, 13.100–101; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[mooring-stones]]). Virgil adds the anchor and the weariness of the ships, which have come through the storm.

**The landing.** Into this harbor Aeneas brings his seven ships, and the Trojans disembark “with great love of the land” (*Aeneid* 1.170–173). The Phaeacians row into the harbor of Phorcys “knowing the place before” and lay Odysseus on the sand asleep (13.113–119; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]); his own love of the land comes later, when the mist is scattered and he kisses the grain-giving earth (13.352–354; see [[grain-giving]]). Virgil sets on one Libyan shore what the Odyssey divides between two books: the storm and the swimmer's landing on Scheria (5.291–463) and the sheltered harbor of the homecoming.
