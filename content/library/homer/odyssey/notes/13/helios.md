summary: The sun marks the end of Odysseus' last day among the Phaeacians: he “kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun, / in haste for it to set”, and the light of the sun sets as gladly for him as for a plowman who longs for his supper (13.28–35). On Ithaca Athena speaks of men who live “toward the dawn and the sun” (13.240).

**Waiting for sunset.** While Demodocus sings at the feast, the poet turns to the guest: “But Odysseus / kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun, / in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (13.28–30; see [[demodocus]] and [[nostos]]). δῦναι ἐπειγόμενος is “in haste, eager for (his) setting” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.30). παμφανόων, “all-gleaming”, is said only here of the sun; elsewhere in Homer it is the gleam of armor and weapons, chariots, walls, cauldrons and flame (see the translation's key-word row on παμφανόων). The simile that follows gives the sunset to a plowman: “And as when a man longs for his supper, for whom all day long / two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field, / and gladly for him the light of the sun sets, / so that he may go about his supper, and his knees are hindered as he goes, / so welcome to Odysseus did the light of the sun set” (13.31–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]] and [[the-plowing]]). κατέδυ φάος ἠελίοιο, “the light of the sun sets”, is a variant of the formula of nightfall (see [[sunset-lines]]); with ἔδυ the half-verse belongs elsewhere to weeping that would have lasted until sunset, the mourning for Patroclus, “And now the light of the sun would have set on them as they mourned” (*Iliad* 23.154), and two scenes of recognition in the Odyssey's later books (16.220, 21.226). Here the sunset is welcome. Monro defends the adverb ἀσπασίως against those who would read an adjective (on 13.33).

**Toward the dawn and the sun.** Athena, in the likeness of a young herdsman, tells Odysseus that very many know his island, “both those who live toward the dawn and the sun / and those behind, toward the misty darkness” (13.240–241; see the Book 13 note on [[eos]]), the east and the west, as in his own account of Ithaca to the Phaeacians (9.26). The sun is not named again: the voyage is by night, the landing comes at the rising of the morning star, and the poet does not tell the sunrise on Ithaca (13.93–95; see the Book 13 note on [[eos]]).
