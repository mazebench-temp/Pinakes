summary: Odysseus wakes “in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it” (13.188); he “looked at his native land” (13.197) and “mourned for his native land” (13.219); he is glad, “rejoicing in his own land, the land of his fathers” (13.251), and asks Athena, “tell me if it is true that I have come to my dear native land” (13.328). Alcinous prays that “we may send the stranger on his way to his native land” (13.52), and the suitors would kill Telemachus “before he reaches his native land” (13.426).
grc: ["γαίῃ πατρωΐῃ"]
en: [land of his fathers]
---

The goal of every return in the poem is reached in this book, and the man who reaches it does not know it: he looks at his native land and mourns for it, dragging himself along the shore (13.197–221), and asks for it as for a place he has not reached (13.324–328; see [[nostos]] and [[ithaca]]). γαίῃ πατρωΐῃ, “the land of his fathers,” is new to the entry: the narrator's words for the land where he sleeps and the place he rejoices in when Athena names it (13.188, 13.251). When she shows it to him the verse returns without πατρωΐῃ, “rejoicing in his own land” (13.354; see [[much-enduring]] and [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]]). 13.426 is the verse of the suitors' plot in Book 4 (4.823; see [[black-ships]]).
