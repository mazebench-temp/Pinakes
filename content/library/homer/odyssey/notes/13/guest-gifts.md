summary: The Phaeacians' guest-gifts are increased when the tale is done, a great tripod and a cauldron from each lord (13.10–22), and Odysseus names them with the escort “gifts of friendship” (13.41). The sailors set them by the olive on Ithaca (13.117–124); waking, he counts them, suspecting his hosts, and misses nothing (13.203–219); and Athena hides them in the cave of the nymphs (13.361–371).
# "φίλα δῶρα" (13.41; also 8.545, a ref in the Book 8 note): the gifts of the hosts, always these two lines.
grc: ["φίλα δῶρα"]
en: [gifts of friendship]
refs: [13.10-15, 13.19-22, 13.117-124, 13.135-136, 13.203-219, 13.304-305, 13.361-371]
---

**More gifts.** The gifts that the kings gave in Book 8 already lie in Arete's chest (8.387–448; see the Book 8 note), and Alcinous adds to them: “but come, let us give him a great tripod and a cauldron, / man by man” (13.13–14; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]], [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]). The lords are to recover the cost from the people, “for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift” (13.15; see [[hard-for-one-man-to-give]]), and at dawn the king stows the bronze under the thwarts himself, so that it may not hinder the rowers (13.19–22).

**Gifts of friendship.** Odysseus' farewell names what he has received: “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship; and may the heavenly gods / make them prosper for me” (13.40–42; see [[pompe]] and [[olbos]]). The words are those with which Alcinous named them at the evening feast, “the escort and the gifts of friendship, which we give him, making him welcome” (8.545). A gift calls for a return (see the entry), and the guest who cannot make one prays for his hosts: “and may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (13.45–46).

**On the shore of Ithaca.** The sailors lift him out asleep, then “the possessions which the noble Phaeacians / had given him as he went home, through great-hearted Athena”, and set them together by the trunk of the olive, away from the road, lest a wayfarer should harm them (13.120–124; see [[possessions]] and [[olive]]); διά is “by the agency of” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.121). Poseidon counts the gift as the Phaeacians' offense: “untold gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing” (13.135–136; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). Odysseus, waking in a land he does not know, thinks that his hosts have broken faith, and counts the gifts “to see whether they have gone off with anything in the hollow ship”; “Of these he missed nothing” (13.209–219; see [[supplication]]). The full count is the proof of the hosts' good faith that his curse denied.

**Hidden.** Athena has come, she says, “to hide the goods, all that the noble Phaeacians / gave you as you went home, by my counsel and my mind” (13.304–305), and together they lay the gold, the bronze and the clothes in the cave of the nymphs and set a stone against the doors (13.363–371; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The gifts of Scheria stay there while their owner goes to his house as a beggar (13.429–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).
