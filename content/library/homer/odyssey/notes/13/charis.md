# χαρίσασθαι (13.15; again 17.452, "in giving freely of what belongs to others").
grc: [χαρίσασθαι]
en: [give as a free gift, favor]
---

**A free gift.** Alcinous bids the twelve kings add a great tripod and a cauldron each to the stranger's gifts: “and we in turn will gather it among the people / and repay ourselves; for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift” (ἀργαλέον γὰρ ἕνα προικὸς χαρίσασθαι, 13.14–15; see [[doron]], [[demos]] and [[poine]]). χαρίζομαι is to give as a favor, and προικός is “as a free gift”, without repayment: originally a partitive genitive, “of his bounty”, used as an adverb like the Attic προῖκα, *gratis*; Monro connects with it προΐκτης, “one who lives on doles”, a name for the beggar in Book 17 (17.352, 17.449), and refers to the verse in which the beggar is to “taste of the Achaeans at no cost” (17.413, a plain rendering), where προικός returns (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.15). The gift is free to Odysseus; the people pay for it (see [[guest-gifts]]).

**Favor refused.** In the tale he tells the disguised Athena, the Cretan killed Orsilochus, son of Idomeneus, because he wished to rob him of his spoil from Troy, “because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (οὐχ ᾧ πατρὶ χαριζόμενος θεράπευον, 13.265–266; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]], [[idomeneus]] and [[hetairos]]). θεράπευον is “served as θεράπων”, and the negative covers χαριζόμενος as well, “I did not court his favour by serving” (Monro on 13.265; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.265). The favor refused is the motive of the killing in the tale.
