summary: Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians' gifts outdo the share of the spoil of Troy that Odysseus would have won by lot (13.137–138); in the false tale the Cretan kills the son of Idomeneus, who tried to rob him of his spoil from Troy (13.262–266).
# "λαχὼν ἀπὸ ληΐδος αἶσαν" (13.138, as 5.40), "won his share of the spoil".
grc: ["λαχὼν ἀπὸ ληΐδος αἶσαν"]
en: [won his share of the spoil]
refs: [13.262-263]
---

**The share of Troy.** The measure of the Phaeacians' generosity is the division at Troy. They have given Odysseus “a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy, / if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (13.137–138; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]), in the verses of Zeus' prophecy in Book 5 (5.39–40). λαχών is “having got by lot”, and αἶσα is the share (see [[aisa]] and [[lots]]): in the Iliad Menoetius was promised that his son would come home “when he had sacked Ilios and won his share of the spoil” (*Iliad* 18.327).

**A share robbed.** The false tale turns on the same division. The Cretan says that he killed Orsilochus, son of Idomeneus, “because he wished to rob me of all my spoil / from Troy, for which I suffered sorrows in my heart” (13.262–263; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]), because he would not serve as Idomeneus' attendant at Troy and led his own companions (13.265–266; see [[raiding]]). It is the quarrel of the first book of the Iliad, a prize won with toil and a powerful man who would take it (*Iliad* 1.161–162; see [[geras]]), and here it ends in a killing. In the tale he tells Eumaeus the Cretan describes the leader's part in the spoil of his raids, “From this I picked out what satisfied my heart, and much afterward / I received by lot” (14.232–233).
