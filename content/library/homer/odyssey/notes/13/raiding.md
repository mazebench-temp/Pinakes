---
summary: Odysseus reminds Athena how “we had sacked the steep city of Priam” (13.316); in his false tale the Cretan is a leader of his own band at Troy, who kills Idomeneus' son for trying to rob him of his spoil and pays his passage with spoil (13.262–273).
---

**The sack of Troy.** Odysseus dates Athena's absence from the fall of the city: “But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans, / after that I did not see you” (13.316–318), in Nestor's verses on the departure from Troy (3.130–131; see [[troy]] and [[priam]]). Later he asks her to stand beside him as she did “when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.388), the phrase of Achilles' wish in the Iliad, “so that we alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy” (*Iliad* 16.100; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.388, takes κρήδεμνα as “the diadem of towers”; see [[veil]]). The treasure that Poseidon resents is measured against the spoil of the sack: the Phaeacians have given him “a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy, / if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (13.137–138, Zeus' words at 5.39–40; see [[division-of-spoils]]).

**The Cretan's spoil.** The false tale gives Odysseus another past at Troy. The Cretan killed Orsilochus, the son of Idomeneus, “because he wished to rob me of all my spoil / from Troy, for which I suffered sorrows in my heart”, and the quarrel began “because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (13.262–266; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]). τῆς ληΐδος is “my spoil”, the article having a possessive sense (Monro on 13.262). The Cretan went to Troy as the leader of his own men, with his own share of the spoil, which the king's son coveted (see [[division-of-spoils]]). After the killing he “begged” a passage from the Phoenicians “and gave them spoil to satisfy their hearts” (13.272–273; see [[phoenicians]]), where Aristophanes read ἤϊα, “provisions” (Monro's critical note on 13.273). In the tale he tells Eumaeus, the Cretan has led raids overseas before the war, “nine times I led men and swift-sailing ships / against foreign men” (14.230–231), and his house grew rich from the spoil (see [[piracy]]).
