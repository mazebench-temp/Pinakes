---
summary: The old in the book: the Phaeacian kings who drink “the sparkling wine of the elders” (13.8), the old age that Odysseus wishes for Arete (13.59), “the old man” Nausithous, whose prophecy comes true (13.178), the Old Man of the Sea, Phorcys, whose harbor receives Odysseus (13.96, 13.345), and “an aged old man”, the likeness Athena gives him (13.432).
---

**Elders.** Alcinous charges “all who in my halls drink the sparkling wine of the elders / always” to add their gifts (γερούσιον αἴθοπα οἶνον, 13.8–9; see [[elders]] and [[wine]]), the Iliad's phrase for the wine of the kings' council (*Iliad* 4.259; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, γερούσιος οἶνος).

**Old age and death.** Odysseus' farewell to Arete wishes her a long life: “Farewell to you, queen, continually, until old age / and death come, which are the lot of men” (γῆρας … καὶ θάνατος, 13.59–60; see [[basileus]] and [[thanatos]]).

**The old man.** Alcinous recognizes the prophecy of his father Nausithous, and ends the account of it, “So the old man used to say; and now it is all being fulfilled” (ὁ γέρων, 13.178; see [[nausithous]] and [[thesphaton]]). The harbor where the Phaeacians land belongs to another old man, “There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea” (ἁλίοιο γέροντος, 13.96, again 13.345; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]), the title that Menelaus gave Proteus (4.349; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]]).

**An old man's skin.** Athena's wand gives Odysseus the body of age: “She withered the fair skin on his supple limbs, / and destroyed the fair hair from his head, and the skin / of an aged old man she put around all his limbs” (παλαιοῦ θῆκε γέροντος, 13.430–432; see [[shape-changing]] and [[wand]]). The old age is real while it lasts: the change is a magical one, not a mere illusion, as Kirchhoff insisted, and when Athena restores him before Telemachus he is really himself again (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §6; 16.172–176).
