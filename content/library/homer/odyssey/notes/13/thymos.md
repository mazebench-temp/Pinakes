---
summary: The θυμός of the book: the heart's desire fulfilled in the escort and the gifts (13.40); the hearts of the gods, Zeus' “as has pleased your heart” and Poseidon's regard for Zeus' spirit (13.145–154), and the grudge that Poseidon has laid up “in his heart” (13.342); the sorrows suffered “in his heart” by the true Odysseus and the false (13.90, 13.263); the life the false Cretan takes (13.270); and Penelope, mourning “in her heart” (13.379).
---

**The heart's desire.** Odysseus' farewell begins, “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship” (ἅ μοι φίλος ἤθελε θυμός, 13.40–41; see [[pompe]] and [[doron]]). The narrator, as the ship runs, recalls the man “who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart” (ὃν κατὰ θυμόν, 13.90; see [[algos]]), and the false Cretan claims the same for himself, “for which I suffered sorrows in my heart” (13.263; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**The hearts of gods.** Zeus leaves Poseidon free, “Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (13.145), and Poseidon answers that he would act at once, “but I always have regard for your spirit, and avoid it” (σὸν αἰεὶ θυμὸν ὀπίζομαι ἠδʼ ἀλεείνω, 13.148; see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]); Zeus then gives his counsel, “this is what seems best to my heart” (13.154; see [[aristos]]). On Ithaca Athena says that she did not wish to fight with Poseidon, “who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart” (κότον ἔνθετο θυμῷ, 13.342; see [[kotos]]), though she herself “never doubted it, but in my heart / I knew that you would come home” (13.339–340; see [[nostos]]).

**The life.** In the lie the Cretan killed Orsilochus on a very dark night, “and no one / of men noticed us, and I took his life unseen” (λάθον δέ ἑ θυμὸν ἀπούρας, 13.269–270; see [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]] and [[ambush]]): θυμός is the life that a killing takes.

**Penelope's heart.** Athena tells Odysseus of his wife, “always mourning in her heart for your homecoming” (13.379; see [[penelope]]).
