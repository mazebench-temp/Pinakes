---
summary: The narrator's verses on the sleeping Odysseus, “who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.90–91), return in his false tale (13.263–264); Athena bids him suffer many sorrows in silence at home (13.310), and he asks whether Telemachus too was sent to suffer sorrows (13.418).
---

**The sleeper.** The Phaeacian ship carries “a man whose counsels were like the gods', / who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves; / but now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered” (13.89–92; see [[hypnos]] and [[polemos]]). The second of these verses recalls the proem, “and on the sea he suffered many sorrows in his heart” (1.4), and the third is the verse in which Odysseus told the Phaeacian Euryalus what he had endured (8.182–183) and in which Achilles, in the last book of the Iliad, remembers the sorrows he had suffered with Patroclus (*Iliad* 24.7–8). πτολέμους and κύματα carry on the construction of πολλὰ πάθʼ ἄλγεα, while πείρων, “passing through”, belongs only to the waves (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.91; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.91). The sorrows are set against the sleep: the last voyage, which ends them, is the one he does not see.

**The lie.** The Cretan of Odysseus' false tale claims the same verses for himself, for the spoil from Troy, “for which I suffered sorrows in my heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.263–264; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). What the narrator said of the true man, the false one says of himself.

**Sorrows at home.** Athena has come to tell him “all the sorrows it is your lot” to endure in his house (κήδεʼ, 13.306–307; see [[aisa]]), and she bids him “in silence / suffer many sorrows, submitting to the violence of men” (13.309–310; see [[silence]]). He answers that after Troy she never boarded his ship “to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.318–319; see [[athena]]), and when she says that she will call Telemachus home from Sparta, he asks, “Was it so that he too, wandering, might suffer sorrows / over the barren sea, while others devour his substance?” (13.418–419; see [[telemachus]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
