heading: The leave-taking
summary: The stay in the house of Alcinous ends. When the tale is over Alcinous asks the leaders for a tripod and a cauldron each (13.4–17); the next day the gifts are stowed, an ox is sacrificed to Zeus and Demodocus sings, while Odysseus watches the sun (13.18–35); at sunset he takes leave of the king and of the Phaeacians, and of Arete with a cup in her hand, and goes down to the ship (13.36–69). The promise of the seventh book is kept in its own words.

**The last gifts.** When the tale ends, Alcinous answers that the stranger, since he has come to his bronze-floored, high-roofed house, will not be beaten back and sail for home again (13.4–6; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[bronze-floored]]). He speaks to the leaders who drink the sparkling wine of the elders in his halls: the clothes, the intricately worked gold and the other gifts lie in the polished chest already (13.7–12; 8.389–441; see [[chest]]), “but come, let us give him a great tripod and a cauldron, / man by man”, and the people shall repay them (13.13–15; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]], [[hard-for-one-man-to-give]], [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]). Arete had asked for more gifts in the pause of the tale, and Alcinous had promised them (11.336–353). Eustathius knew ἠέ, “a tripod or a cauldron”, though he found ἠδέ in the better manuscripts, and a variant ἄνδρα κἀθʼ for ἀνδρακάς (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical notes on 13.13–14). τισόμεθα is “will repay ourselves”, and προικός “as a free gift”, without such repayment, used as an adverb like the Attic προῖκα, *gratis* (Monro on 13.15; see the translation's key-word row for ἀνδρακάς, προικός, τίνομαι). The leaders approve and go to bed, in the verse that ended the first evening (13.16–17; 7.229).

**The last day.** At dawn they hurry to the ship with the bronze, and “the sacred might of Alcinous” stows it himself under the thwarts, so that it should not hinder the rowers (13.18–22; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]); ὑπὸ ζυγά goes with κατέθηκε, “stowed under the thwarts”, and this is the morning of the thirty-fourth day of the action (Monro on 13.21, 13.18). Then he sacrifices an ox “to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all” (13.24–25; see [[sacrifice]] and [[lord-of-dark-clouds]]), the verse in which Odysseus burned the thighs of the Cyclops' ram to Zeus, who took no heed of the offering (9.552–555); ἱέρευσʼ ἱερὸν μένος, “the sacred might … sacrificed”, is one of the poem's plays on language (Monro on 13.24). Demodocus sings for the last time in the poem, “honored by the people” (13.27–28; see [[demodocus]]). Odysseus keeps turning his head toward the sun, δῦναι ἐπειγόμενος, “in haste, eager for (his) setting” (13.29–30; Monro on 13.30), like a plowman who longs for his supper at the end of the day (13.31–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]]).

**The farewell.** At sunset he speaks to the Phaeacians, and to Alcinous most of all, in the address with which he answered the king at the games and in the pause of his tale, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (13.38; 8.382, 11.355; see [[preeminent]] and [[powerful]]): “send me on my way unharmed, when you have poured libations”, for now “all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship” (13.39–41), the words in which Alcinous, stopping the song that made his guest weep, named what the Phaeacians had made ready for him (8.545; see [[pompe]]). He prays that he may find his wife at home and his dear ones unhurt, and that the gods may grant the Phaeacians good of every kind (13.42–46); ἀρετή is “good”, not restricted, as in later Greek, to merit or excellence (Monro on 13.45; see the translation's note on 13.45 and [[arete]]). They approve and bid him be sent home, “since he had spoken fair and right” (13.47–48 = 7.226–227). Alcinous bids Pontonous mix the wine for a prayer to Zeus and a libation, in the verses of the first evening (13.49–53, after 7.178–182; see [[pontonous]] and [[libation]]); the herald serves it ἐπισταδόν, going up to each in turn, and they pour αὐτόθεν ἐξ ἑδρέων, “from where they sat” (13.54–56; Monro on 13.54, 13.56).

**Arete.** Then Odysseus stands and puts the two-handled cup in Arete's hand (13.56–57; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[cup]]); some manuscripts have χερσί, “hands”, for χειρί (Monro's critical note on 13.57). His farewell to her, “Farewell to you, queen, continually, until old age / and death come, which are the lot of men” (13.59–60), sends her back to her house, “with your children and your people and King Alcinous” (13.61–62), παισί being instrumental with a partly “comitative” sense (Monro on 13.62). It was to the queen's knees that he came as a suppliant (7.139–152), and it is to her that he speaks last. He steps over the threshold that he crossed on the first evening (13.63; 7.135; see [[threshold]]); a herald leads him to the ship, and Arete sends three slave women with the mantle and tunic, the chest, and bread and red wine (13.64–69; see [[clothing]] and [[heralds]]).

**The promise kept.** Alcinous promised on the first evening that the stranger would lie overcome by sleep while his young men rowed him home (7.317–328), and the voyage is told in the words of the promise. The young men “tossed up the sea with the oar-blade”, as he boasted they could (13.78; 7.328); sleep falls upon Odysseus (13.79–80); and the crew lay him on the shore of Ithaca δεδμημένον ὕπνῳ, “overcome by sleep” (13.119; 7.318). For the voyage and the landing, see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]].
