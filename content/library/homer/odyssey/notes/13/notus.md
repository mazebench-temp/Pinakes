summary: The South Wind is named once, as a quarter of the sky: of the two doors of the cave of the Naiads, the one “toward the South Wind, is for gods; by that one / men do not enter, but it is the way of the immortals” (13.111–112).
# The entry's Νότου claims 13.111.
---

**The door of the gods.** The cave at the head of the harbor of Phorcys has two doors, “one toward the North Wind, by which men go down, / and the other, toward the South Wind, is for gods; by that one / men do not enter, but it is the way of the immortals” (13.110–112; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[boreas]] and [[olympians]]). θεώτεραι is not “more divine” but “divine”, the gods' door in contrast to the human one (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.111; see the translation's note on 13.111). The poem does not say that anyone uses it: Athena and Odysseus carry the gifts into the cave, and she sets a stone against “the doors” (13.366–371; see [[athena]]). Elsewhere in the poem the South Wind is weather, the wind that kept the ship a month on Thrinacia and carried Odysseus back to Charybdis (12.325–326, 12.427; see the Book 12 note).

**Later readers.** Strabo quotes the verses, with πρὸς νότον, among the proofs that Homer knew the quarters of the sky and the winds (*Geography* 1.2.20; see [[strabo]]). The Platonist Numenius, as Proclus reports him, read them as a witness to the two openings in the heavens by which souls descend into birth and ascend from it: the doors toward the South, through which men may not enter, are “the ways of immortals only”, since the sign of the winter turning leads souls upward, frees them from the life among men and admits only the immortal and divine (Proclus, *On the Republic* II 129 Kroll, a damaged passage; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and the Book 13 note on [[boreas]]). Proclus goes on to reject the theory as at odds with Plato's own words (*On the Republic* II 130 Kroll).
