---
summary: The only tears of the book are Penelope's, told by Athena: “always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (13.337–338), in verses that Aristarchus rejected.
---

Athena tells Odysseus that another man would have hurried home to his wife, but that he means first to make trial of her, “who sits, just as she was, / in your halls; and always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (13.336–338; see [[penelope]] and [[peira]]). The words are Anticleia's in the land of the dead, when her son asked her about his wife (11.181–183), and the swineherd will give them to Telemachus (16.37–39, a plain rendering): Penelope's weeping is something that each of them reports, in the same verses. Aristarchus rejected 13.333–338, the verses on the trial of the wife and her tears (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.333–338; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

Odysseus himself does not weep in the book. On Scheria he wept at the songs of Demodocus, hiding his face in his mantle (8.83–92, 8.521–531); waking on Ithaca in a land he does not know, he groans, strikes his thighs and laments, but no tear is told (13.197–221; see [[pity]] and [[lament]]).
