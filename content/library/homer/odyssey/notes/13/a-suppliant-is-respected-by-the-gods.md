**The god of suppliants.** In Book 5 Odysseus claimed from a river god the respect that even the immortals owe to “whoever of men comes as a wanderer” (5.447–450; see the entry), and on Scheria the rule became a libation to Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.165, 7.181; see the Book 7 note). On Ithaca, thinking that the Phaeacians have left him in a strange land, he calls the same god down on them: “May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over / other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong” (13.213–214; see [[zeus]] and [[supplication]]). ἱκετήσιος, “of suppliants”, is found only here in Homer, and ἐφορᾷ, “watches over”, is the verb of the suitor's warning that the gods go about the cities in the likeness of strangers, “watching over the arrogance and the lawfulness of men” (17.487, a plain rendering; see [[shape-changing]]). The curse is mistaken, for his hosts have kept faith.

**The wanderer and the god.** The next person he meets is a god in a stranger's likeness, and he comes to her as a suppliant: “My friend, since you are the first I find in this place, / greetings to you, and may you not meet me with evil intent, / but save these things, and save me; for to you / I pray as to a god, and I come to your dear knees” (13.228–231; see [[athena]] and [[gounata]]). The rule of Book 5 is kept to the letter. Athena saves both him and his goods: she tells him where he is, hides the gifts in the cave of the nymphs, and plans his return with him, for he has “come back from your wanderings” (13.237–249, 13.303–310, 13.361–373; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).
