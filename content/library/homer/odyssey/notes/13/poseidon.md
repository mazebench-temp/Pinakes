summary: Poseidon's last act in the poem. When the Phaeacians have set Odysseus ashore, the earth-shaker complains to Zeus that he will no longer be honored among the gods, since the Phaeacians, “sprung from my own stock”, have carried his enemy home with untold gifts; with Zeus' leave he turns their ship to stone within sight of their city (13.125–164). Alcinous recognizes his father's prophecy, and the Phaeacians pray to “lord Poseidon” around the altar with twelve bulls made ready (13.172–187). On Ithaca Athena tells Odysseus that she would not fight her father's brother, angry because Odysseus blinded his son (13.341–343).

**The complaint.** As the Phaeacians go home, “the earth-shaker / did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first, and he asked Zeus his plan” (13.125–127; see [[earth-shaker]], [[zeus]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). πρῶτον is “once” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.127). His grievance is honor: “Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods / be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all, / the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130; see [[time]] and [[phaeacians]]). τοί πέρ τοι is “who after all (τοι) are sprung from me” (Monro on 13.130); the vulgate has τε for the second τοι (Monro's critical note on 13.130). They are his own people: Nausithous, their founder, was his son by Periboea (7.56–62; see [[nausithous]]), and the gift of their ships was his (7.34–35; see the Book 7 note). He does not dispute the homecoming, which Zeus had promised (13.131–133), only the manner of it, asleep in a swift ship with more gifts than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy (13.134–138; see [[possessions]]).

**The eldest.** Zeus answers that the gods do not dishonor him, for “it would be hard / to assail the eldest and the best with acts of dishonor” (13.141–142; see the Book 13 note on [[zeus]]). In the Iliad Zeus is the elder of the two brothers: “Zeus was born first and knew more” (*Iliad* 13.355), Zeus sends word that he is “the elder by birth”, and Iris reminds Poseidon that “the Erinyes always follow the elder” (*Iliad* 15.165–166, 15.204; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]); here Zeus gives the first place to Poseidon (see the general article). The Iliad's Poseidon resents his brother's command as from an equal in honor, ὁμότιμος, and says that he will not live by the mind of Zeus, before he gives way (*Iliad* 15.185–199, 15.211). This one defers at once: “I would do it at once, lord of the dark clouds, as you say; / but I always have regard for your spirit, and avoid it” (13.147–148; see [[lord-of-dark-clouds]]). ὀπίζομαι, “have regard for”, is the verb, with ἐπί, of Hermes' warning to Calypso to beware the wrath of Zeus, Διὸς δʼ ἐποπίζεο μῆνιν (5.146). Monro contrasts the peaceful assemblies of the Odyssey's Olympus, where “There is now an Olympian concert that carries on something like a moral government of the world”, with the strife of the Iliad's gods (Monro, Appendix II §5; see [[olympians]]).

**The stone.** He would shatter the ship as it comes back from the escort, “so that at last they may hold back and cease from escorting / men, and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.151–152; see [[pompe]]). ἀμφικαλύψαι is rather far from ἐθέλω, and the scholia show an ancient variant, probably the future ἀμφικαλύψω (Monro on 13.152); some manuscripts read πόλιν for πόλει (Monro's critical note on 13.152). Zeus counsels stone instead of wreckage (13.154–158), and the god goes about it at once: “But when Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this, / he went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live. / There he waited; and the seafaring ship came very near, / coursing swiftly along; and the earth-shaker came near her, / and turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath / with a blow of the flat of his hand; and then he was gone” (13.159–164; see [[scheria]]). The first verse is the one in which he hears Hera's answer about Aeneas in the Iliad (*Iliad* 20.318); διωκομένη is “coursing along”, as in the phrase ἅρμα διώκειν, “drive a chariot” (Monro on 13.162); and χειρὶ καταπρηνεῖ, “with the flat of his hand”, is the stroke with which Apollo, standing behind Patroclus, struck his back and shoulders (*Iliad* 16.791–792; see [[patroclus]]). The Phaeacians on the shore ask one another “who has fettered the swift ship on the sea” (13.168); they do not know.

**The prophecy and the bulls.** Alcinous knows: his father Nausithous “used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.173–174, the verses of 8.565–566; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). ἀγάσασθαι is “had been surprised”, that is, offended, made jealous, and ἔφασκε, “alleged”, differs from φῆ, “said”, with which the prophecy itself begins (13.175); ἀγάασθαι, in some manuscripts, was probably an ancient variant, while the future ἀγάσσεσθαι of modern editions has little support (Monro on 13.173 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 13.173, where the English keeps the “begrudged” of 8.565). The Phaeacians give up their escorts and make twelve picked bulls ready: “So they prayed to lord Poseidon, / the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians, / standing around the altar” (13.181–187; see [[hecatomb]], [[prayer]] and [[altar]]). In the Odyssey Ποσειδάωνι ἄνακτι, “to lord Poseidon”, ends the verse of prayers and sacrifices to him: at Pylos (3.43, 3.54), in the Cyclops' prayer for vengeance (9.526), and in Teiresias' command to sacrifice to him at the end of the wanderings (11.130 = 23.277; see [[anax]]). The poem does not say whether the god accepted the bulls or covered the city.

**His father's brother.** On Ithaca Athena gives Odysseus the reason why she did not help him on the sea: “but I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who has laid up a grudge against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343; see [[athena]], [[kotos]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The end of the second verse and the whole of the third are Teiresias' words (11.102–103; see the Book 11 note); the manuscripts are divided between κότον, “grudge”, and χόλον, “anger” (Monro's critical note on 13.342). The narrator had given the same reason in Scheria, where Athena “respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330), and Apollo in the Iliad shrinks from coming to blows with him for the same cause (*Iliad* 21.468–469). The anger that Zeus foretold Poseidon would let go (1.76–79) has its last effect in this book, on the Phaeacians; after it he is named only in a simile, in the command of Teiresias that Odysseus repeats to Penelope, and in a question among the dead (23.234, 23.277, 24.109; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).
