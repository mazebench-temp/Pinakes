# Unnamed: Agamemnon's fate as Odysseus' example (13.383-384). Agamemnon is named at 13.383.

**Like Agamemnon.** Athena has told Odysseus of the suitors who for three years have been wooing his wife in his hall, and of Penelope, who gives hope to all while her mind has other things in view (13.375–381; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). He answers: “Ah, truly, like Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / I was about to perish by an evil fate in my halls, / had you not told me each thing, goddess, fair and right” (13.383–385; see [[agamemnon]]). The story has been told to his son, by Nestor and by Menelaus (3.254–312, 4.512–537), and Agamemnon's soul told it to Odysseus himself in the land of the dead, with the warning to bring his ship to shore secretly, since there is no trusting women any more (11.405–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). Now Odysseus applies it to himself. The danger he sees is not his wife: the dead king had excepted Penelope, “for she has great good sense” (11.444–446), and the goddess has just told him that she never ceases to mourn for his homecoming (13.379). It is the coming home unawares into a house full of enemies, as Agamemnon came to the feast where Aegisthus killed him “as one kills an ox at the manger” (11.409–411; 4.535 = 11.411). What saves Odysseus is what Agamemnon lacked, a warning, and the plan that follows it (13.386–396; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
