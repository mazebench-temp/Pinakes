**Like Agamemnon.** Sitting with Athena under the sacred olive, Odysseus hears for the first time what waits for him at home: the suitors, “who for three years now have been lording it in your hall, / wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (13.377–378; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). He answers:

The death of Agamemnon, killed at a feast in his own house on the day of his return, has been told and retold since the poem's first council of the gods: by Zeus, to set the guilt of Aegisthus against the gods' warning (1.35–43); by Athena in the likeness of Mentor, who would rather come home after many sorrows “than come home and perish at my own hearth, as Agamemnon / perished by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.232–235); by Proteus to Menelaus (4.512–537); and by the soul of Agamemnon to Odysseus himself (11.405–434; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[clytemnestra]]). Here, for the first time, Odysseus sets it beside his own homecoming. κακὸν οἶτον, “an evil fate”, is the phrase of the Achaeans' returns, the “evil fate of the Danaans” of Phemius' song and the evil fate that, Nestor says, many of them met (1.350, 3.134; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**The advice taken.** Among the dead Agamemnon had told him to come home “secretly, and not openly, to your dear native land / bring your ship to shore; for there is no trusting women any more” (11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). He comes so: asleep, in a ship that leaves at once, wrapped in a mist that keeps him unknown “lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends” (13.190–193), and then changed by Athena into an old beggar whom no one will know (13.397–403; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[odysseus]]). But in this book the danger in the house is the suitors, and Athena speaks of his wife only to praise her (13.379–381).
