---
summary: Horace's Ulysses asks Tiresias how to repair his fortune, for he comes home “naked and destitute”, the opposite of the Odysseus whom the Phaeacians send home with more treasure than the spoil of Troy. Monro explains the epithet of the islands in Odysseus' question, εὐδείελος, by Horace's “shining Cyclades”.
---

- **Naked and destitute.** The fifth satire of the second book continues the consultation of the dead. “Answer me this too, Tiresias, besides what you have told: by what arts and ways can I recover my lost property? Why do you laugh?” “Is it not enough now for the trickster to be carried back to Ithaca and to see his ancestral household gods?” “O you who have never lied to anyone, you see how, as you foretold, I come home naked and destitute, and there neither the wine store nor the herd is left untouched by the suitors; and yet birth and worth, without wealth, are cheaper than seaweed” (*nudus inopsque domum redeam te vate*, *Satires* 2.5.1–8, plain renderings). Tiresias' prophecy in the Odyssey is that Odysseus will come home “late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house” (11.114–115). The ship is the Phaeacians', and in this book they send him home with bronze and gold and woven clothing, “more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.135–138). He counts them on waking and finds nothing missing (13.215–219), and Athena hides them in the cave of the nymphs (13.361–371; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). Horace's Ulysses, who goes on to learn the art of hunting legacies, comes home with nothing at all: [[horace-on-the-suitors]], [[teiresias]] and [[possessions]].
- **The shining islands.** Odysseus asks the disguised Athena whether this land is “one of the clear-seen islands”, νήσων εὐδείελος (13.234). Monro renders the epithet “shining”, citing Horace's ode to a ship, which he bids avoid the waters poured between “the shining Cyclades” (*interfusa nitentis / vites aequora Cycladas*, *Odes* 1.14.19–20, a plain rendering; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.234): [[clear-seen]].
