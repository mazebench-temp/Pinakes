summary: Poseidon complains to Zeus that he will no longer be honored among the gods when the Phaeacians, his own stock, carry his enemy home with untold gifts (13.128–138), and Zeus lets him take his vengeance on their ship; Alcinous recognizes his father's prophecy that Poseidon “begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.172–178).

**The god's honor.** The grudge foretold in Book 8 is told now from the god's side. Poseidon “did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first” (13.125–127; see [[poseidon]]), and he puts his case to Zeus: “Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods / be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all, / the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130). τοι is “after all” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.130): his own descendants have slighted him (see [[phaeacians]] and [[nausithous]]). He did not take Odysseus' homecoming from him altogether, since Zeus had promised it, but the Phaeacians have carried him home asleep and given him more than he would ever have carried off from Troy (13.131–138; see [[gold-and-silver]]). What Poseidon begrudges is the gift, and the safe escort that made it possible. Zeus answers in the verse with which he answered Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall, “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!” (13.140 = *Iliad* 7.455), and in the Iliad too he let the god destroy the mortals' work (*Iliad* 7.459–463). “The gods do not dishonor you at all” (13.141), he says, and if a mortal fails to honor him, “vengeance is always yours, even hereafter” (13.144): there is a play of words between τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of vengeance (Monro on 13.144; see [[time]]).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** When the ship has been turned to stone within sight of the city, Alcinous knows what has happened: “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me, / the oracles of my father, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.172–174; see [[thesphaton]]). The first verse is the Cyclops' when he learns that the man who blinded him is Odysseus, as the seer Telemus had foretold (9.507): both prophecies concern Poseidon's own offspring, and both are fulfilled through Odysseus. ἀγάσασθαι is “had been surprised”, that is, “offended”, “made jealous”, and Monro distinguishes ἔφασκε, “alleged”, from φῆ, “said”: the prophecy itself begins at φῆ (on 13.173). In Book 8 Alcinous left the outcome to the god, “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” (8.570–571; see the Book 8 note); now “it is all being fulfilled” (13.178). The Phaeacians give up the escort that the god begrudged them and pray to him for pity (13.179–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[pompe]] and [[pity]]).
