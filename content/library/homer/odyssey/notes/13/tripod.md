summary: After the tale Alcinous bids each of the Phaeacian lords give the stranger a great tripod and a cauldron, to be made good by a levy on the people (13.13–15); the bronze goes to the ship at dawn, and on the shore of Ithaca Odysseus counts “the beautiful tripods and cauldrons” and finds nothing missing (13.19–22, 13.217–219).

**The last gift.** When the tale is over, Alcinous turns to the lords who drink “the sparkling wine of the elders” in his halls (13.7–9; see [[alcinous]] and [[wine]]). The clothes, the gold and the other gifts already lie in the chest (13.10–12; see [[chest]]), “but come, let us give him a great tripod and a cauldron, / man by man; and we in turn will gather it among the people / and repay ourselves; for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift” (13.13–15; see [[cauldron]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). τισόμεθα is “will repay ourselves”, and προικός “as a free gift”, without such repayment: originally a partitive genitive, “of his bounty”, used as an adverb like the Attic προῖκα, *gratis* (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.15; see the translation's row ἀνδρακάς; προικός; τίνομαι). The kings of the Phaeacians had given a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold each, twelve of them with Alcinous the thirteenth (8.390–393; see [[kings]] and [[talent]]); now each adds a tripod, the great treasure of the Iliad's gift lists and prizes (see the entry). Eustathius knew the reading ἠέ, “a tripod or a cauldron”, though he found ἠδέ in the better manuscripts, and a variant ἄνδρα κἀθʼ for ἀνδρακάς (Monro's critical notes on 13.13–14).

**To the ship.** At dawn the lords “hurried to the ship, and brought the bronze that makes men strong”, and Alcinous himself stowed it under the thwarts, where it would not hinder the rowers (13.18–22; see [[bronze]], [[rowing-benches]] and [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]). εὐήνωρ, “that makes men strong”, is said of wine at 4.622 (see the translation's row εὐήνωρ).

**Counted on the shore.** Waking on Ithaca and not knowing the land, Odysseus asks “Where am I taking all these goods?” (13.203), and ends his lament with them: “But come, let me count my goods and look at them, / to see whether they have gone off with anything in the hollow ship” (13.215–216; see [[possessions]]). μὴ οἴχωνται is “to see whether they have not gone” (Monro on 13.216). “So saying, he counted the beautiful tripods and cauldrons / and the gold and the fine woven clothes. / Of these he missed nothing; but he mourned for his native land” (13.217–219; see [[clothing]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). With the gold and the clothes the tripods are “the unyielding bronze” that he carries into the cave of the nymphs, where Athena hides them (13.367–371; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).
