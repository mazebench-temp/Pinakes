---
summary: The winged words of the farewell and of the meeting on the shore, once followed by a lie (13.253–254); the word that Odysseus speaks in lament on waking (13.199); and the words with which Athena encouraged him in Scheria (13.323).
---

**Winged words and a lie.** The formula “and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words” introduces Odysseus' farewell to Arete and his speeches to the disguised Athena (ἔπεα πτερόεντα, 13.58, 13.227; of Athena, revealed, 13.290; see [[winged-words]]), and the Phaeacians, seeing their ship turned to stone, “spoke winged words to one another” (13.165). Once the formula is followed by a warning: “and he spoke to her, addressing her with winged words: / yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word” (13.253–254; see [[mythos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). What follows is the tale of the Cretan exile (see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**A word of lament.** On waking in a land he does not know, Odysseus “groaned aloud then, and struck both his thighs / with the flat of his hands, and lamenting he spoke a word” (ὀλοφυρόμενος δʼ ἔπος ηὔδα, 13.198–199; see [[lament]]): the verses of Patroclus in *Iliad* 15, when he sees from Eurypylus' hut that the Trojans have broken through to the ships (*Iliad* 15.396–398; see [[patroclus]]).

**Words of encouragement.** Odysseus reminds Athena that in Scheria “you encouraged me with words and led me into the city yourself” (θάρσυνάς τε ἔπεσσι, 13.323; see [[tharsos]]), one of four verses that are generally condemned (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.320–323; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.320–323).
