summary: The birds of Book 13 are the hawk and the falcon by which the poet measures the speed of the Phaeacian ship, “the nimblest of winged birds” (13.86–87), and the raven in the name of the Rock of Corax (13.408).
# πετεηνῶν (13.87), "winged birds", claimed here for the Odyssey, where it is found only in this verse; the Iliad's are
# in its notes. πετεηνά (16.218) is another form.

**Winged birds.** ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, “the nimblest of winged birds”, is said of the falcon that could not have kept pace with the ship that carried Odysseus home (13.87; see [[falcon]], [[hawk]] and [[the-four-stallions]]). πετεηνά, “winged things”, from πέτομαι, “fly”, is the word of the Iliad's superlatives of birds: the falcon is the nimblest of winged birds, the hawk the swiftest, the eagle “at once the strongest and the swiftest”, “surest in omen”, and the one with “the sharpest sight” (*Iliad* 22.139, 15.238, 21.253, 8.247, 17.675; see [[like-a-hawk]], [[surest-of-birds]] and [[eagle]]). In the Odyssey the word comes again only once, of the young of sea eagles and vultures, which countrymen take from the nest “before they could fly” (16.216–218, a plain rendering).

**The Raven's rock.** Athena tells Odysseus that he will find the swineherd's swine feeding “by the Rock of Corax and at the spring Arethusa” (13.408; see [[the-rock-of-corax]] and [[arethusa]]). κόραξ is the raven, which is not named anywhere else in Homer, and W. W. Merry calls the place the “Raven's rock” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 556); the text prints the word as a name.
