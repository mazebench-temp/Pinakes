**The oracles come home.** The Phaeacians see their ship stopped on the sea “as she was driven home”, and “they did not know how things had been brought about” (13.168–170; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[phaeacians]] and [[poseidon]]). Alcinous knows:

{{quote:6d52491b-74a4-530d-9b05-4069e6cf4175}}

He had told the same prophecy to his guest at the end of the day of the games, naming his father: πατρὸς … Ναυσιθόου, “my father, Nausithous” (8.564–565); now πατρὸς ἐμοῦ, “of my father”, takes the place of the name (13.173). The ship, ἐυεργέα, “well-made”, in Book 8 (8.567), is περικαλλέα, “beautiful”, here, as Poseidon called it to Zeus (13.149), though three manuscripts have εὐεργέα here too (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.175). And the end has changed. Then it was “and these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.570–571); now it is “and now it is all being fulfilled” (13.178), the verse-end with which Halitherses declared his old prophecy of Odysseus' return in the course of fulfilment, and Odysseus on his raft feared that Calypso's warning was coming true (2.176, 5.302; see [[thesphaton]] and [[prophecy]]). The first verse is the Cyclops' when he hears the name of Odysseus and remembers the seer Telemus (9.507; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]): two of Poseidon's line meet their old prophecy in the same words.

**The god's grudge.** ἀγάσασθαι, “begrudged”, is “had been surprised”, that is, offended, made jealous; ἔφασκε, “used to say”, “alleged”, is to be distinguished from φῆ, “said”, with which the prophecy itself begins, and with it the need for a future infinitive. A few manuscripts have ἀγάασθαι, probably an ancient variant, and the present is perhaps the more suitable tense, as not implying that the god's jealousy had reached its height (Monro on 13.173 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 13.173). The reason is the one Nausithous gave: “because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.174 = 8.566; see [[pompe]]). His son answers it by ending the escorts and offering Poseidon twelve bulls (13.179–187; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).
