---
summary: When Poseidon has turned their ship to stone, Alcinous bids the Phaeacians sacrifice twelve picked bulls to him, “in the hope that he may have pity”; they make the bulls ready and stand praying around the altar, and there the poem leaves them (13.179–187).
passages:
  - 13.171-187 | Twelve bulls for Poseidon
---

**Twelve picked bulls.** In the stone ship Alcinous sees the prophecy of his father fulfilled, that Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship on its way back from an escort and cover their city with a great mountain (13.172–178; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[nausithous]]). He bids his people give up their escorts, “and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity / and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain” (13.181–183; see [[poseidon]]). The bull is the Odyssey's victim for Poseidon: the Pylians sacrifice bulls all black to him on the shore, Nestor's companions burned the thighs of bulls for him at Geraestus, and Teiresias bids Odysseus appease him with a ram, a bull and a boar (3.5–8, 3.178–179, 11.130–131; see the Book 3 and Book 11 notes). κεκριμένους, “picked”, chosen out of the herd, is said of victims only here; elsewhere in the Odyssey the picked are young men, the fifty-two suitors from Dulichium (16.247–248) and the dead suitors in the underworld, “all picked men and of the same age” (24.107, a plain rendering). The words ἱερεύσομεν, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ, “let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity”, are those of the Trojan women's prayer to Athena, to whom they vow twelve cows “if you will pity / the city” (ἱερεύσομεν, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃς, *Iliad* 6.308–310), and Athena refused it (*Iliad* 6.311; see the Book 13 note on [[hecatomb]]).

**Honor for the god.** The sacrifice answers Poseidon's complaint. He had told Zeus that he would no longer be held in honor among the gods, since mortals did not honor him at all, the Phaeacians, “who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130), and Zeus had answered that if any man did not honor him, vengeance was always his (13.143–144; see [[zeus]] and [[poine]]), with a play of words between τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of “vengeance” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.144). The bulls are the honor that mortals pay a god (see [[sacrifice]] and [[time]]). The Phaeacians' last day with Odysseus had begun with an ox sacrificed to Zeus at the farewell feast (13.24–25; see the Book 13 note on [[cattle]]); the next day, with their ship turned to stone, they make bulls ready for Poseidon in fear.

**At the altar.** “So he spoke, and they were afraid, and made the bulls ready. / So they prayed to lord Poseidon, / the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians, / standing around the altar” (13.184–187; see [[prayer]], [[altar]] and [[propitiation]]). ἑτοιμάσσαντο, “made ready”, is all that is told of the sacrifice. The leaders stand around the altar, περὶ βωμόν, as the victims of the hecatomb at Chryse were set in order around the well-built altar (*Iliad* 1.448) and as sacrificers stand around the victim (*Iliad* 2.410; 12.356). In the middle of the verse the poem turns away to Odysseus waking in Ithaca, ὁ δʼ ἔγρετο δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς (13.187), and the Phaeacians are not seen again. Whether the god took the bulls and spared the city is not told (see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).
