---
summary: Earth in Book 13 is above all the land Odysseus comes home to. He wakes “in the land of his fathers” and does not know it, asks “to the land of what mortals” he has come, and mourns for his native land (13.187–219); when the mist is scattered and “the land came into view”, he kisses the grain-giving earth (13.352–354). Zeus would set the stone ship “near the land” (13.156), and Athena foretells that the suitors' blood will spatter the floor and that “the earth will hold fast” some of them (13.395, 13.427).
---

**The native land.** The book's γαῖα is most often πατρὶς γαῖα, the native land: Alcinous will send the stranger “to his native land” (13.52); Odysseus wakes “in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it—”, looks at “his native land” and mourns for it (13.188, 13.197, 13.219), and rejoices in “his own land, the land of his fathers” when Athena names it (13.251); and the suitors mean to kill Telemachus “before he reaches his native land” (13.426, as 4.823; see [[dear-native-land]], [[ithaca]] and [[ambush]]). γαίῃ πατρωΐῃ, “the land of his fathers”, is the adjective of inheritance, “of one's fathers”.

**Another land.** Not knowing his own island, he asks the question of the shore of Scheria, “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?” (13.200 = 6.119; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]), complains that the Phaeacians have brought him “to another land” (13.211), and tells the goddess that he thinks he is roaming “some other / land” (13.325–326). The herdsman answers that “this land” is not nameless (13.238), and the goddess mocks him for his lies “even in your own land” (13.294).

**The grain-giving earth.** “So saying, the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view; / and then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / rejoicing in his own land, and kissed the grain-giving earth” (13.352–354; see [[grain-giving]]). χθών, the land that comes into view, and ζείδωρος ἄρουρα, the grain-giving earth that he kisses, are the island itself; he kissed the earth of Scheria in the same words when he came out of the river (5.463).

**Near the land.** Zeus advises Poseidon “to turn her to stone near the land” (θεῖναι λίθον ἐγγύθι γαίης, 13.156; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]), so that the stone ship stands by the shore of Scheria in sight of the city.

**The floor and the grave.** Athena promises that when the fight comes, “one or another / will spatter the boundless floor with blood and brains / among the suitors” (13.394–396; see [[the-suitors]]). ἄσπετον οὖδας, “the boundless floor”, here the floor of the hall, is in the Iliad the ground that dying men bite with their teeth (*Iliad* 19.61, and without the epithet 11.749; see the translation's key-word row). And of the suitors' ambush she says, “before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors” (13.427–428): γαῖα καθέξει has the verb in which the Iliad says of Helen's brothers that “the life-giving earth already held them fast” (*Iliad* 3.243).
