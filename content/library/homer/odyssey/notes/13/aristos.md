---
summary: Zeus calls Poseidon “the eldest and the best” of the gods (13.142), and Athena calls Odysseus “by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words” (13.297–298).
---

**The eldest and the best.** Zeus answers Poseidon's complaint: “The gods do not dishonor you at all; it would be hard / to assail the eldest and the best with acts of dishonor” (πρεσβύτατον καὶ ἄριστον ἀτιμίῃσιν ἰάλλειν, 13.141–142; see [[poseidon]], [[zeus]] and [[time]]). In the Iliad the elder of the two brothers is Zeus, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (*Iliad* 13.355), and Zeus calls himself “the elder by birth” (*Iliad* 15.166); here he gives the title to Poseidon. When he goes on to his counsel, the superlative is his own judgment: “My friend, this is what seems best to my heart” (ὡς μὲν ἐμῷ θυμῷ δοκεῖ εἶναι ἄριστα, 13.154). The infinitives that follow, to turn the ship to stone and to cover the city with a mountain, explain what seems best, so that the clause with ὡς μέν has no apodosis (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.154–158).

**The best of mortals.** Athena, revealed, sets Odysseus beside herself: “for you are by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words, and I among all the gods / am famed for cunning and crafty ways” (βροτῶν ὄχʼ ἄριστος ἁπάντων / βουλῇ καὶ μύθοισιν, 13.297–299; see [[metis]], [[kerdos]] and [[boule]]). ὄχʼ ἄριστος, “by far the best”, is the Iliad's praise of a man at the head of his kind, as Calchas is “by far the best of the bird-seers” (*Iliad* 1.69); here the measure is counsel and speech, not war (see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). The scene ends with the same words, “and let us ourselves consider how all may turn out best by far” (ὄχʼ ἄριστα, 13.365).
