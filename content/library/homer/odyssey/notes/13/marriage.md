summary: Odysseus' farewell asks for himself a noble wife at home and for the Phaeacians the joy of their wedded wives (13.42–45); on Ithaca Athena hides him so that his wife may not know him first (13.192), and tells him that he means to make trial of his wife before he goes home to her (13.333–336), while the suitors woo her (13.378).
# κουριδίας (13.45), only here. ἀλόχου (13.336; also 11.67, 14.202, 23.165, 23.346), the bare genitive: always "wife".
grc: [κουριδίας, ἀλόχου]
---

**The farewell.** Odysseus' last wishes in the house of Alcinous are for wives. For himself: “and may I find my noble wife at home / when I return, and my dear ones unhurt” (13.42–43; see [[penelope]] and [[nostos]]); for his hosts: “And may you, staying here, gladden your wives, / your wedded wives, and your children” (13.44–45). κουριδίας is the word of the honorable bond of husband and wife (see the Book 11 note). The poem began with him “wanting his homecoming and his wife”, held by a nymph “longing for him to be her husband” (1.13–15; see [[calypso]]); he leaves Scheria wishing his hosts the marriage he is going back to (see [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**Hidden from his wife.** On Ithaca Athena pours a mist around him “lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends, / before the suitors had paid for all their transgression” (13.192–193; see [[the-suitors]]). When he begs her to tell him whether this is his own land, she answers: “For gladly would another man, coming home from his wanderings, / have been eager to see his children and his wife in his halls; / but it is not yet your pleasure to learn or to hear, / before you have made trial of your wife yourself” (13.333–336). πειρήσεαι is “observe for yourself”, not trusting to report, “while any other man would have made for his home without even enquiring” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.336; see [[peira]]). Aristarchus rejected the six verses on the trial of the wife, 13.333–338 (Monro's critical note; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). Then Athena tells him of the suitors “wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (13.378; see [[bride-price]]), and makes him an old beggar, unsightly “to all the suitors / and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls” (13.402–403; see [[shape-changing]]).
