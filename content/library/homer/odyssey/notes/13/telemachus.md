**The prayer for the son.** When the mist is gone and Odysseus knows his land, he prays to the Naiads: “and we will give you gifts as well, as before, / if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants / that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow” (13.358–360; see [[nymphs]], [[driver-of-spoil]] and [[odysseus]]). In διδώσομεν, “we will give”, Odysseus associates Telemachus with himself, as the last verse shows, and ἀέξῃ is an anacoluthon (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.358, 13.360). The child at the breast whom he left behind (11.448–449) is a young man now, as Athena's news will show.

**Where he is.** Athena will go “to Sparta of the lovely women / to call Telemachus, your dear son, Odysseus, / who went to Lacedaemon with its wide dancing floors, to Menelaus, / to learn the report of you, whether you were still alive anywhere” (13.412–415; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]], [[sparta]], [[lacedaemon]] and [[menelaus]]). Odysseus answers as a father: “Why then did you not tell him, you who know all things in your mind? / Was it so that he too, wandering, might suffer sorrows / over the barren sea, while others devour his substance?” (13.417–419), ἔδουσι being “while others devour” (Monro on 13.419). The goddess answers that he need not be on his mind: “I myself escorted him, so that he might win noble fame / by going there; and he has no toil, but sits at his ease / in the house of the son of Atreus, and untold plenty lies beside him” (13.422–424; see [[kleos]] and [[pompe]]). It is the purpose she gave the journey at the first council of the gods, and the poet gives again when she puts daring in his mind at Pylos, “so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.95, 3.78), and κλέος ἐσθλὸν ἄροιτο is the Iliad's phrase for the fame she gave Diomedes, “so that he would stand out among all / the Argives and win noble fame” (*Iliad* 5.2–3; see [[diomedes]]).

**The ambush.** “Truly young men in a black ship lie in ambush for him, / eager to kill him before he reaches his native land; / but I do not think it will be so; before that the earth will hold fast some / of the suitors, who are devouring your substance” (13.425–428; see [[ambush]] and [[the-suitors]]). The ambush was laid in Book 4, at the island of Asteris (4.842–847), and the second verse is Penelope's fear (4.823); Athena gives Telemachus the same warning and the same assurance at Sparta (15.28–32). The last verse is omitted in one manuscript and by Eustathius (Monro's critical note on 13.428).

**Unknown to his son.** Athena will make Odysseus unsightly to the suitors “and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls” (13.403; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]), and the swineherd to whom she sends him “loves your son and sensible Penelope” (13.406; see [[eumaeus]]). The book ends with the goddess gone “to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (13.440; see [[athena]]). Aristotle, asking why Odysseus made himself known to his young son before his wife, answered that Telemachus could master his feelings, while Penelope was used only to weeping (see [[odysseus]]).
