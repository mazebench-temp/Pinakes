---
summary: Alcinous stows the gifts where they will not hinder the rowers (13.21–22); the Phaeacian crew lean back and toss up the sea with the oar-blade as Odysseus falls asleep (13.76–80), and row the ship so hard into the harbor of Phorcys that she runs half her length up the shore (13.113–115). In his false tale Odysseus' Phoenicians row into the harbor with difficulty (13.279).
# πηδῷ, "with the oar-blade" (13.78, in the verse of 7.328, the only other place in Homer), first met here; it applies in
# the Odyssey only.
grc: [πηδῷ]
en: [oar-blade]
---

**The rowers' room.** Alcinous stows the bronze himself “under the thwarts, going himself through the ship, so that it might not / hinder any of the companions as they rowed, whenever they were in haste with their oars” (13.21–22; see [[rowing-benches]], [[tripod]] and [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]). For ἐρετμοῖς, “with their oars”, Monro suggests ἐρετμῷ, the singular, as πηδῷ is used at 13.78 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.22).

**The voyage.** The crew sit down at the benches and loose the cable, “When they leaned back and tossed up the sea with the oar-blade, / then sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids” (13.76–79; see [[stern-cables]], [[sweet-sleep]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). ἀναρρίπτειν ἅλα πηδῷ is the phrase of Alcinous' boast on the night the stranger came, that he would see “how far the best / are my ships, and my young men at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.327–328; see the translation's row πηδόν, ἀναρρίπτω), and here the boast is made good. The Phaeacians are “lovers of the oar” in the verse that introduces Odysseus' farewell, and “the Phaeacians of the long oars” when their ship has been turned to stone (13.36, 13.166; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]] and [[long-oared]]).

**Into the harbor.** “There they rowed in, knowing the place before; and the ship / ran up on the land as much as half her length, / in her haste; so hard was she driven by the hands of the rowers” (13.113–115; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[ships]]). ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ ἥμισυ is “to half its length” (Monro on 13.114; see the translation's row ἐπικέλλω, of a ship).

**In the false tale.** In the story he tells Athena, the Phoenicians who were to take him to Pylos were driven off their course by the wind, and “With difficulty we rowed forward into the harbor”; they all lay down on the shore without a thought of supper (13.276–281; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[phoenicians]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). προερέσσαμεν is the reading of Aristarchus, which Didymus says all the editions had, and of some manuscripts; others have προερύσσαμεν, from προερύω, the verb of drawing a ship forward (Monro's critical note on 13.279; *Iliad* 1.308).
