**The goddess in two shapes.** While Odysseus laments on the shore, “Athena came near to him, / in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep, / very delicate, such as are the sons of lords, / with a well-made cape folded double around her shoulders; / and under her shining feet she had sandals, and in her hands a javelin” (13.221–225; see [[athena]]). It is the likeness in which Hermes met him on Circe's island, “a young man” (10.278; see the Book 10 note). Odysseus greets the shepherd as a suppliant and tells him a false tale (13.226–286; see [[supplication]] and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]); then the goddess smiles, strokes him with her hand, “and she was like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.287–289), the shape in which she will appear to him at the swineherd's hut (16.157–158; see [[weaving]]). Some manuscripts omit 13.289 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.289). Odysseus' answer gives the rule: “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]).

**The land in another shape.** The first change in Ithaca is in what Odysseus sees. Athena has poured a mist around him as he slept, “so that she might make him / unknown, and tell him each thing” (13.189–191), and when he wakes, “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (13.194–196; see [[ithaca]]). For ἀλλοειδέα the manuscripts are divided between φαίνετο and φαινέσκετο, and Monro prints ἀλλοειδέʼ ἐφαίνετο (Monro on 13.194; see the translation's note on 13.194). The mist was not meant to make him invisible, Monro explains: Athena wished to prepare him by consultation and by changing his appearance, as she does at 13.429–438, for ἄγνωστον τεύξειεν, “make him unknown”, means what ἄγνωστον τεύξω means at 13.397; “If Athene had not taken these measures, Ulysses would have gone straight to his palace, and all would have been lost” (on 13.191). When she has told him what he must know, “the goddess scattered the mist, and the land came into view” (13.352).

**The old beggar.** Athena tells him what she will do: “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals; / I will wither the fair skin on your supple limbs, / and destroy the fair hair from your head” and dim his eyes, “so that you may seem unsightly to all the suitors / and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls” (13.397–403; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). The wand is the instrument of Circe, who struck the companions with hers and made them swine (10.238; see [[wand]] and [[circe]]); with a golden wand Athena will give Odysseus back his youth for his son, and with her wand make him old again (16.172–176, 16.454–459). The rag, the deerskin, the staff and the pouch are the outfit of the beggar he is to be in his own house (see [[rags]], [[clothing]] and [[scepter]]). Monro notes that Odysseus is here supposed to be ξανθός, “fair”, but compares 16.176, where his beard grows dark again, and 6.231 (on 13.431; see the translation's note on 6.231); ἄλλο is “other than his own”, and ψιλόν “bare, the wool worn off” (on 13.434, 13.437). He counts the change among the incidents of the fairy-tale order in the story of the return: on the landing in Ithaca, “as Kirchhoff has rightly insisted, the change wrought is a magical one,—not a mere illusion”. Kirchhoff, as Monro reports him, held that it was inserted to join two poems, since in the later recognitions Odysseus is only disguised by his rags; Monro answers that this is “an example of the practical difficulty of dealing with supernatural machinery in a logical and consistent way. The poet has made a somewhat excessive use of the marvellous, and afterwards returns unconsciously to a more natural point of view” (Monro, Appendix I §6). Since Odysseus would otherwise have run the risk of being recognized, the transformation, “or some equivalent means of concealment”, was in Monro's view “a poetical necessity”.
