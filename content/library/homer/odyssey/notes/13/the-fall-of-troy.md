---
summary: Odysseus remembers the sack twice in his talk with Athena on Ithaca: after “we had sacked the steep city of Priam” he saw her no more (13.316–319), and he asks her to stand beside him against the suitors with the daring she gave him “when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.387–388).
# Unnamed: the sack of Priam's city (13.316); the loosing of the veil of Troy (13.388).
refs: [13.316, 13.388]
---

**The steep city of Priam.** Odysseus tells the goddess that she was kind to him while the sons of the Achaeans fought at Troy, “But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans, / after that I did not see you” (13.314–318; see [[priam]], [[steep]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The first two verses are Nestor's, who told Telemachus the returns in them (3.130–131).

**The veil of Troy.** When she has told him of the suitors, he asks her to stand beside him, “putting in me a might full of daring, / as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.387–388; see [[full-of-daring]] and [[veil]]). κρήδεμνα is “the diadem of towers”, from Achilles' wish to Patroclus that the two of them alone might survive the war, “so that we alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy” (*Iliad* 16.100), a picturesque refinement, Monro says, on the more ordinary metaphor by which the battlements of a town are called its head (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.388; see the translation's note on 13.388 and [[battlements]]). Achilles did not live to loose it; Odysseus did, and the poem has shown him doing it with Athena beside him. In Demodocus' song he went to the house of Deiphobus like Ares, dared the most dread fight there “and conquered in the end, through great-hearted Athena” (8.517–520; see [[the-wooden-horse]] and [[athena]]), the phrase in which the Phaeacians give him their gifts “through great-hearted Athena” in this book (13.121). Now he asks for the same help in his own house: with her beside him he would fight three hundred men (13.389–391; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).
