# Unnamed: the end of the escorts and the twelve bulls, the fear and the prayer (13.180-187).
refs: [13.180-187]
---

**Appeasing Poseidon.** Alcinous sees in the stone ship his father's prophecy, that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians their safe escort of all men (13.172–178; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]), and his proposal gives the god both of the things that appease an angry power. The offense stops: “stop escorting mortals, whenever anyone comes / to our city” (13.180–181; see [[pompe]]). And the god has his offering: “and to Poseidon twelve picked bulls / let us sacrifice, in the hope that he may have pity / and not cover our city round about with a towering mountain” (13.181–183; see [[sacrifice]] and [[hecatomb]]). So in the Iliad Calchas told the Achaeans that Apollo would not end the plague until the girl was given back to her father and a holy hecatomb led to Chryse, “Then we might appease him and win him over” (*Iliad* 1.97–100; see the Iliad Book 1 note).

**Prayers without an answer.** “So he spoke, and they were afraid, and made the bulls ready. / So they prayed to lord Poseidon, / the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians, / standing around the altar” (13.184–187; see [[prayer]]). The poem leaves them there. Their hope is in the words of the Trojan women's vow to Athena, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ, “if she will pity”, and that appeasing failed (*Iliad* 6.93–95, 6.311; see the Iliad Book 6 note and [[pity]]). Aristophanes read μηδέ for μέγα δέ in Zeus' advice, so that Zeus would forbid the mountain, “no doubt in view of the prayers of the Phaeacian elders (l. 183). But these need not affect what Zeus says now” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.158; see the translation's note on 13.158). Odysseus himself has still to appease the god: Teiresias bade him, when the suitors are dead, carry an oar inland and there “make fine sacrifices to lord Poseidon” (11.121–131; see [[poseidon]]).
