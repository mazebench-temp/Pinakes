summary: The book completes the gifts and brings them home. Alcinous asks a great tripod and a cauldron of each lord (13.7–15) and stows the bronze in the ship himself (13.18–22); the sailors set the gifts by the olive on Ithaca (13.117–124); Poseidon names them in his complaint (13.135–138); and Odysseus counts them, puts them into his false tale, and with Athena hides them in the cave of the Naiads (13.203–219, 13.257–258, 13.283–284, 13.303–305, 13.361–371).

**The last gift.** After the tale Alcinous reminds the lords that the clothes, the gold and the other gifts already lie in the chest, and asks each of them for a great tripod and a cauldron besides, “and we in turn will gather it among the people / and repay ourselves; for it is hard for one man to give as a free gift” (13.10–15; see [[alcinous]], [[tripod]], [[cauldron]], [[chest]] and [[hard-for-one-man-to-give]]). At dawn they bring “the bronze that makes men strong” to the ship, and Alcinous stows it under the thwarts himself (13.18–22; see [[bronze]] and [[rowing-benches]]). In his farewell Odysseus counts the gifts among the things his heart desired: “an escort and gifts of friendship; and may the heavenly gods / make them prosper for me” (13.41–42; see [[pompe]] and [[doron]]). A slave woman of Arete carries the chest to the ship (13.68).

**Ashore.** On Ithaca the sailors lift out “the possessions which the noble Phaeacians / had given him as he went home, through great-hearted Athena”, and set them by the trunk of the olive, away from the road (13.120–124; see [[possessions]] and [[olive]]). Poseidon, complaining to Zeus that the Phaeacians honor him no longer, names them: they “gave him untold gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.135–137; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]), in the verses of Zeus' promise (5.38–40).

**Counted.** Odysseus wakes in a land he does not know and asks where he is to take “all these goods”; he will not leave them where they may “become a prey for others”, and he counts “the beautiful tripods and cauldrons / and the gold and the fine woven clothes”, and misses nothing (13.203–219; see [[clothing]] and [[gold-and-silver]]).

**In the false tale.** He tells the young herdsman, who is Athena, that he has come from Crete “with these goods”, leaving as much again to his children, and that the Phoenicians set them down beside him on the sand before they sailed for Sidon (13.257–259, 13.283–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**Hidden.** Athena has come “to hide the goods, all that the noble Phaeacians / gave you as you went home, by my counsel and my mind” (13.304–305; see [[athena]]).
