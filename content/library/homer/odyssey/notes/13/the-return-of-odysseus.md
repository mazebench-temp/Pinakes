summary: The return reaches Ithaca. The Phaeacians carry Odysseus home with the gifts that Zeus decreed (13.134–138, after 5.38–40); he comes as the Cyclops' curse and the prophecy of Teiresias foretold, late, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, to find bane in his house (13.339–340, 13.375–381); and Athena turns the homecoming into the plan of vengeance against the suitors (13.372–440).

**Home.** The decree of the fifth book is carried out to the letter. Zeus said that the Phaeacians would honor Odysseus like a god and send him home in a ship, “giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (5.36–40; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and Poseidon, complaining of what they have done, uses nearly the same verses (13.134–138; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). Odysseus' last wish in Scheria was to find his noble wife at home and his dear ones unhurt (13.42–43; see [[nostos]]). He reaches Ithaca asleep, and wakes there without knowing it (13.187–196; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). In Aristotle's outline of the Odyssey this is the turn of the story: “he himself arrives, storm-tossed, and after making himself known to certain people he attacks” (*Poetics* 1455b21–22; see the Poetics note).

**The curse fulfilled.** The blinded Cyclops prayed that Odysseus, if it was his fate to reach home, might “come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house” (9.534–535; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[curses]]), and Teiresias repeated it as a prophecy and named the bane, “overbearing men, who are devouring your substance, / wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (11.114–117; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). The thirteenth book brings each clause to pass. The ship is the Phaeacians', and Athena tells him that she always knew he would come home ὀλέσας ἄπο πάντας ἑταίρους, “having lost all your companions” (13.340); then she describes the bane in Teiresias' own verse, the suitors “wooing your godlike wife and giving bride-price” (13.378 = 11.117), who “are devouring your substance” (13.396; 11.116; see [[the-suitors]]). The rage of Poseidon, which the proem limited “until he reached his own land” (1.20–21; 6.331 = 1.21), has its last act on the same morning, against the Phaeacians (13.125–187; see [[poseidon]]).

**The return becomes the revenge.** Beneath the sacred olive the homecoming turns into a plan. Odysseus, hearing of the suitors, sees that he was about to perish like Agamemnon (13.383–385; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), and asks the goddess to “weave a plan, how I may take vengeance on them” (13.386), where Monro takes the pronoun to be perhaps emphatic, “Now I shall take vengeance on the men in their turn” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Additional Notes, on 13.386). She promises to stand by him, and makes him unknown to all mortals, so that the stranger who goes to the swineherd's hut is an old beggar (13.393–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[athena]]). The order of the later books is set here: first the swineherd, while Athena calls Telemachus home from Sparta (13.404–415; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
