# βουληφόροι (13.12; again 9.112, the Cyclopes' lack of "assemblies that bring counsel").
grc: [βουληφόροι]
en: [counselors]
---

**Counselors.** The chest in the hall holds “intricately worked gold and all the other / gifts that the counselors of the Phaeacians brought here” (Φαιήκων βουληφόροι, 13.11–12; see [[phaeacians]] and [[doron]]): the twelve kings who rule the land with Alcinous, each of whom had brought “a freshly washed mantle and a tunic / and a talent of precious gold” (8.390–393), and who now are to add a great tripod and a cauldron each (13.13–15; see [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]). βουληφόρος, “counsel-bearing”, names the men who sit in council; the Cyclopes have no ἀγοραὶ βουληφόροι, “assemblies that bring counsel” (9.112).

**The plan of Zeus.** Poseidon did not forget his threats, “and he asked Zeus his plan” (Διὸς δʼ ἐξείρετο βουλήν, 13.127; see [[poseidon]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]), the half-verse with which he asks Zeus in *Iliad* 20 why he has called the gods to assembly (*Iliad* 20.15–18). The plan he is given is his own: “Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (13.145).

**Counsel and mind.** Athena gives the word to Odysseus and takes it for herself. He is the best of all mortals “in counsel and in words” (βουλῇ καὶ μύθοισιν, 13.298; see [[aristos]] and [[mythos]]), and the Phaeacians gave him his goods “by my counsel and my mind” (ἐμῇ βουλῇ τε νόῳ τε, 13.305; see [[noos]]), the words in which he had claimed the escape from the Cyclops for himself, “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind” (12.211; see [[arete]]). In Scheria it was she who had guided him unseen (7.14–81; see [[athena]]).

**The plan made.** The two sit “beside the trunk of the sacred olive” and plan “destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[olive]] and [[the-suitors]]), and then part: “When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (τώ γʼ ὣς βουλεύσαντε διέτμαγεν, 13.439–440). The first of these verses is the one with which Zeus and Thetis part in *Iliad* 1, when he has promised to honor her son (*Iliad* 1.531; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). The book ends in the middle of the sentence, ἡ μὲν … αὐτὰρ ὁ, “she went to Sparta, while he …”, whose second half opens Book 14 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.440).
