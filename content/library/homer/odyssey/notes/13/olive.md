summary: A long-leaved olive stands at the head of the harbor of Phorcys, beside the cave of the Naiads (13.102). The Phaeacians set Odysseus' gifts by its trunk (13.122), Athena points it out to him among the landmarks of Ithaca (13.346), and the two sit beside “the trunk of the sacred olive” to plan the death of the suitors (13.372–373).
# ἐλαίη (13.102, 13.346; again 24.246, Laertes' orchard), first met here; it applies in the Odyssey only. The Iliad has no

**At the head of the harbor.** “And at the head of the harbor is a long-leaved olive, / and near it a lovely cave, misty, / sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads” (13.102–104; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[trees]]). τανύφυλλος, “long-leaved”, is said in Homer only of this olive and of the olive round which Odysseus built his bedchamber, “A bush of long-leaved olive grew within the court” (23.190, a plain rendering; see the translation's row τανύφυλλος ἐλαίη).

**The gifts by the trunk.** The Phaeacians lift out the possessions they had given him, “These they set all together by the trunk of the olive, / away from the road, lest some wayfarer / should come upon them and do them harm before Odysseus woke” (13.122–124; see [[possessions]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**A landmark.** When Odysseus will not believe that he is on Ithaca, Athena shows him the land: “This is the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / and here at the head of the harbor is the long-leaved olive” (13.345–346; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]), and then the cave and Mount Neritum (13.347–351; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[neritum]]); she scatters the mist, and he knows his land (13.352–354; see [[athena]]).

**The plan.** When the gifts are hidden, “Then the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive / and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[the-suitors]], [[overbearing]] and [[hieros]]). παρὰ πυθμένʼ ἐλαίης is the place of the gifts at 13.122, and πυθμένʼ ἐλαίης returns at the end of the bed's story, where Odysseus asks whether some man has set his bed elsewhere, “cutting through the trunk of the olive beneath” (23.204, a plain rendering).
