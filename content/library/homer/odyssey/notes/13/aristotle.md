---
summary: The Poetics makes this book's landing its example of an irrational incident inside a plot, made bearable by Homer's other virtues, and its outline of the Odyssey turns on the arrival, “he himself arrives, storm-tossed”. A note in the scholia on the first line of the book, gathered among the fragments of the Homeric Problems, asks why Odysseus made himself known to his son and his servants and not to Penelope. Athena's choice of Odysseus, the Rhetoric's example of what the wise prefer, is explained by the goddess herself in this book, and the Divisions handed down under Aristotle's name make the landing a set problem.
---

- **The putting ashore.** Epic has more room than tragedy for the irrational, but a plot should have nothing irrational in it, or else keep it outside the plot; and then the Odyssey: “Even the irrational things in the Odyssey about Odysseus' being put ashore would clearly be intolerable, if a bad poet had made them; as it is, the poet conceals the absurdity, sweetening it with his other virtues” (*Poetics* 1460a35–b2). The things about the putting ashore, τὰ περὶ τὴν ἔκθεσιν, are this book's: Odysseus sleeps through the voyage, and the Phaeacians lift him out of the ship with his bedding, lay him on the sand still asleep and set his gifts by the olive (13.70–125): [[aristotle-the-putting-ashore]], [[alogon]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]].
- **He arrives.** The story of the Odyssey, Aristotle says, is not long: a man is away from home for many years, watched by Poseidon and alone, while at home his property is wasted by suitors and his son plotted against; “he himself arrives, storm-tossed, and after making himself known to certain people he attacks; he himself is saved, and his enemies he destroys. This is what belongs to it; the rest is episodes” (*Poetics* 1455b17–23). The arrival, ἀφικνεῖται, is told in this book, and the heading of the book names it with the noun of the same verb, ἄφιξις: [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]].
- **Why not to Penelope?** A note in the scholia on 13.1, among the fragments of the *Homeric Problems*, asks why Odysseus did not make himself known to Penelope, mature and loving as she was, when he did to Telemachus, young as he was, and to the swineherd and the cowherd. Aristotle answers, first, that he had to tell those who would share the danger, without whom he could not attack the suitors, and that he told them one at a time and in private, so that none could lay the blame on another; and, second, that he was afraid that Penelope's joy would betray him, for she was used only to weeping, and if she stopped weeping suspicion would arise (*Homeric Problems* fr. 399 Gigon, paraphrased). In this book Athena tells him to bear everything in silence and to speak to no one, “man or woman” (13.307–310), praises him because he will not hurry home to see his wife before he has made trial of her (13.333–336; Aristarchus rejected 13.333–338, D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.333–338), and disguises him so that he may seem unsightly “to all the suitors / and to your wife and your son” (13.402–403): [[odysseus]], [[penelope]] and [[peira]].
- **As Athena chose Odysseus.** Among the things that are good, the *Rhetoric* counts what one of the wise or of the good has preferred, “as Athena chose Odysseus” (*Rhetoric* 1363a16–18). In this book the goddess gives her reasons: he is “by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words”, as she is famed among the gods for cunning (13.297–299), and she cannot abandon him, “because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible” (13.331–332): [[athena]].
- **A problem for the schools.** The *Divisions* handed down under Aristotle's name give the landing as their example of a rhetorical problem, the kind that arises when someone accuses or defends the poets: why did the companions put Odysseus, most prudent as he was, ashore on the island asleep? (*Divisions* 2.42): [[aristotle-the-putting-ashore]].
