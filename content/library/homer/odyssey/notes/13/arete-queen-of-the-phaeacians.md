**The cup.** When Pontonous has served the wine and the Phaeacians have poured libations from their seats, “brilliant Odysseus stood up, / and placed the two-handled cup in the hand of Arete” (13.56–57; see [[odysseus]], [[cup]] and [[libation]]); χειρί is the vulgate's reading, χερσί that of M and others (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.57). He came into the house as her suppliant, clasping her knees (7.142–152; see [[supplication]]), and he leaves it with his last words to her:

{{quote:dd59ecb4-90b6-53f3-aa2a-fcecfcc64406}}

βασίλεια, “queen”, is the word with which he began his story to her on the night he came (7.241; see [[basileus]]). In παισί, “with your children”, the dative is instrumental, with a partly “comitative” sense (Monro on 13.62), and for οἴκῳ, “house”, some manuscripts have χώρῳ (Monro's critical note on 13.61). The verse names her children, her people and the king (see [[nausicaa]] and [[alcinous]]).

**Her women.** “and Arete sent slave women with him, / one carrying a freshly washed mantle and a tunic, / and another she granted him, to carry the close-fitted chest along; / and a third brought bread and red wine” (13.66–69; see [[handmaids]], [[cloak]], [[tunic]], [[chest]] and [[wine]]). The chest is the one she brought out from her chamber on the evening of the games, packed with the Phaeacians' gifts and a mantle and tunic of her own, and bade the stranger tie fast, so that no one should do him harm on the way while he slept in the ship (8.438–445); he tied it with the knot that Circe taught him (8.447–448). For ὄπασσε, “granted”, some manuscripts and Eustathius have ἔπεμπε, “sent” (Monro's critical note on 13.68). In the ship the escorts take the food and the drink and stow them (13.71–72), and Odysseus sleeps on the deck as she foresaw (13.73–80; 8.443–445; see [[sleep]]).
