summary: The gods as a body. Odysseus' farewell prays that “the heavenly gods” may make his gifts prosper and that the gods may grant the Phaeacians good (13.41–46), and the Phaeacians pour libations “to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky” (13.54–55). Poseidon complains that he will no longer be honored “among the immortal gods”, and Zeus answers that the gods do not dishonor him (13.128–129, 13.141). One door of the cave of the Naiads is “the way of the immortals” (13.112), and on Ithaca Athena is famed for cunning “among all the gods” (13.298–299).

**The heavenly gods.** Odysseus takes his leave of the Phaeacians with a prayer for them and for himself: “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship; and may the heavenly gods / make them prosper for me” (13.40–42; see [[heavenly-gods]], [[pompe]] and [[doron]]). The Uranians are the gods who, as he told the Phaeacians twice, had given him many sorrows (7.242, 9.15); now they are asked to make his gifts prosper. For his hosts he asks, “may the gods grant you good / of every kind, and may there be no evil among the people” (13.45–46). ἀρετή is “good”, not restricted, as in later Greek, to merit or excellence (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.45; see [[arete]] and the translation's note on 13.45). Then the Phaeacians pour libations “to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky, / from where they sat” (13.54–56; see [[libation]] and [[wide-sky]]).

**Like the gods.** The ship carries “a man whose counsels were like the gods'” (13.89), θεοῖς ἐναλίγκια μήδεʼ ἔχοντα, a brachylogy for “like the (counsels of the) gods”, as the Iliad has Euphorbus' “hair like the Graces'” (Monro on 13.89, comparing *Iliad* 17.51; see the translation's note on 13.89 and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**The way of the immortals.** The cave of the Naiads has two doors, “one toward the North Wind, by which men go down, / and the other, toward the South Wind, is for gods; by that one / men do not enter, but it is the way of the immortals” (13.110–112; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[immortals]], [[boreas]] and [[notus]]). θεώτεραι is not “more divine” but “divine”, in contrast to the human door (Monro on 13.111; see the translation's note on 13.111).

**The honor of the gods.** Poseidon's complaint is that he will lose his standing among the gods because of mortals: “Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods / be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all” (13.128–129; see [[poseidon]] and [[time]]). Zeus answers for the gods as a body, “The gods do not dishonor you at all” (13.141; see [[zeus]]): the dishonor that a god need fear comes from men, and vengeance for it is his (13.143–144). On the peace among the Odyssey's gods, as Monro sees it, see the Book 13 note on [[poseidon]].

**Among all the gods.** On Ithaca Athena sets herself among the gods as Odysseus stands among men: “you are by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words, and I among all the gods / am famed for cunning and crafty ways” (13.297–299; see [[athena]] and [[metis]]). Odysseus' answer, that he wandered “until the gods freed me from misery” (13.321), is in a verse that is generally condemned (Monro on 13.320–323; see the Book 13 note on [[athena]]).
