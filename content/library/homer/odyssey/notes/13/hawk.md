---
summary: Not even a hawk, a falcon, could have kept pace with the Phaeacian ship that carried Odysseus home through the night (13.86–87).
---

**Swifter than a hawk.** “And she ran on very surely and unchecked; not even a hawk, / a falcon, the nimblest of winged birds, could have kept pace with her” (13.86–87; see [[the-four-stallions]]). The ἴρηξ is Homer's measure of speed. In the Iliad gods go down like hawks, Poseidon leaving the two Ajaxes and Apollo coming down from Ida, “a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds”, and Ajax tells Hector that the time is near when he will pray for horses “swifter than hawks” (*Iliad* 13.62–65, 15.237–238, 13.819; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]] and [[like-a-hawk]]). Here the measure follows the simile of the four stallions: the ship has outrun the team, and the bird could not keep up with her.

**A hawk, a falcon.** The hawk is named first and then the falcon. In such pairs, D. B. Monro observes, the second term is usually the specific one, as in βοῦς ταῦρος and σῦς κάπρος (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.86; see the translation's note on 13.86): ἴρηξ is the hawk in general, and the κίρκος a kind of it (see [[falcon]]). The hawks of the Odyssey's other book are birds at rest, roosting with owls and crows of the sea in the wood around Calypso's cave (5.65–67; see the Book 5 note).
