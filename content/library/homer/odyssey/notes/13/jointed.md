---
summary: The simile of Odysseus' longing for the sunset: “And as when a man longs for his supper, for whom all day long / two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field” (13.31–32).
---

βόε οἴνοπε πηκτὸν ἄροτρον is the second half of the verse in the simile of the two Ajaxes (*Iliad* 13.703), and the plowman's knees are hindered as he goes, as the hungry fighter's are in Odysseus' speech in the Iliad (13.34; *Iliad* 19.166; see the Odyssey translation's key words, νειός; πηκτὸν ἄροτρον). Monro takes πηκτόν as “put together,” an epithet of the plow's workmanship, like ποιητός of a house (13.306): “The point is that the object (whatever it is) is a work of mechanical skill” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.32). See [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]], [[oxen-at-the-plow]], [[the-plowing]] and [[wine-dark]].
