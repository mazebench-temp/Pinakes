**Hives of stone.** “In it are mixing bowls and two-handled jars / of stone; and there the bees store up honey” (13.105–106; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[two-handled-jar]]). The vessels of the cave are λάϊνοι, “of stone”, the furniture of the nymphs, and the bees have made them their hives. τιθαιβώσσουσι, “store up honey”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's row τιθαιβώσσω). W. W. Merry, describing the grotto, has the bees storing their honey “for them”, for the Naiads who weave on the stone looms beside it, “in the hollows of the rock” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 556; see [[nymphs]] and [[weaving]]). The poem says only that the bees are there.

**Bees of the rock.** Homer's bees are wild, and they live in rock. The Achaeans pour out to the assembly like bees “pouring from a hollow rock, always coming on anew” (*Iliad* 2.87–90), and Asius likens the two Lapiths at the gate to wasps or bees that have made their homes beside a rocky road and stand their ground for their young (*Iliad* 12.167–172; see the general article). In the cave the bees are at peace (see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]), and the honey is their work. Hesiod's bees, which feed the idle drones, live σμήνεσσι κατηρεφέεσσι, “in roofed hives” (*Theogony* 594); κατηρεφής is the word Athena uses of the cave itself, “the vaulted cave”, when she shows it to Odysseus (13.349; see [[roofed]]).
