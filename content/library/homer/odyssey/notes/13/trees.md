summary: The trees of Book 13 are the long-leaved olive at the head of the harbor of Phorcys, by whose trunk the Phaeacians set the gifts and Athena and Odysseus sit to plan (13.102, 13.122, 13.346, 13.372); the “flourishing trees” of Ithaca, which look strange to Odysseus through Athena's mist (13.196); and the acorns on which the swineherd's sows feed (13.409).
# The English "acorns" (13.409) for βάλανον, which the Book 10 note claims. The olive's forms are olive's.

**The olive of the harbor.** “And at the head of the harbor is a long-leaved olive, / and near it a lovely cave, misty” (13.102–103; again 13.346–347; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[olive]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). τανύφυλλος, “long-leaved”, is said again only of the olive in Odysseus' court around which he built his bedchamber (23.190, 23.195; see the translation's row τανύφυλλος ἐλαίη). The tree marks the place. By its trunk, παρὰ πυθμένʼ ἐλαίης, the Phaeacians set Odysseus' gifts, away from the road, while he sleeps (13.122–124; see [[possessions]]); Athena points to it when she shows him that he is in Ithaca (13.345–346); and when the gifts are hidden in the cave, “the two sat down beside the trunk of the sacred olive / and planned destruction for the overbearing suitors” (13.372–373; see [[the-suitors]]). Athena's account of the island also praises its forest, “of every kind” (13.246–247), and she shows him Mount Neritum, “clothed in forest” (13.351; see [[neritum]]).

**The flourishing trees.** When Odysseus wakes, Athena has poured a mist around him: “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (13.194–196; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and the translation's note on 13.194). δένδρεα τηλεθόωντα, “flourishing trees”, are the words of the orchard of Alcinous, “There tall trees grow, flourishing” (7.114; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]), and the wood around Calypso's cave “had grown, flourishing” (5.63; see [[calypsos-cave]]). D. B. Monro suggests that the strangeness of Ithaca to a man after his long absence may be only the exaggeration of a natural effect, and quotes as a parallel Wordsworth's *The Brothers*, where a man walking through fields that were once well known to him looks around and “imagined that he saw / Strange alteration wrought on every side / Among the woods and fields, and that the rocks / And everlasting hills themselves were changed” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Additional Notes, on 13.194). When the mist is scattered, “the land came into view” (13.352).

**Acorns.** The swineherd's sows feed by the Rock of Corax, “eating acorns to satisfy their hearts and the dark water / drinking” (13.409–410; see [[the-rock-of-corax]] and the Book 13 note on [[boar]] and [[satisfying]]). βάλανος is the acorn, the fruit of the oak, which Circe threw to the companions she had turned into swine, “such as swine that sleep on the ground always eat” (10.242–243; see the Book 10 note).
