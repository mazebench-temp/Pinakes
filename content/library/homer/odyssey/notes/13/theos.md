---
summary: The book in which a mortal meets a goddess face to face and does not know her. The Phaeacian ship carries “a man whose counsels were like the gods'” (13.89); the gods debate their honor (13.128–145); Odysseus prays “as to a god” to the young herdsman who is Athena (13.231), and she, revealed, tells him that only a god might outwit him (13.292); he answers that it is hard for a mortal to know her (13.312).
---

**Like the gods.** As the ship runs to Ithaca it bears “a man whose counsels were like the gods'” (θεοῖς ἐναλίγκια μήδεʼ ἔχοντα, 13.89; see [[metis]]), a brachylogy, “like the (counsels of the) gods” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.89; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.89). Before he leaves, Odysseus prays that the gods may bless the gifts and grant the Phaeacians good of every kind, and the Phaeacians pour libations “to the blessed gods, who hold the wide sky” (13.41–46, 13.54–55; see [[heavenly-gods]], [[arete]] and [[libation]]).

**The gods' honor.** Poseidon complains that he will no longer be honored “among the immortal gods” since mortals slight him, and Zeus assures him, “The gods do not dishonor you at all” (13.128–129, 13.141; see [[poseidon]], [[time]] and [[aristos]]).

**A goddess unrecognized.** On Ithaca “the goddess had poured a mist around him” (13.189; see [[eidos]]), and she comes to him as a young herdsman. He greets the herdsman as a suppliant, “for to you / I pray as to a god” (εὔχομαι ὥς τε θεῷ, 13.230–231; see [[euchomai]]), and is speaking to a god. Revealed, she tells him that a man would have to be crafty to outdo him, “even if it were a god who met you” (13.292; see [[dolos]]), and that she among all the gods is famed for cunning (13.298–299). His answer names the condition of every meeting between gods and men: “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything” (13.312–313; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]] and [[shape-changing]]). He reminds her that after the sack of Troy “a god scattered the Achaeans” (13.317 = 3.131), and that he wandered “until the gods freed me from misery” (13.321), in a verse generally condemned (Monro on 13.320–323).
