summary: Troy is the measure of the book's homecoming. Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians' gifts are more than Odysseus “would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.137); Athena says that the name of Ithaca “has reached even to Troy” (13.248); the Cretan of the false tale quarreled over his spoil from Troy (13.262–263); and Odysseus remembers Athena's help “while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy” and asks for it again, “as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.314–319, 13.388).

**More than the spoil of Troy.** Poseidon tells Zeus that the Phaeacians have set Odysseus down in Ithaca with “bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy, / if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (13.136–138; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). The last two verses are those in which Zeus foretold the gifts to the gods in Book 5 (5.39–40; see [[aisa]]); the god who resents them now uses the words of the god who granted them.

**Even to Troy.** The herdsman's praise of the island ends with Troy: “Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy, / which they say is far from the Achaean land” (13.248–249; see [[ithaca]] and [[achaea]]). The speaker, in Athena's disguise, talks of Troy as a far-off place known by report.

**Spoil from Troy.** The Cretan of Odysseus' lie killed Orsilochus “because he wished to rob me of all my spoil / from Troy, for which I suffered sorrows in my heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (13.262–264; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]). τῆς ληΐδος … Τρωϊάδος is “the Trojan spoil”, and the article is perhaps possessive, “my spoil” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.262); he had served “in the land of the Trojans” with companions of his own (13.266; see [[trojans]]).

**Athena at Troy.** Odysseus answers the goddess with his memory of the war and its end: “But this I know well, that before now you were kind to me, / while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy. / But when we had sacked the steep city of Priam / and gone aboard the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans, / after that I did not see you, daughter of Zeus, nor did I notice you / boarding my ship, to ward off some sorrow from me” (13.314–319; see [[athena]], [[priam]] and [[achaeans]]). The third and fourth verses are Nestor's (3.130–131), whose tale went on to the grim homecoming that Zeus devised for the Argives “through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father, / who set strife between the two sons of Atreus” (3.132–136; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]); the third is also the verse with which Odysseus began the story of Neoptolemus' homecoming for Achilles (11.533).

**The veil of Troy.** When the plan against the suitors is made, Odysseus asks Athena to stand beside him “as when we loosed the shining veil of Troy” (13.387–388; see [[veil]] and [[battlements]]). κρήδεμνα is “the diadem of towers”, a phrase from the Iliad, where Achilles wishes that he and Patroclus alone might survive “so that we alone might loose the sacred veil of Troy” (*Iliad* 16.100), a refinement on the commoner figure that calls the battlements of a city its “head”, κάρηνα, as of Zeus, “who has brought down the heights of many cities” (Monro on 13.388, citing *Iliad* 2.117; see the translation's note on 13.388). The wish of the Iliad is spoken here as a memory: the sack of Troy, by Odysseus and the goddess together.
