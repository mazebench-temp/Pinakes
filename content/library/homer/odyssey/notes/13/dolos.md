---
summary: Athena, smiling at Odysseus' false tale, says that only a crafty cheat could get past him “in tricks of every kind”, and calls him “insatiable of tricks” (13.291–293).
---

“Crafty would he be, and a cheat, who should get past you / in tricks of every kind, even if it were a god who met you. / Hard man, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks, you were not, then, / even in your own land, to leave off your deceits / and thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up” (ἐν πάντεσσι δόλοισι … δόλων ἆτʼ, 13.291–295; see [[kerdos]], [[apate]], [[of-subtle-wiles]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The tricks are the lie he has just told her, the Cretan exile who killed Idomeneus' son (13.256–286). δόλων ἆτʼ is the Trojan Socus' taunt in battle, “Much-praised Odysseus, insatiable of tricks and toil” (*Iliad* 11.430); in the goddess's mouth the words are praise, as σχέτλιε, “Hard man”, is said in a friendly and admiring tone (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.293), and Monro would write ἆατʼ for ἆτʼ (his critical note on 13.293). “Even if it were a god who met you” (καὶ εἰ θεὸς ἀντιάσειε, 13.292) looks forward to Odysseus' answer, that it is hard for a mortal who meets her to know her (13.312; Monro on 13.292; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]). Only a god might get past him, and he has not known the goddess who met him (13.299–300).
