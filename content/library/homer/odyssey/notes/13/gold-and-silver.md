---
summary: The gold of the book is the Phaeacians' gift, from the “intricately worked gold” in the chest (13.11) to the “bronze and gold in plenty” that Poseidon complains of (13.136); Odysseus counts it on the shore of Ithaca and Athena hides it in the cave of the nymphs (13.217–218, 13.367–371).
---

**The gift.** Alcinous reminds the Phaeacians of what already lies in the chest, clothes “and intricately worked gold and all the other / gifts that the counselors of the Phaeacians brought here” (13.10–12; see [[chest]] and [[doron]]). It is the gold that Arete laid in the chest with the clothes, “the clothing and the gold, which the Phaeacians had given him” (8.438–440), a talent from each of the twelve kings (8.390–393; see [[kings]]). To Poseidon it is the measure of the insult: they gave the man he hates “untold gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.135–137, in the words of Zeus' prophecy, 5.38–39; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**Counted and hidden.** Waking in a land he does not know, Odysseus ends his lament by counting his treasure: “But come, let me count my goods and look at them, / to see whether they have gone off with anything in the hollow ship”, and he counts “the beautiful tripods and cauldrons / and the gold and the fine woven clothes” (13.215–218; see [[tripod]] and [[cauldron]]). “Of these he missed nothing” (13.219). When he knows his land, Athena finds the hiding places in the cave, “and Odysseus / brought everything near, the gold and the unyielding bronze / and the well-made clothes, which the Phaeacians had given him” (13.367–369), and she sets a stone against the doors (13.370; see [[possessions]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The gold that came home with him stays in the cave, and he goes to his house as a beggar (13.429–438; see [[shape-changing]]).
