---
summary: Waking in a land he does not know, Odysseus mourns for his native land as he drags himself along the shore (13.219–221), though he is standing on it; and Athena tells him that Penelope is “always mourning in her heart for your homecoming” (13.379).
---

**Mourning on his own shore.** Odysseus wakes, does not know the land, groans and strikes his thighs (13.197–199; see [[pity]]), and counts his gifts, missing nothing; “but he mourned for his native land, / dragging himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, / lamenting much” (13.219–221; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[ithaca]]). The land he mourns is the land under his feet, which Athena's mist has made strange to him (13.189–196; see [[shape-changing]]). ὀδύρετο and ἑρπύζων, “mourned” and “dragging himself”, are the two words of Achilles at the pyre of Patroclus, “so Achilles mourned as he burned the bones of his companion, / dragging himself beside the pyre, groaning without ceasing” (*Iliad* 23.224–225); ἑρπύζω is found elsewhere only of old Laertes creeping along his vineyard (1.193). So he sat on the shore of Calypso's island, where she found him with his eyes never dry of tears, “as he mourned for his homecoming” (5.151–158; see [[nostos]]).

**Penelope's mourning.** Athena tells him how his wife waits: “and she, always mourning in her heart for your homecoming, / gives hope to all” (13.379–380; see [[penelope]]). ὀδυρομένη is “crying for it”, as the Achaeans in the Iliad “wail to one another about going home” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.379, comparing *Iliad* 2.290). The words of her mourning, νόστον ὀδυρομένη, are those of his on Calypso's shore (νόστον ὀδυρομένῳ, 5.153): the husband and the wife have mourned for the same homecoming, apart. Earlier in the book Athena has said it of her in the verses of Anticleia's report, “and always in misery for her / the nights and the days waste away, as she sheds tears” (13.337–338, as 11.182–183; see [[tears]]).
