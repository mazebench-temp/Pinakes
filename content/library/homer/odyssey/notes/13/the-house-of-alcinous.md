summary: The house is the scene of the book's first part, the end of the last night and the last day in Scheria. The tale ends in its shadowy halls, and Alcinous, speaking of “my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed”, bids the elders add their gifts (13.1–15); after the sacrifice the Phaeacians go “to the house of Alcinous” and feast (13.23–28); the libation is poured throughout the hall, and Odysseus, bidding Arete take her delight “in this house”, steps over its threshold and goes down to the ship (13.49–69).

**The end of the tale.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2 = 11.333–334; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[silence]]). Alcinous answers: “Odysseus, since you have come to my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed, so I think that you will not be beaten back / and sail for home again” (13.4–6; see [[alcinous]], [[bronze-floored]] and [[high-roofed]]). ποτὶ χαλκοβατὲς δῶ is the verse-end of the house of Zeus on Olympus, where Thetis will go to plead for her son (*Iliad* 1.426), and of the house of Hephaestus, to which the gods gather to see Ares and Aphrodite in the bonds (8.321); the king's house has the gods' formula. The words that follow come from the Iliad with a change of meaning: there Achilles thinks that the Achaeans will be beaten back and go home, if they escape death, a return in disappointment, while here a return home is no disappointment (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.5–6; *Iliad* 1.59–60; see the translation's note on 13.5–6). The gifts already given lie in “the polished chest” in the house, and the elders who “always” drink the wine of the elders in Alcinous' halls are to add a tripod and a cauldron each (13.7–15; see [[chest]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**The last feast.** In the morning, when the gifts are stowed, “they went to the house of Alcinous and made ready a feast” (13.23), εἰς Ἀλκινόοιο, with the house understood, as ἐν Ἀλκινόοιο of the gifts of the gods “at the house of Alcinous” and ἐς Ἀλκινόοιο of the gifts that the heralds carried there (7.132, 8.418). Alcinous sacrifices an ox to Zeus, Demodocus sings, and Odysseus keeps turning his head toward the sun, eager for it to set (13.24–35; see [[feast]] and [[demodocus]]).

**Over the threshold.** At sunset Pontonous serves the wine “to all throughout the hall” for the libation (13.50–56; see [[pontonous]] and [[libation]]), and Odysseus places the two-handled cup in Arete's hand and bids her farewell: “But I am going home; and you, take your delight in this house / with your children and your people and King Alcinous” (13.57–62; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[cup]]). παισί, “with your children”, is an instrumental dative with a partly comitative sense (Monro on 13.62). “So saying, brilliant Odysseus stepped over the threshold” (13.63; see [[threshold]]): he had first stood and pondered “before he reached the bronze threshold” of the house as a stranger and a suppliant (7.83), and he leaves it with gifts and an escort, followed by the slave women whom Arete sends after him with a mantle, a tunic, the chest, and bread and wine (13.64–69; see [[handmaids]]).
