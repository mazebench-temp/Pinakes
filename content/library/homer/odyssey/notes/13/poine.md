summary: The book's paying and punishing: the Phaeacian kings will repay themselves from the people (13.15); Zeus tells Poseidon that “vengeance is always yours” (13.144); Athena keeps Odysseus unknown until the suitors “had paid for all their transgression” (13.193); Odysseus prays that Zeus of suppliants may punish the Phaeacians (13.213–214); and he asks Athena for a plan “how I may take vengeance on them” (13.386).

# ἀποτίσομαι (13.386), each first met here among the Odyssey's notes.

en: [paid for, punishes]

**Repayment.** The kings who give the stranger a tripod and a cauldron each will not bear the cost: “and we in turn will gather it among the people / and repay ourselves” (τισόμεθʼ, 13.14–15; see [[charis]] and [[demos]]), the middle of τίνω, “will repay ourselves” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.15).

**Honor and vengeance.** Zeus answers Poseidon: “But if any man, yielding to his own force and strength, / does not honor you at all, vengeance is always yours, even hereafter” (οὔ τι τίει. σοὶ δʼ ἐστὶ καὶ ἐξοπίσω τίσις αἰεί, 13.143–144; see [[time]] and [[kratos]]). There is a play of words between τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of vengeance (Monro on 13.144): the honor not paid is paid for. The text prints a period after τίει, which cuts the clause from its answer; Monro's text has a comma, and the English reads the two verses as one sentence (see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.144).

**The suitors' payment.** Athena hides the land from Odysseus so that she may make him unknown, “lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends, / before the suitors had paid for all their transgression” (ἀποτῖσαι, 13.192–193; see [[the-suitors]]). The verse returns in the hall, when Odysseus refuses Eurymachus' offer of compensation and will not stop his hands from slaughter until it is paid (22.63–64). At the end of the book he asks the goddess, “But come, weave a plan, how I may take vengeance on them” (ἀποτίσομαι, 13.386; see [[metis]]).

**Zeus of suppliants.** Believing that the Phaeacians have left him in a strange land, Odysseus prays against them: “May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over / other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong” (Ζεὺς σφέας τίσαιτο ἱκετήσιος, ὅς τε καὶ ἄλλους / ἀνθρώπους ἐφορᾷ καὶ τίνυται, 13.213–214; see [[zeus]], [[supplication]] and [[phaeacians]]). τίσαιτο is Aristarchus' reading; Zenodotus read the imperative τισάσθω, which may have seemed improper applied to a god (Monro's critical note on 13.213). The curse is unjust, and in the same hour the Phaeacians are being punished by Poseidon for the escort they gave him (13.159–187; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).
