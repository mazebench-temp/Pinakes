---
summary: “So saying, Athena touched him with her wand” (13.429): with a touch of her wand she withers Odysseus' skin, destroys his hair, dims his eyes and dresses him as an old beggar (13.429–438).
---

When she has told Odysseus what she will do, “So saying, Athena touched him with her wand” (13.429; see [[athena]], [[shape-changing]], [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[odysseus]]), and the change follows in the words of her promise: the fair skin withered, the fair hair gone, “the skin / of an aged old man” around all his limbs, the eyes dimmed (13.430–433; see [[eyes]] and [[hair]]), and then the rag, the tunic, the deerskin, the staff and the pouch (13.434–438; see [[clothing]], [[scepter]] and [[pouch]]). The same words, ῥάβδῳ ἐπεμάσσατʼ, with the wand now golden, give him back his looks in the swineherd's hut, “Athena touched him with her golden wand” (16.172, a plain rendering), and when he has made himself known to his son she strikes him with her wand “and made him an old man again” (16.456, a plain rendering). The wand is a goddess' instrument, as Circe's is when she strikes the companions and pens them as swine (10.238, 10.293, 10.319; see [[circe]]). The change that Athena works here is a magical one, not a mere illusion or the heightening of a natural effect, as Kirchhoff insisted, and when Odysseus is revealed to his son he is really himself again (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §6).
