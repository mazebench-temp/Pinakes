summary: The book carries out the counsel of Agamemnon's soul. Odysseus comes home secretly, hidden by Athena's mist “lest his wife should know him first” (13.189–193); Athena bids him tell no one, “man or woman” (13.307–310), and sees that he will make trial of his wife before he makes himself known (13.333–338); and he remembers Agamemnon's death in his halls (13.383–385).
refs: [13.192, 13.308, 13.336, 13.383-385]
---

**The counsel kept.** In the land of the dead Agamemnon told Odysseus to bring his ship home “secretly, and not openly, to your dear native land”, and to tell his wife one part of what he knew and hide the rest (11.441–443, 11.454–456; see [[agamemnon]]). He comes home so, though not by his own design. The Phaeacians set him ashore asleep in the harbor of Phorcys and are gone (13.116–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]), and Athena pours a mist around him, so that she might make him unknown, “lest his wife should know him first, and his townsmen and friends, / before the suitors had paid for all their transgression” (13.189–193; see [[shape-changing]]). When she has revealed herself she lays down the rule of silence: he must “not speak out to anyone, man or woman, / of them all, that you have come back from your wanderings” (13.308–309; see [[silence]]). And she sees in him the wariness that Agamemnon urged: another man would have hurried home to see his wife and children, “but it is not yet your pleasure to learn or to hear, / before you have made trial of your wife yourself” (13.333–336; see [[peira]] and [[marriage]]). πειρήσεαι is “observe for yourself”, not trusting to report (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.336). In the same breath Athena confirms what Agamemnon allowed of Penelope (11.444–446): his wife “sits, just as she was, / in your halls”, weeping (13.336–338; see [[penelope]]), and she gives hope to every suitor, “but her mind has other things in view” (13.381). As some ancient copies lacked Agamemnon's last counsel (see the entry), Aristarchus rejected Athena's six verses on the trial of the wife, 13.333–338 (Monro's critical note).

**Agamemnon remembered.** When Athena has told him how the suitors are wooing his wife, Odysseus answers with the fate he has escaped: “Ah, truly, like Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / I was about to perish by an evil fate in my halls, / had you not told me each thing, goddess, fair and right” (13.383–385; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The danger he names is the death in the halls; what saves him is the knowledge that Agamemnon lacked, and the secrecy that Agamemnon commended.
