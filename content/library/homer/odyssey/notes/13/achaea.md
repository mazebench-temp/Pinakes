---
summary: Athena, in the likeness of a young herdsman, ends her praise of Ithaca with its fame: its name has reached even to Troy, “which they say is far from the Achaean land” (13.248–249).
---

“Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy, / which they say is far from the Achaean land” (13.248–249; see [[ithaca]] and [[troy]]). τήν refers to Troy, and Ἀχαιΐδος … αἴης, “the Achaean land”, is the Greek homeland as a whole, in which Ithaca lies: the phrase that Odysseus had used in the land of the dead when he said that he had not yet come near it (11.166, 11.481; see the Book 11 note) is now spoken to him on Ithaca, by a goddess who knows that he has come home. Ἀχαιΐδος has αἴης with it here; in Book 11 it stood alone, with the noun understood (see the Book 11 note). φασίν, “they say”, makes the distance a matter of report, as a herdsman of the island might speak of Troy.
