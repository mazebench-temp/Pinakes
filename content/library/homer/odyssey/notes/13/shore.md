summary: Odysseus leaves Scheria by the shore, led by a herald “to the swift ship and the shore of the sea” (13.65), and wakes on the shore of Ithaca: laid asleep “on the sand” (13.119), he drags himself “along the shore of the loud-roaring sea”, mourning for the land he does not know (13.220), and asks the herdsman whether this is an island “or some shore / of the deep-soiled mainland” (13.234–235).
# Book 13's other words for the shore: the headlands of the harbor of Phorcys (13.98), "some shore" of the mainland
# (13.234), and the sand on which Odysseus is laid and on which the Cretan of his tale says he lay (13.119, 13.284).
# All four phrases are found only here in Homer.

**To the shore of the sea.** Odysseus steps over the threshold of Alcinous' house, and the king sends a herald “to lead him to the swift ship and the shore of the sea” (13.63–65; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[heralds]]). θῖνα θαλάσσης is the strand where the ship lies ready.

**Asleep on the sand.** At the harbor of Phorcys two sheer headlands, ἀκταὶ ἀπορρῶγες, keep out the swell (13.97–99; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]); ἀκταί are “headlands” here, as the “jutting headlands” of the coast of Scheria and of the Laestrygonians' harbor, while ἀκτή alone is the shore (5.405, 10.89; see the translation's key-word row for ἠιών and ἀκτή). The Phaeacians lift Odysseus out of the ship and lay him “on the sand, overcome by sleep”, ἐπὶ ψαμάθῳ (13.119; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**Along the shore.** Waking, he counts his gifts, “but he mourned for his native land, / dragging himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, / lamenting much” (13.219–221; see [[loud-roaring-sea]] and [[lament]]). παρὰ θῖνα πολυφλοίσβοιο θαλάσσης is the verse-end of the Iliad's Chryses, who “went in silence along the shore of the loud-roaring sea” when Agamemnon sent him away (*Iliad* 1.34), and the shore is again a place of grief; for βῆ δʼ ἀκέων, “he went in silence”, the verse has ἑρπύζων, “dragging himself”, the word of Achilles “dragging himself beside the pyre” of Patroclus and of Laertes, who “creeps along the slope of his vineyard” (*Iliad* 23.225; 1.193).

**Island or mainland.** He asks the herdsman: “Is it one of the clear-seen islands, or some shore / of the deep-soiled mainland that lies upon the sea?” (13.234–235; see [[clear-seen]] and [[deep-soiled]]). ἀκτὴ is to be joined with ἠπείροιο, “a shore of the mainland”, and ἁλὶ κεκλιμένη, “that lies upon the sea”, is said of the islands in Telemachus' account of Ithaca, “those that lie upon the sea” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.235, comparing 4.608). In his false tale the Cretan lay down on this shore with the Phoenicians, who set his goods beside him “where I myself lay on the sands” (13.284; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).
