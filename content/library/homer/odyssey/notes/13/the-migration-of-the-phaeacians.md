---
heading: The founder's prophecy fulfilled
summary: The story of the founding comes to its end in the god's anger. Poseidon calls the Phaeacians “sprung from my own stock” (13.130), and when he has turned their ship to stone Alcinous recognizes the prophecy of his father, the founder, and bids the people give up the escorts for which the god begrudged them (13.172–183). The city that Nausithous walled is left under the threat of the mountain.
# Unnamed: Poseidon's stock (13.130); the father's oracles (13.172-178); the end of the escorts (13.180-181).
# Nausithous is not named in the book (refs in his own Book 13 note).
refs: [13.130, 13.172-178, 13.180-181]
---

**Poseidon's stock.** Poseidon complains to Zeus that the Phaeacians do not honor him, τοί πέρ τοι ἐμῆς ἔξ εἰσι γενέθλης, “who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.130; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.130). The founder of their city was his son by Periboea (7.56–62; see [[nausithous]] and [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]]), and so was the Cyclops whose blinding the Phaeacians have now, in the god's eyes, rewarded with an escort and gifts (1.68–73; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]). The people whom Nausithous led away from the Cyclopes are punished for the man who blinded one of them.

**The father's oracles.** When the ship is turned to stone in sight of the city, Alcinous remembers “the oracles of my father, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (13.172–174). He does not name his father here, as he did in the eighth book (8.564–565), but calls him, as he did there, “the old man”: “So the old man used to say; and now it is all being fulfilled” (13.178; 8.570; see [[alcinous]] and [[thesphaton]]). In the eighth book the end of the saying left the god free, “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” (8.570–571); in the thirteenth the god has chosen.

**The end of the escorts.** The founder settled his people “far from toiling men” (6.8), and they became the escorts of all men who came to them (8.566 = 13.174; see [[pompe]]). Alcinous' first command after the stone is to end that life: “stop escorting mortals, whenever anyone comes / to our city” (13.180–181). The mountain that the god has threatened would end it for good: Merry took it for a wall of mountain to pen the Phaeacians in and cut them off from the sea, not a mountain hurled on the city (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.569). The city that Nausithous built, with its wall, its houses and its temples (6.9–10), is left with its leaders praying to Poseidon around his altar (13.185–187), and the poem does not say whether the god heard them.
