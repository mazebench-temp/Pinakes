---
summary: φίλος in the book: “an escort and gifts of friendship” (13.41); “My friend”, Odysseus' greeting to the young herdsman who is Athena (13.228); the goddess who “made you dear to all the Phaeacians” (13.302) and the “thievish words” that are “dear to you from the ground up” (13.295); and the dear sons of the book, Idomeneus' (13.259), the Cyclops' (13.343) and Odysseus' own (13.360, 13.413).
---

**Friendship.** Odysseus' farewell gathers the word: “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship” (φίλος … θυμός, πομπὴ καὶ φίλα δῶρα, 13.40–41; see [[doron]] and [[pompe]]), and he prays to find his wife at home “and my dear ones unhurt” (13.43). On Ithaca he thinks that the kings he might have reached instead “would have welcomed me” (ἐφίλει, 13.206; see [[xenia]]), and greets the first man he meets, “My friend, since you are the first I find in this place, / greetings to you” (ὦ φίλʼ, 13.228–229), coming as a suppliant to “your dear knees” (13.231; see [[supplication]]). The friend is a goddess, who tells him that she “made you dear to all the Phaeacians” (φίλον πάντεσσιν ἔθηκα, 13.302; see [[athena]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**What is dear.** What is φίλος to someone is what he likes. Zeus tells Poseidon, “Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (13.145); Athena tells Odysseus that his “thievish words” are “dear to you from the ground up” (οἵ τοι πεδόθεν φίλοι εἰσίν, 13.295; see [[mythos]]), and that “it is not yet your pleasure to learn or to hear” of his wife and children before he has tested her (σοὶ δʼ οὔ πω φίλον ἐστί, 13.335; see [[peira]]). πεδόθεν is “from the ground up”, like *funditus*, but probably with the notion of constancy, as in ἔμπεδος (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.295; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.295). He asks her whether he has truly come “to my dear native land” (13.328; see [[dear-native-land]]).

**Dear sons.** The book's sons are φίλοι: Orsilochus, “the dear son of Idomeneus”, whom the false Cretan says he killed (13.259; see [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]); the Cyclops, Poseidon's “dear son” whom Odysseus blinded (13.343; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]); and Telemachus, for whom Odysseus prays to the Naiads, “and makes my dear son grow” (13.360), and whom Athena goes to call home, “your dear son, Odysseus” (13.413; see [[telemachus]]), a son whom the swineherd “loves” (φιλέει, 13.406; see [[eumaeus]]).
