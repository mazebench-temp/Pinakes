---
summary: The false Cretan is left on the shore “grieving at heart” (13.286), and Odysseus tells Athena that after Troy he wandered “bearing a torn heart in my breast” (13.320), in a verse the critics condemn.
---

**Grieving at heart.** The lying tale ends with the Phoenicians gone, “but I was left behind, grieving at heart” (ἀκαχήμενος ἦτορ, 13.286; see [[achos]] and [[phoenicians]]).

**A torn heart.** Odysseus reproaches Athena for leaving him after Troy: “But always, bearing a torn heart in my breast, / I wandered, until the gods freed me from misery” (ἀλλʼ αἰεὶ φρεσὶν ᾗσιν ἔχων δεδαϊγμένον ἦτορ, 13.320–321; see [[phrenes]] and [[athena]]). The two verses are generally condemned: the clause that follows, “until the time when, in the rich land of the Phaeacian men …”, is construed with “after that I did not see you” (13.318), and a parenthesis such as they form is quite un-Homeric (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.320–323; see the Odyssey translation's note on 13.320–323). The participle is the Iliad's word for the dead: the Achaeans left Aretus “with his heart torn” by Automedon's spear (δεδαϊγμένον ἦτορ, *Iliad* 17.535), and Patroclus lies “torn by the sharp bronze” (*Iliad* 19.211, 19.283). This is its only use in the Odyssey, and the only one of grief.
