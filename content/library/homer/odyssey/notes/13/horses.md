summary: The Phaeacian ship that carries Odysseus home runs like a team of four stallions under the whip (13.81–84), and Athena tells him that Ithaca is “rugged and not good for driving horses” (13.242).
# ἱππήλατος, "good for driving horses" (13.242; again 4.607), claimed here for the Odyssey; the Iliad's and the
# Odyssey's ἱππηλάτα, the epithet of old horsemen, is another form (horse-driving). ἵπποι (13.81) is the Book 3
# note's; the English "stallions" (13.81) is added here.
grc: [ἱππήλατος]
en: [stallions]

**Four stallions.** “And she, as four stallions yoked together on the plain, / all rushing on together under the blows of the whip, / lift themselves high as they swiftly make their way, / so her stern lifted” (13.81–84; see [[the-four-stallions]] and [[ships]]). ἄρσενες ἵπποι, “stallions”, are the horses of Diomedes in the chariot race for Patroclus, and the third verse is made like theirs, in the plural for their dual (*Iliad* 23.377, 23.501; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). A team of four is rare in Homer: Hector calls four horses by name, and the trick rider of the Iliad's simile harnesses four together and leaps from one to another as they run (*Iliad* 8.185, 15.679–684; see [[the-horses-of-hector]] and [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). W. W. Merry, who finds the art of riding alluded to only twice in Homer, notes that this simile refers to a team of horses drawing a chariot (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.371; see the Book 5 note). Penelope had called ships “horses of the sea / for men” (4.708–709; see the Book 4 note); here the ship of the Phaeacians is a chariot team, and the sea its plain.

**No land for horses.** Athena, still in the likeness of a young herdsman, describes the island to the stranger: “Truly it is rugged and not good for driving horses, / but it is not very poor, though it is not broad” (13.242–243; see [[ithaca]]). ἱππήλατος, “good for driving horses”, is Telemachus' word when he refused the three horses and the chariot that Menelaus offered him: “For none of the islands is good for driving horses or rich in meadows, / those that lie upon the sea; and Ithaca above them all” (4.607–608; see the Book 4 note). Merry finds Athena's account of a rugged land, unfit for horse-driving, in harmony with the reasons that Telemachus gave (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 555).
