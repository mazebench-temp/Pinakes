---
summary: The harbor of Phorcys, where ships stay without a mooring rope, receives the Phaeacian ship at dawn (13.96–114), and it is the first place Athena names when she shows Odysseus his island (13.345–346). Between, waking in the mist, he finds “the harbors fit for mooring” strange to him (13.195), and in his false tale the Phoenicians row “into the harbor” by night (13.279).
---

**The harbor of Phorcys.** Five of the book's seven λιμήν lines are the harbor of Phorcys, named for the old man of the sea (13.96, 13.98, 13.102, 13.345, 13.346; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). It is a harbor of the kind the Odyssey's sailors prize. Its headlands keep off the waves raised by the winds, so that “within, without a mooring rope, there stay / the well-benched ships, once they have reached the measure of anchorage” (13.100–101); on the island off the land of the Cyclopes there is likewise “a harbor with good anchorage, where there is no need of a cable, / neither to cast out mooring-stones nor to make fast stern-cables” (9.136–137; see [[mooring-stones]] and [[stern-cables]]). ὅρμου μέτρον, “the measure of anchorage”, is the distance at which ships are made fast by a cable to the shore, if fastening is needed (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.101); ὅρμος in this sense comes twice more, when a ship is brought forward with oars “to the anchorage”, εἰς ὅρμον (*Iliad* 1.435, at Chryse; 15.497, at Ithaca); beside it is εὔορμος, “with good anchorage”, of the harbors of the island of goats, of Pharos, and of the Iliad's simile of the dolphin (9.136, 4.358; *Iliad* 21.23).

**Harbors fit for mooring.** When Odysseus wakes in the mist, the harbors of his own island look strange to him with everything else: “Therefore all things appeared in another shape to their lord, / the long paths and the harbors fit for mooring, / and the lofty rocks and the flourishing trees” (13.194–196; see [[ithaca]]). λιμένες πάνορμοι, “the harbors fit for mooring”, has an adjective found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row).

**The harbor of the lie.** In the tale he tells Athena, the Phoenicians, driven off course, came by night: “With difficulty we rowed forward into the harbor, and none of us / had any thought of supper” (13.279–280; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). It is the harbor in which he is standing, where the Phaeacians had in fact brought him (13.113–125; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]).
