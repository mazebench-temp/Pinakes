---
summary: Proclus reports, in order to reject it, Numenius' reading of the two doors of the cave of the Naiads as the gates of the sky by which souls go down into birth and up again; he takes Athena's “I am famed for cunning” as the reason why she is called Metis among the gods; and, defending Plato's Atlantis, he denies that Plato did what Homer did with the Phaeacians and with the Achaean wall, making the evidence vanish.
---

- **The two doors.** Commenting on the place of judgment in the myth of Er, where the judges sit between two chasms in the earth and two in the sky (Plato, *Republic* 614c), Proclus reports Numenius' account: the place of the judges is the center of the cosmos, the sky is the sphere of the fixed stars, and its two chasms are the tropic signs, Cancer the chasm of the souls' descent into birth and Capricorn the chasm of their ascent. Numenius called Homer as witness, since the poet speaks of the doors “toward Boreas” as ways down for men, and of those toward Notus, by which men may not enter, as ways of the immortals only; for Capricorn, leading the souls up, frees them from their life among men and receives only the immortal and divine (*On the Republic* II 128–129 Kroll, plain renderings; 13.109–112). Numenius found the same tropic signs in Homer's “gates of the Sun”, and the Milky Way in his “people of dreams” (*Odyssey* 24.12). Proclus answers that the account cannot be squared with Plato's own words (II 130 Kroll): [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[nymphs]] and [[plato]].
- **Metis.** The *Timaeus* calls Athena both a lover of war and a lover of wisdom (24c–d). She is a philosopher, Proclus explains, as demiurgic intellection and as wisdom separate and immaterial, “and so she is called Metis among the gods; she herself says of herself, ‘I am famed for cunning and crafty ways’” (μήτι τε κλέομαι καὶ κέρδεσι, *On the Timaeus* I 168 Diehl, a plain rendering; 13.299). In the poem the words close her contest of wits with Odysseus, the best of mortals “in counsel and in words”, as she is among the gods (13.296–299): [[athena]], [[metis]] and [[kerdos]].
- **The Phaeacians and the wall.** Plato's Egyptian priests tell Solon that Atlantis sank into the sea in one day and night (*Timaeus* 25c–d). Proclus, who holds the story true, adds that one must not say that the writer makes the evidence vanish in order to take away the things he described, “as Homer did with the Phaeacians and with the wall that the Greeks built”; for what Plato tells is not made up, since many parts of the earth are flooded by the sea (*On the Timaeus* I 190 Diehl, a plain rendering). The charge about the wall goes back to Aristotle, who said that the poet who invented it made it vanish, and Posidonius had set the same phrase against Atlantis, as Strabo reports (*Geography* 13.1.36, 2.3.6). Proclus' Phaeacians belong with it on a reading in which the poet does away with them: Poseidon turns their ship to stone and means to cover their city with a great mountain, and the poem leaves them praying at his altar and does not say what became of them (13.149–187). Aristophanes, the Alexandrian editor, read Zeus' words so that he forbids the mountain (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.158; see the translation's note on 13.158): [[aristotle-and-the-wall]], [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[phaeacians]].
