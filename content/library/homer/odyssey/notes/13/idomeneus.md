summary: In Odysseus' false tale to Athena, Idomeneus is the father of the Cretan's victim: the stranger says that he killed Orsilochus, the son of Idomeneus, who tried to rob him of his spoil from Troy because he would not serve Idomeneus at Troy as his attendant (13.259–266).
# Ἰδομενῆ* (in the entry) claims Ἰδομενῆος (13.259). Unnamed: "his father" (13.265).

**The father in the tale.** Posing as a Cretan, Odysseus tells Athena, whom he takes for a young herdsman, “I am an exile, since I killed the dear son of Idomeneus, / swift-footed Orsilochus” (13.259–260; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]], [[odysseus]] and [[crete]]). The son wished to rob him of his spoil from Troy, “because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (13.265–266). θεράπευον is “served as θεράπων”, and the negative covers χαριζόμενος too, “I did not court his favour by serving” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.265; see the translation's note on 13.265). The Iliad's Idomeneus had such an attendant, Meriones, “the good attendant of Idomeneus” (*Iliad* 23.528; 13.246; see [[meriones]]), who led the eighty Cretan ships with him (*Iliad* 2.645–652). The man of the tale claims a place beside him, not under him.

**The tale and the facts.** Nestor had heard that Idomeneus brought all his companions home to Crete (3.191–192), and the tale puts the quarrel there, after the war, over the spoil of Troy. It is the first of the stories in which Odysseus poses as a Cretan and borrows the Cretan king. To Eumaeus he will say that the people made him and Idomeneus lead the ships to Ilios (14.237–239, a plain rendering), and to Penelope that he is Aethon, the younger brother of Idomeneus, “both older and braver” (19.181–184, a plain rendering). The false stories told by Ulysses, Monro observes, all turn on events and characters of the Trojan war, as do the songs of the Odyssey's singers, while the Iliad's few digressions belong to other cycles of legend (Monro, Appendix I §3, citing 13.256 ff., 14.235 ff., 14.468 ff. and 19.172 ff.; see [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Outside Homer.** The Suda explains Κρητίζειν, “to play the Cretan”, as “to lie”: “For Idomeneus, entrusted with dividing the bronze from the spoils, picked out the best for himself”; another proverb is “to play the Cretan with Cretans”, since they are liars and cheats (Suda κ 2407): the quarrel over spoil and the lie are both Cretan in the proverb. A rationalized story in the Suda, under Χάρυβδις, joins the tale's people to Odysseus' real return: after Charybdis some Phoenicians saw him floating on a plank, took him up, and brought him naked to Idomeneus in Crete, who entertained him through the winter and sent him to Phaeacia, “now called Corcyra” (Suda χ 144; see [[phoenicians]] and [[scheria]]).
