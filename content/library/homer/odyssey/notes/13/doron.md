---
summary: The Phaeacians' gifts: those already in the chest (13.10–12), “an escort and gifts of friendship” (13.41), the “untold gifts” of which Poseidon complains (13.135); and the gifts that Odysseus promises the Naiads (13.358).
---

**The Phaeacians' gifts.** The gifts fill the first half of the book. Alcinous points to those already given, “Clothes for the stranger lie in the polished chest, / and intricately worked gold and all the other / gifts that the counselors of the Phaeacians brought here” (13.10–12; see [[boule]] and [[chest]]), and asks for a tripod and a cauldron more from each of the kings (13.13–15; see [[charis]]). Odysseus' farewell names what he has received: “for now all is accomplished that my dear heart desired, / an escort and gifts of friendship; and may the heavenly gods / make them prosper for me” (πομπὴ καὶ φίλα δῶρα, 13.40–42; see [[pompe]], [[olbos]] and [[heavenly-gods]]), the pair that Alcinous had named as the host's due, “the escort and the gifts of friendship” (8.545; see [[xenia]]). To Poseidon the same gifts are the offense: the Phaeacians “gave him untold gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (ἄσπετα δῶρα, 13.135–137; see [[untold]] and [[aisa]]), where some manuscripts read ἀγλαά, “splendid” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 13.135). Odysseus counts them on the shore and finds nothing missing, and Athena helps him hide them in the cave of the nymphs (13.215–219, 13.361–371; see [[possessions]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).

**Gifts to the nymphs.** When the mist is gone, Odysseus prays to the Naiads: “and we will give you gifts as well, as before, / if the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil, readily grants / that I myself may live, and makes my dear son grow” (δῶρα διδώσομεν, 13.358–360; see [[nymphs]], [[prayer]] and [[driver-of-spoil]]). The plural associates Telemachus with him, as the next verse shows, and for διδώσομεν Aristophanes read παρέξομεν (Monro on 13.358 and his critical note). Athena had reminded him of the hecatombs he used to offer them in the cave (13.349–350; see [[hecatomb]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]): the gifts promised are the renewal of a custom.
