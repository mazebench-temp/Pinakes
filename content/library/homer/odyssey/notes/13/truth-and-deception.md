summary: Told that he is in Ithaca, Odysseus “did not speak the truth” but told the tale of a Cretan exile (13.254–286). Athena smiles at the lie and names him the master of tricks and thievish words, as she is famed among the gods for cunning (13.287–302). He fears that she is deceiving him until she shows him the land (13.324–352), and their plan is a deception, the beggar's disguise (13.397–403).
# "ἀληθέα εἶπε" (13.254), only here; bare ἀληθέα is left alone.
grc: ["ἀληθέα εἶπε"]
en: [did not speak the truth]
refs: [13.254-286, 13.291-302, 13.324-328, 13.397-403]
---

**The lie.** The shepherd has told him the name of the land, and he is glad, “yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word, / always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (13.254–255; see [[kerdos]] and [[noos]]). He took back his speech, that is, he left unsaid what he would have said if he had spoken the truth (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.254), in the words with which Agamemnon took back his rebuke of Odysseus in the Iliad (*Iliad* 4.357). The tale he tells instead is made from true things: Crete and Idomeneus, the spoil of Troy, Phoenician sailors, and at the end a man asleep on the sand with his goods beside him, as the Phaeacians had left Odysseus a few hours before (13.256–286, 13.116–124; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). Its sleep is told in the verse of his own sleep before the bag of the winds was opened (13.282 = 10.31). The poem will say of his later tales that he “made the many lies he told like the truth” (19.203, a plain rendering; see the entry).

**The answer.** Athena smiles at it, strokes him with her hand and takes a woman's shape, and answers the lie with praise: only a crafty man and a cheat could get past him in tricks, and even in his own land he will not leave off “your deceits / and thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up” (13.287–295; see [[crafty-would-he-be]], [[apate]] and [[dolos]]). The two are matched, “since we both know / crafty ways” (13.296–297), and yet the liar was deceived: “yet you did not know / Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus” (13.299–300; see [[shape-changing]]).

**Fear of deceit.** Now it is Odysseus who suspects a lie. “I do not think / that I have come to clear-seen Ithaca, but I am roaming some other / land; and I think that you are taunting me / in saying these things, to deceive my mind— / tell me if it is true that I have come to my dear native land” (13.324–328; see [[eperopeuo]]). Athena answers not with an oath but with the sight of the place: the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave and Mount Neritum, and then she scatters the mist (13.344–352; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The deception planned.** The rest of the book plans a long deceit. He is to tell no one, man or woman, that he has come home, and to suffer in silence (13.307–310; see [[silence]] and [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]), and Athena makes him “unknown to all mortals”, an old beggar, “so that you may seem unsightly to all the suitors / and to your wife and your son, whom you left in your halls” (13.397–403; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).
