**The threats remembered.** “But the earth-shaker / did not forget the threats which he had made against godlike Odysseus / at first” (13.125–127): πρῶτον is “once”, as again in 13.133 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.127; see [[poseidon]] and [[earth-shaker]]). The threats are those of the fifth book, when the god saw Odysseus on his raft near the land of the Phaeacians: “But I say I will yet give him his fill of misery” (5.286–290, 5.377–379); the cause of his anger is the blinding of his son (1.68–75; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

**The complaint.** “Father Zeus, no longer shall I among the immortal gods / be held in honor, since mortals do not honor me at all, / the Phaeacians, who after all are sprung from my own stock” (13.128–130; see [[time]] and [[phaeacians]]). ὅ τε is “in respect that”, and τοί πέρ τοι “who after all are sprung from me”; τοι is the reading of one manuscript, where the vulgate has τε (Monro on 13.129, 13.130, and his critical note on 13.130). Their founder Nausithous was Poseidon's son (7.56–62; see [[nausithous]]). Poseidon had meant Odysseus to come home only after many sufferings: “For even now I said that Odysseus, after suffering many evils, / would come home”, in nearly the words of Halitherses' prophecy, φῆν κακὰ πολλὰ παθόντʼ … οἴκαδʼ ἐλεύσεσθαι (13.131–132; 2.174–176; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]), and “his homecoming I never took from him / altogether, since you first promised it and nodded your assent” (13.132–133; see [[zeus]]). Instead the Phaeacians carried him asleep and gave him untold gifts, “bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy, / if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (13.134–138), the verses in which Zeus decreed the gifts in the council of the gods (5.38–40; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). For ἄσπετα, “untold”, some manuscripts have ἀγλαά, “splendid”, the word of Odysseus' own account to his son (Monro's critical note on 13.135; 16.230; see [[untold]]).

**Zeus' answer.** “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!” (13.140), the verse in which Zeus answers Poseidon's complaint against the Achaean wall (*Iliad* 7.455; see [[the-achaean-wall]], [[o-popoi]] and [[shaker-of-the-earth]]). It would be hard “to assail the eldest and the best with acts of dishonor” (13.141–142): ἀτιμίῃσιν ἰάλλειν is “to fling into dishonour”, or better “to fling at, assail, with acts of dishonour”, ἰάλλω being elsewhere found only with an accusative of the thing thrown (Monro on 13.142). πρεσβύτατον, “the eldest”, makes Poseidon older than Zeus, where the Iliad makes Zeus the elder (*Iliad* 13.355, 15.166, 15.182). If any man, εἴκων, “giving way to” his own force and strength, does not honor him, “vengeance is always yours, even hereafter” (13.143–144; Monro on 13.143): there is a play of words between τίει, “pays honor”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of vengeance (Monro on 13.144). The text prints a period after τίει, which cuts the condition from its answer; Monro's text has a comma, and the English reads the two verses as one sentence (see the translation's note on 13.144). “Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (13.145).

**The proposal.** Poseidon would do it at once, “but I always have regard for your spirit, and avoid it” (13.147–148). Now he wishes to shatter the beautiful ship of the Phaeacians “as it comes back from an escort on the misty sea” (13.149–150, as 8.568), “so that at last they may hold back and cease from escorting / men, and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (13.151–152; see [[pompe]] and [[misty]]). ἀμφικαλύψαι stands far from its governing verb, ἐθέλω in 13.149, and the scholia seem to show an ancient variant, probably the future indicative ἀμφικαλύψω; some manuscripts read πόλιν for πόλει (Monro on 13.152 and his critical note).

**Zeus' advice.** “My friend, this is what seems best to my heart”: when all the people see her from the city, to turn her to stone near the land, “like a swift ship, so that all men may marvel”, and to cover their city with a great mountain (13.154–158). The infinitives θεῖναι and ἀμφικαλύψαι explain δοκεῖ εἶναι ἄριστα, so that the sentence has no grammatical apodosis: “as seems best to me, viz. to turn their ship into stone” (Monro on 13.154–158). For μέγα δέ in the last verse Aristophanes read μηδέ, “and not to cover”, no doubt in view of the prayers of the Phaeacian elders (13.183); “but these need not affect what Zeus says now” (Monro on 13.158; see the translation's note on 13.158). The English follows the text, in which Zeus approves both the stone and the mountain.

**The stone.** “But when Poseidon the earth-shaker heard this” (13.159), the verse that introduces him in the Iliad when he goes to save Aeneas (*Iliad* 20.318), “he went to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live” (13.160; see [[scheria]]). The ship comes near ῥίμφα διωκομένη, “coursing swiftly along”, as one says ἅρμα διώκειν of driving a chariot (13.161–162; Monro on 13.162; see the translation's note on 13.162), and the god “turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath / with a blow of the flat of his hand; and then he was gone” (13.163–164). ὅς μιν λᾶαν ἔθηκε, “turned her to stone”, is said in the Iliad of the serpent at Aulis, λᾶαν γάρ μιν ἔθηκε (*Iliad* 2.319; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]); χειρὶ καταπρηνεῖ, “with the flat of his hand”, is the gesture with which Odysseus, waking, strikes his thighs on the shore of Ithaca (13.199).

**The Phaeacians' wonder.** “And they spoke winged words to one another, / the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships” (13.165–166, the verse of 8.191 and 8.369; see [[winged-words]], [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). Each man says to his neighbor, “Ah me, who has fettered the swift ship on the sea / as she was driven home? And she was already in plain sight” (13.167–169; see [[tis-speech]]); ἐπέδησε, “fettered”, is the verb of the fate that fetters a man (see the translation's key-word row for πεδάω). “So a man would say; but they did not know how things had been brought about” (13.170), the verse that closes the suitors' boast that Penelope, preparing her marriage, does not know that slaughter has been made ready for her son (4.769–772).

**Alcinous.** He knows. “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me, / the oracles of my father” (13.172–173; see [[thesphaton]] and [[o-popoi]]): the verse with which the blinded Cyclops recognized the prophecy of Telemus (9.507). ἀγάσασθαι, “begrudged”, is “had been surprised”, that is, offended or made jealous (Monro on 13.173; see the translation's note on 13.173 and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Monro distinguishes ἔφασκε, “alleged”, in which the father's general belief is reported, from φῆ, “said”: the prophecy proper begins at φῆ, and with it the need for a future infinitive; a few manuscripts have ἀγάασθαι, probably an ancient variant, and the ἀγάσσεσθαι of modern editions has little support (on 13.173 and his critical note). The verses repeat his words in the eighth book, with περικαλλέα, “beautiful”, for ἐυεργέα, “well-made”, which three manuscripts read here too (13.173–177, after 8.565–569; Monro's critical note on 13.175). There he added that the god might fulfill it or leave it unfulfilled (8.570–571); here the old man's word is fulfilled, τὰ δὲ δὴ νῦν πάντα τελεῖται (13.178; see [[prophecy]]).

**The bulls.** “Come then, let us all do as I say” (13.179), the verse with which Agamemnon, Polydamas, Poseidon and Hector call for a decision in the Iliad (*Iliad* 2.139, 12.75, 14.370, 18.297): they are to stop escorting mortals who come to their city, and to sacrifice twelve picked bulls to Poseidon, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃ, “in the hope that he may have pity”, and not cover the city with a towering mountain (13.180–183; see [[sacrifice]] and [[pity]]). The Trojan women vowed twelve cows to Athena in the same words, αἴ κʼ ἐλεήσῃς, and Athena refused them (*Iliad* 6.308–311). For παύσασθε some manuscripts read the present παύεσθε (Monro's critical note on 13.180). They are afraid and make the bulls ready, and “the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians” stand praying to lord Poseidon around the altar (13.184–187; see [[prayer]] and [[altar]]). The poem does not tell the sacrifice or its outcome: in the middle of the verse it turns from the altar to Odysseus waking in Ithaca, ὁ δʼ ἔγρετο δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “And brilliant Odysseus woke” (13.187; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).
