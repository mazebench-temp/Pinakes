summary: The Phaeacians sacrifice an ox to Zeus on their last day with Odysseus and pray to him over the parting libation (13.24–26, 13.51–52). When Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians have brought Odysseus home, Zeus lets him do as he wishes and counsels him to turn their ship to stone in sight of the city (13.125–158). On Ithaca the waking Odysseus calls on “Zeus, the god of suppliants” to punish the Phaeacians (13.213–214), and in the rest of the book Zeus is named as the father of Athena and of the Naiads.

**The ox for Zeus.** On the last day in Scheria “the sacred might of Alcinous sacrificed an ox / to Zeus, lord of the dark clouds, son of Cronus, who rules over all, / and when they had burned the thigh-pieces they feasted on a glorious feast” (13.24–26; see [[sacrifice]], [[lord-of-dark-clouds]], [[son-of-cronus]] and [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]). The second verse is the one in which Odysseus, escaped from the Cyclops, sacrificed the ram on the shore to a Zeus who “took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.552–555; see the Book 9 note); this time it is the Phaeacians' offering, on the day they send him home alone. D. B. Monro notes the play of language in ἱέρευσʼ ἱερόν, “sacrificed … sacred”, of the kind that he finds again in Zeus' τίει … τίσις (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.24 and 13.144). At sunset Alcinous bids Pontonous mix the wine, “so that when we have prayed to father Zeus / we may send the stranger on his way to his native land” (13.51–52; see [[father-zeus]], [[libation]] and [[prayer]]); in Book 7 the same verses had called for a libation to Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.179–181; see the Book 7 note).

**Poseidon asks for leave.** When the Phaeacians have set Odysseus ashore, the earth-shaker remembers his threats “and he asked Zeus his plan” (13.127; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]), the half-verse with which he came up out of the sea to the assembly of the gods in the Iliad and asked Zeus why he had called them (*Iliad* 20.13–15). He grants Zeus his part in the homecoming: he never took it from Odysseus altogether, “since you first promised it and nodded your assent” (13.132–133). πρῶτον is “once” (Monro on 13.127). At the first council of the poem Zeus had said that Poseidon would let go his anger, since he could not strive alone against all the immortals (1.76–79; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and at the second he had foretold the Phaeacians' gifts in the very verses with which Poseidon now complains of them, “bronze and gold in plenty, … / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (13.136–138, after 5.38–40).

**Zeus' answer.** “Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said” (13.139) is the verse of his answer to Poseidon at that assembly (*Iliad* 20.19; see [[cloud-gatherer]]), and the next is his answer to Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall, “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!” (13.140 = *Iliad* 7.455; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]], [[wide-in-strength]], [[o-popoi]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). Then: “The gods do not dishonor you at all; it would be hard / to assail the eldest and the best with acts of dishonor. / But if any man, yielding to his own force and strength, / does not honor you at all, vengeance is always yours, even hereafter. / Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (13.141–145; see [[time]]). ἀτιμίῃσιν ἰάλλειν is “to fling at, assail, with acts of dishonour”; εἴκων is “allowing himself to be moved by”; and there is a play of words between τίει, “pays honour”, and τίσις, “payment” in the sense of vengeance (Monro on 13.142–144; see the translation's note on 13.144). ἔρξον ὅπως ἐθέλεις, “Do as you wish”, is his leave to Hera when she asks for the ruin of Troy (*Iliad* 4.37), and to Athena when she asks at the end of the poem whether he will bring war or friendship to Ithaca (24.473–481). On “the eldest” see the Book 13 note on [[poseidon]].

The infinitives are an epexegesis of δοκεῖ εἶναι ἄριστα, so that ὡς μέν has no grammatical apodosis: “as seems best to me, viz. to turn their ship into stone, &c.” (Monro on 13.154–158). In the Iliad Zeus himself turned the serpent of Aulis to stone, “and we stood there and marveled at what had come to pass” (*Iliad* 2.319–320; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]); here he leaves the deed to Poseidon and keeps the marvel, ἵνα θαυμάζωσιν ἅπαντες, “so that all men may marvel”. On Aristophanes' reading of the last verse, which would have Zeus forbid the mountain, see the story (Monro on 13.158).

**The god of suppliants.** Odysseus wakes, does not know his land, and thinks that the Phaeacians have broken their word: “May Zeus, the god of suppliants, punish them, who watches over / other men as well, and punishes whoever does wrong” (13.213–214; see [[supplication]] and [[poine]]). ἱκετήσιος is found only here in Homer (see [[god-of-suppliants]], the translation's key-word row on Ζεὺς ἱκετήσιος, and the Book 13 note on [[supplication]] for Aristarchus' optative and Zenodotus' imperative). ἐφορᾷ, “watches over”, is the gods' office in the warning that one of the suitors will give Antinous, that the gods go about the cities in the likeness of strangers, “watching over the arrogance and the lawfulness of men” (17.485–487, a plain rendering; see [[hybris]]). The poet has just shown Zeus giving Poseidon leave to punish the Phaeacians for the very escort that Odysseus thinks they have betrayed (13.141–158). Aeschylus calls him ἱκέσιος and ἱκτήρ: in his *Suppliants* the daughters of Danaus warn the king of Argos that “heavy indeed is the anger of Zeus of suppliants”, Ζηνὸς ἱκεσίου κότος, and the king concludes that “we must respect the anger of Zeus, / protector of suppliants: it is mortals' highest fear”, Ζηνὸς αἰδεῖσθαι κότον / ἱκτῆρος (*Suppliants* 347, 478–479, and again 616; see [[aeschylus]]).

**The father.** In the second half of the book Zeus is named chiefly as a father. Athena is Παλλὰς Ἀθηναίη, κούρη Διός, “Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus”, or “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (13.190, 13.252, 13.300, 13.371; see [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[aegis-bearing]]), Odysseus calls her “daughter of Zeus” (13.318) and in his prayer “the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil” (13.359; see [[driver-of-spoil]]), and the Naiads are “daughters of Zeus” (13.356; see [[nymphs]]). He implores Athena “by your father” (13.324; see [[supplication]]), and she will not fight Poseidon, “my father's brother” (13.342; see [[athena]]).
