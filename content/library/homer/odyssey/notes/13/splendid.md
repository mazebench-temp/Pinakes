---
summary: Athena changes her form: “and she was like a woman in form, / beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (13.288–289).
---

ἀγλαὰ ἔργα ἰδυῖα, the woman's skill, which in Homer is above all weaving: the same words describe the fine work of Circe's loom, “the fine and lovely and splendid works of goddesses” (10.223), and in this book the Naiads weave sea-purple webs in their cave (13.107–108; see [[weaving]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The couplet returns when the goddess appears to Odysseus at the swineherd's hut (16.157–158), and its second verse describes the Phoenician woman of Eumaeus' story (15.417–418). Two manuscripts omit 13.289 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.289). See [[divine-epiphany]] and [[athena]].
