---
summary: Odysseus fears that Athena speaks “to deceive my mind” (13.327); she answers, “do not let these things trouble your mind” (13.362), and he asks why she, “who know all things in your mind”, did not tell his son (13.417).
---

**A mind deceived.** Told that he is in Ithaca, Odysseus suspects the goddess: “and I think that you are taunting me / in saying these things, to deceive my mind” (ἵνʼ ἐμὰς φρένας ἠπεροπεύσῃς, 13.326–327; see [[eperopeuo]] and [[kertomia]]). In the verses before, which are generally condemned, he says he wandered after Troy “bearing a torn heart in my breast” (φρεσὶν ᾗσιν, 13.320; see [[etor]]); Aristarchus athetized the verse, in which ᾗσιν stands for ἐμῇσιν, “my”, a use of the possessive that Walter Leaf, as Monro reports, took to be a relic of the oldest text (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix IV §16; Monro on 13.320–323).

**A mind untroubled.** When he has kissed the earth and prayed to the nymphs, Athena answers him: “Take heart; do not let these things trouble your mind” (μή τοι ταῦτα μετὰ φρεσὶ σῇσι μελόντων, 13.362; see [[tharsos]]), the verse with which Hephaestus received Thetis when she came to ask for armor for her son (*Iliad* 18.463).

**A mind that knows.** Told that his son is in Sparta, Odysseus asks, “Why then did you not tell him, you who know all things in your mind?” (ἐνὶ φρεσὶ πάντα ἰδυῖα, 13.417; see [[telemachus]] and [[kleos]]), and she answers that she sent him to win fame (13.421–423).
