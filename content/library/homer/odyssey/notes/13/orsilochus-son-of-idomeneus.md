summary: In his first false tale on Ithaca, Odysseus, posing as a Cretan exile, tells Athena that he killed Orsilochus, the swift-footed son of Idomeneus, from ambush by night, because Orsilochus wished to rob him of his spoil from Troy (13.259–271).

**The exile.** Athena, in the likeness of a young herdsman, has told the stranger that he is on Ithaca; he is glad, “yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word, / always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (13.254–255; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]], [[odysseus]] and [[athena]]). He has heard of Ithaca in Crete, he says, and has come with these goods, leaving as much again to his children:

{{quote:559bb847-225f-509e-a52d-5234a048eb1b}}

φεύγω is the word of the man who must leave his country because he has killed (see the translation's key words, [[exile-and-refuge]] and [[idomeneus]]). Orsilochus is given the Iliad's formula of Achilles, πόδας ὠκύς, in a case it never has there, and a runner's fame among the ἀλφησταί, the “toiling men” of the earth (see [[swift-footed]] and [[toiling-men]]).

**The quarrel.** He “wished to rob me of all my spoil / from Troy, for which I suffered sorrows in my heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves, / because I would not court his father's favor by serving as his attendant / in the land of the Trojans, but led other companions of my own” (13.262–266). τῆς ληΐδος, “my spoil”, is perhaps the article in a possessive sense, and θεράπευον is “served as θεράπων”, the negative covering χαριζόμενος too, “I did not court his favour by serving” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.262, 13.265; see the translation's note on 13.265). The Cretan claims to have been a leader at Troy in his own right. In the Iliad the man who served Idomeneus so was Meriones, “the good attendant of Idomeneus” (*Iliad* 23.528; 13.246; see [[meriones]]), who led the eighty Cretan ships with him (*Iliad* 2.645–652). The middle verse, the sufferings of the war and the sea, is the one the poet used of Odysseus himself asleep on the Phaeacian ship (13.264 = 13.91).

**The ambush.** “Him I struck with my bronze-fitted spear as he came down / from the country, lying in ambush near the road with one companion. / A very dark night covered the sky, and no one / of men noticed us, and I took his life unseen” (13.267–270; see [[ambush]], [[spear]] and [[bronze-fitted]]). ἀγρόθεν goes with κατιόντα, as at 15.428 (Monro on 13.268). Then he begged passage from Phoenicians, who were to set him down at Pylos or Elis (13.271–275; see [[phoenicians]]). The rest of the tale, the wind, the landing by night and the sleep on the sand, is Odysseus' own coming to Ithaca told over again (see [[odysseus]]).
