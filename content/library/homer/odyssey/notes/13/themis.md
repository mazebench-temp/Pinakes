---
summary: Waking in a land he does not know, Odysseus asks whether its people are “unjust” (13.201), and judges the Phaeacians, who have in fact brought him home, “not altogether discerning or just” (13.209).
---

Odysseus' first words on Ithaca are the question of his landings in Scheria and among the Cyclopes: “Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (ἦ ῥʼ οἵ γʼ ὑβρισταί τε καὶ ἄγριοι οὐδὲ δίκαιοι, 13.201–202 = 6.120–121, 9.175–176; see [[hybris]], [[xenia]] and [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). δίκαιος is the man who keeps to δίκη, what is right (see the entry): among strangers, the man who will not harm a guest. A few verses later the word is turned on his hosts: “Ah, then they were not altogether discerning or just, / the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians, / who have brought me away to another land; truly they told me / they would bring me to clear-seen Ithaca, and they have not done it” (οὐδὲ δίκαιοι, 13.209–212; see [[hegemon]], [[noos]] and [[phaeacians]]). οὐκ ἄρα … ἦσαν is “they are not, as I thought they were” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.209). He calls on Zeus, who “punishes whoever does wrong” (13.214; see [[poine]]), against men who have done him none: the injustice he sees is the work of Athena's mist, which hides his own land from him (13.189–196; see [[eidos]]).
