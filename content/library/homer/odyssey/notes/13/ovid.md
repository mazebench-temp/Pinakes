---
summary: In the Metamorphoses the ships of Aeneas, changed into sea nymphs, watch with glad faces as the ship of Alcinous stiffens and stone grows over its wood, and Aeneas sails past the realm of “deceitful Ulysses”. In exile Ovid sets his own sufferings against those of “the Neritian leader”, who had a warrior goddess to help him, whose labors were mostly fiction, and who at last reached the household gods he sought.
---

- **The ship of Alcinous.** When Turnus sets fire to the fleet of Aeneas, the Mother of the gods, remembering that its pines were cut on Ida, breaks the ships' cables and sinks them, and they rise again as sea nymphs, their sterns turned into faces, their oars into fingers and legs, their keels into backbones (*Metamorphoses* 14.527–557). The nymphs help ships in danger, unless one carries Greeks; still remembering the fall of Troy, they hate the Pelasgians, “and with glad faces they saw the wreckage of the Neritian ship, and with glad faces saw the ship of Alcinous stiffen and stone grow over the wood” (*Neritiaeque ratis viderunt fragmina laetis / vultibus et laetis videre rigescere puppim / vultibus Alcinoi saxumque increscere ligno*, *Metamorphoses* 14.559–565). The Neritian ship is Odysseus' own, broken by the thunderbolt (12.403–419); the ship of Alcinous is the Phaeacian ship that brought him to Ithaca, which Poseidon turned to stone with a blow of his hand as it came home (13.159–164). Ovid sets one metamorphosis against another, ships turned into nymphs and a ship turned into stone: [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], [[alcinous]] and [[shape-changing]].
- **The realm of deceitful Ulysses.** On their way from Crete to Italy, Ovid's Trojans sail past “the harbors of Dulichium, and Ithaca and Samos, and the Neritian houses, the realm of deceitful Ulysses” (*regnum fallacis Ulixis*, *Metamorphoses* 13.711–713, a plain rendering). In this book Athena calls Odysseus a man who would not leave off, “even in your own land”, his deceits and thievish words (13.293–295): [[ithaca]], [[neritum]] and [[dolos]].
- **The Neritian leader.** Writing to a faithful friend from exile, Ovid bids the learned poets write of his woes instead of those of “the Neritian leader”, for he has suffered more. Ulysses wandered for many years over a narrow space, between Dulichium and Troy; he had a loyal band of companions; he sought his country in joy and as a victor; his Ithaca was a place from which absence was no great punishment; “the warrior goddess brought him help” (*bellatrix illi diva ferebat opem*), and the anger that pressed him was Neptune's, not Jupiter's. “Add that the greatest part of his labors is fiction”, while in Ovid's woes there is no fable, and “at last he touched the household gods he sought, and reached the fields he had long sought” (*denique quaesitos tetigit tamen ille Penates, / quaeque diu petiit, contigit arva tamen*, *Tristia* 1.5.57–84, plain renderings). The goddess of this book stands by him “in all your toils” (13.300–301), and he kisses the grain-giving earth of his own land (13.354): [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]], [[athena]] and [[odysseus]].
