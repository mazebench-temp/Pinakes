**The last song.** On the day of the escort, after the sacrifice of an ox to Zeus, the Phaeacians “feasted on a glorious feast, / delighting in it; and among them a divine singer sang / Demodocus, honored by the people. But Odysseus / kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun” (13.26–29; see [[feast]], [[divine]], [[singers]] and [[odysseus]]). The middle verse is the one that brings in the singer at Menelaus' wedding feast and the singer on the Shield of Achilles, where it goes on “to the lyre” (4.17; *Iliad* 18.604; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.27, compares 4.17, and refers to Lehrs on μέλπεσθαι used of singing). Here it goes on with his name, and with the words that honored him in Book 8, now in the nominative: Δημόδοκος, λαοῖσι τετιμένος, “Demodocus, honored by the people” (13.28; 8.472), which W. W. Merry took for an interpretation of the name (see the entry).

**Unheard.** What he sings is not told. In Book 8 his songs of the quarrel at Troy and of the Wooden Horse brought the tears that betrayed the stranger, and led the king to ask his name (8.83–95, 8.521–586; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Now the stranger, who has since told his own tale, is not listening: his eyes are on the sun, “in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (13.30; see [[nostos]]). Demodocus is not named again in the poem.
