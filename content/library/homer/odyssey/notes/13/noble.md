---
summary: The crew who take Odysseus home are “the noble escorts” (13.71); the gifts are those “which the noble Phaeacians / had given him as he went home” (13.120–121), as Athena says again (13.304–305); and in the false tale “noble Phoenicians” take the Cretan exile on board (13.272–273).
---

πομπῆες ἀγαυοί names the crew by their office, as κήρυκες ἀγαυοί names the heralds (8.418): πομπῆες, “escorts,” beside πομποί in Alcinous' memory of his father's prophecy (13.174; see the Odyssey translation's key words, and [[pompe]]). Φαίηκες ἀγαυοί, the people who called the king to the council (6.55), ends the verse of the gifts twice, in the narrator's words, “through great-hearted Athena” (13.120–121), and in the goddess's own, “by my counsel and my mind” (13.304–305); some manuscripts close 13.369 with the same verse-end and add 13.121 after it (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 13.369; see [[phaeacians]] and [[possessions]]). The Phoenicians of the false tale are noble too: they “did not wish to deceive” the man who begged them for passage, and were driven to Ithaca against their will (13.272–278; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). The Phoenician of the next Cretan tale is “skilled in deceits, / a greedy rogue” (14.288–289, a plain rendering; see [[phoenicians]]).
