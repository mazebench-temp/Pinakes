summary: Book 8 opens with the escort the stranger asked for. Alcinous proposes it to the assembly, “as has been our custom”, and a black ship on her first voyage is launched with fifty-two young men (8.28–55). At the evening feast the escort and the gifts are named together (8.545), and Alcinous describes the ships that need no helmsmen, and his father's prophecy against the people who are “escorts of all men” (8.555–571).

**The proposal.** Alcinous presents the stranger to the Phaeacian leaders: “he urges an escort, and begs that it be made sure” (8.30). ἔμπεδον εἶναι is “that it should be assured”: a promised boon is certain beforehand when it is promised, and afterward when the promise holds good (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.30; see the translation's note on 8.30). The king's answer is the custom of his people: “So let us, as has been our custom, urge on an escort. / For no other man either, who comes to my house, / stays here long, mourning, for want of an escort” (8.31–33), with πάρος in its idiomatic sense of custom (Merry on 8.31; see the translation's note on 8.31, 8.36). The ship is to be “on her first voyage”, and two and fifty young men, the best, are to be chosen to row her (8.34–36); they drag her down, set the mast and the sails, fit the oars in their leather straps and moor her out in the water before they go to the feast (8.48–55; see [[ships]] and [[oars]]). Laodamas reminds the stranger at the games that his journey is near: “already / the ship has been drawn down, and the companions are ready” (8.150–151).

**Escort and gifts.** At the evening feast Alcinous names what has been made ready for the revered stranger, “the escort and the gifts of friendship, which we give him, making him welcome” (8.545; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[xenia]]). It was not improper to count the escort among the things before them, since the farewell supper at which the keepsakes were presented was, as it were, the first act of the escort (Merry on 8.544).

**Ships that know men's minds.** The second question, the stranger's land, people and city, is asked for the sake of the escort: “so that our ships may carry you there, aiming with their minds” (8.555–556). τιτυσκόμεναι is literally “aiming at it with their minds”, as Antilochus “took aim in his mind / either to throw at someone, or to rush at him close at hand” (*Iliad* 13.558–559); these marvelous ships, endowed with human sense and anticipating the will of their masters, flit unseen over the sea, and that is the meaning of “wrapped in mist and cloud” (Merry on 8.556; see the translation's note on 8.556–563, [[steering-oar]] and [[phrenes]]). Merry compares the legend of the Flying Dutchman, and reports that Welcker saw in the Phaeacians and their ghostly ships, which carry Odysseus home in a deep sleep, a Greek form of the northern legend of the ferrymen of the dead, carrying souls over to the island of Brittia, as Procopius tells it; but that dismal office, Merry answers, seems little suited to “the gay and easy Phaeacians, who are πομποὶ ἀπήμονες”, and the mist is only the natural concealment of the working of supernatural power (on 8.556).

**Escorts of all men.** Alcinous ends with his father's prophecy. Nausithous used to say that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians, “because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (πομποὶ ἀπήμονές εἰμεν ἁπάντων, 8.565–566): Poseidon was jealous of their immunity from harm, which amounted to independence of his authority and power (Merry on 8.566; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]] and [[poseidon]]). One day he would shatter a well-made ship of theirs “as it came back from an escort on the misty sea”, and cover their city round about with a great mountain (8.567–569): not a mountain hurled upon the city, but a mountain wall that would pen them in, cut them off from the water and put an end to their seafaring life (Merry on 8.569; see the translation's note on 8.569). “These things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.570–571), a truism that Merry likens to Tiresias' *aut erit aut non* in Horace (on 8.571). The escort that is promised in this book brings the first half of the prophecy to pass in Book 13, when Poseidon turns the returning ship to stone (13.159–164; see [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).
