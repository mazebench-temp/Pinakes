**Poseidon's grudge.** Alcinous has just told the stranger that the Phaeacian ships need no helmsmen, know the minds of men, and cross the sea wrapped in mist and cloud, “and never do they / have any fear of coming to harm or of perishing” (8.557–563; see [[phaeacians]] and [[pompe]]). Then he remembers his father's warning:

{{quote:d33f4ce4-73b7-5faa-9c41-f66d384f57b0}}

ἀγάσασθαι, “begrudged”, is the verb of the gods' grudge in the Odyssey, Calypso's word for the gods who begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands (5.119, 5.122, 5.129; see the Book 5 note) and Menelaus' for the god who begrudged him Odysseus' company (4.181–182; see the translation's key-word row on ἄγαμαι). What Poseidon begrudges is what the verse names, πομποὶ ἀπήμονες, escorts of all men who come to no harm themselves (8.566): Merry explains that their immunity from harm was equivalent to independence of his authority and power (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.566; see [[poseidon]]). The punishment would take away what the god gave, the crossing of the sea (7.35; see the Book 7 note): Merry takes the mountain as a wall to pen them in and cut off their access to the water, not a mountain hurled on the city (on 8.569; see the translation's note on 8.569). Alcinous leaves the outcome to the god's pleasure, “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” (8.570–571), and goes on with his questions (see [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** The grudge comes to pass in Book 13. When the Phaeacians have carried Odysseus home, Poseidon complains to Zeus that he will no longer be honored among the gods when mortals do not honor him, and Zeus lets him turn their ship to stone in sight of the city; Alcinous recognizes his father's prophecy in the verses of this book, and the Phaeacians give up their escorts and sacrifice twelve bulls to Poseidon, in the hope that he will not cover their city with the mountain (13.125–187; see the Book 8 note of [[poseidon]]). As with Orion and Iasion in Calypso's protest (5.121–128), the grudge falls on mortals who have come too near what belongs to the gods: the Phaeacians are “near kin to the gods” (5.35; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]), the gods appear to them in plain sight, “since we are near to them” (7.201–205), and they cross the sea unharmed.
