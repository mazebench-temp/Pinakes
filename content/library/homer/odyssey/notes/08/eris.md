summary: The book's strife is rivalry. The stranger will not bring strife on his host in the contests (8.210), will not vie with the men of former days, Heracles and Eurytus, who vied even with the immortals in archery (8.223–225), and Halius and Laodamas dance alone, “since no one vied with them” (8.371).
# ἔριδα (8.210; again 6.92), ἐριζέμεν (8.223), ἐρίζεσκον (8.225), ἔριζεν (8.371).
grc: [ἔριδα, ἐριζέμεν, ἐρίζεσκον, ἔριζεν]
en: [strife, vie, vied]
---

ἔρις in Book 8 is the strife of contest, as Nausicaa and her maids trample the clothes in the pits, “vying with one another in strife” (ἔριδα προφέρουσαι, 6.92). The stranger names its limit in a maxim: the man who “brings on strife in contests with his host / in a foreign land” is witless (ἔριδα προφέρηται ἀέθλων, 8.210–211), and Merry compares Sophocles' ἔριδος ἀγών (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.210; see [[strife-with-ones-host]]). He will vie with any man now living, but not with the men of former days, who vied even with the immortals in archery (8.221–225); ἐρίζεσκον only means “matched themselves”, and of the two only Eurytus actually contended with a god, and died for it (Merry on 8.225; see [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[men-of-today]]). At the dancing Alcinous bids Halius and Laodamas dance alone, “since no one vied with them” (8.370–371; see [[halius-son-of-alcinous]] and [[ball]]). The quarrel of the first song is not ἔρις but νεῖκος, and its verb δηρίομαι, “contend” (8.75–78; see [[neikos]]).
