**The farewell.** After his bath, as the stranger goes among the men drinking wine, Nausicaa, “who had her beauty from the gods”, stands by the doorpost of the hall and marvels at him (8.457–459; see [[nausicaa]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]). It is her last appearance in the poem. ἵνα is final: “may it be well with thee, stranger, in order that” you may remember me; she wishes him well for his own sake, but the wish is quickened by affection, which now takes the form of a hope that she may live in his memory as his benefactress (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.461; see the translation's note on 8.461–462 and [[memory-and-the-muses]]). πρώτῃ, “to me first”: she was the first to help him, on the shore in Book 6 (see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).

**The debt.** ζῳάγρια is a payment for a life. In its one use in the Iliad Hephaestus must pay Thetis “the full price for my life”, since she and Eurynome saved him when he fell from heaven (*Iliad* 18.405–407; see the entry). Merry, after Nitzsch, sets the two uses apart, taking the Iliad's to be the price paid to a conqueror for sparing a defeated foe, and Nausicaa's the debt of gratitude for a life saved by timely help, and finds that “the price of life” expresses both (Merry on 8.462); but in the Iliad passage too the debt is for a life saved, and Nausicaa uses the word as Hephaestus does. The price he paid was the armor of Achilles. Hers is to be remembered.

**The answer.** The stranger answers with a prayer: so may Zeus grant that he come home and see the day of his homecoming, and “then even there I would pray to you as to a god / always, all my days; for you gave me my life, maiden” (8.465–468; see [[euchomai]] and [[nostos]]). ἐβιώσαο is “didst give me my life”, more vivid than the scholium's “brought me into life” (Merry on 8.468). The debt is acknowledged in the word that names it, and the girl who saved him is to be honored as a god.
