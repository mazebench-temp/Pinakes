**The sack in the song.** The singer has told the Trojans' three counsels and the fate that the horse was to bring (8.505–513; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-wooden-horse]]); the sack follows in seven verses of reported song:

{{quote:9b19e946-f7e4-5650-a1c1-14c3dde24097}}

ἱππόθεν ἐκχύμενοι, κοῖλον λόχον ἐκπρολιπόντες, “pouring out from the horse, leaving the hollow ambush” (8.515): κοῖλος λόχος is Menelaus' name for the horse in his story of Helen's walk around it (4.277; see [[ambush]]). The city is αἰπήν, “steep” (8.516; see [[steep]]). Of all the leaders only two are named, Odysseus and Menelaus, and only one house, that of Deiphobus; the poem does not say why they went there (8.517–518; see [[deiphobus]] and [[menelaus]]).

**Through Athena.** νικῆσαι καὶ ἔπειτα is “did after all conquer”, καὶ ἔπειτα taking up the most dread fight that he dared, and διὰ … Ἀθήνην, “through great-hearted Athena”, has the accusative, as διά always has in Homer when it gives the cause (8.520; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.520; see [[athena]] and [[great-hearted]]). A scholium rationalizes the goddess away, taking “through Athena” as “through his own intelligence and cunning” (Merry on 8.520). The goddess who helped Epeius to make the horse (8.493) gives Odysseus his victory at the end of it.

**The other side.** At the song Odysseus weeps “as a woman weeps, flinging herself upon her dear husband, / who has fallen before his city and his people, / keeping off the pitiless day from his town and his children”, and the enemy drive her away into slavery (8.523–531; see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). The sack sung as a victory is pictured, in the weeping of the victor, from the side of the women of the sacked city (see [[women-and-war]]).

**Song for those yet to be.** Alcinous, not knowing whom he asks, asks why the stranger weeps “as you hear the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios. / This the gods wrought, and they spun destruction / for men, so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.578–580; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[troy]]). The fall of the city is the gods' work, and the song is its purpose.
