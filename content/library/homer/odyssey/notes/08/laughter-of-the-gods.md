---
summary: The song of Ares and Aphrodite is the Odyssey's scene of the gods' laughter. Hephaestus calls the gods to see “deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear”; when they see his snare, “unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods”, in the verse that ends the quarrel on Olympus in *Iliad* 1; Hermes' answer to Apollo makes them laugh again; only Poseidon does not laugh. The Phaeacians who hear the song are delighted.
refs: [8.307, 8.326-327, 8.343-344]

**Laughable deeds.** Hephaestus calls the gods: “come here, so that you may see deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear” (8.307; see [[hephaestus]] and [[olympians]]). ἔργα γελαστά is the reading of the manuscripts, of Aristarchus and of Herodian; Apollonius' lexicon, the *Etymologicum Magnum* and Eustathius have ἔργʼ ἀγέλαστα, “no laughing matter”, which Nauck follows (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 8.307). Merry prefers “deeds of mockery”: “deeds to raise your laughter”, as the gods' laughter at 8.326 might suggest, is hardly the tone of indignation the husband should take, and οὐκ ἐπιεικτά is “unyielding”, and so “hard”, “cruel” (on 8.307; see the translation's note on 8.307). The husband calls the gods to see an outrage, and they see a joke.

**Unquenchable laughter.** “and unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods / as they saw the craft of Hephaestus of much wisdom” (8.326–327; see [[unquenchable-laughter]]). The first verse ends the quarrel of Zeus and Hera in *Iliad* 1, where the gods laugh “as they saw Hephaestus bustling through the halls” (*Iliad* 1.599–600; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]). Both times Hephaestus is the cause, there by his bustling service as he pours their nectar (*Iliad* 1.597–598), here by his craft and his captives. One of them puts the laughter into a saying, introduced by the verse with which the Iliad gives the army's comment after it has laughed at Thersites, “And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor” (8.328; *Iliad* 2.270–271; see [[thersites]] and [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]).

**Laughter again.** Apollo asks Hermes whether he would lie in such bonds beside golden Aphrodite, and Hermes answers that he would, with three times as many bonds and all the gods and goddesses looking on: “So he spoke, and laughter rose among the immortal gods” (8.343; see [[hermes]] and [[apollo]]). The scholia report that the exchange was missing in some copies because it seemed unseemly (see the translation's note on 8.266–366).

**The god who does not laugh.** “But laughter did not hold Poseidon, and he kept begging / Hephaestus, famed for his work, to set Ares free” (8.344–345; see [[poseidon]]). Merry gives the reason for his part: he is the most venerable of the gods of Olympus after Zeus, and Ares' uncle (on 8.344).

**The hearers.** The song that tells of the gods' laughter ends with the delight of its audience: “This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus / was delighted in his heart as he listened, and so were the others, / the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships” (8.367–369; see [[demodocus]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). It is the one song of the three at which Odysseus does not weep (8.83–92, 8.521–531; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Plato's objection to the laughing gods, made against the verse of *Iliad* 1, is told in [[plato-homeric-laughter]].
