**Present or told.** Before the Catalogue the poet of the Iliad set the Muses, who “are present, and know all things”, against mortals, who “hear only the report, and know nothing” (*Iliad* 2.484–486; see the entry). Odysseus' praise of Demodocus names the two mortal ways of knowing: to have been present oneself (παρεών, the Muses' word), or to have heard it from another (ἄλλου ἀκούσας), where with ἄλλου one supplies παρεόντος, “from another who was there” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.491). The blind singer was neither, and he sings λίην … κατὰ κόσμον, “all too well, in due order”, the woe of the Achaeans, “all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through” (8.489–490). οἶτος, “fate”, “woe”, is generally referred to the root of οἴσω, “bear”, as Latin *fors* to *fero*, and the next verse describes it (Merry on 8.489; Bekker rejected 8.490 without manuscript authority: see the translation's note on 8.489–490).

**Taught by a god.** The singer's knowledge must then come from a teacher: “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo” (8.488; see [[muses]], [[apollo]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]). Merry explains why Apollo stands beside the Muse. It is not the music that is in question: the singer was the historian of early times, and he owes his knowledge of the past to the god of prophecy, who knows the past as well as the present and the future (on 8.488), as Calchas “knew the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before” (*Iliad* 1.70; see [[calchas]] and [[prophecy]]). Hesiod too makes singers come from the Muses and from Apollo (*Theogony* 94–95, which Merry quotes).

**The witness in the hall.** The one man in the hall who was present at Troy is the hearer, and his praise is the judgment of an eyewitness. He asks next for the song of the horse that he himself brought into the citadel, and promises that if the singer tells it “fair and right” (κατὰ μοῖραν), he will tell all men “how the god has readily granted you wondrous song” (8.492–498; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[moira]]). When the song is sung, he weeps (8.521–531; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).
