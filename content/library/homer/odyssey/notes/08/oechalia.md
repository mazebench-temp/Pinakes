---
summary: Odysseus will not vie with the archers of former days, “neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia, / who vied even with the immortals in archery” (8.224–225).
---

Boasting of his archery among the Phaeacians, Odysseus sets two men of former days above himself: “neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia, / who vied even with the immortals in archery” (8.224–225; see [[heracles]], [[eurytus]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Εὐρύτῳ Οἰχαλιῆι is the Iliad's Εὐρύτου Οἰχαλιῆος, the phrase of both passages of the Catalogue that name the city (*Iliad* 2.596, 2.730): the archer is known by his city. Merry takes the epithet to refer to Oechalia, a Thessalian town on the Peneus. Towns of the name in Euboea, in Aetolia and in Messenia all claimed Eurytus as their countryman, but in this passage, he thinks, the claim lies only between the Thessalian town and the Messenian, as in the Iliad's two passages, and the story of the bow in the twenty-first book, where Iphitus, the son of Eurytus, meets Odysseus in Messene in the house of Ortilochus and gives him his father's bow, seems to suggest a preference for the Messenian (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.224; 21.13–16, 21.31–33; see [[ortilochus]]).
