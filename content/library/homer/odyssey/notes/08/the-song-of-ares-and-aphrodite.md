summary: Demodocus sings the song to the lyre in the pause of the games, after the young men's dance and before the ball dance of the king's sons, and Odysseus and the Phaeacians delight in it (8.262–369). Ancient critics who disliked its subject rejected it; Aristarchus, Rhianus and Herodian commented on its readings, and Merry defends it as worthy of Homer and fitted to its place.

**Genuine or not.** Repugnance at its morality, W. W. Merry thinks, would lead ancient critics to reject the song as an interpolation (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.267); yet Aristarchus, Rhianus and Herodian commented on its readings at 8.288, 8.299, 8.307, 8.352 and 8.355, and no rejection by them is reported, and Apion defended it against those who athetized it (the scholia on Aristophanes' *Peace* 788, as Merry reports them; see the translation's note on 8.266–366). Merry, after Gladstone, grants what can be said against it: the singer had been fetched to play for the dancers, as the singer on the Shield of Achilles plays for the dance (*Iliad* 18.590–606), and the song is an addition to the day's mirth; the Sun acts in person more than elsewhere in the poems, and Apollo speaks in a tone found nowhere else (8.335); and when Odysseus asks for the third song he recalls the first and passes over this one in silence (8.489–492). The verbal oddities he sets aside: ἀμφί with the genitive, found again only at *Iliad* 16.825; an opening like that of the Hymn to Dionysus; Ἥλιος for Ἠέλιος; the rare μιγάζεσθαι; δωτῆρες ἑάων, “the givers of blessings”, of the gods. The song as a whole, in the place where it stands, is “neither unworthy of Homer nor unlike him” (Merry on 8.267). Some copies lacked the ten verses of Apollo and Hermes, the scholia say, because they seemed improper (8.333–342; Merry's critical note on 8.333–342).

**The opening.** The singer plays the lyre and strikes up, ἀνεβάλλετο (8.266; see [[lyre]] and [[demodocus]]), and the song begins as the seventh Homeric Hymn begins, with ἀμφί and a name and then ὡς, “how”: ἀμφὶ Διώνυσον … μνήσομαι, ὡς ἐφάνη (*Hymn* 7.1–2). πολλὰ δʼ ἔδωκε, “and he gave her many gifts”, refers to the gifts with which Ares won Aphrodite's consent; one scholium strangely anticipates the story and makes them the adulterer's fine (8.269; Merry on 8.269). With ἄφαρ, “at once”, the direct narrative begins, after clauses governed by ὡς (8.270; Merry on 8.270). The messenger is Ἥλιος, contracted, only here in Homer (8.271; see [[helios]] and the translation's note on 8.271).

**The snare.** The bonds are a δόλος, “snare”, the word the poem uses again for the wooden horse (8.276, 8.282, 8.317, 8.494; Merry on 8.276; see [[dolos]], [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and the translation's key words). They are poured around the ἑρμῖνες, the bedposts, and down from the μέλαθρον, the roof beam (8.278–279; Merry on 8.278, 8.279; see [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]] and [[bed]]); τεχνήεντες, “skillfully made”, is kept apart from δολόεντα, “cunning” (8.281, 8.297; see the translation's key words). εἴσατʼ ἴμεν is “made as if to go” (8.283; Merry on 8.283; see [[lemnos]]). The lover, οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε, “kept no blind man's watch” (8.285; Merry on 8.285; see the translation's note on 8.285).

**Ares' invitation.** δεῦρο, φίλη, λέκτρονδε τραπείομεν εὐνηθέντες, “Come here, my dear, let us go to bed and take our pleasure, lying together” (8.292): τραπείομεν is from τέρπω, and Merry joins the verse with the words of Paris to Helen after his duel with Menelaus, ἀλλʼ ἄγε δὴ φιλότητι τραπείομεν εὐνηθέντε, “But come, let us go to bed and take our pleasure in love” (*Iliad* 3.441; Merry on 8.292; see the translation's note on 8.292). The two adulterers of the poems invite in the same phrase.

**Hephaestus' complaint.** He calls the gods to see ἔργα γελαστὰ καὶ οὐκ ἐπιεικτά, “deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear”: γελαστά is the reading of the manuscripts, of Aristarchus and of Herodian, while Apollonius' lexicon and Eustathius had ἔργʼ ἀγέλαστα, “no laughing matter”; Merry prefers “deeds of mockery”, and hears in οὐκ ἐπιεικτά the indignation of “unyielding, hard, cruel” (8.307; Merry on 8.307 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 8.307). The goddess is Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “the daughter of Zeus” (8.308; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]), and Ares ἀίδηλος, “destructive” (8.309; see [[aidelos]]). The last words of the speech, ἀτὰρ οὐκ ἐχέθυμος, “but she has no self-control”, are rendered after the scholium (8.320; see the translation's note on 8.320).

**The doorway.** The gods stand in the doorway, θεοί, δωτῆρες ἑάων, “the gods, the givers of blessings”, the phrase of Achilles' two jars of Zeus (8.325; *Iliad* 24.528; Merry on 8.325; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]), and their laughter is the laughter of *Iliad* 1, at Hephaestus' craft instead of his limping (8.326, after *Iliad* 1.599; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]). The verse of their talk, “And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor”, is the verse of the Achaeans' talk when Odysseus has beaten Thersites and when they stand over the body of Hector (8.328 = *Iliad* 2.271, 22.372; Merry on 8.328; see [[tis-speech]] and [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]).

**Poseidon's pledge.** οὐδὲ Ποσειδάωνα γέλως ἔχε: οὐδέ has the force of “but not” (8.344; Merry on 8.344). He promises that Ares himself “will pay all that is due among the immortal gods”, a just requital in the public presence of the gods (8.347–348; Merry on 8.347). Hephaestus' answer, δειλαί τοι δειλῶν γε καὶ ἐγγύαι ἐγγυάασθαι, “pledges for the worthless are worthless things even to accept”, Merry renders “Even surety for a sorry fellow is a sorry thing to hold in hand”, against Nitzsch, who took δειλός as “weak”, of Hephaestus himself (8.351; Merry on 8.351; see the translation's note on 8.351 and [[pledges-for-the-worthless]]); and δέοιμι in the next verse is “put thee in chains” (8.352; Merry on 8.352; see the translation's note on 8.352–353). His last word, οὐκ ἔστʼ οὐδὲ ἔοικε, is “non licet neque decet” (8.358; Merry on 8.358).

**The audience.** “This the renowned singer sang” closes each of Demodocus' songs (8.83 = 8.367 = 8.521), and what follows it here is delight instead of tears: Odysseus “was delighted in his heart as he listened, and so were the others” (8.367–369; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Alcinous at once bids his sons dance alone (8.370–371; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).
