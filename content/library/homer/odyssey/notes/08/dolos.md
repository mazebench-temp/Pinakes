---
summary: Two snares are sung in the book. In the song of Ares and Aphrodite Hephaestus' net of bonds is a δόλος (8.276, 8.282, 8.317); in the third song the wooden horse is the snare that Odysseus brought into the citadel of Troy (8.494).
---

**The net.** Hephaestus forges bonds “unbreakable, not to be loosed”, and “when he had made the snare (δόλον), in his anger at Ares”, he pours it round the bed and down from the roof beam, fine as spider's webs, which not even a god could see (8.274–282; see [[hephaestus]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). δόλος here is a “snare” or “trap”, the sense it has of the wooden horse and of the fisherman's bait (12.252), and of a mousetrap in the *Battle of Frogs and Mice* (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.276; see the translation's table of key words). The bonds themselves are δολόεντα, “cunning” (8.281; see [[wily]]). Hephaestus tells the gods what will hold the lovers: “the snare and the bonds” (δόλος καὶ δεσμός, 8.317), until the bride-price is returned (see [[bride-price]] and [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]).

**The horse.** The stranger asks Demodocus for the song of the horse “which once brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a snare” (ὅν ποτʼ ἐς ἀκρόπολιν δόλον ἤγαγε, 8.494; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). Aristarchus and Aristophanes read δόλῳ, “by a trick” (the scholia, in Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 8.494). A scholium finds here the ground of Odysseus' epithet “sacker of cities”: the device was the work of Epeius and Athena, the enterprise that of Odysseus (Merry on 8.494; see [[sacker-of-cities]]). Both songs tell of a trap made by craft: the god's catches the swift Ares, and the man's takes Troy.
