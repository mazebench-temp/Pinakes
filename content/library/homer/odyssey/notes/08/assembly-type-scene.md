summary: The assembly of the Phaeacians is told in the order of the pattern: the king leads the people to the place of assembly at dawn (8.4–7), Athena, in the likeness of his herald, summons the leaders (8.7–15), the places of assembly and the seats are filled (8.16–17), “And when they had gathered and were all assembled” (8.24) Alcinous proposes the escort and the feast (8.25–45), and he leads the kings away to his house (8.46–47).
refs: [8.4-7, 8.10-17, 8.24-25, 8.46-47]

---

**Summons.** The summons comes from a goddess in a herald's likeness, who stands beside each man and ends with the stranger's looks, “like the immortals in form” (8.10–14), and the verse that follows is the Iliad's close of an exhortation to battle (8.15; [[exhortation]], [[divine-epiphany]]). The people come to look at the stranger, and Athena's grace makes him a sight worth seeing (8.17–20; see the translation's note on 8.16 for “the places of assembly and the seats”).

**Speech and dissolution.** One speaker only: Alcinous, with the Phaeacian address, “Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (8.26; [[hear-me]]). No one dissolves the assembly. The king “led the way, and they followed with him” (8.46), the verse with which Telemachus leads his companions to fetch the provisions for his voyage (2.413): the young men go to launch the ship and the kings to the feast in the palace (8.46–56). The place of assembly by the ships is used again in the afternoon for the games (8.109; [[assembly]], [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]], [[when-they-had-gathered]]).
