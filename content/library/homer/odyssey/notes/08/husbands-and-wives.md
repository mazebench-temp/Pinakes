summary: The book's marriage is a god's, and it is wronged: Ares shames the bed of Hephaestus, who catches the lovers in his bonds and calls the gods to see (8.266–320). The Phaeacians wish the stranger home to his wife (8.243, 8.410), and the simile of the third song gives his tears to a wife over her dying husband (8.523–530).

The song of Ares and Aphrodite is the Odyssey's tale of a wife unfaithful in her husband's house, told among the gods (see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). The husband speaks for himself: his wife dishonors him because he is lame and loves Ares because he is handsome; he will hold the lovers until her father gives back the bride-price, “because his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control” (8.308–320; see [[hephaestus]], [[bride-price]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]). A scholium says that Homer does not know of Hephaestus married to Aphrodite but to Charis (*Iliad* 18.382–383), and that the story is Demodocus' own making; Merry acknowledges separate myths of the god's partner, and quotes Nitzsch's remark that the same idea lies beneath both, the union of a goddess of grace with the god of the highest art (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.267; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]). Against it stand the wishes of the Phaeacians for the stranger: that he may feast at home beside his wife and children, and see his wife and reach his native land (8.243, 8.410–411; see [[marriage]]). The wife of the simile is the wife the war makes a widow: she throws herself on her husband as he dies before his city, and is led away into slavery (8.523–530; see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]] and [[posis]]).
