summary: In his challenge to the Phaeacians Odysseus boasts of the bow: he would be the first to strike his man in a throng, and only Philoctetes surpassed him at Troy; but he will not vie with the men of former days, Heracles and Eurytus of Oechalia, whom Apollo killed for challenging him to shoot (8.215–228). He does not shoot in Book 8.

**The boast.** Odysseus offers to meet any Phaeacian but Laodamas in boxing, wrestling or running, “For I am no mean man at all the contests there are among men” (8.214), and the bow comes first:

{{quote:83e982e5-3ade-5ec5-ac6a-f341730e012e}}

τόξον ἐύξοον, “the polished bow” (8.215), is the phrase of Pandarus' bow in the Iliad (*Iliad* 4.105; see [[polished]]). What Odysseus claims, W. W. Merry explains, is speed and accuracy: though all his comrades were shooting at the enemy, he would be the first to pick off his man in a close throng (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.216; see [[hostile-men]]). Philoctetes led his men to Troy “well skilled with the bow” (*Iliad* 2.718–720; see [[philoctetes]]); as Nitzsch observes, the bow was his weapon of battle, while Odysseus used it in contests of skill and only now and then in a fight, so that Philoctetes might well surpass him (Merry on 8.219). In the Iliad Odysseus carries a bow only on the night raid, the bow that Meriones gives him (*Iliad* 10.260).

**The men of former days.** ἐρίζεσκον, “vied”, means only that they matched themselves with the immortals, and of the two Eurytus alone actually contended with a god (Merry on 8.225; see the translation's note on 8.225, [[men-of-today]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Nitzsch notes that Homer gives Heracles the bow, as his shade carries it in the underworld (11.606–608, Greek only), and that the club and the lion skin were first given him by Peisander of Rhodes, or a little later by Stesichorus (as Merry reports him on 8.225; see [[heracles]]). Eurytus died for his challenge (8.226–228; see [[eurytus]] and [[apollo]]). His bow survived him: his son Iphitus gave it to Odysseus, who never took it with him to war but kept it in his halls in memory of his friend (21.31–41, Greek only); it is the bow of the contest in Book 21.

**The spear and the arrow.** The boast ends: “And I throw the spear as far as no other man shoots an arrow” (8.229; see [[spear]] and [[arrows]]), where the verb of the spear is stretched to the arrow (Merry on 8.229).
