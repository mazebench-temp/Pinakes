---
summary: Virgil's nymph Clymene sings of Vulcan's vain care and the sweet thefts of Mars; Merry sets the Trojans' divided counsel over the horse and its “hollow oak” in the Aeneid beside Demodocus' third song, and the blessed in Elysium who “beat the dances with their feet” beside the young Phaeacians.
---

- **Clymene's song.** Among Cyrene's nymphs, spinning wool beneath their river, “Clymene was telling of the vain care of Vulcan, and the tricks and sweet thefts of Mars” (*Georgics* 4.345–347, a plain rendering), the song of Ares and Aphrodite (8.266–366) sung by a nymph to nymphs: [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]].
- **The Trojans' debate.** Demodocus sings that the Trojans, sitting around the horse, “talked on and on, confusedly”, divided three ways: to split the hollow timber with the bronze, to throw it down the rocks from the height, or to let it stand as an offering to appease the gods (8.505–510). In Virgil's account some marvel at the horse, Thymoetes would have it drawn inside the walls and set on the citadel, and Capys and the wiser would hurl it into the sea or burn it, or bore through its hollow belly; “the uncertain crowd is split into opposing views”, *scinditur incertum studia in contraria vulgus* (*Aeneid* 2.31–39, a plain rendering). Merry finds in the verse two of the meanings of ἄκριτα, the want of decision and the clash of views (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.505), and he renders κοῖλον δόρυ, “the hollow timber” (8.507), by Virgil's *cavum robur*, the “hollow oak” from which the Greeks come out gladly when Sinon opens the horse (*Aeneid* 2.258–260; Merry on 8.507): [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]].
- **The dance.** For πέπληγον δὲ χορὸν θεῖον ποσίν, “and they struck the divine dancing floor with their feet” (8.264), Merry cites *pars pedibus plaudunt choreas et carmina dicunt*, “some beat out the dances with their feet and sing songs” (*Aeneid* 6.644; Merry on 8.264). The verse is from Virgil's Elysium, where the blessed also exercise in grassy wrestling grounds and contend in games on the yellow sand, and the Thracian priest in his long robe plays the seven notes to their measures (*Aeneid* 6.642–647; plain renderings): [[dance]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]].
