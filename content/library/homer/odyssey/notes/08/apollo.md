summary: Apollo has a part in two of Demodocus' songs, in the stranger's boast and in his praise of the singer. His oracle at Pytho foretold to Agamemnon the quarrel of the best of the Achaeans, when the beginning of bane was rolling upon Trojans and Danaans; he killed Eurytus of Oechalia, who challenged him with the bow; in the song of Ares and Aphrodite he comes with Poseidon and Hermes, and asks Hermes whether he would lie in such bonds beside golden Aphrodite; and Odysseus says that either the Muse or Apollo has taught Demodocus.

**The oracle at Pytho.** Demodocus' first song tells of a quarrel at a feast of the gods between Odysseus and Achilles, at which Agamemnon rejoiced, “For so Phoebus Apollo had told him in prophecy / in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold / to consult the oracle; for then the beginning of bane was rolling upon / Trojans and Danaans through the counsels of great Zeus” (8.79–82; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]], [[pytho]], [[agamemnon]], [[phoebus]] and [[zeus]]). Homer tells of no other consultation of the oracle at Pytho. χρείων is “giving response”, the god's own word in the *Hymn to Apollo*, χρήσω δʼ ἀνθρώποισι Διὸς νημερτέα βουλήν, “and I will declare to men the unerring will of Zeus” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.79; *Hymn* 3.132): the oracle tells what the counsels of Zeus will bring. The stone threshold is the one Achilles names among the riches he would not take for his life, “the stone threshold of the Archer, / Phoebus Apollo, in rocky Pytho” (*Iliad* 9.404–405; see [[aphetor]] and the translation's note on 8.80). The scholia tell the oracle: Ilium would be taken when the best of the Greeks quarreled; Merry places the consultation at the beginning of the war, when the bane was just beginning to roll (on 8.75, 8.81; see the translation's note on 8.74–82).

**Eurytus.** Odysseus will not vie with the archers of former days, “neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia, / who vied even with the immortals in archery. / That is why great Eurytus died early, and did not reach old age / in his halls; for Apollo in anger / killed him, because he challenged him to shoot with the bow” (8.224–228; see [[eurytus]], [[heracles]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). ἐρίζεσκον means only that they matched themselves; of the two, Eurytus alone contended with the gods (Merry on 8.225; see the translation's note on 8.225). In Book 7 he struck down Rhexenor in his hall, and the poem gave no reason (7.64–65; see the Book 7 note); here the archer god kills the archer who challenged him.

**At the doorway.** In the second song he comes with the other gods to see Ares and Aphrodite in the bonds: “and far-working lord Apollo came” (8.323; see [[far-worker]] and [[olympians]]). ἑκάεργος is generally taken as ἑκηβόλος, “working”, that is shooting, “from afar”; others compare it with the Latin *averruncus*, from ἑκάς and εἴργειν, the god who keeps evil away (Merry on 8.323). The verse that introduces his question, “and lord Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to Hermes” (8.334), ends as the Iliad's verses for him do, ἄναξ Διὸς υἱὸς Ἀπόλλων (*Iliad* 7.23, 7.37, 20.103; see [[son-of-zeus]]). The question, whether Hermes would be willing, held fast in strong bonds, to sleep beside golden Aphrodite, and Hermes' answer, “If only this might come to pass, lord Apollo who shoots from afar!”, are quoted in the Book 8 note of [[hermes]] (8.335–342; see [[far-shooter]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). Gladstone, in a passage Merry quotes, found that Apollo is made to assume here a tone wholly singular and unsupported by what is told of him in the rest of the poems (Merry on 8.267; see the translation's note on 8.266–366).

**The singer's teacher.** At supper Odysseus praises Demodocus: “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo; / for all too well, in due order, you sing the fate of the Achaeans” (8.488–489; see [[demodocus]] and [[muses]]). Merry compares Hesiod, “from the Muses and far-shooting Apollo are singers and lyre players on the earth” (*Theogony* 94–95), and the same verses in the Hymns (*Hymn* 25.2–3). In the Iliad Apollo plays the lyre while the Muses sing (*Iliad* 1.603–604), but here, Merry says, it is not the music that counts: the singer was the historian of early times, and owes to Apollo, the god of prophecy, who knows the past as well as the present and the future, his power to tell of the wooden horse as if he had been there himself (on 8.488). Nägelsbach, he adds, recalls that Calchas, under Apollo's inspiration, knew the past as well as the future (see [[calchas]]).

**Lyre, bow and oracle.** In the *Hymn to Apollo* the newborn god claims three things: “May the lyre be dear to me, and the curved bow, / and I will declare to men the unerring will of Zeus” (*Hymn* 3.131–132). All three are his in this book: the singer's teaching, the bow with which he killed Eurytus, and the oracle that foretold the quarrel.
