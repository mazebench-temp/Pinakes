# Unnamed: the city Athena goes through (8.7) and "our city" of the prophecy (8.569).
refs: [8.7, 8.569]
---

**The city by day.** In the seventh book Odysseus walked through the city hidden in Athena's mist (7.14–45); now, at dawn, the goddess herself goes through it: “And Pallas Athena went up and down through the city / in the likeness of the herald of wise-hearted Alcinous”, standing beside each man to summon him (8.7–14; see [[athena]] and [[heralds]]). The people gather at “the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, which was built for them beside the ships” (8.5; see [[assembly]]), as Nausicaa had placed it, by the slips where the ships are drawn up along the road (6.263–267; see [[harbor]]). The plural of “the places of assembly and the seats” (8.16) is the plural of the sights Odysseus marveled at in the seventh book, “the places of assembly of the heroes themselves” (7.44; see the translation's note on 8.16). From there the young men chosen for the escort go down “to the shore of the barren sea”, drag the black ship down into deep water, fit her out and anchor her offshore, before they go up to the king's house (8.48–56; see [[ships]], [[shore]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**Under the mountain.** At night Alcinous tells the stranger what his father, Nausithous, used to say: “He said that one day a well-made ship of the Phaeacian men, / as it came back from an escort on the misty sea, / he would shatter, and would cover our city round about with a great mountain” (8.567–569; see [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]] and [[phaeacians]]). The threat, Merry explains, is not that the god will crush the city by hurling a mountain upon it, but that he will pen the people in with a wall of mountain, cut off their way to the water, and so put an end to their seafaring. He renders ὄρος … ἀμφικαλύψειν “would throw a mountain round about the city”, and compares Ajax, who ran and covered his fallen brother with his shield, σάκος ἀμφεκάλυψε (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.569; *Iliad* 8.330–331; see the translation's note on 8.569). Poseidon's own words in the thirteenth book give the same purpose: he will shatter the ship “so that they may now hold back and cease from escorting men”, and cover their city round about with a great mountain (13.149–152, a plain rendering). The poem leaves the city's fate open there, with the Phaeacians making ready twelve chosen bulls for him and praying around his altar, in the hope that he will pity them and not cover their city with a towering mountain (13.180–187, a plain rendering; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). The verb has been heard once before in this book, of another city: Troy was fated to perish “once it had enfolded / the great wooden horse” (8.511–512; see [[troy]]).
