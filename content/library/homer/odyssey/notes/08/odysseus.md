summary: The stranger's day among the Phaeacians, still unnamed. Athena makes him taller and stouter for the assembly (8.17–23); at the feast he hides his face in his mantle and weeps at the singer's lay of his own quarrel with Achilles (8.83–95). At the games he declines Laodamas' invitation, answers Euryalus' taunt, throws the discus beyond all the marks and challenges the Phaeacians to any contest but running (8.131–233); he praises the dancers, receives the gifts of the kings and Euryalus' sword, ties his chest with Circe's knot and bathes (8.381–456). Nausicaa bids him farewell at the door (8.457–468); he honors Demodocus with a portion of the chine and asks for the song of the Wooden Horse, weeps at it like a captive woman, and is asked his name (8.469–586).

**Sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities.** The book begins with him and the king rising at dawn: “and up rose Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities” (8.3; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]). πτολίπορθος, which the Iliad gives to Ares, Achilles and others, is in the Odyssey used only of Odysseus, with special reference to the craft by which he enabled the Greeks to take Ilium (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.3), and the day will end with that craft in the singer's song (8.492–520). To the Phaeacians he is only ξεῖνος, “the stranger”, whom the king does not know: “This stranger, I do not know who he is, has come to my house in his wanderings” (8.28; see [[alcinous]] and [[xenia]]).

**The grace of Athena.** While the king leads the way to the assembly, Athena, in the likeness of his herald, summons the leaders to learn about “the stranger / who has newly come to the house of wise-hearted Alcinous, / after wandering over the sea, like the immortals in form” (8.12–14; see [[athena]] and [[like-the-immortals]]), and makes him fit for the day:

{{quote:a458fcb4-b12f-5cb6-b6e4-34c7954eb8a0}}

The grace is the one she poured on him when he came from the river, in the verse of 6.235 (see [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[charis]]). He is to be δεινός τʼ αἰδοῖός τε, “feared and respected” (8.22; see [[aidos]]). He takes part in only one contest, the discus, and Zenodotus athetized 8.23 for that reason; Merry reports and rejects Crates' attempt to take the “contests” as the full tale of his past sufferings (the scholia, in Merry's critical note, and Merry on 8.22; see the translation's note on 8.22–23 and [[contests]]).

**The first song.** At the feast the singer sings “the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus” (8.75; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[demodocus]]), and the hero of the lay hides his face: “but Odysseus / took his great purple mantle in his strong hands / and drew it down over his head, and covered his handsome face; / for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians, letting tears fall from beneath his brows” (8.83–86; see [[purple]], [[cloak]] and [[aidos]]). The φᾶρος was a square cloth worn over the left arm and shoulder, with a long corner hanging from the right, and he threw this corner over his head from behind (Merry on 8.85; see the translation's note on 8.85). At every pause he wiped his tears, uncovered his head and poured a libation from the two-handled cup, and at every new beginning covered his head again and lamented (8.87–92), the optatives being iterative: at each pause, Merry explains, he poured a thank-offering to the gods in remembrance of their constant care of him (Merry on 8.87; see [[libation]] and [[lament]]). Only Alcinous, sitting near him, notices (8.93–95; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[shedding-tears]]).

**At the games.** Laodamas proposes to ask him whether he knows any contest, since he is well built but broken by the sea, and challenges him (8.131–151; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]], [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]] and [[no-greater-fame]]). He answers:

“Laodamas, why do you bid me do these things, taunting me? / Sorrows are on my mind far more than contests, / who before have suffered very much and toiled much, / and now in the midst of your assembly, in need of my homecoming, / I sit, begging the king and all the people” (8.153–157; see [[algos]], [[nostos]] and [[supplication]]). μεθʼ ὑμετέρῃ ἀγορῇ is “in the midst of your assembly”, the preposition taking a collective noun (Merry on 8.156). Euryalus' taunt, that he is not like an athlete but like a captain of traders mindful of his cargo (8.158–164; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[trade]]), draws from him the first anger of the book:

{{quote:ced495d1-d20c-559d-aaba-fbb4e86dc6c2}}

On the gods' unequal gifts of looks, mind and eloquence, see [[different-gifts]] and the translation's notes on 8.169, 8.170, 8.171–172 and 8.177. The asyndeton of “You have stirred the heart in my breast” is significant: carried by his warmth to the limits of the freedom he would allow himself as a stranger, he hastens to excuse himself to the company (Merry on 8.178). νῆις is “untrained”, μυθεῖαι describes the high-flown manner of the imputation, and ἔμμεναι is a past tense, as πεποίθεα: “I was among the first, when I trusted in my youth” (Merry on 8.179, 8.181; see the translation's note on 8.180–181). The verse “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (8.183) returns when the narrator shows him asleep in the Phaeacian ship and in the tale he invents for Athena on Ithaca (13.91, 13.264, Greek only), and in the Iliad of all that Achilles endured with Patroclus (*Iliad* 24.8); the participle belongs properly to the waves, and is extended by zeugma to the wars (Merry on 8.183).

**The discus.** “He spoke, and sprang up with his mantle still on, and seized a discus / bigger and thicker, and heavier by no little, / than those with which the Phaeacians threw among themselves” (8.186–188): αὐτῷ φάρει is the sociative dative, “mantle and all” (Merry on 8.186; see the translation's note on 8.186). The stone hums, and “down to the earth they cowered, / the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships, / under the rush of the stone” (8.190–192), the high-sounding epithets set in intentional sarcasm against their terror at the whiz of the quoit (Merry on 8.191; see [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). Athena, “in form like a man”, sets the mark and tells him that even a blind man could find it, and none of the Phaeacians will reach it (8.193–198; see [[athena]], [[discus]] and the translation's note on 8.192–193). “So she spoke, and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / rejoicing that he saw a gentle companion in the gathering” (8.199–200): ἐνηής is always used of close friends (Merry on 8.200; see [[hetairos]]).

**The challenge.** “And then he spoke more lightly among the Phaeacians” (8.201): κουφότερον is “more gaily”, his face until now having worn an anxious look (Merry on 8.201). He will throw another as far or farther, and challenges any of them, in boxing or wrestling or even in running, except Laodamas, his host (8.202–211; see [[strife-with-ones-host]]). He knows how to handle the polished bow, and would be the first to strike his man in a throng of enemies; Philoctetes alone surpassed him at Troy, but he will not vie with the men of former days, Heracles and Eurytus, whom Apollo killed for challenging him (8.214–228; see [[bow]], [[philoctetes]], [[heracles]], [[eurytus]], [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[men-of-today]]): Philoctetes used the bow as his weapon of battle, while Odysseus used it in contests of skill or rarely in the fight (Merry on 8.219). “And I throw the spear as far as no other man shoots an arrow” (8.229; see [[spear]]). The one contest he fears is running, for the waves have overcome him, “since on board the ship my keep was not / unfailing” (8.230–233; see the translation's key words): Merry finds a real difficulty here, since Calypso had provisioned the raft (5.265–267), and suggests that the shipwreck cut short his enjoyment of the stores on board (Merry on 8.232; see the translation's note on 8.232–233). His skill with the bow is tried at last in his own house, when he strings the great bow and shoots through the axes (21.404–423, Greek only).

**The dance and the song.** After the king's answer (8.235–255; see [[alcinous]] and [[phaeacians]]) he watches the young dancers: “And Odysseus / gazed at the flashing of their feet, and marveled in his heart” (8.264–265; see [[dance]] and [[thambos]]). At the song of Ares and Aphrodite he “was delighted in his heart as he listened” (8.367–368; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]), the only one of the three songs that does not make him weep. Then he praises the ball dance to the king, “awe holds me as I look” (8.382–384; see [[awe-holds-me]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]).

**The gifts.** The king calls him “a very wise man” and has the twelve kings bring him a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold each (8.388–393; see [[kings]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Euryalus gives him his sword with the silver hilt and ivory sheath and bids him farewell, and he returns the blessing: “And you too, my friend, fare very well, and may the gods grant you prosperity. / And may no longing for this sword ever come upon you hereafter, / which you have given me, making amends with your words” (8.413–415; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]], [[sword]] and [[olbos]]). “He spoke, and around his shoulders he slung the silver-studded sword” (8.416; see [[silver-studded]]).

**The knot and the bath.** Arete packs the gifts in a chest and bids him tie the lid himself (8.438–445; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[chest]]): “at once he fitted on the lid, and quickly tied a knot on it, / a complicated one, which lady Circe had once taught him” (8.447–448; see [[circe]]). δέδαε is the factitive reduplicated aorist, “made him learn it in his mind”, and what she taught him was the particular complicated knot in which to tie the fastening (Merry on 8.448; see the translation's key words). Circe is named here for the first time in the poem, in passing, before the tale in which she appears (Book 10). Then the bath:

{{quote:d92c823b-0080-5c67-9e14-aa00513764ef}}

οὔ τι … θάμιζεν, “he had not often been cared for so”, is a litotes, “he was not wont to be so cared for” (Merry on 8.451). τόφρα δέ, “but all that time”, is the whole of his stay with Calypso, and θεῷ ὣς, “as for a god”, accords with her promise to make him immortal and ageless, as a scholium notes (Merry on 8.453; see [[calypso]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**Nausicaa.** As he goes from the bath to the men drinking wine, Nausicaa, at the doorpost, bids him remember her, since to her first he owes the price for his life, and he answers: “Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, / so may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera, / that I may come home and see the day of my homecoming; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god / always, all my days; for you gave me my life, maiden” (8.464–468; see [[nausicaa]], [[zoagria]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).

**The singer honored.** At supper he cuts a portion from the chine that had been served to him in his honor and sends it to Demodocus, “for all my grief”, since singers have their share of honor and respect among all men (8.474–483; see [[demodocus]], [[singers]] and [[feast]]): “that I may do homage to him, though sad at heart” alludes chiefly to the tears the first lay drew from him (Merry on 8.477). Then he praises the singer above all mortals, as taught by the Muse or Apollo, for singing the fate of the Achaeans “as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another” (8.487–491; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]]), and asks for the song of the horse “which once brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a snare, / having filled it with the men who sacked Ilios” (8.494–495; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[dolos]]). He asks for his own exploit and names himself only in the third person. Through this deed, a scholium says, Odysseus too is a sacker of cities: the making was Epeius' and Athena's, the undertaking was his (Merry on 8.494; see [[epeius]]).

**The second tears.** The song tells of the Argives in the horse “around famous Odysseus” (8.502), and of how “Odysseus went to the house of Deiphobus / like Ares, together with godlike Menelaus. / There, he said, he dared the most dread fight, / and conquered in the end, through great-hearted Athena” (8.517–520; see [[the-fall-of-troy]], [[deiphobus]] and [[menelaus]]). Then:

{{quote:1235ba38-4d06-5fc4-abc9-ab2ab9b1c14f}}

The sacker of the city weeps like a woman of a sacked city, led away into slavery over her husband's body (see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]], [[pity]] and the translation's note on 8.529). Alcinous alone notices again, in the verses of the morning (8.532–534 = 8.93–95), stops the singer and asks the stranger his name, his land and his city, the lands and men he has seen in his wanderings, and why he weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilios (8.535–586; see [[alcinous]], [[no-one-is-nameless]] and [[give-me-an-exact-account]]). The answer begins the next book: “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (9.19, Greek only, a plain rendering; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**His epithets and names in this book.** διογενὴς … πτολίπορθος Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities” (8.3; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (8.9; see [[great-hearted]]); υἱὸν Λαέρταο δαΐφρονα, “the wise-hearted son of Laertes” (8.18; see [[laertes]] and [[wise-hearted]]); πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles” (8.152, 8.165, 8.412, 8.463, 8.474, 8.486; see [[of-many-wiles]]); πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (8.199, 8.446; see [[much-enduring]]); δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (8.381, 8.494; see [[brilliant]]); ἀγακλυτός, “famous” (8.502; see [[famous]]). To the Phaeacians he is ξεῖνος, “the stranger”, from Athena's summons to the king's last question (8.12, 8.28, 8.42, 8.101, 8.133, 8.388, 8.402, 8.438, 8.541, 8.543), ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father” (8.145), and πάτερ ὦ ξεῖνε (8.408), and the king's αἰδοῖος ξεῖνος, “the revered stranger” (8.544).
