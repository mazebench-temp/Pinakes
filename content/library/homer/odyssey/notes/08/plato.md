---
summary: Plato's Socrates would not let the young hear of the binding of Ares and Aphrodite, and in the myth of Er he sends the soul of Epeius, maker of the wooden horse, into the life of a craftswoman; Proclus answers the one and explains the other with this book's verses. The Phaedrus' remark on Homer's blindness, which Proclus meets with Demodocus, Ajax's chine in the Republic, and two of Merry's grammatical parallels from the Republic belong here too.
---

- **The binding of Ares and Aphrodite.** Reviewing what the young guardians may hear for the sake of self-control, Socrates sets beside Zeus' desire for Hera on Ida “the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus, for other reasons of the same kind” (*Republic* 3.390c, a plain rendering), and Adeimantus agrees that such things are not fit to hear: [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]], [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[plato-zeus-deceived]].
- **The soul of Epeius.** In the myth of Er the souls choose their next lives, and after Atalanta's soul takes the life of a male athlete, Er sees “the soul of Epeius son of Panopeus going into the nature of a craftswoman” (*Republic* 10.620c, a plain rendering). Proclus explains the choice from Homer: among men Epeius was a craftsman, since he even crafted the horse, “which Epeius made with Athena's help” (8.493), but had no share in a man's life of war, since he says himself that he falls short in battle (*Iliad* 23.670; Proclus, *On the Republic* II 317 Kroll): [[epeius]], [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]].
- **Blind Homer.** In the *Phaedrus* Socrates says that Homer, unlike Stesichorus, never understood why he lost his sight (*Phaedrus* 243a). Proclus answers that Homer was said to be blind because he turned from visible beauty to the true, and suffered what he says Demodocus suffered, whom the Muse robbed of his eyes and gave sweet song (8.63–64; *On the Republic* I 173–175 Kroll): [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]] and [[stesichorus-palinode]].
- **The chine.** Socrates' honor for the brave, “according to Homer”, is the long chine with which Agamemnon honored Ajax (*Republic* 5.468c–d; *Iliad* 7.321). In this book Odysseus cuts a portion from the chine, which had been served to him, Merry says, as a mark of honor, and sends it to the singer (8.474–483; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.475): [[plato-chine-of-ajax]] and [[demodocus]].
- **“You do not often come down.”** For θαμίζειν with a participle, “he had not often been cared for so” (οὐ … κομιζόμενός γε θάμιζεν, 8.451), Merry cites Cephalus' greeting to Socrates at the opening of the *Republic*, ὦ Σώκρατες, οὐδὲ θαμίζεις ἡμῖν καταβαίνων εἰς τὸν Πειραιᾶ, “Socrates, you do not often come down to us to the Piraeus” (*Republic* 1.328c, a plain rendering; Merry, who cites it as 328a, on 8.451): [[bathing]].
- **“From where.”** For ἔνθεν ἑλών, Demodocus “taking it up from where” the Argives sailed away (8.500), Merry compares Socrates' “let us hear and speak, recalling from where” the argument left off (*Republic* 6.489e, a plain rendering; Merry on 8.500): [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]].
