**The revered mother.** The queen, who was the goal of the stranger's walk to the palace in Book 7, says nothing through the day of the games. When the gifts are brought in at sunset, “the sons of noble Alcinous received them / and set the beautiful gifts beside their revered mother” (8.419–420; see [[alcinous]] and [[aidos]]): the gifts of guest-friendship go into the keeping of the lady of the house (see [[guest-gifts]]). While the heralds, the queen and her sons are busy with the stowage, the king leads the stranger and the chieftains to their seats (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.421).

**The king's charge.** “Then the might of Alcinous spoke to Arete: / ‘Come here, lady, bring a splendid chest, the best there is, / and put in it yourself a freshly washed mantle and a tunic’” (8.423–425; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]], [[chest]] and [[preeminent]]). γύναι, “lady”, is the word with which Odysseus addressed Nausicaa on the shore (6.168), here a husband's address to his wife. With Bekker's αὐτή, “put in it yourself”, the queen is to give her own gift besides those of the others, as she does (Merry's critical note and note on 8.425; see the translation's note on 8.425, 8.441); a scholium sets her part beside that of the chieftains. Then the bath, the feast and the song, and the king's own gift, a golden goblet (8.426–432; see [[handmaids]] and [[bathing]]).

**The chest.**

{{quote:1d4e47a6-a68d-5a5b-8ad9-57492c7669b7}}

ἴδε πῶμα is “look to the lid”, and δηλήσεται has the contents of the chest for its object, “should spoil them for thee” (Merry on 8.443, 8.444). Most modern commentators took αὖτε as “again”, as if she knew of the sleep in which Odysseus lost the winds of Aeolus (10.31, Greek only), so that Köchly thought the verses moved here from after the tale of the wanderings; it is far simpler, Merry answers, to take it of the contrast between then and now, “by and by” (Merry on 8.444; see the translation's note on 8.444). Her care foresees the voyage home, when a deep sleep falls on Odysseus in the Phaeacian ship (13.79–80, Greek only; see [[pompe]] and [[sleep]]). He ties the lid with the knot that lady Circe had taught him (8.446–448; see [[circe]] and [[chest]]).

**Her epithets in this book.** μητρὶ … αἰδοίῃ, “their revered mother” (8.420); γύναι, “lady”, in her husband's address (8.424). See the entry.
