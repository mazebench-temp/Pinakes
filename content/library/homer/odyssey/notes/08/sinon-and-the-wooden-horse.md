heading: The song of the wooden horse
summary: At supper Odysseus asks Demodocus for the song of the horse that Epeius made with Athena's help and that he himself brought into the citadel as a snare (8.492–495). The singer begins where the Argives sail away, leaving the best of them hidden in the horse in the Trojans' place of assembly, and sings the Trojans' three counsels and the fate that the horse was to bring (8.499–513).

**The request.** Odysseus has praised Demodocus for singing the fate of the Achaeans as if he had been there himself; now he asks for another theme:

{{quote:833afaab-c52b-560e-ba54-37f6f2e9eea4}}

The theme he leaves is the morning's lay of the quarrel with Achilles, and W. W. Merry notes that the one lay would belong to the beginning, the other to the end of the ten years' siege, and that the horse lies within the range of the later *Little Iliad*, from which Aristotle lists the tragedies of the Judgment of Arms, the Philoctetes, the Neoptolemus, the Eurypylus, the Beggary, the Laconian Women, the Sack of Troy and the Sailing Away, “and the Sinon and the Trojan Women” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.492; *Poetics* 1459b4–7; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]], [[little-iliad]], [[sinon-play]] and [[the-wooden-horse]]). The making is Epeius' with Athena's help, the bringing in is Odysseus': a scholium draws from this verse that Odysseus too is a “sacker of cities”, since the work was Epeius' and Athena's, the undertaking Odysseus' (Merry on 8.494; see [[epeius]], [[athena]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]). δόλον, “as a snare”, is the word of Hephaestus' bonds in the song before (8.276; see [[dolos]] and [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]]); Aristarchus and Aristophanes read δόλῳ, “by a trick” (see the translation's note on 8.494).

**Where the song begins.** ὁρμηθεὶς θεοῦ, “stirred by the god”, or, as a scholium has it, “starting from the god”, with a prelude (8.499; Merry on 8.499; see the translation's note on 8.499). ἔνθεν ἑλών is “having taken it up at that point”: the singer begins where the Argives had gone on board their ships and were sailing away, “having thrown fire on their huts”, while the others sat around Odysseus in the Trojans' place of assembly, hidden in the horse (8.500–504; Merry on 8.500; see [[argives]]). Odysseus, Merry observes, is not only the contriver of the plot but its leading man (on 8.502), and ἀγορῇ is the Trojans' assembly, not a market (on 8.503; see [[assembly]]).

**The three counsels.** “So it stood there, and they talked on and on, confusedly, / sitting around it; and they were divided three ways in their counsel: / either to split the hollow timber with the pitiless bronze, / or to drag it to the top of the height and throw it down the rocks, / or to let it stand as a great offering to appease the gods” (8.505–509; see [[trojans]] and [[pitiless-bronze]]). ἄκριτα, “confusedly”, Merry takes of the contradictions between speakers, and he compares Virgil's account of the same debate, *Scinditur incertum studia in contraria vulgus* (Merry on 8.505; Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.39; see the translation's note on 8.505 and [[virgil]]). ἥνδανε, “was finding favor”: only one of the three was chosen in the end (Merry on 8.506). κοῖλον δόρυ, “the hollow timber”, is Virgil's *cavum robur* (Merry on 8.507; *Aeneid* 2.260), and διαπλῆξαι, “to split”, is Aristarchus' reading (see the translation's note on 8.507). The height, ἐπʼ ἄκρης, is the rocky edge of the citadel (Merry on 8.508). θελκτήριον, “to appease”, is a propitiation of the gods (Merry on 8.509; see the translation's note on 8.509), and Düntzer, as Merry reports, quotes the summary of Arctinus, in which some of the Trojans said that the horse should be dedicated to Athena (on 8.509).

**The fate.** “and that is how it was to be accomplished in the end; / for it was fated that the city should perish once it had enfolded / the great wooden horse” (8.510–512; see [[aisa]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). τῇ περ is “the very way in which things were fated in the end to be accomplished”, and the subjunctive ἀμφικαλύψῃ, where an optative might be expected, keeps as nearly as it can the very wording of the oracle (Merry on 8.510, 8.511). The verses that close the passage, ὅθʼ ἥατο πάντες ἄριστοι / Ἀργείων Τρώεσσι φόνον καὶ κῆρα φέροντες, “where they were sitting, all the best / of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (8.512–513), are made on those with which Menelaus, at Sparta, began his own story of the horse, “where we were sitting, all the best / of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans!” (4.272–273; see [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]): what Menelaus told in the first person, as one of the men inside, the singer tells in the third, to another of them.
