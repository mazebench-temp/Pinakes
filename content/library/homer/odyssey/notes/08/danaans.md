---
summary: In Demodocus' first song the quarrel at the feast of the gods was the time when “the beginning of bane was rolling upon / Trojans and Danaans” (8.81–82); and Alcinous asks why the stranger weeps at “the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios” (8.578).
---

**Trojans and Danaans.** Apollo's oracle was given at the beginning of the war, “for then the beginning of bane was rolling upon / Trojans and Danaans through the counsels of great Zeus” (8.81–82; see [[pytho]], [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[trojans]]). Τρωσί τε καὶ Δαναοῖσι opens the verse in the Iliad too, for the two armies together under the will of Zeus: in the narrator's words on Agamemnon's dream, Zeus “was yet to lay more sorrows and groaning / on Trojans and Danaans alike” (*Iliad* 2.39–40), and Hera would let him “judge between Trojans and Danaans” (*Iliad* 8.431; see [[zeus]]). The scholia reported that some editions lacked 8.81–82, and that they were athetized; Merry finds the notice incomplete, since no reason is given (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 8.81–82; see the translation's note on 8.74–82).

**“The Argive Danaans.”** Alcinous' last question joins two of the army's names: “And tell me why you weep and mourn within your heart / as you hear the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios” (8.577–578; see [[argives]] and [[troy]]). Merry takes Ἀργείων as an epithet, “Argive”, as in “Argive Helen”, referring to the Danaans' home, and reports that Gladstone made Δαναοί the epithet and Bekker preferred ἡρώων Δαναῶν, “of the Danaan heroes” (on 8.578). It is the fate that Demodocus has just sung, and that the gods wrought “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580; see [[song-and-fame]]); in the first book Telemachus defended Phemius' song of “the evil fate of the Danaans” in the same word, οἶτος (1.350).
