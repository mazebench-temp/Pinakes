---
summary: Odysseus names Heracles with Eurytus of Oechalia as the men of former days with whom he will not vie at archery, “who vied even with the immortals in archery” (8.223–225).
---

“But with men of former days I will not wish to vie, / neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia, / who vied even with the immortals in archery” (8.223–225; see [[eurytus]], [[men-of-today]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). ἐρίζεσκον, W. W. Merry notes, means only that they matched themselves, and of the two Eurytus alone actually contended with the gods (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.225; see the translation's note on 8.225). The Iliad tells of Heracles shooting gods, though not in a contest: he struck Hera in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow, and Hades “in Pylos among the dead” (*Iliad* 5.392–397; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]).

**The archer.** Nitzsch, as Merry reports him, observes that Homer represents Heracles with the bow, as Odysseus sees his image among the dead, bare bow in hand and an arrow on the string (11.606–608, Greek only), and that the club and the lion's skin were first given to him by Peisander of Rhodes or by Stesichorus somewhat later (Merry on 8.225). Merry points also to Amphitryon's defense of archery in Euripides' *Heracles*: the man-at-arms is a slave to his arms and dies by the cowardice of those beside him, while the archer, shooting from afar, harms the enemy without giving his body to them (Euripides, *Heracles* 188–203, answering Lycus' scorn of the bow at 157–164; see [[euripides]]).

The Odyssey remembers him again as the killer of Iphitus, the son of Eurytus, who had given Odysseus his bow; Heracles killed him while he was his guest in his own house (21.22–30, Greek only).
