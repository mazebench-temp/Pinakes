summary: In Demodocus' second song Hephaestus is the wronged husband of Aphrodite. Told by the Sun that Ares lies with her in his house, he forges at his anvil bonds that no one can see, pours them around his bed and makes as if to go to Lemnos; with the lovers caught, he stands in the doorway and calls the gods to see. The lame and slow god has caught the swiftest by his craft, and he lets Ares go only when Poseidon stands surety for the fine.

**The husband in the song.** Demodocus' second song, sung to the young men dancing on the leveled floor, is “about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite, / how they first lay together in the house of Hephaestus / in secret” (8.267–269; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]], [[demodocus]], [[ares]] and [[aphrodite]]). The craftsman of the Iliad's divine scenes, who built the gods' houses and forged the arms of Achilles, is here a husband in a comic tale, and the gods who laughed at him as he bustled through the halls at the end of *Iliad* 1 laugh again, this time at the snare he has made (8.326–327; *Iliad* 1.599–600; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

**The smith at work.** “And when Hephaestus heard the heart-grieving tale, / he went to his smithy, brooding evil deep in his mind, / and set the great anvil on the anvil block, and forged bonds / unbreakable, not to be loosed, so that they might stay there steadfast” (8.272–275; see [[helios]] and [[heart-grieving]]). χαλκεών, “smithy”, is named only here in Homer, and the half-verse of the anvil is the one with which he begins the shield of Achilles, θῆκεν ἐν ἀκμοθέτῳ μέγαν ἄκμονα, “he set a great anvil on the anvil block” (*Iliad* 18.476; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]): he makes the bonds as he made the arms. What he makes is a δόλος, a “snare” (8.276, 8.282, 8.317; see [[dolos]] and the translation's key-word row), poured around the bedposts and down from the roof beam, “fine as spider's webs, which no one could even see, / not even one of the blessed gods; for they were made exceedingly cunning” (8.280–281; see [[fine-as-spiders-webs]] and [[wily]]). Merry hears in χέε, “dropped”, and ἐξεκέχυντο the subtle and pliable nature of the net, and joins πέρι with δολόεντα, “exceeding cunning” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.278, 8.281). The song keeps returning to his craft: the bonds that close on the lovers are τεχνήεντες, “skillfully made” (8.297), the gods laugh “as they saw the craft”, τέχνας, “of Hephaestus of much wisdom” (8.327), and their saying ends on it, “lame as he is, by his craft” (8.332).

**Lemnos.** “he made as if to go to Lemnos, the well-built citadel, / which is by far the dearest to him of all lands” (8.283–284; see [[lemnos]] and [[well-built]]). εἴσατʼ ἴμεν is “made show of going”, and Merry compares Ovid's *fingit iter Lemnon* in the same story (on 8.283, citing *Art of Love* 2.579). Lemnos is the island where he came down when Zeus threw him from the divine threshold, and where the Sintians cared for him (*Iliad* 1.590–594; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]); Merry adds that the soil of Lemnos is volcanic and that the Sintians, as their connection with Hephaestus would suggest, seem to have been smiths by trade (on 8.283). Ares, who thinks him gone, tells Aphrodite that he is away “to Lemnos, to the Sintians of savage speech” (8.294; see [[sintians]]). The manuscripts vary between ἐστίν and ἔσκεν in 8.284, and the scholia record ἔπλετο πασέων (Merry's critical note on 8.284; see the translation's note on 8.284).

**Lame and slow.** “And the renowned god, lame in both legs, came near them, / having turned back before he reached the land of Lemnos; / for the Sun had kept watch for him and told him the tale” (8.300–302). He “stood in the doorway, and a savage anger gripped him; / and he shouted terribly, and called out to all the gods” (8.304–305):

{{quote:9f5bf51a-c958-5959-a8c9-ff73e4e0ebf4}}

ἀμφιγυήεις is commonly rendered “lame of both feet”; since it is generally joined with κλυτός, περικλυτός or κλυτοτέχνης, some have taken it as “of the two stout arms”, and Merry notes that Hephaestus was lame from his birth (on 8.300, citing *Iliad* 18.397; see [[lame-in-both-legs]] and [[crook-footed]]). The “two parents” he blames are, in the Iliad, Zeus, “our dear father” (*Iliad* 1.578), and Hera, who wished to hide him away because he was lame (*Iliad* 18.395–397; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]); Hesiod makes Hera bear him without a father (*Theogony* 927–929). His rival is ἀρτίπος, “sound of foot” (8.310), a word Homer uses otherwise only of Blind Folly (*Iliad* 9.505; see [[litai]]), and the gods turn the contrast into a saying (see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]): “the slow overtakes the swift, / as now Hephaestus, slow as he is, has caught Ares, / though he is the swiftest of the gods who hold Olympus, / lame as he is, by his craft” (8.329–332). On ἔργα γελαστά, the “laughable” deeds he calls them to see (8.307), see [[laughter-of-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 8.307.

**The husband of Aphrodite.** In the Iliad his wife is Charis, “Grace” (*Iliad* 18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]); in the song it is Aphrodite. Merry quotes the scholium that notes the difference: Homer as a whole does not know of Hephaestus living with Aphrodite, but with Charis, and Demodocus tells it “in his own story-making”. Hesiod gives him Aglaea, the youngest of the Graces (*Theogony* 945–946), and Merry grants that there were separate myths about the god's partner. The Chorizontes, who gave the two poems to different poets, argued from the difference for a long interval between them; Nitzsch answered that the same idea lies at the bottom of both forms of the legend, the union of a goddess of grace with the god who represents the highest development of art (Merry on 8.267; see the translation's note on 8.266–366). He means to hold the lovers until her father, Zeus, gives back the whole bride-price that he paid for “the dog-faced girl” (8.317–320; see [[bride-price]] and [[dog-face]]). κυνῶπις, “dog-faced”, is his word in the Iliad for his mother, who wished to hide him (*Iliad* 18.396), and Helen's for herself, at Troy and at Sparta (*Iliad* 3.180; 4.145). On ἐχέθυμος, “self-control”, see the translation's note on 8.320.

**Poseidon's surety.** Poseidon alone does not laugh, and keeps begging “Hephaestus, famed for his work, to set Ares free” (8.344–345; see [[poseidon]]); κλυτοεργός, “famed for his work”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row). Hephaestus answers with a saying, “pledges for the worthless are worthless things even to accept” (see [[pledges-for-the-worthless]]), and a question, “How could I bind you among the immortal gods, / if Ares should go off, escaping both the debt and the bonds?” (8.351–353). Merry takes δέοιμι at its full strength, “put thee in chains”, and paraphrases: if Ares, a slippery fellow, refuses to pay, what is the good of your promise, since I cannot put you in chains until the debt is paid (on 8.351–352; see the translation's notes on 8.351 and 8.352–353). When Poseidon promises to pay it himself, Hephaestus yields, “It is not possible, nor would it be fitting, to refuse your word” (8.358), and “So saying, the might of Hephaestus loosed the bonds” (8.359). μένος Ἡφαίστοιο is said only here, as “the sacred might of Alcinous” names the king in the same book (8.2, 8.4; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]).

**His names in the song.** ἄναξ, “lord Hephaestus” (8.270); κλυτοτέχνης, “famed for his craft” (8.286), as *Iliad* 1.571; περικλυτός, “renowned” (8.287); πολύφρων, “of much wisdom” (8.297, 8.327), as *Iliad* 21.367; περικλυτὸς ἀμφιγυήεις, “the renowned god, lame in both legs” (8.300, 8.349, 8.357); κλυτοεργός, “famed for his work” (8.345). See [[famed-for-his-craft]] and [[of-much-wisdom]]. The house to which the gods come is χαλκοβατὲς δῶ, “the bronze-floored house” (8.321; see [[bronze-floored]]), and in the Iliad his own house on Olympus is of bronze (*Iliad* 18.369–371).
