---
summary: Odysseus asks Demodocus to sing of the horse of wood that Epeius made with Athena's help and that he himself brought into the citadel as a snare (8.492–495). The song tells how the Argives burned their huts and sailed away while the best of them sat hidden in the horse in the Trojans' place of assembly, how the Trojans debated whether to split it, throw it from the heights or keep it as an offering, and how the Argives poured out of it and sacked the city (8.499–520).

**The request.** After the chine has been sent to the singer, Odysseus praises him for singing the fate of the Achaeans “as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another” (8.489–491), and asks for one part of it:

{{quote:833afaab-c52b-560e-ba54-37f6f2e9eea4}}

μετάβηθι, “change your theme”, is a change from the first lay, the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles at the beginning of the war, to its end (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.492; see the translation's note on 8.492, [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[demodocus]]); these subjects, Merry adds, fall within the range of the *Little Iliad*, from which Aristotle names the tragedies made (*Poetics* 1459b4–7). ἵππου κόσμον is “the fashioning of the horse”. For δόλον, “as a snare”, Aristarchus and Aristophanes read δόλῳ, “by a trick” (see the translation's note on 8.494, and [[dolos]]). The scholium on ἤγαγε, “brought”, makes the horse the reason Odysseus is called “sacker of cities”: the making was Epeius' and Athena's, the enterprise his; Köchly thought ἐς ἀκρόπολιν ἤγαγε badly drawn from 8.504 and “having filled it with the men” almost ridiculous (Merry on 8.494; see [[epeius]], [[athena]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]). Odysseus asks for the song of his own stratagem and names himself in it only as “brilliant Odysseus” (8.494), and the reward he offers is fame for the singer (8.497–498; see [[wondrous]]).

**The horse in the song.** Demodocus begins where the Argives “were sailing away, having thrown fire on their huts” (8.501), though they went no farther than Tenedos, Merry notes (on 8.501), “while the others, around famous Odysseus, were already / sitting in the place of assembly of the Trojans, hidden in the horse; / for the Trojans themselves had dragged it into the citadel” (8.502–504; see [[hut]], [[fire]] and [[assembly]]). Odysseus is the leader of the men inside as well as the contriver (Merry on 8.502). The Trojans sit around it and talk on and on, divided three ways, “either to split the hollow timber with the pitiless bronze, / or to drag it to the top of the height and throw it down the rocks, / or to let it stand as a great offering to appease the gods” (8.505–509; see the translation's notes on 8.505, 8.507 and 8.509, [[pitiless-bronze]] and [[trojans]]). Merry compares Virgil's description of the same debate, *scinditur incertum studia in contraria vulgus* (on 8.505; *Aeneid* 2.39), and takes ἐπʼ ἄκρης of the rocky edge of the citadel of Ilium above the Scamander (on 8.508). θελκτήριον, “to appease”, is the word of the “enchantments” in Aphrodite's strap (see [[aphrodites-band]]). The third counsel prevailed, “for it was fated that the city should perish once it had enfolded / the great wooden horse, where they were sitting, all the best / of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (8.511–513, the last verse as 4.273; see [[aisa]]); the subjunctive keeps, as far as it can, the words of the oracle (Merry on 8.511). Then the sons of the Achaeans sacked the city, “pouring out from the horse, leaving the hollow ambush” (8.514–515; see [[ambush]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]).
