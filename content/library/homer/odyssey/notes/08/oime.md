---
summary: The Muse sets Demodocus on to sing “from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky”, the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles (8.73–75); and Odysseus honors the singer with a portion of the chine because “the Muse has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers” (8.479–481).
---

**The lay of the quarrel.** When the feast is over, “the Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men, / from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky”, and the lay is named in apposition: “the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles” (8.73–75; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[demodocus]]). κλέα ἀνδρῶν are what Achilles sings to the lyre in his hut (*Iliad* 9.189); stories that the heroes of the Iliad sang themselves are in the Odyssey the property of the professional singer (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.73; see [[kleos]] and [[singers]]). The genitive οἴμης need not be explained by attraction: Merry takes it as partitive, “from that tale the fame whereof”, as Nicanor explained it, “from that lay whose fame was wide” (on 8.74), and a scholium prefers a stop after ἀνδρῶν to joining κλέα ἀνδρῶν οἴμης (Merry's critical note on 8.73). The translation follows them. The fame of the lay had reached the sky “at that time”, τότε; the man it sang of, when he begins his tale that same night, will tell his hosts that his own fame reaches the sky (καί μευ κλέος οὐρανὸν ἵκει, 9.20; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]).

**The lays the Muse teaches.** At the evening feast Odysseus sends Demodocus a portion of the chine and gives his reason: “For among all men upon the earth singers / have their share of honor and respect, because / the Muse has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers” (8.479–481; see [[time]], [[aidos]], [[phylon]] and [[muses]]). The verb is ἐδίδαξε, “taught”, and he uses it again when he praises the singer: “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo” (8.488; see [[memory-and-the-muses]]). The lay is not called οἴμη when he asks for the third song; he asks for “the fashioning of the horse” (ἵππου κόσμον, 8.492), and the singer begins “stirred by the god” (8.499; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).
