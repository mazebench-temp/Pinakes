summary: The handsomest of the Phaeacians and the best boxer at the games, where he stands up with his brothers (8.116–119, 8.130). He proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows any contest, and at Euryalus' word challenges him; the stranger answers him by name, and afterwards challenges all the Phaeacians except Laodamas, his host (8.131–157, 8.204–211). He dances the ball dance with Halius (8.370–380).

**At the games.** When the young men rise for the contests, Euryalus is “the best in looks and form / of all the Phaeacians after noble Laodamas” (8.116–117), the verses with which the poem measures Ajax by Achilles (11.469–470 = 24.17–18; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[ajax]]). “And up stood the three sons of noble Alcinous, / Laodamas and Halius and godlike Clytoneus” (8.118–119; see [[halius-son-of-alcinous]] and [[clytoneus]]). Of the five events he wins the last: “and in boxing, again, Laodamas, the good son of Alcinous” (8.130; see [[contests]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The invitation.** He proposes to try the guest:

{{quote:3d0b7348-92e8-536b-b8b7-6d24da53d964}}

οἶδέ τε καὶ δεδάηκε may mean “knows by having learned” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.134); χεῖρας ὕπερθεν, “his hands above”, is a fixed description of the arms as attached to the upper part of the body, and μέγα τε σθένος goes with φυήν, the limbs being its explanation (Merry on 8.135, 8.136). The poem describes Odysseus' athletic form in the same way when he strips to fight the beggar Irus (18.67–69, Greek only; Merry on 8.136; see [[odysseus]]). συγχεῦαι, “to unstring”, is a word generally used of the mind, here of the body (Merry on 8.139; see the translation's note on 8.139, [[sea]] and [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]]). Euryalus approves and bids him go and challenge the stranger himself (8.140–142), and he does:

{{quote:c0049dc6-d9c9-5ed3-9e78-f6534c80508b}}

ἔοικε, “it is fitting”, is “the primitive praise of γυμναστική”, and ὄφρα κʼ ἔῃσιν is “as long as he lives” (Merry on 8.146, 8.147; see [[kleos]] and [[no-greater-fame]]). ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father”, is the respectful address of the young to an older man (see the translation's key words). His promise that the ship has been drawn down and the companions are ready looks back to the morning, when the fifty-two young men launched the ship on her first voyage (8.34–55; see [[pompe]]).

**The stranger's answer.** “Laodamas, why do you bid me do these things, taunting me? / Sorrows are on my mind far more than contests” (8.153–154; see [[odysseus]], [[algos]] and [[achos]]): the verb is plural, κελεύετε, and the answer goes to the young men who have urged the challenge; καὶ μᾶλλον is not a higher degree but the emphatic καί often prefixed to adverbs of intensity (Merry on 8.154). It is Euryalus' taunt that moves him to throw the discus (8.158–198; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[discus]]).

**The host.** When the stranger has thrown, he challenges any of the Phaeacians in boxing, wrestling or running, “except Laodamas himself. / For he is my host; who would fight with one who makes him welcome? / Witless indeed and a nobody is that man / who brings on strife in contests with his host / in a foreign land; he cuts short all that is his own” (8.207–211; see [[xenia]] and [[strife-with-ones-host]]). τῶν δʼ ἄλλων, “of the rest” (8.204), looks forward to the exception, and Odysseus names only Laodamas because he stood prominently before him as the challenger: if guestship barred even the challenger, it would be understood to bar the other two sons of the house (Merry on 8.204). τίς ἂν … μάχοιτο is “Who would care to strive with his entertainer?” (Merry on 8.208). It was Laodamas whom the king made stand up from the chair beside him for the suppliant on the evening before (7.170–171).

**The ball dance.** When Demodocus has sung of Ares and Aphrodite, the king calls on his sons:

{{quote:420914f9-ca36-5160-95fd-372aa296aed8}}

The game here described, in which the ball is tossed up and caught before the leaper reaches the ground, was called, Eustathius says, the οὐρανία, and much on the ball-play of the ancients is collected by Athenaeus and Pollux (Merry on 8.372, as he reports them; see [[ball]] and [[polybus-the-phaeacian]]). ἀνʼ ἰθύν is “straight upwards” (Merry on 8.377; see the translation's note on 8.377), and with ταρφέʼ ἀμειβομένω, “changing places again and again”, the play becomes a dance in which the ball flies from one to the other, in time to the music, while the others beat time with their feet (Merry on 8.379; see [[dance]]). His sister's play with her handmaids on the shore was a ball game too, and her throw that missed the handmaid woke the stranger (6.100–101, 6.115–117). The stranger tells the king: “you boasted that your dancers were the best, / and indeed it has proved true; awe holds me as I look” (8.383–384; see [[awe-holds-me]] and [[alcinous]]). He is not named again in the poem.

**His epithets in this book.** ἀμύμων, “noble” (8.117; see [[blameless]]); ἀγαθὸς πάϊς Ἀλκινόοιο, “the good son of Alcinous” (8.130, 8.143); πάϊς Ἀλκινόοιο, “the son of Alcinous” (8.132).
