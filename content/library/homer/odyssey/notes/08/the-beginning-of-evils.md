**The oracle.** The lay is “the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus, / how once they contended at a rich feast of the gods / with terrible words” (8.75–77; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[oime]]), and Agamemnon “rejoiced in his mind”, secretly (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.78), “that the best of the Achaeans were contending” (8.77–78; see [[aristos]]). He rejoiced because the quarrel answered an oracle: “For so Phoebus Apollo had told him in prophecy / in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold / to consult the oracle” (8.79–81; see [[pytho]] and [[apollo]]). The scholia tell what the oracle said, that Ilium would be taken when the best of the Greeks quarreled (Merry on 8.75); the poem gives only its result.

**The beginning of bane.** τότε, “then”, is the time when Agamemnon consulted the oracle: the war with Troy was just beginning, and the great wave of evils, κακῶν τρικυμία, was just rolling on, soon to sweep so many lives away (Merry on 8.81). κυλίνδω is the verb of a rolling wave (“many a swollen wave rolls on”, *Iliad* 11.307), and of bane rolling upon men: “Here is this bane rolling down on us, mighty Hector” (*Iliad* 11.347), “a god is rolling a bane upon the Danaans” (*Iliad* 17.688), and, of the suitors, “a great bane is rolling upon them” (2.163). πῆμα is “bane” (see the translation's table of key words); Hector calls Paris a great bane to the Trojans (*Iliad* 6.282–283). The phrase πήματος ἀρχή stands with the Iliad's κακοῦ ἀρχή and νείκεος ἀρχή (*Iliad* 11.604, 22.116; see the entry), but here the beginning is not one man's act. It falls on both sides, “Trojans and Danaans”, and it comes “through the counsels of great Zeus” (Διὸς μεγάλου διὰ βουλάς, 8.82), as the Iliad's proem sets its whole story under the plan of Zeus (*Iliad* 1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[boule]]). Some editions lacked the two verses, and they were athetized; Merry finds the notice in the scholia incomplete, since no sufficient reason is given (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 8.74–82).

**Another version.** Merry reports a different shape of the story, from Demetrius of Phalerum as the scholia on 3.267 quote him: it was Menelaus, with Odysseus, who went to Delphi and asked the god about the coming expedition to Ilium (Merry on 8.81). Merry takes the quarrel to belong to the beginning of the war, not to its end (on 8.75; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]]). The song Odysseus asks for at the evening feast is of the war's end, the horse that brought the sack of the city (8.492–520; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).
