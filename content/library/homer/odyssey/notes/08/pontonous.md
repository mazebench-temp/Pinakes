**The singer's chair.** The king had bidden the Phaeacians call the divine singer, and “a herald went to fetch the divine singer” (8.43–47; see [[alcinous]]). When the sacrifice is made ready, “the herald came near, leading the trusty singer”, and the herald is named as he serves him: “And Pontonous set for him a silver-studded chair / in the midst of the banqueters, leaning it against a tall pillar; / and he hung the clear-toned lyre from its peg / right above his head, and showed him where to take it in his hands, / the herald; and beside him he set a basket and a beautiful table, / and beside it a cup of wine, to drink whenever his heart bade him” (8.62–70; see [[demodocus]], [[throne]], [[silver-studded]], [[lyre]], [[basket]], [[table]] and [[cup]]). ἐπέφραδε χερσὶν ἑλέσθαι means that he guided the blind singer's hand to the place where the lyre was hanging, and need not mean that he told him (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.68; see the translation's note on 8.68). He serves the singer as on the evening before he served the wine for Zeus (7.178–183), the herald of the house at the king's feasts (see [[heralds]]).

**The other heralds of the book.** The herald who hangs the lyre on its peg again and leads the singer out to the games (8.105–107), who brings the lyre from the king's house for the dance (8.256–262), and who leads the singer in again at the evening feast and carries him Odysseus' portion of the chine (8.471–483) is not named; the verses that bring him in repeat the morning's (8.105 = 8.67, 8.471 = 8.62, 8.473 = 8.66), but the poem does not say that he is Pontonous. On the day of the escort it is Pontonous whom the king bids mix the wine for the last libation (13.49–53, Greek only; see the entry).
