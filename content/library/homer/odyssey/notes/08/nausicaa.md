summary: Her farewell. As Odysseus comes from the bath to the evening feast, she stands by the doorpost of the hall, marvels at him, and bids him remember her in his own land, since to her first he owes the price for his life; he answers that if he reaches home he will pray to her there as to a god all his days, “for you gave me my life, maiden” (8.457–468). She is not seen or named again in the poem.

**At the doorpost.** She has not been seen since she came home with the washing on the evening of his arrival (7.1–13). Now, as the stranger, bathed and dressed, goes to join the men drinking wine:

{{quote:5fbc1686-4549-5401-9ced-42c4d03d7f1b}}

θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, “who had her beauty from the gods” (8.457), is the phrase of her handmaids, who had their beauty from the Graces (Χαρίτων ἄπο, 6.18; see [[beauty-and-its-price]] and [[graces]]). She stands where Penelope stands when she comes down among the suitors, in the same verse: “she stood by the doorpost of the strongly made hall” (8.458 = 1.333; again 16.415, 18.209, 21.64; see [[penelope]]). The daughter of the house does not come into the hall among the men; she marvels at him from its door (see [[thambos]]), as on the shore, when Athena had poured grace upon him after his bath in the river, “the girl gazed at him” (6.235–237).

**Her farewell.** χαῖρε, ξεῖνʼ, ἵνα is “may it be well with thee, stranger, in order that”: she wishes him well for his own sake, but the wish is quickened by the affection she showed on the shore (6.239–245), and now takes the form of a hope that she may live in his memory as his benefactress (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.461; see [[nausicaas-wish]]). Merry sees in it one form of a saying of the later Greeks, that benefactors wish those they have helped to live, as men who will repay the favor. Aristotle reports it as the common opinion, which likens the benefactor to a creditor, and himself prefers to compare the benefactor's love for the one he has helped to a craftsman's love for his own work, and above all a poet's for his poems (Merry on 8.461; *Nicomachean Ethics* 9.7, 1167b17–1168a3). ζωάγρια, in the Iliad the price paid to a conqueror for sparing a defeated man's life (*Iliad* 18.407), is here the debt of gratitude for a life saved, and “the price for your life” covers both (Merry on 8.462; see [[zoagria]] and the translation's note on 8.461–462). πρώτῃ, “to me first”, recalls her father's words, “it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (7.301; see [[supplication]]).

**His answer.** For the first time he calls her by her name: “Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (8.464), with the verse-end that the Iliad gives Andromache, the daughter of great-hearted Eetion (*Iliad* 6.395; see [[alcinous]] and [[great-hearted]]). οὕτω means “even as thou wishest”, looking back to her words, with the infinitives explaining it, and τῷ is “in that case”, should he reach home (Merry on 8.465, 8.467). The two verses of the wish and the prayer are those with which Telemachus answers Helen's reading of an omen at Sparta (8.465, 8.467 = 15.180–181; see [[helen]]), and in the Iliad Achilles boasts over Hector, “to whom the Trojans throughout the city prayed as to a god” (*Iliad* 22.394; see [[euchomai]], [[prayer]] and [[like-a-god]]). σὺ γάρ μʼ ἐβιώσαο, “for you gave me my life”, is the only causative use of βιόω, “live”, in Homer, and the aorist middle is found only here; Merry finds it “more graphic” than the scholium's “brought me into life” (Merry on 8.468). Then “He spoke, and sat down on a chair beside King Alcinous” (8.469; see [[odysseus]]).

**The last of her.** The poem does not tell her answer, nor bring her back: after this she is not named again. Her wish to have such a man for her husband (6.244–245) and her father's wish to have him for a son-in-law (7.311–316) end in a farewell at the door (see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).

**Her epithets in this book.** θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, “who had her beauty from the gods” (8.457); θύγατερ μεγαλήτορος Ἀλκινόοιο, “daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (8.464); κούρη, “maiden” (8.468).
