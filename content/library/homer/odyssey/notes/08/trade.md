summary: Euryalus' taunt likens the stranger to a merchant captain, “a leader of sailors who are traders, / and who is mindful of his freight, or watches over his homeward cargo / and his greedily snatched gains; you are not like an athlete” (8.161–164). The insult rests on the gulf between the trader and the nobleman who competes in the games.

**The merchant captain.** Laodamas has invited the stranger to the games, and the stranger has answered that sorrows are on his mind far more than contests (8.145–157; see [[no-greater-fame]]). Euryalus takes him at his word and finds another trade for him: one who goes to and fro with a many-benched ship, captain of a crew of traders, πρηκτῆρες (see [[prexis]]). Merry's version runs: “But to such an one as plies with a many-benched ship, captain of a crew that are traders, and is either in charge of a freight or vigilant over a home-cargo of greedily gotten gains; no athlete's mould is thine”, the τε … καί being disjunctive (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.161, 8.162–164; see the translation's note on 8.161–164). Some read ἐπίστροφος, with Aristophanes, for ἐπίσκοπος, the one who “watches over” the cargo, and ἑταίρων for ὁδαίων (Merry's critical notes). The gains are “greedily snatched”, κερδέων θʼ ἁρπαλέων (8.164; see [[kerdos]]).

**The insult.** The stranger's answer shows how it was meant: “you have not spoken well; you are like a reckless man”, and “your word bites the heart” (8.166, 8.185; see [[different-gifts]]). The trader is the one who thinks of his cargo and his profit; the athlete is the nobleman who competes for fame (see [[contests]]). The Odyssey's traders are mostly Phoenicians, “greedy knaves” (τρῶκται) who come in a black ship with countless trinkets (15.415–416, a plain rendering), and in his Cretan tale the stranger will make himself the dupe of one (14.287–291). Athena, in the likeness of the Taphian Mentes, had passed herself off as a trader sailing for bronze with a cargo of iron (1.180–184; see the entry). The discus thrown beyond all the marks is the stranger's answer to the insult (8.186–198; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).
