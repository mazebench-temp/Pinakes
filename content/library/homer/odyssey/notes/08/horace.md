---
summary: Merry's notes set three of Horace's poems beside this book. The Phaeacians who love the feast, the lyre and warm baths become Horace's byword for a well-fed man of pleasure; Euryalus' wish that the storm winds carry off his words is Horace's gift of his cares to the winds; and Alcinous' “the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” is Tiresias' “whatever I say will either be or not be”.
---

- **The Phaeacians' pleasures.** “Always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances / and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” (8.248–249). Merry, noting the color of luxury in the description, cites Horace's wish to come home from the coast of Velia and Salernum “fat and a Phaeacian”, *pinguis ut inde domum possim Phaeaxque reverti* (*Epistles* 1.15.24), and his “young men of Alcinous, busier than was right in caring for their skin”, who thought it fine to sleep till noon and to lull their cares to the sound of the lyre (*Epistles* 1.2.28–31; plain renderings). He reports too the scholiast's view of a peaceful people, and the harsher judgment of Heracleides of Pontus, quoted in the scholia on 13.110, that their hospitality and their quick escorts were a pretense, the selfishness of a pleasure-loving people afraid that strangers might take their land (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.248): [[phaeacians]], [[different-gifts]] and [[horace-on-the-suitors]].
- **To the winds.** Euryalus, making amends, prays that if any word was spoken that was terrible, “may the storm winds at once snatch it up and carry it away” (8.408–409). Merry cites Horace's “a friend of the Muses, I will hand my sadness and my fears to the wanton winds to carry into the Cretan sea” (*Musis amicus tristitiam et metus / tradam protervis in mare Creticum / portare ventis*, *Odes* 1.26.1–3, a plain rendering), with like phrases of Apollonius and Theocritus (Merry on 8.409): [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[apollonius]].
- **Either be or not be.** Of his father's prophecy that Poseidon would shatter a Phaeacian ship and cover the city with a mountain, Alcinous says, “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.570–571). Merry finds the truism not unlike Tiresias' in Horace, who answers Ulysses, *o Laertiade, quidquid dicam, aut erit aut non*, “son of Laertes, whatever I say will either be or not be: for great Apollo grants me the gift of prophecy” (*Satires* 2.5.59–60, a plain rendering; Merry on 8.571): [[nausithous]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[horace]].
