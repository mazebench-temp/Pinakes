summary: Alcinous' people do not keep a stranger long “mourning, for want of an escort” (8.33). At the first song the stranger covers his head and laments (8.92), at the third he has “never ceased from miserable lamentation” (8.540), and the king asks why he weeps and mourns (8.577); in the simile the captive woman “wails shrilly” over her husband (8.527).
# γοάασκεν (8.92), κωκύει (8.527).
grc: [γοάασκεν, κωκύει]
en: [lament, wails shrilly]
---

The king's custom forbids a stranger's mourning in his house: “no other man either, who comes to my house, / stays here long, mourning, for want of an escort” (ὀδυρόμενος, 8.32–33; see [[pompe]]). Yet the stranger mourns twice at the singer's songs. At the first, whenever the singing begins again, “Odysseus would cover his head again and lament” (γοάασκεν, 8.92), the iterative of γοάω, the verb of the wailing for the dead (see [[tears]]). At the third Alcinous stops the singer: “from then on he has never ceased from miserable lamentation, / the stranger” (ὀιζυροῖο γόοιο, 8.540–541), and asks him “why you weep and mourn within your heart” (ὀδύρεαι, 8.577). Between them stands the simile of a true lament, the woman who throws herself around her dying husband “and wails shrilly” (λίγα κωκύει, 8.527; see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]]), the cry of the women of the Iliad over Hector (*Iliad* 22.407, 24.703).
