---
summary: Circe is first named here. The complicated knot with which Odysseus ties the chest of his gifts is one that “lady Circe had once taught him” (8.447–448); the story of her island waits for his tale to the Phaeacians.
---

**The knot.** Arete has packed the gifts in a chest and bids the stranger see to the lid himself and tie a knot on it, “so that no one may do you harm on the way, when hereafter / you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship” (8.443–445; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[chest]]). “But when much-enduring brilliant Odysseus heard this, / at once he fitted on the lid, and quickly tied a knot on it, / a complicated one, which lady Circe had once taught him” (8.446–448; see [[queenly]]). δέδαε is the factitive reduplicated aorist, “made him learn it in his mind”, and what she taught him was the particular complicated knot with which to tie the fastening (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.448). δεσμός, “knot” (see the translation's key-word row), is the word of the bonds of Hephaestus earlier in the book (8.274–360; see [[hephaestus]]).

**Named before her story.** The Phaeacians are not told who Circe is. Odysseus names her again at the beginning of his tale, beside Calypso, as a goddess who held him back in her halls, longing for him to be her husband (9.29–32; see [[calypso]]), and tells her story in Books 10–12: her island of Aeaea, the men turned into swine, the year he stayed with her, and the journey to the house of Hades on which she sent him (see the general article). πότνια Κίρκη, “lady Circe”, is her title there too (10.394, 10.549, 12.36; see the translation's formula row).
