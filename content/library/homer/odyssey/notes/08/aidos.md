summary: Athena makes the stranger “feared and respected” among the Phaeacians (8.22); he hides his face and weeps, “for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians” (8.86); the goddesses stay at home “for shame” (8.324); and singers have their share “of honor and respect” (8.480). The revered of the book are the queen and the stranger (8.420, 8.544).
# αἰδοῖος (8.22; again 5.88, 14.234, 17.578, 19.254), αἰδοῖ (8.172, 8.324; again 14.505), αἰδοίοιο (8.544).
grc: [αἰδοῖος, αἰδοῖ, αἰδοίοιο]
en: [modesty]
---

**Feared and respected.** Athena pours grace on the stranger's head and shoulders “so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians, / and feared and respected” (δεινός τʼ αἰδοῖός τε, 8.21–22; see [[charis]] and [[athena]]). The pair is the Iliad's: Helen greets Priam, “You are one I respect, dear father-in-law, and one I fear” (*Iliad* 3.172), and Hephaestus calls Thetis “a goddess I fear and respect” (*Iliad* 18.394). In his Cretan tale to Eumaeus the stranger will say the same of himself, that he was feared and respected among the Cretans (14.234).

**Ashamed.** At the first song he draws his purple mantle over his head and covers his face, “for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians, letting tears fall from beneath his brows” (αἴδετο γὰρ Φαίηκας, 8.84–86; see [[tears]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). The verb takes the people before whom one feels shame, as Hector feels it before the Trojans (*Iliad* 6.442, 22.105).

**Modesty and reverence.** In the stranger's answer to Euryalus, the man whose words a god crowns with beauty speaks surely, αἰδοῖ μειλιχίῃ, “with soothing modesty” (8.172). Merry, from the parallel portrait of a king in Hesiod (*Theogony* 92), takes the words with the onlookers, who look on the speaker “with submissive reverence”, since αἰδώς does not take the meaning of dignity; the nearest approach to it, he says, is the Hymn to Demeter, where αἰδώς and χάρις shine in a woman's eyes “as in the eyes of kings who give judgment” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.170–172; *Hymn to Demeter* 213–215; see the translation's note on 8.171–172 and [[different-gifts]]).

**For shame.** When Hephaestus calls the gods to see Ares and Aphrodite in his bonds, “the goddesses, being female, stayed each at home for shame” (θηλύτεραι δὲ θεαὶ μένον αἰδοῖ οἴκοι ἑκάστη, 8.324; see [[olympians]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). θηλύτεραι is rendered “being female” (see the translation's table of key words). The gods come, and laugh (see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

**Revered and respected.** The queen is the sons' “revered mother”, μητρὶ παρʼ αἰδοίῃ, beside whom they set the gifts (8.419–420; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The guest is “the revered stranger”, ξείνοιο … αἰδοίοιο, for whose sake the escort and the gifts have been made ready (8.544; see [[xenia]]). And singers “have their share of honor and respect” (τιμῆς ἔμμοροί εἰσι καὶ αἰδοῦς), because the Muse has taught them their lays (8.479–481; see [[time]] and [[singers]]).
