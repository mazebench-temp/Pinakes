---
summary: Two prophecies frame the book. In the first song Agamemnon rejoices at the quarrel of the best of the Achaeans because Apollo had foretold it at Pytho (8.77–82); at the end Alcinous tells his father's prophecy, that Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship returning from an escort and cover their city with a great mountain, “and these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” (8.564–571).
# Unnamed: Apollo's oracle (8.79-82); Nausithous' prophecy (8.564-571).
refs: [8.79-82, 8.564-571]
---

**Apollo's oracle.** “For so Phoebus Apollo had told him in prophecy / in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold / to consult the oracle” (8.79–81; see [[pytho]] and [[apollo]]). χρείων is “giving response” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.79), and the stone threshold is that of the temple, as in Achilles' words of “all that is shut within the stone threshold of the Archer, / Phoebus Apollo, in rocky Pytho” (*Iliad* 9.404–405; Merry on 8.80; see the translation's note on 8.80). The scholia say that the oracle foretold the fall of Troy when the best of the Greeks quarreled (Merry on 8.75); Merry takes it to have been given before the war (on 8.75, 8.81; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]).

**Nausithous' prophecy.** At the end of the day Alcinous tells what he heard from his father: Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians their escorts, and one day would shatter a ship of theirs as it came back from an escort and cover their city round about with a great mountain (8.564–569; see [[nausithous]], [[pompe]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). The king's last words on it leave it to the god, τὰ δέ κεν θεὸς ἢ τελέσειεν, / ἤ κʼ ἀτέλεστʼ εἴη, “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled” (8.570–571), with the verb of fulfillment, τελέω, and its negative; Merry likens the truism to Tiresias' *aut erit aut non* in Horace (on 8.571). In Book 13 the first part comes to pass (13.159–164; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).
