---
summary: In Book 8 the sea is the Phaeacians' element and Odysseus' ordeal. The young men drag the ship for his escort down to the shining sea (8.34–55); Laodamas holds that nothing is worse than the sea to unstring a man, and Odysseus speaks of the grievous waves and of being worn down among many waves (8.138–139, 8.183, 8.231–233); and Alcinous tells of the ships that cross the gulf of the sea wrapped in mist and cloud, and of Poseidon's threat to one of them on the misty sea (8.555–569).
# Unnamed: the waves, κύματα, κύμασιν (8.183, 8.232).
refs: [8.183, 8.232]
---

**Over the sea.** Athena, in the likeness of the herald, calls the Phaeacians to see the stranger “who has newly come to the house of wise-hearted Alcinous, / after wandering over the sea, like the immortals in form” (8.13–14; see [[athena]] and [[like-the-immortals]]).

**The launching.** Alcinous bids them “drag a black ship down to the shining sea / on her first voyage” (8.34–35; see [[ships]], [[black-ships]] and [[shining-sea]]), and the fifty-two young men go “to the shore of the barren sea” (8.49; see [[shore]] and [[barren-sea]]): “And when they had come down to the ship and the sea, / they dragged the black ship down into deep water” (8.50–51). The launch is told, almost word for word, in the verses in which the suitors launch their ship to lie in wait for Telemachus, from ἁλὸς βένθοσδε, “into deep water”, to the anchoring (8.49–55; 4.779–785).

**Worse than the sea.** Laodamas, looking the stranger over, finds him strong in build and not lacking youth, “but he has been broken by many evils; / for I, for my part, say there is nothing else worse than the sea / to unstring a man, however strong he may be” (8.137–139; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). συγχεῦαι, “to unstring”, is a word generally used of the mind, as in “do not confuse my heart” (*Iliad* 9.612), and here of the body; Livius Andronicus, in his Latin translation of the line, rendered it with *macerare*, “wear down” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.139; see the translation's note on 8.139).

**The waves.** Odysseus speaks of the sea himself in his answer to Euryalus: “But now I am held fast by misery and sorrows; for I have endured much, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (8.182–183; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]). The second verse is the one with which the Iliad recalls all that Achilles had endured with Patroclus (*Iliad* 24.8), and it returns twice in Book 13 (13.91, 13.264). πείρων, “passing through”, belongs properly to the waves, as in πέλαγος μέσον … / τέμνειν, “cut across the middle of the open sea” (3.174–175), and is extended to the wars by zeugma (Merry on 8.183). At the end of his boast he excepts the running: “for I have been too shamefully overcome / among many waves, since on board the ship my keep was not / unfailing; and so my limbs are loosened” (8.231–233; see the translation's note on 8.232–233).

**The Phaeacians' sea.** Alcinous concedes the boxing and the wrestling but claims for his people that they “are the best with ships”, and bids the dancers show the stranger how far they surpass others “in seafaring and in running and in dancing and in song” (8.247, 8.253; see [[phaeacians]]). At the end of the day he asks the stranger's land, so that the ships may carry him there: they know the minds of men and their cities, “and most swiftly they cross the gulf of the sea, / wrapped in mist and cloud; and never do they / have any fear of coming to harm or of perishing” (8.561–563; see [[pompe]]). λαῖτμα, “gulf”, is the open sea that Odysseus swam across to Scheria (5.409, 7.276; see the Book 5 note), and “wrapped in mist and cloud” means that the ships pass over the sea unseen (Merry on 8.556). Then he tells his father's prophecy, that Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship “as it came back from an escort on the misty sea” (8.564–569; see [[poseidon]], [[misty]] and [[nausithous]]). The words return when Poseidon tells Zeus that he means to do it (13.149–152).
