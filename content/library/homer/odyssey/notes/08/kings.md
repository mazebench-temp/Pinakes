**The scepter-bearing kings.** At the place of assembly Alcinous lays the launching of the ship on the young men, “but the rest of you, / the scepter-bearing kings, come to my beautiful house, / so that we may make the stranger welcome in the halls” (8.40–42), and “they followed with him, / the scepter-bearing kings” (8.46–47; see [[scepter-bearing]], [[alcinous]] and [[xenia]]). οἱ ἄλλοι / σκηπτοῦχοι βασιλῆες is the epexegetic use of a noun after ἄλλος, “the rest of you, the scepter-bearing kings”, as ἔκτοθεν ἄλλων / μνηστήρων, “apart from the others, the suitors” (1.132–133; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.40 and 1.132). They are the men whom the king and Athena address as “leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (8.11, 8.26, 8.97, 8.387, 8.536; see [[hegemon]]), and Odysseus sits in their assembly “begging the king and all the people” (8.157; see [[supplication]]).

**Twelve kings and a thirteenth.** When the stranger has praised the dancers, the king proposes a guest-gift: “For twelve distinguished kings throughout the land / rule as leaders, and I myself am the thirteenth; / of these let each of you bring him a freshly washed mantle and a tunic / and a talent of precious gold” (8.390–393; see [[guest-gifts]], [[preeminent]] and [[talent]]). A scholium draws from the verses that the constitution was an aristocracy, with Alcinous preeminent among the kings; on Ithaca there are many kings (1.394), the word meaning, as here, dependent princes, and others have compared the twelve districts that Theseus brought under the central authority of one king at Athens (Merry on 8.390; see [[basileus]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). “So he spoke, and they all approved and bade it be done, / and each sent forth a herald to bring the gifts” (8.398–399), the second verse being the one with which the suitors send for their gifts to Penelope (18.291, Greek only): those who approve are the chieftains to whom the proposal was made, as the next verse shows, not the whole company (Merry on 8.398; see [[heralds]]). The king himself adds a golden goblet of his own, and his wife a mantle and a tunic (8.424–432, 8.441; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The king among them.** Alcinous is the king of the book, βασιλῆα in the stranger's plea (8.157) and in the narrator's “He spoke, and sat down on a chair beside King Alcinous” (8.469; see [[alcinous]]); the herald brings the lyre δόμου ἐκ βασιλῆος, “from the king's house” (8.257).
