summary: The colonnades, courts and rooms of Alcinous' house fill with the Phaeacians for the feast (8.56–58); the singer's chair stands against a pillar of the hall, Arete brings the chest from her chamber, and Nausicaa stands by the doorpost of the hall (8.66, 8.439, 8.458). In the song the house of Hephaestus is the scene of the adultery and the trap, from his chamber and its roof beam to the doorway where the gods stand (8.268–325).

**The house fills.** When the escort ship is moored, the young men “went to the great house of wise-hearted Alcinous. / And the colonnades and the courts and the rooms were filled with men / as they gathered” (8.56–58; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[feast]]). αἴθουσαι is plural, W. W. Merry explains, because it takes in both the colonnade of the court and the colonnade of the house; the ἕρκεα are the outdoor premises enclosed by the yard wall, nearly the same as the court, αὐλή; and the δόμοι are the various rooms of the house, not, as a scholium took δόμοι ἀνδρῶν, the men's halls (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.57). Inside are the hall, μέγαρον, where the stranger is welcomed and the singer is led out (8.42, 8.106), the pillar against which the singer's chair is set (8.66, 8.473; see [[throne]]), the chamber, θάλαμος, from which Arete brings out the chest (8.439; see [[chest]]), and the hall's doorpost, where Nausicaa stands “by the doorpost of the strongly made hall” (8.458; see [[door]] and the translation's row πύκα ποιητός).

**The house of Hephaestus.** The song is set in a house. Ares and Aphrodite “first lay together in the house of Hephaestus” (8.268); Hephaestus goes “to the chamber where his bed lay” and pours the bonds around it and “from above, from the roof beam” (8.277–279; see [[bed]] and [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]]); Ares, seeing Hephaestus go, “went to the house of renowned Hephaestus” and comes inside (8.287–290), and the husband comes home “grieving at heart” and stands in the doorway to call the gods (8.303–305). μέλαθρον is the roof beam, the topmost beam in the middle of the roof, as a scholium explains, which derives it from μέλας, “black”, because the smoke blackened it (Merry on 8.279). The gods gather “to the bronze-floored house” (8.321; see [[bronze-floored]]).

**Other houses.** Alcinous hopes that the stranger will tell of the Phaeacians “when in your own halls / you feast beside your wife and your children” (8.242–243); Eurytus died young and “did not reach old age / in his halls” (8.226–227; see [[eurytus]]); Odysseus has not been cared for so “since he left the house of Calypso of the lovely hair” (8.452; see [[calypso]]); and in the last song he goes “to the house of Deiphobus” in the sack of Troy (8.517; see [[deiphobus]]).
