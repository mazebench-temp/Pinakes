summary: Odysseus draws his great purple mantle over his head to hide his tears at the first song and takes it off at every pause (8.84–88), and throws the discus with the mantle still on (8.186). A freshly washed mantle and a tunic are the gift of each of the thirteen kings, and Arete adds her own to the chest (8.392, 8.425, 8.441); after the bath the slave women put a fine cloak and a tunic around him (8.455).
# φάρει (8.186), first met in the Odyssey here; the Iliad's φάρεϊ (18.353), the same letters, is in its Book 18 note.
grc: [φάρει]

**The mantle over his head.** At the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles the stranger “took his great purple mantle in his strong hands / and drew it down over his head, and covered his handsome face; / for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians” (8.84–86; see [[purple]], [[aidos]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). W. W. Merry describes the garment: the φᾶρος, a square piece of cloth, was put on so as to cover the left arm and shoulder, leaving the right arm bare, with a long corner hanging from the right shoulder; this corner Odysseus throws over his head from behind (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.85; see the translation's note on 8.85). At every pause he takes the mantle from his head and pours a libation, and at every new beginning he covers his head again (8.87–92; see [[cup]]).

**With his mantle on.** Stung by Euryalus, “he spoke, and sprang up with his mantle still on, and seized a discus” (8.186; see [[discus]]). αὐτῷ φάρει is the sociative dative strengthened by αὐτός, “mantle and all”, just as he was (Merry on 8.186, after Monro; see the translation's note on 8.186).

**The gifts.** Alcinous bids each of the twelve kings bring “a freshly washed mantle and a tunic / and a talent of precious gold” (8.392–393; see [[kings]], [[tunic]], [[talent]] and [[guest-gifts]]); the phrase returns when he bids Arete put “a freshly washed mantle and a tunic” of her own in the chest, and she “herself put a mantle and a fine tunic” in it (8.425, 8.441; see [[chest]], [[washing-troughs]] and the translation's note on 8.425, 8.441). After the bath “the slave women … put a fine cloak and a tunic around him” (8.454–455; see [[bathing]] and [[handmaids]]). The verse that dresses a man after his bath names either garment: a mantle at Pylos (3.467), woolly cloaks at Sparta (4.50), and here the χλαῖνα, as in Circe's house (10.365, Greek only).
