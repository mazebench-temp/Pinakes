summary: The games of the Phaeacians keep the frame of the contests at Patroclus' funeral without its prizes: the young men rise one by one, “There rose up Acroneus and Ocyalus and Elatreus” and “up rose Euryalus too” (8.111–119); the footrace is told in the Iliad's verse, “For them the running was strained to the full from the post” (8.121); and the winners of the five contests are named in a few verses (8.120–130).
refs: [8.110-130, 8.143-145, 8.186-198]
passages:
  - 8.109-130 | The games of the Phaeacians
---

**The rising.** The competitors stand up as the Iliad's do, each with a verse or a half-verse, ὦρτο μέν … ἂν δὲ καί … ἂν δʼ ἔσταν, and the last are the king's three sons (8.111–119; see the general article, step 3). 8.121 is the first half of the Iliad's footrace verse (*Iliad* 23.758), and the winner's lead is the furrow-length of a pair of mules, the distance by which Odysseus and Diomedes let Dolon run past them in the Iliad (8.124; *Iliad* 10.350–352; see the translation's note on 8.121–125).

**No prizes.** No one sets out prizes and no one receives them: Alcinous calls the games so that the stranger may tell his friends how far the Phaeacians surpass others (8.100–103). The call to the stranger comes from a competitor, Laodamas, who goes and stands in the middle (8.143–145; see the translation's note on 8.144), and the stranger's own throw stands outside the frame, one throw against all the marks, which a goddess sets as Achilles points out the turning post (8.186–198; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.193, comparing *Iliad* 23.358; [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]], [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]], [[contests]]).
