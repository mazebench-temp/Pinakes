**The chest for the gifts.** When the gifts of the kings have been carried to the house, Alcinous bids his wife: “Come here, lady, bring a splendid chest, the best there is, / and put in it yourself a freshly washed mantle and a tunic” (8.424–425; see [[alcinous]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[guest-gifts]]). If αὐτή is read, “yourself”, the queen is to put in a gift of her own besides the gifts of the others (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.425; see the translation's note on 8.425, 8.441). While the bath is heating she does so:

**The lid and the knot.** ἴδε πῶμα is “look to the lid”, and αὐτός is emphatic: Arete leaves the tying to him (Merry on 8.443). δηλήσεται is of the contents, “should spoil them for you”, and αὖτε is “by and by”. Those who render it “again” make Arete allude to his sleep on the voyage from the island of Aeolus, when the companions opened the bag of winds (10.31, Greek only), and Köchly accordingly held the lines to belong after Odysseus' tales; Merry takes the word simply of the contrast between now and the voyage to come (on 8.444; see the translation's note on 8.444). The knot is a δεσμός, the word for the bonds of Hephaestus in the song (see [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]] and the translation's row δεσμός of a chest). Merry recalls that Plutarch always calls the knot of Gordius, which Alexander cut with his sword, a δεσμός, and that Düntzer compared the chests that Oroetes filled with stones and gold and then tied fast, καταδήσας, in Herodotus (on 8.443; Herodotus 3.123.2; see [[larnax]] and [[herodotus]]). What Circe taught him was the particular complicated knot, ποικίλος, in which to tie the fastening: δέδαε is “made him learn it in his mind” (Merry on 8.448; see [[circe]], [[queenly]] and the translation's row ποικίλος of a knot). This is the first time Circe is named in the poem; [[inlaid]], whose ποικίλος is a work of art, excepts the line.

**The chest of Thetis.** The lid and the gifts recall the Iliad's one chest, the chest that Thetis filled with tunics and cloaks and put on Achilles' ship, in which he kept the cup from which he poured libations to Zeus alone (*Iliad* 16.221–227; see the entry). Alcinous' own gift is a golden goblet, so that the stranger may remember him whenever he pours libations at home (8.430–432; see [[cup]]). The chest goes down to the ship the next day (13.10–12, 13.68, Greek only).
