summary: The book lays its sufferings on many causes. The war began “through the counsels of great Zeus” (8.82), and its end, Alcinous says, was the gods' work, “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580); Hephaestus blames his parents for his lameness (8.311–312); Nausithous foretold the Phaeacians' disaster from Poseidon's grudge (8.565–569); and Euryalus' insult is laid on his own lack of sense (8.177, 8.397).
refs: [8.82, 8.311-312, 8.397, 8.565-566, 8.579-580]
---

The two songs of Troy name the gods as causes. In the first, “the beginning of bane was rolling upon / Trojans and Danaans through the counsels of great Zeus” (8.81–82; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). After the third, Alcinous asks why the stranger weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilium, which “the gods wrought, and they spun destruction / for men, so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.578–580; see [[song-and-fame]]). The Phaeacians' own future disaster is laid on a god's grudge: Poseidon begrudged them, “because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (8.565–566; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]] and [[pompe]]). In the song Hephaestus lays his lameness on his parents (8.311–312; see [[aitios]]). The one fault the book lays on a man is Euryalus': he is “in mind … worthless” (8.177), and he must make amends, “since the word he spoke was by no means fair and right” (8.397; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
