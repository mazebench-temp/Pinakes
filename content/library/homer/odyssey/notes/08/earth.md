summary: In Book 8 the earth is the ground, where the Phaeacians cower at the rush of Odysseus' discus (8.190) and from which the princes leap in the ball dance before they dance “on the bountiful earth” (8.375–378); it is the earth of mortals, who “eat bread” (8.222); and it is a land, Lemnos, the dearest of all lands to Hephaestus (8.283–284, 8.301), the native land where Nausicaa hopes the stranger will remember her (8.461), and his own land, which Alcinous asks him to name (8.555).
# γαιάων "of lands" (8.284; again 12.404, 14.302) and οὖδας "ground" (8.376; again 9.135, 13.395, 22.269, 22.467,
# 23.46), first met in the Odyssey here; in this poem they are always the earth or the ground. The Iliad's οὖδας
# (11.749, 17.457, 19.61, 19.406, 24.738) is not reached by this note.

**The ground.** When Odysseus throws, “the stone hummed; and down to the earth they cowered, / the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships, / under the rush of the stone” (8.190–192; see [[discus]], [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). W. W. Merry sees an intentional sarcasm in the high-sounding epithets set beside their terror at the whiz of the quoit, and compares the irony of the Iliad's epithet for Ares as he bellows with his wound, “so loud did Ares, insatiable of war, bellow” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.191; *Iliad* 5.862–863). In the ball dance one prince flings the ball toward the clouds, “and the other, leaping high from the ground, / would catch it easily before his feet touched the ground”; then they danced “on the bountiful earth” (8.375–378; see [[ball]], [[bountiful-earth]] and [[dance]]). The catcher leaps up to meet the ball in the air, as Eustathius explains it (Merry on 8.375).

**Those who eat bread.** Odysseus claims that he is far better with the bow than “all the mortals who are now on the earth and eat bread” (8.221–222; see [[men-of-today]]). σῖτον ἔδοντες marks men off from the gods, who feed on ambrosia, from wild beasts, and perhaps from the ogres and the like who are uncivilized and plant no corn (Merry on 8.222, comparing “one of the mortals who eat the fruit of the field” and a man “who was mortal and ate the grain of Demeter”, *Iliad* 6.142, 13.322). The half-verse returns in the next two books, when Odysseus sends men ahead to learn who the people are that eat bread upon the earth, and finds the Lotus-eaters and the Laestrygonians (9.89, 10.101). Singers, he says later at the feast, are honored “among all men upon the earth” (8.479; see [[upon-the-earth]] and [[singers]]).

**Lands.** In the song of Ares and Aphrodite Hephaestus makes as if to go to Lemnos, “which is by far the dearest to him of all lands”, and turns back “before he reached the land of Lemnos” (8.283–284, 8.301; see [[lemnos]] and [[hephaestus]]). Nausicaa bids the stranger farewell, “so that someday, when you are in your native land, / you may remember me” (8.461–462; see [[nausicaa]] and [[dear-native-land]]), and Alcinous asks him at last, “And tell me your land and your people and your city” (8.555; see [[alcinous]]). He answers in the next book with his name and his island, Ithaca (9.19–21).
