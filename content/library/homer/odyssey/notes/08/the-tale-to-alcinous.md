---
heading: The prelude
summary: The questions with which Alcinous ends the book are the prelude to the tale. He asks the stranger's name, his land, his people and his city (8.550–556), where he was driven in his wanderings and among what men, harsh or kind to strangers (8.572–576), and why he weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilios (8.577–586); the tale of the next four books is the answer.
# Unnamed: the questions that the tale answers (8.550-556, 8.572-578).
refs: [8.550-556, 8.572-578]
---

**The name.** “Tell me the name by which they called you there, your mother and your father, / and the others who live in the city and round about it” (8.550–551). κεῖθι, “there”, is a general way of pointing to a home whose name the king does not yet know (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.550). The reason he gives is a saying, “For no one at all among men is nameless” (8.552–554; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]), and the land, the people and the city are wanted so that the Phaeacian ships may carry him there (8.555–556; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**The wanderings.** “But come, tell me this, and give me an exact account” (8.572; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]): where he was driven, ὅππῃ, “in what direction” (Merry on 8.573), and to what lands of men, “both those who are harsh and savage and unjust, / and those who are kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (8.573–576). The two verses are made on those with which Odysseus, waking on the shore of Scheria, asked himself what people he had come to, “Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (6.120–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]). The king now asks him the question he asked himself, and the tale is to answer it people by people.

**The tears.** Last he asks why the stranger weeps at the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios, and whether a kinsman by marriage or a companion fell before Ilios (8.577–586; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).

**The answer.** The ninth book begins with the reply. Odysseus praises the singer, says that the king's heart has turned to asking of his sorrows, and gives his name first (9.2–18, Greek only): “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, … and my fame reaches the sky” (9.19–20, a plain rendering). καί μευ κλέος οὐρανὸν ἵκει (9.20) is said in the words of the morning's lay of his quarrel with Achilles, “whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky”, κλέος οὐρανὸν εὐρὺν ἵκανε (8.74; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[kleos]]).
