summary: The suppliant of Book 7 still begs for his escort: “he urges an escort, and begs that it be made sure” (8.30), and he sits in the assembly “begging the king and all the people” (8.157). By the evening he is the guest of the house, and Alcinous' maxim sets “a stranger and a suppliant” beside a brother (8.546). In the song Poseidon “kept begging” Hephaestus to set Ares free (8.344).
# λίσσεται (8.30), λισσόμενος (8.157; again 22.311, 22.343, 22.366).
grc: [λίσσεται, λισσόμενος]
en: [begs, begging]
---

**The suppliant's request.** The night before, the stranger threw his arms around the queen's knees and asked for an escort home (7.142–152; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). Alcinous now puts the request to his people in the suppliant's own terms: “he urges an escort, and begs that it be made sure” (λίσσεται ἔμπεδον εἶναι, 8.30; see [[pompe]]). When Laodamas invites him to the games, he answers that sorrows weigh on him more than contests, since “now in the midst of your assembly, in need of my homecoming, / I sit, begging the king and all the people” (8.156–157; see [[nostos]] and [[no-greater-fame]]). μεθʼ ὑμετέρῃ ἀγορῇ is “in the midst of your assembly” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.156).

**From suppliant to guest.** The day ends with the suppliant's place made good: gifts from the twelve kings and the king, a bath, and a seat beside the king at supper (8.387–469; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[xenia]]). At the evening feast Alcinous names the two together in a maxim, “As good as a brother are a stranger and a suppliant / to a man who has even a little hold on wisdom” (ξεῖνός θʼ ἱκέτης τε, 8.546–547; see [[as-good-as-a-brother]]).

**A god begging.** In Demodocus' song the word is used of a god. Laughter did not hold Poseidon, “and he kept begging / Hephaestus, famed for his work, to set Ares free” (λίσσετο δʼ αἰεί, 8.344–345), and he got what he asked by offering himself as surety (8.347–359; see [[pledges-for-the-worthless]] and [[poseidon]]).
