---
summary: The Theogony gives the king whom the Muses honor the very verses with which Odysseus describes the speaker on whom a god sets grace. It marries Hephaestus to Aglaia, youngest of the Graces, where Demodocus' song gives him Aphrodite; it derives singers from the Muses and Apollo, as Odysseus does, and Aphrodite's “laughter-loving” from her birth. The gods' “givers of blessings”, the oracle “in sacred Pytho” and “a rich feast of the gods” are Hesiod's phrases too.
---

- **The speaker men look on as a god.** Odysseus answers Euryalus that the gods do not give all their gifts to every man: one man is less in looks, “but a god crowns his words with beauty, and men look on him / with delight; and he speaks surely, / with soothing modesty, and stands out among those gathered, / and as he goes through the city they look on him as a god” (8.170–173). The Theogony says the same of the king whom the daughters of Zeus honor at his birth: they pour sweet dew on his tongue, all the people look to him as he gives judgment, ὃ δʼ ἀσφαλέως ἀγορεύων, “and he, speaking surely”, ends even a great quarrel, and ἐρχόμενον δʼ ἀνʼ ἀγῶνα θεὸν ὣς ἱλάσκονται / αἰδοῖ μειλιχίῃ, μετὰ δὲ πρέπει ἀγρομένοισιν, “as he goes through the gathering they greet him like a god / with soothing reverence, and he stands out among those gathered” (*Theogony* 81–93, plain renderings). The second verse is 8.172 word for word. Merry uses the passage to divide the Homeric clauses, joining “with soothing modesty” to the onlookers, “with submissive reverence”, and “he speaks surely” to “stands out among those gathered”; he quotes the first verse with Homer's ἀνὰ ἄστυ, “through the city” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.170–172; see the translation's note on 8.171–172): [[different-gifts]], [[aidos]] and [[muses]].
- **Hephaestus' wife.** “Hephaestus, the renowned god, lame in both legs, made Aglaia, youngest of the Graces, his flourishing wife” (*Theogony* 945–946, a plain rendering). Merry quotes the scholiast's remark that Homer knows Hephaestus as the husband not of Aphrodite but of Charis (*Iliad* 18.382–383), and that Demodocus made the story up himself, and adds that Hesiod calls this Charis Aglaia (Merry on 8.267): [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]], [[graces]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]].
- **Singers from the Muses and Apollo.** “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo” (8.488): Merry cites the Theogony, “for from the Muses and Apollo who shoots from afar come singers and lyre players on the earth” (*Theogony* 94–95; Merry on 8.488). The next verses fit Demodocus too: “happy is the man whom the Muses love: sweet speech flows from his mouth” (*Theogony* 96–97; plain renderings), as the Muse “loved above all” the singer to whom she gave “sweet song” (8.63–64): [[muses]], [[apollo]] and [[demodocus]].
- **Laughter-loving.** For φιλομμειδής, “laughter-loving” (8.362), Merry notes that the Theogony gives the form φιλομμηδέα and derives it from the μήδεα of Uranus, from which Aphrodite came to light, and that Gaisford rightly rejected the line (*Theogony* 200; Merry on 8.362). The same passage names her “well-crowned Cytherea”, ἐυστέφανον Κυθέρειαν, in a verse the text read here brackets (*Theogony* 196), the epithet of the song (8.267, 8.288): [[laughter-loving]], [[well-crowned]] and [[aphrodite]].
- **Shared phrases.** The gods are δωτῆρες ἑάων, “givers of blessings”, only in this book of Homer (8.325, 8.335), and in the Theogony three times, with a fourth in a verse that the text read here brackets (*Theogony* 46, 633, 664; 111); Merry counts the phrase among the song's verbal peculiarities (on 8.267). The stone that Cronus swallowed is set up “in sacred Pytho”, Πυθοῖ ἐν ἠγαθέῃ (*Theogony* 499), the words of the oracle that Agamemnon consulted (8.80). The Works and Days forbids a man to pare his nails θεῶν ἐν δαιτὶ θαλείῃ, “at a rich feast of the gods” (*Works and Days* 742–743), a sacrifice held by men, in the words of the feast at which Odysseus and Achilles quarreled (8.76): [[pytho]], [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[olympians]].
- **Dances to the lyre.** Arguing that Demodocus was fetched to play for the dancers, Merry compares the dances of the city on the Hesiodic *Shield*, where women lead a lovely dance to lyres and young men revel to the pipe, “sporting with dance and song” (*Shield* 280–282, a plain rendering; Merry on 8.267): [[dance]] and [[shield-of-heracles]].
- **Beauty from the gods.** Nausicaa stands by the doorpost, θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, “who had her beauty from the gods” (8.457). The Catalogue of Women introduces Cyrene with the same turn, “or such as she who in Phthia, with beauty from the Graces, dwelt by the water of Peneus” (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 92 Evelyn-White, a plain rendering), and in Book 6 Nausicaa's handmaids have their beauty “from the Graces” (6.18): [[nausicaa]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]].
