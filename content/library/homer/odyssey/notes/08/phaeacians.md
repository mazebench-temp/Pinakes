summary: The book shows the Phaeacians in public, in their place of assembly, at the games and the dance, and at the feast. Their king tells the stranger what they are best at, running and ships, the feast, the lyre and the dance (8.246–253), and how their ships, which need no helmsmen, know the minds of men (8.555–563); and he tells of the prophecy that Poseidon, who begrudges them their escorts, will one day shatter one of their ships and cover their city with a mountain (8.564–571).

**The leaders and the people.** At dawn Alcinous leads the way “to the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, which was built for them beside the ships” (8.5; see [[assembly]] and [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]), and Athena, in the likeness of his herald, summons the “leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (8.11; see [[athena]], [[heralds]] and [[hegemon]]). Alcinous addresses them four times in the book with the verse of the seventh book, “Hear me, leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians” (8.26, 8.97, 8.387, 8.536; 7.186; see [[hear-me]]), and the poet introduces his speeches with “spoke among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (8.96, 8.386, 8.535; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]]). Under him twelve kings rule as leaders, and he himself is the thirteenth (8.390–391; see [[kings]] and [[alcinous]]). Athena pours grace on the stranger “so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians” (8.21), all, that is, who were in the assembly (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.21; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). The young men who rise for the games bear names of the sea and of ships, Nauteus, Prymneus, Eretmeus, Ponteus, Proreus and the rest (8.111–119; Merry on 8.111; see the translation's note on 8.111–119 and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The men of the east and of the west.** Alcinous does not know who the stranger is, “whether from the men of the east or from the men of the west” (8.29). The two clauses expand ὅς τις, “whoever he may be”, and a scholium explains that the ancients divided the world into west and east, quoting Odysseus' words on Circe's island, “for we do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn” (10.190, a plain rendering; Merry on 8.29; see the translation's note on 8.29). Whichever end of the earth he comes from, it is the Phaeacians' custom to send him on: “So let us, as has been our custom, urge on an escort. / For no other man either, who comes to my house, / stays here long, mourning, for want of an escort” (8.31–33; see [[pompe]]).

**Famed for ships.** Their epithets are their seafaring: “lovers of the oar”, and “the Phaeacians of the long oars, men famed for ships” (8.191, 8.369; see [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]). The fuller formula is first heard when they cower to the earth under the rush of the stranger's discus (8.190–192; see [[discus]]), and Merry finds an intentional sarcasm in the high-sounding epithets set against their terror at the whiz of the quoit, as in the Iliad Ares, “insatiable of war”, bellows with the pain of his wound (on 8.191; *Iliad* 5.863). The same words return when they delight in the song of Ares and Aphrodite (8.367–369; see [[demodocus]]).

**What they are best at.** Before the games Alcinous wants the stranger to tell his friends at home “how far we surpass others / in boxing and in wrestling and in jumping and in running” (8.101–103; see [[contests]]). {{quote:257154be-b9d9-5029-8a0e-28e5d3d66cb8}}

The boast is said again with a new list: let the dancers play, “so that the stranger may tell his friends, / when he has returned home, how far we surpass others / in seafaring and in running and in dancing and in song” (8.251–253; see [[dance]], [[lyre]] and [[different-gifts]]). ἐξημοιβά are changes of clothing worn one after another, and εὐναί need mean no more than plenty of sleep in warm and comfortable beds, though Eustathius, Merry notes, seems to see in the word something more (Merry on 8.249; see [[clothing]], [[bathing]] and [[bed]]). Nitzsch, with a scholium, saw in the lines a people devoted to peace; but there is a certain color of luxury in it all, Merry thinks, a common theme of the Sophists. Bothe, defending the genuineness of the passage against those who called it in question, saw the life of a people in a blessed land, safe from enemies, with nothing serious to do but seafaring, and that the easiest of tasks, so that they spend their time in feasts, lyre players, dancing, soft clothing, baths and sleep, as a poet true to the old report of them was bound to describe them (Merry on 8.244). For the later picture of the Phaeacians as idlers, in Heracleides of Pontus and Horace, see the general article. Merry adds that this dance of the Phaeacians, with Demodocus singing, was figured, as Pausanias describes it, on the throne of the Amyclaean Apollo made by Bathycles of Magnesia, evidence, even at the later of the dates assigned to the artist, that the Homeric poems were popularly known before the recension under the Peisistratids, and that the Odyssey then contained the episode of the Phaeacians (on 8.251).

**Their ships.** Alcinous asks the stranger for his land, his people and his city, “so that our ships may carry you there, aiming with their minds” (8.555–556; see the translation's note on 8.555):

{{quote:331dc94c-a450-5635-ab86-8457b7d8cb23}}

τιτυσκόμεναι is “aiming at it with their minds”, as Antilochus in the Iliad “took aim in his mind / either to throw at someone, or to rush at him close at hand” (Merry on 8.556; *Iliad* 13.558–559; see [[steering-oar]] and [[ships]]). Merry describes the marvelous ships as endued with human sense, anticipating their masters' will and flitting unseen over the sea, immune from all danger, and compares the legend of the Flying Dutchman and Hiawatha's canoe. He reports that Welcker saw in the Phaeacians and their ghostly ships, which carry Odysseus home in a deep sleep, a Greek form of the northern legend of the Ferrymen of Death, who carry the souls of the departed over to the island of Brittia, as Procopius tells it; but that dismal office, Merry answers, seems little suited to “the gay and easy Phaeacians”, and the mist and cloud that wrap their ships are but the natural concealment of the working of supernatural power (on 8.556; see the translation's note on 8.556–563).

**Poseidon's grudge.** The ships' safety is the reason for the threat that hangs over them. Alcinous' father, Nausithous, “used to say that Poseidon begrudged / us, because we are escorts of all men and come to no harm”, and that one day the god would shatter a Phaeacian ship on its way back from an escort and cover their city round about with a great mountain (8.564–569; see [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]], [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). Poseidon was jealous of their immunity from harm, Merry explains, which amounted to independence of his authority and power (on 8.566). Alcinous leaves the outcome to the god: “these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.570–571). In the thirteenth book, when Poseidon has turned their ship to stone, Alcinous recalls the prophecy in the same verses (13.172–177).

**Harsh or kind to strangers.** Alcinous ends by asking the stranger about the lands and men of his wanderings, “both those who are harsh and savage and unjust, / and those who are kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (8.575–576), the question Odysseus asked himself when he woke on the Phaeacians' own shore, with χαλεποί, “harsh”, for ὑβρισταί, “arrogant” (6.120–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]). He has just named what the Phaeacians give their guest, the escort and “the gifts of friendship, which we give him, making him welcome” (8.545; see [[guest-gifts]]), since “As good as a brother are a stranger and a suppliant / to a man who has even a little hold on wisdom” (8.546–547; see [[supplication]]).
