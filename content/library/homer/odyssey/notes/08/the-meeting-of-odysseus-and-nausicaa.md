---
heading: Nausicaa's farewell
summary: The end of the meeting. On the evening of the feast, as Odysseus comes from his bath to join the men at their wine, Nausicaa stands by the doorpost of the hall and bids him farewell, asking him to remember her, since to her first he owes the price for his life; he answers that if he reaches home he will pray to her there as to a god all his days, for she gave him his life (8.457–468). She is not seen again in the poem.

**After the bath.** The scene repeats the end of the meeting at the river. There Odysseus came from his bath, and Athena made him taller and poured grace on him, and “the girl gazed at him” (6.224–237); here he comes from the bath in her father's house, and she “marveled at Odysseus as she looked at him with her eyes” (8.454–459; see [[bathing]] and [[thambos]]). θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, “who had her beauty from the gods” (8.457; see [[beauty-and-its-price]]), recalls the handmaids of her chamber, “who had their beauty from the Graces” (6.18). She stands παρὰ σταθμὸν τέγεος πύκα ποιητοῖο, “by the doorpost of the strongly made hall” (8.458), the verse in which Penelope comes down among the suitors and stops at the doorpost of the hall (1.333; again 16.415, 18.209, 21.64; see [[penelope]] and [[door]]): the place of a woman of the house at the edge of the men's feast.

**Farewell.** χαῖρε, ξεῖνʼ, ἵνα: “may it be well with thee, stranger, in order that” you may remember me (8.461). W. W. Merry hears in it a wish for his own sake, quickened by the affection of the sixth book, which now takes the form of a hope to live in his memory as his benefactress, and he finds in it a trait of what became a favorite saying of the later Greeks (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.461, citing 6.240): most men think, as Aristotle reports, that benefactors wish those they have helped to live, as creditors wish their debtors to, so that they may get back the favor (*Nicomachean Ethics* 9.7, 1167b17–25; see [[aristotle]]). ζωάγρια, “the price for your life”, is the word of Hephaestus, who must pay it to Thetis because she took him in when he fell (*Iliad* 18.394–407; see [[zoagria]]). Merry, after Nitzsch, sets an Iliadic sense, the price paid to a conqueror for sparing a defeated enemy, against the debt of gratitude for a life saved by timely help, and finds that “the price of life” covers both (on 8.462; see the translation's note on 8.461–462). πρώτῃ, “to me first”: before her father and her mother, it was to her that he came as a suppliant, as Alcinous had said (7.301; see [[supplication]]).

**As to a god.** Odysseus answers with her name, “Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (8.464), which he had never spoken to her (see [[nausicaa]]). His first words to her at the river were a question, “are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (6.149); his last are a promise to pray to her “as to a god / always, all my days; for you gave me my life, maiden” (8.467–468; see [[euchomai]] and [[prayer]]). ἐβιώσαο, “you gave me my life”, is more vivid than the scholium's “brought me into life” (Merry on 8.468). Telemachus answers Helen in the same words when she reads the omen of his father's return at Sparta, “so may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (15.180–181, Greek only; see [[telemachus]] and [[helen]]): father and son pay the same honor to the women who helped them on their way.

**The end of her part.** The farewell is the last time Nausicaa appears. Her father's wish that such a man might be his son-in-law (7.311–316) is not spoken again, and when Odysseus gives Penelope the story of his wanderings, the Phaeacians honor him and send him home, but the girl is not named (23.338–341, Greek only; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[marriage]]).
