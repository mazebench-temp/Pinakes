summary: Demodocus' lyre is the book's instrument. The herald hangs it on a peg above the blind singer's head and shows him where to take it (8.67–68), hangs it up again when the games begin (8.105), and fetches it from the king's house for the dance and the song of Ares and Aphrodite (8.254–266); Alcinous calls it the companion of the feast and counts it among the Phaeacians' pleasures (8.99, 8.248), and stops it when the stranger weeps (8.537).

**The lyre on its peg.** When the herald has seated Demodocus, “he hung the clear-toned lyre from its peg / right above his head, and showed him where to take it in his hands” (8.67–68; see [[heralds]], [[pontonous]] and [[demodocus]]). The clear-toned lyre, φόρμιγξ λίγεια, is the one Achilles plays in his hut (*Iliad* 9.186; see the translation's row φόρμιγξ λίγεια), and πασσαλόφι, “from its peg”, is the peg from which Priam's sons take down the mule yoke (*Iliad* 24.268). The herald does not merely tell the blind man where the lyre is: ἐπέφραδε means that he guided his hand to it (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.68; see the translation's note on 8.68). Aristophanes read δῆσε, “tied”, for κρέμασεν, “hung”, here and where the verse returns (Merry's critical notes on 8.67 and 8.105). When Alcinous takes his guests out to the games, the herald hangs the lyre up again and leads the singer out by the hand (8.105–107).

**The companion of the feast.** Alcinous ends the morning meal: “Already we have had our hearts' fill of our equal share of the feast / and of the lyre, which is the companion of the rich feast” (8.98–99; see [[feast]]); the lyre is called the feast's companion in the house of Odysseus too (17.270–271, Greek only; Merry on 8.99). The lyre is the gods' at their feast, played by Apollo while the Muses sing (*Iliad* 1.603–604), and Odysseus will wonder whether the Muse or Apollo taught Demodocus (8.488; see [[apollo]] and [[muses]]). Among the Phaeacians' pleasures Alcinous names “the feast and the lyre and the dances” (8.248; see [[phaeacians]] and [[dance]]): κίθαρις and φόρμιγξ are one instrument (see the Book 1 note).

**The lyre for the dance.** For the dancing Alcinous sends for “the clear-toned lyre / that lies somewhere in our house” (8.254–255), and the herald rises “to bring the hollow lyre from the king's house” and brings it to Demodocus, who goes into the middle of the young men (8.256–262; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). γλαφυρή, “hollow”, is the lyre's other epithet (see the translation's row φόρμιγξ λίγεια, φόρμιγξ γλαφυρή, κίθαρις, and [[clear-toned]]). Then “he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song / about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite” (8.266–267), in nearly the words of Phemius' prelude (1.155; see the Book 1 note on ἀνεβάλλετο, and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). The singer was fetched to play for the dancers, Merry observes, as on the shield of Achilles a divine singer plays the lyre among the dancers (on 8.267; *Iliad* 18.603–606).

**The lyre stopped.** At the evening meal Demodocus sings the wooden horse, and the stranger weeps; Alcinous ends it: “let Demodocus now stop his clear-toned lyre; / for it is not to all of us that he gives delight by singing this” (8.537–538; see [[alcinous]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).
