summary: After the contests Alcinous calls on the Phaeacians' best dancers to show the stranger what they excel in; the nine stewards level a dancing floor, Demodocus plays in the middle, and the young men strike the floor with their feet while Odysseus marvels at their flashing (8.246–265). After the song of Ares and Aphrodite Halius and Laodamas dance alone with a purple ball, and Odysseus tells the king that his boast has proved true (8.370–384).

**What the Phaeacians excel in.** Alcinous answers the stranger's boast with his people's: they are not flawless boxers or wrestlers, but swift runners and the best with ships, “and always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances” (8.246–248; see [[phaeacians]] and [[different-gifts]]). So he calls the dancers out: “But come, you Phaeacian dancers, all who are the best, / make your sport, so that the stranger may tell his friends, / when he has returned home, how far we surpass others / in seafaring and in running and in dancing and in song” (8.250–253). The βητάρμονες are named from their step, βαίνω, as a scholium explains (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.250), and παίσατε, “make your sport”, is from παίζω, “play” (Merry on 8.251, citing 6.106), the verb of Nausicaa's girls at their ball (6.100; see the translation's note on 8.251 and [[ball]]).

The αἰσυμνῆται are not umpires but regulators of the games, “masters of the lists”, public officers, δήμιοι, not servants of the king's household (Merry on 8.258; see the translation's row αἰσυμνῆται, δήμιοι). χορός is the ground they danced on, here and at 8.264, and ἀγών the ring of the spectators, which the stewards widen (Merry on 8.260; see the translation's row χορός, ὀρχηστύς, ὀρχηθμός, βητάρμονες). The young men are πρωθῆβαι, “in their first youth” (8.263; see [[in-first-youth]]), δαήμονες ὀρχηθμοῖο, “skilled in dancing”, with the Iliad's word for “flawless dancing” (*Iliad* 13.637), and “they struck the divine dancing floor with their feet”: Merry compares Virgil's *pars pedibus plaudunt choreas* (on 8.264; *Aeneid* 6.644). Demodocus stands in the middle with the lyre (8.261–262; see [[lyre]] and [[demodocus]]); the singer, Merry notes, was fetched to play for the dancers, as a divine singer plays among the dancers on the shield of Achilles, and his song of Ares and Aphrodite is a gift added to the day's merriment (on 8.267; *Iliad* 18.603–606; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). Odysseus watches “the flashing of their feet” (8.265; see [[thambos]]).

**The ball dance.** When the song is over, “Alcinous bade Halius and Laodamas / dance alone, since no one vied with them” (8.370–371; see [[halius-son-of-alcinous]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). After throwing the purple ball high and catching it, “they danced on the bountiful earth, / changing places again and again; and the other young men beat time, / standing about the gathering, and a great din arose” (8.378–380; see [[ball]] and [[bountiful-earth]]). ταρφέʼ ἀμειβομένω brings in a new figure, Merry explains: the ball was no longer only thrown up and caught, but passed from one to the other as they danced, all in time to the music (on 8.379). Odysseus answers the king's boast: “you boasted that your dancers were the best, / and indeed it has proved true; awe holds me as I look” (8.383–384; see [[awe-holds-me]] and [[sebas]]), and Alcinous, delighted, proposes the gifts (8.385–389; see [[guest-gifts]]).

**The dance on the throne at Amyclae.** Merry reports that this dance of the Phaeacians, with Demodocus singing, was figured on the throne of Apollo at Amyclae by Bathycles of Magnesia, as Pausanias describes it, and argues from the date of the work that the Odyssey's episode of the Phaeacians was known before the Peisistratean recension (on 8.251, quoting Pausanias).
