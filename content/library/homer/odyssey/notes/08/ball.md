---
summary: Alcinous bids his sons Halius and Laodamas dance alone, since no one can rival them. With a purple ball that Polybus made for them, one throws it toward the clouds, bending back, and the other leaps up and catches it before he touches the ground; then they dance, passing it to and fro, while the young men beat time (8.370–380).

{{quote:420914f9-ca36-5160-95fd-372aa296aed8}}

**The game.** The ball is “a purple one, which wise-hearted Polybus had made for them” (8.373; see [[purple]], [[polybus-the-phaeacian]] and [[wise-hearted]]). The players are two of the three sons of Alcinous who rose for the games (8.118–119; see [[halius-son-of-alcinous]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). W. W. Merry reports that the game described, in which the ball is tossed up and caught before the leaper reaches the ground, was called ἡ οὐρανία (Eustathius on the passage), and that much on the ball games of the ancients is collected by Athenaeus and Pollux (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.372). ἀερθείς is the catcher leaping up to meet the ball in the air, as Eustathius explains it (Merry on 8.375), and ἀνʼ ἰθύν is “straight upwards” (Merry on 8.377; see the translation's note on 8.377).

**The dance.** “Changing places again and again” brings in a new figure: the ball was no longer only thrown up and caught, but the two danced, and as they danced it flew from one to the other, all in time to the music (Merry on 8.379; see [[dance]]). Merry takes Nausicaa's game with her handmaids, which the poem calls μολπή, for the same union of ball and dance (on 6.101; see the Book 6 note). The other young men stand about the gathering and beat time (8.379–380), and Odysseus tells the king that his boast of his dancers has proved true: “awe holds me as I look” (8.384; see [[awe-holds-me]]).
