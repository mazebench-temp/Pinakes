summary: In Demodocus' second song the Sun is Hephaestus' informer. He saw Ares and Aphrodite lying together and came to tell him (8.270–271), and kept watch for him while he made as if to go to Lemnos (8.302). At the end of the day the sun sets as the guest-gifts are brought in (8.417).
# Ἥλιος, the contracted name, is found only here in Homer (8.271). It is claimed in this note, which applies to the
# Odyssey only, since in later prose ἥλιος is the sun of every day. Unnamed: "a messenger came to him" (8.270).

**The messenger.** Ares has lain with Aphrodite in the house of Hephaestus, “But at once a messenger came to him, / the Sun, who had seen them lying together in love” (8.270–271; see [[hephaestus]], [[ares]] and [[aphrodite]]). Ἥλιος is the contracted form, found only here in Homer, where the poems otherwise always say Ἠέλιος; the scholia note it, and Nauck suggests Ἠέλιος, ὃ νόησε (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 8.271; see the translation's note on 8.271 and its key-word row). Merry notes that the same form opens the *Hymn to Helios* (on 8.271), ἥλιον ὑμνεῖν αὖτε Διὸς τέκος ἄρχεο Μοῦσα (*Hymn* 31.1). Those who doubted the song counted the name among its verbal peculiarities, which Merry thinks of no real weight; and he quotes Gladstone, for whom there is “something rather more marked in the personal agency of the Sun than the poems elsewhere present” (on 8.267; see the translation's note on 8.266–366).

The Sun sees what others do not: Agamemnon calls him to witness the oaths as the god “who see all things and hear all things” (*Iliad* 3.277), and Teiresias and Odysseus name him in the same words when they warn of his cattle (11.109, 12.323; see [[who-sees-all-things]]). In Book 12 he is the one who receives a message: Lampetie comes to him as a messenger, ἄγγελος ἦλθε, to tell him that the companions have killed his cattle (12.374–375; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**The watch.** The trap is sprung, and Hephaestus comes back before he has reached Lemnos, “for the Sun had kept watch for him and told him the tale” (8.302). The Sun's watch, σκοπιὴν ἔχεν, answers the lover's: “Nor did Ares of the golden reins keep a blind watch”, οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε, when he saw the husband go (8.285). Ares watched for the husband to go, and the Sun kept watch on the husband's behalf.

**Sunset.** After the gifts of the kings and Euryalus' sword, “And the sun set, and the glorious gifts were there for him” (8.417; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[guest-gifts]]). By sunset all the presents had come in; the day that closes is the one that began with the book's first line, and after it the stories of Odysseus keep the household up until the time given in Book 13 (Merry on 8.417; 13.17–18). δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος, joined to the next clause by καί, is the half-verse that closed the day of Book 6 (6.321; see the Book 6 note). The day it ends is the thirty-third of the poem (see [[eos]]).
