summary: In Demodocus' second song Hermes, “the great helper”, comes with Poseidon and Apollo to the doorway of Hephaestus' house. Apollo asks him whether he would be willing, held fast in strong bonds, to sleep beside golden Aphrodite, and he answers that he would, with three times as many bonds and all the gods and goddesses looking on; the gods laugh again.

**The great helper.** “there came Poseidon, the earth-holder; there came the great helper / Hermes; and far-working lord Apollo came” (8.322–323; see [[great-helper]], [[poseidon]], [[apollo]] and [[olympians]]). ἐριούνης is found in this form only here and in the Iliad's list of the gods who go to the battle, where it also follows Poseidon the earth-holder, Ποσειδάων γαιήοχος ἠδʼ ἐριούνης / Ἑρμείας (*Iliad* 20.34–35); elsewhere the word is ἐριούνιος (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.322).

**Apollo's question.** The gods have laughed at the lovers in the bonds, and one of them has spoken the saying of the slow and the swift (8.326–332; see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]). Then Apollo turns to Hermes:

{{quote:bbd52fa5-1e8d-54ac-9976-4dddcc5cdb3d}}

Apollo calls him by four names, “Hermes, son of Zeus, guide, giver of blessings” (8.335; see [[son-of-zeus]] and [[guide]]). δῶτορ ἑάων is the singular of the title the singer has just given all the gods in the doorway, δωτῆρες ἑάων, “the givers of blessings” (8.325; see [[givers-of-blessings]] and the translation's key-word row), and ἑάων are the “blessings” of the second of the two jars of Zeus (*Iliad* 24.528; Merry on 8.325; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). In Homer the phrase is found only in these two verses; two of the Homeric Hymns greet Hermes with it, Διὸς καὶ Μαιάδος υἱέ … δῶτορ ἐάων (*Hymn* 29.7–8), and Ἑρμῆ χαριδῶτα, διάκτορε, δῶτορ ἐάων, in a closing verse that the text of the short *Hymn to Hermes* brackets (*Hymn* 18.12). Merry takes αἲ γάρ to govern all four of Hermes' optatives: τοῦτο γένοιτο points on to “let me sleep”, and the bonds and the onlookers are undesirable circumstances, wished for only for the sake of that (on 8.339).

**An unseemly exchange.** The scholia report that 8.333–342 were missing in some copies because they seemed unseemly, and that some athetized them (Merry's critical note on 8.333–342; see the translation's note on 8.266–366). Gladstone, whom Merry quotes, found Apollo made to assume a tone wholly singular and unsupported by what is told of him in the rest of the poems (Merry on 8.267). Hermes' answer brings the second laugh of the song (8.343; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

**The guide, the slayer of Argus.** “Then the guide, the slayer of Argus, answered him” (8.338; see [[argeiphontes]]). The same verse introduces Hermes' words to Leto in the Iliad's battle of the gods, when he declines to fight Apollo's mother and bids her boast among the gods that she overcame him (*Iliad* 21.497–501; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). In the Odyssey he has come before as Zeus' messenger to Calypso (5.28–148), and at the Phaeacians' table as the god to whom they poured the last libation of the evening (7.136–138; see the Book 7 note).
