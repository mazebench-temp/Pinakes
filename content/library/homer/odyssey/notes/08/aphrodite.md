summary: In Demodocus' second song Aphrodite is the wife of Hephaestus and the lover of Ares. She has just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus, when Ares finds her alone; caught with him in her husband's bonds, she is loosed at Poseidon's word and goes to Paphos in Cyprus, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar, and where the Graces bathe her, anoint her with immortal oil and clothe her in lovely garments. She does not speak in the song.

**The wife of Hephaestus.** Demodocus sings “about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite, / how they first lay together in the house of Hephaestus / in secret” (8.267–269; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]], [[ares]], [[hephaestus]] and [[demodocus]]). In the Iliad the wife of Hephaestus is Charis (*Iliad* 18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]), and the scholia note the difference, saying that Demodocus tells it in his own story-making (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.267; see the Book 8 note of [[hephaestus]] and the translation's note on 8.266–366). In the Iliad Aphrodite is close to Ares in other ways: she calls him “dear brother” (*Iliad* 5.359), and leads him away by the hand when Athena has felled him (*Iliad* 21.416–417); Hesiod makes their children Phobos, Deimos and Harmonia (*Theogony* 933–937).

**The daughter of Zeus.** When Ares comes, “She had just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus, / and had sat down” (8.289–290; see [[zeus]] and [[eristhenes]]). Hephaestus gives her the Iliad's title in his complaint, “how Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus, because I am lame, / always dishonors me” (8.308–309; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]), as she is Διὸς θυγάτηρ Ἀφροδίτη in the Iliad (*Iliad* 3.374, 5.312, 21.416), where her mother is Dione (*Iliad* 5.370–371; see [[dione]]). Her father has had the bride-price, and her husband means to hold the lovers in the bonds “until her father gives me back the whole bride-price, / all that I put into his hands for the sake of the dog-faced girl, / because his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control” (8.318–320; see [[bride-price]] and [[dog-face]]). On ἐχέθυμος, which the translation, after a scholium, takes as “restraining desire”, and which Merry would render “heartless”, see the translation's note on 8.320.

**Her names in the song.** εὐστέφανος, “well-crowned” (8.267, 8.288), is said of her again in Book 18 (18.193), and in the Iliad of Artemis (*Iliad* 21.511; see [[well-crowned]]). Κυθέρεια, “Cytherea”, is in Homer only here and at 18.193, both times with the same epithet (see [[cytherea]] and [[cythera]]); Hesiod explains the name from Cythera, which the goddess first drew near on her way from the sea foam to Cyprus (*Theogony* 192–198), and has the same pair, ἐυστέφανον Κυθέρειαν, in a verse the text brackets (*Theogony* 196). For Κυθερείης the scholia record a reading Ἀφροδίτης (Merry's critical note on 8.288; see the translation's note on 8.288). χρυσέη, “golden”, comes in Apollo's question and Hermes' answer, “to sleep in bed beside golden Aphrodite” (8.337, 8.342; see [[golden]], [[apollo]] and [[hermes]]), the Iliad's title (*Iliad* 3.64, 9.389, 19.282). φιλομμειδής, “laughter-loving” (8.362; see [[laughter-loving]]), Merry derives from φιλο-σμειδής, from the root of a “smile”; Hesiod's verse gives it as φιλομμηδέα, “because she appeared from the genitals”, μήδεα, a line that Merry says Gaisford rightly rejects (on 8.362; *Theogony* 200).

**Silent.** She has no words in the song. Ares speaks to her and she lies down with him, “and it seemed welcome to her to lie down to sleep” (8.295); Hephaestus speaks of her; Apollo and Hermes speak of lying beside her (8.335–342). When the bonds are loosed she goes to her own place.

**Paphos.**

{{quote:5e1f3571-61c4-540b-af34-18a9d6e10069}}

Three of the verses return in the *Hymn to Aphrodite*, with θυώδης for θυήεις, when she goes to her temple at Paphos to be bathed and anointed by the Graces before she visits Anchises (*Hymn* 5.58–63; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]] and [[graces]]). ἐπενήνοθεν: “such as deck the everliving gods”, or, with the oil as subject, it “is shed over” them (Merry on 8.365; see the translation's note on 8.365). The bath, the immortal oil and the garments are the goddess's adornment (see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). In Book 18 Athena makes Penelope beautiful with the ambrosial beauty with which well-crowned Cytherea anoints herself when she goes to the lovely dance of the Graces (18.192–194). For Paphos and Cyprus see [[paphos]] and [[cyprus]].
