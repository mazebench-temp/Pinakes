summary: Odysseus names Eurytus of Oechalia with Heracles among the men of former days with whom he will not vie at archery, and tells his death: Apollo in anger killed great Eurytus before he reached old age, because he challenged the god to shoot with the bow (8.223–228).
# Εὐρύτῳ Οἰχαλιῆι (8.224) and μέγας Εὔρυτος (8.226) are claimed by the entry.
---

**The men of former days.** “But with men of former days I will not wish to vie, / neither with Heracles nor with Eurytus of Oechalia, / who vied even with the immortals in archery” (8.223–225; see [[heracles]], [[oechalia]] and [[men-of-today]]). Εὐρύτῳ Οἰχαλιῆι is the Iliad's Εὐρύτου Οἰχαλιῆος, “Eurytus of Oechalia” (*Iliad* 2.596, 2.730), from whose house Thamyris was coming when the Muses met him and took away his song (*Iliad* 2.594–600; see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). ἐρίζεσκον is only “matched themselves”, and of the two, W. W. Merry notes, Eurytus alone actually contended with the gods (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.225; see the translation's note on 8.225).

**His death.** “That is why great Eurytus died early, and did not reach old age / in his halls; for Apollo in anger / killed him, because he challenged him to shoot with the bow” (8.226–228; see [[apollo]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Merry sets beside it Diomedes' story of Lycurgus: “For not even the son of Dryas, mighty Lycurgus, / lived long, who strove with the gods of heaven” (*Iliad* 6.130–131; Merry on 8.226; see [[lycurgus]]). τῷ, “that is why”, makes the early death the price of the challenge. Odysseus tells it to the Phaeacians to set a limit to his own boast, after claiming that he is far better with the bow than all the mortals now on earth, save Philoctetes (8.219–222; see [[philoctetes]]).

**His bow.** The Odyssey remembers him once more in the twenty-first book. The bow with which Odysseus will kill the suitors “great Eurytus used to carry before, and he left it to his son, dying in his high halls” (21.32–33, Greek only, a plain rendering): Iphitus, son of Eurytus, gave it to Odysseus when they met in Messene, and Odysseus never took it to war but kept it at home in memory of his friend (21.11–41; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).
