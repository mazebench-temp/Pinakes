---
summary: Merry refers Odysseus' boast of the bow, in which he names Heracles and Eurytus, to Amphitryon's defense of archery in Euripides' Heracles, and cites the Orestes for the order of the words in Euryalus' “stranger, father”.
---

- **The archer.** Odysseus knows well how to handle the polished bow and would be the first to strike his man in a throng of hostile men, though Philoctetes alone surpassed him at Troy, and he will not vie with Heracles or Eurytus (8.215–228). Merry notes, after Nitzsch, that Homer arms Heracles with the bow, and that the club and the lion's skin were first given him by Peisander of Rhodes or by Stesichorus, and refers to Amphitryon's praise of archery in Euripides (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.225). Answering Lycus, who calls Heracles a coward for fighting with the bow, Amphitryon says that the man in armor is a slave to his weapons and dies by the cowardice of the men beside him, while the archer, shooting countless arrows from afar, wards off the enemy and keeps his body safe: “this is the wisest thing in battle” (*Heracles* 188–203, a plain rendering): [[bow]], [[heracles]], [[eurytus]] and [[philoctetes]].
- **“Stranger, father.”** For πάτερ ὦ ξεῖνε (8.408), the vocative divided by ὦ, Merry cites Electra's Μυκηνίδες ὦ φίλαι, “Mycenaean women, my friends” (*Orestes* 1246), with like orders in Sophocles and Plato (Merry on 8.408): [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]].
