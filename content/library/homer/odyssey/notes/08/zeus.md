summary: Zeus does not act in the book; he is named in song, in rite and in prayer. The quarrel of Demodocus' first song began the bane of Trojans and Danaans “through the counsels of great Zeus”; Alcinous says that Zeus lays their deeds on the Phaeacians; in the second song he is Aphrodite's father, to whom Hephaestus paid the bride-price, and the first of the gods whom Hephaestus calls to see; Alcinous gives the stranger a golden goblet to pour libations to Zeus; Odysseus answers Nausicaa, “so may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera”; and the Muse is “the child of Zeus”.

**The counsels of great Zeus.** Apollo foretold the quarrel at Pytho, “for then the beginning of bane was rolling upon / Trojans and Danaans through the counsels of great Zeus” (8.81–82; see [[apollo]], [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[the-beginning-of-evils]]). Διὸς μεγάλου διὰ βουλάς is found only here; the Odyssey has θεῶν ὀλοὰς διὰ βουλάς, “through the baneful counsels of the gods”, of Oedipus (11.276), and the Iliad Ἀθηναίης διὰ βουλάς, of the fall of Ilium (*Iliad* 15.71). The Iliad, too, begins with a quarrel of the best of the Achaeans, during which “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (*Iliad* 1.5–7; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Some editions lacked 8.81–82, and the scholia report that they were athetized (see the translation's note on 8.74–82).

**What Zeus lays on men.** Alcinous asks the stranger to remember the Phaeacians' excellence, “the kind of deeds that on us too / Zeus lays, continually, from the days of our fathers” (8.244–245; see [[alcinous]] and [[arete]]): not boxing and wrestling, but running, ships, the feast, the lyre and the dance (8.246–249). He is answering Odysseus, who has told Euryalus that “the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all / men alike” (8.167–168; see [[different-gifts]]).

**The father of the bride.** In the song of Ares and Aphrodite Zeus is named as the bride's father: she “had just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus” (8.289; see [[aphrodite]] and [[eristhenes]]), and Hephaestus calls her “Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus” (8.308; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]). He calls Zeus first of all the gods, “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever” (8.306 = 5.7; see [[father-zeus]] and [[olympians]]), and means to hold the lovers in the bonds “until her father gives me back the whole bride-price, / all that I put into his hands for the sake of the dog-faced girl” (8.318–319; see [[bride-price]] and [[hephaestus]]). The “two parents” whom Hephaestus blames for his lameness are, in the Iliad, Zeus and Hera (8.312; *Iliad* 1.577–578, 18.395–397). The gods who come are named, Poseidon, Hermes and Apollo (8.322–323); the song does not say that Zeus came.

**Libations.** Alcinous adds his own gift to the guest-gifts: “And I will grant him this beautiful goblet of mine, / of gold, so that remembering me all his days / he may pour libations in his hall to Zeus and the other gods” (8.430–432; see [[libation]], [[cup]] and [[guest-gifts]]). At the first song, each time the singer stopped, Odysseus would “take the two-handled cup and pour libations to the gods” (8.89).

**The husband of Hera.** Nausicaa bids the stranger remember her in his native land, since to her first he owes the price for his life, and he answers: “so may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera, / that I may come home and see the day of my homecoming; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god / always, all my days” (8.465–468; see [[nausicaa]], [[loud-thundering]], [[husband-of-hera]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). Telemachus answers Helen in two of the same verses when she has read an omen of his father's return (15.180–181), and Menelaus prays in the same title for Telemachus' homecoming (15.111–112); in the Iliad ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης ends oaths and wishes (*Iliad* 7.411, 10.329, 16.88; see [[hera]]).

**The children of Zeus.** The book names four: Aphrodite, “the daughter of Zeus” (8.308); Apollo, “son of Zeus” (8.334), and Hermes, “son of Zeus” (8.335; see [[son-of-zeus]]); and the Muse, “the child of Zeus” (8.488; see [[muses]]).
