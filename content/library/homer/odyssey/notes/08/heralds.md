summary: Athena summons the Phaeacians to their assembly in the likeness of Alcinous' herald (8.7–15). The herald of the house, Pontonous, leads the blind singer in to the feast and seats him (8.47, 8.62–70), and a herald leads him out to the games, brings him his lyre for the dance and leads him in again to supper, where he carries him Odysseus' portion of the chine (8.105–107, 8.256–262, 8.471–483); each of the twelve kings sends a herald for the guest-gifts, and the heralds carry them to the king's house (8.399, 8.417–418).

**Athena as herald.** The day begins with a summons. While the king leads the way to the place of assembly by the ships, “Pallas Athena went up and down through the city / in the likeness of the herald of wise-hearted Alcinous” (8.7–8), and standing beside each man she bids the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians come and learn about the stranger (8.10–14; see [[athena]] and [[assembly]]). She does a herald's work in a herald's shape, as at Troy, “in the likeness of a herald”, she ordered the people to be silent so that they might hear Odysseus' words (εἰδομένη κήρυκι, *Iliad* 2.279–282). The places of assembly fill, “and many gazed when they saw / the wise-hearted son of Laertes” (8.16–18; see [[odysseus]]).

**The herald and the singer.** The herald of the house serves the blind singer through the day. “A herald went to fetch the divine singer” (8.47), μετῴχετο being “went for” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.47); “the herald came near, leading the trusty singer”, and Pontonous, named as he sets the chair, hangs the lyre on its peg and guides the blind man's hand to it (8.62–69; see [[pontonous]], [[demodocus]] and [[lyre]]). For the games the herald hangs the lyre up again, takes the singer by the hand and leads him out by the road the others went (8.105–108); for the dance, at the king's word, “the herald rose / to bring the hollow lyre from the king's house”, and “the herald came near, bringing the clear-toned lyre / for Demodocus” (8.256–262); at supper he leads him in and seats him against the pillar in the verses of the morning (8.471–473 = 8.62, 8.66). The herald is also the guest's messenger to the singer: “Then Odysseus of many wiles spoke to the herald”, “Herald, here, give this meat, so that he may eat, / to Demodocus”, and “the herald took it and put it in the hands / of the hero Demodocus” (8.474–483; see [[odysseus]] and [[singers]]). Of these heralds only Pontonous is named; on whether the others are he, see his note.

**The heralds of the kings.** When the king bids the twelve kings each bring the stranger a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold, “they all approved and bade it be done, / and each sent forth a herald to bring the gifts” (8.398–399; see [[kings]] and [[guest-gifts]]), the second verse being the one with which the suitors send for their gifts to Penelope (18.291, Greek only); and at sunset “the noble heralds carried them to the house of Alcinous” (8.417–418; see [[noble]]), where the king's sons received them and set them beside their mother (8.419–420). While the heralds, the queen and her sons were busy with the stowage of the gifts, the king led Odysseus and the chieftains to their seats of honor (Merry on 8.421; see [[alcinous]]).

**Public officers.** The nine stewards of the games who level the dancing floor are δήμιοι, “officers of the people” (8.258–259): public officers, Merry explains, not servants of the king's household, like the public heralds whom Penelope calls δημιοεργοί, “who serve the people” (19.135, Greek only; cp. 1.109), and distinguished from them by their duties at the gatherings (Merry on 8.258; see [[dance]] and the translation's key words).
