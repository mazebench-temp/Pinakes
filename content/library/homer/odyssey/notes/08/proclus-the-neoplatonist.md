---
summary: Proclus' essays on Plato's Republic draw on this book more than once. The bonds of Hephaestus become the craftsman's binding of the world's opposites; Demodocus is Homer's pattern of inspired poetry and of his own blindness; the gods who laugh in the doorway are, in their epithet, “givers of blessings”; and Epeius, maker of the horse, explains a choice in the myth of Er. In his commentary on the First Alcibiades Odysseus' refusal to vie with the men of former days is a lesson in moderation.
---

- **The bonds of Hephaestus.** Answering Socrates' objection to the story, Proclus reads it as theology: Ares divides the opposites of the cosmos and Hephaestus makes its visible order, both need Aphrodite, and the bonds that Hephaestus pours around the lovers, invisible to the other gods, are the reasons by which the craftsman makes one order out of strife and harmony; he looses them at Poseidon's request, since what is below the moon must perish and be born again (*On the Republic* I 140–143 Kroll, on 8.266–366). When Homer tells of these bonds, Proclus says, he is plainly inspired, speaking from his possession by the Muses (*On the Republic* I 193 Kroll): [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]].
- **Demodocus.** Homer was said to be blind because he turned from the beauty of the senses to the true beauty, and to have suffered what he says Demodocus suffered, whom “the god who leads music”, in Proclus' words, where Homer has the Muse, robbed of his eyes and gave sweet song (8.64; *On the Republic* I 173–175 Kroll). In the essay on the kinds of poetry Demodocus is Homer's inspired singer, who began “stirred by the god” and was taught by the Muse or Apollo (8.488–499); that Homer sets him forth as himself was “among the things much talked of”; and Phemius, Clytemnestra's singer and Thamyris complete the series of singers (*On the Republic* I 193–195 Kroll): [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]], [[demodocus]], [[phemius]], [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]] and [[thamyris]].
- **Givers of blessings.** Homer, Proclus says, marks the nature of the gods by goodness, unfailing power and knowledge, for he calls them θεοὶ δωτῆρες ἑάων, “gods, givers of blessings”, and says that the gods can do all things and know all things (*On the Republic* I 167 Kroll). In Homer the phrase belongs only to this book, to the gods who come to Hephaestus' doorway to laugh at the trapped lovers (8.325; δῶτορ ἑάων, of Hermes, 8.335): [[olympians]] and [[laughter-of-the-gods]].
- **Epeius.** In the myth of Er the soul of Epeius goes into the life of a craftswoman (Plato, *Republic* 10.620c). Proclus explains: among men Epeius was a craftsman, since he made the horse, “which Epeius made with Athena's help” (8.493), but without a share in a man's life of war, as he admits himself that he falls short in battle (*Iliad* 23.670; *On the Republic* II 317 Kroll): [[epeius]] and [[plato]].
- **Men of former days.** Commenting on Alcibiades' ambition to surpass not only Pericles but the statesmen before him, Proclus asks how his passion for honor is not insatiable, when he does not consider the words of “the temperate Odysseus”: ἀνδράσι δὲ προτέροισιν ἐριζέμεν οὐκ ἐθελήσω, “But with men of former days I will not wish to vie” (8.223; *Commentary on the First Alcibiades* 147, a plain rendering). In the poem the words come in a boast: Odysseus will vie with all men now living, and not with Heracles or Eurytus, who vied with the gods (8.221–228): [[men-of-today]], [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[eurytus]].
