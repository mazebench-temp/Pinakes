---
summary: Laodamas and Euryalus address the stranger as “stranger, father” (8.145, 8.408); the Phaeacians' excellence comes “from the days of our fathers” (8.245); Hephaestus calls on “Father Zeus” and asks the bride-price back from Aphrodite's father (8.306, 8.318); and Alcinous asks the name the stranger's mother and father gave him and tells his own father's prophecy (8.550, 8.564).
---

ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father”, is the respectful address of the young to an older man (see the translation's table of key words): Laodamas uses it in his invitation to the games (8.145), and Euryalus in his apology, πάτερ ὦ ξεῖνε, with the order that Merry compares with Sophocles' ἔρεβος ὦ φαεννότατον (8.408; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.408; see [[xenia]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). The fathers of the Phaeacians are the source of their gifts: Zeus has laid them on them “from the days of our fathers” (ἐξ ἔτι πατρῶν, 8.245; see [[different-gifts]]). In the song Hephaestus calls “Father Zeus” to see the lovers, and asks Aphrodite's father for the bride-price (8.306, 8.318; see [[father-zeus]] and [[bride-price]]). At the end Alcinous asks for the name “by which they called you there, your mother and your father” (8.550; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]), and tells what he once heard from his own father, Nausithous (8.564–565; see [[nausithous]]).
