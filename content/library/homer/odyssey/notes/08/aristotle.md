---
summary: Aristotle quotes no line of this book, but his example of recognition through memory is its scene, the stranger weeping as he hears “the lyre player”. Merry sets the Rhetoric's Achilles, angry at Tenedos for not being invited to dinner, beside Demodocus' first song, and the Poetics' tragedies from the Little Iliad beside his third; a fragment of the Homeric Problems weighs Alcinous' talents of gold, the Symposium praises the bath before the feast, and the Politics explains the names of the stewards of the games and of the merchant “mindful of his freight”.
---

- **The lyre player.** The third kind of recognition is through memory, “the one in the tale told to Alcinous, where, hearing the lyre player, he remembered and shed tears, and so they were recognized” (*Poetics* 1455a2–3). The tears are this book's, at the first song and the third (8.83–95, 8.521–534), and Merry reports Mayor's remark that Aristotle's reference is “rather to b. 8” than to the tale itself (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1). Aristotle names the singer only by his art: [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]], [[demodocus]], [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-8]].
- **Not invited to dinner.** Among the apparent enthymemes from the accidental: “if one were to say that being invited to dinner is the greatest honor; for it was because he was not invited that Achilles conceived his wrath against the Achaeans at Tenedos; but he conceived his wrath as one dishonored, and this happened to come about through his not being invited” (*Rhetoric* 1401b16–19). Merry, after Nitzsch, sets the quarrel of the first song (8.75–82) at that feast on Tenedos, and finds the best evidence for it in a satyr play of Sophocles, to which he refers this passage (Merry on 8.75): [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[tenedos]].
- **The Little Iliad.** The subjects of the third song, the wooden horse and the sack (8.492–520), fall, Merry says, within the range of the Little Iliad, from which Aristotle counts more than eight tragedies, “the Judgment of Arms, the Philoctetes, the Neoptolemus, the Eurypylus, the Beggary, the Laconian Women, the Sack of Troy and the Sailing Away”, with the Sinon and the Trojan Women in a bracketed clause (*Poetics* 1459b4–7; Merry on 8.492): [[little-iliad]], [[sack-of-troy]], [[sinon-play]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]].
- **The talent.** Why does Achilles set the greatest prize, two talents of gold, for the fourth man in the chariot race (*Iliad* 23.269)? Aristotle answers that the talent was not a fixed measure: it is less than a horse there, and greatest in the gifts of the embassy, and Alcinous asks each king for a mantle, a tunic “and a talent of precious gold” (8.392–393), but when he himself gives a greater gift he gives his golden goblet (8.430) (*Homeric Problems* fr. 388 Gigon, from the scholia on *Iliad* 23.269): [[talent]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[cup]].
- **The bath before the feast.** Homer, exact in everything, did not leave out that one must bathe before going to dinner: so the housekeeper bids Odysseus bathe before the feast of the Phaeacians (8.449), and Telemachus and Peisistratus bathe at Sparta (4.48); for it was not fitting, Aristotle says, to come to a symposium covered in sweat and dust (*Symposium* fr. 50 Gigon, from Athenaeus): [[bathing]] and [[feast]].
- **Stewards and recorders.** For the αἰσυμνῆται, the nine “stewards” who level the dancing floor (8.258–260), Merry refers to Aristotle's account of the aisymnetes of the old Greeks, an elected tyranny held for life or for a set time, as the Mytilenaeans once elected Pittacus against the exiles (*Politics* 3.14, 1285a29–b3; Merry on 8.258). For the merchant “mindful of his freight”, φόρτου τε μνήμων (8.163), Nitzsch, Merry reports, cited the officials at whom contracts and judgments are registered, who are called “sacred recorders and overseers and recorders” (*Politics* 6.8, 1321b34–40; Merry on 8.163): [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]], [[trade]] and [[politics-of-aristotle]].
