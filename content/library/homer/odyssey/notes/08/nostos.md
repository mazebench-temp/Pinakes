---
summary: Athena goes through the city “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (8.9). The Phaeacians want the stranger to tell his friends of their prowess “when he has returned home” (8.102, 8.252); he sits among them “in need of my homecoming” (8.156); and he answers Nausicaa with a prayer “that I may come home and see the day of my homecoming” (8.466).
---

The book is a day of waiting for the homecoming the Phaeacians have promised. Athena's purpose is in the verse with which she set out in Book 6, νόστον Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μητιόωσα, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (8.9 = 6.14; see [[athena]] and [[metis]]). Alcinous twice imagines the stranger at home, telling his friends “when he has returned home” how far the Phaeacians surpass others, first in the contests and then in seafaring, running, dancing and song (8.101–103, 8.251–253; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). The stranger has no heart for the games: “now in the midst of your assembly, in need of my homecoming, / I sit, begging the king and all the people” (νόστοιο χατίζων, 8.156–157; see [[supplication]]). To Nausicaa he answers with a prayer, “so may Zeus now grant it … that I may come home and see the day of my homecoming” (νόστιμον ἦμαρ, 8.465–466; see [[day-of-homecoming]] and [[prayer]]). The escort that will bring him there is ready by the evening (see [[pompe]]).
