**The Muse in place of Zeus.** Helen said at Sparta that Zeus gives good and evil, now to one and now to another, for he can do all things (4.236–237; see the entry). The singer's portrait uses the same words, δίδου δʼ ἀγαθόν τε κακόν τε, of the Muse, and it names the two gifts, as the maxim does not. The evil is blindness: ὀφθαλμῶν μὲν ἄμερσε, “she robbed him of his eyes”, where ἀμέρδω has its first sense, “deprive”, *expertem facere*, as Merry gives it after Curtius (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.64). The good is “sweet song” (8.64; see [[demodocus]] and [[muses]]). The gifts come together with love, τὸν πέρι μοῦσʼ ἐφίλησε, “whom the Muse loved above all” (8.63), as Alcinous has said that “to him above all the god has given song” (8.44). Love and loss come from the same giver, as the gifts of Zeus come mixed to some men in Achilles' parable of the two jars (*Iliad* 24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]).

**Sight and song.** The blind singer sings what he did not see. Odysseus will praise him for singing the fate of the Achaeans “as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another”, and will name the Muse or Apollo as his teacher (8.487–491; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]). In the hall the herald hangs the lyre above his head and shows him where to take it in his hands (8.67–69; see the translation's note on 8.68 and [[heralds]]).
