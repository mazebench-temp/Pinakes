summary: The king of the day of the games. At dawn he leads the Phaeacians to their assembly by the ships and proposes an escort and a feast for the stranger whose name he does not know (8.1–47); twice he alone notices the stranger weeping at the singer's songs, and turns the company to the games, and later stops the lyre (8.93–103, 8.532–543). He answers the stranger's challenge with the Phaeacians' own boast (8.235–255), bids the twelve kings and Euryalus give him gifts and adds a golden goblet of his own (8.385–432), and at last asks his name, his land, and why he weeps, telling him of the Phaeacian ships and of his father's prophecy (8.536–586).

**At the assembly.** The book opens on the king: “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / the sacred might of Alcinous rose from his bed, / and up rose Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities. / And the sacred might of Alcinous led the way for them / to the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, which was built for them beside the ships” (8.1–5; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]], [[assembly]] and [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). He keeps the promise of the night before, to call more of the elders together at dawn, entertain the stranger, sacrifice to the gods and then take thought for his escort (7.189–192), while Athena, “in the likeness of the herald of wise-hearted Alcinous”, goes through the city to bring them there (8.7–15; see [[athena]], [[heralds]] and [[wise-hearted]]).

**The escort and the feast.** When they are all assembled, he speaks:

{{quote:2b8c7465-7dcf-5ed0-a0cf-a3bd025c5b95}}

The two clauses of 8.29 expand ὅς τις, “whoever he may be, whether he be come from the men of the East or of the West” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.29; see the translation's note on 8.29). ἔμπεδον εἶναι, “that it be made sure”, has the ambiguity of the English “certain”: a promised boon is certain before it is given, and certain again when the promise holds good (Merry on 8.30). πάρος, “as has been our custom”, is the idiomatic use for custom, again in “as always” (Merry on 8.31; see the translation's note on 8.31, 8.36), and in οὐδὲ γὰρ οὐδέ the second οὐδέ fixes the negative on ἄλλος τις, “no other man either” (Merry on 8.32). He knows only that the stranger is a wanderer who wants to go home: “I do not know who he is” (8.28) opens the day, as “Tell me the name by which they called you there” will close it (8.550). On the ship on her first voyage and the fifty-two young men, see [[ships]], [[pompe]] and the translation's note on 8.35–36. The rest of his speech gives the young men the ship and the kings the feast: “but the rest of you, / the scepter-bearing kings, come to my beautiful house, / so that we may make the stranger welcome in the halls; / and let no one refuse. And call the divine singer, / Demodocus” (8.40–44; see [[kings]], [[xenia]] and [[demodocus]]). In his house he sacrifices twelve sheep, eight white-tusked hogs and two shambling cattle for the feast (8.59–61; see [[feast]] and [[sacrifice]]).

**The first tears.** When the stranger hides his face in his mantle and weeps at the singer's lay of Troy, “Then he escaped the notice of all the others as he let tears fall, / but Alcinous alone noticed him and observed him, / sitting near him, and heard him groaning heavily” (8.93–95), the verses that return when he weeps at the third song (8.532–534; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[shedding-tears]]). He says nothing of the tears, of which the stranger was ashamed (8.86), but turns the company from the feast to the games: “Already we have had our hearts' fill of our equal share of the feast / and of the lyre, which is the companion of the rich feast; / but now let us go out and make trial of the contests, / all of them, so that the stranger may tell his friends, / when he has returned home, how far we surpass others / in boxing and in wrestling and in jumping and in running” (8.98–103; see [[contests]], [[lyre]] and the translation's key words). δαιτός is Aristarchus' reading; others inverted δαιτός and θυμόν (Merry's critical note on 8.98).

**His answer to the challenge.** After Euryalus' taunt and the discus, the stranger challenges the Phaeacians to any contest but running, “and they all fell hushed in silence. / But Alcinous alone, answering him, said” (8.234–235; see [[silence]]). He grants that the stranger wishes to show “the excellence that attends you” (8.237; see [[arete]] and the translation's note on 8.237–239), and asks him to tell another of the heroes, when he feasts at home beside his wife and children, of the excellence that Zeus has laid on the Phaeacians “continually, from the days of our fathers” (8.241–245): δαινύῃ, for which Ameis suggests δαινύεαι (Merry on 8.243; see the translation's note on 8.243), and οἷα … πατρῶν is “what prowess Zeus bestoweth on us, from our fathers' times right on” (Merry on 8.244). Then the boast of his people: “For we are not flawless boxers, nor wrestlers, / but we run swiftly with our feet, and are the best with ships; / and always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances / and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” (8.246–249; see [[phaeacians]] and [[different-gifts]]). Nitzsch read the lines as the picture of a people devoted to peace, as a scholium does, but Merry finds “a certain colour of luxury in it all, which was a common theme of the Sophists” (on 8.244). He calls for the dancers and for Demodocus' lyre, “that lies somewhere in our house” (8.250–255; see [[dance]]): “So spoke godlike Alcinous” (8.256; see [[godlike]]).

**The guest-gifts.** When the stranger praises the dancers, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people, / you boasted that your dancers were the best, / and indeed it has proved true; awe holds me as I look” (8.382–384; see [[preeminent]] and [[awe-holds-me]]), ἀπείλησας being “you boasted” and ἑτοῖμα τέτυκτο “lo! thy words are fulfilled” (Merry on 8.383, 8.384), “the sacred might of Alcinous rejoiced” (8.385). “The stranger seems to me to be a very wise man. / But come, let us give him a guest-gift, as is fitting” (8.388–389): each of the twelve kings who rule the land under him, the thirteenth, is to bring a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold, and Euryalus is to make amends with words and a gift (8.390–397; see [[kings]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]). Euryalus answers him with his formula of address and obeys, “as you bid” (8.401–402).

**The goblet.** At sunset he leads the stranger and the chieftains to their seats while the heralds, the queen and his sons stow the gifts (8.417–422; Merry on 8.421), and bids Arete bring a chest, put in a mantle and a tunic of her own, and heat water for the bath, “so that when he has bathed and seen all the gifts well laid away, / which the noble Phaeacians have brought here for him, / he may take delight in the feast and in hearing the strains of the song” (8.423–429; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[chest]] and [[bathing]]). Then his own gift: “And I will grant him this beautiful goblet of mine, / of gold, so that remembering me all his days / he may pour libations in his hall to Zeus and the other gods” (8.430–432; see [[cup]], [[libation]] and [[zeus]]). He asks to be remembered, as he has asked the stranger to remember the Phaeacians' excellence (8.244) and as his daughter will ask at the door (8.461–462; see [[nausicaa]]). ὕμνος, “the strains of the song”, is found only here in Homer (Merry on 8.429; see the translation's note on 8.429).

**The questions.** When the stranger weeps at the song of the horse, the verses of the morning return (8.532–534 = 8.93–95), and the king stops the singer: “and let Demodocus now stop his clear-toned lyre; / for it is not to all of us that he gives delight by singing this” (8.537–538; see [[demodocus]]). The escort and the gifts are for the stranger's sake, and “As good as a brother are a stranger and a suppliant / to a man who has even a little hold on wisdom” (8.544–547; see [[as-good-as-a-brother]], [[xenia]], [[supplication]] and [[kasignetos]]); ἀντί is “as good as” (Merry on 8.546; see the translation's note on 8.546), and τῷ, “So now”, is “wherefore”, because he is treated with such brotherly kindness (Merry on 8.548). Then the question that the day has put off:

{{quote:863c6e48-f86a-5c10-a8ad-3b86da5a1526}}

κεῖθι, “there”, is a general way of referring to the stranger's home, which he did not yet know by name (Merry on 8.550), and πάμπαν goes with οὔ τις, “no one at all” (Merry on 8.552; see the translation's note on 8.552 and [[no-one-is-nameless]]). He tells of the Phaeacian ships, which need no helmsmen or steering oars and know the thoughts and minds of men (8.556–563; see [[phaeacians]], [[steering-oar]] and the translation's notes on 8.555 and 8.556–563), and of his father's prophecy that Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship returning from an escort and cover their city with a great mountain, leaving it to the god to bring it to pass or not (8.564–571; see [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). Then he asks where the stranger's wanderings have taken him, among men harsh and savage and unjust or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods (8.572–576; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]), ἠμὲν … δίκαιοι being the contrast to οἵ τε φιλόξεινοι (Merry on 8.575), and why he weeps at the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios, which the gods wrought “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.577–580; see [[song-and-fame]]): did he lose a kinsman by marriage at Troy, or a companion, “Since in no way inferior to a brother / is a companion who has wise understanding” (8.581–586; see [[in-laws]], [[hetairos]] and [[a-companion-no-worse-than-a-brother]]). The book ends on his question; the answer is the tale of the wanderings, which begins with praise of his feast and his singer (9.1–11, Greek only; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**His epithets in this book.** ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the sacred might of Alcinous” (8.2, 8.4, 8.385, 8.421), and μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the might of Alcinous” (8.423; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]); δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (8.8, 8.13, 8.56; see [[wise-hearted]]); ἀμύμων, “noble” (8.118, 8.419; see [[blameless]]); θεοείκελος, “godlike” (8.256; see [[godlike]]); Ἀλκίνοε κρεῖον, πάντων ἀριδείκετε λαῶν, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (8.382, 8.401; see [[preeminent]] and the translation's formulas); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, in his daughter's title (8.464; see [[great-hearted]]); Ἀλκίνοον βασιλῆα, “King Alcinous” (8.469; see [[kings]]).
