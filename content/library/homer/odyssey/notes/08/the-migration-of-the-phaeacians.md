---
heading: Nausithous' prophecy
summary: Alcinous ends his account of the Phaeacian ships, which need no helmsmen and fear no harm, with what his father Nausithous used to say: that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians their safe escorts, and would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship returning from an escort and cover their city round about with a great mountain (8.564–571). Whether the god will do it, he says, is the god's pleasure.
# Unnamed: the founder's prophecy (8.564-571), told by his son; Nausithous and Poseidon are named in their own entries.

**The occasion.** Alcinous asks the stranger his land, his people and his city, “so that our ships may carry you there, aiming with their minds”, for the Phaeacian ships know the thoughts of men and the cities of all men, cross the sea wrapped in mist and cloud, “and never do they / have any fear of coming to harm or of perishing” (8.555–563; see [[phaeacians]], [[ships]] and [[pompe]]). The prophecy follows at once, and it names the one harm that they have to fear:

**The grudge.** ἀγάσασθαι is “begrudged”, the word of the gods' grudge against goddesses' mortal husbands in Calypso's complaint (8.565; 5.119–129; see the translation's key words and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Its cause is in the formula of the next verse, πομποὶ ἀπήμονές εἰμεν ἁπάντων, “we are escorts of all men and come to no harm” (8.566): W. W. Merry takes Poseidon to be jealous of an immunity from harm that amounted to independence of his authority and power (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.566; see [[poseidon]]). The god of the sea begrudges the people who cross it unharmed.

**Ship and mountain.** ῥαισέμεναι, “he would shatter”, has Poseidon for its subject (Merry on 8.569; variants ῥαίσεσθαι and ῥαίσασθαι, see the translation's note on 8.569). The mountain, Merry thinks, is not one hurled upon the city to crush it, but a wall of mountain to pen the Phaeacians in, cutting off their access to the sea and ending their seafaring life (on 8.569); ἀμφικαλύψειν, “cover … round about”, is the verb of the city that “enfolded” the wooden horse in the song just ended (8.511; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

**Fulfilled or not.** “So the old man used to say; and these things the god may bring to pass, / or they may be left unfulfilled, as is the pleasure of his heart” (8.570–571; see [[nausithous]]). ἔπλετο marks a culminating point, “has come to be”, and Merry likens the truism to Teiresias' *aut erit aut non*, “it will be or it will not”, in Horace (on 8.571). The god's choice is told in the thirteenth book. When the ship that carried Odysseus home comes back in sight of the city, Poseidon turns it to stone, and Alcinous recognizes “the prophecies spoken long ago by my father” in the verses he speaks here, with one change: “so the old man used to say; and now all of it is being fulfilled”. He bids the Phaeacians give up escorting mortals and sacrifice twelve bulls to Poseidon, that he may pity them and not cover their city with a great mountain (13.172–184, Greek only; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
