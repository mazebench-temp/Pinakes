A husband wronged by his wife asks for the gifts he gave her father.

**The price of a wife.** In the song Hephaestus has caught Ares and Aphrodite in his bonds, and calls the gods to see them (8.304–320; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[hephaestus]]). His demand is a husband's: the gifts with which he won Aphrodite from her father's house, “all that I put into his hands” (ἐγγυάλιξα, 8.319), must come back from Zeus (see [[zeus]] and [[aphrodite]]). ἀποδῷσιν is the reading of this text and of La Roche and Nauck; most manuscripts have the future ἀποδώσει, but εἰς ὅ κε is naturally followed in Homer by the subjunctive (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.318 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 8.318). The reason he gives is the bride's: “his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control” (οὐκ ἐχέθυμος, 8.320; see the translation's note on 8.320 and [[beauty-and-its-price]]).

**Fine and pledge.** The gods in the doorway reckon the wrong in the same terms: Ares “owes the adulterer's fine” (8.332; see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]), and Poseidon becomes surety that he will pay all that is due (8.344–356; see [[pledges-for-the-worthless]]). The song does not say whether the bride-price was returned. In the Iliad Hephaestus' wife is Charis (*Iliad* 18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]).
