summary: In Demodocus' second song Ares is the lover of Aphrodite. He lies with her in the house of Hephaestus and gives her many gifts, and when he sees the husband go off toward Lemnos he comes again; the two are caught in the bonds that no one can see. The swiftest of the gods, caught by the lame one, owes the adulterer's fine; Poseidon stands surety for him, and when he is loosed he goes off to Thrace. Outside the song he is the measure of warriors: Euryalus is “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals”, and Odysseus went to the house of Deiphobus “like Ares”.

**The lover.** The song is “about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite, / how they first lay together in the house of Hephaestus / in secret; and he gave her many gifts, and shamed the bed and couch / of lord Hephaestus” (8.267–270; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]], [[aphrodite]], [[hephaestus]] and [[demodocus]]). Ἄρεος is the rarer genitive, found in Homer otherwise only twice in the Iliad (*Iliad* 4.441, 19.47). A scholium takes the many gifts for the adulterer's fine paid in advance, which Merry finds a strange anticipation of the story; they are the presents Ares gave Aphrodite to win her consent (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.269; see the translation's note on 8.269).

When the Sun has told Hephaestus and the husband has gone, “Nor did Ares of the golden reins keep a blind watch, / when he saw Hephaestus, famed for his craft, going away” (8.285–286; see [[of-the-golden-reins]]). οὐδʼ ἀλαοσκοπιὴν εἶχε is said in the Iliad of Apollo and of Poseidon keeping watch on the battle (*Iliad* 10.515, 13.10, 14.135); Merry renders it “kept no blind man's watch”, an oxymoron, and for χρυσήνιος compares the horses of Ares “with golden headbands” (on 8.285; *Iliad* 5.358). He takes Aphrodite's hand: “Come here, my dear, let us go to bed and take our pleasure, lying together; / for Hephaestus is no longer at home, but has gone already, I think, / to Lemnos, to the Sintians of savage speech” (8.292–294; see [[sintians]]). τραπείομεν εὐνηθέντε is the half-verse of Paris to Helen and of Zeus to Hera (*Iliad* 3.441, 14.314), and Merry takes τραπείομεν from τέρπω, “take our pleasure” (on 8.292; see the translation's note on 8.292).

**Caught.** Hephaestus has made the snare “in his anger at Ares”, κεχολωμένος Ἄρει (8.276), and the bonds close around the two, “and they could not move or lift any of their limbs” (8.296–299). Hephaestus complains that Aphrodite “loves destructive Ares, / because he is handsome and sound of foot” (8.309–310). ἀίδηλος is the word with which Zeus rebukes Ares in the Iliad (*Iliad* 5.897), and Ares' own word for Athena (*Iliad* 5.880); Merry explains it as “annihilating”, “making unseen” (on 8.309; see [[aidelos]] and the translation's note on 8.309). ἀρτίπος, “sound of foot”, is said in Homer otherwise only of Blind Folly (*Iliad* 9.505; see [[litai]]). His speed is the point of the gods' saying (see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]): Hephaestus, slow as he is, “has caught Ares, / though he is the swiftest of the gods who hold Olympus” (8.330–331). In the Iliad the swiftness is his mark, since warriors are “the equal of swift Ares”, θοῷ ἀτάλαντος Ἄρηϊ (*Iliad* 8.215, 13.295, 16.784; see [[equal-of-ares]]), and the verse-end θεῶν οἳ Ὄλυμπον ἔχουσιν, “of the gods who hold Olympus”, is the one in which Zeus tells him that of all of them he is the most hateful (*Iliad* 5.890). “And so he owes the adulterer's fine”, μοιχάγρια (8.332), a word found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row).

**The surety and Thrace.** Ares says nothing after he is caught. Poseidon pleads for him and stands surety for the fine (8.344–356; see [[poseidon]]); Merry notes that Poseidon, the most venerable of the gods of Olympus after Zeus, was besides Ares' uncle (on 8.344). Loosed, the two spring up, “and he went his way to Thrace” (8.361; see [[thrace]]). Thrace is his country in the Iliad too, where Ares and his son Rout arm themselves to go out from Thrace to war (*Iliad* 13.298–303; see [[rout]]).

**Aphrodite's Ares.** In the Iliad Aphrodite calls Ares “dear brother” when she begs his horses (*Iliad* 5.359), and when Athena has felled him in the battle of the gods, “Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took him by the hand and led him away” (*Iliad* 21.416; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Merry, after Gladstone, finds in the lay an “undesigned coincidence” that gives point to that otherwise unexplained trait (on 8.267). Hesiod makes Phobos, Deimos and Harmonia the children whom Cytherea bore to Ares (*Theogony* 933–937; see the general article).

**The measure of warriors.** Outside the song he is a standard of comparison. Among the Phaeacian young men “up rose Euryalus too, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.115; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[ruin-of-mortals]]), in the words the Iliad uses of Hector (βροτολοιγῷ ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, *Iliad* 11.295, 13.802). In the third song Odysseus went to the house of Deiphobus “like Ares, together with godlike Menelaus” (8.517–518; see [[deiphobus]], [[menelaus]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]).
