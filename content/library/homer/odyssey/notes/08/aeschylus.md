summary: Merry, after Nägelsbach, sets beside Odysseus' praise of Demodocus, who sings the fate of the Achaeans “as if perhaps you had been present yourself”, Cassandra in the Agamemnon, whose knowledge of the old crimes of the house of Atreus amazes the chorus “as if you had stood beside us”.
---

- **As if present.** Odysseus says that the Muse or Apollo must have taught Demodocus, “for all too well, in due order, you sing the fate of the Achaeans, / all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through, / as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another” (8.488–491). Merry explains the singer's debt to Apollo, the god of prophecy, who knows the past as well as the future, and reports Nägelsbach's reminder that Calchas, inspired by Apollo, knew the past as well as the future, “the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before” (*Iliad* 1.70), and that the same is said of Cassandra in Aeschylus (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.488). Cassandra bids the chorus swear that she knows the ancient sins of the house, and the chorus marvels that she, raised beyond the sea in a city of another tongue, hits the truth ὥσπερ εἰ παρεστάτεις, “as if you had stood beside us” (*Agamemnon* 1196–1201, a plain rendering): [[demodocus]], [[muses]], [[apollo]] and [[calchas]].
