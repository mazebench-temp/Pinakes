summary: Warm baths are among the Phaeacians' pleasures (8.249). In the song the Graces bathe and anoint Aphrodite at Paphos (8.364–366). In the evening Alcinous bids his household heat water for the stranger; the women set a tripod on the fire in the verses of the Iliad's washing of Patroclus, and Odysseus steps gladly into the warm bath, the first care of the kind he has had since he left Calypso (8.426–457).

**Warm baths.** Alcinous counts “changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” among the things always dear to his people (8.249; see [[phaeacians]] and [[clothing]]).

**The bath of the goddess.** At the end of Demodocus' song Aphrodite goes to Paphos, and “there the Graces washed her and anointed her with oil / immortal, such as is upon the gods who live forever” (8.364–365; see [[graces]], [[aphrodite]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* has the same two verses of her bath at Paphos (61–62).

**The bath is heated.** Alcinous bids his household: “And warm a cauldron on the fire for him, and heat water, / so that when he has bathed and seen all the gifts well laid away, / … / he may take delight in the feast and in hearing the strains of the song” (8.426–429; see [[cauldron]] and [[feast]]). ἀμφί goes with πυρί: “warm for him a cauldron over the fire”, the legs of the tripod bestriding the fire, as Sophocles imitates it in the *Ajax*, and the tripod is stand and cauldron in one (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.426). Arete gives the order to her slave women, and the heating is told in the verses of the washing of Patroclus' body in the Iliad: “And they set the tripod for the bath on the blazing fire, / and poured water into it, and took wood and kindled it beneath. / The fire played around the belly of the tripod, and the water grew warm” (8.435–437, after *Iliad* 18.344–348; see [[tripod]], [[fire]] and [[blazing-fire]]). λοετροχόος is “for filling the bath” (Merry on 8.435).

**The stranger's bath.** While the water heats, Arete packs the chest (8.438–448; see [[chest]]). Then:

{{quote:d92c823b-0080-5c67-9e14-aa00513764ef}}

οὔ τι … θάμιζεν, “he had not often been cared for so”, is a litotes (Merry on 8.451): the last bath he was given was in Calypso's house, before the raft (5.264), and since then he has washed the brine off himself in the river (6.224–227; see the Book 6 note). “As for a god” recalls the life Calypso offered him, to make him immortal and ageless all his days, as a scholium notes (Merry on 8.453; see [[calypso]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and the translation's row κομιδή). The housekeeper, ταμίη, gives the order, and the slave women bathe and anoint him and put a cloak and a tunic on him (8.449, 8.454–455; see [[handmaids]], [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]). He comes from the bathtub to the men at their wine, and Nausicaa stands by the doorpost to bid him farewell (8.456–462; see [[nausicaa]] and [[door]]).
