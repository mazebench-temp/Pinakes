**The prophecy.** The king has been telling the stranger of the Phaeacian ships, which need no helmsmen and cross the sea wrapped in mist and cloud, never fearing harm (8.555–563; see [[phaeacians]] and [[pompe]]). Then he adds a warning:

{{quote:d33f4ce4-73b7-5faa-9c41-f66d384f57b0}}

ἀλλὰ τόδʼ ὥς ποτε … ἄκουσα is “But this story I once heard thus told by my father's lips” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.564), and the imperfects ἔφασκε, “used to say”, and ἀγόρευʼ, “used to say”, make it a thing the old king said more than once (see [[alcinous]]). ἀπήμονες, “and come to no harm”, is the ground of the god's grudge: Poseidon was jealous of their immunity from harm, which was equivalent to independence of his authority and power (Merry on 8.566; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Nausithous was the god's own son (7.56–63), so that his people's founder foretold their punishment by his father. The subject of ῥαισέμεναι is Poseidon, “that he would crush” (Merry on 8.569), and the mountain is not to be hurled upon the city: Poseidon will pen the Phaeacians in with a mountain wall, cutting them off from the sea and ending their seafaring life (Merry on 8.569; see the translation's note on 8.569). The king leaves the outcome to the god's pleasure (8.570–571), ἔπλετο expressing a culminating point, “has come to be” (Merry on 8.571).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** In Book 13 Poseidon turns the ship that brought Odysseus home to stone in sight of the city, and Alcinous repeats his father's words, this time with “and now all these things are being fulfilled” (13.172–178, Greek only, a plain rendering; see the entry and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**His epithet.** ὁ γέρων, “the old man” (8.570), in his son's memory of him.
