summary: Athena makes the stranger taller and stouter for the Phaeacians (8.18–20); Euryalus is the best in looks after Laodamas, and the stranger tells him that looks are one gift of the gods and words another (8.116–117, 8.169–177). In the song Aphrodite loves Ares “because he is handsome and sound of foot”, and her lame husband says, “his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control” (8.308–320). Nausicaa, at her last appearance, “had her beauty from the gods” (8.457).
refs: [8.18-20, 8.116-117, 8.169-177, 8.310, 8.320, 8.457]
---

**The stranger's beauty.** On the morning of the assembly Athena pours grace on the stranger's head and shoulders and makes him “taller and stouter to look upon”, so that the Phaeacians may hold him dear (8.18–22; see [[charis]] and [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). The young men judge him by his body: Laodamas praises his thighs and shins, arms and neck, and sees that the sea has broken him (8.134–139; see [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]]). At the singer's song he covers “his handsome face” with his mantle (καλὰ πρόσωπα, 8.85; see [[tears]]).

**Looks and words.** Euryalus is “the best in looks and form / of all the Phaeacians after noble Laodamas” (8.116–117; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]), and the stranger answers his taunt with a lesson on looks. One man is less in looks, but a god crowns his words with beauty, μορφή; another is like the immortals in looks and has no grace in his words, “so with you too, your looks are outstanding, and not even a god / would fashion them otherwise, but in mind you are worthless” (8.169–177; see [[different-gifts]] and [[eidos]]). The beauty the stranger claims is that of speech.

**Beauty in the song.** The song turns on looks. Aphrodite dishonors her husband, Hephaestus says, “because I am lame”, and loves Ares, “because he is handsome and sound of foot, while I / was born a weakling” (8.308–311; see [[hephaestus]] and [[ares]]), and he sums up his wife's case: “because his daughter is beautiful, but she has no self-control” (οὕνεκά οἱ καλὴ θυγάτηρ, ἀτὰρ οὐκ ἐχέθυμος, 8.320). The translation follows the scholium's sense of ἐχέθυμος, “restraining desire”; Merry, taking θυμός as the heart and affections, would render “heartless” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.320; see the translation's note on 8.320). After the bonds are loosed the Graces wash and anoint her at Paphos and clothe her in lovely garments, “a wonder to see” (8.364–366; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**Nausicaa.** At the evening feast Nausicaa, “who had her beauty from the gods”, stands by the doorpost of the hall and marvels at him (θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, 8.457–459; see [[nausicaa]] and [[thambos]]), and asks only to be remembered (8.461–462; see [[zoagria]]).
