summary: In the simile of the third song the captive woman's cheeks waste away “with most pitiful grief” (ἐλεεινοτάτῳ ἄχεϊ, 8.530), “so Odysseus let a pitiful tear fall from beneath his brows” (ἐλεεινὸν … δάκρυον, 8.531).
# ἐλεεινοτάτῳ (8.530), only here in Homer; ἐλεεινόν is in the Book 5 note.
grc: [ἐλεεινοτάτῳ]
en: [most pitiful]
---

The pitiful of the book is a simile. A woman flings herself upon her dying husband before his city; the enemy beat her back and shoulders with their spears and lead her into slavery, “to have toil and misery, / and her cheeks waste away with most pitiful grief” (8.523–530; see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]], [[ponos]] and [[achos]]). The man who weeps like her is the sacker of cities, hearing the song of the city he sacked (8.514–521; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). ἐλεεινόν, said of his tear, is not an idle word: it marks the tears as springing from feeling, as tears of bodily pain would not (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.530; see [[tears]]). No one in the hall pities him aloud. Alcinous notices, stops the song, and asks why he weeps (8.532–541, 8.577–578).
