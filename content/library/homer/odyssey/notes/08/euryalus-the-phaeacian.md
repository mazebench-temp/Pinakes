summary: The handsomest of the Phaeacians after Laodamas, he wins the wrestling (8.115–127); when the stranger declines Laodamas' challenge, he rebukes him to his face as a captain of traders mindful of his cargo, “you are not like an athlete” (8.158–164), and is answered with the gods' unequal gifts and the discus (8.165–198). At the king's bidding he makes amends with his sword, and the two part with blessings (8.396–416).

**At the games.** “And up rose Euryalus too, the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals, / the son of Naubolus, who was the best in looks and form / of all the Phaeacians after noble Laodamas” (8.115–117; see [[ruin-of-mortals]], [[naubolus-father-of-euryalus]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). The verse-end of Hector, βροτολοιγῷ ἶσος Ἄρηϊ (*Iliad* 11.295, 13.802), is given to a young man at a festival of games (see the entry). On the reading without θʼ, which makes Ναυβολίδης his patronymic and not another competitor, see the translation's note on 8.116. In the second contest “Euryalus surpassed all the best” (8.127): the wrestling is his, as the running is Clytoneus' and the boxing Laodamas' (8.120–130; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[contests]]).

**“Now go yourself.”** When Laodamas proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows any contest, Euryalus answers him: “Laodamas, this word you have spoken is fair and right. / Now go yourself and challenge him, and make your word known” (8.141–142). πέφραδε μῦθον is “make known thy will” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.142), and the second verse was not known to Aristarchus, Zenodotus or Aristophanes (see the translation's note on 8.142).

**The taunt.** The stranger pleads his sorrows (8.153–157), and Euryalus answers him:

{{quote:31663da5-457a-51e2-acbc-f95dda7c5ee7}}

For νείκεσέ τʼ ἄντην, “and rebuked him to his face”, the scholia record a reading φώνησέν τε (see the translation's note on 8.158). The repeated negative points to the single word δαήμονι, “skilled”, and γάρ is a sneering reference to the stranger's confession that sorrows are on his mind far more than contests (Merry on 8.159; 8.154). Merry renders: “But to such an one as plies with a many-benched ship, captain of a crew that are traders, and is either in charge of a freight or vigilant over a home-cargo of greedily gotten gains; no athlete's mould is thine” (Merry on 8.161), the τε … καί being disjunctive. φόρτος is the freight that a merchant takes out to exchange for the ὁδαῖα, the cargo he means to bring home, as the Phoenicians of Book 15 spend a whole year at the island of Syria gathering their cargo (15.403, 15.415–457, Greek only; Merry on 8.162–164; see [[trade]], [[kerdos]], [[prexis]] and the translation's note on 8.161–164). ἀθλητῆρι, “an athlete”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key words).

**The answer.** “Looking at him darkly, Odysseus of many wiles said: / ‘Stranger, you have not spoken well; you are like a reckless man’” (8.165–166; see [[looking-darkly]]): each calls the other ξεῖνε, “stranger” (8.159, 8.166). The gods, he says, do not give all their gracious gifts to all men alike; one man is less in looks, but speaks so that men look on him as a god, “Another again in looks is like the immortals, / but no grace is set as a crown around his words; / so with you too, your looks are outstanding, and not even a god / would fashion them otherwise, but in mind you are worthless” (8.167–177; see [[different-gifts]], [[eidos]], [[charis]] and [[noos]]). On ἀποφώλιος, “worthless”, of uncertain derivation, see the translation's note on 8.177. The asyndeton of “You have stirred the heart in my breast” is significant: Odysseus, carried by his warmth to the limits of the freedom he could allow himself as a stranger, hastens to excuse himself to the company (Merry on 8.178). Then he seizes a heavier discus and throws it beyond all the marks (8.186–198; see [[discus]] and [[odysseus]]). The king, answering the stranger, puts the quarrel in a sentence: “angry because this man stood beside you in the gathering / and rebuked you, in such a way as no mortal would make light of your excellence / who knew in his mind how to speak fitting words” (8.238–240; see [[alcinous]] and the translation's note on 8.237–239).

**The amends.** At sunset the king proposes the guest-gifts, and ends: “And let Euryalus make amends to the man himself with words / and with a gift, since the word he spoke was by no means fair and right” (8.396–397). ἕ αὐτόν refers to Odysseus, “him in his personal feelings”, and the middle ἀρεσσάσθω is “get him to be reconciled to himself” (Merry on 8.396; on Düntzer's and Nauck's αὐτός, see the translation's note on 8.396). The king turns Euryalus' own words back on him: Euryalus had told Laodamas that his word was “fair and right” (μάλα τοῦτο ἔπος κατὰ μοῖραν ἔειπες, 8.141), and the king says that his own “was by no means fair and right” (οὔ τι ἔπος κατὰ μοῖραν ἔειπεν, 8.397; see [[moira]]). He answers in the verse of his first answer (8.400 = 8.140), and with a gift:

{{quote:73af7a18-3ede-53bb-9323-26248ce89cd5}}

πολέος δέ οἱ ἄξιον ἔσται may mean simply that the stranger will find it a thing of price, since men in those days did not hesitate to value presents at their worth, or that the gift will make up for much, for the injury he has suffered (Merry on 8.405; see the translation's note on 8.405, [[sword]], [[silver-studded]] and [[ivory]]). πάτερ ὦ ξεῖνε, “stranger, father”, is the respectful address of the young to an older man (see the translation's key words), with ὦ set between the words, an order for which Merry compares Sophocles and Euripides (on 8.408); for the wish that the storm winds may snatch a hard word away he compares Horace, Apollonius and Theocritus (on 8.409; see [[horace]] and [[apollonius]]). Odysseus returns the blessing in its own words, θεοὶ … δοῖεν, “may the gods grant” (8.410–411, 8.413; see [[olbos]]), and his hope that Euryalus will never miss the sword closes the quarrel with a courtesy: “He spoke, and around his shoulders he slung the silver-studded sword” (8.416). Euryalus is not named again in the poem.

**His epithets.** βροτολοιγῷ ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.115); Ναυβολίδης, “the son of Naubolus” (8.116).
