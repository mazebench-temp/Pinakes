---
summary: The fame of the lay from which Demodocus sings the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles “had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.73–74), and the ball of the dancing princes is flung “toward the shadowy clouds” (8.374).
---

**Fame that reaches the sky.** “The Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men, / from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.73–74; see [[demodocus]], [[song-and-fame]], [[kleos]] and [[wide-sky]]). The phrase is said of the greatest fame: of Nestor's shield, “whose fame now reaches the sky” (*Iliad* 8.192), and of Penelope, whose fame, the beggar tells her, reaches the wide sky (19.107–108). Odysseus says it of himself when at last he gives his name: “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, … and my fame reaches the sky” (9.19–20, a plain rendering). The lay whose fame had reached the sky was a lay about him.

**Toward the clouds.** In the ball dance of Halius and Laodamas, “one of them would fling it toward the shadowy clouds, / bending back, and the other, leaping high from the ground, / would catch it easily before his feet touched the ground” (8.374–376; see [[ball]], [[polybus-the-phaeacian]] and [[shadowy-mountains]]). Eustathius, W. W. Merry reports, gave a name of its own, taken from the sky, to this game, in which the ball is tossed up and caught before the leaper reaches the ground (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.372). The words of the throw return in Odysseus' tale of the underworld, where the wind flings the fruit trees toward the shadowy clouds whenever Tantalus reaches out for their fruit, ῥίπτασκε ποτὶ νέφεα σκιόεντα (11.588–592, a plain rendering).
