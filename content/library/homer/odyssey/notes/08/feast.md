summary: The day of the games opens and closes with a feast in the house of Alcinous. In the morning he sacrifices twelve sheep, eight hogs and two cattle, and Demodocus sings among the banqueters (8.38–99); in the evening, after the bath, the portions are served again, and Odysseus sends the singer a cut of the chine of a white-tusked boar from his own portion (8.469–485). The Phaeacians count the feast first among their pleasures (8.248), and Demodocus' first song is of a quarrel at a feast of the gods (8.76).
# Forms first met here (they apply in the Odyssey only): δαιτυμόνων, "banqueters" (8.66, 8.473; later 15.467,
# 17.605); δαινύῃ, "you feast" (8.243; later 19.328); δορπέομεν, "we have been at supper" (8.539, only here); and
# "νώτου ἀποπροταμών" (8.475), since νώτου alone is a man's back in the Iliad (5.147, 20.279, 21.69).

**A quick feast.** Alcinous bids the crew of the escort ship come up to his house when they have moored her, and “then make ready a quick feast, / coming to our house; and I will provide well for all” (8.38–39; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). θοήν, “quick”, is proleptic, W. W. Merry says, a feast “which shall be soon ready”, and perhaps “hasty meal” comes near it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.38). The kings are to come too, “so that we may make the stranger welcome in the halls” (8.42; see [[xenia]] and [[kings]]).

**The sacrifice.** “And for them Alcinous sacrificed twelve sheep, / and eight white-tusked hogs, and two shambling cattle; / these they flayed and dressed, and made ready a lovely feast” (8.59–61; see [[sacrifice]], [[white-tusked]], [[shambling]] and [[lovely]]). ἀμφί θʼ ἕπον, “dressed”, is the work between the flaying and the table, as ἀμφιέπειν is used of meat and of an ox in the Iliad (Merry on 8.61; *Iliad* 11.776, 18.559). The singer is seated “in the midst of the banqueters”, with his own basket, table and cup (8.66–70; see [[demodocus]], [[throne]], [[basket]], [[table]] and [[cup]]), and the meal is told in the verses of the suitors' meals: “And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them. / But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (8.71–72 = 1.149–150; again 8.484–485; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).

**The lyre, companion of the feast.** When the stranger has wept through the first song, Alcinous ends the meal: “Already we have had our hearts' fill of our equal share of the feast / and of the lyre, which is the companion of the rich feast” (8.98–99; see [[equal-feast]], [[rich-feast]] and [[lyre]]). συνήορος is “linked with”, as the lyre is called the feast's companion, ἑταίρη, in the house of Odysseus (Merry on 8.99; 17.271, Greek only); some wrote ἑταίρῃ for θαλείῃ here, which Merry takes for a gloss on συνήορος drawn from that verse (his critical note; see the translation's note on 8.99). The feast of Demodocus' first song is a feast of the gods: Odysseus and Achilles “contended at a rich feast of the gods” (8.76; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]]).

**The Phaeacian life.** Alcinous puts the feast first among his people's pleasures: “and always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances / and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” (8.248–249; see [[phaeacians]], [[dance]], [[clothing]], [[bathing]] and [[bed]]). He wants the stranger to remember it “when in your own halls / you feast beside your wife and your children” (8.242–243). The gifts are gathered “so that with them in his hands / the stranger may go to supper rejoicing in his heart” (8.394–395), and the bath is heated so that “he may take delight in the feast and in hearing the strains of the song” (8.429; see [[guest-gifts]]).

**The chine.** In the evening the stranger sits down beside the king, and “they were already serving out portions and mixing the wine” (8.469–470; see [[wine]]). Then Odysseus honors the singer from his own portion:

{{quote:26ea00c1-6616-59c0-b202-5d1715ddba4e}}

The chine, νῶτον, had been served to Odysseus as a mark of honor; the clause “and yet more was left over” shows how great it was, and the rich lard on either side how fat, not that the guest still had enough for his supper (Merry on 8.475). The chine is the portion of honor: Agamemnon honors Ajax with the long chine after his duel with Hector (*Iliad* 7.321), Menelaus gives his guests the chine that had been set before him (4.65–66), and Eumaeus will honor the disguised Odysseus with the chine of a white-tusked boar in the words of the Iliad's verse (14.437–438, Greek only; see [[geras]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]). Here the guest so honored passes the honor on to the singer, “for among all men upon the earth singers / have their share of honor and respect” (8.479–480; see [[singers]] and [[time]]).

**Supper interrupted.** The supper ends as the morning's meal did, with the stranger's tears. “Ever since we have been at supper and the divine singer was roused to sing, / from then on he has never ceased from miserable lamentation”, Alcinous tells the Phaeacians, and stops the song “so that we may all take delight alike, / hosts and guest” (8.539–543; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).
