---
summary: Athena takes two disguises in the book and is known in neither. In the likeness of Alcinous' herald she goes through the city and summons the leaders to the place of assembly (8.7–15); and “in form like a man” she sets the mark of the stranger's discus and tells him that none of the Phaeacians will reach it (8.193–198), “and much-enduring brilliant Odysseus was glad, / rejoicing that he saw a gentle companion in the gathering” (8.199–200).

**The herald.** The goddess speaks as a herald speaks, standing beside each man, but the message is her own plan: the stranger has come “after wandering over the sea, like the immortals in form” (8.14), and she is “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (8.9). Her grace follows, poured on his head and shoulders as the people gaze (8.17–20; [[heralds]], [[exhortation]], [[gold-poured-around-silver]]).

**The man at the mark.** The second disguise is told in the half-verse of her coming to Odysseus on Ithaca as a young shepherd, ἀνδρὶ δέμας ἐικυῖα (8.194; 13.222). She sets the mark, as Achilles points out the turning post for the chariot race (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.193, comparing *Iliad* 23.358), and calls the stranger by name with a word of courage: “Even a blind man, stranger, could sort out your mark” (8.195; [[called-him-by-name]]). Odysseus is glad of her as of a man: ἑταῖρον ἐνηέα, “a gentle companion,” is said in the Iliad of Patroclus (*Iliad* 17.204, 21.96), and Merry notes that the word is always used of close friends (Merry on 8.200). Whether he knew her the poem does not say; heartened, “he spoke more lightly among the Phaeacians” and challenged them all (8.201–233; [[athena]], [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).
