**The bath.** When the gifts have been brought in at sunset, Alcinous bids the queen bring a chest and put in it a mantle and a tunic, “And warm a cauldron on the fire for him, and heat water” (8.424–426; see [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The imperatives are plural, for the queen and her women. ἀμφί goes with πυρί, “warm for him a cauldron over the fire”, and refers to the legs of the tripod that bestride the fire (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.426). “So he spoke, and Arete told her slave women / to set a great tripod over the fire with all speed. / And they set the tripod for the bath on the blazing fire, / and poured water into it, and took wood and kindled it beneath” (8.433–436; see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[bathing]]), verses built on those with which the companions of Achilles heat the water to wash the body of Patroclus (*Iliad* 18.344–348; 8.437 = *Iliad* 18.348). While the water heats, the queen packs the chest herself (8.438–445).

“And straightway the housekeeper bade him bathe, / stepping into the bathtub” (8.449–450): αὐτόδιον is “straightway”, like the Latin *e vestigio* (Merry on 8.449). The women serve him in the verse with which the slave women of Menelaus bathe Telemachus and Peisistratus at Sparta (4.49; again 17.88), and with which, in the Iliad, Achilles' slave women wash the body of Hector for his father (*Iliad* 24.587): “And when the slave women had washed him and anointed him with oil, / and put a fine cloak and a tunic around him, / he stepped out of the bathtub and went among the men / drinking wine” (8.454–457; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]). He had not often been cared for so since he left Calypso (8.450–453; see [[odysseus]] and [[calypso]]). The housekeeper's word and the slave women's hands make the last service of the house's women to him in the book; Nausicaa is waiting at the doorpost (8.457–468; see [[nausicaa]]).
