---
summary: The man without a name is ξεῖνος, “stranger”, from Athena's first words in the book to Alcinous' last, and on the second day he becomes the guest of the house. Book 8 shows the bond from both sides: the host the stranger will not fight (8.208–211), the guest-gift “as is fitting” (8.389), the farewell supper “for the sake of the revered stranger” and the maxim that a stranger and a suppliant are as good as a brother (8.543–547), and the last questions, which peoples are savage and which “kind to strangers” (8.575–576).

**The stranger.** Athena, in the likeness of the herald, calls the Phaeacian leaders to the assembly “so that you may learn about the stranger” (ξείνοιο, 8.12, “about the stranger”: W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.12), and Alcinous presents him without a name: “This stranger, I do not know who he is, has come to my house in his wanderings” (8.28). The king proposes the escort and a feast, “so that we may make the stranger welcome in the halls” (ξεῖνον … φιλέωμεν, 8.42; see [[philos]] and [[pompe]]). At the games the young men address him as ξεῖνε, Laodamas with respect, “stranger, father” (8.145; see [[pater]]), Euryalus with contempt, “No, stranger” (8.159), and Odysseus turns the word back on Euryalus, “Stranger, you have not spoken well” (ξεῖνʼ, 8.166). Even Athena, in the form of a man, calls him so when she marks his throw (8.195).

**The host.** The stranger challenges all the Phaeacians but Laodamas: “For he is my host; who would fight with one who makes him welcome?” (8.208). ξεῖνος is here the host, as ξεινοδόκος is in the next verses, “who brings on strife in contests with his host / in a foreign land” (8.210–211; see the translation's table of key words and [[strife-with-ones-host]]). ξεινοδόκος is the word of Menelaus' prayer in the Iliad, that later men may shudder “to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (*Iliad* 3.351–354; see [[paris]]).

**The guest-gift.** When the stranger has praised the dancers, Alcinous finds him “a very wise man” and proposes the gift that makes a stranger a guest: “But come, let us give him a guest-gift, as is fitting” (ξεινήιον, 8.388–389; see [[guest-gifts]]). Each of the twelve kings and the king himself gives a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold, Euryalus a sword with his apology, and the king his own golden goblet (8.390–432; see [[doron]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). At sunset the gifts are in the house, and the queen lays them in a chest for the voyage (8.417–448; see [[chest]]).

**Hosts and guest.** At the evening feast Alcinous stops the song that makes his guest weep, so that they may all take delight alike, “hosts and guest” (ξεινοδόκοι καὶ ξεῖνος, 8.543), for everything has been made ready “for the sake of the revered stranger, … the escort and the gifts of friendship” (8.544–545; see [[aidos]]). τάδε, “all this”, is what is before them, the escort and the gifts; the farewell supper at which the keepsakes are presented is, as it were, the first act of the escort (Merry on 8.544). Then the maxim, “As good as a brother are a stranger and a suppliant / to a man who has even a little hold on wisdom” (8.546–547; see [[as-good-as-a-brother]] and [[supplication]]), and the request it grounds: let the stranger tell his name (8.548–554; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]).

**Kind to strangers.** Alcinous' last questions turn the rule into a test of peoples: let the stranger tell where his wanderings took him, “both those who are harsh and savage and unjust, / and those who are kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (8.575–576; Merry on 8.575; see [[themis]] and [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). They echo his own question when he woke on Scheria (6.119–121), where the savage are “arrogant” (6.120) rather than “harsh”, and he will ask it again setting out for the Cyclopes and waking on Ithaca (9.175–176, 13.200–202). The tale that follows is the answer (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The Phaeacians whom Athena said did not much put up with strangers (7.32–33) have given him everything; a later critic, Heracleides of Pontus, as the scholia report him, held their hospitality and their quick escorts to be a pretense, kept up by men who loved their ease and feared that some stranger might drive them from their land (Merry on 8.244; see [[phaeacians]]).
