summary: The book of the singer is also the book of fame carried home. The Phaeacians want the stranger to tell his friends of their prowess (8.101–103, 8.241–253); he promises to tell all men of the singer's gift (8.496–498); and Alcinous tells him that the gods wrought the fate of Ilium “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580). The war is sung before a man who lived it.

**Song for those yet to be.** Alcinous' words answer Helen's in the Iliad, that Zeus set an evil fate on her and Paris “so that hereafter / we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (*Iliad* 6.357–358; see the entry and [[helen]]). Here the whole war is the gods' work, and the song its purpose: “This the gods wrought, and they spun destruction / for men” (8.579–580), with ἐπεκλώσαντο, the verb of spinning that gives the Spinners their name, the κλῶθες who with fate spun the stranger's lot at his birth, in Alcinous' words of Book 7 (7.197–198; see [[fates]] and [[aisa]]). The listener is the man who suffered what is sung, and the king does not yet know it (see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[tears]]).

**Fame carried home.** The Phaeacians want their own fame carried. Alcinous proposes the games “so that the stranger may tell his friends, / when he has returned home, how far we surpass others” in boxing, wrestling, jumping and running (8.101–103), and the dancing, so that he may tell of their seafaring, running, dancing and song (8.251–253); he asks him to tell their excellence “to another of the heroes too” at home (8.241–245; see [[arete]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]). The stranger promises the singer the same service: if he sings the horse fairly, “at once I will tell all men / how the god has readily granted you wondrous song” (8.496–498; see [[singers]] and [[demodocus]]).

**The singer's matter.** The singer sings κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men”, the matter that Achilles sang to the lyre in his hut (8.73; *Iliad* 9.189; see [[kleos]]), and the fame of the lay itself “had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.74; see [[oime]]). Its subjects are the quarrel at the beginning of the war and the horse at its end (8.75–82, 8.492–520; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.492), and the song of Ares and Aphrodite sung between them is the gods' (8.266–366; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]).
