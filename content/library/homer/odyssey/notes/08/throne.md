summary: The herald sets a silver-studded chair for the blind singer in the midst of the banqueters, against a tall pillar (8.65–66, 8.472–473); in the evening Alcinous leads the Phaeacian leaders to the high chairs of his hall, and Odysseus, fresh from the bath, sits down on a chair beside the king (8.421–422, 8.469).
# θρόνοισι (8.422), first met here; θρόνον (8.65, 8.469) is in the Book 1 note.
grc: [θρόνοισι]
---

**The singer's chair.** “And Pontonous set for him a silver-studded chair / in the midst of the banqueters, leaning it against a tall pillar” (8.65–66; see [[pontonous]], [[demodocus]], [[silver-studded]] and [[house]]). A silver-studded chair is what the old Echeneus asked for the suppliant the evening before (7.162; see the Book 7 note, and [[echeneus]]), and Arete too sits at her hearth “leaning against a pillar” (6.307). When the singer is brought in again in the evening, it is he who is leaned against the pillar, ἐρείσας now of the man, not the chair (8.472–473; see the translation's row μέσσῳ δαιτυμόνων).

**The high chairs.** At sunset the gifts are carried to the house, and “the sacred might of Alcinous led the way for them, / and they came and sat down on the high chairs” (8.421–422; see [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]] and [[the-house-of-alcinous]]): while the queen and her sons see to the gifts, W. W. Merry explains, Alcinous leads the stranger and the Phaeacian leaders to the seats of honor in the house (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.421). After the bath and Nausicaa's farewell Odysseus “sat down on a chair beside King Alcinous” (8.469; see [[alcinous]]), the seat the king gave him when he raised him from the hearth (7.169–171).
