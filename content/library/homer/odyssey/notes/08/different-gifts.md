summary: Odysseus answers Euryalus' taunt with the thought of Polydamas' maxim: “So true is it that the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all / men alike, neither stature nor mind nor eloquence” (8.167–168). One man is less in looks, but a god crowns his words with beauty; another, like Euryalus, is like the immortals in looks and worthless in mind. Alcinous answers with the gifts of his own people: not boxing or wrestling, but running and ships, the feast, the lyre and the dances (8.244–249).

**The answer to Euryalus.** Euryalus has likened the stranger to a merchant captain mindful of his cargo, and not to an athlete (8.158–164; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[trade]]). Odysseus looks at him darkly, tells him that he is like a reckless man (8.165–166; see [[atasthalie]]), and gives the maxim. οὕτως is “so true is it that”, like the Latin *adeo*. χαρίεντα can be taken as a predicate with each noun, “the gods give not in perfection to all men either form or mind or eloquence”, but Merry, comparing *Iliad* 4.320 and 13.729–734, takes the general sense to be that the gods do not give all their gifts to all men (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.167; see [[all-things-at-once]]). The gifts are φυή, “stature”, φρένες, “mind”, and ἀγορητύς, “eloquence” (8.168; see [[phrenes]]). Then come two portraits. The first man is less in looks, “but a god crowns his words with beauty” (μορφὴν ἔπεσι στέφει): μορφήν is a quasi-cognate accusative and ἔπεσι the remoter object, “puts a crown of grace upon his words”, as the passive of the second portrait shows, χάρις ἀμφιπεριστέφεται ἐπέεσσιν (8.175; Merry on 8.170; see the translation's note on 8.170 and [[charis]]). Men look on him with delight, “and he speaks surely, / with soothing modesty, and stands out among those gathered, / and as he goes through the city they look on him as a god” (8.171–173). The second man is like the immortals in looks, “but no grace is set as a crown around his words”, and so is Euryalus: not even a god would make his looks otherwise, “but in mind you are worthless” (8.174–177; see [[eidos]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]). ἄλλως … τεύξειε is “change it” for the better (Merry on 8.176); of ἀποφώλιος, “worthless”, Merry says that the derivation is most uncertain (on 8.177; see the translation's note on 8.177).

**The king whom the Muses honor.** The portrait of the speaker is Hesiod's portrait of a king. The Muses pour sweet dew on the tongue of the king they honor at his birth; the people look to him as he gives judgment, and “speaking surely” (ἀσφαλέως ἀγορεύων) he quickly ends even a great quarrel; “as he goes through the gathering they greet him like a god / with gentle reverence, and he stands out among those gathered” (*Theogony* 81–92, a plain rendering), verses that the Odyssey shares nearly word for word; “such is the holy gift of the Muses to men” (*Theogony* 93). From the parallel Merry joins αἰδοῖ μειλιχίῃ to the onlookers, “with submissive reverence”; the translation joins it to the speaker, “with soothing modesty” (Merry on 8.170–172; see the translation's note on 8.171–172 and [[aidos]]). Of ἀσφαλέως ἀγορεύει, “his address goes surely on to its point”, Merry quotes Xenophon's report that Socrates said Homer gave Odysseus the name of a sure speaker, since he could lead his argument through what men accept (*Memorabilia* 4.6.15, as Merry quotes it): a sureness “better than fluency” (Merry on 8.171).

**The speaker himself.** The first portrait is the speaker's own. Antenor remembered Odysseus at Troy, standing to speak like a man who knows nothing, until the words came “like the snowflakes of winter”: “then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks” (*Iliad* 3.216–224). When the stranger pauses in his tale, Alcinous will say what Euryalus could not see, with the verb of Euryalus' taunt (ἐίσκομεν, as ἐίσκω, 8.159): “we do not liken you to a cheat … you have grace of words, and a noble mind within” (σοὶ δʼ ἔπι μὲν μορφὴ ἐπέων, ἔνι δὲ φρένες ἐσθλαί, 11.363–367, a plain rendering), the verse Merry sets beside this one (on 8.170). The answer ends with a check on himself: “You have stirred the heart in my breast / by speaking in no seemly way” (8.178–179), where the asyndeton shows his haste to make amends for his own warmth (Merry on 8.178). Then he throws the discus (8.186–198; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The Phaeacians' share.** Alcinous grants the stranger's excellence and answers with his people's share of gifts, “the kind of deeds that on us too / Zeus lays, continually, from the days of our fathers” (8.244–245):

{{quote:257154be-b9d9-5029-8a0e-28e5d3d66cb8}}

ἀμύμονες here is “flawless”, of men at a skill (see the translation's table of key words and [[blameless]]). Merry sets beside the passage Odysseus' words in his false tale, that ships and wars were dear to him, the things a god put in his mind, for different men delight in different works (14.227–228, a plain rendering; Merry on 8.244). The scholia read the lines as a picture of the Phaeacians' peaceful life; Merry finds a color of luxury in them, a common theme of the Sophists, and quotes the scholia's report of Heracleides of Pontus, who judged the Phaeacians' hospitality and quick escort a pretense of selfish men afraid of losing their land, and Horace's “pinguis Phaeaxque” (*Epistles* 1.15.24, as Merry quotes it; on 8.244; see [[phaeacians]] and [[pompe]]). Two of the gifts in Polydamas' list are theirs, the dances and the lyre (*Iliad* 13.731; see [[dance]] and [[lyre]]), and the king's next words call for the dancers and the singer (8.250–255).
