**The land of the Trojans.** Boasting of his archery, Odysseus allows one better: “Philoctetes alone surpassed me with the bow / in the land of the Trojans, when we Achaeans shot with the bow” (8.219–220; see [[philoctetes]] and [[trojans]]). δήμῳ ἔνι Τρώων, “in the land of the Trojans”, opens the verse in which the Odyssey remembers the war, as in Telemachus' words to Nestor, “where you Achaeans suffered woes” (3.100; again 3.220, 4.243, 4.330, and in later books 13.266, 24.27, 24.31).

**The citadel.** Odysseus asks Demodocus to sing of the horse “which once brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a snare, / having filled it with the men who sacked Ilios” (8.494–495; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). ἀκρόπολις, “citadel”, is found in Homer only in this song (8.494, 8.504); the Iliad speaks of the “high city”, πόλις ἄκρη, where Athena's temple and the houses of Priam and Hector stood and where the Trojans held an assembly beside Priam's doors (*Iliad* 6.88, 6.317, 7.345), and of Pergamus, Apollo's height (see [[pergamus]]). In the song the Trojans have dragged the horse into the citadel themselves, and the men inside it sit “in the place of assembly of the Trojans” (8.503–504; see [[assembly]]). One of their three counsels is “to drag it to the top of the height and throw it down the rocks” (8.508): the Acropolis of Ilium, Merry explains, which he calls Pergamos, presented a rocky escarpment on the side of the Scamander, and he follows a scholium that takes ἐπʼ ἄκρης as “on the crags”, the highest point of the ridge that was in his day called Bunarbaschi (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.508; see [[scamander]]).

**The city sacked.** The third counsel prevailed, “for it was fated that the city should perish once it had enfolded / the great wooden horse” (8.511–512): the subjunctive, where the optative might be expected, keeps as nearly as possible the wording of the oracle (Merry on 8.511; see [[aisa]]). Then the singer tells “how the sons of the Achaeans sacked the city, / pouring out from the horse”, and “how each in his own place ravaged the steep city” (8.514–516; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and [[steep]]). πόλιν αἰπήν, “the steep city”, is the Iliad's phrase in Menelaus' threat to the Trojans, that Zeus, the god of guests, “will one day destroy your steep city” (*Iliad* 13.625).

**Ilios.** The name is the city's in the song, “the men who sacked Ilios” (8.495), and in Alcinous' last questions: why does the stranger weep “as you hear the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios”, a fate the gods wrought “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.578–580; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[argives]]), and did some kinsman by marriage “perish before Ilios” (8.581; see [[in-laws]])? Ἰλιόθι πρό, “before Ilios”, is a verse-end of the Iliad, of the Trojans' fires on the plain and of the Achaean army that Zeus did not wish to perish there (*Iliad* 8.561, 10.12, 13.349). On Ἴλιον Merry reports the view of Oscar Meyer, who connected the name with a Sanskrit word for a fortress, used in the Vedas of a citadel of the sky surrounded by clouds, and so made the war at Troy one of many ways of telling the great sun myth, the struggle of the powers of light with those of darkness (on 8.495).
