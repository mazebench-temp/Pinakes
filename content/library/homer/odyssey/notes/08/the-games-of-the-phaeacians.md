**The many contests.** Athena's grace was poured on Odysseus so that he “might accomplish the many contests / in which the Phaeacians made trial of Odysseus” (8.22–23; see [[athena]]). Zenodotus athetized the second verse, “for he did not accomplish many contests in Phaeacia, but only threw the discus” (the scholia, in W. W. Merry's critical note, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.23; see the translation's note on 8.22–23). Eustathius took the verb of capacity rather than performance: the Phaeacians gave way to Odysseus in the contests in which he said he excelled, and so in a way he accomplished them all; Crates, as Nitzsch reports, made the “contests” the full tale of his past sufferings, which Merry finds disproved by the contest of the bow, τόξου πειρώμεσθα καὶ ἐκτελέωμεν ἄεθλον (21.180; Merry on 8.22). Merry's own answer is that the one contest discharged him in full of all the contests to which he might else have been challenged, and that for all of them the care of Athena had fitted him (on 8.22).

**Five contests in a dozen verses.** ἀέθλια are “the games”, the tests of prowess, as the bow and the axes of Ithaca will be ἀέθλια καὶ φόνου ἀρχήν, “the games and the beginning of slaughter” (21.4, Greek only; Merry on 8.108; see [[contests]]). Alcinous names four contests in one verse, πύξ τε παλαιμοσύνῃ τε καὶ ἅλμασιν ἠδὲ πόδεσσιν (8.103; see the translation's note on 8.103), and the young men add the discus (8.129; see [[discus]]). Only the footrace is told at any length. ἀπὸ νύσσης is “from the starting line”, and τέτατο δρόμος, “the running was strained to the full”, is the pace, not the course (Merry on 8.121; see [[turning-post]]); Clytoneus' lead, “the furrow-length of a pair of mules in the fallow”, is the distance that Dolon had run ahead of Odysseus and Diomedes when they ran after him in the Iliad (8.124; *Iliad* 10.351–354; Merry on 8.124; see [[mules]] and [[clytoneus]]). The winners are named in the order of the contests, and the king's son, the best in looks, wins the boxing, the contest the Phaeacians will soon concede (8.130, 8.246; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]).

**The invitation.** “and it is fitting that you know contests” (8.146): “This is the primitive praise of γυμναστική” (Merry on 8.146). Laodamas speaks courteously, ξεῖνε πάτερ, “stranger, father” (8.145), and Odysseus still hears a taunt in it, κερτομέοντες, “taunting me” (8.153). Euryalus' agreement, “Now go yourself and challenge him, and make your word known” (8.142), was a verse that neither Aristarchus nor Zenodotus nor Aristophanes knew (see the translation's note on 8.142).

**The taunt and the answer.** Euryalus' γάρ, Merry thinks, sneers at the stranger's own confession that sorrows weigh on him more than contests (on 8.159; 8.154), and he renders the rest “But to such an one as plies with a many-benched ship, captain of a crew that are traders” (on 8.161; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[trade]]). Odysseus' answer begins with a saying on the gods' gifts and ends in an apology: the asyndeton of ὤρινάς μοι θυμόν, “You have stirred the heart in my breast”, shows a stranger who has gone to the limit of the freedom he can allow himself and hastens to excuse himself to the company (8.178; Merry on 8.178; see [[different-gifts]]). His claim to have been “among the foremost” is in the past, ἔμμεναι beside the pluperfect πεποίθεα (8.180–181; Merry on 8.181; see the translation's note on 8.180–181).

**The throw.** He throws αὐτῷ φάρει, “with his mantle still on” (8.186; Merry, after Monro). The discus was of stone, and the Phaeacians had already thrown theirs, Elatreus winning; Odysseus chose one greater than those lying by it and heavier than those they had thrown (Merry on 8.187; see the translation's note on 8.187). The σήματα that it overflies were probably pegs stuck in the ground where each discus fell, the discus itself being picked up again, and the ὅμιλος with which his mark is not mingled is the cluster of their marks, close together because the Phaeacians were evenly matched (Merry on 8.192, 8.196; see the translation's note on 8.192–193). ἔθηκε, “set the mark”, is explained by the verse in which Achilles “pointed out the turning post” for the chariot race, σήμηνε δὲ τέρματʼ (*Iliad* 23.358; Merry on 8.193), and her “Even a blind man … could sort out your mark” is said as Nausicaa said that even a child could lead him to her father's house (8.195; 6.300; Merry on 8.195).

**The challenge.** κουφότερον, “more lightly”: his face had been anxious, and now, Merry says, he challenges them gaily to beat him in a second throw (8.201; Merry on 8.201). He excepts Laodamas because Laodamas is his host (8.207–211); of the five sons of Alcinous, three entered the games, probably, Merry thinks, the three unmarried ones (on 8.204; 6.62–63). ἢ καὶ ποσίν, “or even in running”, is a reservation that he explains at the end, when he admits that the sea has loosened his limbs (8.206, 8.230–233; Merry on 8.206; see the translation's note on 8.232–233).

**The concession.** Alcinous gives up the boxing and the wrestling and keeps the running, the ships, and the pleasures of peace (8.246–249; see [[alcinous]] and [[phaeacians]]). The scholia and Nitzsch read in the list a people devoted to peace; Merry finds in it “a certain colour of luxury”, a common theme of the Sophists, and reports the harsher judgment of Heracleides of Pontus, quoted in the scholia, for whom the Phaeacians' kindness to strangers and their speed in sending them away were the policy of a people conscious of their love of pleasure and afraid of being driven from their land (Merry on 8.244). The dancers' show, in the verses of the first proposal, has nine stewards to level the ground, αἰσυμνῆται, “not exactly umpires” but public officers who regulated the games (8.258–260; Merry on 8.258; see [[dance]]).
