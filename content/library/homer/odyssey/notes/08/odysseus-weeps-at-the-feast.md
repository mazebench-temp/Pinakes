summary: The book's two weepings frame its day. At the morning feast Odysseus hides his face in his mantle whenever Demodocus sings of the quarrel with Achilles (8.83–95); at supper, hearing the song of the horse that he asked for, he melts in tears like a woman over her husband fallen before his city (8.521–534). Both times Alcinous alone notices, and the second time he stops the song and asks the stranger who he is and why he weeps (8.535–586).

**The mantle.** “but Odysseus / took his great purple mantle in his strong hands / and drew it down over his head, and covered his handsome face; / for he was ashamed before the Phaeacians, letting tears fall from beneath his brows” (8.83–86; see [[purple]], [[cloak]] and [[aidos]]). The φᾶρος, W. W. Merry explains, was a square cloth worn over the left arm and shoulder, with a long corner hanging from the right shoulder, and it is this corner that Odysseus throws over his head from behind (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.85; see the translation's note on 8.85). The optatives that follow are iterative: each time the singer stops, Odysseus wipes his tears, takes the mantle from his head and pours a libation from the two-handled cup, and each time the singer begins again he covers his head and laments (8.87–92). Merry sees in the libation at every pause a thank-offering to the gods for their constant care of him (on 8.87; see the translation's note on 8.87–92 and [[libation]]).

**Alcinous alone.** “Then he escaped the notice of all the others as he let tears fall, / but Alcinous alone noticed him and observed him, / sitting near him, and heard him groaning heavily” (8.93–95 = 8.532–534; see [[alcinous]] and [[shedding-tears]]). The first time the king says nothing of the tears: the company has had its fill of the feast “and of the lyre”, and they will go out to the games (8.97–103; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). The guest's tears are covered by the host's tact, as they were by the guest's mantle.

**Melting.** At the third song “Odysseus / was melting, and tears wet his cheeks beneath his eyelids” (8.521–522). τήκετο, “was melting”, is the verb with which the poem will tell of Penelope's tears in the nineteenth book, when she listens to her disguised husband's false tale of Odysseus and her cheeks melt as snow melts on the high mountains (19.204–208, Greek only; see [[penelope]]).

**The simile.** The weeping man is likened to a woman who throws herself on her husband as he dies before his city, “keeping off the pitiless day from his town and his children”, and is driven off into slavery with spears at her back (8.523–531; see [[the-woman-weeping-over-her-husband]] and [[pity]]). The song he wept at has just told how he went “like Ares” to the house of Deiphobus in the sack of Troy (8.517–520); in the simile the sacker of cities weeps the tears of the city's captive women. εἴρερον, “slavery”, is found only here in Homer, and Merry thinks the customs described more barbarous than Homer's elsewhere, so that the passage is “not free from suspicion” (on 8.529; see the translation's note on 8.529). The “pitiful tear” of 8.531 springs from feeling, as Thersites' tears of pain would not (Merry on 8.530).

**The song stopped.** Alcinous now names what he saw: “let Demodocus now stop his clear-toned lyre; / for it is not to all of us that he gives delight by singing this”; ever since supper the stranger “has never ceased from miserable lamentation … surely grief has beset his mind” (8.536–541; see [[lament]] and [[achos]]). The feast was made for the stranger, and host and guest should take delight alike (8.542–545; see [[xenia]] and [[pompe]]).

**The last question.** After the name, the land and the wanderings, Alcinous asks the question of the tears: “And tell me why you weep and mourn within your heart / as you hear the fate of the Argive Danaans and of Ilios” (8.577–578). The gods wrought that fate “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580; see [[song-and-fame]]), and the king guesses at a reason: a kinsman by marriage who fell before Ilios, a son-in-law or a father-in-law, or a companion “in no way inferior to a brother” (8.581–586; see [[in-laws]], [[hetairos]] and [[a-companion-no-worse-than-a-brother]]). He does not guess that the stranger is one of the men of the song. The book ends on the question, and the next begins with the answer, in which the stranger gives his name (9.2–20, Greek only; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).
