---
summary: Cicero takes the songs sung at the feasts of the Phaeacians for proof that there were poets before Homer, and turns the tradition of Homer's blindness to his praise; Merry's note on Arete's “See to the lid” cites one of his letters.
---

- **Poets before Homer.** Nothing is invented and perfected at once, Cicero says in the *Brutus*, and there is no doubt that there were poets before Homer, “as can be understood from the songs which in his poems are sung at the feasts of the Phaeacians and of the suitors” (*Brutus* 71, a plain rendering). The songs of the Phaeacian feasts are Demodocus' (8.73–82, 8.266–366, 8.499–520), those of the suitors Phemius' (1.325–327): [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]], [[demodocus]], [[phemius]] and [[singers]].
- **The blind painter.** Arguing that the wise man can be happy though blind, as Democritus was, Cicero adds: “It has come down to us that Homer was blind; but it is his painting, not his poetry, that we see” (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.114, a plain rendering), so vividly has he made us see what he did not see himself. The poems' own blind singer is Demodocus: “she robbed him of his eyes, but gave him sweet song” (8.64): [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]] and [[homer]].
- **“See to the lid.”** For Arete's αὐτὸς νῦν ἴδε πῶμα, “See to the lid yourself now” (8.443), Merry cites Theocritus and a letter of Cicero, in which Pomponia complains that she is a guest in her own house because Statius had gone ahead *ut prandium nobis videret*, “to see to our lunch” (*Letters to Atticus* 5.1.3, a plain rendering; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.443): [[chest]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]].
