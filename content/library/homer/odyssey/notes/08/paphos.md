# "There" (8.364): at Paphos.
refs: [8.364]
---

**Home to Paphos.** The lovers part as soon as they are loosed: “he went his way to Thrace, / and she, laughter-loving Aphrodite, came to Cyprus, / to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar” (8.361–363; see [[thrace]], [[cyprus]] and [[laughter-loving]]). The dual participle ἀναΐξαντε, “sprang up”, is followed by a distribution of the subject, ὁ μέν … ἡ δʼ ἄρα, “he … and she”, as in the dog and the fawn on Odysseus' brooch (19.230), and without a participle in the two springs of Alcinous' garden, “one … and the other” (7.129–130; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.361). Each goes to a place of its own. Thrace is the land of Ares, from which he and his son Rout arm themselves for war in an Iliad simile (*Iliad* 13.301; see [[ares]]); Paphos is the goddess's sanctuary. The verse is built on the Iliad's verse for Zeus coming to Ida, “to Gargarus, where he has a precinct and a fragrant altar” (*Iliad* 8.48; see [[gargarus]], [[fragrant]] and [[altar]]): a god's arrival at a holy place of his own. Merry compares Virgil's Venus, who goes away to Paphos, to her temple and her hundred altars (on 8.363; *Aeneid* 1.415–417; see [[virgil]]).

**The Graces.** “There the Graces washed her and anointed her with oil / immortal, such as is upon the gods who live forever, / and they clothed her in lovely garments, a wonder to see” (8.364–366; see [[graces]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). Merry renders the verse “[such divine splendors] as deck the everliving gods”, taking οἷα with the idea of the emphatic ἀμβρότῳ, “immortal”; others make the oil the subject of ἐπενήνοθεν, “is shed over” the gods (Merry on 8.365; see the translation's note on 8.365). The Homeric *Hymn to Aphrodite* has the verses of Paphos and the Graces almost word for word, with θυώδης for θυήεις in the verse of the altar, when the goddess goes there to make herself beautiful before she seeks out Anchises (*Hymn* 5.58–63). In the Odyssey the bath and the oil end the song, and the mortal audience delights in it with Odysseus (8.367–369; see [[demodocus]] and [[phaeacians]]).
