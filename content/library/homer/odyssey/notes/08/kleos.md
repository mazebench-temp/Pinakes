summary: The Muse sets the singer on to sing “the famous deeds of men”, “from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.73–74); and Laodamas tells the stranger that “there is no greater fame for a man, as long as he lives, / than what he achieves with his feet and with his hands” (8.147–148).
# κλέα (8.73; the Iliad's κλέα ἀνδρῶν, 9.189, 9.524, are in its notes).
grc: [κλέα]
en: [the famous deeds of men]
---

The book's fame is the fame of song and of games. κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men”, are what Achilles sang to the lyre in his hut and what Phoenix heard told of the heroes of old (*Iliad* 9.189, 9.524); in the Odyssey they are the professional singer's matter (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.73; see [[singers]] and [[song-and-fame]]). The lay Demodocus sings from had its own fame, which “had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.74; see [[oime]]), the height the stranger will claim for his own fame when he gives his name (9.20; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]). Laodamas offers another way to fame, the games: no greater fame for a man in his lifetime than what he achieves with feet and hands (8.147–148; see [[no-greater-fame]]).
