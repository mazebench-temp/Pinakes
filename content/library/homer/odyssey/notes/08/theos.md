summary: The gods are the givers of the book: the god has given Demodocus song (8.44, 8.498), the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all, and a god crowns a man's words with beauty (8.167–177); they wrought the fate of Ilium so that there might be song (8.579–580). In the song the gods come to see Hephaestus' snare and laugh, “But the goddesses, being female, stayed each at home for shame” (8.324).
# θεαὶ (8.324; again 12.131), θέαιναι (8.341).
grc: [θεαὶ, θέαιναι]
en: [goddesses]
---

**The gods' gifts.** The singer has his song from a god: “to him above all the god has given song” (8.44), and the stranger will tell all men “how the god has readily granted you wondrous song” (8.498; see [[singers]]). The gods give looks, mind and eloquence, but not all to one man, and a god crowns one man's words with beauty, while not even a god would make Euryalus' looks otherwise (8.167–177; see [[different-gifts]]). The fate of Troy, too, is their work: “This the gods wrought, and they spun destruction / for men, so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580; see [[song-and-fame]]). Men are honored “as a god” (θεὸν ὥς): the speaker the people look on as a god (8.173), the girl to whom the stranger will pray as to a god (8.467; see [[euchomai]]).

**The gods in the doorway.** In the song the gods come to see Hephaestus' snare, and the goddesses stay away: θηλύτεραι δὲ θεαὶ μένον αἰδοῖ οἴκοι ἑκάστη (8.324; see [[aidos]] and [[olympians]]). Hermes would gladly lie in the bonds “and you gods look on, and all the goddesses too” (θεοὶ πᾶσαί τε θέαιναι, 8.341; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]). The gods are “the givers of blessings” who stand in the doorway (8.325; see the translation's table of key words).
