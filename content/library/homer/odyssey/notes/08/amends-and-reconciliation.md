The young man gives the stranger a sword of bronze with a silver hilt and an ivory sheath, and wishes that the storm winds may carry off any terrible word; the stranger blesses him in return (8.400–415).

**The king's ruling.** When the kings have agreed to give the stranger a guest-gift, Alcinous adds a ruling of his own: “And let Euryalus make amends to the man himself with words / and with a gift, since the word he spoke was by no means fair and right” (8.396–397; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]). The emphasis is on αὐτόν, the man in his personal feelings, and the middle ἀρεσσάσθω is “get him to be reconciled to himself” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.396); οὔ τι ἔπος is “the word he spoke was by no means right” (Merry on 8.397). The amends are made in the Iliad's way, with gifts and words: Nestor proposed to win Achilles over “with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (*Iliad* 9.112–113; see the entry).

**The sword and the words.** Euryalus gives “this sword, all of bronze, which has a hilt / of silver, and a sheath of newly sawn ivory” (8.403–405; see [[sword]] and [[ivory]]), as Hector gave Ajax a silver-studded sword when their duel ended in friendship (*Iliad* 7.303–304; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). It “will be worth much to him”: either a thing of price, or a gift that makes up for much, for the injury sustained (Merry on 8.405; see the translation's note on 8.405). The words follow: “Farewell, stranger, father; and if any word has been spoken / that was terrible, may the storm winds at once snatch it up and carry it away” (8.408–409). Merry compares Horace's sadness given to the wild winds to carry into the Cretan sea, and Apollonius and Theocritus (on 8.409); in the Iliad Agamemnon takes back his rebuke of Odysseus with the same thought, “and may the gods make it all blow away” (*Iliad* 4.362–363). He ends with a blessing, that the gods may grant the stranger to see his wife and his native land (8.410–411; see [[marriage]]).

**The answer.** The stranger accepts in kind: “And you too, my friend, fare very well, and may the gods grant you prosperity. / And may no longing for this sword ever come upon you hereafter, / which you have given me, making amends with your words” (8.413–415; see [[olbos]] and [[pothe]]), and hangs the sword on his shoulders (8.416). The quarrel that began with a taunt at the games ends before sunset.
