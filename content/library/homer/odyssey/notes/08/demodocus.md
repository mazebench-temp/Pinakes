summary: The singer's book. Alcinous calls for him, and the herald leads him in and seats him against a pillar, with the lyre on a peg above his head (8.43–70); he sings of the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles (8.72–82), of the love of Ares and Aphrodite at the dance (8.256–367), and, at the stranger's asking, of the Wooden Horse (8.487–520). The stranger weeps at the first song and the last; between them he sends the singer a portion of the chine and praises him above all mortals (8.474–498), and the king stops the lyre (8.536–543).

**The singer called.** At the place of assembly Alcinous asks for an escort for the stranger and a feast in his house, and ends: “And call the divine singer, / Demodocus; for to him above all the god has given song, / to give delight in whatever way his heart urges him to sing” (8.43–45; see [[alcinous]] and [[divine]]). τῷ γάρ ῥα is “for he above all men hath from heaven the gift of minstrelsy, to please therewith, on whatever theme his spirit prompts him to sing”, and hence the name, Demodocus, *populo acceptus*, “welcome to the people” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.44; see the entry). “And a herald went to fetch the divine singer” (8.47), μετῴχετο being “went for” (Merry on 8.47; see [[heralds]]).

**The blind singer.** When the sacrifice is made ready, the herald brings him in:

{{quote:acb467d2-f50d-5a80-9fe0-fd067b0d0a44}}

ἐρίηρον, “trusty”, may be rendered “worthy”: the prefix ἐρι- merely strengthens the sense, and this form of the second declension is found only here, at 8.471 and of Phemius (1.346), beside ἐρίηρος (*Iliad* 4.266) and the commoner ἐρίηρες, ἐρίηρας (Merry on 8.62; see [[trusty]] and [[phemius]]). Of ἄμερσε Merry reports Curtius' view that ἀμέρδειν, “deprive”, comes from a root meaning “apportion”, while in the sense “blind” it should be referred to a root meaning “be bright”, and compared with ἀμαυροῦν, “darken” (Merry on 8.64). The Muse who “gave him both good and evil” gives as Zeus gives to men from his two jars (*Iliad* 24.527–533; see [[muses]], [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]); on the tradition that the poet drew himself in the blind singer, see [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]]. The herald is Pontonous, and ἐπέφραδε χερσὶν ἑλέσθαι means that he guided the blind man's hand to the place where the lyre hung (Merry on 8.68; see [[pontonous]], [[lyre]] and the translation's notes on 8.67 and 8.68). The silver-studded chair, the basket, the table and the cup are the singer's share of the feast (see [[throne]], [[silver-studded]], [[basket]], [[table]] and [[cup]]).

**The first song.** When they have had their fill, “the Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men, / from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky, / the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, son of Peleus” (8.73–75; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]], [[oime]], [[kleos]] and the translation's note on 8.74–82). In the Iliad Achilles himself sings “the famous deeds of men” to his lyre (*Iliad* 9.189): these stories, sung by the heroes themselves in the time of the Iliad, are in the Odyssey the property of the professional singer (Merry on 8.73; see [[singers]]). Everything that Phemius sings for the suitors, or Demodocus for the Phaeacians, is taken from the Trojan war, except the lay of Ares and Aphrodite, and the singer began at whatever point of the story his hearers desired (Merry on 1.326). “This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus / took his great purple mantle in his strong hands / and drew it down over his head” (8.83–85), and wept each time the singer began again (8.86–92; see [[odysseus]], [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[famous]]). “Each time the divine singer stopped singing” (8.87) is the iterative optative: at every pause Odysseus wiped his tears and poured a libation (Merry on 8.87). Alcinous alone notices, and turns the company from the feast and “the lyre, which is the companion of the rich feast”, to the games (8.93–103).

**To the games and the dance.** The herald hangs the lyre on its peg again, in the verse of 8.67, takes him by the hand and leads him out by the road the others took, “to marvel at the games” (8.105–108). There, when the stranger's boasts have been answered, the king sends for him: “And let someone go at once and bring Demodocus the clear-toned lyre / that lies somewhere in our house” (8.254–255). The stewards level a dancing floor, “And the herald came near, bringing the clear-toned lyre / for Demodocus; and he then went into the middle; and around him young men / in their first youth took their stand, skilled in dancing” (8.261–263; see [[dance]], [[in-first-youth]] and the translation's key words).

**The second song.** “And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song / about the love of Ares and well-crowned Aphrodite” (8.266–267; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]), and the song is given whole, in the singer's own words, to its end: “This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus / was delighted in his heart as he listened” (8.367–368). Of his three songs that day, Merry observes, the two serious ones, the first and the last, are told only in outline, while of the merry one, in the vein most characteristic of the Phaeacians, the poem gives a specimen; and though the singer was fetched to play for the dancers, as the singer plays at the dance on the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.604–605; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]), the lay is a voluntary contribution added to the pleasure of the day (Merry on 8.267). On its genuineness, see the translation's note on 8.266–366.

**The portion of honor.** At the evening feast “the herald came near, leading the trusty singer, / Demodocus, honored by the people; and he seated him / in the midst of the banqueters, leaning him against a tall pillar” (8.471–473), with the verses of the morning (8.62, 8.66). Odysseus cuts off a portion of the chine of a white-tusked boar, the cut that had been served to him in his honor (Merry on 8.475), and sends it with the herald: “Herald, here, give this meat, so that he may eat, / to Demodocus; and I will greet him, for all my grief. / For among all men upon the earth singers / have their share of honor and respect, because / the Muse has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers” (8.477–481; see [[singers]], [[time]] and [[aidos]]). “That I may do homage to him, though sad at heart” alludes chiefly to the tears the first lay drew from him, and perhaps to the anxiety his delayed departure was causing him, since the king had promised that he should go that very day (Merry on 8.477; 7.317–318). “The herald took it and put it in the hands / of the hero Demodocus; and he received it and rejoiced in his heart” (8.482–483; see [[heros]]).

**The praise.** When they have eaten, Odysseus speaks to him by name:

{{quote:62a32495-919a-531c-a6eb-25c61c092deb}}

“Either the Muse … or Apollo”: Hesiod says that singers and lyre players come from the Muses and far-shooting Apollo (*Theogony* 94–95), but it is not the music that is in question here: the singer was the historian of early times, indebted to Apollo, the god of prophecy who knows the past as well as the future, and to the Muses, the daughters of Memory, for his power to tell of the horse as if he had been there himself (Merry on 8.488; see [[apollo]], [[memory-and-the-muses]] and [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). Then the request:

{{quote:833afaab-c52b-560e-ba54-37f6f2e9eea4}}

μετάβηθι, “change your theme”, turns from the first lay, the quarrel at the beginning of the siege, not from the lay of Ares (Merry on 8.492; see the translation's note on 8.492). The stranger asks for his own exploit, and names himself in it only as “brilliant Odysseus” (8.494; see [[odysseus]]).

**The third song.** “So he spoke, and he, stirred by the god, began, and showed forth his song” (8.499): ὁρμηθεὶς θεοῦ may be “stirred by the god” or, as a scholium has it, “starting from the god”, with a prelude to the god (Merry on 8.499; see the translation's note on 8.499). He takes it up from where the Argives sailed away and the others sat in the horse in the place of assembly of the Trojans (8.500–503), ἔνθεν ἑλών being “having taken it up at that point” (Merry on 8.500); the song, in reported speech, runs to the fight at the house of Deiphobus (8.500–520; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). “This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus / was melting, and tears wet his cheeks beneath his eyelids” (8.521–522; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).

**The lyre stopped.** Alcinous, who alone has noticed the tears, speaks to the Phaeacians: “and let Demodocus now stop his clear-toned lyre; / for it is not to all of us that he gives delight by singing this. / Ever since we have been at supper and the divine singer was roused to sing, / from then on he has never ceased from miserable lamentation, / the stranger” (8.537–541). σχεθέτω is “hush” (Merry on 8.537), and the clause means “since the bard was moved to sing as we sat at supper” (Merry on 8.539). Then the king asks the stranger who he is, and why he weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilios, which the gods wrought “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.577–580; see [[alcinous]] and [[song-and-fame]]).

**His epithets in this book.** θεῖος ἀοιδός, “the divine singer” (8.43, 8.47, 8.87, 8.539); ἐρίηρος ἀοιδός, “the trusty singer” (8.62, 8.471); ἀοιδὸς … περικλυτός, “the renowned singer” (8.83, 8.367, 8.521); λαοῖσι τετιμένον, “honored by the people” (8.472); ἥρως, “the hero” (8.483).
