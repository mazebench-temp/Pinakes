ἄναξ δʼ ἀνδρῶν Ἀγαμέμνων / χαῖρε νόῳ, “yet Agamemnon, lord of men, / rejoiced in his mind that the best of the Achaeans were contending” (8.77–78; see [[lord-of-men]] and [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]]). νόῳ, “in his mind”, is secretly, and ἐκπάγλοις ἐπέεσσιν, “with terrible words”, are words that dismayed the bystanders, “but Agamemnon rejoiced” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.77, 8.78). The reason is an oracle: Phoebus Apollo had told him so “in sacred Pytho, when he crossed the stone threshold / to consult the oracle” (8.79–81; see [[apollo]] and [[pytho]]). The poem does not give the oracle's words. Merry takes the consultation to have been made before the war, when it was just beginning (on 8.81), and reports another story of it from Demetrius of Phalerum, in which Menelaus and Odysseus went to Delphi to ask about the coming expedition (Merry on 8.81; see [[menelaus]]).

The king who rejoices at a quarrel of the best of the Achaeans is the king whose own quarrel with Achilles, “the son of Atreus, lord of men, and brilliant Achilles”, begins the Iliad (*Iliad* 1.7; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). Earlier in the Odyssey his name has stood for a homecoming ended by murder (1.35–43, 3.254–312, 4.512–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]); in the song he is still the commander at Troy, remembering, on Merry's reading, an oracle given at the beginning of the war.
