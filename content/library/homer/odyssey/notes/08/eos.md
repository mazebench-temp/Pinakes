---
summary: The book's day, the thirty-third of the poem, opens with the verse of the Odyssey's mornings, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (8.1 = 2.1). No other dawn comes in the book: the evening's feast runs on into the night of Odysseus' tale, and the next dawn rises in Book 13 (13.18).
---

**The dawn of the assembly.** “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / the sacred might of Alcinous rose from his bed, / and up rose Odysseus, sprung from Zeus, sacker of cities” (8.1–3; see [[early-born]], [[rosy-fingered]], [[dawn-line]], [[alcinous]] and [[sacred-might-of-alcinous]]). The verse opens the day of the assembly in Ithaca (2.1), the morning of the sacrifice at Pylos (3.404), the second day of the drive to Sparta (3.491) and the morning of Menelaus' tale at Sparta (4.306); in that tale it brings his two mornings on Pharos (4.431, 4.576), and in Book 5 the day on which Odysseus begins his raft (5.228). It is the dawn that Alcinous fixed the night before for the stranger's escort and for calling the elders (7.189–191, 7.317–318; see the Book 7 note).

**The next dawn.** The day ends when “the sun set, and the glorious gifts were there for him” (8.417; see [[helios]]); the supper, the third song and Alcinous' questions belong to its night, and Odysseus' answer runs on through Books 9–12. Merry notes that the absorbing interest of his stories keeps all the household and guests up till the time given in Book 13 (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.417), when the Phaeacians go home to sleep, and at the next dawn, which rises in the same verse (13.18), they hurry to the ship with gifts of bronze (13.17–19).
