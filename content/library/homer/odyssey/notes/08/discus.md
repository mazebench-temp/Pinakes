summary: At the games of the Phaeacians Elatreus is the best with the discus (8.129). Stung by Euryalus' taunt, Odysseus seizes a stone bigger and heavier than theirs and throws it beyond all their marks; Athena, in the likeness of a man, sets the mark and tells him that none of the Phaeacians will reach it (8.186–198). It is his only contest of the day.

**Elatreus' event.** The young men contend in running, wrestling, jumping, the discus and boxing, “and with the discus, again, Elatreus was by far the strongest of all” (8.129; see [[elatreus]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). Alcinous had named only four of the contests, “in boxing and in wrestling and in jumping and in running” (8.103; see the translation's row πύξ, παλαιμοσύνη, ἅλμα, πόδες, δίσκος).

**The stranger's throw.** Euryalus tells the stranger that he does not look like an athlete, and Odysseus answers him and then shows him (8.158–185; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]):

{{quote:e286411a-15ee-543e-92c5-1f8e87259a06}}

The weight is a stone, as the discus generally was, W. W. Merry notes, while the σόλος of the Iliad's games was of iron, bronze or wood, though the descriptions of the two do not agree (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.187, referring to Nitzsch; *Iliad* 23.826; see the Iliad's Book 23 note). The Phaeacians had already thrown, and Elatreus had won (Merry on 8.187). μείζονα, “bigger”, is set beside πάχετον, which Merry takes for a positive, “thick”, against the scholia's comparative (see the translation's note on 8.187), and περιστρέψας is “with one whirl”, as Zeus whirls Ate from the sky in the Iliad (Merry on 8.189; *Iliad* 19.130–131). The Phaeacians “of the long oars, men famed for ships” cower to the ground at the stone's whizzing: Merry finds an intended irony in the high-sounding epithets set beside their fright (on 8.191; see [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]).

**The marks.** The stone “flew beyond the marks of all”, and “Athena set the mark, / in form like a man” (8.192–194; see [[athena]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). The σήματα were probably pegs stuck in the ground where each discus fell, the discus itself being taken up again, and the τέρματα the place where his fell; ἔθηκε, “set”, is explained by the verse in which Achilles “pointed out the turning post” for the chariot race (Merry on 8.192, 8.193; *Iliad* 23.358; see the translation's note on 8.192–193). The goddess's words make the same point: “Even a blind man, stranger, could sort out your mark / by feeling for it, since it is not mingled with the throng, / but far the first” (8.195–197); the “throng” is the cluster of the other marks, close together because the Phaeacians were evenly matched (Merry on 8.196). The marks are excepted by [[teras]] and [[tomb]], whose σῆμα is a sign and a grave, and τέρματα are claimed by [[turning-post]] only in the Iliad.

**One contest.** Odysseus offers to throw another “as far, I think, or even farther still” (8.203), and challenges the Phaeacians to box, wrestle or run (8.204–207), but no one takes him up, and the discus is the only contest he accomplishes. Zenodotus athetized 8.23, where Athena's grace is to help him “accomplish the many contests”, because he only threw the discus (see the translation's note on 8.22–23, and [[contests]]).
