---
summary: Book 8 is the Odyssey's book of the singer. The Muse loves Demodocus and gave him good and evil (8.63–64), sets him on to sing the famous deeds of men (8.73), and teaches singers their lays (8.481); Odysseus praises him as one taught by the Muse or Apollo, who sings the Achaeans' woe as if he had been there (8.487–491). The farewells are about memory too: the Phaeacians, Alcinous and Nausicaa each ask to be remembered (8.244, 8.431, 8.461–462).
refs: [8.63-64, 8.73-74, 8.479-481, 8.487-491, 8.241-245, 8.430-432, 8.461-462]
---

**The Muse and the singer.** The book gives the Odyssey's fullest account of where a singer's song comes from (see [[singers]] and [[demodocus]]). The Muse loved Demodocus above all, and gave him both good and evil, taking his eyes and giving him sweet song (8.63–64; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). When the feasting is over, “the Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men” (8.73; see [[oime]] and [[kleos]]); the Muse “has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers” (8.481; see [[phylon]]). In his praise Odysseus gives the singer two possible teachers, “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo”, and the proof is the singer's knowledge of what he did not see: he sings the Achaeans' woe “as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another” (8.487–491; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]], [[muses]] and [[apollo]]). Merry joins the two: the singer, as the historian of early times, owes his knowledge of the past to the god of prophecy, and the Muses too were daughters of Mnemosyne, “Memory”, in Hesiod and the Hymns, and their very name seems to point the same way (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.488). When the third song begins, the singer is “stirred by the god” (ὁρμηθεὶς θεοῦ, 8.499), or, in a scholium's alternative, “starting from the god”, with a prelude to a god (Merry on 8.499; see the translation's note on 8.499).

**To be remembered.** The book's farewells turn on memory, μιμνῄσκομαι. Alcinous asks the stranger to tell the heroes at home of the Phaeacians, “remembering our excellence” (8.241–245; see [[arete]]); he gives him a golden goblet, “so that remembering me all his days / he may pour libations in his hall” (8.430–432); and Nausicaa asks only this, “that someday, when you are in your native land, / you may remember me, since to me first you owe the price for your life” (8.461–462; see [[zoagria]] and [[nausicaa]]). The man who is asked to remember them weeps at the singer's memory of his own war.
