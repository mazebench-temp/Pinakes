**The Muse's gift.** The herald leads in “the trusty singer, / whom the Muse loved above all, and she gave him both good and evil: / she robbed him of his eyes, but gave him sweet song” (8.62–64; see [[demodocus]] and [[trusty]]). The gift of good and evil together is the lot that Zeus gives men from his two jars (*Iliad* 24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]); here it is the Muse's, and both are named: his eyes taken, sweet song given. The Muse's love is Hesiod's mark of the blessed singer, ὃ δʼ ὄλβιος, ὅν τινα Μοῦσαι / φίλωνται, “blessed is he whom the Muses love” (*Theogony* 96–97). In Alcinous' words it is “the god” who has given Demodocus song above all (8.44), and Odysseus ends his request for the song of the horse with “how the god has readily granted you wondrous song” (8.498; see [[wondrous]]); the singer begins “stirred by the god” (8.499; see the translation's note on 8.499).

**The famous deeds of men.** After the meal “the Muse set the singer on to sing the famous deeds of men, / from a lay whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.73–74; see [[kleos]], [[song-and-fame]] and [[oime]]). κλέα ἀνδρῶν is what Achilles sang to himself to the lyre in his hut (*Iliad* 9.189); Merry notes that the stories which the heroes sang themselves in the Iliad are in the Odyssey the property of the professional singer (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.73). Hesiod's singer, “the servant of the Muses”, sings the famous deeds of former men, κλέεα προτέρων ἀνθρώπων, and the man with fresh grief in his heart forgets his sorrows (*Theogony* 98–103); at the Phaeacian feast the famous deeds of men bring tears to the one man there whose deeds they are (8.83–92; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).

**The tribe of singers.** Sending Demodocus a portion of the chine, Odysseus gives the reason: “For among all men upon the earth singers / have their share of honor and respect, because / the Muse has taught them their lays, and loves the tribe of singers” (8.479–481; see [[singers]], [[phylon]] and [[time]]). In Ithaca the singer Phemius, pleading for his life, says that he is self-taught, and that a god has planted in his mind lays of every kind, θεὸς δέ μοι ἐν φρεσὶν οἴμας / παντοίας ἐνέφυσεν (22.347–348; see [[phemius]]): in both places the lays, οἶμαι, come from a god.

**The child of Zeus.** At supper Odysseus praises the singer: “Either the Muse, the child of Zeus, has taught you, or Apollo; / for all too well, in due order, you sing the fate of the Achaeans, / all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through, / as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another” (8.488–491; see [[apollo]] and [[zeus]]). The Muses of the Iliad are present and know all things, while men hear only the report (*Iliad* 2.484–486; see [[we-hear-only-the-report]] and [[invocation-of-the-muses]]): the singer who sings as if he had been present has his knowledge from them. Merry ties it to their mother Mnemosyne, Memory, in Hesiod and the Hymns, and to the etymology of their name, and compares Hesiod's verse that singers are from the Muses and Apollo (on 8.488; *Theogony* 94–95; see the Book 1 note and the Book 8 note of [[apollo]]). The poem names the Muse once more, at the funeral of Achilles, where the nine Muses sang the lament and no Argive was left without tears, so did the clear-voiced Muse stir them (24.60–62).
