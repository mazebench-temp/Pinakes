---
summary: Virgil's Trojans sail by night past Circe's shore and hear the beasts that had been men; Helenus names Aeaea among the stages of Aeneas' voyage; and the Aeneid's Aeolus is a king of the winds, as Homer's is not. Merry finds this book's words in Dido's hall, Cacus' smoke, Euryalus' first youth and the thin lives of the shades, and the Aeneid's dead companions, Misenus, whom Aeneas must bury before he goes down, and Palinurus, whom he meets unburied, stand where Elpenor stands.
---

- **Circe's shore.** After burying his nurse Caieta, Aeneas sails on by moonlight along “the nearest shores of Circe's land, where the rich daughter of the Sun makes her untrodden groves resound with constant song”, and in her proud house burns fragrant cedar to light the night as she runs her shrill comb through the fine web. From there are heard the groans and rage of lions that refuse their chains, bristling boars and bears raging in their pens, and great wolves howling, “whom the cruel goddess with her potent herbs had changed from the faces of men into the faces and backs of beasts”; Neptune fills the Trojans' sails with favoring winds and carries them past (*Aeneid* 7.1–24, plain renderings). Homer's Circe also sings at her loom among lions and wolves, and turns the companions into swine (10.210–243). Merry reports Scaliger's distinction on the Virgilian lines: Homer depicts wild beasts tamed by drugs, Virgil men turned into the likeness of beasts; the scholia offer both readings of κατέθελξεν, and Merry prefers the second, since Eurylochus too fears that Circe will make them all “either swine or wolves or lions” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.213; 10.433; see the translation's note on 10.212–213): [[circe]], [[odysseus-and-circe]], [[shape-changing]] and [[thelgo]].
- **Circe in Latium.** In Latium Circe is the “wife” of Picus, who, seized by desire, struck him with her golden wand and with her drugs made him a bird, sprinkling his wings with colors (*Aeneid* 7.189–191), and the fire-breathing horses that Latinus sends to Aeneas are of the breed that cunning Circe raised by stealth from her father's horses, with a mare put in their way (*Aeneid* 7.280–283). Latinus himself is the son of Faunus and the nymph Marica (*Aeneid* 7.47–48); Merry reports a later legend in which Circe is the mother of Faunus, beside Hesiod's Latinus, son of Circe and Odysseus (Merry on 10.135): [[circe]], [[wand]] and [[hesiod]].
- **Aeaea on the way.** Helenus tells Aeneas that Italy is far off: before he can found his city in a safe land he must bend the oar in the Sicilian sea, pass with his ships over the Ausonian water, “and the lakes of the underworld and the island of Aeaean Circe” (*infernique lacus Aeaeaeque insula Circae*, *Aeneid* 3.384–387, a plain rendering). Merry sets Virgil's *ante*, “first”, beside Circe's πρῶτον, the other journey that Odysseus must make first, to the house of Hades (Merry on 10.490): [[aeaea]] and [[circe]].
- **King Aeolus.** In Aeolia, the land of storm clouds, “king Aeolus” keeps the struggling winds in a vast cave and curbs them with chains and a prison; the Father put mountains over them and gave them a king who should know how to rein them in and to let them go when ordered; Juno addresses him, “Aeolus, for to you the father of gods and king of men has given the power to calm the waves and to raise them with the wind”, and he answers that he owes her his scepter (*Aeneid* 1.52–80, plain renderings). Homer's Aeolus, Merry notes, is not a king of the winds but only their manager by leave of Zeus, as Athena, Calypso and Circe can also send a favoring wind (Merry on 10.21; “dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes”, 10.21–22): [[aeolus]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[virgil-juno-and-aeolus]].
- **Dido's hall.** For the noise of Aeolus' feasting house, which “resounds round about in the court” (10.10), Merry finds that Virgil's version at Dido's banquet, “a din rises in the house, and they roll their voices through the broad halls”, *atria* (*Aeneid* 1.725–726), points to αὐλή, the court, rather than to the conjectures that would make the house resound with fluting or with song (Merry on 10.10; see the translation's note on 10.10): [[aeolus]] and [[house]].
- **The shades.** The rest of the dead “flit as shadows” (10.495); they are, Merry says, not invisible but without substance, as Virgil describes them when the Sibyl warns Aeneas, who would have rushed with his sword at the forms of the monsters at the gate of the underworld, that they are “thin lives without body, flitting under the hollow semblance of a form” (*Aeneid* 6.292–294, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.495): [[teiresias]] and [[psyche]].
- **Misenus and Palinurus.** Elpenor dies in Circe's house on the morning of the departure for Hades and is the first soul to meet Odysseus there, asking to be buried with his oar (10.551–560; 11.51–78; 12.8–15). Before Aeneas goes down, the Sibyl tells him that the body of a friend lies unburied and pollutes the fleet, and must be laid in a tomb; it is Misenus, son of Aeolus, whom they bury under a headland with his arms, his oar and his trumpet, and the mountain keeps his name (*Aeneid* 6.149–152, 6.162–164, 6.232–235). Among the unburied Aeneas meets his helmsman Palinurus, who fell from the stern on the voyage from Africa (*Aeneid* 6.337–339; see [[virgils-palinurus]] and [[virgil-the-unburied]]): [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]].
- **Words.** Merry also sets beside this book's “fiery smoke” (10.152) Cacus in his cave, rolling up “a smoky night, darkness mixed with fire” (*Aeneid* 8.252–255), and beside Hermes “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip” (10.278–279) Euryalus, “a boy marking his unshorn face with first youth” (*Aeneid* 9.179–181; plain renderings; Merry on 10.152, 10.279): [[hermes]].
