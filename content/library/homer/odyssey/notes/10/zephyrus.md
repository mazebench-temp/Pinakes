summary: Aeolus binds the other winds in the skin and sends the West Wind alone to carry Odysseus home: “but for me he sent forth the breath of the West Wind to blow, / to carry the ships and ourselves” (10.25–26). It brings the ships within sight of Ithaca in nine days and nights, before the companions let the other winds out.
# Ζεφύρου (10.25), the genitive, is claimed by the entry.
---

**The wind for Ithaca.** When Aeolus has bound up the paths of the winds in the skin and tied it fast in the ship, “but for me he sent forth the breath of the West Wind to blow, / to carry the ships and ourselves; but he was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (10.25–27; see [[aeolus]], [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[the-winds]]). Merry draws the geography from it: since Aeolus means to send Odysseus straight home by confining every wind but the West Wind, his island must lie to the west of Ithaca, with open sea between them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.1; see [[aeolia]] and [[ithaca]]). For nine days the ships sail on, night and day alike, and on the tenth the men can see their native fields and “men tending fires” (10.28–30; Merry on 10.28 and 10.30). The West Wind is here the fair wind of a voyage, as when Athena sent Telemachus “a fresh West Wind” (2.420–421), not the “stormy” West Wind that clashed with the others in Poseidon's storm (5.295; see the Book 5 note and [[stormy]]). Its errand fails through the companions' folly, when they loose the other winds (10.46–49; see [[the-winds]]).
