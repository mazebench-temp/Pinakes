summary: The narrator goes on. Sent home by Aeolus with the winds bound, he falls asleep at the sheet within sight of Ithaca, and when the companions loose the winds he thinks of throwing himself into the sea; Aeolus drives him away as hated by the gods. At Telepylus he keeps his own ship outside the harbor and alone escapes the Laestrygonians. On Aeaea he kills a great stag for his grieving men, goes alone after Eurylochus' band to Circe's house, withstands her drug with Hermes' help, makes her swear the oath of the gods, and will not eat until his companions are men again; after a year he begs to be sent home, and learns, weeping, that he must first go to the house of Hades (10.1–574).

**The narrator goes on.** The book is Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians, going on without a pause from Book 9 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]): from the land of the Cyclopes the ships come to the island of Aeolus, and the words of Aeolus, the companions, Polites, Eurylochus, Hermes and Circe are speeches within his speech.

**At the sheet.** Aeolus binds the winds for him and sends the West Wind to carry him home, and on the tenth day “our native fields were already coming into sight”: “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was, / for I was always handling the sheet of the ship, and would not give it / to any other of my companions, so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.29–33; see [[aeolus]], [[sweet-sleep]] and [[hypnos]]). The πόδες are the two ropes at the lower corners of the sail, and since the ship was running before the wind, W. W. Merry explains, he had only to trim the sail to make the most of it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.32; see the translation's key words). While he sleeps the companions open the skin, sure that it holds gold and silver, and the winds rush out (10.34–47; see [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[hetairos]]).

**Whether to perish.** He wakes to the storm:

{{quote:3793518e-3cea-5352-8e24-5af09ab72c1c}}

καλυψάμενος, “covering my head”, is the sign of abandonment to grief, as when he hid his face at Demodocus' song (8.92; Merry on 10.53; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]); his companions, too, have their heads covered in their despair on Aeaea until he rouses them (10.179; Merry on 10.179). ἔτλην, “I endured”, is the verb of his epithet πολύτλας, “much-enduring” (see [[much-enduring]]).

**On the threshold.** Back on the Aeolian island he takes a herald and one companion to the house of Aeolus, and they sit “beside the doorposts, on the threshold” (10.59–63; see [[heralds]]): so he will sit as a beggar in his own house (17.339), a humbler posture than in the house of Alcinous, where he went up the hall and sat by the hearth (7.153; Merry on 10.62; see [[supplication]]). To the household's question, what evil god has attacked him (10.64), he answers “grieving at heart”: “My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep, / cruel sleep. But make it good, my friends; for you have the strength” (10.67–69; see [[ate]] and [[hard]]). σχέτλιος is said of sleep as the Latin *improbus* is said of a goose or of toil (Merry on 10.69). He speaks “approaching them with gentle words” (10.70): Zenodotus read “answering them with gentle words”, which a scholiast preferred, since he does not reproach Aeolus but supplicates him (see the translation's note on 10.70). Aeolus drives him out as a man hated by the gods: “So saying, he sent me from the house, groaning heavily” (10.72–76; see [[aeolus]] and [[groaning-heavily]]). In ἡμετέρῃ ματίῃ, “through our own folly” (10.79), the scholiast rightly saw, Merry says, that Odysseus counts himself in out of fellow-feeling, because he slept: his sleep gave the crew their chance to satisfy their fatal curiosity (Merry on 10.79; see the translation's key words).

**Outside the harbor.** At Telepylus the others steer their ships into the closed harbor and moor them close together, “but I alone kept my black ship outside, / there at the far edge, making the cables fast to a rock” (10.91–96; see [[harbor]] and [[stern-cables]]). From a rugged lookout he sees no works of oxen or of men, only smoke, and sends two men and a herald to find out what men live there (10.97–102; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). When the Laestrygonians stone the ships in the harbor and spear the men like fish, “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and with it cut the cables of my dark-prowed ship”, and bids his men row for their lives; his ship alone gets out to sea, “but the others perished there in a body” (10.118–132; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]] and [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]).

**The stag.** On Aeaea, after two days lying on the shore in weariness and grief, he takes his spear and sword and climbs to a lookout, “in the hope that I might see the works of mortals and hear their voices” (10.142–148; see the translation's note on 10.147). He sees “the fiery smoke” rising from Circe's halls, ponders, and decides first to feed his men and send others to find out (10.149–155; see [[decision-scene]]). On the way back, “one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was, / and sent a great high-horned stag into my very path” (10.157–158; see [[pity]] and [[deer]]). He strikes it on the spine, sets his foot on it to pull out the spear and lays the spear down to have his hands free, binds its feet with a cable of brushwood and willow, and carries it to the ship on his neck, leaning on his spear, “for it was a very great beast” (10.161–171; Merry on 10.164, 10.169). Then he rouses his men “with soothing words, standing beside each man” (10.173; see [[soothing-words]]):

{{quote:cf1db42d-790e-539d-b1ba-fa6a7adf4774}}

They uncover their heads, gaze at the stag, wash their hands and feast until sunset (10.178–186; see [[feast]]).

**No plan.** At dawn he calls an assembly and tells them what he saw from the lookout (10.188–189; see [[assembly]] and [[aeaea]]):

{{quote:f71ab41b-72a7-5a69-ac8b-9f7065601408}}

The verse troubled the critics, since the speaker has just seen the sun set and the dawn appear. Crates and Strabo took ζόφος for the north; the scholia supposed him dismayed, or carried where the ordinary signs of the world are confused; Ukert thought the days had been cloudy. Merry takes the words to mean only that he has not the least idea where they are, east and west dividing the world between them, as when Hector cares nothing whether the birds fly to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, or to the left, toward the misty darkness (*Iliad* 12.239–240; Merry on 10.190; see the translation's note on 10.190 and [[eos]]). The word for the plan he cannot find is μῆτις, the word of his standing epithet πολύμητις (see [[metis]] and [[of-many-wiles]]). The double opening, κέκλυτε … ὦ φίλοι, is contrary to Homeric custom (Merry on 10.189; see the translation's note on 10.189). His men weep, remembering the Laestrygonian and the Cyclops (10.198–202; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

**The lots.** He divides the companions into two bands, “one I led myself, and godlike Eurylochus the other”, and the lot that leaps out of the helmet is Eurylochus' (10.203–207; see [[lots]] and [[eurylochus]]).

**Harsh necessity.** When Eurylochus brings back the news that the others have vanished into Circe's house, “I slung about my shoulders my silver-studded sword, / great and made of bronze, and my bow about them; / and bade him lead me back by the same road” (10.261–263; see [[silver-studded]], [[sword]] and [[bow]]). Eurylochus clasps his knees and begs to be left behind, and he answers: “Eurylochus, you may stay here in this place, / eating and drinking beside the hollow black ship; / but I will go, for harsh necessity has come upon me” (10.264–273; see [[anankaion]]).

**Hermes.** Hermes meets him in the sacred glens, “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip”, clings to his hand and calls him by name: “Where are you going now again, unhappy man, alone across the heights, / not knowing the place?” (10.275–282; see [[hermes]] and [[called-him-by-name]]). The god gives him the good drug, the moly, and tells him what to do: when Circe strikes him with her wand, to draw his sword and spring at her, and then not to refuse her bed, but first to make her swear the great oath of the gods (10.283–306; see [[drugs]], [[moly]] and [[oath]]). He goes on to the house, “and my heart heaved with many thoughts as I went” (10.309; see [[pondering-two-ways]]).

**The drug that fails.** He stands at Circe's doors and shouts, and she leads him in, seats him on a silver-studded chair and gives him the mixed drink in a golden cup (10.310–317; see [[circe]] and [[kykeon]]). It does not bewitch him; she strikes him with her wand and bids him go to the sty with his companions, and “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and sprang at Circe as though eager to kill her” (10.318–322; see [[wand]] and [[sties]]). She runs in under the sword, clasps his knees and knows him (10.323–332; see [[supplication]]): “But there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched. / Surely you are Odysseus of many turns; he always told me / you would come, the slayer of Argus of the golden wand” (10.329–331; see [[noos]], [[of-many-turns]] and [[of-the-golden-wand]]). She means, Merry explains, that he has the strong and stubborn mind that is generally proof against sorcery, for she does not know that he carries an amulet (Merry on 10.329). πολύτροπος is said of him only here and in the poem's first verse (1.1; see the translation's note on 1.1).

**The oath.** To her invitation to her bed he answers with the charge, “Circe, how can you bid me be kind to you, / you who turned my companions into swine in your halls”, and will not go up into her bed unless she swears that she will plan no other evil against him (10.337–344; see [[bed]] and [[shape-changing]]); the contrast lies in the words ἑταίρους, “my companions”, and αὐτόν, “me myself”, set side by side (Merry on 10.338). “So I spoke, and at once she swore that she would not, as I bade her” (10.345; see [[oath]]).

**He will not eat.** Bathed and dressed, and seated before a polished table spread with bread and dainties, he will not touch the food: “but I sat with my thoughts elsewhere, and my heart foreboded evil” (10.348–374; see [[handmaids]] and [[bathing]]); ἀλλοφρονέων is “with his thoughts elsewhere” (Merry on 10.374). When Circe asks why he sits “like a man without speech, / eating your heart” (10.378–379; see [[like-a-man-without-speech]]), he answers:

{{quote:7b28bfb6-f356-5708-b860-0458a02a0fbe}}

λύσασθαι, the middle, is said of his own act in setting them free, λῦσον of hers (Merry on 10.385). Circe lets the swine out and anoints them, they become men again, and “they knew me, and each of them clung to my hands” (10.388–397; see [[shape-changing]]).

**The companions at the ship.** He goes back to the ship, and his men pour around him “as when the calves of the fields” skip to meet their mothers coming home (10.407–417; see [[the-calves-and-the-cows]]); he speaks to them “with gentle words” and bids them beach the ship and follow him (10.422–427; see [[gentle-words]]). Only Eurylochus holds back, calling the venture as reckless as the visit to the Cyclops, “and with them went this bold Odysseus; / for it was through his recklessness that they too perished” (10.429–437; see [[atasthalie]] and [[tharsos]]).

**A kinsman spared.** “So he spoke, and I pondered in my mind / whether to draw the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh, / and strike off his head with it and bring it to the ground, / kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near” (10.438–441; see [[long-edged]] and [[eurylochus]]). His companions hold him back “with soothing words, one from one side and one from another”, the verses in which they held him back from taunting the Cyclops (10.441–442 = 9.492–493), and Eurylochus follows, “for he was afraid of my terrible rebuke” (10.443–448).

**The year.** “There every day, for a full year's round, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (10.467–468), until his companions call him out: “Strange one, think now at last of your native land” (10.472; see [[strange-one]] and [[nostos]]). δαιμόνιε, Merry notes, always implies that a man is under the influence of something more than human, and is used when his conduct is such as to excite wonder (Merry on 10.472). That night he goes up into Circe's bed and entreats her by her knees to keep her promise and send him home (10.480–486; see [[supplication]]): the promise, Merry notes, is nowhere given in so many words, but may lie in her oath (10.345) or her welcome (10.460–465), and a whole year has gone unrecorded (Merry on 10.483; see the translation's note on 10.483).

**To the house of Hades.** Circe answers that he must first go to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, to consult the soul of Teiresias (10.488–495; see [[teiresias]]):

{{quote:0521bd93-d3a7-5af2-8107-7e65ec17e8c6}}

εἰς Ἄϊδος, “to the house of Hades”: Ἀΐδης is always a person in Homer, never a place (see the translation's note on 10.502 and [[hades]]). The light of the sun is the poem's word for life (see [[while-i-live]]). Circe's instructions name him “hero” and “leader of the people” (10.516, 10.538; see [[heros]], [[leader-of-men]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). At dawn he rouses his men “with soothing words” (10.546–549); Elpenor falls from the roof (10.551–560; see [[elpenor]]); and he tells the rest: “You think, no doubt, that you are going home to your dear native land; / but Circe has ordained another journey for us” (10.562–565). They sit down and lament, and at the ship they find the ram and the black ewe that Circe has tied there, slipping past them unseen (10.566–574; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**His names and epithets in this book.** Ὀδυσεῦ, in the mouth of Aeolus' household and of Circe (10.64, 10.378); φαίδιμʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “shining Odysseus”, in Eurylochus' report (10.251; see [[phaidimos]]); ἦ σύ γʼ Ὀδυσσεύς ἐσσι πολύτροπος, “Surely you are Odysseus of many turns”, in Circe's mouth (10.330; see [[of-many-turns]]); διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices”, Circe's address three times (10.401, 10.488, 10.504; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]], [[of-many-devices]] and [[laertes]]); ὁ θρασὺς … Ὀδυσσεύς, “this bold Odysseus”, in Eurylochus' charge (10.436). Unnamed, he is διοτρεφές, “you who are nurtured by Zeus” (10.266, 10.419; see [[zeus-nurtured]]); διογενές, “Sprung from Zeus” (10.443); δαιμόνιε, “Strange one” (10.472); ὦ δύστηνε, “unhappy man”, in Hermes' greeting (10.281); ἥρως, “hero” (10.516); and ὄρχαμε λαῶν, “leader of the people” (10.538).
