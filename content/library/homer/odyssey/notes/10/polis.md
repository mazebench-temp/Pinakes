---
summary: Aeolus' household has “their city and their fine house” on the floating island (10.13); the Laestrygonians have a steep citadel, Telepylus, and a city supplied with water from the spring Artacia (10.81–82, 10.104–108); Circe asks Odysseus “Where are your city and your parents?” (10.325); and the companions welcome him as if they had come to “the very city / of rugged Ithaca” (10.416–417).
---

**Cities of the tale.** On the floating island “we came to their city and their fine house” (πόλιν καὶ δώματα καλά, 10.13), where a scholium reads καὶ τείχεα μακρά, “and long walls” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 10.13; see [[aeolus]] and [[aeolia]]). The companions envy Odysseus as a man honored by all men “to whatever people's city and land he comes” (10.39; see [[time]]). The Laestrygonians' land has “the steep citadel of Lamus, / Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (Λάμου αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον, 10.81–82; see [[telepylus]]), a road by which the wagons bring wood down to the city, and a spring, Artacia, “for from there they used to carry water to the city” (10.103–108; see [[artacia]] and [[laestrygonians]]); its king is called from the place of assembly and raises the cry “throughout the city” (10.114–118).

**Ithaca.** Circe's first question, when her drug has failed, is the stranger's question: “Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?” (10.325 = 1.170; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). The companions, seeing Odysseus come back alive, feel “as if they had come to their native land and to the very city / of rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised” (πόλιν αὐτήν, 10.416–417; see [[ithaca]] and [[nostos]]), αὐτήν being Aristarchus' reading against the αὐτῶν of all the manuscripts but one (Merry's critical note on 10.416; see the translation's note on 10.416).
