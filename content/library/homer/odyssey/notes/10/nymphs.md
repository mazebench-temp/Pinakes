summary: Circe's four handmaids are born, like the nymphs, “of the springs and of the groves / and of the sacred rivers that flow on to the sea” (10.350–351), and Circe herself is “the nymph” when she dresses at dawn in the verses of Calypso's robing (10.543).
# Unnamed: the handmaids born of the springs, the groves and the rivers (10.350-351).
refs: [10.350-351]
---

**The handmaids of Circe.** While Odysseus is in Circe's bed, “the handmaids were busy in the halls, / four of them, who are her serving women in the house; / and they are born of the springs and of the groves / and of the sacred rivers that flow on to the sea” (10.348–351; see [[handmaids]] and [[circe]]). The poem does not call them nymphs, but the places of their birth are the places of the nymphs, who “dwell in the lovely groves / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (*Iliad* 20.8–9), and “hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (6.123–124; see the Book 6 note). W. W. Merry suspects the two verses of being a later addition, since the present γίγνονται, “are born”, is very awkward, and the words read like a description of Naiads, Dryads and Nereids, nymphs of the springs, the trees and the sea (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.350–351; see the translation's note on 10.348–351). He reports too the scholia's allegory, in which Circe is the year and her four maids the seasons, fit attendants for a daughter of the Sun: Spring spreading her carpet of grass and flowers, Summer bringing her store of fruit and corn, Autumn supplying her wine, and Winter lighting the fire on the hearth (Merry on 10.350–351; see [[hours]]).

**The nymph's robing.** On the last morning Circe dresses Odysseus and herself: “And she put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing; / and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle, / fine and lovely, and around her waist she cast a girdle, / fine and golden, and on her head she set a head covering” (10.542–545; see [[clothing]] and [[girdle]]). The last three verses are those of Calypso's robing on the morning when she sends Odysseus to build his raft, but for the last half-verse (5.230–232; see the Book 5 note and the translation's formula row); there Odysseus dressed himself (5.229), here the goddess dresses him. νύμφη, “the nymph”, is Calypso's standing name in the Odyssey (1.14, 5.153; see [[calypso]]); this is the only time it is said of Circe (see the general article).
