---
summary: Eurylochus, holding the companions back from Circe's house, turns against Odysseus the words of the proem: the men the Cyclops ate perished “through his recklessness” (10.435–437).
---

Eurylochus warns the companions that Circe will make them all swine or wolves or lions, to guard her house under compulsion, “as the Cyclops penned them in, when to his farmyard came / our companions, and with them went this bold Odysseus; / for it was through his recklessness that they too perished” (τούτου γὰρ καὶ κεῖνοι ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο, 10.433–437; see [[eurylochus]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). τούτου takes up θρασύς and explains it: “Foolhardy, I say, for it was through his infatuation” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.437); καὶ κεῖνοι is the reading La Roche gives to Aristarchus, against the manuscripts' κἀκεῖνοι (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 10.437). The verse-end is the one the proem gives the companions who ate the Sun's cattle, “for they perished through their own recklessness” (1.7; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]), and the one Sthenelus gives the Seven, the fathers who fell at Thebes (*Iliad* 4.409). Here the recklessness is not the men's but their leader's, as Hector reproaches himself outside the walls, “since I have destroyed the army by my own recklessness” (*Iliad* 22.104). Odysseus answers with his hand on his sword, and the companions hold him back (10.438–445; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[hetairos]]).
