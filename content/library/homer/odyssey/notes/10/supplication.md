summary: Eurylochus takes Odysseus by the knees and begs not to be led back to Circe's house (10.264–269); Circe, her drug defeated, runs in under his sword and clasps his knees (10.323–324); and Odysseus entreats Circe by her knees to send him home (10.480–486). At the pit he is to implore the dead with vows (10.521).
# γουνοῦσθαι "implore" (10.521), only here; γουνούμην (11.29) is the first person of Book 11.
grc: [γουνοῦσθαι]
---

**At Aeolus' door.** Driven back to the Aeolian island, Odysseus sits on the threshold and asks “with gentle words” (10.62–70), as a suppliant rather than an accuser in the view of the scholium that prefers Zenodotus' reading (see [[xenia]] and the translation's note on 10.70). Aeolus drives him away (10.72–76).

**Eurylochus.** When Odysseus bids him lead the way back to Circe's house, Eurylochus “took hold of my knees with both hands and begged me, / and lamenting addressed me with winged words: / ‘Do not lead me there against my will, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me here’” (10.264–266; see [[eurylochus]], [[gounata]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]). The middle verse is omitted by most manuscripts and has been bracketed since Wolf (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 10.265; see the translation's note on 10.265). Odysseus grants the request: he may stay by the ship, eating and drinking (10.271–273).

**Circe.** When Odysseus springs at her with drawn sword, “she, with a great cry, ran in under it and clasped my knees, / and lamenting addressed me with winged words” (λάβε γούνων, 10.323–324; see [[circe]]). The goddess who meant to bewitch him becomes his suppliant, as Hermes foretold that she would yield in fear (10.295–296).

**Odysseus.** After the year it is his turn: “But I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe / and entreated her by her knees, and the goddess heard my voice” (γούνων ἐλλιτάνευσα, 10.480–481), γούνων being “by her knees”, as in Telemachus' “I beg you by Olympian Zeus” (λίσσομαι … Ζηνός, 2.68) and in Hector's plea to Achilles, “I beg you by your life and your knees and your parents” (*Iliad* 22.338; Merry on 10.481). He asks for the escort she promised (10.483–486; see [[pompe]]).

**The dead.** At the pit he is to “earnestly implore the strengthless heads of the dead” (πολλὰ δὲ γουνοῦσθαι, 10.521), an entreaty whose vow the next verse gives in its very words (Merry on 10.522), and to beg “the glorious tribes of the dead with prayers” (λίσῃ, 10.526; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and [[prayer]]).
