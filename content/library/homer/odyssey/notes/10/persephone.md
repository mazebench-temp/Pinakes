---
summary: Persephone is named five times, all in Circe's instructions for the journey to the dead and in Odysseus' report of them. The house of the dead is “the house of Hades and of dread Persephone” (10.491, 10.564); Teiresias alone keeps his wits there, for “to him even in death Persephone has given a mind” (10.494); her groves of poplars and willows stand on the farther shore of Oceanus (10.509–510); and the prayer after the sacrifice is to “mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (10.534).
passages:
  - 10.490-495 | Persephone's gift to Teiresias
---

**Dread Persephone.** When Odysseus begs to be sent home, Circe answers that he must first go “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (10.491–492), and he repeats the words to his companions (10.564–565; see [[hades]], [[teiresias]] and [[circe]]). This is the first time the Odyssey names her. ἐπαινή, “dread”, is said in Homer of Persephone alone, and always, Merry observes, of Persephone as the companion of Hades (*Iliad* 9.457, 9.569; 10.491, 10.534, 10.564; 11.47; see [[dread]]). Some took the word for “praiseworthy”, like her other epithets ἀγαυή and ἁγνή, as if from ἔπαινος, “praise”; Merry thinks that in this inseparable connection with Hades she is likely to have a more terrible title, and takes it from ἐπί and αἰνός, “fearful”, or with Buttmann writes καὶ ἐπʼ αἰνή, “and along with him, awful Persephone”; in later Greek, he notes, the word was wrongly used for “laudable” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.491; see the translation's note on 10.491).

**Her gift to Teiresias.** Teiresias is “the blind seer, whose wits are steadfast; / to him even in death Persephone has given a mind, / that he alone should be wise; but the others flit as shadows” (10.493–495; see [[phrenes]], [[noos]] and [[psyche]]). Merry renders: “to whom even after his death did Persephone grant sense, for him alone of all men to have wisdom” (on 10.494). οἴῳ is drawn into the dative of τῷ; the rest of the dead are unsubstantial, and it was a special privilege to keep even the φρένες, as Achilles learned when the soul of Patroclus slipped from his arms: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.103–104; Merry on 10.495). Cicero puts the line into Latin when he speaks of Teiresias, “of whom Homer says that even among the dead he alone is wise, and the rest flit about like shadows” (*On Divination* 1.88; see [[cicero]]), and Plato's Socrates names it among the verses about Hades that he would strike out of the poems, so that the guardians may not fear death (*Republic* 386c–387b, where the line is read ταὶ δέ, 386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).

**Her groves.** Her realm begins on the farther shore of Oceanus, “where there is a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone, / tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit” (10.509–510; see [[the-groves-of-persephone]] and [[oceanus]]). The scholia, as Merry reports them, would not read ἐλάχεια, “smallest”, since her precinct would not be called that (Merry's critical note on 10.509), and the willows that shed their fruit unripe seemed to him fitting trees for her, for “fruitless things belong to the dead”, as the scholium says (on 10.510; see [[willow]]).

**The prayer.** After the sacrifice of the ram and the black ewe, the companions are to burn the victims “and pray to the gods, / to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (10.533–534; see [[mighty]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). Book 11 performs the rite in the same verses (11.46–47), and there Persephone begins to act: she sends up the souls of the women and scatters them again, and Odysseus fears that she will send the Gorgon's head against him (see the general article).
