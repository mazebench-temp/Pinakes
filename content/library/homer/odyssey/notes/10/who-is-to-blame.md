---
summary: The first disaster of the book is given three causes in three voices. In the tale the folly is “our own” (10.27, 10.79); to Aeolus Odysseus blames his companions and sleep (10.68–69); and Aeolus answers that he comes as one hated by the gods (10.73–75). On Aeaea Eurylochus blames the leader's recklessness for the men the Cyclops ate (10.435–437).
refs: [10.27, 10.68-69, 10.73-75, 10.79, 10.437]
---

**Three voices.** The narrator says it at once: Aeolus' escort “was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (αὐτῶν … ἀφραδίῃσιν, 10.26–27), and again when the men must row, “through our own folly” (ἡμετέρῃ ματίῃ, 10.79; see the translation's table of key words, ἀφραδίη, ματίη). The scholium, as Merry reports it, explains the first person: Odysseus counts himself in out of sympathy, because he had slept, and his sleep gave the crew their chance to satisfy a fatal curiosity (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.79). To Aeolus he names others: “My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (10.68–69; see [[ate]], [[hetairos]] and [[hypnos]]). Aeolus reads the gods in it: “off with you, since it is as one hated by the gods that you come here” (10.75; see [[themis]] and [[aeolus]]).

**The leader blamed.** Zeus' complaint in the first council, that mortals bring sorrows on themselves by their own recklessness and blame the gods (1.32–34), is turned on Odysseus by his own man. Eurylochus says that the companions eaten by the Cyclops perished “through his recklessness” (10.437; see [[atasthalie]] and [[eurylochus]]), the word with which the proem judged the companions themselves (1.7). In Book 11 Elpenor's soul blames “an evil fate from a god … and endless wine” for his fall (11.61, a plain rendering; see [[elpenor]]).
