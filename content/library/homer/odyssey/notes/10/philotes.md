---
summary: The companions call Aeolus' gift “a favor done in friendship” (10.43). Circe, her drug defeated, bids Odysseus to her bed, “so that, having lain together / in bed and love, we may trust one another” (10.334–335), and he goes up to it only when she has sworn the oath (10.336–347).
---

**Friendship.** φιλότης is first the bond of host and guest. The companions, imagining treasure in the skin, grudge Odysseus Aeolus' gifts: “and now these things have been given him, a favor done in friendship, / by Aeolus” (χαριζόμενος φιλότητι, 10.43–44; see [[charis]], [[doron]] and [[xenia]]).

**Love and trust.** Then it is love. Hermes foretells that Circe, in fear of Odysseus' sword, “will bid you lie with her; / then do not refuse the bed of the goddess any longer, / so that she may free your companions and care for you yourself” (10.296–298; see [[hermes]]), and she does: “But come, put your sword in its sheath, and then let us two / go up into our bed, so that, having lain together / in bed and love, we may trust one another” (μιγέντε / εὐνῇ καὶ φιλότητι πεποίθομεν ἀλλήλοισιν, 10.333–335; see [[circe]] and [[bed]]). ἡμετέρης, “our bed”, is used proleptically, and πεποίθομεν is a subjunctive of the perfect πέποιθα (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.334, 10.335). The pair of words is the formula of love, as when Paris reminds Helen how he lay with her “in love and bed” on the island of Cranae (*Iliad* 3.445), and Calypso tells how Demeter lay with Iasion “in love and bed” (5.125–126; see [[calypso]]). Here love is to be the ground of trust, and Odysseus will not trust it without an oath (10.336–347; see [[oath]] and [[marriage]]).
