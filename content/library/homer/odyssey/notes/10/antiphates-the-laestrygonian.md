summary: The king of the Laestrygonians. Odysseus' scouts meet his strong daughter at the spring and are shown his house, where his huge wife calls him from the place of assembly; he seizes one of them for his meal and raises the cry that brings his people down on the ships (10.105–124). On Aeaea the companions weep at the memory of his deeds (10.199).

**The daughter at the spring.** The two men and the herald whom Odysseus sends to find out who lives in the land go up the smooth road by which the wagons bring wood down to the city, and before the city they meet a girl drawing water, “the strong daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian”, who has come down to the spring Artacia (10.103–108; see [[artacia]], [[wagon]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). ἰφθίμη, “strong”, is said of women in the Iliad too (see the translation's note on 10.106). W. W. Merry compares Nausicaa, who drives out with her father's wagon to wash the clothes (6.57–59), and the daughters of Celeus in the Hymn to Demeter, who come for water to carry in bronze pitchers to their father's house (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 105–107; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.105). They ask her “who was king of these people and over whom he ruled”, a double question asking the names of both the king and the people (Merry on 10.110), “and she at once pointed out the high-roofed house of her father” (10.109–111; see [[high-roofed]]).

**The queen.** “And when they had entered the glorious house, they found his wife / as huge as a mountain peak, and they shuddered at her” (10.112–113): στυγεῖν is the word for the horror with which even the gods look on the dank realm of Hades (*Iliad* 20.65; Merry on 10.113). The poem names neither her nor the daughter.

**The king.** “And at once she called from the place of assembly famous Antiphates, / her husband, who devised a grim destruction for them. / At once he seized one of my companions and made ready his meal” (10.114–116; see [[famous]] and [[assembly]]). Having a king and a place of assembly, Merry observes, the Laestrygonians are further advanced in the outward forms of civilization than the Cyclopes (Merry on 10.114; see [[cyclopes]]). The other two dart off and reach the ships, and the king “raised a cry throughout the city”, and the Laestrygonians come “countless, not like men, but like the Giants”, to stone the ships and spear the men (10.117–124; see [[laestrygonians]], [[giants]] and [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). The poem does not say which of the three men he took.

**Remembered.** On Aeaea, when Odysseus tells of the smoke he has seen, his companions' dear heart is broken “as they remembered the deeds of Antiphates the Laestrygonian / and of the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men” (10.198–200; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
