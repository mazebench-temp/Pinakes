---
summary: The Cyclops is remembered twice on Circe's island. The companions weep when they hear of smoke on Aeaea, remembering the Laestrygonian Antiphates and “the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men” (10.198–202); and Eurylochus, refusing to go to Circe's house, tells the adventure again in three verses as a charge against Odysseus: the companions perished in the Cyclops' farmyard “through his recklessness” (10.429–437).
# Unnamed: the memory (10.198-200); Eurylochus' retelling (10.435-437).
refs: [10.198-200, 10.435-437]
---

**The memory.** When Odysseus tells the companions on Aeaea of the smoke in the middle of the island, their dear heart is broken, “as they remembered the deeds of Antiphates the Laestrygonian / and of the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men” (10.198–200; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). Κύκλωπός τε βίης, “the mighty … Cyclops”, is the Iliad's periphrasis of a person's might for the person (see the translation's note on 10.200 and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]); a variant reported in the scholia reads ἀνδροφόνοιο, “man-slaying”, for ἀνδροφάγοιο, “eater of men” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 10.200). μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, may be a standing epithet or mean “stout-hearted”, but it can also mean “arrogant”: the king of the Giants is μεγαλήτωρ (7.58), and so is the headstrong temper of Agamemnon (*Iliad* 9.109; Merry on 10.200; see [[great-hearted]]). The memory of the two man-eaters is the reason for their tears, and the tears avail nothing: “but there was no profit for them in their weeping” (10.201–202; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]).

**Eurylochus' version.** When Odysseus comes back from Circe's house to fetch the rest of the crew, Eurylochus alone tries to hold them back. Circe will make them all swine or wolves or lions, to guard her house for her under compulsion, “as the Cyclops penned them in, when to his farmyard came / our companions, and with them went this bold Odysseus; / for it was through his recklessness that they too perished” (10.431–437; see [[eurylochus]]). Eustathius found the comparison senseless, since the Cyclops did not set his prisoners to guard his cave but ate them; Merry answers that it illustrates above all the compulsion, καὶ ἀνάγκῃ: Eurylochus remembers how the companions entered the Cyclops' dwelling and were kept there fast prisoners, and ἔρξε is from ἔργω, “penned them in”, as at 14.411, not from ἔρδω, “did” (Merry on 10.435; see the translation's note on 10.435). μέσσαυλος, “farmyard”, is in the Iliad the farmyard from which the lion is driven (*Iliad* 11.548, 17.112, 17.657; see [[fold]]), and the half-verse ὅτε οἱ μέσσαυλον ἵκοντο, “when to his farmyard came”, is found elsewhere only of the goddesses who came to the farmyard of Paris (*Iliad* 24.29; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). τούτου γάρ takes up θρασύς, “this bold Odysseus”: “foolhardy, I say, for it was through his infatuation” (10.436–437; Merry on 10.437; see [[atasthalie]]).

**The charge.** The charge is one that Odysseus has already made against himself in telling the adventure, when he would not listen to the companions who begged him to leave the cave: “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—” (9.224–228). Here he meets it in anger: he pondered whether to draw his sword and strike off Eurylochus' head, kinsman by marriage though he was, until the companions held him back (10.438–445). On Thrinacia it is Eurylochus who leads the companions to kill the cattle of the Sun, the recklessness of which the proem speaks (12.339–352; 1.7; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
