---
summary: The Giants are named in a comparison: the Laestrygonians come running at their king's cry, “countless, not like men, but like the Giants”, and stone the ships from the cliffs (10.118–122).
---

**Not like men.** μυρίοι, οὐκ ἄνδρεσσιν ἐοικότες, ἀλλὰ Γίγασιν (10.120): the Laestrygonians come “one from one place and one from another”, countless, and the comparison is in the second half of the verse, set against men (see [[laestrygonians]]). In Book 7 the Giants were a people of the past, “high-hearted” and “savage”, whom their king Eurymedon destroyed with himself, and near to the gods, as the Cyclopes are (7.58–60, 7.205–206); here they are a measure of size and violence for a living people whom Odysseus has met. The stones the Laestrygonians throw are each “a load for a man” (10.121), and their queen is “as huge as a mountain peak” (10.113); W. W. Merry makes the city's name, Telepylus, “big-gated”, gates on a scale for a people “not like men, but like the Giants” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.81; see [[telepylus]]). Pausanias, as Merry quotes him, cited this verse with Alcinous' words on the Giants to show that Homer made them mortal and not a race of gods (see the entry).
