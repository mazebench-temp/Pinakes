**The four.** While Odysseus is with Circe, “the handmaids were busy in the halls, / four of them, who are her serving women in the house” (10.348–349; see the translation's key words). One throws fine purple blankets over the chairs, with linen cloths beneath; the second draws up silver tables before them and sets golden baskets on them; the third mixes wine in a silver bowl and serves out golden cups; and the fourth brings water and kindles a great fire under a tripod (10.352–359; see [[blankets]], [[throne]], [[table]], [[basket]], [[mixing-bowl]], [[cup]] and [[tripod]]). There is a τράπεζα for each chair, not, as Eustathius thought, one table for the meat and another for the drink (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.354).

**Born of springs and groves.** “and they are born of the springs and of the groves / and of the sacred rivers that flow on to the sea” (10.350–351; see [[nymphs]]). Merry thought the two verses a later addition: the present γίγνονται is awkward, and the words read like a description of Naiads, Dryads and Nereids. He reports the scholia's allegory, that Circe is the year and her four maids the seasons, fit attendants for a daughter of the Sun: Spring spreading her carpet of grass and flowers, Summer bringing her store of fruit and corn, Autumn her wine, and Winter lighting the fire (Merry on 10.350–351; see the translation's note on 10.348–351 and [[hours]]).

**The meal.** “And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it, / a fine golden one, over a silver basin”, and “the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before me, / adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had” (10.368–372; see [[hand-washing]], [[basin-and-pitcher]] and [[water-for-the-hands]]). The five verses are those of the meal in Telemachus' house and in Menelaus' (1.136–140, 4.52–56). Köchly, whom Merry quotes, held that they first stood in the fourth book and were borrowed, not badly, for the other places, but are brought in most ineptly in Circe's house, where the four maids have just been seen making the meal ready; La Roche thought them no older than the thirteenth century, since Eustathius and the scholia do not know them (Merry's critical notes on 10.348, 10.368–372; see the translation's note on 10.368–372).

**Circe herself.** When Odysseus brings the rest of the crew, it is Circe who has bathed the companions in her house, anointed them with oil and put woolly cloaks and tunics about them (10.449–451; see [[circe]] and [[bathing]]).
