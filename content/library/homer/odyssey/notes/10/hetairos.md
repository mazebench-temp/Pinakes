---
summary: The companions lose the homecoming twice. They open the skin of the winds within sight of Ithaca, “and the evil counsel of my companions prevailed” (10.34–47); in the Laestrygonians' harbor all the ships but Odysseus' perish, “though we had lost our dear companions” (10.116–134); on Aeaea half of those left are turned into swine and restored (10.203–243, 10.388–399), and after a year it is they who bid Odysseus think of home (10.471–474). The book ends with the first of them to die there, Elpenor (10.551–560).

**The skin.** For nine days Odysseus handles the sheet himself, “and would not give it / to any other of my companions, so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.32–33). When sleep overcomes him the companions talk among themselves of the gold and silver he is bringing home, “while we, who have gone the same road to its end, / are going home, all of us, with empty hands” (10.34–42), σύν meaning “all of us together” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.42). “So they spoke, and the evil counsel of my companions prevailed; / they loosed the skin, and all the winds rushed out” (10.46–47; see [[skin-of-the-winds]], [[odysseus-and-aeolus]], [[boule]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The storm carries them off “weeping”, and on the Aeolian island again “my companions groaned” (10.48–55); there were several ships together, as αἱ δέ shows (Merry on 10.56). To Aeolus Odysseus puts the blame on them and on sleep, “My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (10.68–69; see [[ate]] and [[hypnos]]), but in his tale the rowing that follows wears down “the men's spirit … through our own folly” (10.78–79): the scholium on ἡμετέρῃ, as Merry reports it, says that he counts himself in out of sympathy, since it was his sleep that gave the crew their chance (Merry on 10.79; see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The Laestrygonians.** He sends two companions and a herald to find out what men live there (10.100–102; see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[heralds]]); Antiphates seizes one of them “and made ready his meal”, and the other two escape to the ships (10.116–117; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]). The Laestrygonians stone the ships in the harbor and spear the men like fish; Odysseus cuts his cable and bids his companions “lay to their oars” (10.121–130), “but the others perished there in a body” (10.132; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). The refrain counts what is left: “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (10.133–134 = 9.565–566; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]).

**Aeaea.** On Aeaea he keeps them alive. He brings down the stag and rouses them “with soothing words, standing beside each man” (10.172–173), where ἄνδρα ἕκαστον gives the collective ἑταίρους a distributive form (Merry on 10.173; see [[soothing-words]] and [[deer]]), and bids them eat, “for all our grief” (10.174–177). In the morning he tells them what he has seen, and their dear heart is broken as they remember Antiphates and the Cyclops (10.188–202). He counts them off “into two bands” and gives one to Eurylochus; the lot leaps out for Eurylochus, “and with him went two and twenty companions, / weeping; and they left us behind them lamenting” (10.203–209; see [[lots]] and [[eurylochus]]). Polites, “the nearest and dearest to me of my companions”, speaks first at Circe's door (10.224–228; see [[polites-the-companion]]); all but Eurylochus go in, drink the drug and are penned in the sties as swine (10.229–243; see [[circe]], [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[shape-changing]]). Hermes tells Odysseus that they “are penned like swine, and keep to close-packed sties” (10.282–283), and Odysseus will not eat at Circe's table until he has seen them free: no man who was fair-minded would taste food “before he had set his companions free and seen them with his eyes” (10.383–387; see [[fasting]]), λύσασθαι, the middle, of his own act and λῦσον of Circe's (Merry on 10.385). She frees them, and each of them clings to his hands (10.388–399).

**The trusty companions.** Odysseus brings the rest from the ship, “my trusty companions” (ἐρίηρας ἑταίρους, 10.387, 10.405, 10.408; see [[trusty]]), who pour round him in tears as calves run lowing round their mothers coming home from pasture (10.408–420). Only Eurylochus holds back, calling the journey to Circe's house the folly of the Cyclops' cave again (10.429–437; see [[atasthalie]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]); when Odysseus thinks of killing him, the companions hold him back “with soothing words, one from one side and one from another” (10.441–445), the verse with which they tried to stop him taunting the Cyclops (9.493). A year later it is the companions who call him out: “Strange one, think now at last of your native land, / if it is decreed for you to be saved and to reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (10.471–474; see [[strange-one]], [[thesphaton]] and [[nostos]]). He asks Circe to keep her promise, for his heart is eager, “and the hearts of my other companions, who waste my dear heart away / mourning around me, whenever you are away” (10.484–486), where ἑτάρων goes closely with θυμός rather than with the dative μοι (Merry on 10.484).

**Elpenor.** “But not even from there did I lead my companions away unharmed” (10.551), οὐδὲ μὲν οὐδʼ standing for ἀλλʼ οὐ μὴν οὐδέ (Merry on 10.551). Elpenor, the youngest, had lain down “apart from my companions” on Circe's roof, and falls to his death (10.552–560; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). Odysseus goes through the house to rouse the rest, for he slept, Merry supposes, in the inner part of the palace and they in the porticoes (on 10.546). When they hear where they must go, their dear heart is broken again, and they sit and lament and tear their hair (10.566–568; see [[lament]]).
