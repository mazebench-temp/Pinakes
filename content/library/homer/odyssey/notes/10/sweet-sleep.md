---
summary: Off Ithaca “sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (10.31), and at the end of the book Odysseus wakes his companions: “No longer sleep now, drowsing in sweet sleep” (10.548).
---

The first sleep lets the companions open the skin of the winds (10.31–47), and Odysseus blames it to Aeolus as “cruel sleep” (10.68–69; [[hard]], [[hypnos]]). For ἀωτεῖτε, “drowsing,” Merry suggests the root of ἄημι, “breathing sleep's deep breath,” and reports the scholia's “plucking the flower of sleep,” like Latin *carpere somnos* (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.548; see the translation's note on 10.548). Elpenor, asleep apart on the roof, wakes to the din and falls (10.552–560; [[elpenor]]).
