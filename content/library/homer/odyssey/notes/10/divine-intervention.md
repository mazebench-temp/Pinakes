summary: The tale names the gods behind the book's turns, and leaves some unnamed: Zeus made Aeolus dispenser of the winds (10.21); “some god guided us” into the harbor of Aeaea (10.141); “one of the gods pitied me” and sent the stag (10.157–158); Hermes comes in person with the moly (10.277–306), and had foretold Odysseus' coming to Circe (10.330–332).
refs: [10.141, 10.157-158, 10.277-279, 10.330-332]
---

As in Book 9, Odysseus tells his story as a man who sees the gods' hands, now named and now not. Aeolus manages the winds by the grant of Zeus, “for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (10.21–22; see [[aeolus]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[zeus]]), and when the winds have driven Odysseus back, Aeolus reads in it the hatred of the gods (10.73–75; see [[themis]]). After the Laestrygonians the one ship comes in to Aeaea “in silence, / into a harbor where ships can lie, and some god guided us” (10.140–141), the half-verse of the landing on the island of goats on a night without light (9.142; see [[harbor]] and [[aeaea]]). On the way back from the lookout, “one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was, / and sent a great high-horned stag into my very path” (10.157–158; see [[pity]] and [[deer]]).

The god who comes in person is Hermes, “in the likeness of a young man”, with the drug and the counsel that save Odysseus from Circe (10.277–306; see [[hermes]], [[moly]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). Circe knows from the failure of her drug who her guest must be, for Hermes “always told me / you would come” (10.330–332; see [[prophecy]]). Then Circe herself, a goddess, frees the companions, gives the instructions for the journey, and sends the North Wind to carry the ship (10.388–399, 10.488–540; see [[circe]] and [[boreas]]).
