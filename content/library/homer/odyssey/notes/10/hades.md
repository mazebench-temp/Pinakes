---
summary: The house of Hades is the end of Book 10. On the shore of Aeaea Odysseus tells his companions that they shall not go down to it yet; after a year Circe tells him that before he goes home he must go there alive, to “the house of Hades and of dread Persephone”, across Oceanus to “the dank house of Hades”, and pray “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone”; and Elpenor, falling from her roof, goes down before him.
# Ἀίδεω (10.512) and Ἀίδῃ (10.534) are claimed by the entry, with Ἀίδαο, Ἄϊδος and Ἄϊδόσδε.
passages:
  - 10.488-540 | Circe sends Odysseus to the house of Hades
---

**Not yet.** When Odysseus has carried the great stag to the ship, he rouses his despairing companions: “Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief, / into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us; / but come, as long as there is food and drink in the swift ship, / let us think of eating” (10.174–177; see [[odysseus]] and [[deer]]). εἰς Ἀίδαο δόμους is the Iliad's phrase for death, in Andromache's lament: “now you go to the house of Hades beneath the depths of the earth” (*Iliad* 22.482), and μόρσιμον ἦμαρ, the “fated day”, is the day of Hector's death that Pallas Athena was already rousing against him (*Iliad* 15.613–614).

**Going there alive.** When the year is over and Odysseus begs Circe to send him home, she answers that he must first go there: “But first you must accomplish another journey and come / to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (10.490–492; see [[circe]], [[persephone]] and [[teiresias]]). The house is theirs together, as in Hesiod, whose dog keeps watch before the echoing halls “of mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (*Theogony* 767–773). Odysseus weeps and no longer wishes to live (10.496–499; see [[helios]]), and asks: “Circe, who will guide us on this journey? / No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship” (10.501–502). εἰς Ἄϊδος is “to the house of Hades”, the genitive of the god's name with δόμον understood: Ἀΐδης in Homer is always a person and not a place (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 10.502; see the translation's key-word row and its note on 10.502). He tells his companions of the journey in the same verses (10.564–565 = 10.491–492), and on their return Circe greets them as men “twice-dying”, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades (12.21–22, a plain rendering; see the general article).

**The dank house beyond Oceanus.** Circe's way leads across Oceanus to the groves of Persephone: “beach your ship there by deep-eddying Oceanus, / and go yourself into the dank house of Hades” (10.511–512; see [[oceanus]] and [[dank]]). εὐρώεις, “dank”, is said of the dwellings of Hades in the Iliad too, “terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (*Iliad* 20.65). Merry finds it hard to realize the Homeric conception of the kingdom. In general the place of the dead is beneath the earth: Andromache wishes “to go down beneath the earth” (*Iliad* 6.411), the house of Hades is “beneath the depths of the earth” (*Iliad* 22.482; in the Odyssey, 24.204), and the soul of Patroclus “went beneath the earth like smoke” (*Iliad* 23.100). But the voyage on which Circe sends Odysseus puts a very different picture before us: he sails across the ocean stream to the shore of the groves of Persephone, finds the Cimmerians beyond the west, and digs his pit there, still on the surface of the earth, without any descent into the depths like that of Heracles, Orpheus or Peirithous (on 10.512). ἔνθα, “there”, where the rivers meet, must mean at the entrance to the house of Hades (on 10.513; see [[acheron]], [[pyriphlegethon]], [[cocytus]] and [[styx]]).

**Mighty Hades.** After the sacrifice of the ram and the black ewe, the companions are to flay and burn the victims “and pray to the gods, / to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (10.533–534; see [[mighty]], [[dread]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]), and Book 11 repeats the verses in the doing (11.46–47). ἴφθιμος, “mighty”, is Hades' epithet in Hesiod as well (*Theogony* 455), and Hesiod has the whole verse in the genitive, ἰφθίμου τʼ Ἀίδεω καὶ ἐπαινῆς Περσεφονείης (*Theogony* 768; see [[hesiod]]).

**Elpenor's soul.** On the last morning the youngest of the companions, asleep on Circe's roof, wakes at the noise and forgets to come down by the ladder, “but fell straight down from the roof; and his neck / was broken from the vertebrae, and his soul went down to Hades” (10.559–560; see [[elpenor]] and [[psyche]]). Ἄϊδόσδε, “to Hades”, is found only in the Odyssey (see the general article and the note on Book 3); his soul tells the death again in the same words when it meets Odysseus first among the dead (11.64–65).
