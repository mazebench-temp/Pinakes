---
summary: Strabo finds history under this book's fables: Aeolus a ruler of the Liparaean islands, whose title of dispenser of the winds hides the islanders' forecasting of the winds; the Laestrygonians an inhospitable people of Sicily; Circe shaped after Medea and Aeaea named after Aea. He describes the Circaeum of Latium, with its temple of Circe and a cup said to be Odysseus', and the lake Avernus, where the Homeric Nekyia was placed, with its Pyriphlegethon and a spring shunned as the water of Styx.
---

- **Aeolus and the Liparaean islands.** Homer, Strabo says, took the beginnings of the wandering from history: Aeolus is said to have ruled the islands around Lipara, and inhospitable men, the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians, the country around Aetna and Leontini (*Geography* 1.2.9). The seven Liparaean islands are those “which some call the islands of Aeolus”, and on Strongyle “they say that Aeolus lived” (*Geography* 6.2.10–11, plain renderings; also *Geography* 2.5.19); Merry quotes the second when he notes that the legend was later moved to the Liparaean or Aeolian islands (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.1). From Polybius Strabo takes the reason for the title of dispenser of the winds (10.21): Aeolus was a king who foretold the times for sailing out of the strait, where the currents turn, and from the roaring of the craters on Hiera the islanders could foretell the wind that would blow on the third day, so that the poet's most fabulous saying hints at the truth (*Geography* 1.2.15 and 6.2.10): [[aeolus]], [[aeolia]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[polybius]].
- **The cobbler of the skin.** Eratosthenes said that one would find where Odysseus had wandered when one found the cobbler who stitched the skin of the winds; Polybius, Strabo reports, did not approve the saying (*Geography* 1.2.15): [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[polybius]].
- **The Laestrygonians.** Those who take the poet's elaboration for history are wrong, Strabo says, for he plainly writes of marvels: Ocean and Hades, the cattle of the Sun, entertainment by goddesses and transformations, the sizes of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians, the shape of Scylla and the distances of the voyage (*Geography* 1.2.11). Odysseus' arrivals are landings on a coast: he says that he came to the land of the Cyclopes because he went from the sea as far as the cave, and that he came to Aeolia and to the Laestrygonians wherever he happened to anchor (*Geography* 1.2.32): [[laestrygonians]], [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]] and [[shape-changing]].
- **Circe and Medea.** Homer knew of Jason's voyage to Aea and of the stories told about Circe and Medea and their drugs, and made the two kin, though they lived far apart, one in the recess of the Pontus and the other in Italy; so the name Aeaea was suggested by Aea (*Geography* 1.2.10). He agrees with history when he names Aeetes and models the sorceress Circe on Medea, calling her “own sister of baleful Aeetes” (αὐτοκασιγνήτην ὀλοόφρονος Αἰήταο, *Geography* 1.2.40, quoting 10.137); his mythical addition is to move out into the Ocean the wandering that followed Jason's voyage. Merry sets the same two forms of the story, an eastern Circe of the Argonauts' legend and a western one, side by side (Merry on 10.135): [[circe]], [[aeetes]] and [[aeaea]].
- **The Circaeum.** On the coast of Latium, past Antium, the Circaeum is a mountain made an island by the sea and the marshes; “they say that it has many roots, perhaps fitting it to the myth of Circe”, and it has a small town, a temple of Circe and an altar of Athena, and they say a cup of Odysseus is shown there (*Geography* 5.3.6, a plain rendering), the passage Merry quotes for Circe's later home on Cape Circaeum (Merry on 10.135). On the larger of two islets off the coast of Attica, the Pharmacussae, the tomb of Circe is shown (*Geography* 9.1.13): [[circe]] and [[drugs]].
- **The Nekyia at Avernus.** Strabo asks what poet or historian persuaded the people of Cyme, Dicaearchia and Vesuvius to speak of a Pyriphlegethon, an Acherusian lake and an oracle of the dead at Avernus, and of Baius and Misenus as companions of Odysseus (*Geography* 1.2.18). Earlier writers placed the Homeric Nekyia at Avernus, a deep gulf shut in by steep brows once covered with wild forest; they told of an oracle of the dead there, which Odysseus visited, and of men who sailed in after sacrificing to the powers below; a spring of drinking water by the sea was shunned by all as the water of Styx, and the Pyriphlegethon was inferred from the hot waters near the Acherusian lake. When Agrippa cut down the forest and the place was built over, all this was shown to be myth (*Geography* 5.4.5). Another river Acheron, in Thesprotia, flows out of an Acherusian lake into the Sweet Harbor (*Geography* 7.7.5): [[pyriphlegethon]], [[acheron]], [[styx]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]].
- **The tomb of Teiresias.** Near Haliartus the spring Tilphossa flows below the mountain Tilphossium, and by it is the tomb of Teiresias, who died there during the flight (*Geography* 9.2.27 and 9.2.36). Merry gives the same story, that he died near Haliartus after the war of the Epigoni and that his tomb was shown there in Pausanias' time (Merry on 10.492): [[teiresias]] and [[pausanias]].
