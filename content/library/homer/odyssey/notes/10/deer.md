summary: On Aeaea a god sends a great high-horned stag into Odysseus' path as he comes back from the lookout; he kills it with his spear, binds its feet with a cable of willow shoots and carries it on his neck to the ship, where his companions, who had lain two days in grief, gaze at it and feast on it until sunset (10.156–186).

**The stag.** Odysseus has climbed to a lookout on Aeaea and seen the smoke of Circe's house, and he decides to go back to the ship first and give his companions their meal (10.144–155; see [[aeaea]] and [[odysseus]]). “then one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was, / and sent a great high-horned stag into my very path. / It was coming down to the river from its pasture in the woods / to drink; for the might of the sun had hold of it” (10.157–160; see [[pity]] and [[helios]]). εἰς ὁδὸν αὐτήν is “just on my path” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.158). ὑψίκερως, “high-horned”, is found only here in Homer; the stag of the Iliad's similes is κεραός, “antlered” (*Iliad* 3.24, 16.158; see [[antlered]]). ἐκ νομοῦ ὕλης is “from its pasture in the woods”, νομοῦ a word of place and ὕλης explaining it (Merry on 10.159; see [[trees]]). πιόμενος is a future participle, “to drink”, and the sun's might “had hold of it” as sleep or panic has hold of a man; Zenodotus read δήν, “long”, for δή (Merry on 10.160 and his critical note). The god who sends the beast is not named, as on the island of goats, where “the god gave us game to satisfy the heart” (9.158; see [[goats]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

**The kill.** “And as it came out, I struck it on the spine, in the middle of the back, / and the bronze spear passed straight through, / and it fell down in the dust with a cry, and its spirit flew away” (10.161–163; see [[spear]]). ἄκνηστις is the spine, and μέσα νῶτα explains it. Merry connects the word with the root of ἄκανθα, “thorn”, as English “spine” goes with Latin *spina*, and reports the derivation of the scholia and of Hesychius, from the negative prefix and κνήσασθαι, “scratch”, because the middle of the back is the place that an animal cannot scratch (on 10.161; see the translation's row ἄκνηστις; καταλοφάδεια). The third verse is a formula of the fall: it is said of the trace horse Pedasus and of the boar that wounded the young Odysseus on Parnassus, and, with a comic turn, of the beggar Irus (*Iliad* 16.469; 19.454; 18.98; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]). μακών, “with a cry”, is a sound-word, from μηκάομαι, and its tense gives a single loud cry (Merry on 10.163). Odysseus sets his foot on the body to pull out the spear, then lays the spear on the ground and leaves it there, to have his hands free (10.164–166; Merry on 10.164).

**On his neck.** “then I plucked brushwood and willow shoots, / and plaiting a cable, about a fathom's length, well twisted from both ends, / I bound together the feet of the dread monster” (10.166–168; see [[willow]]). ὅσον τʼ ὄργυιαν, “about a fathom's length”, has the construction of the Cyclops' club, ὅσσον θʼ ἱστόν, “as great as a mast” (Merry on 10.167, referring to his note on 9.322; see [[the-mast-of-twenty-oars]]), and ἀμφοτέρωθεν goes with πλεξάμενος: the cable is plaited of two strands passing from side to side, “across and across”, until it is well twisted (Merry on 10.167). The stag is “the dread monster”, δεινοῖο πελώρου, the words used of the Gorgon's head (*Iliad* 5.741; 11.634). He carries it καταλοφάδεια, “on my neck”, an accusative plural used as an adverb, from κατά and λόφος, “neck”, as κατωμάδιος from κατά and ὦμος, “shoulder” (*Iliad* 23.431; Merry on 10.169), “leaning on my spear, since there was no way to carry it on the shoulder / with one hand; for it was a very great beast” (10.170–171). Four manuscripts divide the word in two, but the scholion bids read it as one (Merry's critical note on 10.169). θηρίον, “beast”, is said in Homer only of this stag (10.171, 10.180; see the translation's row θηρίον).

**The meal.** He throws it down before the ship and rouses his companions with soothing words: “Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief, / into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us” (10.172–175; see [[hades]]). They had covered their heads in their despair, as Odysseus had covered his when the winds broke loose (10.53; Merry on 10.179), “and uncovering their heads, along the shore of the barren sea / they gazed at the stag; for it was a very great beast” (10.179–180); θηήσαντο is “stared at”, with wonder in it (Merry on 10.180). Then they wash their hands and make ready a glorious feast, and all day long, until the sun goes down, they sit “feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (10.181–186; see [[feast]] and [[sunset-lines]]).
