**The first landfall.** No days are counted for the crossing from the land of the Cyclopes, although Homer gives the number of days of a long voyage (9.82, 10.28, 10.80): the island, Merry infers, lay at no great distance from them, with open sea between it and Ithaca, and to the west of Ithaca, since Aeolus sends Odysseus straight home with the West Wind alone (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.1; see [[aeolia]]). The month's welcome is told in three verses, the host's questions and the guest's answer: Aeolus asked about “Ilios and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans”, and Odysseus “told him all of it fair and right” (10.14–16; see [[nostos]] and [[xenia]]). τῶν, “their” city and house, takes in the whole family, but Aeolus, as head of the household, is the one who “made me welcome” (10.13–14; Merry on 10.13). Merry prints αὐτὰρ ἐγώ at 10.16, where Murray prints καὶ μὲν ἐγώ (see the translation's note on 10.16); with his reading the verse differs only in its pronoun from the one in which Odysseus tells Circe of the journey to the dead (12.35, Greek only).

**The escort.** καὶ ἐγὼν … ᾔτεον, “I in my turn asked”, after Aeolus had finished his questioning, and ὁδόν is leave to depart (10.17; Merry on 10.17). The escort that Aeolus makes ready (τεῦχε δὲ πομπήν, 10.18) is a wind; when it is gone, “no escort showed itself any longer” (10.79), and πομπή there means the wind, the means by which the journey is accomplished (Merry on 10.79; see [[pompe]]). ἐκδείρας tells how Aeolus came by the skin, not when he gave it (10.19; Merry on 10.19; see the translation's note on 10.19 and [[skin-of-the-winds]]). Aeolus is not in Homer the king of the winds that he is in Virgil, but their manager by permission of Zeus, and Athena, Calypso and Circe also send a favoring wind when they please (Merry on 10.21; see [[aeolus]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[the-winds]]): Circe sends one behind the ship when it leaves for the house of Hades (11.6–8). The silver cord that ties the skin is wire fine enough to plait into a cord, drawn so tight that not a breath could slip past (10.23–24; Merry on 10.23).

**Nine days.** The ships sail ὁμῶς … νύκτας τε καὶ ἦμαρ, “night and day alike”, instead of running for a harbor or beaching every night, as was the custom (10.28; Merry on 10.28); the six days' rowing to Telepylus is told in the same verse, with six days for nine (10.80). Merry points to the tenses: the imperfects of the voyage and of the sight of land (πλέομεν, ἀνεφαίνετο, ἐλεύσσομεν) against the aorist of the sleep that ends them (ἐπήλυθε, 10.28–31; on 10.28). Of the men tending fires (πυρπολέοντας, 10.30) he thinks, since day had dawned, that the ship had been sighted and a fire lit to guide her in, or else that a fire on a farm shows how near they had come (on 10.30, comparing the fire seen by sailors in *Iliad* 19.375–378; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]). Odysseus had handled the sheet himself, πόδα νηὸς ἐνώμων (10.32): running before the wind, he had only to trim the sail to make the most of it (Merry on 10.32; see [[sails]] and the translation's key-word row for πόδα νηὸς νωμάω).

**The companions' counsel.** The talk is what a man would say to his neighbor (10.37 = 8.328), and its subject is gifts: Aeolus' gold and silver (10.35–36, 10.45) and the treasures from the spoil of Troy (10.40–41), against their own empty hands, σὺν χεῖρας, “all of us together” (10.42; Merry on 10.42). ἐκ Τροίης is “from the land of Troy”: Aristarchus took Τροίης as an adjective with ληίδος, “Trojan spoil” (10.40; Merry on 10.40; see the translation's note on 10.40). “The evil counsel of my companions prevailed”: νίκησεν, “carried the day”, with ἑταίρων depending on βουλή (10.46; Merry on 10.46; see [[boule]] and [[hetairos]]). Odysseus woke and covered his head (10.53), καλυψάμενος, the sign of abandonment to grief (Merry on 10.53, comparing 8.92); the companions on Aeaea will be found muffled in the same despair (10.179; Merry on 10.179).

**Back to Aeolus.** ἠπείρου is only the coast of the island (10.56), and the ships came back together, as αἱ δέ shows (10.54): Odysseus led the way in his own (Merry on 10.56). The landing and the meal are told in the verses of the land of the Lotus-eaters (10.56–58 = 9.85–87; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). Odysseus takes a herald and one companion (10.59) and sits on the threshold (10.62): a more modest posture than at the house of Alcinous, where he went up the hall and sat by the hearth, and the one he takes as a beggar in his own house (Merry on 10.62, comparing 7.153 and 17.339; see [[threshold]] and [[supplication]]). He answers καθαπτόμενος, “approaching them” with gentle words (10.70); Zenodotus read μαλακοῖσιν ἀμειβόμενος, which the scholion preferred, “for he does not reproach him, but supplicates him” (Merry's critical note on 10.70; see the translation's note on 10.70). In his blame of “cruel sleep”, σχέτλιος is used as the Latin *improbus* (10.69; Merry on 10.69; see [[hypnos]]).

**Off with you.** ἔρρε is “Off with you”, and θᾶσσον, “quickly”, more speedily, Merry explains, than the suppliant's present mood seems to imply (10.72; Merry on 10.72; see the translation's key-word row for ἔρρε). ἐλέγχιστε ζωόντων, “most despised of the living”: the Iliad has the superlative three times, of the shame that Agamemnon would bear and of the taunt that Hyperenor threw at Menelaus (*Iliad* 2.285, 4.171, 17.26). The answer turns on θέμις (10.73; see [[themis]]): Aeolus will not care for or escort a man hated by the gods, and the second coming is the sign of that hatred (10.73–75). Of ἡμετέρῃ ματίῃ, “through our own folly” (10.79), Merry takes the scholion's sense, “our folly and error”, not Nitzsch's “our fruitless endeavour”: the scholion rightly explains that Odysseus includes himself out of fellow feeling, because he had fallen asleep; the sleep of Odysseus gave the crew the opportunity of satisfying their fatal curiosity (on 10.79; see [[atasthalie]]).
