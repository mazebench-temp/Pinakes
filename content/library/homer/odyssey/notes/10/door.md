summary: Odysseus and his herald sit down at Aeolus' house beside the doorposts, on the threshold (10.62). At Circe's house the companions stand in the doorway, and Odysseus at the doors; three times Circe comes out and opens the shining doors and calls them in (10.220, 10.230, 10.256, 10.310–312), and at last she opens the doors of the sty (10.389).

**Aeolus' doorway.** Driven back to the Aeolian island, Odysseus goes to the house: “And coming into the house, beside the doorposts, on the threshold / we sat down” (10.62–63; see [[threshold]] and [[aeolus]]).

**Circe's doors.** The companions sent with Eurylochus “stood in the doorway of the goddess of the lovely tresses” (10.220; see [[of-the-lovely-tresses]] and [[circe]]). ἐν προθύροισι, Aristarchus' reading, seems to mean “at the door of the courtyard”: Merry quotes Rumpf, who argues that it was the gate of the court that was shut, as in Orestes' knocking at the gate in the *Choephori* and the visitors who stand in the πρόθυρον before the door is opened in Plato's *Protagoras*; nearly all the manuscripts read εἰνὶ θύρῃσι (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.220 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.220). “And at once she came out and opened the shining doors / and called them in” (10.230–231): the verses are said of the companions, again in Eurylochus' report, and of Odysseus himself, who “stood at the doors of the goddess” and shouted (10.256–257, 10.310–313; see [[phaeinos]]). Each time the doors open on the same welcome, and only Odysseus is proof against what lies behind them (see [[drugs]]).

**The doors of the sty.** At his demand Circe goes out through the hall “with her wand in her hand, and opened the doors of the sty” (10.389; see [[sties]] and [[wand]]).
