---
summary: In Book 10 the winds are shut in a skin. Aeolus, whom the son of Cronus made their dispenser, binds up “the paths of the blustering winds” in the skin of an ox and leaves the West Wind alone to blow; within sight of Ithaca the companions loose the skin, “and all the winds rushed out”, and the storm carries the ships back to his island. At the end of the book Circe promises Odysseus the North Wind to carry his ship to Oceanus.

**The paths of the winds.** When Odysseus asks to be sent home, Aeolus gives him the skin of an ox nine years old, “and in it he bound up the paths of the blustering winds; / for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (10.19–22; see [[aeolus]], [[skin-of-the-winds]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[zeus]]). βυκτάων, “blustering”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row). κατέδησε κέλευθα, “bound up the paths”, is said of Athena when she stills the winds of Poseidon's storm: “She bound up the paths of the other winds, / and bade them all cease and lie down to rest” (5.383–384); and Odysseus says of Poseidon that he “roused the winds against me and bound up my path” (7.272). Aeolus binds them in earnest. He ties the skin fast in the ship “with a shining cord / of silver, so that not even a little breath might slip past”, and sends the West Wind alone to carry the ships home (10.23–26; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.23; see [[zephyrus]]).

**All the winds rushed out.** On the tenth day, within sight of Ithaca, the companions untie the skin to see “how much gold and silver is in the skin” (10.45): “they loosed the skin, and all the winds rushed out. / And at once the storm wind seized them and carried them out to sea, / weeping, away from their native land” (10.47–49; see [[hetairos]] and [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]). Odysseus wakes, and the ships “were borne by the evil storm of wind / back to the Aeolian island” (10.54–55). κακῇ ἀνέμοιο θυέλλῃ is the storm of Helen's wish in the Iliad, that on the day she was born “an evil storm of wind had carried me away” (*Iliad* 6.346); and Zeus roused from the mountains of Ida “a storm of wind / that carried the dust straight at the ships” (*Iliad* 12.253–254). When Odysseus tells Penelope the story, the winds are a storm that “snatched him up again and carried him over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily” (23.316–317, a plain rendering).

**The North Wind to Oceanus.** For the journey to the dead Circe promises a wind instead of a guide: “set up your mast and spread the white sails, / and sit; and the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.506–507; see [[boreas]]). Merry takes the subjunctive φέρῃσιν to express, as it were, the North Wind's intention (on 10.507), and he notes that the wind sends the ship on a southwesterly course from Circe's island (on 10.512; see [[oceanus]]). In Book 11 the wind comes from Circe herself: she sends behind the ship “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion” (11.7; again 12.149), as Calypso had sent Odysseus off with a fair wind (5.268; see [[circe]]).
