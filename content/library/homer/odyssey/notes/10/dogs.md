---
summary: The dogs of Book 10 are in a simile: Circe's bewitched wolves and lions fawn on Odysseus' men as dogs fawn around their master when he comes from a feast, “for he always brings them tidbits to please their hearts” (10.216–219).
---

“And as when dogs fawn around their master as he comes from a feast, / for he always brings them tidbits to please their hearts, / so the strong-clawed wolves and lions fawned / around these men” (10.216–219; see [[the-dogs-fawning-on-their-master]], [[wolves]] and [[lion]]). They are house dogs, fed from their master's hand: ἄναξ is here only the master of the house (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.216; see [[anax]]), and the Iliad knows such “dogs that fed at his table” (τραπεζῆες κύνες, *Iliad* 23.173). σαίνω, “fawn”, is a verb of the Odyssey's dogs: Eumaeus' dogs will fawn around Telemachus instead of barking (16.4–10), and old Argos will wag his tail when he knows his master (17.301–302); both are plain renderings.
