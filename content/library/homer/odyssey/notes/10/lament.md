summary: Those left at the ship lament the companions who go to Circe's house (10.209); Eurylochus comes back unable to speak, “and his heart was set on lamenting” (10.248). The restored companions and Odysseus weep together until Circe bids them “Rouse this swelling lament no longer” (10.398–399, 10.453–457), and at the news of the journey to Hades they sit and lament and tear their hair (10.566–568).
# γοόωντας (10.209), γόος (10.398), γόων (10.567), each only here in the Odyssey.
grc: [γοόωντας, γόος, γόων]
---

**Partings.** The two bands part in tears: Eurylochus' twenty-two go “weeping; and they left us behind them lamenting” (γοόωντας, 10.208–209; see [[lots]] and [[tears]]). Eurylochus comes back alone and cannot speak a word, “stricken at heart with great grief; and his eyes / were filled with tears, and his heart was set on lamenting” (γόον δʼ ὠίετο θυμός, 10.247–248; see [[achos]]). The thought of his heart was to cry aloud, but no sound came to his lips, only the tears to his eyes, as the same verse says of the suitors in their foreboding in Book 20 (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.248; 20.349; see the translation's table of key words).

**Reunions.** When Circe has restored the companions and they know Odysseus, “on them all came the longing for lament, and all round the house / rang terribly; and the goddess herself was moved to pity” (ἱμερόεις ὑπέδυ γόος, 10.398–399; see [[pity]] and [[himeros]]), a variation on the verse “in them all he roused the longing for lament” (ὑφʼ ἵμερον ὦρσε γόοιο, 4.183; *Iliad* 23.108). The two bands meet again in her house: “they wept and mourned, and the house groaned round about” (10.453–454), until Circe stops them: “Rouse this swelling lament no longer” (θαλερὸν γόον, 10.457; see the translation's table of key words, θαλερὸς γόος). θαλερόν is to be understood, Eustathius says, as in θαλερὸν δάκρυ, of what is ceaseless and ever fresh; Aristophanes read στυγερόν (Merry on 10.457 and his critical note).

**Departure.** Odysseus tells Circe that his companions “waste my dear heart away / mourning around me, whenever you are away” (ὀδυρόμενοι, 10.485–486; see [[hetairos]]), and when he tells them that they must go to the house of Hades, “they sat down there and lamented, and tore their hair; / but there was no profit for them in their weeping” (γόων τίλλοντό τε χαίτας, 10.567–568; see [[prexis]]), as Andromache and Hecuba tore their hair over the body of Hector (*Iliad* 24.710–711).
