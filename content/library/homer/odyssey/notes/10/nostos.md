summary: Aeolus asks about the homecoming of the Achaeans and sends Odysseus home with the winds bound, and Ithaca is in sight when the companions loose them (10.14–49). Hermes warns him that he will not come home himself if he goes to Circe unprotected (10.284–285); the companions greet him as if they had come home to Ithaca (10.415–420), and after a year call him to his homecoming (10.472–474). Circe sends him first to Teiresias, who will tell him “the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming” (10.490–540).

**The homecoming of the Achaeans.** For a month Aeolus questions Odysseus about “Ilios and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans” (10.14–15; see [[aeolus]]), the theme that Phemius sang in Ithaca, “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.326–327; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Home in sight.** With the winds bound in the skin and the West Wind blowing, “on the tenth our native fields were already coming into sight, / and already we could see men tending fires, we were so near” (10.29–30). Merry would rather see in the fires some work on the farm, or a fire lit to guide in a ship that had been sighted, than the shepherds' watchfires, since day had dawned: they are put in the picture to show how near the men had come to home (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.30; see [[ithaca]] and the translation's table of key words, πυρπολέω). Odysseus kept the sheet himself “so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.33; see [[dear-native-land]]), and the companions, thinking him rich, complain that they are “going home, all of us, with empty hands” (10.42). The winds carry them off “away from their native land” (10.49; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]).

**Forgetting.** Circe mixes her drugs into the food “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.236), as the lotus made men “forget their homecoming” (9.97; see [[thelgo]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]).

**Not coming home.** Hermes meets Odysseus going alone to Circe's house: “Is it to free them that you come here? I tell you that you / will not come home yourself, but will stay where the others are” (10.284–285; see [[hermes]]).

**As if at home.** When Odysseus comes back to the ship the companions pour round him, “and it seemed to their hearts / as if they had come to their native land and to the very city / of rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised” (10.415–417), and they say it: “At your return, you who are nurtured by Zeus, we are as glad / as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land” (10.419–420), the dative participle as in χαῖρον νοστήσαντι, “they rejoiced at his return” (19.463, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.419).

**The year and the call.** After a year of feasting it is the companions who call him out: “Strange one, think now at last of your native land, / if it is decreed for you to be saved and to reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (10.472–474; see [[thesphaton]] and [[hetairos]]), and he asks Circe to keep her promise, “to send me home” (10.483–484; see [[pompe]]).

**Another journey.** Circe answers that he must first “accomplish another journey” to the house of Hades (10.490–491), where Teiresias “will tell you the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea” (νόστον, 10.539–540; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]), in the verses of Eidothea's promise to Menelaus of what her father would tell him (4.389–390; see [[proteus]]). The companions, setting out, think they are going home, and he tells them otherwise: “You think, no doubt, that you are going home to your dear native land; / but Circe has ordained another journey for us” (10.562–563).
