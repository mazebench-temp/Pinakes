summary: The leader of the second band, on whom the lot falls. He alone stays outside Circe's house, suspecting a trick, and comes back to the ship too stricken to speak; he tells how the others vanished and begs Odysseus not to take him there again. When the crew is called to Circe's house he tries to hold them back, calling the venture as reckless as the Cyclops' cave, and Odysseus nearly strikes off his head; he follows for fear of his rebuke (10.203–273, 10.429–448).

**The lot.** Odysseus divides the companions into two bands and gives each a leader, “one I led myself, and godlike Eurylochus the other”, and the lot that leaps out of the bronze-fitted helmet is “the lot of great-hearted Eurylochus” (10.203–207; see [[lots]], [[helmet]], [[godlike]] and [[great-hearted]]): the lots were pebbles shaken in a helmet, and the one that leaped out first decided (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.207). He sets out with two and twenty companions, weeping, and leaves the others lamenting (10.208–209).

**He suspected a trick.** At Circe's door the others follow her in “in their ignorance; / but Eurylochus stayed behind, for he suspected a trick” (10.231–232; see [[dolos]]).

**His report.** He comes straight back to the ship “to bring the news of his companions and of their bitter fate”, but cannot speak a word, “stricken at heart with great grief; and his eyes / were filled with tears, and his heart was set on lamenting” (10.244–248; see [[achos]] and [[lament]]): the thought of his heart, Merry explains, was to cry aloud, but no sound came to his lips, only the tears stood in his eyes, as with the suitors when their laughter turns to foreboding (20.348–349; Merry on 10.248). When they question him he tells it in the very words of the narrative, “We went, as you bade us, through the brush, shining Odysseus; / in the glens we found a fine house, built / of polished stones” (10.251–253; see [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]), and Merry takes the abrupt joining of the clauses for the broken speech of an excited messenger, comparing Longinus on the agitation that asyndeton conveys (*On the Sublime* 19, as Merry cites it; Merry on 10.251). “but I stayed behind, for I suspected a trick. / And all at once they vanished in a body, and not one of them / came to light again” (10.258–260).

**Leave me here.** When Odysseus arms himself and bids him lead the way back, “he took hold of my knees with both hands and begged me”: “Do not lead me there against my will, you who are nurtured by Zeus, but leave me here. / For I know that you will neither come back yourself nor bring back / any other of your companions. No, let us flee quickly with these here; / for we may still escape the evil day” (10.264–269; see [[supplication]], [[zeus-nurtured]] and [[evil-day]]). σῶν, “your”, Aristarchus took for σῶον, “safe” (see the translation's note on 10.268). Odysseus leaves him at the ship, “eating and drinking beside the hollow black ship”, and goes alone (10.271–273; see [[odysseus]]).

**The protest.** When Odysseus comes back for the rest of the crew, “Only Eurylochus tried to hold back all my companions” (10.429):

{{quote:71e149a2-9022-5b96-9b65-5e94b3edabc4}}

By ἅπαντας he means “all of us”, and the verb passes into the first person: “so that we should have perforce to keep Circe's great house for her”, the beasts kept as warders, Merry thinks, like the gold and silver dogs that guard the house of Alcinous (7.91–93; Merry on 10.434). καταβήμεναι, “to go down”, may recall that the house lay in the glens; Eustathius' notion that he speaks as of going down to Hades Merry calls merely fanciful (Merry on 10.432). Eustathius objected, too, that the Cyclops did not set the companions to guard his cave but ate them; the comparison, Merry answers, is with the compulsion, how the companions who came into his dwelling were kept fast prisoners there, ἔρξʼ being “penned them in” (Merry on 10.435; see the translation's note on 10.435 and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). τούτου γάρ takes up θρασύς: “foolhardy, I say, for it was through his infatuation” (Merry on 10.437; see [[atasthalie]] and [[tharsos]]).

**Spared.** Odysseus ponders whether to strike off his head, “kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near” (10.438–441; Merry on 10.441; see [[in-laws]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]), and the companions offer to leave “this man” to guard the ship (10.443–445). “Nor was Eurylochus left behind beside the hollow ship, / but he followed; for he was afraid of my terrible rebuke” (10.447–448).
