summary: Aeolus “did not refuse at all, but made ready my escort”: the winds bound in a skin and the West Wind to carry the ships (10.17–26). When the companions have loosed the winds he refuses a second escort to a man hated by the gods (10.64–75), and the men must row, “since no escort showed itself any longer” (10.79). Odysseus asks Circe to keep her promise “to send me home” (10.483–484), and she sends him first to the house of Hades, with the North Wind for his guide (10.501–507).

**The escort of the winds.** When Odysseus asks leave to go, Aeolus “did not refuse at all, but made ready my escort” (10.17–18; see [[aeolus]]), ὁδόν being “leave to depart” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.17). The escort is the winds themselves. He binds the others in the skin and sends forth “the breath of the West Wind to blow, / to carry the ships and ourselves” (10.19–26; see [[skin-of-the-winds]], [[the-winds]], [[zephyrus]] and [[dispenser-of-the-winds]]). Aeolus is not in Homer the king of the winds, as Virgil made him, but only their manager by the leave of Zeus, and other gods, Pallas, Calypso and Circe among them, can send a favoring wind when they please (Merry on 10.21). “But he was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (10.26–27).

**Refused.** Driven back by the storm, Odysseus sits on Aeolus' threshold and asks again (10.60–69). “Surely we sent you off with care,” the household answers, “so that you might reach / your native land and your house” (10.65–66), and Aeolus will not send him a second time: “for it is not lawful for me to care for or to send on his way / a man who is hated by the blessed gods” (10.73–74; see [[themis]] and [[xenia]]). Then the men must row, “since no escort showed itself any longer” (10.78–79): πομπή is the wind itself, the means by which the voyage is accomplished (Merry on 10.79), as the winds are “the escorts / of ships” in Menelaus' tale (4.361–362).

**Circe's escort.** After the year on Aeaea Odysseus asks Circe to fulfill “the promise you made, / to send me home” (10.483–484), a promise nowhere distinctly given (Merry on 10.483; see the translation's note on 10.483, 10.501). She sends him first to the house of Hades, and when he asks, “Circe, who will guide us on this journey?” (10.501), she answers that he needs no guide: “let no longing for a guide trouble you beside your ship, / but set up your mast and spread the white sails, / and sit; and the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.505–507; see [[boreas]] and [[pothe]]). The subjunctive φέρῃσι implies, as it were, the intention of the North Wind (Merry on 10.507).
