**Aeolus' house.** By day the six sons and six daughters of Aeolus feast with their parents, “and at night again, beside their modest wives, / they sleep on rugs and on corded beds” (10.11–12; see [[aeolus]], [[blankets]] and [[corded]]).

**The bed of the goddess.** Hermes foretells it: “And in fear of you she will bid you lie with her; / then do not refuse the bed of the goddess any longer, / so that she may free your companions and care for you yourself; / but bid her swear a great oath by the blessed gods” (10.296–299; see [[hermes]] and [[oath]]). When her drug has failed and he has drawn his sword, Circe says: “let us two / go up into our bed, so that, having lain together / in bed and love, we may trust one another” (10.333–335; see [[circe]] and [[philotes]]). ἡμετέρης, “our”, is said by anticipation, and πεποίθομεν is a subjunctive of the perfect πέποιθα (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.334, 10.335). Odysseus answers that he will not go up into her bed unless she swears (10.337–344), and when she has sworn, “then I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe” (10.347).

**The year's end.** A year later, “I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe / and entreated her by her knees” to send him home (10.480–481; see [[supplication]]), and when she tells him of the journey to the house of Hades, “I wept as I sat on the bed, and my heart / no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun” (10.497–498; see [[odysseus]]).
