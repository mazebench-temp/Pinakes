summary: Odysseus plaits a cable of brushwood and willow shoots to bind the feet of the great stag (10.166–168), and Circe tells him that beyond Oceanus he will find the groves of Persephone, “tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit” (10.509–510).
# λύγους (10.166), found only here in Homer; ἰτέαι (10.510) is the entry's.
grc: [λύγους]
---

**The stag's cable.** “then I plucked brushwood and willow shoots, / and plaiting a cable, about a fathom's length, well twisted from both ends, / I bound together the feet of the dread monster” (10.166–168; see [[deer]] and the translation's row λύγοι). The willow shoots are the withies with which Odysseus had bound the Cyclops' rams three together (9.427; see the Book 9 note), and the brushwood, ῥῶπες, is the undergrowth that Eumaeus will strew under a skin or a fleece for his guests to sit on (14.49–51, 16.47, plain renderings). ἀμφοτέρωθεν goes with πλεξάμενος, W. W. Merry explains: the cable is plaited of two strands passing from side to side, “across and across”, until it is well twisted (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.167).

**The groves of Persephone.** When Odysseus has crossed Oceanus, Circe says, he will come “where there is a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone, / tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit”, and there he is to beach his ship and go into the dank house of Hades (10.508–512; see [[the-groves-of-persephone]], [[persephone]], [[oceanus]], [[hades]] and [[trees]]). The trees of the groves are trees of water, as the willows of the Iliad grow along the Xanthus (*Iliad* 21.350) and the Odyssey's poplars by springs (see the Book 9 note on [[trees]]). ὠλεσίκαρπος, “that lose their fruit”, is found only here (see the translation's row ὠλεσίκαρπος). Merry quotes Pliny, who says that the willow sheds its seed very quickly, before it is fully ripe, and that Homer therefore called it “fruit-losing”, and he suggests that this notion of a waste of life made the willow seem a fit tree for the groves of Persephone, as the scholion says that fruitless things belong to the dead (Merry on 10.510, quoting Pliny, *Natural History* 16.26 in his numbering). The scholia give the same reason for the barren heifer vowed to the dead (10.522; Merry on 10.522; see the Book 10 note on [[cattle]]). ἰτέα began with a digamma, as the entry says, and Merry sets beside it Latin *vitta*, *vitex* and *vimen* and English *withy* (on 10.510).
