---
summary: The book's θυμός ponders, grieves, eats itself, forebodes and is persuaded. Odysseus ponders “in my noble heart” whether to throw himself into the sea (10.50); the men lie two days on Aeaea “eating our hearts out” (10.143); the stag's “spirit flew away” (10.163); at Circe's table his heart forebodes evil, and she asks why he sits “eating your heart” (10.373–379); she bids the companions eat “until you take heart again in your breasts” (10.460–465); and four times a “proud spirit was persuaded” (10.406, 10.466, 10.475, 10.550).
---

**Pondering.** Waking to the storm that carries the ships back, Odysseus “pondered in my noble heart / whether I should fall from the ship and perish in the sea, / or endure in silence and remain still among the living” (κατὰ θυμὸν ἀμύμονα μερμήριξα, 10.50–52; see [[decision-scene]], [[silence]] and [[odysseus]]). On Aeaea he ponders “in my mind and in my heart” whether to go toward the smoke (10.151; see [[phrenes]]).

**Eating the heart.** For two days on Aeaea the men lay “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together” (θυμὸν ἔδοντες, 10.142–143; see [[algos]]). At Circe's table the heart refuses food: the meal “did not please my heart, / but I sat with my thoughts elsewhere, and my heart foreboded evil” (10.373–374), and Circe asks, “Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man without speech, / eating your heart, and do not touch food or drink?” (θυμὸν ἔδων, 10.378–379; see [[fasting]]).

**Life and appetite.** The stag struck by Odysseus' spear “fell down in the dust with a cry, and its spirit flew away” (ἀπὸ δʼ ἔπτατο θυμός, 10.163; see [[deer]]), the full phrase used of the horse Pedasus in the Iliad and of the boar wounded on Parnassus (*Iliad* 16.469; 19.454; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.163; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]). The θυμός is also appetite: the dogs fawn on their master for “tidbits to please their hearts” (μειλίγματα θυμοῦ, 10.217), “tit-bits to appease their appetite”, as no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast (*Iliad* 1.468; Merry on 10.217; see [[dogs]] and the translation's table of key words).

**Heart restored.** When the two bands of companions have wept together, Circe bids them “eat food and drink wine, / until you take heart again in your breasts, / such as you had when you first left your native land / of rugged Ithaca. But now you are withered and spiritless, / always remembering your hard wandering, and never is your heart / in gladness, since you have suffered very much” (θυμὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσι λάβητε … ἄθυμοι, 10.460–465; see [[stethos]]), as Diomedes bade the kings sleep when they had satisfied their hearts with food and wine, “for in that is might and courage” (*Iliad* 9.705–706; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]). ἀσκελέες, “withered”, is meant in its physical sense, as αὐσταλέος of a man unwashed (19.327; Merry on 10.463; see the translation's note on 10.463).

**Persuaded.** Four times the verse of consent closes a speech: “So she spoke, and my proud spirit was persuaded” (θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, 10.406, 10.466, 10.475, 10.550; see [[our-proud-spirit-was-persuaded]], [[peitho]] and [[proud]]). And the heart is eager for home: “my heart is eager now” (10.484; see [[nostos]]).
