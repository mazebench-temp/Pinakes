---
summary: Two of Horace's examples come from this book: Antiphates, first of the striking wonders that Homer keeps for later, and Circe's cups, which in the Epistles the companions drank and Ulysses did not. In the poem Odysseus drinks too, and his companions keep their minds.
refs: [10.114-124, 10.234-240, 10.316-318]
---

**Antiphates.** Horace's list of the wonders, “Antiphates and Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” (*Ars Poetica* 145), begins with the king of the Laestrygonians, whom his wife called from the place of assembly, “her husband, who devised a grim destruction for them”: he seized one of the scouts for his meal and raised the cry that brought the Laestrygonians down on the ships (10.114–124; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). Horace's order is not the tale's, in which the Cyclops comes first (Book 9), Antiphates second, and Scylla and Charybdis last (Book 12).

**Circe's cups.** In the *Epistles* Ulysses' wisdom is shown in what he did not drink: “you know the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups; if he had drunk them with his companions, foolish and greedy, he would have been, under a harlot mistress, base and senseless, and would have lived an unclean dog or a sow that loves the mud” (*Epistles* 1.2.23–26, a plain rendering). In this book Odysseus does drink. Circe makes him “the mixed drink in a golden cup” and puts the drug in it, and he drinks it off, but it does not bewitch him, for Hermes has given him the good drug (10.316–318; see [[moly]] and [[drugs]]). And the companions who drank were not made senseless, *excors*, as Horace's would have been: they had the heads, the voice, the bristles and the form of swine, “but their minds were steadfast, as before” (10.237–240; see [[noos]] and [[sties]]).
