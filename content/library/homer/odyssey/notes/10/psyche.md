Elpenor falls from Circe's roof, “and his soul went down to Hades” (10.560).

**A soul to consult.** The journey to the house of Hades is made for one soul (ψυχῇ χρησομένους, 10.492; see [[teiresias]] and [[persephone]]):

{{quote:2905de1b-f92a-5ff5-b1c5-a39269cff5ce}}

The rest of the dead have no mind: in Merry's rendering, “to whom even after his death did Persephone grant sense, for him alone of all men to have wisdom” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.494). The dead, he adds, are pictured here not as invisible but as unsubstantial, and to keep even the φρένες, as Teiresias did, was a special privilege: the shade of Patroclus had none, “soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.103–104; Merry on 10.495; see [[phrenes]], [[noos]] and [[eidolon]]). Cicero gives the verse in Latin: Homer says of Tiresias that even among the dead he alone is wise, and the rest wander like shadows (*solum sapere, ceteros umbrarum vagari modo*, *On Divination* 1.88; Merry on 10.495), and Plato, quoting it with ταὶ δέ, found in it a false picture of the underworld (Merry on 10.495 and his critical note; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).

**Shadows.** Only here are the dead called σκιαί, “shadows” (see the translation's table of key words, σκιαί). In Book 11 Odysseus' mother flies from his arms three times “like a shadow or a dream” (σκιῇ εἴκελον ἢ καὶ ὀνείρῳ, 11.207), and she tells him that this is the way of mortals when they die: “the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers” (11.222, plain renderings).

**At the pit.** When the victims have been slaughtered, “there many / souls of the dead who have died will come” (ψυχαὶ … νεκύων κατατεθνηώτων, 10.529–530), with the Iliad's phrase for the corpses on the battlefield, here used of the souls (see the translation's table of key words, [[the-dead-who-have-died]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). The souls must be kept from the blood with the sword until Teiresias has drunk and spoken (10.535–537).

**Elpenor.** The book ends with the first of the companions to go there. Elpenor wakes on Circe's roof, forgets the ladder, and falls; “his neck / was broken from the vertebrae, and his soul went down to Hades” (ψυχὴ δʼ Ἄϊδόσδε κατῆλθεν, 10.559–560; see [[elpenor]], [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[hades]]). His soul will be the first to come to the pit, and will tell its death in the same words (11.51–65).
