---
summary: Circe promises Odysseus the North Wind for the journey to the house of Hades: he need not look for a guide, but set up the mast, spread the sails and sit, “and the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.505–507).
---

**A wind for a guide.** “Circe, who will guide us on this journey? / No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship”, Odysseus asks (10.501–502; see [[hades]]), and she answers: “let no longing for a guide trouble you beside your ship, / but set up your mast and spread the white sails, / and sit; and the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.505–507; see [[circe]], [[mast]] and [[sails]]). Merry takes παρὰ νηί with μελέσθω, as though Odysseus might be hanging about the ship, waiting for a pilot (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.505), and the subjunctive φέρῃσιν as expressing, as it were, the North Wind's own intention (on 10.507). The wind blows the ship south and west from Aeaea, across Oceanus to the shore of the groves of Persephone (10.508–511; Merry on 10.512; see [[oceanus]] and [[persephone]]). The North Wind is the wind of the tale's first storm, which Zeus roused against the ships after the raid on the Cicones (9.67–71; see the Book 9 note); here, as when Athena roused the swift North Wind to carry Odysseus toward the Phaeacians (5.385), it is a wind that carries a ship where a goddess sends it. When the ship sails, the wind is Circe's own gift, “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion” (11.7, a plain rendering; see [[the-winds]]).
