summary: Circe turns the companions of Eurylochus' band into swine with her drugged drink and her wand and pens them in her sties, with the heads, the voice, the bristles and the form of swine but their minds as before, and throws them acorns and cornel to eat; when Odysseus has her release them, they come out like fat hogs nine years old and are made men again (10.233–243, 10.282–283, 10.388–396).

**The change.** Circe seats the men of Eurylochus' band, gives them her mixed drink with the drugs in it, “so that they might utterly forget their native land”, and then strikes them with her wand and pens them in the sties (10.233–238; see [[kykeon]], [[drugs]], [[wand]] and [[sties]]). “And they had the heads of swine, and the voice, and the bristles, / and the form, but their minds were steadfast, as before” (10.239–240; see [[noos]] and [[shape-changing]]). Zenodotus read καὶ πόδας, “and the feet”, for καὶ δέμας, “and the form”; and since νόος is nowhere else contracted in Homer, W. W. Merry would read νόος for νοῦς (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.240 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.240). He asks whether the sorcery was wholly successful: the drugs were to make the men forget their native land, and their minds were unchanged (on 10.240). “So they were penned there weeping” (10.241).

**The food of swine.** “and before them Circe / threw ilex acorns and oak acorns and the fruit of the cornel / to eat, such as swine that sleep on the ground always eat” (10.241–243; see [[trees]]). χαμαιευνάς, “that sleep on the ground”, is said again of Eumaeus' sows in their sties (14.15, a plain rendering; see [[with-unwashed-feet]] and the translation's row χαμαιευνάς; ἀδευκὴς πότμος), and Athena tells Odysseus that he will find Eumaeus' swine feeding on the acorn by the rock of Corax (13.407–409; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-rock-of-corax]]). Aristarchus, the scholia say, did not know 10.242, and Callistratus wrote another verse in its place; if that is true, Merry observes, Aristarchus cannot have known 10.243 either (Merry's critical note on 10.242; see the translation's note on 10.242).

**Penned like swine.** Hermes tells Odysseus that his companions “are penned like swine, and keep to close-packed sties” (10.282–283): ἔρχαται, “are penned”, is a perfect of the stem of ἔργω, “shut in” (Merry on 10.283). Circe bids Odysseus himself, when her drug has failed to bewitch him, “Go now to the sty” (10.320), and he answers her, “you who turned my companions into swine in your halls” (10.338): ἑταίρους and αὐτόν, the companions and himself, stand side by side at the end of one verse and the head of the next, and make the contrast (Merry on 10.338). Eurylochus fears that she will make them all “either swine or wolves or lions” (10.433; see [[wolves]] and [[lion]]).

**Fat hogs nine years old.** When Odysseus will not eat until his companions are free, Circe goes out with her wand in her hand, opens the doors of the sty, “and drove them out, looking like fat hogs nine years old” (10.388–390). σίαλος is the fatted hog (see the Book 2 note), and ἐννέωρος, “nine years old”, is the age of Aeolus' ox (10.19; see [[cattle]]). Eustathius, Merry reports, made it “of nine seasons”, two years and a month, which Merry calls very far-fetched, though a fair attempt to escape the difficulty of supposing swine fit for food at nine years old; Merry takes the word as “nine years old”, and so “of full maturity” (Merry on 10.19 and 10.390; see the translation's note on 10.390). Then she anoints each of them with another drug: “And from their limbs fell the bristles that had been made to grow before / by the ruinous drug that lady Circe had given them; / and they became men again, younger than they had been before, / and much handsomer and taller to look upon” (10.391–396; see [[circe]]). Ovid tells the change back in more detail, the bristles falling, the cloven hoofs becoming feet, the shoulders and arms returning (*Metamorphoses* 14.302–305, which Merry quotes on 10.393; see [[ovid]]).
