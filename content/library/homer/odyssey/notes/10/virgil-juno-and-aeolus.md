---
summary: Virgil's Aeolus is this book's son of Hippotes made a king. Juno names his office much as Homer does, the power to calm and to raise; but Homer's Aeolus is the gods' friend whom Zeus made dispenser of the winds, raises no storm himself, and needs no bride, for he lives with his wife and their twelve children.
refs: [10.2, 10.5-12, 10.19-27]
---

**The office.** Homer's Aeolus is “dear to the immortal gods” (10.2), “for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (10.21–22; see [[aeolus]] and [[dispenser-of-the-winds]]). Juno gives Virgil's Aeolus the same office, with a like pair of verbs: “to you the father of gods and king of men has given the power to calm the waves and to raise them with the wind” (*Aeneid* 1.65–66, a plain rendering). But Virgil makes him “king Aeolus”, who keeps the winds in a vast cave and curbs them with chains and a prison (*Aeneid* 1.52–54), while Homer's, Merry notes, is not the king of the winds but only their manager by leave of Zeus (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.21).

**The winds let out.** Homer's Aeolus binds the paths of the blustering winds in the skin of an ox and lets only the West Wind blow, to carry Odysseus home (10.19–27; see [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[zephyrus]]); the storm that drives the ships back is let loose by the companions, who open the skin while Odysseus sleeps (10.46–49). Virgil's Aeolus lets the winds out himself, at Juno's request: he turns his spear and strikes the hollow mountain on its side, and the winds rush out in a column where a door is given them (*Aeneid* 1.81–83).

**The bride.** Juno's price is a bride, the nymph Deiopea, to spend all her years with him and make him the father of fine children (*Aeneid* 1.71–75). Homer's Aeolus has a wife already, the “devoted mother” of their twelve children, six daughters and six sons, whom he has married to one another (10.5–12; see [[marriage]]).
