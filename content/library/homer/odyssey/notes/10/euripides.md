---
summary: In the Trojan Women Cassandra foretells Odysseus' wanderings and names among them “Ligurian Circe, the shaper of swine”, already a goddess of the West; Merry sets the libations of honey and milk in the Orestes and the Iphigenia among the Taurians beside the drink offering to the dead in Circe's directions, and finds several of this book's words in Euripides.
---

- **Ligurian Circe.** Told that her mother will be Odysseus' slave, Cassandra pities him: what he has yet to suffer will make her woes and the Trojans' seem gold to him; after ten years more he will come home alone; she names dread Charybdis in her narrow strait of rock, the mountain-roaming Cyclops who eats raw flesh, “and Ligurian Circe, the shaper of swine” (Λιγυστίς θʼ ἡ συῶν μορφώτρια Κίρκη), the shipwrecks on the salt sea, the longing for the lotus and the holy cattle of the Sun, whose flesh will one day speak with a voice bitter to Odysseus; “to cut it short, he will go living to Hades” (*Trojan Women* 431–443, plain renderings). Merry cites the Ligurian Circe for her early transfer to the West (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.135). In this book Circe turns the companions into swine (10.237–243) and sends Odysseus to the house of Hades while he lives (10.490–495): [[circe]], [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[sties]].
- **Honey and milk.** For μελίκρητον, the honey and milk of the first drink offering to the dead (10.519), Merry cites Helen's charge to Hermione to pour honey mixed with milk and the wine-dark foam at Clytemnestra's tomb (*Orestes* 113–115), and Iphigenia's offerings for the brother she thinks dead, “streams of milk from mountain heifers, / the wine offerings of Bacchus, / and the labor of the tawny bees, / which are laid down as soothing charms for the dead” (*Iphigenia among the Taurians* 159–166, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.519). The soothing charms, θελκτήρια, are of the family of θέλγω, the word for Circe's bewitching (10.213, 10.291, 10.326): [[the-offering-to-the-dead]], [[milk]] and [[thelgo]].
- **Words.** Merry also finds parallels in Euripides for words of this book: αἶθοψ, the “fiery smoke” that Odysseus sees rising from Circe's halls (10.149–152), used of a blaze and of a torch (*Suppliants* 1018; *Bacchae* 594; Merry on 10.152); Cassandra's πύκαζε, “wreathe”, beside the island “set as a crown” by the sea (*Trojan Women* 353; Merry on 10.195); and ὑπῆλθε, the longing that “came over” Medea's nurse, beside the longing for lament that came on the companions (*Medea* 57; Merry on 10.398): [[aeaea]] and [[lament]].
