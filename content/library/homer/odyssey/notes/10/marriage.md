# ἀκοίτις (10.7; again 11.452), the contracted accusative plural of ἄκοιτις.
grc: [ἀκοίτις]
---

**Aeolus' children.** “And he has twelve children too in his halls, / six daughters and six sons in their prime. / And he gave his daughters to his sons to be their wives” (10.5–7; see [[aeolus]]). The marriage of brothers and sisters, Merry thinks, points to no custom of any age or country, but gives an idea of the loneliness of the island: the brothers married their sisters because there were no other women to marry; the scholiast who praised such unions, citing Zeus and Hera, was perhaps flattering the Ptolemies, who married their sisters (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.5). ἀκοίτις is the contracted accusative plural, like ἦνις (*Iliad* 6.94; Merry on 10.7). By day the household feasts, “and at night again, beside their modest wives, / they sleep on rugs and on corded beds” (παρʼ αἰδοίῃς ἀλόχοισιν, 10.11–12; see [[aidos]] and [[hypnos]]), and when Odysseus comes back he finds Aeolus “feasting beside his wife and his children” (10.61).

**The Laestrygonian queen.** In the king's house the scouts find “his wife / as huge as a mountain peak”, and she calls from the place of assembly “famous Antiphates, / her husband, who devised a grim destruction for them” (10.112–115; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[posis]]).

**Kin by marriage.** Eurylochus, whom Odysseus thinks of killing, is “kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near” (10.441; see [[in-laws]] and [[eurylochus]]).
