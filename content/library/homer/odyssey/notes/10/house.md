summary: Aeolus' house resounds all day with the feasting of his family (10.10–13), and in Antiphates' high-roofed house the scouts find his giant wife (10.111–113). Circe's house of polished stones stands in the glens, its floor ringing with her song (10.210–211, 10.227); Odysseus and his companions spend a year in its halls, and from its roof Elpenor falls to his death (10.467–479, 10.552–560).

**Aeolus' house.** On the floating island “the house, full of the savor of meat, resounds round about in the court / by day” (10.10–11; see [[aeolus]] and [[feast]]). κνισῆεν δῶμα is the house steaming with the smoke of roasting meat, and αὐλῇ is a local dative: the sound of the feasting, and perhaps of music, could be heard as one entered the court. Whatever the reading, W. W. Merry adds, the phrase fits the house of the master of the winds, full of strange sounds; but the emphatic word is κνισῆεν, since the poet means that the six couples feast all day with their parents (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.10; see the translation's note on 10.10). “And so we came to their city and their fine house” (10.13), where a scholion records the variant “and long walls” (Merry's critical note). Driven back by the winds, Odysseus goes again “to the glorious house of Aeolus”, sits on its threshold, and is sent “from the house” (10.60–76; see [[threshold]] and [[door]]).

**Antiphates' house.** The king's daughter “at once pointed out the high-roofed house of her father”, and in “the glorious house” the scouts find his wife “as huge as a mountain peak” (10.111–113; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]], [[high-roofed]] and [[like-a-wooded-peak]]).

**Circe's house.** “In the glens they found the house of Circe, built / of polished stones, in a place with a view all round” (10.210–211, again in Eurylochus' report, 10.252–253; see [[polished]], [[in-a-place-with-a-view]] and the translation's notes on 10.211 and 10.253). Circe sings within at her loom, and “the whole floor rings all round with it” (10.227; see [[loom]]): μυκάομαι is said not only of cattle but of the creaking of hinges and the ringing of a shield under a spear, Merry notes (on 10.227; *Iliad* 5.749, 20.260). It is “the great house of Circe” (10.276) and her “sacred house” (10.426, 10.445, 10.554), ἱερός because it is hers (Merry on 10.275). In her halls she turned the first band into swine (10.338), and from them she invites Odysseus into her chamber (10.340; see [[bed]]); the house rings with the companions' lament when they are men again and groans round about when the two bands meet (10.398–399, 10.454; see [[lament]]). For a year they feast there, and at the end of each day “they lay down to sleep throughout the shadowy halls” (10.479; see [[shadowy-mountains]]). On the last morning Odysseus “went through the house” to rouse them: he probably slept in the inner part of the house and his companions in the colonnades, Merry thinks, so that he passed down its whole length (on 10.546; 3.402).

**The roof and the ladder.** Elpenor had lain down apart on the roof for the cool air, and woken by the noise of the departure he forgot “to come back down by going to the long ladder, / but fell straight down from the roof” (10.557–559; see [[elpenor]] and the translation's row ψῦχος; κλῖμαξ). The ladder was the only safe way down from the flat roof, and Eustathius, as Merry reports him, explains that the houses were not tiled but had earth laid on their beams, so that the roof could be slept on (Merry on 10.558). κλῖμαξ is the stairway down which Penelope came in Book 1 (1.330).
