---
summary: Aeolus is “dear to the immortal gods” (10.2); the companions call Odysseus “dear and honored” by all men (10.38); Odysseus addresses Aeolus' household and his own men as “friends” (10.69, 10.174, 10.190), and so does Polites (10.226); and the companions' “dear heart was broken” (10.198, 10.566).
---

φίλος runs through the book in three uses. As a standing epithet it marks what belongs to a man: Aeolus' children feast beside their “dear father” (10.8), the companions lost to the Laestrygonians are “our dear companions” (10.134; see [[hetairos]]), and “their dear heart was broken” (10.198, 10.566; see [[etor]]). As a word of favor it measures standing with gods and men: Aeolus is “dear to the immortal gods” (10.2; see [[aeolus]]), and the companions grumble that Odysseus is “dear and honored” by all men (10.38; see [[time]]), until Aeolus finds him “hated by the blessed gods” (10.74; see [[themis]]). As an address, ὦ φίλοι, “friends”, it is Odysseus' word to Aeolus' household, “But make it good, my friends” (10.69), and to his own men, “Friends, we shall not go down yet” (10.174) and “friends, we do not know where the darkness is” (10.190), and Polites' at Circe's door (10.226; see [[polites-the-companion]]).
