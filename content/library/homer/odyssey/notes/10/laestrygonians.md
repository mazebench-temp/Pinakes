---
summary: The Laestrygonians are the second people of the book. Odysseus' scouts meet the king's daughter at the spring and find the queen “as huge as a mountain peak”, and Antiphates seizes one of them for his meal; then the people, countless, “not like men, but like the Giants”, stone the ships in the harbor and spear the men like fish, and only Odysseus' ship escapes (10.80–132). On Aeaea the memory of “the deeds of Antiphates the Laestrygonian” breaks the companions' hearts (10.198–200).
# The people without the name: "what men they might be" (10.101), "they" who used to carry water to the city
# (10.108), "these people" over whom the king ruled (10.110), and the men who kill the companions in the harbor (10.125).
refs: [10.101, 10.108, 10.110, 10.125]
---

**Who eat bread upon the earth.** From his lookout Odysseus sees no works of oxen or of men, only smoke rising from the land, and sends two men and a herald “to go and find out / what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (10.97–102; see [[spies-and-scouts]]), the verses with which he sent his scouts to the Lotus-eaters (9.88–90; see [[lotus-eaters]]). As the Laestrygonians, like the Cyclopes, were only graziers, W. W. Merry explains, there were no plowed fields and no vineyards or gardens to be seen, but the smoke showed that the land was inhabited (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.97; see [[cyclopes]]). The scouts take a smooth road, cleared for the wagons that bring wood down to the city from the mountains (10.103–104; Merry on 10.103), and before the city they meet a girl drawing water at the spring Artacia, “the strong daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian” (10.105–108; see [[artacia]]).

**The king's house.** They ask her “who was king of these people and over whom he ruled” (10.110), a double question, Merry notes, for the name of the king and of his people (on 10.110), and she points out her father's high-roofed house (10.111). Inside they find his wife, “as huge as a mountain peak, and they shuddered at her” (10.113): κατὰ δʼ ἔστυγον is the verb of the gods' horror at the dank house of Hades in the Iliad (*Iliad* 20.65; Merry on 10.113). She calls her husband from the place of assembly, “famous Antiphates, / her husband, who devised a grim destruction for them”, and he seizes one of the scouts and makes ready his meal (10.114–116; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]). The Laestrygonians, having a king and an assembly, are more advanced in the outward forms of civilization than the Cyclopes (Merry on 10.114; see [[assembly]]), but the king, like the Cyclops, eats his visitors.

**Not like men, but like the Giants.** The two who escape run to the ships, and the king raises the cry through the city:

They throw from the tops of the cliffs that command the harbor, and each stone, as Eustathius puts it, is a load that would burden an ordinary man (Merry on 10.121; see [[giants]]). The harsh sounds of κακὸς κόναβος κατὰ νῆας, Merry notes, and the repeated sounds of the next verse are meant to render the crushing volley (on 10.122). The ships are broken up and the men thrown into the water, where the Laestrygonians spear them as they float, “with tridents or other sharpened tools” in Eustathius' words, and carry them off like a catch of fish to a joyless meal (Merry on 10.124; see the translation's note on 10.124 for the ancient variants). The comparison makes them fishermen and the men their fish (see [[like-fish]]); the Cyclops dashed his victims to the ground like puppies (9.289; see [[like-puppies]]).

**One ship.** The harbor that had seemed safe becomes the trap: its entrance was narrow, and Eustathius explains the great loss by the narrows, through which the ships could not escape (Merry on 10.90; see [[harbor]]). Odysseus, who alone had moored his ship outside, cuts the cables with his sword, his men row for their lives, and “Gladly out to the open sea, away from the overhanging rocks, fled / my ship; but the others perished there in a body” (10.125–132; see [[stern-cables]] and [[overhanging]]). The others are eleven ships of the twelve (9.159), each with its crew; from here on the tale has one ship and one crew (see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]).

**Remembered on Aeaea.** When Odysseus tells his men of the smoke on Aeaea, “their dear heart was broken / as they remembered the deeds of Antiphates the Laestrygonian / and of the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men” (10.198–200; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). They had put in there in silence, Eustathius says, out of fear after the inhospitality of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians (Merry on 10.140; see [[aeaea]]). Circe, too, knows “all the harm that merciless men did you on land” (10.459), and Eurylochus, refusing to go to her house, remembers only the Cyclops (10.435–437).
