---
summary: Hermes ends his account of the moly with the maxim: it is hard for mortal men to dig up, “but the gods can do all things” (10.305–306). The book shows the gods doing easily what men cannot, down to its last verses, where Circe slips past the men unseen.
---

The maxim closes a scene in which a god does for a mortal what he could not do for himself. Hermes meets Odysseus “in the likeness of a young man”, calls him “unhappy man”, going alone and “not knowing the place”, promises to free him from evils, tells him “the baneful thoughts of Circe”, and pulls the plant out of the ground for him (10.277–303; see [[hermes]] and [[moly]]). The gods' ease runs through the book. “Some god guided us” into the harbor of Aeaea (10.141); “one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was,” and sent the stag into Odysseus' path (10.157–158; see [[pity]] and [[deer]]); and at the end Circe ties the ram and the ewe beside the ship, “slipping past us easily” (ῥεῖα, 10.571–573), with the adverb of the gods “who live at ease” (θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, 4.805, 5.122; *Iliad* 6.138; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The mortal's part is still the hard one: with the moly in his hand Odysseus must spring at Circe with drawn sword and bind her by a great oath (10.293–301; see [[oath]]).
