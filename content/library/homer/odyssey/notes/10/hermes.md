summary: Hermes meets Odysseus in the glens of Aeaea “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful”, takes his hand, tells him what Circe will do and how to master her, and gives him the good drug, pulling the moly from the ground and showing him its nature; then he goes away to Olympus. Circe, when the drug fails, knows her guest at once, for “the slayer of Argus of the golden wand” had always told her that Odysseus would come.

**In the likeness of a young man.** Odysseus has left Eurylochus at the ship and goes up alone through the sacred glens: “there Hermes of the golden wand met me / as I was going toward the house, in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful” (10.277–279; see [[of-the-golden-wand]] and [[aeaea]]). The last verse is the one of his meeting with Priam in the Iliad, where he goes “in the likeness of a young prince” (*Iliad* 24.347–348; see the general article). Merry notes that the ῥάβδος of Hermes in Homer is only a plain wand, and that the caduceus with two serpents winding round it is a later invention (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.277; see [[wand]]). ὑπηνήτης is one who has hair growing on the ὑπήνη, which Hesychius explains as the place under the nose, the upper lip, where the beard shows first; Merry quotes Lucian on the artists who made Zeus bearded, Apollo always a boy, and Hermes ὑπηνήτης, and since Odysseus seems to know the god at once, he thinks that a characteristic style may already have been assigned to the principal gods in painting or sculpture (on 10.279). The tale does not say who sent him. In Book 5 Zeus sent him to Calypso (5.28–42); here he meets a man on his way into danger, as he met Priam on his way to Achilles.

**His counsel.** “And he clung to my hand, and spoke to me, and called me by name” (10.280; see [[called-him-by-name]]), as in the Iliad he took Priam by the hand and asked, “Where, father, are you guiding your horses and mules like this …?” (*Iliad* 24.360–362). His question is like it: “Where are you going now again, unhappy man, alone across the heights, / not knowing the place? And your companions there in the house of Circe / are penned like swine, and keep to close-packed sties” (10.281–283; see [[sties]]). He tells Odysseus that he will not come home himself, but will stay where the others are, and offers to save him (10.284–286):

{{quote:4ee995ce-0931-526a-80ee-a9db91993d23}}

κρατός is “from your head”, as in Achilles' answer to the dying Hector, that there is no one who will keep the dogs “from your head” (*Iliad* 22.348; Merry on 10.288). Then Hermes tells “all the baneful thoughts of Circe” (10.289; see [[circe]], [[drugs]], [[kykeon]] and [[thelgo]]): when she strikes him with her very long wand, Odysseus is to draw his sword and spring at her as though eager to kill her; in fear she will bid him lie with her, and he is not to refuse the bed of the goddess, so that she may free his companions, but first make her swear a great oath by the blessed gods, “lest, when you are stripped, she make you a coward and unmanned” (10.293–301; see [[sword]], [[oath]] and [[bed]]). ἀπογυμνωθέντα is not “without his sword”, as the scholion explains it, but “when stripped”, in the simpler sense suggested by εὐνηθῆναι (Merry on 10.301; see the translation's note on 10.301). The counsel is followed to the letter, and the event is told in nearly the verses of Hermes' instructions (10.321–322 ~ 10.294–295; 10.343–344 ~ 10.299–300; see [[circe]]).

**The moly.** Then the god gives him the drug:

{{quote:970c20ba-74bf-5084-b5f8-4e15d4f00c4c}}

He pulls up from the ground what is hard for mortal men to dig, and shows Odysseus its φύσις, a word found only here in Homer, its outward form (Merry on 10.303; see [[moly]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). μῶλυ is the name the gods give it (see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Merry thinks the plant a fanciful creation of the poet (on 10.305; see the translation's note on 10.305).

**To Olympus.** “Then Hermes went away to high Olympus / across the wooded island” (10.307–308; see [[olympus]]), as he left Priam at the hut of Achilles, “So he spoke, and went away to high Olympus, / Hermes” (*Iliad* 24.468–469). He is named once more, by Circe, when the drug has not worked and Odysseus has drawn his sword on her: “Surely you are Odysseus of many turns; he always told me / you would come, the slayer of Argus of the golden wand, / returning from Troy in your swift black ship” (10.330–332; see [[of-many-turns]] and [[argeiphontes]]). The god who armed Odysseus against her had long foretold his coming to her, as the Cyclops' seer Telemus had foretold his blindness at the hands of Odysseus (9.507–512; see [[telemus]]).
