---
summary: A handmaid of Circe draws up silver tables before the chairs and sets golden baskets on them (10.354–355), and Odysseus, seated after his bath, is given a polished table beside him and food from the housekeeper, which he will not touch (10.368–374).
---

**The silver tables.** While Circe and Odysseus are in her chamber, “another drew up tables before the chairs, / silver ones, and set golden baskets on them” (10.354–355; see [[handmaids]], [[basket]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). W. W. Merry takes τραπέζας as a table to each chair, not several tables for meat and drink, as Eustathius interpreted (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.354), the custom he finds in Book 1, where the attendants set tables in front of the seats, comparing Tacitus' description of the Germans, each with a seat apart and his own table; but two sit at one table at 17.93, and in Achilles' hut one table serves all the guests (Merry on 1.112; *Iliad* 9.216).

**The guest's table.** After his bath Odysseus is seated, and “a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it, / a fine golden one, over a silver basin, / for me to wash; and she drew up a polished table beside me” (10.368–370; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[basin-and-pitcher]]). The housekeeper sets bread and many dainties on it, but he sits with his thoughts elsewhere and will not eat until his companions are set free (10.371–387; see [[odysseus]]). Köchly, as Merry quotes him, thought these verses, the verses of every guest's meal, put in most ineptly in a house where four handmaids had just been seen preparing the meal (Merry's critical note on 10.348; see the translation's note on 10.368–372).
