**Circe's drink.** Circe leads the companions in and seats them on chairs and seats (see [[throne]]), “and for them she made a mixture of cheese and barley meal and pale honey / with Pramnian wine; and she mixed into the food / grim drugs, so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.234–236; see [[cheese]], [[barley-meal]], [[honey]], [[wine]], [[pramnian]] and [[drugs]]). Hecamede's drink for Nestor and Machaon was mixed in the same words, κύκησε … οἴνῳ Πραμνείῳ, “made a mixture … with Pramnian wine” (*Iliad* 11.638–639; see the translation's row κυκάω, κυκεών). This mess, W. W. Merry explains, must have been about the consistency of porridge, which is why it is called σῖτος, “food”; it was not a regular article of food but a stimulant where special strength or refreshment was needed, like Hecamede's κυκεών, which leaves out the honey that is in Circe's (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.234).

**The drink for Odysseus.** Hermes tells Odysseus what to expect: “She will make you a mixed drink, and put drugs in the food” (10.290; see [[hermes]]). The strong word φάρμακα between ἐν and σίτῳ inclines Merry to read ἐνί with Bekker, a true tmesis of ἐμβαλέει, where the text keeps ἐν; others take σίτῳ as a local dative explaining the adverb ἐν (Merry on 10.290 and his critical note). Circe seats Odysseus on a silver-studded chair, “and she made me the mixed drink in a golden cup, for me to drink, / and put the drug in it, meaning evil in her heart” (10.316–317; see [[cup]]). He drinks it off, and it does not bewitch him (10.318; see [[drugs]] and [[moly]]).
