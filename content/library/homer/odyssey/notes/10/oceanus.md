---
summary: Oceanus is Circe's grandfather, the father of Perse (10.139), and the river that Odysseus must cross to reach the house of Hades: “But when in your ship you have crossed Oceanus, / where there is a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone, / … beach your ship there by deep-eddying Oceanus” (10.508–511).
---

**The father of Perse.** Circe and her brother Aeetes were born of the Sun “and of their mother Perse, whom Oceanus begot as his child” (10.139; see [[perse]], [[circe]] and [[helios]]). The Iliad names another of his daughters, Eurynome, who took in Hephaestus (*Iliad* 18.398–399; see [[eurynome]]), and calls Oceanus “the origin of the gods” (*Iliad* 14.201).

**Across Oceanus.** Circe's instructions for the journey to the dead begin with the river: “But when in your ship you have crossed Oceanus, / where there is a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone, / tall poplars and willows that lose their fruit, / beach your ship there by deep-eddying Oceanus, / and go yourself into the dank house of Hades” (10.508–512; see [[persephone]], [[hades]] and [[deep-eddying]]). Merry draws Homer's picture of the world from the passage. The earth is roughly circular, its lands grouped around a central sea, and the whole is surrounded by a ring of water, Oceanus, with the heavens arched over it like a dome. Oceanus is not a sea but a river, ποταμός (*Iliad* 18.607), with a current and perhaps rapids and swirling eddies, βαθυδίνης, but a gentle general movement, ἀκαλαρρείτης (*Iliad* 7.422). It is found at every point of the compass: on the east, since the sun rises from it; on the west, since the sun sets in it (*Iliad* 8.485); on the south, where Iris goes “to the streams of Oceanus, / to the land of the Ethiopians” (*Iliad* 23.205–206); and on the north, as Strabo inferred from the Bear, which “alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus” (5.275; Strabo, *Geography* 1.1.6). It is the limit of the earth on every side, and the ship comes to its πείρατα, its “limits” (11.13); its water, flowing round the sea yet unmixed with it, is that “from whom all rivers and all the sea / and all springs and deep wells flow” (*Iliad* 21.196–197). It was possible to sail from the sea across Oceanus to the shore on the farther side (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.508). On that shore Odysseus is still on the surface of the earth: Merry finds no descent into a subterranean darkness, but the land of the Cimmerians beyond the west, and the pit dug by the stream (on 10.512, 10.529; see [[hades]]).

**The stream.** When the victims have been slaughtered, Odysseus is to turn away from them, “making toward the streams of the river” (10.528–529). Merry takes the river to be the stream of Oceanus: Odysseus stands on the farther shore facing the western darkness, and turns back toward the stream, and so toward the living world (on 10.529). In Book 11 the ship comes to “the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus”, and the men walk “beside the stream of Oceanus” to the place that Circe had pointed out (11.13, 11.21–22, a plain rendering).
