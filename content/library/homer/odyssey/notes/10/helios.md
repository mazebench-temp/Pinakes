summary: The Sun is the father of Circe and Aeetes, “the Sun, who brings light to mortals” (10.138). Elsewhere in the book he tells the hours and the quarters of the sky: the stag goes down to the river because “the might of the sun had hold of it” (10.160); the companions feast “until the sun went down” on Aeaea's shore and in Circe's house (10.183–186, 10.476–478); Odysseus does not know where the sun sets or rises (10.191–192); and at Circe's word he no longer wishes “to live and see the light of the sun” (10.498).
# Unnamed: "nor where he rises" (10.192).

**The father of Circe.** Odysseus gives Circe's descent when she first appears in his tale: “own sister of baleful Aeetes; / both were born of the Sun, who brings light to mortals, / and of their mother Perse, whom Oceanus begot as his child” (10.137–139; see [[circe]], [[aeetes]] and [[perse]]). φαεσίμβροτος is said of the Sun again in the same book (10.191), and in the Iliad of the dawn: “when the tenth dawn appeared, bringing light to mortals” (*Iliad* 24.785; see [[bringing-light-to-mortals]] and the translation's key-word row). Merry takes φαεσί-μβροτος, like φθισίμβροτος, “destroying mortals” (22.297), to show the connection of βροτός, “mortal”, with the root μορ: μορτός, with β inserted, becomes μβροτός, and the first letter falling off leaves βροτός (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.138). The Odyssey has named the Sun's cattle since its proem (1.8–9; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]); here it names his children, and in Book 12 the nymphs Phaethusa and Lampetie who herd his flocks on Thrinacia (12.131–133).

**The sun of the day.** The stag that Odysseus kills was “coming down to the river from its pasture in the woods / to drink; for the might of the sun had hold of it” (10.159–160; see [[deer]]). Merry renders “for sorely the sun's power oppressed him”, and notes ἔχειν used with μένος on the analogy of sleep or panic that “has hold of” a man (on 10.160). Twice the companions sit “all day long, until the sun went down, / … feasting on untold meat and sweet wine”, on the shore after the hunt and in Circe's house on the last day of the year (10.183–184, 10.476–477), and twice “the sun went down and darkness came on” (10.185, 10.478; see [[sunset-lines]]).

**Where the sun goes down.** At the assembly on Aeaea Odysseus says that they do not know where the darkness is or the dawn, “nor where the sun, who brings light to mortals, goes beneath the earth, / nor where he rises” (10.191–192). Merry takes the words as no more than an expansion of ἠώς and ζόφος, east and west: Odysseus has no idea where they are (on 10.190; see [[eos]] and the translation's note on 10.190). The island to which they have come is placed, when they return to it, where “the house and dancing floors of early-born Dawn / and the risings of the Sun” are (12.3–4, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.135; see [[aeaea]]).

**The light of the sun.** When Circe tells him that he must first go to the house of Hades, “my dear heart was broken, / and I wept as I sat on the bed, and my heart / no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun” (10.496–498; see [[hades]]). The words are Menelaus' on the sands of Pharos, when Proteus told him of his brother's murder (4.538–540; see the Book 4 note), where he wept “as I sat on the sand” (4.539) and Odysseus “as I sat on the bed” (10.497); and the Iliad's “lives and looks on the light of the sun” is life itself (*Iliad* 18.61).
