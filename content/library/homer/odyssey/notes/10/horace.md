---
summary: Horace makes Circe's cups, which his Ulysses did not drink, a lesson in wisdom, and puts the Laestrygonian Antiphates first among the wonders of the Odyssey. His odes trace Aelius Lamia's line to Lamus, founder of Formiae, the name that Merry takes for the Laestrygonian king's, and name the ridges of Telegonus, Circe's son.
---

- **Circe's cups.** Homer set before us Ulysses as a useful pattern of what virtue and wisdom can do: “you know the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups; if he had drunk them with his companions, foolish and greedy, he would have been, under a harlot mistress, base and senseless, and would have lived an unclean dog or a sow that loves the mud” (*Sirenum voces et Circae pocula nosti*, *Epistles* 1.2.17–26, a plain rendering; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). In Homer Odysseus does drink Circe's drink and is not bewitched, for Hermes' drug protects him (10.316–318); it is the companions who drink and are penned as swine (10.237–240): [[circe]], [[drugs]] and [[sties]].
- **Antiphates first.** Homer does not open with a blaze but means to bring light out of smoke, “so that he may then bring out his striking wonders, Antiphates and Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” (*Antiphaten Scyllamque et cum Cyclope Charybdim*, *Ars Poetica* 143–145, a plain rendering). The first of the four is the Laestrygonian king of this book (10.106–132): [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]].
- **Lamus.** Horace's ode to Aelius Lamia derives his friend's line “from ancient Lamus”, from whom the earlier Lamiae took their name, “that founder who is said to have first held the walls of Formiae and the Liris flooding Marica's shores, a lord far and wide” (*Odes* 3.17.1–9, a plain rendering). Merry, who takes Lamus of “the steep citadel of Lamus” (10.81) for a name of the Laestrygonian king, cites the ode for that use of the name, with Ovid (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.81): [[telepylus]], [[laestrygonians]] and [[ovid]].
- **Telegonus.** In an ode to Maecenas, beside Tibur and the slopes of Aefula, Horace names “the ridges of Telegonus the parricide” (*Telegoni iuga parricidae*, *Odes* 3.29.6–8); Merry cites the line for Telegonus, Circe's son by Odysseus in later legend, as the founder of Tusculum (Merry on 10.135; see [[hesiod]]): [[circe]].
