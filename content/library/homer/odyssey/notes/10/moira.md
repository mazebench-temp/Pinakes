summary: Odysseus tells Aeolus the story of Troy “fair and right” (κατὰ μοῖραν, 10.16); on Aeaea he bids his men eat, for they will not go down to the house of Hades “before our fated day comes upon us” (10.175); and Eurylochus brings “the news of his companions and of their bitter fate” (10.245).
# μόρσιμον (10.175), only here in the Odyssey.
grc: [μόρσιμον]
en: [fated day]
---

For a month Aeolus asks about Troy and the homecoming of the Achaeans, “and I told him all of it fair and right” (πάντα κατὰ μοῖραν κατέλεξα, 10.16; see [[aeolus]] and [[nostos]]). On Aeaea, after the Laestrygonians, Odysseus rouses his men to eat with a word of fate: “Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief, / into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us” (πρὶν μόρσιμον ἦμαρ ἐπέλθῃ, 10.174–175; see [[achos]] and [[hades]]), πρίν taking a subjunctive without ἄν or κέν, as it does after a negative (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.175). The thought is Hector's comfort to Andromache, “for no man will hurl me down to Hades beyond my portion; / and no man, I say, has ever escaped his fate” (*Iliad* 6.487–488; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). Eurylochus comes back from Circe's house “to bring the news of his companions and of their bitter fate” (ἀδευκέα πότμον, 10.245; see the translation's table of key words, ἀδευκὴς πότμος, and [[eurylochus]]).
