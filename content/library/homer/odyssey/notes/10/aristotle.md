---
summary: The History of Animals defends Homer's “ox nine years old”, and a note in the scholia, collected among the fragments of the Homeric Problems, gives Aristotle's reading of Circe's epithet αὐδήεσσα. The Latin third book of the Economics makes Odysseus lie with Circe only for his companions' sake. The Meteorology and the collection of marvels handed down under his name know the Liparaean islands as the islands of Aeolus, a deadly drug of the Circaean mountain and a place called Pyriphlegethon near Cyme.
---

- **The ox nine years old.** The ox is at its prime at five years old, “and that is why some say that Homer composed rightly when he wrote ‘a male five years old’ and ‘of an ox nine years old’; for they mean the same” (*History of Animals* 6.21, a plain rendering). The first phrase is a sacrificial bull's (*Iliad* 7.315; *Odyssey* 19.420); the second is in Homer only here, of the ox whose hide Aeolus flayed for the skin of the winds (10.19). Merry, quoting the passage on this line, finds the two ages hard to reconcile unless a ὥρη is a half-year, reports Bothe's reading that oxen are in their prime at both five and nine, and himself takes ἐννέωρος for “nine years old” from the verse on the Aloadae (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.19): [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[cattle]].
- **Circe's voice.** Circe is δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, “a dread goddess with human voice” (10.136). In the scholia gathered as a fragment of the *Homeric Problems*, Aristophanes takes the goddesses so called to be those in human form who have a share of voice, Aristotle reads οὐδήεσσα, “of the earth”, and another note gives Aristotle the view that the word means speaking in a human way, not as a goddess, since the gods speak through signs, birds, victims, sacrifices and dreams, and that Circe is called a fine singer as a human being is (*Homeric Problems* fr. 394 Gigon, from the scholia on 5.334 and 10.136; 10.227). Merry adds what the scholia say of the two places: one manuscript's note on this line gives the reading οὐδήεσσα to Aristarchus, which Dindorf changed to Aristotle, and other scholia say that Aristotle wrote οὐδήεσσα of Ino but αὐλήεσσα of Circe and Calypso, as living alone in their own halls, which Merry thinks impossible (Merry's critical notes on 5.334 and 10.136; see the translation's note on 10.135): [[with-human-voice]], [[circe]] and [[leucothea]].
- **For his companions' sake.** In the third book of the *Economics*, extant only in Latin, Odysseus is the model of a husband's faith. When the daughter of Atlas asked him to live with her and promised to make him immortal, he would not betray his wife even for immortality; “for he did not wish to lie with Circe except for the safety of his friends”, and answered her that nothing could seem sweeter to him than his own country, though it was rugged (*Economics* 3.3, a plain rendering; fr. 101 Gigon). Hermes gives that reason in this book, “so that she may free your companions and care for you yourself” (10.298), and Odysseus makes it his condition before he eats (10.383–387); the answer about his rugged country is the one he gives Alcinous (9.27–28): [[circe]], [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]].
- **The islands of Aeolus.** The Liparaean islands are, for Aristotle, “the islands called of Aeolus”. On Hiera, one of them, the earth once swelled up into a mound with a roar and burst, throwing out wind, sparks and ash that covered the city of the Liparaeans and reached some of the cities of Italy; and around these islands, when the south wind is about to blow, the places from which the blasts come give warning with a roar (*Meteorology* 2.8, 367a1–17). The collection of marvels names Lipara as one of “the seven islands called of Aeolus” (*On Marvellous Things Heard* 101). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, explained the dispenser of the winds by such forecasts (*Geography* 6.2.10): [[aeolus]], [[aeolia]] and [[polybius]].
- **The Circaean mountain and Pyriphlegethon.** The same collection tells of a deadly drug that grows on the Circaean mountain in Italy, which, sprinkled on a man, makes him fall at once, his hair drop off and his limbs fall apart (*On Marvellous Things Heard* 78), and of the lake Aornos near Cyme in Italy: on the mainland not far from it hot water breaks out in many places, “and the whole place is called Pyriphlegethon” (*On Marvellous Things Heard* 102, a plain rendering): [[drugs]] and [[pyriphlegethon]].
