---
summary: Plato strikes Circe's verse on the shadows of the dead out of the poetry fit for his guardians, and in the Meno turns the same verse into praise of a statesman who could teach his art. The rivers of the Phaedo's underworld are the rivers of Circe's directions, and the Cratylus explains why men feared the name of Persephone.
---

- **The shadows.** Among the verses that make Hades fearful and must be struck out is the end of Circe's words on Teiresias, “that he alone should be wise; but the others flit as shadows” (*Republic* 386d, quoting 10.495 with ταί for τοί; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 10.495), and with such verses go the dreadful names, Cocytus and Styx (*Republic* 387b–c): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]], [[teiresias]], [[cocytus]] and [[styx]].
- **A true thing among shadows.** Virtue, Socrates concludes in the *Meno*, comes to those who have it by a divine dispensation, unless there were a statesman able to make another man a statesman; he would be among the living what Homer said Teiresias was among the dead, “he alone of those in Hades has his wits, but the others flit as shadows”, a true thing among shadows as regards virtue (*Meno* 99e–100a, plain renderings; 10.494–495): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]] and [[teiresias]].
- **The rivers.** In the myth of the *Phaedo* four of the many streams under the earth are named. Oceanus is the greatest and flows outermost in a circle; opposite it flows the Acheron, through desert places and under the earth into the Acherusian lake, where the souls of most of the dead come and wait their appointed times before they are sent back to be born. The third, between them, falls into a great place burning with fire and makes a lake of boiling water and mud, winds round under the earth past the Acherusian lake without mixing with it, and plunges into Tartarus: this is the one “they call Pyriphlegethon”, whose fragments the streams of lava blow up wherever they reach the surface. Opposite it the fourth falls into a wild place the color of dark blue enamel and makes the lake called Styx, then winds round the other way and meets the Pyriphlegethon at the Acherusian lake, and its name, “as the poets say, is Cocytus”. Souls whose crimes can be healed are thrown into Tartarus and cast out after a year, killers by the Cocytus and those who struck father or mother by the Pyriphlegethon, and at the Acherusian lake they cry out to those they wronged (*Phaedo* 112e–114b, plain renderings). In Circe's directions the ship crosses Oceanus, and the Pyriphlegethon and the Cocytus, “a branch of the water of Styx”, flow into the Acheron (10.508–515): [[acheron]], [[pyriphlegethon]], [[cocytus]], [[styx]] and [[oceanus]].
- **Dread Persephone.** Many fear the name Pherrephatta, Socrates says in the *Cratylus*, and changing it they look at “Phersephone” and it seems dreadful to them; but the name shows that the goddess is wise, for she touches and follows what is in motion, and that is why Hades, who is wise, lives with her (*Cratylus* 404c–d). Merry cites the passage on ἐπαινή, “dread”, this book's epithet of Persephone, but reports it as saying that the name was avoided as too awful and Persephassa or Phersephassa preferred (Merry on 10.491; see the translation's note on 10.491): [[persephone]] and [[dread]].
