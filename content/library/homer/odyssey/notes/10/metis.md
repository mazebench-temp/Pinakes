---
summary: On Aeaea, not knowing where the sun rises or sets, Odysseus bids his men consider “whether there will still be any plan. I for my part do not think there is” (10.192–193).
---

After the night on the shore of Aeaea, Odysseus calls an assembly and speaks of his helplessness: “friends, we do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn, / nor where the sun, who brings light to mortals, goes beneath the earth, / nor where he rises; but let us consider quickly / whether there will still be any plan. I for my part do not think there is” (εἴ τις ἔτʼ ἔσται μῆτις, 10.190–193; see [[assembly]] and [[eos]]). ζόφος and ἠώς together are, in Merry's view, only a general way of saying “everywhere”: Odysseus has not the least idea where they are. Crates and Strabo took ζόφος for the north, and the scholia thought the hero aghast at a wandering that had confused all the ordinary signs (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.190; see the translation's note on 10.190). The word comes only here in the book, from the man Circe calls “Odysseus of many devices” (10.401, 10.488, 10.504; see [[of-many-devices]]). His plan, when he makes one, is to divide the men and let the lot decide who goes to the smoke (10.203–207; see [[lots]]).
