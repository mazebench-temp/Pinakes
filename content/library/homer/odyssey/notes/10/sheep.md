summary: The sheep of Book 10 are the silver-white flocks of the Laestrygonians, driven home at nightfall as the cattle go out (10.82–86); the black sheep that Odysseus is to vow to Teiresias, the best of his flocks (10.524–525); and the ram and the black ewe that he is to sacrifice to the dead and that Circe ties up beside his ship (10.527–528, 10.571–572), the sheep that will lie slaughtered at the pit (10.532).

**The flocks of Telepylus.** In the land of the Laestrygonians, “herdsman hails herdsman / as he drives his flock in, and the other answers as he drives his herd out. / There a man who took no sleep could have earned two wages, / one by herding cattle, the other by pasturing silver-white sheep; / for the paths of night and of day are close together” (10.82–86; see [[telepylus]], [[cattle]] and [[silver-white]]). ποιμήν is here the herdsman of either kind: the one who drives his flock in at nightfall is the shepherd, the one who drives his herd out is the neatherd, and the sheep, ἄργυφα μῆλα, are coming home as the cattle go out to pasture (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, in his note on 10.81; see the translation's row ποιμήν (of herdsmen) and its note on 10.81–86).

**The black sheep for Teiresias.** Circe bids Odysseus vow to the dead a barren heifer in Ithaca, “and that to Teiresias apart you will sacrifice a sheep, to him alone, / all black, that stands out among your flocks” (10.524–525; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). ἀπάνευθεν is “at a different spot”, so that the offering to him should not be confounded with the general offering to the dead (Merry on 10.524). παμμέλας, “all black”: white victims, Merry notes, were offered to the gods of the upper world and black to those of the world below, and he compares Virgil (on 10.525), whose Aeneas, before he goes down to the dead, strikes with his sword a lamb of black fleece for the mother of the Eumenides and her great sister, and a barren cow for Proserpina (*Aeneid* 6.249–251; see [[virgil]]). Homer does not keep to the rule: the bulls that Nestor's people sacrifice to Poseidon on the shore at Pylos are “all black” too (3.6). Merry cites these black victims, with the silver-white sheep of Telepylus, when he defends the dark wool of the Cyclops' rams against Ernesti, who would make it white: Homer's sheep are white and black (Merry on 9.426; see the Book 9 note).

**The ram and the black ewe.** “then sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, / turning them toward Erebus” (10.527–528; see [[ram]] and [[erebus]]). ὄις is a sheep of either sex: ὄιν ἀρνειόν is “a ram”, ὄιν θῆλυν “a ewe”, θῆλυς serving with the masculine ending for the feminine, as in θῆλυς ἐέρση, “the fresh dew” (5.467; Merry on 10.527). When the vows are made and the victims killed, the companions are to flay and burn “the sheep that lie there slaughtered with the pitiless bronze” and pray to Hades and Persephone (10.531–534; see [[hades]] and [[persephone]]). κατάκειτʼ, “lie”, is the right reading, Merry says, and κατέκειτʼ has come in from the doing of the rite in Book 11 (Merry on 10.532 and his critical note; 11.45). Circe herself brings the two beasts and ties them up beside the ship before he sails (10.571–572; see the Book 10 note on [[ram]]).
