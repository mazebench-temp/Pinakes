summary: Sent away by Aeolus, the men row on, worn down by the painful rowing (10.78–79). In the harbor of the Laestrygonians Odysseus bids his crew lay to their oars, and his ship alone escapes (10.128–132).
# εἰρεσίης, "rowing" (10.78; later 12.225, and εἰρεσίῃ 11.640), first met here; it applies in the Odyssey only.
grc: [εἰρεσίης]
en: [rowing]
---

**Without a wind.** When Aeolus has sent them away, “the men's spirit was worn down by the painful rowing / through our own folly, since no escort showed itself any longer” (10.78–79; see [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[pompe]]): πομπή is the wafting wind that had carried them, and ἐπεί gives the reason why they had to take to their oars (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.79).

**The escape from the Laestrygonians.** Odysseus cuts his ship's cables, “And at once I urged on my companions and bade them / lay to their oars, so that we might escape from evil, / and they all tossed up the sea with their oars, fearing destruction” (10.128–130; see [[stern-cables]] and [[laestrygonians]]). The verse of 10.129 is the escape from the Cyclops (9.489; see the Book 9 note). ἅλα, “the sea”, is the reading of Callistratus and Rhianus, which Merry prefers for the formula of the Phaeacian oarsmen, ἀναρρίπτειν ἅλα πηδῷ, “tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.328, 13.78); most manuscripts have ἅμα (Merry on 10.130 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.130). “Gladly out to the open sea, away from the overhanging rocks, fled / my ship; but the others perished there in a body” (10.131–132; see [[ships]]).
