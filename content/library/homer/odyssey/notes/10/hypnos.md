---
summary: Aeolus' children sleep beside their wives on corded beds (10.11–12). Sleep overcomes Odysseus within sight of Ithaca, and he names it with his companions as the cause of his ruin, “cruel sleep” (10.31–33, 10.68–69). Elpenor, “sodden with wine”, sleeps on Circe's roof and falls to his death when the companions rouse one another (10.548–560).
# εὕδουσʼ (10.12), εὕδοντες (10.548), κατελέξατο "lay down to sleep" (10.555), each only here in the Odyssey.
grc: [εὕδουσʼ, εὕδοντες, κατελέξατο]
---

**Aeolus' house.** The children of Aeolus feast all day, “and at night again, beside their modest wives, / they sleep on rugs and on corded beds” (10.11–12; see [[aeolus]], [[marriage]] and [[corded]]).

**The fatal sleep.** For nine days Odysseus holds the sheet himself, and on the tenth, with the fires of Ithaca in sight, “sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (10.31; see [[sweet-sleep]]). The aorist stands against the imperfects of the verses before, the long sailing and the sudden sleep (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.28), and while he sleeps the companions open the skin; he wakes to the storm (10.34–50; see [[hetairos]] and [[skin-of-the-winds]]). He tells Aeolus that his companions led him into blind folly, “and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (ὕπνος / σχέτλιος, 10.68–69; see [[ate]] and [[hard]]), and the scholium on “our own folly” (10.79) puts his sleep among the causes (Merry on 10.79; see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**A land for the sleepless.** In the land of the Laestrygonians, “There a man who took no sleep could have earned two wages, / one by herding cattle, the other by pasturing silver-white sheep; / for the paths of night and of day are close together” (ἄυπνος, 10.84–86; see [[telepylus]], [[sleepless]] and the translation's note on 10.81–86).

**Sleep on Aeaea.** Sleep returns with the feast: on the shore after the stag, “and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore” (κοιμήθημεν, 10.186), and in Circe's house through the year, “they lay down to sleep throughout the shadowy halls” (κοιμήσαντο, 10.479; see [[sunset-lines]]).

**Elpenor.** On the morning of departure Odysseus goes through the house rousing the companions: “No longer sleep now, drowsing in sweet sleep” (μηκέτι νῦν εὕδοντες ἀωτεῖτε γλυκὺν ὕπνον, 10.548). ἀωτεῖν may come from the root of ἄημι, “breathe”, “breathing sleep's deep breath”, or, as a scholium has it, “pluck the flower of sleep”, like the Latin *carpere somnos* (Merry on 10.548; see the translation's note on 10.548). Elpenor, “longing for the cool air, had lain down to sleep, sodden with wine” on the flat roof (κατελέξατο οἰνοβαρείων, 10.555; see [[wine-sodden]]), and waking at the din he forgets the ladder and falls (10.556–560; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).
