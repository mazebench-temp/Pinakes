---
summary: Dawn brings three days on Aeaea: “Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day” after the landing, when Odysseus climbs to the lookout and kills the stag (10.144); “early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” on the morning of the assembly and the lots (10.187); and “Dawn of the golden throne came” at the end of Circe's instructions for the journey to the dead (10.541). In between, the lost companions do not know “where the darkness is, nor where the dawn” (10.190).
# ἠώς at 10.190 is the east, the quarter of the sky where Dawn rises, and is excepted in the entry.
---

**The third day.** The one ship left from the harbor of the Laestrygonians comes to Aeaea, and the men lie on the shore for two days and two nights, “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together. / But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day, / then I took my spear and my sharp sword / and went up quickly from beside the ship to a lookout” (10.143–146; see [[aeaea]] and [[with-lovely-tresses]]). The verse brought Odysseus in sight of Scheria after two days in the sea (5.390) and ended the two days of rest after the storm of Zeus in Book 9 (9.76; see the Book 9 note), each time after two days and two nights of suffering (5.388, 9.74, 10.142; see the translation's formula row).

**The morning of the lots.** After the feast on the stag and a night on the shore (10.183–186; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[helios]]), “when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / then I called an assembly and spoke among them all” (10.187–188 = 9.170–171; see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]] and [[rosy-fingered]]). Odysseus divides the companions into two bands, and the lot sends Eurylochus to Circe's house (10.203–209; see [[lots]] and [[eurylochus]]).

**Where the dawn is.** In the assembly Odysseus tells his companions: “friends, we do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn, / nor where the sun, who brings light to mortals, goes beneath the earth, / nor where he rises” (10.190–192). How could a man who has just seen the sun set and the Dawn appear profess not to know east from west? Crates and Strabo took ζόφος for the north, but Merry answers that the words that follow make that impossible: ἠώς and ζόφος are a kind of exhaustive division of the world, as in the Ethiopians “some where Hyperion sets and some where he rises” (1.23–24), the Phaeacians' question “whether from the men of the east or from the men of the west” (8.29), and Hector's birds that fly “toward the dawn and the sun” or “toward the misty darkness” (*Iliad* 12.239–240), and all Odysseus means is that he has not the least idea where they are (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.190; see the translation's note on 10.190). ἠώς here is the quarter of the sky, as in Book 9's “toward the dawn and the sun” (9.26; see the Book 9 note), and the line is not counted for the goddess.

**Dawn of the golden throne.** Circe ends her instructions for the journey to the house of Hades, “So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (10.541; see [[circe]] and [[golden-throned]]). The verse returns when she has told Odysseus the way past the Sirens and Scylla (12.142), and again in Telemachus' departure from Sparta and on the day of the suitors' death (15.56, 20.91; see the translation's formula row). The goddess dresses at dawn: “And she put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing; / and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle” (10.542–543; see [[nymphs]]).
