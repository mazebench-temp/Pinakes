---
summary: In the fourteenth book of the Metamorphoses Odysseus' companion Macareus tells this book's adventures in order: Aeolus' gift of the winds shut in an ox's hide, the city of the Laestrygonian Lamus and its king Antiphates, and the year with Circe, among her fawning beasts, her drugged cups and her sties, Mercury's moly and the companions made men again. Merry cites Ovid for the tame beasts, the restoration and a grief that swallows speech.
---

- **Aeolus.** Macareus tells of “Aeolus, son of Hippotes”, who reigns over the Tuscan deep and holds the winds in prison; the Dulichian leader took them shut in an ox's hide, a memorable gift, sailed nine days with a following wind and saw the land he sought; on the next dawn the companions, overcome by envy and the desire for plunder and thinking it gold, untied the winds, and were carried back over the waters they had crossed to the harbor of the Aeolian king (*Metamorphoses* 14.223–232, plain renderings; 10.1–55): [[aeolus]], [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[odysseus-and-aeolus]].
- **The Laestrygonians.** “From there we came to the ancient city of the Laestrygonian Lamus; Antiphates ruled in that land.” Macareus himself was one of the three sent to the king, and he and one companion barely escaped, while the third stained the Laestrygonian's impious mouth with his blood; Antiphates roused his people, who hurled rocks and beams and sank men and ships, and only the ship that carried Ulysses escaped (*Metamorphoses* 14.233–242, plain renderings; 10.100–132). Merry cites the first verse for Lamus as the name of the king (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.81): [[laestrygonians]], [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]], [[telepylus]] and [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]].
- **Circe's house.** Remembering Antiphates and the Cyclops, the men refused to go; the lot sent Macareus, faithful Polites, Eurylochus, “Elpenor, too fond of wine”, and eighteen others to Circe's walls. A thousand wolves, with bears and lionesses, met them at the threshold and frightened them, but none was to be feared or would wound them, and they wagged their tails and fawned on them as they followed; Merry quotes the lines on the beasts' tameness (*Metamorphoses* 14.248–259; Merry on 10.213). Handmaids led them through marble halls to their mistress, who sat on a throne in a shining robe with a golden mantle over it, while her Nereids and nymphs, who card no wool and spin no thread, sorted herbs and flowers into baskets and she weighed the herbs and knew their uses (*Metamorphoses* 14.260–270). She had roasted barley mixed with honey, strong wine and curdled milk, with juices hidden under the sweetness, and when they had drunk and her wand touched the tops of their hair they grew bristles, lost their speech, fell forward on the ground with snouts and swollen necks, and were shut in a sty; Eurylochus alone had shunned the cup (*Metamorphoses* 14.271–290; 10.210–260): [[circe]], [[polites-the-companion]], [[eurylochus]], [[elpenor]], [[kykeon]] and [[sties]].
- **The moly and the restoration.** “The peace-bringing Cyllenian had given him a white flower; the gods call it moly; it is held by a black root.” Safe with it, Ulysses entered, thrust back Circe's wand as she tried to stroke his hair, and frightened her with his drawn sword; then pledges were given, and received into her bed he asked as his marriage gift his companions' bodies (*Metamorphoses* 14.291–298, plain renderings; 10.302–347). They were sprinkled with the better juices of an unknown herb, struck on the head with the reversed wand, and words were spoken against the former words; the bristles fell, the cloven feet were gone, shoulders and arms came back, and weeping they clung to their weeping leader's neck (*Metamorphoses* 14.299–307), the lines Merry sets beside 10.393–399 (Merry on 10.393). A year held them there, and one of the four handmaids told Macareus the story of Picus (*Metamorphoses* 14.308–319; 10.348–351, 10.467): [[moly]], [[hermes]], [[wand]] and [[handmaids]].
- **Grief without words.** For Eurylochus, who came back unable to speak a word for grief, his eyes filled with tears (10.244–248), Merry quotes Hecuba at the sight of Polydorus' body, whose voice and rising tears grief itself swallowed (*Metamorphoses* 13.538–540; Merry on 10.248): [[eurylochus]].
- **A floating island.** For πλωτή, the floating island of Aeolus (10.3), Merry compares Pindar's Delos, carried by the waves and the winds, and Ovid's “wandering Delos”, which received Latona “when it was a light island that floated” (*Metamorphoses* 6.333–334, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.3): [[aeolia]] and [[pindar]].
