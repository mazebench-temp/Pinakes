---
summary: The sea of Book 10 takes the ships back from within sight of Ithaca, when the storm carries the men “out to sea” and Odysseus thinks of throwing himself into it (10.47–55); it is the open water to which the last ship escapes from the Laestrygonians' harbor (10.130–131), and the boundless sea that rings Aeaea (10.195). Circe sums up the wanderings as “the sorrows you suffered on the fish-filled sea” and the harm men did them “on land” (10.457–459).
---

**Out to sea.** When the companions open the skin, “at once the storm wind seized them and carried them out to sea, / weeping, away from their native land” (10.48–49; see [[aeolia]] and [[ithaca]]). Odysseus wakes and ponders “whether I should fall from the ship and perish in the sea, / or endure in silence and remain still among the living”, and endures, lying in the ship with his head covered (10.50–54; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The open sea.** At Telepylus the men row for their lives: “and they all tossed up the sea with their oars, fearing destruction. / Gladly out to the open sea, away from the overhanging rocks, fled / my ship” (10.130–132; see [[oars]] and [[laestrygonians]]). ἅλα, “the sea”, is the reading of Callistratus and Rhianus, where most manuscripts have ἅμα; W. W. Merry prefers it for the formula ἀναρρίπτειν ἅλα πηδῷ, Alcinous' boast of his young men “tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (7.328; again 13.78; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.130; see the translation's note on 10.130). The harbor's calm water was the trap, and the open sea is safety (see [[harbor]]).

**Sea and land.** From the lookout on Aeaea Odysseus sees an island “all around which the boundless sea is set as a crown” (10.195; see [[aeaea]]). Circe, bidding the men eat and drink until their spirit comes back, sums up all they have been through in a pair: “I myself know too / both all the sorrows you suffered on the fish-filled sea, / and all the harm that merciless men did you on land” (10.457–459; see [[fish-filled]] and [[earth]]), the storms on the one hand and the Cicones, the Cyclops and the Laestrygonians on the other; and the last word of her instructions is the sea still to be crossed, Teiresias “will tell you the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea” (10.539–540).
