---
summary: Aeolus ties the skin of the winds with a cord of silver, and the companions imagine gold and silver inside it (10.23–24, 10.35, 10.45). In Circe's house the welcome is served with silver tables, golden baskets and cups, a silver bowl, and a golden pitcher over a silver basin (10.354–357, 10.368–369), and Circe dresses in a silver-white mantle and a golden girdle (10.543–545).
---

**The skin.** Aeolus binds the skin in the ship “with a shining cord / of silver, so that not even a little breath might slip past” (10.23–24). This implies, Merry says, a somewhat advanced metallurgy: the silver drawn into a wire fine enough to plait into a cord, which could tie the neck of the leather bag so tight that no breath of wind could slip through (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.23; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]). The companions think it holds treasure: “But come, quickly, let us see what these are, / how much gold and silver is in the skin” (10.44–45; see [[doron]] and [[hetairos]]).

**Circe's house.** In Circe's house the precious metals are the furniture of welcome and of guile. The drugged drink is served in a golden cup (10.316; see [[kykeon]]). After the oath the handmaids set silver tables before the chairs and golden baskets on them, mix the wine in a silver bowl and serve it in golden cups (10.354–357), and the water for the hands is poured from a golden pitcher over a silver basin (10.368–369; see [[handmaids]] and [[basin-and-pitcher]]). At dawn the nymph puts on “a great silver-white mantle, / fine and lovely”, and “a girdle, / fine and golden” (10.543–545; see [[clothing]] and [[girdle]]). The gods of the book are golden too: Hermes “of the golden wand” (10.277, 10.331; see [[of-the-golden-wand]]) and “Dawn of the golden throne” (10.541; see [[golden-throned]]).
