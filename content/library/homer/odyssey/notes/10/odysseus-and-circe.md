summary: The adventure fills the rest of the book (10.133–574) and more than a year of the tale. Two days of grief on the shore of Aeaea; a day of the lookout and the stag; a day of the lots, the swine, Hermes and the moly, Circe overcome and the companions restored; a year of feasting; and a last night of entreaty and instructions, ended by the dawn of departure and the death of Elpenor. Odysseus' word on the shore, “we shall not go down yet … into the house of Hades” (10.174–175), is answered at the end by Circe's command to go there (10.490–491).

**The days.** The ship comes in to the shore in silence, σιωπῇ, as men frightened, Eustathius explains, by the inhospitality of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians, “and some god guided us”, as at the island of goats (10.140–141; 9.142; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.140). The days are counted as they were after the storm that followed the raid on the Cicones: two days and two nights on the shore, eating their hearts out, and the third brought by Dawn of the lovely tresses (10.142–144; 10.143–144 = 9.75–76). The third day ends with the feast on the stag and the sunset verses of the island of goats (10.183–186; 9.161–162, 9.168–169; see [[sunset-lines]]); the fourth, which begins with the dawn verse (10.187; see [[dawn-line]]), holds everything from the lots to the welcome of the whole crew in Circe's house (10.187–466). Then the year passes in four verses (10.467–470), and a last day, again closed by the sunset verses, gives way to a night of entreaty and instructions, until “at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (10.476–541).

**Not yet to the house of Hades.** On the third day Odysseus rouses his companions over the stag: “Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief, / into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us” (10.174–175; see [[deer]] and [[hades]]). The book ends with the same house as their road, εἰς Ἀίδαο δόμους, “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone” (10.491, 10.564). The companions had lain on the shore with their heads covered, in despair, as Odysseus lay in the ship after the winds escaped (10.179, 10.53; Merry on 10.179). His speech at the assembly opens twice, κέκλυτε … ὦ φίλοι, quite contrary to Homeric custom, and Callistratus thought its first verse put in front by someone who did not know Homer's way (10.189; Merry on 10.189 and his critical note). “We do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn” (10.190) troubled the commentators, since Odysseus has just seen the sun set and the dawn appear; Crates and Strabo took ζόφος as the north, but Merry takes the pair as a general division of the world, “he has not the least idea where they are” (on 10.190; see the translation's note on 10.190). The weeping that follows, “but there was no profit for them in their weeping” (10.202), returns in the same verse when they hear of the journey to the dead (10.568).

**The lots and the beasts.** The lots were pebbles shaken in a helmet, and the one that leapt out first decided (10.206–207; Merry on 10.207; see [[lots]] and [[helmet]]). With Eurylochus go two and twenty companions, weeping, and those left behind lament (10.208–209). Of Circe's beasts Merry weighs the two explanations of the scholia, wild animals made tame by her drugs, as Scaliger took it, or men transformed: κατέθελξεν is the verb of the sorcery that ends in the sty (10.213, 10.291), Eurylochus speaks of Circe making them all swine or wolves or lions (10.433), and her “evil drugs” are set against Hermes' “good drug” (10.213, 10.292), so he prefers the second (on 10.213; see the translation's note on 10.212–213, [[thelgo]] and [[shape-changing]]).

**The swine.** The men go in “in their ignorance”, ἀιδρείῃσιν (10.231), and Odysseus tells what happened inside, the mixture, the drugs, the wand and the sty (10.233–243), which Eurylochus, waiting outside, could not see. Circe's drugs were given “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.236), yet the minds of the swine were steadfast, as before (10.240): Merry asks whether her sorcery had fully succeeded, and points out that the men did not know Odysseus again until she anointed them with another drug (on 10.240; 10.392–397; see [[drugs]] and [[sties]]). Zenodotus read καὶ πόδας, “and the feet”, for καὶ δέμας, and “Aristarchus did not know” the verse of the acorns and the cornel (10.240, 10.242; Merry's critical notes; see the translation's notes on 10.240 and 10.242).

**Eurylochus' report.** The manuscripts have Eurylochus come back αἶψʼ, “at once” (10.244); Voss conjectured ἂψ, “back”, which most editors accept, because he says he “sat a long while and kept watch” (10.260); if αἶψα is kept, it is the haste with which he came once he knew the companions were lost (Merry's critical note on 10.244; see the translation's note on 10.244). He cannot speak at first, and then tells his news in a broken asyndeton, ἤιομεν … εὕρομεν, “We went … we found”, the broken utterance of an excited messenger (10.246–251; Merry on 10.251). His report is made of the narrator's verses, down to “but I stayed behind” for “but Eurylochus stayed behind” (10.252–258; 10.210–211, 10.226–232); 10.253 is wanting in most manuscripts (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 10.253). Of his plea at Odysseus' knees, 10.265 is wanting in most manuscripts and bracketed since Wolf (Merry's critical note), and in σῶν ἑτάρων, “your companions”, Aristarchus read σῶον, “safe”, perhaps thinking “your” wrong in the mouth of Eurylochus, whose comrades they were too: a pedantic distinction, Merry thinks (10.268; Merry on 10.268; see [[eurylochus]] and [[supplication]]).

**The plan foretold.** Hermes meets Odysseus “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful” (10.278–279), the second verse being the verse of his likeness when he met Priam (*Iliad* 24.348; see [[hermes]]); Odysseus seems to know him at once, and Merry suggests that painting or sculpture had already given the gods their characteristic types (on 10.279). Hermes tells him beforehand everything that will happen: the mixed drink and the drugs, the blow of the wand, the drawn sword, Circe's fear, her bed, and the oath to be demanded first (10.286–301). The scene then follows his words point by point, many of them in his own verses (10.316–347; 10.293–301), so that the danger is told twice, once as foreknowledge and once as event. Circe herself does not know that Odysseus carries an amulet against her drugs: when she says “there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched” (10.329), Merry explains, she means the strong and stubborn mind that was generally proof against sorcery, and the objection of the scholia, that the swine kept their minds too, is more apparent than real (on 10.329; see [[moly]] and the translation's note on 10.329).

**The bath and the meal.** Merry suspects the verses that make Circe's four handmaids daughters of springs, groves and rivers (10.350–351), and the scholia made them the four seasons, fit servants for a daughter of the Sun (on 10.350–351; see [[handmaids]]). The verses of the handwashing and the housekeeper's bread, found in other houses of the poem (10.368–372 = 1.136–140, 4.52–56, 7.172–176), Köchly thought borrowed, and inept in a house where four maids have just been seen preparing the meal; La Roche judged them no older than the thirteenth century, since Eustathius and the scholia do not know them (Merry's critical note on 10.348; see the translation's note on 10.368–372). Odysseus sits ἀλλοφρονέων, “with my thoughts elsewhere” (10.374; Merry on 10.374). His answer to Circe puts its first πρίν before the second as a preparation, and distinguishes his act from hers: λύσασθαι, the middle, of his own freeing of his companions, λῦσον of hers (10.383–387; Merry on 10.384, 10.385).

**The crew at Circe's house.** The men left at the ship ask, “tell us of the destruction of the rest of our companions” (10.421), in the words in which Eurylochus told it (10.250); Odysseus answers that they will find them “drinking and eating; for they have unfailing plenty” (10.427). Eurylochus fears that Circe will make them all beasts “to guard her great house for her even under compulsion” (10.434): like the gold and silver dogs that guard the house of Alcinous, Merry explains (on 10.434; 7.91–94). When the two halves of the crew meet in the hall, φράσσαντό τʼ ἐσάντα, “they knew each other face to face”, is the reading preserved in the scholia for the manuscripts' πάντα (10.453; Merry on 10.453 and his critical note). Murray omits 10.456, which Wolf bracketed, since it is wanting in most manuscripts and in Eustathius, and an address to Odysseus is followed by a plural imperative; Merry would keep it, since Odysseus shows by ἡμῖν, “our proud spirit was persuaded”, that he felt himself included (10.466; Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 10.456). The men are “withered and spiritless” (10.463), the second band rather than the first, whose good condition had been renewed when they were made men again (Merry on 10.463).

**The year and the reminder.** For the year, see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]. The companions call Odysseus δαιμόνιε, “Strange one”, a word that always implies that a man is under some influence beyond the human (10.472; Merry on 10.472; see [[strange-one]]). Five of the verses that follow (10.475–479) and 10.482 are wanting in three manuscripts and in Eustathius; Wolf rejected them, and Merry suggests that they were put in as a stop-gap, since the passage from 474 to 480 is abrupt and an answer from Odysseus to his companions would be expected (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 10.475–479, 482).

**The entreaty.** Odysseus begs Circe by her knees, γούνων (10.481; Merry on 10.481), to fulfill “the promise you made” (10.483). The promise is nowhere stated: it may lie in her oath (10.345) or in her words of welcome (10.460–465), but with the doings of a whole year unrecorded there was room enough for it (Merry on 10.483; see the translation's note on 10.483). Her answer begins with πρῶτον, “first” (10.490), for which Merry compares Helenus' prophecy to Aeneas, whose road too must first pass by the lakes of the underworld (on 10.490, quoting *Aeneid* 3.384–386). Odysseus weeps on the bed until he has had his fill (10.496–499), and asks who will guide them (10.501–502); she tells him to let no longing for a guide trouble him “beside your ship”, as though he might hang about it waiting for a pilot (10.505; Merry on 10.505). Her directions go as far as the beach on Oceanus; in the eleventh book they go along the stream to the place she had pointed out, of which no such definite directions are given here (Merry on 10.511; 11.20–22). For the rite at the pit, see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]].

**The departure.** Dawn comes at once at the end of her instructions (10.541), and Circe dresses Odysseus and herself, in the verses in which Calypso dressed herself on the morning of his departure from Ogygia (10.542–545; 5.230–232; see [[calypso]]). Odysseus had probably slept in the inner part of the house and his companions in the porticoes, so that he had to pass through the whole house to rouse them (10.546; Merry on 10.546). After the fall of Elpenor (10.551–560; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]) he tells the others, φάσθε νύ που, “You think, no doubt”, that they are going home, but Circe has ordained another journey (10.562–565). At the ship they find the ram and the black ewe tethered for the sacrifice: Circe, οἰχομένη, “having withdrawn from us”, and ῥεῖα παρεξελθοῦσα, “slipping past us easily”, had tied them there; the gods are visible and invisible as they please (10.571–574; Merry on 10.573–574, comparing 16.161; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).
