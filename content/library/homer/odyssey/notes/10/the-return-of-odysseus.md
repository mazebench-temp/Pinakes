summary: The homecoming comes within sight and is lost again. On the tenth day from Aeolus' island the native fields are in sight when the companions open the skin of the winds (10.28–55); eleven ships perish in the harbor of the Laestrygonians, as Zeus had pondered after the Cyclops' curse (10.121–132); and after a year with Circe the companions bid Odysseus think of his native land, “if it is decreed for you”, but Circe sends him first to the house of Hades, where Teiresias will tell him of his homecoming (10.472–474, 10.490–495, 10.538–540).

**Within sight.** “For nine days we sailed on, night and day alike, / and on the tenth our native fields were already coming into sight, / and already we could see men tending fires, we were so near” (10.28–30). It is the second time that the tale brings Odysseus all but home: at Malea the North Wind drove him off course (9.79–81; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]), here the companions' folly sets the winds loose while he sleeps (10.31–55; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]). He had kept the sheet himself “so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.33), and the teller gives the outcome before the event: “but he was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (10.26–27). In the summary that he gives Penelope the cause is fate: it was not yet his lot, αἶσα, to reach his dear native land (23.315–316, a plain rendering; see [[aisa]]). Aeolus draws his own conclusion from the return: “off with you, since it is as one hated by the gods that you come here” (10.75).

**The ships.** After the Cyclops' curse Zeus “was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.554–555). The ships go first: eleven of the twelve perish in the harbor of the Laestrygonians with their crews, and only Odysseus' own escapes (10.121–132; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). From here on the tale is the story of one ship, and of the companions who are left in it until Thrinacia (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).

**If it is decreed.** At the end of the year on Aeaea the companions call Odysseus out: “Strange one, think now at last of your native land, / if it is decreed for you to be saved and to reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (10.472–474; see [[thesphaton]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). They speak in the words of the decree that Zeus gave in the council of the gods, “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), which the Cyclops had turned into the condition of his curse, “But if it is his fate to see his friends and reach / his well-built house and his native land” (9.532–533). The text of Merry and Riddell has the curse's οἶκον ἐυκτίμενον at 10.474, where Murray prints οἶκον ἐς ὑψόροφον (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, text of 10.474; see the translation's note on 10.474).

**The way home.** Circe will not send him home directly: “But first you must accomplish another journey” (10.490). Teiresias in the house of Hades “will tell you the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming” (10.539–540), the words with which Eidothea promised Menelaus that Proteus would tell him his way home (4.389–390; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The companions, who thought they were going home, sit down and weep when they hear where they must go (10.562–568). In the next book the seer tells Odysseus that he may still reach Ithaca if he spares the cattle of the Sun, and, if not, that he will come home “late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house”, the Cyclops' curse in its own words (11.100–117; 9.534–535; see [[teiresias]] and [[curses]]).
