**The speaker of the band.** The companions stand in the doorway listening to Circe singing within “as she walked to and fro at a great immortal loom” (10.220–223; see [[circe]] and [[loom]]), and it is Polites who speaks, while Eurylochus, their leader, will stay outside (10.232; see [[eurylochus]]). He is ὄρχαμος ἀνδρῶν, “leader of men” (see [[leader-of-men]]), and Odysseus calls him κήδιστος … κεδνότατός τε, “the nearest and dearest”, a play of sound that W. W. Merry renders in the same English, comparing Ajax's words to Achilles, κήδιστοί τʼ ἔμεναι καὶ φίλτατοι, “to be the nearest and dearest to you” (*Iliad* 9.642; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.225; see the translation's key words and [[hetairos]]).

**His words.** ἀμφιμέμυκεν, “rings all round”, is a verb of lowing: μυκάομαι is said in Homer not only of cattle but of the creaking of hinges and of the ringing blow of a javelin on a shield (Merry on 10.227; see the translation's key words). He cannot tell whether the singer is “either a goddess or a woman”, and Eurylochus repeats the words in his report (10.228, 10.255; see [[lovely-voice]]). They call to her, she opens the shining doors, “and they all went with her in their ignorance” (10.229–231).

**His fate.** Odysseus does not name him again: he drinks the mixed drink with the rest, is struck with the wand and penned in the sties with the heads of swine and a mind unchanged, and is made a man again when Odysseus has bound Circe by her oath (10.233–243, 10.388–399; see [[kykeon]], [[sties]] and [[shape-changing]]).
