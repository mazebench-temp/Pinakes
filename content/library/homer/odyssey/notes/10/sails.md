**The sheet.** With the winds shut in the skin and only the West Wind blowing, the ships sail nine days and nights toward Ithaca (10.25–30; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]). “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was, / for I was always handling the sheet of the ship, and would not give it / to any other of my companions, so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.31–33; see [[odysseus]] and [[hypnos]]). The πόδες are the two ropes at the lower corners of the sail, used to draw it to one side or the other with the set of the wind; only one is named here, W. W. Merry explains, because with the ship running before the wind the sail stays at nearly the same angle, and Odysseus had only to trim it to make the most of the wind (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.32; see the translation's row πόδα νηὸς νωμάω). The scholion, as Merry reports it, offered two senses of πόδα, the rope that pulls the yard round or the steering oar itself; he doubts that πούς ever bears the second meaning (on 10.32; see [[steering-oar]]).

**The white sails.** For the voyage to the house of Hades Circe tells Odysseus to “set up your mast and spread the white sails, / and sit; and the breath of the North Wind will carry her” (10.506–507; see [[mast]], [[white]] and [[boreas]]).
