---
summary: The rivers of Book 10 are the river on Aeaea where the stag comes down to drink (10.159), the sacred rivers that flow to the sea, of which Circe's handmaids are born (10.350–351), and the rivers of the world of the dead in Circe's directions: Acheron, into which Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus flow, “the two loud-resounding rivers” (10.513–515), and the stream toward which Odysseus is to turn from the victims (10.529).
---

**On Aeaea.** On his way back from the lookout Odysseus meets the great stag, “coming down to the river from its pasture in the woods / to drink; for the might of the sun had hold of it” (10.159–160; see [[deer]] and [[aeaea]]). ποταμόνδε, “to the river”, is the only river the book shows on the island. Circe's four handmaids “are born of the springs and of the groves / and of the sacred rivers that flow on to the sea” (10.350–351; see [[handmaids]] and [[nymphs]]): the nymphs of the springs and rivers whom the Iliad calls to the assembly of the gods (*Iliad* 20.7–9). W. W. Merry suspects the two verses, since the present γίγνονται is awkward and the words read like a description of Naiads, Dryads and Nereids (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.350–351; see the translation's note on 10.348–351); εἰς ἅλαδε, “on to the sea”, joins a preposition to an adverb of direction, as ἀπὸ Τροίηθεν, “from Troy” (9.38; Merry on 10.351).

**The rivers of the dead.** Beyond Oceanus, at the house of Hades, “There into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon / and Cocytus, which is a branch of the water of Styx, / and there is a rock, and the meeting of the two loud-resounding rivers” (10.513–515; see [[acheron]], [[pyriphlegethon]], [[cocytus]], [[styx]] and [[loud-resounding]]). ξύνεσις, “the meeting”, is found only here: Merry pictures Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon running higher than Acheron and plunging together over a ledge of rock into it, with ἐριδούπων for the noise of a cataract (on 10.515). After the sacrifice Odysseus is to turn away “making toward the streams of the river” (10.529): the river is Oceanus, Merry explains, toward which he turns his back on the western darkness and faces the living world (on 10.529; see [[oceanus]] and [[erebus]]). Oceanus is itself a river, crossed by ship (10.508), and in the next book Odysseus' mother, among the dead, tells him that between the living and the dead “are great rivers and dread streams, / Oceanus first of all, which no one can cross / on foot, unless he has a well-made ship” (11.157–159, a plain rendering).
