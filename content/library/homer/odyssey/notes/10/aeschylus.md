---
summary: Aeschylus wrote a satyr play called Circe, of which two words survive, and in the Soul-Summoners brought on the consultation of Teiresias for which Circe sends Odysseus. Merry cites the Libation Bearers for the drink offerings to the dead, and the helmsman of the Seven against Thebes for the steering of Odysseus' ship before he fell asleep.
---

- **Circe.** The satyr play *Circe* survives in two words quoted by the lexicographers, αὐτόφορβος, “feeding on oneself”, and ζυγώσω, “I shall yoke” (*Circe* frs. 114–115 Nauck): [[circe]] and [[satyr-play]].
- **The Soul-Summoners.** In the *Soul-Summoners* a chorus of the people who live by a lake honors Hermes as its ancestor, and Teiresias foretells Odysseus' death: a heron flying overhead will strike him with its dung, and from it the spine of a sea creature will rot his aged skin (*Soul-Summoners* frs. 273, 275 Nauck, the second quoted in the scholia on *Odyssey* 11.134); another fragment is the name Daira, which the scholiast who quotes it takes for Persephone (fr. 277 Nauck). The prophecy is of the death that Teiresias foretells in the next book, when Odysseus consults him as Circe bids in this one (10.490–495, 10.538–540; 11.134–137): [[teiresias]], [[hermes]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]].
- **Drink offerings.** χοή, the drink offering “to all the dead” (10.518), is the proper word for libations to the dead, Merry notes, and he cites the libation bearers whom Orestes sees carrying “drink offerings, soothing gifts for those below” (*Libation Bearers* 14–15, a plain rendering), and Electra's question what she is to say as she pours them (*Libation Bearers* 87; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.518): [[the-offering-to-the-dead]].
- **The helmsman.** Odysseus fell asleep because “I was always handling the sheet of the ship, and would not give it / to any other of my companions” (10.32–33). For πόδα … ἐνώμων the scholia offer two meanings, the rope that hauls round the yard and the steering oar itself, and Merry sets beside the second Eteocles, who guards the city's affairs “at the stern, handling the tiller, not lulling his eyelids in sleep” (οἴακα νωμῶν, *Seven against Thebes* 2–3, a plain rendering), though he doubts that πούς ever means the steering oar (Merry on 10.32): [[odysseus-and-aeolus]] and [[sweet-sleep]].
