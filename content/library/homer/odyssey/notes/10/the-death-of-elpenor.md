---
summary: Ten verses at the end of the book tell the first part of the story: Elpenor, the youngest of the companions, sleeping off his wine on the roof of Circe's house, wakes at the noise of the departure, forgets the ladder and falls to his death (10.551–560). The book leaves him there; his soul and his burial belong to the eleventh and twelfth books.
# Unnamed: the warning of a loss (10.551); the fall and the death (10.556-560).
refs: [10.551, 10.556-560]
passages:

**Not even from there.** The story is introduced as a loss: οὐδὲ μὲν οὐδʼ ἔνθεν περ ἀπήμονας ἦγον ἑταίρους, “But not even from there did I lead my companions away unharmed” (10.551), where οὐδὲ μὲν οὐδέ stands for ἀλλʼ οὐ μὴν οὐδέ, “but not even thence could I carry off my comrades unharmed” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.551). The verse comes between the companions' consent to the journey (10.550) and the speech in which Odysseus tells them where it leads (10.561–565), so that the death falls in the bustle of the departure (see [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[hetairos]]).

**The youngest.** Elpenor is named only here in the book, and described by what he lacked: “the youngest, not very / valiant in war nor sound in his wits” (10.552–553; see [[elpenor]]). φρεσὶν ᾗσιν ἀρηρώς, “sound in his wits”, is “steady in mind”, like the later φρενήρης (Merry on 10.553; see [[phrenes]] and the translation's key-word row for φρεσὶν ᾗσιν ἀρηρώς). He lay down “apart from my companions”, “longing for the cool air”, and “sodden with wine” (10.554–555), the word the tale used of the drunken Cyclops (9.374; see [[wine-sodden]] and [[wine]]).

**The roof and the ladder.** The important clause is ἰὼν ἐς κλίμακα μακρήν: he forgot that the long ladder was the only safe way down from the flat roof where he had been lying to cool himself, and ἄψορρον means only “back again”, after he had climbed up (10.557–558; Merry on 10.558). Eustathius, as Merry reports, explains how he came to be sleeping in the open: the houses were not tiled, but had earth beaten down on the beams, as was still done in many places, so that the roof could be slept on (on 10.558; see [[house]]). Instead he “fell straight down from the roof” (10.559): καταντικρύ, “straight down”, is found again only where Elpenor's soul tells the fall (11.64; see the translation's note on 10.552–560).

**The death.** ἐκ δέ οἱ αὐχὴν / ἀστραγάλων ἐάγη, his neck “was broken from the vertebrae”, wrenched out of them (10.559–560; Merry on 10.560): without ἀστραγάλων the phrase is used of a lion breaking the neck of a cow (*Iliad* 11.175). “and his soul went down to Hades”, ψυχὴ δʼ Ἄϊδόσδε κατῆλθεν (10.560; see [[psyche]] and [[hades]]): it goes down before the living who are setting out for the same place, and it will be the first to meet them there (11.51). The two verses of the fall and the death come back in the soul's own account, changed only to the first person (10.559–560; 11.64–65).

**Unburied.** The book does not stop for a burial. Odysseus goes on at once to tell the others of the journey (10.561–565), and when the soul comes to him in the eleventh book he gives the reason: “for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (11.53–54). Merry found it hard to understand how Odysseus, who knew the manner of the death, could still ask the soul “how did you come beneath the misty darkness?” (on 11.51; 11.57). The burial is made when the ship comes back to Aeaea, with the oar the soul asked for on the mound (12.8–15, Greek only).
