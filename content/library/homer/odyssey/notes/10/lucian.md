---
summary: Three of Merry's notes on this book bring in Lucian: his Menippus, at an oracle of the dead in Babylon, digs a pit, slaughters sheep and pours the blood round it as Circe bids Odysseus do; Hermes, in the likeness of a youth with the first down on his lip, is the god whom the sculptors, Lucian says, model with his first beard; and a sentence on men who flayed beasts for clothing explains the order of Aeolus' gift.
---

- **The pit.** The pit, instead of an altar, was common in the worship of the gods below, Merry notes, and he quotes Lucian's Menippus on an oracle of the dead at Babylon: “we dug a pit, slaughtered the sheep, and poured the blood around the pit” (*Menippus* or *Necyomantia* 9, as Merry quotes it, a plain rendering; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.517; 10.516–527): [[the-offering-to-the-dead]].
- **Hermes with his first beard.** Hermes meets Odysseus “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip” (10.278–279). Merry takes the down to grow first on the upper lip, and quotes Lucian on the sculptors, who model, from where they saw it Lucian does not know, Zeus bearded, Apollo forever a boy, and Hermes with his first beard; since Odysseus knows him at once, Merry thinks that painting or sculpture had already fixed a likeness for the principal gods (*On Sacrifices* 11, as Merry quotes it; Merry on 10.279): [[hermes]].
- **The flayed ox.** Aeolus “gave me a skin of an ox nine years old that he had flayed” (10.19): the participle tells how Aeolus came by the skin, not when he gave it, and Merry reports Nitzsch's comparison with Lucian's sentence on men who, needing shelter, flayed beasts and clothed themselves in their skins (*Amores* 34, as Merry quotes it; Merry on 10.19): [[skin-of-the-winds]].
