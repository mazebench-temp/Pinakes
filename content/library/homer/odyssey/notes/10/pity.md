---
summary: “Then one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was”, and sent the stag into Odysseus' path (10.157–158). When the restored companions fill Circe's house with weeping, “the goddess herself was moved to pity” (10.399). ὀλοφύρομαι is also the lament of those who beg: Eurylochus and Circe at Odysseus' knees (10.265, 10.324) and the companions at the ship (10.409, 10.418).
---

**A god's pity.** On the way back from the lookout, at the moment of need, “one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was, / and sent a great high-horned stag into my very path” (τίς με θεῶν ὀλοφύρατο μοῦνον ἐόντα, 10.157–158; see [[deer]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The god is not named.

**Circe's pity.** When the companions, restored to themselves, know Odysseus and weep until the house rings, “the goddess herself was moved to pity” (θεὰ δʼ ἐλέαιρε καὶ αὐτή, 10.398–399; see [[circe]] and [[lament]]). The witch who penned them in the sties now pities them, and when the two bands meet in her house she stops their lament with sympathy: “I myself know too / both all the sorrows you suffered on the fish-filled sea, / and all the harm that merciless men did you on land” (10.457–459; see [[algos]]).

**Lamenting.** The same verb, ὀλοφύρομαι, is also the lament of those who beg. Eurylochus “lamenting addressed me with winged words” (10.265), and Circe, at Odysseus' knees, “lamenting addressed me” in the same verse (10.324; see [[supplication]]); at the ship the companions are “lamenting piteously, shedding big tears” (οἴκτρʼ ὀλοφυρομένους, 10.409), and “lamenting they addressed me with winged words” (10.418; see [[hetairos]] and [[winged-words]]).
