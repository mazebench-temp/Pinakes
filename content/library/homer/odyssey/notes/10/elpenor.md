**The last of the losses.** “But not even from there did I lead my companions away unharmed” (10.551): οὐδὲ μὲν οὐδέ is “but not even thence” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.551), as companions had been lost at Ismarus, in the Cyclops' cave and among the Laestrygonians (see [[hetairos]]). Elpenor is introduced by what he lacked: φρεσὶν … ἀρηρώς is “steady in mind”, like the later φρενήρης (Merry on 10.553; see [[phrenes]] and [[alke]]).

**The roof and the ladder.** He has lain down “apart from my companions, in the sacred house of Circe, / longing for the cool air”, οἰνοβαρείων, “sodden with wine” (10.554–555; see [[wine-sodden]] and [[wine]]), and when the noise of the others moving about wakes him, he forgets “to come back down by going to the long ladder, / but fell straight down from the roof” (10.556–559). The important words, Merry explains, are ἰὼν ἐς κλίμακα μακρήν: he forgot that the ladder was the only safe way down from the flat roof where he had been lying to cool himself; Eustathius describes him lying in the open air above, since the houses were not tiled but had earth laid on their beams, so that the roof could be used for sleeping; and ἄψορρον means only “back again”, after going up (Merry on 10.558; see [[house]] and the translation's key words). ἐκ … ἀστραγάλων ἐάγη is “wrenched out of its vertebrae”, as the lion of the simile breaks the cow's neck (*Iliad* 11.175; Merry on 10.559). “and his soul went down to Hades” (10.560; see [[psyche]] and [[hades]]).

**Left unburied.** Odysseus goes on with his speech to the companions as they set out, and the poem leaves the body where it lay: in Book 11 his soul comes first to the pit, “for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us”, and tells his fall in the verses of this book (11.53–54, 11.62–65, a plain rendering; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]); the companions bury him when they come back to Aeaea (12.8–15).
