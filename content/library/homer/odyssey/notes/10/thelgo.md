---
summary: Circe's wolves and lions are the beasts “whom she herself had bewitched” with evil drugs (10.213). Hermes promises Odysseus that she will not be able to bewitch him (10.291), and when her drink has failed she wonders how he was not bewitched, for “there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched” (10.318, 10.326–329).
---

**The beasts.** Around Circe's house stand mountain wolves and lions, “whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs” (κατέθελξεν, 10.212–213; see [[circe]], [[wolves]] and [[lion]]). The scholia, as Merry reports them, take the word two ways: that she had made wild beasts tame and gentle, or that she had changed men into lions. Scaliger held the first, contrasting Homer's tamed beasts with Virgil's men changed into beasts (on *Aeneid* 7.19), and the beasts' fawning, instead of rushing on the men, supports it; but Merry prefers the second, because θέλγειν is the word for the sorcery that ends in the sty (10.291, 10.320), because Eurylochus fears that Circe will make them all “either swine or wolves or lions” (10.433), and because her “evil drugs” stand against Hermes' “good drug” (10.292) (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.213; see the translation's note on 10.212–213 and [[shape-changing]]).

**The spell that fails.** Hermes tells Odysseus what Circe will do, “but even so she will not be able to bewitch you; for it will not let her, / the good drug that I will give you” (10.291–292; see [[hermes]] and [[moly]]). It happens as he said: “it did not bewitch me” (οὐδέ μʼ ἔθελξε, 10.318), and Circe, struck with wonder, says what no man had done before: “Wonder holds me, how you drank these drugs and were not bewitched at all; / for no other man either has withstood these drugs” (10.326–327; see [[thambos]]). Her explanation is the mind: “But there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched” (ἀκήλητος νόος, 10.329; see [[noos]]). The scholia objected that the companions too kept their minds, “steadfast, as before” (10.240), so that the verse says nothing of Odysseus alone; Merry answers that she means the strong and stubborn mind that was generally proof against sorcery, since she does not know that he carries an amulet (on 10.329 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.329).

**Forgetting.** What the drugs were meant to do was to make the men “utterly forget their native land” (10.236), as Calypso bewitches Odysseus “so that he may forget Ithaca” (1.57; see [[nostos]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). Merry asks whether the sorcery had been perfectly successful: it turned them into swine, but their minds were unaltered; still, they knew Odysseus only when Circe had anointed each of them with another drug (on 10.240; 10.391–397).
