---
summary: Mountain wolves and lions, bewitched by Circe's evil drugs, stand around her house and fawn on Odysseus' men as dogs fawn on their master (10.212–219), and Eurylochus fears that she will make them all “either swine or wolves or lions” to guard her house (10.432–434). They are the only wolves in the Odyssey.
# λύκους (10.433), found only here in Homer; λύκοι (10.212, 10.218) is the entry's.
grc: [λύκους]
---

**Circe's beasts.** When Eurylochus' band comes to Circe's house in the glens, “around her were mountain wolves and lions, / whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs. / Nor did these rush upon the men, but they / stood up on their hind legs, fawning on them with their long tails” (10.212–215; see [[circe]], [[lion]] and [[the-dogs-fawning-on-their-master]]). ὀρέστερος, “of the mountains”, is said once more in Homer, of the serpent that waits for a man at its hole (*Iliad* 22.93; see [[the-serpent-at-its-hole]] and the translation's row λύκοι ὀρέστεροι; σαίνω, περισσαίνω); the Iliad's wolves too hunt in the mountains (*Iliad* 16.156–159). μιν, “her”, is Circe rather than her house: the beasts are in attendance upon her (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.212; see the translation's note on 10.212–213). In the simile that follows they are κρατερώνυχες, “strong-clawed” (10.218; see [[strong-hoofed]]).

**Tamed or transformed?** κατέθελξεν, “had bewitched” (10.213; see [[thelgo]] and [[drugs]]), was read in two ways, Merry reports: the scholia say either that Circe made the beasts gentle and tame, or that she changed men into lions. Scaliger took the first, and set Homer's wild beasts tamed with drugs against Virgil's men changed into the shape of beasts (on *Aeneid* 7.19, as Merry quotes him; see [[virgil]]). Merry prefers the second, because θέλγω is the word of the sorcery that ends in the sty (10.291, 10.318–320), because Eurylochus looks on the wolves and lions as Circe's creatures no less than the swine (10.432–433), and because her drugs are “evil”, as against Hermes' “good drug” (10.292; Merry on 10.213; see [[moly]]). The poem itself does not say: it tells only that the beasts did not rush upon the men, and that the men were afraid “when they saw the dread monsters” (10.214, 10.219).

**Guards of the house.** When Odysseus would lead the rest of the crew up to Circe's house, Eurylochus tries to hold them back: she “will make all of us / either swine or wolves or lions, / to guard her great house for her even under compulsion” (10.432–434; see [[eurylochus]] and [[shape-changing]]). Merry renders “So that we should have perforce to keep Circe's great house for her”, the verb drawn into the first person because Eurylochus means “all of us” (on 10.434). In the Iliad wolves are fighters that hunt in packs and the likeness of armies (see the entry); the only wolves of the Odyssey are Circe's, and they fawn.
