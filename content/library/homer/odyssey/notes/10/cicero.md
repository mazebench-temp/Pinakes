---
summary: Writing from Formiae, Cicero calls the town in Homer's Greek “this Telepylus of the Laestrygonians”, and against Verres he turns Circe's cup into a pun on his opponent's name. His Teiresias, alone wise among the shadows, and his Circe, child of the Sun and of Perseis and worshipped at Circeii, are this book's.
---

- **Telepylus of the Laestrygonians.** Writing to Atticus from his villa at Formiae, Cicero says that if men keep silent at Rome, in the country they do not, and the very fields can no longer bear “your tyranny”; “but if you come to this Τηλέπυλον … Λαιστρυγονίην, Formiae I mean, what a roar of men! what angry spirits!”, and how hated is “our friend Magnus” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.13.2, a plain rendering). The Greek words are Homer's name for the Laestrygonians' city, “Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (10.82): [[telepylus]] and [[laestrygonians]].
- **Circe's cup.** Pleading against Caecilius for the right to prosecute Verres, Cicero tells how Verres, coming to Lilybaeum, condemned his quaestor's seizure of a freedwoman's property and made him pay the money back to her: at that moment he was “not Verres but Quintus Mucius”; “but suddenly, on the spot, from a man he was made Verres, as if by some Circaean cup”, came back to himself and his ways, and kept most of the money (*ex homine tamquam aliquo Circaeo poculo factus est Verres*, *Against Caecilius* 57, a plain rendering). *Verres* is also the Latin for a boar, and the cup is Circe's drink, into which she mixed her drugs before she struck the companions with her wand and penned them in her sties as swine (10.234–240): [[circe]], [[kykeon]] and [[sties]].
- **The children of the Sun.** In *On the Nature of the Gods* the Academic Cotta asks whether, if Ino is a goddess, “Circe and Pasiphae and Aeetes, born of Perseis, daughter of Oceanus, by the Sun their father” are not to be counted among the gods, “although our colonists at Circeii also worship Circe devoutly”; and if Circe is a goddess, what of Medea, granddaughter of the Sun and of Oceanus? Later he counts five Suns, the fifth “who is said to have begotten Aeetes and Circe at Colchis” (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.48, 3.54, plain renderings). The parents are this book's, the Sun and Perse, “whom Oceanus begot as his child” (10.138–139), and Merry cites the first passage for Circe's later home at Circeii (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.135, who gives the chapter): [[circe]], [[aeetes]], [[perse]] and [[helios]].
- **Teiresias.** In *On Divination* Cicero's brother Quintus counts Amphiaraus and Tiresias among the augurs of old, famous men who foretold the future from birds and signs, “of one of whom Homer says that even among the dead he alone is wise, the rest wander like shadows” (*solum sapere, ceteros umbrarum vagari modo*, *On Divination* 1.88, a plain rendering; 10.494–495), the Latin version that Merry quotes (Merry on 10.495). In the second book Cicero answers that a seer who was blind, as Tiresias was, could not say what is white and what black (*On Divination* 2.9), and in the *Tusculan Disputations* the poets never bring on the wise augur Tiresias lamenting his blindness (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.115): [[teiresias]], [[the-blind-seer]] and [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]].
- **The rivers.** If Jupiter and Neptune are gods, Cotta argues, then so is their brother Orcus, “and those that are said to flow among the dead, Acheron, Cocytus, Pyriphlegethon”, and Charon and Cerberus as well (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.43, a plain rendering), the three rivers of Circe's directions (10.513–514). In the *Tusculan Disputations* the roaring of Cocytus and the crossing of Acheron are among the terrors of the underworld that no one believes any longer (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.10): [[acheron]], [[cocytus]] and [[pyriphlegethon]].
