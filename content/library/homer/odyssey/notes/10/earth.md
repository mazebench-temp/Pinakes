---
summary: In Book 10 the earth is above all the native land, which comes into sight and is lost (10.29–49), which Circe's drugs make men forget (10.236), and which the companions name on Aeaea and remind Odysseus of after a year (10.415–420, 10.462–463, 10.472–474), until he tells them that another journey comes first (10.562–563). It is also the ground from which Hermes pulls the moly (10.303) and the earth beneath which the sun goes (10.191).
---

**The native land.** The book's refrain is πατρὶς γαῖα, πατρὶς αἶα, “the native land”. On the tenth day out from Aeolia “our native fields were already coming into sight” (10.29), and Odysseus held the sheet himself “so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (10.33), until the storm carried the men “away from their native land” (10.49; see [[ithaca]] and [[dear-native-land]]). Circe mixes her drugs into the food “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.236; see [[circe]]), as the lotus made men forget their homecoming (9.94–97; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). On Aeaea the companions greet Odysseus as gladly “as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land” (10.420), Circe remembers the day they “first left your native land / of rugged Ithaca” (10.462–463), and after a year the companions say, “Strange one, think now at last of your native land” (10.472–474; see [[nostos]]). The last time in the book, the words are Odysseus' own and they are turned against the men's hope: “You think, no doubt, that you are going home to your dear native land; / but Circe has ordained another journey for us” (10.562–563).

**The ground and the earth.** The earth is also the ground under the feet of the tale. From his lookout in the land of the Laestrygonians Odysseus sees no works of oxen or of men: “only smoke we saw rising from the land” (10.98–99), and he sends scouts to find out “what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (10.101), the formula of mortal men (see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]); on Aeaea the smoke rises “from the wide-wayed earth” (10.149; see [[wide-wayed-earth]]), and he lays the great stag down “on the ground” (10.165). Hermes gives him the moly “pulling it from the ground” (10.303; see [[moly]]), the plant that is hard for mortal men to dig up. And in his despair on Aeaea Odysseus tells his men that they do not know where the sun “goes beneath the earth” (10.191; see [[helios]]).
