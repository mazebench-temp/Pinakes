summary: The cattle of Book 10 are the ox nine years old in whose skin Aeolus binds the winds (10.19); the herds of the Laestrygonians, driven out to pasture as the flocks come home, in a land that shows no works of oxen (10.82–86, 10.98); the cows of the herd to which the companions' welcome is likened (10.410–414); and the barren heifer that Odysseus is to vow to the dead (10.522).

**The ox of the winds.** Aeolus' gift is “a skin of an ox nine years old that he had flayed” (10.19; see [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[aeolus]]). ἐννέωρος, “nine years old”, is commonly taken, W. W. Merry says, to mean no more than full-grown, nine being a conventional number for maturity (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.19). Aristotle, noting that the ox is at its prime at five years old, reports that some held Homer right to write both “a male, five years old” and “of an ox nine years old”, since the two mean the same (*History of Animals* 6.21); the first is the ox that Agamemnon sacrifices to Zeus (*Iliad* 7.314–315) and the ox that the sons of Autolycus bring in for their feast (19.420, a plain rendering). Merry finds decisive the verse on Otus and Ephialtes, who “at nine years old”, ἐννέωροι, were nine cubits in breadth and nine fathoms in height (11.311–312; see [[otus]] and [[ephialtes]]), and takes the word as “nine years old”, and so “of full maturity” (on 10.19). It is said again of the fat hogs that Circe drives out of the sty (10.390; see [[boar]]).

**The herds of Telepylus.** “There a man who took no sleep could have earned two wages, / one by herding cattle, the other by pasturing silver-white sheep” (10.84–85; see [[telepylus]] and [[sheep]]). As Merry reads the verses, the neatherd drives his herd out to pasture as night falls, while the shepherd brings his flock home (10.82–83). The scholia, he reports, explained it by the gadflies, which made it dangerous for cattle to feed except after sundown, while the sheep, protected by their fleeces, could pasture by day; Crates by the short nights of the far north; Merry himself by an evening twilight that melts into the dawn (in his note on 10.81; see the translation's note on 10.81–86). From his lookout Odysseus sees “no works of oxen or of men” (10.98): no plowed land, Merry explains, and no vineyards or gardens, for the Laestrygonians, like the Cyclopes, were only graziers (on 10.97).

**The cows of the herd.** In the simile of the companions' welcome the calves of the fields run lowing around their mothers, “the cows of the herd”, as they come back to the dung-yard from pasture (10.410–414; see [[the-calves-and-the-cows]]). πόρις, more commonly πόρτις, is the calf or young heifer (Merry on 10.410; *Iliad* 5.162), and the cows have been out to pasture in a drove while the calves stayed penned at the farm (Merry on 10.412).

**The barren heifer.** Circe tells Odysseus to implore the dead, “vowing that, when you come to Ithaca, a barren heifer, the best you have, / you will sacrifice in your halls, and heap the pyre with good things” (10.522–523; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and [[ithaca]]). στεῖρα, “barren”: the scholion explains that barren and sterile things belong to the dead (Merry on 10.522; see the translation's row στεῖρα βοῦς; ἔθνεα νεκρῶν), as the willows of Persephone's groves lose their fruit (10.510; see the Book 10 note on [[willow]]). The vow is made in those words in Book 11 (11.30), and Virgil's Aeneas, before he goes down to the dead, sacrifices a barren cow to Proserpina (*sterilemque tibi, Proserpina, vaccam*, *Aeneid* 6.251; see [[virgil]]). In Ithaca a barren heifer is the cowherd's gift to the suitors' feast (20.185–186, a plain rendering).
