summary: Circe's drugs tame the wolves and lions round her house, and mixed into the food of Odysseus' companions they make them forget their native land and, with a stroke of her wand, turn them into swine (10.212–243). Hermes gives Odysseus a good drug against them, so that her drug fails to bewitch him (10.286–306, 10.316–329); with another drug she gives the companions back their shape (10.391–396).

**Circe's drugs.** The mountain wolves and lions round Circe's house are those “whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs” (10.212–213; see [[circe]], [[thelgo]] and [[shape-changing]]). The scholia took κατέθελξεν two ways, as beasts made gentle or as men turned into beasts; Scaliger read Homer's as wild beasts tamed by drugs and Virgil's as men in the shape of beasts, but θέλγειν describes the sorcery that sends men to the sty, Eurylochus takes the wolves and lions for creatures of her witchcraft as much as the swine, and the “evil drugs” set against Hermes' “good drug” incline Merry to the second sense (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.213; 10.291–292, 10.320, 10.433; see the translation's note on 10.212–213). For the companions she mixes cheese, barley meal and honey with Pramnian wine, “and she mixed into the food / grim drugs, so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.235–236; see [[kykeon]] and [[nostos]]), as the lotus made those who ate it forget their homecoming (9.94–97; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). When they have drunk it off she strikes them with her wand and pens them in the sties: they have the heads, the voice, the bristles and the form of swine, “but their minds were steadfast, as before” (10.237–240; see [[wand]] and [[sties]]). Merry asks whether the sorcery wholly succeeded, since the drugs were meant to make them forget, yet their minds were unchanged; still, they do not know Odysseus until Circe has anointed each of them with another drug (on 10.240).

**The good drug.** Hermes meets Odysseus on the way to the house (see [[hermes]]):

{{quote:4ee995ce-0931-526a-80ee-a9db91993d23}}

κρατός, “from your head”, goes with ἀλάλκῃσιν as κεφαλῆς with ἀπαλάλκοι in Achilles' threat to Hector (*Iliad* 22.348; Merry on 10.288). The god pulls the drug from the ground and shows Odysseus its nature, black at the root with a flower like milk, the plant the gods call moly (10.302–306; see [[moly]]).

**The drug that fails.** Circe makes him the mixed drink in a golden cup “and put the drug in it, meaning evil in her heart” (10.316–317; see [[cup]]). When it does not bewitch him she cries, “Wonder holds me, how you drank these drugs and were not bewitched at all; / for no other man either has withstood these drugs, / whoever drinks them, once they have passed the barrier of his teeth” (10.326–328). The verse that follows, “But there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched” (10.329), was objected to in the scholia, since the swine's minds were steadfast too; Merry answers that Circe means the strong mind that is proof against sorcery, not knowing that Odysseus carries an amulet (on 10.329; see the translation's note on 10.329).

**The other drug.** To free the companions Circe drives them out of the sty, goes among them “and anointed each of them with another drug. / And from their limbs fell the bristles that had been made to grow before / by the ruinous drug that lady Circe had given them” (10.392–394); they become men again, “younger than they had been before, / and much handsomer and taller to look upon” (10.395–396; see [[hetairos]]).
