summary: Ithaca is in sight on the tenth day after Aeolia, near enough to see men tending fires, when the companions open the skin of the winds (10.28–49). On Aeaea it is the measure of the companions' joy, “as if they had come to their native land and to the very city / of rugged Ithaca” (10.415–420); Circe reminds them of the day they left it (10.462–463), and Odysseus is to vow a heifer to the dead when he comes home to it (10.521–523).
# Ithaca without its name: "our native fields were already coming into sight" (10.29), the native land he would
# reach the sooner (10.33), and from which the storm carries them away (10.49).
refs: [10.29-30, 10.33, 10.49]
---

**So near.** With the West Wind behind them they sail nine days and nights, “and on the tenth our native fields were already coming into sight, / and already we could see men tending fires, we were so near” (10.29–30). The fires, W. W. Merry notes, are commonly taken for the watchfires of shepherds; he would rather suppose that the ship had been sighted and a fire lit to guide her in, or that it was a fire for some purpose on a farm, put into the picture to show how near home they had come (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.30). He compares the Iliad's simile of the fire that sailors see burning in a lonely farmstead in the mountains while the storm carries them away from their friends (*Iliad* 19.375–378; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]), and that is what happens here: Odysseus, who had handled the sheet himself “so that we might reach our native land the sooner”, falls asleep, the companions open the skin, and the storm carries them, weeping, “away from their native land” (10.31–49; see [[aeolia]] and [[dear-native-land]]). The island is not named; it is πατρὶς ἄρουρα, “our native fields”, and πατρὶς γαῖα, “our native land”.

**As if they had come home.** On Aeaea, when Odysseus comes back to the ship from Circe's house, the companions crowd round him like calves round the cows coming home from pasture, “and it seemed to their hearts / as if they had come to their native land and to the very city / of rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised” (10.410–417; see [[the-calves-and-the-cows]] and [[rugged]]), and they say it too: “we are as glad / as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land” (10.419–420). πόλιν αὐτήν, “the very city”, is Aristarchus' reading; the manuscripts have αὐτῶν, “their own” (Merry's critical note on 10.416; see the translation's note on 10.416). τρηχεῖα, “rugged”, is the word Odysseus used of the island when he first named it to the Phaeacians, “rugged, but a good nurse of young men” (9.27).

**Rugged Ithaca again.** Circe, bidding the men eat and drink until they have the heart again that they had “when you first left your native land / of rugged Ithaca” (10.461–463), names the island in the same words. When the companions, a year later, bid Odysseus think at last of his native land (10.472–474), she sends him first to the dead, and among the vows he is to make there is a barren heifer, the best he has, to be sacrificed “when you come to Ithaca” in his halls (10.521–523; see [[cattle]]).
