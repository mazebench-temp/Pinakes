---
summary: Hesiod gives Circe and Aeetes the parents of this book, the Sun and the Oceanid Perseis, and in the last lines of the Theogony makes Circe the mother of sons by Odysseus who ruled the Tyrrhenians; a fragment of the Catalogue sends her west on the Sun's chariot. Merry finds in the Theogony's house of Night, where Night and Day greet each other at the threshold, a likeness to the Laestrygonians' short nights, and in its verses on the year one of this book's.
---

- **The children of the Sun.** “To the tireless Sun the famous daughter of Oceanus, / Perseis, bore Circe and Aeetes the king”, and Aeetes married Idyia, a daughter of Oceanus, who bore him Medea (*Theogony* 956–962, plain renderings). The Odyssey's Circe and Aeetes are “born of the Sun, who brings light to mortals, / and of their mother Perse, whom Oceanus begot as his child” (10.138–139); Hesiod, Merry notes, gives the same account, with Perseis for Perse, and adds the mother of Medea (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.135): [[circe]], [[aeetes]], [[perse]], [[helios]] and [[oceanus]].
- **Circe's sons.** “And Circe, daughter of the Sun, Hyperion's son, / bore in love to enduring Odysseus / Agrius and Latinus, blameless and strong”; a verse printed in brackets adds Telegonus; “and they, far away in a recess of the holy islands, / ruled over all the famous Tyrrhenians” (*Theogony* 1011–1016, plain renderings). Merry thinks the passage spurious, but useful as pointing to an early transfer of Circe to Italy and the cities of Magna Graecia (Merry on 10.135). A scholiast reports that Apollonius, following Hesiod, brought Circe on the chariot of the Sun to the island off Tyrrhenia, and called it Hesperian because it lies toward the sunset (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 46 Evelyn-White): [[circe]], [[odysseus]] and [[apollonius]].
- **Night and Day.** Merry explains the Laestrygonian herdsmen, “for the paths of night and of day are close together” (10.86), by the short nights of a land at the western turning point of the sun's course, and finds support in Hesiod's house of Night in the west, where Atlas holds up the sky, “where Night and Day draw near / and greet one another as they cross the great threshold / of bronze”, one going in as the other goes out, though, he adds, the rest of Hesiod's description does not tally (*Theogony* 744–757, a plain rendering; Merry on 10.82–86): [[telepylus]] and [[laestrygonians]].
- **The year.** “But when a year had passed, and the seasons returned in their course / as the months waned, and many days were accomplished”, the Muses were born (*Theogony* 58–59, a plain rendering). The same two verses end Odysseus' year with Circe, with μακρά, “long”, for πόλλʼ, “many” (10.469–470); Merry thinks the second probably borrowed from Hesiod, and it has been rejected since Wolf (Merry on 10.470 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.469–470): [[odysseus-and-circe]].
