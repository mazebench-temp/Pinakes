summary: The book opens on Aeolia, the floating island walled with unbreakable bronze, where Aeolus and his twelve children feast and Odysseus is made welcome for a month (10.1–16); the storm brings the ships back to “the Aeolian island”, and Aeolus drives him from it (10.54–76).
# The island without its name: "on a floating island" (10.3), its city and house (10.13), the land where the men go up
# and draw water (10.56), "Off with you from the island" (10.72).
refs: [10.3, 10.13, 10.56, 10.72]
---

**The floating island.** The tale goes on from the Cyclops with the next landfall and its lord:

{{quote:cd60f052-705e-5a98-b432-aeb61da69137}}

πλωτῇ ἐνὶ νήσῳ: Aristarchus explained πλωτή as “borne about”, since it was fitting that the island of the winds should have no roots, and W. W. Merry finds this sense of “floating” “by far the simplest and the most picturesque”, comparing Pindar's words on Delos, once borne about by the waves and by the blasts of every wind, and Herodotus' report that the Egyptians call Chemmis, an island in the lake by Buto, a floating island (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.3; see the translation's note on 10.1–3 and [[aristarchus]]). Herodotus adds that he never saw it float or move, and was astonished to hear that an island could really float (*Histories* 2.156.2; see [[herodotus]]). Crates took πλωτή as “accessible to ships”, and Nitzsch inclines that way; Merry answers that so sheer an island would be harborless and dangerous, and wonders whether the floating island is not a poet's version of a tale told by Phoenician sailors who had come upon an iceberg, its sheer face and glittering summit given by the bronze wall and the sheer rock (on 10.3). The wall runs πᾶσαν … πέρι, “all around” the island, and the rock ἀναδέδρομε, “runs up sheer”, from the sea (10.3–4).

**A lonely household.** On the island live Aeolus, his wife and their twelve children, six daughters and six sons, the sons married to the daughters, who feast with their parents by day and sleep beside their wives at night (10.5–12; see [[aeolus]]). Merry finds in the marriages no custom of any age or country but a picture of the island's loneliness, “the scanty intercourse its inhabitants enjoyed with the rest of mankind”: the brothers married the sisters because there were no other women to marry (on 10.5). The scholia, he reports, made Aeolus' wife Telepatra, a daughter of Laestrygon, and the allegorizers made Aeolus the year, his six sons the colder months and his six daughters the warmer (on 10.5; see [[laestrygonians]]). The house is κνισῆεν, “full of the savor of meat”, and “resounds round about in the court” (10.10): the sound of the feast, Merry explains, was heard even in the outer court, so that the house of the master of the winds is “full of strange moans and sounds” (on 10.10; see the translation's note on 10.10 and [[house]]). “And so we came to their city and their fine house” (10.13): for δώματα καλά the scholia knew a variant, καὶ τείχεα μακρά, “and long walls” (Merry's critical note on 10.13).

**Back to the Aeolian island.** The month's welcome ends with the skin of the winds and nine days' sailing, and on the tenth “our native fields were already coming into sight” (10.14–30; see [[ithaca]]). While Odysseus sleeps the companions open the skin, and “the ships were borne by the evil storm of wind / back to the Aeolian island, and my companions groaned” (10.54–55). They go up on the land, draw water and take their meal beside the swift ships (10.56–58), the verses of the landing among the Lotus-eaters (9.85–87; see [[lotus-eaters]]); ἠπείρου here means no more than the coast of the island, and αἱ δέ shows that the other ships came back with Odysseus' own (Merry on 10.56). He goes with a herald and one companion to “the glorious house of Aeolus”, and they sit down on the threshold beside the doorposts (10.59–63; see [[heralds]]), a more modest place, Merry notes, than the hearth by which he sat in Alcinous' hall, and the place where he sits as a beggar in his own (on 10.62, comparing 7.153 and 17.339). The island that made him welcome for a month (10.14) now casts him out: “Off with you from the island, quickly, most despised of the living; / for it is not lawful for me to care for or to send on his way / a man who is hated by the blessed gods” (10.72–74; see [[themis]] and [[xenia]]). It is the first of the two islands that Odysseus reaches twice in his tale; the other is Aeaea, to which he comes back from the house of Hades (12.1–7; see [[aeaea]]).
