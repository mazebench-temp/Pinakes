summary: For Odysseus' escort Aeolus binds the paths of the blustering winds in the skin of an ox, ties it in the ship with a cord of silver and sends the West Wind alone to carry them home (10.17–27). On the tenth day, with Ithaca in sight and Odysseus asleep, the companions loose it to see how much gold and silver it holds, and the winds carry the ships back to the Aeolian island (10.28–55).

**The gift.** When Odysseus asks leave to go, Aeolus “did not refuse at all, but made ready my escort” (10.18; see [[aeolus]] and [[pompe]]):

{{quote:893fb025-6f2b-54ce-80cb-983480bd09a9}}

Aeolus binds the winds as their dispenser by the gift of Zeus (10.21–22; see [[dispenser-of-the-winds]]): he is not the King of the Winds that Virgil makes him, W. W. Merry notes, but only their manager by leave of Zeus, and Athena, Calypso and Circe can send a favoring wind as well (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.21). Only the West Wind is left free, “to carry the ships and ourselves” (10.25–26; see [[zephyrus]]), and from this Merry reasons that the island lay to the west of Ithaca, since Aeolus meant to send Odysseus straight home (on 10.1; see [[aeolia]]). The poet tells the end at once: “but he was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (10.26–27).

**The tenth day.** “For nine days we sailed on, night and day alike”, not beaching the ships each night as was the custom (10.28; Merry on 10.28), and on the tenth the men of Ithaca could be seen tending their fires (10.29–30; see [[ithaca]] and [[fire]]). Odysseus has held the sheet the whole way and given it to no one, and sleep comes over him (10.31–33; see [[sails]] and [[hypnos]]). The companions talk among themselves: their captain is honored wherever he goes and brings home treasures from Troy, “while we, who have gone the same road to its end, / are going home, all of us, with empty hands” (10.38–42; see [[hetairos]] and [[possessions]]). “But come, quickly, let us see what these are, / how much gold and silver is in the skin” (10.44–45; see [[gold-and-silver]]).

**The opening.** “So they spoke, and the evil counsel of my companions prevailed; / they loosed the skin, and all the winds rushed out” (10.46–47). The storm wind carries them out to sea, weeping, and back to the Aeolian island, while Odysseus, waking, wonders whether to throw himself into the sea, and endures, lying covered in the ship (10.48–55; see [[odysseus]]). To Aeolus he lays the blame on his evil companions “and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (10.68–69; see [[ate]]); turned away as a man hated by the gods, they row on “through our own folly, since no escort showed itself any longer” (10.79): πομπή here is the wafting wind itself, the means of the journey (Merry on 10.79).
