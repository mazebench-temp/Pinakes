**The steep citadel.** Driven from Aeolia, the men row on for six days and nights, and on the seventh come to the city:

{{quote:d71806a1-cc1a-58cc-b6b0-59513d122511}}

αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον, “the steep citadel”, is elsewhere said with the town's own name, “the steep citadel of Dium” in the Catalogue (*Iliad* 2.538) and “the steep citadel of Pylos” (3.485; again 15.193; see [[steep]]); so some took Λάμου for the name of the town, but W. W. Merry, with Fäsi, takes Lamus for a name of the Laestrygonian king, as Pylos is “the well-built citadel of Neleus” (3.4), and Τηλέπυλος for the city's own name, “big-gated” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.81; see [[lamus]], the entry, and the translation's note on 10.81–86).

**Herdsman hails herdsman.** ποιμένα ποιμήν: ποιμήν is commonly a shepherd, but often a herdsman of any kind, and Merry takes the one who drives in to be the shepherd with his flock and the one who drives out the neatherd with his cattle; the shepherd hails (ἠπύει) the other, who answers (ὑπακούει) as they pass in the gateway (on 10.81). The scene, as he reads it, is evening: the sheep are coming home, and at the same moment the cattle are going out to pasture, so that a man who did without sleep could earn wages twice over, “one by herding cattle, the other by pasturing silver-white sheep” (10.84–85; see [[cattle]] and [[sheep]]). The scholia, laying the scene in Sicily, explained it by the gadflies, which made it dangerous for the cattle to graze except at night, while the sheep, protected by their fleeces, could graze by day, and they took the paths of night and day to be the roads to the day and night pastures, near the city; Merry calls this “nothing more than a simple invention to explain the meaning of the text” (on 10.81). Crates gave the right line, he thinks, with the short nights of the north (see the entry).

**The paths of night and of day.** ἐγγὺς γὰρ νυκτός τε καὶ ἤματός εἰσι κέλευθοι (10.86): the outgoings of night and of day are so close together that the interval of darkness between the two periods of light is hardly to be noticed, and the evening twilight melts into the dawn (Merry on 10.81). Merry explains the marvel by the poet's picture of the sun's course as a chariot race, which starts in the east and turns at a post in the extreme west: Telepylus lies at the turning post, so that the sun's passage round it is the only darkness there (on 10.81). He compares the island of Syrie, “where are the turnings of the sun” (15.404, a plain rendering), and the description of Aeaea, “where are the house and dancing floors of early-born Dawn, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4, a plain rendering; see [[aeaea]] and [[helios]]). The land where night is short is followed in the tale by the island where Odysseus does not know “where the darkness is, nor where the dawn” (10.190), and then by the land of the Cimmerians, on whom the sun never looks (11.14–19).
