---
summary: Zeus is named once in Book 10, as the son of Cronus who made Aeolus “dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (10.21–22), and otherwise only in the titles of Odysseus, “you who are nurtured by Zeus” (10.266, 10.419) and “sprung from Zeus” (10.401, 10.443, 10.488, 10.504).
---

**The giver of Aeolus' office.** Aeolus binds up the paths of the winds in the skin of an ox, “for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (10.21–22; see [[aeolus]], [[the-winds]], [[son-of-cronus]] and [[cronus]]). ταμίης is Zeus' own title in the Iliad, where he is “the dispenser of war for men” (*Iliad* 4.84, 19.224; see [[dispenser-of-war]] and [[dispenser-of-the-winds]]). Merry insists that Homer's Aeolus is not the king of the winds of Virgil's *Aeneid*, but only their manager, by the permission of Zeus (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.21). No later act in the book is given to Zeus by name. When the ships come back, Aeolus does not name him, but reads the disaster as the sign of a man “who is hated by the blessed gods” (10.73–75; see [[themis]] and [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]); Odysseus lays it on his companions and on sleep, “My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (10.68–69; see [[ate]] and [[hypnos]]), and on “our own folly” (10.27, 10.79), as Zeus said at the first council that mortals blame the gods for evils which they suffer “through their own recklessness” (1.32–34; see [[atasthalie]]). On Aeaea “some god guided us” into the harbor, and “one of the gods pitied me” and sent the stag (10.141, 10.157; see [[theos]]).

**Nurtured by Zeus, sprung from Zeus.** Eurylochus, begging not to be led back to Circe's house, and the companions who waited at the ship, welcoming Odysseus back, call him διοτρεφές, “you who are nurtured by Zeus” (10.266, 10.419; see [[zeus-nurtured]]); Circe addresses him three times in the full verse “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (10.401, 10.488, 10.504), and the companions, holding him back from Eurylochus, call him “Sprung from Zeus” (10.443; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]).
