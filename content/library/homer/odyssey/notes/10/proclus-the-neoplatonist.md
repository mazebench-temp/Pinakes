---
summary: Proclus reads Circe as the power of the region about the earth, whose drink turns most souls into beasts, and quotes her wonder at the man her drugs could not bewitch for the soul that stays sober in the body; on the Cratylus he makes her singing at the loom the weaving of life in the world below the moon. In his essays on the Republic he explains the shadows of the dead in Circe's words on Teiresias and defends the rivers of the underworld.
---

- **Circe's drink.** The wonder, Proclus says, is not that souls clothed in bodies, bound and surrounded by so much change, go astray, but that some stay sober, pure and unmoved, living immaterially among material things; to such souls, and to the man who lives undefiled among mortal things, one might say, “Wonder holds me, how you drank these drugs and were not bewitched at all” (10.326). Forgetfulness, wandering and ignorance are like a sorcery that drags souls down to the place of unlikeness: why wonder that many in their lives have put on the shape of wolves, many of swine, many of some other beast, when the region about the earth is Circe's dwelling and the souls of the many are caught by her drink through immoderate desire? Wonder rather that some here are undrugged and unbewitched and Hermaic by nature, passing over to reason and knowledge (*Commentary on the First Alcibiades* 257, a plain rendering). The drink, the swine, the wolves and Hermes' protection are this book's (10.212–243, 10.281–306): [[circe]], [[drugs]], [[thelgo]], [[shape-changing]] and [[hermes]].
- **Circe at the loom.** The art of weaving, Proclus writes, begins with Athena, passes to the life-giving chain of Kore, and ends in the gods who preside over becoming, “among whom is Homer's Circe, who weaves all the life in the four elements and with her songs makes the region under the moon harmonious” (*On the Cratylus* 53, a plain rendering). In this book Circe sings at a great immortal loom (10.220–228): [[circe]] and [[loom]].
- **The shadows.** Souls drawn to the body carry shadowy and heavy vehicles, which is why Socrates in the *Phaedo* says that they roll about the tombs as shadowy phantoms, “and the poet has told that they flit about like shadows” (*On the Republic* I 119 Kroll, a plain rendering; 10.495): [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]] and [[psyche]].
- **The rivers.** The places in Hades, the courts under the earth and the rivers that Homer and Plato taught are not empty fancies or mythical marvels: as many regions are marked out for the souls that go to heaven, so the places under the earth receive the souls that still need punishment or cleansing, with outflows of the elements above the earth, which have been called rivers and streams, and orders of daemons set over them (*On the Republic* I 121–122 Kroll; 10.513–515): [[acheron]], [[pyriphlegethon]] and [[cocytus]].
