---
summary: Troy is the past of the tale in Book 10: Aeolus questions Odysseus for a month about Ilios, the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans (10.14–16); the companions envy the treasures he is bringing “from Troy” (10.40–42); and Circe knows him as the man whom Hermes foretold, “returning from Troy in your swift black ship” (10.330–332).
---

**Aeolus' questions.** “And for a whole month he made me welcome and questioned me about each thing, / Ilios and the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans; / and I told him all of it fair and right” (10.14–16; see [[aeolus]], [[argives]] and [[achaeans]]). The island at the edge of the world is eager for news of the war, as Alcinous was when he asked the stranger why he wept at Demodocus' song of Ilios (8.577–580; see the Book 8 note), and Odysseus tells it κατὰ μοῖραν, “fair and right” (see [[moira]]). Aeolus' three questions, the city, the fleet and the return, are the matter of the Iliad and of the returns (see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**From Troy.** The companions, sure that the skin holds gold and silver, grumble that Odysseus is honored by all men: “Many fine treasures he is bringing from Troy / out of the spoil, while we, who have gone the same road to its end, / are going home, all of us, with empty hands” (10.40–42; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[hetairos]]). Τροίης is properly an adjective with γαίης understood, “from the land of Troy”; Aristarchus read Τροΐης and took it with ληίδος, “the Trojan spoil”, but ληίδος, W. W. Merry explains, is a genitive of the material with κειμήλια, “treasures out of the spoil” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.40; see the translation's note on 10.40, and on 5.39, where Zeus promises Odysseus more than he would have carried off from Troy). The spoil of Troy was each man's share; the envy over it opens the skin.

**Returning from Troy.** Circe recognizes the man her drugs cannot bewitch: “Surely you are Odysseus of many turns; he always told me / you would come, the slayer of Argus of the golden wand, / returning from Troy in your swift black ship” (10.330–332; see [[of-many-turns]] and [[hermes]]). ἐκ Τροίης ἀνιόντα: to the gods, and to Circe, he is the man coming home from Troy.
