heading: The second book of the tale
summary: The tale goes on without a break from the ninth book. The tenth tells three adventures in the order that Odysseus keeps for Penelope, Aeolus and the winds, the Laestrygonians, and Circe (10.1–574; 23.314–321), and ends with the departure from Aeaea for the house of Hades, which the eleventh book tells. Merry heads it Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης.

**The heading.** Merry heads the book Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης, “the story of Aeolus, the Laestrygonians and Circe”, naming its three adventures (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 10; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-10]]). The book has no beginning of its own. The ninth ends inside the speech, with the departure from the island of goats, “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.565–566), and the tenth goes straight on: “And we came to the island of Aeolia” (10.1). The whole book is the one speech of Odysseus to Alcinous and the Phaeacians, and the eleventh continues it (11.1, Greek only; see [[odysseus]]).

**Three landfalls.** The adventures are told in the order of the voyage, and in the order that Odysseus keeps when he tells Penelope his wanderings, where Aeolus has four verses, the Laestrygonians three and Circe one (23.314–321, Greek only; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]], [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). The first and the last begin in the same verse with a different island: Αἰολίην δʼ ἐς νῆσον ἀφικόμεθʼ· ἔνθα δʼ ἔναιεν, “And we came to the island of Aeolia; and there lived” (10.1), and Αἰαίην δʼ ἐς νῆσον ἀφικόμεθʼ, “And we came to the island of Aeaea” (10.135; see [[aeolia]] and [[aeaea]]). The voyages are counted in days, as Homer counts a long voyage (Merry on 10.1, comparing 9.82): nine days and nights from Aeolia until Ithaca is in sight, and six from Aeolia to Telepylus, in the same verse with ἑξῆμαρ for ἐννῆμαρ (10.28, 10.80). For the crossing from the Laestrygonians to Aeaea no days are given, and Merry infers that the two were not thought to lie far apart (on 10.135).

**The losses.** The formula of departure measures the losses. After Aeolus, who cost no lives, it has its first verse only, “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart” (10.77 = 9.62); after the Laestrygonians it has both, “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (10.133–134 = 9.62–63; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]). Eleven of the twelve ships perish in the harbor of the Laestrygonians with their crews (10.132), and from there the tale follows one ship. On Aeaea half of that ship's crew is turned to swine and given back (10.237–243, 10.388–399), and the book ends with one more death, Elpenor's (10.551–560; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]], [[hetairos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**Voices within the tale.** Odysseus' speech holds the speeches of others: what the companions said to one another while he slept (10.37–45), the questions of Aeolus' household and Aeolus' answer (10.64–66, 10.72–75), Polites at the door of Circe's house (10.226–228), Eurylochus' report, plea and protest (10.251–260, 10.266–269, 10.431–437), Hermes' warning (10.281–301), Circe's speeches, from her command at the sty to her instructions for the journey (10.320, 10.325–335, 10.378–381, 10.401–405, 10.457–465, 10.488–495, 10.504–540), and the companions' (10.419–421, 10.443–445, 10.472–474). Some things he tells he cannot have witnessed. The companions' talk is given as what a man would say, εἴπεσκεν, in the verse that gives the talk of the gods watching Ares and Aphrodite (10.37 = 8.328). What Circe did to the men inside her house (10.233–243) Eurylochus did not see, for he stayed outside, and his report ends where they vanish (10.259–260). The ram and the ewe tied by the ship Odysseus found there, but no one saw Circe bring them: “who could see a god against his will / with his eyes, going either this way or that?” (10.573–574; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**A month and a year.** The book holds a month on Aeolus' island (10.14) and a year on Aeaea; of the stays of the wanderings only the seven years with Calypso were longer (7.259; see [[calypso]]). The year is told in four verses (10.467–470), and Merry, explaining a promise of Circe's that is nowhere told, observes that we have “the doings of a whole year unrecorded” (on 10.483). The verses of the year's turning, ἀλλʼ ὅτε δή ῥʼ ἐνιαυτὸς ἔην, περὶ δʼ ἔτραπον ὧραι / μηνῶν φθινόντων (10.469–470), are also Hesiod's, for the year before Mnemosyne bore the Muses (*Theogony* 58–59, with πόλλʼ ἐτελέσθη for μακρὰ τελέσθη); the second returns in the story of Penelope's web (19.153, 24.143, Greek only). Merry thinks it borrowed from Hesiod, and it has been rejected since Wolf, being wanting in the best manuscripts, as it is where it recurs, and in Eustathius (on 10.470 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 10.469–470 and [[the-web-of-penelope]]).
