**The verse.** Circe tells Odysseus that before he goes home he must go to the house of Hades “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias”, and she says why to him: the seer's wits are steadfast, for Persephone has given him a mind even in death, while the rest of the dead are shadows (10.490–495; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-blind-seer]]). Socrates quotes only the last verse, οἴῳ πεπνῦσθαι, ταὶ δὲ σκιαὶ ἀΐσσουσι, between Achilles' cry that there is a soul and a phantom in the house of Hades but no wits in it at all and the soul that flies to Hades lamenting its fate (*Republic* 386d; *Iliad* 23.103–104, 16.856–857). Cut off from the verse before it, the dative οἴῳ, “for him alone”, has nothing to depend on, for in Circe's sentence it is attracted into the case of τῷ, “to him”, to whom Persephone gave a mind (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.495). What the guardians are not to hear is the second half, the dead as shadows that flit about; in the poem the first half is the reason for the journey, since it is the seer who will tell Odysseus “the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming” (10.539–540). Plato's text reads ταί, where ours has τοί (Merry's critical note on 10.495; see the translation's note on 10.495).

**The shadows.** Proclus, answering Socrates, explains the verse by the vehicles that souls carry when they leave the body. Souls not purified by philosophy, and still drawn to the body and to its life, have vehicles that are shadowy, heavy and full of matter, and that is why Socrates in the *Phaedo* says that such souls roll about the tombs as shadowy phantoms, “and the poet has told that they flit about like shadows” (*On the Republic* I 119 Kroll, a plain rendering; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[psyche]]). The rest of his answer is told in the entry above.

**The Meno.** Plato quotes the line once more, not to strike it out but as praise. Virtue, Socrates concludes, comes to those who have it by a divine dispensation, unless there were a statesman able to make another man a statesman; such a man would be among the living what Homer said Teiresias was among the dead, “he alone of those in Hades has his wits, but the others flit as shadows”, and like a true thing among shadows as regards virtue (*Meno* 99e–100a, plain renderings). The verse is recast as prose: the infinitive becomes a finite verb, οἶος πέπνυται, the words “of those in Hades” are added, and “the others” are τοί, as in our text (see [[plato]]).
