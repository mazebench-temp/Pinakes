---
summary: Odysseus refuses Circe's meal while his companions are swine: “Circe, what man who was fair-minded / would bring himself to taste food and drink / before he had set his companions free and seen them with his eyes?” (10.383–385).
en: [fair-minded]
---

ἐναίσιμος, “fair-minded”, is the man whose mind gives each thing its due (αἶσα). When Circe asks why he sits without eating, Odysseus answers (ὃς ἐναίσιμος εἴη, 10.383; see [[fasting]] and [[hetairos]]):

{{quote:7b28bfb6-f356-5708-b860-0458a02a0fbe}}

The first πρίν only prepares for the second, and Merry renders: “Why, what man who was right-minded could have the heart to taste meat and drink before that he had rescued his comrades?” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.384). Calypso claimed the same word for herself when she promised him his raft, “for my mind too is right” (5.190), and the beggar's test in Ithaca is to learn which of the suitors are ἐναίσιμοι and which lawless (ἀθέμιστοι, 17.363; Merry on 10.110; see [[themis]]).
