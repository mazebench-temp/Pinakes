---
summary: The scouts ask the Laestrygonian girl who is king of her people “and over whom he ruled” (10.110); Circe's wolves and lions fawn on the men “as when dogs fawn around their master” (10.216).
---

The scouts at the spring ask the king's daughter “who was king of these people and over whom he ruled” (καὶ οἷσιν ἀνάσσοι, 10.110; see [[basileus]] and [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]): with οἷσιν the question is double, asking the name both of the king and of the people (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.110). In the simile of Circe's beasts, ἄναξ is the master of the house, as in the Cyclops' words to his ram (9.452; Merry on 10.216): “And as when dogs fawn around their master as he comes from a feast” (ἀμφὶ ἄνακτα, 10.216; see [[the-dogs-fawning-on-their-master]] and [[dogs]]).
