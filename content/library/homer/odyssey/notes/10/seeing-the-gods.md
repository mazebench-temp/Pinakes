summary: Circe goes down to the ship before the men and ties the ram and the ewe beside it unseen: “who could see a god against his will / with his eyes, going either this way or that?” (10.573–574). Hermes comes to Odysseus in the likeness of a young man (10.277–279), and at Circe's door the companions cannot tell whether the singer within is “either a goddess or a woman” (10.228, 10.255).
refs: [10.228, 10.255, 10.278, 10.571-574]

**Unseen.** The book ends with the gods' privilege (see [[circe]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]):

{{quote:6cb2568c-e57a-5bd5-9f49-23336b4ea922}}

ῥεῖα παρεξελθοῦσα is “having easily slipped past us”, and ἢ ἔνθʼ ἢ ἔνθα, “backward or forward, to and fro”, a phrase for “at any time” or “in any place”: the gods are visible and invisible at pleasure, as Athena is seen by Odysseus and not by Telemachus, and by Achilles alone in the Iliad (16.161; *Iliad* 1.198; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.573, 10.574).

**In a mortal's likeness.** Hermes meets Odysseus “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip” (10.278–279; see [[hermes]] and [[shape-changing]]). The teller names him from the first, “Hermes of the golden wand met me” (10.277), and he goes away to Olympus across the wooded island when he has given the drug (10.307–308).

**Goddess or woman.** At Circe's door the companions hear a voice singing at the loom, and Polites cannot say whose it is, “either a goddess or a woman” (ἢ θεὸς ἠὲ γυνή, 10.228; again 10.255; see [[weaving]] and [[polites-the-companion]]). The narrator has told who she is, “a dread goddess with human voice” (δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, 10.136; see [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]).
