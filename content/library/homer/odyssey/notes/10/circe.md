summary: Circe is the goddess of the book. On Aeaea she lives in a house of polished stone among the wolves and lions she has bewitched, and with her mixed drink, her drugs and her wand she turns the companions whom Eurylochus led into swine. Odysseus, armed by Hermes with the good drug, drinks her mixture unharmed, draws his sword on her, makes her swear the great oath and goes up into her bed; she frees the companions, keeps them all a year, and at his entreaty sends him first to the house of Hades to consult Teiresias, and on the last morning sets the victims by the ship unseen.

**The goddess of Aeaea.** Odysseus begins her story as he began the story of Aeolus, with the island and the power who lived there: “And we came to the island of Aeaea; and there lived / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice, / own sister of baleful Aeetes” (10.135–137; the first verse is 10.1 with another island; see [[aeaea]], [[aeetes]], [[aeolus]] and the general article). ἐυπλόκαμος, δεινὴ θεός are the words of Calypso too (7.246, 7.255, 12.449; see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[dread-goddess]]), and αὐδήεσσα, “with human voice”, is the word the ancients doubted in Ino's verse; at 10.136 Merry reports a reading οὐδήεσσα, “earthly”, which a scholium, as Dindorf corrected it, gives to Aristotle, and from the scholia on Ino's verse he supposes that Aristotle wrote αὐλήεσσα, “dwelling in a hall” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 10.136; see [[with-human-voice]] and the translation's note on 10.135). Odysseus names her before he knew her: from the lookout he sees smoke rising “in the halls of Circe, through the thick brush and the woods” (10.150). The teller knows what the explorer did not.

**The house in the glens.** “In the glens they found the house of Circe, built / of polished stones, in a place with a view all round; / and around her were mountain wolves and lions, / whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs” (10.210–213; see [[house]], [[wolves]], [[lion]] and [[drugs]]). περισκέπτῳ ἐνὶ χώρῳ is open ground, since the island lies low (Merry on 10.211). μιν, Merry argues, refers to Circe, not to the house: the beasts are in attendance upon her, whether near her house or about her person, and he compares Apollonius, whose beasts follow her like sheep from the fold, and the queen of the *Arabian Nights* who changed her tired lovers into beasts (on 10.212). κατέθελξεν, “bewitched”, the scholia explain in two ways, as made tame and gentle, or as changed from men into the form of lions. Scaliger, on Virgil's Circe, took the first view; Merry prefers the second, since θέλγειν is the word of the sorcery that ends in the sty (10.291, 10.320), Eurylochus fears that she will make them all “either swine or wolves or lions” (10.433), and her drugs here are κακά, “evil”, against Hermes' “good drug” (10.292; on 10.213; see [[thelgo]] and [[shape-changing]]). The beasts do not attack the men, but stand on their hind legs and fawn on them, “as when dogs fawn around their master as he comes from a feast” (10.214–219; Merry on 10.215; see [[the-dogs-fawning-on-their-master]]).

**At the loom.** The men stand in the doorway “of the goddess of the lovely tresses” (10.220; see [[of-the-lovely-tresses]]), “and they heard Circe within singing with a lovely voice / as she walked to and fro at a great immortal loom, such as are / the fine and lovely and splendid works of goddesses” (10.221–223; see [[lovely-voice]], [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). So Hermes found Calypso, “singing with a lovely voice”, walking to and fro at her loom (5.61–62). Polites, the dearest of the companions, hears “either a goddess or a woman” (10.228; see [[polites-the-companion]]), and Eurylochus repeats the words when he tells the story (10.254–255). The Suda derives her name from the weaver's shuttle (see the general article).

**The drink and the wand.** She opens the shining doors and calls them in, and all but Eurylochus follow “in their ignorance” (10.230–232; see [[eurylochus]]):

{{quote:37aa1a94-d3d6-500d-ac11-7d962bcf6675}}

The mixture of cheese, barley meal and honey in Pramnian wine is the κυκεών that Hecamede made for Nestor and Machaon in the Iliad, there without the honey (*Iliad* 11.624–641): a sort of stimulant, not a regular article of food (Merry on 10.234; see [[kykeon]], [[pramnian]], [[cheese]], [[barley-meal]] and [[honey]]). The drugs are φάρμακα λυγρά, “grim drugs”, given “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (10.236), as the lotus made men forget their homecoming (9.97; see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[nostos]]). Her wand strikes, and the men are penned in the sties (10.238; see [[wand]] and [[sties]]). Merry asks whether her sorcery was perfectly successful: the drugs were meant to make them forget, but though she turned them into swine, the νοῦς remained (on 10.240; see [[noos]]). For καὶ δέμας, “and the form”, Zenodotus read καὶ πόδας, “and the feet” (Merry's critical note on 10.240; see the translation's note on 10.240).

**Her baneful thoughts.** Hermes, meeting Odysseus in the glens, calls her house the place where the companions “are penned like swine” and tells him “all the baneful thoughts of Circe” (10.283, 10.289; see [[hermes]]): she will make him a mixed drink and put drugs in the food, but she will not be able to bewitch him; when she strikes him with “her very long wand”, he is to draw his sword and spring at her; in fear she will bid him lie with her, and he must not refuse “the bed of the goddess”, but make her swear a great oath (10.290–301). δήνεα, “thoughts”, is the word of the Iliad's “kindly thoughts”, which Agamemnon says Odysseus' heart knows (*Iliad* 4.360–361; see the translation's key-word row). πολυφάρμακος, “skilled in many drugs”, is her epithet as Odysseus reaches her house (10.276; see [[with-many-drugs]]). The glens and the house are ἱερός, “sacred”, as belonging to her (10.275, 10.426, 10.445, 10.554; Merry on 10.275; see [[hieros]]).

**The drug that failed.** Circe receives Odysseus as she received his men, opens the shining doors and calls him in, seats him on a silver-studded chair with a footstool, and makes him the mixed drink in a golden cup, “meaning evil in her heart” (10.310–317; see [[throne]], [[footstool]] and [[cup]]). The drink does not bewitch him; she strikes him with her wand: “Go now to the sty, and lie down with the rest of your companions” (10.318–320). He draws his sword and springs at her “as though eager to kill her”, and she, “with a great cry, ran in under it and clasped my knees” (10.321–323; see [[sword]] and [[supplication]]):

{{quote:8ffb766d-8f4e-53f8-b163-b98b2375f7fc}}

Her first verse is the stranger's question that Telemachus put to Athena (1.170; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). ὡς, “how”, suggests not only the fact but the manner, and need not be changed to πῶς (Merry on 10.326). The scholia objected to 10.329, since the companions too had kept their minds; Merry answers that she means the strong and stubborn mind that was generally proof against sorcery, for she does not know that he carries an amulet (Merry on 10.329; see the translation's note on 10.329). Hermes, who armed Odysseus against her, had long foretold his coming to her (10.330–332; see [[of-many-turns]] and [[argeiphontes]]), as Telemus foretold the Cyclops' blinding (9.507–512). The bed of 10.334–335 is to be a pledge: ἡμετέρης is proleptic, “our bed”, and πεποίθομεν is the subjunctive of the perfect, “that we may trust” (Merry on 10.334, 10.335; see [[philotes]] and [[bed]]).

**The oath.** Odysseus will not go up to her bed: “Circe, how can you bid me be kind to you, / you who turned my companions into swine in your halls, / and keeping me myself here, with treachery in your heart, you bid me / go into your chamber and go up into your bed, / so that when I am stripped you may make me a coward and unmanned?” (10.337–341; see [[with-treachery-in-her-heart]]). He asks for the oath in the words he asked of Calypso, “unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath / that you will not plan some other evil bane against me myself” (10.343–344 = 5.178–179; see [[oath]] and [[calypso]]). “So I spoke, and at once she swore that she would not, as I bade her” (10.345), “then I went up into the beautiful bed of Circe” (10.347).

**The companions restored.** While her four handmaids, born of the springs, the groves and the rivers, make ready the chairs, the tables, the wine and the bath (10.348–372; see [[handmaids]], [[nymphs]] and [[bathing]]), Odysseus sits without eating. Circe asks him: “Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man without speech, / eating your heart, and do not touch food or drink? / Do you suspect some other trick? You have no need / to fear; for I have already sworn you a mighty oath” (10.378–381; see [[like-a-man-without-speech]] and [[dolos]]). His answer, “Circe, what man who was fair-minded / would bring himself to taste food and drink / before he had set his companions free and seen them with his eyes?” (10.383–385), uses the middle λύσασθαι of his own act and the active λῦσον, “release them”, of hers (10.387; Merry on 10.385). She goes out “with her wand in her hand”, drives the swine out of the sty, “looking like fat hogs nine years old”, and anoints each of them with another drug (10.388–392):

{{quote:180cfc6a-0da3-5f35-8fc2-f92bae4ee05a}}

πότνια Κίρκη, “lady Circe”, is her title in the verse that names her drug “ruinous” (10.394; see [[queenly]] and the translation's formula row). Merry notes that Ovid goes into the details of the change back (on 10.393). The goddess who made them swine now pities them (10.399; see [[pity]]), and as host she bids Odysseus beach the ship and bring the rest of his men (10.400–405; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]).

**Her welcome.** Eurylochus fears her still: she “will make all of us / either swine or wolves or lions, / to guard her great house for her even under compulsion” (10.432–434). But she bathes the newcomers and anoints and clothes them (10.449–451), and when the two bands have wept together she stops their lament: “Rouse this swelling lament no longer; I myself know too / both all the sorrows you suffered on the fish-filled sea, / and all the harm that merciless men did you on land” (10.457–459; see [[lament]]). She bids them eat and drink until they have the heart they had when they left Ithaca: “But now you are withered and spiritless, / always remembering your hard wandering” (10.463–464; Merry on 10.463). Murray omits 10.456, which Wolf bracketed because a plural imperative follows an address to Odysseus (see the translation's note on 10.456).

**The way to the dead.** After a year, in her bed, Odysseus “entreated her by her knees, and the goddess heard my voice” (10.481), and reminds her of “the promise you made, / to send me home” (10.483–484). Merry finds no such promise stated, unless it was in her oath or in her welcome (on 10.483). She answers at once: “stay no longer in my house against your will” (10.489). Calypso held him “by force” (5.14–15), and he lay beside her “unwilling beside the willing” (5.155); Circe lets him go when he asks. But she sends him first “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (10.490–492; see [[hades]], [[persephone]] and [[teiresias]]), and when he asks who will guide them, she gives him the North Wind for a guide and the whole rite of the dead: the crossing of Oceanus, the pit, the drink offerings, the vows, the ram and the black ewe, and the drawn sword that keeps the dead from the blood until he has questioned the seer (10.504–540; see [[boreas]], [[oceanus]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). “Lady Circe has shown me the way”, he tells his companions (10.549; ἐπέφραδε, “given her directions”, Merry on 10.549), and “Circe has ordained another journey for us” (10.563).

**Unseen.** At dawn she dresses Odysseus and herself (10.542–545; see [[nymphs]] and [[eos]]). As the men go down weeping to the ship, “meanwhile Circe had gone and tied up beside the black ship / a ram and a black ewe, / slipping past us easily; who could see a god against his will / with his eyes, going either this way or that?” (10.571–574; see [[ram]], [[sheep]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). ῥεῖα παρεξελθοῦσα is “having easily slipped past us”, and ἢ ἔνθʼ ἢ ἔνθα “to and fro”, a phrase for “at any time” or “in any place”: the gods are visible and invisible at pleasure, as Athena appears to Achilles “to him alone; none of the others saw her” (*Iliad* 1.198; Merry on 10.573, 10.574). She goes without a farewell; in Book 12 she will come to meet them again (12.16–27; see the general article).
