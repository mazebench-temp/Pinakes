---
summary: Aeaea is the island of the book's second half: the one ship comes in to its harbor in silence (10.133–143); from a lookout Odysseus sees the smoke of Circe's house in the middle of a low island ringed by the sea (10.144–152, 10.194–197); in its glens the companions find the house (10.210–211); the ship is drawn up and the tackle stored in its caves (10.401–405, 10.423–424), and they stay there a year (10.467–470) before Circe sends them to the house of Hades.
# The island without its name: "an island" seen from the lookout (10.195-196), "across the wooded island" (10.308).
refs: [10.195-196, 10.308]
passages:
  - 10.133-197 | The landing on Aeaea
---

**The landing.** From the Laestrygonians the one ship sails on, “grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (10.133–134), and comes to the island:

{{quote:13a3f402-6b39-5553-953a-8c11586ebced}}

The island is named at once, and its mistress with it, before the men know who lives there (see [[circe]], [[aeetes]], [[helios]] and [[perse]]). The ship comes in “into a harbor where ships can lie”, ναύλοχον ἐς λιμένα, the word for the harbors of the islet of Asteris (4.846; see [[harbor]]), “and some god guided us”, as on the night they came to the island of goats (9.142; see [[the-island-of-goats]]). They put in σιωπῇ, “in silence”, out of fear, Eustathius says, after the inhospitality of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.140; see [[laestrygonians]]), and for two days and nights they lie on the shore, “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together” (10.142–143).

**The island from the lookout.** On the third day Odysseus climbs to a lookout and sees smoke rising “in the halls of Circe, through the thick brush and the woods” (10.144–150); on the way back to the ship a god sends a great stag into his path, coming down from its pasture in the woods to the river to drink (10.156–171; see [[deer]] and [[rivers]]). The next morning he tells his companions what he saw:

{{quote:f71ab41b-72a7-5a69-ac8b-9f7065601408}}

ζόφος and ἠώς are the darkness and the dawn. Crates and Strabo took ζόφος here for the north rather than the west; Merry takes the pair, as in Hector's words on the birds that fly “toward the dawn and the sun” or “toward the misty darkness” (*Iliad* 12.239–240), for the whole world divided between east and west, so that Odysseus means only that he has no idea where they are (on 10.190; see the translation's note on 10.190). The island is “set as a crown” by the boundless sea, ἐστεφάνωται, the verb of the shield of Achilles, where the sky is “crowned” with all the constellations (*Iliad* 18.485; Merry on 10.195); and αὐτὴ δὲ χθαμαλὴ κεῖται, “and the island itself lies low”, sets the island against the lookout from which he saw it (Merry on 10.196), in the words he used of Ithaca, “my island itself lies low in the sea” (9.25; see [[ithaca]]). The men weep, remembering the Laestrygonians and the Cyclops (10.198–202), and the lot sends Eurylochus' band to the smoke (10.203–209).

**The glens.** “In the glens they found the house of Circe, built / of polished stones, in a place with a view all round” (10.210–211, again in Eurylochus' report, 10.252–253). περισκέπτῳ ἐνὶ χώρῳ must mean open ground, Merry argues, since the men had come down from the lookout and the island is low: a clearing in the wood, or a place where the valleys meet (on 10.211; see [[in-a-place-with-a-view]] and the translation's note on 10.211). Odysseus goes up alone “through the sacred glens”, ἱερὰς ἀνὰ βήσσας (10.275), sacred, Merry explains, as Circe's (on 10.275), as her house is “the sacred house of Circe” (10.426, 10.445, 10.554), and there Hermes meets him and goes away again to Olympus “across the wooded island” (10.307–308; see [[hermes]] and [[olympus]]).

**A year on the island.** Circe bids Odysseus drag the ship up on the land and put the possessions and all the tackle away in caves, and he gives his men the same orders in the same words (10.401–405, 10.423–424; see [[possessions]]): the ship is laid up for a long stay. “There every day, for a full year's round, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (10.467–468), until the seasons come round and the companions remind Odysseus of home (10.469–474). As they leave for the house of Hades, Elpenor falls to his death from Circe's roof (10.552–560; see [[elpenor]]), and Circe, unseen, ties a ram and a black ewe beside the ship (10.569–574); the ship will come back to Aeaea to bury him (11.69–72; 12.1–15).
