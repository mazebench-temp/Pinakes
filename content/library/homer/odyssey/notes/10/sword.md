---
summary: Odysseus cuts his ship's cables with his sword to escape the Laestrygonians (10.126–127), carries sword and spear to the lookout on Aeaea (10.145), and slings on his silver-studded sword to go to Circe (10.261–262). Hermes bids him draw it when her wand strikes him, and he springs at her until she clasps his knees and bids him sheathe it (10.293–295, 10.321–333); he thinks of striking off Eurylochus' head with it (10.438–440), and Circe bids him sit with it drawn at the pit of the dead (10.535–537).
---

**The verse of the drawn sword.** The verse with which Achilles draws on Agamemnon, ξίφος ὀξὺ ἐρυσσάμενος παρὰ μηροῦ, “drawing the sharp sword from beside his thigh” (*Iliad* 1.190, with φάσγανον), returns four times in this book, and each time it marks a turn of the tale (see the Book 9 note). In the harbor of the Laestrygonians, while the other ships are being stoned, “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and with it cut the cables of my dark-prowed ship” (10.126–127; see [[stern-cables]] and [[laestrygonians]]). Hermes gives Odysseus the counsel he will follow: “When Circe strikes you with her very long wand, / then draw the sharp sword from beside your thigh / and spring at Circe as though eager to kill her” (10.293–295; see [[wand]] and [[hermes]]), and so he does: “I drew the sharp sword from beside my thigh / and sprang at Circe as though eager to kill her. / And she, with a great cry, ran in under it and clasped my knees” (10.321–323; see [[circe]] and [[supplication]]). Last, Circe bids him draw it at the pit of the dead and sit there, keeping the strengthless heads from the blood until he has questioned Teiresias (10.535–537; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]] and [[the-strengthless-heads]]).

**The silver-studded sword.** Before he goes alone to Circe's house, “I slung about my shoulders my silver-studded sword, / great and made of bronze, and my bow about them” (10.261–262; see [[silver-studded]], [[bronze]] and [[bow]]). On the first morning on Aeaea he had gone up to the lookout with “my spear and my sharp sword” (10.145; see [[spear]]).

**Sheathed and drawn.** Circe, finding him proof against her drug, says, “But come, put your sword in its sheath, and then let us two / go up into our bed” (10.333–334; see [[sheath]] and [[bed]]). Hermes had told him to make her swear an oath first, “lest, when you are stripped, she make you a coward and unmanned” (10.299–301; see [[oath]]): the scholion took ἀπογυμνωθέντα as “without his sword”, but it is simply “when stripped”, W. W. Merry explains, as εὐνηθῆναι suggests (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.301; see the translation's note on 10.301). When Eurylochus calls him reckless, Odysseus ponders “whether to draw the long-edged sword from beside my stout thigh, / and strike off his head with it and bring it to the ground, / kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near” (10.439–441; see [[long-edged]] and [[eurylochus]]), until the companions hold him back.
