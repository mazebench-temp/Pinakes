summary: Aeolus is “dear to the immortal gods” and drives Odysseus away as a man the gods hate (10.2, 10.73–75); “some god guided us” to Aeaea, and “one of the gods pitied me” (10.141, 10.157); Circe is “a dread goddess with human voice” (10.136), whom the companions take for “either a goddess or a woman” (10.228); the gods call the plant moly, and “the gods can do all things” (10.305–306); and “who could see a god against his will” (10.573).
---

The book divides men by the gods' favor. Aeolus is “dear to the immortal gods” (10.2), and when the winds bring Odysseus back he will not help “a man who is hated by the blessed gods” (10.73–75; see [[themis]] and [[aeolus]]). The gods who help are mostly unnamed: “some god guided us” into the harbor of Aeaea (10.141), and “one of the gods pitied me, alone as I was” (10.157; see [[divine-intervention]]). The goddess of the book is Circe, “a dread goddess with human voice” (δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, 10.136; see [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]), whom the companions hear singing and take for “either a goddess or a woman” (10.228, 10.255), whose bed Odysseus must not refuse (10.297), and whom the formulas call “the goddess of the lovely tresses” and “brilliant among goddesses” (10.220, 10.310; 10.400, 10.455, 10.487, 10.503; see [[of-the-lovely-tresses]] and [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]). The gods have their own word for the moly and their own power: “moly the gods call it; and it is hard to dig up / for mortal men; but the gods can do all things” (10.305–306; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). At the pit the companions are to pray to the gods below, “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (10.533–534; see [[prayer]]), and the book ends with the gods' freedom to come and go unseen (10.573–574; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).
