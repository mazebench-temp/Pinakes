**The warning.** The Sirens are the first of Circe's dangers, Σειρῆνας μὲν πρῶτον (12.39); after them comes the choice of two ways, which she leaves to Odysseus (12.55–58; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). They bewitch all men, θέλγουσιν (12.40, 12.44; see [[thelgo]]); the man who comes near does so “in his ignorance”, ἀιδρείῃ (12.41), as the companions went into Circe's house “in their ignorance” (10.231). For λιγυρῇ … ἀοιδῇ, “their clear song” (12.44), Merry compares Alcman, who calls the Muse “the clear-voiced Siren” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.44; see the translation's key-word row for λιγυρὴ ἀοιδή). The heap of bones of rotting men, with the skins dwindling about them, is set beside the meadow in which they sit (12.45–46); Merry weighs the construction of ὀστεόφιν and takes ῥινοί for flesh and skin together (on 12.45; see the translation's note on 12.45).

**The remedy.** “But row on past, and anoint the ears of your companions, / kneading honey-sweet wax” (12.47–48): the infinitives have the force of commands (Merry on 12.47; see the translation's note on 12.47–50). Odysseus may listen if he wishes, bound hand and foot, “upright in the mast-step, and let the rope-ends be fastened to the mast itself” (12.50–51). The mast-step, ἱστοπέδη, is virtually the “hollow mast-box”, μεσόδμη, of 2.424 and 15.289, into which the mast is set (Merry, Appendix I §12, on the Homeric ship); πείρατα are the rope-ends, the free ends of the cords with which he is tied, made fast to the mast (Merry on 12.51; see [[mast]]). If he begs to be set free, they are to bind him with more bonds (12.53–54). Aristophanes athetized the two verses, asking why a man once bound should be bound again; διδέντων, “let them bind”, is Aristarchus' reading, where the manuscripts have δεόντων (Merry's critical notes on 12.53–54; see the translation's note on 12.53–54).

**The instructions passed on.** Odysseus tells his companions “the oracles that Circe told me”, since it is not right that only one or two should know them, “so that knowing them we may either die / or else avoid death and doom and escape” (12.154–157). ἵνα with κε is found only here: Merry renders “in which case, when we have learned our lesson, we may either die or escape”, and prefers the optative φύγοιμεν because escape seemed less likely than death (on 12.156–157; see the translation's note on 12.156–157). The Sirens are θεσπεσιάων, “wondrous”, for the charm of their song, not for the revelations they profess to make (Merry on 12.158). He tells only of the Sirens: of the strait he says nothing yet, and of Scylla nothing at all (12.223; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). He tells the voice and the flowering meadow, not the bones, and turns Circe's “if you yourself are willing to listen” (12.49) into “She bade me alone to hear their voice” (12.160). The binding is repeated in her verses, now in the second person plural (12.162 = 12.51; 12.163–164 after 12.53–54), and the scholia report that these two verses too were obelized (Merry's critical note on 12.163–164).

**The calm.** “For a harmless wind drove it on” (12.167): the island is νῆσον Σειρήνοιιν, “the island of the two Sirens”, in the dual (12.167; see the translation's note on 12.39 and [[sirens]]). Then the wind ceases, γαλήνη / ἔπλετο νηνεμίη, “a calm, / a windless calm”, in the verses of the calm that came on Odysseus' third day in the sea after the wreck of the raft (12.168–169; 5.390–392), and “a god lulled the waves to sleep” (12.169; see the translation's note on 12.168). Merry, explaining the legend, sees in the Sirens the hidden dangers of the calm sea, and in their song perhaps the sound of water splashing in caves and over broken rocks (on 12.39). The companions stow the sail, which must therefore have been lowered from the mast, not merely brailed up (Merry on 12.170), and make the water white with their polished oars of fir (12.172).

**The wax.** Odysseus cuts “a great round of wax” into small pieces with the sharp bronze (12.173–174): the wax was a disc, Merry supposes, because it had been melted, clarified and poured into a pan whose shape it kept as it cooled (on 12.174). It soon grows warm, “since the great strength bade it / and the ray of the lord Sun, son of Hyperion” (12.175–176; see [[hyperion]] and [[helios]]). κέλετο is used of persons everywhere else in Homer and nowhere else of an external force; Nitzsch would delete the second verse, but the scrapings of wax would soften far more readily when kneaded in the full sun, which suits the windless calm, and Merry finds a conscious touch of humour in such majestic language for the kneading of a cake of wax (Merry on 12.175; see the translation's note on 12.175–176). The Sun, whose cattle the companions will kill on the next island, helps them past the Sirens (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Then the companions bind him, in the verses of Circe's instruction turned to the narrative, and row on (12.178–180; 12.179 = 12.51, with ἀνῆπτον).

**The song.** “But when we were as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout” (12.181) is the verse in which Odysseus, leaving the island of the Cyclops, called out to Polyphemus (9.473), except that the manuscripts and the text read ἀπῆμεν ὅσον, “we were”, where Merry prints ἀπῆν ὅσσον with the scholia and the verse elsewhere: ἀπῆμεν may be an early correction to suit διώκοντες (Merry's critical note and on 12.181; see the translation's note on 12.181). The swift ship does not escape the Sirens' notice, and they raise their clear song (12.182–183). νωιτέρην ὄπʼ, “the voice of us two” (12.185), shows again that Homer's Sirens are two, though the dual was forgotten in the plurals τάς and ἔντυνον just before (Merry on 12.185). The man who has heard them “goes home delighted, and knowing more” (12.188): the aorist τερψάμενος, “he has had his pleasure”, is set against εἰδώς, “the knowledge abides with him”; μόγησαν tells of the one completed war, γένηται of what comes to pass at all times (Merry on 12.188). For Cicero's reading of the song as a promise of knowledge, see the general article.

**The bonds.** “And my heart wished to listen, and I bade my companions set me free, / nodding with my brows” (12.192–194): it was no use to speak, since their ears were stopped (Merry on 12.194). Perimedes and Eurylochus, the two who held the victims at the pit in the land of the dead (11.23), stand up and bind him tighter (12.195–196; see [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]). At the end of the sentence ἀοιδῆς, “their song”, names the more definite thing and explains φθογγῆς, “the voice” (12.198; Merry on 12.198). Then “my trusty companions took away the wax / with which I had anointed their ears, and freed me from my bonds” (12.199–200). The next thing he sees is the smoke of the strait (12.201–202).
