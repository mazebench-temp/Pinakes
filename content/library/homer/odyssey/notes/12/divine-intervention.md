---
summary: The gods of the book act on the sea and on sleep. Circe promises that “a god himself will remind you” (12.38) and sends a following wind (12.148–150); Hera once brought the Argo past the Wanderers (12.72); a god lulls the waves before the Sirens (12.169); the gods pour sleep on Odysseus and show portents to the companions (12.338, 12.394–396); Zeus wrecks the ship (12.405–417); and Zeus keeps Scylla from seeing him, and the gods bring him to Ogygia (12.445–448).
refs: [12.38, 12.72, 12.169, 12.338, 12.394-396, 12.445-446, 12.448]
---

**Help.** Circe ends her prologue with a promise, “and a god himself will remind you” (12.38): no special god is meant, neither Poseidon, as the scholium suggests, nor the Sun, but some kindly power in the most general sense (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.38; see [[theos]]). She sends the following wind herself, “a good companion” (12.148–150; see [[circe]]). Of the ships that have tried the Wanderers only the Argo came through, “but Hera sent her past, since Jason was dear to her” (12.72; see [[hera]] and [[jason]]). Before the Sirens “a god lulled the waves to sleep” (12.169; see [[daimon]]); after the wreck Zeus does not let Scylla see him, “for then I would not have escaped sheer destruction” (12.445–446), and the gods bring him to Ogygia (12.448; see [[ogygia]]). The scholia rejected 12.445–446, since Scylla could not leave her cave; Merry suggests taking ἐμέ as the subject, so that Zeus pitied Odysseus and did not let him set eyes on Scylla again (Merry on 12.445–446; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.445–446).

**Ruin.** The same gods bring the companions down. On Thrinacia Odysseus prays, “and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids” (12.338; see [[hypnos]] and [[prayer]]); when the cattle are dead “the gods showed signs to them” (12.394; see [[teras]]); and Zeus, who promised the Sun to destroy the ship, sets a dark cloud above it, sends the West Wind and strikes with the thunderbolt (12.385–388, 12.405–417; see [[zeus]] and [[thunderbolt]]). Each act of the gods meets a human choice: the companions chose the landing and the cattle, and Odysseus chose to hear the Sirens bound to the mast (see [[who-is-to-blame]]).
