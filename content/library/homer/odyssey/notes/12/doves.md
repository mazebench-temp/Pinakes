**The doves of Zeus.** τῇ μέν τʼ οὐδὲ ποτητὰ παρέρχεται οὐδὲ πέλειαι / τρήρωνες, ταί τʼ ἀμβροσίην Διὶ πατρὶ φέρουσιν (12.62–63; see [[the-wanderers]], [[birds]] and [[ambrosia]]). W. W. Merry renders “there, not even can bird pass by, no! not the timorous doves that carry ambrosia for Father Zeus: but even of them the sheer rock ever steals away one, and the Father sends in another one to make up the tale” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.62; see the translation's row ποτητά; λὶς πέτρη; ἐναρίθμιος). πέλειαι τρήρωνες runs over the verse, the noun at the end of one line and its epithet at the head of the next. τρήρων, from τρέω, “tremble, flee”, implies, Merry says, “the timidity that shows itself in rapid flight” (on 12.63; see [[timid]]): the dove that the eagle holds in its claws in a later book is τρήρωνα πέλειαν (20.243), as is the dove that the falcon hunts in the Iliad (*Iliad* 22.140). Nowhere else does Homer say that doves bring the gods their food. The danger is told only as a passing: παρέρχεται, “pass by”, and nothing is said of rocks that close upon the birds; yet ἀφαιρεῖται λὶς πέτρη, “the sheer rock … takes one away”, may be a hint of the danger that later legend developed, and Merry recalls the dove that Euphemus sends ahead of the Argo to see whether the passage through the Clashing Rocks can be made (on 12.63, citing Apollonius, *Argonautica* 2.561).

**Doves and Pleiads.** Merry denies any connection with the later story that doves fed the infant Zeus when he was hidden in Crete from his father, and thinks there is probably an allusion to the Pleiads, at whose rising in May the harvest begins, “which fact may be veiled under the story of the ambrosia” (on 12.62; see [[pleiades]]); Hesiod's farmer begins the harvest when the Pleiades rise (*Works and Days* 383–384). The rock that always takes one away, he adds, may contain the fact that of the seven Pleiads only six are ordinarily seen, and some of the ancient scholars, as the scholia report, explained the verse “physically”: when the Pleiad came to that place, one of its seven stars vanished in the smoke of the strait (on 12.62; 12.202). In his note on the Pleiads of the fifth book Merry gives the view that the stars were pictured as a flock of doves, πελειάδες, fleeing the hunter Orion (on 5.272; see [[orion]]); Aeschylus says that the seven daughters of Atlas, weeping for their father's labor of holding up the sky, have the shapes of phantoms of the night, ἄπτεροι πελειάδες, “wingless doves” (Aeschylus fr. 312 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]).

**The number.** ἐναρίθμιος, “to make up the number”, is “counted in”: the Iliad's only other use of the word is for the man who is “never counted for anything in war or in council” (*Iliad* 2.202). The father replaces each lost dove, as the herds of the Sun on Thrinacia never grow fewer or more (12.129–131; see [[cattle]]). Merry contrasts the doves with the ships of men, which the Wanderers never let through (on 12.66): only the Argo passed them, because Hera loved Jason (12.69–72; see [[the-argo]]).
