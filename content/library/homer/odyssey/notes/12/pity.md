---
summary: Two superlatives of pity: the six companions in Scylla's jaws were “the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes” (12.258), and to Eurylochus death by hunger is “the most pitiable” of deaths (12.342).
# οἴκτιστον (12.258, 12.342), first met here in the Odyssey; Book 11 has οἰκτίστῳ.
grc: [οἴκτιστον]
---

**What Odysseus saw.** Scylla devours the six men at her doors, screaming and stretching out their hands to him, “That was the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (οἴκτιστον δὴ κεῖνο, 12.256–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[scylla]]). The order of the words is that of Book 11, where the most pitiable voice he heard among the dead was Cassandra's (11.421), and of the Iliad's verse of Bellerophon's hardest fight (*Iliad* 6.185; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.258). ἐν αἰνῇ δηιοτῆτι, “in grim combat”, is more graphic than a word for death or pain, for it implies an unavailing struggle with an overmastering power, a “death-struggle” (Merry on 12.257; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.256–257). It is the same superlative as Agamemnon's “So I died a most pitiable death” (11.412).

**What Eurylochus fears.** “All deaths are hateful to wretched mortals, / but to die of hunger and meet one's fate is the most pitiable” (12.341–342; see [[all-deaths-are-hateful]]), as Priam calls the old man's body torn by dogs “the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals” (*Iliad* 22.76). The pity Odysseus felt at the sight of the six becomes, in Eurylochus' mouth, a reason for the deed that will kill the rest.
