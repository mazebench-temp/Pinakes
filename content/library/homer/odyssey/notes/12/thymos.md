---
summary: The θυμός of the book: the companions' proud spirit is persuaded (12.28, 12.324); Odysseus must plan his course in his heart and bids the helmsman lay his orders to heart (12.58, 12.217); the seer's word falls upon his heart (12.266); Eurylochus would lose his life with one gulp of the wave (12.350); the helmsman's proud spirit leaves his bones (12.414); and the South Wind brings sorrows to Odysseus' heart (12.427).
---

**The heart that decides.** Circe leaves the choice of the way to Odysseus, “you yourself / must plan it in your heart” (θυμῷ βουλεύειν, 12.57–58; see [[boule]]); he gives the helmsman his orders, “lay it / to heart” (ἀλλʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ / βάλλευ, 12.217–218; see [[steering-oar]]); and off Thrinacia “there fell upon my heart the word / of the blind seer” (ἔμπεσε θυμῷ, 12.266–267; see [[epos]]). The companions' θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, their proud spirit, is persuaded by Circe and by Odysseus (12.28, 12.324; see [[our-proud-spirit-was-persuaded]] and [[peitho]]).

**The life.** θυμός is the life that leaves: Eurylochus would rather “lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave” (ἀπὸ θυμὸν ὀλέσσαι, 12.350; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]), and when the mast strikes the helmsman, “he, like a diver, / fell from the deck, and his proud spirit left his bones” (λίπε δʼ ὀστέα θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, 12.413–414; see [[like-a-diver]]). The proud spirit of the formula of persuasion is the same words as the life that leaves the helmsman. Adrift on the mast and keel, Odysseus feels the South Wind come “bringing sorrows to my heart” (ἄλγεα θυμῷ, 12.427; see [[algos]] and [[notus]]).
