---
summary: At the Wanderers the waves and “the storms of deadly fire” sweep away the planks of ships and the bodies of men (12.67–68), and their smoke is what Odysseus sees after the Sirens (12.202); Charybdis, vomiting up the sea, seethes “like a cauldron on a great fire” (12.237–238).
---

**The storms of fire.** “But the planks of ships and the bodies of men together / the waves of the sea and the storms of deadly fire sweep away” (12.67–68; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[ships]]). φορέουσι, “sweep away”, belongs closely to the waves, and only by a slight zeugma to the storms of fire (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.68). When the ship leaves the Sirens' island Odysseus sees “smoke and a great wave” and hears a roar (12.202): καπνός cannot be spray, Merry argues, for which Homer has ἄχνη (12.238), but takes up the storms of deadly fire, which by day would show only as smoke, as Pindar's Aetna sends out streams of smoke by day and red flame by night (on 12.202). Merry would find in the Wanderers the volcanic islets of the Mediterranean, rising out of the sea amid surf, smoke and flame (on 12.61; see the translation's note on 12.61).

**The cauldron.** “Truly, whenever she vomited it up, like a cauldron on a great fire / she would seethe up, all of her, churning, and high overhead the foam / fell on the tops of both crags” (12.237–239; see [[charybdis]], [[cauldron]] and [[the-boiling-cauldron]]). λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ is the simile of the Iliad's river, which boils as a cauldron boils on a great fire when Hephaestus burns its streams (*Iliad* 21.361–365; see the translation's row λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ).
