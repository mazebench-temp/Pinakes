summary: The title of gods in the book: the warmth of “the lord Sun, son of Hyperion” softens the wax for the companions' ears (12.175–176), and Eurylochus fears the winds that shatter ships “against the will of the sovereign gods” (12.290), the only place where the gods together are called ἄνακτες.
# ἀνάκτων (12.290; later 13.223, 17.189, of human lords), first met here in the Odyssey.
grc: [ἀνάκτων]
en: [sovereign gods]
---

**The lord Sun.** Odysseus cuts the wax, and it grows warm, “since the great strength bade it / and the ray of the lord Sun, son of Hyperion” (Ἠελίου τʼ αὐγὴ Ὑπεριονίδαο ἄνακτος, 12.175–176; see [[helios]] and [[hyperion]]): W. W. Merry finds a conscious touch of humor in such majestic language for the kneading of a cake of wax, and notes Nitzsch's objection to the patronymic as un-Homeric (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.175; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.175–176). The god whose title is given here is the one whose cattle the companions will kill.

**The sovereign gods.** Eurylochus fears the South and the West Winds, “which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods” (12.289–290; see [[eurylochus]] and [[the-winds]]). ἄναξ is the title of single gods, Zeus, Apollo, Hermes, Poseidon; this is the only passage where the gods have it together, and the phrase, which recalls “against fate” in Zeus' first speech (1.34), is probably no more than a graphic way of describing the wildness and waywardness of the winds (Merry on 12.290; see [[theos]]).
