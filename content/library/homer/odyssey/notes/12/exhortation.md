---
summary: Odysseus goes through the ship “with soothing words, standing beside each man: / ‘Friends, we are not without knowledge of evils at all’” (12.207–208), and bids the rowers row and the helmsman steer clear of the smoke (12.213–221).
refs: [12.206-221]
---

The parts of the speech are those of the exhortation: the address “Friends,” the reasons, and the orders. The reason is the men's own past, the escape from the Cyclops “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind” (12.209–212); the orders open with “But now come, let us all do as I say” (12.213), the Iliad's verse of the councils with νῦν for ἀλλʼ (*Iliad* 2.139, 9.26, 18.297). Merry sets beside it Aeneas' speech to his men after the storm, which imitates the opening and the hope of remembering (*Aeneid* 1.198, 1.203), though Homer's τῶνδε means the dangers ahead and Virgil's *haec* the sufferings already borne (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.208, 12.212; see the translation's note on 12.212). Of Scylla the speech says nothing (12.223–225; see [[soothing-words]], [[lay-it-to-heart]] and [[retelling]]).
