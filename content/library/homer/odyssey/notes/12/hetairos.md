---
summary: The book of the companions' end. They bury Elpenor, hear Circe's oracles from Odysseus, stop their ears and row past the Sirens, lose six of their strongest to Scylla, carry the landing on Thrinacia against Odysseus, swear an oath and break it, and drown in the wreck, “and the god took away their homecoming” (12.419). Circe's following wind is “a good companion” (12.149).
---

**Companions and leader.** The word runs through every scene. Odysseus sends companions for Elpenor's body (12.9), anoints the ears “of all my companions in turn” (12.177), and is freed by “my trusty companions” (12.199–200; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]] and [[trusty]]). Circe's warnings are counted in companions: it is better “to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (12.110; see [[pothe]]), and if the cattle are harmed he will come home “having lost all your companions” (12.141; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). The wind she sends is “a good companion” (ἐσθλὸν ἑταῖρον, 12.149 = 11.7).

**The break.** On Thrinacia the companions act for the first time against him. Eurylochus speaks for men “worn out by weariness and by sleep” (12.281), and “the other companions applauded” (12.294, 12.352; see [[eurylochus]]). Odysseus gets away from them to pray (ἤλυξα ἑταίρους, 12.335; see [[prayer]]), and Eurylochus leads “the way in evil counsel among my companions” (12.339; see [[boule]]). When the Sun asks the gods to punish the companions of Odysseus (12.378; see [[poine]]), the word becomes the name of the guilty: “and my companions fell out of the ship” (12.417), and the god took away their homecoming (12.419; see [[nostos]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
