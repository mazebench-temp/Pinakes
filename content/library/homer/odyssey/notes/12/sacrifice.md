summary: The companions sacrifice the Sun's own cattle (12.343–365): Eurylochus proposes it, and they perform the rite in the verses of the Iliad's sacrifices, with oak leaves for the barley and water for the wine. The smell of the fat wakes Odysseus to his ruin (12.369).
# "ῥέξομεν ἀθανάτοισι" (12.344), "αἰθομένοις ἱεροῖσιν" (12.362), only here; the entry's forms claim the rest.
grc: ["ῥέξομεν ἀθανάτοισι", "αἰθομένοις ἱεροῖσιν"]
en: [burning offerings]
---

**The proposal.** “But come, let us drive off the best of the cattle of the Sun / and sacrifice to the immortals, who hold the wide sky” (12.343–344): Eurylochus turns the slaughter of the god's herd into an offering, and the sacrifice, as always, implies a meal to be enjoyed by the worshipers (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.344; see [[all-deaths-are-hateful]] and [[feast]]). He adds a vow of a temple for later (12.345–347; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).

**The rite.** They drive off the cattle that graze near the ship, stand around them and pray, and then the verses of the Iliad's sacrifices follow, as at Chryse and in Agamemnon's tent: “And when they had prayed and slaughtered and flayed them, / and cut out the thigh-bones and wrapped them in fat, / making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them” (12.359–361; *Iliad* 1.458–461, 2.421–424; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). The first verse joins two of the Iliad's into one, leaving out the barley grains scattered after the prayer and the victims' heads drawn back (*Iliad* 1.458–459). The thigh-pieces burn, they taste the entrails, and “they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits” (12.364–365, as *Iliad* 1.464–465; see [[spits]]). Nestor's sacrifice at Pylos was told in the same verses (3.447–462).

**What is missing.** The poem marks two gaps in the rite. In the Iliad the men stand around the ox and take up the barley grains (*Iliad* 2.410); here they strip tender leaves from a high-leafed oak, “for they had no white barley on board the well-benched ship” (12.357–358; Merry on 12.356, comparing Nestor's barley, 3.441–447; see [[barley]]). And “they had no wine to pour over the burning offerings, / but poured libations with water as they roasted all the entrails” (12.362–363; see [[libation]] and [[wine]]). The provisions are gone (12.329), and the sacrifice is made from what the island offers: the god's own cattle, leaves and water.

**The answer.** Odysseus, waking, smells it from the shore: “then the sweet fragrance of the fat came round me” (12.369). ἀμφήλυθεν is used of a scent that floats around the nostrils, as of a sound around the ears at 6.122 (Merry on 12.369). The Sun learns of it from Lampetie and demands vengeance (12.374–383; see [[helios]] and [[lampetie]]), and the gods show signs over the meat itself, which lows on the spits, “both roast and raw” (12.394–396; see [[teras]]). A sacrifice of stolen victims is no gift: the gods do not accept it, and the companions feast for six days on what they have taken (12.397–398; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
