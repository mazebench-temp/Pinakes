---
summary: As the ship passes the Sirens, when Odysseus, bound to the mast, nods to his men to set him free, Perimedes and Eurylochus stand up and bind him with more bonds (12.195–196).
# Περιμήδης (12.195) and the English "Perimedes" are claimed by the entry; perimedes, the father of Schedius in the
# Iliad, excepts the line.
---

“And at once Perimedes and Eurylochus stood up / and bound me with more bonds and pressed me tighter” (12.195–196; see [[eurylochus]], [[odysseus]] and [[mast]]). The Sirens are singing, and Odysseus bids his companions set him free, “nodding with my brows” (12.192–194), since their ears are stopped with wax (see [[wax]]); the two who stand up from the rowing do what he had told them before the song began, in Circe's words: “And if I beg you and bid you set me free, / then you must press me with still more bonds” (12.163–164, after 12.53–54; see [[circe]]). Aristophanes athetized Circe's two verses, the scholion asking why she bids them bind again a man already bound, and a scholion reports that Odysseus' two verses are obelized too (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical notes on 12.53–54 and 12.163–164; see the translation's note on 12.53–54).

The poem names him only here and at the pit of the dead, both times beside Eurylochus (11.23), the two most important of the companions, as Merry calls them (Merry on 11.23). He is not named again, and with the rest of the crew he is lost when the thunderbolt strikes the ship (12.415–419; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
