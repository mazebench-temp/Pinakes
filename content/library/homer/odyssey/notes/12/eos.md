summary: Dawn has her house and her dancing floors on Aeaea, beside the risings of the Sun (12.3–4). She brings two mornings there, the morning of Elpenor's burial (12.7–8) and the morning of the departure, when “Dawn of the golden throne came” at Circe's last word (12.142), and a third on Thrinacia after the night of the storm (12.316). After that the book counts its time without her: a month of the South Wind, six days of feasting, nine days on the sea.

**The house of Dawn.** The ship comes back from Oceanus “to the island of Aeaea, where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4; see [[aeaea]], [[early-born]], [[house]] and [[helios]]). W. W. Merry renders χοροί “dancing lawns”, “for Eos must be supposed to have a train of nymphs, symbolising perhaps the quick-glancing rays of light” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.4; see [[nymphs]]). Völcker, he reports, would escape the difficulty of an Aeaea in the east by taking Eos here as a goddess rather than the physical dawn, but that leaves “the risings of the Sun” unexplained; Merry's own answer is that the far east and the far west meet in Homer's world (on 12.3; see the Book 12 note on [[helios]] and the translation's note on 12.1–4). The nymphs have dancing floors too, in the cave on Thrinacia where the ship is anchored (12.318; see the Book 12 note on [[nymphs]]).

**Three dawns.** On the night of the return the men sleep on the shore, “There we fell asleep and waited for brilliant Dawn. / And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / then I sent some of my companions to the house of Circe” (12.7–9; see [[dawn-line]], [[brilliant]] and [[rosy-fingered]]): the first verse is that of the night on the island of goats (9.151; see the Book 9 note), the second the verse of the Odyssey's mornings (2.1 and often). It is the day of Elpenor's burial and of the feast with Circe, who bids them sail “when dawn appears” (12.24; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[circe]]). Circe's counsel fills the night, and the next morning comes as she ends it: “So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (12.142; see [[golden-throned]]), the verse that ended her instructions for the journey to the dead (10.541; see the Book 10 note). The third dawn follows the storm that Zeus raises on Thrinacia in the third part of the night: “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / we anchored the ship, dragging it into a hollow cave” (12.316–317; see [[zeus]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Eurylochus had promised that they would put out again “at dawn” (12.293), and they do not.

**Time without Dawn.** The rest of the tale is counted in days and nights, and Dawn is not named again: the South Wind blows “for a whole month” (12.325; see [[notus]]), the companions feast “For six days” until Zeus adds “the seventh day” (12.397–399), Odysseus is carried “All night long” on the keel and mast and comes to Charybdis “with the rising sun” (12.429–430; see [[helios]]), and “for nine days” he is borne along, until “on the tenth night” the gods bring him to Ogygia (12.447–448; see [[ogygia]]).
