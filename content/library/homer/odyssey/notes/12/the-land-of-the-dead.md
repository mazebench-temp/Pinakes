---
summary: The voyage to the dead is behind the book, and the dead are on its edges: the ship comes back out of the stream of Oceanus to the light of Aeaea (12.1–4), Circe greets men who have gone down alive into the house of Hades (12.21–22), Elpenor is buried as his soul asked (12.8–15), and the Sun threatens to go down to Hades and shine among the dead (12.383).
refs: [12.21-22, 12.383]
---

**Out of the dark.** The ship leaves the stream of Oceanus and comes to Aeaea, “where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4; see [[aeaea]] and [[eos]]). The ancient commentators took the verses as a contrast with Hades, a passage out of the darkness into the light, though in Book 10 Aeaea lay toward the west (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.3; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.1–4). The first act on shore is the burial that Elpenor's soul begged for (12.8–15; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**Twice dying.** Circe's greeting makes the visit a death: “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22; see [[thanatos]] and [[circe]]). Of all mortals only these have seen the dead and come back, and they must die again; Virgil's Sibyl says the same to Aeneas (*Aeneid* 6.134–135; see [[virgil]]).

**The Sun among the dead.** The last mention of Hades is a threat to the order of the world. If the companions do not pay for his cattle, the Sun says, “I will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.383; see [[helios]] and [[hades]]). The land of the dead is the place without sun. The Cimmerians at its edge are never seen by the sun, “neither when he climbs toward the starry sky, / nor when he turns back again from the sky toward the earth, / but deadly night is spread over those wretched mortals” (11.17–19; see [[cimmerians]]), and the Sun describes his delight in his cattle in the same terms, “as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.380–381). Zeus answers by keeping the Sun among the immortals and mortal men, and promising to destroy the ship himself (12.385–388; see [[zeus]]). φαείνω is a subjunctive, Merry thinks, though the Scholiast took it as a present standing for the future (on 12.383; see the translation's note on 12.383, 12.388).
