summary: The first of the two ways that Circe sets before Odysseus beyond the Sirens: the overhanging rocks that the gods call the Wanderers, where the great wave roars and the storms of fire sweep away planks and bodies, which not even the doves of Zeus pass unharmed and no ship has escaped but the Argo. Odysseus takes the other way; in Merry's reading the smoke and the great wave that he steers away from as he leaves the Sirens are theirs (12.55–72, 12.201–221).

**Two ways.** Beyond the Sirens Circe will no longer tell Odysseus “which of the two ways shall be yours, but you yourself / must plan it in your heart; but I will tell you of both” (12.57–58). The first way is the Wanderers:

{{quote:d3b36c30-a315-5a0a-8919-5554a0838e6b}}

The rocks are ἐπηρεφέες, “overhanging” (12.59; see [[overhanging]]), and the wave of Amphitrite “roars” against them, ῥοχθεῖ (12.60; see [[amphitrite]]). Their name is the gods' (12.61; see the general article). τῇ μέν (12.62) and τῇ δέ (12.66) are not set against each other: the second only goes on with the first, from the birds to the ships (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.55). Not even the doves that carry ambrosia to Zeus all pass, for the sheer rock, λὶς πέτρη, always takes one away (12.62–65; see [[doves]]); the same words are said of Scylla's crag, “for the rock is sheer” (12.79). Only the danger of passing by is told, Merry notes, nothing of rocks closing on the birds, though ἀφαιρεῖται λὶς πέτρη, “the sheer rock … takes one away”, may be a mysterious hint of the danger that later legend develops (on 12.63). Of the ships, φύγεν, “has escaped”, states a general fact, like παρέρχεται, as the mood of ἵκηται and the tense of φορέουσι show (Merry on 12.66); φορέουσι, “sweep away”, belongs to κύματα, “the waves”, and by a slight zeugma to πυρὸς … θύελλαι, “the storms of … fire”, as storm winds snatch away Telemachus in Penelope's complaint (4.727; Merry on 12.68; see the translation's key-word rows for πίνακες νεῶν and πυρὸς ὀλοοῖο θύελλαι).

**The Argo.** The rule has one exception: “Only one seafaring ship has sailed past there, / the Argo, in the thoughts of all, sailing from Aeetes”, and Hera brought her past “since Jason was dear to her” (12.69–72; see [[the-argo]], [[jason]], [[hera]] and [[aeetes]]). Merry thinks the four verses a later addition (on 12.70; see the translation's note on 12.69–72).

**The way not taken.** Odysseus does not tell how he chose between the two ways. As the ship leaves the Sirens' island he sees “smoke and a great wave” and hears “a roar” (12.202), and he tells the helmsman to “Keep the ship away from this smoke and wave, / and make for the crag, lest without your noticing / she rush off there and you throw us into evil” (12.219–221). Merry takes the smoke for the storms of deadly fire of the Wanderers (12.68), which by day would show only as smoke, and κεῖσε, “there”, set against the crag, for the direction of the Wanderers; the subject of ἐξορμήσασα is the ship (Merry on 12.202, 12.219, 12.221; see the translation's note on 12.219–221 and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). On this reading the ship passes near the Wanderers before it enters the strait. Some modern commentators, Merry reports, took the smoke for spray, which he rejects (on 12.202; see [[charybdis]]).
