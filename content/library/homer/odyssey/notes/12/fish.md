summary: Scylla fishes from her cave for dolphins and dogfish and the greater sea beasts (12.95–97); she lifts six of Odysseus' companions as an angler on a jutting rock flings a gasping fish ashore (12.251–255); and on Thrinacia the starving companions hunt fish and birds with curved hooks, beside the cattle they have sworn not to touch (12.329–332).

# Book 24 note.

**Scylla fishes.** Circe tells how Scylla, sunk to her middle in her cave, holds her heads out of it, “and there she fishes, questing round the crag, / for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster” (12.95–97; see [[scylla]], [[dolphins]] and [[sea-beasts]]). ἰχθυάᾳ is the verb of Menelaus' hungry men on Pharos (ἰχθυάασκον, 4.368; see the Book 4 note), here said of a monster whose prey is the larger creatures of the sea. Polybius, as Strabo reports, recognized in the verses the swordfish hunt of Scyllaeum on the strait (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.15–16; see the Book 12 note on [[dolphins]]).

**The angler.** When Scylla seizes six companions from the ship, Odysseus sees them lifted up toward the rocks, gasping, and the likeness is of a man fishing:

{{quote:a38c5860-0417-5e19-a0b1-1dc8f30f515a}}

ἰχθύσι τοῖς ὀλίγοισι, “for the little fishes”, are the small fry that an angler takes with a rod (see [[the-fisherman]]). W. W. Merry takes ῥάβδος as a fishing rod, not a spear, since a spearman would not throw in bait or go after little fish; the line is not named here, but it is in the Iliad's fisherman, “with line and gleaming bronze” (*Iliad* 16.408), and the horn on it, by Aristarchus' explanation, was a small tube of horn that kept the fish from biting through the line above the hook (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.251; see [[the-lead-weight]]). δόλον, “a snare”, is the bait itself: the fish is tricked. ἀσπαίροντα, “gasping”, is the fish as it comes up, each in turn, as the aorist of custom ἔρριψε, “flings”, suggests (Merry on 12.254), and the participle passes from the fish to the men in the next verse, ἀσπαίροντες (12.255). The six are the catch, and Scylla's crag is the jutting rock.

**Hunger.** On Thrinacia the South Wind blows for a month, and when the provisions are spent the companions roam the island “in quest of game, as they had to, / fish and birds, whatever came to their hands, / with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies” (12.330–332; see [[birds]] and [[gaster]]). The last verse is Pharos' (4.369), and its curved hooks, ἀγκίστροισιν, are named in Homer only in these two places. The fishing marks the extremity of their need, as it did for Menelaus' men (see the Book 4 note), and it is not enough: while Odysseus is away praying, Eurylochus argues that of all deaths “to die of hunger and meet one's fate is the most pitiable” (12.342; see [[eurylochus]]), and they kill the Sun's cattle.
