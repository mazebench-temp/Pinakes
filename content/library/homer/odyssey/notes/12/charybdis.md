summary: Circe describes Charybdis under the wild fig tree on the lower crag, sucking down the black water three times a day, and bids Odysseus keep close to Scylla rather than be caught by her. In the strait the companions watch her seethe up like a cauldron and show the sand at the bottom of her gulf; after the wreck the South Wind carries Odysseus back to her, and he clings to the fig tree until she gives back his mast and keel (12.101–113, 12.234–244, 12.426–444).

**The lower crag.** Opposite Scylla's crag is the other:

{{quote:68de0fcf-a73c-5028-a303-4d7a445582c6}}

πλησίον, “near one another”, stands with εἰσί understood, and the gap between the crags is measured by the bowshot (12.102); the punctuation is Nicanor's, and some wrote πλησίοι (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.102 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 12.101–102). τῷ δʼ ὑπό is “beneath this”, the fig tree (Merry on 12.104; see [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]]). ἀναρροιβδεῖ and ἀναροιβδεῖ, with the double and the single ρ, are both required by the meter (see the translation's note on 12.104–105).

**Three times a day.** Merry sees in the number an intended connection between the ebb and flow of the tide and the whirlpool's engulfing and disgorging. Since ebb and flow come only twice a day, τρίς has been felt as a difficulty. Merry answers that we are still in the land of marvel, and that the completeness of the number three would naturally suggest itself to the poet for the whirlpool's full day's work; that in narrow straits the ebb and flow do not follow the tide outside, as the frequent changes in the Euripus show; and he reports Heller's table, which Buchholz cites, by which τρίς describes the normal changes of the sea in a day and a night exactly (on 12.105). Virgil and Ovid keep the number, though with nothing that answers to ἐπʼ ἤματι, “a day” (Merry on 12.105). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, thought the true number δίς, “twice”, and the τρίς an error of the copyist or of the information (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.16; Merry's critical note on 12.105; see the general article and [[polybius]]).

**The counsel.** “May you not be there when she sucks it down; / for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil” (12.106–107; see [[earth-shaker]] and [[poseidon]]). So Odysseus must draw close to Scylla's crag and lose six men rather than all (12.108–110; see [[scylla]]). He asks “whether I might somehow escape from deadly Charybdis” (12.113; see [[deadly]]): the prepositions of ὑπεκπροφύγοιμι each count, “secretly escape and get forward” (Merry on 12.113). Circe does not answer for Charybdis, only for Scylla (12.116–126).

**In the strait.** The “smoke and a great wave” that the ship meets after the Sirens (12.202) are not the spray of Charybdis, Merry argues, since Homer's word for spray is ἄχνη (12.238), but the storms of fire of the Wanderers (on 12.202; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). Charybdis herself is seen in the strait:

{{quote:83a653c2-292e-51f1-a3eb-43c8e78b69d6}}

ἔνθεν μὲν Σκύλλη needs ἦν, since the verb ἀνερροίβδησε belongs only to Charybdis (Merry on 12.235). Merry draws the scene: when the maelstrom discharged its waters they came boiling and bursting up from below with a roar like thunder and amid clouds of spray; when the sea was sucked in, one might look down into the whirling gulf as into a monstrous funnel, and see the sea floor far below between its liquid sides; he compares Tibullus' Charybdis, rising from the depths or laying the sea bare (on 12.237). She seethes “like a cauldron on a great fire” (12.237), as the Iliad's river boils (*Iliad* 21.362; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]). ἀναμορμύρεσκε is preferred to ἀνεμορμύρεσκε, since the iterative aorists seldom take the augment (Merry's critical note on 12.238). The sand at the bottom is “dark”, ψάμμῳ κυανέη, not yellow, because it lies in the depths (Merry on 12.243; see the translation's note on 12.234–245), and “pale fear took hold of them” (12.243; see [[pale-fear]]). “We looked toward her, fearing destruction” (12.244), and in that moment Scylla took six of the companions (12.245–259; see [[scylla]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). Then “we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla” (12.260–261).

**Back to Charybdis.** After the thunderbolt Odysseus rides the keel and the mast, lashed together with the backstay (12.420–425; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). “Then the West Wind ceased raging with its storm, / and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (12.426–428; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]). ὄφρα follows on ἦλθε, the words between being parenthetical, and ἀναμετρήσαιμι Χάρυβδιν is a compressed phrase, “retrace my course to Charybdis” (Merry on 12.428; see the translation's note on 12.428). For Χάρυβδιν, Apollonius the Sophist has θάλασσαν, which Nauck thought right, though Plato and Plutarch quote the verse with Χάρυβδιν (Merry's critical note on 12.428); Plato quotes it of his own third voyage to Sicily (*Letters* 7.345e; see [[plato]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). At sunrise Odysseus comes “to Scylla's crag and to dread Charybdis” (12.429–430), just as she is sucking down the sea:

{{quote:d7a85426-58f3-587b-8298-59150f127c7b}}

“And I held on unceasingly, until she should vomit up again / the mast and keel; and as I yearned for them they came, / late” (12.437–439), at the hour when a judge rises from the assembly for his supper (12.439–441; see [[like-a-bat]] and [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]). The scholia report that the verses 430–441 were doubted in many copies, since Odysseus reaches Charybdis at sunrise but the timbers come up only toward evening, against what Circe says of the hours of her ebb and flow (Merry on 12.439–441 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 12.430–441). Then, “at that hour the timbers came to light out of Charybdis” (12.441), and he drops beside them into the water and rows away with his hands (12.442–444).
