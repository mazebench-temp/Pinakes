---
summary: The South Wind is named four times. Eurylochus fears it, with the stormy West Wind, as the wind that most of all shatters ships (12.289); it blows without ceasing for a month and keeps the ship on Thrinacia, with no wind but the East beside it (12.325–326); and after the wreck it comes quickly, “bringing sorrows to my heart”, and carries Odysseus back to Charybdis (12.427–428).
# Νότου (12.289) is claimed by the entry, with Νότος (12.325, 12.326, 12.427).
---

**The wind that shatters ships.** Eurylochus asks how anyone could escape “if suddenly a storm of wind should come, / of the South Wind or the stormy West Wind, which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods?” (12.288–290; see [[eurylochus]], [[zephyrus]] and [[the-winds]]). W. W. Merry calls the South Wind the stormy rain-wind of Homer, which often wraps the mountains in mist (*Iliad* 3.10; see the general article), and in the Iliad's simile of the Argives' shout it drives the wave against a high shore (*Iliad* 2.394–396; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]). Its epithet ἀργεστής (*Iliad* 11.306) Merry refers to the foam into which it lashes the water, like a “white squall”, though others render it “swift” or “sky-clearing”, like Horace's *albus Notus* (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.290).

**A month of the South Wind.** After the storm of the first night on Thrinacia, “for a whole month the South Wind blew without ceasing, nor did any other / of the winds arise then, except the East Wind and the South Wind” (12.325–326; see [[eurus]], [[thrinacia]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The two winds go together, as in the Iliad's similes of the waves of the Icarian sea and of the forest in the glens (*Iliad* 2.145, 16.765) and in Poseidon's storm on the raft (5.295; see the Book 5 note). While they blow the ship cannot leave, the food runs out, and hunger drives the companions to the cattle (12.327–353). Merry, comparing this month and the South Wind that afterward carries Odysseus back to Charybdis in a night, would place Thrinacia to the northwest of Scylla's rock and not far from it (on 12.127).

**Back to Charybdis.** On the wreck of the keel and mast, “Then the West Wind ceased raging with its storm, / and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (12.426–428; see [[charybdis]]). ὄφρα depends on ἦλθε, with the words between in parenthesis: “The South came swooping down upon me, so as to make me retrace my course to Charybdis” (Merry on 12.428). Apollonius the Sophist read θάλασσαν, “the sea”, for Χάρυβδιν, as Nauck preferred, but the *Seventh Letter* and Plutarch have Χάρυβδιν (Merry's critical note on 12.428; see the translation's note on 12.428): the writer of the *Seventh Letter*, handed down among Plato's works, sailing to Sicily for the third time, quoted the verse of himself, “for the third time to the strait by Scylla, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (*Seventh Letter* 345e; see [[plato]]). All night Odysseus is borne along, and at sunrise he comes to Scylla's crag and Charybdis (12.429–430).
