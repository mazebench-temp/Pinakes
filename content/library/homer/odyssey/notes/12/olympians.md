summary: The gods as a body name the Wanderers in their own language (12.61), and Eurylochus fears the winds that shatter ships “against the will of the sovereign gods” (12.290). On Thrinacia Odysseus prays “to all the gods who hold Olympus”, and they pour sleep on his eyelids (12.337–338); waking, he cries out to “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever” (12.371), and the Sun calls on them in the same verse (12.377). The gods show portents over the slaughtered cattle (12.394–396), and at the end they bring Odysseus to Ogygia (12.448).

**The gods' name.** Of the first of the two ways Circe says, “On the one side are overhanging rocks, and against them / roars the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite; / the Wanderers, as the blessed gods call them” (12.59–61; see [[the-wanderers]], [[blessed-gods]] and [[amphitrite]]). The gods have names of their own for things, as for the plant moly (10.305), and in the Iliad for Briareus, the bird chalcis and the river Xanthus, whom men call by other names (*Iliad* 1.403–404, 14.290–291, 20.74; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]); here, as with moly, the poem gives only the gods' name. On Πλαγκταί, “the Wanderers”, see the translation's note on 12.61.

**The sovereign gods.** Eurylochus asks how anyone could escape “if suddenly a storm of wind should come, / of the South Wind or the stormy West Wind, which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods?” (12.288–290; see [[the-winds]] and [[eurylochus]]). W. W. Merry notes that this is the only place where the gods together are called ἄνακτες, “sovereigns”, though ἄναξ is given singly to Zeus, Apollo, Hermes, Poseidon and others; the phrase recalls Zeus' ὑπὲρ μόρον, “against fate”, in the first council of the gods (1.34–35), but is probably no more than a graphic expression for the wildness and waywardness of the winds (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.290); Zenodotus wrote φίλων ἀέκητι ἑταίρων, “against the will of our dear companions” (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 12.290). Later Eurylochus reckons with the gods as a body again: if the Sun is angry and wishes to destroy the ship, “and the other gods follow his lead” (12.349; see [[helios]]).

**Prayer and sleep.** When the food is gone, “then I went away up the island, so that I might pray / to the gods, in case one of them might show me a way to go home” (12.333–334), and having washed his hands in a sheltered place, “and prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus; / and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids” (12.337–338; see [[prayer]], [[homes-on-olympus]], [[hypnos]] and [[sweet-sleep]]). Merry thinks that Odysseus withdraws because he was more likely to hold communion with the gods alone, as Eidothea met Menelaus as he went “alone, apart from my companions” (4.367), and because the confidence between him and his men was broken, so that he had misgivings about their loyalty which he could not utter to the gods in their presence (on 12.333). It was in his sleep that the companions had brought trouble upon him before, by opening the bag of Aeolus (10.31), and γλυκύν, “sweet”, stands in contrast with the evil counsel that follows (on 12.338). When he wakes and smells the fat, “And groaning I called out loudly to the immortal gods: / ‘Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever, / truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep’” (12.370–372; see [[zeus]] and [[ate]]). The manuscripts read μετʼ ἀθανάτοισι, “among the immortals”, where the text prints Bekker's conjecture μέγʼ, “loudly”; if the manuscripts are right, Merry says, the words are a standing formula not to be pressed, since Odysseus was not in the presence of the gods, unless we suppose them gathered, though not visibly, around the sacrifice (Merry on 12.370 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 12.369–370).

**Among the immortals.** The Sun hears the news, “And at once he spoke among the immortals, angry at heart” (12.376), and he calls on the gods in the verse of Odysseus' cry, as Athena did at the council of Book 5 and Hephaestus when he called the gods to see Ares and Aphrodite (12.377; 5.7, 8.306; see the Book 8 note). Zeus answers him alone, and bids him keep giving light “among the immortals / and to mortal men” (12.385–386; see [[helios]]). The gods as a body act once more over the dead cattle: “And at once the gods showed signs to them: / the hides crawled, and the meat on the spits lowed, / both roast and raw, and there was a sound as of cattle” (12.394–396; see [[teras]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). And when Odysseus has drifted nine days on the keel and mast, “the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia” (12.448; see [[calypso]] and [[ogygia]]), the verse of his story to Arete (7.254).
