summary: Calypso closes Odysseus' tale. She is his source for what the Sun and Zeus said in heaven, “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390); and after the wreck and nine days on the sea the gods bring him to Ogygia, “where Calypso / lives, she of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice, / who loved me and looked after me” (12.448–450). There the tale stops, since he told the rest the day before.

**The source.** When he has told of the Sun's complaint and Zeus' answer, Odysseus says how he knows what was said among the gods: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[hermes]], [[helios]], [[zeus]] and [[lovely-haired]]). ἠύκομος, “of the lovely hair”, is her epithet when the Phaeacians' bath reminds him of her care, “since he left the house of Calypso of the lovely hair” (8.452; see the Book 8 note). A scholion says that Odysseus lies here, since Calypso had not yet seen Hermes; W. W. Merry refers to his note on Book 5, where the scholia explain that she knew Hermes when he came, not because she had seen him before but by a divine power (5.77–80), and answers that the falsehood rather rests with Calypso (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 12.389–390). Eustathius takes the verses as the poet's own answer to anyone who might ask how Odysseus could tell what passed between the Sun and Zeus, at which he was not present; Merry thinks such a solution very suspicious, as pointing to too punctilious a literary criticism for the period (Merry on 12.389; see the translation's note on 12.389–390). The verses fall within the passage that Aristarchus probably rejected, 12.374–390 (see the Book 12 note on [[helios]]).

**Ogygia.** After the wreck and the second passage of Charybdis, “From there for nine days I was borne along, and on the tenth night / the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso / lives, she of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice, / who loved me and looked after me. But why do I tell you this tale?” (12.447–450; see [[ogygia]], [[with-lovely-tresses]], [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]). The verses follow his story to Arete closely (7.253–255; see the Book 7 note), where she “took me in / and loved me with care and cared for me, and said / that she would make me immortal and ageless all my days” (7.255–257); here the third verse ends with the titles that Circe has in the book, δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα (12.150; see [[circe]]). ἐκόμει, “looked after”, is the verb of the care that the old Sicilian woman gives Laertes on his farm (24.212, 24.390). The tale breaks off at Ogygia: “For I already told it to you yesterday in your house, / to you and to your strong wife; and it is hateful to me / to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told” (12.451–453; see [[alcinous]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Odysseus' first answer to Arete had begun at Ogygia, with Calypso (7.244–266; Merry on 12.451), and the seven years with her are the story of Book 5 (see the Book 5 note).
