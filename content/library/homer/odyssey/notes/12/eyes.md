---
summary: Watching from the prow for Scylla, Odysseus glances all around until his eyes grow weary (12.232–233); her seizure of six companions is “the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes” (12.258).
---

**Watching.** “But I could not catch sight of her anywhere, and my eyes grew weary / as I glanced all around toward the misty rock” (12.232–233; see [[scylla]], [[deck]] and [[misty]]). The clause in parataxis means “though my eyes were wearied out as I kept gazing”, W. W. Merry explains (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.232). While he looks for Scylla, the companions look toward Charybdis (12.244), and she takes six of them.

**Seen with his own eyes.** “That was the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (12.258–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). The superlative stands first, as in Agamemnon's “the most pitiable voice I heard” among the dead (11.421) and Bellerophon's fiercest battle (*Iliad* 6.185; Merry on 12.258). What he did not see he reports at second hand: the Sun's complaint and Zeus' answer he learned from Calypso, who had it from Hermes (12.389–390; see [[calypso]]).
