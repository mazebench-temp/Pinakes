---
summary: Fear in the strait. Circe fears that Scylla will catch Odysseus if he lingers to fight (12.122); the oars fly from the companions' hands in terror at the smoke and the roar (12.203); Odysseus keeps Scylla secret lest they stop rowing in fear (12.224); and at the sight of Charybdis “pale fear took hold of them” while they look on her “fearing destruction” (12.243–244).
---

**Circe's fear.** “I fear that she will rush at you again and reach you / with as many heads, and seize as many men” (δείδω, 12.122–123): the goddess fears for Odysseus, who has asked how he might fight Scylla, and tells him to row past with all his force and call on Scylla's mother (12.112–126; see [[scylla]] and [[alke]]).

**The companions' fear.** In the strait fear is what Odysseus must manage. At the smoke, the great wave and the roar, “in their terror the oars flew from their hands” (τῶν δʼ ἄρα δεισάντων, 12.203), τῶν being demonstrative, with the oars left hanging in their loops (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.203; see [[oars]]). He rallies them with the memory of the Cyclops (12.208–212; see [[not-without-knowledge-of-evils]]) and says nothing of Scylla, “lest my companions in fear should stop / rowing, and huddle themselves together inside the ship” (12.224–225; see [[silence]]). Before Charybdis the fear is visible: “and pale fear took hold of them” (τοὺς δὲ χλωρὸν δέος ᾕρει, 12.243), the half-verse of the Iliad's night of Zeus' thunder (*Iliad* 7.479) and, in the first person, of Odysseus' fear at the coming and going of the dead (11.43, 11.633; see [[pale-fear]]). “We looked toward her, fearing destruction” (12.244), and while they look, Scylla takes six of them from the other side (12.245–246; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).
