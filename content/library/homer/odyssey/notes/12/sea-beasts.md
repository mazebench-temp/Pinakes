# Unnamed: the dolphins and dogfish of 12.96, lesser κήτεα beside the "greater" one (μεῖζον … κῆτος, 12.96-97).
refs: [12.96]
---

**The greater beast.** δελφῖνάς τε κύνας τε, καὶ εἴ ποθι μεῖζον ἕλῃσι / κῆτος, ἃ μυρία βόσκει ἀγάστονος Ἀμφιτρίτη: “for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster, of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers” (12.96–97; see [[scylla]], [[amphitrite]] and the translation's row κύνες (of the sea); κῆτος). μεῖζον, “a greater”, counts the dolphins and the dogfish as κήτεα themselves, lesser beasts of the same kind (see [[dolphins]] and [[dogs]]). The singular κῆτος is followed by the plural relative ἅ, “of which”, the kind standing for its many members, and W. W. Merry compares the same turn at 4.176–177, μίαν πόλιν … αἵ, and at 5.438, κύματος … τά (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.97). The verse answers Odysseus' fear off Scheria, “or a god may even send some great sea monster against me / out of the sea, such as famous Amphitrite breeds in great numbers” (5.421–422; see the Book 5 note): there the sea beast threatens the swimmer, here it is the prey of the monster. Amphitrite has her epithet of this verse, ἀγάστονος, “loud-moaning”, found only here (see [[loud-moaning]]).

**The fishing of the strait.** Polybius, as Strabo reports him, explained the verses by the fisheries of Scyllaeum: the tunny that run in shoals along Italy are kept by the currents from crossing to Sicily and fall among the larger creatures, “such as dolphins and dogfish and other beasts of the κῆτος kind”, οἷον δελφίνων καὶ κυνῶν καὶ ἄλλων κητωδῶν, while the swordfish grow fat by hunting the tunny (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.15; see the Book 12 note on [[dolphins]]). Polybius' κητώδη are Homer's κήτεα, the great creatures of the sea of whatever kind.
