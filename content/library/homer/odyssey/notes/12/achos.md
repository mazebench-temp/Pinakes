---
summary: The book's grief: the companions bury Elpenor “grieving” (12.12); twice Odysseus speaks to his companions “grieving at heart”, before the Sirens and at the island of the Sun (12.153, 12.270); and the six men Scylla lifts from the ship call on him by name “then for the last time, grieving at heart” (12.250).
---

**Odysseus' grief.** The verse “Then I spoke among my companions, grieving at heart” (δὴ τότʼ ἐγὼν ἑτάροισι μετηύδων ἀχνύμενος κῆρ) introduces his two speeches of warning, when he tells them Circe's oracles after leaving Aeaea and when they hear the lowing cattle of the Sun (12.153 = 12.270; see [[ker-heart]] and the Odyssey translation's table of formulas). Both times the grief is foreknowledge: he knows what Circe and Teiresias have said, and what it will cost (see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[thesphaton]]).

**The companions' grief.** They bury Elpenor “grieving and shedding big tears” (12.12; see [[tears]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The six men whom Scylla lifts from the ship cry out to Odysseus “on me by name, then for the last time, grieving at heart” (ἀχνύμενοι κῆρ, 12.249–250; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[pity]]): the half-verse of his own grief is theirs as they die. ἐμὲ … καλεῦντες ἐξονομακλήδην, “called on me by name”, go together (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.249).
