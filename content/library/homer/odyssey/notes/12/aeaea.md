summary: The ship comes back from the dead to Aeaea, which the poem now places “where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4). On its shore the companions bury Elpenor, and Circe feeds them and tells Odysseus the way; at dawn she goes back up the island and sends the ship off with a following wind (12.1–150). On Thrinacia Odysseus remembers her as “Circe of Aeaea” (12.268, 12.273), and the name is not heard again.
# The island without its name: "She then went away up the island" (12.143).
refs: [12.143]
passages:
  - 12.1-35 | The return to Aeaea
---

**The return.** The book opens with the ship's return: “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea, / and to the island of Aeaea, where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun, / there we came and ran the ship ashore on the sand” (12.1–5; see [[oceanus]], [[eos]] and [[helios]]). It is one sentence, whose main clause, νῆα μὲν ἔνθʼ ἐλθόντες, begins only at “there we came” (12.5; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.2).

**The house of Dawn.** There is, Merry says, a real contradiction in the place it gives to Circe's island. From Book 10 we gather that Odysseus sailed northwest from the island of Aeolus to the Laestrygonians, and from there, keeping the same course, came to Aeaea, farther still to the north and west (see [[aeolia]] and [[telepylus]]); here the island seems to be placed in the extreme east, at the sunrise, and in the most definite language. The ancient commentators evaded the difficulty rather than explained it: the scholia (B) read the verses as a contrast with Hades, as if Odysseus meant that out of Hades they had passed into the realm of light, and Voss, Nitzsch and Klausen took the same view. Grotefend placed Aeaea in the east, a view held in later times but not, Merry says, found in Homer; Völcker took Dawn here for the goddess rather than the daybreak, which leaves “the risings of the Sun” unexplained. Merry's own answer is that Homer regards the extreme West and the extreme East as almost one point, as in the short nights of the Laestrygonians, where the scholia quote the verse of Aratus on the place where the settings and the risings mingle; Gladstone, whom he quotes, also held that the island is identified with the East, at a point where the darkness and the dawn draw near each other and the Sun rises not far from where he set (on 12.3; see the translation's note on 12.1–4). χοροί are Dawn's “dancing lawns”, since she must be supposed to have a train of nymphs, perhaps the quick-glancing rays of light; and the plural ἀντολαί, “risings”, alludes to the sun's rising at different points of the horizon as the seasons change (Merry on 12.4; see [[dance]]).

**Elpenor's mound.** The rest of the ship's stay is laid on the shore. At dawn Odysseus sends companions to the house of Circe for the body of Elpenor, and “where the headland jutted out farthest / we buried him” (12.9–12; see [[elpenor]], [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[shore]]); they burn him with his armor, heap up a tomb, drag a pillar onto it, and fix his oar on the top, as his soul had asked (12.13–15, 11.72–78; see [[tomb]] and [[oars]]).

**Circe on the shore.** Circe knows that they have come back from Hades and comes down with her handmaids, bringing bread, meat and wine (12.16–19; see [[circe]] and [[handmaids]]). They feast all day; at nightfall the others lie down beside the stern-cables of the ship, and she takes Odysseus by the hand apart from them, seats him, and questions him about each thing (12.29–35; see [[stern-cables]]). They were not in her house, Merry thinks, since she goes back to it after the talk, but probably in some grotto or bower (on 12.33). At dawn “She then went away up the island” (12.143): ἀνὰ νῆσον, “up the island”, away from the shore where the scene has been laid until now (Merry on 12.143). As the ship sails, Circe sends behind it “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion” (12.148–150; see [[the-fair-wind]]).

**Circe of Aeaea.** On Thrinacia the island's name comes back once more, as Circe's. Hearing the cattle of the Sun, Odysseus remembers “the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again / to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (12.266–269), and repeats it to his companions (12.272–274; see [[thrinacia]] and [[teiresias]]). Κίρκης τʼ Αἰαίης answers Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, “the wily one of Aeaea”, in the preface to the tale (9.32; see the Book 9 note). The singular ἐπέτελλε, “charged”, with ἔφασκεν, “she said”, at 12.275, is the reading of the best manuscripts, adopted by Ameis and La Roche (Merry's critical note on 12.268, 12.273; see the translation's note on 12.265–275).
