---
summary: The anger of the Sun: Eurylochus foresees it, “if, angry over his straight-horned cattle, / he wishes to destroy our ship” (12.348–349), and when Lampetie brings the news the Sun speaks among the immortals “angry at heart” (12.376).
---

Eurylochus plans for the god's anger before the deed: they will build him a temple at home, “But if, angry over his straight-horned cattle, / he wishes to destroy our ship, and the other gods follow his lead”, he would rather drown at once (χολωσάμενός, 12.348–351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). ὀρθοκραιράων, “straight-horned”, is shortened from ὀρθοκεραιράων, and does not contradict ἕλικες, “with curving horns”, a few verses later, unless that means “crumpled” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.348; 12.355; see [[cattle]]). The anger comes as he foresaw. “And at once he spoke among the immortals, angry at heart” (χωόμενος κῆρ, 12.376; see [[helios]] and [[ker-heart]]), and asks Zeus to punish the companions, threatening to shine among the dead (12.377–383; see [[poine]]). The two verbs of anger, χολόομαι and χώομαι, frame the deed: the companions' guess and the god's fact.
