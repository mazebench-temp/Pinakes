---
summary: At the sacrifice of the Sun's cattle the companions cut out the thigh-bones and wrap them in fat, in the verses of the Iliad's sacrifice at Chryse, and burn them with water poured for wine (12.359–364).
---

Eurylochus' counsel is to “drive off the best of the cattle of the Sun / and sacrifice to the immortals, who hold the wide sky” (12.343–344; see [[eurylochus]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]): a sacrifice implies a meal for the worshippers, as W. W. Merry notes (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.344). The rite follows the Iliad's pattern: “And when they had prayed and slaughtered and flayed them, / and cut out the thigh-bones and wrapped them in fat, / making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them” (12.359–361; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]), where 360–361 are the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse (*Iliad* 1.460–461). But they have no barley and no wine, “but poured libations with water as they roasted all the entrails. / But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails” (12.363–364; see [[barley]], [[wine]] and [[libation]]), they cut up the rest for the spits (12.365; see [[spits]]). The god's portion is burned for the gods from cattle that belong to a god, and the Sun asks Zeus to punish the men who killed them (12.377–383; see [[helios]]). Zeus had taken no heed of the thigh-pieces Odysseus burned for him after the escape from the Cyclops (9.551–555; see the Book 9 note).
