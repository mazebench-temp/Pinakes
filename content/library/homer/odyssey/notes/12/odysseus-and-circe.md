---
summary: The last part of the story. Circe greets the men back from the dead and feasts them for a day (12.16–31), takes Odysseus apart for the night and tells him the dangers to come in the order of the voyage (12.32–141), and at dawn goes away up the island and sends the wind (12.142–152). The rest of the book recalls her at each danger: her oracles, her command, her warning and her food (12.155, 12.226, 12.268, 12.302).

**The welcome.** Circe knows that the men have come back from Hades, and comes quickly while they are burying Elpenor, ἐντυναμένη, “having made herself ready”, as Hera made herself ready for Zeus (12.16–18; *Iliad* 14.162; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.18; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). Her handmaids bring bread, meat and αἴθοπα οἶνον ἐρυθρόν, “sparkling red wine”, where the color word settles the sense of αἴθοψ as “bright” (12.18–19; Merry on 12.19; see [[handmaids]] and [[wine]]). She greets them: “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22; see [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). σχέτλιοι, “hard men”, is the word she will use again of Odysseus when he asks to fight Scylla (12.116), and that Eurylochus will throw at him on Thrinacia (12.279). Then the welcome of the tenth book, in its own verses: “But come, eat food and drink wine” (12.23 = 10.460), and the men's “proud spirit was persuaded” (12.28 = 10.466; see [[our-proud-spirit-was-persuaded]]). They feast all day on untold meat and sweet wine until the sun goes down, in the verses of the year they spent with her (12.29–31; 10.468, 10.476–478; see [[sunset-lines]]). She promises to show them the way and point out each thing, so that they may not suffer “through some grievous mischief, / either on sea or on land” (12.25–27; κακορραφίη, Merry on 12.26; see the translation's key-word row for κακορραφίη).

**The night.** The others lie down to sleep beside the stern-cables, “but she took me by the hand, away from my dear companions, / and seated me, and reclined near me, and questioned me about each thing; / and I told her all of it fair and right” (12.32–35). They were not in her house, for she goes back to it afterward (12.143), but probably in some grotto or bower; and προσέλεκτο means “reclined at my feet” rather than “lay by me”, since there is no mention of a bed, as there was in the tenth book (Merry on 12.33–34, comparing 10.334; see the translation's note on 12.33–34). The scholia's explanation, “spoke to me”, is impossible, since the word belongs to the stem λεχ-, not λεγ- (Merry on 12.34).

**The instructions.** “All these things have been brought to an end” (12.37): ταῦτα takes in the whole journey to Hades, and the god who “himself will remind you” (12.38) is no particular god, neither Poseidon nor the Sun, but some kindly power (Merry on 12.37–38; see the translation's note on 12.38). The speech follows the order of the voyage in three parts: the Sirens and the remedy against them (12.39–54; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]); the two ways, the Wanderers and the crags of Scylla and Charybdis, with her counsel to take the strait and lose six men rather than all, broken by Odysseus' question and her rebuke (12.55–126; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]); and Thrinacia, the herds and their nymphs, and the condition of the homecoming in Teiresias' verses (12.127–141; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). She leaves the choice of ways to him, though she plainly means to turn him from the Wanderers (12.56–58; Merry on 12.55). Of Thrinacia she says nothing of how to pass it, only what will happen if the cattle are harmed.

**The wind.** “So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (12.142), the verse that ended her instructions for the journey to the dead (10.541). She goes away “up the island”, away from the shore (12.143; Merry on 12.143). The men board, and she sends behind the ship “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion, / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (12.148–150), the verses with which the ship left her island for the house of Hades (11.6–8; see [[circe]], [[dread-goddess]], [[with-human-voice]] and [[the-fair-wind]]). The verse in which the companions row (12.147) is wanting in several manuscripts and has been bracketed by nearly all editors since Wolf, since there was no need of oars with a following wind (Merry's critical note on 12.147; see the translation's note on 12.147). She does not appear again in the poem.

**Her word remembered.** Odysseus tells the companions “the oracles that Circe told me” about the Sirens (12.154–164). In the strait he lets “the grievous command of Circe / slip from my mind” and arms himself (12.226–227). On Thrinacia “there fell upon my heart the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again / to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (12.266–269, again 12.272–274); the singular verbs that make the charge hers alone are the reading of the best manuscripts (Merry's critical note on 12.268, 12.273; see the translation's note on 12.265–275 and [[aeaea]]). In her speech as he has reported it, Circe, like the seer, did not forbid the island, but set a condition on the cattle (11.110, 12.137). The companions swear to eat “the food that immortal Circe gave” (12.302), and live on her bread and wine until it is gone (12.327–329; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
