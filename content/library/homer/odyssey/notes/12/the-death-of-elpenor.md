On the first morning after the return from the dead the companions bring Elpenor's body from Circe's house and give it the burial that his soul asked for: the pyre, the armor, the tears, the mound on the headland with a pillar on it, and the oar fixed on the top.

**The return.** The soul of Elpenor knew that Odysseus, going back from the house of Hades, would put in at the island of Aeaea (11.69–70), and the twelfth book begins there. The ship is run ashore on the sand, the men sleep on the beach, and at dawn the first thing Odysseus does is to send companions to Circe's house “to bring the body of Elpenor, who had died” (12.5–10; see [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[elpenor]]). The body has lain in her hall, unwept and unburied, through the whole journey to the dead (11.52–54; see [[unwept-and-unburied]]).

**The burial.** The story ends in eight verses:

{{quote:3bbd0cf7-c67f-5482-87b4-82a98bcb5885}}

Each thing the soul asked for is done, in its order: “burn me with my armor”, “heap up a mound for me on the shore of the gray sea”, “and fix on the tomb the oar” (11.74–77). The tears it asked for come first, for it had begged not to be left “unwept” (11.72), and the mound stands where the headland juts out farthest, so that men to come may hear of him (11.76; see [[cremation]], [[tomb]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Odysseus tells it in the first person plural, θάπτομεν, χεύαντες, πήξαμεν: the whole crew buries its youngest companion (10.552). For Merry's notes on the headland, the pillar and the oar, and Zenodotus' different ending of the last verse, see [[elpenor]].

**The three tellings.** Each of the three books that tell the story gives one part: the fall from Circe's roof, told by Odysseus (10.551–560); the soul's account of it and its plea, told by the soul at the pit (11.51–83); and the burial (12.8–15). The oar on the mound is the last word of the story, and of Elpenor in the poem; Teiresias, in the scene that follows the soul's plea, bids Odysseus fix an oar of his own in the ground, in the same words, far inland (11.121–129; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[oars]]). While they are still busy with these rites, Circe comes with her handmaids, bringing bread, meat and wine (12.16–19).
