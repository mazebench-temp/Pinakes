**The angler.** ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἐπὶ προβόλῳ ἁλιεὺς περιμήκεϊ ῥάβδῳ / ἰχθύσι τοῖς ὀλίγοισι δόλον κατὰ εἴδατα βάλλων / ἐς πόντον προΐησι βοὸς κέρας ἀγραύλοιο (12.251–253; see [[fish]] and the translation's row πρόβολος; ῥάβδος (of a fisherman); εἴδατα; βοὸς κέρας ἀγραύλοιο). He fishes ἐπὶ προβόλῳ, “on a jutting rock”, as the Iliad's fisherman sits πέτρῃ ἔπι προβλῆτι, “on a jutting rock” (*Iliad* 16.407); ἁλιεύς, “fisherman”, is named in Homer only here and in the last book (24.419). W. W. Merry takes ῥάβδος as a fishing rod, not, as some suppose, a fishing spear, which would not be used for small fish, nor would a man with a spear throw in food as bait; the line is not named, but the Iliad names it, “with line and gleaming bronze” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.251; *Iliad* 16.408). περιμήκεϊ ῥάβδῳ, “with a very long rod”, are the words of Circe's wand at 10.293 (see [[wand]], which excepts this line). εἴδατα, “bait”, is Aristarchus' reading; Callistratus read δείλατα (Merry's critical note on 12.252). The horn of an ox of the fields, βοὸς κέρας ἀγραύλοιο, was explained by Aristarchus as a little pipe of horn set on the line, so that the fish should not bite through it: it protected the line where it joined the hook, “and served the same purpose as the ‘gimp’ on a trolling-line”, Merry says, and the hook itself was of bronze (on 12.251; see [[cattle]]). The Iliad has the same horn, with the words reversed, on the lead weight to which Iris is likened (*Iliad* 24.80–82; see [[the-lead-weight]]).

**Gasping.** ἀσπαίροντα δʼ ἔπειτα λαβὼν ἔρριψε θύραζε, / ὣς οἵ γʼ ἀσπαίροντες ἀείροντο προτὶ πέτρας: “and then catches one and flings it out, gasping, / so they were lifted, gasping, toward the rocks” (12.254–255). The fish is singular, ἀσπαίροντα, each fish as it comes up, as the aorist of custom ἔρριψε, “flings”, also suggests (Merry on 12.254). θύραζε, “out”, is out of the water, as when the Iliad's fisherman draws his fish out of the sea (*Iliad* 16.408). The participle passes from the fish to the men in the next verse: the six companions gasp like hooked fish as they are lifted up, and the rocks are the shore onto which the fish is flung.

**The likeness and the scene.** Odysseus sees it from the deck, where he had armed himself to fight: “I saw already their feet and their hands above / as they were lifted on high; and they lifted up their voices and called / on me by name, then for the last time, grieving at heart” (12.248–250; see [[called-him-by-name]]). After the simile Scylla devours them at her doors, “screaming, / stretching out their hands to me in grim combat”, and Odysseus calls it “the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (12.256–259; see [[scylla]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[grim-combat]]). Merry takes ἐν αἰνῇ δηιοτῆτι, “in grim combat”, as more graphic than a word for death or pain, since it implies an unavailing conflict with an overmastering power, “as we speak of ‘death-struggle’” (on 12.257). In the Iliad's simile the catch is one man, speared through the teeth by Patroclus (*Iliad* 16.401–410; see the entry); here it is six, and the angler is a monster.
