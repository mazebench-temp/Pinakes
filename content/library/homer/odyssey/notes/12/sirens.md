summary: The first of the dangers Circe foretells. The two Sirens sit in a meadow on their island, among the bones of rotting men, and bewitch with their clear song whoever comes near, so that he never comes home; when the ship reaches them in a windless calm, they call Odysseus by name and offer him the knowledge of all that was suffered at Troy and of all that comes to pass on the earth (12.39–54, 12.158–200).

**Circe's account.** The Sirens are the first thing Circe tells of the voyage:

{{quote:8e7ac848-f152-5dea-8136-2402328d3bbc}}

She tells what they do, not what they are. ὅτις σφεας εἰσαφίκηται, “whoever comes to them” (12.40), is read in several forms: the readings vary between ὅτε σφέας, ὅτι σφέας, ὅ τις σφέας and ὅς τις, and La Roche held ὅ τέ σφεας the best established (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 12.40). She does not say that they kill. The man who hears them never reaches his wife and children (12.42–43; see [[nostos]]), and about the singers lies “a great heap of bones / of rotting men” (12.45–46). θίς, the “heap”, is elsewhere in Homer the sandy shore, θῖνα θαλάσσης, “the shore of the sea” (12.367). They sit, ἥμεναι (12.45), and in the book they never leave their meadow: they sing to the ship as it passes.

**Two Sirens.** Circe speaks of them in the plural (12.39, 12.42, 12.44) until “the voice of the two Sirens” (ὄπʼ … Σειρήνοιιν, 12.52); the ship comes “to the island of the two Sirens” (νῆσον Σειρήνοιιν, 12.167); and they themselves sing of “the voice of us two” (νωιτέρην ὄπα, 12.185), though the narrative and their own ἡμέων, “our” (12.187), are plural. Merry concludes that Homer's Sirens are two, living on one island (on 12.39, 12.185; see the translation's note on 12.39, 12.52, 12.167, 12.185). The island is not described; the meadow is, and when Odysseus repeats Circe's words to his companions it is “the flowering meadow” (λειμῶνʼ ἀνθεμόεντα, 12.159; see [[flowering]]), with the epithet of the meadow of the Scamander where the Achaean army stood “numberless, as the leaves and flowers” (*Iliad* 2.467–468).

**The song.** The ship comes within call, and they sing:

{{quote:942650c5-b2f0-5e90-b429-e58644022ae1}}

They name what they know twice, ἴδμεν … ἴδμεν, “we know … we know” (12.189, 12.191): the war at Troy, the past that Odysseus shared, and “all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (see [[bountiful-earth]]). It is the knowledge the Iliad's poet asks of the Muses, who “are present, and know all things”, ἴστέ τε πάντα, while men “hear only the report, and know nothing” (*Iliad* 2.485–486; see [[muses]]). Merry calls it their superhuman knowledge of the past and the future (on 12.39). The song is λιγυρή, “clear” (12.183; see [[shrill]]), the voice μελίγηρυς, “honeyed” (12.187), and κάλλιμος, “beautiful” (12.192), and Odysseus' heart “wished to listen” (12.192–193). The Sirens speak to him alone: his companions, their ears anointed with wax, row on until “we could no longer / hear the voice of the Sirens or their song” (12.197–198). For the passage, the wax and the bonds, see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]].
