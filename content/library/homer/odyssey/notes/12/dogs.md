summary: The dog of Book 12 is a newborn puppy, the measure of Scylla's voice: she yelps terribly, but her voice is “only as loud as a newborn puppy's” (12.85–86). The κύνες that she fishes for are dogfish of the sea (12.96).
# σκύλακος (12.86), the puppy, found only here; σκύλαξ is the Odyssey's word (σκύλακας 9.289, the Book 9 note;
# σκυλάκεσσι 20.14). κύνας at 12.96 is the dogfish, excepted in the entry.

**A puppy's voice.** ἔνθα δʼ ἐνὶ Σκύλλη ναίει δεινὸν λελακυῖα. / τῆς ἦ τοι φωνὴ μὲν ὅση σκύλακος νεογιλῆς / γίγνεται, αὐτὴ δʼ αὖτε πέλωρ κακόν: “There within dwells Scylla, yelping terribly. / Her voice indeed is only as loud as a newborn puppy's, / but she herself is an evil monster” (12.85–87; see [[scylla]] and the translation's row λελακυῖα; σκύλαξ νεογιλής; πέλωρ κακόν). λελακυῖα, “yelping”, is the perfect participle of λάσκω, a verb of shrill cries. σκύλαξ is a young dog, a whelp (see the Book 9 note), and νεογιλής, “newborn”, found only here, is explained by Eustathius and the Etymologicum Magnum as νεογνός, “new-born”; the variants are νεογιλλῆς and νεογηλῆς (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.86). The scholia rejected 86–88, asking how a creature that yelps terribly could have the voice of a newborn puppy (Schol. H. Q., in Merry's critical note), and the same scholia try to explain the difficulty away by taking ὅση as οἵα, so that the likeness would be of the kind of sound and not of its size (Merry on 12.86). Merry keeps the lines: “There is an intentionally grotesque contrast between the vast size of Scylla, and the thin, shrill, cry she utters. Although it is a horrible shriek, yet the volume (ὅση) of sound is not greater than the yelp of a puppy (σκύλαξ)” (on 12.86; see the translation's note on 12.85–88).

**Scylla and the whelp.** The name and the word sound alike, Σκύλλη and σκύλαξ. Merry takes Σκύλλη as “properly the ‘render’ or ‘tearer’”, and thinks the connection with σκύλαξ probably later; but it may account, he says, for these lines, and for the dogs with which the Latin poets surrounded Scylla, her “caerulei canes” and “latrantia monstra” (on 12.85). Homer's Scylla has no dogs: she has twelve dangling feet and six heads on long necks, each with three rows of teeth (12.89–92).

**Dogfish.** Among her prey are κύνες, “dogfish”: “for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster” (12.96–97; see [[dolphins]] and [[sea-beasts]]). They are “dogs” of the sea. Polybius, as Strabo reports him, names κύνες with the dolphins among the larger creatures that prey on the tunny in the strait, and says that the γαλεῶται hunted at Scyllaeum are also called swordfish and κύνες (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.15; see the Book 12 note on [[dolphins]]). They are not dogs, and the entry excepts the line.
