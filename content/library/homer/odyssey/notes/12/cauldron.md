summary: When Charybdis vomits up the sea, she seethes up from her depths “like a cauldron on a great fire” (12.237–238).
# λέβης (12.237), the nominative, first met in the Odyssey here; it applies in the Odyssey only (the Iliad's λέβης,
# 21.362, is in its Book 21 note).
grc: [λέβης]
en: [cauldron]
---

“Truly, whenever she vomited it up, like a cauldron on a great fire / she would seethe up, all of her, churning” (12.237–238; see [[charybdis]], [[fire]] and [[the-boiling-cauldron]]). The cauldron of the simile is the one set over the fire to heat water, as in the Iliad's simile of the river, which boils within as a cauldron does on a great fire when Hephaestus burns its streams (*Iliad* 21.361–365), and λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ shares λέβης and πυρὶ πολλῷ with that verse (*Iliad* 21.362; see the translation's row λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ). W. W. Merry pictures the whirlpool: when it discharged its waters they seemed to come boiling and bursting up from below with a roar like thunder and amid clouds of spray, and when it sucked the sea in, one might look down into the whirling gulf as into a monstrous funnel and see the sea floor far below (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.237).
