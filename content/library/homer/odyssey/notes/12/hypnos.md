---
summary: Sleep decides the book. Back on Aeaea the men sleep on the shore and beside the ship (12.7, 12.32); a god lulls the waves to sleep before the Sirens (12.169); Eurylochus pleads for men worn out by weariness and sleep (12.281); and on Thrinacia the gods pour sweet sleep on Odysseus while the companions kill the cattle, so that he wakes to cry that they lulled him “in pitiless sleep” (12.338, 12.366–372).
---

**On Aeaea and at sea.** The book begins with sleep on the shore, “There we fell asleep and waited for brilliant Dawn” (12.7 = 9.151), and on the night of Circe's instructions “the others lay down to sleep beside the stern-cables of the ship”, while she takes Odysseus apart (12.32–33; see [[circe]] and [[sunset-lines]]). Before the Sirens the wind drops, “and a god lulled the waves to sleep” (κοίμησε δὲ κύματα δαίμων, 12.169; see [[daimon]] and [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]): the verb of putting a man to sleep is used of the sea.

**Eurylochus' plea.** At the island of the Sun Eurylochus calls Odysseus a man of iron, who will not let his companions, “worn out by weariness and by sleep, / set foot on land” (καμάτῳ ἁδηκότας ἠδὲ καὶ ὕπνῳ, 12.281–282; see [[eurylochus]]). The combination is found only here and in the Iliad, where Agamemnon fears that the guards may be asleep at their posts (*Iliad* 10.98; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.281), and Merry renders “overdone with weariness and sleepiness too”. That night, after they have eaten and wept for the six men Scylla took, “sweet sleep came upon them” (12.311; see [[tears]]).

**The fatal sleep.** On Thrinacia Odysseus goes up the island to pray, “and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids” (12.338; see [[prayer]] and [[sweet-sleep]]). It was in his sleep, too, that the companions had opened the bag of the winds within sight of Ithaca, and the sweet sleep of this verse stands against the “evil counsel” of the next (Merry on 12.338; 10.31; see [[skin-of-the-winds]] and [[boule]]). He wakes only when the deed is done, “And then sweet sleep rushed from my eyelids” (12.366), smells the fat, and cries out to the gods: “truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep” (κοιμήσατε νηλέι ὕπνῳ, 12.372; see [[ate]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). The sleep that was sweet when it came is pitiless when he wakes; in Book 10 he had called it “cruel sleep” (10.68–69; see [[hard]]).
