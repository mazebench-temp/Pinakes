**The two ways.** After the Sirens Circe will no longer tell Odysseus “from beginning to end / which of the two ways shall be yours, but you yourself / must plan it in your heart; but I will tell you of both” (12.56–58). ἀμφοτέρωθεν is divided into ἔνθεν μέν, the Wanderers (12.59), and οἱ δὲ δύω σκόπελοι, the two crags (12.73); though she professes not to decide, she plainly means to dissuade him from the Wanderers (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.55 and 12.73). No ship has passed them but the Argo, and not a word is said of the rocks closing upon her: the waves would have dashed her against them (12.69–72; Merry on 12.71; see [[the-wanderers]], [[the-argo]] and [[jason]]). Merry thinks the four verses of the Argo a later addition (on 12.70; see the translation's note on 12.69–72).

**The two crags.** The first is too high and sheer to climb, its cave too high for an arrow to reach from a ship (12.73–84; Merry on 12.83); in it lives Scylla, whose six heads each take a man from every ship (12.85–100; see [[scylla]]). The second is lower, an arrow's shot away, with the fig tree and Charybdis beneath it (12.101–107; see [[charybdis]]). Circe's counsel follows: “But be sure to draw close to Scylla's crag, and quickly / row your ship past” (12.108–109). πεπλημένος is from πελάζω, “draw near” (Merry on 12.108). The reason is a reckoning: “it is far better / to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (12.109–110).

**Odysseus asks to fight.** He interrupts with a question: could he escape Charybdis and ward off Scylla when she would harm his companions? (12.112–114). Two manuscripts have in their margin ἀτυζόμενος, “bewildered”, for ἀμειβόμενος, “answering”, a reading that Buttmann thought very good, since Odysseus breaks into Circe's speech (12.111; Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 12.111). ὑπεκπροφύγοιμι gathers three prepositions, “secretly escape and get forward” (Merry on 12.113). Circe answers “Hard man”, σχέτλιε, which here means something like “irrepressible”: at the first chance his mind is on war again (12.116–117; Merry on 12.116). Scylla “is not mortal, but an immortal evil”, where κακόν is almost “fiend” (12.118; Merry on 12.118), and “the strongest course is to flee from her” (12.120), an intentional oxymoron, “the bravest course is to run away” (Merry on 12.120; see the translation's key-word row for φυγέειν κάρτιστον). If he lingers arming himself by the rock, she will take as many men again; he is to row past with all his force and call on Crataeis, her mother (12.121–126; see [[crataeis]]). The scholia athetized these last three verses, objecting, it seems, that they make Scylla able to pursue her victims, not fixed to her rock (Merry's critical note on 12.124–126; see the translation's note on 12.124–126).

**The smoke and the roar.** As they leave the Sirens' island Odysseus sees smoke and a great wave and hears a roar (12.201–202). καπνός cannot be spray, for which Homer has ἄχνη: it takes up “the storms of deadly fire” of the Wanderers (12.68), which by day would show only as smoke, as Pindar says of Etna, streams of smoke by day and flame by night (Merry on 12.202). The oars fly from the men's hands and splash in the current; they hung in their leather loops at the tholes and dragged at the ship's side, and the ship stops (12.203–205; Merry on 12.203). Odysseus, who was at the stern, goes the length of the ship, standing beside each man in turn (12.206–207; Merry on 12.206), in the verse with which he roused them on Aeaea (10.173, 10.547).

**The speech.** “Friends, we are not without knowledge of evils at all” (12.208): he sets the danger beside the Cyclops' cave, from which they escaped by his prowess, his counsel and his mind (12.209–212; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Then the orders: the men are to keep striking the deep breakers with their oars, “if ever Zeus / will grant us to escape this destruction and get away” (12.213–216), and the helmsman to “Keep the ship away from this smoke and wave, / and make for the crag” (12.217–221). The helmsman is to give the Wanderers a wide berth and keep near the rocks, and κεῖσε, “there”, points to the Wanderers; Murray prints σκοπέλου, “the crag”, with a scholion, where most editors since Wolf and Merry read σκοπέλων, “the cliffs”, both rocks together (Merry's critical note and on 12.219–221; see the translation's note on 12.219–221 and [[steering-oar]]).

**The silence.** “But of Scylla I said nothing more, a vexation past remedy, / lest my companions in fear should stop / rowing, and huddle themselves together inside the ship” (12.223–225). He dared not tell them the danger, since he knew from Circe that he must lose at least six men, and he contented himself with the vague word about the crag (Merry on 12.223); πυκάζοιεν is sheltering by huddling together under the decks or benches (Merry on 12.225). He had told them only of the Sirens (12.154–164; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]).

**The arming.** “And then I let the grievous command of Circe / slip from my mind, since she had told me not to arm myself” (12.226–227): the command is “hard”, ἀλεγεινῆς, to a fighting man, who chafes at passive endurance, and λανθανόμην is “I let myself forget”, “disregarded” (Merry on 12.226–227; see the translation's note on 12.226–227). He puts on his armor, takes two long spears and goes onto the deck at the prow, where he expects her to appear first, “Scylla of the rock, who brought a bane on my companions” (12.228–231; see [[deck]] and [[spear]]): φέρε is simply “brought”, the narrator anticipating what is to come (Merry on 12.231). His eyes grow weary from gazing at the misty rock (12.232–233).

**The passage.** “We sailed up the narrow strait, lamenting” (12.234), with Scylla on one side and Charybdis on the other, who seethes up like a cauldron when she vomits the water and shows the dark sand of the sea floor when she sucks it down (12.235–243; Merry on 12.237; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]] and the translation's note on 12.234–245). “And pale fear took hold of them” (12.243). All eyes were on Charybdis; Odysseus, hearing a cry, turns to look at the ship and his companions, and sees their hands and feet above him as they are lifted (12.244–249; Merry on 12.247). They call on him by name, “then for the last time” (12.249–250), and Scylla lifts them as a fisherman on a jutting rock flings out his gasping catch (12.251–255; see [[the-fisherman]]). She devours them at her doors, screaming and stretching out their hands to him “in grim combat” (12.256–257), an unavailing struggle with an overmastering power, a death-struggle (Merry on 12.257). “That was the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (12.258–259; see the translation's key-word row for πόροι ἁλός).

**After.** “But when we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla” (12.260–261), the ship comes to Thrinacia, where at supper the companions remember and weep for those whom Scylla seized from the hollow ship and devoured (12.309–310; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Odysseus will see the two crags once more, alone on the keel, and Zeus will not let Scylla see him (12.426–446; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
