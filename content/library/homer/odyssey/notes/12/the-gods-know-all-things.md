---
summary: Two claims to know everything, and a test of one. The Sirens boast that they know all that the Argives and Trojans toiled through at Troy and all that comes to pass upon the earth (12.189–191); Odysseus calls the Sun the god “who sees all things and hears all things” (12.323), yet the Sun learns of the killing of his cattle from a messenger (12.374–375).
---

**The Sirens.** “For we know all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods, / and we know all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (12.189–191; see [[sirens]] and [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]). The boast is the knowledge of the Muses, to whom the poet of the Iliad prays “for you are goddesses, and are present, and know all things” (*Iliad* 2.485; see [[memory-and-the-muses]]), offered as a lure: the man who hears them “goes home delighted, and knowing more” (12.188), and their meadow is heaped with the bones of those who stayed to listen (12.45–46). W. W. Merry counts superhuman knowledge of the past and the future among the Sirens' peculiar marks (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.39).

**The Sun.** Odysseus warns the companions to keep away from the cattle of “a dread god, / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (12.322–323 = 11.109; see [[helios]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]). But when the cattle are killed, “swiftly to Hyperion the Sun came a messenger, / Lampetie of the long robes, to tell him that we had killed his cattle” (12.374–375; see [[lampetie]]). The ancient critics objected that these verses contradict Agamemnon's oath, “and you, Sun, who see all things and hear all things” (*Iliad* 3.277), since the god should have known for himself, and Aristarchus probably rejected the whole passage, 12.374–390 (Merry's critical note on 12.374–390; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.374–390). Aristotle defended it: the Sun sees everything, but not all at once, and being then in another part of his course he did not see what was done on Thrinacia; or Lampetie reports to him as sight reports to a man (*Homeric Problems* fr. 373 Gigon; see [[aristotle]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Odysseus himself knows what the gods said only at second hand, from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes (12.389–390; see [[truth-and-deception]]).
