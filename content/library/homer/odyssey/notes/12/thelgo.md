---
summary: Circe's first warning: the Sirens “bewitch / all men, whoever comes to them”, with their clear song, sitting in a meadow among the bones of the men they have held (12.39–46).
---

The verb comes twice in Circe's account of the Sirens, at its beginning and its turn: “First you will come to the Sirens, who bewitch / all men, whoever comes to them”, and the man who hears them never comes home, “but the Sirens bewitch him with their clear song, / sitting in a meadow” (12.39–40, 12.44–45; see [[sirens]] and [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]). The spell of the song is the spell of Circe's drugs in another form: what bewitches keeps a man from his homecoming, as the companions who ate her food forgot their native land (10.236; see [[nostos]] and [[drugs]]). The remedy is wax for the companions' ears and bonds for Odysseus, who alone hears the song and cannot act on it (12.47–54, 12.173–200; see [[mast]]). The song itself promises knowledge: the man who hears it “goes home delighted, and knowing more” (12.188), knowing all that the Argives and Trojans toiled through at Troy and all that comes to pass upon the earth (12.189–191; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]). Their island is the only place in the book where the word is used; the Phaeacians, held by a spell when Odysseus pauses and when he ends, are bewitched by his tale of it (11.333–334, 13.1–2; see the Book 11 note and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).
