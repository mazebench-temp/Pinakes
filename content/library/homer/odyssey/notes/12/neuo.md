summary: Bound to the mast while the Sirens sing, Odysseus bids his companions set him free “nodding with my brows”, since their ears are stopped with wax (12.193–194).
# "ὀφρύσι νευστάζων" (12.194), the frequentative νευστάζω; the participle alone is also the drooping head of a man
# in sorrow or drink (18.154, 18.240), which the phrase leaves out.
grc: ["ὀφρύσι νευστάζων"]
en: [nodding with my brows]
---

“So they spoke, sending out their beautiful voice; and my heart / wished to listen, and I bade my companions set me free, / nodding with my brows; but they bent forward and rowed” (12.192–194; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]] and [[brows]]). It was no use to speak, W. W. Merry notes, since the companions' ears were stopped with wax (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.194; see [[silence]]). As in the flight from the Cyclops, when he signed no with his brows to stop his men from weeping and nodded his head to bid them row (9.468, 9.490; see the Book 9 note), the order is given by a sign; but this time the men do not obey it, as Circe had told them not to (12.53–54, 12.163–164), and Perimedes and Eurylochus bind him tighter (12.195–196; see [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]). νευστάζω, a frequentative of νεύω, is a repeated nodding; in the Iliad Aeneas strides out against Achilles “tossing his heavy helmet” (*Iliad* 20.161–162).
