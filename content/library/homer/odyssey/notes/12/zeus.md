---
summary: Zeus ends the companions' story. Circe names him in her account of the Wanderers, past which the doves carry ambrosia to “father Zeus” (12.62–65); Odysseus bids his rowers pull “if ever Zeus / will grant us to escape this destruction” (12.215–216). On Thrinacia Zeus rouses the storm that keeps the ship a month (12.313–315), Odysseus cries out to him when he finds the cattle killed (12.371–373), and the Sun appeals to him; Zeus promises to strike the ship with a gleaming thunderbolt (12.377–388), adds the seventh day of the feast (12.399), and when the ship is out of sight of land sets a dark cloud over it and strikes it (12.405–417). At Charybdis “the father of men and gods” keeps Scylla from seeing Odysseus (12.445–446).

**Father Zeus and the doves.** At the Wanderers, Circe says, “not even winged things pass, not even the timid doves / that carry ambrosia to father Zeus, / but even of these the sheer rock always takes one away, / and the father sends in another to make up the number” (12.62–65; see [[doves]] and [[ambrosia]]). W. W. Merry denies any connection with the later story of the doves that fed Zeus when he was hidden in Crete from his father, which he cites from Athenaeus, and finds rather an allusion to the Pleiads, at whose rising the harvest begins, a fact that may be veiled under the story of the ambrosia (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.62; see the translation's note on 12.62–65): Hesiod bids the farmer begin the harvest when the Pleiads, the daughters of Atlas, rise (*Works and Days* 383–384; see [[pleiades]] and [[hesiod]]).

**If ever Zeus will grant.** When the smoke and the great wave come in sight beyond the Sirens' island, Odysseus orders the rowers: “You keep striking the deep breakers of the sea with your oars, / sitting at the rowing benches, if ever Zeus / will grant us to escape this destruction and get away” (12.214–216; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). The hope opens as Telemachus' threat to the suitors opens, “if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done” (1.379), and the last words are those of Agamemnon's prayer in the Iliad, “let us at least escape and get away” (*Iliad* 8.243; see the translation's formula row).

**The storm on Thrinacia.** After the oath and the supper on the shore, “when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over, / Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused a blustering wind against us / with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (12.312–315; see [[cloud-gatherer]], [[the-winds]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The last two verses are those of the storm that Zeus sent against the ships after Ismarus (9.68–69; see the Book 9 note), and the last of them ends Poseidon's storm on the raft (5.294). Merry renders “in the third watch of the night, and the stars had southed”, and divides the night, like the day, into three parts (on 12.312; see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]); ὦρσεν ἔπι is Aristarchus' reading, against ὦρσε δʼ ἐπί, and ζαῆν an Aeolic accusative (on 12.313 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 12.312–313). The storm becomes a month of the South Wind and the East Wind, and the ship cannot leave (12.325–326; see [[notus]] and [[eurus]]).

**Father Zeus and you other gods.** When Odysseus wakes and smells the fat, he cries out to the gods who put him to sleep: “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever, / truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep, / and my companions, staying behind, have devised a great deed” (12.371–373; see [[ate]], [[hypnos]] and [[olympians]]). The Sun, a few verses later, calls on the gods in the same verse (12.377), Athena's address at the council of Book 5 and Hephaestus' in Demodocus' song (5.7, 8.306; see the translation's formula row).

**The thunderbolt promised.** The Sun asks Zeus and the gods to punish the companions, or he will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead (12.377–383; see [[helios]]). “Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said: / ‘Sun, do you keep giving light among the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth; / and as for them, I will soon strike their swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt / and shatter it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea’” (12.384–388; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[wine-dark]]). The first verse is the Iliad's (*Iliad* 20.19). The promise is told as done in the same words by Calypso to Hermes and by Odysseus to Arete, “alone, when my swift ship, with a gleaming thunderbolt, / Zeus struck and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (7.249–250; 5.131–132; see [[calypso]]). τυτθά, “to pieces”, Zenodotus read τριχθά, “in three” (Merry's critical note on 12.388).

**The seventh day.** “For six days then my trusty companions / feasted, having driven off the best of the cattle of the Sun; / but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day, / then the wind ceased raging with its storm” (12.397–400; see [[cronus]] and [[son-of-cronus]]). ἐπὶ θῆκε is “added thereto”, the seventh to the tale of six (Merry on 12.399). Eumaeus counts the days of the voyage on which he was carried off as a child in the same verse (15.477), and in the false tale of Book 14 the stranger's companions feast for six days in the verse of 12.397 (14.249; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

**The thunderbolt.** “But when we were leaving the island, and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea, / then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud / above the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it” (12.403–406). The West Wind breaks the forestays and the falling mast kills the helmsman (12.407–414; see [[zephyrus]] and [[mast]]), “And at the same moment Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt at the ship; / and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus, / and was filled with sulfur, and my companions fell out of the ship” (12.415–417). ἄμυδις, “at the same moment”, may join the thunder to the wind and rain, or answer the καί that follows, “in the same moment that he thundered, he did even smite the ship” (Merry on 12.415); the sulfur goes with the thunderbolt, as in the Iliad, where Zeus, “Thundering terribly”, lets fly a gleaming thunderbolt in front of Diomedes' horses, and “a terrible flame of burning sulfur rose up” (*Iliad* 8.133–135; Merry on 12.417). The companions are carried on the waves like sea crows, “and the god took away their homecoming” (12.419; see [[like-sea-crows]] and [[nostos]]). The stranger of Book 14 tells his own shipwreck in the same verses, with Crete for the island he had left (14.301–309; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).

**The father of men and gods.** On the mast and keel Odysseus comes back to Charybdis, clings to the fig tree until the timbers rise, and rows away with his hands: “And the father of men and gods no longer let Scylla / see me; for then I would not have escaped sheer destruction” (12.445–446; see [[father-of-men-and-gods]] and [[scylla]]). The scholia rejected the two verses, since Scylla could not leave her cave. Merry brackets them, and suggests that the difficulty would be removed by taking ἐμέ as the subject of εἰσιδέειν: Zeus pitied Odysseus and did not let him set eyes on Scylla again. That would also explain how Zeus comes to be his protector from Scylla here, when Circe had bidden him in his extremity call on Crataeis (12.124; Merry's critical note and note on 12.445–446; see [[crataeis]] and the translation's note on 12.445–446). The English follows the scholia's construal, with Scylla seeing him.
