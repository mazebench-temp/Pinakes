---
summary: The verb in both its senses: no sailors “can boast” that they have fled past Scylla unharmed (12.98), and on Thrinacia Odysseus goes up the island to pray (12.334) and the companions pray over the stolen cattle (12.356, 12.359).
---

**Boast.** Circe's account of Scylla ends with a claim no one can make: “By her no sailors yet can boast that they have fled / unharmed with their ship” (εὐχετόωνται, 12.98–99; see [[scylla]]). εὔχομαι here is to declare before men, the sense of the Iliad's heroes who “claim” a birth or a victory; the claim would be a sailor's boast, and none has been able to make it, for with each head she snatches a man from the ship (12.99–100). Odysseus will not make it either: he passes, but six men are taken (12.245–250; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).

**Pray.** The same verb names the prayers of Thrinacia. Odysseus goes up the island “so that I might pray / to the gods” (εὐξαίμην, 12.333–334), and the companions, before they kill the cattle, “stood around them and prayed to the gods” (εὐχετόωντο, 12.356) and “prayed and slaughtered and flayed them” (εὔξαντο, 12.359; see [[prayer]] and [[sacrifice]]). His prayer is answered with sleep, theirs with portents (12.338, 12.394–396; see [[hypnos]] and [[teras]]).
