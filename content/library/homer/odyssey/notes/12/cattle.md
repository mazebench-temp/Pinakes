summary: The cattle of Book 12 are the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia, seven herds of fifty that neither breed nor die (12.127–136); their lowing, which Odysseus hears from the sea (12.265); the oath not to kill a cow or a sheep (12.299–301); the best of them, driven off and sacrificed with oak leaves for barley (12.343–365), whose hides crawled and whose meat lowed on the spits (12.395–396); and the ox horn of the angler's simile and the oxhide backstay of the wreck (12.253, 12.423).

**The herds of the Sun.** Circe tells Odysseus that on Thrinacia “in great numbers / graze the cattle of the Sun and his fat sheep, / seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, / fifty in each. And no young are born of them, / nor do they ever die” (12.127–131; see [[thrinacia]], [[helios]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[sheep]]). Their herders are goddesses, the Sun's daughters Phaethusa and Lampetie, whom their mother settled on Thrinacia “to dwell far off / and guard their father's sheep and his cattle with curving horns” (12.131–136; see [[phaethusa]], [[lampetie]] and [[with-curving-horns]]). The number was read as an allegory. Eustathius, quoted by W. W. Merry, reports that Aristotle took the herds, above all the herds of cattle, for the days of the twelve lunar months, three hundred and fifty, as many as seven herds of fifty, and that Homer therefore says they have neither young nor death, since the number of such days always stays the same (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.129; *Homeric Problems* fr. 398 Gigon, whose sources add that it was held accursed to kill a plowing ox, and that those who did so suffered at the hands of men or gods, as Odysseus' companions did). Merry would not follow Eustathius further, to the eating of the cows as a poetical way of saying that time was wasted; but Eustathius, he says, is right that the Greeks set apart whole herds of tame animals for the gods, which no one might touch, and he reports Gladstone's view that the cattle and sheep of the Sun-god reflect Egyptian usage, and adds that in the Vedic hymns the rays of the sun are spoken of as his “cows” (on 12.129; for the sacred sheep of the Sun in Herodotus see the Book 12 note on [[sheep]]).

**Lowing at nightfall.** When they come to the island, “there were the fine broad-browed cattle, / and many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun” (12.262–263; see [[broad-browed]] and [[fat-sheep]]). καλαὶ βόες, “fine cattle”, are feminine: the herds of the Sun are cows. Odysseus hears them before he lands, “the lowing of the cattle being stalled for the night / and the bleating of the sheep” (12.265–266), and the sound brings back to him the warnings of Teiresias and Circe (12.266–269; see [[teiresias]] and [[circe]]). μυκηθμός, “lowing”, is found once more in Homer, of the cows on the shield of Achilles: “and lowing they hurried out of the dung-yard to the pasture” (μυκηθμῷ, *Iliad* 18.575). The genitive μυκηθμοῦ and the accusative βληχήν, “bleating”, stand together after one verb; Merry reports Nitzsch's parallel from Euripides, τίνων γόων ἤκουσα καὶ στέρνων κτύπον, “what laments have I heard, and beating of breasts” (on 12.265–266; Euripides, *Suppliants* 87), and Bekker read μυκηθμόν, from Eustathius, to make the cases agree (Merry's critical note on 12.265).

**The oath and the hunger.** On the beach Odysseus makes his men swear: “if we find a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep, / that no one, in evil recklessness, / will kill a cow or a sheep” (12.299–301; see [[oath-formulas]] and [[atasthalie]]). βοῦν is a cow or an ox alike. While the bread and wine lasted, “they kept away from the cattle, longing for life” (12.327–328), λιλαιόμενοι βιότοιο, “vitae servandae studiosi”, as Merry takes it, since they still had food (on 12.328).

**The best of the herd.** Eurylochus' counsel is to drive off “the best of the cattle of the Sun” and sacrifice them, and to build the Sun a temple in Ithaca, or else, if the god is angry “over his straight-horned cattle”, to die at sea at a gulp (12.343–351; see [[eurylochus]] and [[straight-horned]]). ὀρθοκραιράων is shortened from ὀρθοκεραιράων, and Merry finds no contradiction with ἕλικες, “with curving horns”, in the next lines, unless ἕλικες is taken to mean “with crumpled horns” (on 12.348). The cattle were grazing near the ship, “the fine broad-browed cattle with curving horns” (12.355), and the companions “stood around them and prayed to the gods” (12.356). The verse is built like the Iliad's βοῦν δὲ περιστήσαντο καὶ οὐλοχύτας ἀνέλοντο, “They stood around the ox and took up the barley grains” (*Iliad* 2.410), and the manuscripts read περιστήσαντο here too, where the text prints Bekker's conjecture (see the translation's note on 12.351, 12.356). But here they have no barley to throw, and use the leaves of an oak instead, as they use water for wine (12.357–363; Merry on 12.356; see [[trees]], [[barley]] and [[sacrifice]]). Then the gods showed signs: “the hides crawled, and the meat on the spits lowed, / both roast and raw, and there was a sound as of cattle” (12.395–396; see [[teras]]). εἷρπον is “crawled on the ground” (Merry on 12.395), and the dead meat lows as the living cattle had lowed when Odysseus heard them from the sea, βοῶν δʼ ὣς γίγνετο φωνή. For six days the companions feasted on the best of the cattle (12.397–398). The Sun's complaint to Zeus and the gods is for his cattle, “in which I / always took delight as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.379–381).

**Ox horn and oxhide.** Two other oxen are in the book. The angler of Scylla's simile lets “the horn of an ox of the fields” down into the sea, βοὸς κέρας ἀγραύλοιο, a little tube of horn on the line above the hook as Aristarchus explained it (12.253; Merry on 12.251; see [[the-fisherman]] and [[the-lead-weight]]), and in the wreck the backstay that fell across the mast was “made of oxhide”, βοὸς ῥινοῖο τετευχώς, and with it Odysseus lashed the keel and the mast together (12.423–424; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The last of the ship is held together by the hide of an ox.
