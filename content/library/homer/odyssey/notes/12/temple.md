---
summary: Eurylochus, urging the companions to kill the Sun's cattle, vows that if they reach Ithaca they will at once build a rich temple to Hyperion the Sun and set many fine offerings in it (12.345–347).
---

“And if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land, / we will at once build a rich temple to Hyperion the Sun / and put in it many fine offerings” (12.345–347; see [[eurylochus]], [[hyperion]], [[helios]] and [[ithaca]]). Nitzsch remarked that this is the only vow of its kind in Homer, W. W. Merry reports (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.347); τεύξομεν is a future with κε, as Merry takes it, or a short-vowel aorist subjunctive (see the translation's note on 12.347). Chryses reminds Apollo that he has roofed a temple to his pleasure (*Iliad* 1.39); Eurylochus promises a temple in return for cattle taken without the god's leave. The ἀγάλματα, “offerings”, are things to delight a god, such as Aegisthus hung up for the gods, woven things and gold, after he had won Clytemnestra (3.273–275; see [[aegisthus]]). Eurylochus reckons with failure too: if the Sun, angry over his cattle, wishes to destroy the ship, he would rather drown at once than starve (12.348–351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]).
