summary: The last of Odysseus' twelve ships carries the end of the tale: from the stream of Oceanus back to Aeaea, past the Sirens and through the strait, where Scylla takes six men from it, to a cave on Thrinacia, and out to sea again, where Zeus' thunderbolt breaks it apart (12.1–7, 12.144–259, 12.305–317, 12.401–425). In the speeches it is what the dangers put at risk, “your ship and your companions” (12.140).

**Back to Aeaea.** The book opens with the ship: “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea”, “there we came and ran the ship ashore on the sand, / and we ourselves too stepped out where the sea breaks upon the shore” (12.1–6; see [[oceanus]] and [[aeaea]]), in the verses of the landings on the island of goats (9.150, 9.546–547). Circe comes down to the shore with her handmaids, bringing bread, meat and wine (12.16–19; see [[provisions]] and [[wine]]), and that night the companions sleep beside the stern-cables (12.32; see [[stern-cables]]). At dawn they go on board, loose the cables, sit down at the benches and row, and Circe sends “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion” (12.144–150, where 148–150 = 11.6–8; see [[rowing-benches]], [[oars]], [[sails]] and [[the-fair-wind]]): “At once, when we had busied ourselves with all the tackle throughout the ship, / we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her” (12.151–152, as 11.9–10; see [[steering-oar]]).

**The ship in Circe's warnings.** Every danger she names is a danger to the ship. At the Wanderers “no ship of men that comes there has ever yet escaped”, and only the Argo came through (12.66–72; see [[the-argo]]). From every ship that passes, Scylla takes a man for each head, “snatching him from the dark-prowed ship”, and “it is far better / to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (12.99–100, 12.109–110; see [[scylla]] and [[dark-prowed]]). If the companions harm the cattle of the Sun, “I foretell destruction for you, / for your ship and your companions” (12.139–140, = 11.112–113; see [[thrinacia]]). In the Sirens' song “no one has ever yet rowed past here in his black ship” without hearing them (12.186; see [[sirens]] and [[black-ships]]).

**Past the Sirens and through the strait.** Driven by a harmless wind, “the well-made ship quickly came / to the island of the two Sirens”; then the wind drops, and the companions furl the sails, stow them “in the hollow ship” and row (12.166–172; see [[well-made]] and [[hollow-ships]]). At the smoke and the roar the oars fall from their hands, “and the ship stopped there, / since they no longer plied the tapering oars with their hands” (12.204–205), and Odysseus goes “through the ship” to hearten them (12.206; see [[stern]]); he bids the helmsman keep the ship away from the smoke and the wave (12.219–220) and goes up on the deck at the prow to meet Scylla, who takes six companions “from the hollow ship” (12.229–230, 12.245; see [[deck]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).

**On Thrinacia.** Odysseus would “drive the black ship on past the island” (12.276), but Eurylochus fears the night, when come “harsh winds, the ruin of ships”, and the South Wind and the West Wind, “which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods” (12.286–290; see [[eurylochus]] and [[the-winds]]). They “moored the well-made ship in the hollow harbor / near sweet water” (12.305–306; see [[harbor]]), and after the storm of the night “we anchored the ship, dragging it into a hollow cave” (12.317; see [[nymphs]]), εἰσερύσαντες standing for εἰς σπέος ἐρύσαντες (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.317). There the provisions of the ship run out, and the ship is named again and again: Eurylochus fears that the Sun will wish “to destroy our ship” (12.349); they have “no white barley on board the well-benched ship” (12.358; see [[barley]] and [[well-benched]]); Odysseus smells the fat as he comes near “the curving ship” (12.368; see [[curving]]); and Zeus promises the Sun to “strike their swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt / and shatter it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (12.387–388; see [[provisions]], [[swift-ships]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**The ship destroyed.** “But when we were leaving the island, and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea, / then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud / above the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it. / And she ran on for no long time” (12.403–407; see [[zeus]]). The verses return, with those of the thunderbolt, in Odysseus' Cretan tale to Eumaeus (14.302–306). The West Wind breaks the forestays and the mast falls back, spilling the tackle “into the bilge” and killing the helmsman (12.408–414; see [[forestays]] and [[mast]]). ἄντλος is the hold, the hollow of the ship, which Attic writers used rather of the sea water that ran into it, as Elmsley explained it (Merry, Appendix I §11): the deck covered only the bow and the stern, and the waist between was open (Appendix I §5). Then the thunderbolt: “and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus, / and was filled with sulfur, and my companions fell out of the ship”, and they float about the black ship like sea crows (12.415–419; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[like-sea-crows]]). The surge looses her sides, τοῖχοι, from the keel (12.420–421; see [[keel]]). Odysseus' twelve ships (9.159) had been one since the harbor of the Laestrygonians (10.131–132), and with this ship the last of his companions are lost (see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
