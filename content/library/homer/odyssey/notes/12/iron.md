---
summary: Eurylochus, opposing Odysseus' order to row past Thrinacia, tells him, “truly you are made all of iron” (12.279–280).
---

“You are a hard man, Odysseus; your might is beyond measure, and your limbs / never grow weary; truly you are made all of iron, / you who do not let your companions, worn out by weariness and by sleep, / set foot on land” (12.279–282; see [[eurylochus]] and [[hard]]). W. W. Merry renders, “verily, everything about thee is made of iron”, and notes that the scholion explains σχέτλιος well as καρτερικός, “enduring” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.279–280; see the translation's row σιδήρεος). In the Iliad a heart of iron is one that does not yield: Hector finds it in Achilles, and Hecuba and Achilles in Priam (*Iliad* 22.357, 24.205, 24.521; see the entry); here the whole man is iron, and the reproach is that of tired men to a leader who does not tire. The others applaud, and Odysseus yields, knowing that a god is devising evil (12.294–297).
