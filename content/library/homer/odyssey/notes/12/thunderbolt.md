---
summary: Zeus promises the Sun to strike the companions' swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt and shatter it in the middle of the wine-dark sea, as Odysseus later heard from Calypso (12.385–390); when the ship has put out from Thrinacia he thunders and hurls the bolt, the ship shakes and is filled with sulfur, and the companions fall into the sea (12.415–419).
passages:
  - 12.415-419 | The thunderbolt strikes the ship
---

**The promise.** To the Sun's threat to go down to Hades and shine among the dead, Zeus answers: “Sun, do you keep giving light among the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth; / and as for them, I will soon strike their swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt / and shatter it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (12.385–388; see [[zeus]], [[helios]], [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]] and [[wine-dark]]), nearly the verses in which Calypso and Odysseus had told it (5.131–132, 7.249–250; see the Book 5 and Book 7 notes). τυτθά goes closely with κεάσαιμι, “shatter to pieces”, and βαλών with κεραυνῷ (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.388); Zenodotus read τριχθά, “in three”, for τυτθά (Merry's critical note on 12.388). Odysseus, who was not there, says how he knows: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[calypso]] and [[hermes]]).

**The stroke.** The ship has been out of sight of land for no long time when the West Wind breaks the mast; then:

{{quote:4a65fb39-9ebe-523d-b5fb-040d55a3ce9f}}

ἄμυδις is “along with” the wind and rain, or else it answers the καί that follows, “in the same moment that he thundered, he did even smite the ship” (Merry on 12.415). For the sulfur Merry compares the flame of burning sulfur that leaps up before Diomedes' horses and the oak struck down by Zeus (*Iliad* 8.133–135, 14.414–417; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]]), quotes Pliny that thunderbolts and lightning have the smell of sulfur, and connects θέειον through the root θυ with θύος and θύω (on 12.417). The companions are carried on the waves like sea crows around the black ship, “and the god took away their homecoming” (12.418–419; see [[like-sea-crows]] and [[nostos]]). The verses of the stroke return in Odysseus' Cretan tale to Eumaeus (14.305–306), where Zeus strikes the ship of the Cretan wanderer (see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
