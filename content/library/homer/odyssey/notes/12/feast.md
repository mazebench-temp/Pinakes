summary: The book's meals mark its turns: the day-long feast on Circe's food after Elpenor's burial (12.23–30); the supper Eurylochus demands on Thrinacia, made ready and eaten before the companions weep for the dead (12.283–311); the food and drink still in the ship (12.320); the six days' feast on the Sun's cattle (12.397–398); and the judge's supper that tells the hour at Charybdis (12.439).
# "βρῶσίς τε πόσις τε" (12.320; also 10.176), the nominative of the entry's phrase.
grc: ["βρῶσίς τε πόσις τε"]
---

**Circe's table.** Circe comes to the shore with bread, meat and wine, and bids the men “eat food and drink wine, / here all day long” before they sail (12.18–25); “So all day long, until the sun went down, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (12.29–30), the verses of the feasts after their landings in Books 9 and 10 (9.161–162, 10.183–184; see [[circe]] and [[sunset-lines]]).

**Supper on Thrinacia.** Eurylochus' argument for landing is a meal: on the island “we might make ready a pleasant supper”, and at night they should yield to the dark and make ready their supper beside the ship (12.282–293; see [[eurylochus]] and [[yield-to-night]]). Night, as Merry notes, suggests supper-time (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.291). They land and “skillfully made ready their supper”; “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food”, they weep for the men Scylla took (12.307–311; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]] and [[tears]]). In the assembly next morning Odysseus points to the ship's stores, “there is food and drink in the swift ship” (12.320), but a month of the South Wind eats them up (12.325–329; see [[fasting]] and [[provisions]]).

**The stolen feast.** The sacrifice of the cattle is also a meal, for a sacrifice implies a feast for the worshipers (Merry on 12.344; see [[sacrifice]]), and the companions keep it up for six days, “having driven off the best of the cattle of the Sun” (12.397–398), after the gods have shown them signs on the spits (12.394–396; see [[teras]]). Merry finds the epithet ἐρίηρες, “trusty”, merely an epic formula here, not ironical (on 12.397; see [[trusty]]). On the seventh day they put out to sea and drown (12.399–419). The book's last supper belongs to a simile: the judge who rises from the assembly “for his supper” marks the hour when Odysseus' timbers come up from Charybdis (12.439–441; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]] and [[assembly]]).
