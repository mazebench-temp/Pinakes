summary: The sheep of Book 12 are the fat sheep of the Sun, seven flocks of fifty that his daughters guard on Thrinacia with his cattle (12.127–136, 12.263); their bleating as they are penned for the night (12.266); and the flocks of Odysseus' oath, “a great flock of sheep” and “a sheep” that no one is to kill (12.299–301, 12.322). The companions kill only the cattle.

**The flocks of the Sun.** On Thrinacia “in great numbers / graze the cattle of the Sun and his fat sheep, / seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, / fifty in each” (12.127–130; see [[thrinacia]], [[helios]], [[cattle]] and [[fat-sheep]]): ἴφια μῆλα, “fat sheep”, the formula of Teiresias' warning (11.108), and οἰῶν πώεα καλά, “fine flocks of sheep” (see the Book 11 note). Like the cattle, they neither breed nor die, and the Sun's daughters guard “their father's sheep and his cattle with curving horns” (12.130–136; see [[phaethusa]] and [[lampetie]]). The flocks of a god are a known thing in Greek cult. Eustathius, as W. W. Merry quotes him, notes that the Greeks dedicated whole herds of tame animals to the gods, which no one might touch, and points to the sacred sheep of the Sun in Herodotus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.129, where the reference is printed 9.92): at Apollonia on the Ionian Gulf the sheep of the Sun grazed by day beside the river Chon and were kept by night in a cave far from the city, guarded in turn by the citizens of most wealth and birth, each for a year; when the guard Evenius fell asleep at his post, wolves got into the cave and killed some sixty of them, and after the Apolloniates had blinded him for it their flocks ceased to bear and their land to yield, until the oracles at Dodona and Delphi made them pay him amends (Herodotus 9.93–94; see [[herodotus]]). There too the god's flock comes to harm while its keeper sleeps.

**Bleating at nightfall.** From the ship Odysseus hears “the lowing of the cattle being stalled for the night / and the bleating of the sheep” (12.265–266). βληχή, “bleating”, is found only here in Homer; the bleating ewes of the Cyclops' cave and of the Iliad's simile have the verb μηκάομαι (μέμηκον, 9.439; μεμακυῖαι, *Iliad* 4.435). The genitive and the accusative after one verb, μυκηθμοῦ … βληχήν, are discussed in the Book 12 note on [[cattle]].

**The oath.** “if we find a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep, / that no one, in evil recklessness, / will kill a cow or a sheep” (12.299–301; see [[oath-formulas]]). The first verse is the Iliad's pair of herd and flock: ἠὲ βοῶν ἀγέλην ἢ πῶϋ μέγʼ οἰῶν is a herd and a flock thrown into confusion by two wild beasts “in the dead of black night, / coming on them suddenly when the herdsman is not there” (*Iliad* 15.323–325), and Nestor took a herd of cattle and a great flock of sheep as his spoil from Elis (*Iliad* 11.696). μῆλον, “a sheep”, is the singular of μῆλα, the flocks (see the Book 9 note). Odysseus names the herds again when he warns his men to keep away from them, “for these are the cattle and fat sheep of a dread god, / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (12.322–323; see [[who-sees-all-things]]).

**Spared.** In the event only the cattle die. Eurylochus' counsel is to drive off “the best of the cattle of the Sun” (12.343), the companions slaughter cows (12.353–365; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]), and the Sun's complaint to the gods is for his cattle alone (12.379–382). The sheep are not named again.
