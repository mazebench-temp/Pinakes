summary: The prophecy is heard again and comes true. Circe, who had sent Odysseus to the seer for the way home, gives the way herself and repeats the warning about the cattle in five of the seer's verses (12.25–26, 12.137–141); on Thrinacia Odysseus remembers “the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias”, and tells the companions “the prophecies of Teiresias” (12.266–275); and by the end of the book the ship and the companions are lost and Odysseus alone escapes, as the seer foretold (12.403–450).
# Unnamed: the way that Circe gives (12.25-26); the warning repeated (12.137-141); the prophecy remembered and told
# (12.266-276); the destruction of the ship and the companions (12.415-419).

**What Circe gives.** Circe had promised that the seer would tell Odysseus “the way and the measures of your path” (10.539), and he described the measures but not the way (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.136). Now she gives it herself: “I will show you the way and point out / each thing” (12.25–26), and her instructions run from the Sirens to Thrinacia (12.37–141; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]).

**The warning repeated.** Of Thrinacia Circe adds what the seer did not say, the number of the herds, that they neither breed nor die, and the nymphs who tend them (12.127–136; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Then she speaks the condition in his own five verses, from “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming” to “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see the translation's key-word rows for ἀσινής and τεκμαίρομαι). Of the two verses of the Cyclops' curse that the seer spoke she gives only the first; the ship of another and the bane at home she leaves out (11.114–115; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Her last two verses are omitted in most manuscripts, or written in their margins, and La Roche brackets them; Murray prints them (12.140–141; Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 12.140–141).

**Remembered on Thrinacia.** When Odysseus hears the cattle lowing from the sea, “there fell upon my heart the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea” (12.266–268; see [[teiresias]] and [[the-blind-seer]]). μάντηος is the text's form where the manuscripts have μάντιος (Merry's critical note on 12.267, referring to 10.493). He tells the companions “the prophecies of Teiresias / and of Circe of Aeaea” (12.272–273), μαντήια, a word found only here in Homer, and the warning he gives is Circe's: “for there, she said, was the most dread evil for us” (12.275; see the translation's key-word row for μαντήια). ἔμμεναι, not ἔσεσθαι: the evil was already there, in the shape of the herds (Merry on 12.275). His order is to drive the ship past the island (12.276); Eurylochus prevails, and the companions swear instead to spare the cattle (12.278–304).

**Fulfilled.** The book carries out the first part of the prophecy. The companions harm the cattle, and destruction comes, as the seer said, “for your ship and your companions” (11.113 = 12.140): Zeus strikes the ship with his thunderbolt, the companions are carried off on the waves, “and the god took away their homecoming” (12.415–419; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Odysseus alone escapes, and the tale ends with him on Ogygia (12.447–450). The rest, the late homecoming on another man's ship and the suitors in his house, is left for the books to come (11.114–118; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
