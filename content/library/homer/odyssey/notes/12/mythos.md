**Speaking and not speaking.** Odysseus will not keep to himself “the oracles that Circe told me” (θέσφαθʼ ἅ μοι Κίρκη μυθήσατο, 12.155; see [[thesphaton]]), but in the strait “of Scylla I said nothing more” (Σκύλλην δʼ οὐκέτʼ ἐμυθεόμην, 12.223): he did not go on to say more, for he knew from Circe that he must lose six men (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.223; see [[silence]]). “Hear my words, my companions, though you are suffering evils” opens his warning of the cattle and then Eurylochus' counsel to kill them (12.271, 12.340 = 10.189). Eurylochus answers Odysseus “with a hateful word” (στυγερῷ … μύθῳ, 12.278; see [[eurylochus]]), the charge that he is made of iron (12.279–293), and Odysseus calls an assembly and speaks to them, μετὰ μῦθον ἔειπον (12.319; see [[assembly]]).

**The tale.** The last word of the book is the verb of tale-telling. Having reached Ogygia, Odysseus breaks off: “But why do I tell you this tale? / For I already told it to you yesterday in your house” (τί τοι τάδε μυθολογεύω; / ἤδη γάρ τοι χθιζὸς ἐμυθεόμην, 12.450–451), “and it is hateful to me / to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told” (12.452–453; see [[a-tale-plainly-told]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). μυθολογεύω, found only in these two verses, makes a verb of the μῦθος that Alcinous praised in the pause of the tale, “you have told your tale skillfully, as a singer does” (11.368; see [[singers]]).
