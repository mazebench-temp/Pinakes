heading: The fourth book of the tale
summary: The tale goes on from the eleventh book without a break and comes to its end. The book tells the return to Aeaea and Circe's instructions, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, Thrinacia and the cattle of the Sun, the wreck and the drift to Ogygia (12.1–449), in the order that Odysseus keeps for Penelope (23.326–333), and breaks off with Calypso, since he had told the king and queen of her the day before (12.450–453). Merry heads it Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου.
# Unnamed: the return from the dead (12.1-4); the source of what the gods said (12.389-390); the end (12.447-453).

**The heading.** Merry heads the book Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). The book has no beginning of its own: the eleventh ends with the ship carried down the stream of Oceanus (11.638–640), and the twelfth goes on, “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus” (12.1), a clause that runs on through four verses before the main verb (Merry on 12.2). The whole book is the one speech of Odysseus to Alcinous and the Phaeacians, told on the night of the feast, and within it Circe, the Sirens, Eurylochus, the Sun and Zeus speak in speeches of their own (see [[odysseus]]).

**The order of the tale.** The adventures follow one another as the voyage went: the return to Aeaea and the burial of Elpenor, Circe's instructions, the Sirens, the strait, Thrinacia, the wreck, Charybdis again and the drift to Ogygia (see [[the-death-of-elpenor]], [[odysseus-and-circe]], [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Circe tells them first, in the order in which they will come, and Odysseus tells them again as they came, so that the hearers know each danger before the ship reaches it; only the choice between the Wanderers and the strait is left open, and only the loss to Scylla is kept from the companions (12.55–58, 12.223–225). The summary that Odysseus gives Penelope keeps the same order: the Sirens, the Wanderers, Charybdis and Scylla, the cattle of the Sun, the thunderbolt, and Ogygia and Calypso (23.326–337, Greek only).

**What the teller could not see.** Odysseus tells things he did not witness. He tells what Eurylochus said to the companions while he slept (12.339–352), as in the tenth book he told their talk over the skin of the winds (10.34–46), and he tells the Sun's complaint to the gods and Zeus' answer (12.374–388). For the second he names his source: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[calypso]] and [[hermes]]). A scholion says that Odysseus lies here, since Calypso had never seen Hermes before the visit of the fifth book (5.79–88); Merry answers that the falsehood rather rests with Calypso, and calls Eustathius' explanation, that the poet foresaw the question how Odysseus could know and answered it in these verses, “very suspicious”, since it points to too punctilious a literary criticism for the period (Merry's critical note and on 12.389; see the translation's note on 12.389–390). Kirchhoff took such passages, the doings of the crew while their leader slept and in his absence, for signs that the stories of the tenth and twelfth books were first told of Odysseus in the third person; D. B. Monro answered that an ancient poet would hardly feel the need of that kind of verisimilitude, least of all if a hero's narrative of his own adventures was a new form of epic (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §13).

**The end.** The tale ends in nearly the verses of his first account, given to Arete the evening before (12.447–449, after 7.253–255; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]):

With “But why do I tell you this tale?” Odysseus turns and speaks to Alcinous directly (Merry on 12.450). μυθολογεύω, “tell a tale”, is found in Homer only in these two verses (12.450, 12.453), and ἀριζήλως, “plainly”, only here, though the Iliad knows the adjective ἀρίζηλος (*Iliad* 2.318, 18.219); αὖτις goes with μυθολογεύειν and ἀριζήλως with εἰρημένα, “to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told” (Merry on 12.453; see [[a-tale-plainly-told]] and the translation's key-word row for μυθολογεύω). For “yesterday” Merry refers to the account of the seventh book (on 12.451), which began where the great tale now ends, with the wreck, the nine days' drift and the welcome on Ogygia (7.244–255). There the scholia report that eight verses were athetized because the story is told later, in these verses (see the translation's note on 7.251–258).

**After the tale.** The thirteenth book opens with the two verses that met the pause in the middle of the tale: “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence, / and they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2 = 11.333–334; see [[thelgo]]). The tale has run from the evening of the feast through the night; Alcinous had said that he could stay awake till dawn to hear it (11.373–376). In four books Odysseus has told what he will tell Penelope in some thirty verses (23.310–343); Aristotle, citing that retelling, gives it sixty (*Rhetoric* 1417a14–15; see the general article).
