summary: The burial that Elpenor's soul asked for is carried out at the beginning of this book, on the farthest headland, with a mound, a pillar and his oar fixed on top. Virgil gives Misenus, whose unburied body the Sibyl says pollutes the fleet, the same funeral, with his oar and his trumpet on the mound under the headland that keeps his name; and his helmsman Palinurus is lost on the night the fleet passes the rocks of the Sirens.

**Elpenor's tomb.** Back from the house of Hades, Odysseus sends companions to bring the body from Circe's house, and they bury it “where the headland jutted out farthest”, burn it with its armor, heap up a tomb, drag a pillar onto it and fix the oar on top (12.9–15; see [[elpenor]], [[the-death-of-elpenor]], [[tomb]] and [[oars]]). It is all that the soul asked: to be burned with his armor and given a mound on the shore of the gray sea, with the oar he rowed with fixed on it (11.72–78). He is buried before the next voyage begins, and the unburied soul is the first to come to the pit, not kept back by any river (11.51–54).

**Misenus.** Merry compares Virgil's burial of Misenus (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.15). In the sixth book of the *Aeneid* the Sibyl tells Aeneas that, besides the golden bough, he must first bury a friend whose lifeless body lies unknown to him and pollutes the whole fleet with death while he lingers at her threshold; only then will he see the groves of the Styx (*Aeneid* 6.149–155, plain renderings throughout; see [[virgil]]). It is the trumpeter Misenus. The Trojans build his pyre, wash and anoint the body, burn it with offerings, quench the ashes with wine and gather the bones into a bronze urn, “but pious Aeneas sets over him a tomb of huge mass, with the man's own arms, his oar and his trumpet, under a lofty mountain, which now from him is called Misenus and keeps an eternal name through the ages” (*at pius Aeneas ingenti mole sepulcrum / imponit suaque arma viro remumque tubamque / monte sub aerio, qui nunc Misenus ab illo / dicitur aeternumque tenet per saecula nomen*, *Aeneid* 6.212–235). Elpenor asked for a mound “even for men to come to hear of” (11.76); Misenus' headland keeps his name, as the Sibyl promises the place of Palinurus will keep his (*Aeneid* 6.381). Strabo knew a story that made Misenus a companion of Odysseus (*Geography* 1.2.18).

**The helmsman.** Virgil's Palinurus, whom the god Sleep throws from the stern with part of the ship and the rudder, is lost on the night when the fleet comes near “the rocks of the Sirens, once dangerous and white with the bones of many” (*Aeneid* 5.833–871; see [[the-sirens-in-later-writers]]). Odysseus loses his helmsman in this book too, in the storm after the cattle of the Sun: the falling mast smashes his head on the stern, and he falls from the deck “like a diver” (12.411–414; see [[like-a-diver]]). He is not named, and no one buries him.
