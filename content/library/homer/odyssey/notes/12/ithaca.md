---
summary: Circe repeats Teiresias' promise that the companions “may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils”, if they leave the Sun's herds unharmed (12.137–138); on Thrinacia Eurylochus vows that “if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land”, they will build the Sun a rich temple (12.345–347).
---

**The promise repeated.** Circe's warning about the herds of Thrinacia is Teiresias' prophecy in his own verses (12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[thrinacia]], [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and the Book 11 note): “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming, / you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils” (12.137–138; see [[nostos]]). The homecoming of the companions depends on the herds; that of Odysseus does not, for even if he escapes, “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (12.140–141).

**A temple on Ithaca.** Eurylochus, urging the companions to kill the cattle, offers the Sun a vow: “And if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land, / we will at once build a rich temple to Hyperion the Sun / and put in it many fine offerings” (12.345–347; see [[eurylochus]], [[hyperion]], [[temple]] and [[dear-native-land]]). εἰ δέ κεν … ἀφικοίμεθα, with the optative, puts the homecoming as a possibility, while the subjunctives that follow, if the Sun “wishes to destroy our ship, and the other gods follow his lead” (12.349), put the god's anger as the likelier event (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.345). None of them comes home to build it: when the ship is struck, “the god took away their homecoming” (12.419).
