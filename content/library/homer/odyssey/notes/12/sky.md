---
summary: The sky of Book 12 is the height of Scylla's crag, whose peak “reaches the broad sky” in a cloud that never leaves it (12.73–76); the sky from which night falls in Zeus' storm (12.312–315); the road of the Sun, who took delight in his cattle as he went up into it and turned back from it (12.379–381); and, when the ship leaves Thrinacia, all that can be seen besides the sea (12.404).
---

**The crag that reaches the sky.** Across from the Wanderers are two crags: “the one reaches the broad sky / with its sharp peak, and a cloud has settled about it, / a dark cloud; it never departs, and clear sky never / holds its peak, neither in summer nor in late summer” (12.73–76; see [[the-wanderers]], [[wide-sky]] and [[scylla]]). No mortal could climb it, “not even if he had twenty hands and feet” (12.77–78). αἴθρη, “clear sky”, is the cloudless sky that spreads over Olympus in the sixth book (6.44–45; see [[olympus]]), and here it never comes to the peak. νεφέλη … κυανέη runs over the verse, “a cloud has settled about it, / a dark cloud”, as in the Iliad (*Iliad* 20.417–418), and τὸ μέν, “it”, which some made agree with a νέφος implied in νεφέλη, is taken by W. W. Merry, with Aristarchus, as a vague neuter referring to the whole clause (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.75; see the translation's note on 12.74–75).

**Night from the sky.** On the first night on Thrinacia, “when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over” (12.312), Zeus sends the storm, “and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (12.314–315; see [[zeus]], [[earth]] and [[sea]]), the verses of Zeus' storm off the land of the Cicones and of Poseidon's against the raft (9.68–69; 5.293–294; see the Book 9 note). μετὰ δʼ ἄστρα βεβήκει means that the stars had crossed the zenith and begun to decline, “had southed”, as Merry puts it (on 12.312; see the translation's note on 12.312–313).

**The gods of the wide sky.** Eurylochus would sacrifice the best of the cattle “to the immortals, who hold the wide sky” (12.344), the verse-end of Teiresias' command to sacrifice to all the gods in order (11.133; see [[wide-sky]], [[immortals]] and the Book 11 note).

**The Sun's road.** The Sun complains to Zeus of the companions “who overweeningly have killed my cattle, in which I / always took delight as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.379–381; see [[helios]], [[starry]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The last verse is the one that described his daily course over the Cimmerians, who never see his light (11.17–18; see [[cimmerians]] and the Book 11 note). Merry cites the passage for Homer's picture of the world: the poet nowhere says in so many words that the earth is flat, but seems to take it for granted, and the Sun can look upon his pastures in Thrinacia both when he rises and when he sets (on 10.508; see [[oceanus]] and [[thrinacia]]). When the Sun threatens to go down among the dead, Zeus bids him go on giving light “among the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth” (12.383–386; see [[hades]] and [[earth]]).

**Only sky and sea.** When the ship leaves Thrinacia, “no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (12.403–404; see [[sea]]), and the son of Cronus sets “a dark cloud / above the hollow ship” (12.405–406), the cloud of the storm that destroys it.
