# πολεμήια (12.116), Murray's spelling without the diaeresis of the Iliad's πολεμήϊα.
grc: [πολεμήια]
en: [works of war]
---

Odysseus' first question about the strait is how to fight: whether he might escape Charybdis “and ward off the other, when she would harm my companions” (12.113–114). Circe answers, “Hard man, once again your mind is on the works of war / and toil; will you not give way even to the immortal gods?” (12.116–117; see [[ponos]], [[hard]] and [[scylla]]). σχέτλιε here means something like “irrepressible”: at the first chance he is thinking of fighting again (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.116). The phrase is the Iliad's: Nestor scolds the Achaeans as “foolish little ones, who care nothing for the works of war” (οὔ τι μέλει πολεμήϊα ἔργα, *Iliad* 2.338), and Zeus tells Aphrodite, “Not to you, my child, are given the works of war” (*Iliad* 5.428; see [[the-works-of-war]]). The goddess here blames the opposite fault, a man who cares for the works of war where war can do nothing, against an immortal evil (12.118–120; see [[alke]]). He arms anyway, and the armor is no use (12.226–233; see [[ephemosyne]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).
