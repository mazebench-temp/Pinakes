summary: The book is the main telling. Circe repeats Teiresias' warning and describes the herds and their nymphs (12.127–141); on Thrinacia Odysseus remembers both warnings, Eurylochus forces the landing, and the companions swear to spare the cattle (12.260–304); a month of contrary winds starves them, and while Odysseus sleeps Eurylochus persuades them to sacrifice the best of the herd (12.305–365); Odysseus wakes, the Sun demands vengeance, the meat lows on the spits, and on the seventh day they sail to their death (12.366–419).

**Circe's warning.** The cattle are the last of the dangers that Circe tells: “And you will come to the island of Thrinacia; and there in great numbers / graze the cattle of the Sun and his fat sheep” (12.127–128; see [[thrinacia]]). She gives what the seer did not, the number of the herds, “seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, / fifty in each. And no young are born of them, / nor do they ever die” (12.129–131), and the herders, two nymphs, daughters of the Sun by Neaera, whom their mother settled on the island to guard their father's flocks (12.131–136; see [[lampetie]], [[phaethusa]] and [[neaera]]). The names are, as Merry puts it, “Bright” and “Shining”, and the mother “Younger”, fit daughters and wife for the Sun (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.132); ἐπιποιμένες, “herders”, is Aristarchus' reading (see the translation's note on 12.131). Eustathius, as Merry reports, recalls that the Greeks dedicated whole herds of tame beasts to the gods, which no one might touch, and cites Herodotus' sheep of the Sun at Apollonia (Merry on 12.129); for these, and for Aristotle's reading of the numbers, see the general article. Then comes the condition in five of Teiresias' verses: “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming, / you may still reach Ithaca” (12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]).

**The landing.** After the strait the ship comes at once to “the flawless island of the god”, where the fine broad-browed cattle and the many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun are (12.260–263). It is evening: Odysseus hears from the sea “the lowing of the cattle being stalled for the night / and the bleating of the sheep” (12.265–266; Merry on 12.265, comparing 12.283), and the two warnings fall upon his heart (12.266–269). He tells the companions, whom he addresses as men “suffering evils”, of the prophecies, “for there, she said, was the most dread evil for us” (12.271–275). ἔμμεναι, not ἔσεσθαι: the worst evil actually lay there, in the shape of the Sun's herds (Merry on 12.275). His command is to “drive the black ship on past the island” (12.276), and “their dear heart was broken” (12.277), in the verse that met his news of the house of Hades (10.566).

**Eurylochus' reproach.** Eurylochus answers “with a hateful word”, as is consistent with his character (12.278; Merry on 12.278, comparing 10.429; see [[eurylochus]]): “You are a hard man, Odysseus; your might is beyond measure, and your limbs / never grow weary; truly you are made all of iron” (12.279–280). σχέτλιος is explained by a scholion as καρτερικός, “enduring” (Merry on 12.279). The companions are “worn out by weariness and by sleep”, a phrase found again only in the night of *Iliad* 10, of the Achaean sentries (12.281; *Iliad* 10.98; Merry on 12.281), and Odysseus would have them wander on αὔτως, “just as we are”, tired, sleepy and supperless (12.284; Merry on 12.284). “Out of the nights come harsh winds, the ruin of ships”, the South Wind or the West, which shatter ships “against the will of the sovereign gods” (12.286–290): this is the only place where the gods together are ἄνακτες, “probably no more than a graphic expression” for the wildness of the winds (Merry on 12.290; see the translation's note on 12.290). His counsel, “But now let us yield to black night / and make ready our supper”, is the counsel of Hector to the Trojans and of Nestor to the Achaeans at nightfall before Troy, where nightfall suggests supper (12.291–292; *Iliad* 8.502–503, 9.65–66; Merry on 12.291). The others applaud (12.294), as they will applaud his second speech (12.352).

**The oath.** “And then indeed I knew that a god was devising evil” (12.295), the words in which Nestor told how he fled from Troy (3.166). Odysseus yields: “Eurylochus, truly you are pressing me hard, one man alone” (12.297), μοῦνον, “in a minority of one”; Zenodotus read βιάζεσθʼ οἷον ἐόντα, as in the other Cyclopes' question to Polyphemus (9.410), not seeing that the active is admissible (Merry's critical note and on 12.297; see the translation's note on 12.297). He makes them all swear that no one, “in evil recklessness, / will kill a cow or a sheep”, but they will eat the food that immortal Circe gave (12.298–302; see [[oath]], [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[atasthalie]]). They swear in the verse of Circe's oath to him (12.303; 10.345), and the ship is moored in the harbor near sweet water (12.304–306).

**The winds and the hunger.** At supper the companions weep for those whom Scylla seized and devoured, until sleep comes upon them (12.308–311; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). In the third watch of the night, when the stars had passed their height (Merry on 12.312; see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]), Zeus raises a storm in the verses of the storm that followed the raid on the Cicones and of Poseidon's storm on the raft (12.314–315 = 9.68–69; 5.293–294; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). At dawn they drag the ship into a hollow cave, where “There were the fair dancing floors and seats of the nymphs” (12.316–318; see [[nymphs]]), and Odysseus warns them again in assembly (12.319–323). “And for a whole month the South Wind blew without ceasing, nor did any other / of the winds arise then, except the East Wind and the South Wind” (12.325–326; see [[notus]] and [[eurus]]). While the bread and wine last they keep away from the cattle, λιλαιόμενοι βιότοιο, “longing for life”, *vitae servandae studiosi* as Merry renders it, not *victum quaerentes*, since they still had bread and wine (12.327–328; Merry on 12.328). Then they hunt fish and birds “with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies” (12.329–332), a verse of Menelaus' starving crew on Pharos, bracketed by Bekker as borrowed from there, though Athenaeus quotes it in this place (12.332 = 4.369; see the translation's note on 12.332).

**The prayer and the sleep.** Odysseus goes up the island to pray, “in case one of them might show me a way to go home” (12.333–334). He goes alone, Merry suggests, because a man would more readily hold communion with the gods away from others, and because the confidence between him and his men was broken: he had misgivings about their loyalty that he could not utter before them (on 12.333). He washes his hands where there is shelter from the wind and prays to all the gods who hold Olympus, “and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids” (12.336–338; see [[prayer]] and [[hypnos]]). It was in his sleep that the companions opened the skin of the winds (10.31), and γλυκύν, “sweet”, makes a fine contrast with the “evil counsel” of the next verse (Merry on 12.338; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]).

**Eurylochus' counsel.** “And Eurylochus led the way in evil counsel among my companions” (12.339), opening with the verse of Odysseus' own address to them (12.340 = 12.271, 10.189):

{{quote:fcf9c9e4-6b4c-5214-8869-d490bbe01a68}}

θάνατοι are “forms of death”, called elsewhere κῆρες θανάτοιο (Merry on 12.341; see [[all-deaths-are-hateful]]). οἴκτιστον, “most pitiable”, is the word in which Odysseus has just spoken of Scylla's victims, “the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes” (12.258, 12.342). To sacrifice is to feast, for the offering implies a meal for the worshippers (Merry on 12.344; see [[sacrifice]]). The vow of a temple with offerings is, as Nitzsch remarks, the only such vow in Homer (12.346–347; Merry on 12.347; see [[temple]]), though Chryses reminds Apollo of a temple he once roofed for him (*Iliad* 1.39). The end of the speech is a choice of deaths: if the Sun destroys the ship, “I would rather lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave / than be slowly drained away on a desolate island” (12.350–351). πρὸς κῦμα χανών is “with one gasp open-mouthed at the wave”, and στρεύγεσθαι “to be drained of strength”, as of a thing squeezed out drop by drop (Merry on 12.350–351); the verse begins as Ajax's call to the Achaeans at the ships, that it is better to perish once and for all, or live, than to be slowly drained away in grim combat (12.351; *Iliad* 15.511–513; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). ἅπαξ, “once”, is the word of Circe's greeting to the men who came back from the dead, “twice dying, when other men die once” (12.22).

**The sacrifice.** They drive off the best of the cattle, which are grazing near the ship (12.353–355), stand around them and pray, “having stripped tender leaves from a high-leafed oak; / for they had no white barley on board the well-benched ship” (12.356–358). Having no barley grains for the rite, they use tender oak leaves in their place (Merry on 12.356, comparing the barley of Nestor's sacrifice at 3.441–447), and “they had no wine to pour over the burning offerings, / but poured libations with water as they roasted all the entrails” (12.362–363; see [[libation]]). The rest is told in the verses of the Iliad's sacrifices, the thigh-bones wrapped in a double fold of fat, the entrails tasted, the meat threaded on spits (12.359–365, after *Iliad* 1.458–465 and 2.421–428; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]] and [[spits]]). περίστησάν τε at 12.356 is Bekker's conjecture for the manuscripts' περιστήσαντο (see the translation's note on 12.351, 12.356).

**The waking.** “And then sweet sleep rushed from my eyelids” (12.366); near the ship “the sweet fragrance of the fat came round me” (12.369). Groaning he calls out to the gods in the verse with which Athena and Hephaestus address the assembled gods, “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever” (12.371; 5.7, 8.306): “truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep, / and my companions, staying behind, have devised a great deed” (12.372–373; see [[ate]]). The sleep is νηλέι, “pitiless”, where the sleep that came upon the weeping companions, and the sleep that has just left his eyelids, was νήδυμος, “sweet” (12.311, 12.366). For μέγα ἔργον Merry refers to Epicaste, “who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind” (11.272; Merry on 12.373); the words are used of Aegisthus' crime (3.275), and Halitherses will say of the suitors, in the words of the oath on Thrinacia, that they “did a great deed in their evil recklessness” (24.458, a plain rendering; see [[atasthalie]]). For the reading μέγʼ, “loudly”, at 12.370, see the translation's note on 12.369–370.

**The Sun's complaint.** “And swiftly to Hyperion the Sun came a messenger, / Lampetie of the long robes, to tell him that we had killed his cattle” (12.374–375). “We had killed”, ἔκταμεν ἡμεῖς, is the reading of Aristarchus' copies; the manuscripts have ἔκταν ἑταῖροι, “the companions had killed”, from a wish to clear Odysseus of the blame (Merry's critical note and on 12.375), and the words they give are those of the stranger's tale to Penelope, τοῦ γὰρ βόας ἔκταν ἑταῖροι, “for the companions had killed his cattle” (19.276, a plain rendering). The passage carries the obelos in one manuscript and was probably rejected by Aristarchus: a Sun who sees and hears all things (*Iliad* 3.277) should have known for himself (12.374–390; Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 12.374–390 and, for Aristotle's answers, the general article). The god asks the gods to punish the companions of Odysseus, “who overweeningly have killed my cattle, in which I / always took delight as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.379–381; ὑπέρβιον adverbial, Merry on 12.379), in the words of his course above the Cimmerians, on whom he never looks down (11.15–18; see [[cimmerians]]). Unless they pay “fitting recompense”, “I will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.382–383; φαείνω a subjunctive of resolve, Merry on 12.383; see [[helios]] and the translation's note on 12.383, 12.388). Zeus bids him go on giving light to gods and men, and promises to strike the ship with a thunderbolt and shatter it in the middle of the sea (12.385–388; see [[zeus]], [[thunderbolt]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Odysseus heard it from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes (12.389–390; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The portents and the feast.** Odysseus rebukes them, going up to each in turn, but there is no remedy, “for the cattle were already dead” (12.391–393; ἐπισταδόν, “walking up to each”, Merry on 12.392). Then the gods show signs: “the hides crawled, and the meat on the spits lowed, / both roast and raw, and there was a sound as of cattle” (12.395–396; see [[cattle]]). εἷρπον, “crawled on the ground”: Merry sets beside it Euripides' cattle of the Sun, whose flesh will one day speak, and a portent in Herodotus (on 12.395; see the general article). For six days the companions feast on the best of the Sun's cattle, ἐρίηρες, “trusty”, an epithet of formula here, not irony (12.397–398; Merry on 12.397). When Zeus has added the seventh day to the six, the wind ceases, and they set up the mast and hoist the white sails (12.399–402; Merry on 12.399). The ship's end is told under [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]; it comes, as Circe had said, “for your ship and your companions” (12.140).
