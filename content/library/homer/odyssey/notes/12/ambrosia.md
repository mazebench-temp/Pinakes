# "ἀμβροσίην Διὶ" (12.63), as a phrase, since ἀμβροσίην is also the ambrosial night (see the entry); it applies in the
# Odyssey only.
grc: ["ἀμβροσίην Διὶ"]
---

“By that way not even winged things pass, not even the timid doves / that carry ambrosia to father Zeus, / but even of these the sheer rock always takes one away, / and the father sends in another to make up the number” (12.62–65; see [[timid-doves]], [[zeus]] and [[the-wanderers]]). Nowhere else in Homer do birds carry the gods' food. W. W. Merry denies any connection with the later story of the doves that fed Zeus when he was hidden in Crete from Cronus, which he cites from Athenaeus, and thinks the verses probably allude to the Pleiads: their rising begins the harvest, which may be veiled in the story of the ambrosia, and the rock that always takes one away may hide the fact that of the seven Pleiads only six are ordinarily seen, as some of the scholia already explained it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.62; see the translation's note on 12.62–65). Hesiod bids the farmer begin the harvest when the Pleiads, the daughters of Atlas, rise (*Works and Days* 383–384; see [[pleiades]] and [[hesiod]]).
