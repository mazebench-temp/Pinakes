summary: The book in which the companions lose their homecoming. The Sirens' victim never comes home to wife and children (12.42–43), though they promise that the man who hears them “goes home delighted” (12.188); Circe repeats Teiresias' condition, “think of your homecoming” (12.137–141); Odysseus prays on Thrinacia for a way home (12.334); and in the wreck “the god took away their homecoming” (12.419).
# νεῖται (12.188; again 14.152), the present of νέομαι, "goes home".
grc: [νεῖται]
en: [goes home]
---

**Two promises.** The first danger of the way home is a song about the way home. The man who hears the Sirens “in his ignorance” never returns: “for him his wife and little children / do not stand beside him when he comes home, nor are they glad” (οἴκαδε νοστήσαντι, 12.41–43; see [[sirens]] and [[nepios]]), the singular verb going with the wife and the children added to her as the subject of the next (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.42). The Sirens themselves promise the opposite: no one has rowed past “before he has heard the honeyed voice from our lips; / but he goes home delighted, and knowing more” (12.187–188; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]).

**The condition.** Circe repeats the condition that Teiresias set on the cattle, in his verses: “If you leave them unharmed and think of your homecoming, / you may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils”, but if they are harmed, “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The verses 140–141 are omitted or written in the margin in most manuscripts (see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.140–141).

**The prayer and the loss.** On Thrinacia Odysseus goes up the island to pray, “in case one of them might show me a way to go home” (νέεσθαι, 12.334; see [[prayer]]). The gods answer with sleep, and the companions eat the cattle; Eurylochus' vow of a temple “if we ever come to Ithaca” (12.345) is his last word on the homecoming. In the wreck “the god took away their homecoming” (θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον, 12.419), the end the proem foretold, “and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.9). The verse returns in the false tale Odysseus tells Eumaeus, of a Cretan ship wrecked in the same words (14.308–309; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).
