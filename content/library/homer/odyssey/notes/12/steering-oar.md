summary: The wind and the helmsman steer the ship from Aeaea (12.152). Before the strait Odysseus charges the helmsman, “since you handle the steering oar of the hollow ship”, to keep the ship away from the smoke and the wave and make for the crag (12.217–221); in the storm after Thrinacia the falling mast crushes the helmsman's head on the stern, and he falls from the deck like a diver (12.411–414).

**The helmsman.** “we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her” (12.152; see [[ships]]), the verse of the voyage to the limits of Oceanus (11.10), with one helmsman for the one ship that is left, where the fleet had “helmsmen” (9.78). He is never named.

**Odysseus' charge.** At the smoke and the roar Odysseus goes through the ship and speaks to each man, and to the helmsman last: “and to you, helmsman, I give this charge; lay it / to heart, since you handle the steering oar of the hollow ship. / Keep the ship away from this smoke and wave, / and make for the crag, lest without your noticing / she rush off there and you throw us into evil” (12.217–221; see [[lay-it-to-heart]], [[hollow-ships]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). The plural οἰήια is used of one ship, as in the Iliad it is of many (*Iliad* 19.43; see the entry). W. W. Merry translates, “From this smoke and surf keep the ship away and get close to the cliffs, lest unawares she shoot off yonder, and thou bring us into trouble”: the ship is the subject of ἐξορμήσασα, and the helmsman is to give the Wanderers a wide berth and keep within reach of the rocks (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.219). Murray prints σκοπέλου, “the crag”, Scylla's rock, against the σκοπέλων, “the cliffs”, of most editors and of Merry (see the translation's note on 12.219–221 and [[the-argo]]).

**His death.** In the storm after Thrinacia “the mast fell backward, and all the tackle / was spilled into the bilge. And on the stern of the ship / it struck the helmsman's head, and smashed the bones / of the head all together; and he, like a diver, / fell from the deck, and his proud spirit left his bones” (12.410–414; see [[mast]], [[stern]], [[deck]] and [[like-a-diver]]). He was standing or sitting by the steering oar on the deck at the stern when the mast fell aft, as Merry pictures it (Appendix I §5). The last three verses are nearly those of the death of Epicles, whom Ajax's boulder struck down from a tower of the Achaean wall (*Iliad* 12.384–386; see [[epicles]]).
