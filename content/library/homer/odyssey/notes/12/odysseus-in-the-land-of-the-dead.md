summary: The return from the dead. The ship leaves the stream of Oceanus for the open sea and comes back to Aeaea, where the house of Dawn is and the risings of the Sun (12.1–4); Circe greets the men as “twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22), and hears from Odysseus all that happened, “all these things” that are now at an end (12.33–37).
# Unnamed: the return (12.1-4); "from Hades" (12.17); the greeting (12.21-22); the journey told and ended (12.34-37).

**Out of the dark.** “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea, / and to the island of Aeaea, where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.1–4). εὐρυπόροιο, “wide-wayed”, marks the open sea against the narrow stream of Oceanus (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.2; see [[oceanus]]). Merry gathers from the tenth book that the ship had sailed north and west to reach Aeaea, yet here the island lies at the sunrise. The scholia explain the words by contrast with Hades, as if Odysseus said that they had passed out of Hades into the regions of light, a view that Voss, Nitzsch and Klausen adopt; Merry finds the solution rather in Homer's way of making the farthest west and the farthest east almost one point, as in the short nights of the Laestrygonians, where the paths of night and day are near (Merry on 12.3, referring to his note on 10.82–86; see the translation's note on 12.1–4, [[aeaea]] and [[eos]]). The land of the dead was where the sun never looks (11.15–19; see [[cimmerians]]).

**Twice dying.** Circe knows that they have come “from Hades” (12.17), and greets them: “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22). ὑπήλθετε, “have gone down”, is one of the words in which the poem speaks of the journey as a descent, though Odysseus never says that he went down (see the general article). δισθανέες is found only here, and one scholion has it as two words, δὶς θανέες (Merry's critical note on 12.22; see the translation's note on 12.22). ὅτε properly means “when”, of the time they chose for their visit to the dead, but the sense passes into a logical one, like Latin *cum* and English “while” (Merry on 12.22). Merry compares the Sibyl's words to Aeneas, who wishes “bis Stygios innare lacus, bis nigra videre / Tartara”, “twice to swim the Stygian lakes, twice to see black Tartarus” (*Aeneid* 6.133–135; Merry on 12.22; see [[virgil]]).

**At an end.** That night Circe questions Odysseus about each thing, and he tells her “all of it fair and right” (12.34–35). Then she begins: “All these things have been brought to an end” (12.37), where ταῦτα takes in all that happened on the voyage to Hades (Merry on 12.37), and turns to what he must still pass (see [[odysseus-and-circe]]). The journey had been her errand: Teiresias was to tell him the way, and told him part of it; the rest she now tells herself (12.25–26, 12.37–141; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). Of the dead the book has two more echoes. On Thrinacia the word of the seer falls upon Odysseus' heart (12.266–267), and the Sun threatens, if he is not paid for his cattle, to go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead (12.383; see [[helios]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
