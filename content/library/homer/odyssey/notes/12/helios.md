summary: The Sun is the god of the second half of the book. Circe warns of his cattle and fat sheep on Thrinacia, which his daughters Phaethusa and Lampetie herd (12.127–141); on the island Odysseus reminds his men that they are the cattle “of a dread god, / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (12.322–323), and Eurylochus vows him a temple (12.346–347). When the companions have killed them, Lampetie brings him the news, and he threatens to “go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” unless he is repaid (12.374–383); Zeus bids him keep giving light and promises the thunderbolt (12.385–388). Earlier, Aeaea lies where “the risings of the Sun” are (12.4), and his ray softens the wax at the Sirens' island (12.175–176).

**The risings of the Sun.** The ship comes back from Oceanus “to the island of Aeaea, where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (12.3–4; see [[aeaea]] and [[eos]]). W. W. Merry, who reads Book 10 as bringing the ship there on a course to the northwest, finds a real contradiction in setting the island at the sunrise. The scholia explained the verses as a contrast with Hades, the passage out of darkness into the light; Merry's solution is that Homer regards the extreme west and the extreme east as almost one point, as in the verse of Aratus that a scholion quotes on the short nights of the Laestrygonians (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.3; see the translation's note on 12.1–4). The plural ἀντολαί, “risings”, he takes to reflect the sun's rising at different points of the horizon as the seasons change (on 12.4).

**The cattle of the Sun.** Circe's warning names the god's herds: “And you will come to the island of Thrinacia; and there in great numbers / graze the cattle of the Sun and his fat sheep, / seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, / fifty in each. And no young are born of them, / nor do they ever die” (12.127–131; see [[thrinacia]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Their herders are his daughters, “Phaethusa and Lampetie, / whom brilliant Neaera bore to Hyperion the Sun”, and they guard “their father's sheep and his cattle with curving horns” (12.132–136; see [[phaethusa]], [[lampetie]], [[neaera]] and [[hyperion]]). Circe, who speaks of them, is herself a daughter of the Sun (10.138–139; see [[circe]]). Aristotle read the herds as the days of the year: seven herds of fifty make the three hundred and fifty days of twelve lunar months, and the poet says that no young are born to them and none die because the number of such days never changes (*Homeric Problems* fr. 398 Gigon, from the scholia and Eustathius on 12.129–130; Merry on 12.129). Merry declines to follow Eustathius further, or to suppose with him that the eating of the cows was a poetical way of expressing waste of time, but accepts his remark that the Greeks consecrated whole herds of tame animals to the gods, which no one might touch, and his example, the sheep sacred to the Sun at Apollonia on the Ionian Gulf (Herodotus 9.93; see the general article). He reports too Gladstone's view that the sanctity of the Sun's cows and sheep, and the punishment of those who laid hands on them, reflect Egyptian usage, and notes that in the Vedic hymns the rays of the sun are spoken of as his cows (on 12.129).

**The ray of the Sun.** At the Sirens' island the wind drops and the sea lies calm, and Odysseus cuts a great round of wax into small pieces and presses them, “and at once the wax grew warm, since the great strength bade it / and the ray of the lord Sun, son of Hyperion” (12.175–176; see [[hyperion]]). Merry notes that κέλετο, “bade”, is used of persons everywhere else in Homer, and nowhere else of the effect of an external force. Nitzsch would cut the second verse as superfluous, since either the pressing or the warmth is all that is wanted, and finds Ὑπεριονίδης un-Homeric; Merry answers that the shavings would soften far more readily in the full sunlight, and that the hot sun suits the windless calm, and he sees a conscious touch of humour in so majestic a phrase for the kneading of a cake of wax (Merry on 12.175; see the translation's note on 12.175–176).

**The island of the Sun.** After Scylla and Charybdis “we came to the flawless island of the god; / and there were the fine broad-browed cattle, / and many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun” (12.261–263). The lowing and bleating bring to Odysseus' mind the word of Teiresias “and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again / to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (12.268–269; again 12.273–274; see [[teiresias]]). τερψίμβροτος, “who gladdens mortals”, is said in Homer only in these two verses (see [[who-gladdens-mortals]] and the translation's key-word row); a scholion records the variant φαεσιμβρότου, “who brings light to mortals” (Merry's critical note on 12.269), the Sun's epithet in Book 10 (10.138, 10.191; see the Book 10 note). The *Hymn to Apollo* ends a verse with τερψιμβρότου Ἠελίοιο when the Cretan ship comes to Taenarum, the country of the Sun, where his thick-fleeced sheep always graze (*Hymn to Apollo* 411–413; see the general article). Kept on the island by the storm, Odysseus warns his companions, “so let us keep away from the cattle, lest we suffer harm; / for these are the cattle and fat sheep of a dread god, / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (12.321–323; see [[who-sees-all-things]]): the verse of Teiresias' warning (11.109; see the Book 11 note) and of the Iliad's oath, where Agamemnon calls the Sun to witness (*Iliad* 3.277).

**The vow.** Eurylochus, urging the starving companions to kill the cattle, promises the god his due: “let us drive off the best of the cattle of the Sun / and sacrifice to the immortals, who hold the wide sky. / And if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land, / we will at once build a rich temple to Hyperion the Sun / and put in it many fine offerings” (12.343–347; see [[eurylochus]] and [[temple]]). Nitzsch remarked that it is the only vow of its kind in Homer (Merry on 12.347). Eurylochus expects the god's anger, “But if, angry over his straight-horned cattle, / he wishes to destroy our ship, and the other gods follow his lead”, and would rather drown at once than starve (12.348–351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). The Sun's own cattle are sacrificed to the immortals (12.344, 12.356).

**The anger of the Sun.** Lampetie brings him the news (12.374–375; see [[lampetie]]), “And at once he spoke among the immortals, angry at heart” (12.376). He calls on the gods in the verse with which Athena opened the council of the gods in Book 5, Hephaestus called them to see Ares and Aphrodite, and Odysseus has just cried out (5.7, 8.306, 12.371; see [[olympians]]): “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever, / punish the companions of Odysseus, son of Laertes, / who overweeningly have killed my cattle” (12.377–379; see [[zeus]]). His delight in them is told in the verses of the sun that never shines on the Cimmerians: “in which I / always took delight as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.379–381; 11.17–18; see the Book 11 note). The threat turns the order of the world over: “And if they do not pay me fitting recompense for the cattle, / I will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.382–383; see [[hades]]). The house of Hades is the place without the sun: Teiresias asked Odysseus why he had “left the light of the sun” to see the dead (11.93–94; see [[the-land-of-the-dead]]). Merry takes φαείνω as a subjunctive of resolve following the future δύσομαι, the use that Monro illustrates from this passage, though it may be a present: “I will dive into Hades, and, there am I giving light among the dead!” (Merry on 12.383; see the translation's note on 12.383, 12.388).

**Zeus' answer.** “Answering him, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said: / ‘Sun, do you keep giving light among the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth; / and as for them, I will soon strike their swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt / and shatter it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea’” (12.384–388; see [[zeus]], [[cloud-gatherer]] and [[thunderbolt]]). Ἠέλιʼ is the only vocative of the name in Homer; Agamemnon's oath addresses him in the nominative, Ἠέλιός θʼ (*Iliad* 3.277). Zeus' first words take up the sunrise of Book 3, where the sun springs up “to give light to the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth” (3.2–3; see the Book 3 note): he bids the Sun keep to his course and takes the punishment on himself, and the thunderbolt falls when the ship leaves the island (12.415–417). The proem gave the last act to the Sun, “and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.9; see the Book 1 note); in the tale the Sun asks, Zeus strikes, and “the god took away their homecoming” (12.419; see [[day-of-homecoming]]). Odysseus tells how he knew what was said in heaven: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[calypso]] and [[hermes]]).

**The god who sees all things.** Why should the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things, need a messenger? The scholia give this as the reason for rejecting 12.374–390, which contradict the Iliad's verse, since he should have known for himself (Merry's critical note on 12.374–390; see the translation's note). Aristotle offered several answers: that the Sun sees everything, but not everything at once; that Lampetie reported to him as sight reports to a man; that the words are fitting in Agamemnon's oath; and that the poet makes the Sun see what faces him, the things of the east when he is in the east, and those of the west at his setting, so that, being in another part of his course, he did not see what was done on Thrinacia. He oversees all things, but does not watch all things at the same time (*Homeric Problems* fr. 373 Gigon, from the scholia on *Iliad* 3.277; see [[aristotle]]).

**The sun of the day.** On Aeaea the companions feast with Circe's food “all day long, until the sun went down”, and lie down by the ship “When the sun went down and darkness came on” (12.29–31; see [[sunset-lines]]), the verses of the island of goats and of the earlier days on Aeaea (9.161–162; 10.183–185, 10.476–478). After the wreck Odysseus is carried all night on the keel and mast, “and with the rising sun / I came to Scylla's crag and to dread Charybdis” (12.429–430; see [[charybdis]]). ἅμα δʼ ἠελίῳ ἀνιόντι, “with the rising sun”, is the half-verse of Thetis' promise to Achilles, “for at dawn I will return with the rising sun” (*Iliad* 18.136), and Hephaestus' fall from heaven has the same verse turned to the evening: “and all day long I was carried down, and as the sun was setting / I fell on Lemnos” (*Iliad* 1.592–593).
