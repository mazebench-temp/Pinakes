summary: The oath Odysseus demands on Thrinacia forbids the deed the proem calls the companions' recklessness: that no one, “in evil recklessness”, will kill a cow or a sheep (12.300).
# ἀτασθαλίῃσι (12.300; again 24.458, "ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσι"), the shorter dative of the entry's ἀτασθαλίῃσιν.
grc: [ἀτασθαλίῃσι]
---

The word comes at the moment of warning. Odysseus makes the companions swear “that no one, in evil recklessness, / will kill a cow or a sheep” (μή πού τις ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσιν / ἢ βοῦν ἠέ τι μῆλον ἀποκτάνῃ, 12.300–301; see [[oath]]). The proem has already told the end: “for they perished through their own recklessness, / fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun” (1.7–8; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[nepios]]). The book shows the pattern that the word names everywhere, a warning given and ignored and a ruin that is the doers' own: Teiresias and Circe gave the warning (11.110–114; 12.137–141), Odysseus repeated it twice (12.271–276, 12.320–323), and the companions swore to keep it (12.303–304). When they break it, it is with Eurylochus' reasons, hunger and the hope of a temple to appease the god (12.339–351; see [[all-deaths-are-hateful]]).

Odysseus, waking, blames the gods' sleep for his own part, “truly to my blind folly you lulled me” (εἰς ἄτην, 12.372): the folly that a god sends is [[ate|ἄτη]], the recklessness that men commit is ἀτασθαλίη, and the book has both, his and theirs (see [[who-is-to-blame]]). The phrase ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσι returns in Book 24, when Halitherses tells the Ithacans that their sons, the suitors, “did a great deed in their evil recklessness” (24.458, a plain rendering), the μέγα ἔργον that Odysseus here says his companions have devised (12.373).
