**Scylla's catch.** αὐτοῦ δʼ ἰχθυάᾳ, σκόπελον περιμαιμώωσα, / δελφῖνάς τε κύνας τε, καὶ εἴ ποθι μεῖζον ἕλῃσι / κῆτος: “and there she fishes, questing round the crag, / for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster” (12.95–97; see [[scylla]], [[fish]] and the translation's rows ἰχθυάω; περιμαιμάω and κύνες (of the sea); κῆτος). W. W. Merry joins περιμαιμώωσα with εἴ ποθι, “questing round the rock if anywhere she may catch” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.95). μεῖζον, “a greater”, makes the dolphins and the dogfish lesser κήτεα, beasts of the sea of the kind that loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds in countless numbers (12.97; see [[sea-beasts]] and [[amphitrite]]). The dogfish, κύνες, are “dogs” of the sea, not the dogs of the land (see [[dogs]]). In the Iliad the dolphin is the hunter before which the other fishes flee into the recesses of a harbor, the likeness of Achilles in the river (*Iliad* 21.22–26; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]); Scylla fishes for the hunters of the sea.

**The fishing of the strait.** Polybius, as Strabo reports him, found Homer's verses true to what happens at Scyllaeum, on the strait between Italy and Sicily. The shoals of tunny that run along the coast of Italy are kept from crossing to Sicily by the currents and fall among the larger creatures, dolphins and dogfish and other beasts of the κῆτος kind, and the γαλεῶται, which are also called swordfish and dogfish, grow fat by hunting the tunny. The fishermen of Scyllaeum hunt them from small two-oared boats with harpoons, and from such details, Polybius held, one may judge that Homer set the wanderings around Sicily: he gave Scylla the hunt most native to Scyllaeum (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.15–16; see [[strabo]] and [[polybius]]).
