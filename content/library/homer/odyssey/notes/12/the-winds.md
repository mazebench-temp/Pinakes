---
summary: The winds carry the ship and hold it. Circe sends a following wind from Aeaea, and “the wind and the helmsman steered her” (12.148–152); a harmless wind brings the ship to the Sirens' island and drops to a calm (12.167–169). Eurylochus fears the harsh winds that come out of the night (12.286–290); Zeus rouses a blustering wind on Thrinacia, and for a month no winds blow but the South and the East (12.313, 12.325–326). When the wind ceases and the ship sails, the West Wind wrecks it, and Odysseus is borne on the keel and mast “by deadly winds” (12.400–427).
# οὖρον, the following wind that Circe sends (12.149), and οὖρος, the harmless wind that drives the ship to the Sirens
# (12.167), are not forms of ἄνεμος and are counted here, as in the Book 11 note.

**Circe's wind.** The ship leaves Aeaea as it left for the dead: “And behind our dark-prowed ship / she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion, / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice. / At once, when we had busied ourselves with all the tackle throughout the ship, / we sat; and the wind and the helmsman steered her” (12.148–152; see [[circe]] and the Book 11 note). The verses are those of 11.6–10, all but the fourth, which begins otherwise (see the translation's formula rows).

**The calm.** “and meanwhile the well-made ship quickly came / to the island of the two Sirens; for a harmless wind drove it on. / Then at once the wind ceased, and there was a calm, / a windless calm, and a god lulled the waves to sleep” (12.166–169; see [[sirens]] and [[daimon]]). The calm is the one that brought Odysseus in sight of Scheria (5.391–392; see the translation's notes on 5.391 and 12.168), and the men furl the sails and take to the oars (12.170–172). For ἀπήμων, “harmless”, a scholion records ἀμύμων (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 12.167). In the windless heat the wax softens in the sun (12.175–176; see the Book 12 note on [[helios]]).

**The winds of the night.** On Thrinacia Eurylochus argues for a night ashore: “Out of the nights come harsh winds, the ruin of ships; / how could anyone escape sheer destruction, / if suddenly a storm of wind should come, / of the South Wind or the stormy West Wind, which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods?” (12.286–290; see [[eurylochus]], [[notus]], [[zephyrus]] and [[olympians]]). Merry renders ἐκ νυκτῶν “by night”, perhaps “after the night-watches have set in”, and thinks the phrase may also mean that the wild winds come actually out of the darkness (on 12.286; see the translation's note on 12.284–286). The fear comes true that night, when Zeus “roused a blustering wind against us” (12.313; see [[zeus]]), and the wind holds them: “And for a whole month the South Wind blew without ceasing, nor did any other / of the winds arise then, except the East Wind and the South Wind” (12.325–326; see [[eurus]]). Odysseus prays to the gods “where there was shelter from the wind” (12.336), the half-verse of the river's mouth where he came ashore on Scheria, in the narrator's telling and in his own (5.443, 7.282).

**The wreck.** On the seventh day of the feast “the wind ceased raging with its storm” (12.400), and they put to sea; but when no land is in sight, “at once there came / the screaming West Wind, raging with a great storm, / and the storm of wind broke the forestays of the mast, / both of them” (12.407–410; see [[forestays]] and [[mast]]). Lashing the keel and the mast together, “and sitting on them I was borne by deadly winds” (12.425): ὀλοοῖς ἀνέμοισιν, the deadly winds that carried the ships for nine days from Cythera to the land of the Lotus-eaters (9.81–82; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). Then “the West Wind ceased raging with its storm, / and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart” (12.426–427), and carried him back to Charybdis (see the Book 12 note on [[notus]]).
