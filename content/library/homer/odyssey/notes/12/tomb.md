summary: Back on Aeaea the companions burn Elpenor's body with his armor where the headland juts out farthest, heap up a mound, drag a pillar up onto it and fix his oar on the top, as he had asked among the dead (12.11–15).
# στήλην, the pillar on the mound (12.14, the only place in the Odyssey), first met here; it applies in the Odyssey only,
# since στήλη in the Iliad is also the pillar of a simile (13.437, 17.434) and the buttresses of the wall (12.259).
grc: [στήλην]
en: [pillar]
---

“And quickly we cut logs, and where the headland jutted out farthest / we buried him, grieving and shedding big tears. / But when the body was burned, and the armor of the body, / we heaped up a tomb, and dragged a pillar up onto it, / and on the top of the tomb we fixed the well-fitted oar” (12.11–15; see [[elpenor]], [[the-death-of-elpenor]], [[cremation]] and [[pyres]]). It is what Elpenor's soul asked for among the dead: to be burned with his armor and given a mound on the shore of the gray sea, “the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of”, with the oar he rowed with fixed on it (11.74–78; see the Book 11 note and [[oars]]).

θάπτομεν is imperfect, “we buried him where the beach ran out to its furthest point”, and W. W. Merry compares the tomb of Achilles, heaped up on a jutting headland (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.12; 24.80–82, a plain rendering; see [[shore]]). ἐπὶ … ἐρύσαντες go together, “having dragged up a stone pillar thereon”, onto the mound (Merry on 12.14). Mound and pillar together are “the prize of the dead” in the Iliad, τύμβῳ τε στήλῃ τε (*Iliad* 16.457 = 16.675; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). Zenodotus, the scholia report, read the last verse with ἵνα σῆμα πέλοιτο, “so that it might be a marker” (Merry's critical note on 12.15), where the text has the oar; and Merry compares the mound that Aeneas heaped over Misenus, setting on it the man's own arms, his oar and his trumpet (on 12.15; Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.232–233; see [[virgil-the-unburied]]).
