summary: Odysseus tells the wreck itself, the end of the tale. On the seventh day the ship puts out from Thrinacia; Zeus' storm breaks the mast, which kills the helmsman, and his thunderbolt strikes the ship, as he had promised the Sun (12.397–419). Odysseus rides the keel and the mast back to Charybdis, clings to the fig tree until she gives up the timbers, and drifts for nine days to Ogygia (12.420–450).

**The seventh day.** For six days the companions feast on the Sun's cattle, “but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day”, that is, added it to the six, the wind ceases, and they set up the mast, hoist the white sails and put out into the wide sea (12.397–402; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.399; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The storm that held them for a month ends only to send them to their death.

**The wreck.** “But when we were leaving the island”, the half-verse with which they left the island of the Sirens (12.403, 12.201), “and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (12.403–404; γαιάων an unusual plural, again in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] of 14.302, Merry on 12.404), Zeus sets a dark cloud over the ship, and the sea grows dark beneath it (12.405–406). “And she ran on for no long time” (12.407; Merry on 12.407): the West Wind comes screaming and breaks both forestays. Two forestays ran from the masthead to either side of the bows and a backstay to the stern, so that if one forestay had snapped, the other and the backstay would have held the mast; but both broke at once, ἀμφοτέρους standing first in its verse for emphasis (12.408–410; Merry on 12.409; see [[forestays]] and [[mast]]). The mast falls backward, spilling all its tackle into the bilge, and on the stern it strikes the helmsman's head and smashes the bones of his skull, and he falls from the deck “like a diver”, in the verses in which Ajax's stone kills Epicles on the Achaean wall (12.410–414; 12.412–414 after *Iliad* 12.384–386; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[steering-oar]]). Then Zeus keeps his word to the Sun:

{{quote:4a65fb39-9ebe-523d-b5fb-040d55a3ce9f}}

ἄμυδις may mean that the thunderbolt came along with the wind, or, answering καί, that in the same moment that he thundered he struck the ship (Merry on 12.415). The sulfur is the smell of the thunderbolt, as when Zeus' bolt falls before Diomedes' horses (12.417; *Iliad* 8.133–135; Merry on 12.417, who quotes Pliny that lightning smells of sulfur; see [[thunderbolt]]). The companions are carried “like sea crows around the black ship” (12.418; see [[like-sea-crows]]), and “the god took away their homecoming” (12.419), as the proem foretold, where it is the Sun who “took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.9; see [[nostos]]). Odysseus will use nine of these verses again, with Crete for the island, for the wreck of the Phoenician ship in his false tale to Eumaeus (12.403–406, 12.415–419; 14.301–309; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

**The keel and the mast.** “But I kept pacing through the ship, until her sides / were loosed from the keel by the surge, and the wave carried it along bare, / and snapped the mast off at the keel” (12.420–422). The keel was left bare, the sides of the ship torn away from it; the mast was stepped into the solid wood of the keel, so that, snapped at its lowest point, it broke “at the keel” (Merry, Appendix I §2 and §12, on the Homeric ship; see [[keel]]). Zenodotus read ἔαξε for ἄραξε (Merry's critical note on 12.422). Over the mast hung the backstay of oxhide, ἐπίτονος, the rope that ran from the masthead to the stern; its verse opens with a short syllable, the kind of line the ancients called “headless”, and Athenaeus quotes it with τετάνυστο for βέβλητο (12.423; Merry on 12.423; see the translation's note on 12.420–423). With it Odysseus lashes keel and mast together, “and sitting on them I was borne by deadly winds” (12.424–425). To Arete he said that he took the keel in his arms (7.252), and Calypso that she saved him “astride the keel” (5.130).

**Back to Charybdis.** “Then the West Wind ceased raging with its storm, / and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (12.426–428). ὄφρα follows on ἦλθε, “so as to make me retrace my course”, the words about the sorrows standing apart; ἀναμετρήσαιμι Χάρυβδιν is as if “that I might measure out the sea and come to Charybdis”, as Nestor's ships “measured the great open sea” (Merry on 12.428; 3.179; see the translation's note on 12.428). “All night long I was borne along, and with the rising sun” (12.429) is made on the verse in which Hephaestus tells how he fell all day from heaven, “and as the sun was setting” came down on Lemnos (*Iliad* 1.592; see [[hephaestus]]). At sunrise he comes to Scylla's crag and dread Charybdis, and she sucks down the sea (12.430–431):

{{quote:d7a85426-58f3-587b-8298-59150f127c7b}}

The fig tree is the one Circe pointed out, “a great wild fig tree, in full leaf”, beneath which Charybdis sucks down the black water (12.103–104; see [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]] and [[like-a-bat]]). He holds on “unceasingly” until she vomits up the mast and keel again, and “as I yearned for them they came, / late; at the hour when a man rises from the assembly for his supper, / one who judges the many quarrels of young men pleading their cases” (12.437–440; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]). The hour is toward evening, when a judge has got through his cases and the market place begins to empty, and ἀνέστη is an aorist of custom (Merry on 12.439–441). The scholia report that the verses 12.430–441 were doubted in many copies: Odysseus reached Charybdis at sunrise, yet the timbers came up only at evening, though she sucks down and sends up the sea three times a day (12.105); and ἦμος δέ elsewhere in Homer always begins a verse (Merry's critical note and on 12.439–441; see the translation's note on 12.430–441).

**The escape.** “And I let myself drop from above, feet and hands, / and fell with a splash in the middle, beyond the very long timbers, / and sitting on them I rowed with my hands” (12.442–444), with a plural and a dual side by side in πόδας καὶ χεῖρε (Merry on 12.442). “And the father of men and gods no longer let Scylla / see me; for then I would not have escaped sheer destruction” (12.445–446). The scholia rejected the two verses, since Scylla could not leave her cave, and could hardly reach as far as Charybdis. Merry brackets them and suggests ἐμέ as the subject of εἰσιδέειν: Zeus, pitying Odysseus, did not suffer him to set eyes on Scylla again, a reading that would also explain why Zeus is named as his protector where Circe had bidden him call on Crataeis (12.124); Ameis thought the lines compiled from 12.223 and other verses by an editor who wished to explain why Scylla did not seize Odysseus (Merry's critical note and on 12.445–446; see the translation's note on 12.445–446 and [[crataeis]]). He came through the strait the first time at the cost of six companions (12.234–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]); the second time he is alone.

**Nine days.** “From there for nine days I was borne along, and on the tenth night / the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso / lives” (12.447–449; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]). The verses are nearly those of his account to Arete, ἐννῆμαρ φερόμην, δεκάτῃ δέ με νυκτὶ μελαίνῃ (7.253–255), and the half-verse is the one with which the deadly winds carried the ships from Malea out of the known world (9.82). The tale ends here, for he had told the king and queen of Calypso the day before (12.450–453; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).
