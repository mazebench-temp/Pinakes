summary: Circe leaves the choice of the two ways to Odysseus, “you yourself / must plan it in your heart” (12.57–58); he reminds his companions that his counsel brought them out of the Cyclops' cave (12.211); Eurylochus “led the way in evil counsel” on Thrinacia (12.339); and he would rather drown at once than starve (βούλομʼ, 12.350).
# βουλῆς (12.339), first met here in the Odyssey.
grc: [βουλῆς]
---

**Planning.** After the Sirens Circe will not decide for Odysseus “which of the two ways shall be yours, but you yourself / must plan it in your heart; but I will tell you of both” (θυμῷ βουλεύειν, 12.57–58; see [[circe]] and [[thymos]]); she professes not to decide, though she plainly means to dissuade him from the Wanderers (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.55). In the strait he puts counsel beside prowess and mind as what saved them from the Cyclops, “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind” (ἐμῇ ἀρετῇ, βουλῇ τε νόῳ τε, 12.211; see [[arete]], [[noos]] and [[not-without-knowledge-of-evils]]).

**Evil counsel.** On Thrinacia the counsel passes to another: “And Eurylochus led the way in evil counsel among my companions” (κακῆς ἐξήρχετο βουλῆς, 12.339; see [[eurylochus]] and [[all-deaths-are-hateful]]), the phrase in which the Achaeans had praised Odysseus himself, “leading the way in good counsel” (*Iliad* 2.273), now turned to evil and to another man (see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, ἐξάρχω βουλῆς). Merry sets it against the sweet sleep of the verse before (on 12.338; see [[hypnos]]). The counsel ends in a preference, βούλομʼ … ἤ, “I would rather lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave / than be slowly drained away on a desolate island” (12.350–351; Merry on 12.350; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]).
