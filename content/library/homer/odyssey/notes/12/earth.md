summary: The earth of Book 12 is land as against sea, “either on sea or on land” (12.27), the land on which the weary companions long to set foot (12.282) and their native land (12.345); the bountiful earth on which all things come to pass that the Sirens know (12.191); the sea floor laid bare by Charybdis, “dark with sand” (12.242–243); the earth toward which the Sun turns back at evening (12.381); and the land that no longer appears when the ship leaves Thrinacia (12.404).
# γῆς (12.27), in the Odyssey only: it also takes 11.167, 11.482 ("our own land") and 11.302 ("beneath the earth"),
# the earth in each, and no Iliad line; as an entry form it would also take three lines of the Rhetoric (1360a34,

grc: [γῆς]

**Sea and land.** Circe promises to show the way so that they may not suffer “either on sea or on land” (12.26–27; see [[sea]]), the pair in which she had summed up their wanderings in Book 10 (10.457–459). The short form of the word, γῆ, is rare in Homer beside γαῖα and χθών: γῆς is also “our own land” on which Odysseus has not yet set foot (11.167, 11.482) and the earth beneath which the Dioscuri live on alternate days (11.302; see the Book 11 note), and in the Iliad Γῆ is Earth called on in oaths (*Iliad* 3.104, 19.259).

**The earth the Sirens know.** The Sirens promise Odysseus the war at Troy, “and we know all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (12.191; see [[sirens]], [[bountiful-earth]] and [[troy]]). γένηται, W. W. Merry notes, is a general statement, serving for all time, set against μόγησαν, the one completed fact of the war (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.188).

**The bottom of the sea.** When Charybdis swallows the salt water, “all within her appeared, churning, and around her the rock / roared terribly, and beneath, the earth appeared, / dark with sand” (12.240–243; see [[charybdis]]). φάνεσκε, “appeared”, is the word for the black earth that appeared at the feet of Tantalus whenever the water was swallowed up (11.586–587; Merry on 12.241; see [[tantalus]]). The sand does not look yellow but dark, Merry says, because it lies in what Sophocles calls the “undersea darkness”, ὕφαλον ἔρεβος (on 12.243, quoting *Antigone* 589; see [[erebus]] and the translation's note on 12.234–245).

**Setting foot on land.** On Thrinacia Eurylochus reproaches Odysseus, who will not let his weary companions “set foot on land” to make their supper on the island (12.281–283; see [[eurylochus]] and [[thrinacia]]). In the storm of that night Zeus covers “land and sea alike” with clouds (12.314–315; see [[sky]]), and in his speech to the companions Eurylochus speaks of the homecoming, “if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land” (12.345; see [[ithaca]] and [[dear-native-land]]).

**The Sun's earth.** The Sun took delight in his cattle as he went up into the sky “and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (12.381; see [[helios]] and [[sky]]), and Zeus bids him go on giving light “to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth” (12.386; see [[grain-giving]] and [[mortal-men]]).

**No other land.** When the ship leaves Thrinacia, “no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (12.403–404; see [[sea]]). γαιάων, “of the lands”, is an unusual plural, found again only at 8.284 and 14.302: Nitzsch compares Pindar's γαίας τε πάσας, “and all lands”, and Krüger refers it to the Homeric use of the plural for a general idea, as ἱπποσυνάων, “horsemanship” (*Iliad* 16.776), and τεκτοσυνάων, “carpentry” (5.250) (Merry on 12.404).
