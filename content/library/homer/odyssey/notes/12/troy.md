---
summary: The Sirens offer Odysseus the knowledge of his own war: “For we know all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods” (12.189–190).
---

**The Sirens' Troy.** The Sirens greet Odysseus with his title from the Iliad and promise a song of the war he fought: “For we know all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods, / and we know all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (12.189–191; see [[sirens]], [[achaeans]], [[argives]], [[trojans]] and [[earth]]). ἐνὶ Τροίῃ εὐρείῃ, “in wide Troy”, ends the verse in which Achilles, among the dead, remembers what he was at Troy (11.499; see the Book 11 note), and two verses of the Iliad (*Iliad* 13.433, 24.774). μόγησαν, “toiled through”, is the word of Odysseus' praise of Demodocus, who sang “all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through” (8.490; see [[demodocus]]), and Odysseus uses it of himself later in the book, of all that he has toiled through on the paths of the sea (12.259; see [[sea]]). The whole verse comes back in Book 17, when Telemachus tells his mother that he saw in Sparta Argive Helen, for whose sake the Argives and Trojans toiled much by the will of the gods (17.118–119, a plain rendering; see [[helen]]). In Cicero's Latin the Sirens know the heavy contest of the war and the disaster that Greece, by the will of the gods, brought upon Troy: “Nos grave certamen belli clademque tenemus, / Graecia quam Troiae divino numine vexit” (*De Finibus* 5.49; see [[cicero]]).
