---
summary: Hades is the house of the dead in Book 12, never the god in person. Circe notices that the men “had come from Hades” and greets them as “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.17–22); and the Sun threatens that unless he is repaid for his cattle he “will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.383).
# The entry's exact forms claim Ἀίδεω (12.17) and Ἀίδαο (12.21, 12.383).
---

**Back from Hades.** The book begins as the ship comes out of the stream of Oceanus into the sea (12.1–2; see [[oceanus]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]), and Circe knows where the men have been: “nor did Circe / fail to notice that we had come from Hades” (12.16–17; see [[circe]]). ἐξ Ἀίδεω, “from Hades”, is the genitive in -εω of “the dank house of Hades” in her instructions (10.512) and of Odysseus' last fear among the dead, that Persephone would send the Gorgon's head against him “out of Hades” (11.635; see the Book 11 note). Her greeting makes the visit a death: “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22). δῶμʼ Ἀίδαο is the house to which every man goes once; those who have gone down alive will go there again. W. W. Merry takes ὅτε as referring properly to the time they had chosen for their voluntary visit to the realm of the dead, though the temporal sense is passing into a logical one (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.22; see the Book 12 note on [[circe]]). The living and the dead of Book 11 spoke of the voyage in the same way, as a going down: “My mother, need brought me down to the house of Hades” (11.164), and Achilles' “How did you dare to come down to Hades” (11.475).

**The Sun among the dead.** The other mention is a threat to the order of the world. If the companions do not pay for his cattle, the Sun tells Zeus and the gods, “I will go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead” (12.383; see [[helios]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). His share is the light, and the share of Hades is the dark: when the brothers drew lots, “Hades got the misty darkness” (*Iliad* 15.191; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]), and Anticleia asks her son how he came “beneath the misty darkness / while you are alive” (11.155–156). The Iliad knows the fear of such a disclosure: when Poseidon shakes the earth, Aidoneus leaps from his throne and cries out, “lest above him / Poseidon the earth-shaker should break the earth open, / and his dwellings be laid bare to mortals and immortals, / terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (*Iliad* 20.62–65; see [[poseidon]]). Zeus answers the Sun by bidding him keep giving light among the immortals and to mortal men, and the house of Hades is left in its darkness (12.385–386; see [[zeus]]).
