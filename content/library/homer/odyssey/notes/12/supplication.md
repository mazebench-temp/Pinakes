summary: Circe foresees that Odysseus, bound to the mast, will beg his companions to set him free, and tells them to bind him tighter (12.53–54); he passes the instruction on (12.163–164), and when the Sirens sing he begs them, nodding with his brows (12.192–196).
# λίσσηαι (12.53), λίσσωμαι (12.163), each only here.
grc: [λίσσηαι, λίσσωμαι]
---

“And if you beg your companions and bid them set you free, / then let them bind you with still more bonds” (εἰ δέ κε λίσσηαι ἑτάρους λῦσαί τε κελεύῃς, 12.53–54; see [[circe]] and [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]). Odysseus tells his companions the same in the first person, “And if I beg you and bid you set me free, / then you must press me with still more bonds” (12.163–164). The entreaty is foreseen and disarmed before it is made: when the song comes, he can only nod with his brows, and Perimedes and Eurylochus bind him the tighter (12.192–196; see [[neuo]]). Aristophanes athetized 12.53–54, asking why a man once bound should be bound again (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 12.53–54; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.53–54). The begging of a man to his own men is not the formal supplication of the knees and chin; it is the same verb, and the companions are told to refuse it.
