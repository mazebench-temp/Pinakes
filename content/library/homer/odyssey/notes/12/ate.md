---
summary: Odysseus, waking on Thrinacia to the smell of the roasting cattle, cries out that the gods lulled him in pitiless sleep “to my blind folly” (12.372).
---

“Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever, / truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep, / and my companions, staying behind, have devised a great deed” (12.371–373; see [[prayer]] and [[hypnos]]). εἰς marks the result, as in εἰπεῖν εἰς ἀγαθόν, “speak for the good” (*Iliad* 9.102; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.372). The blindness is not in his mind but in his sleep, sent by the gods at the one moment when he was needed awake, as in the sleep that let the companions open the bag of the winds within sight of Ithaca, when he named sleep with them as the cause of his folly (10.31, 10.68–69; Merry on 12.338; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]). The gods whom he had prayed to on the island answered with that sleep (12.333–338), and he puts the blame on them; the deed is the companions' (see [[atasthalie]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). μέγα ἔργον, “a great deed”, is a monstrous one, as of Epicaste's marriage to her son: μέγας takes the bad sense from the Greek feeling that greatness beyond measure is wrong (11.272; Merry on 11.272, to which he refers on 12.373).
