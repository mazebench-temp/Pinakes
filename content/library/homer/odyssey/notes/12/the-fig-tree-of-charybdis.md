**Circe's crag.** After the higher crag with Scylla's cave, Circe tells of the other: “The other crag, lower, you will see, Odysseus. / They are near one another; you could even shoot an arrow across. / On it is a great wild fig tree, in full leaf; / and beneath it brilliant Charybdis sucks down the black water” (12.101–104; see [[charybdis]] and the translation's row ἐρινεός). χθαμαλώτερον, “lower”, is predicative with ὄψει, “you will see” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.101), and τῷ δʼ ὑπό is “beneath this”, the fig tree (Merry on 12.104). φύλλοισι τεθηλώς, “in full leaf”, is found only here; the verb is the one with which the garden vine over Calypso's cave “flourished with clusters of grapes” (τεθήλει δὲ σταφυλῇσι, 5.69; see [[vine]]). Circe gives the tree as a mark of the place to avoid: “May you not be there when she sucks it down” (12.106).

**The fig tree saves him.** On the last morning of the tale Odysseus comes back to the strait alone, riding the keel and the mast, just as Charybdis is sucking down the sea (12.429–431; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). “but I, springing up on high toward the tall wild fig tree, / clung to it and held on like a bat” (12.432–433; see [[like-a-bat]]). The tree is now seen from beneath, as a man hanging from it sees it: “for its roots were far below, and its branches hung high out of reach, / long and great, and they overshadowed Charybdis” (12.435–436). Merry renders “for the roots stretched far away [below], and the boughs were high up out of reach” (on 12.432); the scholia explain εἶχον as ὑπῆρχον, “were”, and some read ἦσαν or ἔσσαν (Merry's critical note on 12.435). κατεσκίαον, “overshadowed”, is also found only here. The boughs that shade the whirlpool hold him above it all day: “And I held on unceasingly, until she should vomit up again / the mast and keel” (12.437–438), until the timbers come to light at the hour when a judge rises for his supper (12.439–441; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]). Then he lets go, drops beside them into the water and rows with his hands (12.442–444).

**The tree and the whirlpool.** Circe had named the fig tree as a landmark of death; in the event it is the one thing in the strait that holds still while the sea moves. Strabo makes it his proof that Circe exaggerated the danger to frighten Odysseus away (*Geography* 1.2.36; see the general article and [[strabo]]).
