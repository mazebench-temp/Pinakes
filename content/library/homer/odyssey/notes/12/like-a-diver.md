**The helmsman.** The man who dies first in the storm has no name. He is the helmsman, κυβερνήτης, who steered the ship with the wind when they left Aeaea (12.152) and to whom Odysseus gave his charge in the strait: “and to you, helmsman, I give this charge; lay it / to heart, since you handle the steering oar of the hollow ship” (12.217–218; see [[steering-oar]] and [[lay-it-to-heart]]). Out of sight of land the West Wind broke both forestays, “and the mast fell backward, and all the tackle / was spilled into the bilge. And on the stern of the ship / it struck the helmsman's head” (12.410–412; see [[forestays]], [[mast]] and [[stern]]). ὁ δʼ ἄρα is the mast (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.411), which falls aft toward the stern, where the helmsman sits; the two forestays had run from the masthead to either side of the bows (Merry on 12.409).

**The Iliad's verses.** σὺν δʼ ὀστέʼ ἄραξε / πάντʼ ἄμυδις κεφαλῆς· ὁ δʼ ἄρʼ ἀρνευτῆρι ἐοικὼς / κάππεσʼ ἀπʼ ἰκριόφιν, λίπε δʼ ὀστέα θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, “and smashed the bones / of the head all together; and he, like a diver, / fell from the deck, and his proud spirit left his bones” (12.412–414; see the translation's row ἀρνευτήρ). The words are the death of Epicles, whom Ajax's boulder strikes on the battlements of the Achaean wall, and who falls “from the high tower” (*Iliad* 12.384–386; see the entry and [[epicles]]), and of Cebriones, who falls “from the well-made chariot” under Patroclus' stone (*Iliad* 16.742–743; see [[cebriones]]). Here the man falls ἀπʼ ἰκριόφιν, “from the deck”, the deck at the stern where he sat (see [[deck]]), and since the phrase is shorter than the Iliad's tower and chariot, the verse has room at its end for θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, “his proud spirit”, where the Iliad has θυμός alone (see [[proud]]). Each time the blow is to the head and the fall is headlong: the stone crushes Epicles' skull and Cebriones' brows, the mast the helmsman's head.

**The diver.** ἀρνευτήρ, as Merry reports, was referred by the ancient interpreters to ἄρνες, “lambs”, as if the word meant “skipped like rams”; Döderlein would write ἐρευνητήρ, and Curtius gives it an initial digamma and connects it with Sanskrit *vâri*, “water”, and Latin *urinator*, “diver”. At *Iliad* 12.385 the Syriac palimpsest reads ὁ δʼ ἄρα νευτῆρι, but Bekker insists that the form would have to be νευστήρ; Hesychius, however, has νευτήρ· κολυμβητής, “a diver” (on 12.413). For a diver's action Merry points to Patroclus' jest over Cebriones, who would feed many “diving for oysters, / leaping from a ship” (*Iliad* 16.747–748), and he compares Virgil's helmsman of Orontes' ship, thrown headlong from the stern by a sea that strikes it from above: *excutitur pronusque magister / volvitur in caput*, “the helmsman is shaken out and rolls headlong” (*Aeneid* 1.115–116; Merry on 12.413; see [[virgils-storm]]).

**After him.** Zeus' thunderbolt follows at once, and the companions fall out of the ship and float around it “like sea crows” (12.415–419; see [[like-sea-crows]] and [[thunderbolt]]). The helmsman's fall like a diver and the crew's like sea birds are the last pictures of Odysseus' men; he alone stays on the ship until the sea breaks it apart (12.420–425; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
