summary: The narrator ends his tale. Back on Aeaea he buries Elpenor, and Circe takes him apart and tells him the way. He tells his men of the Sirens, hears their song bound to the mast, and says nothing of Scylla, who snatches six of them while he stands armed at the prow. On Thrinacia Eurylochus forces him to land; he binds the crew by oath, but falls asleep while they kill the Sun's cattle. When Zeus wrecks the ship he alone survives, rides the keel and mast back to Charybdis, clings to the fig tree, and drifts for nine days to Calypso's island, where he breaks off, since he told that part the day before (12.1–453).

**The tale ends.** The book is the fourth and last of Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians, going on from Book 11 without a pause (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). The words of Circe, the Sirens, Eurylochus, the Sun and Zeus are speeches within his speech, and what the Sun and Zeus said he tells as he heard it later from Calypso (12.389–390). Its first verses bring the ship out of the stream of Oceanus, “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea” (12.1–2), the great and wide sea, as against the narrow Ocean-stream (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.2; see [[oceanus]]), and back to Aeaea, which is set here at the house of Dawn and the risings of the Sun (12.3–4; see [[aeaea]] and the translation's note on 12.1–4).

**Elpenor buried.** At dawn he sends companions to Circe's house “to bring the body of Elpenor, who had died” (12.10), and they burn him with his armor, heap up his tomb and fix his oar on it (12.8–15; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]): the promise he made the soul at the pit, “All this, unhappy man, I will accomplish and do for you” (11.80), is kept.

**Circe's counsel.** Circe comes with bread, meat and wine, and greets them as “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (12.21–22; see [[circe]], [[hard]] and the translation's note on 12.22). After a day of feasting, when the others lie down by the ship, “she took me by the hand, away from my dear companions, / and seated me, and reclined near me, and questioned me about each thing; / and I told her all of it fair and right” (12.33–35). They were not in her house, Merry notes, since she goes back to it afterward (12.143), but probably in some grotto or bower; and προσέλεκτο is “reclined at my feet” rather than “lay by me”, for nothing is said of a bed, as in Book 10 (Merry on 12.33, 12.34; see the translation's note on 12.33–34). Then she tells him the way, “and a god himself will remind you” (12.38), some kindly power, Merry thinks, neither Poseidon nor the Sun (Merry on 12.38): the Sirens first; then two ways, between which “you yourself / must plan it in your heart” (12.57–58), though she plainly means to turn him from the Wanderers (Merry on 12.55; see [[the-wanderers]]); then Scylla and Charybdis (see [[charybdis]]); and last Thrinacia, where her final verses are those of Teiresias' prophecy (12.137–141, as 11.110–114; see [[teiresias]] and [[thrinacia]]).

**Will you not give way?** When she has told of the two crags, he asks: “Come now, goddess, tell me this truly, / whether I might somehow escape from deadly Charybdis / and ward off the other, when she would harm my companions” (12.112–114). τὴν δέ, “the other”, is Scylla, and ὑπεκπροφύγοιμι, with its three prepositions, is “secretly escape and get forward” (Merry on 12.113, 12.114). Two manuscripts have ἀτυζόμενος, “bewildered”, in the margin of the verse that brings in his question, for ἀμειβόμενος, “answered”; Buttmann thought it a very good reading, since ἀμειβόμενος is properly said only when the other has finished, and Odysseus breaks in on Circe (Merry's critical note on 12.111; see the translation's note on 12.111). She answers with a reproach: “Hard man, once again your mind is on the works of war / and toil; will you not give way even to the immortal gods?” (12.116–117), σχέτλιε being here something like “irrepressible”: at the very first chance he is thinking of deeds of war (Merry on 12.116). Scylla is “not mortal, but an immortal evil” (12.118), and “the strongest course is to flee from her” (12.120), an intentional oxymoron, “the bravest course is to run away” (Merry on 12.120): if he lingers beside the rock to arm himself, she will take as many men again (12.121–123; see [[scylla]]).

**What the men are told.** At dawn Circe goes away up the island; the crew rows out, and her wind follows the ship, “a good companion” (12.142–150). Then, “grieving at heart” (12.153), he tells them her oracles: “Friends, it is not right that only one or two should know / the oracles that Circe told me, brilliant among goddesses; / but I will tell them, so that knowing them we may either die / or else avoid death and doom and escape” (12.154–157; see [[thesphaton]]). ἵνα with κε is found only here, and Merry renders, “but I will speak, in which case, when we have learned our lesson, we may either die or escape”; the optative φύγοιμεν is to be preferred to the subjunctive, escape seeming less likely than death (Merry on 12.156, 12.157; see the translation's note on 12.156–157). But what he tells them is the Sirens alone, and in one thing he changes Circe's word: she had left the hearing to his choice, “if you yourself are willing to listen” (12.49); to them it is her command, “She bade me alone to hear their voice; but you must bind me / in a grievous bond, so that I may stay there steadfast” (12.160–161).

**The wax and the mast.** As they near the Sirens' island the wind drops, “and a god lulled the waves to sleep” (12.169), and the men furl the sail and take to the oars (12.170–172). “But I with the sharp bronze cut a great round of wax / into small pieces, and pressed them with my strong hands; / and at once the wax grew warm, since the great strength bade it / and the ray of the lord Sun, son of Hyperion” (12.173–176; see [[wax]] and [[helios]]). κέλετο is nowhere else in Homer used of an external force, and Merry finds a conscious touch of humor in such majestic language for the kneading of a cake of wax (Merry on 12.175; see the translation's note on 12.175–176). He anoints the ears of all of them in turn, and they bind him “upright in the mast-step” and fasten the rope-ends to the mast itself (12.177–179; see [[mast]]).

**The song.** The Sirens see the ship and sing, hailing him with the Iliad's address, “Come here, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans” (12.184), the words with which Agamemnon and Nestor greet him when he comes back from the embassy to Achilles and from the night raid (*Iliad* 9.673, 10.544; see [[much-praised]], [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]], [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[the-doloneia]]); they know “all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods” (12.189–190; see [[sirens]] and [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]). “and my heart / wished to listen, and I bade my companions set me free, / nodding with my brows; but they bent forward and rowed” (12.192–194): it was no good to speak, Merry notes, since the companions' ears were stopped with wax (Merry on 12.194). Perimedes and Eurylochus stand up and bind him tighter, as he had bidden them (12.195–196, 12.163–164; see [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]), and when the voice and the song can no longer be heard, “at once my trusty companions took away the wax / with which I had anointed their ears, and freed me from my bonds” (12.199–200).

**We are not without knowledge of evils.** As they leave the island he sees smoke and a great wave and hears a roar, and the oars fly from the men's hands (12.201–205). He was at the stern, Merry supposes, and now walks the whole length of the ship, passing through the rowers and speaking to each man in turn (Merry on 12.206), “with soothing words, standing beside each man” (12.207), as he roused them on Aeaea (10.173, 10.547; see [[soothing-words]]):

{{quote:3c546328-62a5-5b3f-a4fc-8b49207a6254}}

The danger he names is the Cyclops' cave, and the escape was his own work, “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind” (12.211; see [[not-without-knowledge-of-evils]], [[we-will-remember-these-things-too]], [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[arete]], [[boule]] and [[noos]]). τῶνδε, “these things”, are the dangers that threaten, Merry explains after Ameis, where Virgil's Aeneas, who borrows the verses to hearten his men after the storm, “O socii—neque enim ignari sumus ante malorum”, “forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit”, means by *haec* the sufferings they are already enduring (*Aeneid* 1.198, 1.203; Merry on 12.208, 12.212; see the translation's note on 12.212); Aeneas, too, reminds them of Scylla and of the rocks of the Cyclops (*Aeneid* 1.200–201; see [[virgil]]). Then come the orders: the rowers are to strike the deep breakers, “if ever Zeus / will grant us to escape this destruction and get away” (12.215–216), and the helmsman is to keep the ship from the smoke and the wave and make for the crag (12.217–221; see [[steering-oar]]). κεῖσε, “there”, points toward the Wanderers, whose surf the helmsman is to shun by keeping close to the rocks (Merry on 12.219, 12.221; see the translation's note on 12.219–221): so he takes the way past Scylla, as Circe had advised (12.108–110).

**Nothing of Scylla.** “But of Scylla I said nothing more, a vexation past remedy, / lest my companions in fear should stop / rowing, and huddle themselves together inside the ship” (12.223–225). He dared not reveal the details of the danger, Merry explains, for he knew from Circe's prediction that he must lose at least six men, and he contented himself with the vague mention of the crag; πυκάζοιεν is to shelter by huddling together under the decks or benches (Merry on 12.223, 12.225; see the translation's note on 12.223).

**Armed at the prow.** “And then I let the grievous command of Circe / slip from my mind, since she had told me not to arm myself; / but I put on my glorious armor, and taking two long spears / in my hands I went onto the deck of the ship / at the prow” (12.226–230). The command is “hard”, ἀλεγεινῆς, Merry explains, because a fighting man chafes at passive endurance, and λανθανόμην is “I let myself forget”, that is, “disregarded” (Merry on 12.226, 12.227; see the translation's note on 12.226–227). From the prow he expects her to appear, “Scylla of the rock, who brought a bane on my companions” (12.231), where φέρε is simply “brought”: the narrator anticipates what is coming (Merry on 12.231). “But I could not catch sight of her anywhere, and my eyes grew weary / as I glanced all around toward the misty rock” (12.232–233; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).

**The six.** They sail up the strait lamenting, all looking toward Charybdis as she sucks down and spews up the sea (12.234–244; see [[charybdis]]), “and meanwhile Scylla seized my companions from the hollow ship, / six of them, who were the strongest with their hands and in strength” (12.245–246). Merry pictures the moment: all eyes had been on the whirlpool; suddenly Odysseus hears a cry, turns round, glances at the ship and after his comrades, and catches sight of them swinging aloft in Scylla's clutches (Merry on 12.247). “they lifted up their voices and called / on me by name, then for the last time, grieving at heart” (12.249–250), and the narrator likens them to the little fishes that a fisherman flings out gasping (12.251–255; see [[the-fisherman]]):

{{quote:c01aa351-752d-5e61-98df-75231f1daa75}}

ἐν αἰνῇ δηιοτῆτι, “in grim combat”, is more graphic than a word such as ὄλεθρος or ἄλγος, since it implies an unavailing struggle with an overmastering power, a “death-struggle” (Merry on 12.257; see the translation's note on 12.256–257). For the order of the words Merry compares Agamemnon's “And the most pitiable voice I heard was that of Priam's daughter” (11.421; Merry on 12.258).

**Thrinacia.** They come to the island of the Sun, and from the sea he hears the cattle lowing, “and there fell upon my heart the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea” (12.266–268; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]); he bids the men drive past, “for there, she said, was the most dread evil for us” (12.275): κακὸν ἔμμεναι, not ἔσεσθαι, since their most disastrous misfortune actually lay there, in the shape of the Sun's herds (Merry on 12.275). “So I spoke, and their dear heart was broken” (12.277). Eurylochus answers with a reproach, “You are a hard man, Odysseus” (12.279), and the others applaud (12.278–294). “And then indeed I knew that a god was devising evil” (12.295), and he gives way, “Eurylochus, truly you are pressing me hard, one man alone” (12.297; see the translation's note on 12.297). He holds them by an oath: “But come now, all of you, swear me a mighty oath: / if we find a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep, / that no one, in evil recklessness, / will kill a cow or a sheep; but at your ease / eat the food that immortal Circe gave” (12.298–302; see [[oath]]). ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσιν, “in evil recklessness”, is the word of the proem, where the companions “perished through their own recklessness” (1.7; see [[atasthalie]]), and “So I spoke, and at once they swore that they would not, as I bade them” (12.303) is the verse in which Circe swore him her oath on Aeaea (10.345).

**Sleep.** In the harbor he warns them once more: “Friends, there is food and drink in the swift ship; / so let us keep away from the cattle, lest we suffer harm; / for these are the cattle and fat sheep of a dread god, / of the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things” (12.320–323; see [[who-sees-all-things]]). For a month the South Wind blows, and when the provisions are spent the men hunt fish and birds, “and hunger wore at their bellies” (12.325–332). “then I went away up the island, so that I might pray / to the gods, in case one of them might show me a way to go home” (12.333–334); out of the companions' sight he washed his hands and prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus, “and they poured sweet sleep upon my eyelids” (12.335–338; see [[prayer]], [[sweet-sleep]] and [[hypnos]]). He withdraws, Merry thinks, because he was more likely to hold communion with the gods alone, as Eidothea met Menelaus apart from his companions and Hermes met Odysseus on his way to Circe's house (4.367, 10.277); besides, the confidence between him and his men was broken, and he had misgivings about their loyalty that he could not utter to the gods in their presence (Merry on 12.333). It was during his sleep, Merry adds, that the companions had brought trouble on him before, when they opened the bag of the winds (10.31–47; Merry on 12.338; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]).

**Waking.** While he sleeps Eurylochus leads the crew to kill the cattle (12.339–365; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). “And then sweet sleep rushed from my eyelids” (12.366), and as he nears the ship “the sweet fragrance of the fat came round me” (12.369): “And groaning I called out loudly to the immortal gods: / ‘Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever, / truly to my blind folly you lulled me in pitiless sleep, / and my companions, staying behind, have devised a great deed’” (12.370–373; see [[ate]]). μέγʼ, “loudly”, is Bekker's conjecture, which Murray prints, for the manuscripts' μετʼ, “among”, which Merry keeps as a standing formula not to be pressed, since Odysseus was not in the presence of the gods (Merry on 12.370; see the translation's note on 12.369–370). The sleep that the gods poured on him as “sweet” (γλυκύν, 12.338) is now νηλέι ὕπνῳ, “pitiless sleep” (12.372), and for μέγα ἔργον, “a great deed”, Merry refers to Epicaste, “who did a great deed in the ignorance of her mind” (11.272; Merry on 12.373).

**What the gods said.** Lampetie brings the Sun the news “that we had killed his cattle” (12.375): ἔκταμεν ἡμεῖς, “we had killed”, is the reading of Aristarchus' copies, where the manuscripts have ἔκταν ἑταῖροι, “the companions killed”, from a wish, Merry thinks, to exonerate Odysseus from the blame (Merry's critical note on 12.375; see the translation's note on 12.374–390). The Sun asks the gods to punish “the companions of Odysseus, son of Laertes” (12.378; see [[helios]] and [[son-of-laertes]]), and Zeus promises to strike the ship with his thunderbolt (12.385–388; see [[zeus]] and [[thunderbolt]]), in nearly the words in which Calypso told Hermes of the wreck and Odysseus told it to Arete (5.131–132, 7.249–250). How he knows what the gods said he tells at once: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[calypso]] and [[hermes]]). A scholion says that Odysseus lies here, since Calypso had never yet seen Hermes; the falsehood, Merry answers, rather rests with Calypso. Eustathius thought that the poet foresaw the question how Odysseus could know what passed between the Sun and Zeus, where he was not present, and answered it in these verses; Merry finds that solution very suspicious, as pointing to too punctilious a literary criticism for the period (Merry's critical note and note on 12.389; see the translation's note on 12.389–390). The whole passage carries the obelos in one manuscript and was probably rejected by Aristarchus, since the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things (12.323; *Iliad* 3.277), should have known for himself (see the translation's note on 12.374–390).

**No remedy.** Back at the ship “I rebuked them, going up to each in turn, but we could find / no remedy, for the cattle were already dead” (12.392–393); ἐπισταδόν is walking up to each man in turn and then standing near him, like παρασταδόν before the strait (12.207; Merry on 12.392). The gods show portents, the hides crawling and the meat lowing on the spits (12.394–396), and for six days “my trusty companions” feast on the cattle (12.397–398), an epithet that Merry calls inappropriate here, a mere epic formula, not to be read as irony (Merry on 12.397; see [[trusty]]).

**The wreck.** On the seventh day the wind drops and they put out to sea, “setting up the mast and hoisting the white sails” (12.399–402). Out of sight of land Zeus sets a dark cloud over the ship; the West Wind breaks both forestays, and the falling mast smashes the skull of the helmsman whom Odysseus had charged at the strait (12.217–221), “and he, like a diver, / fell from the deck, and his proud spirit left his bones” (12.403–414; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[steering-oar]]). Zeus' thunderbolt strikes the ship, and the companions fall out of it, “and like sea crows around the black ship / they were carried on the waves, and the god took away their homecoming” (12.415–419; see [[like-sea-crows]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]), as the proem had said, “and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.9), and as Teiresias and Circe had foretold, “having lost all your companions” (11.114 = 12.141). “But I kept pacing through the ship”, until the surge loosed her sides from the keel and snapped off the mast; with the oxhide backstay he lashed keel and mast together and sat on them, borne along by the winds (12.420–425; see [[keel]], [[mast]] and the translation's note on 12.420–423).

**Charybdis again.** Then the South Wind comes, “bringing sorrows to my heart, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (12.427–428): Circe had wished that he might not be there when she sucks down the water (12.106), and he had asked her whether he might somehow escape her (12.113). At sunrise, as Charybdis swallows the sea, “I, springing up on high toward the tall wild fig tree, / clung to it and held on like a bat” (12.432–433; see [[like-a-bat]]), unable to plant his feet or to climb, “for its roots were far below, and its branches hung high out of reach” (12.435). He held on νωλεμέως, “unceasingly” (12.437), as he held the ram's fleece in the Cyclops' cave, where Merry takes him to have hung head downward, just as he clings to the fig tree here (9.435; Merry on 9.435; see the translation's note on 9.434–435). The mast and keel come up “late”, at the hour when a judge rises from the assembly for his supper (12.438–441); the verses were doubted in many copies, since he reached Charybdis at sunrise and the timbers return only toward evening (Merry's critical note and note on 12.439–441; see the translation's note on 12.430–441). He drops onto them with a splash and rows with his hands (12.442–444), and “the father of men and gods no longer let Scylla / see me; for then I would not have escaped sheer destruction” (12.445–446), verses that the scholia rejected because Scylla could not leave her cave; Merry would take ἐμέ as the subject, so that Zeus “suffered him not to set eyes on Scylla again” (Merry on 12.445–446; see the translation's note on 12.445–446).

**The end of the tale.**

{{quote:752e25e4-3fa3-5b5c-b75e-035d71a665b7}}

Here Odysseus turns and addresses Alcinous directly (Merry on 12.450; see [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The nine days and the tenth night, and the island of Ogygia “where Calypso lives”, are the words of his answer to Arete the night before (7.253–255; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]), and the scholia record that eight verses of that answer were athetized because the story is told here (see the translation's note on 7.251–258). μυθολογεύω, “tell a tale”, is found in Homer only in these last verses (12.450, 12.453), where αὖτις goes with μυθολογεύειν and ἀριζήλως with εἰρημένα: “to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told” (Merry on 12.453; see [[a-tale-plainly-told]]). The Phaeacians fall silent, as at the pause, “held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (13.1–2, a plain rendering; 11.333–334).

**His names and epithets in this book.** φαίδιμʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ, “shining Odysseus”, and Ὀδυσσεῦ in Circe's counsel (12.82, 12.101; see [[phaidimos]]); πολύαινʼ Ὀδυσεῦ, μέγα κῦδος Ἀχαιῶν, “much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans”, in the Sirens' song (12.184; see [[much-praised]] and [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]); Ὀδυσεῦ in the reproach of Eurylochus (12.279); and Λαερτιάδεω Ὀδυσῆος, “Odysseus, son of Laertes”, in the Sun's complaint (12.378; see [[son-of-laertes]]). Unnamed, he is σχέτλιε, “Hard man”, to Circe (12.116), who greets him and his men as σχέτλιοι, “Hard men” (12.21), and σχέτλιος, “a hard man”, to Eurylochus, who adds that he is “made all of iron” (12.279–280; see [[hard]]); the companions had called him σχέτλιε when he taunted the Cyclops (9.494), and so had Achilles among the dead (11.474).
