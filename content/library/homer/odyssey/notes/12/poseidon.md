summary: Poseidon is not named in Book 12. Circe gives him his title once, when she warns Odysseus against Charybdis: “for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil” (12.107). The companions are lost through the cattle of the Sun, not through his anger, but Circe's warning ends in the words of the Cyclops' prayer to him (12.141).
# The entry's "ἐνοσίχθ*" claims ἐνοσίχθων (12.107).
---

**Not even the earth-shaker.** Of the whirlpool under the fig tree Circe says, “For three times a day she sends it up, and three times she sucks it down, / terribly. May you not be there when she sucks it down; / for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil” (12.105–107; see [[charybdis]] and [[earth-shaker]]). The lord of the sea is named by his title alone, as the one power that might be thought able to save a man there. He has no other part in the book. The scholia found him in the god who “will remind” Odysseus of Circe's counsel (12.38), but W. W. Merry denies that any particular god is meant (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.38; see the Book 12 note on [[circe]]). Later legend, Merry notes, made Charybdis a daughter of Poseidon, perhaps from an extension of her epithet δῖα, “brilliant” (on 12.104); and he derives the name of Thrinacia from θρῖναξ, “a trident”, which may tie the island to some legend of Poseidon, perhaps that he raised it from the sea bed with his trident (on 12.127; see [[thrinacia]]).

**The prayer fulfilled.** Teiresias had warned that Odysseus would not escape the shaker of the earth, angry because he blinded his son (11.101–103; see the Book 11 note), but in Book 12 the companions perish through their own act on Thrinacia and Zeus' thunderbolt (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[zeus]]). Circe's warning, though, ends in the words of the Cyclops' prayer to his father. He asked that Odysseus “come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions” (9.534; see [[curses]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]); Teiresias foretold it in the second person (11.114), and Circe repeats the seer: “And even if you yourself escape, / you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (12.140–141). The book ends with Odysseus alone on Ogygia (12.447–450; see [[calypso]]).
