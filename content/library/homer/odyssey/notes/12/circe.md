summary: Circe is the counselor of the first half of the book. When the ship comes back from the dead she comes to the shore with bread, meat and wine and greets the men as “twice dying” (12.16–27); that night she takes Odysseus apart, hears his story and tells him the way home, past the Sirens, by the Wanderers or between Scylla and Charybdis, and to the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia (12.33–141). At dawn she goes away up the island and sends the ship off with a following wind (12.142–150). Afterward her words go with Odysseus: he tells her oracles to his men (12.154–164), lets her command not to arm himself slip from his mind at Scylla's rock (12.226–227), and off Thrinacia remembers her warning with Teiresias' (12.266–275).

**Back from Hades.** On the morning after the ship's return Odysseus sends companions “to the house of Circe / to bring the body of Elpenor, who had died” (12.9–10; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). While they bury him, “nor did Circe / fail to notice that we had come from Hades, but very quickly / she came, having made herself ready; and her handmaids brought with her / bread and meat in plenty and sparkling red wine” (12.16–19; see [[handmaids]] and [[hades]]). The first verse is the Iliad's, “We were arranging all these things” (*Iliad* 11.706), and W. W. Merry distinguishes διείπομεν, from the root of ὅπλον, from the Iliad's δίειπε (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.16; see the translation's note on 12.16). ἐντυναμένη, “having made herself ready”, he compares with Hera, who adorns herself to go to Ida (*Iliad* 14.162), and αἴθοπα beside ἐρυθρόν, he says, settles the meaning of the epithet as “bright” or “sparkling” (on 12.18, 12.19). Then, “standing in our midst she spoke, brilliant among goddesses: / ‘Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once’” (12.20–22; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]). For δισθανέες, “twice dying”, a scholion has the lemma δὶς θανέες, as two words, which it gives to Apollonius, and Eustathius to Apollodorus; Merry compares Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.134, and notes that ὅτε, “when”, is passing, like Latin *cum*, into a logical conjunction (Merry's critical note and note on 12.22; see the translation's note on 12.22). She bids them feast all day and sail at dawn: “and I will show you the way and point out / each thing, so that you may not, through some grievous mischief, / either on sea or on land, suffer pain and meet with bane” (12.25–27). In Book 10 she had promised that the seer would tell him “the way and the measures of your path” (10.539); Teiresias told him of the cattle and of his end, and now Circe tells him the way (see the Book 11 note).

**The night.** After the feast on the shore, when the others lie down by the stern-cables, “she took me by the hand, away from my dear companions, / and seated me, and reclined near me, and questioned me about each thing; / and I told her all of it fair and right” (12.33–35). Merry thinks they were not in her house, since she goes back up the island afterward (12.143), but in some grotto or bower; and he takes προσέλεκτο as “reclined at my feet” rather than “lay by me”, for it is night but no bed is named, as when she bade him go up into her bed (10.334), and the scholia's προσεῖπε, “addressed”, is impossible, since the word belongs to the stem λεχ- (Merry on 12.33, 12.34; see the translation's note on 12.33–34). “Then lady Circe spoke to me with words: / ‘All these things have been brought to an end; but you, listen / to what I shall tell you, and a god himself will remind you’” (12.36–38; see [[queenly]]). ταῦτα takes in all the voyage to Hades (Merry on 12.37), and the god who will remind him is no special god, neither Poseidon, as the scholia suggested, nor the Sun, but “some kindly power” (on 12.38; see the translation's note on 12.38).

**The counsel.** Her speech is the map of the book. She tells him first of the Sirens and how to pass them, with the wax for the companions' ears and the bonds for himself (12.39–54; see [[sirens]]). Alcman, as Nitzsch quotes him, made Circe herself anoint the ears of the companions of Odysseus, which Nitzsch called the earliest testimony to any particular scene of the Odyssey (Merry on 12.47; see the translation's note on 12.47–50). Then she leaves the choice to him: “then I will no longer tell you from beginning to end / which of the two ways shall be yours, but you yourself / must plan it in your heart; but I will tell you of both” (12.56–58; Merry on 12.55): the Wanderers, past which only the Argo came (12.59–72; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[the-argo]]), or the two crags of Scylla and Charybdis (12.73–110; see [[scylla]] and [[charybdis]]), and she advises Scylla's, “since it is far better / to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (12.109–110). Odysseus asks whether he might escape Charybdis and ward off Scylla when she would harm his companions (12.112–114), and she rebukes him: “Hard man, once again your mind is on the works of war / and toil; will you not give way even to the immortal gods? / She is not mortal, but an immortal evil” (12.116–118). σχέτλιε here is something like “irrepressible”, and the advice that follows, “the strongest course is to flee from her”, couples courage with running away in an intended oxymoron (Merry on 12.116, 12.120). She bids him call on Crataeis, Scylla's mother (12.124–126; see [[crataeis]]), and ends with the island of Thrinacia and the herds of the Sun, her own father (12.127–141; see [[thrinacia]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[helios]]). Her last five verses repeat Teiresias' warning (12.137–141; 11.110–114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]), and most manuscripts omit the last two or have them in the margin (Merry's critical note on 12.140–141; see the translation's note).

**The parting.** “So she spoke, and at once Dawn of the golden throne came. / She then went away up the island, brilliant among goddesses” (12.142–143; see [[eos]] and [[golden-throned]]). The first verse ended her instructions for the journey to the dead (10.541), and ἀνὰ νῆσον is “up the island”, away from the shore (Merry on 12.143). As she sent the ship to the dead, so she sends it now: “And behind our dark-prowed ship / she sent a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion, / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice” (12.148–150 = 11.6–8; see [[the-winds]], [[with-lovely-tresses]], [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]). The third verse named her when the ship first came to Aeaea (10.136), and its titles are given to Calypso at the end of the book (12.449; see [[calypso]]). She does not appear again in the Odyssey.

**Her words remembered.** Odysseus tells his men “the oracles that Circe told me, brilliant among goddesses” (12.155; see [[thesphaton]]): what she bade of the Sirens, and the bonds (12.158–164). Of Scylla he says nothing (12.223–225), and at her rock he forgets her: “And then I let the grievous command of Circe / slip from my mind, since she had told me not to arm myself” (12.226–227; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). ἀλεγεινῆς Merry renders “hard”, because a fighting man chafes at passive endurance, and λανθανόμην “I let myself forget”, that is, “disregarded” (Merry on 12.226, 12.227; see the translation's note on 12.226–227). Off Thrinacia her warning comes back with the seer's: “there fell upon my heart the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea, who charged me again and again / to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (12.266–269), and he tells the men in the same verses, “for there, she said, was the most dread evil for us” (12.272–275; see [[aeaea]] and [[teiresias]]). The best manuscripts have the singular verbs, ἐπέτελλε and ἔφασκεν, which give the charge to Circe alone (Merry's critical notes on 12.268, 12.273 and 12.275; see the translation's note on 12.265–275). As Odysseus reported them, neither she nor Teiresias had bidden him avoid the island; both had said that the cattle must be left unharmed (11.110, 12.137).

**Immortal Circe.** Her food keeps the companions from the cattle while it lasts. Odysseus makes them swear to kill no cow or sheep, “but at your ease / eat the food that immortal Circe gave” (12.301–302; see [[oath]] and [[provisions]]), “And as long as they had bread and red wine, / they kept away from the cattle, longing for life” (12.327–328). ἀθανάτη, “immortal”, is said of her only here, as the Iliad says it of Thetis, the immortal mother of Achilles (*Iliad* 10.404, 17.78; see [[immortals]]).
