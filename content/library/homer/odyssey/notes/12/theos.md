---
summary: The gods of the book: the goddesses Circe and Calypso, “a dread goddess with human voice” (12.150, 12.449), and the Sun's daughters, goddesses who herd his cattle (12.131); the gods' own name for the Wanderers (12.61); an unnamed god who will remind Odysseus (12.38) and the god who took away the companions' homecoming (12.419); and the gods as a body, “sovereign gods” against whose will the winds wreck ships (12.290), whom Odysseus and the Sun address in the same verse (12.371, 12.377).
---

**Goddesses.** Circe is “brilliant among goddesses” (12.20, 12.115, 12.143, 12.155) and “a dread goddess with human voice” when she sends the wind, as Calypso is at the end of the tale (12.150, 12.449; see [[dread-goddess]], [[with-human-voice]] and [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]). The herders of the Sun's cattle are goddesses too, “And goddesses are their herders, / nymphs of the lovely tresses, Phaethusa and Lampetie” (12.131–132; see [[phaethusa]], [[lampetie]] and [[nymphs]]).

**A god.** Circe promises that “a god himself will remind you” (12.38), some kindly power, no special god (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.38); Scylla is a sight that would gladden no one, “not even if a god should meet her” (12.88; see [[scylla]]); Thrinacia is “the flawless island of the god” (12.261) and its cattle are those “of a dread god” (12.322; see [[helios]]). At the wreck it is “the god” who takes away the companions' homecoming (12.419; see [[nostos]]), Zeus, who has just thundered (12.415).

**The gods together.** The Wanderers have their name from the gods, “as the blessed gods call them” (12.61; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Eurylochus fears the winds “which most of all / shatter ships, against the will of the sovereign gods” (θεῶν ἀέκητι ἀνάκτων, 12.289–290), the only place in Homer where the gods together are called ἄνακτες, probably no more than a graphic way of saying how wild the winds are (Merry on 12.290; see [[anax]]). He imagines the other gods following the Sun's lead (12.349), and Odysseus and the Sun both call on “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever” (12.371, 12.377; see [[prayer]]). The last act of the gods in the tale is a kindness: “the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia” (12.448; see [[divine-intervention]]).
