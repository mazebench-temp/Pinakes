# The entry claims Κράταιιν (12.124). Unnamed: Scylla's mother, who bore her and will stop her (12.125-126).

Odysseus has asked Circe whether he might ward Scylla off, and she has told him that Scylla is an immortal evil, not to be fought, and that if he lingers to arm himself she will take as many men again (12.112–123; see [[scylla]]). Her last word is the mother:

{{quote:b3fa7a4f-103e-502b-8c17-5b2d4292a4d0}}

βωστρεῖν, “call on”, is a loud cry, formed as if from βωστής for βοητής, “shouter”, from βοάω, “shout”, as ἐλαστρέω beside ἐλάω (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.124). ἀποπαύσει takes the infinitive, “she will stop her from rushing out”, as in ἀλητεύειν ἀπέπαυσας, “you have stopped this insatiable fellow from wandering”, said of Irus (18.114, a plain rendering; Merry on 12.126). The scholia preferred to write κραταιίς, an adverb, “mightily”, instead of the name (Merry's critical note on 12.124), and a scholiast reports that the three verses were athetized because they imply that Scylla was not fixed to her rock (Merry's critical note on 12.124–126; see the translation's note on 12.124–126).

Odysseus never calls on her. He says nothing of Scylla to his companions and arms himself against Circe's word (12.223–231). When he passes the crag again after the wreck, it is Zeus who keeps Scylla from seeing him (12.445–446), and Merry, who would rather read that Zeus did not let Odysseus see Scylla again, notes that this would explain why Zeus is his protector there, when Circe had bidden him call on Crataeis (on 12.445–446).
