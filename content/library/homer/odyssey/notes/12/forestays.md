summary: In the storm after Thrinacia the West Wind breaks both forestays, and the mast falls backward (12.407–410). The backstay of oxhide, thrown over the mast when the surge snaps it off at the keel, is the rope with which Odysseus lashes mast and keel together (12.422–424).

# backstay, ἐπίτονος (12.423, the only place in Homer), which in later Greek is also an adjective, "strained, intense".

**Both forestays.** “And she ran on for no long time; for at once there came / the screaming West Wind, raging with a great storm, / and the storm of wind broke the forestays of the mast, / both of them; and the mast fell backward, and all the tackle / was spilled into the bilge” (12.407–411; see [[mast]], [[zephyrus]] and [[ships]]). Two forestays went from the masthead to the bows, one made fast on each side, and the backstay to the stern, so that the strain on the mast was divided between three ropes; had one forestay snapped, the other and the backstay would have kept the mast from falling clean aft, “but both broke at once, ἀμφοτέρους being emphatic from its position in the verse” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.409). This verse is the evidence that the forestays were two, and there were two forward because the mast-box was open toward the stern, so that the mast would rather fall that way, while one rope abaft was enough to check it forward (Merry, Appendix I §15; see [[mast-box]]). The falling mast strikes the helmsman at the stern (12.411–414; see [[steering-oar]]).

**The backstay.** The ἐπίτονος, “made of oxhide”, ran from the masthead to the stern, Merry explains, and Hesychius, as he reports, calls it the hide by which a ship's mast is held fast; the Suda's ἐπίτονος is instead ὁ δεσμεύων ἱμὰς πρὸς τὸν ἱστὸν τὸ κέρας, “the strap that binds the yard to the mast” (Suda ε 2713), as Merry says of Suidas (Appendix I §15). When the surge snapped the mast off at the keel, the backstay “had been flung” over it, and with it Odysseus lashed mast and keel together and rode on them (12.422–425; see [[keel]]). ἐπίτονος opens its verse with a short syllable, a στίχος ἀκέφαλος, “headless verse”; τετευχώς, “made”, is the only perfect active of τεύχω with a passive sense; and Athenaeus quotes the line with τετάνυστο for βέβλητο (Merry on 12.423; see the translation's note on 12.420–423).
