---
summary: The book begins as the ship leaves the river Oceanus for “the wave of the wide-wayed sea” (12.1–2). In Circe's account the sea is full of perils, the waves that sweep away ships at the Wanderers and Charybdis sucking down the salt water; it falls calm by the Sirens' island; and after Thrinacia it is all that is left, “only sky and sea”, until the storm takes the ship (12.403–419). Odysseus sums up his wanderings as “searching out the paths of the sea” (12.259).
---

**From the river to the sea.** “And when the ship had left the stream of the river Oceanus / and had come to the wave of the wide-wayed sea” (12.1–2; see [[oceanus]], [[rivers]] and [[wide-wayed]]): εὐρυπόροιο marks “the great and wide sea, as distinguished from the narrow Ocean-stream” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.2). Circe promises to show the way so that they may not, “either on sea or on land, suffer pain and meet with bane” (12.26–27; see [[earth]]), the pair in which she had summed up their wanderings in Book 10, the sorrows on the fish-filled sea and the harm done to them on land (10.457–459; see the Book 10 note).

**Circe's sea.** At the Wanderers “roars the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite”, and no ship that comes there escapes: “the planks of ships and the bodies of men together / the waves of the sea and the storms of deadly fire sweep away” (12.59–68; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[amphitrite]]). Scylla fishes from her cave for dolphins and dogfish and any greater sea monster, “of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers” (12.95–97; see [[scylla]] and [[sea-beasts]]), and three times a day Charybdis sucks down the black water and sends it up again (12.104–106; see [[charybdis]]). When the ship comes to the strait, Charybdis “terribly sucked down the salt water of the sea”, and when she swallowed it the earth appeared beneath (12.235–243; see [[earth]]).

**Calm and breakers.** As the ship nears the Sirens' island, “the wind ceased, and there was a calm, / a windless calm, and a god lulled the waves to sleep”; the men furl the sails and make “the water white with their polished oars of fir” (12.168–172; see [[sirens]] and [[oars]]). Before Scylla, Odysseus bids them keep striking “the deep breakers of the sea” (12.214). ῥηγμῖνα is found only here; elsewhere the word is the edge of the sea, ἐπὶ ῥηγμῖνι θαλάσσης, “where the sea breaks upon the shore” (12.6; see [[shore]]). Merry finds it best described by the wave of the Iliad's simile that “breaks upon the land and roars loud” (*Iliad* 4.425), which is why it can be called “deep” (on 4.430).

**The sea and the island.** On Thrinacia, Eurylochus fears the winds of the night that shatter ships if they wander “on the misty sea” (12.285–290; see [[misty]] and [[the-winds]]), and later he chooses the sea's death over the island's: “I would rather lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave / than be slowly drained away on a desolate island” (12.350–351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). In the storm of the first night Zeus covers “land and sea alike” (12.315), and he promises the Sun to shatter the ship “in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (12.388; see [[wine-dark]] and [[zeus]]). When the ship leaves the island, “no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea”, and the son of Cronus sets a dark cloud above it, “and the sea grew dark beneath it” (12.403–406; see [[sky]]); the companions fall out of the ship, and “like sea crows around the black ship / they were carried on the waves” (12.417–419; see [[thrinacia]]). The four verses of the open sea and the cloud come back, with Crete for the island, in the false tale of Book 14 (14.301–304).

**The paths of the sea.** Odysseus measures what he has suffered by the sea itself. Scylla's six victims were “the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (12.258–259). πόρους ἁλός, “the paths of the sea”, is found only here; elsewhere in Homer πόρος is the ford of a river (*Iliad* 2.592, 14.433). Merry compares it with εὐρύπορος, “wide-wayed”, the epithet of the sea in the book's second verse, and cites πόροι ἁλίρροθοι, the “surging paths” of Aeschylus' *Persians* (on 4.432). In the last verses of the tale Odysseus is borne over the sea alone for nine days, until the gods bring him to Ogygia (12.447–448; see [[ogygia]]).
