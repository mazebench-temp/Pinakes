summary: Odysseus asks Circe to tell him “truly” how to pass the strait (12.112), and tells the Phaeacians how he knows what the Sun and Zeus said among the gods: he heard it from Calypso, who said she had heard it herself from Hermes (12.389–390). A scholion calls this a lie, since Calypso had not yet seen Hermes; W. W. Merry answers that the falsehood, if any, is Calypso's.
refs: [12.112, 12.389-390]
---

**A question.** “Come now, goddess, tell me this truly” (νημερτὲς ἐνίσπες, 12.112; see [[circe]]): Odysseus asks for the plain truth, and Circe gives it, even the loss of six men (12.109–110; see [[scylla]]).

**A source.** The council of the gods is the one scene in the tale that Odysseus could not have witnessed: the Sun's demand, his threat to shine among the dead, and Zeus' promise of the thunderbolt (12.374–388; see [[helios]] and [[zeus]]). He names his source at once: “This I heard from Calypso of the lovely hair; / and she said she had heard it herself from Hermes the guide” (12.389–390; see [[calypso]] and [[hermes]]). In the pause of the tale Alcinous had praised him as no deceiver, “fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see” (11.366), and here he tells a thing no mortal could see, and says how he came to know it. Eustathius took the verses for the poet's answer to the question how Odysseus could know; Merry thinks such a defense points to too punctilious a literary criticism for the period (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.389). A scholion on 5.79 says that Odysseus lies here, since Calypso had not yet seen Hermes when the companions were lost; Merry replies that the falsehood rather rests with Calypso (his critical note on 12.389–390; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.389–390). When Hermes does come to Ogygia in Book 5, she knows him at once, “for the gods are not unknown to one another” (5.79; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]] and [[the-gods-know-all-things]]).
