summary: Two of the wonders that Horace says Homer keeps for later, Scylla and Charybdis, are this book's, and his verse sets the Cyclops beside them, as Odysseus himself remembers the Cyclops' cave at the strait. The Sirens' voices that the Epistles name are the song Odysseus heard bound to the mast, while his companions, their ears stopped with wax, heard nothing.
refs: [12.85-110, 12.173-200, 12.208-212, 12.234-259]
---

**Scylla and Charybdis.** Horace's list of the striking wonders that come after Homer's modest beginning, “Antiphates and Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” (*Antiphaten Scyllamque et cum Cyclope Charybdim*, *Ars Poetica* 145), ends with the two dangers of this book, which Circe describes and the ship passes (12.85–110, 12.234–259; see [[scylla]], [[charybdis]] and [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]]). The verse joins Charybdis to the Cyclops. In the book the Cyclops comes back only in Odysseus' memory, when the oars fly from the rowers' hands at the sight of smoke and a great wave and the sound of a roar, and he goes from man to man before the strait: “Friends, we are not without knowledge of evils at all; / surely this evil that besets us is no greater than when the Cyclops / penned us in his hollow cave with his mighty strength” (12.201–210; see [[not-without-knowledge-of-evils]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The danger he is about to meet is Scylla, of whom he says nothing to them (12.223–225).

**The Sirens' voices.** In the *Epistles* Horace reminds Lollius, “you know the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups”, and goes on to the cups, which would have made Ulysses base and senseless had he drunk them with his foolish and greedy companions (*Sirenum voces et Circae pocula nosti*, *Epistles* 1.2.23–26; see the Book 10 note). The voices are this book's. Circe tells Odysseus how to hear them and live (12.47–54), and he does as she says: he stops his companions' ears with wax and is bound upright in the mast-step, and when the song makes his heart wish to listen and he nods to them to set him free, Perimedes and Eurylochus bind him tighter (12.173–196; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]], [[the-sirens-in-later-writers]] and [[perimedes-the-companion]]). The companions are not tempted, for they hear nothing; the one man who hears the Sirens is held by the ropes.
