summary: Circe names the island again and says what grazes on it, the cattle and sheep of the Sun, seven herds and seven flocks of fifty, tended by his daughters (12.127–141). After Scylla and Charybdis the ship reaches “the flawless island of the god” at evening; Eurylochus forces a landing, contrary winds hold them there for a month, and when at last they leave it “no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (12.260–404).

**Circe's warning.** Circe names Thrinacia last among the dangers of the voyage, after the strait:

{{quote:3aad2485-debf-5df1-84bf-7270060f03ad}}

τηλόθι, “far off”, joined with ἀπῴκισε, “she settled them”, seems to mean far from the place where the daughters were born; it may be only a way of saying that the island lay in the far west, or the word may go with ναίειν, “to dwell far off” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.135; see [[phaethusa]], [[lampetie]], [[neaera]] and [[helios]]). The warning that follows is Teiresias' own, in his verses: if the herds are left unharmed, the companions may still reach Ithaca, though suffering evils; if they are harmed, Odysseus will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions (12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[teiresias]], [[ithaca]] and the Book 11 note). Most manuscripts omit the last two verses here or write them in the margin (Merry's critical note on 12.140–141; see the translation's note on 12.140–141).

**The island of the god.** When they have passed the strait, “then at once we came to the flawless island of the god; / and there were the fine broad-browed cattle, / and many fat sheep of Hyperion the Sun” (12.261–263; see [[blameless]]). No other island in Homer is ἀμύμων, “flawless”. Still at sea, Odysseus hears “the lowing of the cattle being stalled for the night / and the bleating of the sheep” (12.265–266): αὐλιζομενάων, “being housed for the night”, since it was now evening (Merry on 12.265). The sound brings back the warnings of Teiresias and of Circe, “who charged me again and again / to avoid the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (12.266–269; see [[the-blind-seer]] and [[aeaea]]). τερψίμβροτος, “who gladdens mortals”, is said of the Sun only in this verse and its repetition (12.269, 12.274); Schol. H. reports the variant φαεσιμβρότου, “who brings light to mortals”, his epithet when Circe's parents are named (10.138; Merry's critical note on 12.269). Odysseus tells the companions that there, Circe said, “was the most dread evil for us” (12.275): ἔμμεναι, not ἔσεσθαι, Merry notes, since the evil already lay there in the shape of the herds of Helios (on 12.275); and he bids them “drive the black ship on past the island” (12.276).

**The landing.** Eurylochus answers for the weary men: Odysseus will not let them set foot on land, “where once more / on the sea-washed island we might make ready a pleasant supper” (12.282–283; see [[eurylochus]] and [[sea-washed]]), the words in which Athena described Calypso's island (1.50; see [[ogygia]]). Odysseus makes them swear to kill no cow or sheep (12.297–304; see [[oath]]), and they moor the ship “in the hollow harbor / near sweet water” (12.305–306; see [[harbor]]). In the night Zeus sends a storm, and at dawn they drag the ship into a hollow cave: “There were the fair dancing floors and seats of the nymphs” (12.316–318; see [[dance]]). The cattle are the Sun's delight, as he goes up into the starry sky and turns back again toward the earth (12.379–381; see [[sky]]).

**A month on the island.** For a whole month the South Wind blows without ceasing, with only the East Wind besides (12.325–326; see [[notus]] and [[eurus]]). While the bread and wine from Aeaea last, “the food that immortal Circe gave”, the companions keep away from the cattle; when the provisions are spent, they roam the island for fish and birds (12.302, 12.327–332; see [[provisions]]). Odysseus goes “up the island” to pray, ἀνὰ νῆσον, as Circe had gone up her own island from the shore at dawn (12.333, 12.143; Merry on 12.143), and, “going through the island”, gets away from his companions to a place sheltered from the wind (12.335–336; see [[prayer]]). While he sleeps, Eurylochus says that he would rather die at once in the sea “than be slowly drained away on a desolate island” (12.351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]): νῆσος ἐρήμη is also the desert island where Aegisthus left Agamemnon's singer “to become the prey and spoil of birds” (3.270–271; see [[aegisthus]]). The cattle are grazing “not far from the dark-prowed ship” (12.354), and an oak gives the leaves that stand for barley in the sacrifice (12.357; see [[sacrifice]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**Leaving it.** On the seventh day the wind drops and they put out to sea: “But when we were leaving the island, and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (12.403–404; see [[sea]]). The first half-verse is the one with which they left the Sirens' island (12.201), and the verses come back, with Crete for the island, in the false tale Odysseus tells Eumaeus (14.301–304; see the translation's note on 12.403).
