summary: Circe tells Odysseus to knead honey-sweet wax and anoint his companions' ears with it as they row past the Sirens (12.47–49). When the wind drops he cuts a great round of wax into pieces with the bronze, presses it until it grows warm in his hands and the sun, and anoints their ears, and when the song can no longer be heard they take the wax out (12.173–177, 12.199–200).

**Circe's counsel.** “But row on past, and anoint the ears of your companions, / kneading honey-sweet wax, so that none of the others / may hear” (12.47–49; see [[sirens]], [[circe]] and [[honey-sweet]]). The infinitives παρεξελάαν and ἀλεῖψαι have the force of commands (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.47); ἐπὶ … ἀλεῖψαι is ἀλείφω, “anoint”, with ἐπί, “on”, as at 177 and 200, and δεψέω is “knead” (see the translation's row δεψέω and its note on 12.47). Odysseus alone may hear, bound to the mast (12.49–54; see [[mast]]).

**The round of wax.** When the wind has dropped near the Sirens' island and the companions have taken to their oars, Odysseus does as he was told:

{{quote:38246904-6d54-5026-ae7e-a8b449c88bb0}}

τυτθά, “into small pieces”, is a predicate of the result, found in the plural only here and at 388, where Zeus will shatter the ship “to pieces” (Merry on 12.174; see [[thunderbolt]]). The sharp bronze that cuts it is the blade of the formula (see [[sharp]] and [[bronze]]). κέλετο, “bade”, is said of persons everywhere else in Homer, and nowhere else of an outside force; Nitzsch would cut 176 as superfluous, since either the pressing or the warmth would do, and objected to Ὑπεριονίδης, “son of Hyperion”, as un-Homeric. Merry answers that scrapings of wax soften far more readily when squeezed and kneaded in full sunlight, that the hot sun suits the windless calm of 168–169, and that “There seems to be a conscious touch of humour in the use of such majestic language to describe the kneading of a cake of wax” (on 12.175; see the translation's note on 12.175–176 and [[hyperion]]).

**Taken out.** Bound, Odysseus can signal only with his brows, “It was no good to speak”, Merry says (on 12.194; see [[brows]]); and “when they had rowed past them, and we could no longer / hear the voice of the Sirens or their song, / at once my trusty companions took away the wax / with which I had anointed their ears, and freed me from my bonds” (12.197–200; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]).
