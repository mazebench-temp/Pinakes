---
summary: Amphitrite is the sea twice in Circe's warnings: at the Wanderers “roars the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite” (12.60), and Scylla fishes for dolphins and dogfish and any greater sea monster, “of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers” (12.97). Each verse gives her an epithet found nowhere else in Homer.
# The entry's stem claims Ἀμφιτρίτης (12.60) and Ἀμφιτρίτη (12.97).
---

**The wave of Amphitrite.** Of the first of the two ways Circe says, “On the one side are overhanging rocks, and against them / roars the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite; / the Wanderers, as the blessed gods call them” (12.59–61; see [[the-wanderers]]). The waves are hers, as in Nestor's “or on the open sea among the waves of Amphitrite” (3.91; see the Book 3 note), and the great wave roars, ῥοχθεῖ, as it roared against the dry land of Scheria when Odysseus swam for the shore (5.402). κυανῶπις, “dark-eyed”, from κύανος, the dark blue enamel, and ὤψ, “eye”, is found only here in Homer (see the general article).

**The feeder of sea monsters.** Scylla holds her heads out of her cave, “and there she fishes, questing round the crag, / for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster, of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers” (12.95–97; see [[scylla]], [[sea-beasts]] and [[the-fisherman]]). It is the sea that rears the monsters, as when Odysseus off Scheria feared “some great sea monster … / out of the sea, such as famous Amphitrite breeds in great numbers” (5.421–422; see the Book 5 note). ἀγάστονος, “loud-moaning”, from ἀγα-, “very”, and στένω, “groan”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row), and the *Hymn to Apollo* gives it to her again, among the goddesses who were with Leto on Delos (*Hymn to Apollo* 94; see the general article). In both verses W. W. Merry's judgment of her in Book 3 holds: so far as she is personified in Homer, she is the sea itself rather than a goddess (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.91).
