**The cauldron.** ἦ τοι ὅτʼ ἐξεμέσειε, λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ / πᾶσʼ ἀναμορμύρεσκε κυκωμένη, ὑψόσε δʼ ἄχνη / ἄκροισι σκοπέλοισιν ἐπʼ ἀμφοτέροισιν ἔπιπτεν: “Truly, whenever she vomited it up, like a cauldron on a great fire / she would seethe up, all of her, churning, and high overhead the foam / fell on the tops of both crags” (12.237–239; see [[charybdis]] and the translation's rows λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ and ἀναμορμύρω; κυκάω (of the sea); ἀναβρόχω). λέβης is the bronze cauldron of the hearth (see [[cauldron]]), and ὥς follows its noun. ἀναμορμύρεσκε, “would seethe up”, an iterative found only here, is the better reading, W. W. Merry holds, against ἀνεμορμύρεσκε, since the iterative aorists are seldom found with the augment (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 12.238). He renders “now whenever she vomited it forth, like a cauldron on a big fire, she seethed up swirling from her inmost depth (πᾶσα), and the spray fell aloft on the tops of the two cliffs”, that is, the rock of Scylla and the rock of Charybdis, and draws the scene: when the maelstrom was discharging its waters “they seemed to come boiling and bursting up from below, with a roar like thunder and amid clouds of spray”, and when the sea was being sucked in, “one might look down into the whirling gulf as into a monstrous funnel”, with the sea floor visible far below (on 12.237, comparing Tibullus 4.1.73).

**Out and in.** The likeness belongs to one half of the whirlpool's motion. When she spews out the sea she seethes like a cauldron; when she swallows it, ἀναβρόξειε, “all within her appeared, churning, and around her the rock / roared terribly, and beneath, the earth appeared, / dark with sand” (12.240–243). Both halves are κυκωμένη, “churning”. The same verb, ἐξεμέσειε, “vomit up”, is used when she gives back the mast and keel to Odysseus at the end of the book (12.437; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]).

**From the river to the whirlpool.** In the Iliad the river Xanthus, burned by Hephaestus, bubbles up “As a cauldron boils within, pressed by a great fire, / as it melts the fat of a tender-fed hog” (*Iliad* 21.362–363; see the entry and [[scamander]]). The Odyssey keeps the cauldron and the great fire, πυρὶ πολλῷ, and leaves out the fat and the dry sticks beneath it; it keeps too the river's words, for the Xanthus rushed on Achilles κυκώμενος, “churning”, and μορμύρων, “seething” with foam (*Iliad* 21.235, 21.324–325). In the Iliad the fire is real and the river truly boils; at Charybdis there is no fire, and the cauldron is only the likeness of a sea that heaves up from below. Pale fear takes the companions as they watch, and while they look at Charybdis Scylla seizes six of them from the ship (12.243–246; see [[scylla]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).
