---
summary: Before Scylla, Odysseus lets “the grievous command of Circe” slip from his mind, puts on his armor and takes two spears onto the deck at the prow (12.226–231).
---

“And then I let the grievous command of Circe / slip from my mind, since she had told me not to arm myself” (Κίρκης μὲν ἐφημοσύνης ἀλεγεινῆς / λανθανόμην, 12.226–227; see [[circe]]). Circe had told him that against Scylla “there is no help; the strongest course is to flee from her” (12.120; see [[alke]]). ἀλεγεινῆς is “hard” to W. W. Merry, because a fighting man chafes at passive endurance, and λανθανόμην is “I let myself forget”, that is, disregarded; the negative before ἀνώγει passes over to θωρήσσεσθαι, “bade me not to arm” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.226–227; see the Odyssey translation's note on 12.226–227). He puts on his glorious armor, takes two long spears and goes onto the deck at the prow, where he expects her to appear first; but his eyes grow weary searching the misty rock, and he cannot catch sight of her anywhere (12.228–233; see [[deck]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). The command he neglects was meant to save him the futile effort; Antilochus in the Iliad, though struck speechless by the news of Patroclus' death, “did not neglect the command of Menelaus” (*Iliad* 17.697; see the entry).
