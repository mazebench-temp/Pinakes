---
summary: The nymphs of Book 12 are the Sun's daughters, the goddesses who herd his cattle on Thrinacia, “nymphs of the lovely tresses, Phaethusa and Lampetie” (12.132), and the unnamed nymphs whose “fair dancing floors and seats” are in the cave on the island where the ship is anchored (12.318).
# The entry claims νύμφαι (12.132) and νυμφέων (12.318), the Ionic genitive, found only there in Homer.
---

**The daughters of the Sun.** Circe tells Odysseus who keeps the herds of the Sun: “And goddesses are their herders, / nymphs of the lovely tresses, Phaethusa and Lampetie, / whom brilliant Neaera bore to Hyperion the Sun” (12.131–133; see [[phaethusa]], [[lampetie]], [[neaera]], [[helios]] and [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Homer's nymphs are usually “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus”, the nymphs of the mountains in the Iliad and those who roused the goats on the island of the Cyclopes (*Iliad* 6.420; 9.154; see the Book 9 note and the general article). These two have a father of their own, as Calypso is “the daughter of baleful Atlas” (1.52; see [[calypso]]) and the nymph Thoosa the daughter of Phorcys (1.71–72; see [[thoosa]]). W. W. Merry calls them “Bright” and “Shining”, and their mother “Younger”, “fit wife and daughters for the Sun” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.132).

**The dancing floors.** After the storm on Thrinacia, “when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, / we anchored the ship, dragging it into a hollow cave. / There were the fair dancing floors and seats of the nymphs” (12.316–318; see [[thrinacia]]). νυμφέων is the Ionic genitive, found only here in Homer, where the other books have νυμφάων (6.123, 13.104; *Iliad* 20.8, 24.616); Hesiod has it in the mountains, “the graceful haunts of the goddess nymphs” (*Theogony* 129–130; see the general article and [[hesiod]]). The poem does not say who these nymphs are, or that the companions saw them. χοροί, “dancing floors”, are also Dawn's on Aeaea, for which Merry imagines a train of nymphs (12.4; Merry on 12.4; see [[eos]]); and the cave with its seats is like the cave sacred to the Naiads at the head of the harbor of Ithaca, with its stone bowls and looms where the nymphs weave (13.102–112; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]], [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[ithaca]]).
