---
summary: Penelope remembers Odysseus as the man “whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.344).
---

Penelope asks Phemius to stop his song of the Achaeans' grim homecoming: “Such a head do I long for, remembering always / the man whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.343–344; see [[penelope]], [[kleos]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). The verse returns twice in Book 4 (4.726, 4.816), and Menelaus offers Telemachus a journey “through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (15.80).

**Hellas and Argos.** Merry takes καθʼ Ἑλλάδα καὶ μέσον Ἄργος for a phrase meaning the whole of Greece. Ἑλλάς is properly a town of Phthiotis, and then Peleus' kingdom in southern Thessaly (*Iliad* 2.683, 9.395, 9.447, 16.595); it first names all Greece in Hesiod, if that passage is genuine (*Works and Days* 651–653), and it was often used of Greece outside the Peloponnese, so that Hellas and “the heart of Argos” may name the north and the south, “like the expression from ‘Dan to Beersheba’”. Or the two towns stand for their lands, the domains of the greatest hero and the greatest king, and μέσον, “the heart of”, sets the heart of the Peloponnese against the farthest province: “His fame spread from the most outlying province of Greece to the very heart of the Peloponnese” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.344; see [[argos]]). See the translation's note on 1.344.

**In the ancient debate.** Thucydides held that Homer never uses the name Hellenes of all the Greeks (1.3.3; see [[thucydides-hellenes]]). Strabo answers with this verse and with 15.80: the poet himself shows that the Hellenes were already one people (*Geography* 14.2.28; compare 8.6.6, where others are said to cite the two verses for the same point). In another place he reports that the phrase is given as an example of the poet's way of naming a part together with the whole, as in “Dulichium and the holy Echinae”, Dulichium being one of the Echinae (*Geography* 8.3.8; see [[hellenes]] and [[dulichium]]).
