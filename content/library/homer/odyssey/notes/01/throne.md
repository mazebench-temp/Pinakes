**The guest's chair.** Telemachus “led her and seated her on a chair—spreading a cloth beneath her— / fine and intricately worked; and there was a footstool under her feet. / And beside her he set an inlaid chair for himself, apart from the others, / the suitors” (1.130–133; see [[footstool]] and [[xenia]]). The two chairs are of two kinds. The scholia distinguish the κλισμός, which had a back sloping for the shoulders to rest on, from the θρόνος, which had none; W. W. Merry concludes that if the distinction holds, the θρόνος had a straight back and the κλισμός a sloping one, and that in practice the θρόνος stood higher and is the word for a chair of state (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.132; see the translation's note on 1.132). The host takes the lower chair and gives his guest the higher.

**The cloth.** ὑπὸ λῖτα πετάσσας, “spreading a cloth beneath her”, is best taken as a parenthesis, so that “fine and intricately worked” describes the chair. λῖτα is the accusative singular of λίς, a smooth cloth, as the dative λιτί is the linen laid over Patroclus' body (*Iliad* 18.352), and Poseidon spreads a cloth over the chariot of Zeus (κατὰ λῖτα πετάσσας, *Iliad* 8.441); Wolf and Nitzsch took it for a neuter plural (Merry on 1.130; see the translation's note on 1.130).

**The suitors' seats.** The suitors come in and sit “on the chairs and the seats” (κατὰ κλισμούς τε θρόνους τε, 1.145; see [[feast-type-scene]]), the pair in which both words are rendered as seats of men; the same verse seats Nestor's guests and Laertes' household (*Odyssey* 3.389, 24.385).
