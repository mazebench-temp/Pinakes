**The son's doubt.** Asked whether he is truly the son of Odysseus, Telemachus answers with his mother's word: “My mother says that I am his; but I for my part / do not know; for no one yet has known his own parentage himself” (1.215–216; see [[telemachus]]). Merry hears in it the humor of the proverb “It's a wise child that knows its own father” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.173), and reports that the scholia set beside the verse a saying of Euripides, that a mother loves her child more than a father does, since she knows that it is hers and he only supposes it (on 1.216; see the translation's note on 1.216). Athena answers with the mother: the gods have not made his lineage nameless, “since Penelope bore such a son as you” (1.222–223; see [[penelope]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**The mother wooed.** The suitors woo her in her son's house (1.248–251), and the goddess's counsel disposes of her twice: if her heart is eager to marry, she is to go back to her father's hall (1.275–278); if Odysseus is dead, Telemachus is to “give your mother to a husband” (1.292). He addresses the suitors as “Suitors of my mother” (1.368; see [[the-suitors]]), and tells Eurymachus that he no longer heeds the oracles his mother asks of prophets (1.415–416).

**The rebuke.** When Penelope comes down in tears and asks Phemius to stop his song of the returns, her son answers her for the first time in the poem, “My mother, why do you begrudge the trusty singer / giving delight in whatever way his mind is roused?” (1.346–347), and ends by sending her back:

{{quote:44074f0e-5ddb-578c-a671-2dc98a0d8bab}}

They are the verses with which Hector sends Andromache back to her loom in the Iliad, with μῦθος, “speech”, for πόλεμος, “war”, and a new close to the last line (*Iliad* 6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]] and the translation's note on 1.356–359): a husband's words to his wife become a son's to his mother. “And she, amazed, went back into the house; / for she laid her son's wise word to heart” (1.360–361; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Merry finds the cause of her amazement in the unexpected wisdom of her young son, and on that ground doubts Aristarchus' rejection of the four verses (on 1.360).

**Other mothers.** In the council the Cyclops is named by his mother: the nymph Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys, bore Polyphemus to Poseidon in the hollow caves (1.71–73; see [[thoosa]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). At the end of the day the old nurse Eurycleia lights Telemachus to his chamber; she loved him most of the slave women, “and had reared him when he was small” (1.434–435; see [[eurycleia]]).
