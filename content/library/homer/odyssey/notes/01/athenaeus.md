---
summary: The first book of the Deipnosophistae draws on this book twice: for the simple fare that Homer gives to kings and commoners alike, which even the insolent suitors do not exceed, and for the suitors' game of game pieces, which Apion says was called Penelope.
---

- **The simple fare.** Homer, seeing that temperance is the virtue most proper to the young, gave everyone the same plain fare, kings and commoners, young and old, and Athenaeus quotes the guest's meal: “and she drew up a polished table beside them. / And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them”, and “a carver lifted up platters of meat” (1.138–139, 1.141). He passes over the line between, in which the housekeeper adds “many dainties, giving freely of what she had” (1.140). Even the suitors, insolent and given to pleasure as they are, Homer never shows eating fish or birds or honey cakes (*Deipnosophistae* 1.15, in Kaibel's chapters; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[table]]).
- **The game of Penelope.** The suitors who delighted their hearts “with game pieces in front of the doors” (1.107), and the account of their game that Apion of Alexandria says he heard from Cteson of Ithaca, with a hundred and eight pebbles in two rows and one in the middle called Penelope; Eurymachus won most often (*Deipnosophistae* 1.29, in Kaibel's chapters): [[the-game-of-penelope]] and [[game-pieces]].
