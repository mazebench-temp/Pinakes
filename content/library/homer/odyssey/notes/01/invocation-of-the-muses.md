---
summary: The Odyssey's only invocation. Its first line asks the Muse to tell of the man, and its tenth asks the goddess, daughter of Zeus, to begin where she will and tell “us too”; the poem does not call on her again.
refs: [1.10]
---

The request comes twice, at the beginning and at the end of the proem (see [[proem]]).

1. **The imperative.** ἔννεπε, “tell” (1.1), as in the Iliad's “But who was the best of them, tell me, Muse” (*Iliad* 2.761), and at the end εἰπέ, “tell” (1.10).
2. **The address.** μοῦσα, “Muse” (1.1), and then θεά, θύγατερ Διός, “goddess, daughter of Zeus” (1.10), as the Muses of the Catalogue are daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus (*Iliad* 2.491–492; see [[muses]]).
3. **The question.** Not who or what came first, as in the Iliad, but where to begin: ἁμόθεν, “beginning where you will” (1.10). The choice is left to the Muse, and the telling is asked for “us too”, the poet and his hearers (see [[muses]]).

The Iliad calls on the Muses again before the Catalogue and at the turning points of the battle. The Odyssey's vocative μοῦσα stands only in its first line, and later the Muse is spoken of, not to: she loved the Phaeacian singer Demodocus and gave him both good and evil, taking his sight and giving him sweet song (8.63–64), and Odysseus says that the Muse, daughter of Zeus, or Apollo must have taught him (8.488). In Book 1 the singer Phemius strikes up his song without any appeal (1.155), and Penelope tells him that he knows many enchantments, “deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous” (1.338; see [[phemius]]).
