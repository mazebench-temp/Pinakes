---
summary: Horace turns the opening of this book into Latin twice, in the Ars Poetica as the model of a modest beginning and in the Epistles as the example of Ulysses' virtue and wisdom, and in the same letter makes himself and his friends the suitors of Penelope.
---

- *Dic mihi, Musa, virum, captae post tempora Troiae / qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes*, the modest beginning set against the cyclic poet's boast, light from smoke (*Ars Poetica* 136–145, on 1.1–3): [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]] and [[proem]].
- The listener snatched into the middle of things (*Ars Poetica* 146–152), which the Odyssey does by beginning in the last year of the return (on 1.10–21): [[in-medias-res]].
- Ulysses, tamer of Troy, who looked on the cities and the ways of many men and endured hardships on the sea while winning a return for himself and his companions (*Epistles* 1.2.17–22, on 1.2–5): [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]].
- “The suitors of Penelope, good-for-nothings”, born to consume the fruits of the earth (*Epistles* 1.2.27–31, on the suitors' feasting in this book): [[the-suitors]].
- *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum*, the first line of the Latin Odyssey of Livius Andronicus, whose poems Horace was taught as a boy (*Epistles* 2.1.69–72): [[odusia]].
