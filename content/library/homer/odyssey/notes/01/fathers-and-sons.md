---
summary: Telemachus is a son who has grown up without his father: “My mother says that I am his; but I for my part / do not know” (1.215–216). The goddess finds the father in his face (1.207–209), holds up Orestes, who avenged his father (1.298–302), and speaks to him “as a father to his son” (1.308); she leaves him thinking of his father “even more than before” (1.321–322).

**The son who does not know.** Asked whether he is truly Odysseus' son, Telemachus answers with a doubt in which Merry hears the naive humour of his question whether the stranger came on foot (1.173), and gives it the English proverb, “It's a wise child that knows its own father” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.173); the scholia, he reports, set beside the line sayings of Euripides and Menander on a man's not knowing who his father was (on 1.216; see the translation's note on 1.216). ἑὸν γόνον is “his parentage” (Merry on 1.216; see [[genee]]). He would rather have been the son “of some blessed man / whom old age overtook among his own possessions” than of “the most ill-starred of mortal men” (1.217–219; see [[geron]]).

**The likeness.** The goddess answers with his looks, “Terribly like him are you, in your head and your fine eyes” (1.208), and with his mother: “The gods have not made your lineage nameless for the time to come, / since Penelope bore such a son as you” (1.222–223; see [[eidos]]). Merry compares αἰνῶς of the startling likeness of Helen to the goddesses (*Iliad* 3.158; on 1.208).

**Orestes.** Her example is a son who avenged his father: “Or have you not heard what fame brilliant Orestes won / among all men, when he killed his father's slayer, / scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father?” (1.298–300). It is the story Zeus told in the council from the gods' side (1.29–43), told now to a son (see [[orestes]] and [[kleos]]). Merry reports that Nauck marked 1.293–302 as spurious, and Hentze–Ameis with him, as inconsistent with the advice of 1.274–278 (on 1.293; see the translation's note on 1.293–302).

**As a father to his son.** Telemachus, who sat among the suitors “seeing in his mind his brave father” (1.115), thanks the stranger for speaking “in friendship, / as a father to his son” (1.307–308), and when she has gone she has made him think of his father “even more than before” (1.321–322). That evening Antinous measures him by his father too, praying that Zeus may never make him king in Ithaca, “which is yours by birth from your father” (1.386–387; see [[basileus]]).

**Three generations.** Laertes, “the old man”, lives far away in the country (1.188–193; see [[laertes]]); Odysseus is gone; the son is left in the house. On Olympus, Athena pleads for Odysseus with “Our father, son of Cronus” (1.45, 1.81; see [[zeus]]).
