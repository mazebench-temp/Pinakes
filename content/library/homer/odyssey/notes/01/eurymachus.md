summary: Eurymachus answers Telemachus after Antinous, and courteously: who will be king of the Achaeans in Ithaca lies on the knees of the gods, but may Telemachus keep his possessions. Then he asks about the stranger who left so quickly, and Telemachus tells him that his father's homecoming is lost and that the guest was Mentes of Taphos, though in his heart he knows the goddess.
# Unnamed: his speech (1.400-411) and Telemachus' answer to him (1.414-419).
refs: [1.400-411, 1.414-419]
passages:
  - 1.399-420 | Eurymachus and Telemachus
---

**The courteous suitor.** He is brought on by his father's name, “Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face” (1.399; see [[polybus-father-of-eurymachus]]), and leaves the question of the kingship where Athena left Odysseus' return, with the half-verse of the knees of the gods: “Telemachus, truly these things lie on the knees of the gods, / who will be king of the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca” (1.400–401; 1.267; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). He wishes Telemachus to keep his possessions and rule his own house, “while Ithaca is still inhabited” (1.402–404), and calls him φέριστε, “good sir” (1.405; see [[good-sir]]).

**The stranger.** His questions are a host's questions about a guest: where the man comes from, from what land he claims to be, where his birthplace and his native fields are, and whether he brought news of Odysseus' coming or came on business of his own (1.405–409; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). “How he sprang up and was gone at once, and did not wait / to be known; for he was not like a base man to look on” (1.410–411). Merry takes γνώμεναι as “for one to know him”, the subject left unexpressed (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.411).

**The answer.** Telemachus tells him that his father's homecoming is lost, that he no longer trusts in news, “nor do I heed any oracle that my mother / may ask of a prophet when she has called him to the hall” (1.413–416; see [[divination]]), and gives him the stranger's own account of himself: Mentes, son of Anchialus, lord of the Taphians, a guest-friend from his father's time (1.417–419; see [[mentes-the-taphian]] and [[taphos]]). “So Telemachus spoke, but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (1.420). In the assembly of the next day Eurymachus dismisses the prophecy of Halitherses with Telemachus' own words: “nor do we heed the oracle” (2.201; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).
