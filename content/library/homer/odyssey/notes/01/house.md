summary: The house of Odysseus on Ithaca is the scene of the book from Athena's arrival to the night, from the doorway and the courtyard where the suitors play to the hall where they feast, the women's rooms and the upper room, and the chamber in the courtyard where Telemachus lies awake. Telemachus and the goddess speak of it as a house being eaten up.

**The house of Odysseus.** Athena comes down from Olympus and stands “at the doorway of Odysseus, / on the threshold of the courtyard” (1.103–104), “in the outer porch at the threshold of the court”, as W. W. Merry renders it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.103; see [[door]] and [[threshold]]). The suitors are playing in front of the doors (1.107; see [[game-pieces]]). Telemachus brings the stranger “inside the high house” (1.126), stands her spear against a tall pillar in the rack where the spears of Odysseus stand, and seats her apart from the suitors (1.127–135; see [[spear-rack]] and [[throne]]). The hall is “strongly made” (πύκα ποιητοῖο, 1.333) and “shadowy” (μέγαρα σκιόεντα, 1.365), an epithet, Merry notes, for the gloom of large rooms lit only through narrow openings (Merry on 1.365; see [[shadowy-mountains]]).

**A house being eaten.** Telemachus, sitting among the suitors, sees his father in his mind making “a scattering of the suitors here through the house” and ruling “over his own house” (1.115–117). He tells the stranger that “this house was once likely to be rich and flawless, / while that man was still at home” (1.232–233), but now the best men of the islands “woo my mother and wear down my house”, “and with their eating they are wasting away / my house” (1.248–251; see [[the-suitors]]). Athena bids him consider “how you may drive the suitors out of the hall” (1.270), and, if his father is dead, “how you may kill the suitors in your halls” (1.295; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). Telemachus tells the suitors to go and feast at their own cost, “eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house” (1.375), the shared feast (ἔρανος) held by each house in turn, which the stranger said this feast was not (1.226; see [[feast]] and the translation's note on 1.226), or else, “unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (1.380; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). The kingship of Ithaca he would leave to one of the other kings of the Achaeans, but he claims the house: “but I will be lord of our own house / and of the slaves whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil for me” (1.396–398), and Eurymachus grants it, “may you keep your possessions yourself and rule over your own house” (1.402; see [[possessions]] and [[basileus]]). A king's house grows rich, as Telemachus says: “at once his house / grows rich” (1.392–393).

**The women's rooms.** Penelope hears the song from her upper room and comes down “the high stairway of her house” (1.328–330; see [[upper-room]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Telemachus sends her back: “But go into the house and attend to your own work” (1.356). The οἶκος here is not the whole house but the women's quarters, the sitting room of the mistress and her maids behind the hall, from which there was a way up to the upper room (Merry on 1.356); “she, amazed, went back into the house” (1.360) and up to her room (1.362).

**The chamber in the courtyard.** At night Telemachus goes to “his chamber in the beautiful courtyard”, which “had been built high, in a place with a view all round” (1.425–426; see [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]): Merry would place it at the corner of the colonnade that ran along the courtyard (Merry on 1.425). Eurycleia lights him to it, and he opens “the doors of the strongly made chamber” (1.428–436; see [[torches]], [[door]] and [[eurycleia]]). θάλαμος, the chamber, the innermost room of a house, αὐλή, the courtyard, and μέγαρον, the hall, are the parts of the house through which the book moves.

**Other houses.** The gods meet “in the halls of Olympian Zeus” (1.27; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and on her island a goddess “has her dwelling” (1.51; see [[calypso]]). The stranger first saw Odysseus “in our house, drinking and taking his delight” (1.258), and would send Penelope back “to the hall of her father, a man of great power” (1.276; see [[icarius]]). The suitors, at evening, go “each to his own house” (1.424), as the gods go home at the end of the Iliad's first book (*Iliad* 1.606).
