---
summary: Zeus says that “from Orestes vengeance will come for the son of Atreus” (τίσις, 1.40), and Aegisthus “has paid back all at once” (1.43). Athena leaves it to the gods whether Odysseus “will return and take vengeance” (1.268); the suitors eat his substance “without payment” (1.160, 1.377), and Telemachus warns them that they would die in his house “unavenged” (1.380).
grc: [τίσις, ἀπέτισεν, ἀποτίσεται, νήποινον, νήποινοι, παλίντιτα]
en: [paid back, without payment, unavenged, requital]
---

**Vengeance for Agamemnon.** τίσις Ἀτρεΐδαο is vengeance for the son of Atreus: Merry compares the penalty for Patroclus, ποινὴν Πατρόκλοιο (*Iliad* 21.28), and rejects taking Ὀρέσταο Ἀτρεΐδαο together, since Homer names no one by his grandfather except Achilles (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.40; see the translation's note on 1.40 and [[orestes]]). It will come “when he comes of age and longs for his own land” (1.41). ἁθρόα, “all at once”, makes Aegisthus' death, in Merry's words, “the one lump sum” paid as the price of his adultery and murder (on 1.43; see [[aegisthus]]).

**Vengeance for Odysseus.** Odysseus' homecoming may be a vengeance too, νοστήσας ἀποτίσεται (1.268; see [[nostos]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]), and Athena tells Telemachus to plan the suitors' death if his father is dead, with Orestes as his example (1.293–302; see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Without payment.** ποινή is the payment that settles a wrong, and νήποινος is “without payment”: the suitors eat another man's substance and pay nothing for it (1.160, 1.377). Turned on them, the word means “unavenged”: if after his warning they go on, “then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (1.380). Merry explains it from the custom by which ποινή was paid in place of private revenge (τίσις): had Telemachus killed the suitors under his own roof without warning, he would have been liable to it; having given them formal notice to quit, he would owe nothing for their deaths (on 1.377, citing *Iliad* 18.498 and 9.632–633). Between the two lines he calls on Zeus to grant “deeds of requital” (παλίντιτα ἔργα, 1.379), as Hecuba wishes that “there would be requital” on Achilles (ἄντιτα ἔργα, *Iliad* 24.213), and Merry compares ἄντιτα ἔργα in the Odyssey (17.60; on 1.379). Telemachus repeats the four lines in the assembly the next day (2.142–145; see [[who-is-to-blame]]).
