---
summary: Aristotle quotes this book's first line for what a proem does and its stranger's ship for a metaphor, and answers the critics of Penelope's father; the outline of the Odyssey in the Poetics begins with the situation that this book sets out.
---

- “Tell me, Muse, of the man”, with the Iliad's “Sing, goddess, the wrath”, as the sample of the subject that a proem puts into the hearer's hand (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415a12–17, quoting 1.1): [[proem]] and [[prooimion]].
- The story of the Odyssey: a man away from home for many years, watched by Poseidon and alone, his property wasted by suitors and his son plotted against; and the Odyssey built around one action, without everything that happened to Odysseus (*Poetics* 17, 1455b16–23; 8, 1451a22–29): [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]].
- “My ship stands over there”, from the stranger's account of herself, for a metaphor from genus to species, “for lying at anchor is a kind of standing” (*Poetics* 21, 1457b10–11, quoting 1.185): [[here-my-ship-stands]].
- Icarius or Icadius: critics who took Penelope's father for a Laconian found it absurd that Telemachus did not meet him in Lacedaemon (*Poetics* 25, 1461b4–9, on 1.329 and 1.275–278): [[the-icarius-problem]].
- The shared feast of the stranger's question, “A banquet, or a wedding? For this is surely no shared feast” (1.226). Merry notes that the ἔρανος was already the least splendid of the three, as it was later, and that this gives point to Aristotle's vulgar man, who entertains the members of a shared feast as if at a wedding (ἐρανιστὰς γαμικῶς ἑστιῶν, *Nicomachean Ethics* 4.2, 1123a19–24; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.226; see the translation's note on 1.226).
