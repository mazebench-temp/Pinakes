summary: The gods decide Odysseus' homecoming in council, and Athena carries out her part in person, promising Zeus to put might in Telemachus' heart and leaving him with might and daring. The mortals of the book see the gods' hand everywhere, in the year of return the gods have spun, in the gods who hinder Odysseus and have put him out of sight, and in a boldness the suitors think the gods must have taught.

**Promise and fulfillment.** In the council Athena proposes to go to Ithaca “so that I may urge his son on / the more, and put might in his heart” (1.88–89; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). As she leaves him, “in his heart / she put might and daring, and made him think of his father / even more than before” (1.320–322; see [[athena]], [[menos]] and [[tharsos]]). What follows is his own doing: he goes among the suitors “a man equal to the gods” (1.324; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]), rebukes his mother (1.346–359), and tells the suitors that at dawn, in the assembly, he will order them out of his house (1.368–380). They marvel “that he spoke so boldly” (1.381–382), and Antinous sneers: “surely the gods themselves are teaching you / to be a man of lofty speech and to speak boldly” (1.384–385; see [[antinous]]). Merry renders it “It must be the gods themselves that are thy teachers” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.384). The sneer is true, as the hearer knows; the god's gift and the man's act stand side by side.

**What mortals say.** The characters explain their lot by the gods. Even the year of Odysseus' return is one “in which the gods had spun the thread for him to return home” (1.17; see [[fates]]). As Mentes, Athena says that “the gods are hindering him from his journey” (1.195); Telemachus, that “the gods have willed otherwise, devising evil, / who have put him out of sight beyond all other / men” (1.234–236), and have brought other sorrows on him (1.244); Athena and Eurymachus, that the outcome lies “on the knees of the gods” (1.267, 1.400; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). The council frames these complaints. Zeus has already answered that mortals blame the gods for what their own recklessness brings them (1.32–34; see [[who-is-to-blame]]), and the gods have already resolved on the homecoming (1.76–79). At night it is Athena again who sends Penelope to sleep (1.363–364; see [[hypnos]]).
