---
summary: The war is over before the poem begins. Odysseus is the man who sacked Troy; the others who escaped are at home; and Phemius sings of the grim homecoming that Athena laid on the Achaeans from Troy, where Odysseus was not the only one to lose the day of his return (1.1–12, 1.62, 1.210–211, 1.327, 1.354–355).
---

**The sacker of Troy.** The first thing the proem tells of the man of many turns is that he “sacked the sacred citadel of Troy” (1.2; see [[proem]] and [[odysseus]]). Merry explains ἔπερσεν by Athena's words to Odysseus in Book 22, that by his counsel the city of Priam was taken, and compares his title πτολιπόρθιος, “sacker of cities” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.2; 22.230, 9.504; see [[sacker-of-cities]]). The war and the sea are behind the others: “all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction, / were at home, having escaped both the war and the sea” (1.11–12).

**Troy remembered.** In the council Athena reminds Zeus of the sacrifices Odysseus offered “beside the ships of the Argives … in wide Troy” (1.61–62; see [[argives]]). Mentes last saw Odysseus before he embarked for Troy with the best of the Argives (1.210–211). Telemachus would grieve less if his father had been brought down “in the land of the Trojans”, for then all the Achaeans would have made him a tomb (1.237–240; see [[trojans]]).

**The homecoming from Troy.** Phemius sings of “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]), and when Penelope cannot bear it, Telemachus answers that Odysseus “was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming / in Troy, but many other men perished too” (1.354–355).
