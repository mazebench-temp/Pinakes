**The warning to Aegisthus.** Zeus' example of men who bring sorrow on themselves is a man the gods had warned. Aegisthus killed Agamemnon “knowing it was sheer destruction, since we ourselves had told him beforehand, / sending Hermes, the keen-sighted slayer of Argus, / neither to kill the man himself nor to woo his wife” (1.37–39; see [[aegisthus]], [[agamemnon]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). The warning then passes into Hermes' own words, “for from Orestes vengeance will come for the son of Atreus” (1.40), a change from reported to direct speech that Merry compares with 1.276 and 1.374 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.40; see [[orestes]]). “So Hermes spoke, but the mind of Aegisthus / he did not persuade, for all his good intent” (1.42–43). The god's errand failed, and the man has paid for it.

**To Ogygia.** When Zeus has granted the homecoming, Athena proposes the second errand: “then let us urge on Hermes, the guide, the slayer of Argus, / to the island of Ogygia, so that with all speed / he may tell the nymph of the lovely tresses our unerring counsel, / the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus, that he may return” (1.84–87; see [[calypso]] and [[ogygia]]). It waits until Book 5, where Zeus sends him, calling him the gods' messenger “in all things else” too, and gives him the order in Athena's words (5.28–31). He binds on his sandals there in nearly the verses with which Athena binds on hers in this book (5.44–46; 1.96–98; see [[sandals]] and [[athena]]). Iris, the messenger of the Iliad, is never named in the Odyssey, and its messenger is Hermes; in the Iliad Zeus sends him on one errand only, to guide Priam (*Iliad* 24.331–338; see [[iris]]).

**His titles.** In the first errand he is ἐύσκοπος ἀργεϊφόντης, “the keen-sighted slayer of Argus” (1.38), in the second διάκτορος ἀργεϊφόντης, “the guide, the slayer of Argus” (1.84; see [[keen-sighted]], [[argeiphontes]] and [[guide]]). Both titles were already obscure in antiquity. For ἀργεϊφόντης Merry reports a scholion's “he who appears swiftly and clearly”, Alcman's use of the word, as Athenaeus quotes him, for a white cheese, and its use, according to the *Etymologicum Magnum*, as a title of the Sun; from these he infers an old myth that connected Hermes with the brightness of day. Another view made him a wind god who drives his flocks of cloud, and the word “sky-clearing”, which would give new point to ἐύσκοπος, “keen-sighted”, as of a careful shepherd. The scholia's “slayer of Argus”, the many-eyed guard of Io, belongs, he thinks, to a later age that had lost the old sense (on 1.38). Of διάκτορος he reports three explanations: Eustathius' “he who carries the messages through”, Buttmann's “runner”, from διάκω, “pursue”, and Nitzsch's “conductor”, from διάγω, as when Hermes stole Ares out of his prison in the Iliad and guided Heracles on his quest for Cerberus (on 1.84; *Iliad* 5.390; *Odyssey* 11.623–626).
