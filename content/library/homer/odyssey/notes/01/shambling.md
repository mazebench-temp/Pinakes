# εἰλίποδας (1.92; again 4.320, 8.60, 9.46), always of cattle.
grc: [εἰλίποδας]
---

“who are forever / slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (1.91–92; see [[cattle]], [[with-curving-horns]] and [[the-suitors]]). Merry finds Buttmann's “heavy-treading” doubtful: the sense of “roll” in the root is probably the first one, the word is not used of the downward thrust of a dead weight, and it does not suit its other uses, by the comic poet Eupolis of women, and by the Hippocratic writer of oxen, because their joints are loosely set. The scholiast comes nearer the truth, that each foot, lifted only a little, describes part of a circle as it is set forward; hence “shambling”, the swaying of the hind feet by which, in Lobeck's words, oxen differ from other animals, and a pointed contrast with ἀερσίποδες, “high-stepping”, of horses (*Iliad* 3.327; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.92). The translation keeps the Iliad's “shambling”.
