---
summary: Telemachus is indignant “that a stranger should stand long at the doors” (1.119–120), and asks the guest, and later Antinous, “will you be indignant with me for what I say?” (1.158, 1.389). The stranger says that “a man would be indignant” at the suitors' feast (1.228); Ilus “feared the indignation of the gods who live forever” (1.263); and Telemachus tells his mother that there is “no blame on this man” for his song (1.350).
---

**A wrong to a guest.** νεμεσσήθη δʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ (1.119): the first feeling the poem gives Telemachus is indignation at a breach of hospitality, a stranger left standing at the doors (see [[xenia]]). Merry notes the syntax, δέ where Attic would write γάρ (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.119).

**An apology.** “Will you be indignant with me for what I say?” opens Telemachus' complaint to the stranger (1.158) and his answer to Antinous (1.389). Merry explains the apologetic tone of the first by the unwillingness to broach a painful subject at a meal (on 1.158). Said to Antinous after his sneer, the same words introduce a retort (1.389–398; see [[basileus]]).

**The onlooker's blame.** The stranger says what a decent man would feel at the suitors' feast: “A man would be indignant / seeing so much that is shameful, any man of good sense who came among them” (1.228–229; see [[aischros]] and [[hybris]]). νέμεσις is the judgment of those who look on (see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**The gods' indignation.** Ilus of Ephyra would not give Odysseus a drug for his arrows, “since he feared the indignation of the gods who live forever” (1.263; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]). Merry notes that νεμεσίζομαι takes an accusative only here, “to stand in awe of”, and compares αἰδῶ καὶ νέμεσιν (*Iliad* 13.122); poisoned arrows appear nowhere in the Iliad, and the Odyssey alludes to them in words that show a strong feeling against them as barbarous or impious (on 1.263; see the translation's note on 1.263 and [[aidos]]).

**No blame.** Telemachus defends the singer: τούτῳ δʼ οὐ νέμεσις, “There is no blame on this man for singing the evil fate of the Danaans” (1.350), which Merry renders “no ill-will can be felt” (on 1.350). The words are those of the Trojan elders at the sight of Helen (*Iliad* 3.156; see [[no-blame]]).
