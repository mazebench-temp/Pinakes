# οἰωνῶν (1.202), the birds of omen; ὄρνις (1.320), the bird of the simile. οἰωνῶν differs from οἰῶν, "of sheep".
grc: [οἰωνῶν, ὄρνις]
en: [birds]
---

**Knowledge of birds.** The stranger prophesies that Odysseus will soon come home: “But now I will prophesy to you, as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled, / though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (1.200–202; see [[divination]] and [[athena]]). οἰωνός is the large bird whose flight is watched for signs, and to have clear knowledge of birds is the seer's art: Calchas in the Iliad is “by far the best of the bird-seers” (οἰωνοπόλων ὄχʼ ἄριστος, *Iliad* 1.69; see [[best-of-bird-seers]]), and in the next book of the Odyssey old Halitherses, who surpassed all men of his age in knowing birds and speaking what was fated, reads the two eagles that Zeus sends over the assembly of Ithaca (2.146–176). The goddess disclaims the art and prophesies all the same, from what the immortals put in her heart; she is one of them. Telemachus, for his part, tells Eurymachus that he no longer trusts in news or heeds the oracles his mother asks of prophets (1.413–416).

**Like a bird.** When she has given her counsel, “gray-eyed Athena went away, / and like a bird she flew upward and away” (1.319–320; see [[like-a-bird]]). ὄρνις is the common word for a bird, and here no kind is named. Merry reports La Roche's observation that where Homer adds the kind to ὄρνις, an eagle or a falcon, the bird is an omen, as with the eagle that flies by on the right as Telemachus takes leave of Menelaus and the falcon that flies by on the right when he lands on Ithaca (15.160–161, 15.525–526); here the bird is a likeness (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.320).
