---
summary: The same words describe Odysseus' companions in the proem, who “perished through their own recklessness” (1.7), and mortals in Zeus' first speech, who “through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate” (1.34).
---

**The proem and Zeus.** σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο (1.7) is the verse-end of Sthenelus on the Seven against Thebes, “but they perished through their own recklessness” (*Iliad* 4.409). The narrator says it of the companions, who ate the cattle of the Sun (1.7–9; see [[nepios]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]), and a few lines on Zeus makes it the gods' answer to mortals who blame them for their evils: “but they themselves / through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate” (σφῇσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, 1.33–34; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[moira]]). His example is Aegisthus, who was warned and did it all the same (1.35–43; see [[aegisthus]]).

**Later in the poem.** The word returns in the poem's judgment on the suitors, whom their recklessness brought to a shameful end (22.416, 23.67; and 24.458), and they are men who in their outrage devise reckless deeds, ὑβρίζοντες ἀτάσθαλα μηχανόωνται (3.207, 17.588, 20.170, 20.370; see [[hybris]]). Eurylochus turns it against Odysseus himself, saying that the men the Cyclops ate perished by their leader's recklessness (10.435–437).
