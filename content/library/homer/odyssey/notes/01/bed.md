**The suitors' prayer.** When Penelope has gone up to her room, “the suitors raised a din throughout the shadowy halls, / and they all prayed to lie beside her in bed” (1.365–366; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). ἠρήσαντο is more than a wish: they said it aloud, and their talk of her is the “arrogance” that Telemachus rebukes in his next words (1.368); παραί goes with κλιθῆναι, “to lie by her”, and λεχέεσσι, “in bed”, is a local addition (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.366; see [[hybris]] and [[marriage]]).

**Laertes and Eurycleia.** Laertes honored the slave he had bought as he honored his wife, “but he never lay with her in bed, for he shunned his wife's anger” (1.433; see [[eurycleia]] and [[laertes]]): εὐνῇ … ἔμικτο, “lay with her in bed”, is the phrase of love.

**Telemachus' bed.** At night Telemachus “went there to his bed, pondering many things in his mind” (1.427). In his chamber he “sat down on the bed, and took off his soft tunic” (1.437; see [[tunic]]), and Eurycleia hung it on a peg “beside the corded bed” (1.440). τρητοῖσι λέχεσσι is the bedstead, not the bedding: Merry takes τρητός as “morticed”, the posts fitted into holes in the frame, with the plural for a bed of many pieces, and finds this settled by Plato's division of composite things into those that are “pierced” (τρητά) and those bound together without piercing (*Statesman* 279e), against the holes bored for cords, though he grants that the strap Odysseus stretched through the bed he made favors the cords (*Odyssey* 23.195–201; Merry on 1.440; see [[corded]] and the translation's note on 1.440). There, “covered with sheep's wool”, he lies awake all night planning the journey Athena has shown him (1.443–444; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
