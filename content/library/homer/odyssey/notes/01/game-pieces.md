summary: When Athena comes to the doors of Odysseus' house, she finds the suitors in front of them delighting their hearts with game pieces, sitting on the hides of oxen they themselves had killed, while heralds and attendants make ready their meal (1.106–112).
passages:
  - 1.106-112 | The suitors before the doors
---

Athena comes down from Olympus and stands at the doorway of the court in the likeness of Mentes, and the first thing she finds is the suitors at their game: “They at that time / were delighting their hearts with game pieces in front of the doors, / sitting on the hides of oxen that they themselves had killed” (1.106–108; see [[the-suitors]] and [[athena]]). εὗρε δʼ ἄρα, “and she found”, does not mean that she was looking for them, but that she came upon them there, and ἔπειτα marks the next thing she saw (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.106). θυμὸν ἔτερπον, “were delighting their hearts”, is the suitors' pleasure, and the book returns to it: after the meal they turn to song and dancing, “for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.150–152), and at evening “they turned to dancing and to lovely song / and took their delight” (1.421–422).

The scene is a picture of the suitors' life in the house. They sit at leisure before another man's doors, on the hides of oxen they have slaughtered from his herds (see [[cattle]]), while heralds and ready attendants mix the wine and water, wash the tables with sponges and set them out, and divide the meat (1.109–112; see [[heralds]] and [[feast]]). Telemachus sits among them, grieving at heart and seeing in his mind his father come to scatter them (1.113–117), and so he is the first to see the stranger at the door.

πεσσοί are named only here in Homer, and the poem does not say how the suitors played. Merry (on 1.107) quotes Athenaeus' account of their game, in which each suitor aimed his pebble at one set in the middle and called Penelope (see [[the-game-of-penelope]]).
