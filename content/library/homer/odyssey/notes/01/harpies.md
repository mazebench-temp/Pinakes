---
summary: Telemachus imagines that the Harpies, the snatching storm winds, have carried his father off: had he fallen at Troy he would have had a tomb and fame, but now he is gone “without report”, out of sight and unheard of.
passages:
  - 1.234-243 | The Harpies have snatched him away
---

**Snatched away.** Telemachus tells the stranger that the gods have put his father out of sight beyond all men. He would not grieve so if Odysseus had died at Troy or in the arms of his friends, for then the Achaeans would have made him a tomb and he would have won great fame for his son too (1.234–240; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[kleos]]). Instead: “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report; / he is gone, out of sight, unheard of” (1.241–242). The text prints ἅρπυιαι in lowercase, and the word is still half a common noun, “the snatchers”: Merry takes them as the spirits of the storm, the storm winds personified, since in Penelope's prayer the daughters of Pandareus are snatched by θύελλαι, “storm winds”, and at the end of her story by ἅρπυιαι (20.66, 20.77), and she says that storm winds have snatched her own son away (4.727), as Helen wishes a storm wind had carried her off on the day she was born (*Iliad* 6.345–348; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.241). He compares Hesiod's Harpies, Aello and Ocypete, who keep pace with the blasts of the winds and the birds (*Theogony* 267–269), and sets them beside other powers of the poem that are personified but have no myth, like the Spinners of fate (7.197; on 1.241).

**The verb.** ἀνηρείψαντο, “have snatched … away”, is the verb of each of these passages (1.241 = 14.371, 4.727, 20.77). Merry observes that, if the reading is sound, it gives ἐρείπω a sense unlike its usual one, “throw down”, and reports that Fick would read ἀνηρέψαντο, from the root he finds in the name, which he would write Ἀρέπυιαι (on 1.241).

**Without report.** ἀκλειῶς is “without report” here, where the Iliad has “without glory” (*Iliad* 22.304): Merry holds that κλέος in Homer is properly “report”, “tidings”, as when Athena tells Telemachus of the rumor from Zeus “which most of all brings report to men” (1.283), so that no tidings of Odysseus have come (on 1.241 and 1.283; see the translation's note on 1.241). The swineherd Eumaeus will say the same three verses of his lost master (14.369–371).
