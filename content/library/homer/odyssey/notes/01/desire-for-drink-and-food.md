---
summary: The suitors' meal ends with the formula, and they turn to “song and dancing; for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.150–152).
---

The verse moves the feast from food to music: when the suitors have put away the desire for drink and food, a herald puts the lyre in Phemius' hands (1.150–155; [[feast-type-scene]], [[phemius]]). Merry gives the formula a long note: the ἔρος is thought of not as a feeling that arises in a man but as a property of the thing he enjoys, so that to “send away” the desire is to stop at the natural limit, when satisfaction comes, after which there is satiety; he quotes the poem's other uses, of sleep, love, song and dance, war and lament (*Iliad* 13.636–639, 24.227), and reports that Nitzsch thought Virgil's version of the phrase (*Aeneid* 1.216) a poor rendering (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.150).
