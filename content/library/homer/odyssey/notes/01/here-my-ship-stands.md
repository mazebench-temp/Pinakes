summary: Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, tells Telemachus where she has left her ship, in the half-line that Aristotle takes for a metaphor from genus to species. Whether her ship is afloat or drawn up on the shore, Merry finds, the words do not settle.

{{quote:30dcb255-4540-5f2b-93da-83f4fd03fe53}}

The lines belong to the stranger's account of herself. She claims to be Mentes, son of Anchialus, lord of the Taphians, sailing over the wine-dark sea to Temese for bronze with a cargo of iron, and she says where her ship is: in a harbor by the fields, away from the city (1.180–186; see [[mentes-the-taphian]], [[rheithron]] and [[neion]]). ἥδε, “over there”, is pointed with a gesture (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.185).

Aristotle reads ἕστηκεν, “stands”, as the general word put for a particular one, “for lying at anchor is a kind of standing” (*Poetics* 21, 1457b10–11; see the entry above and [[aristotle]]). Merry is less sure that the ship lies at anchor. ἐπʼ ἀγροῦ, “by the fields”, is not the usual phrase for a ship drawn up on the beach, and the ship might still be afloat; but when the ship that brought Telemachus from Pylos comes home in Book 16, its crew draw it up ἐπʼ ἠπείροιο, “on the land”, an expression as unusual, and Merry supposes that Athena's ship lay drawn up on the margin of the creek that served as a harbor, which the name Rheithron, “stream”, would suit (on 1.185, comparing 16.324–325; see the translation's note on 1.185). Odysseus says the same line of his own invented ship when he speaks to Laertes, unrecognized, in the last book (24.308).
