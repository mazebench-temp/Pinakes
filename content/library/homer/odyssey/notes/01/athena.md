summary: Athena turns the council of the gods to Odysseus and sets out the plan of the poem, Hermes to Calypso and she herself to Ithaca. In the likeness of Mentes, lord of the Taphians, she is welcomed by Telemachus, tells him his father is alive, counsels him to call an assembly and sail to Pylos and Sparta, and flies off like a bird, leaving him with new courage. The homecoming that Phemius sings is the grim one she laid on the Achaeans; at night she sends Penelope to sleep.

**The plea.** When Zeus has spoken of Aegisthus, the gray-eyed goddess takes up his example and turns it: “yes, truly that man lies fallen in a fitting destruction; / so may any other perish too who did such things. / But my heart is torn for wise-hearted Odysseus” (1.46–48; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[aegisthus]] and [[odysseus]]). Her address, “Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords” (1.45, again at 1.81), is a verse that only she speaks in Homer: to Zeus in the Iliad, when he has forbidden the gods to help either side (*Iliad* 8.31), and again at the end of this poem (24.473; see [[zeus]]). The Iliad's speech ends “so that they do not all perish because of your anger”, ὀδυσσαμένοιο τεοῖο (*Iliad* 8.37); this one ends with the same verb, “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (τί νύ οἱ τόσον ὠδύσαο, 1.62), which Merry hears as a play on the name of Odysseus, “a man of wrath” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.62; see the translation's note on 1.62). Her argument is the worshiper's: “Did not Odysseus / please you beside the ships of the Argives, offering sacrifices / in wide Troy?” (1.60–62; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Merry takes γλαυκῶπις, her epithet in the verse that brings her in (1.44), of the brightness and flashing of her eyes rather than their color (on 1.44, comparing *Iliad* 1.200; see [[gray-eyed]]).

**The plan.** When Zeus has granted the homecoming, she sets out both halves of the poem: Hermes is to go to Ogygia, while “I will go to Ithaca, so that I may urge his son on / the more, and put might in his heart” (1.84–89; see [[hermes]], [[calypso]] and [[telemachus]]). Telemachus is to call the Achaeans to an assembly and speak out to the suitors, and she will send him to Sparta and Pylos to learn of his father, “and so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.90–95; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[kleos]]).

**Down to Ithaca.** She arms as for a journey and a battle:

{{quote:52285d0c-c0df-5e41-9296-1d5868a42367}}

The sandals that carry her over sea and land are those of Hermes in the Iliad and in Book 5 of the Odyssey (1.97–98 = 5.45–46 and *Iliad* 24.341–342; see [[sandals]]); the spear with which she overpowers the ranks of heroes is the spear she takes up for battle in the Iliad (1.100–101 = *Iliad* 8.390–391, and nearly *Iliad* 5.746–747; see [[spear]]); and the verse of her descent is the one that takes her down from Olympus to the Achaeans at Troy (1.102 = *Iliad* 2.167, 4.74, 22.187; see [[olympus]]). ὀβριμοπάτρη, “the daughter of the mighty father” (1.101; see [[daughter-of-a-mighty-father]]), Merry connects with βρίθω, βριαρός and βαρύς, “heavy”, with a prefixed ὀ- (on 1.101): it shares its root with βριθύ, “heavy”, the first word of the verse before.

**In the likeness of Mentes.** She stands at the doorway of Odysseus, spear in hand, “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (1.103–105; see [[mentes-the-taphian]] and [[taphians]]). The phrase answers the Iliad's Κικόνων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, the leader of the Cicones whose likeness Apollo takes (*Iliad* 17.73; see the translation's note on 1.105). Telemachus is the first to see her; he takes her hand and her spear, sets the spear in the rack among the many spears of Odysseus, and seats her apart from the suitors (1.113–135; see [[spear-rack]], [[xenia]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). To him she tells the tale of Mentes, son of Anchialus, on his way to Temese for bronze, with his ship at Rheithron under Neion, a guest-friend of the house from of old (1.179–193; see [[taphos]] and [[temese]]), and then, in the stranger's voice, a true prophecy: “But now I will prophesy to you, as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled, / though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (1.200–202; see [[divination]]). Odysseus will not be away much longer, “not even if bonds of iron hold him” (1.204).

**The counsel.** Telemachus' account of the suitors moves her: “And chafing at this, Pallas Athena spoke to him” (1.252); ἐπαλαστήσασα marks a new state of mind, “with a burst of passion” (Merry on 1.252; see [[pallas]]). She wishes Odysseus would come home as she first saw him, drinking in her house on his way back from Ephyra (1.255–264; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]), and then gives her counsel: the assembly, the suitors sent home, the voyage to Pylos and Sparta, and, if his father is dead, the mound, his mother's marriage, and the suitors' death, by guile or openly, with Orestes as the example (1.269–302; see [[orestes]]). She leaves the rest to him: “let all this be your own care, and heed my words” (1.305). She refuses his guest-gift, to be given “on my way back” (1.315–318; see [[guest-gifts]]).

**Like a bird.** Then she goes:

{{quote:f1981b7f-6fc0-5b6c-aaca-71eee703010f}}

ὄρνις δʼ ὣς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο, “like a bird she flew upward and away”: Merry gives the ancient readings of ἀνόπαια, a bird's name, “unseen”, or “through the smoke-vent”, and prefers “upwards”, after Eustathius, who drew on Empedocles; and he takes ὄρνις ὥς as a brief simile, not a change of shape, though Athena does leave in the likeness of a sea eagle at Pylos (3.371–372) and sits on the roof-beam like a swallow in the fight with the suitors (22.239–240; Merry on 1.320; see the translation's note on 1.320 and [[like-a-bird]]). The might she promised Zeus to put in his heart (1.89) she puts there now, μένος καὶ θάρσος, “might and daring” (1.321; see [[menos]] and [[tharsos]]). He perceives it, is amazed, and thinks it was a god (1.322–323; see [[divine-epiphany]]); the aorists mark each step, “He noticed it—he was amazed—for the idea struck him that it was a god” (Merry on 1.323). Before the suitors he calls the stranger Mentes, “but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (1.417–420).

**The grim homecoming.** The song that follows her departure is of her anger: Phemius sings “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327; see [[phemius]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Merry takes the anger to be hers for the outrage of Ajax, son of Oileus, against Cassandra in her temple, and notes that at 4.499–511 Ajax dies for his insolence against Poseidon (on 1.326), though even there he is “hated by Athena” (4.502). Nestor too tells how many of the Achaeans met an evil fate on the way home “through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father” (3.132–135). The goddess of Odysseus' return is the goddess of the others' grief (see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Sleep and the journey.** When Penelope has gone up to her room in tears, she weeps for Odysseus “until sweet / sleep was cast upon her eyelids by gray-eyed Athena” (1.363–364; see [[penelope]]). The verses return three times more when she goes up to weep (16.449–451, 19.602–604, 21.356–358). The book ends with Telemachus awake all night, planning “the journey that Athena had shown him” (1.444).
