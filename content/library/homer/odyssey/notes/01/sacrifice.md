# ἱερὰ ῥέζων (1.61), "offering sacrifices"; ἱρὰ θεοῖσιν (1.66; again 3.159), sacrifices to the gods. ἱερά alone is also "sacred" (10.445).
grc: ["ἱερὰ ῥέζων", "ἱρὰ θεοῖσιν"]
en: [offering sacrifices, sacrifices]
---

**Athena's plea.** Athena ends her plea for Odysseus with a reproach to her father: “Did not Odysseus / please you beside the ships of the Argives, offering sacrifices / in wide Troy? Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (1.60–62; see [[athena]] and [[zeus]]). χαρίζετο, “please”, is the language of favors and gifts: the sacrifices were favors done to Zeus, and Athena asks why they have been repaid with anger (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). ἱερὰ ῥέζειν, “to do sacred things”, is Homer's phrase for offering sacrifice, and the Pylians are found doing it on the shore when Telemachus lands (ἱερὰ ῥέζον, 3.5).

**Zeus' answer.** Zeus has not forgotten: “How then could I forget divine Odysseus, / who is first among mortals in mind, and first in sacrifices / given to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky?” (1.65–67). Merry takes βροτῶν with the first περί, “beyond mortals in wisdom”, and the second περί as adverbial with ἔδωκε, “beyond all others gave sacrifices to the gods”, and compares *Iliad* 1.258, where Nestor calls Agamemnon and Achilles “first among the Danaans in counsel, and first in battle” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.66). It is not Zeus who keeps Odysseus from home, he says, but Poseidon, angry for his son the Cyclops (1.68–75; see [[poseidon]]).

The exchange has its counterpart at the end of the Iliad. There Apollo reproaches the gods with Hector, who “burned thighs of oxen and unblemished goats” for them, and Zeus grants that Hector was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios, since through him the altar of Zeus never lacked its equal share of the feast (*Iliad* 24.33–34, 24.66–70). In both a god's favor is claimed by the sacrifices a mortal has given.

**Other sacrifices in the book.** The god who is Odysseus' enemy is himself away at a sacrifice, sharing in a hecatomb of bulls and rams among the Ethiopians (1.22–26; see [[hecatomb]]). And in Ithaca the beasts of Odysseus' own herds are slaughtered every day for the suitors' feasts (σφάζουσι, 1.91–92; see [[sheep]] and [[cattle]]).
