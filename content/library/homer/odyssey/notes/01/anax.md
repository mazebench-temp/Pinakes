---
summary: Telemachus imagines his father home again to “rule over his own house” (1.117), and when Antinous wishes that he may never be king, he gives up the kingship and keeps the house: “but I will be lord of our own house / and of the slaves whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil for me” (1.397–398). Eurymachus grants it: “may you keep your possessions yourself and rule over your own house” (1.402).
grc: [ἀνάσσοι, ἀνάσσω, ἀνάσσοις]
---

**King and lord.** The exchange with the two suitors sets two words apart. βασιλεύς is the king of Ithaca, a place that others may hold (1.386–396; see [[basileus]]); ἄναξ is the master of a house and its slaves (1.397–398). Merry notes that ἡμετέροιο, “our house”, is the familiar language of one of a household, not the plural of royalty (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.397), and reports the view that τοῦ in “for mine is the power in this house” (1.359) stands for the ἄναξ of the house, as Telemachus calls himself here (on 1.359; see [[kratos]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). Eurymachus' wish uses the verb of 1.117: what Telemachus had imagined for his father, the suitor allows to the son (1.402).

**Lord of the Taphians.** Mentes, whose likeness the goddess takes, is a lord too: “I am lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar” (1.181), as Telemachus repeats (1.419; see [[taphians]] and [[mentes-the-taphian]]).
