All who escaped the war are at home (1.11–12); Menelaus was the last of them to come (1.286); and Phemius sings “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy”, the song Penelope cannot bear and Telemachus defends, for many others lost their homecoming too (1.325–327, 1.350–355).

**When the returns are over.** The poem begins at the point where the homecomings of the others are past: Merry takes ἔνθα, “Now”, as the express mark of the time at which the Odyssey begins, the end of Odysseus' seventh year on Calypso's island (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.11; see the translation's note on 1.11). By his reckoning the return of Menelaus and Orestes' revenge on Aegisthus fell in the ninth year after the fall of Troy, and Odysseus comes home in the tenth (on 1.16; see [[menelaus]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). Of those who came home the book names two, Nestor, to whom Telemachus is sent first, and Menelaus, “for he was the last of the bronze-armored Achaeans to come home” (1.284–286; see [[nestor]], [[bronze-armored]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). δεύτατος, “last”, is the superlative of δεύτερος in the sense of “later”, an illogical but natural meaning, Merry says (on 1.286). The one return the book tells is the worst: Agamemnon came home and was killed (1.35–36; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

**The song.** Phemius sings it to the suitors, who listen in silence: “he sang of the homecoming of the Achaeans, / the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.326–327; see [[phemius]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). λυγρόν, “grim” (see [[grim]]), is Nestor's word when he tells the same story: Zeus devised a grim homecoming for the Argives, and many of them met an evil fate through the wrath of Athena (λυγρὸν … νόστον, 3.132–135; see [[athena]]). Merry explains her anger by the outrage of Ajax, son of Oileus, to Cassandra in the goddess's temple, though in the fourth book his death is laid to his insolence against Poseidon (on 1.326; 4.499–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and the translation's note on 1.327). He takes the song for a single lay from the cycle of the heroes' homeward voyages, the war having become, ten years after, a matter of national history (on 1.326). The renderings of other books are plain renderings, not the translation.

**Not the only one.** Penelope asks the singer to sing something else (1.337–344). Telemachus answers that the singer is not to blame for singing “the evil fate of the Danaans” (Δαναῶν κακὸν οἶτον, 1.350; see [[danaans]] and [[no-blame]]), the words Nestor uses of the many who “met an evil fate” on the way home (κακὸν οἶτον ἐπέσπον, 3.134), and that the song is the newest (1.351–352; see [[plato-the-newest-song]]). Then he gives the returns as his mother's consolation: “Odysseus was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming / in Troy, but many other men perished too” (1.354–355; see [[nostos]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]).
