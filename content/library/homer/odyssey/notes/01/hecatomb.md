---
summary: Poseidon has gone to the far-off Ethiopians “to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams”, and sits at their feast while the other gods meet in the halls of Zeus (1.22–27).
---

{{quote:9b59389f-705b-5304-8d8c-c1c3d91ebaca}}

Poseidon, who rages against Odysseus, is away at the ends of the earth (see [[poseidon]] and [[ethiopians]]). ἀντιόων, “to share in”, takes the genitive of the offering, and the context, not the form, makes it future, of the purpose of his going (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.25). The god goes in person to take his part. So Athena comes to Nestor's sacrifice at Pylos “to share in the rites” (ἱρῶν ἀντιόωσα, 3.435–436), and in the Iliad Iris will not sit with the winds, since she is going back to the land of the Ethiopians, “where they are offering hecatombs / to the immortals, so that I too may feast among them on the sacrifices” (*Iliad* 23.205–207; see [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]). Alcinous says that the gods appear plainly to the Phaeacians whenever they offer glorious hecatombs, and feast beside them, sitting where they sit (7.201–203).

As always in Homer, the hecatomb is a great sacrifice, not a hundred oxen: this one is of bulls and rams (see [[bulls]] and [[ram]]). Its place in the story is the god's absence. While Poseidon feasts at the ends of the earth, “the others / were all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus” (1.26–27), and the return of Odysseus is decided without him (see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]).
