**The first homecoming.** Before the poem has told anything of Odysseus' return, it tells Agamemnon's, and it ends in murder: τὸν δʼ ἔκτανε νοστήσαντα, “and killed him on his return” (1.36), with the participle of νοστέω, the verb of homecoming (see [[nostos]] and [[agamemnon]]). Zeus tells it as proof of his complaint that mortals blame the gods for sorrows they bring on themselves (1.32–34; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]).

**The crime.** Zeus names two acts, γῆμʼ ἄλοχον μνηστήν, “married … his wedded wife”, and the killing (1.35–36), and the gods' warning forbade both: “neither to kill the man himself nor to woo his wife” (1.39; see [[aegisthus]] and [[hermes]]). In Zeus' story neither husband nor wife is named: Agamemnon is “the son of Atreus” (1.35, 1.40; see [[son-of-atreus]]), and Clytemnestra only his wife (see [[clytemnestra]]). μνηστή, “wooed, wedded”, and μνάασθαι, “to woo”, are words of the same family as μνηστῆρες, the suitors (see [[the-suitors]]). Merry sets beside εἰδὼς αἰπὺν ὄλεθρον, “knowing it was sheer destruction” (1.37), the converse phrase in Proteus' account of the murder, where Aegisthus leads Agamemnon up “not knowing his destruction” (τὸν δʼ οὐκ εἰδότʼ ὄλεθρον, 4.534), and takes the ἡμεῖς of 1.37 as emphatic, to show that the gods were not the cause (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.37). The punishment is ἁθρόα, “all at once” (1.43; see [[poine]]). The rendering of the fourth book is a plain rendering, not the translation.

**Athena's verdict.** Athena answers, “yes, truly that man lies fallen in a fitting destruction; / so may any other perish too who did such things” (1.46–47; see [[athena]] and [[scipio-and-tiberius-gracchus]]). She accepts the example and turns it at once to a man who has done no such thing, Odysseus, kept from home (1.48–62).

**Told to the son.** In the likeness of Mentes she tells the same story from the son's side: Orestes won fame “when he killed his father's slayer, / scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father” (1.299–300; see [[orestes]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). Nestor will repeat the verses (3.197–198), and tell how the murder came about (3.254–312); Menelaus has heard it from Proteus (4.512–537), and Odysseus will hear it from Agamemnon's shade (11.405–434).
