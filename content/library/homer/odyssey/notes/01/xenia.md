summary: Athena comes to Ithaca “in the likeness of a stranger” (1.105), and Telemachus receives her by the rules of the house, meal first and questions after (1.119–143). She claims an old bond between the houses, “each other's guest-friends from our fathers' time” (1.187), and puts off the gift he offers, “such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends”, until her return (1.309–318). The suitors, who eat another man's substance, are no guests (1.225–229, 1.374–375).

**The rule of the house.** The suitors do not see the stranger at the door; Telemachus does, and is indignant that “a stranger should stand long at the doors” (1.119–120; see [[nemesis]]). He takes her right hand and her spear, and greets her with the order of a Homeric welcome:

{{quote:2401a35a-7faa-5a6c-ad44-a0c6ba8e12cc}}

φιλήσεαι, “you will be treated as a friend”, is in Merry's words “shalt be entreated well”: in primitive society the showing of friendliness precedes the feeling, and its most definite form is hospitality (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.123; see [[philos]]). He seats the guest on a fine chair with a footstool, apart from the suitors, “so that the stranger, troubled by the din, / should not loathe his meal, coming among overbearing men” (1.130–134), and the meal of the formula verses is served (1.136–143; see [[hand-washing]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). To the objection that the line of the hasty meal does not suit a banquet, Merry answers that the separate provision for the guest marks the unfriendly relations between the young prince and the suitors (on 1.140).

**Guest-friends from our fathers' time.** Only after the meal does Telemachus ask who the stranger is (1.169–173; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]), and whether he is new or “a guest-friend / from our fathers' time; for many other men used to come / to our house, since he too was one who went about among men” (1.175–177). Merry takes ἴσαν as “used to come”, from εἶμι, against Nitzsch's “knew” (on 1.176), and ἐπίστροφος as “a visitor of men” (on 1.177). The goddess claims the inherited bond: “We claim to be each other's guest-friends from our fathers' time, / from of old, if you will only go and ask the old man, / the hero Laertes” (1.187–189; see [[laertes]] and [[euchomai]]). ξεῖνος πατρώιος is Diomedes' word to Glaucus, “an old guest-friend of mine from our fathers' time” (*Iliad* 6.215), the bond that kept two enemies from fighting; here it opens a house to a goddess in disguise. Telemachus repeats the claim to Eurymachus, “This man is my guest-friend from our fathers' time, from Taphos” (1.417), though “in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (1.420; see [[mentes-the-taphian]]).

**A friendship of houses.** Mentes' tale makes the two houses old friends. Odysseus once drank in his father's house on his way back from Ephyra, and when Ilus would not give him a drug for his arrows, “my father gave it to him; for he loved him terribly” (1.257–264; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]).

**The gift.** Telemachus asks the guest to stay, bathe, and go to his ship with a gift, “a precious one, very beautiful, which will be a treasure for you / from me, such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends” (1.309–313; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[doron]]). She asks him to give it on her way back, “and it will bring you its worth in return” (1.315–318). Merry reads in the answer Telemachus' misgiving, that a guest who puts off his gift may be unable to return it, and the goddess's pledge to repay it handsomely, so that the gift will be “no bad investment” (on 1.318).

**The feast that is not hospitality.** The suitors are not guests. The stranger asks, “What feast, what throng is this? … / A banquet, or a wedding? For this is surely no shared feast” (1.225–226), and Merry distinguishes the εἰλαπίνη, given by one host, from the ἔρανος, held at each house in turn (on 1.226; see the translation's note on 1.226 and [[feast]]). The suitors “eat another man's substance without payment” (1.160; see [[poine]]), and Telemachus bids them “provide other feasts, / eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house” (1.374–375; see [[the-suitors]]).
