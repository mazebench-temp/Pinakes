**The first man named.** After Odysseus (1.21), Aegisthus is the first mortal the poem names, and he comes into it as a memory in the mind of Zeus, already dead at the hands of Orestes (1.29–31; see [[zeus]] and [[orestes]]). Zeus calls him ἀμύμων, “noble”. Merry (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.29; see the translation's note on 1.29) explains that the epithet marks some special excellence, such as birth or beauty, with no necessary moral sense, that it is “almost titular, like our epithet ‘gallant’”, and he sets beside it δῖα, “brilliant”, said of Clytemnestra (3.266; see [[blameless]]). The rendering of other books is a plain rendering, not the translation.

**Zeus' example.** He is Zeus' instance of the mortals who “through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate” (1.34; see [[atasthalie]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]): “against fate” he married the wife of the son of Atreus and killed him on his return, though the gods had sent Hermes to warn him, and “the mind of Aegisthus / he did not persuade, for all his good intent” (1.35–43; see [[hermes]], [[peitho]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Now “he has paid back all at once” (1.43; see [[poine]]). Athena agrees that “that man lies fallen in a fitting destruction” (1.46–47), and the verse that follows is the one Scipio quoted at the death of Tiberius Gracchus (see [[scipio-and-tiberius-gracchus]]).

**His father's slayer.** Athena, as Mentes, gives him his epithet: Orestes “killed his father's slayer, / scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father” (1.299–300). The third and fourth books give him δολόμητις, “scheming”, four times more (3.198, 3.250, 3.308; 4.525), and the eleventh gives it once to Clytemnestra (11.422; see [[schemer]]). πατροφονεύς, which would ordinarily mean a man who kills his own father, is here one who killed another's, and the relative clause ὅ οἱ πατέρα κλυτὸν ἔκτα gives the needful explanation (Merry on 1.300).
