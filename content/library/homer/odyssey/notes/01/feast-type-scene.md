summary: The book has two meals in one hall. The guest's meal is told in the Odyssey's verses of welcome, water for the hands, a table, bread, meat and wine; the suitors' feast follows in the verses the two poems share, from the washing of the hands to the singer's song; and at evening the suitors dance and sing until dark and go home to sleep (1.109–112, 1.136–155, 1.421–424).

**Making ready.** When Athena comes to the doorway, the feast is being made ready: “some mixing wine and water in the mixing bowls, / and others again with sponges full of holes were washing the tables / and setting them out, and others were dividing much meat” (1.110–112; see [[heralds]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[table]]).

**The guest's meal.** Telemachus seats the stranger apart, and a meal is served to the two of them before the suitors come in: water for the hands, a polished table, bread and dainties, in the five verses that the Odyssey uses whenever a guest has just been seated (1.136–140; see [[water-for-the-hands]]); then “a carver lifted up platters of meat and set them before them, / of every kind, and set golden cups beside them” (1.141–142, as at Menelaus' table, *Odyssey* 4.57–58), and “a herald went to and fro, pouring wine for them” (1.143). It is a meal of welcome, set apart from the suitors' feast, and its parts have their own entries: [[hand-washing]], [[basin-and-pitcher]], [[table]], [[handmaids]] and [[cupbearing]].

**The suitors' feast.** Then the suitors come in, and their feast runs through the pattern of the feasts of the Iliad:

1. **Seats.** “Then they / sat down in order on the chairs and the seats” (1.144–145; see [[throne]]); the verse returns in Nestor's house and in Laertes' (*Odyssey* 3.389, 24.385).
2. **Water.** “and the heralds poured water over their hands” (1.146), the verse of the libation before the embassy in the Iliad (*Iliad* 9.174).
3. **Bread and wine.** Slave women heap bread in baskets, “and the young men filled the mixing bowls brim-full with drink” (1.147–148), the verse of the drinking at Chryse and before the embassy (*Iliad* 1.470, 9.175).
4. **Eating.** “And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them” (1.149), the verse of the three meals in Achilles' hut and in Agamemnon's (*Iliad* 9.91, 9.221, 24.627), and of meals in a house throughout the Odyssey.
5. **Enough.** “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (1.150; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).
6. **Song.** “the suitors had other things on their minds, / song and dancing; for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.151–152; see [[feast]]). A herald gives the lyre to Phemius, “And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song” (1.153–155; see [[lyre]] and [[phemius]]): ἀνεβάλλετο is the regular word for the prelude to a song (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.155).

**Night.** The day ends as feasts end in the Iliad, with song until dark and then sleep: “And they turned to dancing and to lovely song / and took their delight, and waited for evening to come; / and as they took their delight black evening came on. / Then they went to lie down, each to his own house” (1.421–424). The first three verses return on the evening when the suitors have brought their gifts for Penelope (*Odyssey* 18.304–306), and the last ends the gods' feast at the close of the Iliad's first book (*Iliad* 1.606; see [[dance]]). The suitors go home to sleep; Telemachus lies awake (1.425–444; see [[bed]]).
