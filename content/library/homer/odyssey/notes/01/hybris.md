---
summary: The stranger sees the suitors feast “so overbearingly, like men committing outrage” (ὑβρίζοντες, 1.227), and Telemachus addresses them as “Suitors of my mother, overweening in your arrogance” (ὑπέρβιον ὕβριν ἔχοντες, 1.368).
grc: [ὑβρίζοντες]
---

Merry prefers to take ὥς τε as comparative, “like men committing outrage” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.227; see the translation's note on 1.227). In ὕβριν ἔχοντες he hears a settled, deliberate behavior, as in ὀχέειν (on 1.297; see [[nepios]]), and an allusion to what the suitors had said aloud of Penelope when they clamored in the hall (on 1.366). They are ὑπερφίαλοι, “overbearing” (1.134; ὑπερφιάλως, 1.227), a word Merry derives from the root of φύω, “overgrown” and so “overweening” (on 1.134). Later in the poem the suitors are said again and again to devise reckless deeds in their outrage, ὑβρίζοντες ἀτάσθαλα μηχανόωνται (3.207, 17.588, 20.170, 20.370; see [[atasthalie]] and [[the-suitors]]).
