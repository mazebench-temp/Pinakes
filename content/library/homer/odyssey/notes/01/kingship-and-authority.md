summary: With the king away, the kingship of Ithaca is in question from the first evening: Antinous prays that it may never be Telemachus', Telemachus answers that there are many kings in the island and claims only his own house, and Eurymachus leaves it “on the knees of the gods” (1.383–404).

Merry refers for the place of the βασιλεύς in the heroic age, and the hereditary succession, to Grote's *History of Greece* (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.394), and gives Nitzsch's list of the prerogatives of a Homeric king: the royal estate, tribute and fees, special gifts, maintenance at the public cost and invitations to feasts (on 1.117; see [[time]]). Antinous grants that the kingship is Telemachus' “by birth from your father” (1.387); Telemachus answers that a king's house grows rich and he himself is honored (1.392–393), yields the kingship to the island's other nobles, and claims to be lord of his own house (1.394–398; see [[basileus]] and [[anax]]). The suitors themselves are the island's lords, “all who rule over craggy Ithaca” (1.247; see [[koiranos]] and [[the-suitors]]).
