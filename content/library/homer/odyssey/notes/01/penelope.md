summary: Penelope is spoken of before she appears: her son doubts that anyone knows his father, the suitors woo her, and Athena would send her back to her father's hall. Hearing Phemius sing of the Achaeans' grim homecoming, she comes down from her upper room in tears and asks him to sing something else; Telemachus sends her back to her work, and she goes up amazed and weeps for Odysseus until Athena sends her sleep.

**Named by her father.** The book names her twice: in Athena's words to her son, “since Penelope bore such a son as you” (1.223), and in the verse with which the narrator brings her on, “the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope” (1.329; see [[daughter-of-icarius]] and [[periphron]]). Her father's hall is where Athena would send her if she wished to marry (1.275–278). Merry reports that where Icarius lived was an old question: Aristotle leans to the Cephallenians' story that Odysseus married among them and that her father was Icadius (*Poetics* 1461b4–9), and Strabo makes Icarius a brother of Tyndareus who settled in Acarnania (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.276; see [[icarius]], [[the-icarius-problem]] and the translation's note on 1.276).

**Before she appears.** The others speak of her first. Odysseus on Calypso's island is “wanting his homecoming and his wife” (1.13). Telemachus says, “My mother says that I am his” (1.215), and tells the stranger that all the best men of the islands “woo my mother and wear down my house. / And she neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can she / make an end” (1.248–250; see [[the-suitors]]). Merry takes ἀρνεῖται as “declines”, and δύναται of a moral impossibility: she has not the heart to end it (on 1.249 and 1.250). The shade of the suitor Amphimedon says the same of her in the underworld, in nearly the same words (24.126). Athena's counsel disposes of her twice, sending her back to her father if her heart is eager to marry (1.275–278), and giving her to a husband if Odysseus is dead (1.292).

**She comes down.** Phemius sings of the grim homecoming of the Achaeans, and she hears it “from her upper room”; Merry notes that the word gives both her place and the distance across which the song reaches her (1.325–328; on 1.328; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[upper-room]]). She comes down the high stairway “not alone; two handmaids went along with her”, as a lady did in the presence of men, and Merry cites her own later words, that she will not go among the men alone (1.330–331; on 1.331; 18.182–184; see [[handmaids]]). She stands by the doorpost of the hall, “holding her shining veil before her cheeks”, bursts into tears, and speaks to the singer (1.332–336; see [[veil]]):

The song, Merry observes, was already well known, since it “always” wears down her heart (on 1.341; see [[phemius]] and [[penthos]]). τοίην κεφαλήν, “Such a head”, gives the measure of her loss: a man noble enough to cause a grief not to be forgotten (on 1.343; see [[kleos]]).

**Sent back.** Telemachus answers that the singer is not to blame but Zeus, that men praise the newest song, and that Odysseus was not the only man who lost his homecoming; then he sends her back to her loom and distaff, “for mine is the power in this house” (1.346–359; see [[telemachus]], [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). “And she, amazed, went back into the house; / for she laid her son's wise word to heart” (1.360–361). Merry takes the cause of her amazement to be the unexpected wisdom of her young son's words (on 1.360). Going up to her upper room with her handmaids, she weeps for Odysseus, her dear husband, until gray-eyed Athena casts sweet sleep on her eyelids (1.362–364; see [[hypnos]] and [[athena]]).

**The suitors' din.** “And the suitors raised a din throughout the shadowy halls, / and they all prayed to lie beside her in bed” (1.365–366). Merry reports the scholiast's gloss that they were talking among themselves of Penelope, how wise and how beautiful she was, and takes ἠρήσαντο as a wish spoken aloud, to which Telemachus alludes when he calls them “overweening in your arrogance” (on 1.365 and 1.366; 1.368; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).
