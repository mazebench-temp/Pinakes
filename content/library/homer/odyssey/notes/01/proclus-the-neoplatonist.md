---
summary: Proclus quotes this book four times in his essays on the Republic, for the Muse of its first line, the fame of Orestes, the pillars of Atlas and Phemius, whom he makes the type of the singer who knows; in his commentary on the Parmenides he quotes Telemachus' lines on the newest song for newness of form.
---

- The Muses as a chain, which the poet invokes now in its many members and now in its unity, “Tell me now, Muses” and “Tell me, Muse, of the man” (*On the Republic* I 184 Kroll, quoting *Iliad* 2.484 and 1.1): [[invocation-of-the-muses]].
- The fame of Orestes among the examples by which poetry teaches, through the praise of good men and the wish to be like them, not by the lawgiver's universal rules; Proclus has “do you not see” for Homer's “have you not heard” (*On the Republic* I 58 Kroll, quoting 1.298–299): [[orestes]].
- Phemius of Ithaca as the singer marked above all by knowledge of divine and human things, as Penelope says to him, “Phemius, you know many other enchantments for mortals, / deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous”; Demodocus is the inspired singer, Clytemnestra's singer the imitative one, and Thamyris, whom the Muses stopped from singing, belongs with the poetry of mere appearance (*On the Republic* I 194 Kroll, quoting 1.337–338): [[phemius]].
- Homer's Atlas, ruler of the pillars “that hold earth and heaven apart”, which Proclus takes for a riddling name of the axes of the heavenly revolutions (*On the Republic* II 200 Kroll, quoting 1.54): [[atlas]].
- “Men praise that song the more / which comes newest to those who listen”, for the advice to keep the measures of virtue and vary the harmonies and rhythms (*Commentary on the Parmenides*, col. 701 Cousin, quoting 1.351–352): [[plato-the-newest-song]].
