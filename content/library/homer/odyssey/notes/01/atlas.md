**In Athena's plea.** Atlas enters the poem for his daughter's sake. Athena is telling Zeus where Odysseus suffers, on a sea-washed island full of trees where a goddess dwells, “the daughter of baleful Atlas” (1.50–52; see [[calypso]], [[ogygia]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). Three marks follow: he is ὀλοόφρων, “baleful”, and, in one relative clause (ὅς τε … οἶδεν, ἔχει δέ), he knows the depths of the whole sea and himself holds the tall pillars that hold earth and heaven apart (1.52–54; see [[oloophron]]). Then the sentence returns to the daughter, τοῦ θυγάτηρ, “It is his daughter who holds the unhappy man back” (1.55). The general article gives Merry's account of the epithet and of the pillars (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.52 and 1.53; see the translation's note on 1.52–54).

**Other explanations.** Merry reports two more. F. G. Welcker referred ὀλοόφρων to the guilt of Atlas as leader of a rebellion of the Titans, stirred up by Hera and put down by Zeus with the help of Athena and Apollo, a story Merry cites from Hyginus (*Fabulae* 150); and Nägelsbach saw in Atlas and his daughter, “the concealer”, a memory of the Phoenician traders who hid the far western sources of their wealth beyond the Pillars of Heracles, so that Atlas and Calypso in the West would answer to Proteus and his daughter Eidothea in the East (on 1.52; 4.365–366, 4.384–386). Merry himself places the Atlas of this passage in the far West, where the sun sets and the darkness comes from (on 1.53).
