---
summary: The suitors' game is the first thing Athena sees at Odysseus' door, and Homer names it in a single word. The story of a game called Penelope, in which Eurymachus won most often, is Apion's.
refs: [1.107]
---

Athena comes down from Olympus and stands at the doorway of the court in the likeness of Mentes, and she finds the suitors “delighting their hearts with game pieces in front of the doors, / sitting on the hides of oxen that they themselves had killed” (1.107–108), while heralds and attendants mix the wine, wash and set out the tables and divide the meat (1.109–112; see [[game-pieces]] and [[the-suitors]]). πεσσοῖσι, “with game pieces”, is the only mention of the game in Homer, and nothing in the book says how it was played or what the suitors played for. The ox-hides they sit on and the meat the servants divide come from the herds of the absent master, whose “thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” Athena has just told the gods they are forever slaughtering (1.91–92).

Apion's version gives the game the point that the book gives the suitors' whole life in the house: they are wooing Penelope at another man's table (1.245–251; see [[penelope]]). Eurymachus, who in Apion's account had won most often, appears in Book 1 only at its end, when he asks Telemachus about the stranger and is told that he is a guest-friend of the house from Taphos, Mentes, son of Anchialus, though in his heart Telemachus knows the goddess (1.399–420; see [[eurymachus]] and [[mentes-the-taphian]]).
