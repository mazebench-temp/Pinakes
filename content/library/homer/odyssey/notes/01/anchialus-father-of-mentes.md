summary: Anchialus is named twice, in the verse that gives the stranger's name, first by Athena and then by Telemachus (1.180, 1.418). In Athena's tale he is the father who gave Odysseus the man-slaying drug that Ilus of Ephyra refused, “for he loved him terribly” (1.264).
# Unnamed: "but my father gave it to him; for he loved him terribly" (1.264).
refs: [1.264]
---

Μέντης Ἀγχιάλοιο δαΐφρονος εὔχομαι εἶναι / υἱός, “I claim to be Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus” (1.180–181): Athena gives the father's name with her own, as a stranger does who is asked who he is and who his parents are (1.170; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[mentes-the-taphian]]). Telemachus repeats the verse to Eurymachus with εὔχεται, “he claims”, for εὔχομαι (1.418; see [[euchomai]]). δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted”, is Odysseus' epithet in the same book (1.48); the word is from δαῆναι, “to learn”, not from δαΐς, “battle”, as Merry argues against Buttmann (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.48; see [[wise-hearted]] and the translation's note on 1.48).

The father has a part in the tale. Odysseus came to his house on his way back from Ephyra, where Ilus son of Mermerus had refused him a drug for his arrows, fearing the gods, “but my father gave it to him; for he loved him terribly” (1.263–264; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]] and [[ilus-son-of-mermerus]]). Ilus dared not give the poison, Merry comments, but in Anchialus' case his deep love for Odysseus overcame every other consideration (on 1.263). φιλέεσκε, “he loved”, is the iterative of φιλέω, and Merry sets it beside Telemachus' welcome, “Here with us you will be treated as a friend” (φιλήσεαι, 1.123), where to be loved is to be entertained as a guest, though the other sense is not excluded (on 1.123; see [[xenia]] and [[philos]]).
