**A prophecy without a seer's art.** The stranger has told Telemachus that his father is alive, held on a sea-washed island, and then speaks as a prophet:

{{quote:3a6202af-62bb-5ae7-8d22-a601980cbd57}}

She disowns the two arts of divination, the seer's (μάντις) and the reading of birds (οἰωνῶν σάφα εἰδώς; see [[birds]]), and claims only what “the immortals / put it in my heart”. Merry takes βάλλουσι, “put”, perhaps of a thought that comes suddenly, a stronger word than ἐνὶ θυμῷ τιθέναι, used when the goddess puts might in Telemachus' heart as she leaves (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.201, comparing 1.320–321). The speaker who disclaims the seer's knowledge is herself a goddess, and the prophecy is true: “he will devise how to return, since he is a man of many devices” (1.205; see [[athena]] and [[odysseus]]). μαντεύσομαι, “I will prophesy”, is formed from μάντις, the seer's own name.

**No oracle.** When Eurymachus asks whether the stranger brought news of his father, Telemachus answers: “Eurymachus, truly my father's homecoming is lost; / so I no longer trust in news, if any should come from somewhere, / nor do I heed any oracle that my mother / may ask of a prophet when she has called him to the hall” (1.413–416; see [[eurymachus]] and [[penelope]]). θεοπροπίη is the oracle and θεοπρόπος the prophet who gives it (see [[theopropion]]); the translation keeps “prophet” for θεοπρόπος and “seer” for μάντις (see the translation's key-word rows). Penelope, it appears, still calls prophets to the hall. Telemachus says this to the suitors, “but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (1.420), though he has just heard a prophecy from her.
