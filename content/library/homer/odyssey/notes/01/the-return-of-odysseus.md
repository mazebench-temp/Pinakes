summary: The first book sets the return in motion. The year of Odysseus' homecoming has come, though Poseidon is still angry; the gods resolve that he shall come home, Athena in the likeness of Mentes foretells it, and her wish, her counsel and Telemachus' warning to the suitors look forward to their death.

**The year has come.** The poem begins when the others are home and the time is fixed: “But when, as the years circled, the year had come / in which the gods had spun the thread for him to return home / to Ithaca, not even then was he free of his trials, / or among his friends” (1.16–19). Merry takes ἔνθα, “Now” (1.11), for the exact point at which the Odyssey begins, the end of the seventh year of Odysseus' stay on Calypso's island, and reckons the ten years from the fall of Troy: two to reach Calypso, including a year with Circe, seven with Calypso, and the last for the voyage home (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.11 and 1.16; 7.259, 10.467). The trials, ἄεθλοι, are in his view the struggle against what keeps Odysseus from home, not what waits for him in Ithaca (on 1.19; see the translation's notes on 1.11 and 1.18–19).

**Decided.** The gods resolve it in council while Poseidon is away: “But come, let all of us here take thought for / his homecoming, how he may come” (1.76–77), and Hermes is to tell the nymph “our unerring counsel, / the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus” (1.86–87; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[nostos]]). Merry joins νημερτέα βουλήν with νόστον: “that which is infallibly purposed, viz. that Odysseus shall return” (on 1.86).

**Foretold.** In Ithaca no one believes it. Telemachus thinks his father's bones are rotting, “and there is no / comfort for us, even if some one of the men on earth / says that he will come” (1.166–168). The prophecy is the goddess's own knowledge, given in a mortal's words, “though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (1.202; see [[divination]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]).

**Wished for.** Telemachus dreams of his father's coming to scatter the suitors (1.115–117), and Athena wishes that Odysseus would stand at the doors now as she first saw him, with helmet, shield and two spears: “they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (1.255–266; see [[the-suitors]]). She leaves it to the gods: “But these things lie on the knees of the gods, / whether he will return and take vengeance, or not, / in his own halls” (1.267–269; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). Her counsel looks to the same end, by guile or openly (1.293–296), and Telemachus' warning to the suitors calls on Zeus for “deeds of requital” (1.379; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).
