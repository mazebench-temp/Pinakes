summary: The proem's word. Odysseus strove for “the homecoming of his companions”, who lost “the day of their homecoming” (1.5–9), and he alone, “wanting his homecoming and his wife”, is held by Calypso (1.13). The gods resolve on his homecoming (1.76–87), Telemachus is sent to learn of it (1.93–95, 1.281–287), and Phemius sings the Achaeans' “grim homecoming” (1.326–327), while Telemachus holds that his father's “homecoming is lost” (1.413).

**The proem.** The poem's first account of its hero's purpose is a homecoming that failed. Odysseus suffered on the sea “seeking to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions” (1.4–5), but they ate the cattle of the Sun, and the god “took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.7–9; see [[atasthalie]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). νόστιμον ἦμαρ, “the day of homecoming”, comes back twice in the book: Telemachus says that his father's is lost, and tells his mother that Odysseus “was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming” (1.168, 1.354; see [[day-of-homecoming]]).

**Him alone.** The narrative begins when the returns are over: “all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction, / were at home” (1.11–12), and only Odysseus, “wanting his homecoming and his wife” (νόστου κεχρημένον, 1.13), is held by Calypso, who longs for him to be her husband (1.14–15; see [[calypso]] and [[posis]]). Merry glosses κεχρημένον as “desiring, requiring” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.13). Even in the year in which “the gods had spun the thread for him to return home” (οἶκόνδε νέεσθαι, 1.17), he was not free of his trials (1.18; see [[moira]]). Merry reckons the ten years from the fall of Troy: two to reach Calypso's island, seven with her, and one for the voyage home (on 1.16).

**A homecoming murdered.** The first homecoming the poem tells is Agamemnon's: Aegisthus married his wife “and killed him on his return” (νοστήσαντα, 1.36; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[aegisthus]]). The example stands beside Odysseus' own. Athena, as Mentes, leaves it to the gods “whether he will return and take vengeance, or not, / in his own halls” (νοστήσας ἀποτίσεται, 1.268–269; see [[poine]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). Agamemnon was killed by the man who had taken his wife; Odysseus, if he comes home, may take vengeance on the men who woo his.

**The gods' counsel.** Zeus proposes that the gods “take thought for / his homecoming, how he may come” (1.76–77), and Athena asks that Hermes tell Calypso “our unerring counsel, / the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus, that he may return” (1.86–87). Merry takes νημερτέα βουλὴν νόστον together, “that which is infallibly purposed, viz. that Odysseus shall return” (on 1.86; see [[boule]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]).

**News of it.** Athena sends Telemachus to Sparta and Pylos “to learn of his dear father's homecoming, if he may hear of it somewhere” (1.94), and tells him what to do if he hears “of your father's life and homecoming” (1.287) or that he is dead (1.289–292; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Telemachus has given it up. “If they were to see him come home to Ithaca”, the suitors would pray for nimbler feet rather than for riches (1.163–165), but “the day of his homecoming is lost” (1.168), and to Eurymachus he says, “truly my father's homecoming is lost” (1.413). The goddess says the opposite: “he will devise how to return, since he is a man of many devices” (1.205). The son is given a homecoming of his own: if his father is dead, he is to “come home to your dear native land” and raise a mound for him (1.290–291).

**The song of the Achaeans' homecoming.** Phemius sings “the homecoming of the Achaeans, / the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.326–327), and Penelope comes down to ask him to stop (1.336–344; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[phemius]]). Merry supposes the song a single lay from the cycle of the homeward voyages of all the Greek heroes, the Trojan war having become, ten years after, “matter of national history”; he explains Athena's anger by the outrage that Ajax son of Oileus did to Cassandra in her temple, though at 4.499 his death is laid to his insolence against Poseidon (on 1.326; see the translation's note on 1.327 and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Telemachus' answer makes the song's subject a consolation: Odysseus was not the only one to lose his homecoming at Troy, “but many other men perished too” (1.354–355).
