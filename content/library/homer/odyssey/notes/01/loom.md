---
summary: Telemachus sends his mother back to “the loom and the distaff”, in Hector's words to Andromache (1.356–358). The loom is Penelope's work and, as the next book tells, her stratagem.
---

“But go into the house and attend to your own work, / the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids / to go about their work” (1.356–358; see [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). The verses are Hector's to Andromache when he leaves her for the last time, word for word, until Telemachus puts “speech” where Hector put “war” (*Iliad* 6.490–493). The loom and the distaff stand for the women's part of the house, as the work of weaving goes with the handmaids (see [[weaving]] and [[handmaids]]). ἐποίχεσθαι, “to go about” the work, is properly “to ply” by moving to and fro: W. W. Merry takes it of work at the upright loom, where the weaver walks from side to side after her shuttle, and compares Sophocles' Egyptians, who weave sitting (*Oedipus at Colonus* 340), and Pindar's “back-and-forth paths of the loom” (*Pythian* 9.18), as he reports them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.358); the Iliad's Chryseis “walks to and fro at the loom” (ἱστὸν ἐποιχομένην, *Iliad* 1.31).

The loom is also Penelope's stratagem. In the assembly of the next book Antinous tells how she set up a great loom in the halls and wove a shroud for Laertes, promising to marry when it was finished, and unwove it every night by torchlight, until one of her women told the suitors (*Odyssey* 2.93–110; again 19.137–156, 24.128–146; see [[penelope]] and [[torches]]). In her upper room she still weaves at her loom, seldom seen by the suitors (*Odyssey* 15.517; see [[upper-room]]).
