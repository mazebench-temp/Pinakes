---
summary: Ithaca is the home the gods have spun for Odysseus' return and the island Calypso would make him forget. Athena goes there to rouse his son, and finds the best men of Ithaca and the islands around it wooing Penelope and wasting his house; in “sea-girt Ithaca”, Telemachus says, there are many kings (1.16–18, 1.57, 1.88, 1.103, 1.245–251, 1.386–404).
---

**The goal of the return.** The gods have spun the thread for Odysseus “to return home / to Ithaca” (1.17–18; see [[nostos]]). Calypso holds him with gentle and wheedling talk “so that he may forget Ithaca”, while he longs to see even the smoke leaping up from his own land (1.56–59; see [[ogygia]]). Telemachus, sure that his father is dead, says that if the suitors were to see him “come home to Ithaca” they would pray to be nimbler of foot rather than richer in gold and clothing (1.163–165).

**Athena on Ithaca.** “I will go to Ithaca”, Athena tells Zeus (1.88): Ἰθάκηνδʼ ἐσελεύσομαι, which Merry renders “will go to the island and into the town” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.88). She comes down from Olympus and stands “in the land of Ithaca at the doorway of Odysseus” (1.102–103; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). Telemachus asks the stranger how sailors brought him to Ithaca, “For I do not think that you came here on foot” (1.171–173), a touch of humor, Merry says, in the mouth of an islander (on 1.173). The answer gives the poem's first view of the island beyond the house: the stranger's ship lies “by the fields, away from the city”, in the harbor of Rheithron under wooded Neion, and Laertes lives “far away in the country” on his vineyard (1.185–193; see [[rheithron]], [[neion]] and [[laertes]]).

The verses return when Telemachus speaks to the disguised Odysseus and when Penelope does, the second time with εὐδείελον, “clear-seen”, in place of κραναήν, “craggy” (16.122–124, 19.130–132; see [[the-suitors]], [[dulichium]], [[samos]], [[zacynthus]] and [[craggy]]).

**Sea-girt Ithaca and its kings.** ἐν ἀμφιάλῳ Ἰθάκῃ, “in sea-girt Ithaca”, comes three times in the exchange with Antinous and Eurymachus (1.386, 1.395, 1.401; see [[sea-girt]]). Antinous prays that Zeus may never make Telemachus king in Ithaca, “which is yours by birth from your father” (1.386–387); Telemachus answers that there are “other kings of the Achaeans, / many in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old” (1.394–395; see [[kings]]); and Eurymachus wishes that no man may rob him of his possessions “while Ithaca is still inhabited” (1.404). Merry takes Ἰθάκης ἔτι ναιετοώσης as the island standing for its people, “while Ithaca stands” (on 1.404). See [[antinous]] and [[eurymachus]].
