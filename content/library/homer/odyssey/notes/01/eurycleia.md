summary: Eurycleia's first appearance closes the book. She lights Telemachus to his chamber with blazing torches, and the poem gives her lineage and her history, bought by Laertes for the worth of twenty oxen and honored like his wife; she takes his tunic, hangs it up, and shuts him in for the night (1.428–442).

**Her history.** As she comes into the story, the narrator pauses to tell who she is: her father and grandfather (see [[ops]] and [[peisenor-father-of-ops]]), her price, her place in the house. ἐεικοσάβοια, “the worth of twenty oxen”, is a high price for a slave, where at the funeral games of Patroclus a woman skilled in many works is valued at four oxen (*Iliad* 23.704–705; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.431; see the translation's note on 1.431). Laertes honored her “as he honored his devoted wife”, but did not take her to his bed, “for he shunned his wife's anger” (1.432–433; see [[laertes]] and [[bed]]). Of all the slave women of the house she loved Telemachus most, and she had nursed him as a child (1.434–435; see [[handmaids]]).

**The night.** She walks with him carrying the torches, τῷ … φέρε, “carried for him”, ἅμα, “going along with him” (Merry on 1.428; see [[torches]]); οἱ and ἑ in 1.434 are both Telemachus (Merry on 1.434). In the chamber he gives her his tunic, and “She folded and smoothed the tunic, / hung it on a peg beside the corded bed, / and went out of the chamber, and pulled the door to by the handle / of silver, and drew home the bolt with its strap” (1.439–442; see [[tunic]] and [[door]]). Her epithets in the book are κεδνὰ ἰδυῖα, “true-hearted” (1.428), and πυκιμηδής, “shrewd” (γραίης πυκιμηδέος, “the shrewd old woman”, 1.438), a word found only here in Homer.
