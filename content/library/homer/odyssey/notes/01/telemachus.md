summary: The book of Telemachus' waking. Sitting among the suitors and dreaming of his father's return, he is the first to see the stranger at the door; he welcomes the guest, doubts his own parentage, and pours out his grief, and Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, sends him to call an assembly and to sail for news of his father. When she leaves he knows a god has been with him: he sends his mother back to her rooms, faces the suitors, and lies awake all night planning his journey.

**Among the suitors.** He is first seen as the goddess sees him, sitting among the suitors and thinking of his father:

{{quote:3940ed53-6d6c-50aa-9ac4-2ba33d694e66}}

ὀσσόμενος is “seeing in his mind's eye”, Merry explains, and μνηστήρων τῶν μέν, “the suitors here”, is set against αὐτός, the father who would have his honor and his house again (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.115 and 1.116).

**The host.** He is indignant that a stranger should stand long at the doors, and he welcomes the guest, meal first and questions after (1.119–124; see [[nemesis]] and [[xenia]]). He seats the stranger apart from the suitors, “so that he might ask him about his absent father” (1.135). His first words of substance begin with an apology, “Dear stranger, will you be indignant with me for what I say?” (1.158), which Merry explains by the unwillingness to raise a painful subject at a meal (on 1.158). He speaks of his father as dead, his white bones rotting in the rain, and then asks the stranger who he is, from what city and what parents, and whether he is a guest-friend of the house (1.158–177; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]]). Merry hears “a touch of naïve humour, in the mouth of an islander” in “For I do not think that you came here on foot” (1.173), and the same tone in his answer about his birth (on 1.173).

**My mother says that I am his.** The goddess asks whether he, so tall, is truly the son of Odysseus, “Terribly like him are you, in your head and your fine eyes” (1.207–208), and he answers:

{{quote:f79afc23-3e13-5a0b-a4fd-9dc4d1950199}}

The scholia set beside 1.216 a saying of Euripides, that a mother loves her child more than a father does, since she knows it is hers and he only supposes so, and one of Menander, that no one knows whose son he is, but all of us suppose or believe it (Merry on 1.216; see the translation's note on 1.216). Athena answers that the gods have not made his lineage nameless, “since Penelope bore such a son as you” (1.222–223; see [[genee]], [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**The counsel received.** Athena tells him that he has no need “to keep up childish ways, since you are no longer of that age” (1.296–297; see [[nepios]]), and sets before him the fame of Orestes (1.298–302; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). He answers that the stranger has spoken “in friendship, / as a father to his son” (1.307–308), and offers a gift (1.309–313; see [[guest-gifts]]). When she has gone, she has made him think of his father “even more than before”; he is amazed, “for he thought that it was a god”, and goes among the suitors “a man equal to the gods” (1.321–324; see [[equal-to-the-gods]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**Master of the house.** His first act is to send his mother back to her work, “for mine is the power in this house” (1.356–359; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Aristarchus rejected the four verses; Merry doubts the rejection, since something startling in her son's words must account for Penelope's amazement (on 1.360; see the translation's note on 1.356–359). She goes, “for she laid her son's wise word to heart” (1.361), μῦθον πεπνυμένον, with the epithet of the son given to his word. To the suitors he announces an assembly and a warning, and to Antinous he claims his house: “but I will be lord of our own house / and of the slaves whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil for me” (1.397–398; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]] and [[anax]]).

**Wise Telemachus.** The book brings in six of his answers with one verse, “And wise Telemachus answered her face to face” (1.213, 1.230, 1.306, 1.345), with “him” for “her” when he answers Antinous and Eurymachus (1.388, 1.412), shaped like the Iliad's “And wise Antenor answered her face to face” (*Iliad* 3.203; see the Odyssey translation's table of formulas), and his speech to the suitors with “And among them wise Telemachus began to speak” (1.367). Merry derives πεπνυμένος from πνέω, “breathe”, comparing *anima* and *animus* (on 1.213; see [[wise]]). The narrator calls him θεοειδής, “godlike”, when he first sees the goddess (1.113; see [[godlike]]).

**The sleepless night.** Eurycleia lights him to his chamber, built high “in a place with a view all round”; she had loved him most of the slave women, “and had reared him when he was small” (1.425–435; see [[eurycleia]] and [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]). He gives her his tunic, she bolts the door, and “There all night long, covered with sheep's wool, / he planned in his mind the journey that Athena had shown him” (1.436–444; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
