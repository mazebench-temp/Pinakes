summary: In his halls on Olympus Zeus speaks first in the council of the gods. Remembering Aegisthus, he complains that mortals blame the gods for the sorrows their own recklessness brings them; to Athena he answers that he has not forgotten Odysseus, that the anger is Poseidon's, and that the gods will bring him home. On Ithaca Telemachus says that Zeus, not the singer, is to blame for the Achaeans' grim homecoming, and calls on him for requital.

**In the halls of Olympian Zeus.** While Poseidon feasts among the Ethiopians, the other gods are “all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus”, Ζηνὸς ἐνὶ μεγάροισιν Ὀλυμπίου, “and among them the father of men and gods began to speak” (1.27–28; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[olympians]], [[olympian]] and [[father-of-men-and-gods]]). The narrator gives no reason for the gathering; Zeus speaks because he has remembered Aegisthus (1.29–31).

**Mortals blame the gods.** His first words in the poem are a complaint:

{{quote:1645d478-ddf8-5153-88a0-08b2242fcf43}}

Aegisthus is his proof. The gods sent Hermes to warn him neither to kill Agamemnon nor to woo his wife, since vengeance would come from Orestes; he would not be persuaded, “and now he has paid back all at once” (1.35–43; see [[aegisthus]], [[hermes]] and [[orestes]]). ἡμεῖς, “we ourselves” (1.37), is emphatic: the gods were not the cause of his ruin (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.37). His words for the fault, σφῇσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, “through their own recklessness” (1.34), are the proem's words for the companions who ate the cattle of the Sun (1.7), and the poem will use them again of the suitors at their death (22.317, 22.416, 23.67; see [[atasthalie]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**Athena's plea and his answer.** Athena twice addresses him with the verse “Our father, son of Cronus, highest of lords” (1.45, 1.81; see [[son-of-cronus]]), the verse with which she speaks to him in the Iliad, when he has forbidden the gods to help either side (*Iliad* 8.31), and again at the end of the Odyssey (24.473; see [[athena]]). She ends her plea with a reproach, “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (1.62). Zeus, “the cloud-gatherer” (1.63; see [[cloud-gatherer]]), answers his daughter:

{{quote:0363f96f-20b8-5d3a-8923-f4e7a4795248}}

He names the cause, Poseidon's anger for the blinding of his son, and gives his ruling: “But come, let all of us here take thought for / his homecoming, how he may come” (1.76–77; see [[poseidon]] and [[nostos]]). ἡμεῖς οἵδε, “all of us here”, points to the gods present (Merry on 1.76), and the one who is not present will have to yield (1.77–79). In this book he sends no one himself. The plan, Hermes to Ogygia and Athena to Ithaca, is hers, and when the gods sit in council again Zeus answers her with the same verse, “My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!”, and reminds her that the plan was her own (5.22–27).

**Zeus among mortals.** On Ithaca the god of the council becomes the god men speak of. Athena tells Telemachus to listen for “a rumor / from Zeus, which most of all brings report to men” (1.282–283), a report whose source no one can trace, as against what a mortal may tell (Merry on 1.282, comparing *Iliad* 2.93–94, where Rumor, “the messenger of Zeus”, blazes among the Achaeans). When Penelope asks the singer to stop his song of the grim homecoming, Telemachus answers that “It is not the singers / who are to blame, but Zeus, I think, is to blame, who gives / to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (1.347–349; see [[aitios]] and [[phemius]]). The words ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν, ἑκάστῳ, “to each, as he wills”, are Nausicaa's for the prosperity Zeus gives to good and bad alike (6.188–189). His answer is the view Zeus complained of in the council, that mortals blame the gods (1.32–34). Later, before the suitors, Telemachus says that he will call upon the gods “if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done” (1.379); παλίντιτα ἔργα are “deeds of requital” (Merry on 1.379, comparing ἄντιτα ἔργα at 17.60; also 17.51), and he says the same verses in the assembly (2.143–144). Antinous wishes that “the son of Cronus” may never make him king, and Telemachus answers that he would take even that, “if Zeus should grant it” (1.386–390; see [[antinous]]).

**Daughter of Zeus.** The Muse is “goddess, daughter of Zeus” (1.10; see [[muses]]), as Athena is his “child” (1.64).
