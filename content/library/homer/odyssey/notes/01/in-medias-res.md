---
summary: The Odyssey begins in the last year of Odysseus' absence, when all the other Achaeans who escaped are home and the gods decide on his return. The war and the wanderings come into the poem later, in songs and in other men's tales, and in Odysseus' own tale to the Phaeacians.
passages:
  - 1.11-21 | Him alone
---

**Where the story begins.** The proem leaves the starting point to the Muse, “beginning where you will” (1.10), and the story begins at once with “Now”: “Now all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction, / were at home”, and him alone the nymph Calypso held back (1.11–15). Merry takes ἔνθα, “Now”, for the point of time at which the Odyssey begins, the end of the seventh year of Odysseus' stay on Calypso's island (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.11). Then, “as the years circled, the year had come” in which the gods had spun the thread for his return (1.16–17), and later books count it the twentieth year since he left (2.175, 23.102). The first scene is the gods' council on Olympus, and the first human action is in Ithaca, where Athena finds the suitors at their games and Telemachus among them (1.26–112; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]).

**What comes later.** Odysseus is not seen until Book 5, sitting on the shore of Calypso's island and weeping as he looks out over the barren sea (5.81–84). Everything before that comes into the poem as song and story. In this book Phemius sings the grim homecoming of the Achaeans (1.326–327; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]); in the next books Nestor tells Telemachus how Zeus planned a grim homecoming for the Achaeans after Troy (3.130–132), and Menelaus how the gods held him back in Egypt (4.351–352). The wanderings that the proem sums up in ten lines Odysseus tells himself, at the Phaeacian king's table: he names himself and begins on “my homecoming, full of sorrows, which Zeus laid on me as I came from Troy” (9.19, 9.37–38), and the tale runs to the end of Book 12. Horace's wonders, Antiphates, Scylla, the Cyclops and Charybdis, all belong to it (see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]).
