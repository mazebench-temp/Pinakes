---
summary: Telemachus speaks the lines to his mother, in defense of Phemius' song of the Achaeans' grim homecoming. The newest song is the story of the returns, sung in the house of the one man who has not yet come home.
refs: [1.351-352]
---

**The song.** After Athena's departure Phemius is singing to the suitors, who sit listening in silence, “the homecoming of the Achaeans, / the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.326–327; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[phemius]]). Penelope hears it from her upper room, comes down in tears, and asks him to sing one of the many other enchantments he knows and to stop this one, “which always wears down my dear heart in my breast” (1.337–344). Merry takes her “always” to show that the song was already popular (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.341).

**Telemachus' answer.** Telemachus asks why she begrudges the singer giving delight in whatever way his mind is roused. The singers are not to blame, but Zeus, who gives to toiling men, to each, as he wills; there is no blame on Phemius for singing the evil fate of the Danaans, “for men praise that song the more / which comes newest to those who listen” (1.346–352; see [[no-blame]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Let her heart bear to listen, “for Odysseus was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming / in Troy, but many other men perished too” (1.353–355). Then he sends her back to her loom and her handmaids (1.356–359; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]).

**The newest song.** What makes the song new is its subject. Everything that Phemius or Demodocus sings, Merry notes, is taken from the Trojan war, with the one exception of the loves of Ares and Aphrodite, and he supposes that the homecoming of the Achaeans was a single lay from the cycle of the returns (on 1.326). In Book 1 that story is not finished. Odysseus is the one Achaean still away, “him alone, wanting his homecoming and his wife” (1.13), and the song that the suitors listen to in his hall is the story to which his own return will belong. Plato's Socrates takes the newness for a newness of manner, which a city must fear; in Telemachus' mouth it is the newness of the news (see the entry above).
