---
summary: Zeus complains, “Ah, how mortals now blame the gods!” (αἰτιόωνται, 1.32), and in the hall Telemachus says it himself: “It is not the singers / who are to blame, but Zeus, I think, is to blame” (1.347–348).
grc: [αἴτιος, αἰτιόωνται]
en: [blame]
---

The two speeches answer each other across the book (see [[who-is-to-blame]]). Zeus' verb is αἰτιάομαι, “lay the blame on”; Telemachus' word is the adjective, αἴτιος, the one against whom the charge lies, used, as most often in Homer, to deny it of one party and lay it on another: not the singers, but Zeus, “who gives / to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (1.348–349; see the translation's note on 1.349). Merry gives Eustathius' sense, that the singers are not to blame for the misfortunes they sing (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.347). The verse-turn αἴτιος, ἀλλὰ Ζεύς comes back when Odysseus tells the shade of Ajax that no one else was to blame for his death, but Zeus (11.558–559).
