summary: Book 1 sets out the first half of Aristotle's outline. The man is away, alone and held back, Poseidon's anger keeps him from home, and in his house the suitors eat up his substance while his son fears for his life; the second half, the return and the revenge, is foretold by the gods and by Athena.

  - 1.11-21 | Him alone
---

Each clause of Aristotle's first sentence has its place in the opening of the poem.

- **Away from home for many years.** All the others who escaped destruction are at home, “but him alone, wanting his homecoming and his wife, / the lady nymph Calypso … held back” (1.11–15), and the year has come in which the gods have spun the thread for his return (1.16–17; see [[calypso]]).
- **Watched closely by Poseidon.** All the gods pity him except Poseidon, who rages relentlessly against him until he reaches his own land (1.19–21). Zeus gives the reason: Poseidon is angry because Odysseus blinded his son the Cyclops, and “does not kill him, but drives him wandering from his native land” (1.68–75; see [[poseidon]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
- **Alone.** The proem has already told how he lost his companions, “eager though he was” (1.5–9), and Athena tells the gods that he “long now suffers woes far from his friends / on a sea-washed island” (1.49–50).
- **His property wasted by suitors.** Athena means to send Telemachus to speak out to the suitors, “who are forever / slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (1.91–92), and Telemachus tells the stranger that the best men of the islands woo his mother “and wear down my house” (1.245–251; see [[the-suitors]]).
- **His son plotted against.** In this book the plot is still only Telemachus' fear: “soon they will tear me apart as well” (1.251). The plot itself comes in Book 4, when Antinous asks for a ship and twenty companions to lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and Same (4.669–672; see [[antinous]]).

The second sentence, the return, the recognitions and the revenge, is in Book 1 only as hope and prophecy. Athena wishes that Odysseus might stand at the doors with helmet, shield and spears and engage the suitors (1.255–266), tells Telemachus to think how he may kill them in his halls, “whether by guile or openly” (1.294–296), and foretells that his father will not be away much longer (1.203–205); Telemachus warns the suitors that they may yet perish inside his house unavenged (1.379–380). What the book itself tells, the gods' council, Athena's visit and Telemachus' first stand, is in Aristotle's terms episode (see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[athena-visits-telemachus]] and [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).
