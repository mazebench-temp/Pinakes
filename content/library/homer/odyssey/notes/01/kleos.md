---
summary: Athena sends Telemachus out “so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.95), as Orestes won fame by avenging his father (1.298–300). Telemachus grieves that his father, who would have left “great fame for his son” had he died at Troy, has been snatched away “without report” (1.239–241); Penelope speaks of the deeds “which singers make famous” and of her husband's fame, “wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.338–344).
grc: [ἀκλειῶς, κλείουσιν, ἐπικλείουσʼ]
en: [praise]
---

**Fame and report.** κλέος is what is heard, and the book uses it both ways. With an epithet it is “fame”: κλέος ἐσθλόν, “noble fame” (1.95), μέγα κλέος, “great fame” (1.240), κλέος εὐρύ, “whose fame is wide” (1.344). Bare, it is the report that travels: Telemachus may hear “a rumor / from Zeus, which most of all brings report to men” (1.282–283). Merry holds that κλέος in Homer means only “report”, “rumour”, “tidings”, as its kinship with κλύω, “hear”, suggests, and that the sense of “fame” resides in an epithet such as ἐσθλόν, εὐρύ or μέγα, except in a few passages of the Iliad where it is won in war (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.241; see the translation's note on 1.241).

**Without report.** What Telemachus cannot bear is that no report has come. Had his father died at Troy, “all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him, / and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come” (1.239–240); Merry takes the κλέος as the glory of which the tomb would be the lasting memorial (on 1.240; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report; / he is gone, out of sight, unheard of” (ἀκλειῶς … ἄιστος ἄπυστος, 1.241–242). Merry explains the pair as “out of sight, out of hearing”: no one had seen him, and no one had heard of him from one who had (on 1.242). The Iliad's ἀκλειῶς is Hector's “without glory” (*Iliad* 22.304); here it is the absence of news, which Athena's journey is meant to supply (1.281–283).

**Fame for the son.** Athena gives the journey a second purpose, “so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.95); Eustathius, as Merry reports, explains it as the fame of one who toiled for his father (on 1.95). Her example is Orestes: “have you not heard what fame brilliant Orestes won / among all men, when he killed his father's slayer” (1.298–299), and she ends, “be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (1.302; see [[orestes]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**The singers' fame.** Penelope calls Phemius' songs “deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous” (κλείουσιν, 1.338), and her husband the man “whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.344), a phrase for the whole of Greece (Merry on 1.344). Telemachus answers that “men praise that song the more / which comes newest to those who listen” (ἐπικλείουσʼ, 1.351–352). Merry hears in the compound the applause men add when the song is done, and reports that Plato, quoting the lines, has ἐπιφρονέουσιν, which suggests a variant ἐπικλύουσʼ (on 1.351; see the translation's note on 1.351–352 and [[song-and-fame]]).
