summary: Two examples from the house of Atreus: Zeus cites Aegisthus to show that mortals bring their sorrows on themselves (1.32–43), and Athena cites Orestes to show Telemachus what fame he might win (1.298–302).
refs: [1.35, 1.298]
passages:
  - 1.32-43 | Zeus' example of Aegisthus
  - 1.298-302 | Athena's example of Orestes
---

The poem's first speech is built on an example. Zeus states the general case, that mortals blame the gods though they have sorrows against fate through their own recklessness, and proves it with the story of Aegisthus, who was warned and did not listen, “and now he has paid back all at once” (1.32–43; [[who-is-to-blame]], [[atasthalie]]). Athena turns the same story the other way when she counsels Telemachus: “Or have you not heard what fame brilliant Orestes won / among all men, when he killed his father's slayer, / scheming Aegisthus” (1.298–300), and the example ends in the exhortation it was told for, “You too, my friend … be valiant” (1.301–302; [[born-hereafter]]). Merry finds the passage inconsistent with the counsel of 1.274–278, and reports that Nauck marks 1.293–302 as spurious, and Hentze–Ameis with him (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.293; see the translation's note on 1.293–302).
