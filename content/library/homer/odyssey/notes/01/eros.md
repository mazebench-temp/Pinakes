---
summary: The word comes once, in the formula of the meal's end, “when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (1.150; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).
---

Merry explains the phrase from the Homeric psychology of desire. A man is passive to it, and ἔρος is conceived not as an emotion arising in him but as a property of the object, taken in with enjoyment and sent away at its natural limit, “the satisfaction of the natural want” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.150). He compares Menelaus' saying that men would rather have their fill of sleep, love, song and dance than of war (*Iliad* 13.636–639), and Priam's wish to put away his desire for lament (*Iliad* 24.227), which shows, he says, how early men found that there is a luxury in grief.
