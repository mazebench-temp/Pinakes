---
summary: The end of the suitors' day: they turned to dancing and song “and waited for evening to come; / and as they took their delight black evening came on” (1.422–423), and they went home to sleep.
grc: ["μέλας ἐπὶ ἕσπερος ἦλθε", "ἐπὶ ἕσπερον ἐλθεῖν"]
en: [waited for evening to come, black evening came on]
---

The book closes its day with an evening formula of the Odyssey, μέλας ἐπὶ ἕσπερος ἦλθε, “black evening came on,” after the half-verse μένον δʼ ἐπὶ ἕσπερον ἐλθεῖν, “and waited for evening to come” (1.421–423). The three verses come again when the suitors end another day of feasting in Book 18 (18.304–306). Merry notes that ἐπί in 1.422 is an adverb with ἐλθεῖν, “to come on,” as the next verse shows (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.422). Then “they went to lie down, each to his own house” (1.424), in the verse with which the gods go to sleep at the end of the first book of the *Iliad* (*Iliad* 1.606).
