**The absent god.** The god who hates Odysseus is at the edge of the world, at the feast, while the others are all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus (1.26–27; see [[poseidon]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). ἀντιόων, Merry explains, is a future, “to take part in” the hecatomb (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.25; see [[hecatomb]]). In the Iliad it is Zeus who has gone to the blameless Ethiopians with all the gods, so that Thetis must wait twelve days to plead with him (*Iliad* 1.423–427; see [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]).

**Divided in two.** Merry compares Herodotus' two kinds of Ethiopians in Xerxes' army, those above Egypt and those “from the sunrise” (on 1.23; Herodotus 7.69–70). The verse was disputed in antiquity. Strabo reports that Aristarchus read οἱ μὲν δυσομένου Ὑπερίονος, οἱ δʼ ἀνιόντος, as the text has it, and Crates ἠμὲν δυσομένου Ὑπερίονος ἠδʼ ἀνιόντος, “both where Hyperion sets and where he rises”. Crates, following the mathematicians, held that Oceanus fills the torrid zone, so that there are Ethiopians on either side of it, divided by the Ocean; Aristarchus took the verse to speak of the Ethiopia known to the Greeks, by Egypt. Strabo thought the reading made no difference to either view. In his own judgment the Ethiopians, like the Egyptians, are divided in two by the Nile, toward the sunrise and toward the sunset; or Homer, who heard from those who had sailed along Libya that they called the farthest lands they reached Ethiopian, divided them into an eastern and a western people, not knowing what lay between. He adds Ephorus' report of a story told at Tartessus, that Ethiopians overran Libya, and that some of them stayed there and others held much of the coast (*Geography* 1.2.24–26; see [[oceanus]]).
