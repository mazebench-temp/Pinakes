Athena proposes it in the council of the gods (1.88–95), gives it to Telemachus as counsel in the likeness of Mentes, a ship with twenty oarsmen, Nestor at Pylos first and then Menelaus at Sparta (1.279–292), and he lies awake all night planning it (1.443–444).

**The plan.** In the council Athena says what she will do in Ithaca: rouse Odysseus' son to call an assembly and speak out to the suitors, “And I will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos / to learn of his dear father's homecoming, if he may hear of it somewhere, / and so that noble fame may be his among men” (1.88–95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[pylos]] and [[sparta]]). Merry reports Eustathius' gloss on the fame: that of a man who has labored on his father's behalf (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.95; see [[kleos]]).

**The counsel.** In the likeness of Mentes she gives him the plan as advice, after the assembly and the sending home of the suitors:

{{quote:26a8dbae-ffdc-506b-bc92-369cf885b518}}

Merry sets the “some mortal” who may tell him, an eyewitness, against the rumor “from Zeus”, a report whose human source cannot be traced; the pair answers Telemachus' lament that his father is “out of sight, unheard of” (1.242), since no one has seen him or heard of him from one who has (on 1.282 and 1.242; see the translation's note on 1.241). Menelaus “was the last of the bronze-armored Achaeans to come home”: δεύτατος, Merry explains, is the superlative of δεύτερος in the sense of “later” (on 1.286; see [[nestor]], [[menelaus]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). What Telemachus does after the journey depends on what he hears: if his father is alive, he can endure another year; if he is dead, he is to heap up a mound for him and give his mother to a husband (1.287–292; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).

**The night.** At the end of the day he lies awake: “There all night long, covered with sheep's wool, / he planned in his mind the journey that Athena had shown him” (1.443–444; see [[telemachus]]). πέφραδε, “had shown”, is the verb of her counsel, μῦθον πέφραδε πᾶσι, “declare your word to all” (1.273). The journey ends as it begins: at Sparta, when Athena comes to send him home, sleep does not hold him either, and he lies awake all night with cares for his father (15.7–8, a plain rendering).
