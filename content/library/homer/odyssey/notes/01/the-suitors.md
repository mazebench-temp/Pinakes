summary: The suitors first appear through Athena's eyes, at their game pieces before the doors of Odysseus' house, and then at their feast, with Phemius singing for them under compulsion. Telemachus and the goddess describe them as men who eat another man's substance, the nobles of four islands wooing his mother; Athena foresees their bitter wedding, and Telemachus warns them, before Antinous and Eurymachus, that he will order them out of his house.

**At the doors.** Athena, coming down to Ithaca, finds them first:

{{quote:72ad5563-f42a-5b64-ac3c-010bd4a32226}}

εὗρε, Merry explains, does not mean that she was looking for them, but that she “came on them” there (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.106). For their game he quotes Athenaeus' account of a game in which each suitor cast at a piece called Penelope (on 1.107; see [[game-pieces]] and [[the-game-of-penelope]]). Heralds and attendants mix their wine and wash and set their tables (1.109–112; see [[heralds]]).

**The feast.** The proud suitors come in, sit down in order, wash their hands and eat, and when they have put away the desire for food and drink they turn to “song and dancing; for these are the accompaniments of a feast”; a herald gives the lyre to Phemius, “who sang among the suitors under compulsion” (1.144–155; see [[feast]], [[feast-type-scene]] and [[phemius]]). Telemachus has given his guest a meal apart, and Merry sees in the separate provision a sign of the unfriendly relations between the young prince and the suitors (on 1.140; see the translation's note on 1.140).

**As others see them.** To the stranger Telemachus says that “These men care for these things, the lyre and song, / easily, since they eat another man's substance without payment”, and that if they saw Odysseus come home, “they would all pray to be nimbler of foot / rather than richer in gold and clothing” (1.159–165). The goddess asks what feast this is, and answers herself: “For this is surely no shared feast; / so overbearingly, like men committing outrage, they seem to me / to feast through the house” (1.226–228; see [[hybris]]). The shared feast, ἔρανος, Merry explains, is held at each house in turn, which is what Telemachus will bid the suitors do, “eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house” (1.374–375; on 1.226 and 1.375; see the translation's note on 1.226 and [[xenia]]). Telemachus tells who they are:

{{quote:f301d159-791c-5972-b640-83f79a3e5f96}}

They are the four islands of his count in Book 16, where the suitors number a hundred and eight (16.247–251; see [[dulichium]], [[samos]], [[zacynthus]] and [[ithaca]]).

**Their epithets.** The narrator twice calls them μνηστῆρες ἀγήνορες, “the proud suitors” (1.106, 1.144; see [[proud]]). They are ὑπερφίαλοι, “overbearing” (1.134; see [[overbearing]]), and to Athena “shameless”, ἀναιδέσι (1.254; see [[anaideia]]); Telemachus addresses them as “Suitors of my mother, overweening in your arrogance” (ὑπέρβιον ὕβριν ἔχοντες, 1.368).

**Their end foreseen.** Athena wishes that Odysseus would come and engage them: “they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (1.266). Merry renders πικρόγαμοι “would rue their wooing”, a word coined in anger, and compares Antinous' threat to the beggar of a bitter Egypt and Cyprus (on 1.266; 17.448). Her counsel ends with the killing, “how you may kill the suitors in your halls, / whether by guile or openly” (1.295–296; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]), and Telemachus' warning ends with their death: “then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (1.380; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). The evening ends with dancing and song, and they go home each to his own house (1.421–424).
