**The proem.** The proem tells of the man who wandered far after the sack of Troy and suffered many sorrows on the sea, “seeking to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions” (1.1–5; see [[proem]] and [[odysseus]]). Of everything he saw and suffered it names one event only, the one in which he lost the last of them. νήπιοι, “fools”, is the narrator's exclamation over men who did not see where their deed would lead (see [[nepios]]), and σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο, “they perished through their own recklessness”, is the verse-end of Sthenelus on the Seven against Thebes (*Iliad* 4.409; see [[atasthalie]]). κατὰ … ἤσθιον, “ate up”, is κατεσθίω with the preposition standing apart from its verb, the separation that grammarians call tmesis (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.8). The Sun is named by both his names, “Hyperion the Sun” (see [[hyperion]] and [[helios]]), and the proem makes him the one who “took away from them the day of their homecoming” (1.9; see [[nostos]]). In Odysseus' own telling Zeus strikes the ship at the Sun's demand, and “the god” takes away their homecoming (12.415–419).

**The cause of what follows.** The loss of the companions is why the story begins as it does. “All the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction, / were at home”, and Odysseus alone was held by Calypso (1.11–15; see [[calypso]]), to whose island the gods brought him after the wreck (12.447–450). The proem's word for the companions' folly comes back a few lines later in Zeus' first speech: mortals blame the gods, “but they themselves / through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate” (1.33–34; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). His example is Aegisthus, who was warned and did it all the same (1.35–43; see [[aegisthus]]); the companions too were warned, by Teiresias and by Circe (11.104–117, 12.127–141).

**The suitors.** Book 1 also shows the cattle of another master being eaten. Athena will have Telemachus speak out to the suitors, “who are forever / slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (1.91–92), and Telemachus says that they eat another man's substance without payment (1.160, 1.377; see [[cattle]] and [[the-suitors]]). Teiresias foretold that Odysseus would find them in his house (11.115–117), and at the end of the poem the suitors too are said to have met a shameful doom through their recklessness (22.416).
