---
summary: Phemius sings for the suitors under compulsion. After their meal a herald gives him the lyre; at evening he sings the grim homecoming of the Achaeans, and Penelope comes down to ask him to stop; Telemachus defends him, and praises him to the suitors as a singer “like the gods in his voice” (1.153–155, 1.325–371).
# The singer unnamed: ὁ φορμίζων (1.155), ἀοιδός (1.325, 1.336, 1.346, 1.370), the man Penelope bids sing (1.339–340),
# τούτῳ, "this man" (1.350).
refs: [1.155, 1.325-326, 1.336, 1.339-340, 1.346-347, 1.350, 1.370-371]
---

**Under compulsion.** He enters the poem when the suitors have eaten: “And a herald put a beautiful lyre in the hands / of Phemius, who sang among the suitors under compulsion. / And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song” (1.153–155; see [[lyre]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). ἀνάγκῃ, “under compulsion”, is said once more of him, on the day the suitors die, when he clasps Odysseus' knees and pleads that he came to the house unwilling, as Telemachus could tell (*Odyssey* 22.330–331, 22.350–353; see [[i-am-self-taught]]). Telemachus then calls him ἀναίτιος, “not to blame” (*Odyssey* 22.356), as in this book he says that the singers are not the ones to blame (αἴτιοι, 1.347–348).

**The song of the returns.** At evening “The renowned singer was singing to them, and they in silence / sat listening; and he sang of the homecoming of the Achaeans” (1.325–326). Penelope comes down weeping and asks him, “Phemius, you know many other enchantments for mortals, / deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous”, to sing one of those and stop this one (1.337–341); Telemachus answers that the singer is not to blame for what Zeus gives, and that men praise the newest song (1.346–355; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]], [[aitios]] and [[penelope]]). Her words are the Odyssey's description of the singer's matter, “deeds of men and gods”, and its word for the song's effect, θελκτήρια, “enchantments”, the word of Aphrodite's charms in the Iliad (*Iliad* 14.215; see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[song-and-fame]]).

**A singer like the gods.** When his mother has gone, Telemachus quiets the suitors, “since it is a fine thing to listen to a singer / such as this one is, like the gods in his voice” (1.370–371; see [[like-a-god-in-his-voice]]). The epithets of the book give him all the singer's honors: περικλυτός, “renowned” (1.325), θεῖος, “divine” (1.336), ἐρίηρος, “trusty” (1.346). Merry notes that everything Phemius sings for the suitors, or Demodocus for the Phaeacians, is taken from the Trojan war, except the song of Ares and Aphrodite (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.326; see [[demodocus]]).
