---
summary: Antinous prays that Zeus may never make Telemachus king in sea-girt Ithaca; Telemachus answers that it is no bad thing to be a king, but that there are many other kings of the Achaeans in Ithaca, and that he will be lord of his own house (1.384–402).
---

**Who will be king.** Antinous meets Telemachus' bold words with a prayer: “May the son of Cronus never make you king in sea-girt Ithaca, / which is yours by birth from your father” (1.386–387; see [[antinous]]). The emphatic σέ γε, Merry explains, shows that Antinous would wish the kingship for himself, or points a sneer at so young a firebrand (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.386). Telemachus answers that he would take it if Zeus gave it: “For it is no bad thing to be a king; at once his house / grows rich, and he himself is more honored. / But there are other kings of the Achaeans, / many in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old; / let one of them have this, since brilliant Odysseus is dead; / but I will be lord of our own house” (1.390–397). The taunt, Merry says, lies in pretending to find in Antinous' words a kindly wish to spare him the troubles of a king, when Antinous himself is eager to be one (on 1.391).

**Many kings.** βασιλῆες are here the nobles of the island, young and old, as Antinous and Eurymachus are called kings later in the poem (18.64, 24.179), and as twelve kings of the Phaeacians rule under Alcinous (8.390–391), the parallels Merry gives (on 1.394; see [[alcinous]]). Eurymachus leaves to the gods the question who will be king of the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca, “these things lie on the knees of the gods”, and wishes that Telemachus may keep his possessions and rule over his own house (1.400–402; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]] and [[eurymachus]]). The kingship of the island is left open; the lordship of the house, ἄναξ (1.397), Telemachus claims as his own (see [[anax]], [[basileus]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]).
