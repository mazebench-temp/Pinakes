The book is a sequence of exchanges, and each speaker has a verse of reply of the right length for his name. Zeus answers Athena's reproach with τὴν δʼ ἀπαμειβόμενος προσέφη νεφεληγερέτα Ζεύς (1.63; [[cloud-gatherer]]). In the house of Odysseus the stranger answers Telemachus with τὸν δʼ αὖτε προσέειπε θεά, γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him in turn” (1.178, 1.221), and Telemachus has a verse of his own, τὴν (τὸν) δʼ αὖ Τηλέμαχος πεπνυμένος ἀντίον ηὔδα, built like the verse of the wise Trojan elder in the *Iliad*, “And wise Antenor answered her face to face” (*Iliad* 3.203; [[wise]]). He answers the stranger with it three times, his mother once, and Antinous and Eurymachus at the end of the day (1.213, 1.230, 1.306, 1.345, 1.388, 1.412).

The two suitors are given verses with their fathers' names: “Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him in turn” (1.383), which keeps the shape of “Then Automedon, son of Diores, spoke to him in turn” in the *Iliad* (*Iliad* 17.474), and “Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face” (1.399). The frame can also carry the speaker's mood, as when Athena hears Telemachus' complaint: τὸν δʼ ἐπαλαστήσασα προσηύδα Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, “And chafing at this, Pallas Athena spoke to him” (1.252).
