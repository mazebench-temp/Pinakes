summary: The first ten lines of the Odyssey. The poet asks the Muse to tell of the man of many turns, who sacked Troy, saw the cities of many men and suffered on the sea trying to bring his companions home, and lost them through their own recklessness; he does not name the man, and leaves it to the goddess where to begin.

The proem runs as one long period, from the man to the god who took away his companions' homecoming (1.1–9), and ends with the request (1.10).

1. **The man.** ἄνδρα, “the man”, is the first word, as μῆνιν, “wrath”, is the first word of the Iliad. He has an epithet, πολύτροπον, “of many turns”, and no name: Odysseus is first named at 1.21 (see [[of-many-turns]] and [[odysseus]]).
2. **His story.** Relative clauses unfold the epithet. He was driven far astray after he had sacked Troy (1.1–2), he saw the cities of many men and learned their mind (1.3), and on the sea he suffered many sorrows, “seeking to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions” (1.4–5; see [[nostos]] and [[algos]]).
3. **The failure.** “But even so he did not save his companions, eager though he was” (1.6). They perished through their own recklessness, fools who ate the cattle of Hyperion the Sun, and the god took away the day of their homecoming (1.7–9). This is the only adventure the proem names. Odysseus tells it himself in Book 12, and ends it with the shipwreck, in which “the god took away their homecoming” (θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον, 12.419; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). Their ἀτασθαλίαι, “recklessness” (1.7), is the word that Zeus uses a few lines later of mortals who “through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate”, with Aegisthus for his example (1.32–35; see [[atasthalie]]).
4. **The request.** “Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, beginning where you will, tell us too” (1.10). With ἁμόθεν, “from some point”, the Muse chooses where the singer takes up the story, as Demodocus begins “taking it up from where” the Greeks sailed away (ἔνθεν ἑλών, 8.500); with καὶ ἡμῖν, “us too”, the poet asks for himself and his hearers, that they may know as she knows, or be told as others have been. Aristarchus thought the καί superfluous (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.10). The point she chooses follows at once: “Now all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction, / were at home” (1.11–12), which Merry takes for the end of the seventh year of Odysseus' stay on Calypso's island (on 1.11; see [[in-medias-res]]).

**The two proems.** The Iliad's proem names Achilles in its first line and the plan of Zeus in its fifth; the Odyssey's names neither its hero nor any plan, and its only gods are the Muse, Zeus, named as her father, and the Sun. The Iliad counts the cost of the wrath to the Achaeans; the Odyssey begins with a man who could not save his own men. Both end by fixing the point from which the song begins (see the entry above).

**Another reading.** The Suda, explaining the names for a knave, says that men who are mischievous and not simple are called περιτρίμματα, “worn-out rogues”, κρόταλα, “rattles”, and πολύκροτοι, a word of the same family as the rattle, and that some therefore think the first line of the Odyssey should be written ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, πολύκροτον (Suda τ 988, s.v. τρῖμμα; see [[of-many-turns]]).

**Later readers.** Aristotle quotes the first line, with the Iliad's, as a proem that gives a sample of the subject, so that the hearers know in advance what the story is about (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415a12–17; see [[prooimion]] and [[aristotle]]). Strabo takes the third line as the poets' praise of travel: the poets count as the wisest of the heroes those who went abroad and wandered, “for they set great store on seeing the cities of many men and knowing their mind”, and the poet gave this to Odysseus, whom he adorns above all with every excellence (*Geography* 1.1.16, 1.2.4; see [[strabo]]). Beside the sack of Troy in the second line Merry sets Cicero's remark that Homer called not Ajax or Achilles but Ulysses the sacker of cities (*Letters to Friends* 10.13.2; Merry on 1.2; see [[sacker-of-cities]] and [[cicero]]). Livius Andronicus began his Latin Odyssey with the first line, *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum* (see [[odusia]]), and Horace turned the opening into Latin twice (see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]).
