summary: In the first book γαῖα and αἶα are above all the land of home, which Odysseus longs for and Poseidon keeps him from; the earth is also what Atlas' pillars hold apart from heaven and what Athena's golden sandals cross (1.21, 1.41, 1.54, 1.58–59, 1.75, 1.98, 1.203, 1.290, 1.407).
# αἴης (1.41, 1.75, 1.203) and ἄρουρα, "fields" (1.407), are claimed in the Iliad by the Book 2 note.
grc: [αἴης, ἄρουρα]
en: [fields]
---

**The land of home.** Poseidon rages against Odysseus “until he reached his own land” (1.20–21) and “drives him wandering from his native land” (1.75; see [[poseidon]]); Odysseus, eager to see even the smoke leaping up “from his own land”, longs to die (1.58–59). Orestes takes his vengeance “when he comes of age and longs for his own land” (1.40–41; see [[orestes]]). The stranger promises that Odysseus “will not be away much longer from his dear native land” (1.203), and tells Telemachus, if he hears his father is dead, to “come home to your dear native land” and heap up a mound (1.290–291; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[nostos]]). Eurymachus asks from what land the stranger claims to be, “and where are his birthplace and his native fields” (1.406–407).

**Earth and heaven.** Atlas holds the tall pillars “that hold earth and heaven apart” (1.53–54; see [[atlas]] and [[sky]]). Athena's golden sandals carry her “over the waters / and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind” (1.97–98; see [[sandals]]). The stranger insists that Odysseus “has not yet died upon the earth” (1.196; see [[upon-the-earth]]).
