**An appeal to the gods.** Telemachus ends his warning to the suitors: “then eat it up; but I will call upon the gods who live forever, / if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done; / then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (1.378–380; see [[zeus]], [[the-suitors]] and [[gods-who-live-forever]]). ἐπιβώσομαι, “I will call upon”, is a contracted future of ἐπιβοάω (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.378), and παλίντιτα ἔργα are “deeds of requital” (on 1.379). He says the same verses to the people in the assembly (2.143–145).

**Claims and wishes.** εὔχομαι, the verb of prayer, means “claim to be” whenever it occurs in this book, in the questions and answers about the stranger (1.172, 1.180, 1.187, 1.406, 1.418; see [[euchomai]]). ἀράομαι, “pray”, is used of the suitors' wishes. If Odysseus came home, “they would all pray to be nimbler of foot / rather than richer in gold and clothing” (1.164–165), and when Penelope goes up to her room, “they all prayed to lie beside her in bed” (1.366, as 18.213). Like the horsemen of the Iliad who “have prayed for” the ivory cheekpiece (*Iliad* 4.143), these are wishes, not prayers to a god. The one plea to a god in the book is a goddess's: Athena reminds Zeus of Odysseus' sacrifices at Troy, the worshiper's claim to be heard (1.60–62; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
