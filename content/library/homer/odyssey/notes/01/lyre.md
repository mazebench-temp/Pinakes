# κίθαριν (1.153), φορμίζων (1.155), κίθαρις (1.159): in the Iliad these are claimed only by its notes. κίθαρις and
# φόρμιγξ name one instrument: the singer who takes the κίθαρις plays it, φορμίζων.
grc: [κίθαριν, φορμίζων, κίθαρις]

**The singer's lyre.** When the suitors have eaten, “a herald put a beautiful lyre in the hands / of Phemius, who sang among the suitors under compulsion. / And he, playing the lyre, struck up a fine song” (1.153–155; see [[phemius]] and [[heralds]]). The instrument is called κίθαρις as it is handed to him and played with the verb of the φόρμιγξ, φορμίζων, “playing the lyre”: the two words name one instrument. ἀνεβάλλετο, “struck up”, is the regular word for the prelude to a song, a few chords before the singing begins (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.155). Later in the book he sings the homecoming of the Achaeans, and the suitors turn to dancing and song until evening (1.325–327, 1.421–422; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[feast-type-scene]]).

**The lyre and song.** Telemachus speaks of the suitors' pleasure with contempt: “These men care for these things, the lyre and song, / easily, since they eat another man's substance without payment” (1.159–160; see [[the-suitors]]). The pair is the Iliad's: Polydamas, telling Hector that the gods give different men different gifts, gives “to another dancing, to another the lyre and song” (*Iliad* 13.730–731; see [[different-gifts]]), and Hector tells Paris that his lyre would be no help to him in the dust (*Iliad* 3.54–55). In the Odyssey the lyre is the companion of the feast, “which the gods have made the companion of the feast” (*Odyssey* 17.270–271), and Phemius' lyre goes with him to the end (*Odyssey* 22.332–341, 23.143–145).
