---
summary: The women of Odysseus' house serve the guest and the suitors: a handmaid brings the water for the hands, the housekeeper the bread, and slave women heap bread for the suitors in baskets. Two handmaids attend Penelope when she comes down to the hall, and Telemachus sends her back to set them to their work. Laertes on his farm is cared for by an old handmaid, and Eurycleia, whom Laertes bought, loved Telemachus most of the slave women.
---

**At the meal.** For the guest, “And a handmaid brought water for the hands in a pitcher and poured it” (1.136), and “the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them, / adding many dainties, giving freely of what she had” (1.139–140; see [[hand-washing]], [[basin-and-pitcher]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). The ταμίη, the housekeeper who gives the household its portions, does what she can for an unexpected guest, and W. W. Merry takes her dainties to be probably the leavings of an earlier meal (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.140). For the suitors “slave women heaped bread beside them in baskets” (1.147).

**Laertes' handmaid.** The stranger says that old Laertes lives on his farm “with an old handmaid, who sets before him food and drink / whenever weariness takes hold of his limbs” (1.191–192; see [[laertes]]). At the end of the poem she is the old Sicilian woman who looks after him in the country (γυνὴ Σικελὴ γρηΰς, *Odyssey* 24.211–212; ἀμφίπολος Σικελή, 24.366), and Merry gives Σικελή as her name (Merry on 1.191).

**Penelope's handmaids.** Penelope comes down to the suitors “not alone; two handmaids went along with her” (1.331), the verse of Helen going to the wall (*Iliad* 3.143), and when she stands by the doorpost, “a devoted handmaid stood on either side of her” (1.335; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Merry notes that ladies were attended by handmaids when they came among men, as Helen and Andromache are in the Iliad, and that Penelope herself says so (*Odyssey* 18.182–184: “I will not go among the men alone, for I feel shame”); ἀμφίπολος is always feminine in Homer, and its formation points to the custom (Merry on 1.331). Telemachus sends her back to the loom and the distaff, to “tell your handmaids / to go about their work” (1.357–358; see [[weaving]] and [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]), and she goes up to her room “with her handmaids” (1.362; see [[upper-room]]).

**Eurycleia.** At night the old nurse lights Telemachus to his chamber; Laertes had bought her in her first youth, and “she loved him most / of the slave women, and had reared him when he was small” (1.430–435; see [[eurycleia]]). The household has its men slaves as well, “the slaves whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil for me”, Telemachus says (1.398; see [[house]]).
