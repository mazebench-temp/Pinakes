summary: Athena comes to Ithaca “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (1.105). As Mentes she tells Telemachus who she is and where she is bound, claims the old guest-friendship of their houses, and remembers Odysseus in her father's house; when she is gone, Telemachus gives the suitors the same account of the stranger, though in his heart he knows the goddess (1.417–420).

**The likeness.** εἰδομένη ξείνῳ, Ταφίων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (1.105; see [[athena]], [[divine-epiphany]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). The verse ends as the Iliad's verse does when Apollo comes to Hector as “Mentes, the leader of the Cicones” (*Iliad* 17.73; see [[mentes]]): the same name and the same office, but another people and another man (see the translation's note on 1.105). The narrator names the man whose shape the goddess takes, and so Mentes is a real lord of Taphos in the poem, as Mentor of Ithaca is when she takes his shape in the next book (2.268). Merry describes the Taphian islands as islets off the coast of Leucas, inhabited by Taphians and Teleboans, a race of pirates (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.105; see [[taphians]] and [[taphos]]).

**Who he is.** Telemachus asks the stranger the host's questions: who he is and from where, on what ship he came, and whether he is newly come or “a guest-friend / from our fathers' time” (1.169–177; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[xenia]]). The answer comes in the same order. “I claim to be Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus, / and I am lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar” (1.180–181; see [[anchialus-father-of-mentes]] and [[lovers-of-the-oar]]). He is sailing to men of another tongue, to Temese, for bronze, with a cargo of iron, and his ship lies away from the city, in the harbor of Rheithron under wooded Neion (1.182–186; see [[temese]], [[rheithron]], [[neion]] and [[here-my-ship-stands]]). Aristarchus held that ὧδε, “as you see” (1.182), never means “here” in Homer, and Buttmann disputed it (Merry on 1.182; see the translation's note on 1.182). The houses are guest-friends “from our fathers' time, / from of old”, as the old man Laertes can tell him (1.187–193; see [[laertes]]), and Mentes has come because he heard that Odysseus was at home (1.194–195).

**What the stranger knows.** The tale is false in its persons and true in its news. “For brilliant Odysseus has not yet died upon the earth, / but somewhere, still alive, he is held back on the wide sea, / on a sea-washed island” (1.196–198): the second verse is the one with which Proteus tells Menelaus of the one Achaean leader still alive (εἷς δʼ ἔτι που ζωὸς κατερύκεται εὐρέι πόντῳ, 4.498; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]), and only the captors are changed, “harsh men … savage men” (1.198–199) for a nymph (see [[calypso]]). Mentes prophesies, “though I am no seer” (1.200–205; see [[divination]]), and sees the father in the son, whom he used to meet “so very often, / before he embarked for Troy” (1.208–212). In the counsel that follows, he remembers Odysseus in his father's house, back from Ephyra with a man-slaying drug for his arrows (1.255–266; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]). The renderings of other books are plain renderings, not the translation.

**The departure.** Mentes cannot stay for a bath and a gift: his companions are waiting at the ship, and the gift is to be given “on my way back, to carry home” (1.303–304, 1.315–318; see [[guest-gifts]]). Then the goddess goes, like a bird, and Telemachus thinks that it was a god (1.319–323; see [[like-a-bird]]).
