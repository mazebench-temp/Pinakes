---
summary: Antinous is the first suitor to answer Telemachus' warning. The gods themselves, he sneers, must be teaching him to be a man of lofty speech; may the son of Cronus never make him king in sea-girt Ithaca, which is his by birth. Telemachus answers that he would take the kingship if Zeus gave it, but will in any case be lord of his own house.

**The first answer.** He is brought on by his father's name, τὸν δʼ αὖτʼ Ἀντίνοος προσέφη, Εὐπείθεος υἱός, “Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him in turn” (1.383; see [[eupeithes]]), a verse the translation's table of formulas sets beside “Then Automedon, son of Diores, spoke to him in turn” (*Iliad* 17.474). He answers when the suitors have bitten their lips and marveled at Telemachus' boldness (1.381–382; see [[biting-their-lips]]): the gods themselves must be teaching him “to be a man of lofty speech and to speak boldly” (1.384–385). Merry renders the line “It must be the gods themselves that are thy teachers” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.384). ὑψαγόρης, “a man of lofty speech”, is Antinous' word: Homer has it four times, always in his mouth, and in later books he addresses Telemachus as Τηλέμαχʼ ὑψαγόρη, μένος ἄσχετε, “Telemachus, lofty in speech, uncontrollable in your fury” (2.85, 2.303, and again 17.406; see [[lofty-in-speech]]).

**The kingship.** “May the son of Cronus never make you king in sea-girt Ithaca, / which is yours by birth from your father” (1.386–387; see [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[sea-girt]]). Merry hears the emphasis of σέ γε: it is not Telemachus whom Antinous would wish to see king in Ithaca, but himself, unless the words are a sneer at a young fire-eater (on 1.386). Telemachus begins his answer with the words he used to the stranger, “Antinous, will you be indignant with me for what I say?” (1.389, as 1.158), and in Merry's reading pretends to find kindness in Antinous' wish to spare him the troubles of a throne (on 1.391). He will leave the kingship to one of the many kings of the Achaeans in Ithaca, and be lord of his own house (1.392–398; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). The poem bears out Merry's reading: Eurymachus says that Antinous wanted to be king of Ithaca himself and to kill Telemachus (22.48–53; see [[eurymachus]]).
