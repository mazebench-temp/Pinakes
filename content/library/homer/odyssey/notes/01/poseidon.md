summary: Alone of the gods Poseidon does not pity Odysseus, and he rages against him until he reaches his own land. He is away feasting among the Ethiopians when the others decide on Odysseus' homecoming. Zeus explains his anger as a father's, for the blinding of his son Polyphemus, and foretells that he will let it go, since he cannot strive alone against all the gods.

**All but one.** The year of homecoming has come (1.16–17), and “all the gods pitied him / except Poseidon; and he raged relentlessly / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (1.19–21). The poem names its divine adversary before it names the island where Odysseus is held, and it sets a limit to his rage in the same breath: πάρος ἣν γαῖαν ἱκέσθαι, “until he reached his own land”. Merry reads 1.18–19, “not even then was he free of his trials, / or among his friends”, of the struggle against the powers that hinder his return, not of what awaits him in Ithaca (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.19; see the translation's note on 1.18–19).

**Among the Ethiopians.** The god is far away when the council meets:

{{quote:9b59389f-705b-5304-8d8c-c1c3d91ebaca}}

ἀντιόων, “to share in”, is a future here, fixed by the context, though the form could be present (Merry on 1.25). The absence is the occasion of the council, where the other gods decide on Odysseus' homecoming without him (1.26–95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[ethiopians]] and [[hecatomb]]). In the Iliad Zeus goes with all the gods to feast among the Ethiopians, and Thetis must wait for his return (*Iliad* 1.423–427; see [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]). The other side of this absence comes in Book 5: on his way back from the Ethiopians Poseidon sees Odysseus on his raft, says that the gods have changed their minds about him while he was among the Ethiopians, and raises the storm (5.282–296).

**The father of the Cyclops.** Athena asks Zeus why he is angry with Odysseus, and Zeus answers that the anger is Poseidon's:

{{quote:ad8a8a30-fde5-5e81-be18-d99f13a91350}}

The god's anger is a father's, for Polyphemus is his son by the nymph Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys (see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]], [[thoosa]], [[phorcys-the-sea-god]] and [[cyclopes]]). The blinding itself, and the Cyclops' prayer to his father that Odysseus may come home late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, are told by Odysseus in Book 9 (9.375–394, 9.526–536). Here Zeus gives only the effect: “From that time Poseidon the earth-shaker, as for Odysseus, / does not kill him, but drives him wandering from his native land” (1.74–75). Merry takes οὔ τι κατακτείνει either as a present of attempt or, better, as a clause of concession, “without, indeed, slaying him” (on 1.75). When Athena at last reveals herself to Odysseus on Ithaca, she gives the same cause for keeping away from him: she would not fight her father's brother, angry because Odysseus had blinded his dear son (13.341–343; see [[athena]]).

**Earth-holder and earth-shaker.** Zeus names him with both his titles. γαιήοχος, “the earth-holder” (1.68), was “earth-surrounding” for the older commentators, and has also been taken as “earth-supporting”, or, with Autenrieth, “lord of earth” (Merry on 1.68; see the translation's note on 1.68 and [[earth-holder]]); ἐνοσίχθων, “the earth-shaker” (1.74), is the god who makes the earth tremble (see [[earth-shaker]]).

**Not against all.** Zeus closes with a prophecy: “Poseidon will let go / his anger; for he will not be able, against all / the immortals, against the will of the gods, to strive alone” (1.77–79). Merry joins ἀντία πάντων … ἐριδαινέμεν οἶος, “to contend by himself against all”, and takes “the immortals, against the will of the gods” as a separate phrase that shows who the “all” are (on 1.78). The anger is ἀσκελὲς αἰεί, “stubbornly forever” (1.68), and yet the narrator has already bounded it by the homecoming (1.21). After the homecoming it falls once more, on the Phaeacians who bring Odysseus home (13.125–187).
