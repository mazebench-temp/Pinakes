---
summary: Horace's two renderings take from the proem the sack of Troy, the cities and the minds of many men, and the return of Odysseus and his companions; neither keeps the epithet, the companions' recklessness or the cattle of the Sun. His suitors of Penelope, born to consume the fruits of the earth, are the feasters of this book.
refs: [1.1-5]
---

**What Horace keeps.** Both versions begin from the second and third lines of the proem: “after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy; / and he saw the cities of many men and learned their mind” (1.2–3). The *Ars Poetica* stops there. The *Epistles* go on to the fourth and fifth, “and on the sea he suffered many sorrows in his heart, / seeking to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions” (1.4–5), and make of them an example of endurance. In both, νόον, the “mind” of men that Odysseus learned, becomes *mores*, their “ways”.

**What he leaves out.** Neither version has the epithet πολύτροπον, “of many turns” (1.1; see [[of-many-turns]]), or the end of the proem, where Odysseus fails to save his companions, who perished “through their own recklessness” when they ate the cattle of Hyperion the Sun (1.6–9; see [[atasthalie]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The *Epistles* have their own version of the companions' folly, Circe's cups, which they drank and Ulysses did not.

**The suitors.** Horace's idlers, “the suitors of Penelope, good-for-nothings”, born to consume the fruits of the earth (*Epistles* 1.2.27–28), are the men of this book, who “eat another man's substance without payment” (1.160) and spend the day at game pieces, meat and wine, and then at song and dancing, “for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.106–112, 1.144–152; see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-game-of-penelope]]). Telemachus' answer to them is to call an assembly and tell them to eat their own possessions, “taking turns from house to house” (1.374–375).
