summary: The cattle of Book 1 are the cattle of the Sun, which Odysseus' companions ate and so lost their homecoming (1.7–9); Odysseus' own “shambling cattle with curving horns”, which the suitors are forever slaughtering (1.91–92) and on whose hides they sit at their game (1.107–108); and the worth of twenty oxen that Laertes paid for Eurycleia (1.430–431).
# ῥινοῖσι βοῶν (1.108), the oxhides; the bare βοῶν is also the participle of βοάω, "shouting". ἐεικοσάβοια (1.431), "the worth of twenty oxen".

**The cattle of the Sun.** The proem names the one crime of the wanderings: the companions perished, “fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun” (1.7–9; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[helios]]). βοῦς is the contracted accusative plural, as at 1.92.

**The suitors' slaughter.** Athena will rouse Telemachus to call the Achaeans to an assembly and speak out to the suitors, “who are forever / slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (1.90–92; see [[the-suitors]], [[sheep]], [[shambling]] and [[with-curving-horns]]). The verse-end εἰλίποδας ἕλικας βοῦς is the Iliad's, and so is the verb. In Phoenix's story his kinsmen, keeping him at home in his father's halls, slaughtered “many fat sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” and drank the old man's wine (ἔσφαζον, *Iliad* 9.464–469; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]); at Ismarus Odysseus' men, refusing to leave, slaughtered sheep and cattle by the shore (ἔσφαζον, 9.43–46). Telemachus repeats the suitors' verse to Menelaus, together with the verse of his rebuke of them (4.320–321 = 1.92, 1.368). The two epithets are of the gait and the horns: εἰλίποδας, “shambling”, of the rolling walk of oxen, as Merry takes it against Buttmann's “heavy-treading”, and ἕλικας, “with curving horns”, the first of the two senses that the ancient lexicon gives (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.92; see the translation's note on 1.92). The herds were Odysseus' wealth: the swineherd counts twelve herds of cattle on the mainland and as many flocks of sheep, droves of swine and flocks of goats, more than any twenty men possess (14.96–102).

**The hides.** When Athena comes to the doors, the suitors are at their game pieces, “sitting on the hides of oxen that they themselves had killed” (1.107–108; see [[game-pieces]]). The relative clause, οὓς ἔκτανον αὐτοί, makes their seats a record of the slaughter of 1.92: the oxen were Odysseus'. In the Iliad ῥινοὶ βοῶν are the oxhides of war, with which the Achaeans fence the battlements of their wall and of which Idomeneus' round shield is made (*Iliad* 12.263, 13.406).

**The worth of twenty oxen.** Eurycleia, who lights Telemachus to his chamber, is the woman “whom Laertes once bought with his own possessions / when she was still in her first youth, and gave the worth of twenty oxen for her” (1.430–431; see [[eurycleia]] and [[laertes]]). Merry (on 1.431) suggests that a word such as κτέατα, “possessions”, is understood with ἐεικοσάβοια, and notes that twenty oxen was a high price for a slave, where the woman skilled in much handiwork whom Achilles sets as the wrestlers' second prize is valued at four (*Iliad* 23.704–705; the tripod for the winner at twelve, 23.702–703). Cattle were the measure of value. The only other ἐεικοσάβοιος in Homer is in Eurymachus' offer to Odysseus after the death of Antinous, that each suitor will make amends for what has been eaten and drunk in the halls with bronze and gold, a recompense worth twenty oxen (22.55–59; see [[eurymachus]]).
