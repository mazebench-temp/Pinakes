**All but one.** The gods appear first as a body: they “had spun the thread” for Odysseus' return, and “all the gods pitied him / except Poseidon” (1.17–20; see [[poseidon]] and [[pity]]). While he is away among the Ethiopians “the others / were all together in the halls of Olympian Zeus” (1.26–27), ἁθρόοι, and it is “among the immortals” that Zeus speaks (1.31; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[zeus]]). His complaint is spoken for them all: mortals blame “us”, ἐξ ἡμέων, though “we ourselves” warned Aegisthus (1.33, 1.37).

**The will of the gods.** Athena appeals to what is “dear to the blessed gods” (1.82; see [[blessed-gods]]), and Zeus answers with a ruling for the whole company: “let all of us here take thought for / his homecoming, how he may come” (1.76–77). Poseidon “will not be able, against all / the immortals, against the will of the gods, to strive alone” (1.78–79; see [[immortals]]). The gods who dwell on Olympus are “the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky” (1.67; see [[wide-sky]]). On Ithaca men speak of them as “the gods who live forever” (1.263, 1.378; see [[gods-who-live-forever]]).
