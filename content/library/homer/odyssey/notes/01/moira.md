---
summary: Zeus says that mortals suffer “against fate” (ὑπὲρ μόρον) by their own recklessness, as Aegisthus married Agamemnon's wife “against fate” (1.34–35). Athena calls Odysseus “ill-fated” (1.49), Telemachus believes that he has perished “by an evil fate” (1.166), and the goddess wishes the suitors “swift-fated” (1.266).
grc: [μόρον, δυσμόρῳ, ὠκύμοροί]
en: [fate, ill-fated]
---

**Against fate.** ὑπὲρ μόρον comes twice in two lines (1.34–35). Merry calls it a popular solution of the difficulty of reconciling divine power with human free will: a certain measure of fate was ordained as each man's lot, but misconduct could aggravate or hasten it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.34; see the translation's note on 1.34 and [[who-is-to-blame]]). He compares ὑπὲρ μοῖραν (*Iliad* 20.336), the adverb ὑπέρμορα (*Iliad* 2.155), and the oracle that Herodotus applies to the battle of Plataea, where many will fall “beyond their lot and fate”, ὑπὲρ λάχεσίν τε μόρον τε (Herodotus 9.43.2). Elsewhere in Homer the phrase is said of what might happen, or nearly happened, and did not: the army's homecoming (*Iliad* 2.155), the sack of Troy that Apollo fears (*Iliad* 21.517), Odysseus' death in the surf (*Odyssey* 5.436). Zeus uses it of what men have done.

**The thread.** In the year of his return the gods “had spun the thread for him to return home” (ἐπεκλώσαντο, 1.17), the verb of Achilles' “so the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals” (*Iliad* 24.525; see [[nostos]]).

**An evil fate.** Telemachus takes his father's death for certain: “he has perished so, by an evil fate” (κακὸν μόρον, 1.166), and Merry compares ἀπόλωλε … ὄλεθρον (9.303; on 1.166). The goddess answers that he is alive and will find a way home (1.196–205).
