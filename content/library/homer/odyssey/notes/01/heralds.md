**At the feast.** When Athena arrives, “heralds and ready attendants were busy for them, / some mixing wine and water in the mixing bowls, / and others again with sponges full of holes were washing the tables / and setting them out” (1.109–112; see [[ready-attendants]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[table]]). For Telemachus and his guest “a herald went to and fro, pouring wine for them” (1.143; see [[cupbearing]]); for the suitors “the heralds poured water over their hands” (1.146; see [[hand-washing]]), the verse of the libation before the envoys set out from Agamemnon's hut (*Iliad* 9.174); and when they have eaten, “a herald put a beautiful lyre in the hands / of Phemius” (1.153–154; see [[lyre]] and [[phemius]]). The heralds of the Iliad summoned the army and served at oaths and sacrifices; these serve at a feast, in a house whose master is away, for the men who are eating it up (see [[the-suitors]]).

**Heralds and attendants.** W. W. Merry distinguishes the heralds who are public servants, “workers for the people” (δημιοεργοί, *Odyssey* 19.135), from the private heralds in the households of kings and chieftains, such as these. Heralds were of noble blood, with the epithets ἀγαυοί, “noble”, and θεῖοι, “divine” (*Iliad* 3.268, 4.192); they summoned assemblies, treated with enemies, prepared sacrifices and waited at sacrificial banquets, and the private herald, attending at his master's feasts, came near the θεράπων, the attendant, as the herald Mulius is called “the attendant of Amphinomus” (*Odyssey* 18.423–424). Merry quotes Gladstone, that the heralds are “the only executive officers that are found in Homer” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.109). The suitors' herald is named in later books: Medon, who hears their plot against Telemachus and tells Penelope, and whom Odysseus spares with the singer (*Odyssey* 4.677–679, 22.357–377).
