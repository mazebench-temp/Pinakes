# ὀρχηστύς (1.152) and ὀρχηστύν (1.421): ὀρχηστύς, "dancing", is in the Iliad only at 13.731, claimed by its note.
grc: [ὀρχηστύς, ὀρχηστύν]
en: [dancing]
---

After the meal “the suitors had other things on their minds, / song and dancing; for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.151–152; see [[feast]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). μολπή, “song”, is a word that Aristarchus held to mean play, above all dancing, never singing; W. W. Merry, after Lehrs, finds that here, with Phemius' lyre, it inclines to the wider sense (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.152; see the translation's note on 1.152). At evening, when the stranger has gone and Telemachus has spoken, “they turned to dancing and to lovely song / and took their delight, and waited for evening to come” (1.421–422). In the Iliad dancing is the gift that a god gives to one man as war to another and the lyre and song to a third (*Iliad* 13.730–731; see [[different-gifts]]); here it is the pastime of the suitors in another man's hall.
