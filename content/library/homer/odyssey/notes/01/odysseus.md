**The man of many turns.** The poem's first word is ἄνδρα, “the man”, and the proem does not name him. He is the man “of many turns, who was driven / far astray, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy”, who saw the cities of many men, suffered on the sea, and could not save his companions (1.1–9; see [[proem]], [[of-many-turns]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The narrative finds him alone, “wanting his homecoming and his wife”, held by Calypso in her caves in the year in which the gods had spun the thread for his return, and pitied by all the gods but Poseidon (1.11–20; see [[calypso]] and [[nostos]]). His name comes only at the end of the passage, in the verse of Poseidon's rage, “against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (1.21; see [[antitheos]]).

**Before the gods.** In the council Athena speaks for him (see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]):

{{quote:2e726887-2a82-50a7-a803-7dc6ef136da9}}

Her reproach, τί νύ οἱ τόσον ὠδύσαο, Ζεῦ; “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (1.62), plays on his name, which Merry renders by this etymology “a man of wrath”, one who suffers the wrath of the gods or deals wrath to his enemies (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.62; compare 19.406–409). Zeus answers that he could not forget him:

{{quote:0363f96f-20b8-5d3a-8923-f4e7a4795248}}

Merry construes the two halves of 1.66 differently: περί with βροτῶν in the first, “beyond mortals in wisdom”, and adverbial in the second, “beyond all others gave sacrifices” (on 1.66; see [[noos]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The god who keeps him from home is Poseidon, whose son, the Cyclops, he blinded (1.68–75; see [[poseidon]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**His epithets.** The first book gives him the epithets of mind and endurance: δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (1.48), πολύφρων, “of much wisdom” (1.83), ταλασίφρων, “stout-hearted” (1.87, 1.129), and πολυμήχανος, “a man of many devices” (1.205); with them θεῖος, “divine” (1.65), ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (1.21), and δῖος, “brilliant” (1.196, 1.396, 1.398). See [[wise-hearted]], [[of-much-wisdom]], [[stout-hearted]], [[of-many-devices]], [[divine]] and [[brilliant]].

**The absent father.** In Ithaca he is everywhere and nowhere. His spears stand in the rack where Telemachus sets the stranger's spear (1.127–129; see [[spear-rack]]). His son sits among the suitors imagining his return (1.115–117), and believes that his bones are rotting in the rain or rolling in the sea (1.161–162); many guests used to come to the house, “since he too was one who went about among men” (1.176–177). Had he fallen at Troy, he would have had a tomb and left his son great fame, but now “the Harpies have snatched him away without report; / he is gone, out of sight, unheard of” (1.239–242; see [[harpies]], [[kleos]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Telemachus calls him “the most ill-starred of mortal men” (1.219), and tells the suitors that “brilliant Odysseus is dead” (1.396) and Eurymachus that “my father's homecoming is lost” (1.413). Merry observes on the slaves “whom brilliant Odysseus took as spoil” for his son (1.398) that Odysseus was not above such freebooting even after his return, when he means to make good by raiding the flocks the suitors have eaten (on 1.398; 23.356–357).

**Alive.** The disguised goddess brings the truth. Brilliant Odysseus has not yet died upon the earth, but is held on a sea-washed island by harsh men, and he will not be away much longer, “since he is a man of many devices” (1.196–205; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). She remembers him as she first saw him in her father's house, on his way back from Ephyra with a drug for his arrows, and wishes that he would come now and stand at the doors with helmet, shield and two spears: “if only Odysseus, such as that, would engage the suitors” (1.255–266; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]). His return is still on the knees of the gods (1.267–268; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**Husband.** Penelope asks the singer to stop his song of the returns, since she longs for “such a head … the man whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (1.343–344), and goes up to weep “for Odysseus, her dear husband” (1.363; see [[penelope]] and [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). Telemachus answers her that “Odysseus was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming / in Troy” (1.354–355; see [[day-of-homecoming]]).
