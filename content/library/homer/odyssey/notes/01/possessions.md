summary: The suitors are eating up the possessions of Odysseus' house; Telemachus bids them eat their own, taking turns from house to house, and Eurymachus wishes that he may keep his. A blessed man grows old among his possessions, and Laertes bought Eurycleia with his (1.218, 1.375, 1.402–404, 1.430).

**Eaten up.** The suitors “eat another man's substance without payment” (1.160) and “wear down my house” (1.248; see [[the-suitors]]). Telemachus bids them “provide other feasts, / eating your own possessions, taking turns from house to house” (1.374–375): the ἔρανος, the feast held at each house in turn, which Merry sets apart from the εἰλαπίνη given by a single host (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.226; see the translation's note on 1.226 and [[feast]]). If they prefer that one man's substance be destroyed without payment, he will call on the gods for requital (1.376–380).

**Kept.** Eurymachus answers: “but may you keep your possessions yourself and rule over your own house. / May that man never come who by force, against your will, / will rob you of your possessions, while Ithaca is still inhabited” (1.402–404; see [[eurymachus]]). ἀπορραίσει takes two accusatives, “rob you of your possessions”, as verbs of taking away do, Merry notes (on 1.404).

**Gained and spent.** Telemachus wishes that he had been the son of “some blessed man / whom old age overtook among his own possessions” (1.217–218; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). Laertes bought Eurycleia “with his own possessions”, giving the worth of twenty oxen for her (1.430–431; see [[eurycleia]] and [[laertes]]). The gift Telemachus offers the stranger is to be a κειμήλιον, a treasure laid up in the house (1.311–313; see [[guest-gifts]]).
