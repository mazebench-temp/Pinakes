---
summary: Athena comes to Ithaca in the likeness of Mentes, “the leader of the Taphians”, and in his name says that she is “lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar”, sailing with iron to Temese for bronze; Telemachus repeats the verse to the suitors (1.105, 1.180–184, 1.419).
---

**The disguise.** Athena stands at Odysseus' door “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (1.105). The half-verse Ταφίων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ answers the Iliad's Κικόνων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, the leader of the Cicones, whose likeness Apollo takes (*Iliad* 17.73; see the translation's note on 1.105 and [[mentes-the-taphian]]). Merry explains the Taphians as a race of pirates who lived with the Teleboans on islets off the coast of Leucas (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.105; see [[taphos]]).

**Lovers of the oar.** In Mentes' name the goddess says, “I am lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar” (1.181), and tells of a trader's voyage: with ship and companions, over the wine-dark sea “to men of another tongue, / to Temese, for bronze”, with a cargo of “gleaming iron” (1.182–184; see [[temese]], [[iron]], [[bronze]] and [[lovers-of-the-oar]]). The ship waits in the harbor of Rheithron, under wooded Neion (1.185–186; see [[rheithron]] and [[neion]]). It is the life the rest of the Odyssey gives the Taphians, who buy and sell and carry off captives (14.452, 15.427, 16.426). Their lord's house and Odysseus' are guest-friends “from of old” (1.187–188), and Strabo infers that in Homer the Taphians were friends of the Ithacans (*Geography* 10.2.24).

**The verse repeated.** When Eurymachus asks who the stranger was, Telemachus gives him the same two verses, with ἀνάσσει, “he is lord”, for ἀνάσσω: “and he claims to be Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus, / and he is lord over the Taphians, lovers of the oar” (1.418–419; see the translation's table of formulas). The suitors hear the stranger's own account, and Telemachus keeps to himself that he knew the immortal goddess (1.420).
