---
summary: Athena comes to Telemachus “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (εἰδομένη, 1.105). The book then turns on looks: she finds the father in the son's head and eyes (1.207–209), and Eurymachus judges the vanished stranger “not like a base man to look on” (1.411).
refs: [1.208, 1.301, 1.411]
---

**The disguise.** εἴδομαι is the verb of a god who takes a mortal's likeness (see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). The Odyssey's first disguise is a stranger's, and the goddess takes a name, a father and a people with it, Mentes, son of Anchialus, lord of the Taphians (1.180–181; see [[mentes-the-taphian]] and [[euchomai]]). The phrase Ταφίων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ answers Κικόνων ἡγήτορι Μέντῃ, the leader of the Cicones whose likeness Apollo takes in the Iliad (*Iliad* 17.73; see the translation's note on 1.105). She leaves “like a bird”, and Telemachus knows that a god has been with him (1.319–323; see [[like-a-bird]] and [[thambos]]).

**Looks.** The stranger tells Telemachus that he is “terribly like” his father in his head and his fine eyes (1.208), αἰνῶς of a startling likeness, as of Helen's to the goddesses (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.208; *Iliad* 3.158), and that he is “very handsome and tall” (1.301). The suitors judge by looks too: asking who the stranger was, Eurymachus says that he did not wait to be known, “for he was not like a base man to look on” (1.410–411).
