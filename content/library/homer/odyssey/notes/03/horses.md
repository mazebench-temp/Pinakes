**By land.** Nestor offers Telemachus a choice: he may go on with his ship and companions, “or if you wish to go by land, here are a chariot and horses for you, / and here are my sons, who will be your escorts / to shining Lacedaemon” (3.324–326; see [[chariot]] and [[lacedaemon]]). Athena chooses for him. She will go back to the ship herself, and Nestor is to send the young man on “with a chariot and your son; and give him horses, / the nimblest of yours at running and the best in strength” (3.369–370; see [[athena]]).

**The yoking.** On the next day, after the sacrifice, Nestor gives the order, “My sons, come, bring fine-maned horses for Telemachus / and yoke them under the chariot” (3.475–476), and they yoke “the swift horses under the chariot” (3.478; see [[fine-maned]]). ὑφʼ ἅρματα, “under the chariot”, is a plural for one complex thing, and the yoke is part of it: Merry describes the yoke as a bar lashed across the end of the pole, with a pad at each end resting on the horses' necks, straps that served as collars, and rings through which the reins passed (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.476, 3.486, comparing *Iliad* 5.729–731 and 24.266–274). The pair “shook the yoke, holding it on either side” all day long (3.486).

**The pair.** The horses are two, and the drive is told in duals: μάστιξεν δʼ ἐλάαν, τὼ δʼ οὐκ ἀέκοντε πετέσθην, “and whipped them to a run, and the pair flew on not unwilling” (3.484, 3.494; see [[chariot-journey]]). It is a verse of the Iliad's chariots, of gods driving through the sky (*Iliad* 5.366, 5.768, 8.45) and of heroes driving away to the ships (*Iliad* 10.530, 22.400), and once of Nestor himself, when he drives the wounded Machaon to the ships (*Iliad* 11.517–520). Now his son holds the reins (3.482–483; see [[peisistratus]]). They lodge at Pherae with Diocles and drive on at dawn, “so well did the swift horses carry them on” (3.488–496; see [[pherae-in-messenia]] and [[diocles]]). The same verses bring them back from Sparta in Book 15, and Peisistratus drives the fine-maned horses home to Pylos alone (15.182–193, 15.215–216).

**Horses and islands.** Nestor is the horseman of the poem: “tamer of horses” (3.17), “the Gerenian horseman”, “the old horse-driving Nestor” (3.436, 3.444; see [[tamer-of-horses]], [[gerenian-horseman]] and [[horse-driving]]). Merry notes that ἱππόδαμος, so common in the Iliad, is given in the Odyssey only to Nestor, to Diomedes (3.181) and to Castor (11.300; Merry on 3.17). Telemachus goes to Sparta with borrowed horses, and when Menelaus offers him three horses and a chariot of his own, he refuses them: he will not take horses to Ithaca, where “there are no wide courses for running, nor any meadow”, “a goat-pasturing land, and lovelier than a horse-pasturing one”, for “none of the islands is good for driving horses” (4.589–608; see [[ithaca]]).
