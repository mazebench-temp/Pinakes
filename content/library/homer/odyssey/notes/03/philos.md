summary: Nestor wishes that Athena “were willing to love you / as she cared then for glorious Odysseus”, “for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly” (3.218–223), and Athena answers that not even the gods can ward off death “from a man they love” (3.237). Nestor calls Telemachus “My friend” and “dear child” (3.103, 3.184, 3.211, 3.375).
grc: [φιλέειν, φιλεῦντας]
en: [love]
---

**The love of the gods.** Nestor's wish is the book's measure of a god's favor: Athena “cared” for Odysseus at Troy (περικήδετο, 3.219) and “openly stood beside him” (3.222), and Merry compares the Iliad's complaint of the Locrian Ajax, that the goddess “stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (*Iliad* 23.782–783; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.222; see [[athena]] and [[odysseus]]). If she loved the son so, one of the suitors would forget all about marriage, which Merry takes of forgetting it in death (3.223–224; on 3.224). Athena's own words set the limit of that love: not even the gods can ward off death καὶ φίλῳ ἀνδρί (3.237; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]).

**Friends.** Nestor's address to the young man is ὦ φίλʼ, “My friend” (3.103, 3.211, 3.375), and φίλε τέκνον, “dear child” (3.184); he and Menelaus sailed home φίλα εἰδότες ἀλλήλοισιν, “with friendly hearts toward one another” (3.277), where Merry renders “with kindly feeling” (on 3.277). Athena's young crew follow her φιλότητι, “out of friendship” (3.363; see [[hetairos]]).
