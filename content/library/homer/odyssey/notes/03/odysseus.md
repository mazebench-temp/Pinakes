summary: The father his son is looking for. Telemachus asks Nestor for the truth of a death that Zeus has made unknown; Nestor remembers Odysseus at Troy, surpassing all in tricks and counsel, never at odds with him in assembly or council, turning back at Tenedos to please Agamemnon, and openly loved by Athena. Telemachus gives up hope of his return, and the goddess answers that a god can bring a man safe home from far away.

**A death unknown.** Telemachus has come “to learn / of your father, where the earth hid him and what fate he has met” (3.15–16), and he asks Nestor for the truth: “but of that man the son of Cronus has made even the destruction unknown” (3.88; see [[zeus]]). He has heard where each of the others perished; of his father no one can tell whether he died on land or at sea, “for beyond all men his mother bore him to misery” (3.89–95). πλαζομένου (3.95) belongs with μῦθον and refers to Odysseus: “word of him … as he wandered” (see the translation's note on 3.95). He begs Nestor by any word or deed his father promised and fulfilled “in the land of the Trojans” (3.98–101); ἔπος and ἔργον make one thing here, the act that the word foretold (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.99).

**Nestor remembers.** The old man's answer comes round to him after the dead and the sorrows of the war:

{{quote:c7293f84-c800-5042-a9b6-acd722e8dcee}}

οὔ τις … ἤθελε is “no one chose to match himself”, a decision rather than a wish (Merry on 3.120), and φραζόμεθʼ is an imperfect, as ῥάπτομεν, “we stitched”, is (3.118; Merry on 3.129; see [[metis]], [[dolos]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Then the fleet broke up, and at Tenedos “Some turned back and went off in their curving ships / with lord Odysseus, wise-hearted, of subtle wiles, / to do a kindness once more to Agamemnon, son of Atreus” (3.162–164; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]], [[wise-hearted]] and [[of-subtle-wiles]]). ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆα is “Odysseus and his comrades” (Merry on 3.162). Nestor knows nothing more of him: κείνων, “of those”, are the men who stayed with Agamemnon and those who turned back with Odysseus (3.184–185; Merry on 3.185).

**Athena's love.** If only Athena would love the son, Nestor says, “as she cared then for glorious Odysseus / in the land of the Trojans”, “for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly / as Pallas Athena openly stood beside him” (3.218–222; see [[athena]]). Merry compares the complaint of Ajax, son of Oileus, at the games, that the goddess “always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (*Iliad* 23.782–783; Merry on 3.222). When Athena leaves, Nestor knows her for the goddess “who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (3.379). Nestor asks, too, whether the father may come someday and take vengeance on the suitors, “either he alone or all the Achaeans together” (3.216–217; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**No homecoming.** Telemachus cannot believe it: “for him there is no longer any true homecoming, but already / the immortals have devised for him death and black doom” (3.241–242; see [[nostos]] and [[black-doom]]). The goddess has already answered him, “A god, if he wished, could easily save a man even from far away” (3.231; see [[telemachus]]), and she goes on to set a late homecoming after many sorrows against Agamemnon's death at his own hearth (3.232–235; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

**His epithets.** δῖος, “brilliant” (3.84, 3.121, 3.126); ταλασίφρων, “stout-hearted” (3.84); ἐσθλός, “brave” (3.98, 3.379); ἄναξ, “lord”, with δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted”, and ποικιλομήτης, “of subtle wiles” (3.163); κυδάλιμος, “glorious” (3.219); θεῖος, “divine” (3.398). Three times his son is “the dear son of Odysseus” (3.64, 3.352, 3.398). See [[brilliant]], [[stout-hearted]], [[brave]], [[glorious]], [[divine]] and [[dear-son-of-odysseus]].
