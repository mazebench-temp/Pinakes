---
summary: In Nestor's account of Menelaus' voyage the South Wind drives the great wave against the headland near Phaestus, on the coast of Gortyn, where part of the fleet is wrecked (3.293–298).
---

**The wreck on Crete.** When Zeus has split Menelaus' fleet off Malea, some of the ships are driven to Crete, to a place that Nestor describes as it always is, in the present tense: “There is a smooth rock, steep to the sea, / at the far edge of Gortyn, in the misty sea; / there the South Wind drives the great wave against the left headland, / toward Phaestus, and a small rock holds back the great wave” (3.293–296; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[gortyn]], [[phaestus]] and [[crete]]). The men escape with difficulty, but the waves smash the ships against the reefs (3.297–299). The wind is the one the Iliad gives to its waves on the shore, in the simile of the Argives' shout: “like a wave / against a high shore, when the South Wind comes and stirs it, / against a jutting crag” (*Iliad* 2.394–396; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]). Merry sets the scene on the south coast of Crete, where the cliffs between Phaestus and the southern cape take the full force of the waves, and reports the scholia that took the small rock for a breakwater sheltering the harbor of Phaestus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.292, 3.293).
