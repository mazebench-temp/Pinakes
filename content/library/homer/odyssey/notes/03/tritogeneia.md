---
summary: Nestor names the goddess who has just left in the likeness of a sea eagle “the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia” (3.378), the title's only use in the Odyssey.
---

**Nestor's name for her.** When the bird has gone, Nestor tells Telemachus who his companion was: “no other of those who have their homes on Olympus, / but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia, / who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (3.377–379; see [[athena]], [[daughter-of-zeus]], [[most-glorious]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Διὸς θυγάτηρ κυδίστη Τριτογένεια is the end of the verse in which Athena rouses the Achaeans (*Iliad* 4.515), where the narrator gives her the title; in the Iliad's other two uses it is Zeus' address to his daughter (*Iliad* 8.39, 22.183). Here a mortal speaks it, at the moment he knows her.

**The meaning.** Merry gathers the explanations (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.378). The ancients derived the title from Triton, a stream in Boeotia flowing into Lake Copais, as Athena is Ἀλαλκομενηίς from Boeotian Alalcomenae (*Iliad* 4.8, 5.908; see [[of-alalcomenae]]), and Herodotus tells a Greek story of the Libyan lake Tritonis (Herodotus 4.179). Merry refers the stem to water, as in Ἀμφιτρίτη (3.91; see [[amphitrite]]), and allows an allusion to the birth of the gods from Oceanus and Tethys (*Iliad* 14.201). He reports Eustathius' derivation from a Cretan τριτώ, “head”, which would point to her birth from the head of Zeus, “not alluded to in Homer”, and another from τρίτος, “third”, as if she were the third after Zeus and Hera.
