---
summary: The Achaeans will carry Orestes' fame wide, “even for men to come to hear of” (3.203–204), where the manuscripts read ἀοιδήν, making the deed a song for men to come; Nestor bids Telemachus be valiant, “so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (3.200); and Agamemnon left a singer to guard his wife (3.267–271).
refs: [3.200, 3.203-204, 3.267-271]
---

**A song for men to come.** The verse of Orestes' fame ends καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι, Wolf's reading from Eustathius; the manuscripts have καὶ ἐσσομένοισιν ἀοιδήν, which would make the deed a song for later men, as the gods spun destruction for the Trojans “so that there might be a song for men to come” (8.579–580), and as the immortals will make a lovely song for Penelope (24.197–198). Merry finds sufficient analogy for the manuscripts' reading, and suggests that κλέος may be the approval of the present generation and ἀοιδή the praise of posterity (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.204; see the translation's note on 3.204 and [[kleos]]). Helen in the Iliad says it of herself and Paris, that Zeus set an evil fate on them “so that hereafter / we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (*Iliad* 6.357–358).

**Those born hereafter.** Nestor's charge to Telemachus is Athena's of Book 1, word for word: “be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (3.200, as 1.302; see [[born-hereafter]] and [[so-good-a-thing-is-a-son]]).

**The singer.** Agamemnon left beside his wife ἀοιδὸς ἀνήρ, a singer, with many charges to guard her, and Aegisthus left him on a desert island before he could lead her home (3.267–272; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). The singer at home is the guardian the murderer must first remove.
