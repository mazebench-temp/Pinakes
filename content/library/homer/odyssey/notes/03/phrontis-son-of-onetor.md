**The gentle arrows.** οἷς ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος κατέπεφνε, “with his gentle arrows and killed him” (3.280; see [[apollo]]), is a formula of sudden death: Odysseus asks his mother's shade whether a long disease killed her or Artemis with her gentle arrows, and she answers in the same verse that it was neither (11.171–173, 11.198–199), and Apollo and Artemis kill the people of the island Syrie in this way when they grow old, for no disease comes there (15.407–411; see [[artemis]]). Merry explains it of a death without suffering, brought by Apollo to men and by Artemis to women, set against a long disease; such a death need not be a mercy, since Artemis kills Ariadne in anger, and he wonders whether the phrase describes sunstroke (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.280). Nestor gives no cause, and says nothing of an offense against the god.

**The helmsman.** κυβερνήτης, “helmsman”: in the Iliad the helmsmen stay in the gathering of the ships and hold their steering oars (*Iliad* 19.42–43), and Nestor himself, teaching his son to drive, makes the helmsman the example of skill against the elements, “by cunning again a helmsman on the wine-dark sea / steers a swift ship buffeted by the winds” (*Iliad* 23.316–317; see [[nestor]] and [[metis]]). Phrontis dies πηδάλιον μετὰ χερσὶ θεούσης νηὸς ἔχοντα, “as he held the steering oar of the speeding ship in his hands” (3.281); Merry takes μετὰ χερσί as proof that the Homeric rudder was a paddle, which would be grasped between the hands, not a tiller (on 3.281). The ship is still running when he falls. His praise is his art: he “surpassed the tribes of men / in steering a ship whenever storms rushed on” (3.282–283), and the storms come after he is dead, off Malea, where the fleet is split and driven to Crete and Egypt (3.286–300; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[malea]]).

**The burial.** Menelaus “was held back there, eager though he was for his journey, / to bury his companion and give him funeral rites” (3.284–285; see [[menelaus]] and [[sunium]]). ἐπὶ κτέρεα κτερίσειεν, “give him funeral rites”, is the phrase of Apollo's complaint to the gods that the Trojans would “burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites”, if Hector's body were given back to them (*Iliad* 24.38; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). The halt is the first delay of Menelaus' long homecoming, and it parts him from Nestor, who sails on to Pylos (3.182–183).
