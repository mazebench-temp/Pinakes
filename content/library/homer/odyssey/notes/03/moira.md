summary: μοῖρα is both a share and a fate in Book 3. It is the portions of the entrails at the feast on the shore (3.40, 3.66), “the fate of the gods” that fettered Clytemnestra (3.269), and “the deadly fate of death” from which not even the gods can save a man they love (3.238). κατὰ μοῖραν is Athena's “fair and right” for Nestor's tale (3.331) and the “good order” of the rite (3.457).
# "portions" renders μοίρας (3.40, 3.66), "in good order" κατὰ μοῖραν (3.457); πότμον "fate" (3.16), as the Iliad notes.

**Portions.** Peisistratus seats the strangers and “gave them portions of the entrails” (3.40), and when the outer flesh is roasted the company “divided the portions and feasted on a glorious feast” (3.66; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]] and [[feast]]). This is the first sense of the word, a share of the meat (see the general article). Both verses return, nearly or exactly, at the feast in Odysseus' hall in Book 20 (20.260, 20.280).

**The fate of the gods.** Clytemnestra at first refused the shameful deed, and when “the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome”, Aegisthus left the singer who guarded her on a desert island and led her home, willing (3.265–272; see [[clytemnestra]], [[aegisthus]], [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The verb is the Iliad's for the fate that binds a man to his death, μοῖρα πέδησε (*Iliad* 4.517, 22.5). Whom μιν means is disputed: Clytemnestra, as the translation takes it with the view that Eustathius prefers and Ameis defends; Aegisthus, “fate-bound to his ruin”, as Merry thinks simplest, though he notes that Zeus says Aegisthus acted “against fate” (ὑπὲρ μόρον, 1.35); or, with Nitzsch, the singer (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.269; see the translation's note on 3.269).

**The deadly fate of death.** Athena, as Mentor, ends her rebuke with the limit of the gods' power: not even they can ward off death from a man they love, “whenever / the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down” (3.237–238; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]], [[thanatos]] and [[that-lays-men-low]]). The verse is Penelope's, of the day for which she wove Laertes' shroud, in the words that Antinous reports in Book 2 (2.100).

**Fair and right.** Athena's verdict on Nestor's tale, “Old man, truly you have told all this fair and right” (3.331), answers to Agamemnon's “Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right”, said to Nestor in the quarrel of *Iliad* 1 (*Iliad* 1.286), as Leiocritus' “what you have said is not fair and right” was said to Mentor in the assembly of Book 2 (2.251). At the sacrifice to Athena the thigh-pieces are cut out “all in good order” (3.456–457), words that the Iliad's sacrifice at Chryse does not have (*Iliad* 1.460; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]); the phrase is the Iliad's of the Trojans who “did not cross back in good order” over the ditch (*Iliad* 16.367).
