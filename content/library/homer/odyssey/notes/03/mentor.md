summary: Athena is still in Mentor's likeness at Pylos. Telemachus calls her by his name when he fears to question Nestor (3.22) and when he gives up hope of his father (3.240), and as Mentor she claims to be the only older man among the crew (3.362), until she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle (3.371–372).
# Unnamed: the goddess speaking of herself as Mentor, the elder of the crew (3.362).
refs: [3.362]
---

**Mentor at Pylos.** The narrator does not name Mentor in this book; the goddess is “gray-eyed Athena” and “Pallas Athena”, and only Telemachus calls her by the name she wears: “Mentor, how then shall I go? How shall I greet him?” (3.22), and “Mentor, let us no longer talk like this, grieved though we are” (3.240; see [[athena]] and [[telemachus]]). Peisistratus gives the cup first to the stranger who seems the elder, since Telemachus “is younger, of an age with me myself” (3.49–50; see [[peisistratus]]), and the goddess keeps up the part of the older man when she will not sleep in Nestor's house: “For I claim to be the only older man among them; / the others are younger men who follow out of friendship, / all of an age with great-hearted Telemachus” (3.362–364; see [[euchomai]]). At dawn, she says, she will go to the Caucones to collect a debt (3.366–368; see [[caucones]]); Merry notes that “Mentor” has too small a force with him to take it by violence (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.367).

**The likeness left behind.** She goes away “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (3.371–372; see [[phene]]), and Nestor knows her for the daughter of Zeus (3.377–378). When she comes to the sacrifice at dawn, it is not in the person of Mentor but in her godhead, unseen (3.435–436; Merry on 3.435; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).
