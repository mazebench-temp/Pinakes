summary: Nestor's housekeeper opens the eleven-year-old wine for the evening's libation and stows bread, wine and meats in the chariot for Telemachus' journey (3.391–392, 3.479–480), and the slave women make ready the feast after the sacrifice, with seats, wood and water (3.427–429).
# The English "slave women" of 3.427 renders δμῳῇσιν on the next line (3.428).
refs: [3.427]
---

**The housekeeper.** The ταμίη keeps the stores of Nestor's house. It is she who opened, in its eleventh year, the wine that Nestor mixes, and loosed its cover (3.391–392; see [[wine]] and [[storage-jar]]), as the housekeeper of Odysseus' house keeps his storeroom night and day (2.345–347; see [[eurycleia]]). When Telemachus leaves, “a woman, the housekeeper, put bread and wine in it, / and meats, such as kings whom Zeus has nurtured eat” (3.479–480; see [[provisions]] and [[zeus-nurtured]]). ὄψα are flesh-meat, what is cooked, beside the bread (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.480).

**The slave women.** On the morning of the sacrifice Nestor tells the sons who stay at home to “tell the slave women / inside to prepare a feast throughout the famous house, / and to bring seats and wood all about, and bright water” (3.427–429; see [[feast]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). The seats and the wood both go with the verb “bring”, Merry explains: the servants are to set seats and logs on either side of the altar, which stood in the middle, as well as fetch the water (on 3.429).

**The bath.** The guest is bathed by Nestor's youngest daughter, Polycaste, and not by a slave woman (3.464–468; see [[polycaste]] and [[bathing]]). Merry, after Gladstone, reads the Odyssey's baths as a single custom: the bath was a chief part of a guest's welcome, the mistress of the house or a grown daughter gave orders for it, and the slave women carried them out, so that the bath is sometimes said to be given by the mistress or the daughter and sometimes by the handmaids (on 3.465).
