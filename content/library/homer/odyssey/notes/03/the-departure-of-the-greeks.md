summary: Nestor tells how the fleet broke up after the sack. Athena's wrath set the two sons of Atreus at strife; they called the army to an assembly at sunset, against all order, when the men were heavy with wine; Menelaus urged them to sail home and Agamemnon to stay and appease Athena with hecatombs; half the army sailed at dawn, and at Tenedos a second strife sent Odysseus back to Agamemnon while Nestor and Diomedes fled on (3.130–167).

**The wrath and the strife.** The quarrel is the work of “the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father, / who set strife between the two sons of Atreus” (3.135–136; see [[athena]], [[menis]], [[eris]] and [[atreidae]]). Nestor gives the Achaeans' fault as the reason for Zeus' grim homecoming, “since in no way discerning or just / were they all” (3.133–134), and does not say what Athena was angry at; Merry takes her wrath to have been aimed first at Locrian Ajax for his outrage to Cassandra, and at the chieftains who left it unpunished (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.135, comparing 1.327 and Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.39–45; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**The assembly at sunset.** “And the two of them called all the Achaeans to assembly, / idly and against all order, at the setting of the sun, / and the sons of the Achaeans came heavy with wine” (3.137–139; see [[assembly]] and [[wine]]). The fault, Merry argues, was not the hour as such, though assemblies were held in the morning, but the hour in the circumstances, when the men had come from feasting; he sets beside the line the Achaeans in Aeschylus' *Agamemnon*, whom the toil of battle brings hungry, after a night of wandering, to breakfast on whatever the captured city holds (on 3.138; *Agamemnon* 330–333). βεβαρηότες, “heavy”, is intransitive, from βαρέω, the verb of Plato's Poros, who, drunk with nectar, slept βεβαρημένος, “weighed down” (Merry on 3.139; *Symposium* 203b). The brothers speak in the dual, τὼ δὲ καλεσσαμένω, μῦθον μυθείσθην, “the two of them”, “the two told them the word” (3.137, 3.140), a pairing of verb and noun that the grammarians called the etymological figure (Merry on 3.140).

**The two counsels.** Menelaus is for sailing home at once; Agamemnon, “the fool”, wishes to hold the army back and offer holy hecatombs to heal Athena's anger, not knowing that she would not be persuaded (3.141–146; see [[menelaus]], [[agamemnon]], [[hecatomb]] and [[nepios]]). The verse of his folly is the Iliad's verse for Tros, son of Alastor, who clasped Achilles' knees and begged for his life, “the fool, and he did not know that the other would not be persuaded” (*Iliad* 20.463–466; see the translation's note on 3.146): here the one who will not be persuaded is a goddess. The reason follows as a maxim: “for the mind of the gods who live forever is not quickly turned” (3.147; see [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]). The brothers stand answering each other with harsh words, the Achaeans spring up “with a wondrous din, and they were divided in their counsel” (3.148–150); they spend the night “pondering harsh things in our minds / against one another”, for Zeus was preparing a bane (3.151–152; see [[zeus]]). At dawn half of them drag their ships down and put their possessions on board, “and the deep-girdled women”, the captives of the sack, while half stay with Agamemnon (3.153–156; see [[deep-girdled]] and [[possessions]]).

Tenedos was the first day's voyage from Troy, and the men's eagerness for home explains the sacrifice there (Merry on 3.159, 3.160; see [[tenedos]]). σχέτλιος, “the hard god”: the scholiast derives the word from σχεῖν, “hold”, of one who holds his anger long; Merry from σχέσθαι, “holding out”, “holding with a firm grip”, and renders it “hard”, like Latin *improbus* (on 3.161; see [[hard]]). ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆα, “with lord Odysseus”, means Odysseus and his men, the company that turned back (Merry on 3.162; see [[odysseus]]). ἐπʼ … ἦρα φέροντες, “to do a kindness”: Merry follows Buttmann in joining ἐπί to the phrase ἦρα φέρειν, with ἦρα a noun, “favor”, as Herodian too took it, against Aristarchus, who read a single word, ἐπίηρα (on 3.164; see the translation's key-word row for ἦρα φέρειν). Nestor fled with the ships that followed him, “since I knew that a god was devising evil”, and Diomedes fled with him (3.165–167; see [[nestor]], [[diomedes]] and [[daimon]]). What became of those who turned back, and of the half who stayed, Nestor does not know (3.184–185; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).
