---
summary: The immortals seven times, from the sunrise “to give light to the immortals / and to mortal men” (3.2–3) to Telemachus after the bath, “like the immortals in form” (3.468), and between them Telemachus says of Nestor, “he appears to me like an immortal to look upon” (3.246).
---

The book sets the gods against men in its first verses ([[mortal-men]]), in the libations “to Poseidon and to the other immortals” (3.333; [[libation]]), and in Peisistratus' maxim, “all men have need of the gods” (3.47–48; [[all-men-need-the-gods]]). The immortals who have devised death for Odysseus (3.242; [[black-doom]]) are those who, Athena has just said, cannot ward off death from a man they love (3.236–238). Merry takes ὥς τε of 3.246 as comparative, “as it were an immortal in look” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.246; see the translation's note on 3.246).
