---
summary: Pylos is a house of fathers and sons. Nestor, with six sons around him, mourns a seventh dead at Troy, hears the father in the son's words, and holds up Orestes, who avenged his father; he sits on the stones where his own father sat, and looks to his sons to entertain the guests after him. Telemachus, who has come for news of his father, wishes for Orestes' strength.
refs: [3.83, 3.111-112, 3.122-125, 3.195-200, 3.208-209, 3.354-355, 3.379, 3.408-412, 3.488-489]
---

**Like his father.** Nestor breaks off his tale of the war to look at the son of the man who surpassed all in tricks: “your father, if truly / you are his offspring; awe holds me as I look at you. / For truly your words are like his, and you would not say / that a younger man would speak so like him” (3.122–125; see [[telemachus]], [[odysseus]] and [[sebas]]). In the first book Athena saw the likeness in his head and his fine eyes (1.208–209); Nestor hears it in his speech. Merry takes ἐοικότες and ἐοικότα as “like his” and “so like him”, against Nitzsch's “fitting”: a young man would not speak so like his elder unless the two were father and son, and the same words, ἐοικότα ὧδε, are spoken of the likeness by Helen and by Eurycleia (4.141–143, 19.380–381; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.124; see the translation's note on 3.124–125).

**A son left behind.** From Agamemnon's murder and Orestes' vengeance Nestor draws a lesson for a son:

{{quote:47d468f8-aad2-5da3-ab23-972ab9f6e63a}}

Bothe's paraphrase, which Merry quotes, gives the force of καὶ παῖδα: blessed is the man who leaves behind not only wealth but a son to guard it and avert ruin (on 3.196; see [[orestes]] and [[poine]]). Telemachus answers with a wish for the same strength, but “the gods have spun no such prosperity for me, / for my father and for me” (3.205–209; see [[olbos]]). In Book 2 the goddess, as Mentor, told him that he would be no coward and no fool if his father's might was in him (2.270–272); here Nestor, when she has gone, tells him that he will be no coward and no weakling if the gods go with him, the goddess “who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (3.375–379; see [[athena]]).

**The sons of Nestor.** Nestor sits at the feast with his sons (3.32), and Athena prays for glory for him and for them (3.57). His own dear son, “at once strong and noble”, lies at Troy (3.111–112; see [[antilochus]]). The others gather around him at dawn and each has his part in the sacrifice (3.412–454; see [[thrasymedes]], [[peisistratus]], [[echephron]], [[stratius]], [[perseus-son-of-nestor]] and [[aretus-son-of-nestor]]), and he counts on them as his house's hosts when he is gone: “while I / am alive, and after me my sons are left in the halls / to entertain the guests, whoever comes to my house” (3.353–355; see [[xenia]]).

**The stones of Neleus.** The seat before his doors was his father's: “on them in former days / Neleus used to sit, a counselor equal to the gods; / but he had already been overcome by his doom and gone to the house of Hades, / and now Nestor in his turn sat on them” (3.408–411; see [[neleus]]). That evening at Pherae Telemachus and Peisistratus lodge with Diocles, “son of Ortilochus, whom Alpheus begot as his son” (3.489; see [[diocles]], [[ortilochus]] and [[alpheus]]): the Iliad gives the same line from the river to Diocles and on to his twin sons, whom Aeneas killed at Troy (*Iliad* 5.541–549).
