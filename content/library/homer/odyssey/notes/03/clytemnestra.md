summary: The poem names her for the first time: “brilliant Clytemnestra; for her mind was good”. She refused Aegisthus at first, and a singer guarded her for Agamemnon, until the fate of the gods fettered her and she went with Aegisthus, willing (3.264–272). Athena gives her a share in the guile that killed her husband (3.235), and Nestor calls her Orestes' “hateful mother” (3.310).

**Named at last.** The first book left her unnamed (1.35–39). Here she is Ἀγαμεμνονέη ἄλοχος, “the wife of Agamemnon” (3.264), and then, at the head of the verse, δῖα Κλυταιμνήστρη, “brilliant Clytemnestra” (3.266; see [[brilliant]]). δῖα is “queenly”, Merry says, and refers only to rank or birth, as διογενής and διοτρεφής are ordinary epithets of kings (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.266); he sets it beside the “noble” Aegisthus of the first book (on 1.29; see [[aegisthus]]).

**Her good mind.** “And at first she refused the shameful deed, / brilliant Clytemnestra; for her mind was good” (3.265–266). φρεσὶ γὰρ κέχρητʼ ἀγαθῇσι is a formula of the Odyssey: the same half-verse explains why the swineherd Eumaeus did not forget the gods, and why the suitor Amphinomus pleased Penelope with his words (14.421, 16.398, plain renderings; Merry on 3.266). The good mind is set against what came after, not denied by it: she held out τὸ πρίν, “at first” (3.265), while the singer was beside her (3.267–268; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). In the last book Agamemnon's shade praises Penelope in the same terms, ὡς ἀγαθαὶ φρένες ἦσαν ἀμύμονι Πηνελοπείῃ, “how good was the mind of noble Penelope”, and sets her against the daughter of Tyndareus, who killed her wedded husband (24.194–200, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]]).

**Fettered.** “But when the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome” (3.269; see [[moira]]): the English takes μιν as Clytemnestra, with the view Eustathius preferred, which Ameis argued from the sense of δαμῆναι, “to be overcome”, said of yielding to passion, as of Zeus “overcome by sleep and love” (*Iliad* 14.353); Merry himself thinks Aegisthus the simplest reading, “fate-bound to his ruin” (on 3.269; see the translation's note on 3.269). On Ameis' reading the next verse agrees, since her scruples once overcome she followed gladly: τὴν δʼ ἐθέλων ἐθέλουσαν, “and, willing, he led her, willing, to his own house” (3.272; Merry on 3.269).

**Guile and hatred.** Athena, as Mentor, says that Agamemnon “perished by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.235; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), but in Nestor's long account she does nothing after she yields, and the killing is Aegisthus' (3.304). She appears once more, dead: Orestes gave the Argives a funeral feast “for his hateful mother and for the coward Aegisthus” (3.309–310; see [[orestes]]). How she died the poem does not say (see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). στυγερή, “hateful”, is the word that Agamemnon's shade will use of the song men will sing of her (στυγερὴ δέ τʼ ἀοιδή, 24.200).
