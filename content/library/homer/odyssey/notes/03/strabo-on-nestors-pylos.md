---
summary: Strabo reads Book 3 for the site of Nestor's Pylos. He sets the sacrifice on the shore at the sanctuary of Samian Poseidon in Triphylia, takes the messenger sent to Telemachus' ship for proof that the city lay inland, derives “sandy” from a river, and sends Athena to Caucones in the north of Elis. Merry answers him with the two days' drive to Sparta by way of Pherae.
refs: [3.4-6, 3.366-370, 3.423-424, 3.484-488]
---

**The sacrifice.** Strabo names the place where Telemachus found the Pylians at their sacrifice. It is the sanctuary of Samian Poseidon in Triphylia, between Lepreum and the river Annius, a hundred stades from each, and he quotes the verses: “and they came to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus. / There on the shore of the sea the people were offering sacrifice, / bulls all black, to the dark-haired earth-shaker” (3.4–6; *Geography* 8.3.16; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). A poet may invent what does not exist, he adds, but where he can, he fits his verses to what does, and so keeps his story.

**Inland.** Strabo's Pylos stood more than thirty stades from the sea, “as is clear from the verses”. A messenger is sent to Telemachus' companions at the ship to call them to the feast, and on the way back from Sparta Telemachus will not let Peisistratus drive him to the city, but has him turn aside to the ship, as though the road to the city and the road to the anchorage were not the same (*Geography* 8.3.26). The first is Nestor's order on the morning of the sacrifice: “and let one go to the black ship of great-hearted Telemachus / and bring all his companions, and leave two only” (3.423–424); the second is in Book 15 (15.199–216).

**Sandy Pylos.** Homer knows Pylos as a city as well as a land, Strabo says, quoting “they came to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus” (3.4). The Alpheus flows through the land of the Pylians but not past the city. Past the city flows another river, which some call the Pamisus and others the Amathus, and from it, he thinks, this Pylos was called ἠμαθόεις (*Geography* 8.3.1; see [[sandy]] and [[alpheus]]). Merry calls this reading of the epithet “contrary to all usage”, and finds that “sandy” suits the Messenian Pylos well (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.4).

**The Caucones.** Athena, leaving Nestor's company, says that she will spend the night by the ship and go at dawn “to the great-hearted Caucones, / where a debt is owed me”, and asks Nestor to send Telemachus on with a chariot and his son (3.360–370). If the Caucones lived only in Triphylia, Strabo argues, on the road from Pylos to Lacedaemon and under Nestor's rule, her plan would make no sense: she would go back west to the ship and then east again, when she might have traveled with Telemachus, and anyone going to collect a debt from Nestor's own people would have asked him for help. The Caucones she means must be those of the north of Elis, near Dyme, whose road lay the other way (*Geography* 8.3.17; see [[caucones]]).

**Pherae.** The house of Diocles, where the two young men spend the night, is the house of Ortilochus, his father, at Pherae in Messenia: “they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, / son of Ortilochus” (3.488–489; *Geography* 8.5.8; see [[pherae-in-messenia]] and [[diocles]]).

**Merry's answer.** Merry finds for the Messenian Pylos (on 3.4). He grants that Strabo's argument from Nestor's raid on the Epeians is a strong one (see the entry above), but the messenger to the ship leaves the distance between town and harbor unknown, and the testimony of the ancients, he says, goes the other way: Pindar calls Nestor “the Messenian old man” (*Pythian* 6.35), and the scholia on this line and Eustathius declare for Messenia. His strongest argument is the drive. Telemachus goes from Pylos to Sparta in two days without a change of horses, spending the night at Pherae (3.484–497; 4.1), and the Messenian Pylos, Pherae at the head of the Messenian gulf, and Sparta lie nearly in a line, about thirty miles apart; a traveler from the Triphylian Pylos to Lacedaemon would come down the valley of the Eurotas without coming near Pherae.
