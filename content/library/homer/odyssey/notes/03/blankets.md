---
summary: Nestor will not let Telemachus sleep on his ship, as if from the house of a poor man who has no cloaks and blankets for himself and his guests: “But I have cloaks and fine blankets” (3.348–351).
---

When Athena and Telemachus make to go back to their ship for the night, Nestor holds them back: Zeus forbid that they should go from his house as from a man without clothing and poor, “who has no cloaks and blankets in plenty in his house / for himself or his guests to sleep softly in. / But I have cloaks and fine blankets” (3.346–351; see [[xenia]], [[cloak]] and [[nestor]]). ἀνείμονος is the man without changes of raiment, and πενιχρός the man without the furnishings of beds and chairs, the χλαῖναι and ῥήγεα of the next verse (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.348). The blankets are the measure of a house's wealth and of its welcome: to sleep “softly” (μαλακῶς, 3.350) is what a host owes his guests, and Telemachus sleeps that night on a corded bed beneath the colonnade (3.399; see [[bed]]). The phrase χλαῖναι καὶ ῥήγεα returns when Anticleia's shade tells Odysseus that Laertes has none of them (11.189), and when the disguised Odysseus says that cloaks and shining blankets have been hateful to him (19.337; plain renderings).
