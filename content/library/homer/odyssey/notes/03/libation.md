summary: Libations mark the day at Pylos. Peisistratus bids the elder stranger pour libation and pray to Poseidon before passing the cup (3.43–64); at sunset the company pours libations to Poseidon and the other immortals over the tongues of the victims before bed (3.332–342); in his house Nestor pours one to Athena from his eleven-year-old wine (3.390–395); and at the sacrifice he pours sparkling wine over the burning thigh-pieces (3.459–460).

**At the feast of Poseidon.** Peisistratus puts the cup into the elder stranger's hand with the order of the rite: “Pray now, stranger, to lord Poseidon; / for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here. / And when you have poured libation and prayed, as is the custom, / then give this man too the cup of honey-sweet wine / to pour libation” (3.43–47; see [[cup]], [[prayer]] and [[xenia]]). The goddess prays earnestly to the god of the feast (3.54–62; see [[athena]] and [[poseidon]]), and Telemachus, given the two-handled cup, “in the same way” prays (3.63–64). The libation goes with the prayer, as the poem says of Nestor that evening, ἀποσπένδων, “as he poured the libation” (3.394).

**At sunset.** When the sun has set, Athena bids the company “cut out the tongues and mix the wine, / so that to Poseidon and to the other immortals / we may pour libations and then think of bed; for it is the hour” (3.332–334; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). The heralds pour water over their hands, the young men fill the mixing bowls and serve the wine, “first pouring drops for the libation into the cups” (3.338–340; see [[feast-type-scene]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[cupbearing]]). W. W. Merry explains the rite from the suitors' evening libation in Book 18. The wine-pourer went round the company and at each man poured a first drop into his cup, to be poured out in libation, and then the full draught: the first drop is ἐπάρχεσθαι, the pouring out σπένδειν, and the round went from left to right of the company, the way of the sun (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.340; 18.418–427, 21.141–142). Then “they threw the tongues into the fire, and standing up poured libations over them” (3.341): ἀνιστάμενοι suggests that they rose and went to the hearth to pour the wine over the burning offerings, as Nestor pours it over the thigh-pieces the next morning, and as Odysseus' men, killing the cattle of the Sun, had no wine to pour on the burning offerings and poured water (3.459–460, 12.362–363; Merry on 3.341). The day ends “when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired” (3.342), the verse of the libation before the embassy sets out (*Iliad* 9.177).

**Nestor's libation.** In his house Nestor mixes a bowl of the wine that the housekeeper has opened in its eleventh year, “and earnestly to Athena / he prayed as he poured the libation, to the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (3.393–394; see [[wine]]). It is the first offering of the night to the goddess he has just recognized, and the vow of the evening is kept with the sacrifice of the morning (3.382–384; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). Then “when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired”, the company goes home to sleep (3.395–396).

**At the sacrifice.** When the thigh-pieces of the heifer are burned, “The old man burned them on split wood, and over them poured / sparkling wine” (3.459–460, as *Iliad* 1.462–463; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]): λεῖβε is the libation on the altar fire.
