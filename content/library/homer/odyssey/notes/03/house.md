summary: Nestor's house at Pylos receives its guest. Nestor leads his sons and sons-in-law to his famous house, where they sit on chairs and seats and drink (3.386–395); Telemachus sleeps beneath the loud-resounding colonnade and Nestor in the inner part of the high house (3.396–403); at dawn the king sits on the polished stones before his high doors, and his sons come from their chambers (3.405–416); the slave women make ready the feast throughout the house (3.427–428); and Telemachus drives out from the doorway and the colonnade (3.493). Of his own house in Ithaca Nestor warns him not to stay long away while the suitors devour it (3.313–316).

**Nestor's house.** Nestor leads his sons and sons-in-law “to his beautiful house” (3.387), “the famous house of that lord” (3.388; see [[famous]] and [[nestor]]), and they sit “in order on the chairs and the seats” (3.389; see [[throne]]). The house has the parts of the house of Odysseus in Book 1. Its colonnade, the αἴθουσα along the courtyard, is where the guest sleeps, on a corded bed (3.399; see [[bed]], [[corded]] and [[loud-resounding]]), as guests do at Sparta and in Achilles' hut (4.297; *Iliad* 24.644). The master sleeps “in the inner part of the high house” with his wife (3.402–403; see [[high-roofed]]). His sons come at dawn “from their chambers” (3.413), and Aretus brings the water and the barley “from the chamber” (3.441). The married sons, W. W. Merry thinks, had chambers in buildings of their own, as the sons and sons-in-law of Priam have their chambers of polished stone within his courtyard (*Iliad* 6.242–250), so that “they went to lie down, each to his own house” (3.396) means the married men, while Peisistratus, the one unmarried son, sleeps beside the guest (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.396; see [[peisistratus]]).

**Before the doors.** At dawn Nestor “went out and sat down on the polished stones / which stood before his high doors, / white, shining with oil” (3.406–408; see [[polished]], [[door]] and [[neleus]]). The seats are usually placed outside the courtyard, on the public way; Merry follows Gerlach, who put them in the porch on either side of the door, comparing the stone seats in the temple of Themis at Rhamnus (on 3.407), and he takes the shine of the stones for a polish or varnish laid on the stone, though the scholiasts took it as “glistening as if with oil” (on 3.408). There the king gives his orders for the sacrifice, and the slave women make ready the feast “throughout the famous house” (3.428; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]] and [[handmaids]]). When Telemachus leaves, he and Peisistratus “drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade” (3.493), the verse of Priam's departure from his house in the Iliad and of the two young men's departures in Book 15 (*Iliad* 24.323; 15.146, 15.191). Merry notes that the order is reversed, since one passes the colonnade before reaching the outer gateway (on 3.493).

**The house in Ithaca.** Nestor has heard that the suitors “devise evils in your halls against your will” (3.213), and warns Telemachus not to wander long “far from your home, / leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house / so overbearing, lest they devour everything you have” (3.313–315; see [[the-suitors]] and [[possessions]]). His own house is the measure of what Telemachus' should be: a house with cloaks and blankets for its guests and sons to entertain them after him (3.348–355; see [[xenia]]).

**Other houses.** Aegisthus led Clytemnestra “to his own house” (3.272; see [[aegisthus]]); the gods are “those who have their homes on Olympus” (3.377; see [[homes-on-olympus]]); and on the first evening of the drive Telemachus and Peisistratus come “to Pherae, to the house of Diocles” (3.488; see [[diocles]]).
