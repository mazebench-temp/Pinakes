# οἴνου (3.46, 3.51, 3.391) and οἴνῳ (3.139), first met in the Odyssey here.
grc: [οἴνου, οἴνῳ]
---

**The wine of the welcome.** Peisistratus pours wine into the golden cup for the strangers (3.40–41), and bids the elder give the younger “the cup of honey-sweet wine” in turn (3.46), and then “he put the cup of sweet wine in her hand” (3.51; see [[cup]], [[honey-sweet]] and [[xenia]]).

**Heavy with wine.** In Nestor's tale the sons of Atreus called the Achaeans to assembly after the sack of Troy, at sunset and against all order, “and the sons of the Achaeans came heavy with wine” (3.137–139; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). The wine belongs to the disorder of that evening, which ended in strife (3.138–152). The same participle is in the disguised Odysseus' fear that one of the slave women, or Penelope herself, would say that he wept because his mind was “heavy with wine” (19.121–122, a plain rendering).

**The old wine.** At sunset Athena bids the company mix the wine for the libation (3.332; see [[libation]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). In his own house Nestor opens wine of a great age:

{{quote:9f1ab674-614a-53a8-b1ce-6bedb07c7076}}

The wine had been kept ten years, and the housekeeper has opened it; κρήδεμνον, the jar's “cover”, is not the lid itself, W. W. Merry explains, but the string passed over the lid to hold it fast (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.392; see [[handmaids]] and [[storage-jar]]). ἀνὰ … κέρασσεν (3.390) is “mixed up”, the wine spread through the water in the bowl, not “mixed a second time”, as the scholiast and Eustathius took it; Merry prefers Fäsi's rendering, *permiscuit*, in the sense “bade it be mixed”, and compares κεράσσατο (3.393; Merry on 3.390). ἡδύποτος, “sweet to drink”, is the epithet of the old wine in Odysseus' storeroom, where the jars stand kept for his return (2.340–342; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]]).

**On the altar and at the table.** At the sacrifice to Athena the old man pours “sparkling wine” over the burning thigh-pieces, in the verse of the sacrifice at Chryse (3.459–460, as *Iliad* 1.462–463; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]), and at the feast that follows good men stand by, “pouring wine into golden cups” (3.472): οἶνον οἰνοχοεῦντες is one of the pleonasms Merry sets beside βοῶν ἐπιβουκόλος, “the herdsman of the cattle” (3.422; Merry on 3.422; see [[cupbearing]]). When Telemachus leaves, the housekeeper puts bread and wine and meats in the chariot (3.479–480; see [[provisions]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
