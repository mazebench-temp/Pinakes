summary: The book's meals are told in the verses of the feasts of both poems. On the shore the strangers are seated and given their portions before the Pylians feast and Nestor speaks (3.31–68); at sunset the heralds' water, the brimming bowls and the libation end the day (3.338–342), and in Nestor's house the company sits in order, drinks the old wine and goes home to sleep (3.388–396); the next morning the feast after the sacrifice is told in the verses of the first (3.470–474).

**The feast on the shore.** The strangers come upon the feast already begun, the Pylians “roasting meat and threading more on spits” (3.33): some of the meat was on the fire and some was being spitted, W. W. Merry explains, taking ἄλλα with both verbs (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.33; see [[spits]]). The guests are seated beside the king and given the first course of the sacrifice, the entrails, with the wine (3.35–41; see [[xenia]] and [[cup]]). The tasting of the entrails was a foretaste before the sacrificial feast proper, as the first drops of wine were a rite before the drinking (Merry on 3.461). Then the meal runs in the formula verses: “And when they had roasted the outer flesh and drawn it off, / they divided the portions and feasted on a glorious feast. / But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (3.65–67; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). κρέʼ ὑπέρτερα is the flesh, as against the entrails (Merry on 3.65). What follows the meal is not song but talk, as at the feasts of the kings in the Iliad: “the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them” (3.68; see [[nestor]]).

**Water, wine and libation.** At sunset Athena bids the company cut out the tongues and mix the wine, and the evening's drinking is told in the verses of the libation before the embassy sets out in *Iliad* 9: the heralds pour water over the hands, the young men fill the mixing bowls brim-full and serve the wine with the first drops for the libation, and “when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired”, Athena and Telemachus rise to go to their ship (3.338–344; 3.338–340 and 3.342 as *Iliad* 9.174–177; see [[hand-washing]], [[heralds]], [[mixing-bowl]], [[cupbearing]] and [[libation]]). One verse is added for the tongues of the victims, thrown into the fire as the company stands and pours libations over them (3.341; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). The same verses come in Odysseus' hall before he asks to try the bow (21.270–273).

**Wine and sleep in Nestor's house.** In Nestor's house the guests “sat down in order on the chairs and the seats” (3.389; see [[throne]]), the verse of the suitors' feast in Book 1 and of Laertes' meal at the end of the poem (1.145, 24.385). Nestor mixes a bowl of his eleven-year-old wine and pours a libation to Athena (3.390–394; see [[wine]]); “But when they had poured libations and drunk as much as their hearts desired, / they went to lie down, each to his own house” (3.395–396), as the gods go home at the end of the Iliad's first book (*Iliad* 1.606) and the suitors at the end of the Odyssey's (1.424). The guest stays to sleep in the colonnade (3.397–403; see [[bed]]).

**The feast after the sacrifice.** When the heifer has been sacrificed to Athena, the meal is told in the verses of the feast on the shore: “And when they had roasted the outer flesh and drawn it off” (3.470 = 3.65), “they sat and feasted; and good men stood by to serve, / pouring wine into golden cups” (3.471–472), “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (3.473 = 3.67), and again Nestor speaks (3.474; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). Merry takes ἐπὶ … ὄροντο, of the men who serve, as “looked after them”, from the root of ὁράω and οὖρος, “warder”, against the common rendering “moved along them”; the swineherd says the same of the good men who watch his master's herds (14.104; Merry on 3.471). Between the sacrifice and the meal Telemachus is bathed and dressed (3.464–469; see [[bathing]]).
