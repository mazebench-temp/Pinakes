summary: Pylos, the first stage of the journey. Telemachus lands at sunrise while the Pylians sacrifice to Poseidon, learns from Nestor all he knows of the returns but nothing of his father, and is sent on to Menelaus, who has lately come home from far away. Athena leaves the ship to the crew and goes to the Caucones, and Telemachus goes on by land, in Nestor's chariot with Peisistratus driving, by Pherae toward Sparta.

**The landing.** The ship that sailed from Ithaca at nightfall comes in to Pylos at sunrise (3.1–5; see [[pylos]]), while the Pylians on the shore are offering black bulls to Poseidon, and the crew put straight in, furl the sail, moor the ship and step out (3.9–11; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). The sail is brailed up to the yard, ready to be shaken out again, not lowered and stowed as in the landing at Chryse (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.11, comparing *Iliad* 1.433; see the translation's note on 3.11). “And Telemachus stepped out of the ship, and Athena went before him” (3.12; see [[athena]]).

**The business.** Athena's prayer to Poseidon asks that Telemachus and she “may return when we have done the business / for which we came here in our swift black ship” (3.60–61; see [[prexis]]), and Telemachus tells Nestor that “this business I speak of is private, not public”: he is in quest of the wide report of his father (3.82–83; see [[kleos]]). Nestor came home “without tidings” and knows only what he has heard (3.184–187; see [[nestor]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**On to Menelaus.** Nestor's counsel turns the journey toward Sparta: Telemachus is not to wander long from home, leaving his possessions to overbearing men, but to go to Menelaus:

{{quote:c0775557-3dee-5885-ad86-fbafabcb3b34}}

“But the illustration here is only half serious”, Merry writes of the birds that do not come back within the year, and the scholiast calls it hyperbole (on 3.321; see [[menelaus]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Nestor offers both ways, by ship or by land with a chariot and his sons as escorts to Lacedaemon (3.323–326; see [[lacedaemon]]).

**By land.** At nightfall Athena and Telemachus mean to go back to the ship (3.343–344), but Nestor will not let his guest sleep on the deck (3.346–355; see [[xenia]]). Athena changes the plan: Telemachus will sleep in Nestor's house, and she will go to the ship to encourage the companions and at dawn to the Caucones, where a debt is owed her; Nestor is to send Telemachus on “with a chariot and your son”, and give him his best horses (3.357–370; see [[caucones]] and [[mentor]]). Then she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle, and the voyage goes on without her (3.371–372; see [[phene]]). The crew are called from the ship to the sacrifice (3.423–424, 3.431–432), and the ship waits at Pylos for Telemachus' return (15.193–219).

**The drive.** After the sacrifice and the bath Nestor's sons yoke the horses, the housekeeper puts in bread and wine and meats fit for kings, and Peisistratus takes the reins: “and the pair flew on not unwilling / to the plain, and left the steep citadel of Pylos behind” (3.475–485; see [[peisistratus]], [[chariot]] and [[chariot-journey]]). They drive all day, spend the night at Pherae in the house of Diocles, son of Ortilochus, and at dawn drive on to the wheat-bearing plain until the sun sets (3.486–497; see [[pherae-in-messenia]] and [[diocles]]). The verses 3.486–490 come again when they drive back the same way (15.184–188). The two days' drive with one team of horses, by way of Pherae at the head of the Messenian gulf, seemed to Merry the strongest argument that Nestor's Pylos was the Messenian one, since Pylos, Pherae and Sparta lie nearly in a line at intervals of about thirty miles (on 3.4; see the translation's note on 3.4). He doubts that the poet knew the country beyond, since the upper valley of the Eurotas, rugged and narrow, is no “wheat-bearing plain” (on 3.495). They reach Lacedaemon at the start of Book 4 (4.1).
