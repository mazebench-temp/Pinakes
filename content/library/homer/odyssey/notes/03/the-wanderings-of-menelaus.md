summary: Nestor tells Menelaus' homecoming in two pieces that frame the murder of Agamemnon. Apollo killed his helmsman at Sunium, a storm off Malea split his fleet between the reefs of Crete and Egypt, and he wandered among men of another tongue, gathering wealth (3.276–302); he came home on the very day of the funeral feast for Aegisthus (3.311–312). Then Nestor sends Telemachus to him (3.317–328).

**Where was Menelaus?** Telemachus asks it of Nestor among his questions about Agamemnon's death (3.249), and Nestor answers it only after he has told how Aegisthus won Clytemnestra: “Now we were sailing together on our way from Troy, / the son of Atreus and I, with friendly hearts toward one another” (3.276–277). ἡμεῖς μὲν γάρ, “Now we”, brings in a change of scene, as at 3.262, where it took the story back to the Achaeans at Troy (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.276; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Menelaus had joined Nestor late, “after us two”, at Lesbos (3.168–169; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). At Sunium Apollo killed his helmsman, and he stayed behind, “eager though he was for his journey, / to bury his companion and give him funeral rites” (3.284–285; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]] and [[sunium]]).

**The storm off Malea.**

{{quote:d0b366b0-604f-56a9-a9d1-fee6e53bacec}}

Μαλειάων ὄρος αἰπύ, “the steep mountain of Malea”, is the verse-end at which a storm catches Agamemnon too in the next book, carrying him toward the land where Aegisthus lived (4.514–518; see [[malea]] and [[agamemnon]]). Merry reports the proverb that Eustathius quotes, “round Malea and forget your home” (on 3.287), and sets beside the passage Herodotus' story that Jason, sailing round the Peloponnese, was caught off Malea by a north wind and carried to Libya (on 3.299; Herodotus 4.179). Zeus “devised a hateful journey” for Menelaus (3.288; see [[zeus]] and [[far-seeing]]); the waves τροφέοντο, “swelled”, as the text prints it, where Merry's text reads τροφόεντα, “big” (see the translation's note on 3.290). διατμήξας, “split”, divides the fleet in two, the ships that were wrecked on Crete (τὰς μέν, 3.291) and “the five dark-prowed ships” (τὰς πέντε, 3.299) that reached Egypt (Merry on 3.291; see [[crete]], [[gortyn]], [[phaestus]] and [[egypt]]). Merry would bring the first group to Crete at the western end of its south coast and let it drift east under the cliffs to the bend toward Phaestus, where the ships broke and the men escaped (on 3.293).

**Among men of another tongue.** “So he, gathering much substance and gold there, / wandered with his ships among men of another tongue; / and meanwhile Aegisthus devised these grim things at home” (3.301–303; see [[possessions]] and [[aegisthus]]). The text prints ὥς, “so”, and the English follows it; Merry would read ὡς, “while”, so that the verse is the first half of a sentence answered by τόφρα, “meanwhile”, as Menelaus puts it himself in the next book, “While I, gathering much substance in those lands, / was wandering, meanwhile another man killed my brother” (on 3.301; 4.90–91). ἀγείρων, “gathering”, points to presents received, as Menelaus' gifts from Egyptian Thebes and from the king of the Sidonians show (Merry on 3.301; 4.125–132, 15.117–119). ἀλλόθροος, “of another tongue”, is the word of 1.183. The verb of his wandering, ἠλᾶτο (3.302), returns at once in Nestor's advice to Telemachus, μὴ δηθὰ δόμων ἄπο τῆλʼ ἀλάλησο, “do not wander long far from your home” (3.313; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**On the same day.** αὐτῆμαρ: “and on the same day Menelaus, good at the war cry, came to him, / bringing many possessions, as much as his ships could carry as their load” (3.311–312; see [[menelaus]] and [[good-at-the-war-cry]]). ἄχθος is the ships' freight (Merry on 3.312). He comes home with his wealth on the day Orestes gives the Argives the funeral feast for his mother and Aegisthus (3.309–310; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]): had he come in time, Nestor said, Aegisthus would have had no grave (3.255–261), and the vengeance is the son's.

**The farthest sea.** Nestor sends Telemachus to him, “for he has lately come home from abroad, / from men from whom no one would expect in his heart / to come home” (3.318–320), out of a sea so great and terrible that not even the birds come back from it within the year (3.320–322; see [[birds]]). The scholia call this a hyperbole, and Merry contrasts the five days' run from Crete to Egypt in Odysseus' Cretan tale; he takes the comparison as only half serious (on 3.321; 14.252–257). The wanderings that kept him far from his brother make him the man to ask about another wanderer (see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).
