summary: The birds of Book 3 are the eaters of the dead and the travelers over the sea. Dogs and birds would have devoured Aegisthus if Menelaus had found him alive (3.259); Aegisthus left Clytemnestra's singer on a desert island to become the prey and spoil of birds (3.271); and the sea from which Menelaus has come home is so great that not even the birds come back from it within the year (3.321–322).
# οἰωνοί (3.259, 3.321) and οἰωνοῖσιν (3.271), first met in the Odyssey here; in the later books they are always birds
# (14.133, 16.216, 24.292).
grc: [οἰωνοὶ, οἰωνοῖσιν]
---

**Eaters of the dead.** Had Menelaus found Aegisthus alive, “the dogs and the birds would have devoured him / as he lay on the plain far from the city” (3.259–260; see [[dogs]] and [[aegisthus]]). The fate that Nestor imagines for Aegisthus is the one Aegisthus gave the singer whom Agamemnon had left to guard his wife: he took him to a desert island “and left him there to become the prey and spoil of birds” (3.270–271; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]] and [[clytemnestra]]). ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα γενέσθαι, “to become the prey and spoil”, is a verse-end of the Iliad, and but for its dative the verse is Glaucus' reproach to Hector, who has left Sarpedon “to become the prey and spoil of the Argives” (κάλλιπες Ἀργείοισιν, *Iliad* 17.151). Sarpedon had warned Hector that the Trojans might “become the prey and spoil of hostile men” (*Iliad* 5.488), and Odysseus, cast ashore on Scheria, fears that he will become the prey and spoil of wild beasts (5.473). οἰωνός is the large bird, often the carrion bird, and in this book it is never an omen: the sign that Nestor and his companions asked of the god at Lesbos is not said to have been a bird (3.173–175; see [[divination]]).

**Travelers over the sea.** Nestor sends Telemachus to Menelaus, who has lately come home “from men from whom no one would expect in his heart / to come home, once the storms have driven him astray / into a sea so great, from which not even the birds / come back within the year, since it is so great and terrible” (3.319–322; see [[menelaus]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The scholia call it a hyperbole. Merry sets beside it Odysseus' false tale, in which a fresh north wind carries him from Crete to Egypt in five days (14.252–257), and finds the illustration only half serious (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.321). οἰχνεῖν, he notes, is used of customary going, and he renders αὐτόετες οἰχνεῦσιν “can wend in the space of one year” (on 3.322).

**The bird named.** The book names one kind of bird: Athena leaves the company “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (3.372; see [[phene]]). The birds of 3.259, 3.271 and 3.321 are only οἰωνοί.
