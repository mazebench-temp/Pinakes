---
summary: After killing Agamemnon, Aegisthus “for seven years … ruled over Mycenae rich in gold”, until Orestes came back in the eighth (3.303–308); Merry takes the city “far from” which Aegisthus would have lain unburied for Mycenae, and quotes Curtius, who finds Mycenae in the “corner of horse-pasturing Argos” where Aegisthus courted Clytemnestra (3.260, 3.263).
---

**Aegisthus' seven years.** “And for seven years he ruled over Mycenae rich in gold; / but in the eighth brilliant Orestes came, an evil for him, / back from Athens” (3.305–307; see [[aegisthus]], [[orestes]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης, “Mycenae rich in gold”, is the Iliad's phrase for Agamemnon's city (*Iliad* 7.180, 11.46; see [[rich-in-gold]]). Murray prints 305 before 304, as Merry's text does, after Bergk and La Roche and the scholiast on Sophocles' *Electra*, who quotes the line as “Aegisthus ruled over Mycenae rich in gold, having killed the son of Atreus”; the text here keeps the order of the numbers (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.304; see the translation's note on 3.303–305). The city is Μυκήνη, in the singular, as in the Iliad's “wide-streeted Mycenae” (*Iliad* 4.52), where the Catalogue has the plural, Μυκῆναι (*Iliad* 2.569; Merry on 3.307).

**The city of the murder.** Nestor does not name Mycenae when he tells how Aegisthus would have lain unburied “on the plain far from the city”, but Merry takes the city to be Mycenae, and rejects a reading “far from Argos” (3.260; Merry on 3.260; see [[achaean-women]]). Where Aegisthus sat at ease, “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos”, while the army was at Troy (3.263), Ernst Curtius saw Mycenae again, at the innermost corner of the plain of the Inachus (Merry on 3.263; see [[argos]]).
