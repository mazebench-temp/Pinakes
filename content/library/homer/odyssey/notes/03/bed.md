**Think of bed.** At sunset Athena tells the company to pour libations to Poseidon and the other immortals “and then think of bed; for it is the hour” (3.334; see [[libation]]). κοίτοιο μεδώμεθα is the end of a feast: the Phaeacians pour their last libation of the day to Hermes “when they thought of bed” (7.137–138, a plain rendering).

**Not on the deck.** Athena and Telemachus mean to sleep at their ship, and Nestor will not allow it: “Surely the dear son of this man Odysseus will not / lie down on the deck of a ship while I / am alive” (3.352–354; see [[xenia]] and [[blankets]]). A guest sleeps in the house, on bedding fit for a guest. Athena alone goes back to the ship, to lie down beside it with the crew (3.365; see [[athena]]).

**In Nestor's house.** When the others have gone home, Nestor “had him sleep there, / Telemachus, the dear son of divine Odysseus, / on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade, / and beside him Peisistratus” (3.397–400; see [[corded]], [[house]] and [[peisistratus]]). The verse of the guest's bed returns for Odysseus in the house of Alcinous (3.399 = 7.345), and at Sparta and in Achilles' hut too beds are made for guests beneath the colonnade (4.297; *Iliad* 24.644). Nestor himself “slept in the inner part of the high house, / and the lady his wife shared his bed and couch” (3.402–403; see [[eurydice]]). W. W. Merry distinguishes the words: λέχος is properly the bedstead, εὐνή the bedding; πορσύνω is used always of a wife who shares the bed, from the plain fact that she alone had free access to her husband's chamber, which lay at the back of the house (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.403). The verse is said again of Arete (7.347). At dawn “the Gerenian horseman Nestor rose from his bed” (3.405), as Menelaus does at Sparta on the morning after Telemachus arrives (4.306–307).
