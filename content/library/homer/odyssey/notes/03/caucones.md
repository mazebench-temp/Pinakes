---
summary: Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, tells Nestor that she will sleep by the ship and go at dawn “to the great-hearted Caucones, / where a debt is owed me, not a recent one at all / nor a small one” (3.365–368).
---

Mentor's errand among the Caucones is Athena's reason for leaving Telemachus to Nestor: she will lie down by the black ship tonight, “but at dawn I will go to the great-hearted Caucones, / where a debt is owed me, not a recent one at all / nor a small one” (3.366–368; see [[athena]], [[mentor]] and [[great-hearted]]). The poem does not return to it. χρεῖος, “debt”, is property carried off in a raid or wrongfully kept back, as the “great debt” owed to Neleus in Elis, the four prize-winning horses that Augeas kept (*Iliad* 11.698–702), for which the Pylians took reprisals by force, or the sheep that the Messenians carried off from Ithaca, for which the young Odysseus was sent to Messene (21.15–21); Mentor, with his small crew, can only press his claim in peace (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.367).

Merry takes these Caucones for a Pelasgian people between Arcadia and the coast of Elis, divided by Strabo into two tribes, in Triphylia and in Hollow Elis; Herodotus' “Pylian Caucones” (Herodotus 1.147) are the Triphylian branch, and they must be kept apart from the Caucones of Paphlagonia, allies of Troy (*Iliad* 10.429; Merry on 3.366). Strabo argues from this passage that Athena means those of the north of Elis, near Dyme, since the road to the Triphylian Caucones lay with Telemachus' own (*Geography* 8.3.17; see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]).
