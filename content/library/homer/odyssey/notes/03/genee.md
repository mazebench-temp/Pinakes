---
summary: Nestor doubts whether Telemachus is truly Odysseus' “offspring” (3.123), and Telemachus says that Nestor “has been king for three generations of men” (3.245).
grc: ["γένεʼ ἀνδρῶν", ἔκγονος]
---

**Three generations.** τρὶς γὰρ δή μίν φασιν ἀνάξασθαι γένεʼ ἀνδρῶν (3.245) is the Iliad's Nestor, in whose lifetime two generations of men had passed away, “and now he was king among the third” (*Iliad* 1.250–252; see [[nestor]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). Merry finds the verse suspect: ἀνάξασθαι is the only middle of ἀνάσσω in Homer, Nauck conjectured ἀνασσέμεναι, and τρίς stands for τρία; if it is genuine, γένεα is an accusative of duration, “hath been king during three generations of men”. He notes that a generation was reckoned differently, by the Greeks at something over thirty years, as Herodotus counts three generations of men to a hundred years (*Histories* 2.142), and suggests that the line may have been added to explain the next, “and he appears to me like an immortal to look upon” (3.246), which spoke only of his noble look (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.245; see the translation's note on 3.244–245 and [[anax]]).

**His offspring.** Nestor's doubt is the book's question of the son: πατὴρ τεός, εἰ ἐτεόν γε / κείνου ἔκγονός ἐσσι (3.122–123). It is answered at once by the likeness of his words (3.123–125; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[sebas]]), as Athena in Book 2 made his success depend on whether he was the offspring of Odysseus and Penelope (2.274).
