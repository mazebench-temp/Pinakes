---
summary: Nestor offers Telemachus a chariot, horses and his sons as escorts “to shining Lacedaemon, where fair-haired Menelaus is” (3.324–326); the two young men drive there by way of Pherae and reach the wheat-bearing plain on the second evening (3.484–497).
---

**The goal of the journey.** Menelaus has lately come home, and Nestor sends Telemachus to him: “or if you wish to go by land, here are a chariot and horses for you, / and here are my sons, who will be your escorts / to shining Lacedaemon, where fair-haired Menelaus is” (3.324–326; see [[menelaus]], [[chariot]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Lacedaemon is Menelaus' land, and Telemachus reaches it overland: Pylos to Pherae in a day's drive, and Pherae to “the wheat-bearing plain” on the next (3.484–497; see [[pylos]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]). Merry finds the epithet πυρηφόρον, “wheat-bearing”, hard to square with the rugged and narrow upper valley of the Eurotas, and concludes that the poet's knowledge of the country had a blank here, which he filled up by guess (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.495). The next book opens with the arrival, in the Catalogue's words for Menelaus' land, “hollow Lacedaemon with its many ravines” (4.1, as *Iliad* 2.581).
