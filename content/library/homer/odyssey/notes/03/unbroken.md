---
summary: ἀδμήτην, “unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke”, describes the heifer of Nestor's vow to Athena (3.383), in the verse of Diomedes' vow in the Iliad.
---

**The epithet explained.** The heifer is ἀδμήτην, ἣν οὔ πω ὑπὸ ζυγὸν ἤγαγεν ἀνήρ, “unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke” (3.383; see [[cattle]], where the vow is quoted, [[yearling]] and [[broad-browed]]). The relative clause explains the word (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.383): she has never worked. The verse is Diomedes' (*Iliad* 10.293), and at Pylos the vow is kept the next morning (3.418–463; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).

**The verb in the book.** The verb of the epithet, δάμνημι, “tame, break, overcome”, comes back in Book 3 in its other senses. Odysseus may have been “brought down” by hostile men (δάμη, 3.90); Nestor asks Telemachus whether he “submits” willingly to the suitors (ὑποδάμνασαι, 3.214); the fate of the gods fettered Clytemnestra “to be overcome” (δαμῆναι, 3.269, as the translation reads the line; see the translation's note on 3.269); the people of Mycenae were “made subject” to Aegisthus (δέδμητο, 3.304); and Neleus “had already been overcome by his doom” (κηρὶ δαμείς, 3.410). The entry notes that the adjective describes an unwed girl as well as an unbroken beast (see the entry).
