summary: The book's speeches close with the formula nine times, from “So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way” (3.29) to “So he spoke, and they listened closely to him and obeyed” (3.477); among them “So she prayed” after Athena's prayer (3.62), “So spoke the daughter of Zeus” (3.337), and “So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away” (3.371).
grc: ["ὣς ἄρʼ ἔπειτʼ ἠρᾶτο", "=ἦ ῥα Διὸς θυγάτηρ"]
en: [So she prayed]
---

**The effect of the speech.** The rest of the verse turns to what the words do. Athena's prayer closes with a verse found only here, ὣς ἄρʼ ἔπειτʼ ἠρᾶτο καὶ αὐτὴ πάντα τελεύτα, “So she prayed, and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.62): as a seeming mortal she prays to a higher power, as a goddess she answers her own prayer, and ἔπειτα resumes the act already told (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.62; [[prayer-type-scene]]). At 3.329 the speech ends with the sunset ([[sunset-lines]]); at 3.371 the goddess goes away in the likeness of a sea eagle ([[divine-epiphany]], [[phene]]); at 3.385 the verse of prayer, ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος, is answered ([[hear-me]]); and the orders of 3.430 and 3.477 are carried out at once ([[the-sacrifice-to-athena]], [[chariot-journey]]).

**Variants.** ὣς εἰπών, “So saying” (3.51), as Peisistratus puts the cup in the stranger's hand. ἦ ῥα Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “So spoke the daughter of Zeus” (3.337): Merry explains ἦ as the imperfect of a defective verb “say,” which, as Aristarchus observed, Homer always puts after a speech, never before it (Merry on 3.337).
