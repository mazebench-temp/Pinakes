summary: Nestor's Aegisthus sat at ease in a corner of Argos while the Achaeans toiled at Troy, bewitched Agamemnon's wife, marooned her singer, and burned thank-offerings for a deed beyond his hopes; he killed the son of Atreus, ruled Mycenae for seven years, and in the eighth was killed by Orestes, who gave the Argives a funeral feast for his mother and “the coward Aegisthus” (3.193–198, 3.250–275, 3.303–310). Had Menelaus found him alive, he would have had no grave (3.255–261).

**Scheming.** δολόμητις, “scheming”, three times in the book: in the verse that Nestor shares with Athena's speech as Mentes, Αἴγισθον δολόμητιν, ὅ οἱ πατέρα κλυτὸν ἔκτα, “scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father” (3.198 = 3.308 = 1.300), and in Telemachus' question, “scheming Aegisthus, since he killed a far better man” (3.250; see [[schemer]]). Athena, as Mentor, lays Agamemnon's death to his guile, δόλῳ (3.235; see [[dolos]]).

**No grave.** The loose earth, χυτὴ γαῖα, is the heaped mound of a burial (3.258; see [[tomb]]): Aegisthus would have been refused it, and left to the dogs and birds on the plain far from the city, with no Achaean woman to weep for him (see [[dogs]], [[birds]] and [[achaean-women]]). ἑκὰς ἄστεος, “far from the city”, Merry takes of Mycenae (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.260). In the Iliad the bodies left to dogs and birds are the dead of battle, from the proem on (*Iliad* 1.4–5); here it is the end that Menelaus would have given his brother's murderer.

**At ease.** While the Achaeans sat at Troy, “toiling through many trials”, he sat εὔκηλος, “undisturbed”, in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos, and kept bewitching Agamemnon's wife with many words (3.262–264; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[clytemnestra]]). When the fate of the gods had fettered her, he took her singer to a desert island and left him to the birds, and led her, willing, to his house (3.269–272; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]).

**The thank-offerings.** “And he burned many thigh-pieces on the holy altars of the gods, / and hung up many offerings, woven things and gold, / having accomplished a great deed, which he had never expected in his heart” (3.273–275; see [[sacrifice]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). ἀγάλματα, “offerings”, are explained by what follows, the woven things and the gold, in Merry's term an epexegesis (on 3.274). μέγα ἔργον is here a hard task accomplished, as it was a bold and impious one fourteen verses before, “for very great was the deed he devised” (3.261), and Merry notes how hard the senses are to keep apart (on 3.261). Ameis, as Merry reports, took the offerings as proof of how hard the conquest of Clytemnestra's scruples was: Aegisthus was surprised at his own success (on 3.269).

**The ruler and the coward.** He killed the son of Atreus, the people were made subject to him, and for seven years he ruled over Mycenae rich in gold (3.304–305; see [[mycenae]] and the translation's note on 3.303–305). In the eighth Orestes came, κακόν, “an evil for him” (3.306), which Merry takes as a predicate, “came as a disaster upon him” (on 3.306; see [[orestes]]). His last epithet is ἄναλκις: Orestes gave the Argives a funeral feast “for his hateful mother and for the coward Aegisthus” (3.310; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]] and the translation's key-word row for ἄναλκις). Merry compares Cassandra's name for him in Aeschylus, λέοντʼ ἄναλκιν, “a cowardly lion”, who stays at home in the bed and plots vengeance against the returning master (on 3.310; *Agamemnon* 1223–1225; see [[aeschylus]]). The Suda, glossing ἄναλκις as “powerless, weak”, quotes a tragedy on Aegisthus, ὁ πάντʼ ἄναλκις οὗτος, “this utter coward”, “who fights his battles with women”, and explains that he killed Agamemnon with Clytemnestra's help (Suda α 1941).
