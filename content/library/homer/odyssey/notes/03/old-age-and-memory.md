summary: Nestor is the book's rememberer. Reminded by Telemachus, he tells of the misery at Troy, the dead, the quarrel of the kings and his own voyage, and what he has heard since (3.103–200); reminded again, he tells of Agamemnon's death and Menelaus' wanderings (3.211–312). He “has been king for three generations of men” (3.245).
refs: [3.103-117, 3.184-187, 3.211, 3.245-246, 3.253-254]
---

**Reminded.** Both of Nestor's tales begin with the young man's reminder, ὦ φίλʼ, ἐπεί μʼ ἔμνησας ὀιζύος, “My friend, since you have reminded me of the misery that we endured” (3.103), and “My friend, since you have reminded me of this and spoken of it” (3.211; see the translation's table of formulas). The first ἐπεί never finds its clause: Merry notes that the form of the sentence is forgotten by the speaker in the crowd of details that throng into his mind, the virtual answer coming only at 3.120 (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.103). Nor can memory tell it all: “who could ever / tell them all, of mortal men? / Not even if you stayed five years, or six” (3.113–117), and Merry paraphrases its end, “You would be off at once, and never stop till you got home” (on 3.117).

**What the old man knows.** Nestor's knowledge has its limits, and he states them: he came home “without tidings”, and knows of the others only what he has heard sitting in his halls (3.184–187; see [[kleos]]). His authority is his age: he “has been king for three generations of men”, the Iliad's Nestor among the third generation (3.245; *Iliad* 1.250–252; see [[genee]]), and “he appears to me like an immortal to look upon” (3.246). His sons do what he bids, the young man is sent where he advises, and the goddess praises his tale as told “fair and right” (3.331; see [[nestor]], [[geron]] and [[moira]]).
