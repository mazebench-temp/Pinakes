summary: ἄναξ is a title of gods and men alike: “lord Poseidon” (3.43, 3.54), “lord Priam” (3.107), “lord Odysseus” (3.163), and Nestor, “that lord” (3.388); Nestor addresses Athena as ἄνασσα, “queen” (3.380). The verb names rule: Nestor “has been king for three generations of men” (3.245), and Aegisthus “ruled over Mycenae rich in gold” for seven years (3.305).
grc: [ἀνάξασθαι, ἤνασσε, ἄνασσʼ]
en: [queen, ruled, has been king]
---

**Lord.** ἄναξ is the title of a god at his own feast, Poseidon, to whom the Pylians sacrifice on the shore (3.43, 3.54; see [[poseidon]]), of the king whose city fell, Priam (3.107; see [[priam]]), of Odysseus, who turned back at Tenedos (3.163; see [[odysseus]]), and of Nestor in his famous house (3.388; see [[nestor]]). When Nestor knows the goddess he prays to her as ἄνασσα, “queen” (3.380; see [[athena]] and [[kleos]]), the address Odysseus will use to Nausicaa when he cannot tell whether she is a goddess or a mortal (6.149, 6.175).

**Rule.** The verb names the long rule and the usurped one. Nestor “has been king for three generations of men” (3.245), with the only middle of ἀνάσσω in Homer, which made Merry suspect the line (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.245; see [[genee]] and the translation's note on 3.244–245). Aegisthus, having killed the son of Atreus, ἑπτάετες δʼ ἤνασσε πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης, until Orestes came back in the eighth year (3.304–306; see [[aegisthus]], [[mycenae]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]); the line stands before 3.304 in Murray's text (see the translation's note on 3.303–305).
