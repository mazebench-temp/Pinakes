**The welcome.** When the Pylians see the strangers they all come to greet them, but Peisistratus is first: “First Peisistratus, son of Nestor, came close / and took them both by the hand and seated them at the feast / on soft fleeces on the sands of the sea, / beside his brother Thrasymedes and his father” (3.36–39; see [[thrasymedes]], [[nestor]] and [[xenia]]). He gives them portions of the entrails and pours wine into a golden cup, “and pledging her he spoke / to Pallas Athena” (3.40–42; see [[cup]]): δειδισκόμενος is “welcoming”, holding out the full cup (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.41).

**The cup.** He bids the stranger pray to the god whose feast they have come upon, and then pass the cup:

{{quote:4407cd74-1806-5550-8074-3639e34bc81a}}

Merry renders εὔχεσθαι (3.48) “a worshipper”, and reports Nitzsch's comparison of the half-verse πάντες δὲ θεῶν χατέουσʼ ἄνθρωποι, “and all men have need of the gods”, with Aratus, “in every way we all have need of Zeus” (*Phaenomena* 4; Merry on 3.48). ὁμηλικίη is the abstract for the concrete, “he is my own equal in years” (Merry on 3.49), the word Athena, as Mentor, uses of the young crew, “all of an age with great-hearted Telemachus” (3.364). The goddess is pleased with him, “and Athena rejoiced in the wise and just man, / because he gave the golden goblet to her first” (3.52–53; see [[wise]]): δίκαιος is one who knows and keeps the usage of men, Merry explains, as Nestor “knows judgments” (3.244; Merry on 3.52).

**At night and at dawn.** When the others go home to sleep, Nestor keeps Telemachus in the colonnade, “and beside him Peisistratus of the good ash spear, leader of men, / who of his sons was still a young unmarried man in his halls” (3.400–401; see [[with-good-ash-spear]] and [[leader-of-men]]). The married sons and the sons-in-law have their own chambers, Merry thinks detached buildings, and Peisistratus, the one ἠίθεος, has none (on 3.396). At dawn he comes last of the six sons to his father's seat, “and then the hero Peisistratus came to them as the sixth” (3.415; see [[heros]]). Merry takes him for the youngest of the six (on 3.36).

**The sacrifice.** When Thrasymedes has felled the heifer and the women have raised the cry, the others lift her head, “and Peisistratus, leader of men, cut her throat” (3.453–454; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). Merry takes οἱ μέν, who lift the head and hold her, for the other sons of Nestor, set against Peisistratus (on 3.453), and notes that the blow of the axe only stunned the heifer, which dies under the knife (3.454–455; Merry on 3.450).

**The drive.** Athena asks Nestor to send Telemachus on “with a chariot and your son” (3.369), and the son is Peisistratus: “and beside him Peisistratus, son of Nestor, leader of men, / mounted the chariot and took the reins in his hands, / and whipped them to a run” (3.482–484; see [[chariot]] and [[chariot-journey]]). They spend the night at Pherae with Diocles and drive on at dawn across the wheat-bearing plain (3.487–497; see [[diocles]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**His names.** Νεστορίδης, “son of Nestor” (3.36, 3.482), is the patronymic the Iliad gives to Antilochus and to Thrasymedes (*Iliad* 6.33, 9.81, 15.589; see [[antilochus]]). Three times he is ὄρχαμος ἀνδρῶν, “leader of men” (3.400, 3.454, 3.482), as the Iliad calls Achilles (*Iliad* 6.99).
