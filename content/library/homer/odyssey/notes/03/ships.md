---
summary: Telemachus' balanced ship puts in at Pylos and waits by the shore while he goes on by land (3.10–12, 3.344–365, 3.423–432). In Nestor's tales the ships are the fleet going home from Troy, launched, divided and scattered, and Menelaus' ships, five of which reach Egypt and come home laden (3.131–182, 3.279–312).
---

**The fleet going home.** After the sack Nestor and his men had “gone aboard the ships”, βῆμεν δʼ ἐν νήεσσι, with a preposition of rest after a verb of motion (3.131; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.131). At dawn half of the army dragged their ships down into the sea and put their possessions on board, and the deep-girdled women (3.153–154; see [[possessions]] and [[captive-women]]); at Tenedos some turned back “in their curving ships” with Odysseus (3.162; see [[curving]]), and Nestor fled “with all the ships that followed me in a body” (3.165), ἀολλέσιν, “crowded together” (Merry on 3.165). The balanced ships of Diomedes' companions were moored at Argos on the fourth day (3.180–181; see [[balanced-ships]] and [[argos]]).

**Menelaus' ships.** Apollo killed Menelaus' helmsman “as he held the steering oar of the speeding ship in his hands” (3.281; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]]); μετὰ χερσί, “in his hands”, shows, Merry says, that the Homeric rudder was a paddle, since no one grasps a tiller between his hands (on 3.281). The fleet went on “in the hollow ships” to Malea (3.287; see [[hollow-ships]] and [[malea]]); some were smashed on the reefs of Crete, and “the five dark-prowed ships” were carried to Egypt (3.298–300; see [[dark-prowed]], [[crete]] and [[egypt]]). They came home with “as much as his ships could carry as their load” (3.312), ἄχθος, “as freight” (Merry on 3.312; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**Telemachus' ship.** The ship from Ithaca comes in at sunrise, and the crew furl the sails of “the balanced ship”, anchor her and step out (3.10–12; see [[sails]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). When Telemachus and Athena want to go back to the hollow ship at nightfall, Nestor will not have it: “Surely the dear son of this man Odysseus will not / lie down on the deck of a ship while I / am alive” (3.344, 3.352–354; see [[xenia]] and [[nestor]]). Athena goes back alone to sleep “by the hollow black ship” among the young companions (3.360–365; see [[black-ships]]), and the next morning a man is sent to bring the companions from the ship to the sacrifice, leaving two only (3.423–424, 3.431–432).
