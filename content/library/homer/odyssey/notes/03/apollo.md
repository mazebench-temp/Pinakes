**The helmsman at Sunium.** Nestor and Menelaus sail together from Troy, “with friendly hearts toward one another” (3.276–277), until they come to the cape:

{{quote:45b72652-bc8d-5d04-8e4a-b0fbe094578a}}

The god is named with his title, Φοῖβος Ἀπόλλων (see [[phoebus]]), and the helmsman by his skill: Phrontis, son of Onetor, was the best of men at the steering oar in a storm (see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]] and [[sunium]]). Menelaus, eager though he was for his journey, stays to give him burial, and only then, off Malea, does Zeus send the storm that splits the fleet (3.286–300; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[menelaus]] and [[zeus]]). Merry takes μετὰ χερσί, “in his hands”, as proof that the Homeric rudder is a paddle, grasped between the hands and not worked by a tiller (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.281). Nothing is said of why the god struck.

**The gentle arrows.** οἷς ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος κατέπεφνε is the Odyssey's verse for a sudden death sent by Apollo or by Artemis, and this is its first use. Merry explains the gentle arrows as a death without suffering, brought as a rule by the god to men and by the goddess to women, which Odysseus sets against a long illness when he asks his mother how she died (11.171–173), and which the people of Syria receive in old age, free of all illness (15.407–411); Penelope prays to Artemis for such a death (18.202, 20.61–63). It was not always a mercy, he adds, since Artemis killed Ariadne in anger and Orion out of jealousy (11.324, 5.121–124), and he asks whether the phrase may be a poet's way of describing the sudden effect of sunstroke (on 3.280; see [[artemis]] and [[fresh-as-dew]]). The Iliad has the verse once, in Hecuba's lament, where Hector lies as fresh as a man whom Apollo has killed so (*Iliad* 24.757–759).
