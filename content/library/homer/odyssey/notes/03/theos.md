---
summary: “and all men have need of the gods” (3.48): Nestor's tale is full of gods, most of them unnamed. A god scattered the Achaeans after Troy (3.131) and made smooth the sea (3.158), the god showed a sign at Lesbos and sent the fair wind (3.173–183); a god can save a man from afar, but not even the gods can ward off death (3.231–238).
---

**The unnamed god.** Nestor's tale gives most of its gods no name: θεὸς δʼ ἐκέδασσεν Ἀχαιούς, “a god scattered the Achaeans” (3.131), ἐστόρεσεν δὲ θεὸς μεγακήτεα πόντον, “a god made smooth the vast-gulfed sea” (3.158), the god whom they asked to show a sign, and who showed it (3.173–175; see [[teras]]), the god who first sent the fair wind (3.183), and the δαίμων who “was devising evil” (3.166; see [[daimon]] and [[divine-intervention]]). Where he names one it is Zeus, who devised the grim homecoming (3.132), Athena, whose wrath set the brothers at strife (3.135), Poseidon, on whose altar they laid thigh-pieces at Geraestus (3.178–179), or Apollo, who killed Menelaus' helmsman (3.279–280). On Nestor's “following the voice of a god” (θεοῦ ὀμφῇ, 3.215), Merry takes the god's voice for the unspoken, contagious feeling of a multitude (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.214; see [[laos]]).

**Maxims of the gods.** The sayings of the book are about the gods: all men need them (3.48; see [[all-men-need-the-gods]]), their mind is not quickly turned (3.147; see [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]), a god can save a man even from far away (3.231; see [[a-god-can-save-a-man-from-afar]]), and not even they can ward off death from a man they love (3.236–237; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]). The goddess who sits among the Pylians while these things are said is known only when she goes (3.371–379; see [[athena]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]), and she comes the next morning to share in the sacrifice, no longer as Mentor but in her godhead, unseen, as Merry takes it (on 3.435), and the horns are gilded “so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (3.435–438; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).
