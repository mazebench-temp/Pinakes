summary: Nestor's tale is a tale of homecomings. Zeus devised “a grim homecoming / for the Argives” (3.132–133); Menelaus bade the army “think of their homecoming”, and Agamemnon would not (3.141–146); at Tenedos “Zeus was not yet devising our homecoming” (3.160). Athena would rather “come home and see the day of my homecoming” late than die at her own hearth, and Telemachus answers that for his father “there is no longer any true homecoming” (3.232–241).
grc: [νεοίμεθα]
except: [3.336, 3.344]
---

**The grim homecoming.** Phemius sang it in Book 1, “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327); Nestor tells it as one who came home. Zeus devised λυγρὸν … νόστον because the Argives were not all discerning or just, and many met an evil end through Athena's wrath (3.132–135; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]], [[the-song-of-the-returns]] and [[menis]]). The quarrel of the brothers turns on the word: Menelaus bade the Achaeans νόστου μιμνήσκεσθαι, “to think of their homecoming over the broad back of the sea”, and Agamemnon wished to stay and appease the goddess (3.141–147; see [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]). Merry joins νόστου with ἐπʼ εὐρέα νῶτα, as νόστου γαίης Φαιήκων (5.344) (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.142). At Tenedos the half that sailed sacrificed “eager to go home”, a desire in which Merry finds the reason for the sacrifice, “but Zeus was not yet devising our homecoming, / the hard god” (3.159–161; Merry on 3.160; see [[zeus]] and [[hard]]).

**The homecomings told.** Nestor sailed home ἀπευθής, “without tidings” (3.184), and tells two kinds of homecoming: his own, which went well, by Lesbos, the god's sign and Geraestus, debating “whether we should go home above rocky Chios” (νεοίμεθα, 3.169–183; see [[teras]]), and those he has heard of, the Myrmidons', Philoctetes', Idomeneus' and Agamemnon's (3.188–198; see [[so-good-a-thing-is-a-son]]). Menelaus' homecoming is the longest: from Sunium and Malea to Crete and Egypt, home at last on the day of the funeral feast for Aegisthus (3.276–312; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**Late, or not at all.** Athena prefers a late homecoming to an early death: “And I for my part would rather, even after toiling through many sorrows, / come home and see the day of my homecoming” (3.232–233; see [[a-god-can-save-a-man-from-afar]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). Telemachus answers that there is none: κείνῳ δʼ οὐκέτι νόστος ἐτήτυμος (3.241; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]). The son's own homecoming is in Athena's prayer on the shore, that she and Telemachus may return when they have done their business (3.60; see [[prexis]]); Nestor sends him on to Menelaus, but warns him not to wander long far from his home, with overbearing men in his house (3.313–316; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**Not homecomings.** νέεσθαι at 3.336 is Athena's “to go home” from the feast at nightfall, and at 3.344 the strangers' wish “to go back to the hollow ship”: both lines are excepted.
