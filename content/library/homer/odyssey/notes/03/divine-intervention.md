summary: The goddess at work in disguise: Athena, as Mentor, prays to Poseidon, “and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.62); she puts daring in Telemachus' mind (3.76–77) and leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle (3.371–372). In Nestor's tale the gods scatter the fleet, smooth the sea, show a sign and send the wind (3.131–183), and Apollo and Zeus take Menelaus' helmsman and his ships (3.279–300).

**The goddess among men.** Athena acts in Book 3 as Mentor, and the narrator shows what the mortals do not see. She prays to Poseidon as a seeming mortal and answers the prayer as a goddess (3.55–62; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.62; see [[athena]] and [[all-men-need-the-gods]]); she puts daring in Telemachus' mind (3.76–77; see [[tharsos]]) and tells him that a god will give him counsel (3.26–28; see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]]); and she leaves the feast as a bird, so that Nestor knows her (3.371–379; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[phene]]).

**The gods of the voyage.** Nestor's tale of the returns is full of gods at work: a god scatters the Achaeans (3.131), smooths the vast-gulfed sea (3.158), shows a sign when asked (3.173–175; see [[teras]]) and sends a wind that never fails (3.176–183; see [[theos]]). Apollo kills Menelaus' helmsman “with his gentle arrows” off Sunium (3.279–283; see [[apollo]] and [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]]), and Zeus devises a hateful journey for him off Malea and pours the breath of shrill winds on his ships (3.286–290; see [[zeus]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Against these Nestor sets the god's help for Telemachus: “if, young as you are, the gods go with you as your escorts” (3.375–376).
