summary: After the god's sign at Lesbos, Nestor's ships run across the fish-filled ways and put in at Geraestus by night, and there they lay many thigh-pieces of bulls on Poseidon's altar, “having measured the great open sea” (3.176–179).

{{quote:b872446d-64c3-5038-a513-bdcac9a81998}}

Geraestus ends the third day out of Troy: the first day's sail was to Tenedos, the second to Lesbos and the third across the open sea to Geraestus, and so the ships reach Argos on “the fourth day”, as Nestor says (3.180; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.169; see [[tenedos]], [[lesbos]] and [[argos]]). ἐννύχιαι is “by night”, not, as Eustathius took it, “in the course of a single night”; Merry compares ἔνδιος, “in the full day” (Merry on 3.178). The crossing is a measuring of the sea, πέλαγος μέγα μετρήσαντες, and Merry sets beside it Virgil's *magnum curru aequor metitur* (*Georgics* 4.388) and Ovid's *celerique carina Aegaeas metiris aquas* (*Metamorphoses* 9.447; Merry on 3.179; see [[sea]]).

The sacrifice is the first thing done on land, and it goes to the god of the sea just crossed, Poseidon; ἐπὶ … ἔθεμεν go together, “laid on his altar” (Merry on 3.179; see [[poseidon]], [[bulls]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). The book has begun with another sacrifice of bulls to Poseidon, on the shore at Pylos (3.5–8; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). Merry adds that Geraestus had a temple of Poseidon, quoting Strabo (on 3.179). From here Nestor holds on for Pylos with the same fair wind, which never drops (3.182–183; see [[pylos]]).
