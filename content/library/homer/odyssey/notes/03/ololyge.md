---
summary: At the sacrifice to Athena, when Thrasymedes' axe cuts through the sinews of the heifer's neck, “the women raised the cry, / his daughters and his sons' wives and the revered wife / of Nestor, Eurydice” (3.450–452).
---

**At the stroke.** The cry comes at the moment the axe falls, before the throat is cut: Thrasymedes strikes, the blow “loosed the strength of the heifer”, the women cry out, and only then do the sons lift her head from the earth and Peisistratus cuts her throat (3.448–454; see [[thrasymedes]] and [[peisistratus]]). Merry notes that the single blow only stunned the victim, and that the cry is no shriek of horror but a religious shout of joy at the consummation of the sacrifice (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.450). The women who raise it are the household of the king who leads the rite: his daughters, his sons' wives (νυοί; see [[in-laws]]), and his wife Eurydice (see [[eurydice]] and [[aidos]]). The rite is framed by the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse (3.447 and 3.458–462, as *Iliad* 1.458 and 1.461–465), where Odysseus' men slaughter the hecatomb for Apollo with no women and no cry; Nestor's sacrifice adds the stroke of the axe, the cry and the cutting of the throat between them (see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]).
