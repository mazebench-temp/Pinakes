summary: Nestor offers Telemachus a chariot and horses for the journey by land, and Athena bids him send the young man on “with a chariot and your son” (3.324, 3.369). After the sacrifice Nestor's sons yoke the horses, and Peisistratus drives Telemachus by Pherae toward Sparta in two days (3.475–497).

**By land.** The chariot is Nestor's alternative to the ship: “But go now with your ship and your companions; / or if you wish to go by land, here are a chariot and horses for you” (3.323–324; see [[lacedaemon]]). Athena, departing, makes it the plan: “And this man, since he has come to your house, / send on with a chariot and your son” (3.368–369; see [[peisistratus]] and [[horses]]). δίφρος, “chariot”, the car on which driver and passenger stand, is the word of both offers.

**The drive.** When the sacrifice and the meal are done, Nestor bids his sons yoke fine-maned horses “under the chariot, so that he may make his way” (3.475–476; see [[fine-maned]] and [[prexis]]). ὑφʼ ἅρματα, “under the chariot”, means the yoke, part of the whole apparatus, the plural being used, as Merry says, for a complex structure like ἱστία, “sails” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.476). The housekeeper puts bread, wine and meats in it, Telemachus mounts, Peisistratus takes the reins and whips the horses to a run, and the pair fly on to the plain (3.479–485; see [[handmaids]] and [[chariot-journey]]). “And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side” (3.486): ἀμφὶς ἔχοντες, “supporting it at either end”, of the two horses. Merry describes the yoke, a bar lashed across the end of the pole, with a pad at either end resting on the horses' necks, the straps that served as collars, and rings for the reins, and refers to the yoking of Priam's wagon for the whole account (Merry on 3.486; *Iliad* 24.266–274). On the second day they drive out from the doorway and the colonnade of Diocles' house in “the inlaid chariot” (3.492–493; see [[inlaid]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]); Merry notes the reversed order of the verse, since the colonnade is passed before the gateway (on 3.493). The same verses bring them back in Book 15 (15.182–192).
