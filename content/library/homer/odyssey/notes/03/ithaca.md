---
summary: Telemachus answers Nestor's question about the strangers: they have come “from Ithaca under Neion”, on a private business, not a public one (3.80–82). Nestor's counsel turns on the island he has left, where the suitors devour his house (3.211–216, 3.313–316).
---

**From Ithaca.** Asked who the strangers are and where they sail from, Telemachus names his island first: “We have come from Ithaca under Neion; / and this business I speak of is private, not public” (3.81–82; see [[neion]] and [[prexis]]). ὑπονήιος, “under Neion”, is said of the town, not of the whole island, as Merry takes it; the scholia compare Θήβη ὑποπλακίη, “Thebe under Placus” (*Iliad* 6.397; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.81). Strabo asks the same question of the Catalogue's men “who held Ithaca and Neritum”, whether Ithaca there is the city or the island, and leaves it open (*Geography* 10.2.11, on *Iliad* 2.632).

**The house at home.** Ithaca is in Nestor's mind when he speaks of the suitors: “they say that many suitors, for your mother's sake, / devise evils in your halls against your will” (3.212–213; see [[the-suitors]]), and he asks whether the people hate Telemachus throughout the land, following the voice of a god (3.214–215; see [[demos]]). When he has told of Menelaus' wanderings, he draws the lesson for the young man who has left his island: “And you too, my friend, do not wander long far from your home, / leaving your possessions behind, and men in your house / so overbearing” (3.313–315; see [[possessions]] and [[overbearing]]). Athena's prayer at the feast has already asked for the return: “grant also that Telemachus and I may return when we have done the business / for which we came here in our swift black ship” (3.60–61; see [[nostos]]).
