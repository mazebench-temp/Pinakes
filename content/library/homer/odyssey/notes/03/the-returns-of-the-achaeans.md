summary: Nestor tells Telemachus the returns as he saw them and as he has heard them. A god scattered the Achaeans after the sack, Zeus devised a grim homecoming, and Athena's wrath divided the fleet; Nestor and Diomedes came home quickly, the Myrmidons, Philoctetes and Idomeneus safely, Agamemnon to his death, and Menelaus last, by way of Egypt. Of Odysseus after Tenedos he knows nothing (3.130–200, 3.276–312).

**The grim homecoming.** Nestor begins where Phemius' song began, with the homecoming “that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). λυγρὸν … νόστον, “a grim homecoming” (3.132), is the song's phrase, νόστον … λυγρόν (1.326–327; see [[grim]] and [[nostos]]), and κακὸν οἶτον ἐπέσπον, “met an evil fate” (3.134), is the fate Telemachus said the singer sang, Δαναῶν κακὸν οἶτον, “the evil fate of the Danaans” (1.350). Nestor gives two causes: the Argives were “in no way discerning or just” (3.133), and Athena was angry (3.135; see [[athena]] and [[menis]]). He does not say why. Merry takes the goddess's wrath to have been aimed first at Locrian Ajax, for his outrage to Cassandra, and then at the chieftains who left it unpunished (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.135; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[cassandra]]); in the next book Menelaus tells how Ajax died (4.499–511, a plain rendering). The wrath sets the sons of Atreus at strife and splits the army (3.136–167; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]).

**Four days home.** Nestor's own homecoming was swift. Tenedos was the first day's run, Lesbos the second, Geraestus the third, and on the fourth Diomedes' companions moored at Argos (Merry on 3.169, from 3.159–180; see [[tenedos]], [[lesbos]] and [[geraestus]]). At Lesbos they pondered “the long voyage”, the straight run across the open Aegean, shorter than the course by the islands but without a port of call, whether above Chios by Psyria or below it past Mimas; Nitzsch remarks, Merry reports, that in seamanship the Achaeans lagged behind the Phoenicians, the Taphians and the Cretans (on 3.169; see [[chios]], [[psyria]], [[mimas]] and [[cicero-the-long-voyage]]). They asked the god for a sign, and he bade them “cut across the middle of the open sea to Euboea” (3.173–175; see [[teras]] and [[euboea]]); at Geraestus they laid many thigh-pieces of bulls on Poseidon's altar, “having measured the great open sea” (3.178–179; see [[poseidon]]). Diomedes' ships came to Argos, “but I held on for Pylos, and the fair wind never / died down, once the god had first sent it forth to blow” (3.182–183; see [[diomedes]], [[argos]] and [[pylos]]).

**What he has heard.**

{{quote:7626f442-09cd-5c4e-8787-a8e800f1a54f}}

ἀπευθής, “without tidings”, is active here, of the man who has heard nothing (Merry on 3.184); Telemachus has used the same word of his father, of whom “the son of Cronus has made even the destruction unknown”, ἀπευθέα (3.88). κείνων, “of those”, Merry takes of the Achaeans who stayed at Troy with Agamemnon and of those who turned back with Odysseus, since Nestor must have known the fate of the men who sailed with him (on 3.185). What he tells next is hearsay, πεύθομαι, “I have heard”, “as is the custom” (3.186–187; see [[themis]]): the Myrmidons came home under “the shining son of great-hearted Achilles” (3.188–189; see [[myrmidons]] and [[neoptolemus]]), Philoctetes, “the splendid son of Poias”, came home safely (3.190; see [[philoctetes]] and [[poias]]), and Idomeneus lost none of his men to the sea (3.191–192; see [[idomeneus]] and [[crete]]). Of the son of Atreus, Telemachus has heard himself “how he came, and how Aegisthus devised a grim destruction for him” (3.193–194; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Nestor names no one who perished at sea.

**The one he cannot tell.** Telemachus asked for one homecoming, his father's (3.83–101), and it is the one Nestor's account leaves out: Odysseus turned back at Tenedos to Agamemnon (3.162–164), and of those men Nestor knows nothing (3.184–185; see [[odysseus]]). So the story of the returns ends, for Telemachus, in the advice to go to the man who came home last. Menelaus sailed with Nestor as far as Sunium and then wandered for years, and Nestor tells that return only when Telemachus asks about Agamemnon (3.276–312; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]); “he has lately come home from abroad”, and Telemachus is to ask him (3.317–328; see [[menelaus]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
