---
summary: Clytemnestra at first refused Aegisthus, “for her mind was good” (3.266); Zeus “devised in his mind a grim homecoming” for the Argives (3.132); Athena put daring in Telemachus' mind (3.76), and tells him that some things he will think of himself “in your own mind” (3.26).
---

**A good mind.** φρεσὶ γὰρ κέχρητʼ ἀγαθῇσι (3.266) is the book's defense of Clytemnestra: she refused the shameful deed at first, while the singer guarded her (3.265–268; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). Merry refers to the “good wits” Athena gave Penelope (2.117), and points above all to this line for the Homeric idea that wickedness and folly are one, her fall long delayed by her good sense (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.266, 3.20; see [[pseudos]] and [[wise]]). The poem's last word on the two wives sets them apart in the same terms: the shade of Agamemnon praises Penelope's good mind (24.194).

**Minds in the book.** Zeus devises the homecoming “in his mind” (3.132); after the assembly the Achaeans spend the night “pondering harsh things in our minds” (3.151); Athena puts daring in the young man's mind (3.76; see [[tharsos]]) and leaves some things to it (3.26; see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]]).
