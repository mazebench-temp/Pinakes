---
summary: Nestor's Achaeans are the army at Troy and on the way home: the sons of the Achaeans who suffered nine years of war, the men heavy with wine whom the sons of Atreus called to the assembly at sunset, and those of whom he knows neither who was saved nor who perished (3.100–116, 3.131–150, 3.185). Telemachus expects the Achaeans to carry Orestes' fame wide (3.203–204), and Nestor is twice their “great glory” and once their “guardian” (3.79, 3.202, 3.411).
---

**The army at Troy.** Nestor answers Telemachus as one of the army: “the misery that we endured / in that land, we sons of the Achaeans, uncontrollable in our might” (3.103–104; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]]). Not even in five years or six could he tell “all the evils the brilliant Achaeans suffered there” (3.115–116; see [[brilliant]]). Telemachus, too, speaks of the land of the Trojans “where you Achaeans suffered woes” (3.100), and Nestor answers in the same words, “where we Achaeans suffered sorrows” (3.220; see [[trojans]]).

**The assembly at sunset.** After the sack “a god scattered the Achaeans” (3.131). The sons of Atreus called “all the Achaeans to assembly, / idly and against all order, at the setting of the sun, / and the sons of the Achaeans came heavy with wine” (3.137–139); the fault, Merry explains, was not the hour itself but the summons at that hour, when the men had come from a banquet and were heated with wine, and he compares the soldiers of Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* in the sacked city (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.138). Menelaus bade them think of their homecoming, Agamemnon to stay and sacrifice, and “the well-greaved Achaeans sprang up / with a wondrous din” (3.141–150; see [[well-greaved]], [[assembly]] and [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). Nestor sailed home “without tidings”, knowing nothing “of those, which of the Achaeans were saved and which perished” (3.184–185; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Fame and home.** Of Orestes Telemachus says that “the Achaeans / will carry his fame wide, even for men to come to hear of” (3.203–204; see [[kleos]] and [[orestes]]). When Nestor wonders whether Odysseus will come home and punish the suitors, “either he alone or all the Achaeans together” (3.217), the Achaeans are the people of Ithaca, as in the first two books (1.90, 1.394; see [[ithaca]]). Ἀχαιικοῦ, “Achaean”, the adjective, is said of Argos (3.251; see [[argos]]). Nestor is μέγα κῦδος Ἀχαιῶν, “great glory of the Achaeans” (3.79, 3.202; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]), and on the stones before his house οὖρος Ἀχαιῶν, “the guardian of the Achaeans”, the warder, as Merry glosses it, from the root of ὁράω, “see” (3.411; Merry on 3.411; see [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]] and [[nestor]]).
