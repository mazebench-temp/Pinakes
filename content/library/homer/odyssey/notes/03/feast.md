summary: The book begins and ends at a sacrificial feast. The strangers come upon the feast of Poseidon on the shore, where they are given their portions and the Pylians feast on a glorious feast (3.31–67), and Athena ends the day with a reminder that it is not fitting to sit long at the feast of the gods (3.335–336); the next morning Nestor's household feasts on the heifer sacrificed to Athena (3.428, 3.470–474). Nestor tells of a third, the funeral feast that Orestes gave the Argives (3.309).
# δαῖτʼ (3.33), δαίτης (3.44) and δαίνυνθʼ (3.471), first met in the Odyssey here (δαῖτʼ and δαίνυνθʼ are in the
# Iliad's Book 9 note); "δαίνυ τάφον" is the funeral feast (3.309), since τάφος alone is also the burial (Il. 24.660).

**The feast of the god.** The Pylians on the shore are “making ready the feast” (δαῖτʼ ἐντυνόμενοι, 3.33) when the strangers come, and Peisistratus seats them “at the feast” (3.37) and tells the elder why she should pray to Poseidon: “for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here” (3.44; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]] and [[xenia]]). The feast is the god's: its meat is the meat of his victims, and when they have roasted the outer flesh and drawn it off, “they divided the portions and feasted on a glorious feast” (3.65–66; see [[spits]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). The two verses return at the feast of Apollo on Ithaca on the day of the bow (*Odyssey* 20.279–280), and ἐρικυδέα δαῖτα, “a glorious feast”, is also the feast after Hector's burial (*Iliad* 24.802) and the feast after Alcinous' sacrifice to Zeus (*Odyssey* 13.26; see [[glorious]]). Nestor, recalling it the next morning, calls it “the rich feast of the god” where Athena came to him in plain sight (3.420; see [[rich-feast]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). At sunset the goddess herself closes it: “it is not fitting / to sit long at the feast of the gods, but to go home” (3.335–336). θεῶν ἐν δαιτί, “at the feast of the gods”, is the feast of the sacrifice, held in the gods' honor, and the day ends with the tongues of the victims burned and the libation poured before the company breaks up for the night (3.332–342; see [[libation]]).

**The feast in Nestor's house.** The second feast is made ready while the sacrifice is still in hand. Nestor bids the sons who stay at home tell the slave women “to prepare a feast throughout the famous house” (3.428; see [[handmaids]]), and when the heifer has been killed and roasted, “they sat and feasted” (δαίνυνθʼ ἑζόμενοι, 3.471), with good men pouring the wine (3.471–472; see [[cupbearing]]). Both feasts end with the same verse, “But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (3.67 = 3.473), and both are followed by Nestor's words, first his questions to the strangers and then his order to yoke the horses for Telemachus (3.68–74, 3.474–476; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).

**The funeral feast.** Orestes, Nestor says, when he had killed Aegisthus, “gave the Argives a funeral feast / for his hateful mother and for the coward Aegisthus” (3.309–310; see [[orestes]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The Iliad has the same words for the funeral feast for Patroclus, when Achilles, back at the ships after killing Hector, “gave them a funeral feast to satisfy the heart” (τάφον μενοεικέα δαίνυ, *Iliad* 23.29); the feast after Hector's burial is held in Priam's house (*Iliad* 24.801–803).
