---
summary: Book 3 opens with the sunrise, the sun springing up from the beautiful water into the brazen sky to give light to the immortals and to mortal men, in words that Zeus speaks to the Sun himself in Book 12. In the rest of the book the sun marks the time of day, from the assembly called at sunset after the sack of Troy to the two sunsets of the drive toward Sparta.
---

**Sunrise.** The book begins as the ship comes to Pylos: “And the sun sprang up, leaving the beautiful water, / into the brazen sky, to give light to the immortals / and to mortal men upon the grain-giving earth” (3.1–3; see [[brazen]], [[sky]], [[immortals]] and [[pylos]]). The sun rises with a purpose, ἵνα … φαείνοι, to shine for gods and men alike, and the same words come in Zeus' answer when the Sun threatens to go down to Hades and shine among the dead: “Helios, shine on among the immortals / and mortal men upon the grain-giving earth” (12.385–386; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The water he leaves, λίμνη, is the sea, which Merry takes here for the farther margin of the stream of Ocean, beyond which the sun sets and beside which he rises; he compares the gates of the Sun beside the streams of Ocean (24.11–12) and Dawn held back at Ocean on the night that Odysseus and Penelope are reunited (23.243–246; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.1; see the translation's note on 3.1). The Iliad's sunrise, too, brings the Sun up out of Ocean into the sky (*Iliad* 7.421–423; see [[oceanus]]). οὐρανὸν ἐς πολύχαλκον, “into the brazen sky”, is an Iliad phrase (*Iliad* 5.504); Merry reports that the older commentators took the epithet as “solid, firm”, comparing the bronze sky and the iron sky (*Iliad* 17.425; *Odyssey* 15.329), and others as “bright-flashing”, and he finds the sky as a vault of burnished metal the most picturesque of the readings (on 3.2).

**Sunset.** Everywhere else in the book the sun is the time of day. The sons of Atreus call the Achaeans to assembly “at the setting of the sun” (3.138), and Merry explains that the fault was not the hour but the men, who had feasted and were heavy with wine (on 3.138; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]] and [[assembly]]). When Nestor has finished his second tale, “the sun went down and darkness came on” (3.329; again 5.225), and Athena ends the feast on the shore, “For already the light has gone down under the darkness” (3.335). The verse of the sunset in Book 2, “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (2.388; see [[sunset-lines]]), closes both days of the drive, at Pherae and on the wheat-bearing plain (3.487, 3.497).
