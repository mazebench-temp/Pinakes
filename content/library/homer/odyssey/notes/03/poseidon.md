summary: Book 3 opens on Poseidon's feast. The Pylians are sacrificing all-black bulls to the dark-haired earth-shaker on the shore when Telemachus lands, and the disguised Athena is bidden to pray to him first. Nestor remembers the thigh-pieces he laid on Poseidon's altar at Geraestus after the crossing from Lesbos, and at sunset the tongues are burned and libations poured to Poseidon and the other immortals.

**The feast of the earth-shaker.** When the ship comes in, the people of Pylos are offering “bulls all black, to the dark-haired earth-shaker”, nine companies of five hundred men with nine bulls before each, and are burning the thigh-pieces “for the god” (3.5–9; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]], [[bulls]], [[earth-shaker]] and [[dark-haired]]). The narrator gives him only his titles. His name comes when Peisistratus bids the older stranger “Pray now, stranger, to lord Poseidon; / for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here” (3.43–44; see [[peisistratus]]). παμμέλας, “all black”, is said in Homer only of these bulls and of the ram that Odysseus is to sacrifice to Teiresias (10.524–525 = 11.32–33). Merry notes that black victims were generally offered to the powers below the earth, or to Earth (*Iliad* 3.103–104), and that the color here may stand for the dark sea or, as Nitzsch suggested, for the terrible power that set Poseidon beside the gods below (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.6). He points also to the god's kinship with the house: Neleus, whose citadel Pylos is (3.4), was Poseidon's son by Tyro (11.235–257; Merry on 3.6; see [[neleus]] and the general article).

**Athena's prayer.** The older stranger receives the golden cup first, and prays:

{{quote:070a61be-1f7b-5436-b376-995293bb1a6b}}

The prayer opens with κλῦθι, “Hear me”, the name, and the title γαιήοχε, “earth-holder” (see [[hear-me]] and [[earth-holder]]), the words with which the Cyclops begins his curse on Odysseus (9.528). μεγήρῃς, “begrudge”, is from μεγαίρω, “think too great”, and so “refuse” (Merry on 3.55, after Buttmann). She asks a gracious return for the hecatomb, the worshiper's claim on the god (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]] and [[hecatomb]]); the eighty-one bulls are not strictly a hecatomb, a word used loosely of any great offering (Merry on 3.58). “So she prayed, and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.62; see [[athena]]). The god she prays to is the one whose anger keeps Odysseus from home (1.19–21, 1.68–79), and she asks him for a safe return for Telemachus and herself.

**At Geraestus.** Nestor tells how his ships, crossing the open sea from Lesbos at a god's sign, put in at Geraestus by night, “and for Poseidon, of bulls, / we laid many thigh-pieces on the altar, having measured the great open sea” (3.177–179; see [[geraestus]], [[thigh-pieces]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Merry notes the temple of Poseidon that Strabo records there (on 3.179; see the general article).

**Libations at sunset.** At sunset Athena, still as Mentor, bids them “cut out the tongues and mix the wine, / so that to Poseidon and to the other immortals / we may pour libations and then think of bed” (3.332–334), and the tongues are thrown into the fire (3.341; see [[libation]]). Merry, with Nitzsch, takes this last offering for Poseidon, in whose honor the whole day had been kept (on 3.332). The next morning Nestor recalls that Athena came to him “at the rich feast of the god” (3.420), and the second sacrifice of the book is hers (see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).
