**A singer.** ἀοιδὸς ἀνήρ, “a singer” (3.267), is not named, and the poem says nothing of his songs. The word is the one the first book used for Phemius in the house of Odysseus (1.325, 1.346; see [[phemius]]); Agamemnon left this one beside his wife, πὰρ δʼ ἄρʼ ἔην, “there was also a singer beside her” (3.267). εἴρυσθαι ἄκοιτιν, “to guard his wife” (3.268), uses the verb of the servant who kept the doors of Odysseus' bedchamber (23.229), as Merry compares it; he sets the charge against the story, which Eustathius reports from one Timolaus, that Phemius went with Penelope to Ithaca as her guardian, since Odysseus left no one to watch Penelope, and Mentor's charge was the house and its goods (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.267; 2.225–227; see [[mentor]] and [[penelope]]).

**The desert island.** “But when the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome, / then he took the singer away to a desert island / and left him there to become the prey and spoil of birds” (3.269–271). The guard has to be removed before the deed: Nestor's order of events makes the singer the last of Clytemnestra's defenses, after her own good mind (3.265–266; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The verse is all but Glaucus' reproach to Hector, who left Sarpedon “to become the prey and spoil of the Argives”, κάλλιπες Ἀργείοισιν ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα γενέσθαι (*Iliad* 17.150–151; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]), with birds for the enemy (see [[birds]]); the Iliad's prey and spoil are men fallen or caught in battle (*Iliad* 5.488), and here it is a living man left to die. Who is meant by μιν, “her”, whom the fate of the gods fettered (3.269), was disputed: Eustathius preferred Clytemnestra, as the English takes it, Merry thinks Aegisthus the simplest, and Nitzsch took it of the singer (see the translation's note on 3.269).
