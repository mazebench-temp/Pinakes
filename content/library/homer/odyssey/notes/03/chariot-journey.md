**Yoking.** Nestor bids his sons yoke fine-maned horses under the chariot, and the housekeeper puts in bread and wine and meats (3.475–480; [[fine-maned]], [[swift-footed]], [[zeus-nurtured]]).

**The drive.** Peisistratus mounts beside Telemachus, takes the reins and whips the horses on in the two verses of Iris' drive with Aphrodite to Olympus, καὶ ἡνία λάζετο χερσί, / μάστιξεν δʼ ἐλάαν, τὼ δʼ οὐκ ἀέκοντε πετέσθην (3.483–484 = *Iliad* 5.365–366), and they leave “the steep citadel of Pylos behind” (3.485; [[steep]]).

**The way and the arrival.** οἱ δὲ πανημέριοι σεῖον ζυγὸν ἀμφὶς ἔχοντες, “And all day long they shook the yoke, holding it on either side”: Merry takes ἀμφὶς ἔχοντες of the horses supporting the yoke at either end, and describes the yoke, its pads and straps, and the pin and ring that joined it to the pole (3.486; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.486). At sunset they come to Pherae and spend the night with Diocles (3.487–490; [[sunset-lines]], [[pherae-in-messenia]], [[diocles]], [[xenia]]).

**The second day.** At dawn the verses begin again: they yoke the horses, mount the inlaid chariot, drive out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade, and the pair fly on, until the swift horses bring them to the wheat-bearing plain at sunset (3.491–497; [[inlaid]], [[loud-resounding]], [[rich-in-wheat]]). The same verses bring the two young men back from Sparta in Book 15 (15.145–146, 15.184–192).
