**The departure.** Athena has asked Nestor to send Telemachus on by chariot with his son, and she ends with the verse of departure, “So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away” (3.371), which comes once more when she leaves Scheria for Marathon and Athens (7.78). Here the next verse adds the bird: φήνῃ εἰδομένη, “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (3.372; see [[athena]] and [[mentor]]). φήνη occurs only here and in one simile of Book 16 (see the entry).

**A likeness that is seen.** εἰδομένη is the participle of the goddess's disguises in the poem: she came to Ithaca “in the likeness of a stranger, the leader of the Taphians, Mentes” (1.105), and went with Telemachus “in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice” (2.268, 2.401). Merry reports Seiler's observation that εἰδόμενος and εἰδομένη are regularly used in Homer of a likeness of body, not of movement or action only; read so, the goddess is seen to change into the bird (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.372; see the translation's note on 3.372 and [[eidos]]). The narrator tells what the company saw: amazement seized them all, and the old man marveled “when he saw it with his eyes” (3.372–373; see [[thambos]]). When she left Telemachus in the first book, her going was told with a comparison, “like a bird she flew upward and away”, and he perceived it in his mind and was amazed (1.319–323; see [[like-a-bird]]). Here the bird is named, and the change is seen by all. In the battle in the hall she will sit on the roof beam like a swallow (22.239–240, a plain rendering), and in the Iliad she and Apollo sit on the oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures” (*Iliad* 7.58–59; see [[like-vultures]]).

**Nestor knows the goddess.** Nestor takes Telemachus by the hand and tells him that he will be no coward, if the gods go with him as escorts: the guest was “the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia, / who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (3.374–379; see [[nestor]], [[tritogeneia]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). He knows her by the way she went. He prays to her at once and vows her a heifer with gilded horns (3.380–384; see [[yearling]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]), and the next morning he says that she came to him “in plain sight” (3.420; see [[divine-epiphany]]).
