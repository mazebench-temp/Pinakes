summary: No god speaks among the gods in Book 3, and the gods as a body are what men speak of. All men have need of them; their mind is not quickly turned; they have spun no prosperity for Telemachus and cannot ward off death from a man they love; they share in men's sacrifices and libations. Nestor knows the departing stranger for no other “of those who have their homes on Olympus” but the daughter of Zeus.
# Unnamed: the gods as a body (3.2, 3.28, 3.47-48, 3.147, 3.159, 3.205, 3.208, 3.221, 3.228, 3.236-237, 3.242,

**Men's need of the gods.** The sun rises “to give light to the immortals / and to mortal men” (3.2–3; see [[helios]] and [[immortals]]). Peisistratus gives the cup to the older stranger, “since I think that to the immortals he as well / prays; and all men have need of the gods” (3.47–48; see [[all-men-need-the-gods]] and [[prayer]]). Merry renders εὔχεσθαι here as “a worshipper”, and reports Nitzsch's comparison with Aratus, “everywhere we all have need of Zeus” (*Phaenomena* 4; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.48). Athena, as Mentor, tells Telemachus that he was not “born and bred against the will of the gods” (3.28).

**What the gods will and cannot do.** In Nestor's tale Agamemnon hoped to heal Athena's anger with hecatombs, “for the mind of the gods who live forever is not quickly turned” (3.147; see [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]] and [[gods-who-live-forever]]). Telemachus wishes that “the gods would clothe me with such strength” to punish the suitors, but “the gods have spun no such prosperity for me” (3.205, 3.208), the verb of the gods who “had spun the thread” of his father's return in Book 1 (ἐπεκλώσαντο, 1.17; see [[moira]]); and when Nestor says that he never saw “the gods show love so openly” as Athena to Odysseus (3.221), Telemachus answers that it will not happen, “not even if the gods so willed” (3.228). Athena's rebuke sets the limit: a god can save a man even from far away, “But death, which is common to all, not even the gods / can ward off from a man they love” (3.231, 3.236–237; see [[a-god-can-save-a-man-from-afar]], [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]] and [[thanatos]]). Telemachus gives up hope: “the immortals have devised for him death and black doom” (3.242; see [[ker-doom]]). In the story of Aegisthus it is “the fate of the gods” that fettered Clytemnestra (3.269; see the translation's note on 3.269).

**Sacrifice and libation.** Men sacrifice to the gods at Tenedos on the way home (3.159), and Aegisthus, when he has won Agamemnon's wife, burns many thigh-pieces “on the holy altars of the gods” and hangs up offerings (3.273–275; see [[aegisthus]]). At Pylos the libations of the evening go “to Poseidon and to the other immortals”, since it is not fitting “to sit long at the feast of the gods” (3.333, 3.336; see [[libation]]), and Nestor, refusing to let his guests sleep on their ship, says “May Zeus ward this off, and the other immortal gods” (3.346). When Athena has gone, he tells Telemachus that he will be no coward “if, young as you are, the gods go with you as your escorts” (3.376), and at dawn he will propitiate Athena “first of all the gods” (3.419).

**On Olympus.** Nestor knows the stranger who has left in the likeness of a sea eagle: “For this was no other of those who have their homes on Olympus, / but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia” (3.377–378; see [[homes-on-olympus]], [[olympus]], [[athena]] and [[tritogeneia]]).
