summary: The book of Nestor at home. He is sacrificing to Poseidon with his sons on the shore of Pylos when the strangers come; when they have eaten he asks who they are, and in two long tales he tells Telemachus all he knows of the war, the breaking up of the fleet, the returns and the murder of Agamemnon, and sends him on to Menelaus. He will not let his guest sleep on the ship, knows Athena when she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle and vows her a heifer, and at dawn, sitting with his six sons on the stones where Neleus sat, leads the sacrifice and sends Telemachus to Sparta in his chariot.

**On the shore.** Athena sends Telemachus straight to him: “But come now, go straight to Nestor, tamer of horses; / let us learn what counsel he keeps hidden in his breast” (3.17–18; see [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[metis]]). The epithet is rare in the Odyssey, three times against forty-five in the Iliad, where it is mostly of the Trojans (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.17). “He will not tell a lie, for he is very wise”, she adds (3.20), and Nestor will say nearly the same two verses of Menelaus when he sends the young man on (3.19–20, 3.327–328; see [[pseudos]] and [[wise]]): for Homer, Merry notes, wisdom and right go together, as folly and wickedness do (on 3.20, 3.52). They find him sitting with his sons among the Pylians at the sacrifice of black bulls to Poseidon, while his companions roast the meat (3.31–33; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]] and [[pylians]]), and the goddess, praying to Poseidon, asks for glory “To Nestor first of all and to his sons” (3.57; see [[kydos]]).

**The questions.** He lets the strangers eat before he asks: “Now it is more fitting to question and to ask / the strangers who they are, now that they have taken their fill of food” (3.69–70; see [[xenia]]). κάλλιον is “more proper” than to have questioned them before they were refreshed (Merry on 3.69). Then he asks, in four verses that Polyphemus will put to Odysseus in his cave (3.71–74 = 9.252–255), whether they sail “on some business” or “at random / over the sea, like pirates” (3.72–73; see [[prexis]] and the translation's table of key words).

**The first tale.** “My friend, since you have reminded me of the misery that we endured / in that land” (3.103–104): the sentence begun with ἐπεί is never finished, Merry notes, “forgotten by the speaker in the crowd of details that come thronging into his mind”, and the real answer to Telemachus comes at 3.120, with his father (on 3.103). Nestor names the dead at Troy, Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus “and there my own dear son, at once strong and noble, / Antilochus” (3.109–112; see [[antilochus]]), tells of evils that five years or six would not be enough to hear (3.113–117), and of the nine years of tricks in which no one could match Odysseus (3.118–122; see [[odysseus]]). The son's words are so like the father's that “awe holds me as I look at you” (3.123–125; see [[sebas]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]), and he remembers that he and Odysseus never spoke at odds in assembly or council (3.126–129). Then he tells how the fleet broke up after the sack: Athena's wrath, the two sons of Atreus quarreling in an assembly at sunset, half the army sailing, and a second strife at Tenedos, where Odysseus turned back to Agamemnon (3.130–164; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). He himself fled, “since I knew that a god was devising evil” (3.166; see [[daimon]]), with Diomedes, and Menelaus overtook them at Lesbos; the god showed them a sign, they cut across the open sea to Euboea and put in at Geraestus by night, and he held on for Pylos with a fair wind that never died (3.167–183; see [[diomedes]], [[lesbos]], [[teras]] and [[geraestus]]).

**What he has heard.** “So I came, dear child, without tidings, and I know nothing / of those, which of the Achaeans were saved and which perished” (3.184–185): ἀπευθής is active here, “without getting tidings”, where at 3.88 it was passive, of the death Zeus has made unknown (Merry on 3.184). The rest he has heard “as I sit in our halls” (3.186): of the Myrmidons, of Philoctetes and Idomeneus, and of Agamemnon and Orestes, whom he holds up to Telemachus: “You too, my friend—for I see that you are very handsome and tall— / be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (3.188–200; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]], [[orestes]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). He asks whether the people hate Telemachus, “following the voice of a god”, where Merry sees no oracle or sign (3.215; Merry on 3.214), and wishes that Athena would love him as she loved Odysseus at Troy, “for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly” (3.218–224; see [[athena]]).

**Judgments and wisdom.** When Telemachus turns to him with a second question, he says why: “of Nestor, since beyond all others he knows judgments and wisdom; / for they say he has been king for three generations of men, / and he appears to me like an immortal to look upon” (3.244–246). δίκας may be “rules of right”, so that Nestor is the man to ask about the dealings of men with one another (Merry on 3.244). ἀνάξασθαι is the only middle of ἀνάσσω in Homer, and Merry suspects the verse; if it is genuine, γένεʼ is the time he has reigned, the tradition of the Iliad's “now he was king among the third” (*Iliad* 1.250–252; Merry on 3.245; see the translation's note on 3.244–245, [[genee]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**The second tale.** “Well then, my child, I will tell you all the truth” (3.254). He tells how Aegisthus, at ease in Argos while the Achaeans toiled at Troy, bewitched Clytemnestra and left on a desert island the singer who guarded her (3.262–275; see [[aegisthus]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]); how he and Menelaus sailed together until Apollo killed Menelaus' helmsman at Sunium (3.276–285; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]], [[sunium]] and [[apollo]]); how a storm off Malea split Menelaus' fleet between Crete and Egypt (3.286–302; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]); and how Orestes came back in the eighth year and killed Aegisthus, and Menelaus came home on the day of the funeral feast (3.303–312). Then he gives his counsel. Telemachus is not to wander long from home while overbearing men devour his possessions, but to go to Menelaus, by ship or by land, with Nestor's sons as escorts, and to beg him to tell the truth, “he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise” (3.313–328; see [[menelaus]], [[lacedaemon]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]), the verse Athena had spoken of Nestor himself (3.20).

**The guest on the deck.** At sunset Athena bids them pour libations to Poseidon and go to rest (3.329–342), and when she and Telemachus rise to go back to the ship, Nestor holds them back:

{{quote:2b75e7be-8aac-5757-ad7d-c56abef871da}}

ἀνείμονος is not “unclothed” but “without changes of raiment”, and πενιχροῦ is explained by the next verse, the man who has nothing to spread on his beds and chairs (Merry on 3.348); the ῥήγεα were costly blankets, laid on chairs and on beds, and the χλαῖνα served as a counterpane (Merry on 3.349; see [[cloak]] and [[bed]]). τοῦδʼ ἀνδρός stands first for Odysseus, present to the speaker's thought (Merry on 3.352). The last verses are the host's creed: as long as he lives, and his sons after him, whoever comes to his house will be entertained (3.353–355; see [[xenia]]). Athena agrees that Telemachus should stay, and asks Nestor to send him on “with a chariot and your son” (3.357–370; see [[peisistratus]]).

**He knows the goddess.** When she has given her last counsel, Athena goes:

{{quote:e6a279b6-cef6-538b-a7a2-75d980352dc5}}

All who see her go are amazed, and the old man, who saw it with his own eyes, knows her for the daughter of Zeus (see [[phene]], [[thambos]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The vow is the one Diomedes makes to the same goddess on the night of the raid (3.382–384 = *Iliad* 10.292–294), and the last verse is that of his prayer in *Iliad* 5 (*Iliad* 5.121; see the translation's table of formulas, [[tritogeneia]], [[divine-epiphany]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). At home he mixes a bowl of wine in its eleventh year, which the housekeeper has opened, and prays to Athena as he pours the libation (3.386–394; see [[wine]] and [[libation]]); ἀνὰ … κέρασσεν is “mixed up”, the wine spread through the water (Merry on 3.390). Telemachus sleeps in the colonnade beside Peisistratus, and Nestor “in the inner part of the high house” beside his wife (3.397–403; see [[eurydice]]).

**The stones of Neleus.** At dawn he goes out and sits on the polished stones before his high doors, where Neleus used to sit, “the Gerenian, the guardian of the Achaeans, / holding the scepter” (3.404–412; see [[neleus]], [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]] and [[scepter]]). οὖρος is “warder”, from the root of ὁράω, “see” (Merry on 3.411). The stones stood, Merry thinks with Gerlach, in the forecourt (πρόδομος) on either side of the door, and the oil that makes them shine is a polish on the stone (on 3.407, 3.408; see [[house]]). His sons come from their chambers and gather around him, Peisistratus the sixth, and they seat Telemachus beside them (3.412–416; see [[echephron]], [[stratius]], [[perseus-son-of-nestor]], [[aretus-son-of-nestor]], [[thrasymedes]] and [[peisistratus]]).

**The sacrifice.** “Quickly, dear children, fulfill my wish, / so that first of all the gods I may propitiate Athena, / who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (3.418–420): ἐναργής is “in visible presence” (Merry on 3.420). One son is to fetch a heifer from the plain, one to bring Telemachus' crew from the ship, one to call the goldsmith Laerces, and the rest are to have the slave women make ready a feast (3.421–429; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]] and [[handmaids]]). “The old horse-driving Nestor” gives the gold for the horns, begins the rite with the water for the hands and the barley grains, and prays, throwing hairs from the victim's head into the fire (3.436–446; see [[horse-driving]], [[barley-grains]] and [[prayer]]); when the heifer is dead he burns the thigh-pieces on split wood and pours sparkling wine over them (3.459–460; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]). Merry reads it as a sacrifice of the household, at which Nestor is priest only as every head of a family is his own priest at home, and notes, after Lobeck, that at the state sacrifice with which the book opens the sacrificer is not named (on 3.444; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). After the bath Telemachus sits down “beside Nestor, shepherd of the people” (3.469; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]), and when they have eaten Nestor bids his sons yoke fine-maned horses to the chariot (3.474–478; see [[horses]]).

**His names.** “The Gerenian horseman Nestor” brings in his speeches and acts nine times (3.68, 3.102, 3.210, 3.253, 3.386, 3.397, 3.405, 3.417, 3.474; see [[gerenian-horseman]]). Telemachus calls him Νέστορ Νηληϊάδη, “Nestor, son of Neleus”, with “great glory of the Achaeans” (3.79, 3.202) and with “tell me the truth” (3.247; see [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]] and [[son-of-neleus]]). He is “tamer of horses” (3.17), “the old horse-driving Nestor” (3.436, 3.444), as the Iliad calls old Peleus (*Iliad* 7.125), “the guardian of the Achaeans” (3.411) and “shepherd of the people” (3.469). The narrator calls him “the old man” (ὁ γεραιός 3.373; ὁ γέρων 3.390, 3.393, 3.459), and Telemachus and the goddess address him so (ὦ γέρον 3.226, 3.331; γέρον φίλε, “dear old man”, 3.357; see [[geron]]). He calls Telemachus “my friend” and “my child” (3.103, 3.184, 3.199, 3.211, 3.254, 3.313, 3.375).
