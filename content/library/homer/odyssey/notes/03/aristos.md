summary: “and there all our best were killed” (3.108): Nestor's dead at Troy, Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus and his own son Antilochus. With Odysseus he planned “how all might turn out best by far” (3.129), and Aegisthus killed “a far better man” (3.250).
grc: [ἄριστοι, ἄριστα, ἀρείω]
---

ἔνθα δʼ ἔπειτα κατέκταθεν ὅσσοι ἄριστοι (3.108) is Nestor's own half-line in the Iliad, where Heracles had come and ill-used Pylos, “and all our best were killed”, eleven of the twelve sons of Neleus (*Iliad* 11.690–693; see [[neleus]]). Here the best are the dead of Troy: Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus, and Antilochus, “at once strong and noble” (3.109–112; see [[ajax]], [[achilles]], [[patroclus]] and [[antilochus]]); Merry notes that Αἴας alone always means the Telamonian (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.109). The counsel of Nestor and Odysseus, ὅπως ὄχʼ ἄριστα γένοιτο (3.129), is the Iliad's phrase for the old man's foresight, “so that things turn out far the best for both sides” (*Iliad* 3.110; see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[metis]]).
