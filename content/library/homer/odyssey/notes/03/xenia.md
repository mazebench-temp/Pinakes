**Questions after the meal.** The strangers are welcomed before anyone asks who they are. When the Pylians see them, “they all came together, / and welcomed them with their hands, and bade them sit down” (3.34–35), and Peisistratus seats them on soft fleeces beside his father and his brother, gives them portions of the entrails and puts the golden cup in their hands (3.36–53; see [[peisistratus]], [[cup]] and [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). Only when they have eaten does Nestor speak: “Now it is more fitting to question and to ask / the strangers who they are, now that they have taken their fill of food” (3.69–70). κάλλιον, “more fitting”, means more proper than it would have been to question them before they had refreshed themselves (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.69). Telemachus kept the same order with the stranger in Book 1, the meal first and the questions after (1.123–124, 1.169–170; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]), and so does Menelaus at Sparta: take food and be glad, and when you have dined we will ask who you are (4.60–62, a plain rendering). In the Iliad the king of Lycia entertained Bellerophon for nine days and sacrificed nine oxen before he asked on the tenth to see the sign he had brought (*Iliad* 6.173–177; see [[bellerophon]]). Nestor's question, when it comes, is whether they travel on some business or roam the sea like pirates (3.71–74; see [[strangers-who-are-you]], [[prexis]] and [[piracy]]).

**The courtesies of the welcome.** The welcome has its order. Peisistratus bids the older stranger pray to lord Poseidon and pour a libation “as is the custom” (ἣ θέμις ἐστί), and then pass the cup to the younger, “since I think that to the immortals he as well / prays” (3.43–48; see [[themis]] and [[libation]]). He gives the cup to the elder first because the other is of his own age (3.49–50), and Athena rejoices in “the wise and just man” for it (3.52–53; see [[wise]]). The guest who comes upon a sacrifice is given a share of it, as the two strangers are given portions of the entrails (3.40): nearly the same verse returns when Telemachus seats the disguised Odysseus in his hall on the feast day of Apollo and sets before him portions of the entrails and wine in a golden cup (20.257–261; see [[feast]]).

Only a poor house, Nestor argues, would send its guest to sleep elsewhere. ἀνείμονος is not “unclothed” but a man without changes of raiment, and πενιχρός, as the next verse explains, is one who lacks the furnishings of beds and chairs (Merry on 3.348; see [[cloak]] and [[blankets]]). The deck of a ship (ἰκριόφιν, 3.353) is where the Phaeacians spread a blanket and linen for Odysseus to sleep on as they carry him home (13.73–75); in Nestor's house the guest is given a corded bed beneath the colonnade, with Nestor's unmarried son beside him (3.397–401; see [[bed]] and [[house]]). The duty passes from father to sons: “and after me my sons are left in the halls / to entertain the guests, whoever comes to my house” (3.354–355). Athena grants that it is fitting for Telemachus to obey (3.357–358).

The same insistence becomes a danger on the way home. Driving back from Sparta, Telemachus asks Peisistratus to set him down at his ship, lest the old man keep him in his house against his will in his eagerness to show him kindness, and Peisistratus agrees, for his father's heart is overbearing and he will not let him go (15.195–214, plain renderings). Menelaus, letting Telemachus go, states the rule that Nestor's eagerness strains: it is as bad to hurry off a guest who would stay as to hold back one who is eager to go, and a host should treat a guest kindly while he is there and send him on when he wishes (15.68–74, a plain rendering). His word for holding back, κατερύκει (15.73), is the narrator's word for Nestor's, κατέρυκε (3.345).

**The escort.** A host sends his guest on his way. Nestor offers Telemachus a chariot and horses and his sons as escorts to Lacedaemon (3.324–326), and Athena, leaving, asks him to send the young man “with a chariot and your son” and to give him his fastest horses (3.369–370). Nestor sees in her departure that the gods themselves go with Telemachus “as your escorts” (3.376; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). The housekeeper stows bread and wine for the journey, “and meats, such as kings whom Zeus has nurtured eat” (3.479–480; see [[handmaids]] and [[provisions]]), and Peisistratus drives (3.481–485; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The two young men are of an age (3.49), and in Book 15 Telemachus calls the bond between them an old one: they claim to be guest-friends through and through from their fathers' friendship, and they are of an age as well (15.196–197, a plain rendering).

**Good cheer at Pherae.** The first night of the drive is spent at Pherae, in the house of Diocles, son of Ortilochus: “There they spent the night, and he set good cheer before them” (3.488–490; see [[diocles]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]). ξείνια are the things a host sets before a guest. The Iliad has the word in Nestor's account of his youth, when Achilles welcomed him and Odysseus at Peleus' house and “set good cheer before us, as is the custom with guests” (*Iliad* 11.779), a verse Merry cites beside the θέμις of Peisistratus' welcome (Merry on 3.45). The verses of the night at Pherae return when the two drive back the same way (15.184–188).
