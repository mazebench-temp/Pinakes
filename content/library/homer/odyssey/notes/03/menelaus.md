summary: Menelaus urged the Achaeans to sail home at once and fell out with his brother (3.141–150); he overtook Nestor at Lesbos and sailed with him until Apollo killed his helmsman at Sunium; a storm off Malea drove him to Crete and Egypt, and he came home with his wealth on the day of Orestes' funeral feast for Aegisthus (3.168–169, 3.276–312). Had he found Aegisthus alive, the murderer would have had no grave (3.255–261). Nestor sends Telemachus to him (3.317–328).

**For sailing at once.** “Then Menelaus bade all the Achaeans / to think of their homecoming over the broad back of the sea” (3.141–142; see [[broad-back-of-the-sea]] and [[nostos]]), and the brothers stood answering each other with harsh words (3.148; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]] and [[agamemnon]]). Yet he came “late, after us two”, to Lesbos, where Nestor and Diomedes were pondering the long voyage: ὀψέ is late after a long interval, and μετὰ νῶι means that he came to join the two of them (3.168–169; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.168; see [[lesbos]]).

**Fair-haired Menelaus, good at the war cry.** ξανθὸς Μενέλαος, “fair-haired Menelaus”, is his name at the verse-end (3.168, 3.257, 3.326; see [[fair-haired]]). When he comes home, on the day of the funeral feast, he is βοὴν ἀγαθὸς Μενέλαος, “Menelaus, good at the war cry” (3.311), his standing epithet, which Merry counts twenty-five times for him and twenty-one for Diomedes, and explains from the loud shout with which a captain marshals or rallies his men rather than from βοή in the scholiast's sense of battle (on 3.311; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]]).

**Too late.** Telemachus asks “Where was Menelaus?” (3.249), and Nestor answers first with what would have been: had the son of Atreus, coming from Troy, found Aegisthus alive in the halls, they would not have heaped the earth over him even in death (3.255–258; see [[aegisthus]]). He was far away. At Sunium he stayed to bury his helmsman, “eager though he was for his journey”; off Malea a storm drove part of his fleet onto the reefs of Crete and five of his ships to Egypt, and there he wandered among men of another tongue, gathering wealth, while Aegisthus ruled his brother's city (3.276–305; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). He came home “bringing many possessions, as much as his ships could carry as their load” (3.312; see [[possessions]]).

**The man to ask.** Nestor ends his story with advice and a direction: let Telemachus not wander long from home, “But to Menelaus I urge and bid you / to go; for he has lately come home from abroad” (3.317–318). If he goes by land, Nestor's chariot, horses and sons will take him “to shining Lacedaemon, where fair-haired Menelaus is” (3.324–326; see [[lacedaemon]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The visit is to be a supplication for the truth, in the verses Athena used of Nestor himself: “And you yourself must beg him to tell you truly; / he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise” (3.327–328, as 3.19–20; see [[nestor]] and [[wise]]).
