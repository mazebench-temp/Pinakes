summary: Nestor lays the grim homecoming on Zeus and on Athena's wrath, “since in no way discerning or just / were they all” (3.132–135); at Tenedos he fled, knowing “that a god was devising evil” (3.166). Clytemnestra yielded when “the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome” (3.269), and Agamemnon perished “by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.235).
refs: [3.132-136, 3.160-161, 3.166, 3.235, 3.265-272]
---

**The gods and the Argives.** Nestor gives both causes in one sentence: Zeus devised a grim homecoming “for the Argives, since in no way discerning or just / were they all”, and many of them perished through Athena's wrath (3.132–135; see [[menis]], [[noos]] and [[themis]]). It is Zeus' account of Book 1 in a man's mouth: mortals suffer by their own recklessness, and the gods are not to be blamed for it (1.32–34). The rest of the tale gives the gods' part without a reason: the mind of the goddess is not quickly turned (3.147; see [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]), Zeus prepares a bane of evil and rouses strife a second time (3.152, 3.160–161; see [[eris]]), and Nestor flees because a δαίμων is devising evil (3.166; see [[daimon]]).

**The wife and the fate.** In Book 1 Zeus blamed Aegisthus alone, who married Agamemnon's wife against fate and killed him on his return (1.35–43). Nestor's tale divides the guilt. Clytemnestra at first refused, “for her mind was good”; when “the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome”, Aegisthus marooned the singer who guarded her, and “willing, he led her, willing, to his own house” (3.265–272; see [[clytemnestra]], [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]] and the translation's note on 3.269). Athena's line names both, and the guile: Agamemnon perished “by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.235; see [[dolos]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).
