---
summary: Telemachus asks Nestor, “how did the son of Atreus die” (3.248); had Menelaus found Aegisthus alive, the loose earth would not have been heaped over him “even in death” (3.258); and Athena says that death, “which is common to all”, not even the gods can ward off from a man they love (3.236–238).
grc: ["ἔθανʼ", θανόντι, θνητοῖσι, καταθνητῶν]
en: [die, mortal]
---

**How did he die.** πῶς ἔθανʼ Ἀτρεΐδης (3.248): Telemachus knows the fact and something of the manner (3.193–198), and Merry takes πῶς as wonder that Agamemnon came to such an end rather than a request for details (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.248; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Nestor answers with the death Aegisthus would have died had Menelaus come home first: no mound even in death, the dogs and birds devouring him on the plain far from the city, and no Achaean woman weeping for him (3.255–261; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[dogs]]).

**Common to all.** Athena's maxim makes θάνατος the bound of the gods' power (3.236–238; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]), and Telemachus answers with “death and black doom” for his father (3.242; see [[ker-doom]]). The book begins with the sun rising to give light “to the immortals / and to mortal men” (3.2–3; see [[immortals]] and [[mortal-men]]), the line between the two that the maxim draws.
