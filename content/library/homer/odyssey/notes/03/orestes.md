summary: Nestor holds Orestes up to Telemachus twice, first as the son who took vengeance on his father's slayer, “how good a thing it is for a son to be left behind” (3.195–200), and then as the avenger who came back from Athens in the eighth year, killed Aegisthus and gave the Argives a funeral feast for his hateful mother and the coward (3.306–310). Telemachus answers that the Achaeans will carry his fame wide (3.202–204).

**The son left behind.** ὡς ἀγαθὸν καὶ παῖδα καταφθιμένοιο λιπέσθαι / ἀνδρός, “how good a thing it is for a son to be left behind by a man / who has perished” (3.196–197; see [[so-good-a-thing-is-a-son]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]): Merry reads ὡς as an exclamation, as the English takes it, better and simpler, he thinks, than to accent it ὥς, takes λιπέσθαι in the sense “remain behind”, as of the son a man leaves over his possessions (*Iliad* 5.154), and quotes Bothe's paraphrase, that Nestor calls happy the man who leaves not only wealth but a son to guard it and avert ruin (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.196). κεῖνος, “that son”, is Orestes, as again in Telemachus' answer (Merry on 3.197). The last three verses repeat Athena's words as Mentes, with “scheming Aegisthus” and the call to be valiant (3.198–200 = 1.300–302; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[telemachus]]); Nestor does not know that the stranger beside him said them first.

**Fame for men to come.** Telemachus takes up the example: “yes, truly that son took vengeance, and the Achaeans / will carry his fame wide, even for men to come to hear of” (3.203–204; see [[kleos]]). καὶ ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι, “even for men to come to hear of”, is Wolf's reading from Eustathius; the manuscripts have καὶ ἐσσομένοισιν ἀοιδήν, “a song even for men to come” (see the translation's note on 3.204). Merry finds analogy enough for the manuscripts' reading and reports Nitzsch's preference for the infinitive; κλέος would then be the praise of the present generation and ἀοιδή that of posterity (on 3.204; see [[song-and-fame]]). Then Telemachus wishes the gods had given him such strength against the suitors (3.205–209).

**Back from Athens.**

{{quote:89aadfe4-3414-500d-9364-57a0fd51ab14}}

τῷ δέ οἱ ὀγδοάτῳ, “in the eighth”, understands ἔτει, “year”, from ἑπτάετες, “for seven years” (3.305), and κακόν, “an evil”, is a predicate: he came as a disaster upon Aegisthus (Merry on 3.306). δῖος Ὀρέστης, “brilliant Orestes”, is his title as in the first book (1.298; see [[brilliant]]). ἀπʼ Ἀθηνάων, “from Athens”: the tragic legend brought him from Phocis, and Aeschylus sends him to Athens only after the deed, Merry notes (on 3.307; see [[athens]] and the translation's note on 3.307). Of Erinyes pursuing him the poem says nothing (Merry on 3.309; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]).
