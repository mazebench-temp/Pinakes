summary: At Pylos Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, leads Telemachus to Nestor and tells him to put away his shame. She receives the golden cup first and prays to Poseidon for what she herself brings to pass, and she rebukes Telemachus' despair. In Nestor's tale it was her wrath that set the sons of Atreus at strife after the sack of Troy, and at Troy she loved Odysseus more openly than Nestor ever saw a god love a man. She leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle; Nestor knows her for the daughter of Zeus and vows her a heifer, and at dawn she comes to the sacrifice.

**Ashore at Pylos.** At sunrise the ship comes in, and when Telemachus steps out, “Athena went before him” (3.12; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[pylos]]). She speaks first, in the likeness of Mentor (see [[mentor]] and [[divine-epiphany]]): “Telemachus, you have no more need of shame, not even a little” (3.14; see [[aidos]]). He has sailed to learn of his father, and must go straight to Nestor and beg him to tell him truly, for “he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise” (3.15–20; see [[nestor]]). Telemachus answers her as Mentor, afraid that he has no practice in speech and that “there is shame for a young man in questioning an older one” (3.22–24; see [[shame-for-a-young-man]]). She answers:

{{quote:d3defb82-8f92-577c-b49f-fa97900526aa}}

The second οὐ belongs closely with θεῶν ἀέκητι, “against the will of the gods”: by litotes, he was born and bred under the favor of heaven (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.27; see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]] and [[daimon]]). “So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way / swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess” (3.29–30), the verses with which she led him to the ship in Book 2 (2.405–406; see [[followed-in-the-footprints]]).

**The golden cup.** Peisistratus, Nestor's son, seats the strangers at the feast and gives the golden cup first to her, since the other is younger, “of an age with me myself”, and “all men have need of the gods” (3.36–50; see [[peisistratus]], [[cup]] and [[all-men-need-the-gods]]). “And Athena rejoiced in the wise and just man, / because he gave the golden goblet to her first” (3.52–53). Merry takes δικαίῳ as “proper”, one who observes the usage common among men, and sees knowledge and propriety joined in πεπνυμένῳ ἀνδρὶ δικαίῳ, as their want is joined in the suitors, “in no way discerning or just” (on 3.52, comparing 2.282 and 3.133; see [[wise]]).

**The prayer to Poseidon.** She prays to the lord of the feast for glory for Nestor and his sons, a gracious return for the Pylians' hecatomb, and a safe return for Telemachus and herself “when we have done the business / for which we came here” (3.55–61; see [[poseidon]] and [[prexis]]). “So she prayed, and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.62). Merry: “As a seeming mortal, she was making a prayer to a higher power; as an actual goddess she was answering it herself” (on 3.62); he reports Classen's reading ἐπεί τʼ for ἔπειτʼ, “as she prayed, so she fulfilled it”. Then she gives Telemachus the fine two-handled cup, and he prays in the same way (3.63–64; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]).

**Daring.** When Nestor asks who they are, Telemachus answers “taking heart; for Athena herself had put daring in his mind, / so that he might ask him about his absent father, / and so that noble fame may be his among men” (3.76–78; see [[tharsos]] and [[kleos]]). The last verse ends her plan in the council of the gods (3.78 = 1.95), and the daring is what she left in his heart when she flew away in Book 1, “might and daring” (1.320–321).

**The wrath of Athena.** In Nestor's tale of the return the gray-eyed goddess is the cause of the Achaeans' grief. Zeus devised a grim homecoming for them, “so many of them met an evil fate / through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father, / who set strife between the two sons of Atreus” (3.134–136; see [[menis]], [[daughter-of-a-mighty-father]], [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Menelaus wanted to sail at once, Agamemnon to stay:

{{quote:c104366d-eb93-58d6-b081-17af025d9ed3}}

The verse of 3.146 is *Iliad* 20.466, where the one who would not be persuaded is Achilles (see the translation's note on 3.146 and [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]). Nestor does not say what angered her. Merry takes the wrath to be aimed first at the Locrian Ajax for his outrage on Cassandra and then at the chiefs who left it unpunished (on 3.135, comparing 1.327; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[cassandra]]); at 4.502 Ajax is “hated by Athena”, though there he dies for his insolence to Poseidon. Phemius sang “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327); Nestor makes Zeus its author and her wrath its means (3.132–135; see [[zeus]]).

**Her love for Odysseus.** Nestor, who thinks that Odysseus may yet come home and punish the suitors (3.216–217), wishes for his son what he saw at Troy: “If only gray-eyed Athena were willing to love you / as she cared then for glorious Odysseus / in the land of the Trojans, where we Achaeans suffered sorrows— / for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly / as Pallas Athena openly stood beside him—” (3.218–222; see [[odysseus]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Merry compares the lesser Ajax's complaint in the footrace of *Iliad* 23, that the goddess “always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (*Iliad* 23.782–783; on 3.222). Nestor says it in the goddess's presence, not knowing her. Telemachus answers that it will never be, “not even if the gods so willed” (3.226–228).

**The rebuke.** Then she speaks, as Mentor: “Telemachus, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (3.230; see [[what-a-word]]).

{{quote:4ebe3965-6757-5b6c-aa9a-28942cd8abf3}}

{{quote:7b795634-63f7-571e-88b6-e2637a4982e9}}

τηλόθεν belongs with the man, not with the god: a god can bring a man home safe even from far away, but he does not help him from a distance, for it is not the way of the Homeric gods to help without being present (Merry on 3.231, after Nitzsch; see [[a-god-can-save-a-man-from-afar]] and [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]). The goddess who says it is present, and Telemachus, who answers “Mentor, let us no longer talk like this” (3.240), does not know it. Her example of a homecoming worse than a late one is Agamemnon's, “by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.234–235; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), the story that Zeus told in the council of the gods (1.35–43).

**At sunset.** When Nestor has finished, she has them cut the tongues and mix the wine for the libations to Poseidon and the other immortals, since “it is not fitting / to sit long at the feast of the gods” (3.331–336; see [[libation]]); “So spoke the daughter of Zeus” (3.337; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]). When Nestor will not let his guest sleep on the ship (3.346–355; see [[xenia]]), she agrees that Telemachus should stay and says that she will go to the ship to encourage the companions, “For I claim to be the only older man among them” (3.362), and at dawn to the great-hearted Caucones, “where a debt is owed me, not a recent one at all / nor a small one” (3.366–368; see [[caucones]]). Merry takes the χρεῖος for property carried off in a raid or wrongfully kept, and the claim as one to be pressed peaceably, since Mentor has too few men for reprisals, as Odysseus as a young man went to Messene for the sheep that men of Messene had carried off from Ithaca (on 3.367, comparing *Iliad* 11.698 and *Odyssey* 21.16–21). She asks Nestor to send Telemachus on with a chariot and his son and his best horses (3.368–370).

**In the likeness of a sea eagle.** Then she goes:

{{quote:d65a1fa4-7274-5c80-8fc8-432e52478363}}

Merry, after Seiler, takes εἰδομένη of bodily likeness, as when she came “in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice” (2.268), so that the goddess is seen to change, and names the bird as the sea eagle or the lammergeier (on 3.372; see the translation's note on 3.372 and [[phene]]). In Book 1, “like a bird she flew upward and away” (1.320), which Merry read as a simile, not a change of shape (on 1.320; see [[like-a-bird]]). Nestor takes Telemachus by the hand: he will be no coward “if, young as you are, the gods go with you as your escorts. / For this was no other of those who have their homes on Olympus, / but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia, / who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (3.376–379; see [[tritogeneia]], [[most-glorious]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). He calls her ἄνασσα, “queen”, and prays for noble fame for himself, his children and his revered wife (3.380–381), and vows her a heifer:

{{quote:dbdb9966-e203-53a3-957d-7effb131a589}}

The three verses are Diomedes' vow to her on the night raid (*Iliad* 10.292–294), and the next, “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (3.385), is the verse that follows Diomedes' prayer to her in the Iliad's fifth book (*Iliad* 5.121; see [[yearling]], [[broad-browed]] and [[unbroken]]).

**The sacrifice.** That night Nestor mixes wine eleven years old and prays earnestly to Athena as he pours the libation, “to the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (3.390–394). At dawn he gives his orders, “so that first of all the gods I may propitiate Athena, / who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (3.419–420); ἐναργής, “in plain sight”, is the word of Hera's “gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (*Iliad* 20.131; Merry on 3.420; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]). When the goldsmith comes with his tools, “Athena came / to share in the sacrifice” (3.435–436), ἱρῶν ἀντιόωσα, as Poseidon went to the Ethiopians “to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams” (ἀντιόων, 1.25). She comes now, Merry notes, not as Mentor but in her own godhead, and unseen (on 3.435). The smith pours the gold around the heifer's horns “so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (3.437–438), and Nestor prays to her again as he begins the rite (3.445–446; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]] and [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).
