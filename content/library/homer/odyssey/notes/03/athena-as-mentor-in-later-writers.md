**The question and the answer.** On the shore Telemachus asks the goddess, “Mentor, how then shall I go? How shall I greet him? / I am not yet practiced at all in shrewd words” (3.22–23; see [[shame-for-a-young-man]]), and she answers with the verses above (see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]]). Plato's Athenian quotes her answer for the young citizens of his *Laws* (*Laws* 7.803e–804a), as the entry above tells. Cicero, waiting for Caesar, who was expected at Formiae, wished he had “that Minerva of Homer's, disguised as Mentor”, to put Telemachus' question to her (*Letters to Atticus* 9.8.2). In a later letter, when Caesar had come on him so suddenly that he had to act unprepared, he gave himself the goddess's answer: “but still, ἄλλα μὲν αὐτός, as he says, ἄλλα δὲ καὶ δαίμων ὑποθήσεται” (*Letters to Atticus* 9.15.4; see [[cicero]]). Proclus quotes the second half, “and in others a god too will give you counsel”, among Homer's many words for the daemon who moves the impulses within us (*On the Republic* II 298 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**The likeness left behind.** Telemachus calls the goddess Mentor once more, when he gives up hope of his father (3.240), and in Nestor's company she speaks as the old man of the crew: “For I claim to be the only older man among them; / the others are younger men who follow out of friendship” (3.362–363). Then she goes: “So she spoke, and gray-eyed Athena went away / in the likeness of a sea eagle; and amazement seized all who saw it” (3.371–372; see [[phene]]), and Nestor knows her for “the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia” (3.378; see [[tritogeneia]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The bird is a likeness of the kind in Proclus' third way, in which a god come down to the last of its orders is likened to a particular creature, as Hermes to the gull and Apollo to the hawk (see the entry above). The disguise is found out at home: in the next book Noemon tells the suitors that he saw Mentor go on board as leader of the crew, “or a god”, and yet saw Mentor in Ithaca at dawn the day before (4.653–656, Greek only).
