**One cup, three names.** Peisistratus “poured wine / into a golden cup, and pledging her he spoke / to Pallas Athena” (3.40–42; see [[peisistratus]] and [[xenia]]). In the verses that follow, the one vessel is a δέπας, “cup” (3.41, 3.46, 3.51), a χρύσειον ἄλεισον, “golden goblet” (3.50, 3.53), and a καλὸν δέπας ἀμφικύπελλον, “the fine two-handled cup”, when the goddess passes it to Telemachus (3.63), as the cup of Hephaestus is a δέπας and a κύπελλον in a few verses of the Iliad. W. W. Merry takes δέπας to have meant first a measure of wine, from the root of δάπτω, δαπάνη and the Latin *daps*, words of sharing out (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.41), and reports three accounts of ἄλεισον: a cup rough with embossed work, or one smooth all over, both from λεῖος, “smooth”, or, as Benfey suggested, a round, bowl-shaped cup (on 3.50). Of ἀμφικύπελλον he sets out Buttmann's “double cup”, like two cups set base to base so that it could stand on either end, against Aristarchus' cup with two handles, which Schliemann upheld from the long goblets with two great handles that he found at Troy (on 3.63; see the entry's Outside Homer).

**The cup in the welcome.** The cup is the guest's first honor. It comes with his share of the entrails, and it goes to the elder first: “So saying, he put the cup of sweet wine in her hand” (3.51), the verse with which Menelaus puts his parting gift, a two-handled cup, into Telemachus' hand (15.120), and Odysseus hands the cup to Arete in farewell (13.57). In Nestor's own memory in the Iliad the golden goblet belongs to a rite at a king's house: when he and Odysseus came to Peleus to gather men for the war, “the old horse-driving Peleus / was burning the fat thigh pieces of an ox to Zeus who delights in thunder / in the enclosure of the courtyard; and he was holding a golden goblet, / pouring sparkling wine over the burning offerings” (*Iliad* 11.772–775). Menelaus will offer Telemachus a fine goblet as a gift, “so that you may pour libations to the immortal gods remembering me all your days” (4.590–592, a plain rendering).

**At evening and after the sacrifice.** At sunset the young men serve the wine, “first pouring drops for the libation into the cups” (3.340; see [[libation]] and [[cupbearing]]), and after the sacrifice to Athena good men stand by to serve, “pouring wine into golden cups” (3.471–472; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[gold-and-silver]]).
