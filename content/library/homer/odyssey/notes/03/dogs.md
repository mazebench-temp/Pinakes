---
summary: Had Menelaus come home from Troy and found Aegisthus alive, Nestor says, no mound would have been heaped over him even in death, but dogs and birds would have devoured him on the plain far from the city (3.256–261).
---

**Food for dogs and birds.** Telemachus has asked where Menelaus was when Agamemnon was killed (3.249–252). Nestor answers with what would have happened if Menelaus had found Aegisthus alive in the halls: “then they would not have heaped up the loose earth over him even in death, / but the dogs and the birds would have devoured him / as he lay on the plain far from the city, and no one / of the Achaean women would have wept for him” (3.258–261; see [[aegisthus]], [[menelaus]] and [[birds]]). Aegisthus would have had no mound heaped over him, his body would have been left to the dogs and birds, and no woman would have lamented him: he would have been denied every honor owed to the dead (see [[honoring-the-dead]], [[tomb]] and [[lament]]). κύνες τε καὶ οἰωνοί, “the dogs and the birds”, is the pair of Achilles' threat to Hector, “but the dogs and the birds will divide you all among them” (*Iliad* 22.354), and of Hermes' comfort to Priam, “the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds” (*Iliad* 24.411). In the Odyssey Eumaeus fears the same fate for his master, whose skin the dogs and swift birds may already have torn from his bones (14.133–134, a plain rendering). ἑκὰς ἄστεος, “far from the city”, is Mycenae. Merry reports a reading Ἄργεος, “far from Argos”, which he finds hard to fit to the place: it cannot be the city of Argos, where Diomedes was king (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.260; see [[mycenae]]).

**What happened.** The punishment did not happen. Menelaus came home only on the day when Orestes, having killed Aegisthus, gave the Argives a funeral feast “for his hateful mother and for the coward Aegisthus” (3.309–311; see [[orestes]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The man whom the dogs would have eaten had his funeral feast, and it was the singer whom he took to a desert island who was left “to become the prey and spoil of birds” (3.270–271; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]).
