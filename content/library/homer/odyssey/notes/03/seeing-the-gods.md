summary: All through the feast at Pylos Athena sits among men as an older stranger, and no one knows her. Nestor speaks of her open love for Odysseus at Troy in her presence; he knows her only when she leaves in the likeness of a sea eagle, and the next morning he says that she came to him “in plain sight”. When she comes to the sacrifice, no one sees her.

**A stranger among the Pylians.** To the Pylians Athena is a stranger, the elder of two guests, to whom Peisistratus gives the golden cup first because the other is younger (3.43–50); to Telemachus she is Mentor (3.22, 3.240; see [[mentor]] and [[athena]]). Only the narrator says more: as she prays to Poseidon, “she herself was bringing it all to pass” (3.62).

**Love shown openly.** Nestor has seen the gods' favor in its effects: “never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly / as Pallas Athena openly stood beside him” (3.221–222), ἀναφανδά, “openly”, twice in two verses. He says it of Odysseus at Troy, in the presence of the goddess herself, and does not know her (3.218–224).

**Known by her going.** Recognition comes at the departure. She goes “in the likeness of a sea eagle; and amazement seized all who saw it”, and the old man “marveled, when he saw it with his eyes” (3.372–373; see [[phene]] and [[thambos]]). Then he knows her: “For this was no other of those who have their homes on Olympus, / but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia” (3.377–378; see [[tritogeneia]]). So the lesser Ajax knows Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, from behind “as he went away” (*Iliad* 13.71–72; see the general article). In Book 1 Telemachus, when she flew off like a bird, “was amazed in his heart; for he thought that it was a god”, and kept his knowledge to himself (1.323, 1.420); here the whole company sees, and Nestor says aloud who it was.

**In plain sight.** At dawn Nestor gives his orders for a sacrifice to Athena, “who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (3.420). ἐναργής is the word of Hera's “gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (*Iliad* 20.131; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.420; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]). At the feast he had seen a man; what was plain to him was her going.

**Unseen at the sacrifice.** When the goldsmith comes, “Athena came / to share in the sacrifice” (3.435–436), and no one in the scene is said to see her. Merry: “Not now in the person of Mentor, but in her godhead, yet invisible” (on 3.435). The gold is poured around the heifer's horns “so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (3.438; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).
