**The vow.** When Athena has gone, Nestor prays to her and promises her a victim:

{{quote:dbdb9966-e203-53a3-957d-7effb131a589}}

The three verses are Diomedes' vow on the night of the raid (*Iliad* 10.292–294), and they promise the same kind of beast, a young cow that has never worked (see [[yearling]], [[broad-browed]] and [[unbroken]]). βοῦς is ox or cow alike; the feminine adjectives and pronouns make this one a cow, and the translation calls her “heifer”. The Pylians gave Athena a cow in Nestor's youth as well: by the Alpheus they offered a bull to Poseidon, “and to gray-eyed Athena a cow from the herd” (*Iliad* 11.729), as in this book the bulls go to Poseidon on the shore and the heifer to Athena at the house (see [[bulls]] and [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]).

**From the plain.** At dawn Nestor gives his orders: “let one go to the plain for a heifer, so that she may come / as soon as may be, and the herdsman of the cattle drive her here” (3.421–422; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]). The herds are kept out on the plain, not at the house. Merry, quoting Lehrs on a kind of compound that Aristarchus had noticed, takes ἐπιβουκόλος as the herdsman who is over the cattle, not, as Nitzsch would have it, a man set over the herdsmen, and compares the fullness of βοῶν ἐπιβουκόλος with αἰπόλος αἰγῶν, “goatherd of the goats”, Melanthius' title (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.422, citing 17.247). In the later books the phrase is the formula of Philoetius, Odysseus' cowherd (20.235, 21.199, 22.268, 22.285; see the translation's formula row). “The heifer came / from the plain” (3.430–431), and the goldsmith poured gold around her horns, “so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (3.437–438).

**The killing.** Stratius and Echephron lead her by the horns, and Thrasymedes stands by with a sharp axe “to strike the heifer down” (3.439–443; see [[stratius]], [[echephron]], [[thrasymedes]] and [[axe]]). The blow cuts through the sinews of the neck “and loosed the strength of the heifer” (3.449–450). Merry points out that the blow only stuns her: she is lifted and her throat cut by Peisistratus, and the black blood flows and the spirit leaves her bones, only at 3.453–455 (on 3.450; see [[peisistratus]]). The women raise their cry at the blow (see [[ololyge]]). The sons lift her head from the ground to bare the throat (3.453); Merry adds that the head is drawn back so that the victim faces the gods above, while a victim for the powers below is turned toward Erebus (on 3.453, comparing 10.527–528; see the translation's note on 3.453). Then she is jointed, and her thighs are cut out and burned for the goddess in the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse (3.456–462; *Iliad* 1.460–465; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]).
