**The sign at Lesbos.** After the second quarrel at Tenedos, Nestor fled with his ships, “since I knew that a god was devising evil” (3.166), and Diomedes fled with him. Menelaus overtook them at Lesbos “as we pondered the long voyage”: whether to go home above rocky Chios, by the island of Psyria, or below Chios, past windy Mimas (3.168–172; see [[lesbos]], [[chios]], [[psyria]] and [[mimas]]). Then they asked the god for a sign:

{{quote:39992830-8ea6-52dd-9e78-34532c545635}}

Nestor does not name the god or say what the sign was. He tells only what it bade them do: the god “showed” it (δεῖξε) and so “bade” them (ἠνώγει, 3.174), and the sign is told as a command (see [[teras]]). It sent them the bolder way. The long voyage, Merry explains, is the passage straight across the Aegean, shorter than the way round by the islands but a longer run without touching at any port, and he reports Nitzsch's remark that in seamanship the Achaeans were behind the Phoenicians, the Taphians and the Cretans (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.169). The sign proved good. A shrill fair wind rose, the ships ran over the fish-filled ways, and they put in at Geraestus by night and laid thighs of bulls on Poseidon's altar (3.176–179; see [[geraestus]] and [[bulls]]).

**The voice of a god.** Later Nestor asks Telemachus whether he submits to the suitors willingly, “or do the people / throughout the land hate you, following the voice of a god?” (3.214–215; see [[telemachus]]). Eustathius and Nitzsch, as Merry reports, took θεοῦ ὀμφή for an oracle or an omen that sanctioned the setting aside of a king. Merry denies it: such a practice is scarcely attested, and the suitors' wish to ask the oracles of Zeus before they kill Telemachus shows only wickedness leaning on superstition (16.400–405). The voice of a god, he argues, is an impulse in men that they cannot trace to its source, as a rumor “from Zeus” is a report that no man has started (1.282–283), and he compares Agamemnon waking from his dream, when “the divine voice was poured around him” (Merry on 3.214, citing *Iliad* 2.41; see [[dream]]). Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, asks his son the same question in the same verse (16.95–96).
