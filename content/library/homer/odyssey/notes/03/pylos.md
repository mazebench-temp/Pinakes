---
summary: Telemachus and Athena reach Pylos, “the well-built citadel of Neleus”, at sunrise (3.4), and he leaves “the steep citadel of Pylos” by chariot for Sparta (3.485); Nestor came home there from Troy with a fair wind (3.182–183). Merry finds in the drive to Sparta the best argument that the poet's Pylos is the Messenian one.
---

**The arrival.** The ship from Ithaca comes in at sunrise: “and they came to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus” (3.4; see [[well-built]] and [[neleus]]). The citadel is called by the name of Nestor's father, who once sat on the polished stones before the house where Nestor sits now (3.406–411; see [[nestor]]). The people are on the shore, sacrificing black bulls to Poseidon (3.5–8; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]] and [[pylians]]). The book does not call Pylos “sandy”, its epithet in the first two books (1.93, 2.214, 2.326, 2.359; see [[sandy]]); here it is “well-built” and, as Telemachus leaves it, “steep”: “and left the steep citadel of Pylos behind” (3.485; see [[steep]]).

**Nestor's homecoming.** Nestor's own return was the quickest of all: “but I held on for Pylos, and the fair wind never / died down, once the god had first sent it forth to blow” (3.182–183; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). ἔχον, “held on”, is the verb of a voyage in progress, as σχεῖν is of its end (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.182). Πύλονδε, “to Pylos”, is the form of Telemachus' “whether I go to Pylos” (2.317).

**Which Pylos.** The book is the chief evidence in the old dispute over the site, which Strabo argued from its verses (see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]). Nestor has to send a man to bring Telemachus' companions from the ship (3.423–424), and Strabo took this for proof that the city lay inland, in Triphylia; the ship lies some way from the town again in Book 15 (15.199–201). Merry, who sums up the case on the verse of the arrival, decides for the Messenian Pylos on the promontory of Coryphasium, at the north of the bay of Navarino, which suits “sandy”; his strongest argument is the drive to Sparta, two days without a change of horses and a night at Pherae, since the Messenian Pylos, Pherae and Sparta lie nearly in a line, about thirty miles apart (3.484–497, 4.1; Merry on 3.4; see [[pherae-in-messenia]], [[lacedaemon]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). See also the translation's note on 3.4.
