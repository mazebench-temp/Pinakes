---
summary: Nestor tells the murder twice. First, in brief, he recalls how Agamemnon came home and Aegisthus devised his destruction, and holds up Orestes' vengeance to Telemachus (3.193–200); then, asked how it happened, he tells how Aegisthus sat at ease in Argos while the Achaeans toiled at Troy, bewitched Clytemnestra and removed the singer who guarded her, and, while Menelaus wandered, killed the son of Atreus and ruled Mycenae seven years, until Orestes came back from Athens (3.248–310). Athena, as Mentor, would rather come home after many sorrows than die at her own hearth as Agamemnon died (3.232–235).

**In brief.** “Of the son of Atreus you yourselves have heard, far off though you are, / how he came, and how Aegisthus devised a grim destruction for him” (3.193–194; see [[agamemnon]] and [[aegisthus]]). The double τε, Merry says, marks a rapid succession, almost the same moment for the two acts (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.194). Nestor goes straight on to the vengeance and the example, in verses that Athena, as Mentes, used in the first book: “scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father” (3.198 = 1.300), and the call to Telemachus to “be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (3.199–200 = 1.301–302; see [[orestes]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Athena's comparison.** “And I for my part would rather, even after toiling through many sorrows, / come home and see the day of my homecoming, / than come home and perish at my own hearth, as Agamemnon / perished by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (3.232–235; see [[athena]], [[mentor]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). Merry puts the weight on ἄλγεα πολλὰ μογήσας, “after toiling through many sorrows”: better to bear one's share of misery on the way than to come home at once and be murdered (on 3.232). In ὑπʼ Αἰγίσθοιο δόλῳ καὶ ἧς ἀλόχοιο the preposition governs both genitives, and δόλῳ, “by the guile”, is added as a dative of circumstance (Merry on 3.235; see [[dolos]]): the wife is an agent with the lover, as she will be in the shade's own account (11.409–410, 11.429–430; see [[clytemnestra]]).

**The questions.** Telemachus asks Nestor: “how did the son of Atreus die, wide-ruling Agamemnon? / Where was Menelaus? What destruction did he devise for him, / scheming Aegisthus, since he killed a far better man?” (3.248–250; see [[wide-ruling]] and [[menelaus]]). He knows the fact, Merry says, and πῶς ἔθανε expresses wonder that such a man came to such an end, rather than a request for details (on 3.248). Was Menelaus away from Achaean Argos, “so that the other took heart and killed him?” (3.251–252; see [[argos]]).

μέγα ἔργον, “very great was the deed” (3.261), is a great deed in the bad sense, audacious, impious, and γάρ gives the reason: because the Achaeans were held at Troy, Aegisthus was left to himself (Merry on 3.261, 3.262). μυχῷ Ἄργεος ἱπποβότοιο, “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos” (3.263), is in Merry's view the kingdom of Agamemnon, and the corner Mycenae, which lies, as E. Curtius described it, at the innermost corner of the plain of Inachus where the rocks close in (on 3.263; see [[mycenae]] and [[horse-pasturing]]). θέλγεσκʼ, “kept bewitching”, is the iterative of θέλγω, the verb of enchantment. ἔργον ἀεικές, “the shameful deed” that Clytemnestra at first refused (3.265), is the phrase with which Agamemnon's shade will name the murder she devised for her wedded husband (11.429–430; see the translation's key-word row for ἀεικής). Her good mind was one defense, and the singer whom Agamemnon left beside her the other, until the fate of the gods fettered her (3.266–272; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). Then Aegisthus burned thigh-pieces and hung up offerings for a success he had never expected (3.273–275; see [[aegisthus]]).

**Meanwhile.** While Menelaus wandered, “Aegisthus devised these grim things at home, / killing the son of Atreus, and the people were made subject to him; / and for seven years he ruled over Mycenae rich in gold” (3.303–305; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[rich-in-gold]]). Murray prints 3.305 before 3.304, as does Merry's text, following Bergk and La Roche and the scholiast on Sophocles' *Electra* 267, who quotes the two verses in that order; the rows here keep the order of the numbers (see the translation's note on 3.303–305). In the eighth year Orestes came back and killed him (3.306–310; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]).

**Who struck.** Every verse of the book that names the killer names Aegisthus (3.194, 3.250, 3.304, 3.308). Clytemnestra's part ends when she goes, willing, to his house (3.272), and she is named again only as Orestes' “hateful mother” (3.310); the guilt that Athena gives her (3.235) is not told as a deed. In Aeschylus she strikes the blow herself (see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]]).
