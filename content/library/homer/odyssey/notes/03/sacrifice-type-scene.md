summary: Book 3 has the fullest sacrifice in Homer. Nestor's sacrifice to Athena runs through the pattern of the sacrifice at Chryse, in many of the same verses, and adds what the Iliad leaves out: the gilding of the horns, the water in a flowered basin and the barley in a basket, the hairs thrown into the fire, the blow of the axe, the women's cry and the bowl for the blood (3.418–463). The sacrifice to Poseidon on the shore is shown only at its end, and Nestor's tale has sacrifices told in a verse (3.9, 3.159, 3.178–179, 3.273).

**In brief.** The sacrifice to Poseidon on the shore is caught near its end: the ship from Ithaca comes in “When they had tasted the entrails and were burning the thigh-pieces on the altar for the god” (3.9; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). In Nestor's tale the pattern shrinks to a verse. At Tenedos “we offered sacrifice to the gods” (3.159), and at Geraestus “for Poseidon, of bulls, / we laid many thigh-pieces on the altar” (3.178–179); Agamemnon wished to hold back the army “and offer holy hecatombs” to heal Athena's anger (3.144; see [[hecatomb]]); and Aegisthus, when he had led Clytemnestra to his house, “burned many thigh-pieces on the holy altars of the gods” (3.273; see [[aegisthus]] and [[altar]]).

**In full.** Nestor's sacrifice to Athena takes the steps of the pattern one by one (see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).

1. **Preparation.** Instead of victims set in order around the altar (*Iliad* 1.447–448), a single heifer is fetched from the plain, gilded and led in by the horns (3.421–422, 3.430–439; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]] and [[cattle]]). The water and the barley are brought by one of Nestor's sons, the water for the hands in a flowered basin and the barley grains in a basket (3.440–441). Where at Chryse “they washed their hands and took up the barley grains” (*Iliad* 1.449), here Nestor “began the rite with the water for the hands and the barley grains” (3.445; see [[hand-washing]] and [[barley-grains]]). κατάρχεσθαι is a word of ritual, “to perform the preliminary rites”, and οὐλοχύτας is used before the fact: the grain is not yet scattered, only lifted from the basket (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.445).
2. **Prayer.** Nestor “prayed as he made the first offering, throwing hairs from the victim's head into the fire” (3.445–446). Agamemnon makes the same first offering of hair at the oath of *Iliad* 19, “cutting the first hairs from the boar” (*Iliad* 19.254; Merry on 3.445). The prayer is not given in words: Nestor made his vow the evening before (3.380–384).
3. **Killing.** “And when they had prayed and scattered the barley grains” (3.447 = *Iliad* 1.458), the killing that the Iliad tells in one verse (*Iliad* 1.459) is told in its parts: Thrasymedes' axe cuts the sinews of the neck, the women raise the cry, Nestor's sons lift the heifer's head from the ground, and Peisistratus cuts her throat, while Perseus holds the bowl for the blood (3.442–455; see [[axe]], [[ololyge]] and [[bowl-for-the-blood]]). No flaying is told.
4. **The god's portion.** “quickly they jointed her, and at once cut out the thigh-pieces / all in good order, and wrapped them in fat, / making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them” (3.456–458, the last verse as *Iliad* 1.461). διέχευαν, “jointed”, Merry takes as “broke up” or “dismembered”, the cutting into small pieces being μίστυλλον (3.462); and Nitzsch observed that Homer generally burns the μηρία or μῆρα but cuts out the μηροί, so that μηρός is the whole thigh and μηρία the pieces cut from it, wrapped in a layer of fat below and one above to burn and give the savor (Merry on 3.456; see [[thigh-pieces]]). “The old man burned them on split wood, and over them poured / sparkling wine; and young men beside him held five-pronged forks in their hands” (3.459–460 = *Iliad* 1.462–463): the old man is now Nestor, not the priest Chryses (see [[five-pronged-forks]] and [[wine]]).
5. **The men's portion.** “But when the thigh-pieces were burned up and they had tasted the entrails, / they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits” (3.461–462, as *Iliad* 1.464–465), “and roasted it, holding the sharp-pointed spits in their hands” (3.463), a verse found only here, where the Iliad has “and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off” (*Iliad* 1.466; see [[spits]]).
6. **The meal.** The outer flesh is drawn off and the household feasts (3.470–473; see [[feast-type-scene]]). While it roasts, the guest is bathed (3.464–469; see [[bathing]]).
