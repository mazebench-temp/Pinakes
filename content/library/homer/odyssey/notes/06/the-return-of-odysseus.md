summary: The return passes through the Phaeacians. Athena goes to their city “devising a homecoming” for Odysseus; he tells Nausicaa that he came ashore yesterday, on the twentieth day from Ogygia, as Zeus had decreed; she promises him escort and a homecoming from her father and tells him whose knees to clasp; and the goddess who hears his prayer at the grove does not yet show herself, for Poseidon rages against him “until he reached his own land” (6.331).

**Devising a homecoming.** The book begins with Athena's errand: she goes to the house of Alcinous, νόστον Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μητιόωσα, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.14; see [[athena]] and [[nostos]]). The dream she sends Nausicaa speaks only of the girl's washing and her marriage, but its end is the stranger's: Athena wakes him so that he may see the girl, “who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.112–114; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).

**The twentieth day.** Zeus decreed that Odysseus should reach Scheria on a raft “on the twentieth day” (5.34; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and Odysseus tells Nausicaa that it has been so: “Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped from the wine-dark sea; / and all that time the wave and the swift storm winds were carrying me / from the island of Ogygia. And now some god has cast me ashore here” (6.170–172; see [[ogygia]] and [[daimon]]). χθιζός has the force of the adverb “yesterday”, and τόφρα, “all that time”, is the whole space of twenty days (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.170, 6.171). κάββαλε is “cast me ashore”: not a safe landing, the scholium remarks, but a casting out by terrible winds (Merry on 6.172). He does not expect his troubles to end yet: “for I do not think / it will cease, but the gods will yet accomplish much before that” (6.173–174), πάροιθεν being “ere that”, before the respite comes, not “hereafter” (Merry on 6.174; see the translation's note on 6.174). Of the earlier wreck, which took his last ship and brought him to Ogygia, he says nothing to her; he tells it first to Arete (7.244–255; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).

**Escort and homecoming.** Zeus promised that the Phaeacians would send Odysseus home in a ship (5.36–37; see [[phaeacians]]), and Nausicaa's directions are the first step to it: “Stranger, listen to my word quickly, so that as soon as may be / you may win escort and a homecoming from my father” (6.289–290; see [[alcinous]]). The way runs through her mother: he is to throw his arms around her knees, “so that you may see the day of your homecoming / rejoicing, and quickly, even if you are from very far away” (6.311–312; see [[day-of-homecoming]] and [[supplication]]), and “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your well-built house and your native land” (6.313–315; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[well-built]]). Athena repeats the promise in the next book, with “high-roofed house” for “well-built house” (7.75–77); here the three verses are wanting in several manuscripts and found only in the margin of the Harleian, and modern editors generally reject them as adding nothing new (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.313–315).

**The prayer and the anger.** At the grove Odysseus prays to Athena: “listen to me now at least, since before you never listened / to me when I was being wrecked, when the famous shaker of the earth wrecked me. / Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied” (6.325–327; see [[prayer]] and [[hear-me]]). He did not see her in the storm, though the poem has shown her binding the other winds, and putting it in his mind to cling to the rock and to swim along the coast (5.382–387, 5.427, 5.437). She hears him, but “she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother; and he raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (6.329–331; see [[poseidon]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The last verse is the proem's (6.331 = 1.21), and it sets the same limit to the anger: Poseidon's rage will end only in Ithaca. There Athena throws off her reserve, Merry notes, as soon as Odysseus is beyond Poseidon's power (on 6.330), and she gives him the reason herself: she always knew that he would come home, having lost all his companions, “but I would not fight with Poseidon, / my father's brother, who laid up anger against you in his heart, / angry because you blinded his dear son” (13.339–343, a plain rendering). Odysseus, for his part, tells her that after Troy he never saw her again until in the land of the Phaeacians she encouraged him with words and led him into the city herself (13.316–323).
