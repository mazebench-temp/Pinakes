summary: The book is one goddess's plan carried out through mortals. Athena sends a dream, and the girl goes to the river; Athena thinks that Odysseus should wake, and the girl's throw goes wide; Athena puts daring in her, and she stands her ground; Athena pours grace on Odysseus, and the girl wishes for such a husband. The characters see only the outcome, and give it to the gods at large: “some god” cast Odysseus ashore, Zeus gives men their lot, and the stranger has not come “against the will of all the gods” (6.240).

**Through mortal hands.** Athena does not show herself to anyone awake in this book, and most of what she does is done through the girl. She comes to Nausicaa in a dream, in the likeness of a friend, and sends her to wash the clothes at the river where Odysseus is lying (6.20–40; see [[athena]] and [[dream]]). She “thought of another thing, / that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl” (6.112–113), and it is the princess who throws the ball wide into the eddy, so that the girls shout and he wakes (6.115–117; see [[thought-of-another-thing]] and [[ball]]). When he comes out of the bushes and the handmaids run, Nausicaa stands her ground, “for Athena / put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs” (6.139–140; see [[tharsos]]). The goddess's purpose and the girl's acts are one event told twice, as the general article describes: the girl is sent, and she goes with a wish of her own, the marriage of which she is ashamed to speak to her father (6.66–67; see [[aidos]] and [[marriage]]). Odysseus' own choices are left to him. He decides by himself to beg her from a distance rather than clasp her knees (6.141–148; see [[supplication]] and [[kerdos]]), and only after his bath does the goddess change him, making him taller and pouring grace on him (6.229–235; see [[charis]]).

**What the characters see.** Neither Odysseus nor Nausicaa knows what Athena has done. Odysseus gives his landing to an unnamed god, “And now some god has cast me ashore here, / so that, I suppose, I may yet suffer evil here too” (6.172–173; see [[daimon]]): κάββαλε is a casting ashore by violent winds, not a safe landing, as the scholiast notes (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.172). Nausicaa gives his misery to Zeus, who deals out prosperity to men as he wishes (6.188–190; see [[zeus]] and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]), and when she sees the change in him she reads it rightly but without a name: “Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus / does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians” (6.240–241; see [[olympians]]). Odysseus, for his part, complains to Athena that she never listened to him while he was being wrecked (6.325–326), not knowing that she had saved him on the rocks of Scheria (5.427, 5.437).
