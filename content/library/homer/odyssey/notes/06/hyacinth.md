# ὑακινθίνῳ (6.231 = 23.158), the adjective, found only in this verse; the entry's ὑάκινθον is the flower of Il. 14.348.
grc: [ὑακινθίνῳ]
---

**Like the hyacinth flower.** κὰδ δὲ κάρητος / οὔλας ἧκε κόμας, ὑακινθίνῳ ἄνθει ὁμοίας, “and down from his head / she made the thick hair fall, like the hyacinth flower” (6.230–231; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]], [[hair]] and [[thick-and-woolly]]). οὖλαι κόμαι, W. W. Merry explains, are the thick hair of a vigorous man, against the bare head of the old beggar from which, Eurymachus mocks, the torchlight seems to shine (18.354–355); the same οὖλος is the epithet of cloaks and rugs, “woolly” (4.50; *Iliad* 10.134, 16.224), and Curtius referred it to the root of ἔριον and of English “wool” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.231; see the translation's table of key words).

**Which flower, and why.** Authorities were divided, Merry reports, between a variety of the gladiolus, the hyacinth as we know it, and, with Voss and Nitzsch, the blue iris. He argues from the Iliad: there ὑάκινθος, with the crocus and the lotus, makes a carpet for Zeus and Hera on the top of Gargarus, an unlikely soil for the iris but one that might well be covered with the common hyacinth or bluebell; and its epithets there, πυκνὸν καὶ μαλακόν, “thick and soft”, suggest that the point of likeness is the clustering flowers with their curling petals, not the color (on 6.231; *Iliad* 14.347–349). The Greeks seem to have thought the hyacinth dark, as Merry shows from Theocritus (*Idylls* 10.28), and Eustathius and Hesychius took the likeness of the color: black hair like the dark flower. But Odysseus' hair is ξανθαί, “auburn”, when Athena withers it to disguise him (13.399, 13.431), though his beard is dark when she restores him for Telemachus (16.175–176). Merry thinks the two not irreconcilable, the beard being darker than the hair, and quotes Houben's view that the words go with οὔλας κόμας as a whole: they picture the thickness and fullness of the hair, long, falling and curling, soft rather than bristling; and Eustathius himself had offered this alternative, that the likeness is not to the blackness but to the curl (on 6.231; see the translation's note on 6.231).

**At home.** The verses come again when the housekeeper has bathed Odysseus in his own house, after the killing of the suitors: Athena pours beauty down over his head, makes him taller and stouter, and sends down his thick hair like the hyacinth flower, before he sits down opposite Penelope (23.156–158; see [[penelope]]). In the Iliad the hyacinth is a flower of the ground on Ida; in the Odyssey it is only a likeness, twice, for the same hair.
