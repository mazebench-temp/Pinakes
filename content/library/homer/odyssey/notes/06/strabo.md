---
summary: Strabo turns the simile of the craftsman who pours gold around silver into a figure for Homer's way of adding myth to truth, quotes Nausicaa's verses on her people, who live far apart in the sea, the farthest of men, as proof that the poet imagined the Phaeacians in the Atlantic, and records that Corcyra was once called Scheria.
---

- **Gold around silver.** Homer, referring his myths to education, took great care for the truth and added myth to it, “as when a man pours gold around silver”, adorning the Trojan War and the wandering of Odysseus (*Geography* 1.2.9, quoting 6.232): [[strabo-gold-around-silver]] and [[gold-poured-around-silver]].
- **The farthest of men.** Against Polybius, who reduced the wanderings to days and distances, Strabo sets the verses that place them in the Ocean, among them Nausicaa's “And we live far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men, and no other mortal mingles with us”; all these, he says, plainly describe scenes imagined in the Atlantic (*Geography* 1.2.18, quoting 6.204–205): [[phaeacians]] and [[scheria]].
- **Scheria.** Corcyra was “formerly called Scheria”, settled by Chersicrates when Archias sailed to found Syracuse (*Geography* 6.2.4, on 6.8); and Apollodorus blamed Callimachus for calling Corcyra Scheria (*Geography* 7.3.6): [[scheria]].
