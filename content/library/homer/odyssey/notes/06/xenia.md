summary: Odysseus wakes and asks whether the people of the land are “arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (6.119–121). Nausicaa answers for her people: he shall not lack clothing, “nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant” (6.192–193), for strangers and beggars are from Zeus (6.207–208); her handmaids give him clothes, oil, food and drink (6.209–250), and she sends him to her father's house to win escort and a homecoming (6.255–257, 6.289–290).
# φιλόξεινοι "kind to strangers" (6.121 = 9.176 = 13.202; 8.576), the verses of the question at every landfall.
grc: [φιλόξεινοι]
en: [kind to strangers]
passages:
  - 6.186-210 | Nausicaa receives the stranger
---

**The question of every landfall.** Woken by the girls' cry, Odysseus asks what he asks wherever he comes ashore: “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time? / Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (6.119–121). The three verses come again when he wakes on the shore of Ithaca and does not know it (13.200–202), and the last two when he goes to see what men the Cyclopes are (9.175–176); Alcinous will ask him which of the peoples he met were of the one kind and which of the other (8.575–576; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). The opposition is the poem's measure of a people. To be φιλόξεινος, “kind to strangers”, is to have a mind that fears the gods: Merry takes νόος θεουδής as an explanation of φιλόξεινοι, men who fear the wrath of Zeus, god of guests (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.121, comparing *Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[hybris]], [[themis]] and [[noos]]).

**The answer.** Nausicaa answers the question for her people without having heard it. He looks neither base nor witless; Zeus deals out his lot, and he must bear it; “now, since you have come to our city and our land, / you shall not lack clothing, nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (6.187–193; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[supplication]]). Merry supplies “not to lack” with ὧν ἐπέοιχʼ, “which it is right that a woebegone suppliant should not lack”, comparing the good cheer Achilles set before his guests, “as is the custom with guests” (*Iliad* 11.779; Merry on 6.193); Eumaeus will make the same promise to the beggar in the same verses (14.510–511). Then, before she has asked him anything, she tells him the name of the people and her own (6.194–197; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). His name is not asked until Arete asks it in Book 7, “Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (7.237–238, a plain rendering), and he does not give it until Book 9 (9.19).

**From Zeus.** The handmaids have fled, and she calls them back: no enemy comes to the Phaeacians, but this is an unhappy wanderer, “whom we must look after now; for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear” (6.199–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]). κομέειν, “look after”, is the care of a host (see the translation's table of key words). The orders follow: “give the stranger food and drink, / and bathe him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind” (6.209–210).

**The welcome in its parts.** The welcome is given in its usual parts, ordered by the place. The handmaids set a mantle and a tunic beside him and oil in the golden flask (6.214–215; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]); he bathes alone, out of shame before the girls (6.218–227; see [[bathing]] and [[aidos]]), and when Athena has made him beautiful the handmaids set food and drink beside him, and he eats and drinks greedily, “for he had long tasted no food” (6.246–250; see [[fasting]]). The clothes will tell their own story: Arete knows the mantle and the tunic, which she made herself with her handmaids, and asks who gave them to him (7.233–238).

**A people that sees few strangers.** The Phaeacians live far apart in the sea, “the farthest of men, and no other mortal mingles with us” (6.204–205; see [[phaeacians]]), the word ἔσχατοι that the poem uses of the Ethiopians (1.23; Merry on 6.205). Their king gives strangers escort, and no one who comes to his house waits long, grieving, for it (8.31–33); Nausicaa sends him to her father, “so that as soon as may be / you may win escort and a homecoming from my father” (6.289–290; see [[nostos]]). Yet in her fear of the town the stranger becomes something else, a husband she has found from elsewhere: “Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall, / a stranger?” (6.276–277; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). Alcinous will state the Phaeacian rule at his own table: to a man who has even a little understanding, the stranger and the suppliant are in the place of a brother (8.546–547, a plain rendering).
