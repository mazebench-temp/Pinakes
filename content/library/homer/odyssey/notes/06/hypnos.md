# κοιμᾶτʼ (6.16).
grc: ["κοιμᾶτʼ"]
---

Two sleepers frame the night. Odysseus lies in his bed of leaves, ὕπνῳ καὶ καμάτῳ ἀρημένος, “worn out by sleep and weariness” (6.1–2; see [[much-enduring]]), the verse that comes again when he sleeps in Alcinous' palace (7.344). ἀρημένος is a word of uncertain origin; unless ὕπνῳ can mean “sleepiness”, Merry takes ὕπνῳ καὶ καμάτῳ as a syllepsis, or as one idea in two words, “oppressed with the sleep that weariness brings” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.2; see the translation's note on 6.2). Nausicaa sleeps in the intricately worked chamber with two handmaids by the doors, who slept, Merry thinks, on mattresses near enough to the door that it could not be opened without waking them (6.15–19; Merry on 6.18; see [[handmaids]] and [[house]]). Athena comes like a breath of wind to the girl's bed and stands above her head (6.20–21; see [[dream-scene]] and [[dream]]), and Dawn wakes her (6.48–49). Odysseus wakes only at the girls' cry (6.117).
