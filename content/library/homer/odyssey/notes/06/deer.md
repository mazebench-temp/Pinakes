**Artemis' deer.** In the simile of Nausicaa among her handmaids, Artemis goes over Taygetus or Erymanthus τερπομένη κάπροισι καὶ ὠκείῃς ἐλάφοισι, “delighting in boars and swift deer” (6.104; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]], [[artemis]] and [[boar]]). ὠκεῖα, “swift”, is the deer's quality as in the Iliad, where the hind is ταχεῖα (*Iliad* 8.248, 11.113). The deer is the goddess' proper game: Hera, taunting her in the battle of the gods, tells her that it is better “to slay wild beasts on the mountains / and wild deer” than to fight with those stronger (*Iliad* 21.485–486), and Artemis taught the hunter Scamandrius to shoot all the wild things of the mountain forest (*Iliad* 5.51–52; see [[scamandrius]]).

**The lion's deer.** A few verses later Odysseus comes out of the bushes like a mountain lion that goes after the cattle or the sheep, ἠὲ μετʼ ἀγροτέρας ἐλάφους, “or after the wild deer” (6.132–133; see [[the-hungry-lion]] and [[lion]]). ἀγροτέρας ἐλάφους are the same words as Hera's (*Iliad* 21.486). The change of case is exact, W. W. Merry notes: the lion goes among the cattle (βουσί, dative) but after the deer (μετʼ ἐλάφους, accusative), pursuing the flying deer, while his ravages in the farmyard keep within a narrower circle (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.132). In Book 4 the deer was the hind of Menelaus' simile, who laid her fawns in a lion's lair (4.335–339; see the Book 4 note); here the deer is the quarry of the goddess and of the lion in two similes a few verses apart, the one for the princess, the other for the stranger who comes to her.
