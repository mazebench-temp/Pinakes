summary: The king who has ruled the Phaeacians since Nausithous died, “who knew counsels from the gods”, and Nausicaa's “Papa dear”. Met at the door on his way to the council, he understands what she does not say and has the wagon made ready (6.12, 6.50–71); she promises Odysseus an escort from him, and describes his house, unlike all others, and his chair beside her mother's, where he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal (6.255–310).

**The king.** The founder is dead, “and Alcinous was ruling then, who knew counsels from the gods” (6.12; see [[nausithous]] and [[who-knew-counsels]]): μήδεα are counsels and plans (see the translation's key words), and his come from the gods. It is to his house that Athena goes, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.13–14; see [[athena]]). His daughter names him to the stranger with his people: “and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, / on whom the strength and the force of the Phaeacians depend” (6.196–197; see [[great-hearted]]). ἐκ … ἔχεται is “depends on”: W. W. Merry compares the words of old Echeneus, that deed and word among the Phaeacians depend on Alcinous (11.346), and, without the preposition, Nestor's to Agamemnon, “whatever he begins will rest with you” (*Iliad* 9.102; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.197).

**At the door.** Nausicaa finds her parents within: her mother at the hearth, her father going out “to join the famous kings / at the council, to which the noble Phaeacians had called him” (6.54–55; see [[boule]] and [[kings]]). ξύμβλητο, “met”: she hastened down the hall and caught him at the door (Merry on 6.54). Her “Papa dear” comes with a reason fit for a king, “And for you yourself it is fitting, when you are among the foremost, / to take counsel with clean clothes on your body” (6.57–61; see [[nausicaa]]). She does not name her marriage, “But he understood everything, and answered her” (6.67), and he grants more than she asked: “I do not grudge you the mules, my child, nor anything else. / Go; the slaves will make ready the wagon for you, / high, with good wheels, fitted with a frame above” (6.68–70; see [[wagon]] and [[mules]]). ἔρχευ is “away then!” (Merry on 6.69); on the frame above, see the translation's note on 6.70. He gives the order to the slaves himself (6.71).

**What his daughter promises.** In the house of her “wise-hearted father” the stranger will come to know the best of the Phaeacians (6.256–257; see [[wise-hearted]]), and from him he may win “escort and a homecoming” (6.290; see [[nostos]]). By Athena's grove near the road lie “my father's domain and his flourishing orchard, / as far from the city as a man can make himself heard with a shout” (6.293–294); τέμενος is “the royal park” (Merry on 6.293; see the translation's note on 6.293 and [[the-grove-of-athena]]).

**His house and his chair.** “It is easy to know, and even a child could lead you, / a little one; for not at all like it are they built, / the houses of the Phaeacians, like the house of Alcinous / the hero” (6.300–303; see [[house]] and [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). The clause οἷος δόμος Ἀλκινόοιο is an awkward explanation of “like it” (Merry on 6.301), and ἥρωος, “the hero”, is generally read as a dactyl, though Ahrens would make it a spondee (Merry on 6.303; see the translation's note on 6.301–303). At the upper end of the hall, beside the queen's chair against the pillar, “my father's chair leans against hers, / on which he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal” (6.308–309; see [[throne]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]): Merry compares the gods seated at their wine beside Zeus, while Hebe pours the nectar (*Iliad* 4.1–4; Merry on 6.309). His chair stands somewhat nearer the door than his wife's (Merry on 6.308), and the suppliant is to pass it: “Pass him by, and throw your arms around the knees / of my mother” (6.310–311; see [[supplication]]). In the next book it is he who raises the suppliant from the hearth and seats him in the chair of his son Laodamas (7.167–171).
