---
summary: When Athena has made the stranger beautiful, Nausicaa says to her handmaids, “Would that such a man might be called my husband, / living here, and that it might please him to stay here” (6.244–245). In the gossip she fears, the town says of him, “He will be her own husband, surely”, and that it is better so, if she has found herself a husband from elsewhere (6.277, 6.282).
---

**The wish.** αἲ γὰρ ἐμοὶ τοιόσδε πόσις κεκλημένος εἴη (6.244): “Would that such an one might be called my husband, and that it might please him to bide here!” is Merry's rendering, and κεκλημένος εἴη is the periphrasis that Homer makes only with the perfect passive participle, as in Odysseus' threat to Thersites, “may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus” (*Iliad* 2.260), and Hera's “I am called your wife” (*Iliad* 4.60–61; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.244). Ameis, as Merry reports, took the emphatic οἱ of the next verse as the turn from the general wish to the man before her. The verses had a history of censure. Aristarchus athetized both, hesitating over the first, since Alcman had imitated it, bringing in maidens who say “Father Zeus, would that he were my husband” (the scholia, in Merry's critical note); Plutarch, in his essay on how the young should listen to poetry, blamed the girl's boldness and want of restraint (as Merry reports him), and Ephorus, as a scholium reports, praised the words as coming from a soul well made for virtue, while the scholiast saw in them the luxury of the Phaeacians. Merry sides with Ephorus: the words are the thoughts of an innocent girl to whom marriage was a certainty and not a mere possibility, spoken to no hearers but her own handmaids, for Odysseus is sitting apart (on 6.244; see the translation's note on 6.244–245, [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[aidos]]).

**The town's word.** The husband comes back in the speech of the baser man she imagines: “Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall, / a stranger? Where did she find him? He will be her own husband, surely” (6.276–277), and, with bitter sarcasm, “It is better so, if she has gone about and found herself a husband / from elsewhere”, since none of the many noble Phaeacians who woo her is good enough (6.282–284; Merry on 6.282; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]] and [[marriage]]). The wish she made in private is what the town would say in public.

**The other πόσις.** In the formula βρῶσίν τε πόσιν τε, “food and drink” (6.209, 6.246, 6.248), πόσις is the noun of πίνω, “drink”, not the husband; the entry excepts the three lines.
