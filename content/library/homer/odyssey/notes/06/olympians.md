**At home.** When Athena goes back to Olympus, the poem shows the gods' life there in a verse: “in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days” (6.46; see [[olympus]] and [[blessed-gods]]). μάκαρες, “blessed”, is the gods' condition, and the verse is the whole picture of their ease, as Calypso called them “you gods who live at ease” (5.122; see [[who-live-at-ease]]).

**In men's words.** The rest of the book speaks of them from below. Nausithous “made temples of the gods” when he founded the city (6.10; see [[nausithous]] and [[temple]]), and Alcinous rules “who knew counsels from the gods” (6.12; see [[alcinous]]). Odysseus asks the girl whether she is “one of the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.150; see [[wide-sky]] and [[divine-epiphany]]), expects that “the gods will yet accomplish much” evil for him before his troubles end (6.173–174; see the translation's note on 6.174), and wishes her a husband, a home and oneness of mind: “And may the gods give you all that you have in your mind” (6.180; see [[husbands-and-wives]]). Nausicaa answers for her people that no enemy comes to them, “for we are very dear to the immortals” (6.203; see [[phaeacians]] and [[immortals]]), and when she sees the stranger bathed and anointed, she tells her handmaids: “Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus / does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians”, for “now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.240–243). The townsmen she imagines would say the same in mockery, that some god has come down from the sky at her prayer to take her for his wife (6.280–281; see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]).
