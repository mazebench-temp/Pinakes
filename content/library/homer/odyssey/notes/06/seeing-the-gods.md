summary: No one in this book sees a god awake. Athena comes to Nausicaa only in a dream, in the likeness of a friend; at the end she hears Odysseus' prayer but does not yet appear to him face to face. In their place mortals are taken for gods: Odysseus asks whether Nausicaa is a goddess and likens her to Artemis, and Nausicaa sees in the bathed stranger a man “like the gods who hold the wide sky”, and imagines the town saying that some god has come down to her from the sky.

**In a friend's likeness.** Athena comes to Nausicaa as a dream, “in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships, / who was of an age with her, and dear to her heart” (6.22–23), and the girl, waking, marvels at the dream (6.49; see [[dream]] and [[athena]]). Nothing in the poem shows that she knew who had come. The verse that introduces the speech, “In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her” (6.24), is the Iliad's for gods who speak in the shape of men and women (*Iliad* 2.22, 3.389, 16.720; see the general article).

**Mortals taken for gods.** The rest of the book turns the theme about. Odysseus, seeing the girl who alone stood her ground, cannot tell whether she is a goddess: “I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (6.149), and if a goddess, he likens her most nearly to Artemis (6.150–152; see [[artemis]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). In the simile of the game the poet had given her Artemis' stature among her nymphs, where the goddess “is easy to know, though all are beautiful” (6.108), the word of Ajax's “gods are easy to know” (*Iliad* 13.72). Nausicaa in turn sees the bathed and anointed Odysseus as Athena has made him: “before he seemed to me unsightly, / but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243; see [[wide-sky]]), and she imagines the townsmen's gossip, that “some god has come to her at her prayer, much prayed for, / down from the sky” (6.280–281). Odysseus had wondered whether the cry that woke him was the cry of nymphs (6.122–124; see [[nymphs]]).

**Not yet face to face.** At sunset Odysseus prays to Athena in her grove, and “Pallas Athena heard him. / But she did not yet appear to him face to face” (6.328–329). He had told her that she never listened to him while he was being wrecked (6.325–326); she had in fact helped him unseen, in the winds and in his mind (5.382–387, 5.427, 5.437). Her reserve is for Poseidon's sake (6.329–331; see [[poseidon]]). In the next book she pours a mist about him, lest any Phaeacian meeting him should taunt him and ask who he was, and meets him in disguise, as a young girl carrying a pitcher (7.14–20); not until he is on Ithaca does she show herself to him as a goddess, and he tells her then that he had never seen her since Troy until she led him into the city of the Phaeacians (13.287–323).
