summary: In Book 6 the sea is behind Odysseus: it washes the pebbles of the shore where the clothes are spread to dry (6.94–95); its brine disfigures him until he washes it off in the river (6.137, 6.219–226); he tells Nausicaa how he escaped from the wine-dark sea on the twentieth day from Ogygia (6.170–172); and Nausicaa tells how her people live far apart in the sea of many waves and cross it in the ships they glory in (6.204–205, 6.270–272).

**The shore of the washing.** When the clothes are clean, the girls spread them “in a row along the shore of the sea, just where / the sea, beating on the land, used to wash the pebbles clean” (6.94–95; see [[shore]] and [[washing-troughs]]). The English follows Nitzsch, who joins ποτὶ χέρσον, “against the land”, with θάλασσα; a variant ἀποπτύεσκε would make the sea cast up the line of shingle instead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.95; see the translation's note on 6.95). The place would be almost a fixed point of the beach, Merry adds, for the tide in the Mediterranean rises and falls very little, and the poet gives that to his Phaeacian coast (on 6.95).

**The brine.** The sea is still on Odysseus' body when he comes out of the bushes: “And terrible he appeared to them, afflicted by the brine” (6.137; see [[the-hungry-lion]]). κεκακωμένος ἅλμῃ, “afflicted by the brine”, has the verb of Eurycleia's plea for the old Laertes, “do not afflict an afflicted old man” (4.754; see the translation's table of key words). He asks the handmaids to stand off, “so that I myself / may wash the brine from my shoulders” (6.218–219), and in the river he washes from his skin “the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders, / and from his head he wiped the scurf of the barren sea” (6.225–226; see [[bathing]], [[rivers]] and [[barren-sea]]). χνόος, “scurf”, the salt crust left by the sea, is found only here in Homer; Merry connects it with κνάω, “scrape”, and κόνις, “dust” (on 6.226). In the storm Odysseus spat out “the bitter brine, that ran streaming in plenty from his head” (5.322–323), and at the end of the poem Penelope's joy in her husband is likened to the joy of shipwrecked swimmers who reach the land with “much brine caked on their skin” (πολλὴ δὲ περὶ χροῒ τέτροφεν ἅλμη, 23.237, a plain rendering; see [[the-fathers-recovery]]).

**The wine-dark sea.** “Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped from the wine-dark sea; / and all that time the wave and the swift storm winds were carrying me / from the island of Ogygia” (6.170–172; see [[wine-dark]], [[ogygia]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The twenty days are the ones Zeus foretold, “on the twentieth day he may reach deep-soiled Scheria” (5.34): seventeen days of sailing, the sight of the land on the eighteenth, and then the storm and two nights and two days in the sea (5.278–280, 5.388–390).

**The Phaeacians' sea.** Nausicaa tells her handmaids that no enemy will ever come to their land, “And we live far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men, and no other mortal mingles with us” (6.204–205; see [[of-many-waves]] and [[phaeacians]]). ἐνὶ πόντῳ does not show that Scheria is an island, Merry notes, only that the Phaeacian land lay far across the sea (on 6.204; see the translation's note on 6.204). The sea is their craft: they care nothing for bow or quiver, “but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships, / in which they glory as they cross the gray sea” (6.271–272; see [[ships]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[gray-sea]]); ἀγαλλόμενοι, “glorying”, is said of the ships themselves that cross the sea “glorying in the wind of Zeus” (5.176).

**On the shore.** Washed and made beautiful, Odysseus “went apart along the shore of the sea and sat down, / shining with beauty and grace” (6.236–237; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). The man whom the sea had made terrible to look at sits beside it transformed.
