summary: Poseidon takes no part in the action of the book, but he is in its city and in its last verses. His precinct stands in the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, a people descended from him; Odysseus names him in his prayer as “the famous shaker of the earth” who wrecked him; and his anger, which lasts until Odysseus reaches his own land, is the reason Athena does not yet appear to Odysseus face to face.
# Ποσιδήιον, "the precinct of Poseidon" (6.266), is the adjective of his name, as Ποσιδήϊον, the grove of Poseidon at
# Onchestus (Il. 2.506), which the Iliad's Book 2 note claims within the Iliad. In the Odyssey it occurs only here.

**His precinct.** Nausicaa describes the city that Odysseus must enter alone: “And there is their place of assembly, around the fine precinct of Poseidon, / fitted with hauled stones set deep in the earth” (6.266–267; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]], [[assembly]] and [[scheria]]). Ποσιδήιον is the adjective of his name, “Poseidon's”, as in the Catalogue's “splendid grove of Poseidon” at Onchestus (Ποσιδήϊον ἀγλαὸν ἄλσος, *Iliad* 2.506; see [[onchestus]]); here it stands for his precinct (see the translation's key-word row). The god of the sea fits a people who “care nothing for bow or quiver, / but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships” (6.270–271; see [[phaeacians]]), and in the next book they prove to be his own kin: Nausithous, who led them to Scheria, was the son of Poseidon and Periboea, and Alcinous is the son of Nausithous (7.56–63; see [[nausithous]] and [[alcinous]]). Odysseus has come, without knowing it, among the descendants of the god who hates him. At the end of Book 8 Alcinous recalls his father's saying that Poseidon was jealous of the Phaeacians because they gave safe escort to all men, and would one day shatter one of their ships on its way home from an escort (8.564–571), with the verb of Odysseus' prayer in this book, ῥαίω, “wreck”.

**The shaker of the earth.** In Athena's grove Odysseus prays to her: “listen to me now at least, since before you never listened / to me when I was being wrecked, when the famous shaker of the earth wrecked me” (6.325–326; see [[athena]], [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[famous]]). On the raft he had given the storm to Zeus (5.303–305); on the rocks of Scheria he knew the god, “for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (5.423), and now he names him again with the same title. The verb, repeated in ῥαιομένου, ὅτε μʼ ἔρραιε (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.326), is the one he used to Calypso before he set out, “And if again some god wrecks me on the wine-dark sea” (5.221): the god has a name now.

**Until he reached his own land.** The book ends with him. Athena hears the prayer, “But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother; and he raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (6.329–331). The last two verses are those of the proem, where all the gods pitied Odysseus “except Poseidon; and he raged relentlessly / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (1.20–21; see [[cholos]]), with ἐπιζαφελῶς, “furiously”, for ἀσπερχές, “relentlessly”: it is Phoenix's word in the Iliad for an anger that is not appeased, of Agamemnon if he were still “always furiously angry” (*Iliad* 9.516; see the translation's key-word row). His brother's daughter will not oppose him openly; on Ithaca she tells Odysseus that she would not fight Poseidon, her father's brother, who was angry with him for the blinding of his son (13.341–343), and Merry notes that she throws off her reserve as soon as Odysseus is beyond Poseidon's power (on 6.330; see the translation's note on 6.329–331).
