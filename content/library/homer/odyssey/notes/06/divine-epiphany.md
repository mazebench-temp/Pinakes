summary: The book has an epiphany in a dream and the forms of an epiphany without a god. Athena comes to the sleeping Nausicaa like a breath of wind in a friend's likeness, speaks and goes back to Olympus, and the girl marvels at the dream. Odysseus addresses the girl as one addresses a goddess, “are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (6.149); and at the end Athena hears his prayer but does not appear.
# Unnamed: Athena's coming, likeness and departure (6.20-24, 6.41, 6.47); "she marveled at the dream" (6.49); "are you
# some goddess, or a mortal?" (6.149-153); "she did not yet appear to him face to face" (6.329).

**In a dream.** The pattern is complete in Athena's visit, though the girl is asleep: the goddess comes “like a breath of wind”, stands above her head in the likeness of a friend, speaks her message and goes away to Olympus (6.20–47; see [[athena]], [[like-a-breath-of-wind]], [[dream-scene]] and [[olympus]]). The recognition is missing: the girl wakes and “marveled at the dream” (6.49; see [[dream]]), but does not know that a god has come.

**A mortal addressed as a goddess.** Odysseus' first words to Nausicaa take the form of a mortal's address to a god he has met: “I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal? / If you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky, / to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, / I liken you most nearly, in looks and stature and build” (6.149–152; see [[artemis]] and [[supplication]]). ἄνασσα, “queen”, is used elsewhere in Homer only of goddesses, of Athena in Nestor's prayer and of Demeter, so that it pays a girl a special compliment (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.149; 3.380; *Iliad* 14.326). The *Hymn to Aphrodite* has the same scene the other way about: Anchises, seeing the goddess disguised as a maiden, greets her “Hail, queen”, asks which of the blessed ones she is, Artemis or Leto or Aphrodite or Themis or Athena, or one of the Graces or the nymphs, and promises her an altar; she answers that she is no goddess but a mortal woman (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 92–110). Odysseus speaks to a mortal girl as if she might be a goddess; Anchises to a goddess who claims to be a mortal girl. The scholia record two readings of the question: as Ptolemy of Ascalon took it, “are you some goddess, or a mortal?”, or as others, “whether a god or a mortal” (see the translation's note on 6.149).

**The epiphany withheld.** The book ends with the parts of the pattern that do not come. Odysseus prays to Athena in her grove, and she hears him, “But she did not yet appear to him face to face” (6.328–329), out of respect for Poseidon (see [[poseidon]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The epiphany is put off to Ithaca, where she comes to him first as a young shepherd, and then, in the likeness of a woman tall and fair and skilled in glorious works, tells him that he did not know Pallas Athena, who always stands beside him (13.221–225, 13.287–301).
