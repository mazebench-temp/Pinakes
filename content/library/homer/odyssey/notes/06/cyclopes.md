# 6.6: the relative clause, "who used to plunder them".
refs: [6.6]
---

The Cyclopes come into the poem's story of the Phaeacians as the reason for their move: “near the Cyclopes, overweening men, / who used to plunder them, and were the stronger in strength” (6.5–6; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). ὑπερηνορεόντων, “overweening”, is the Odyssey's usual word for the suitors, and the Iliad gives it only to the Trojans and to Deiphobus (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.5; *Iliad* 4.176, 13.258; see [[overweening]]). βίηφι δέ, “and … in strength”, gives the reason why they could oppress their Phaeacian neighbors (Merry on 6.6).

The book does not say whether these are the Cyclopes of Odysseus' later tale, the lawless herdsmen of the caves, among whom he blinded Polyphemus (9.106–115; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Alcinous names them with the Giants as peoples near to the gods, as the Phaeacians are (7.205–206), and Aristotle took the old enmity of 6.4–8 to explain why Odysseus could tell the Phaeacians, Poseidon's descendants, that he had blinded Poseidon's son (*Homeric Problems* fr. 396 Gigon; see [[aristotle]]).
