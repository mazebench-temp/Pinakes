summary: Athena makes Odysseus taller and pours grace on him “as when a man pours gold around silver”: “so she poured grace upon his head and shoulders” (6.229–235), and he sits on the shore “shining with beauty and grace” (6.237). The handmaids have their beauty from the Graces (6.18), and Dymas' daughter is “dear to her heart” (6.23).
# χάρισι (6.237), χαρίεντα "lovely" (6.234 = 23.161; 8.167, 10.223), κεχάριστο "dear" (6.23).
grc: [χάρισι, χαρίεντα, κεχάριστο]
en: [grace, dear to her heart]
---

**Poured grace.** After the bath Athena makes the stranger “taller to look upon and stouter”, makes the thick hair fall from his head like the hyacinth flower, and pours grace on him as a skilled man, taught every craft by Hephaestus and Pallas Athena, pours gold around silver and “accomplishes lovely works” (χαρίεντα δὲ ἔργα, 6.229–234; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[athena]]): ὣς ἄρα τῷ κατέχευε χάριν κεφαλῇ τε καὶ ὤμοις, “so she poured grace upon his head and shoulders” (6.235). χάρις here is the charm of a person, the beauty that draws the eye, and the goddess gives it as she gave it to Telemachus going to the assembly, θεσπεσίην δʼ ἄρα τῷ γε χάριν κατέχευεν Ἀθήνη, “And Athena poured a wondrous grace upon him” (2.12). She will pour it on Odysseus again the next morning among the Phaeacians (8.18–20), and after his bath at home, in the same verses as here (23.156–163).

**Beauty and grace.** “Then he went apart along the shore of the sea and sat down, / shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (κάλλεϊ καὶ χάρισι στίλβων, 6.236–237). What the girl sees changes her words: before, he seemed unsightly, “but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky”, and she wishes that such a man might be called her husband (6.242–245; see [[eidos]] and [[posis]]).

**The Graces and their gift.** The two handmaids who sleep by Nausicaa's door “had their beauty from the Graces” (Χαρίτων ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσαι, 6.18; see [[graces]]), and the friend whose likeness Athena takes is one who “was of an age with her, and dear to her heart” (κεχάριστο δὲ θυμῷ, 6.23), the perfect of χαρίζομαι, as κεχαρισμένος of the suitor who comes “pleasing” to Penelope's father (2.54).
