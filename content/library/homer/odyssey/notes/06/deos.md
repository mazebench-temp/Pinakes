---
summary: Athena “took the fear from her limbs”, and Nausicaa alone stands her ground (6.140); Odysseus tells her that he marvels at her and is “terribly afraid / to clasp your knees” (6.168–169).
---

Fear runs both ways on the shore. The handmaids flee from a man made terrible by the brine, but Athena puts daring in Nausicaa's mind and takes the fear from her limbs, ἐκ δέος εἵλετο γυίων (6.137–140; see [[tharsos]]); she calls them back with a question, “Surely you do not think that he is one of the hostile men?” (6.200), ἦ μή που, “you don't mean that you think …?” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.200). The naked man is afraid too. The narrator gives his reason for keeping his distance, “lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (6.147; see [[supplication]]); in his speech he gives another, wonder: “as I marvel at you, lady, and am astounded, and I am terribly afraid / to clasp your knees” (ὡς σέ, γύναι, ἄγαμαί τε τέθηπά τε, δείδια δʼ αἰνῶς / γούνων ἅψασθαι, 6.168–169; see [[sebas]] and [[gounata]]). The fear is the awe due to a girl who may be a goddess, and it serves the suppliant's tact: it turns the gesture he will not make into a compliment.
