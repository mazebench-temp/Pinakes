summary: Odysseus blesses Nausicaa with a husband and a home and oneness of mind, “for there is nothing stronger or better than this, / than when, of one mind in their thoughts, they keep house together, / a man and a woman” (6.180–185). Her own parents sit together at the hearth, the queen spinning by a pillar and the king's chair leaning against hers (6.305–309), and it is the queen whose knees the stranger must clasp (6.310–315).
refs: [6.180-185, 6.305-311]
---

**The blessing.** Homer's fullest statement of the ideal of marriage is a wish, spoken by a shipwrecked man to a girl he has just met: may the gods give her “a husband and a home, and … oneness of mind, / a noble one; for there is nothing stronger or better than this, / than when, of one mind in their thoughts, they keep house together, / a man and a woman” (6.180–184; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]). The couple of the wish is a pair against the world, “many sorrows to their enemies, / and joy to their well-wishers” (6.184–185; see [[algos]]). The man who speaks it is on his way home to his own wife (see [[penelope]]).

**The king and queen.** Nausicaa's directions end in her parents' hall, and the picture she draws is of a married pair: her mother “sits at the hearth in the light of the fire, / turning the sea-purple yarn, a wonder to see, / leaning against a pillar; and her slave women sit behind her. / And there my father's chair leans against hers, / on which he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal” (6.305–309; see [[house]] and [[alcinous]]). Arete is seated, with her chair against the pillar, and the king's seat leans close to hers, αὐτῇ standing for her seat, a little nearer the door (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.307–308; see the translation's note on 6.307–309). The morning had shown them apart, the queen at the hearth with her handmaids and the king going out to the council (6.52–55). The stranger is to pass the king by and throw his arms around the queen's knees, for if she thinks of him in friendship he will see his home (6.310–315; see [[supplication]]); the queen, not yet named in the book, is Arete (7.54).

**Other marriages.** The book's talk of husbands is all of the one Nausicaa has not yet chosen. Two of her five brothers are married (6.62–63; see [[marriage]]); she wishes that such a man as the stranger might be called her husband (6.244–245; see [[posis]]); and the town, she fears, will say that he will be her husband, or that a god has come down at her prayer and “he will have her all his days” (6.276–284). Merry hears in “all his days” an ironical glance at the gods' inconstancy toward their mortal loves (on 6.281; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]).
