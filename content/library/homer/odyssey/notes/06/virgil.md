---
summary: The first book of the Aeneid follows this book three times. The shipwrecked Aeneas meets his mother disguised as a huntress and asks whether she is a goddess; Dido comes to the temple like Diana among her nymphs, the simile that Valerius Probus thought Virgil's least happy borrowing from Homer; and Venus makes her son beautiful, as Athena does Odysseus, like silver set in gold. Merry also hears the hungry lion of this book in Nisus among the sleeping Rutulians, and Nausicaa's “not against the will of all the gods” in Creusa's farewell.
---

- Aeneas meets Venus in the guise of a huntress and asks, “surely a goddess: Phoebus' sister, or one of the race of the nymphs?” (*Aeneid* 1.314–334, after 6.149–152 and 6.176–178; Merry on 6.149, who cites the verses as *Aeneid* 1.331): [[virgils-dido-as-diana]].
- Dido comes to the temple “as on the banks of the Eurotas or along the ridges of Cynthus Diana leads her dances” (*Aeneid* 1.494–504, after 6.102–109), and Valerius Probus' judgment that Virgil translated nothing from Homer so unhappily (Gellius, *Attic Nights* 9.9.12–17; Merry on 6.102): [[virgils-dido-as-diana]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]].
- Venus breathes beauty on her son before he appears to Dido, “such beauty as hands add to ivory, or when silver or Parian marble is set in yellow gold” (*Aeneid* 1.586–593, after 6.229–235; Merry on 6.232, who cites the verse as *Aeneid* 1.596): [[virgils-dido-as-diana]] and [[gold-poured-around-silver]].
- Nisus among the sleeping Rutulians is “like an unfed lion rampaging through full sheepfolds, for maddening hunger urges him” (*impastus ceu plena leo per ovilia turbans / (suadet enim vesana fames)*, *Aeneid* 9.339–341), which Merry sets beside the belly that bids the lion of this book “make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (6.133–134; Merry on 6.133): [[the-hungry-lion]] and [[nisus-and-euryalus]].
- Creusa's ghost tells Aeneas that these things “do not happen without the will of the gods” (*non haec sine numine divum / eveniunt*, *Aeneid* 2.777–778), which Merry compares with Nausicaa's “Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus” (6.240; Merry on 6.240): [[olympus]].
