summary: The trees of Book 6 are the thick wood above the river, from which Odysseus breaks a leafy branch to cover himself (6.127–129); the young palm on Delos, “such a tree” as never came up from the earth, to which he likens Nausicaa (6.162–167); and the poplars of Athena's grove beside the road to the city, near Alcinous' flourishing orchard (6.291–293).

**The branch.** “So saying, brilliant Odysseus came out from under the bushes, / and from the thick forest with his strong hand he broke off a branch / of leaves, to cover the man's nakedness about his body” (6.127–129; see [[the-hungry-lion]] and [[strong-hand]]). The bushes, θάμνοι, are the two under which he slept, the wild olive and the olive that grew from one spot (5.476–477; see [[olive]]), and the forest is the wood near the water to which he climbed from the river, though he feared that there he might become the prey and spoil of wild beasts (5.470–475; see the Book 5 note). θάμνων ὑπεδύσετο takes the genitive with the notion of escape, as κακῶν ὑποδύσεαι, “you will come out from under your troubles” (20.53), and πτόρθον … φύλλων, “a branch of leaves”, is a loose genitive like the material genitive of ἄλσος … αἰγείρων, the “grove … of poplars” (6.291–292), W. W. Merry explains (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.127, 6.128).

**The palm.** In his speech to Nausicaa Odysseus recalls the young palm he saw springing up by the altar of Apollo on Delos, and his wonder, “since never yet had such a tree come up from the earth” (6.162–167; see [[palm]] and [[the-palm-on-delos]]). δόρυ, elsewhere the spear or the ship's timber, is the living tree.

**The poplars of the grove.** “You will find a splendid grove of Athena near the road, / of poplars; and in it a spring flows, and around it is a meadow; / and there is my father's domain and his flourishing orchard” (6.291–293; see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[alcinous]]). ἄλσος … αἰγείρων is “a grove of Athena, of poplars”, the poplars being the stuff the grove is made of, as in the grove of water-nurtured poplars, αἰγείρων ὑδατοτρεφέων … ἄλσος, that ringed the spring of the town of Ithaca (17.208; Merry on 6.291). The poplar of Homer grows by water, as Merry notes on Calypso's island (on 5.64; see the Book 5 note): here a spring flows in the grove, and in Persephone's groves tall poplars grow with willows by the stream of Oceanus (10.509–510; see [[willow]]). The orchard of 6.293 is Alcinous' land outside the city; the orchard by the doors of his palace, where pears, pomegranates, apples, figs and olives bear fruit winter and summer, is described in the next book (7.112–121). At sunset Odysseus comes to the grove, “the famous grove, / sacred to Athena”, sits down there and prays to her (6.321–328; see [[athena]] and [[prayer]]).
