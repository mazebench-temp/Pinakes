---
summary: Odysseus, waking naked among strangers, comes out of the bushes toward Nausicaa and her handmaids like a mountain-bred lion that goes on through rain and wind with blazing eyes, driven by its belly after cattle, sheep or wild deer and even into the close-built fold (6.130–136). It is the first lion simile of the poem for Odysseus in action.
---

**The lion in the rain.** “And he went on like a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage, / that goes on, rained upon and blown by the wind, and his eyes / blaze” (6.130–132; see [[the-hungry-lion]] and [[mountain-bred]]). The opening is the Iliad's: Sarpedon goes at the Achaean wall in the same words, but for a particle, and a later verse, “make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold”, is the Iliad's word for word (6.134 = *Iliad* 12.301; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). Sarpedon's lion is driven by his proud spirit; this one by his belly, κέλεται δέ ἑ γαστήρ (6.133). Merry puts the point of the likeness in the hunger: both the lion and Odysseus are pushed by it into an act of unusual boldness, “for need had come upon him” (6.136; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.135).

**The herd and the one who stands.** The lion goes after cattle, sheep or wild deer (6.132–133; see [[cattle]], [[sheep]] and [[deer]]), and the girls behave like the herd: “they fled in fear this way and that over the jutting beaches” (6.138; see [[handmaids]] and [[shore]]). Only Nausicaa stands her ground, for Athena puts daring in her mind (6.139–140; see [[nausicaa]] and [[tharsos]]). The lion of the simile does not spring: Odysseus decides to beg her with soothing words, standing apart, rather than clasp her knees (6.141–148; see [[supplication]]).

**Lions of the poem.** Before Book 6 the Odyssey's lions belong to others or to the future: Menelaus foretells that Odysseus will come home to the suitors as a lion to his lair (4.333–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]), Proteus turns first into a lion (4.456; see [[proteus]]), and Penelope, in fear for her son, is likened to a lion hemmed in by hunters (4.791–793; see [[the-lion-encircled]]). Here for the first time the narrator likens Odysseus himself to a lion. The next lions are the Cyclops, who eats his men like a mountain-bred lion (9.292), and Odysseus among the dead suitors, spattered with blood like a lion that has eaten an ox (22.401–406); the lion that comes out of the bushes on Scheria wants clothes and a meal.
