**The wagon asked for.** Athena tells Nausicaa to urge her father “to make ready the mules and the wagon, which may carry / the sashes and the robes and the glossy blankets”, for it is finer to ride than to go on foot when the washing troughs are far from the city (6.36–40; see [[washing-troughs]], [[girdle]], [[robe]] and [[blankets]]). ἅμαξα is the four-wheeled cart, as distinct from the two-wheeled chariot; its name was derived from ἅμα and ἄγω, or, by Grashof, from ἀμφί and ἄξων, “with two axles” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.37; see [[chariot]]). Nausicaa asks for “the wagon … / high, with good wheels” (6.57–58), and her father repeats her words and adds a frame: “the slaves will make ready the wagon for you, / high, with good wheels, fitted with a frame above” (6.69–70; see [[alcinous]] and the translation's row ἀπήνην / ὑψηλὴν ἐύκυκλον). The scholia took the ὑπερτερίη for a box for baggage, like the wicker body bound on Priam's wagon; others for a movable hood or awning against sun and rain, which Merry thinks the likelier, since the participle ἀραρυῖαν suggests a part of the cart itself (on 6.70; see the translation's note on 6.70).

**Priam's wagon and Nausicaa's.** The wagon is made ready in the verses of the last book of the Iliad. “Outside they made ready the well-wheeled mule wagon” (6.72) is the wagon that Priam's sons bring out, “the well-wheeled mule wagon” (*Iliad* 24.266; see [[well-wheeled]] and [[mules]]); the girl brings the shining clothing out of her chamber and lays it “on the polished wagon” (6.74–75), as the sons bring the ransom “from the chamber” and heap it “on the polished wagon” (*Iliad* 24.275); and on the way home she “yoked the strong-hoofed mules” (6.253), in the words of *Iliad* 24.277 (see [[strong-hoofed]]). The mules are led under and yoked “beneath the wagon” (6.73): Merry explains the phrase from the beasts brought up and put with their necks under the yoke, as the oxen and mules are yoked “under the wagons” for Hector's pyre (on 6.73; *Iliad* 24.782). The wagon that carried the ransom for a dead son carries a princess's washing.

**To the river.** Nausicaa drives herself:

{{quote:de6033d5-4571-502b-88da-33832c41bd1d}}

The pace was steady, not fast, Merry notes, since the handmaids went on foot: “they stepped straight on without flagging” (on 6.83; see the translation's note on 6.83). At the troughs the girls unyoke the mules and turn them loose to graze (6.88–90): in ὑπεκπροέλυσαν, Merry says, they took them from under the yoke, loosed them from the cart and sent them off (on 6.86). The clothes are unloaded from the wagon into the water (6.90–91).

**The drive toward the city.** When Odysseus has eaten, Nausicaa folds the clothes and puts them “on the fine wagon”, yokes the mules and mounts (6.252–253), and bids the stranger walk with the handmaids “behind the mules and the wagon” while they go through the fields, and wait outside the city (6.259–261, 6.295; see [[nausicaa]] and [[odysseus]]). Then she drives:

{{quote:cdbe6584-861b-5bf1-ba73-7f3133c8502f}}

Merry renders, “she drove just so that they might keep up on foot, … and she laid on the lash with judgment”, and finds νόῳ, “with judgment”, unusual without a preposition (on 6.319–320; see the translation's note on 6.319–320). The whip is “shining”, he thinks, from its polished or colored leather or handle, as the strap of Odysseus' bed is bright with purple (on 6.316; 23.201, a plain rendering; see [[phaeinos]]). The word for the wagon changes with the verse: ἀπήνη in most of the book, ἅμαξα with the mules (6.37, 6.72, 6.260; see the entry).
