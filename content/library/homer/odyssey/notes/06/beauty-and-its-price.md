summary: Nausicaa sleeps “like the immortals in stature and in looks” (6.16) and stands out among her handmaids as Artemis among her nymphs (6.102–109). Athena makes the brine-crusted stranger “taller to look upon and stouter” and pours grace on him, and the girl who had thought him unsightly says that he is like the gods (6.229–245); but the town would say, “Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall, / a stranger?” (6.276–277).
en: [beauty, beautiful, handsome]
refs: [6.16, 6.108, 6.229-237, 6.242-243, 6.276]

**The girl.** The book introduces Nausicaa by her likeness to the immortals, “in stature and in looks” (6.16), with handmaids who have their beauty from the Graces (6.18; see [[eidos]] and [[graces]]). At the washing the simile gives her the beauty of Artemis among her nymphs: “she is easy to know, though all are beautiful; / so she stood out among her handmaids, a maiden unwed” (6.108–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]), and Odysseus, seeing her, likens her to Artemis “in looks and stature and build” (6.151–152). When she takes leave of him in Book 8 she still has “beauty from the gods” (θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, 8.457, a plain rendering).

**The stranger.** He comes out of the bushes “terrible … afflicted by the brine” (6.137), and after the bath the goddess remakes him: “Athena, child of Zeus, made him / taller to look upon and stouter, and down from his head / she made the thick hair fall, like the hyacinth flower” (6.229–231), and poured grace upon his head and shoulders as a craftsman pours gold around silver (6.232–235; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]], [[charis]] and [[athena]]). The thick hair is the hair of a vigorous man, against the bald head of the beggar the suitors mock (18.354–355), and the flower is compared for its clustering curls, not its color, since the Greeks thought the hyacinth dark and Odysseus' hair is ξανθαί, “auburn”, at 13.399 (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.231; see [[hyacinth]] and the translation's note on 6.231). The gift is a goddess's, as Hesiod's Aphrodite pours grace about the head of Pandora (*Works and Days* 65), and it is given again: the next morning among the Phaeacians (8.18–20) and after his bath at home, in the same verses (23.156–163).

**Its effect and its price.** “Shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (6.237), and her judgment turns: “for before he seemed to me unsightly, / but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243), and she wishes for such a husband (6.244–245; see [[posis]]). The beauty that wins her favor is also what the town would see: τίς δʼ ὅδε Ναυσικάᾳ ἕπεται καλός τε μέγας τε / ξεῖνος; “Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall, / a stranger?” (6.276–277). The stranger the goddess has made handsome must wait in the grove and come into the city alone (6.289–299; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]).
