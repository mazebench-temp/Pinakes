---
summary: Euripides' choruses and his Creusa sing of the palm and the laurel of Delos, by which Leto bore her children, the tree to which the scholia referred the palm that Odysseus saw; and his Artemis states the custom of the gods by which Athena, at the end of this book, will not openly oppose her father's brother.
---

- **The palm of Delos.** The captive Trojan women of the *Hecuba* ask whether they will be carried to the island “where the first-born palm and the laurel raised their sacred shoots for dear Leto” (*Hecuba* 455–461); Creusa tells Apollo that Delos hates him, “and the shoots of the laurel beside the soft-tressed palm”, where Leto bore him (*Ion* 919–922); and the Greek women of the *Iphigenia among the Taurians* long for Artemis of childbirth, who dwells by the Cynthian hill, the soft-tressed palm, the laurel and the olive, “dear birth-pang of Leto” (*Iphigenia among the Taurians* 1097–1102; plain renderings). Merry cites the *Hecuba* for the scholium that refers Odysseus' palm to the first palm of Leto's childbirth (on 6.162): [[the-palm-on-delos]], [[delos]], [[leto]] and [[cicero-the-palm-of-delos]].
- **The custom of the gods.** Artemis tells Theseus why she did not save Hippolytus: “this is the custom among the gods: none of us will set himself against the eager will of another, but we always stand aside”, and but for fear of Zeus she would never have let the man dearest to her die (*Hippolytus* 1328–1334, a plain rendering). Merry quotes the first words for Athena, who does not yet appear to Odysseus face to face, “for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; Merry on 6.330; see the translation's note on 6.329–331): [[athena]] and [[poseidon]].
