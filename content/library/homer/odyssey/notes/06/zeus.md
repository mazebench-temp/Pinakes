---
summary: Zeus does not act in this book; he is named as the father of goddesses and the giver of men's lot. The nymphs who play with Artemis are his daughters, Odysseus likens Nausicaa to Artemis, “the daughter of great Zeus”, and prays to Athena by the same title; Nausicaa tells the stranger that Zeus deals out prosperity to the good and the bad as he wishes, and bids her handmaids care for him, since strangers and beggars are from Zeus.
# Unnamed: "to each as he wishes; and he has given you these things" (6.189-190).
refs: [6.189-190]
---

**His daughters.** The goddesses of the book are named by their father. The nymphs who play with Artemis on the mountains are “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.105), their title in the Iliad and again in Book 9 (*Iliad* 6.420; 9.154; see [[nymphs]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). Odysseus likens Nausicaa to “Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus” (6.151; see [[artemis]]), and at sunset he prays “to the daughter of great Zeus”, Athena, as “child of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.323–324; see [[athena]], [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]); in between, “Athena, child of Zeus” makes him taller (6.229). Διὸς κούρῃ μεγάλοιο, “the daughter of great Zeus”, is the half-verse of the Trojan women's prayer to Athena in the Iliad (*Iliad* 6.304, 6.312), with the same verb as Odysseus' prayer here, ἠρᾶτο, “prayed” (6.323; *Iliad* 6.304), and in the plural it names the Prayers (*Iliad* 9.502; see [[litai]]).

**As he wishes.** Nausicaa answers the suppliant with a reason, breaks off into a reflection, and resumes: “Stranger, since you seem like neither a base man nor a witless one— / and it is Zeus himself, the Olympian, who deals out prosperity to men, / to the good and to the bad, to each as he wishes; / and he has given you these things, I suppose, and you must endure them all the same— / now, since you have come to our city and our land” (6.187–191; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]], [[olbos]] and [[olympian]]). The sentence begun with ἐπεί is taken up again after the parenthesis (see the translation's note on 6.187–193). Merry gives the thought: the stranger looks neither a villain nor a fool, only miserable, and misery as well as prosperity is dispensed to men by no rule but the will of the gods; besides, he is a suppliant on their shores, and so she will befriend him. The point lies in ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν, “as he wishes”, the arbitrary dispensation of good and evil, and Merry compares Helen's maxim at Sparta (4.236–237), Eumaeus' words to his guest (14.444–445) and the two jars of Zeus (*Iliad* 24.527–533) (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.187, 6.188–189; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). τάδε, “these things”, are the hardships she sees before her (Merry on 6.190).

**From Zeus.** When the handmaids run, she calls them back: “this is some unhappy wanderer who has come here, / whom we must look after now; for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear” (6.206–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]). πρὸς Διός is literally “sent by Zeus”, and then “under the protection of Zeus”, as the judges guard the ordinances “that come from Zeus” (*Iliad* 1.238–239); for the sentiment Merry compares the Zeus to whom the Phaeacians pour libations when Odysseus has come into the hall, the god who attends upon reverend suppliants (Merry on 6.207; 7.165). Eumaeus says the same verses to the disguised Odysseus (14.57–58), and the poem names the god as the protector of strangers and suppliants, ξείνιος and ἱκετήσιος (9.270–271, 13.213, 14.283–284). The girl's piety answers the question Odysseus asked on waking, whether the people of this land are kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods (6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]).
