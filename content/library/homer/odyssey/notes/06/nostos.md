summary: Athena goes to the Phaeacians “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.14). Nausicaa sends him to her father, “so that as soon as may be / you may win escort and a homecoming” (6.289–290), and to her mother's knees, “so that you may see the day of your homecoming”, with hope of reaching his house and his native land (6.311–315).
# νόστοιο (6.290; again 8.156, 9.102, 11.350, 15.69, 22.323).
grc: [νόστοιο]
---

**The goddess's purpose.** The book's first action is set in the service of the return: Athena goes to the house of Alcinous, νόστον Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μητιόωσα, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.13–14; see [[athena]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). The dream she sends and the ball she makes Nausicaa throw serve it (6.20–47, 6.112–117).

**Escort and homecoming.** The Phaeacians are the people who will carry him home, and Nausicaa names the two things her father can give him, πομπῆς καὶ νόστοιο, “escort and a homecoming” (6.289–290; see [[phaeacians]] and [[alcinous]]). Her directions end in the hope of the voyage: if her mother thinks of him in friendship, “then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your well-built house and your native land” (6.313–315; see [[day-of-homecoming]] and [[dear-native-land]]). The verses are those in which Proteus told Menelaus that it was not his fate to see his friends and reach his house until he had gone back to Egypt (4.475–476), turned from a denial to a hope; they return at Athena's direction to the palace (7.76–77), and the book's last verse is the proem's, Poseidon raging against godlike Odysseus “until he reached his own land” (6.331 = 1.21; see [[poseidon]]).

**Going home.** The girl's going home is the same verb, νέεσθαι (6.110; see the translation's note on 6.110–111): she is about to go back home from the washing when the ball falls into the eddy, and her return to the city with the stranger behind her wagon is the book's journey (6.110–117, 6.316–322).
