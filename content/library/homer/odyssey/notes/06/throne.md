---
summary: At the hearth the king's chair leans against the queen's, and on it Alcinous sits and drinks his wine like an immortal; the stranger is to pass him by and clasp the queen's knees (6.308–310).
---

“And there my father's chair leans against hers, / on which he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal. / Pass him by, and throw your arms around the knees / of my mother” (6.308–311; see [[alcinous]], [[wine]] and [[supplication]]). In the houses of men the Odyssey's θρόνος is a “chair” (see the translation's row κλισμός, θρόνος). The queen's chair stands against the pillar by the hearth (6.307; see [[hearth]] and [[house]]). αὐτῇ could mean that the king's chair leans against the same pillar, but that would set king and queen almost back to back; W. W. Merry prefers to take it, by a common brachylogy, as the queen's seat, so that his chair stands against hers, a little nearer the door (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.308; see the translation's note on 6.307–309). For αὐτῇ a scholium has αὐγῇ (Merry's critical note). ἀθάνατος ὥς, “like an immortal”, recalls the gods sitting beside Zeus while Hebe pours their nectar (Merry on 6.309; *Iliad* 4.1–4). In the next book Alcinous raises the suppliant from the hearth and seats him on a shining chair (7.167–171).
