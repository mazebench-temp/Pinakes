summary: Two baths in the river. When the washing is done, the girls bathe and anoint themselves with the oil that Nausicaa's mother gave her (6.79–80, 6.96); then Odysseus, whom Nausicaa bids her handmaids bathe, sends them apart, since he is ashamed to strip before them, washes the brine from his body himself and anoints himself (6.209–227).

**The girls' bath.** Nausicaa's mother gives her olive oil in a golden flask, “so that she might bathe and anoint herself, with her handmaids” (6.79–80; see [[oil-flask]]). χυτλώσαιτο covers both: χύτλον, related to χυτός, is properly anything “poured”, and in its technical sense a mixture of oil and water used by bathers; since the ancients had only alkali for soap, W. W. Merry adds, oil in the water would make a true soap (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.80; see the translation's note on 6.80). When the clothes are spread to dry, “they had bathed and anointed themselves richly with olive oil” (6.96), and then they eat by the river (6.97; see [[washing-troughs]] and [[feast]]).

**The stranger's bath.** Nausicaa tells her handmaids to give the stranger food and drink, “and bathe him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind” (6.209–210; see [[xenia]] and [[handmaids]]). They set him in the shelter, lay a mantle and a tunic beside him with the golden flask, “and bade him bathe in the streams of the river” (6.212–216; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]): Nausicaa had said λούσατε, “bathe him” (6.210), and the handmaids bid him bathe himself (Merry on 6.216). In the Odyssey the slave women bathe a guest (4.49; see the entry), but this guest will not be bathed by them:

{{quote:8c5e41e1-cb5e-5844-8506-55f475fc4dcf}}

οὕτω, “like that”, is “yonder”: he shows the distance with a wave of his hand (Merry on 6.218). His shame is the shame of nakedness before girls (see [[aidos]]), and the poem has already shown the brine on him, when he came out of the bushes “afflicted by the brine” and the handmaids fled (6.137–138).

**The washing off of the sea.** “But brilliant Odysseus washed from his skin with the river water / the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders, / and from his head he wiped the scurf of the barren sea” (6.224–226). ἐκ ποταμοῦ is the source of the water, as Circe's handmaid bathes him ἐκ τρίποδος, “from the tripod” (Merry on 6.224; 10.361); χνόος, “scurf”, is found only here (Merry on 6.226; see the translation's row χνόος and [[barren-sea]]). “But when he had bathed all over and anointed himself richly, / and had put on the clothes that the maiden unwed had given him” (6.227–228; see [[unbroken]]), Athena makes him taller and stronger and pours grace on him (6.229–235; see [[athena]] and [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). λίπʼ ἄλειψεν, without ἐλαίῳ, is the only place where λίπα stands alone; Merry takes it, against those who read an adverb, “smoothly”, for a dative of an old noun, “with oil” (on 3.466, citing Eustathius on 6.227). The same verses of Athena's grace follow Odysseus' bath in his own house, when the housekeeper has bathed and anointed him and he is about to sit down before Penelope (23.153–163).
