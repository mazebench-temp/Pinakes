summary: Hephaestus is named in the simile of Odysseus' grace: Athena pours it on him as a skilled man “whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught / every kind of craft” pours gold around silver (6.232–235).
---

**The teacher of craftsmen.** After the bath Athena makes Odysseus taller and pours grace on his head and shoulders, “as when a man pours gold around silver, / a skilled man whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught / every kind of craft, and he accomplishes lovely works” (6.232–234; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[athena]]). The verses return when she does the same for Odysseus before Penelope (23.159–161). In the Iliad Hephaestus is the craftsman himself, who builds the gods' houses and forges the arms of Achilles (see the general article); here he is the teacher of human craftsmen, with Athena beside him. So the *Hymn to Hephaestus* praises him as the god who “with gray-eyed Athena taught glorious works to men upon the earth”, men who before lived in caves in the mountains like wild beasts (*Hymn to Hephaestus* 2–4), and Plato's Stranger in the *Statesman* counts among the gods' gifts to men fire from Prometheus and the arts from Hephaestus and his fellow-artisan (*Statesman* 274c–d). Merry cites both passages on the verse, the Plato after Nitzsch, and takes παντοίην τέχνην, “every kind of craft”, as art in all its branches (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.233). Merry adds that Athena, usually the patroness of women's work, spinning and weaving, was worshiped as Ergane, the goddess of all artisans (on 6.233). ἀργύρῳ is the ground on which the gold is laid, as in Nestor's vow of a heifer “with gold poured around her horns” (Merry on 6.232; 3.384), which the goldsmith Laerces fulfilled at Pylos, “working it with skill, so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (3.425–438; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]]).
