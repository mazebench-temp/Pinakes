**The game.** The girls have eaten their fill by the river while the clothes dry:

{{quote:6a27dcb8-fbb5-58f3-8dad-f37df82de315}}

The handmaids are δμῳαί here, “the slave women” (6.99), and ἀμφίπολοι in the simile that follows (6.109; see [[handmaids]]). They take off their veils to play (see [[veil]]), and the princess leads the μολπή, the “song and dance” (see the translation's row μολπή). The scholia explained the word as “the game”; W. W. Merry holds that the play went with a measured chant and a dance, the ball thrown and caught in time, as in the ball dance of the Phaeacian youths (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.101; 8.370–380). Aristarchus' copies all read σφαίρῃ ταὶ δʼ ἄρʼ ἔπαιζον, with δέ in the third place of the verse (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.100). The leader of the game is the tallest of the girls, as Artemis among her nymphs (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[nausicaa]]).

**The ball in the eddy.** As they make ready to go home, “the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing, / that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl” (6.112–113; see [[thought-of-another-thing]] and [[athena]]):

{{quote:c3705a0f-85b9-522d-bc2a-42df62be486f}}

Nausicaa is the subject of both verbs, the missing and the throwing (Merry on 6.116). Some read ἔμπεσε, “it fell”, for ἔμβαλε; Nitzsch thought that the original reading, changed so that no one should imagine that Nausicaa herself fell into the water (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.116). Eustathius took the game for one in which the thrower makes a show of tossing the ball to one player and then flings it to another (Merry on 6.115; see the translation's note on 6.115). The shout is the girls' cry that Odysseus, waking, takes for the voices of nymphs (6.122–124; see [[nymphs]] and [[odysseus]]).
