**The houses of the Phaeacians.** Nausithous, who led the Phaeacians to Scheria, “drove a wall around the city, and built houses, / and made temples of the gods, and divided the fields” (6.9–10; see [[nausithous]] and [[scheria]]).

**Nausicaa's chamber.** Athena goes to the house of Alcinous (6.13), and into “the intricately worked chamber, in which a girl / was sleeping” (6.15–16; see [[intricately-worked]] and [[nausicaa]]), “and beside her two handmaids, who had their beauty from the Graces, / one on either side, by the doorposts; and the shining doors were closed” (6.18–19; see [[handmaids]], [[graces]] and [[door]]). The handmaids, W. W. Merry infers, slept in their young mistress's room at night, probably on mattresses on the floor, so near the door that it could not be opened without waking them; Penelope too goes attended by two handmaids (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.18; 1.331). The goddess comes through the closed doors “like a breath of wind” to the girl's bed (6.20; see [[bed]]).

**Through the house.** At dawn Nausicaa “went through the house to bring the news to her parents” and found them within, her mother at the hearth among her handmaids and her father going out of doors to the council (6.50–55; see [[hearth]] and [[alcinous]]); some read κατά for διά (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.50). Her five brothers live “in the halls”, two married and three unmarried (6.62–63), and she brings the clothing for the washing out of her chamber (6.74; see [[washing-troughs]]).

**The house of Alcinous.** Nausicaa tells Odysseus to ask in the city “for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous” (6.299):

{{quote:5f814f8d-209d-5f54-bd62-c749286974cd}}

Merry finds οἷος δόμος Ἀλκινόοιο an awkward explanation of ἐοικότα τοῖσι, with δόμος put for the δώματα of the verse before (on 6.301; see the translation's note on 6.301–303). “But when the house and the court have hidden you, / go very quickly through the hall, until you come / to my mother” (6.303–305). The house hides him, Merry explains, because a man is lost to sight from outside as soon as he has passed the door; the court is named, somewhat out of place, as the first part of the premises he must cross, and then he is to walk right through the hall to its upper end, where the king and queen sit (on 6.303; see the translation's row κεύθω). There the queen sits at the hearth “leaning against a pillar”, and the king's chair leans against hers (6.305–309; see [[hearth]] and [[throne]]). The words do not mean that Arete stands leaning on the pillar, since she is seated (see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]); they explain where her chair stands, as the herald sets Demodocus' chair against a tall pillar in the next book but one (Merry on 6.307; 8.65–66, a plain rendering). The house itself, with its walls of bronze, its golden doors and the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard it, is described when Odysseus stands at its threshold (7.84–94; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**Home.** The house is also the home that a marriage makes. Odysseus prays that the gods may give Nausicaa “a husband and a home”, and praises the life of a man and a woman when, “of one mind in their thoughts, they keep house together” (6.181–184; see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[marriage]]); the most blessed of men will be he “who prevails with his bride-price and leads you to his home” (6.159; see [[bride-price]]); and the end of her directions is the hope that he will reach “your well-built house and your native land” (6.315; see [[well-built]] and [[nostos]]).
