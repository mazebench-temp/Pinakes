summary: Asleep under the olives when the book begins, Odysseus wakes to the cry of the girls at their game, wonders among what people he has come, and comes out of the thicket naked, holding a leafy branch, like a mountain lion driven by hunger. He chooses to beg Nausicaa from a distance, likens her to Artemis and to the palm on Delos, and asks only for the way to the city and a rag to wear; bathed apart from the girls and made taller and more graceful by Athena, he eats, follows the wagon, and at sunset prays to Athena in her grove (6.1–331).

**Asleep.** “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus, / worn out by sleep and weariness” (6.1–2), the verse that will leave him asleep again in Alcinous' house (7.344). Unless ὕπνῳ can mean “sleepiness”, the phrase joins two ideas, “oppressed with the sleep that weariness brings” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.2; see the translation's note on 6.2). While he sleeps Athena goes to the city of the Phaeacians, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.14; see [[athena]] and [[nostos]]).

**The waking.** When Nausicaa is ready to go home, Athena contrives “that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl, / who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.113–114): the ball falls into the eddy, the girls shout, and he wakes (6.115–117; see [[ball]] and [[nausicaa]]). Sitting up, he ponders:

{{quote:6640e6be-5081-54fe-8f25-deb6178e4e78}}

αὖτε, “this time”, has the impatience of our “now!” (Merry on 6.119), and θεουδής, “god-fearing”, explains φιλόξεινοι: men who fear the wrath of Zeus, the god of strangers (Merry on 6.121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]). He will ask the same question when he wakes on Ithaca and does not know his own land (13.200–202), and the last two verses when he goes to see what men the Cyclopes are (9.175–176). The cry he heard sounds to him like the cry of nymphs, “who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (6.123–124), or perhaps of men with human voice, “But come, I will make trial myself and see” (6.126; see [[nymphs]] and the translation's note on 6.122–125).

**The lion.** He comes out from under the bushes and breaks a leafy branch from the thick wood “to cover the man's nakedness about his body” (6.127–129), and goes like a mountain-bred lion, rained upon and blown by the wind, whose belly bids it go even into the close-built fold (6.130–134): “so Odysseus was about to mingle with the girls of the lovely tresses, / naked though he was; for need had come upon him” (6.135–136; see [[the-hungry-lion]]). The main point of the comparison, Merry says, is that both are pushed by hunger into an act of unusual boldness (on 6.135). “And terrible he appeared to them, afflicted by the brine” (6.137), and all but Nausicaa flee (6.138–141).

**The choice.** “And Odysseus pondered / whether he should clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and beg her, / or, as he was, with soothing words, standing apart, / beg her” (6.141–144; see [[decision-scene]]): αὔτως, “as he was”, is defined by ἀποσταδά, “standing apart” (Merry on 6.143), and on the doubt about 6.144, see the translation's note on 6.144. “This seemed to him the better way”: to keep his distance, “lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (6.145–147; see [[kerdos]] and [[supplication]]). So “At once he spoke a soothing and shrewd word” (6.148): the derivatives of κέρδος take almost any coloring, from the wise counsel of a goddess to the craft of Sisyphus (Merry on 6.148).

**The supplication.** “I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (6.149): he says the suppliant's word, γουνοῦμαι, from the knees (γούνατα), without touching hers, and ἄνασσα pays a woman a special compliment (Merry on 6.149; see the translation's note on 6.149). If she is a goddess, he likens her to Artemis; if a mortal, three times blessed are her parents and her brothers, and most blessed the man who wins her with his bride-price (6.150–159; see [[artemis]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[bride-price]]). “Awe holds me as I look” (6.161; see [[awe-holds-me]]): he has seen nothing like her but the young palm by Apollo's altar on Delos, where he went with many people following him, “on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows” (6.162–169; see [[the-palm-on-delos]]), the voyage from Aulis to Troy (Merry on 6.162). Then his own case: “Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped from the wine-dark sea”, cast ashore by some god from Ogygia, and the gods will yet bring much upon him (6.170–174; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]], [[ogygia]] and [[daimon]]). He asks for little. “But pity me, queen” (6.175) is his prayer to the river, “But pity me, lord” (5.450); “Show me the city, and give me a rag to throw around me, / if you had perhaps some wrapping for the clothes when you came here” (6.178–179), a wrapper of the coarse cloth the washing was packed in, so that his request is a very modest one (Merry on 6.179). He ends with a blessing on her: a husband, a home and a noble oneness of mind, “for there is nothing stronger or better than this” (6.180–185; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**The stranger.** From now on he is ξεῖνος, “stranger” (6.187, 6.209, 6.246, 6.255, 6.277, 6.289; see [[xenia]]), “a much-tried suppliant” (ἱκέτην ταλαπείριον, 6.193; see [[much-tried]]) and “some unhappy wanderer” (δύστηνος ἀλώμενος, 6.206). Nausicaa promises him clothing and all a suppliant should have, and tells her handmaids that strangers and beggars are from Zeus (6.191–193, 6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).

**The bath.** The handmaids set him in the shelter and lay a mantle, a tunic and the oil beside him (6.211–216), and he asks them to stand apart: “Handmaids, stand off there like that” (6.218), οὕτω, “yonder”, with a wave of the hand (Merry on 6.218). “But I will not bathe in front of you; for I am ashamed / to strip naked when I have come among girls of the lovely tresses” (6.221–222; see [[aidos]] and [[bathing]]). He washes the brine from his back and broad shoulders and wipes from his head “the scurf of the barren sea” (6.224–226), χνόος, the scurf of dry salt (Merry on 6.226). Then Athena:

{{quote:a6ec73a0-f38a-572b-8989-7a8ddeb51ace}}

Merry takes the hyacinth to be compared for the clustering curls, not for the color, since the hair Athena will take from his head on Ithaca is ξανθάς, auburn (13.399; Merry on 6.231; see the translation's note on 6.231 and [[hyacinth]]). The craftsman's gilding is the simile of [[gold-poured-around-silver]] (see also [[charis]]), and the same verses make him beautiful again before Penelope knows him (23.156–162). “Then he went apart along the shore of the sea and sat down, / shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (6.236–237). The girl who saw him unsightly now finds him “like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243).

**The meal and the road.** “Then much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate / greedily; for he had long tasted no food” (6.249–250; see [[fasting]]). Nausicaa bids him follow the wagon through the fields with her handmaids, wait in Athena's grove, and in her father's house pass the king by and clasp the queen's knees (6.255–315; see [[the-grove-of-athena]], [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), and she drives “just so that they could follow on foot, / the handmaids and Odysseus” (6.319–320).

**The prayer.** At sunset they come to the famous grove, sacred to Athena, and he sits down there (6.321–322):

{{quote:e2842380-ef94-57e3-b1d3-de8ef2ab1e17}}

The first verse of the prayer is Penelope's (4.762; see [[hear-me]]). He reproaches the goddess for not listening while the earth-shaker wrecked him, ῥαιομένου, ὅτε μʼ ἔρραιε, the participle and the verb of the same root together (Merry on 6.326; see [[poseidon]]), though in the storm she had bound the other winds and twice given him presence of mind (5.382–387, 5.427, 5.436–437). His last verse is the one Aristotle quotes for the two things a speaker should aim at, to be welcomed and to be pitied (*Rhetoric* 1415b26–27; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]] and the translation's note on 6.327). Athena hears him but does not yet show herself, out of regard for her father's brother, who rages against him “until he reached his own land” (6.328–331, the last verse as 1.21; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 6.329–331). She throws off this reserve once he is in Ithaca, beyond Poseidon's power (Merry on 6.330).

**His epithets.** πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (6.1, 6.249); δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (6.117, 6.127, 6.217, 6.224, 6.322); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (6.14); ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (6.331). See [[much-enduring]], [[brilliant]], [[great-hearted]] and [[antitheos]].
