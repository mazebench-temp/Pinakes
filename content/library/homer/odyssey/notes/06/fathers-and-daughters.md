But he understood everything” and granted it (6.56–70).

**Papa dear.** Athena's dream sends the girl to her father, “But come, urge your famous father in the early dawn / to make ready the mules and the wagon” (6.36–37), and at dawn she finds him going out to the council and stands very close to him (6.50–56; see [[alcinous]] and [[boule]]). Her first words are a child's: πάππα φίλʼ, “Papa dear” (6.57), the only πάππας in Homer, where the Iliad has the verb, of the children who do not call a doomed man papa at his knees (*Iliad* 5.408; see the translation's table of key words). The question is a tentative, beseeching one, “Could you not get me ready?” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.57), and the reasons are all for others: his clean clothes among the foremost at the council, her brothers' freshly washed clothes for the dance, “and all these things are a care to my mind” (6.60–65), which she says, Merry thinks, in a tone of sportive seriousness, as the only daughter of the house on whom that care falls (on 6.65; see [[kasignetos]]).

**He understood everything.** The reason she leaves out is her own: “for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father. But he understood everything, and answered her: / ‘I do not grudge you the mules, my child, nor anything else. / Go; the slaves will make ready the wagon for you’” (6.66–70; see [[aidos]], [[noos]] and [[marriage]]). ἔρχευ is “away then!” (Merry on 6.69). The father grants the request without naming what he has understood, and the slaves yoke the mules (6.71–73; see [[wagon]] and [[mules]]).

**Her father's daughter.** The girl is named by her father throughout the book, “the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (6.17, 6.196, 6.213), and it is as “the daughter of Alcinous” that she alone stands her ground before the stranger (6.139; see [[tharsos]]). She tells him her father's name before her own, and sends him to her father's house for his escort home (6.196–197, 6.255–257, 6.289–290; see [[nostos]]). Her fear of the town is a daughter's: she would blame a girl who mingled with men “against the will of her friends, her father and mother, still living” (6.287–288; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). In Book 7 Alcinous will wish that a man like the stranger might have his daughter (7.311–315, a plain rendering; see [[husbands-and-wives]]).
