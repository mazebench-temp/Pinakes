**A parenthesis.** The thought comes between the two halves of Nausicaa's answer. The sentence begun with ἐπεί, “since you seem like neither a base man nor a witless one”, is resumed at 6.191 with a second clause of the same kind, “now, since you have come to our city and our land”, and completed only at 6.192, “you shall not lack clothing”; the verses on Zeus between are a reflection suggested by the stranger's state (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.187; see the translation's note on 6.187–193). Merry paraphrases her reasoning: he looks neither a villain nor a fool, only miserable; misery as well as prosperity is dealt out to men by no rule but the will of the gods; besides, he is here as a suppliant; therefore she will befriend him (on 6.187; see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]).

**As he wishes.** The point of the verses lies in ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν, the arbitrary dispensation of good and evil, and Merry sets them beside Helen's maxim (4.236–237), Eumaeus' word to his guest, that the god will give one thing and let another be, as he wishes in his heart, for he can do all things (14.444–445), and Achilles' two jars (*Iliad* 24.527–533; Merry on 6.188–189; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). The second verse ends as Telemachus' answer to his mother on the singer, that Zeus is to blame, “who gives / to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (1.348–349; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). What Zeus deals out is ὄλβος, “prosperity” (see [[olbos]] and [[zeus]]), and its lack: τάδε are “these hardships which I see” (Merry on 6.190).

**Endure them.** The last half-verse, σὲ δὲ χρὴ τετλάμεν ἔμπης, is Telemachus' at Pylos, when he tells Nestor that the gods have spun no such prosperity for him and his father, “and now I must endure it all the same” (3.208–209). It is said here to the much-enduring man himself (see [[much-enduring]]), by a girl who does not know who he is, and its consolation is practical: since he has come to their city, he shall not lack clothing or anything else a suppliant should have (6.191–193).
