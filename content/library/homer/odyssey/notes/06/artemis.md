summary: Artemis is the measure of Nausicaa. In the simile of the game of ball she ranges over Taygetus or Erymanthus among her nymphs, delighting in boars and swift deer, taller than all of them, to the joy of her mother Leto; and Odysseus, unsure whether the girl is a goddess or a mortal, likens her most nearly to Artemis, “the daughter of great Zeus” (6.151).

**Among her nymphs.** When Nausicaa leads the song and dance of the game of ball (6.101), the poet gives her the likeness of the goddess:

{{quote:e782403c-e446-5763-80f0-de37e83064c1}}

It is the Iliad's huntress, whom Hera bade kill wild beasts and deer on the mountains rather than fight her betters (*Iliad* 21.485–486), and who taught Scamandrius “to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains” (*Iliad* 5.52). The simile keeps her at the chase, “delighting in boars and swift deer” (6.104), with her nymphs at play about her as the girls play at ball (see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]], [[taygetus]], [[erymanthus]], [[nymphs]] and [[leto]]). παίζουσι, “play”, Merry takes with the notion of hunting, the sport of the chase (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.106), and ὑπὲρ … κάρη ἔχει as “lifts her head above all”, or “overtops them by head and shoulders” (Merry on 6.107; see the translation's note on 6.107). The goddess stands out by her stature, as the girl does among her handmaids (6.109); ῥεῖά τʼ ἀριγνώτη πέλεται, “and she is easy to know”, is said here of a goddess among goddesses, and the lesser Ajax says in the Iliad that “gods are easy to know” (*Iliad* 13.72; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Nausicaa will use the same words of her father's house in the city, “It is easy to know” (6.300). Her epithet is the Iliad's, ἰοχέαιρα, “of the showering arrows” (6.102; see [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). Virgil turned the simile to Dido walking among the Tyrians (*Aeneid* 1.498–504; see [[virgils-dido-as-diana]]), and Valerius Probus, as Gellius reports, judged that Virgil had rendered no passage of Homer less happily (see the translation's note on 6.102–109).

**Like Artemis.** Odysseus begins his supplication with the same comparison, put as a question: “I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal? / If you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky, / to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, / I liken you most nearly, in looks and stature and build” (6.149–152; see [[nausicaa]], [[divine-epiphany]] and [[supplication]]). The last verse is the Iliad's for the Dream that came to Agamemnon in Nestor's shape, “very close to him in looks and stature and build” (*Iliad* 2.58), and the Odyssey compares its women to Artemis again: Helen comes from her chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.122), and Penelope like Artemis or golden Aphrodite (17.37, 19.54; see [[helen]] and [[penelope]]). The narrator's simile and the hero's compliment meet: what the poet has shown of Nausicaa among her handmaids, Odysseus sees in her at once.
