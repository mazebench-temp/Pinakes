summary: Clothes are the business of the book. Nausicaa's glossy clothes lie uncared for, though her marriage is near, and her father and brothers want clean clothes for the council and the dance (6.25–65); the girls wash them at the river and fold them again (6.85–111, 6.252); and the naked stranger begs for a rag and is given a mantle and a tunic (6.144, 6.178–179, 6.192, 6.214, 6.228).

**Clothes for a wedding.** In the likeness of Dymas' daughter Athena scolds the girl: “Your glossy clothes lie uncared for, / and your marriage is near, when you must wear fine clothes yourself / and provide others for those who will lead you home. / For it is from these things that talk goes up among men, / good talk, and your father and lady mother rejoice” (6.26–30; see [[marriage]] and [[nausicaa]]). ἐκ τούτων is “from such sumptuous style” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.29; see the translation's note on 6.29), and Merry refers the clothes given to others to the torchlight procession that brought a bride to her husband's house (on 6.28; see [[robe]]). To her father Nausicaa gives the reasons of the household instead: a man among the foremost should take counsel “with clean clothes on your body”, and her brothers “always want to have freshly washed clothes / when they go to the dance” (6.60–65; see [[alcinous]]).

**The washing.** The clothing is brought out of her chamber and laid on the wagon (6.74–75, 6.83; see [[wagon]]), trodden in the pits at the river, spread on the shingle and left to dry in the sun (6.91–98; see [[washing-troughs]]), and folded again for the drive home (6.111, 6.252). The sashes, robes and blankets go with it (6.38; see [[girdle]], [[robe]] and [[blankets]]).

**Clothes for the stranger.** Odysseus comes out of the bushes with a leafy branch to cover his nakedness, “naked though he was” (6.127–136; see [[odysseus]]), and decides to beg the girl from a distance, “if she would show him the city and give him clothes” (6.144; Athenocles suspected the verse, see the translation's note on 6.144). He asks for “a rag to throw around me, / if you had perhaps some wrapping for the clothes when you came here” (6.178–179): the εἴλυμα is the coarse wrapper in which the washing was packed, so that he asks for very little (Merry on 6.179; see the translation's note on 6.179 and [[cloak]]). Nausicaa answers that he “shall not lack clothing, nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant” (6.192–193; see the translation's note on 6.193 and [[supplication]]), and her handmaids lay “a mantle and a tunic for clothing” beside him (6.214; see [[tunic]]). He puts on “the clothes that the maiden unwed had given him” (6.228), and Athena pours grace on him (6.229–237; see [[athena]]). In the next book the clothes betray him: Arete knows the mantle and the tunic for her own work and asks who gave them to him (7.233–239).
