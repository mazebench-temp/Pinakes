---
summary: Nausicaa sleeps “like the immortals in stature and in looks” (6.16); Athena comes to her “in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas” (6.22–24); and Odysseus likens her to Artemis “in looks and stature and build” (6.151–152).
---

**Looks.** The book measures beauty by the gods. Nausicaa is introduced asleep, ἀθανάτῃσι φυὴν καὶ εἶδος ὁμοίη, “like the immortals in stature and in looks” (6.16), and her handmaids have their beauty from the Graces (6.18; see [[graces]]). Odysseus, who does not know whether she is goddess or mortal, likens her to Artemis, “in looks and stature and build” (εἶδός τε μέγεθός τε φυήν τε, 6.151–152; see [[artemis]] and [[divine-epiphany]]), and the narrator's simile at the washing has already set her among her handmaids like Artemis among her nymphs (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). His own looks are changed in the course of the day. When he comes out of the bushes he is “terrible … afflicted by the brine” (6.137), and Nausicaa says that he seemed to her unsightly (ἀεικέλιος, 6.242); after the bath Athena makes him “taller to look upon and stouter” and pours grace on him, “but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.229–243; see [[charis]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]).

**Likeness.** The aorist participles of εἴδομαι are the words for a god in a mortal's shape. Athena comes to the girl's bed εἰδομένη κούρῃ ναυσικλειτοῖο Δύμαντος, “in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships”, her age-mate and friend, “In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her” (6.22–24; see [[dream-scene]] and [[athena]]), in the verse with which Aphrodite spoke to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-comber (*Iliad* 3.386–389; see the translation's table of formulas). The dream is a dream of likeness: the girl wakes and marvels at it (6.49; see [[dream]]).
