---
summary: Odysseus remembers the journey to Troy, “on which I was to have evil sorrows” (κήδεʼ, 6.165), and blesses the married pair who are “many sorrows to their enemies, / and joy to their well-wishers” (ἄλγεα, 6.184–185).
---

**The journey of sorrows.** The palm on Delos was seen on a journey he now knows the end of: “for I went there too, and many people followed me, / on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows” (6.164–165; see [[the-palm-on-delos]] and [[laos]]). The voyage is the one from Aulis to Troy, through the Cyclades to Delos (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.162), and the κήδεα of it are counted with ἄλγεα, as in Book 5 (see the note there). The sorrows go on: “and hard grief has come upon me”, and he does not think his suffering will cease (6.169–174; see [[penthos]] and [[daimon]]).

**Sorrows to enemies.** In the blessing of marriage the sorrows are the couple's gift to their enemies: πόλλʼ ἄλγεα δυσμενέεσσι, / χάρματα δʼ εὐμενέτῃσι (6.184–185). The accusatives stand in apposition to the whole clause, as in Hector's reproach to Paris, “a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (*Iliad* 3.51), and in Agamemnon's word on Menelaus' wound, “fame for him, and grief for us” (*Iliad* 4.197; Merry on 6.184; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]] and [[fame-for-him-grief-for-us]]).
