summary: Leto is named once, in the simile of Artemis among her nymphs: as her daughter outshines them all on the mountains, “Leto is glad at heart” (6.106).
---

**A mother's gladness.** In the simile of the game of ball, Artemis goes over the mountains with her nymphs, “and Leto is glad at heart” (6.106; see [[artemis]], [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[nymphs]]). γέγηθε δέ τε φρένα is the half-verse of another simile in the Iliad, the stars shining round the moon when the upper air is windless, “and the shepherd is glad at heart” (*Iliad* 8.559); in both pictures an onlooker's gladness is part of the scene, and here the onlooker is the goddess's mother. The *Hymn to Apollo* gives her the same pride in her other child: when Apollo comes to the house of Zeus with his bow, “lady Leto rejoices, because she bore an archer son, and a strong one” (*Hymn to Apollo* 12–13). A parent's joy returns when Odysseus speaks to the girl: “three times blessed are your father and your lady mother”, whose hearts, he says, are always warmed with gladness as they watch such a scion going into the dance (6.154–157; see [[three-times-blessed]]). The young palm by Apollo's altar on Delos that Odysseus remembers a little later (6.162–163) the scholia referred to the first palm, by which Leto gave birth (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.162; see [[the-palm-on-delos]], [[palm]] and [[apollo]]). In the *Hymn to Apollo* she bears him on Delos “nearest the palm”, and throws her arms around it in her labor (*Hymn to Apollo* 16–18, 117–119); in Euripides' *Hecuba* the captive Trojan women imagine the island where the first-born palm and the laurel put forth their sacred shoots for Leto, a glory of her birth-pangs (*Hecuba* 455–461).
