---
summary: When the handmaids have set food and drink beside him, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus drank and ate / greedily; for he had long tasted no food” (6.249–250).
en: [tasted no food]
---

The meal ends the stranger's welcome on the shore: clothes, oil and the bath first, then food and drink, which Nausicaa ordered twice (6.209, 6.246–248; see [[xenia]]). ἄπαστος ἐδητύος, “having tasted no food”, is the phrase used of Penelope in her fear for her son (4.788; see the note there); here it is the plain hunger of a man whose raft was scattered by Poseidon's wave and who was driven two nights and two days on the sea before he came ashore (5.366–371, 5.388–390). ἁρπαλέως, “greedily”, gives the measure. Hunger has driven him all day: the lion of the simile, rained on and blown by the wind, goes after the sheep because “his belly bids him” (6.130–134), and the point of the comparison, Merry says, is that both are pushed by hunger into an act of unusual boldness, “for need had come upon him” (6.136; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.135; see [[the-hungry-lion]]).
