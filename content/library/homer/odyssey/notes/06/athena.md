summary: The book is Athena's from its second verse. While Odysseus sleeps she goes to the city of the Phaeacians, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus”, comes to Nausicaa in a dream in the likeness of a friend and sends her to the river with the washing, contrives that Odysseus should wake and see her, gives the girl daring to stand her ground, and after his bath makes him taller and pours grace on him. At sunset he prays to her in her grove; she hears him, but does not yet appear to him face to face, out of respect for her father's brother.

**To the city of the Phaeacians.** The book turns from the sleeper to the goddess in its second verse: Odysseus lies “worn out by sleep and weariness; but Athena / went to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.2–3; see [[phaeacians]]). The last act of Book 5 was hers, the sleep she poured on his eyes (5.491–493). After the story of the people, how Nausithous led them from Hypereia to Scheria and how Alcinous rules them now (6.4–12; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]], [[nausithous]] and [[alcinous]]), “To his house went the gray-eyed goddess Athena, / devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.13–14; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]], [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]] and [[gray-eyed]]). The second verse returns at the start of Book 8, when she goes through the city in the likeness of Alcinous' herald to call the Phaeacians to their assembly (8.7–9).

**The dream.** She comes to the girl's chamber: “And she rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed, / and stood above her head and spoke to her, / in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships, / who was of an age with her, and dear to her heart” (6.20–23; see [[nausicaa]], [[dymas-the-phaeacian]], [[like-a-breath-of-wind]] and [[dream-scene]]). For the swift and silent coming Merry compares Hermes in the *Hymn to Hermes*, who slipped sideways into the hall through the keyhole, like an autumn breeze (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.20; *Hymn to Hermes* 145–147); the phantom she sent to Penelope came in past the strap of the bolt and went away into the blasts of the winds (4.802, 4.838–839). τῇ μιν ἐεισαμένη προσέφη γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her” (6.24), is the Iliad's verse for a divine visitor who speaks in a mortal's shape: the Dream as Nestor, Aphrodite as Helen's old wool-worker, Apollo as Asius, son of Dymas (*Iliad* 2.22, 3.389, 16.720; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). The goddess chooses the likeness as they did, someone the hearer loves: the Dream took the shape of the elder “whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders” (*Iliad* 2.21), and Aphrodite that of the old woman who “loved her most of all” (*Iliad* 3.388). Her speech is a rebuke and a plan: the clothes lie uncared for, the marriage is near, and the girl must ask her father for the mules and the wagon and go to the washing troughs at dawn, “and I will go with you to help” (6.25–40; see [[dream]] and [[marriage]]). No friend comes in the morning; Nausicaa drives out with her handmaids (6.84). The goddess has gone “to Olympus, where, they say, the seat of the gods stands / firm forever” (6.42–43; see [[olympus]]), with the verse of her leaving Telemachus in Book 1 and Diomedes in the Iliad, “So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away” (6.41 = 1.319; *Iliad* 5.133).

**The ball.** Later in the day, as the girls make ready to go home, “then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing, / that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl, / who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.112–114). It is the verse with which she turns the action in Books 2 and 4 (2.382, 2.393, 4.795; see [[thought-of-another-thing]]), and later in the book Nausicaa has its second half for herself (6.251). The plan is carried out by the girl's hand: “So then the princess flung the ball to one of her handmaids; / she missed the handmaid, and threw it into a deep eddy” (6.115–116; see [[ball]]). ἔπειτα, “so then”, introduces the first stage in carrying out Athena's intention (Merry on 6.115), and Nausicaa is the subject of both verbs, “missed” and “threw” (Merry on 6.116; see the translation's note on 6.116). The goddess's thought and the girl's bad throw are one event, and the girls' shout wakes Odysseus (6.117; see [[divine-intervention]]).

**Daring.** When he comes out of the bushes, naked and caked with brine, the handmaids scatter, and “only the daughter of Alcinous stood her ground; for Athena / put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs” (6.139–140; see [[tharsos]] and [[deos]]). It is her gift to the young in the poem: Telemachus answers Nestor “taking heart; for Athena herself had put daring in his mind” (3.76–77), and when she left him in Book 1, “in his heart / she put might and daring” (1.320–321), as at Troy “Pallas Athena / gave might and daring” to Diomedes (*Iliad* 5.1–2).

**Grace.** After the bath, “Athena, child of Zeus, made him / taller to look upon and stouter, and down from his head / she made the thick hair fall, like the hyacinth flower” (6.229–231; see [[hyacinth]]), and she poured grace on his head and shoulders as a skilled man whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught every kind of craft pours gold around silver (6.232–235; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]], [[hephaestus]] and [[charis]]). Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, “child of Zeus”, is everywhere else in Homer the title of Helen (*Iliad* 3.199, 3.418; 4.184, 4.219, 23.218; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]). The craftsman's teacher is the goddess herself: in the Iliad she loved the builder's son Phereclus, “who knew how to make with his hands every kind / of intricate work” (*Iliad* 5.60–61), and the carpenter knows “well all his craft by the promptings of Athena” (*Iliad* 15.411–412); in the Odyssey she gives the Phaeacian women their skill at the loom (7.110–111), and taught the daughters of Pandareus to make glorious works, with the verb of this simile, δέδαε (20.72). Odysseus sits apart on the shore, “shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (6.237), as the people gazed at Telemachus when she poured a wondrous grace on him (2.12–13), and Nausicaa, who had seen him come out of the thicket, sees him now “like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.243). The gift returns: she makes Odysseus taller and stouter before the Phaeacians' games (8.18–20) and before Penelope, where five verses of this book return (23.157–161 = 6.230–234), and Laertes after his bath (24.367–369); she makes Penelope taller and fuller for the suitors (18.195). On Ithaca she does the reverse, withering his skin and taking away his hair to make him an old beggar (13.429–433).

**In her grove.** At sunset they come to the grove that Nausicaa had described, “a splendid grove of Athena near the road, / of poplars” (6.291–292), and there Odysseus prays (see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[prayer]]):

{{quote:e2842380-ef94-57e3-b1d3-de8ef2ab1e17}}

The invocation is Penelope's prayer for her son in Book 4 and Diomedes' in the Iliad (4.762; *Iliad* 5.115; see [[hear-me]], [[atrytone]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]), and Odysseus used it himself in the night raid at Troy, praying to the goddess “you who always / stand by me in all my toils, and whatever I do / you see it” (*Iliad* 10.278–280). Now he says the opposite: she never listened when he was being wrecked. He does not know that she bound the winds and twice put the saving thought in his mind on the rocks of Scheria (5.382–387, 5.427, 5.437; see the note on Book 5). The last verse of the prayer is Priam's to Zeus as he sets out for Achilles' hut, “grant that I come to Achilles as one welcomed and pitied” (*Iliad* 24.309), with the Phaeacians for Achilles; Aristotle quotes it for the two things a speaker must aim at, to be liked and to be pitied (*Rhetoric* 1415b26–27; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]] and the translation's note on 6.327). “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” is the verse that follows Nestor's prayer to her at Pylos (6.328 = 3.385), and in the Iliad the prayers of Diomedes, of the two spies in the night, and of Odysseus himself in the footrace, where she made his limbs light (*Iliad* 5.121, 10.295, 23.771–772; see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**Not yet face to face.** She hears, and does not show herself: “for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; see [[poseidon]] and [[aidos]]). Apollo turns away from Poseidon in the battle of the gods with the same Greek words, αἴδετο γάρ ῥα / πατροκασιγνήτοιο, “for he was ashamed / to come to blows with his father's brother” (*Iliad* 21.468–469), and on Ithaca Athena tells Odysseus herself that she would not fight Poseidon, her father's brother, who was angry with him for the blinding of his son (13.341–343). Merry: she throws off this reserve as soon as Odysseus has landed in Ithaca and is beyond Poseidon's power, since it was the custom of the gods not to oppose one another openly (on 6.330), as Artemis tells Theseus that no god will set himself against the eager will of another (Euripides, *Hippolytus* 1328–1330; see the translation's note on 6.329–331). Until then she helps him unseen or in disguise. When he goes into the city she pours a mist about him and meets him in the likeness of a young girl carrying a water jar (7.14–20), and on Ithaca, when she has made herself known, she tells him that she is the goddess who always stands beside him in all his toils, the words of his prayer in the Iliad, and that she made him dear to all the Phaeacians, which is what he asked of her in the grove (13.299–302); he answers that he never saw her after Troy until in the land of the Phaeacians she encouraged him and led him into the city herself (13.316–323; see [[divine-epiphany]]).
