summary: Odysseus, naked on the shore, ponders “whether he should clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and beg her, / or, as he was, with soothing words, standing apart, / beg her”, and chooses words, “lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (6.141–147). He begins “I implore you, queen” (6.149), pleads that he is “terribly afraid / to clasp your knees” (6.168–169), and asks for pity, the city and a rag (6.175–179). Nausicaa receives him as “a much-tried suppliant” (6.193), and tells him to throw his arms around her mother's knees (6.310–315).

**A supplication without touch.** The suppliant's gesture is to clasp the knees of the one he begs (see [[gounata]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]), and a naked man, terrible to look at and crusted with brine, can hardly make that gesture to a girl. Odysseus weighs the two ways in the verse of deliberation: “whether he should clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and beg her, / or, as he was, with soothing words, standing apart, / beg her, if she would show him the city and give him clothes. / And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way, / to beg her with soothing words, standing apart, / lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (6.141–147; see [[decision-scene]], [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[kerdos]]). αὔτως is “as he was”, further defined by ἀποσταδά, “standing apart” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.143). Athenocles, a pupil of Aristarchus, suspected 6.144 for the repeated λίσσοιτο and because the only question is whether to clasp her knees or speak from a distance (the scholia, in Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.144).

**The words of the gesture.** He speaks “a soothing and shrewd word” (6.148), and the first word is the gesture he has not made: γουνοῦμαί σε, ἄνασσα, “I implore you, queen” (6.149). γουνοῦμαι is “take by the knees”. Elsewhere it is said by men who hold the knees in their hands, Lycaon clasping Achilles' knees with one hand and his spear with the other (*Iliad* 21.71–74), and Leodes and Phemius clasping Odysseus' knees in his hall (22.310–312, 22.342–344); here the word stands in place of the act. The speech that follows is built to win her: the praise of her beauty, the likeness to Artemis and to the palm on Delos, the felicitation of her parents and her husband-to-be (6.149–169; see [[artemis]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[sebas]]); then the plea, set on his fear, “I am terribly afraid / to clasp your knees; and hard grief has come upon me” (6.168–169; see [[deos]] and [[penthos]]), and on his sufferings at sea (6.170–174); then the request, “But pity me, queen” (6.175), the words of his prayer to the river, with “queen” for “lord” (5.450; see [[pity]] and [[anax]]). He asks for little, to be shown the city and given a rag to throw around him, the wrapping in which the washing was packed (6.178–179; Merry on 6.179), and ends with a blessing, a husband and a home and oneness of mind (6.180–185; see [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]).

**Received.** Nausicaa accepts him in the suppliant's own terms: he shall not lack clothing, “nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (6.192–193). ταλαπείριος, “much-tried”, Aristarchus took as “wretched”, others as a stranger “come from afar” (the scholia on 7.24, as Merry quotes them on 6.193; see the translation's note on 6.193). The same verse is Eumaeus' promise to the beggar (14.511), and Odysseus calls himself ξεῖνος ταλαπείριος, “a much-tried stranger”, when he asks Athena, in the likeness of a girl, the way to the palace (7.22–24, a plain rendering; see [[xenia]]).

**The knees of the queen.** The supplication that will win him his escort is still to come, and Nausicaa tells him how to make it: pass by her father on his chair and “throw your arms around the knees / of my mother, so that you may see the day of your homecoming” (6.310–311; see [[house]]), for if she thinks of him in friendship there is hope that he will reach his home (6.313–315; see [[nostos]]). He does as she says. Hidden in Athena's mist he goes through the hall and throws his arms around Arete's knees, and comes to her with the verse he spoke to the river, “to your husband and to your knees I come, after toiling much” (7.142–147, a plain rendering; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). His prayer to Athena at the end of the book asks for what a suppliant asks, to come to the Phaeacians “as one welcomed and pitied” (6.327; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]] and [[prayer]]).
