---
summary: The Rhetoric calls the last verse of Odysseus' prayer well said and takes from it the two aims of a proem. A fragment of the Homeric Problems explains from this book why Odysseus could tell the Phaeacians that he had blinded Poseidon's son; the Constitution of the Corcyraeans explains the name of Scheria, and the Constitution of the Ithacans married Nausicaa to Telemachus. The treatise On the Cosmos quotes the book's Olympus, the Latin Economics that bears Aristotle's name takes Odysseus' words to Nausicaa for a lesson in marriage, and a guest in Plutarch's Table Talk gives Aristotle's reason why she washed the clothes in the river and not in the sea.
---

- **Welcomed and pitied.** “And since it is well said, ‘Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied,’ these are the two things to aim at” in a proem (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415b25–28, quoting 6.327): [[welcomed-and-pitied]] and [[prooimion]].
- **The Phaeacians and the Cyclopes.** Why was Odysseus not afraid to tell the Phaeacians, who were descended from Poseidon, that he had blinded the Cyclops, the god's son? Aristotle answers that he knew them to be hostile to the Cyclopes, who “used to plunder them” (6.6); a second version of the problem adds that they had been driven out by the Cyclopes and had come to Scheria (6.4–8), and gives the words to Alcinous, where the poem tells them in its own voice (*Homeric Problems* fr. 396 Gigon, from the scholia on 9.333 and 9.345): [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]], [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[cyclopes]].
- **The name of Scheria.** In the *Constitution of the Corcyraeans* Aristotle explained why Corcyra was once called Scheria (6.8): Demeter, fearing that the rivers from the mainland would join the island to it, prayed to Poseidon to turn their streams back, and when they were checked the island was called Scheria instead of Drepane, “Sickle” (fr. 517 Gigon, from the scholia on Apollonius Rhodius 4.982–992 and from Stephanus of Byzantium). The scholium explains the older name by the sickle that Demeter had from Hephaestus to teach the Titans to reap, and hid on the island's shore: [[scheria]].
- **Nausicaa's marriage.** Eustathius sets beside the Hesiodic genealogy, in which Telemachus has a son by Polycaste, the report that Aristotle, in the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, and Hellanicus married Telemachus to Nausicaa, the daughter of Alcinous, and made her the mother of his son Perseptolis (fr. 512 Gigon, from Eustathius on 16.118): [[nausicaa]], [[telemachus]] and [[polycaste]].
- **Olympus.** The treatise *On the Cosmos*, which has come down among Aristotle's works but is generally thought not to be his, quotes the verses on Olympus, unshaken by winds and unwet by rain under a cloudless sky, for the pure region above the storms where god dwells (*On the Cosmos* 6, 400a, quoting 6.42–45): [[the-seat-of-the-gods-in-later-writers]] and [[olympus]].
- **A lesson in marriage.** The third book of the *Economics*, which survives only in Latin and whose Aristotelian authorship is doubtful, takes Odysseus' words to Nausicaa, “as I marvel at you, lady, and am astounded, and I am terribly afraid” (6.168), in its Latin “woman, I greatly wonder at you and fear you”, for the love joined with modest fear that Homer thought husband and wife should feel for each other, beside Helen's words to Priam (*Iliad* 3.172). His wish for her, “a husband and a home, and may they grant you oneness of mind, / a noble one” (6.181–182), shows that Homer honored above all the chaste partnership of marriage, and praised not any concord, but a good one. The last words of the wish, “and they themselves know it best” (6.185), it takes as a saying about hearing: men listen to him above all as one who speaks the truth (*Economics* 3, in Rose's Latin text, fr. 184, pp. 145–146; see the translation's note on 6.185): [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]], [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[nausicaa]].
- **The river and the sea.** Asked at a dinner in Plutarch's *Table Talk* why Homer made Nausicaa wash in the river and not in the sea, the grammarian Theon gives Aristotle's answer: the sea is salt from a rough, earthy matter scattered through it, while fresh water is light and fine, passes through the cloth and draws out the stains; and Aristotle says in the same book that bathers who come out of the sea dry less well in the sun than those who have used fresh water (*Physical Problems* fr. 724 Gigon, from Plutarch, *Table Talk* 1.9, on 6.85–95 and 6.218–226): [[washing-troughs]] and [[plutarch]].
