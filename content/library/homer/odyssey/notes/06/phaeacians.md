summary: The book introduces the Phaeacians. The poet tells how they came from Hypereia to Scheria (6.3–12); Nausicaa tells Odysseus that they are “very dear to the immortals”, living “far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men” (6.201–205), and that they care nothing for bow or quiver but glory in their ships (6.270–272); and she fears the talk of the overbearing men among them (6.273–288).

**Their past.** Athena goes “to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.3), and the poet pauses to tell who they are: a people who once lived in Hypereia, near the Cyclopes, who used to plunder them, until godlike Nausithous led them to Scheria, walled the city, built houses and temples and divided the fields; he is dead, and Alcinous rules (6.4–12; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]], [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]], [[cyclopes]], [[nausithous]], [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[alcinous]]).

**Their lords.** Athena, in the likeness of Nausicaa's friend, urges her to wash her clothes, “for already the best men in the land are wooing you, / of all the Phaeacians, where you yourself have your birth” (6.34–35; see [[nausicaa]]). ὅθι takes up δῆμον rather than ἀριστῆες, so that Athena reminds the girl that she is wooed by the noblest men of her own people (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.35; see the translation's note on 6.35). At dawn Alcinous is going out “to join the famous kings / at the council, to which the noble Phaeacians had called him” (6.54–55; see [[noble]] and [[boule]]); in the eighth book he counts twelve distinguished kings who rule as leaders throughout the land, and himself the thirteenth (8.390–391). The same lords are the suitors of the gossip Nausicaa imagines, “the Phaeacians, many and noble, who woo her” (6.284).

**Dear to the gods.** When her handmaids run from the naked stranger, Nausicaa calls them back:

{{quote:54655088-81f0-5088-a9ae-9ce22129e83b}}

οὗτος does not point at Odysseus: “That man exists not as a living mortal, nor ever will be born, who shall come as a foeman to the Phaeacians' land” (Merry on 6.201, after the scholia; see the translation's note on 6.201); when she comes to Odysseus himself she says ὅδε, “this” (6.206; Merry on 6.206). “Very dear to the immortals” says what ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods”, said in Zeus' words (5.35; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). ἐνὶ πόντῳ does not show that Scheria is an island, only that the Phaeacians live far across the sea (Merry on 6.204; see [[scheria]] and [[of-many-waves]]), and ἔσχατοι, “the farthest”, is said also of the Ethiopians, “the farthest of men” (1.23; Merry on 6.205; see [[ethiopians]]). No other mortal mingles with them, ἐπιμίσγεται, she says; and when Athena has poured grace on Odysseus she uses the verb again: “Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus / does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians” (6.240–241; see [[antitheos]]).

**Their ships.** Before the city she tells him of the harbors, the ships drawn up along the road, the place of assembly and the tackle, cables and sails and oars (6.262–269; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]), and then of the people:

{{quote:8a7b99e0-613f-531a-b1ce-89b6b6ba8818}}

Bow and quiver, the gear of the hunter and the archer, mean nothing to them (see [[bow]] and [[quiver]]); their pride is the ship (see [[ships]], [[mast]], [[oars]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[gray-sea]]). To do justice to ἀγαλλόμενοι, “in which they glory”, Merry recalls the personal friendship between the Phaeacians and their ships, which “themselves know the thoughts and minds of men” (8.559), and compares Hiawatha and his magic canoe (on 6.272). In the eighth book Alcinous owns that they are not flawless boxers or wrestlers, but swift runners and the best with ships (8.246–247).

**Their talk.** It is “their bitter talk” that Nausicaa shuns, “for there are very overbearing men in the land” (6.273–274; see [[overbearing]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). The baser man she imagines meeting them knows the Phaeacians' isolation as well as she does: the stranger must have come from far away, “since none live near us” (6.279; Merry on 6.279), unless a god has come down for her; it is better so, he sneers, since she dishonors the Phaeacians who woo her (6.280–284). Merry reads the end of the speech as bitter sarcasm: nothing in Phaeacia was good enough for her (on 6.282). The scholia report that the fourteen verses from 6.275 to 6.288 were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.275–288).

**His prayer.** The book ends with Odysseus' prayer in the grove of Athena: “Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied” (6.327; see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[athena]]). Aristotle quotes the verse for the two things a speaker's opening should aim at (*Rhetoric* 1415b26–28; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]] and [[prooimion]]).
