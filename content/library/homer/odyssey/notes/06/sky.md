summary: In Book 6 the sky is the gods' realm: they are “the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.150, 6.243), and the gossips Nausicaa fears would say that a god had come “down from the sky” to her at her prayer (6.280–281). Over Olympus, where Athena goes after the dream, the clear sky is spread wholly cloudless (6.44–45).
# αἴθρη "clear sky" (6.44), found again only at 12.75, the clear sky that never holds the peak of Scylla's rock. The
# Iliad's αἴθρην (17.646) and Αἴθρη, Helen's handmaid (Il. 3.144), are outside this note's reach.

en: [clear sky]

**The gods who hold the wide sky.** Odysseus' first question divides the world: “If you are one of the gods who hold the wide sky, / to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, / I liken you most nearly … but if you are one of the mortals who live upon the earth” (6.150–153; see [[wide-sky]], [[divine-epiphany]] and [[earth]]). Nausicaa, when Athena has made him beautiful, answers it without knowing: “before he seemed to me unsightly, / but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243). Three verses earlier she has said “the gods who hold Olympus” (6.240; see [[olympus]]): the two formulas name the same gods by their two homes.

**Down from the sky.** In the talk that Nausicaa imagines in the town, a base man guesses who the handsome stranger is: “or some god has come to her at her prayer, much prayed for, / down from the sky, and he will have her all his days” (6.280–281; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]] and [[prayer]]). οὐρανόθεν καταβάς is the gods' way to the earth, as Athena goes “down from the sky” to stop Achilles (*Iliad* 1.195). In ἤματα πάντα, “all his days”, W. W. Merry suspects an ironical glance at the gods' proverbial inconstancy toward their mortal loves (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.281; see the translation's note on 6.281).

**The clear sky of Olympus.** Athena goes back to Olympus, “where, they say, the seat of the gods stands / firm forever. It is not shaken by winds, nor ever / wet with rain, nor does snow come near it, but the clear sky / is spread wholly cloudless, and a white gleam runs over it” (6.42–45; see [[olympus]]). αἴθρη is the clear sky, the weather that Ajax begs of Zeus when mist hides the battle, “make clear sky, and grant us to see with our eyes” (*Iliad* 17.646). Rhianus read αἰθήρ, “upper air” (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.42–46 and [[upper-air]]). πέπταται, “is spread”, is said of light, as when the sharp light of the sun “was spread” over the men fighting at ease on the other part of the plain, “and no cloud was seen” (πέπτατο, *Iliad* 17.371–372; Merry on 6.45). The scholia found ὅθι φασί, “where, they say”, fitting if Olympus is the mountain, not if it is the sky itself (Merry on 6.42). The opposite place is Scylla's rock, whose sharp peak reaches the wide sky, wrapped in a dark cloud that never leaves it, “nor does clear sky ever hold its peak” (12.73–76, a plain rendering).
