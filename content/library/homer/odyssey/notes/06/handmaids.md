summary: Nausicaa's handmaids. Two sleep by the doors of her chamber (6.18–19); the others go with her to the river, wash the clothes, bathe, eat and play at ball with her, and flee from the naked stranger. Called back, they lay clothes and oil beside him, stand apart while he bathes, set food and drink before him, and walk behind the wagon with him to the grove (6.84–320). In the palace the queen sits among her women at the hearth, her slave women behind her (6.52, 6.307).

**In the chamber.** Nausicaa sleeps “and beside her two handmaids, who had their beauty from the Graces, / one on either side, by the doorposts; and the shining doors were closed” (6.18–19; see [[graces]] and [[nausicaa]]). So Penelope comes down among the suitors with two handmaids (1.331), and the passage shows that the maidens slept in their young mistress's room at night, probably on mattresses on the floor, so near the door that it could not be opened without waking them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.18). In the hall Nausicaa's mother sits at the hearth “with her handmaids” (6.52), and “her slave women sit behind her” (6.307; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**At the river.** The wagon carries Nausicaa “not alone; the other handmaids too went along with her” (6.84), the half-verse of Helen and Penelope, who go among men “not alone; two handmaids went along with her” (*Iliad* 3.143; 1.331). The oil is given “so that she might bathe and anoint herself, with her handmaids” (6.80; see [[oil-flask]]). They wash and tread the clothes in the pits, vying with one another, bathe and anoint themselves, and eat by the river (6.85–98; see [[washing-troughs]] and [[bathing]]); then “the slave women and she herself” play at ball, throwing off their veils (6.99–100; see [[ball]] and [[veil]]), and she stands out among them as Artemis among her nymphs (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). She throws the ball to one of them and misses, and their shout wakes Odysseus (6.115–117).

**Flight and return.** At the sight of the naked stranger “they fled in fear this way and that over the jutting beaches” (6.138), all but Nausicaa. τρέσσαν is “fled scared”: Aristarchus held that τρεῖν always has the notion of fleeing, though Merry finds it not strongly brought out in every passage (on 6.138). She calls them back, “Stand still, my handmaids: where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?” (6.199), and bids them give the stranger food and drink and bathe him in the river where there is shelter from the wind (6.209–210). “So she spoke, and they stood still and called to one another” (6.211); they set him in the shelter, lay a mantle and a tunic beside him with the oil in its golden flask, and bid him bathe himself (6.212–216), where Nausicaa had told them to “bathe him in the river” (6.210; Merry on 6.216). He will not bathe before them, “Handmaids, stand off there like that” (6.218–222; see [[aidos]]), “and they went apart and told the girl” (6.223).

**Her confidantes.** After Athena's grace Nausicaa speaks to them again: “Hear me, white-armed handmaids, so that I may tell you something” (6.239). It is to them alone that she wishes that such a man might be called her husband (6.240–245): she has no hearers but her own familiar handmaidens, Merry notes, since Odysseus sits apart (on 6.244; see [[nausicaa]]). “So she spoke, and they listened closely to her and obeyed, / and set food and drink beside Odysseus” (6.247–248).

**On the road.** Odysseus walks with them behind the mules and the wagon (6.260), and Nausicaa drives “just so that they could follow on foot, / the handmaids and Odysseus” (6.319–320; see [[odysseus]]).

**Their epithets.** ἐυπλόκαμοι, “of the lovely tresses” (6.198, 6.238; κούρῃσιν ἐυπλοκάμοισι, “girls of the lovely tresses”, 6.135, 6.222; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]), and λευκώλενοι, “white-armed” (6.239; see [[white-armed]]): epithets of goddesses and noble women, given here to a princess' girls.
