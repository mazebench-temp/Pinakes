**The founding.** Athena goes “to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.3), and the poet tells how the city came to be: Nausithous “drove a wall around the city, and built houses, / and made temples of the gods, and divided the fields” (6.9–10; see [[nausithous]], [[temple]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). The wall comes first, Merry explains, because walls mark the site and size of a city, and because the Phaeacians had learned from their old neighbors to live in a fenced city (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.9; see [[cyclopes]]).

**Nausicaa's description.** Nausicaa will lead the way through the fields with the wagon and the handmaids, and Odysseus is to follow (6.259–261; see [[nausicaa]]). Then she begins to tell what they will find:

{{quote:86193b54-7ba7-5a32-927a-484dffdf82f6}}

The sentence never finds its main clause, unless it is sought at 6.295, where her directions resume: the form of the sentence is forgotten as the description of the city lengthens (Merry on 6.262; see the translation's note on 6.262). Merry reads the city as a town on a peninsula, joined to the mainland only by a narrow neck, with a harbor on either side, and finds a remarkable illustration in Phoenician Tyre, built on an island off the coast and joined to the mainland by a causeway and the sand that gathered against it, with the Sidonian harbor to the north and the Egyptian to the south (on 6.263; see [[harbor]]). Aristophanes' reading εἰσίσθμη, suggested by ἰσθμός, “isthmus”, supports him (Merry on 6.263 and his critical note on 6.264; see the translation's note on 6.262–265). εἰσίθμη, “the way in”, from the root of ἰέναι, “go”, is found only here in Homer. Asteris too has harbors “with an entrance on either side” (4.846–847; see [[asteris]]).

**The ships and the slips.** νῆες ὁδὸν … εἰρύαται is “are drawn up along the road”, not “guard the road”, for guard ships seem un-Homeric (Merry on 6.264; see [[ships]] and [[curving]]). The clause with γάρ gives the reason: “for every man has a slip of his own” (6.265). ἐπίστιον, found only here, is “a word of most doubtful meaning”. Merry doubts that it is compounded of ἐπί and ἑστία, “hearth”, since Homer's compound of those is ἐφέστιος (7.248); Eustathius, he thinks, gives the right sense, a dockyard, and he would connect the word with ἐπίστασις, a “halting-place”. He pictures a line of open slips on which the ships were hauled, the road sloping gently down on either side to the water of the two harbors, marked off into divisions, one for each ship (on 6.265).

**The precinct of Poseidon.** “And there is their place of assembly, around the fine precinct of Poseidon” (6.266; see [[assembly]]). Ποσιδήιον is the neuter of the adjective Ποσιδήϊος, “Poseidon's”, used as a noun; the Catalogue's Onchestus is Ποσιδήϊον ἀγλαὸν ἄλσος, “the splendid grove of Poseidon” (*Iliad* 2.506; see [[onchestus]] and [[poseidon]]). The word is ἀμφίς, not περί, Merry explains, because the precinct does not stand in the middle of the place of assembly “like a garden in the middle of a square”: it breaks it in two, so that the assembly ground, which would otherwise stretch right across the neck of land, lies on both sides of the precinct (on 6.266). The god of the precinct is the father of the city's founder (7.56–62) and the giver of the Phaeacians' seafaring (7.34–35); he is also the god whose anger ends the book (6.329–331). Near the slips and the place of assembly the men take care of their ships' gear: “There they take care of the tackle of the black ships, / cables and sails, and they taper the oars” (6.268–269; see [[sails]] and [[oars]]).

**The houses.** Inside the city Odysseus is to ask for the house of Alcinous, “easy to know, and even a child could lead you, / a little one”, since the houses of the Phaeacians are not built like it (6.300–303; see [[alcinous]] and [[house]]). The clause οἷος δόμος Ἀλκινόοιο is an awkward explanation of ἐοικότα τοῖσι, “like it” (Merry on 6.301; see the translation's note on 6.301–303). The city is now πόλις, now ἄστυ, both “city”: Odysseus uses both in two successive verses, of the people “who hold this city and land” and in “Show me the city” (6.177–178), and Merry sees no intended distinction between them, though πόλις points to the settlement of a multitude and ἄστυ to a dwelling place (on 6.177–178; see [[polis]]).
