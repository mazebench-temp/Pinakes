---
summary: Odysseus asks whether the people of the land are “arrogant and savage and unjust” (οὐδὲ δίκαιοι, 6.120); Nausicaa promises him all “that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant” (6.193), for strangers and beggars are from Zeus (6.207–208).
en: [unjust]
---

δίκαιος is the man who gives others what is due, and in Odysseus' question what is due is the stranger's welcome: the unjust are set beside the arrogant and the savage, the just beside those who are kind to strangers and fear the gods (6.120–121; see [[hybris]] and [[xenia]]). Nausicaa's answer speaks in the same terms. ὧν ἐπέοιχʼ ἱκέτην, “that is fitting for a suppliant”, is what custom requires, and Merry compares the good cheer Achilles set before his guests, ἅ τε ξείνοις θέμις ἐστίν, “as is the custom with guests” (*Iliad* 11.779; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.193; see [[supplication]]). The ground of the custom is Zeus: strangers and beggars are πρὸς Διός, “from Zeus”, as the judges guard the ordinances that come from Zeus, θέμιστας / πρὸς Διὸς εἰρύαται (*Iliad* 1.238–239; Merry on 6.207; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).
