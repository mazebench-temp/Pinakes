summary: Nausicaa's dream is Athena herself, who comes to her bed like a breath of wind in the likeness of a friend, scolds her for her unwashed clothes, reminds her that her marriage is near and sends her to the washing troughs. The girl wakes at dawn and “marveled at the dream”, and goes to her father; of the marriage she says nothing.

**The goddess as the dream.** The god-sent dreams of the poems so far are figures that a god sends: Zeus sends the Dream to Agamemnon in the likeness of Nestor (*Iliad* 2.5–34; see [[agamemnons-dream]]), and Athena makes a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister and sends it to her bed (4.795–807; see [[eidolon]] and [[iphthime]]). Nausicaa's dream is the goddess in person. Athena “rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed, / and stood above her head and spoke to her, / in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships” (6.20–22; see [[athena]], [[dymas-the-phaeacian]], [[like-a-breath-of-wind]] and [[dream-scene]]), and when she had said what she came to say, she went back to Olympus, “when she had told the girl all” (6.47). The poem calls it a dream only when the girl wakes: “And at once Dawn of the fair throne came, and she woke her, / well-robed Nausicaa; and at once she marveled at the dream” (6.48–49; see [[nausicaa]] and [[of-the-fair-throne]]). ἀπεθαύμασε is “was lost in wonder at”, the preposition giving intensity, as Merry renders it, comparing Herodotus' ἀποθωμάσας τὸ λεχθέν, of Croesus wondering at Solon's answer (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.49; Herodotus 1.30).

**A dream with a purpose.** Like Agamemnon's dream, this one opens with a rebuke: “Nausicaa, how did your mother bear you so slack?” (6.25), where the Dream began “You are asleep, son of wise-hearted Atreus” (*Iliad* 2.23). Unlike his, it tells the truth, as far as it goes: the clothes are dirty, the best of the Phaeacians are wooing her (6.34–35; see [[marriage]]), and the washing will bring good talk (6.29–30). What it leaves out is its purpose: the goddess wants the girl at the river when Odysseus wakes (6.112–114). The marriage it holds before her is in her mind when, later on the shore, she wishes that such a man as the stranger might be called her husband (6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish]]). She goes through the house “to bring the news to her parents” (6.50), but asks her father only for the wagon, “for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father. But he understood everything” (6.66–67; see [[alcinous]] and [[aidos]]).
