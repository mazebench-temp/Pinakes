summary: Odysseus, who has nothing but a leafy branch, begs Nausicaa for a rag to throw around him (6.178–179); the handmaids lay a mantle and a tunic beside him for clothing, and he puts them on after his bath (6.214, 6.228).
refs: [6.228]
---

**A rag to throw around him.** Odysseus came out of the bushes holding a leafy branch before his nakedness (6.127–129; see [[odysseus]]), and his supplication ends with a modest request: “Show me the city, and give me a rag to throw around me, / if you had perhaps some wrapping for the clothes when you came here” (6.178–179; see [[supplication]]). The εἴλυμα σπείρων is the wrapper of coarse cloth in which the washing was packed (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.179, after the scholia; see the translation's note on 6.179 and [[washing-troughs]]). Nausicaa promises more: “you shall not lack clothing, nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (6.192–193; see [[much-tried]] and [[nausicaa]]).

**A mantle and a tunic.** “and beside him they laid a mantle and a tunic for clothing” (6.214; see [[tunic]] and [[handmaids]]). εἵματα stands in apposition to the two garments, “for raiment” (Merry on 6.214). They are the clothes of a guest after his bath, the mantle and tunic that Polycaste puts on Telemachus and the housekeeper on Odysseus at the end of the poem (3.467; 23.155; see [[bathing]]); here they are laid out before the bath, and he puts them on after it, “the clothes that the maiden unwed had given him” (6.228; see [[unbroken]]). They are the first clothes he has worn since he stripped off Calypso's garments in the sea (5.343, 5.372). In the next book Arete knows the mantle and the tunic, fine garments that she had made herself with her handmaids, and asks him who gave him these clothes (7.234–238, a plain rendering): the clothes from the washing bring on the stranger's story.
