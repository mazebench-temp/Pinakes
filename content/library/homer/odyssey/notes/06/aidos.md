---
summary: Three kinds of αἰδώς in one book. Nausicaa “was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father” (6.66–67); Odysseus will not bathe in front of the handmaids, “for I am ashamed / to strip naked when I have come among girls of the lovely tresses” (6.221–222); and Athena does not yet appear to him face to face, “for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330).
# αἴδετο (6.66, 6.329; 8.86), αἰδέομαι (6.221; 14.146, 17.188, 18.184, 20.343): all of this word.
grc: [αἴδετο, αἰδέομαι]
en: [ashamed, respected]
---

**A girl before her father.** Nausicaa asks her father for the mules and the wagon and gives every reason but the real one: his own clean clothes at the council, her brothers' at the dance, “and all these things are a care to my mind”. “So she spoke; for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father. But he understood everything” (6.57–67; see [[marriage]] and [[alcinous]]). The goddess had told her in the dream that her wedding was near (6.27). Merry suggests that θαλερός, if it is a fixed epithet of γάμος in the sense of “fruitful”, would further explain the αἰδώς that kept her silent (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.66). With the handmaids she is freer: “Would that such a man might be called my husband” (6.244), and Merry, siding with Ephorus against Plutarch's censure, points the contrast: in the presence of men, “even before her father”, she was ashamed to name her marriage (on 6.244; see [[posis]] and the translation's note on 6.244–245).

**A man before the girls.** Odysseus came out of the bushes holding a branch “to cover the man's nakedness” (μήδεα, 6.129, rendered with the English that the Iliad's translation gives αἰδώς, *Iliad* 2.262 and 22.75: see the table of key words), and when the handmaids would bathe him in the river he sends them off: “Handmaids, stand off there like that, so that I myself / may wash the brine from my shoulders, and with olive oil / anoint myself all over; for truly it is long since oil was on my skin. / But I will not bathe in front of you; for I am ashamed / to strip naked when I have come among girls of the lovely tresses” (6.218–222; see [[bathing]]). οὕτω is “yonder”, with a wave of the hand (Merry on 6.218). In Alcinous' palace the slave women will bathe and anoint him (8.454), and at home the housekeeper Eurynome (23.153–155); on the shore, among girls of the lovely tresses, he bathes alone. Penelope uses the same words when she will not go down among the suitors without two handmaids, “for I am ashamed” (αἰδέομαι γάρ, 18.182–184, a plain rendering).

**A goddess before her uncle.** Athena hears his prayer in her grove, “But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother; and he raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (6.329–331; see [[athena]] and [[poseidon]]). The words αἴδετο γάρ ῥα are Apollo's in the battle of the gods, when he will not fight Poseidon, “for he was ashamed / to come to blows with his father's brother” (*Iliad* 21.468–469; see [[kasignetos]]). It was the custom of the gods not to oppose one another openly, and Athena throws off her reserve only when Odysseus has landed in Ithaca, out of Poseidon's power (Merry on 6.330; see the translation's note on 6.329–331 and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Later in the poem the same verb is Odysseus' at the feast, ashamed before the Phaeacians to let them see his tears (8.86; see [[tears]]).
