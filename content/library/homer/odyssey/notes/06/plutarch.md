summary: In the Table Talk the question is put why Homer made Nausicaa wash the clothes in the river and not in the sea, and the company argue it out with Aristotle and with Homer's own Odysseus, who washes the brine from his shoulders in the river. In his essay on how the young should listen to poetry Plutarch, as Merry reports him, blamed the girl's wish for such a husband.

- **The river and the sea.** At a dinner in the house of Mestrius Florus the question is put why Homer made Nausicaa wash in the river and not in the sea, though the sea was near and would likely have been warmer, clearer and better for washing. The grammarian Theon answers with Aristotle: the sea is salt from a rough, earthy matter scattered through it, while fresh water, light and fine, passes through the cloth and draws out the stains. Plutarch finds the answer plausible but not true: fullers thicken their water with ash and natron, since earthy roughness scours out dirt the better, and the sea's sharpness should help the washing rather than hinder it; the cause is rather that the sea is oily, as Aristotle himself says, and that what is washed must dry, while salt water, held in the pores by its roughness, is hard to dry. When Theon objects that Aristotle says bathers who come out of the sea dry less well in the sun, the answer is drawn from Homer. Odysseus, come from the sea “afflicted by the brine” (6.137), tells the handmaids to stand away so that he may wash the brine from his shoulders, and washes it from his back and head in the river (6.218–226): the poet had observed that when men come out of the sea and stand in the sun, the heat carries off the finest and lightest part of the moisture, and the salt and rough part stays crusted on the body until fresh water washes it away (*Table Talk* 1.9, 626e–627f, printed among Aristotle's fragments as *Physical Problems* fr. 724 Gigon; on 6.85–95): [[washing-troughs]], [[the-river-of-scheria]], [[bathing]] and [[aristotle]].
- **Nausicaa's wish.** Plutarch, in his essay on how the young man should listen to poetry, was offended by Nausicaa's wish that such a man might be called her husband (6.244–245), and Merry quotes his words, that her boldness and her licentiousness are to be blamed (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.244): [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[nausicaa]].
