---
summary: The river of Book 6 is the river of Scheria at whose mouth Odysseus came ashore, now the place of the washing: the girls wash the clothes in its troughs, bathe, eat on its banks and play beside it until the ball falls into an eddy; Odysseus washes the brine from his body with its water, and when they set out for the city the mules leave its streams (6.59–97, 6.116, 6.209–224, 6.317). The nymphs of Odysseus' guess hold “the springs of rivers” (6.124).
# Unnamed: the deep eddy into which the ball falls (6.116).
refs: [6.116]
---

**The washing place.** Nausicaa asks her father for the wagon “so that I may take the glorious clothes / to the river to wash them, for they lie soiled” (6.58–59; see [[wagon]] and [[nausicaa]]), and the girls come “to the beautiful stream of the river, / where the washing troughs were” (6.85–86; see [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[washing-troughs]]). They turn the mules loose “along the eddying river” to graze (6.89; see [[eddying]] and [[grass]]), carry the clothes “into the dark water” (6.91), and eat “beside the banks of the river” (6.97). The Iliad has the same use of a river in time of peace: by the springs of the Scamander are wide washing troughs of stone, where the wives and daughters of the Trojans used to wash their glossy clothes “in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (*Iliad* 22.153–156; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). Achilles chases Hector past them; on Scheria the girls are at the troughs, and the war is far away.

**The eddy.** At the end of the game Nausicaa throws the ball to one of her handmaids, misses her, “and threw it into a deep eddy” (6.116; see [[ball]]). In the Iliad the eddies, δῖναι, are the Scamander's, where the routed Trojans swim this way and that and from which the river god speaks to Achilles (*Iliad* 21.11, 21.213; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]); here the eddy catches a girl's ball, and the girls' cry wakes Odysseus (6.117).

**The springs of rivers.** Waking, Odysseus wonders whether the cry he heard was of nymphs, “who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (6.123–124; see [[nymphs]], [[steep]] and [[grassy]]). The second verse is the Iliad's, where the nymphs of the groves, the springs and the meadows come to the assembly of the gods (*Iliad* 20.8–9); Nitzsch, as W. W. Merry reports, noted the repetition among his reasons for doubting the two verses here (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.122; see the translation's note on 6.122–125).

**The river washes away the sea.** Nausicaa bids her handmaids “bathe him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind” (6.210), and Odysseus, standing apart from them, “washed from his skin with the river water / the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders” (6.224–225; see [[bathing]] and [[sea]]). The river that brought him in out of the sea the day before (5.441–453) now washes the sea from his body. When they set out for the city, the mules “quickly left the streams of the river” (6.317).
