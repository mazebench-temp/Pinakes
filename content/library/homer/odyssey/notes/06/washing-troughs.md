summary: The washing is the errand of the book. Athena bids Nausicaa go at dawn to the washing troughs, far from the city, since her marriage is near (6.25–40); at the river the girls tread the clothes in the pits, vying with one another, spread them on the shingle and wait for them to dry (6.85–98).

  - 6.85-98 | The washing at the river
---

**The errand.** In the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, Athena scolds the sleeping girl: “Your glossy clothes lie uncared for, / and your marriage is near” (6.26–27), when she must wear fine clothes herself and give others to those who will lead her home; “But let us go to wash when dawn appears” (6.31; see [[nausicaa]], [[dymas-the-phaeacian]] and [[marriage]]). The fixed epithet stays with the clothes though they lie dirty, W. W. Merry notes, as the clothing that Nausicaa brings out of her chamber is “shining” before it has been washed (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.26; 6.74; see [[glossy]]). The troughs are “far from the city” (6.40), and so she is to ask her father for the mules and the wagon (6.36–40; see [[wagon]] and [[mules]]).

**The request.** To her father Nausicaa speaks of the washing and not of the marriage. The glorious clothes “lie soiled”, a king should take counsel “with clean clothes on your body”, and her five brothers “always want to have freshly washed clothes / when they go to the dance” (6.58–65; see [[alcinous]] and [[aidos]]). On Nausicaa, Merry says, the only daughter of the house, fell this part of the household's care, which she speaks of “in a tone of sportive seriousness” (on 6.65). Her father understands everything, and gives her the wagon (6.66–70).

**At the river.** The troughs are “unfailing”, and “much fair water / welled up and flowed through, to clean even what is very dirty” (6.86–87): tanks dug at the river's side, Merry thinks, open to it above and below, so that the water rose into them, passed on and ran out again (on 6.86; see the translation's note on 6.87 and [[the-river-of-scheria]]). When the mules have been turned loose to graze, the girls set to work:

{{quote:0411e4bc-beb2-5383-a266-4e67b9d1b114}}

ἔριδα προφέρουσαι, “vying with one another in strife”, makes the work a match, in words that the Iliad uses of the cranes who bring their strife against the Pygmies (*Iliad* 3.7; see the translation's row ἔριδα προφέρω). They spread the clothes where the sea scoured the pebbles on the beach, almost a fixed line, Merry notes, since there is hardly any tide in the Mediterranean; the variant ἀποπτύεσκε would make the sea throw up the shingle instead (on 6.95; see the translation's note on 6.95 and [[shore]]). Then they bathe and anoint themselves, take their meal by the river, “and waited for the clothes to dry in the rays of the sun” (6.96–98; see [[bathing]], [[feast]] and [[helios]]), and play at ball until it is time to go home (6.99–101; see [[ball]]).

**The clothes folded.** “yoking the mules and folding the fine clothes” (6.111) is what Nausicaa has in mind when Athena devises Odysseus' waking; the folding itself waits until he has eaten, when “She folded the clothes and put them on the fine wagon” (6.252; Merry on 6.110; see the translation's note on 6.110–111). The washed clothes have become the stranger's: the handmaids lay a mantle and a tunic beside him (6.214; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]), and in the next book Arete knows them as the garments she made with her women (7.234–235).

**The troughs of Troy.** Merry compares the washing tanks of the Trojan women at the springs of the Scamander, which the Iliad calls “fair, and of stone” (on 6.40; *Iliad* 22.153–156; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]).
