summary: Athena tells Nausicaa that from fine clothes “talk goes up among men, / good talk” (6.29–30). At the end of the day Nausicaa tells the stranger that she shuns the bitter talk of the overbearing Phaeacians, quotes what some baser man would say if he met them together, and ends, “So they will say, and this would be a reproach to me” (6.273–288).
# 6.30: "good talk", the second half of the phrase of 6.29.

**Good talk.** Athena, in the likeness of Dymas' daughter, sends Nausicaa to the washing with the thought of her wedding and of what people will say of it: “For it is from these things that talk goes up among men, / good talk, and your father and lady mother rejoice” (6.29–30; see [[marriage]]). ἐκ τούτων is “from such sumptuous style”, the fine clothes she must wear herself and give to those who will lead her home (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.27, 6.29). Callistratus read χάρις, the pleasure of those who see it, and said that Aristophanes had changed it to φάτις (the scholia, in Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.29).

**Bitter talk.** The same girl, at the end of the day, tells the stranger why he must not come into the city with her: “It is their bitter talk I shun, lest someone hereafter / should blame me; for there are very overbearing men in the land” (6.273–274). φῆμιν ἀδευκέα is “bitter talk”, as ὀλέθρῳ ἀδευκέι is “a bitter destruction” (4.489; see the translation's table of key words), and ὀπίσσω is “hereafter”, not “behind my back” (Merry on 6.273). μωμεύω, “blame”, is found only here in Homer; Helen's fear of the Trojan women has the verb μωμάομαι, “the Trojan women would all hereafter / blame me” (*Iliad* 3.411–412). The overbearing men are her own people (see [[phaeacians]] and [[overbearing]]).

**What a baser man would say.** She gives the talk a voice: “and this is what some baser man might say, if he met us” (6.275), ἀντιβολήσας, if he met the two of them together (Merry on 6.275). Who is this tall and handsome stranger following Nausicaa? Where did she find him (ξεῖνος stands first, “a stranger?”, Merry on 6.277)? He will be her husband; perhaps she has taken in some wanderer from his ship, or some god has come down at her prayer, and he will have her all his days. It is better so, if she has found a husband from elsewhere, since she dishonors the many noble Phaeacians who woo her (6.276–284; see [[posis]]). Merry hears bitter sarcasm in the end, as if nothing in Phaeacia were good enough for her (on 6.282), and an ironical glance at the gods' inconstancy toward their mortal loves in “all his days” (on 6.281). The speech is the imagined speech of “someone” (see [[tis-speech]]), and it is framed as Hector frames his: “lest some day another man, baser than I, should say”, and after the words, ὣς ἐρέουσιν, “So they will say” (*Iliad* 22.106–108; compare καί νύ τις ὧδʼ εἴπῃσι κακώτερος, 6.275, and ὣς ἐρέουσιν, 6.285).

**Her own judgment.** “So they will say, and this would be a reproach to me. / And I would be indignant with another girl who did such things” (6.285–286; see [[oneidos]] and [[nemesis]]). She measures herself by the blame she would give another, as Achilles tells Ajax and Idomeneus that they would be indignant with another man who behaved as they do (*Iliad* 23.494; Merry on 6.286): a girl who, against the will of her father and mother, still living, mingled with men before she came openly to her marriage (6.287–288; see [[aidos]]). Merry takes the parents as the “friends” of the verse: the inexperience of a girl with no one to teach her would be pardonable, but not a breach of modesty in defiance of her home (on 6.287). The scholia report that the fourteen verses from 6.275 to 6.288 were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 6.275–288).

**The plan it shapes.** The fear governs her instructions. He is to follow the wagon with the handmaids only while they cross the fields, then sit in the grove of Athena until she has reached her father's house, and come into the city by himself and ask the way (6.259–261, 6.289–299; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]). At Alcinous' table the king will blame his daughter for not bringing the stranger home with her handmaids, and Odysseus will take the plan on himself: she told him to follow, but he would not, from fear and shame, lest the king be angry at the sight, “for we tribes of men upon the earth are quick to resentment” (7.299–307, plain renderings; see [[alcinous]]).
