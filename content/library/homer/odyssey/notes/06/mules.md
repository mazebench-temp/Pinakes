summary: The mules draw Nausicaa's wagon. Her father does not grudge them (6.68); the slaves yoke them, and they strain on to the river, where they are turned loose to graze on the honey-sweet grass while the clothes are washed (6.72–90); at evening they run well toward the city, at a pace that the walkers can keep (6.253, 6.316–320).

**The mules of the wagon.** Athena bids Nausicaa ask her father for “the mules and the wagon” (6.37), and Alcinous answers, “I do not grudge you the mules, my child, nor anything else” (6.68; see [[alcinous]]). The slaves make ready “the well-wheeled mule wagon, / and led the mules under and yoked them beneath the wagon” (6.72–73; see [[wagon]]). As Nausicaa drives, “there was a clatter of the mules, / and they strained on without end” (6.82–83): ἄμοτον, “without end”, is the Iliad's word for an unflagging pace (see the translation's note on 6.83), and τανύοντο, “strained”, is said of the horses of the Trojans' chariots in their flight (*Iliad* 16.375). At the troughs the girls unyoke them, “and drove them along the eddying river / to graze on the honey-sweet grass” (6.89–90; see [[the-river-of-scheria]], [[grass]] and [[washing-troughs]]).

**The drive home.** Nausicaa thinks of going home, “yoking the mules and folding the fine clothes” (6.111), and when Odysseus has eaten she does so: she “yoked the strong-hoofed mules”, the words of Priam's mules in the Iliad (6.253, as *Iliad* 24.277; see [[strong-hoofed]]). The stranger is to follow “behind the mules and the wagon” with the handmaids (6.260). Then “with the shining whip she lashed / the mules; and they quickly left the streams of the river. / And they ran well, and well they stepped out with their feet” (6.316–318). Eustathius set the two verbs against each other, a brisk pace and a walk, “And well they trotted, and well they walked”; W. W. Merry finds no change of pace, and takes πλίσσοντο as explaining τρώχων, “they sped on with prancing feet” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.318; see the translation's note on 6.318). Callistratus read τρεχέτην (Merry's critical note). In the next book the strength of the mules carries the girl to the city, and her brothers unyoke them at the house (7.2–6).
