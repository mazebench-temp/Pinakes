summary: The heart of her book. Athena, in the likeness of her friend, the daughter of Dymas, sends her in a dream to wash her clothes, since her marriage is near; she asks her father for the wagon, ashamed to name her marriage, washes at the river and plays at ball, standing out among her handmaids like Artemis among the nymphs. When the naked stranger comes out of the thicket she alone stands her ground, answers his supplication, has him fed, bathed and clothed, wishes for such a husband, and sends him to her father's house and her mother's knees, for fear of the town's talk (6.15–321).

**The dream.** She sleeps in the intricately worked chamber, “like the immortals in stature and in looks”, with two handmaids by the doorposts and the shining doors closed (6.15–19; see [[handmaids]]). Athena “rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed, / and stood above her head and spoke to her, / in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships, / who was of an age with her, and dear to her heart” (6.20–23; see [[like-a-breath-of-wind]], [[dream-scene]], [[dymas-the-phaeacian]] and [[athena]]). The goddess speaks as a friend of her own age would: “Nausicaa, how did your mother bear you so slack? / Your glossy clothes lie uncared for, / and your marriage is near” (6.25–27). W. W. Merry renders the first verse “Why hath thy mother such a lazy daughter in thee?”, and hears in it a contrast between the thrifty housewifery of the queen and the idleness of the princess (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.25). At her wedding she must wear fine clothes herself and give others to “those who will lead you home” (6.28): the bridegroom's family, or more likely those who led a bride to her new home by torchlight, as in the city at peace on the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.491–493), a pageant to which the bride could add by her gifts of clothing (Merry on 6.28; see [[marriage]]). Good clothes make good talk, and her parents rejoice (6.29–30; see the translation's note on 6.29). The suitors are the reason: “you will not be a maiden much longer; / for already the best men in the land are wooing you, / of all the Phaeacians, where you yourself have your birth” (6.33–35), the noblest of her own people (see the translation's note on 6.35 and [[phaeacians]]). The goddess promises to come and help, bids her ask her father for the mules and the wagon, since the washing troughs are far from the city, and goes back to Olympus (6.31–47; see [[wagon]] and [[olympus]]). For the whole episode, see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]].

**Her father.** At dawn she marvels at the dream and goes through the house to tell her parents. Her mother sits at the hearth among her women, spinning (see [[hearth]]); her father she meets as he goes out to the council of the kings, hastening down the hall to catch him at the door (6.48–55; Merry on 6.54; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[alcinous]]). She stands very close to him and asks:

{{quote:9a0482ae-f178-593c-9a52-245e106b1d92}}

οὐκ ἂν δή … ἐφοπλίσσειας is “a tentative, beseeching, form of question”, “Could you not get me ready?” (Merry on 6.57), and πάππας, “papa”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's key words). Her reasons are all the men's: a king among the foremost should sit in council in clean clothes, and her five brothers want freshly washed clothes for the dance. On her, the only daughter of the house, fell this part of the household's care, as she says “in a tone of sportive seriousness” (Merry on 6.65). The reason the dream gave her she keeps to herself: “So she spoke; for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father. But he understood everything, and answered her” (6.66–67; see [[aidos]]). θαλερός is said of young men in the flower of their age, and may pass to marriage, “marriage in her maiden-prime”, or it may be a fixed epithet of marriage, “fruitful”, which would further explain her shame (Merry on 6.66). He grants her the mules, “nor anything else” (6.68), and her mother packs food and wine and gives her the olive oil (6.76–80; see [[oil-flask]]).

**At the river.** She drives the mules herself: “And she took the whip and the glossy reins, / and whipped them to a run” (6.81–82; see [[mules]]). At the troughs the girls tread the clothes in the pits, “vying with one another in strife”, spread them on the shingle, bathe, anoint themselves and eat by the river while the clothes dry (6.85–98; see [[washing-troughs]], [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[bathing]]). Then “they played with a ball, throwing off their veils; / and among them white-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance” (6.100–101). The scholiast takes μολπή here for the game itself; Merry sees no reason to doubt that a measured chant and a dance went with it, the throwing and catching of the ball keeping time, as in the ball dance of her brothers before Odysseus (8.370–380; Merry on 6.101; see [[ball]]). The narrator likens her to the goddess of the wild:

{{quote:e782403c-e446-5763-80f0-de37e83064c1}}

She is easy to know among them, as Artemis is among the nymphs, though all are beautiful (see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]], [[artemis]] and [[nymphs]]).

**The ball in the eddy.** When she is ready to go home, Athena plans “that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl, / who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.113–114). “So then the princess flung the ball to one of her handmaids; / she missed the handmaid, and threw it into a deep eddy” (6.115–116). Nausicaa is the subject of both verbs (Merry on 6.116); on the other reading, ἔμπεσε, “fell”, see the translation's note on 6.116, and on the game Eustathius supposed she was playing, the note on 6.115. The girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.117).

**She stands her ground.** When the naked man comes out of the thicket, terrible and crusted with brine, the handmaids flee this way and that, but “only the daughter of Alcinous stood her ground; for Athena / put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs. / She checked herself and stood facing him” (6.139–141; see [[tharsos]] and [[deos]]). σχομένη is “having checked herself from flight” (Merry on 6.141). Odysseus keeps his distance, “lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (6.147; see [[odysseus]] and [[supplication]]), and his speech is all praise of her: if she is a goddess, she is Artemis; if a mortal, three times blessed are her parents and her brothers, who watch “such a scion going into the dance”, and most blessed the man who wins her with his bride-price; he has seen nothing like her but the young palm by Apollo's altar on Delos; may the gods give her a husband, a home and oneness of mind (6.149–185; see [[three-times-blessed]], [[bride-price]], [[the-palm-on-delos]] and [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]).

**Her answer.** She answers as a king's daughter. He seems neither base nor witless; it is Zeus who deals out prosperity to good and bad as he wishes, and he must endure what Zeus has given him; but now that he has come to their land he shall lack neither clothing nor anything a much-tried suppliant should have (see [[much-tried]]), and she will show him the city and tell him the people's name: “and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, / on whom the strength and the force of the Phaeacians depend” (6.187–197; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[xenia]]). Merry reads her reasoning in the sentence that her parenthesis breaks: he looks neither a villain nor a fool, only miserable, and misery like prosperity is dealt out to men by no rule but the will of the gods; besides, he has come to their shores as a suppliant; therefore she will befriend him (on 6.187). Then she calls back her handmaids, “Stand still, my handmaids: where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?” (6.199). No enemy comes to the Phaeacians, dear to the immortals and living far apart in the sea; this is an unhappy wanderer, “for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear” (6.200–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]). Food and drink, and a bath in the river where there is shelter from the wind, she leaves to her women (6.209–216), and when he asks them to stand apart they go and tell her (6.218–223).

**Her wish.** When Athena has made him taller and poured grace on him, he sits apart on the shore, “and the girl gazed at him” (6.236–237). She tells her handmaids that he does not come among the godlike Phaeacians against the will of the gods: “for before he seemed to me unsightly, / but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky. / Would that such a man might be called my husband, / living here, and that it might please him to stay here” (6.240–245; see [[posis]]). Aristarchus athetized the last two verses, hesitating over the first, since Alcman had taken it over, bringing in maidens who say it; Plutarch blamed her boldness, and Ephorus praised the words (see the translation's note on 6.244–245 and [[nausicaas-wish]]). Merry sides with Ephorus: they are the thoughts of an innocent girl, to whom marriage was a certainty and not a mere possibility, and she speaks them only to her own handmaids, for Odysseus is apart; before men she is very different, and even to her father she was ashamed to name her marriage (on 6.244, citing 6.66).

**Her instructions.** She folds the clothes, yokes the mules and mounts (6.251–253), and gives the stranger the longest speech of her book (6.255–315). Through the fields he is to follow the wagon with the handmaids; she describes the city with its rampart, its harbors and its ships, a people who care for ships and not for bows (6.259–272; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[phaeacians]]); and she explains why he must not come in with her: “It is their bitter talk I shun, lest someone hereafter / should blame me” (6.273–274; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). She puts the talk in the mouth of some baser man who might meet them: “Who is this following Nausicaa, so handsome and tall, / a stranger? Where did she find him? He will be her own husband, surely”, a wanderer from some ship or a god come down at her prayer, and better so, since she scorns the many and noble Phaeacians who woo her (6.275–284). Merry hears bitter sarcasm in it, “see how the fastidious Nausicaa has been husband-hunting” (on 6.282); the scholia report that the fourteen verses 6.275–288 were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (see the translation's note on 6.275–288). She ends with her own judgment: she would be indignant with another girl who, against the will of her father and mother, still living, “mingled with men before she came openly to her marriage” (6.286–288; see [[nemesis]], [[oneidos]] and [[marriage]]). The inexperience of a friendless girl would be pardonable, Merry explains, but not the boldness of one who defies the influences of her home (on 6.287). Her “quickly” (6.289) means that evening is drawing on, and Merry observes that she has not spared time in putting her case about the gossip (on 6.289). She sends Odysseus to wait in Athena's grove by her father's domain, then to find her father's house, which even a child could show him, to pass her father by on his chair and clasp her mother's knees (6.289–315; see [[the-grove-of-athena]], [[alcinous]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The drive.** “And she drove just so that they could follow on foot, / the handmaids and Odysseus, and she laid on the whip with judgment” (6.319–320; Merry on 6.319). At sunset they come to Athena's grove, where Odysseus sits down (6.321–322); while he prays there, the mules carry her on to the city and her father's house (7.1–3).
