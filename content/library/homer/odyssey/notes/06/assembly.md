---
summary: The Phaeacians' ἀγορή is a place in their city, “around the fine precinct of Poseidon, / fitted with hauled stones set deep in the earth” (6.266–267).
---

ἀγορή here is the place, not the gathering: “And there is their place of assembly, around the fine precinct of Poseidon, / fitted with hauled stones set deep in the earth” (6.266–267; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[poseidon]]). It lies on both sides of the precinct, which breaks it in two across the neck of land on which the city stands (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.266). ῥυτοῖσιν λάεσσι are “hauled” stones, too big to be carried, like those of the wall around Eumaeus' yard (14.10), and κατωρυχέεσσι, “set deep in the earth”, is the word for the stones of the Cyclops' yard (9.185), from which, Merry notes, the name of “Cyclopean” building comes. He does not think the place was paved with them, for paving belongs to Roman road-making: they made a low wall around it, and perhaps its seats within (Merry on 6.267; see the translation's note on 6.267), as at the assembly of the eighth book, where the Phaeacians sit “on polished stones” (8.5–6, a plain rendering). The Cyclopes, the Phaeacians' old neighbors, have “no assemblies that bring counsel” (9.112, a plain rendering; see [[cyclopes]]).

The book has the council as well. At dawn Alcinous goes out “to join the famous kings / at the council, to which the noble Phaeacians had called him” (6.54–55), and Nausicaa tells him that he should have clean clothes when he takes counsel among the foremost (6.60–61; see [[boule]] and [[alcinous]]).
