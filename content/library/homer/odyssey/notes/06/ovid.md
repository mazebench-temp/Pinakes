---
summary: Ovid's Salmacis greets the boy Hermaphroditus with the praise that Odysseus gives Nausicaa: if he is a god, he may be Cupid; if a mortal, blessed are his parents, happy his brother and his sister and the nurse who suckled him, and far more blessed than all his bride.
---

- “Boy, most worthy to be thought a god, if you are a god, you may be Cupid; or if you are mortal, blessed are those who begot you, and happy your brother, and fortunate indeed your sister, if you have one, and the nurse who gave you the breast; but far, far more blessed than all is she, if there is one betrothed to you, if you think anyone worthy of the marriage torch” (*qui te genuere, beati, / et frater felix, et fortunata profecto, / si qua tibi soror est, et quae dedit ubera nutrix; / sed longe cunctis longeque beatior illa, / si qua tibi sponsa est, si quam dignabere taeda*, *Metamorphoses* 4.320–326, a plain rendering). Merry sets the verses beside Odysseus' first words to Nausicaa, “are you some goddess, or a mortal?”, with his “three times blessed are your father and your lady mother” and the most blessed man who will lead her home (6.149–159; Merry on 6.149, who cites the passage as *Metamorphoses* 4.312). The parts are reversed: the speaker is the nymph who dwells in the pool (*Metamorphoses* 4.302), the one she praises a boy, the son of Mercury and Venus, and she goes on to offer herself as his bride (*Metamorphoses* 4.327–328): [[three-times-blessed]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]].
