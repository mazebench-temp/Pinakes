summary: The nymphs appear twice. In the simile of the game they are the company of Artemis, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, / who haunt the fields”, at play on the mountains; and when the girls' cry wakes Odysseus he wonders whether it is the cry of nymphs, “who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (6.123–124), or of mortals.

**The company of Artemis.** In the simile, Artemis goes over the mountains “and with her the nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, / who haunt the fields, play, and Leto is glad at heart” (6.105–106; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]], [[artemis]] and [[leto]]). κοῦραι Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο is their title in both poems: the mountain nymphs planted elms around the tomb of Eetion (*Iliad* 6.419–420), and on the island off the land of the Cyclopes the nymphs rouse the mountain goats for Odysseus' men (9.154–155; see [[zeus]]). ἀγρονόμοι, “who haunt the fields”, is rightly accented on the last syllable but one, since νέμειν and νέμεσθαι are used of haunting a place; some put the accent a syllable earlier, and one scholium proposed ἄγραν νέμουσαι, “who range over the chase”; Merry compares the nymphs of the Iliad's assembly of the gods, “who dwell in the lovely groves” (αἵ τʼ ἄλσεα καλὰ νέμονται, *Iliad* 20.8), and takes παίζουσι, “play”, with the notion of hunting (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.106; see the translation's note on 6.106). The nymphs are the handmaids' part in the simile, as Artemis is Nausicaa's.

**Nymphs or mortals?** When the ball falls into the eddy and the girls shout, Odysseus wakes and wonders where he is: “It is as though a female cry of girls came round me, / of nymphs, who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows. / Am I perhaps somewhere near men with human voice?” (6.122–125; see [[odysseus]] and [[with-human-voice]]). The simile has just set the girls beside the nymphs, and the man who hears them makes the same comparison. ὥς τε goes with κουράων, “as it were the voice of girls”, and κουράων is defined by νυμφάων (Merry on 6.122). The places he names are the places of nymphs in Homer: 6.124 is a verse of the Iliad's assembly of the gods, where no nymph stays away of those who dwell “in the lovely groves / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (*Iliad* 20.8–9), and of the *Hymn to Aphrodite*, where Anchises asks the disguised goddess whether she is one of the Graces or of the nymphs (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 95–99; see [[graces]]). Merry suggests that the voice of nymphs might itself suggest mortals near by, since men set up altars to the nymphs and offered them sacrifices (on 6.122; 13.350, 17.210–211). Nitzsch objected that a cry of nymphs would give little hint that the place was inhabited, and noted that 6.124 is the Iliad's verse; many modern editors suspect or reject 6.123–124, which the scholia accept (Merry on 6.122; see the translation's note on 6.122–125). The cry comes from a game of girls, and the one who stands her ground before him is the daughter of Alcinous (6.137–141).
