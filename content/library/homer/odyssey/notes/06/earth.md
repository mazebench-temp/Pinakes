summary: In Book 6 the earth is first of all a land: the unknown land Odysseus wakes in (6.119), the city and land that the Phaeacians hold (6.177, 6.191, 6.195, 6.202), the fields that Nausithous divided (6.10), and the native land he still has to reach (6.315, 6.331). It is also the earth on which mortals live (6.153) and from which the palm of Delos came up (6.167).
# ἀρούρας "the fields" (6.10), claimed in the Odyssey only now; it also takes 19.433, the fields the sun strikes at dawn

**The land of what mortals?** Odysseus wakes to the girls' cry with the question he will ask again on Ithaca: “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?” (6.119 = 13.200; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). He tells Nausicaa that he knows no one “of the people who hold this city and land” (6.177), and she answers him in the same words: “now, since you have come to our city and our land” (6.191), and “The Phaeacians hold this city and land” (6.195; see [[phaeacians]] and [[scheria]]). The pair πόλις καὶ γαῖα, “city and land”, is the town and the country around it, and no man, Nausicaa says, will ever “come to the land of the Phaeacian men / bringing combat” (6.202–203).

**The fields.** The founder of the city, Nausithous, “divided the fields” when he led his people to Scheria (ἐδάσσατʼ ἀρούρας, 6.10; see [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). The ἄρουραι are the plowland of the new country, given out with the wall, the houses and the temples.

**Mortals on the earth.** Odysseus sets the earth against the sky in his question to Nausicaa: is she one of the gods “who hold the wide sky”, or “one of the mortals who live upon the earth” (6.150, 6.153; see [[sky]] and [[divine-epiphany]])? And his wonder at the young palm on Delos was that “never yet had such a tree come up from the earth” (6.167; see [[the-palm-on-delos]]).

**The native land.** Nausicaa ends her instructions with a hope: if her mother is kind to him, “then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your well-built house and your native land” (6.314–315, the second verse as Proteus' words to Menelaus, 4.476; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The three verses of the hope are wanting in several manuscripts, and their place is in the next book (7.75–77; see the translation's note on 6.313–315). The last verse of the book returns to the land from the god's side: Poseidon raged furiously “against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (6.331 = 1.21; see [[poseidon]]).
