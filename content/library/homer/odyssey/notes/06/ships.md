summary: The ships of the Phaeacians, as Nausicaa describes their city: the curving ships drawn up along the road, each man with a slip of his own, their tackle cared for at the place of assembly, a people who care nothing for bows but glory in their ships as they cross the sea (6.264–272).
passages:
  - 6.262-272 | The city and its ships
---

**Ships along the road.** The city has a fine harbor on either side, “and the way in is narrow; and the curving ships are drawn up along the road; / for every man has a slip of his own” (6.263–265; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]], [[curving]] and [[harbor]]). εἰρύαται could mean that the ships “guard” the road, but guard ships seem un-Homeric to W. W. Merry, who renders “are drawn up along the road”, as the Iliad uses the same verb of the ships drawn up on the shore (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.264; *Iliad* 4.247–248, 18.68–69; see the translation's note on 6.262–265). ἐπίστιον, found only here, is the slip, the berth of each man's ship, and γάρ gives the reason why the ships lie along the road (Merry on 6.265; see the translation's row ἐπίστιον).

**A people of ships.** “There they take care of the tackle of the black ships, / cables and sails, and they taper the oars. / For the Phaeacians care nothing for bow or quiver, / but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships, / in which they glory as they cross the gray sea” (6.268–272; see [[stern-cables]], [[sails]], [[oars]], [[mast]], [[bow]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[gray-sea]]). To do justice to ἀγαλλόμενοι, Merry says, one must remember the friendship between the Phaeacians and their famous ships, which in Alcinous' words know the thoughts and minds of men and need no steersmen (on 6.272; 8.557–559, a plain rendering). The gossip that Nausicaa fears imagines the stranger as “some wanderer from his ship, / a man from a far-off people” (6.278–279; see [[phaeacians]]), and it is a Phaeacian ship that will carry him home (13.70–125).
