---
summary: Nausicaa, having quoted the talk she fears, turns it on herself: “And I would be indignant with another girl who did such things, / who against the will of her friends, her father and mother, still living, / mingled with men before she came openly to her marriage” (6.286–288).
---

The indignation Nausicaa fears from the town is the indignation she would feel herself. She has given the gossip a voice, “So they will say, and this would be a reproach to me” (6.285; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]] and [[oneidos]]), and then she judges by her own measure: καὶ δʼ ἄλλῃ νεμεσῶ, ἥ τις τοιαῦτά γε ῥέζοι (6.286). Merry compares Achilles' word to Ajax and Idomeneus at the chariot race, “You would be indignant with another man who did such things” (καὶ δʼ ἄλλῳ νεμεσᾶτον, *Iliad* 23.494), and notes the change of mood from ῥέζοι to μίσγηται: the optative puts a purely hypothetical case, the subjunctive a particular one (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.286). What she would blame is a girl who goes among men against the will of her father and mother, “still living”, before her marriage is made openly (6.287–288; Merry on 6.287; see [[marriage]] and [[aidos]]). νέμεσις is the onlooker's judgment, and αἰδώς the feeling that keeps one from deserving it: the girl who will not name her marriage to her father (6.66) is the one who will not be seen coming into the city with a stranger (see [[nausicaa]]). The scholia report that the verses from 6.275 to 6.288 were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (the translation's note on 6.275–288).
