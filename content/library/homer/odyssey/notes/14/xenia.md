summary: The book of the swineherd's welcome. Eumaeus will not “dishonor a stranger”, since “they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars” (14.57–58); the beggar swears by “the table of hospitality” (14.158); in his tale the king of Egypt spares him for fear of “the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.283–284); and Eumaeus, who will not believe its news of his master, keeps him all the same “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (14.389).
# ξενίη (14.158; again 17.155, 20.230, and ξενίῃ 24.286, 24.314) and ξένιον (14.389) are the ξεν- spellings of Murray's text,
# where the other forms of the word have ξειν-; the Iliad has none.

**Strangers and beggars.** The swineherd's first words are the rule he lives by: “Stranger, it is not lawful for me, not even if one more wretched than you should come, / to dishonor a stranger; for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear, / when it comes from us” (ξεῖνʼ, οὔ μοι θέμις ἔστʼ … ξεῖνον ἀτιμῆσαι, 14.56–59; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]], [[themis]] and [[time]]). The second and third verses are Nausicaa's to her maids (6.207–208). The welcome keeps the order of the custom: Eumaeus seats the stranger on his own bedding (14.49–51), feeds him (14.72–79), and asks who he is only when he has eaten and drunk (14.45–47, 14.185–190; see [[visit-scene]] and [[who-are-you-among-men]]). The guest has already answered with the same word, calling his host ξεῖνε: “May Zeus grant you, stranger, and the other immortal gods, / what you most desire, since you have received me gladly” (14.53–54). Each is the other's ξεῖνος, and the stranger in the hut is its master (14.36; see [[anax]]).

**The table of hospitality.** The beggar who offers his oath swears by the meal he has eaten: “Now let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the table of hospitality, / and the hearth of noble Odysseus, to which I have come” (ξενίη τε τράπεζα, 14.158–159; see [[oath]], [[table]], [[hearth]] and [[let-zeus-be-witness]]). He swears the same oath again, with the same table, to Philoetius (20.230–231), and Theoclymenus to Penelope (17.155–156).

**The god of guests.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan tale]] the raider, beaten in Egypt, throws down his arms and clasps the king's knees, and the king protects him from his angry men: “but he kept them off, and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (Διὸς δʼ ὠπίζετο μῆνιν / ξεινίου, 14.283–284; see [[god-of-guests]], [[menis]], [[nemesis]], [[supplication]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). The words are those with which Menelaus reproaches the Trojans, who did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests” (*Iliad* 13.624–625), and Zeus is the god whom Odysseus named to the Cyclops, “the avenger of suppliants and strangers” (9.270–271; see [[zeus]]). The tale has another host: in [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]], Pheidon said that he had “entertained him and made him welcome on his way to his native land”, of Odysseus himself (ξεινίσαι ἠδὲ φιλῆσαι, 14.322; see [[pheidon]] and [[philos]]).

**In fear of Zeus.** Eumaeus believes the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] except where it is true, and says why he will keep the teller all the same: “for it is not for that reason that I shall respect you or make you welcome, / but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (Δία ξένιον δείσας αὐτόν τʼ ἐλεαίρων, 14.388–389; see [[god-of-guests]], [[aidos]], [[deos]] and [[pity]]): the two motives that the tale gave the king of Egypt, who “protected me and pitied me” (14.279; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). Some manuscripts read ξείνιον, and since ξεῖνος, not ξένος, is the Homeric form, Monro thinks they may be right, and ξεινίη with them at 14.158 (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.389). When the beggar proposes a wager on his life, the host answers with the irony of the bond: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life; / readily, after that, would I pray to Zeus, son of Cronus!” (ξείνια δῶκα, 14.402–406; see [[rhetre]], [[kleos]] and [[guest-gifts]]): he would have to make his peace with Zeus, the god of guests (Monro on 14.406). He sacrifices his best boar “for the stranger / from a far land” (ξείνῳ / τηλεδαπῷ, 14.414–415; see [[sacrifice]]), and offers him the meal with “Eat, strange guest” (δαιμόνιε ξείνων, 14.443; see [[daimon]]).
