summary: The treasures that the king of the Thesprotians shows the Cretan, the possessions Odysseus has gathered, are “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” (14.321–326); and Dawn is “of the golden throne” when the tale of the cloak ends (14.502).
# χρυσόν (14.324) is in the Book 3 note, χρυσόθρονος (14.502) in the entry.
---

**The treasure in the tale.** The Cretan says that he saw what Odysseus had gathered on his travels, “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil. / It would feed another man to the tenth generation, / so many treasures of his lay stored in the halls of the lord” (14.324–326; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[possessions]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The verse is the Iliad's verse for a captured man's wealth at home, from which his father would pay a ransom, as Dolon offers it (*Iliad* 10.378–380; 6.48, 11.133; see [[apoina]]), and it is the verse for the treasures of Odysseus' own storeroom, where Penelope will go for the bow (21.8–10). The gifts that Odysseus has really brought home are described otherwise: the Phaeacians gave him “bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing” (13.135–136; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**Golden.** Dawn comes at the end of the night in the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale]], “and Dawn of the golden throne shone forth” (14.502; see [[golden-throned]] and [[eos]]).
