**The suitors' ambush.** Eumaeus' grief for Telemachus ends with the danger he knows of: “and the noble suitors / lie in ambush for him on his way home, so that the race / of godlike Arceisius may perish utterly from Ithaca, nameless” (14.180–182; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]] and [[arceisius]]). The ambush was laid in Book 4, when Antinous asked for a ship and twenty men to watch for him in the strait between Ithaca and Samos (4.669–672; 4.842–847), and Athena has already told Odysseus of it (13.425–428; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**The Cretan's ambushes.** The false self-portrait is that of the brave man in Idomeneus' account of the ambush (*Iliad* 13.276–286): “whenever I picked for an ambush / the best men, planting evils for the enemy, / never did my proud spirit foresee death, / but leaping out far the first I would kill with my spear / whatever man of the enemy gave way before me with his feet” (14.217–221; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). The coward of the Iliad sits in the ambush “foreseeing the fates” (*Iliad* 13.283); the Cretan never foresaw death.

**Beneath Troy.** The tale of the cloak begins with a wish and an ambush: “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast, / as when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it” (14.468–469; see [[if-only-i-were-young]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). Odysseus and Menelaus led it, and the teller was third in command (14.470–471). They lay in the thick brushwood around the city, among the reeds and the marsh, “crouching under our armor” (14.473–475), until a night of snow and frost came on and ice grew thick on their shields (14.475–477; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]]). An ambush is waiting in silence, and Odysseus' first answer to the freezing man is “Be silent now, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears you” (14.493; see [[silence]]). The night, the cold, the armor and Odysseus' quick device are those of the night expedition of *Iliad* 10 (see [[the-doloneia-question]]).
