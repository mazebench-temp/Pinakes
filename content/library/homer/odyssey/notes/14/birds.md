---
summary: The birds of Book 14 are the swift birds that, with the dogs, Eumaeus imagines tearing the flesh from his master's bones (14.133–134), and the sea crows of the false tale, the Phoenician's crew carried on the waves around the black ship (14.308).
---

**Dogs and swift birds.** Eumaeus has said that the wanderers who bring news of Odysseus tell lies for their keep, and he gives his own account of his master's end: “Already the dogs and the swift birds must have / torn the skin from his bones, and his soul has left him; / or else the fish have eaten him in the sea, and his bones / lie on the mainland, wrapped in deep sand” (14.133–136; see [[dogs]], [[fish]] and [[psyche]]). μέλλουσι with the aorist infinitive is “must have”, a guess at what is already done (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.133). οἰωνοί are the large birds that feed on the unburied dead (see the entry and the Book 3 note), and the pair is the Iliad's from its fifth verse, where the wrath of Achilles made the heroes “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (*Iliad* 1.4–5). Achilles promised it to the dying Hector (*Iliad* 22.335–336), and Hermes told Priam that it had not yet come to pass: “Old man, the dogs have not yet eaten him, nor the birds” (*Iliad* 24.411). Nestor said the same of Aegisthus, had Menelaus found him alive (3.258–260). Eumaeus imagines it for the master who sits at his table, a beggar whom he has just saved from the dogs (14.29–38).

**Sea crows.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] the Phoenician's ship is struck by the thunderbolt off Crete, “And like sea crows around the black ship / they were carried on the waves, and the god took away their homecoming” (14.308–309; see [[like-sea-crows]] and the Book 12 note). The sea crows are the birds of the same verses in Odysseus' account of his own wreck (12.418), and of the wood around Calypso's cave (5.66; see the Book 5 note).
