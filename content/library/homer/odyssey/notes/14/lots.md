summary: In the false tale the Cretan's half-brothers cast lots for their father's livelihood and left him little (14.207–210), and he received his share of the spoil of his raids by lot (14.231–233). κλῆρος is also the allotted land a kind master gives a faithful servant (14.63–64), and the Cretan married into “people of many allotments” (14.211).

**The inheritance.** The Cretan was the son of a rich man by a bought concubine, honored equally with the lawful sons while his father lived. Then the father died, “and his livelihood was divided / by his high-hearted sons, who cast lots for it, / but to me they gave very little, and allotted me a house” (14.208–210; see [[nothos]], [[concubines]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). “Cast lots for it” is “divided it and assigned the shares by lot”: the order of the verbs, ἐδάσαντο καὶ … ἐβάλοντο, marks the division as the main thing and the lot as a detail of it, as in the Cretan's tale to Athena (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.209, comparing 13.274). The lawful sons share the estate by lot among themselves, as the sons of Cronus shared the world (*Iliad* 15.187–193; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]); the bastard is not among those who cast lots, and gets what they choose to give him.

**Spoil by lot.** Before the war he led nine raids by sea “against foreign men, and very much fell to my lot. / From this I picked out what satisfied my heart, and much afterward / I received by lot” (14.231–233; see [[piracy]]). The leader chooses first, and the rest is shared out by lot afterward: ὀπίσσω is “afterwards”, in the regular division of the spoil, after the prizes of honor had been set apart for the leaders (Monro on 14.232, comparing *Iliad* 1.368; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[geras]]). So on the island of goats the companions had nine goats each by lot, and ten were chosen out for Odysseus (9.159–160).

**The allotment.** κλῆρος is also land that has been allotted, as in Hector's promise that the man who dies for his country leaves his wife and children safe, “and his house and his allotment unharmed” (*Iliad* 15.497–498). Eumaeus names it among the gifts a kind master gives the servant who has toiled for him, “a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife” (14.63–64; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[much-wooed]]), and the Cretan, poor as his brothers left him, took a wife “from people of many allotments / for my excellence” (14.211–212; πολύκληρος, “of many allotments”: the translation's table of key words; see [[arete]]), where the plural ἀνθρώπων stands for the family or tribe of the wife (Monro on 14.211). Monro took the two words as evidence for the later date of the Odyssey. In the Iliad κλῆρος means only the right to an allotment in the common fields; here it is a portion of land given by a master to a deserving slave, which must have been cut out of a demesne of his own, and πολύκληρος implies an inequality in land that can only have arisen when land was often held by individuals (Monro, Appendix II §6).
