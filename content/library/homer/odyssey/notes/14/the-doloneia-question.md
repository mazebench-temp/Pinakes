summary: The beggar's story of the cloak is the Odyssey's own night ambush at Troy, and it is made of the matter and the verses of the Iliad's night book and its neighbors, the freezing night, the chiefs in their cloaks, the third part of the night, the verse of the Dream, a crimson cloak laid aside for a run to the ships. Monro, who held the Doloneia to be later than the rest of the Iliad, counted Odysseus' stealthy adventure at Troy in *Odyssey* 4 among the Odyssey's imitations of the Iliad; Strabo cites the tale beside the Doloneia's spies as evidence for the plain of Troy.

**A night ambush at Troy.** The tale is a story of the war that the Iliad does not tell, of the time “when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it. / Odysseus and Menelaus, son of Atreus, were the leaders” (14.469–470): a λόχος by night under the wall, among reeds and marsh (14.472–475; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[ambush]]). In the Iliad's night book Menelaus, too, cannot sleep, and among the volunteers for the night mission are Menelaus and “enduring Odysseus”, who is chosen because, as Diomedes says, “he is wise beyond all others” (*Iliad* 10.25–31, 10.229–247; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). The beggar says of his Odysseus, “such a man was he at planning and at fighting” (14.491).

**The words of the night.** The cold that the Iliad's chiefs dress against is the subject of the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale]]: “an evil night came on, when the North Wind fell, / freezing; and from above came snow like hoarfrost, / cold, and ice grew thick on our shields” (14.475–477; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]] and [[boreas]]). In *Iliad* 10 Agamemnon and Diomedes put on lion skins, Menelaus a leopard's skin, and Nestor “a crimson cloak, / double and wide, with a thick woolly nap upon it” (*Iliad* 10.23–24, 10.29–31, 10.133–134, 10.177–178); in the tale the others sleep in their cloaks and the teller has left his behind (14.478–482), and the man who gives up his cloak to him, Thoas, “laid aside his crimson cloak” (χλαῖναν … φοινικόεσσαν, 14.500), the cloak of Nestor's verse (see [[cloak]], [[crimson]] and [[thoas]]). The hour is told by the stars: “But when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over” (14.483, the verse of *Odyssey* 12.312). Monro refers the phrase to the note on that verse (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.483), where Merry divides the night, like the day, into three portions and compares Odysseus' reckoning in the night book, “the stars have moved on, and the greater part of the night has gone, / two portions of it, and a third portion still remains” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.312; *Iliad* 10.252–253; see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]). The trick is made of other Iliadic verses. Odysseus begins, “Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep”, the words in which Agamemnon reports the Dream to the council (14.495 = *Iliad* 2.56; see [[dream]]). Monro thought the line “perhaps an interpolation, as Aristarchus thought, from Il. 2.56”, though “a form of address, such as κλῦτε φίλοι, cannot well be dispensed with” (Monro on 14.495). The message is for “Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people” (14.497), the king whom the night book shows sleepless while the others sleep (*Iliad* 10.1–4). And Thoas “set off running to the ships” after laying aside his cloak (14.500–501), as Odysseus himself “set off running, and threw off his cloak” at Athena's word in *Iliad* 2 (βῆ δὲ θέειν, ἀπὸ δὲ χλαῖναν βάλε, *Iliad* 2.183; here ἀπὸ δὲ χλαῖναν θέτο … βῆ δὲ θέειν).

**A night arming.** The book ends with another piece of the night book's furniture. The swineherd, unwilling to sleep away from the boars, arms himself for the stormy night: “First he slung his sharp sword about his massive shoulders, / and put on a cloak that kept off the wind, very thick, / and took up the fleece of a large, well-fed goat, / and took a sharp javelin” (εἵλετο δʼ ὀξὺν ἄκοντα, 14.528–531), as the chiefs of *Iliad* 10 put on their skins and each “took up his spear” (εἵλετο δʼ ἔγχος, *Iliad* 10.24, 10.178).

**The question.** For Monro the Doloneia was “undoubtedly later than the rest of the Iliad”. It is not marvelous, like the Odyssey, “but it falls in with the Odyssey as an indication of the advance of Ulysses in popular favour, and of the disposition to see in him the type of adventurous boldness and resource” (Monro, Appendix I §1). He took the story of Odysseus entering Troy in disguise (*Odyssey* 4.240–264; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]) to be perhaps “an imitation of the Doloneia”, set beside it “the examples of parody, &c. quoted in the notes on 14.13 ff.”, and added that the poet of the Odyssey “never repeats what has been told in the Iliad” (Monro, Appendix I §3, note; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]). The [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] would be a case of the same kind, a night at Troy that the Iliad does not have, made largely of Iliadic words. He found one of its verses ill placed: Nestor's wish, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (14.468; *Iliad* 23.629; see [[if-only-i-were-young]]), “is less appropriate here, where the story turns upon cunning rather than prowess” (Monro, Additional Notes and Corrections, p. 287, on 14.468). Those who hold, as the entry sets out, that the night book's differences belong to the traditional poetry of ambush can read the likeness the other way, as two uses of a common stock. Strabo, who was not asking the question, reads the two nights together: arguing that the naval station lay far from Homer's city, he cites “the narrative to Eumaeus, contrived by Odysseus”, with its “For we have come very far from the ships” (14.496), and in the same breath the spies of the night book, sent to learn whether the Trojans would stay by the ships, far from their own wall, or go back to the city (*Geography* 13.1.36, plain renderings; *Iliad* 10.208–210; see [[strabo]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).
