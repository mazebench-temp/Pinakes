summary: Persuasion fails in the hut: no wanderer “could persuade” Penelope and Telemachus (14.123); Eumaeus tells the beggar, “nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus” (14.363–364), and the beggar admits, “not even with an oath have I won you over, nor do I persuade you” (14.392). Only the Phoenician of the tale succeeds, “winning me over by his cunning” (14.290).
# πείσειε (14.123), παρπεπιθὼν (14.290) and πείθω (14.392), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [πείσειε, παρπεπιθὼν, πείθω]
en: [winning me over]
---

“Old man, no man who comes here from his wanderings / bringing news of him could persuade his wife and his dear son” (πείσειε, 14.122–123; see [[pseudos]]), the optative after οὐ of what could not happen (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.123). Eumaeus holds to it with the beggar: “But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus” (οὐδέ με πείσεις, 14.363–364), and the beggar gives up persuasion for a wager: “Truly the heart in your breast is very unbelieving, / seeing that not even with an oath have I won you over, nor do I persuade you” (οὐδέ σε πείθω, 14.391–392; see [[oath]] and [[rhetre]]), where οἷον means “seeing that in such wise” (Monro on 14.392). The one persuader of the book is a cheat in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]]: “he took me away, winning me over by his cunning” (ὅς μʼ ἄγε παρπεπιθὼν ᾗσι φρεσίν, 14.290; see [[phoenicians]] and [[phrenes]]).
