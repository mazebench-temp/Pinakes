**The Egyptian army.** The Cretan's men have plundered the fields of the Egyptians, carried off their women and children and killed the men, and the cry has reached the city: “And they heard the shout, and when dawn appeared / they came; and the whole plain was filled with foot soldiers and horses / and the flash of bronze” (14.266–268, told again to Antinous at 17.435–437; see [[egypt]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). χαλκοῦ … στεροπῆς, “the flash of bronze”, is the gleam of the Achaeans arming in the Iliad, under which all the earth laughs (*Iliad* 19.362–363; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]] and [[bronze]]). The raid is told like Odysseus' own raid on the Cicones, who came at dawn, men “who knew how to fight with men from chariots, / and on foot too, where there was need” (9.49–50; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]] and [[cicones]]). Egypt's horses are known to the Iliad too: Achilles speaks of Egyptian Thebes, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses, and through each of whose hundred gates two hundred men march out “with horses and chariots” (*Iliad* 9.381–384; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]).

**The king's chariot.** When Zeus puts the thought into his mind, the Cretan throws away helmet, shield and spear: “and I went to meet the horses of the king / and clasped his knees and kissed them; and he protected me and pitied me, / and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears” (14.278–280; see [[supplication]], [[pity]] and [[chariot]]). The suppliant goes straight to the king's horses, and the king takes him up into his own chariot. Ithaca, as Telemachus told Menelaus when he refused his gift of horses, has “no wide courses for running, nor any meadow” (4.605; see the Book 4 note and [[horse-pasturing]]); the horses of the beggar's tale belong to the Egyptians and their king (see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).
