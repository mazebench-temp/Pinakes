summary: The suitors “eat his substance without payment” (14.377), and others “eat up our labor without payment” (14.417); the beggar swears that Odysseus “will take vengeance on whoever / here dishonors his wife” (14.163–164); and Eumaeus will never “pay you that reward for good news” (14.166).
# τίσεται (14.163; again 15.177) and τίσω (14.166; again 8.356), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [τίσεται, τίσω]
en: [without payment]
---

**Without payment.** νήποινος is “with no price paid”. The household that hears the news of every wanderer is divided between “those who grieve for their master, who has long been gone, / and those who rejoice as they eat his substance without payment” (βίοτον νήποινον ἔδοντες, 14.376–377; see [[the-suitors]]), and Eumaeus says of the boar he kills for the stranger, “we ourselves shall have profit of it too, who have endured misery / for long, suffering because of the white-tusked hogs, / while others eat up our labor without payment” (ἡμέτερον κάματον νήποινον ἔδουσιν, 14.415–417), with the verse-end of Telemachus' complaint to the disguised Athena, that the suitors “eat another man's substance without payment” (1.160).

**Vengeance and payment.** The beggar's oath ends with the punishment: “he will come home, and will take vengeance on whoever / here dishonors his wife and his shining son” (καὶ τίσεται ὅς τις ἐκείνου / ἐνθάδʼ ἀτιμάζει, 14.163–164; see [[oath]] and [[time]]), a second conclusion after the naming of the day, “which is something of an anti-climax” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.158–164). Eumaeus answers with the same verb in its plain sense, to pay what is owed: “Old man, I shall never pay you that reward for good news” (εὐαγγέλιον τόδε τίσω, 14.166; see [[euangelion]]).
