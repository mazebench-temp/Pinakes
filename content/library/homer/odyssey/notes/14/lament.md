summary: Eumaeus mourns his master as a man dead and unburied, and speaks of him as a mourner speaks of the dead, of what he was and what is lost (14.39–44, 14.133–147); Penelope weeps as she questions every wanderer, “as is the custom of a woman, when her husband has perished far away” (14.128–130); and the swineherd mourns Telemachus, whom the suitors lie in wait to kill (14.174–184).
# ὀδυρόμενος, ὀδυρομένῃ, ὀδύρομαι (14.40, 14.129, 14.142, 14.174) are caught by the Book 4 note's ὀδυρ*.

**For the master.** The swineherd's first words to the stranger turn to his grief: “And the gods have given me other sorrows and groaning; / for, mourning and grieving for my godlike master, / I sit here, and rear fat hogs for others / to eat” (14.39–42; see [[achos]] and [[algos]]). He believes his master dead and without burial, his body torn by dogs and birds or eaten by fish, his bones lying on the mainland “wrapped in deep sand” (14.133–136; see [[unwept-and-unburied]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). What follows is spoken as a lament is spoken, of what the dead man was to the mourner and of what the mourner has lost: “for never again another / master so kind shall I find, wherever I go, / not even if I should come again to my father's and mother's / house, where I was first born” (14.138–141). He no longer mourns so much for his own parents, “but longing for Odysseus, who is gone, takes hold of me” (14.142–144; see [[pothe]]), and he will not even name him (14.145–147; see [[dear-brother]]). The lament is made to the man himself, who sits at his table in disguise.

**The custom of a woman.** Penelope's mourning is renewed by every wanderer who comes with a tale: “and she receives him well and makes him welcome and questions him about each thing, / and as she mourns, the tears fall from her eyelids, / as is the custom of a woman, when her husband has perished far away” (14.128–130; see [[penelope]], [[themis]] and [[tears]]). θέμις, “custom”, is what is right and due, and the women's lament in the Iliad is the lament of a wife over a husband's body (see the entry). Penelope has no body to mourn, only reports, and Eumaeus says that she is deceived by them (14.124–127; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]]).

**For the living.** “But now again I mourn, past forgetting, for the son whom Odysseus begot, / Telemachus” (14.174–175): the gods made him grow like a sapling, but he has gone to Pylos for news of his father, and the suitors lie in wait for him on his way home (14.175–182; see [[like-a-sapling]], [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]] and [[ambush]]). As in the Iliad, where Hector's household mourns him while he is still alive (*Iliad* 6.500), the lament runs ahead of the death; here neither death will come.
