summary: Troy is in the background of every story told at the hut. Eumaeus' master went “to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (14.71); the Cretan of the tale led ships to Ilios with Idomeneus, fought there nine years and sacked the city of Priam in the tenth (14.229–242); and the tale of the cloak goes back to a winter night in ambush beneath Troy (14.468–502).
# Unnamed: "the city of Priam" (14.241), and the city of the ambush (14.472-473).
refs: [14.241, 14.472-473]
---

**Ilios of the fine horses.** Eumaeus' complaint against Helen ends with his master's departure: “for he too went, to win satisfaction for Agamemnon, / to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (70–71; see [[helen]], [[agamemnon]] and [[of-the-fine-horses]]). The second verse is the one in which the Iliad tells of young men who followed the army to Troy, and in which Odysseus tells his mother's shade that he has wandered ever since he first followed Agamemnon there (*Iliad* 5.551, 16.576; 11.168–169; see [[trojans]]); here it is the war as a household saw it, a master gone.

**Nine years and the tenth.** In the stranger's [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] Troy is a stage in a career. Before the war the Cretan led ships nine times against foreign men; then the people bade him and Idomeneus “lead the ships to Ilios”, and “There we sons of the Achaeans fought for nine years, / and in the tenth we sacked the city of Priam and set out / for home with the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans” (229–242; see [[cretans]], [[achaeans]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). The nine years and the tenth are the count of Calchas' sign at Aulis, “and in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (*Iliad* 2.328–329), and the end of the last verse is Nestor's and Odysseus' own for the scattering of the fleet (3.131, 13.317; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Beneath Troy.** The tale of the cloak begins “as when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it” (469; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[ambush]]). The ambush lay “around the city in the thick brushwood, / among the reeds and the marsh” (473–474; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]); περὶ ἄστυ is used, with some vagueness, of besiegers (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.473). Strabo, arguing with Demetrius of Scepsis that Homer's Troy stood farther from the Achaeans' naval station than the Ilium of his own day, takes this tale as one of his proofs: “the story Odysseus made up for Eumaeus also shows the great distance from the naval station to the city”, for the ambush lay “beneath Troy” (469), and a little later the speaker says, “For we have come very far from the ships” (496; *Geography* 13.1.36; see [[strabo]]). Strabo's text of 469 has the dative ὑπὸ Τροίῃ where Murray prints ὑπὸ Τροίην.
