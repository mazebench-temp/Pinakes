---
summary: Horace's Homer “mixes false with true” so that the parts of his story agree, and in this book his hero does the same, building his false tale out of pieces of his true one. Horace's Ulysses comes home “naked and destitute” to find that the suitors have not spared his flock, the state in which the beggar of this book sits in the swineherd's hut, counting his master's wasted herds and asking for a cloak.
---

- **False mixed with true.** Homer, Horace says, leaves out what he despairs of making shine in the telling, “and so he invents, so he mixes false with true, that the middle does not clash with the beginning, nor the end with the middle” (*atque ita mentitur, sic veris falsa remiscet, / primo ne medium, medio ne discrepet imum*, *Ars Poetica* 151–152, plain renderings), and later he bids poets “let fictions made for pleasure be close to the truth” (*ficta voluptatis causa sint proxima veris*, *Ars Poetica* 338, a plain rendering). In this book the poet's art is his hero's. The beggar's tale to the swineherd is false from the Cretan father to the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors, but much of it is the truth in other clothes: the wreck is told in the verses of the wreck of *Odyssey* 12 (14.301–309; 12.403–406, 12.415–419); the nine days on the mast and the landfall on the tenth black night are those of his drift to Calypso's island (14.314 = 7.253); and “the seven years in Egypt take the place of the seven years that in the true history were spent in Calypso's isle” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.285). Eumaeus believes all of it but the one true thing, the news of his master (14.361–365): [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]], [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[truth-and-deception]].
- **Naked and destitute.** In the fifth satire of the second book Ulysses asks Tiresias how he may recover his lost property: “O you who have never lied to anyone, you see how, as you foretold, I come home naked and destitute, and there neither the wine store nor the flock is left untouched by the suitors; and yet birth and worth, without wealth, are cheaper than seaweed” (*neque illic / aut apotheca procis intacta est aut pecus: atqui / et genus et virtus, nisi cum re, vilior alga est*, *Satires* 2.5.5–8, plain renderings). This book shows the same at the swineherd's farm: the boars grow fewer every day because he must send the best of them to the suitors, and he tells the beggar that they also “waste the wine, drawing it off overweeningly” (14.17–20, 14.80–95). The beggar, for his part, makes his birth and worth the substance of his [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]], a rich man's son who was “no worthless man / nor one who fled from war”, and of whom now only the stubble is left (14.199–215; see [[the-stubble]]), and he ends his [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|story of the night at Troy]] with a hint at his rags: “but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body” (14.506): [[horace-on-the-suitors]], [[the-suitors]] and [[rags]].
