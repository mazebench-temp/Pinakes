summary: The gods as a body run through the swineherd's speech and the beggar's tale. “The blessed gods do not love cruel deeds, / but honor justice and the righteous deeds of men”, Eumaeus says of the suitors (14.83–84); the gods have bound up his master's homecoming, and he “was hated by all the gods” (14.61, 14.366); in the tale “the gods themselves” loose the beggar's bonds and hide him (14.348, 14.357), and he calls “the gods who hold Olympus” to witness the wager (14.394). At the sacrifice Eumaeus does not forget the immortals (14.420–423, 14.446).

**The gods who honor justice.** Setting the porkers before his guest, Eumaeus tells him that the suitors eat the fat hogs “with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard, nor any pity” (14.81–82; see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-gods-honor-justice]]):

{{quote:b4c3339e-c919-5fc6-99d3-f84fff1fc858}}

Even raiders, he goes on, who land on another's shore and sail home laden with the spoil that Zeus gives them, feel “a mighty fear of the gods' regard” (14.85–88; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]], [[piracy]] and [[zeus]]), but the suitors, who “have heard some voice of a god”, go on wasting the house (14.89–95). ὄπις is properly “the regard of the gods”, their watch on human wrongdoing; here and at 14.88 the word stands alone in that sense (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.82; see the translation's key-word row on ὄπις). θεοὶ μάκαρες, “the blessed gods”, is the phrase of Circe's warning, “the Wanderers, as the blessed gods call them” (12.61; see the Book 12 note and [[blessed-gods]]).

**Hated by the gods.** Eumaeus explains his master's disappearance by the gods: “For truly the gods have bound up the homecoming of that man / who would have loved me with care and granted me wealth” (14.61–62), and he tells the stranger that he knows of his master's homecoming, “that he was hated by all the gods / utterly, since they did not bring him down among the Trojans / or in the arms of his friends” (14.366–368; see [[harpies]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). νόστον is “I know of his return that he has come to be hated”, that is, that the hatred has prevented the return (Monro on 14.366). The disguised master answers with the gods too: he has toiled through all his sorrows “by the will of the gods” (14.198), and in his [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] “the gods themselves undid my bonds / easily” and “the gods themselves hid me / easily” (14.348–349, 14.357–358).

**Witnesses.** When Eumaeus will not believe him, the beggar proposes a compact: “But come now, let us make a compact; and hereafter / let the gods who hold Olympus be witnesses for us both” (14.393–394; see [[rhetre]] and [[oath]]). “The gods who hold Olympus” is the Odyssey's phrase for the gods as a body (6.240, 8.331, 12.337; see the Book 8 note); μάρτυροι, “witnesses”, is the word with which Achilles makes the heralds witnesses “before the blessed gods and before mortal men” (*Iliad* 1.338–339; see the translation's key-word row).

**The sacrifice.** At the evening sacrifice, “nor did the swineherd / forget the immortals; for his mind was good”, and as he threw the boar's hairs into the fire “he prayed to all the gods / that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (14.420–424; see [[sacrifice]] and [[prayer]]). When the meat has been divided and the portion of the nymphs and Hermes set aside, “He spoke, and made an offering of the firstling pieces to the everlasting gods” (14.446; see [[hermes]] and [[nymphs]]).
