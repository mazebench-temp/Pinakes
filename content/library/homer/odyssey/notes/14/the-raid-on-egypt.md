summary: The first telling, in the tale to Eumaeus. A month after his return from Troy the Cretan sails to Egypt with nine ships; his men plunder the fields of the Egyptians against his orders and are routed at dawn (14.243–272); he clasps the knees of the king, who spares him for fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and he stays seven years (14.273–286).
# Unnamed: the whole episode (14.243-286). Egypt, the river Aegyptus and the supplication have their own entries.

**The voyage.** “But for me, wretched man, Zeus the counselor devised evils” (14.243; see [[counselor]]): the verse turns the Cretan's story from the war to his wanderings, as Zeus had devised “that hateful journey” to Troy (14.235; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). A month at home, and “my heart bade me sail to Egypt” (14.244–246). D. B. Monro thinks that Αἴγυπτος “may mean the river here, as in 257–258, and elsewhere in Homer” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.246); the translation takes it as the land (see [[egypt]] and [[aegyptus-river]]). Nine ships are fitted out, “and the men quickly gathered”, ἐσαγείρατο, for which Aristarchus read ἐσαγείρετο (14.248; Monro's critical note); six days of feasting follow, with victims for the gods (14.249–251; see [[feast]] and [[sacrifice]]). On the seventh day they sail “with a fresh and fair North Wind” (14.253). Βορέῃ is an instrumental “with a partly comitative force”, a use of the dative that Monro calls “comparatively rare in the singular”, and ἀκραής “seems to mean ‘blowing at its height,’ with the subsidiary notion of rightness or perfection”, a metaphorical use of ἄκρος common in later Greek but found nowhere else in Homer (Monro on 14.253; see the translation's note on 14.253 and [[boreas]]). The same wind will carry the Phoenician's ship to its wreck (14.299). They sail “easily, as if downstream”, and “unharmed and free of sickness / we sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (14.254–256; see [[as-if-downstream]]): the last verse is the one in which Odysseus' own fleet sailed on after the raid on the Cicones (9.78; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). On the fifth day they reach the river (14.257–258).

**Against orders.** The Cretan bids his companions “stay there beside the ships and guard the ships” (14.260), ἔρυσθαι meaning “to cover,” “defend” (Monro on 14.260), and sends scouts to the lookouts; “but they, giving way to arrogance, following their own might”, μένεϊ σφῷ, for which some manuscripts read σφῶν (14.262; Monro's critical note; see [[hybris]]), plunder the fields, carry off the women and children and kill the men (14.263–265; see [[piracy]]). The cry reaches the city, and the Egyptians come out “when dawn appeared”, ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφιν, with foot soldiers, horses and “the flash of bronze”; Zeus casts panic among the raiders, “and no one dared / to stand and face them” (14.266–270; see [[zeus]]). For μεῖναι, “to stand”, the repetition in the seventeenth book has στῆναι, which Monro first thinks “may be right” (critical note on 14.270), and then, on that verse, finds that it “makes an awkward jingle” with ἔστη at the end of the line, adding that most manuscripts here have μεῖναι (Monro on 17.439). Many are killed, “and others they led up inland alive, to work for them under compulsion” (14.271–272): ἄναγον is “‘took up,’ i.e. inland. Or perhaps ‘into their hands’”, as ἀνελοίμην is “if I took you into my service” (Monro on 14.272, citing 18.357; see the translation's key-word row for ἄναγον). Some manuscripts have ἄγον or ἄγαγον, and the gloss ἀπέφερον in the scholia, “carried off”, points to a reading ἄπαγον (Monro's critical note on 14.272).

**At the king's knees.** Zeus himself puts the saving thought in his mind, though the teller breaks off to wish that he had died there in Egypt, “for a bane was still waiting for me” (14.273–275). He takes off his helmet and shield and throws away his spear, goes to meet the king's horses, and clasps and kisses the king's knees, “and he protected me and pitied me” (14.276–279; see [[supplication]] and [[pity]]). ἐρύσατο καί μʼ ἐλέησεν “is a prothysteron of the common type” (Monro on 14.279; see [[hysteron-proteron]]); two witnesses, G and Eustathius, have μʼ ἐσάωσεν, “saved me”, for μʼ ἐλέησεν (Monro's critical note). For δέ μʼ ἕσας, “seating me” in the chariot, the “Aeolic” edition read δʼ ἀνέσας (14.280; Monro's critical note), an old copy that the scholia name only in the Odyssey, here and at 14.331 and 18.98 (Monro, Appendix IV §14, note). Then the king holds back his angry people, “and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.281–284; see [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]], [[menis]] and [[nemesis]]).

**Seven years.** “There I stayed for seven years, and I gathered many / goods among the Egyptian men; for they all gave to me” (14.285–286; see [[guest-gifts]]). “This chronology is not quite arbitrary”, Monro says: the seven years in Egypt take the place of the seven that Odysseus spent on Calypso's island (on 14.285), and the verse begins as his account of those years to Arete, ἔνθα μὲν ἑπτάετες μένον (7.259; see [[calypso]]). The next verse, which ends the episode, is that account's “But when the eighth year came round in its course” (14.287 = 7.261). There this text prints Dindorf's ὀγδόατον, and here the manuscripts' ὄγδοον; Monro explains the place of μοι after the numeral by its emphasis, and suggests that the unmetrical ὄγδοον may keep a trace of an older form, ὄγδωος (Monro on 14.287; see the translation's note on 7.261).
