---
summary: Ovid's Penelope questions every stranger who lands and gives him a letter, counts the faithful keeper of the sty among her few allies and fears the ambush laid for her son, all things that the swineherd speaks of in this book. Ovid's story of Baucis and Philemon, the poor old couple who receive Jupiter and Mercury in disguise, has the elements of the welcome in the hut, a poor host, a disguised guest, a rough seat and a chine of pork, though no ancient writer connects it with Homer.
---

- **Penelope's letter.** “Whoever turns a foreign ship toward these shores goes away from me much questioned about you” (*Heroides* 1.59–62, plain renderings), as the swineherd says that every wanderer goes to his mistress and is questioned about everything (14.126–129); on her side stand the cowherd, the old nurse and, third, “the faithful keeper of the filthy sty” (*Heroides* 1.103–104), the host of this book; Melanthius drives in the flock to be eaten (*Heroides* 1.95), as the swineherd must send the suitors his best boars (14.18–19, 14.26–28); and Telemachus was lately almost taken by an ambush (*Heroides* 1.99–100), the ambush the swineherd mourns (14.179–182): [[ovid-penelopes-letter]].
- **Baucis and Philemon.** Jupiter came there “in mortal form, and with his father came the grandson of Atlas, the wand-bearer, his wings laid aside”; they went to a thousand houses asking for a place to rest, bolts shut a thousand houses, “yet one received them”, the home of the pious old Baucis and of Philemon, her equal in age (*Iuppiter huc specie mortali cumque parente / venit Atlantiades positis caducifer alis*, *Metamorphoses* 8.626–631, plain renderings). The old man bids them rest on a bench, over which Baucis throws a rough cloth; from the grimy chine of a pig that hangs from a blackened beam, long kept, a small part is cut and boiled; and above all the food there are “kind faces, and a goodwill neither idle nor poor”, while the couple's one goose, the guardian of their tiny farm, they made ready to kill for their divine guests (*Metamorphoses* 8.639–640, 8.648–650, 8.677–678, 8.684–685, plain renderings). The swineherd receives his disguised master with the same poor means, though he does not know whom he receives: a seat of brushwood with a goatskin, his own bedding (14.49–51), the long chine of his best boar (14.437–438), a portion set aside for the god whom Ovid calls the grandson of Atlas, “Hermes, son of Maia” (14.435; see [[maia]]), and the word for such a welcome, “a gift is small but dear” (14.58). No ancient writer connects the two stories; the likeness is one of kind: [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]], [[xenia]] and [[hermes]].
