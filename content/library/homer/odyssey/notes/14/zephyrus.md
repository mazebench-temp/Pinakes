summary: The West Wind blows on the night in the swineherd's hut: “And an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained / all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet” (14.457–458). The cold night prompts the beggar's tale of the cloak.
# The entry claims Ζέφυρος (14.458).
---

**A wet West Wind.** When the meal is over and the men have gone to bed, the weather turns: “And an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained / all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet” (14.457–458; see [[zeus]] and [[moon]]). ἔφυδρος, “wet”, is found only here in Homer, and σκοτομήνιος, “without a moon”, is “in the dark (part of the) month”, when there was no moon; it was then, D. B. Monro reckons, four days to the new moon (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.457; see the translation's key-word row on σκοτομήνιος and ἔφυδρος). This is the West Wind of storms, “stormy” in Poseidon's storm and in Eurylochus' fear on Thrinacia, and the wind that wrecks the ship in Book 12 (5.295, 12.289, 12.408–410; see the Book 12 note), not the breeze of the Elysian plain (4.567). The night is the occasion of the beggar's test: “And Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him” (14.459–460; see [[cloak]]), and his [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale]] is of another cold night, at Troy, when the North Wind fell and snow came (14.475–477; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]] and [[boreas]]). Eumaeus does not sleep in the hut; he goes out to the hogs in a thick cloak “that kept off the wind” (14.529).
