---
heading: The wreck in the Cretan tale
summary: In his false tale to Eumaeus Odysseus gives his last shipwreck to the Cretan of the tale: Zeus strikes the Phoenician's ship bound for Libya when Crete is left behind, in the verses of the true wreck, and the teller, the only man saved, drifts on the mast for nine days to the land of the Thesprotians (14.299–315).
# Unnamed: the wreck of the tale (14.301-315), told in the verses of 12.403-406, 12.415-419, 12.425 and 7.253.
refs: [14.301-315]
---

**The course past Crete.** The ship that is to carry the Cretan to Libya “ran before a fresh and fair North Wind, / on the mid-sea course past Crete; and Zeus devised destruction for them” (14.299–300; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[phoenicians]]). μέσσον is an adverb with ἔθεεν, “ran before the north wind (taking) the mid-sea course”, and ὑπὲρ Κρήτης is usually taken as “far past Crete”, to the south, which D. B. Monro thinks “not a Homeric use of ὑπέρ”. He takes it, like καθύπερθε Χίοιο (3.170), “to denote the side on which they passed the island, viz. by the N.W. or windward side”, μέσσον implying that they kept off the lee shore. The alternative was to follow the chain of islands, Cos, Rhodes and Carpathus, and run under the lee of Crete along its southern coast, “the course taken on St. Paul's voyage to Rome” (Acts 27:7), and “Here the fair N.E. wind made it possible to take the ‘upper’ or windward course” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.300; see [[crete]] and [[boreas]]).

**The verses of the true wreck.** “But when we were leaving Crete, and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (14.301–302): the wreck is told in the verses of the wreck after Thrinacia, with Crete for “the island” and “all” for “my companions” (14.301–309; 12.403–406, 12.415–419). The dark cloud, the thunderbolt, the sulfur and the men carried on the waves like sea crows are the same, and so is the end: “the god took away their homecoming” (14.309; see [[like-sea-crows]], where the verses are set side by side, [[thunderbolt]] and [[nostos]]). The [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] gives no cause for the wrath, and no cattle of the Sun have been eaten.

**The mast.** In the true wreck Odysseus lashed the keel and the mast together with the backstay and sat on them (12.420–425); here “Zeus himself, though I had sorrows in my heart, / put the mast of vast length of the dark-prowed ship / into my hands, so that I might still escape bane” (14.310–312; see [[mast]] and [[dark-prowed]]). ἀμαιμάκετον is “of vast length”, probably formed by intensive reduplication from the root μακ-; the derivation from μαιμάσσω, “to rage”, gives senses, “invincible” or “furious”, that hardly suit a mast, though “Such an epithet might however be applied to a mast in a spirit of burlesque, such as we occasionally trace in the Odyssey” (Monro on 14.311; see the translation's note on 14.311 and [[irresistible]]).

**The drift.** “Clinging to it I was borne along by the deadly winds. / For nine days I was borne along; and on the tenth black night” (14.313–314): the first verse ends as the verse of the keel and mast (12.425), and the second is the one in which Odysseus told Arete of his drift to Ogygia (7.253). But the wave brings the Cretan “to the land of the Thesprotians” (14.315; see [[thesprotians]]). Monro: “The wind must now have gone round from north-east to south or south-east”, a change that would come with such a storm; and he quotes J. L. Myres, “from Crete to Thesprotia is the regular course of drift (independent of the wind), owing to the set of the main current of circulation in the Mediterranean. In the same way St. Paul is ‘driven about in Adria’ (Acts xxvii. 27) for fourteen days, before getting to Malta”. “The story, however, is none the less improbable, considering the distance” (Monro on 14.315). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, had reasoned the other way about the same words in the ninth book, “from there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds”: since deadly winds do not blow in a straight line, the nine days need not have carried the ship far (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.17, on 9.82; see [[strabo]]).

**The order changed.** The lie keeps the parts of the true story and changes their order. The seven years come first, in Egypt instead of on Ogygia, and the Cretan leaves in the eighth year in the verse in which Odysseus told of leaving Calypso (14.285–287; 7.259–261; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]); then comes the wreck of the twelfth book, and the drift that ended on Ogygia ends at the house of a king whose son clothes the castaway and who sends him on in a ship, as the Phaeacians did after the wreck of the raft (14.316–336; see [[pheidon]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).
