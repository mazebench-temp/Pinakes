**The swineherd's dogs.** Beside the boars that lie outside the sties “always lay the dogs, like wild beasts, / four of them, which the swineherd, leader of men, had reared” (14.21–22; see [[like-a-wild-beast]] and [[boar]]). They are the herdsman's dogs of the Iliad's similes, which watch over the flocks with the herdsmen and their spears (*Iliad* 10.183–186, 12.302–303; see [[the-watchdogs]]). Callistratus, as D. B. Monro reports, suspected verse 22 because of the counting of the dogs and the epithet; Monro finds it singular that the dogs should be as many as Eumaeus' men, and suggests that the verse once spoke of the slaves, in a passage now lost, since as the text stands “the others” (14.24) is obscure (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, his critical note on 14.22). ὑλακόμωροι, “barking” (14.29), from ὑλακή, “barking”, is said only of these dogs, here and when they fawn around Telemachus (16.4). Monro calls it a kind of parody of the heroic epithets ἐγχεσίμωρος and ἰόμωρος, and adds that we cannot tell what meaning, if any, was given by the second part of the word (on 14.29; see [[obscure-iomoroi]] and the translation's row ἐϋχροής; ὑλακόμωροι; κεκλήγοντες). The Suda explains it as ὑλακτικοί, “given to barking” (Suda υ 87). κεκλήγοντες, “baying” (14.30), is said elsewhere of screaming birds and of shouting warriors (*Iliad* 17.756, 16.430), and κεκληγῶτας of the men screaming in Scylla's jaws (12.256).

**Sitting down.** “And they ran at him, baying; but Odysseus / sat down, by craft, and the staff fell from his hand” (14.30–31; see [[kerdos]] and [[scepter]]). The poet calls the old man's posture κερδοσύνη, a device: he sits on the ground and lets fall the staff that Athena gave him (13.437). “There by his own farmstead he would have suffered shameful pain; / but the swineherd” (14.32–33; see [[would-have-had-not]]) “rushed out through the gateway, and the leather fell from his hand”, the leather that he was cutting into sandals (14.23–24, 14.34; Monro on 14.34; see [[sandals]]). “He shouted at the dogs and drove them this way and that, / with showers of stones” (14.35–36), and his first words to the stranger are of the dogs: “Old man, truly the dogs came near to tearing you to pieces / all of a sudden, and you would have poured disgrace on me” (14.37–38; see [[elenchos]] and [[eumaeus]]). The disgrace would have been his, as master of the dogs and host at the fold.

**Dogs and birds.** In his grief Eumaeus pictures his master unburied: “Already the dogs and the swift birds must have / torn the skin from his bones, and his soul has left him; / or else the fish have eaten him in the sea” (14.133–135; see [[birds]] and [[fish]]). It is the fate with which the Iliad begins, the heroes made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (*Iliad* 1.4–5), and Laertes will fear the same for his son, that he became “a prey to wild beasts and birds” (24.292, a plain rendering).

**A defense against dogs and men.** When the others sleep, Eumaeus goes out to lie down beside the boars, and arms himself for the night: his sword, a thick cloak, a goat's fleece, “and took a sharp javelin, a defense against dogs and men” (14.528–531; see [[spear]] and [[cloak]]). The half-verse returns when Penelope promises the beggar a javelin, “a defense against dogs and men” (21.340, a plain rendering). In the books that follow the same dogs fawn around Telemachus without barking, so that Odysseus knows a friend is coming (16.4–10), and run whimpering from Athena, whom they see when Telemachus cannot (16.161–163); in the town the old dog Argos knows his master and dies (17.291–327); these passages are given in plain renderings.
