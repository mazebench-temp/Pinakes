summary: The Cretan of the beggar's tale killed with his spear in ambush (14.220) and loved well-polished javelins (14.225); before the king of Egypt he threw his spear away, when many rushed at him with ash spears (14.277–281). Eumaeus goes out for the night with a sharp javelin, “a defense against dogs and men” (14.531).
# Forms first met here; they apply in the Odyssey only: ἄκοντες, "javelins" (14.225; the Iliad's ἄκοντες, 11.552 and
# 17.661, are the javelins of a simile), μελίῃσιν, "ash spears" (14.281, only here), and the phrase "δόρυ δʼ ἔκβαλον"
# (14.277), since δόρυ in the Odyssey is also a tree (6.167) and timber (8.507, 9.384).

**The Cretan's spear.** “whenever I picked for an ambush / the best men, planting evils for the enemy, / never did my proud spirit foresee death, / but leaping out far the first I would kill with my spear / whatever man of the enemy gave way before me with his feet” (14.217–221; see [[ambush]], [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). The last words are obscure: the usual renderings give a poor sense in a boast of courage, and Monro would read, with Bothe, ὅ τε μὴ εἴξειε, “whoever did not save himself by speedy retreat” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.221). Not work, he says, “but ships with oars were always dear to me, / and wars, and well-polished javelins, and arrows” (14.222–225; see [[arrows]], [[oars]] and [[different-gifts]]).

**Thrown away.** In Egypt, when Zeus cast panic among his men, “at once I took the well-made helmet from my head / and the shield from my shoulders, and threw the spear from my hand; / and I went to meet the horses of the king / and clasped his knees and kissed them” (14.276–279; see [[helmet]], [[shield]], [[supplication]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]): he lays down all his arms to become a suppliant. “Truly very many rushed at me with their ash spears, / eager to kill me”, but the king kept them off (14.281–283): μελίῃσιν, the spears named from the ash wood of their shafts (see the entry).

**The swineherd's javelin.** Going out to sleep by the boars, Eumaeus “took a sharp javelin, a defense against dogs and men” (14.531; see [[eumaeus]], [[dogs]] and [[sword]]), the verse in which Penelope will promise the beggar a javelin if he strings the bow (21.340, a plain rendering).
