summary: The book of a slave's life, in δμώς, Homer's ordinary word for a slave: Eumaeus' “way of slaves” (14.59), the porkers that “slaves have here” (14.80), and the beggar's wager, “set your slaves on me” (14.399); and in the Cretan tale the sailors who “contrived the day of slavery” for him (14.340).
# δμώων (14.59), δμώεσσι (14.80), δμῶας (14.399), forms of δμώς, first met here among the Odyssey's notes. They are
# accent-sensitive, so that δμῳῶν and δμῳάς, the slave women, stay with handmaids.

**The way of slaves.** Eumaeus excuses the smallness of his gift by his condition: “and a gift is small but dear, / when it comes from us; for that is the way of slaves, / who are always afraid, when masters hold command over them, / the young ones” (ἡ γὰρ δμώων δίκη ἐστίν, 14.58–61; see [[the-way-of-mortals]], [[themis]] and [[anax]]), “the manner of bondsmen”, that is, to be cautious and penurious (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.59). He serves the stranger the slaves' fare: “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat” (δμώεσσι, 14.80–81; see [[the-suitors]]). The master who never came home would have given him what a master gives a good servant, a house, an allotment and a wife (14.62–64; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Slaves in the Odyssey are bought as well as taken in war, and the slave has a slave of his own: Mesaulius, whom the swineherd “had acquired by himself alone, while his master was away”, and “bought him from the Taphians with his own possessions” (14.449–452; see [[mesaulius]] and [[taphians]]), the verse with which the beggar asks who bought Eumaeus himself (14.115) and with which Laertes bought Eurycleia (1.430). Capture in war may have been the chief or only way of getting slaves in the Iliad, but that “is certainly not true for the Odyssey” (Monro, Appendix II §2, citing 1.430 and 14.450).

**The day of slavery.** In the Cretan tale the Phoenician meant to sell the Cretan in Libya (14.295–297), and the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew meant to sell him too: “But when the seafaring ship had sailed far from the land, / at once they contrived the day of slavery for me” (δούλιον ἦμαρ, 14.339–340; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), and they took his cloak and tunic and put a rag on him (14.341–343; see [[cloak]] and [[rags]]). The phrase is Hector's, in his vision of Andromache's captivity (*Iliad* 6.463; see the entry). The beggar's wager makes Eumaeus' slaves the executioners of a liar: “set your slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock” (δμῶας ἐπισσεύας, 14.399; see [[rhetre]]).
