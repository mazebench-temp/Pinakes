summary: Honor and dishonor in the book: Eumaeus may not “dishonor a stranger” (14.57); Odysseus went to Troy “to win satisfaction for Agamemnon” (14.70, 14.117); the gods “honor justice” (14.84); the Cretan's father “honored me equally with his true-born sons” and was himself “honored … like a god” (14.203–205); and the beggar ends his story, “but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body” (14.506).
# ἀτιμῆσαι (14.57), τίουσι (14.84) and τίετο (14.205), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [ἀτιμῆσαι, τίουσι, τίετο]
---

**Satisfaction for Agamemnon.** The war's cause is told in a slave's words: Odysseus “too went, to win satisfaction for Agamemnon, / to Ilios of the fine horses, to fight the Trojans” (Ἀγαμέμνονος εἵνεκα τιμῆς, 14.70–71; see [[agamemnon]] and [[troy]]), and the beggar repeats it back to him (14.117). τιμή here is the satisfaction owed for a wrong, as in the oath of *Iliad* 3, by which the Trojans are to “pay the Argives such satisfaction as is fitting” (*Iliad* 3.286).

**The honor due.** “Stranger, it is not lawful for me, not even if one more wretched than you should come, / to dishonor a stranger” (ξεῖνον ἀτιμῆσαι, 14.56–57; see [[xenia]] and [[themis]]). The gods “honor justice and the righteous deeds of men” (δίκην τίουσι, 14.84; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). The beggar's oath promises that Odysseus “will take vengeance on whoever / here dishonors his wife and his shining son” (ἀτιμάζει, 14.163–164; see [[poine]]), and he ends the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] with his own dishonor: “but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body” (νῦν δέ μʼ ἀτιμάζουσι, 14.506; see [[ainos]] and [[cloak]]), the verb that Poseidon used of mortals in Book 13 (13.141).

**The bastard's honor.** The Cretan's father raised him with his lawful sons: “yet he honored me equally with his true-born sons, / Castor, son of Hylax, of whose line I claim to be, / who was honored then among the Cretans by the people like a god / for his prosperity and wealth and his glorious sons” (ἐτίμα … θεὸς ὣς τίετο δήμῳ, 14.203–206; see [[nothos]], [[castor-son-of-hylax]], [[honored-like-a-god]] and [[olbos]]). The half-verse θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ is the Iliad's for priests and princes (*Iliad* 5.78, 10.33, 11.58, 13.218, 16.605). The honor did not outlive the father: his sons divided the livelihood by lot and left the bastard very little (14.207–210; see [[lots]]).
