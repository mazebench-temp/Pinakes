---
summary: Two sets of companions: Eumaeus' fellow herdsmen, whom he calls “my companions” and gives his orders (14.407, 14.413, 14.460, 14.462), and the companions of the Cretan tale, who feasted on his victims, would not obey him in Egypt, and were killed or led away to slavery (14.247–272).
---

**The swineherd's companions.** Eumaeus' men are his ἑταῖροι, as a crew are their captain's. “But now it is time for supper; may my companions be inside / very soon” (14.407–408), he says, and when they come, “the brilliant swineherd called to his companions: / ‘Bring the best of the swine’” (14.413–414; see [[sacrifice]]). The beggar's test is whether Eumaeus will give up his own cloak “or urge one of his companions / to do so” (14.460–461; see [[peira]]), and he addresses the whole company as a captain would: “Hear me now, Eumaeus, and all you other companions” (14.462).

**The Cretan's companions.** The tale gives the raider a crew like Odysseus' own. For six days “my trusty companions / feasted” on the victims he provided (14.249–251; see [[feast]]); in Egypt, “Then indeed I bade my trusty companions / stay there beside the ships and guard the ships” (14.259–260), but they, “giving way to arrogance, following their own might”, ravaged the fields, and were killed or led away “to work for them under compulsion” (14.262–272; see [[hybris]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), as Odysseus' men would not obey him among the Cicones (9.43–44). At Troy the teller “had left my cloak with my companions” (14.480; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). In [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] [[pheidon|Pheidon]] swore that a ship and companions were ready to take Odysseus home (14.331–333), and the Aetolian's lie promised Odysseus' return “with his godlike companions” (14.385).
