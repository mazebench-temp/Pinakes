---
summary: The cloak is the book's thread. It is the beggar's reward for good news and the stake of his wager (14.132, 14.154, 14.396); in his tale a Thesprotian prince clothes him and sailors strip him (14.320, 14.341); his story of a winter night at Troy, when he had no cloak and Thoas laid aside his crimson one, wins him a cloak for the night (14.459–522); and Eumaeus goes out into the storm in his own (14.529).
# Forms first met here; they apply in the Odyssey only: ἀμοιβάς, "to change into" (14.521, only here), and
# ἀλεξάνεμον, "that kept off the wind" (14.529, only here), another word than the Iliad's ἀνεμοσκεπής
# (to-keep-off-the-wind).
grc: [ἀμοιβάς, ἀλεξάνεμον]
en: [to change into, that kept off the wind]
---

**The reward.** Eumaeus' first suspicion of any vagrant is that he will “fashion a tale, / if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (14.131–132; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[tunic]] and [[clothing]]). The beggar names that very thing as his reward for good news, once Odysseus is home: “clothe me in a cloak and a tunic, fine clothes; / but before that, though I am in great want, I would accept nothing” (14.154–155; see [[euangelion]]), and he stakes it again in his wager (14.396; see [[rhetre]]). χλαῖνάν τε χιτῶνά τε εἵματα, “a cloak and a tunic as clothing”, is the guest's dress, given or promised four times in the book (14.132, 14.320, 14.396, 14.516; see the translation's formula row).

**In the tale.** The son of the Thesprotian king “put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing” (14.320; see [[thesprotians]]), the verse in which Circe dressed Odysseus after his bath (10.542). The sailors who meant to sell him “stripped me of my cloak and my tunic, my clothing, / and around me they put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic” (14.341–342; see [[rags]] and [[doulios]]): the guest's dress taken away, the beggar's put on.

**The tale of the cloak.** On a night of rain and wind the guest speaks, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him” (14.459–460; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[eumaeus]]). His story is of an ambush beneath Troy in the snow, when “all the others had cloaks and tunics” and slept wrapped in their shields, but he “had left my cloak with my companions when I went, / in my folly, since I did not think I would be cold even so” (14.478–481; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]], [[shield]] and [[kilt]]): ἔμπης, “even so”, is “even without a χλαῖνα” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.481). At the third watch he woke Odysseus: “the cold / is bringing me down; for I have no cloak” (14.487–488; see [[odysseus]]). Odysseus asked for someone to carry word to Agamemnon at the ships, and Thoas, son of Andraemon, “laid aside his crimson cloak, / and set off running to the ships; and in his clothing / I lay gladly” (14.500–502; see [[thoas]] and [[crimson]]). It is a night like that of *Iliad* 10, when the chiefs are woken in the dark and dress against the cold, and Nestor pins on “a crimson cloak, double and wide” (*Iliad* 10.133–134); and Thoas leaves his cloak as Odysseus once threw off his to run through the camp, βῆ δὲ θέειν, ἀπὸ δὲ χλαῖναν βάλε (*Iliad* 2.183; 14.500–501).

The beggar ends with the wish that someone in the farmstead would give him a cloak, if he were young like that now (14.503–506; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[if-only-i-were-young]]). Ancient critics rejected 504–506: they are obelized in one manuscript, and a scholium says that Athenocles had already athetized them (Monro's critical note on 14.504–506). Monro finds them weak, since they spoil a story whose point is “that it hints at the need of a cloak without directly asking for one” (on 14.504–506).

**The cloak to change into.** Eumaeus takes the point: “the tale you have told is flawless” (14.508; see [[ainos]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]); but “we have not many cloaks here, or tunics to change into / to put on, but only one for each man”, and Telemachus will give him a cloak and a tunic when he comes (14.513–516; see [[telemachus]]), lines that several manuscripts omit, with a reference to 15.337–339, where they stand again (Monro's critical note on 14.515–517; see the translation's note on 14.515–517). For tonight he throws over the guest the cloak “thick and large, which he kept by him to change into, / to wear whenever some terrible storm arose” (14.520–522): ἀμοιβάς is “a change”, the same as ἐπημοιβός (Monro on 14.521), the spare garment of 14.513 (see the translation's row δνοπαλίζω; ἐπημοιβοί). For μεγάλην, “large”, some manuscripts have μαλακήν, “soft”, and for παρεκέσκετʼ, “kept by him”, Aristarchus' reading, others read παραχέσκετʼ (Monro's critical note on 14.521). Then he dresses for the night outside, in “a cloak that kept off the wind, very thick”, with the fleece of a goat over it (14.529–530; see [[goats]]). ἀλεξάνεμος, “warding off the wind”, is found only here; the Iliad's word for such cloaks, ἀνεμοσκεπής, is said only of those that Thetis packed for her son (*Iliad* 16.224; see [[to-keep-off-the-wind]]).
