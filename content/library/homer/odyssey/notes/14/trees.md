summary: The trees of Book 14 are the wild pear that tops the wall of Eumaeus' fold and the oak whose dark heart he split for its stakes (14.10–12), the high-leafed oak of Zeus at Dodona (14.328), the thicket in the flowering forest where the Cretan of the false tale hides (14.353), and the billet of oak with which Eumaeus strikes down the boar (14.425).
# ἀχέρδῳ (14.10), the wild pear, found only here in Homer, claimed here for the Odyssey. δρυὸς and δρυός (14.12, 14.328,
# 14.425) are the Book 12 note's.

**The wood of the fold.** Eumaeus built the wall of his fold “with hauled stones, and had topped it with a coping of wild pear; / and outside he had driven stakes from end to end, this way and that, / thick and close-set, splitting off the dark heart of the oak” (14.10–12; see [[fold]] and the translation's rows ῥυτὸς λᾶας; θριγκόω; ἄχερδος and τὸ μέλαν δρυός; ἀμφικεάζω). ἄχερδος, the wild pear, is named only here in Homer; the Suda knows the word from a verse of Sophocles, κοίλης τʼ ἀχέρδου, “of the hollow wild pear” (Suda α 4685). τὸ μέλαν is “the dark part”, the heart of the oak, and ἀμφικεάσσας is splitting so as to separate the softer outside and leave the heart. Aristarchus took τὸ μέλαν to be the bark, and Ameis followed him; but the bark, D. B. Monro objects, would hardly be called the black part of the wood, and stripping it off would not be splitting (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.12; see the translation's note on 14.12).

**The oak of Dodona.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] the king of the [[thesprotians|Thesprotians]] says that Odysseus “had gone to Dodona, so that from the god's / high-leafed oak he might hear the will of Zeus” (14.327–328 = 19.296–297; see [[dodona]], [[high-leafed]] and [[boule]]). δρυὸς ὑψικόμοιο, “of the high-leafed oak”, is the half-verse of the oak whose tender leaves the companions plucked to sacrifice the cattle of the Sun (12.357; see the Book 12 note), and the oaks themselves are high-leafed in the Cyclops' fold (9.186), in the wind of the Iliad's simile and on Ida, where they are felled for the pyre of Patroclus (*Iliad* 14.398, 23.118; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]). The oak of Dodona is Zeus' own tree, and his will is heard from it (see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]]); it is a δρῦς, where the oak of Zeus before the Scaean gates of Troy is a φηγός (see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]).

**The thicket.** Escaping from the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors, the Cretan of the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] swims ashore: “There I went up to where there was a thicket in the flowering forest, / and lay crouching” (14.353–354; see the translation's row δρίος). δρίος, “thicket”, is found only here in Homer. Hesiod's plural is the wooded glades through which the beasts flee from the North Wind in winter, ἀνὰ δρία βησσήεντα (*Works and Days* 530).

**The billet of oak.** At the evening sacrifice Eumaeus “split wood with the pitiless bronze” (14.418), and “raising his arm he struck it with a billet of oak, which he had kept back when splitting, / and its soul left it” (14.425–426; see [[boar]], [[sacrifice]], the translation's row σχίζη; ἐλεοί; δαιτρεύω; ἕπταχα and its note on 14.425, 14.532). σχίζαι are elsewhere the split wood on which the thigh-pieces burn (*Iliad* 1.462, 2.425; 3.459); here a billet kept back from the fire is the weapon of the sacrifice, where at Pylos Thrasymedes struck the heifer down with an axe (3.442–450).

**Brushwood and reeds.** Eumaeus' seat for his guest is of brushwood, ῥῶπες, strewn under a goat's hide (14.49–50; see [[goats]]). In the tale of the cloak the men of the ambush lie around Troy “in the thick brushwood, / among the reeds and the marsh, crouching under our armor” (14.473–474; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[snow-like-hoarfrost]]): κατὰ ῥωπήϊα πυκνά is the brushwood through which two lions carry off a goat in an Iliad simile (*Iliad* 13.199), and δόνακες, “reeds”, are named once in the Iliad, on another night by Troy, when Odysseus gathers reeds and the branches of a tamarisk to mark the spoils of Dolon (*Iliad* 10.465–468; see [[tamarisk]] and [[the-doloneia-question]]).
