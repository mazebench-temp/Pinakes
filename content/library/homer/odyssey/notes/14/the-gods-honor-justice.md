---
summary: At the first meal Eumaeus sets the maxim between the suitors, who eat the fat hogs with no thought of the gods' regard, and the raider who sails home with spoil and still fears it (14.80–92).
refs: [14.82, 14.85-88]
---

**The suitors and the raiders.** The swineherd sets the stranger's meal before him, porkers, “what slaves have here”, while the fat hogs go to the suitors, “with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard, nor any pity” (14.80–82; see [[the-suitors]] and [[pity]]). The maxim follows (14.83–84), and then a comparison. Even raiders, “men hostile and merciless, who set foot on another's / land, and Zeus gives them spoil”, feel on their way home “a mighty fear of the gods' regard” (14.85–88; see [[piracy]]). The sentence begins with the raiders as its subject and breaks off, taken up again with a new construction at καὶ μὲν τοῖς (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.85). The suitors are worse than raiders. They “know something—they have heard some voice of a god— / of that man's grim destruction”, and still they “will not woo / justly, nor go back to their own”, but devour his possessions at their ease, without sparing (14.89–92; see the translation's note on 14.89–90, and [[possessions]]). δικαίως, “justly”, names the wooing they refuse, which Penelope will describe in Book 18: suitors of a good woman bring their own cattle and sheep and give splendid gifts, “but they do not eat another man's substance without payment” (18.275–280, a plain rendering).

**The swineherd's measure.** The maxim is the measure Eumaeus lives by. He fears the gods' regard where the suitors do not, and the book shows it: he does not forget the immortals at the evening sacrifice, “for his mind was good” (14.420–421; see [[sacrifice]]), and he takes in the stranger “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (14.389; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]).
