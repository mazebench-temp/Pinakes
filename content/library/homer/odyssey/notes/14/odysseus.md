summary: The first day and night of his homecoming, spent as an old beggar in the swineherd's hut. Saved from the dogs and fed, he hears himself mourned and his son endangered, swears that Odysseus will come home this very year, tells a long false tale of a Cretan exile that carries true news of Odysseus, stakes his life on a wager, is honored with the chine, and wins a cloak for the night with a tale of an ambush at Troy (14.1–533).

**Up to the swineherd.** “But he went up from the harbor by the rugged path / through the wooded country, among the heights, to where Athena / had shown him the brilliant swineherd” (14.1–3; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[eumaeus]]). The book takes up the sentence that the last one left open, “she went to Sparta, while he &c.” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 13.440); διʼ ἄκριας is “‘through’ or ‘among the hill-tops’”, and πέφραδε “showed” (the way to) (Monro on 14.2, 14.3). He comes as Athena has made him, an old man in [[rags|rags]] with a staff and a pouch (13.429–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]), and he stays in the hut through the book that is headed “the talk of Odysseus with Eumaeus” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]).

**The dogs.** The barking dogs run at him: “but Odysseus / sat down, by craft, and the staff fell from his hand. / There by his own farmstead he would have suffered shameful pain” (14.30–32; see [[dogs]], [[kerdos]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). The farmstead is his own, and so are the dogs. The swineherd drives them off and speaks to him as to a stranger, while the poet calls him what he is, the swineherd's ἄναξ: “and he spoke to his master” (14.36), as he will again when Eumaeus gives his guest the chine, “and gladdened the heart of his master” (14.438; see [[anax]]).

**Mourned to his face.** In the hut he hears from his servant what his absence has meant: Eumaeus mourns his godlike master while he fattens hogs for others to eat (14.40–44); the master would have given him a house, an allotment and a wife, had he grown old at home, “but he has perished” (14.61–68); the suitors devour his swine and his wine (14.80–95; see [[the-suitors]]); and his wealth, greater than that of twenty men, is counted out to him (14.96–108; see [[possessions]]). He thanks his host with a prayer, calling him “stranger” (14.53–54), and then he eats: “So he spoke; and the other heartily ate the meat and drank the wine, / greedily, in silence, and he was planting evils for the suitors” (14.109–110; see [[silence]]), ἐνδυκέως qualifying the whole clause and explained by ἁρπαλέως ἀκέων (Monro on 14.109). He asks who the master was, “in case I may know him, being such a man” (14.118), “in case I shall know, i.e. ‘find that I know’” (Monro on 14.118), and is told of his own death, eaten by dogs and birds or by the fish of the sea (14.133–137), and that the swineherd feels shame to name him (14.145–147; see [[dear-brother]]).

**The oath.** “My friend, since you deny it utterly, and say no longer / that he will come, and your heart is always unbelieving, / yet I will not speak idly, but with an oath” (14.149–151), ἀναίνεαι being properly “refuse” (Monro on 14.149). He asks for the reward of good news, a cloak and a tunic, only when Odysseus comes home (14.152–155; see [[euangelion]]), and adds: “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man / who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (14.156–157; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]). It is the verse with which Achilles answered Odysseus in the embassy (*Iliad* 9.312), spoken now by Odysseus himself, a beggar who is about to tell a long tale of lies. Then the oath, by Zeus, by the table of hospitality, and by his own hearth:

What he swears is true, and it is the one thing in his speeches that Eumaeus will not believe (14.166–167; see [[oath]], [[hearth]] and [[table]]). The verses 158–162 return when he talks with Penelope (19.303–307), and there, Monro observes, the last of them “even fixes the day”: “as the old month wanes and the new begins” describes the day called at Athens ἔνη καὶ νέα, “the last of one month and first of another, which was the very day following the dialogue with Penelope” (Monro on 14.158–162). Monro holds it more probable that 161–162, “or perhaps, as Kirchhoff held, the seven lines 158-164 that contain the oath”, belong originally to Book 19 and have been brought in here wrongly; he takes λυκάβας to mean “a day”, and “in the dialogue with Eumaeus here Ulysses could not say τοῦδʼ αὐτοῦ λυκάβαντος, since it still wanted four days to the μνηστηροφονία” (Monro on 14.158–162; see the translation's note on 14.158–164, where the English keeps “year”).

**The Cretan tale.** Eumaeus asks the host's questions, and he answers, “Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly” (14.185–192; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). He is a Cretan, the bastard son of Castor, son of Hylax, by a bought concubine (14.199–210; see [[castor-son-of-hylax]]), a fighter who loved ships and raids and had no love of work (14.211–234), who with Idomeneus led the Cretan ships to Troy (14.235–242; see [[idomeneus]]). After the war his raid on Egypt failed, and he lived there seven years by the king's mercy (14.243–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[egypt]]); a Phoenician cheat took him to Phoenicia and on toward Libya to sell him, Zeus wrecked the ship, and he drifted on the mast to the Thesprotians (14.287–315; see [[phoenicians]], [[libya]], [[thesprotians]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]); their king Pheidon sent him on toward Dulichium, and the sailors bound him to sell him, but he escaped on Ithaca (14.316–359; see [[pheidon]] and [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]]). The lie is made of his own life. “This chronology is not quite arbitrary: the seven years in Egypt take the place of the seven years that in true history were spent in Calypso's isle” (Monro on 14.285), and the verses are the ones in which he told Arete of those years (14.285–287 ≈ 7.259–261; see [[calypso]]). The wreck is told in the verses of his real wreck after Thrinacia, with Κρήτην, Crete, in place of τὴν νῆσον, the island (14.301–309; 12.403–406, 12.415–419; see [[like-sea-crows]]), the nine days on the mast in the verse he spoke to Arete (14.314 = 7.253), and the clothing given him by the Thesprotian prince in the verse of Circe (14.320 = 10.542). The [[rags|rag]] and the tunic of the tale are the ones “which you see with your own eyes” (14.343), Athena's disguise (13.434–435), and “the gods themselves hid me” (14.357) is near the truth (13.189–191). In Pheidon's house the Cretan hears news of Odysseus himself: that he has been entertained there, has gathered a treasure that would feed a man to the tenth generation, and has gone to Dodona to ask how he should come home, “openly or in secret” (14.321–330; see [[dodona]]). The first false tale, to Athena, said nothing of him (13.256–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]); this one brings him home as news, as the tale to Penelope will (19.269–299). Like all his false stories it turns upon events and characters of the Trojan war (Monro, Appendix I §3, citing 14.235 ff. and 14.468 ff.).

**Not believed.** Eumaeus believes all but what concerns Odysseus (14.361–389; see [[eumaeus]]), and the stranger answers with a wager: “Truly the heart in your breast is very unbelieving, / seeing that not even with an oath have I won you over, nor do I persuade you” (14.391–392). If the master comes, let him be clothed and sent to Dulichium; if not, “set your slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock, / so that another beggar too may avoid deceiving” (14.399–400; see [[rhetre]] and [[eperopeuo]]). Eumaeus refuses it with irony (14.402–406).

**The chine.** At the evening sacrifice the swineherd prays “that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (14.424), and honors his guest with the long chine (14.437–438; see [[sacrifice]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]), the honor that Agamemnon gave Ajax after his duel with Hector, in the same verse (*Iliad* 7.321; see [[ajax]]). He answers with a blessing: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am” (14.440–441). In the next book he says the first verse to him again, with another ending: “since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery” (15.341–342; 15.342).

**The tale of the cloak.** The night is stormy and moonless (14.457–458), “And Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him, or urge one of his companions / to do so, since he cared for him greatly” (14.459–461; see [[peira]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). He lays the blame on the wine, “the mad wine, which sets even a man of much wisdom to sing” (14.464; see [[the-mad-wine]] and [[of-much-wisdom]]): εὐξάμενος ἐρέω is “I will say with a wish”, that is, give utterance to a wish (Monro on 14.463; see [[euchomai]]); ἠλεός is “mad”, “as being the cause of madness” (Monro on 14.464); and ἀνέκραγον, “raised my voice”, “spoke out”, has “a colloquial stamp” (Monro on 14.467). The wish is Nestor's, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (14.468 = *Iliad* 23.629; see [[if-only-i-were-young]] and [[nestor]]), and the tale is about himself. “Odysseus and Menelaus, son of Atreus, were the leaders, / and with them I was third in command” (14.470–471; see [[menelaus]] and [[ambush]]). On the freezing night beneath the wall the teller, who had left his cloak behind, nudges Odysseus and addresses him in the Iliad's verse, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (14.486; see [[son-of-laertes]]), and Odysseus, “such a man was he at planning and at fighting” (14.491), finds a way: τόνδε is “this” (which follows), the device of sending Thoas (Monro on 14.490). He tells the men of a dream and asks for someone to go and ask Agamemnon for more men from the ships, and Thoas runs off, leaving his crimson cloak to the teller (14.492–502; see [[thoas]] and [[dream]]). Of the dream verse (14.495) Monro says that it “is perhaps an interpolation, as Aristarchus thought, from Il. 2.56” (Monro on 14.495). The teller praises Odysseus to the swineherd in the third person. The close returns to the wish, “If only I were young like that now” (14.503), and its last three verses, from “then someone in the farmstead of the swineherds would give me a cloak” (14.504–506), were rejected by ancient critics; they “spoil the preceding story, the point of which is that it hints at the need of a cloak without directly asking for one” (Monro on 14.504–506). Eumaeus takes the hint, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless” (14.508; see [[ainos]]), and covers him for the night with his own spare cloak (14.518–522; see [[cloak]]). Strabo cites the tale that Odysseus contrived for Eumaeus, “as when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it” (14.469, quoted with ὑπὸ Τροίῃ for ὑπὸ Τροίην) and, a little later, “For we have come very far from the ships” (14.496), as evidence that the Achaeans' naval station lay far from the city (*Geography* 13.1.36; see [[strabo]]).

**The swineherd's care.** When the swineherd goes out armed to sleep beside the boars, “Odysseus rejoiced / that he cared so much for his substance while he was far away” (14.526–527), the last words about him in the book.

**His names and epithets in this book.** δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (14.4; see [[brilliant]]); πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (14.148; see [[much-enduring]]); πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles” (14.191, 14.390, 14.439; see [[of-many-wiles]]); Ὀδυσῆος ἀμύμονος, “noble Odysseus”, in his own oath (14.159; see [[noble]]); Ὀδυσῆα πολύφρονα, “Odysseus of much wisdom”, in Eumaeus' prayer (14.424; see [[of-much-wisdom]]); Ὀδυσσῆϊ πτολιπόρθῳ, “Odysseus, sacker of cities” (14.447; see [[sacker-of-cities]]); and, in his own tale, διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ (14.486; see [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]). Unnamed, he is “old man” (γέρον, 14.37, 14.45, 14.122, 14.166, 14.508; γεραιέ, 14.131, 14.185), “old man of many sorrows” (14.386; see [[of-many-sorrows]]), “stranger” (ξεῖνε, 14.56, 14.80, 14.145, 14.402), “the stranger from a far land” (14.414–415), “Ah, wretched stranger” (14.361), “strange guest” (14.443) and “a much-tried suppliant” (14.511; see [[much-tried]]), and to the poet the swineherd's master (14.36, 14.438).

**Outside Homer.** Plato's Hippias holds that Homer made Odysseus false “in many places, both in the Iliad and in the Odyssey” (*Hippias Minor* 365c), and that where Achilles says what is false unwillingly, Odysseus does it ἑκών τε καὶ ἐξ ἐπιβουλῆς, “willingly and by design” (*Hippias Minor* 370e; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]).
