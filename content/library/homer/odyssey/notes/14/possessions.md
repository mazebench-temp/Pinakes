**The master's wealth.** A kind master, says Eumaeus, would have loved him and “granted me wealth”, κτῆσιν (14.62; see [[eumaeus]] and [[house]]), in the words of Hermes' favor to Phorbas, “rich in flocks”, whom he “loved, and granted him wealth” (*Iliad* 14.490–491; see [[hermes]]). The suitors “devour his possessions overweeningly, and there is no sparing” (14.92; see [[the-suitors]]). “For truly his livelihood was untold; no one of the heroes / had so much, neither on the dark mainland / nor in Ithaca itself; not even twenty men together / have so much wealth” (14.96–99): ζωή is the master's living, his herds (see the translation's row ζωή), and Eumaeus counts them, twelve herds of cattle on the mainland, as many flocks of sheep, droves of swine and herds of goats, and eleven herds of goats grazing on Ithaca (14.100–106; see [[cattle]], [[sheep]], [[goats]] and [[numbers-and-counting]]). The Suda takes ἄφενος for the wealth of a single year and πλοῦτος for wealth gathered over a long time (α 4633).

**Bought with possessions.** The beggar asks Eumaeus “who was it that bought you with his own possessions” (14.115), and the swineherd himself bought his slave Mesaulius “with his own possessions” (14.452; see [[mesaulius]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]): the verse-end πρίατο κτεάτεσσιν ἑοῖσιν is the one in which Laertes bought Eurycleia (1.430), and in which Eumaeus will tell how Laertes bought him (15.483).

**In the tales.** The Cretan of the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] took his choice of the booty from his raids, and his house grew (14.232–233; see [[piracy]]); after Troy he stayed a month at home, “taking delight in my children / and my wedded wife and my possessions” (14.244–245), and in Egypt “I gathered many / goods among the Egyptian men; for they all gave to me” (14.285–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). The Phoenician took him home “to Phoenicia, where his house and his possessions lay” (14.291; see [[phoenicians]]). The king of the Thesprotians “showed me the possessions that Odysseus had gathered, / bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil. / It would feed another man to the tenth generation, / so many treasures of his lay stored in the halls of the lord” (14.323–326; see [[bronze]], [[iron]], [[gold-and-silver]] and [[thesprotians]]): one owner and then another “to ten generations” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.325). An Aetolian vagrant had told Eumaeus that Odysseus would come “bringing many goods” (14.385; see [[aetolians]]).
