---
summary: Eumaeus no longer mourns so much for his own parents, “but longing for Odysseus, who is gone, takes hold of me” (14.144).
---

The longing for the absent master outweighs the longing for home. No other master will be so kind to him, “not even if I should come again to my father's and mother's / house, where I was first born, and they themselves reared me. / Nor do I mourn so much for them any longer, eager though I am / to see them with my eyes, being in my native land; / but longing for Odysseus, who is gone, takes hold of me” (ἀλλά μʼ Ὀδυσσῆος πόθος αἴνυται οἰχομένοιο, 14.140–144; see [[pater]], [[anax]] and [[eumaeus]]). It is the longing of which Anticleia died, “longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus” (11.202–203; see the Book 11 note), and in the next breath Eumaeus feels shame to say his name (14.145–147; see [[aidos]]).
