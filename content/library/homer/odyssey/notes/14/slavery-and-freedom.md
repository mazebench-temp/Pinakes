---
summary: The book is told from a slave's hut. Eumaeus, one of the servants Odysseus acquired (14.3–4), gives what “the way of slaves” allows and mourns the master who would have given him a house, an allotment and a wife (14.56–67); he has bought a slave of his own (14.449–452); and in the false tale captives are led off to forced labor, the Cretan is to be sold in Libya, and the Thesprotian sailors plot “the day of slavery” for him (14.262–272, 14.295–297, 14.334–347).
refs: [14.3-4, 14.59-67, 14.80, 14.340, 14.399, 14.449-452]
---

**The way of slaves.** The swineherd is introduced as the one “who cared most for his substance / of all the servants that brilliant Odysseus had acquired” (14.3–4; see [[eumaeus]]). His welcome ends with an apology: the gift is small, “when it comes from us; for that is the way of slaves, / who are always afraid, when masters hold command over them, / the young ones” (14.58–61; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]). “The manner of bondsmen” is to be cautious and sparing (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.59), and the masters who command him now are “the young ones”. δίκη here is the way a kind of person is, as in “the way of mortals” (see [[the-way-of-mortals]] and [[themis]]). The meal he offers is “what slaves have here, / porkers”, while the suitors eat the fat hogs (14.80–81).

**The master's gifts.** What a slave may hope for is in Eumaeus' grief for his master, “who would have loved me with care and granted me wealth, / such as a kind-hearted master gives to his servant, / a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife, / when he has toiled much for him, and a god makes his work grow” (14.62–65; see [[lots]] and [[marriage]]). The half-verse ἐφίλει καὶ κτῆσιν ὄπασσεν, “loved … and granted wealth”, is the Iliad's for the favor of Hermes to Phorbas, the Trojan rich in flocks (*Iliad* 14.490–491; see [[hermes]]). Monro saw in the passage a change in the land system: κλῆρος, in the Iliad only a right to an allotment in the common fields, has come to mean land a master gives a deserving slave out of a demesne of his own (Monro, Appendix II §6). The hope is answered in Book 21, when Odysseus, having made himself known, promises the swineherd and the cowherd wives, possessions and houses near his own, and that they shall be companions and brothers of Telemachus (21.213–216).

**A slave who buys a slave.** Mesaulius, who serves the bread at supper, is the swineherd's own: “whom the swineherd / had acquired by himself alone, while his master was away, / without his mistress and old Laertes; / and he had bought him from the Taphians with his own possessions” (14.449–452; see [[mesaulius]] and [[trade]]). The middle two verses are, but for one verb, those with which the book described the fold Eumaeus built for the swine (14.8–9), and the last is the verse of Laertes' purchase of Eurycleia (1.430) and of Eumaeus himself (15.483).

**Slavery in the false tale.** The Cretan's companions carry off the women and little children of the Egyptians, and the Egyptians lead the raiders inland alive, “to work for them under compulsion” (14.264, 14.272; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). The Phoenician means to sell him in Libya (14.297), and the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew, out of sight of land, “contrived the day of slavery for me”, put a [[rags|rag]] on him and bound him (14.339–346; see [[doulios]] and [[piracy]]). The stake he offers Eumaeus is his life at the hands of the swineherd's slaves: “set your slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock” (14.399; see [[oath]]).
