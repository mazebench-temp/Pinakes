---
summary: The verse that Plato's Hippias quotes to prove Achilles truthful and Odysseus a liar is spoken in this book by Odysseus himself, as he begins to lie; the chine with which the Republic would honor the young and brave goes here to an old beggar. The Laws join fear of Zeus of strangers to pity for the stranger, as the swineherd does, but banish from the city the beggars whom the swineherd calls “from Zeus”, and they set the slave who saves his master's house against the swineherd's own verses on the day of slavery.
---

- **Hateful as the gates of Hades.** In the *Hippias Minor* the sophist quotes Achilles' answer “in the Prayers”, “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man / who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (*Iliad* 9.312–313), as proof that Homer made Achilles “the simplest and truest” of men and Odysseus “wily and false”, “for Homer has made Odysseus such in many places, both in the Iliad and in the Odyssey” (*Hippias Minor* 364e–365c, plain renderings); when Socrates shows that Achilles too says false things, Hippias answers that Achilles lies unwillingly, “but Odysseus willingly and by design” (*Hippias Minor* 370e, a plain rendering). In this book Odysseus speaks the first of those verses himself, of the poor man who lies, just before he swears to the truth that Odysseus is coming home and then tells the swineherd a long [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]]: “For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man / who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (14.156–157): [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]], [[plato-hippias-minor]] and [[truth-and-deception]].
- **The chine.** It is right, Socrates says, “according to Homer too”, to honor those of the young who are good, for Homer said that Ajax, when he had distinguished himself in war, was honored with the long chine, “as this is the fitting honor for a man in his prime and brave” (*Republic* 468c–d). The verse is used in this book of the swineherd honoring the old beggar (14.437 = *Iliad* 7.321), who that night twice wishes that he were young (14.468, 14.503): [[plato-chine-of-ajax]].
- **The stranger's god.** Offenses against strangers, the Athenian says, are more than those against citizens referred to a god as avenger, “for the stranger, being without companions and kin, is more pitiable to men and gods”, and the stranger's spirit and god, “who follow Zeus of strangers”, have the most power to avenge him; the gravest offense of all is against suppliants (*Laws* 729e–730a, plain renderings). The swineherd gives the same two reasons for his welcome, “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.389), and the king of Egypt spares the suppliant raider because he “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284): [[god-of-guests]], [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[xenia]], [[supplication]] and [[pity]].
- **No beggars in the city.** The man to be pitied, the Athenian says, is not the hungry man but the temperate man who has met with misfortune, and it would be strange if such a man were so neglected in a decently governed city that he came to utter beggary; so the law shall be, “Let no one be a beggar in our city”, and whoever tries to gather his living by endless prayers shall be driven from the market, the town and the country, “so that the land may be wholly clean of such a creature” (*Laws* 936b–c, plain renderings). The swineherd's rule is the opposite: “Stranger, it is not lawful for me, not even if one more wretched than you should come, / to dishonor a stranger; for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars” (14.56–58), and the beggar's own tale is of a man of good birth and “excellence” whom misfortune has brought to this (14.199–215): [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]].
- **The day of slavery.** Everyone would say, the Athenian goes on, that one should have slaves as well-disposed and as good as possible, “for many slaves have before now proved better in every virtue than brothers or sons, and have saved their masters, their possessions and their whole houses”; against this stands the view that nothing in a slave's soul is sound, with the wisest of the poets as its witness, who says that “wide-seeing Zeus takes away half the mind of men whom the day of slavery seizes” (*Laws* 776d–777a, plain renderings). The verses are the swineherd's, “for far-seeing Zeus takes away half the good / of a man, when the day of slavery takes him” (*Odyssey* 17.322–323, a plain rendering), which Plato quotes with νόου, “mind”, for ἀρετῆς. In this book the swineherd is himself the slave who keeps his master's substance (14.3–4, 14.526–527), and in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|beggar's tale]] the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors “contrived the day of slavery” for him (14.340): [[eumaeus]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[doulios]].
