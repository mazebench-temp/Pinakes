summary: In the tale he tells Eumaeus, Odysseus says that after seven years in Egypt “a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits” took him home to Phoenicia, kept him a year, and then shipped him toward Libya meaning to sell him there (14.287–298).
# Φοῖνιξ (14.288), "a Phoenician", the only capitalized Φοῖνιξ in the Odyssey; in the Iliad the same form is Phoenix,
# Achilles' tutor (phoenix, which excepts Od. 14.288). Unnamed: the Phoenician of the rest of the tale (14.289-298).

**A Phoenician man.** In the eighth year in Egypt, “then came a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits, / a greedy rogue, who had already done many evils to men; / he took me away, winning me over by his cunning, until we came / to Phoenicia, where his house and his possessions lay” (288–291; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Φοῖνιξ ἀνήρ, “a Phoenician man”, has the people's name in the singular, as Αἰτωλὸς ἀνήρ, “an Aetolian man”, has at 379 (see [[aetolians]]); the same capitalized Φοῖνιξ is the name of Achilles' old tutor in the Iliad (see [[phoenix]]). ἀπατήλια εἰδώς, “skilled in deceits”, is built like Proteus' ὀλοφώια εἰδώς, “skilled in wiles” (4.460), and ἀπατήλια is the word that Eumaeus and the stranger have just used of the vagrants who come to Penelope with lies, “and speaks deceits” (127, 157; see the translation's key-word row, [[vagrants-tell-lies]] and [[apate]]); in Homer it is found only in these three verses. τρώκτης, “a greedy rogue”, is a “gnawer”, from τρώγω: barytone nouns in -της, D. B. Monro notes, those not accented on the last syllable, often have a hostile or contemptuous sense, as δέκτης, σίντης, ἀλήτης, προΐκτης, ἀγύρτης, and “‘Gnawer’ may suggest thieving vermin, mice, &c.” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.289). The Suda explains it as “one who profits from everything, as if gnawing it off” (Suda τ 1082). The word returns once, of the Phoenician traders who carry off the boy Eumaeus (15.416). ᾗσι φρεσίν is “by his wit” or “cunning” (Monro on 14.290), and for ἀνθρώποισιν, “to men”, several manuscripts have ἀνθρώπους, and one ἐεώργει for ἐώργει (Monro's critical note on 14.289).

**A year in Phoenicia.** “There I stayed with him for a full year's round. / But when the months and the days were being fulfilled / as the year circled round again, and the seasons came round” (292–294). τελεσφόρον εἰς ἐνιαυτόν is said of the year of feasting in Circe's house (10.467), and the next two verses are those in which the seer Melampus, held for a year in bonds by Iphiclus, is at last set free (11.294–295; see [[melampus]]): the year of the Cretan's stay is also a year in another man's power.

**Toward Libya.** Then the Phoenician “put me on board a seafaring ship bound for Libya, / having planned lies, so that I might take a cargo there with him, / but there, in truth, so that he might sell me and get an untold price” (295–297; see [[libya]] and [[trade]]). The Cretan follows him “suspecting it, but under compulsion” (298), and Zeus wrecks the ship off Crete (299–309; see [[crete]]).

**Two kinds of Phoenicians.** In the tale Odysseus told Athena that morning, the Phoenicians were honest men who took his spoil as fare and “did not wish to deceive me” (13.272–277; see the Book 13 note and [[the-first-cretan-tale]]); in this one the Phoenician is the deceiver. In Eumaeus' own story, in the next book, Phoenician traders carry off a child to sell (15.415–484; see [[eumaeus]]). Plato names love of money as the mark of the Phoenicians and of the men of Egypt, and sets the Egyptians and Phoenicians beside each other as peoples whose education produced cunning rather than wisdom (*Republic* 435e–436a; *Laws* 747c; see [[plato]]).
