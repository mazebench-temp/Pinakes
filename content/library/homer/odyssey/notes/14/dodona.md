---
summary: Pheidon, king of the Thesprotians, tells the stranger that Odysseus has gone to Dodona, “so that from the god's / high-leafed oak he might hear the will of Zeus, / how he should return to the rich land of Ithaca” (14.327–330), the first mention of the oracle in the Odyssey.
---

**The oak of Dodona.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]], the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] king has entertained Odysseus, shown the stranger his treasure, and adds where Odysseus has gone:

{{quote:b10e659f-b344-5852-9fc2-a47b47f12779}}

The four verses come again in the tale Odysseus tells Penelope, with “to his dear native land” for “to the rich land of Ithaca” (19.296–299; 19.298, a plain rendering; see [[thesprotians]] and [[rich-land]]). The Iliad's Dodona is the sanctuary of the Selli, to whose Zeus Achilles prays (*Iliad* 16.233–235; see [[selli]]); only here and in the repetition does Homer tell how the god spoke there: from a tree. D. B. Monro counts the consultation among the signs that “the resort to local oracles is distinctive of the Odyssey” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix II §5), naming with it the consultation of Teiresias among the dead, and he notes that the Odyssey is the first to tell of the oracle of Delphi (8.80). δρῦς ὑψίκομος, “the high-leafed oak”, is the common oak of the poems: the oaks felled for Patroclus' pyre and those of the Cyclops' fold, and the oak whose leaves the companions strip for the sacrifice of the Sun's cattle (*Iliad* 23.118; 9.186, 12.357; see [[high-leafed]] and [[trees]]). It is a δρῦς; the god's tree before the gates of Troy is a φηγός (*Iliad* 5.693; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]), as is the tree of Dodona in the story Herodotus heard from its priestesses (*Histories* 2.55.2, 2.56.2).

**The will of Zeus.** Διὸς βουλή, “the will of Zeus”, is the phrase that the Iliad's proem renders “the plan of Zeus” (*Iliad* 1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[boule]]); here it is what the oracle tells. Strabo, discussing the words of oracles in Homer, holds that his θέμιστες and βουλαί, by an extension of their sense, are the commands and wishes given by an oracle, “for such is also ‘to hear the will of Zeus from the high-leafed oak’” (*Geography* 7.7.11; see [[strabo]] and [[themis]]). Strabo's quotation has the infinitive ἐπακοῦσαι, which Monro reports as the reading of Aristophanes and Herodian; Aristarchus and the common text have the subjunctive ἐπακούσῃ, and Murray prints the optative ἐπακούσαι, “he might hear” (Monro's critical note on 14.328).

**Openly or in secret.** Odysseus wished to learn “how he should return to the rich land of Ithaca, / after being away so long, openly or in secret” (329–330; see the translation's key-word row for ἀμφαδὸν ἦε κρυφηδόν): “how he should return—whether openly or secretly” (Monro on 14.329). The manuscripts have νοστήσῃ here, but in Book 19 the metre requires νοστήσειε (Monro's critical note on 14.329), the form that Murray prints in both places. In the underworld Agamemnon had already given him the answer: “secretly, and not openly, to your dear native land / bring your ship to shore; for there is no trusting women any more” (11.455–456; see [[agamemnon]] and [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

**Thesprotian Dodona.** The tale puts the oracle within reach of the Thesprotian king's house, and Odysseus is to sail home from his land; later writers called Dodona Thesprotian, as Aeschylus does with “Thesprotian Zeus” (*Prometheus Bound* 831), and Strabo says that it was under the Thesprotians in ancient times, before the Molossians (*Geography* 7.7.11; see [[thesprotians]]). A scholium in the Harleian manuscript, which Monro reports, suspected three lines of the stranger's oath that Odysseus would come home “as the old month wanes and the new begins” (162): how could the stranger know when Odysseus, on his way back from Dodona, would reach Ithaca? (Monro's critical note on 14.162–164; see [[oath]].)
