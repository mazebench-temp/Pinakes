summary: The homecoming that everyone in the book has given up, while the man is already home. Eumaeus says that “the gods have bound up the homecoming” of his master (14.61) and that he was “hated by all the gods” (14.366); the beggar swears that Odysseus “will come home” within the year (14.163), and Eumaeus prays at the sacrifice that he “should return to his own house” (14.424); in the Cretan tale the god takes away the homecoming of the drowned crew (14.309), and Odysseus has gone to the oak of Dodona to ask “how he should return” (14.329).
# νοστήσει (14.163, 14.329; again 15.177) and νοστήσῃ (14.395), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [νοστήσει, νοστήσῃ]
en: [comes home]
refs: [14.242]
---

**Given up.** For Eumaeus the homecoming is settled, and lost: “For truly the gods have bound up the homecoming of that man” (κατὰ νόστον ἔδησαν, 14.61; see [[theos]]). He refuses the beggar's news and its reward, “nor will Odysseus ever come home again” (14.167; see [[euangelion]]), and he knows the truth of it: “I know well myself / of my master's homecoming, that he was hated by all the gods / utterly, since they did not bring him down among the Trojans / or in the arms of his friends” (νόστον ἐμοῖο ἄνακτος, 14.365–368; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[harpies]]). “I know of his return that he has come to be hated”, that is, that his return has been prevented by the gods' hatred (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.366). Odysseus' homecoming is the news that every wanderer brings Penelope (14.122–130; see [[pseudos]]).

**Sworn and prayed for.** The beggar swears the opposite, “that Odysseus is coming home” (14.152), and fixes the time: “as the old month wanes and the new begins, / he will come home, and will take vengeance on whoever / here dishonors his wife and his shining son” (οἴκαδε νοστήσει, 14.162–164; see [[oath]] and [[poine]]). His wager turns on the same condition, “If your master comes home to this house” (14.395; see [[rhetre]]). At the sacrifice Eumaeus prays “to all the gods / that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (νοστῆσαι Ὀδυσῆα πολύφρονα ὅνδε δόμονδε, 14.423–424; see [[euchomai]] and [[of-much-wisdom]]), with the man beside him; he prays the same verses at the contest of the bow (21.203–204).

**Homecomings of the tale.** The [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] is full of them. The Achaeans sacked Priam's city “and set out / for home with the ships, and a god scattered the Achaeans” (14.241–242; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]); the Cretan's crew, struck by the thunderbolt, were carried on the waves like sea crows, “and the god took away their homecoming” (θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον, 14.308–309), the verse of the wreck of Odysseus' own crew (12.418–419; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[like-sea-crows]]); and in [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] Odysseus himself has gone to Dodona to hear the will of Zeus, “how he should return to the rich land of Ithaca, / after being away so long, openly or in secret” (ὅππως νοστήσειʼ, 14.329–330; see [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]): “how he should return—whether openly or secretly” (Monro on 14.329). The question is the one Agamemnon's shade answered, to put in to his own land in secret (11.455–456).
