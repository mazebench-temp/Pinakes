summary: The winds carry the ships of the Cretan tale. “A fresh and fair North Wind” brings the raiders from Crete to Egypt, when “the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (14.253–256), and carries the Phoenician's ship toward Libya (14.299); after the wreck the beggar is “borne along by the deadly winds” for nine days to the land of the Thesprotians (14.313–315). The tale tells the false voyages in the verses of the true ones.

**The wind and the helmsmen.** The raiders sail from Crete “with a fresh and fair North Wind, / easily, as if downstream; and not one of my / ships came to harm, but unharmed and free of sickness / we sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (14.253–256; see [[boreas]], [[as-if-downstream]] and [[steering-oar]]). The last verse is Odysseus' own, of the true voyage home from Ismarus, when after the storm “we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails / and sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (9.77–78; see the Book 9 note), and Circe's following wind steered the single ship in the same way (11.10, 12.152). In the true story the fair voyage ended at Malea, where the North Wind drove him off course (9.79–81); in the false one it brings the raiders to Egypt on the fifth day (14.257; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).

**The deadly winds.** After the wreck the beggar clings to the mast: “Clinging to it I was borne along by the deadly winds. / For nine days I was borne along; and on the tenth black night / a great rolling wave brought me to the land of the Thesprotians” (14.313–315; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[thesprotians]]). These are the words of Odysseus' true drifts: “From there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds” after Malea (9.82), “and sitting on them I was borne by deadly winds” on the keel and mast after the wreck of Book 12, and “From there for nine days I was borne along, and on the tenth night / the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia” (12.425, 12.447–448). The [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] puts the land of the Thesprotians where the true one had Calypso's island. D. B. Monro supposes that the north-easterly wind went round with the storm, and quotes J. L. Myres that from Crete to Thesprotia is the regular course of drift, though the story remains improbable, considering the distance (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.315; see the Book 14 note on [[boreas]]).

**Storm winds.** The Aetolian who once lied to Eumaeus said that he had seen Odysseus among the Cretans, with Idomeneus, “mending his ships, which the storm winds had shattered” (14.382–383; see [[aetolians]] and [[idomeneus]]): the beggar's own kind of tale, which Eumaeus has learned not to believe (14.378–389).
