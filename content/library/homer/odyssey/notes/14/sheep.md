---
summary: The sheep of Book 14 are part of the master's wealth that Eumaeus counts, twelve flocks on the mainland, in the verses of Nestor's raid on Elis (14.100–102), and their skins make the bed that he lays for his guest by the fire (14.518–519).
---

**The flocks of the mainland.** “Twelve herds of cattle on the mainland; as many flocks of sheep, / as many droves of pigs, as many wide-ranging herds of goats, / which strangers and his own herdsmen graze” (14.100–102; see [[possessions]], [[cattle]], [[boar]] and [[goats]]). The list is Nestor's. Telling Patroclus of his first war, when the Pylians raided the cattle of the Epeians, Nestor says that they drove off from the plain “fifty herds of cattle, as many flocks of sheep, / as many droves of pigs, as many wide-ranging herds of goats” (*Iliad* 11.678–679; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]); Eumaeus repeats the second verse whole (14.101 = *Iliad* 11.679) and keeps the end of the first. What was plunder in Nestor's tale is in Eumaeus' account the standing wealth of a master whom “not even twenty men together” could match (14.98–99). The cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia are counted in the same way, “seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, / fifty in each” (12.129–130; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). πώεα, “flocks”, is always a word of sheep in the Odyssey (see the Book 11 note). The flocks are grazed by strangers and by βώτορες, “herdsmen”, of Odysseus' own: αὐτοῦ is “his own” (14.102; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.102, comparing 14.51). Of the herds on Ithaca Eumaeus names only the goats and his own swine (14.103–108); the μῆλον that the goatherds bring the suitors every day is a goat (14.105; see the Book 14 note on [[goats]]).

**Sheepskins.** At bedtime Eumaeus “set for him near the fire / a bed, and threw on it the skins of sheep and goats” (14.518–519; see [[goats]]). The text prints ὀΐων, “of sheep”. Monro observes that the manuscripts favor οἰῶν in this verse, that Aristarchus wrote οἰῶν while Herodian preferred ὀΐων, and that the meter admits either; he holds ὀΐων and the genitive ὄϊος to be fictitious forms (on 14.519). A fleece keeps out the cold: Hesiod says that the North Wind in winter goes through an oxhide and through a long-haired goat, but not through the flocks of sheep, since their wool is thick (*Works and Days* 515–518).
