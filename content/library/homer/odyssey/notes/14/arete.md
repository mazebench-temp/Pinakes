---
summary: The Cretan won his wife “for my excellence, since I was no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (14.212–213); and Eumaeus, refusing the wager, says he would have “good fame and prosperity” among men if he killed his guest (14.402).
en: [excellence, prosperity]
---

**Excellence.** The bastard son, left with little when his father died, made his own way: “And I took a wife from people of many allotments / for my excellence, since I was no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (εἵνεκʼ ἐμῆς ἀρετῆς, 14.211–213; see [[nothos]], [[marriage]] and [[polemos]]). ἀρετή is here “used of any advantages, not only one in war” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.212), the worth that made him a match for a family of many allotments; the plural ἀνθρώπων stands for the family or tribe of the wife (Monro on 14.211). His advantages were a fighter's all the same, “daring / and the strength that breaks men”, the gifts of Ares and Athena (14.216–217; see [[tharsos]] and [[breaker-of-men]]), and “now all that has left me; / but even so, looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was” (14.213–215; see [[the-stubble]]).

**Prosperity.** “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter” (ἐϋκλείη τʼ ἀρετή τε, 14.402–403; see [[kleos]] and [[rhetre]]). Here ἀρετή is good fortune, the “good / of every kind” that Odysseus prayed the gods might grant the Phaeacians (13.45–46; see the translation's note on 13.45), to which Monro refers for “Homeric ἀρετή” (Monro on 14.402 ff.).
