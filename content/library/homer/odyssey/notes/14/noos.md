---
summary: Two thoughts put in a mind: in Egypt “Zeus himself put this thought in my mind”, to throw down his arms and supplicate the king (14.273–279); and at Troy Odysseus “had this thought in his heart”, the device that won the cold man a cloak (14.490).
en: [thought]
---

“But Zeus himself put this thought in my mind / —would that I had died and met my fate / there in Egypt; for a bane was still waiting for me— / at once I took the well-made helmet from my head / and the shield from my shoulders, and threw the spear from my hand” (Ζεὺς αὐτὸς ἐνὶ φρεσὶν ὧδε νόημα / ποίησʼ, 14.273–277; see [[supplication]], [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[moira]]): the thought is broken off by a wish for death, and the deed follows without it. At Troy, when the narrator says he will die of cold, “So I spoke, and then he had this thought in his heart, / such a man was he at planning and at fighting” (νόον σχέθε τόνδʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 14.490–491; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[metis]]): τόνδε, “this”, points to what follows, the device of sending Thoas to the ships (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.490).
