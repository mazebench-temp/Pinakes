---
summary: Hesiod's winter, the month when the North Wind blows, is the weather of this book's night and of the night at Troy in the beggar's story, and his farmer dresses against it as the swineherd does, in cloak and tunic, oxhide sandals and goatskins, while the beasts shelter in a hollow of the rock like the swineherd's boars. His Zeus punishes “cruel deeds” as the swineherd's gods do not love them, and his Maia is the mother of the Hermes to whom the swineherd sets aside a portion, the god who with Hecate makes the herds in the steadings increase.
---

- **The winter.** Avoid the month of Lenaion, Hesiod tells his brother, with its bad days and its frosts, when the North Wind blows over the sea from Thrace and the forest roars. The wind goes through an ox's hide and through a long-haired goat, though not through the sheep's thick fleece; the horned and hornless beasts of the woods flee through the glens, all with one care, to find shelter in close coverts “and a hollow in the rock” (γλάφυ πετρῆεν). Then put on a defense for the body, “a soft cloak and a tunic reaching to the feet”, bind on your feet sandals of the hide of an ox killed by force, lined with felt, and when the cold comes, stitch the skins of firstling kids with ox sinew to throw over your back against the rain, “for cold is the dawn when the North Wind has fallen” (ψυχρὴ γάρ τʼ ἠὼς πέλεται Βορέαο πεσόντος); at times it rains toward evening, and the nights are long (*Works and Days* 504–560, plain renderings). The book is set in such weather. The swineherd is cutting an oxhide for his sandals when the stranger comes (14.23–24); the night is stormy, “Zeus rained / all night long” (14.457–458); in the beggar's story “an evil night came on, when the North Wind fell, / freezing”, in Hesiod's very phrase, Βορέαο πεσόντος, and he had no cloak (14.475–489; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]]). At the end the swineherd puts on “a cloak that kept off the wind, very thick”, takes the fleece of a goat, and goes out to lie where the boars “slept beneath a hollow rock, sheltered from the North Wind” (14.529–533): [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[boreas]], [[cloak]], [[sandals]] and [[goats]].
- **Cruel deeds.** For those who care for evil outrage and cruel deeds, σχέτλια ἔργα, far-seeing Zeus, son of Cronus, ordains justice; and men who grind each other down with crooked judgments, “not heeding the regard of the gods” (θεῶν ὄπιν οὐκ ἀλέγοντες), should know that thirty thousand immortal watchers of Zeus “watch over judgments and cruel deeds”, going everywhere over the earth clothed in mist (*Works and Days* 238–239, 250–255, plain renderings). The swineherd says the same of the suitors, who eat with “no thought in their minds of the gods' regard” (ὄπιδα): “The blessed gods do not love cruel deeds” (οὐ μὲν σχέτλια ἔργα θεοὶ μάκαρες φιλέουσιν), “but honor justice and the righteous deeds of men”, and even raiders feel “a mighty fear of the gods' regard” as they sail home with their spoil (14.82–88): [[the-gods-honor-justice]] and [[the-suitors]].
- **Maia and Hermes.** “Maia, daughter of Atlas, bore to Zeus glorious Hermes” (*Theogony* 938, a plain rendering), the god to whom, with the nymphs, the swineherd sets aside one of the seven portions of his sacrifice, “Hermes, son of Maia” (14.435), the only mention of Maia in Homer. Hesiod also makes Hermes a god of the steadings: Hecate is good “in the steadings, with Hermes, at increasing the stock”, the herds of cattle, the wide herds of goats and the flocks of sheep (ἐσθλὴ δʼ ἐν σταθμοῖσι σὺν Ἑρμῇ ληίδʼ ἀέξειν, *Theogony* 444–447, plain renderings), with the dative Ἑρμῇ of this book's verse, where Homer's usual name is Ἑρμείας (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.435): [[maia]], [[hermes]] and [[sacrifice]].
