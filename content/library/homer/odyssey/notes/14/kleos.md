summary: Eumaeus' lament for a master lost without a tomb: had he died at Troy, “he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come. / But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (14.370–371); and his ironic answer to the beggar's wager, that he would indeed have “good fame and prosperity / among men” if he killed his guest (14.402–403).
# ἐϋκλείη (14.402), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [ἐϋκλείη]
en: [good fame]
---

**Without report.** Eumaeus knows his master's homecoming was hated by the gods, “since they did not bring him down among the Trojans / or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war. / Then all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him, / and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come. / But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (μέγα κλέος … ἀκλειῶς, 14.367–371; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]], [[harpies]] and [[nostos]]). A tomb at Troy would have kept the dead man's fame and passed it to his son; a death no one has heard of leaves neither. ἀκλειῶς is “without report”, unheard of, as at 1.241, where the Iliad has “without glory” (see the translation's table of key words, κλέος / ἀκλειῶς). The verses are those in which Telemachus speaks of his father to Athena (1.238–241). Most good manuscripts omit 14.369–370 here, and Monro thinks they came in from Book 1, so that the only true repetition is the last verse, “probably an epic commonplace” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Additional Notes, on 14.368–371). ἤρατο, “he would have won”, is a false form for ἤρετο, from ἄρνυμαι, “win” (Monro on 14.370), and “the snatchers have snatched up” plays on the Harpies' name (Monro on 14.371).

**Good fame.** When the beggar offers his life as the stake of his wager, Eumaeus answers: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life” (ἐϋκλείη τʼ ἀρετή τε, 14.402–405; see [[rhetre]], [[arete]] and [[xenia]]). What he would do is left unexpressed, “being sufficiently conveyed by the ironical tone of what has been already said” (Monro on 14.402 ff.).
