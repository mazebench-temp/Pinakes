summary: At the hut the journey is news from home. Eumaeus tells the stranger that Telemachus has gone to Pylos for news of his father and that the suitors lie in ambush for him on his way home (14.174–184), and at night he says that Telemachus will clothe the stranger when he comes (14.515–517).
# Unnamed: the son gone to Pylos and the ambush (14.178-184); "when the dear son of Odysseus comes" (14.515-517).
refs: [14.178-184, 14.515-517]
---

**Gone to Pylos.** Eumaeus has declined the stranger's oath and prayed that Odysseus may come, “as I wish it, and as Penelope does, / and the old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus” (14.171–173), and the last name sets him grieving for the son (14.174–177; see [[like-a-sapling]] and [[telemachus]]). In the verses of his hope for the boy, “and I thought that he would be among men in no way inferior / to his dear father, admirable in form and looks” (14.176–177), this text prints χέρηα, where Aristarchus read χέρεια and the manuscripts χερείω; ἀγητόν is masculine, in apposition to οὔ τι χέρεια, and for δέμας, “form”, the vulgate has φρένας (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.177, and his critical notes on 14.176–177). The next clause, “then one of the immortals harmed the well-balanced mind within him, / or one of men” (14.178–179), is the main one: τὸν δέ, “then”, is the apodosis to ἐπεί, “when”, three verses before (Monro on 14.178; see [[phrenes]]). To the swineherd the journey is folly, sent by a god or put into the boy's head by some man, and he does not know that Athena sent him (1.88–95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). He has gone μετὰ πατρὸς ἀκουήν, “in search of news of his father”, “to sacred Pylos” (14.179–180; see [[pylos]] and [[egatheos]]): the phrase is like μετὰ σὸν κλέος, “to learn the report of you”, in Athena's words to Odysseus that morning (Monro on 14.179, comparing 13.415; see [[to-learn-of-his-fathers-homecoming]]).

**The ambush.** Meanwhile, “the noble suitors / lie in ambush for him on his way home, so that the race / of godlike Arceisius may perish utterly from Ithaca, nameless” (14.180–182; see [[ambush]], [[the-suitors]] and [[arceisius]]). The ambush is the one laid in the fourth book, when Antinous took twenty men to watch for the ship in the strait between Ithaca and Samos (4.660–672, 4.842–847; see [[antinous]] and [[asteris]]), and Athena has told Odysseus of it that morning (13.425–428). The verses 14.174–184 are obelized in one manuscript, perhaps, Monro suggests, “because Eumaeus could not know of the ambush” (Monro's critical note on 14.174–184). Eumaeus leaves the boy to his fate, “whether he is caught / or escapes, and the son of Cronus holds his hand over him” (14.183–184; see [[son-of-cronus]]), the hope of Agamemnon's taunt to the laggards, “to see if the son of Cronus will hold his hand over you?” (*Iliad* 4.249).

**When he comes.** At the end of the evening, when he has told the stranger that he may have a cloak only for the night, Eumaeus promises him one from Telemachus: “But when the dear son of Odysseus comes, / he himself will give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing, / and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (14.515–517; see [[cloak]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). Many manuscripts omit the three verses, which stand again, nearly unchanged, in the next book (15.337–339; Monro's critical note on 14.515–517; see the translation's note on 14.515–517). The poem goes back to Telemachus at Sparta in the fifteenth book, and he comes to the hut at the beginning of the sixteenth (15.1–3; 16.1–16).
