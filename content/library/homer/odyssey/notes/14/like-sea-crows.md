**The wreck borrowed.** In the beggar's tale a Phoenician, “skilled in deceits, / a greedy rogue, who had already done many evils to men”, has kept him a year in Phoenicia and then put him on board a ship bound for Libya, meaning to sell him there (14.288–297; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). “And she ran before a fresh and fair North Wind, / on the mid-sea course past Crete; and Zeus devised destruction for them” (14.299–300; see [[boreas]] and [[crete]]). From here the tale is told in the verses with which Odysseus told the Phaeacians of the last wreck of his own ship: 14.301–304 are 12.403–406, with Κρήτην, “Crete”, for τὴν νῆσον, “the island” of the Sun, and 14.305–309 are 12.415–419, with ἅπαντες, “all”, for ἑταῖροι, “my companions” (see the entry and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The dark cloud over the hollow ship, the thunderbolt, the sulfur and the men carried on the waves are the same; only the men are no longer Odysseus' companions, and no cattle of the Sun have been eaten. The tale does not say why Zeus devised their destruction; it has only told what kind of man the Phoenician was.

**The survivor.** Then the verses part from Book 12. There Odysseus stayed aboard until the sea broke the ship apart, lashed the keel and the mast together with the backstay, and sat on them (12.420–425); here “Zeus himself, though I had sorrows in my heart, / put the mast of vast length of the dark-prowed ship / into my hands, so that I might still escape bane” (14.310–312; see [[mast]] and the translation's note on 14.311). The next verses come back to the true story: “Clinging to it I was borne along by the deadly winds. / For nine days I was borne along; and on the tenth black night” (14.313–314) answer “sitting on them I was borne by deadly winds” (12.425) and the verse in which Odysseus told Arete how he came to Ogygia (7.253; 12.447, without “black”; see [[ogygia]]). But the wave that brought Odysseus to Calypso brings the Cretan of the tale to the land of the Thesprotians (14.315; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[thesprotians]]). The lie keeps the shape of the truth, and the only man saved is again the teller. Eumaeus, who believes the rest of the story, does not believe what the stranger goes on to say of Odysseus (14.361–365).
