summary: Dawn is named once, at the end of the night in the tale of the cloak: the beggar lay gladly in Thoas' cloak, “and Dawn of the golden throne shone forth” (14.502). The book's own night, wet and without a moon, does not reach its dawn.
# The entry claims Ἠώς (14.502). Not counted: ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφιν, "when dawn appeared" (14.266; again 17.435), the
# hour at which the Egyptians come out against the raiders, and ἠῶθεν, "at dawn" (14.512), both the time of day.
---

**Dawn in the tale.** In the beggar's [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the ambush beneath Troy]], the night is cold, with snow and ice on the shields, and he has left his cloak behind; Odysseus sends Thoas running to the ships, and the beggar takes his cloak: “and in his clothing / I lay gladly, and Dawn of the golden throne shone forth” (14.501–502; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]], [[thoas]] and [[golden-throned]]). χρυσόθρονος is Dawn's epithet in the Odyssey's verse of morning, αὐτίκα δὲ χρυσόθρονος ἤλυθεν Ἠώς, “and at once Dawn of the golden throne came” (10.541, 12.142; later 15.56, 20.91; see the Book 10 note); here alone she “shone forth”, φάε, a form found only in this verse. The tale's night had been told in the Iliad's way, by its thirds: “But when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over” (14.483), as Odysseus reckons the night in the Doloneia (*Iliad* 10.252–253; see [[the-doloneia-question]]).

**The time of day.** Twice the word is only the hour. In the [[the-raid-on-egypt|raid on Egypt]] the people of the city “heard the shout, and when dawn appeared / they came” (14.266–267), the verse that returns in the tale told to Antinous (17.435–436); and Eumaeus promises the beggar clothing for the night, “for now; but at dawn you will knock about in your own rags” (14.510–512; see [[cloak]] and [[rags]]). Neither is counted for the goddess. The night of the hut itself, “an evil night … a night without a moon”, ends the book (14.457–533; see [[zephyrus]] and [[boreas]]).
