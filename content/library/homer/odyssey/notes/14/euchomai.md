summary: The verb in three senses: “claim”, when Eumaeus asks who the sailors “claim to be” (14.189) and the beggar claims his line from Crete and from Castor, son of Hylax (14.199, 14.204); “pray”, when Eumaeus prays at the evening sacrifice that Odysseus may come home (14.423) and sets aside a portion for the nymphs and Hermes “with a prayer” (14.436); and “with a wish”, the beggar's word for the story he tells over the wine (14.463).
# ἐπεύχετο (14.423; again 21.203) and ἐπευξάμενος (14.436), first met here among the Odyssey's notes: the entry's stems do not catch ἐπευχ-.
# ἐϋχροές (14.24, only here in Homer) is "good-colored", of the oxhide, another word that the stem εὐχ* catches.

**Claims.** The host's questions are Telemachus' to the disguised Athena, verse for verse: “Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents? / And on what sort of ship did you come? And how did sailors / bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?” (τίνες ἔμμεναι εὐχετόωντο, 14.187–189 = 1.170–172; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and the Book 1 note). The answer is a claim of birth, the verb's commonest use among men: “I claim that my line is from wide Crete, / the son of a rich man” (ἐκ μὲν Κρητάων γένος εὔχομαι εὐρειάων, 14.199–200; see [[crete]] and [[genee]]), and, after the confession of a bought mother, the father's name: “Castor, son of Hylax, of whose line I claim to be” (τοῦ ἐγὼ γένος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 14.204; see [[castor-son-of-hylax]] and [[nothos]]), the formula with which Glaucus ends his genealogy (*Iliad* 6.211). The claim is false, and Eumaeus passes it on to Telemachus as the stranger's own: “He claims that his line is from wide Crete” (16.62, a plain rendering; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

**Prayers.** At the sacrifice “nor did the swineherd / forget the immortals; for his mind was good; / but he made the first offering, throwing into the fire hairs from the head / of the white-tusked boar, and he prayed to all the gods / that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (ἐπεύχετο πᾶσι θεοῖσιν, 14.420–424; see [[prayer]], [[sacrifice]] and [[nostos]]). The man he prays for is sitting at his fire. Of the seven portions, “one he set aside for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia, / with a prayer” (θῆκεν ἐπευξάμενος, 14.435–436; see [[nymphs]], [[hermes]] and [[maia]]). He prays the same verses again at the contest of the bow (21.203–204).

**With a wish.** The beggar begins the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]]: “Hear me now, Eumaeus, and all you other companions; / I will speak a word with a wish; for the wine bids me” (εὐξάμενός τι ἔπος ἐρέω, 14.462–463; see [[the-mad-wine]] and [[ainos]]). “I will say with a wish”, that is, give utterance to a wish; the aorist participle expresses coincidence with the action of the verb (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.463). The wish follows: “If only I were young like that” (14.468; see [[if-only-i-were-young]]).
