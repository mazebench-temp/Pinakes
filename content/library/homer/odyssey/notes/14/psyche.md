---
summary: Eumaeus imagines his master's body torn by dogs and birds, “and his soul has left him” (14.134); and at the evening sacrifice the boar, struck with a billet of oak, “and its soul left it” (14.426).
en: [soul]
---

“Already the dogs and the swift birds must have / torn the skin from his bones, and his soul has left him; / or else the fish have eaten him in the sea, and his bones / lie on the mainland, wrapped in deep sand” (ψυχὴ δὲ λέλοιπεν, 14.133–136; see [[dogs]], [[birds]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]): μέλλουσι with the aorist means “must have” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.133). It is the fate of the unburied that the Iliad's proem names, men made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (*Iliad* 1.4–5). The beast's life is a ψυχή too: “And raising his arm he struck it with a billet of oak, which he had kept back when splitting, / and its soul left it” (τὸν δʼ ἔλιπε ψυχή, 14.425–426; see [[sacrifice]] and [[boar]]), in the words the Iliad uses when Sarpedon faints from his wound (*Iliad* 5.696).
