**The server.** Eumaeus has sacrificed a boar for his guest, divided it into seven portions, and given the long chine to the stranger (14.414–448; see [[eumaeus]] and [[sacrifice]]). Then:

{{quote:f68ce815-cd53-5895-80f8-69993ebdbbbe}}

Handing round the bread is the work of an attendant beside the one who carves the meat: so Patroclus sets out the bread while Achilles carves (*Iliad* 9.216), Automedon when Achilles entertains Priam (*Iliad* 24.625), and the cowherd Philoetius in Odysseus' hall, in a verse much like this one (20.254, where G has σφʼ ἐπένειμε for σφιν ἔνειμε here: D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.449). The meal is ended in the formula of the feast (14.454 = 1.150; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).

**Bought by a slave.** The words are those with which the book began, of the fold that the swineherd built himself: “while his master was away, / without his mistress and old Laertes” (14.450–451, as 14.8–9; see [[fold]], [[penelope]] and [[laertes]]). Here what Eumaeus did “by himself alone” is to buy a man, and the last verse is the verse-end of buying slaves, πρίατο κτεάτεσσιν ἑοῖσιν, “bought … with his own possessions”: Laertes so bought Eurycleia (1.430; see [[eurycleia]]), the stranger has asked Eumaeus who so bought him (14.115), and Eumaeus will tell how Laertes bought him from the Phoenicians who had stolen him (15.483; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Capture in war may have been the chief or only way of getting slaves in the Iliad, Monro observes, but certainly not in the Odyssey (Monro, Appendix II §2, citing 1.430 and 14.450; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). The Taphians, who sold him, are the poem's traders and raiders of the sea (see [[taphians]] and [[piracy]]).
