summary: The night of Odysseus' first evening at the hut is “a night without a moon”, σκοτομήνιος, with rain and a wet West Wind (14.457–458); earlier that day the stranger swore that Odysseus would come “as the old month wanes and the new begins” (14.162).
# σκοτομήνιος (14.457), "in the dark of the month", when the moon does not show: only here in Homer.
grc: [σκοτομήνιος]
---

“And an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained / all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet” (457–458; see [[zephyrus]] and [[night]]). σκοτομήνιος, found only here in Homer, is “in the dark (part of the) month”, when there was no moon (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.457; see the translation's key-word row); the Suda glosses the noun σκοτομήνη as “a moonless night” (Suda σ 679). The darkness and the storm are the setting for the tale of the cloak that the stranger tells to get a cloak for the night (459–506; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).

The moonless night also dates the story. The stranger's oath that Odysseus will come home “as the old month wanes and the new begins” (162) names, Monro explains, the day between one month and the next, which the Athenians called ἔνη καὶ νέα. When Odysseus repeats the oath to Penelope, that day is about to dawn, the feast of Apollo on which the suitors die (19.306–307; 20.156, 21.258); here it is still four days to the new moon and to the killing of the suitors (Monro on 14.158–162 and 14.457; see the translation's note on 14.158–164).
