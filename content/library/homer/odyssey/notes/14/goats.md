**The goat's hide.** When Eumaeus brings the stranger into the hut, he “strewed thick brushwood beneath him, / and spread on it the hide of a shaggy wild goat, / his own bedding, great and thick” (14.49–51; see [[hut]] and the translation's row ῥῶπες; ἰονθάς; ἐνεύναιον). ἰονθάς, “shaggy”, is found only here in Homer. The wild goat is the beast of the hunt: Pandarus' bow was made from the horns of one that he shot himself (*Iliad* 4.105–111), and Odysseus' companions hunt the wild goats of the island off the Cyclopes' coast (9.118–124, 9.154–158; see [[the-island-of-goats]]). The swineherd's bedding becomes the guest's seat, and when Telemachus comes the seat is made again, with “green brushwood beneath and a fleece on top” (16.47, a plain rendering).

**Odysseus' herds.** The goats are part of the master's untold livelihood: on the mainland “as many wide-ranging herds of goats” as of cattle, sheep and pigs, and on the island itself, “And here, in all, eleven wide-ranging herds of goats / graze at the far edge, and good men keep watch over them” (14.101–104; see [[possessions]] and the Book 13 note). αἰπόλια πλατέʼ αἰγῶν, “wide-ranging herds of goats”, is a phrase of the Iliad, where it comes in a simile of goatherds sorting their herds in the pasture (*Iliad* 2.474) and in the verse of Nestor's raid on Elis that Eumaeus repeats (*Iliad* 11.679 = 14.101; see the Book 14 note on [[sheep]]). The suitors are fed from these herds too: “Each one of these always brings them a beast for the day, / whichever of the well-fed goats seems the best” (14.105–106; see [[well-fed]] and [[the-suitors]]). μῆλον, the word of the flocks, is here a goat (see the Book 9 note on [[sheep]]). In the books that follow, the goatherd Melanthius drives in goats “which stood out among all the herds, / as a meal for the suitors” (17.213–214 = 20.174–175, a plain rendering).

**Skins for the night.** At bedtime Eumaeus “set for him near the fire / a bed, and threw on it the skins of sheep and goats” (14.518–519), and when he goes out to sleep beside the swine he puts on a thick cloak and “took up the fleece of a large, well-fed goat” (14.529–530; see [[cloak]], [[boar]] and the translation's row ἀλεξάνεμος; νάκη; ἀλκτήρ). νάκη, “fleece”, is found only here in Homer; the Suda explains νάκος as a hide with the hair on, a sheepskin, “but properly the skin of a goat” (Suda ν 21). Hesiod, describing the winter month of Lenaion, says that the North Wind blows through a long-haired goat (*Works and Days* 516), and tells the farmer to stitch together the skins of firstborn kids with ox sinew, to throw over his back as a shelter from the rain (*Works and Days* 543–545).
