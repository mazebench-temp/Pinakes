summary: The kings of the Cretan tale: the king of Egypt, whose knees the beaten raider clasped (14.278–279), Pheidon, “the king of the Thesprotians” (14.316), and “King Acastus” of Dulichium, to whom Pheidon sent him (14.336).
---

The kings of the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] are kind to the stranger, and their men are not. In Egypt the raider threw down his arms and went to meet “the horses of the king / and clasped his knees and kissed them; and he protected me and pitied me, / and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears” (14.278–280; see [[supplication]], [[menis]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), while his angry men would have killed the suppliant (14.281–282). “He protected me and pitied me” puts the second act first, a hysteron proteron of the common type (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.279; see [[hysteron-proteron]]). In Thesprotia “the king of the Thesprotians, Pheidon, took care of me, / the hero, without price” and clothed him (Θεσπρωτῶν βασιλεύς, 14.316–320; see [[pheidon]], [[thesprotians]] and [[apoina]]), and sent him on in a Thesprotian ship, bidding the crew take him “to King Acastus / with care” (βασιλῆϊ Ἀκάστῳ, 14.336–337; see [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]] and [[dulichium]]); but the crew plotted to sell him (14.337–340; see [[doulios]]).
