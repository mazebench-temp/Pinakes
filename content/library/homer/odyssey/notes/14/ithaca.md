summary: The book is spent on Ithaca, at the swineherd's farmstead, and Ithaca is named in its talk: Eumaeus counts his master's herds on the mainland and on the island (14.96–104), and complains of the vagrants who come “to the land of Ithaca” with lies (14.126); he asks the stranger how sailors brought him to Ithaca (14.189), and the stranger answers that Odysseus asked the oracle how to return “to the rich land of Ithaca” (14.329) and that Thesprotian sailors brought him “to the tilled fields of clear-seen Ithaca” (14.344).

**The mainland and the island.** Eumaeus counts the wealth of his absent master, which no hero had, “neither on the dark mainland / nor in Ithaca itself”:

{{quote:87d144e4-e2ef-5538-9d15-5a186ecbc4a6}}

The herds of cattle, sheep, pigs and goats are kept on the mainland by strangers and by Odysseus' own herdsmen; on Ithaca there are eleven herds of goats “at the far edge”, ἐσχατιῇ, and the swine of Eumaeus' own farmstead (103–108; see [[goats]], [[possessions]] and [[eumaeus]]). Ithaca is “a goat-pasturing land” in Telemachus' account (4.606), and Athena calls it “good goat-pasturing and cattle-pasturing land” (13.246; see the Book 13 note), but Eumaeus puts all his master's cattle across the water. ἤπειρος, “the mainland”, is the coast opposite the islands, which Odysseus' Cephallenians held in the Iliad's Catalogue, “and held the mainland and lived on the coast across from them” (*Iliad* 2.635; see [[cephallenians]]). In Book 20 the cowherd Philoetius brings a heifer and goats for the suitors, brought across by ferrymen, and says that Odysseus set him over the cattle “in the land of the Cephallenians” (20.185–188, 20.209–210; 20.210, a plain rendering); D. B. Monro takes this to be the mainland of these verses, “where the herds of cattle were”, and notes that nothing in the Odyssey connects the name with the island later called Cephallenia (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 20.187, 20.210). Strabo reads the Catalogue's verse and this book's together: by “mainland” Homer means the country opposite the islands, Leucas and the rest of Acarnania, “of which he also says, ‘Twelve herds on the mainland, as many flocks of sheep’”, perhaps because Epirus once reached that far and was called by the common name of “mainland” (*Geography* 10.2.10; see [[strabo]]). His quotation has μήλων, “sheep”, where the text has οἰῶν.

**The land of Ithaca.** Vagrants who want keep come wandering ἐς δῆμον Ἰθάκης, “to the land of Ithaca”, and tell Penelope lies (126–127; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[penelope]] and [[demos]]), the phrase in which the poet set the harbor of Phorcys, ἐν δήμῳ Ἰθάκης (13.97). The stranger's Odysseus asks the oracle at Dodona how he should return Ἰθάκης ἐς πίονα δῆμον, “to the rich land of Ithaca” (329; see [[dodona]] and [[rich-land]]). In the tale's end it is Thesprotian sailors who bring him: “in the evening they came to the tilled fields of clear-seen Ithaca” (344; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[thesprotians]] and [[clear-seen]]), ἔργα, “works”, the farmland of the island.

**How did sailors bring you?** Eumaeus asks the host's questions in Telemachus' words to Athena-Mentes: “And on what sort of ship did you come? And how did sailors / bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be? / For I do not think that you came here on foot” (188–190 = 1.171–173; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]). On an island every stranger has come by ship, and the stranger's answer is the whole tale, which ends with him swimming ashore from a ship that had brought him against his will (345–359).

**Nameless from Ithaca.** Of the suitors' ambush Eumaeus says that they lie in wait for Telemachus “so that the race / of godlike Arceisius may perish utterly from Ithaca, nameless” (181–182; see [[arceisius]] and [[ambush]]). The house has had a single son in each generation, as Telemachus will tell his father (16.117–120), and its name on the island hangs on him.
