---
summary: The beggar tells a story of a freezing night at Troy, when Odysseus found him a cloak, and Eumaeus answers, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless” (14.508), and gives him a cloak for the night.
en: [tale]
---

**The tale.** On a night of rain and wind the beggar tests his host, to see whether he will give up his cloak (14.457–461; see [[peira]]). He speaks “with a wish”, blaming the wine (14.463–467; see [[euchomai]] and [[the-mad-wine]]), and tells how he lay in an ambush under the wall of Troy with Odysseus and Menelaus, without a cloak, in the snow, and how Odysseus got him one by sending Thoas running to the ships (14.468–502; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[snow-like-hoarfrost]] and [[thoas]]). He ends with the point, stated only as a wish: if he were young, someone in the farmstead would give him a cloak, “for both reasons, out of friendship and respect for a good man; / but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body” (14.503–506; see [[philotes]], [[aidos]] and [[time]]).

**Flawless.** Eumaeus takes the point: “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless, / and you have not yet spoken any word amiss or profitless; / so you shall not lack clothing, nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way, / for now” (αἶνος μέν τοι ἀμύμων, ὃν κατέλεξας, 14.508–512; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[epos]], [[profitless]] and [[supplication]]), and he lays over the guest the thick cloak that he keeps to change into in a storm (14.520–522; see [[cloak]]). For Monro αἶνος here is simply “a tale”, “an account”: “The meanings ‘fable’ and ‘praise’ are not clearly to be traced in Homer” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.508), and the English renders it “tale” (see the translation's note on 14.508), where the Iliad's αἶνος is “praise” (*Iliad* 23.652). Whether or not the word already names a story with a hidden purpose, as the Suda defines the αἶνος (see the entry), this story has one. Ancient critics rejected its last three verses, the wish that ends it, and Monro agrees that they “spoil the preceding story, the point of which is that it hints at the need of a cloak without directly asking for one” (Monro on 14.504–506).
