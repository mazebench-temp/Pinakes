---
summary: Crete is the homeland that Odysseus claims in his tale to Eumaeus, “I claim that my line is from wide Crete” (14.199). From Crete the Cretan sails to Egypt in five days with a fair North Wind (14.252–257), and on the mid-sea course past it the Phoenician's ship is wrecked (14.299–304).
---

**Wide Crete.** The stranger answers Eumaeus' questions with a lineage: “I claim that my line is from wide Crete, / the son of a rich man” (199–200; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Κρητάων … εὐρειάων is a plural of the island's name, as if of the parts of Crete, found again only when Eumaeus repeats the claim to Telemachus, “He claims that his line is from wide Crete” (16.62, a plain rendering); the singular is Book 13's “wide Crete” (13.256, 13.260) and the book's own “from wide Crete” (252; see the Book 13 note). Crete is the homeland of the persona in three of Odysseus' false tales, to Athena, to Eumaeus and to Penelope (13.256–286; 14.199–359; 19.165–202; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]), and the story of this one is Cretan through its first part: the father honored “among the Cretans”, the raids before the war, and the people who sent the speaker to Troy with Idomeneus (204–239; see [[cretans]] and [[idomeneus]]).

**From Crete to Egypt.** After a month at home the Cretan fits out nine ships, feasts his men for six days, and on the seventh, “from wide Crete / we sailed with a fresh and fair North Wind, / easily, as if downstream”: “On the fifth day we came to the fair-flowing Aegyptus” (252–257; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[boreas]], [[as-if-downstream]], [[aegyptus-river]] and [[egypt]]). It is the sea on which, in Nestor's account, Zeus split Menelaus' fleet off Malea, driving some ships onto the reefs of Crete while the wind and the water carried five of them to Egypt (3.286–300; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Strabo reckons the voyage from Samonium, the eastern cape of Crete, to Egypt at four days and nights, though some say three (*Geography* 10.4.5; see [[strabo]]).

**Past Crete.** The ship on which the Phoenician means to sell him in Libya “ran before a fresh and fair North Wind, / on the mid-sea course past Crete; and Zeus devised destruction for them” (299–300; see [[phoenicians]] and [[libya]]). D. B. Monro takes μέσσον as an adverb with ἔθεεν, “ran (taking) the mid-sea course”, and denies that ὑπὲρ Κρήτης can mean “far past Crete”, to the south, which is not a Homeric use of ὑπέρ: it marks the side on which they passed the island, the northwest or windward side, as καθύπερθε Χίοιο does at 3.170, and μέσσον implies keeping off the lee shore. The other course from Phoenicia, by Cos, Rhodes and Carpathus and then under the lee of Crete along its southern coast, was the one St. Paul's ship took on its way to Rome (Acts 27:7); here the fair northeast wind allowed the windward course (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.300). The wreck that follows is told in the verses of the wreck after Thrinacia, with Crete in place of the island: “But when we were leaving Crete, and no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (301–302; 12.403–404; see the translation's note on 12.403 and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
