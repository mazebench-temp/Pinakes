summary: Eumaeus says that even raiders who land on another's shore and fill their ships with the spoil Zeus gives them feel the fear of the gods' regard (14.85–88); in the false tale the Cretan led nine raids by sea against foreign men before the war (14.229–234), and after it raided Egypt, where his men, giving way to arrogance, ravaged the fields and were cut down (14.245–272).
refs: [14.85-88, 14.230-233, 14.246-248, 14.262-265]
---

**Raiders and the gods' regard.** Eumaeus measures the suitors against the raider, the man who sails to plunder: “Even men hostile and merciless, who set foot on another's / land, and Zeus gives them spoil, / and they fill their ships and set out to go home, / even on their minds falls a mighty fear of the gods' regard” (14.85–88; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]] and [[raiding]]). The sentence begins with the raiders and breaks off, to be taken up again with a different construction, καὶ μὲν τοῖς, “even on their minds”, an anacoluthon softened by the intervening clause (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.85). Even such men go home in fear; the suitors, who “know something—they have heard some voice of a god— / of that man's grim destruction” (14.89–90: see the translation's note), devour his goods at their ease (14.91–92; see [[the-suitors]]).

**The Cretan raider.** The stranger's [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] makes him a man of the sea and of war, to whom “ships with oars were always dear” (14.224; see [[different-gifts]]). Before the war, “nine times I led men and swift-sailing ships / against foreign men, and very much fell to my lot” (14.230–231; see [[lots]]): ἄνδρας ἐς ἀλλοδαπούς, the men of another land on whom the pirates of the host's question bring evil (ἀλλοδαποῖσι, 3.74, 9.255). A month after his return from Troy his heart bade him sail to Egypt with nine ships (14.245–248). There he told his companions to guard the ships and sent out scouts, “but they, giving way to arrogance, following their own might, / at once the beautiful fields of the Egyptian men / they laid waste, and carried off the women and the little children, / and killed the men” (14.262–265; see [[hybris]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). At dawn the Egyptians came with foot soldiers and horses, killed many and led the rest inland to forced labor (14.266–272). The story is shaped like Odysseus' own raid on the Cicones, where the companions would not obey him and stayed feasting by the ships until the Cicones came in the morning, foot soldiers and men who fought from chariots (9.39–61).

**Sold by sailors.** The raider becomes the prey. The Phoenician who takes him from Egypt means to sell him in Libya (14.288–297; see [[trade]]), and the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew who are to carry him to Dulichium strip him, put a [[rags|rag]] on him and bind him, to sell him into slavery (14.334–347; see [[doulios]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]).
