---
summary: The beggar is “old man” to Eumaeus from his first words, “Old man, truly the dogs came near to tearing you to pieces” (14.37), to his last, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless” (14.508), and twice “old sir” (14.131, 14.185); Laertes is “old Laertes”, without whom the swineherd built his fold and bought his slave (14.9, 14.451), and “the old man Laertes” who longs for his son's return (14.173).
---

**The old man.** Athena gave Odysseus the skin “of an aged old man” (13.432; see the Book 13 note), and Eumaeus addresses him so throughout. γέρον opens his first words, “Old man, truly the dogs came near to tearing you to pieces / all of a sudden” (14.37–38), and his invitation, “let us go to the hut, old man” (14.45); it opens his refusals, “Old man, no man who comes here from his wanderings / bringing news of him could persuade his wife and his dear son” (14.122–123) and “Old man, I shall never pay you that reward for good news” (14.166; see [[euangelion]]), and his plea, “And you too, old man of many sorrows” (γέρον πολυπενθές, 14.386; see [[of-many-sorrows]]); and it opens his surrender, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless” (14.508; see [[ainos]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). The fuller γεραιέ, “old sir”, comes twice, each time of a tale: the stranger too, Eumaeus says, would “fashion a tale” for a cloak (14.131; see [[epos]]), and “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows” (14.185; see [[algos]]).

**Old Laertes.** Twice the narrator says what Eumaeus did “without his mistress and old Laertes” (νόσφιν δεσποίνης καὶ Λαέρταο γέροντος, 14.9 = 14.451; see [[laertes]]): he built the fold and bought Mesaulius on his own (see [[mesaulius]]). Eumaeus wishes Odysseus home “as I wish it, and as Penelope does, / and the old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus” (14.172–173). For the beggar, age is the mask he wears and the youth he wishes back: “If only I were young like that” (14.468, 14.503; see [[if-only-i-were-young]] and [[hebe]]).
