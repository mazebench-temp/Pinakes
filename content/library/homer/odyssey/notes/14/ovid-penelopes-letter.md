summary: Three things in Penelope's letter are told in this book from the swineherd's side. She questions every stranger who lands and gives him a letter for Ulysses, while Eumaeus says that every wanderer goes to his mistress and speaks deceits, and will believe none of them. The faithful keeper of the sty on her side is the host of this book, and the ambush she fears for Telemachus is the one he mourns.
refs: [14.122-130, 14.174-184]
---

**Every stranger.** “Whoever turns a foreign ship toward these shores goes away from me much questioned about you, and to him is given a paper marked by my fingers, to give back to you if only he sees you anywhere” (*Quisquis ad haec vertit peregrinam litora puppim, / ille mihi de te multa rogatus abit*, *Heroides* 1.59–62, plain renderings). The swineherd tells of the same welcome from the farm: “Whoever comes wandering to the land of Ithaca / goes to my mistress and speaks deceits; / and she receives him well and makes him welcome and questions him about each thing, / and as she mourns, the tears fall from her eyelids” (14.126–129). For that reason he will believe no one's news of his master, the beggar's included: “vagrants wanting keep tell lies at random” (14.124; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[penelope]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). Ovid's Penelope gives her visitors letters; the Odyssey's household has learned to doubt them.

**The faithful keeper of the sty.** On her side, she writes, are the keeper of the cattle and the long-lived nurse, “and third the faithful keeper of the filthy sty” (*tertius inmundae cura fidelis harae*, *Heroides* 1.103–104, a plain rendering). He is the host of this book, which begins and ends with his care for his master's substance (14.3–4, 14.526–527; see [[eumaeus]]). The waste she laments is his daily burden too. Among the last shames of the house she names “Melanthius, driver of the flock to be eaten” (*pecorisque Melanthius actor edendi*, *Heroides* 1.95, a plain rendering); here the swineherd must send the suitors the best of his boars, one of his men driving one to the city “under compulsion” (14.18–19, 14.26–28, 14.105–108; see [[the-suitors]]).

**The ambush.** “He was almost taken from me lately by an ambush, while he made ready to go to Pylos against the will of all” (*ille per insidias paene est mihi nuper ademptus, / dum parat invitis omnibus ire Pylon*, *Heroides* 1.99–100, plain renderings). In this book the swineherd grieves for him: “he has gone in search of news of his father / to sacred Pylos; and the noble suitors / lie in ambush for him on his way home” (14.179–181; see [[telemachus]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Ovid's Penelope speaks of the danger as past; in the poem it still lies ahead, on the voyage home.
