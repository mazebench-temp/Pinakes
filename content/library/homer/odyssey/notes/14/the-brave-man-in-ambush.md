---
summary: The Cretan of the false tale describes himself as Idomeneus describes the brave man: when he picked the best men for an ambush, his proud spirit never foresaw death, and he leapt out first of all (14.216–221).
refs: [14.217-221]
---

The stranger's portrait of his younger self is the brave man of Idomeneus' speech in *Iliad* 13: “Truly Ares and Athena gave me daring / and the strength that breaks men; whenever I picked for an ambush / the best men, planting evils for the enemy, / never did my proud spirit foresee death, / but leaping out far the first I would kill with my spear / whatever man of the enemy gave way before me with his feet” (14.216–221; see [[ambush]], [[tharsos]] and [[breaker-of-men]]). The ambush is again the place where a man's worth is shown, and the best men are chosen for it (*Iliad* 13.276–277); the coward of the Iliad sits in it “foreseeing the fates” (*Iliad* 13.283), while the Cretan never foresaw death, θάνατον προτιόσσετο. ὁπότε κρίνοιμι, “whenever I picked”, belongs with what goes before, not with what follows, and the next clause repeats in a new form the boast of daring with which the sentence began (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.217). The last words are obscure: the usual renderings, “whoever was inferior to me in speed” or “whoever fled before me”, give a poor sense in a boast of courage, and Monro would read, with Bothe, ὅ τε μὴ εἴξειε, “whoever did not save himself by speedy retreat” (on 14.221). The man who talks so is the Odysseus who praised Neoptolemus for the same calm in the wooden horse (11.523–532), and who will lead an ambush beneath Troy in the tale of the cloak (14.469–471; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).
