---
summary: Herodotus has a story that passes through every station of the beggar's false tale, Phoenician slavers, Libya, the Thesprotians and the oak of Dodona. He also has a king of Egypt who will not kill a stranger brought by the winds, and Greek raiders in bronze who plunder the Egyptian plain and end in the king's service, both told more fully with the raid on Egypt.
---

- **Phoenicians, Libya and Dodona.** The priests of Theban Zeus told Herodotus that Phoenicians had carried off two priestesses from Thebes, and that one was sold into Libya and the other to the Greeks, and that these women founded the first oracles among those peoples; the priestesses of Dodona tell instead of two black doves, one of which settled on an oak and said with a human voice that there should be an oracle of Zeus there. Herodotus' own view is that the woman sold to Greece was sold “to the Thesprotians”, and that, a slave there, she founded a shrine of Zeus “under a growing oak” (ὑπὸ φηγῷ πεφυκυίῃ), and later an oracle (*Histories* 2.54–57, plain renderings). The beggar's tale runs through the same places: a Phoenician “skilled in deceits” takes him on board for Libya, meaning to sell him there (14.288–297), the wreck carries him to the land of the Thesprotians (14.314–315), and their king tells him that Odysseus has gone to Dodona to hear the will of Zeus from the god's high-leafed oak (14.327–328): [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[phoenicians]], [[libya]], [[thesprotians]], [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]].
- **The king who spares strangers.** Proteus, king of Egypt, will not kill a stranger whom the winds have brought to his land, and sends Paris away unpunished (*Histories* 2.115.4–6), as the king of Egypt in the tale spares the suppliant raider, for he “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.276–284): [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[god-of-guests]].
- **Raiders in bronze.** Ionians and Carians who had sailed out for plunder were driven to Egypt and plundered the plain in bronze armor, and Psammetichus took them into his service and gave them land (*Histories* 2.152.3–154.1), as the Cretan's men lay waste “the beautiful fields of the Egyptian men”, and the Cretan himself, spared by the king, stays seven years and gathers goods (14.257–286): [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[piracy]].
