summary: The book is his. The swineherd who built his master's fold himself drives off his dogs from the beggar, takes him in and feeds him the slaves' fare, mourns his master to his master's face, refuses the stranger's oath and his wager, sacrifices his best boar for him and gives him the chine, lends him his own spare cloak for the night, and goes out armed to sleep beside the boars (14.1–533).

**The swineherd.** Odysseus goes up from the harbor to the place “where Athena / had shown him the brilliant swineherd, who cared most for his substance / of all the servants that brilliant Odysseus had acquired” (14.2–4; 13.404–411; see [[brilliant]]). The poem shows the man before it names him. He sits in the porch of the fold he had built himself, “while his master was away, / without his mistress and old Laertes”, of hauled stones topped with wild pear and fenced with split oak (14.5–12; see [[fold]], [[penelope]] and [[laertes]]); within it are twelve sties of fifty breeding sows each, while the boars, three hundred and sixty, lie outside, “far fewer; for these kept dwindling, eaten / by the godlike suitors, since the swineherd sent them / always the best of all the well-fed fat hogs” (14.13–20; see [[sties]], [[boar]] and [[the-suitors]]). Four dogs that he had reared lie beside them (14.21–22; see [[dogs]]). He himself is cutting an oxhide to make sandals; three of his men are out with the herds, and the fourth he has sent to the city with a boar for the suitors, “under compulsion” (14.23–28; see [[sandals]]). συβώτης, ὄρχαμος ἀνδρῶν, “the swineherd, leader of men” (14.22, 14.121), gives him a title of heroes (see [[leader-of-men]]). Callistratus suspected 14.22 for its count of the dogs and for the epithet; D. B. Monro finds the epithet “not unusual”, but thinks it singular that the dogs should be as many as Eumaeus' servants, and suggests that the verse once spoke of the δμῶες, in lines now lost, since “As the text stands οἱ ἄλλοι in l. 24 is obscure” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.22).

**The welcome.** The dogs rush at the stranger, and the swineherd, following quickly, drops his leather, drives them off with stones and speaks (14.29–37; see the translation's note on 14.33). His first words are of the disgrace the dogs would have brought on him, and his second of his master: “for, mourning and grieving for my godlike master, / I sit here, and rear fat hogs for others / to eat; while he, perhaps yearning for food, / wanders over the land and the city of men of another tongue, / if he still lives somewhere and looks on the light of the sun” (14.40–44). ἧμαι is “I bide”, though the metre is defective and the imperfect ἥμην may be the true reading (Monro on 14.41). Then food comes before questions: “But follow me, let us go to the hut, old man, so that you too, / when you have had your heart's fill of bread and wine, / may tell me where you are from and how many sorrows you have endured” (14.45–47; see [[hut]] and [[xenia]]). He seats the stranger on brushwood under the hide of a wild goat (14.49–51), αὐτοῦ ἐνεύναιον, “his own very bed-covering” (Monro on 14.51).

**The poet's address.** The stranger thanks him with a prayer, calling his host “stranger” (14.53–54), and the poet first names the swineherd by speaking to him: “Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus” (14.55). He is the only character of the Odyssey whom the poet addresses so, five times in this book (14.55, 14.165, 14.360, 14.442, 14.507) and fifteen in the poem, as the poet of the Iliad addresses Patroclus and Menelaus (see [[apostrophe]] and [[answering-him]]). His answer is the law of the host:

{{quote:e0ac437b-193a-5013-92c3-0ff09af9bc99}}

κακίων is “one more miserable” (Monro on 14.56), and πρὸς Διός recalls Nausicaa's words to her maids, “for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear” (6.207–208; Monro on 14.57; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[nausicaa]]). Eumaeus “means simply ὀλίγη, as the context shows: but he uses the set phrase ὀλίγη τε φίλη τε as a euphemism, in order to soften what he wishes to say” (Monro on 14.58; see the translation's note on 14.58). The reason he gives is his station, “the way of slaves” (14.59), that is, to be cautious and penurious (Monro on 14.59; see [[themis]], [[the-way-of-mortals]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]), and the slaves' fear of masters who are young, οἱ νέοι, with the article as in 14.12 (Monro on 14.61).

**What his master would have given him.** The master who is gone was a different master:

{{quote:28a39d1a-d29e-56b9-b7d2-39cdfabab936}}

ἐνδυκέως means “kindly”, “in gentle fashion”, or else “zealously”, *con amore* (Monro on 14.62), and ἐπὶ … ἀέξῃ “makes to grow onwards” (Monro on 14.65). ἐφίλει καὶ κτῆσιν ὄπασσεν is the half-verse of the Iliad's Phorbas, “whom most / of the Trojans Hermes loved, and granted him wealth” (*Iliad* 14.490–491; see [[phorbas-father-of-ilioneus]]). The κλῆρος, “allotment”, which in the Iliad means only a share in the common fields, is here a portion of land that a master gives a deserving slave, and must have been cut out of the master's own estate (Monro, Appendix II §6, citing 14.63–64). The wife is πολυμνήστη, “much-wooed” (see [[much-wooed]]). Odysseus will keep the promise before the contest of the bow, offering Eumaeus and the cowherd wives, possessions, and “houses built near my own” (21.214–215, a plain rendering). But the master “has perished”, and Eumaeus curses the race of Helen, since he too went to Ilios “to win satisfaction for Agamemnon” (14.68–71; see [[helen]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**The slaves' fare.** He kills two piglets, singes them, cuts them up and roasts them on spits, sets them hot before his guest with white barley meal, mixes wine in an ivy-wood bowl, and sits facing him (14.72–79; see [[spits]], [[barley-meal]] and [[cup]]): “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat” (14.80–81). The suitors, he says, have no regard for the gods, who “do not love cruel deeds, / but honor justice and the righteous deeds of men” (14.82–84; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]); they waste the swine and the wine (14.85–95); and he counts out his master's wealth to the master: twelve herds of cattle on the mainland and as many flocks of sheep, droves of pigs and herds of goats, and on Ithaca eleven herds of goats, “But I guard these swine here and protect them, / and I pick out the best of the swine with care and send it to them” (14.96–108; see [[possessions]]). When the guest has eaten, “then he filled the bowl from which he himself drank and gave it to him” (14.112): “The act being necessarily that of the host, the name of Eumaeus is not added” (Monro on 14.112).

**The master he will not name.** The stranger asks who bought him, in case he has seen the man (14.115–120). Every wanderer, Eumaeus answers, comes to Penelope with lies for the sake of his keep, “Quickly you too, old sir, would fashion a tale, / if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (14.122–132; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[pseudos]] and [[penelope]]); in Monro's paraphrase, “There are so many false tales brought by wanderers that we must disbelieve you too” (Monro on 14.122–132). The dogs and birds have eaten his master, or the fish (14.133–137), “for never again another / master so kind shall I find, wherever I go, / not even if I should come again to my father's and mother's / house, where I was first born, and they themselves reared me” (14.138–141): the parents of whom he will tell in the next book (15.403–484). “Him, stranger, even though he is not here, I feel shame / to name; for he loved me greatly and cared for me in his heart; / but I call him dear master, even though he is far away” (14.145–147; see [[dear-brother]]). He does not name Odysseus in speaking of him but uses ἠθεῖος, “properly a form of respectful address”, found elsewhere only in the vocative (Monro on 14.145–147).

**The oath refused.** The stranger swears by Zeus, the table of hospitality and the hearth of Odysseus that Odysseus will come home this very year, and asks his reward for the good news, a cloak and a tunic, only then (14.149–164; see [[oath]], [[hearth]], [[table]] and [[euangelion]]). The hearth was not Eumaeus': “The house of Eumaeus was not the ‘hearth of Ulysses.’ The words apply only to his own palace” (Monro on 14.159). Eumaeus answers: “Old man, I shall never pay you that reward for good news, / nor will Odysseus ever come home again” (14.166–167), and “let us leave the oath alone” (14.171). He declines the oath which the stranger offered, “but which (if 158 ff. are rejected) he had not actually taken” (Monro on 14.171); for ὅρκον Zenodotus read κεῖνον (Monro's critical note on 14.171). He grieves instead for Telemachus, gone to Pylos, for whom the suitors lie in ambush (14.174–184; see [[telemachus]] and [[ambush]]), lines that M marks with an obelus, “perhaps because Eumaeus could not know of the ambush” (Monro's critical note on 14.174–184), and then he asks the stranger who he is, in the host's questions of Book 1 (14.187–190 = 1.170–173; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]]).

**The listener.** The Cretan tale is told to him (14.192–359; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). The stranger asks him to see the man in the old beggar, “looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was” (14.214–215; see [[the-stubble]]), shows him the [[rags|rags]] he wears, “which you see with your own eyes” (14.343), and ends the tale at his door: the gods set the castaway “at the farmstead / of a man of understanding” (14.358–359).

**Doubt.** He is moved, but he does not believe the one thing that is true:

{{quote:4a9bcdaa-33ce-5cdc-8e67-767a5b921f9f}}

κατὰ κόσμον is “aright”: the poet meant to say εἶπες, but the parenthesis οὐδέ με πείσεις, coming before it, changed it to εἰπών (Monro on 14.363). He knows of his master's homecoming, νόστον being an accusative of the thing spoken of, “that he has come to be hated” by the gods, who let him die neither at Troy nor among his friends, where he would have had a tomb and fame for his son; “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (14.366–371; Monro on 14.366; see [[harpies]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). He lives apart among the swine and goes to the city only when Penelope sends for him because news has come (14.372–377), “but it is no pleasure to me to question and to ask, / ever since an Aetolian man beguiled me with a tale” (14.378–379): a homicide and wanderer who said that he had seen Odysseus among the Cretans with Idomeneus (14.379–385; see [[aetolians]] and [[idomeneus]]). “And you too, old man of many sorrows, since a god has brought you to me, / do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them; / for it is not for that reason that I shall respect you or make you welcome, / but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.386–389; see [[thelgo]], [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]] and [[pity]]). The stranger's tales will bewitch him all the same: he will tell Penelope that the man sat in his hut three days and nights, telling his woes like a singer, “so he bewitched me, sitting in my halls” (17.513–521; 17.521, a plain rendering).

**The wager declined.** The stranger offers a compact: if the master comes home, Eumaeus shall clothe him and send him to Dulichium; if not, let him set his slaves on him and throw him from a great rock (14.391–400; see [[rhetre]]). Eumaeus answers with irony: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life; / readily, after that, would I pray to Zeus, son of Cronus!” (14.402–406; see [[kleos]] and [[arete]]). The sentence gives the reason and leaves the rest unexpressed, “being sufficiently conveyed by the ironical tone” (Monro on 14.402 ff.). λιτοίμην is “I should be fain thereupon to entreat Zeus”, to make my peace with him; the reading ἀλιτοίμην, “I should (thereby) sin”, “is tempting, but gives a forced meaning to πρόφρων” (Monro on 14.406). “But now it is time for supper” (14.407).

**The sacrifice.** When the herds come home he calls to his men: “Bring the best of the swine, so that I may sacrifice it for the stranger / from a far land; and we ourselves shall have profit of it too, who have endured misery / for long, suffering because of the white-tusked hogs, / while others eat up our labor without payment” (14.414–417; see [[white-tusked]]). The victim is a boar five years old (14.419; see [[five-years-old]]), an epithet “proper for beef, but not for pork” (Monro on 14.419). Then, “nor did the swineherd / forget the immortals; for his mind was good” (14.420–421): he throws hairs from its head into the fire and prays “that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (14.422–424), with Odysseus beside him; he kills it with a billet of oak, lays raw pieces from all the limbs in the fat as first offerings, and stands up to carve, “for in his mind he knew well what was due” (14.425–433; see [[sacrifice]] and [[sacrifice-type-scene]]). The raw pieces were properly the gods' share, “but in the division among the guests one share was reserved for the host's especial patrons” (Monro on 14.428): of the seven portions “one he set aside for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia, / with a prayer” (14.434–436; see [[nymphs]], [[hermes]] and [[maia]]). Such carving, J. L. Myres told Monro, is still done in Greece with great ceremony, the host standing and putting fair equivalents towards each of the guests, “a clear survival from the plateless stage” (Monro on 14.432). He honors his guest with the long chine, “and gladdened the heart of his master” (14.437–438; see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]), and the guest answers with a blessing: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am” (14.440–441; see the translation's note on 14.441). Eumaeus: “Eat, strange guest, and take your delight in these things, / such as they are; and the god will give one thing and let another be, / whatever he wishes in his heart; for he can do all things” (14.443–445; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). He burns the firstling pieces, pours a libation, and puts the cup into the stranger's hands (14.446–448; see [[libation]]), and his own slave Mesaulius, bought from the Taphians, serves the bread (14.449–456; see [[mesaulius]]).

**The cloak.** On the stormy night the stranger tells a tale of a cold night at Troy, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him, or urge one of his companions / to do so, since he cared for him greatly” (14.459–461; see [[peira]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). Eumaeus takes the point: “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless, / and you have not yet spoken any word amiss or profitless” (14.508–509; see [[ainos]] and [[profitless]]), αἶνος being “a tale”, “account”, since “The meanings ‘fable’ and ‘praise’ are not clearly to be traced in Homer” (Monro on 14.508). The guest shall have what a suppliant should, in the words of Nausicaa's promise (14.510–511, after 6.192–193; Monro on 14.511), “for now; but at dawn you will knock about in your own rags. / For we have not many cloaks here, or tunics to change into / to put on, but only one for each man” (14.512–514; see [[rags]]): δνοπαλίξεις means that “Ulysses must wear his own rags again next day”, a word “doubtless colloquial, like so much of the vocabulary of the Odyssey” (Monro on 14.512). Telemachus will clothe him when he comes (14.515–517), three lines missing in several manuscripts and in place at 15.337–339 (Monro's critical note on 14.515–517; see the translation's note on 14.515–517). Then he makes the guest a bed by the fire and throws over him a cloak, “thick and large, which he kept by him to change into, / to wear whenever some terrible storm arose” (14.518–522; see [[cloak]]).

**The watch.** He will not sleep in the hut:

{{quote:78fbc443-3d2e-56bb-b986-3f3a7d8fadab}}

ὑῶν are “the boars” of 14.16 (Monro on 14.525); ὡπλίζετο is “furnished himself”, “took what he needed for his bivouac”, and the going out, as the main action, is told first (Monro on 14.526); κείων is a future participle, “to lie down” (Monro on 14.532; see the translation's note on 14.425, 14.532). The book began with the swineherd “who cared most for his substance” (14.3–4) and ends with the master's joy “that he cared so much for his substance while he was far away” (14.527; see [[sword]], [[spear]] and [[boreas]]).
