# 14.13 is the swineherd's fold, which the fold's Book 14 note claims, and this note excepts the line.

**The swineherd's house.** Odysseus finds Eumaeus “sitting in the porch” (ἐνὶ προδόμῳ, 14.5; see [[fold]] and [[sandals]]), the front of a house where Menelaus' guests are bedded at Sparta (4.302; see the Book 4 note). Eumaeus calls his dwelling a house: an Aetolian vagrant, a killer wandering far over the earth, “came to my house; and I welcomed him warmly” (14.379–381; see [[aetolians]] and [[the-killer-in-exile]]). The hut in which he lodges the beggar is his κλισίη (see [[hut]]).

**A house of his own.** A kind master, Eumaeus says, gives a servant who has toiled for him “a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife” (14.63–65; see [[eumaeus]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[much-wooed]]): so Odysseus will promise his two herdsmen wives, possessions “and houses built near my own” (21.214–215, a plain rendering). Eumaeus' first home was his father's and mother's house, “where I was first born”, which he names only to say that he could not find so kind a master even there (14.139–141); its story is told in the next book (15.403–484).

**The master's house.** The beggar asks for his reward “when he comes and reaches his own house” (14.153; see [[euangelion]]) and lays his wager on the master's coming “home to this house” (14.395; see [[rhetre]]); Eumaeus prays at the sacrifice “that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (14.424; see [[prayer]] and [[nostos]]).

**The houses of the tale.** The Cretan is one of the many sons “born and reared in his halls” (14.201); at their father's death the true-born sons give him “very little, and allotted me a house” (14.209–210; see [[nothos]] and [[lots]]), where μάλα παῦρα, “very little”, is to be understood again with the house, οἰκία (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.210); after nine raids “quickly my house grew” (14.233; see [[piracy]]). The Phoenician takes him “to Phoenicia, where his house and his possessions lay” (14.291; see [[phoenicians]]), a zeugma, since ἔκειτο, “lay”, suits the possessions but not the house (Monro on 14.291); the son of the king of the Thesprotians leads him “until he came to his father's house” (14.319), where the treasures of Odysseus lay stored “in the halls of the lord” and the king swore to him, “pouring libations in his house” (14.326–331; see [[possessions]], [[libation]] and [[thesprotians]]).
