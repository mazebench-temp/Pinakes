---
summary: The Cretan whom Odysseus pretends to be is the son of a concubine, “a bought mother”, honored by his father equally with his lawful sons but given very little when they divided the inheritance (14.199–210).
---

“I claim that my line is from wide Crete, / the son of a rich man; and many other / sons too were born and reared in his halls, / lawful sons of his wife; but me a bought mother bore, / a concubine; yet he honored me equally with his true-born sons, / Castor, son of Hylax” (199–204; see [[castor-son-of-hylax]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). This is the only παλλακίς of the Odyssey, and the only one in Homer said to be a son's mother; the Iliad's concubine is the woman over whom Phoenix quarreled with his father (*Iliad* 9.449–452). ὠνητὴ μήτηρ, “a bought mother”, makes her a slave, bought as Laertes bought Eurycleia (1.430), and γνήσιοι, “lawful sons”, and ἰθαιγενέες, “true-born”, set her son apart from the sons of the wife (see the translation's key-word row and [[nothos]]).

The father's honor lasts his lifetime. When the fates of death carried him off, “his livelihood was divided / by his high-hearted sons, who cast lots for it, / but to me they gave very little, and allotted me a house” (208–210; see [[lots]]). They divided it and assigned the shares by lot: the order of the verbs makes the division the main thing, “of which casting lots was a detail” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.209). The bastard makes his own fortune by marriage, by his excellence and by war, and becomes “feared and respected among the Cretans” (211–234; see [[cretans]]). In the Iliad two sons of Priam, “one a bastard and one born in wedlock”, ride in one chariot, and Telamon cared for Teucer in his own house, “bastard though you were”, as Agamemnon tells him (*Iliad* 11.101–103; 8.283–284).
