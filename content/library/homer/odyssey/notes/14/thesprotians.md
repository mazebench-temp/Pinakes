---
summary: In the tale he tells Eumaeus, after nine days at sea a great wave brings Odysseus “to the land of the Thesprotians”, where their king Pheidon takes him in without price, tells him that Odysseus has been there and has gone to Dodona, and sends him on toward Dulichium in a ship of Thesprotian men, whose crew plot to sell him (14.314–347).
passages:
  - 14.314-335 | In the land of the Thesprotians
---

**Cast up in Thesprotia.** “For nine days I was borne along; and on the tenth black night / a great rolling wave brought me to the land of the Thesprotians” (314–315). The first verse is the one in which Odysseus told Arete of his drift to Ogygia after the last shipwreck (7.253; nearly so 12.447), and in the second γαίῃ Θεσπρωτῶν, “the land of the Thesprotians”, stands where that account has “the island of Ogygia” (7.254, 12.448; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[black-night]]). The Thesprotians are named here for the first time in Homer. The wind, D. B. Monro notes, must have gone round in the storm from northeast to south or southeast, and he quotes J. L. Myres: from Crete to Thesprotia is the regular course of drift, whatever the wind, with the set of the main current of the Mediterranean, as St. Paul was “driven about in Adria” for fourteen days before he came to Malta (Acts 27:27). “The story, however, is none the less improbable, considering the distance” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.315).

**The king and his son.** “There the king of the Thesprotians, Pheidon, took care of me, / the hero, without price; for his dear son came upon me / overcome by cold and weariness, and led me home, / raising me by the hand, until he came to his father's house; / and he put a cloak and a tunic about me as clothing” (316–320; see [[pheidon]]). ἀπριάτην, “without price”, is the word of the Iliad's Chryseis, who is to be given back “unbought” (*Iliad* 1.99; see the translation's key-word row). The welcome follows the pattern of the Phaeacians': there too the king's child finds the castaway worn out by the sea, has him given clothing, and sends him to the father's house (6.127–261), and the king has a ship made ready to take his guest home (8.31–36; here 331–333). The last verse is the one in which Circe clothes Odysseus (10.542; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]).

**News of Odysseus.** “There I heard of Odysseus; for the king said / that he had entertained him and made him welcome on his way to his native land” (321–322). [[pheidon|Pheidon]] showed the stranger the treasure that Odysseus had gathered, “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil”, enough to “feed another man to the tenth generation” (323–326; see [[possessions]]), said that Odysseus had gone to Dodona to hear the will of Zeus from the oak, and swore, pouring libations, that a ship and companions were ready to take him home (327–333; see [[dodona]]). Odysseus tells Penelope the same in Book 19, in part in the same verses (323, 325, 327–328, 330, 332–335 = 19.289–299), and adds that Odysseus is now “near at hand, in the rich land of the Thesprotian men” (19.270–299; 19.271, a plain rendering).

**The Thesprotian ship.** “But he sent me off first; for a ship happened to be going / of Thesprotian men to Dulichium, rich in wheat” (334–335; see [[dulichium]] and [[rich-in-wheat]]). The king bade the crew take him “to King Acastus / with care” (336–337; see [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]]), ἐνδυκέως, “with all kindness”, in contrast to the crew's κακὴ βουλή (Monro on 14.337), for “an evil counsel pleased their minds”: out at sea they “contrived the day of slavery” for him, took the cloak and tunic and put a vile [[rags|rag]] and a rent tunic on him, and bound him in the ship when they put in at Ithaca for supper (337–347; see [[doulios]]). The sailors whom the king of the Thesprotians trusted break faith with his guest, as the Phoenician of the earlier part of the tale had done (288–297; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Eumaeus repeats this part of the story to Telemachus: the stranger “has escaped from a ship of Thesprotian men” (16.65, a plain rendering).

**Phoenicians, Libya, Thesprotia.** The second half of the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] passes through the same peoples and places as the story of the oracles that Herodotus heard in Egypt: Phoenicians carried off two priestesses of Theban Zeus and sold one into Libya, and the other, Herodotus thinks, among the Thesprotians, where she founded a sanctuary of Zeus under an oak, the oracle of Dodona (*Histories* 2.54.1, 2.56.1–3; see [[herodotus]], [[libya]] and [[dodona]]). Herodotus does not mention the Odyssey there, and nothing shows that either story knew the other.
