**Bought with his own possessions.** The stranger takes the swineherd for a bought slave and asks about his master: “My friend, who was it that bought you with his own possessions, / so very rich and mighty as you say?” (14.115–116). The verse-end is the Odyssey's formula for buying a slave. Laertes bought Eurycleia “with his own possessions” for the worth of twenty oxen (1.430–431), and in Book 15 Eumaeus will tell how Laertes bought him from the Phoenicians who had stolen him as a child (15.483; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Eumaeus has bought a slave of his own in the same way: “he had bought him from the Taphians with his own possessions” (14.452; see [[mesaulius]], [[taphians]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). The Cretan of the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] was the son of a woman bought for his father's bed, ὠνητὴ μήτηρ, “a bought mother” (14.202; see [[concubines]]).

**The Phoenician trader.** In the eighth year of the Cretan's stay in Egypt “came a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits, / a greedy rogue, who had already done many evils to men” (14.288–289; see [[phoenicians]] and [[apate]]). τρώκτης, “a greedy rogue”, is the word for the Phoenician traders who come to Eumaeus' island with countless baubles and carry the boy away (15.415–416; see [[syrie]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Barytone nouns in -της often have a hostile or contemptuous sense, as δέκτης, ἀλήτης and προΐκτης, the beggar and the vagrant, and “Gnawer” may suggest thieving vermin (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.289). He keeps the Cretan a year in Phoenicia and then “put me on board a seafaring ship bound for Libya, / having planned lies, so that I might take a cargo there with him, / but there, in truth, so that he might sell me and get an untold price” (14.295–297; see [[libya]] and [[pseudos]]). The partnership in a cargo was the purpose he avowed; the emphatic κεῖθι, “there”, and the change from ἵνα to ὡς mark the sale as the real purpose, not avowed (Monro on 14.296, 14.297). The cargo is the pretext; the man is the merchandise. The Phoenicians of the first false tale, told to Athena, were honest men, who did not wish to deceive him (13.272–277; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**Sold or set to work.** The [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors who are to take the Cretan to Dulichium plot the same end: “at once they contrived the day of slavery for me” (14.340; see [[doulios]]), and the Egyptians lead the captured raiders inland alive, “to work for them under compulsion” (14.272; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).
