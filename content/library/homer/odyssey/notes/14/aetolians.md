summary: Eumaeus will not believe news of his master because “an Aetolian man” once deceived him, a homicide in exile who came to his house and said that he had seen Odysseus in Crete with Idomeneus and that he would come home by summer or late summer (14.379–385).
# Unnamed: the Aetolian of the rest of the story (14.380-385).

**The Aetolian's tale.** Eumaeus explains why he no longer questions strangers:

{{quote:74854c60-cceb-59b2-be7d-557a1768c8cb}}

Αἰτωλὸς ἀνήρ, “an Aetolian man”, gives him only his people's name, as Φοῖνιξ ἀνήρ gives the Phoenician of the stranger's tale (288; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]); later in the evening the stranger names the Aetolians' leader at Troy, Thoas son of Andraemon (499; see [[thoas]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). The man is a homicide in exile, ἄνδρα κτείνας, “who had killed a man”, like the exile of the Iliad's simile who comes to a rich man's house in another land (*Iliad* 24.480–482; see [[the-killer-in-exile]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]), and Eumaeus “welcomed him warmly” (ἀμφαγάπαζον). ἐξήπαφε μύθῳ, “beguiled me with a tale”, is the verb of deceit (see [[apate]]).

**A lie like the stranger's.** The Aetolian's story is the kind of thing Eumaeus has just said that vagrants bring to Penelope for the sake of a cloak and a tunic (124–132; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), and it is made of the same things as the stranger's own: Crete and Idomeneus, and Odysseus soon to come home with many goods (382–385; see [[cretans]] and [[idomeneus]]). It was not far from the truth: Odysseus has come home bringing many goods, though without his companions (see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). Eumaeus says to the man he does not know is his master, “do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them” (387).
