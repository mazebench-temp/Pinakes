---
summary: In the raid on Egypt the Cretan throws down his arms and goes to the king: “and I went to meet the horses of the king / and clasped his knees and kissed them; and he protected me and pitied me, / and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears” (14.278–280); and Eumaeus promises the beggar what is fitting “for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (14.511).
refs: [14.276-284, 14.510-511]
---

The approach and the gesture are told, and the appeal is not: the man who has thrown away helmet, shield and spear goes to meet the chariot and kisses the king's knees, as Thetis kissed the knees of Zeus (14.276–279; *Iliad* 8.371; see [[arming-scene]] and [[gounata]]). The answer is acceptance and protection. The king pities him, takes him home in his chariot, and holds off the men who rush at him with spears, “and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.279–284; see [[supplication]], [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]). D. B. Monro takes “he protected me and pitied me” for a prothysteron (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.279; see [[hysteron-proteron]]). When the raid is told again to Antinous, in the same verses up to the killing and the capture, the speaker has no supplication: the Egyptians give him to a stranger they meet, Dmetor son of Iasus, to take to Cyprus (17.442–443, a plain rendering; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). 14.511 is Nausicaa's promise to the shipwrecked stranger (6.193; see [[much-tried]]).
