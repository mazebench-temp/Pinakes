summary: The suitors eat the master's swine with no thought of the gods' regard “nor any pity” (14.81–82); in the false tale the king of Egypt protects and pities the suppliant raider (14.279–280); and Eumaeus, moved by the stranger's sorrows, takes him in “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.361–362, 14.386–389).
# ἐλεητύν (14.82; ἐλεητὺς 17.451), the noun, only in these two lines; ἐλέησεν and ἐλεαίρων are the entry's.
grc: ["ἐλεητ*"]
---

**Without pity.** “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat, / with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard, nor any pity” (14.80–82; see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). ὄπις, “the gods' regard”, is the watch the gods keep on what men do, and ἐλεητύς, “pity”, the regard a man owes to others (see the translation's table of key words). The suitors have neither. The noun returns in the hall, when Antinous mocks the other suitors' gifts to the beggar, given “since there is no restraint or pity / in giving freely of what belongs to others” (17.451–452, a plain rendering).

**The king's pity.** In the false tale the Cretan, his men routed, clasps the knees of the king of Egypt, “and he protected me and pitied me, / and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears” (14.279–280; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[supplication]] and [[tears]]). The pity is given for the god's sake as well as the man's: the king holds back his people in regard for “the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.283–284; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]).

**The swineherd's pity.** The tale moves Eumaeus, though he does not believe it: “Ah, wretched stranger, truly you have stirred my heart greatly / by telling all these things, how much you have suffered and how far you have wandered” (14.361–362). He takes the stranger in, he says, not for his lies, “for it is not for that reason that I shall respect you or make you welcome, / but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.388–389; see [[aidos]] and [[god-of-guests]]). Respect and pity are what the suppliant asks for in the Iliad, “respect me and pity me” (*Iliad* 21.74, 22.82); the swineherd gives both to a man who has not asked. The poet's own word for his care follows the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]]: Odysseus tests him, “since he cared for him greatly” (14.459–461; see [[eumaeus]]).
