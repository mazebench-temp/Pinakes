summary: Eumaeus draws his tunic tight with his belt before he goes to the sties (14.72). With the cloak, the tunic is the guest's dress that the beggar asks for and is promised (14.132–516); the sailors of his tale leave him a rag and a tunic (14.342), and on the night at Troy he had gone out “wearing only a tunic” (14.489). The swineherd has no tunics to change into (14.513).

**The swineherd's tunic.** “So saying, he quickly drew his tunic tight with his belt, / and went to the sties” (14.72–73; see [[eumaeus]], [[belt]] and [[sties]]): the tunic is girded up for work.

**The guest's dress.** With the cloak, the tunic is the clothing the beggar asks for as his reward and is promised (14.132, 14.154, 14.320, 14.396, 14.516; see [[euangelion]], [[cloak]] and [[clothing]]). The sailors of his [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]] strip him of both, “and around me they put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, which you see with your own eyes” (14.342–343; see [[rags]]), in the words in which Athena had dressed him as a beggar (13.434–435; see the Book 13 note).

**Only a tunic.** On the night at Troy “all the others had cloaks and tunics” (14.478), but he had gone out with only his shield and his kilt, and woke Odysseus to say that “a god beguiled me / into wearing only a tunic” (14.488–489; see [[kilt]], [[shield]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). οἰοχίτων is found only here (see the translation's row οἰοχίτων).

**Tunics to change into.** “For we have not many cloaks here, or tunics to change into / to put on, but only one for each man” (14.513–514). ἐπημοιβοί are tunics for a change, as the spare cloak that Eumaeus throws over his guest is kept ἀμοιβάς, “to change into” (14.521; see [[cloak]]); the same word is said of the two bars that cross each other inside a gate of the Achaean wall (*Iliad* 12.456; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).
