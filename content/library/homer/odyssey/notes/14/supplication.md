summary: In the false tale the Cretan, his men routed in Egypt, throws down his arms and clasps the knees of the king, who protects and pities him and keeps off the angry Egyptians in regard for the wrath of Zeus, the god of guests (14.276–284); and Eumaeus promises the stranger what is fitting for “a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (14.510–511).

**At the king's knees.** The Cretan's companions, “giving way to arrogance”, have ravaged the fields of the Egyptians, and at dawn the plain fills with foot soldiers and horses; many are killed, others led off to forced labor (14.262–272; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[piracy]]). Then Zeus puts a thought in the raider's mind:

{{quote:1b47b74e-a622-556f-a889-709c7c714aa5}}

The suppliant disarms himself before he kneels: helmet, shield and spear are put away, and he comes to the king's chariot on foot and kisses his knees (see [[gounata]]). On the battlefield of the Iliad the gesture fails. Adrastus clasps the knees of Menelaus and is killed at Agamemnon's word (*Iliad* 6.45–65), and Tros and Lycaon clasp the knees of Achilles in vain (*Iliad* 20.463–472, 21.71–119). The king of Egypt accepts it: “he protected me and pitied me” (14.279; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[pity]]), the protection told before the pity that moved it, a hysteron proteron “of the common type” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.279; see [[hysteron-proteron]]), and takes the man home in his chariot “as I shed tears” (14.280; see [[tears]]). He holds back his own angry people because he fears the god who protects suppliants and strangers, Zeus “the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284; see [[god-of-guests]], [[zeus]], [[xenia]], [[menis]] and [[nemesis]]). The suppliant becomes a guest, and stays seven years (14.285–286; see [[guest-gifts]]).

**A much-tried suppliant.** In the hut the stranger never kneels. He is a guest and a beggar, under Zeus as strangers and beggars are (14.56–58; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]), and the swineherd calls him a suppliant only at the end, when the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] has been told: he shall not lack clothing, “nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way, / for now” (14.510–512), the words with which Nausicaa received the naked man on the shore (6.192–193; see [[much-tried]]).
