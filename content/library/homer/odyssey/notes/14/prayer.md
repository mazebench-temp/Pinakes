summary: At the evening sacrifice Eumaeus prays to all the gods that Odysseus may come home (14.422–424) and sets aside a portion for the nymphs and Hermes “with a prayer” (14.435–436); to kill his guest and then pray to Zeus, he says, would be fine fame indeed (14.402–406). The stranger blesses his host in Zeus' name (14.53–54, 14.440–441), but in the host's questions and the Cretan tale εὔχομαι is “claim” (14.189, 14.199, 14.204).

**At the sacrifice.** Before the boar is killed, the swineherd makes the first offering of hairs, “and he prayed to all the gods / that Odysseus of much wisdom should return to his own house” (14.423–424; see [[sacrifice]] and [[nostos]]). The prayer is told, not spoken, and has no invocation or claim, only the request that is the swineherd's one wish; when the meat is divided, the portion of the nymphs and Hermes is set aside “with a prayer” (ἐπευξάμενος, 14.436; see [[nymphs]] and [[hermes]]). The second verse is said again of Eumaeus in Book 21, when the cowherd has prayed to Zeus for his master's return and the swineherd prays the same; then Odysseus reveals himself to them (21.200–207).

**A prayer for the god of guests.** When the stranger offers his life as a wager on his master's return, Eumaeus answers with irony: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life; / readily, after that, would I pray to Zeus, son of Cronus!” (14.402–406; see [[rhetre]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). The killer would then be eager to entreat Zeus, that is, to make his peace with the god of guests: the aorist λιτοίμην refers to a single prayer to be made, and πρόφρων is eagerness to do a thing, not confidence in doing it. The reading ἀλιτοίμην, “I should (thereby) sin”, is tempting, but gives a forced meaning to πρόφρων and to ἔπειτα (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.406). The god to be appeased is the one in fear of whom, Eumaeus has just said, he takes the stranger in, “Zeus, the god of guests” (14.389; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]).

**Blessings, wishes and claims.** The stranger's first words to the swineherd are a blessing in the gods' name: “May Zeus grant you, stranger, and the other immortal gods, / what you most desire, since you have received me gladly” (14.53–54), and he thanks him for the chine with another: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me” (14.440–441). Elsewhere in the book εὔχομαι is not prayer. It is “claim” in the host's questions, “Who did they claim to be?” (14.189 = 1.172; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]), and twice in the Cretan's account of his birth, “I claim that my line is from wide Crete” and “of whose line I claim to be” (14.199, 14.204; see [[euchomai]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]); and when the wine moves the stranger to speak, εὐξάμενος is “with a wish” (14.463), the wish “If only I were young like that” (14.468; see [[the-mad-wine]] and [[if-only-i-were-young]]).
