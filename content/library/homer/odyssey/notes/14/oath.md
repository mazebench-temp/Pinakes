summary: The stranger offers to swear that Odysseus will come home within the year, by Zeus, the table of hospitality and the hearth of Odysseus (14.149–164), and Eumaeus will not take the oath (14.171); when he still does not believe, the stranger proposes a compact before the gods of Olympus, staking his life (14.391–400). In the false tale the king of the Thesprotians swore, pouring libations, that a ship was ready for Odysseus (14.331–333).

**The oath offered.** The swineherd has said that his master is dead and that wanderers who bring news of him are liars (14.122–147; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]]). The stranger answers that since his heart is “always unbelieving”, he “will not speak idly, but with an oath, / that Odysseus is coming home” (14.150–152), and names his reward if it proves true, a cloak and a tunic, which he will not take before (14.152–155; see [[euangelion]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Before he swears he declares his hatred of the man “who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (14.156–157; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]). Then the oath:

The guarantors are Zeus first, and with him the two things that bind a host to a guest, the table at which he has eaten and the hearth of the house he has come to (see [[let-zeus-be-witness]], [[table]], [[hearth]] and [[xenia]]). ἱστίη, “hearth”, is a sacred object in Homer but not yet the goddess Hestia, and it occurs only in this form of oath; the ordinary word is ἐσχάρη. The swineherd's hut was not the hearth of Odysseus, and the words fit only his own palace (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.159), where they are sworn again: by the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] to Penelope (17.155–156), by the beggar to Penelope, with “highest and best of the gods” for the table (19.303–307), and by the beggar to the cowherd (20.230–231). In Book 19 the last verse, “as the old month wanes and the new begins”, fixes the day of the return, the day after the beggar's talk with Penelope, which is the feast of Apollo (20.156, 20.276–278, 21.258), and the promise of return “on the day then about to dawn” ends that speech with full effect (Monro on 14.158–162). Here the oath is followed by a second, weaker conclusion (14.163–164), and Monro thinks it more probable that 14.161–162, or with Kirchhoff the seven lines of the oath, 14.158–164, were brought in here wrongly from Book 19: the prophecy to Eumaeus should be general, “he will come home, and will take vengeance”, and the precise promise is best kept for the last and most critical point of the story. He takes λυκάβας to mean a day of twenty-four hours, which suits Book 19, where the slaying of the suitors comes the next day; to Eumaeus the stranger could not have promised the return within the day, since it was still four days away (Monro on 14.158–162; see the translation's note on 14.158–164, where the English keeps “year”, and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**Refused.** Eumaeus will not pay the reward for good news, “nor will Odysseus ever come home again”, and he puts the oath aside: “But let us leave the oath alone, and may Odysseus / come, as I wish it, and as Penelope does, / and the old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus” (14.166–173; see [[euangelion]] and [[eumaeus]]). He declines the oath that was offered, though if 14.158 and the lines after it are rejected, it was never actually sworn (Monro on 14.171); Zenodotus read κεῖνον, “him”, for ὅρκον (Monro's critical note on 14.171). An oath, it seems, is not enough for a man who has been deceived before by a wanderer with a tale (14.378–385). The stranger reproaches him for it after his [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]]: “Truly the heart in your breast is very unbelieving, / seeing that not even with an oath have I won you over, nor do I persuade you” (14.391–392).

**The compact.** He offers instead a ῥήτρη, a compact, “and hereafter / let the gods who hold Olympus be witnesses for us both” (14.393–394; see [[rhetre]] and [[olympians]]). If the master comes home, Eumaeus is to give him a cloak and a tunic and send him to Dulichium; if not, “set your slaves on me and throw me down from a great rock, / so that another beggar too may avoid deceiving” (14.395–400). The stake is his own life, the penalty an oath calls down on the man who swears falsely. Eumaeus refuses this too, with irony: fine fame he would have if he killed the guest he had fed, and then had to make his peace with Zeus (14.402–406; see [[prayer]]).

**In the false tale.** The Cretan says that at the court of the [[thesprotians|Thesprotians]] the king “swore to me myself, pouring libations in his house, / that the ship had been drawn down and the companions were ready / who would take him to his dear native land” (14.331–333; see [[pheidon]] and [[libation]]). It is a sworn word inside a lie, and the same verses will be told to Penelope (19.288–290).
