summary: The stranger excuses the tale he is about to tell by the wine, which sets even a man of much wisdom to sing, to laugh softly and to dance, and brings out a word better left unsaid (14.463–466).
# ὀρχήσασθαι (14.465) is in the Book 8 note.
---

No one dances in the swineherd's hut. The dance is named in the stranger's preface to the tale of the cloak, when the cups have gone round on a stormy night: “the mad wine, which sets even a man of much wisdom to sing / and to laugh softly, and stirs him up to dance, / and brings out a word that is better left unsaid” (14.464–466; see [[the-mad-wine]] and [[wine]]). Song and dancing are the pleasures of a feast, as the suitors have them in *Odyssey* 1, “for these are the accompaniments of a feast” (1.152; see [[feast]]), and they stand together in Menelaus' list of the things of which a man has his fill sooner than of war (*Iliad* 13.636–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]). Here they lead to the last thing the wine does, the word that should not be spoken, and the word is the stranger's hint that he needs a cloak (14.467–506; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).
