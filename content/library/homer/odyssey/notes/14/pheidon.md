**The castaway taken in.** On the tenth night of his drifting on the mast a great wave brings the Cretan of the tale “to the land of the Thesprotians” (14.313–315; see [[thesprotians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]):

{{quote:c3713b8f-5548-5e6f-af7f-b3c285deda0f}}

ἥρως, “the hero”, is Pheidon, and ἀπριάτην, “without price”, sets him against everyone else in the tale who has the stranger in his power: the Phoenician who took him on board to sell him “and get an untold price” (14.297; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), and the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew who will contrive “the day of slavery” for him (14.340; see [[doulios]]). Rhianus read ἀπριάδην (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.317). αἶθρος, “cold”, is found only here and seems to mean exposure to the air; Zenodotus, Aristophanes and Aristarchus read αἴθρῳ, other copies λύθρῳ (Monro on 14.318, and his critical note; see the translation's note on 14.318). A king's child finds the castaway and brings him to the father's house, as Nausicaa found Odysseus on Scheria, clothed him and sent him to her father's house (6.110–315; see [[nausicaa]]), and the last verse is the one in which Circe clothed Odysseus (14.320 = 10.542; see [[circe]] and [[cloak]]).

**News of Odysseus.** In the king's house the stranger hears of Odysseus himself:

{{quote:0182867e-2108-5a0e-a6bf-7d155c1c0286}}

The treasure would feed “another (and so on) to the tenth generation”, one owner after another (Monro on 14.325), and its list, “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil”, is the ransom that the Iliad's suppliants offer for their lives, Adrastus, Dolon, and the sons of Antimachus (*Iliad* 6.48, 10.379, 11.133; see [[apoina]] and [[worked-with-much-toil]]), and the treasure in Odysseus' storeroom (21.10). Odysseus had come home that morning with such a treasure, the gifts of the Phaeacians, and had hidden it in the cave of the Naiads (13.361–371; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). Pheidon says that Odysseus has gone to Dodona to hear the will of Zeus from the oak, “how he should return to the rich land of Ithaca, / after being away so long, openly or in secret” (14.327–330; see [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]), ὅππως νοστήσειε being “how he should return—whether openly or secretly” (Monro on 14.329): the question that the soul of Agamemnon had answered for him among the dead, “secretly, and not openly” (11.455; see [[agamemnon]]).

**The oath and the ship.** “And he swore to me myself, pouring libations in his house, / that the ship had been drawn down and the companions were ready / who would take him to his dear native land” (14.331–333; see [[oath]] and [[libation]]). But the king sent the Cretan first, on a [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] ship that happened to be sailing for Dulichium, “rich in wheat”, and bade the crew take him to King Acastus “with care” (14.334–337; see [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]], [[dulichium]] and [[rich-in-wheat]]). ἐνδυκέως is “with all kindness”, in contrast to the evil counsel that pleased the crew (Monro on 14.337).

**Told again.** Eumaeus believes the tale except for what it says of Odysseus (14.361–365; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Odysseus will give Penelope the same report of Pheidon in nearly the same verses, the oath first and the treasure after it (19.287–299), and with “to his dear native land” (19.298, a plain rendering) for “to the rich land of Ithaca” (14.329).
