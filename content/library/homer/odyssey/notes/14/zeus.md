summary: Zeus is named in blessing, in the swineherd's creed and in the beggar's oath, and he governs every turn of the Cretan tale. Eumaeus receives the stranger because “they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars” (14.57–58); the beggar swears by Zeus and the table of hospitality (14.158–159); in his tale Zeus sends him to Troy and to Egypt, casts panic among his men and strikes his ship with the thunderbolt, and the king of Egypt spares him for fear of “Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.283–284), as Eumaeus will be kind to him “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (14.389). The night ends in the rain of Zeus (14.457).

**From Zeus.** The first words Odysseus speaks in the hut are a blessing: “May Zeus grant you, stranger, and the other immortal gods, / what you most desire, since you have received me gladly” (14.53–54; see [[olympians]]). Eumaeus answers with the rule of his house, “for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear” (14.57–58; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]), the words with which Nausicaa sent her handmaids to look after the stranger on the shore (6.207–208). In his account of the suitors Zeus gives the days, “as many nights and days as come from Zeus” (14.93), a verse in which D. B. Monro finds the day reckoned from sunset to sunset, night first (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.158–162), and he gives the raiders their spoil, “and Zeus gives them spoil”, though even they fear the gods' regard (14.85–88; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). The stranger leaves his news to Zeus: “Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods, / whether I might bring news of having seen him” (14.119–120).

**The oath.** When Eumaeus will not believe that his master is alive, the stranger swears: “Now let Zeus be witness first of the gods, and the table of hospitality, / and the hearth of noble Odysseus, to which I have come” (14.158–159; see [[let-zeus-be-witness]], [[oath]], [[table]] and [[hearth]]). ἴστω νῦν Ζεύς opens oaths in both poems, Hector's to Dolon and Agamemnon's over the boar (*Iliad* 10.329, 19.258). The beggar's form of it, with the table and the hearth, is sworn again by [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] to Penelope and by the beggar himself to Philoetius (17.155–156, 20.230–231), and in his oath to Penelope Zeus is “highest and best of the gods” (19.303–304, a plain rendering), where the lines that follow this oath stand again (see the translation's formula row and its note on 14.158–164). Eumaeus refuses it: “But let us leave the oath alone” (14.171).

**The god of the tale.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan tale]] every turn is from Zeus. “But when far-seeing Zeus devised that hateful journey, / which loosed the knees of many men” (14.235–236; see [[far-seeing]]), the people made the speaker lead the Cretan ships to Troy with Idomeneus; at home again, “for me, wretched man, Zeus the counselor devised evils” (14.243; see [[counselor]]), and his heart sent him to Egypt. There “Zeus who delights in thunder / cast evil panic among my companions” (14.268–269; see [[delighting-in-thunder]]), and “Zeus himself put this thought in my mind”, to throw down helmet, shield and spear and go to the king (14.273–278). On the voyage to Libya “Zeus devised destruction for them” (14.300), and the storm is told in the verses of the true storm of Book 12: “then the son of Cronus set a dark cloud / above the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it. / And at the same moment Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt at the ship; / and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus” (14.303–306 = 12.405–406, 12.415–416; see [[thunderbolt]], [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[cronus]]). In the true story the stroke was the Sun's revenge for his cattle (12.374–388; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]); in the false one Zeus gives no reason. “But for me, Zeus himself, though I had sorrows in my heart, / put the mast of vast length of the dark-prowed ship / into my hands, so that I might still escape bane” (14.310–312), where in the true story Odysseus lashed the mast and the keel together with the backstay himself (12.420–425). In [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] the king tells him that Odysseus has gone to Dodona, to hear “the will of Zeus” from the god's “high-leafed oak” (14.327–328; see [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). When the beggar tells the [[the-raid-on-egypt|raid on Egypt]] again, to Antinous, it is Zeus who “wrecked it all—for so he wished, I suppose” (17.424, a plain rendering).

**The god of guests.** In the tale Zeus also saves. The beggar runs to the king of Egypt and clasps his knees, and the king pities him and takes him home in his chariot (14.276–280; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[supplication]]); the Egyptians rush at the stranger with their spears, “but he kept them off, and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284; see [[god-of-guests]]). μῆνιν … ξεινίου is the Iliad's phrase: Menelaus says that the Trojans did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests” (*Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[menis]]), and Odysseus warned the Cyclops of “the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (9.271; see the Book 9 note and [[god-of-suppliants]]). Eumaeus answers the tale with the same god:

{{quote:e57e3fb6-e94d-5f44-82dc-746233a69e98}}

The text prints ξένιον, the only form of the title without ει in Homer. Monro reports ξείνιον in some manuscripts and, since ξεῖνος and not ξένος is the Homeric form, thinks that these readings may be right, and ξεινίη in the oath as well (Monro's critical note on 14.389; see [[god-of-guests]] and the translation's key-word row on Ζεὺς ξένιος). When the stranger offers to stake his life on his word (14.393–400; see [[rhetre]]), Eumaeus answers in irony: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life; / readily, after that, would I pray to Zeus, son of Cronus!” (14.402–406; see [[son-of-cronus]]). Monro renders the last verse “I should be fain thereupon to entreat Zeus” (Zeus of guests, that is), “to make my peace with him”; some manuscripts read ἀλιτοίμην, “I should (thereby) sin”, which he finds tempting but forced (on 14.406 and his critical note). In the tale told to Antinous there is no merciful king: the Egyptians give the captive to a stranger, Dmetor son of Iasus, to take to Cyprus (17.442–443; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).

**Father Zeus.** When Eumaeus honors him with the chine, the stranger blesses him: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am” (14.440–441; see [[father-zeus]]). He says the first verse again in the next book, when the swineherd bids him stay at the hut, with a new second: “as you are to me, since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery” (15.341–342). Eumaeus answers with the swineherd's piety: “and the god will give one thing and let another be, / whatever he wishes in his heart; for he can do all things” (14.444–445; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]).

**The rain of Zeus.** “And an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained / all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet” (14.457–458; see [[zephyrus]]). ὗε δʼ ἄρα Ζεύς is the half-verse of the rain that Zeus sent against the Achaean wall, “and Zeus rained / without ceasing” (*Iliad* 12.25–26). The cold of the night is the beggar's occasion for the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] (14.459–506).
