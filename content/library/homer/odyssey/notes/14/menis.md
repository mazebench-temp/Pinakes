---
summary: In the Cretan tale the king of Egypt spares the suppliant raider in regard for “the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.283–284).
---

The raider's men have ravaged the fields of the Egyptians, the Egyptians have routed them, and their leader, unarmed, has clasped the king's knees: “Truly very many rushed at me with their ash spears, / eager to kill me—for they were very angry— / but he kept them off, and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (Διὸς δʼ ὠπίζετο μῆνιν / ξεινίου, 14.281–284; see [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]], [[nemesis]], [[supplication]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). The anger of the king's men is human χόλος (κεχολώατο, 14.282; see [[cholos]]); the king fears the lasting wrath of a god, the μῆνις that Homer gives only to gods and to Achilles (see the entry). Menelaus reproaches the Trojans with the same words, that they did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests” (*Iliad* 13.624–625). The verb ὀπίζομαι, “have regard for”, belongs with ὄπις, the gods' regard for what men do, of which the suitors have no thought (14.82, 14.88; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]] and [[themis]]).
