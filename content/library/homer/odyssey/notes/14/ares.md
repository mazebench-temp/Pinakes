summary: Ares is named once, in the Cretan tale: “Truly Ares and Athena gave me daring / and the strength that breaks men” (14.216–217), the boast of a raider who loved ships and wars and javelins more than work.
# The Book 8 note's "^Ἄρης" claims Ἄρης (14.216).
---

**Ares and Athena.** The stranger asks Eumaeus to judge the man he was from what is left of him, “looking at the stubble” (14.214–215; see [[the-stubble]]), and goes on: “Truly Ares and Athena gave me daring / and the strength that breaks men; whenever I picked for an ambush / the best men, planting evils for the enemy, / never did my proud spirit foresee death” (14.216–219; see [[athena]], [[tharsos]], [[ambush]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). ῥηξηνορίη is found only here; it is formed from ῥηξήνωρ, “breaker of men”, the epithet of Achilles (*Iliad* 7.228, 13.324, 16.146, 16.575; 4.5; see [[breaker-of-men]]). The text and the translation put a stop after ῥηξηνορίην; D. B. Monro would rather join ὁπότε κρίνοιμι to the words before it, since it is not Homeric to begin a sentence with such a word in the middle of a line, and he takes the next clause as a fresh form of the boast of 14.216 (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.217). In the Iliad the two gods fight on opposite sides, and Athena brings Ares down twice, driving Diomedes' spear into his flank and striking him with a stone (*Iliad* 5.855–863, 21.403–408; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]); but on the shield of Achilles they lead out the sortie from the besieged city together, “and Ares led them and Pallas Athena, / both of gold” (*Iliad* 18.516–517). The Cretan names them as the two gods of war, and makes his love of war a god's gift as well: “But to me those things were dear which, I suppose, a god put in my mind” (14.227; see [[different-gifts]]).
