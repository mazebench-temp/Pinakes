summary: Pain and sorrows: the “shameful pain” the dogs nearly gave the stranger at the gate (14.32); the “other sorrows and groaning” the gods have given Eumaeus (14.39) and the sorrows left for his master's friends (14.137); and the sorrows a host asks to hear, “how many sorrows you have endured” (14.47, 14.185), which the beggar says a year would not be long enough to tell (14.196–198).
en: [pain]
---

**Pain.** The dogs rush at the stranger, who sits down: “There by his own farmstead he would have suffered shameful pain” (ἀεικέλιον πάθεν ἄλγος, 14.32; see [[dogs]] and [[would-have-had-not]]), but the swineherd runs out and drives them off.

**Sorrows.** Eumaeus speaks of his own: “And the gods have given me other sorrows and groaning” (14.39; see [[theos]]), and of his master's death, “So he has perished there, and sorrows hereafter are made ready / for all his friends, and for me most of all” (14.137–138). The sorrows of a stranger are what a host asks for once the stranger has eaten: “so that you too, / when you have had your heart's fill of bread and wine, / may tell me where you are from and how many sorrows you have endured” (14.45–47; see [[visit-scene]]), and at evening, “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows” (14.185; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). The beggar answers that “even for a whole year, / I could not come to the end of telling the sorrows of my heart, / all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods” (14.196–198), where we expect a word meaning “I could go on”, to which οὐ διαπρήξαιμι is equivalent (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.197). In the tale Zeus put the mast into his hands, “though I had sorrows in my heart” (14.310; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
