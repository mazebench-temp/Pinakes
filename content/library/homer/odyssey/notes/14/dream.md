---
summary: In the tale of the cloak Odysseus, lying in ambush beneath Troy, tells his companions “a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” (14.495), the verse of Agamemnon's report of the Dream in the Iliad, as the pretext for sending a man running to the ships. Aristarchus, as Monro reports, thought the line an interpolation.
# The entry's ὄνειρος claims the line (14.495).
---

**The Dream as a pretext.** In the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|beggar's tale]], in the third part of the cold night, he nudges Odysseus and tells him that he will die without a cloak (14.483–489). Odysseus “had this thought in his heart” (14.490), and propping his head on his elbow he says: “Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep. / For we have come very far from the ships; but would that someone / would go and tell Agamemnon, son of Atreus, shepherd of the people, / whether he might urge more men to come from the ships” (14.495–498; see [[odysseus]] and [[agamemnon]]). Thoas rises, lays aside his crimson cloak and runs to the ships, and the speaker lies warm in it till dawn (14.499–502; see [[thoas]] and [[eos]]). τόνδε, “this”, points to what follows, the device of sending Thoas (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.490), and the Dream is its pretext.

**Agamemnon's verse.** The first verse is the one with which Agamemnon tells the council of the elders of the Dream that Zeus sent him, in the likeness of Nestor, to bid him arm the Achaeans: “Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” (*Iliad* 2.56; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). That Dream lied at Zeus' bidding; this one serves Odysseus' device, and the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale]] does not say what it told him. Monro: “This line is perhaps an interpolation, as Aristarchus thought, from Il. 2.56. We expect to be told what this dream was, what it said, &c.”; the speech really begins with λίην γάρ, “For we have come very far”, but a form of address such as κλῦτε φίλοι cannot well be dispensed with (on 14.495; see [[aristarchus]]).
