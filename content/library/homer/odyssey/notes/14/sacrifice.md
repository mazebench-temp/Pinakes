**The first meal.** Eumaeus' welcome begins at the sties: he “went to the sties, where the herds of porkers were penned. / From there he chose two and brought them, and sacrificed them both, / and singed them and cut them up and threaded them on spits” (14.73–75; see [[sties]] and [[spits]]). The meat comes to the guest “hot, on the spits as they were”, with white barley meal sprinkled on it (14.77; see [[barley-meal]]). ἱερεύω, “sacrifice”, is the verb of every killing for a meal, but nothing is said here of the gods' share: the porkers are “what slaves have here” (14.80–81; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

**The suitors' victims.** The best boar goes to the city every day, and one of the four herdsmen is away taking it there, “so that they might sacrifice it and have their hearts' fill of meat” (14.26–28; see [[the-suitors]]). Eumaeus counts the waste: “For as many nights and days as come from Zeus, / they never sacrifice one victim only, nor two” (14.93–94), as he has just said that the gods do not love cruel deeds (14.83–84; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). In the false tale the Cretan, before he sailed for Egypt, provided his companions with many victims for six days “to sacrifice to the gods and to prepare a feast for themselves” (14.249–251; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).

**The evening sacrifice.** At nightfall the swineherd sacrifices again, now for the guest: “Bring the best of the swine, so that I may sacrifice it for the stranger / from a far land” (14.414–415). The best boar is the one the suitors are always sent (14.19, 14.108), and the victim, “a very fat boar, five years old” (14.419), is described in the words of Agamemnon's ox for Zeus (*Iliad* 2.402–403), an imitation, or a parody: “five years old” is proper for beef, but not for pork (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.419, after Pierron; see [[five-years-old]] and [[boar]]). Then the rite is told in full:

The boar is set by the hearth (ἐσχάρη; see [[hearth]]); no altar is named in the hut. The first offering is hairs from the head thrown into the fire: ἀπαρχόμενος means cutting off as a firstling piece (ἀποτέμνων ὡς ἄργμα), and so governs τρίχας, as in Agamemnon's oath over the boar, κάπρου ἀπὸ τρίχας ἀρξάμενος (Monro on 14.422, comparing *Iliad* 19.254); Nestor made the same offering at Pylos (3.446; ἀπάρχομαι and ὠμοθετέω: the translation's table of key words). With it goes the prayer “to all the gods” that Odysseus should come home (14.423–424; see [[prayer]] and [[nostos]]). The blow is struck with a billet of oak kept back from the wood he split, κείων, “splitting”, a homograph of the κείων, “to lie down”, of the book's last lines (see the translation's note on 14.425, 14.532). The swineherd cuts pieces of raw meat from each of the limbs as first offerings, lays them in the fat and burns them with barley meal (14.427–429): this, properly speaking, is the gods' share of the sacrifice (Monro on 14.428), and the rest is cut up, spitted and roasted in the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse (14.430–431 = *Iliad* 1.465–466; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]).

**The portions.** Eumaeus carves, “for in his mind he knew well what was due”, and divides the meat into seven portions: “one he set aside for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia, / with a prayer, and the others he gave to each” (14.433–436; see [[nymphs]], [[hermes]] and [[maia]]). The gods have had their share; this portion, set apart in the division among the guests, is for the host's own patrons (Monro on 14.428). Monro doubted that the two verses are genuine, since the usual Homeric form of the name is Ἑρμείας, not Ἑρμῇ; he notes too that the chine, the portion of honor, would be given before the division (on 14.435). The nymphs are the herdsman's gods: Eumaeus will pray to the nymphs of the spring, reminding them of the thigh-pieces Odysseus burned to them (17.240–243), as Autolycus used to burn thigh-pieces of lambs and kids to Hermes (19.396–398). The guest's honor is the long chine, the portion with which Agamemnon honored Ajax (14.437–438; *Iliad* 7.321; see [[feast]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]). When the meal is ready Eumaeus “made an offering of the firstling pieces to the everlasting gods”, ἄργματα, pieces such as those described at 14.428 (Monro on 14.446), and poured a libation before he gave the cup to his guest (14.446–447; see [[libation]]). The beggar will boast in the next book that, by the favor of Hermes, who gives grace to the works of all men, no mortal could rival him at a servant's work, at building a fire, splitting wood, carving, roasting and pouring wine (15.319–324): it is the work Eumaeus does here.
