summary: The verse with which Agamemnon honors Ajax is used in this book, with another name, of the swineherd honoring his disguised master, and the feast before it follows the Iliad's, from the five-year-old victim to the long chine. Plato calls the chine the fitting honor for a man in his prime and brave; here it goes to an old beggar, who thanks his host for honoring him “such as I am” and that night twice wishes that he were young.

**The same feast.** In the Iliad Agamemnon sacrifices an ox, “a male, five years old, to the son of Cronus, supreme in might”, the meat is cut up, “and roasted it carefully, and drew it all off”, and “Ajax was honored with the long chine / by the hero, son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon” (*Iliad* 7.314–322). In the swineherd's hut the men bring in “a very fat boar, five years old” (14.419), the meat is roasted in the same verse (14.431; *Iliad* 7.318, 1.466), and “he honored Odysseus with the long chine / of the white-tusked boar, and gladdened the heart of his master” (14.437–438). The first of these lines is the verse of Ajax with another name, νώτοισιν δʼ Ὀδυσῆα διηνεκέεσσι γέραιρεν (*Iliad* 7.321). Monro takes the boar's age from another sacrifice of Agamemnon's, the ox “a fat one, five years old” of *Iliad* 2.402–403, as an “imitation (or parody?)”, since “‘five years old’ is right for an ox, but does not apply in the case of a pig, which at that age is too old for use” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.419, and Appendix II §2; see [[five-years-old]] and [[sacrifice]]). The honor comes after the division into seven portions, one of them set aside for the nymphs and for Hermes (14.434–436). Monro, who doubts those two lines because Ἑρμῇ is not the usual Homeric form of the god's name, adds that “the giving of the chine as γέρας would come before the division” (Monro on 14.435; see [[hermes]] and [[geras]]).

**The honor and the man.** Socrates gives the honor its reason: Homer said that Ajax, when he had distinguished himself in war, was honored with the long chine, “as this is the fitting honor for a man in his prime and brave”, τῷ ἡβῶντί τε καὶ ἀνδρείῳ, “from which, together with the honor, he will also increase his strength” (*Republic* 468c–d). In this book the chine goes to an old beggar, whom the swineherd had first fed on porkers, “what slaves have here”, because the fat hogs go to the suitors (14.80–81). Odysseus thanks him with the verb of the honor: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am” (ὅττι τε τοῖον ἐόντʼ ἀγαθοῖσι γεραίρεις, 14.440–441). Later that night, beginning his [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|story of an ambush at Troy]], he twice speaks Nestor's wish, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (εἴθʼ ὣς ἡβώοιμι βίη τέ μοι ἔμπεδος εἴη, 14.468, 14.503; *Iliad* 7.157, 11.670, 23.629; see [[if-only-i-were-young]]), with the verb of Plato's “in his prime”; Monro found the formula “less appropriate here, where the story turns upon cunning rather than prowess” (Monro, Additional Notes and Corrections, p. 287, on 14.468). The poet does not let the honor go astray. The heart that the chine gladdens is that of the ἄναξ, “his master” (14.438): the swineherd honors his lord without knowing him (see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]).
