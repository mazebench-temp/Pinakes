---
summary: The Cretan of the beggar's tale is a bastard in all but the word: the son of a rich man by “a bought mother … / a concubine”, honored by his father “equally with his true-born sons”, and left with very little by them when the father died (14.199–210).
refs: [14.202-203]
---

“I claim that my line is from wide Crete, / the son of a rich man; and many other / sons too were born and reared in his halls, / lawful sons of his wife; but me a bought mother bore, / a concubine; yet he honored me equally with his true-born sons” (γνήσιοι ἐξ ἀλόχου· ἐμὲ δʼ ὠνητὴ τέκε μήτηρ / παλλακίς, 14.199–203; see [[concubines]], [[meter]] and [[time]]). The word νόθος is not used, but the case is the entry's: γνήσιοι, “lawful sons”, is its opposite, and ἰθαιγενεῖς, “true-born”, is found only here in Homer (ἰθαιγενέεσσιν, 14.203), a word whose ι is of uncertain quantity (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.203). The father's favor ends with his life: “his livelihood was divided / by his high-hearted sons, who cast lots for it, / but to me they gave very little, and allotted me a house” (14.208–210; see [[lots]], [[kasignetos]] and [[olbos]]); they “cast lots for it”, that is, divided it and assigned the shares by lot (Monro on 14.209). Where the Iliad's bastards fight beside their brothers, this one makes his fortune alone, by marriage and by raiding (14.211–234; see [[arete]] and [[piracy]]).
