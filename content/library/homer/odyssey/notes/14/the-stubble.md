---
summary: The figure closes the first part of the Cretan tale, the birth, the inheritance and the marriage of the bastard son, and opens the boast of his prowess in war (14.199–221).
---

The stranger has given his line and his fortunes: the son of a rich Cretan by a bought concubine, honored by his father, left little by his half-brothers, married into a wealthy family “for my excellence, since I was no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (14.199–213; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[nothos]] and [[marriage]]). Then he turns to the old man the swineherd sees before him: “but now all that has left me; / but even so, looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was; for truly great misery holds me, beyond measure” (14.213–215). δύη, “misery”, is the word of the evil he will come to again at the hands of the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors, “a bane of misery” (14.338; the translation's table of key words). The crop the stubble shows is the warrior of the next verses, given daring by Ares and Athena and first out of every ambush (14.216–221; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). Eumaeus will doubt only what the tale says of his master; the stranger's sufferings and wanderings stir his heart (14.361–365; see [[pity]]).
