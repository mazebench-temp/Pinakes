---
summary: In the false tale the king of Egypt “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284); and Eumaeus keeps the stranger “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.389).
---

Two of the four uses of the title in Homer are in this book, one in the tale and one in the answer to it (see the general article). The god whom the king respects when he spares a raider at his knees is the god whose wrath, Menelaus said, would destroy Troy (*Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[supplication]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). On the printed ξένιον, and on the prayer that Eumaeus says he would have to make if he killed his guest (14.405–406), see the Book 14 note on [[zeus]].
