summary: A kind master gives a faithful servant “a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife” (14.63–64); in the false tale the Cretan is the son of a concubine beside the lawful sons of his father's wife (14.199–206), wins a wife of a wealthy family by his prowess (14.211–213) and leaves his wedded wife a month after coming home from Troy (14.244–246); and the stranger swears that Odysseus will punish whoever dishonors his wife and son (14.163–164).
# "ἠγαγόμην δὲ γυναῖκα" (14.211), only here; ἄλοχον, ἀλόχου and ἀλόχῳ (14.164, 14.202, 14.245) are claimed.
grc: ["ἠγαγόμην δὲ γυναῖκα"]
en: [took a wife]
refs: [14.64]
---

**A wife from the master.** Eumaeus' complaint is that the master who loved him is gone, the master who would have given him “such as a kind-hearted master gives to his servant, / a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife, / when he has toiled much for him” (14.63–65; see [[slavery-and-freedom]], [[lots]] and [[much-wooed]]). πολύμνηστος is the epithet of Penelope herself, “the much-wooed queen” (4.770; 23.149): a wife whom many sought is the reward of the best service. The hope is answered in Book 21, when Odysseus makes himself known to the swineherd and the cowherd and promises them wives and possessions and houses near his own (21.213–216).

**The wife and the concubine.** The Cretan's father had many sons “born and reared in his halls, / lawful sons of his wife; but me a bought mother bore, / a concubine” (14.201–203; see [[concubines]] and [[nothos]]). The γνήσιοι are the sons of the ἄλοχος, the wedded wife; the son of the bought woman is honored equally while his father lives and loses his share when he dies (14.203–210). He makes his own way into a family of standing: “And I took a wife from people of many allotments / for my excellence, since I was no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (14.211–213; see [[arete]]). Back from Troy, he stays only a month, “taking delight in my children / and my wedded wife and my possessions”, before his heart bids him sail to Egypt (14.244–246; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).

**Penelope.** The wife in the background of every speech is Penelope. Eumaeus says she weeps over each wanderer's tale, “as is the custom of a woman, when her husband has perished far away” (14.130; see [[lament]] and [[posis]]); the suitors “will not woo / justly, nor go back to their own” (14.90–91; see [[the-suitors]]); and the stranger's oath ends with the vengeance Odysseus will take “on whoever / here dishonors his wife and his shining son” (14.163–164; see [[oath]] and [[penelope]]).
