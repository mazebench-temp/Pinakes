**The father in the tale.** Eumaeus has asked the stranger who he is among men and where he comes from (14.187–190; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[who-are-you-among-men]]), and the answer begins with a father:

{{quote:7e3a8dcd-0a82-5cc1-835c-f2052417114c}}

εὔχομαι is “claim” here, twice: “I claim that my line is from wide Crete”, “of whose line I claim to be” (14.199, 14.204; see [[euchomai]] and [[crete]]). The other sons are γνήσιοι ἐξ ἀλόχου, “lawful sons of his wife”, a word that the Iliad uses once, of Priam's son Antiphus beside his bastard brother Isus (*Iliad* 11.102; see [[isus]]); the teller's mother was ὠνητή, “bought”, a παλλακίς, “concubine” (see [[concubines]] and [[nothos]]). Yet Castor “honored me equally with his true-born sons”, as Theano reared Antenor's bastard Pedaeus “like her own dear children” (*Iliad* 5.70–71; see [[pedaeus]]) and Telamon cared for Teucer in his own house, “bastard though you were” (*Iliad* 8.283–284; see [[teucer]]). The quantity of the ι in ἰθαιγενέεσσι, “true-born”, is not certain (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.203). The father himself is “honored … by the people like a god / for his prosperity and wealth and his glorious sons” (14.205–206), in the verse-end that the Iliad gives to priests and leaders (see [[honored-like-a-god]] and [[olbos]]).

**The division.** “But the fates of death came and carried him away / to the house of Hades; and his livelihood was divided / by his high-hearted sons, who cast lots for it, / but to me they gave very little, and allotted me a house” (14.207–210; see [[ker-doom]], [[hades]] and [[lots]]). They divided the estate and assigned the shares by lot, and the order of the verbs marks the division as the main thing, the casting of lots as a detail (Monro on 14.209); with οἰκία, “a house”, μάλα παῦρα, “very little”, is to be understood again from the clause before (Monro on 14.210). So the bastard makes his own way. He takes a wife “from people of many allotments / for my excellence, since I was no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (14.211–213; see [[arete]]). ἀνθρώπων, “people”, stands for the wife's family or tribe (Monro on 14.211), and πολύκληρος, “of many allotments”, implies for Monro an inequality in land that could arise only where land was held by private owners (Monro, Appendix II §6). Then he grows rich by raiding before the war at Troy (14.229–234; see [[piracy]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).
