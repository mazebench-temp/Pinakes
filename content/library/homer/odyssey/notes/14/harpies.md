summary: Eumaeus explains his master's disappearance with the words of Telemachus in Book 1: had Odysseus fallen at Troy, the Achaeans would have made him a tomb and he would have won fame for his son, “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (14.368–371).
# The entry's Ἅρπυι* claims ἅρπυιαι (14.371).
---

**Snatched away.** Eumaeus will not believe the stranger's oath, for he knows that his master “was hated by all the gods / utterly, since they did not bring him down among the Trojans / or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war. / Then all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him, / and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come. / But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (14.366–371; see [[olympians]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[kleos]]). The last four verses are Telemachus' words to the disguised Athena (1.238–241; see the Book 1 note): the son and the servant explain the loss of Odysseus in the same way. D. B. Monro holds that the only true repetition is the last verse: 14.369–370 are wanting in some manuscripts and have come in from 1.239–240, and 1.238 has come in from 14.368, since in Book 1 “Then all the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him” can refer only to death among the Trojans (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §10, and his critical note on 14.369–370). ἅρπυιαι are “the snatchers”, a word formed like ὄργυια and ἄγυια, and the verb from which it comes is probably hidden in ἀνηρείψαντο, for which Fick proposed a form of ἀν-αρέπω, “to snatch up”: “Thus there is a play of language—‘the snatchers have snatched up’” (Monro on 14.371). The verb is used of the storm winds that snatched Telemachus away, in Penelope's fear, of the Harpies that carried off the daughters of Pandareus, and of the gods who snatched up Ganymede to pour wine for Zeus (4.727, 20.77; *Iliad* 20.234; see [[ganymede]]).
