---
summary: In the tale of the cloak, Odysseus, lying with the others in ambush beneath Troy, props his head on his elbow and says: “Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” (14.495).
---

The verse is Agamemnon's report to the council in *Iliad* 2, the beginning of a deception (*Iliad* 2.56; see the general article and [[dream]]). Aristarchus thought it an interpolation from there, and Monro agrees that it may be: the dream is never told, and the speech really begins with the next verse, “For we have come very far from the ships” (14.496; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.495). In the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale]] it is a stratagem: Odysseus “had this thought in his heart” (14.490), and on the pretext of the dream a man is sent running to the ships and leaves his cloak behind for the teller, who was freezing without one (14.496–502; see [[crimson]] and [[thoas]]). The tale is itself a device, to get a cloak for the night (14.459–461); its point, Monro says, is that it hints at the need of a cloak without asking for one, and the closing lines, which ancient critics rejected, spoil it (14.504–506; Monro on 14.504–506).
