summary: φίλος and φιλέω in the book of a welcome: the gift “small but dear” (14.58); the master who “would have loved me with care” (14.62) and “loved me greatly” (14.146); Penelope, who receives every wanderer with news and “makes him welcome” (14.128); Eumaeus, who will “make you welcome” for Zeus' sake, not for a lie (14.388); the Cretan raider to whom “work was never dear” but ships and wars were (14.222–227); and Odysseus' wish that his host “might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me” (14.440–441).
# φιλέουσιν (14.83), φιλῆσαι (14.322) and φιλήσω (14.388), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [φιλέουσιν, φιλῆσαι, φιλήσω]
en: [my friend]
---

**Love and welcome.** φιλέω is to treat as one's own, and so to love and to make a guest welcome. Eumaeus remembers the master “who would have loved me with care and granted me wealth” (ἐνδυκέως ἐφίλει, 14.62; see [[anax]]) and “loved me greatly and cared for me in his heart” (14.146; see [[aidos]]). Penelope receives every wanderer “well and makes him welcome and questions him about each thing” (φιλέει, 14.128; see [[penelope]] and [[pseudos]]), as Pheidon in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan tale]] said that he had “entertained him and made him welcome” on his way home (ξεινίσαι ἠδὲ φιλῆσαι, 14.322; see [[xenia]] and [[pheidon]]). Eumaeus will make the stranger welcome too, but not for his tale: “for it is not for that reason that I shall respect you or make you welcome, / but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (οὐδὲ φιλήσω, 14.388–389; see [[god-of-guests]]). Of the gods he says, “The blessed gods do not love cruel deeds” (14.83; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]).

**What is dear.** The gift of a slave is “small but dear” (δόσις δʼ ὀλίγη τε φίλη τε, 14.58): Eumaeus means simply that it is small, but uses the set phrase as a euphemism, to soften what he wishes to say (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.58; see the translation's note on 14.58). The Cretan's tastes are a confession: “work was never dear to me, / nor the care of a household … / but ships with oars were always dear to me, / and wars” (14.222–225), “But to me those things were dear which, I suppose, a god put in my mind; / for different men take delight in different works” (14.227–228; see [[different-gifts]]): “things which a god made dear to me”, not being the things that would naturally be so (Monro on 14.227). He wished to sail to Dulichium, “where my heart was glad to go” (ὅθι μοι φίλον ἔπλετο θυμῷ, 14.397), and Eumaeus finds it “no pleasure to me to question and to ask” since the Aetolian deceived him (14.378).

**Dear ones.** The family words keep their epithet: Telemachus, “his dear son” (14.123) and “the dear son of Odysseus” (14.515), who was to be equal to “his dear father” (14.177); [[pheidon|Pheidon]]'s “dear son” (14.317); “his dear native land” (14.333; see [[dear-native-land]]); the friends in whose arms Odysseus did not die (14.368); and the companions at Troy, “Hear me, friends” (κλῦτε, φίλοι, 14.495). Twice the beggar greets his host “My friend” (ὦ φίλε, 14.115; ὦ φίλʼ, 14.149), and at the evening meal he blesses him: “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me, since you honor me with good things, such as I am” (14.440–441; see [[zeus]]).
