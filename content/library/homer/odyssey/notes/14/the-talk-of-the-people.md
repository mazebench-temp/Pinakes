summary: In the false tale the Cretan and Idomeneus could not refuse to lead the ships of Crete to Troy, “for the harsh talk of the people held us” (14.235–239); and Eumaeus measures what he does by what men would say, the disgrace if the dogs had torn his guest (14.37–38) and the fine fame he would have if he killed him (14.402–405).
# φῆμις (14.239), the only nominative in Homer, is now in the entry.
refs: [14.37-38, 14.402-403]
---

**The harsh talk.** The Cretan's account of how he went to Troy: “But when far-seeing Zeus devised that hateful journey, / which loosed the knees of many men, / then they bade me and far-famed Idomeneus / lead the ships to Ilios; and there was no means / to refuse, for the harsh talk of the people held us” (14.235–239; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[idomeneus]] and [[crete]]). The subject of “bade” is the Cretans (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.237), and the talk that held the leaders is theirs. For δήμου the archaic δήμοο may be restored (Monro's critical note on 14.239). The man who says that ships and wars were dear to him (14.224–226) went to the great war because the people would not let him stay; the talk of the people works here as it does on Penelope, who respects her husband's bed and “the talk of the people” (16.75, a plain rendering), and as the blame of men would have worked on Phoenix (*Iliad* 9.459–461; see [[nemesis]] and [[oneidos]]).

**What men would say.** Eumaeus' first words to the stranger are about reproach: “Old man, truly the dogs came near to tearing you to pieces / all of a sudden, and you would have poured disgrace on me” (14.37–38; see [[elenchos]] and [[dogs]]). A guest torn by his dogs would have shamed the host. The same reckoning, turned to irony, answers the stranger's wager: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life” (14.402–405; see [[rhetre]], [[kleos]], [[prayer]] and [[guest-gifts]]). ἐϋκλείη, “good fame”, is what men say of a man, and a host who killed his guest would have the opposite.
