summary: The swineherd's farmstead on Ithaca. Eumaeus built its high fold himself, of hauled stones topped with wild pear and fenced outside with stakes of oak, and made twelve sties within it (14.5–16); at nightfall the swine are stalled there (14.410–412). The poet, the beggar's tale and the beggar's hint all name the farmstead (14.32, 14.358, 14.504).

**The fold.** Odysseus finds the swineherd at work in the porch of his dwelling, beside the fold (14.5; see [[eumaeus]], [[house]] and [[sandals]]). It stands high, “in a place with a view all round” (14.6; see [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]), and it is περίδρομος, “with a clear space all around” (14.7), an open space “serving as the glacis of the fortress, to guard against surprise” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.7). Its high wall is built in the words of the Cyclops' fold, αὐλὴ / ὑψηλὴ δέδμητο (9.184–185; see the Book 9 note), of “hauled” stones, as the Phaeacians' place of assembly is fitted with hauled stones (6.267); Monro takes ῥυτοί rather as “dug”, “quarried” (on 14.10; see the translation's notes on 6.267 and 14.10). It is topped with a coping of wild pear (see the translation's row ῥυτὸς λᾶας; θριγκόω; ἄχερδος). Outside runs a fence of stakes split from “the dark heart of the oak”: τὸ μέλαν is the heart, and ἀμφικεάσσας is “splitting off all round”, so as to separate the softer outside and leave the heart. Aristarchus took τὸ μέλαν for the bark, but the bark, Monro objects, would hardly be called the black part of the wood, and stripping it off would not be splitting (on 14.12; see the translation's note on 14.12 and [[trees]]). So close-set stakes fenced the courtyard of Achilles' hut (*Iliad* 24.452–453; see [[hut]]). The swineherd “had built it himself for the swine, while his master was away, / without his mistress and old Laertes” (14.8–9), the words in which he is said to have acquired the slave Mesaulius by himself (14.450–451; see [[mesaulius]] and [[laertes]]). Monro sees the splendor of the Odyssey's world “in the palaces of Alcinous and Menelaus, and even (in its way) in the homestead of Eumaeus” (Monro, Appendix II §6).

**Within the fold.** Inside it the swineherd “had made twelve sties”, with fifty breeding sows to a sty; the boars lay outside, ἐκτὸς ἴαυον, “passed the night” (Monro on 14.16), with the four dogs, “like wild beasts”, beside them (14.13–22; see [[sties]], [[boar]] and [[dogs]]). When the herds come home in the evening the swineherds pen the sows in their haunts, “and an untold clamor arose from the swine as they were stalled for the night” (14.410–412): αὐλίζομαι is to be lodged in the αὐλή, the verb of the Sun's cattle that Odysseus hears lowing at nightfall on Thrinacia (12.265; see [[cattle]]). Eumaeus himself will not sleep in the hut, away from the boars: he goes out to lie where they sleep, “beneath a hollow rock, sheltered from the North Wind” (14.524–533; see [[hut]] and [[boreas]]).

**The farmstead.** σταθμός is the herdsman's whole station, the fold, the hut and the sties together (see the entry), and the book names it three times. “There by his own farmstead he would have suffered shameful pain”, the poet says when the dogs run at the beggar (14.32; see [[would-have-had-not]]): the farmstead is his own, Odysseus' (see [[possessions]]). In the tale he tells, the gods “brought me and set me at the farmstead / of a man of understanding” (14.358–359; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), and his story of the cloak ends with the hint that “someone in the farmstead of the swineherds would give me a cloak” (14.504; see [[cloak]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).
