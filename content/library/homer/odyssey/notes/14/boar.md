**The herd.** The fold that Eumaeus built in his master's absence was built “for the swine”, ὕεσσιν (14.8; see [[fold]] and [[sties]]). Within it are twelve sties “close to one another, beds for the swine”, and in each fifty “swine that sleep on the ground”, χαμαιευνάδες, the word of the swine that Circe fed on acorns (14.14–15; 10.243; see the Book 10 note). D. B. Monro calls these lines almost a parody of the description of Priam's palace (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.13–16), where the sons of Priam slept beside their wedded wives in fifty chambers of polished stone, “built close to one another” (*Iliad* 6.242–250; see [[priams-palace]]). The sows are “breeding sows”, θήλειαι τοκάδες (14.16; see the translation's row θήλειαι τοκάδες; ζατρεφής; σίαλος). The boars lie outside, ἐκτὸς ἴαυον, “lay” or passed the night there (Monro on 14.16), “far fewer; for these kept dwindling, eaten / by the godlike suitors, since the swineherd sent them / always the best of all the well-fed fat hogs” (14.17–19; see [[the-suitors]] and [[well-fed]]), and Eumaeus has counted them: three hundred and sixty (14.20). With them lie the four dogs “like wild beasts” (14.21–22; see [[dogs]] and [[like-a-wild-beast]]). Three of his men are out with “the herded swine”, and the fourth has gone to the city “to take a boar to the overbearing suitors, under compulsion” (14.24–28). Eumaeus' own account of his life is a line of this book: “I sit here, and rear fat hogs for others / to eat” (14.41–42).

**Porkers and fat hogs.** The swine are graded by who eats them. For his guest's first meal Eumaeus goes to the sties “where the herds of porkers were penned”, and roasts two of them (14.73–77; see [[sacrifice]] and [[spits]]): “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat” (14.80–81; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). χοῖρος, the young pig, is named in Homer only in these two verses. σίαλος is the fatted hog, the suitors' meat in Ithaca from the second book to the twentieth (2.300; 20.162–163, 20.251; see the Book 2 note). Swine are counted among his master's wealth on the mainland, “as many droves of pigs” as there are herds of cattle and flocks of sheep (14.101; see [[possessions]]), in the verse with which Nestor tells of the droves he drove off from Elis in his first war (*Iliad* 11.679; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and the Book 14 note on [[sheep]]). What Eumaeus guards on Ithaca he guards for the suitors' table: “But I guard these swine here and protect them, / and I pick out the best of the swine with care and send it to them” (14.107–108). He lives “apart, among the swine” (14.372).

**The herds come home.** At evening “the swine and the men, the swineherds, came near. / They penned the sows in their haunts to sleep, / and an untold clamor arose from the swine as they were stalled for the night” (14.410–412). ἤθεα, “haunts”, are the haunts and pasture of the horses in the Iliad's simile of the runaway stallion (*Iliad* 6.511 = 15.268), and αὐλιζομενάων, “stalled for the night”, is said once more in Homer, of the cattle of the Sun, whose lowing Odysseus heard from the sea (12.265).

**The boar of the sacrifice.** “Bring the best of the swine, so that I may sacrifice it for the stranger / from a far land; and we ourselves shall have profit of it too, who have endured misery / for long, suffering because of the white-tusked hogs, / while others eat up our labor without payment” (14.414–417; see [[white-tusked]] and [[poine]]). The best boar, which goes to the suitors every day, goes this once to the guest: “and they brought in a very fat boar, five years old” (14.419). πενταέτηρος is the age of the Iliad's oxen of sacrifice, which Agamemnon kills for Zeus (*Iliad* 2.402–403, 7.314–315; see [[five-years-old]]), and Monro calls the verse an imitation, or perhaps a parody, of the first of them; he reports Pierron's objection that the epithet is proper for beef, not for pork (on 14.419). By Aristotle's reckoning a boar is good for breeding only up to its third year, and its offspring are worse after that, since it no longer grows in size or strength (*History of Animals* 5.14): a boar of five years is full-grown and past its use as a sire. The rite itself is told in the Book 14 note on [[sacrifice]] (14.420–438): hairs from the head of the white-tusked boar are thrown into the fire, the boar is struck down with a billet of oak, and Eumaeus honors his guest “with the long chine / of the white-tusked boar” (14.422–425, 14.437–438; see [[trees]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]).

**Asleep beside the herd.** The book ends with the swine. When the men lie down in the hut, “the swineherd / did not like to sleep there, away from the boars”, and he arms himself against the night and goes out “where the white-tusked hogs / slept beneath a hollow rock, sheltered from the North Wind” (14.524–533; see [[boreas]], [[cloak]] and [[dogs]]). ὑῶν at 14.525 means the boars, which lie outside the sties (Monro on 14.525, referring to 14.16), and Βορέω ὑπʼ ἰωγῇ is shelter from the North Wind (Monro on 14.533; see the translation's row ἰωγή). So in Hesiod's month of Lenaion, when the North Wind blows, the horned and hornless beasts of the woods seek shelter in close lairs and “a hollow in the rock”, γλάφυ πετρῆεν (*Works and Days* 504–506, 529–533). The book began with the swineherd “who cared most for his substance / of all the servants that brilliant Odysseus had acquired” (14.3–4), and it ends with Odysseus' joy “that he cared so much for his substance while he was far away” (14.526–527; see [[eumaeus]]). In the next book Eumaeus sends the stranger to follow “the master's swine” (15.397), and at dawn the swineherds drive them out to pasture (16.3).
