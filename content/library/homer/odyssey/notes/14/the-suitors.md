summary: The suitors are seen from the swineherd's farmstead: they eat the best of the swine every day, waste the wine and devour the master's goods “with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard”, and lie in ambush for Telemachus on his way home from Pylos (14.17–28, 14.80–108, 14.180–182, 14.415–417); the stranger, eating Eumaeus' meat in silence, “was planting evils for the suitors” (14.110).

**The swine and the suitors.** The book counts the suitors' appetite before it names them. Of the boars outside the sties, “far fewer; for these kept dwindling, eaten / by the godlike suitors, since the swineherd sent them / always the best of all the well-fed fat hogs” (17–19; see [[antitheos]], [[sties]] and [[boar]]); one of the four herdsmen has gone to the city “to take a boar to the overbearing suitors, under compulsion” (26–28; see [[overbearing]]). Eumaeus complains to the stranger that he rears fat hogs “for others / to eat” (41–42), and serves him piglets: “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat” (80–81). Each of the goatherds, he says, brings them the best goat every day, and he himself sends them the best of the swine (103–108; see [[goats]]).

**No regard for the gods.** The suitors eat “with no thought in their minds of the gods' regard, nor any pity” (82; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). ὄπις by itself is the regard of the gods, their watch over human wrongdoing (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.82; see the translation's key-word row). Even raiders, Eumaeus says, fear it when they sail home with their spoil, but the suitors “know something—they have heard some voice of a god— / of that man's grim destruction, since they will not woo / justly, nor go back to their own, but at their ease / they devour his possessions overweeningly, and there is no sparing” (85–92; see [[possessions]]). ὑπέρβιον, “overweeningly”, is adverbial there and in the verse of the wine, “and they waste the wine, drawing it off overweeningly” (95; Monro on 14.95; see [[wine]]).

**Without payment.** When a stranger comes with news, those who sit beside him and question him in the palace are “both those who grieve for their master, who has long been gone, / and those who rejoice as they eat his substance without payment” (376–377), and at the evening sacrifice Eumaeus says that he and his men will have their share of the boar, “while others eat up our labor without payment” (417). νήποινον, “without payment”, is Telemachus' word for the suitors' feasting in the first book (1.160, 1.377).

**Evils for the suitors.** The stranger eats Eumaeus' meat “greedily, in silence, and he was planting evils for the suitors” (110); the poem gives the same half-verse to Telemachus as he goes from the farmstead to the city (17.27). Eumaeus knows of their ambush for Telemachus: “and the noble suitors / lie in ambush for him on his way home, so that the race / of godlike Arceisius may perish utterly from Ithaca, nameless” (180–182; see [[ambush]], [[noble]] and [[pylos]]).
