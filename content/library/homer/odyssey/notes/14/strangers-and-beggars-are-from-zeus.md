---
summary: Eumaeus answers the disguised Odysseus' thanks with Nausicaa's verses: it is not lawful for him to dishonor a stranger, “for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear, / when it comes from us”, since that is the way of slaves (14.56–61).
---

**The welcome.** The swineherd has driven off the dogs, brought the stranger into the hut and made him a seat of brushwood with his own goatskin on it, and the stranger thanks him with a blessing: “May Zeus grant you, stranger, and the other immortal gods, / what you most desire, since you have received me gladly” (14.53–54; see [[prayer]]). The answer is introduced by the poet's address to the swineherd himself, “Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus” (14.55; see [[apostrophe]]):

{{quote:e0ac437b-193a-5013-92c3-0ff09af9bc99}}

θέμις, “lawful”, is what is right by the order of things (see [[themis]]), and κακίων is “one more miserable” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.56). πρὸς Διός is explained on Nausicaa's verse (Monro on 14.57, referring to the note on 6.207; see the entry), whose words Eumaeus repeats: 14.57–58 = 6.207–208. The beggar of Nausicaa's maxim, πτωχός, is now literally before him, a beggar in rags (see [[rags]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).

**Small but dear.** Eumaeus means simply that the gift is small, as the context shows, but he uses the set phrase “small but dear” as a euphemism, to soften what he wishes to say (Monro on 14.58; see the translation's note on 14.58). The reason he gives is his condition: “for that is the way of slaves, / who are always afraid, when masters hold command over them, / the young ones”, and the way of bondsmen is to be cautious and sparing (Monro on 14.59; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]). Nausicaa, a king's daughter, gave the stranger clothes and food at once (6.209–214); the swineherd gives porkers, “what slaves have here” (14.80–81).

**Under the god of guests.** The rule holds through the book. Eumaeus will not believe the stranger's news, but he takes him in “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.388–389; see [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]] and [[pity]]); in the false tale the king of Egypt spares the suppliant raider in regard for “the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (14.283–284; see [[supplication]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]); and when the stranger stakes his life on his news, the swineherd answers that he would have fine fame indeed if he killed a guest he had fed (14.402–406; see [[guest-gifts]]).
