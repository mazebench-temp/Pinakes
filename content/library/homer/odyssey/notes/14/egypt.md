**To Egypt.** “For only one month I stayed, taking delight in my children / and my wedded wife and my possessions; but then / my heart bade me sail to Egypt” (244–246; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). Αἴγυπτόνδε, “to Egypt”, is the word of the command that sent Menelaus back “to Egypt, a long and grievous way” (4.483); five days out of Crete the Cretan halts his ships in the river, which the poem calls by the same name (257–258; see [[aegyptus-river]] and [[crete]]), and D. B. Monro thinks that Αἴγυπτος may mean the river here too, “as in 257–258, and elsewhere in Homer” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.246). At 275, “there in Egypt”, it is the land.

**The fields of the Egyptians.** His men, “giving way to arrogance”, laid waste “the beautiful fields of the Egyptian men”, carried off the women and the little children, and killed the men; the cry came to the city, and at dawn “the whole plain was filled with foot soldiers and horses / and the flash of bronze” (262–268; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[hybris]] and [[piracy]]). The Egypt of the tale has a city, a plain and an army with horses, and the survivors are led “up inland alive, to work for them under compulsion” (272): ἄναγον is “took up”, that is, inland, or perhaps “into their hands” (Monro on 14.272; see the translation's key-word row). The raid has the shape of Odysseus' own among the Cicones, where his men would not obey him and stayed feasting by the shore, and the Cicones came against them in the morning (9.39–61; see [[cicones]]).

**The king of Egypt.** The Cretan throws off his helmet, shield and spear, goes to meet the king's horses and clasps his knees; the king pities him, seats him in his chariot and takes him home, and keeps off the spearmen, “and had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (276–284; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[supplication]], [[xenia]], [[zeus]] and [[god-of-guests]]). The Egyptians of the tale keep the law of Zeus that Eumaeus is keeping as he listens (56–58).

**Seven years among the Egyptians.** “There I stayed for seven years, and I gathered many / goods among the Egyptian men; for they all gave to me” (285–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). The verse begins as Odysseus' account of his years with Calypso, ἔνθα μὲν ἑπτάετες μένον (7.259), and the next verse is that account's “But when the eighth year came round in its course” (287 = 7.261, with ὄγδοον for ὀγδόατον). “This chronology is not quite arbitrary”, Monro notes: “the seven years in Egypt take the place of the seven years that in the true history were spent in Calypso's isle” (on 14.285); for the unmetrical ὄγδοον Dindorf read ὀγδόατον, as in Book 7, and Monro suspects a trace of an older form of the numeral (on 14.287). Like Menelaus, who gathered “much substance and gold” in Egypt (3.301–302; see [[menelaus]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]), the Cretan leaves Egypt rich, and Egypt is again the land where a wanderer grows wealthy.

**Egypt in the Odyssey.** The tale's Egypt is the Odyssey's: a rich land at the end of a voyage that the poem describes, Monro observes, “more than once, and with a fair approach to correctness” (Monro, Appendix II §6). For the later Greek stories of raiders in Egypt and of Egyptian kings who spared or kept off strangers, see [[the-raid-on-egypt]].
