---
summary: Eumaeus, moved by the Cretan tale but not believing its news of Odysseus, tells the teller, “do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them” (14.387).
---

“Ah, wretched stranger, truly you have stirred my heart greatly / by telling all these things” (14.361–362), Eumaeus begins, and he ends by refusing the spell: “And you too, old man of many sorrows, since a god has brought you to me, / do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them” (μήτε τί μοι ψεύδεσσι χαρίζεο μήτε τι θέλγε, 14.386–387; see [[pseudos]] and [[daimon]]). Words bewitch the heart as Calypso's wheedling talk bewitched Odysseus (1.56–57) and as the Sirens' song bewitches sailors (12.39–44; see the entry). Eumaeus has been bewitched once before, by the Aetolian who “beguiled me with a tale” (14.379; see [[apate]]), and will not be again. He will be all the same: he tells Penelope that for three days and nights the stranger held him in his hut like a singer, “so he bewitched me, sitting in my halls” (ὣς ἐμὲ κεῖνος ἔθελγε, 17.521, a plain rendering; see [[singers]]), as Alcinous said of the same teller that he told his tale “skillfully, as a singer does” (11.368).
