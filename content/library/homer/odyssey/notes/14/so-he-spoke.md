summary: Ten closes. “So saying, the brilliant swineherd led the way to the hut” (14.48), and the swineherd's other speeches end in work: the belt drawn tight, the wood split, the firstlings burned, the bed made (14.72, 14.418, 14.446, 14.518). The stranger eats in silence after the account of the master's wealth (14.109); the talk ends as the herds come home, “So they spoke such words to one another” (14.409); and in the tale of the cloak the speaker closes his own words and Odysseus', “So I spoke” (14.490, 14.494, 14.499).
---

ὣς οἱ μὲν τοιαῦτα πρὸς ἀλλήλους ἀγόρευον (14.409) is the verse that ends a conversation as the story moves on, here with the swine and the herdsmen coming home (14.410), as in the Iliad when Aeneas and Pandarus drive up, and in Sparta when the banqueters come to Menelaus' house (*Iliad* 5.274–275; 4.620–621). ἦ ῥα καί at 14.446 is the form of 2.321 (see the note on Book 2). In the tale of the cloak the closes frame the night's exchange: “So I spoke,” ὣς ἐφάμην, after the speaker's plea (14.490), “He spoke,” ἦ καί, after Odysseus' word to keep silent (14.494), and “So he spoke, and then Thoas, son of Andraemon, rose” after his call for a messenger (14.499), the verse of the contestants' rising at the games of Patroclus (see [[contest-scene]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).
