---
summary: The beggar asks for “a reward for good news” if Odysseus comes home, a cloak and a tunic, and will take nothing before then (14.152–155); Eumaeus answers that he will never pay it (14.166).
---

**The reward claimed.** The stranger first offers news: “Zeus knows, I suppose, and the other immortal gods, / whether I might bring news of having seen him; for I have wandered far” (ἀγγείλαιμι, 14.119–120). Eumaeus answers that no wanderer “bringing news of him” could persuade Penelope, since vagrants lie for their keep, and that the stranger too would fashion a tale “if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (14.122–132; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[pseudos]] and [[epos]]). So the beggar names his reward and refuses to take it before the news comes true: “yet I will not speak idly, but with an oath, / that Odysseus is coming home; and let me have a reward for good news / at once, when he comes and reaches his own house: / clothe me in a cloak and a tunic, fine clothes; / but before that, though I am in great want, I would accept nothing” (εὐαγγέλιον δέ μοι ἔστω, 14.151–155; see [[oath]] and [[cloak]]). The reward is the very gift that Eumaeus said a liar would invent a tale to win, and the refusal to take it now is the answer to the charge, sealed with the curse on the man “who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (14.156–157; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]] and [[apate]]).

**The reward refused.** “Old man, I shall never pay you that reward for good news, / nor will Odysseus ever come home again” (εὐαγγέλιον τόδε τίσω, 14.166–167; see [[nostos]]). Eumaeus refuses the reward because he refuses the news, and he leaves the oath alone (14.171). The beggar restates the same terms as a wager on his life (14.393–400; see [[rhetre]]); the cloak he gets that night is given for a story, not for news (14.508–522; see [[ainos]]).
