**The night and the test.** “And an evil night came on, a night without a moon” (14.457): σκοτομήνιος is “in the dark (part of the) month”, when there was no moon, and it was then four days to the new moon (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.457, referring to his note on 14.158–162; see [[moon]]). Zeus rains all night and the West Wind blows, “always wet” (14.457–458; see [[zephyrus]]). Then Odysseus speaks, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him, or urge one of his companions / to do so” (14.459–461; see [[peira]] and [[eumaeus]]).

**The preface.** “Hear me now, Eumaeus, and all you other companions; / I will speak a word with a wish” (14.462–463): εὐξάμενος ἐρέω is “I will say with a wish”, that is, “give utterance to a wish”, the aorist participle expressing coincidence with the action of the verb (Monro on 14.463; see [[euchomai]]), and the wish comes five verses later. The wine is to blame, οἶνος … ἠλεός, “mad”, “as being the cause of madness”, as Dionysus is the “mad god”, μαινόμενος (Monro on 14.464, citing *Iliad* 6.132; see [[the-mad-wine]] and [[wine]]). It “brings out a word that is better left unsaid” (14.466), προέηκεν, the reading of Aristarchus and the manuscripts; Aristophanes read παρέθηκεν, probably taking it in the sense of the Attic παρέστησεν, “put into his mind” (Monro's critical note on 14.466). “But since I have once spoken out, I will not hide it” (14.467): τὸ πρῶτον is “once”, and ἀνέκραγον, “raised my voice”, “spoke out”, has “a colloquial stamp” (Monro on 14.467).

**The ambush.** “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast, / as when we prepared an ambush beneath Troy and led it” (14.468–469): the first verse is Nestor's, who opens his stories of his youth with it (*Iliad* 23.629; 7.157, 11.670; see [[if-only-i-were-young]] and [[nestor]]). The leaders are Odysseus and Menelaus, and the teller is third in command, “for they themselves bade me” (14.470–471; see [[menelaus]] and [[son-of-atreus]]). They lie “around the city”, περὶ ἄστυ, a phrase “used (with some vagueness) of besiegers” (14.473; Monro on 14.473), among reeds and marsh, “crouching under our armor”, τεύχεσι, Aristarchus' reading, for which an ancient variant had τείχεσι, “walls” (14.474; Monro's critical note). The night comes on “when the North Wind fell”, πεσόντος, “having fallen”, that is, “ceased to blow”, as ἄνεμος πέσε at 19.202 (14.475; Monro on 14.475; see [[boreas]]). Then “from above came snow like hoarfrost”, ἠΰτε πάχνη (14.476; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]]), for which Monro finds Naber's ἠΰτε λάχνη, “like wool”, “very plausible” (on 14.476): λάχνη is the woolly nap of the cloak that Nestor fastens on in the night of *Iliad* 10 (*Iliad* 10.134). Ice “grew thick” on the shields, περιτρέφετο, as milk curdles (14.477; Monro on 14.477, citing 9.246).

**Without a cloak.** The others “slept at ease, their shoulders wrapped in their shields” (14.479): “The large oval ‘Mycenaean’ shield was swung round to the back, and served as a shelter, almost as an outer garment” (Monro on 14.479; see [[shield]]). The teller had left his cloak behind, “since I did not think I would be cold even so” (14.481), ἔμπης meaning “even without a χλαῖνα” (Monro on 14.481; see [[cloak]]), and came with only his shield and his shining kilt (14.482). He had a tunic too, as 14.489 shows, “but that is always taken for granted”, as Hector is called γυμνός, “naked”, when he has put off his shield and helmet; ζῶμα is “a leathern apron or kilt”, and he has no corslet (Monro on 14.482, citing *Iliad* 22.124; see [[kilt]] and [[tunic]]).

**The device.** “But when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had passed over” (14.483), the verse of the night on Thrinacia when Zeus sent the storm (12.312; Monro on 14.483), he nudges Odysseus with his elbow and speaks to him with the full address, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (14.484–486; see [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]] and [[of-many-devices]]): the cold is killing him, for “a god beguiled me / into wearing only a tunic” (14.488–489). “So I spoke, and then he had this thought in his heart”: τόνδε is “‘this’ (which follows), viz. the device of sending Thoas” (14.490; Monro on 14.490). For “such a man was he at planning and at fighting”, an old variant reported by Eustathius reads τελέσαι ἔργον τε ἔπος τε, “at doing deed and word” (14.491; Monro's critical note). Odysseus bids him be silent, in nearly the words with which he once silenced Agamemnon (14.493; *Iliad* 14.90), and then speaks to all: “Hear me, friends: a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” (14.495), the verse in which Agamemnon reports the Dream sent by Zeus (*Iliad* 2.56; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[dream]]). Monro thinks it “perhaps an interpolation, as Aristarchus thought”: “We expect to be told what this dream was, what it said, &c.”, and λίην γὰρ κτλ., “For we have come very far from the ships”, is “the real beginning of the speech”; but “a form of address, such as κλῦτε φίλοι, cannot well be dispensed with” (Monro on 14.495). Someone should tell Agamemnon to send more men from the ships (14.496–498; see [[agamemnon]] and [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). Thoas, son of Andraemon, rises at once, lays aside his crimson cloak and runs to the ships (14.499–501; see [[thoas]] and [[crimson]]); for θέτο, “laid aside”, four manuscripts read βάλε, “threw off”, the verb of Odysseus' own cloak at *Iliad* 2.183 (Monro's critical note on 14.500). The teller ends the story, “and in his clothing / I lay gladly, and Dawn of the golden throne shone forth” (14.501–502; see [[eos]] and [[golden-throned]]).

**The wish.** The tale ends as it began, with the wish for youth, and adds its point: someone in the farmstead would give him a cloak, “out of friendship and respect for a good man” (14.503–506; see [[philotes]] and [[aidos]]). The three verses are obelized in one manuscript, and a scholion says that Athenocles had already rejected them (Monro's critical note on 14.504–506); for Monro's judgment on them, see the general article.

**Eumaeus' answer.** “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless, / and you have not yet spoken any word amiss or profitless” (14.508–509; see [[ainos]] and [[profitless]]). He shall have what a suppliant should, “for now; but at dawn you will knock about in your own rags” (14.511–512; see [[rags]]): δνοπαλίξεις “has caused some difficulty”, but “the meaning evidently is that Ulysses must wear his own rags again next day”; in the Iliad the verb seems to mean “to knock aside”, and “the word is doubtless colloquial, like so much of the vocabulary of the Odyssey” (Monro on 14.512; see [[supplication]]). There is only one cloak for each man in the hut; Telemachus will clothe him when he comes, in three verses that many manuscripts omit and that stand again, nearly unchanged, in the next book (14.513–517; 15.337–339; see the translation's note on 14.515–517 and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Then Eumaeus spreads a bed by the fire and throws over the stranger the thick cloak he keeps for storms (14.518–522; see [[cloak]]). The test is passed, and the swineherd goes out into the storm to sleep beside the boars (14.523–533).
