---
summary: The stranger offers to swear that Odysseus will come home, “yet I will not speak idly, but with an oath” (14.151), and swears with ἦ μέν, “truly all these things will be fulfilled as I say” (14.160); Eumaeus will have none of it, “But let us leave the oath alone” (14.171). In the tale the king of the Thesprotians swears “pouring libations in his house” (14.331), and at the end the stranger, “not even with an oath” believed, proposes a compact instead (14.392–394).
---

The oath has two of the parts of the Iliad's oaths: the witnesses, called with ἴστω, Zeus first and then the table and the hearth (14.158–159; see [[let-zeus-be-witness]]), and the terms, sworn with ἦ μέν and the future, as Calchas asked Achilles to swear (*Iliad* 1.77; 14.160–164). There is no curse. D. B. Monro takes the swineherd's refusal as declining an oath “which (if 158 ff. are rejected) he had not actually taken,” and reports that Zenodotus read κεῖνον, “him,” for ὅρκον at 14.171 (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.171, and his critical note). The king's oath is told, not given in its words, in verses that return when the stranger tells Penelope the same story (14.331–333 ≈ 19.288–290; see [[pheidon]] and [[libation]]). The compact of 14.393–400 is a wager, not an oath, with the gods as μάρτυροι, “witnesses,” the word Achilles used of the heralds (*Iliad* 1.338; see [[rhetre]] and [[oath]]).
