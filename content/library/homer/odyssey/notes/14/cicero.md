---
summary: Cicero's Ulysses, an example of a man true to his own nature, wished to be affable to all in every conversation and at home bore even the insults of slaves; the first slave he meets at home, in this book, honors him instead. Cicero's third Mercury, the son of Maia, to whom the swineherd sets aside a portion, is said to have fathered Pan on Penelope, and the oracle of Dodona, where the beggar says Odysseus went to ask the will of Zeus, is in Cicero the oracle of the Spartans and of the Greek colonies.
---

- **Affable to all.** Each man should hold to what is his own, Cicero argues, and some men must put up with things that others could not bear: “How much Ulysses endured in that long wandering, when he served women, if Circe and Calypso are to be called women, and wished to be affable to all in every conversation! And at home he even bore the insults of slaves and maidservants, so as to come at last to what he desired. But Ajax, with the spirit with which he is represented, would rather have met death a thousand times than suffer such things” (*in omni sermone omnibus affabilem esse se vellet*, *On Duties* 1.113, plain renderings). This book is the first conversation of the disguised Odysseus at home, a whole day and night of it with the swineherd, and the first slave he meets there gives him no insult: he drives off the dogs, gives him his own bedding to sit on, feeds him and honors him with the chine (14.29–51, 14.72–79, 14.437–438). The insults of slaves and maidservants come in later books, from the goatherd Melanthius, who kicks him on the road to the town, and from the maid Melantho (17.212–234, 18.321–336): [[odysseus]], [[eumaeus]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]].
- **The son of Maia.** Among the many gods of one name, Cotta counts five Mercuries, the third “born of the third Jupiter and Maia, from whom and Penelope, they say, Pan was born” (*tertius Iove tertio natus et Maia, ex quo et Penelopa Pana natum ferunt*, *On the Nature of the Gods* 3.56, a plain rendering). In this book the swineherd sets aside one of the seven portions of his sacrifice “for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia” (14.435), the only mention of Maia in Homer: [[hermes]], [[maia]], [[pan]] and [[penelope]].
- **Dodona.** What colony, Quintus asks, did Greece ever send out without the oracle of Pytho, or of Dodona, or of Ammon (*On Divination* 1.3)? The greatest portent the Spartans ever had came when they asked Jupiter of Dodona about victory: the king of the Molossians' pet monkey overturned the urn and scattered the lots, and the priestess told them to think of their safety, not of victory (*On Divination* 1.76); Cicero answers that nothing could be less wonderful than that such a beast should upset an urn (*On Divination* 2.69). In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|beggar's tale]] the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] king says that Odysseus had gone to Dodona, “so that from the god's / high-leafed oak he might hear the will of Zeus” (14.327–328): [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]].
