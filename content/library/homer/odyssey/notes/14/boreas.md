---
summary: The North Wind blows four times. In the Cretan tale “a fresh and fair North Wind” carries the ships from Crete to Egypt, and again the Phoenician's ship toward Libya (14.253, 14.299); in the tale of the cloak the night at Troy falls cold “when the North Wind fell”, with snow (14.475–477); and at the end of the book Eumaeus goes out to sleep with the hogs beneath a hollow rock, “sheltered from the North Wind” (14.533).
# The entry's Βορέ* claims Βορέῃ (14.253, 14.299), Βορέαο (14.475) and Βορέω (14.533), the genitive in -εω, which
# the Odyssey has only here (as Il. 14.395, 23.692).
---

**A fresh and fair North Wind.** The raiders of the tale leave Crete on the seventh day, “and from wide Crete / we sailed with a fresh and fair North Wind, / easily, as if downstream” (14.252–254; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[as-if-downstream]], [[the-winds]] and [[crete]]), and come to the river of Egypt on the fifth day (14.257). Βορέῃ is an instrumental, with a partly comitative force, and ἀκραής seems to mean “blowing at its height”, with a further sense of rightness (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.253; see the translation's note on 14.253 and its key-word row on ἀκραής, “fresh”, as 2.421 “a fresh West Wind”). The same verse carries the Phoenician's ship: “And she ran before a fresh and fair North Wind, / on the mid-sea course past Crete; and Zeus devised destruction for them” (14.299–300; see [[phoenicians]] and [[zeus]]). Monro takes ὑπὲρ Κρήτης of the windward side of the island, by the northwest, a course that the fair north-easterly wind made possible, and supposes that with the storm the wind went round to the south or southeast; J. L. Myres, whom he quotes, adds that from Crete to [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] is the regular course of drift, whatever the wind (on 14.300 and 14.315; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). In the beggar's false tale to Penelope the North Wind is the Cretan's enemy, penning the ships at Amnisus for twelve days, until “on the thirteenth day the wind fell” (19.200–202, a plain rendering).

**When the North Wind fell.** In the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] the ambushers lie in the reeds below the wall of Troy: “And an evil night came on, when the North Wind fell, / freezing; and from above came snow like hoarfrost, / cold, and ice grew thick on our shields” (14.475–477; see [[snow-like-hoarfrost]] and [[the-doloneia-question]]). Βορέαο πεσόντος is “having fallen”, that is, ceased to blow, as in that tale to Penelope (Monro on 14.475, comparing 19.202). Hesiod has the same words in his winter month, “for cold is the dawn when the North Wind falls” (ψυχρὴ γάρ τʼ ἠὼς πέλεται Βορέαο πεσόντος, *Works and Days* 547; see [[hesiod]]). In the Iliad snow flies cold “under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky” (*Iliad* 15.170–171, 19.357–358).

**Sheltered from the North Wind.** When the others have gone to sleep by the fire, Eumaeus arms himself against the night and goes out to the swine: “And he went to lie down where the white-tusked hogs / slept beneath a hollow rock, sheltered from the North Wind” (14.532–533; see [[eumaeus]] and [[boar]]). Βορέω ἰωγή is “shelter from the north wind” (Monro on 14.533; see the translation's key-word row on ἰωγή). Hesiod's beasts do the same in the winter month of the North Wind: they flee through the woods, seeking shelter in thick coverts “and a rocky hollow” (καὶ γλάφυ πετρῆεν, *Works and Days* 532–533).
