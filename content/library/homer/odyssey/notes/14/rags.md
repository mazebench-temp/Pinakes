---
summary: In his tale the beggar says that the Thesprotian sailors stripped him and put “a vile rag and a tunic” on him, and that he slipped into the sea with the rag over his head (14.341–352); Eumaeus lends him a cloak for the night, but at dawn he must go about in his own rags (14.512).
---

**The rag of the tale.** The sailors “stripped me of my cloak and my tunic, my clothing, / and around me they put, instead, a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, which you see with your own eyes” (14.341–343; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[cloak]], [[tunic]] and [[doulios]]): Athena's words when she dressed him as a beggar (13.434–435), and the rags he wears as he speaks are his evidence. For ῥωγαλέα, “rent”, Rhianus read ῥωγαλέον (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.343). When the gods have undone his bonds, “covering my head with the rag, / I slid down the polished lading plank”, and swam away (14.348–352; see [[ships]]): his head is disguised, “so as to look like flotsam as he swims away” (Monro on 14.350–351).

**Back in his rags.** Eumaeus answers the [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] with a cloak for the night, “for now; but at dawn you will knock about in your own rags” (14.512; see [[eumaeus]]). δνοπαλίξεις means that Odysseus must wear his own rags again next day: in the Iliad δνοπαλίζω seems to mean “knock aside”, and so a man may be said to knock about the clothes he wears, a colloquial word, “like so much of the vocabulary of the Odyssey” (Monro on 14.512; see the translation's row δνοπαλίζω; ἐπημοιβοί).
