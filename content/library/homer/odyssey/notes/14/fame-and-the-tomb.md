summary: Eumaeus, refusing to believe the stranger's news, says in nearly the verses of Telemachus that the gods hated his master: had he died at Troy or among his friends, the Achaeans would have made him a tomb and he would have won great fame for his son, but now the Harpies have snatched him away “without report” (14.364–371).

**The swineherd's reckoning.** The stranger has just told him that Odysseus is alive and on his way home, and Eumaeus answers that he knows better: “I know well myself / of my master's homecoming, that he was hated by all the gods” (14.365–366). νόστον is the thing known about, “I know of his return that he has come to be hated”, that is, that his return has been prevented by the gods' hatred (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.366; see [[nostos]]). The proof is the absence of a tomb. A death at Troy or in the arms of his friends would have brought the mound that the Achaeans heap for their dead, and with it fame that would pass to his son (see [[tomb]] and [[kleos]]); a man carried off by the Harpies has neither, and is gone ἀκλειῶς, “without report” (see [[harpies]]). ἅρπυιαι are “the snatchers”, and if, as Fick proposed, a form of ἀναρέπω, “snatch up”, is read for ἀνηρείψαντο, there is a play of language, “the snatchers have snatched up” (Monro on 14.371).

**Telemachus' verses.** The lines are those of Telemachus to the disguised Athena, who said the same of his father (1.236–241), and the shade of Achilles says the like to the shade of Agamemnon (24.30–34); Odysseus on his raft made the same reckoning for himself (5.306–312; see [[three-times-blessed]]). The two verses of the tomb and the fame (14.369–370) are wanting in most of the good manuscripts here, and Monro takes them to have come from Book 1, where in turn 1.238, “or in the arms of his friends”, does not fit, since the tomb and the fame belong to a death before Troy; the one true repetition is 14.371 = 1.241, “probably an epic commonplace” (Monro's additional note on 14.368–371; his critical note on 14.369–370; Appendix I §10). Monro also takes ἤρατο, “he would have won”, for a false form, and would restore ἤρετο (on 14.370).

**Another fame.** Eumaeus speaks of fame again when the stranger offers to stake his life: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter”, if he killed a guest he had fed (14.402–405; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]] and [[prayer]]). The fame of the dead man depends on his tomb; the fame of the host depends on how he treats his guest.
