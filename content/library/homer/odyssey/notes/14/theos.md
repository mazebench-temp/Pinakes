---
summary: The gods of men who suffer and do not see them. They “have given me other sorrows” (14.39), “have bound up the homecoming” of Odysseus (14.61) and hated him (14.366), yet “do not love cruel deeds” (14.83); in the Cretan tale “a god” put war in the teller's mind (14.227), scattered the Achaeans (14.242) and took away the crew's homecoming (14.309), and “the gods themselves” freed and hid him (14.348, 14.357); and Eumaeus, who prays “to all the gods” (14.423), says, “the god will give one thing and let another be” (14.444).
---

**The swineherd's gods.** Eumaeus' gods give and withhold. “And the gods have given me other sorrows and groaning” (14.39; see [[algos]]); “For truly the gods have bound up the homecoming of that man” (14.61; see [[nostos]]), who “was hated by all the gods / utterly” (14.366–367); yet a god prospers a faithful servant's labor, “when he has toiled much for him, and a god makes his work grow” (14.65). He measures the suitors by the gods' justice: “The blessed gods do not love cruel deeds, / but honor justice and the righteous deeds of men” (14.83–84; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]], [[blessed-gods]] and [[themis]]). Even raiders fear the gods' regard, while the suitors act as though “they have heard some voice of a god” of their master's death (14.85–92).

**The gods of the tale.** The Cretan gives the gods every turn of his life. War was dear to him, “which, I suppose, a god put in my mind; / for different men take delight in different works” (14.227–228; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[different-gifts]]); after Troy “a god scattered the Achaeans” (14.242, the half-verse of Nestor's account, 3.131; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]); when the ship broke up, “the god took away their homecoming” (14.309); and on Ithaca “the gods themselves undid my bonds / easily” and “the gods themselves hid me / easily” (14.348–349, 14.357–358). The disguised man says “the gods”, and the reader knows the goddess: Athena poured a mist around him on Ithaca and made him an old beggar (13.189–191, 13.429–438; see [[athena]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).

**Prayer and gift.** At the sacrifice “nor did the swineherd / forget the immortals; for his mind was good” (14.420–421); he prays “to all the gods” for his master's return (14.423; see [[euchomai]]) and offers the firstling pieces “to the everlasting gods” (14.446; see [[sacrifice]]), and he gives the stranger his meal with a saying: “the god will give one thing and let another be, / whatever he wishes in his heart; for he can do all things” (14.444–445; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). The beggar's oath calls on “Zeus … first of the gods” (14.158), and his wager on “the gods who hold Olympus” as witnesses (14.394; see [[rhetre]]).
