**The questions.** The stranger has eaten and drunk, sworn that Odysseus will come home and seen the oath declined (14.109–171; see [[oath]]), when Eumaeus turns to him: “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows” (14.185). The questions that follow are those that Telemachus put to the disguised Athena, “and tell me this truly, so that I may know it well” standing first instead of last (14.186–190 = 1.174, 1.170–173; 13.232; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]]), and the stranger answers with the goddess' first verse, “Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly” (14.192 = 1.179; see the general article). He asks for food and wine enough to sit in the hut and talk while others work: δαίνυσθαι is an infinitive of consequence, “wherewith to feast” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.195), and even “for a whole year”, εἰς ἐνιαυτὸν ἅπαντα, he could not come to the end of his sorrows (14.193–198; Monro on 14.196, comparing 15.455). “We expect a word meaning ‘I could go on’”, Monro says of οὔ τι διαπρήξαιμι, “I could not come to the end”, to which the negative phrase is equivalent (on 14.197). Nestor told Telemachus the same of the sorrows of the Achaeans: not even five years' stay or six would be enough to hear them all (3.113–117).

**The bastard son.** For the father, Castor, son of Hylax, and the brothers who cast lots for his livelihood, see [[castor-son-of-hylax]] and [[lots]] (14.199–210). For “lawful sons of his wife”, ἀλόχου, the variant ἀλόχων would make them the sons “of his wives” (Monro's critical note on 14.202). The Cretan married “for my excellence”, ἀρετῆς, a word “used of any advantages, not only one in war” (Monro on 14.212, comparing 13.45; see [[arete]]); and he was no ἀποφώλιος, “useless”, which stands for ἀπ-οφόλ-ιος, from ὄφελος, “use”, with ω for ο for the sake of the metre (Monro on 14.212). Of what is left of him he says, “looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was” (14.214–215; see [[the-stubble]] and [[geron]]).

**The fighter.** “Truly Ares and Athena gave me daring / and the strength that breaks men” (14.216–217; see [[breaker-of-men]] and [[tharsos]]). Editors put a colon after ῥηξηνορίην and take “whenever I picked for an ambush” with what follows; Monro, holding that a Homeric sentence does not begin with such a word in the middle of a line, takes it with what precedes, so that “never did my proud spirit foresee death” is a new form of “Ares and Athena gave me daring”, set down without a connective (Monro on 14.217; see [[ambush]] and [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). The last clause of the boast, ὅ τέ μοι εἴξειε πόδεσσιν, “whatever man of the enemy gave way before me with his feet”, gives “a poor sense” in the usual renderings, since the context is “a boast of courage and prowess rather than of speed”, and Monro would read with Bothe ὅ τε μὴ εἴξειε, “whoever did not save himself by speedy retreat” (on 14.221). Such a man had no love of work or of “the care of a household”, but of ships, wars, javelins and arrows: “But to me those things were dear which, I suppose, a god put in my mind; / for different men take delight in different works” (14.222–228; see [[different-gifts]]). The first verse means “things which a god made dear to me (not being the things that would naturally be so)”, and the second, Monro says, “is doubtless of later date” (on 14.227, 14.228; see the translation's note on 14.228). The Cretan who scorns work is not the man who will challenge Eurymachus to mow and plow against him (18.366–375).

**Raids and the war.** Before the war, “nine times I led men and swift-sailing ships / against foreign men”, and the spoil made him rich (14.230–234; see [[piracy]] and [[swift-sailing-ships]]). He picked out what he wanted and received much “afterward” by lot: ὀπίσσω refers to the regular division of the spoil, after the prizes had been set apart for the leaders (Monro on 14.232, citing *Iliad* 1.368; see [[division-of-spoils]]). “But when far-seeing Zeus devised that hateful journey” (14.235): the article, τήν γε, “expresses aversion or disgust” (Monro on 14.235). The same words are said of the storm that drove Menelaus' ships to Egypt (3.288–289), and Monro, weighing which place is the original, finds that they apply “rather better to the Trojan war than to the voyage of Menelaus”, though so well to both that nothing follows (Appendix I §10, note). Then, he says, “they bade me and far-famed Idomeneus / lead the ships to Ilios”, the subject of ἤνωγον being the Cretans (Monro on 14.237; see [[cretans]] and [[idomeneus]]), and the two could not refuse, “for the harsh talk of the people held us”, where the archaic genitive δήμοο “may be restored” for δήμου (14.237–239; Monro's critical note on 14.239; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). Nine years of war, the sack in the tenth, “and a god scattered the Achaeans” (14.240–242), the verse-end of Nestor's account of the departure from Troy and of Odysseus' reproach to Athena that morning (3.131, 13.317; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The Egyptian venture follows (14.243–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]).

**The Phoenician.** In the eighth year (14.287 = 7.261, with ὄγδοον for ὀγδόατον: see the general article) “a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits, / a greedy rogue” carried him off (14.288–298; see [[phoenicians]]). Monro hears contempt in τρώκτης: barytone nouns in -της “seem often to have a hostile or contemptuous meaning”, and “‘Gnawer’ may suggest thieving vermin, mice, &c.” (on 14.289). The voyage to Libya was a pretext for a sale, and “the change from ἵνα to ὡς” in the purpose clauses shows that the second purpose “was not avowed, but was the real purpose” (Monro on 14.297; see [[libya]] and [[trade]]). The wreck past Crete and the drift to Thesprotia are told in the verses of Odysseus' last shipwreck (14.299–315; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[like-sea-crows]]); Pheidon's welcome and his news of Odysseus fill the next part (14.316–336; see [[pheidon]], [[thesprotians]] and [[dodona]]).

**The escape on Ithaca.** The [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew were to take him to King Acastus “with care”, “but an evil counsel pleased their minds / concerning me, so that I might yet come utterly to a bane of misery” (14.336–338; see [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]]): ἐπὶ πῆμα γενοίμην is “might be brought into sorrow”, sorrow “regarded as a place reached” (Monro on 14.338, comparing *Iliad* 2.234). They contrived “the day of slavery”, stripped him of his cloak and tunic and put on him the rag and tunic, “rent, which you see with your own eyes” (14.340–343; see [[doulios]], [[cloak]] and [[rags]]), the rag that Athena had put on him that morning (13.434–435; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). ὅρηαι is formed as from a present ὅρημαι; many manuscripts have the Doric ὁρῆαι (Monro on 14.343). In the evening they reached Ithaca, bound him “with a well-twisted rope” and went ashore for supper (14.344–347). “But the gods themselves undid my bonds / easily”: ἀνέγναμψαν is “bent back”, that is, untied, as Hephaestus “loosed the bonds” of Ares and Aphrodite (14.348–349; Monro on 14.348, citing 8.359; see [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]]). He covered his head with the rag and slid down the ship's ἐφόλκαιον, “probably a ‘lading plank,’ to take in (ἐφέλκεσθαι) cargo”, and Monro quotes J. L. Myres: “They are quite common on sailing boats, and on steamers in the Mediterranean. I have seen the lading plank used in shoal water as a gangway” (on 14.350). He went into the sea without a splash, and his covered head would “look like flotsam as he swims away” (Monro's notes on 14.350–351); θύρηθʼ is “out of the sea”, as the way out of the gray sea that Odysseus could not find off Scheria (14.352; Monro on 14.352, citing 5.410). Then the thicket, the search abandoned, and the gods who hid him and set him “at the farmstead / of a man of understanding” (14.353–359): the tale ends at the hut where it is told.

**Eumaeus' answer.** The swineherd, addressed by the poet in the second person as the answer begins (14.360; see [[apostrophe]]), is moved “by telling all these things, how much you have suffered and how far you have wandered” (14.361–362), but not persuaded: “But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus” (14.363–364). Monro supplies εἶπες, “you have spoken”, with κατὰ κόσμον, “aright”: the poet meant to write εἶπες, but the parenthetical οὐδέ με πείσεις, coming first, turned it into εἰπών, and ὀΐομαι, “I suspect”, is a parenthesis (on 14.363). “Why should a man like you / lie for no reason?” (14.364–365; see [[pseudos]]). He knows of his master's homecoming, νόστον, “that he was hated by all the gods”, the accusative meaning “I know of his return that he has come to be hated”, that is, that his return has been prevented by the hatred (14.365–366; Monro on 14.366; see [[nostos]]). The rest of the answer, the tomb that the Achaeans would have built and the Harpies who snatched him away, the Aetolian's lie and the refusal to be bewitched, is told under its own entries (14.367–389; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]], [[harpies]], [[aetolians]] and [[thelgo]]). The beggar answers the disbelief with a wager (14.391–400; see [[rhetre]]).
