---
summary: The swineherd of this book is one of Aristotle's “swineherds”, to whom the scar is shown later as proof, and one of the “certain people” to whom, in the Poetics' outline, the returning hero makes himself known before he attacks. The Rhetoric takes the beggar's “stubble” as its example of a metaphor from the genus; a fragment of the Homeric Problems explains the cloak that Odysseus throws off to run, the gesture of Thoas in the tale of the cloak; a story of the Koliadai, printed among the fragments of the Constitution of the Ithacans, gives the swineherd the reward he hoped for; and the History of Animals defends the five-year-old ox of the verse that this book gives to a boar.
---

- **The swineherds.** Recognition by signs can be used better or worse: “Odysseus, for example, was recognized through his scar in one way by the nurse and in another by the swineherds”, and recognitions made for the sake of proof are less artistic than those that come out of a reversal, like the one in the Bath (*Poetics* 1454b26–30). The proof is given to Eumaeus and the cowherd in *Odyssey* 21 (21.205–225). In this book the swineherd is shown no sign and asks for none; he refuses the beggar's news of his master and his oath, and is not told who his guest is (14.149–171, 14.360–389). In the Poetics' outline of the Odyssey the hero “arrives, storm-tossed, and after making himself known to certain people he attacks” (*Poetics* 1455b21–22): the swineherd is one of those people, the first mortal whom Odysseus meets on Ithaca, and the whole of this book passes before he knows him: [[the-scar-of-odysseus]], [[anagnorisis]], [[eumaeus]] and [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]].
- **Stubble.** It is metaphor above all that gives us learning: “When the poet calls old age ‘stubble’, he gives us learning and knowledge through the genus, for both have lost their bloom” (*Rhetoric* 1410b14–15). The poet's stubble is the beggar's figure for himself in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan tale]], “but even so, looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was” (14.214–215): [[the-stubble]] and [[metaphora]].
- **The cloak thrown off.** A note in the scholia on *Iliad* 2.183, printed among the fragments of the *Homeric Problems*, asks why Odysseus throws off his cloak and runs through the camp in his tunic alone, which seems unseemly. Aristotle's answer is that he did it so that the crowd, marveling at it, would turn back; others said that the cloak hindered his running, or that it fell off by itself as he ran (*Homeric Problems* fr. 368 Gigon, paraphrased). In the beggar's story Thoas does the same, “and laid aside his crimson cloak, / and set off running to the ships” (14.500–501), in nearly the words of *Iliad* 2.183, and there the reason is the plainest one, for the trick of Odysseus is to make a man run and leave his cloak behind: [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[the-doloneia-question]] and [[cloak]].
- **The Koliadai.** Plutarch, in a passage that Gigon prints among the fragments of the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, tells that after the slaughter of the suitors Telemachus freed the people around Eumaeus and settled them among the citizens, and that the family of the Koliadai descends from Eumaeus, as the Boukoloi from the cowherd Philoetius; Aristotle is named there for the word *phagilos*, “a lamb” (fr. 511 Gigon, paraphrased). In this book Eumaeus says what his master would have given him had he come home, “a house and an allotment and a much-wooed wife” (14.62–64): [[eumaeus]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]].
- **Five years old.** The ox is at its prime at five years, “which is why some say that Homer too was right to write ‘a male, five years old’” (*History of Animals* 6.21, a plain rendering), the phrase of the ox that Agamemnon sacrifices before he honors Ajax with the chine (*Iliad* 7.315), and of the ox that the sons of Autolycus sacrifice (19.420). This book gives the same age to the swineherd's boar (14.419), and the same honor to its guest (14.437); a boar, Aristotle says, is good for breeding only up to three years, since after that he no longer grows or gains strength (*History of Animals* 5.14): [[five-years-old]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]].
