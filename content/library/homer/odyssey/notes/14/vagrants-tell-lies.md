summary: The stranger asks who the swineherd's master was, in case he can bring news of him (14.115–120); Eumaeus answers that no wanderer's news can be believed, since every one of them goes to Penelope with lies, and the stranger would do the same for a cloak (14.121–132). He has been deceived before, by an Aetolian (14.378–385).
refs: [14.126-127, 14.131-132]
---

**The swineherd's reply.** The stranger has asked who the master was who bought Eumaeus, the rich man who went to war for Agamemnon and perished, “in case I may know him”, for he has wandered far (14.115–120). The swineherd's answer is a refusal to believe in advance. Monro gives the connection of the speech: “We cannot believe any of the wanderers who bring news of him; they make up false tales in order to get entertainment: you may be one of them”, that is, there are so many false tales brought by wanderers that the stranger too must be disbelieved (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.122–132). The lies go to Penelope: “Whoever comes wandering to the land of Ithaca / goes to my mistress and speaks deceits” (14.126–127; see [[apate]]), and she receives him, questions him and weeps (14.128–130; see [[penelope]] and [[tears]]). Then the suspicion turns on the stranger: “Quickly you too, old sir, would fashion a tale, / if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (14.131–132; see [[cloak]]). παρατεκταίνομαι, “fashion”, is made from τέκτων, the builder (see the translation's table of key words).

**The Aetolian.** Eumaeus' distrust has a history. An Aetolian who had killed a man and wandered far came to his house, and he welcomed him warmly; the man said he had seen Odysseus with Idomeneus in Crete, mending his ships, and that he would come home by summer or late summer, bringing many goods (14.378–385; see [[aetolians]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). He was such a vagrant, and the stranger's [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|tale]], with its Cretans and Idomeneus and its treasures, has the same shape. So the swineherd asks him not to please him with lies (14.387; see [[thelgo]]), and the stranger's last word in the matter is his wager, offered “so that another beggar too may avoid deceiving” (14.400; see [[eperopeuo]] and [[rhetre]]).
