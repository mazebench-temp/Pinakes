**The fellow commander.** When far-seeing Zeus devised the hateful journey, “then they bade me and far-famed Idomeneus / lead the ships to Ilios; and there was no means / to refuse, for the harsh talk of the people held us” (14.235–239; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[crete]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). ἤνωγον, “they bade”, has the Cretans for its subject (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.237; see [[cretans]]), and ἀγακλυτὸν Ἰδομενῆα is “far-famed Idomeneus” (see [[famous]]). In the tale to Athena the Cretan was the man who would not serve under Idomeneus at Troy and who killed his son (13.259–270; see [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]); here he shares the command with him, as Meriones does in the Iliad's Catalogue (*Iliad* 2.650–651; see [[meriones]]), and to Penelope he will be Aethon, Idomeneus' younger brother (19.181–184).

**The Aetolian's lie.** Eumaeus will not believe the stranger's news, for an Aetolian once beguiled him: “He said he had seen him among the Cretans, with Idomeneus, / mending his ships, which the storm winds had shattered; / and he said he would come either by summer or by the late summer, / bringing many goods, with his godlike companions” (14.382–385; see [[aetolians]] and [[eumaeus]]). It is the shape of the tale that Odysseus will tell Penelope: that in Crete, when Idomeneus had already gone to Troy, he entertained Odysseus, whom the storm had driven there (19.185–202).
