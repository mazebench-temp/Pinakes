---
summary: The one line left of Aeschylus' Penelope has a speaker claim Cretan birth in nearly the words with which the beggar begins his tale to the swineherd, and Aeschylus' choruses revere the Zeus of strangers whose wrath the king of Egypt respects in the tale and whom the swineherd fears.
---

- **A Cretan by birth.** The *Penelope* survives in a single line: ἐγὼ γένος μέν εἰμι Κρὴς ἀρχέστατον, “By birth I am a Cretan, of the oldest stock” (*Penelope* fr. 187 Nauck, a plain rendering). The speaker is not named. The beggar begins his tale to the swineherd with the same claim and the same noun, ἐκ μὲν Κρητάων γένος εὔχομαι εὐρειάων, “I claim that my line is from wide Crete” (14.199), and his father was a rich man “honored then among the Cretans by the people like a god” (14.205): [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[crete]].
- **Zeus of strangers.** The elders of the *Agamemnon* revere “great Zeus of strangers”, who has long drawn his bow against Alexander, and sing that Helen came to the sons of Priam “by the escort of Zeus of strangers”, a Fury who brought tears to brides (*Agamemnon* 362–366, 748–749, plain renderings); the daughters of Danaus pray that Zeus of strangers may watch over the honors paid by strangers' lips, and that the city may be well governed by men who revere great Zeus, “Zeus of strangers above all” (*Suppliants* 627–629, 670–673, plain renderings). In the tale the king of Egypt spares the suppliant raider because he “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and the swineherd welcomes the beggar “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.389): [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]].
