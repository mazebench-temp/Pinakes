summary: Penelope weeps over every wanderer's tale of her husband, “as is the custom of a woman” (14.126–130); and in the false tale the king of Egypt takes the suppliant raider home in his chariot “as I shed tears” (14.279–280).
# δάκρυα (14.129) and δάκρυ (14.280) are the entry's.
---

**Penelope's tears.** Eumaeus tells the stranger why he will not believe him: whoever comes wandering to Ithaca goes to his mistress and speaks deceits, “and she receives him well and makes him welcome and questions him about each thing, / and as she mourns, the tears fall from her eyelids, / as is the custom of a woman, when her husband has perished far away” (14.128–130; see [[penelope]], [[lament]] and [[vagrants-tell-lies]]). Her tears are what the wanderers trade on: “Quickly you too, old sir, would fashion a tale, / if someone would give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing” (14.131–132). The tears that fall from her eyelids at every false report will fall again when the old nurse brings her the true news of his return: “she clung to the old woman, and let a tear fall from her eyelids” (23.33, a plain rendering; see [[eurycleia]]).

**The suppliant's tears.** In the false tale the Cretan, having clasped the knees of the king of Egypt, is spared: “and seating me in his chariot he took me home as I shed tears” (14.280; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[supplication]], [[pity]] and [[shedding-tears]]). δάκρυ χέοντα is the formula of weeping, here the tears of a man whose companions have been killed or led away and who owes his life to a stranger's pity.
