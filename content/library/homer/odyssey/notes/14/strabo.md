---
summary: Strabo reads this book as geography. The rugged path from the harbor is one of Homer's signs that Ithaca is high, which he reconciles with the “low” island of Odysseus' description; the twelve herds on the mainland put Acarnania under Odysseus; the oak of Dodona shows what Homer's “will of Zeus” is; and the beggar's story of the cloak, “the narrative to Eumaeus”, proves the distance between the Achaeans' ships and Troy. The tale's five days from Crete to Egypt and its Greek raiders have their measure in his account of Crete and of the early kings of Egypt.
---

- **The rugged path.** Odysseus' verse on his island, “But my island itself lies low in the sea, farthest out of all” (*Odyssey* 9.25), seems to contradict itself, Strabo says, for χθαμαλή means “low” and πανυπερτάτη “highest”, and the poet shows Ithaca high in many places, calling it “rocky” and the road from the harbor “a rugged path through the wooded country” (τρηχεῖαν ἀταρπὸν χῶρον ἀνʼ ὑλήεντα). The commentators explain it well: χθαμαλή means here “near the mainland”, and πανυπερτάτη not “highest” but farthest out “toward the darkness”, that is, to the north (*Geography* 10.2.12, plain renderings). The phrase is the opening of this book, “But he went up from the harbor by the rugged path / through the wooded country, among the heights” (14.1–2): [[ithaca]], [[rugged]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]].
- **Twelve herds on the mainland.** In the Catalogue the men of Odysseus “held the mainland and lived on the coast across from them” (*Iliad* 2.635). By “mainland” Homer means the coast opposite the islands, Strabo says, with Leucas and the rest of Acarnania, “about which he also says, ‘twelve herds on the mainland, as many flocks of sheep’”, perhaps because the land of Epirus reached so far in old times and was called by the common name “mainland” (*Geography* 10.2.10, plain renderings). The verse is the swineherd's count of his master's wealth, “Twelve herds of cattle on the mainland; as many flocks of sheep” (14.100), which Strabo quotes with μήλων for οἰῶν: [[possessions]] and [[ithaca]].
- **The oak of Dodona.** Dodona, Strabo says, was under the Thesprotians in old times, and the tragedians and Pindar call it Thesprotian; later it came under the Molossians. In Homer θέμιστες and βουλαί are to be taken simply as the oracle's commands and wishes, “for such is also ‘to hear the will of Zeus from the high-leafed oak’” (*Geography* 7.7.11, plain renderings). In the sixteenth book he gives the verse again, of the ancients who honored the commands of the gods above those of men, so that the consulter of oracles was then common, running to Dodona “to hear the will of Zeus from the high-leafed oak”, using Zeus as a counselor (*Geography* 16.2.38, a plain rendering). The first quotation reads ἐπακοῦσαι, which Monro reports as the reading of Aristophanes and Herodian; Aristarchus and the common text have ἐπακούσῃ, the form of the second, and Murray's text prints ἐπακούσαι (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on 14.328). The verse is the beggar's report of the Thesprotian king, that Odysseus “had gone to Dodona, so that from the god's / high-leafed oak he might hear the will of Zeus” (14.327–328, again 19.296–297): [[dodona]], [[thesprotians]], [[the-oak-of-zeus]] and [[high-leafed]].
- **Five days to Egypt.** From Samonium, the eastern cape of Crete, to Egypt is “a voyage of four days and nights; some say three” (*Geography* 10.4.5, a plain rendering). The Cretan of the tale sails from Crete with a fresh North Wind, “easily, as if downstream”, and comes to the river Aegyptus “on the fifth day” (14.252–258): [[the-raid-on-egypt]], [[as-if-downstream]], [[crete]] and [[aegyptus-river]].
- **Greek raiders.** The early kings of Egypt distrusted all who sailed to them, “and especially the Greeks”, sackers and covetous of other men's land, and set a guard on the coast to keep them off (*Geography* 17.1.6, a plain rendering). The tale's Cretans are such sackers (14.259–272): [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[egypt]].
- **The narrative to Eumaeus.** Arguing after Demetrius of Scepsis that the naval station lies too near the Ilium of his own day for that city to be Homer's Troy, Strabo cites the poet's own distances: “the narrative to Eumaeus, contrived by Odysseus” (ἡ διήγησις ἡ πρὸς τὸν Εὔμαιον ὑπὸ τοῦ Ὀδυσσέως διασκευασθεῖσα) shows that the city was far from the station, “as when we led an ambush beneath Troy”, for further on he says, “For we have come very far from the ships”; so do the spies of the night book and Polydamas' “we are far from the wall” (*Geography* 13.1.36, plain renderings; 14.469, 14.496; *Iliad* 10.208–210, 18.254–256). His verb for Odysseus' invention is the one he uses of Homer's own working-up of a true story, which the poet “elaborated poetically” (ποιητικῶς διεσκεύασε, *Geography* 1.2.11, a plain rendering). He quotes 14.469 with ὑπὸ Τροίῃ for the text's ὑπὸ Τροίην: [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]], [[the-doloneia-question]] and [[troy]].
