**Food before questions.** The swineherd's first words after driving off the dogs are an invitation: “But follow me, let us go to the hut, old man, so that you too, / when you have had your heart's fill of bread and wine, / may tell me where you are from and how many sorrows you have endured” (14.45–47). The guest eats first and is asked afterward, as Telemachus told the disguised Athena and Menelaus his two young guests (1.123–124; 4.60–62; see [[xenia]] and [[visit-scene]]). The meal is the two porkers roasted on the spits, with barley meal and wine mixed in an ivy-wood bowl (14.72–79; see [[sacrifice]] and [[wine]]), and the guest “heartily ate the meat and drank the wine, / greedily, in silence, and he was planting evils for the suitors” (14.109–110; see [[silence]]). Only “when he had dined and satisfied his heart with food” (14.111 = 5.95) does the host fill the bowl from which he himself drank and give it to his guest, an act so plainly the host's that his name is not added (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.112); then the talk turns to the master, and Eumaeus asks the host's questions only after a long talk of his own (14.185–190; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]).

**The suitors' table.** “Eat now, stranger, what slaves have here, / porkers; but the fat hogs the suitors eat” (14.80–81). The suitors “devour his possessions overweeningly, and there is no sparing”, and they waste the wine (14.92–95; see [[the-suitors]] and [[possessions]]); the herdsmen, Eumaeus says, have endured misery for long, “while others eat up our labor without payment” (14.415–417).

**In the false tale.** The Cretan begins with a wish for an endless meal: “May there now be, for us two, for a long time, both food / and sweet wine, as we stay inside the hut, / to feast on in silence, while others go about the work” (14.193–195), for a whole year would not be enough to tell his sorrows (see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[a-time-for-many-words]]). Before the voyage to Egypt his companions feasted for six days on the victims he gave them (14.249–251; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and at the end the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] crew, having bound him, “hastily took their supper beside the shore of the sea” while the gods loosed his bonds (14.345–348).

**The supper.** “But now it is time for supper” (14.407). When the boar has been sacrificed and roasted, Eumaeus stands up to carve, “for in his mind he knew well what was due”, and divides it into seven portions, one for the nymphs and Hermes and the others for each of the men (14.432–436; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]). J. L. Myres told Monro that in Greece this was still done with great ceremony: the host stands and picks over the whole dish, putting fair equivalents toward each guest before the meat goes on the plates, “a clear survival from the plateless stage” (Monro on 14.432). δαίς, the feast, is a meal divided into shares, and the host gives the honored share, the long chine, to his guest (14.437–438; see [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]), with the words “Eat, strange guest, and take your delight in these things, / such as they are” (14.443–444; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). Mesaulius, the swineherd's own slave, serves the bread and clears it away (14.449–455; see [[mesaulius]]), and the meal ends in the Odyssey's verses: “And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready before them. / But when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (14.453–454 = 1.149–150; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]).
