summary: Eumaeus has given the stranger “good cheer”, ξείνια, and would not kill a guest he has fed (14.404–405); in the false tale the Egyptians all gave the Cretan goods (14.285–286), and the king of the Thesprotians, who had entertained Odysseus, showed him the treasures Odysseus had gathered (14.321–326). The gift the stranger asks for is clothing (14.152–154, 14.395–397), and the swineherd promises it from Telemachus (14.510–517).
# ξείνια (14.404) is claimed by the Book 9 note, with its English "good cheer".
refs: [14.285-286, 14.321-326, 14.515-517]
---

**Good cheer.** ξείνια are what a host gives a guest, and here they are the hut, the seat of brushwood and goatskin and the meal (see the Book 9 note and [[xenia]]). Eumaeus names them when the stranger offers to stake his life on his news: “Stranger, so indeed would I have good fame and prosperity / among men, both now and hereafter, / if, after I had brought you into my hut and given you good cheer, / I should then kill you and take away your dear life” (14.402–405; see [[prayer]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). The guest who has eaten at the table is safe. Heracles was remembered for the opposite: he killed his guest Iphitus in his own house, respecting “neither the regard of the gods nor the table” he had set before him (21.27–30, a plain rendering; see [[the-gods-honor-justice]] and [[table]]).

**Gifts in the false tale.** The Cretan, spared by the king of Egypt, stayed seven years: “and I gathered many / goods among the Egyptian men; for they all gave to me” (14.285–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), as Menelaus gathered wealth among men of another tongue on his way home from Troy (3.301–302; 4.81–91). In [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] the king “said / that he had entertained him and made him welcome on his way to his native land, / and he showed me the possessions that Odysseus had gathered, / bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil. / It would feed another man to the tenth generation, / so many treasures of his lay stored in the halls of the lord” (14.321–326; see [[pheidon]] and [[possessions]]). κειμήλια, “treasures”, are things laid up, as guest-gifts are laid up in a house, and the verse of the treasure is the Iliad's verse for the ransom a captured man's father would pay (*Iliad* 6.48, 10.379, 11.133; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[apoina]]). The lie stands near the truth: the stranger has just been set ashore with the gifts of the Phaeacians and hidden them in the cave of the nymphs (13.363–371; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The gift asked for.** What the beggar wants is clothing. He asks it as the reward for good news, “clothe me in a cloak and a tunic, fine clothes” (14.154; see [[euangelion]]), and as the stake of his wager (14.395–397; see [[rhetre]] and [[oath]]); and when his [[the-tale-of-the-cloak|tale of the cloak]] at Troy has hinted at it without asking, Eumaeus answers in Nausicaa's words (6.192–193) that he shall not lack clothing, “nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way, / for now” (14.510–512; see [[supplication]]). The gift itself is left to the master's son: “he himself will give you a cloak and a tunic as clothing, / and will send you wherever your heart and spirit bid you” (14.516–517; see [[cloak]] and [[telemachus]]).
