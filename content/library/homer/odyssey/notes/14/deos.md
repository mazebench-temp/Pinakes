summary: Fear in the book: “the way of slaves, / who are always afraid” (14.59–60); the “mighty fear of the gods' regard” that falls even on raiders laden with spoil (14.88); and Eumaeus, who keeps the stranger “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (14.389).
# δειδιότων (14.60), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.
grc: [δειδιότων]
---

**Afraid.** Slaves are “always afraid, when masters hold command over them, / the young ones” (αἰεὶ δειδιότων, 14.60–61; see [[doulios]] and [[anax]]): the reason Eumaeus gives for the smallness of his gift (14.58–59).

**Fear of the gods.** “Even men hostile and merciless, who set foot on another's / land, and Zeus gives them spoil, / and they fill their ships and set out to go home, / even on their minds falls a mighty fear of the gods' regard” (ὄπιδος κρατερὸν δέος, 14.85–88; see [[piracy]] and [[the-gods-honor-justice]]). The sentence begins with a nominative that has no verb and is taken up again in 88, καὶ μὲν τοῖς, with a different construction (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.85). The suitors have no such fear (14.82; see [[themis]]). Eumaeus has it, and says so: “but in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (Δία ξένιον δείσας, 14.389; see [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]] and [[pity]]).
