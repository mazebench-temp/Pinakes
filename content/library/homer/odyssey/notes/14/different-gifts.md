# "ἀνὴρ ἐπιτέρπεται ἔργοις" (14.228), only here.

{{quote:eae9f0e0-24f2-5fbc-9f5d-468c5ac5ca65}}

**The raider's gift.** The Cretan has just described himself in the ambush, first out of it and never foreseeing death (14.216–221; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]). Now he gives the other half of the portrait: “Such was I in war; but work was never dear to me, / nor the care of a household, which rears splendid children” (14.222–223). ἔργον here is the work of the farm, and οἰκωφελίη, “the care of a household”, the thrift that makes a house prosper (see the translation's table of key words). What he loved instead were the things that are “grim” and “things to shudder at” to others (14.226; see [[piracy]]). τά που κτλ. is “things which a god made dear to me”, not being the things that would naturally be so (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on 14.227): the god gave him his tastes as he gives men their gifts (see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]).

**A later gnome.** The last verse is a maxim, and Monro held that it is doubtless of later date, since the form ἔργοις, for ϝέργοις, is doubly post-Homeric (on 14.228; see the translation's note on 14.228). The thought is the one Odysseus gave the Phaeacians, that the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all men alike (8.167–177), and Polydamas' to Hector in the Iliad (*Iliad* 13.726–734).

**The man who loved raids.** The Cretan's distaste for farm work is the opposite of the real Odysseus, who will challenge the suitor Eurymachus to a contest of mowing in the springtime and of plowing a field, “so that we might test each other in work” (18.366–375, a plain rendering). The false self he presents to the swineherd, the rich man's bastard who lived by war and raiding, is a man the swineherd's master is not.
