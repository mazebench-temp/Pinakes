**The mistress.** Eumaeus built the fold and bought his own slave “without his mistress and old Laertes” (14.9, 14.451; see [[eumaeus]] and [[laertes]]), δέσποινα being the lady of the house. No wanderer bringing news of Odysseus could persuade “his wife and his dear son” (14.123), but each tries:

{{quote:c46dccde-bfd3-545a-ba79-2810c5c303bc}}

ἣ θέμις ἐστὶ γυναικός, “as is the custom of a woman” (see [[themis]] and [[tears]]). The stranger who hears this is the husband himself, and he will come to her as such a wanderer, with a false tale of having seen Odysseus in Crete (19.165–202; see [[odysseus]]). Eumaeus goes to the city only when “prudent Penelope / urges me to come, when news comes from somewhere”, and the news is questioned by those who grieve for the master and those who eat his substance (14.373–377; see [[periphron]] and [[the-suitors]]). The stranger's oath promises vengeance on whoever “here dishonors his wife and his shining son” (14.164), and Eumaeus wishes that Odysseus may come home “as I wish it, and as Penelope does” (14.172).

**Outside Homer.** In Ovid's letter of Penelope she questions every stranger who lands on Ithaca and gives him a letter for her husband: *quisquis ad haec vertit peregrinam litora puppim, / ille mihi de te multa rogatus abit*, “whoever turns a foreign ship to these shores goes away from me asked many things about you” (*Heroides* 1.59–62; see [[ovid-penelopes-letter]] and [[ovid]]).
