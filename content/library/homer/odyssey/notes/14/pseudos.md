summary: The book of the lying tale. Eumaeus says that “vagrants wanting keep tell lies at random” (14.124–125); in the tale the Phoenician sails off with the Cretan “having planned lies” (14.296); and Eumaeus, who believes the tale except where it is true, asks the teller, “Why should a man like you / lie for no reason?” (14.364–365), and begs him, “do not try to please me with lies” (14.387).
# ψεύδοντʼ (14.125), ψεύδεσθαι (14.365) and ψεύδεσσι (14.387), first met here among the Odyssey's notes.

en: [lies]
---

**Vagrants.** Eumaeus disbelieves every traveler on principle: “Old man, no man who comes here from his wanderings / bringing news of him could persuade his wife and his dear son; / but vagrants wanting keep tell lies at random, / and do not wish to tell the truth” (ψεύδοντʼ, οὐδʼ ἐθέλουσιν ἀληθέα μυθήσασθαι, 14.122–125; see [[vagrants-tell-lies]], [[mythos]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). Each of them goes to Penelope and “speaks deceits”, and she questions him in tears; the stranger too, Eumaeus says, would fashion a tale for a cloak and a tunic (14.126–132; see [[apate]] and [[epos]]). Alcinous had said the opposite of Odysseus, that he was no deceiver like the many “fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see” (11.364–366; see [[eperopeuo]]). The beggar answers with a curse on the man “who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (14.156–157; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]]).

**The tale's liars.** In the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|false tale]] the liars are other men. The Phoenician, “skilled in deceits”, kept the Cretan a year and then put him on a ship for Libya, “having planned lies, so that I might take a cargo there with him, / but there, in truth, so that he might sell me and get an untold price” (ψεύδεα βουλεύσας, 14.288, 14.295–297; see [[phoenicians]], [[boule]] and [[trade]]). Two purposes are named, the one stated and the real one.

**For no reason.** Eumaeus believes the wanderings and rejects the one true thing in them: “But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus. Why should a man like you / lie for no reason?” (μαψιδίως ψεύδεσθαι, 14.363–365; see [[peitho]]). He has been deceived before, by an Aetolian who said he had seen Odysseus in Crete (14.379–385; see [[aetolians]] and [[apate]]), and he ends, “do not try to please me with lies, nor bewitch me with them” (μήτε τί μοι ψεύδεσσι χαρίζεο μήτε τι θέλγε, 14.387; see [[thelgo]]). The beggar, who lies throughout the book, is disbelieved only in his one true word, that Odysseus is coming home (14.151–152, 14.161).
