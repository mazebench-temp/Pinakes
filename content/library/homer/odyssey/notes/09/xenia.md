summary: The stranger names himself so that he may be the Phaeacians' guest-friend (9.16–18), and his tale tests the Cyclopes by the question he asks on every shore, whether they are savage “or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (9.175–176). Polyphemus asks the strangers who they are before he feeds them, scorns Zeus the god of guests, eats them, and promises Nobody the guest-gift of being eaten last (9.252–370); Odysseus' last word is that he did not shrink from eating strangers in his own house (9.477–479).

**The guest-friend.** Odysseus gives his name “so that you too / may know it, and I hereafter, when I have escaped the pitiless day, / may be your guest-friend, though I live in a house far away” (9.16–18). ξεῖνος is here the guest-friend of the bond between houses (see the translation's table of key words), the bond that the guest-gifts of the day before have made (8.389; see [[guest-gifts]]), and it will hold when he is far away.

**The test.** On every shore the question is the one he asked waking on Scheria and the one Alcinous asked at the end of Book 8 (6.119–121, 8.572–576): leaving the fleet at the island of goats, he goes with his own ship to “make trial of these men, who they are, / whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (9.174–176; see [[peira]], [[hybris]], [[themis]] and [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). The narrator has given the answer already: the Cyclopes care nothing for one another (9.115), and they have no ships to carry them “to the cities of men, as men often / cross the sea in ships to visit one another” (9.128–129; see [[cyclopes]]). In the cave Odysseus will not take the cheeses and lambs and go, as his companions beg, “so that I might see him himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.228–229): εἰ “to see whether”, as if a word of seeking or testing were understood (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.229; see [[kerdos]]). ξείνια here are the gifts a host gives a guest (see the translation's table of key words).

**The strangers' question.** Polyphemus' first words are a host's: “Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the watery ways? / Is it on some business, or do you wander at random / over the sea, like pirates” (9.252–255 = 3.71–74; see [[strangers-who-are-you]], [[prexis]] and [[piracy]]). A host asks them after the meal: Telemachus bids the stranger tell his need “when you have tasted the meal” (1.123–124), and Nestor finds it “more fitting to question and to ask / the strangers who they are, now that they have taken their fill of food” (3.69–70). The Cyclops asks before any meal, and gives none.

ξεινήιον πορεῖν is the most general expression for hospitality, δωτίνη a more particular gift, and θέμις is regular custom, as δίκη can be (Merry on 9.267; see [[themis]] and [[doron]]); αἰδοίοισιν means only that all guests are revered as guests (Merry on 9.271; see [[aidos]], [[supplication]] and [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). ξείνιος, “the god of guests”, is Zeus' title as Menelaus gave it in the Iliad (*Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[zeus]]). The Cyclops calls the appeal a fool's: “for the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus / nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger” (9.273–276; see [[nepios]] and [[theos]]), a braggart's boast that his own neighbors' words and his claim to be Poseidon's son contradict (Merry on 9.275).

**The guest-gift of Nobody.** The wine turns the custom inside out. The Cyclops asks the stranger's name “so that I may give you a guest-gift, in which you may rejoice” (9.356); Odysseus asks for it, “as you promised” (9.365), and is told: “Nobody I will eat last among his companions, / and the others before him; that shall be your guest-gift” (9.369–370; see [[nobody]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The wine itself was offered “as a libation”, flattery as to a god rather than a gift to a host (9.349; see [[libation]]).

**The verdict.** From the ship Odysseus names the crime: “Yes, truly your evil deeds were to catch up with you, / hard man, since you did not shrink from eating strangers / in your own house; therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.477–479). σῷ ἐνὶ οἴκῳ, “in your own house”, makes it the worst violation of hospitality (Merry on 9.478; see [[poine]] and [[hard]]). The Cyclops answers with a mock host's offer, “But come here, Odysseus, so that I may set good cheer before you / and speed your escort” (9.517–518): ξείνια, “good cheer”, the welcome a host sets before a guest, in the verse with which Calypso receives Hermes and Charis receives Thetis (5.91; *Iliad* 18.387; see [[pompe]]).
