---
summary: Ismarus is the first landfall of the tale. From Ilios the wind brings Odysseus to the Cicones, to Ismarus, and he sacks the city (9.39–42); the wine taken from “the sacred citadel of the Cicones” is still in the ships at the island of goats (9.163–165), and the best of it, the gift of Maron, the priest of Apollo there, goes with him to the Cyclops' cave (9.196–211).
---

**From Ilios to Ismarus.** The tale begins where the war ended:

{{quote:71e76b53-aa60-587d-a68a-9447c65e9833}}

No god is named for this first wind, as Zeus is for the storm that follows (9.67; see [[boreas]]). The Cicones had fought for Troy (*Iliad* 2.846; see [[cicones]]), which, Merry suggests, accounts for a Greek hero's raid on their city (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.39; see [[troy]]). αὐτούς, “the men themselves”, is set against πόλιν, “the city”, as in the Cretan's tale of a [[the-raid-on-egypt|raid in Egypt]], where the raiders carried off the women and children “and killed the men” (αὐτούς τʼ ἔκτεινον, 14.264–265; Merry on 9.40). The spoil is shared out “so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share” (9.42), nearly the verse with which, in Nestor's tale, Neleus gives the plunder of Elis to the people to divide (*Iliad* 11.704–705; see the translation's note on 9.42); Odysseus says it again over the Cyclops' flocks (9.549; see [[odysseus]]).

**The wine of Ismarus.** The city's wine is the men's undoing: “But there much wine was drunk, and many sheep / they slaughtered beside the shore” (9.45–46), while the Cicones gathered their neighbors (9.47–50; see [[cicones]] and [[wine]]). It lasts the voyage. On the island of goats “the red wine was not yet spent from the ships, / but there was still some in them; for we had each drawn off much in two-handled jars / when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones” (9.163–165; see [[the-island-of-goats]] and [[two-handled-jar]]). ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον, “the sacred citadel”, is said in Homer only of Ismarus and of Troy, which Odysseus had sacked before it (1.2; see [[hieros]]). The text prints a full stop after Κικόνων, which leaves the participle without a verb: Κικόνων goes with πτολίεθρον, as Τροίης with πτολίεθρον in the proem (see the translation's note on 9.165).

**The priest of Apollo.** The best wine of Ismarus was a gift. Odysseus carries to the Cyclops' cave “a goatskin of dark wine, / sweet wine, which Maron, son of Euanthes, had given me, / the priest of Apollo, who used to stand guard over Ismarus” (9.196–198; see [[maron]] and [[apollo]]). The raiders had protected him with his child and his wife “in reverence; for he lived in the grove full of trees / of Phoebus Apollo” (9.199–201; see [[full-of-trees]]): they spared the priest out of awe for the god whose grove he kept. ἀμφιβεβήκει, “used to stand guard”, Merry takes of the god, as Chryses prays to Apollo “who stand guard over Chryse” (*Iliad* 1.37), and the pluperfect of the time before the city fell, since a god deserts a city whose doom has been decreed; Nitzsch's present ἀμφιβέβηκε is needless (Merry on 9.198; see the translation's note on 9.198). By this reading Apollo had already left Ismarus to its fate, and the raiders spared his priest and his household. The gifts were seven talents of gold, a mixing bowl all of silver, and twelve jars of sweet unmixed wine, “a divine drink”, which only Maron, his wife and one housekeeper knew of (9.201–207; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[mixing-bowl]]); the wine of Ismarus thus outlasts the city, and blinds the Cyclops (9.345–394; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
