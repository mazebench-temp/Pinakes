summary: Each evening the Cyclops shuts the mouth of his cave with a door-stone that two and twenty wagons could not have heaved from the ground (9.240–243, 9.340); in the morning he lifts it away easily and sets it back “as one sets the lid on a quiver” (9.313–314). It keeps Odysseus from killing the sleeping giant (9.302–305), and when the Cyclops is blind he takes it away himself and sits in the doorway (9.415–418).

**The stone.** The Cyclops throws down his firewood, drives in the flocks he milks, and closes the cave (see [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]]):

{{quote:5d39d57b-9d25-5125-a212-771be23c3922}}

The twenty-two wagons, W. W. Merry thinks, are probably a hyperbolic parody of the proverbial expression of the Iliad, where not even two men, the best of the people, “could easily heave it from the ground onto a wagon” (*Iliad* 12.447–448; see [[wagon]] and [[stone-throwing]]); there is something intentionally comic in the grave exactness of the number, since twenty-two wagons cannot be imagined working together, and ὀχλίσσειαν, “heave”, hardly describes the work of a wagon (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.241). The Iliad has the same number for great size: Ajax's “great spear for fighting at sea” is “twenty-two cubits long”, and a prize tripod is one “holding twenty-two measures” (*Iliad* 15.677–678, 23.264; Merry on 9.241). ἠλίβατον, “lofty”, said of a block of stone, disproves Apollonius' derivation from ἥλιος, “sun”, and βαίνειν, “go”, as of a height only the sun can climb; Merry prefers Buttmann's, after the scholia, close to δύσβατος, “hard to climb” (on 9.243; see the translation's note on 9.243 and [[lofty]]). On the reading τετράκυκλοι, “four-wheeled”, see the translation's note on 9.241–242.

**The stone as a prison.** That night Odysseus thinks of stabbing the sleeping Cyclops in the chest, “but a second thought held me back. / For there we too would have perished by sheer destruction; / for we could not have pushed away from the high doorway / with our hands the heavy stone that he had set against it” (9.302–305; see [[sword]] and [[door]]). In the morning the Cyclops drives out his flocks, “easily taking away the great door-stone; and then / he set it back again, as one sets the lid on a quiver” (9.313–314; see [[the-lid-on-a-quiver]] and [[quiver]]): the weight that no team could move is a lid in his hand.

**The open door.** Blinded, the Cyclops gropes his way to the door, “took the stone away from the doorway, / and sat down himself in the doorway, stretching out both his hands, / to see if he could catch anyone going out of the door among the sheep” (9.416–418; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]], [[ram]] and [[sheep]]). The stone that had kept the men in no longer stands between them and the morning: they go out under the rams.
