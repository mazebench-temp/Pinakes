---
summary: Odysseus' tale gives its losses more than one cause: his homecoming of sorrows “which Zeus sent upon me” (9.38) and “an evil fate from Zeus” at Ismarus (9.52), but also the men, “the great fools”, who would not obey (9.44), and his own refusal to leave the cave, “it would have been far better” (9.228). To the Cyclops he says “so, I suppose, Zeus wished to devise it” (9.262), and from the ship he tells him that his own evil deeds have caught up with him (9.477–479).
refs: [9.38, 9.44, 9.52, 9.228, 9.262, 9.477-479]
---

Zeus' complaint in the first council of the gods, that mortals blame the gods for the sorrows that their own recklessness brings them beyond their share (1.32–34), is played out in the tale. Odysseus begins with the god: “my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]]), and at Ismarus “an evil fate from Zeus stood beside us” (9.52; see [[aisa]]). But he has just told how his men stayed to drink and slaughter on the shore, “the great fools” who would not obey him (9.43–46; see [[nepios]]). In the cave the fault is his own, and he says so: “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—” (9.228; see [[kerdos]]). To the Cyclops he presents the voyage as the will of Zeus, “so, I suppose, Zeus wished to devise it” (9.262; see [[metis]]), and the Cyclops' own fate as the work of his deeds: “Yes, truly your evil deeds were to catch up with you, / hard man, since you did not shrink from eating strangers / in your own house; therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.477–479; see [[poine]]). The Cyclops, for his part, blames Nobody, “a bad man”, and the wine that overpowered his wits (9.453–455; see [[nobody]] and [[phrenes]]).
