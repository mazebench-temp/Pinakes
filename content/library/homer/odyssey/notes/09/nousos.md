---
summary: Told that “Nobody” is killing him, the Cyclopes answer Polyphemus, “If then nobody is doing you violence, alone as you are, / there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus; / but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon” (9.410–412).
---

The neighbors take the cry for madness. If no one is doing him violence, what ails him comes from a god: Διὸς μεγάλου, “of great Zeus”, is the genitive of the source, as in ἀνέμων … κῦμα, the wave raised by the winds (13.99), and their meaning was probably that they thought he had gone hopelessly mad (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.411; see [[nobody]]). The sentiment is common: Merry compares Eurymachus' saying that what comes from a god cannot be escaped (θεόθεν δʼ οὐκ ἔστʼ ἀλέασθαι, 16.447). οἶον ἐόντα is “defenceless, with no neighbors to help you” (Merry on 9.410). The advice that follows, to pray to his father Poseidon (9.412), is the one the Cyclops takes once he knows the name of the man who blinded him (9.526–536; see [[prayer]] and [[poseidon]]). Polyphemus had boasted that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (9.275); his neighbors grant Zeus the power to send sickness, an inconsistency that Merry finds true to the braggart's nature (Merry on 9.275; see [[zeus]] and [[theos]]).
