summary: The first adventure of the tale, in twenty-eight verses. The wind from Ilios brings the ships to Ismarus; Odysseus sacks the city and shares out the spoil, his men stay to feast against his word, and the Cicones of the inland come at dawn and fight until the afternoon, when six companions from each ship lie dead (9.39–61); the ships sail on only after calling three times on each of the dead (9.62–66). The wine taken from the city comes back in the cave of the Cyclops (9.163–165, 9.196–211).

**Ismarus.** “From Ilios the wind bore me and brought me near the Cicones, / to Ismarus” (9.39–40). Ἰσμάρῳ stands in apposition to Κικόνεσσι and defines it more closely, as “came to Cyprus, / to Paphos” does (8.362–363; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.39). Merry puts the Cicones on the south coast of Thrace and their town at the foot of a mountain of the same name, and explains the attack by the war: the Thracians were allies of Troy (on 9.39; *Iliad* 2.846; see [[cicones]], [[ismarus]] and [[thrace]]). In πόλιν ἔπραθον, ὤλεσα δʼ αὐτούς, “I sacked the city and destroyed the men themselves”, αὐτούς is set against the city (9.40; Merry on 9.40). The wives and possessions are divided “so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share” (9.42): μοι is “as far as I could help it” (Merry on 9.42; see the translation's note on 9.42). The words after the verb are those of Neleus' division of the spoil from Elis (*Iliad* 11.705), and the whole verse returns when the Cyclops' flocks are divided at the end of the book (9.549; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[possessions]]).

**The folly.** Odysseus bade them flee διερῷ ποδί, “with nimble foot” (9.43; see the translation's note on 9.43), “but they, the great fools, would not obey” (9.44; see [[nepios]]). The subject of ἔσφαζον, “they slaughtered”, is the companions, as of ἐπίθοντο before it (9.46; Merry on 9.46); the sheep and the “shambling cattle with curving horns” are the Iliad's (*Iliad* 9.466; see [[shambling]] and [[with-curving-horns]]).

**The Cicones of the inland.** ἤπειρον ναίοντες, “living inland”, sets them against the Cicones of Ismarus, who lived by the sea (Merry on 9.49). They “knew how to fight with men from chariots, / and on foot too, where there was need” (9.49–50): ἀφʼ ἵππων is “from the war-chariot”, not from horseback, and the sense is that they knew “how to fight from the chariot, and how to fight on foot in that part of the battle where one ought to do so” (Merry on 9.49–50; see the translation's note on 9.49–50 and [[chariot]]). They come ἠέριοι, “early in the morning” (9.52), a word that some connect with the morning mists, others with ἦρι, “early” (Merry on 9.52).

**The battle.** “Then an evil fate from Zeus stood beside us, / men of dread fate, so that we might suffer many sorrows” (9.52–53; see [[aisa]] and [[zeus]]). στησάμενοι … μάχην is “having set the battle in array”; the subject of the fighting is both sides, which explains ἀλλήλους, “one another”, while πάθοιμεν, “we might suffer”, and μένομεν, “we held our ground”, are Odysseus and his men alone (9.53–57; Merry on 9.54). The hours are told as the Iliad tells them (see the general article): the morning, while “the sacred day was growing” (9.56), and the afternoon, βουλυτόνδε, “toward the time for unyoking oxen” (9.58). The preposition in μετενίσσετο marks the change in the sun's course after noon, when his rising becomes a descent; βουλυτόνδε is found only here and at *Iliad* 16.779, one of the graphic phrases for the parts of the day that were in use before the day was divided into hours (Merry on 9.58; see the translation's note on 9.58 and [[helios]]).

**Six from each ship.** ἓξ δʼ ἀφʼ ἑκάστης νηός, “from each ship six” (9.60). Crates took it to mean no more than seventy-two in all, an average of six from each of the twelve ships (9.159); Zoilus, called the scourge of Homer, was offended at the suspicious symmetry of exactly six from each (Merry on 9.60; see the translation's note on 9.60). The companions are ἐυκνήμιδες, “well-greaved” (see [[well-greaved]] and [[hetairos]]).

**Glad to have escaped.** ἄσμενοι ἐκ θανάτοιο, “glad to have escaped death” (9.63), is the shortened form of φύγεν ἄσμενος ἐκ θανάτοιο, “was glad to escape from death” (*Iliad* 20.350; Merry on 9.63). The two verses of the departure close the adventure of the Cyclops as well (9.62–63 = 9.565–566), and the first of them leads into it (9.105; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]).

**The triple call.** “Nor did my curving ships go on farther / until we had called three times on each of our wretched companions / who had died on the plain, cut down by the Cicones” (9.64–66). The ships are in flight, and still they wait: οὐδʼ ἄρα μοι προτέρω is, in Merry's rendering, “yet, for all that, I let not my ships sail on” (on 9.64). For the rite and the ancient explanations of it, see [[the-call-to-the-dead]] and the translation's note on 9.65.

**The wine of the sacked city.** The red wine was “not yet spent from the ships” on the island of goats, “for we had each drawn off much in two-handled jars / when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones” (9.163–165; see [[two-handled-jar]] and the translation's note on 9.165). The wine that blinds the Cyclops is the gift of Maron, the priest of Apollo whom they spared at Ismarus (9.196–211; see [[maron]] and [[wine]]). Merry takes ὃς Ἴσμαρον ἀμφιβεβήκει, “who used to stand guard over Ismarus”, of the god, comparing Chryses' prayer to Apollo, “who stand guard over Chryse” (*Iliad* 1.37), and the pluperfect as looking back to the time before the city fell: a god could not resist the necessity of fate, and would desert a city when the sentence of its doom had gone forth (on 9.198; see [[apollo]] and the translation's note on 9.198).
