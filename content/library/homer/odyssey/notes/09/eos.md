**Five dawns.** The verse of the Odyssey's mornings, ἦμος δʼ ἠριγένεια φάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered”, comes five times in the book, more often than in any other book of either poem (see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]] and [[rosy-fingered]]). The tale of the Cyclops is told day by day. The first dawn finds the men on the island of goats, where they hunt and feast until sunset (9.152–169; see [[the-island-of-goats]]); at the second Odysseus calls an assembly and crosses with his own ship to the land of the Cyclopes (9.170–180); the third and the fourth come in the cave, the morning of the Cyclops' second meal of men and the morning of the escape (9.307–312, 9.437–440); and at the fifth the ships sail from the island (9.560–564; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[sunset-lines]]).

**Waiting for Dawn.** On the night of the landing, “There we fell asleep and waited for brilliant Dawn” (9.151; again 12.7), and in the cave, on the night of the first meal and on the night of the blinding, “So then, groaning, we waited for brilliant Dawn” (9.306, 9.436; see the translation's formula row). Ἠῶ δῖαν, “brilliant Dawn”, is her name in the Iliad's verse of Phoenix, who “lay down and waited for brilliant Dawn” (*Iliad* 9.662; see [[brilliant]]). ἀποβρίξαντες is “having fallen asleep”, properly “heavy with sleep” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.151).

**Dawn of the lovely tresses.** After the storm that Zeus sends against the ships, “There for two nights and two days without ceasing / we lay, eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together. / But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day, / we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails” (9.74–77; see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[boreas]]). The third verse brought Odysseus in sight of Scheria after two nights and two days in the sea (5.388–392; see the Book 5 note), and it comes again in Book 10 (10.144).

**Morning.** At Ismarus the Cicones of the inland come “early in the morning” (ἠέριοι, 9.52), a word that some connect with the morning mists, ἀήρ, and others with ἦρι, “early” (Merry on 9.52). The battle is told by the parts of the day: “As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing, / so long we held our ground and fought them off, though they were more. / But when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen, / then at last the Cicones overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (9.56–59; see [[helios]], [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). ἠώς is the morning here, the part of the day before noon, as in the same verse of the Iliad (*Iliad* 8.66, 11.84), and the line is not counted for the goddess.

**Toward the dawn.** Ithaca lies farthest out “toward the darkness, while the others lie apart toward the dawn and the sun” (9.26; see [[ithaca]]). πρὸς ἠῶ τʼ ἠέλιόν τε is the east, as in the Iliad's “toward the dawn and the sun” (*Iliad* 12.239), and Merry finds the words so distinctly descriptive of the east that they settle the meaning of ζόφος, the darkness, to be the west, against Strabo's “northward” (Merry's appendix on Ithaca; see the translation's note on 9.21–28).
