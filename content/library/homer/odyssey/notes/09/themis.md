summary: The Cyclopes are “overbearing and lawless”: “They have no assemblies that bring counsel, and no ordinances”, and each one “gives the law / to his children and his wives” (9.106–115). **A people without ordinances.** The tale's first word of the Cyclopes is a judgment: “the land of the Cyclopes, overbearing and lawless” (ὑπερφιάλων ἀθεμίστων, 9.106; see [[overbearing]] and the translation's table of key words). They neither plant nor plow, “trusting in the immortal gods”, and the earth bears for them unsown (9.107–111; see [[peitho]]). Then the want of law:

θεμιστεύει is “is lord and master over” his household, and Merry finds a sort of irony in the Cyclopes' knowing no θέμιστες while each of them θεμιστεύει: “Laws imply Society: the Cyclopes are a law to themselves” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.114; see [[cyclopes]], [[assembly]] and [[boule]]). In the Iliad the θέμιστες are what Zeus has put into a king's hands with the scepter (*Iliad* 9.98–99; see the entry); the Cyclopes have no king and no assembly, only fathers. Plato and Aristotle took the verses for a picture of the first government: Plato's Athenian cites them for the “lordship” (δυναστεία) of the men scattered in single households after the great destructions, where the eldest rules and the rest follow him “like birds”, which still survives among Greeks and barbarians (*Laws* 680b–e), and Aristotle for the household ruled by its eldest, “for they lived scattered” (*Politics* 1252b19–24), and for the man who lives as he pleases, “laying down the law for children and wife in the manner of the Cyclopes” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 1180a26–29; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]]).

**Lawless ways.** Polyphemus is the type of his people. He lives apart from the others, “and knew lawless ways” (ἀθεμίστια ᾔδη, 9.189), and again when he sleeps on the rams, “the monster, who knew lawless ways” (9.428). The neuter plural with οἶδα expresses a disposition and the practice that goes with it, “of lawless mood”, or, in the language of the Psalms, “with his mind set upon unrighteousness”, a use found only in the Odyssey (Merry on 9.189; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Odysseus' spirit had foreboded such a man before he saw him, “a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (δίκας … θέμιστας, 9.215; see the translation's table of key words).

**The test and the custom.** The question Odysseus asks on landing sets justice beside the welcome of strangers: “whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust (οὐδὲ δίκαιοι), / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (9.175–176; see [[xenia]] and [[hybris]]). His plea in the cave appeals to θέμις itself: a guest-gift, or some present, “as is the custom with strangers” (ἥ τε ξείνων θέμις ἐστίν, 9.268), where θέμις is regular custom, as δίκη can be (Merry on 9.267; see [[guest-gifts]]).
