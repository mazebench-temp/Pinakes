---
summary: Odysseus escapes from the cave “curled up under his shaggy belly”, clinging beneath the best of the rams (9.432–435).
---

The book's one γαστήρ is an animal's: “I grasped him by the back, and curled up under his shaggy belly / I lay” (λασίην ὑπὸ γαστέρʼ ἐλυσθείς, 9.433–434; see [[ram]] and [[sheep]]). κατὰ … λαβών go together, “having grasped his back”, and ἐλυσθείς is “curled up”, the scholium's συστραφείς; κεῖσθαι suits a man stretched out back downward, holding to the fleece with his hands, since there was no one to tie him (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.433–434; see [[metis]]). The Cyclops' own belly has another word: when he has eaten two men he lies down, having “filled his great belly” (μεγάλην ἐμπλήσατο νηδύν, 9.296; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Euripides' Cyclops makes his belly his god: he sacrifices to no one but himself, “and to the greatest of the gods, this belly” (γαστρὶ τῇδε, *Cyclops* 334–335).
