---
summary: The North Wind comes twice in the tale. After the raid on the Cicones, Zeus the cloud-gatherer rouses it against the ships with a heaven-sent storm, and its force tears the sails; and when the ships are rounding Malea on the way home, the wave, the current and the North Wind drive Odysseus off course past Cythera, into nine days of deadly winds.
---

**The storm of Zeus.** “But against the ships Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused the North Wind / with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (9.67–69; see [[zeus]] and [[cloud-gatherer]]). The ships are driven slantwise, with the wind abeam, and “their sails / the force of the wind tore in three and four pieces” (9.70–71; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.70; see the translation's note on 9.70 and [[sails]]); the men lower the sails, row to land, and lie there two nights and two days (9.72–75). The Cicones lived on the south coast of Thrace (Merry on 9.39; see [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]), and the North Wind is the wind of Thrace: in the Iliad it blows “from Thrace” with the West Wind (*Iliad* 9.5; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]] and [[thrace]]).

**Off Malea.** “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera” (9.79–81; see [[malea]], [[cythera]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Malea is where the storms of the homecomings fall: when Menelaus reached it, far-seeing Zeus “poured on him the breath of shrill winds” (3.286–290), and a storm wind caught up Agamemnon there (4.514–516). Merry recalls the proverb, quoted by Strabo, “After doubling Malea forget your home” (on 9.80; see the translation's note on 9.80). “From there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds / over the fish-filled sea” to the land of the Lotus-eaters (9.82–84; see [[fish-filled]], [[lotus-eaters]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). The swift North Wind that Athena roused to carry Odysseus toward the Phaeacians (5.385–386; see the Book 5 note) is here the wind that drives him away from home.
