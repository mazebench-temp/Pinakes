summary: The tale begins with the singer: “truly it is a fine thing to listen to a singer / such as this one is, like the gods in his voice” (9.3–4), and the feast where the people listen to him is “something like the finest thing” (9.11). Then the stranger names himself as one whose “fame reaches the sky” (9.20), and begins the story that the singer had sung only in part.
refs: [9.3-4, 9.7, 9.19-20]
---

The tale to Alcinous begins as a reply to a song. Demodocus has sung of the wooden horse, the stranger has wept, and Alcinous has told him that the gods spun destruction for men “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (8.579–580; see the entry and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Odysseus answers with praise of the singer in the words Telemachus used of Phemius, “truly it is a fine thing to listen to a singer / such as this one is, like the gods in his voice” (9.3–4 = 1.370–371; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.3–4; see [[singers]], [[demodocus]] and [[like-a-god-in-his-voice]]), and of the feast where “the banqueters throughout the house listen to a singer” (9.7; see [[feast]]). Then he gives his name with his fame, “my fame reaches the sky” (9.20; see [[kleos]]), as the lay that Demodocus sang from had a fame that “had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.74). The man of the songs becomes their singer: Alcinous will say that he told his tale “as a singer does, with skill” (11.368, a plain rendering; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).
