On the night of the blinding the other Cyclopes bid the Cyclops pray to his father, lord Poseidon; when he has heard Odysseus' name, the Cyclops offers him an escort from the famous shaker of the earth, who alone can heal his eye, and then prays to Poseidon that Odysseus may never reach home, or, if it is his fate, come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house. “and the dark-haired god heard him”: the anger of the god begins here.

**The god of the lie.** The Cyclops asks the strangers where they moored their ship, and Odysseus sees the trap: “So he spoke, testing me, but he did not escape my notice, for I knew much” (9.281). His answer is a lie: “My ship Poseidon the earth-shaker shattered, / dashing it against the rocks at the borders of your land, / driving it on a headland; and a wind from the sea had borne it there; / but I with these escaped sheer destruction” (9.283–286; see [[earth-shaker]], [[steep]], [[odysseus]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). εἰδότα πολλά is “with my knowledge of the world” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.281), and νέα, scanned as one syllable, is Aristarchus' reading, where several manuscripts have νῆα (Merry on 9.283 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 9.283). The lie keeps the ship hidden and gives the wreck to the god of the sea. Odysseus does not yet know that he has named the Cyclops' father; he hears it from the other Cyclopes on the next night (9.412). In Euripides' satyr play he knows it from the first: he begs the Cyclops as the “noble son of the sea god”, by the shrines of his father that the Greeks had saved, at Taenarum, Malea and Geraestus (*Cyclops* 286–298; see [[euripides-cyclops]]).

**Pray to your father.** After the blinding the Cyclopes come to the cave at his cry, hear that Nobody is killing him, and go away again: “there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus; / but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon” (9.411–412; see [[nobody]], [[cyclopes]], [[zeus]] and [[prayer]]). They give Zeus the sickness and Poseidon the cure. The Cyclops does not pray until he has heard the name of the man who blinded him.

**The famous shaker of the earth.** When Odysseus, from the ship, has told his name and his father's, the Cyclops remembers the old prophecy of the seer Telemus (9.507–516; see [[telemus]]), and offers: “But come here, Odysseus, so that I may set good cheer before you / and speed your escort, that the famous shaker of the earth may grant it; / for I am his son, and he claims to be my father. / And he himself, if he wishes, will heal me, and no one else, / neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men” (9.517–521; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]], [[pompe]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Merry takes πομπήν τʼ ὀτρύνω together, a sort of Homeric formula, as in Odysseus' own words to the Phaeacians, “But for me, speed my escort” (7.151), and δόμεναι κλυτὸν ἐννοσίγαιον as defining the escort: “and let me make speedy preparation for your return—that the famous Earthshaker may grant you one” (Merry on 9.518; see the translation's note on 9.518). Odysseus answers: “If only I could make you bereft of soul and life / and send you into the house of Hades, / as surely as not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye” (9.523–525; see [[hades]]), that is, “as certainly as Poseidon himself shall never heal thine eye” (Merry on 9.523). The son calls him κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος, “the famous shaker of the earth”, and the enemy ἐνοσίχθων, “the earth-shaker”, two titles that the translation keeps apart (see the translation's key-word row).

**The prayer.**

{{quote:24d8dea1-9845-56cb-b017-fc735ce6ef09}}

The verse that brings in the prayer is the Iliad's verse for Nestor praying to Zeus, “prayed, stretching out his hands to the starry sky” (9.527 = *Iliad* 15.371). χεῖρʼ is the dual, and ἀστερόεντα is a standing epithet, since all this takes place by day (Merry on 9.527; see [[starry]]). The address, κλῦθι, Ποσείδαον γαιήοχε, opens the prayer that Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, made to him at Pylos (3.55), and the *Hymn to Poseidon* takes leave of him with the three vocatives of this verse (*Hymn* 22.6; see the general article, [[hear-me]], [[earth-holder]] and [[dark-haired]]). The Cyclops claims a hearing as a son, “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (9.529; see [[prayer]]), as the priest Chryses claimed one from Apollo by the temple he had roofed and the thighs he had burned (*Iliad* 1.39–41; see [[chryses]]); and he names his enemy as his enemy named himself, “Odysseus, sacker of cities, … / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531 = 9.504–505; see [[sacker-of-cities]], [[laertes]], [[ithaca]] and [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). The second of those verses is wanting in twelve manuscripts and in the text of Eustathius, and nearly all editors since Wolf have bracketed it here (Merry's critical note on 9.531; see the translation's note on 9.531).

The curse asks for what the rest of the poem tells. The words “on someone else's ship” mean the Phaeacian ship that brought Odysseus home (Merry on 9.535). Teiresias and Circe will give him its words as prophecy, ὀψὲ κακῶς νεῖαι, ὀλέσας ἄπο πάντας ἑταίρους, “you will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions” (11.114; 12.141; see the translation's formula row and [[curses]]), and in his house he finds the suitors (see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). “So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536) is the verse that tells a god's hearing of a prayer, of Athena at Pylos and on the way to the Phaeacian city (3.385, 6.328) and in the Iliad of Apollo and of Zeus (*Iliad* 1.43, 16.249); here κυανοχαίτης, “the dark-haired god”, stands alone in the place of the name.

**The anger.** This is the anger that the poem names at its beginning: all the gods pitied Odysseus “except Poseidon; and he raged relentlessly / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (1.20–21), and Zeus gives its cause at the first council, “angry because of the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye” (1.69; see the Book 1 note). It raises the storm that breaks the raft (5.282–381; see the Book 5 note), and it is the reason why Athena did not appear to Odysseus face to face, “for she respected / her father's brother” (6.329–330; see [[athena]]). After the homecoming it falls on the Phaeacians whose ship brought him home (13.125–187).
