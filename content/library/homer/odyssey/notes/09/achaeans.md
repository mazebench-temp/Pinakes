---
summary: The Achaeans are named twice in the tale, both times as the army of Troy: the Cicones of the inland “overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (9.59), and Odysseus tells the Cyclops that he and his men are “Achaeans, driven off course from Troy”, the people of Agamemnon (9.259–263).
---

**At Ismarus.** “But when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen, / then at last the Cicones overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (9.58–59; see [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). The verse of the hour is the Iliad's, where the same afternoon makes “the Achaeans … stronger beyond their portion” in the fight over Cebriones (*Iliad* 16.779–780); here the Achaeans are the losers. κλῖναν, “turned them back”, is the verb with which, in the Iliad, “the Danaans turned the Trojans back” (*Iliad* 5.37).

**Before the Cyclops.** ἡμεῖς τοι Τροίηθεν ἀποπλαγχθέντες Ἀχαιοί, “We, you must know, are Achaeans, driven off course from Troy / by winds of every kind over the great gulf of the sea, / eager to go home, by another way, by other paths / we have come” (9.259–262). ἄλλην ὁδὸν ἄλλα κέλευθα is generally taken to repeat the sense of “driven off course”, “by various ways and various wanderings”, but Merry prefers, with the scholia, the simpler sense “a different route and a different journey” from the homeward direction, ἄλλος being then a sort of euphemism for “unfortunate” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.261; see the translation's note on 9.261). The men are Achaeans of the army at Troy, and the people of Agamemnon (9.263; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).
