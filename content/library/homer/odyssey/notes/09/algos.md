**Where to begin.** The tale opens as a burden. Alcinous has asked the stranger why he wept at the song of Troy (8.577–586), and Odysseus answers:

{{quote:60a4299f-cb3d-5c83-94a7-c62a83316a28}}

The sorrows are given by “the heavenly gods” (see [[lament]] and [[heavenly-gods]]). To tell the κήδεα is to renew them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.13), and the stress lies on πολλά: it is the multitude of the sorrows that makes it hard to know in what order to tell them (Merry on 9.14). The tale he does tell is “my homecoming of many sorrows” (νόστον ἐμὸν πολυκηδέα, 9.37; see [[nostos]]), the phrase he will use of it again to Penelope (23.351).

**The sorrows of the voyage.** At Ismarus an evil fate from Zeus stood beside the men, “so that we might suffer many sorrows” (ἄλγεα, 9.53; see [[aisa]]); in the storm after it they lay two nights and two days on the shore, “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together” (9.74–75; see [[thymos]]). The island of goats is free of the hunters “who in the woods / suffer pains as they range over the peaks of the mountains” (9.120–121): ἄλγεα of bodily hardship, “pains”, as of Philoctetes on his island (*Iliad* 2.721; see the translation's table of key words and [[the-island-of-goats]]).
