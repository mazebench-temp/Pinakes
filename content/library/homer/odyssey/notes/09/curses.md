summary: The Cyclops' prayer to his father Poseidon is the Odyssey's great curse: that Odysseus may never reach home, or, if it is his fate, may come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house; “and the dark-haired god heard him” (9.526–536).

**The curse.** It is made as a prayer, with the god's titles, a claim and a request (9.526–529; see [[prayer]]). The claim is kinship, not service: “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (9.529; see [[poseidon]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]), and the curse falls not on a child, as the Iliad's curses do, but on a stranger, named in full in the very words of his own boast, “Odysseus, sacker of cities, … / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531 = 9.504–505; see [[nobody]] and [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). It was Odysseus' own shout that gave the Cyclops the name to curse. Verse 531 is wanting in twelve manuscripts and in Eustathius' text, and has been bracketed by nearly all editors since Wolf (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, his critical note on 9.531; see the translation's note on 9.531).

**Its fulfillment.** The prayer asks two things, the second in case fate allows the homecoming: if Odysseus must reach home, let it be late, without his companions, “on someone else's ship”, and to trouble in his house (9.532–535; see [[moira]] and [[nostos]]). νηὸς ἐπʼ ἀλλοτρίης is the Phaeacian ship that brings him home (Merry on 9.535). “So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536), and the next offering Odysseus makes, the ram, Zeus refuses to notice, because the prayer had been heard and would be answered (9.551–555; Merry on 9.553; see [[sacrifice]] and [[zeus]]). The anger of Poseidon that drives Odysseus from his native land begins here (1.68–75; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Teiresias will repeat the second half of the curse to Odysseus as a prophecy, in nearly the same words: “late and in evil plight you will come home, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and you will find bane in your house” (11.114–115, a plain rendering; the first verse again in Circe's words, 12.141; see the translation's table of formulas).
