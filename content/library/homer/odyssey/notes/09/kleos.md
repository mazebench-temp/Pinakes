---
summary: Odysseus gives his name with his fame: “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who for tricks of every kind / am in the thoughts of men, and my fame reaches the sky” (9.19–20). To the Cyclops he claims to be of the people of Agamemnon, “whose fame is now the greatest under heaven” (9.263–264); he offers him a false name as “my famous name” (9.364), and from the ship shouts his true one, so that it may be known who blinded him (9.502–505).
refs: [9.364, 9.502-505]
---

**Fame that reaches the sky.** At the beginning of Book 8 the Muse set Demodocus on to sing from a lay “whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.74), the lay of Odysseus' own quarrel with Achilles; now the stranger claims the same height for himself: “my fame reaches the sky” (9.20; see [[oime]] and [[no-one-is-nameless]]). It is fame for tricks, δόλοι (9.19; see [[dolos]]). Virgil gave his hero the same self-introduction: “I am pious Aeneas, … known by fame above the sky” (*Aeneid* 1.378–379; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.19; see [[virgil]]).

**Agamemnon's fame.** To the Cyclops Odysseus names the Achaeans as “the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / whose fame is now the greatest under heaven; / so great a city did he sack” (9.263–265; see [[agamemnon]] and [[under-heaven]]). μέγιστον is the predicate: “his fame, far as the heaven covers, is the greatest” (Merry on 9.264). The fame was won at Troy (see [[polis]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]), and it moves the Cyclops no more than the god of guests (9.273–278; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]).

**The famous name.** Over the wine Odysseus offers “my famous name” (ὄνομα κλυτόν, 9.364; see [[famous]]), and gives as his name the word for no one (9.366; see [[nobody]]). From the ship he gives the other: “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities, blinded you, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.504–505). Aristotle praised the verse as the voice of anger, “as though he would not have been avenged unless the other perceived both by whom and for what” (*Rhetoric* 1380b22–24; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). With the name the Cyclops can curse him, and his prayer to Poseidon repeats it word for word (9.530–531; see [[curses]]).
