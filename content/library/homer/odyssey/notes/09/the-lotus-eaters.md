summary: The second adventure of the tale. Zeus' storm tears the sails and keeps the ships two days on shore; rounding Malea, when he was all but home, Odysseus is driven past Cythera (9.67–81); nine days of deadly winds bring him to the Lotus-eaters, whose lotus makes the scouts forget their homecoming, and he drags them weeping to the ships (9.82–104).

**The storm.** Zeus “roused the North Wind / with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (9.67–69; see [[zeus]], [[boreas]] and [[cloud-gatherer]]): the last verse and a half are the verses of Poseidon's storm on the raft (5.293–294), and 9.68–69 return, after a verse that ends like 9.67, when Zeus raises the storm at Thrinacia (12.313–315). ἐπικάρσιαι, “slantwise” (9.70): Eustathius took it as “head downwards”, of ships plunging their bows as they run before the wind; Nitzsch preferred “slantwise”, after Apollonius' lexicon, and Merry regards the ships as drifting on with the wind abeam (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.70; see the translation's note on 9.70). The sails are torn τριχθά τε καὶ τετραχθά, “in three and four pieces” (9.71), the words of Menelaus' sword shattered on the helmet of Paris (*Iliad* 3.363). προερέσσαμεν, “rowed”, is according to Eustathius Aristarchus' reading for the common προερύσσαμεν (Merry's critical note on 9.73). The men lie on the shore θυμὸν ἔδοντες, “eating our hearts out” (9.75), for which Merry compares Cicero's *ipse suum cor edens* (on 9.75), Cicero's Latin for the Iliad's Bellerophon, “eating his heart out” (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.63; *Iliad* 6.202). The third day comes in the verse that ended Odysseus' two nights and two days in the sea after Poseidon's storm (9.74–76, after 5.388–390).

**Malea.** “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera” (9.79–81; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[malea]] and [[cythera]]). ἀσκηθής, “unharmed”, Merry compares with “unscathed” (on 9.79). The wind caught Odysseus as he was rounding the cape, Merry explains, and drifted him past Cythera, when he had meant to pass between the cape and the island; he cites the proverb “After doubling Malea forget your home”, which Strabo quotes to show the dangers of the sea off the cape, and sets beside the passage the story of Jason, whom a north wind caught off Malea and carried away to Libya (on 9.80). Strabo gives the proverb as Μαλέας δὲ κάμψας ἐπιλάθου τῶν οἴκαδε, on account of the contrary winds in the sea beyond Malea (*Geography* 8.6.20), and Herodotus tells that Jason, sailing round the Peloponnese for Delphi, was caught off Malea by a north wind and carried to Libya (Herodotus 4.179.2). The verb of the drift, παρέπλαγξεν, “drove me off course”, is the verb of the false tale to Penelope, in which the wind drove Odysseus off course from Malea to Crete (19.186–187, Greek only).

**Nine days.** “From there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds / over the fish-filled sea; but on the tenth we set foot / on the land of the Lotus-eaters” (9.82–84; see [[fish-filled]] and [[lotus-eaters]]). The half-verse ἔνθεν δʼ ἐννῆμαρ φερόμην is the beginning of the last drift of the tale, which brings Odysseus to Ogygia on the tenth night (12.447–448; compare 7.253; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). ἀτάρ, at 9.83, is the reading of Bekker and most modern editors and of several manuscripts, for the common αὐτάρ (Merry's critical note on 9.83).

**The scouts.** The landing and the meal, and then the sending of two men with a herald “to go and find out / what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (9.85–90; see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[heralds]]), come again in the same verses in the tenth book (10.56–58, 10.100–102). The phrase σῖτον ἔδοντες, “who eat bread”, is used merely to mark them as men, as it marks men against gods in the Iliad (Merry on 9.89; see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]). Ameis rejected 9.90, which in the oldest and best manuscripts and in Eustathius stands before 9.89: he took it for an interpolation from 10.102, irreconcilable with 9.94–95, which imply that more than three men went, and foreign to Homer's way of telling such a reconnaissance as a formal embassy (Merry's critical note on 9.89–90; see the translation's note on 9.89–90).

**The lotus.** The Lotus-eaters “gave them some of the lotus to taste” (9.93), λωτοῖο, the plant whose fruit is μελιηδέα καρπόν, “the honey-sweet fruit” (9.94; see [[lotus]] and [[honey-sweet]]): Merry warns that it is not to be confused with the grass of that name, the lotus on which the Achaeans' horses feed (*Iliad* 2.776), and sets beside the honey-sweetness Herodotus' description of the fruit, as sweet as the date (on 9.94; Herodotus 4.177.1). βούλοντο … μενέμεν is “would rather remain” (Merry on 9.96). λωτὸν ἐρεπτόμενοι, “munching the lotus” (9.97), is the phrase of the Achaeans' horses idle beside the chariots, “munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley” (*Iliad* 2.776): ἐρέπτομαι is said elsewhere of beasts feeding, and here of men, as it were browsing (Merry on 9.97). In μενέμεν νόστου τε λαθέσθαι the present and the aorist differ: λαθέσθαι implies the instant abandonment of all thought of return, μενέμεν the continuous stay in the Lotus-eaters' land (Merry on 9.97; see [[nostos]] and the translation's note on 9.96–99).

**The rescue.** “These I brought back to the ships by force, weeping, / and in the hollow ships I dragged them under the thwarts and bound them” (9.98–99): νηυσὶ … ἔνι answers ἐπὶ νῆας, “I took them to the ships, and when I had got them there” (Merry on 9.98–99). The space ὑπὸ ζυγά, “under the thwarts”, was a safe place (Merry on 9.99): there Alcinous himself stows the Phaeacians' gifts to Odysseus (13.20–21, Greek only; see [[rowing-benches]]). μή πως, “for fear that”, must be read at 9.102 for μή πω, which Homer uses only with an imperative (Merry on 9.102 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 9.102). The adventure ends with the boarding verses (9.103–104; see [[sailing-type-scene]]) and the first verse of the departure from Ismarus, “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart” (9.105 = 9.62; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]).
