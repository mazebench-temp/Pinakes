# "ἀνεσχέθομεν Διὶ χεῖρας" (9.294), the gesture without words. εὔχομαι "claim" and εὖχος "triumph" (9.263, 9.317, 9.519,

**Hands lifted to Zeus.** When the Cyclops dashes two companions to the ground and eats them, the others can only weep and pray: “And we, weeping, lifted up our hands to Zeus, / seeing the cruel deeds, and helplessness held our hearts” (9.294–295; see [[zeus]] and [[tears]]). The gesture is the prayer's, without its words.

**The Cyclops' prayer.** The other Cyclopes, thinking him mad, tell Polyphemus to pray to his father (9.412; see [[nousos]]); he does so only when he has learned Odysseus' name (9.502–525; see [[nobody]]):

{{quote:24d8dea1-9845-56cb-b017-fc735ce6ef09}}

The prayer has the three parts of the form (see the entry). He stretches out his hands to the starry sky, in the verse of Nestor's prayer at the ships (9.527 = *Iliad* 15.371): ἀστερόεντα is a standing epithet, since it is day (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.527; see [[starry]]). The invocation names the god with his titles, “Hear me, Poseidon, dark-haired earth-holder” (9.528; see [[hear-me]] and [[earth-holder]]). The claim is not a gift given but a kinship: “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (9.529; see [[euchomai]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). And the request has two degrees, the second for the case that fate does not allow the first (9.530–535). Odysseus reaches home in the end as the second request allows: late, without his companions, on the Phaeacians' ship, to find the suitors in his house (see [[curses]] and [[moira]]). The last verse, “So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536), is the verse with which Athena hears the prayers of Nestor and of Odysseus, “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (3.385, 6.328; see the translation's table of formulas).

**The other offering.** Odysseus' own appeal to a god in the book is a sacrifice: on the island he burns the thigh-pieces of the ram to Zeus, “but he took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish” (9.551–555; see [[sacrifice]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Zeus refused the offering, Merry says, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (on 9.553).
