---
summary: Cicero misremembers the Cyclops' words to his ram, calls Verres a second Cyclops, turns the first words of the Cyclops' complaint into a byword of Roman gossip, applies Odysseus' praise of rugged Ithaca to his own Arpinum, and counts Ulysses' love of Ithaca above immortality. He also notes that Homer called Ulysses, not Ajax or Achilles, the sacker of cities.
---

- **The ram.** Homer made Polyphemus talk with his ram and praise its fortune, that it could go where it wished and touch what it wished, “and rightly; for the Cyclops himself was no wiser than that ram” (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.115, a plain rendering; 9.447–460); Merry thought that Cicero had forgotten the scene or confused it with some later version (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.447): [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]].
- **A second Cyclops.** Verres in Sicily was “a second Cyclops, far more ruthless”, for he besieged the whole island, while the other held only Etna and that part of Sicily (*Against Verres* 2.5.146, a plain rendering): [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]] and [[cyclopes]].
- **Some great man.** The talk that would have greeted his brother's return with Statius was used up beforehand, and many remarks had been let fly of the kind “ἀλλὰ αἰεί τινα φῶτα μέγαν”, “but always some great man”, the first words of the Cyclops' complaint (*Letters to Quintus* 1.2.1; 9.513): [[small-and-of-no-account]].
- **Arpinum and Ithaca.** Writing to Atticus from Formiae, Cicero asks why he should invite him to Arpinum, and answers with Odysseus' words on Ithaca, “rugged, but a good nurse of young men; and I, for my part, / can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land”, quoted in Greek (*Letters to Atticus* 2.11.1; 9.27–28). In *On the Orator* love of country is so strong that the wisest of men preferred his Ithaca, “fixed like a little nest on the roughest of rocks”, to immortality (*On the Orator* 1.196, a plain rendering), and at Arpinum, his birthplace, Cicero says that the place may delight him the more, “if indeed that wisest of men is written to have refused immortality so that he might see Ithaca” (*On the Laws* 2.3, a plain rendering; 9.29–36): [[ithaca]], [[rugged]], [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]].
- **Sacker of cities.** He who crushes Antony will end the war, Cicero writes, and so Homer called not Ajax nor Achilles but Ulysses πτολίπορθος, “sacker of cities” (*Letters to Friends* 10.13.2, a plain rendering), the title that Odysseus, in its longer form πτολιπόρθιος, gives himself in his boast to the Cyclops and that the Cyclops repeats in his prayer (9.504, 9.530); the Iliad gives it to Achilles as well (*Iliad* 8.372, 21.550): [[sacker-of-cities]].
- **Eating one's heart.** For θυμὸν ἔδοντες, “eating our hearts out” (9.75), Merry cites Cicero's rendering of the verse on Bellerophon, *ipse suum cor edens*, “himself eating his own heart” (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.63; *Iliad* 6.202; Merry on 9.75, who gives the chapter): [[thymos]].
