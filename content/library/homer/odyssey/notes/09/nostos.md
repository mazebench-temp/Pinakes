**The tale of a homecoming.** The story that Alcinous asked for begins with its subject: “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). It nearly ended in a few days: after the Cicones and the storm, “now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back” (9.79–81; see [[dear-native-land]], [[malea]] and [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Forgetting.** The lotus takes away the wish for home. Whoever ate it “no longer wished to bring back word or to return, / but they wished to remain there among the Lotus-eaters, / munching the lotus, and to forget their homecoming” (9.95–97), and Odysseus hurries the rest on board “for fear that one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming” (9.102; see [[lotus]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). μενέμεν, the present, is the lasting stay, λαθέσθαι, the aorist, the instant loss of all thought of return (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.97); βούλοντο is “would rather” (Merry on 9.96; see [[boule]]).

**The homecoming in the cave.** To the Cyclops Odysseus presents his men as travelers “eager to go home, by another way, by other paths” (οἴκαδε ἱέμενοι, 9.261): “a different route and a different journey” from the homeward one, with ἄλλος as a kind of euphemism for an unlucky way (Merry on 9.261). He offers the wine “in the hope that you would pity me / and send me home” (9.349–350; see [[pity]] and [[pompe]]).

**The curse on the homecoming.** Blinded and named, the Cyclops prays to Poseidon against the very thing: “grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may not reach home” (οἴκαδʼ ἱκέσθαι, 9.530), or, if it is his fate to reach “his well-built house and his native land, / let him come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house” (9.532–535; see [[curses]] and [[poseidon]]). The second half of the prayer is the shape of the rest of the poem: Teiresias and Circe will foretell the late and evil homecoming in the same words (11.114–115, 12.141; see the translation's table of formulas), and the “someone else's ship” is the Phaeacians' (Merry on 9.535).
