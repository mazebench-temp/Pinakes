summary: The men who ate the lotus are dragged back to the ships “weeping” (9.98); the companions, watching the Cyclops eat two of them, lift up their hands to Zeus “weeping” (9.294); and at the ship Odysseus forbids those who waited to weep for the dead (9.468–470).
# κλαίοντας (9.98; again 10.49), κλαίοντες (9.294; again 10.209, 10.241).
grc: [κλαίοντας, κλαίοντες]
en: [weeping]
---

The book's tears are men's. The scouts who ate the lotus weep when they are taken from it: “These I brought back to the ships by force, weeping” (9.98; see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[nostos]]). In the cave the companions can only weep and pray as the Cyclops eats two of them: “And we, weeping, lifted up our hands to Zeus, / seeing the cruel deeds” (9.294–295; see [[prayer]]). At the ship the men who waited begin to lament the six who were eaten, but there is no time for it: “But I would not let them—I signed no with my brows to each— / weep, but bade them quickly throw the many fine-fleeced sheep / on board the ship” (9.468–470): κλαίειν depends on οὐκ εἴων, the words between being parenthetical (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.469; see [[neuo]] and [[lament]]). The tale is told by a man who has wept at the singer's songs the evening before (8.83–92, 8.521–534; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).
