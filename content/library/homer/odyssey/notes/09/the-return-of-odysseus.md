summary: In his tale Odysseus tells how the homecoming began and how it was cursed. The homecoming “of many sorrows” that Zeus sent upon him as he came from Troy (9.37–38) is within reach at Malea and lost there (9.79–81); the blinded Cyclops prays that Odysseus may not reach home, or, if that is his fate, may come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house (9.528–535); and Zeus, refusing the ram, ponders how all the ships and the companions may perish (9.553–555).

**The homecoming of many sorrows.** “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). ἀπὸ Τροίηθεν, “from Troy”, puts the preposition before a word that already means “from”, as ἀπʼ οὐρανόθεν, “from the sky” (11.18; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.38).

**All but home.** At Malea, after the storm, he was almost there: “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back” (9.79–81; see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). He comes as near once more in the tenth book, when the ships sail nine days and nights from Aeolus' island and on the tenth the native land comes into sight, so near that they see men tending fires (10.28–30, Greek only).

**The curse.** The Cyclops prays to his father that Odysseus may not reach home at all (9.528–531), and then grants the god what is fated: “But if it is his fate to see his friends and reach / his well-built house and his native land, / let him come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house” (9.532–535; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[poseidon]] and [[curses]]). The condition is made of the words of Zeus' decree, “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). The ship of another is the Phaeacian ship that brings Odysseus home (Merry on 9.535; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). Teiresias repeats the curse as a prophecy, in its own verses turned to the second person, and names the bane: overbearing men who devour his livelihood and woo his wife (11.112–117, Greek only; see [[the-suitors]]); Circe repeats its first verse (12.141, Greek only), and Athena, when he is home on Ithaca, says that she always knew he would come home “having lost all his companions” (13.339–340, a plain rendering). In the assembly of the second book Halitherses recalls his own prophecy, made when the Argives were embarking for Ilios, in the same words: “after suffering many evils, and losing all his companions” (2.174; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]).

**The ships and the companions.** “And the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536). On the island Odysseus sacrifices the Cyclops' ram to Zeus, “but he took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.553–555; see [[zeus]]): he refused the offering, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (on 9.553). The tale goes on to tell it. Eleven ships are destroyed with their crews in the harbor of the Laestrygonians, and only Odysseus' own escapes (10.121–132, Greek only); its crew perishes when Zeus strikes it after Thrinacia (12.403–419, Greek only; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The book's own losses are the first of them, six men from every ship at Ismarus and six in the Cyclops' cave (9.60–61, 9.289–344; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]).
