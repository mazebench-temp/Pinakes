---
summary: The sky is the measure of the fame Odysseus claims when he names himself, “my fame reaches the sky” (9.20). From it night falls in Zeus' storm, and no moon shines on the night of the landing (9.69, 9.145); toward it the Cyclops stretches his hands to pray to his father Poseidon (9.527).
---

**Fame that reaches the sky.** Odysseus names himself at last: “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who for tricks of every kind / am in the thoughts of men, and my fame reaches the sky” (9.19–20; see [[odysseus]], [[kleos]] and [[dolos]]). The words had been said the evening before of the lay from which Demodocus sang of Odysseus' quarrel with Achilles, whose fame “had at that time reached the wide sky” (8.73–74; see [[wide-sky]] and the Book 8 note), and in the Iliad of Nestor's shield, “whose fame now reaches the sky” (*Iliad* 8.192): now the hero of that lay claims it for himself. Merry compares Theognis' promise that his friend will be in the thoughts of men forever, and Virgil's imitation of the self-naming, “Sum pius Aeneas … fama super aethera notus”, “I am dutiful Aeneas, … known by fame above the sky” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.19; *Aeneid* 1.378–379; see [[virgil]]). Later in the book Odysseus gives the Cyclops another fame of the same kind, Agamemnon's, “the greatest under heaven” (9.264; see [[under-heaven]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**Night from the sky.** Zeus sends the North Wind against the ships, “and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (9.68–69; see [[zeus]], [[boreas]] and [[earth]]), the verses of Poseidon's storm against the raft (5.293–294). On the night when the ships reach the island of goats “the moon / did not shine from the sky, but was held fast in clouds” (9.144–145; see [[moon]] and [[the-island-of-goats]]).

**The starry sky.** The Cyclops prays to Poseidon “stretching out his hands to the starry sky” (9.527; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[poseidon]]), the verse with which Nestor prays to Zeus when the Trojans are at the ships (*Iliad* 15.371): the son of the sea god prays with his hands raised to the sky. ἀστερόεντα, “starry”, is a good instance of a standing epithet, Merry notes, since all this takes place by day (on 9.527; see [[starry]]).
