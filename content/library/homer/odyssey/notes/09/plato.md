---
summary: Plato's Athenian takes the Cyclopes' households for the first constitution after the flood, and Socrates finds Odysseus' praise of the feast unfit for the young and begins the myth of Er as no tale to Alcinous but the tale of a valiant man. One of Merry's grammatical parallels is Thrasymachus' taunt about Socrates' nurse.
---

- **The Cyclopes in the Laws.** The constitution of the first men after the flood was a lordship, “and Homer says somewhere that it existed in the dwelling of the Cyclopes”; the Athenian quotes the four verses on their way of life and takes the poet for a witness to the rule of the eldest in scattered households (*Laws* 680b–e, 682a, plain renderings; 9.112–115): [[plato-the-cyclopes]], [[cyclopes]] and [[themis]].
- **The finest thing.** Is it fit for a young man to hear, for his self-mastery, the wisest of men saying that it seems to him the finest thing of all when the tables are laden with bread and meat and the wine-pourer fills the cups? (*Republic* 389d–390b; 9.8–10): [[the-praise-of-the-feast-in-later-writers]] and [[plato-zeus-deceived]].
- **No tale to Alcinous.** Socrates introduces the myth of Er: “But it is not a tale to Alcinous that I will tell you, but the tale of a valiant man”, Er the son of Armenius (*Republic* 10.614b, a plain rendering), the name of Odysseus' story in Books 9 to 12 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1): [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]].
- **“As far as she is concerned.”** For μοι in 9.42, “so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share”, μοι in the sense of the later “as far as I could help it”, Merry cites Thrasymachus' question whether Socrates has a nurse, since she lets him go about with a running nose and does not wipe it, “you, who thanks to her (αὐτῇ) do not know sheep from shepherd” (*Republic* 343a, a plain rendering; Merry on 9.42; see the translation's note on 9.42): [[odysseus]].
