**The name and the guest-gift.** When the Cyclops has drunk the first bowl of Maron's wine, he asks for more, “and tell me your name / right now, so that I may give you a guest-gift, in which you may rejoice” (9.355–356; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[xenia]]). Odysseus answers after the third bowl, with soothing words: “Cyclops, you ask me my famous name, and I will tell it / to you; and you give me a guest-gift, as you promised” (9.363–365; see [[soothing-words]]).

The false name is given in the words of a true one. Alcinous asked for the name “by which they called you there, your mother and your father, / and the others” (8.550–551; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]), and the answer in the tale is that his mother and his father and all the others call him Nobody (see [[meter]] and [[pater]]). The Cyclops' gift is the last place at his meals: πύματον … μετὰ οἷς ἑτάροισιν is not “last, after his companions”, but “last, in the list of his companions” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.369). The accusative Οὖτιν, where the pronoun has οὔτινα, marks the word as a name (Merry on 9.366; see the entry).

**“Nobody is killing me.”** Blinded, the Cyclops shouts to his neighbors, who come to the cave and ask, in the pronoun from which the name is made, whether “no one among mortals” is driving off his flocks, or “no one” is killing him by trickery or by force (ἦ μή τις, 9.403–406). His answer is true and is heard as false: “Friends, Nobody is killing me by trickery, and not by force” (9.408). The ambiguity of Οὖτις carries over into οὐδέ: the Cyclopes understand that no one is killing him, by craft or by violence, while he meant that he was being slain “by craft, and not by violence”, which Merry renders “It is no man's craft, no violence that is murdering me” (on 9.408; see [[dolos]] and [[cunning-not-strength]]). They answer, “If then nobody is doing you violence, alone as you are, / there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus” (9.410–411): alone, with no neighbors to help him, and, as they suppose, gone mad (Merry on 9.410, 9.411; see [[nousos]]). They go, and Odysseus' heart laughs “how my name and my flawless plan had deceived him” (9.413–414): Merry finds it difficult not to suppose a pun on μή τις and μῆτις, “for the Cyclops has been over-reached by the μῆτις of the οὔτις or Οὖτις” (on 9.408; see [[metis]], [[apate]] and [[blameless]]).

**“Nobody, a nobody.”** At dawn the Cyclops speaks to the ram that carries Odysseus out of the cave: his master's eye was blinded by “a bad man … / with his wretched companions, … / Nobody, who I say has not yet escaped destruction” (9.453–455), and if the ram could speak, his heart “would find relief / from the evils that Nobody, a nobody, gave me” (9.459–460). οὐτιδανός, “good for nothing”, is set beside the name it seems to explain; Merry renders it “the trouble which good-for-nothing No-man gave me”, and finds “a grim sort of reference” in πόρεν, “gave”, the verb used when Odysseus handed him the wine (Merry on 9.456; 9.360).

**The name thrown off.** Out of range, Odysseus will not leave the Cyclops with a false name: “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities, blinded you, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.504–505; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). The Cyclops recognizes the name of an old prophecy, that he would lose his sight “at the hands of Odysseus”; he had looked for “some tall and handsome man”, “but now one who is small and a nobody and feeble / has blinded my eye” (9.507–516; see [[telemus]] and [[small-and-of-no-account]]). With the true name he can curse the man, and he prays to Poseidon in Odysseus' own words, “grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may not reach home, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531, as 9.504–505; see [[curses]] and the translation's note on 9.531).
