summary: The book's refrain is escape from death: “the rest of us escaped death and fate” (9.61), “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.63, 9.566), “we who had escaped death” (9.467). In the cave Odysseus plans “some release from death” (9.421); the Cyclops boasts that no god or mortal can heal him (9.520–521).
# θάνον (9.66) and the phrase "θανάτου λύσιν" (9.421).
grc: [θάνον, "θανάτου λύσιν"]
en: [died, release from death]
---

**Escaping death.** Each episode ends with the same reckoning. At Ismarus six men of each ship perished, “but the rest of us escaped death and fate” (9.60–61), and the ships sail on “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.63), the verse that closes the book (9.566; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]] and [[hetairos]]). ἄσμενοι ἐκ θανάτοιο is a shortened form of the Iliad's “he who even now was glad to escape from death” (φύγεν ἄσμενος ἐκ θανάτοιο, *Iliad* 20.350; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.63). The dead of Ismarus “had died on the plain” (θάνον, 9.66) and are called three times before the ships sail (see [[the-call-to-the-dead]]). Back from the cave, “we were a welcome sight to our dear companions, / we who had escaped death; but for the others they groaned and lamented” (9.466–467; see [[lament]]).

**Release from death.** In the cave the danger is death for all: had Odysseus stabbed the Cyclops as he lay among his flocks, “there we too would have perished by sheer destruction”, since no one could move the stone from the door (9.303–305; Merry on 9.303, “we as well as he”, assassins and victim together). Blinded, the Cyclops sits in the doorway, and Odysseus plans “if I might find some release from death for my companions and for myself” (9.421; see [[metis]]).

**Mortal men.** The other words of the family mark the line between gods and men. Odysseus bids the Cyclops, if any “mortal man” asks who blinded him, to give the name of Odysseus (9.502–505), and the Cyclops answers that his father will heal him, “and no one else, / neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men” (9.520–521; see [[poseidon]] and [[mortal-men]]).
