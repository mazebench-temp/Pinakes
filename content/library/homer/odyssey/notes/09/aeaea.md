---
summary: Aeaea is named first as Circe's epithet, when Odysseus, before he begins his tale, sets the two goddesses who kept him side by side: Calypso in her hollow caves, and Circe in her halls, “the wily one of Aeaea” (9.29–33).
---

**Circe of Aeaea.** Having named himself and his island, Odysseus names the two goddesses who kept him from it, in matching verses that end alike: “Truly Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, held me back there / in her hollow caves, longing for me to be her husband; / and in the same way Circe kept me in her halls, / the wily one of Aeaea, longing for me to be her husband; / but they never persuaded the heart in my breast” (9.29–33; see [[calypso]], [[circe]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). The order runs back in time: Calypso is the last of his hosts before the Phaeacians, and Circe's island will come in the tale's next book. Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, “the wily one of Aeaea”, gives Circe the epithet he had given Calypso when he began his story to Arete (7.245; see [[wily]]), and the name of her island as an adjective: the island where Circe lived, Merry notes, is also called Αἰαίη in Book 10 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.32; 10.135). ὣς δʼ αὔτως, “in the same way”, is Homer's regular order for the later ὡσαύτως δέ (Merry on 9.31).

**A suspected passage.** Verse 30 is missing from most manuscripts and Eustathius does not quote it, and Merry thinks it interpolated from 1.15; verses 34–36 are marked with an obelus in the Ambrosian manuscript Q and one or two others, and Merry quotes Köchly's report that Sengebusch held it certain that the ancient athetesis ran from verse 29 to verse 36, so that “one's own land” in verse 28 was followed directly by “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming” in 37 (Merry's critical note on 9.30; see the translation's note on 9.29–36). On that view Calypso and Circe, and Aeaea with them, are not named at all in the preface. The text prints the verses, and the translation keeps them.
