---
summary: The gods are everywhere in the tale and seldom named: “some god guided us” to the island of goats (9.142), “the god gave us game” (9.158), a god may have bidden the Cyclops bring his rams into the cave (9.339). The Cyclopes live “trusting in the immortal gods” (9.107) and boast that they care nothing for them (9.274–276); Odysseus' verdict is that “Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.479).
---

**Unnamed gods.** Odysseus' tale, like his men's experience, often knows only that a god was at work. The fleet comes to the island of goats in a dark night, “and some god guided us” (καί τις θεὸς ἡγεμόνευεν, 9.142): there was not light enough to see, and so they needed the gods' guidance (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.143; see [[divine-intervention]] and [[the-island-of-goats]]). The hunt goes well, “and at once the god gave us game to satisfy the heart” (9.158); the nymphs had roused the goats (9.154; see [[nymphs]]). On the second evening the Cyclops drives all his flocks into the cave, “either because he suspected something, or because a god so bade him” (9.339; see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]]), and at the stake “a god breathed great daring into us” (9.381; see [[daimon]]).

**The gods scorned.** The Cyclopes trust the gods only as they trust the soil: they live “trusting in the immortal gods” and neither plant nor plow (9.107), a careless confidence rather than devotion (Merry on 9.107; see [[peitho]]). Asked to respect the gods, Polyphemus answers: “you who bid me either fear or avoid the gods; / for the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus / nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger” (9.274–276; see [[xenia]] and [[blessed-gods]]). Odysseus' verdict from the ship is that “Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.479; see [[poine]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]), and the Cyclops answers that his father will heal him, “and no one else, / neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men” (9.520–521).
