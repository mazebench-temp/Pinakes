summary: Odysseus waits in the cave to see the Cyclops “and whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.229), and asks the Cyclops for “a guest-gift, or otherwise / … some present, as is the custom with strangers” (9.267–268). The Cyclops promises one for the stranger's name and gives it: “Nobody I will eat last among his companions … that shall be your guest-gift” (9.355–370). Blinded, he calls Odysseus back to set good cheer before him (9.517).

**Waiting for gifts.** The companions want to take some of the cheeses and drive off the lambs and kids; Odysseus will not go, “so that I might see him himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.228–229). He has brought a goatskin of wine that was itself a gift, Maron's, given in thanks for the protection of his household at Ismarus (9.196–211; see [[maron]] and [[doron]]), because his spirit foreboded a savage who knew neither judgments nor ordinances (9.213–215); and he has come, he will say, “to see if you will give us a guest-gift, or otherwise / give us some present, as is the custom with strangers” (9.267–268). ξεινήιον πορεῖν is the most general expression for hospitality, and δωτίνη, “some present”, a more particular gift (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.267; see [[xenia]] and [[themis]]). “It would have been far better” to go, he says in the telling (9.228; see [[kerdos]]).

**The gift of Nobody.** The Cyclops' first answer is two of the companions (9.288–293). The wine brings out a host's words: “Give me more, and gladly, and tell me your name / right now, so that I may give you a guest-gift, in which you may rejoice” (9.355–356). Odysseus holds him to it, “and you give me a guest-gift, as you promised” (9.365), and the gift is named in the formula of hospitality turned to its opposite:

The Phaeacians, to whom the tale is told, have given the stranger a guest-gift “as is fitting” (8.389; see the Book 8 note); the Cyclops' is the last place at his meals (see [[nobody]]).

**Good cheer.** From the shore the blinded Cyclops calls Odysseus back, “so that I may set good cheer before you / and speed your escort” (9.517–518). The translation renders ξείνια “guest-gifts” when they are given (9.229) and “good cheer” when they are set before a guest, as Calypso sets them before Hermes (5.91) and Diocles at Pherae before Telemachus and Peisistratus (3.490) (see the translation's table of key words and [[pompe]]). Odysseus answers with a wish to send him to the house of Hades, and the Cyclops prays to Poseidon that he may never reach home (9.523–535; see [[curses]]).
