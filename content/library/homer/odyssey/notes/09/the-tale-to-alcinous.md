---
heading: The first book of the tale
summary: The tale begins. Odysseus answers Alcinous with praise of the feast and the singer, asks where to begin among the many sorrows the gods have given him, gives his name and his island, and begins his homecoming from Troy (9.1–38). The book then tells the first three adventures in the order that he will keep for Penelope, the Cicones, the Lotus-eaters and the Cyclops (9.39–566; 23.310–313). Merry heads it Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, Κυκλώπεια.

**The evening.** The time of the book is the evening of the second day after Odysseus came to Scheria; his first words refer back to what Alcinous said when he stopped the singer (8.536–586), and with 9.16 he answers the king's request, εἴπʼ ὄνομʼ, “Tell me the name” (8.550; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Merry's heading for the book is Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, Κυκλώπεια, and he reports the name of the tale from Plato and Aristotle (on 9.1; see the general article, the translation's note on 9.1 and [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]]). The tale runs on into the night: in the eleventh book Odysseus offers to break off, since it is time to sleep, and Alcinous answers that the night is long and untold, and that he could stay up until dawn to hear his sorrows (11.330–332, 11.373–376, Greek only).

**Where to begin.** “But your heart is set on my groaning sorrows, / to ask about them, so that I may groan and mourn still more. / What then shall I recount to you first, and what last? / For the heavenly gods have given me many sorrows” (9.12–15). ἔπειτα introduces a new stage, “What shall I tell thee then first?”, and the stress of the next verse falls on πολλά: it is the multitude of his sorrows that makes it hard for him to know in what order to tell them (Merry on 9.14; see the translation's note on 9.14). The second verse is the verse with which he began his answer to Arete, when he said that it was hard to tell his sorrows from beginning to end, and told her only the last of them (9.15 = 7.242; 7.241–297; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). Here he tells them in order. He begins with his name, “so that you too / may know it” (9.16–20), and his island (9.21–28; see [[ithaca]]); he names the two goddesses who held him back, Calypso and Circe, the last of his hosts and the one before her (9.29–33; see [[calypso]] and [[circe]]); and then he turns to the homecoming itself, “my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**The order of the tale.** The book tells three adventures: the raid on Ismarus (9.39–66), the storm off Malea and the Lotus-eaters (9.67–104), and the Cyclops (9.105–566; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]], [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). They stand in the same order at the head of the summary that Odysseus gives Penelope: the Cicones, “the rich plowland of the Lotus-eater men”, and “all that the Cyclops did” (23.310–313, a plain rendering). The first adventure and the storm that begins the second take place on coasts the Greeks knew, Thrace and the cape of Malea, where he “would have reached my native land unharmed” (9.79); then nine days of deadly winds carry him out of the known world (9.82). That half-verse, ἔνθεν δʼ ἐννῆμαρ φερόμην, “From there for nine days I was borne”, comes back at the end of the tale for the drift to Ogygia (12.447, Greek only; compare 7.253). Of the lands that follow Merry writes that “throughout these books we are in a wonder-land, which we shall look in vain for on the map” (on 9.106).

**The companions lost.** The book ends two of its adventures with the same two verses, “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.62–63 = 9.565–566; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]] and [[hetairos]]). The losses are told as they come: six men from every ship at Ismarus (9.60–61) and six in the Cyclops' cave (9.289–293, 9.311, 9.344). The book's last adventure also brings the curse under which the rest of the tale is told, that Odysseus should come home late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions (9.534; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**The teller.** The book after its first verse is one speech, and within it the Cyclops, the companions and the other Cyclopes speak in speeches of their own (see [[odysseus]]). Odysseus tells the adventures knowing their end: he reports his spirit's foreboding of a savage man (9.213–215), judges his refusal to leave the cave, “it would have been far better” (9.228), and tells even the thoughts of Zeus, who “took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish” (9.553–554). For one piece of divine knowledge, the anger of the Sun, he names his source in the twelfth book: he heard it from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes (12.389–390, Greek only; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
