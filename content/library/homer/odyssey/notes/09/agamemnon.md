summary: Odysseus names Agamemnon once, to the Cyclops: he and his men “claim to be the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / whose fame is now the greatest under heaven; / so great a city did he sack, and destroyed many / people” (9.263–266). # Named at 9.263 (Ἀτρεΐδεω Ἀγαμέμνονος); unnamed: his fame and the city he sacked (9.264-266).
refs: [9.264-266]
---

**The people of Agamemnon.** λαοὶ δʼ Ἀτρεΐδεω Ἀγαμέμνονος εὐχόμεθʼ εἶναι, “And we claim to be the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus” (9.263; see [[son-of-atreus]], [[laos]] and [[euchomai]]): the men who have just called themselves “Achaeans, driven off course from Troy” (9.259; see [[achaeans]]) give the Cyclops the name of their commander, not their own. The claim rests on his fame, τοῦ δὴ νῦν γε μέγιστον ὑπουράνιον κλέος ἐστί, in which μέγιστον is the predicate, “his fame, far as the heaven covers, is the greatest” (9.264; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.264; see [[kleos]] and [[under-heaven]]), and the fame rests on the sack: “so great a city did he sack, and destroyed many / people” (9.265–266; see [[the-fall-of-troy]]). ἡμεῖς δʼ αὖτε, “But we in our turn”, stands in antithesis to Agamemnon (Merry on 9.266): the men of the most famous of kings have come as suppliants to the Cyclops' knees (9.266–271; see [[supplication]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

**A name instead of his own.** Odysseus has just told the Phaeacians that his own fame “reaches the sky” (9.20); to the Cyclops he gives no name but Agamemnon's, and when he is asked his own he answers “Nobody” (9.364–367; see [[nobody]]). The appeal fails. The Cyclops calls him a fool, or a man come from far away, and answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus “nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger” (9.273–276). The poem has already told how the king's own homecoming ended, in his murder at the feast of Aegisthus (1.35–43, 3.254–312, 4.512–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), and in the eleventh book Odysseus will meet his shade among the dead (11.387–466, Greek only).
