---
summary: On the night the ships come to the island of goats there is no moon: “for a thick mist was around the ships, and the moon / did not shine from the sky, but was held fast in clouds” (9.144–145), and “some god guided us” to land (9.142).
---

“There we sailed in, and some god guided us / through the dark night, and nothing could be seen; / for a thick mist was around the ships, and the moon / did not shine from the sky, but was held fast in clouds” (9.142–145; see [[the-island-of-goats]] and [[dark]]). ὀρφναίην, “dark”, is not a standing epithet of night but belongs to this misty night (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.143), and περὶ νηυσί, “around the ships”, is the reading of the scholia and of Eustathius for the common παρά (Merry's critical note on 9.144; see the translation's note on 9.143–145). ἀήρ in Homer is mist or haze, as over the fight for the body of Patroclus, where “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon; / for they were covered in mist” (*Iliad* 17.366–368). The men see neither the island nor “the long waves rolling toward the shore” until the ships are aground, and they sleep where they land until dawn (9.146–151; see [[shore]]). Without a moon the landing is the god's: no one can see where they are going, and the harbor that needs no cable takes them in (9.136–138; see [[harbor]]).
