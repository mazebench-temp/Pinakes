---
summary: On the tenth day after Malea Odysseus reaches the land of the Lotus-eaters, “who eat a flowery food” (9.82–84). They do his scouts no harm, but the lotus they give them makes the men forget their homecoming, and he drags them back weeping to the ships (9.88–104).
---

**The tenth day.** After nine days of “deadly winds” over the fish-filled sea, “on the tenth we set foot / on the land of the Lotus-eaters, who eat a flowery food” (9.82–84; see [[malea]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). They are the first people of the tale whom the Iliad does not know: the Cicones fought for Troy (*Iliad* 2.846; see [[cicones]]), but of the Lotus-eaters the poem gives only the name and the food. ἄνθινον εἶδαρ Merry takes as “vegetable” food (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.84; see the translation's note on 9.84).

**The scouts.** The landing is told in verses that return at the island of Aeolus (9.85–87 = 10.56–58), and the sending of the scouts in verses that return at the land of the Laestrygonians (9.88–90 = 10.100–102): “then I sent some of my companions to go and find out / what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth, / choosing two men, and sending a herald with them as the third” (see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[heralds]]). σῖτον ἔδοντες, “who eat bread”, merely marks human beings, as in the Iliad it marks men against the gods (Merry on 9.89; *Iliad* 5.341; see [[earth]]). Ameis, as Merry reports, rejected verse 90, which the oldest and best manuscripts and Eustathius place before 89, as interpolated from 10.102 and as irreconcilable with 94–95, which imply that more than three men ate the lotus (Merry's critical note on 9.89–90; see the translation's note on 9.89–90).

**No harm intended.** “They went at once and mingled with the Lotus-eaters; / and the Lotus-eaters devised no destruction for our companions, / but gave them some of the lotus to taste” (9.91–93). The verb and its object return in the next book with the opposite sense: of the Laestrygonian king Antiphates, whom his wife calls from the place of assembly, the poem says that he “devised grievous destruction for them”, and he seizes one of the companions for his meal (ἐμήσατο λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον, 10.114–116, a plain rendering). The danger of the Lotus-eaters lies in what they give, not in what they intend.

**Forgetting.** “And whoever of them ate the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus / no longer wished to bring back word or to return, / but they wished to remain there among the Lotus-eaters, / munching the lotus, and to forget their homecoming” (9.94–97; see [[lotus]] and [[nostos]]). βούλοντο is “would rather” (Merry on 9.96), and ἐρεπτόμενοι, used in the Iliad of horses feeding, here has men “browsing” (Merry on 9.97). Odysseus takes them back to the ships by force, weeping, ties them under the thwarts, and orders the rest on board “for fear that one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming” (9.98–102). The Lotus-eaters do not appear again in the tale, except in its summary to Penelope, where they come second, after the Cicones (23.310–311).
