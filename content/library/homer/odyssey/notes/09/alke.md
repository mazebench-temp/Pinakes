summary: Odysseus' spirit foreboded “that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage” (9.214), and the blinded Cyclops says that he always expected someone “clothed in great courage” (9.514). From the ship Odysseus tells him that he had not been eating “a weakling's companions” (9.475).
# "ἀνάλκιδος ἀνδρὸς" (9.475); bare ἀνάλκιδος would also take Aegisthus at 3.310.
grc: ["ἀνάλκιδος ἀνδρὸς"]
en: [a weakling's]
---

The book's two uses of the formula look at the same man from both sides (see [[clothed-in-courage]]). Before he has seen the Cyclops, Odysseus takes the strong wine because his spirit foreboded “that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage, / a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (9.213–215): the idea came at once that the man they would meet would be a huge and mighty creature, who would want his wine strong (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.214; see [[wine]] and [[themis]]). After the blinding the Cyclops uses the words of the man he expected, “some tall and handsome man / to come here, clothed in great courage”, and complains that “one who is small and a nobody and feeble” has blinded him instead (9.513–516; see [[small-and-of-no-account]]). Odysseus' taunt answers the complaint before it is made: “Cyclops, it was not a weakling's companions after all that you were / to eat in your hollow cave with your mighty strength” (9.475–476), where the negative goes closely with ἀνάλκιδος, “He was no weakling whose comrades thou wast minded to eat” (Merry on 9.475; see [[kertomia]]).
