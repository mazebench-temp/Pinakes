summary: The gods as a body are named in the speeches of the tale. Odysseus begins with the sorrows the heavenly gods have given him; he bids the Cyclops respect the gods, and the Cyclops answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus “nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger”; Odysseus taunts him that Zeus and the other gods have punished him; and the Cyclops says that his father alone can heal him, “neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men”.

# and the other gods" (9.479).

**The heavenly gods.** “For the heavenly gods have given me many sorrows” (9.15): Odysseus begins his tale to Alcinous with the verse with which he began his story to Arete (7.242; see the Book 7 note and [[heavenly-gods]]).

**Far the stronger.** Odysseus bids the Cyclops respect the gods (9.269), and the Cyclops answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus “nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger” (9.276; see [[blessed-gods]] and [[zeus]]). The verse-end is what the gods say of themselves, and men of the gods: in the Iliad Poseidon tells Hera that he would not drive the gods together in strife, “we others, since we are far the stronger” (*Iliad* 20.135), and Odysseus tells Nestor that a god could easily give even better horses, “since the gods are far stronger than we are” (*Iliad* 10.557; Hector says it too, *Iliad* 20.368). The Cyclops claims for his race what the gods claim against men. Odysseus has said that the Cyclopes live “trusting in the immortal gods” (9.107), words in which Merry finds no devoutness, only a careless confidence in the bounty of nature (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.107; see [[immortals]] and [[cyclopes]]).

**The other gods.** Odysseus' first taunt ends “therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.479; see [[zeus]]), and the Cyclops, naming his father, says that no one else will heal him, “neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men” (9.521; see [[poseidon]] and [[mortal-men]]), the phrase of Odysseus' story to Arete, where no one, “neither of the gods nor of mortal men”, comes near Calypso (7.247).
