**The two taunts.** Odysseus taunts the Cyclops twice from the ship. The first time, “as far away as a man can make himself heard with a shout” (9.473), he names no one: “Yes, truly your evil deeds were to catch up with you, / hard man, since you did not shrink from eating strangers / in your own house; therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.477–479; see [[kertomia]] and [[xenia]]). The rock that answers him falls in front of the ship and its backwash drives her toward the land (9.480–486). Twice as far out, against his companions' plea, “Hard man, why do you want to provoke a savage man?” (9.494; see [[hard]]), he speaks again “with resentful heart” (9.501), and this time he gives his name, his title, his father and his house (9.502–505). The longer form of the epithet, πτολιπόρθιος, “sacker of cities”, is in the poems only here and in the prayer that answers it (9.504, 9.530; see [[sacker-of-cities]]).

**The name and the curse.** The name undoes the trick of the night before. To the other Cyclopes the blinded giant could only cry that Nobody was killing him (9.408), and his neighbors went away. Now he has a name for his father. He remembers the seer Telemus, who told him that he “would lose my sight at the hands of Odysseus” (9.507–512; see [[small-and-of-no-account]]), and he prays to Poseidon in Odysseus' own words: “grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may not reach home, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531), the two verses of the boast with “may not reach home” in place of “blinded you”. “So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536; see [[poseidon]] and [[curses]]). The second of the two verses, 9.531, has been bracketed by nearly all editors since Wolf, because twelve manuscripts and the text of Eustathius lack it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 9.531; see the translation's note on 9.531). Odysseus began his tale to the Phaeacians with the same name and his fame, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (9.19–20; see [[kleos]]); given to an enemy, the name carries the curse.

**Aristotle.** Merry notes that Aristotle alludes to this passage when he remarks that vengeance is incomplete until the guilty one knows by whose hands and for what cause it has been inflicted (Merry on 9.504; see the translation's note on 9.504). The remark belongs to the chapter of the *Rhetoric* on calming anger: men are calm toward those they think will not perceive that they suffer at their hands and in return for what they did, “for anger is against the individual”, and so the poet rightly wrote “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities”, “as though he would not have been avenged unless the other perceived both by whom and for what” (*Rhetoric* 1380b20–25; see [[praotes]] and [[orge]]). Aristotle quotes only the first words of 9.504, the words that say by whom. The cause was in the first taunt, the strangers eaten in the Cyclops' own house (9.478–479), so that between them the two speeches give the blinded man both of the things that Aristotle says anger wants him to know.
