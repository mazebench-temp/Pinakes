summary: Odysseus describes the land of the Cyclopes, overbearing and lawless herdsmen without assemblies, ordinances or ships, each giving the law to his own household (9.105–130). Polyphemus boasts that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (9.275–276); at his cry his neighbors come to the cave, and when he answers that Nobody is killing him, they bid him pray to his father and go away (9.399–413).

**Overbearing and lawless.** From the Lotus-eaters the ships come to their land:

{{quote:877815ae-6d50-5581-a2ee-c617721550b3}}

ὑπερφίαλοι, ἀθέμιστοι, “overbearing and lawless” (9.106; see [[overbearing]] and the translation's key words): ἀθέμιστος is the word of the Iliad's man “without law”, who loves war among his own people (*Iliad* 9.63; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]]). θεοῖσι πεποιθότες, “trusting in the immortal gods” (9.107), implies no devoutness, as the Cyclops' boast will show (9.275–276), but a careless confidence in the bounty of nature, which saves them all trouble; W. W. Merry compares the Cyclops of Euripides, whose earth bears grass for his flocks whether it will or not, and Herodotus' nomad Scythians, who neither sow nor plow (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.107; Euripides, *Cyclops* 332–333; Herodotus 4.19.1). The rain of Zeus makes their vines grow (9.111), and the Cyclops repeats the verse when he praises Odysseus' wine above the wine of his own land (9.357–358; see [[wine]] and [[zeus]]).

{{quote:ff4c6428-150f-5f30-9ce0-a0d53f8a6a8c}}

**A law to themselves.** θεμιστεύει is “is lord and master over”, Merry explains, and there is a sort of irony in a people who know no θέμιστες while each of them θεμιστεύει: “Laws imply Society: the Cyclopes are a law to themselves” (Merry on 9.114; see [[themis]] and [[assembly]]). Plato's Athenian quotes the four verses for the earliest form of government, the rule of the eldest in each household (*Laws* 680b–e; see [[plato-the-cyclopes]]), and Aristotle quotes the last two for the same rule in the *Politics* (1252b19–23; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]]), and again in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, with ἀλόχου, “his wife”, for the man who lives as he likes where the city neglects the upbringing of the young (1180a24–29; see the translation's note on 9.114–115). The isolation goes further in Polyphemus, who “did not go among the others, / but lived apart, and knew lawless ways” (9.188–189; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**No ships.** “For the Cyclopes have no ships with red-painted prows, / nor are there shipwrights among them” (9.125–126): μιλτοπάρῃοι is the epithet of Odysseus' own ships in the Catalogue (*Iliad* 2.637), a standing epithet here, which does not imply that the Cyclopes had ships of another color (Merry on 9.125; see [[red-painted]]). Such craftsmen would have made the island off their harbor “well settled for them”, cultivating it as well as building ships (9.130; Merry on 9.130; see [[the-island-of-goats]]). From the island the Greeks could see their smoke and hear their voices and their sheep and goats, and judged by these that the land was near (9.166–167; Merry on 9.167).

**The Cyclopes and the gods.** “For the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus / nor for the blessed gods, since we are far the stronger”, says Polyphemus (9.275–276). Merry finds the boast inconsistent with what the Cyclopes themselves acknowledge of the power of Zeus (9.411) and with Polyphemus' own claim to be Poseidon's son, but, as a piece of impious boasting, quite true to nature (Merry on 9.275; see [[aegis-bearing]]).

**The neighbors.** With the stake in his eye, Polyphemus calls “to the Cyclopes, who lived around him / in caves among the windy heights” (9.399–400). They come from all sides and stand around the cave, ask whether anyone is driving off his flocks or killing him “by trickery or by force”, and, hearing that Nobody is, answer: “If then nobody is doing you violence, alone as you are, / there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus; / but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon” (9.401–412; see [[nobody]], [[nousos]] and [[poseidon]]). οἶον ἐόντα, “alone as you are”, is “defenceless”, with no neighbors to help; they think that he has gone hopelessly mad (Merry on 9.410–411). Then they go away (9.413). Among them the seer Telemus had grown old, who foretold the blinding (9.508–512; see [[telemus]]).
