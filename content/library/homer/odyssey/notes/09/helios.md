---
summary: The sun tells the hours of the tale. At Ismarus the Achaeans hold their ground through the morning, and “when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen” the Cicones overpower them; on the island of goats the companions twice feast “all day long, until the sun went down”, and twice lie down to sleep on the shore when “the sun went down and darkness came on”. Ithaca's neighbors lie “toward the dawn and the sun”.
---

**The time for unyoking oxen.** The battle with the Cicones turns with the sun: “But when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen, / then at last the Cicones overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (9.58–59; see [[cicones]], [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]] and [[eos]]). The first verse is the Iliad's verse for the turn of the battle over Cebriones (*Iliad* 16.779; see the Iliad's Book 16 note). Merry explains the preposition in μετενίσσετο by the changed course of the sun after he has passed the zenith: up to that point his course is a rise, ἀέξετο ἦμαρ, “the sacred day was growing” (9.56), and afterwards a descent. The Sun-God is a charioteer, and at the end of the day's work he unyokes his steeds, as the plowman unyokes his oxen; Merry quotes Sophocles' sun driving his chariot over the steep sky (*Ajax* 845) and Horace's sun taking the yoke from the tired oxen (*Odes* 3.6.42), and notes that βουλυτόνδε is found in Homer only here and in the Iliad's verse (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.58; see the translation's note on 9.58). Eustathius mentions the forms μετενείσετο and μετενίσετο (Merry's critical note on 9.58), and the Iliad's text prints μετενίσετο and βουλυτὸν δέ.

**Until the sun went down.** Twice the companions feast on the shore of the island, after the hunt of the goats and after the sacrifice of the ram: “So all day long, until the sun went down, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (9.161–162 = 9.556–557; see [[the-island-of-goats]], [[feast]] and [[untold]]); and twice they sleep where they landed: “When the sun went down and darkness came on, / and then we lay down to sleep where the sea breaks upon the shore” (9.168–169 = 9.558–559; see [[sunset-lines]]). The first verse of each pair is the Iliad's. The gods feast on Olympus “all day long, until the sun went down” (*Iliad* 1.601), and the same verse ends the day of Odysseus' crew at Chryse, after the sacrifice that ends the plague: “When the sun went down and darkness came on, / they lay down to sleep beside the stern-cables of the ship” (*Iliad* 1.475–476; see the translation's formula rows).

**Toward the dawn and the sun.** Ithaca's neighbors lie apart “toward the dawn and the sun” (9.26), the east (see [[eos]], [[ithaca]] and the translation's note on 9.21–28). The god himself has no part in the book: his island and his cattle come in Book 12 (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).
