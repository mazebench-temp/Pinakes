summary: The sheep of Book 9 are the flocks slaughtered on the shore of Ismarus (9.45–46) and the Cyclops' flocks, which he herds alone and milks in his cave (9.187–188, 9.237–249); the rams under which Odysseus' men escape, while the blind master feels along the backs of his sheep (9.425–443); and the flocks driven down to the ship and divided on the island (9.464–470, 9.548–551).
# The flocks, μῆλα, μήλων, μῆλʼ (sheep and goats together, 9.184), here claimed as bare forms: in the Odyssey every
# μῆλα and μήλων is the flocks, and the apple of the orchard is μῆλον, μήλῳ (7.120), which these forms do not take.

**At Ismarus.** After the sack of the city the companions would not leave, “But there much wine was drunk, and many sheep / they slaughtered beside the shore, and shambling cattle with curving horns” (9.45–46; see [[cattle]] and [[shore]]), until the Cicones of the inland came upon them.

**The Cyclops' flocks.** The cave is the fold of “many / flocks, sheep and goats” (9.183–184; see [[goats]] and [[fold]]). μῆλα are the lesser cattle, sheep and goats together; the derivation of the word is uncertain, and W. W. Merry sets out the guesses, from μαλακός, “soft” or “woolly”, from a word for “bright” that would also suit μῆλον, the apple, and J. Grimm's, from the root of English “small” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.184). The Cyclops “herded his flocks / alone, far off” (9.187–188; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), and his day is the herdsman's day: he pastures them, drives into the cave “all those that he milked” and leaves the males outside, and then “sat down and milked the sheep and the bleating goats, / all in good order, and put her young one under each” (9.217, 9.237–245; again 9.308–309, 9.341–342). ὄις is the accusative plural (see the Book 2 note). The flocks have their epithets: πίονα μῆλα, “fat flocks” (9.217, 9.237, 9.312, 9.315, 9.337), κλυτὰ μῆλα, “his fine flocks”, which Merry, with Eustathius, takes as “fine” for their number, their fleece or their fatness (on 9.308; see the translation's table of key words), καλλίτριχα μῆλα, “fine-fleeced” (9.336, 9.469; see [[fine-maned]]), and ταναύποδα, “long-legged” (9.464). He drives them to the mountain “with much whistling” (9.315), πολλῇ ῥοίζῳ, which Merry renders “with frequent whistle” and compares with the call of the satyrs to their flock in Euripides' *Cyclops*, ψύττʼ (on 9.315; *Cyclops* 49). In the tale, apart from the men he eats, he seems to live on their milk and cheese; the Cyclopes of Euripides' *Cyclops* feed on “milk and cheeses and the meat of flocks” (*Cyclops* 122, as Merry notes on 9.234).

**Under the sheep.** The plan of escape uses the rams: “there were rams, well fed and thick of fleece, / fine and large, with violet-dark wool” (9.425–426; see [[ram]]). ἰοδνεφές is “dark”, as Eustathius takes it, not “white”, as Ernesti would have it: Homer's sheep are white, but there are black sheep too, μέλας and παμμέλας, among the victims for the dead (10.525, 10.527; Merry on 9.426). Odysseus binds them three together with willow shoots, and “Three sheep carried each man” (9.427–431; see [[willow]]). In the morning “the males of the flock rushed out to the pasture, / and the females bleated unmilked around the pens, / for their udders were bursting” (9.438–440), and the blind master “felt along the backs of all the sheep / as they stood upright; but the fool did not notice / how the men were bound under the chests of the woolly sheep” (9.441–443; see [[woolly]]). Last of all comes the ram that carries Odysseus (9.444–461).

**The flocks taken.** “And quickly the long-legged flocks, rich with fat, / we drove off, often turning them about, until we came / to the ship” (9.464–466): περιτροπέοντες, “often turning them about”, Merry would rather render “driving in” to a compact flock to bring them to the ship (on 9.465; see the translation's note on 9.465). Odysseus has “the many fine-fleeced sheep” thrown on board (9.469–470), and on the island “we took the Cyclops' flocks out of the hollow ship / and divided them” (9.548–549), as the spoil of Ismarus was divided, in the same verse (9.42).
