**The priest of Ismarus.** Maron served Apollo, who had a grove at Ismarus, the city of the Cicones that Odysseus sacked on his way from Troy (9.39–42; see [[ismarus]], [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]], [[apollo]] and [[full-of-trees]]). W. W. Merry takes the guardian of ὃς Ἴσμαρον ἀμφιβεβήκει, “who used to stand guard over Ismarus”, to be the god, as Chryses calls on Apollo, “who stand guard over Chryse” (*Iliad* 1.37), and explains the pluperfect: it looks back to the time before Ismarus fell, since a god deserted a city once the sentence of its doom had gone forth (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.198; see the translation's note on 9.198). Odysseus and his men had protected the priest, with his child and his wife, ἁζόμενοι, “in reverence”, and the reason Odysseus gives is the god: “for he lived in the grove full of trees / of Phoebus Apollo” (9.199–201). σὺν παιδί, “with his child”, is the reading of Aristarchus and Aristophanes; others read σὺν παισί, “with his children” (Merry's critical note on 9.199; see the translation's note on 9.199).

**The gifts.** In return he gave “splendid gifts” (9.201; see [[splendid]]): seven talents of well-made gold, a talent being a definite weight of gold, not coined money (Merry on 9.202; see [[talent]] and [[gold-and-silver]]), a mixing bowl all of silver (see [[mixing-bowl]]), and twelve two-handled jars of “sweet unmixed wine, a divine drink” (9.205; see [[two-handled-jar]], [[wine]] and [[divine]]). The wine was the house's secret: no one of the slaves or the handmaids knew of it, “but only he himself and his dear wife and one housekeeper” (9.207; see [[handmaids]]). When they drank it, he poured one cupful into twenty measures of water, the wine into the water, as was the custom of the earliest times (9.208–209; Merry on 9.209), and its fragrance was such that no one would have wished to hold back (9.210–211).

**The wine in the cave.** Odysseus filled a great skin with it and carried it with him, “for at once my proud spirit foreboded / that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage, / a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (9.212–215): the idea came into his mind, Merry explains, that the man would be a huge and mighty creature who would want his wine strong (on 9.214). The Cyclops, whose own land bears wine, calls it “a branch of ambrosia and nectar” (9.357–359), and it overpowers him (9.454, 9.516; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]], [[stake]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). The priest's gift is the means of the blinding.
