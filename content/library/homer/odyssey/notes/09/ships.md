summary: Odysseus' twelve ships lose six men each at Ismarus, are driven by the storm off Malea, and lie at the island off the Cyclopes' land, a people who have no ships and no shipwrights (9.60–66, 9.67–81, 9.125–130, 9.142–169). He takes his own ship to the Cyclopes; after the escape the Cyclops' rocks drive it back to the shore and forward to the island, where the other ships wait (9.173–180, 9.480–545). The Cyclops prays that Odysseus may come home on someone else's ship (9.535).

**The fleet.** “Twelve ships followed me” (9.159): at Ismarus “from each ship six of my well-greaved companions / perished” (9.60–61; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]), a symmetry that offended Zoilus, while Crates took it as an average (see the translation's note on 9.60), and the curving ships do not go on until the dead have been called three times (9.64–66; see [[curving]] and [[lament]]). The storm of Zeus drives them on slantwise and tears their sails, and they row to land (9.67–75; see [[sails]] and [[oars]]); rounding Malea, the wave, the current and the North Wind carry them off course (9.80–81; see [[malea]]). On the island off the Cyclopes' land “nine goats fell by lot” to each ship, and ten to Odysseus (9.159–160; see [[goats]]).

**A people without ships.** The island lies empty because its neighbors cannot reach it: “For the Cyclopes have no ships with red-painted prows, / nor are there shipwrights among them, who might build / well-benched ships, which might accomplish each errand, / going to the cities of men, as men often / cross the sea in ships to visit one another; / such men would also have made the island well settled for them” (9.125–130; see [[cyclopes]], [[red-painted]], [[well-benched]] and [[the-island-of-goats]]). μιλτοπάρῃοι is the epithet of Odysseus' own ships in the Catalogue (*Iliad* 2.637; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.125), and the craftsmen would have worked the island as well as built the ships (Merry on 9.130; see the translation's notes on 9.125 and 9.130). The ships come to the island blind, “some god guided us / through the dark night”, and are run ashore before the men see the land (9.142–150; see the translation's row ἐπικέλλω, κέλλω).

**Odysseus' ship.** He takes his own ship and crew to make trial of the Cyclopes (9.173–180), and leaves it by the shore with the rest of the crew to guard it (9.193–194). The Cyclops asks where it is moored, and Odysseus lies that Poseidon shattered it on the rocks (9.279–286; see [[poseidon]]): νέα, “ship”, is Aristarchus' reading, pronounced as one syllable (Merry on 9.283; see the translation's note on 9.283). The Cyclops' club is as great as the mast of “a black ship of twenty oars, / a broad freight ship, which crosses the great gulf” (9.322–323; see [[the-mast-of-twenty-oars]], [[mace]] and [[mast]]), the measure by which Odysseus also built the floor of his raft (5.249–250; see [[raft]]).

**The flight.** The men throw the Cyclops' fine-fleeced sheep on board and row away (9.469–472). The first rock falls in front of the ship, the backwash carries it back to the land, and “I took a very long pole in my hands / and pushed the ship off and away” (9.480–488; see [[steering-oar]]). If κοντός is connected with κεντέω, “prick”, it must be a pole sharp at the point; ὦσα παρέξ is more than “pushed her off”: Odysseus punts the ship some way along the shore (Merry on 9.488; see the translation's notes on 9.485–486 and 9.488–491). The second rock falls behind the ship and drives it forward to the island, “where the other / well-benched ships waited all together” (9.539–545).

**The curse.** The Cyclops prays that if Odysseus must come home, he may come “late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship” (9.534–535; see [[curses]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]): νηὸς ἐπʼ ἀλλοτρίης, the Phaeacian ship on which Odysseus was brought home (Merry on 9.535; see the translation's note on 9.535 and [[phaeacians]]). Zeus, refusing the sacrifice of the ram, ponders “how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.554–555; see [[zeus]]).
