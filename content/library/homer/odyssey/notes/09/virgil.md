---
summary: In the third book of the Aeneid the Trojans take on board Achaemenides of Ithaca, left behind in the Cyclops' cave, who tells the story of this book from the cave, and they see the blinded shepherd himself among his flocks. Merry compares Virgil's drunken Cyclops with Homer's and reports Macrobius' judgment of the two tellings.
---

- **Achaemenides.** On the Sicilian shore a starving stranger begs the Trojans to take him: “I am from my country Ithaca, a companion of unlucky Ulysses, / Achaemenides by name”, left behind by his comrades in the Cyclops' vast cave (*Aeneid* 3.588–618, a plain rendering). He saw the Cyclops seize two of their number and dash them against the rock and eat their limbs; “nor did Ulysses suffer this unpunished”: when the giant, gorged and buried in wine, lay through the cave in his sleep, vomiting gore and gobbets mixed with wine, they prayed to the great gods, drew lots for their parts, poured round him, and bored out with a sharp weapon the one huge eye that lay under his brow, “like an Argive shield or the lamp of Phoebus” (*Aeneid* 3.623–638, plain renderings; compare 9.288–293, 9.331–335, 9.371–394). Then the Trojans see the shepherd Polyphemus himself coming down to the shore among his flocks, “a monster horrible, shapeless, huge, whose eye had been taken away”, and washing the blood from the socket in the sea, and the Cyclopes run to the shore, the brothers of Etna (*Aeneid* 3.655–678); the last verse on Achaemenides repeats his words, “companion of unlucky Ulysses” (*Aeneid* 3.691): [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[cyclopes]].
- **The bent neck.** For ἀποδοχμώσας, the Cyclops lying “with his thick neck bent aside” (9.372), Merry cites Virgil's *cervicem inflexam posuit*, “he laid down his bent neck”, with the vomit of the next verses (*Aeneid* 3.631–633; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.372).
- **Macrobius' judgment.** Merry reports Macrobius' comparison of the two scenes of eating: Virgil set down the bare and brief narration of the deed, while Homer mixed in feeling and made the pain of the telling equal to the horror of the cruelty (Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 5.13, as Merry quotes him on 9.288).
