---
summary: Hesiod's Cyclopes are the three sons of Earth and Sky who made Zeus' thunderbolt, named for the one round eye in the middle of their foreheads, which the Odyssey never describes; Merry keeps them apart from Homer's herdsmen. Hesiod also tells the birth of Pegasus from Poseidon and Medusa, one of Aristotle's answers to the question how Poseidon's son could be a Cyclops.
---

- **The round eye.** Earth bore the Cyclopes, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, who gave Zeus his thunder and made his thunderbolt; they were like the gods in all else, but a single eye lay in the middle of the forehead, and they were called Cyclopes because it was round (*Theogony* 139–146). Merry notes that Hesiod, “or his interpolator”, makes the name mean “round-eyed”, and that the Homeric Cyclopes must be carefully distinguished from the Hesiodic, the children of Uranus and Gaea who forge the thunderbolts (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.106; see the translation's note on 9.106). The Odyssey speaks of the Cyclops' eye only in the singular (9.333, 9.383, 9.503): [[cyclopes]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]].
- **Pegasus.** With Medusa alone of the Gorgons the dark-haired god lay, and when Perseus cut off her head, great Chrysaor and the horse Pegasus sprang out (*Theogony* 278–281); Aristotle answered the question how a son of Poseidon could be a Cyclops with this birth and with the foals of Boreas (*Homeric Problems* fr. 395 Gigon): [[aristotle-the-cyclops-and-poseidon]] and [[gorgon]].
