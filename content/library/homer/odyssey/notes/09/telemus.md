**The oracles come home.** The Cyclops answers Odysseus' name with a groan: ὢ πόποι, “Ah” (9.506–507; see [[o-popoi]]). θέσφατα are the oracles of a seer (see [[thesphaton]]), παλαίφατα, “spoken long ago”, and ἱκάνει is “are come home to me”; W. W. Merry compares the Cyclops of Euripides, who cries out that an old oracle is fulfilled, that he was to be blinded by Odysseus on his way from Troy (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.507; Euripides, *Cyclops* 696–700; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Alcinous will speak the same verse when the prophecy of his father Nausithous (8.564–571) is fulfilled and Poseidon turns the Phaeacian ship to stone (13.172; see [[alcinous]] and [[nausithous]]).

**The seer.** The man was “tall and brave” (9.508), and he “excelled in prophecy” (9.509; see [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]). The seer, Merry notes, followed a regular profession: he was one of the public servants, δημιοεργοί, with the singer, the healer and the carpenter (17.383–385, Greek only), and a seer could read the future from the flight of birds or from dreams. Merry takes Κυκλώπεσσιν, by its place in the verse, as local: he lived and grew old among the Cyclopes, rather than prophesied for them (Merry on 9.510). χειρῶν ἐξ Ὀδυσῆος is “at the hands of Odysseus”, and ἁμαρτάνω, “lose”, is not used so elsewhere in Homer, though it is common in the tragedians (Merry on 9.512).

**The man he expected.** He had looked for “some tall and handsome man / to come here, clothed in great courage” (9.513–514), the words of Odysseus' own foreboding of him (9.214; see [[clothed-in-courage]]), and the man who came was “one who is small and a nobody and feeble” and blinded his eye with the help of wine (9.515–516; see [[small-and-of-no-account]] and [[nobody]]). For the story, see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]].
