summary: The Cicones are the first people of the tale, and the only one the Iliad knows. Odysseus sacks their city, Ismarus (9.39–42); the Cicones of the inland, who fight from chariots and on foot, come at dawn and by evening have killed six men of each ship (9.47–61), and the survivors call three times on the dead who lie “on the plain” (9.64–66).
# The Cicones of the inland without the name: their neighbors' summons, their coming and the battle (9.48-57).
refs: [9.48-57]
---

**Two kinds of Cicones.** The name comes first with the town, when the wind brings Odysseus Κικόνεσσι … Ἰσμάρῳ, “near the Cicones, / to Ismarus” (9.39–40; see [[ismarus]]), and then twice in one verse, when his men linger by the shore: “the Cicones went off and called to other Cicones, / who were their neighbors, more numerous and braver too, / living inland” (9.47–49). The people of the sacked town live by the sea; their neighbors, ἤπειρον ναίοντες, “living inland”, are set against them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.49; see the translation's note on 9.49–50). They “knew how to fight with men from chariots, / and on foot too, where there was need” (9.49–50): ἀφʼ ἵππων is the war-chariot of the Iliad, not horseback (Merry on 9.49–50; see the translation's key words and [[chariot]]). They are the allies of Troy whom the Catalogue calls αἰχμηταί, “spearmen” (*Iliad* 2.846; see [[spearmen]] and [[euphemus]]), and they fight like the Iliad's warriors, with bronze-fitted spears, from morning until the afternoon (9.54–58; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]).

**Their victory.** They come “as many as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season” (9.51; see [[leaves-and-flowers]]), and the verse of the outcome names them again: “then at last the Cicones overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (9.59; see [[achaeans]]). Six well-greaved companions from each ship perish, and before the ships go on the survivors call three times on each of those “who had died on the plain, cut down by the Cicones” (9.60–66; see [[the-call-to-the-dead]]). The battle was fought beside the ships (9.54), and the dead lie ἐν πεδίῳ, “on the plain” (9.66).

**A raid that goes wrong.** In the tale he later tells Eumaeus, the Cretan whose part Odysseus plays leads a raid in Egypt that ends the same way: his men, giving way to insolence, ravage the fields, carry off the women and children and kill the men, and at dawn, at the cry, the Egyptians come and fill the plain with foot soldiers and chariots, and kill many of the raiders (14.259–271; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[egypt]]). In both the leader bids the men stay with the ships or flee, and they will not, and in both the help that comes from the country fights both on foot and from chariots. The Cicones are named once more in the poem, at the head of the summary Odysseus gives Penelope: he told her “how he first overcame the Cicones” (23.310, a plain rendering).
