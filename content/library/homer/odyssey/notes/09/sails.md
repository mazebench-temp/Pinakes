---
summary: In the storm after Ismarus the wind tears the sails in three and four pieces, and the men lower them and row to land; on the third day they set up the masts and hoist the white sails again (9.70–78). At the island off the Cyclopes' land they lower all the sails when the ships are beached (9.149).
---

“Then the ships were driven on slantwise, and their sails / the force of the wind tore in three and four pieces. / And we lowered them into the ships, fearing destruction, / and rowed the ships themselves with all speed toward the land” (9.70–73; see [[ships]], [[oars]] and [[boreas]]). ἐπικάρσιαι is “slantwise”, with the wind abeam, Apollonius' and Nitzsch's sense, which W. W. Merry adopts against Eustathius' “head downwards” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.70; see the translation's row ἐπικάρσιος and its note on 9.70). After two days and nights on shore, “we set up the masts and hoisted the white sails / and sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (9.77–78; see [[mast]], [[white]] and [[steering-oar]]), until the wave, the current and the North Wind drive them off course at Malea (9.80–81). On the island they come ashore in the dark, “And when the ships were beached we lowered all the sails” (9.149): κελσάσῃσι νηυσί is a dative where a genitive absolute would be expected, as if the ships had their sails taken down for them (Merry on 9.149; see the translation's note on 9.149).
