summary: The Cyclops' complaint after the blinding, that a prophecy had warned him of Odysseus, but he had looked for a tall and handsome man and was overcome by a small one, a nobody. The line plays οὐτιδανός against the false name Nobody; Aristotle quotes it with ἀεικής, “unseemly”, where our text has ἄκικυς, “feeble”, and Cicero turned its first words into a joke.

**The expected man.** Odysseus has just shouted his name from the ship, and the Cyclops recognizes it: the seer Telemus, son of Eurymus, “a man tall and brave”, who grew old among the Cyclopes, had told him “that I would lose my sight at the hands of Odysseus” (9.507–512; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). He had expected the blinder to be like the seer, “some tall and handsome man / to come here, clothed in great courage” (9.513–514). Odysseus had foreseen him in the same words before he climbed to the cave, “that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage, / a savage” (9.214–215; see [[clothed-in-courage]]): each expected the other to come “clothed in great courage”, and the one found a monster and the other a small man.

**A nobody.** οὐτιδανός, “a nobody”, is a word of contempt in the Iliad, for the men Agamemnon rules, for what Achilles would be called if he yielded, and for the archer whose shaft is blunt (*Iliad* 1.231, 1.293, 11.390; see the translation's key words), and the Cyclops has already set it beside the false name, “the evils that Nobody, a nobody, gave me” (9.460). Here he no longer says Οὖτις, “Nobody”: he has the true name and keeps the insult. ἄκικυς, “feeble”, is the word Telemachus will use of himself when he fails to string the bow (21.131). It is formed on κῖκυς, “vigor”, which, Merry suggests, may belong to the root of κινέω and mean strength shown in movement; the noun comes once in the poem, of the shade of Agamemnon, in whom there was no longer steadfast strength “or any vigor” (11.393; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.515).

**The variant.** Merry reports ἀεικής, “unseemly”, as a reading in a scholium and in Eustathius, and Aristotle quotes the verse with that word (*Poetics* 1458b25–26; Merry's critical note on 9.515; see the translation's note on 9.515–516). His point is the diction. Put into standard words, “but now one who is little and weakly and ugly”, the verse keeps its sense and its meter and loses its distinction (*Poetics* 1458b25–28; see [[kyrion]] and [[aristotle]]).

**Cicero's joke.** Cicero wrote to his brother Quintus, still away in his province, that the arrival of Statius, whom Quintus had sent to him, had one advantage: had Statius come home with Quintus, unseen before, there would have been expectation and a crowd, but now “people's talk is exhausted, and many remarks of this kind have already been let fly: ‘ἀλλὰ αἰεί τινα φῶτα μέγαν’”, “but always some great man”, and he was glad that they had been spent in his brother's absence (*Letters to Quintus* 1.2.1, a plain rendering; see [[cicero]]). The Greek is the beginning of the Cyclops' verse, “But I always expected some tall and handsome man” (9.513).
