summary: The verb's two senses meet in the Cyclops' prayer. Odysseus claims to be one of Agamemnon's people (9.263) and hopes that Athena may grant him “triumph” (9.317); the Cyclopes tell Polyphemus to pray to his father (9.412); and Polyphemus, who says of Poseidon “he claims to be my father” (9.519), prays to him “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (9.527–536).
# εὖχος "triumph" (9.317).
en: [triumph]
---

**Claims.** εὔχομαι εἶναι is “claim to be” (see the translation's table of key words): “And we claim to be the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus” (9.263), Odysseus tells the Cyclops, as a title to respect (see [[agamemnon]] and [[kleos]]). The Cyclops makes the same claim for his father, the other way round: “for I am his son, and he claims to be my father” (πατὴρ δʼ ἐμὸς εὔχεται εἶναι, 9.519; see [[poseidon]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Triumph.** In the cave, left alone with the plan, Odysseus broods “to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph” (δοίη δέ μοι εὖχος Ἀθήνη, 9.317; see [[athena]] and [[poine]]): εὖχος is the boast of victory, the thing a man may claim.

**Prayer.** The other Cyclopes give the blinded Polyphemus a god's remedy: “but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon” (εὔχεο, 9.412; see [[nousos]]). He does, once he knows the name: he “prayed, stretching out his hands to the starry sky” (εὔχετο, 9.527), and his prayer joins both senses of the verb, “Hear me, Poseidon, dark-haired earth-holder, / if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (εὔχεαι εἶναι, 9.528–529); “So he spoke in prayer, and the dark-haired god heard him” (εὐχόμενος, 9.536; see [[prayer]] and [[curses]]). The claim of kinship is the ground of the prayer, as a worshiper's past gifts are elsewhere (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
